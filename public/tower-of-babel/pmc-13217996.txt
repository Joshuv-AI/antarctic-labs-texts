Development of a one-tube PAM-independent RCNPM platform using Cas12a for ultra-rapid simultaneous miR-499 and cTnT detection in early acute myocardial infarction diagnosis
Authors: Jianguang Guo, Qingsong Wu, Fei Jiang, Meiling Cai, Linfeng Xie, Yuling Xie, Xinfan Lin, Xingfeng Chen, Siying Luo, Xiaofu Dai, Liangwan Chen
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundAcute myocardial infarction (AMI) benefits from biomarkers and assays that support early triage. Cardiac troponin T (cTnT) is widely used, but may be less informative early after ischemia and can be elevated in non-AMI conditions. Circulating miR-499 has been reported to rise early after myocardial injury, yet miRNA detection is complicated by short target length and PAM constraints in many CRISPR-Cas12a designs. We developed a PAM-independent, one-tube isothermal RT–RPA–Cas12a platform (RCNPM) for miR-499 and evaluated its analytical performance and clinical discrimination, with routine clinical cTnT analyzed in parallel.MethodsRCNPM integrates stem-loop reverse transcription, recombinase polymerase amplification (RPA), and Cas12a trans-cleavage fluorescence readout at 37 °C in a single vessel. Primers and reaction components were screened/optimized, and specificity was assessed using non-target miRNAs. Clinical evaluation was performed in a single-center prospective cohort (n = 150; 75 AMI meeting ESC criteria, 75 controls) with random coding and blinded miR-499 testing. RCNPM was benchmarked against RT–qPCR (Spearman correlation; Bland–Altman). Serum cTnT was obtained from routine hospital immunoassay testing and was not measured within the RCNPM reaction. ROC analyses were performed with 95% CIs.ResultsRCNPM detected miR-499 down to 1 aM and showed no detectable signal in the non-target miRNA panel. The RT–RPA–Cas12a step required ~ 30 min (workflow including extraction ~ 45–60 min). In the cohort, RCNPM agreed with RT–qPCR (Spearman r = 0.96, p < 0.001) with minimal mean bias. In this dataset, ROC analyses yielded AUC = 1.00 (95% CI 1.00–1.00) for miR-499 (RCNPM and RT–qPCR) and routine cTnT.ConclusionsRCNPM enables PAM-independent, one-tube isothermal miR-499 detection with strong agreement to RT–qPCR in a single-center cohort. External validation in larger, multi-center and more heterogeneous disease-control cohorts is warranted.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12967-026-08116-y.Keywords: Acute myocardial infarction, RCNP, miR-499, cTnT, Cas12a, Translational diagnostics

Introduction

Acute myocardial infarction (AMI), most commonly triggered by acute coronary artery occlusion, causes irreversible myocardial ischemic necrosis and remains a leading cause of death worldwide [1]. The first hour after symptom onset carries a high risk of sudden death; without timely treatment, mortality can reach 30–50% [2]. Survivors continue to face elevated long-term mortality and a higher risk of impaired cardiac function. Clinical diagnosis relies on electrocardiography and circulating biomarkers, including creatine kinase-MB (CK-MB), cardiac troponin T (cTnT), cardiac troponin I (cTnI), and myoglobin (Myo); cardiac troponins are widely used for diagnosis and risk stratification [3]. However, troponin elevations are also observed in heart failure, renal disease, and infection, among other conditions [4]. In addition, troponin often becomes detectable only several hours after ischemia (typically 3–6 h), leaving a time window in which early cases may be difficult to classify. Biomarkers that provide earlier information, together with detection strategies compatible with point-of-care testing (POCT), remain needed.

Circulating microRNAs (miRNAs) have emerged as candidate molecular markers of myocardial injury. Multiple studies report early increases in miR-1, miR-133a, miR-208a, and miR-499 after AMI; miR-499 correlates with the extent of myocardial necrosis and shows relatively high early detectability in several cohorts [5–7]. Circulating miRNAs can be stabilized in extracellular vesicles or protein complexes, supporting their measurement in clinical specimens [8, 9]. Reverse transcription quantitative PCR (RT-qPCR) remains the dominant analytical approach, offering high sensitivity, yet it typically requires multi-step workflows, prolonged turnaround time, and dedicated instrumentation. These features limit direct deployment in POCT settings that prioritize speed, procedural simplicity, and minimized open handling [10].

Technical barriers in miRNA detection stem from short sequence length, low abundance, and high sequence homology, which complicate primer and probe design [11]. Isothermal amplification methods such as recombinase polymerase amplification (RPA), together with CRISPR-based diagnostics, provide a potential route toward nucleic-acid POCT [12, 13]. Many Cas12a assays require a protospacer adjacent motif (PAM) proximal to the target sequence, a constraint that is particularly restrictive for short miRNA targets, reducing design flexibility and limiting assay generalizability [14]. A constant-temperature, closed-tube workflow that integrates reverse transcription, isothermal amplification, and CRISPR-enabled signal readout, while reducing dependence on PAM availability, would be expected to improve practicality and broaden applicability for miRNA-based POCT.

This study establishes a PAM-independent, one-tube isothermal detection system (RCNPM) for rapid measurement of AMI-related miRNA biomarkers. The system integrates reverse transcription, RPA amplification, and Cas12a-mediated signal readout under isothermal conditions. Analytical performance is characterized using miR-499 as the primary target, followed by evaluation of diagnostic-relevant performance and sample tolerance in clinical AMI specimens. The study aims to provide a nucleic-acid testing strategy aligned with POCT needs for early AMI screening and risk assessment.

Materials and methods

Study design and ethical approval

This prospective methodological validation study developed and validated a PAM-independent, one-tube RT–RPA–Cas12a detection system (RCNPM) for quantifying plasma miR-499, and evaluated its diagnostic-relevant performance in clinical samples from patients with AMI. A total of 150 participants were enrolled, including 75 patients with AMI and 75 controls. All participants provided written informed consent. The study protocol was approved by the Ethics Committee of the First Affiliated Hospital of Nanjing Medical University and conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki (2013).

Materials and reagents

Details of the manufacturers, catalog numbers, and batch numbers for all key reagents are summarized in Table S1.

Design and purification of crRNA

miR-499, an AMI-associated miRNA biomarker, was selected as the target (miRBase ID: MIMAT0002870). A 17–20 nt target-specific region was used as the crRNA spacer. The crRNA template was constructed by appending a T7 promoter sequence at the 5′ end (5′-TAATACGACTCACTATA-3′) and a hairpin structure at the 3′ end; template sequences are provided in Table S2.

The DNA template was generated from two complementary synthetic oligonucleotides (HPLC purity ≥ 98%). Forward and reverse oligonucleotides (1 µL each) were mixed with annealing buffer (10 mM Tris-HCl, 50 mM NaCl, 1 mM EDTA, pH 8.0) to a final volume of 50 µL, heated at 95 °C for 5 min, and allowed to cool to 25 °C to form double-stranded DNA.

In vitro transcription was performed using the HiScribe® T7 High Yield RNA Synthesis Kit (NEB, E2040) at 37 °C for 16 h in a 20 µL reaction containing 1 µg DNA template. Residual DNA template was removed by treatment with RNase-free DNase I (NEB, M0303) at 37 °C for 30 min. The RNA product was purified by phenol/chloroform extraction followed by ethanol precipitation, washed twice with 75% ethanol, air-dried, resuspended in 10–20 µL DEPC-treated water, and stored at − 80 °C.

crRNA concentration and purity were measured using a NanoDrop 2000 spectrophotometer (A260/A280 = 1.9–2.1). Size and integrity were assessed using an Agilent 2100 Bioanalyzer (RNA 6000 Nano kit); where indicated, denaturing urea-PAGE was used to confirm a single band [15].

RPA primer design and screening

Typical recombinase polymerase amplification (RPA) primers are ~ 30 nt in length, whereas miRNA targets are short (20–23 nt). To generate an amplicon compatible with RPA primer binding, a stem-loop reverse-transcription strategy was used to extend miR-499 and provide an appropriate sequence context for downstream amplification.

Candidate primers were designed using miRNA Design v1.01 and evaluated with OligoAnalyzer 3.1 (IDT) for GC content, secondary structure, and dimerization propensity. To improve binding stability, a 3–6 bp GC-rich segment was introduced at the 5′ end of the template, yielding multiple candidates forward primers (F1–F3 and F2-1–F2-3). Reverse primers of different lengths (R1–R2) were designed to target the downstream region.

All primer pairs were tested under identical amplification conditions (37 °C, 30 min) using the TwistAmp Basic Kit (TwistDx, UK). Amplification performance was assessed by real-time fluorescence monitoring, including time-to-threshold (Tt), amplification efficiency (E = (10^(− 1/slope) − 1) × 100%), the linear correlation coefficient (R²), and ΔCt relative to qPCR. Each experiment was performed in triplicate (n = 3), and data are reported as mean ± standard deviation (SD). The selected primer sequences are listed in Table S3.

RCNPM assay and fluorescence readout

RCNPM (Reverse Transcription–Coupled Nucleic acid–mediated PAM-free Cas12a Module) was used to detect miR-499 in plasma total RNA. The assay integrates stem-loop reverse transcription, RPA-based isothermal amplification, and Cas12a-mediated fluorescent reporter cleavage in a single tube; RNA templates were added directly to the reaction without a separate reverse-transcription step. Serum cTnT concentrations were obtained from routine hospital laboratory testing (automated Roche Elecsys) and analyzed in parallel with miR-499 results within the same cohort; cTnT was not included in the RCNPM reaction.

The total reaction volume was 25 µL. The RT–RPA mixture contained 4.25 µL Buffer A, 0.8 µL dNTPs (25 mM), 1 µL miR-499a-5p forward primer (10 µM), 1 µL miR-499a-5p reverse primer (10 µM), 0.5 µL reverse transcriptase (20 U/µL), 2 µL stem-loop primer (2 µM), 9.7 µL RNase-free water, and the RPA lyophilized enzyme pellet (TwistDx, UK). Buffer B (1.25 µL) was placed in the tube cap and mixed into the reaction by brief centrifugation (500 rpm, 10 s). RNA template (2 µL; total RNA 100 ng) was then added.

The Cas12a readout mixture comprised 0.5 µL Cas12a protein (2 µM), 1 µL fluorescent reporter (50 µM; 5′-FAM-TTATT-BHQ1-3′), 1 µL crRNA (0.68 ng/µL), and 17.5 µL Cas12a reaction buffer (NEBuffer 2.1; NEB, USA). After combining the two components, reactions were incubated at 37 °C with real-time monitoring for up to 90 min. Fluorescence was recorded on a Bio-Rad CFX96 Real-Time PCR System (Ex/Em = 485/528 nm) at 30 s intervals.

No-template controls (NTC) were prepared by replacing the RNA template with RNase-free/nuclease-free water while keeping all other components identical, and were used to monitor background signal and potential contamination. A synthetic miR-499 oligonucleotide (10⁶ copies/µL) served as the positive control. The fluorescence threshold was defined as the NTC mean plus 3× s.d. The limit of detection (LOD) was calculated from fitted signal–concentration curves, with the lowest concentration meeting S/N ≥ 3 defined as the LOD.

Plasma total RNA extraction and template input

Thawed plasma samples were processed on ice. Total RNA, including small RNA species, was extracted from plasma using a commercial miRNA/total RNA extraction kit under RNase-free conditions. Briefly, 200 µL plasma was mixed with lysis buffer, followed by chloroform addition and centrifugation for phase separation where applicable. The aqueous phase was mixed with an equal volume of absolute ethanol and loaded onto a spin column or magnetic beads according to the manufacturer’s instructions. After sequential washes and drying, RNA was eluted in 20 µL RNase-free water. RNA concentration was measured by NanoDrop or Qubit and samples were stored at − 80 °C to avoid repeated freeze–thaw cycles (doi: [16]).

For each reaction, 100 ng total RNA (2 µL) was used as the template input and applied consistently across analytical evaluation and clinical sample testing [17, 18].

RT-qPCR detection

qRT–PCR was performed to validate miR-499 expression. Reactions were run in a total volume of 20 µL for both the target and the reference gene (U6). For the target assay, the reaction contained 10 µL 2× miRNA Unimodal SYBR qPCR Master Mix (Vazyme), 0.4 µL forward primer (10 µM), 0.4 µL reverse primer (10 µM), 2 µL cDNA template generated by stem-loop reverse transcription, and nuclease-free water to 20 µL. The U6 assay used the same formulation with U6 primer pairs substituted. Reactions were performed on a Bio-Rad CFX96 system with the following program: 95 °C for 5 min; 40 cycles of 95 °C for 10 s and 60 °C for 30 s; followed by melt-curve analysis from 65 °C to 95 °C (0.5 °C per 5 s). NTC and positive controls were included. Ct values were computed automatically. Relative expression was calculated using the 2−ΔΔCt method. Amplification efficiency was calculated from the standard curve as E (%) = (10− 1/slope-1) × 100%. Each experiment was performed in triplicate (n = 3), and data are presented as mean ± s.d. Primer sequences are listed in Table S3.

Clinical sample collection and processing

For the AMI group, blood was collected at the first venipuncture upon emergency presentation or admission, coinciding with routine clinical sampling for the initial cTnT test, and whenever possible before thrombolysis, coronary intervention, or other treatments that could affect circulating biomarkers. The interval from symptom onset to blood draw (symptom-to-draw time) was recorded for prespecified stratified analyses. Controls comprised (i) disease controls presenting with chest pain in whom AMI was ultimately excluded (first emergency-department venipuncture) and (ii) healthy controls recruited from health examinations (routine venipuncture on the examination day).

Blood was processed within 2 h. Plasma was separated by centrifugation at 3,000 × g for 10 min at 4 °C, transferred to RNase-free microtubes, and immediately stored at − 80 °C. Sample quality control followed the original protocol; samples were considered inadequate if centrifugation was delayed for > 2 h, or if severe hemolysis, lipemia, icterus, or insufficient volume was present. Samples were randomly coded and tested in a blinded manner.

Inclusion criteria were age ≥ 18 years; AMI cases meeting ESC diagnostic criteria (dynamic cTn elevation plus evidence of ischemia) with symptom-to-draw time recorded; and controls classified as disease controls or healthy controls. Exclusion criteria were lack of informed consent; inadequate sample processing/quality; or other conditions precluding reliable assessment (e.g., missing key data). Common comorbidities (hypertension, diabetes, dyslipidemia, stable chronic diseases) were not used as exclusion criteria (Figure S1). Diagnostic-relevant performance was reported separately for disease controls. The corresponding cTnT values for all participants were retrieved from the hospital’s laboratory information system (LIS) to benchmark the diagnostic performance of RCNPM. Prespecified stratification by symptom-to-draw time was applied, and time-stratum metrics were reported in the Statistical analysis and Results sections.

Sample size assessment

The sample size assessment is based on the receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curve power analysis [19]. Assuming a significance level of α = 0.05 (two-tailed), an expected diagnostic performance area under the curve (AUC) = 0.90, and a group ratio of 1:1, calculations indicate that a minimum sample size of 75 subjects per group (AMI group = 75, control group = 75, total n = 150) is required to achieve adequate statistical power.

Precision assessment

Intra-assay and inter-assay variability were assessed. Quantitative output was defined as the net fluorescence signal at 30 min (ΔF₃₀min = F₃₀min − F₀min) after incubation at 37 °C; where indicated, ΔF₃₀min was converted to concentration using the standard curve. A single run was defined as one round of testing performed on the same day using a freshly prepared RCNPM master mix from the same reagent batch. Inter-assay experiments were conducted on different days, with the reaction mixture prepared de novo each day and including negative controls and NTC to monitor background signals.

Intra-assay precision was evaluated on the same day under fixed operator and instrument conditions by performing 20 replicate measurements at each of three concentration levels (low, medium, and high) and calculating the coefficient of variation (CV%). Inter-assay precision was evaluated across three separate days by performing at least five replicate measurements per day at the same three levels and calculating the across-day CV%.

To assess process-level variability, representative plasma samples were subjected to independent miRNA extractions prior to RCNPM testing, and CV% was calculated for both the “assay-only” workflow and the full workflow (extraction plus assay) using the same intra- and inter-assay designs. CV% was calculated as CV% = SD/Mean × 100%.

Statistical analysis

All experiments were conducted with at least three independent biological replicates (n ≥ 3), and the results are presented as mean ± SD. Statistical analyses were performed using the following methods: comparisons between two groups were analyzed using a two-tailed unpaired Student’s t-test; comparisons among multiple groups were conducted using one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) followed by Tukey’s post hoc correction. Depending on the data distribution, Pearson or Spearman correlation tests were applied. The LOD was calculated using the formula LOD = 3 × SD/slope, where slope refers to the regression slope of the standard curve. The R2 was derived by fitting the concentration gradient standard curve (10− 6-10− 15 M). The ROC curve and AUC (95% confidence interval [CI]) were plotted and analyzed separately for RCNPM-detected miR-499 and clinically measured cTnT to evaluate diagnostic performance (AMI vs. Control group). Sensitivity and specificity were optimized to determine the best cut-off point using the Youden index. Data processing and visualization were completed using GraphPad Prism v10.0 (GraphPad Software, USA), SPSS v26.0 (IBM, USA), and R v4.2.2 (R Foundation, Austria). Statistical significance was defined as p < 0.05, and all statistical tests were performed as two-tailed analyses.

Results

RCNPM workflow and PAM-independent Cas12a activation for miR-499

As shown in Fig. 1, RCNPM provides an integrated workflow from plasma samples to a fluorescence readout. Following miRNA extraction, miR-499 is added directly to a single-tube reaction and generates a fluorescence signal at 37 °C. The overall turnaround time for extraction plus detection is approximately 45–60 min, with the RT–RPA–Cas12a reaction step requiring ~ 30 min.

Note: At 37 °C, miR-499 is reverse-transcribed using a stem-loop primer to generate cDNA, followed by RPA-based isothermal amplification to produce a target sequence. The amplicon is recognized by the Cas12a/crRNA-499a-5p complex, activating Cas12a trans-cleavage activity and resulting in cleavage of the fluorescent reporter (FAM–TCCCT–BHQ) to generate a fluorescence signal that can be recorded in real time or visualized at the endpoint. Schematic of the main reaction components, including the miR-499-specific RPA primer pair (F2-3/R1), the Cas12a–crRNA complex, the fluorescent reporter, dNTPs, reverse transcriptase, and RPA amplification reagents

Mechanistic validation is shown in Figure S2. Stem-loop priming generates a cDNA template for amplification (Figure S2A), followed by RPA-based isothermal amplification using the selected primer pair (F2-3/R1) (Figure S2B). The amplification product is recognized by crRNA-499a-5p, which activates Cas12a trans-cleavage activity (Figure S2C–D), leading to cleavage of the fluorescent reporter (FAM–TCCCT–BHQ) and a measurable fluorescence signal. Fluorescence was recorded in real time using a fluorescence reader and could also be visualized at the endpoint under blue or UV illumination (Fig. 1). In this assay, Cas12a activation does not require a canonical PAM (5′-TTTV-3′) sequence (Figure S2).

PAM-independent Cas12a activation in the RCNPM workflow

To assess whether Cas12a activation depends on a canonical TTTV PAM, dsDNA activators with an identical protospacer but different PAM contexts were generated, together with a 2-nt mismatch activator (MM(2nt)) and a NTC (Figure S3A). Under identical Cas12a/crRNA and reporter conditions, both PAM (+) and PAM (−) activators produced clear increases in fluorescence over time (Figure S3B). In contrast, MM(2nt) yielded markedly reduced fluorescence signals that approached the NTC background, indicating that the reaction is primarily driven by crRNA–protospacer complementarity. Endpoint analysis at 30 min (ΔRFU₃₀min) was consistent with these observations, with PAM (+) and PAM (−) showing higher ΔRFU₃₀min values than MM(2nt) and NTC (Figure S3C).

RPA primer optimization and specificity assessment

RPA primer structures were systematically screened and optimized within the RCNPM workflow (Fig. 2A). Multiple candidates forward primers were generated by introducing a non-complementary 5′ synthetic tail while maintaining complementarity at the 3′ end to the target sequence. A panel of reverse primers partially overlapping the target region was designed and paired with the candidate forward primers for screening. Fluorescence readouts varied across primer combinations, with several pairs producing more pronounced fluorescence increases (Fig. 2B–C).

Based on the initial screen, the effect of overlap (complementarity) length at the template 3′ end on amplification performance was further evaluated (Fig. 2D). Heatmap and real-time fluorescence analyses showed a progressive increase in signal with increasing complementarity, with the signal becoming more stable when the complementary region reached 7–11 bp (Fig. 2E–F). Potential cross-reactivity was assessed using homologous miR-449a family members and mismatch controls (Figure S4). At both 10×LOD and 10³×LOD input levels, miR-449a produced a clear fluorescence signal, whereas miR-449b, miR-449c, and the 1-nt and 2-nt mismatch controls (MM1 and MM2) remained at low background levels comparable to the NTC (Figure S4).

Validation of system stability, reproducibility, and linearity

To characterize the linear range and limit of detection, serial dilutions of miR-499 were tested in parallel with RT–qPCR (Fig. 4). RT–qPCR yielded an LOD of 10 aM across the 0.1 aM–100 fM dilution series (Fig. 4A). Ct values showed a linear relationship with log concentration over 10 aM–100 fM (R² = 0.9766) (Fig. 4B), consistent with the concentration-dependent trends observed in the amplification curves and heatmap (Fig. 4C). Under the same dilution conditions, RCNPM produced a distinguishable fluorescence readout at lower inputs, with an LOD of 0.1 aM (Fig. 4D). Endpoint fluorescence was also linearly related to log concentration over 10 aM–100 fM (R² = 0.9569) (Fig. 4E), and the heatmap showed a progressive decrease in signal with decreasing concentration (Fig. 4F). A summary of analytical performance and operational features is provided in Table S4: the miR-499 LOD was 0.1 aM for RCNPM and 10 aM for RT–qPCR, with the same sample input volume (2 µL) for both assays. The core RCNPM reaction was performed isothermally at 37 °C (~ 30 min), whereas RT–qPCR required separate reverse transcription and thermal cycling steps (~ 110 min in this study). As a reference, serum hs-cTnT was measured by clinical ECLIA (manufacturer-reported LOD, 3.0 ng/L) and analyzed in parallel with miR-499 within the same cohort; cTnT was not a target within the RCNPM reaction (Table S4).

Analytical sensitivity and specificity of the one-tube RCNPM assay

To compare analytical sensitivity between the stepwise and one-tube formats, both workflows were optimized and evaluated using the same dilution framework (Fig. 5A, Figures S5A–D and S6A–D). Under the optimized conditions, the stepwise assay achieved an LOD of 10 aM (Fig. 5B and Figure S7A). The one-tube RCNPM assay achieved an LOD of 1 aM (Fig. 5D and Figure S7B) and supported endpoint visualization under blue-light illumination (Figure S8).

Cohort characteristics and clinical cTnT distribution (AMI vs. controls)

Figure 6A summarizes the overall analytical framework; 75 patients with AMI and 75 healthy controls were enrolled, and baseline characteristics are provided in Table S5. Figure 6B shows concordance between the RCNPM miRNA readout and RT–qPCR. Serum cTnT concentrations were obtained from routine hospital laboratory testing, with higher cTnT levels in the AMI group than in controls [2.60 (2.13–3.61) ng/mL vs. 0.02 (0.01–0.03) ng/mL; p = 1.64 × 10⁻²⁶] (Fig. 6C). Age and sex distributions were comparable between groups, with a modestly higher BMI in the AMI group (Fig. 6D); hemolysis indices and the proportion of hemolyzed samples did not differ between groups (Fig. 6E). Sample-handling analyses indicated no significant differences in cTnT across 1–3 freeze–thaw cycles (p = 0.36) (Fig. 6F), and no significant associations between cTnT and either hemolysis index or processing delay time (Fig. 6G–H). In this cohort, the cTnT distributions for AMI and controls did not overlap (Fig. 6C), yielding an ROC AUC of 1.00 (95% CI, 1.00–1.00); results were consistent across stratified analyses by sex, age, and sample-quality metrics (Figure S9).

Agreement between RCNPM and RT–qPCR for miR-499 quantification

To assess agreement between miRNA measurements, miR-499 levels in AMI and control samples were measured in parallel by RCNPM and RT–qPCR. The two readouts were positively correlated (Spearman r = 0.96; p < 0.001) (Fig. 7A). Bland–Altman analysis showed a mean difference close to zero with relatively narrow 95% limits of agreement, with no evident systematic bias (Fig. 7B). In this cohort, the distributions of miR-499 did not overlap between the AMI and control groups, yielding ROC AUC values of 1.00 (95% CI, 1.00–1.00) for both RCNPM and RT–qPCR; DeLong’s test did not indicate a difference between the two curves (p = 1.000) (Fig. 7C). Similar patterns were observed in the prespecified high-quality subset (hemolysis index ≤ 30 and freeze–thaw cycles ≤ 2) (Fig. 7D).

Discussion

Currently, AMI is typically diagnosed by integrating ECG with biochemical biomarkers (Figure S10). Although these approaches are well established in clinical workflows, differences remain in their effective diagnostic window, operational requirements and accessibility in early presentation. Circulating miRNAs have been proposed as complementary early markers, but their clinical translation has been limited by analytical sensitivity, workflow complexity and contamination control [14]. Cas12a-based strategies have been explored for miRNA detection; however, sensitivity and generalizability remain constrained in several implementations. Here, a one-tube integrated strategy was developed and evaluated for miR-499 detection, coupling reverse transcription, RPA amplification and Cas12a-mediated reporter cleavage within a single reaction vessel.

To address the short length of miRNA targets and associated primer-design constraints, an artificial 5′ extension was introduced into the upstream primer design space to expand the effective amplification template and enable systematic screening of RPA primers. Cas12a activation in this system did not require a canonical PAM (5′-TTTV-3′), a property with practical relevance for short miRNA targets. Signal generation relied on a single-stranded fluorescent reporter that is cleaved upon target recognition and Cas12a trans-cleavage activation. A comparison with representative molecular diagnostic approaches is summarized in Table S6, and the clinical-sample data support feasibility of this workflow within the present cohort.

A key design feature of RCNPM is the coordination of reverse transcription with RPA/Cas12a readout under isothermal conditions, reducing the need for stepwise transfer between separate reactions. A split-crRNA Cas12a strategy has been reported for direct RNA detection without prior reverse transcription or amplification, but it still relies on PAM-containing double-stranded DNA activators to stabilize the Cas12a–crRNA complex and requires dual Cas12a components, increasing system complexity and cost [20]. In contrast, the present workflow employed a reverse transcriptase compatible with 37 °C, enabling coordinated reverse transcription, amplification and Cas12a readout within a single temperature range.

Importantly, in this study the RCNPM reaction targets miR-499. Serum cTnT values were obtained through routine hospital laboratory testing and used for parallel, cohort-level analyses alongside the miRNA readout. The cTnT protein itself was not introduced into the RCNPM reaction. A related question is how protein biomarkers could be integrated into a nucleic-acid readout module, which can be discussed as a potential extension rather than an implemented component of the present study. Prior work has described frameworks that convert protein-binding events into amplifiable nucleic-acid signals compatible with unified readout schemes [21, 22]. One feasible approach is to use dual-site affinity probes carrying oligonucleotide tags (for example, antibody–oligonucleotide conjugates or aptamer probes). When both probes bind the same protein molecule, proximity-driven extension or ligation can generate a double-stranded DNA barcode that encodes RPA primer-binding sites and a crRNA recognition site, thereby enabling isothermal amplification and Cas12a readout [23]. Related “protein-to-DNA” proximity strategies have been validated in proximity extension assays (PEA) and proximity-enabled CRISPR protein detection frameworks [24, 25]. These examples are included to clarify a plausible technical route and do not represent an experimental procedure performed in this study.

Across the overall cohort and prespecified strata, ROC analyses yielded AUC values of 1.00. This indicates clear separation of marker distributions between AMI and controls in the present dataset. An AUC of 1.00 should not be interpreted as an expected outcome across all real-world clinical settings. The estimate may be influenced by sample size, reduced numbers after stratification and cohort composition. Because controls primarily comprised healthy individuals or patients in whom AMI was excluded, additional disease-control groups that can elevate cTnT or alter circulating miRNAs were not fully represented. External validation in larger cohorts with broader disease controls will therefore be required to assess generalizability under more heterogeneous clinical conditions.

Although the RT–RPA–Cas12a core reaction is conducted within a closed tube, upstream handling steps (RNA extraction, pipetting and sample aliquoting) remain potential sources of contamination. Accordingly, contamination risk management still depends on standardized handling and spatial separation practices. In addition, the current cohort did not include other myocardial injury conditions such as myocarditis or heart failure, and specificity under more complex clinical contexts requires further evaluation. While the study supports parallel interpretation of nucleic-acid and protein biomarkers at the cohort level, the intrinsic properties of RPA impose constraints on multiplexing and primer design, which will require additional optimization to mitigate potential cross-reactivity. Validation was performed under laboratory conditions, and operational robustness, cost-effectiveness and lot-to-lot reproducibility in true POCT environments require systematic assessment. Dependence on temperature control and enzymatic stability further motivates formulation and device-level optimization.

Future work could explore multi-marker panels by extending primer and crRNA design strategies to additional miRNAs and evaluating feasible multiplex architectures. Integration with microfluidics and portable optical readout modules may further support higher levels of workflow integration. Finally, PAM-independent activation observed in this system motivates testable questions for mechanistic and engineering studies of Cas12a; the boundary conditions across target sequences, reaction environments and clinical matrices remain to be established with expanded datasets.

Conclusion

In this study, we developed and evaluated a one-tube RT–RPA–Cas12a workflow (RCNPM) for detecting the AMI-associated miRNA miR-499 and assessed its performance in a clinical cohort. Serum cTnT concentrations were obtained from routine hospital laboratory testing and were analyzed in parallel with the miR-499 readout as a clinical reference. In the present cohort, cTnT values separated AMI cases from controls, yielding an ROC AUC of 1.00 across the overall analysis and prespecified stratifications.

Across AMI and control samples, miR-499 measurements by RCNPM were strongly correlated with RT–qPCR (Spearman r = 0.96, p < 0.001), and Bland–Altman analysis indicated a mean difference close to zero with no evident systematic bias. ROC analyses based on miR-499 produced an AUC of 1.00 for both RCNPM and RT–qPCR in this cohort, and DeLong’s test did not indicate a difference between the two curves (p = 1.000). Similar patterns were observed in the prespecified subset defined by sample-quality metrics.

Together, these results support the feasibility of integrating reverse transcription, isothermal amplification and Cas12a-mediated signal generation within a single reaction format for miR-499 detection. Further evaluation in larger and more heterogeneous cohorts, including broader disease-control groups and external validation datasets, will be required to define performance under more complex clinical conditions and to establish operational characteristics in intended-use settings.
