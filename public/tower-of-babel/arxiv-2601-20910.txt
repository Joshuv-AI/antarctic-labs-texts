On Approximate Nash Equilibria in Mean Field Games
Mao Fabrice Djete, Nizar Touzi

Abstract
In the context of large population symmetric games, approximate Nash equilibria are introduced through equilibrium solutions of the corresponding mean field game in the sense that the individual gain from optimal unilateral deviation under such strategies converges to zero in the large population size asymptotic. We show that these strategies satisfy an 𝕃 ∞ \mathbb{L}^{\infty} notion of approximate Nash equilibrium which guarantees that the individual gain from optimal unilateral deviation is small uniformly among players and uniformly on their initial characteristics. We establish these results in the context of static models and in the dynamic continuous time setting, and we cover situations where the agents’ criteria depend on the conditional law of the controlled state process.



Keywords.
Approximate Nash equilibrium, mean field McKean-Vlasov stochastic differential equation, mean field game.

Introduction

Games with a large number of strategic agents arise naturally in economics, engineering, and social sciences, including models of financial engineering, congestion, auctions, communication networks, and population dynamics. While Nash equilibrium provides a canonical solution concept, its computation and characterization quickly become intractable as the number of players grows. Mean field game (MFG) theory addresses this challenge by studying the limiting behavior of symmetric finite player games as the population size
n
increases to infinity, replacing direct strategic interaction with interaction through an aggregate distribution, or mean field. Since the seminal work of
Lasry and Lions 2006a

[
16
,
17
,
18
]
and
Huang et al. 2006

[
14
]
, MFG theory has become a central tool for analyzing large-population strategic systems. In the mean field framework, each agent solves an individual optimization problem against an exogenously given flow of population states or actions, while equilibrium requires consistency between the optimal policy and the induced population distribution. The resulting solution concept, called mean field equilibrium (MFE), is substantially simpler than Nash equilibrium of the finite-player game. A central theoretical question is therefore whether, and in what sense, an MFE provides a good approximation to a Nash equilibrium when the number of players is large but finite.

A large body of work establishes that strategies derived from an MFE yield an approximate Nash equilibrium - also called
\varepsilon-
Nash equilibrium - in the sense that, in the corresponding
n
–population size game, the individual gain from optimal unilateral deviation under such MFE based strategies vanishes as the size of the population
n
converges to infinity. In continuous-time stochastic differential games, this program was first carried out for general nonlinear large-population stochastic dynamic games by
Huang et al. 2006

[
14
]
who showed that this gain from optimal unilateral deviation is of order
n^{-\frac{1}{2}}
. We also refer to
Cardaliaguet 2010

[
5
]
for the first order context.
Carmona and Delarue 2013

[
7
]
obtained this result by following the Pontryagin maximum principle approach to the representative agent control problem.
These results were specialized and sharpened for cost-coupled Linear-Quadratic-Gaussian models in
Bensoussan et al. 2014

[
3
]
and
[
15
]
. The corresponding result in the context of closed loop strategies was obtained by
Cardaliaguet et al. 2019

[
6
]
by using PDE techniques at the level of the master equation on the torus. Subsequent extensions cover more general dynamics and cost structures; see, among others,
Carmona and Delarue 2018

[
8
]
. These results rely on propagation-of-chaos arguments and stability estimates showing that the impact of a single agent’s deviation on the empirical distribution vanishes asymptotically.

The notion of an
\varepsilon-
Nash equilibrium in static games was introduced explicitly by
Radner 1980

[
20
]
who defined
\varepsilon-
equilibria as strategy profiles in which no player can gain more than
\varepsilon
by a unilateral deviation. Radner’s motivation was to model approximate rationality and robustness in large economic systems, particularly oligopolistic markets, where exact best responses may be unrealistic or analytically fragile. This concept provided a formal relaxation of Nash equilibrium while retaining clear strategic meaning. Subsequent work in static game theory adopted
\varepsilon-
Nash equilibria as a tool for studying stability and approximation of equilibria, especially in large games where individual players have limited influence. In parallel, the literature on large games and continuum limits - building on earlier nonatomic game theory of e.g.,
Schmeidler 1973

[
22
]
- recognized that equilibria of nonatomic games typically correspond, when interpreted in finite-player settings, to approximate equilibria rather than exact Nash equilibria, even though
\varepsilon-
Nash equilibria were not always introduced explicitly in that early work. Over time,
\varepsilon-
Nash equilibria became the standard language for formalizing this approximation viewpoint. More recently,
\varepsilon-
Nash equilibria have also played a central role in algorithmic game theory, where exact Nash equilibria may be computationally intractable; see, for instance,
Lipton et al. 2003

[
19
]
on approximate Nash equilibria in large normal-form games, and
Daskalakis et al. 2009

[
9
]
on the complexity of computing approximate equilibria. Collectively, this literature establishes
\varepsilon-
Nash equilibria as a fundamental solution concept for static games, serving both as a robustness notion in economic modeling and as a practical approximation tool in large and complex strategic environments.

A common feature of nearly all these approximation results is that convergence of the gain from optimal deviating is established in an averaged sense, typically in expectation or in probability. More precisely, the standard definition of approximate Nash equilibrium bounds the expected gain from unilateral deviation, leading naturally to
\mathbb{L}^{1}-
type estimates with respect to the underlying probability measure governing the state dynamics. In continuous-time stochastic models, error bounds are usually derived for expected costs conditional on the common filtration, see e.g.
Carmona and Delarue 2018

[
8
]
,
Huang et al. 2006

[
14
]
, while in discrete-time models the approximation holds in expectation over the empirical distribution of states or actions, see e.g.
Adlakha et al. 2015

[
1
]
. Although such results are sufficient for many purposes, they do not provide uniform or worst-case guarantees on the approximation quality, and possibly allow for rare but potentially large deviations of realized payoffs from their mean field limits.

This paper contributes to the literature by strengthening the mode of convergence of the gain from optimally deviating. Our focus is on
\mathbb{L}^{\infty}-
convergence results of the Nash equilibrium error, yielding uniform bounds over the relevant state space. Of course, when the underlying state space is compact, this does not differ from the previously established results. Our stronger notion of approximation provides robust guarantees that the MFE-based strategies provide equal benefit for all agents regardless of their individual characteristics and uniformly across realizations and initial conditions. To the best of our knowledge, such
\mathbb{L}^{\infty}
bounds are largely absent from the existing MFG approximation literature, both in continuous and discrete time, and require new analytical techniques beyond standard propagation-of-chaos arguments. This refinement strengthens the economic and algorithmic interpretation of MFE and broadens its applicability in settings where worst-case performance guarantees are essential.

Throughout this paper, we consider the general situation where the individual player’s criterion is a function of the conditional law of its state, conditional on its own initial characteristics. We first derive our main approximate Nash equilibrium results in discrete time - actually in a one-period model - which raises some special difficulties due to some features related to the discrete time setting. We next cover the continuous time setting in the simple uncontrolled diffusion setting, and open-loop finite population game. Due to the nature of the agents’ performance criterion, we also complement our analysis with the corresponding existence results of MFE.

Our main findings are established under several refinements of the standard Lipschitz condition on the coefficients driving the dynamics of the game problem:

•

We derive an
\mathbb{L}^{\infty}-
approximate Nash result under the strong
{\cal W}_{0}-
Lipschitz condition; this is our first
\varepsilon-
Nash equilibrium result in the
\mathbb{L}^{\infty}-
sense.

•

Next, under the weaker, and more standard,
{\cal W}_{p}-
Lipschitz condition, we derive a new
\mathbb{L}^{\infty}-
approximate Nash result for a large portion of the population which increases as an appropriate power
n^{\delta}
of the population size. For this reason, we call
(\varepsilon,\delta)-
Nash equilibrium this new notion of approximate Nash-equilibria.

The paper is organized as follows. Section
2
introduces the static symmetric finite population game problem and the corresponding mean field limit, and reports our main approximate Nash equilibrium results. We next derive in Section
3
similar results in the uncontrolled diffusion continuous time setting. We finally report the existence results of mean field equilibrium in the static and continuous settings in Sections
5
and
6
, respectively.

For fixed

Remark 2.3
.

Definition
2.2
introduces a hierarchy of equilibrium notions tailored to different levels of approximation in large population games. By construction, a Nash equilibrium corresponds to the strongest requirement, enforcing the absence of profitable unilateral deviations for every agent in the population. The notion of
\varepsilon
–Nash equilibrium relaxes this condition by allowing deviation gains of order at most
\varepsilon
, uniformly over all agents
i\leq n
.

The
(\varepsilon,\delta)
–Nash equilibrium further weakens the equilibrium requirement by restricting the uniform control of deviation gains to agents whose initial states belong to the expanding ball
B(n^{\delta})
. Importantly, this control remains uniform with respect to the agent index
i
; the relaxation concerns only the region of the state space on which the equilibrium condition is enforced, and not the uniformity of the deviation bound across the relevant subpopulation.

This notion is particularly well suited to asymptotic regimes in which the initial states
\{X^{i}_{0}\}_{i\leq n}
may have unbounded support. In such settings, uniform estimates can typically be established on compact subsets of the state space, while the contribution of agents outside
B(n^{\delta})
is handled via tail estimates or moment conditions. As
n\to\infty
, choosing
\delta
sufficiently large allows the truncated region to capture an overwhelming fraction of the support of the initial distribution, so that
(\varepsilon,\delta)
–Nash equilibria provide a meaningful approximation of full
\varepsilon
–Nash equilibria in the large population limit.

Finally, this notion is well adapted to obtaining
\mathbb{L}^{\infty}
–type approximations of Nash equilibria, in contrast with the
\mathbb{L}^{1}
or averaged notions more commonly considered in the literature; see Theorem
2.7
below for a precise illustration of this feature.

Remark 2.8
.

Theorem
2.7
establishes two complementary notions of approximate Nash equilibrium for the strategy profile
\boldsymbol{\alpha}^{n}
induced by the mean field equilibrium
\widehat{\alpha}
. The first statement, (ANE
{}_{\mathbb{L}^{r}}
), yields convergence of the population maximum deviation gain in
\mathbb{L}^{r}(\mu_{0})
, showing that deviation incentives vanish on average with respect to the distribution of initial states. In particular, this implies that profitable unilateral deviations become asymptotically negligible for a typical agent as the population size grows.

The second statement, (ANE
{}^{\delta}_{\mathbb{L}^{\infty}}
), is stronger, as it provides a uniform control of deviation gains for all agents whose initial states belong to the expanding ball
B(n^{\delta})
. This control is also uniform with respect to the agent index
i
, since it bounds the supremum of deviation gains simultaneously over the entire truncated population. The restriction
\delta<1/p
(with
\delta=\infty
when
p=0
) reflects the trade-off between uniform estimates on compact subsets of the state space and the regularity assumptions imposed on the data, in particular on the functional
F
.

Taken together, these results show that the only potential obstruction to a full
\varepsilon
–Nash equilibrium arises from agents whose initial states lie in the far tails of the initial distribution. As the radius
n^{\delta}
diverges with
n
, the truncated region captures an increasingly large portion of the population starting points. Consequently, for large population sizes, the strategy
\boldsymbol{\alpha}^{n}
satisfies the equilibrium condition of Definition
2.2
up to an arbitrarily small error
\varepsilon
on this dominant subpopulation. This uniform approximation result refines existing asymptotic Nash equilibrium notions and provides a sharper connection between finite-player symmetric games and their mean field game limits.

X^{\mu,\alpha}
through the controlled SDE

\displaystyle\mathrm{d}X_{t}=b\big(t,\underline{X}_{t},\mu_{t},\alpha(t,\underline{X}_{t})\big)\,\mathrm{d}t+\mathrm{d}W_{t},~\mbox{with}~\underline{X}_{t}:=(X_{0},X_{t}),~t\in[0,T].

(3.1)

Here, the control strategy
\alpha
lies in the set
{\cal A}
of bounded Borel
\mathbb{F}-
progressively measurable maps from
[0,T]\times\mathbb{R}^{2d}
to some compact set
A
of a Polish space. We shall impose appropriate conditions on

to guarantee the existence of a unique solution

.

Similar to the static setting, we consider a performance criterion defined through a map
U:{\cal P}_{p}(\mathbb{R}^{d})\longrightarrow\mathbb{R}
, and we define the optimization problem

V(\mu):=\sup_{\alpha\in{\cal A}}J(\mu,\alpha)~\mbox{where}~J(\mu,\alpha):=\mathbb{E}\big[U\big({\cal L}^{|X_{0}}_{X^{\mu,\alpha}_{T}}\big)\big],

By standard measurable selection, it follows that

V(\mu):=\mathbb{E}[V(X_{0},\mu)]~\mbox{with}~V(X_{0},\mu)=\esup_{\alpha}U\big({\cal L}^{|X_{0}}_{X^{\mu,\alpha}_{T}}\big),

where the dependence of
\alpha
on
X_{0}
in the last
\esup
is superfluous. We next introduce a precise definition of a solution to the mean field game by the standard representative agent individual optimality (IO), given the environment, and the consistency property expressed as a fixed point (FP) condition.

Definition 3.1
.

A measurable flow
\widehat{\mu}=(\widehat{\mu}_{t})_{t\in[0,T]}\subset{\cal P}(\mathbb{R}^{2d})

is a mean field game (MFG) solution associated with a control

\widehat{\alpha}\in{\cal A}
if the following two conditions hold:

(IO)

Given
\widehat{\mu}
, the control
\widehat{\alpha}
solves the individual objective, i.e.
J(\widehat{\mu},\widehat{\alpha})=\sup_{\alpha\in{\cal A}}J(\widehat{\mu},\alpha);

(FP)

The optimally controlled dynamics is consistent with the given envioronment
\widehat{\mu}
, i.e.
\mathbb{P}\circ(X_{0},X^{\hat{\mu},\hat{\alpha}}_{t})^{-1}=\widehat{\mu}_{t},
for all
t\in[0,T].

The following condition is needed to guarantee the existence of a solution to the MFG problem.

Assumption
\mathbf{E}_{p}
.

(i)

U
is bounded, concave and continuous in
{\cal P}_{p}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
;

(i)

b
is bounded, Lipschitz in
(x,m)
uniformly in
(t,a)
, and continuous in
(x,m,a)
for all fixed
t
;

(iii)
The control space
A
is convex and,
(t,x,m)\in[0,T]\times\mathbb{R}^{2d}\times{\cal P}(\mathbb{R}^{2d})
the set

\big\{b(t,x,m,a)\in\mathbb{R}^{d}:~a\in A\big\}~\mbox{is convex.}

Theorem 3.2
.

Let Assumption
{\rm E}_{p}
hold and let
\mu_{0}\in{\cal P}_{q}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
, for some
q>p
. Then, there exists an

solution.

The proof is reported in Section
5
.

\displaystyle\mu^{n}_{t}:=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{j=1}^{n}\delta_{\underline{X}^{j}_{t}},~t\in[0,T].

The maximal value that player

\displaystyle V_{i}^{n}(X_{0}^{i},\boldsymbol{\alpha}^{-i}):=\sup_{\alpha^{i}\in{\cal A}_{n}}J_{i}^{n}(X_{0}^{i},\boldsymbol{\alpha}),

\displaystyle J_{i}^{n}(X_{0}^{i},\boldsymbol{\alpha}):=U\Big({\cal L}^{|X_{0}^{i}}_{X^{i,\boldsymbol{\alpha}}_{T}}\Big).

(Open–loop Nash equilibirum)

Nash equilibrium for
n-
player game if
\overline{\mathbf{D}}^{\delta}_{n}[\boldsymbol{\alpha}]\leq\varepsilon

Let
\widehat{\mu}
be a solution of the mean field game (MFG) associated with the closed–loop control
\widehat{\alpha}\in{\cal A}

\displaystyle X^{\hat{\mu},\hat{\alpha}}_{t}=\Phi(t,X_{0},W_{\wedge t}),~~t\leq T,

For all

Proof.

First, (ANE
{}_{\mathbb{L}^{r}}

Step 1: Convergence of Nash equilibria.

Let
(\overline{X}^{i})_{i\geq 1}

\displaystyle\mathrm{d}\overline{X}^{i}_{t}

\displaystyle=b\bigl(t,\overline{X}^{i}_{t},\widehat{\mu}_{t},\alpha^{i,n}_{t}\bigr)\mathrm{d}t+\mathrm{d}W^{i}_{t},\qquad t\in[0,T].

By construction,

forms a sequence of i.i.d. copies of the mean–field state process

\displaystyle\lim_{n\to\infty}{\cal W}_{p}\bigl(\overline{\mu}^{n},\,{\cal L}_{X^{\hat{\mu},\hat{\alpha}}}\bigr)=0.

(4.4)

For later use, we also introduce the notation

Let

\sup_{i\leq n}\sup_{x\in B(n^{\delta})}w_{n}(i,x)-2^{-n}\leq w_{n}(i_{n},x_{n}),~\mbox{where}~w_{n}(i,x):={\cal W}_{p}\Bigl({\cal L}^{|X_{0}^{i}=x}_{X^{i,\hat{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}}_{T}},{\cal L}^{|X_{0}=x}_{X^{\mu,\hat{\alpha}}_{T}}\Bigr),

(4.5)

and let us prove that

\displaystyle\lim_{n\to\infty}w_{n}(i_{n},x_{n})=0,

(4.6)

which implies by the continuity of the reward functional
U
that

\big\|\overline{\mathbf{G}}^{\delta}_{n}[x_{0},\boldsymbol{\alpha}^{n}]\big\|_{\mathbb{L}^{\infty}(\mathbb{S})}=\max_{i\leq n}\sup_{x_{0}\in B(n^{\delta})}\Bigl|J_{i}^{n}(x_{0},\boldsymbol{\alpha}^{n})-U\Big({\cal L}^{|X_{0}=x_{0}}_{X^{\hat{\mu},\hat{\alpha}}_{T}}\Big)\Bigr|\underset{n\to\infty}{\longrightarrow}0.

To see that (

in

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\bigl[{\cal W}_{p}(\mu^{n}_{t},\overline{\mu}^{n}_{t})\,\big|\,X^{i_{n}}_{0}=x_{0}^{n}\bigr]

\displaystyle\leq\mathbb{E}\Bigl[\sup_{s\leq t}\!|X^{j,\boldsymbol{\alpha}}_{s}-\overline{X}^{j}_{s}|\,\Big|\,X^{i_{n}}_{0}=x_{0}^{n}\Bigr]

\displaystyle\leq C\!\int_{0}^{t}\!\mathbb{E}\bigl[{\cal W}_{p}(\mu^{n}_{s},\widehat{\mu}_{s})\,\big|\,X^{i_{n}}_{0}=x_{0}^{n}\bigr]\mathrm{d}s

\displaystyle\leq C\!\int_{0}^{t}\!\mathbb{E}\bigl[{\cal W}_{p}(\mu^{n}_{s},\overline{\mu}^{n}_{s})+{\cal W}_{p}(\overline{\mu}^{n}_{s},\widehat{\mu}_{s})\,\big|\,X^{i_{n}}_{0}=x_{0}^{n}\bigr]\mathrm{d}s,

By Gronwall’s lemma and direct manipulation, this provides

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\!\left[\sup_{s\leq T}|X^{j,\boldsymbol{\alpha}}_{s}-\overline{X}^{j}_{s}|\,\Big|\,X^{i_{n}}_{0}=x_{0}^{n}\right]

\displaystyle\leq K\!\int_{0}^{T}\!\mathbb{E}\bigl[{\cal W}_{p}(\overline{\mu}^{n}_{s},\widehat{\mu}_{s})\,\big|\,X^{i_{n}}_{0}=x_{0}^{n}\bigr]\mathrm{d}s

(4.7)

for some

As

As

are iid, this implies by the law of large numbers and dominated convergence imply that
\lim_{n\to\infty}\mathbb{E}[|\langle\varphi,\overline{\mu}^{n}\rangle-\langle\varphi,{\cal L}_{X^{\hat{\mu},\hat{\alpha}}}\rangle||X^{i_{n}}_{0}=x_{0}^{n}]=0
, and by the arbitrariness of

\lim_{n\to\infty}\mathbb{E}\bigl[{\cal W}_{p}(\overline{\mu}^{n},{\cal L}_{X^{\hat{\mu},\hat{\alpha}}})\,\big|\,X^{i_{n}}_{0}=x_{0}^{n}\bigr]=0.

Substituting in (
4

\begin{array}[]{c}\displaystyle\lim_{n\to\infty}\mathbb{E}\Big[\sup_{t\leq T}|X^{i_{n},\boldsymbol{\alpha}}_{t}-\overline{X}^{i_{n}}_{t}|\Big|X^{i_{n}}_{0}=x_{0}^{n}\Big]=0,\\
\displaystyle\mbox{and}~\lim_{n\to\infty}\sup_{i\leq n}\sup_{x\in B(n^{\delta})}w_{n}(i,x)=\lim_{n\to\infty}w_{n}(i_{n},x_{0}^{n})=0.\end{array}

(4.8)

Step 2: Optimality condition.

Let

\displaystyle\gamma^{n}-2^{-n}\;\leq\;J_{i_{n}}^{n}\bigl(x_{0}^{n},\boldsymbol{\alpha}^{n,(-i_{n})}\!\oplus\beta^{n}\bigr)-J_{i_{n}}^{n}(x_{0}^{n},\boldsymbol{\alpha}^{n}).

(4.9)

We consider the situation when Player
i

\alpha^{i,n}

where

By the Lipschitz continuity of

\displaystyle\leq C\!\int_{0}^{t}\!\mathbb{E}\!\left[{\cal W}_{p}(\mu^{n,i_{n}}_{s},\widehat{\mu}_{s})\;\middle|\;X^{i_{n}}_{0}=x_{0}^{n}\right]\mathrm{d}s

\displaystyle\hskip-113.81102pt\leq C\!\int_{0}^{t}\!\!\Big(\mathbb{E}\!\left[{\cal W}_{p}(\mu^{n,i_{n}}_{s},\overline{\mu}^{n}_{s})\,\middle|\,X^{i_{n}}_{0}=x_{0}^{n}\right]+\mathbb{E}\!\left[{\cal W}_{p}(\overline{\mu}^{n}_{s},\widehat{\mu}_{s})\,\middle|\,X^{i_{n}}_{0}=x_{0}^{n}\right]\Big)\mathrm{d}s.

(4.10)

Then

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\!\big[{\cal W}_{p}(\mu^{n,i_{n}}_{t},\overline{\mu}^{n}_{t})\big|X^{i_{n}}_{0}=x_{0}^{n}\big]

\displaystyle\leq\mathbb{E}\!\Big[\frac{1}{n}\sum_{j\neq i_{n}}\sup_{s\leq t}|X^{j,i_{n}}_{s}-\overline{X}^{j}_{s}|+\frac{1}{n}\sup_{s\leq t}|X^{i_{n},i_{n}}_{s}-\overline{X}^{i_{n}}_{s}|\big|X^{i_{n}}_{0}=x_{0}^{n}\Big]

Applying Gronwall’s lemma to the above inequality yields

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\!\left[{\cal W}_{p}(\mu^{n,i_{n}}_{t},\overline{\mu}^{n}_{t})\,\middle|\,X^{i_{n}}_{0}=x_{0}^{n}\right]

\displaystyle\leq C\!\int_{0}^{t}\!\mathbb{E}\!\left[{\cal W}_{p}(\overline{\mu}^{n}_{s},\widehat{\mu}_{s})\,\middle|\,X^{i_{n}}_{0}=x_{0}^{n}\right]\mathrm{d}s

\displaystyle\sup_{j\neq i_{n}}\mathbb{E}\!\left[\sup_{s\leq T}|X^{j,i_{n}}_{s}-\overline{X}^{j}_{s}|\,\middle|\,X^{i_{n}}_{0}=x_{0}^{n}\right]

\displaystyle\leq C\!\int_{0}^{T}\!\mathbb{E}\!\left[{\cal W}_{p}(\overline{\mu}^{n}_{s},\widehat{\mu}_{s})\,\middle|\,X^{i_{n}}_{0}=x_{0}^{n}\right]\mathrm{d}s

\displaystyle\quad+\frac{C}{n}\mathbb{E}\!\left[\sup_{s\leq T}|X^{i_{n},i_{n}}_{s}-\overline{X}^{i_{n}}_{s}|\,\middle|\,X^{i_{n}}_{0}=x_{0}^{n}\right].

As for the deviating player, we get from Step 1 together with the boundedness of

that

\lim_{n\to\infty}\frac{1}{n}\mathbb{E}\!\Big[\sup_{t\leq T}|X^{i_{n},i_{n}}_{t}-\overline{X}^{i_{n}}_{t}|\big|X^{i_{n}}_{0}=x_{0}^{n}\Big]=0.

Combining the preceding estimates with the convergence established in Step 1, we finally deduce that

\displaystyle\lim_{n\to\infty}\sup_{t_{0}\in[0,T]}\mathbb{E}\!\big[{\cal W}_{p}(\mu^{n,i_{n}}_{t_{0}},\widehat{\mu}_{t_{0}})\big|X^{i_{n}}_{0}=x_{0}^{n}\big]=0,~\lim_{n\to\infty}\sup_{j\neq i_{n}}\mathbb{E}\!\big[\sup_{t\leq T}|X^{j,i_{n}}_{t}-\overline{X}^{j}_{t}|\big|X^{i_{n}}_{0}=x_{0}^{n}\big]=0.

(4.11)

\overline{X}^{i,\beta^{n}}

\displaystyle\overline{X}^{i,\beta^{n}}_{0}=X^{i}_{0}~\mbox{and}~\mathrm{d}\overline{X}^{i,\beta^{n}}_{t}

\displaystyle=b(t,\overline{X}^{i,\beta^{n}}_{t},\widehat{\mu}_{t},\beta^{n}_{t})\,\mathrm{d}t+\mathrm{d}W^{i}_{t},\quad t\in[0,T],

By (

\displaystyle\lim_{n\to\infty}{\cal W}_{p}\left({\cal L}^{|X^{i}_{0}=x^{n}_{0}}_{X^{i_{n},i_{n}}_{T}},\,{\cal L}^{|X^{i_{n}}_{0}=x^{n}_{0}}_{\overline{X}^{i_{n},\,\beta^{n}}_{T}}\right)=0\quad\mbox{and}\quad\lim_{n\to\infty}{\cal W}_{p}\left({\cal L}^{|X^{i_{n}}_{0}=x^{n}_{0}}_{X^{i_{n},\boldsymbol{\alpha}}_{T}},\,{\cal L}^{|X^{i_{n}}_{0}=x^{n}_{0}}_{\overline{X}^{i_{n},\,\alpha^{i_{n},n}}_{T}}\right)=0.

We deduce that

\displaystyle 0\leq\limsup_{n\to\infty}\gamma^{n}

\displaystyle\leq\limsup_{n\to\infty}J_{i_{n}}^{n}\big(x_{0}^{n},\boldsymbol{\alpha}^{n,(-i_{n})}\oplus\beta^{n}\big)-J_{i_{n}}^{n}(x_{0}^{n},\boldsymbol{\alpha}^{n})

\displaystyle\leq\limsup_{n\to\infty}U\left({\cal L}^{|X^{i_{n}}_{0}=x^{n}_{0}}_{\overline{X}^{i_{n},\,\beta^{n}}_{T}}\right)-U\left({\cal L}^{|X^{i_{n}}_{0}=x^{n}_{0}}_{\overline{X}^{i_{n},\,\alpha^{i_{n},n}}_{T}}\right)

\displaystyle=\limsup_{n\to\infty}U\left({\cal L}^{|X^{i_{n}}_{0}=x^{n}_{0}}_{\overline{X}^{i_{n},\,\beta^{n}}_{T}}\right)-U\left({\cal L}^{|X^{i_{n}}_{0}=x^{n}_{0}}_{\overline{X}^{\hat{\mu},\hat{\alpha}}_{T}}\right),

(4.12)

as
{\cal L}^{|X^{i}_{0}=x^{i,n}_{0}}_{\overline{X}^{i,\,\alpha^{i,n}}_{T}}={\cal L}^{|X_{0}=x^{i,n}_{0}}_{\overline{X}^{\mu,\alpha}_{T}}
. Moreover, by using the equivalence between weak and strong open–loop formulation, and strong open–loop and closed–loop formulation in

[

Since
\hat{\mu}
is a MFG solution associated to

, we deduce from (

) that
\lim_{n\to\infty}\delta^{n}=0
, thus completing the proof.

Y_{0}=X_{0},\qquad\mathrm{d}Y_{t}=\int_{A}b\bigl(t,X_{0},Y_{t},\mu_{t}^{\mathrm{P}},a\bigr)\overline{K}(t,X_{0},Y_{t})(\mathrm{d}a)\,\mathrm{d}t+\mathrm{d}W_{t},

such that the joint conditional law satisfies

{\cal L}^{\mathrm{P}}_{X_{0},\mathbb{E}^{\mathrm{P}}[\widehat{\mu}\circ\widehat{X}_{t}^{-1}|X_{0}]}={\cal L}_{X_{0},{\cal L}^{|X_{0}}_{Y_{t}}}.

In words, the process
Y
reproduces the same conditional marginal distributions as the relaxed process
\widehat{X}
. We next exploit the convexity of the control problem to replace the measure-valued control
\overline{K}
by a point-valued one.
Since
A
is convex and the set
\{b(\cdot,a):a\in A\}
is convex in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
, the measurable selection theorem (see, e.g.,
[
11
]
) guarantees the existence of a Borel measurable map
{\rm a}:[0,T]\times\mathbb{R}^{2d}\times{\cal P}(\mathbb{R}^{2d})\longrightarrow A,

such that

\displaystyle\int_{A}b(t,X_{0},Y_{t},\mu_{t}^{\mathrm{P}},a)\overline{K}(t,X_{0},Y_{t})(\mathrm{d}a)=b(t,X_{0},Y_{t},\mu^{\mathrm{P}}_{t},{\rm a}(t,X_{0},Y_{t},\mu^{\mathrm{P}}_{t})),

and consequently

{\cal L}^{|X_{0}=x_{0}}_{Y_{t}}={\cal L}^{|X_{0}=x_{0}}_{X_{t}^{\mu^{\mathrm{P}},\alpha}},~\mbox{with}~\alpha(t,x_{0},x):={\rm a}(t,x_{0},x,\mu_{t}^{\mathrm{P}})~\mbox{for all}~x_{0}.

That is, the strict control
\alpha
reproduces the same conditional law as the relaxed one. In addition, for each
t\in[0,T]
,

\displaystyle\mu^{\mathrm{P}}_{t}=\mathbb{E}^{\mathrm{P}}\!\left[\widehat{\mu}\circ(\widehat{X}_{t})^{-1}(\mathrm{d}x)\,\delta_{X_{0}}(\mathrm{d}x_{0})\right]=\mathbb{E}^{\mathrm{P}}\!\left[{\cal L}(Y_{t}\mid X_{0})(\mathrm{d}x)\,\delta_{X_{0}}(\mathrm{d}x_{0})\right]={\cal L}(X_{t}^{\mu^{\mathrm{P}},\alpha},X_{0}).

Finally, by conditional Jensen’s inequality and the concavity of
U
,

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}^{\mathrm{P}}\!\left[U\bigl(\widehat{\mu}\circ\widehat{X}_{T}^{-1}\bigr)\right]=\mathbb{E}^{\mathrm{P}}\!\left[\mathbb{E}^{\mathrm{P}}\!\left[U\bigl(\widehat{\mu}\circ\widehat{X}_{T}^{-1}\bigr)\,\big|\,X_{0}\right]\right]

\displaystyle\leq\mathbb{E}^{\mathrm{P}}\!\left[U\Bigl(\mathbb{E}^{\mathrm{P}}\!\bigl[\widehat{\mu}\circ\widehat{X}_{T}^{-1}\,\big|\,X_{0}\bigr]\Bigr)\right]

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}\!\left[U\bigl({\cal L}(X_{T}^{\mu^{\mathrm{P}},\alpha}\mid X_{0})\bigr)\right].

Hence, the strict control
\alpha
attains the same performance as the relaxed equilibrium. The optimality property of
\alpha
follows immediately from that of the relaxed equilibrium
\mathrm{P}
.
Hence, the flow of measures
({\cal L}_{X_{0},X_{t}^{\mu^{\mathrm{P}},\alpha}})_{t\in[0,T]}

satisfies both the fixed-point and individual optimality conditions, and it is therefore an MFG solution associated with the strict control
\alpha
