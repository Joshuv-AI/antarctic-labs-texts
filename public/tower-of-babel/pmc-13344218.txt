Virality versus veracity: A systematic review of TikTok information on prenatal aneuploidy testing
Authors: Ashley S Houlihan, Justine Elshama, Emma Kelley, Adam K Lewkowitz, Melissa L Russo, Margaret M Thorsen
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectivePregnant people are increasingly utilizing TikTok to understand medical information. However, the accuracy and reliability of this content remain largely unexamined. This study aimed to evaluate videos on prenatal aneuploidy testing by engagement, reliability, and quality, comparing results by content category and account type.Study designResearchers conducted TikTok searches (May–July 2025) using 11 sets of keywords. The first 100 videos for each search were collected and screened with inclusion and exclusion criteria. Videos were manually categorized based on content type and account type (i.e., healthcare professionals, laypeople, or other). Objective data collected directly from TikTok included video length and engagement metrics (views, likes, and comments). Two independent reviewers used the modified DISCERN (mDISCERN) criteria to assess reliability and the Global Quality Scale (GQS) to assess quality. Engagement metrics, mDISCERN, and GQS were compared across video category and account type using Wilcoxon rank‐sum test.ResultsA total of 1100 videos were identified, and 541 videos met the inclusion criteria for full review. A total of 69.9% (n = 378) of the videos were posted by laypeople and 23.8% (n = 129) of the videos were posted by healthcare professionals. Videos posted by healthcare professionals had a significantly higher mDISCERN (median 8, interquartile range [IQR] 7–9) and GQS (median 3.5, IQR 3–4) compared to videos posted by laypeople (mDISCERN = 3, IQR 2–4; GQS = 2, IQR 1.5–2.5), but significantly fewer median views (28,900 vs. 32,150, respectively), p < 0.01. Fearmongering and crowdsourcing videos (posted mainly by laypeople) were among the highest engagement categories, but had low mDISCERN and GQS scores.ConclusionTikTok provides unreliable and low‐quality medical information on aneuploidy testing in pregnancy. Healthcare professionals’ videos are higher in quality and reliability but have lower views than those by laypeople. These findings highlight the need for strategies to improve the reach of accurate, evidence‐based prenatal information on social media platforms.Keywords: medical misinformation, patient education, prenatal aneuploidy screening, prenatal genetic testing, social media, TikTok

INTRODUCTION

In the era of infinite resources through the internet and social media, more pregnant people are seeking health information through TikTok. There is a potential concern with these resources for misinformation that may influence patient decision‐making.

Since its release date in 2016, TikTok has dominated social media platforms. As one of the world's most popular apps, it has one billion users globally, including over 170 million American users [1]. As it has gained traction, it has also become an increasingly popular source of health information [2, 3, 4, 5]. Healthcare professionals use the platform to post educational videos, and patients may use it to share their experiences with the healthcare system [6]. In the field of obstetrics and gynecology, TikTok and social media have been shown to spread misinformation.

A prior study of TikTok videos relating to oral contraceptive pills (OCPs) found that videos posted by healthcare professionals were of higher quality and were more reliable than videos posted by laypeople [6]. However, videos posted by laypeople scored significantly higher on metrics of engagement, such as likes, comments, shares, etc. The authors cautioned that misinformation perpetuated on TikTok has the potential to create mistrust of medical professionals, cause patients to delay seeking care, and negatively influence patient decision‐making related to contraception. Similar incorrect notions about contraception were found in a patient survey related to Instagram and YouTube education [7]. These studies call for additional research to evaluate the accuracy, reliability, and quality of healthcare‐related social media content and its influence on patient decision‐making.

Prenatal genetic testing is an increasingly complex topic that has the potential for moral and ethical dilemmas. It provides time‐sensitive information needed to make choices regarding reproductive health [8]. With competing demands at the new obstetric visit, there is increasingly limited time for providers to educate patients on prenatal genetics [9]. With its complexities, there is a known misunderstanding among both patients and their prenatal care providers of the benefits, risks, and limitations of carrier screening, aneuploidy screening, and diagnostic tests [10]. Prior literature has shown that patients may not recognize they have autonomy and choice in the decision to pursue testing [11] and are not always aware that screening was ordered [12]. Furthermore, it is well‐established that patients struggle to understand pretest absolute risk and how to interpret posttest results [13, 14]. These major knowledge gaps make the subject of prenatal genetics particularly prone to patient self‐directed research on the internet and social media. A recent study showed that while YouTube and Google searches about prenatal genetic testing yield some balanced and informative information, much of the content is challenging to read and missing key decision‐making factors [15]. Despite its global popularity, there is a lack of research characterizing TikTok videos related to prenatal genetics.

This study aims to evaluate the reliability and quality of TikTok videos related to prenatal aneuploidy testing to provide clarity and transparency surrounding healthcare‐related videos on social media. Ultimately, this information may guide healthcare professionals and obstetric organizations with an understanding of misconceptions to address when working toward the creation of reliable, relevant, and accessible patient education materials.

MATERIALS AND METHODS

Search strategy and screening process

Prior to data collection, the Women and Infants Hospital IRB deemed the study to be exempt from human subjects research (#2315177‐1).

A new TikTok account was created specifically for the study with no prior search history and an uninfluenced algorithm. Researchers conducted 11 independent TikTok searches (May–July 2025) using 11 sets of keywords related to prenatal aneuploidy testing: “Prenatal genetic screening,” “Prenatal genetic diagnosis,” “Cell‐free DNA, cfDNA, cffDNA, cell‐free fetal DNA,” “Prenatal serum screening,” “NIPT, non‐invasive prenatal testing,” “Amniocentesis, amnio,” “Chorionic villus sampling, CVS,” “Fetal aneuploidy, fetal chromosomal aneuploidy,” “Down syndrome, trisomy 21, T21,” “Trisomy 13/18, T13, T18,” and “Nuchal translucency, NT ultrasound.” The first 100 videos for each set of keywords were collected and screened with inclusion and exclusion criteria (Figure 1). Flow chart of video screening process. IVF, in vitro fertilization; PGTA, preimplantation genetic testing for aneuploidy.

Videos were included if they were videos uploaded to TikTok through a public account and were relevant to prenatal aneuploidy testing. Videos were excluded if they were not in English, deleted after initial collection, or were duplicates within the same keyword category. Additional videos were excluded if they were unrelated to pregnancy (n = 136), focused solely on preimplantation genetic testing for aneuploidy (PGTA)/in vitro fertilization (IVF) (n = 56) or sex chromosomes (n = 38), or did not address fetal aneuploidy testing (n = 282). Videos were screened by two authors, and disagreements were decided by a third author. Duplicate videos within the same keyword category were excluded, so only one video remained within the category.

Data collection

Videos were manually scored independently by two authors and characterized through the following means. Videos were classified by account type into three categories: healthcare professionals (physicians, genetic counselors, certified midwives, etc.), laypeople (patients), or others (community organizations/nonprofits, other organizations). The number of followers and verification status were also recorded. Metrics of video engagement were collected directly from TikTok by one researcher. Metrics included video length in seconds, total views, likes, and number of comments.

Videos were also categorized manually by the authors based on primary content type as being educational, patient experience/testimonial, advertising products or services, antiabortion/prolife messaging, fearmongering, humorous, or crowdsourcing. Videos were coded as fearmongering if the authors perceived the main goal to be instilling fear of genetic screening or diagnostic testing. Crowdsourcing videos were categorized as such if they solicited a question or feedback from the TikTok audience. Videos were assigned a secondary content category if applicable and were included in the analysis equally under both categories. The content of each video was also assessed with a checklist for discussion points recommended by the American College of Obstetricians and Gynecologists (Figure 2). If one researcher deemed a video to have discussed a topic, the video was given full points.

Quality and reliability of information

To determine reliability, a modified DISCERN (mDISCERN) criterion was used. DISCERN is a rating tool that has been widely used to score videos on social media in various healthcare education studies [6, 16, 17]. The DISCERN criteria were modified to seven questions that speak to important factors in prenatal genetic testing education and counseling: Are the aims clear and achieved? Is it clear if reliable sources of information were used? Is it balanced and unbiased? Does it refer to areas of uncertainty or limitations of the tests? Does it describe the benefits of each test? Does it describe the risks of each test? And does it describe what would happen if no test is used? The scoring system was modified to a three‐point scale to reduce inter‐rater variability. Videos scored a 0 do not fulfill the criteria, videos scored a 1 partially fulfill the criteria, and videos scored a 2 fulfill the criteria. Videos were determined to be biased if they recommend for or against prenatal genetic testing, or if they mention a commercial relationship, advertisement for a product, or sponsorship. Videos posted by a healthcare professional were considered to completely fulfill mDISCERN criterion 2 (Is it clear if reliable sources of information were used?). The Global Quality Scale (GQS) was used to assess the overall quality of the video. Videos were scored 1–5 based on the quality, flow, and information discussed in the videos. Videos scoring a 1 indicate poor quality, and videos scoring a 5 indicate excellent quality.

Statistical analysis

The average of reviewer mDISCERN and GQS scores was used to produce a single value for each video. Median values with IQR were used for analyzing metrics of engagement, mDISCERN, and GQS scores. Statistical significance between categories was determined using Wilcoxon rank‐sum tests.

RESULTS

Among the 541 videos that met the inclusion criteria, 69.9% (n = 378) of the videos were posted by laypeople, 23.8% (n = 129) of the videos were posted by healthcare professionals, and 6.3% (n = 34) of the videos were posted by others (Table 1). These videos had a median: length of 82 s, 10,500 followers, 30,500 views, 378 likes, and 38 comments. The median mDISCERN was 3.5 out of 14, and the median GQS was 2.5 out of 5 (Table 2). The largest video content category was patient experience/testimonial (74.31%), followed by educational (39%) and crowdsourcing (interacting with the audience) (10.54%) (Table 1).

DISCUSSION

Videos on fetal aneuploidy screening were viewed tens of thousands of times, indicating many pregnant people use TikTok as a reference for genetic education. The majority of these videos were posted by laypeople and were of significantly lower quality and reliability compared to videos posted by healthcare professionals. Layperson videos received significantly more views compared to videos posted by healthcare professionals, suggesting that the most popular content related to fetal aneuploidy screening is of low quality and reliability. Fearmongering videos received the highest engagement metrics in nearly every category, suggesting that videos with a negative tone are more popular and more likely to “go viral.” These videos were among the lowest quality and reliability categories. Collectively, these findings raise the concern that TikTok can perpetuate negative misinformation about prenatal aneuploidy screening, which could sway patient decision‐making.

A potential explanation for the discrepancy in engagement and quality could be that provider and patient priorities are not well aligned. In the TikTok videos reviewed, healthcare professionals tended to focus on the benefits of genetic testing while laypeople focused on the risks. Additionally, patient experience/testimonial was the largest video category, suggesting that personal experiences may be the most relevant topic to prenatal genetic testing. This is consistent with established health communication literature—patient decision‐making is known to be impacted by storytelling and personal connection [18, 19]. Patients may go to TikTok seeking advice, experiences, or opinions, and find that videos posted by other patients are more relatable and understandable.

It is important that providers are aware of TikTok as a source of medical misinformation so they can properly address patient concerns and beliefs. Patients who observe negative outcomes on TikTok may be less likely to seek care or consent to certain tests, as there is a greater unmet need for medical care among individuals who consume medical misinformation through social media [20]. Providers must also be cognizant that cell‐free DNA companies may use TikTok to promote and advertise novel genetic testing directly to patients (such as using cell‐free DNA to assess fetal risk of monogenic disorders or fetal red cell antigen status) before clinical implementation trials to understand best practices for real‐world application.

It is equally important that healthcare providers work to increase the visibility of accurate, high‐quality, and reliable videos on TikTok about prenatal aneuploidy screening. This may be best accomplished through a public health approach using primary, secondary, and tertiary strategies. For instance, laws to regulate the social media industry (primary), education to build information literacy (secondary), and warning labels on social media posts at high risk of spreading misinformation (tertiary) [21]. As supported by our findings, social media algorithms promote engagement of fearmongering, sensationalized content, regardless of quality or accuracy, exposing even those who may not actively seek out misinformation on medical topics. Therefore, other proposed primary prevention methods include changes to social media algorithms to limit the reach of misinformation [22].

Reduction of medical misinformation on social media can also be accomplished on an individual level. Given that personal experience videos have high engagement, there may be a benefit in providers opening up about their own experiences as patients or sharing Health Insurance Portability and Accountability Act (HIPAA)‐compliant stories to provide accurate medical information through a patient‐centered lens. To increase video popularity, we suggest that providers who wish to engage with social media platforms could consider opening videos with a hook or an enticing story to capture viewers’ attention; after the hook, they can weave medical information throughout the video. Other strategies suggested by TikTok include using trending hashtags and audio or music. Creators should prioritize featuring people instead of only graphics or text [23]. To directly combat the perpetuation of misinformation on TikTok, providers can stitch or duet videos claiming incorrect medical advice. Stitching allows providers to play 5 s of another creator's video before including their own video or reaction after it. Dueting means creating a new video that plays side by side with the original creator's video.

The strengths of the study include the rigor of manual video review by two researchers with a shared coding model, the size of the video pool assessed, and the specific, relevant focus of aneuploidy in pregnancy.

While manual review of each included video allowed for trained researchers to assess quality and reliability (artificial intelligence was not employed to review video scripts), there is a subjective nature to content categorization and mDISCERN. For instance, Question 2 on the mDISCERN criteria asks, “Is it clear if a reliable source of information was used?” This question may be difficult to answer without bias. In addition, the researchers used a rule‐in method for analysis of the content covered in each video, meaning that if one rater determined the video mentioned a topic, it received full points. This method was used to avoid missing content; however, it is still possible that some videos were not coded for all possible topics due to human error or reviewer bias. Additionally, it is possible that videos collected in our TikTok searches do not reflect what's represented on people's For You Pages, as it is curated to the user. TikTok's algorithm is not publicly available for review.

Lastly, the researchers were unable to assess if the author of the TikTok content had financial stakes or conflicts of interest in promoting aneuploidy screening or diagnostic testing. Another study assessing TikTok and Instagram videos on popular elective medical tests (such as full body MRI and gut microbiome testing) assessed whether the account holders had a financial interest in the test discussed [24]. This analysis could be a future direction for our work.

CONCLUSION

TikTok provides unreliable and low‐quality medical information on aneuploidy testing in pregnancy. While healthcare professionals’ videos are higher in quality and reliability, they are viewed significantly less than those posted by laypeople. These findings highlight the need for strategies to improve the reach of accurate, evidence‐based prenatal information on social media platforms.
