Decay of correlation for edge colorings when q > 3 ​ Δ q>3\Delta
Zejia Chen, Yulin Wang, Chihao Zhang, Zihan Zhang

Abstract
We examine various perspectives on the decay of correlation for the uniform distribution over proper q q -edge colorings of graphs with maximum degree Δ \Delta . First, we establish the coupling independence property when q ≥ 3 ​ Δ q\geq 3\Delta for general graphs. Together with the work of Chen et al. (2024), this result implies a fully polynomial-time approximation scheme ( FPTAS ) for counting the number of proper q q -edge colorings. Next, we prove the strong spatial mixing property on trees , provided that q > ( 3 + o ⁡ ( 1 ) ) ​ Δ q>(3+o(1))\Delta . The strong spatial mixing property is derived from the spectral independence property of a version of the weighted edge coloring distribution, which is established using the matrix trickle-down method developed in Abdolazimi, Liu and Oveis Gharan (FOCS, 2021) and Wang, Zhang and Zhang (STOC, 2024). Finally, we show that the weak spatial mixing property holds on trees with maximum degree Δ \Delta if and only if q ≥ 2 ​ Δ − 1 q\geq 2\Delta-1 .

1.1
Technical contribution

A new coupling strategy

The coupling independence property is established via a new local coupling for edge colorings. Our coupling can somehow be viewed as a multi-spin version of Chen and Gu’s coupling
[
CG24
]
for Holant problems with boolean domain. Their coupling, using the problem of
b
-matching as an example, begins with two instances differing at one pendant edge, or equivalently, two instances with a single constraint discrepancy. During the coupling process, the number of discrepancies can never increase but has a nonzero probability of decreasing to zero. Therefore, the coupling process terminates in expected constant number of steps.

In the problem of edge coloring, we can design a local coupling starting from a single discrepancy so that the number of discrepancies can either increase to two, decrease to zero, or remain unchanged. We then use marginal probability bounds to control the probability of discrepancy increasing, while ensuring that the number of discrepancies decreases in expectation.

Dimension reduction

We establish the strong spatial mixing property on trees by analyzing marginal recursions, which is similar to
[
CLMM23
]
. However, unlike previous work for spin systems where the marginal on a single site is considered, we study the recursion for marginals on a “broom”, namely all edges incident to the root. For each partial coloring on the broom, we can represent its marginal as a function of marginal probabilities of partial colorings in subtrees. However, the Jacobian matrix of this system can be as large as
q^{\Delta}\times\Delta q^{\Delta}
, and is technically very hard to analyze. Our key observation is that the Jacobian matrix is of low rank, and therefore we can apply a trace trick to bound its
2
-norm by the norm of a much smaller matrix. We call this step
dimension reduction
.

From spectral independence to strong spatial mixing

It is still challenging to directly bound the
2
-norm of the small matrix. We then observe that it can be written as the product of certain covariance matrices of marginal distributions on brooms. Therefore, ideally we can apply the known bounds for these covariance matrices, or equivalently the spectral independence bound for these marginals. However, these marginal probabilities are from the distribution of certain “weighted edge colorings” and one cannot directly apply previous spectral independence result for edge colorings. As a result, we apply the machinery of matrix trickle-down developed in
[
ALG21a
]
in the way of
[
WZZ24
]
to establish the desired spectral independence result.

Top-down analysis of recursion

There is another subtle technical issue in the above approach. When analyzing the contraction of marginal recursion, one needs to analyze the gradient / Jacobian at certain ‘‘midpoint’’ between two boundary conditions due to the application of fundamental theorem of calculus or mean-value theorem in the analysis. These midpoints, however, are not necessarily probabilities because the recursion may involve a potential function
1
1

1

Theses quantities are referred to as “subdistributions” in
[
CLMM23
]
. In previous work, only certain marginal bounds are used to prove the contraction, and these bounds are also satisfied by the midpoints. However, in our case, we require these midpoints to satisfy refined properties, such as spectral independence, which does not hold in general. Therefore, we cannot apply the recursion in the traditional bottom-to-top manner, where one fixes the boundary value at level
L
, analyze the contraction at level
L-1
, then fixes boundary value at level
L-1
and analyze the contraction at level
L-2
, and so on. Instead, we only fix boundary value at the leaves and analyze the composition of the recursion at each level as a whole. Therefore, we need to take “midpoints” only at the leaves, which defines our “weighted edge coloring” instance. Its spectral independence property can be established by the matrix trickle-down method, as discussed earlier.

2.2
The Wasserstein distance

In this work, we restrict our discussions and terminologies to finite probability spaces without invoking general measure theory.

Definition 4

(Wasserstein distance)
.

Let
\mu,\nu
be two distributions defined on the same finite set
\Omega
equipped with a metric
d(\cdot,\cdot)
. We define
\Gamma(\mu,\nu)
as the set of couplings of
\mu
and
\nu
. Then the
Wasserstein (
1
-)distance
is defined by

\mathcal{W}_{1}\left(\mu,\nu\right):=\sup_{\tau\in\Gamma(\mu,\nu)}\mathbf{E}_{(x,y)\sim\tau}\left[d(x,y)\right].

In this paper, our metric
d
is always the Hamming distance. For two configurations
\sigma,\tau
on
[q]^{V}
, their Hamming distance is defined as
d(\sigma,\tau)=\left|\left\{v\in V\;\middle|\;\sigma(v)\neq\tau(v)\right\}\right|
. We define the notion of coupling independence for Gibbs distribution here.

Definition 5

(Coupling independence)
.

We say a Gibbs distribution
\mu
over
[q]^{V}
satisfies
C
-coupling independence if for any two partial configurations
\sigma,\tau\in[q]^{\Lambda}
on
\Lambda\subseteq V
such that
d(\sigma,\tau)=1
,

\mathcal{W}_{1}\left(\mu^{\sigma},\mu^{\tau}\right)\leq C

where
\mu^{\sigma}
and
\mu^{\tau}
denote the Gibbs distribution conditional on
\sigma
and
\tau
, respectively.

We will use the following inequality w.r.t Wasserstein distance in later proof.

Proposition 6
.

Let
\mu,\nu
be arbitrary distributions on a common finite metric space
(\Omega,d)
.
If there exists non-negative constants
\lambda^{i},1\leq i\leq k

and distributions
\left\{\mu_{i}\right\}_{1\leq i\leq k}
,
\left\{\nu_{i}\right\}_{1\leq i\leq k}
on
\Omega
such that

\mu-\nu=\sum_{i=1}^{k}\lambda^{i}(\mu_{i}-\nu_{i}),

where we regard both sides as functions on
\Omega
.
Then

\mathcal{W}_{1}\left(\mu,\nu\right)\leq\sum_{i=1}^{k}\lambda^{i}{\mathcal{W}_{1}\left(\mu_{i},\nu_{i}\right)}.

Proof.

The Kantotrovich-Rubinstein duality theorem (see Theorem 1.14 in
[
Vil21
]

for the proof) states a equivalent form of Wasserstein distance:

\displaystyle\mathcal{W}_{1}\left(\mu,\nu\right)=\sup_{f\in L^{1}(\Omega)}\langle f,\mu-\nu\rangle,

where
\langle f,\mu-\nu\rangle:=\sum_{x\in\Omega}f(x)(\mu(x)-\nu(x))
and

L^{1}(\Omega):=\left\{f:\Omega\to\mathbb{R}\;\middle|\;\forall x,y\in\Omega:f(x)-f(y)\leq d(x,y)\right\}

is the space of
1
-Lipschiz functions.
Then

\displaystyle\mathcal{W}_{1}\left(\mu,\nu\right)=\sup_{f\in L^{1}(\Omega)}\langle f,\mu-\nu\rangle=\sup_{f\in L^{1}(\Omega)}\langle f,\sum_{i=1}^{k}\lambda^{i}(\mu_{i}-\nu_{i})\rangle\leq\sum_{i=1}^{k}\lambda^{i}\sup_{f\in L^{1}(\Omega)}\langle f,\sum_{i=1}^{k}\lambda^{i}(\mu_{i}-\nu_{i})\rangle=\sum_{i=1}^{k}\lambda^{i}\mathcal{W}_{1}\left(\mu_{i},\nu_{i}\right).

∎

2.3
Correlation Decay

Correlation decay refers to the phenomenon that the correlation between the color assignments of edges diminishes as their distance in the graph increases. Specifically, there are two primary notions of correlation decay: strong spatial mixing and weak spatial mixing. These two notions differ in how they measure the “distance” over which the correlation should decay.

Definition 7

(Strong spatial mixing)
.

The Gibbs distribution
\mu
of the list edge coloring instance
(G=(V,E),\mathcal{L})
satisfies strong spatial mixing (SSM) with exponential decay rate
1-\delta
and constant
C=C(q,\Delta)
if for any
e\in E
, every subset
\Lambda\subseteq E\setminus\{e\}
and every pair of feasible pinning
\tau_{1},\tau_{2}
on
\Lambda
which differ on
\mathinner{\partial}\mathchoice{\mskip-3.0mu}{\mskip-3.0mu}{}{}{}_{\tau_{1},\tau_{2}}=\left\{e\in\Lambda\;\middle|\;\tau_{1}(e)\neq\tau_{2}(e)\right\}
, we have that

\|\mu_{e}^{\sigma}-\mu_{e}^{\tau}\|_{\mathrm{TV}}\leq C(1-\delta)^{K}

where
K=\min_{e^{\prime}\in\mathinner{\partial}\mathchoice{\mskip-3.0mu}{\mskip-3.0mu}{}{}{}_{\tau_{1},\tau_{2}}}\mathrm{dist}_{G}(e,e^{\prime})
.

Definition 8

(Weak spatial mixing)
.

The Gibbs distribution
\mu
of the list edge coloring instance
(G=(V,E),\mathcal{L})
satisfies weak spatial mixing (WSM) with exponential decay rate
1-\delta
and constant
C=C(q,\Delta)
if for any
e\in E
, every subset
\Lambda\subseteq E\setminus\{e\}
and every pair of feasible pinning
\sigma,\tau
on
\Lambda
, we have that

\|\mu_{e}^{\sigma}-\mu_{e}^{\tau}\|_{\mathrm{TV}}\leq C(1-\delta)^{K}

where
K=\min_{e^{\prime}\in\Lambda}\mathrm{dist}_{G}(e,e^{\prime})
.

3.1
Marginal bounds for general edge coloring

We begin by examining the marginal bounds on general graphs.

Lemma 9
.

Given a list-edge-coloring instance
(G,\mathcal{L})
where
G=(V,E)
,
and a vertex
v\in V
such that
\forall e\in E_{v}:\left|\mathcal{L}(v)\right|-\deg(e)\geq\beta\geq 2
.
Then for any
v\in V
,
F\subseteq E(v)
and color
a
:

\mathbb{P}_{G,\mathcal{L}}\left[a\in c(F)\right]\leq\frac{|F|}{\beta-1+|F|}\quad\mbox{and}\quad\frac{\mathbb{P}_{G,\mathcal{L}}\left[a\in c(F)\right]}{\mathbb{P}_{G,\mathcal{L}}\left[a\notin c(E(v))\right]}\leq\frac{|F|}{\beta-1}.

Proof.

We denote the set of all proper colorings on
(G,\mathcal{L})
by
\Omega
, and define

A:=\left\{\omega\in\Omega\;\middle|\;\exists e\in F:\omega(e)=a\right\}.

Then define a function
\iota:(\Omega\setminus A)\times A\to\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}
such that for any
\omega^{\prime}\in A
:

\sum_{\omega\in\Omega\setminus A}\iota(\omega,\omega^{\prime})=1.

(1)

To continue with the construction of
\iota
, for any
\omega^{\prime}\in A
:

B(\omega^{\prime}):=\left\{\omega\in\Omega\setminus A:d(\omega,\omega^{\prime})=1\right\}.

where
d
is the Hamming distance, i.e., the number of edges colored differently between the two colorings.
Then

\displaystyle\iota(\omega,\omega^{\prime}):=\begin{cases}\frac{1}{|B(\omega^{\prime})|},&\omega\in B(\omega^{\prime})\\
0,&\text{otherwise}\end{cases}.

It is easy to verify
eq.

1
holds.
Notice that if
\omega\in B(\omega^{\prime})
such that
\omega^{\prime}(e)=a
for some
e\in F
,
then
\omega
is obtained from
\omega^{\prime}
by recoloring
e
to another color other than
a
.
There are at least
\beta-1
choices according to the assumption, so
|B(\omega^{\prime})|\geq\beta-1
for any
\omega^{\prime}\in A
.

Similarly, if
\omega^{\prime}\in B(\omega)
, then
\omega^{\prime}
is obtained from
\omega
by recoloring an edge
e\in F
to
a
,
there are at most
|F|
choices,
so we have
\sum_{\omega^{\prime}}\iota(\omega,\omega^{\prime})\leq\frac{|F|}{\beta-1}
for all
\omega\in\Omega\setminus A
.

Then

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}_{G,\mathcal{L}}\left[a\in c(F)\right]

\displaystyle=\frac{\sum_{\omega^{\prime}\in A}1}{\sum_{\omega\in\Omega\setminus A}1+\sum_{\omega^{\prime}\in A}1}

\displaystyle=\frac{\sum_{\omega^{\prime}\in A}\sum_{\omega\in\Omega\setminus A}\iota(\omega,\omega^{\prime})}{\sum_{\omega\in\Omega\setminus A}1+\sum_{\omega^{\prime}\in A}\sum_{\omega\in\Omega\setminus A}\iota(\omega,\omega^{\prime})}

\displaystyle=\frac{\sum_{\omega\in\Omega\setminus A}\sum_{\omega^{\prime}\in A}\iota(\omega,\omega^{\prime})}{\sum_{\omega\in\Omega\setminus A}\left(1+\sum_{\omega^{\prime}\in A}\iota(\omega,\omega^{\prime})\right)}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{|F|/(\beta-1)}{1+|F|/(\beta-1)}=\frac{|F|}{\beta-1+|F|},

proving the first part of the lemma.

Similarly, we define
\Omega_{0}:=\left\{\omega\in\Omega\;\middle|\;\not\exists e\in E(v):\omega(e)=a\right\}
.
Note that by definition,
\iota(\cdot,\cdot)
is supported on
\Omega\times A
, so we have

\displaystyle\frac{\mathbb{P}_{G,\mathcal{L}}\left[a\in c(F)\right]}{\mathbb{P}_{G,\mathcal{L}}\left[a\notin c(E(v))\right]}

\displaystyle=\frac{\sum_{\omega^{\prime}\in A}1}{\sum_{\omega\in\Omega_{0}}1}

\displaystyle=\frac{\sum_{\omega^{\prime}\in A}\sum_{\omega\in\Omega\setminus A}\iota(\omega,\omega^{\prime})}{\sum_{\omega\in\Omega_{0}}1}

\displaystyle=\frac{\sum_{\omega\in\Omega_{0}}\sum_{\omega^{\prime}\in A}\iota(\omega,\omega^{\prime})}{\sum_{\omega\in\Omega_{0}}1}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{|F|}{\beta-1}.

∎

Especially, when
\left|F\right|=1
, we have the following corollary.

Corollary 10
.

Let
(G=(V,E),\mathcal{L})
be a
\beta
-extra list-edge-coloring instance. Then for any
e\in E,v\in V,a\in\mathcal{L}(e)
,

\frac{\mathbb{P}_{G,\mathcal{L}}\left[c(e)=a\right]}{\mathbb{P}_{G,\mathcal{L}}\left[a\notin c(E_{v})\right]}\leq\frac{1}{\beta-1}.

(\mathcal{J}f)_{\pi,(i,\tau)}(\mathbf{p})=\frac{\mathinner{\partial}\mathchoice{\mskip-3.0mu}{\mskip-3.0mu}{}{}{}f_{\pi}}{\mathinner{\partial}\mathchoice{\mskip-3.0mu}{\mskip-3.0mu}{}{}{}\mathbf{p}_{i}(\tau)}=\sum_{\rho\in C_{r}}\frac{\mathinner{\partial}\mathchoice{\mskip-3.0mu}{\mskip-3.0mu}{}{}{}f_{\pi}}{\mathinner{\partial}\mathchoice{\mskip-3.0mu}{\mskip-3.0mu}{}{}{}q_{i,\rho}}\frac{\mathinner{\partial}\mathchoice{\mskip-3.0mu}{\mskip-3.0mu}{}{}{}q_{i,\rho}}{\mathinner{\partial}\mathchoice{\mskip-3.0mu}{\mskip-3.0mu}{}{}{}\mathbf{p}_{i}(\tau)}=\sum_{\rho\in C_{r}}\frac{1}{q_{i,\rho}}(\mathbb{1}\left[\rho=\pi\right]-f_{\rho})f_{\pi}\cdot\mathbb{1}\left[\rho(e_{i})\notin\tau\right]

We write
\mathcal{J}_{i}f
explicitly:

\displaystyle(\mathcal{J}_{i}f)(\mathbf{p})=\operatorname{diag}(f(\mathbf{p}))\cdot\sum_{\rho\in C_{r}}\frac{1}{q_{i,\rho}}\cdot\Big[\mathbb{1}\left[\pi=\rho\right]-f_{\rho}(\mathbf{p})\Big]_{\pi\in C_{r}}\Big[\mathbb{1}\left[\rho(e_{i})\notin\tau\right]\Big]_{\tau\in C_{v_{i}}}^{\top}

Noting that
q_{i,\rho}=\mathbb{P}_{T_{i}}\left[\rho(e_{i})\notin c(E_{T_{i}}(v_{i}))\right]
only relies on the color
\rho(e_{i})
, we have

\displaystyle(\mathcal{J}_{i}f)(\mathbf{p})=\operatorname{diag}(f(\mathbf{p}))\sum_{c^{*}\in L({e_{i}})}\Big[\mathbb{1}\left[\pi(e_{i})=c^{*}\right]-\sum_{\pi^{\prime}\in C_{r}:\pi^{\prime}(e_{i})=c^{*}}\mathbf{p}_{r}(\pi)\Big]_{\pi\in C_{r}}\Big[\frac{\mathbb{1}\left[c^{*}\notin\tau\right]}{\sum_{\tau^{\prime}\in C_{v_{i}}:c^{*}\notin\tau^{\prime}}\mathbf{p}_{i}(\tau^{\prime})}\Big]_{\tau\in C_{v_{i}}}^{\top}.

∎

A well-known trick in the analysis of decay of correlation is to apply a potential function on the marginal recursion to amortize the contraction rate. Given an increasing potential function
\phi:[0,1]\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
, we define
f^{\phi}
such that for any
\pi\in C_{r}
and
\mathbf{m}\in\mathbb{R}^{C_{v_{1}}\times C_{v_{2}}\times\dots\times C_{v_{d}}}
,

f^{\phi}_{\pi}(\mathbf{m})=\phi\left(f_{\pi}\left(\left(\phi^{-1}(\mathbf{m}_{1}),\phi^{-1}(\mathbf{m}_{2}),\dots,\phi^{-1}(\mathbf{m}_{d})\right)\right)\right).

As a result, the Jacobian of
f^{\phi}
can be obtained by the chain rule and the inverse function theorem as follows.

Proposition 24
.

Given a smooth increasing function
\phi:[0,1]\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
with derivative
\Phi=\phi^{\prime}
, let
\mathbf{p}=\phi^{-1}(\mathbf{m})
. Then we have

\displaystyle(\mathcal{J}_{i}f^{\phi})(\mathbf{m})=\sum_{c}(\Phi(f(\mathbf{p}))\odot\mathbf{a}_{i,c})(\mathbf{b}_{i,c}\odot\Phi^{-1}(\mathbf{p}_{i}))^{\top}.

Taking
\Phi(x)=\frac{1}{\sqrt{x}}
, we have

\displaystyle(\mathcal{J}_{i}f^{\phi})(\mathbf{m})=\sum_{c}\mathbf{a}^{\phi}_{i,c}(\mathbf{b}^{\phi}_{i,c})^{\top},

where
\mathbf{a}^{\phi}_{i,c^{*}}=\sqrt{f(\mathbf{p})}\odot\Big[\mathbb{1}\left[\pi(e_{i})=c^{*}\right]-\sum_{\pi^{\prime}\in C_{r}:\pi^{\prime}(e_{i})=c^{*}}\mathbf{p}_{r}(\pi^{\prime})\Big]_{\pi\in C_{r}}
and
\mathbf{b}^{\phi}_{i,c^{*}}=\Big[\frac{\mathbb{1}\left[c^{*}\notin\tau\right]\sqrt{\mathbf{p}_{i}(\tau)}}{\sum_{\tau^{\prime}\in C_{v_{i}}:c^{*}\notin\tau^{\prime}}\mathbf{p}_{i}(\tau^{\prime})}\Big]_{\tau\in C_{v_{i}}}
.

5.1.2
Bounding

In this section, we aim to derive an upper bound for the 2-norm of the Jacobian of the tree recursion.
For brevity, we follow some notations which is defined in

of marginal probabilities w.r.t

and

.

Also we introduce some notations of matrices used in later proof.

Definition 25

Definition 26
.

For a distribution over colorings
\mathbf{p}

The following is the main result in this section.

Condition 1

Theorem 28

For any

5.1.3
Dimension reduction

By the definition of 2-norm, we have that
\left\|(\mathcal{J}f^{\phi})(\mathbf{p})\right\|_{2}=\sqrt{\lambda_{\max}((\mathcal{J}f^{\phi})(\mathbf{p})(\mathcal{J}f^{\phi})(\mathbf{p})^{\top})}
. Let
\mathbf{A}:=(\mathcal{J}f^{\phi})(\mathbf{p})(\mathcal{J}f^{\phi})(\mathbf{p})^{\top}
. We have that

\displaystyle\mathbf{A}=\sum_{i=1}^{d}(\mathcal{J}_{i}f^{\phi})(\mathbf{p})(\mathcal{J}_{i}f^{\phi})(\mathbf{p})^{\top}=\sum_{i=1}^{d}\sum_{c_{1},c_{2}\in\mathcal{L}({e_{i}})}\langle\mathbf{b}_{i,c_{1}}^{\phi},\mathbf{b}_{i,c_{2}}^{\phi}\rangle\mathbf{a}_{i,c_{1}}^{\phi}(\mathbf{a}_{i,c_{2}}^{\phi})^{\top}.

The last equation simply follows from
Proposition

24
. The above calculation suggests that although the dimension of
\mathbf{A}
is exponential in
d
, its rank is polynomial in
d
. In the following, we will find a much smaller matrix which can be used to upper bound
\mathbf{A}
. The idea is to use the trace method, namely to study
\Tr\left(\mathbf{A}^{k}\right)
. We have the following lemma.

Lemma 29
.

For any positive semi-definite matrix
\mathbf{M}\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times n}
, we have that

\lambda_{\max}(\mathbf{M})=\lim_{k\to\infty}\left(\Tr(\*M^k)\right)^{\frac{1}{k}}.

Proof of
Lemma

29
.

Assume that
\lambda_{1},...,\lambda_{n}
are eigenvalues of
\mathbf{M}
and
0\leq\lambda_{1}\leq\lambda_{2}\leq...\leq\lambda_{n}
.

\mathbf{M}
can be factored as
\mathbf{Q}\mathbf{\Lambda}\mathbf{Q}^{-1}
where
\mathbf{\Lambda}
is a diagonal matrix satisfying
\mathbf{\Lambda}(i,i)=\lambda_{i}
.
Therefore,

\lim_{k\to\infty}\Tr\left(\mathbf{M}^{k}\right)^{\frac{1}{k}}=\lim_{k\to\infty}\Tr\left(\mathbf{Q}\mathbf{\Lambda}^{k}\mathbf{Q}^{-1}\right)^{\frac{1}{k}}=\lim_{k\to\infty}\Tr\left(\mathbf{\Lambda}^{k}\mathbf{Q}^{-1}\mathbf{Q}\right)^{\frac{1}{k}}=\lim_{k\to\infty}\left(\sum_{i=1}^{n}\lambda_{i}^{k}\right)^{\frac{1}{k}}=\lambda_{n}.

∎

To simplify notations, we let

V(i,z_{1},c_{1},z_{2},c_{2}):=\langle\mathbf{b}_{i,c_{1}}^{\phi},\mathbf{b}_{i,c_{2}}^{\phi}\rangle\left(\mathbb{1}\left[z_{1}=c_{1}\right]-\mathbf{p}_{r}(i,c_{1})\right)\left(\mathbb{1}\left[z_{2}=c_{2}\right]-\mathbf{p}_{r}(i,c_{2})\right).

Then we can write
\mathbf{A}
explicitly.

\mathbf{A}(\pi,\tau)=\sqrt{f(\mathbf{p})(\pi)f(\mathbf{p})(\tau)}\sum_{i=1}^{d}\sum_{c_{1},c_{2}\in\mathcal{L}(e_{i})}V(i,\pi(e_{i}),c_{1},\tau(e_{i}),c_{2}).

(6)

Let
g_{k}^{\pi}(i,c)
denote

\displaystyle\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\tau_{1},...,\tau_{k-1}\in C_{r}\\
\tau_{0}=\pi\end{subarray}}\prod_{j=1}^{k-1}f(\mathbf{p})(\tau_{j})\sum_{i_{1},...,i_{k-1}\in[d],i_{k}=i}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}c_{1,1}\in\mathcal{L}(e_{i_{1}})\\
...\\
c_{1,k}\in\mathcal{L}(e_{i_{k}})\end{subarray}}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}c_{2,1}\in\mathcal{L}(e_{i_{1}})\\
...\\
c_{2,k-1}\in\mathcal{L}(e_{i_{k-1})}\\
c_{2,k}=c\end{subarray}}\prod_{j=1}^{k-1}V(i_{j},\tau_{j-1}(i_{j}),c_{1,j},\tau_{j}(i_{j}),c_{2,j})

\displaystyle\times\langle\mathbf{b}_{i,c_{1,k}}^{\phi},\mathbf{b}_{i,c}^{\phi}\rangle(\mathbb{1}\left[\tau_{k-1}(i)=c_{1,k}\right]-\mathbf{p}_{r}(i,c_{1,k})).

We omit
\pi
in

(7)

Fix
\pi

Lemma 30

If

Proof of
Lemma

30
.

For any

\displaystyle g_{k}(i,c)

Let

5.1.4
Bound the transition matrix

Let

be the diagonal matrix where

.
In this section, we give an upper bound for

as
\mathbf{B}
can be represented as the product of covariance matrices of
\mathbf{p}_{r}
,
\mathbf{p}_{i}
and some auxiliary diagonal matrices.

Proposition 32
.

Let
C_{i}\in\mathbb{R}^{X_{v_{i}}\times|\mathcal{L}(e_{i})|}
denote the matrix satisfying that
C_{i}((j,c_{1}),c_{2})=\mathbb{1}\left[c_{1}=c_{2}\right]

.

\mathbf{R}\in\mathbb{R}^{X\times X}

\mathbf{B}=\mathtt{Cov}(\mathbf{p}_{r})D_{T}^{-1}\mathbf{R}D_{T}^{-1}.

Proof of
Proposition

32
.

Note that for any
i\in[d]
and
c_{1},c_{2}\in\mathcal{L}(e_{i})
,

\displaystyle\mathbf{R}((i,c_{1}),(i,c_{2}))

\displaystyle=C_{i}^{\top}\mathtt{Cov}(\mathbf{p}_{i})C_{i}(c_{1},c_{2})

\displaystyle=\mathbf{p}_{i}(\bar{c_{1}},\bar{c_{2}})-\mathbf{p}_{i}(\bar{c_{1}})\mathbf{p}_{i}(\bar{c_{2}}).

Therefore, for any

and

\displaystyle\mathbf{B}((i,c_{2}),(j,c_{4}))

\displaystyle=\sum_{c_{3}\in\mathcal{L}(e_{j})}(\mathbf{p}_{r}(j,c_{3},i,c_{2})-\mathbf{p}_{r}(j,c_{3})\mathbf{p}_{r}(i,c_{2}))\times\frac{\mathbf{p}_{i}(\bar{c_{3}},\bar{c_{4}})}{\mathbf{p}_{i}(\bar{c_{3}})\mathbf{p}_{i}(\bar{c_{4}})}

\displaystyle=\sum_{c_{3}\in\mathcal{L}(e_{j})}\mathtt{Cov}(\mathbf{p}_{r})((i,c_{2}),(j,c_{3}))\left(\frac{\mathbf{p}_{i}(\bar{c_{3}},\bar{c_{4}})}{\mathbf{p}_{i}(\bar{c_{3}})\mathbf{p}_{i}(\bar{c_{4}})}-1\right)

.

The Loewner Order still holds under the Congruent transformation.

\displaystyle\mathbf{R}\preceq(1+\eta)\operatorname{diag}\{C^{\top}_{i}\Pi(\mathbf{p}_{i})C_{i}\}_{i\in[d]}=(1+\eta)(I-D_{T}).

Plugging in

Applying marginal bounds for

6.2
Simplicial complexes

First we introduce simplicial complexes to encode the edge coloring instance. Given a universe
U
, a
simplicial complex

\mathcal{C}\subseteq 2^{U}
is a collection of subsets of
U
that is downward close, which means that if
\sigma\in\mathcal{C}
and
\sigma^{\prime}\subseteq\sigma
, then
\sigma^{\prime}\in\mathcal{C}
. Every element
\sigma\in\mathcal{C}
is called a
face
, and a face that is not a proper subset of any other face is called a
maximal face
or a
facet
. The dimension of a face
\sigma
is
\mathtt{dim}(\sigma):=\left|\sigma\right|
, namely the size of
\sigma
. For every
k\geq 0
, let
\mathcal{C}_{k}:=\left\{\sigma\in\mathcal{C}\,:\,\left|\sigma\right|=k\right\}
be the set of faces of dimension
k
. Specifically,
\mathcal{C}_{0}=\left\{\varnothing\right\}
. The dimension of
\mathcal{C}
is the maximum dimension of faces in
\mathcal{C}
.

Besides, we say
\mathcal{C}
is a
pure

n
-dimensional simplicial complex if all maximal faces in
\mathcal{C}
are of dimension
n
. In this work we only deal with pure simplicial complexes. In a pure simplicial complexe, we define the co-dimension of a face
\sigma
as
\mathtt{codim}(\sigma):=n-\mathtt{dim}(\sigma)
.

Let
\pi_{n}
be a distribution over the maximal faces
\mathcal{C}_{n}
. We use the pair
(\mathcal{C},\pi_{n})
to denote a
weighted simplicial complex
where for each
1\leq k<n
, the distribution
\pi_{n}
induces a distribution
\pi_{k}
over
\mathcal{C}_{k}
. Formally, for every
1\leq k<n
and every
\sigma^{\prime}\in\mathcal{C}_{k}
,
\pi_{k}(\sigma^{\prime})
is proportional to the sum of weights of maximal faces containing
\sigma
. Formally,

\displaystyle\pi_{k}(\sigma^{\prime}):=\frac{1}{\binom{n}{k}}\sum_{\sigma\in\mathcal{C}_{n}\,:\,\sigma^{\prime}\subset\sigma}\pi_{n}(\sigma).

It can be easily verified that
\pi_{k}
is a distribution on
\mathcal{C}_{k}
. Sometimes, we omit the subscript when
k=1
, i.e., we write
\pi
for
\pi_{1}
.

Also we define the simplicial complexes generated by pinning a face in
\mathcal{C}
. For a face
\tau\in\mathcal{C}
of dimension
k
, we define its
link
as

\displaystyle\mathcal{C}_{\tau}=\left\{\sigma\setminus\tau\,:\,\sigma\in\mathcal{C}\land\tau\subseteq\sigma\right\}.

Obviously,
\mathcal{C}_{\tau}
is a pure
(n-k)
-dimensional simplicial complex. Similarly, for every
1\leq j\leq n-k
,
\mathcal{C}_{\tau,j}
is the set of faces in
\mathcal{C}_{\tau}
of dimension
j
. We also use
\pi_{\tau,j}
to denote the
marginal distribution
on
\mathcal{C}_{\tau,j}
. Formally, for every
\sigma\in\mathcal{C}_{\tau,j}
,

\displaystyle\pi_{\tau,j}(\sigma):=\mathbb{P}_{\alpha\sim\pi_{k+j}}\left[\alpha=\tau\cup\sigma\,:\,\tau\subseteq\alpha\right]=\frac{\pi_{k+j}(\tau\cup\sigma)}{\binom{k+j}{k}\cdot\pi_{k}(\tau)}.

We also drop the subscript when
j=1
, i.e., we write
\pi_{\tau}
for
\pi_{\tau,1}
. We define a random walk
P_{\tau}
with stationary distribution
\pi_{\tau}
as

P_{\tau}(x,y)=\frac{\pi_{\tau,2}(\left\{x,y\right\})}{2\pi_{\tau}(x)}.

Tight bound for weak spatial mixing

In this section, we prove the tight bound for weak spatial mixing of
q
-edge coloring instance on trees.
Note that the strong spatial mixing of
q
-edge coloring instance is a special case of spatial mixing of list instance (for list coloring instance, weak spatial mixing is equivalent to strong spatial mixing).
Therefore the theorem stated in this section is a weak version of spatial mixing conclusions and thus we can give a tight bound for trees.

The main theorem for weak spatial mixing on trees is as follows.

Theorem 47
.

Given a tree

with
n

m

.
Suppose that instance

1.

\varepsilon=\max\{\frac{\Delta-1}{\Delta},\frac{e-1}{e}\}
.

2.

If

, there exists an instance that does not satisfy weak spatial mixing.

Consider the following example which simply shows that hardness of weak spatial mixing:

Example 1

Consider a
(\Delta-1)
-regular tree with depth
