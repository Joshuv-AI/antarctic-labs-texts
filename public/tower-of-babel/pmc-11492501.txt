How social class shapes breast cancer risk perspectives and prevention practices of Australian midlife women: a qualitative study using the concept of ‘breast cancer candidacy’
Authors: Samantha Batchelor, Belinda Lunnay, Sara Macdonald, Paul R. Ward
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
BackgroundThe increasing incidence of breast cancer and disease burden is a significant public health concern. While 30% of breast cancers could be prevented through addressing modifiable risk factors, misconceptions among women about breast cancer risks hamper primary prevention. In the absence of primary prevention, secondary prevention such as mammography increases the early detection of breast cancer and improves health outcomes. However, current population-level screening rates indicate secondary prevention is suboptimal. More effective public health efforts to improve breast cancer prevention are required. Given breast cancer is socially patterned, this work explores how social class impacts women’s breast cancer prevention practices. This study uses the concepts of lay epidemiology and candidacy as a mechanism to understand women’s breast cancer risk perspectives. It engages Bourdieu’s relational social class theory to unpack how women’s social, cultural, and structured life contexts shape these perspectives and their considerations regarding primary and secondary prevention.MethodsIn this qualitative study 43 Australian midlife women (aged 45–64 years), were interviewed to explore their understandings of breast cancer risks, how they perceived their own risk, and how this shaped their prevention behaviours. A theory-informed thematic analysis applying Bourdieu’s concepts of habitus, capital, and fields to understand how women’s social class positions shapes risk perspectives and prevention practices was conducted.ResultsThis social class analysis showed differences in how women engage in breast cancer discourse, consider risks, and participate in breast cancer prevention. Middle-class women prioritise health promoting practices and were more likely than working-class and affluent women to attend mammography screening. Working-class women experience structural factors, like low income, stress and difficult life circumstances, which hamper primary prevention practices and for some screening is not considered or prioritised, and their decisions not to screen are less active. Affluent women often do not consider themselves at-risk due to their healthier ‘lifestyles. ’They suggest that this, and their knowledge of screening benefits and harms allows them to make informed decisions not to screen.ConclusionsWomen interpret and understand breast cancer risks differently and enact prevention practices within the parameters afforded by their social class positions. These findings are useful to inform improved public health approaches regarding both modifiable breast cancer risks and increasing mammography screening. To improve equity in breast cancer prevention efforts, such approaches must respond to limitations based on social class and address structural factors that impact prevention practices.

Background

While breast cancer survival rates have improved, its increasing incidence remains a significant public health concern [1]. Globally, breast cancer now surpasses lung cancer as the most commonly diagnosed cancer [2], and it is the leading cause of years of life lost, years lived with disability, and disability-adjusted life years for women [3]. To address this burgeoning incidence [4] and consequent burden, effective primary and secondary prevention strategies are crucial.

Up to 30% of breast cancer cases are potentially preventable by addressing modifiable risk factors (maintaining a healthy weight, adequate physical activity, and decreasing alcohol consumption) [5–8]. However, primary prevention efforts may be hindered by ongoing misperceptions among women about breast cancer risk factors, causes, modifiability and risk reduction [9–11]. Indeed, many women see genetic or family history as the greatest risk for breast cancer, and if this is not present, they tend to suggest breast cancer is not a concern for them [12–14]. Many other women are unaware and surprised by breast cancer risks like alcohol [10, 11, 15–18]. Additionally, a greater emphasis on fundraising campaigns (for treatment and cure) rather than sustained educational campaigns (for prevention and screening) [19] may also hinder understanding that some breast cancer risks are modifiable.

Secondary prevention via mammography screening is also not without issues. Despite public support for mammography screening [20], many high-income countries, including Australia, experience suboptimal screening rates (less than 70%) [21–23]. Barriers at the individual (e.g., fear, risk perceptions and beliefs), relational (e.g., relationships with health care staff) and systems levels (e.g., access to screening services) are possible explanations [22, 24–26]. Factors such as low socioeconomic status [27] and health literacy [28, 29], being an immigrant [30–33], lack of health insurance [27, 34], and concerns about screening discomfort, are associated with nonparticipation in screening [21, 35, 36]. Moreover, controversies regarding the efficacy and safety of population-level screening [37] may also negatively impact participation.

These complexities and challenges highlight the need for improved public health approaches for increasing primary and secondary breast cancer prevention. This paper reports on a study that explored how social class impacts women’s breast cancer prevention practices. A social class lens is important, since breast cancer incidence, outcomes, and the role of modifiable risk factors in disease aetiology exhibit a social patterning [38–42]. Moreover, social contexts inequitably influence individuals’ capacity to act on modifiable risk factors [43] and lifestyle approaches are critiqued for emphasising individual responsibility for health outcomes, ignoring the role of inequitable structures and social contexts that impact practices [44].

Therefore, to inform equitable breast cancer prevention approaches, this study engaged with Bourdieu’s relational social class theory. The study aim was to understand how women’s social class shapes their practice of lay epidemiology and perceptions of their breast cancer ‘candidacy’ and if/how this impacts their responses to modifiable breast cancer risks or participation in mammography screening. By blending the sociology of candidacy with Bourdieu’s relational social class theory, a novel approach to uncovering some of the complexities shaping midlife women’s participation in breast cancer prevention is offered. This study envisions candidacy on a continuum, where women’s risk perspectives may fluctuate between positioning themselves as candidates (at-risk), noncandidates (not-at-risk), or somewhere ‘in between’. Prior to outlining the study methodology, an overview of the concepts of lay epidemiology and candidacy is provided and the key aspects of Bourdieu’s social class theory used to inform the analysis are discussed.

An overview of lay epidemiology and candidacy

Candidacy and lay epidemiology are central concepts in understanding how individuals perceive and respond to disease risk. Candidacy - lay individuals’ assessment of their own and others’ disease risk is informed by lay epidemiology - the observations of patterns of disease individuals make at micro, meso, and macro levels, being first described in the context of coronary heart disease [45]. These concepts have since been applied in various health contexts including cancer [46, 47], diabetes [48], COVID-19 [49], and breast cancer [50, 51]. Within these contexts, enactments of candidacy such as resisting candidacy (individuals denying or justifying risks) and competing candidacy (individuals prioritising other life issues over disease risks), provide mechanisms that lead individuals to dismiss prevention messages [52].

Importantly, these enactments have been shown to be socially patterned. Research examining women’s alcohol-related breast cancer risk perceptions that engaged Bourdieu’s relational social class theory showed that middle-class and affluent women resisted candidacy, relative to alcohol consumption, while competing candidacy is observed among working-class women [14]. Furthermore, a ‘fluidity’ to candidacy was revealed according to women’s agency to act on their health. Middle-class and affluent women could respond to prevention messages and modify behaviour with the view to prevent breast cancer, whereas there were structural limitations on working-class women’s agency to change - even if risks were identified, they could not necessarily be prevented [14].

This study builds upon this previous work. Using Bourdieu’s social class theory as an interpretive lens [53], the study aims to facilitate an understanding of the social reasons and logic behind women’s participation in primary and secondary breast cancer prevention and how this connects with candidacy. Below, the key elements of Bourdieu’s social class theory that are applied in this study are discussed.

Thinking with Bourdieu – Habitus, Capitals, and Fields

Using Bourdieu’s ‘thinking tools’ [54], the concepts of habitus, capital, and fields are applied—to illuminate the intricacies of agency and structure, providing explanatory heft to women’s primary and secondary breast cancer prevention practices and how lay epidemiologies and understandings of cancer candidacy are shaped. Bourdieu posits that an individual’s habitus is acquired and ‘structures’ their experiences and socialisation; it is ‘structuring’ in that it shapes individuals’ practices, and the ‘structure’ is ordered [55]. Therefore, habitus dictates and structures dispositions, ways of being, tastes and inclinations, which become embodied at an individual level [53, 54]. Habitus, along with access to varying amounts and compositions of economic (wealth, financial assets), cultural (knowledge, skills, and education) and social (social connections and networks) culminating and expressed in symbolic capital (prestige), enables or constrains action within social class positions, which in turn impacts ‘lifestyles’ [53]. Both habitus and capital shape position and behaviour in ‘fields’. For Bourdieu, ‘fields’ are social spaces or spheres within which people ‘perform’ different activities and identities, such as work, education, and importantly to this work, to access healthcare. Within these varying fields, individuals compete for social power by utilising and accruing capital. Individuals have more or less ‘feel for the game’ within specific fields, and as comfort within fields reflects habitus and access to capitals, some may feel like ‘fish out of water’ [55]. As such, habitus, and capital interlock to produce social practices surrounding breast cancer prevention. These concepts are used throughout the analysis to explore how women’s experiences, resources, and social positioning shapes their risk perceptions, ability to address modifiable risks (primary prevention) and their participation in mammography screening (secondary prevention).

Methods

Participants and procedures

The study sample was recruited from women living on the Central Coast, New South Wales (NSW), Australia. The region includes areas of higher/lower socioeconomic advantage/disadvantage [56] and the population is characterised by a high prevalence of breast cancer risk factors (smoking, alcohol consumption inactivity, and obesity) [57] compared to NSW overall. The region also has lower rates of mammography screening compared to the NSW state average; in 2022–2023 screening rates were 48.4% and 51.9% respectively [58], while most recent national data reported the screening rate for the period 2021–2022 as 49.6% [59].

Midlife women (45–64 years) with no previous or current breast cancer diagnosis were considered eligible to enable an understanding on women’s differing perspectives on candidacy. Sampling was purposive to obtain variability in social class, screening status (screeners, non-screeners, irregular screeners), and levels of alcohol consumption (low, moderate, high). Recruitment occurred locally through targeted Facebook advertising, flyers distributed in community settings (e.g., residential communities for adults over 50 years of age, and sporting clubs), and Author SB attended local women’s community events (e.g., International Women’s and Multicultural Health Day) to speak about the research and generate interest in participation. When local recruitment slowed, a broader national Facebook advertising campaign was conducted.

To measure women’s social class, all participants completed the Australian social class survey by Sheppard and Biddle [60], which was adapted from the work of Savage et al. (2013) [61]. This survey actualises the relational social class theory of Bourdieu [53, 62]. It measures varying compositions and volumes of capital—economic (income and assets), social capital (occupational prestige of people they know) and cultural capital (activities they participate in)-to allocate women to either: established or emerging affluent, mobile or established middle and established working social class groups. For further details, readers are referred to the author’s earlier work [14, 63, 64]. For reporting purposes, the five social class groups were collapsed into affluent, middle, and working classes.

Forty-three semi-structured interviews were conducted via Microsoft Teams (n = 36) and telephone (n = 7). Telephone interviews were conducted where participants preferred or because access to online technology was difficult. Online interviews were auto-transcribed while using Microsoft Teams. Telephone interviews were recorded and transcribed using software (Otter Ai). All interviews were conducted by Author SB, a midlife woman living on the NSW Central Coast. Issues of positionality, including the author’s ‘insider-outsider’ status [65] and the alignment with/differences between her own screening and drinking status and participant’s status, was important to consider. Author SB thoughtfully considered the disclosure of this information, given it could potentially either skew participants’ responses or build rapport - to balance this, she decided only to disclose this information if participants directly asked.

Interviews were also conducted using ‘empathetic neutrality’ [66]. Each interview commenced with participants’ descriptions of their family life, social contexts they engage with, work and leisure activities, and alcohol consumption practices. This fostered rapport and was intended to encourage women to explore their own subjectivities and positioned them as experts in commenting on their own lives/the lives of midlife women and women occupying particular social class positions. It provided context to their habitus, the capitals afforded to them, and the fields in which they engaged and were comfortable in. These attributes were later used to analyse the data by social class. Interviews explored women’s experiences with breast cancer and their understanding of breast cancer risks, including whether they perceived certain types of women to be ‘at risk’ and how they judged their own risk or ‘candidacy’. Women’s perspectives on their control over health, including whether breast cancer risk can be reduced and what preventative action they did or did (could) not take, were explored. The interviews commenced 26th August 2022, as NSW was emerging from early COVID-19 pandemic countermeasures. Women were asked about the impacts of COVID-19 restrictions (lockdowns and social distancing) on their daily lives, including health-related practices, and access to health care. The study was conducted with transparency and rigor, as outlined using COREQ-32 [67]. Data collection ceased when the data set was deemed to have adequate depth, breadth and richness to answer the research question [68]. Ethical approval was provided by Torrens University Australia Human Research Ethics Committee (project code 0184). All participants provided informed consent. Analysis

The study was guided by the author’s earlier work on candidacy [14, 52]. This allowed the thematic analysis of data reported here to be considered through the lens of social class (i.e. theory-informed and to verify/extend the concept of candidacy All transcripts were verified against the audio files to ensure completeness and accuracy and as a mechanism for data immersion and to sensitise to the data before commencing analysis. The analysis continued with open coding using a pluralistic deductive approach (whereby candidacy constructs were identified in the data) and an inductive approach [69]. Guided by the category framework from the earlier study [14], all open codes were then categorised. Relevant and connected categories were merged and through this process four themes were constructed (candidacy, lay epidemiology, primary prevention, secondary prevention). We explored different categories/themes by social class that were most relevant to answer the key research questions about differences in women’s lay epidemiology and the implications for candidacy, and with implications for primary and secondary breast cancer prevention. This exploration identified patterns in the data that highlighted excerpts for further exploration and facilitated reading across excerpts to increase understanding of these patterns. Being cognisant of how social class was revealed through women’s narratives, attention was paid to women’s levels of access to capital, and how this was shaped by their habitus, and how this ‘played out’ within their social fields. Accordingly, explanations could be inferred about differences in the data, both within and across social class groups. Next, the findings are presented to demonstrate how breast cancer candidacy, choices/decisions about prevention, and social class fuse in complex and shifting ways.

Results

Through the analysis process, four themes were constructed. Candidacy explores how women position their own breast cancer risk. Lay epidemiology relates to women’s varying breast cancer understandings/experiences/awareness and the information/observations they gather about disease risks and prevention. Primary prevention relates to women’s perspectives of their own health and wellbeing, how they maintain and are able to prioritise (or not) their health, the health-related practices they participate in and thoughts about reducing disease risk. Secondary prevention explores women’s rationales and perspectives about their mammography screening choices/decisions. The results are presented as outlined in Fig. 1.

Candidacy and Lay Epidemiology

Drawing from Bourdieu’s theory, breast cancer experiences and information are considered as a ‘field’ where women develop their lay epidemiology based on their observations at the micro, meso, and macro levels. The findings reveal distinct social class-based patterns in women’s engagement within this field, shaping breast cancer risk perceptions and preventive health practices. Consistent with earlier research, identifying a culturally familiar breast cancer candidate was difficult, and many women positioned themselves as noncandidates, showing a reluctance to assign candidacy to oneself [14, 45, 50, 51].

Working-class women

While some working-class women reported experiencing breast cancer among family and friends, the data suggest that breast cancer was often on their ‘perimeters,’ and infrequently discussed within their social circles. Not knowing anyone with breast cancer seemed surprising, unusual, and interesting, ‘When you think of the prevalence’ (Tara, aged 52, working-class). Consequently, some women had little interest in nor actively engaged with breast cancer information. Such information was either overlooked— ‘I just sort of scroll past stuff that I’m not really interested in’ (Vicki, aged 63, working-class) or dismissed due to feeling overloaded with health messaging: ‘We’re completely bombarded with health scares […] everyone’s wearing a ribbon every second day for ****ing something’ (Renee, aged 49, working-class).

As breast cancer was ‘not front of mind,’ identifying risks and envisioning candidacy were challenging if women had ‘never ever thought about the risks.’ Therefore, familiar tropes regarding the randomness of cancer were recalled and women’s observations of anomalous cases confirmed this view: ‘But then there are people that have a really healthy, wonderful lifestyle and they get breast cancer. So, it seems to be a bit of a lottery.’ (Kelly, aged 49, working-class). Consequently, breast cancer risk could be multifactorial and difficult to isolate, given that ‘every woman, or every person is at risk’ (Susan, aged 47, working-class). Women realised that genetic or family history of breast cancer increased breast cancer risk, with this being the only factor some women had clarity about: ‘the main risk I can think of offhand is if you’re relatives have had it, you know, if somebody in your family. That’s about it.’ (Grace, aged 56, working-class).

Certainly, women tended to discount their breast cancer risk if there was no family/genetic reasons that raised concern: ‘I didn’t think I came from a cancery type family’ (Rhonda, aged 62, working-class). However, women’s perceptions of their candidacy seemed to shift as more factors were considered within her assessment of her own risk. For example, later in the same interview, Rhonda identified other risks like no biological children nor breast-feeding experience and her mother’s breast cancer, and reflected: ‘Yeah, I’m probably more a high candidate than I ever thought of myself as being.’ Similarly, Lisa (aged 56, working-class) re-evaluated her risk based on her sister’s recent breast cancer diagnosis. Lisa perceived she did not have the characteristics of a candidate: ‘I’ve never been sick …everyone that I do know of that had cancer has been, like an unwell person […] I’m super healthy, I’m fit, I eat well, I don’t drink a lot.’ However, her candidacy remained uncertain, pending the outcomes of genetic testing: ‘My chances are lower, but if I have a genetic predisposition to it, I don’t think those things will help me.’

While some working-class women fluctuated between being ‘at-risk’ or ‘not at-risk,’ typically ‘noncandidate’ positions were assumed, but there were exceptions. Early menopause made Jasmine (aged 49, working-class) feel vulnerable and concerns about her health generally, and her friends’ recent breast cancer diagnosis heightened her own sense of candidacy: ‘I think it definitely made me think that I will. I can get it, like I’m three years older than her, of course I can get it. And she hasn’t gone through menopause.’ Here, Jasmine is assessing the likeness of her own characteristics against those of a ‘confirmed’ breast cancer candidate. This and her thoughts about breast cancer prevalence: ‘You’ll be very lucky if you avoid it’ seemed to provide Jasmine with sufficient evidence to position herself as a candidate. Nicole (age 46, working-class) considered her candidacy in accordance with her former heavy-drinking, and her physicality and large volume of breast tissue: ‘this sounds really stupid because I know sensibly it’s not a reason, but I do have quite big breasts […] they’re quite big, I’m bound to get it because there’s a lot of it.’

Middle-class women

Middle-class women often spoke about breast cancer in their circles and only two women had not known anyone with breast cancer. Sometimes, a diagnosis within women’s circles prompted preventive action, which Maggie (aged 52, middle-class) described: ‘Obviously all of us went and started having our scans done then… We had all just turned 40.’ Middle-class women also seemed attuned to breast cancer in public discourse, connecting with the ‘pink ribbon’ symbol of breast cancer and viewing breast cancer as ‘one of the most discussed cancers’ and something ‘you can’t be ignoring.’

Although women were aware of breast cancer, confusion about risks remained, as Tess (aged 46, middle-class) described: ‘Even though it’s talked about a lot, whether it’s super clear, … probably smoking and drinking, sort of that’s a familiar link, the gene increases your factors, but I wouldn’t be 100% sure what all the risk factors are.’ Some middle-class women identified those with unhealthy lifestyles as likely candidates, and to support their claims they provided examples of women they knew with breast cancer who were overweight and drank alcohol ‘heavily’. Mostly, women did not recognise lifestyle risks within themselves, and those assuming candidate positions tended to base this on risks beyond their control, like genetic/family risks. An exception was Cheryl (aged 63, middle-class), who referred to herself as a ‘functioning alcoholic’ and recognised that this and her overweight status positioned her as a candidate. Noncandidate positions reflected perceived healthy lifestyle practices, and maintaining this status was viewed as an individual responsibility to ‘do everything I can to have a healthy lifestyle.’ (Melinda, aged 56, middle-class).

Affluent-class women

The most affluent women interviewed had varied experiences with breast cancer within their networks - it remained on the fringes of some women’s social circles, but others had closer experiences. Liz (aged 51, affluent-class) said that a diagnosis within her friendship group bought it ‘to the fore’, where breast cancer was discussed ‘an awful lot’, generating concern about one’s own level of risk. However, when the diagnosis was an older relative rather than a peer, there was much less concern about one’s own risk. In connecting with breast cancer information, affluent women echoed the ‘disinterest’ of working-class women contrasting with the engagement of middle-class women. Some were aware of breast cancer risks but did not actively seek information, as Emily (aged 50, affluent-class) pondered: ‘am I meant to be informing myself about every single thing I could get and prevent it?’

Some women felt breast cancer lacks visibility, which they attributed to their disconnection from traditional media (avoiding the news, not watching commercial television). Algorithmic culture was also felt to contribute. For example, Rose (aged 60, affluent-class), conveyed that as she was not searching online for breast cancer information, it was unlikely to be delivered to her and thus ‘not in my field of vision.’ Other women were more cognisant of breast cancer, resulting from their cultural capital developed through tertiary studies, healthcare careers and personal networks with health qualifications. This allowed access to scientific information related to breast cancer risks but also about disease prevention generally: ‘So with my lifestyle medicine studies, we talk a lot about prevention, and we talk a lot about the lifestyle causes of certain types of cancer.’ (Tanya, aged 52, affluent-class).

Despite recognising modifiable risks such as alcohol consumption, diet, as well as genetics and family history, like working-class women described, many affluent women conveyed that ultimately they felt luck played a significant role in who was a candidate for breast cancer: ‘it’s just obviously the hereditary factor, … but otherwise I just thought it was a, you know, like a lot of cancers. Your bad luck. Like, your numbers up your numbers up type thing’ (Liz, aged 51, affluent-class). However, when discussing their personal risk, healthy lifestyle discourse was deployed by affluent women, to offer a logic for their ‘noncandidate’ positions. For example, Heather (aged 45, affluent-class) noted: ‘I’m not a big drinker, …I’ve got a good diet… I’m very conscious to look after my health and to manage stress and get my 8 hours sleep.’ Although her mother survived breast cancer, Heather said she had not given her own risk much thought but surmised it was low and distanced herself from the idea that she was ‘at risk’ and speculated her mother’s diagnosis was related to HRT. Similarly, May (aged 60, affluent-class) attributed her aunt’s breast cancer (developed in her 70s) to prolonged unhealthy behaviours across her life course: ‘She was a heavy smoker through her life. She used to drink quite a bit.’ In this way, affluent-class women can use their privileged class positions to distance themselves from risk despite familial experiences of breast cancer and, like middle-class women, their access to economic, social, and cultural capitals afforded opportunities to pursue healthy practices that they felt prevented breast cancer risk.

An exception was Chloe (aged 45, affluent-class), who was cognisant of her risks given her family history of breast cancer and accordingly, she undertook regular monitoring for breast changes. While she had considered genetic testing, she felt that a positive result could generate more concern: ‘just having that in the back of my mind all the time. If I am at higher risk knowing that just one day, like.’ She was comfortable that she was doing enough to monitor herself and manage her risk.

Engagement with breast cancer discourse is a ‘field’ in which lay epidemiology develops and these findings show this is social class-based, shaping the ways women perceive risks and informing their breast cancer candidacy. This has implications for improving primary prevention efforts, as the findings described next demonstrate.

Candidacy and primary prevention

Enactments of candidacy were observed in women’s narratives. Resisting candidacy - denying the existence of risk factors in one’s risk assessment, competing candidacy - prioritising other health and/or life circumstances over risk considerations, and fluidity of candidacy - the ability to identify and modify behaviours and practices for cancer prevention [14, 52] emerged in the data in socially patterned ways.

Working-class women

Typically, resisting candidacy, particularly relative to alcohol consumption, was rare among working-class women as half of the working-class women interviewed consumed alcohol only occasionally or not at all. Women reported varied reasons for this, ranging from the negative impacts of alcohol as they aged and medication interactions, past problematic alcohol use (resulting in sobriety) or a history of minimal drinking. Thus, alcohol risk seemed irrelevant, as they drank ‘once in a blue moon.’ Among the few working-class women who drank alcohol at self-reported moderate-heavy levels, alcohol risk was acknowledged, yet these women tended to accept risk in the absence of being able to renegotiate the reasons for drinking alcohol, as Janet (aged 52, working-class) noted: ‘I understand the risks. I understand the outcomes, but it’s not going to stop me from drinking.’ Alcohol offered Janet respite from difficult life circumstances, and the ‘need’ for alcohol to manage stress was more pressing than reducing alcohol for reducing breast cancer risk: ‘I’ve weighed that and compared that to my stress right at the moment… some days I’ve got the choice of I’m gonna drink or I’m gonna kill myself. And I know which ones the healthier option here.’

Consistent with the intensely difficult life circumstances described by Janet, enactments of competing candidacy were evident through working-class women’s narratives and were more commonly heard than from other women interviewed (middle-class and affluent women). Breast cancer had little relevance for Renee (aged 49, working-class) when mental health concerns associate with neurodivergence, and maintaining sobriety were more proximal issues to manage. Additionally, she described her mother’s recent death while living with dementia and how it meant that she was ‘definitely more worried about cognitive decline than I am about getting breast cancer.’

Daily struggles and difficult life circumstances hindered working-class women’s abilities to prioritise health practices generally, despite some women recognising the importance of these practices for breast cancer risk reduction. Tracy (aged 53, working-class) suggested: ‘the perceived stress in someone’s life’ might mean that health is ‘not their priority.’ Georgia (aged 52, working-class) reiterated this, recognising that women with more disadvantage are ‘really struggling on a day-to-day basis… it’s hard to do the things to be able to survive day-to-day, rather than […] than adding in trying to be healthful as well. I think maybe sometimes it is out of some people’s control and out of their reach of being able to do something.’ Moreover, Georgia said she was struggling to focus on her own health and was impeded by ‘competing factors.’

Certainly, this was a common theme among working-class women for whom health ‘fell off the radar.’ The ‘clamouring demands’ of single parenting, caring for elderly parents while also working and studying, impacted Kelly’s (aged 49, working-class) ability to ‘follow good health directions’ and when life became overwhelming, she said: ‘the first thing that I sacrifice is my own needs and my own time.’ Sacrifice to meet others’ needs was commonplace among working-class women interviewed. Laura (aged 56, working-class) noted that while ‘you put yourself last. Yeah, we don’t do healthy things for yourself,’ there was also a felt need (and worry) to maintain health to allow these caring roles to continue, as she added: ‘I think if I’m not healthy, I can’t help nobody else.’

Working class women’s preventive health practices were impeded by structural factors such as low income, resulting in cyclic stress, poor health, and discomforts in accessing health care and some prevention activities (e.g. exercise). Subsequently, the fluidity of candidacy [14] or the ability to identify risks and then act on these by modifying practices for cancer prevention, is limited, because of limited agency to control inequitable life conditions. Kylie (aged 62, working-class) said: ‘I’m definitely overweight and I probably could do more exercise, but yeah, that’s probably not gonna happen. Certainly, Jessica (aged 47, working-class) had attempted this by purchasing a gym membership. However, her experiences in the fitness environment had been uncomfortable, and her membership was unused. She described finding out that her large body type meant suitable gym attire was difficult to locate, and gym equipment difficult to use: ‘my boobs are too big. It’s really hard. I couldn’t get a sports bra, so I ended up just going well. I’ll just wear my normal bra and like a tight singlet or something and then something over the top of that. But it wasn’t just that, it was the equipment. I can’t get physically get into certain things, cause you sort of have to lean on something and then put your arms in and do this sort of. Like I just don’t fit.’

Drawing on Bourdieu’s field theory, her discomfort and feelings of being ‘a fish out of water’ [53] stemmed from not fitting the stereotypical gym-goer identity, either in her attire, physicality or capacities in utilising the gym. Instead of using the gym, she sought solace at home and blamed herself: ‘Oh, I’m just lazy. There’s more to it than that. There’s some comfort in sitting at home and doing nothing.’ Ultimately, she avoided the gym, as it did not align with her social class identity and habitus [70].

Middle-class women

Middle-class women’s narratives revealed instances of resisting candidacy relative to alcohol consumption. Ivy (aged 62, middle-class), a self-reported moderate drinker who assumed a noncandidate position, was nonchalant about alcohol risk. Taking a numbers approach, Ivy deduced that her risk was small: ‘One in 10. Now, given that, maybe what 20–25% of the population are affected by breast cancer? One in ten of those, that’s really small odds. That’s odds I can live with.’ This extract highlights the public health challenges encountered in addressing public understandings of absolute and relative risk. Here, Ivy extrapolates her own risk on the basis of absolute risk, and her access to knowledge about relative risk seems limited. Her logic does not reflect relative risk that moderate to high consumption doubles the risk compared to instances where alcohol is consumed at low levels across the lifespan [71].

Consistent with earlier research [14], women in this study drew on their healthy practices, rationalising that they ‘compensated’ for risk: ‘we don’t take drugs, we don’t smoke, we do exercise, we eat healthily… like we’ve got one vice’ (Bethany, aged 60, middle-class). Indeed, middle-class women seemed to be afforded agency to leverage their ‘healthy lifestyles’ and ‘offset’ modifiable risks. The women we interviewed had access to economic and social capital that enabled them to ‘make time for me’ (Maggie, aged 52, middle-class) and increased their capacities to engage in physical activity (e.g. gym memberships, dance classes, yoga, and cycling). This was seen as not only important for women’s current wellbeing, but also for the future, as Diana (aged 54, middle-class) explained: ‘I’m really quite conscious of keeping up on absolutely everything, ‘cause I plan to be here for the long haul.’

Tess’ (aged 46, middle-class) excerpt illustrates how access to moderate levels of all capitals enables some women to identify and then to mitigate risk, demonstrating the fluidity of candidacy. Her social class position allowed her agency to leave her corporate job, move away from the city, study yoga, reduce heavy alcohol consumption, and shed her ‘party girl’ identity. In doing so, she shifted her perceived breast cancer risk. Although Tess previously considered breast cancer was ‘a possibility for me,’ at the time of the interview Tess perceived herself at low risk: ‘the changes I’ve made in my life are a really good foundation to help minimise the chances…I still don’t consider myself risk free… I won’t say invincible, I feel, it doesn’t worry me so much now.’

This agency for change, shifting risk and thus fluid candidacy, is broadly reflective of other middle-class women’s beliefs that individuals have control over their health through individual lifestyle choices and self-responsibility: ‘we’re all given the education…it really is a conscious choice to [choose health-enhancing practices]’ (Diana, aged 54, middle-class). Conversely, Ivy (aged 62, middle-class) commented that the rhetoric of responsibility sounded ‘pretty smug’, recognising the privileged positions that middle-class women are in, whereby their access to resources allows them to make such choices.

Affluent-class women

As affluent women mostly assumed noncandidate positions, considering themselves not ‘at-risk,’ enactments of candidacy did not readily emerge in their explanations. However, their narratives suggest that access to capital enables a comfort in the knowledge they could manage any health concern that may arise: ‘I do think that the more affluent you are […] that you’re more likely to survive it if you’ve got education and money behind you. I think women from means probably do better than women who come out of […] a working class or a more you know, a poorer background’ (Rose, aged 60, affluent-class). This quote is consistent with explanations from other affluent women interviewed who acknowledged that an individual’s ability to control health and participate in healthy practices is influenced by structural factors: ‘according to your awareness and your opportunities’ (Erin, aged 47, affluent-class). This is juxtaposed to the judgements about working-class women’s participation in health-promoting behaviours observable in middle-class women’s narratives. Applying relational social class theory, it could be posited that while middle-class women judge other women as a strategy to consolidate their own position in the social order, affluent women do not need to commit such ‘symbolic violence’ [53], as they are already in a position of social dominance and are not seeking to increase their social mobility. Having discussed the ways candidacy impacts primary prevention, how women’s risk perspectives impact screening participation are now considered.

Noncandidates – not at risk

Of the women who positioned themselves as ‘noncandidates,’ over half still screened, despite not perceiving themselves particularly ‘at-risk.’ Although they did not see themselves as breast cancer candidates, they did consider themselves candidates for screening. Screening was considered something women ‘just do’, which provided reassurance: ‘I’d rather do all the tests.than miss the boat’ (Tara, aged 52, working-class). Among middle-class and affluent women, screening aligned with other preventive practices such as pap smears and dental visits and was an investment in health, enabled by their privileged positions: ‘Women who have more education and money have more luxury to spend time looking after their health…. it is a little bit like a luxury because it’s not easy to even get to a GP [General Practitioner] or even like, pay or get into a GP anymore’ (Alison, aged 54, middle-class). The economic capital and cultural health capital [72] embodied by middle-class and affluent women enabled a focus on health, unlike the experiences of working-class women, where limited capital imposed a ‘taste for necessity’ [53], and prioritising healthcare and prevention may not be a possibility.

For women positioned as noncandidates and non-screeners, differences were apparent between affluent- and working-class women. Affluent-class women used their cultural capital for informed evidence-based decisions about their screening participation, and their ‘noncandidate’ position—linked to their perceived healthy lifestyle — seemed to justify non-participation in screening. Tanya (age 52, affluent class), a healthcare practitioner, illustrated this: ‘I haven’t had a mammogram… that’s an informed choice … I’m vegetarian, I’m very fit. I’m not overweight, I sleep well.’ Moreover, Tanya’s ‘informed choice’ was based on her understanding of the benefits and harms of screening, which her cultural capital allowed her to explore.

Conversely, less ‘active’ screening decisions were enacted among working-class women. For example, Grace (aged 56, working-class) attended screening in her 20s, describing it as trying to fit her breast into ‘a sandwich toaster.’ She never returned, after being told ‘this really doesn’t work very well on you. You’ve got fairly small breasts. It’s really hard to get a good X-ray. You’d be way better off getting an ultrasound.’ Grace may have been told screening does not work for younger women, and this miscommunication means she now believes screening: ‘is really not going to work for me.’ This could be interpreted as a passive decision, simply following advice from three decades ago, but her low levels of economic capital precludes her seeking an ultrasound instead, as she said she would have to pay for that. Greater cultural health capital and/or social capital might also have afforded Grace an opportunity to explore whether screening was an option for her and to make an informed decision; she rarely engaged in health care and her social circle was small, so opportunities to explore the issue never arose. Similarly, Helen (aged 59, working-class) never really gave screening a second thought: ‘I get my referrals to go and get one and then I put it in my pile, and I don’t bother.’ For Helen, the importance of screening faded compared to other health issues; she felt that breast cancer was something that would not ‘touch’ her, and screening was difficult to access from her regional location.

Candidates ‘at-risk’

Assigning candidacy to oneself prompted some women to attend screening, given that they saw themselves as ‘at-risk’ while other women declined screening despite feeling vulnerable.

Candidates who attended screening were mostly middle-class women who connected their candidacy to family/genetic risk. Consequently, some commenced screening earlier than the Australian recommendation of 50 years [73]: ‘when I realised that it was my mum and my dad side. They allowed me to go and get screening with the mammogram earlier, so I think I started to go at about 47’ (Alison, aged 54, middle-class). Like their ‘noncandidate’ screening counterparts, screening also provided reassurance for candidates. It allowed Janet (aged 52, working-class) an opportunity to manage risk, given her inability to curtail alcohol consumption due to its role in managing through her difficult life circumstances: ‘I’m not going to stop my every year screening because I’m homeless. But I’m going to certainly not stop drinking either.’

Only two women who considered themselves candidates for breast cancer were non-screeners. Although fearful of breast cancer, Jasmine (aged 49, working-class) was also very apprehensive about screening: ‘the radiation … that can actually cause more trouble, than it gives you of you know, um, insight to what’s going on inside you…And I haven’t heard anything nice about these things. I mean, it sounds like it’s an absolute torture. Sounds like awful.’ Similarly, Cheryl (aged 63, middle-class) declined screening based on concerns about radiation and being ‘very distrustful of all things medical.’

Finally, despite her heightened thoughts of risk, Liz (aged 51, affluent-class) screened irregularly, as ‘she nearly ripped your boob off and pulls you around’, but when her thoughts of risk became overwhelming, her GP referred her for private screening. Here, Liz’s narrative exemplifies privilege and how having sufficient cultural health capital and economic capital enables access to healthcare at her own convenience and to negotiate her needs, ‘I’m lucky to be in a position where you know I can afford to do it whenever you want.’

Shifting candidacy

Shifting candidacy was mostly apparent among working-class women, with women seeing themselves both ‘at risk’ and ‘not at risk,’ and this uncertainty typically prompted screening. Kelly (aged 49, working-class) considered herself low-risk based on her healthy lifestyle, and her family history did not concern her, yet her fibrocystic breasts made her feel vulnerable, with ‘tricky breasts’ where she might ‘miss something.’ This fluctuating perception of risk meant that screening provided reassurance for Kelly and other women vacillating along the candidacy continuum.

Rose (aged 60, affluent-class) and Lisa (aged 56, working-class) also described shifting views of their risk, and neither had screened. Rose had never screened due to ‘medical phobia,’ but her recent interactions with the health system for the treatment of endometrial cancer meant that she was reconsidering her screening position. In contrast, Lisa, who had a sister recently diagnosed with breast cancer, continued to consider screening irrelevant, given her GP’s recommendation that her small breasts were unsuitable for mammography. In these ways, perceptions of candidacy differentially impact participation in mammography screening. Some women screened because ‘that’s what women do,’ regardless of how they perceived their risk, while others screened because they did feel ‘at risk.’ Some (mostly affluent) women could use their assessment of risk to justify non-screening decisions or readily access screening if their risk perceptions changed.

Discussion

This study has shown how breast cancer risk perceptions and candidacy are shaped by women’s social class positions, with implications for improving public health approaches to breast cancer prevention. Through a social class analysis, differences in how women engage with breast cancer discourse, consider risks, assign candidacy, address modifiable factors, and participate in screening were revealed and warrant consideration. Compared to middle-class women, working- and affluent-class women were less engaged with breast cancer discourse and less concerned about the possibility and impacts of breast cancer in their lives; it was on their ‘perimeters,’ or not in their ‘field of vision,’ but for differing reasons which indicate structural inequities.

Considering their own risks, across the sample, most women considered themselves ‘noncandidates’, they tended to distance themselves from risk and ‘negated’ breast cancer candidacy [50]. Working-class women were more likely than other women to identify shifting candidacy. Middle-class women who positioned themselves as candidates tended to do so based on family/genetic risk, while some other women identified similarities between them and women they knew with breast cancer, and this confirmed for them that they could be candidates and at risk. While there was some understanding of breast cancer risks, consistent with other literature, women across social classes perceived family and genetic risk to be most relevant [11–13], which could be used to negate their own candidacy, while ‘luck’, ‘chance’ and the ‘randomness of cancer’ were tropes women accessed to explain breast cancer occurrences [74, 75].

Some middle- and affluent women engaged with ‘lifestyle’ discourse throughout interviews. While some women participated in healthy lifestyle behaviours such as physical activity or monitoring their weight, in their explanations this was not related to breast cancer specifically, but rather to maintaining general health. However, when discussing breast cancer risks and/or their own personal perception of risk, ‘healthy lifestyle’ narratives were deployed. Like earlier work undertaken by the authors [14], affluent women were the least likely to consider themselves ‘at risk’ due to their healthy lifestyles. Middle-class women deployed their healthy lifestyles to negate risk, particularly relative to alcohol and they resisted candidacy, but working-class women who consumed alcohol were resigned to risk in accordance with structural inequities that created a reliance on alcohol in the absence of other supports. Moreover, working-class women were more likely to discuss competing candidacy, where more proximal difficult life issues had to take precedence over breast cancer concerns [14, 51], and in the context of these circumstances, the capacity to engage in prevention practices was adversely impacted. Although, maintaining health was seen as important for enabling care for others, this was also positioned as a reason for not engaging in prevention, as some working-class women identified: ‘you put yourself last.’ Middle-class women, however, aligned with a ‘healthism’ [76] ideology, where women viewed their health as an asset (in the present and also for the future), that was proactively cultivated and prioritised. They were able to prioritise their own health through self-care activities for self-improvement and future wellness, and along with the most affluent women, they had access to a range of activities in their wellness toolkits [64]. This correlated with differing health risk horizons contoured by women’s social class positions, and it is understandable that working-class women must give focus to proximal concerns while middle-class and affluent women could look toward the future [14].

The data also verify earlier findings regarding the fluidity of candidacy, which found that ‘women’s desire to reshape or act on risk’ [14], is a function of their social class position (more/less agency to control the conditions that impact prevention practices). Moreover, while other research suggests that women need education regarding modifiable risk factors and cancer prevention [10, 12], our data suggest that along with awareness of risk factors, attention must be given to women’s capacities to address them. Among participants in this study, women’s engagement with neoliberal health discourses was apparent, as a modicum for rationalising and giving value to their preventive health practices. This was particularly the case among middle-class women who emphasised self-responsibility, said that they prioritised self-care and some seemed to judge ‘other’ women for not making ‘right’ choices in terms of participation in prevention practices, and mammography specifically. Within Bourdieu’s social class framework, various practices produced by varying access to capital and one’s habitus reproduce social stratification and perpetuate inequities. Indeed, working-class women were aware of risks, but were impeded from making the ‘right choices’ due to structural barriers and their discomfort in certain fields (such as for Jessica), which stemmed from their stigmatised social class positions. The findings herein highlight the potential shortfalls of public health approaches aligned with neoliberal philosophy for meeting the needs of working-class women. Moreover, findings align with calls for action on mid- and upstream determinants of health for equitable breast cancer prevention, enabling all women to enact modifiable risk factors at the individual level by mediating their environments [43].

Regarding secondary prevention, the connections between candidate and noncandidate positions and implications for participation in screening, although not straightforward, are evident in these findings. Most women described a ‘noncandidate’ position, perceiving themselves as not ‘at risk’ for breast cancer; however, importantly many noncandidates saw themselves as candidates for screening. This coheres with the work of Bikker et al. (2019) [77], who explored candidacy in the context of colorectal cancer. Similarly, they found no clear link between candidacy for disease (i.e. feeling ‘at risk’) and therefore screening, but rather a sense of obligation being an impetus for screening participation. Certainly, the screeners in this study echo Bikker et al’s findings and desired to meet normative screening expectations [78], considering screening to be something women ‘just do, ’regardless of their candidacy assessment. However, when familial risk was apparent among women who considered themselves ‘at risk,’ candidacy and screening did clearly align, a finding which is consistent with earlier work exploring breast cancer candidacy and mammography screening [50].

Women who assumed ‘noncandidate’ positions also used this to justify their non-screening, but this was nuanced by social class, and social class differences in the screening logics among non and irregular-screeners are important. Findings herein show that affluent non-screeners and irregular screeners are afforded this position as a result of cultural health capital. Affluent women can make evidence-based informed decisions or ‘choices’ based on their review of the science behind the benefits and potential harms of mammography screening. Additionally, cultural health capital [72] enabled better GP access and provider-patient relationships to be experienced by affluent women, where they could assert and negotiate their screening needs, and where economic capital allowed them the opportunity to seek private screening, which provided convenience and avoided wait times.

Public health attention must be given to the non-screening and irregular screening status of working-class women. Compared to affluent women, working-class women described more ‘passive’ decisions not to screen. Working-class women were not afforded the same level of health literacy that informed the decisions of affluent women, due to limited cultural and social capital. Findings indicate some working-class women had never had capacity to consider and/or were unsure of risk factors and/or be discerning about mammography screening. Indeed, reduced cultural health capital [72] impeded access to ‘second opinions’ within health systems, whereby working-class women (such as Lisa and Grace) unquestioningly accepted health professionals’ advice against screening due to small breast size, despite scientific evidence that screening is appropriate regardless of breast size. This may also be indicative of a sense of compliance that characterises working-class groups, and perhaps explains why these women fully accepted their GPs’ recommendations not to screen.

The narratives of working-class women (like Helen) also suggest that competing candidacies, and the perceived relevance of screening, impacts mammography participation. This is consistent with early work exploring breast cancer candidacy among disadvantaged African American women, which concluded that ‘individual deliberations about breast cancer risk and prevention may be at odds with a socioeconomic and sociopolitical environment that prioritises issues of daily survival,’ [51] and observes what is described herein as ‘competing candidacy’.

In summary, this study highlights how social class shapes breast cancer candidacy and impacts midlife women’s primary and secondary prevention practices. This study shows that the synergies between candidacy and prevention behaviours are complex and are influenced at various levels by social class, warranting attention in future public health approaches.

Strengths/Limitations

This study has several strengths. First, it extends scant literature exploring candidacy, women’s perspectives regarding breast cancer risks, primary prevention and screening through a social class lens. A large qualitative study is reported, with a varied sample of women from different social class positions and screening stances (regular, non, and irregular screeners) and different levels of alcohol consumption (a modifiable risk factor). Additionally, the study was conducted in a transparent and rigorous manner, as demonstrated by COREQ-32. The study limitations include that diversity was not specifically sampled for, due to study resource constraints. However, several women (n = 4) disclosed in the interview that they were part of the Australian LGBTIQ + community and other women (n = 4) disclosed they migrated to Australia and were from Asian, European and Islander backgrounds. No women identified as First Nations; however, given the low screening rates among this sub-population of Australians, culturally-informed research exploring candidacy would be valuable in future studies. While the proportion of screening and non-screening women was reflective of broader population-level data, a more equal distribution of women across the three social groups may have been desirable.

Conclusion

This study has shown a social patterning in the ways in which women with differing capitals and habitus connect with breast cancer information, their understanding of risks, and their assignation of candidacy. These factors or ‘fields’ coalesce to facilitate or constrain preventive health practices, such as addressing modifiable breast cancer risks and participation in mammography screening.

Consequently, there is a need to reconsider how public health breast cancer messages reach and impact women across/within differing social class positions. Improved public health messaging that is open, transparent, readily accessible and responds to the social contexts and conditions experienced by women with varying health capitals is required. Reframing the message that only women with a family history/genetic risk is necessary to ensure that women are aware that the absence of these risk factors, does not preclude the possibility of breast cancer. Moreover, messaging that highlights the marketing practices of other actors (like alcohol companies) rather than just the individual is required to recognise how for-profit sectors confuse breast cancer risk messaging for women [79].

While providing information about breast cancer risks at the point of screening has been shown to be acceptable [80–82], given that not all women screen, this approach to literacy will have limited reach while screening rates are sub-optimal. Improved screening rates could be achieved by ensuring that all women have clear and transparent information about the benefits and harms of screening to enable informed choice, and a particular focus on increasing access to screening among sub-populations currently unable to make informed choices. Moreover, screening programs must also be delivered in ways that increase equity in accessibility. Given the difficulties some women experience in accessing primary health care, such as GPs, the opportunity to increase access to population-level screening programs by increasing access within/to the primary health care sector must be considered. Furthermore, structural-level approaches to ensure equitable breast cancer prevention are needed, rather than approaches that focus solely on individual practices, recognising women’s competing priorities and competing candidacies [83]. Finally, these findings could have implications for the growing body of research that calls for stratified screening approaches. Again, attention to how women in differing social class groups engage with breast cancer discourse and access (or cannot access) health care is required, as this will likely impact their connection with stratified screening programs should they become standard practice.

In summary, given the increasing incidence of breast cancer, the difficulties some women experience in addressing modifiable risk factors, and consistently suboptimal mammography screening rates, considering breast cancer prevention at the structural level and through a social class lens is important to improve public health.
