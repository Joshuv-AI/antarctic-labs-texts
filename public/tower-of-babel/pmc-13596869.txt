Characteristics of hepatocellular carcinoma in a Saudi tertiary care center: a 10-year retrospective study
Authors: Rana Bokhary
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractAimsTo evaluate the clinical and pathological characteristics of hepatocellular carcinoma (HCC) diagnosed at a tertiary care center in Saudi Arabia over a 10-year period.Materials and MethodsThis retrospective observational study included 44 patients diagnosed with HCC between January 2014 and December 2023. Demographic data, risk factors, tumor characteristics, and histopathological findings were extracted from medical records and pathology reports.ResultsThe cohort showed a male predominance (77.3%), with a mean age of 65.8 years. Nearly half of patients (45.5%) had negative viral hepatitis tests. Vascular invasion and portal vein thrombosis occurred in 25.0% and 22.7%, respectively. The trabecular growth pattern predominated, and many patients presented with advanced disease features and substantial tumor burden.ConclusionHCC was frequently diagnosed at an advanced stage, with a substantial proportion of viral-negative cases. These exploratory findings are consistent with an evolving epidemiologic profile of HCC in Saudi Arabia but do not establish a causal relationship between metabolic factors and HCC. Despite its retrospective single-center design and small sample size, this study provides contemporary real-world data and highlights the need for improved surveillance, earlier detection, and larger multicenter studies.Keywords: Hepatocellular carcinoma, clinicopathological characteristics, epidemiology, tertiary care center, Saudi Arabia, retrospective study

Introduction

Hepatocellular carcinoma (HCC) is the most common primary malignancy of the liver and represents a major global health burden. According to the Global Cancer Observatory of the World Health Organization (WHO), liver cancer accounted for more than 900,000 new cases and over 800,000 deaths worldwide in 2020, making it one of the leading causes of cancer-related mortality [1]. The incidence of HCC varies considerably across geographic regions and largely reflects the distribution of underlying etiologic factors. Chronic infection with hepatitis B virus (HBV) and hepatitis C virus (HCV) remains a dominant cause of HCC globally, while other important contributors include metabolic-associated fatty liver disease (MAFLD), nonalcoholic steatohepatitis (NASH), alcohol-related liver disease, diabetes mellitus (DM), and obesity [2–5].

Over the past decade, the epidemiology of HCC has been evolving in many regions of the world. This evolution reflects both improved control of viral hepatitis and growing impact of metabolic liver disease. At the same time, the increasing prevalence of metabolic disorders such as obesity, insulin resistance, and fatty liver disease has led to a growing proportion of HCC cases arising from non-viral etiologies [4,5]. These shifting patterns highlight the importance of understanding local epidemiologic trends in order to guide surveillance strategies and improve disease prevention and management.

In Saudi Arabia, HCC constitutes the majority of primary liver cancers and remains a significant public health concern. National cancer registry data indicate that HCC accounts for approximately two-thirds of all reported liver cancer cases in the country, with an age-standardized incidence rate of approximately 5.2 per 100,000 population [6]. Historically, viral hepatitis - particularly HCV - has played a major role in the development of HCC in the region. However, increasing rates of metabolic syndrome, obesity, and DM are expected to influence the future epidemiological profile of HCC. These trends have been reported in regional and local cohorts that describe a shifting balance between viral and metabolic risk factors [7,8]. Understanding the clinical presentation and tumor characteristics of HCC within the local population is therefore essential for improving early detection and optimizing patient management.

From a pathological perspective, HCC demonstrates a range of histological growth patterns and degrees of differentiation. The trabecular pattern is the most frequently observed architectural arrangement, although other patterns such as pseudoacinar, compact, and sarcomatoid configurations may also occur. Tumor differentiation is commonly graded using the Edmondson–Steiner system, which categorizes tumors into well-differentiated (G1), moderately differentiated (G2), and poorly differentiated (G3) grades based on cytologic atypia and architectural features [9,10]. Histopathological evaluation remains essential for confirming the diagnosis, assessing tumor characteristics, and supporting clinical decision-making [11].

Despite increasing recognition of HCC in Saudi Arabia, comprehensive data integrating both clinical and histopathological characteristics of the disease remain relatively limited. Most available studies focus primarily on clinical and epidemiologic aspects without detailed correlation with tumor pathology [7,8]. Generating local data that combines clinical and pathological findings is important for understanding disease patterns and identifying opportunities to improve surveillance and management strategies.

Therefore, this retrospective study aimed to evaluate the clinical and pathological characteristics of HCC diagnosed at a tertiary care center in Saudi Arabia over a 10-year period.

Materials and methods

Study design and setting

This retrospective observational cohort study was conducted at a tertiary-care academic medical center in Jeddah, western Saudi Arabia. The study included patients diagnosed with HCC between January 2014 and December 2023. The institution functions as a major referral center for the western region of the country, providing specialized hepatology, oncology, and pathology services. The 10-year study period was selected to capture contemporary patterns of HCC presentation within the institution.

Study population

All patients with a confirmed diagnosis of HCC during the study period were eligible for inclusion. HCC diagnosis was established either by histopathological confirmation or by characteristic imaging findings on multiphasic CT or MRI documented in the medical records, demonstrating features diagnostic of HCC according to accepted international diagnostic guidelines (AASLD/EASL criteria) [12,13]. Patients with incomplete medical records or insufficient clinical data were excluded from the analysis.

As this was a retrospective observational study, all eligible cases diagnosed during the study period were included. Therefore, the study sample size was determined by case availability, and no formal sample size calculation was performed.

Data collection and variables

Clinical and pathological data were extracted from electronic medical records, pathology reports, and radiological documentation. The study data set was maintained by the participating tertiary care center and consisted of routinely collected clinical, pathological, and radiological information. Access to and use of this data for research purposes was approved by the Institutional Review Board. The variables collected included patient demographics (age, sex, and nationality), underlying risk factors such as HBV, HCV, NASH, alcohol consumption, and cirrhosis status. HBV and HCV status was determined by retrospective review of laboratory results available in the electronic medical records and was predominantly based on serological testing, including hepatitis B surface antigen (HBsAg) and anti-HCV antibody. Tumor multiplicity was recorded according to the available radiological and/or pathological documentation using the designations of solitary or multiple provided in the source reports.

Comorbid medical conditions including hypertension (HTN), DM, dyslipidemia, ischemic heart disease, and benign prostatic hyperplasia (BPH) were also documented. Tumor-related variables included tumor size, number of lesions, anatomic location, histological growth pattern, tumor grade, vascular invasion, portal vein thrombosis, and presence of metastatic disease.

Histopathological evaluation was performed according to the World Health Organization classification of liver tumors, and tumor grading was assessed using the Edmondson–Steiner grading system [9,10].

Treatment modalities recorded included surgical resection, systemic therapy, transarterial chemoembolization or radioembolization (TACE/TARE), ablation therapies, and palliative care.

Missing data were handled using complete-case analysis. The extent of missing data for each study variable is summarized in Supplementary Table S1. Variables with unavailable information were excluded from the corresponding analyses, and no imputation procedures were performed. Missing data primarily reflected incomplete clinical information in consultation cases referred from external institutions or loss to follow-up at our institution. Given the retrospective nature of the study, the possibility of selection bias related to missing data cannot be excluded.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analyses were performed using IBM SPSS version 27 (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA). Continuous variables were summarized as means with standard deviations (SD), whereas categorical variables were presented as frequencies and percentages.

Pearson’s correlation analysis was used to evaluate the association between tumor size and alpha-fetoprotein (AFP) levels. Statistical significance for the Pearson correlation analysis was assessed using a two-sided test with a significance threshold of P < 0.05. Logistic regression analysis was performed to explore potential predictors of metastatic disease, including demographic variables, viral hepatitis status, metabolic risk factors, and comorbid conditions. A backward stepwise model was applied with entry and removal criteria of 0.05 and 0.10, respectively. Given the exploratory nature of the analysis and the limited number of metastatic events, formal multicollinearity diagnostics were not performed. Accordingly, the regression analysis was considered hypothesis-generating rather than confirmatory.

This study was conducted and reported in accordance with the Strengthening the Reporting of Observational Studies in Epidemiology (STROBE) guidelines, and a completed STROBE checklist is provided as supplementary material.

Results

Patient characteristics

A total of 44 patients diagnosed with HCC were included in the study. The mean age at diagnosis was 65.8 years (standard deviation [SD] = 15.9). The cohort demonstrated a marked male predominance, with 77.3% (n = 34) of patients being male and 22.7% (n = 10) female. Most patients were Saudi nationals (63.6%), while 36.4% were non-Saudi.

Regarding underlying risk factors, HCV infection was identified in 15.9% (n = 7) of patients, and HBV infection in 6.8% (n = 3). Cirrhosis was documented in 31.8% (n = 14) of cases. Notably, 45.5% (n = 20) of patients tested negative for viral hepatitis, suggesting the possible contribution of nonviral etiologies. Data on risk factors were unavailable for 29.5% (n = 13) of patients. The etiologic distribution of HCC, including the substantial proportion of viral-negative cases, is illustrated in Figure 1.

Tumor characteristics and histopathological findings

The mean maximum tumor diameter was 8.75 cm (SD = 6.0). Tumors were documented as solitary in 31.8% (n = 14) of patients and as multiple in 25.0% (n = 11). Tumor multiplicity could not be determined from the available radiological and/or pathological documentation in the remaining 43.2% (n = 19) of cases. The right hepatic lobe was the most frequently affected site (54.5%, n = 24), followed by the left lobe.

Evidence of vascular invasion was identified in 25.0% (n = 11) of patients, while portal vein thrombosis was observed in 22.7% (n = 10). Metastatic disease at diagnosis was present in 22.7% (n = 10) of cases.

Histopathological evaluation demonstrated that the trabecular growth pattern was the predominant architectural pattern, observed in 63.6% (n = 28) of tumors. Other patterns included pseudoacinar (4.5%) and solid (2.3%) patterns. Tumor grading revealed that moderately differentiated tumors (Grade 2) were most common (50%, n = 22), followed by well-differentiated tumors (Grade 1) (22.7%, n = 10) and poorly differentiated tumors (Grade 3) (9.1%, n = 4). Treatment modalities

Regarding treatment strategies, palliative care was the most frequently documented management approach, recorded in 29.5% (n = 13) of patients. Systemic therapy, including targeted therapy or immunotherapy, was administered in 13.6% (n = 6) of cases, while surgical resection was performed in 13.6% (n = 6). Detailed information regarding the specific systemic agents, lines of therapy, and sequential treatment strategies was not consistently available in the medical records and therefore could not be reliably analyzed.

Locoregional therapies were less frequently documented, with transarterial chemoembolization or radioembolization (TACE/TARE) performed in 9.1% (n = 4) of patients and ablation therapies performed in 6.8% (n = 3). These treatment patterns are summarized in Table 4.

Exploratory correlation and regression analysis

Pearson’s correlation analysis demonstrated no statistically significant association between tumor size and alpha-fetoprotein (AFP) levels (r = -0.046, P = 0.821).

Logistic regression analysis was performed to explore potential predictors of metastatic disease, including age, sex, viral hepatitis status, metabolic risk factors, and comorbid conditions. No statistically significant associations were identified for any of the variables evaluated (all P > 0.05).

These analyses should be interpreted with considerable caution given the small sample size, limited number of metastatic events, and missing data for several clinical variables. Consequently, the regression analyses should be regarded as exploratory and hypothesis-generating rather than confirmatory.

Discussion

This retrospective cohort study provides clinically relevant insights into the epidemiologic and clinicopathological characteristics of HCC diagnosed at a tertiary referral center in Saudi Arabia. The findings highlight several important features related to patient risk factors, tumor characteristics, and patterns of disease presentation within a real-world clinical setting. Integrating clinical and pathological observations offers a comprehensive perspective on the current burden of HCC and underscores the ongoing challenges associated with late-stage diagnosis and limited eligibility for curative treatment among many patients.

Interpretation of key findings

The mean age at diagnosis in this cohort was approximately 66 years, which is consistent with international reports indicating that HCC is most commonly diagnosed in older adults [7,14]. The marked male predominance observed in this study (77.3%) also aligns with global epidemiologic patterns [7,14]. Previous research has proposed that gender differences in HCC incidence may be influenced by hormonal factors, variations in exposure to risk factors, and differences in immune response mechanisms [15–17].

Historically, viral hepatitis represented a major driver of HCC in Saudi Arabia, although substantial geographic variation has been documented. In the Jazan region of southwestern Saudi Arabia, for example, early studies demonstrated a particularly high HBV burden, with HBsAg carriage reported in 12.7% of individuals studied in 1985 [18]. Subsequent data demonstrated a marked decline in HBV prevalence, particularly following the introduction of universal HBV vaccination, with HBsAg positivity among Saudi blood donors in Jazan decreasing from 5.6% in 2004 to 2.3% in 2009 [19]. At the national level, childhood HBV prevalence similarly declined from approximately 7% in 1989 to 0.3% by 1997 [20].

Of particular relevance to the present study, an earlier HCC cohort from the same tertiary center in Jeddah, including 128 patients diagnosed between 2008 and 2014, reported HCV and HBV infection in 33.6% and 24.2% of cases, respectively [21]. In comparison, HCV and HBV were documented in 15.9% and 6.8% of the present cohort, respectively, while 45.5% were documented as viral-negative. Although the small sample size, incomplete etiologic data, and retrospective design of the present study preclude inference of a temporal trend, this descriptive contrast is consistent with broader evidence of a changing etiologic profile of HCC in Saudi Arabia.

Geographic heterogeneity should also be considered when interpreting HCC epidemiology within Saudi Arabia. The Kingdom encompasses geographically and demographically diverse regions, and the distribution of viral hepatitis and other HCC risk factors may vary between western and southwestern regions along the Red Sea coast and other parts of the country, including the Eastern Province. Accordingly, findings from the present single-center cohort in Jeddah should not be assumed to represent the epidemiologic profile of HCC across Saudi Arabia as a whole.

The nationality composition of the cohort should also be considered when interpreting these findings. Non-Saudi patients accounted for 36.4% of the studied population. Jeddah is a major cosmopolitan city with a substantial expatriate population, many of whom reside in Saudi Arabia for extended periods and form part of the population served by local healthcare institutions. Accordingly, inclusion of non-Saudi residents reflects the demographic diversity of the population encountered at this tertiary referral center. Nevertheless, nationality-specific differences in underlying risk factors and prior exposures cannot be excluded, and the findings should therefore not be interpreted as representing epidemiologic trends among Saudi nationals alone.

Given the high prevalence of obesity and DM in Saudi Arabia and the growing burden of MASLD, metabolic risk factors warrant increasing consideration in the epidemiology of HCC. Recent national analyses have identified metabolic liver disease as an emerging contributor to HCC burden, highlighting the importance of expanding surveillance and risk stratification strategies beyond traditional viral hepatitis-associated disease [4,22–25].

Cirrhosis was documented in 31.8% of the present cohort, a relatively low proportion for an HCC population [26]. This finding should be interpreted cautiously, as cirrhosis status was retrospectively extracted from available clinical, radiological, and pathological documentation and was not uniformly established by histological assessment of the background liver. Therefore, the absence of documented cirrhosis in the remaining cases should not be interpreted as confirmation of a noncirrhotic background. Similarly, although metabolic comorbidities were common, NASH was specifically documented in only two patients (4.5%). Consequently, the substantial proportion of viral-negative HCC in this cohort cannot be assumed to represent NASH- or MASLD-associated HCC, and the underlying etiology of a proportion of these tumors remains uncertain.

Tumor characteristics and pathological findings

Histopathological evaluation in this cohort demonstrated that the trabecular growth pattern was the predominant architectural pattern, consistent with previously reported morphological features of HCC [10,27,28]. Tumor grading revealed that moderately differentiated tumors (Grade 2) were the most frequent in this series. From an epidemiological perspective, tumor differentiation and architectural patterns provide important information regarding tumor biology and may influence clinical decision-making and prognosis.

The apparently low frequencies of glypican-3 and HepPar-1 positivity in the present cohort should be interpreted cautiously. Immunohistochemical markers were used selectively as part of routine diagnostic evaluation and were not systematically applied to all HCC cases. Accordingly, the reported frequencies represent the proportion of positive cases within the entire study cohort rather than marker-specific positivity rates among tested tumors and therefore cannot be directly compared with studies in which these markers were uniformly assessed. For example, a regional Egyptian study in which both markers were systematically evaluated reported glypican-3 and HepPar-1 positivity in 97% and 93% of HCC cases, respectively [29]. Thus, the apparent discrepancy is likely attributable, at least in part, to differences in the denominator and the selective use of immunohistochemistry in the present retrospective cohort.

The presence of vascular invasion in approximately one quarter of patients is particularly noteworthy, as vascular invasion is widely recognized as an adverse prognostic factor associated with increased risk of tumor progression and recurrence [13,30]. Similarly, portal vein thrombosis was identified in more than one fifth of cases, further supporting the observation that many patients in this cohort presented with advanced-stage disease [13]. These findings underscore the importance of early detection strategies aimed at identifying HCC at stages when curative treatment options are still feasible.

Treatment patterns and clinical implications

The treatment patterns observed in this cohort further illustrate the challenges associated with late diagnosis of HCC. Palliative care was the most frequently documented management approach, reflecting the substantial proportion of patients presenting with advanced-stage disease. This observation is consistent with previous reports indicating that many patients with HCC are diagnosed at stages when curative treatment options such as surgical resection or liver transplantation are no longer possible [7,8,13]. This pattern is consistent with recent Saudi reports demonstrating that a substantial proportion of patients are managed with systemic therapies because of advanced disease at presentation [31]. Collectively, these findings underscore the ongoing challenge of delayed diagnosis and reinforce the need for earlier detection through effective surveillance programs.

Locoregional therapies, including transarterial chemoembolization or radioembolization, were documented in a relatively small proportion of patients. Although these interventions play an established role in the management of intermediate-stage HCC, their limited use in this cohort likely reflects delayed presentation rather than treatment selection preferences [13,32]. Similarly, ablation therapies were infrequently documented, further emphasizing the importance of strengthening surveillance strategies to facilitate earlier diagnosis and expand eligibility for potentially curative interventions [32].

The systemic treatment landscape for advanced HCC has evolved substantially during the study period. Historically, sorafenib represented the principal first-line systemic therapy, while regorafenib subsequently emerged as a second-line option for selected patients with disease progression after sorafenib, demonstrating an overall survival benefit in the phase III RESORCE trial [33]. More recently, additional tyrosine kinase inhibitors and immunotherapy-based regimens have expanded systemic treatment options, with increasing emphasis on sequential treatment strategies. Contemporary real-world studies have further demonstrated the feasibility and clinical effectiveness of these approaches in routine practice. However, detailed information regarding individual systemic agents and treatment sequences was not consistently available in the present cohort, precluding meaningful evaluation of specific systemic treatment patterns or their outcomes.

Public health implications

The findings of this study have important implications for public health and healthcare planning in Saudi Arabia. Recent population-based estimates have further demonstrated that HCC remains an important healthcare burden in Saudi Arabia, with disease prevalence increasing markedly with age and occurring more frequently in males [27]. These national observations are consistent with the demographic profile observed in the present cohort and support the need for targeted preventive and surveillance strategies in high-risk populations. The relatively high proportion of patients presenting with advanced tumor burden underscores the need to strengthen surveillance programs targeting individuals at increased risk for HCC. Current international guidelines recommend regular screening for individuals with cirrhosis or chronic viral hepatitis using imaging and serum biomarkers [26,31]. However, adherence to surveillance protocols remains variable in many healthcare settings.

As metabolic risk factors become increasingly prevalent, further refinement of risk-stratified surveillance strategies for individuals with metabolic liver disease may become increasingly important [4,28–30]. In addition, improving access to multidisciplinary care, including specialized hepatology, oncology, and interventional radiology services, may help facilitate earlier diagnosis and improve treatment eligibility. Emerging systemic therapies, including immune checkpoint inhibitors (ICIs) and combination therapeutic regimens, have shown promising clinical outcomes in selected patients with advanced HCC [34].

Taken together, these findings extend the existing Saudi literature on HCC by providing contemporary data up to 2023 and integrating clinical, pathological, immunohistochemical, and treatment-related characteristics within a single tertiary referral center cohort. Given the limited availability of contemporary Saudi datasets comprehensively characterizing HCC over an extended study period, the present study helps address an important knowledge gap by providing real-world insight into evolving disease patterns, risk factor profiles, and treatment practices within the region.

The observed predominance of viral-negative HCC, coupled with a substantial burden of metabolic comorbidities, is consistent with emerging national evidence suggesting a gradual shift in HCC epidemiology from predominantly viral etiologies toward increasing contributions from metabolic liver disease. Furthermore, the frequent presentation with advanced disease features, including vascular invasion and portal vein thrombosis, mirrors broader national concerns regarding delayed diagnosis and limited eligibility for curative therapies. Although limited by sample size, these findings contribute additional real-world evidence supporting the need for enhanced surveillance, earlier detection, and continued adaptation of HCC prevention and management strategies within Saudi Arabia.

Study limitations

Several limitations should be considered when interpreting the findings of this study. First, the retrospective design and single-center setting may limit the generalizability of the results to the broader Saudi population, particularly given the potential geographic and demographic heterogeneity in HCC risk factors across different regions of the Kingdom and the diverse nationality composition of the study population. Second, the relatively small sample size reduced the statistical power to detect significant associations between clinical variables and metastatic disease, and the analysis should therefore be interpreted as exploratory. Furthermore, the limited number of metastatic cases relative to the number of candidate predictor variables restricted the robustness of the multivariable analyses and may have increased the risk of model overfitting. Formal multicollinearity diagnostics were not performed, representing an additional limitation of the exploratory regression analysis. Third, missing data for certain variables, including risk factors and treatment information, may have influenced the completeness of the dataset. In particular, detailed information regarding specific systemic agents, lines of therapy, and treatment sequences was not consistently documented, precluding a more detailed assessment of systemic treatment patterns. In addition, survival outcomes and comprehensive tumor staging information were not consistently available. Key clinical variables required for accurate staging, particularly performance status and liver function assessment, were incompletely documented in the medical records. Consequently, stage-specific analyses and evaluation of treatment allocation according to established management algorithms could not be performed, and the ability to assess long-term prognosis and treatment effectiveness was limited.

Despite these limitations, the study provides valuable real-world insights from a tertiary referral center managing patients with HCC within the region. Future multicenter studies with larger cohorts and prospective follow-up are needed to further characterize disease patterns and evaluate clinical outcomes in the Saudi population.

Conclusion

This study provides a comprehensive overview of the clinical and pathological characteristics of HCC diagnosed at a tertiary care center in Saudi Arabia over a 10-year period. The findings indicate that many patients presented with substantial tumor burden and advanced-stage disease at the time of diagnosis, which limited the availability of potentially curative treatment options.

A considerable proportion of patients lacked identifiable viral risk factors, while metabolic comorbidities were common within the cohort. These exploratory observations are consistent with, but do not establish, an evolving contribution of nonviral and metabolic risk factors to the epidemiologic profile of HCC in the region. These findings contribute contemporary real-world data to the limited literature describing the evolving clinicopathological profile of HCC in Saudi Arabia and provide additional insight into changing disease patterns within the region. While these observations should be interpreted cautiously because of the small sample size and retrospective single-center design, they may reflect evolving epidemiologic patterns of HCC in the region and support the need for larger multicenter studies to better define disease trends and optimize surveillance strategies for high-risk populations.

Improving early detection through structured screening programs and timely referral to specialized care may help increase the proportion of patients eligible for curative therapies and improve clinical outcomes in Saudi Arabia.
