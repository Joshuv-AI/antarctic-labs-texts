Macrophage membrane-functionalized manganese dioxide nanomedicine for synergistic treatment of atherosclerosis by mitigating inflammatory storms and promoting cholesterol efflux
Authors: Sijin Chen, Wenli Zhang, Chun Tang, Xiyue Rong, Yun Liu, Ying Luo, Lian Xu, Zhongsheng Xu, Junrui Wang, Yi Wang, Qianying Du, Bo Liu, Yu Zhang, Jia Liu, Dajing Guo
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractAtherosclerosis (AS) poses a significant threat to human life and health. However, conventional antiatherogenic medications exhibit insufficient targeting precision and restricted therapeutic effectiveness. Moreover, during the progression of AS, macrophages undergo polarization toward the proinflammatory M1 phenotype and generate reactive oxygen species (ROS) to accelerate the occurrence of inflammatory storms, and ingest excess lipids to form foam cells by inhibiting cholesterol efflux. In our study, we developed a macrophage membrane-functionalized hollow mesoporous manganese dioxide nanomedicine (Col@HMnO2-MM). This nanomedicine has the ability to evade immune cell phagocytosis, enables prolonged circulation within the body, targets the inflammatory site of AS for effective drug release, and alleviates the inflammatory storm at the AS site by eliminating ROS. Furthermore, Col@HMnO2-MM has the ability to generate oxygen autonomously by breaking down surplus hydrogen peroxide generated at the inflammatory AS site, thereby reducing the hypoxic microenvironment of the plaque by downregulating hypoxia-inducible factor (HIF-1α), which in turn enhances cholesterol efflux to inhibit foam cell formation. In an APOE−/− mouse model, Col@HMnO2-MM significantly reduced inflammatory factor levels, lipid storage, and plaque formation without significant long-term toxicity. In summary, this synergistic treatment significantly improved the effectiveness of nanomedicine and may offer a novel strategy for precise AS therapy.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12951-024-02939-x.Keywords: Atherosclerosis, Macrophage membranes, Hollow mesoporous manganese dioxide, Inflammatory storm, Cholesterol efflux

Introduction

Atherosclerosis (AS) is a prevalent and serious cardiovascular health issue [1–5]. As lipids build up and plaques develop, the strain on the circulatory system grows, leading to a greater risk of vessel constriction and increased vulnerability of plaques [6]. Once a vulnerable plaque forms, it may result in a chain of irreversible complications, including myocardial infarction and heart failure, significantly jeopardizing patients’ health and lives [7, 8]. Consequently, managing AS has consistently remained a key concern in clinical practice.

Studies have indicated that macrophages are crucial in the development of AS as they polarize, produce inflammatory factors and accumulate lipids [9]. Monocytes are recruited to atherosclerotic sites and transform into proinflammatory M1-type macrophages, which are known for generating reactive oxygen species (ROS) [10, 11]. Excessive ROS production at the inflamed site initiates an inflammatory chain reaction through lipid peroxidation [12]. Consequently, research efforts have aimed to mitigate AS by lowering ROS levels and suppressing the release of inflammatory factors [13, 14]. In addition, internalized lipids, such as ATP-binding cassette transporter A1 (ABCA1) and G1 (ABCG1), are normally removed from cells by transporters; however, hypoxia at the AS site leads to the upregulation of hypoxia-inducing factor 1α (HIF-1α), which can reduce the ability of ABCA1 or ABCG1 to inhibit cholesterol transport and increase lipid accumulation at the AS site [15–18]. The increased lipid accumulation led to dysregulation of lipid metabolism, which exacerbated the transformation of M1 macrophages and impaired their key immune functions. Macrophages with lipid overload tend to transform into foam cells with reduced migration capacity. Many foam cells accumulate and undergo apoptosis, forming the necrotic core of a vulnerable plaque [19–22]. Thus, some studies have also attempted to treat AS by regulating cholesterol efflux [23–25].

However, the mechanism of atherosclerosis is a dynamic process. Inflammation and lipid imbalance are inextricably linked in the pathological microenvironment of AS [26]. In recent years, treatments for plaques have rarely regulated the inflammatory microenvironment of plaques as well as cholesterol efflux. In addition, traditional monotherapies for AS (e.g., statins and antioxidants) have weak plaque-targeting ability, strong hydrophobicity, great side effects and limited therapeutic effect [27, 28], which hinders their further application in medicine to a certain extent. Plaques at different stages of development can be found in individual AS patients [29, 30]. Therefore, compared with a single treatment strategy, a synergistic treatment strategy can help us achieve the desired AS treatment effect. In addition, to combat the chronic disease of AS, most treatments usually require long-term treatment, thus there is a special focus on plaque- targeting therapies with reduced side effects.

The traditional therapeutic medicine colchicine (Col) is widely used as an anti-inflammatory alkaloid medicine, and several large-scale clinical trials have recently reported that Col can improve survival in patients with cardiovascular disease (CVD) by approximately 26–29% by reducing the risk of ischemic stroke and myocardial infarction [31–33]. However, the narrow therapeutic range and lack of targeting of Col have hindered its clinical use [34–37]. Therefore, we need to explore a drug carrier that can be loaded with Col to achieve drug targeting and synergistically enhance the therapeutic effect of AS. To this end, an increasing number of studies have shown that nanodrug carriers have the potential to be used as precision treatments for AS [38]. Currently, a series of metal nanozymes that are considered as promising candidates due to their long-term stability, low cost, suitability for large-scale production and controlled catalytic activity have been developed to clear ROS in oxidative stress-related diseases [39]. Among them, manganese-based nanozymes, which can simulate the activity of catalase (CAT) and superoxide dismutase (SOD), play important roles. In addition, a series of drugs can be loaded into the empty structure and the hollow structure provides a high surface-area-to-volume ratio, which provides more reaction sites than the corresponding solid structure does, resulting in greater enzyme-like activity [40, 41]. On the basis of the above research, we believed that hollow mesoporous manganese based nanocarriers can not only load Col, but also enhance the synergistic therapeutic effect of drugs due to their CAT effect. In recent years, membrane-coated nanotherapeutics have attracted widespread attention [42]. Among them, nanoparticles coated with a macrophage membrane (MM) can naturally target inflammatory sites, evade immune system phagocytosis, and circulate in the body for a long time [43, 44]. Therefore, the use of manganese-based nanozymes as carriers to load Col and coat them with MM is expected to prevent phagocytosis and endow the drug with a long circulation time and enhanced targeting properties to improve its clinical application.

Based on the above studies, our group carefully designed a nanomedicine (Col@HMnO2-MM) to clear ROS, reprogram macrophages and promote cholesterol efflux for AS treatment. As shown in Scheme 1a, Col@HMnO2-MM uses a hollow mesoporous manganese dioxide (HMnO2) nanozyme as a carrier, which has a hollow mesoporous structure to deliver the anti-inflammatory drug Col and is coated with MM. Owing to the targeting and immune escape abilities of MM, nanomedicine can accumulate rapidly at the AS site and release the drug in the acidic inflammatory microenvironment. In addition, Col@HMnO2-MM has high antioxidant enzyme activity and can effectively eliminate ROS, inhibit the release of inflammatory factors, promote the reprogramming of macrophages from the M1 to the M2 type, and relieve hypoxia to promote cholesterol efflux (Scheme 1b). Consequently, Col@HMnO2-MM may be developed into a targeted drug for the synergistic treatment of AS by mitigating inflammatory storms and promoting cholesterol efflux and may encourage further exploration of nanomedicines for the treatment of various inflammatory cardiovascular diseases.

Materials and methods

Materials

Tetraethyl orthosilicate (TEOS), polyvinylpyrrolidone (PVP, Mw = 10000), polyallylamine hydrochloride (PAH, Mw ≈ 15000), polyacrylic acid (PAA, Mw ≈ 1800), resorcinol (A.R., 99%), formaldehyde solution, sodium carbonate (NaOH), potassium permanganate (KMnO4), ammonia solution, and hexadecyltrimethoxysilane (HDTMS, G.C.≥85%) were purchased from Sino-­pharm Chemical Reagent Co., Ltd. Colchicine was purchased from Chengdu Must Biotechnology Co., Ltd. (Chengdu, China). RAW 264.7 cells were purchased from the Chinese Academy of Science (Shanghai, China). Fetal bovine serum (FBS) was purchased from Inner Mongolia Opcel Biotechnology Co., Ltd. (Inner-­Mongolia, China). Calcein-AM, propidium iodide (PI), DiI, DiR, DHE, 4,6-diamidino-2-phenylindole (DAPI), paraformaldehyde, DCFH-DA, DAF-FM DA and Actin-Tracker Red-Rhodamine were purchased from Beyotime Biotechnology (Shanghai, China). Cholesterol was obtained from Avanti Polar Lipids, Inc. (USA). A Cell Counting Kit-8 (CCK-8) was purchased from Beyotime Institute of Biotechnology (Shanghai, China). CD86, CD206, HIF-1α and ABCG1 antibodies were purchased from Beyotime Biotechnology (Shanghai, China). Integrin α, Integrin β, CD47 and CCR2 antibodies were obtained from AiFang Biological (Hunan, China). ELISA kits for IL-6, IL-1β, TNF-α, IL-18, and CRP were obtained from Jiangsu Sumeike Biological Technology Co., Ltd. (Jiangsu, China).

Synthesis of mesoporous manganese dioxide and the successful coating of macrophage membrane

Preparation of mesoporous manganese dioxide

First, we need to synthesize the silica spheres (SiO2) in the center. The synthesis procedure was derived from an earlier method with several alterations [45]. 98 mL of ethanol and 10 mL of water were mixed together, and then 1.25 mL of ammonia (28%) and 5 mL of TEOS were added. After stirring at room temperature (25 °C) for 20 h, the SiO2 was collected through centrifugation and rinsed multiple times with ethanol and water.

In the second step, we synthesized SiO2@MnO2. 50 mg of SiO2 and 0.5 g of PVP were stirred at room temperature overnight, after which the mixture was washed with 8,000 r/min of centrifugal water three times to obtain the SiO2@PVP product. Then, 25.5 mL of water, 20 mg of metiodephenol, and 28 µL of formaldehyde were added to the obtained product. After thorough mixing, 100 µL of ammonia water (2.8%) was added, the temperature was increased to 60 °C, the mixture was stirred for 2 h, the temperature was increased to 100 °C, the mixture was stirred for 2 h, and the reaction was terminated by cooling and washing with centrifuged water three times to obtain the SiO2@RF product. 5 mL water was added to 10 mg of SiO2@RF, mixed thoroughly, and then 3 mL of KMnO4 (0.2 M) was added. The mixture was stirred at room temperature for 4 h, followed by three washes with centrifuged water to obtain the desired SiO2@MnO2 product.

Finally, we successfully prepared the hollow mesoporous manganese dioxide (HMnO2). To achieve a hollow structure, a mixture of 20 mL of 1 M NaOH and 50 mg of SiO2@MnO2 was stirred overnight at room temperature. The mixture was then centrifuged and washed three times with water to obtain HMnO2. The obtained HMnO2 was dispersed in 5 mL of H2O, 5 mL of PAH solution (5 mg/mL) was added under ultrasonic stirring, the mixture was stirred for 3 h, and the solution was collected. The sample was rinsed with deionized water to yield a colorless and transparent supernatant. The synthesized HMnO2-PAH was slowly added to 5 mL of PAA solution (5 mg/mL) and then subjected to ultrasonic stirring for 3 h, followed by washing and collection for future use.

The successful coating of macrophage membrane

We prepared the macrophage membranes (MM). RAW264.7 cells were inoculated into a culture dish and incubated for 24 h. Next, the culture medium was collected, and the cells were centrifuged to separate the cells and debris. The supernatant was concentrated in a rotating column (Millipore, USA) with an IOkDa molecular weight at 2500 × g and 4 °C for 8 min. The collected liquid was subsequently centrifuged at 800 × g and 4 °C for 40 min, after which the resulting precipitate was resuspended in sterile phosphate-buffered saline (PBS) and stored at -80 °C for subsequent experiments.

Finally, we successfully wrapped the macrophage membrane to obtain the Col@HMnO2-MM. Owing to the modification of PAA/PAH, HMnO2 was stirred with Col at room temperature for 24 h to obtain Col@HMnO2. To prepare the MM coating Col@HMnO2-MM, the mixture of Col@HMnO2 and the MM was extruded through a polycarbonate membrane with 400 nm pores using a liposome extruder, which was repeated 18 times, followed by centrifugation at 13,000 rpm for 10 min to obtain the Col@HMnO2-MM nanomedicine.

Characterization of Col@HMnO2-MM

The morphologies of SiO2, SiO2@RF, SiO2@MnO2, HMnO2, and Col@HMnO2-MM were observed via transmission electron microscopy (TEM) (FEI Tecnai G2 F30, USA). The elemental mapping and EDX spectrum of Col@HMnO2-MM were also examined by TEM. The structure of HMnO2 was investigated by XRD (BRUKER D8 ADVANCE, Germany), XPS (Thermo Scientific K-Alpha), and FTIR (Thermo Scientific Nicolet iS5). The specific surface area and corresponding pore size distribution of HMnO2 were measured by a BET surface area adsorption analyzer (ASAP 2460, USA). The unloaded Col in the supernatant of Col@HMnO2 was measured using a BCA protein assay kit, and the drug loading and encapsulation efficiency were calculated as follows: encapsulation efficiency = (WTotal added Col -WUnloaded Col) / WTotal added Col × 100%; loading efficiency = (WTotal added Col -WUnloaded Col) / (WLoaded Col + WTotal added HMnO2) × 100%. A dynamic light scattering (DLS) laser particle size analyzer (Zetasizer Nano ZS90, UK) was used to analyze the size and zeta potential of MM, Col@HMnO2, and Col@HMnO2-MM, and to observe the size stability of Col@HMnO2-MM at 1, 3, 5 and 7 d. The protein profiles of HMnO2, MM and Col@HMnO2-MM were validated via western blot analysis.

Release property of col

To assess the drug release pattern of Col, 1 mL of either Col@HMnO2-MM (1 mg/mL) or Col@HMnO2 (2 mg/mL) was placed into dialysis bags with a molecular weight cutoff of 3500 Da. These dialysis bags were immersed in 50 mL of PBS at either pH 7.4 or 5.5 and maintained at 37 °C. At specific time intervals, samples of the release medium (500 µL) were obtained. The Col concentration was measured using UV − vis spectroscopy.

ROS scavenging properties of Col@HMnO2-MM in vitro

•O2− scavenging property

The superoxide anion (•O2−) was prepared by reacting xanthine oxidase with xanthine. Then, Col@HMnO2-MM was added and allowed to react for 5 min and 10 min, and the residual •O2− was measured by electron paramagnetic resonance (EPR).

•OH scavenging property

Hydroxyl radicals (•OH) were prepared by a TiO2/UV system under 340 nm ultraviolet light. Then, 30 µg/mL Col@HMnO2-MM was added and allowed to react for 5 min and 10 min, and the residual •OH was measured by EPR.

H2O2 scavenging property

H2O2 (10 mM) was used as the control, the Col@HMnO2-MM sample was added to the reaction mixture for 5 min and 10 min, and hydrogen peroxide clearance was observed through EPR.

O2 concentration monitoring

Col@HMnO2-MM (30 µg/mL) was added to 10 mL of H2O2 (10 mM) solution. Col@HMnO2-MM alone and H2O2 solution alone were used as the control groups. Dissolved oxygen was utilized to measure the O2 levels in the solution for 10 min.

DPPH scavenging property

DPPH solution (0.04 mg/mL) was prepared with anhydrous ethanol. Then, Col@HMnO2-MM was added and allowed to react for 5 min and 10 min, and the residual DPPH was measured by EPR.

Removal of alkyl and alkoxy radicals

Alkyl radicals and alkyl radicals were prepared via a PAA/UV system. Then, Col@HMnO2-MM was added and allowed to react for 5 min and 10 min, and the residual alkyl radicals and alkoxy radicals were measured by EPR.

LPO inhibition assay of Col@HMnO2-MM

400 µL different Col@HMnO2-MM solutions (10, 20, 30, 40, and 50 µg/mL) were mixed with the as-prepared MDA working solution. Next, the samples were heated in boiling water at 95 °C for 50 min. After cooling, the tested solution was centrifuged at 15,000 rpm for 20 min, and the absorbance of the supernatant was measured at 535 nm via a UV − vis spectrophotometer.

Biosafety of Col@HMnO2-MM

We first completed the hemolysis experiment of nanomedicine in vitro. Red blood cells were obtained from fresh blood samples from SD rats by centrifugation (3000 r/min) and washing three times. The erythrocytes were incubated with HMnO2-MM, Col, or Col@HMnO2-MM for 4 h and then centrifuged at 13,500 rpm for 5 min. The absorbance of the supernatant at 540 nm was assessed via a microplate reader. Red blood cells were placed in water as the positive control and in PBS as the negative control. The hemolysis rate was calculated as follows: hemolysis rate% = (experimental group absorbance − negative group absorbance) / (positive group absorbance – negative group absorbance) × 100%.

We investigated the cytotoxicity of nanomedicine. First, we explored the optimal concentration of colchicine [46, 47]. RAW264.7 cells were inoculated in 96-well plates, different concentrations of colchicine (0.5, 1, 2, 4, 8, 16, 32, or 64 µg/mL) were added to the cell cultures for 24 h, and then the CCK8 assay was used to detect cytotoxicity. Similarly, we explored the safety of different doses of Col@HMnO2-MM (10, 20, 30, 40, and 50 µg/mL) on Raw264.7 cells. Various concentrations of nanomedicine were added to the cells for 24 h, and CCK-8 assays were performed to evaluate their cytotoxicity.

Finally, we verified the in vivo biosafety of nanomedicine. HMnO2-MM, Col, and Col@HMnO2-MM were injected into healthy C57BL/6J mice. Three days later, we conducted weight testing on the mice in the various groups. Fresh blood was collected for biochemical and routine blood tests, and then organs and tissues were collected for H&E staining.

Cellular uptake

We tested the phagocytosis of nanomedicine in vitro with activated and un-activated macrophages. We first explored the nanomedicine uptake by un-activated macrophages. RAW 264.7 cells were obtained from the Chongqing Key Laboratory of Ultrasound Molecular Imaging. RAW 264.7 cells (5 × 104) were seeded in confocal dishes and co-incubated for 24 h. DiI-labeled Col@HMnO2 and Col@HMnO2-MM (30 µg/mL) were added, and the cells were cultured for 4 h. The cell nuclei were stained with DAPI for 15 min after fixation with paraformaldehyde. Finally, the cells were observed via CLSM.

Subsequently, we explored the nanomedicine uptake by activated macrophages. RAW264.7 cells (5 × 104) were plated in confocal dishes for 24 h and then treated with LPS (1 µg/mL). DiI-labeled Col@HMnO2 and Col@HMnO2-MM were added, and the cells were cultured for 4 h. The cells were washed with PBS, and the nuclei were stained with DAPI for 15 min after fixation with paraformaldehyde. The cells were subsequently visualized via CLSM. In addition, biological TEM was used to observe Col@HMnO2-MM in the activated macrophages after 0.5 h and 4 h.

In vitro antioxidative protective effects

We used the CCK8 assay to evaluate cell survival. RAW 264.7 macrophages were plated in 96-well plates and treated with HMnO2-MM, free Col or Col@HMnO2-MM for 2 h. For the control groups, the cells were cultured in medium without any treatment. The cells were treated with 400 µM H2O2 for 24 h. For the control, the cells were treated with fresh medium for comparison. CCK-8 assays were performed to evaluate the cell survival rate on the second day.

The mitochondrial membrane potential changes were detected by JC-1. RAW264.7 cells (5 × 104) were seeded in confocal dishes and incubated with HMnO2-MM, free Col or Col@HMnO2-MM for 2 h. Next, the cells were exposed to H2O2 (400 µM) for a period of 24 h. A JC-1 detection kit was used to identify alterations in the mitochondrial membrane potential.

We also evaluated the apoptosis detection by flow cytometry. RAW 264.7 cells were plated at a density of 1 × 106 cells per well in six-well plates and incubated overnight for adhesion. HMnO2-MM, Col, and Col@HMnO2-MM were added to the wells for a 2 h pretreatment. The cells were subsequently stimulated with H2O2 (at a final concentration of 400 µM) for 24 h. Flow cytometry was used to detect macrophage apoptosis.

Intracellular ROS scavenging activity

RAW 264.7 macrophages were cultured in confocal dishes and treated with HMnO2-MM, free Col or Col@HMnO2-MM for 2 h. For the control groups, the cells were cultured in medium without any treatment. Then, the cells were treated with 1 µg/mL LPS for 24 h, while the negative control was treated with fresh medium again. After washing with PBS, the cells were treated with 10 µM DCFH-DA in serum-free medium for 30 min of incubation. The cells were visualized via CLSM.

In vitro anti‑inflammatory effects

After receiving the same treatment described above, the cells were stimulated with LPS, an ELISA kit was used to detect various inflammatory factors in the culture supernatant, and a BCA kit was used to detect total protein levels.

M1 to M2 polarization of macrophages

For the RT-PCR experiments, RAW 264.7 macrophages were seeded into a 6-well plate and activated with LPS (1 µg/mL) to induce M1 polarization. Afterward, HMnO2-MM, free Col or Col@HMnO2-MM was added, and the mixture was incubated for 24 h. After the cells were collected, total RNA was extracted with TRIzol reagent. The mRNA levels of the macrophages were measured via RT‒PCR. Then, we observed the expression of CD86 and CD206 via CLSM. The cells were subjected to identical treatments and fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde for immunofluorescence staining. The membranes were exposed to the primary antibody and incubated overnight at 4 °C. Secondary antibodies labeled with fluorescent tags were added and incubated for 1 h. The nuclei were costained with DAPI, and CLSM was used for observation and imaging. LPS-stimulated RAW 264.7 cells were cocultured with different nanomedicine treatment groups. We used a FITC-labeled anti-mouse CD86 antibody and an APC- labeled anti-mouse CD206 antibody. The expression of CD86 and CD206 surface proteins was detected via flow cytometry.

Cellular uptake of ox-LDL

Confocal dishes were used to detect the inhibitory effect of Col@HMnO2-MM on the uptake of oxidized low-density lipoprotein (ox-LDL). RAW 264.7 cells were initially exposed to 1 µg/mL LPS for 24 h, followed by a 2-hour incubation with Col@HMnO2-MM. Second, the above cells were cultured with DiI-oxLDL at a concentration of 40 µg/mL for 4 h. Observation of ox-LDL uptake by the cells was performed via CLSM after staining with Hoechst 33,342 for 30 min.

Regulation of HIF-1α, ABCA1 and ABCG1 protein expression

To induce the upregulation of HIF-1α protein expression, the cells were incubated with CoCl2 (100 µM) in serum-free medium for 4 h. Later, the cells were subjected to various treatments for 24 h, and protein expression was detected via CLSM, RT-PCR and Western blotting. To detect the expression of ABCA1, the cells were subjected to different treatments. Following the extraction of total RNA via TRIzol reagent, the relative expression of ABCA1 mRNA was determined via RT-PCR. To detect the expression of ABCG1, the cells were subjected to different treatments. The results were visualized using CLSM. After the qPCR and immunofluorescence results were obtained, we used the same treatment methods to verify the expression of the ABCA1 and ABCG1 proteins via Western blotting.

Cholesterol efflux assay

We utilized fluorescent NBD-cholesterol to investigate cholesterol efflux from macrophages. After the cells were treated in different ways for 12 h, 1 µg/mL NBD-cholesterol was added to the cell culture dishes. Then, the cells were washed with PBS and cultured for an additional 6 h. The supernatant of the medium was collected and centrifuged (14000 rpm, 6 min) to remove debris, while the cells were lysed with sodium hydroxide (1 mL, 0.05 M). The concentration of NBD-cholesterol was detected by a fluorescence enzyme spectrometer at Ex = 500 nm and Em = 535 nm, and the percentage of cholesterol efflux was calculated.

Oil red O staining for the formation of foam cells

After the cells were treated with 30 µg/mL ox-LDL, the prepared nanomedicine was added to the different groups and incubated for 24 h. The cells were subsequently fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde for 25 min, followed by staining with oil red O for 15 min. Then, the cells were washed with isopropyl alcohol once and washed with water twice before they were stained with hematoxylin for 1 min. After being washed with water, lipid droplets in the cells were observed under a light microscope.

Establishment of the APOE−/− mouse AS model

All animal procedures and protocols were approved by the Experimental Animal Ethics Committee of the Second Affiliated Hospital of Chongqing Medical University. The experiments were performed according to the National Regulations on the Use of Experimental Animals. Male C57BL/6 mice (6–8 weeks) and male apolipoprotein E-deficient (ApoE−/−) mice (approximately 6–8 weeks old) were provided by Ensville Biotechnology Co., Ltd (Chengdu, China). APOE−/− mice were fed a high-fat diet for 13 weeks to establish the atherosclerosis model.

Distribution and pharmacokinetic studies in vivo

Preparation of DiR-labeled nanomedicine

6 µL DiR (10 mg/mL) was added to 0.6 mg/mL Col@HMnO2-MM solutions (20 mL), which were stirred completely at room temperature for 2 h, centrifuged (10000 r *8 min), washed twice, and finally dispersed in PBS [48, 49].

Biological targeting of DiR-labeled nanomedicine in vivo

DiR-labeled Col@HMnO2-MM were injected into the tail veins of AS model mice (3 mg/kg), and after different cycle times (2, 4, and 6 h), the aortas were removed for fluorescence imaging in vivo to evaluate the targeting of the plaque sites in the atherosclerotic mice. Afterward, 6 h was selected as the time point to compare the targeting performance of the nanomedicine in vivo.

Metabolism of DiR-labeled nanomedicine in vivo

Fluorescence imaging was conducted on the heart, liver, spleen, lungs and kidneys at 6 h and 48 h postadministration of the nanomedicine to evaluate their metabolism in the organs. In the pharmacokinetic study, the ocular blood of the mice was injected with DiR-labeled Col@HMnO2-MM through the tail vein and collected at specific time points (2, 4, 6, 8, 10, 12, 24 and 48 h). Blood samples (100 µL) were obtained by static storage and centrifugation (3000 r*10 min) for fluorescence imaging to evaluate the half-life of the nanomedicine Col@HMnO2-MM.

In vivo evaluation of therapeutic effects and study of therapeutic mechanisms

In vivo animal experiments, APOE−/− mice were fed a Western diet to establish an AS model, whereas control mice were fed a normal diet. At the end of the fifth week, the mice in the above model groups were randomly divided into four groups with different treatment methods (the normal saline group, the HMnO2-MM group, the Col group and the Col@HMnO2-MM group), and nanomedicine was injected through the tail vein twice a week. After 8 weeks of treatment, the aorta was excised, and the mice were killed. The aorta was washed with 10% paraformaldehyde for 30 min for follow-up evaluation. The entire aorta was stained with oil red O, and the plaque area was subsequently analyzed with Image-Pro Plus 6.0 software. Aorta sections were fixed in 10% paraformaldehyde and then embedded in 3.5 μm thick paraffin for H&E staining prior to histological examination. The subsequent paraffin sections were evaluated by Masson’s trichrome staining and with CD68, MMP-9 and α-SMA antibodies. Aortic sections were frozen and DHE fluorescent dye was utilized to assess ROS production at the plaque location. To study the polarization of macrophages, aortic sections were incubated with anti-Arg-1 and anti-iNOS antibodies. Serum samples were collected, and the levels of TNF-α, IL-6, CRP, IL-1β and IL-18 were measured by ELISA kits to evaluate the level of serum inflammation. We collected the eye blood of the mice and measured their serum cholesterol levels. During the treatment period, major organs were collected and stained with H&E for biosafety assessment.

Statistical analysis

The data used in the experiments are expressed as the means ± standard deviations. The variances were compared via Student’s t-test and one-way ANOVA. (*p < 0.05, **p < 0.01, ***p < 0.001, and ****p < 0.0001).

Results and discussion

Characterization of Col@HMnO2-MM

The hollow mesopore HMnO2 nanozyme was constructed by using SiO2 as the initial core structure, which was covered with MnO2, and then the inner SiO2 layer was etched with NaOH (Scheme 1a). Transmission electron microscopy (TEM) revealed that SiO2 (Fig. 1a), SiO2@RF (Fig. S1), SiO2@MnO2 (Fig. 1b) and HMnO2 (Fig. 1c) were successfully composited, especially the mesoporous structure of HMnO2. Scanning electron microscopy (SEM) revealed the structures of SiO2, SiO2@MnO2, and HMnO2 (Fig. 1e-g). X-ray diffraction (XRD) analysis of SiO2, SiO2@MnO2 and HMnO2 revealed that HMnO2 was composed of birnessite-type manganese oxide (δ-MnO2, JCPDS card no. 18–0802) (Fig. 1j). X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy (XPS) revealed a strong Mn signal (Fig. 1k, 1 L), which exhibited two characteristic peaks at 642.4 eV and 654.2 eV. The spin–orbit peaks of HMnO2 assigned to Mn (IV) 2p1/2 and Mn (IV) 2p3/2 further reinforced its formation. We further analyzed the valence state of manganese and found that the synthesized hollow mesoporous manganese dioxide had two and four valences (Fig. S2). The surface area of HMnO2 as determined via the Brunauer–Emmett–Teller (BET) method, was 86.3 m2/g, with a mean pore size of approximately 11.7 nm. These results provided strong evidence for the mesoporous structure of HMnO2 and hinted at its potential for drug loading, as illustrated in Fig. S3. Subsequently, Col was encapsulated in the HMnO2 nanozyme carrier to obtain Col@HMnO2 (Fig. 1p). Based on the standard curve generated via a BCA kit (Fig. S4), the percentage of Col in HMnO2 was estimated to be approximately 84.98%, with a drug loading content of approximately 37.77%. Furthermore, to prevent drug leakage, Col@HMnO2 was coated with MM to obtain Col@HMnO2-MM. The successful preparation of Col@HMnO2-MM was confirmed through the TEM images in Fig. 1d and the high-resolution transmission electron microscopy (HRTEM) results in Fig. 1i and S5, which visually demonstrated the presence of the MM coating. Additionally, elemental mapping analysis (Fig. 1m) and energy-dispersive X-ray (EDX) spectroscopy (Fig. S6) validated the elemental ratios of Mn, O, and P in Col@HMnO2-MM, with a specific focus on the phosphorus (P) content in the MM. Dynamic light scattering (DLS) revealed that the mean diameter of the Col@HMnO2 particles was approximately 181.10 ± 4.36 nm, which increased to 211.63 ± 8.71 nm after the addition of the MM coating (307.47 ± 8.15 nm) (Fig. 1n). The zeta potentials of SiO2, SiO2@MnO2, HMnO2, MM and Col@HMnO2-MM were − 37.83 ± 0.42 mV, -35.40 ± 0.61 mV, -34.60 ± 1.14 mV, -16.17 ± 0.55 mV and − 22.87 ± 0.61 mV, respectively (Fig. 1o). The above results showed that coating with MM was successful. Moreover, the particle size and dispersion of SiO2, SiO2@RF, SiO2@MnO2 and Col@HMnO2-MM remained stable for one week (Fig. S7). Integrin α, CD47, integrin β and CCR2 are widely expressed on the surface of MM and play vital roles in the targeting and activation of macrophages in plaques. Herein, western blot analysis revealed that these characteristic protein markers were present in both MM and Col@HMnO2-MM, confirming that Col@HMnO2-MM could replicate the corresponding effects of macrophages in plaques (Fig. 1h). In vitro release experiments were completed at pH 7.4 or 5.5 to prove that Col was slowly released into the inflammatory environment (Fig. 1q). Almost 80% of the Col was released from Col@HMnO2-MM or Col@HMnO2 after 4 h at pH 5.5, whereas only 40% of the Col was released from Col@HMnO2-MM within 4 h at pH 7.4. These results indicated that the Col@HMnO2-MM nanomedicine was successfully synthesized and can be degraded in an atherosclerotic inflammatory acid environment, which is expected to achieve therapeutic effects [50–52].

ROS scavenging efficiency of the Col@HMnO2-MM

Highly reactive molecules known as ROS play a unique role in preserving vascular homeostasis [9–11]. However, excessive production of ROS can damage blood vessels, which are challenging to recover from, ultimately initiating a cascade of inflammatory responses that worsen the progression of AS [53]. Hence, there is a critical need to investigate novel nanomedicines with ROS scavenging capabilities. We tested the ROS scavenging performance of Col@HMnO2-MM, and its general antioxidant performance is shown in Fig. 2a. Among the kinds of ROS, H2O2, ·OH and ·O2− are the main pathological types of free radicals. We first detected these three molecules, as shown in Fig. 2c, d and e, all of which significantly decreased after 5 min and 10 min of treatment with Col@HMnO2-MM. Moreover, analysis of the dissolved oxygen levels revealed that the O2 concentration in the Col@HMnO2-MM + H2O2 reaction system noticeably rose, suggesting that Col@HMnO2-MM facilitated the transformation of H2O2 into O2 (Fig. 2b). Inspired by the above results, we used DPPH (a common free radical compound) to further explore the ability of this nanomedicine to remove reactive oxygen and nitrogen species (RONS). As expected, Col@HMnO2-MM similarly showed high nitrogen scavenging activity (Fig. 2f). Alkyl free radicals (·R) and alkoxy free radicals (·OR) are free radicals that are specific to lipid peroxidation. Therefore, we also confirmed the Col@HMnO2-MM’s capability to remove lipid peroxides. As shown in Fig. 2g h, the levels of ·R and ·OR decreased significantly after 5 min and 10 min of treatment. Similarly, lipid peroxidation (LPO) is closely involved in the oxidative damage process, and various excess ROS-related free radicals can damage blood vessel cells by inducing LPO. Thus, we evaluated the ability of the nanomedicine to clear lipid peroxides. As shown in Fig. 2i, the nanomedicine Col@HMnO2-MM reduced the level of lipid peroxides in a concentration-dependent manner, thereby hindering LPO to some extent. All of the above in vitro results proved that this nanomedicine has exceptional ROS scavenging ability and plays an important role in regulating vascular homeostasis.

Safety evaluation

We tested whether this nanomedicine can cause hemolysis in biological systems. The hemolysis rates in all the experimental groups were markedly lower than those in the positive control group (water) and much lower than 5%, which verified the good blood compatibility of the nanomedicine (Fig. 2j, S8). We studied the optimal concentration of the traditional medicine colchicine. According to the CCK8 results shown in Fig. S9, the survival rate after treatment with 16 µg/mL colchicine was above 80%, however, at 32 µg/mL colchicine, the cell survival rate decreased to approximately 60%. In contrast, as shown in Fig. 2k, when the nanomedicine concentration reached 50 µg/ml, the cell survival rates were still greater than 80%, which indicated that our synthesized nanomedicine was less toxic than traditional therapeutic drugs. We subsequently evaluated the damage caused by different treatments to macrophages. As shown in Fig. S10, live/dead fluorescence staining revealed no significant abnormalities in nanomedicine-treated RAW264.7 cells. An in vivo biosafety evaluation, which included body weight measurements, hematological analysis, blood biochemical analysis, and histological analysis of the main organs via hematoxylin and eosin (H&E) staining, was employed to systemically evaluate the biosafety of Col@HMnO2-MM. Initially, following intravenous injection, there were no notable distinctions in the body weights of the mice among the four groups (normal saline, HMnO2-MM, free Col, and Col@HMnO2-MM), as depicted in Fig. S11a. Additionally, clinical biochemical analysis revealed no differences in the ALT, AST, CREA or urea levels among the four groups, indicating that the biological functions of the liver and kidney were not affected by systemic administration of the nanomedicine (Fig. S11b). Additionally, hematological analysis revealed no significant differences between the treatment groups and the untreated normal group of mice (Fig. S11c). H&E staining histologically confirmed that there were no significant alterations in the major organs (including the heart, liver, spleen, lung, and kidney) of the four groups of mice (Fig. S11d). In summary, the above results confirmed that Col@HMnO2-MM has good biocompatibility and safety both in vivo and in vitro.

Endocytosis of Col@HMnO2-MM by macrophages

To explore whether the MM endows the nanomedicine with immune escape effects and can enhance the targeting effect under inflammatory conditions, Col@HMnO2-MM was labeled with the probe DiI, which emits red fluorescence. In this study, we utilized confocal laser microscopy (CLSM) to examine the uptake and internalization of DiI-Col@HMnO2 and DiI-Col@HMnO2-MM by macrophages under lipopolysaccharide (LPS) stimulation and unstimulated conditions. CLSM revealed the highly efficient uptake and internalization of DiI-Col@HMnO2-MM by macrophages, especially those stimulated with LPS (Fig. 3a and b). Compared with those of DiI-Col@HMnO2, there was significantly greater uptake and internalization of DiI-Col@HMnO2-MM in LPS-activated macrophages. The resting macrophages exhibited significantly lower uptake and internalization of DiI-Col@HMnO2-MM (Fig. S12). The MM coating promoted the uptake and internalization of Col@HMnO2-MM by activated macrophages and reduced the likelihood of phagocytosis by phagocytic cells. To explore the immune escape effects of the nanomedicine, we used LysoTracker Green to specifically label the lysosomes and DiI to label the Col@HMnO2-MM. As shown in Fig. S13, after being taken up and internalized by LPS-activated macrophages, the Col@HMnO2-MM was highly colocalized with lysosomes in the early stage. The intensity of the intracellular lysosomal fluorescence signal decreased with time, which indicated that the lysosomes and nanomedicine subsequently gradually separated. The position of Col@HMnO2-MM in LPS-activated macrophages was more intuitively observed via biological transmission electron microscopy (Bio-TEM). The Col@HMnO2-MM was mainly distributed in the cytoplasmic matrix over time (Fig. 3c). Taken together, these results demonstrated that the nanomedicine has the ability to escape immune cells and target inflammatory sites.

In vitro antioxidant activity of Col@HMnO2-MM

In contrast to normal tissue, AS lesions produce excess ROS, causing irreversible damage to intracellular lipids, carbohydrates, proteins, and DNA [54]. Based on the above analysis, we investigated the cytoprotective effects of Col@HMnO2-MM on the oxidative stress induced by hydrogen peroxide. We used CCK-8 assays to evaluate the effect of hydrogen peroxide on macrophages by determining changes in the cell survival rate. As shown in Fig. 3d, 24 h after the addition of H2O2, cell viability decreased significantly, suggesting that H2O2 could cause macrophage injury. Compared with those in the model group (H2O2), the macrophage survival rates improved after pretreatment with HMnO2-MM, free Col or Col@HMnO2-MM, indicating that the Col@HMnO2-MM could eliminate the damage caused by H2O2. Similarly, flow cytometry was used to detect the ability of Col@HMnO2-MM to protect against apoptosis induced by oxidative stress in macrophages. As shown in Fig. 3e, compared with those in the HMnO2-MM and free Col groups, the proportions of apoptotic and necrotic Col@HMnO2-MM-pretreated RAW264.7 cells were significantly lower. Similar outcomes were achieved through live/dead staining, with live cells colored green with fluorescein (AM) and dead cells colored red with propidium iodide (PI) (Fig. 3f). Changes in the mitochondrial membrane potential (MMP) during apoptosis are widely regarded as a marker of cell death. To assess the effect of Col@HMnO2-MM on the MMP, macrophages were stained with 5,5’,6,6’– tetrachloro–1,1’,3,3’–tetraethylbenzimidazole–carbonyl carbocyanine iodide (JC-1). JC-1 aggregates in the mitochondria of cells with a high MMP to form J-aggregates, leading to the generation of I ntense red fluorescence. Following H2O2 treatment, there were a decrease in red fluorescence intensity and an increase in green fluorescence intensity, suggesting a change in the MMP. However, pretreatment with Col@HMnO2-MM led to significantly weakened green fluorescence, suggesting that Col@HMnO2-MM could prevent alterations in the MMP and thus inhibit apoptosis (Fig. 3g). As shown in Fig. 3h, untreated macrophages presented a greater ratio of red to green fluorescence intensity, whereas H2O2 stimulation reduced this ratio. Notably, after pretreatment with the nanomedicine, the ratio increased again. Overall, Col@HMnO2-MM shielded macrophages from oxidative stress-induced damage triggered by hydrogen peroxide.

ROS elimination, anti‑inflammatory activity and M1‑to‑M2 macrophage reprogramming activity of Col@HMnO2-MM

We investigated the mechanism through which Col@HMnO2-MM suppressed ROS generation in cells. After 12 h of stimulation, the cells in the model group presented a significantly elevated level of ROS. On the other hand, following co-incubation of activated macrophages with HMnO2-MM, free Col or Col@HMnO2-MM for 4 h, there was a notable decrease in the intensity of intracellular green fluorescence (from DCFH-DA), which was particularly evident in the Col@HMnO2-MM group. This indicated a decrease in total intracellular ROS levels (Fig. 4a and b, S14). Consistent with the above results, after staining with the H2O2 probe, which produces a green fluorescence signal, DHE (red) and DAF-FM DA (green), Col@HMnO2-MM minimized the levels of H2O2, ·O2− and ·NO in the cells (Fig. 4a, c and d, S13). Additionally, quantitative analysis of total intracellular ROS levels via flow cytometry confirmed that treatment with Col@HMnO2-MM effectively inhibited ROS production in RAW264.7 cells (Fig. 4e). These results confirmed that Col@HMnO2-MM can protect macrophages from damage by clearing intracellular ROS.

The intracellular overproduction of ROS is known to activate a series of signal transduction cascades that induce inflammation and aggravate the formation of AS [55, 56]. The Col@HMnO2-MM showed favorable ROS scavenging activity. We investigated whether this scavenging activity can suppress inflammation. The expression of IL-1β (Fig. 4f), IL-6 (Fig. 4g), TNF-α (Fig. 4h) and other inflammatory factors in LPS-treated macrophages was significantly upregulated, indicating that macrophage polarization toward the M1 proinflammatory phenotype had occurred. Preincubation with HMnO2-MM or Col partially inhibited the expression of these cytokines, but notably, treatment with Col@HMnO2-MM resulted in significant suppression of these cytokines, indicating the potent anti-inflammatory properties of the nanomedicine. Therefore, compared with the Col group and the HMnO2-MM group, the Col@HMnO2-MM group effectively decreased intracellular ROS levels and subsequently attenuated inflammation in macrophages.

During the development of AS, two main types of macrophages are M1-type proinflammatory macrophages and M2-type anti-inflammatory macrophages [12]. In atherosclerotic lesions, macrophages are predominantly polarized toward the M1 phenotype. One potential therapeutic approach for managing AS involves reprogramming M1-type macrophages into M2-type macrophages [11]. As ROS and cytokines are crucial in this process, a growing number of antioxidant and anti-inflammatory nanomedicines have been designed to facilitate the transformation of M1 to M2 macrophages. Given the ROS scavenging and anti-inflammatory abilities of the Col@HMnO2-MM, we explored its ability to reprogram macrophages. For this purpose, we first analyzed the mRNA levels of markers associated with type M1 and type M2 macrophages (Fig. S15). After LPS treatment, the expression of M1-type macrophage markers (such as iNOS) was significantly upregulated, while the expression of M2-type macrophage markers (i.e., CD206 and Arg-1) was relatively reduced, demonstrating that we successfully induced M1-type macrophage polarization. After preincubation with HMnO2-MM or free Col, M1 marker expression decreased to some extent, and M2 marker expression increased. Notably, compared with the HMnO2-MM group and the Col group, the Col@HMnO2-MM group exhibited a better therapeutic effect on M1 to M2 macrophage reprogramming. Encouraged by the above results, we used CLSM to observe the morphological changes in the macrophages. After treatment with the Col@HMnO2-MM nanomedicine, the characteristic polyantennal structure of M1-type macrophages disappeared (Fig. 4i). To verify this effect, we performed immunofluorescence staining and flow cytometry analysis to identify surface biomarkers of macrophage phenotype. As shown in Fig. S16, Raw 264.7 cells treated with both LPS and nanomedicine presented weaker CD86 protein (M1 macrophage surface marker) and significantly greater CD206 protein expression (M2 macrophage surface marker) than cells treated with LPS alone. The above results were also similar to the data detected by flow cytometry (Fig. 4j and k), where after treatment with different groups, the proportion of M1 macrophages decreased from 59.79 to 11.61% and the proportion of M2-type macrophages increased from 2.49 to 59.9%. In brief, Col@HMnO2-MM can effectively induce M1-to-M2 macrophage reprogramming for AS therapy.

In vitro inhibition of the cellular uptake of oxidized low-density lipoprotein (ox-LDL) and promotion of cholesterol transport by Col@HMnO2-MM

The ability of macrophages to take up ox-LDL and the foam cells formed by dysregulated cholesterol transport are closely related to the formation of plaques [17]. Therefore, we investigated whether the nanomedicine released under acidic conditions could effectively promote cholesterol transport pathways (Fig. 5a). Initially, we explored whether macrophages could take up ox-LDL, as shown in Fig. S17. Over time, the phagocytosis of ox-LDL increased, and the phagocytosis was essentially complete at 4 h. Our research subsequently investigated the inhibitory effect of nanomedicine on the uptake of ox-LDL by macrophages [57, 58]. After coincubation with DiI-labeled ox-LDL (DiI-ox-LDL) for 4 h, as shown in Fig. 5b and Fig. S18, untreated activated macrophages (model group) presented more prominent red fluorescence signals, whereas the fluorescence intensities in the HMnO2-MM, Col and Col@HMnO2-MM groups notably decreased, particularly those in the Col@HMnO2-MM group. These findings suggested that the nanomedicine could effectively decrease the cellular uptake of ox-LDL.

Normally, lipids are internalized by macrophages and hydrolyzed in lysosomes to generate free cholesterol [59]. After some cholesterol is converted into cholesterol esters, the remaining cholesterol combines with transporters such as ABCA1 for expulsion from the cell to guide lipid metabolism [60]. Nevertheless, owing to extreme hypoxia at the atherosclerotic site, more lipids accumulate in abnormally aggregated macrophages than in the normal vasculature. This is mainly due to the significant upregulation of HIF-1α [61]. The aberrant upregulation of HIF-1α can counteract the impact of cholesterol on macrophages through the inhibition of the cholesterol transporters ABCA1 and ABCG1, leading to increased foam cell formation [62]. The carrier HMnO2 facilitates the breakdown of H2O2 to produce O2, thereby alleviating the hypoxic conditions in the atherosclerotic region. To test these hypotheses, macrophages were treated with CoCl2 to induce hypoxia, which regulated the protein expression level of HIF-1α in the cells. As expected, effective downregulation of HIF-1α expression was observed in the groups pretreated with HMnO2 and Col@HMnO2-MM, showing that both the HMnO2 group and the Col@HMnO2-MM group can improve the anoxic microenvironment in the plaque site by producing oxygen (Fig. 5e). The immunofluorescence staining (Fig. 5c) and qPCR results (Fig. 5d) revealed the same trend. After verifying the inhibition of HIF-1α protein expression, we further investigated the regulatory effect of the nanomedicine on the transporters ABCA1 and ABCG1. We detected the expression of ABCA1 and ABCG1 by immunofluorescence staining and mRNA respectively, and found that both the HMnO2 group and the Col@HMnO2-MM group demonstrated a significant increase in the expression of the transporters ABCG1 and ABCA1 (Fig. 5f, 5 g). We subsequently used Western blotting to verify the above results, as shown in the Fig. 5h and Fig. S19. This effect was attributed mainly to the inhibitory effects on the protein expression of HIF-1α, suggesting that the nanomedicine enhanced cholesterol transport. Thus, we verified the roles of ABCA1 and ABCG1, which are important cholesterol efflux pumps that can effectively reduce the excessive accumulation of lipids in cells.

Because the ATP transporters ABCA1 and ABCG1 were upregulated, we next investigated the cholesterol transport pathway by detecting high-density lipoprotein (HDL)-mediated cholesterol efflux. We utilized CLSM to visually examine intracellular residual cholesterol. The CLSM results revealed that the intracellular cholesterol retention (green) in the Col@HMnO2-MM group was the lowest. We also observed that the remaining intracellular cholesterol content in the free Col group was relatively low, which may be due to its anti-inflammatory effect resulting in a reduced inflow of lipids (Fig. 5i). The fluorescence enzyme spectrometer yielded identical results. As shown in Fig. 5j, compared with that in the untreated group, the HDL-mediated cholesterol outflow in the HMnO2-MM and free Col groups was slightly greater, while that in the Col@HMnO2-MM treatment group was significantly greater due to synergistic effects. As a result, Col@HMnO2-MM could decrease lipid buildup in macrophages.

We subsequently investigated whether Col@HMnO2-MM can inhibit foam cells by reducing LDL uptake while increasing cholesterol efflux. The macrophages were pretreated with different nanomedicines and then incubated with ox-LDL (60 µg/mL) for 24 h, after which they were stained with Oil Red O (ORO). Abundant lipid droplets were observed in the cells in the model group, indicating the formation of foam cells (Fig. 5k). In contrast, the Col@HMnO2-MM group showed the most significant inhibition of foam cell formation. The results of the quantitative analysis of ORO intracellular deposition supported the microscopic observations presented in Fig. 5l. In summary, these results suggested that Col@HMnO2-MM can enhance the inhibition of foam cell formation by synergistically reducing the uptake of LDL and increasing the efflux of cholesterol.

Targeting and biodistribution of Col@HMnO2-MM in vivo

Fluorescence imaging (FLI) can be used to visualize the major accumulation sites and metabolism of Col@HMnO2-MM in the body. As shown in Fig. S20a and Fig. S20b, we first demonstrated that the nanomedicine can be used for FLI in vitro. The fluorescence (FL) signal intensities of the Col@HMnO2-MM (marked with DiR) increased with increasing concentration and finally reached equilibrium. These results were verified by quantitative fluorescence analysis. Afterward, we examined the capacity of Col@HMnO2-MM to specifically target plaque sites in apolipoprotein e-deficient (APOE−/−) mice. DiR-labeled Col@HMnO2-MM accumulated in the aorta 2 h after injection via the tail vein and reached a relatively high concentration 6 h after injection (Fig. S21). We selected 6 h as the time point of fluorescence images to observe the targeting difference of nanomedicine more directly (Fig. 6a). As shown in Fig. S22, the quantitative fluorescence intensities of nanomedicine with membranes were approximately two times greater than those of nanomedicine used DiR only. These results might indicate that the Col@HMnO2-MM had the expected plaque-targeting properties, which may be attributed to modification with the MM. We also observed that at 6 h, the nanomedicine had accumulated mainly in the liver and spleen, and there were no notable disparities in the fluorescence signal strengths across different organs between the Col@HMnO2-MM group and the Col@HMnO2 group (Fig. S23a). In addition, the nanomedicine was almost eliminated by 48 h, indicating that the nanomedicine could be metabolized by the liver and spleen and had certain biosafety (Fig. S23b). Finally, we examined the in vivo pharmacokinetic parameters of the nanomedicine. DiR-labeled Col@HMnO2-MM was administered via the tail vein to the mice. We collected blood samples from the mice at different time points and found that the fluorescence intensity of the blood samples gradually decreased with time (Fig. S24a). The nanomedicine Col@HMnO2-MM has a half-life of 14.997 h (Fig. S24b), which extends the half-life of Col in the blood [63]. This may be attributed to the ability of the nanomedicine to escape phagocytes. Thus, Col@HMnO2-MM has the potential to improve the therapeutic effect of Col. We also used immunofluorescence staining to visualize the ability of the synthesized nanomedicine to target the AS site. As shown in Fig. 6b, DiR-labeled Col@HMnO2-MM (red) colocalized with the macrophage-specific protein CD68 (green). In vivo therapeutic effects and mechanism of Col@HMnO2-MM on AS plaques

Based on the encouraging results presented above, we investigated the effect of Col@HMnO2-MM on plaques in vivo. APOE−/− mice were fed a Western diet for 5 weeks and then randomly divided into different groups in the sixth week. The following groups were given different drug treatments: control (C57BL/6 mice), model (APOE−/− mice), HMnO2-MM, free Col and Col@HMnO2-MM. Injections were then given twice a week for 8 weeks (Fig. 6c). In this way, we clearly compared each treatment with the control and model treatments to explore the effects of the drug on plaques. At the end of week 13, the entire aorta was removed from each mouse and stained with ORO (Fig. 6d and f). As shown in Fig. 6d and f, the ORO-stained areas were the largest in the model group, indicating successful construction of the AS model. The ORO-positive areas were smaller in the HMnO2-MM group and the Col group, whereas the Col@HMnO2-MM group presented the smallest area. Identical findings were noted in the ORO-stained aortic sections across the different groups (Fig. S25). In accordance with the macroscopic observations and ORO staining findings, the H&E-stained sections of the aortic root confirmed that the nanomedicine Col@HMnO2-MM was the most effective treatment for plaques, as shown in Fig. 6e and g. As shown in Fig. 6e, many necrotic nuclei rich in fat were observed in the model group. However, in the HMnO2-MM and Col groups, the plaque areas were somewhat reduced, indicating that single-drug treatment could alleviate the progression of AS to a certain extent. Compared with the Col group or the HMnO2-MM group, the Col@HMnO2-MM group presented a significant decrease in plaque area, indicating a synergistic effect that was significantly stronger than that of monotherapy.

We performed further histochemical assessments on aortic sections from the mice to better understand the mechanism of synergistic plaque therapy. Anti-CD68, anti-matrix metalloproteinase-9 (MMP-9) and alpha-smooth muscle actin (α-SMA) antibodies, along with Masson’s trichrome staining, were used to assess plaque composition following various treatments (Fig. 7a). The anti-CD68 antibody results showed that the synthesized nanomedicine Col@HMnO2-MM reduced macrophage infiltration at the plaque site to the greatest extent, while the large decrease in the MMP-9 antibody concentration indicated a decrease in plaque vulnerability. Similarly, α-SMA antibody staining and Masson’s trichrome staining revealed that treatment with the Col@HMnO2-MM resulted in a large decrease in the number of VSMCs, thus preventing the excessive accumulation of collagen, which is beneficial for stabilizing AS plaques (Fig. 7b–e).

Considering the exceptional ROS scavenging and anti-inflammatory properties of our recently developed nanomedicine, we conducted a DHE assay to assess the in vivo ROS scavenging ability of Col@HMnO2-MM in vivo. Compared with the model group, which presented a strong red fluorescence signal, the Col@HMnO2-MM group presented the lowest red signal intensity, indicating that the Col@HMnO2-MM might inhibit plaque formation by removing ROS (Fig. 7f, 7 g). Encouraged by the above findings, we investigated whether the synthesized nanomedicine affected the reprogramming of macrophages at the plaque site via immunofluorescence staining for iNOS (an M1 macrophage marker) and Arg-1 (an M2 macrophage marker). As shown in Fig. 7h and Fig. S26, a strong iNOS (M1) fluorescence signal and a relatively weak Arg-1 (M2) fluorescence signal were observed at AS sites in the mice in the model group, which is considered to indicate the unfavorable polarization of macrophages at the pathological AS site. In contrast, the opposite intensities of these fluorescence signals were observed in the Col@HMnO2-MM treatment group, indicating that M1-to-M2 reprogramming was achieved. Moreover, we examined the anti-inflammatory activity of the nanomedicine in vivo. According to the data in Fig. 7i–l, compared with those in the HMnO2-MM and Col groups, the levels of various representative proinflammatory factors (including CRP, IL-6, IL-1β, TNF-α) decreased more markedly, suggesting synergistic anti-inflammatory effects of the nanomedicine in vivo. These results demonstrated the efficacy of nanomedicine in treating plaques from an anti-inflammatory perspective.

It is often difficult for people with cardiovascular disease to follow a healthy lifestyle, especially a healthy diet [38, 39]. For this reason, we also investigated whether the nanomedicine Col@HMnO2-MM could delay the progression of AS by reducing lipid accumulation in mice fed a sustained Western diet. As shown in Fig. S27a, the body weights of the mice in the Col@HMnO2-MM group were effectively controlled. In addition, we explored the plasma cholesterol levels among the groups (Fig. S27b, Fig. S27c), and similarly found that the mice in the Col@HMnO2-MM group had the lowest plasma cholesterol content.

Finally, we investigated whether the nanomedicine Col@HMnO2-MM could restore plaque areas after 14 days of treatment (Fig. S28) and evaluated the biocompatibility of the nanomedicine throughout the treatment period. On the basis of the H&E staining results, no notable pathological alterations were observed in the major organs during the 2-month treatment (Fig. S29), suggesting that prolonged Col@HMnO2-MM treatment is safe.

Conclusion

In summary, we designed and fabricated the multifunctional nanomedicine Col@HMnO2-MM to achieve macrophage targeting AS therapy. The nanomedicine can evade immune system phagocytosis to achieve long-term circulation and can target the inflammatory site of the AS to achieve drug release. The Col@HMnO2-MM had the expected ROS scavenging ability, and could reprogram macrophages, thus inhibiting the inflammatory storm of AS. In addition, nanomedicine can downregulate the expression of HIF-1α through self-production of oxygen to improve the hypoxic microenvironment of AS and thus promote cholesterol efflux. The synergistic combination of the two treatment pathways improved the efficacy of Col@HMnO2-MM against AS compared with that of the single treatment pathway. Col@HMnO2-MM has been administered to APOE−/− mice and has been proven to have enhanced pharmacokinetic performance and active targeting properties with expected efficacy against AS. Overall, Col@HMnO2-MM provides a more comprehensive and targeted treatment strategy for the management of AS. Nevertheless, there are numerous methods that can lead to the development of AS. We studied only the two possible therapeutic pathways of this nanomedicine for AS, and other therapeutic mechanisms of AS need to be further studied. In addition, manganese-based nanozymes can also be used in magnetic resonance imaging, and dynamic monitoring of their therapeutic effects may be a hot topic in the future.
