Amiodarone‐ and Lithium‐Induced Thyroid Dysfunction—Prescriber Specialties Involved in Potential Prescribing Cascades: A Retrospective Study
Authors: J. de Ruijter – van Dalem, M. de Weerd – Slot, M. H. Schipper, C. E. H. Siegert, A. Marmorale, M. L. Becker, F. Karapinar‐Çarkit
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTBackground and AimsPrescribing cascades occur when an adverse drug reaction (ADR) leads to the prescription of additional medications. It remains unclear whether different prescribers are involved in potential prescribing cascades that develop over time and whether ADRs are communicated across the care continuum. This study aimed to investigate whether the specialty of the prescriber initiating amiodarone or lithium differs from the specialty of the prescriber starting the thyroid medication and the communication of this ADR.MethodsA retrospective study, combined with a structured questionnaire among community pharmacists, was conducted in two teaching hospitals and 22 community pharmacies from February 2023 to June 2023, including patients who initiated amiodarone or lithium and subsequently received thyroid medication within 24 months, regardless of whether this was intentional or unintentional. Primary outcome was whether prescribers' specialties differed. Secondary outcomes included the documentation of the ADR when thyroid medication was started and ADR communication in discharge letters. Community pharmacists were questioned to assess their awareness of this ADR. Descriptive statistics were used for data analysis.ResultsWe included 67 hospital patients and 57 community pharmacy patients. Different specialties were involved in 49% of hospital patients and 81% of patients in the community setting with a potential amiodarone‐ or lithium‐induced thyroid dysfunction. In the hospital, the risk of thyroid dysfunction or the occurrence of the amiodarone‐induced prescribing cascade was documented in 78% of the patient records and/or discharge letters. Three percent of discharge letters noted thyroid dysfunction risks at amiodarone initiation. Questionnaires completed by community pharmacists revealed limited self‐reported awareness of this ADR and potential cascade.ConclusionPotential prescribing cascades with amiodarone and lithium‐induced thyroid dysfunction frequently involve different specialties. However, in this study, risks of ADRs were rarely communicated to subsequent healthcare providers. This discontinuity in information transfer represents opportunities for optimization of care.

Introduction

Prescribing cascades occur when an adverse drug reaction (ADR) associated with an initial (index) medication leads to the prescription of an additional (marker) medication [1]. Treatment of the ADR can be either intentional or unintentional. The management of prescribing cascades can be particularly complex in cases involving multiple healthcare professionals throughout the continuum of care [2]. This is especially the case for hospitalized patients, as medication adjustments are common during hospitalization; however, many ADRs manifest after the patient has been discharged [3].

Amiodarone and lithium are two medications known to trigger prescribing cascades. Both are typically initiated by medical specialists: cardiologists prescribe amiodarone for the management of cardiac arrhythmias, while psychiatrists initiate lithium for the treatment of bipolar disorder or treatment‐resistant depression. A well‐known ADR of both medications is thyroid dysfunction [4, 5, 6].

Clear communication between healthcare professionals is essential to recognize and manage prescribing cascades, particularly regarding potential ADRs of newly prescribed drugs during hospitalization and their monitoring. A previous study evaluated the impact of the involvement of various healthcare professionals in prescribing practices [7]. Their results indicated that the prescribing cascade involving a dihydropyridine calcium channel blocker and loop diuretic was more prevalent when different healthcare professionals and pharmacies were involved. However, they did not specifically assess the individual role of different prescribers or the communication of ADRs in discharge letters. Additionally, only few studies investigated the awareness of community pharmacists regarding prescribing cascades and their potential management strategies [8]. Integrating perspectives from both the hospital and community could provide a more comprehensive understanding of prescribing cascades during transitions of care.

Therefore, this study aimed to evaluate whether the specialty of the prescribers who start amiodarone or lithium therapy, differs from the specialty of the prescriber starting the thyroid medication. Secondary objectives included assessing whether ADR risk or the potential prescribing cascade was documented in the patient record, whether the ADR or potential prescribing cascade was communicated in discharge letters to subsequent healthcare professionals, whether there was direct communication between the index and marker prescribers in the hospital, and exploring community pharmacists’ knowledge of this ADR and their management strategies.

Methods

Design and Setting

This retrospective study, combined with a structured questionnaire among community pharmacists, was conducted from February 2023 to June 2023 in collaboration with two teaching hospitals (OLVG, Amsterdam and Spaarne Gasthuis, Haarlem/Hoofddorp) and 22 community pharmacies willing to participate within the catchment area of these hospitals. By focusing on both hospital and community setting it was possible to determine whether the ADR was documented and communicated in discharge letters, to identify the prescribers of the index and marker medication and to evaluate the knowledge and management strategies of community pharmacists regarding prescribing cascades. The Medical Ethics Committee of the OLVG hospital and Spaarne Gasthuis (registration number OLVG: WO22169 and Spaarne Gasthuis: 2022.0115) waived ethical approval as the study fell outside the scope of the Dutch Medical Research Involving Human Subjects Act (WMO).

Study Population

Patients were included from two distinct settings: the hospital setting and the community pharmacy setting. In the hospital setting, patients admitted to OLVG and Spaarne Gasthuis between January 1, 2019, and February 16, 2023, were included. This period was selected because the hospitals began importing data from the Nationwide Medication Record System (NMRS) starting on January 1, 2019. The NMRS contains medication dispensing data from patients' community pharmacies, providing information about medication dispensed in the previous 8 months and is used in hospitals to perform medication reconciliation with the patient [9]. Every time the patient had contact with the hospital (including admissions, outpatient visits and consultation by phone), prescribing data from the NMRS were uploaded to the hospital information system (HIS). In the community pharmacy setting, patients from January 1, 2012 to February 16, 2023 were included, as community pharmacies began utilizing the NMRS starting in January 2012.

For both settings, the remaining inclusion criteria were consistent. Patients aged 18+ years were eligible if they were first prescribed the index medication (either amiodarone or lithium) followed by the marker medication (levothyroxine for hypothyroidism or thyreostatics for hyperthyroidism), regardless of whether this represented intentional management of an ADR or an unintentional prescribing cascade. Several parameters, used to assess prescribing cascades, were set [10]. First, an 8 month washout window (look back‐period to exclude prevalent users) was applied to ensure that only incident users of the index medication were included. This was verified through dispensing records from community pharmacies or hospital records. Second, a blackout period of 1 month was imposed in which prescriptions of marker medications were not taken into account as it is unlikely for thyroid dysfunction to develop within 1 month [11]. Third, the exposure window (maximum allowed time interval between the initiation of the index medication and the commencement of the marker medication) was limited to a maximum of 24 months. This duration was selected as thyroid dysfunction generally presents within this timeframe [6, 12, 13]. Fourth, the period of continued exposure (maximum allowed interval between the expected end date of the index medication and the start date of the marker medication) was set at 4 months for lithium and 6 months for amiodarone. Since most repeat prescriptions in the Netherlands are dispensed for 3 months, and patients often have some medication stock, a 4‐month continued exposure was deemed reasonable to assume that the marker medication is potentially started due to the index medication [14]. However, this period was extended to 6 months for amiodarone due to its long plasma half‐life and evidence suggesting that amiodarone could lead to thyroid dysfunction within 6 to 9 months after discontinuation [15, 16]. Finally, only patients who consented to share their prescription data via the NMRS or with original prescriptions available, were included.

Data Collection

Patients from both hospital and community pharmacy settings were included. Hospital data included prescriptions and clinical documentation recorded during inpatient care, whereas community data are based on dispensing records and reflect medication use in the primary care setting.

Hospital Setting

Patient data retrieved from the HIS included the patient identifier, year of birth, sex, length of hospital stay, department of admission, index and/or marker medication prescriptions (including start and stop dates), the setting of the prescriber for the index and marker medication (inpatient, outpatient or primary care) as well as the Anatomical Therapeutic Chemical (ATC) code of the prescribed medication. Dispensing data extracted from the NMRS encompassed the index and marker medication orders along with the corresponding dispensing dates. Patients who initiated amiodarone or lithium in the hospital were followed during 24 months post‐discharge, linking the hospital inpatient and outpatient prescriptions and the dispensing records retrieved from the NMRS.

For the included patients, medical records were reviewed from the initiation of the index medication until the commencement of the marker medication. This review focused on identifying the prescriber and investigating discharge letters (for communication to subsequent healthcare professionals) and medical notes (for internal communication within the hospital). Discharge letters were examined to determine whether thyroid dysfunction, as a potential ADR at the start of the index medication, was mentioned, or if the prescribing cascade was referenced at the start of the marker medication. Medical notes were reviewed to determine whether the prescriber of the marker medication was aware of the thyroid dysfunction resulting from the initiation of the index medication, and to assess whether any consultations between prescribers regarding the initiation of the medications were documented. The keywords searched in the notes and discharge letters were “hypothyroidism,” “hyperthyroidism,” “amiodarone,” “lithium,” “thyroid,” “levothyroxine,” and “thyreostatic.” When one of these keywords was identified, the complete discharge letter and relevant sections of the medical notes were thoroughly reviewed. Awareness of the ADR was defined as explicit documentation in the patient record linking the adverse event to the suspected medication. Communication between prescribers was defined as documented consultation between healthcare professionals in the medical record (e.g., notes indicating discussion with another specialist). Communication not documented in the patient record (e.g., telephone contact) could not be assessed.

Chart abstraction was performed by a single researcher (not blinded) using a structured approach and checked by a second reviewer. Predefined variables and criteria were consistently applied across all cases. No duplicate chart abstraction or formal inter‐rater agreement analysis was conducted. To enhance reliability, the methodology and extracted data were discussed with a second researcher, and all cases were manually cross‐checked with the source data.

Community Pharmacies

Community pharmacies were contacted to obtain their consent to participate in the study. To minimize bias, community pharmacists were not informed about which potential prescribing cascades were of interest. Initially, a questionnaire (see Appendix) was administered either by phone or in person to participating pharmacists by one of the researchers. The questionnaire focused on assessing their recognition of thyroid dysfunction related to lithium and/or amiodarone use. Additionally, pharmacists were asked whether any monitoring or medication surveillance signals were noted when the index and marker medications were prescribed together, and how they believed the prescribing cascades should be managed.

Another query was conducted in the community pharmacy's electronic information system to identify patients who were prescribed both the index and marker medications using the inclusion criteria. Anonymized data on patient characteristics, including age and sex, were collected. In addition to the use of the NMRS, the original prescriptions for both the index and marker medications were retrieved to confirm the medical specialties of the prescribers who initiated these prescriptions.

Outcomes

The primary outcome was the percentage of patients for whom the prescriber's specialty who started the index medication differed from that of the marker medication. Secondary outcomes included any documentation in the patient chart of the ADR risk or the prescribing cascade, the percentage of patients in whom the risk of thyroid dysfunction was addressed in discharge letters (at the initiation of the index medication), the mention of the potential prescribing cascade itself in the discharge letter (at the initiation of the marker medication), and whether there was documented communication between the various prescribers involved in the potential prescribing cascade. Additionally, the knowledge of community pharmacists and percentage of community pharmacists who had communicated with prescribers regarding the cascade was measured. Finally, mitigation strategies by community pharmacists were assessed. All outcomes were predefined prior to start of the study.

Data Analysis

Hospital data were queried using Microsoft SQL Server Management Studio 2016 (Microsoft Corporation, Redmond, WA, USA) and processed using R (R Foundation for Statistical Computing, Vienna, Austria, version 4.2.2). All data were compiled in Microsoft Excel 2016 (Microsoft Corporation, Redmond, WA, USA). Descriptive data analysis was performed. Continuous variables were presented as median values with ranges, while dichotomous variables were expressed as percentages of the total patient population. Confidence intervals for the proportions were calculated using the normal approximation for sufficiently large sample sizes. For smaller sample sizes (< 30) or proportions close to 0% or 100%, the Wilson score interval was used. Missing data were reported as a separate category. Responses from the questionnaire were categorized into mutually exclusive categories (three to four categories) and were verified by a senior researcher (FK).

Results

Participants Characteristics

In the hospital setting, 363 patients using amiodarone and 5 patients using lithium were identified as taking thyroid medication. After applying the inclusion criteria, 67 amiodarone patients were included (Figure 1). No lithium patients met the inclusion criteria and could be included in the analysis. The majority of exclusions were due to patients not being incident users of the index medication (n = 166) or already using thyroid medication prior to starting the index medication (n = 102).

Workflow diagram illustrating the process of selection and inclusion of users of amiodarone and lithium in both hospital and community setting. Patients were exluded if they were < 18 years, no incident user of the index medication, used the marker medication before start of the index medication, started the marker medication outside the predefined exposure window, had no continued exposure of the medication or had no data prescription data available.

In the community pharmacy setting, 34 pharmacies were approached, and 20 community pharmacists, operating 22 pharmacies, agreed to participate in the study. Non‐participation was due to lack of interest (n = 3) or time constraints (n = 9). A total of 99 amiodarone users and 61 lithium users who were also using thyroid medication were identified. After applying the inclusion criteria, 41 amiodarone users and 16 lithium users were included (see Figure 1). Most patients were excluded because the marker medication was prescribed outside the defined window (1 to 24 months) following the initiation of the index medication (n = 39).

Overall, the median age for amiodarone users (n = 108) was 77 years (range 48–99), for lithium users (n = 16) 54 years (range 32–70) and 60% of all patients were female (Table 1). Depending on the cases, the median interval between the start of the index medication and the initiation of marker medication ranged from 217 to 253 days.

Prescribers of Index and Marker Medication

Amiodarone was primarily initiated by a cardiologist (98%, 108 prescriptions), while lithium was exclusively initiated by a psychiatrist (16 prescriptions, Table 2). The marker medication was most commonly prescribed by a general practitioner (GP), internal medicine physician, or cardiologist. Different specialties were involved in 47% of 58 patients in the hospital setting and 84% of 37 patients in the community setting regarding amiodarone‐levothyroxine cases. For amiodarone‐thyreostatic cases, different specialties were involved in 62% of the eight cases in the hospital setting and 75% of 4 cases in the community pharmacy setting. In the case of lithium‐levothyroxine cases in the community setting, different specialties were involved in 75% of 16 cases.

Secondary Outcomes

In the hospital setting (n = 67), the risk of thyroid dysfunction or the occurrence of the amiodarone‐induced prescribing cascade was documented in 78% (52 of 67) of the patient records and/or discharge letters. Communication regarding the risk of thyroid dysfunction to other healthcare professionals was noted in 3% (2 of 67) of discharge letters at the initiation of amiodarone. At initiation of the marker medication, 64% (43 of 67) of discharge letters mentioned that the hypothyroidism or hyperthyroidism could potentially be an amiodarone‐induced ADR. In potential cascades where a prescriber from a different specialty initiated the marker medication, 79% (26 of 33) of patient notes documented a consultation with a cardiologist prior to starting the medication. In 50% (11 of 22) of the cases where the cardiologist was the marker prescriber, it was documented that the cardiologist contacted the internal medicine physician when initiating thyroid medication.

Twenty community pharmacists were questioned about their knowledge of potential prescribing cascades related to amiodarone and lithium. Sixty‐five percent of the pharmacists were unaware of these potential prescribing cascades. None of the pharmacists communicated with prescribers when a marker medication was initiated. Additionally, all pharmacists stated that there is currently no monitoring available for these potential prescribing cascades. Ninety‐five percent of the pharmacists would like to be able to monitor these cascades through their information systems, not only at the start of the marker medication but especially also when the index medication is discontinued or a dose is decreased as that could also necessitate changes to the marker medication. They indicated that they would like to take a more prominent role in recognizing, managing and preventing these cascades, however, only if automated signals were available in their pharmacy information system.

Discussion

The results of this study indicate that, in the majority of the potential prescribing cascades, the specialty of the index prescriber differs from that of the marker prescriber. However, communication from hospitals to the next healthcare professional about the risk of thyroid dysfunction occurred in only 3% of the patients at start of amiodarone treatment. Furthermore, when the marker medication was prescribed, only 64% of discharge letters mentioned the possibility of amiodarone‐induced thyroid dysfunction as an ADR. Additionally, our findings revealed that a considerable percentage of community pharmacists are unaware of these potential prescribing cascades.

To our knowledge, this is one of the first studies examining whether potential prescribing cascades involve multiple prescribers across healthcare settings (including both hospital and complementary primary care data) and how associated ADRs are documented and communicated. Farrell et al. highlighted that information gaps, lack of communication and differences in knowledge of potential ADRs on specific medications, are risk factors for prescribing cascades [17]. Similarly, Narayan et al. reported that different prescribers are involved in 47% of anticholinergic medication prescribing cascades in dementia treatment [18]. Another study found that 64% of prescribing cascades involving calcium channel blockers and loop diuretics were managed by different prescribers [7]. These findings are consistent with our own research, which demonstrates that the majority of patients experiencing intentional and/or unintentional prescribing cascades (67% for amiodarone and 75% for lithium) are treated by different prescribers. We also showed that communication between healthcare providers about the risks and occurrences of these cascades is often lacking.

A previous study examining community pharmacists' views and knowledge of prescribing cascades found that only 51% of the community pharmacists were familiar with the term “prescribing cascade” [8]. Moreover, 55% expressed concern that their patients might be experiencing unidentified prescribing cascades. These results further underscore the lack of awareness among pharmacists regarding prescribing cascades, aligning with our own findings.

Importantly, the potential cascades identified in this study do not represent inappropriate care. Thyroid dysfunction is a known adverse effect of amiodarone and lithium, and treatment with thyroid medication may be clinically appropriate. Our findings should therefore be interpreted as reflecting potential gaps in the recognition or communication of potential prescribing cascades, rather than inappropriate prescribing. Moreover, our findings relate to documented communication and self‐reported awareness, and do not allow conclusions about actual knowledge or clinical decision‐making.

Clinical Impact

The overall prevalence of thyroid dysfunction in Europe is 3.82% [19]. However, this prevalence is significantly higher in patients taking amiodarone (up to 30%) or lithium (up to 35%) [4, 20, 21]. Given the substantial impact of thyroid dysfunction on patients' quality of life, it is essential for healthcare professionals to recognize and communicate this potential ADR. Especially as care is shifted from hospital to primary care and repeat prescriptions are often prescribed by general practitioners, who have limited experience with specialist medication. While it is not feasible to address every potential ADR in discharge letters, highlighting common ADRs for specialist medication might be relevant considering the impact on patients' well‐being and frequency. This is particularly important as previous research has shown that, in the context of the loop diuretics–potassium supplementation prescribing cascade, a substantial proportion of patients (over 30%) continued potassium supplementation after discontinuation of loop diuretics. Patients with the same prescriber or pharmacy involved in the management of both medications were less likely to experience this issue [22].

Strengths and Limitations

The main strength of this study is the inclusion of patients from both hospital and community pharmacy settings, which provides a comprehensive overview of the prescribers involved in these cascades and their communication. Hospital systems offered valuable insights into whether prescribing cascades were documented and communicated to the next healthcare professional, while community pharmacies provided more detailed information about the identification of the initial prescribers. Furthermore, including multiple community pharmacies helped mitigate the likelihood that the results are pharmacy‐specific, making our findings more applicable to a broader population.

This study does have several limitations. First, for secondary outcomes in the hospital setting, we relied on documented information, which may not capture all communication between prescribers regarding a prescribing cascade. As a result, these prescribing cascades may be more frequently recognized than documented in the medical records. Second, no patients receiving lithium met the inclusion criteria in the hospital setting. This could be due to patients either not consenting to share NMRS data with hospitals or lithium treatment being initiated in clinics outside the hospital setting. This limitation underscores the importance of including the community setting. Third, the keywords used to search the notes and discharge letters were limited. It is possible that we may have missed information from prescribers using other terminology. However, we do expect that searching for parts of the names of the medication should include all patients. Moreover, chart abstraction and assessment of outcomes were performed by a single reviewer, which may have introduced subjective interpretation and limits reproducibility. However, data was checked by a second reviewer. Fourth, it is possible that several potential prescribing cascades went undetected, as thyroid dysfunction may develop after a median of 2 years. This may have led to underreporting of the potential prescribing cascades, however, we do not expect it to have affected the proportions in our primary results. Fifth, patients with pre‐existing thyroid dysfunction were excluded from the study, which may lead to an underestimation. Prescribing cascades could still affect these patients after initiating the index medication, due to possible further deterioration of the thyroid dysfunction.

Future Research

Our research highlights that the ADR thyroid dysfunction is rarely communicated to the next healthcare provider. This may obstruct recognition of potential prescribing cascades, particularly when the index medication is dose‐adjusted or discontinued. The development and evaluation of targeted interventions aimed at improving the detection and continuous management of potential prescribing cascades should be prioritized, especially when changes are initiated to the index medication. Possible strategies are automated signals, evidence‐based guidelines about prescribing cascades and targeted education. Furthermore, more research is needed on whether patients prefer to use a marker medication to treat the ADR or prefer a different medication with potential less effect but also less impactful ADRs.

Conclusion

This study reveals that in most cases the prescribers initiating the index medication belong to a different medical specialty than the marker prescriber. Despite the known risk of thyroid dysfunction associated with initiating amiodarone therapy, information regarding this risk was communicated to the next healthcare professionals in only 3% of the patients at start of the treatment. Moreover, at the time the marker medication was initiated, only 64% of discharge summaries included a reference to the potential for thyroid dysfunction as an ADR to amiodarone. This discontinuity in information transfer represents opportunities for optimization of care. Furthermore, our study revealed a limited self‐reported awareness of potential prescribing cascades among community pharmacists. It can be useful to clearly define which healthcare professional holds responsibility for monitoring thyroid function during index medication treatment and changes in dose or a discontinuation, in order to ensure continuity and optimization of patient care in the care continuum.
