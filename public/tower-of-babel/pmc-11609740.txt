Interferon gamma‐mediated prevention of tumor progression in a mouse model of multiple myeloma
Authors: Zoltán Kellermayer, Sabrin Tahri, Madelon M E de Jong, Natalie Papazian, Cathelijne Fokkema, Elodie C G Stoetman, Remco Hoogenboezem, Gregory van Beek, Mathijs A Sanders, Louis Boon, Chelsea Den Hollander, Annemiek Broijl, Pieter Sonneveld, Tom Cupedo
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractMalignant plasma cells in multiple myeloma patients reside in the bone marrow and continuously interact with local immune cells. Progression and therapy response are influenced by this immune environment, highlighting the need for a detailed understanding of endogenous immune responses to malignant plasma cells. Here we used the 5TGM1 murine transfer model of multiple myeloma to dissect early immune responses to myeloma cells. We modeled stable and progressive disease by transferring 5TGM1 murine myeloma cells into C57Bl/6 mice and KaLwRij mice, respectively. We used flow cytometry and single‐cell and bulk transcriptomic analyses to characterize differential immune responses in stable and progressive disease. Transfer of 5TGM1 cells in C57Bl/6 mice led to stable disease with low tumor burden in a subset of animals. Stable disease was associated with sustained activation and expansion of NK cells, ILC1, and CD8+ T cells, a response that was lost upon disease progression. Single‐cell RNA‐sequencing of immune cells and bulk RNA sequencing of immune and mesenchymal stromal cells implicated the activation of interferon responses as a central immune pathway during stable disease. Experimentally, neutralization of IFNγ significantly increased myeloma development and progression in C57Bl/6 mice, testifying to the importance of this pathway in early disease control. In conclusion, we provide a framework for studying immune responses to multiple myeloma progression in immunocompetent and genetically modifiable mice and highlight the importance of bone marrow immunity in tumor control.

INTRODUCTION

Multiple myeloma (MM) is the second most common hematological cancer in adults and is characterized by the accumulation of malignant plasma cells in the bone marrow (BM). 1 , 2 Interactions with the local immune system affect MM disease progression and therapy response. First, BM immune status influences progression from asymptomatic precursor stages (Monoclonal Gammopathy of Undetermined Significance [MGUS] and smoldering MM [SMM]) toward symptomatic disease. 3 Second, the efficacy of immunotherapy modalities, including immunomodulatory imide drugs (IMiDs), monoclonal antibodies, and bispecific T cell engagers (BiTEs), relies to a great extent on a functionally intact endogenous immune system. 4 , 5 , 6 Combined, these data highlight the importance of a detailed understanding of BM immune responses to MM cells.

Ex vivo studies using human BM aspirates and biopsies have provided valuable insights into the BM microenvironment and the MM niche. 7 , 8 , 9 , 10 However, the complexity of the BM microenvironment, including various immune and nonhematopoietic cells, emphasizes the need for in vivo studies. The Radl/5 T cell transfer models 11 are a series of well‐established murine models that recapitulate MM pathogenesis, response to therapy, MM‐related bone disease, and the presence of serum paraprotein with renal dysfunction. 12 , 13 In these models, 5T MM cells (derived from a spontaneously arising MM in a KaLwRij mouse) are transferred into syngeneic recipients leading to rapid and reproducible development of MM within 2–4 weeks. Of the several 5T subclones that have evolved from the original clone, only the 5TGM1 cell line can be cultured in vitro, 14 and these cells have also been engineered to express eGFP for easier visualization and cell tracing. 15

Myeloma‐prone KaLwRij mice are derived from back‐crossing of C57Bl/6 mice, generating a mouse strain that is much more susceptible to MM compared to the parental C57Bl/6. 16 Since disease course of MM is profoundly influenced by the local tissue immune microenvironment, 17 we hypothesized that alterations in BM immune status and immunological responses to MM cells contribute to the different susceptibility between C57Bl/6 and KaLwRij mice, allowing for the identification of productive anti‐myeloma immune responses.

In this study, we analyzed anti‐myeloma immunity in C57Bl/6 mice that developed progressive disease versus those with stable disease. Both were compared to KaLwRij mice as the gold standard of failed anti‐myeloma immunity. We show that stable disease in C57Bl/6 mice is associated with an increase of NK and CD8+ T cells and activation of interferon‐gamma (IFNγ) signaling. Similarly, BM of MM patients relapsing after the first‐line therapy contains an NK cell population characterized by IFN responses. IFNγ had a causal role in 5TGM1 MM pathobiology as neutralization of this cytokine increased the incidence of disease development and impaired the ability of the BM immune system to control the disease. Together, our data identify productive immune responses associated with stable disease and highlight the importance of studying MM in an immunocompetent environment.

MATERIALS AND METHODS

Mice

Eight‐week‐old female C57Bl/6JOlaHsd (referred to as C57Bl/6) and C57Bl/KaLwRijHsd (referred to as KaLwRij) mice (Envigo) were housed in the animal facilities of the Erasmus MC, Rotterdam, The Netherlands. 5TGM1‐GFP cells were kindly provided by Prof. Babatunde Oyajobi (UT Health) 15 and were cultured in RPMI‐1640 supplemented with 10% FCS, 1% PS, 1% Glutamax, 1% NEAA, and 1% Na‐Pyruvate. To induce MM, 106 5TGM1‐GFP cells in sterile saline were injected into the lateral tail vein of a total of 22 C57Bl/6 and 21 KaLwRij mice. For IFNγ neutralization, C57Bl/6 mice received 500 μg anti‐IFNγ antibody (clone XMG1.2, 28 mice in total) or isotype control (clone GL113, 22 mice in total) intraperitoneally (ip.) on Days 0 (prior to the transfer of 5TGM1 cells), 3, 7, 10, 14, and 17. To deplete NK cells, C57Bl/6 mice received 300 μg anti‐NK1.1 antibodies (clone PK136, 16 mice in total) or isotype control (16 mice in total) ip. on Days 7, 9, 12, 14, 16, and 19. For M‐protein measurement, 50 μL of blood was collected weekly (Days 0, 7, 14, and 21) into K2 EDTA tubes (16.444; Sarstedt). Enzyme‐linked immunosorbent assay was performed using a purified goat anti‐mouse IgG2b (A90‐109A; Bethyl Laboratories, Inc.) as capture antibody and an HRP‐labeled goat anti‐mouse IgG2b (A90‐109P; Bethyl Laboratories, Inc.) as detection antibody, and mouse IgG2b (Cat# 0104‐01; Southern Biotech) as standard. Tetramethylbenzidine (TMB; Thermo Fisher) was used as the substrate solution. Mice were sacrificed on Day 21 unless stated otherwise or were euthanized when any of the following symptoms appeared: high IgG2b levels, sudden and/or large weight loss, hind limb paralysis, poor general condition; in compliance with local animal welfare guidelines. Animal studies were approved by the institutional review board of the Erasmus University Medical Center as part of the animal experiment license # AVD1010020197844.

Flow cytometry and fluorescence‐activated cell sorting (FACS)

For FACS of BM mesenchymal stromal cells (MSCs), two femurs and two tibias were used per mouse. The ends of cleaned bones were cut off and marrow was flushed out. The remaining bone was cut into small pieces and gently crushed with a mortar and pestle. Bone fragments were filtered through a 100 μm cell strainer and digested for 45 min at 37°C with 0.25% Collagenase Type I (Stem Cell). Bone chips were washed on a 100 μm cell strainer and MSCs were collected into PBS containing 1% FCS and 1 mM EDTA. Erythrocytes were lysed and cells were counted. For flow cytometry and FACS of BM immune cells, 1 femur and 2 tibias were used per mouse. After isolating and cleaning the bones, one end was cut off and bones were placed into a 0.5 mL microcentrifuge tube with a hole punched in the bottom. This was nested into a 1.5 mL microcentrifuge tube and spun down at 10,000 RPM for 15 s to collect BM. 13 Following resuspension in PBS containing 1% FCS and 1 mM EDTA, erythrocytes were lysed. Cells were washed with PBS containing 2% FCS, filtered through a 100 μm cell strainer and counted. Spleens were mechanically disrupted to obtain a single‐cell suspension. Following erythrocyte lysis, samples were filtered through a 100 μm cell strainer and counted. For all flow cytometry and FACS experiments, Fc‐receptors were blocked with 10% normal mouse serum and anti‐CD16/32 (2.4G2; BD) prior to incubation with appropriate antibodies for 25 min on ice in the dark. Following staining, cells were washed, resuspended in PBS +2% FCS containing DAPI, and measured on a BD LSRII or sorted with a BD FACS Aria III. Results were analyzed using FlowJo v10.

Single‐cell RNA sequencing of mouse samples

Sorted T (CD3+) and B (CD19+) cells (sorted with the same gate into one tube due to gating limits as described in Supporting Information S1: Figure 2A), NK cells (CD3−CD19−NKp46+), monocytes/macrophages (CD3−CD19−NKp46−CD11b+Gr1loLyC6int/hi), and neutrophils (CD3−CD19−NKp46−CD11b+Gr1+LyC6int) were counted using a Countess II Automated Cell Counter (ThermoFisher) and then combined and processed as one sample per mouse. Single cells were encapsulated for cDNA synthesis and barcoded using the Chromium Single Cell 3′ Reagent Kit v3 (10X Genomics), followed by library construction according to the manufacturer's recommendations. The Agilent 2100 Bio‐Analyzer and the High Sensitivity DNA kit were used to determine the quality and quantity of libraries, which were sequenced on the NovaSeq 6000 platform (Illumina), paired‐end mode, at a sequencing depth of ~20,000 reads/cell, followed by computational alignment using CellRanger (v3.0.2, v4.0.0, and v6.0.1, 10X Genomics). Data sets were subjected to quality control steps using Seurat (R package, v 4.0), 19 including selecting cells with a library complexity of more than 200 features, removing doublets, and filtering out cells with high percentages of mitochondrial genes (>10%). NK cells (Ncr1 + ) and T cells (Cd3e + ) were subsetted from each mouse, merged by integration and label transfer 20 into one NK/T data set, and normalized and analyzed by principal‐component analysis (PCA) on the most variable genes (k = 2000) across all cells. Samples were integrated in the following order: first the biological duplicates, then the two different conditions (noninjected vs. tumor‐bearing) per strain, and finally the two strains. From this data set, NK cells were subsetted, and unsupervised clustering was performed using a shared nearest neighbor (SNN) modularity optimization‐based clustering algorithm (resolution 0.3–0.4). 21 Cells were projected in two dimensions using Uniform Manifold Approximation and Projection (UMAP). 22 Significantly upregulated genes in each cluster compared to all other clusters (adjusted p < 0.05) were identified using the Seurat Function FindMarkers.

Single‐cell transcriptomics of human samples

CD38+CD45+CD34− cells were sorted from BM of MM patients experiencing relapse after first‐line treatment with bortezomib, thalidomide, and dexamethasone, in the presence or absence of anti‐CD38 antibodies. Control BM samples were obtained from patients undergoing hip replacement surgery (femur heads) or cardiothoracic surgery (sternal aspiration) and previously reported in de Jong et al. 7 All materials were obtained after written informed consent approved by the Institutional Review Boards of the Erasmus Medical Center in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki. Details on sample processing are described in de Jong et al. 7 Patient characteristics are described in Supporting Information S1: Table III. Fastq files were processed with 10X Genomics CellRanger (version 3.0.2., 10X Genomics) and analyzed using the Seurat 4.2.0 package 19 in R 4.2.2. Ambient RNA was removed with SoupX, 23 and quality controls included removal of doublets, cells with less than 200 reads, and cells with high mitochondrial gene content. Dimensionality reduction was done with principal component analysis (PCA) in the 2000 most variable genes. Cells were clustered using the Louvain algorithm in Seurat's FindClusters function and visualized as a UMAP. Individual patients were integrated using the IntegrateData function in Seurat, followed by scaling, normalization, PCA, clustering, and UMAP visualization. NK cells were identified based on transcription of KLRF1, KLRD1, NKG7, and GNLY, 24 leading to 12,914 BM NK cells for further analysis. Potential contaminating cell populations were removed based on cell type‐specific genes. One cluster containing a cell type admixture was removed and immunoglobulin and hemoglobin genes were removed prior to subsequent analysis. Finally, we performed differential expression analysis on the integrated data set to identify genes significantly upregulated in each cluster compared to all other clusters (FDR‐adjusted p ≤ 0.05) using the Seurat function FindMarkers.

Bulk RNA sequencing

RNA was isolated from sorted Nkp46+, CD8+, or CD45−CD31−Ter119−CD71− MSCs with the NucleoSpin RNA XS kit (Macherey‐Nagel), and cDNA was prepared using the SMARTer Ultra Low RNA kit (Clontech Laboratories) for Illumina Sequencing following the manufacturer's protocol. The Agilent 2100 Bio‐Analyzer and the High Sensitivity DNA kit were used to verify the quality and quantity of cDNA samples. cDNA libraries were generated using the TruSeq Sample Preparation v.2 Guide (Illumina) and paired end‐sequenced on the NovaSeq 6000 (Illumina). Adaptor sequences and polyT tails were trimmed from unprocessed reads using fqtrim version v0.9.7 (http://ccb.jhu.edu/software/fqtrim/). Read counts and TPMs were determined with Salmon version v1.4.0. 25 Per gene pseudocount estimates were determined from the Salmon output with the tximport R package. 26 These per gene pseudocount estimates were in turn used for determining differential expression of genes between groups of interest through the DESeq2 package. 27 GSEA was performed with the GSEA software (Broad Institute) using predefined gene sets from the Molecular Signatures Database (MSigDB 7.2). Gene lists were ranked on the basis of the shrunken log 2‐fold change based on the ashr method 28 made available through the DESeq2 package. Classical enrichment statistics with 10,000 permutations was used to determine significant enrichment within gene sets. Gene Ontology analysis was done with the PANTER Overrepresentation Test (GO database release: 2019‐09‐03).

Statistics

Statistical analyses were performed in GraphPad Prism9 or R v4.1. Statistical significance was assessed using Mann–Whitney U tests, Fisher's exact test, Pearson's test, or Wald test followed by a Benjamini–Hochberg correction. ns, p > 0.05; *p < 0.05; **p < 0.01; ***p < 0.001; ****p < 0.0001.

RESULTS

Development of MM in C57Bl/6 mice

To generate stable and progressive MM, 5TGM1‐GFP cells were intravenously injected into C57Bl/6 mice. Peripheral blood was analyzed weekly to determine IgG2b M‐protein level as a measure for tumor growth. KaLwRij mice were used as positive controls for progressive disease (Figure 1A). As expected, 14 incidence of MM in KaLwRij mice was 100%, with elevated levels of IgG2b from day 14 post‐inoculation onward (median at Day 21: 4.54 mg/mL) (Figure 1B). In C67Bl/6 mice, IgG2b levels were still close to baseline at Day 14; by Day 21, this increased to low but detectable levels, compared to that seen in KaLwRij at Day 14 (Figure 1B). Experiments were terminated at Day 21, the time when all KaLwRij mice, but none of the C67Bl/6 mice reached humane endpoints. In line with the detectable M‐protein levels, a subset of the C57Bl/6 mice contained scattered aggregates of GFP+ tumor cells in femurs, while all KaLwRij mice had significant numbers of GFP+ cells (Figure 1C). Histology revealed the presence of occasional large tumor foci in C57Bl/6 mice, coupled with dispersed GFP+ myeloma cells (Figure 1D). BM architecture was usually preserved. In contrast, in KaLwRij BM, 5TGM1 cells usually filled the entire BM space, causing disruption of normal marrow structure (Figure 1D). Flow cytometric assessment of BM cells showed that 59% (13 out of 22) of C57Bl/6 mice developed MM, defined as >0.01% GFP+ tumor cells of all live cells in the flushed BM samples (Figure 1E,F, and Supporting Information S1: Figure 1A). The percentage of BM GFP+ cells in C57Bl/6 mice was significantly lower compared to KaLwRij (Figure 1F), also when excluding C57Bl/6 mice with no tumors (Supporting Information S1: Figure 1B). We used BM tumor burden at Day 21 to identify animals that might have stable versus progressive disease. The vast majority of KaLwRij mice (85%, 18 out of 21 mice) presented with progressive disease, defined as >5% GFP+ cells in the BM (Figure 1F,G). Conversely, in tumor‐bearing C57Bl/6 mice, the majority of mice had stable disease (77%, 10 out of 13; 0.01%–5% GFP of live BM cells), while a limited number of animals presented with progressive disease (Figure 1F,G and Supporting Information S1: Table I).

Although MM cells are rarely found in the spleen of patients, splenic dissemination (GFP+ cells >0.01% of all live spleen cells) is a well‐known feature of 5TGM1 cells when injected into KaLwRij mice 29 (Figure 1H) and is accompanied by a significant increase in spleen weight (Supporting Information S1: Figure 1C). In contrast, half of the tumor‐bearing C57Bl/6 mice (53.8%, 7 out of 13) had no splenic involvement (Figure 1H, Supporting Information S1: Table I and Supporting Information S1: Figure 1D); in the C57Bl/6 mice with splenic involvement, the splenic tumor load (defined as % of GFP+ cells) was significantly lower compared to KaLwRij mice (Supporting Information S1: Figure 1E). In KaLwRij mice, splenic tumor involvement showed a significant positive correlation with the BM tumor load (r 2 = 0.9271, p < 0.0001) while this was not the case in C57Bl/6 mice (r 2 = 0.0632, p = 0.3476) (Figure 1I).

Based on IgG2b levels, the kinetics of tumor development in C57Bl/6 mice seemed to lag by approximately 1 week compared to KaLwRij mice (Figure 1B). To define whether the lower disease burden observed in part of the C57Bl/6 mice was indeed stable disease, we extended the follow‐up period in C57Bl/6 mice. From Day 21 posttumor inoculation onward, mice were visually inspected two times per day, and upon the appearance of symptoms (described in the Materials and Methods section), animals were euthanized. Between Day 28 and Day 32, five out of 12 C57Bl/6 mice (41.7%) developed progressive disease (Figure 1J). These animals had a median of 2.6 mg/mL plasma IgG2b (Figure 1K, euthanized mice indicated with blue symbols) and a median of 20.3% GFP cells in the BM with three mice having values above 20% (Figure 1L,M). In addition, all five mice had developed splenic involvement (splenic GFP% > 0.01%) (Figure 1N). Correlation between BM and splenic GFP content proved to be significant, although with a low r 2 value (r 2 = 0.5720, p = 0.0044; Figure 1O,P).

The remaining seven mice never developed signs of distress and the experiment was concluded at 6 weeks posttransfer. Subsequent flow cytometric analyses indicated that two of the seven mice had a true stable disease (0.01%–5% tumor cells) with splenic involvement being below 0.01%. The other five mice had no detectable tumor cells (Figure 1K–P, mice with stable disease indicated with yellow symbols; mice with no tumor indicated with green symbols). Altogether, after 6 weeks, 58.3% of C57Bl/6 mice (7 out of 12) had developed a tumor, indicating that incidence at 6 weeks was similar to incidence at 3 weeks (Figure 1Q and Supporting Information S1: Table I). However, with time, a trend toward progressive disease became apparent (71.4%, 5 out of 7 mice vs. 23.1%, p = 0.0623).

In sum, these data indicate that approximately 60% of C57Bl/6 mice develop MM after intravenous transfer of 106 5TGM1 cells. The majority of mice have stable disease at 2–3 weeks post‐inoculation after which disease will progress in most animals. These findings suggest the possibility of initial immune‐mediated tumor control of MM progression.

Changes in BM NK cell composition in response to MM cells

To gain more insight into the alterations in BM immune cells in response to the presence of 5TGM1 MM cells, we analyzed the localization of T and NK cells using immunofluorescence microscopy. In both strains of mice, we observed the presence of NKp46+ NK cells and CD3+ T cells around dispersed GFP+ cells and also at the edges of large tumor foci (Figure 2A). Next, we generated single‐cell RNA sequencing (scRNA‐seq) libraries from a combined pool of BM NK and T cells (together with B cells, neutrophils and monocytes for future analyses, sorting strategy described in Supporting Information S1: Figure 2A). Cells were isolated from non‐injected C57Bl/6 and KaLwRij mice and from mice 21 days after intravenous injection of 5TGM1 cells that had developed stable (C57Bl/6) or progressive (KaLwRij) disease. Characteristics of the mice used for scRNA‐seq are in Supporting Information S1: Table II. After quality control and preprocessing, a total of 45,623 transcriptomes from 8 mice at two time points were obtained (Supporting Information S1: Figure 2B). We identified and subsetted NK (Ncr1) and T (Cd3e) cells in silico and integrated the objects of the respective cell types for downstream analyses (Supporting Information S1: Figure 2C).

The NK cell object contained 5126 cells in 10 clusters (Figure 2B), including small numbers of contaminating neutrophils (Ngp, Retnlg) and B cells (Ms4a1, Cd19), which were excluded from downstream analyses (Figure 2C). Continuous stages of NK cell development have been transcriptomically characterized in murine BM. 30 Based on this transcriptomic dataset, we identified proliferating NK cells (transcribing high levels of Mki67 and Stmn1), transitional NK1 cells (Eif5a, Mif, Nme1), transitional NK2 cells (Klra4, Klra8), transitional NK3 cells (Ccl3, Xcl1, Nr4a1), terminally differentiated NK cells (Irf8, Lgals1, Cma1), and IFN‐responsive NK cells (Isg15, Ifit3) (Figure 2C). Besides NK cells, we also observed a cluster of Tmem176a‐ and Tmem176b‐expressing ILCs (“Tmem176+ ILC”) and a cluster with low transcription of Tmem176a and Tmem176b, but transcription of other ILC1‐related genes (Il7r, Cxcr6, Ikzf2; “ILC1”). 31

Based on surface marker expression, NK cell maturation progresses from CD11blowCD27low cells, through upregulation of CD27 (CD11blowCD27high) and upregulation of CD11b (CD11bhighCD27high), before losing CD27 in the most mature cells (CD11bhighCD27low), 32 with CD11b expression serving as a marker of functional maturity. 33 Based on the gene expression levels of Itgam (encoding CD11b), Cd27, Klrg1, and S1pr5 (the latter two expressed on terminally differentiated NK cells 34 , 35 ) (Supporting Information S1: Figure 3A), we labeled the “Transitional NK1” and “Transitional NK3” clusters as immature NK cells (both being Cd11b‐), and the “Transitional NK2” (Cd11b+Cd27+) and “Terminally differentiated” (CD11bhighCD27low) clusters as mature NK cells, respectively (Supporting Information S1: Figure 3B). Split UMAP representations (Figure 2D) and enumerations (Figure 2E–G and Supporting Information S1: Figure 3C) revealed that at steady state the frequency of total mature NK cells was comparable between the two strains. In C57Bl/6 mice with stable disease, the percentage of mature NK cells had increased compared to steady state (Figure 2F,G). In sharp contrast, the frequency of mature NK cells in KaLwRij was lower compared to pre‐inoculation. Interestingly, this decrease was coupled to a significant increase of the “IFN responsive NK” cluster, a change that was absent from C57Bl/6 mice with stable disease.

We next set out to characterize the functional features of the NK cell transcriptome. NK cells can be grouped into cytotoxic and cytokine‐producing cells. 36 The frequency of mature NK cell transcribing genes encoding the cytotoxic proteins perforin (encoded by Prf1), granzyme A (encoded by Gzma), granzyme B (encoded by Gzmb), and Fasl (Figure 2H) had increased in stable disease, yet had decreased in mice with progressive disease (Figure 2H). Cytokine‐producing NK cells mainly secrete TNFα and IFNγ for both cytokines, and the number of positive cells showed a slight increase in stable disease, with minimal change observed in progressive disease (Figure 2I). Interestingly, the activation marker Cd69 was transcribed by mature “Transitional NK2” and “Terminally differentiated NK” clusters in stable disease, while during progression, Cd69 transcription was largely absent from these clusters, yet transcribed in the “IFN‐responsive NK,” “Transitional NK3,” and “ILC1” cluster (Figure 2J), suggestive of differential NK cell/ILC activation. We next generated violin plots to better quantify expression levels of these marker genes in the various NK clusters (Supporting Information S1: Figure 4). This further suggested differential activation of especially the “Terminally differentiated NK,” “Transitional NK3,” and “IFN responsive NK” clusters. IFNγ transcription was only detectable at reasonable levels in the “IFN responsive NK” cluster of C57Bl/6 with stable disease, while being absent from this cluster in KaLwRij mice.

Besides their preferential activation and expansion in progressive disease, cells in the “ILC1” cluster were also numerically overrepresented in KaLwRij compared to C57Bl/6 mice (Figure 2D,E). Although this cluster was characterized by genes associated with ILC1s (Ikzf2 [encoding Helios], Cxcr6, and Il7r) 31 (Figure 2C), feature plots suggested further heterogeneity within the cluster (Supporting Information S1: Figure 3D). Subsetting and reanalyzing this cluster (Supporting Information S1: Figure 3E) indeed revealed a cluster transcribing ILC1‐related genes, consisting mainly of cells derived from progressive disease; but also a second cluster resembling the “Transitional NK2” cluster from the original data set (named “Transitional NK2‐like”) (Supporting Information S1: Figure 3F). This “Transitional NK2‐like” cluster was characterized by transcription of cytotoxicity‐related genes and contained cells from both progressive and stable disease (Supporting Information S1: Figure 3F,G). Split UMAPs and enumeration of cluster contribution to the total NK cell pool revealed a clear overrepresentation of the “ILC1” cluster in progressive disease compared to stable disease at Day 21 (Supporting Information S1: Figure 3G,H).

Together, these transcriptomic data identify differential NK cell and ILC1 activation following the development of either stable or progressive MM.

MM progression is associated with loss of activated NK cells

As our transcriptomic analyses suggested differential NK cell responses associated with stable and progressive disease, we next analyzed the BM immune response to transferred tumor cells by flow cytometry. At steady state, total BM cellularity did not differ between C57Bl/6 and KaLwRij mice (Figure 3A), while in both strains, progressive disease at Day 21 was accompanied by a significant decrease in the number of non‐tumor BM cells. At steady state, NK cell (NKp46+CD3−CD19−) frequencies, but not absolute numbers, were significantly higher in C57Bl/6 mice (Figure 3B), and this was maintained in animals with stable disease. In contrast, progressive disease in KaLwRij and C57Bl/6 mice was associated with a relative and absolute reduction of NK cells (Figure 3B).

To better understand NK differences, we first set out to identify NK cell populations differentially present at steady state. The percentage of mature CD11b+ NK cells (corresponding with the mature “Transitional NK2” and “Terminally differentiated” NK clusters from the scRNA‐seq data set) was significantly higher in C57Bl/6 mice at steady state compared to KaLwRij (Figure 3C,F,I), and this difference was due to alterations in the highly cytotoxic CD11bhighCD27high NK cell subset 33 (Figure 3J and Supporting Information S1: Figure 5A,B).

The development of stable disease in C57Bl/6 mice coincided with a significant increase in both relative and absolute numbers of mature CD11b+ NK cells (Figure 3D,G,I). In contrast, the frequency and absolute number of total CD11b+ and CD11bhighCD27high NK cells were reduced in mice with progressive disease (Figure 3E,H,I,J). Inversely, the percentage of CD11b− NK cells (corresponding with the immature “Transitional NK1,” “Transitional NK3,” and the “Tmem176+ ILC” and “ILC1” subsets from the transcriptomic data) increased in mice with progressive disease and was higher in KaLwRij compared to C57Bl/6 mice (Supporting Information S1: Figure 5C). However, this relative increase was not coupled to an increase in absolute numbers of CD11b− cells.

To analyze whether the differential kinetics of NK cells were also related to the altered activation that we had observed on the transcriptome level, we determined CD69 protein expression on mature NK cells as a proxy for cellular activation 37 (Figure 3K–M and Supporting Information S1: Figure 5D). The percentage and absolute number of activated total mature CD11b+ NK cells (Figure 3N) and in specific activated mature CD11bhighCD27high NK cells (Figure 3O) increased in C57Bl/6 mice with stable disease. In contrast, progressive disease was associated with a loss of absolute numbers of activated NK cells, despite higher percentages (Figure 3N,O and Supporting Information S1: Figure 5E,F). The changes in relative and absolute numbers of CD11b−CD69+ NK cells were similar to those observed in the CD11b+ NK cells (Supporting Information S1: Figure 5G).

In summary, these results suggest that disease progression in the 5TGM1 MM model is associated with changes in NK cell frequencies and activation status.

MM progression is associated with loss of activated CD8+ T cells

We next investigated whether similar associations between cellular activation and tumor behavior were apparent for cytotoxic T cells. In our scRNA‐seq data sets, variations in the number of both sorted and in silico recovered cells per mouse were highly variable (in part due to the combined sorting of T and B cells), precluding reliable analyses of the T cell compartment on the RNA levels (Supporting Information S1: Table II). We therefore opted for T cell analyses by flow cytometry.

At steady state, both the percentage (of all T cells) and the absolute number of BM CD8+ T cells were higher in C57Bl/6 mice compared to KaLwRij (Figure 4A). During stable disease in C57Bl/6, relative numbers of CD8+ T cells remained constant, with a slight increase in absolute numbers explained by an increased number of total T cells (Supporting Information S1: Figure 6A,B). Progressive disease was accompanied by a significant decrease in the absolute numbers of CD8+ T cells (Figure 4A). A similar pattern was apparent in KaLwRij, where the progressive disease was associated with a further decrease in numbers and percentages of CD8+ T cells (Figure 4A). In contrast, we did not observe significant changes among CD4+ T (helper) cells throughout the disease course (Supporting Information S1: Figure 6C), although number of replicates was lower in these analyses.

At steady state, most cytotoxic T cells lacked signs of cellular activation as measured by CD69 expression (Figure 4B,C). In mice with stable disease, the percentage and absolute numbers of activated CD8+ T cells had increased, and progressive disease was associated with a further increase in percentages of CD69+ cytotoxic T cells, but a reduction in absolute numbers (Figure 4B,D,E). Nonetheless, inter‐mouse variability in this group was high. Interestingly, CD69 expression levels on CD8+ T cells during stable disease were moderately positive (Figure 4D). In contrast, in mice with progressive disease, especially those with the highest tumor burden, cytotoxic T cells expressed CD69 at higher levels (Figure 4E,F), suggestive of altered or incomplete activation. To further characterize BM CD69+ cytotoxic T cells, other commonly used activation markers were analyzed. During stable disease, the majority of CD69+ CD8+ T cells in C57Bl/6 mice had an activated CD44+L‐Selectin (Lsel)‐ phenotype, with varying expression of PD1 and Tim‐3 (Figure 4G–I). In contrast, progressive disease in KaLwRij mice was associated with reduced downregulation of surface L‐Selectin+ and virtual absence of PD1 and Tim‐3 (Figure 4G–I). The same discrepancy was seen when total CD8+ T cells were analyzed (Supporting Information S1: Figure 6D). There was no association between splenic tumor burden and alterations in splenic cytotoxic immune cells (Supporting Information S1: Figure 6E–G).

IFN signaling in the BM microenvironment during stable disease

Analyses of the BM immune response to 5TGM1 cells indicated that alterations in the cytotoxic immune response were associated with disease progression. To gain further insights into the mechanistic underpinnings of anti‐myeloma immune responses, we purified BM NK cells, cytotoxic T cells, and CD45−CD31−Ter119−CD71− MSCs from steady state and Day 21 post 5TGM1‐inoculation C57Bl/6 mice with stable disease (sorting strategy in Supporting Information S1: Figure 7A,B) and performed bulk mRNA‐sequencing. Ranked gene set enrichment analysis (GSEA) on differentially expressed genes between steady state and stable disease was performed to identify pathways and genes associated with tumor responses. In NK cells of mice with stable disease, pathways related to metabolism and cell cycle were enriched (Figure 5A). In addition, GSEA indicated activation of immune effector pathways including “Interferon gamma response” and “Interferon alpha response” (Figure 5A–D and Supporting Information S1: Figure 7C). The “Interferon gamma response” and “Interferon alpha response” gene sets were also activated in cytotoxic T cells (Figure 5E–H and Supporting Information S1: Figure 7D) and MSCs (Supporting Information S1: Figure 7F–I). Projection of both the IFNα and IFNγ genesets on the scRNA sequencing data sets from tumor‐bearing mice indicated enrichment of these genes in the IFN‐responsive NK cell cluster (Supporting Information S1: Figure 8A). To distinguish the IFNα and IFNγ responses and define the relative contribution of the two pathways, we first analyzed the contribution of unique and overlapping genes to the enrichment score (Supporting Information S1: Figure 8B–D). This indicated the preferential presence of genes from the IFNγ response pathway in NK cells and T cells, while MSCs were enriched for IFNα response genes. To better quantify these differences, we generated new gene sets containing only the unique genes for each pathway. In NK and CD8+ T cells from mice with stable disease, the “Interferon gamma response” excluding genes from the “Interferon alpha response” remained enriched compared to the steady state (Figure 5I,J), while the “Interferon alpha response” without overlapping genes was not significantly altered (Figure 5I,J). In MSCs, we found a reversed pattern (Supporting Information S1: Figure 8E), with only IFN alpha genes significantly enriched. Published human IFN‐response signature genes from either NK cells or MM cells 38 , 39 were significantly increased in NK (Figure 5K) and CD8+ T cells (Supporting Information S1: Figure 8F) from mice with stable disease, while only showing a non‐significant trend of increase in MSCs (Supporting Information S1: Figure 8G) compared to steady state.

Together, these data suggest that the MM presence evokes a compound IFN response with IFNγ pathways being dominant in NK cells and CD8+ T cells, while IFNα responses are more prominent in MSCs.

IFNγ limits the incidence and progression of 5TGM1 myeloma

To compare our murine results to human disease, we created a single‐cell transcriptomic data set of BM NK cells from five non‐tumor controls and six MM patients relapsing after first‐line treatment with bortezomib, thalidomide, and dexamethasone with or without anti‐CD38 antibodies (average age of control: 75 ± 2.86 years, average age of relapse patients: 50.33 ± 3.84 years [mean ± SEM], p = 0.0087; patient characteristics described in Supporting Information S1: Table III). We obtained a total of 12,914 NK cells (control: 9146; relapse: 3768) and identified seven NK cell clusters (Figure 6A) which we annotated using previously reported canonical markers 24 , 40 , 41 (Supporting Information S1: Table IV). Annotation included a cluster defined by IFN‐response genes (IFN‐responsive NK), similar to that reported in Zavidij et al. 8 Permutation analyses revealed that the IFN‐responsive NK cluster was among the significantly increased clusters in BM of relapse patients compared to control samples (Figure 6B,C), in line with the increase in NK cells and the increased presence of IFN‐response signatures in BM of mice following transfer of 5TGM1 MM cells.

As transcriptomic data from both mouse and human BM samples converged on IFNγ signaling, we set out to test the contribution of the IFNγ pathway to anti‐myeloma immune responses in vivo. C57Bl/6 mice received IFNγ‐neutralizing antibodies or isotype control intraperitonially on Day 0 (prior to 5TGM1 transfer) and on Days 3, 7, 10, 14, and 17, before being sacrificed on Day 21 (Figure 6D). Following IFNγ‐neutralization, serum IgG2b levels at Day 21 were significantly increased compared to isotype controls (Figure 6E). The increase in percentages of mice with progressive disease (58.3%, 14 out of 24 vs. 25%, p = 0.0815, 3 out of 12) and splenic involvement (66.7%, 16 out of 24 vs. 33.3%, 4 out of 12) did not reach statistical significance, yet importantly, BM tumor burden (Figure 6F) and splenic tumor load (Figure 6G) were increased upon IFNγ neutralization. Of note, tumor incidence was also increased after IFNγ neutralization (85.7%, 24 out of 28 vs. 54.5%, 12 out of 22, p = 0.0253) (Figure 6H and Supporting Information S1: Table V). This latter finding suggests that the absence of tumor development in part of the C57Bl/6 mice in our previous experiments was the result of rapid immune‐mediated eradication of the transferred cells. Together, these results identify IFNγ as a relevant mediator of successful anti‐myeloma immune responses in the 5TGM1 transfer model.

Finally, since IFN responses were enriched in both NK cells and CD8+ T cells, we set out to investigate whether NK cells are necessary for successful IFNγ−mediated anti‐tumor defense in the 5TGM1 model. To this end, NK cells were depleted from C57Bl/6 mice that had received 5TGM1 cells by ip injection of anti‐NK1.1 antibodies every other day between Days 7 and 21 post‐tumor cell inoculation (experimental setup shown in Supporting Information S1: Figure 8H). On average, the depletion efficiency of BM NK cells was over 87% compared to isotype control‐treated mice (Supporting Information S1: Figure 8I,J). Three weeks after 5TGM1 cell transfer, tumor load (measured either as IgG2b levels from serum, Supporting Information S1: Figure 8K; or as BM GFP content, Supporting Information S1: Figure 8L) did not differ between NK‐depleted and control mice. In addition, the distribution of mice with no tumor, stable disease, or progressive disease was comparable between the two conditions and in line with previous experiments (Supporting Information S1: Figure 8M and Supporting Information S1: Table VI). These results suggest that IFNγ‐driven anti‐MM immunity in this mouse model is not exclusively dependent on NK cells.

DISCUSSION

The tumor immune microenvironment is increasingly recognized as a determinant of tumor progression and therapy response. Here, we analyzed the immune response associated with stable versus progressive disease in the murine 5TGM1 MM model.

While MM develops in 100% of injected KaLwRij mice, the parental C57Bl/6 strain is less permissive to tumor development. 16 We hypothesized that the difference in disease onset and severity between these two strains could be due to differential immune control of the transferred cells. Already at steady state, KaLwRij mice had a lower number of mature BM NK cells, raising the possibility of an impaired first response to tumor cells. Functional defects and exhaustion of BM NK cells in newly diagnosed MM patients have been shown to correlate with poor survival and/or response to immunotherapies. 42 , 43 , 44 In the 5TGM1 system, MM development is associated with the activation of NK cells and CD8+ T cells, yet the progression of disease coincides with a decrease of these activated cell populations. This could be the result of failure to expand or decreased recruitment from the circulation. The latter is supported by reduced chemokine‐mediated recruitment of NK cells in KaLwRij mice. 45 Our findings on NK cells are in line with recent human results showing increased numbers of NK cells in the early stages of MM (SMM and NDMM). 8

Disease progression was accompanied by altered CD8+ T cell activation, evidenced by L‐Selectin expression and absence of PD‐1. Surface expression of PD‐1 is a feature of exhausted T cells, 46 but importantly, also identifies tumor‐specific cells in solid tumors. 47 Our findings diverge from recent data by Fernandez‐Poma et al. 48 who identified and isolated PD‐1+ cytotoxic T cells from tumor‐bearing KaLwRij BM. This discrepancy might be due to experimental differences, as fivefold more 5TGM1‐GFP cells were injected, yet time to tumor development was doubled and tumor load and IgG2b levels were much lower compared to our findings described here. 48 A more detailed analysis of CD8+ T cells under various conditions of tumor inoculation will be required to reconcile these findings.

Analyses of BM cells from human MM patients 7 , 8 , 9 and Vk‐Myc mice 49 have consistently identified IFN‐responsive cell subsets. We extend these findings by presenting data on the presence of a transcriptionally defined cluster of IFN‐responsive NK cells, that was increased in frequency in the BM of MM patients relapsing after first‐line triplet or quadruplet treatment. IFNγ signaling also emerged as a central immune pathway from our transcriptomic studies, and neutralization of IFNγ significantly impacted disease development. Importantly, the increased tumor incidence after IFNγ neutralization suggests that this cytokine is involved in the early immune clearance of MM cells, leading to the prevention of measurable tumor outgrowth in approximately 30% of mice. IFNγ can be secreted by many different cell types within the tumor microenvironment, including NK cells 50 and cytotoxic T cells, 51 but also TH1 cells, TH17 cells, and γδ T cells [reviewed in Gocher et al. 52 ]. The similar incidence and severity of disease in NK‐depleted and control animals suggests that in the 5TGM1 model, NK cells are not the sole source of IFNγ. Downstream effects of IFNγ are broad and include direct anti‐tumor responses, activation of cytotoxic T cells 53 and NK cells, 54 and recruitment of effector cytotoxic cells by induction of CXCL9, CXCL10, and CXCL11 in the tumor microenvironment. 55 Importantly, blockade of IFNγ (and subsequent cytotoxic effects) had a result opposite to the previously described inhibition of Type I IFN (by blockade of IFN α and β receptor 1, IFNAR1). The latter prevented the formation of a pro‐myeloma BM environment induced by myeloma‐associated IFNAR1+ regulatory T cells, resulting in decreased MM progression. 56 It will be of interest to investigate a potential role for IFNα‐responsive BM MSCs in the formation of this pro‐MM environment.

Importantly, an IFN‐response signature was upregulated in malignant tumor cells of MM patients whose microenvironment also exhibited increased IFN‐induced genes, 39 again suggesting active IFN pathways during the course of the disease. Furthermore, an IFN‐responsive cluster of BM antibody‐secreting cells has been identified in healthy samples. 38 Although our investigation did not extend to transcriptomic analysis of 5TGM1 cells, the IFN‐responsive genes identified in these studies were also upregulated in NK cells and cytotoxic T cells from mice with stable disease.

In summary, we show that a subset of C57Bl/6 mice develops a stable disease for up to 5 weeks after the transfer of 5TGM1 cells. The development of progressive disease is accompanied by profound cellular and molecular changes in cytotoxic immune cell subsets. We also provide evidence of IFNγ‐mediated tumor immunological eradication and control during the early phases post‐inoculation, providing a novel window of opportunity for studying early anti‐myeloma immunity. Interestingly, our data also show that anti‐myeloma immune responses in KaLwRij mice differ from those in the parental C57Bl/6 strain. This will be an important consideration in studies necessitating a functional immune system. Finally, as most immunologically relevant transgenic and knockout mice are in the C57Bl/6 genetic background, the ability to use C57Bl/6 mice for MM studies will expand the possibilities of manipulating the BM immune microenvironment in order to better understand its role in the progression and therapy response of MM.
