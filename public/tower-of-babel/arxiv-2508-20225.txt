Optimal Quoting under Adverse Selection and Price Reading
Alexander Barzykin, Philippe Bergault, Olivier Guéant, Malo Lemmel

Abstract
Over the past decade, many dealers have implemented algorithmic models to automatically respond to RFQs and manage flows originating from their electronic platforms. In parallel, building on the foundational work of Ho and Stoll, and later Avellaneda and Stoikov, the academic literature on market making has expanded to address trade size distributions, client tiering, complex price dynamics, alpha signals, and the internalization versus externalization dilemma in markets with dealer-to-client and interdealer-broker segments. In this paper, we tackle two critical dimensions: adverse selection, arising from the presence of informed traders, and price reading, whereby the market maker’s own quotes inadvertently reveal the direction of their inventory. These risks are well known to practitioners, who routinely face informed flows and algorithms capable of extracting signals from quoting behavior. Yet they have received limited attention in the quantitative finance literature, beyond stylized toy models with limited actionability. Extending the existing literature, we propose a tractable and implementable framework that enables market makers to adjust their quotes with greater awareness of informational risk.



Key words:
Market Making, Stochastic Optimal Control, Adverse Selection, Price Reading, Informational Risk.

Model Setup

Throughout this paper, we work on a filtered probability space
\displaystyle\left(\Omega,\mathcal{F},\mathbb{P};\mathbb{F}=\left(\mathcal{F}_{t}\right)_{t\geq 0}\right)
satisfying the usual conditions. We assume that this space is rich enough to support all the stochastic processes introduced in the model.

2.1
Modeling Framework and Notation

We consider a market maker responsible for quoting bid and ask prices on a single asset. The market maker continuously sets quotes and can stream different pricing ladders to different categories of clients. Specifically, clients are divided into
\displaystyle N
tiers, indexed by the set
\displaystyle\mathcal{N}:=\{1,\ldots,N\}
.

Transaction sizes are assumed to be discrete, taking values
\displaystyle 0<\Delta^{1}<\ldots<\Delta^{K}
for some
\displaystyle K\geq 1
, with index set
\displaystyle\mathcal{K}:=\{1,\ldots,K\}
. We assume
\displaystyle\Delta^{k}\in\Delta^{1}\mathbb{N}
for all
\displaystyle k\in\mathcal{K}
, i.e., each size is an integer multiple of the smallest one.

For each tier
\displaystyle n\in\mathcal{N}
and size index
\displaystyle k\in\mathcal{K}
, the bid and ask prices quoted at time
\displaystyle t
are modeled by stochastic processes
\displaystyle\big(S^{n,k,b}_{t}\big)_{t\geq 0}
and
\displaystyle\big(S^{n,k,a}_{t}\big)_{t\geq 0}
, respectively. These quotes determine the distribution of trade arrivals, which will be formalized later.

We introduce a reference price process
\displaystyle\big(S_{t}\big)_{t\geq 0}
, representing a composite price computed by the market maker based on public and private information. It evolves under both exogenous volatility and endogenous reactions to trading activity and quote asymmetry. Its dynamics is given by:

\displaystyle\displaystyle dS_{t}=\

\displaystyle\displaystyle\sigma\,dB_{t}-\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}n\in\mathcal{N}\\
k\in\mathcal{K}\end{subarray}}\left(\tilde{\zeta}^{n,k}\left(S_{t}-S^{n,k,b}_{t}\right)\,dN^{n,k,b}_{t}-\tilde{\zeta}^{n,k}\left(S^{n,k,a}_{t}-S_{t}\right)\,dN^{n,k,a}_{t}\right)

(1)

\displaystyle\displaystyle+\sum_{n\in\mathcal{N}}\tilde{J}^{n}\left(\sum_{k\in\mathcal{K}}w^{n,k}\left(S^{n,k,a}_{t}-2S_{t}+S^{n,k,b}_{t}\right)\right)\,dt,\qquad S_{0}\text{ given,}

where
\displaystyle\sigma>0
is a volatility parameter and
\displaystyle\big(B_{t}\big)_{t\geq 0}
is a standard Brownian motion adapted to the filtration
\displaystyle\left(\mathcal{F}_{t}\right)_{t\geq 0}
. The point processes
\displaystyle\big(N^{n,k,b}_{t}\big)_{t\geq 0}
and
\displaystyle\big(N^{n,k,a}_{t}\big)_{t\geq 0}
represent the number of trades of size
\displaystyle\Delta^{k}
at the bid and ask with clients from tier
\displaystyle n
, respectively.

The model includes two key features that govern the endogenous reaction of the reference price to trade flow and quoting behavior:

•

Adverse selection.
For each
\displaystyle(n,k)\in\mathcal{N}\times\mathcal{K}
, the function
\displaystyle\tilde{\zeta}^{n,k}:\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R}_{+}
is non-decreasing, and satisfies

\displaystyle\lim_{\delta\to-\infty}\tilde{\zeta}^{n,k}(\delta)=0.

This function captures the impact of informed trading on prices. It is tier-specific (and possibly size-specific), allowing the market maker to account for varying levels of expected informativeness across clients (and transaction sizes). In particular, when
\displaystyle\tilde{\zeta}^{n,k}
is a rapidly decreasing function, executions at prices that deviate significantly from the reference price in the “wrong” direction – that is, at prices seemingly unfavorable to the client from the market maker’s perspective – can have a strong impact on the reference price.

•

Price reading.
For each
\displaystyle n\in\mathcal{N}
, the function
\displaystyle\tilde{J}^{n}:\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R}
is non-decreasing and satisfies
\displaystyle\tilde{J}^{n}(0)=0
. It models the way the reference price reacts to asymmetry in the quotes streamed to tier
\displaystyle n
. This asymmetry, or skew, is computed as a weighted average over the quote skewness for each trade size
\displaystyle\Delta^{k}
, where the inner term
\displaystyle S^{n,k,a}_{t}-2S_{t}+S^{n,k,b}_{t}
captures the relative placement of bid and ask quotes with respect to the reference price. The weights
\displaystyle w^{n,\cdot}
control the contribution of each size in the price ladder to the total skew associated with the quotes streamed to clients of tier
\displaystyle n
. This mechanism captures the phenomenon commonly referred to as price reading or skew sniffing, in which certain market participants infer the direction – or evolution – of the market maker’s inventory from the quote ladder structure and adjust their trading behavior accordingly, generating a feedback loop that moves the reference price.

Remark 2
.

Although the reference price – whose dynamics is given by Eq. (
1
) – is primarily driven by its exogenous Brownian component, it also depends on the market maker’s clients’ flows and own quoting behavior. As such, it is a price specific to the market maker under consideration. In light of this, it may seem questionable at first glance to define the skew terms in Eq. (
1

This modeling choice is, in fact, a conservative one: we assume that the market maker never benefits from any informational advantage, even though they observe clients’ flows. While restrictive, this assumption appears natural in the context of a paper focused on the informational risk faced by market makers. It is recognized that adverse selection can originate from the market maker not knowing the true reference price at the right time due to natural market fragmentation and the ensuing latency.

The market maker’s cash process
\displaystyle\left(X_{t}\right)_{t\geq 0}
evolves according to:

\displaystyle dX_{t}

\displaystyle=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}n\in\mathcal{N}\\
k\in\mathcal{K}\end{subarray}}\Delta^{k}\left(-S^{n,k,b}_{t}\,dN^{n,k,b}_{t}+S^{n,k,a}_{t}\,dN^{n,k,a}_{t}\right)

(4)

\displaystyle\delta^{n,k,b}_{t}:=S_{t}-S^{n,k,b}_{t}

\displaystyle\delta^{n,k,a}_{t}:=S^{n,k,a}_{t}-S_{t}

\displaystyle S_{t}

2.2
The Optimization Problem

In the above discussion, we introduced the three processes central to most market making models: the reference price process
\displaystyle\left(S_{t}\right)_{t\geq 0}
, the inventory process
\displaystyle\left(q_{t}\right)_{t\geq 0}
, and the cash process
\displaystyle\left(X_{t}\right)_{t\geq 0}
.

Assuming that the market maker continuously marks their inventory to the reference price, the dynamics of the profit and loss process
\displaystyle\left(\mathrm{PnL}_{t}\right)_{t\geq 0}
of the market maker can be computed from Eqs. (
1
), (
2
) and (
4
):

\displaystyle\displaystyle d\mathrm{PnL}_{t}

\displaystyle\displaystyle=dX_{t}+S_{t}\,dq_{t}+q_{t-}\,dS_{t}+d\langle q_{t},S_{t}\rangle

\displaystyle\displaystyle=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}n\in\mathcal{N}\\
k\in\mathcal{K}\end{subarray}}\left(\big(\Delta^{k}\delta^{n,k,b}_{t}-(q_{t-}+\Delta^{k})\,\tilde{\zeta}^{n,k}(\delta^{n,k,b}_{t})\big)dN^{n,k,b}_{t}+\big(\Delta^{k}\delta^{n,k,a}_{t}+(q_{t-}-\Delta^{k})\,\tilde{\zeta}^{n,k}(\delta^{n,k,a}_{t})\big)dN^{n,k,a}_{t}\right)

\displaystyle\displaystyle\quad+q_{t-}\,\sigma\,dB_{t}+q_{t-}\sum_{n\in\mathcal{N}}\tilde{J}^{n}\left(\sum_{k\in\mathcal{K}}w^{n,k}\left(\delta^{n,k,a}_{t}-\delta^{n,k,b}_{t}\right)\right)dt.

A classical optimization approach is then to consider a finite-horizon objective, where the market maker maximizes the expected PnL at a given time
\displaystyle T
, while penalizing inventory risk over the interval
\displaystyle[0,T]
. This leads to the following risk-adjusted expected PnL objective, common in the works of Cartea, Jaimungal, and coauthors (see e.g.
[
8
]

\mathbb{E}\left[\mathrm{PnL}_{T}-\frac{1}{2}\gamma\int_{0}^{T}\sigma^{2}q_{t}^{2}\,dt\right],

maximized over the family of predictable and bounded-from-below control processes
\displaystyle\left(\left(\delta_{t}^{n,k,b},\delta_{t}^{n,k,a}\right)_{t\geq 0}\right)_{(n,k)\in\mathcal{N}\times\mathcal{K}}
. See also
[
13
]
for a broader perspective on market making objectives.

In this paper, we instead adopt a stationary infinite-horizon perspective,
2

2

In market making models, the focus naturally lies on the optimal behavior far from the terminal time (which is often arbitrary). In the analysis that follows, we use Taylor expansions to capture the first-order impact of informational risk, working directly in an infinite-horizon framework. Performing these expansions in a finite-horizon setting and then taking the limit as
\displaystyle T\to+\infty

and consider the discounted objective:

\mathbb{E}\left[\int_{0}^{+\infty}e^{-\rho t}\left(d\mathrm{PnL}_{t}-\frac{\gamma}{2}\sigma^{2}q_{t}^{2}\,dt\right)\right],

\displaystyle\rho>0

Using the form of intensities given by Eq. (

\mathbb{E}\left[\int_{0}^{+\infty}e^{-\rho t}\left(\sum_{n\in\mathcal{N}}\beta^{n}\left(q_{t-},\delta^{n,\cdot,b}_{t},\delta^{n,\cdot,a}_{t}\right)-\frac{\gamma}{2}\sigma^{2}q_{t}^{2}\right)dt\right],

(5)

where for each
\displaystyle n\in\mathcal{N}
, the function
\displaystyle\beta^{n}

\displaystyle\displaystyle\beta^{n}\Big(q,\delta^{n,\cdot,b},\delta^{n,\cdot,a}\Big)

\displaystyle\displaystyle=q\,\tilde{J}^{n}\left(\sum_{k\in\mathcal{K}}w^{n,k}\left(\delta^{n,k,a}-\delta^{n,k,b}\right)\right)

\displaystyle\displaystyle\quad+\sum_{k\in\mathcal{K}}\Bigg(\Delta^{k}\,\Lambda^{n,k,b}\left(\delta^{n,k,b}\right)\left(\delta^{n,k,b}-\frac{q+\Delta^{k}}{\Delta^{k}}\tilde{\zeta}^{n,k}\left(\delta^{n,k,b}\right)\right)

\displaystyle\displaystyle\qquad\qquad+\Delta^{k}\,\Lambda^{n,k,a}\left(\delta^{n,k,a}\right)\left(\delta^{n,k,a}+\frac{q-\Delta^{k}}{\Delta^{k}}\tilde{\zeta}^{n,k}\left(\delta^{n,k,a}\right)\right)\Bigg).

3
Informational Risk: A First-Order Perspective

The aim of this section is to understand the influence of informational risk on the optimal behavior of a market maker. To this end, we analyze the optimal control problem associated with the objective function in Eq. (
5
). Rather than solving the problem fully, we aim to characterize the first-order effects of informational risk. We begin by presenting the Hamilton-Jacobi-Bellman (HJB) equation associated with the general model, then recall classical results in the absence of informational risk, and finally compute first-order adjustments.

3.1
The Hamilton-Jacobi-Bellman Equation

The HJB equation corresponding to the infinite horizon problem (
5
) is a nonlinear system for the value function

\displaystyle\vartheta:\Delta^{1}\mathbb{Z}\to\mathbb{R}
, given by:

\displaystyle\displaystyle 0

\displaystyle\displaystyle=-\rho\,\vartheta(q)-\frac{1}{2}\gamma\sigma^{2}q^{2}+\sum_{n\in\mathcal{N}}\tilde{\mathcal{H}}^{n}\Bigl[\tilde{J}^{n},\tilde{\zeta}^{n,\cdot}\Bigr]\left(q,D^{\cdot}_{+}\vartheta(q),D^{\cdot}_{-}\vartheta(q)\right),

(6)

where the finite-difference operators are defined, for all
\displaystyle k\in\mathcal{K}
, by

D^{k}_{+}\vartheta(q):=\frac{\vartheta(q)-\vartheta(q+\Delta^{k})}{\Delta^{k}}\quad\text{and}\quad D^{k}_{-}\vartheta(q):=\frac{\vartheta(q)-\vartheta(q-\Delta^{k})}{\Delta^{k}},

and for each
\displaystyle n\in\mathcal{N}
, the Hamiltonian
\displaystyle\tilde{\mathcal{H}}^{n}
is given by

\displaystyle\displaystyle\tilde{\mathcal{H}}^{n}\left[\tilde{J}^{n},\tilde{\zeta}^{n,\cdot}\right]\left(q,p^{\cdot,b},p^{\cdot,a}\right):=\ \sup_{\delta^{n,\cdot,b},\delta^{n,\cdot,a}}\Bigg\{\beta^{n}\left(q,\delta^{n,\cdot,b},\delta^{n,\cdot,a}\right)

\displaystyle\displaystyle\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad-\sum_{k\in\mathcal{K}}\Delta^{k}\left(\Lambda^{n,k,b}(\delta^{n,k,b})\,p^{k,b}+\Lambda^{n,k,a}(\delta^{n,k,a})\,p^{k,a}\right)\Bigg\}.

When they exist, we denote by
\displaystyle\tilde{\mathfrak{d}}^{n,\cdot,b/a*}(q,p^{\cdot,b},p^{\cdot,a})
the maximizers in the above definition. The optimal quotes for tier
\displaystyle n
in feedback form, denoted by
\displaystyle\mathfrak{d}^{n,\cdot,b/a*}(q)
, are then given by
\displaystyle\tilde{\mathfrak{d}}^{n,\cdot,b/a*}\left(q,D^{\cdot}_{+}\vartheta(q),D^{\cdot}_{-}\vartheta(q)\right)
.

Remark 3
.

The value function
\displaystyle\vartheta
and the optimal quotes depend on the discount rate
\displaystyle\rho
whose value may appear arbitrary. When
\displaystyle\rho\to 0
, the value function blows up, but
\displaystyle\rho\vartheta
converges towards a finite value called the ergodic constant of the problem. More importantly, finite differences of the value function, i.e. terms of the forms
\displaystyle D^{k}_{+}\vartheta(q)
and
\displaystyle D^{k}_{-}\vartheta(q)
, converge, hence the convergence of optimal quotes when
\displaystyle\rho\to 0
.

3.2
The Baseline without Informational Risk

Solving Eq. (
6
) in full generality requires the introduction of assumptions on the functions
\displaystyle\tilde{J}^{\cdot}
and
\displaystyle\tilde{\zeta}^{\cdot,\cdot}
. Since our objective is instead to investigate first-order informational effects, we begin with the classical baseline case where there is no informational risk – that is, we assume
\displaystyle\tilde{J}^{n}=0
and
\displaystyle\tilde{\zeta}^{n,k}=0
for all
\displaystyle(n,k)\in\mathcal{N}\times\mathcal{K}

\displaystyle\theta:\Delta^{1}\mathbb{Z}\to\mathbb{R}
solves the simpler HJB equation:

0=\ -\rho\,\theta(q)-\frac{1}{2}\gamma\sigma^{2}q^{2}+\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}n\in\mathcal{N}\\
k\in\mathcal{K}\end{subarray}}\Delta^{k}H^{n,k,b}\left(D^{k}_{+}\theta(q)\right)+\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}n\in\mathcal{N}\\
k\in\mathcal{K}\end{subarray}}\Delta^{k}H^{n,k,a}\left(D^{k}_{-}\theta(q)\right),

where the Hamiltonian functions are defined, for all
\displaystyle(n,k)\in\mathcal{N}\times\mathcal{K}
, by

H^{n,k,b/a}(p):=\sup_{\delta\in\mathbb{R}}\Lambda^{n,k,b/a}(\delta)\,(\delta-p),

and the associated maximizers
3
3

3

They verify the simple ordinary differential equation
\displaystyle\tilde{\delta}^{n,k,b/a*^{\prime}}(p)=\frac{1}{c^{n,k,b/a}(\tilde{\delta}^{n,k,b/a*}(p))}
.
are given by

\tilde{\delta}^{n,k,b/a*}(p):=\text{argmax}_{\delta\in\mathbb{R}}\Lambda^{n,k,b/a}(\delta)\,(\delta-p)=\left(\Lambda^{n,k,b/a}\right)^{-1}\left(-{H^{n,k,b/a}}^{\prime}(p)\right).

Given the baseline value function
\displaystyle\theta

\displaystyle(n,k)\in\mathcal{N}\times\mathcal{K}

3.3
First-Order Effects

3.3.1
Framework

In order to study the first-order effects of adverse selection and price reading, we introduce a perturbation parameter
\displaystyle\varepsilon>0
and set

\tilde{J}^{n}(x)=\varepsilon J^{n}(x)\quad\text{for all }n\in\mathcal{N}\qquad\text{and}\qquad\tilde{\zeta}^{n,k}(\delta)=\varepsilon\zeta^{n,k}(\delta)\quad\text{for all }(n,k)\in\mathcal{N}\times\mathcal{K}.

For notational convenience, we define the
\displaystyle\varepsilon
-dependent Hamiltonian functions by

\mathcal{H}^{n}\left(q,p^{\cdot,b},p^{\cdot,a},\varepsilon\right):=\ \tilde{\mathcal{H}}^{n}\left[\varepsilon J^{n},\varepsilon\zeta^{n,\cdot}\right]\left(q,p^{\cdot,b},p^{\cdot,a}\right)=\ \sup_{\delta^{n,\cdot,b},\delta^{n,\cdot,a}}\mathcal{B}^{n}\Big(q,p^{\cdot,b},p^{\cdot,a},\delta^{n,\cdot,b},\delta^{n,\cdot,a},\varepsilon\Big),

where

\displaystyle\displaystyle\mathcal{B}^{n}\Big(

\displaystyle\displaystyle q,p^{\cdot,b},p^{\cdot,a},\delta^{n,\cdot,b},\delta^{n,\cdot,a},\varepsilon\Big):=\ q\,\varepsilon J^{n}\left(\sum_{k\in\mathcal{K}}w^{n,k}\left(\delta^{n,k,a}-\delta^{n,k,b}\right)\right)

\displaystyle\displaystyle+\sum_{k\in\mathcal{K}}\Delta^{k}\,\Lambda^{n,k,b}\left(\delta^{n,k,b}\right)\left(\delta^{n,k,b}-p^{k,b}-\frac{q+\Delta^{k}}{\Delta^{k}}\varepsilon\zeta^{n,k}\left(\delta^{n,k,b}\right)\right)

\displaystyle\displaystyle+\sum_{k\in\mathcal{K}}\Delta^{k}\,\Lambda^{n,k,a}\left(\delta^{n,k,a}\right)\left(\delta^{n,k,a}-p^{k,a}+\frac{q-\Delta^{k}}{\Delta^{k}}\varepsilon\zeta^{n,k}\left(\delta^{n,k,a}\right)\right).

3.3.2
Value Function

We then seek a solution to Eq. (
6

\vartheta(q)=\theta(q)+\varepsilon f(q)+o(\varepsilon),

where
\displaystyle f
captures the first-order impact of informational effects on the value function.

To compute the equation for
\displaystyle f
, we need the gradient of the Hamiltonian functions. Notice that:

\nabla_{\left(\begin{smallmatrix}p^{\cdot,b}\\

\Delta^{\cdot}\,H^{n,\cdot,a}\,{}^{\prime}\left(p^{\cdot,a}\right)\end{matrix}\right)

\displaystyle\displaystyle\partial_{\varepsilon}\mathcal{H}^{n}(q,p^{\cdot,b},p^{\cdot,a},0)

\displaystyle\displaystyle=qJ^{n}\left(\sum_{k\in\mathcal{K}}w^{n,k}\big(\tilde{\delta}^{n,k,a*}(p^{k,a})-\tilde{\delta}^{n,k,b*}(p^{k,b})\big)\right)-\sum_{k\in\mathcal{K}}(q+\Delta^{k})\left(\Lambda^{n,k,b}\zeta^{n,k}\right)\big(\tilde{\delta}^{n,k,b*}(p^{k,b})\big)

\displaystyle\displaystyle\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad\quad+\sum_{k\in\mathcal{K}}(q-\Delta^{k})\left(\Lambda^{n,k,a}\zeta^{n,k}\right)\big(\tilde{\delta}^{n,k,a*}(p^{k,a})\big).

Injecting
\displaystyle\vartheta(q)=\theta(q)+\varepsilon f(q)+o(\varepsilon)
into Eq. (
6

\displaystyle\mathcal{O}(\varepsilon)
terms yields the linear equation:

\displaystyle\displaystyle 0=

3.3.3
Optimal Quotes

The above first-order expansion quantifies the impact of informational risk on the risk-adjusted expected PnL of the market maker. In order to address the optimal behavior of the market maker, we also need a first-order expansion of the maximizers in the definition of the Hamiltonian functions.

Writing

These expressions highlight that the influence of informational risk on optimal quotes can be decomposed into two components. The first component arises from the terms
\displaystyle D^{k}_{\pm}f
, which are finite differences of the function
\displaystyle f
. By definition, these terms reflect the marginal impact of informational risk on the risk-adjusted expected PnL, assuming the market maker does not update their quotes. As such, they aggregate the effects of informational risk across all tiers and are identical for all
\displaystyle n
. The second component is represented by the terms
\displaystyle g^{n,k,b/a}
, which quantify the impact of informational risk on the greedy quotes derived from the baseline value function in the absence of informational risk. Unlike the
\displaystyle D^{k}_{\pm}f
terms, the
\displaystyle g^{n,k,b/a}
terms are specific to the considered tier and trade size.

These two components may push optimal quotes in opposite directions, and their net effect typically requires numerical evaluation. Fortunately, this computation is straightforward, as it relies only on the optimal quotes in the baseline setting and the function
\displaystyle f
, which solves the infinite linear system (
7
) that can meaningfully be truncated. To shed further light on the mechanisms at play, we now turn to a class of closed-form approximations.

4
Quadratic Hamiltonian Approximation

In the algorithmic market making literature, closed-form approximations of the value function and optimal quotes are frequently used. The standard approach (see
[
6
]
) consists in replacing the HJB equation associated with the control problem by an alternative HJB equation in which the Hamiltonian functions are approximated to second order. The resulting modified HJB equation admits a solution that is quadratic in the inventory variable, with coefficients computable through a Riccati equation and a linear equation.

j\in\mathcal{K}\end{subarray}}\frac{H^{m,j\,\prime\prime}(0)}{\Lambda^{m,j\,\prime}\left(\tilde{\delta}^{m,j*}(0)\right)}\left(w^{m,j}J^{m\,\prime}\!\left(0\right)+\left(\Lambda^{m,j}\zeta^{m,j}\right)^{\prime}\left(\tilde{\delta}^{m,j*}(0)\right)\right)\quad\text{and}\quad F^{2,1}_{+}=2\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}m\in\mathcal{N}\\
j\in\mathcal{K}\end{subarray}}\Delta^{j}H^{m,j\,\prime\prime}(0).

Because
\displaystyle J^{n}
is non-decreasing, price reading contributes to increasing the concavity of the value function. The intuition is as follows: in the absence of informational risk, the market maker skews quotes to attract offsetting flows and deter trades that would increase the absolute value of the inventory. Skew sniffers detect this behavior and push the price in a direction unfavorable to the market maker, thereby amplifying inventory risk.

In contrast, the impact of adverse selection on the value function requires a more nuanced discussion. Each tier
\displaystyle n\in\mathcal{N}
and size index
\displaystyle k\in\mathcal{K}
contributes either positively or negatively to the change in concavity of the value function, depending on the sign of
\displaystyle\left(\zeta^{n,k}\Lambda^{n,k}\right)^{\prime}
. The sign of this term is determined by the nature of adverse selection within each tier.

Let us first consider a tier where clients trade on strong signals and would execute in the direction of their signal at virtually any reasonable price given prevailing market conditions. Such behavior is plausible for clients acting on mid- or long-term signals. In this case, the slopes of the corresponding
\displaystyle\zeta
functions are insufficiently steep to compensate the decrease of intensity functions, resulting in a reduction in the concavity of the value function. The mechanism is as follows: if the market maker holds a positive (resp. negative) inventory, then by skewing – through a decrease (resp. increase) in both bid and ask prices – they raise the absolute and relative probability of executing a sell (resp. buy) order that moves the inventory closer to zero and therefore the likelihood that adverse selection – whose dependence on quotes is mild – induces a price increase (resp. decrease), thereby artificially increasing the value of the remaining inventory. Although adverse selection reduces the constant term in the approximation of the value function, this mechanism tends to flatten it, thus lowering its concavity.

Conversely, consider a tier where adverse selection predominantly arises when clients execute trades at prices set by the market maker with large offsets – typically the case of clients acting on short-term signals with a technological speed advantage. In such a scenario, the corresponding
\displaystyle\zeta
functions may exhibit steep slopes, and the products
\displaystyle\zeta^{n,k}\Lambda^{n,k}
may cease to be decreasing. This contributes to an increase in the concavity of the value function.

This analysis of the concavity of the value function provides valuable insight into the first component – common to all tiers – describing the impact of informational risk on optimal quotes. This component is proportional (with a positive sign on the bid side and a negative sign on the ask side) to the terms

D^{k}_{\pm}\widehat{f}(q)=-\frac{\Sigma_{+}^{1,0}}{F_{+}^{2,1}}\left(\pm q+\frac{\Delta^{k}}{2}\right).

When the inventory is zero, the preceding discussion justifies that this component leads to spread widening in the presence of price reading: the market maker compensates for the added informational risk by increasing the profit margin per trade. When the inventory is nonzero, the effect of price reading on the value function encourages the market maker to skew more aggressively, in order to shorten the time spent with a long or short position. The case of adverse selection is more nuanced, as it depends on the cumulative effect of the different tiers and sizes.

The second component of the optimal quotes, which is tier-specific and relates to the local adjustments of greedy quotes, consists of two terms: one related to price reading and the other to adverse selection.

Focusing first on price reading, the corresponding term in the quote offset has the sign of
\displaystyle-q
on the bid side and
\displaystyle+q
on the ask side. In particular, it vanishes when the inventory is zero and contributes to reducing the existing skew when the inventory is nonzero. This effect reflects the market maker’s incentive to reveal less information through their quotes in order to mitigate the impact of skew sniffers.

Taken together, the two components of the impact of price reading on optimal quotes lead to a subtle trade-off. When the inventory is zero, the second component vanishes and only the first component remains: a widening of the bid-ask spread to account for informational risk. When the inventory is nonzero, the first component encourages stronger skewing to reduce the time spent in unbalanced positions, while the second component pushes in the opposite direction, urging the market maker to reduce the skew in order to avoid revealing inventory information to skew sniffers. The second effect can dominate, for instance, in tiers with a large proportion of skew sniffers, especially when these clients trade infrequently but are highly sensitive to revealed information.

The case of adverse selection once again depends on the relative shape of the
\displaystyle\zeta
and intensity functions. When adverse selection depends only mildly on the quotes, the second component affects the quote offsets according to the sign of
\displaystyle q+\Delta^{k}
for bid transactions of size
\displaystyle\Delta^{k}
and
\displaystyle-q+\Delta^{k}
for ask transactions of the same size. In such cases, the
\displaystyle\Delta^{k}
terms contribute to widening the spread, while the
\displaystyle q
terms encourage additional skewing – opposite to the incentives from the first component. The reasoning is reversed when adverse selection primarily occurs at seemingly unfavorable prices, that is, when informed clients exploit short-term mispricings. However, the second component still pushes in the opposite direction to the first. In both scenarios, the second component may dominate the first in tiers with highly informed clients, especially when trades have a strong impact on prices. However, unlike in the case of price reading, liquidity – through the amplitude of
\displaystyle\Lambda^{n,k}
– does not play a clear role in this mechanism.

Conclusion

In this article, we have proposed a tractable and implementable stochastic optimal control framework that enables market makers to adjust their quotes with greater awareness of informational risk arising from both price reading and adverse selection.

Using perturbation analysis, we have found that the impact of informational risk can be split into two components: (i) a global component related to induced changes of the value function that applies to pricing for all clients, and (ii) an individual component that applies to specific tiers to mitigate their perceived informational risk.

In the presence of price reading, the market maker acts as if they were more risk averse when showing prices to their global franchise. At the same time, they reduce the informational content of their quotes shown to skew sniffers. The extent of skew reduction strongly depends on liquidity, specifically on the contribution of price readers to market maker’s risk reduction. In particular, it is not beneficial to show skew to price readers with little liquidity contribution.

Optimal response to adverse selection depends on the relative shape of informed flow intensity and price impact functions. The market maker is able to become less risk averse for their global franchise when informed clients trade on long-term signals. Top-of-book prices for risk-reducing trades will be very attractive for informed clients. This is the cost the market maker is willing to pay to probe price direction for the benefit of a better risk management of their overall portfolio. On the contrary, adverse selection with sharp signals forces the market marker to be protective by showing defensive quotes even for risk-reducing trades with informed clients and behave as if they were more risk averse when it comes to their global franchise.
