Pharmacological targeting of bunyavirus-induced synaptic impairment prevents neuronal hyperexcitability
Authors: Barbara Gorda, Jules Bouget, Vaishali Saini, Willy Lutz, Aurélie Hirschler, Glaucia M Almeida, Diana Brychka, Corentin Bernou, Emma Partiot, François Delalande, Pramesh Sinha, Hamendra Singh Parmar, Solange Desagher, Gowrishankar Ganesh, Hem Chandra Jha, Sophie Colomb, Christine Carapito, Raphael Gaudin
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractOrthobunyaviruses are linked to a broad spectrum of neurological disorders. However, the molecular determinants governing bunyavirus neuropathogenesis remain poorly understood, precluding the identification of drugs able to alleviate virus-induced neuronal dysfunctions. Here, we show that the prevalent, yet neglected, orthobunyavirus Ťahyňa virus (TAHV) disrupts neuronal integrity and proteostasis in human cerebral organoids (hCO), significantly decreasing synaptic protein expression and density. Moreover, TAHV-induced synaptic remodeling is associated with perturbed local field potential in organotypic cultures of human post-mortem adult brain explants (OPAB) and higher expression of the metabotropic Glutamate receptor 1 (mGluR1) in mice in the absence of overt neurological disease or neuroinflammation. Using proteome-based network medicine, we identified gabapentin as a therapeutic candidate that restores neurotransmitter receptor levels back to baseline in mice, and prevents TAHV-induced hyperexcitability in OPAB. Together, our data highlights the underappreciated neuropathological potential of a neglected orthobunyavirus, that interferes with neural integrity and neurotransmission, while neuromodulatory interventions may help to preserve cerebral functions.Subject terms: Virus-host interactions, Cellular neuroscience, Antiviral agents, Synaptic transmission

Introduction

Virus-induced encephalitis are life-threatening infections of the central nervous system (CNS), mostly driven by exacerbated neuroinflammation. Viral neuroinfections can also result in more subtle neurocognitive dysfunctions1–3, as illustrated by the well-described HIV-associated neurocognitive disorders (HAND)4, and the variety of neurological symptoms associated to COVID-195,6. Viral exposure is also correlated to significant risk of neurodevelopmental, neurodegenerative, neurocognitive and psychiatric disorders4,7–11. Arboviruses are remarkably efficient at neuroinvasion and inducing neurological disease, notably the flaviviruses12–14, alphaviruses15–17 and bunyaviruses18,19. Because of the relatively low number of severe neurosymptomatic cases in humans, the impact of bunyavirus infection on neurocognitive functions and neurological health remains unclear and might be underestimated.

Orthobunyaviruses of the California serogroup (CSG) are mosquito-borne viruses reported to induce neuropathogenesis in mice and significant neurologic deficits in humans. In particular, La Crosse virus (LACV) is a leading cause of pediatric encephalitis in the USA and causes significant psychological sequalae20,21. Other bunyaviruses from the CSG, including snowshoe hare virus (SSHV), Inkoo virus (INKV), Jamestown canyon virus (JCV), and Ťahyňa virus (TAHV), can also result in neurological impairments in mice and humans21–24.

TAHV is an orthobunyavirus of worldwide prevalence25, especially in Europe25–27, shown to induce neuromotor dysfunctions in mice and is associated with febrile illness in patients21,22,28. However, the precise clinical spectrum of TAHV infection remains poorly described due to limited clinical investigations, despite high seroprevalence in endemic areas29,30. Its wide host range, including the diversity of its mosquito species’ vectors31, combined to its tri-segmented negative strand RNA genome32, enhancing the risk of virus reassortment and thus represent significant risk factors for the emergence of viruses of potential increased pathogenicity. The current outbreak of the orthobunyavirus Oropouche (OROV)33, and its increasingly recognized impact on the CNS34, well exemplifies the urgent need to deepen our understanding of the virus-host interactions governing the neuropathogenicity of this virus family.

There is currently no approved vaccine or specific antiviral to treat bunyaviruses infections, even if research efforts identify promising molecules35, and most of the therapeutic strategies include supportive care. The identification of the precise functional impairment driving viral disease is necessary to provide relevant and specific supportive treatment, in addition to antivirals when available. The development of both types of complementary treatments suffers from the tremendous gap between the molecular perturbations observed in vitro and their transposition in integrated human-based models before clinical trials. This concern can nowadays be better apprehended through the development of 3D multicellular models in vitro and ex vivo that partly mimic not only the remarkable complexity of the organization and cellular diversity of the human CNS, but also its electrophysiological features. Both organoids and brain explants generate spontaneous electrical activity that can be readily recorded on microelectrode arrays (MEA)36–40, providing a platform for a kind of in vitro micro-electroencephalograms (µEEG). Such µEEGs are particularly relevant for monitoring the functional impact of viral infections on neuronal activity in vitro, and can help identify drugs that mitigate virus-induced perturbations in neuronal activity.

Here, we show that TAHV productively infects human cerebral organoids (hCO), inducing profound alterations in protein composition and network integrity. Proteomic profiling reveals that TAHV infection of hCO is associated with translational modulation, disruption of synaptic composition and neuronal projection homeostasis. Furthermore, we demonstrate that TAHV infection increases neuronal firing activity in organotypic culture of post-mortem human adult brain explants (OPAB). Using a proteome-based network medicine approach, we identify gabapentin (GBP) as a therapeutic candidate to preserve physiological activity in infected OPAB without affecting viral replication. In immunocompetent mice, while causing only subclinical symptoms, TAHV infection is neuroinvasive and significantly upregulates metabotropic Glutamate Receptor 1 (mGluR1) mRNA, which can be rescued upon GBP treatment. Together, our study reveals that clinically neglected orthobunyavirus can perturb neuronal integrity and activity in human neural models and in mice, and highlights the potential of proteome-based network medicine approaches to identify neuromodulatory strategies to mitigate virus-induced neuronal dysfunction.

Results

Productive TAHV infection of human cerebral organoids disrupts neuronal integrity

Susceptibility of human cerebral organoids (hCO) to TAHV was assessed using RT-qPCR and plaque assays. We found that TAHV viral RNA (vRNA) was significantly increased 2 days post-infection (dpi) and kept increasing over 10 dpi (Fig. 1A). This vRNA expression was specific to active viral replication, as UV-inactivated TAHV was unable to replicate over the same period. Moreover, virus replication was also significantly decreased at 7 and 10 dpi upon treatment with the antiviral compound RG10b, which we previously showed to inhibit TAHV infection in other neural models36. Virus release from organoids measured by plaque assays remained low over the 10 days, likely reflecting the limited ability of newly produced virions to exit the matrix-dense hCO (Fig. 1B, supernatant). Dissociation and homogenization of the hCO allows the titration of the trapped viruses, which showed a peak of infectious virus production at 48 hpi followed by a decreased titer over the following days (Fig. 1B, homogenates). Using the lactate dehydrogenase (LDH) cytotoxicity assay, that relies on the quantification of LDH release in the extracellular medium, a hallmark of cell death, we showed that TAHV and RG10b treatment did not induce significant cytotoxicity (Fig. 1C), although this assay may not be sensitive enough to detect the whole extent of damage induced by the TAHV, as we and others have shown that TAHV induces neurotoxicity in 2D neurons35,36.

To evaluate virus tropism and the impact of TAHV infection on hCO morphology and composition, confocal imaging was performed. Upon infection, a decreased expression of neuronal and astrocytic markers (MAP2 and GFAP, respectively) was observed by imaging (Fig. 1D, E), and further confirmed by Western blot (Fig. 1F). As a consequence, these markers could not be reliably used to investigate TAHV tropism. Using an anti-CTIP2 antibody, a nuclear marker of post-mitotic neurons, we observed that TAHV staining was in close vicinity to CTIP2 staining (Fig. 2A), indicating that neurons are permissive to TAHV, as previously reported by us and others21,35,36. Using anti-S100B antibody to stain for astrocytes, we found that TAHV staining was sometimes found next to S100B, but not colocalizing, and the two stainings seemed to belong to different cells (Fig. 2B), suggesting that TAHV does not target astrocytes for efficient replication.

To get a clearer understanding of the TAHV tropism, we optimized a hCO dissociation protocol and performed flow cytometry analysis on the surface proteins NCAM1 (also named CD56), a marker associated with neuronal lineage enrichment41. We found that 17 to 22% of hCO cells were infected at 2 dpi, and about 97% of them were NCAM1+ cells (Fig. 2C). NCAM1 is most expressed in differentiated post-mitotic neurons, although neural stem cells also harbor some level of NCAM1. Thus, we also labeled hCO dissociated cells with CD15, a marker specific for neural stem cells that is not expressed by differentiated neurons42, and found that about 58% of the TAHV-infected cells were CD15 + (Fig. 2D). Hence, TAHV infects primarily neuronal lineages, including neural stem cells and differentiated neurons in brain organoids.

Differential mass spectrometry analysis of TAHV-infected cerebral organoids reveals profound proteomic remodeling

To gain further insights into the molecular determinants underlying TAHV-induced neuronal structure disruptions, we performed liquid chromatography with tandem mass spectrometry (LC-MS/MS) from non-infected or TAHV-infected hCO for 10 days (Fig. 3A). We identified 596 differentially expressed proteins (DEPs) with a false-discovery rate of 2.65%, including 50 upregulated proteins with fold change (FC) > 2, and 87 downregulated proteins with FC > 3 (Fig. 3B and Supplementary data S1). The expression of proteins associated with “Cytoplasmic Translation” were among the most significantly downregulated (Fig. 3C; NES < -2.24, pval <0.001 and Supplementary Data S2), consistent with reported bunyavirus-induced translation shutdown32,43–45. Similarly, expression of proteins included in the pathway “modification of Postsynaptic structures” showed significant downregulation (Fig. 3C; NES < 1.67, pval < 0.02), consistent with the reported TAHV-induced perturbation of electrical neurotransmission36. In contrast, the expression of many proteins of the “inflammatory response” pathway were dysregulated, but no significant tilt towards up- or down-regulation was observed (Fig. 3C; NES < 1.18, pval < 0.19).

To validate our proteomic dataset, three proteins with significantly modulated abundance, namely neuronal cell adhesion protein 1 (NCAM1), VAMP-Associated Protein A (VAP-A), and Neural cadherin (N-Cad), were chosen based on their differential abundance obtained by mass spectrometry (Suppl. Supplementary Fig. S1A and Supplementary Data S1). We validated the decreased expression of the VAP-A and N-Cad proteins by western blot, while NCAM1 expression tended to increase, although it did not reach significance (Supplementary Fig. S1B, C). Interestingly, VAP-A is known to exert key roles in synaptic vesicle fusion and neurotransmission46, and N-Cadherin is an adhesion molecule important for dendritic arborization, and structural and functional organization of synapses47, suggesting that TAHV infection may induce neuronal network remodeling.

TAHV infection disrupts synaptic homeostasis and functions in human-based models

Additional gene ontology (GO) analyses revealed that differentially expressed proteins in TAHV-infected hCO (TAHV perturbome) were associated with “axonogenesis”, “axon extension” and “neuron projection organization” (Fig. 3D and Supplementary Data S3), compatible with the morphological alterations of neuronal projections identified in Fig. 1D. Furthermore, TAHV perturbome was also significantly associated with “postsynapse organization”, “modification of synaptic structure” and “postsynaptic modulation of chemical synaptic transmission” biological processes (Fig. 3D). SynGO protein mapping (from ref. 48) identified more than 20 proteins from the TAHV perturbome associated with pre- and post-synaptic localization (Fig. 3E). This result is consistent with the decreased ontology terms associated with synapses (Fig. 3C) and loss of expression of the synaptic proteins NCAM1 and N-Cad (Supplementary Fig. S1) among other synaptic proteins (Supplementary Fig. S2A).

Western blot analyses revealed the loss of synaptopodin, PSD-95, and Homer1 over the course of TAHV infection (Fig. 3F). Confocal imaging of immunofluorescence staining of Mock or TAHV-infected hCOs further highlighted the decreased expression of synaptic proteins upon infection, including synaptophysin (SYP), PSD-95, and bassoon (Fig. 3G and Supplementary Fig. S2B). Finally, quantification of trans-synaptic structures in post-mitotic neuronal LUHMES cells, identified by Homer-1/Bassoon colocalization, revealed that TAHV infection significantly decreases the density of synaptic structures (Fig. 3H), consistent with the major loss of synaptic protein expression identified in hCOs.

TAHV dysregulates the electrophysiological properties of neurons ex vivo

To evaluate whether TAHV-induced molecular disorganization of synapses results in impaired neuronal activity, the local field potentials (LFPs) of OPAB were recorded using 3D MEA. Using a machine learning framework based on random forest classifier (RFC) using optimized parameters (see details in the Methods section, Supplementary Data S4, and ref. 36), we demonstrated that LFP profiles of Mock versus TAHV-infected OPAB can readily be segregated for 3 brain donors, while the electrical signal from donor 4 was not accurately discriminated between mock- and TAHV-infected OPAB (Fig. 4A). Indeed, while donor 1, 2, and 3, show accuracy superior to 0.88, 0.93, and 0.80 respectively, it remained < 0.7 for donor 4. For each donor, a separated model has been fitted to maximize accuracy (Supplementary Data S4). The models for the first two donors present robust performance, as indicated by 10-fold cross validation, f1-score, recall, and precision metrics, whereas the model trained on the third donor was moderate and the fourth model weak (Fig. 4B). Of note, training of the RFC model was done separately for each donor, using train and test slices, but a larger pool of donors would be needed to temper individual signatures and build a generalizable model, as four donors are insufficient to design a robust framework to study cross-donor effect of TAHV infection on LFPs. In contrast, the study of each donor’s signature through the analysis of relative feature importance showed that donor 1 and 2 had similar patterns of feature importance, while donor 3 and 4 exhibited unique patterns (Fig. 4B). This observation could be explained by the different genetic background of these donors, the different susceptibility to the infection, and/or the insufficient amount of data to accurately train the model. Together, our data demonstrate that TAHV infection considerably affects synaptic integrity, resulting in neuronal activity alterations in complex primary 3D human-based models.

Proteome-based network medicine identifies gabapentin as a modulator of TAHV-induced neural hyperexcitability

Bioinformatic analyses can assist in the identification of protein-disease association, linking diseases or phenotypes to a molecular perturbome. To correlate the extent of perturbations induced by TAHV to clinical phenotypes, we used the PhenoExam R package, which allows to determine phenotype and disease enrichment analysis on a protein dataset49. TAHV perturbome was significantly associated with neurological phenotypes, including “global developmental delay”, “intellectual disability” and “depressivity” (p < 0.05) (Fig. 4C and Supplementary Data S5). Interestingly, TAHV perturbome was also associated with the phenotype “seizure”, and several movement disorders (“spasticity”, “dystonia”, “gait ataxia”, and “unsteady gait”; Fig. 4C and Supplementary Data S5), that is consistent with the main neurological symptoms reported in TAHV-infected mice21. To identify potential drugs acting on TAHV perturbome protein networks, we ran a proteome-based in silico drug repurposing screen using SAveRUNNER50. This tool predicts the association between FDA-approved drugs and diseases by quantifying the interplay between the drug targets and the disease-associated protein networks. By adding the TAHV perturbome as a new “disease” in the dataset, SAveRUNNER computed a list of drugs that exhibit potential interactions or similarities with TAHV perturbome (Fig. 4D and Supplementary Data S6). Among the most promising hits, gabapentin (GBP) and other gabapentinoids exhibited significant proximity with TAHV-associated perturbome (Fig. 4D, red text). GBP was initially developed as an anticonvulsant51, but is now also approved to treat pain (post- herpetic neuralgia)52, and is prescribed off-label in different medical contexts including psychiatric disorders53,54. Its identification is consistent with the human phenotypes associated with TAHV perturbome in silico (Fig. 4C) and in mice21. Seizures and epileptogenesis are common complications of viral neuroinfections55,56, particularly following CSG viruses infections such as La Crosse virus (LACV), a closely related orthobunyavirus20,57. Hence, we compared the differential proteome of epileptic human brain tissue58 and TAHV-infected hCO, and found that about 20% (120/590) of the TAHV perturbome was also identified as differentially expressed proteins in epileptic patients (Suppl. Figure S3A). GBP is an FDA-approved drug that inhibits neurotransmitter release and has been shown to prevent infection by the Junin virus (arenavirus)59,60. Therefore, we next evaluated the impact of GBP on the infection of hCO and LUHMES cells by TAHV, but we found that GBP is unable to inhibit TAHV infection (Fig. 4E, F). Furthermore, a good similarity score was found for the small molecule ivermectin (Supplementary Data S6), a drug used to treat parasite infection and with a potential for broad-spectrum antiviral activity61. Treatment of hCO with ivermectin tended to decrease TAHV infection in hCO, although it did not reach significance (Fig. 4E). In addition, ivermectin was toxic to LUHMES cells (Supplementary Fig. S3B), and thus, investigation of the antiviral activity of ivermectin to treat TAHV infection was not pursued.

TAHV increases neuronal excitability which can be restored upon treatment with Gabapentin

The perturbation in neuronal spontaneous activity induced by TAHV (Fig. 4A, B) and the in silico association to motor disorders including seizure (Fig. 4C) suggested alterations in neuronal excitability following the infection. Further analysis revealed that TAHV infection significantly increased the firing rate of OPAB compared to their mock-infected counterparts (Fig. 4G, H), reflecting virus-induced increase in neuronal activity. Strikingly, treatment with GBP could preserve physiological neuronal firing rate during TAHV infection (Fig. 4G, H), possibly by maintaining physiological neuronal network excitability.

Using machine learning signal classification, we found that GBP potently protects against TAHV-induced LFP perturbations in brain explants from 2 donors (Fig. 4I; TAHV + GBP recordings classified as Mock in > 88% and > 96% for donor 1 and 2, respectively). However, although GBP treatment neutralized the increase in firing rate in donor 3 (Supplementary Fig. S3C), our machine learning framework did not show a restoration of the LFP profile (Fig. 4I, cyan diamond), suggesting that additional factors contribute to the TAHV-induced electrical perturbations. Finally, neither GBP nor TAHV had a clear effect on the activity of brain explants from donor 4, which reflects host-dependent heterogeneity. Together, this data indicates that GBP may be a potent drug for the prevention of TAHV-induced electrical perturbations, in a donor-dependent manner, and that targeting neurotransmission can represent a relevant strategy to overcome virus-induced neuronal dysfunctions in complement to antiviral treatments.

TAHV infection in mice induces differences in the transcription of genes related to neurotransmission without neurological disease

To test the effect of GBP in vivo, immunocompetent Swiss albino adult male mice were infected with TAHV in the presence or absence of GBP treatment (Fig. 5A). Similar to the clinical outcomes in humans, infected mice did not show dramatic neurological impairments, and thus, we could not evaluate the beneficial effect of GBP at this level, although we cannot exclude that neurocognitive and behavioral impairments occurred in the infected mice. Of note, three mice died of non-neurological causes in the TAHV and TAHV-GBP groups, suggesting that TAHV can also cause peripheral insults. Among the alive mice, body weight did not significantly vary in any of the conditions (Fig. 5B). Even without signs of neurological disease, TAHV vRNA was detectable in the brain of all the mice of the TAHV-infected group, and treatment with GBP tended to reduce vRNA load, although it did not reach significance (Fig. 5C), a finding consistent with our experiments performed in hCO and LUHMES cells (Fig. 4E, F). Furthermore, mRNA expression levels of Iba1 and Gfap, microglial and astroglial activation markers respectively, were not significantly modulated in the brain of infected mice (Fig. 5D, E), and mRNA from proinflammatory and antiviral factors IL1b, IL6, and IFNB1 also remained non-significantly affected upon TAHV infection (Fig. 5F–H). This observation is consistent with the low neuropathogenicity of TAHV in humans. Of note, however, microinflammation may occur in localized brain areas as IL6 and Iba1 mRNAs show a tendency toward higher levels. Strikingly, we observed that the mRNA of the metabotropic glutamate receptor 1 (Grm1), a major player of neurotransmission contributing to fine-tuning of synaptic plasticity62, was significantly upregulated upon TAHV infection, a phenotype that was potently reverted upon GBP treatment (Fig. 5I). Similar pattern was observed for the mRNA levels of Gria1, a subunit of the AMPA receptor involved in main excitatory neurotransmission (Fig. 5J), consistent with the increased firing rates observed in OPAB (Fig. 4G, H). However, mRNA levels of other neurotransmission receptors, including Grin1, Hcn1, Camk2, and Slc8a2 remained non-significantly altered by neither TAHV nor GBP treatment (Fig. 5K and Supplementary Fig. S4A–C). Moreover, we could not detect difference of expression for mRNAs coding for structural synaptic components (Supplementary Fig. S4D–F), a difference with our hCO data, which may be due to the experimental model used, or the different types of readouts (mRNA versus protein). Together, our data highlights that, even in the absence of clear neurological symptoms, TAHV augments the expression of critical synaptic receptors involved in neuronal excitability and plasticity, while GBP can prevent such phenomena.

Discussion

In this study, we show that TAHV efficiently infects hCO and that replication occurs for at least 10 days in this model. TAHV infection drives important reorganization of hCO cellular and molecular composition. Notably, TAHV infection leads to significant alterations in the maintenance of hCO proteostasis. Bunyaviruses are known to reorganize the host cell’s translation machinery, leading to translation decay44,45. They also perform 5’ mRNA “cap-snatching”43, thereby modulating the ability of the host to translate its own mRNAs. In neurons, the synaptic proteome comprises thousands of proteins whose lifetime ranges from hours to days, and up to weeks63,64, and protein insertion in synaptic compartments is tightly regulated by neuronal activity and plasticity65. Consequently, an acute viral neuroinfection that impairs proteostasis is a serious threat to synaptic functions, including neurotransmission and adaptive plasticity. Here, TAHV resulted in important structural reorganizations of hCO, including the loss of crucial dendritic and synaptic proteins and ultimately led to a decreased synaptic density.

We additionally showed that TAHV infection induces hyperexcitability in primary human brain explants. Seizures and epilepsy are common acute and chronic complications of viral neuroinfections56, and anti-epileptic interventions are thought to hold potential to counteract viral effects on neurotransmission during the acute phase, but their efficacy remains to be clearly established56. The identification of GBP as a potential modulator of TAHV perturbome was possible thanks to a pipeline based on in silico proteome-based network medicine, an approach particularly promising in the context of viral emergence preparedness66. Together with the observation that GBP treatment neutralizes TAHV-induced hyperexcitability, these findings illustrate how viral infections can impair neuronal communication through molecular rewiring. However, how TAHV-induced synaptic remodeling and the increase in neuronal activity correlate, and which precedes or causes the other, remains ambiguous. GSEA analyses revealed that components of GABAergic synapses tended to be decreased while the ones of excitatory synapses tended to be increased upon TAHV infection (Supplementary Fig. S3D, E). The perturbation of neurotransmission by TAHV may also result from low and local levels of cytokine production that influence synaptic activity67,68. Viral infections of neurons can result in neuronal hyperexcitation that precedes cell death69, and thus, a possibility is that the increased neuronal activity induced by TAHV causes synaptic and neuronal damage. Hyperactivity-induced glutamate excitotoxicity is a known driver of virus-caused neuronal death69–71, and exacerbated firing activity can drive synaptic downscaling via regulating homeostatic plasticity mechanisms72. Thus, it appears that pharmacological intervention aiming at reducing neuronal firing in infectious contexts, such as GBP treatment, may be relevant both to reduce neuronal damage and death (by lowering the associated glutamate excitotoxicity) and to attenuate the pathological synaptic downscaling.

The neurotransmission changes we observed ex vivo did not translate into significant neuropathological phenotypes, such as seizures and ataxia, in TAHV-infected mice. Orthobunyavirus neuropathogenicity has previously been tested in C57BL/6 mice, revealing age- and dose-dependent outcomes21,22, and thus, the discrepancy between our observations and previous reports could be attributed to the different mouse models employed. Despite the absence of overt neurological phenotypes, infected mice exhibited molecular signatures of altered neuronal activity. We noticed an increase in Grm1 and Gria1 mRNA levels, two neurotransmitter receptors regulating synaptic plasticity and neural circuitry. Importantly, these elevations could be rescued to physiological levels by gabapentin treatment. It has been previously shown that Gria1 transcription is modulated by higher neuronal activity73, as elevation of Gria1 mRNA in the layers II/III and V pyramidal cells is associated with neurocognitive disease in humans74. On the other hand, enhanced Grm1 function leads to motor deficits and abnormal neuronal activity in mice75, and Grm1 is involved in regulating neuronal excitability in pathological neurocognitive contexts in mice76. A strength of our study is that it combines several layers of evidence (proteomics, immunostaining, electrophysiology and transcriptomics) from different experimental models, but future work should aim to consolidate and integrate these layers of evidence within a unified frame.

In addition to encephalitis, other, more subtle neurological or cognitive deficits, such as learning impairment, depression, or anxiety, can be caused by altered neuronal activity and viral infections3,77,78. Further research is needed to investigate whether TAHV can induce neurocognitive decline or other behavioral changes, as previously reported for CSG orthobunyaviruses79. One could hypothesize that despite the high prevalence of orthobunyavirus in humans, subtle neurocognitive symptoms escaped traditional clinical surveillance. Higher-order brain functions rely on fine-tuned neurotransmission, which is highly sensitive to molecular perturbations that can be triggered by viral infections. Overall, leveraging the power of network medicine pipelines to identify molecular networks rewired upon infection, and drugs that restore physiological neurotransmission, represents a promising strategy to alleviate neurological and cognitive symptoms experienced upon a viral infection.

Cell culture and human cerebral organoids

Vero E6 cells (CRL-1586) were obtained from the American Type Culture Collection and grown in DMEM (Sigma-Aldrich, Missouri, US) supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS, non-USA, Sigma-Aldrich) and 1% penicillin-streptomycin (P/S, Sigma-Aldrich). H9 human embryonic stem cells (hESCs; female, WA09, WiCell) were maintained in mTeSR Plus medium (STEMCELL Technologies, Vancouver, Canada) supplemented with 1% penicillin-streptomycin in 8 mg/ml Matrigel (Corning, New York, US) coated 6 cm dishes (Corning). The medium was changed daily, and cells were passaged by manual cutting of colonies every 5-6 days using a 27¾ gauge needle. The human induced pluripotent stem cells (iPSCs; HPSI0214i-wibj_2; HipSci, Culture Collections) were maintained under feeder-free conditions on Matrigel-coated 6 cm dishes (8–12 mg/mL) in Essential 8 medium (E8 medium; Thermo Fisher Scientific).

The LUHMES cells (were obtained from the Leist’s lab) were cultured and differentiated as in ref. 36 and ref. 80. Briefly, LUHMES cells were maintained in culture as proliferating undifferentiated monolayers at 37 °C, % CO2 and 95% humidified atmosphere, in dishes coated with 50 µg/mL poly-L-ornithine and 1 µg/mL fibronectin. Proliferation medium contained Advanced DMEM/F12 medium supplemented with N2 supplement, 2 mM GlutaMAX, 40 ng/ml bFGF, and 1 X penicillin-streptomycin. Proliferating LUHMES cells were split three times a week (detached using TrypLE, diluted in Advanced DMEM/F12 and centrifuged at 300 × g for 5 min before resuspension in proliferation medium). The day before starting differentiation into non-dopaminergic neurons, LUHMES cells were seeded in 10 cm dish (2.6 × 106 cells/dish) in proliferation medium. On the following day, cells were switched to differentiation medium, containing Advanced DMEM/F12 medium supplemented with N2 supplement, 2 mM GlutaMAX, 1 μg/ml tetracycline, and 1X penicillin-streptomycin. After 2 days of differentiation, cells were detached using TrypLE, counted and seeded at a density of 1.6 × 105 cells/cm2 in differentiation medium. LUHMES cells were used at postmitotic neurons after 5 days of differentiation, when cells were washed with Advanced DMEM/F12 medium and incubated in tetracycline- and antibiotic-free differentiation medium for an additional 1–2 days.

For hESC-derived cerebral organoid differentiation, mouse embryonic fibroblasts (MEF, MitC treated) were seeded in 10 cm dishes coated with 0.1% gelatin and grown in DMEM (10% FBS, 1% P/S, 1 mM Sodium pyruvate). hESCs were transferred onto the MEF feeder layer and maintained in ESC medium with daily medium changes (DMEM F12 + L-glutamine (Gibco), 20% KnockOut Serum Replacement (Gibco), 1x MEM Non-Essential Amino Acid Solution (Gibco), 100 μM β-mercaptoethanol (Gibco), 1% P/S with freshly added 8 ng/mL human bFGF (Sigma-Aldrich)). When hESC colonies reached 1.5-2 mm, the medium was changed to differentiation medium (ESC medium with no bFGP, 1 mM Sodium Pyruvate) and colonies were scooped using a cell lifter (Corning) and transferred to 6 cm ultra-low attachment dishes. On day 0, (24 h post-lifting), half of the medium was replaced with fresh differentiation medium supplemented with 1 µM Dorsomorphin (Sigma-Aldrich), 3 µM IWR-1-Endo (Sigma), 10 µM SB-431542 (Sigma-Aldrich) and 1 µM Cyclopamine (Sigma-Aldrich) final concentration. From this day, the medium was fully changed every other day until the organoids were mature. On day 3, the organoids were placed on a tissue-culture orbital shaker. On day 18, the medium was switched to Neurobasal medium (N2, Gibco) supplemented with 1x N2 supplement (Gibco), 1% P/S, 1x GlutaMAX and 1 µM Cyclopamine. On day 24, Cyclopamine was removed from the N2 medium. Organoids were utilized for experiments from 35 days post-differentiation. Alternatively, hCO were grown from iPSCs cultured in E8 medium for 4–7 days after the last passage until colonies reached ~ 80% confluency. Cerebral organoid differentiation was performed based on ref. 81.

Obtention of post-mortem adult brain explants and Organotypic culture

The obtention and maintenance of OPAB were extensively described in ref. 36. Post-mortem interval (PMI) was between 0 and 8 h and additional relevant clinical information are provided in Supplementary Data S7. The explants were embedded in 3% low melting point agarose (ThermoFisher) and sliced using a PELCO Easislicer (Ted Pella) in 300 μm thick sections containing cortical layers and some white matter. The slices were deposited on cell culture inserts with 0.4 μm pore PET membranes (Millicell, Millipore) hanging in 6-well plates containing N2 medium in the lower chamber, while the upper chamber was left in the open air, allowing ex vivo culture at the air–liquid interface (ALI). The resulting OPABs were cultured at 37 °C, 5% CO2 and 95% humidity, and the medium was changed twice a week.

Virus production, infection and treatment

Tahyna virus (TAHV, NR-541) was obtained from the American Type Culture Collection (Virginia, WV, USA) through BEI Resources. Propagation and production of TAHV stocks was performed in Vero E6 cells. The cells were seeded in T175 flasks and incubated at 37 °C, 5% CO2 to reach 80–90% confluency the following day. The medium was removed, and cells were absorbed with TAHV at a multiplicity of infection (MOI) of 0.0001 per cell (viral stock diluted in DMEM culture with 2% FBS, 1% P/S), for 1 h at 37 °C, with gentle moving from side to side every 15 min. The inoculum was removed, and the cells were incubated for 48–72 h at 37 °C with fresh DMEM, 2% FBS, 1% P/S. The virus was collected by spinning the medium at 2000 × g for 10 min and harvesting the supernatant. Viral titers were determined by plaque assay in Vero E6 cells as described previously36, and measured as plaque-forming units (PFU) per ml.

The MOCK condition represents the same volume of supernatant from non-infected Vero cells, prepared in parallel of a TAHV production.

Organoids were infected in 24-well ultra-low attachment plates (Corning) by placing them in 500 µl of N2 medium containing 1 × 105 PFU TAHV per single organoid. The organoids were incubated without shaking for 5 h, then the virus- containing medium was removed, organoids washed in N2 medium two times and fresh N2 medium added. The organoids were then placed on the shaker for the remainder of the experiment and medium changed every 2-3 days. At each time point, the organoids were washed two times with PBS and lysed in 350 µl of RLT buffer for RNA extraction using the RNeasy kit (Qiagen, Hilden, Germany), or fixed for 1 hour with 4% paraformaldehyde (PFA) for immunofluorescence staining. For virus titration, the supernatant was collected and titrated by plaque assay to quantify infectious particles released in the extracellular milieu. To titrate viruses retained within the organoids, hCO were washed twice in PBS, and 500 µl of media was added before freezing at − 80 °C, followed by thawing and dissociation by pipetting. Virus titration from the resulting homogenates was performed by plaque assay.

Differentiated LUHMES cells were infected on coverslips in 24 well plates with TAHV at a MOI of 0.1 for 3 h at 37 °C as previously described36. The viral inoculum was then removed, and fresh tetracycline- and antibiotic-free differentiation medium (Advanced DMEM/F12 medium supplemented with N2 supplement, 2 mM GlutaMAX) added. 24 hpi the medium was removed, cells washed in PBS and fixed in 4% PFA for 30 minutes at RT.

Treatment with Gabapentin (GBP; MedChem Express) or Ivermectin (MedChem Express) were performed concomitantly to the infection for an indicated period of time.

Cytotoxicity assay

Cytotoxicity was assessed using the CyQUANT LDH Cytotoxicity Assay (Thermo Fisher, Massachusetts, United States) and the experiments were performed according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Briefly, medium from organoids or LUHMES cells was harvested and incubated in 96-well plates with the LDH substrate for 30 min at RT. The stop solution was added to stop the reaction, and the absorbance was read with a microplate reader (TECAN Infinite M Plex, Männedorf, Switzerland). Cytotoxicity was normalized to a positive control (lysed organoid) = 100%.

RT-qPCR in mammalian samples

TAHV RNA levels in LUHMES-derived neurons and human cerebral organoids were measured utilizing the Luna® Universal One-Step RT-qPCR kit (New-England Biolabs (NEB), Massachusetts, United States), using the following conditions: 25 °C for 2 min; reverse transcription step at 50 °C for 15 min; initial denaturation step at 95 °C for 2 min; 40 cycles of denaturation at 95 °C for 15 s and extension at 55 °C for 30 s. TAHV-specific primers targeted the M segment, and the RNA levels were normalized to Glyceraldehyde 3-phosphate dehydrogenase (GAPDH) using the following primers: TAHVFor GGTCCTA CATTGCCGTTCAAG, TAHVRev TGGTCTACAGGTGCTAGCTC, GAPDHFor GAGTCAACGGATTTGGTCGT and GAPDHRev TTGATTTTGGAGGGATCTCG. The RT-qPCR reaction was performed on a CFX Opus 384 Real-Time PCR system (Bio-Rad, California, USA).

Immunofluorescence staining and Imaging

Organoids were permeabilized with permeabilization buffer (0.5% BSA, 0.5% Triton in PBS) for 24 h on a rocker. Organoids were labeled with primary rabbit polyclonal anti-MAP2 (GeneTex, GTX133109), rabbit monoclonal anti-Homer1 (Synaptic Systems, 160 003), mouse monoclonal anti-Bassoon (Abcam, ab82958, clone [SAP7F407]), guinea pig anti-synaptophysin (Synaptic System, 101 308), rabbit anti-synaptopodin (Synaptic Systems, 163 002), rabbit anti-PSD95 (Synaptic System, 124 008), goat anti-GFAP (Novus Biologicals, NB100-53809). The antibodies were diluted in permeabilization buffer (1:100 dilution) and incubated overnight. The organoids were washed three times in PBS and labeled with appropriate secondary antibodies; donkey anti-rabbit IgG Alexa Fluor Plus 488 (Thermo Fisher Scientific, A21206), donkey anti-guinea pig IgG Alexa Fluor 647 (JacksonImmuno, RRID: AB_2340476), donkey anti-mouse IgG Alexa Fluor Plus 647 at 1:1000 (Thermo Fisher Scientific, A32787) and Dapi nuclear counterstain (Pierce) at a final concentration of 1 µg/ml. The organoids were once again washed in PBS and fully immerged in RapiClear 1.52 reagent (Nikon) overnight at RT to clarify the samples before imaging. LUHMES cells differentiated on #1.5 coverslips were blocked with 0.5% BSA, 0.1% Triton in 1X PBS for 30 min at RT and incubated with primary Homer1 and Bassoon antibodies (1:250 dilution) for 1 h at RT. The coverslips were washed in PBS and labeled with appropriate secondary antibodies and DAPI (as for organoids) for 1 h at RT. Sufficient PBS washes were performed prior to mounting the coverslips on glass slides with Mowiol (Sigma-Aldrich). The images were taken on two spinning disk confocal microscopes: a Dragonfly microscope (Oxford Instruments) equipped with an ultrasensitive 1024 × 1024 EMCCD camera (iXon Life 888, Andor) using the 405, 488, 561, and 637 nm laser lines with a 20 × 0.75 NA, 40 × 1.3 NA, 60 × 1.4NA, and 100 × 1.45 NA oil-immersion objectives; or a spinning disk Olympus SR microscope equipped with a sCMOS Fusion BT camera (Hamamatsu), four laser lines, and 40 × 1.4 and 60 × 1.42 oil objectives. Images were processed using FiJi 2.18 (ImageJ) and Bitplane Imaris x64 (Oxford Instruments) version 9.7.

Preparation and immunofluorescence of organoid cryosections

Organoids were fixed with 4% PFA for 1 h, washed in PBS and submerged in 30% sucrose overnight. The following day, organoids were embedded in Neg-50 OCT embedding medium (Epredia, 6502 G) and placed in the chamber of the Minux FS800 cryostat (RWD Life Science) set to − 30 °C for 10 min. Sections of 30-µm thickness were cut and immediately transferred to slides or to wells containing PBS. A hydrophobic pen (ImmEdge Pen, Vector Laboratories, H-4000) was used to draw around the tissue, and the sections were permeabilized and blocked in 0.5% BSA, 0.5% Triton in PBS for 2 h. The primary rat anti-CTIP2 (Bcl11b) at 1:200 (BioLegend, #650602, Clone 25B6), mouse anti-S100β at 1:200 (Sigma-Aldrich, S2657, clone SH-B4), and rabbit anti-LACV cross-reactive hyperimmune plasma (cross-reactivity with TAHV) at 1:50 (gift from Dr. Karin Peterson) and appropriate secondary antibodies were incubated in 0.5% BSA, 0.1% Triton in PBS for 2 and 1 h, respectively. Washes were performed with PBS by gently aspirating liquid from the slide and adding PBS 3-5 times. The sections were mounted with Fluromount Aqueous mounting medium (Sigma-Aldrich, F4680) and imaged on the spinning disk Olympus SR microscope.

Synapse quantification

LUHMES-differentiated neurons were infected with TAHV or maintained as mock-infected controls and analyzed at 24 hpi. Cells were immunostained for Homer1, a postsynaptic marker, and Bassoon, a presynaptic marker, with nuclei counterstained using DAPI. Three-dimensional confocal images were acquired using a 100 × objective, collecting Z-stacks spanning 10 µm. Imaging parameters, including contrast settings, were kept constant across all experimental conditions to allow comparative analysis. Synapses were identified based on the colocalization of Homer1 and Bassoon puncta. Synapse numbers were quantified by counting Homer1/Bassoon colocalization events and normalizing the values to the number of cells identified by DAPI staining. Data were further normalized to the mock-infected condition, which was set to a value of 1. Quantification was performed from 25 images collected from two independent experiments (10 and 15 images per experiment).

Western blotting

Organoids were lysed in cold NP-40 buffer (50 mM Tris (pH 7.4), 150 mM NaCl, 1% NP-40 and 1X Protease Inhibitor Cocktail (Promega) and incubated on ice for 10 min. The lysates were cleared by centrifugation at 14,000 x g for 15 min, NuPAGE LDS Sample Buffer (Invitrogen) with 4% [v/v] β-mercaptoethanol added and boiled at 95 °C for 5 min. The samples were resolved on NuPAGE™ 4 to 12%, Bis-Tris, 1.0 mm gels with 1X MES running buffer (Invitrogen). Resolved proteins on the SDS-PAGE gels were transferred to nitrocellulose membranes (Bio-Rad) using the Trans-Blot Turbo Transfer System (Bio-Rad) using the mixed molecular weight setting (10 min, 2.5 A, 25 V). The membranes were blocked with TBST (20 mM Tris pH 7.5, 150 mM NaCl, 0.1% [v/v] Tween-20) with 5 % skimmed milk powder for 1 h at RT and incubated with primary rabbit polyclonal anti-MAP2 (GeneTex, GTX133109), goat anti-GFAP (Novus Biologicals, NB100-53809), mouse anti-CD56/NCAM1 (BioLegend, 304601, clone MEM-188), rabbit anti N-cadherin, (GeneTex, GTX127345), mouse anti-VAP-A, (Santa Cruz Biotechnology, SC-293278, clone 4C12), rabbit anti-Calreticulin, (Thermo Fisher Scientific, PA3-900), mouse anti-PSD95 (GeneTex, GTX634291, clone GT1234), mouse anti-synaptophysin (BioLegend, 837103, clone SP17), rabbit anti-synaptopodin (Synaptic Systems, 163 002) and rabbit anti-homer1 (Synaptic Systems, 160 003) at a 1:200 dilution, overnight at 4 °C. The blots were washed three times in TBST and revealed using Clarity Western ECL substrate and the BioRad ChemiDoc. Flow Cytometry of TAHV-infected human brain organoids

Organoids were washed twice with HBSS and incubated in dissociation medium (HBSS supplemented with 20 mM HEPES, 2.5% TrypLE, and 200 µg/mL DNase I) at 37 °C for 5 min under agitation. Organoids were then gently triturated using a pipette, and incubated for an additional 5 min under agitation. Enzymatic dissociation was stopped by the addition of B27 supplement, followed by further mechanical dissociation through repeated gentle pipetting. Cell suspensions were filtered through a 40 µm cell strainer, and centrifuged at 500 × g for 5 min. Cells were resuspended in 1% BSA diluted in PBS and distributed into U-bottom 96-well plates. Surface staining was performed for 45 min at 4 °C using APC-conjugated anti-human NCAM1/CD56 antibody 1:200 (BioLegend, 362503, clone 5.1H11) and FITC-conjugated anti-human CD15 antibody 1:200 (BioLegend, 301903, clone HI98). Following two washes with 1% BSA, cells were fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde for 10 min and permeabilized with 0.05% Triton X-100 in 1% BSA for 10 min on ice. For intracellular viral detection, permeabilized cells were incubated for 45 min on ice with rabbit anti-LACV serum (cross-reactivity with TAHV) diluted 1:50 in 1% BSA. Cells were then washed twice and incubated for 30 min on ice with secondary antibodies Alexa Fluor 488 or anti-rabbit Alexa Fluor 647. After final washes, cells were resuspended in 1% BSA, and data were acquired with the NovoCyte Flow cytometer (Agilent Technologies). Data were analyzed using FlowJo v10.2.

Mass spectrometry-based quantitative proteomics

Sample preparation. Individual brain organoids were lysed in Laemmli buffer composed of 10 mM Tris pH 6.8, 1 mM EDTA, 5% SDS and 10% glycerol. Samples were sonicated for 2 min. Protein concentration of all samples was determined using the DC protein assay (Bio-Rad) according to the manufacturer’s instructions and a standard curve was established using BSA. Dithiothreitol (DTT) was added at a final concentration of 50 mM and cell lysates were heated at 95 °C for 5 min. Protein extracts were stacked in an in-house prepared 5% acrylamide SDS-PAGE stacking gel at 50 V. Proteins in the gel were fixed, washed and colored with Silver Blue. Gel bands were cut, washed with ammonium hydrogen carbonate and acetonitrile, reduced with 10 mM DTT and alkylated using 55 mM iodoacetamide prior to overnight digestion at 37 °C with modified porcine trypsin (Promega) at a final trypsin/protein ratio of 1/50. Trypsin-digested peptides were extracted with 60% acetonitrile in 0.1% formic acid, followed by a second extraction with 100% acetonitrile. Acetonitrile (ACN) was evaporated under vacuum, and the peptides were resuspended in H20 and 0.1% formic acid (FA) before nanoLC-MS/MS analysis.

NanoLC-MS/MS analysis. NanoLC-MS/MS analyses were performed on a nanoAcquity UltraPerformance Liquid Chromatography device (Waters Corporation) coupled to a quadrupole-Orbitrap mass spectrometer (Q-Exactive HF-X, Thermo Fisher Scientific). Peptide separation was performed on an ACQUITY UPLC Peptide BEH C18 Column (250 mm × 75 µm with 1.7 µm diameter particles) and an ACQUITY UPLC M-Class Symmetry C18 Trap Column (20 mm × 180 µm with 5 µm diameter particles; Waters Corporation). The solvent system consisted of 0.1% FA in water (solvent A) and 0.1% FA in ACN (solvent B). Samples were loaded into the enrichment column over 3 min at 5 μl/min with 99% of solvent A and 1% of solvent B. Chromatographic separation was conducted over a 60 min gradient. The mass spectrometer was operated in data-dependent acquisition mode. Survey full scan MS spectra (mass range 300–1800) were acquired in the Orbitrap at a resolution of 60 K at 200 m/z with an automatic gain control (AGC) fixed at 3 × 106 and a maximal injection time set to 50 ms. The ten most intense peptide ions in each survey scan with a charge state ≥ 2 were selected for fragmentation. MS/MS spectra were acquired at a resolution of 15 K at 200 m/z, AGC was set to 1 × 105, and the maximal injection time was set to 50 ms. Peptides were fragmented by higher-energy collisional dissociation with a normalized collision energy set to 27. Peaks selected for fragmentation were automatically included in a dynamic exclusion list for 60 s. All samples were injected using a randomized injection sequence. To minimize carry-over, two solvent blank injections were performed after each sample.

Data interpretation. Raw files were converted to.mgf peaklists using MsConvert and were submitted to Mascot database searches (version 2.5.1, MatrixScience, London, UK) against a human (20 399 sequences, 2022-09-07, Taxonomy ID 9606), Tahyna (74 sequences, 2022-09-08, Taxonomy ID 45270) protein sequences database downloaded from UniProtKB-SwissProt, to which common contaminants and decoy sequences were added. Spectra were searched with a mass tolerance of 10 ppm in MS mode and 0.05 Da in MS/MS mode. One trypsin missed cleavage was tolerated. Carbamidomethylation of cysteine residues was set as a fixed modification. Oxidation of methionine residues and acetylation of proteins n-termini were set as variable modifications. Identification results were imported into the Proline software (version 2.6.2)82 for validation and label-free quantification. Peptide Spectrum Matches (PSM) with a pretty rank equal to one were retained. False Discovery Rate (FDR) was then optimized to be below 1% at the PSM level using Mascot Adjusted E-value and below 1% at Protein Level using Mascot Mudpit score. For label-free quantification, extracted ion chromatograms were used to derive peptide abundances. An m/z tolerance of 10 ppm was used. Alignment of the LC-MS/MS runs was performed using Loess smoothing. Cross assignment of peptide ion abundances was performed among the samples. Protein abundances were computed using the sum of the unique peptide abundances. The imputation of the missing values and differential data analysis were performed using the open-source ProStaR software83. Imputation of missing values was done using the approximation of the lower limit of quantification by the 2.5% lower quantile of each replicate intensity distribution (“det quantile”). A Limma moderatedt test was applied on the dataset to perform differential analysis. The adaptive Benjamini-Hochberg procedure was applied to adjust the p-values and False Discovery Rate.

GSEA, GO and PhenoExam analysis

Gene ontology analysis was performed on R using clusterProfiler package84 and enrichGO() function set with the following parameters: minGSSize = 1, maxGSSize = 500, pvalueCutoff = 0.05, pAdjustMethod = “fdr”. The protein set used for GO analysis comprised all TAHV-induced differentially expressed proteins (FDR < 2.65%). GSEA analysis was performed on R using the GSEA() function from the fgsea package85, and conducted with the following parameters: minGSSize = 15, maxGSSize = 500, pvalueCutoff = 1 with all other parameters set to their default values. Proteins were ranked in descending order according to a signed Z-score. Signed Z-scores were used to summarize differential effects, with the sign reflecting the direction of the log2 fold change and the magnitude derived from the corresponding two-sided p-value (Limma moderated t test), calculated as follow: z = sign(log2(FC)) × qnorm(1-pvalue2)). Phenotype enrichment analysis was performed on R using the PhenoEnrichGenes() function from the PhenoExam package. TAHV-induced differentially-expressed proteins (FDR < 2.65%) were mapped to the Human Phenotype Ontology (HPO) database.

Proteome analysis using SaveRUNNER

The SaveRUNNER R package50 was downloaded from GitHub (https://github.com/sportingCode/SAveRUNNER) and installed on R Studio (version 4.4.1). This software extracts the target name of the DrugBank database and compares it to the disease-associated proteins of human interactome databases. Using a network-based similarity measure, the algorithm identifies drugs and diseases located in the same network neighborhoods to predict drug–disease associations. Proteomic hits using 2.65% FDR from TAHV-infected/Mock-infected organoids were added to the “Phenopedia” file as dysregulated proteins of the newly created “TAHV disease” entry. The SAveRUNNER script was then run to compute a list of drugs potentially targeting the dysregulated proteins in TAHV-induced neurological disorders or their interacting partners. For each drug, a similarity value (and adjusted similarity value) was computed, indicating how close the drug’s targets are to the dysregulated proteins of TAHV neurological disease. The resulting list of drugs is presented in Supplementary Data S6.

Electrical activity measurement

OPABs were placed on 3D microelectrode arrays (3D-MEA, 60 electrodes, Multichannel systems) with 150 µl of BrainPhys medium (Stemcell Technologies) supplemented with 1X N2, 1% P/S and 1X Glutamax (Gibco). OPABs were infected by depositing a drop containing 106 PFU TAHV per OPAB, in the presence or absence of 100 µM GBP in the BrainPhys medium.

Three recordings of 1 min each at a sampling frequency of 10 kHz were taken at 48 hpi per OBAP and organoid using the MEA2100-Mini-System with the Multi Channel Experimenter software v2.21 (Multichannel system).

Spike detection

Spikes were counted using a homemade R script. Only spikes with an amplitude > 5,5*SD were considered as real spikes. A dead window of 100 ms was used to avoid multiple counting of one single spike. The firing rate (Hz) of one OPAB was calculated by dividing the total number of spikes across 60 electrodes by the time (in s). LFP data processing

Considering that OPABs may not cover the whole electrode array, to avoid the recording of non-covered areas, we selected the 35 electrodes that presented the largest standard deviation across the whole recording, thus considered the most active electrodes. Once selected, each recording was cut into 30 pieces of 2 s, which were each used as single entries in our dataset. Each 1-min recording thus provided 30 data points. Each sample was first high-pass filtered over 50 Hz with a Butter filter of order 3. Then, harmonics of 50 Hz were filtered up to the 15th harmonic (equivalent to 50 Hz, 100 Hz, 150 Hz… up to 1450 Hz). Each recording was then processed by a Fast Fourier Transform (FFT) to obtain amplitude-frequency data. The FFT data were averaged across all the selected electrodes of the same 2-s recording. Because of the high sampling frequency (10 kHz), the number of frequency features were reduced to optimize the quality/computation ratio. To this end, each recording was interpolated using a linear interpolation in frequency space to give 300 points each of 16.6 Hz width. For each donor, we obtained a 301-column dataset in which each row corresponds to a processed 2 s recording. In this dataset, 300 columns indicate the average amplitude for the corresponding 16.6 Hz width frequency interval, and 1 column indicates the label, thus the infection/status of the recording. The processed dataset used in the subsequent machine learning framework are provided in Supplementary Data S8.

Machine learning algorithm for LFP analysis

To analyze the processed data, we implemented a Random Forest Classifier (RFC) algorithm as in ref. 36. Each donor gave rise to one dataset. This dataset for each donor comprises the processed recordings of all the infection and treatment status evaluated in our study. Briefly, each whole dataset was separated into a training dataset to fit an RFC model, and a testing part, which served as unseen data to test the previously established model. Three recordings per OPAB of one minute each (prior to processing) were acquired, and two of them were used for training, and the remaining one was used for testing purposes. For the K-Fold Cross Validation, the training and testing datasets were concatenated and fed to the function ‘cross_val_score’ of the scikit-learn Python library. Due to a small number of recordings per donor, we could not use one separate donor as the test fold itself and the others as different training fold. The different instances of RFC were trained using an exhaustive grid search method for a few selected parameters. The searched parameters were the following (according to scikit-learn python library version 1.3.1 of the RandomForestClassifier class): n_estimator (values: 100, 200, 350), criterion (values: gini, entropy, log_loss), max_features (values: sqrt, log2, None), max_depth (5, 10, 15) and min_sample_split (2, 4, 6). The other parameters were kept as the default. All of the 108 possible combinations of parameters were tested and only the best performing one was kept as final model and reported in Supplementary Data S4.

For each donor, we obtained an RFC model to discriminate between TAHV-infected and MOCK-infected conditions. These models were used to classify unseen TAHV-infected, MOCK-infected and TAHV-infected GBP-treated processed recordings. When testing, we computed the following metrics: training accuracy, testing accuracy, 10-fold cross-validation accuracy, f1-score, recall, and precision to know the reliability of the model. The implemented RFC-based analysis pipeline is being developed as a software (v1.0.9), accessible on GitHub depository via: https://github.com/membrane-dynamics-and-viruses.

Animal experiment

Swiss Albino wildtype male mice were utilized for the experiment, housed in polypropylene cages and acclimated for 2 weeks prior to experimentation in a 14 h light:10 h dark cycle in 23 ± 2 °C and humidity under control, with free access to laboratory feed and drinking water. A total of four groups were determined: untreated mice (n = 6), TAHV-infected mice (n = 7), GBP-treated mice (n = 4), and TAHV-infected and GBP-treated mice (n = 7). Mice were aged around 5-6 weeks during the time of inoculation. Tahyna virus (TAHV) was diluted in phosphate-buffered saline to 103 PFU of virus in a volume of 20 μL administered intranasally. The GBP treatment was administered intraperitoneally at a dose of 150 mg/kg. Following infection of TAHV, the gabapentin treatment was given daily for 5 consecutive days before sacrifice.

RNA isolation from mouse brain and RT-qPCR

For isolating RNA from brain tissue and serum, the sections were stored in RNA Later (Cat.R0901, Sigma-Aldrich). The RNA was isolated using the TRIzol method, utilizing the standard protocol. Briefly, the brain tissue was shifted from RNA Later to 600 μL TRI reagent (Cat. T9424, Sigma-Aldrich) and homogenized and incubated for 5 min. Further, 200 μL chloroform was added, gently mixed by inverting, and incubated at room temperature for 3 min. The solution was centrifuged at 12000 g for 15 min and the aqueous phase was transferred to a fresh tube. To this, 250 μL isopropanol was added and incubated at RT for 10 min. This was then centrifuged at 12000 × g for 15 min to observe a pellet to which 500 μL of 75% ethanol was added and incubated at RT for 5 min. A final centrifuge at 8000 × g for 7.5 min was done, and the obtained pellet was air-dried and further dissolved in autoclaved distilled water. The quantification of RNA was done using NanoPhotometer N60 (Implen). From the RNA obtained, cDNA was prepared using Superscript II Reverse Transcriptase kit (Cat. 18064014, Thermo Fisher Scientific) in the presence of 2.5 μM N6 random primers and 0.5 mM dNTP using the manufacturer’s protocol. RT-qPCR was performed to analyze host and viral gene expression among various groups (primers, designed with PrimerBLAST, are list below). The equivalent of 6 ng of resulting cDNA was used as a template for qPCR using a CFX Opus 384 real-time PCR system (BioRad) with a home-made SYBR Green PCR master mix86. PCR reactions were performed in a final volume of 10 μl in the presence of 200 nM primers. Thermal cycling parameters were 5 min at 95 °C, followed by 40 cycles of 95 °C for 20 s, 64 °C for 20 s and 72 °C for 20 s, and a melt curve from 64 °C to 95 °C with 0.5 °C increments. Data were analyzed, and relative amounts of specifically amplified cDNA were calculated with CFX Maestro software (BioRad), using two different reference genes. For normalization of mouse brain RNAs, the two most appropriate reference genes as determined by the geNorm software87 were Gapdh and Hmbs among eleven house-keeping genes tested (B2m, Gapdh, Gusb, Hmbs, Hprt1, Rpl21, Aldoc, Tubb2b, 24p3, Tbp, and Ywhaz). An ordinary one-way ANOVA followed by Tukey’s multiple comparisons test was used to evaluate significance, and the data was plotted using GraphPad Prism software.
