“Cake causes herpes?” - promiscuous dichotomisation induces false positives
Authors: David Robert Grimes, James Heathers
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundContinuous biomedical data is often dichotomized into two or more groups for analysis, despite long-standing warnings from statisticians that this constitutes bad practice. This dichotomisation is typically discouraged because it reduces statistical power and may obscure important trends. This paper considers another reason to discourage this practice: that dichotomisation is a powerful tool to manipulate data, as dichotomising at an arbitrary yet flexible threshold (which we term ’promiscuous dichotomisation’) represents a powerful researcher degree of freedom.MethodsThe motivating question is how probable is it that given a set of uniformly distributed data a threshold can be engineered to produce the illusion of a true effect when none exists? Introduction

Much of biomedical science employs thresholds or binary cut-offs of continuous data, from which conclusions and inferences are drawn. Vitamin D deficiency, for example, is defined at a threshold that may vary substantially (from 8–30 ng/L; cited in different works [1]). Similarly, scores from the Alcohol Use Disorders Identification Test (AUDIT) for ‘harmful’ have been variously given from >5 to >16 at a minimum from a 40 point scale [2]. In both examples, the decision of where to set a threshold of interest has straightforward diagnostic implications, but has no precise physiological, medical, or practical determinant.

Take for instance a scenario where a researcher collects a dataset of environmental factors, seeking to relate them to fluctuations in a chronic health condition. Several continuous variables (for instance, sun exposure in lumens, temperature, and humidity) are collected for each subject, alongside physical symptoms and perceived symptoms (e.g. skin redness, fatigue). Given this dataset, continuous variables may be treated as point-to-point measurements, binned into time periods, transformed into central tendencies, quartiles, and more. Measurements may be combined into omnibus indicators and transformed, combined, or categorized. Subgroups may be defined for any given value and analysis repeated within a constrained sample. Without a pre-defined analysis plan or a physiological hypothesis relating a particular environmental exposure to symptoms, such a dataset permits an extreme amount of analytical flexibility. nfortunately for research accuracy, this is the case in most datasets.

One ubiquitous transform in medical science worthy of special attention is dichotomisation, the selection of a cut-point that collapses a continuous variable into a categorical one, forming two or more groups. By trying cut-points exhaustively, a maximal difference in group characteristics on other variables can be engineered. For example, an experimenter with continuous data about height and binary data about some health outcome might dichotomize the continuum of heights into just “short” and “tall”, choosing some line of demarcation and then counting the instances of a related health event in both groups. This type of approach is alluring to many researchers as it seems simple, but advice from statisticians regarding such an analytical decision is quite explicit - this practice should be avoided [3–7].

One major objection to this binarization is that it “wastes” valuable information, and can substantially decrease statistical power to report an effect. In previous investigations, dichotomisation at the median value was akin to jettisoning a third of the data from a statistical power perspective [8], while an even starker example demonstrated that sample size could be reduced from 800 to 88, almost an order of magnitude, by switching from a dichotomous to continuous analysis [9]. Even so, the practice of arbitrarily introducing thresholds to data is ubiquitous, and often contradictory. If the data of these papers is incompletely reported – and it often is – the collected findings can be impossible to synthesize elsewhere and contribute to research waste [10].

Another problem alluded to has been the reality that selection of a cut-off point constitutes a researcher degree of freedom, and can lead to spurious positive findings. A researcher could run several analyses and select the optimal cut-off [11] to see their expected result, with Altman and Royston [3] noting that “..use of this so called “optimal” cutpoint (usually that giving the minimum P value) runs a high risk of a spuriously significant result; the difference in the outcome variable between the groups will be overestimated, perhaps considerably; and the confidence interval will be too narrow. This strategy should never be used”. This practice can create misleading results and mislead researchers and clinicians, like the classic example in the case of unruptured aneurysms[12] and a more recent example investigated by the authors [13] concerning misleading claims that WiFi caused miscarriages, chiefly resulting from inappropriate dichotomisation. While this tendency of dichotomisation to produce misleading results is well-known, it remains largely unquantified. Consider the following illustrative example:

Herpes Simplex Type 2 (HSV-2) spreads via sexual contact and causes genital herpes. Blood glucose (BG) level is the amount of glucose in free circulation within the blood stream. Taking the National Health and Nutrition Examination Survey Data cohort dataset (2001-2002), blood glucose level (measured to 0.1 mmol/L) and HSV-2 seropositivity data were compared in a random sample of American adults () with complete information for both. Contrasting ‘low’ (<4.3 mmol/L) and ‘high’ (>4.3/mmol/L) group with a test (), it was found that herpes prevalence in the LowBG group () was significantly lower than the HighBG group (; HR=2.04, ).

This reporting is superficially correct but utterly misleading. A logistic regression on the complete data reveals absolutely no evidence of an association between plasma glucose and herpes incidence (, Fig. 1a). The spurious apparent relationship (1(b)) was derived by testing every possible cutoff value and choosing a threshold that engineered maximum difference between the samples. The sample sizes (; ) were unstated, preventing awkward questions about why groups are so unbalanced. If these analytical choices remain unspecified or unchallenged, as is frequently the case, a researcher might well get away with concluding high blood glucose is a risk factor for herpes. Worse, an irresponsible researcher might editorialize the relationship, imply a causality and a direction within this relationship, and spuriously conclude ’cake causes herpes’, ’herpes causes overeating’,’biscuit consumption leads to promiscuity’ or some other desired distortion.

The motivation of this paper stems from an attempt to answer a critical question: if one has any collection of hypothetical continuous predictor data and a binary outcome, then what is the probability that a maximally flexible dichotomisation threshold could create the illusion of a significant finding? This is illustrated in Fig. 2, and we call this hypothetical phenomena promiscuous dichotomisation, a crucially under-discussed source of analytical flexibility. With each subsequent cut in the data drawn without replacement, the non-independence of cuts renders the questions pertaining to possible false positive rates distinctly non-trivial undertaking. Quantifying the likely impact is crucial, because the choice of boundary for a comparison can be entirely flexible. The increased ease of access to large datasets regarding human health means that such quantification is timely. Accordingly, we establish both an analytical method for exactly estimating the probability of at least one dichotmisation cut existing that yields a false positive result for a small number of discrete cuts. We show that this is impractical to implement, and so derive a general Agent-based Monte-Carlo method to estimate the probability of a dichotimsation cut existing that yields at least one false positive in a set of n elements when no true relationship exists. Implications of this for scientific trustworthiness are also developed and discussed.

Methods

The motivating question of this work is given any arbitrary array of data, what is the probability of obtaining a false positive when we slice the data multiple into dichotomous cuts multiple times. If data sets regenerated with each cut and replacement, then this would be a straight-forward multiple-testing issue, where the effective false positive rate with nominal level and m tests would be . However, promiscious dichotomisation is more involved. As illustrated in Fig. 2, it concerns multiple dichotomies in any arbitrary but unchanged data set. Consider n elements, of which a proportion p events or features, uniformly distributed at random in a set of n. If v is a sorted vector of n fractional cuts to be considered, the question becomes whether we can obtain a spurious positive contrasting any lower cut of to the corresponding upper cut of . Crucially, the sorting itself is largely irrelevant: we might for example sort patients by height in centimeters, and demarcate events by height. We could sort by time since an intervention, or by dose received, or by number of records owned. Once this is specified, we ask the question of how likely are we to observe a seemingly meaningful trend in uniformly distributed random data.

Ordinal data application and note on categorical data

it is not uncommon for candidate continuous data predictors to be stratified into ordinal bands. For example, the continuous measure of hours of activity per week might be binned into “sedentary”, “low exercise”, “moderate exercise” and “high exercise” groups and health outcomes compared. This introduces yet another arbitrary choice into the data, and is akin to the model above with a vector v of three cuts. Similarly, this might occur if a measurement tool lacks specific resolution to produces a band estimate, with the same principle applying. Note that for categorical data without ordinal considerations, analytical flexibility becomes even greater - one could compare for example the “low exercise” group with a combination of all the other groups, in which cases comparisons are effectively independent and false positive probability increases, as previously discussed.

Analytical limitations

While the derived equation works in principle for all values of n and p, in practice it rapidly becomes unwieldy due to the sheer amount of permutations involved. For example, quartile cuts at with and () yields permutations and an exact solution is easily obtainable. By contrast, decile cuts ( for the same n and p yields permutations, rendering an analytical approach unfeasible. Given that a set of n items has possible integer cuts, this would result in a number of permutations greater than the upper limit of the IEEE 754 Double Precision Floating Point standard () for typical cases in order of . Given these difficulties, ascertaining the effects of more cuts requires a simulation approach, as analytical methods become unfeasible as n increases, and alternatives are required.

Agent-based Monte-Carlo simulation

To make this tractable by simulation, we again exploit the fact that a statistic relates to an ellipse of insignificance, which is defined by parameters n and p as in the analytical scenario. Instead of explicitly calculating all allowed values, we implement a simulation where an agent starting at the origin randomly moves in either the x-direction (non-events) or y-direction (events), with respective probability

As these probabilities change through the simulation, this constitutes a biased random walk, which corresponds to sampling without replacement. At each step of the walk, the co-ordinates at that position are inputted into the ellipse equation given in Eq. 1. If , the point lies within the insignificance ellipse (true negative) and a new random step is calculated. If occurs on a random walk, a false positive is recorded. Alternatively, when for all steps, the agent stayed within the ellipse for the entire biased walk and a true negative is recorded. This simulation was run 10,000 times for varying values of n and p, with the proportion of results leading to false positives recorded. Discussion

Results of this analysis illustrate that for any arbitrary large dataset that contains a continuous predictor and a binary outcome, it is highly probable at typical significance thresholds that at least one cutoff exists that would yield a spurious positive finding when none truly exist. Promiscuous dichotomisation is a surprisingly effective strategy for engineering group differences, but likely less effective than other strategies such as strategically trimming outliers (redaction bias), or changing group designation (say, swapping the labels of ‘highly performing’ subjects from Control to Intervention groups). However, honest researchers cannot deploy some forms of outlier trimming or a relabeling strategy without the knowledge that they are falsifying their data, but it seems plausible that researchers simply search through the existing literature for previous dichotomisation thresholds, and then cite the papers listing the thresholds which produce the correct outcome without the slightest knowledge that wishful thinking and analytical flexibility have lead them to a spurious conclusion. In that way, this may be an equally dangerous threat to scientific accuracy similar forms of falsification.

It is important to note there are times when dichotomisation is a reasonable scientific approach. If there is a solid biological or physical reason that an effect manifests at a given threshold, then it may be reasonable to dichotomise at this point for a binary outcome. There may also be instances when a researcher is looking for a particular point where a given effect might manifest in an exploratory capacity. There are a variety of methods for mitigating the problems of false positives in arbitrary data [17, 18], from correcting for multiple comparison and bootstrap methods, or even sample splitting analogous to that employed in machine learning literature, where a threshold determined on a random subset of the sample is performed on the independent remainder. If adequately reported, then this may constitute perfectly acceptable practice.

The problem arises when continuous scales are swept without justification or even elucidation until a desired result is obtained, which this work shows is likely to be spurious. This analytical-flexibility coupled with subpar or even dishonest reporting lends itself to spurious results, and is an urgent problem in the era of large datasets. Recently, researchers have noticed a dramatic increase in the volume of NHANES-derived papers asserting a cross-sectional relationship where an independent predictor is being compared (often singly) to dependent health conditions, and that these papers may also use strategically chosen subsets of the data available [19]. These conditions are the perfect environment to employ a flexible dichotomisation strategy if ‘significant results’ are sought in isolation, especially as free and readily available R and Python packages can simplify the process data extraction and analysis.

This problem also naturally compounds over time – say, for instance, a single threshold is typically used for a measurement with a strong justification (i.e. 10), but it is porous to any alternative measurement. Only one time does a researcher need to test dichotomisations until threshold at 8 is discovered to produce ’more significance’, and publish that result. Subsequently, any other researcher may assert ’a threshold of 10 is used, but 8 seems to be meaningful, too’, be correct in that assertion, and then themselves use a threshold of 6. This is presumably a strong portion of the reason that Vitamin D thresholds for deficiency can be defined at 10, 11, 12, 15, 19, 20, or 30ng/mL. In such a research environment, it is trivial to justify the methodology of a study with a stated threshold of, say, 25 or 13. It is also unlikely that this problem is sufficiently present in the mind of any given peer reviewer that they might insist on testing alternative thresholds to any presented analysis.

It is also possible to nest dichotomisations: say, for instance, the effect of a drug is being investigated, and a sample is split into users and non-users who are measured according to a series of health markers. The threshold to divide users into ’occasional’ or ’heavy’ use might be flexible, and in these fluid groups the thresholds of the health markers might themselves be flexible. Scenarios like this are hard to produce general models for, but it is likely that for many scenarios the proportion of datasets where an effect could be ’found’ approaches 1. Results from recent work on an explosion of formulaic research suggest this is already a serious issue [19] but there is a silver lining here: for studies of this type, the data is available, is public, cannot be hidden cynically behind a veil of privacy, and no careful inspection of the results for the above checklist containing the hallmarks of dichotomisation is necessary. This strategy is impossible to hide in re-analysis, and requires almost no knowledge of any given programming language or environment. Simply altering the dichotomisation threshold entered and re-running the statistical analysis underlying the critical results of any given paper should immediately reveal its fragility – in our example, using any other threshold, including those that might be considered typically meaningful for blood glucose content (for instance, 3.9, 5.6, or 6.9 mmol/L) would destroy the cake-herpes illusion entirely (the p-values for those thresholds are , and respectively). This should be additionally simplified by the fact that researchers are required to declare how analysis was performed, what packages were used, etc. While there are many strategies to produce dishonest results[20, 21], many of which can be well hidden, continuing to normalize open code and computational reproducibility can do a substantial amount to detect and prevent spurious analyses using a promiscuous dichotomisation strategy.

Crucially, it is trivial to find apparent associations in health data. But unless this search is rooted on a solid mechanistic hypothesis, anything garnered from these expeditions is at best exploratory and at worst completely spurious. This leads to formulaic and empty research that is far more likely to mislead than illuminate, and such vapid research is easier than ever to produce. One worrying illustration has been the explosion of papers that simply mine the FDA Adverse Events Reporting System (FAERS) for ostensible signals of harm, which grew from about 100 published studies in 2021 to over 600 in 2024, with more again published this year. The dubious quality of such investigations has led several journals to ban such papers [22]. The reality is that health science is complicated and nuanced, and empty associations risk not only oversimplifying real complexity, but can positively mislead us. Promiscuous dichotomisation is yet another way of torturing the data to produce false confessions of which we must be eternally wary.
