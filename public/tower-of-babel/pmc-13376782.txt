Condom use and associated factors among people living with HIV/AIDS on Anti-Retroviral Therapy (ART) in Ethiopia: a systematic review and meta-analysis
Authors: Sintayehu Shiferaw Gelaw, Tadele Derbew Kassie, Aysheshim Asnake Abneh, Elyas Melaku Mazengia
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractCondom use remains a crucial preventive measure for individuals undergoing antiretroviral therapy (ART) in the fight against HIV/AIDS. This review and meta-analysis aimed to determine the pooled prevalence of condom use and the factors associated with it among people living with HIV/AIDS (PLWHA) on ART in Ethiopia. We conducted systematic searches across various databases, including PubMed, Google Scholar, Embase, and Cochrane Library, to identify relevant research articles. The review followed the PRISMA 2020 guidelines. We included cross-sectional studies from Ethiopia reporting condom use prevalence and associated factors among people living with HIV/AIDS (PLWHA) on antiretroviral therapy (ART). Two authors independently extracted data using a standardized Microsoft Excel format, which was then analyzed in STATA 17. Heterogeneity was assessed using the I² test, and pooled odds ratios (OR) with 95% confidence intervals (CI) were calculated to examine the relationship between determinant factors and condom use. In this study, 1,360 articles were identified from various databases, and twelve were selected for the final systematic review and meta-analysis. The pooled prevalence of condom use was 56% (95% CI: 51, 61). PLWHA who are married less likely to use condom (AOR = 0.40, 95% CI: 0.13, 0.66), urban residence was more likely to use condom (AOR = 2.63, 95% CI: 1.29, 3.97), and HIV status disclosure (AOR = 0.21, 95% CI: 0.12, 0.31) were significantly associated with condom use in Ethiopia. low percentage of respondents indicated that they use condoms. Marital status, urban residence, and receiving counseling about condom use, are associated with a higher probability of condom usage. Introduction

HIV/AIDS remains a significant public health concern worldwide1. Ethiopia, with over 110 million people, has been significantly affected by the HIV/AIDS epidemic2. In 2025, Antiretroviral therapy (ART) is currently reaching about 503,000 people through approximately 1,400 health facilities across Ethiopia. Despite this significant progress in expanding access to care, the country continues to face persistent challenges within its health system and broader socio-economic environment. As a result, Ethiopia still depends heavily on external donor support to sustain and advance its HIV response3. The HIV positivity rate has also risen by 22%4.

Consistent condom use refers to the practice of using condoms correctly and consistently during sexual activities5. Condom use is vital in HIV/AIDS prevention, especially for those on ART6. Male condoms reduce HIV transmission by 80% in heterosexual sex and 64% in anal sex among men who have sex with men when used correctly. They also prevent other sexually transmitted infections (STIs)7,8.

Over the past decade, condom use has increased in most sub-Saharan African countries. Among 17 nations surveyed since 2008, 13 countries saw higher use among young women15–24 in high-risk sex, while Benin, Ethiopia, Ghana, and Madagascar saw declines9.

Studies conducted in Ethiopia have shown varied prevalence condom use among HIV/AIDS patients on ART, ranging from 44%10 to 78.911 with the most prevalent factors associated to condom use were residence, marital status, sex, receiving counseling about condom use, educational status, and disclosure status11–20. However, the wide variation in findings across these studies makes it difficult to draw a clear and consistent conclusion. This variation highlights the need for a systematic review and meta-analysis to bring together the available evidence, provide a more reliable overall estimate, and better understand the key factors affecting condom use in Ethiopia.

While previous systematic reviews conducted at the Sub-Saharan Africa level have included Ethiopian studies21, these analyses were broader in scope and primarily reported subgroup findings without providing a detailed, country-specific synthesis. In contrast, our study focuses exclusively on Ethiopia, allowing for a more context-specific and in-depth analysis. Additionally, our review includes 12 primary studies, incorporating more recent evidence compared to earlier reviews, and provides a more comprehensive evaluation of methodological differences and heterogeneity across studies. By concentrating on individuals receiving antiretroviral therapy (ART), our study also addresses a more specific population with unique behavioral and clinical characteristics. We also acknowledge that a recent systematic review and meta-analysis conducted in Ethiopia included 10 primary studies and provided initial evidence on condom use among people living with HIV/AIDS22. However, our study differs in several important ways. First, we included 12 studies, incorporating more recent evidence and improving the precision and representativeness of the pooled estimate. Second, our review specifically focuses on individuals receiving ART, a subgroup with distinct behavioral and clinical characteristics that were not explicitly emphasized in the previous review. Therefore, the objective of this systematic review and meta-analysis was to determine the pooled prevalence of condom use and associated factors among HIV/AIDS clients who are on ART in Ethiopia.

Objective of the review.

This systematic review and meta-analysis had two main objectives:

To estimate the pooled prevalence of condom use among people living with HIV/AIDS on Anti-Retroviral Therapy (ART) in Ethiopia.

To estimate the pooled prevalence of condom use among people living with HIV/AIDS on Anti-Retroviral Therapy (ART) in Ethiopia.

To identify the pooled effect sizes of factors associated with condom use among people living with HIV/AIDS on ART in Ethiopia.

Methods and materials

Protocol and registration

The study adhered to the guidelines specified in the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analysis statement (PRISMA 2020) when determining the inclusion criteria and conducting the analysis23. The systematic review and meta-analysis protocol were registered at PROSPERO on June 24, 2023, under the record ID CRD42023435003.

Eligibility criteria

Two authors (SSG, TDK) reviewed study titles and abstracts, resolving disagreements with other reviewers. The review included Ethiopian observational studies on HIV/AIDS patients on ART, reporting condom use and its factors, in English due to there is no articles published other than English language in Ethiopia context and also there was limitation of translation feasibility during data collection period. Excluded were studies lacking condom use data, on newly diagnosed patients, qualitative studies, editorials, commentaries, and inaccessible full texts.

Search strategy

A systematic search was conducted across multiple databases, including PubMed, Google Scholar, Embase, and Cochrane Library, with no publication year restrictions. Google Scholar was not used as a primary database for the main systematic search. Instead, it was used to identify grey literature and to ensure the comprehensiveness of the search. Reference lists were also examined. To minimize the exclusion of potentially eligible studies due to inaccessible full texts or unclear results, we made several efforts. First, we conducted additional searches through institutional library subscriptions, Google Scholar, and direct journal websites to retrieve the full texts. In addition, we contacted corresponding authors via email to request the full text or to clarify unclear or missing data where necessary. For studies with unclear results, we carefully reviewed the methods and results sections and, when possible, cross-checked related publications from the same authors. Despite these efforts, studies that remained inaccessible or had insufficient data for extraction were excluded from the analysis. Endnote X7 managed study retrieval and removed duplicates. We have rigorously updated the literature search to include all studies published up to January 2025; however, this comprehensive effort did not identify any additional relevant literature beyond what was already included, used terms related to condom use, associated factors, HIV/AIDS, ART, and Ethiopia, applying Boolean operators like “OR” and “AND” for comprehensive results.

Data extraction process

Data were independently extracted by two authors using a standardized Excel format, covering study details and outcome data. The level of agreement was strong with a percentage agreement of 91% and manual screening supported by EndNote was used. Log odds ratios were calculated for associated factors. Disagreements were resolved through discussion, and missing data were requested from authors. SSG supervised the process.

Outcome measurement

This review examined condom use prevalence among PLWHA on ART in Ethiopia and its associated factors. Prevalence was calculated as the percentage of PLWHA on ART who reported condom use. Associated factors were identified using pooled odds ratios from two-by-two tables based on primary study data.

Operational definition

In this review, “condom use” refers to consistent condom use during sexual intercourse, including with HIV-negative, HIV-positive, or partners of unknown status, as reported in the studies.

Quality assessment

The Newcastle–Ottawa Scale (NOS) was chosen to assess the quality of the included studies because it is widely used and well-suited for evaluating observational study designs such as cross-sectional, case–control, and cohort studies. It offers a clear and structured way to assess study quality by focusing on three important areas: how participants are selected, how comparable the study groups are, and how outcomes or exposures are measured. In addition, the tool is straightforward to apply and helps ensure consistency across reviewers. Its scoring system also makes it easier to compare the quality of different studies and to consider study quality during data analysis24. SSG and EMM independently assessed study quality, resolving disagreements with TDA and AAA. The assessment used nine scores across three categories: Selection (sample representativeness, size, response rate, exposure), Comparability (control of confounders), and Outcome (assessment and statistical test appropriateness) scoring six or higher out of nine were deemed high quality and included in the meta-analysis (Table 1).

Data analysis

Data were extracted into Excel and analyzed using STATA/SE version 17. Heterogeneity was assessed with the I² statistic, where values of 25%, 50%, and 75% indicated low, moderate, and high heterogeneity, respectively25. Due to high heterogeneity (I² = 98.1%, p < 0.001), a random-effects model was used to estimate pooled condom use prevalence. Publication bias was assessed using Egger’s and Begg’s tests (p ≤ 0.05). A Higgins’ I² statistic illustrated heterogeneity26. For the second outcome OR with 95% CI assessed about condom use and its associated factors. Tables and forest plots summarized the selected studies and findings.

Results

Study selection and identification

A search of electronic databases, including PubMed, Google Scholar, Embase, and Cochrane Library, identified 1360 articles. After removing duplicates and irrelevant studies based on titles and abstracts, 675 articles were excluded. Fourteen papers were removed due to unclear results or lack of full text. From these three articles were not retrieved. Ultimately, 12 articles met the inclusion criteria and were included in the review. The PRISMA flow diagram (Fig. 1) illustrates this process.

The PRISMA flow diagram illustrates the article selection process for the systematic review and meta-analysis, based on PRISMA (2020) guidelines.

Characteristics of included studies

This review incorporated a total of 12 cross-sectional studies conducted between 2012 and 2022, all of which were carried out in health institution settings, with a meta-analysis of 4,657 participants aged 15 and older, and sample sizes ranging from 233 to 67710,17. Condom use prevalence varied across Ethiopian healthcare settings, with a minimum of 44% at Felegehiwot Referral Hospital and a maximum of 78.9% at University of Gondar Hospital10,11. Four regions and one city administration (Addis Ababa) were represented for this review. Eight from the Amhara region10–12,15,18–20,27, one from Addis Ababa city administration17, one from Sidama Region13, one from Tigray region16 and one from Benshangul Gumuz region14. No studies were found in Afar, Somali, South Nations Nationalities, Gambella, Southwest Ethiopia, or Harari regions. Most studies reported a high response rate (> 90%), likely due to the use of interviewer-administered questionnaires (Table 2).

Quality of included study

Our quality assessment showed that all included studies had strong methodological reliability. Newcastle-Ottawa Scale (NOS) scores ranged from 6 to 9 out of 9, indicating high quality. The level of bias among the studies included in the final analysis were determined by NOS scoring and agreement.

Prevalence of condom use among PLWHA on ART

The pooled prevalence of condom use in Ethiopia was found to be 56% based on the overall analysis (95% CI: (51, 61)). Significant heterogeneity was found (I2 = 93.01%, p < 0.001), so the random-effects model with the Der Simonian and Laird method was used to estimate the overall effect (Fig. 2).

Forest plot illustrating the overall pooled prevalence of condom use in Ethiopia.

Sub-group analysis

A high level of heterogeneity was observed across the included studies (I² = 93.01%). To explore potential sources of this variation, subgroup analyses were performed based on study region. Regionally, the highest pooled prevalence of condom use among PLWHA on ART was found in the Amhara region at 58% (95% CI: 51–66, I² = 94.79%), while the lowest was reported in the Tigray region at 49% (95% CI: 42–56, I² =. % reported). In terms of sample size, studies with < 400 participants showed a higher pooled prevalence of 57.0% (95% CI: 49.0–65.0, I² = 95.39%) (Table 3).

Factors associated with condom use

Previous studies identified marital status, sex, education, residence, disclosure status, and condom use counseling as key factors influencing condom use in Ethiopia. This study focused on the relationship between condom use and marital status across five studies14–17,19. The random-effects model estimate showed that the odds of condom use were 60% lower among married PLWHA compared to those who were not married (AOR = 0.40, 95% CI: 0.13, 0.66). Statistically significant heterogeneity was observed between the studies (I² = 41.29%, p < 0.001) (Fig. 4).

In order to assess the association between condom use and sex, three studies11,13,17 were evaluated. Based on the findings of these studies, condom use is more common among men than women but, it is not statistically significant (AOR = 2.11; 95%CI: 0.10, 4.12).

In order to examine the association between condom use and educational status, three studies11,13,14 were evaluated. Based on the findings of these studies, no statistically significant association was found between condom use and educational status (AOR = 1.01; 95%CI: −0.30, 2.32).

In this study, the relationship between condom use and residence was investigated by examining three studies12–14. According to the random-effects model estimate, PLWHA in urban areas were 2.63 times more likely to use condoms compared to those residing in rural areas (AOR = 2.63, 95% CI: 1.29, 3.97). There was statistically significant heterogeneity observed between the studies (I2 = 0.00%, p-value < 0.001) (Fig. 5).

In this study, the relationship between condom use and disclosure of HIV status was investigated by examining two studies16,17. According to the random-effects model estimate, PLWHA who had not disclosed their HIV status were 79% less likely to use condoms compared to those who had disclosed their HIV status (AOR = 0.21, 95% CI: 0.12, 0.31). Discussions

The systematic review and meta-analysis on condom use among people living with HIV/AIDS on ART in Ethiopia highlights prevalence and key factors. This discussion explores its findings and implications.

The study conducted a comprehensive search and identified a total of 12 studies that met the inclusion criteria. The pooled prevalence of condom use among PLWHA on ART was found to be 56% (95% CI: 51%, 61%). This finding aligns with a study conducted in the Asian-Pacific region, which reported that 57% of PLWHA consistently practiced condom use during sexual intercourse28. Studies conducted in USA and Nigeria were 53% and 51.5%, respectively29,30.

This finding indicates a lower prevalence of consistent condom use compared to other studies conducted in different regions. For instance, a study conducted in Brazil reported a higher rate of 71.3% of PLWHA practicing consistent condom use31. Similarly, studies conducted in Tanzania, Kenya, and Namibia reported a higher prevalence of 77%32 while a study conducted in Uganda reported a rate of 65%33 for consistent condom use among PLWHA. However, this study reported a higher prevalence of condom use compared to studies conducted in rural China, Egypt, and Nigeria, which reported condom use prevalence rates of 23.5%, 45%, and 48.4% respectively34–36. The difference in findings could be attributed to variations in study methodology and socioeconomic factors across the different regions. This study also higher than the systematic review and meta-analysis done in Sub-Saharan Africa and Ethiopia which is 42.52%21 and 50.56%22 respectively. This difference could be attributed to variations in study population that means this study on PLWHA who take ART and socioeconomic factors across the different regions. Generally this finding highlights the importance of condom use as a protective measure against HIV transmission, even among individuals who are already receiving ART.

Marital status significantly influenced condom use, with married PLWHA 60% less likely to use condoms than unmarried individuals. This aligns with a Ugandan study33. The study did not find any significant associations between condom use and variables such as sex, educational status, and counseling about condom use among the study group.

Our meta-analysis found that urban PLWHA were 2.63 times more likely to use condoms than those in rural areas, highlighting the need for targeted rural interventions.

PLWHA who had not disclosed their status were 79% less likely to use condoms. This aligns with studies from Tanzania, Kenya, and Namibia, where disclosure was linked to higher condom use32.

Despite these important findings, some limitations should be considered. First, all the included studies were cross-sectional, which means they capture information at one point in time and cannot establish cause-and-effect relationships. Second, since all articles were in English, language bias may affect generalizability. Third, condom use was self-reported, which can lead to over-reporting due to social desirability or recall bias. In addition, since some regions of Ethiopia were underrepresented, the findings may not fully reflect the national situation.

Even with these limitations, the study has several strengths that support the reliability of its findings. A systematic and transparent approach was used to identify and select studies, and standard methods were applied to assess study quality and combine results. The fact that the findings are generally consistent with studies from other countries also adds confidence in their validity.

Looking ahead, these findings provide useful guidance for both research and practice. Future studies should consider using longitudinal designs to better understand how and why condom use behaviors change over time. More research is also needed in rural and underrepresented areas to ensure a more complete picture. From a practical perspective, interventions should focus on promoting condom use within marriage, encouraging safe and supportive disclosure of HIV status, and addressing the specific challenges faced by rural communities. Strengthening health education, improving access to services, and integrating behavioral support into ART programs could all help improve condom use.

Conclusion and recommendation

A small proportion of respondents reported using condoms, but marital status, urban residence, and receiving counseling were linked to higher condom use, which could reduce the risk of sexually transmitted infections and HIV transmission. To improve outcomes, health education and counseling on consistent condom use during ART appointments are essential. Encouraging open communication about safe sex and tailoring counseling to individual needs, including marital status and urban residence, can further promote condom use. In summary, health education, counseling, and personalized support are key strategies to increase condom use and reduce health risks.
