Multi-Pass Streaming Lower Bounds for Uniformity Testing
Qian Li, Xin Lyu

Abstract
We prove multi-pass streaming lower bounds for uniformity testing over a domain of size 2 ​ m 2m . The tester receives a stream of n n i.i.d. samples and must distinguish (i) the uniform distribution on [ 2 ​ m ] [2m] from (ii) a Paninski-style planted distribution in which, for each pair ( 2 ​ i − 1 , 2 ​ i ) (2i-1,2i) , the probabilities are biased left or right by ϵ / 2 ​ m \epsilon/2m . We show that any ℓ \ell -pass streaming algorithm using space s s and achieving constant advantage must satisfy the tradeoff s ​ n ​ ℓ = Ω ~ ​ ( m / ϵ 2 ) sn\ell=\tilde{\Omega}(m/\epsilon^{2}) . This extends the one-pass lower bound of Diakonikolas, Gouleakis, Kane, and Rao (2019) to multiple passes. Our proof has two components. First, we develop a hybrid argument, inspired by Dinur (2020), that reduces streaming to two-player communication problems. This reduction relies on a new perspective on hardness: we identify the source of hardness as uncertainty in the bias directions, rather than the collision locations. Second, we prove a strong lower bound for a basic two-player communication task, in which Alice and Bob must decide whether two random sign vectors Y a , Y b ∈ { ± 1 } m Y^{a},Y^{b}\in\{\pm 1\}^{m} are independent or identical, yet they cannot observe the signs directly—only noisy local views of each coordinate. Our techniques may be of independent use for other streaming problems with stochastic inputs.

Introduction

Uniformity testing is one of the most fundamental distribution testing tasks: given
n
independent samples from an unknown distribution
P
over a domain of size
2m
, the goal is to distinguish whether
P
is uniform or
\epsilon
-far from uniform under the total variation distance. Its simplicity makes it a canonical benchmark for understanding the interplay between statistical and computational resources, and insights obtained here routinely inform more complex testing problems; see
C+ 22
for a recent survey on uniformity testing and related problems.

In this paper, we will focus on the streaming model, in which samples
X_{1},\ldots,X_{n}
are given as a stream and the algorithm must operate with limited memory
s
. The streaming setting captures modern large-scale learning scenarios: the learning algorithm scans the massive dataset sequentially, processing samples one at a time and updating the parameters continuously.

Uniformity testing in the streaming model has been studied in several works
(
DGKR 19
;
Mei 20
;
BOS 22
;
CY 24
)
.
On the upper bound side, there is a folklore one-pass tester (see e.g.,
BOS 22
) which uses
s=\tilde{O}(1)
bits of memory and
n=O(m/\epsilon^{2})
samples. Here,
\tilde{O}
and
\tilde{\Omega}
omit
\mathrm{polylog}(m+n+1/\epsilon)
terms. Beyond this small space regime,
DGKR 19
developed a streaming algorithm achieving the sample-space tradeoff
sn=\tilde{O}(m/\epsilon^{4})
when
s\leq\min\{m^{0.9},n\log m\}
. The applicable space regime was later extended to
s\leq\min\{m\log m,n\log m\}
by
CY 24
. On the lower bound side,
DGKR 19
established an unconditional lower bound
sn=\Omega(m/\epsilon^{2})
, matching the folklore tester when
s=\tilde{O}(1)
; they also showed that the upper bound
sn=\tilde{O}(m/\epsilon^{4})
is tight for large memory size, specifically for
s=\tilde{\Omega}\left(m^{0.24}/\epsilon^{8/3}+m^{0.1}/\epsilon^{4}\right)
.

All of the above results, however, apply only to one-pass streaming algorithms. In practice, learning algorithms typically make multiple scans over the training samples, especially when the available data is limited. This naturally motivates the study of the multi-pass setting.
DGKR 19
identified the analysis of uniform testing under multiple passes as an open problem; however, extending their information-theoretic lower bound technique to the multi-pass setting seems challenging.

In this paper, we extend the unconditional lower bound
sn=\tilde{\Omega}(m/\epsilon^{2})
to the multi-pass streaming model (Theorem
1.1
), showing that the folklore tester remains tight even when a polylogarithmic number of passes is allowed.
Following prior works, we employ the Paninski problems as the hard instance: given a stream

1.1
Proof Approach

The proof of Theorem
1.1
consists of two steps.

Step I: hybrid argument

We develop a hybrid argument that reduces streaming to two-player communication problems. It hinges on a different view of the source of hardness: rather than in locating collisions, we understand the hardness stemming from resolving the unknown bias directions
Y\in\{0,1\}^{m}
. Indeed, if
Y
were revealed, by mapping each sample to the heavy side, the Paninski problem collapses to distinguishing a fair coin from a
\epsilon
-biased one, and thus can be solved quite efficiently.

Motivated by this perspective, we define a family of hybrid distributions
\{D^{k,n/k}\}
over streams of length
n
: partition the stream into
k
contiguous blocks of size
n/k
, and draw each block from the planted bias distribution with a fresh bias direction
Y^{i}
. The planted case corresponds to
D^{1,n}
(a single hidden direction used for the entire stream), and the extreme
D_{n,1}
(a fresh direction per sample) is essentially the uniform case. For two distributions
D_{0},D_{1}
over random streams, let
\mathrm{Adv}_{s,\ell}(D_{0},D_{1})
denote the maximum distinguishing advantage achievable by an
\ell
-pass streaming algorithm using
s
bits of space. Then we can obtain a recurrence:

f(n):=\mathrm{Adv}_{s,\ell}(D_{n,1},D_{1,n})\leq 2f(n/2)+\mathrm{Adv}_{s,\ell}(D_{2,n/2},D_{1,n}),

so it suffices to upper bound the advantage in the one-swap case
\mathrm{Adv}_{s,\ell}(D_{2,n/2},D_{1,n})
. Equivalently, we partition the stream into two halves, each generated with a single bias direction, and ask whether the two halves use identical or independent directions.

Step II: the hidden-sign problem

In this step, we analyze a clean two-player communication problem (Definition
3.4
): Alice and Bob must decide whether two hidden sign vectors
Y^{a},Y^{b}\in\{\pm 1\}^{m}
are identical or independent; however neither player can see the signs directly, but only noisy local views per coordinate. In our setting, for each sign
Y_{i}\in\{-1,1\}
, the player can see
\mathrm{Poi}(n/m)
independent samples from
\mathrm{Bernuolli}(1/2+Y_{i}\epsilon/2)
.

We prove that any
C
-bit communication protocol has distinguishing advantage at most
\tilde{O}(C\epsilon^{2}\cdot(n/m))
. Depending on whether
n
is greater than
m
or otherwise, the argument is slightly different.

When
n\leq m
.
Here the two sides observe (independent) fractions of the
m
coordinates. Intuitively it is the case that both players must first search for “shared coordinates”— indices
i
such that both players observe samples about the
i
-th coordinate. The hardness of this problem can be connected to the well-known problem of Set Disjointness, where players hold subsets of
[n]
and they want to decide/search for intersections. A general phenomenon here is that on average they need to communicate
m/n
bits to agree on a new common coordinate. Moreover, every new coordinate only offers
\epsilon^{2}
distinguishing advantage: essentially, this stems from the fact that the TV distance between a pair of uniform and independent bits
(X,Y)
, and a pair of marginally-uniform but
\epsilon^{2}
-correlated bits
(X^{\prime},Y^{\prime})
, is exactly
\epsilon^{2}
.

So, overall, the intuition of the lower bound can be described as: with
C
bits of communication, the two players can find roughly
\frac{Cn}{m}
“common” coordinates, on which both of them receive samples. Each of these coordinates increases their advantage by at most
\epsilon^{2}
, and we take a union bound to obtain the conclusion.

Our proof formalizes the intuition through a series of reductions, starting from the lower bound for the Unique Set Disjointness problem with small advantage
BM 13
;
GW 16
;
Din 20
.

When
n\geq m
.
This is the case where the two sides (with high probability) observe samples from most if not all coordinates. Moreover, players typically get multiple independent samples per coordinate. Intuitively this makes the distinguishing task easier: consider an extreme case, where players receive arbitrarily many samples per coordinate. Then, by taking the majority votes per coordinate, players can recover
Y^{a}
and
Y^{b}
locally and solve the problem with a constant communication complexity. This shows that the number of available samples per coordinate (namely
n/m
) will play a key role in the analysis.

We will prove a key reduction result, which intuitively says that for any
k
moderately large (say, larger than
\log(m)
),
\mathrm{Poi}(k)
many samples from
\mathrm{Bernuolli}(1/2+Y_{i}\epsilon/2)
is roughly as useful as a single sample from
\mathrm{Bernuolli}(1/2+Y_{i}\epsilon^{\prime}/2)
for some
\epsilon^{\prime}\approx\epsilon\sqrt{k\log(1/\delta)}
, up to a statistical slackness of
\delta
. Our reduction draws on amplification/composition techniques from differential privacy (e.g.
DRV 10
). In the end, we can reduce the case of
n\gg m
to the case that
n\approx m
but with larger
\epsilon^{\prime}\approx\epsilon\sqrt{n/m}
. Then we use the already established bound of
\tilde{O}(C(\epsilon^{\prime})^{2}(n/n))=\tilde{O}(C\epsilon^{2}\cdot(n/m))
to complete the proof.

3.2
The Hybrid Method

The core of our argument is the following derivation. Using the triangle inequality, we see that:

\displaystyle\mathrm{Adv}(D_{\mathrm{unif}},D_{\mathrm{bias}})\pm O(\frac{1}{n})

\displaystyle=\mathrm{Adv}\left(D^{n,1},D^{1,n}\right)

\displaystyle\leq\mathrm{Adv}\left(D^{n,1},D^{2,\frac{n}{2}}\right)+\mathrm{Adv}\left(D^{2,\frac{n}{2}},D^{1,n}\right)

\displaystyle\leq\mathrm{Adv}\left(D^{n,1},D^{\frac{n}{2},1}\circ D^{1,\frac{n}{2}}\right)+\mathrm{Adv}\left(D^{\frac{n}{2},1}\circ D^{1,\frac{n}{2}},D^{2,\frac{n}{2}}\right)+\mathrm{Adv}\left(D^{2,\frac{n}{2}},D^{1,n}\right)

\displaystyle\leq 2\mathrm{Adv}\left(D^{\frac{n}{2},1},D^{1,\frac{n}{2}}\right)+\mathrm{Adv}\left(D^{2,\frac{n}{2}},D^{1,n}\right)

We justify the inequalities. The second line is clearly the triangle inequality. The third line is by applying the triangle inequality among the triple
(D^{n,1}\to D^{n/2,1}\to D^{1,n/2},D^{2,n/2})

To see the last line, we can first write

The recursion.

Next, we perform a worst-case to average-case reduction, to lift the lower bound of UDISJ to a lower bound for the HIP problem.

The Noisy HIP.

In order to facilitate the analysis of the Hidden Sign Problem, we introduce the following
noisy
Hidden Index Problem, NHIP for short.

Definition 4.4

(Noisy Hidden Index Problem,
\mathrm{NHIP}_{n}
)
.

Alice and Bob each is given a randomized string
a,b\in\{1,0,\star\}^{3n}
. First
\mathrm{supp}(a)
and
\mathrm{supp}(b)
are sampled as follows:

•

Draw
i\sim[3n]
, and two
disjoint
random subsets
S_{1},S_{2}
each of size
n-1
from
[3n]\setminus\{i\}
.

•

\mathrm{supp}(a)=S_{1}\sqcup\{i\}
and
\mathrm{supp}(a)=S_{2}\sqcup\{i\}
.

Note that
|a|=|b|=n
and
|a\cap b|=1
. Their goal is to distinguish the following two cases:

•

Case “
=
”: Draw
Y^{a}_{i}=Y^{b}_{i}\sim\{\pm 1\}
. For any other
a_{j}
or
b_{k}
in the support of
a
and
b
, draw
Y^{a}_{j}
(
Y^{b}_{k}
) independently from
\{\pm 1\}
.

•

Case “
\neq
”: Draw
Y^{a}_{i}=1-{Y^{b}_{i}}\sim\{\pm 1\}
. For any other
a_{j}
or
b_{k}
in the supports of
a
and
b
, draw
Y^{a}_{j}
(
Y^{b}_{k}
) independently from
\{\pm 1\}
.

From the hidden
Y^{a}_{j}
, Alice observes an
a_{j}\sim\mathrm{Bernuolli}(\frac{1}{2}-\epsilon Y_{j}^{a})
for every
j\in\mathrm{supp}(a)
. Symmetrically, from the hidden
Y^{b}_{j}
, Bob observes a
b_{j}\sim\mathrm{Bernuolli}(\frac{1}{2}-\epsilon Y_{j}^{b})
for every
j\in\mathrm{supp}(b)
. We emphasize that Alice and Bob cannot observe
Y^{a}
(
Y^{b}
) directly.

For NHIP, we prove a lower bound that is, roughly speaking, stronger by a factor of
\frac{1}{\varepsilon^{2}}
than that for HIP.

Lemma 4.5
.

\mathrm{Adv}_{n,\epsilon}^{NHIP}(\Pi)\leq 2\epsilon^{2}\left(\frac{400C}{n}+\frac{160000\log n}{n}\right)
for any
C
-bit public-coin randomized protocol
\Pi
.

Proof.

We perform a reduction. Let
\Pi
be a
C
-bit public-coin protocol
\Pi
for NHIP.

Let
D_{=}
be the joint (between Alice and Bob) input distribution of NHIP for the “
=
” case, and let
D_{\neq}
be that for the “
\neq
” case. Also, let
E_{=}
(resp.
E_{\neq}
) be the input distribution of HIP (
Definition

4.1
) for the “
=
” (resp. “
\neq
”) case.

Note that
D_{=}
can be written as a mixture of
D^{i}_{=}
’s where
D^{i}_{\neq}
denotes the input distribution conditioned on the event that
a\cap b=\{i\}
. Similar decompositions exist for
D_{\neq},E_{=},E_{\neq}
. Fix one such
i
. Let us study
D_{=}^{i}
and
D_{\neq}^{i}
closely. One can easily see that

\displaystyle D_{=}^{i}=\left(\left(\frac{1}{2}+\epsilon\right)^{2}+\left(\frac{1}{2}-\epsilon\right)^{2}\right)E_{=}^{i}+2\left(\frac{1}{2}-\epsilon\right)\left(\frac{1}{2}+\epsilon\right)E_{\neq}^{i}.

To see this, simply note that the inputs are induced from the same value of
Y_{i}^{a}
and
Y_{i}^{b}
. Through the Bernoulli sampling with bias
\frac{1}{2}\pm\epsilon
, it follows that the both inputs agree on the
i
-th coordinate with probability
(\frac{1}{2}-\epsilon)^{2}+(\frac{1}{2}-\epsilon)^{2}
, and, if they agree, they agree on either
0
or
1
with equal probability. Similar reasoning applies for the other case of non-agreement on the
i
-th coordinate.

Using a similar reasoning, we also obtain that

\displaystyle D_{\neq}^{i}=\left(\left(\frac{1}{2}+\epsilon\right)^{2}+\left(\frac{1}{2}-\epsilon\right)^{2}\right)E_{\neq}^{i}+2\left(\frac{1}{2}-\epsilon\right)\left(\frac{1}{2}+\epsilon\right)E_{=}^{i}.

Let us now define
E^{i}_{\text{common}}=\frac{1}{2}E^{i}_{\neq}+\frac{1}{2}E^{i}_{=}
, and write

\displaystyle D_{=}^{i}

\displaystyle=(1-2\epsilon^{2})E^{i}_{\text{common}}+2\epsilon^{2}E^{i}_{=},

\displaystyle D_{\neq}^{i}

\displaystyle=(1-2\epsilon^{2})E^{i}_{\text{common}}+2\epsilon^{2}E^{i}_{\neq}.

Define
E_{\text{common}}
to be the uniform mixture of
E^{i}_{\text{common}}
. We see that

\displaystyle D_{=}

\displaystyle=(1-2\epsilon^{2})E_{\text{common}}+2\epsilon^{2}E_{=},

\displaystyle D_{\neq}

\displaystyle=(1-2\epsilon^{2})E_{\text{common}}+2\epsilon^{2}E_{\neq}.

We now make use of
Lemma

4.3
and it immediately follows that

\displaystyle\mathrm{Adv}_{n}^{NHIP}(\Pi)=2\epsilon^{2}\mathrm{Adv}_{n}^{HIP}(\Pi)=2\epsilon^{2}\left(\frac{400C}{n}+\frac{160000\log n}{n}\right),

as claimed.
∎

Noisy HIP with general observations.

Finally, in order to analyze the game from
Definition

3.4
, we will have to work with a variant of Noisy HIP where we observe not bits but some other forms of signal emitted from two similar sources.

Definition 4.6

(Noisy Hidden Index Problem with General Observation, NHIPG)
.

Let
\epsilon,\delta
be parameters, and
\Gamma_{0},\Gamma_{1}
be a pair of
(\epsilon,\delta)
-indistinguishable distributions.

We consider a similar setup as in
Definition

4.4
: Alice and Bob are given uniformly random subsets
A
and
B
of
[3n]
, each of size
n
, subject to the condition that
|A\cap B|=1
. Then, the implicit variables
Y^{a}\in\{\pm 1\}^{A}
and
Y^{b}\in\{\pm 1\}^{B}
are drawn according to the “
=
” or the “
\neq
” case. Then, Alice and Bob each observe the following:

•

From Alice’s side, for each
j\in A
, Alice observes
a_{j}\sim\Gamma_{Y^{a}_{j}}
.

•

From Bob’s side, for each
k\in B
, Bob observes
b_{k}\sim\Gamma_{Y^{b}_{k}}
.

The goal for Alice and Bob is to distinguish between the case of “
=
” and that of “
\neq
”.

Generally, the instantiation of NHIPG will depend on the choice of
\Gamma_{0},\Gamma_{1}
. However, we will still use the notation
\mathrm{Adv}_{n,\epsilon,\delta}^{NHIPG}(\Pi)
to denote the
maximum
achievable advantage of the protocol
\Pi
on solving
any
instantiation of NHIPG with a pair of
(\epsilon,\delta)
-indistinguishable source distributions
\Pi_{0},\Pi_{1}
. With this notational convention, we state and prove the following lemma.

Lemma 4.7
.

Assume
\epsilon<0.5
. We have
\mathrm{Adv}_{n,\epsilon,\delta}^{NHIPG}(\Pi)\leq\epsilon^{2}\left(\frac{400C}{n}+\frac{160000\log n}{n}\right)+2\delta n
for any
C
-bit public-coin protocol
\Pi
.

Proof.

An easy fact is that
\mathrm{Adv}_{n,\epsilon,\delta}^{NHIPG}(\Pi)\leq\mathrm{Adv}_{n,\epsilon,0}^{NHIPG}(\Pi)+2\delta n
. This follows for a simple reason: suppose the observations are drawn from
\Gamma_{0},\Gamma_{1}
. Then, whenever Alice (resp. Bob) is to observe a sample from
\Gamma_{b}
, we can think of it as observing a sample from the mixture distribution
(1-\delta)\Gamma^{\prime}_{b}+\delta\Gamma^{e}_{b}
, and it follows that the sample is drawn from
\Gamma^{e}_{b}
with probability
\delta
. Thus, with probability
1-2\delta n
, none of the samples given to Alice/Bob is drawn from
\Gamma^{e}_{b}
. We can therefore analyze the advantage for the pair of
(\epsilon,0)
-indistinguishable source distributions
\Gamma^{\prime}_{0},\Gamma^{\prime}_{1}
.

In the following, we will just assume
\Gamma_{0},\Gamma_{1}
are
(\epsilon,0)
-indistinguishable and prove an upper bound on
\mathrm{Adv}_{n,\epsilon,0}^{NHIPG}(\Pi)
. We will make use of a well-known fact: if
\Gamma_{0},\Gamma_{1}
are
(\epsilon,0)
-indistinguishable, then there exists a pair of distributions
\Gamma^{c}_{0},\Gamma^{c}_{1}
such that we can write
\Gamma_{0},\Gamma_{1}
as the following mixture distributions:

\displaystyle\Gamma_{0}

\displaystyle=\frac{e^{\epsilon}}{1+e^{\epsilon}}\Gamma^{c}_{0}+\frac{1}{1+e^{\epsilon}}\Gamma^{c}_{1},

\displaystyle\Gamma_{1}

\displaystyle=\frac{e^{\epsilon}}{1+e^{\epsilon}}\Gamma^{c}_{1}+\frac{1}{1+e^{\epsilon}}\Gamma^{c}_{0}.

We now describe a reduction from NHIP to NHIPG. Suppose now we are tasked to solve NHIP with noise level
\epsilon^{\prime}=\frac{1}{2}-\frac{1}{1+e^{\epsilon}}\leq\epsilon
. We design a protocol
\Pi^{\prime}
for this using
\Pi
as a black box. Let
(a,b)
be the (randomized) inputs to Alice and Bob in the NHIP problem. We know that, for each
j\in\mathrm{supp}(a)
, it holds that
a_{j}\sim\mathrm{Bernuolli}(\frac{1}{2}-\epsilon^{\prime}Y^{a}_{j})
. Let us post-process
a_{j}
by drawing
a^{\prime}_{j}\sim\Gamma^{c}_{a_{j}}
using independent random coins available to us (by the public-randomness assumption). Composing this sampling step with
a_{j}\sim\mathrm{Bernuolli}(\frac{1}{2}-\epsilon^{\prime}Y^{a}_{j})
, we see that conditioning on
Y_{a}^{j}
,
a^{\prime}_{j}
is distributed as

\displaystyle a^{\prime}_{j}=(\frac{1}{2}-\epsilon^{\prime})\Gamma^{c}_{Y_{j}^{a}}+(\frac{1}{2}+\epsilon^{\prime})\Gamma^{c}_{1-Y_{j}^{a}}=\Gamma_{Y_{j}^{a}}.

Similar reasoning holds for the Bob’s side as well. Hence, it follows that if we independently post-process every
a_{j}
and
b_{k}
for
j\in\mathrm{supp}(a)
and
k\in\mathrm{supp}(b)
, we obtain an instance
(a^{\prime},b^{\prime})
for the NHIPG problem. We then run the protocol
\Pi
between Alice and Bob on the instance.

To analyze the advantage, note that if
(a,b)
was drawn from the “
=
” (resp. “
\neq
”) case of NHIP,
(a^{\prime},b^{\prime})
is distributed as the “
=
” (resp. “
\neq
”) case of NHIPG. Hence, it immediately follows that

\displaystyle\mathrm{Adv}_{n,\epsilon,0}^{NHIPG}(\Pi)=\mathrm{Adv}_{n,\epsilon^{\prime}}^{NHIP}(\Pi^{\prime})\leq\epsilon^{2}\left(\frac{400C}{n}+\frac{160000\log n}{n}\right),

as desired.
∎

4.2
From Unique Intersection to Statistical Subsamples

In the last section, we were always analyzing the case where Alice and Bob each observe a subset
[3n]
with the promise that their observations intersect at exactly one coordinate. There is an evident gap from this to the Hidden Sign Problem: namely in the Hidden Sign Problem, each party observes a random subset with expected size
\theta n
, with
no
promise on the intersection size between the two observed subsets from two parties.

Generally speaking, all the variants of hidden index problems from the last section can be described by a pair of source distributions
\Gamma_{0},\Gamma_{1}
, from which Alice and Bob draw observations. With this in mind, we consider the following generalization of Hidden Sign Problem as follows:

Definition 4.8

(Generalized Hidden Sign, GHS)
.

Let
\Gamma_{0},\Gamma_{1}
be a pair of distributions. Let
n\in\mathbb{N},\theta\in[0,1]
be parameters. Define the statistical HiddenSign problem: Alice and Bob first sample subsets
\mathrm{supp}(a)
and
\mathrm{supp}(b)
via Poisson subsampling: namely each
i\in[n]
is independently included in
\mathrm{supp}(a)
with probability
\theta
. Similarly, each
j
is included in
\mathrm{supp}(b)
with probability
\theta
.

Let
Y^{a},Y^{b}\in\{\pm 1\}^{n}
be two strings of length
n
, sampled dependent on the case as follows:

•

Case “
=
”:
Y^{a}=Y^{b}\sim\{\pm 1\}^{n}
.

•

Case “
\perp
”:
Y^{a}
and
Y^{b}
are independently drawn from
\{\pm 1\}^{n}
.

Then, Alice and Bob receive inputs sampled from the following distribution:

•

Alice receives, for each
i\in\mathrm{supp}(a)
, a sample
a_{i}\sim\Gamma_{Y^{a}_{i}}
.

•

Bob receives, for each
i\in\mathrm{supp}(b)
, a sample
b_{i}\sim\Gamma_{Y^{b}_{i}}
.

The goal for Alice and Bob is to distinguish between the two cases.

We are ready to state the meta-theorem, as described below.

Theorem 4.9
.

Suppose
\Gamma_{0}
and
\Gamma_{1}
are
(\epsilon,\delta)
-indistinguishable. Consider the General Hidden Sign problem with
\Gamma_{0},\Gamma_{1}
and parameters
n,\theta
. Then, for every
C\geq\log(n)
, it holds that

\displaystyle\mathrm{Adv^{GHS}_{\theta,\epsilon,\delta}}(\Pi)\leq O(\epsilon^{2}C\theta+2n\delta).

Proof.

First of all, we can “pay” the price of
2n\delta
and turn to analyze a pair of sources
(\Gamma_{0},\Gamma_{1})
that is
(\epsilon,0)
-indistinguishable.

Proof for
\theta\in[0,1/2)
.

We first prove the bound for the case of
\theta<\frac{1}{2}
.

We use
q_{\cap}
,
q_{a}
, and
q_{b}
to denote the sizes of
|\mathrm{supp}(a)\cap\mathrm{supp}(b)|
,
|\mathrm{supp}(a)|
, and
|\mathrm{supp}(b)|
respectively. We use
\mathrm{Adv}_{q_{\cap},q_{a},q_{b}}
to denote the maximum advantage of
\Pi
conditioned on
(q_{\cap},q_{a},q_{b})
; that is,

\displaystyle\mathrm{Adv}_{q_{\cap},q_{a},q_{b}}=\sup_{\Pi}\left|\Pr_{D_{=}}[\Pi(a,b)\mbox{ outputs }\text{``$=$''}\mid q_{\cap},q_{a},q_{b}]-\Pr_{D_{\perp}}[\Pi(a,b)\mbox{ outputs }\text{``$=$''}\mid q_{\cap},q_{a},q_{b}]\right|.

We have

\mathrm{Adv}^{GHS}_{\theta,\epsilon,\delta}\leq\mathbb{E}\left[\mathrm{Adv}_{q_{\cap},q_{a},q_{b}}\right].

One way to see this is to note that we can grant Alice and Bob the knowledge of
q_{\cap},q_{a},q_{b}
, which only make the distinguishing task easier. Moreover, conditioned on
q_{\cap},q_{a},q_{b}
, we know that
\mathrm{supp}(a)
and
\mathrm{supp}(b)
are two uniform subsets of size
q_{a}
and
q_{b}
with an intersection size
q_{\cap}
.

Furthermore, let
\mathrm{Adv}^{k}_{q_{\cap},q_{a},q_{b}}
(for
0\leq k\leq q_{\cap}
) denote the maximum advantage of any protocol
\Pi
on distinguishing the following two cases:

•

D_{=^{k}\perp^{q_{\cap}-k}}
:
Y^{a}_{i}=Y^{b}_{i}
for
k
random indices
i
in
\mathrm{supp}(a)\cap\mathrm{supp}(b)
, and all other
Y_{a}^{j}
and
Y^{j}_{b}
are independently drawn.

•

D_{=^{k+1}\perp^{q_{\cap}-k-1}}
: similarly
Y^{a}_{i}=Y^{b}_{i}
for
(k+1)
random indices in
\mathrm{supp}(a)\cap\mathrm{supp}(b)
, and other bits of
Y_{a}
and
Y_{b}
are independently drawn.

Formally, we define

\mathrm{Adv}^{k}_{q_{\cap},q_{a},q_{b}}:=\sup_{\Pi}\left|\Pr_{D_{=^{k+1}\perp^{q_{\cap}-k-1}}}[\Pi(a,b)\mbox{ outputs }=\mid q_{\cap},q_{a},q_{b}]-\Pr_{D_{=^{k}\perp^{q_{\cap}-k}}}[\Pi(a,b)\mbox{ outputs }=\mid q_{\cap},q_{a},q_{b}]\right|.

The following claim will be central to us.

Claim 4.10
.

It holds that

\displaystyle\mathrm{Adv}_{q_{\cap},q_{a},q_{b}}\leq\sum_{k=0}^{q_{\cap}}\mathrm{Adv}^{k}_{q_{\cap},q_{a},q_{b}}\leq q_{\cap}\cdot\mathrm{Adv}_{1,q_{a}-q_{\cap}+1,q_{b}-q_{\cap}+1}\leq q_{\cap}\cdot\epsilon^{2}C\cdot O\left(\frac{1}{q_{a}-q_{\cap}+1}+\frac{1}{q_{b}-q_{\cap}+1}\right).

Proof.

The first inequality is by the triangle inequality. For the second inequality, we prove that
\mathrm{Adv}^{k}_{n,q_{\cap},q_{a},q_{b}}\leq\mathrm{Adv}_{n-q_{\cap}+1,1,q_{a}-q_{\cap}+1,q_{b}-q_{\cap}+1}
. Indeed, consider the natural coupling between
D_{=^{k}\perp^{q_{\cap}-k}}
and
D_{=^{k+1}\perp^{q_{\cap}-k-1}}
. Namely, we couple the realizations of
D_{=^{k}\perp^{q_{\cap}-k}}
and
D_{=^{k+1}\perp^{q_{\cap}-k-1}}
in a way that, for both cases there are
k
common indices
i\in\mathrm{supp}(a)\cap\mathrm{supp}(b)
such that
Y_{i}^{a}=Y_{i}^{b}
, and there is exactly one additional
i^{\prime}\in\mathrm{supp}(a)\cap\mathrm{supp}(b)
such that
Y_{i^{\prime}}^{a}=Y_{i^{\prime}}^{b}
for the case of
D_{=^{k+1}\perp^{q_{\cap}-k-1}}
.

Now, we can grant Alice and Bob the knowledge of the
k
common indices for which
Y^{a}_{i}=Y^{b}_{i}
. This does not make the distinguishing game harder. Consequently,

\displaystyle\mathrm{Adv}^{k}_{q_{\cap},q_{a},q_{b}}\leq\mathrm{Adv}_{1,q_{a}-q_{\cap}+1,q_{b}-q_{\cap}+1}.

It remains to justify the last inequality.
Let
n^{*}=\min(q_{a}-q_{\cap}+1,q_{b}-q_{\cap}+1)
. Assume without loss of generality that
q_{a}-q_{\cap}+1
is smaller. Our last observation is that we can grant Alice and Bob the knowledge of a
random
subset of
\mathrm{supp}(b)\setminus\mathrm{supp}(a)
of size
q_{b}-q_{a}
. This implies that

\displaystyle~~~~\mathrm{Adv}_{1,q_{a}-q_{\cap}+1,q_{b}-q_{\cap}+1}

\displaystyle\leq\mathrm{Adv}_{1,q_{a}-q_{\cap}+1,q_{a}-q_{\cap}+1}

\displaystyle\leq\epsilon^{2}C\cdot O\left(\max\left(\frac{1}{q_{a}-q_{\cap}+1},\frac{1}{q_{b}-q_{\cap}+1}\right)\right),

The last line follows because we have reduced the distinguishing game to a version of the Noisy Hidden Index Problem with generalization observations (c.f.
Definition

4.6
), and the inequality follows by
Lemma

4.7
.
∎

Given the claim, we can make use of
Lemma

4.12
(described at the end of the subsection) and deduce that

\mathrm{Adv}\leq O(1)\cdot\epsilon^{2}C\cdot\mathbb{E}\left[\frac{q_{\cap}}{q_{a}-q_{\cap}+1}+\frac{q_{\cap}}{q_{b}-q_{\cap}+1}\right]\leq O(1)\cdot\frac{\epsilon^{2}C\cdot\theta}{1-\theta},

as desired.

Proof for
\theta\in(1/2,1]
.

We now establish the proof for the case of
\theta>\frac{1}{2}
. We will in fact prove a stronger statement, which implies
Theorem

4.9
for all
\theta\geq\frac{1}{2}
. Namely, we prove that, for
\theta=1
, it holds that
\mathrm{Adv}^{GHS}_{1,\epsilon,0}\leq O(\epsilon^{2}\sqrt{C})
.

First, by a reduction argument similar to the proof of
Lemma

4.7
, we can without loss of generality consider the case that
\Gamma_{r}\equiv\mathrm{Bernuolli}(\frac{1}{2}-\epsilon r)
for
r\in\{\pm 1\}
. In this case, with
\theta=1
, Alice and Bob are tasked to distinguish between the following two cases:

•

Case 1: the inputs for Alice and Bob are a pair of independently generated bit strings
a\sim\{0,1\}^{n},b\sim\{0,1\}^{n}
.

•

Case 2: the inputs for Alice and Bob are a pair of
\epsilon^{2}
correlated bit strings: namely
a
and
b
and marginally uniform and every pair of bits
(a_{i},b_{i})
have correlation
\epsilon^{2}
.

We will make use of a powerful result from
HLPS 19
. To describe the result, we begin with necessary notation and setup. Let
(X,Y)
be the inputs to Alice and Bob in the correlated case, and
(\overline{X},\overline{Y})
be the inputs in the uniform case. Let
\Pi
be a communication protocol between Alice and Bob.

Let
P^{1}_{XY\Pi}
be the distribution of
(X,Y,\Pi)
(with the understanding the
\Pi
is induced from the inputs
(X,Y)
), and similarly
\overline{P}^{0}_{XY\Pi}
the distribution of
(\overline{X},\overline{Y},\Pi)
. Let
P^{1}_{X\Pi}
be the marginal distribution of
P^{1}_{XY\Pi}
on the
(X,\Pi)
part. Define
P^{0}_{X\Pi}
similarly.

Now, in our language, a remarkable result of
HLPS 19
says that (see their Remark 3):

\displaystyle D_{KL}(P^{1}_{X\Pi}\|P^{0}_{X\Pi})\leq\epsilon^{4}I(\Pi;X,Y).

(1)

Let
\xi
be the advantage of
\Pi
on distinguishing
(X,Y)
from
(\overline{X},\overline{Y})
. We set up the following experiment: flip a coin
R\sim\{1,2\}
. Depending on
R
being
1
or
2
, draw inputs
(x,y)
from either
(X,Y)
or
(\overline{X},\overline{Y})
. Finally run the protocol
\Pi
on
(x,y)
and obtain a verdict of
R
, denoted by
R^{\prime}
. By the assumed advantage of
\Pi
, we know that
\Pr[R=R^{\prime}]\geq\frac{1+\xi}{2}
. As a consequence, by Pinsker’s inequality it follows that
I(R;R^{\prime})\geq\frac{\xi^{2}}{4}
. Since we can obtain
R^{\prime}
from the communication protocol
\Pi
, it follows that

\displaystyle\frac{\xi^{2}}{4}

\displaystyle\leq I(R;\Pi)\leq I(R;\Pi,X)

\displaystyle\leq\frac{1}{2}D_{KL}(P^{1}_{X\Pi}\|P^{0}_{X\Pi})+\frac{1}{2}D_{KL}(P^{0}_{X\Pi}\|P^{0}_{X\Pi})

(the “radius” property of mutual info)

\displaystyle\leq\frac{1}{2}\epsilon^{4}I(\Pi;X,Y)\leq\frac{\epsilon^{4}}{2}H(\Pi)\leq\frac{\epsilon^{4}C}{2}.

By (
1
)

Re-arranging the inequality gives the desired upper bound on
\xi
, namely
\xi\leq O(\epsilon^{2}\sqrt{C})
.

Here we briefly explain the “radius property” of mutual information: for a joint distribution
P_{UV}
with marginals
P_{U}
and
P_{V}
, and for an arbitrary distribution
Q_{V}
, we have

\displaystyle I(U;V)

\displaystyle=D_{KL}(P_{UV}\|P_{U}\times P_{V})

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}_{u\sim P_{U}}D_{KL}(P_{V\mid U=u}\|P_{V})

\displaystyle\leq\mathbb{E}_{u\sim P_{U}}D_{KL}(P_{V|U=u}\|Q_{V}).

In our derivation, we used the property on the term
I(R;\Pi,X)
, using a reference distribution
Q_{X\Pi}=P^{0}_{X\Pi}
.
∎

Lemma 4.11
.

[Lemma 3 in
(
RBWJ 19
)
]
Let
K\sim\mathrm{Binomial}(t,\theta)
, then
\mathbb{E}[\frac{1}{K+1}]\leq\frac{1}{\theta(t+1)}
.

Lemma 4.12
.

Let
A\sim\mathrm{Binomial}([n],\theta)
,
B\sim\mathrm{Binomial}([n],\theta)
. Then we have

\mathbb{E}\left[\frac{|A\cap B|}{|A\setminus B|+1}\right]\leq\frac{\theta}{1-\theta}.

Proof.

Let
X_{i}:=[i\in A\cap B]
,
D_{i}:=[i\in A\setminus B]
. Let
X=\sum X_{i}
,
D=\sum_{i}D_{i}
. Then

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[\frac{|A\cap B|}{|A\setminus B|+1}\right]

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}\left[\frac{X}{D+1}\right]=n\cdot\mathbb{E}\left[\frac{X_{1}}{D+1}\right]=n\left(\Pr[X_{1}=1]\cdot\mathbb{E}\left[\frac{1}{Y+1}\mid X_{1}=1\right]\right)

\displaystyle\leq n\cdot\theta^{2}\cdot\frac{1}{\theta(1-\theta)n}=\frac{\theta}{(1-\theta)}.

Here, the last inequality is by the fact that
\Pr[X_{1}]=\theta^{2}
and Lemma
4.11
.
∎

4.3
Concluding the Proof

We are now ready to analyze the game from
Definition

3.4
. We state the following theorem.

Theorem 4.13
.

For every
\epsilon,m
,
n\leq O(m/\epsilon^{2})
, and
C\geq\log m
, it holds that that any
C
-bit protocol for the Hidden Sign Problem achieves a distinguishing advantage of at most
O\left(\log^{2}(m)\cdot\frac{n}{m}\cdot C\cdot\epsilon^{2}\right)
.

Proof.

Depending on whether
n\geq\frac{m}{10}
or not, we consider two cases.

Case 1:
n\geq\frac{m}{10}
.

We first argue for the case of
n\geq\Omega(m)
. In this case, define
\Gamma_{r}
for
r\in\{\pm 1\}
to be the following distribution:

•

Draw
t\sim\mathrm{Poi}(n/m)
, and sample
t
independent bits
x_{1},\dots,x_{t}\sim\mathrm{Bernuolli}(\frac{1}{2}-\epsilon r)
. Output
(t,x_{1},\dots,x_{t})
.

Our primary claim is that
\Gamma_{0}
and
\Gamma_{1}
are
(\epsilon^{\prime},\delta)
-indistinguishable for
\delta=\frac{1}{m^{10}}
and
\epsilon^{\prime}=O(\epsilon\sqrt{\frac{n}{m}}\log(1/\delta))
. To see this, first note that
\Pr[\mathrm{Poi}(n/m)\leq\frac{n}{m}\log(1/\delta)]\geq 1-\frac{1}{10\delta}
. We first condition on this event. Then, letting
t=O(\frac{n}{m}\log(1/\delta))
and by
Lemma

2.2
, we have that
\mathrm{Binomial}(t,\frac{1}{2}+\epsilon)
and
\mathrm{Binomial}(t,\frac{1}{2}-\epsilon)
are
(O(\epsilon\sqrt{t\log(1/\delta)}),\delta/2)
-indistinguishable. Combining these two pieces of observation, we conclude that
\Gamma_{0}
and
\Gamma_{1}
are
(\epsilon^{\prime},\delta)
-indistinguishable.

With this in mind, we can use
Theorem

4.9
with
\Gamma_{0},\Gamma_{1},\theta=1
to deduce that the distinguishing advantage is at most
O(C{\epsilon^{\prime}}^{2})\leq O(\log^{2}(m)\cdot\frac{n}{m}\cdot C\cdot\epsilon^{2})
, as claimed.

Case 2:
n\leq\frac{m}{10}
.

We turn to analyze the case of
n\leq\frac{m}{10}
. We first note that
\Pr[\mathrm{Poi}(n/m)>0]=1-e^{-n/m}\leq\frac{n}{m}
. With this in mind, define
\Gamma_{r}
for
r\in\{\pm 1\}
as follows:

•

Draw
t\sim\mathrm{Poi}(n/m)|_{\mathrm{Poi}(n/m)\geq 1}
and sample
t
independent bits
x_{1},\dots,x_{t}\sim\mathrm{Bernuolli}(\frac{1}{2}-\epsilon r)
. Output
(t,x_{1},\dots,x_{t})
.

In this case, we claim that
\Gamma_{0}
and
\Gamma_{1}
are
(\epsilon^{\prime},\delta)
-indistinguishable with
\delta=\frac{1}{m^{10}}
and
\epsilon^{\prime}=O(\epsilon\log(1/\delta))
. To see this, we first observe that
\Pr[\mathrm{Poi}(n/m)\leq 5\log(1/\delta)\mid\mathrm{Poi}(n/m)\geq 1]\leq\delta/10
. Let us condition on this event that
t\in[1,5\log(1/\delta)]
. Then, similarly as the above reasoning, we use the fact that
\mathrm{Binomial}(t,\frac{1}{2}-\epsilon)
and
\mathrm{Binomial}(t,\frac{1}{2}+\epsilon)
are
(O(\epsilon\sqrt{t\log(1/\delta)}),\delta/2)
-indistinguishable to deduce that
\Gamma_{0},\Gamma_{1}
are
(\epsilon^{\prime},\delta)
-indistinguishable.

Finally, we can make use of
Theorem

4.9
to deduce that the distinguishing advantage in this case is upper-bounded by
O(C{\epsilon^{\prime}}^{2}\theta)\leq O(\log^{2}(m)\frac{n}{m}C\epsilon^{2})
, as claimed.
∎

5
Conclusion

We established a multi-pass streaming lower bound for uniformity testing over a domain of size
[2m]
: any
\ell
-pass algorithm that uses
n

s
bits of memory must satisfy
\ell sn=\tilde{\Omega}(m/\epsilon^{2})
. This extends the unconditional one-pass bound of
