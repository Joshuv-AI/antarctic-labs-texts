Isavuconazole as an adjunctive antifungal in the multimodal management of pulmonary aspergilloma and chronic cavitary pulmonary aspergillosis: a single-centre case series
Authors: Meagan Deviaene, Elaine Dumoulin, Ranjani Somayaji, Christopher H. Mody, Christina S. Thornton
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Symptomatic pulmonary aspergilloma and chronic cavitary pulmonary aspergillosis (CCPA) are typically managed with surgical resection or prolonged triazole therapy, but many patients are poor surgical candidates, and many cannot tolerate voriconazole. Our centre has previously described bronchoscopic debridement combined with antifungal therapy as a minimally invasive alternative for patients with accessible intracavitary fungal balls. Isavuconazole, a newer triazole with a more favourable adverse-event and drug–drug interaction profile, has not been well characterised in this setting. We performed a retrospective single-centre review of nine adults with pulmonary aspergilloma or CCPA referred to our interventional pulmonology service who received isavuconazole between September 2022 and September 2025. Of these, six underwent attempted bronchoscopic debridement (four successful, two with limited or unsuccessful access), one required surgical resection after bronchoscopy failed, one was managed medically because the cavity was inaccessible, and one declined further procedure. Three patients had previously received voriconazole; two were switched to isavuconazole because of intolerance and one was re-treated with isavuconazole at recurrence. One patient developed isavuconazole-associated drug-induced liver injury requiring discontinuation. Three patients died during follow-up: two from causes unrelated to aspergillosis (acute coronary event during dialysis; progression of metastatic colorectal cancer) and one from refractory hemoptysis after isavuconazole was stopped for hepatotoxicity. In this small heterogeneous cohort, isavuconazole appeared well tolerated and was a feasible alternative to voriconazole when prolonged adjunctive therapy was required alongside bronchoscopic or medical management of pulmonary aspergilloma and CCPA. Larger comparative studies are needed to define its place in multimodal protocols.

Background

Aspergillus species are saprophytic moulds that produce a wide spectrum of pulmonary disease, ranging from allergic syndromes and acute invasive infection in the immunocompromised host to chronic cavitary and fibrotic forms in patients with structurally abnormal lungs [1]. Aspergilloma, classically a discrete intracavitary fungal ball within a pre-existing pulmonary cavity, sits within this spectrum and is often encountered together with, or as a manifestation of, chronic pulmonary aspergillosis (CPA). A simple aspergilloma occurs when a single isolated fungal ball was present in a stable cavity without surrounding parenchymal change or constitutional symptoms, and chronic cavitary pulmonary aspergillosis (CCPA) or complex aspergilloma when there was expanding or multiple cavitation, pericavitary infiltrate, or systemic features such as weight loss and night sweats persisting beyond three months [1,2].

Cavities arise most often from prior tuberculosis or non-tuberculous mycobacterial disease, sarcoidosis, bronchiectasis, emphysema, post-infectious necrosis or prior empyema [1,3]. Hemoptysis is the most common presentation and can be life threatening in up to 30 % of patients [1,[3], [4], [5]]. First-line management of symptomatic simple aspergilloma is surgical resection, but many patients are not operative candidates because of poor pulmonary reserve, comorbidity and/or age [6]. CCPA requires prolonged systemic azole therapy, typically for at least six months, and recurrence is common when treatment is shortened [6,7].

Our group has previously described a multimodal approach combining flexible bronchoscopic debridement of accessible intracavitary fungal balls with prolonged adjunctive triazole therapy, with high rates of clinical and radiographic improvement in non-surgical candidates [[8], [9], [10], [11]]. In those reports, voriconazole was the primary antifungal partner; however, voriconazole is limited by frequent and serious adverse effects with tolerability as a major obstacle when treatment durations of >6 months are required [12]. Isavuconazole, approved in 2015, shows activity against Aspergillus comparable to that of voriconazole with a more favourable adverse-event and drug–drug interaction profile, but published experience in the specific setting of bronchoscopically managed aspergilloma and CCPA is limited [13,14]. We therefore reviewed our consecutive experience with isavuconazole as the adjunctive azole in patients with pulmonary aspergilloma or CCPA referred to a non-surgical multimodal management pathway, focusing on tolerability, procedural feasibility and short-term clinical and radiographic outcomes.

Methods

Patient identification

We retrospectively identified all adult patients (≥18 years) seen at the Foothills Medical Centre, Calgary, Alberta, between September 2022 (when isavuconazole was first prescribed at our site for this indication) and September 2025, who carried a diagnosis of pulmonary aspergilloma or CCPA and received isavuconazole as part of management. Cases were drawn from the interventional pulmonology aspergilloma referral list; this is the same prospectively maintained database used in our earlier publications [[8], [9], [10], [11]]. Of the nine patients reported here, none have been included in our previously published series [[8], [9], [10]]. One patient (Case 1 in the present series) was also included in our recent multimodal cohort report [11]; the present series follows that patient further forward in time and reports new bronchoscopic and isavuconazole-related data, and we explicitly flag this overlap to the reader. Inclusion criteria were: (i) diagnosis of pulmonary aspergilloma based on characteristic radiographic findings defined as a mobile or non-mobile intracavitary soft-tissue mass within a pre-existing pulmonary cavity on CT imaging (with or without an air crescent sign), and either positive fungal culture, elevated galactomannan antigen, or both; (ii) treatment with isavuconazole at any point during management; and (iii) referral to the interventional pulmonary service after being deemed non-surgical candidates but requiring treatment due to symptoms, progressive radiographic disease, or impending immunosuppression. Exclusion criteria included absence of a cavitary lung lesion on CT imaging, alternative diagnoses explaining intracavitary debris, receipt of isavuconazole for indications other than aspergilloma, or insufficient clinical follow-up to assess treatment response. Prespecified outcomes included: (i) tolerability of isavuconazole, defined by treatment discontinuation or dose modification due to adverse events; (ii) procedural feasibility of bronchoscopic debridement; and (iii) clinical and/or radiographic response, assessed by symptom improvement and interval CT imaging. Mortality during follow-up was recorded and adjudicated as related or unrelated to aspergilloma or its treatment. All patients received oral isavuconazole at a maintenance dose of 200 mg once daily following a loading regimen (200mg every 8 hours for 6 doses) as per institutional practice.

Bronchoscopic procedure

For patients deemed non-surgical, multimodal management combined adjunctive azole therapy with attempted bronchoscopic debridement when pre-procedural multi-slice CT and direct flexible bronchoscopy suggested an airway communicated with the cavity. Flexible bronchoscopy was performed using an Olympus IT190, P190 or MP190 bronchoscope (Olympus, Tokyo, Japan), selected by the calibre of the feeding airway. Procedures were performed either in the bronchoscopy suite under conscious sedation or in the operating room under general anaesthesia with an endotracheal tube of at least 7.5 mm internal diameter, with the choice individualised to patient tolerance and operator judgement. Intracavitary debris was fragmented using a Zero Tip Airway Retrieval Basket (Boston Scientific, Marlborough, MA, USA) and removed through the working channel. Procedures lasted 30–120 minutes and multiple sessions were anticipated. The detailed procedural technique with video has been published [[8], [9], [10], [11]].

Data collection and outcomes

Data were extracted by detailed chart review and included demographic variables (age, sex), comorbidities (including underlying pulmonary disease), baseline clinical characteristics (including spirometry values where available), treatment course, and outcomes. Particular attention was given to adverse events attributable to antifungal therapy, the need for change or discontinuation of therapy and clinical or radiographic response to treatment. Follow-up was typically conducted in the interventional pulmonary clinic every 3–6 months. Data were analyzed descriptively due to the small sample size.

Case series

Case 1

A 62-year-old man receiving methotrexate and prednisone for rheumatoid arthritis presented with productive cough and small-volume hemoptysis. CT chest demonstrated a thick-walled, pericavitary-infiltrate left upper lobe cavity containing a mobile soft-tissue mass; the imaging plus chronic constitutional symptoms over six months met criteria for chronic cavitary pulmonary aspergillosis with an intracavitary aspergilloma. BAL grew Aspergillus fumigatus and BAL galactomannan was >3.5. Voriconazole was started but discontinued after two months because of grade 3 transaminitis and visual hallucinations, and he was transitioned to isavuconazole. Five months later he underwent flexible bronchoscopic debridement (Olympus MP190, basket retrieval) of the cavity. He developed a post-procedural bacterial pneumonia treated with two weeks of oral antibiotics. Persistent radiographic debris prompted a second debridement two months later, which was technically successful. Case 2

A 46-year-old man with no prior pulmonary disease, no immunosuppression and no constitutional symptoms beyond cough and halitosis was found on CT to have a single thin-walled right upper lobe cavity with a mobile intracavitary mass and an air-crescent sign. BAL grew Aspergillus nidulans with BAL galactomannan >3.5. Isavuconazole was started at standard dose. After approximately four months of therapy, he underwent uncomplicated bronchoscopic debridement and the cavity was emptied in a single session. A further five months of post-procedural isavuconazole was completed with no clinical or radiographic recurrence at last follow-up.

Case 3

A 51-year-old woman with severe COPD and centrilobular emphysema (ppFEV1 53 %) was referred for hemoptysis and productive cough. CT chest showed a 30 mm cavity in the right upper lobe with a 19 mm intracavitary nodule and surrounding parenchymal scarring, consistent with a complex aspergilloma. BAL grew Aspergillus flavus with BAL galactomannan 1.79. Voriconazole was started but discontinued after she developed cholestatic drug-induced liver injury (alkaline phosphatase >2× upper limit of normal with abnormal aspartate and alanine transaminases), and she was switched to isavuconazole. Three flexible bronchoscopic attempts at the cavity were unsuccessful because no patent airway communication could be identified; she therefore proceeded to lobectomy. She completed 477 days of total antifungal therapy and remained clinically and radiographically stable at last follow-up.

Case 4

A 63-year-old man with moderate COPD and prior necrotising bacterial pneumonia with residual right-sided cavitation, presented with persistent cough, a 9 kg unintentional weight loss and night sweats. CT chest demonstrated new fluid and soft-tissue debris within a previously empty residual cavity. Although BAL fungal culture was negative, BAL galactomannan was 4.49 and with new fluid/debris in one of the known cavities on imaging. He had a delay in starting therapy because of loss to follow-up; once re-engaged, he received approximately six months of isavuconazole pre-procedurally before successful bronchoscopic debridement, with a further ICU admission for an unrelated bacterial pneumonia delaying the procedure. He completed 424 days of isavuconazole without recurrence.

Case 5

A 41-year-old woman with idiopathic bronchiectasis presented with cough and small-volume hemoptysis. CT demonstrated a small thin-walled cavity containing a soft-tissue mass within an area of bronchiectatic lung. BAL fungal culture was negative, but BAL galactomannan was >3.5. Given her underlying bronchiectasis, allergic bronchopulmonary aspergillosis (ABPA) was specifically considered in the differential diagnosis; she had no history of asthma or cystic fibrosis, did not meet ISHAM criteria for ABPA, and imaging demonstrated a discrete intracavitary fungal ball rather than the central bronchiectasis with mucoid impaction characteristic of ABPA, supporting a diagnosis of aspergilloma. Pre-bronchoscopy assessment, and direct visualisation at flexible bronchoscopy, showed that no airway communicated with the cavity, and she was therefore managed medically. As of 1 January 2026, she had completed more than 300 days of isavuconazole with both symptomatic and radiographic improvement and remained on treatment.

Case 6

A 63-year-old woman on chronic haemodialysis for diabetic nephropathy, with a remotely diagnosed Aspergillus fumigatus aspergilloma identified more than ten years earlier, was re-referred during pre-renal-transplant assessment. Repeat BAL was culture-negative but BAL galactomannan was >3.5. She delayed starting isavuconazole for several weeks because of concern regarding adverse effects; debridement at her first bronchoscopy was therefore deferred. At a subsequent procedure, approximately 50 % of intracavitary debris was removed. She experienced acute worsening of infectious signs and symptoms post both attempted debridements, treated with 1-week courses of antibiotics. She had further interruptions in therapy due to accessibility of the medication, however had been on and off treatment for 504 days and tolerating when she died of unrelated reasons.

Case 7

An 80-year-old female with history of pulmonary mycobacterium avium complex infection as well as obstructive lung disease presented with subacute fevers, cough and dyspnea and CT demonstrated a new cavitary masslike opacity in the left upper lobe. Diagnostic BAL grew Aspergillus fumigatus with a galactomannan of >3.5. She was treated with 395 days of antifungal treatment with radiographic improvement. She remained on therapy until her death, which was not due to aspergilloma.

Case 8

A 68-year-old man with a prior right-sided empyema after oesophageal perforation and residual thick-walled cavitation was followed radiographically until he developed worsening cough, hemoptysis and night sweats, with progressive enlargement of the cavity and new internal debris. BAL grew fungal elements with a modestly positive BAL galactomannan (index 0.7). He completed approximately twelve months of voriconazole with clinical and radiographic improvement but relapsed sixteen months after stopping treatment with recurrent symptoms and radiographic progression – a clinical course in keeping with complex aspergilloma rather than a self-limited simple fungal ball. He was restarted on isavuconazole. He underwent two further bronchoscopic debridements (only 75 % debulking at the first), with the second technically successful; tissue cultures grew Aspergillus fumigatus. As of 1 January 2026, he remained on isavuconazole (409 days).

Case 9

A 66-year-old man with severe COPD (ppFEV1 28 %) on long-term inhaled corticosteroid plus long-acting bronchodilators and frequent short systemic-steroid courses for exacerbations was being assessed for lung transplantation when an intracavitary mass was incidentally identified during admission for influenza A and COVID-19 co-infection. Sputum from that admission grew Aspergillus fumigatus. Isavuconazole was started; attempted bronchoscopic debridement six months later was unsuccessful because of inaccessible cavity anatomy. After 217 days of isavuconazole he developed grade 4 hepatocellular injury (ALT >5× the upper limit of normal) meeting AASLD criteria for drug-induced liver injury, and isavuconazole was stopped while transplant candidacy was reviewed. While off antifungal therapy he developed refractory hemoptysis with respiratory failure, was admitted to ICU and died. This death was adjudicated as directly related to chronic cavitary Aspergillus disease.

Discussion

We describe nine adults with pulmonary aspergilloma or CCPA managed through a non-surgical multimodal pathway at a single Canadian referral centre in whom isavuconazole was used as the adjunctive azole (Table 1). Six of the nine patients had attempted bronchoscopic debridement and four had a technically successful procedure; one patient required surgical resection after failed bronchoscopic access, one was managed medically because the cavity was inaccessible, and one declined further procedure. The mean total duration of isavuconazole therapy was 329 days (SD = 100.; range 208–462). The medication was generally well tolerated despite this prolonged use, with a single case of treatment-limiting hepatotoxicity (Case 9). Importantly, two patients previously intolerant of voriconazole (Cases 1 and 3) were successfully switched to isavuconazole without recurrence of those toxicities, and one further patient (Case 8) was successfully re-treated with isavuconazole after voriconazole relapse. Our experience expands on existing literature. Although isavuconazole is widely used for invasive aspergillosis in many jurisdictions and is included in updated practice guidance for chronic forms [6,13,14], dedicated cohort-level data describing its tolerability in patients with aspergilloma and CCPA in whom adjunctive bronchoscopic debridement is part of the strategy remain sparse. Our findings are concordant with the SECURE trial and recent meta-analyses, which have shown that isavuconazole is non-inferior to voriconazole for invasive mould disease with fewer adverse events and lower discontinuation rates [13,14], and with the smaller Japanese cohort of CPA treated with isavuconazole [15]. We included patients across the chronic Aspergillus spectrum, from a single isolated fungal ball to expanding cavitary disease with constitutional symptoms and report each patient's diagnostic category transparently, in line with the recognition that the boundary between simple aspergilloma, complex aspergilloma and CCPA is often blurred in real-world practice. Moreover, one patient (Case 5) achieved sustained symptomatic and radiographic improvement on antifungal therapy alone when bronchoscopic access was not feasible, consistent with case reports of conservative management with newer triazoles when intervention is impossible [16] and with broader cohort data suggesting that prolonged triazole therapy can stabilise or reduce lesion size in selected patients with chronic Aspergillus cavitary disease, albeit with substantial recurrence and toxicity risk [7].

Management of pulmonary aspergilloma and CCPA in non-surgical candidates remains difficult. Surgical resection still offers definitive therapy when feasible [17,18], but operative risk is high in patients with limited pulmonary reserve, age or comorbidity. A multidisciplinary approach incorporating respirology, interventional pulmonology, infectious diseases and thoracic surgery remain essential. Antifungal susceptibility testing for Aspergillus is currently complicated by the absence of universally accepted clinical breakpoints for isavuconazole [19], and future work should prioritise establishing reliable thresholds to guide therapy and defining whether isavuconazole should replace voriconazole as the default azole partner for multimodal aspergilloma protocols. Trials of shorter, individualised treatment courses (pre-procedure only, post-procedure only) and biomarkers that predict bronchoscopic benefit are warranted.

This case series has some limitations. The small sample size lacked a contemporaneous control group, and outcomes were based on clinical and radiographic assessment rather than standardized endpoints. Serum Aspergillus-specific IgG serology was not done at our centre, and diagnosis relied on radiographic findings together with BAL fungal culture and/or BAL galactomannan. Antifungal susceptibility testing, including isavuconazole and voriconazole minimum inhibitory concentrations, was not performed, so in-vitro activity could not be correlated with outcome. Post-treatment follow-up after isavuconazole discontinuation was of variable and, in some patients, limited duration, which constrains assessment of the durability of response and of long-term relapse. Microbiological confirmation was not available for all patients and susceptibility testing lacks defined breakpoints for some Aspergillus–drug combinations, thus limiting the interpretation of efficacy. As a single-centre experience, generalizability is restricted. Larger comparative studies or prospective registries are needed to clarify the optimal antifungal partner for bronchoscopic resection, although head-to-head randomized trials may face ethical and feasibility challenges given the rarity and heterogeneity of aspergilloma. As described, therapy for aspergilloma in the absence of concomitant chronic forms is only indicated in a select few symptomatic, immunocompromised patients or those with unstable growing lesions. This series only included patients with these considerations who were not candidates for first line management in the form of surgical resection, which limits the ability to generalize these findings to all comers with aspergilloma.

Conclusion

In this small case series, isavuconazole appeared to be generally well tolerated when used as part of a multimodal management strategy for pulmonary aspergilloma. Clinical and radiographic improvement was observed in selected patients treated with isavuconazole, with or without bronchoscopic intervention. These findings are descriptive and hypothesis-generating, and larger studies are needed to better define the role of isavuconazole within combined medical and bronchoscopic management pathways. Further studies, including multicentre collaborations, are warranted to define the role of isavuconazole in this setting and to refine treatment protocols that optimize duration, sequencing, and patient selection.
