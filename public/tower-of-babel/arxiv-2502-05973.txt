On local smoothing estimates for wave equations
Shengwen Gan, Danqing He, Xiaochun Li, Shukun Wu

Abstract
. We prove sharp local smoothing estimates for wave equations on compact Riemannian manifolds in n + 1 n+1 dimensions for odd n n and obtain improved estimates in even dimensions. This is achieved by deriving local smoothing estimates for certain Fourier integral operators. We also obtain improved local smoothing estimates for wave equations in Euclidean spaces.



1.
Introduction

1.1.
Overview

The study of local smoothing originated in the late 1980s with independent work of Sjölin
[
23
]
and Vega
[
26
]
, who observed that solutions to the Schrödinger equation gain fractional regularity when localized in space and averaged in time.
Sogge
[
24
]
subsequently identified analogous phenomena for the wave equation, formulated the local smoothing problem within the framework of Fourier integral operators, and emphasized its geometric character.
In the 1990s, Wolff
[
28
]
revealed that sharp local smoothing estimates are closely linked to Kakeya-type phenomena, showing that geometric obstructions arise from configurations of thin tubes.
This connection placed local smoothing at the intersection of dispersive PDE, harmonic analysis, and geometric measure theory.

This paper establishes local smoothing estimates for wave equations on both Euclidean spaces and general compact Riemannian manifolds.
Let
(M,g)
denote either
\mathbb{R}^{n}
or a smooth, compact
n
-dimensional Riemannian manifold equipped with the corresponding Laplace–Beltrami operator
\Delta_{g}
. Consider
u
as the solution to the Cauchy problem:

(1.1)

\begin{cases}(\partial^{2}_{t}-\Delta_{g})u(x,t)=0,\hskip 14.22636pt(x,t)\in M\times\mathbb{R},\\[2.15277pt]
u(x,0)=f_{0}(x),\hskip 28.45274pt\partial_{t}u(x,0)=f_{1}(x).\end{cases}

When the underlying manifold is
M=\mathbb{R}^{n}
with the flat metric, Peral
[
19
]
showed that for each fixed time
t
,

\|u(\cdot,t)\|_{L^{p}(M)}\lesssim_{M,g}\|f_{0}\|_{L^{p}_{s_{p}}(M)}+\|f_{1}\|_{L^{p}_{s_{p}-1}(M)},

for
p\in(1,\infty)
, where
s_{p}=(n-1)\big|\tfrac{1}{2}-\tfrac{1}{p}\big|
. The corresponding estimate for a general Riemannian manifold
(M,g)
was later proved by Seeger-Sogge-Stein
[
22
]
. In subsequent work, Sogge
[
24
]
observed that averaging the solution over time intervals
t\sim 1
yields an
L^{p}
regularity gain for
2<p<\infty
. In the same paper, he formulated the following
local smoothing
conjecture on
\mathbb{R}^{n}
:

Conjecture 1.1
.

Let
M=\mathbb{R}^{n}
equipped with the flat metric, let
s_{p}=\frac{(n-1)}{2}-\frac{(n-1)}{p}
, and let
u
solve the Cauchy problem (
1.1
).
Then for all
p\geq 2+\frac{2}{n-1}
and
\sigma<\frac{1}{p}
,

(1.2)

\|u\|_{L^{p}(\mathbb{R}^{n}\times[1,2])}\leq C_{p,\sigma}\big(\|f_{0}\|_{L^{p}_{s_{p}-\sigma}(\mathbb{R}^{n})}+\|f_{1}\|_{L^{p}_{s_{p}-1-\sigma}(\mathbb{R}^{n})}\big).

The local smoothing phenomenon also occurs on a general compact manifold
(M,g)
.
However, the range of admissible exponents is quite different from the flat case in Conjecture
1.1
.
Miniccozi and Sogge
[
18
]
constructed examples to show that (
1.2
) fails for all
\sigma<\frac{1}{p}
when
p<p_{n}^{+}
, where

p_{n}^{+}:=\begin{cases}2+\frac{8}{3n-3},\hskip 8.5359pt\text{if $n$ is odd},\\[2.15277pt]
2+\frac{8}{3n-2},\hskip 8.5359pt\text{if $n$ is even}.\end{cases}

This observation naturally leads to the following conjecture:

Conjecture 1.2
.

For
n\geq 2
, let
(M,g)
be an
n
-dimensional compact Riemannian manifold, and let
u
solve the Cauchy problem (
1.1
).
Then for all
p\geq p_{n}^{+}
and
\sigma<\frac{1}{p}
,

(1.3)

\|u\|_{L^{p}(M\times[1,2])}\leq C_{p,\sigma}\big(\|f_{0}\|_{L^{p}_{s_{p}-\sigma}(M)}+\|f_{1}\|_{L^{p}_{s_{p}-1-\sigma}(M)}\big).

Our main result is the following theorem:

Theorem 1.3
.

Conjectures
1.1
and
1.2
are true when
n\geq 3
and
p\geq p(n)
, where

(1.4)

p(n):=\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}p_{n}^{+}&\textrm{$n$ is odd}\\[4.30554pt]
2+\frac{8}{3n-2-\omega(n)}&\textrm{$n$ is even.}\end{array}\right.

Here,
\omega(n)=\frac{4(7n-2)}{3n^{2}+5n+14}=O(n^{-1})
.

Theorem
1.3
confirms Conjecture
1.2
for all odd dimensions.
Note that
p(n)=p_{n}^{+}+O(n^{-3})
for all
n
even.
The even-dimensional case appears to require new ideas and techniques.

Remark 1.4
.

One may form a stronger conjecture by including the endpoint
\sigma=\frac{1}{p}
.
For example, it was proved by Heo, Nazarov, and Seeger
[
15
]
that Conjecture
1.1
is true when
p\geq 2+\frac{4}{n-3}
and
\sigma=\frac{1}{p}
for
n\geq 4
.
It is open whether there are local smoothing estimates at the endpoint
\sigma=\frac{1}{p}
for dimensions
n=2,3
.

To study Conjecture
1.1
, Wolff
[
28
]
initiated a program based on decoupling inequalities.
The investigation of decoupling inequalities culminated in Bourgain-Demeter’s resolution on the
\ell^{2}
-decoupling theorem
[
4
]
.
As a direct corollary, they showed that Conjecture
1.1
is true when
p>2+\frac{4}{n-1}
, where
2+\frac{4}{n-1}
is the decoupling exponent for the light cone.
The approach was adapted to general compact Riemannian manifolds by Beltran-Hickman-Sogge
[
1
]
, where they proved the same range of
p
for Conjecture
1.2
.
We refer to
[
2
]
for more historical information.

Conjecture
1.1
was verified when
n=2
by Guth-Wang-Zhang
[
14
]
, however, via a different approach.
The authors there proved a sharp reverse square function estimate, which implies Conjecture
1.1
.
This approach was modified by Gao-Liu-Miao-Xi
[
9
]
to prove Conjecture
1.2
in the same dimensions.

In this paper, we follow Wolff’s footsteps and use refined decoupling inequalities to study the local smoothing problem.
Prior to this work, the decoupling range
p>2+\frac{4}{n-1}
is the best-known result for both Conjecture
1.1
and
1.2
when
n\geq 3
.
Further discussion of our methods will be provided after an introduction to the Fourier integral operator, a standard tool for the local smoothing problem on manifolds.

1.2.
Fourier integral operator (FIO)

Through a standard parametrix construction of the half wave operator
e^{it\sqrt{-\Delta_{g}}}
(see
[
2
]
for details), the study of Conjecture
1.2
boils down to the analysis of certain Fourier integral operators:

(1.5)

\mathcal{F}f(x,t):=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}e^{i\phi(x,t;\xi)}a(x,t;\xi)\widehat{f}(\xi)\mathrm{d}\xi.

Here, the symbol
a\in S^{0}(\mathbb{R}^{n+1}\times\mathbb{R}^{n})
is supported in
\{(x,t;\xi):|x|^{2}+t^{2}\leq 1\}
.
The phase function
\phi(x,t;\xi)
is homogeneous of degree
1
in
\xi
and smooth away from
\xi=0
. Also,
\phi
satisfies

\bullet

(H1) Non-degeneracy condition.

rank
\partial^{2}_{x\xi}\phi(x,t;\xi)=n
for all
(x,t;\xi)\in{\rm supp}\ a
.

\bullet

(H2) Positive definiteness condition.

Consider the Gauss map
G:{\rm supp}\ a\rightarrow S^{n}
by
G(z;\xi):=\frac{G_{0}(z;\xi)}{|G_{0}(z;\xi)|}
, where

G_{0}(z;\xi):=\bigwedge_{j=1}^{n}\partial_{\xi_{j}}\partial_{z}\phi(z;\xi).

Then for all
(z,\xi_{0})\in{\rm supp}\ a
,

\partial^{2}_{\xi\xi}\langle\partial_{z}\phi(z,\xi),G(z,\xi_{0})\rangle|_{\xi=\xi_{0}}

has rank
n-1
with
n-1
positive eigenvalues.

A prototypical example for the phase function is given by
\phi(x,t;\xi)=\langle x,\xi\rangle+t|\xi|
.
The Fourier integral operator defined using this phase function corresponds to the classical half-wave propagator
e^{it\sqrt{-\Delta}}f
.
One may also keep in mind another example
\phi(x,t;\xi)=\langle x,\xi\rangle+t|\xi^{\prime}|^{2}/\xi_{n}
. Here
\xi^{\prime}=(\xi_{1},\dots,\xi_{n-1})
.

As shown in
[
2
]
, to prove Conjecture
1.2
, it suffices to prove the following conjecture for FIOs satisfying the two conditions
(H1)
and
(H2)
.

Conjecture 1.5
.

Let
\mathcal{F}
be defined in (
1.5
).
Suppose
\mathcal{F}
satisfies conditions
(H1)
and
(H2)
.
Then for
p\geq p_{n}^{+}
and
\sigma>(n-1)(\frac{1}{2}-\frac{1}{p})-\frac{1}{p}
,

(1.6)

\|\mathcal{F}f\|_{L^{p}(\mathbb{R}^{n}\times[1,2])}\lesssim\|f\|_{L^{p}_{\sigma}(\mathbb{R}^{n})}.

Remark 1.6
.

Via a similar argument, to prove Conjecture
1.1
, it suffices to prove (
1.6
) with
\mathcal{F}=e^{it\sqrt{-\Delta}}
for the given range of
p
.

Similarly, to establish Theorem
1.3
, we only need to prove

Theorem 1.7
.

Conjecture
1.5
is true when
p\geq p(n)
, where
p(n)
is given in (
1.4
).

1.3.
Main ideas

To explain our ideas, we take
\mathcal{F}=e^{it\sqrt{-\Delta}}
in Conjecture
1.5
.
The same ideas apply to general FIOs.

Since the kernel of the operator
e^{it\sqrt{-\Delta}}
is essentially the Fourier inversion of the surface measure of the cone
\{(\xi,|\xi|):\xi\in\mathbb{R}^{n}\}
, it is natural to connect the local smoothing problem with Fourier restriction theory.
This connection was shown to be successful by Rogers
[
20
]
in the study of the Schrödinger equations (with the half-wave operator
e^{it\sqrt{-\Delta}}
being replaced by the Schrödinger operator
e^{it\Delta}
).
In fact, Rogers showed that the local smoothing conjecture for Schrödinger equations is equivalent to the Fourier restriction conjecture for paraboloid.
Moreover, since the tools developed for the parabolic restriction conjecture are versatile and apply equally well to hypersurfaces with a positive definite second fundamental form, it is reasonable to believe that the same ideas will extend to the local smoothing problem of the same type.
Successful examples can be found in
[
7
]
, where fractional Schrödinger equations with the operators
e^{it(-\Delta)^{\alpha/2}},\,\alpha>1,
are investigated.

However, there is a significant difference when one attempts to relate the local smoothing problem for wave equations to the conic restriction problem.
Indeed, the local smoothing problem is generally believed to be strictly harder than the conic and parabolic restriction problem (see
[
25
]
).
For example, the exponent
2+\frac{4}{n-1}
, also known as the Stein-Tomas exponent, was established for the restriction problem back in the 1970s.
In contrast, for the local smoothing problem, this exponent was only obtained much later by Bourgain and Demeter in 2014.

One key difference between paraboloid and light cone is that the former possesses a better symmetry: the natural rescaling for paraboloid, the parabolic rescaling (see
[
11
]
), is an isotropic rescaling when restricting to the initial data, whereas the natural rescaling for the light cone, the Lorentz rescaling (see Section
5.1
), is non-isotropic.
As a consequence, although induction is well-established in the study of the parabolic restriction problem and hence the related local smoothing problem for Schrödinger equations, there seems to be no satisfactory induction on scales scheme for wave equations.

In this paper, we propose a possible candidate for induction on scales for the wave equations.
To better explain our idea, we will compare the half-wave operator
e^{it\sqrt{-\Delta}}
with the Schrödinger operator
e^{it\Delta}
.
After standard reductions, the desired local smoothing estimates for the two operators and the parabolic restriction problem are reduced to the following local forms:

Local smoothing estimate for wave equations
:
For
{\rm supp}\widehat{f}\subset B^{n}_{1}\setminus B^{n}_{1/2}
,

(1.7)

\|e^{it\sqrt{-\Delta}}f\|_{L^{p}(B^{n+1}_{R})}\lessapprox R^{(n-1)(\frac{1}{2}-\frac{1}{p})}\|f\|_{p}.

Local smoothing estimate for Schrödinger equations
:
For
{\rm supp}\widehat{f}\subset B^{n}_{1}
,

(1.8)

\|e^{it\Delta}f\|_{L^{p}(B^{n+1}_{R})}\lessapprox R^{n(\frac{1}{2}-\frac{1}{p})}\|f\|_{p}.

Fourier restriction estimate for paraboloid
:
For
{\rm supp}\widehat{f}\subset B^{n}_{1}
,

(1.9)

\|e^{it\Delta}f\|_{L^{p}(B^{n+1}_{R})}\lessapprox\|\widehat{f}\|_{p}.

To study the restriction estimate (
1.9
) via induction on scales, Guth
[
11
]
introduced the mixed norm

(1.10)

\|\widehat{f}\|_{2}^{\frac{2}{p}}\sup_{\theta}\|\widehat{f}_{\theta}\|_{L^{2}_{avg}(\theta)}^{1-\frac{2}{p}}

in place of the
L^{p}
-norm
\|\widehat{f}\|_{L^{p}(B^{n}_{1})}
, where
\|\widehat{f}_{\theta}\|_{L^{2}_{avg}(\theta)}^{2}=|\theta|^{-1}\|\widehat{f}_{\theta}\|_{L^{2}(\theta)}^{2}
, and
\theta
ranges over
R^{-1/2}
-balls in
B^{n}_{1}
.
The mixed norm (
1.10
) can be viewed as the
L^{p}
-norm by interpolation, in which the quantity
\sup_{\theta}\|\widehat{f}_{\theta}\|_{L^{2}_{avg}(\theta)}
plays the roll of
\|\widehat{f}\|_{L^{\infty}}
.
It is crucial that
\sup_{\theta}\|\widehat{f}_{\theta}\|_{L^{2}_{avg}(\theta)}
is an
L^{2}
-average.
If this is simply replaced by
\|\widehat{f}\|_{L^{\infty}}
, inductions will generally fail.

Given the connection between the parabolic restriction conjecture and the local smoothing conjecture for Schrödinger equations, it is thus natural to consider a suitable mixed norm similar to (
1.10
) to investigate (
1.8
).
One subtlety is that the right-hand side of (
1.8
) lies in the physical space, while the right-hand side of (
1.9
) lies in the frequency space.
This leads to a different mixed norm, which may be chosen as follows:

R^{n(\frac{1}{2}-\frac{1}{p})}\|f\|_{2}^{\frac{2}{p}}\sup_{B}\|f\|_{L^{2}_{avg}(B)}^{1-\frac{2}{p}},

where
B
ranges over
R^{1/2}
-balls in
B^{n}_{R}
.
Using this mixed norm, it is possible that the recent framework developed by Wang and the second author in
[
27
]
for the restriction conjecture can lead to parallel results for local smoothing estimate for Schrödinger equations (
1.8
).

Based on the above discussion, it seems natural to study the local smoothing estimate for wave equations (
1.7
) by considering a similar mixed norm:

(1.11)

R^{(n-1)(\frac{1}{2}-\frac{1}{p})}\|f\|_{2}^{\frac{2}{p}}\sup_{B}\|f\|_{L^{2}_{avg}(B)}^{1-\frac{2}{p}},

where
B
ranges over
R^{1/2}
-balls in
B^{n}_{R}
.
However, this naive choice of the mixed norm does not work, since the form in (
1.11
) is not invariant under Lorentz rescaling, a non-isotropic one.

Inspired by the work
[
14
]
, we introduce a mixed norm

(1.12)

R^{(n-1)(\frac{1}{2}-\frac{1}{p})}\|f\|_{2}^{\frac{2}{p}}\mathcal{W}(f,B_{R}^{n+1})^{1-\frac{2}{p}},

where the
wave packet density

\mathcal{W}(f,B_{R}^{n+1})
is a rescaling-invariant form of an
L^{2}
average that serves as an analogue of
\|f\|_{\infty}
.
The precise definition of
\mathcal{W}(f,B_{R}^{n+1})
is given in Definition
4.1
.
Roughly speaking, it is the supreme of all the
L^{2}
-average over conic wave packets and their rescaled counterparts under Lorentz rescaling.

The introduction of the wave packet density is one of the main ideas of this work.
With the mixed norm (
1.12
) in hand, induction on scales becomes possible, and we would like to adapt the framework developed in
[
27
]
to study (
1.7
).
After the standard wave packet decomposition and several steps of dyadic pigeonholing, our goal is to establish the following:
Suppose
f=\sum_{T\in\mathbb{T}}f_{T}
is a wave packet decomposition of
f
, where
\mathbb{T}
is a family of
1\times R^{1/2}\times\cdots\times R^{1/2}\times R
-planks.
Given a union of unit balls
X\subset B^{n+1}_{R}
such that each unit ball
B\subset X
intersects with
\lesssim\mu
planks in
\mathbb{T}
, prove that when
p=p(n)
,

(1.13)

\|e^{it\sqrt{-\Delta}}f\|_{L^{p}(X)}\lessapprox R^{(n-1)(\frac{1}{2}-\frac{1}{p})}\|f\|_{2}^{\frac{2}{p}}\mathcal{W}(f,B_{R}^{n+1})^{1-\frac{2}{p}}.

As in
[
27
]
, we aim to

(1)

Prove a refined decoupling inequality for the cone to bound
\|e^{it\sqrt{-\Delta}}f\|_{L^{q_{n}}(X)}
with
q_{n}=\frac{2(n+1)}{n-1}
being the decoupling exponent.

(2)

Explore orthogonality in the
L^{2}
space.
That is, estimate
\|e^{it\sqrt{-\Delta}}f\|_{L^{2}(X)}
.

It is fairly standard to establish item (1), and we prove the refined decoupling inequality for general FIOs in the appendix.
However, it is not clear what the optimal
L^{2}
-orthogonality for
\|e^{it\sqrt{-\Delta}}f\|_{L^{2}(X)}
is, as
X
is a union of unit balls.
One simple approach is to enlarge
X
to its
R^{1/2}
-neighborhood and then use
L^{2}
orthogonality on the
R^{1/2}
-balls.
As we will see below, this already allows us to obtain some useful
L^{2}
estimates.

We introduce a parameter
l
that measures how many
R^{1/2}
-balls interact with a plank
T
.
Specifically, for each plank
T\in\mathbb{T}
, define a shading
Y(T)=X\cap T
, and assume that
Y(T)
intersects with
\sim l
many
R^{1/2}
-balls in
\mathbb{R}^{n+1}
.
Thus, by using
L^{2}
-orthogonality inside each
R^{1/2}
-ball in
N_{R^{1/2}}(X)
, we have the following
L^{2}
-estimate

(1.14)

\|e^{it\sqrt{-\Delta}}f\|_{L^{2}(X)}^{2}\lesssim\|e^{it\sqrt{-\Delta}}f\|_{L^{2}(N_{R^{1/2}}(X))}^{2}\lesssim(lR^{1/2})\|f\|_{2}^{2}.

Next, at the decoupling endpoint
p=\frac{2(n+1)}{n-1}
, we use the refined decoupling inequality for the cone to have

\|e^{it\sqrt{-\Delta}}f\|_{L^{p}(X)}^{p}\lessapprox\mu^{\frac{2}{n-1}}\sum_{T\in\mathbb{T}}\|e^{it\sqrt{-\Delta}}f_{T}\|_{p}^{p},

After normalization, we assume that
|e^{it\sqrt{-\Delta}}f_{T}|
is essentially the characteristic function of the
1\times R^{1/2}\times\cdots\times R^{1/2}\times R
-plank
T
.
Thus, when
p=\frac{2(n+1)}{n-1}
,

(1.15)

\|e^{it\sqrt{-\Delta}}f\|_{L^{p}(X)}^{p}\lessapprox\mu^{\frac{2}{n-1}}(R^{\frac{n+1}{2}}\#\mathbb{T}).

It remains to find an upper bound for
\mu^{\frac{2}{n-1}}\#\mathbb{T}
, which is an incidence problem between the unit balls in
X
and the
1\times R^{1/2}\times\cdots\times R^{1/2}\times R
-planks in
\mathbb{T}
.

Now we are going to use the power of induction on scales.
Broadly speaking, induction on scales allows us to impose a two-ends condition (which is a non-concentration condition) on the shading
Y(T)
.
Also, the wave packet density
\mathcal{W}(f,B_{R}^{n})
imposes a non-concentration condition on the collection of planks
\mathbb{T}
(see Remark
4.8
), which, in particular, implies
\mathcal{W}(f,B_{R}^{n+1})^{2}\gtrsim R^{-\frac{n+1}{4}}(\#\mathbb{T})^{1/2}
.
Therefore, by using the hairbrush structure (see Figure
5.5
) and a “2-broad” assumption on each unit ball in
X
, we have

(1.16)

\mu\lesssim l^{-1/2}(\#\mathbb{T})^{1/2}\lesssim l^{-1/2}R^{\frac{n+1}{4}}\mathcal{W}(f,B_{R}^{n+1})^{2}.

We remark that the hairbrush structure for the cone has the following special property: all the light rays intersecting a fixed line are essentially disjoint.

Finally, we plug the incidence bound back to (
1.15
) to get when
p=\frac{2(n+1)}{n-1}
,

(1.17)

\|e^{it\sqrt{-\Delta}}f\|_{L^{p}(X)}^{p}\lessapprox R^{2}(R^{-\frac{n-3}{2(n-1)}}l^{-\frac{1}{n-1}})\|f\|_{2}^{2}\,\mathcal{W}(f,B_{R}^{n+1})^{p-2}.

Interpolate this with (
1.14
) and use the fact that
l\lesssim R^{1/2}
to conclude (
1.7
) for
p=2+\frac{8}{3n-4}
, which already is a strong bound.

Remark 1.8
.

The wave packet density
\mathcal{W}(f,B_{R}^{n})
is quite versatile.
Modifications to its definition will allow us to impose stronger non-concentration conditions on the set of planks
\mathbb{T}
.
We refer the readers to Remark
4.9
for further discussions.
A similar object is also considered recently in
[
10
]
.

1.4.
Sharp exponents in odd dimensions

To further extend the admissible range of
p
, we refine the aforementioned incidence and
L^{2}
estimates.
Together with some
k
-broad estimates (which is also used in a recent work
[
5
]
), we achieve a complete solution to the problem in odd dimensions.
Let us briefly outline the method here.
We refer to Section
2
for a sketch of the proof.

By the standard broad-narrow argument, the inequality (
1.13
) at
p=p(n)
can be reduced to a
k_{n}
-broad norm, where
k_{n}
is given by

(1.18)

k_{n}=\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}\tfrac{n+5}{2};&\textrm{$n\geq 3$ is odd},\\[4.30554pt]
\frac{n+4}{2};&\textrm{$n\geq 2$ is even}.\end{array}\right.

By pigeonholing, we may assume that
X
can be covered by a collection of
R^{1/2}
-balls
Q
, each of which
satisfies

(1.19)

\big|X\cap Q\big|\sim\sigma,\text{ and }\,\#\mathbb{T}(Q)\sim m\,,

where
\mathbb{T}(Q):=\{T\in\mathbb{T}:X\cap Q\cap T\neq\emptyset\}
, and
m,\sigma
are some dyadic numbers.

To obtain a refined incidence estimate, on the one hand, using a hairbrush argument, we have (note that
m\geq\mu
)

(1.20)

|X|\gtrsim lm\lambda.

On the other hand, a double-counting argument yields

(1.21)

\mu|X|\sim\lambda(\#\mathbb{T}).

Combining(
1.20
) and (
1.21
), we obtain at a refinement of (
1.16
) given by

(1.22)

\mu\lesssim l^{-1}m^{-1}(\#\mathbb{T}).

Clearly, the larger
m
is, the stronger (
1.22
) becomes, while the refinement of the
L^{2}
estimates depends inversely on
m
: the smaller
m
is, the better
L^{2}
estimate we can obtain.

Note that inside each
R^{1/2}
ball
Q
,
T\cap Q
is a slab of dimensions
1\times R^{1/2}\times\cdots\times R^{1/2}
for each
T\in\mathbb{T}(Q)
.
Let
\mathbb{T}(B)=\{T\in\mathbb{T}(Q):B\cap T\not=\varnothing\}
for each unit ball
B\subset X\cap Q
.
From the broad-narrow reduction, we know that the set of planks
\mathbb{T}(B)
is
k_{n}
-broad; that is, the directions of
\mathbb{T}
are
k_{n}
-broad.
One might expect this to imply that the set of slabs
\{T\cap Q:T\in\mathbb{T}(B)\}
is also
k_{n}
-broad, meaning that the normal directions of
\mathbb{T}(B)
are
k_{n}
-broad.
If this expectation holds, a favorable lower bound for

m
can be obtained using a
k_{n}
-linear estimate for the slabs
\{T\cap Q:T\in\mathbb{T}(Q)\}
.
This is indeed the case when the FIO
\mathcal{F}
is the half-wave operator
{e^{it\sqrt{-\Delta}}}
; however, it fails for general FIOs.
Our corresponding treatment is dimension-sensitive, and we outline the corresponding arguments separately.
The cases
n=3
and
n=5
turn out to be the most interesting.

1.4.1.
When

1.5.
A broad & two-ends algorithm

We conclude the introduction with a comment on a technical difficulty encountered during the proof.
Let
e^{it\sqrt{-\Delta}}f=\sum_{T\in\mathbb{T}}e^{it\sqrt{-\Delta}}f_{T}
be the wave packet decomposition.
Via a standard broad-narrow reduction, it suffices to consider the “broad” norm
\|\sum_{T\in\mathbb{T}}e^{it\sqrt{-\Delta}}f_{T}\|_{\textup{BL}^{p}(X)}
.
That is, for each unit ball
B\subset X
,
|\sum_{T\in\mathbb{T}}e^{it\sqrt{-\Delta}}f_{T}|{\bf{1}}_{B}
is “broad”, which essentially means that its contribution comes from groups of wave packets with quantitatively linearly independent directions.
To utilize the “broad” information and the two-ends condition on the shading
Y(T)=X\cap T
, we need the following two conditions to hold simultaneously:

(1)

For each unit ball
B\subset X
,
|\sum_{T\in\mathbb{T}}e^{it\sqrt{-\Delta}}f_{T}|{\bf{1}}_{B}
is broad.

(2)

For each plank
T\in\mathbb{T}
,
Y(T)=X\cap T
satisfies a two-ends condition.

It turns out that achieving the two conditions requires considerable effort, mostly because the sum in
|\sum_{T\in\mathbb{T}}e^{it\sqrt{-\Delta}}f_{T}|{\bf{1}}_{B}
is a sum of oscillatory functions.
To compare, if we were to consider
\sum_{T\in\mathbb{T}}|e^{it\sqrt{-\Delta}}f_{T}|{\bf{1}}_{B}
, where oscillation is removed, then several steps of dyadic pigeonholing would suffice to ensure the two required conditions hold simultaneously.
When there is oscillation, as is the case here, we essentially prove that if the two conditions do not hold simultaneously, then the total incidence
\sum_{B\subset X}\#\{T\in\mathbb{T}:T\cap B\not=\varnothing\}
must decrease by a factor of
R^{\kappa}
for some absolute
\kappa>0
.
As a result, we develop an algorithm to show that the two conditions must hold simultaneously after finite steps of refinement.
See Section
6
, Remark
6.7
, and Section
7
for details.

Structure of the paper

In Section 2, we give a sketch of the proof of our main result.

In Section 6, we introduce an algorithm that, after being iterated in Section 7, helps us realize the broad & two-ends reduction on our operator.

3.
Preliminaries

In this section, we set up some notations and introduce basic properties for Fourier integral operators.
We denote the variables as
z=(x,t)=(x^{\prime},x_{n},t)\in\mathbb{R}^{n-1}\times\mathbb{R}\times\mathbb{R}
and
\xi=(\xi^{\prime},\xi_{n})\in\mathbb{R}^{n-1}\times\mathbb{R}
in the rest of the paper.

3.1.
Reduction to a local estimate

Recall the Fourier integral operator defined in (
1.5
) and the associated assumptions (
H1
) and (
H2
).
By considering the new phase function
\widetilde{\phi}(z;\xi)=\phi(z;\xi)-\phi(0;\xi)
, we can assume that for all
\alpha
,

(3.1)

\partial^{\alpha}_{\xi}\phi(0;\xi)\equiv 0.

Let
\lambda\geq 1
.
Given a Fourier integral operator
\mathcal{F}
defined in (
1.5
), let

(3.2)

\mathcal{F}^{\lambda}f(x,t):=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}e^{i\phi^{\lambda}(x,t;\xi)}a^{\lambda}(x,t;\xi)\widehat{f}(\xi)\mathrm{d}\xi

where
\phi^{\lambda}
and
a^{\lambda}
are defined as

\phi^{\lambda}(x,t;\xi):=\lambda\phi(x/\lambda,t/\lambda;\xi),\ \ a^{\lambda}(x,t;\xi):=a(x/\lambda,t/\lambda;\xi).

By a standard Littlewood-Paley decomposition (see, for example,
[
9
, Section 1.3]
), and by a smooth partition on the unit sphere
S^{n-1}
, we can assume the amplitude function
a
obeys that
{\rm supp}_{\xi}\ a\subset\mathbb{A}^{n}(1)^{\circ}
, the interior of
\mathbb{A}^{n}(1)
, where

\mathbb{A}^{n}(1):=\{(\xi^{\prime},\xi_{n})\in\mathbb{R}^{n}:1/2\leq\xi_{n}\leq 2,|\xi^{\prime}|\leq\xi_{n}\}.

Therefore, Theorem
1.7
reduces to the following result:

Proposition 3.1
.

Let
\mathcal{F}
be a Fourier integral operator given by (
1.5
).
Suppose the phase function
\phi
satisfies (
3.1
), and the amplitude function
a
obeys
{\rm supp}_{\xi}\ a\subset\mathbb{A}^{n}(1)^{\circ}
.
Let
\mathcal{F}^{\lambda}
be given by (
3.2
).
Then for all
\varepsilon>0
and all
p>p(n)
,

(3.3)

\|\mathcal{F}^{\lambda}f\|_{L^{p}(B_{\lambda}^{n+1})}\leq C_{\varepsilon,p}\lambda^{(n-1)(\frac{1}{2}-\frac{1}{p})+\varepsilon}\|f\|_{L^{p}(\mathbb{R}^{n})}.

3.2.
Quantitative conditions

We need to strengthen Proposition
3.1
to obtain uniform estimates for a class of Fourier integral operators (FIOs).
This is necessary because we aim to use induction on scales, but an individual operator
\mathcal{F}
is not invariant under rescaling. Fortunately, rescaling preserves certain properties of the FIOs, enabling us to perform induction for a broader class of operators simultaneously.
We will adopt the framework established in
[
1
, Section 2.3]
.

Let
c_{\text{par}}>0
be a small fixed constant.
For
{\bf A}=(A_{1},A_{2},A_{3})\in[1,\infty)^{3}
, consider the following conditions on the phase function:

(\text{H1}_{{\bf A}})

|\partial^{2}_{\xi x}\phi(z;\xi)-I_{n}|\leq c_{\text{par}}A_{1}
for all
(z;\xi)\in B^{n+1}_{1}\times\mathbb{A}^{n}(1)
.

(\text{H2}_{{\bf A}})

|\partial^{2}_{\xi^{\prime}\xi^{\prime}}\partial_{t}\phi(z;\xi)-\frac{1}{\xi_{n}}I_{n-1}|\leq c_{\text{par}}A_{2}
for all
(z;\xi)\in B^{n+1}_{1}\times\mathbb{A}^{n}(1)
.

(\textup{D1}_{\bf A})

\|\partial_{\xi}^{\beta}\partial_{x_{k}}\phi\|_{L^{\infty}(B_{1}^{n+1}\times\mathbb{A}^{n}(1))}\leq c_{\text{par}}A_{1}
for all
1\leq k\leq n
and
\beta\in\mathbb{N}_{0}^{n}
with
2\leq|\beta|\leq 3
satisfying
|\beta^{\prime}|\geq 2
;

\|\partial_{\xi^{\prime}}^{\beta^{\prime}}\partial_{x_{k}}\phi\|_{L^{\infty}(B_{1}^{n+1}\times\mathbb{A}^{n}(1))}\leq\frac{c_{\text{par}}}{2n}A_{1}
for all
\beta^{\prime}\in\mathbb{N}_{0}^{n-1}
with
|\beta^{\prime}|=3
.

(\textup{D2}_{\bf A})

For some large integer
N=N_{\varepsilon,M,p}\in\mathbb{N}
depending only on the dimension
n
and the fixed choice of
\varepsilon,M
and
p
, one has

\|\partial_{\xi}^{\beta}\partial_{z}^{\alpha}\phi\|_{L^{\infty}(B_{1}^{n+1}\times\mathbb{A}^{n}(1))}\leq\frac{c_{\text{par}}}{2n}A_{3}

for all
(\alpha,\beta)\in\mathbb{N}_{0}^{n+1}\times\mathbb{N}_{0}^{n}
with
2\leq|\alpha|\leq 4N
and
1\leq|\beta|\leq 4N+2
satisfying
1\leq|\beta|\leq 4N
or
|\beta^{\prime}|\geq 2
.

Finally, it is useful to assume a margin condition on the spatial support of the amplitude
a
:

(\textup{M}_{\bf A})

{\rm dist}({\rm supp}_{z}a,\mathbb{R}^{n+1}\setminus B_{1}^{n+1})\geq\frac{1}{4A_{3}}
.

Datum
(\phi,a)
satisfying
(\textup{H1}_{\bf A}),(\textup{H2}_{\bf A}),(\textup{D1}_{\bf A}),(\textup{D2}_{\bf A})
and
(\textup{M}_{\bf A})
(in addition to (
H1
) and (
H2
)) is said to be of
type

{\bf A}
.
By cutting the amplitude function into pieces and various rescaling arguments, it is possible to reduce to the case where
{\bf A}=\mathbf{1}:=(1,1,1)
.
We refer to
[
1
, Section 2.5]
for more details.

Throughout the paper, we will assume the FIOs are of type
1
.
Proposition
3.1
can be strengthened as

Proposition 3.2
.

Let
\mathcal{F}
be a Fourier integral operator of type
\mathbf{1}
given by (
1.5
).
Suppose the phase function
\phi
satisfies (
3.1
), and the amplitude function
a
obeys
{\rm supp}_{\xi}\ a\subset\mathbb{A}^{n}(1)^{\circ}
.
Let
\mathcal{F}^{\lambda}
be given by (
3.2
).
Then for all
\varepsilon>0
and all
p>p(n)
, there exists a constant
C_{\varepsilon,p}
independent to
\mathcal{F}
, such that

(3.4)

\|\mathcal{F}^{\lambda}f\|_{L^{p}(B_{\lambda}^{n+1})}\leq C_{\varepsilon,p}\lambda^{(n-1)(\frac{1}{2}-\frac{1}{p})+\varepsilon}\|f\|_{L^{p}(\mathbb{R}^{n})}.

Here,

is uniform for all

of type

.

3.3.
Wave packet decomposition

Fix
0<\varepsilon<1/100
.
We will perform the wave packet decomposition for a function
f
with
{\rm supp}\widehat{f}\subset\mathbb{A}^{n}(1)
at a scale
R\in[1,\lambda^{1-\varepsilon}]
.

By condition (
H1
), we see that
\partial_{\xi}\phi(\cdot,t;\xi)
is a local diffeomorphism.
Indeed, for fixed
t,\xi
, the map
x\mapsto\partial_{\xi}\phi(x,t;\xi)
has non-vanishing Jacobian.
By rescaling, we may assume
\partial_{\xi}\phi(\cdot,t;\xi)
is a diffeomorphism on the domain
B_{1}^{n}
for any
(t,\xi)\in[-1,1]\times\mathbb{A}^{n}(1)
. Therefore, it has an inverse function which we defined as follows.

Definition 3.3
.

For
(t,\xi)\in[-1,1]\times\mathbb{A}^{n}(1)
, let
\Phi(u,t;\xi)
be the unique solution to

(3.5)

\partial_{\xi}\phi(\Phi(u,t;\xi),t;\xi)=u.

Here, we may assume the equation holds for
u
in a small neighborhood of the origin.

Since
\phi
is
1
-homogeneous in
\xi
, we have
\partial_{\xi}\phi(z;\xi)=\partial_{\xi}\phi(z;s\xi)
. Hence,

(3.6)

\Phi(u,t;\xi)=\Phi(u,t;s\xi)

By the inverse function theorem and conditions
(\textup{D1}_{\bf A}),(\textup{D2}_{\bf A})
, we obtain favorable upper bounds on the
L^{\infty}
norm of
\Phi
and its derivatives.
Taking the derivative in
u
in (
3.5
), we get
\partial^{2}_{\xi x}\phi\cdot\partial_{u}\Phi=I_{n}
.
Thus,

\det\partial_{u}\Phi(u,t;\xi)\gtrsim 1.

In other words,
\partial_{u}\Phi(u,t;\xi)
is a quantitatively non-singular matrix.

We define

\gamma^{\lambda}(u,t;\xi):=\lambda\Phi(u/\lambda,t/\lambda;\xi).

Then
\gamma^{\lambda}
satisfies

(3.7)

\partial_{\xi}\phi^{\lambda}(\gamma^{\lambda}(u,t;\xi),t;\xi)=u.

For fixed
(u;\xi)
,
t\mapsto(\gamma^{\lambda}(u,t;\xi),t)
is a curve, with
t
ranging over
[-R,R]
.
Our wave packets will be certain non-isotropic neighborhoods of such curves.

Now we present the wave packet decomposition for
\mathcal{F}^{\lambda}f
in
B_{R}^{n+1}
.
In fact, we will perform the wave packet decomposition for
f
:
f=\sum_{T}f_{T}
first.
Then, by applying the linear operator
\mathcal{F}^{\lambda}
, we get
\mathcal{F}^{\lambda}f=\sum_{T}\mathcal{F}^{\lambda}f_{T}
.

We begin by decomposing in frequency space.
Fix a maximally
R^{-1/2}
-separated subset of
B^{n-1}_{1}\times\{1\}
.
For each
\xi_{\theta}
belonging to this subset, define

(3.8)

\theta=\{(\xi^{\prime},\xi_{n})\in\mathbb{A}^{n}(1):|\xi^{\prime}/\xi_{n}-\xi_{\theta}|\leq R^{-1/2}\}.

Then
\theta
is roughly a
1\times R^{-1/2}\times\dots\times R^{-1/2}
-box, and
\xi_{\theta}
is the center of
\theta
.
We call such
\theta
an
R^{-1/2}
-cap
and denote the collection of these
\theta
by
\Theta_{R^{-1/2}}
.
Note that
\Theta_{R^{-1/2}}
form a finitely overlapping covering of
\mathbb{A}^{n}(1)
.
Choose
\{\psi_{\theta}(\xi)\}_{\theta\in\Theta_{R^{-1/2}}}
to be a smooth partition of unity adapted to
\Theta_{R^{-1/2}}
, so that
\sum_{\theta\in\Theta_{R^{-1/2}}}\psi_{\theta}(\xi)=1
for
\xi\in\mathbb{A}^{n}(1)
.
Since
{\rm supp}\widehat{f}\subset\mathbb{A}^{n}(1)
, we have the frequency decomposition

\widehat{f}=\sum_{\theta\in\Theta_{R^{-1/2}}}\psi_{\theta}\widehat{f}.

We denote
f_{\theta}:=(\psi_{\theta}\widehat{f})^{\vee}
, and hence

f=\sum_{\theta\in\Theta_{R^{-1/2}}}f_{\theta}.

Before performing decomposition in the physical space, we introduce a notation.

Definition 3.4
.

Let
U\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}
be a box of dimensions
a_{1}\times\dots\times a_{n}
.
Define
U^{*}
to be the
dual box
of
U
, which is a box centered at the origin of dimensions
a_{1}^{-1}\times\dots\times a_{n}^{-1}
, and with edges parallel to the corresponding edges of

.

Now for a fixed

, we cover

by
1\times R^{1/2}\times\dots\times R^{1/2}
-slabs that are parallel to
\theta^{*}
.
Denote this collection of slabs by

(3.9)

\mathbb{T}_{\theta}^{\flat}=\{T^{\flat}\}.

Choose a set of non-negative smooth functions
\{\eta_{T^{\flat}}\}
adapted to
\mathbb{T}^{\flat}_{\theta}
, so that
\eta_{T^{\flat}}
decays rapidly outside

,
\sum\eta_{T^{\flat}}=1
in
B_{R}^{n}
and
{\rm supp}\widehat{\eta}_{T^{\flat}}\subset\theta
.
Thus, inside
B_{R}^{n}
, we have

\widehat{f}=\sum_{\theta\in\Theta_{R^{-1/2}}}\sum_{T^{\flat}\in\mathbb{T}^{\flat}_{\theta}}\widehat{\eta}_{T^{\flat}}*(\psi_{\theta}\widehat{f})+{\rm RapDec}(R)\|f\|_{2}.

Let
\delta=\varepsilon^{1000}

For each

(3.10)

Definition 3.5

(Curved plank)
.

Given
\xi_{0}\in\mathbb{A}^{n}(1)
and a box

\Gamma_{V}(\xi_{0};R):=\{(\gamma^{\lambda}(u,t;\xi_{0}),t):u\in V,|t|\leq R\}.

We primarily focus on the case where

Next, we define the non-isotropic dilation for such slabs.

Definition 3.6
.

For a slab

and a constant

Remark 3.7
.

We will also need the isotropic dilation. Namely, for a

-box

We also define the dilation and comparability for curved planks.
Since a curved plank is not convex, we will define these notions in terms of its base.

Definition 3.8
.

For a curved plank
\Gamma_{V}(\xi_{0};R)
and a constant
C>0

\Gamma_{V}(\xi_{0};R)

\frac{1}{C}\Gamma_{V_{1}}(\xi_{1};R)\subset\Gamma_{V_{2}}(\xi_{2};R)\subset C\Gamma_{V_{1}}(\xi_{1};R)

for some constant

\phi

The next lemma concerns the geometry of curved planks.

Lemma 3.9
.

Let
s\in[R^{-1/2},1]
.
Let
V\subset B_{R}^{n}
be a slab of dimensions
Rs^{2}\times Rs\times\dots\times Rs
.
Then for
|t_{0}|\leq R
, each
t_{0}
-slice
\Gamma_{V}(\xi_{0};R)\cap\{t=t_{0}\}
is comparable to an
Rs^{2}\times Rs\times\dots\times Rs
-slab.

Proof.

Suppose
V
is centered at
u_{0}
.
We express each point in
V
as
u_{0}+u
, where
u
ranges over
V-u_{0}
, an
Rs^{2}\times Rs\times\dots\times Rs
-box centered at the origin.

For fixed
(t_{0},\xi_{0})
, by applying Taylor’s expansion in the
u

(3.12)

\gamma^{\lambda}(u_{0}+u,t_{0};\xi_{0})=\gamma^{\lambda}(u_{0},t_{0};\xi_{0})+\partial_{u}\gamma^{\lambda}(u_{0},t_{0};\xi_{0})\cdot u+O(\|\partial^{2}_{uu}\gamma^{\lambda}(\cdot,t_{0};\xi_{0})\|_{\infty})|u|^{2}.

Since
\partial^{2}_{uu}\gamma^{\lambda}(u_{0},t_{0};\xi_{0})=\lambda^{-1}\partial^{2}_{uu}\Phi(u_{0}/\lambda,t_{0}/\lambda;\xi_{0})
, whose

,

O(\|\partial^{2}_{uu}\gamma^{\lambda}(\cdot,t_{0};\xi_{0})\|_{\infty})|u|^{2}\lesssim\lambda^{-1}(Rs)^{2}\leq Rs^{2}.

Therefore, the sets
\Gamma_{V}(\xi_{0};R)\cap\{t=t_{0}\}
and

\gamma^{\lambda}(u_{0},t_{0};\xi_{0})+\{\partial_{u}\gamma^{\lambda}(u_{0},t_{0};\xi_{0})\cdot x:x\in V-u_{0}\}

are within a distance
\leq Rs^{2}
from each other.
Since
\partial_{u}\gamma^{\lambda}(u_{0},t_{0};\xi_{0})
is quantitative non-singular, the latter is comparable to

which is a
Rs^{2}\times Rs\times\dots\times Rs
-slab.
Therefore,
\Gamma_{V}(\xi_{0};R)\cap\{t=t_{0}\}
is also comparable to such a slab.
∎

Using the notation of curved plank, each wave packet

Note that each
t
-slice
T\cap\{t=t_{0}\}
of
T
is morally an
R^{2\delta}\times R^{1/2+\delta}\times\dots\times R^{1/2+\delta}
-slab.
Moreover,
\Gamma_{R^{\delta}T^{\flat}}(\xi_{\theta};R)
and
\Gamma_{R^{\delta}T^{\flat}}(\xi;R)
are comparable for all
\xi\in\theta
.

Lemma 3.12
.

(\mathcal{F}^{\lambda}f_{T}){\bf{1}}_{B_{R}^{n+1}}
is essentially supported in
T
. In other words,

(\mathcal{F}^{\lambda}f_{T}){\bf{1}}_{B_{R}^{n+1}\setminus T}={\rm RapDec}(R)\|f\|_{2}.

Proof.

Note that

\mathcal{F}^{\lambda}f_{T}(x,t)=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}e^{i\phi^{\lambda}(x,t;\xi)}a^{\lambda}(x,t;\xi)\widehat{\eta}_{T^{\flat}}*(\psi_{\theta}\widehat{f})\mathrm{d}\xi.

Since
a^{\lambda}
is a smoothing symbol and
\widehat{f}_{T}
is supported in
2\theta
, we have

\mathcal{F}^{\lambda}f_{T}(x,t)=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}\Big(e^{i\phi^{\lambda}(x,t;\xi)}a^{\lambda}\widetilde{\psi}_{\theta}\Big)\widehat{\eta}_{T^{\flat}}*(\psi_{\theta}\widehat{f})\mathrm{d}\xi+{\rm RapDec}(R)\|f\|_{2},

where
\widetilde{\psi}_{\theta}
is a bump function on
2\theta
.
Let
G_{x,t}(\xi)
be so that
\overline{G_{x,t}(\xi)}=e^{i\phi^{\lambda}(x,t;\xi)}a^{\lambda}\widetilde{\psi}_{\theta}
.
By Plancherel,

(3.15)

\mathcal{F}^{\lambda}f_{T}(x,t)=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}\overline{\check{G}_{x,t}(y)}\Big(\eta_{T^{\flat}}(\psi_{\theta}^{\vee}*f)\Big)(y)\mathrm{d}y+{\rm RapDec}(R)\|f\|_{2}.

Let us compute

\overline{\check{G}_{x,t}(y)}=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}e^{i(\langle y,\xi\rangle-\phi^{\lambda}(x,t;\xi))}\overline{a^{\lambda}\widetilde{\psi}_{\theta}}\mathrm{d}\xi.

Via the change of variable
\xi=\xi_{\theta}+\eta
, we have

\overline{\check{G}_{x,t}(y)}=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}e^{i(\langle y,\xi_{\theta}+\eta\rangle-\phi^{\lambda}(x,t;\xi_{\theta}+\eta))}\overline{a^{\lambda}(z;\xi_{\theta}+\eta)\widetilde{\psi}_{\theta}(\xi_{\theta}+\eta)}\mathrm{d}\xi.

By the method of non-stationary phase (see
[
12
, Lemma 5.4]
for a similar argument),
|\check{G}_{x,t}(y)|={\rm RapDec}(R)
if

\partial_{\xi}\phi^{\lambda}(x,t;\xi_{\theta}+\eta)-y\notin\theta^{*}.

Recall (
3.14
), which implies that
\partial_{\xi}\phi^{\lambda}(x,t;\xi_{\theta}+\eta)-\partial_{\xi}\phi^{\lambda}(x,t;\xi_{\theta})\in 4\theta^{*}
.
Therefore,
|\check{G}_{x,t}(y)|={\rm RapDec}(R)
if

(3.16)

\partial_{\xi}\phi^{\lambda}(x,t;\xi_{\theta})-y\notin 5\theta^{*}.

Now we are ready to prove the lemma. If
(x_{0},t_{0})\in B_{R}^{n+1}\setminus T
, then

(x_{0},t_{0})\notin(T\cap\{t=t_{0}\})=\{\gamma^{\lambda}(u,t_{0};\xi_{\theta}):u\in R^{\delta}T^{\flat}\}.

By applying
\partial_{\xi}\phi^{\lambda}(\cdot,\cdot;\xi_{\theta})
to both sides (recall (
3.7
)), we see that the above is a consequence of

(3.17)

\partial_{\xi}\phi^{\lambda}(x_{0},t_{0};\xi_{\theta})\notin R^{\delta}T^{\flat}.

We use (
3.15
) to show
\mathcal{F}^{\lambda}f_{T}(x_{0},t_{0})={\rm RapDec}(R)\|f\|_{2}
:
Since
\eta_{T^{\flat}}
decreases rapidly outside
R^{\delta/2}T^{\flat}
, it suffices to show
|\check{G}_{x_{0},t_{0}}(y)|={\rm RapDec}(R)
for
y\in R^{\delta/2}T^{\flat}
.
This boils down to verifying (
3.16
), or equivalently,
\partial_{\xi}\phi^{\lambda}(x_{0},t_{0};\xi_{\theta})\notin y+5\theta^{*}\subset 5R^{\delta/2}T^{\flat}
, since
T^{\flat}
is a translated copy of
\theta^{*}
and since
y\in T^{\flat}
.
However, this is given by (
3.17
).
∎

3.4.
Comparing wave packets at different scales

Next, we discuss the wave packet decomposition for
\mathcal{F}^{\lambda}f
inside a ball not necessarily centered at the origin.
Fix
z_{0}=(x_{0},t_{0})\in B^{n+1}_{\lambda}
and consider the ball
B_{R}^{n+1}(z_{0})
.
Define
\widetilde{f}
so that

(\widetilde{f})^{\wedge}(\xi):=e^{i\phi^{\lambda}(z_{0};\xi)}\widehat{f}(\xi).

As a result,

\mathcal{F}^{\lambda}f(z)=\widetilde{\mathcal{F}}^{\lambda}\widetilde{f}(\widetilde{z})\ \textup{~for~}\widetilde{z}=z-z_{0},

where
\widetilde{\mathcal{F}}^{\lambda}
is the Fourier integral operator whose phase
\widetilde{\phi}^{\lambda}
and amplitude
\widetilde{a}^{\lambda}
are given by

\widetilde{\phi}(z;\xi):=\phi(z+\frac{z_{0}}{\lambda};\xi)-\phi(\frac{z_{0}}{\lambda};\xi)\textup{~and~}\widetilde{a}(z;\xi):=a(z+\frac{z_{0}}{\lambda};\xi).

If
z\in B^{n+1}_{R}(z_{0})
, then
\widetilde{z}\in B^{n+1}_{R}
, and we can therefore apply the wave packet decomposition for
\widetilde{\mathcal{F}}^{\lambda}\widetilde{f}
inside
B^{n+1}_{R}
:

\widetilde{\mathcal{F}}^{\lambda}\widetilde{f}=\sum_{\widetilde{T}}\widetilde{\mathcal{F}}^{\lambda}\widetilde{f}_{\widetilde{T}}+{\rm RapDec}(R)\|f\|_{2}.

Each
\widetilde{T}
is given by the collection of
\widetilde{z}\in B^{n+1}_{R}
satisfying

\partial_{\xi}\phi(\frac{\widetilde{z}}{\lambda}+\frac{z_{0}}{\lambda};\xi_{\theta})=\frac{v}{\lambda}+\partial_{\xi}\phi(\frac{z_{0}}{\lambda};\xi_{\theta}),\textup{~for~some~}v\in R^{\delta}T^{\flat}.

Recall (
3.9
) that
\{T^{\flat}\}
are translated copies of
\theta^{*}
that cover
B_{R}^{n}
.

For a given
z_{0}
, define

(3.18)

T(z_{0}):=\Big\{\Big(\gamma^{\lambda}(v+\partial_{\xi}\phi^{\lambda}(z_{0};\xi_{\theta}),t;\xi_{\theta}),t\Big):v\in R^{\delta}T^{\flat},|t-t_{0}|\leq R\Big\}.

Remark 3.13
.

If we replace
\xi_{\theta}
by any
\xi\in\theta
in the definition of
T(z_{0})
in (
3.18
), then the resulting curved plank is comparable to the original curved plank defined using
\xi_{\theta}
.
This is a consequence of Lemma
3.11
.
We will use this fact later when comparing wave packets in different scales.

Thus, the function

\widetilde{\mathcal{F}}^{\lambda}\widetilde{f}_{\widetilde{T}}(z-z_{0})

is essentially supported in
T(z_{0})
when restricted to
B^{n+1}_{R}(z_{0})
.
We denote the collection of planks
\{T(z_{0}):T^{\flat}\in\mathbb{T}^{\flat}_{\theta}\}
as

(3.19)

\mathbb{T}_{\theta}(z_{0}):=\{T(z_{0}):T^{\flat}\in\mathbb{T}^{\flat}_{\theta}\}.

For each
T\in\bigcup_{\theta}\mathbb{T}_{\theta}(z_{0})
, we also define

(3.20)

\widehat{f}_{T}(\xi):=e^{-i\phi^{\lambda}(z_{0};\xi)}(\widetilde{f}_{\widetilde{T}})^{\wedge}(\xi),

where
T^{\flat}=\widetilde{T}^{\flat}
.
Under this notation,

\mathcal{F}^{\lambda}f=\sum_{T\in\bigcup_{\theta}\mathbb{T}_{\theta}(z_{0})}\mathcal{F}^{\lambda}f_{T}+{\rm RapDec}(R)\|f\|_{2}

in
B^{n+1}_{R}(z_{0})
.
For each
T\in\mathbb{T}_{\theta}(z_{0})
, we remark that
(\mathcal{F}^{\lambda}f_{T}){\bf{1}}_{B_{R}^{n+1}(z_{0})}
is essentially supported in
R^{\delta}T
,
f_{T}
is essentially supported in
T^{\flat}
, and
\widehat{f}_{T}
is supported in
2\theta
.

We have the following lemma regarding the comparison of wave packets at different scales. Its counterpart can be found in
[
12
, Lemma 9.1]
.

Lemma 3.14
.

Let
r\in[1,R)
, and let
\theta\in\Theta_{R^{-1/2}}
,
\widetilde{\theta}\in\Theta_{r^{-1/2}}
.
Suppose
B_{r}^{n+1}(z_{0})\subset B_{R}^{n+1}
, and suppose
T\in\mathbb{T}_{\theta}
,
\widetilde{T}\in\mathbb{T}_{\widetilde{\theta}}(z_{0})
.
If either
\theta\not\subset 4\widetilde{\theta}
, or
100T\cap 100\widetilde{T}=\emptyset
, then

\Big(f_{T}\Big)_{\widetilde{T}}={\rm RapDec}(R)\|f\|_{2}.

Proof.

For simplicity, we denote
e^{i\phi^{\lambda}(z_{0};D)}g:=(e^{i\phi^{\lambda}(z_{0};\cdot)}\widehat{g})^{\vee}
.
Recall (
3.20
) for the definition of the wave packet decomposition inside
B_{r}^{n+1}(z_{0})
, we have

e^{i\phi^{\lambda}(z_{0};D)}\Big(f_{T}\Big)_{\widetilde{T}}=\eta_{\widetilde{T}^{\flat}}\Big(\psi_{\widetilde{\theta}}^{\vee}*(e^{i\phi^{\lambda}(z_{0};D)}f_{T})\Big).

Note that
{\rm supp}(e^{i\phi^{\lambda}(z_{0};D)}f_{T})^{\wedge}={\rm supp}\widehat{f}_{T}
, which is essentially contained in
2\theta
.
Since
{\rm supp}\psi_{\widetilde{\theta}}\subset\widetilde{\theta}
, if
\theta\not\subset 4\widetilde{\theta}
, we have
e^{i\phi^{\lambda}(z_{0};D)}\Big(f_{T}\Big)_{\widetilde{T}}={\rm RapDec}(R)\|f\|_{2}
, which implies
\Big(f_{T}\Big)_{\widetilde{T}}={\rm RapDec}(R)\|f\|_{2}
.

Next, we assume
\theta\subset 4\widetilde{\theta}
and
10T\cap\widetilde{T}=\emptyset
.
Compute

\displaystyle e^{i\phi^{\lambda}(z_{0};D)}f_{T}(x)

\displaystyle=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}e^{i(\phi^{\lambda}(z_{0};\xi)+\langle x,\xi\rangle)}\widehat{\eta}_{T^{\flat}}*(\psi_{\theta}\widehat{f})\mathrm{d}\xi

\displaystyle=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}\Big(e^{i(\phi^{\lambda}(z_{0};\xi)+\langle x,\xi\rangle)}\widetilde{\psi}_{\theta}\Big)\widehat{\eta}_{T^{\flat}}*(\psi_{\theta}\widehat{f})\mathrm{d}\xi+{\rm RapDec}(R)\|f\|_{2},

where
\widetilde{\psi}_{\theta}
is a bump function on
2\theta
.
Introduce
G_{z_{0},x}(\xi)
such that
\overline{G_{z_{0},x}(\xi)}=e^{i(\phi^{\lambda}(z_{0};\xi)+\langle x,\xi\rangle)}\widetilde{\psi}_{\theta}
.
By Plancherel,

(3.21)

e^{i\phi^{\lambda}(z_{0};D)}f_{T}(x)=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}\overline{\check{G}_{z_{0},x}(y)}\Big(\eta_{T^{\flat}}(\psi_{\theta}^{\vee}*f)\Big)(y)\mathrm{d}y+{\rm RapDec}(R)\|f\|_{2}.

Let us compute

\check{G}_{z_{0},x}(y)=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}e^{i(-\phi^{\lambda}(z_{0};\xi)+\langle y-x,\xi\rangle)}\widetilde{\psi}_{\theta}\mathrm{d}\xi.

By the same argument as in the proof of Lemma
3.12
,
|\check{G}_{z_{0},x}(y)|={\rm RapDec}(R)
if

(3.22)

\partial_{\xi}\phi^{\lambda}(z_{0};\xi_{\theta})+x-y\notin 5\theta^{*}.

To show
\eta_{\widetilde{T}^{\flat}}\Big(\psi_{\widetilde{\theta}}^{\vee}*(e^{i\phi^{\lambda}(z_{0};D)}f_{T})\Big)={\rm RapDec}(R)\|f\|_{2}
, we just need to show
\eqref{planch2}={\rm RapDec}(R)\|f\|_{2}
for
x\in r^{\delta/2}\widetilde{T}^{\flat}
.
This boils down to show
|\check{G}_{z_{0},x}(y)|={\rm RapDec}(R)
for
x\in r^{\delta/2}\widetilde{T}^{\flat},y\in R^{\delta/2}T^{\flat}
.
By (
3.22
), it suffices to show

(3.23)

\partial_{\xi}\phi^{\lambda}(z_{0};\xi_{\theta})+x-y\notin\theta^{*}\ \textup{~for~all~}x\in r^{\delta/2}\widetilde{T}^{\flat},y\in R^{\delta/2}T^{\flat}.

Since
T^{\flat}
is a translated copy of
\theta^{*}
, (
3.23
) follows from

2R^{\delta/2}T^{\flat}\cap(\partial_{\xi}\phi^{\lambda}(z_{0};\xi_{\theta})+r^{\delta/2}\widetilde{T}^{\flat})=\emptyset.

By applying the transformation
\partial_{\xi}(\cdot,t;\xi_{\theta})
(recall (
3.7
)), the above is a consequence of the fact that

4T
and

\{\Big(\partial_{\xi}\phi^{\lambda}(v+\partial_{\xi}\phi^{\lambda}(z_{0};\xi_{\theta}),t;\xi_{\theta}),t\Big):v\in r^{\delta/2}\widetilde{T}^{\flat}\}

are disjoint.
However, by Remark
3.13
, the latter one is contained in
100\widetilde{T}
.
Thus, if
4T\cap 100\widetilde{T}=\emptyset
, then the aforementioned two planks are disjoint, yielding (
3.23
).
∎

3.5.
Broad norm

We introduce the broad norm in the variable coefficient setting.
A similar setup can be found in
[
12
, Section 1.5]
.

Let
K=R^{\varepsilon^{50}}
.
Recall (
3.8
) that
\mathbb{A}^{n}(1)
can be partitioned into caps
\Theta_{K^{-1}}=\{\tau\}
, where each
\tau
has dimensions
1\times K^{-1}\times\dots\times K^{-1}
.
In view of the rescaling
\phi^{\lambda}
of the phase function, define the rescaled generalized Gauss map

(3.24)

G^{\lambda}(z;\xi):=G(z/\lambda;\xi)\ \textup{~for~}(z;\xi)\in{\rm supp}\ a^{\lambda}.

For each
z\in B_{\lambda}^{n+1}
, there is a range of normal directions associated with the cap
\tau
, as given by

G^{\lambda}(z;\tau):=\{G^{\lambda}(z;\xi):\xi\in\tau,(z;\xi)\in{\rm supp}\ a^{\lambda}\}.

For any subspace
V\subset\mathbb{R}^{n+1}
, define
\measuredangle(G^{\lambda}(z;\tau),V)
to be the smallest angle between non-zero vectors
v\in V
and
v^{\prime}\in G^{\lambda}(z;\tau)
.

Fix
A\geq 1
and
1\leq k\leq n+1
.
For a
K^{2}
-ball
B_{K^{2}}^{n+1}\subset B_{\lambda}^{n+1}
centered at
z_{0}
, define

\mu_{\mathcal{F}^{\lambda}f}(B_{K^{2}}^{n+1}):=\min_{V_{1},\dots,V_{A}}\max_{\tau\notin V_{i}}\int_{B_{K^{2}}^{n+1}}|\mathcal{F}^{\lambda}f_{\tau}|^{p}.

Here,
V_{1},\dots,V_{A}
are taken over all
(k-1)
-dimensional subspaces, and
\tau\notin V_{i}
refers to those
\tau\in\Theta_{K^{-1}}
such that
\measuredangle(G^{\lambda}(z_{0};\tau),V_{i})>K^{-2}
for all
1\leq i\leq A
.
If
U\subset B_{\lambda}^{n+1}
is a disjoint union of
K^{2}
-balls, then we define the broad norm as

\|\mathcal{F}^{\lambda}f\|_{\textup{BL}^{p}_{k,A}(U)}^{p}:=\sum_{B_{K^{2}}^{n+1}\subset U}\mu_{\mathcal{F}^{\lambda}f}(B_{K^{2}}^{n+1}).

The broad norm enjoys the following properties.
See
[
12
]
for their proofs.

Lemma 3.15
.

Suppose
1\leq p<\infty
,
A_{1},A_{2}\geq 1
and
A=A_{1}+A_{2}
, then

\|\mathcal{F}^{\lambda}(f_{1}+f_{2})\|_{\textup{BL}^{p}_{k,A}(U)}\lesssim\|\mathcal{F}^{\lambda}f_{1}\|_{\textup{BL}^{p}_{k,A_{1}}(U)}+\|\mathcal{F}^{\lambda}f_{2}\|_{\textup{BL}^{p}_{k,A_{2}}(U)}.

Lemma 3.16
.

Suppose
1\leq p,p_{1},p_{2}<\infty
,
0\leq\alpha_{1},\alpha_{2}\leq 1
satisfy
\alpha_{1}+\alpha_{2}=1
and

\frac{1}{p}=\frac{\alpha_{1}}{p_{1}}+\frac{\alpha_{2}}{p_{2}}.

Suppose also
A_{1},A_{2}\geq 1
and
A=A_{1}+A_{2}
.
Then,

\|\mathcal{F}^{\lambda}f\|_{\textup{BL}^{p}_{k,A}(U)}\lesssim\|\mathcal{F}^{\lambda}f\|_{\textup{BL}^{p_{1}}_{k,A_{1}}(U)}^{\alpha_{1}}\|\mathcal{F}^{\lambda}f\|_{\textup{BL}^{p_{2}}_{k,A_{2}}(U)}^{\alpha_{2}}.

3.6.
Some
L^{2}
results

The first one is a local
L^{2}
estimate for a sum of wave packets whose directions are transverse to the integration domain.

Lemma 3.17
.

Let
1\leq K^{1000}\leq R
.
Let
S
be a
1\times R^{1/2}\times\cdots\times R^{1/2}
-slab in
\mathbb{R}^{n+1}
parallel to the
n
-dimensional subspace
V
.
Let
\tau\in\Theta_{K^{-1}}
.
Suppose there exists
z_{0}\in S
such that

\measuredangle(G^{\lambda}(z_{0};\tau),V)>K^{-2}.

Then for any set of distinct
R
-planks
{\bf T}
that
\theta(T)\subset\tau
for all
T\in{\bf T}
, we have

\|\sum_{T\in{\bf T}}\mathcal{F}^{\lambda}f_{T}\|_{L^{2}(S)}^{2}\lesssim K^{O(1)}\sum_{T\in{\bf T}}\|f_{T}\|_{2}^{2}.

Proof.

We refer the reader to Figure
2
, where the planks are curved rather than straight.
Since we are allowed to lose a
K^{O(1)}
factor, by the triangle inequality, we assume the direction caps of all the
T\in{\bf T}^{\prime}
lie in a
K^{-10n}
-cap.
Let
{\bf T}=\cup_{\theta}{\bf T}_{\theta}
, where the directional cap for the planks in
{\bf T}_{\theta}
is
\theta
.
Let
f_{\theta}=\sum_{T\in{\bf T}_{\theta}}f_{T}
,
so that

\sum_{T\in{\bf T}}\mathcal{F}^{\lambda}f_{T}=\sum_{\theta}\mathcal{F}^{\lambda}f_{\theta}.

We will show

(3.25)

\|\sum_{\theta}\mathcal{F}^{\lambda}f_{\theta}\|_{L^{2}(S)}^{2}\lesssim K^{O(1)}\sum_{\theta}\|f_{\theta}\|_{2}^{2}.

Then the lemma follows from the
L^{2}
-orthogonality of
\sum_{T\in{\bf T}_{\theta}}f_{T}
.

Let
{\bf{1}}_{S}^{\ast}
be a bump function that equals 1 on
S
and is supported on
2S
.
By expanding the
L^{2}
norm, the left-hand side of (
3.25
) is bounded above by

(3.26)

\sum_{\theta_{1},\theta_{2}}\int{\bf{1}}_{S}^{\ast}e^{i(\phi^{\lambda}(z;\xi_{1})-\phi^{\lambda}(z;\xi_{2})}\mathrm{d}z\int a^{\lambda}(z;\xi_{1})\overline{a^{\lambda}(z;\xi_{2})}\widehat{f}_{\theta_{1}}(\xi_{1})\overline{\widehat{f}_{\theta_{2}}(\xi_{2})}\mathrm{d}\xi_{1}\mathrm{d}\xi_{2}.

Note that (
3.25
) follows from the estimate

(3.27)

\big|\int{\bf{1}}_{S}^{\ast}e^{i(\phi^{\lambda}(z;\xi_{1})-\phi^{\lambda}(z;\xi_{2}))}\mathrm{d}z\big|=R^{n/2}(1+R^{1/2}K^{-O(1)}|\xi_{1}-\xi_{2}|)^{-10n}.

In fact, for each
\theta
, partition
\theta
into
R^{-1/2}
-balls
\omega
, and by let
\widehat{f}_{\omega}=\widehat{f}_{\theta}{\bf{1}}_{\omega}
.
Then
\widehat{f}_{\omega}
is supported in an
R^{1/2}
-ball, and we have

\eqref{expanding}=\sum_{\omega_{1},\omega_{2}}\int{\bf{1}}_{S}^{\ast}e^{i(\phi^{\lambda}(z;\xi_{1})-\phi^{\lambda}(z;\xi_{2})}\mathrm{d}z\int a^{\lambda}(z;\xi_{1})\overline{a^{\lambda}(z;\xi_{2})}\widehat{f}_{\omega_{1}}(\xi_{1})\overline{\widehat{f}_{\omega_{2}}(\xi_{2})}\mathrm{d}\xi_{1}\mathrm{d}\xi_{2},

which, via (
3.27
) and the estimate
\|\widehat{f}_{\omega}\|_{1}\lesssim R^{-n/4}\|\widehat{f}_{\omega}\|_{2}
, is bounded above by

\displaystyle\sum_{\omega_{1},\omega_{2}}\int|f_{\omega_{1}}(\xi_{1})f_{\omega_{2}}(\xi_{2})|R^{n/2}(1+R^{1/2}K^{-O(1)}|\xi_{1}-\xi_{2}|)^{-10n}\mathrm{d}\xi_{1}\mathrm{d}\xi_{2}

\displaystyle\lesssim

\displaystyle\sum_{\omega_{1},\omega_{2}}(1+R^{1/2}K^{-O(1)}|c(\omega_{1})-c(\omega_{2})|)^{-10n}\|\widehat{f}_{\omega_{1}}\|_{2}\|\widehat{f}_{\omega_{2}}\|_{2}

\displaystyle\lesssim

\displaystyle\,K^{O(1)}\sum_{\omega}\|\widehat{f}_{\omega}\|_{2}^{2}=K^{O(1)}\sum_{\theta}\|\widehat{f}_{\theta}\|_{2}^{2}.

Here
c(\omega)
is the center of
\omega
.
This proves (
3.25
).

By the method of stationary phase, to prove (
3.27
), it suffices to show that there exists
\vec{v}\parallel V
such that for all
z\in 2S
,

(3.28)

|\partial_{z}(\phi^{\lambda}(z;\xi_{1})-\phi^{\lambda}(z;\xi_{2}))\cdot\vec{v}|\gtrsim K^{-O(1)}|\xi_{1}-\xi_{2}|.

Via Taylor’s theorem,
\partial_{z}\phi^{\lambda}(z;\xi_{1})-\partial_{z}\phi^{\lambda}(z;\xi_{2})=\partial^{2}_{z\xi}\phi^{\lambda}(z;\xi_{2})\cdot(\xi_{1}-\xi_{2})+O(|\xi_{1}-\xi_{2}|^{2})
.
Since
|\partial^{2}_{z\xi}\phi^{\lambda}(z;\xi_{2})\cdot(\xi_{1}-\xi_{2})|\gtrsim|\xi_{1}-\xi_{2}|
and since
|\xi_{1}-\xi_{2}|\lesssim K^{-100}
, the following is true:

(1)

|\partial_{z}(\phi^{\lambda}(z;\xi_{1})-\phi^{\lambda}(z;\xi_{2}))|\sim|\xi_{1}-\xi_{2}|
.

(2)

\measuredangle(\partial_{z}(\phi^{\lambda}(z;\xi_{1})-\phi^{\lambda}(z;\xi_{2})),\partial^{2}_{z\xi}\phi^{\lambda}(z;\xi_{2})\cdot(\xi_{1}-\xi_{2}))\lesssim K^{-100}
.

Thus, (
3.28
) boils down to showing that for all
z\in 2S
,

(3.29)

\measuredangle(\partial^{2}_{z\xi}\phi^{\lambda}(z;\xi_{2})\cdot(\xi_{1}-\xi_{2}),V^{\perp})\gtrsim K^{-50}.

Note that when
|z-z_{0}|\lesssim R^{1/2}
,

|\partial^{2}_{z\xi}\phi^{\lambda}(z;\xi_{2})-\partial^{2}_{z\xi}\phi^{\lambda}(z_{0};\xi_{2})|\lesssim|z-z_{0}|\cdot\|\partial^{3}_{zz\xi}\phi^{\lambda}\|_{\infty}\lesssim R^{1/2}\lambda^{-1}<K^{-100}.

Hence, to show (
3.29
), we just need to show

(3.30)

\measuredangle(\partial^{2}_{z\xi}\phi^{\lambda}(z_{0};\xi_{2})\cdot(\xi_{1}-\xi_{2}),V^{\perp})>K^{-30}.

However, by the assumption in the lemma, we have

\measuredangle(G^{\lambda}(z_{0};\xi_{2}),V)>K^{-2},

which, since
G^{\lambda}(z_{0};\xi_{2})
is orthogonal to
\partial^{2}_{z\xi}\phi^{\lambda}(z_{0};\xi_{2})\cdot(\xi_{1}-\xi_{2})
, implies (
3.30
).
∎

As a direct corollary, we have

Lemma 3.18
.

Let
Q
be an
R^{1/2}
-ball and
{\bf T}
is a set of wave packets.
Then

\|\sum_{T\in{\bf T}}\mathcal{F}^{\lambda}f_{T}\|_{L^{2}(Q)}^{2}\lesssim R^{1/2}\sum_{T\in{\bf T}}\|f_{T}\|_{2}^{2}.

4.
Wave packet density

In this section, we introduce the wave packet density
\mathcal{W}(f,B^{n+1}_{R}(z_{0}))
.
Given a
s
-cap
\tau\in\Theta_{s}
with
R^{-1/2}\leq s\leq 1
, recall that

V_{\tau,R}=(Rs^{2})\tau^{*}

is an isotropic dilate of
\tau^{*}
, a box centered at the origin of dimensions
Rs^{2}\times Rs\times\dots\times Rs
.

Definition 4.1

(Wave packet density)
.

Suppose
g
is a function defined in
\mathbb{R}^{n}
such that
{\rm supp}\widehat{g}\subset\mathbb{A}^{n}(1)
, and suppose
B^{n+1}_{R}(z_{0})=B^{n+1}_{R}(x_{0},t_{0})\subset B_{\lambda}^{n+1}
. Define

(4.1)

\begin{split}&\mathcal{W}(g,B_{R}^{n+1}(z_{0})):=\\
&\sup_{R^{-1/2}\leq s\leq 1}\sup_{\tau\in\Theta_{s}}\sup_{V\parallel V_{\tau,R}}\bigg(\frac{1}{|V|}\int\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\theta\subset\tau\\
\theta\in\Theta_{R^{-1/2}}\end{subarray}}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}T\in\mathbb{T}_{\theta}(z_{0})\\

5.1.
Lorentz rescaling

Suppose
\tau\in\Theta_{K^{-1}}
and

We want to find an upper bound for

The idea is to perform a rescaling so that

becomes
\widetilde{\mathcal{F}}^{\lambda/K^{2}}g

\widetilde{\mathcal{F}}
is a new Fourier integral operator, and

R
to

Moreover, when
\mathcal{F}
is of type
\mathbf{1}

is of type
(1,1,C)
for some constant

(see
[
1

).
Therefore, by partitioning the new operator

into

Next, we describe the rescaling and the relevant geometry.

For each

Let us first prove a standard lemma, which provides an upper bound on

Lemma 5.6
.

For any
(k-1)
-dimensional subspace

(5.14)

\#\{\tau\in\Theta_{K^{-1}}:\measuredangle(G^{\lambda}(z_{B},\tau),V)\leq K^{-2}\}\lesssim\max\{1,K^{k-3}\}.

Proof.

Without loss of generality, we assume
z_{B}=0

Via change of variables, we also assume
\phi
is of reduced form as in the proof of Lemma
5.4

5.9

\phi(x,t;\xi)=\langle x,\xi\rangle+t\frac{|\xi^{\prime}|^{2}}{\xi_{n}}+\widetilde{\mathcal{E}}(x,t;\xi),

where
|\widetilde{\mathcal{E}}(x,t;\xi)|=O(|t||\xi^{\prime}|^{3}+|z|^{2}|\xi^{\prime}|^{2})

Applying

\partial^{2}_{z\xi}\phi^{\lambda}\cdot(\frac{d}{dt}\gamma^{\lambda},1)=0.

Hence,
(\frac{d}{dt}\gamma^{\lambda}(z;\xi),1)

One can compute as in (

Since
(-2\xi^{\prime},|\xi^{\prime}|^{2},1)
is parallel to

\#\{\tau\in\Theta_{K^{-1}}:(-2\xi_{\tau}^{\prime},|\xi_{\tau}^{\prime}|^{2},1)\in N_{CK^{-2}}(V)\}.

Let
\mathcal{S}^{n-1}:=\{(-2\xi^{\prime},|\xi^{\prime}|^{2},1):|\xi^{\prime}|\leq 100^{-1}\}

Since

Since

we have

\#\{\tau\in\Theta_{K^{-1}}:(\xi_{\tau}^{\prime},|\xi_{\tau}^{\prime}|^{2},1)\in N_{CK^{-2}}(V)\}\lesssim\max\{1,K^{k-3}\}.\qed

Lemma 5.7
.

There exists a constant

Let

Here

Define

|v_{\xi_{1}}\wedge v_{\xi_{2}}\wedge v_{\xi_{3}}|\gtrsim K^{-O(1)}.

\angle\big(v(\xi_{i}),v(\xi_{j})\big)\gtrsim K^{-2}

Recall (

).
Next, we discuss the intersection of three

Lemma 5.8
.

Let
\vec{v}_{i}=\partial_{z}\phi^{\lambda}(z;\xi_{i})
. Suppose

(5.20)

|\vec{v}_{1}\wedge\vec{v}_{2}\wedge\vec{v}_{3}|\geq K^{-1}.

Then

(5.21)

|T_{1}\cap T_{2}\cap T_{3}|\lesssim K^{O(1)}R^{\frac{n-2}{2}}.

Proof.

Without loss of generality, we may assume that for all
i=1,2,3
,
T_{i}
is centered at
(u,t)=(0,0)
.
That is, the core curve of each
T_{j}
intersects
(0,0)
.

Next, we show that
T\cap B_{R^{1/2}}
is comparable to a slab of dimensions
R^{2\delta}\times R^{1/2}\times\cdots\times R^{1/2}
.
The proof is a refinement of Lemma
3.9
.
Express
\gamma^{\lambda}(u,t;\xi)
as

\gamma^{\lambda}(u,t;\xi)-\gamma^{\lambda}(0,t;\xi)+\gamma^{\lambda}(0,t;\xi)-\gamma^{\lambda}(0,0;\xi)+\gamma^{\lambda}(0,0;\xi).

As we have seen in the proof of Lemma
3.9
, since

, and
R\leq\lambda^{1-\epsilon}
, we have
\gamma^{\lambda}(u,t;\xi)-\gamma^{\lambda}(0,t;\xi)=\partial_{u}\gamma^{\lambda}(0,t;\xi)\cdot u+O(1)
.
Notice that

\partial_{u}\gamma^{\lambda}(0,t;\xi)-\partial_{u}\gamma^{\lambda}(0,0;\xi)=O(\lambda^{-1}t)=O(R^{-1/2})

whenever

.
Consequently, we obtain

\gamma^{\lambda}(u,t;\xi)-\gamma^{\lambda}(0,t;\xi)=\partial_{u}\gamma^{\lambda}(0,0;\xi)\cdot u+O(1).

Similarly, as
\|(\partial_{t})^{2}\gamma^{\lambda}\|_{\infty}\lesssim\lambda^{-1}
, we have

\gamma^{\lambda}(0,t;\xi)-\gamma^{\lambda}(0,0;\xi)=\partial_{t}\gamma^{\lambda}(0,0;\xi)t+O(1).

In summary, we know that
T\cap B^{n+1}_{R^{1/2}}
is comparable to the set

\{\Big(\partial_{t}\gamma^{\lambda}(0,0;\xi)t+\partial_{u}\gamma^{\lambda}(0,0;\xi)\cdot x,t\Big):\ \ x\in T^{\flat},\ |t|\leq\sqrt{R}\},

which is a slab in
\mathbb{R}^{n+1}
of dimensions
R^{2\delta}\times R^{1/2}\times\cdots\times R^{1/2}
.

To describe a normal vector of the slab

\mathbb{R}^{n}
-space, the vector
\xi
is normal to

of

is of the form
([\partial_{u}\gamma^{\lambda}(0,0;\xi)]^{-tran}\cdot\xi,0)+ae_{n+1}
.
Here
[\partial_{u}\gamma^{\lambda}(0,0;\xi)]^{-tran}
is the transpose of
[\partial_{u}\gamma^{\lambda}(0,0;\xi)]^{-1}
, and
e_{n+1}=(0,\dots,0,1)\in\mathbb{R}^{n+1}
.
Since

is orthogonal to
\partial_{t}\gamma^{\lambda}(0,0;\xi)
, we obtain

.
Taking partial derivatives of (
3.7
) in

and

\partial_{u}\gamma^{\lambda}=(\partial_{x}\partial_{\xi}\phi^{\lambda})^{-1},\hskip 8.5359pt\partial_{t}\gamma^{\lambda}=-(\partial_{x}\partial_{\xi}\phi^{\lambda})^{-1}\cdot\partial_{t}\partial_{\xi}\phi^{\lambda}.

Therefore, the vector

is normal to the slab

.
As
\phi^{\lambda}
is

\partial_{z}\phi^{\lambda}=\lim_{s\to 1^{-}}\frac{\partial_{z}\phi^{\lambda}(z;\xi)-\partial_{z}\phi^{\lambda}(z;s\xi)}{1-s}=\partial_{\xi}\partial_{z}\phi^{\lambda}\cdot\xi,

which implies further that

\vec{v}=\partial_{z}\phi^{\lambda}(0,0;\xi).

By above argument, we see that

is normal to

) and that

, we obtain
(

).
∎

Lemma 5.9
.

Let
Q
be an
R^{1/2}
-ball, and let
\mathbb{T}(Q)
be a family of
R
-planks intersecting

.
Let

be a union of
K^{2}
-balls.
Let
\mu\geq 1
.
Suppose for each
K^{2}
-ball
B\subset X
, there exists three distinct caps
\tau_{1},\tau_{2},\tau_{3}\in\Theta_{K^{-1}}
such that

(1)

\#\mathbb{T}_{\tau_{j}}(B)\geq\mu
for all

, where
\mathbb{T}_{\tau_{j}}(B):=\{T\in\mathbb{T}(Q):T\cap B\not=\varnothing,\,T\in\mathbb{T}_{\theta}\text{ for some }\theta\subset\tau_{j}\}
.

(2)

For all
\xi_{j}\in\tau_{j}
,
|v_{\xi_{1}}\wedge v_{\xi_{2}}\wedge v_{\xi_{3}}|\geq C

Then we have

|X|\lesssim(CK)^{O(1)}R^{\frac{n-2}{2}}(\#\mathbb{T}(Q))^{3}\mu^{-3}.

Let
\mathbb{T}_{\tau_{j}}(Q):=\{T\in\mathbb{T}(Q):T\in\mathbb{T}_{\theta}\text{ for some }\theta\subset\tau_{j}\}

We say three caps

5.4.
A covering lemma

In this subsection, we focus on the geometry inside each
R^{1/2}
-ball.
Although the main result is stated for all dimensions, we will only apply it in the context of 3+1 dimensions.

Suppose
T
is an
R
-plank and
Q
is an
R^{1/2}
-ball.
By Taylor’s expansion of the core curve of
T
inside
Q
, one sees that
T\cap Q
is contained in a
R^{2\delta}\times R^{1/2}\times\dots\times R^{1/2}
-slab (see the proof of Lemma
5.8
).
We will prove a covering lemma for
K^{2}\times R^{1/2}\times\dots\times R^{1/2}
-slabs inside an
R^{1/2}
-ball.
As before,
K=R^{\varepsilon^{50}}
.

Definition 5.10
.

Let
R\geq 1
and let
\rho
be such that
1\leq\rho\leq R^{O(1)}
.
Let
{\bf B}
be a set of disjoint
K^{2}
-balls inside an
R^{1/2}
-ball
Q\subset\mathbb{R}^{n+1}
.
We say
{\bf B}
is
\rho
-regular
with respect to
K^{2}\times R^{1/2}\times\dots\times R^{1/2}
-slabs in
Q
if

(1)

For any
K^{2}\times R^{1/2}\times\dots\times R^{1/2}
-slab
S
in
Q
,

\#\{B\in{\bf B}:B\cap S\neq\emptyset\}\leq 2\rho.

(2)

There exists a collection
\mathbb{S}
of
K^{2}\times R^{1/2}\times\dots\times R^{1/2}
-slabs with
\#\mathbb{S}\lesssim\#{\bf B}/\rho
so that the balls in
{\bf B}
all intersect with
\bigcup_{S\in\mathbb{S}}S
, and hence are all contained in
\bigcup_{S\in\mathbb{S}}5S
.

When
Q
is clear and
K,R
are fixed, we simply call
{\bf B}

\rho
-regular.

Definition
5.10
is similar to Definition 1.9 in
[
17
]
.
The next Lemma
5.11
is similar to Lemma 2.1 in the same paper.

Lemma 5.11
.

Suppose
{\bf B}
is a disjoint union of
K^{2}
-balls inside an
R^{1/2}
-ball
Q\subset\mathbb{R}^{4}
.
Then there exists a number
\rho
and a subset
{\bf B}^{\prime}\subset{\bf B}
with
\#{\bf B}^{\prime}\gtrsim(\log R)^{-1}\#{\bf B}
, such that
{\bf B}^{\prime}
is
\rho
-regular.

Proof.

The proof depends on a greedy algorithm.
The algorithm is designed to construct a partition of
{\bf B}
into
O(\log R)
subsets
\{{\bf B}_{\rho}\}_{\rho}
where
\rho
are dyadic numbers in
[1,R^{2}]
, and
{\bf B}_{\rho}
is
\rho
-regular.

Let
{\bf B}_{1}={\bf B}
.
We will construct a sequence
S_{1},S_{2},\dots
, along with
\rho_{1},\rho_{2},\dots
in the following way.
Let
{\bf B}_{j}={\bf B}\setminus\Big(\bigcup_{i\leq j-1}\{B\in{\bf B}:B\cap S_{i}\neq\emptyset\}\Big)
.
One sees that
{\bf B}_{j}={\bf B}_{j+1}\sqcup\{B\in{\bf B}_{j}:B\cap S_{j}\neq\emptyset\}
.
Define

\rho_{j}=\max_{S\text{ is a }K^{2}\times R^{1/2}\times\dots\times R^{1/2}\text{-slab}}\#\{B\in{\bf B}_{j}:B\cap S\neq\emptyset\}.

We use
S_{j}
to denote the
K^{2}\times R^{1/2}\times\dots\times R^{1/2}
-slab that attains the maximum.

We keep doing until
{\bf B}_{m+1}=\emptyset
at certain step. By the definition, we have the monotonicity
\rho_{i}\geq\rho_{i+1}
for any
i
.
For each dyadic
\rho\in[1,R^{O(1)}]
, we let
I_{\rho}\subset[1,m]\cap\mathbb{N}
be the indices such that
\rho_{j}\in[\rho,2\rho)
for any
j\in I_{\rho}
.
We obtain a partition of indices

[1,m]\cap\mathbb{N}=\bigsqcup_{\rho}I_{\rho}.

For each dyadic
\rho
, we inductively define

{\bf B}_{\rho}=\{B\in{\bf B}:B\cap\bigcup_{i\in I_{\rho}}S_{i}\neq\emptyset\}\setminus\Big(\bigcup_{\rho^{\prime}>\rho}{\bf B}_{\rho^{\prime}}\Big).

Here,
\bigcup_{\rho^{\prime}>\rho}
ranges over those dyadic numbers
\rho^{\prime}
bigger that
\rho
.
Thus,

{\bf B}=\bigsqcup_{\rho}{\bf B}_{\rho}.

By pigeonholing, there exists
\rho
such that
\#{\bf B}_{\rho}\gtrsim(\log R)^{-1}\#{\bf B}
.
We claim that
{\bf B}_{\rho}
is
\rho
-regular.
By the definition of
\rho_{j}
, we see that for any
K^{2}\times R^{1/2}\times\dots\times R^{1/2}
-slab
S
,
\#\{B\in{\bf B}_{\rho}:B\cap S\neq\emptyset\}\leq\sup_{i\in I_{\rho}}\rho_{i}\leq 2\rho.

Note that

{\bf B}_{\rho}=\bigcup_{j\in I_{\rho}}{\bf B}_{j}\setminus{\bf B}_{j+1}=\bigsqcup_{j\in I_{\rho}}\{B\in{\bf B}_{j}:B\cap S_{j}\neq\emptyset\}.

We have

\sum_{i\in I_{\rho}}\rho_{i}=\sum_{j\in I_{\rho}}\{B\in{\bf B}_{j}:B\cap S_{j}\neq\emptyset\}=\#{\bf B}_{\rho}.

Therefore,
\#I_{\rho}\lesssim\#{\bf B}_{\rho}/\rho
, and all the balls in
{\bf B}_{\rho}
intersect with
\bigcup_{i\in I_{\rho}}S_{i}
.
∎

Remark 5.12
.

That is to say, a similar covering lemma is expected if the family of

Let
{\bf T}

R

(1)

(2)

To formulate the two-ends property, we introduce the following notion.

Definition 6.1

;

(2)

{\bf T}=\{T\}
is a set of
R
-planks in
B_{R}^{n+1}
;

(3)

\mathcal{G}
is the
shading map
that
\mathcal{G}:{\bf T}\to P(\Omega)
. Here
P(X)
is the collection of subsets of
X
, which is also called the power set of
X
.

We remark that for a given plank
T
,
\mathcal{G}(T)
is a subset of
\Omega
.
For a triple, we will be interested in the following incidence:

\mathcal{I}({\bf B},{\bf T};\mathcal{G}):=\#\{(B,T)\in{\bf B}\times{\bf T}:B\cap T\neq\emptyset,B\subset\cup_{\mathcal{G}(T)}\}.

We also define for any
B\in{\bf B}
,

{\bf T}(B;\mathcal{G}):=\{T\in{\bf T}:B\cap T\neq\emptyset,B\subset\cup_{\mathcal{G}(T)}\},

and for any
T\in{\bf T}
,

{\bf B}(T;\mathcal{G}):=\{B\in{\bf B}:B\cap T\neq\emptyset,B\subset\cup_{\mathcal{G}(T)}\}.

By a double-counting argument, one has

(6.1)

\mathcal{I}({\bf B},{\bf T};\mathcal{G})=\sum_{B\in{\bf B}}\#{\bf T}(B;\mathcal{G})=\sum_{T\in{\bf T}}\#{\bf B}(T;\mathcal{G}).

To formulate the broad property, we introduce the following notions.

Definition 6.2
.

For an
R
-plank
T
, we use
\theta(T)\in\Theta_{R^{-1/2}}
to denote its directional cap.
Let
S
be a union of
\theta\in\Theta_{R^{-1/2}}
and let
{\bf T}
be a set of
R
-planks. We define

{\bf T}[S]:=\{T\in{\bf T}:\theta(T)\subset S\}.

Here are two examples:

(1)

{\bf T}[\theta]=\{T\in{\bf T}:\theta(T)=\theta\}
.

(2)

For
\tau\in\Theta_{K^{-1}}
,
{\bf T}[(3\tau)^{c}]=\{T\in{\bf T}:\theta(T)\not\subset 3\tau\}
.

Definition 6.3

(Broad incidence)
.

Given a triple
({\bf B},{\bf T};\mathcal{G})
and an integer
A\geq 1
, define

\mathcal{I}_{\textup{br},A}({\bf B},{\bf T};\mathcal{G}):=\inf_{{\mathcal{T}}\subset\Theta_{K^{-1}},\#{\mathcal{T}}=A}\mathcal{I}({\bf B},{\bf T}[(\cup_{\tau\in{\mathcal{T}}}3\tau)^{c}];\mathcal{G}).

For a set of
R
-planks
{\bf T}
, we define the broad cardinality as

\#_{\textup{br},A}{\bf T}:=\inf_{{\mathcal{T}}\subset\Theta_{K^{-1}},\#{\mathcal{T}}=A}\#{\bf T}[(\cup_{\tau\in{\mathcal{T}}}3\tau)^{c}].

When
A=1
, we write
\mathcal{I}_{\textup{br}}({\bf B},{\bf T};\mathcal{G})=\mathcal{I}_{\textup{br},A}({\bf B},{\bf T};\mathcal{G})
and
\#_{\textup{br}}{\bf T}=\#_{\textup{br},A}{\bf T}
in short.

Algorithm:

Now we can present the algorithm.
It allows us to refine a given triple
({\bf B},{\bf T},\mathcal{G})
, where
{\bf T}
corresponds to the set of wave packets and
{\bf B}
corresponds to the integration domain.
At the end of the algorithm, we will obtain a “refinement” of
({\bf B},{\bf T},\mathcal{G})
along with several associated parameters.
This will be summarized in Proposition
6.6
. We will use the notation: for two finite sets
E\subset F
, we say
E
is a
refinement
of
F
if
\#E\gtrapprox\#F
.

Initial stage:
Given an incidence triple
({\bf B},{\bf T};\mathcal{G})
and a
\nu>0
such that for
B\in{\bf B}
,

(6.2)

\big\|\sum_{T\in{\bf T}(B;\Sigma,\mathcal{G})}\mathcal{F}^{\lambda}f_{T}\big\|_{L^{p}(B)}\sim\nu.

Step 1: Broad multiplicity in each
K^{2}
-ball
B\in{\bf B}
.

Let
A\geq 1
be an integer.
By dyadic pigeonholing on
\{\#_{\textup{br}}{\bf T}(B;\mathcal{G}):B\in{\bf B}\}
,
we can find a refinement
{\bf B}_{1}
of
{\bf B}
and a
\mu\in[1,R^{O(1)}]\cup\{0\}
such that

(6.3)

\begin{split}&\#_{\textup{br},A}{\bf T}(B;\mathcal{G})\in[\mu,2\mu)\textup{~when~}\mu\geq 1,\\
&\#_{\textup{br},A}{\bf T}(B;\mathcal{G})=0\textup{~when~}\mu=0,\end{split}

for all
B\in{\bf B}_{1}
.
Note that

(6.4)

\mathcal{I}({\bf B}_{1},{\bf T};\mathcal{G})=\sum_{B\in{\bf B}_{1}}\#{\bf T}(B;\mathcal{G})\geq\#{\bf B}_{1}\mu.

Step 2: Two-ends reduction.

From Step 1, we obtain a triple
({\bf B}_{1},{\bf T};\mathcal{G})
.
For a plank
T\in{\bf T}
and a dyadic number
1\leq\lambda\leq R^{O(1)}
, we define

(6.5)

\mathcal{G}_{\lambda}(T):=\{\omega\in\mathcal{G}(T):\#\{B\in{\bf B}_{1}(T;\mathcal{G}):B\subset\omega\}\in[\lambda,2\lambda)\}.

Note that
\mathcal{G}_{\lambda}
is a new shading map.
Since

\mathcal{G}(T)=\bigsqcup_{\lambda}\mathcal{G}_{\lambda}(T)
, we have for each
B
,

{\bf T}(B;\mathcal{G})=\bigsqcup_{\lambda}{\bf T}(B;\mathcal{G}_{\lambda}),

and hence

\begin{split}\sum_{T\in{\bf T}(B;\mathcal{G})}\mathcal{F}^{\lambda}f_{T}=\sum_{\lambda}\sum_{T\in{\bf T}(B;\mathcal{G}_{\lambda})}\mathcal{F}^{\lambda}f_{T}.\end{split}

By pigeonholing, there is a
\lambda(B)
such that

\nu\sim\big\|\sum_{T\in{\bf T}(B;\mathcal{G})}\mathcal{F}^{\lambda}f_{T}\big\|_{L^{p}(B)}\lessapprox\big\|\sum_{T\in{\bf T}(B;\mathcal{G}_{\lambda(B)})}\mathcal{F}^{\lambda}f_{T}\big\|_{L^{p}(B)}.

By dyadic pigeonholing on the tuples

\bigg\{\Big(\lambda(B),\big\|\sum_{T\in{\bf T}(B;\mathcal{G}_{\lambda(B)})}\mathcal{F}^{\lambda}f_{T}\big\|_{L^{p}(B)}\Big)\bigg\}_{B\in{\bf B}_{1}},

there exist
{\bf B}_{2}
,
\lambda
, and
\nu_{1}
, such that the following is true:

(1)

{\bf B}_{2}
is a refinement of
{\bf B}_{1}
.

(2)

\lambda(B)=\lambda
for all
B\in{\bf B}_{2}
.

(3)

For all
B\in{\bf B}_{2}
,
\|\sum_{T\in{\bf T}(B;\mathcal{G}_{\lambda})}\mathcal{F}^{\lambda}f_{T}\|_{L^{p}(B)}\sim\nu_{1}\gtrapprox\nu
.

Next, we will prune the wave packets
{\bf T}
.
Consider the partition
{\bf T}=\bigsqcup_{\beta}{\bf T}_{\beta}
, where
\beta\in[1,2K_{\circ}]
ranges over dyadic numbers and

(6.6)

{\bf T}_{\beta}=\{T\in{\bf T}:\beta\leq\#\mathcal{G}_{\lambda}(T)<2\beta\}.

For each
B\in{\bf B}_{2}
, we have

{\bf T}(B;\mathcal{G}_{\lambda})=\bigsqcup_{\beta}{\bf T}_{\beta}(B;\mathcal{G}_{\lambda}).

As a result, for each
B\in{\bf B}_{2}
,

\sum_{T\in{\bf T}(B;\mathcal{G}_{\lambda})}\mathcal{F}^{\lambda}f_{T}=\sum_{\beta}\sum_{T\in{\bf T}_{\beta}(B;\mathcal{G}_{\lambda})}\mathcal{F}^{\lambda}f_{T}.

By pigeonholing, there is a
\beta(B)
such that

\big\|\sum_{T\in{\bf T}(B;\mathcal{G}_{\lambda})}\mathcal{F}^{\lambda}f_{T}\big\|_{L^{p}(B)}\lessapprox\big\|\sum_{T\in{\bf T}_{\beta(B)}(B;\mathcal{G}_{\lambda})}\mathcal{F}^{\lambda}f_{T}\big\|_{L^{p}(B)}.

Since
\|\sum_{T\in{\bf T}(B;\mathcal{G}_{\lambda})}\mathcal{F}^{\lambda}f_{T}\|_{L^{p}(B)}\sim\nu_{1}
for all
B\in{\bf B}_{2}
, by a similar dyadic pigeonholing argument as before, there exist
{\bf B}_{3}
,
\beta
, and
\nu_{2}
, such that:

(1)

{\bf B}_{3}
is a refinement of
{\bf B}_{2}
.

(2)

\beta(B)=\beta
for all
B\in{\bf B}_{3}
.

(3)

For all
B\in{\bf B}_{3}
,
\|\sum_{T\in{\bf T}_{\beta}(B;\mathcal{G}_{\lambda})}\mathcal{F}^{\lambda}f_{T}\|_{L^{p}(B)}\sim\nu_{2}\gtrapprox\nu_{1}
.

Note that for each
T\in{\bf T}_{\beta}
, one has

\begin{split}\#{\bf B}_{1}(T;\mathcal{G}_{\lambda})=&\,\#\{B\in{\bf B}_{1}:B\cap T\neq\emptyset,B\subset\cup_{\mathcal{G}_{\lambda}(T)}\}\\[4.30554pt]
=&\sum_{\omega\in\mathcal{G}_{\lambda}(T)}\#\{B\in{\bf B}_{1}:B\cap T\neq\emptyset,B\subset\omega\}\\
\leq&\sum_{\omega\in\mathcal{G}_{\lambda}(T)}\#\{B\in{\bf B}_{1}(T;\mathcal{G}):B\subset\omega\}.\end{split}

By (
6.5
) and (
6.6
),
\#\{B\in{\bf B}_{1}(T;\mathcal{G}):B\subset\omega\}<2\lambda
when
\omega\in\mathcal{G}_{\lambda}(T)
;
\#\mathcal{G}_{\lambda}(T)<2\beta
when
T\in{\bf T}_{\beta}
. Therefore,
\#{\bf B}_{1}(T;\mathcal{G}_{\lambda})\lesssim\beta\lambda
for
T\in{\bf T}_{\beta}
, which gives

(6.7)

\mathcal{I}({\bf B}_{1},{\bf T}_{\beta};\mathcal{G}_{\lambda})=\sum_{T\in{\bf T}_{\beta}}\#{\bf B}_{1}(T;\mathcal{G}_{\lambda})\lesssim\#{\bf T}_{\beta}\cdot\beta\lambda.

Step 3: Uniformization on each
R^{1/2}
-ball.

Let
\{Q\}
be a set of
R^{1/2}
-balls that form a partition of
B_{R}^{n+1}
.
For each
R^{1/2}
-ball
Q
, consider the set
{\bf B}_{3}|_{Q}=\{B\in{\bf B}_{3}:B\subset Q\}
. By Lemma
5.11
, there is a refinement
{\bf B}_{3}^{\prime}|_{Q}
of
{\bf B}_{3}|_{Q}
and a number
\rho(Q)\geq 1
such that
{\bf B}_{3}^{\prime}|_{Q}
is
\rho
-regular.
Let
{\bf B}_{3}^{\prime}=\bigcup_{Q}{\bf B}_{3}^{\prime}|_{Q}
, so
{\bf B}_{3}^{\prime}
is a refinement of
{\bf B}_{3}
.
For each
R^{1/2}
-ball
Q
, we consider the following quantities:

(1)

\rho(Q)
.

(2)

\#{\bf B}_{3}^{\prime}|_{Q}
.

(3)

\#_{\textup{br}}\bigcup_{B\in{\bf B}_{4}|_{Q}}{\bf T}_{\beta}(B;\mathcal{G}_{\lambda})
and
\#_{\textup{br}}\bigcup_{B\in{\bf B}_{4}|_{Q}}{\bf T}(B;\mathcal{G}_{\lambda})
.

By pigeonholing, we can find a set of
R^{1/2}
-balls
{\mathcal{Q}}=\{Q\}
and numbers
\rho,\sigma,m,\bar{m},\iota
, such that for all
Q\in{\mathcal{Q}}
the following is true:

(1)

\rho(Q)\sim\rho
.

(2)

\#{\bf B}_{3}^{\prime}|_{Q}\sim\sigma
.

(3)

\#_{\textup{br}}\bigcup_{B\in{\bf B}_{3}^{\prime}|_{Q}}{\bf T}_{\beta}(B;\mathcal{G}_{\lambda})\sim m
and
\#_{\textup{br}}\bigcup_{B\in{\bf B}_{3}^{\prime}|_{Q}}{\bf T}(B;\mathcal{G}_{\lambda})\sim\bar{m}
, so
\bar{m}\gtrsim m\gtrsim\mu
.

(4)

\mathcal{I}_{\textup{br},A}({\bf B}_{3}^{\prime}|_{Q},{\bf T};\mathcal{G})\sim\iota
.

(5)

\#\bigcup_{Q\in{\mathcal{Q}}}{\bf B}_{3}^{\prime}|_{Q}\gtrapprox\#{\bf B}_{3}^{\prime}
.

Let
{\bf B}_{4}
be a refinement of
{\bf B}_{3}^{\prime}
defined as

{\bf B}_{4}:=\bigcup_{Q\in{\mathcal{Q}}}{\bf B}_{3}^{\prime}|_{Q}.

We also define an important quantity:

(6.8)

l:=\sup_{T\in{\bf T}_{\beta}}\#\{Q\in{\mathcal{Q}}:Q\cap T\neq\emptyset\}.

What follows is some numerology regarding the parameters we have introduced.
Recall (
6.3
).
Hence, for any
B\in{\bf B}_{1}
and any
{\mathcal{T}}\subset\Theta_{K^{-1}}
with
\#{\mathcal{T}}=A
,

\#{\bf T}[(\cup_{\tau\in{\mathcal{T}}}3\tau)^{c}](B;\mathcal{G})\geq\#_{\textup{br},A}{\bf T}(B;\mathcal{G})\geq\mu.

Note that
{\bf B}_{4}\subset{\bf B}_{1}
and
\#{\bf B}_{4}|_{Q}\sim\sigma
for all
Q\in{\mathcal{Q}}
(here
{\bf B}_{4}|_{Q}=\{B\in{\bf B}_{4}:B\subset Q\}
).
Therefore, for all
Q\in{\mathcal{Q}}
,

(6.9)

\begin{split}\mathcal{I}_{\textup{br},A}({\bf B}_{4}|_{Q},{\bf T};\mathcal{G})=\inf_{{\mathcal{T}}\subset\Theta_{K^{-1}},\#{\mathcal{T}}=A}\mathcal{I}({\bf B}_{4}|_{Q},{\bf T}[(\cup_{\tau\in{\mathcal{T}}}3\tau)^{c}];\mathcal{G})\\
=\inf_{{\mathcal{T}}\subset\Theta_{K^{-1}},\#{\mathcal{T}}=A}\sum_{B\in{\bf B}_{4}|_{Q}}\#{\bf T}[(\cup_{\tau\in{\mathcal{T}}}3\tau)^{c}](B;\mathcal{G})\gtrsim\sigma\mu.\end{split}

(
6.9
) is our first estimate.
To establish our third estimate, note that by (
6.1
),

\mathcal{I}_{\textup{br},A}({\bf B}_{4}|_{Q},{\bf T}_{\beta};\mathcal{G}_{\lambda})\leq\mathcal{I}_{\textup{br}}({\bf B}_{4}|_{Q},{\bf T}_{\beta};\mathcal{G}_{\lambda})=\inf_{\tau\in\Theta_{K^{-1}}}\sum_{T\in{\bf T}_{\beta}[(3\tau)^{c}]}\#({\bf B}_{4}|_{Q})(T;\mathcal{G}_{\lambda}).

Thus, for any
\tau\in\Theta_{K^{-1}}
, we have

\begin{split}\mathcal{I}_{\textup{br},A}({\bf B}_{4}|_{Q},{\bf T}_{\beta};\mathcal{G}_{\lambda})&\leq\sum_{T\in{\bf T}_{\beta}[(3\tau)^{c}]}\#({\bf B}_{4}|_{Q})(T;\mathcal{G}_{\lambda})\\
&=\sum_{T\in\bigcup_{B\in{\bf B}_{4}|_{Q}}{\bf T}_{\beta}[(3\tau)^{c}](B;\mathcal{G}_{\lambda})}\#({\bf B}_{4}|_{Q})(T;\mathcal{G}_{\lambda}),\end{split}

as
({\bf B}_{4}|_{Q})(T;\mathcal{G}_{\lambda})=\emptyset
when
T\notin\bigcup_{B\in{\bf B}_{4}|_{Q}}{\bf T}_{\beta}[(3\tau)^{c}](B;\mathcal{G}_{\lambda})
.
Choose
\tau
so that

(6.10)

\displaystyle\#\bigcup_{B\in{\bf B}_{4}|_{Q}}{\bf T}_{\beta}[(3\tau)^{c}](B;\mathcal{G}_{\lambda})=\#_{\textup{br}}\bigcup_{B\in{\bf B}_{4}|_{Q}}{\bf T}_{\beta}(B;\mathcal{G}_{\lambda})\lesssim m.

Note that
\#({\bf B}_{4}|_{Q})(T;\mathcal{G}_{\lambda})\leq\#\{B\in{\bf B}_{4}|_{Q}:B\cap T\neq\emptyset\}
.
Since
{\bf B}_{4}|_{Q}
is
\rho
-regular,

\#({\bf B}_{4}|_{Q})(T;\mathcal{G}_{\lambda})\lesssim\rho.

Therefore, we obtain our second estimate

(6.11)

\mathcal{I}_{\textup{br},A}({\bf B}_{4}|_{Q},{\bf T}_{\beta};\mathcal{G}_{\lambda})\lesssim\rho m.

We put the third estimate into a lemma.

Lemma 6.4
.

Suppose
A\geq\log K
.
Then we have

(6.12)

\sigma\lesssim K^{O(1)}R^{\frac{n-2}{2}}\bar{m}^{3}\mu^{-3}.

Proof.

Since
\#_{\textup{br}}\bigcup_{B\in{\bf B}_{4}|_{Q}}{\bf T}(B;\mathcal{G}_{\lambda})\sim\bar{m}
, there exists a cap
\tau_{\ast}\in\Theta_{K^{-1}}
such that, by denoting
\Theta_{K^{-1}}^{\ast}=\{\tau\in\Theta_{K^{-1}}:\tau\cap 3\tau_{\ast}=\varnothing\}
, we have

(6.13)

\#\bigcup_{B\in{\bf B}_{4}|_{Q}}{\bf T}[\cup_{\Theta_{K^{-1}}^{\ast}}](B;\mathcal{G})\lesssim\bar{m}.

Since
\#_{\textup{br},A}{\bf T}(B;\mathcal{G})\geq\mu
for all
B\in{\bf B}_{1}
, there exists
{\mathcal{T}}(B)\subset\Theta_{K^{-1}}^{\ast}
with
\#{\mathcal{T}}(B)\geq\log K-3n
so that for all
\tau\in{\mathcal{T}}(B)
,

\#{\bf T}[\tau](B;\mathcal{G})\gtrsim K^{-(n-1)}\mu.

As
\log K-3n
is bigger than any constant depending only on
\phi
and
n
for large enough
K
, by Lemma
5.7
, there are three caps
\tau_{1},\tau_{2},\tau_{3}\in{\mathcal{T}}(B)
such that for all
\xi_{j}\in\tau_{j}
,

|v_{\xi_{1}}\wedge v_{\xi_{2}}\wedge v_{\xi_{3}}|\gtrsim K^{-O(1)}.

Since
{\bf B}_{4}\subset{\bf B}_{1}
and since
\#{\bf B}_{4}|_{Q}\sim\sigma
for all
Q\in{\mathcal{Q}}
, applying Lemma
5.9
with
\mathbb{T}(Q)=\bigcup_{B\in{\bf B}_{4}|_{Q}}{\bf T}[\cup_{\Theta_{K^{-1}}^{\ast}}](B;\mathcal{G})
and using (
6.13
), we prove (
6.12
).
∎

At this point, the algorithm stops.

Before concluding the algorithm as a theorem, we present an incidence result based on the parameters introduced in the algorithm.

Lemma 6.5
.

Suppose
\beta>100
. Then

(6.14)

\#{\bf B}_{1}\gtrsim(KK_{\circ})^{-O(1)}lm\lambda.

Proof.

By the definition of
l
in (

), there exists

\omega_{1}\in\Omega

Denote
{\mathcal{Q}}^{\prime}=\{Q\in{\mathcal{Q}}:Q\cap T\neq\emptyset,Q\subset\omega_{1}\}
.
Let
\mathcal{T}_{0}
be the

-neighborhood of the

-plank

.
Recall that
\#_{\textup{br}}\bigcup_{B\in{\bf B}_{4}|_{Q}}{\bf T}_{\beta}(B;\mathcal{G}_{\lambda})\sim m

.
Let

be the cap containing the direction of

Now we define

\lesssim K^{O(1)}
different

Remark 6.7
.

The incidence estimates in Proposition

are only useful if we can establish the two bounds

Let

Theorem A.1

(
\ell^{p}
-decoupling)
.

Let
2\leq p\leq\frac{2(n+1)}{n-1}
. For any
\varepsilon>0
, one has

\|\mathcal{F}^{\lambda}f\|_{L^{p}(B_{R})}\lesssim_{\varepsilon,M,\phi,a}R^{\frac{n-1}{2}(\frac{1}{2}-\frac{1}{p})+\varepsilon}(\sum_{\theta\in\Theta_{R^{-1/2}}}\|\mathcal{F}^{\lambda}f_{\theta}\|_{L^{p}(w_{B_{R}})}^{p})^{1/p}+R^{-M}\|f\|_{2}.

Here
w_{B_{R}}
is a weight that is
\sim 1
on
B_{R}
and decreases rapidly outside
B_{R}
.

A slight modification of the proof in
[
1
]
also gives the
\ell^{2}
-decoupling inequality.

Theorem A.2

(
\ell^{2}
-decoupling)
.

Let
2\leq p\leq\frac{2(n+1)}{n-1}
. For any
\varepsilon>0
, one has

\|\mathcal{F}^{\lambda}f\|_{L^{p}(B_{R})}\lesssim_{\varepsilon,M,\phi,a}R^{\varepsilon}(\sum_{\theta\in\Theta_{R^{-1/2}}}\|\mathcal{F}^{\lambda}f_{\theta}\|_{L^{2}(w_{B_{R}})}^{p})^{1/p}+R^{-M}\|f\|_{2}.

In the rest of the appendix, we prove the following refined decoupling inequality, which also implies Theorem
A.2

The implication can be found below Theorem 4.2 in
[
13
]
.
The refined decoupling inequality for the cone was proved in
[
6
, Appendix A]
, when

is of constant coefficient.
Similarly, the proof for the variable coefficient case is based on the
\ell^{2}
-decoupling theorem and an induction on scales argument.

Lemma A.4
.

Given
\tau\in\Theta_{K^{-1}}
and
\Box\in\mathbb{B}_{\tau}
,

{\bf{1}}_{Q}^{*}\sum_{T\in\mathbb{W}_{\Box}}\eta_{Y_{\Box}}\mathcal{F}^{\lambda}f_{T},

as a function in
\mathbb{R}^{n+1}
, has Fourier transform essentially supported in
2P(z_{0};\tau)
.

Proof.

Note that, up to a rapidly decreasing term,

{\bf{1}}_{Q}^{*}\sum_{T\in\mathbb{W}_{\Box}}\eta_{Y_{\Box}}\mathcal{F}^{\lambda}f_{T}={\bf{1}}_{Q}^{*}\sum_{Q_{\Box}\subset Y_{\Box},Q_{\Box}\cap 2Q\neq\emptyset}\eta_{Q_{\Box}}\sum_{T\in\mathbb{W}_{\Box}}\mathcal{F}^{\lambda}f_{T}.

Let
P_{0}(z_{0};\tau)
be a translated copy of
P(z_{0};\tau)
that is centered at the origin.
Since
({\bf{1}}^{*}_{Q}\eta_{Q_{\Box}}\mathcal{F}^{\lambda}f_{T})^{\wedge}=\widehat{\eta}_{Q_{\Box}}*({\bf{1}}^{*}_{Q}\mathcal{F}^{\lambda}f_{T})^{\wedge}
, it suffices to show that

(1)

({\bf{1}}_{Q}^{*}\mathcal{F}^{\lambda}f_{T})^{\wedge}
is essentially supported in
P(z_{0};\tau)
.

(2)

\widehat{\eta}_{Q_{\Box}}
is essentially supported in
P_{0}(z_{0};\tau)
.

We write

(A.8)

({\bf{1}}_{Q}^{*}\mathcal{F}^{\lambda}f_{T})^{\wedge}(\eta)=\int{\bf{1}}_{Q}^{*}e^{i(\phi^{\lambda}(z;\xi)-\langle z,\eta\rangle)}\mathrm{d}z\int a^{\lambda}(z;\xi)\widehat{f}_{T}\mathrm{d}\xi.

Note that
\widehat{f}_{T}
is supported in
2\theta\subset 2\tau
.
Thus, to show item (1), we just need to show that for all
\xi\in 2\tau
,

(A.9)

\int{\bf{1}}_{Q}^{*}e^{i(\phi^{\lambda}(z;\xi)-\langle z,\eta\rangle)}\mathrm{d}z

decays rapidly outside
P(z_{0};\tau)
.
By stationary phase method, this is true if

|\partial_{z}\phi^{\lambda}(z;\xi)-\eta|\geq K^{-2+\delta}\ \textup{~for~all~}z\in 2Q.

Note that
|\partial_{z}\phi^{\lambda}(z;\xi)-\partial_{z}\phi^{\lambda}(z_{0};\xi)|\lesssim|z-z_{0}|\lambda^{-1}\leq K^{-3}
, as
K
is a small power of
\lambda
.
Hence, (
A.9
) is negligible if

|\partial_{z}\phi^{\lambda}(z_{0};\xi)-\eta|\geq K^{-2+\delta}.

Therefore, (
A.8
) is essentially supported in

N_{K^{-2+\delta}}(\{\partial_{z}\phi^{\lambda}(z_{0};\xi):\xi\in 2\tau\})=P(z_{0};\tau).

This proves item (1).

Regarding item (2), we study

\displaystyle\partial_{t}\gamma^{\lambda}(\bar{u}+\partial_{z}\phi^{\lambda}(\bar{z};\xi_{\tau}),\bar{t};\xi_{\tau})(t-\bar{t})+O(\lambda^{-1}(KK_{1})^{2}).
