Dynamic Spatial Treatment Effect Boundaries: A Continuous Functional Framework from Navier-Stokes Equations
Tatsuru Kikuchi

Abstract
I develop a comprehensive theoretical framework for dynamic spatial treatment effect boundaries using continuous functional definitions grounded in Navier-Stokes partial differential equations. Rather than discrete treatment effect estimators, the framework characterizes treatment intensity as a continuous function τ ⁡ ( 𝐱 , t ) \displaystyle\tau(\mathbf{x},t) over space-time, enabling rigorous analysis of propagation dynamics, boundary evolution, and cumulative exposure patterns. Building on exact self-similar solutions expressible through Kummer confluent hypergeometric and modified Bessel functions, I establish that treatment effects follow scaling laws τ ⁡ ( d , t ) = t − α ​ f ​ ( d / t β ) \displaystyle\tau(d,t)=t^{-\alpha}f(d/t^{\beta}) where exponents characterize diffusion mechanisms. The continuous functional approach yields natural definitions of spatial boundaries d ∗ ​ ( t ) \displaystyle d^{*}(t) , boundary velocities v ⁡ ( t ) = ∂ d ∗ / ∂ t \displaystyle v(t)=\partial d^{*}/\partial t , treatment effect gradients ∇ d τ \displaystyle\nabla_{d}\tau , and integrated exposure functionals ∫ 0 T τ ​ 𝑑 t \displaystyle\int_{0}^{T}\tau\,dt . Empirical validation using 42 million TROPOMI satellite observations of NO 2 pollution from U.S. coal-fired power plants demonstrates strong exponential spatial decay ( κ s = 0.004028 \displaystyle\kappa_{s}=0.004028 per km, R 2 = 0.35 \displaystyle R^{2}=0.35 ) with detectable boundaries at d ∗ = 572 \displaystyle d^{*}=572 km from major facilities. Monte Carlo simulations confirm superior performance over discrete parametric methods in boundary detection and false positive avoidance (94% correct rejection rate versus 27% for parametric methods). The framework successfully diagnoses regional heterogeneity: positive decay parameters within 100 km of coal plants validate the theory, while negative decay parameters beyond 100 km correctly signal when alternative pollution sources dominate. This sign reversal demonstrates the framework’s diagnostic capability—it identifies when underlying physical assumptions hold versus when alternative mechanisms dominate. Applications span environmental economics (pollution dispersion fields), banking (spatial credit access functions), and healthcare (hospital accessibility). The continuous functional perspective unifies spatial econometrics with mathematical physics, connecting to recent advances in spatial correlation robust inference Müller and Watson 2022 and addressing spurious spatial regression concerns Müller and Watson 2024 . Introduction

Treatment effects in economics are conventionally represented as scalar parameters or discrete functions—average treatment effects (ATE), treatment on the treated (ATT), local average treatment effects (LATE). While appropriate for many settings, these discrete representations obscure the continuous nature of treatment propagation through space and time. When bank branches open, pollution sources emit, or infrastructure is built, economic impacts do not manifest as step functions at arbitrary cutoffs. Instead, treatment intensity varies smoothly across geographic space, evolves continuously over time, and exhibits rich mathematical structure arising from underlying diffusion processes.

This paper develops a comprehensive framework for
dynamic spatial treatment effects as continuous functionals
defined over space-time domains. Rather than estimating point parameters, the framework characterizes treatment intensity as continuous functions
\displaystyle\tau:\mathbb{R}^{d}\times\mathbb{R}_{+}\to\mathbb{R}
satisfying partial differential equations (PDEs) that govern propagation dynamics. This functional perspective enables rigorous analysis of objects beyond the reach of discrete estimators: boundary evolution rates, spatial gradients, cumulative exposure integrals, and sensitivity functionals.

The motivation for continuous functional definitions comes from recognizing that treatment propagation follows physical principles. Just as heat diffuses continuously from sources according to the heat equation, economic treatments—bank services, pollution exposure, infrastructure accessibility—spread through space following diffusion-advection dynamics. The mathematical structure is captured by the Navier-Stokes system:

\frac{\partial u}{\partial t}+(\mathbf{v}\cdot\nabla)u=\nu\nabla^{2}u+S(\mathbf{x},t)

(1)

where
\displaystyle u(\mathbf{x},t)
represents treatment intensity at location
\displaystyle\mathbf{x}
and time
\displaystyle t
,
\displaystyle\mathbf{v}
is the velocity field,
\displaystyle\nu
is the diffusion coefficient, and
\displaystyle S(\mathbf{x},t)
represents source emissions.

2.1
Spatial Econometrics and Treatment Effect Spillovers

The spatial econometrics literature provides essential foundations.

develops GMM procedures allowing flexible spatial correlation structures.

Recent work formalizes these concerns within causal inference frameworks.
Butts and Gardner 2023

However, their framework requires researchers to specify spatial weights matrices without principled guidance. Our framework addresses this by deriving spatial correlation structures from physical principles.

2.2
Treatment Effect Heterogeneity and Scope Conditions

The treatment effects literature emphasizes that effects vary across units, contexts, and time periods.
Heckman et al. 1997
,
Imbens and Rubin 2015
, and
Athey and Imbens 2017
document extensive heterogeneity and develop characterization methods.
Angrist and Kolesár 2022
discusses how effect heterogeneity complicates identification and generalization.

A related concern is functional form misspecification. Standard causal inference methods are often robust to parametric assumptions about treatment effect form. However, when treatment propagates spatially, the functional form of spatial decay directly affects boundary estimation. Our framework sidesteps these concerns by deriving functional forms from physics rather than assuming them.

2.3
First Principles Approach and Scope Conditions

Our framework seeks middle ground between structural models (transparent mechanisms, restrictive assumptions) and reduced-form methods (flexible, less interpretable). The key innovation is making scope conditions explicit and testable. Rather than invoking exponential decay as maintained assumption, we specify conditions: decay rates depend on Péclet numbers (diffusion versus advection), Reynolds numbers (laminar versus turbulent), and Damköhler numbers (reaction versus transport rates). This provides falsifiable predictions guiding empirical analysis.

Deaton 2010
emphasizes the importance of clear scope conditions in applied work. Our framework operationalizes this guidance through dimensionless numbers indicating applicability.

2.4

Müller and Watson 2022
develop comprehensive framework for spatial correlation robust inference, showing that standard spatial econometrics can fail when correlation structures are misspecified.
Müller and Watson 2024
demonstrate that spatial data can exhibit spurious regression phenomena analogous to time series unit roots.

Our continuous functional framework complements their work in three ways. First, while
Müller and Watson 2022
address nuisance correlation in residuals, I study treatment-induced spatial patterns from point sources. Second, our exponential decay derived from Navier-Stokes corresponds to a specific parametric form for spatial kernels in Müller-Watson’s representation:
\displaystyle K(d)\propto\exp(-\kappa_{s}d)
. When underlying diffusion assumptions hold, this form is theoretically justified. Third, our regional heterogeneity analysis addresses
Müller and Watson 2024
’s spurious regression concerns: the sign reversal at 100 km is inconsistent with spurious trends but consistent with heterogeneous treatment effects.

3
Continuous Functional Framework

3.1
Fundamental Object: Treatment Intensity Field

The foundation of our framework rests on defining treatment effects as continuous functions over space-time domains rather than discrete parameters. This shift from scalars to functionals enables rigorous mathematical analysis through partial differential equation theory while maintaining direct economic interpretation.

Definition 3.1

(Treatment Intensity Functional)
.

The
treatment intensity field
is a continuous function
\displaystyle\tau:\mathcal{D}\times\mathbb{R}_{+}\to\mathbb{R}
where
\displaystyle\mathcal{D}\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{d}
is the spatial domain and
\displaystyle\mathbb{R}_{+}
is the positive time axis, satisfying:

\tau(\mathbf{x},t)=\lim_{r\to 0}\frac{1}{|\mathcal{B}_{r}(\mathbf{x})|}\int_{\mathcal{B}_{r}(\mathbf{x})}u(\mathbf{y},t)\,d\mathbf{y}

(2)

where
\displaystyle u(\mathbf{y},t)
is the microscopic treatment intensity at point
\displaystyle\mathbf{y}
and time
\displaystyle t
, and
\displaystyle\mathcal{B}_{r}(\mathbf{x})
is a ball of radius
\displaystyle r
centered at
\displaystyle\mathbf{x}
with volume
\displaystyle|\mathcal{B}_{r}(\mathbf{x})|
.

Interpretation:
The treatment intensity field
\displaystyle\tau(\mathbf{x},t)
represents the continuum limit of treatment concentration, analogous to density fields in fluid mechanics or concentration fields in chemical diffusion. At each spatial location
\displaystyle\mathbf{x}
and time
\displaystyle t
,
\displaystyle\tau(\mathbf{x},t)
measures the local intensity of treatment exposure. This could represent:

•

Pollution concentration (
\displaystyle\mu
g/m
3
) at location
\displaystyle\mathbf{x}
at time
\displaystyle t

•

Probability of loan approval at distance
\displaystyle|\mathbf{x}|
from bank branch

•

Healthcare service accessibility (visits per capita) near hospital

•

Information diffusion intensity (adoption rate) from technology source

The limit definition ensures that
\displaystyle\tau
is a smooth field averaging out microscopic fluctuations, enabling application of PDE methods.

3.1.0.1
Regularity Conditions

For the treatment intensity functional to admit well-defined mathematical operations (integration, differentiation, boundary identification), we impose regularity conditions:

Assumption 3.1

(Regularity of Treatment Field)
.

The treatment intensity field
\displaystyle\tau
satisfies:

1.

Continuity:

\displaystyle\tau\in C^{k,\ell}(\mathcal{D}\times\mathbb{R}_{+})
, meaning
\displaystyle\tau
is
\displaystyle k
-times continuously differentiable in space and
\displaystyle\ell
-times continuously differentiable in time, where
\displaystyle k,\ell\geq 2
.

2.

Boundedness:
There exists
\displaystyle M<\infty
such that:

\sup_{(\mathbf{x},t)\in\mathcal{D}\times[0,T]}|\tau(\mathbf{x},t)|\leq M

(3)

for any finite time horizon
\displaystyle T<\infty
.

3.

Integrability:
For each time
\displaystyle t
, the spatial integral exists:

\int_{\mathcal{D}}|\tau(\mathbf{x},t)|\,d\mathbf{x}<\infty

(4)

4.

Decay at infinity:
For unbounded domains
\displaystyle\mathcal{D}=\mathbb{R}^{d}
:

\lim_{|\mathbf{x}|\to\infty}\tau(\mathbf{x},t)=0\quad\text{uniformly in }t\in[0,T]

(5)

Justification:
These conditions are standard in PDE theory and are satisfied by physical diffusion processes. Economically, they ensure:

•

Treatment effects vary smoothly across space (no discontinuous jumps)

•

Effects remain finite (no singularities except possibly at sources)

•

Total treatment exposure is finite (resources are bounded)

•

Effects vanish at large distances (local rather than global impacts)

3.2
Governing Equation and Self-Similar Solutions

3.2.0.1
Propagation Dynamics

The treatment intensity field evolves according to the advection-diffusion partial differential equation:

\frac{\partial\tau}{\partial t}+\mathbf{v}(\mathbf{x},t)\cdot\nabla\tau=\nu\nabla^{2}\tau+S(\mathbf{x},t)

(9)

with initial condition
\displaystyle\tau(\mathbf{x},0)=\tau_{0}(\mathbf{x})
and appropriate boundary conditions (typically
\displaystyle\tau\to 0
as
\displaystyle|\mathbf{x}|\to\infty
for unbounded domains).

Terms:

•

\displaystyle\frac{\partial\tau}{\partial t}
: Temporal evolution of treatment intensity

•

\displaystyle\mathbf{v}(\mathbf{x},t)\cdot\nabla\tau
: Advective transport (e.g., wind carrying pollution, migration flows)

•

\displaystyle\nu\nabla^{2}\tau
: Diffusive spreading with diffusion coefficient
\displaystyle\nu>0

•

\displaystyle S(\mathbf{x},t)
: Source/sink term (treatment application, decay)

This PDE is the fundamental equation of transport phenomena, appearing in:

•

Heat conduction (Fourier’s law)

•

Mass diffusion (Fick’s law)

•

Momentum transport (Navier-Stokes equations)

•

Economic information diffusion

3.2.0.2
Self-Similar Solutions

A central result of PDE theory is that certain initial-boundary value problems admit
self-similar
solutions—solutions whose spatial structure scales with time according to power laws.

Definition 3.2

(Self-Similar Solution)
.

A function
\displaystyle\tau(\mathbf{x},t)
is
self-similar
if it can be written in the form:

\tau(\mathbf{x},t)=t^{-\alpha}f\left(\frac{\mathbf{x}}{t^{\beta}}\right)

(10)

where
\displaystyle\alpha,\beta>0
are scaling exponents and
\displaystyle f:\mathbb{R}^{d}\to\mathbb{R}
is the
profile function
.

Equivalently,
introducing the similarity variable
\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\xi}=\mathbf{x}/t^{\beta}
:

\tau(\mathbf{x},t)=t^{-\alpha}f(\boldsymbol{\xi})

(11)

Physical interpretation:
Self-similarity means that spatial profiles at different times have the same shape up to rescaling:

\frac{\tau(\mathbf{x},t_{2})}{\tau(\mathbf{x},t_{1})}=\left(\frac{t_{2}}{t_{1}}\right)^{-\alpha}\quad\text{if }\frac{\mathbf{x}}{t_{2}^{\beta}}=\frac{\mathbf{x}}{t_{1}^{\beta}}

(12)

The treatment effect “spreads” while maintaining its shape, with amplitude decreasing as
\displaystyle t^{-\alpha}
and spatial extent growing as
\displaystyle t^{\beta}
.

Theorem 3.1

(Self-Similar Solution Existence)
.

Consider the advection-diffusion equation with instantaneous point source
\displaystyle S(\mathbf{x},t)=Q\delta(\mathbf{x})\delta(t)
, zero drift (
\displaystyle\mathbf{v}=\mathbf{0}
), constant diffusion coefficient
\displaystyle\nu
, and unbounded domain (
\displaystyle\mathcal{D}=\mathbb{R}^{d}
). Then there exists a unique self-similar solution of the form:

\tau(r,t)=t^{-d/2}f\left(\frac{r}{\sqrt{t}}\right),\quad r=|\mathbf{x}|

(13)

where the profile function
\displaystyle f:\mathbb{R}_{+}\to\mathbb{R}_{+}
satisfies the ordinary differential equation:

\nu f^{\prime\prime}+\nu\frac{d-1}{\xi}f^{\prime}+\frac{1}{2}\xi f^{\prime}+\frac{d}{2}f=0,\quad\xi=\frac{r}{\sqrt{t}}

(14)

with normalization condition:

\omega_{d}\int_{0}^{\infty}f(\xi)\xi^{d-1}\,d\xi=Q

(15)

where
\displaystyle\omega_{d}=2\pi^{d/2}/\Gamma(d/2)
is the surface area of the unit sphere in
\displaystyle d
dimensions.

Proof sketch:

Step 1: Dimensional analysis.
The only parameters with dimensions are
\displaystyle Q
(total intensity),
\displaystyle\nu
(diffusion coefficient),
\displaystyle t
(time), and
\displaystyle r
(distance). Forming dimensionless combinations:

\tau\sim\frac{Q}{\nu^{d/2}t^{d/2}},\quad\xi\sim\frac{r}{\sqrt{\nu t}}

(16)

suggests the scaling
\displaystyle\alpha=d/2
,
\displaystyle\beta=1/2
.

Step 2: Substitution.
Assume
\displaystyle\tau(r,t)=t^{-d/2}f(r/\sqrt{t})
. Let
\displaystyle\xi=r/\sqrt{t}
. Then:

\displaystyle\displaystyle\frac{\partial\tau}{\partial t}

\displaystyle\displaystyle=-\frac{d}{2}t^{-d/2-1}f(\xi)+t^{-d/2}f^{\prime}(\xi)\cdot\left(-\frac{\xi}{2t}\right)

\displaystyle\displaystyle=t^{-d/2-1}\left[-\frac{d}{2}f-\frac{\xi}{2}f^{\prime}\right]

\displaystyle\displaystyle\nabla^{2}\tau

\displaystyle\displaystyle=t^{-d/2}\cdot t^{-1}\left[f^{\prime\prime}+\frac{d-1}{\xi}f^{\prime}\right]

Step 3: Equating powers.
Substitute into
\displaystyle\partial\tau/\partial t=\nu\nabla^{2}\tau
:

-\frac{d}{2}f-\frac{\xi}{2}f^{\prime}=\nu\left[f^{\prime\prime}+\frac{d-1}{\xi}f^{\prime}\right]

(17)

This is the ODE stated in the theorem.

Step 4: Solving the ODE.
This is a second-order linear ODE. Solutions are found via standard techniques (variation of parameters, series solutions, or integral representations). For
\displaystyle d=3
, the solution is the Gaussian (see Corollary
3.1
).

Step 5: Normalization.
The constant
\displaystyle Q
is determined by conservation:

\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\tau(\mathbf{x},t)\,d\mathbf{x}=Q

(18)

\displaystyle\square

Corollary 3.1

(Gaussian Profile in Three Dimensions)
.

For
\displaystyle d=3
spatial dimensions, the profile function is the Gaussian:

f(\xi)=\frac{Q}{(4\pi\nu)^{3/2}}\exp\left(-\frac{\xi^{2}}{4\nu}\right)

(19)

yielding the complete solution:

\tau(r,t)=\frac{Q}{(4\pi\nu t)^{3/2}}\exp\left(-\frac{r^{2}}{4\nu t}\right)

(20)

Proof:
Direct verification by substitution into the ODE, or via Fourier transform methods.
\displaystyle\square

This is the
fundamental solution
(Green’s function) for the 3D heat equation, known since Fourier (1822).

3.3
Hierarchy of Continuous Functionals

Building on the fundamental treatment intensity field
\displaystyle\tau(\mathbf{x},t)
, we now define a hierarchy of derived functionals that capture different aspects of treatment propagation. Each functional admits rigorous mathematical definition, enables closed-form expressions for self-similar solutions, and facilitates hypothesis testing through functional data analysis.

3.3.1
Spatial Boundary Functional

Definition 3.3

(Dynamic Spatial Boundary)
.

The
spatial boundary functional

\displaystyle d^{*}:\mathbb{R}_{+}\to\mathbb{R}_{+}
is defined implicitly by the threshold condition:

\tau(d^{*}(t),t)=\tau_{\min}

(21)

where
\displaystyle\tau_{\min}>0
is a threshold intensity below which effects are economically negligible.

Alternative characterization (relative threshold):

d^{*}(t)=\inf\left\{r>0:\tau(r,t)\leq(1-\varepsilon)\tau(0,t)\right\}

(22)

where
\displaystyle\varepsilon\in(0,1)
is a decay parameter (e.g.,
\displaystyle\varepsilon=0.1
for 10% decay from source).

Economic interpretation:
The boundary
\displaystyle d^{*}(t)
demarcates the spatial extent of economically meaningful treatment effects. Beyond
\displaystyle d^{*}(t)
, treatment intensity falls below policy-relevant thresholds. This generalizes the concept of “treatment group” from discrete settings to continuous space.

Proposition 3.1

(Boundary Scaling for Self-Similar Solutions)
.

For self-similar
\displaystyle\tau(r,t)=t^{-\alpha}f(r/t^{\beta})
, the boundary functional takes the power-law form:

d^{*}(t)=\xi^{*}t^{\beta}

(23)

where
\displaystyle\xi^{*}>0
solves the profile equation:

f(\xi^{*})=(1-\varepsilon)f(0)

(24)

Proof:
From the threshold condition
\displaystyle\tau(d^{*},t)=(1-\varepsilon)\tau(0,t)
:

\displaystyle\displaystyle t^{-\alpha}f(d^{*}/t^{\beta})

\displaystyle\displaystyle=(1-\varepsilon)t^{-\alpha}f(0)

\displaystyle\displaystyle f(d^{*}/t^{\beta})

\displaystyle\displaystyle=(1-\varepsilon)f(0)

Setting
\displaystyle\xi^{*}=d^{*}/t^{\beta}
gives
\displaystyle f(\xi^{*})=(1-\varepsilon)f(0)
, which is independent of
\displaystyle t
. Thus
\displaystyle d^{*}=\xi^{*}t^{\beta}
.
\displaystyle\square

Corollary 3.2

(Diffusive Boundary Growth)
.

For diffusion (
\displaystyle\beta=1/2
):

d^{*}(t)=\xi^{*}\sqrt{t}

(25)

Boundaries expand as
\displaystyle\sqrt{t}
, the hallmark of diffusive processes.

Example 3.2

(Gaussian Boundary)
.

For three-dimensional Gaussian diffusion with
\displaystyle\varepsilon=0.1
(10% decay threshold):

\exp\left(-\frac{(\xi^{*})^{2}}{4\nu}\right)=0.9\quad\Rightarrow\quad\xi^{*}=2\sqrt{\nu\ln(10/9)}

(26)

Thus:

d^{*}(t)=2\sqrt{\nu t\ln(10/9)}\approx 0.645\sqrt{\nu t}

(27)

For
\displaystyle\nu=1
km
2
/year, at
\displaystyle t=4
years:
\displaystyle d^{*}(4)\approx 1.29
km.

\displaystyle\beta=1/2

\displaystyle v(t)=\frac{\xi^{*}}{2\sqrt{t}}

3.3.3
Spatial Gradient Field

Definition 3.5

(Treatment Effect Gradient)
.

The
spatial gradient field
is the vector-valued functional:

\mathbf{G}(\mathbf{x},t)=\nabla_{\mathbf{x}}\tau(\mathbf{x},t)=\left(\frac{\partial\tau}{\partial x_{1}},\ldots,\frac{\partial\tau}{\partial x_{d}}\right)

(31)

Physical interpretation:
The gradient
\displaystyle\mathbf{G}
points in the direction of steepest treatment intensity increase, with magnitude equal to the rate of spatial change.

Economic interpretation:
The gradient magnitude
\displaystyle|\mathbf{G}|
quantifies spatial decay rates. Large
\displaystyle|\mathbf{G}|
indicates sharp falloff (localized effects); small
\displaystyle|\mathbf{G}|
indicates gradual decay (dispersed effects).

Proposition 3.3

(Radial Gradient for Spherical Symmetry)
.

For radially symmetric
\displaystyle\tau(r,t)
where
\displaystyle r=|\mathbf{x}|
:

\mathbf{G}(\mathbf{x},t)=\frac{d\tau}{dr}(r,t)\cdot\frac{\mathbf{x}}{r}

(32)

Proof:
By chain rule:

\nabla\tau=\frac{\partial\tau}{\partial r}\nabla r=\frac{d\tau}{dr}\cdot\frac{\mathbf{x}}{r}

(33)

\displaystyle\square

Example 3.3

(Gaussian Gradient)
.

For Gaussian
\displaystyle\tau(r,t)=A(t)\exp(-r^{2}/4\nu t)
:

\frac{d\tau}{dr}=-\frac{r}{2\nu t}\tau(r,t)

(34)

Thus:

\mathbf{G}(\mathbf{x},t)=-\frac{\tau(r,t)}{2\nu t}\mathbf{x}

(35)

Properties:

•

\displaystyle\mathbf{G}
points radially inward (toward higher intensity at source)

•

Magnitude
\displaystyle|\mathbf{G}|=\frac{r}{2\nu t}\tau(r,t)
increases with distance, decreases with time

•

At boundary
\displaystyle r=d^{*}
:
\displaystyle|\mathbf{G}|=\frac{d^{*}}{2\nu t}\tau_{\min}

Cumulative Exposure Functional

Definition 3.6

(Integrated Exposure)
.

The
cumulative exposure functional

\displaystyle\Phi:\mathcal{D}\to\mathbb{R}
is defined by the temporal integral:

\Phi(\mathbf{x})=\int_{0}^{T}\tau(\mathbf{x},t)\,dt

(36)

where
\displaystyle T
is the time horizon (possibly
\displaystyle T=\infty
).

Interpretation:

\displaystyle\Phi(\mathbf{x})
represents the total “dose” or accumulated exposure received at location
\displaystyle\mathbf{x}

\displaystyle[0,T]

Proposition 3.4

(Exposure Scaling for Self-Similar Solutions)
.

For
\displaystyle\tau(r,t)=t^{-\alpha}f(r/t^{\beta})
and
\displaystyle T=\infty
:

\Phi(r)=\int_{0}^{\infty}t^{-\alpha}f\left(\frac{r}{t^{\beta}}\right)dt=\frac{r^{(1-\alpha/\beta)}}{\beta}\int_{0}^{\infty}\xi^{-1-1/\beta+\alpha/\beta}f(\xi)\,d\xi

(37)

where
\displaystyle\xi=r/t^{\beta}
.

Proof:
Change variables
\displaystyle\xi=r/t^{\beta}\Rightarrow t=(r/\xi)^{1/\beta}
,
\displaystyle dt=-\frac{1}{\beta}(r/\xi)^{1/\beta}\frac{d\xi}{\xi}
:

\displaystyle\displaystyle\Phi(r)

\displaystyle\displaystyle=\int_{\infty}^{0}(r/\xi)^{-\alpha/\beta}f(\xi)\cdot\left(-\frac{1}{\beta}(r/\xi)^{1/\beta}\frac{d\xi}{\xi}\right)

\displaystyle\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\beta}\int_{0}^{\infty}r^{(1-\alpha)/\beta}\xi^{(\alpha-1)/\beta-1}f(\xi)\,d\xi

\displaystyle\displaystyle=\frac{r^{(1-\alpha)/\beta}}{\beta}\int_{0}^{\infty}\xi^{\alpha/\beta-1/\beta-1}f(\xi)\,d\xi

\displaystyle\square

Corollary 3.3

(Exposure Scaling in 3D Diffusion)
.

For
\displaystyle d=3
,
\displaystyle\alpha=3/2
,
\displaystyle\beta=1/2
:

\Phi(r)\propto r^{(1-3/2)/(1/2)}=r^{-1/(1/2)}=r^{-2}

(38)

Total exposure decays as the inverse square of distance, analogous to gravitational or electrostatic fields.

3.3.5
Spatial Moment Functionals

Definition 3.7

(Spatial Moments)
.

The
\displaystyle k
-th
spatial moment functional

\displaystyle M_{k}:\mathbb{R}_{+}\to\mathbb{R}
is:

M_{k}(t)=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}|\mathbf{x}|^{k}\tau(\mathbf{x},t)\,d\mathbf{x}

(39)

For vector-valued (tensor) moments:

\mathbf{M}^{(i_{1},\ldots,i_{k})}(t)=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}x_{i_{1}}\cdots x_{i_{k}}\tau(\mathbf{x},t)\,d\mathbf{x}

(40)

Physical interpretation:
Spatial moments characterize the
spatial distribution
of treatment intensity:

•

\displaystyle M_{0}(t)
: Total intensity (mass conservation)

•

\displaystyle M_{1}(t)
: Mean distance (centroid location)

•

\displaystyle M_{2}(t)
: Second moment (spatial variance, spreading measure)

•

\displaystyle M_{k}(t)
(
\displaystyle k>2
): Higher-order shape descriptors (skewness, kurtosis)

3.3.5.1
Conservation Laws

Theorem 3.2

(Zeroth Moment Conservation)
.

For pure diffusion
\displaystyle\partial\tau/\partial t=\nu\nabla^{2}\tau
on unbounded domain with
\displaystyle\tau\to 0
at infinity:

\frac{dM_{0}}{dt}=0\quad\Rightarrow\quad M_{0}(t)=M_{0}(0)\equiv Q

(41)

where
\displaystyle Q
is the total treatment intensity.

Proof:

\displaystyle\displaystyle\frac{dM_{0}}{dt}

\displaystyle\displaystyle=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\frac{\partial\tau}{\partial t}\,d\mathbf{x}=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\nu\nabla^{2}\tau\,d\mathbf{x}

\displaystyle\displaystyle=\nu\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\nabla\cdot(\nabla\tau)\,d\mathbf{x}=\nu\int_{\partial\mathbb{R}^{d}}\nabla\tau\cdot\mathbf{n}\,dS=0

where the last equality follows from the divergence theorem and
\displaystyle\tau\to 0
at infinity.
\displaystyle\square

3.3.5.2
Second Moment Evolution

Theorem 3.3

(Variance Growth for Diffusion)
.

For diffusive spreading with conservation
\displaystyle M_{0}=Q
:

\frac{dM_{2}}{dt}=2d\nu Q

(42)

Thus
\displaystyle M_{2}(t)=M_{2}(0)+2d\nu Qt
exhibits
linear growth
in time.

Proof:

\displaystyle\displaystyle\frac{dM_{2}}{dt}

\displaystyle\displaystyle=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}|\mathbf{x}|^{2}\frac{\partial\tau}{\partial t}\,d\mathbf{x}=\nu\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}|\mathbf{x}|^{2}\nabla^{2}\tau\,d\mathbf{x}

Using integration by parts (Green’s first identity):

\displaystyle\displaystyle\int|\mathbf{x}|^{2}\nabla^{2}\tau\,d\mathbf{x}

\displaystyle\displaystyle=\int\nabla\cdot(|\mathbf{x}|^{2}\nabla\tau)\,d\mathbf{x}-\int\nabla(|\mathbf{x}|^{2})\cdot\nabla\tau\,d\mathbf{x}

First term vanishes by divergence theorem. For second term:

\displaystyle\displaystyle\nabla(|\mathbf{x}|^{2})=2\mathbf{x}\quad

\displaystyle\displaystyle\Rightarrow\quad\int 2\mathbf{x}\cdot\nabla\tau\,d\mathbf{x}

\displaystyle\displaystyle=-\int 2\tau\,\nabla\cdot\mathbf{x}\,d\mathbf{x}=-2d\int\tau\,d\mathbf{x}=-2dQ

Therefore:

\frac{dM_{2}}{dt}=-\nu(-2dQ)=2d\nu Q

(43)

Integrating:
\displaystyle M_{2}(t)=M_{2}(0)+2d\nu Qt
.
\displaystyle\square

Corollary 3.4

(Root Mean Square Distance)
.

The
root mean square (RMS) distance
evolves as:

\sigma(t)=\sqrt{\frac{M_{2}(t)}{M_{0}}}=\sqrt{\sigma_{0}^{2}+2d\nu t}

(44)

For initially concentrated source (
\displaystyle\sigma_{0}\approx 0
):

\sigma(t)\approx\sqrt{2d\nu t}

(45)

This confirms the
\displaystyle\sqrt{t}
diffusive scaling.

3.3.5.3
Moment Scaling for Self-Similar Solutions

Proposition 3.5

(Moment Scaling Law)
.

For self-similar solutions
\displaystyle\tau(r,t)=t^{-\alpha}f(r/t^{\beta})
with
\displaystyle M_{0}=Q
:

M_{k}(t)=Q\cdot t^{k\beta}\cdot C_{k}

(46)

where
\displaystyle C_{k}=\omega_{d}\int_{0}^{\infty}\xi^{k+d-1}f(\xi)\,d\xi
is a dimensionless constant.

Proof:
In spherical coordinates:

\displaystyle\displaystyle M_{k}(t)

\displaystyle\displaystyle=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}r^{k}\cdot t^{-\alpha}f(r/t^{\beta})\,d\mathbf{x}

\displaystyle\displaystyle=\omega_{d}\int_{0}^{\infty}r^{k+d-1}t^{-\alpha}f(r/t^{\beta})\,dr

where
\displaystyle\omega_{d}
is the surface area of unit sphere. Substitute
\displaystyle r=\xi t^{\beta}
,
\displaystyle dr=t^{\beta}d\xi
:

\displaystyle\displaystyle M_{k}(t)

\displaystyle\displaystyle=\omega_{d}\int_{0}^{\infty}(\xi t^{\beta})^{k+d-1}t^{-\alpha}f(\xi)\cdot t^{\beta}\,d\xi

\displaystyle\displaystyle=\omega_{d}t^{\beta(k+d)-\alpha}\int_{0}^{\infty}\xi^{k+d-1}f(\xi)\,d\xi

Using
\displaystyle\alpha=d\beta
from self-similarity:
\displaystyle M_{k}(t)=t^{k\beta}\cdot C_{k}
.
\displaystyle\square

Example 3.4

(Moments for Gaussian Profile)
.

For
\displaystyle d=3
,
\displaystyle\tau=\frac{Q}{(4\pi\nu t)^{3/2}}\exp(-r^{2}/4\nu t)
:

\displaystyle\displaystyle M_{0}(t)

\displaystyle\displaystyle=Q\quad\text{(conserved)}

\displaystyle\displaystyle M_{2}(t)

\displaystyle\displaystyle=6\nu Qt\quad\text{(linear growth)}

\displaystyle\displaystyle M_{4}(t)

\displaystyle\displaystyle=60(\nu t)^{2}Q\quad\text{(quadratic growth)}

General pattern:
\displaystyle M_{k}(t)\propto t^{k/2}
for even
\displaystyle k
.

3.3.5.4
Empirical Testing via Moments

Strategy:
Estimate moments from data, test theoretical predictions.

Step 1:
Compute empirical moments:

\hat{M}_{k}(t_{j})=\frac{1}{n_{j}}\sum_{i:t_{i}\in[t_{j}-\Delta,t_{j}+\Delta]}r_{i}^{k}Y_{i}

(47)

where
\displaystyle Y_{i}
is outcome at location
\displaystyle i
,
\displaystyle r_{i}
is distance from source.

Step 2:
Test scaling law
\displaystyle M_{k}(t)=A_{k}t^{k\beta}
:

\log\hat{M}_{k}(t)=\log A_{k}+k\beta\log t+\varepsilon_{t}

(48)

Estimate
\displaystyle\beta
via OLS. For diffusion:
\displaystyle\beta=1/2
.

Step 3:
Test second moment evolution:

\hat{M}_{2}(t)=\hat{M}_{2}(0)+2d\hat{\nu}Qt+\varepsilon_{t}

(49)

Estimate
\displaystyle\nu
from slope. Compare to direct estimation from spatial decay.

Remark 3.1

(Robustness to Measurement Error)
.

Moment tests are robust to:

•

Sparse sampling (uses aggregate statistics)

•

Heteroskedasticity (weighted least squares)

•

Missing observations (moments computed from available data)

But sensitive to:

•

Boundary truncation (underestimates
\displaystyle M_{k}
for large
\displaystyle k
)

•

Outliers (higher moments magnify extreme values)

Recommendation: Use
\displaystyle M_{2}
(robust), check
\displaystyle M_{4}
(sensitivity test).

3.3.6
Energy Functional

Definition 3.8

(Energy Functional)
.

The
energy functional

\displaystyle E:\mathbb{R}_{+}\to\mathbb{R}_{+}
is:

E(t)=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\tau^{2}(\mathbf{x},t)\,d\mathbf{x}

(50)

Physical interpretation:
The
\displaystyle L^{2}
norm of the intensity field measures concentration versus dispersal. High energy indicates concentrated treatment; low energy indicates dispersed treatment.

Theorem 3.4

(Energy Dissipation)
.

For pure diffusion
\displaystyle\partial\tau/\partial t=\nu\nabla^{2}\tau
:

\frac{dE}{dt}=-2\nu\int|\nabla\tau|^{2}\,d\mathbf{x}\leq 0

(51)

Energy dissipates monotonically.

Proof:

\displaystyle\displaystyle\frac{dE}{dt}

\displaystyle\displaystyle=2\int\tau\frac{\partial\tau}{\partial t}\,d\mathbf{x}=2\nu\int\tau\nabla^{2}\tau\,d\mathbf{x}

\displaystyle\displaystyle=2\nu\int\nabla\cdot(\tau\nabla\tau)\,d\mathbf{x}-2\nu\int(\nabla\tau)\cdot(\nabla\tau)\,d\mathbf{x}

First term vanishes by divergence theorem. Second term gives:

\frac{dE}{dt}=-2\nu\int|\nabla\tau|^{2}\,d\mathbf{x}<0

(52)

\displaystyle\square

Interpretation:
Diffusion smooths gradients, dissipating energy. Peaked distributions flatten over time. This is the second law of thermodynamics applied to treatment diffusion.

3.4
Special Function Representations: From Gaussian to Kummer

The self-similar solutions of Navier-Stokes equations admit representations through a hierarchy of special functions, ordered by increasing generality. This hierarchy reflects increasing complexity of boundary conditions, geometry, and nonlinearity.

3.4.1
The Function Hierarchy

\text{Gaussian}\subset\text{Bessel}\subset\text{Kummer}\subset\text{Generalized Hypergeometric}

(53)

Each level subsumes the previous as a special case:

•

Gaussian:
Elementary exponential function

•

Bessel:
Infinite series, cylindrical symmetry

•

Kummer:
Confluent hypergeometric, general 3D

•

Hypergeometric:
Most general, rarely needed

3.4.1.1
Level 1: Gaussian Functions (Linear, Isotropic, 3D)

For pure diffusion from point source in three dimensions:

\tau(r,t)=\frac{Q}{(4\pi\nu t)^{3/2}}\exp\left(-\frac{r^{2}}{4\nu t}\right)

(54)

Characteristics:

•

Exponential decay in space:
\displaystyle\tau\sim\exp(-r^{2})

•

Power law decay in time:
\displaystyle\tau\sim t^{-3/2}

•

Self-similar form:
\displaystyle\tau=t^{-3/2}f(r/\sqrt{t})
where
\displaystyle f(\xi)=\frac{Q}{(4\pi\nu)^{3/2}}\exp(-\xi^{2}/(4\nu))

•

Closed-form boundary:
\displaystyle d^{*}=2\sqrt{\nu t\ln(1/(1-\varepsilon))}

Limitations:

•

Requires isotropy (no preferred direction)

•

Requires linearity (no advection, no nonlinear interactions)

•

Three-dimensional geometry only

Economic applications:

•

Pollution dispersion in atmosphere (isotropic conditions)

•

Technology diffusion in homogeneous populations

•

Information spreading in symmetric networks

3.4.1.2
Level 2: Modified Bessel Functions (Cylindrical Symmetry, 2D)

For diffusion from line source (axial symmetry):

\tau(r,t)=\frac{A}{t}K_{0}\left(\frac{r}{2\sqrt{\nu t}}\right)

(55)

where
\displaystyle K_{0}
is the modified Bessel function of the second kind, order zero.

Connection to previous work:
This is the solution derived in
Kikuchi 2024a
for cylindrical diffusion problems involving linear infrastructure (roads, pipelines, borders).

Characteristics:

•

Logarithmic singularity at source:
\displaystyle K_{0}(z)\sim-\ln(z)
as
\displaystyle z\to 0

•

Exponential decay far field:
\displaystyle K_{0}(z)\sim\sqrt{\pi/(2z)}e^{-z}
as
\displaystyle z\to\infty

•

Temporal decay:
\displaystyle\tau\sim t^{-1}
(one power higher than 3D due to dimension reduction)

•

Self-similar:
\displaystyle\tau=t^{-1}f(r/\sqrt{t})

Theorem 3.5

(Boundary Evolution for Bessel Profile)
.

For
\displaystyle\tau(r,t)=(A/t)K_{0}(r/(2\sqrt{\nu t}))
, the boundary
\displaystyle d^{*}(t)
satisfies:

K_{0}\left(\frac{d^{*}(t)}{2\sqrt{\nu t}}\right)=(1-\varepsilon)K_{0}\left(\frac{a}{2\sqrt{\nu t}}\right)

(56)

where
\displaystyle a
is the finite source radius (regularization parameter needed because
\displaystyle K_{0}(0)=\infty
). This implicitly defines:

d^{*}(t)=\xi^{*}\sqrt{t}

(57)

where
\displaystyle\xi^{*}
solves the transcendental equation above.

Asymptotic approximation:
For
\displaystyle a\ll\sqrt{\nu t}
(large time limit):

\xi^{*}\approx 2\sqrt{\nu}\ln\left(\frac{1}{1-\varepsilon}\right)

(58)

yielding:

d^{*}(t)\approx 2\sqrt{\nu t}\ln\left(\frac{1}{1-\varepsilon}\right)

(59)

Note the logarithmic factor absent in the Gaussian case, reflecting the different geometry.

Economic applications:

•

Highway/corridor effects (transportation infrastructure)

•

Border regions (trade, migration perpendicular to border)

•

River/canal systems (water access for agriculture)

•

Transmission lines (electricity, telecommunications)

3.4.1.3
Level 3: Kummer Confluent Hypergeometric Functions (General 3D, Nonlinear)

For full nonlinear Navier-Stokes in three dimensions
Zhdanov and Zhdanov 2010
:

\tau(\mathbf{x},t)=t^{-3/2}\sum_{n,m,k}C_{nmk}M\left(a_{nmk},b_{nmk},\frac{|\mathbf{x}|^{2}}{4\nu t}\right)x^{n}y^{m}z^{k}

(60)

where
\displaystyle M(a,b,z)
is the Kummer confluent hypergeometric function:

M(a,b,z)=\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\frac{(a)_{n}}{(b)_{n}}\frac{z^{n}}{n!}=1+\frac{a}{b}z+\frac{a(a+1)}{b(b+1)}\frac{z^{2}}{2!}+\cdots

(61)

where
\displaystyle(a)_{n}=a(a+1)\cdots(a+n-1)
is the Pochhammer symbol (rising factorial).

Key properties:

1.
Differential equation:

\displaystyle M(a,b,z)
satisfies Kummer’s equation:

z\frac{d^{2}M}{dz^{2}}+(b-z)\frac{dM}{dz}-aM=0

(62)

2.
Asymptotic behavior:

M(a,b,z)\sim\begin{cases}1+\frac{a}{b}z+O(z^{2})&z\to 0\\
\frac{\Gamma(b)}{\Gamma(a)}z^{a-b}e^{z}&z\to\infty\end{cases}

(63)

3.
Connection to exponential (degenerate case):

\lim_{a\to\infty}M(a,a,z/a)=e^{z}

(64)

4.
Recurrence relations:
Kummer functions satisfy numerous identities enabling efficient computation.

Theorem 3.6

(Kummer-Bessel Connection)
.

The Kummer function reduces to modified Bessel functions under specific parameter relationships:

M\left(\nu+\frac{1}{2},2\nu+1,2z\right)=\Gamma(\nu+1)\left(\frac{2}{z}\right)^{\nu}I_{\nu}(z)

(65)

where
\displaystyle I_{\nu}(z)=\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\frac{1}{k!\Gamma(\nu+k+1)}(z/2)^{2k+\nu}
is the modified Bessel function of the first kind.

Furthermore:

K_{\nu}(z)=\frac{\pi}{2}\frac{I_{-\nu}(z)-I_{\nu}(z)}{\sin(\nu\pi)}

(66)

connects to modified Bessel functions of the second kind (Macdonald functions).

Proof:
Direct verification using series expansions and Pochhammer symbol identities. See
Abramowitz and Stegun 1964
, equations 13.6.1–13.6.4.
\displaystyle\square

Corollary 3.5

(Dimensional Reduction)
.

When the Navier-Stokes solution exhibits cylindrical symmetry (independence of one coordinate and azimuthal angle), the Kummer function representation reduces to:

\tau(r,t)=t^{-1}\sum_{n}C_{n}M\left(n+\frac{1}{2},2n+1,\frac{r^{2}}{4\nu t}\right)

(67)

which, by Theorem
3.6
, becomes a sum of modified Bessel functions
\displaystyle I_{n}(r/(2\sqrt{\nu t}))
and
\displaystyle K_{n}(r/(2\sqrt{\nu t}))
.

For physically relevant decay (bounded as
\displaystyle r\to 0
, decaying as
\displaystyle r\to\infty
), only
\displaystyle K_{0}
survives:

\tau(r,t)=\frac{A}{t}K_{0}\left(\frac{r}{2\sqrt{\nu t}}\right)

(68)

recovering the Level 2 (Bessel) solution.

Proof:
The boundary conditions at
\displaystyle r=0
(no singularity stronger than logarithmic) and
\displaystyle r\to\infty
(decay) select the
\displaystyle K_{0}
component. Higher-order terms (
\displaystyle n\geq 1
) vanish for symmetric sources.
\displaystyle\square

Interpretation:
The Kummer function framework
unifies
previous results:

•

Gaussian emerges as linearization (
\displaystyle a,b\to\infty
limit with
\displaystyle a/b
fixed)

•

Bessel emerges from cylindrical symmetry reduction

•

General Kummer needed only for full 3D without symmetry or strong nonlinearity

This hierarchy provides computational efficiency: use simplest form (Gaussian) when applicable, upgrade to Bessel for 2D problems, reserve Kummer for genuinely 3D nonlinear cases.

4
Perturbation Analysis for Time-Varying Environments

4.1
Motivation

Real economic environments are rarely stationary. Technological change, policy interventions, demographic shifts, and market dynamics introduce time-varying parameters into the diffusion process. This section develops perturbation theory to characterize how boundaries evolve when parameters
\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\theta}(t)=(\nu(t),Q(t),\mathbf{v}(t))
vary slowly over time.

The perturbation framework enables:

•

Quantifying sensitivity of boundaries to parameter changes

•

Forecasting boundary evolution under anticipated parameter trends

•

Identifying critical parameters for policy intervention

•

Computing confidence intervals for boundary estimates

4.2
Small Parameter Perturbations

Definition 4.1

(Perturbed System)
.

Consider the advection-diffusion equation with slowly time-varying parameters:

\frac{\partial\tau}{\partial t}+\mathbf{v}(\mathbf{x},t;\epsilon)\cdot\nabla\tau=\nu(t;\epsilon)\nabla^{2}\tau+S(\mathbf{x},t;\epsilon)

(69)

where
\displaystyle\epsilon\ll 1
is a small parameter and:

\displaystyle\displaystyle\nu(t;\epsilon)

\displaystyle\displaystyle=\nu_{0}+\epsilon\nu_{1}(t)+O(\epsilon^{2})

\displaystyle\displaystyle S(\mathbf{x},t;\epsilon)

\displaystyle\displaystyle=S_{0}(\mathbf{x},t)+\epsilon S_{1}(\mathbf{x},t)+O(\epsilon^{2})

Physical interpretation:
The parameter
\displaystyle\epsilon
measures the magnitude of departure from baseline conditions. For example:

•

\displaystyle\epsilon=0.1
might represent a 10% increase in diffusion coefficient due to technological improvement

•

\displaystyle\nu_{1}(t)=\alpha t
represents linear growth in diffusion over time

•

\displaystyle S_{1}(\mathbf{x},t)
represents additional sources introduced by policy

4.3
Regular Perturbation Expansion

Ansatz:
Expand solution in powers of
\displaystyle\epsilon
:

\tau(\mathbf{x},t;\epsilon)=\tau_{0}(\mathbf{x},t)+\epsilon\tau_{1}(\mathbf{x},t)+\epsilon^{2}\tau_{2}(\mathbf{x},t)+O(\epsilon^{3})

(70)

This is a
regular perturbation
(no singular behavior as
\displaystyle\epsilon\to 0
).

4.3.0.1
Zeroth Order (Baseline)

Substitute into PDE and collect
\displaystyle O(1)
terms:

\frac{\partial\tau_{0}}{\partial t}=\nu_{0}\nabla^{2}\tau_{0}+S_{0}(\mathbf{x},t)

(71)

This is the unperturbed (baseline) diffusion equation. For instantaneous point source
\displaystyle S_{0}=Q_{0}\delta(\mathbf{x})\delta(t)
, the solution is:

\tau_{0}(r,t)=\frac{Q_{0}}{(4\pi\nu_{0}t)^{3/2}}\exp\left(-\frac{r^{2}}{4\nu_{0}t}\right)

(72)

4.3.0.2
First Order (Linear Correction)

Collect
\displaystyle O(\epsilon)
terms:

\frac{\partial\tau_{1}}{\partial t}=\nu_{0}\nabla^{2}\tau_{1}+\nu_{1}(t)\nabla^{2}\tau_{0}+S_{1}(\mathbf{x},t)

(73)

This is a
forced
diffusion equation with inhomogeneous source term:

F(\mathbf{x},t)=\nu_{1}(t)\nabla^{2}\tau_{0}+S_{1}(\mathbf{x},t)

(74)

Theorem 4.1

(First-Order Correction via Duhamel’s Principle)
.

For time-varying diffusion coefficient
\displaystyle\nu(t)=\nu_{0}+\epsilon\nu_{1}(t)
, the first-order correction is:

\tau_{1}(\mathbf{x},t)=\int_{0}^{t}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{3}}G(\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{y},t-s;\nu_{0})\cdot\nu_{1}(s)\nabla^{2}\tau_{0}(\mathbf{y},s)\,d\mathbf{y}\,ds

(75)

where
\displaystyle G(\mathbf{x},t;\nu_{0})=(4\pi\nu_{0}t)^{-3/2}\exp(-|\mathbf{x}|^{2}/(4\nu_{0}t))
is the Green’s function.

Proof:
Apply variation of constants (Duhamel’s principle) to the forced diffusion equation. The solution is the convolution of the forcing term with the Green’s function:

\tau_{1}(\mathbf{x},t)=\int_{0}^{t}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{3}}G(\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{y},t-s)F(\mathbf{y},s)\,d\mathbf{y}\,ds

(76)

Substitute
\displaystyle F=\nu_{1}(s)\nabla^{2}\tau_{0}+S_{1}
.
\displaystyle\square

4.3.0.3
Boundary Correction

Proposition 4.1

(First-Order Boundary Shift)
.

The perturbed boundary
\displaystyle d^{*}(\epsilon,t)
satisfies:

d^{*}(\epsilon,t)=d^{*}_{0}(t)+\epsilon\frac{\tau_{1}(d^{*}_{0},t)}{|\partial\tau_{0}/\partial r|_{r=d^{*}_{0}}}+O(\epsilon^{2})

(77)

where
\displaystyle d^{*}_{0}(t)=2\sqrt{\nu_{0}t\ln(1/(1-\varepsilon))}
is the unperturbed boundary.

Proof:
The boundary condition is:

\tau(d^{*},t;\epsilon)=(1-\varepsilon)\tau(0,t;\epsilon)

(78)

Expanding both sides:

\displaystyle\displaystyle\tau_{0}(d^{*},t)+\epsilon\tau_{1}(d^{*},t)

\displaystyle\displaystyle=(1-\varepsilon)[\tau_{0}(0,t)+\epsilon\tau_{1}(0,t)]+O(\epsilon^{2})

Let
\displaystyle d^{*}=d^{*}_{0}+\epsilon\delta d^{*}+O(\epsilon^{2})
. Taylor expand:

\displaystyle\displaystyle\tau_{0}(d^{*}_{0}+\epsilon\delta d^{*},t)

\displaystyle\displaystyle=\tau_{0}(d^{*}_{0},t)+\epsilon\delta d^{*}\frac{\partial\tau_{0}}{\partial r}\bigg|_{r=d^{*}_{0}}+O(\epsilon^{2})

Substitute and collect
\displaystyle O(\epsilon)
terms. Since
\displaystyle\tau_{0}(d^{*}_{0},t)=(1-\varepsilon)\tau_{0}(0,t)
by definition:

\delta d^{*}\frac{\partial\tau_{0}}{\partial r}\bigg|_{d^{*}_{0}}+\tau_{1}(d^{*}_{0},t)=(1-\varepsilon)\tau_{1}(0,t)

(79)

Assuming
\displaystyle\tau_{1}(0,t)\ll\tau_{1}(d^{*}_{0},t)
(source region less affected by perturbations):

\delta d^{*}\approx\frac{\tau_{1}(d^{*}_{0},t)}{|\partial\tau_{0}/\partial r|_{r=d^{*}_{0}}}

(80)

\displaystyle\square

Interpretation:
The boundary shift is proportional to:

•

Numerator:
First-order correction at baseline boundary (effect of perturbation)

•

Denominator:
Spatial gradient at boundary (steepness of baseline profile)

Sharp baseline profiles (large
\displaystyle|\partial\tau_{0}/\partial r|
) are more resistant to boundary shifts than gradual profiles.

4.4
Multiple Time Scales

When parameters vary on different timescales, use
multiple scales analysis
—a powerful technique from applied mathematics.

Definition 4.2

(Multiple Time Variables)
.

Introduce fast time
\displaystyle t
and slow time
\displaystyle T=\epsilon t
. Seek solution:

\tau(\mathbf{x},t,T;\epsilon)=\tau_{0}(\mathbf{x},t,T)+\epsilon\tau_{1}(\mathbf{x},t,T)+O(\epsilon^{2})

(81)

where
\displaystyle\tau_{n}
depends on both time scales.

Time derivative becomes:

\frac{\partial}{\partial t}=\frac{\partial}{\partial t}\bigg|_{T}+\epsilon\frac{\partial}{\partial T}\bigg|_{t}

(82)

This separates fast dynamics (diffusion on scale
\displaystyle t
) from slow evolution (parameter drift on scale
\displaystyle T
).

Leading order (
\displaystyle O(1)
):

\frac{\partial\tau_{0}}{\partial t}=\nu(T)\nabla^{2}\tau_{0}

(83)

where
\displaystyle\nu(T)=\nu_{0}+\epsilon\nu_{1}T+O(\epsilon^{2})
varies slowly.

Solution (quasi-steady):

\tau_{0}(r,t,T)=\frac{Q}{(4\pi\nu(T)t)^{3/2}}\exp\left(-\frac{r^{2}}{4\nu(T)t}\right)

(84)

This is a Gaussian with
adiabatically varying
diffusion coefficient
\displaystyle\nu(T)
.

Boundary evolution:

d^{*}(t,T)=2\sqrt{\nu(T)t\ln(1/(1-\varepsilon))}

(85)

Interpretation:
On fast timescale
\displaystyle t
, solution looks like standard diffusion with “frozen” parameters. On slow timescale
\displaystyle T
, parameters evolve, gradually shifting boundary. The multiple scales method provides uniform approximation valid for all times.

4.5
Applications

4.5.0.1
Application 1: Technological Diffusion

Diffusion coefficient increases over time due to technology adoption (learning by doing, network effects):

\nu(t)=\nu_{0}(1+\alpha t),\quad\alpha\ll 1

(86)

Perturbation parameter:

\displaystyle\epsilon=\alpha
,
\displaystyle\nu_{1}(t)=\nu_{0}t
.

First-order correction:
From Theorem
4.1
:

\tau_{1}(\mathbf{x},t)=\nu_{0}\int_{0}^{t}s\int_{\mathbb{R}^{3}}G(\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{y},t-s)\nabla^{2}\tau_{0}(\mathbf{y},s)\,d\mathbf{y}\,ds

(87)

Boundary shift:
Using Proposition
4.1
:

d^{*}(t)=2\sqrt{\nu_{0}(1+\alpha t)t\ln(1/(1-\varepsilon))}\approx d^{*}_{0}(t)\left(1+\frac{\alpha t}{2}\right)

(88)

where the approximation uses
\displaystyle(1+x)^{1/2}\approx 1+x/2
for small
\displaystyle x=\alpha t
.

Economic insight:
Boundary expands
faster
than
\displaystyle\sqrt{t}
due to technological improvement. The fractional increase in boundary radius is
\displaystyle\alpha t/2
at time
\displaystyle t
.

4.5.0.2
Application 2: Policy Intervention

Source intensity changes abruptly at
\displaystyle t=t_{0}
:

Q(t)=\begin{cases}Q_{0}&t<t_{0}\\
Q_{0}(1+\Delta Q)&t\geq t_{0}\end{cases}

(89)

where
\displaystyle\Delta Q
is the fractional change in source intensity.

For
\displaystyle t>t_{0}
, solution is superposition:

\tau(\mathbf{x},t)=\tau_{\text{old}}(\mathbf{x},t)+\tau_{\text{new}}(\mathbf{x},t-t_{0})

(90)

where:

\displaystyle\displaystyle\tau_{\text{old}}

\displaystyle\displaystyle=\frac{Q_{0}}{(4\pi\nu t)^{3/2}}\exp\left(-\frac{r^{2}}{4\nu t}\right)

\displaystyle\displaystyle\tau_{\text{new}}

\displaystyle\displaystyle=\frac{Q_{0}\Delta Q}{(4\pi\nu(t-t_{0}))^{3/2}}\exp\left(-\frac{r^{2}}{4\nu(t-t_{0})}\right)

Boundary evolution:

For large
\displaystyle t\gg t_{0}
, both components have comparable spatial extent, and the boundary satisfies approximately:

d^{*}(t)\approx 2\sqrt{\nu t\ln\left(\frac{1+\Delta Q}{1-\varepsilon}\right)}

(91)

This shows a logarithmic shift in boundary position proportional to the intensity change
\displaystyle\Delta Q
.

Short-run dynamics (
\displaystyle t
slightly larger than
\displaystyle t_{0}
):
The new component
\displaystyle\tau_{\text{new}}
is more concentrated near the source, creating a sharp gradient. The boundary initially contracts slightly before expanding again.

4.5.0.3
Application 3: Environmental Regulation

A pollution tax induces firms to reduce emissions gradually:

Q(t)=Q_{0}e^{-\lambda t},\quad\lambda>0

(92)

Steady decline:
Source strength decays exponentially with rate
\displaystyle\lambda
.

Solution:
For exponentially decaying source, the fundamental solution involves modified Bessel functions:

\tau(r,t)=\frac{Q_{0}}{(4\pi\nu)^{3/2}}\int_{0}^{t}\frac{e^{-\lambda s}}{(t-s)^{3/2}}\exp\left(-\frac{r^{2}}{4\nu(t-s)}\right)ds

(93)

Asymptotic behavior:
For
\displaystyle\lambda t\gg 1
(long after regulation):

\tau(r,t)\sim\frac{Q_{0}}{\lambda}\frac{1}{r}\exp\left(-r\sqrt{\frac{\lambda}{\nu}}\right)

(94)

The spatial profile transitions from Gaussian to
Yukawa
(screened Coulomb potential), with characteristic screening length
\displaystyle\ell=\sqrt{\nu/\lambda}
.

Boundary evolution:
The boundary approaches a steady state:

\lim_{t\to\infty}d^{*}(t)=d^{*}_{\infty}=\sqrt{\frac{\nu}{\lambda}}\ln\left(\frac{Q_{0}}{\lambda\ell\tau_{\min}}\right)

(95)

No further expansion after sufficient time—the boundary stabilizes when source decay balances diffusive spreading.

5
Functional Calculus for Policy Analysis

5.1
Variational Derivatives and Sensitivity

The continuous functional framework enables rigorous sensitivity analysis through variational calculus. This section develops tools for computing how treatment intensity and boundaries respond to infinitesimal changes in parameters, sources, or policy interventions.

Definition 5.1

(Functional Derivative)
.

The
functional derivative
(also called Gâteaux derivative or first variation) of functional
\displaystyle F[\tau]
with respect to
\displaystyle\tau
at point
\displaystyle(\mathbf{x}_{0},t_{0})
is:

\frac{\delta F}{\delta\tau(\mathbf{x}_{0},t_{0})}=\lim_{\epsilon\to 0}\frac{F[\tau+\epsilon\delta_{\mathbf{x}_{0},t_{0}}]-F[\tau]}{\epsilon}

(96)

where
\displaystyle\delta_{\mathbf{x}_{0},t_{0}}
is the Dirac delta function centered at
\displaystyle(\mathbf{x}_{0},t_{0})
.

Interpretation:
The functional derivative measures the sensitivity of functional
\displaystyle F
to localized perturbations in the treatment field
\displaystyle\tau
. It generalizes the ordinary derivative from finite to infinite dimensions.

5.1.0.1
Basic Functional Derivatives

Example 5.1

(Common Functionals)
.

1.
Total intensity:

F[\tau]=\int_{\mathcal{D}}\tau(\mathbf{x},t)\,d\mathbf{x}\quad\Rightarrow\quad\frac{\delta F}{\delta\tau(\mathbf{x}_{0})}=1

(97)

The functional derivative is constant—every location contributes equally to total intensity.

2.
Energy functional:

E[\tau]=\int_{\mathcal{D}}\tau^{2}(\mathbf{x},t)\,d\mathbf{x}\quad\Rightarrow\quad\frac{\delta E}{\delta\tau(\mathbf{x}_{0})}=2\tau(\mathbf{x}_{0},t)

(98)

Locations with high intensity contribute quadratically to energy.

3.
Gradient energy:

G[\tau]=\int_{\mathcal{D}}|\nabla\tau|^{2}\,d\mathbf{x}\quad\Rightarrow\quad\frac{\delta G}{\delta\tau(\mathbf{x}_{0})}=-2\nabla^{2}\tau(\mathbf{x}_{0})

(99)

This follows from integration by parts:
\displaystyle\int|\nabla\tau|^{2}=-\int\tau\nabla^{2}\tau
(with boundary terms vanishing).

4.
Population-weighted exposure:

W[\tau]=\int_{\mathcal{D}}\rho(\mathbf{x})\tau(\mathbf{x},t)\,d\mathbf{x}\quad\Rightarrow\quad\frac{\delta W}{\delta\tau(\mathbf{x}_{0})}=\rho(\mathbf{x}_{0})

(100)

Sensitivity is proportional to local population density.

5.2
Optimal Source Placement

A fundamental policy question: where should sources be located to maximize social welfare?

5.2.0.1
Problem Formulation

Goal:
Choose source location
\displaystyle\mathbf{x}_{0}
to maximize social welfare:

W[\mathbf{x}_{0}]=\int_{\mathcal{D}}\int_{0}^{T}u(\tau(\mathbf{x};\mathbf{x}_{0},t))\rho(\mathbf{x})\,d\mathbf{x}\,dt-C(\mathbf{x}_{0})

(101)

where:

•

\displaystyle u(\tau)
: Utility function (concave, increasing in
\displaystyle\tau
)

•

\displaystyle\rho(\mathbf{x})
: Population density

•

\displaystyle C(\mathbf{x}_{0})
: Location-specific cost (land price, regulation, etc.)

•

\displaystyle\tau(\mathbf{x};\mathbf{x}_{0},t)
: Treatment intensity with source at
\displaystyle\mathbf{x}_{0}

5.2.0.2
First-Order Condition

Theorem 5.1

(Optimal Location Condition)
.

The optimal source location
\displaystyle\mathbf{x}_{0}^{*}
satisfies:

\int_{\mathcal{D}}\int_{0}^{T}u^{\prime}(\tau)\frac{\partial\tau(\mathbf{x};\mathbf{x}_{0}^{*},t)}{\partial\mathbf{x}_{0}}\rho(\mathbf{x})\,d\mathbf{x}\,dt=\nabla_{\mathbf{x}_{0}}C(\mathbf{x}_{0}^{*})

(102)

Proof:
Differentiate welfare functional with respect to source location:

\nabla_{\mathbf{x}_{0}}W=\int_{\mathcal{D}}\int_{0}^{T}u^{\prime}(\tau)\nabla_{\mathbf{x}_{0}}\tau\,\rho\,d\mathbf{x}\,dt-\nabla_{\mathbf{x}_{0}}C

(103)

Set equal to zero at optimum.
\displaystyle\square

Interpretation:
Marginal benefit (left side) equals marginal cost (right side). The marginal benefit aggregates utility gains across all locations and times, weighted by population and marginal utility.

5.2.0.3
Gaussian Diffusion Case

For Gaussian:

\tau(\mathbf{x};\mathbf{x}_{0},t)=\frac{Q}{(4\pi\nu t)^{3/2}}\exp\left(-\frac{|\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{x}_{0}|^{2}}{4\nu t}\right)

(104)

Taking derivative with respect to source location:

\frac{\partial\tau}{\partial\mathbf{x}_{0}}=\frac{\partial\tau}{\partial r}\cdot\frac{\partial r}{\partial\mathbf{x}_{0}}=\frac{\partial\tau}{\partial r}\cdot\frac{-(\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{x}_{0})}{r}

(105)

where
\displaystyle r=|\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{x}_{0}|
and:

\frac{\partial\tau}{\partial r}=-\frac{r}{2\nu t}\tau(r,t)

(106)

Thus:

\frac{\partial\tau}{\partial\mathbf{x}_{0}}=\frac{(\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{x}_{0})}{2\nu t}\tau(|\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{x}_{0}|,t)

(107)

First-order condition becomes:

\int_{\mathcal{D}}\int_{0}^{T}u^{\prime}(\tau)\frac{(\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{x}_{0}^{*})}{2\nu t}\tau\,\rho\,d\mathbf{x}\,dt=\nabla C(\mathbf{x}_{0}^{*})

(108)

Corollary 5.1

(Centroid Optimality)
.

If utility is linear (
\displaystyle u(\tau)=a\tau
) and cost is location-independent (
\displaystyle\nabla C=0
), the optimal location is the population-weighted centroid:

\mathbf{x}_{0}^{*}=\frac{\int_{\mathcal{D}}\mathbf{x}\rho(\mathbf{x})\,d\mathbf{x}}{\int_{\mathcal{D}}\rho(\mathbf{x})\,d\mathbf{x}}

(109)

Proof:
For linear utility,
\displaystyle u^{\prime}(\tau)=a
is constant. The first-order condition becomes:

a\int_{\mathcal{D}}\int_{0}^{T}\frac{(\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{x}_{0}^{*})}{2\nu t}\tau\,\rho\,d\mathbf{x}\,dt=0

(110)

This simplifies to:

\int_{\mathcal{D}}(\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{x}_{0}^{*})\rho(\mathbf{x})\left[\int_{0}^{T}\frac{\tau}{2\nu t}dt\right]d\mathbf{x}=0

(111)

Since the temporal integral is positive for all
\displaystyle\mathbf{x}
, we require:

\int_{\mathcal{D}}(\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{x}_{0}^{*})\rho(\mathbf{x})\,d\mathbf{x}=0

(112)

Solving for
\displaystyle\mathbf{x}_{0}^{*}
gives the population-weighted centroid.
\displaystyle\square

Economic insight:
With linear utility and no location costs, place the source at the population center to minimize average distance to users.

5.3
Parameter Sensitivity Analysis

5.3.0.1
Question:

How does boundary
\displaystyle d^{*}(t)
respond to changes in diffusion coefficient
\displaystyle\nu
?

Definition 5.2

(Boundary Sensitivity)
.

The
boundary sensitivity functional
is:

S_{\nu}(t)=\frac{\partial d^{*}(t)}{\partial\nu}

(113)

For Gaussian:

\displaystyle d^{*}=2\sqrt{\nu t\ln(1/(1-\varepsilon))}

S_{\nu}(t)=\frac{\partial}{\partial\nu}\left(2\sqrt{\nu t\ln(1/(1-\varepsilon))}\right)=\frac{d^{*}(t)}{2\nu}=\frac{\sqrt{t\ln(1/(1-\varepsilon))}}{\sqrt{\nu}}

(114)

Interpretation:

•

Sensitivity decreases with
\displaystyle\nu
(diminishing returns to increasing diffusion)

•

Sensitivity increases with
\displaystyle t
(long-run impacts are larger)

•

Elasticity is constant:
\displaystyle\frac{\partial\ln d^{*}}{\partial\ln\nu}=1/2

A 10% increase in diffusion coefficient yields a 5% increase in boundary radius.

5.3.0.2
Application: Cost-Benefit of Intervention

Policy intervention
increases diffusion rate from
\displaystyle\nu_{0}
to
\displaystyle\nu_{1}=\nu_{0}(1+\alpha)
.

Boundary expansion:

\Delta d^{*}(t)=d^{*}(t;\nu_{1})-d^{*}(t;\nu_{0})=2\sqrt{t\ln(1/(1-\varepsilon))}\left(\sqrt{\nu_{1}}-\sqrt{\nu_{0}}\right)

(115)

For small
\displaystyle\alpha
:

\Delta d^{*}(t)\approx d^{*}(t;\nu_{0})\cdot\frac{\alpha}{2}

(116)

Benefit:
Additional population covered:

\Delta\text{Pop}=\int_{d^{*}_{0}}^{d^{*}_{1}}\rho(r)\cdot 4\pi r^{2}\,dr\approx 4\pi(d^{*}_{0})^{2}\rho(d^{*}_{0})\cdot\Delta d^{*}

(117)

If per-capita benefit is
\displaystyle B
and intervention cost is
\displaystyle C
:

Net benefit:

\text{NB}=B\cdot\Delta\text{Pop}-C=B\cdot 4\pi(d^{*}_{0})^{2}\rho(d^{*}_{0})\cdot\frac{\alpha}{2}d^{*}_{0}-C

(118)

Optimal intervention level:
Maximize over
\displaystyle\alpha
:

\alpha^{*}=\arg\max_{\alpha}\left[B\cdot 4\pi(d^{*}_{0})^{3}\rho(d^{*}_{0})\cdot\frac{\alpha}{2}-C(\alpha)\right]

(119)

First-order condition:

B\cdot 2\pi(d^{*}_{0})^{3}\rho(d^{*}_{0})=C^{\prime}(\alpha^{*})

(120)

Interpretation:
Optimal intervention equates marginal benefit (left side) to marginal cost (right side). Marginal benefit is proportional to cube of baseline boundary (larger baseline coverage implies larger returns to expansion).

5.4
Calculus of Variations: Optimal Diffusion Path

5.4.0.1
Problem:

Choose time-varying diffusion coefficient
\displaystyle\nu(t)
to maximize welfare subject to resource constraint.

Objective:

\max_{\nu(t)}\quad W[\nu]=\int_{0}^{T}\int_{\mathcal{D}}u(\tau(\mathbf{x},t;\nu))\rho(\mathbf{x})\,d\mathbf{x}\,dt

(121)

Constraint:

\int_{0}^{T}c(\nu(t))\,dt\leq\bar{C}

(122)

where
\displaystyle c(\nu)
is instantaneous cost function (increasing, convex:
\displaystyle c^{\prime}>0
,
\displaystyle c^{\prime\prime}>0
).

5.4.0.2
Euler-Lagrange Equation

Form Lagrangian:

\mathcal{L}=\int_{0}^{T}\left[\int_{\mathcal{D}}u(\tau)\rho\,d\mathbf{x}-\lambda c(\nu(t))\right]dt

(123)

where
\displaystyle\lambda\geq 0
is the Lagrange multiplier on the budget constraint.

Functional derivative with respect to
\displaystyle\nu(t)
:

\frac{\delta\mathcal{L}}{\delta\nu(t)}=\int_{\mathcal{D}}u^{\prime}(\tau)\frac{\partial\tau}{\partial\nu}\rho\,d\mathbf{x}-\lambda c^{\prime}(\nu(t))=0

(124)

Optimal policy:

\lambda c^{\prime}(\nu^{*}(t))=\int_{\mathcal{D}}u^{\prime}(\tau(\mathbf{x},t;\nu^{*}))\frac{\partial\tau}{\partial\nu}\bigg|_{\nu^{*}}\rho(\mathbf{x})\,d\mathbf{x}

(125)

Interpretation:
At each time
\displaystyle t
, choose
\displaystyle\nu(t)
such that marginal cost equals expected marginal benefit across all locations (weighted by population and marginal utility).

5.4.0.3
Bang-Bang Solution

If cost is linear (
\displaystyle c(\nu)=c_{0}\nu
), the solution is typically
bang-bang
:

\nu^{*}(t)=\begin{cases}\nu_{\max}&\text{if }\int u^{\prime}(\tau)\frac{\partial\tau}{\partial\nu}\rho\,d\mathbf{x}>\lambda c_{0}\\
\nu_{\min}&\text{otherwise}\end{cases}

(126)

Economic insight:
With linear costs, either invest maximally or minimally; intermediate levels are suboptimal. This is analogous to the ”invest everything in the risky asset or the safe asset” result in portfolio theory with linear trading costs.

When does interior solution exist?
With strictly convex cost (
\displaystyle c^{\prime\prime}>0
), the solution is interior and satisfies the Euler-Lagrange equation above. The optimal path
\displaystyle\nu^{*}(t)
balances:

•

High
\displaystyle\nu
early (rapid initial diffusion, large marginal benefits)

•

Lower
\displaystyle\nu
later (diminishing returns as treatment spreads)

5.5
Hamilton-Jacobi-Bellman Equation for Dynamic Optimization

For continuous-time stochastic control, the value function satisfies the HJB equation.

State:
Treatment field
\displaystyle\tau(\mathbf{x},t)

Control:
Diffusion coefficient
\displaystyle\nu(t)
or source strength
\displaystyle Q(t)

Value function:

V(\tau,t)=\max_{\{\nu(s)\}_{s\in[t,T]}}\mathbb{E}\left[\int_{t}^{T}\left(\int_{\mathcal{D}}u(\tau(\mathbf{x},s))\rho\,d\mathbf{x}-c(\nu(s))\right)ds\right]

(127)

HJB equation (deterministic case):

-\frac{\partial V}{\partial t}=\max_{\nu}\left\{\int_{\mathcal{D}}u(\tau)\rho\,d\mathbf{x}-c(\nu)+\int_{\mathcal{D}}\frac{\delta V}{\delta\tau(\mathbf{x})}\left[\nu\nabla^{2}\tau\right]d\mathbf{x}\right\}

(128)

First-order condition:

c^{\prime}(\nu^{*})=\int_{\mathcal{D}}\frac{\delta V}{\delta\tau(\mathbf{x})}\nabla^{2}\tau(\mathbf{x},t)\,d\mathbf{x}

(129)

Interpretation:
The optimal control balances instantaneous cost against the value of altering the future state distribution (weighted by the co-state
\displaystyle\delta V/\delta\tau
, which represents the shadow value of treatment intensity at each location).

5.5.0.1
Computational Approach

The HJB equation is an infinite-dimensional PDE (functional PDE). Computational solution requires:

1.
Finite-dimensional approximation:
Project
\displaystyle\tau
onto finite basis:

\tau(\mathbf{x},t)\approx\sum_{i=1}^{N}\tau_{i}(t)\phi_{i}(\mathbf{x})

(130)

where
\displaystyle\{\phi_{i}\}
are basis functions (e.g., Fourier modes, wavelets, finite elements).

2.
Reduced HJB:
Value function becomes
\displaystyle V(\tau_{1},\ldots,\tau_{N},t)
, a finite-dimensional PDE solvable via standard dynamic programming.

3.
Policy iteration:
Alternate between:
-
Policy evaluation:
Solve linear PDE for value given policy
-
Policy improvement:
Update policy via first-order condition

4.
Convergence:
Iterate until policy stabilizes.

Remark 5.1

(Curse of Dimensionality)
.

Even with finite-dimensional projection, computational cost grows exponentially with
\displaystyle N
. For large-scale problems (
\displaystyle N>10
), use:

•

Model reduction (POD, DMD)

•

Reinforcement learning (approximate value function via neural networks)

•

Linearization around equilibrium (LQR/LQG)

5.6
Green’s Function Methods for Policy Design

The Green’s function
\displaystyle G(\mathbf{x},t;\mathbf{y},s)
represents the treatment intensity at location
\displaystyle\mathbf{x}
and time
\displaystyle t
resulting from a unit impulse at location
\displaystyle\mathbf{y}
and time
\displaystyle s
.

Definition 5.3

(Green’s Function)
.

The
Green’s function
for the diffusion operator satisfies:

\frac{\partial G}{\partial t}-\nu\nabla^{2}G=\delta(\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{y})\delta(t-s)

(131)

with initial condition
\displaystyle G(\mathbf{x},t;\mathbf{y},s)=0
for
\displaystyle t<s
.

For unbounded 3D domain:

G(\mathbf{x},t;\mathbf{y},s)=\frac{1}{(4\pi\nu(t-s))^{3/2}}\exp\left(-\frac{|\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{y}|^{2}}{4\nu(t-s)}\right)\cdot H(t-s)

(132)

where
\displaystyle H
is the Heaviside step function.

5.6.0.1
General Solution via Superposition

For arbitrary source distribution
\displaystyle S(\mathbf{x},t)
:

\tau(\mathbf{x},t)=\int_{0}^{t}\int_{\mathcal{D}}G(\mathbf{x},t;\mathbf{y},s)S(\mathbf{y},s)\,d\mathbf{y}\,ds+\int_{\mathcal{D}}G(\mathbf{x},t;\mathbf{y},0)\tau_{0}(\mathbf{y})\,d\mathbf{y}

(133)

First term:
Contribution from sources over time

Second term:
Contribution from initial condition

5.6.0.2
Optimal Source Design

Problem:
Choose spatial-temporal source distribution
\displaystyle S(\mathbf{x},t)
to achieve target profile
\displaystyle\tau_{\text{target}}(\mathbf{x},T)
at time
\displaystyle T
, minimizing control effort:

\min_{S}\quad\frac{1}{2}\int_{0}^{T}\int_{\mathcal{D}}S^{2}(\mathbf{x},t)\,d\mathbf{x}\,dt+\frac{\gamma}{2}\int_{\mathcal{D}}(\tau(\mathbf{x},T)-\tau_{\text{target}}(\mathbf{x}))^{2}d\mathbf{x}

(134)

Solution (adjoint method):

1.
Forward problem:
Solve for
\displaystyle\tau
given
\displaystyle S

2.
Adjoint problem:
Solve backward for co-state
\displaystyle\psi
:

-\frac{\partial\psi}{\partial t}-\nu\nabla^{2}\psi=0,\quad\psi(\mathbf{x},T)=\gamma(\tau(\mathbf{x},T)-\tau_{\text{target}})

(135)

3.
Optimal control:

\displaystyle S^{*}(\mathbf{x},t)=-\psi(\mathbf{x},t)

Interpretation:
The co-state
\displaystyle\psi
represents the shadow value of treatment intensity. Place sources where shadow value is negative (deficits relative to target).

6
Dynamic Boundaries: Time-Varying Functionals

6.1
Non-Self-Similar Evolution

When environments are non-stationary—due to technological change, policy shifts, or evolving markets—boundaries deviate from power-law scaling. This section characterizes general boundary dynamics.

Definition 6.1

(General Dynamic Boundary)
.

The
general dynamic boundary functional

\displaystyle d^{*}:\mathbb{R}_{+}\to\mathbb{R}_{+}
satisfies:

\tau(d^{*}(t),t;\boldsymbol{\theta}(t))=\tau_{\min}

(136)

where
\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\theta}(t)=(\nu(t),Q(t),\mathbf{v}(t))
are time-varying parameters.

Unlike self-similar cases where
\displaystyle d^{*}\propto t^{\beta}
, general boundaries exhibit richer dynamics: acceleration, deceleration, contraction, or oscillation.

6.2
Boundary Evolution Equation

Theorem 6.1

(Boundary Dynamics ODE)
.

The temporal evolution of
\displaystyle d^{*}(t)
satisfies the ordinary differential equation:

\frac{dd^{*}}{dt}=-\frac{\frac{\partial\tau}{\partial t}\bigg|_{r=d^{*},t}}{\frac{\partial\tau}{\partial r}\bigg|_{r=d^{*},t}}

(137)

Proof:
Differentiate the threshold condition
\displaystyle\tau(d^{*}(t),t)=\tau_{\min}
with respect to
\displaystyle t
using the chain rule:

\frac{d}{dt}[\tau(d^{*}(t),t)]=\frac{\partial\tau}{\partial r}\bigg|_{r=d^{*}}\frac{dd^{*}}{dt}+\frac{\partial\tau}{\partial t}\bigg|_{r=d^{*}}=0

(138)

Solving for
\displaystyle dd^{*}/dt
gives the result.
\displaystyle\square

Interpretation:
Boundary velocity depends on the ratio:

•

Numerator

\displaystyle\partial\tau/\partial t
: Temporal change at boundary (intensity evolution)

•

Denominator

\displaystyle\partial\tau/\partial r
: Spatial gradient at boundary (decay steepness)

If

\displaystyle\partial\tau/\partial t<0
(intensity decreasing): boundary contracts (
\displaystyle dd^{*}/dt<0
)

If

\displaystyle\partial\tau/\partial t>0
(intensity increasing): boundary expands (
\displaystyle dd^{*}/dt>0
)

Steep spatial gradients (large
\displaystyle|\partial\tau/\partial r|
) result in slow boundary motion—the threshold is crossed over a narrow spatial range.

Example 6.1

(Exponentially Decaying Source)
.

For
\displaystyle\tau(r,t)=\frac{Q_{0}e^{-\lambda t}}{(4\pi\nu t)^{3/2}}\exp(-r^{2}/(4\nu t))
:

\frac{\partial\tau}{\partial t}=-\left(\lambda+\frac{3}{2t}+\frac{r^{2}}{4\nu t^{2}}\right)\tau

(139)

\frac{\partial\tau}{\partial r}=-\frac{r}{2\nu t}\tau

(140)

Thus:

\frac{dd^{*}}{dt}=-\frac{-\left(\lambda+\frac{3}{2t}+\frac{(d^{*})^{2}}{4\nu t^{2}}\right)}{-\frac{d^{*}}{2\nu t}}=\frac{2\nu t}{d^{*}}\left(\lambda+\frac{3}{2t}+\frac{(d^{*})^{2}}{4\nu t^{2}}\right)

(141)

For large
\displaystyle t
with
\displaystyle\lambda>0
:
\displaystyle dd^{*}/dt\approx 2\nu\lambda t/d^{*}>0
initially, but as
\displaystyle d^{*}
grows, expansion slows and eventually halts.

6.3
Boundary Stability Analysis

Question:
Does the boundary approach a steady state, or does it grow indefinitely?

Definition 6.2

(Asymptotic Boundary)
.

The
asymptotic boundary
is:

d^{*}_{\infty}=\lim_{t\to\infty}d^{*}(t)

(142)

if this limit exists and is finite.

Theorem 6.2

(Steady-State Condition)
.

The boundary reaches a steady state
\displaystyle d^{*}_{\infty}<\infty
if and only if:

\lim_{t\to\infty}\frac{\partial\tau/\partial t}{\partial\tau/\partial r}\bigg|_{r=d^{*}(t)}=0

(143)

Proof:
From Theorem
6.1
,
\displaystyle dd^{*}/dt\to 0
as
\displaystyle t\to\infty
requires the numerator to vanish faster than the denominator.
\displaystyle\square

Examples:

1.
Self-similar diffusion:

\displaystyle\tau\sim t^{-3/2}
, no steady state (
\displaystyle d^{*}\to\infty
)

2.
Exponential decay source:

\displaystyle\tau\sim e^{-\lambda t}
, steady state exists at:

d^{*}_{\infty}=\sqrt{\frac{\nu}{\lambda}}\ln\left(\frac{Q_{0}}{\tau_{\min}\sqrt{4\pi\nu/\lambda}}\right)

(144)

3.
Sustained source:

\displaystyle Q(t)=Q_{0}
constant, no steady state (
\displaystyle d^{*}\sim\sqrt{t}
)

6.4
Boundary Perturbation from Self-Similarity

For small departures from self-similar baseline, linearize around
\displaystyle d^{*}_{\text{ss}}(t)=\xi^{*}t^{\beta}
.

Ansatz:

\tau(r,t)=\tau_{\text{ss}}(r,t)+\epsilon\tau_{1}(r,t)+O(\epsilon^{2})

(145)

where

Conclusion

This paper develops a comprehensive framework for dynamic spatial treatment effects as continuous functionals defined over space-time domains. By grounding treatment intensity in Navier-Stokes partial differential equations, the framework enables rigorous mathematical analysis, admits exact analytical solutions through special functions, and facilitates policy analysis via functional calculus.

The continuous functional perspective offers three principal advantages over conventional discrete estimators.
Theoretically
, it provides unified treatment of boundary evolution, spatial gradients, cumulative exposure, and higher-order functionals through differential geometry and functional analysis.
Methodologically
, it derives functional forms from physical principles rather than imposing them statistically, addressing functional form misspecification concerns.
Empirically
, it enables diagnostic procedures that identify when underlying assumptions hold versus when alternative mechanisms dominate.

Empirical validation demonstrates strong support for exponential decay predictions using 42 million TROPOMI satellite observations of NO
2
pollution from coal-fired power plants. The spatial decay parameter
\displaystyle\kappa_{s}=0.004028
per km (SE = 0.000016,
\displaystyle p<0.001
) implies detectable boundaries at 572 km from major facilities, with the model explaining 35% of spatial variation. Monte Carlo simulations confirm superior boundary detection performance (RMSE below 5% at policy-relevant distances) and substantially better false positive avoidance (94% correct rejection rate versus 27% for parametric methods).

Most critically, regional heterogeneity analysis validates the framework’s diagnostic capability. Positive decay parameters within 100 km confirm coal plants are dominant NO
2
sources and diffusion assumptions hold. Negative decay parameters beyond 100 km correctly signal urban traffic sources dominate, demonstrating the framework successfully identifies when scope conditions are violated. This sign reversal addresses spurious spatial regression concerns
Müller and Watson 2024
: the distance-dependent pattern change is inconsistent with persistent spatial trends but consistent with heterogeneous treatment sources.

The framework’s scope conditions and diagnostic procedures provide actionable guidance for empirical researchers. Rather than assuming exponential decay applies universally, practitioners can test whether positive significant decay parameters emerge, assess model fit, and conduct regional subsample analysis to detect when alternative mechanisms operate. This falsifiable approach bridges the gap between structural models’ theoretical transparency and reduced-form methods’ empirical flexibility.

Applications span environmental economics (pollution dispersion, natural resource depletion), urban economics (transportation infrastructure, housing spillovers), financial economics (bank branch networks, credit access), health economics (hospital catchment areas, disease transmission), and development economics (technology diffusion, agricultural extension). Wherever treatment propagates continuously through geographic space following diffusion-advection dynamics, the continuous functional framework offers theoretically grounded, empirically implementable methods for boundary detection, exposure quantification, and policy evaluation.
