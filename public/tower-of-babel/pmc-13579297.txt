Entrepreneurial and innovative skills in healthcare: Development and preliminary validation of an assessment instrument for Portuguese nurses
Authors: Inês F Almeida, Liliana B Sousa, Paulo Santos-Costa, Filipa Ventura, Amorim Rosa, Pedro Parreira
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundInnovative and entrepreneurial competences, defined as the ability to identify opportunities, mobilize resources, and implement creative solutions responsibly, are essential for tackling complex challenges. In the health sector, particularly in nursing, these skills enhance innovation, quality of care, and sustainability of health services. Currently, there is no validated instrument with good psychometric properties designed for nurses that assesses these competencies.ObjectiveTo develop a content-valid instrument to assess innovative and entrepreneurial competences in nurses.MethodsA three-phase instrument-development study was conducted from November 2024 to August 2025. Phase I comprised a narrative literature review and five focus groups to generate and refine items tailored to nursing practice. Phase II used a two-round Modified Delphi process with seven experts to evaluate the relevance and clarity of the instrument’s title, instructions, response scale, and items; consensus was quantified using the item-level and scale-level Content Validity Index. Phase III involved a pre-test with 15 nurses from the target population to assess comprehensibility and perceived adequacy of each item.ResultsThrough the narrative literature review, seven instruments were identified that served as the basis for the experts’ discussion in the five focus groups and subsequently for the assessment conducted in two rounds of the Delphi method. The resulting questionnaire includes 69 items mapped to the 15 EntreComp competences across three areas (Ideas and Opportunities, Resources, and Into Action) and uses a 7-point Likert response scale (1 – completely disagree to 7 – completely agree), with higher scores indicating higher perceived competence. In the second Delphi round, item-level Content Validity Index ranged from 0.86 to 1.000 and the overall scale Content Validity Index/Average was 0.96. In the pre-test, item comprehensibility/adequacy ranged from 87% to 100%, and minor semantic refinements were made to seven itemsConclusionsThis study produced a content-valid instrument to assess innovative and entrepreneurial competences in nurses, grounded in the EntreComp framework and demonstrating strong expert consensus (scale Content Validity Index/Average = 0.96) and high comprehensibility among the target population. The final 69-item, 7-point Likert scale questionnaire fills an important gap in the field and shows promise for use in academic, clinical, and organizational contexts to foster innovation and entrepreneurship in nursing, pending further psychometric validation.Keywords: Entrepreneurship, Innovation, Instrument validation, Nursing, Psychometrics, Professional competence

Background

Entrepreneurship, as a concept, is rooted in attitudes and behaviours long recognised in society, though its perception has evolved significantly over time. The classical conception of entrepreneurship, rooted in Schumpeter's notion of "creative destruction," defined the entrepreneur as an agent of radical innovation, one who disrupts existing markets by integrating inventions into new business models, thereby driving organisations toward technological superiority and competitive advantage (Ginzberg and Schumpeter, 1947). This classical framework remains influential; however, it was originally conceived for industrial and commercial contexts and does not adequately capture the nature of value creation in public service professions such as nursing, where the primary purpose is not market disruption but the continuous improvement of care quality and patient outcomes. The most significant transformation in how entrepreneurship is understood occurred precisely when it ceased to be framed exclusively through this economic and commercial lens and began to encompass social, political, and environmental dimensions (Bacq and Lumpkin, 2021; Muñoz and Cohen, 2018). In healthcare specifically, the classical approach proves insufficient because it privileges profit-oriented risk-taking and the creation of new ventures, while neglecting the forms of entrepreneurial agency most relevant to clinical professionals: identifying unmet patient needs, redesigning care pathways, challenging inefficient organisational routines, and mobilising interdisciplinary teams around innovative solutions. A nurse who develops a new protocol to reduce hospital-acquired infections, or who leads the implementation of a digital triage system to improve emergency department flow, is exercising genuine entrepreneurial competence, yet such activity falls outside the scope of Schumpeterian entrepreneurship as traditionally defined.

In the context of innovation and entrepreneurship, competences can no longer be reduced to technical skills for business creation and management. Instead, their scope reflects the political and social imperative to foster global, inclusive processes that promote well-being (Bacigalupo et al., 2016). Thus, innovative/entrepreneurial skills can be understood as the ability to identify opportunities and develop creative and sustainable solutions, mobilizing resources and managing change in different social, political, and organizational contexts, with a positive impact on quality, efficiency, and collective well-being (Bacigalupo et al., 2016; Morley and Cashell, 2017).

In the health sector, innovation and entrepreneurship are central elements for enhancing care quality and efficiency (Morley and Cashell, 2017). This centrality is not merely theoretical. Empirical evidence illustrates how entrepreneurial action by healthcare professionals produces measurable improvements in care delivery: the introduction of nurse-led anticoagulation clinics has been shown to reduce adverse events and improve patient satisfaction (Fitzmaurice et al., 2000) nurse-initiated early warning and rapid response systems have demonstrably reduced in-hospital mortality (Jones et al., 2011); and nursing-led models of chronic disease management have improved adherence and reduced costly hospital readmissions (Norris et al., 2002). These examples reveal that entrepreneurial activity is not an optional supplement to nursing practice but a vital component of high-quality, adaptive medical care. Specifically for nursing professionals, innovation and entrepreneurial competences are essential for facing complex challenges, fostering autonomy, leadership and adaptability (Copelli et al., 2019; McSherry et al., 2012) characteristics that contribute to the continuous improvement of health services. However, the development of these competences is hindered by significant obstacles, particularly organisational and cultural barriers that limit their expansion (Rastoka et al., 2022). This difficulty is exacerbated by a methodological gap: the lack of valid and specific instruments for their assessment across the entire health sector, a deficiency particularly acute in the nursing context (Costa et al., 2021; Jakobsen et al., 2021).

Consistently, international research on innovation and entrepreneurship in nursing remains relatively limited (Jakobsen et al., 2021), and the available empirical evidence in healthcare organisations often fails to focus explicitly on nursing practice or on nurses’ innovative and entrepreneurial behaviours (Bagheri and Akbari, 2018; Radaelli et al., 2014; Rigtering et al., 2023). Despite the topic's growing relevance and the increasing volume of literature on innovative and entrepreneurial competences in healthcare, particularly in nursing (Copelli et al., 2019), research remains limited on assessing these competences in healthcare professionals. Studies often focus on the conceptualization or perception of the necessary competences, rather than their formal evaluation (Amaral et al., 2025; Jakobsen et al., 2021) leaving a critical gap in understanding how these competences translate to clinical practice (Bagheri and Akbari, 2018). Consequently, the shortage of formal assessment of entrepreneurial and innovative competences not only highlights the urgent need for valid and reliable measurement tools, but also for evidence-based strategies to effectively inform their development in professional training (Costa et al., 2021).

The Entrepreneurship Competence Framework (EntreComp) has established itself as a pivotal European reference for cultivating an innovative and entrepreneurial society, informing a wide range of educational programmes across the continent (Bacigalupo et al., 2016). Crucially, EntreComp was designed precisely to transcend the classical Schumpeterian focus on business creation and market disruption. Rather than confining entrepreneurship to the domain of business creation, EntreComp reconceptualises it as a broad set of competences encompassing the ability to identify opportunities, mobilise resources, and translate ideas into action with meaningful economic, social, and environmental impact.

The framework is structured around three interconnected areas of competence: Ideas and Opportunities; Resources; and Into Action. Each area comprises five competences, fifteen in total, and each competence is further articulated through progressively complex statements organised across eight levels of learning outcomes. The first area, Ideas and Opportunities, addresses the capacity to spot opportunities, think creatively, develop a vision, recognise the value of ideas, and engage in ethical and sustainable thinking. The second area, Resources, focuses on self-awareness and self-efficacy, motivation and perseverance, the mobilisation of both material and human resources, and financial and economic literacy. The third area, Into Action, encompasses taking initiative, planning and management, coping with uncertainty, ambiguity and risk, collaborative working, and learning through experience.

As a competence reference framework, EntreComp offers a robust and theoretically grounded conceptual foundation. However, its application in empirical contexts, particularly in nursing education, requires careful operationalisation into concrete, measurable indicators capable of supporting systematic assessment.

Against this backdrop, the present study aims to develop an instrument to assess innovative and entrepreneurial competences in nurses, grounded in and operationalising the EntreComp framework.

Methods

Design

Following the recommendations by Boateng et al. (2018) For the development of a new instrument, we conducted a three-phase study from November 2024 to August 2025, that included: i) narrative literature review and focus groups to generate items tailored to healthcare contexts; (ii) modified Delphi panel (Keeney et al., 2011) for validation rounds with experts; (iii) pre-test (as shown in Fig. 1). This approach ensures the instrument is both theoretically robust and pragmatically relevant to nurses, daily realities.

Instrument development process.

Phase i – Narrative review and Focus Groups

In November 2024, 2 researchers from the study team independently conducted an initial narrative literature review (via EBSCOhost) and CINAHL (via EBSCOhost) out to identify tools for assessing innovative and entrepreneurial competences, as well as guiding references. The descriptors used were: (“entrepreneurial skills” OR “entrepreneurial competencies”) AND (“innovative skills” or “innovative competencies”) AND (“assessment instruments”).

It should be noted that all these instruments were presented to the members of the focus groups in case they wished to consult them, both for the formulation of the pool of items and the inclusion of information they considered relevant, and for the construction of the response scale.

The psychometric development of the instrument adhered to a rigorous methodological process focused on content validity and contextual relevance, in strict alignment with the EntreComp framework. This framework was selected because it simultaneously satisfies three essential criteria: (1) theoretical comprehensiveness, covering the full spectrum of entrepreneurial and innovative competences; (2) institutional legitimacy, having been developed through a twelve-phase methodological process commissioned by the European Commission; and (3) structural flexibility, permitting contextualisation to professional domains beyond business creation, including nursing.

The exhaustiveness of the item set was ensured through a two-stage logic: the EntreComp framework itself provides structural coverage through its three areas and fifteen competences, widely regarded as a comprehensive taxonomy of entrepreneurial competence (Bacigalupo et al., 2016); and the focus group process verified that this taxonomy was empirically relevant and recognisable to nursing professionals, confirming whether any dimension of nursing entrepreneurial practice was absent from the framework.

Phase ii – Modified Delphi Panels

The Modified Delphi panel was conducted from March to May 2025. This structured consensus process aimed to finalize the scale's design and format following initial item generation. The panel achieved agreement on the retention and precise wording of all items, as well as the essential structural elements including the title, participant instructions, and the exact specifications of the response scale (format and scoring rules). This step was critical for establishing content and face validity prior to large-scale data collection. Delphi technique is a structured consensus method that gathers expert judgements over repeated rounds until consensus is reached on a given issue (Keeney et al., 2011).

A purposive sampling strategy was adopted to recruit the expert panel for the modified Delphi study. Seven experts were selected based on their academic qualifications, professional experience, and recognized contributions to the fields of entrepreneurship, innovation, and competence development. Experts were identified through their institutional affiliations, publications, professional roles, and involvement in relevant projects and initiatives. Recruitment was conducted directly by the Principal Investigator via email.

The same inclusion criteria used in the focus group phase were applied. However, to ensure sample independence and minimize the risk of bias, individuals who had participated in the focus groups were excluded from the Delphi panel.

The formal invitation included an Informed Consent Form, which detailed the study objectives, the voluntary and confidential nature of participation, and the estimated time required. Consent was formally obtained through an affirmative response to the email and an affirmative response to the consent form.

The content validation procedure commenced with the implementation of five focus group sessions. These sessions involved a panel of professionals possessing a high level of expertise in the fields of innovation and entrepreneurship in healthcare. Participant selection was governed by strict inclusion criteria: being a professor/researcher and/or nurse with proven involvement and affiliation with health innovation and entrepreneurship projects and having over five years of professional experience in the area. Of the five sessions conducted, three took place in person (in-person format) and two were conducted in a synchronous online format.

The objectives of the sessions were sequential and cumulative. Focus Group 1 was dedicated to conceptual delimitation, aiming to clarify fundamental constructs, specifically "Innovation and Entrepreneurship in Nursing" and "Entrepreneurial and Innovative Competencies in Nursing," alongside a critical discussion of existing competence frameworks. Subsequently, focus group 2 focused on the detailed analysis and the cross-cultural and contextual adaptation of the EntreComp framework to the specific practice of Nursing, which resulted in the identification of an initial pool of potential items for the instrument. The aim was to identify the items regarding innovative and entrepreneurial skills that would make sense to include, considering the population for which the assessment instrument was intended. The subsequent sessions, focus group 3, 4, and 5, were wholly dedicated to the formulation, semantic refinement, and structuring of the items. Focus Group 3 was then convened to discuss the proposed concepts, scale structure, item wording, and scoring procedures, and to reach consensus on the revised version of the instrument. Item phrasing was standardized in the first person and contextualized for the clinical setting. with the cognitive structure of each item grounded in the different levels of Bloom's taxonomy.

Bloom's taxonomy was fundamental for the formulation of items in assessment instruments, as it guides the construction of questions that cover different cognitive levels, from memory to creation, ensuring greater validity (Bloom, 1956).

Finally, focus group 5 assumed the crucial responsibility of defining the response metric for the instrument. The consensus-building process achieved through these focus groups permitted the finalization of all instrument components, including the title, administration instructions, and the definitive response scale. A document was prepared with all the response scales used in the instruments found in the literature review.

The online Delphi rounds allowed participants to evaluate the instrument's items, title, instructions, and response scale. They used a 4-point Likert-type scale to rate each component, with the Content Validity Index (CVI) serving as the consensus metric. Only components that failed to achieve a CVI of 0.83 proceeded to the subsequent round (Lynn, 1986). Beyond evaluating individual items, experts were invited to identify any competence dimension missing from the instrument as a whole; the absence of such suggestions across both rounds provided additional evidence that the item pool constitutes a comprehensive operationalisation of the construct. Qualitative feedback was also collected to support rewording of unclear or ambiguous items. The KuboToolbox "Kubo" was used for data collection.

Phase iii – Pre-test

The instrument was pre-tested in august of 2025 with a sample of 15 participants from the target population, that is nurses performing duties in clinical practice, as described by the methodology of Boateng et al. (2018). Participants were recruited via email by the principal investigator of the project. Participants were asked to complete the instrument and, for each item, respond whether the item was understandable and appropriate dichotomously (yes/no). Beyond the quantitative scoring, the participants had the opportunity to provide qualitative feedback to support their rationale on each item to suggest rephrasing or even eliminating specific items. The KoboToolbox “Kubo” software was used for this phase. All results were analyzed by the research team and decisions were made in a meeting after discussion. If the research team does not reach a consensus on all items, or if >20% of the items in the instrument are considered inappropriate and incomprehensible, we will return to the previous phase to improve the instrument and then redo a pre-test.

The inclusion criteria for participants were being a nurse with clinical experience and fluent in both written and spoken European Portuguese. Those who had taken part in previous phases of the instrument's development were excluded.

Framework and sociodemographic questionnaire

EntreComp evaluates the progress of the teaching of entrepreneurial competences on the eight levels of progression, culminating in the 442 learning outcomes. It should be emphasised that, depending on the area in which this competency framework is applied, the results may vary, considering not only what is expected from the assessment but also the progression of teaching (Bacigalupo et al., 2016).

This model, developed by the European Commission, is structured around three fundamental areas – “Ideas and Opportunities”, “Resources” and “In Action” - each of includes five specific competences, assessable at eight levels of proficiency (Bacigalupo et al., 2016). Its construction was based on twelve robust methodological phases, aiming to serve as a reference for initiatives that promote entrepreneurial capacity in various contexts, including social, cultural and environmental (Dias-Trindade et al., 2020). The Portuguese version of the framework has been translated but has not been validated.

A socio-demographic questionnaire, constructed by the research team, was administered to the experts. It collected data on the following variables: age, gender, years in professional practice, academic qualifications, professional occupation, experience in innovation and entrepreneurship contexts, presence and implementation of innovative ideas, and entrepreneurs in the family.

The socio-demographic questionnaire for pre-test participants collected data on age, sex, academic qualifications, specialist career status, and years of professional practice.

Statistical analyses

The data analysis was carried out using IBM Corporation’s Statistical Package for the Social Sciences (SPSS) software version 24.0 in Somers, New York, USA. Descriptive statistics were employed, including measures of central tendency and dispersion. Quantitative data were summarized using mean (M), standard deviation (SD), minimum (Min.), and maximum (Max.), while nominal and ordinal data were presented as absolute (n) and percentage (%) frequencies.

The content validity of the instrument was determined by a panel of experts who rated the relevance of each item on a 4-point ordinal scale (1 = not relevant; 4 = highly relevant). For each item, the Individual Content Validity Index (I-CVI) was calculated, which corresponds to the proportion of jurists who rated the item with a 3 or 4. Items with an I-CVI below the acceptability threshold were subject to review or elimination, with a minimum acceptable score of 0.83 when there were >6 experts (Polit and Beck, 2006).

To assess the validity of the instrument, the Scale Content Validity Index (S-CVI) was calculated. Specifically, the average method (S-CVI/Ave) was used, which consists of the average of the I-CVIs of all items in the instrument. For the final instrument at this stage, the minimum acceptable S-CVI/Ave was set at 0.90 or higher, as suggested by the reference used (Polit and Beck, 2006).

Ethics

The study was favourably reviewed by the Ethics Committee of the Health Sciences Research Unit: Nursing at the Nursing School of Coimbra (references number P1075_09_2024). Ethical and legal standards were strictly followed throughout the study. The research was carried out in strict compliance with the Declaration of Helsinki.

All participants took part in the study on a voluntary basis. They were initially contacted via email by the lead researcher (I.F.A.), who provided detailed information about the study’s aims, procedures, and participation requirements, and clarified any questions regarding their involvement. Prior to participation, all individuals were fully informed about the purpose and nature of the research. Written informed consent was obtained from all participants before data collection commenced. Participants were also explicitly informed that their participation was voluntary and that they could withdraw from the study at any time without any penalty or adverse consequences.

Results

Sociodemographic characterization

Focus groups

The mean age of participants varied slightly across the groups. Focus Group 1 had the highest mean age at 46.8 years (SD=12.4), followed by focus Group 2 at 44.0 years (SD=10.9), and focus group 345 with the lowest mean at 40.75 years (SD=10.3). However, average professional experience was more consistent, ranging from 19.0 to 25.0 years across the groups.

In terms of gender, focus group1 stood out as the only group with a male majority (60.0%), whereas the other two groups were predominantly female (focus group 2: 60.0%; focus group 3: 75.0%). Academic qualifications were consistently high across all groups, with most specialists holding a doctoral degree (80.0% in focus group 1 and focus group 2, and 100.0% in focus group 3,4 and 5). The predominant occupation in all groups was teaching in the field of nursing.

Regarding their innovation and entrepreneurship profile, all participants across all focus groups (100.0%) reported having had innovative ideas. The implementation of these ideas, however, varied considerably: 60.0% in focus group 1.4% in focus group 2, and 75.0% in focus group 3, 4 and 5. Their connection to innovation was primarily through teaching and research. The presence of entrepreneurs in the family was a common trait.

Delphi panels

The panel's comprised seven participants, the average age was 43.7 years (SD=11.1), with a range of 30.0 – 64.0 years. Participants had an average of 19.0 years of professional experience (SD=12.9), ranging from 7.0 to 44.0 years. In terms of gender, the panel was predominantly male, with four male participants (57.1%) and three females (42.9%).

Regarding educational level, most panel members (71.4%) held a doctoral degree, while the remaining 28.6% had a master's degree. Professional roles varied, with most participants being researchers in the innovation and entrepreneurship in nursing (57.1%), followed by nurses in the clinical field (28.6%), and one teacher also in the area (14.3%). The connection to innovation and entrepreneurship was primarily through teaching (57.1%) and research (28.6%), with one participant (14.3%) reporting a different connection, particularly in clinical practice. Data on entrepreneurs in the family were not collected for this panel.

Pre-test

The pre-test sample, consisting of 15 participants, was predominantly female (n = 14; 93.3%). Participants had an average age of 35.5 years and an average of 10.9 years of professional experience in nursing or teaching nursing. In terms of academic qualifications, 60.0% of the sample held a master's degree or specialization, while the remaining 40.0% had a bachelor's degree.

Stages of the study

Narrative review of assessment instruments and focus groups

Although several instruments have been developed to assess entrepreneurial and innovative competences, no validated tool was identified that specifically operationalises the EntreComp framework for practicing nurses and the healthcare context. The literature search identified seven assessment tools with heterogeneous aims, target populations, and theoretical underpinnings (Armuña et al., 2020; Bravo et al., 2024; Castanheira, 2022; Cualheta et al., 2020; Fabio et al., 2016; Morselli and Gorenc, 2022; Štemberger and Žakelj, 2021).

One of the instruments (Armuña et al., 2020) focused on students in Science, Technology, Engineering, and Mathematics fields and assesses entrepreneurship through four dimensions: Ideas and Opportunities, Personal Resources, Specific Knowledge, and Into Action. Internal consistency values or other psychometric properties were not reported.

The second (Cualheta et al., 2020), developed in Brazil for undergraduate students, has 35 items and five dimensions, which include: Ability to develop business models, Sell and take risks, Identify opportunities, Accept mistakes, and Work in teams. The internal consistency of the dimensions ranged from 0.83 – 0.95, using an 11-point Likert-type scale.

Other created in Slovenia, assesses primary school educators. It consists of 24 items, five dimensions (Searching for ideas; Planning and realisation of one’s ideas; Responsible behaviour; Initiative and Teamwork;), and a five-point Likert scale. The instrument demonstrated high overall internal consistency (α = 0.934), with dimensions ranging from 0.73 – 0.85 (Štemberger and Žakelj, 2021).

The fourth (Bravo et al., 2024), was developed for nursing students in Spain and consists of 23 items and four dimensions (Problem management; Risk acceptance; Creativity; Personal confidence), with a four-point scale. The overall reliability was 0.89, and the dimensions ranged from 0.46 – 0.76.

The fifth (Fabio et al., 2016), focused on entrepreneurship, leadership, and professionalism domains in the Spanish nursing students. It has 9 items, with three items for each domain. The total internal consistency was 0.78, and the dimensions ranged from 0.46 – 0.78, using a five-point Likert scale.

Additionally, an instrument developed for higher education and Erasmus students in Portugal also lacked reported internal consistency values or other psychometric validation parameters (Castanheira, 2022). This instrument has 12 items and 3 dimentions (Innovation and Creativity; Personal Resources; Social Sustainability).

Finally, an instrument used for the assessment of entrepreneurship education, originally developed for Italy and Slovenia, also lacked reported internal consistency values for its 15 items and three dimensions (Ideas and opportunities; Resources; In action) The same of EntreComp (Morselli and Gorenc, 2022).

Now, regarding the focus groups, the focus group 1 aimed to analyse the assessment tools found through the narrative review and the EntreComp Framework as a possible tool capable of assessing the innovative and entrepreneurial skills of Portuguese nurses. Participants reported that the EntreComp framework, as it stood, was too long, the model statements were confusing and complex, and the response levels were difficult.

Based on the results of focus group 1, focus group 2 was also conducted with five participants (four from focus group1 and one new member). In this focus group, participants were given, in addition to the informed consent form and the sociodemographic questionnaire, a table with all 60 items from the EntreComp framework and their 44 descriptors.

An initial exploratory meeting was held with the expert group to introduce the aims of the work and discuss preliminary directions for the instrument. Following this session, each expert independently reviewed the draft instrument and provided individual feedback. All contributions were submitted to the research team, which collated the inputs, organised them thematically, and consolidated suggestions into item pools.

In this focus group 3, the experts proposed that the items be written in the first-person singular and in the infinitive, adding the expression “in my practice” to make it easier to direct the population of interest to the phenomenon under study.

Consensus was reached on 5 of the 15 EntreComp innovative and entrepreneurial competencies. The entire first dimension (Ideas and opportunities), resulting in 20 items.

In focus group 4, consensus was reached on five more of the 15 EntreComp innovative and entrepreneurial competencies, i.e., the entire second dimension (In action), resulting in 26 items. In three of the items, it was necessary to restructure them into two separate items due to the presence of two distinct ideas in the same item.

In focus group 5, consensus was reached on the last 5 of the 15 innovative and entrepreneurial competencies of EntreComp, i.e., the entire third dimension (Resources), which was left with 23 items. In this focus group 5, the possible response scale for the instrument was also discussed. Thus, the response scale selected was a 7-point Likert-type scale ranging from no aptitude to full aptitude.

This resulted in a 3-areas instrument with 15 dimensions, a total of 69 items, and a 7-point type Likert-type response scale “supplementary material table 1″.

Delphi panel

During the first Delphi round, the name, instructions, response scale of the instrument and 19 items failed to attain the minimum I-CVI level. Thus, the name of the instrument became “Questionnaire on entrepreneurial and innovative skills in health,” the instructions became more concise, and the response scale remained from 1 (completely disagree) to 7 (completely agree). However, for all items that did not reach the I-CVI (ranging between 0.429 and 1) and the S-CVI was 0.871, options for reformulation were provided by the research team.

A second round of the panel was therefore conducted which included their amendment according to the experts’ suggestions. In the second round, CVI was successfully reached for all items and the instrument’s name, instructions and response scale were aligning with the predefined criteria. The CVI fluctuated between 0.857 and 1, with an average CVI of 0.97 as can be seen in Table 1.

Pre-test

The pre-test with a sample of the target population made it possible to verify the ease of understand and appropriate of the items for the target population ranging from 86.7 to 100%. However, minor adjustments were made to the semantics of seven items to improve their understanding by the target population.

In the first item, formulated as “I analyze the context to anticipate needs in clinical practice” the word “surrounding” was added after “context.” In the second item, “I recognize opportunities for improvement in clinical practice,” the word ‘recognize’ was changed to “I am able to recognize.” In the third item, “I take advantage of opportunities to create value by exploring the cultural, social, and economic context,” the phrase “where I am” was added at the end of the item, while in the fourth item, “I establish innovative connections to respond to challenges in clinical practice,” the phrase “partnerships and/or” was added before the word “connections.” Also, in item 12, “I have a vision to transform ideas into action,” the word “to” was replaced with “that can.”

In item 21, “I make responsible and ethical decisions,” the words “and ethical” were removed and “and in accordance with the ethical principles that govern my profession” was added, while in item 22, “I reflect on my short-, medium-, and long-term aspirations,” the word ‘aspirations’ was replaced by “ambitions”.

Discussion

This study contributes to the literature by developing a content-valid assessment tool for innovation and entrepreneurship competencies in healthcare, which is expected to demonstrate validity and reliability in future applications. The narrative review confirmed the scarcity of valid instruments specific to nurses, corroborating the existing literature (Costa et al., 2021; Jakobsen et al., 2021). Most of the tools identified, such as that of Armuña et al. (2020), are geared toward business contexts or students in areas such as Science, Technology, Engineering, and Mathematics, rather than clinical practice (Bagheri and Akbari, 2018). Analysis of the internal consistency of some instruments revealed inadequate values (between 0.46 and 0.78) (Bravo et al., 2024; Fabio et al., 2016), and the instrument with the context closest to the Portuguese population did not show any reliability value.

The use of the EntreComp framework as a theoretical basis is a strong point of the study, as it is a robust model recognized by the European Commission for the development of entrepreneurial skills (Cualheta et al., 2020). The lack of reliable data or insufficient values in existing tools make them unfeasible for rigorous evaluation, which validated the creation of a new instrument based on this robust framework.

The adaptation of this model to nursing, through focus groups, allowed for the contextualization of items, which is crucial to ensure the practical relevance of the instrument. The same method was used to validate items from other measurement instruments constructed using the same methodology (Heggdal et al., 2025) as well as consecutively using the Delphi panel methodology for content validation (Wang et al., 2019). It is worth noting that the EntreComp framework, although designed within a European policy context, reflects values and competence profiles that may not be universally applicable across all cultural settings. Cultural dimensions such as uncertainty avoidance, power distance, and collectivist versus individualist orientations, as described in cross-cultural management literature, may influence how nurses perceive and enact entrepreneurial competences, and consequently how they respond to items framed around initiative-taking, risk acceptance, or autonomous decision-making. The Portuguese cultural context, characterized by moderate-to-high uncertainty avoidance and a relatively hierarchical healthcare system, may shape the expression of certain EntreComp competences differently from Northern European or Anglo-Saxon contexts in which much of the entrepreneurship education literature is grounded. The focus groups conducted in this study served not only to contextualize item language but also to surface these culturally embedded assumptions, allowing adjustments that reflect how innovation and entrepreneurship are understood and practiced within the Portuguese nursing workforce. Future cross-cultural adaptations of the instrument should explicitly examine whether the EntreComp competence structure retains its factorial validity across different national and professional cultures, given that construct equivalence cannot be assumed a priori.

The Delphi Panel phase was fundamental to the methodological rigor of the study. Achieving an average CVI of 0.96 in the second round demonstrates a high level of consensus and content validity among experts (Armuña et al., 2020). This ensures that the items in the instrument are relevant and adequately represent the construct to be measured.

The decision to rewrite the items in the first-person singular and with the expression “in my practice” reflects a sensitive and pragmatic approach, facilitating nurses' identification with the concepts evaluated. Also, the formulation of all items in the positive facilitates the response to items. According to the literature, this type of item formation demonstrates greater discrimination than negatively worded items, and that the combination of positive and negative items introduces methodological factors that undermine the validity of the measurement (Zeng et al., 2024).

The inclusion of a pretest with the target audience allowed for final semantic adjustments, ensuring the understanding and relevance of the items in clinical practice, with ease of understanding ranging from 86.7% to 100%.

Future studies should first focus on thoroughly evaluating the psychometric properties of the instrument, including its reliability, construct validity, and internal consistency, to ensure its robustness as a measurement tool. Only after establishing these fundamental properties should longitudinal studies be conducted to determine the instrument’s sensitivity to change, for example, by assessing nurses’ competencies before and after participation in educational programs. The rigorous development process followed so far reinforces the instrument’s content validity and its potential to serve as a reliable tool for assessing innovation and entrepreneurship skills among Portuguese nurses.

We would also like to add that following the completion of the full psychometric validation process, an English version of the instrument will be made available to facilitate access by the international academic community and to support future cross-cultural adaptations, translations, and validation studies.

Limitations

One limitation of this study is the reliance on convenience sampling across all stages, which may restrict the generalizability of the findings and the representativeness of the participants, particularly in the pre-test phase. Although the pre-test sample size was adequate, it might not fully reflect the characteristics of the target population. Furthermore, some decisions regarding the post-pre-test item reformulation were based on the research team's judgment. While this ensured methodological coherence, it introduced a potential source of bias.

Conclusions

In conclusion, this study represents a pivotal step in addressing the need for robust instruments to assess innovation and entrepreneurship competencies within the healthcare field, with a particular focus on nursing. The developed tool is grounded in sound theoretical foundations. Its content has been affirmed through expert validation, and its initial acceptance was confirmed during pre-testing. Thus, we can state that, based on the EntreComp, we have developed a tool that is valid both in terms of content and vocabulary for nurses, which had not existed until then.

While comprehensive psychometric validation studies remain necessary to fully establish the instrument's reliability and validity, we anticipate that this article will draw significant scholarly attention to the relevance and critical development of this theme.
