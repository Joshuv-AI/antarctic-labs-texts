Postoperative Complications Following LSCS: Incidence, Risk Factors, and Management—A Prospective Observational Study from the Bundelkhand Region
Authors: Priyanka Patel, Siftie Kaur Banga, Simran Jain, Praveen Khare, Shweta Bhatnagar, Shailendra Patel, Poornima Patel
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Background:Lower Segment Cesarean Section (LSCS) is a common obstetric procedure, yet it carries a significant risk of postoperative complications, especially in resource-limited settings like Bundelkhand. This study aimed to assess the incidence and risk factors of post-LSCS complications and discuss management strategies for improved maternal outcomes.Methods:This prospective observational study was conducted on 300 post-LSCS patients at a tertiary care center over one year. Data on patient demographics, type of LSCS, comorbidities, and postoperative complications were collected and analyzed using SPSS Version 25.0 (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA).Results:32% of patients experienced complications, with surgical site infection (12%) being most common. Emergency LSCS and anemia were significantly associated with higher morbidity (P < 0.05). Most complications were managed conservatively.Conclusion:Post-LSCS morbidity remains high in emergency cases and anemic patients. Early identification, standardized surgical protocols, and region-specific strategies are essential to reduce complications and improve maternal health in underserved areas.

INTRODUCTION

Lower Segment Cesarean Section (LSCS) is one of the most frequently performed surgical procedures in obstetrics and has contributed significantly to the decline in maternal and neonatal mortality worldwide. However, with the rising cesarean section rates—often exceeding the WHO-recommended threshold of 10–15%—there is a concomitant increase in the burden of postoperative complications, which range from mild infections to life-threatening conditions such as postpartum hemorrhage, thromboembolism, and sepsis.[1]

Postoperative complications following LSCS can adversely affect both maternal recovery and neonatal outcomes. Several factors have been identified that predispose to these complications, including pre-existing maternal comorbidities (anemia, obesity, hypertension, diabetes), emergency vs. elective surgery status, intraoperative technical difficulties, inadequate aseptic protocols, and limited access to skilled postoperative care. In resource-limited regions such as Bundelkhand, these factors are compounded by socio-economic disparities, poor antenatal care coverage, and infrastructural constraints in healthcare delivery.[2]

Timely recognition and management of post-LSCS complications are critical to preventing long-term morbidity and ensuring optimal maternal health. Interventions such as early mobilization, rational antibiotic usage, standardized surgical protocols, and effective postnatal follow-up play a pivotal role. Regional data on the incidence and nature of these complications is essential to develop context-specific guidelines and improve obstetric outcomes.[3]

The present study aims to assess the incidence of post-LSCS complications in the Bundelkhand region, identify the underlying contributing factors, and propose evidence-based management strategies tailored to local healthcare realities. By doing so, it seeks to bridge the gap between clinical practice and public health needs in this underserved population.

METHODOLOGY

Study design and setting

This was a prospective observational study conducted in the Department of Obstetrics and Gynecology at a tertiary care teaching hospital in the Bundelkhand region over a period of 12 months.

Study population

All women who underwent LSCS during the study period were considered for inclusion. Patients were followed up for postoperative complications until discharge and at a two-week postnatal visit.

Data collection

Data were collected using a structured proforma, including demographic details, antenatal history, indication for LSCS, intraoperative findings, postoperative course, and documented complications. Both clinical examination and hospital records were used.

Outcome measures

Primary outcome: incidence and type of postoperative complications.

Secondary outcome: associated risk factors and management strategies employed.

Statistical analysis

Data were entered in Microsoft Excel (Microsoft Corp., Redmond, WA, USA) and analyzed using IBM SPSS Statistics for Windows, Version 25.0 (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA). Descriptive statistics were used for incidence and frequency. Chi-square test and logistic regression were applied to assess associations between complications and risk factors. A P-value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

RESULTS

A total of 300 patients who underwent LSCS were included in the study. The mean age of the study population was 26.8 ± 4.2 years, with the majority (58.3%) in the 21–30 years age group. Among them, 182 (60.7%) were emergency LSCS, while 118 (39.3%) were elective procedures.

Out of the total, 96 patients (32%) developed one or more postoperative complications. The most common complication observed was surgical site infection (SSI) in 36 patients (12%), followed by postpartum hemorrhage (PPH) in 18 patients (6%), febrile morbidity in 14 patients (4.7%), urinary tract infection (UTI) in 10 patients (3.3%), and wound dehiscence in 8 patients (2.7%) [Table 1].

DISCUSSION

This study highlights the considerable burden of postoperative complications following LSCS, with a complication rate of 32%. Similar trends have been reported in Indian and global studies, particularly in resource-constrained regions, where access to antenatal care and operative hygiene practices may be suboptimal.[4]

Emergency LSCS was associated with a significantly higher complication rate (38.5%) compared to elective procedures (22%). This aligns with the findings of Ghazi et al.,[5] who reported increased morbidity in emergency cases due to poor patient preparation, prolonged labor, and intraoperative challenges. Surgical Site Infections (SSIs) were the most prevalent complication in our study (12%), which is comparable to the 10–15% range reported by Zandvakili et al.,[6] and indicates the persistent need for rigorous aseptic techniques and postoperative wound care protocols.

Anemia was strongly associated with increased postoperative morbidity, particularly SSIs and delayed wound healing. Studies have shown that hemoglobin levels below 10 g/dL significantly impair healing capacity and increase susceptibility to infections.[7] Febrile morbidity (4.7%) and urinary tract infections (3.3%) were managed effectively with antibiotic therapy and routine monitoring, consistent with management protocols outlined by Ronsmans and colleagues.[8]

No maternal mortality was observed, which is encouraging and reflects the effectiveness of prompt diagnosis and intervention strategies in preventing escalation. However, the necessity for surgical re-intervention in 1% of patients indicates the importance of improving perioperative counseling and early detection systems.

In conclusion, the study underscores the urgent need for strengthening obstetric surgical training, anemia management during antenatal care, and adherence to standardized protocols, especially in underserved regions like Bundelkhand. Tailoring interventions to local healthcare realities can contribute significantly to reducing LSCS-related morbidity and enhancing maternal outcomes.

CONCLUSION

The study reveals a substantial incidence of postoperative complications following LSCS, particularly among emergency cases and anemic patients. Surgical site infections were the most common, highlighting the need for improved intraoperative asepsis and postoperative care. Strengthening antenatal screening, anemia correction, and standardized surgical protocols can significantly reduce maternal morbidity. Tailored strategies for regions like Bundelkhand are essential to ensure safe cesarean outcomes and enhance overall maternal health.
