Impact of digital games on early creative expression in children: longitudinal study based on experimental intervention
Authors: Wenli Zhang, SiJie Sun, Wenwuyu Gao, Pei Chen
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectiveExploring the impact of digital games on children’s early creative expression.Method300 kids aged 4–6 were randomly divided into an experimental group (EG, n = 150) and a control group (CG, n = 150). The EG received a 16-week intervention with three types of digital games (creative painting, digital scenarios, story creation), 30 min per session, three times a week; the CG followed regular activities. Using a pre-test and post-test control group design, the pre-test measured initial creativity (drawing creation, storytelling) via the self-developed "Early Childhood Creative Expression Ability Assessment Scale" and game process observation; the post-test was conducted at the experiment’s end. Quantitative data were analyzed with SPSS26.0, and qualitative data via thematic analysis.ResultsBefore intervention, there was no significant difference in creative expression or game process observation between the two groups (P > 0.05). After intervention, the EG outperformed the CG significantly in drawing innovativeness (P < 0.001), storytelling plot richness (P < 0.001), and most dimensions of drawing creative expression, storytelling multidimensionality, and game process observation (P < 0.05); the EG also showed significant pre-post improvements in all dimensions (P < 0.001), while no gender difference was found (P > 0.05).ConclusionDigital games significantly enhance children’s early creative expression, providing a basis for applying digital resources in early childhood education and optimizing teaching methods to promote children’s creativity.Keywords: Children, Digital games, Creative expression, Drawing creation, Storytelling

Introduction

As information technology advances at a quick pace, digital games have gained popularity in children’s growing environments and have become an essential element of their everyday lives [1]. How to accurately identify the impact of digital games on children’s early development, particularly in the role of creative expression, has become a crucial issue that requires immediate attention as a result of this phenomenon, which has drawn a lot of attention from academics in a variety of fields, including psychology and education [2, 3]. In today’s educational context that emphasizes the cultivation of innovative talents, creative expression is the cornerstone of innovation ability, and exploring the association between digital games and children’s early creative expression is in line with the hot trend of the industry to enhance the innovative literacy of the whole population and help cultivate talents at an early stage [4]. Understanding whether and how digital games can stimulate and promote the transformation of children’s creative thinking into creative expression is not only related to the comprehensive development of individual children, but also provides a theoretical basis for the practice of educational innovation and the child-friendly development of the game industry, which has far-reaching practical significance [5].

Existing research has formed three major exploration directions around the theme of digital games: The first is to focus on the support of digital game design for children’s learning. For instance, the multi-perception interactive digital jigsaw puzzle game developed by Wang et al. [6], although it has confirmed the positive effects of the tangible interface and audio-visual effects, the measurement of children’s subjective experience is limited by their expressive ability and lacks depth. Second, explore the enhancement of specific skills through gamified learning. For instance, Yang et al. [7] integrated the "prediction-observation-explanation" strategy into the mathematical game system. Although it improved academic performance and attention, it lacked verification of long-term memory effects and interdisciplinary applicability. Thirdly, the effect of cognitive intervention games was verified. For instance, the mixed cognitive training program proposed by Shabanali Fami et al. [8] improved the cognitive and academic abilities of six children aged 10–13, but the sample size was extremely small and could not represent the structure of the children’s group. Overall, the existing research has significant common flaws: the research cycles are mostly short, and they are unable to capture the dynamic development process of creative expression,The research dimensions are fragmented, mostly focusing on a single cognition or skill, while neglecting the synergy of cognition, emotion, and social interaction required for creative expression [9, 10],The methodological rigor is insufficient, or the samples lack representativeness, or the experimental design does not exclude confounding variables. For example, [11] adopted a single-group pre—and post-test design and was unable to control irrelevant interference. There is a disconnection between theory and practice. Most studies have not transformed the conclusions into specific game choices and educational guidance strategies, making it difficult to provide actionable suggestions for parents and educators [12, 13]. Based on this, this study is based on the multi-dimensional theory of creative development. Through a 16-week longitudinal experimental intervention, three types of games, namely creative painting, digital scenarios, and story creation, were carefully selected. Combined with multi-source data such as standardized scales (measuring cognitive flexibility, creative conception, etc.), classroom observations, and teacher feedback, The system reveals the intrinsic connection between digital games and children’s early creative expression. Its core contribution lies in filling the gap of long-term, multi-dimensional, and methodologically rigorous empirical research, providing a scientific basis for the application of digital resources in early education and the child-friendly design of the game industry.

The research team believes that the impact of digital games on early creative expression in children depends on two key variables: game design characteristics and intervention duration. Not all digital games can promote creative expression. Games with strong preset rules and a lack of open creative space may limit children’s imagination. However, the creative drawing and story creation games selected in this study are designed with low rule constraints and high autonomy, which better meet the needs of children’s creative expression. At the same time, existing short-term research is difficult to capture the dynamic development of creative expression. Therefore, this study adheres to a 16 week longitudinal intervention design, believing that long-term and systematic digital game exposure can more truly reveal its sustained impact on children’s creative expression. In addition, the research team also advocates that evaluating children’s creative expression should be combined with the output of results (such as paintings, story content) and process performance (such as creative generation and problem-solving in games). A single dimensional evaluation cannot fully reflect the intervention effect, which is also an important basis for the integrated scale evaluation and game process observation in this study.

From an academic value perspective, this research focuses on the less systematically explored field of digital games and early creative expression in children aged 4–6. Through a 16 week longitudinal intervention and multidimensional evaluation (drawing, storytelling, game process), it can fill the gap in existing research with more short-term designs, less long-term tracking, more single dimensions, and less collaborative evaluation, clarify the causal relationship between digital games and children’s creative expression, and provide empirical support for the digital environment theory of children’s creative development. From a practical perspective, answering research questions can provide specific guidance for kindergarten education. Clearly define the types of digital games that can be introduced, intervention duration (3 times a week, 30 min each time), and implementation methods (combined with group sharing) to help educators scientifically utilize digital resources. At the same time, provide reference for parents and guide them to choose high-quality creative digital games during parent–child time; In addition, it can provide direction for the design of child friendly products in the gaming industry, promote the development of educational and creative children’s digital games, and ultimately help the entire population to achieve the educational goal of cultivating innovative literacy from an early stage.

Methods and materials

Test subject and ethical approval information

With a mean age of 5.2 years and an age range of 4–6 years, 300 youngsters in all were enlisted as study participants. The inclusion criteria were as follows: (1) Children need to be enrolled in regular kindergartens with relatively stable learning and living environments. (2) The children should have basic verbal communication skills and be able to understand simple instructions and express themselves to a certain extent. (3) There is no obvious cognitive developmental disorder or special educational needs [14]. Exclusion criteria include: (1) Subjects have had frequent school transfers in the last 6 months. (2) Subjects have received professional and systematic training courses on creative thinking. (3) The subject suffers from a serious physical or psychological illness that prevents him/her from participating in the experimental activities.

This study has been formally approved by the Institutional Review Board (IRB) of Changzhou Institute of Technology, with the approval number 2024CIT0906. The entire research process strictly adhered to the ethical guidelines regarding research involving human subjects (especially minors) as stipulated in the "World Medical Association" (2013). Before recruiting participants aged 4 to 6, the research team provided written informed consent documents to the parents or legal guardians of all children. The documents detailed the research purpose (exploring the impact of digital games on children’s early creative expression) and the specific experimental process (a 16-week intervention period, three 30-min digital game activities per week). The pre—and post-test contents include painting creation and story-telling tasks, potential risks (no known risks at the physical or psychological level have been assessed), data processing methods (all personal information and test data involving children have been anonymized and are only used for statistical analysis in this study and not disclosed to the public), and the right to withdraw (guardians can unconditionally withdraw their consent at any stage of the experiment Children can also express their willingness to withdraw independently, and none of the above behaviors will affect their normal learning and activities in the kindergarten. After the guardians have fully read and understood the content of the documents, the research team will answer all their questions. Only after the guardians voluntarily sign the written informed consent form will the children be included in the formal experimental samples, ensuring the legitimate rights and interests as well as the physical and mental health of minors throughout the process. In addition to obtaining the written informed consent of the children’s parents or legal guardians, the research team fully considered the cognitive characteristics and autonomous will of children aged 4 to 6, and referred to the requirement of "respecting the subjectivity of children" in the "Ethical Guidelines for Child Psychology Research". By using picture cards (simple illustrations showing experimental sections such as number games and painting creation) combined with colloquial expressions (such as "We will play small games of drawing and making up stories together. If you want to participate, you can. If you don’t want to, you can tell the teacher at any time"), explain the experimental content and participation rights to children in a simple and understandable way. Only after children clearly express their willingness to participate through nodding, verbal responses, etc., will they be included in the experiment. During the experiment, if children show resistance (such as refusing to operate the game or being unwilling to complete the task), researchers will immediately terminate their participation, ensuring children’s right to make independent choices on the basis of knowledge throughout the process and strengthening the child-centered ethical considerations of the research.

Experimental design

The study randomly divided the recruited children into EG and CG of 150 each, and the grouping results are shown in Table 1. The study adopted a Pre-T and Pos-T CG experimental design, in which the experimental period lasted for 16 weeks, with three experimental activities per week, each lasting about 30 min. In the Pre-T phase, children in both groups were required to complete a standardized set of creative expression ability tests covering drawing creation and storytelling, and the game process was observed and recorded to obtain data on their initial creativity level [15]. The EG received digital games intervention during the follow-up experiment, while the CG carried out daily activities according to the regular kindergarten curriculum schedule without exposure to experiment-related digital games. At the end of the experiment, the two groups again took the same Pos-T of creative expression ability, and by comparing the Pre-T and Pos-T data, the intervention effect of digital games on children’s creative expression in the EG was analyzed.

Experimental interventions

The children in the EG chose the digital games intervention program, with a variety of game types. Creative drawing games included Children’s Graffiti Paradise and Finger Painting Paradise, which provided a variety of drawing tools and materials to encourage children to create freely. The digital scenario games included Future City Explorer and Fairytale Town Builder, where children could design buildings and plan streets in a virtual city, and utilized their spatial imagination and planning skills. There were also story creation games, including Fairy Tale Adventures and Superhero Legends, in which children were given characters and a simple plot framework to guide them in conceptualizing the development of the story [16]. The difficulty of the game gradually increased according to the children’s age, from the initial simple graphic drawing and basic scene construction to the later complex story logic arrangement and three-dimensional space design. During the experimental period, the researchers accompanied and guided the children to ensure that they operated the game correctly. A weekly group sharing discussion was also organized to encourage children to exchange creative ideas in the game and stimulate more inspirational collisions. After each game, the children’s key behavioral performance, innovative points, and other data in the game were recorded [17].

Experimental data statistics and analysis

This study adopted the self-developed “Early Childhood Creative Expression Ability Assessment Scale” as the pre—and post-test tool, which included two sub-tests: painting creation and story-telling. Moreover, the pre—and post-test tools remained consistent in terms of measurement dimensions and task requirements. First, the content of the question: The sub-test question for the painting creation is “Please draw a ‘magic garden in your imagination’, and you can freely add any elements you like. The completion time is 20 min”. The story-telling sub-test question is: “Here is a card with a picture of ‘a singing duckling’. Please make up a complete story based on this card, which should include the duckling’s experiences, the things it encounters and the final outcome. The telling time is 5 to 8 min”. Second, the indicators of question discrimination and difficulty: Through a pre-experiment (selecting 40 children of the same age as the formal samples who did not participate in the formal experiment), a project analysis was conducted using classical measurement theories. In the sub-test of painting creation, the discrimination index (D value) of the "innovation" dimension was 0.33–0.46 (D ≥ 0.2 was considered good discrimination). The difficulty index (G value) is 0.49–0.57 (a G value of 0.4–0.6 indicates medium difficulty, which is suitable for the cognitive level of children aged 4–6). The D value of the "Color Application" dimension is 0.29–0.42, and the G value is 0.46–0.59. The D value of the "composition complexity" dimension is 0.31–0.44, and the G value is 0.47–0.58. In the story-telling sub-test, the D value of the "plot richness" dimension was 0.32–0.45, and the G value was 0.43–0.54. The D value of the "logical coherence" dimension is 0.30–0.43, and the G value is 0.45–0.56. The D value of the "Vocabulary Usage" dimension ranges from 0.28 to 0.41, and the G value ranges from 0.44 to 0.57. None of the questions showed insufficient discrimination or excessive difficulty. Thirdly, the equivalence of the questions before and after the tests: A review panel composed of three experts in child development psychology and two senior kindergarten teachers evaluated the content consistency of the questions before and after the tests. The Kappa coefficient was 0.87 (P < 0.001), confirming that the coverage of dimensions and the difficulty of tasks were highly consistent. Twenty children in the pre-experiment were retested with an interval of two weeks. The retest reliability r of the drawing subtest was 0.82, that of the storytelling subtest was 0.80, and that of the overall scale was 0.83 (P < 0.001), indicating that the questions before and after the test were equivalent, which could effectively compare the changes in children’s creative expression ability before and after the experiment.

The type of experimental data included quantitative data and qualitative data. In terms of quantitative data, the Pre-T and Pos-T creative expression ability tests were scored, including painting works based on innovativeness, color application, and compositional complexity dimensions [18]. The statistical program SPSS26.0 was used in the study to examine the data. To ascertain whether the digital games intervention had a meaningful impact, the mean values (MVs) of the Pre-T and Pos-Ts of the EG and the CG were compared using an independent samples t-test (P < 0.05). Meanwhile, the paired-samples t-test was used to analyze the changes in the EG’s own Pre-T and Pos-Ts. The qualitative data were derived from observation records of children’s play, transcription of group discussions, and so on. Thematic analysis was used to refine the themes, patterns and changing trends of children’s creative expressions, including the unique images that frequently appeared in creative drawing games and the novel story lines in story creation. Game process observation scale

This study’s assessment of indicators such as “the frequency of generating new ideas”, “drawing on others’ creativity and expanding it”, and “the diversity of problem-solving methods” for children’s creativity has clear and effective support: The frequency of generating new ideas corresponds to the “fluency” dimension of creativity. Bulut et al. [11] confirmed that it is significantly positively correlated with children’s creative thinking scores (r = 0.76, P < 0.001). “Drawing on others’ creativity and expanding it” aligns with the concept of “creative integration” proposed by Montuori et al. [10]. Their research shows that this indicator is highly correlated with the cognitive innovation ability of preschool children. Moreover, the pre-experimental data of this study indicate that the correlation coefficients between these indicators and the scores of painting innovation and story plot richness are both greater than 0.65 (P < 0.001), fully demonstrating the rationality of their measurement of creativity.

From the qualitative observation of the game process and the artwork, it can be seen that in the creative painting game, the experimental group’s children’s intervention in the later stage showed a significant personalized creative extension in their works. 82% of children will incorporate future architectural elements from digital scene games into magical garden paintings, designing unique images such as gardens with flying stairs and glowing flower castles, and actively explaining the source of their creativity to researchers. In the story creation game, the experimental group children often break through the preset framework and add original subplots to the singing duckling. During group discussions, there were multiple instances of active creative communication about wanting to add a talking stone and what the duckling would do if it encountered a storm. The children in the control group tend to repeat the fixed patterns of the sun, flowers, and grass in their paintings, and the storytelling is mostly simple structures with few original details. In addition, in the problem solving scene, when children in the experimental group were unable to complete the scene construction, they would actively try to change elements, ask for help from their peers and adjust plans, while children in the control group mostly gave up or waited for teachers’ help directly. These qualitative performances further confirmed the improvement of creative expression and related abilities of children in the experimental group.

Difference test

The difference in Pos-T creative expression ability between the EG and the CG is shown in Fig. 1. Figure 1 (a) showed the comparison of Pos-T differences in painting works. In terms of innovativeness, the EG was 7.01 ± 2.54, while the CG was 5.36 ± 2.16, P < 0.001. The color application EG was 7.16 ± 3.21, while the CG was 5.89 ± 4.03, P = 0.003. In the dimension of the complexity of painting composition, the post-test mean of the EG (7.36 ± 2.57) was slightly higher than that of the CG (7.02 ± 3.12), but P = 0.304 > 0.05, indicating that there was no statistically significant difference between the two groups in this dimension. This suggests that the intervention of digital games has no significant effect on improving the complexity of children’s painting composition. The total score of the painting works in the EG was 21.53 ± 5.78, while the CG was 18.27 ± 8.67, P < 0.001. Figure 1 (b) showed the comparison of Pos-T differences in storytelling. The richness of plot EG had an MV of 6.28 ± 1.19, while the CG had an MV of 5.28 ± 2.19, P < 0.001. The logical coherence of the EG was 5.98 ± 2.03, while that of the CG was 4.78 ± 2.23, P < 0.001. The vocabulary usage in the EG was 6.95 ± 1.68, while in the CG it was 5.84 ± 2.08, P < 0.001. The total score of storytelling in the EG was 19.21 ± 6.38, while in the CG it was 15.90 ± 7.38, P < 0.001. The total scores of the two groups were 40.74 ± 10.35 in the EG and 34.17 ± 16.24 in the CG, P < 0.001. As a result, the EG significantly outperformed the CG in terms of creative expression on multiple dimensions of drawing work and storytelling.

Overall, the EG scored better than the CG in several dimensions of creativity stimulation performance, problem solving ability, team collaboration and interaction. Moreover, there was a SSD in most of the dimensions (P < 0.05), and the SSD in only the dimension of proactive communication was not significant (P > 0.05).

Discussion

In today’s digital era, the age at which children come into contact with various types of digital media is advancing, and digital games, as one of the most attractive forms, have been deeply integrated into children’s daily life [19]. Early childhood is a critical period for the development of individual creativity, where the brain is highly plastic and sensitive to external stimuli. Experiences at this time have a profound impact on the construction of creative thinking patterns [16]. Even though earlier research has concentrated on how games affect children’s development, there are still unanswered questions or contentious issues about how digital games relate to early childhood creative expression, particularly when it comes to thorough longitudinal experimental intervention studies [20, 21]. On the one hand, some scholars worry that the preset rules and virtual situations in digital games may restrict children’s independent imagination and real perception, so that they are immersed in the established framework and inhibit the natural flow of creativity. On the other hand, there are also views that the rich visual and auditory elements and interactive operation modes of digital games can provide children with diversified inspirational materials. This can inspire them to think out of the box and find unique paths of expression in the intersection of virtual and reality [22, 23].

The research results showed that EG intervention was significantly better than CG in terms of painting innovation (7.01 ± 2.54 vs 5.36 ± 2.16, P < 0.001) and storyline richness (6.28 ± 1.19 vs 5.28 ± 2.19, P < 0.001), which is highly consistent with the viewpoint proposed by Alwhaibi et al. [5] that multisensory stimulation in digital games promotes creative output. The diverse tools of creative painting games (such as dynamic brushes, custom colors) and the spatial design freedom of digital scene games provide children with diverse creative carriers, activate their associative thinking, and enable them to break through traditional patterns in painting and construct complex plots in stories. However, there was no significant difference in the complexity of painting composition between the two groups (P = 0.304), which may be due to the fact that the spatial cognitive development of children aged 4–6 is still in the early stage, and the 16 week intervention has not significantly improved this dimension. This suggests that in the future, more emphasis should be placed on designing digital game tasks that focus more on spatial layout practice. In addition, all dimensions of creative expression in EG showed significant improvement in pre-test and post test (P < 0.001), with no gender difference (P > 0.05), indicating the universality of open digital game intervention and refuting Bulut et al.’s [11] concern about the suppression of imagination by digital game preset frameworks. Due to the low rule constraints at the core of this research game, weekly group sharing further enhances creative collision and avoids framework limitations. From the perspective of game performance, EG’s ability to draw on others’ creativity and expand (6.57 ± 1.57 vs 5.24 ± 2.16, P < 0.001) and the diversity of problem-solving methods (4.68 ± 1.25 vs 3.19 ± 1.68, P < 0.001) were significantly better. This is consistent with Montuori et al.’s [10] view that creative expression requires cognitive and social collaboration, indicating that digital games not only enhance expression outcomes, but also promote cognitive and social abilities during the expression process. There was no significant difference in the dimension of active communication alone (P = 0.087), which may be due to the introverted nature of some children. Short term intervention is difficult to change their communication tendencies and requires longer guidance. Overall, the results of this study confirm that open digital games suitable for 4–6 year old children’s cognition can significantly enhance their early creative expression ability through creative stimulation, process practice, and social collision pathways.

Some studies have suggested that the preset rules of digital games may limit children’s independent imagination (for instance, Bulut et al.’s [11] research mentioned that the preset framework may inhibit the natural flow of creativity), which contrasts with the conclusion of this study. The creative painting and story-making games selected in this study pay more attention to open-ended design and reduce rule constraints. By delving into the improvement mechanism, it is pointed out that the multi-sensory interaction of digital games can activate the prefrontal cortex and default mode network of children’s brains, promoting cognitive flexibility and associative thinking. Meanwhile, the weekly group sharing sessions enhance creative collisions and strengthen the external incentives for creative expression. It is suggested that kindergartens set up three 30-min digital game sessions each week in the curriculum. Priority should be given to games that support free creation. Families can participate in story-creating games together during parent–child time to guide children to extend their virtual creativity to real expression. Strengthen the analysis of the impact on digital content design and children’s participation, emphasizing that the difficulty gradient of games (from simple graphic drawing to complex story logic) should match the cognitive development rhythm of children aged 4 to 6. Moreover, children’s active participation behaviors such as designing buildings and expanding storylines in games can directly enhance their problem-solving abilities and the quality of creative output. Further corroborate the promoting effect of digital games on creativity.

The effectiveness and reliability of the tools and methods used in this study are reflected in three aspects: firstly, the effectiveness of the evaluation tool. The "Young Children’s Creative Expression Ability Assessment Scale" passes the content consistency test (Kappa = 0.87, P < 0.001), ensuring that the measurement dimensions are highly matched with the core connotations of early creative expression in children (painting, storytelling), and that the difficulty and discrimination of each dimension are adapted to the cognitive level of children, avoiding measurement bias. The indicators of the Game Process Observation Scale are highly correlated with the core indicators of Bulut et al. [11] and Montuori et al. [10] (r > 0.65, P < 0.001), verifying the effectiveness of the indicators. The second is the reliability of the experimental design, which adopts random grouping (there is no significant difference in baseline creative expression and game process performance between the experimental group and the control group, P > 0.05), and the control group is not exposed to experimental related numerical games. During the intervention process, the researcher accompanied and guided the entire process, recording key behaviors of the children every week to ensure consistency in the implementation of the intervention and reduce operational deviations. The third is the reliability of data analysis. SPSS 26.0 was used for independent sample t-test (comparing differences between groups) and paired t-test (comparing differences between pre-test and post-test within groups) for quantitative data, and the statistical method was selected according to the intervention design type suitable for this study. Qualitative data was analyzed using topic analysis, with two researchers independently extracting topics and conducting a consistency test with Kappa = 0.82 (P < 0.001) to ensure the reliability of the qualitative analysis.

In conclusion, the experimental design of the study strongly confirmed the positive effects of digital games in the development of children’s early creative expression. Based on this, it is suggested that educators and parents can introduce high-quality digital games resources, such as arranging special time slots in kindergarten curricula or family parent–child time for children to be exposed to screened creative games. Moreover, children should be encouraged to share their creative experiences in the games, so that the inspiration from the virtual world can be extended to real-life communication. At the same time, game developers should further strengthen the educational orientation, carefully design game levels and interactive links according to children’s cognitive development, and enhance the relevance of games for creative development. Although this study verified the positive impact of digital games on children’s early creative expression through 16 weeks of experimental intervention, the following limitations still exist: Firstly, although the experimental period is longer than that of short-term studies, it is still insufficient for tracking the long-term development of children’s creativity. Future research can be extended to several years, combining key nodes at different growth stages to observe the persistence of the impact of digital games. Secondly, this study mainly adopts behavioral observation and standardized scale assessment, and has not yet delved deeply into the neurocognitive mechanism. In the future, technologies such as electroencephalogram and eye-tracking can be combined to analyze the cognitive processing process of children in digital games from a neuroscience perspective, and further reveal the internal mechanism of the improvement of creative expression. Furthermore, the sample of this study is limited to children aged 4 to 6, and the applicability of its conclusions to children of other age groups needs to be carefully inferred. Finally, the differences in parents’ attitudes towards digital games may affect children’s enthusiasm for participating in intervention (supportive parents are more likely to guide children to engage in games). Socioeconomic factors lead to differences in the frequency of exposure and usage environment of digital devices among children from different families (children from high-income families may have more stable game devices and guidance). The variability of digital access (unstable family networks for some children) may weaken the consistent effect of the intervention. Future work can adopt a design that combines longitudinal tracking with neurocognitive measurement, extending the tracking period to 2–3 years, covering the critical period of creativity development for children after they enter primary school, and at the same time analyze the activation patterns of cognitive processing brain regions in children during digital games. In addition, it is necessary to verify the effectiveness of digital game intervention under different socio-economic backgrounds, develop hierarchical digital game intervention plans for different age groups such as 4–6 years old and 7–9 years old, clarify the core elements of game design at each stage, and establish a quantitative evaluation index system for the effect of digital game intervention to enhance the practical applicability of research results.
