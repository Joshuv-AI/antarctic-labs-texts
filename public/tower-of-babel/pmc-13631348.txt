Online health information-seeking behavior during the preoperative period among patients with pancreatic tumors and caregivers in China: A qualitative study
Authors: Jiacheng Cao, Pin Zhang, Lanping Zheng, Lihui Li, Zhenqi Lu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectiveTo describe online health information-seeking behavior during the preoperative period among patients with pancreatic tumors scheduled for pancreatectomy and caregivers in China, and to identify implications for structured information support.MethodsThis qualitative descriptive study was conducted from October to December 2025 in the pancreatic surgery department of a tertiary cancer center in Shanghai, China. Maximum-variation sampling was used to recruit 20 participants (8 patients and 12 caregivers), who were interviewed individually rather than as matched dyads. Individual semistructured interviews were audio-recorded, transcribed, and analyzed using directed content analysis. The Technology Acceptance Model served as a sensitizing framework while allowing themes beyond the model to emerge.ResultsSix themes were identified: (1) platform differentiation and complementary use; (2) structural barriers to accessing online health information; (3) use of online information to address cognitive, emotional, and practical needs; (4) credibility appraisal and verification strategies; (5) influencing factors of online health information-seeking behavior; and (6) expectations for institution-led online information services. Ten participants reported using large language models, generally intermittently and mainly to interpret examination reports. Caregivers more often described leading multiplatform searches, although group-level comparisons could not assess within-family interactions.ConclusionsPatients and caregivers used multiple platforms to address preoperative information needs but encountered fragmented content, limited personalization, and credibility concerns. Structured, plain-language support led by health care institutions and oncology nurses may help patients and caregivers evaluate online information and obtain individualized clinical guidance.Keywords: Pancreatic neoplasms, Information-seeking behavior, Preoperative care, Caregivers, Large language models, Qualitative research

Introduction

Pancreatic tumors represent one of the most challenging tumor types. When malignant, pancreatic tumors carry an extremely poor prognosis, with a five-year relative survival rate of only 13% in the United States.1 Pancreatectomy is the main treatment for pancreatic tumors, including both malignant and non-malignant cases; however, the high procedural complexity and elevated risk of postoperative complications pose significant physical and psychological challenges to patients during the perioperative period.2, 3, 4

Prehabilitation is defined as a multimodal intervention encompassing exercise, nutritional optimization, and psychological support, aimed at optimizing the patient's functional reserve and improving the patient's psychological state during the interval between the decision for surgery and the surgical procedure.5 In the context of pancreatectomy, prehabilitation may improve postoperative outcomes.6 Moreover, patient education has been incorporated into the framework of prehabilitation in recent years.7 Despite this evidence, the clinical implementation of prehabilitation remains limited, leaving the majority of patients scheduled for surgery without formal preoperative support.8 In response to this unmet need, and also driven by an active desire to understand their condition and to prepare for surgery, patients and caregivers increasingly turn to online health information seeking behavior (OHISB) as a self-initiated strategy for preoperative preparation.9,10 Rather than a random or merely reactive act, OHISB in this setting is a deliberate, goal-directed strategy: it is mobilized when formal support is insufficient, but it is also associated with health anxiety.11 Through OHISB, patients and caregivers seek not only factual information but also instrumental guidance, emotional reassurance, and a sense of control, using the information gathered to better cope with the consequences of the illness.10 Prior surveys indicate that a substantial proportion of people affected by cancer engage in OHISB; for example, approximately 46% of patients with gynecologic cancer have been reported to actively seek health information online, while 82% of caregivers seek cancer information from any source.9,12 Nevertheless, the specific patterns and experiences of OHISB among patients with pancreatic tumors remain unexplored. Furthermore, existing research on OHISB has overlooked the rapidly evolving digital ecosystem in China.13 The Chinese internet landscape has undergone transformative changes with the emergence of novel platforms that fundamentally alter how health information is accessed and consumed. Beyond traditional search engines and medical websites, patients now navigate a sophisticated multi-platform environment including AI-powered chatbots (such as Doubao and DeepSeek) capable of interpreting medical reports, short-video platforms (Douyin) providing visual procedural explanations, and lifestyle sharing platforms (Xiaohongshu) offering peer experience narratives.14, 15, 16 However, the behavioral impact of these emerging platforms on preoperative information-seeking patterns and the challenges they present remain insufficiently understood. This study addresses three critical gaps in the literature. First, it focuses on a clinically important but understudied population—patients facing pancreatic tumor surgery who experience heightened uncertainty due to the procedure's complexity and poor prognosis. Second, it examines self-initiated OHISB occurring specifically in the preoperative phase when structured health care support is limited, revealing authentic patient-driven information behaviors. Third, it investigates OHISB within China's unique digital ecosystem, where generative artificial intelligence tools have emerged as entirely new information channels alongside established social media platforms.

To theoretically frame this investigation, the classic Technology Acceptance Model (TAM) was employed.17 The classic TAM contains five constructs, which are perceived usefulness (PU), perceived ease of use (PEU), external factors, behavioral intention, and actual use.17 In TAM, external factors shape PU and PEU; PU and PEU jointly determine BI; and BI leads to actual use.17 Given that the OHISB represents a process of adopting internet-based information technologies, TAM provides an appropriate framework for understanding both the external behavioral patterns and internal decision-making logic of patients and caregivers navigating China's complex digital health landscape. Moreover, TAM has been applied in studies of online platforms, demonstrating its explanatory power.18

As a sensitizing framework, TAM was used to orient the study without constraining it; we remained open to dimensions that TAM does not anticipate. Using TAM as a sensitizing framework, this study adopted directed content analysis to describe the OHISB of patients with pancreatic tumors and caregivers in the preoperative stage, with the aim of generating evidence-based insights for patient- and family-centered preoperative information support.

Methods

Study design

This study utilized a qualitative descriptive design grounded in the philosophy of naturalistic inquiry.19 This approach is suitable for describing events and experiences of online health information seeking and provided a framework for exploring participants' behavior and direct perspectives without requiring abstract explanations.20 The report of this study followed the Consolidated Criteria for Reporting Qualitative Research (COREQ) checklist.21

Setting and sampling

The study was conducted from October to December 2025 in the department of pancreatic surgery of a tertiary specialized cancer center in Shanghai, China. A maximum variation sampling strategy was employed in order to achieve sample heterogeneity. Individuals with a wide range of characteristics were invited to participate, with variation in age, sex, education, income, occupation, and residence.

Inclusion criteria of patients were: (a) initial diagnosis of pancreatic tumor confirmed by imaging, regardless of whether the lesion was suspected to be malignant or non-malignant; (b) proposed pancreatectomy; (c) age ≥ 18 years; (d) awareness of their diagnosis; and (e) provision of written informed consent. Exclusion criteria of patients were: (a) combined severe complications or critical illness; (b) a cognitive or psychiatric condition that precluded informed consent or meaningful interview participation; and (c) inability to participate in a Mandarin-language interview.

Inclusion criteria of caregivers were: (a) caring for a patient meeting the patient inclusion criteria (a) and (b); (b) age ≥ 18 years; (c) providing unpaid care; and (d) provision of written informed consent. Exclusion criteria of caregivers were the same as patients’ exclusion criteria (b) and (c).

Caregivers were interviewed as participants in their own right for three reasons. First, in Chinese cancer care, caregivers frequently assume primary responsibility for seeking, screening, and interpreting health information on behalf of the patient. Second, when patients are older adults or have limited digital literacy, caregivers are often the main online information seekers, so excluding them would systematically omit a large share of the actual OHISB occurring in this population. Third, patients and caregivers may differ in their information needs, motivations, and seeking strategies, and including both allowed us to capture this fuller picture of preoperative OHISB as shaped by patient and caregiver roles. Therefore, analyzing patients and caregivers within a single dataset—while attending to convergence and divergence—better captured preoperative information needs as they unfolded on the care-receiving side of the clinical relationship, rather than limiting the account to patients alone. Data collection continued until no new information emerged from three consecutive interviews, indicating data saturation had been achieved.22

Data collection

Individual semistructured interviews were conducted in the ward office for the convenience of participants. All interviews were conducted by the first author, a male master's student in nursing. Interviews were audio-recorded and field notes were taken to capture key information and non-verbal cues. Only the interviewer and one participant were present. On the day of the interview, participants were introduced to the interviewer face-to-face by ward nurses. Participants were informed of the interviewer's identity and the purpose of the interview. The interview guide was developed with reference to TAM as a sensitizing framework. Questions 1–6 and 8–9 were aligned with TAM constructs (PU, PEU, BI, and external factors), with actual use captured through embedded behavioral descriptions. Question seven was included because information credibility is salient in preoperative OHISB but lies outside TAM. Open-ended prompts allowed participants to raise additional issues beyond these predefined items. Based on the pilot interviews (included in the analysis) conducted with the two caregivers, the interview guide was revised through team discussion. Pilot interviews revealed that the use of overly academic terminology (e.g., “online health information-seeking behavior”) might be difficult for participants to understand. Consequently, the interview guide was revised to plain, conversational wording and more detailed expressions. The refined semi-structured interview guide questions are shown in Table 1.

Data analysis

Audio recordings were transcribed within 24 hours and coded in NVivo 15. We used directed content analysis with an experiential codebook developed before coding began. Consistent with the interview guide, the codebook comprised two components: (a) TAM-aligned experiential codes derived from PU, PEU, and external factors, with behavioral intention and actual use embedded as cross-cutting code families rather than standalone nodes; and (b) beyond-TAM experiential codes corresponding to the guide's credibility-assessment item (Question 7). Themes were built from participants' experiences. The analysis comprised five steps: (1) Both codebook components were operationalized as predefined experiential deductive code families (e.g., usefulness-related experience, platform-related barrier, credibility appraisal). (2) Two researchers independently coded all transcripts to these experiential codes; for example, P4's reassurance from online information was coded within a usefulness-related family, C10's reluctance to seek online within an influencing-factors family, and segments on source authority within the credibility-appraisal family. (3) Inductive coding was reserved for segments that did not fit either predefined codebook component and arose from open-ended discussion; such segments received new labels in the same analytic pass. (4) Constant comparison organized coded content into six themes—four primarily TAM-aligned, Theme four drawn from the beyond-TAM credibility code family, and Theme six organized from inductively coded spontaneous content on unmet service expectations. (5) The research team held regular meetings to critically review and refine the structure of themes and sub themes. Discrepancies in coding and in theme construction were resolved by discussion until consensus; where two analysts disagreed, a third senior researcher reviewed the coded segments and adjudicated. Rigor

The interviewer for this study, who is also the first author, is a male master's student in nursing who has completed coursework in qualitative research. Prior to the interviews, the researcher had completed a three-month internship at the interview site and acquired substantial knowledge of pancreatic tumors. The interviewer was not involved in the clinical care of any participant, had no prior relationship with any participant, and had no conflict of interest. After each interview, the interviewer wrote a reflective note to record new insights. The interview audio recordings were transcribed independently by two researchers, and the transcripts were cross-checked. Prior to analysis, the full transcripts were returned to all participants for proofreading or revision; no issues were reported. Formal post-analysis member checking of the findings was not conducted. Instead, on-the-spot member checking was performed at the end of each interview, where the interviewer verbally summarized the key points and confirmed them with participants. The analysis was conducted by two researchers, with a third researcher reviewing the results, adjudicating disagreements and providing feedback.

Ethical considerations

This study was approved by the Ethics Board of Fudan University Shanghai Cancer Center (Approval No. 2304273-Exp2) on April 14, 2023. Data were collected between October 2025 and December 2025, within the approved period. All procedures strictly adhered to the Declaration of Helsinki.23 Before each interview began, the interviewer explained the study in detail to the participant, and written informed consent was obtained from all participants. Participants were informed of their right to skip any questions or withdraw from the study at any time. Participants were assigned unique codes (e.g., P1) for pseudonymization.

Results

Characteristics of participants

A total of 20 individuals participated, including 8 patients and 12 caregivers. No participant refused to participate or dropped out. No repeat interviews were conducted. Interview duration ranged from 28 to 37 minutes, mean 31.75 minutes (standard deviation [SD] 2.65). Patients' ages ranged from 34 to 75 years, mean 47.25 years (SD 14.85), and caregivers’ ranged from 33 to 63 years, mean 46.75 years (SD 9.23). The number of days between the decision to undergo surgery and admission to the hospital ranged from 2 to 50 days, mean 13.10 days (SD 12.19). With respect to platform use, social media (Xiaohongshu and Douyin) and large language models (LLMs) were the two most frequently used channels, followed by professional medical platforms (Haodaifu) and general search engines (Baidu). 10 of 20 participants, including 5 of 8 patients and 5 of 12 caregivers, reported using at least one LLM during the preoperative waiting period. Among LLM users, eight participants used DeepSeek only; one used both Doubao and DeepSeek; and one used Qwen, Doubao, and DeepSeek. The predominant purpose of LLM use was to upload and interpret examination reports, followed by obtaining general disease knowledge and identifying authoritative hospitals or surgical teams. Reported engagement was generally intermittent and exploratory rather than sustained: LLM users typically described occasional querying when a specific question arose, and only one participant (P1) described LLMs as a primary, habitual channel.

Themes, sub-themes, and codes

The analysis generated six main themes encompassing 15 sub-themes. Because TAM was used as a sensitizing framework, not all TAM constructs were organized as separate content themes. Theme 1 and Theme 2 primarily reflected PEU; Theme three primarily reflected PU; and Theme five primarily reflected external factors. Behavioral intention was represented secondarily within Theme five through participants' expressions of willingness, reluctance, or avoidance of online seeking. Actual use was not organized as a separate content theme; rather, it was evidenced as the enacted online information-seeking behaviors described across Themes 1–4, including multi-platform searching, uploading examination reports to LLMs, and cross-platform verification. Themes four and six were not derived from TAM constructs and lay outside the TAM adoption chain. Theme four corresponded to the beyond-TAM credibility-appraisal codes mirroring Question 7. Theme six reflected expectations for institution-led information services that participants raised spontaneously during open-ended discussion and that were organized through inductive coding. Theme 1: platform differentiation and complementary use

Corresponding to the PEU construct of the TAM, this theme describes how patients and caregivers selected and combined platforms to obtain health information with the least effort and greatest accessibility. They weighed both the differentiated content strengths of platforms and the comprehensiveness and accessibility of information presentation, and then synthesized information across multiple platforms. This systematic, sequential cross-platform behavior—undertaken to assemble comprehensive and complementary information—was most pronounced among caregivers, who often took the lead in organizing the search, whereas patients more often used one or two familiar platforms. The enacted searching behaviors described in this theme also contributed secondarily to the TAM construct of actual use.

Platform selection based on functional positioning and media characteristics

Participants used four main types of platforms with distinct functional roles: social media (Douyin, Xiaohongshu) for peer experience sharing; large language models (Doubao, DeepSeek) for medical report interpretation and knowledge synthesis; professional medical platforms (Haodaifu) for specialist and hospital evaluation; and traditional search engines (Baidu) as a general supplementary source.

C4: “With Xiaohongshu, I mainly look at the sharing of patients' experiences, the hospitalization process for patients with pancreatic tumors, and how much time it takes for surgery … as well as how much the surgery will cost, and the postoperative recovery, and the complications.”

C4: “I upload the examination report (to DeepSeek) and it comes out with relevant information in great detail.”

C1: “I go through Haodaifu to see what the level of the doctor in charge is … There are many specialists on Haodaifu, then we can refer to see who has done more surgeries, then we have a good idea.”

C3: “Baidu has a little bit of information on everything. I basically look at it as a supplementary.”

Platform preferences were also shaped by media format: text-image platforms (Xiaohongshu) offered durable, retrievable content, while short-video platforms (Douyin) delivered intuitive, visual explanations.

P3: “You can't remember the voice of the video in Douyin. It plays over quickly. The platform keeps pushing content, making you feel uncomfortable … But the post in Xiaohongshu will have a matching picture to show.” C3: “Douyin explains things in greater detail and with more specificity because it uses videos. Even the doctors of these wards have videos.”

Cross-platform information access strategy

Distinct from the verification behavior described in Theme 4, the cross-platform behavior here was aimed at achieving comprehensiveness: when a single platform could not provide sufficient information, participants combined the complementary strengths of different platforms to assemble a more complete picture, and they switched the primary platform according to the stage of their care pathway. This collaborative, division-of-labor pattern was especially evident among caregivers, who frequently coordinated several platforms on the patient's behalf.

C8: “I think DeepSeek is for popular science and Xiaohongshu is for life. Searching both is really helpful.” C1: “First, once I confirmed the surgery was feasible, I used Haodaifu to research which hospitals and doctors specialize in this procedure. Second, for preoperative examinations, I analyzed the test reports using DeepSeek. Third, when preparing for admission, I followed personal accounts on Xiaohongshu to prepare for surgery.”

Theme 2: structural barriers to accessing online health information

While participants demonstrated considerable agency in selecting and combining platforms, this process was not without challenges. This theme describes the platform-related barriers attributable to platform design, algorithms, and content, representing the constraining pole of PEU. Although these structural barriers were raised primarily by caregivers, who engaged in more extensive searching, patients also expressed dissatisfaction with the vague outputs of LLMs and the inability to find sufficiently specialized content, indicating that the barriers affected both groups.

Limitations of online health information content distribution

The content limitations of online health information manifested across three dimensions: structural fragmentation, limited personalization, and insufficient specialized depth. Together, these limitations prevented participants from building a coherent, actionable, and clinically adequate knowledge framework.

P6: “Like the posts on Xiaohongshu only have “preoperative sharing one” and “preoperative sharing two”, it wouldn't be very detailed and we wouldn't know which day corresponds to which day after our admission.”

C7: “Because it is certainly not very specific to our situation, but to most people, so I am just using it as a reference as well.”

C6: “I think it is just on the surface. For example, I cannot understand the anastomosis and reconstruction of the digestive tract; and I cannot understand the postoperative problems such as the follow-up surgery and the different results of the postoperative pathology report, including the postoperative medication.”

Limitations of human–computer interaction efficiency and function

A variety of factors led patients and caregivers to perceive constraints in the effectiveness of interacting with LLMs, thereby limiting the practical utility of these models in addressing their knowledge needs. First, participants characterized responses generated by LLMs as vague and non-committal; they noted that these models typically provided only general guidance but rarely offered definitive conclusions, making the information valuable for expanding knowledge but unsuitable as a guide for action.

P1: “It provides both positive and negative information; it does not give conclusions … it could be this or that. Just a reference.”

Additionally, patients and caregivers found that the quality of information generated by LLMs was influenced by the phrasing of their questions, and they attempted to obtain more satisfactory responses by refining their questions and taking other measures.

C2: “How you ask it determines how it responds.” C3: “Sometimes the answers generated by LLMs may not be comprehensive. We'll ask more detailed questions.”

In addition, patients and caregivers expressed frustration with the generalized but uninformative information generated by LLMs, as such information offers no assistance in making decisions.

P1: “It (the large language model) did not give you a conclusion. It just said to seek medical care promptly. I already know that direction—go to see a doctor.”

The formation of negative information cocoons

The recommendation algorithms of social media platforms disproportionately surface severe cases and adverse outcome narratives, creating self-reinforcing “information cocoons” that participants described as difficult to escape. Rather than encountering balanced representations of patient experiences, participants found that the more they searched, the more the algorithm amplified negative content.

C2: “It depends on what you usually search for. These things are all information cocoons. The more you search, the more it pushes to you. It is normal. It is just the recommendation algorithm at work.”

This algorithmic amplification of negative information was associated with greater anxiety in participants' accounts, an effect most often described by caregivers, who searched more intensively.

C8: “Lately I've been scrolling through these posts nonstop, and the more I scroll, the more anxious I feel. My anxiety affects him (the patient) too … It's mainly because of the disease. Plus, when I scroll through Xiaohongshu, seeing those really severe cases can be pretty scary.”

Theme 3: use of online information to address cognitive, emotional, and practical needs

Despite the barriers documented above, participants were nonetheless able to obtain health information that served multiple purposes. Corresponding to the PU construct of the TAM, this theme describes the multidimensional health information that patients and caregivers obtained through online information platforms, examined from three perspectives: cognitive, practical, and emotional.

Systematic acquisition of disease knowledge

Participants used the Internet to learn about the pathology, prognosis, and management of the disease, to interpret examination reports, and to seek medical resources and treatment pathways.

C3: “I'll look up any abnormal lab results. Plus, I'll research diet, prognosis, precautions, and how it might progress.” P6: “Which hospitals in China are most authoritative for this procedure? How many surgeries does this surgical team perform annually? What are the associated risks? I think it (AI) summarized it quite well.”

Managing expectations and emotional adaptation

Participants used online information to learn about the disease, form psychological expectations regarding its outcome, and find emotional resonance through peer narratives, thereby alleviating anxiety. Caregivers particularly emphasized the emotional significance of peer narratives; while patients expressed this aspect less frequently, they did mention it as well.

C4: “Then I can form a general understanding in my mind … It’s like I originally had no clear concept of the specific possible course of this surgery and hospitalization. Now I know roughly what the process may entail and what situations are normal to encounter.” C1: “On platforms like Xiaohongshu, I've seen family members regularly share updates about patients' conditions, including survival status. Since we were initially anxious upon knowing about this disease, seeing these posts helps us better assess the risks.”

Preparation for the anticipated perioperative journey

Participants sought online information covering everything from preoperative preparation to postoperative recovery, including preoperative examinations and parameter adjustments, hospital supplies and daily living arrangements, as well as the surgical procedure and expectations for postoperative recovery.

C3: “If I had looked into this before, I would have known that all these things at home need to be adjusted properly before admission.” P2: “(On Xiaohongshu) they also tell you to pack some things—like towels, basins, and other daily necessities for your hospital stay.” C4: “The hospitalization process for people with pancreatic issues, and how long the surgery takes. Then there’s the post-surgery hospital stay.”

Theme 4: credibility appraisal and verification strategies

Obtaining information was only part of the challenge. This theme concerns how participants appraised the credibility of online health information and the strategies they used to filter it. It captures an information-evaluation and trust dimension that the TAM does not address and was organized from the beyond-TAM credibility-appraisal code family in the initial codebook.

Dimensions for evaluating information credibility

Participants assessed credibility partly by the content's consistency with their own knowledge, but non-content cues—especially the identity, authority, and potential conflicts of interest of the information provider—strongly influenced their judgments.

C2: “It aligns with your common sense, then take a look.” C3: “Because you're a professional hospital. But online sources aren't always reliable. Even if the text content is identical, different individuals delivering it can make you feel significantly more or less confident.” C8: “On Xiaohongshu, it might recommend a certain medication based on a specific case.”

Information screening and verification strategy

Participants developed strategies for filtering online health information based on credibility. They focused on officially verified accounts to obtain authoritative information, and identified and excluded content with commercial interests. Crucially, and in contrast to the comprehensiveness-seeking behavior of Theme 1, when they encountered information whose truthfulness was uncertain, they performed cross-platform comparison specifically to verify that information rather than to broaden it.

C1: “We generally trust the chief specialists at hospitals on official platforms … You also have to judge for yourself (whether there's fake information). For example, many of those promoting traditional Chinese medicine say they can add you to a group chat to buy together. I don't join those.” C3: “If I'm not sure about this question, I go to other platforms and search for the same question, then back to Baidu or Douyin—over and over—until I'm confident it's true.”

Reliance on authoritative sources for final verification

Many participants viewed online information as a supplementary reference rather than an absolute guideline; they still regarded health care professionals as the ultimate authority.

P3: “Even though I've searched all kinds of information, I still have to rely on the hospital. What the hospital says is the standard.”

However, a minority of participants expressed absolute trust in the reliability of LLMs.

P1: “It probably cannot lie at all, because it only retrieves information … everything is sourced from literature. If it lies, it would be the literature lying. It has no brain.”

Theme 5: influencing factors of online health information-seeking behavior

This theme primarily reflects the external factors of TAM. It examines how individual and contextual characteristics shaped participants' capacity and context for online information seeking. Participants' varying willingness, reluctance, or avoidance of online seeking additionally informed the behavioral-intention construct within this theme.

Factors inherent to the individual

Participants believed that digital literacy influenced the willingness to seek health information online. Participants perceived that older age, rural residence, and lower educational attainment could reduce the frequency of online health information seeking due to factors such as low adoption of online platforms or limited access to information channels.

C1: “First, older people don't use these platforms very often. Second, it's their children—different ages have varying interest.” P4: “People from rural areas or with lower levels of education may not know how to use these platforms at all, or their access to information channels is inherently limited and not very accessible.”

They also noted that patients and caregivers who were outgoing, highly educated, eager to learn, and had a positive attitude were perceived as more likely to actively seek online information.

C1: “I think the more educated and relatively open-minded they are, the more willing they are to look up this kind of information. If someone is reluctant to face it, or if many are quite resistant to surgery, they probably will not actively seek it out.”

Furthermore, they noted that their daily habits regarding the use of online platforms and the resulting path dependence influenced their approach to seeking health information online, particularly their choice of platform.

P1: “I only use two platforms for browsing, which are Doubao and DeepSeek. I do not have time for exploring anything else.”

Moreover, they believed that one's coping ability and attitude towards illness influence the motivation to seek information. On the one hand, if a person becomes overwhelmed by the negative emotions caused by their illness, this may reduce their active pursuit of information. On the other hand, in order to avoid the negative emotions associated with understanding their condition, some patients and caregivers may choose to follow their doctor's advice rather than seek information online.

P4: “Many people get stuck in that situation once they fall ill and simply do not have the energy to search for this kind of information.” C10: “Just listen to the doctor. There is no need for me to search online. It only makes you more anxious.”

Factors related to the context

Disease severity, family support, and socioeconomic circumstances shaped seeking. More severe disease intensified information needs; the family support system compensated by mobilizing a family member as the information seeker; and socioeconomic pressure depleted the time and energy available for seeking.

C2: “Patients with benign tumors might not be particularly concerned. They mainly seek general information and may focus more on postoperative care. If it is a more serious condition, they are likely to pay more attention to the causes and consequences. They will definitely seek a more comprehensive scope of information.” C3: “When it is a matter of his life, he wants to search for it. This is not like another knowledge. If he doesn't search for it himself, his family will search for it.” P4: “The pressures of their home environment and living conditions leave them no energy to understand these things, eventually resulting in aimless worry.”

Theme 6: expectations for online information services led by health care institutions

This theme represents participants' unmet service needs and design requirements. It was not solicited by a dedicated interview question; rather, participants spontaneously raised these expectations during open-ended discussion, and the related segments were organized through inductive coding. Taken together, the barriers, needs, and influencing factors in Themes 1–5 shaped a clear gap between participants' current experiences and their ideal support framework. Participants proposed suggestions regarding both the content and the format of such services.

Expectations for information content characteristics

Participants expected authoritative information officially endorsed by health care institutions; a streamlined, timeline-based structure; definitive answers to non-individualized questions; and plain-language translation of specialist knowledge.

P3: “I would like to see more official pushes from the hospital. But now I see mostly pushes from patients whose content is some of their own experiences, that is more cluttered and unsystematic.” P6: “It would be good to have a clear process to tie these together, like the meals, the admission process, what happens at each point in time, what we have to do, and the sharing of cases at each point in time. It would be good to have a demonstration of such a process.” P4: “For things that aren't individualized but consistent for each patient—like pre- and postoperative considerations—I want the exact answer, a standardized response.” P3: “It needs to be more understandable, which means it needs to be explained what the thing is for. Sometimes you feel like just doing an examination and you don't know what it's going to do.”

Expectations for service format design

Participants expected a collaborative AI-plus-human model, a humanized AI persona, concise written summaries of frequently asked questions, and physician-delivered video explanations.

C7: “The AI can answer simple things, and if it's something complex, having a human to answer would give me more peace of mind.” P5: “After you open the program there is going to be an image of a family doctor, then you enter questions to it and it then answers them professionally … Just having the machine there feels a little bit cold, having a human image there feels a little bit more intimate.” C6: “I think it's better to have something in print, that is, a couple of pages with general answers to questions that are commonly asked. Because if you leave it up to us, sometimes we don't know what to ask.” C3: “It's best if doctors provide the introduction. For instance, for a specific topic, have the director record a video explaining what's needed in that area. This approach is more intuitive, alleviates concerns, and demonstrates greater professionalism.”

Discussion

Main findings

This study is among the first qualitative investigations to describe preoperative OHISB among patients with pancreatic tumors and their caregivers within China's rapidly evolving digital ecosystem, where LLM-based AI chatbots have emerged as an entirely new information channel alongside established social media and search platforms. Using TAM as a sensitizing framework, we found that preoperative OHISB is a dynamic process involving platform selection, information gathering, credibility appraisal, barrier navigation, and modulation by multiple factors. Importantly, our analysis shows both where the data align with TAM (multidimensional usefulness; platform-related ease of use and barriers; external factors; willingness or avoidance of seeking; and the enacted seeking behaviors as actual use) and where they exceed it: credibility appraisal (Theme 4) and expectations for institution-led services (Theme 6) fell outside TAM's explanatory scope and were not captured by TAM. These themes are not well captured by TAM's technology-adoption logic alone. To interpret these findings, the Comprehensive Model of Information Seeking (CMIS) was additionally drawn on as a complementary framework.24 CMIS posits that information seeking is shaped by antecedent factors, by the characteristics and utilities of available information carriers, and by users' appraisal of those carriers.24 Viewed through CMIS, preoperative OHISB appears as a coherent process in which antecedents shape the selection and use of diverse carriers, whose credibility is actively appraised and whose limitations may prompt expectations for alternative, institution-led support—thereby offering a perspective beyond TAM on what OHISB entails in this setting. TAM was nevertheless retained as the primary sensitizing framework because the defining novelty of this setting is the adoption of emerging information technologies, whereas CMIS was used only to interpret the antecedent, carrier, and appraisal dimensions that TAM underspecifies.

A core behavioral finding is that participants did not merely use multiple platforms in parallel but assigned distinct functional roles to each platform according to specific information needs. This structured, purpose-driven differentiation extends prior observations by Zhong et al., who reported that Chinese patients with head and neck neoplasms make comprehensive use of short-video apps, search engines, and medical platforms, by showing a more deliberate division of labor across platforms.25 Notably, and in contrast to surgical populations who preferred AI interaction over clinicians, participants here generally regarded health care professionals as the ultimate authority and treated LLM outputs as supplementary references.26,27 This divergence from Chen et al.'s orthopedic sample likely reflects the high mortality and surgical complexity of pancreatic tumor treatment, which strengthens reliance on clinical expertise, and is consistent with prior Chinese qualitative work in which clinicians were the most trusted information source reported by Wu et al.26,28 At the same time, the active integration of AI tools into preoperative seeking corroborates Chen et al.'s finding on the value of AI chatbots for perioperative patients in China.26

Because patients and caregivers may differ in needs, motivations, and strategies, we examined where the two groups converged and diverged. Convergence was strongest in epistemic authority: both groups treated health care professionals as the ultimate standard and online content (including LLM output) as supplementary, and both endorsed institution-led, plain-language, timeline-based support. Divergence was most evident in roles and intensity of seeking. Caregivers more frequently led systematic, multi-platform searches and assumed primary responsibility for evaluating doctors and hospitals on professional platforms. This group also reported the most intensive engagement with peer narratives, along with greater exposure to algorithmically amplified negative content and anxiety. Patients more often relied on one or two familiar platforms, focused on interpreting their own examination reports, and more frequently expressed information avoidance or deference to clinicians as an emotion-protective strategy. This patterning is consistent with a family-mediated, surrogate model of information seeking in Chinese cancer care, and suggests that the aggregated themes reflect both groups, though with differing emphasis between them.28, 29, 30 Practically, it implies that preoperative information support should consider a dyadic approach: addressing caregivers as primary seekers while also engaging patients directly, and providing additional support to patients who lack a capable family seeker.

Participants' seeking addressed three interrelated dimensions—cognitive (disease knowledge, prognosis, treatment pathways), emotional (expectation management and anxiety relief through peer narratives), and practical (perioperative preparation). This three-dimensional structure is consistent with findings among Chinese patients with cancer reported by Wu et al., and can be understood, following Brashers' uncertainty management theory, as a coping response to surgical uncertainty in which individuals either actively seek or deliberately avoid information.28,31 The coexistence of active seeking and avoidance in our sample reinforces that preoperative information support cannot be delivered uniformly but must be calibrated to each person's readiness.

In our data, participants reported substantial structural barriers: fragmented content, insufficient specialization, vague AI outputs, and algorithmic information bias.32 These barriers confirm and extend Guo et al.'s observations among preoperative patients with lung cancer in China, where participants similarly struggled to find sufficiently targeted information and to make sense of conflicting content.33 Our study adds the specific mechanism of negative “information cocoons,” in which recommendation algorithms disproportionately amplify severe cases and poor-prognosis narratives, intensifying preoperative anxiety—paralleling Sezgin et al.'s account of caregivers “going down the rabbit hole” during online searches.34 Together, these findings indicate that, however thorough, self-initiated online seeking cannot replace structured, institution-led preoperative education, and that participant-reported online negative content should be considered when nurses assess preoperative psychological readiness.

Regarding AI specifically, 10 of 20 participants reported LLM use, but principally for a narrow, well-bounded task—interpreting examination reports—and engagement was generally intermittent rather than sustained; only one participant used LLMs as a habitual primary channel, and frequency and duration were not measured. Therefore, broad conclusions about LLM adoption are not drawn. What the data do support is more specific and is consistent with prior work: participants valued LLMs for efficient knowledge synthesis and report interpretation, yet experienced their outputs as vague and non-committal, and a minority over-trusted them.26,27 These bounded findings inform our practice recommendations.

Individual factors (digital literacy, education, platform habits, emotional coping) and contextual factors (disease severity, family support, socioeconomic pressure) jointly shaped willingness and ability to engage in OHISB, supporting the determinants framework proposed by Zhong et al.25 A prominent cultural finding is the family-compensatory pattern: caregivers, especially adult children, assumed primary responsibility for searching, screening, and interpreting information. This family-mediated pattern may reflect family-centered care norms in the Chinese context and is consistent with Wu et al. and with dyadic management findings in Chinese cancer care.28, 29, 30 It implies that preoperative support should target the patient–caregiver dyad, with targeted assistance for patients who have limited family support or low digital literacy, to reduce disparities.35

Implications for nursing practice and research

These findings support five actionable directions for oncology nursing practice in the preoperative pancreatic surgery setting. First, oncology nurses should deliver structured, timeline-based preoperative education covering perioperative processes, risks, preparation, and recovery in plain language, with explicit clinical endorsement to strengthen credibility.28,33 Second, nurses can incorporate brief digital health literacy assessment into preoperative assessment to identify patients and caregivers who need coaching on platform use, report interpretation, or avoidance of misinformation.36 Third, nurses are well positioned to triage online information concerns during preoperative contacts: acknowledging what patients have read online, correcting common misconceptions, and referring complex or individualized clinical questions to surgeons or specialist nurses rather than leaving families to resolve uncertainty alone. Fourth, nursing teams should coach patient–caregiver pairs on collaborative information roles—supporting caregivers who lead searching while directly engaging patients who prefer to defer or avoid online content—and should identify patients without capable family support for additional outreach. Fifth, hospital-endorsed AI tools, where used, should remain supplementary, with clear statements of AI limitations and clear guidance on when to refer questions to clinicians.26

Limitations

This study has several limitations. First, the sample was recruited from a single tertiary cancer center in Shanghai, which may limit transferability to rural or low-resource regions with lower digital literacy. Second, participants were recruited individually rather than as matched patient–caregiver dyads; the analysis can therefore compare group-level patterns of convergence and divergence, but cannot examine interaction or negotiated information roles within the same family, and pooling both groups in one dataset may still obscure some role-specific nuances. Matched dyadic studies are warranted. Third, although 10 of 20 participants reported LLM use, the frequency, duration, and depth of engagement were not systematically quantified; AI-related findings should therefore be read as descriptive of a subset of bounded practices rather than as evidence of broad or intensive adoption. Fourth, the cross-sectional design captured only preoperative experiences; longitudinal studies are needed to explore how information needs and behaviors change across the treatment trajectory. Fifth, the sample included participants with lesions of differing suspected malignancy status, and saturation was assessed for the combined sample rather than separately for patients and caregivers; role-specific variation may therefore be underrepresented.

Conclusions

This study reveals the content and patterns of OHISB among patients with pancreatic tumors and their caregivers in the absence of structured preoperative information support. Through a multi-platform, collaborative approach to information gathering, they sought to address multidimensional information needs and develop strategies for verifying the credibility of information, with caregivers frequently acting as primary seekers. However, because structural barriers to accessing online health information persist and LLM use was intermittent and mainly limited to report interpretation, structured preoperative information support led by health care institutions and oncology nurses remains necessary.
