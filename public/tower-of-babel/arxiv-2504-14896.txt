Vector pulse magnet
Kosuke Noda, Kenta Seki, Dilip Bhoi, Kazuyuki Matsubayashi, Kazuto Akiba, Akihiko Ikeda

Abstract
The underlying symmetry of the crystal, electronic structure, and magnetic structure manifests itself in the anisotropy of materials’ properties, which is a central topic of the present condensed matter research. However, it demands such a considerable effort to fill the explorable space that only a small part has been conquered. We report a vector pulse magnet (VPM) as an alternative experimental technique to control the direction of applied magnetic fields, which may complement the conventional methods with its characteristic features. The VPM combines a conventional pulse magnet and a vector magnet. The VPM can create vector pulsed magnetic fields and swiftly rotating pulsed magnetic fields. As a demonstration, the three-dimensional magnetoresistance measurement of a highly oriented pyrolytic graphite is carried out using the AC four-probe method at 4.5 K and 6 T. The two-dimensional electronic structure of graphite is visualized in the three-dimensional magnetoresistance data. One can uncover the rotational and time-reversal symmetry of materials using a VPM and a variety of measurement techniques.

Anisotropy in solid is the manifestation of the underlying symmetry in the electronic and magnetic structures of the solid, such as the topological electronic structure and exotic orders in correlated electron systems and quantum magnets
Nakatsuji et al. 2015

Crystals show anisotropy in various properties such as elastic modulus, dielectric constant, and magnetization, and in their transport properties, such as resistivity, Hall effect, and thermal Hall effect.
That macroscopic material tensor is expected to have symmetry operations of the crystal’s point group, also known as Neumann’s principle, which is further intricate by magnetism and spin-dependent dispersion.

A magnetic field is a unique stimulus for visualizing the inherent symmetry of the material.
The magnetic field is an axial vector field that breaks the material’s time-reversal symmetry by directly affecting the electrons’ spin and orbital magnetic moment.
Here, we devised an anlternative technique, a vector pulse magnet (VPM), to control the direction of the magnetic field.
The idea of VPM is to make a
pulse
version of the conventional
vector
magnet (VM).
We devised a VPM composed of two pulse magnets that generate a magnetic field perpendicular to each other, as shown in Fig.
1
.
Two independent pulse powers drive the outer and inner coils.
The outer coil is a split pair coil with a 20 mm bore, and a mono-coil with a 10 mm bore is installed as an inner coil.
VPM generates a pulsed magnetic field in an arbitrary direction.
With VPM, one can investigate the material’s rotational and time-reversal symmetry.
VPM has different natures compared to the conventional techniques, such as a VM and a two-axis rotation stage in a DC magnet, which will be reported here.

We give a qualitative discussion on the result.
The magnetoresistance of graphite may be expressed as
\Delta R_{xx}(\theta,\phi,B)/R_{xx}(\theta,\phi,0{\rm\ T})\propto 1/m_{c}^{e}m_{c}^{h}

Akiba et al. 2017

The Fermi surface of graphite shown in the inset of Fig.

m_{zz}>>m_{0}>>m_{xx}\sim m_{yy}

With

\sqrt{m_{xx}m_{yy}}

They make the magnetoresistance considerable with

VPM is an unconventional technique, whose character is not readily comparable to conventional techniques such as VM and a probe with a two-axis rotator in a DC magnet.
VPM has several beauties.
(i) One can use a standard stick-type probe for a usual pulse magnet experiment.
(ii) VPM sweeps the magnetic field as rapidly as a few milliseconds.
(iii) VPM can operate in the
vector
and
rotating
pulse modes.
(iv) VPM has room for improvement in the magnetic field strength.

Point (i) allows us various measurements with VPM, including not only the magnetoresistance measurements in the present study, but also the measurements of Hall effect, magneto-optical effect, and magnetostriction using an optical fiber
Ikeda et al. 2017
;
Ikeda et al. 2018
;
Ikeda et al. 2019
.
Notably, the measurements with optical fibers are suitable for a VPM, unlike the techniques that use a rotating sample stage, where an optical fiber is not allowed.
Points (ii) and (iii) allow us to resort to induction measurement (
dM/dt
) and pyroelectric current measurement (
dC/dt
).
With a VPM in the
vector
pulse mode, one obtains
(\partial M/\partial B)_{\theta,\phi}
which gives a magnetization curve as a function of magnetic field in arbitrary direction
M(B)_{\theta,\phi}
.
With a VPM in the
rotating
pulse mode, one obtains
(\partial M/\partial\theta)_{B,\phi}
which gives a magnetization curve as a function of
\theta
at a constant magnetic field
M(\theta)_{B,\phi}
.
Lastly, we comment on Point (iv).
In the present study, we prototyped a VPM with a typical magnetic field strength of 6 T.
We estimate from our experience in generating 40 T using a mono-coil and the same pulse power
Ikeda et al. 2024
that an improved VPM may have a larger magnetic field approaching 20 T using similar magnets and pulse power to those of the present study.

In summary, we devised a VPM that generates either a vector pulsed magnetic field or a rotating pulse magnetic field up to 6 T by changing the pulse’s delay.
The VPM consists of two pulse magnets with distinct pulse powers whose magnetic fields are oriented orthogonally to each other.
The 2D nature of HOPG’s magnetoresistance is visualized in 3D space, indicating that the Fermi surface’s electronic structure is two-dimensional.
Such manifestation of microscopic symmetry of materials in macroscopic data will be a promising tool for experimental verification of the materials’ anisotropic electronic and magnetic states.
