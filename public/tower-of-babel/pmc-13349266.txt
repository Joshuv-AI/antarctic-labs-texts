The Indian Smell Identification Test for Parkinson’s Disease: Limitations of Binary Scoring and Proposal of a Modified Graded Approach
Authors: Tarunya Nagaraj, Shweta Prasad, Shubha GS Bhat, Mahima Bhardwaj, DK Samartha, Pooja Mailankody, Rohan R Mahale, Nitish Kamble, Vikram V Holla, Ravi Yadav, Pramod K Pal
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Background and Objectives:Olfactory dysfunction is a common nonmotor symptom and prodromal marker of Parkinson’s disease (PD). Despite its early occurrence, hyposmia is frequently underrecognized, emphasizing the need for objective, culturally appropriate tools. The Indian smell identification test (INSIT) is widely used but relies on binary scoring, which may underestimate residual olfactory function. This study determines the prevalence of olfactory dysfunction in Indian persons with PD (PwPD) using INSIT and evaluates a modified version (mINSIT) with graded scoring as a preliminary proof-of-concept.Methods:Two independent cohorts were recruited. Phase 1 included 129 PwPD and 129 age- and sex-matched healthy controls (HCs) assessed with INSIT. Phase 2 included 108 PwPD and 50 HCs tested with INSIT and mINSIT. INSIT used standard binary scoring, while mINSIT captured gradations: nonperception, perception without recognition, cued recognition, and spontaneous recognition.Results:INSIT identified hyposmia in 62% of PwPD versus 32.5% of HCs in phase 1, and 59.3% versus 30% in phase 2 (P < 0.001). mINSIT revealed that many PwPD who failed odor identification still perceived odors, demonstrating perception–identification dissociation in 19–86% of cases. Graded scoring provided a more nuanced assessment of olfactory function, with a broader distribution of scores compared with binary scoring. The odors most discriminative between groups were mango, cardamom, and lemon.Conclusions:INSIT is limited by its binary outcome design, whereas mINSIT provides a potentially useful, culturally relevant preliminary tool that captures gradations of olfactory function and may improve detection. It addresses limitations of conventional binary scoring; however, formal validation in larger cohorts is required before it can be recommended for clinical use.

Introduction

Nonmotor symptoms (NMSs) are increasingly recognized as core features of the clinical spectrum in Parkinson’s disease (PD).[1] Of these, olfactory dysfunction, i.e., hyposmia, is considered to occupy a unique role as a prodromal marker, i.e., pre-motor symptom of PD.[2] Hyposmia may occur 5–20 years before the onset of motor symptoms, and neuropathological studies have demonstrated the deposition of α-synuclein early in the course of disease.[3] Despite the apparent significance, olfactory dysfunction tends to remain underrecognized in clinical practice. Patients seldom spontaneously report hyposmia, particularly owing to the minimal impact it has on daily life in contrast to other NMSs such as sleep disturbance, neuropsychiatric symptoms, cognitive impairment, etc.[4567] Consequently, self-reported hyposmia underestimates the true prevalence, widening the gap between subjective awareness and objective measurement.[8]

Reliable, culturally appropriate testing kits are crucial to ensure accurate, objective identification of hyposmia. In Western populations, the University of Pennsylvania smell identification test (UPSIT)[9] and Sniffin’ Sticks[10] are the most widely used and validated. However, these kits are expensive and comprise odorants that are poorly recognized by non-Western populations. To overcome these limitations, a few region-specific tests have been developed, such as the Barcelona smell test-24 (BAST-24),[11] odor stick identification test for Japanese (OSIT-J),[12] Italian olfactory identification test (IOIT),[13] and Vietnamese smell identification test (VSIT).[14] The Indian smell identification test (INSIT)[15] was developed as a low-cost and culturally relevant alternative, incorporating locally familiar odorants such as cardamom, rose, etc.

While INSIT has proven practical, it suffers from a fundamental limitation common to most smell identification tests: its binary scoring system. By reducing responses to simply correct or incorrect, the test overlooks gradations in olfactory performance. Patients may perceive an odor without identifying it, identify it only with cues, recognize it spontaneously, or fail to perceive it entirely. This variability collapses into a binary outcome, risking reduced sensitivity, underdetection of prodromal changes, and possible misclassification. Against this background, the present study was designed in two stages. In the first stage, we assessed olfactory function in a large cohort of Indian patients with PD and matched healthy controls (HCs) using INSIT, both to establish prevalence and to test the discriminatory capacity of binary scoring. In the second stage, prompted by the limitations identified in the initial analysis, we piloted a modified version of INSIT (m-INSIT) that introduces graded scoring to capture a broader spectrum of olfactory dysfunction and to evaluate its potential as a more sensitive clinical tool.

Methods

Subject recruitment

Subjects included in this study were prospectively recruited between July 2022 and December 2024, in a young and late onset PD (YOPD and LOPD, respectively) study funded by the Scientific Knowledge for Ageing and Neurological ailments (SKAN) research trust and conducted at the Department of Neurology, National Institute of Mental Health and Neuro Sciences, Bengaluru, India. Recruitment was carried out following ethical approval (NIMHANS/34th IEC (BS&NS DIV)/2022). Consecutive persons with PD (PwPD) who consented to participate in the study were recruited from the neurology outpatient and inpatient departments and movement disorder clinics. The diagnosis of PD was based on the Movement Disorders Society clinical diagnostic criteria for PD[16] and confirmed by trained neurologists and movement disorder specialists (PKP, RY, NK, RRM, PM, and VVH).

Two independent cohorts were recruited. The first consisted of 129 PwPD and 129 age- and sex-matched HCs, all of whom underwent testing with INSIT. Analysis of this cohort revealed both high prevalence of hyposmia and limitations of binary scoring, motivating the design of the second cohort. This second group included 108 PwPD and 50 HCs, who were tested using both INSIT and modified INSIT (mINSIT; with graded scoring).

A formal pre-specified sample size calculation was not performed for this exploratory study; the cohort size was determined by the availability of consecutive patients recruited within the study period. The second cohort was a pilot evaluation of mINSIT and was similarly not powered by a formal sample size calculation. We acknowledge these as limitations of the current study. A post-hoc power analysis for phase 1 (independent samples comparison; mean INSIT difference = 1.60, pooled standard deviation [SD] ≈1.90, n = 129 per group, α = 0.05) indicated a statistical power of >99%, confirming that phase 1 was adequately powered to detect the observed between-group difference. For phase 2, the unequal group sizes (108 PwPD, 50 HCs) and exploratory nature of mINSIT evaluation limited formal power estimation; thus, this cohort should be regarded as a pilot.

Data collection and assessments

Data was collected using Research Electronic Data Capture (REDCap) software.[1718] Demographic details, including age, gender, age at onset of disease, and duration of illness, were recorded for all subjects. In addition, the history and duration of hyposmia were also recorded. A detailed clinical assessment was performed, and motor severity was scored in the OFF (12 hours after the last dose of anti-Parkinsonian medication) and ON states using the Movement Disorder Society-unified PD rating scale (MDS-UPDRS).[19] Permission for which was obtained from the International Parkinson and Movement Disorders Society. The Hoehn and Yahr (HY) stage of disease severity was also recorded.

All participants in the study were screened for disorders that can temporarily impair smell perception, such as upper respiratory tract infections, the common cold, allergic rhinitis, and sinusitis. Subjects with nasal polyps, deviated nasal septum, traumatic head injuries, exposure to chemicals/toxins on a regular basis, drug abuse, or alcohol addiction were excluded from the study. Olfactory testing was performed only in eligible participants in a well-ventilated, odor-free environment.

Olfactory testing

Indian smell identification test (INSIT)

This test was administered to both cohorts and comprised ten culturally familiar odorants—cardamom, kewra (screwpine essence), khus (vetiver root oil), lemon, mango, orange, pineapple, rose, thinner, and vanilla.[15] Each subject was provided with a cotton bud dipped in the odorant and instructed to hold it 1 cm from the nostril before sniffing. Participants were then asked to identify the odor from four options. One point was awarded for each correct identification, and total scores were determined based on the number of items correctly identified (maximum score = 10).

Modified Indian smell identification test

A pilot graded-scoring test was administered to the second cohort, in addition to the standard INSIT, to capture the spectrum of olfactory dysfunction. Score was assigned 0 if odor was not perceived, 1 if it was perceived without recognition, 2 if it was recognized only with cues, and 3 if it was spontaneously recognized. The maximum score on mINSIT was 30. It should be noted that mINSIT has not been standardized against normative data before this study, and its findings should be considered preliminary. Importantly, no formal pilot study was conducted to standardize mINSIT before its application in this cohort. The graded scoring categories were developed conceptually, based on established stages of olfactory processing, and applied de novo as part of this investigation to explore the feasibility of graded scoring. Accordingly, this instrument should be regarded as a proof-of-concept tool, and formal standardization against normative data remains to be performed.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analyses were conducted using the Statistical Package for the Social Sciences, version 22.0 (SPSS Inc., Chicago, IL, USA), and Microsoft Excel. Continuous variables were summarized as mean ± SD along with the range, while categorical variables were presented as frequencies and percentages. Normality of continuous variables was assessed using the Kolmogorov–Smirnov test. For normally distributed data, comparisons were performed using the independent samples t-test, whereas the Mann–Whitney U-test was applied to data that did not meet the normality assumption. Associations between categorical variables were evaluated using Pearson’s Chi-square test. A P value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

In subjects belonging to phase 2, 108 PwPD (mean age 55.4 ± 11.3 years; 74 males, 31 females) were compared with 50 HCs (mean age 44.1 ± 12.7 years; 29 males, 21 females). The PD group was significantly older than the HC group (P < 0.001), reflecting the consecutive recruitment design rather than age-matching; there was no significant difference in sex distribution (P = 0.175). Within the PD cohort, the mean duration of illness was 5.5 ± 4.7 years (range 0.5–30.0), and the mean age at onset was 49.9 ± 11.7 years. YOPD accounted for 51.4% (54/105) of cases, reflecting a more balanced YOPD: LOPD distribution compared with phase 1, as this cohort was not subject to the same age-matching constraint. Most PwPD were in HY stages 1–3 (93.3%, n = 98), representing predominantly early-to-moderate disease. The mean MDS-UPDRS Part III OFF score was 41.8 ± 18.8, which improved to 17.8 ± 12.8 in the ON state. The mean total levodopa equivalent daily dose (LEDD) was 536.9 ± 218.2 mg/day (available for 88/108 subjects). The MDS-UPDRS Part I and Part II scores were 6.8 ± 5.8 and 9.8 ± 8.2 respectively [Table 2].

INSIT: Phase 1

In the first phase, 129 PwPD and 129 HCs underwent testing with the Indian Smell Identification Test. Mean total scores were significantly lower in PwPD than in HCs (3.89 ± 1.98 vs. 5.49 ± 1.82; P < 0.001). Using a cut-off score of ≤4 (as derived from the original INSIT validation study by George et al., 2013[15]), 62.0% (80/129) of PwPD were classified as hyposmic compared with 32.5% (42/129) of HCs (P < 0.001). Complete anosmia (score = 0) was observed in 3.8% (5/129) of PwPD and in none of HCs. In this cohort, 10.07% (n = 13) of PwPD reported a history of olfactory disturbance; 3.8% of patients were anosmic, and 62.01% of PwPD and 32.5% of HCs were hyposmic when tested.

Among the odors, thinner was the most frequently identified item in PwPD (81.4%), while cardamom was best recognized by HCs (93.0%). Khus was the least identified in both groups (0% vs. 3.1%). Recognition of cardamom, pineapple, rose, mango, lemon, and thinner was significantly lower in PwPD than in HCs (P values ranging from < 0.001 to 0.031), whereas there were no group differences for kewda, khus, orange, or vanilla [Table 3].

Correlation analysis revealed no significant associations between INSIT scores and gender, total LEDD, or MDS-UPDRS parts I–III (all P > 0.1). A weak negative correlation was observed with disease duration (P = 0.032, r = –0.189), and a stronger negative correlation with HY stage (P < 0.001, r = –0.384).

A subgroup analysis comparing YOPD (AOO ≤50 years, n = 104) and LOPD (AOO >50 years, n = 25) within phase 1 showed no statistically significant difference in overall INSIT scores (YOPD: 4.01 ± 2.04 vs. LOPD: 3.40 ± 1.66, P = 0.174) or hyposmia rates (62.5% vs. 80.0%, P = 0.107). At the odor level, only vanilla recognition differed significantly between subgroups (YOPD 69.2% vs. LOPD 44.0%, P = 0.033).

INSIT and mINSIT performance: Phase 2

In phase 2 (108 PwPD, 50 HCs), similar to phase 1, INSIT scores were lower in PwPD (4.1 ± 2.0 vs. 5.7 ± 1.6; P < 0.001). At the ≤4 cut-off, 59.3% (64/108) of PwPD and 30.0% (15/50) of HCs were classified as hyposmic (P = 0.001). In both cohorts, the majority of PwPD were in HY stages 1–3 (89.1% in cohort 1), representing predominantly early-to-moderate disease.

The modified INSIT revealed that many participants who failed to identify odors on the standard test nevertheless perceived them. This ranged from 19.4% to 86.1% across odors in PwPD and from 10% to 96% in HCs. For example, although khus was rarely identified (0% in PwPD, 3.1% in HCs), 86.1% of PwPD and 90% of HCs reported perceiving the smell but were unable to name it.

Figure 1 illustrates odor-wise recognition rates and the perception–identification gap as observed in cohort 2. The largest differences between PwPD and HCs were observed for mango, lemon, and cardamom, with recognition rates in PwPD more than 20% lower than those in HCs. Odors such as kewda and khus were poorly identified by both groups, suggesting their limited value for discrimination. The distribution observed with mINSIT showed less clustering of PwPD at the lower end of the scale and revealed a broader, more differentiated impairment profile. This pattern suggests that mINSIT is more sensitive to varying degrees of dysfunction, capturing residual olfactory perception that conventional binary scoring misses; however, formal psychometric validation is required to confirm these observations. Across both cohorts, PwPD demonstrated robust, statistically significant deficits in olfactory identification compared with HCs. These deficits were evident in overall INSIT performance and in recognition of specific culturally familiar odorants.

A subgroup analysis comparing YOPD (n = 54) and LOPD (n = 51) within phase 2 revealed a significant difference in olfactory performance, unlike the non-significant trend observed in phase 1. LOPD patients had significantly lower mean INSIT scores (3.24 ± 2.10 vs. YOPD 4.70 ± 2.21, P < 0.001) and a significantly higher hyposmia rate (76.5% vs. 44.4%, P = 0.001). mINSIT-graded total scores were also significantly lower in LOPD (13.98 ± 5.04 vs. 16.83 ± 4.90, P = 0.002). At the odor level, pineapple, vanilla, rose, and mango were identified significantly less often in LOPD compared with YOPD (all P < 0.05). On mINSIT perceptual subscores, LOPD patients demonstrated less spontaneous recognition (1.75 ± 1.60 vs. 2.76 ± 1.90; P = 0.005) and greater perception without recognition (5.73 ± 2.54 vs. 4.74 ± 2.23; P = 0.021), suggesting that the perception–identification dissociation is more pronounced in LOPD.

Discussion

The present study investigated olfactory dysfunction in Indian PwPD using INSIT and introduced mINSIT, which incorporates graded scoring. Our findings reaffirm that hyposmia is a highly prevalent NMS of PD, with approximately two-thirds of patients meeting INSIT criteria for olfactory impairment. Beyond replicating global observations in a relatively large Indian cohort, this study addressed a fundamental limitation of conventional smell identification tests: their reliance on binary scoring. The pilot implementation of mINSIT demonstrated that graded scoring uncovers a broader spectrum of olfactory deficits, offering potentially greater clinical and research utility; however, given the modest size of the second cohort and the absence of formal psychometric data, mINSIT should currently be regarded as a preliminary, proof-of-concept tool requiring validation.

Olfactory dysfunction is well established as a pre-motor symptom in PD. Clinicopathological studies indicate that hyposmia may precede the onset of motor symptoms by 5–20 years, reflecting the early deposition of misfolded α-synuclein in the olfactory bulb, anterior olfactory nucleus, and limbic structures.[2021] Longitudinal cohort studies have further shown that individuals with idiopathic hyposmia are at markedly increased risk of developing PD, thereby establishing olfactory dysfunction as a key prodromal marker.[2422] In this context, objective olfactory testing provides an inexpensive, noninvasive tool with potential applications in risk stratification, early diagnosis, and tracking of disease progression.

Our results align with prior studies reporting prevalence rates of hyposmia ranging from 60% to 90% in PD, regardless of cultural context. However, the choice of odorants is critical to ensure validity across populations. Standardized tools such as UPSIT and Sniffin’ sticks, though widely validated, include odorants that may be unfamiliar to non-Western populations, leading to misclassification unrelated to true olfactory dysfunction. As stated earlier, several culturally adapted tests have been developed such as BAST-24, OSIT-J, VSIT, and INSIT.[1112131415] INSIT is specifically for the Indian population. The present study evaluates the validity of INSIT in PD, confirming both its feasibility and its discriminative capacity relative to matched HCs. Analysis of INSIT scores revealed a conceptual shortcoming inherent to binary scoring systems. Conventional tests assign responses as either correct or incorrect, thereby combining complex gradations of olfactory function into a dichotomous outcome. However, olfaction is a multistage process that includes odorant detection, perceptual awareness, recognition, and semantic labelling, which is supported by distributed neural circuits that span the olfactory bulb, piriform cortex, amygdala, hippocampus, and orbitofrontal cortex.[2324] In PD, neuropathological involvement of these structures is heterogeneous and temporally staggered, such that some patients may retain odor detection while losing the ability to identify or label smells. Our data demonstrate this perception–identification dissociation, in which many participants who apparently fail odor identification on INSIT were able to perceive the odor on mINSIT, with this phenomenon observed in up to 86% of patients with PD for specific odorants. These findings highlight that binary scoring underestimates residual olfactory function and risks misclassifying partial deficits as complete anosmia.

The graded scoring of mINSIT, which distinguishes between nonperception, perception without recognition, cued recognition, and spontaneous recognition, may address this limitation. This approach parallels methodologies in cognitive testing, in which gradations in recall or recognition provide richer information about underlying processes than binary, i.e., all-or-none, outcomes. In our pilot cohort, mINSIT revealed a broader distribution of olfactory performance, reducing clustering of PwPD at the lower end of the scale and thereby improving the resolution of intermediate impairments. Such potential sensitivity is particularly important for identifying prodromal or early PD, where deficits may be subtle and easily overlooked. Furthermore, graded scoring may better capture progression over time, enabling longitudinal monitoring and enhancing the power of clinical trials that utilize olfactory function as an outcome measure.

The odor-wise analysis provides further insights. Certain odorants, notably mango, cardamom, and lemon, exhibited marked perception–identification gaps and robust discrimination between PwPD and HCs, while others, such as kewda and khus, were poorly recognized across both groups, suggesting limited diagnostic value. These findings highlight the importance of empirically refining odorant panels to maximize sensitivity and cultural relevance. In addition, the pattern of impaired recognition with relatively preserved perception in PD supports the possibility that olfactory deficits in PD may primarily reflect central rather than peripheral dysfunction, consistent with neuropathological staging of α-synuclein spread. Neuroimaging studies using functional magnetic resonance imaging have demonstrated differential activation of olfactory brain regions, including the piriform cortex and orbitofrontal cortex, in response to various odorant categories, which may partly explain the differential pattern of odor identification observed between PwPD and HCs.[2324] It is also important to evaluate the categories and types of smell to which each of these odorants belongs. Perhaps there is variability in severity or involvement of different categories.

Clinically, our results carry several implications. The high prevalence of hyposmia in Indian PwPD, including those with young-onset PD, emphasizes the importance of routinely incorporating olfactory assessment into clinical practice. Considering that PwPD seldom spontaneously report hyposmia, the high reliance on self-reporting risks substantial under-detection. Objective tools such as INSIT and potentially mINSIT provide an efficient means of evaluation that may aid in differential diagnosis, particularly in early or atypical presentations. The graded scoring system may improve the ability to distinguish PD from other parkinsonian syndromes, and more refined tools could therefore contribute to diagnostic accuracy in ambiguous cases. Finally, the potential utility of mINSIT in at-risk cohorts, such as the first-degree relatives of PwPD or individuals with isolated REM sleep behavior disorder, may deem exploration. Detection of subtle olfactory changes in these populations could help identify individuals at the highest risk of phenoconversion. Beyond group-level differences, correlation analysis provides an additional dimension for clinical interpretation. Prior studies have demonstrated moderate correlations between olfactory performance and disease duration, cognitive scores, and motor severity.[625]

This study has several limitations. First, the pilot evaluation of mINSIT was conducted in a modestly sized second cohort (108 PwPD and only 50 HCs), without formal psychometric validation or normative data; thus, findings must be interpreted as preliminary and proof-of-concept, and claims of enhanced sensitivity or improved discrimination require confirmation in larger, adequately powered studies. Longitudinal studies are required to determine whether mINSIT scores can track disease progression or predict conversion in prodromal cohorts. Second, the high YOPD proportion is also partly a consequence of the age-matching strategy: HCs were recruited from individuals accompanying patients to clinic—a setting that tends to attract younger caregivers—and the HC pool was consequently younger (mean age 46.1 ± 10.3 years; 62.8% aged ≤50 years). Because PwPD were individually age- and sex-matched to these younger HCs, the PD cohort was enriched for younger-onset cases. The availability of a younger control pool thus drove enrolment of younger PwPD. Subgroup analyses comparing YOPD and LOPD showed no significant difference in phase 1 (where LOPD n = 25 limited power), but a significant difference emerged in phase 2, where the YOPD: LOPD ratio was more balanced (54:51): LOPD patients had lower INSIT scores (P < 0.001), higher hyposmia rates (76.5% vs. 44.4%, P = 0.001), and lower mINSIT-graded scores (P = 0.002). These findings are consistent with the notion that YOPD may have comparatively less severe olfactory impairment than LOPD, and suggest that the higher YOPD proportion in phase 1 modestly attenuated the overall observed hyposmia prevalence. The original INSIT validation study by George et al.[15] did not perform a subgroup analysis stratified by age of onset. Third, the INSIT testing kit was used in accordance with the original methodology described by George et al.;[15] however, standardization of odorant concentrations compared with the original authors’ preparation was not independently verified, which is a potential source of variability. Fourth, the choice of odorant is also crucial and may vary between parts of the same country. For instance, the poorly identified odors of khus and kewda are infrequently used in the southern part of India, and most of the study participants belonged to this region. Perhaps different odorants are required for specific areas of large countries. Additionally, although we excluded participants with overt sinonasal pathology, we cannot fully exclude the influence of unmeasured environmental exposures, dietary factors, or subclinical conditions on olfactory performance. Finally, as our study was cross-sectional, causal inferences regarding the relationship between olfactory dysfunction and disease stage remain limited. Furthermore, a formal screening log was not maintained prospectively, and therefore, the number of patients assessed for eligibility before enrolment cannot be reported; this precludes a formal assessment of recruitment bias.

Despite these limitations, the present study makes several contributions. We provide robust evidence for the high prevalence of hyposmia in Indian PwPD using a culturally validated test, thereby extending the cross-cultural generalizability of olfactory impairment in PD. In addition, we demonstrate, to our knowledge, for the first time, that a graded scoring system applied to INSIT can capture intermediate levels of dysfunction that are lost in binary scoring, thereby improving sensitivity and clinical interpretability. Finally, we offer a practical, low-cost framework for refining olfactory assessment in resource-limited settings, where imported tests may be prohibitively expensive or culturally inappropriate.

Conclusions

Olfactory dysfunction is a significant NMS in PD, and our modified graded scoring approach, i.e., mINSIT, provides a potentially more nuanced preliminary assessment of olfactory capacity, revealing perception–identification dissociations and reducing misclassification inherent to binary scoring. However, mINSIT should be considered a proof-of-concept tool at this stage, and definitive conclusions regarding its clinical utility await formal validation. Future studies should validate mINSIT in larger cohorts and compare its performance with established international instruments to explore its predictive value in prodromal populations. Such efforts may position mINSIT as a valuable instrument for early detection, monitoring, and therapeutic trials in PD.
