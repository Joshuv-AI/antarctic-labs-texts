Abstract
. We establish new intrinsic Strichartz estimates for solutions of the Cauchy problem for a class of possibly degenerate Schrödinger equations with a real drift.

1.
Introduction and statement of the main results

In his seminal paper
[
53
, Cor.1]
Strichartz proved that, if
u
solves the Cauchy problem in
\mathbb{R}^{n}\times[0,\infty)

(1.1)

\partial_{t}u-i\Delta u=F(x,t),\ \ \ \ \ \ \ u(x,0)=\varphi(x),

then there exists a constant
C(n)>0
such that

(1.2)

||u||_{L^{\frac{2(n+2)}{n}}(\mathbb{R}^{n+1})}\leq C(n)\left(||\varphi||_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{n})}+||F||_{L^{\frac{2(n+2)}{n+4}}(\mathbb{R}^{n+1})}\right).

It is well-known that estimates such as (
1.2
) play a basic role in the analysis of nonlinear Schrödinger equations, see
[
9
]
and
[
56
]
. Strichartz established (
1.2
) by generalizing to a paraboloid in
\mathbb{R}^{n+1}
the Tomas-Stein restriction theorem for the sphere in
\mathbb{R}^{n}
. In this context, we note that the exponent
\frac{2(n+2)}{n+4}
on the right-hand side of (
1.2
) is the restriction exponent in
\mathbb{R}^{n+1}
, while
\frac{2(n+2)}{n}
on the left-hand side is its dual. In turn, the (dual of the) estimate (
1.2
) implies the restriction inequality for the paraboloid, see
[
51
, pp.100-101]
, where a similar argument is presented for the cone. Remarkably, such restriction theorem can also be independently established by the ingenious approach of Ginibre-Velo, see the remark in Section
4.1
.

In this paper, we establish some new mixed norm estimates for solutions of a class of dispersive equations with a real drift. To introduce the relevant framework, for
n\geq 2
let
Q,B\in\mathbb{M}_{n\times n}(\mathbb{R})
be two constant matrices, with
Q=Q^{\star}
and
Q\geq 0
. For suitable data
\varphi
and
F
, we consider the Cauchy problem in
\mathbb{R}^{n}\times[0,\infty)

(1.3)

\partial_{t}u-i\operatorname{tr}(Q\nabla^{2}u)-\langle Bx,\nabla u\rangle=F(x,t),\ \ \ \ \ \ \ u(x,0)=\varphi(x).

Schrödinger equations of the form (
1.3
) naturally arise in various contexts of both physical and mathematical significance, see Section
2
for some motivation and background. Here, we highlight three key features of (
1.3
):

(i)

The matrix
Q
can be highly degenerate, with even the possibility of
\operatorname{Rank}(Q)=1
(see Proposition
7.2
). This is a typical situation in the modeling of quantum wires – one-dimensional nanostructures where electrons are confined to two dimensions but free to move in the third. When subjected to a magnetic field, the Hamiltonian governing these systems exhibits degenerate kinetic energy, alongside a drift term that shifts the dynamics in a specific direction.

(ii)

Another challenge is that, due to the drift, the PDE in (
1.3
) lacks a variational structure.

(iii)

A third feature, closely related to the drift, is the general absence of a global homogeneous structure (dilations).

Main assumption.
The following algebraic condition will be assumed throughout the remainder of this paper:

(H)

\operatorname{Ker}Q

does not contain any nontrivial invariant subspace of

B^{\star}
.

This hypothesis is equivalent to the strict positivity of the
controllability Gramian
of the matrices
Q
and
B
defined by

(1.4)

Q(t)=\int_{0}^{t}e^{sB}Qe^{sB^{\star}}ds,

see
[
59
, Theor.1.2, p.17]
. We now recall that in
[
17
, Prop.2.4]
it has been recently shown that, when
F=0
, the problem (
1.3
) admits the following solution

(1.5)

u(x,t)=\frac{(4\pi)^{-\frac{n}{2}}e^{-\frac{i\pi n}{4}}}{\sqrt{\det Q(t)}}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}e^{i\frac{\langle Q(t)^{-1}(e^{tB}x-y),e^{tB}x-y\rangle}{4}}\varphi(y)dy,\ \ \ \ t>0.

The goal of this work is to use the representation (
1.5
) as a starting point for a generalized theory of Strichartz estimates. We aim to show that the broader framework provided by the class of equations (
1.3
) leads to the identification of new a priori inequalities, where the exponents of integrability of the solution are determined exclusively by the
local homogeneous dimension

D
of a nilpotent Lie algebra, intrinsically defined by the matrices
Q
and
B
.
While this idea shares similarities with the works
[
50
]
and
[
47
]
, it introduces a novel perspective in the context of Strichartz estimates for Schrödinger equations such as those in (
1.3
), where the associated Hamiltonian may exhibit significant degeneracies.

Formula (
1.5
) highlights the central role of the function

(1.6)

t\longrightarrow V(t)\overset{def}{=}\det Q(t),

in the analysis of the Schrödinger operators (
1.3
). Since when
Q=I_{n}
and
B=O_{n}
, we have
V(t)=t^{n}
, it is evident that this function can be thought of as a measure of volume.
For general
Q
and
B
, the function
V(t)
offers valuable insights into how the geometry of the problem evolves with time, particularly in the study of dispersive effects and long-time asymptotics. We note in this respect that, if we consider the time-varying, tilted ellipsoids

\mathscr{E}(x,t)=\{y\in\mathbb{R}^{n}\mid\langle Q(t)^{-1}(e^{tB}x-y),e^{tB}x-y\rangle<1\}

in the imaginary Gaussian in (
1.5
),
it is easy to recognize that
V(t)
is connected to the
n
-dimensional volume of
\mathscr{E}(x,t)
by the equation

(1.7)

V(t)=\omega_{n}^{-2}\operatorname{Vol}_{n}(\mathscr{E}(x,t))^{2}.

For these reasons, we will henceforth refer to
V(t)
in (
1.6
) as the
volume function
.

To continue our discussion, we recall that in the paper
[
38
]
it was shown that
(H)
is equivalent to having
Q
and
B
in the following
canonical form

(1.8)

Q=\begin{pmatrix}Q_{0}&O_{p_{0}\times{(n-p_{0})}}\\
O_{{(n-p_{0})}\times p_{0}}&O_{(n-p_{0})\times(n-p_{0})}\end{pmatrix},\ \ \ \ \ \ \ \ B=\begin{pmatrix}\star&\star&\cdot&\cdot&\cdot&\star&\star\\
B_{1}&\star&\cdot&\cdot&\cdot&\star&\star\\
0&B_{2}&\star&\cdot&\cdot&\star&\star\\
\cdot&\cdot&\cdot&\cdot&\cdot&\cdot&\cdot\\
\cdot&\cdot&\cdot&\cdot&\cdot&\cdot&\cdot\\
\cdot&\cdot&\cdot&\cdot&\cdot&\cdot&\cdot\\
0&0&\cdot&\cdot&\cdot&B_{r}&\star\end{pmatrix},

where
Q_{0}=Q_{0}^{\star}
is a strictly positive
p_{0}\times p_{0}
matrix, and for
j=1,...,r
,
B_{j}
is a
p_{j}\times p_{j-1}
matrix such that
\operatorname{Rank}(B_{j})=p_{j}
, with

p_{0}\geq p_{1}\geq...\geq p_{r}\geq 1,\ \ \ \text{and}\ \ \ p_{0}+p_{1}+...+p_{r}=n.

The matrices designated by a
\star
in the above expression of
B
can be arbitrary. When
Q
itself is invertible, and therefore
p_{0}=n
, we have
p_{j}=0
for
j=1,...,r
, and
B
coincides with the
\star
in the upper left corner. We remark that
B
in (
1.8
) need not be nilpotent (see for instance the example (
1.11
) below). Another important point is that the canonical form (
1.8
) must be interpreted dynamically. Specifically, the canonical representation of
B
, and consequently the range of the Strichartz estimates, changes when
Q
is altered. This phenomenon is clearly illustrated in Proposition
7.2
.

Throughout the rest of the paper, we assume that
Q
and
B
in (
1.3
) are in the canonical form (
1.8
).

Definition 1.1
.

We call the number

(1.9)

D=p_{0}+3p_{1}+\ldots+(2r+1)p_{r}

the
local homogeneous dimension
of the Schrödinger operator (
1.3
).

While the motivation for Definition
1.1
will be clarified in Section
8
, we note here that the number
D
will play a pervasive role in our results. Observe that (
1.9
) implies that:

(a)

D\geq p_{0}+p_{1}+\ldots+p_{r}=n\geq 2
;

(b)

If
Q
is invertible, we take
p_{j}=0
for
j=1,...,r
, and therefore
D=n=p_{0}
;

(c)

When
Q
is not invertible, we have
D>n
. This immediately follows from (
1.9
) since
p_{0}<n
;

(d)

An elementary dimension counting argument shows that the maximum value of
D
is given by

(1.10)

D=\sum_{k=1}^{n}(2k-1)=n^{2},

see also (
7.12
).

To illustrate Definition
1.1
, consider for instance the degenerate Schrödinger equation in
\mathbb{R}^{3}\times\mathbb{R}

\partial_{t}u=i(\partial_{xx}u+\partial_{yy}u)+x\partial_{x}u+y\partial_{z}u.

The corresponding matrices are in canonical form

(1.11)

Q=\begin{pmatrix}1&0&0\\
0&1&0\\
0&0&0\end{pmatrix},\ \ \ B=\begin{pmatrix}1&0&0\\
0&0&0\\
0&1&0\end{pmatrix}=\begin{pmatrix}\star_{1}&\star_{2}\\
B_{1}&\star_{3}\end{pmatrix},

with

Q_{0}=\begin{pmatrix}1&0\\
0&1\end{pmatrix},\ \ \star_{1}=\begin{pmatrix}1&0\\
0&0\end{pmatrix},\ \star_{2}\begin{pmatrix}0\\
0\end{pmatrix},\ \ B_{1}=(0\ 1),\ \ \star_{3}=(0).

Note that
B
is not nilpotent and therefore, based on the results in
[
33
]
,
[
38
]
, there can be no global dilations attached to the PDE.
However, following Definition
1.1
, it is possible to assign a local homogeneous dimension
D
. Since
p_{0}=\operatorname{Rank}(Q_{0})=2
and
p_{1}=\operatorname{Rank}(B_{1})=1
, we find that
D=p_{0}+3p_{1}=5
.
This number will determine the integrability range in the Strichartz estimate which results from an application of Theorem A below. Additionally, an exponential weight will arise from the contribution of the eigenvalue
\lambda=1
of
B
, but this will be explained in more detail later.

From now on, the letter
D
will be used exclusively to represent the number defined by (
1.9
).

Definition 1.2
.

Given a number
r
such that
2\leq r<\infty
when
D=2
, or

2\leq r\leq\frac{2D}{D-2}
when
D>2
,
we say that the pair
(q,r)
is
admissible
for (
1.3
) if one has

(1.12)

\frac{2}{q}=D\left(\frac{1}{2}-\frac{1}{r}\right).

We note that in (
1.12
) we can take

(1.13)

q=r=\frac{2(D+2)}{D},\ \ \ \ \ \ \ q^{\prime}=r^{\prime}=\frac{2(D+2)}{D+4}.

This follows from observing that the pair
(r,r)
is admissible for (
1.3
), provided that
r
is given by (
1.13
). Note that, for such
r
, we have
r>2
, and that
r<\frac{2D}{D-2}
when
D>2
. We call the numbers
r,r^{\prime}
in (
1.13
) the
Strichartz pairs
relative to
D
. The motivation for this name is that, as noted in (b) above, when
Q
in (
1.8
) is invertible, we have
D=n
, and the corresponding pair is

(1.14)

q=r=\frac{2(n+2)}{n},\ \ \ \ \ \ \ q^{\prime}=r^{\prime}=\frac{2(n+2)}{n+4},

i.e., the exponents in the original Strichartz inequality (
1.2
).

Theorems A and B below are distinguished by the following different assumptions on the behavior of
V(t)
defined by (
1.6
).

Hypothesis (A).
There exists
\gamma>0
such that for every
t>0
the following inequality holds:

(1.15)

V(t)\geq\gamma\ t^{D}e^{t\operatorname{tr}B}.

Hypothesis (B).
There exists
\gamma>0
and a number
2\leq D_{\infty}<D
such that for every
t>0
the following inequality holds:

(1.16)

V(t)\geq\gamma\ \min\{t^{D},t^{D_{\infty}}\}.

The relevant findings can be summarized as follows:

(a)

Theorem A applies when Hypothesis (A) holds.

(b)

Theorem B applies when Hypothesis (B) holds.

We are finally ready to state our main a priori estimates for solutions of the Cauchy problem (
1.3
).

Theorem A.

Assume (
1.15
) in
Hypothesis (A).
Suppose that
r>2
and that the pair
(q,r)
be admissible for (
1.3
), with
r<\frac{2D}{D-2}
when
D>2
. There exists
C=C(n,r)>0
such that if
u
solves (
1.3
), with
\varphi\in L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
and
F
such that
||e^{\frac{\operatorname{tr}B}{2}t}||F(\cdot,t)||_{L^{r^{\prime}}_{x}}||_{L^{q^{\prime}}_{t}}<\infty
, then the following Strichartz estimate holds

(1.17)

\displaystyle\bigg(\int_{\mathbb{R}}e^{\frac{q\operatorname{tr}B}{2}t}\big(\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}|u(x,t)|^{r}dx\big)^{\frac{q}{r}}dt\bigg)^{\frac{1}{q}}\leq C\bigg\{\|\varphi\|_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{n})}+\bigg(\int_{\mathbb{R}}e^{\frac{q^{\prime}\operatorname{tr}B}{2}t}\big(\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}|F(x,t)|^{r^{\prime}}dx\big)^{\frac{q^{\prime}}{r^{\prime}}}dt\bigg)^{\frac{1}{q^{\prime}}}\bigg\}.

We emphasize that the estimate (
1.17
) holds true for the Strichartz pairs relative to
D
, i.e., when
q=r
are as in (
1.13
). If, additionally,
\operatorname{tr}B=0
, we obtain

(1.18)

||u||_{L^{\frac{2(D+2)}{D}}(\mathbb{R}^{n+1})}\leq C\left(||\varphi||_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{n})}+||F||_{L^{\frac{2(D+2)}{D+4}}(\mathbb{R}^{n+1})}\right).

This estimate applies, in particular, to the case when the matrix
B
in (
1.3
) takes the form (
1.21
). In this case, the non-isotropic dilations (
1.23
) imply that (
1.18
) is best possible.

It is worth noting that, even in the non-degenerate case when
Q>0
, in which
D=n
, the estimate (
1.18
) generalizes (
1.2
), since it allows for a drift, see Example
7.1
, or the case
k=n
of Example
7.2
. Similarly, the more general inequality (
1.17
) extends the well-known mixed-space Strichartz estimates in
[
21
]
,
[
9
]
. However, the full scope of Theorem A goes well beyond the case of an invertible
Q
. For a more comprehensive discussion of such scope, we refer the reader to Sections
1.1
,
6
and
7
. We next introduce our second main result.

Theorem B.

Suppose that (
1.16
) in
Hypothesis (B)
hold. Assume that
r>2
, and that
r<\frac{2D}{D-2}
when
D>2
. Suppose further that
(q,r)
satisfy (
1.12
), and that
1<q_{\infty}<\infty
is such that

(1.19)

\displaystyle\frac{2}{q_{\infty}}\leq D_{\infty}(\frac{1}{2}-\frac{1}{r}).

Then there exists
C=C(n,r)>0
such that for every
\varphi\in L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
and
F
such that

||e^{\frac{\operatorname{tr}B}{2}t}||F(\cdot,t)||_{L^{r^{\prime}}_{x}}||_{L^{q^{\prime}}_{t}\cap L^{q_{\infty}^{\prime}}_{t}}<\infty
, one has

(1.20)

\displaystyle\|e^{\frac{\operatorname{tr}B}{2}t}||u(\cdot,t)||_{L^{r}(\mathbb{R}^{n})}\|_{L^{q}_{t}+L^{q_{\infty}}_{t}}\leq C\bigg\{

\displaystyle\ \|\varphi\|_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{n})}+||e^{\frac{\operatorname{tr}B}{2}t}||F(\cdot,t)||_{L^{r^{\prime}}_{x}}||_{L^{q^{\prime}}_{t}\cap L^{q_{\infty}^{\prime}}_{t}}\bigg\}.

1.1.
Validity of Hypothesis (A) or (B)

Having presented our main results, we next show that Hypothesis (A) or (B) is generically satisfied in
all
possible scenarios for the matrices
Q
and
B
. Our first result in this direction is the following.

Theorem 1.3
.

Suppose that
Q
and
B
satisfy
one
of the following hypothesis
(i)-(iii)
:

(i)

\sigma(B)\not\subset i\mathbb{R}
;

(ii)

\sigma\{B\}\subset i\mathbb{R}
and
\operatorname{Rank}(Q)=n
;

(iii)

B
is nilpotent and of the form

(1.21)

\overline{B}=\begin{pmatrix}0&0&\cdot&\cdot&\cdot&0&0\\
B_{1}&0&\cdot&\cdot&\cdot&0&0\\
0&B_{2}&\cdot&\cdot&\cdot&0&0\\
\cdot&\cdot&\cdot&\cdot&\cdot&\cdot&\cdot\\
\cdot&\cdot&\cdot&\cdot&\cdot&\cdot&\cdot\\
\cdot&\cdot&\cdot&\cdot&\cdot&\cdot&\cdot\\
0&0&\cdot&\cdot&\cdot&B_{r}&0\end{pmatrix}.

Then (
1.15
) in
Hypothesis (A)
holds.

Concerning the hypothesis (iii) in Theorem
1.3
, we emphasise that, when we write
\overline{B}
in the form (
1.21
), we intend that such matrix has been obtained from
B
in the canonical form (
1.8
), by replacing all the blocks designated by a
\star
with a zero matrix of the same dimensions. The matrix
\overline{B}
has a special interest since, by the results in
[
36
,
37
]
,
[
38
]
, we infer that, when
F=0
in (
1.3
), the resulting Schrödinger equation

(1.22)

\partial_{t}u=i\operatorname{tr}(Q\nabla^{2}u)+\langle Bx,\nabla u\rangle

is homogeneous of degree two with respect to a family of non-isotropic dilations
if and only if
the matrix of the drift is in the form (
1.21
). In such case, the relevant dilations are constructed as follows. The ranks
p_{0},p_{1},...,p_{r}
of the matrices
Q_{0},B_{1},...,B_{r}
in (
1.8
) induce a corresponding stratification of the ambient space

\mathbb{R}^{n}=\mathbb{R}^{p_{0}}\oplus\mathbb{R}^{p_{1}}\oplus\cdots\oplus\mathbb{R}^{p_{r}}.

We accordingly denote by
x=\left(x^{(p_{0})},x^{(p_{1})},\ldots,x^{(p_{r})}\right)
a generic point of
\mathbb{R}^{n}
, and define the non-isotropic dilations

(1.23)

\delta_{\lambda}(x,t)=\left(\lambda x^{(p_{0})},\lambda^{3}x^{(p_{1})},\ldots,\lambda^{2r+1}x^{(p_{r})},\lambda^{2}t\right).

Then the free Schrödinger equation

(1.24)

\partial_{t}u=i\operatorname{tr}(Q\nabla^{2}u)+\langle\overline{B}x,\nabla u\rangle

is invariant under the scalings (
1.23
). We stress that, in such case, the number
D
in (
1.9
) is precisely the
homogeneous dimension
, in the sense of
[
14
]
,
[
50
]
,
[
47
]
, of the spatial component of the dilations (
1.23
). We also note that, when
Q
is invertible, the matrix
\overline{B}
must necessarily be the zero matrix.
In Section
6
we will say more about the volume function
\overline{V}(t)
associated with (
1.24
).

The assumption (ii) in Theorem
1.3
does not encompass the case in which
\sigma(B)\subset i\mathbb{R}
, but
\operatorname{Rank}(Q)<n
(hence,
Q
is not invertible). Under such circumstances, the estimate (
1.15
) can fail significantly, as the Example
6.4
shows. This example serves as a paradigm for the situation covered by the next result.

Theorem 1.4
.

Suppose that
\operatorname{Rank}(Q)<n
, and that
B
is similar to a skew-symmetric matrix. Then (
1.16
) in
Hypothesis (B)
holds with
D_{\infty}=n
.

To prove Theorem
1.4
we establish a delicate precise asymptotic estimate of the volume function which is of independent interest. Precisely, we show that for some
\gamma>0
, one has for
t\to\infty

(1.25)

\displaystyle V(t)=\gamma t^{n}+O(t^{n-1}).

We observe that (
1.25
) trivially implies the existence of
C,t_{0}>0
such that:

V(2t)\leq CV(t),\ \ \ \ \ \ \text{for every}\ t\geq t_{0}.

We also note that the hypothesis on the matrix
B
in Theorem
1.4
implies, in particular, that
\sigma(B)\subset i\mathbb{R}
. The reader should also observe that under the assumptions (ii) or (iii) in Theorem
1.3
, or the hypothesis on
B
in Theorem
1.4
, we must necessarily have
\operatorname{tr}B=0
.

The anomalous case:
It is important to note that Theorems
1.3
and
1.4
cover nearly all possible scenarios. However, there is one final possibility which is left out, when the matrices
Q
and
B
satisfy
all
three of the following assumptions:

(iv)

\operatorname{Rank}(Q)<n
;

(v)

\sigma(B)\subset i\mathbb{R}
, and
B
is not similar to a skew-symmetric matrix;

(vi)

B
is not in the form (
1.21
).

Since (v) implies that
\operatorname{tr}B=0
, by (i) of Proposition
8.3
, we know that there exists
D_{\infty}\geq 2
and
\gamma>0
such that for any
t\geq 1
the volume function satisfies

(1.26)

\displaystyle V(t)\geq\gamma t^{D_{\infty}}.

But under the hypothesis (iv)-(vi)
the function
V(t)
exhibits a behaviour that defies a single classification. Specifically, in the Example
7.4
we prove that both the possibilities

D_{\infty}\geq D
and
D_{\infty}<D
can occur. The situation can be thus summarized:

(A)

If
D_{\infty}\geq D
, then (
1.26
) implies that (
1.15
) holds, and Theorem A applies.

(B)

If instead
D_{\infty}<D
, then from (
1.26
) we infer that (
1.16
) holds, and thus Theorem B applies.

1.2.
Description of the paper

A brief outline of the present work is as follows. In Section
2
, we discuss the motivation and background for our main results. Specifically, we show that a change to conformal self-similar coordinates in the Schrödinger equation (
1.1
) leads to a problem of the form (
1.3
). We also explain the emergence of exponential weights in Theorems A and B by showing how they arise naturally through a change to such coordinates in the classical estimates (
2.8
) of Ginibre and Velo.
In Section
3
, starting from the representation (
1.5
), we extend the semigroup defined by (
3.5
) to the entire real line and establish Theorem
3.5
. Section
4
is dedicated to the proof of Theorem A, drawing on the well-established
T^{\star}T
argument introduced by Ginibre and Velo in
[
20
,
21
]
, whereas Section
5
is devoted to the proof of Theorem B. Section
6
focuses on the large-time behavior of the volume function

V(t)
defined in (
1.6
), with the primary goal of proving Theorems
1.3
and
1.4
.
The concluding Section
7
presents several examples that illustrate the results. Of particular interest is Proposition
7.2
, as it demonstrates the variety of situations that can arise. Section
8
collects background results that will be used in the rest of the paper.

As a final note, we mention that this work does not address sharp endpoint estimates, in the spirit of Keel and Tao
[
34
]
, nor other fundamental issues such as short-time existence and regularity, as discussed in
[
20
,
21
]
,
[
9
]
, and
[
56
]
. These topics, along with potential applications of our results to nonlinear Schrödinger equations, will be the focus of a future study.

1.3.
Notation

We use the standard notation
\omega_{n}=\frac{2\pi^{n/2}}{\Gamma(n/2+1)}
for the
n
-dimensional measure of the unit ball in
\mathbb{R}^{n}
. The notation
i\mathbb{R}
indicates the imaginary axis in the complex plane
\mathbb{C}
. We denote by
C^{\star}=[c_{ji}]
the transpose of a matrix
C=[c_{ij}]
.
The notation
\operatorname{tr}C
indicates the trace of
C
,
\sigma(C)
its spectrum. When for a matrix we write
C_{h\times k}
, we want to emphasise that its dimensions are
h\times k
. We use such notation for partitioned matrices. The notation
\nabla^{2}u
indicates the Hessian matrix of a function
u:\mathbb{R}^{n}\times\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R}
with respect to the variable
x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
. Given a measurable function

Motivation and context

We begin by discussing a scenario particularly relevant to the study of blow-up formation and dynamics in semilinear Schrödinger equations. The following considerations illustrate one possible way in which problems such as (

and how the drift contributes to the emergence of exponential weights in the relevant Strichartz estimates.

2.1.
Conformal coordinates and exponential weights

Consider a function
u:\mathbb{R}^{n}\times[0,T)\to\mathbb{R}
. If we make the change of dependent variable

(2.1)

u(x,t)=v(y,\tau),\ \ \ \ \text{where}\ \ \ \ (y,\tau)=\Phi(x,t)=(\alpha(t)x,\beta(t)),

then a computation gives

\partial_{\tau}v+\frac{\alpha^{\prime}(t)}{\alpha(t)\beta^{\prime}(t)}\langle y,\nabla v\rangle-i\frac{\alpha(t)^{2}}{\beta^{\prime}(t)}\Delta_{y}v=\frac{1}{\beta^{\prime}(t)}\left[\partial_{t}u-i\Delta_{x}u\right].

Imposing the conditions

(2.2)

\alpha^{\prime}(t)=\alpha(t)^{3},\ \ \ \ \ \ \beta^{\prime}(t)=\alpha(t)^{2},

we further obtain

(2.3)

\partial_{\tau}v-i\Delta_{y}v+\langle y,\nabla v\rangle=\frac{1}{\alpha(t)^{2}}\left[\partial_{t}u-i\Delta_{x}u\right].

Integrating the equations in (
2.2
), we find

(2.4)

\alpha(t)=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2(T-t)}},\ \ \ \ \ \beta(t)=\frac{1}{2}\log\frac{1}{T-t},\ \ \ \ 0\leq t<T.

The transformation (

3.
The Schrödinger group

We note that the differential operator on the left-hand side of the equation in (

(3.1)

(x,s)\circ(y,t)=(y+e^{-tB}x,s+t),

where

and

forms a non-Abelian Lie group whose identity element is the origin. Such Lie group invariance suggests to transform the solution
u
in (
1.3

F=0
), according to the equation

(3.2)

The function

(3.3)

\partial_{t}v-i\operatorname{tr}(Q^{\prime}(t)\nabla^{2}v)=0,\ \ \ \ \ v(x,0)=\varphi(x),

where we have denoted by
Q^{\prime}(t)=e^{tB}Qe^{tB^{\star}}\geq 0
the derivative of the matrix defined by (
1.4

After taking Fourier transform with respect to

in (

(3.4)

\hat{v}(\xi,t)=\hat{\varphi}(\xi)e^{-4\pi^{2}i\langle Q(t)\xi,\xi\rangle}.

Since, as noted,
(H)
is equivalent to
Q(t)>0
for every

, it is possible to invert the Fourier transform in (

), and obtain the following solution formula for (

) (when

)

(3.5)

[

. The operator

which can be uniquely extended to a strongly continuous one on

), we have

(3.13)

\displaystyle\widehat{U(t)\varphi}(\xi)=\begin{cases}e^{|t|\operatorname{tr}B}e^{4\pi^{2}i\langle Q(|t|)\xi,\xi\rangle}\hat{\varphi}(e^{|t|B^{\star}}\xi)\ \ \ \ \ \ \ \ \ \ \ \ \ \ \ \ \text{if }t<0,\\
\hat{\varphi}(\xi)\ \ \ \text{if }t=0\\
e^{-t\operatorname{tr}B}e^{-4\pi^{2}i\langle Q(t)e^{-tB^{\star}}\xi,e^{-tB^{\star}}\xi\rangle}\hat{\varphi}(e^{-tB^{\star}}\xi)\ \ \ \text{if }t>0.\end{cases}

It will be convenient to have the following corollary of (
3.7
) whose proof we leave to the reader.

Proposition 3.4
.

Suppose that
\varphi\in\mathscr{S}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
. Then

U(t)\varphi(x)=\begin{cases}(4\pi)^{-\frac{n}{2}}\frac{e^{\frac{i\pi n}{4}}e^{|t|\operatorname{tr}B}}{\sqrt{V(|t|)}}e^{-i\frac{|Q(|t|)^{-1/2}x|^{2}}{4}}\mathscr{F}\left(\varphi e^{-i\frac{|Q(|t|)^{-1/2}e^{|t|B}\cdot|^{2}}{4}}\right)(-(4\pi)^{-1}Q(|t|)^{-1}e^{|t|B^{\star}}x),\ \ t<0,\\
(4\pi)^{-\frac{n}{2}}\frac{e^{-\frac{i\pi n}{4}}}{\sqrt{V(t)}}e^{i\frac{|Q(t)^{-1/2}e^{tB}x|^{2}}{4}}\mathscr{F}\left(\varphi e^{i\frac{|Q(t)^{-1/2}\cdot|^{2}}{4}}\right)((4\pi)^{-1}Q(t)^{-1}e^{tB}x),\ \ t>0.\end{cases}

Using Proposition
3.4
, and proceeding as in the proof of
[
17
, Theor.4.1]
, we can now extend to negative times the dispersive estimate in (
3.6
).

Theorem 3.5
.

Let
r\geq 2
. For any
\varphi\in L^{r^{\prime}}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
one has

||U(t)\varphi||_{L^{r}(\mathbb{R}^{n})}\leq\begin{cases}C(n,r)\ \frac{e^{\frac{\operatorname{tr}B}{r^{\prime}}|t|}}{V(|t|)^{\frac{1}{2}-\frac{1}{r}}}\ ||\varphi||_{L^{r^{\prime}}(\mathbb{R}^{n})},\ \ t<0,\\
C(n,r)\ \frac{e^{-\frac{\operatorname{tr}B}{r}t}}{V(t)^{\frac{1}{2}-\frac{1}{r}}}\ ||\varphi||_{L^{r^{\prime}}(\mathbb{R}^{n})},\ \ t>0.\end{cases}

The reader should note the two distinct exponentials on the right-hand side of the inequalities in Theorem
3.5
. At first glance, their discrepancy may seem concerning, but in reality, a small miracle occurs during the proof of Theorem A, and everything will eventually fall into place.

4.
Proof of Theorem A

In this section we suitably adapt to the Cauchy problem (
1.3
) the ideas of Ginibre and Velo in
[
20
,
21
]
. Our final objective is proving Theorem A. We begin by observing that by the Duhamel principle, given
\varphi\in\mathscr{S}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
and
F\in\mathscr{S}(\mathbb{R}^{n+1})
, the solution to (
1.3
) is given by

(4.1)

u(x,t)=U(t)\varphi(x)+\int_{0}^{t}U(t-s)(F(\cdot,s))(x)ds.

We begin by introducing an operator
T^{\star}:\mathscr{S}(\mathbb{R}^{n})\longrightarrow\mathscr{C}^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{n+1})
as follows

(4.2)

T^{\star}(\varphi)(x,t)=e^{\frac{t\operatorname{tr}B}{2}}U(t)\varphi(x),

where
\{U(t)\}_{t\in\mathbb{R}}
is the group defined by (
3.7
).

Proposition 4.1

(Unitarity)
.

We have

T^{\star}:L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{n})\ \longrightarrow\ L^{\infty,2}(\mathbb{R}^{n+1}),

and moreover for every
\varphi\in L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
one has

(4.3)

||T^{\star}(\varphi)||_{L^{\infty}_{t}L^{2}_{x}}=||\varphi||_{L^{2}_{x}}.

Proof.

We have from (
3.13
) for every
t>0

\displaystyle||T^{\star}(\varphi)(\cdot,t)||_{L^{2}_{x}}=||\mathscr{F}(T^{\star}(\varphi))(\cdot,t)||_{L^{2}_{x}}=e^{\frac{t\operatorname{tr}B}{2}}||\widehat{U(t)\varphi}||_{L^{2}_{x}}

\displaystyle=e^{\frac{t\operatorname{tr}B}{2}}e^{-t\operatorname{tr}B}\left(\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}|\hat{\varphi}(e^{-tB^{\star}}\xi)|^{2}d\xi\right)^{1/2}

\displaystyle=e^{\frac{t\operatorname{tr}B}{2}}e^{-t\operatorname{tr}B}e^{\frac{t\operatorname{tr}B}{2}}||\varphi||_{L^{2}_{x}}=||\varphi||_{L^{2}_{x}}.

If instead
t<0
, again from (
3.13
) we have

\displaystyle||T^{\star}(\varphi)(\cdot,t)||_{L^{2}_{x}}=e^{-\frac{|t|\operatorname{tr}B}{2}}e^{|t|\operatorname{tr}B}\left(\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}|\hat{\varphi}(e^{|t|B^{\star}}\xi)|^{2}d\xi\right)^{1/2}=||\varphi||_{L^{2}_{x}}.

These identities prove (
4.3
).

∎

We next compute the operator
T
, whose adjoint is
T^{\star}
. Denoting by
\langle\cdot,\cdot\rangle
the inner product in
L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
, and by
\langle\langle\cdot,\cdot\rangle\rangle
that in
L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{n+1})
, for every
\varphi\in\mathscr{S}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
and
F\in\mathscr{S}(\mathbb{R}^{n+1})
, we have from (
4.2
)

\displaystyle\langle T(F),\varphi\rangle=\langle\langle F,T^{\star}(\varphi)\rangle\rangle=\int_{\mathbb{R}}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}F(x,t)\overline{T^{\star}(\varphi)(x,t)}\ dxdt

\displaystyle=\int_{\mathbb{R}}e^{\frac{t\operatorname{tr}B}{2}}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}F(x,t)\overline{U(t)\varphi(x)}\ dxdt=\int_{\mathbb{R}}e^{\frac{t\operatorname{tr}B}{2}}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}\hat{F}(\xi,t)\overline{\widehat{U(t)\varphi}(\xi)}\ d\xi dt

\displaystyle=\int_{-\infty}^{0}e^{\frac{t\operatorname{tr}B}{2}}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}\hat{F}(\xi,t)\overline{\widehat{U(t)\varphi}(\xi)}\ d\xi dt+\int_{0}^{\infty}e^{\frac{t\operatorname{tr}B}{2}}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}\hat{F}(\xi,t)\overline{\widehat{U(t)\varphi}(\xi)}\ d\xi dt

\displaystyle=\int_{-\infty}^{0}e^{-\frac{|t|\operatorname{tr}B}{2}}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}\hat{F}(\xi,t)e^{|t|\operatorname{tr}B}e^{-4\pi^{2}i\langle Q(|t|)\xi,\xi\rangle}\overline{\hat{\varphi}(e^{|t|B^{\star}}\xi)}\ d\xi dt

\displaystyle+\int_{0}^{\infty}e^{\frac{t\operatorname{tr}B}{2}}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}\hat{F}(\xi,t)e^{-t\operatorname{tr}B}e^{4\pi^{2}i\langle Q(t)e^{-tB^{\star}}\xi,e^{-tB^{\star}}\xi\rangle}\overline{\hat{\varphi}(e^{-tB^{\star}}\xi)}\ d\xi dt

\displaystyle=\int_{-\infty}^{0}e^{\frac{|t|\operatorname{tr}B}{2}}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}e^{-|t|\operatorname{tr}B}\hat{F}(e^{-|t|B^{\star}}\eta,t)e^{-4\pi^{2}i\langle Q(|t|)e^{-|t|B^{\star}}\eta,e^{-|t|B^{\star}\eta}\rangle}\overline{\hat{\varphi}(\eta)}\ d\eta dt

\displaystyle+\int_{0}^{\infty}e^{-\frac{t\operatorname{tr}B}{2}}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}e^{t\operatorname{tr}B}\hat{F}(e^{tB^{\star}}\eta,t)e^{4\pi^{2}i\langle Q(t)\eta,\eta\rangle}\overline{\hat{\varphi}(\eta)}\ d\eta dt

\displaystyle=\int_{-\infty}^{0}e^{\frac{|t|\operatorname{tr}B}{2}}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}\mathscr{F}\left(U(|t|)(F(\cdot,t)\right)(\eta)\ \overline{\hat{\varphi}(\eta)}\ d\eta dt

\displaystyle+\int_{0}^{\infty}e^{-\frac{t\operatorname{tr}B}{2}}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}\mathscr{F}\left(U(|t|)(F(\cdot,t)\right)(\eta)\ \overline{\hat{\varphi}(\eta)}\ d\eta dt

\displaystyle=\int_{-\infty}^{0}e^{\frac{|t|\operatorname{tr}B}{2}}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}U(-t)\left(F(\cdot,t)\right)(y)\ \overline{\varphi(y)}\ dydt

\displaystyle+\int_{0}^{\infty}e^{-\frac{t\operatorname{tr}B}{2}}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}U(-t)\left(F(\cdot,t)\right)(y)\ \overline{\varphi(y)}\ dydt

This chain of equalities, and the definition (
4.2
), show that

(4.4)

T(F)(y)=\int_{\mathbb{R}}e^{-\frac{t\operatorname{tr}B}{2}}U(-t)(F(\cdot,t))(y)dt=\int_{\mathbb{R}}T^{\star}(F(\cdot,t))(y,-t)dt.

It is immediate from (
4.4
) and (
4.3
) that

||T(F)||_{L^{2}_{y}}\leq\int_{\mathbb{R}}||T^{\star}(F(\cdot,t))(\cdot,-t)||_{L^{2}_{y}}dt=\int_{\mathbb{R}}||F(\cdot,-t)||_{L^{2}_{y}}dt=||F||_{L^{1}_{t}L^{2}_{y}}.

We summarize these results in the following.

Proposition 4.2
.

The operator defined by (
4.4
) can be uniquely extended to a bounded linear operator

T:L^{1,2}(\mathbb{R}^{n+1})\ \longrightarrow\ L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{n}),

satisfying the estimate

(4.5)

||T(F)||_{L^{2}_{y}}\leq||F||_{L^{1}_{t}L^{2}_{y}},

for every
F\in L^{1,2}(\mathbb{R}^{n+1})
.

Our next step is to prove the following critical result.

Theorem 4.3

(First Strichartz estimate)
.

Assume (
1.15
) in
Hypothesis (A)
. Suppose that
r>2
, and that
r<\frac{2D}{D-2}
when
D>2
. If
(q,r)
is admissible for (
1.3
), then there exists
C(n,r,q)>0
such that for every
F\in\mathscr{S}(\mathbb{R}^{n+1})
, one has

(4.6)

||T^{\star}T(F)||_{L^{q}_{t}L^{r}_{x}}\leq C(n,r,q)\ ||F||_{L^{q^{\prime}}_{t}L^{r^{\prime}}_{x}}.

Proof.

From (
4.2
) and (
4.4
) we have for
F\in\mathscr{S}(\mathbb{R}^{n+1})
,

\displaystyle T^{\star}(T(F))(x,t)=e^{\frac{t\operatorname{tr}B}{2}}U(t)(T(F))(x)=e^{\frac{t\operatorname{tr}B}{2}}U(t)\int_{\mathbb{R}}e^{-\frac{s\operatorname{tr}B}{2}}U(-s)(F(\cdot,s))(x)ds

\displaystyle=\int_{\mathbb{R}}e^{\frac{(t-s)\operatorname{tr}B}{2}}U(t-s)(F(\cdot,s))(x)ds

\displaystyle=\int_{-\infty}^{t}e^{\frac{(t-s)\operatorname{tr}B}{2}}U(t-s)(F(\cdot,s))(x)ds+\int_{t}^{\infty}e^{\frac{(t-s)\operatorname{tr}B}{2}}U(t-s)(F(\cdot,s))(x)ds

\displaystyle=I(x,t)+II(x,t).

Since in the integral in
I(x,t)
we have
t-s\geq 0
, from the first part of Theorem
3.5
we find for any
r\geq 2

\displaystyle||I(\cdot,t)||_{L^{r}_{x}}\leq\int_{-\infty}^{t}e^{\frac{(t-s)\operatorname{tr}B}{2}}||U(t-s)(F(\cdot,s))||_{L^{r}_{x}}ds

\displaystyle\leq C(n,r)\int_{-\infty}^{t}\frac{e^{(t-s)\operatorname{tr}B\left(\frac{1}{2}-\frac{1}{r}\right)}}{V(t-s)^{\frac{1}{2}-\frac{1}{r}}}\ ||F(\cdot,s)||_{L^{r^{\prime}}_{x}}ds.

Since in the integral in
II(x,t)
we have
t-s\leq 0
, from the second part of Theorem
3.5
we have

\displaystyle||II(\cdot,t)||_{L^{r}_{x}}\leq\int_{t}^{\infty}e^{\frac{(t-s)\operatorname{tr}B}{2}}||U(t-s)(F(\cdot,s))||_{L^{r}_{x}}ds

\displaystyle\leq C(n,r)\int_{t}^{\infty}\frac{e^{(s-t)\operatorname{tr}B\left(\frac{1}{2}-\frac{1}{r}\right)}}{V(s-t)^{\frac{1}{2}-\frac{1}{r}}}\ ||F(\cdot,s)||_{L^{r^{\prime}}_{x}}ds.

Combining the latter three equations, we conclude

(4.7)

\displaystyle||T^{\star}(T(F))(\cdot,t)||_{L^{r}_{x}}\leq C(n,r)\int_{\mathbb{R}}\left\{\frac{e^{|t-s|\operatorname{tr}B}}{V(|t-s|)}\right\}^{\left(\frac{1}{2}-\frac{1}{r}\right)}\ ||F(\cdot,s)||_{L^{r^{\prime}}_{x}}ds.

In view of the assumption (
1.15
), we obtain from (
4.7
) the following conclusion

(4.8)

||T^{\star}(T(F))(\cdot,t)||_{L^{r}_{x}}\leq C(n,\gamma,r)\int_{\mathbb{R}}\frac{||F(\cdot,s)||_{L^{r^{\prime}}_{x}}}{|t-s|^{D\left(\frac{1}{2}-\frac{1}{r}\right)}}\ ds.

If we now introduce the parameter
\beta
by the equation

(4.9)

1-\beta=D(\frac{1}{2}-\frac{1}{r}),

and consider the Riesz operator of fractional integration on the line

\mathcal{I}_{\beta}(h)(t)=\int_{\mathbb{R}}\frac{h(s)}{|t-s|^{1-\beta}}ds,

then it is clear from (
4.8
) that

(4.10)

||T^{\star}(T(F))(\cdot,t)||_{L^{r}_{x}}\leq C(n,\gamma,r)\ \mathcal{I}_{\beta}(||F(\cdot,\cdot)||_{L^{r^{\prime}}_{x}})(t).

Note that the bound
\beta\leq 1
is automatically true since
r\geq 2
. Furthermore,
\beta=1
if and only of
r=2
, so when
r>2
we have
\beta<1
for any
D\geq 2
. The inequality
\beta>0
instead, forces the condition

(4.11)

\frac{1}{2}-\frac{1}{D}<\frac{1}{r}.

When
D=2
this is automatically true for any
2\leq r<\infty
, whereas when
D>2
it imposes that
2\leq r<\frac{2D}{D-2}
. Since Definition
1.2
includes the restrictions
2<r<\infty
, and
r<\frac{2D}{D-2}
when
D>2
, we are guaranteed that the number
\beta
in (
4.9
) satisfy
0<\beta<1
. If we thus take
1<p<\frac{1}{\beta}
, and
\frac{1}{p}-\frac{1}{q}=\beta
, then the Hardy-Littlewood-Sobolev theorem implies that

(4.12)

\mathcal{I}_{\beta}:L^{p}(\mathbb{R})\to L^{q}(\mathbb{R})\ \Longleftrightarrow\ \frac{1}{p}-\frac{1}{q}=\beta=1-D(\frac{1}{2}-\frac{1}{r}),

and moreover

(4.13)

||\mathcal{I}_{\beta}(h)||_{L^{q}(\mathbb{R})}\leq C(p,\beta)||h||_{L^{p}(\mathbb{R})},

see
[
52
, Theor. 1, p. 119]
.
By the hypothesis that
(q,r)
be admissible for (
1.3
), we are allowed to take
p=q^{\prime}<q
in the equation
\frac{1}{p}-\frac{1}{q}=\beta
. This choice gives in fact

\frac{2}{q}=1-\beta=D(\frac{1}{2}-\frac{1}{r}),

which is precisely (
1.12
). From (
4.10
) and (
4.13
), we conclude the validity of (
4.6
).

∎

Next, we use Theorem
4.3
to establish the following result.

Theorem 4.4

(Second Strichartz estimate)
.

Under the hypothesis of Theorem
4.3
, for every
\varphi\in L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
one has

(4.14)

\displaystyle\|T^{\star}(\varphi)\|_{L^{q}_{t}L^{r}_{x}}\leq C(n,r,q)\|\varphi\|_{L^{2}_{x}}.

Proof.

First, one uses Theorem
4.3
to obtain the following basic estimate for the operator
T
. Observe that for every
F\in\mathscr{S}(\mathbb{R}^{n+1})
we have

(4.15)

\displaystyle\|T(F)\|_{L^{2}_{x}}^{2}=|\langle T(F),T(F)\rangle|=|\langle T^{\star}T(F),F\rangle|

\displaystyle\leq\|T^{\star}T(F)\|_{L^{q}_{t}L^{r}_{x}}\|F\|_{L^{q^{\prime}}_{t}L^{r^{\prime}}_{x}}\leq C(n,r,q)\ \|F\|^{2}_{L^{q^{\prime}}_{t}L^{r^{\prime}}_{x}}.

But then we have for
\varphi\in L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{n})

\displaystyle|\langle T^{\star}(\varphi),F\rangle|=|\langle\varphi,T(F)\rangle|\leq\|\varphi\|_{L^{2}_{x}}\|T(F)\|_{L^{2}_{x}}\leq C(n,r,q)\|\varphi\|_{L^{2}_{x}}\|F\|_{L^{q^{\prime}}_{t}L^{r^{\prime}}_{x}}.

By taking the supremum of all
F\in L^{q^{\prime}}_{t}L^{r^{\prime}}_{x}\cap L^{1}_{t}L^{2}_{x}
, we conclude that (
4.14
) holds.

∎

With these results in hand, we can finally give the

Proof of Theorem A.

Recalling (
4.2
), we now reformulate (
4.14
) in the following way:

(4.16)

\left(\int_{\mathbb{R}}e^{\frac{q\operatorname{tr}B}{2}t}\left(\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}|U(t)\varphi(x)|^{r}dx\right)^{\frac{q}{r}}dt\right)^{\frac{1}{q}}\leq C(n,r,q)\|\varphi\|_{L^{2}_{x}}.

This estimate establishes the homogeneous part of (
1.17
). We now turn to analysing the non-homogeneous term in (
4.1
).
From the first estimate in Theorem
3.5
we have

\displaystyle||\int_{0}^{t}U(t-s)(F(\cdot,s))(\cdot)ds||_{L^{r}_{x}}\leq\int_{0}^{t}||U(t-s)(F(\cdot,s))(\cdot)||_{L^{r}_{x}}ds

\displaystyle\leq C(n,r)\int_{0}^{t}\frac{e^{-\frac{\operatorname{tr}B}{r}(t-s)}}{V(t-s)^{\frac{1}{2}-\frac{1}{r}}}\ ||F(\cdot,s)||_{L^{r^{\prime}}_{x}}ds

\displaystyle\leq C(n,r,\gamma)\int_{0}^{t}\frac{e^{-\frac{\operatorname{tr}B}{r}(t-s)}e^{-\left(\frac{1}{2}-\frac{1}{r}\right)\operatorname{tr}B(t-s)}}{(t-s)^{D\left(\frac{1}{2}-\frac{1}{r}\right)}}\ ||F(\cdot,s)||_{L^{r^{\prime}}_{x}}ds,

where in the last inequality we have used the assumption (
1.15
). This estimate implies

\displaystyle e^{\frac{\operatorname{tr}B}{2}t}\ ||\int_{0}^{t}U(t-s)(F(\cdot,s))(\cdot)ds||_{L^{r}_{x}}\leq C(n,r,\gamma)\int_{0}^{t}\frac{e^{\frac{\operatorname{tr}B}{2}s}||F(\cdot,s)||_{L^{r^{\prime}}_{x}}}{(t-s)^{D\left(\frac{1}{2}-\frac{1}{r}\right)}}ds

\displaystyle=C(n,r,\gamma)\int_{\mathbb{R}}\frac{h(s)}{(t-s)^{D\left(\frac{1}{2}-\frac{1}{r}\right)}}ds=C(n,r,\gamma)\ \mathcal{I}_{\beta}(h)(t),

where, with
\beta
as in (
4.9
), we have let

h(s)=e^{\frac{\operatorname{tr}B}{2}s}||F(\cdot,s)||_{L^{r^{\prime}}_{x}}.

Using again (
4.13
), we infer

(4.17)

\displaystyle\left(\int_{\mathbb{R}}e^{t\frac{q\operatorname{tr}B}{2}}||\int_{0}^{t}U(t-s)(F(\cdot,s))(\cdot)ds||^{q}_{L^{r}_{x}}dt\right)^{\frac{1}{q}}\leq C^{\star}(n,r,\gamma,D)\ ||e^{\frac{\operatorname{tr}B}{2}t}||F(\cdot,t)||_{L^{r^{\prime}}_{x}}||_{L^{q^{\prime}}_{t}}.

Combining (
4.16
) and (
4.17
), and keeping the definition of (
4.2
) in mind, we have finally proved (
1.17
), thus completing the proof of Theorem
1
.

∎

4.1.
Ginibre-Velo implies restriction

We close this section with a remark concerning the connection between the estimate (
4.15
) and the restriction theorem for the Fourier transform. Consider the classical Schrödinger equation, for which
Q=I_{n}
and
B=O_{n}
. In such case, if we use Plancherel in the inequality (
4.15
), we obtain

(4.18)

\displaystyle\|\widehat{T(F)}\|_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{n})}\leq C(n,r)\ \|F\|_{L^{q^{\prime}}_{t}L^{r^{\prime}}_{x}}.

Keeping in mind that we presently have
D=n
, if we apply (
4.18
) with
q^{\prime}=r^{\prime}
as in (
1.14
), we obtain

(4.19)

\left(\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}|\widehat{T(F)}(\xi)|^{2}d\xi\right)^{1/2}\leq C(n)\ \|F\|_{L^{\frac{2(n+2)}{n+4}}(\mathbb{R}^{n+1})}.

The inequality (
4.19
) easily implies the Tomas-Stein restriction inequality for the truncated paraboloid. To see this, it suffices to observe that, when
Q=I_{n}
and
B=O_{n}
, the operator (
4.4
) becomes

T(F)(y)=\int_{\mathbb{R}}U(-t)(F(\cdot,t))(y)dt,

where
U(t)=e^{it\Delta}
. Therefore,
if we take the Fourier transform of (
4.4
), we obtain

(4.20)

\widehat{T(F)}(\xi)=\int_{\mathbb{R}}e^{4\pi^{2}it|\xi|^{2}}\hat{F}(\xi,t)dt=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n+1}}e^{-2\pi i\langle(\xi,-2\pi|\xi|^{2}),(x,t)\rangle}F(x,t)dxdt=\hat{F}(\xi,-2\pi|\xi|^{2}).

If for any
M>0
we consider the quadratic hypersurface

S_{M}=\{(\xi,\tau)\in\mathbb{R}^{n+1}\mid\tau=-2\pi|\xi|^{2},\ |\xi|\leq M\},

then the Riemannian measure on
S
is given by

d\sigma=\sqrt{1+16\pi^{2}|\xi|^{2}}.

Combining (
4.19
) with (
4.20
), we conclude that
there exists
C(n,M)>0
such that for any
F\in\mathscr{S}(\mathbb{R}^{n+1})

(4.21)

\left(\int_{S_{M}}|\hat{F}|^{2}d\sigma\right)^{1/2}\leq C(n,M)\ \|F\|_{L^{\frac{2(n+2)}{n+4}}(\mathbb{R}^{n+1})}.

Thus, the approach of Ginibre and Velo provides an elementary proof of the restriction theorem (
4.21
) for the Fourier transform.

5.
Proof of Theorem B

The aim of this section is to establish Strichartz estimates for (
1.3
) in situations, such as Example
6.4
, when the hypothesis (
1.15
) fails, and we must use (
1.16
) instead. Our objective is proving Theorem B.
Recall that a measurable function
g
belongs to the weak
L^{r}
space
L^{r,w}
if

\displaystyle\|g\|_{L^{r,w}}^{r}=\sup_{\lambda>0}\lambda^{r}\mu(\{t\mid|g(t)|>\lambda\})<\infty,

where we have denoted by
\mu
the measure on the relevant space.
Let
1<p,q,r<\infty
satisfy
\frac{1}{p}+\frac{1}{r}=\frac{1}{q}+1
. Then there exists a constant
C
such that for any
f\in L^{p}
and
g\in L^{r,w}
one has

(5.1)

\displaystyle\|f\star g\|_{q}\leq C\|f\|_{p}\|g\|_{r,w}.

For the proof of (
5.1
), see
[
48
]
. The next lemma is well-known, but for completeness we recall its simple proof.

Lemma 5.1
.

Let
0<\gamma_{1},\gamma_{2}<1
,
C_{1},C_{2}>0
. Let
k:\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R}
be such that

\displaystyle|k(t)|\leq\begin{cases}\frac{C_{1}}{|t|^{\gamma_{1}}}\ \ \ \ \text{if }|t|\leq 1,\\
\frac{C_{2}}{|t|^{\gamma_{2}}}\ \ \ \ \text{if }|t|\geq 1.\end{cases}

If
1<p_{1}<q_{1}<\infty
,
1<p_{2},q_{2}<\infty

\displaystyle\gamma_{1}=1+\frac{1}{q_{1}}-\frac{1}{p_{1}},\ \ \ \text{and}\ \ \ \ \gamma_{2}\geq 1+\frac{1}{q_{2}}-\frac{1}{p_{2}},

then one has

(5.2)

\displaystyle\|f\star k\|_{L^{q_{1}}+L^{q_{2}}}\leq C\|f\|_{L^{p_{1}}\cap L^{p_{2}}}.

Proof.

Let
f\in L^{p_{1}}\cap L^{p_{2}}
, and set
I=(-1,1)
. If we let
k_{1}=\mathbf{1}_{I}\ k
and
k_{2}=\mathbf{1}_{\mathbb{R}\setminus I}\ k
, we can write

\displaystyle f\star k=f\star k_{1}+f\star k_{2}.

Since
\frac{1}{p_{1}}-\frac{1}{q_{1}}=1-\gamma_{1}
, the Hardy-Littlewood-Sobolev inequality (or the fact that
k_{1}\in L^{1/\gamma_{1},w}(\mathbb{R})
and (
5.1
)) implies

\displaystyle\|k_{1}\star f\|_{L^{q_{1}}}\lesssim\|f\|_{L^{p_{1}}}.

For
\lambda>0
we now have

\{t\in\mathbb{R}\mid|k_{2}(t)|>\lambda\}\subset\{|t|\geq 1\mid\frac{C_{2}}{|t|^{\gamma_{2}}}>\lambda\}=\{|t|\geq 1\mid|t|<(C_{2}\lambda^{-1})^{1/\gamma_{2}}\},

provided that
0<\lambda\leq C_{2}
. If instead
\lambda>C_{2}
, then

We begin with an elementary consequence of the positivity of the matrix in (

Lemma 6.1
.

The function

.

Proof.

It immediately follows from (

∎

Next, we make the trivial observation that, since
Q
and
B
are assumed to satisfy
(H)

Q
and
-B

(6.1)

\tilde{Q}(t)=\int_{0}^{t}e^{-\tau B}Qe^{-\tau B^{\star}}d\tau,

for every

s=t-\tau
in the definition of

(6.2)

Taking the determinant of both sides of (

(6.3)

\frac{V(t)}{e^{2t\operatorname{tr}B}}=\tilde{V}(t),

The function

\frac{V(t)}{e^{2t\operatorname{tr}B}}\geq\tilde{V}(1)>0.

∎

We will also need the following result.

Proposition 6.3
.

Suppose that
\operatorname{Rank}(Q)=n
, and that
\sigma(B)\subset i\mathbb{R}
. There exist
D_{\infty}\geq n
and
\gamma>0
, such that for
t\geq 1
one has

\displaystyle V(t)\geq\gamma t^{D_{\infty}}.

Proof.

Since
Q>0
, there exist
\lambda>0
such that
Q\geq\lambda I
. For any
\xi\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
with
\xi\not=0
, we have

\displaystyle\langle Q(t)\xi,\xi\rangle=\int_{0}^{t}\langle e^{sB}Qe^{sB^{\star}}\xi,\xi\rangle ds\geq\lambda\int_{0}^{t}\langle e^{sB}e^{sB^{\star}}\xi,\xi\rangle ds=\lambda\langle\left(\int_{0}^{t}e^{sB}e^{sB^{\star}}ds\right)\xi,\xi\rangle.

By Weyl’s monotonicity theorem, we infer that

V(t)\geq\lambda^{n}\det\int_{0}^{t}e^{sB}e^{sB^{\star}}ds.

Writing
B
in its real Jordan form
B=PJP^{-1}
, see
[
24
, Theor.3.4.5]
, we obtain

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{t}e^{sB}e^{sB^{\star}}ds=P\left(\int_{0}^{t}e^{sJ}P^{-1}(P^{-1})^{\star}e^{sJ^{\star}}ds\right)P^{\star},

which implies

\displaystyle V(t)\geq\lambda^{n}(\det P)^{2}\ \det\int_{0}^{t}e^{sJ}P^{-1}(P^{-1})^{\star}e^{sJ^{\star}}ds.

Since
P^{-1}(P^{-1})^{\star}>0
, arguing as before we see that there exists
\mu>0

\displaystyle\det\int_{0}^{t}e^{sJ}P^{-1}(P^{-1})^{\star}e^{sJ^{\star}}ds\geq\mu^{n}\det\int_{0}^{t}e^{sJ}e^{sJ^{\star}}ds.

These considerations show that, to complete the proof, it suffices to prove the existence of
D_{\infty}\geq n
and
\gamma_{0}>0
, such that

(6.5)

\det\int_{0}^{t}e^{sJ}e^{sJ^{\star}}ds\ \geq\ \gamma_{0}\ t^{D_{\infty}}\ \ \ \ \ \ t\geq 1.

Since
\sigma(B)\subset i\mathbb{R}
, we have
\sigma(J)=\sigma(J^{\star})=\{0,\pm i\gamma_{1},...,\pm i\gamma_{p}\}
. Denoting with
n_{1}
the multiplicity of the eigenvalue
\lambda=0
, and with
m_{\ell}
that of the eigenvalue
\lambda=i\gamma_{\ell}
,
\ell=1,...,p
, we have
n_{1}+2m_{1}+...+2m_{p}=n
. Since
J=\operatorname{diag}(J_{n_{1}},C_{m_{1}}(\gamma_{1}),...,C_{m_{p}}(\gamma_{p}))
, by
[
6
, Prop.11.2.8,(vi)]
, we have

\det\int_{0}^{t}e^{sJ}e^{sJ^{\star}}ds=\det\int_{0}^{t}e^{sJ_{n_{1}}}e^{sJ_{n_{1}}^{\star}}ds\ \prod_{\ell=1}^{p}\det\int_{0}^{t}e^{sC_{m_{\ell}}(\gamma_{\ell})}e^{sC_{m_{\ell}}(\gamma_{\ell})^{\star}}ds.

The proof will be completed if we show that there exist numbers

\gamma_{n_{1}}>0,\ \ d_{n_{1}}\geq n_{1},\ \ \ \text{and}\ \ \ \ \ \ \gamma_{m_{\ell}}>0,\ \ d_{m_{\ell}}\geq m_{\ell},\ \ \ \ell=1,....,p,

such that

(6.6)

\det\int_{0}^{t}e^{sJ_{n_{1}}}e^{sJ_{n_{1}}^{\star}}ds\ \geq\ \gamma_{n_{1}}t^{d_{n_{1}}},\ \ \ \ \det\int_{0}^{t}e^{sC_{m_{\ell}}(\gamma_{\ell})}e^{sC_{m_{\ell}}(\gamma_{\ell})^{\star}}ds\ \geq\ \gamma_{m_{\ell}}\ t^{2d_{m_{\ell}}},\ \ \ \ t\geq 1.

We recall that the
n_{1}\times n_{1}
matrix
J_{n_{1}}
and the
2m_{\ell}\times 2m_{\ell}
matrices
C_{m_{\ell}}(\gamma_{\ell})
are respectively in the form

\displaystyle J_{n_{1}}=\begin{pmatrix}0&1&0&\cdot&\cdot&0\\
0&0&1&\cdot&\cdot&0\\
\cdot&\cdot&\cdot&\cdot&\cdot&\cdot\\
\cdot&\cdot&\cdot&\cdot&\cdot&\cdot\\
\cdot&\cdot&\cdot&\cdot&\cdot&1\\
0&\cdot&\cdot&\cdot&\cdot&0\end{pmatrix},\ \ \ C_{m_{\ell}}(\gamma_{\ell})=\begin{pmatrix}C(\gamma_{\ell})&I_{2}&0&\cdot&\cdot&\cdot&0\\
0&C(\gamma_{\ell})&I_{2}&0&\cdot&\cdot&0\\
\cdot&\cdot&\cdot&\cdot&\cdot&\cdot&\cdot\\
0&\cdot&\cdot&\cdot&\cdot&\cdot&I_{2}\\
0&\cdot&\cdot&\cdot&\cdot&0&C(\gamma_{\ell})\end{pmatrix},

where

\displaystyle C(\gamma_{\ell})=\begin{pmatrix}0&-\gamma_{\ell}\\
\gamma_{\ell}&0\end{pmatrix},\ \ \ I_{2}=\begin{pmatrix}1&0\\
0&1\end{pmatrix},

see Theorem 3.4.1.5, (3.1.2) and (3.4.1.4) in
[
24
]
.
Since the matrix
J_{n_{1}}
is nilpotent, we have

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{t}e^{sJ_{n_{1}}}e^{sJ^{\star}_{n_{1}}}ds=\int_{0}^{t}\big(\sum_{i=0}^{n_{1}}\frac{(sJ_{n_{1}})^{i}}{i!}\big)\big(\sum_{i=0}^{n_{1}}\frac{(sJ_{n_{1}}^{\star})^{i}}{i!}\big)ds=tI_{n_{1}}+P^{(n_{1})}(t),

where

is a matrix whose entries are polynomials of degree at least
2
. We infer that
there exist
\gamma_{n_{1}}>0
and
d_{n_{1}}\geq n_{1}
such that if
t\geq 1

\displaystyle\det\int_{0}^{t}e^{sJ_{n_{1}}}e^{sJ_{n_{1}}^{\star}}ds\ \geq\ \gamma_{n_{1}}t^{d_{n_{1}}}.

Next, by (11.2.15) in
[
6
]
, we can write

C_{m_{\ell}}(\gamma_{\ell})=D_{m_{\ell}}(\gamma_{\ell})+N_{m_{\ell}}(\gamma_{\ell}),

where

is skew-symmetric,

is nilpotent, and they commute. We thus have

e^{sC_{m_{\ell}}(\gamma_{\ell})}=e^{sN_{m_{\ell}}(\gamma_{\ell})}e^{sD_{m_{\ell}}(\gamma_{\ell})},

see
[

, Cor.11.1.6]
.
Since
D_{m_{\ell}}(\gamma_{\ell})
is skew-symmetric, the matrix

is orthogonal, and we thus obtain

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{t}e^{sC_{m_{\ell}}(\gamma_{\ell})}e^{sC_{m_{\ell}}^{\star}(\gamma_{\ell})}ds=

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{t}e^{sN_{m_{\ell}}(\gamma_{\ell})}e^{sN^{\star}_{m_{\ell}}(\gamma_{\ell})}ds=

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{t}\big(\sum_{i=0}^{2m_{\ell}}\frac{(sN_{m_{\ell}}(\gamma_{\ell}))^{i}}{i!}\big)\big(\sum_{i=0}^{2m_{\ell}}\frac{(sN_{m_{\ell}}^{\star}(\gamma_{\ell}))^{i}}{i!}\big)ds=tI_{2m_{\ell}}+P^{(m_{\ell})}(t)>0

where

is a matrix whose entries are polynomials of degree at least
2
.
We conclude as before that there exist
\gamma_{m_{\ell}}>0
and

such that, if

,

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{t}e^{sC_{m_{\ell}}(\gamma_{\ell})}e^{sC_{m_{\ell}}^{\star}(\gamma_{\ell})}ds\geq\gamma_{m_{\ell}}t^{2d_{m_{\ell}}}.

This completes the proof of (
6.6
).

∎

We can now give the

Proof of Theorem

.

We begin by noting that, based on (

) holds for

, regardless of the value of

Assume now the hypothesis (i) in the statement of the theorem. There are three possible cases.

Case (1)

\operatorname{tr}B<0
. If (

t\geq 1
the desired conclusion (
1.15

\operatorname{tr}B<0

From this estimate we infer that the inequality (

Since

\operatorname{tr}B>0

t\geq 1

V(t)\geq\gamma_{0}\ e^{2t\operatorname{tr}B},

for some constant
\gamma_{0}>0

\gamma>0

Case (3)

\operatorname{tr}B=0

Example 6.4

(Failure of Hypothesis (A))
.

Consider
2\times 2
matrices
Q
and
B
as in (
1.8
), with
\operatorname{Rank}(Q)<2
, and
\sigma(B)\subset i\mathbb{R}
. Excluding the case
\sigma(B)=\{0\}
, the other possibility is
\sigma(B)=\{-i\gamma,i\gamma\}
with
\gamma\not=0
. Under this assumption, we can assume that

Q=\begin{pmatrix}1&0\\
0&0\end{pmatrix},\ \ \ \ \ B=\begin{pmatrix}a&b\\
c&-a\end{pmatrix},

with
a,b
and
c
satisfying the equation

-bc=a^{2}+\gamma^{2}>0.

The condition
(H)
imposes that
c\not=0
, and therefore also
b\not=0
, but the coefficient
a
can be any real number. By the two-dimensional Rodrigues’ formula, see e.g.
[
6
, Prop.11.3.2]
, we have

e^{sB}=\cos(s\gamma)I+\frac{\sin(s\gamma)}{\gamma}B.

This easily gives

e^{sB}Qe^{sB^{\star}}=\cos^{2}(s\gamma)Q+\frac{\sin(2s\gamma)}{2\gamma}(QB^{\star}+BQ)+\frac{\sin^{2}(s\gamma)}{\gamma^{2}}BQB^{\star}.

Integrating this expression on
(0,t)
, after some elementary computations, we obtain

(6.8)

\displaystyle Q(t)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\left[t+\frac{\sin(2t\gamma)}{2}\right]Q+\frac{1}{4}\left[\frac{1}{2}-\cos(2t\gamma)\right](QB^{\star}+BQ)+\frac{1}{2\gamma^{2}}\left[t-\frac{\sin(2t\gamma)}{2}\right]BQB^{\star}

\displaystyle=\frac{t}{2}\left(C+\frac{A(t)}{t}\right),

where

(6.9)

C=Q+\frac{1}{\gamma^{2}}BQB^{\star}=\begin{pmatrix}1+\frac{a^{2}}{\gamma^{2}}&\frac{ac}{\gamma^{2}}\\
\frac{ac}{\gamma^{2}}&\frac{c^{2}}{\gamma^{2}}\end{pmatrix},

and
A(t)
is a matrix with bounded entries. Note that
\det C=\frac{c^{2}}{\gamma^{2}}>0
, therefore
C>0
. Since we will need a delicate generalization to higher dimension of this critical property (see the proof of Theorem
1.4
), we also give an alternative proof of the positivity of
C
. For every
x\in\mathbb{R}^{2}
, we have

\langle Cx,x\rangle=|Q^{1/2}x|^{2}+\frac{1}{\gamma^{2}}|Q^{1/2}B^{\star}x|^{2}.

Suppose that for some
x\not=0
, we have
\langle Cx,x\rangle=0
. Then we must have both
x,B^{\star}x\in\operatorname{Ker}(Q)
, and therefore
E=\operatorname{span}\{x,B^{\star}x\}\subset\operatorname{Ker}(Q)
. But
E
is a nontrival invariant subspace of
B^{\star}
. To see this, note that the Cayley-Hamilton theorem gives
(B^{\star})^{2}+\gamma^{2}I=0
, hence for any
\alpha,\beta\in\mathbb{R}
we have

B^{\star}(\alpha x+\beta B^{\star}x)=\alpha B^{\star}x-\beta\gamma x\ \in E.

Since this contradicts
(H)
, we infer that it must be
\langle Cx,x\rangle>0
.
Now, note that (
6.8
) implies the existence of
\gamma>0
such that

V(t)=\gamma t^{2}\det(I+C^{-1}\frac{A(t)}{t}).

Applying the formula (0.8.12) in
[
24
]
,

(6.10)

\displaystyle\det(A+B)=\det A+\det B+\det A\operatorname{tr}(A^{-1}B),

with
A=I
and
B=C^{-1}\frac{A(t)}{t}
, we reach the conclusion that

(6.11)

V(t)=\gamma t^{2}(1+O(t^{-1})),\ \ \ \ \ \text{as}\ t\to\infty.

On the other hand, comparing with (
1.8
) we see that, in the present example, we have
p_{0}=\operatorname{Rank}(Q)=1
, and that
B=\begin{pmatrix}\star_{1}&\star_{2}\\
B_{1}&\star_{3}\end{pmatrix}
, with

\star_{1}=(a),\ \ \star_{2}=(b),\ \ B_{1}=(c),\ \ \star_{3}=(-a),

so that
p_{1}=\operatorname{Rank}(B_{1})=1
.
We conclude from (
1.9
) that the local homogeneous dimension is
D=p_{0}+3p_{1}=4
.
Therefore, in view of (
6.11
) it is clear that (
1.15
) cannot possibly hold. Instead, (
1.16
) holds, with
D_{\infty}=2
.

Theorem
1.4
provides a generalization to arbitrary dimension of the large time behavior (

) of the volume function.

Proof of Theorem
1.4
.

Since when
n=2
we have already proved this result in Example

, we can assume

.
As in (
6.11
), it suffices to show that, for some
\gamma>0
, one has for
t\to\infty

(6.12)

\displaystyle V(t)=\gamma t^{n}+O(t^{n-1}).

Since it will be relevant in the proof of (
6.12
), we recall some facts concerning the case
n=3
.
Consider an arbitrary skew-symmetric matrix
B\in M_{3\times 3}(\mathbb{R})

\displaystyle B=\begin{pmatrix}0&-c&b\\
c&0&-a\\
-b&a&0\end{pmatrix}.

We have
\sigma(B)=\{0,\pm i\theta\}
, with
\theta=\sqrt{a^{2}+b^{2}+c^{2}}
, see (1.48) in
[
39
]
. Since the characteristic polynomial is

p_{B}(\lambda)=\lambda(\lambda^{2}+\theta^{2}),

if
\theta>0
the Cayley-Hamilton theorem gives

\displaystyle\frac{1}{\theta^{3}}B^{3}=-\frac{1}{\theta}B.

This identity is the key to the formula of O. Rodrigues which states

(6.13)

\displaystyle e^{B}=I_{3}+\frac{\sin\theta}{\theta}B+\frac{1-\cos\theta}{\theta^{2}}B^{2},

see (2.14) in
[
46
]
. This immediately gives

\displaystyle e^{sB}=I_{3}+\frac{\sin\theta s}{\theta}B+\frac{1-\cos\theta s}{\theta^{2}}B^{2}.

Moving back to the case of arbitrary
n\geq 3
, by the assumption on
B
there exist an invertible matrix
P
, and a matrix
J
, such that
J^{\star}=-J
,
for which

\displaystyle B=PJP^{-1}\ \Longrightarrow\ e^{sB}=Pe^{sJ}P^{-1}.

Denoting by
\tilde{Q}=P^{-1}Q(P^{-1})^{\star}
, we clearly have
\tilde{Q}\geq 0
, and
\operatorname{Rank}(\tilde{Q})<n
. Since moreover

\displaystyle Q(t)

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{t}e^{sB}Qe^{sB^{\star}}ds=P\int_{0}^{t}e^{sJ}\tilde{Q}e^{sJ^{\star}}dsP^{\star},

we have

V(t)=(\det P)^{2}\ \det\int_{0}^{t}e^{sJ}\tilde{Q}e^{sJ^{\star}}ds.

We can thus focus on showing that (
6.12
) holds when we assume from the start that
B=J
, i.e.,

V(t)=\det\int_{0}^{t}e^{sJ}Qe^{sJ^{\star}}ds.

Now, unless
J=O_{n}
(which is ruled out by the fact that, for
(H)
to hold, we would need
Q>0
, against the assumption), from (b) in
[
24
, Cor. 2.5.11]
we know that, for
q\geq 1
, the nonzero eigenvalues of
J
are
\pm i\gamma_{1},...,\pm i\gamma_{q}
,
\gamma_{k}>0
,
k=1,...,q
, and moreover there exists a real orthogonal matrix
U
such that

(6.14)

UJU^{-1}=O_{n-2q}\oplus\gamma_{1}\begin{pmatrix}0&-1\\
1&0\end{pmatrix}\oplus...\oplus\gamma_{q}\begin{pmatrix}0&-1\\
1&0\end{pmatrix}.

If we indicate by
\theta_{1},...,\theta_{p}
the positive imaginary parts of the distinct eigenvalues of
J
, then, using (
6.14
), it was proved in
[
16
, Theor. 2.2]
that there exist
p
unique skew-symmetric matrices
J_{1},...,J_{p}\in M_{n\times n}(\mathbb{R})
,
2p\leq n
, such that

(6.15)

J_{i}J_{j}=J_{j}J_{i}=O_{n},\ \ \ \ \ i\neq j,

(6.16)

J_{i}^{3}=-J_{i},\ \ \ \ \ \ i=1,...,p,

(6.17)

J=\theta_{1}J_{1}+...+\theta_{p}J_{p},

and, furthermore, the following generalization of Rodrigues’ formula (
6.13
) holds

(6.18)

\displaystyle e^{J}=I_{n}+\sum_{i=1}^{p}(\sin\theta_{i}J_{i}+(1-\cos\theta_{i})^{2}J_{i}^{2}).

Before proceeding, if
x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}\setminus\{0\}
, consider the subspace

V(x)=\operatorname{span}\{J_{1}x,J_{1}^{2}x,J_{2}x,J_{2}^{2}x,...,J_{p}x,J_{p}^{2}x\}.

We claim that:

(6.19)

J^{\star}(V(x))\subset V(x),

i.e.,
V(x)
is a nontrivial invariant space of
J^{\star}
. To see (
6.19
), let
y\in V(x)
. There exist
\alpha_{1},\beta_{1},...,\alpha_{p},\beta_{p}\in\mathbb{R}
such that

y=\alpha_{1}J_{1}x+\beta_{1}J_{1}^{2}x+...+\alpha_{p}J_{p}x+\beta_{p}J_{p}^{2}x.

This gives

\displaystyle J^{\star}y

\displaystyle=\sum_{i,j=1}^{p}\theta_{i}\alpha_{j}J^{\star}_{i}J_{j}x+\sum_{i,j=1}^{p}\theta_{i}\beta_{j}J^{\star}_{i}J^{2}_{j}x

\displaystyle=-\sum_{i,j=1}^{p}\theta_{i}\alpha_{j}J_{i}J_{j}x-\sum_{i,j=1}^{p}\theta_{i}\beta_{j}J_{i}J^{2}_{j}x

\displaystyle=-\sum_{i=1}^{p}\theta_{i}\alpha_{i}J_{i}^{2}x-\sum_{i=1}^{p}\theta_{i}\beta_{i}J_{i}^{3}x,

where we have used (
6.15
). By (
6.16
), we conclude that

J^{\star}y=-\sum_{i=1}^{p}\theta_{i}\alpha_{i}J_{i}^{2}x+\sum_{i=1}^{p}\theta_{i}\beta_{i}J_{i}x\ \in V(x),

which proves (
6.19
). Next, we note that, in view of
(H)
(which we are assuming for the matrix
J
), the inclusion (
6.19
) implies that

(6.20)

V(x)\not\subset\ker Q,\ \ \ \ \forall x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}\setminus\{0\}.

For the sequel, it is important to note that (
6.20
) implies that

(6.21)

\sum_{i=1}^{p}(|Q^{1/2}J_{i}x|^{2}+|Q^{1/2}J_{i}^{2}x|^{2})>0\ \ \ \ \forall x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}\setminus\{0\}.

Otherwise, we would have
QJ_{i}x=QJ_{i}^{2}x=0
for
i=1,...,p
, and therefore
V(x)\subset\ker Q
.
For subsequent purposes, we introduce the notation

\hat{J}=J_{1}+...+J_{p}.

We observe that
\hat{J}^{\star}=-\hat{J}
, and that, in view of (
6.15
), we have

(6.22)

\hat{J}^{2}=J_{1}^{2}+...+J_{p}^{2},\ \ \ \ (\hat{J}^{\star})^{2}=(J^{\star}_{1})^{2}+...+(J^{\star}_{p})^{2}.

To continue, note that (
6.18
) implies

\displaystyle e^{sJ}=I_{n}+\sum_{i=1}^{p}(\sin(\theta_{i}s)J_{i}+(1-\cos(\theta_{i}s))J_{i}^{2}).

Consequently, we have

(6.23)

\displaystyle e^{sJ}Qe^{sJ^{\star}}=Q

\displaystyle+\sum_{i=1}^{p}\bigg\{\sin(\theta_{i}s)J_{i}Q+(1-\cos(\theta_{i}s))J_{i}^{2}Q+\sin(\theta_{i}s)QJ_{i}^{\star}+(1-\cos(\theta_{i}s))Q(J_{i}^{\star})^{2}\bigg\}

\displaystyle+\sum_{i,j=1}^{p}\bigg\{\sin(\theta_{i}s)\sin(\theta_{j}s)J_{i}QJ_{j}^{\star}+(1-\cos(\theta_{i}s))(1-\cos(\theta_{j}s))J_{i}^{2}Q(J_{j}^{\star})^{2}\bigg\}

\displaystyle+\sum_{i,j=1}^{p}\bigg\{\sin(\theta_{i}s)(1-\cos(\theta_{j}s))J_{i}Q(J_{j}^{\star})^{2}+(1-\cos(\theta_{i}s))\sin(\theta_{j}s)J_{i}^{2}QJ_{j}^{\star}\bigg\}.

Integrating both sides of (
6.23
) in
s\in[0,t]
, using (
6.22
) and observing that (see (
6.8
))

\int_{0}^{t}\sin^{2}(\theta_{i}s)ds=\frac{t}{2}+O(1),\ \ \ \ \ \ \int_{0}^{t}(1-\cos(\theta_{i}s))^{2}ds=\frac{3t}{2}+O(1),

we reach the conclusion that

(6.24)

\displaystyle Q(t)

\displaystyle=tQ+t\hat{J}^{2}Q+tQ(\hat{J}^{\star})^{2}+t\sum_{i=1}^{p}\left\{\frac{1}{2}J_{i}QJ_{i}^{\star}+\frac{3}{2}J_{i}^{2}Q(J_{i}^{\star})^{2}\right\}+t\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}i,j=1,\\
i\neq j\end{subarray}}^{p}J_{i}^{2}Q(J_{j}^{\star})^{2}+D(t)

\displaystyle=tQ+t\hat{J}^{2}Q+tQ(\hat{J}^{\star})^{2}+t\sum_{i,j=1}^{p}J_{i}^{2}Q(J_{j}^{\star})^{2}+\frac{t}{2}\sum_{i=1}^{p}\left\{J_{i}QJ_{i}^{\star}+J_{i}^{2}Q(J_{i}^{\star})^{2}\right\}+D(t)

\displaystyle=tQ+t\hat{J}^{2}Q+tQ(\hat{J}^{\star})^{2}+t\hat{J}^{2}Q(\hat{J}^{\star})^{2}+\frac{t}{2}\sum_{i=1}^{p}\left\{J_{i}QJ_{i}^{\star}+J_{i}^{2}Q(J_{i}^{\star})^{2}\right\}+D(t)

\displaystyle=tC+D(t),

where

(6.25)

C=Q+\hat{J}^{2}Q+Q(\hat{J}^{\star})^{2}+\hat{J}^{2}Q(\hat{J}^{\star})^{2}+\frac{1}{2}\sum_{i=1}^{p}(J_{i}QJ_{i}^{\star}+J_{i}^{2}Q(J_{i}^{\star})^{2}),

and we have denoted by
D(t)
a matrix with bounded entries. We now make the following crucial

Claim:

C>0
.

To prove the claim, we show that for any
x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}\setminus\{0\}
, we must have

(6.26)

\langle Cx,x\rangle>0.

From (
6.25
) we have

(6.27)

\displaystyle\langle Cx,x\rangle

\displaystyle=|Q^{1/2}x|^{2}-2\langle\hat{J}Qx,\hat{J}x\rangle+|Q^{1/2}\hat{J}^{2}x|^{2}+\frac{1}{2}\sum_{i=1}^{p}(|Q^{1/2}J_{i}x|^{2}+|Q^{1/2}J_{i}^{2}x|^{2})

\displaystyle=|Q^{1/2}x|^{2}+2\langle Q^{1/2}x,Q^{1/2}\hat{J}^{2}x\rangle+|Q^{1/2}\hat{J}^{2}x|^{2}+\frac{1}{2}\sum_{i=1}^{p}(|Q^{1/2}J_{i}x|^{2}+|Q^{1/2}J_{i}^{2}x|^{2}).

There exist two possibilities:

Case (i):

x\in\ker Q
. In such case, we obtain from (
6.27
)

\langle Cx,x\rangle=|Q^{1/2}\hat{J}^{2}x|^{2}+\frac{1}{2}\sum_{i=1}^{p}(|Q^{1/2}J_{i}x|^{2}+|Q^{1/2}J_{i}^{2}x|^{2})>0,

where the last inequality is justified by (
6.21
).

Case (ii):

x\not\in\ker Q
. The Cauchy-Schwarz inequality gives

-2\langle Q^{1/2}x,Q^{1/2}\hat{J}^{2}x\rangle\leq 2|Q^{1/2}x||Q^{1/2}\hat{J}^{2}x|.

Using this inequality in (
6.27
), we obtain

\langle Cx,x\rangle\geq\left(|Q^{1/2}x|-|Q^{1/2}\hat{J}^{2}x|\right)^{2}+\frac{1}{2}\sum_{i=1}^{p}(|Q^{1/2}J_{i}x|^{2}+|Q^{1/2}J_{i}^{2}x|^{2})>0,

where, again, the last inequality follows from (
6.21
). We have thus established (
6.26
).

Returning to (
6.24
), we see that the claim allows to represent

(6.28)

Q(t)=tC\left[I+(tC)^{-1}D(t)\right].

To finish, we apply the formula

(6.29)

\det(I+X)=\sum_{k=0}^{n}\operatorname{tr}\text{adj}_{k}(X),

valid for any
X\in M_{n\times n}(\mathbb{R})
(see
[
24
, p. 29]
), with the choice
X=(tC)^{-1}D(t)
. Here, for
k=0,1,...,n
, we have let
n(k)=\begin{pmatrix}n\\
k\end{pmatrix}
, and denoted by
\text{adj}_{k}(X)\in M_{n(k)\times n(k)}(\mathbb{R})
the
k
-th adjugate matrix of
X
, see
[
24
, (0.8.12.1)]
. Keeping in mind that

\operatorname{adj}_{n}(X)=1,\ \ \ \operatorname{adj}_{0}(X)=\det X,\ \ \ \operatorname{adj}_{1}(X)=X,

and noting that, for
1\leq k\leq(n-1)
,

\displaystyle\operatorname{adj}_{k}((tC)^{-1}D(t))=\frac{1}{t^{n-k}}\operatorname{adj}_{k}(C^{-1}D(t))\implies\operatorname{tr}(\operatorname{adj}_{k}(\frac{1}{t}C^{-1}D(t)))=O(\frac{1}{t^{n-k}}),

we finally obtain from (
6.28
) and (
6.29
)

\displaystyle V(t)=\gamma t^{n}+O(t^{n-1}),

where
\gamma=\det C>0
. This proves (
6.12
) when
B=J
, and we are thus finished.

∎

7.
Examples and final comments

The purpose of this final section is to illustrate the scope of our results with some significant examples. We begin with the following generalization of the problem (
2.5
) in Section
2.1
.

Proposition 7.1
.

In
\mathbb{R}^{n}\times\mathbb{R}
, consider the Cauchy problem

(7.1)

\partial_{t}u-i\Delta u-\langle Bx,\nabla u\rangle=F(x,t),\ \ \ \ \ \ \ u(x,0)=\varphi(x).

Fix
r>2
, such that
r<\frac{2n}{n-2}
when
n\geq 3
, and consider a pair
(q,r)
satisfying
\frac{2}{q}=n\left(\frac{1}{2}-\frac{1}{r}\right)
. Then one has for the
solution
u
of (
7.1
)

(7.2)

\displaystyle\bigg(\int_{\mathbb{R}}e^{\frac{q\operatorname{tr}B}{2}t}\big(\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}|u(x,t)|^{r}dx\big)^{\frac{q}{r}}dt\bigg)^{\frac{1}{q}}\leq C\bigg\{\|\varphi\|_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{n})}+\bigg(\int_{\mathbb{R}}e^{\frac{q^{\prime}\operatorname{tr}B}{2}t}\big(\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}|F(x,t)|^{r^{\prime}}dx\big)^{\frac{q^{\prime}}{r^{\prime}}}dt\bigg)^{\frac{1}{q^{\prime}}}\bigg\}.

Proof.

Since
\operatorname{Rank}(Q)=n
, according to Definition
1.1
the local homogeneous dimension of (
7.1
) is
D=n
. Therefore, the admissibility range from (
1.12
) is the same as in
[
21
]
, and the estimate (
7.2
) follows from Theorem A. When, in particular,
B=-I_{n}
, we recover problem (
2.5
).
If instead the matrix
B
satisfies one of the hypothesis (i), (ii) in Theorem
1.3
, then
\operatorname{tr}B=0
, and therefore (
7.2
) gives for (
7.1
) the same estimate that one would obtain for a zero drift.

∎

Next, we discuss an interesting family of problems in which the Hamiltonian displays a varying degeneracy. Fix
n\geq 2
and for any
1\leq k\leq n
, write points
x=(x_{1},...,x_{n})\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
as
(\hat{x}_{k},x_{k+1},...,x_{n})
, where
\hat{x}_{k}=(x_{1},...,x_{k})
. Let
\hat{\Delta}_{k}=\partial_{x_{1}x_{1}}+...+\partial_{x_{k}x_{k}}
represent the Laplacian with respect to the coordinates
\hat{x}_{k}
.

Proposition 7.2
.

For each
k=1,...,n
, consider the Cauchy problem for the Schrödinger equation in
\mathbb{R}^{n}\times(0,\infty)

(7.3)

\partial_{t}u=i\hat{\Delta}_{k}u+x_{1}\partial_{x_{2}}u+...+x_{n-1}\partial_{x_{n}}u+F(x,t),\ \ \ \ \ u(x,0)=\varphi(x).

Then the local homogeneous dimension of (
7.3
) is given by the number

(7.4)

D_{n}(k)=n+(n-k+1)(n-k),\ \ \ \ \ \ 1\leq k\leq n,

with corresponding
Strichartz pairs

(7.5)

r_{n}(k)=\frac{2(D_{n}(k)+2)}{D_{n}(k)}\nearrow,\ \ \ \ \ r^{\prime}_{n}(k)=\frac{2(D_{n}(k)+2)}{D_{n}(k)+4}\searrow,\ \ \ \ \ \ 1\leq k\leq n.

Let
q_{n}(k)
be the exponent defined by the equation

(7.6)

\frac{2}{q_{n}(k)}=D_{n}(k)\left(\frac{1}{2}-\frac{1}{r_{n}(k)}\right),\ \ \ \ \ \ 1\leq k\leq n.

Then the following Strichartz estimate holds

(7.7)

\displaystyle\bigg(\int_{\mathbb{R}}||u(\cdot,t)||^{q_{n}(k)}_{L^{r_{n}(k)}(\mathbb{R}^{n})}dt\bigg)^{\frac{1}{q_{n}(k)}}\leq C\bigg\{\|\varphi\|_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{n})}+\bigg(\int_{\mathbb{R}}||F(\cdot,t)||^{q^{\prime}_{n}(k)}_{L^{r^{\prime}_{n}(k)}(\mathbb{R}^{n})}dt\bigg)^{\frac{1}{q^{\prime}_{n}(k)}}\bigg\}.

Proof.

The differential equation in (
7.3
) can be represented in the form (
1.3
), if we take

(7.8)

Q=Q(k)=\begin{pmatrix}I_{k}&O_{k\times(n-k)}\\
O_{(n-k)\times k}&O_{(n-k)\times(n-k)}\end{pmatrix},\ \ \ B=\begin{pmatrix}0&0&\cdot&\cdot&\cdot&0&0\\
1&0&\cdot&\cdot&\cdot&0&0\\
0&1&\cdot&\cdot&\cdot&0&0\\
\cdot&\cdot&\cdot&\cdot&\cdot&\cdot&\cdot\\
\cdot&\cdot&\cdot&\cdot&\cdot&\cdot&\cdot\\
\cdot&\cdot&\cdot&\cdot&\cdot&\cdot&\cdot\\
0&0&\cdot&\cdot&\cdot&1&0\end{pmatrix}.

Note that
\sigma(B)=\{0\}
,
B
is nilpotent since
B^{n}=O_{n}
, and
B^{\star}e_{j}=e_{j-1}
for
j=1,...,n
. Therefore, the only nontrivial invariant subspaces of
B^{\star}
are

E_{j}=\operatorname{span}\{e_{1},...,e_{j}\},\ \ \ \ \ \ j=1,...,n-1.

Since
\operatorname{Ker}Q(k)=\operatorname{span}\{e_{k+1},...,e_{n}\}
, it is clear that the matrices
Q(k)
and
B
satisfy the hypothesis
(H)
. To compute the homogeneous dimension of (
7.3
), for each
1\leq k\leq n
, we need to write
B
in the canonical form (
1.8
) corresponding to
Q(k)
. We start by considering
k=n
. In this case we clearly have
p_{0}=n
and
B_{1}=B
. Since
p_{j}=0
if
j>1
, we find

\displaystyle D_{n}(n)=n.

If instead
k\in\{1,...,n-1\}
, we write

(7.9)

\displaystyle B=\begin{pmatrix}\star_{(1)}&\star_{(2)}&\star_{(3)}&\cdot&\star_{(n-k)}&\star_{(n-k+1)}\\
B_{1}&0&\\
0&B_{2}&0\\
\cdot&\cdot&\cdot\\
0&\cdot&\cdot&0&B_{n-k}&0\end{pmatrix},

where

\star_{(1)}
is the
k\times k
matrix

\displaystyle\star_{(1)}=\begin{pmatrix}0&0&\cdot&\cdot&0\\
1&0&\cdot&\cdot&0\\
\cdot&\cdot&\cdot&\cdot&\cdot\\
0&\cdot&\cdot&1&0\end{pmatrix},

\star_{(\ell)}
is the
(k+\ell-2)\times 1
zero matrix, and

\displaystyle B_{1}=\begin{pmatrix}0&\cdot&\cdot&\cdot&1\end{pmatrix}\in M_{1\times k}(\mathbb{R}),\ \ \ \ B_{2}=...=B_{n-k}=(1)\in M_{1\times 1}(\mathbb{R}).

Consequently, we have
p_{0}=k
,
p_{1}=p_{2}=...=p_{n-k}=1
, and
p_{j}=0
if
j>n-k
. It thus follows that

\displaystyle D_{n}(k)

\displaystyle=k+3p_{1}+5p_{2}+...+(2(n-k)+1)p_{n-k}=

\displaystyle=k+\sum_{j=1}^{n-k}(2j+1)=n+(n-k+1)(n-k),

which proves (
7.4
). Once the local homogeneous dimension
D_{n}(k)
is known, the Strichartz pairs (
7.5
), and the admissibility pairs
(q_{n}(k),r_{n}(k))
in (
7.6
) are respectively determined by (
1.13
) and (
1.12
). To complete the proof, we are left with showing (
7.7
). This is a delicate part, as we need to understand whether (
1.15
) or (
1.16
) are valid, and consequently whether Theorem A or B applies. For
1\leq k\leq n
, we denote

V_{k}(t)=\det Q_{k}(t)=\det\int_{0}^{t}e^{sB}Q(k)e^{sB^{\star}}ds.

When
k=n
, the corresponding equation in (
7.3
) is non-degenerate. Since from (
7.4
) we know that
D_{n}(n)=n
, and since
\operatorname{tr}B=0
, Proposition
6.3
guarantees that (
1.15
) is valid for the volume function
V_{n}(t)
. We infer that Theorem A applies, and (
7.7
) is valid with the same admissible pairs of the equation without drift.
When
k<n
, the quadratic form in (
7.3
) becomes increasingly singular as
k\searrow 1
. The case
k=1
is the most degenerate, corresponding to

(7.10)

\partial_{t}u=i\partial_{x_{1}x_{1}}u+x_{1}\partial_{x_{2}}u+...+x_{n-1}\partial_{x_{n}}u+F(x,t),\ \ \ \ \ u(x,0)=\varphi(x).

Interestingly, this case is the easiest to decide since, within the family of problems (
7.3
), it is the only one in which, when
F=0
, the corresponding differential operator admits a global homogeneous structure given by the non-isotropic dilations

(7.11)

\delta_{\lambda}(x,t)=\left(\lambda x_{1},\lambda^{3}x_{2},\ldots,\lambda^{2n-1}x_{n},\lambda^{2}t\right).

This follows from the fact that, when
k=1
, all the
\star
matrices in the canonical representation (
7.9
) are zero matrices, whereas
B_{1}=(1)\in M_{1\times 1}(\mathbb{R})
, and therefore we are in the situation of hypothesis (iii) in Theorem
1.3
. Therefore, Theorem A applies, resulting in (
7.7
), with

(7.12)

D=D_{n}(1)=\sum_{k=1}^{n}(2k-1)=n^{2}.

We observe that

(7.13)

D_{n}(1)=n^{2}\geq...\geq D_{n}(n)=n.

We remark that
D
in (
7.12
) is the spatial
homogeneous dimension
of (
7.11
), in the sense of
[
50
]
,
[
14
]
,
[
47
]
.
It remains to decide the cases
2\leq k\leq n
. We already know from (
8.7
) that

V_{1}(t)=V_{1}(1)\ t^{n^{2}},\ \ \ \ \ \ t>0.

Claim:
For
2\leq k\leq n
we have as
t\to\infty

(7.14)

\displaystyle V_{k}(t)=V_{1}(1)\ t^{n^{2}}(1+o(1)).

The claim can be extracted from the proof of
[
37
, Ex.2, p.287]
, but for the sake of completeness we provide some details. The matrix
B
is the transposed of the Jordan canonical form of a non diagonalizable matrix with zero eigenvalues. It is immediate to check, see (11.2.18) in
[
6
]
, that

\displaystyle e^{sB}=\begin{pmatrix}1&0&\cdot&\cdot&\cdot&\cdot&0\\
s&1&\cdot&\cdot&\cdot&\cdot&0\\
\frac{s^{2}}{2!}&s&1&0&\cdot&\cdot&0\\
\frac{s^{3}}{3!}&\frac{s^{2}}{2!}&s&1&0&\cdot&0\\
\cdot&\cdot&\cdot&\cdot&\cdot&\cdot&\cdot\\
\frac{s^{n-1}}{(n-1)!}&\cdot&\cdot&\cdot&\cdot&\cdot&1\end{pmatrix}.

Let
e_{1},...,e_{n}
be the unit (column) vectors of the standard basis of
\mathbb{R}^{n}
. Observe that we can express the Gramian matrix associated with
Q
and
B
as in (
7.8
), in the following way

\displaystyle Q(t)

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{t}e^{sB}Qe^{sB^{\star}}ds=\int_{0}^{t}e^{sB}e_{1}e_{1}^{\star}e^{sB^{\star}}ds+...+\int_{0}^{t}e^{sB}e_{k}e_{k}^{\star}e^{sB^{\star}}ds=

\displaystyle=Q_{1}(t)+...+Q_{k}(t).

A direct computation shows that for
1\leq r\leq k

\displaystyle Q_{r}(t)=\begin{pmatrix}0_{(r-1)\times(r-1)}&0_{(r-1)\times(n-r+1)}\\
0_{(n-r+1)\times(r-1)}&A_{r}(t)\end{pmatrix},

where
A_{r}(t)
is a
(n-r+1)\times(n-r+1)
matrix whose entires are

Lemma 8.1
.

The following are equivalent:

(i)

Q
and
B
satisfy
(H)
;

(ii)

Q(B^{\star})^{k}x=0\ \forall k\geq 0\ \Longleftrightarrow\ x=0
.

Proof.

We have observed in the introduction that
(H)

\Longleftrightarrow\ Q(t)>0
for all
t>0
. It will thus suffice to prove that

Q(t)>0,\forall t>0\ \Longleftrightarrow\ \text{(ii)}.

Let

. We have

\displaystyle\langle Q(t)x,x\rangle=\int_{0}^{t}\langle e^{sB}Qe^{sB^{\star}}x,x\rangle ds=\int_{0}^{t}|Q^{1/2}e^{sB^{\star}}x|^{2}ds.

Therefore,
\langle Q(t)x,x\rangle=0
if and only if

Qe^{sB^{\star}}x=\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\frac{s^{k}Q(B^{\star})^{k}x}{k!}=0\ \ \ \forall\ 0\leq s\leq t.

This is in turn equivalent to

Q(B^{\star})^{k}x=0,\ \ \ \forall\ k\geq 0.

This shows
Q(t)>0
is equivalent to (ii).
