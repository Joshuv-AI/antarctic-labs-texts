Congenital Heart Defect Screening Among Children with Down Syndrome in Rwanda: A Cross-Sectional Study
Authors: Vedaste Bagweneza, Philomene Uwimana, Leon Mutesa, Glorieuse Uwizeye, Donatilla Mukamana
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundCongenital heart defects affect over half of children with Down syndrome. Screening is recommended within the first six weeks of life to enable timely management and improve survival. Despite this, congenital heart defect screening remains low and often delayed.ObjectiveThis study aimed to assess the uptake of congenital heart defect screening and its associated factors among children with Down syndrome in Rwanda.MethodsThis quantitative cross-sectional study was conducted from February to May 2025. A total of 313 participants were recruited using convenience sampling. The study was conducted in four hospitals and the Rwanda Down Syndrome Organization. Data were collected using a newly developed structured questionnaire that was pilot tested prior to the main data collection. Ethical approval and informed consent were obtained. Descriptive statistics summarized congenital heart defect screening uptake, while multivariable logistic regression was used to identify factors associated with screening uptake.ResultsAmong 313 children with Down syndrome, 159 (51%) had undergone congenital heart defect screening. Among those screened, 45 (28%) were screened within the recommended first 6 weeks after birth. In the multivariable logistic regression analysis, place of residence was significantly associated with congenital heart defect screening uptake, with higher odds of screening among children in urban areas compared with those in rural areas (AOR = 2.17; 95% CI: 1.29–3.68; p = 0.004).ConclusionCongenital heart defect screening among children with Down syndrome remains suboptimal, with delayed screening commonly observed. Urban residence is associated with higher screening uptake in the adjusted model. Targeted interventions are needed to improve timely screening, particularly addressing barriers in rural settings.

Introduction

Down syndrome (DS), the most common chromosomal cause of intellectual disability,1,2 is associated with multiple complications, with congenital heart defects (CHDs) being among the most frequent. Evidence suggests that CHDs occur in approximately 50% or more of individuals with DS.2,3 This high prevalence underscores the need for careful attention to their care, as undiagnosed or untreated congenital cardiac defects may lead to complications such as heart failure and pulmonary hypertension, and are associated with increased risk of morbidity and mortality.3–7 Specifically, evidence indicates that cardiovascular disease is a leading cause of morbidity and mortality in individuals with DS.8

Studies consistently report a high prevalence of CHDs among individuals with DS. In the United States, a population-based study reported that 66% of infants with DS had a CHD, with septal defects being the most common type.9 Similarly, a population-based registry study from France also reported that CHDs were the most frequent anomalies associated with DS, identified in 44% of cases of DS, with atrioventricular septal defect being the most frequently reported lesion.10 A large EUROCAT population-based registry study across 28 European registries also reported a prevalence of approximately 44%.11

Higher prevalence of CHDs in individuals with DS has also been documented in the WHO African region, where studies conducted in Nigeria, Ethiopia and Sudan reported rates of 80%, 67.3% and 43%, respectively.12–14 A systematic review involving studies from multiple regions worldwide, including North America, South America, Europe, Asia, Africa, and Oceania, reported that the prevalence of CHDs generally ranges between 40% and 55%.15

However, the reported prevalence of CHDs may vary widely across studies due to differences in diagnostic criteria and the inclusion of minor or transient lesions such as patent ductus arteriosus (PDA) in preterm infants, potentially leading to an overestimation of overall prevalence.

In Rwanda, studies assessing the prevalence of CHDs among children with DS remain scarce. One of the few available studies, conducted in 2014 among 89 participants, reported that 59.55% had CHDs.16

While the high prevalence of CHDs in children with DS is well established, less attention has been given to the timeliness and uptake of screening services, particularly in low-resource settings.

A study in Bangladesh reported that most children with DS (64%) underwent CHD screening only when visiting a child neurodevelopmental center between 6 months and 2 years of age.17

Similarly, a study in Thailand found that children with DS were often not assessed for all key components of a basic evaluation, potentially resulting in missed opportunities for CHD screening.18

These findings underscore significant gaps in the timely screening of children with Down syndrome for congenital heart defects (CHDs), highlighting a clear contrast with current clinical recommendations. Current guidelines recommend that all children with DS undergo echocardiographic screening for CHDs, ideally within the first six weeks of life, to facilitate early detection and prompt management of any cardiac abnormalities.19,20 Evidence has also shown that early CHD screening improves neonatal outcomes.21

Supporting this, a study conducted in the United States emphasized the need to screen for CHDs within the first month of life and advocated maintaining routine echocardiographic screening at all ages, including adolescence.22 These recommendations reinforce the importance of early screening, suggesting that implementing CHD evaluation at the earliest possible age is critical for optimal outcomes.

These delays in CHD screening have important clinical and public health implications. Undiagnosed CHDs contribute to recurrent respiratory infections, growth failure, pulmonary hypertension, and increased infant mortality among children with DS.23,24

Delays or absence of CHD screening among children with Down syndrome vary widely across countries. Screening is particularly scarce in developing countries, whereas it is more common in developed countries.25,26 This variation reflects differences in access to health services and screening practices across settings.

Evidence has shown that sociodemographic and socioeconomic factors contribute to disparities in the uptake of health screening and care services. A study in the United States indicated that educational level, economic status, and geographical residence influence access to CHD care and related diagnostic services, as well as associated health outcomes.27 Similarly, systematic review evidence indicated that factors such as employment status, marital status, place of residence, income, and level of education are associated with the utilization of healthcare services.28

Based on this evidence, the present study focused on key sociodemographic variables, specifically education level, occupation, marital status, and place of residence, as potential determinants of CHD screening uptake among children with DS.

There is a notable scarcity of studies exploring the uptake of CHD screening among individuals with DS globally. Most existing research has focused on the prevalence and types of CHDs in this population3,6,13,14,16,29. Specifically, in Rwanda, there is a lack of published evidence on this topic, as no studies addressing CHD screening uptake in individuals with DS have been identified. Understanding the uptake of CHD screening is especially important, as many children with DS may only undergo screening due to clinical presentation or complications related to undiagnosed CHDs, rather than through proactive parental initiation.17,30

This situation potentially leaves a significant portion of children in the community undiagnosed and untreated, as caregivers may not seek screening early enough to enable timely interventions. Addressing this knowledge gap is critical for informing strategies that promote early detection, appropriate referral, and timely management of CHDs in children with DS, ultimately reducing preventable morbidity and mortality in this vulnerable population. Therefore, this study aimed to assess the uptake of CHD screening among children with DS in Rwanda, to generate evidence that can guide early detection and intervention strategies.

Materials and Methods

Study Design and Setting

This study employed a quantitative cross-sectional design to assess the uptake of CHD screening among children with DS. It was conducted in four selected hospitals in Rwanda: The University Teaching Hospital of Kigali (CHUK), Rwanda Military Teaching Hospital (RMTH), Kirehe District Hospital, and Rwinkwavu District Hospital. The first two hospitals are located in Kigali City, while the district hospitals are in the Eastern Province. Data were also collected at the Rwanda Down Syndrome Organization (RDSO), an organization that brings together individuals with DS.

The University Teaching Hospital of Kigali and Rwanda Military Teaching Hospital were selected because they serve as referral hospitals with genetic and cardiology services. The two district hospitals were chosen due to support from the non-governmental organization Partners In Health, which provides care for children with disabilities, including those with DS. Finally, RDSO was included as a study site because parents of children with DS often visit this site for regular interactions and services, allowing participants to be met there during their visits.

Study Population, Inclusion Criteria and Study Sample

The study population consisted of parents or guardians of children with DS as they are the key individuals who could reliably provide information about the children. A few guardians participated in the study; they were enrolled only after confirming that they were involved in the daily care of the children and could provide reliable information, particularly if they lived with the child.

Convenience sampling was used to recruit 313 parents or guardians of children with DS, comprising 278 parents and 35 guardians. Parents or guardians were eligible for inclusion if they had a child with DS. In this study, “children” refers to individuals aged less than 18 years.31

The total population was determined using aggregated data from five hospitals that specifically maintain records of individuals with DS, complemented by information provided by the Rwanda Down Syndrome Organization. These five hospitals include four that served as study settings and one additional hospital, the Centre Hospitalier Universitaire de Butare (CHUB). Although CHUB no longer offered genetic services during the study period, it continued to maintain records of individuals with DS. Its records were therefore used exclusively for population estimation and not for data collection in the study.

Data Collection Methods and Instruments

A newly developed structured questionnaire (Supplementary Figure 1) was used to collect data, as no previously validated instrument was available to assess CHD screening uptake among children with DS.

The questionnaire development was informed by existing literature on DS and CHD screening and was further guided by methodological approaches used in studies assessing uptake of other health screening services.33–36

Its development also involved reviewing study objectives and consulting experts in the field of DS, including geneticists, pediatric nurses with experience in developmental and genetic conditions, and nursing academics with expertise in caregiver-related and behavioral health research. The instrument was then pilot tested among a small group of participants who were not included in the main data collection and was refined based on the feedback obtained.

The questionnaire was initially developed in Kinyarwanda to minimize language barriers and ensure clarity for participants. An English translation of the original questionnaire was prepared for reference. The final instrument consisted of two sections: Section A, comprising 15 items, collected participants’ characteristics and those of children with DS, while Section B, comprising 16 items, assessed CHD screening uptake.

Participants were recruited during routine visits to selected hospitals and the RDSO. Data were collected by one member of the research team and four trained data collectors to facilitate convenient recruitment across the study sites. Questionnaires were administered face-to-face by data collectors. Due to varying levels of literacy among participants, data collectors helped clarify questions and completed the questionnaires based on participants’ responses to ensure accurate data collection.

The Kinyarwanda version of the questionnaire was used during data collection to ensure accurate capture of key information, as some participants were only able to understand Kinyarwanda. Data collection lasted four months, from February to May 2025, with each questionnaire taking approximately 30 minutes to complete. All eligible participants invited to the study consented to participate, resulting in a 100% response rate, and all questionnaires were fully completed and included in the analysis.

Psychometric Properties of the Questionnaire

The questionnaire was assessed for validity and reliability prior to the main data collection. Face validity was established through expert review to ensure that all items were clear and appropriately reflected the study objectives. Content and construct validity were evaluated through consultation with the expert panel, ensuring that the questionnaire comprehensively captured the intended constructs and that all items were relevant and adequate for the study context.

To assess reliability and the feasibility of the instrument, a pilot study was conducted prior to the main study. Following methodological guidance suggesting that pilot studies use approximately 10% of the planned full study sample,37 31 participants were included. Internal consistency was evaluated using Cronbach’s alpha, which yielded a coefficient of 0.84, exceeding the acceptable threshold of 0.70.38

Ethical Considerations

Ethical approval for the study was obtained from the University of Rwanda Institutional Review Board (Approval Notice: No 336/CMHS IRB/2024), and written permission was secured from all study sites. Participants were fully informed about the study, assured of voluntary participation, and informed that they could withdraw at any time without consequences. Written informed consent was obtained from all participants prior to data collection. Anonymity was maintained by assigning identification numbers on all data collection tools, and all data were securely stored to ensure confidentiality. Participation posed minimal risk, as the study involved only structured questionnaires and did not interfere with routine care. The study complies with the Declaration of Helsinki.

Data Analysis

Data were analyzed using SPSS Version 25.39 Descriptive statistics (frequencies and percentages) summarized participants’ characteristics and CHD screening uptake. The outcome variable was CHD screening uptake, defined as whether a child with Down syndrome had undergone congenital heart defect screening (Yes/No).

Multivariable binary logistic regression analysis was used to examine factors associated with CHD screening uptake, with all independent variables entered simultaneously to identify independent predictors. Adjusted odds ratios (AOR) with 95% confidence intervals (CI) were reported to measure the strength of associations. Statistical significance was set at p < 0.05.

Multicollinearity among independent variables was assessed using tolerance and variance inflation factor (VIF), with cut-offs of >0.2 for tolerance and <5 for VIF.

Regarding education level, only primary education was significantly associated with lower odds of CHD screening uptake compared with no formal education (AOR = 0.27, 95% CI: 0.08–0.98, p = 0.046). Secondary education (AOR = 0.36, 95% CI: 0.12–1.06, p = 0.064), Technical and Vocational Education and Training (TVET) (AOR = 0.40, 95% CI: 0.15–1.05, p = 0.061), and university education (AOR = 1.19, 95% CI: 0.29–4.97, p = 0.807) were not statistically significant.

For marital status, none of the categories showed a statistically significant association with CHD screening uptake compared with single participants (Married: AOR = 1.57, 95% CI: 0.50–4.93, p = 0.443; Separated/Divorced: AOR = 1.65, 95% CI: 0.90–3.02, p = 0.107; Widowed: AOR = 1.00, 95% CI: 0.32–3.16, p = 0.995; Cohabitant: AOR = 0.35, 95% CI: 0.07–1.75, p = 0.201).

Concerning occupation, public servants had significantly lower odds of CHD screening uptake compared with farmers (AOR = 0.06, 95% CI: 0.01–0.72, p = 0.027), and self-employed participants also had significantly lower odds of uptake (AOR = 0.37, 95% CI: 0.16–0.85, p = 0.019). In contrast, being a student (AOR = 0.62, 95% CI: 0.28–1.34, p = 0.223), casual workers (AOR = 1.00, 95% CI: 0.39–2.56, p = 0.993), and participants with no employment (AOR = 0.67, 95% CI: 0.27–1.68, p = 0.393) were not significantly associated with screening uptake.

Place of residence was significantly associated with CHD screening uptake, with urban residence associated with higher odds of screening uptake compared with rural residence (AOR = 2.17; 95% CI: 1.29–3.68; p = 0.004).

No evidence of multicollinearity was detected among independent variables.

Discussion

This study aimed to assess the uptake of congenital heart defect screening and its associated factors among children with Down syndrome in Rwanda. Approximately half of the children had undergone CHD screening, and among those screened, only 28% were screened within the recommended timeframe. The findings further showed that place of residence was the only factor independently associated with CHD screening uptake. Overall, these results suggest suboptimal uptake and delayed screening of CHD among children with DS, with important disparities related to geographical residence.

In this study, the majority of participants were biological parents, predominantly mothers, indicating that mothers are the primary caregivers of children with DS in Rwanda. This finding aligns with previous studies in Rwanda and other African contexts, which have reported that mothers often assume the main caregiving role, especially for children with disabilities.40,41

This gendered pattern of caregiving may have important implications for the uptake of congenital heart defect (CHD) screening, as female caregivers may face financial and decision-making constraints that can influence timely access to healthcare services. In many contexts, healthcare-seeking decisions are influenced by household dynamics, where financial control and approval from male partners may affect the utilization of preventive child health services.42,43

Most caregivers in this study had attained secondary-level education, while only a small proportion had reached university level, and the majority resided in rural areas. In addition, most participants were married and engaged in farming or informal employment. These characteristics reflect the sociodemographic composition of the study population and may be relevant in understanding access to healthcare services, including CHD screening. Previous studies have similarly reported that factors such as low educational attainment, rural residence, and socioeconomic conditions are associated with variations in the uptake of screening and other preventive health services.44,45

Regarding the overall level of CHD screening uptake among children with DS and the timing of first CHD screening, the study findings indicate suboptimal and inconsistent practices. On the uptake, almost half of the participants (51%) said their children had been screened for CHDs, while nearly the same number reported that their children had not been screened.

For the timing of first CHD screening among those screened, only 28% underwent screening within the first six weeks after birth, yet the guidelines advise that all children with DS should be screened for CHD within the first days of life, preferably within the first six weeks.19,22 This gap in screening uptake and time of screening is concerning, because delays or lack of screening may lead to late diagnosis and poor outcomes.

These findings align with other studies which reported low uptake and delayed CHD screening due to various factors.46,47 Specifically, a study in Nigeria found that the mean age of first CHD screening in children with DS was about 12 months,47 which is far beyond the recommended first six weeks after birth. This shows the urgent need to educate communities so that children with DS are screened for CHDs as early as possible after birth.

This study also examined factors associated with CHD screening among children with Down syndrome. In the multivariable logistic regression analysis, education, marital status, occupation, and place of residence were assessed for their independent association with screening uptake.

Education showed a mixed association with CHD screening uptake. Only primary education was associated with lower odds of screening uptake compared with no formal education, while secondary, TVET, and university education were not statistically significant. Overall, no consistent or monotonic trend was observed across increasing levels of education. This suggests that education alone may not be a strong driver of CHD screening uptake in this context.

Education has been widely recognized in the literature as an important determinant of health service utilization, largely through its influence on health literacy and access to information. However, evidence also suggests that its effect may vary depending on broader socioeconomic and contextual factors. In the present study, the absence of a clear gradient may reflect the influence of other determinants, such as place of residence, referral systems, or limited awareness of CHD-specific screening, although these factors were not directly measured in this study. The lack of a consistent association in this study highlights the need to consider contextual and health system factors when interpreting the role of education in CHD screening uptake.

These findings are in partial agreement with previous literature suggesting that education may influence the utilization of preventive health services, although evidence specific to CHD screening remains limited.36,48,49

Marital status was examined as a potential determinant of CHD screening uptake among children with Down syndrome; however, in the multivariable logistic regression analysis, it was not an independent predictor of screening uptake. None of the marital status categories showed a statistically significant association with CHD screening uptake (all p > 0.05) compared with single participants.

Marital status has been conceptualized in the literature as an important social determinant of health service utilization, largely through its influence on social, emotional, and financial support within households. Married individuals may benefit from shared decision-making and caregiving responsibilities, which can facilitate access to health services. Evidence from previous studies has also reported higher utilization of preventive health services among married individuals compared with unmarried counterparts.48,50,51

However, the present findings suggest that marital status alone may not independently influence CHD screening uptake after adjustment for other sociodemographic factors such as occupation and place of residence, indicating that its effect may be confounded or mediated by these factors.

Occupation was assessed as a determinant of CHD screening uptake among children with Down syndrome. In the multivariable logistic regression analysis, public servants and self-employed participants showed statistically significant lower odds of CHD screening uptake compared with farmers, while other occupational categories were not significantly associated with screening uptake.

These findings suggest that occupational differences may reflect underlying socioeconomic factors that influence access to CHD screening services. Despite expectations that formal employment may facilitate access to care, public servants may face potential barriers such as work-related time constraints or competing responsibilities that could reduce opportunities for preventive health service utilization. Self-employment may also reflect heterogeneous economic conditions, which may influence access to preventive health services, although these factors were not directly assessed in this study.

Evidence from previous studies supports the role of financial protection and employment-related benefits in facilitating access to preventive health services. For example, a study in Taiwan reported that individuals benefiting from government-supported health check programs were more likely to undergo screening compared with those relying on self-paid services.52 Conversely, evidence from Korea has shown lower screening uptake among self-employed individuals, suggesting that the relationship between occupation and screening behavior may vary across contexts due to differences in healthcare systems and employment structures.53

Overall, the present findings indicate that occupation-related differences in CHD screening uptake may be partially explained by broader socioeconomic and contextual factors rather than occupation alone, highlighting the importance of addressing structural barriers to equitable access to screening services among families of children with DS.54

Place of residence was significantly associated with CHD screening uptake among children with Down syndrome. In the multivariable logistic regression analysis, urban residence was associated with higher odds of CHD screening uptake compared with rural residence (AOR = 2.17, 95% CI: 1.29–3.68, p = 0.004).

This finding indicates a rural-urban difference in CHD screening uptake in the study setting. One possible explanation is that urban areas may have better availability of health facilities, higher health literacy, and improved access to information and diagnostic services, which may facilitate higher uptake of CHD screening compared with rural areas.

This finding is consistent with previous studies, including in African regions, which indicate that screening uptake and utilization of healthcare services are generally higher among urban populations, while rural residents are less likely to access screening services due to structural and informational barriers. It has been reported that residents in rural areas are less educated and socioeconomically disadvantaged.49,55,56 In Rwanda, rural residence has also been identified as a barrier to accessing screening services, particularly due to limited awareness, service availability, and distance.57,58

These findings highlight the need for targeted outreach and education interventions in rural communities, which may have populations with low health literacy and are socially and economically vulnerable.

Study Strengths and Limitations

The main strength of this study lies in its novelty. To date, no published study has examined CHD screening uptake among children with Down syndrome in Rwanda. By assessing the uptake of CHD screening in children with DS, this study not only addresses a research gap in Rwanda but also contributes to a globally underexplored area.

This highlights the core contribution of our work to the body of nursing knowledge and provides a foundation for future research as well as targeted interventions addressing the unique cardiac needs of children with Down syndrome. Furthermore, the use of both descriptive and inferential analyses provides comprehensive insights into trends and associations, making the findings practical and applicable in clinical and community settings.

Despite its strengths, this study has some limitations. First, the cross-sectional design limits the ability to establish causal relationships between participant characteristics and CHD screening uptake.59

Second, the study relied on self-reported information from parents and guardians of children with Down syndrome regarding CHD screening status, which may be subject to recall bias and social desirability bias and may not fully reflect documented clinical records. In addition, the findings may have limited generalizability because data were collected from selected health facilities only, which may not represent all children with Down syndrome in Rwanda.

Finally, the limited availability of directly comparable literature on CHD screening uptake among children with Down syndrome highlights the need for further research in this area. Despite these limitations, the study provides valuable insights that can inform future research and guide targeted interventions.

Conclusions

Congenital heart defect screening among children with Down syndrome in Rwanda is low and often delayed. Children in rural areas are less likely to be screened compared to those in urban areas, showing unequal access to screening services. This suggests gaps in awareness, referral, and use of available services. Targeted actions are needed to improve timely CHD screening and ensure better access for all children with Down syndrome.
