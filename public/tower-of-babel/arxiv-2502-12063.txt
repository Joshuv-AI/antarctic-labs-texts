Low-Rank Thinning
Annabelle Michael Carrell, Albert Gong, Abhishek Shetty, Raaz Dwivedi, Lester Mackey

Abstract
The goal in thinning is to summarize a dataset using a small set of representative points. Remarkably, sub-Gaussian thinning algorithms like Kernel Halving and Compress can match the quality of uniform subsampling while substantially reducing the number of summary points. However, existing guarantees cover only a restricted range of distributions and kernel-based quality measures and suffer from pessimistic dimension dependence. To address these deficiencies, we introduce a new low-rank analysis of sub-Gaussian thinning that applies to any distribution and any kernel, guaranteeing high-quality compression whenever the kernel or data matrix is approximately low-rank. To demonstrate the broad applicability of the techniques, we design practical sub-Gaussian thinning approaches that improve upon the best known guarantees for approximating attention in transformers, accelerating stochastic gradient training through reordering, and distinguishing distributions in near-linear time.

Introduction

This work is about thinning, finding a small set of representative points to accurately summarize a larger dataset. Here, we use the term “dataset” liberally to refer to any collection of points, be they experimental observations (as in
Sec.

6
), stochastic gradients (as in
Sec.

5
), or key-value pairs (as in
Sec.

4
).
State-of-the-art thinning
techniques provably improve upon uniform subsampling but only for restricted classes of kernel-based quality measures
and with pessimistic dependence on the data dimension
(
Harvey & Samadi, 2014
;
Phillips & Tai, 2020
;
Alweiss et al., 2021
;
Dwivedi & Mackey, 2024
;
Dwivedi & Mackey, 2022
;
Shetty et al., 2022
;
Li et al., 2024
, see, e.g.,)
.
We introduce a new analysis for sub-Gaussian thinning algorithms that applies to any kernel and shows that one can efficiently identify a better-than-uniform set of representative points whenever the
kernel or data matrix
is nearly low-rank.
This opens the door to a variety of impactful applications including approximate dot-product attention in transformers, accelerating model training through gradient reordering, and distinguishing distributions with deep kernels in near-linear time.

In
Sec.

2
, we introduce our formal definition of thinning, two kernel-based measures of thinning quality, and a suite of candidate thinning algorithms.

Sec.

3
presents our main theorem relating thinning quality to low-rank properties of a dataset and its induced kernel matrix. In
Sec.

4
, we translate the problem of approximating attention into a thinning problem and develop a practical solution, Thinformer, with state-of-the-art quality guarantees. In
Sec.

5
, we develop a thinned stochastic gradient reordering rule that provably accelerates training and bridges the theory-practice gaps left open by prior work.
Finally, in
Sec.

6
we derive new and improved power guarantees for practical thinned hypothesis tests that distinguish distributions in near-linear time.
Each section also includes its own discussion of related work.

We begin with a formal definition of our problem setting. Consider a fixed collection of
n_{\textup{in}}
input points
\mathcal{X}_{\textup{in}}
belonging to a potentially larger universe of datapoints
\mathcal{X}\triangleq\{\boldsymbol{x}_{1},\dots,\boldsymbol{x}_{n}\}
.
The aim of a thinning algorithm is to select
n_{\textup{out}}
points from
\mathcal{X}_{\textup{in}}
that together accurately summarize
\mathcal{X}_{\textup{in}}
.
This is formalized by the following definition.

Definition 1

(
Thinning algorithms
)
.

A
thinning algorithm

Alg
takes as input
\mathcal{X}_{\textup{in}}
and returns a possibly random subset
\mathcal{X}_{\textup{out}}
of size
n_{\textup{out}}
.
We denote the input and output empirical distributions by
\mathbb{P}_{\textup{in}}\triangleq\frac{1}{n_{\textup{in}}}\sum_{\boldsymbol{x}\in\mathcal{X}_{\textup{in}}}\boldsymbol{\delta}_{\boldsymbol{x}}
and
\mathbb{P}_{\textup{out}}\triangleq\frac{1}{n_{\textup{out}}}\sum_{\boldsymbol{x}\in\mathcal{X}_{\textup{out}}}\boldsymbol{\delta}_{\boldsymbol{x}}
and
define the induced probability vectors
\boldsymbol{p}_{\textup{in}},\boldsymbol{p}_{\textup{out}}\in\boldsymbol{\Delta}_{n-1}
over the indices
[n]
by

\textstyle\boldsymbol{p}_{\textup{in},i}=\frac{\mathbf{1}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[{\boldsymbol{x}_{i}\in\mathcal{X}_{\textup{in}}}}}\right]}{n_{\textup{in}}}\text{\ \ and\ \ }\boldsymbol{p}_{\textup{out},i}=\frac{\mathbf{1}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[{\boldsymbol{x}_{i}\in\mathcal{X}_{\textup{out}}}}}\right]}{n_{\textup{out}}}\text{\ \ for all\ \ }i\in[n].

(2)

When
\mathcal{X}\subset\real^{d}
, we use
\mathbf{X}\triangleq[\boldsymbol{x}_{1},\ldots,\boldsymbol{x}_{n}]^{\top}\in\real^{n\times d}
to denote the input point matrix so that

\textstyle\mathbb{E}_{\boldsymbol{x}\sim\mathbb{P}_{\textup{in}}}[\boldsymbol{x}]=\mathbf{X}^{\top}\boldsymbol{p}_{\textup{in}}\quad\text{and}\quad\mathbb{E}_{\boldsymbol{x}\sim\mathbb{P}_{\textup{out}}}[\boldsymbol{x}]=\mathbf{X}^{\top}\boldsymbol{p}_{\textup{out}}.

(3)

We will make use of two common measures of summarization quality.

Definition 2

(
Kernel MMD and max seminorm
)
.

Given two distributions
\mu,\tilde{\mu}
and a reproducing kernel
\mathbf{k}

(
Steinwart & Christmann, 2008
, Def. 4.18)
, the associated kernel
maximum mean discrepancy (MMD)
is the worst-case difference in means for functions in the unit ball
\mathbb{B}_{\mathbf{k}}
of the associated reproducing kernel Hilbert space:

\textstyle\mmd_{\mathbf{k}}(\mu,\tilde{\mu})

\textstyle\triangleq\sup_{f\in\mathbb{B}_{\mathbf{k}}}|\mathbb{E}_{\boldsymbol{x}\sim\mu}f(\boldsymbol{x})-\mathbb{E}_{\boldsymbol{x}\sim\tilde{\mu}}f(\boldsymbol{x})|.

(4)

When
\mu=\mathbb{P}_{\textup{in}}
and
\tilde{\mu}=\mathbb{P}_{\textup{out}}
as in
Def.

1
and
\mathbf{K}\triangleq(\mathbf{k}(\boldsymbol{x}_{i},\boldsymbol{x}_{j}))_{i,j=1}^{n}\in\real^{n\times n}
denotes the induced kernel matrix, then the MMD can be expressed as a Mahalanobis distance between
\boldsymbol{p}_{\textup{in}}
and
\boldsymbol{p}_{\textup{out}}
:

\textstyle\mmd_{\mathbf{k}}(\mathbb{P}_{\textup{in}},\mathbb{P}_{\textup{out}})

\textstyle=\sqrt{(\boldsymbol{p}_{\textup{in}}-\boldsymbol{p}_{\textup{out}})^{\top}\mathbf{K}(\boldsymbol{p}_{\textup{in}}-\boldsymbol{p}_{\textup{out}})}

(5)

\textstyle\triangleq\mmd_{\mathbf{K}}(\boldsymbol{p}_{\textup{in}},\boldsymbol{p}_{\textup{out}}).

(6)

For any indices
\mathcal{I}\subseteq[n]
, we further define the
kernel max seminorm (KMS)

\textstyle\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\|{\mathbf{K}(\boldsymbol{p}_{\textup{in}}-\boldsymbol{p}_{\textup{out}})}}}\right\|_{\mathcal{I}}\triangleq\max_{i\in\mathcal{I}}|\boldsymbol{e}_{i}^{\top}\mathbf{K}(\boldsymbol{p}_{\textup{in}}-\boldsymbol{p}_{\textup{out}})|.

(7)

Notably, when the input points lie in
d
and
\mathbf{k}(\boldsymbol{x}_{i},\boldsymbol{x}_{j})
is the linear kernel
\langle{\boldsymbol{x}_{i}},{\boldsymbol{x}_{j}}\rangle
(so that
\mathbf{K}=\mathbf{X}\mathbf{X}^{\top}
), MMD measures the Euclidean discrepancy in datapoint means between the input and output distributions:

\textstyle\mmd_{\mathbf{K}}(\boldsymbol{p}_{\textup{in}},\boldsymbol{p}_{\textup{out}})=\|{\mathbf{X}^{\top}\boldsymbol{p}_{\textup{in}}-\mathbf{X}^{\top}\boldsymbol{p}_{\textup{out}}}\|_{2}.

(8)

A common strategy for bounding the error of a thinning algorithm is to establish its sub-Gaussianity.

Definition 3

(
Sub-Gaussian thinning algorithm
)
.

We write
\textsc{Alg}\in\mathcal{G}_{\nu,\delta}(\mathbf{K})
and say
Alg
is
(\mathbf{K},\nu,\delta)
-sub-Gaussian
, if
Alg
is a thinning algorithm,
\mathbf{K}
is a symmetric positive semidefinite (SPSD) matrix,
\nu>0
,
\delta\in[0,1)
, and there exists an event
\mathcal{E}
with probability at least
1-\delta/2
such that, the input and output probability vectors satisfy

\textstyle\mathbb{E}_{\mathcal{E}}[\exp\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\langle\boldsymbol{u},\mathbf{K}(\boldsymbol{p}_{\textup{in}}-\boldsymbol{p}_{\textup{out}})}}\right\rangle}}\right)]\leq\exp\big(\frac{\nu^{2}}{2}\boldsymbol{u}^{\top}\mathbf{K}\boldsymbol{u}\big),\forall\boldsymbol{u}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}.

(9)

Here, the sub-Gaussian parameter
\nu
controls the summarization quality of the thinning algorithm, and
we see from
Tab.

1

that a variety of practical thinning algorithms are
(\mathbf{K},\nu,\delta)
-sub-Gaussian for varying levels of
\nu
.

2.1
Examples of sub-Gaussian thinning algorithms

Perhaps the simplest sub-Gaussian thinning algorithm is
uniform subsampling
: by
Prop.

B.1
, selecting
n_{\textup{out}}
points from
\mathcal{X}_{\textup{in}}
uniformly at random (without replacement) is
(\mathbf{K},\nu,0)
-sub-Gaussian with
\nu={\sqrt{\|{\mathbf{K}}\|_{\max}}/\sqrt{n_{\textup{out}}}}
.
Unfortunately, uniform subsampling suffers from relatively poor summarization quality.
As we prove in
Sec.

B.1.1
, its root-mean-squared MMD and KMS are both
\Omega(1/\sqrt{n_{\textup{out}}})
meaning that
n_{\textup{out}}=10000
points are needed to achieve
1\%
relative error.

Proposition 1

(
Quality of uniform subsampling
)
.

For any
\mathcal{I}\subseteq[n]
,
a uniformly subsampled thinning satisfies

\textstyle\mathbb{E}[\mmd^{2}_{\mathbf{K}}(\boldsymbol{p}_{\textup{in}},\boldsymbol{p}_{\textup{out}})]

\textstyle=\frac{1}{n_{\textup{out}}}{\textstyle\frac{n_{\textup{in}}-n_{\textup{out}}}{n_{\textup{in}}-1}}\,C_{\mathbf{K}}\quad\text{and}\quad

(10)

\textstyle\mathbb{E}[\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\|{\mathbf{K}(\boldsymbol{p}_{\textup{in}}-\boldsymbol{p}_{\textup{out}})}}}\right\|_{\mathcal{I}}^{2}]

\textstyle\geq\frac{1}{n_{\textup{out}}}{\textstyle\frac{n_{\textup{in}}-n_{\textup{out}}}{n_{\textup{in}}-1}}\,\max_{i\in\mathcal{I}}C_{\mathbf{K}\boldsymbol{e}_{i}\boldsymbol{e}_{i}^{\top}\mathbf{K}}

(11)

for any SPSD
\mathbf{K}
with
C_{\mathbf{K}}\triangleq\sum_{i=1}^{n}\boldsymbol{p}_{\textup{in},i}\mathbf{K}_{ii}-\boldsymbol{p}_{\textup{in}}^{\top}\mathbf{K}\boldsymbol{p}_{\textup{in}}
.

Fortunately, uniform subsampling is not the only sub-Gaussian thinning algorithm available.
For example, the Kernel Halving (
\hyperref@@ii[algo:khd]{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}{\textsc{KH}}}(\delta)
) algorithm of
Dwivedi & Mackey (2024)
provides a substantially smaller sub-Gaussian parameter,
\nu=O({\sqrt{\log(n_{\textup{out}}/\delta)}}{/n_{\textup{out}}})
, in
n_{\textup{in}}^{2}
time by selecting one out of every two points and biasing selection toward the point that yields smaller approximation error.
Similarly,
the
\hyperref@@ii[algo:khcompressd]{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}{\textsc{KH-Compress}}}(\delta)
algorithm of
Shetty et al. (2022, Ex. 3)
delivers
\nu=O({\sqrt{\log(n_{\textup{out}})\log(n_{\textup{out}}/\delta)}}{/n_{\textup{out}}})
in only
n_{\textup{out}}^{2}\log n_{\textup{out}}
time by halving and concatenating coresets of geometrically increasing size until the target output size is met.
We derive simplified versions of these algorithms with identical sub-Gaussian constants in
Secs.

B.2
and
B.5
and a linear-kernel variant (
\hyperref@@ii[algo:khlin]{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}{\textsc{LKH}}}(\delta)
) with
n_{\textup{in}}d
runtime in
Sec.

B.3
.
To round out our set of examples, we show in
Sec.

B.6.1
that two new thinning algorithms based on the Gram-Schmidt walk of
Bansal et al. (2018)
yield even smaller
\nu
at the cost of increased runtime.
We call these algorithms Gram-Schmidt Thinning
(
GS-Thin
) and
GS-Compress
.

3
Low-rank Sub-Gaussian Thinning

One might hope that the improved sub-Gaussian constants of
Tab.

1
would also translate into improved quality metrics.
Our main result, proved in
App.

C
, shows that this is indeed the case whenever the inputs are approximately low-rank.

Theorem 1

(
Low-rank sub-Gaussian thinning
)
.

Fix any
\delta^{\prime}\in(0,1)
,
r\leq n
, and
\mathcal{I}\subseteq[n]
.
If
\textsc{Alg}\in\mathcal{G}_{\nu,\delta}(\mathbf{K})
, then the following bounds hold individually with probability at least
1-\delta/2-\delta^{\prime}
:

\textstyle\mmd^{2}_{\mathbf{K}}(\boldsymbol{p}_{\textup{in}},\boldsymbol{p}_{\textup{out}})

\textstyle\leq\nu^{2}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[e^{2}r+e\log(\frac{1}{\delta^{\prime}})}}\right]

(12)

\textstyle+\lambda_{r+1}(\frac{1}{n_{\textup{out}}}-\frac{1}{n_{\textup{in}}})\quad\text{and}\quad

(13)

\textstyle\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\|{\mathbf{K}(\boldsymbol{p}_{\textup{in}}-\boldsymbol{p}_{\textup{out}})}}}\right\|_{\mathcal{I}}

\textstyle\leq\nu D_{\mathcal{I}}\sqrt{2\log(\frac{2|\mathcal{I}|}{\delta^{\prime}})}.

(14)

Here,
\lambda_{j}
denotes the
j
-th largest eigenvalue of
\mathbf{K}
,
\lambda_{n+1}\triangleq 0
, and

D_{\mathcal{I}}\triangleq\max_{i\in\mathcal{I}}\sqrt{\mathbf{K}_{ii}}
.

Suppose that, in addition,

\mathcal{X}\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}
and

|\mathbf{K}_{il}-\mathbf{K}_{jl}|\leq L_{\mathbf{K}}\|{\boldsymbol{x}_{i}-\boldsymbol{x}_{j}}\|_{2}
for some
L_{\mathbf{K}}>0
and all
i,j\in\mathcal{I}
and
l\in\mathrm{supp}({\boldsymbol{p}_{\textup{in}}})
.
Then, with probability at least
1-\delta/2-\delta^{\prime}
,

\textstyle\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\|{\mathbf{K}(\boldsymbol{p}_{\textup{in}}-\boldsymbol{p}_{\textup{out}})}}}\right\|_{\mathcal{I}}\leq\nu D_{\mathcal{I}}\sqrt{2\log(4/\delta^{\prime})}(1+\frac{32}{\sqrt{3}})

(15)

\textstyle\ \ \qquad+\nu D_{\mathcal{I}}\,32\sqrt{\frac{2}{3}\,\mathrm{rank}({\mathbf{X}_{\mathcal{I}}})\log(\frac{3e^{2}R_{\mathcal{I}}L_{\mathbf{K}}}{D_{\mathcal{I}}^{2}\wedge(R_{\mathcal{I}}L_{\mathbf{K}})})}

(16)

for
R_{\mathcal{I}}\triangleq\max_{i\in\mathcal{I}}\|{\boldsymbol{x}_{i}}\|_{2}

and
\mathbf{X}_{\mathcal{I}}\triangleq[\boldsymbol{x}_{i}]_{i\in\mathcal{I}}^{\top}
.

Let us unpack the three components of this result.
First,
Thm.

1
provides
a high-probability

O(\nu\sqrt{\log(|\mathcal{I}|)})
bound
14
on the KMS for any kernel and any sub-Gaussian thinning algorithm on any space.
In particular, the non-uniform algorithms of
Tab.

1
all enjoy
O(\log(n_{\textup{out}})\sqrt{\log(|\mathcal{I}|)}/n_{\textup{out}})
KMS, a significant improvement over the
\Omega(1/\sqrt{n_{\textup{out}}})
KMS of uniform subsampling.
The bound
14
follows from the sub-Gaussianity of the thinning algorithm (
Def.

3
) and the union bound over
\pm\boldsymbol{e}_{i}
for each
i\in\mathcal{I}
.

For datapoints in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
,
Thm.

1

also provides a refined
O(\nu\sqrt{\mathrm{rank}({\mathbf{X}_{\mathcal{I}}})\log(R_{\mathcal{I}}L_{\mathbf{K}})})
bound
16
on KMS.
For bounded data, this trades an explicit dependence on the number of query points
|\mathcal{I}|
for a rank factor that is never larger (and sometimes significantly smaller) than
d
.
This refinement follows from a more elaborate chaining argument that frames
(\boldsymbol{e}_{i}^{\top}\mathbf{K}(\boldsymbol{p}_{\textup{in}}-\boldsymbol{p}_{\textup{out}}))_{i\in\mathcal{I}}
as a sub-Gaussian process (
Lem.

C.5
) and uses the Lipschitzness of
K
to control its entropy integral.
We will make use of these results when approximating dot-product attention in
Sec.

4
.

Notably, Thm. 3.1 of
Phillips & Tai (2020)
implies that any thinning algorithm must incur at least
\Omega(\sqrt{d}/n_{\textup{out}})
KMS error for some dataset in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
and many common kernels. Meanwhile, our
Tab.

1
and
Thm.

1
imply that
GS-Thin
has
\nu=O(1/n_{\textup{out}})
and hence KMS
O(\sqrt{d}/n_{\textup{out}})
.
Taken together, these results imply that no algorithm can have sub-Gaussian constant
\nu=o(1/n_{\textup{out}})
and that
GS-Thin
enjoys minimax rate-optimal KMS and a minimax rate-optimal sub-Gaussian constant.

Finally,
Thm.

1
provides an
O(\nu\sqrt{r}+\sqrt{\lambda_{r+1}/n_{\textup{out}}})
high-probability bound on kernel MMD, where the approximate rank parameter
r
can be freely optimized.
We establish this result by projecting
\mathbf{K}^{1/2}(\boldsymbol{p}_{\textup{in}}-\boldsymbol{p}_{\textup{out}})
onto the first
r
eigenvectors of
\mathbf{K}
, bounding the residual error in terms of
\lambda_{r+1}
, and bounding the projection magnitude with high probability using the sub-Gaussianity of
Alg
and a union bound over a finite cover of a Euclidean ball in
\mathbb{R}^{r}
.

When
\mathbf{K}=(\mathbf{k}(\boldsymbol{x}_{i},\boldsymbol{x}_{j}))_{i,j=1}^{n}
is generated by a finite-rank kernel
\mathbf{k}
, like a linear kernel
\langle{\boldsymbol{x}_{i}},{\boldsymbol{x}_{j}}\rangle
, a polynomial kernel
(1+\langle{\boldsymbol{x}_{i}},{\boldsymbol{x}_{j}}\rangle)^{p}
, or a random Fourier feature kernel
(
Rahimi & Recht, 2007
)
, this guarantee becomes
O(\nu)
and improves upon uniform subsampling whenever
\nu=o(1/\sqrt{n_{\textup{out}}})
.
In this case, the non-uniform algorithms of
Tab.

1
all enjoy
O(\log(n_{\textup{out}})/n_{\textup{out}})
MMD, a significant improvement over the
\Omega(1/\sqrt{n_{\textup{out}}})
MMD of uniform subsampling.
We will revisit this finite-rank setting when studying stochastic gradient acceleration strategies in
Sec.

5
.

More generally,
Thm.

1
guarantees improved MMD even for full-rank
\mathbf{K}
, provided that the eigenvalues of
\mathbf{K}
decay sufficiently rapidly.
For example, optimizing over the approximate rank parameter
r
yields an
O(\nu\log^{p/2}(n_{\textup{out}}))
bound under exponential eigenvalue decay
\lambda_{r+1}=O(ne^{-cr^{1/p}})
and an
O(\nu^{\frac{p}{p+1}}(\frac{n}{n_{\textup{out}}})^{\frac{1}{2(p+1)}})
bound under polynomial eigenvalue decay
\lambda_{r+1}=O(n/r^{p})
.
Fortunately, some of the most commonly-used kernels generate kernel matrices with rapid eigenvalue decay.

For example, the popular Gaussian kernel on
\mathbb{R}^{d}
,

\textstyle\textsc{Gauss}(\eta):\ \mathbf{k}(x,y)=\exp(-\eta\|{x-y}\|_{2}^{2})\text{ for }\eta>0,

(17)

generates
\mathbf{K}=(\mathbf{k}(\boldsymbol{x}_{i},\boldsymbol{x}_{j}))_{i,j=1}^{n}
satisfying

\textstyle\lambda_{r+1}\leq ne^{-\frac{d}{2e}r^{1/d}\log\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\frac{dr^{1/d}}{4e^{2}\eta R^{2}}}}\right)}\text{\ \ for\ \ }(2e)^{d}\leq r<n

(18)

whenever

\mathcal{X}\subset\mathbb{B}^{d}(R)

(
Altschuler et al., 2019
, Thm. 3)
.
Combined with
Thm.

1
, this fact immediately yields an MMD guarantee for each algorithm in
Tab.

1
. We present a representative guarantee for
\hyperref@@ii[algo:khd]{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}{\textsc{KH}}}(\delta)
.

Corollary 1

(
Gaussian MMD of
KH
)
.

If
\mathcal{X}_{\textup{in}}\subset\mathbb{B}^{d}(R)
for
R>0
, then
\hyperref@@ii[algo:khd]{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}{\textsc{KH}}}(\delta)
with
\mathbf{k}=\textsc{Gauss}(\eta)
, and
n=n_{\textup{in}}
delivers

\textstyle\mmd_{\mathbf{K}}^{2}(\boldsymbol{p}_{\textup{in}},\boldsymbol{p}_{\textup{out}})\leq

(19)

\textstyle\quad O\bigl(\frac{\log({n_{\textup{out}}}{/\delta})}{n_{\textup{out}}^{2}}\big(\big(\frac{\log(n_{\textup{out}})\vee(R^{2}\eta)}{d}\big)^{d}+\log(\frac{1}{\delta^{\prime}})\big)\bigr)

(20)

with probability at least
1-\delta/2-\delta^{\prime}
.

The proof in
App.

D
provides a fully explicit
and easily computed bound on the Gaussian MMD.
Under the same assumptions, the distinct analysis of

Dwivedi & Mackey (2022, Thm. 2, Prop. 3)
provides a squared MMD bound of size

\Theta\big(\frac{\log(n_{\textup{out}}/\delta)}{n_{\textup{out}}^{2}}(\frac{\log^{d+1}(n_{\textup{out}})R^{d}\eta^{d/2}}{(\log\log(n_{\textup{out}}))^{d}}+\log(\frac{1}{\delta^{\prime}}))\big)
.
Notably,
Cor.

1
improves upon this best known
\hyperref@@ii[algo:khd]{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}{\textsc{KH}}}(\delta)
guarantee whenever the datapoint radius
R=O(\log n_{\textup{out}})
, a property that holds almost surely for any bounded, sub-Gaussian, or subexponential data sequence
(see
Dwivedi & Mackey, 2024
, Prop. 2)
.

Altschuler et al. (2019, Thm. 4)
additionally showed that
Gaussian kernel matrix eigenvalues satisfy

\textstyle\lambda_{r+1}\leq ne^{-cr^{{2}{/(5d^{\star})}}}\quad\text{for}\quad 1\leq r<n

(21)

for a constant
c
independent of
\mathcal{X}
when
\mathcal{X}
belongs to a smooth compact manifold of dimension
d^{\star}<d
.
In this case, our low-rank analysis yields adaptive MMD guarantees that scale with the potentially much smaller intrinsic dimension
d^{\star}
.
We use
Thm.

1
to prove the first such intrinsic-dimension guarantee for
\hyperref@@ii[algo:khd]{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}{\textsc{KH}}}(\delta)
in
App.

E
.

Corollary 2

(
Intrinsic Gaussian MMD of
KH
)
.

If
\mathcal{X}_{\textup{in}}
lies on a smooth manifold
\Omega\subset\mathbb{B}^{d}
of dimension
d^{\star}<d
(
Assump.

E.1
), then
\hyperref@@ii[algo:khd]{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}{\textsc{KH}}}(\delta)
with
\mathbf{k}=\textsc{Gauss}(\eta)
,
and
n=n_{\textup{in}}
delivers

\textstyle\mmd_{\mathbf{K}}^{2}(\boldsymbol{p}_{\textup{in}},\boldsymbol{p}_{\textup{out}})\leq O\big(\frac{\log(\frac{n_{\textup{out}}}{\delta})}{n_{\textup{out}}^{2}}\big((\frac{\log(n_{\textup{out}})}{c})^{\frac{5d^{\star}}{2}}\!+\log(\frac{1}{\delta^{\prime}})\big)\big)

(22)

with probability at least
1-\frac{\delta}{2}-\delta^{\prime}
for
c
independent of
\mathcal{X}_{\textup{in}}

Specifically,
Lu et al. (2022)

Their approach, distilled in

6
Cheap Two-Sample Testing

Our final application is two-sample testing, determining
whether two datasets are drawn from the same underlying distribution. We observe independent samples
\mathcal{X}\triangleq(\boldsymbol{x}_{i})_{i=1}^{m}
and
\mathcal{Y}\triangleq(\boldsymbol{y}_{j})_{j=1}^{n}
from the unknown distributions
\mathbb{P}
and
\mathbb{Q}
respectively, and we seek to accept or reject the null hypothesis that
\mathbb{P}=\mathbb{Q}
.
Standard kernel MMD tests
tackle this task
by computing the empirical MMD

\textstyle\mmd_{\mathbf{k}}(\mathbb{P}_{\textup{in}},\mathbb{Q}_{\textup{in}})\text{ for }\mathbb{P}_{\textup{in}},\mathbb{Q}_{\textup{in}}\triangleq\frac{1}{m}\sum_{\boldsymbol{x}\in\mathcal{X}}\!\boldsymbol{\delta}_{\boldsymbol{x}},\frac{1}{n}\sum_{\boldsymbol{y}\in\mathcal{Y}}\!\boldsymbol{\delta}_{\boldsymbol{y}}

(34)

for an appropriate kernel
\mathbf{k}

and rejecting the null hypothesis whenever
\mmd_{\mathbf{k}}(\mathbb{P}_{\textup{in}},\mathbb{Q}_{\textup{in}})
is sufficiently large
(
Gretton et al., 2012
)
.
Such tests are prized both for their broad applicability and for their high discriminating
power
, that is, their probability of rejecting the null when
\mathbb{P}\neq\mathbb{Q}
.
A standard way to summarize the power properties of a test is through its
detectable separation rate
.

Definition 5

(
Detectable separation rate
)
.

We say a two-sample test has
detectable separation rate

\epsilon_{\mathbf{k},m,n}
if, for any detection probability
1-\beta\in(0,1)
, there exists a constant
c_{\mathbf{k},\beta}>0
such that the test has power at least
1-\beta
of rejecting the null whenever
\mmd_{\mathbf{k}}(\mathbb{P},\mathbb{Q})\geq c_{\mathbf{k},\beta}\cdot\epsilon_{\mathbf{k},m,n}
.

Standard MMD tests can detect distributional differences on the order of

\epsilon_{\mathbf{k},m,n}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{\min(m,n)}}

(
Gretton et al., 2012
, Cor. 9)
,
and this detectable separation rate is known to be the best possible for MMD tests
(
Domingo-Enrich et al., 2023
, Prop. 2)

and minimax optimal for translation invariant kernels
(
Kim & Schrab, 2023
, Thm. 8)
.
However, standard MMD tests also suffer from the
\Theta((m+n)^{2})
time burden of computing the empirical MMD.
Recently,
Domingo-Enrich et al. (2023)
showed that one can improve scalability while preserving power by compressing
\mathbb{P}_{\textup{in}}
and
\mathbb{Q}_{\textup{in}}
using a high-quality thinning algorithm. However, their analysis applies only to a restricted class of distributions and kernels and exhibits a pessimistic dimension dependence on
\mathbb{R}^{d}
. Here, we offer a new analysis of their Compress Then Test approach that applies to any bounded kernel on any domain and, as an application, develop the first non-asymptotic power guarantees for testing with learned deep neural network kernels.

Impact Statement

This work introduced a new analysis of thinning algorithms that adapts to low-rank structures.
We exploited this adaptivity to design fast algorithms with strong quality guarantees for three key applications in machine learning: dot-product attention in Transformers, stochastic gradient training in optimization, and deep kernel testing for distinguishing distributions.
More broadly, our techniques provide a general framework for reducing computational resource use in machine learning. Such tools have the potential to reduce energy costs and environmental harms from model training, inference, and evaluation and to improve accessibility in resource-constrained settings, all while provably maintaining high quality.

Definition A.1

(

)
.

Suppose that, for each

Proposition B.1

(

.

(\mathbf{K},\nu,0)

(62)

Fix any vector
\boldsymbol{u}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
, and
let

be the random indices in
[n]
selected by uniform subsampling.

\boldsymbol{u}^{\top}\mathbf{K}(\boldsymbol{p}_{\textup{in}}-\boldsymbol{p}_{\textup{out}})=\frac{1}{n_{\textup{out}}}\sum_{i=1}^{n_{\textup{out}}}\boldsymbol{u}^{\top}\mathbf{K}(\boldsymbol{p}_{\textup{in}}-\boldsymbol{e}_{J_{i}})

\textstyle|\boldsymbol{u}^{\top}\mathbf{K}\boldsymbol{e}_{J_{i}}|\leq\sqrt{\boldsymbol{u}^{\top}\mathbf{K}\boldsymbol{u}}\sqrt{\boldsymbol{e}_{J_{i}}^{\top}\mathbf{K}\boldsymbol{e}_{J_{i}}}\leq\sqrt{\|{\mathbf{K}}\|_{\max}}\sqrt{\boldsymbol{u}^{\top}\mathbf{K}\boldsymbol{u}}\quad\text{with probability $1$}\quad

Lemma C.3

(

Faster SGD training experiments

The experiment of
Sec.

5.2
was carried out using Python 3.10, PyTorch 2.0.1, a Rocky Linux 8.9 server with 64 CPU cores (Intel(R) Xeon(R) Platinum 8358 CPU @ 2.60GHz), and a NVIDIA A100 GPU (40 GB memory, CUDA 12.4, driver version 550.54.15).

Technically, the CD-GraB: SBW algorithm requires an a priori upper bound on the maximum Euclidean norm
b_{\max}
of any stochastic gradient that it will encounter.
To conduct our experiment, we first estimate
b_{\max}
by calculating the maximum gradient Euclidean encountered across
10
epochs of running SGD with
\hyperref@@ii[algo:khlin]{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}{\textsc{LKH}}}(\frac{1}{2K})
reordering.
One would typically not choose to carry out such a two-step procedure in practice, but the experiment serves to demonstrate that the CD-GraB: SBW leads to overly conservative performance even if reasonable upper bound is known in advance.

The settings and implementation for both random reshuffling (RR) and CD-GraB: Greedy were those used in the original logistic regression on mortgage application experiment of
Cooper et al. (2023)
.
Our experiment code builds on the open-source CD-GraB repository
https://github.com/GarlGuo/CD-GraB
.
As in
Cooper et al. (2023)
, optimization was carried out with a learning rate of
\alpha=0.01
, datapoints were loaded in batches of size
16
, and stochastic gradients were reordered for each datapoint individually.

L.3
Cheap two-sample testing experiment

The experiment of
Sec.

6.2
was carried out using Python 3.12.9, PyTorch 2.6.0+cu124, and an Ubuntu 20.04.6 LTS server with an AMD EPYC 9554 64-Core Processor, 100 GB RAM, and a single NVIDIA H100 GPU (96 GB memory, CUDA 12.2, driver version 535.154.05).
Each test is run with replication count
\mathcal{B}=100
, nominal level
\alpha=0.05
, and failure probability
\delta=0.5
.
The neural network
\phi
was trained exactly as in
Liu et al. (2020)
(with learning rate
5\times 10^{-5}
and batch size equal to the full training sample size), and
runtime measurements exclude the time required to train
\phi
.
Our experiment code builds on the open-source deep kernel testing
(
https://github.com/fengliu90/DK-for-TST
) and Compress Then Test
(
https://github.com/microsoft/goodpoints
) repositories.
