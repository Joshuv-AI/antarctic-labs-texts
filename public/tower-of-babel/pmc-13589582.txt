Injectable Hyaluronic Acid-Functionalized Nanocomposite Hydrogel for Targeted Rheumatoid Arthritis Therapy via Concurrent ROS Attenuation and Macrophage Repolarization
Authors: Ziyan Fan, Runkong Wang, Qiang Zhang, Mingquan Wu, Liyang Zhu, Ruixue Zhong, Jiajie Chen, Dong He, Lei He, Sanjun Shi, Xu Zhou
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundThe clinical treatment of rheumatoid arthritis (RA), a chronic systematic autoimmune disease marked by persistent synovial inflammation and irreversible joint damage, remains substantially challenging due to the limitations of current therapies including insufficient targeting, short intra-articular retention and off-target adverse effects. Therefore, developing an injectable, long-acting and targeted therapeutic hydrogel platform is urgently needed for precise and efficient RA treatment.MethodsWe developed an injectable long-acting nanocomposite hydrogel platform (HA-BR nano-GEL@CLT) which integrated hyaluronic acid (HA)-polyethylene glycol (PEG) co-polymeric matrix encapsulating HA-bilirubin nanoparticles (HA-BR NPs) and celastrol (CLT). The hydrogel was characterized and its in vitro biocompatibility, antioxidant, anti-inflammatory, and macrophage polarization effects were evaluated. In vivo therapeutic efficacy and biosafety were assessed in adjuvant-induced arthritis (AIA) rats.ResultsThe average particle size of fabricated HA-BR NPs@CLT was 118.5 nm and the gel formulation underwent a rapid sol‑to‑gel phase transition within 200 seconds. HA-BR nano-GEL@CLT exhibited strong biomimetic lubrication, anti-inflammatory and antioxidant capacities, and could effectively modulate the polarization of macrophages in vitro, with the result that the M1/M2 ratio in HA-BR nano-GEL@CLT group was only 0.19-fold that of model group. The hydrogel also provided long-term intra-articular retention up to 21 days in AIA rats. It significantly alleviated paw swelling, inhibited bone erosion, modulated the levels of inflammatory cytokines, whereas the expressions of IL-6 and TNF-α were reduced by 27% and 43%, and the expression of IL-10 was increased by 47%, compared to HA GEL@CLT group. Besides, HA-BR nano-GEL@CLT also alleviated oxidative damage, regulated macrophage polarization and reduced systemic toxicity of CLT in vivo.ConclusionThis injectable nanocomposite hydrogel platform offers an efficient and promising approach to suppress RA progression via ROS attenuation and macrophage repolarization.

Introduction

Rheumatoid arthritis (RA) is a chronic autoimmune disease characterized by sustained joint inflammation, abnormal synovial proliferation and permanent destruction of articular cartilage and bone.1–5 The complications such as cardiovascular disease and insulin resistance, also exert significant adverse effects, making it a significant challenge for patients’ mortality.6 The widely used therapeutic agents for RA mainly include nonsteroidal anti-inflammatory drugs (NSAIDs), glucocorticoids (GCs) and disease-modifying antirheumatic drugs (DMARDs), which could induce severe adverse drug reactions, including gastrointestinal symptoms, immunosuppression and osteoporosis.7–11 Although novel biologic agents have demonstrated considerable potential in retarding RA progression and enhancing clinical outcomes, innovative therapeutic approaches for more effective and targeted treatments are still highly required.

Celastrol (CLT) is a unique bioactive compound isolated from the root of Tripterygium wilfordii Hook F, a traditional Chinese medicinal herb with a long-standing application in the clinical management of rheumatic diseases.12 Since CLT exhibits biological functions such as immunomodulatory, anti-inflammatory and anti-oxidant activities, researches have deeply investigated the underlying molecular mechanisms.13–15 Besides suppressing the polarization of macrophages toward a proinflammatory phenotype and decreasing the release of proinflammatory cytokines via modulating the NF‑κB and Notch1 pathways, CLT is also capable of regulating the biological functions of various immune cells, thereby relieving joint lesions and decelerating disease progression.16,17 However, its inherently poor aqueous solubility and systemic toxicity limit its clinical applicability and therapeutic efficacy. Besides, it is critical to overcome the limitations imposed by its narrow therapeutic window and poor bioavailability, making the construction of safe and efficient delivery carriers to enhance the therapeutic efficacy of celastrol against RA a critical and urgent issue.

Despite the complicated pathogenesis of RA, the imbalance between excessive M1 macrophage polarization and diminished M2 macrophage polarization is directly related to the exacerbation of rheumatoid arthritis.18,19 Within the inflammatory microenvironment, proinflammatory M1-type macrophages predominate over anti-inflammatory M2-type macrophages and release large amounts of pro-inflammatory cytokines such as IL‑1β, IL-6 and TNF‑α, which result in inflammatory damage.20,21 Notably, Hyaluronic acid (HA), a natural polysaccharide with nontoxicity and excellent biodegradability, is capable of specifically binding to CD44 receptors overexpressed on the membranes of M1-type macrophages, thereby offering a promising targeting strategy to deliver CLT toward lesion sites.22,23 Under the pathological environment of RA, reactive oxygen species (ROS) levels are persistently elevated in the joint synovium and inflammatory microenvironment, thereby inducing oxidative stress.24 Oxidative stress not only causes damage to biomolecules, but also aggravates local hypoxia and further promotes the activation of proinflammatory signaling pathways.25 Besides, the accumulation of ROS triggers chondrocyte apoptosis, and promotes endothelial dysfunction, thereby further aggravating inflammation and tissue damage.26 Therefore, scavenging intracellular ROS and effectively modulating the joint microenvironment to alleviate oxidative stress are crucial strategies for the treatment of RA.27 Interestingly, Bilirubin (BR), a metabolite of hemoglobin, exhibits potent ROS-scavenging and antioxidant properties, and existing studies have demonstrated that BR can suppress the polarization of macrophages toward the M1 phenotype and downregulate the expression of proinflammatory cytokines.28 Despite its therapeutic potential, the poor solubility and instability of BR restrict its further clinical application.29,30 The material conjugating endogenous hydrophobic BR with hydrophilic HA (HA-BR) possesses both CD44-targeting ability and ROS-scavenging capacity, thus the amphiphilic material represents a promising candidate for the construction of CLT-loaded nanosystem.

Although the nanosystem exhibits considerable potential, it still suffers from critical drawbacks, including rapid elimination and unsatisfactory specific local distribution capability.31,32 To address these challenges, injectable hydrogels are capable of providing a stable microenvironment that preserves the integrity of the nanosystem, and possess excellent cartilage interface compatibility.33,34 As a local delivery platform, injectable hydrogels possess the capacity to prolong intralesional drug retention and minimize systemic exposure. Compared with systemic administration and conventional local injection solutions, injectable hydrogels can form stable drug depots at lesion sites, achieve sustained and controllable drug release, and effectively reduce the repeated administration frequency and systemic side effects. They are capable of directional navigation toward different tissue sites such as tumor tissues, joint cavities and wound areas, depending on the route of administration.35,36 Specifically, injectable hydrogels effectively lubricate the joint cavity and degrade slowly for sustained local drug release, making them ideal intra-articular drug delivery systems.37–39 Coincidentally, HA also serves as a vital component of the extracellular cartilage matrix and inherently acts as an intra-articular injectable therapeutic agent for RA by alleviating the inflammatory response either through the direct effects of HA or by enhancing the viscosity of synovial fluid.27,40,41 Therefore, combining the nanosystem with injectable HA hydrogels may offer a more effective therapeutic strategy for RA.

Although a variety of HA-based, ROS-scavenging, celastrol-loaded and macrophage-targeted delivery systems have been constructed for RA treatment, HA hydrogels lack intrinsic antioxidant capacity, and conventional nano-targeted delivery systems cannot sustain long-term remodeling of macrophage homeostasis.13,42,43 Consequently, these traditional delivery systems are incapable of forming the synergistic cascade reaction of CD44 targeting, ROS scavenging and macrophage repolarization. Herein, we develop a locally injectable, CD44-targeting and ROS-scavenging drug delivery nanomaterial-containing hydrogel (HA-BR nano-GEL@CLT) to overcome the above bottlenecks that existing monofunctional biomaterials fail to achieve simultaneously. First, CLT-loaded HA-BR nanoparticles (HA-BR NPs/CLT) were fabricated, in which CLT served as an anti-inflammatory and antioxidant agent to suppress inflammatory responses, whereas HA-BR nanoparticles targeted M1 macrophages via CD44 receptors and exerted ROS-scavenging effects. Subsequently thiolated hyaluronic acid (HA-SH) was crosslinked with polyethyleneglycol diacrylate (PEG-DA) via a mild Michael addition reaction to form a locally injectable hydrogel, which was employed to load the nanoparticles for the fabrication of HA-BR nano-GEL@CLT. We hypothesize that after intra-articular injection of HA-BR nano-GEL@CLT, the hydrogel undergoes gradual degradation for joint cavity lubrication and sustained release of HA-BR NPs/CLT. The HA-BR derived nanoparticles are internalized into macrophages via CD44-mediated endocytosis, and exert the ROS-scavenging capacity, macrophage phenotype-modulating ability and anti-inflammatory efficacy of CLT. This platform exhibits promising therapeutic potential for the comprehensive management of RA.

Materials and Methods

Materials

Bilirubin (BR, ≥98%) was obtained from Yuanye Bio-Technology Co., Ltd. (Shanghai, China); celastrol (CLT, ≥98%), was purchased from Biopurify Phytochemicals Co., Ltd. (Chengdu, China); sodium hyaluronic acid (HA, 74 kDa, ≥96%) was provided by Bloomage Biotechnology. Co., Ltd (Jinan, China); the lipid peroxidation (MDA) assay kit, and the total SOD activity assay kit were purchased from Biyuntian Biotechnology Co., Ltd. (Shanghai, China). The APC-conjugated anti-mouse CD86 antibody and the PE-conjugated anti-mouse CD163 antibody were obtained from BioLegend (USA). Complete Freund’s adjuvant was provided by Chondrex (Washington, USA).

The mouse macrophage cell line RAW264.7 was purchased from Wuhan Pronose Biotechnology Co., Ltd. (Wuhan, China) and cultured in DMEM medium supplemented with 1% penicillin-streptomycin and 10% fetal bovine serum.

All animal experiments were carried out following the Guide for the Care and Use of Laboratory Animals by the National Committee for Laboratory Animal Research, with ethical approval granted by the Animal Experiment Ethics Committee of Anhui Medical University (LLSC20241707). All male Sprague-Dawley (SD) rats (180–220 g) were purchased from the Animal Research Center of Anhui Medical University. The rats were housed at room temperature (25 ± 1 °C) under a light-dark cycle, with unrestricted access to food and water.

Synthesis of the Hydrogel Precursor

First, the intermediate, ethylenediamine-bilirubin conjugate (ED−BR) was synthesized as mentioned before.44 Then 2 mmol of HA was dissolved in DMSO, followed by the addition of 3 mmol of EDC·HCl and NHS. The resulting mixture was stirred at room temperature (RT) for 1 h, after which 0.5 mmol of ED−BR was added. The reaction system was then stirred at RT for 24 h under nitrogen gas. Subsequently, the mixture was dropped into 50 mL of 0.01 M NaOH solution slowly. Dialysis (MWCO 3500) was sequentially performed against 0.01 M NaOH for 5 h, a 1:1 water/acetonitrile mixture for 1 day, and distilled water for 2 days. HA-BR was then collected via lyophilization and the structure was analyzed using 1H NMR spectroscopy (AVANCE NEO 400M, Bruker). The degree of substitution of HA-BR was calculated by measurement of UV spectra (UV-1900, Shimadzu, Japan).

Thiolated hyaluronic acid (HA‑SH) was synthesized following the route illustrated in Figure 1A. Typically, 1 mmol of HA, 2 mmol of NHS, and 2 mmol of EDC·HCl were dissolved in 25 mL of ultrapure water, and the solution was stirred for 2 h. Meanwhile, 3 mmol of CSA·HCl was dissolved in 5 mL of hydrochloric acid solution (pH 3.0) and then added dropwise into the mixture. The reaction system was continuously stirred for 24 h under nitrogen gas. The mixture was dialyzed against hydrochloric acid solution (pH 3.5) containing 0.1 M NaCl for 2 days. Preparation and Characterization of HA-BR NPs@CLT

To prepare HA-BR NPs@CLT, 3 mg of HA-BR was dissolved in 5 mL of pure water and stirred on a magnetic stirrer for 5 minutes. Separately, 5 mg of CLT was dissolved in 400 μL of ethanol and added slowly dropwise into the HA-BR solution while stirring continuously. After the addition, the mixture was stirred for another 10 minutes. Finally, the mixture was sonicated for 10 minutes in an ice bath (5 seconds on, 5 seconds off; 550 W) to obtain HA-BR NPs@CLT. 0.2 mg of DID, instead of CLT, was added to prepare HA-BR NPs@DID.

The particle size, dispersion, and zeta potential of HA-BR NPs@CLT were measured using dynamic light scattering (Zeta Sizer Nano-ZS, UK). The morphology was characterized through transmission electron microscope (Talos L120C G2, Thermo Fisher, USA).

The stability of HA-BR NPs@CLT was also studied by dynamic light scattering. HA-BR NPs@CLT solution was diluted in water, PBS and 10% FBS respectively, and incubated at 37 °C for 7 days. The particle size and PDI of HA-BR NPs@CLT were measured daily.

Ultrafiltration method was employed to determine the encapsulation efficiency (EE) and drug loading (DL) of HA-BR NPs@CLT.44 Specifically, 200 μL of HA-BR NPs@CLT was loaded into an ultrafiltration tube (10 kDa, microporous, Billerica, MA) and centrifugated at 6000 rpm for an hour. The concentration of CLT in the filtrate was measured using a UV spectrophotometer. Full methodological validation was performed, including specificity, linearity, precision, sample recovery accuracy, and test solution stability. The EE and DL of nanoparticles were calculated according to the following equations:

Preparation and Characterization of HA-BR Nano-GEL@CLT

The detailed steps for preparing HA-BR nano-GEL@CLT were as follows: 10% (w/v) of HA-SH and 5% (w/v) of PEG5000-DA were dissolved in HEPES buffer (0.2 M, pH 7.4) respectively. Next, equal volumes of the two solutions were vortex-mixed and shaken at 37 °C to form the blank hydrogel, HA GEL. To prepare HA GEL@CLT and HA-BR nano-GEL@CLT, CLT and HA-BR NPs@CLT were dispersed in HEPES (0.8 mg/mL of CLT) buffer separately. HA-SH and PEG-DA were dissolved in HEPES buffer that contained CLT and HA-BR NPs@CLT, and then mixed in equal volumes of 500 μL, and shaken at 37 °C to gelate 1 mL of HA GEL@CLT and HA-BR nano-GEL@CLT.

The morphology of HA-BR nano-GEL@CLT was observed using scanning electron microscopy (SEM, ZEISS Gemini SEM 300, Germany). In brief, HA-BR nano-GEL@CLT samples were rapidly frozen in liquid nitrogen for approximately 5 minutes, followed by lyophilization for 24 hours. The freeze-dried samples were subsequently cut to obtain fractured cross-sections and subjected to gold sputter-coating, and the internal structure of the hydrogel was observed under a scanning electron microscopy.

To investigate the rheological properties of HA-BR nano-GEL@CLT, relevant experimental parameters were carried out on a rotational rheometer (MCR 302, Austria) equipped with a 25 mm diameter. Specifically, sol‒gel transition point was monitored over a 1200 s scanning period at 37 °C with a fixed frequency of 1 Hz; frequency sweeps for storage modulus (G′) and loss modulus (G″) were conducted at 37 °C across a frequency range of 0.1–10 Hz. Strain amplitude sweeps (strain: 0.1%–1000%, angular frequency: 10 rad/s, temperature: 25 °C) were further carried out to record changes in G′ and G′′. The coefficient of friction of HA-BR nano-GEL@CLT was determined via a friction testing system (Bruker UMT-2, Germany). Sliding friction experiments were implemented by reciprocating a 6 mm-diameter alumina ball on top of the hydrogel matrix. The reciprocating motion was applied at a frequency of 1 Hz for 300 s under a load of 1 N.

Additionally, in vitro swelling and degradation properties were explored as previously described.47 HA-BR nano-GEL@CLT samples were freeze-dried and weighted (Wf). The samples were then placed in a 1×PBS solution for swelling at 37 °C. At specified time points, excess liquid was removed from the surface of hydrogels, and the weight of hydrogels was recorded (Wg). Following each gravimetric measurement, the samples were reintroduced into PBS solution to proceed with the swelling process. The swelling ratio (SR) was calculated using the following formula: SR = (Wg – Wf)/Wf × 100%. As for degradation measurement, at predetermined time intervals, hydrogel specimens in PBS were harvested and lyophilized, with their residual weights denoted as Wt. The degradation was expressed as the percentage of weight loss (Wt%) using the following formula: Wt% = (Wf – Wt)/Wf × 100%.

To explore the in vitro drug release behaviors of HA-BR nano-GEL@CLT under acidic and ROS-rich conditions, PBS solutions with different pH values (5.8, 6.5 and 7.4) and different concentrations of H2O2 solutions were used as the release medium. 1 mL of the hydrogel was placed into a centrifuge tube. Subsequently, 15 mL of solution was added, and the tube was incubated in a constant-temperature shaking incubator. At scheduled time intervals, 1 mL of release medium was collected, followed by immediate replacement with an equal volume of fresh preheated release medium. The collected supernatant was stored at 4 °C prior to the detection. The absorbance of the collected samples was measured at 425 nm via UV-Vis spectrophotometry. The concentration of CLT was calculated according to the standard calibration curve, and the cumulative drug release percentage was further calculated accordingly.

In vitro Hemolysis Test

An in vitro hemolysis test was conducted using red blood cells isolated from whole blood of SD rats, with 0.09% and 0.9% sodium chloride solutions serving as positive and negative controls, respectively.44,48 CLT solution and the precursor solutions of hydrogels were mixed with the red blood cell suspension and incubated at 37°C for 2 hours. After incubation, the mixture was centrifuged at 3000 rpm for 10 minutes, and the supernatant was collected. In vitro Cell Viability

To determine the cell viability of RAW264.7 cells incubated with HA-BR nano-GEL@CLT, CCK-8 assay was carried out. Briefly, RAW264.7 cells were seeded in a 96-well plate and incubated in a CO2 incubator at 37 °C for 1 day. Once the cells had fully adhered, the culture medium was discarded and replaced with the hydrogel extracts. 1 mL of the hydrogel was immersed in 10 mL of the culture medium, and incubated at 37 °C for 72 h, then the medium was collected as the hydrogel extracts. After incubation, CCK-8 solution was added. Followed by 1 hour of incubation at 37°C, the absorbance was determined via a microplate reader to measure the cell viability.

In vitro Antioxidant Performance Test

The free radical scavenging activity was evaluated using a DPPH assay.49 Specifically, an appropriate volume of hydrogel precursor solution was added into the DPPH reaction system, with an equal volume of PBS serving as the control. After incubation at 37 °C under dark conditions for 30 min, the absorbance of the solutions was measured at a wavelength of 517 nm using a microplate reader.

Intracellular ROS scavenging capacity of HA-BR nano-GEL@CLT was assessed by DCFH-DA staining. First, an in vitro inflammatory model was established by stimulating RAW264.7 cells with 150 ng/mL lipopolysaccharide (LPS) and 10 ng/mL interferon (IFN-γ). The cells were then cultured with hydrogel extracts from different treatment groups for 24 h. After incubation, the cells were treated with DCFH-DA probe in the dark for 30 min. The intracellular fluorescence intensity was detected using a flow cytometer (Beckmann, USA) and visualized by a confocal laser scanning microscope (CLSM, Zeiss, Germany).

Cellular Uptake Efficiency of Hydrogels

To evaluate the uptake behaviors of hydrogels in macrophages of different phenotypes, the hydrogels were first labeled with DID, a fluorescent dye. RAW264.7 cells with or without LPS-stimulation were seeded at a density of 1×105 cells/well in 12-well plates and confocal dishes, respectively. Following cell culture overnight, hydrogel extracts from different treatment groups (with 1 μg/mL of DID) were added and co-incubated with the cells for 1, 2, and 4 hours. After incubation, the cells were thoroughly washed with PBS and fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde. The fluorescence intensity was measured via flow cytometry. The uptake behaviors in activated macrophages were also visualized by CLSM. To explore CD44 receptor-mediated competitive inhibition, RAW264.7 cells were preincubated with free HA (500 μg/mL) for 1 h. Then HA-BR nano-GEL@CLT extract was added and incubated with the cells for 4 h. The fluorescence intensity was determined via CLSM.

In vitro Anti-Inflammatory Efficacy

To evaluate the in vitro anti-inflammatory ability of drug-loaded hydrogels, the expression of inflammatory cytokines was measured. Briefly, RAW264.7 cells were seeded at a density of 1×105 cells/well and activated as described previously. After treating with PBS, CLT solution and the hydrogel extracts of HA GEL@CLT and HA-BR nano-GEL@CLT, the culture supernatants were collected, and the contents of tumor necrosis factor-α (TNF-α), interleukin-6 (IL-6), and interleukin-10 (IL-10) in the supernatants were detected via an ELISA kit.

Regulatory Effects on Macrophage Polarization

To evaluate the regulatory effect of HA-BR nano-GEL@CLT on macrophage polarization, RAW264.7 cells were seeded in a 12-well plate and cultured overnight, followed by LPS-stimulation. Unstimulated cells were served as the control group. After co-treatment with PBS, CLT solution and the hydrogel extracts of HA GEL@CLT and HA-BR nano-GEL@CLT, the cells were washed with PBS and then incubated with CD86 antibody to label M1 macrophages and CD163 antibody to label M2 macrophages separately. The fluorescence intensity was analyzed by flow cytometry. Besides, the cells were also incubated with iNOS (M1) antibody and Arg1 (M2) antibody for staining. Nuclei were stained with DAPI for 10 min and the cells were observed using CLSM.

Evaluation of the Retention Time in the Joint of Adjuvant-Induced Arthritis Model

To establish an adjuvant-induced arthritis (AIA) model, 70 μL of CFA (Chondrex 10 mg/mL) was administered via subcutaneous injection into the hind paws of SD rats. At 14 days of post-adjuvant administration, rats reaching significant inflammatory plateau were selected as AIA model rats.

To investigate the in vivo retention behavior of HA GEL and HA-BR nano-GEL, DID fluorescent labeling was employed to prepare HA GEL@DID and HA-BR nano-GEL@DID, which were then monitored using an in vivo imaging system (IVIS, Spectral Instruments, USA). Briefly, rats were randomly assigned into three groups: (1) free DID group; (2) HA GEL@DID; (3) HA-BR nano-GEL@DID group administered with 100 μL of preparations (an equivalent dose of 80 μg/kg DID) via intra-articular injection, respectively. At predetermined time points (0.25, 1, 3, 5, 7, 14 and 21 days post-administration), the rats were anesthetized with chloral hydrate (300 mg/kg) by intraperitoneal injection and the fluorescence images were acquired on an IVIS system with DID excitation/emission wavelengths set to 633 nm/665 nm. The exposure time and field of view were kept consistent across all samples to eliminate systematic errors during image acquisition. The rats were euthanized by intraperitoneal administration of excessive sodium pentobarbital at a dosage of 150 mg/kg on day 1, 7 and 21. All euthanasia operations were performed in accordance with the animal euthanasia guidelines by the American Veterinary Medical Association (AVMA), and the articular knee joints and hind paws were excised, followed by the collection of major organs. At each time point, ex vivo fluorescence images were acquired by the IVIS system. For quantitative biodistribution analysis, uniform-sized and position-fixed regions of interest (ROIs) were drawn on imaging graphs, and ROI values were normalized to the background signal. Paw tissues were fixed in paraformaldehyde for 4 days, decalcified in 15% neutral EDTA solution for 15 days, and then paraffin-embedded. Subsequent immunofluorescence staining was performed using CD44 and CD68 antibodies.

In vivo Therapeutic Efficacy on AIA Rats

The AIA rats were randomly divided into 5 groups using a random number table (n=6): (1) normal group; (2) model group (PBS); (3) free CLT (1 mg/kg); (4) HA GEL@CLT; (5) HA-BR nano-GEL@CLT. The operators responsible for pharmacodynamic experiments were blinded to the group allocation throughout the whole experiment. The sample quantity of animals was determined according to published references combined with our previous study.50,51 The target therapeutic dose was set at 1 mg/kg for CLT, so the required injection volumes of hydrogels were calculated according to the rat body weights. On day 15 after adjuvant administration, each formulation was injected directly into the knee joints of the AIA rats, respectively and the progressions of joint inflammation and swelling were monitored every other day. Simultaneously, the thickness of the hind paws, clinical scores of arthritis, and body weight changes were measured every other day. Clinical scores of arthritis were assessed by two independent observers based on arthritis scoring. The scoring criteria were as follows: a score of 0 signified normal physiological status without edema or swelling; a score of 1 was assigned for mild swelling and erythema limited to the toes and ankle region; a score of 2 referred to moderate swelling and redness spreading from the ankle up to the tarsus; a score of 3 stood for severe edema together with erythema across the entire paw and ankle; a score of 4 represented diffuse severe swelling and erythema accompanied by impaired limb mobility.51 All quantitative data were acquired and recorded by two independent experimenters to ensure reliability. Subsequently, the rats were euthanized by the method mentioned before after 30 days’ treatment, and blood was collected from the abdominal aorta, and the hearts, livers, spleens, lungs, kidneys, and hind paws were excised. After complete decalcification, rat ankle joint tissues were embedded in paraffin, sectioned, and stained with H&E, SO-FG, and TRAP. Immunohistochemical analysis was conducted to evaluate the expression of HIF-1α, VEGFA, iNOS, TNF-α, IL-6, and IL-10, while immunofluorescence staining was applied to detect CD86 and CD163 receptors. Serum samples were harvested for the quantification of inflammatory cytokines using commercial ELISA kits. Also, the levels of SOD, MDA in rat paw tissues were determined.

Preliminary Safety Evaluation

After the assessment of therapeutic efficacy, major organs of AIA rats, including hearts, livers, spleens, lungs, and kidneys were excised, fixed, paraffin-embedded, and sectioned for H&E staining. Histological analysis of the sections was performed to evaluate systemic toxicity associated with the treatment. Additionally, ALT, AST, BUN, and CREA levels in serum were detected to evaluate potential hepatic and renal toxicity.

Data Analysis

Data are presented as the mean ± standard deviation (SD). Statistical comparisons were performed using Student’s t-test, one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA), or two-way ANOVA. A P-value < 0.05 was regarded as statistically significant. All data processing and graphical presentation were conducted via GraphPad Prism 10 software.

Results and Discussion

Preparation and Characterization of HA-BR NPs@CLT

To prepare HA-BR NPs@CLT, HA-BR conjugate, the key material was first successfully synthesized, and its structure was confirmed by 1H NMR and UV spectroscopy. The 1H NMR spectrum of HA-BR showed a characteristic proton peak at about 5.6 ppm, corresponding to the methine bridge linking the pyrrole rings of BR (Figure 1B). The UV spectrum showed that HA-BR exhibited a characteristic absorption peak at approximately 430 nm, which was consistent with that of bilirubin (Figure 1C). The degree of substitution was calculated based on the absorbance to give HA-BR conjugates with an average conjugation density of ~3 BR molecules per HA molecule.30,52–54 It is well known that bilirubin is poorly soluble in water, whereas the synthesized HA-BR conjugate exhibits excellent dispersibility and solubility in water (Figure 1D).52 All these results indicated the successful synthesis of HA-BR conjugate.

Subsequently, the corresponding HA-BR NPs@CLT were fabricated. The size and dispersity of HA-BR NPs@CLT were determined using a dynamic light scattering (DLS) instrument. The average particle size of HA-BR NPs@CLT was 118.5 nm, with a polydispersity index (PDI) of 0.186. TEM results showed that the nanoparticles exhibited a spherical-like morphology, and the distribution of particle size was consistent with that determined by DLS (Figure 2A) and the encapsulation efficiency was 80.3 ± 4.63%, while the drug loading achieved 7.5 ± 0.4%, respectively. Besides, the stability of size distribution for HA-BR NPs@CLT in water, PBS and serum for 7 days was also systematically investigated. Preparation, Characterization and Rheological Properties of HA-BR Nano-GEL@CLT

First, HA-SH, the basic material for hydrogel formation, was successfully synthesized and its structure was confirmed by 1H NMR spectroscopy (Figure 1B). Thiol modification ratio of HA‑SH was quantified by Ellman test and the degree of thiol substitution was 65.6%.46 Subsequently, the optimal gel formulation and preparation process were obtained after optimization and the morphology was observed (Figure 2B). The basic formulation of the HA hydrogel was described as follows: 10% HA-SH and 5% PEG-DA5000 were separately dissolved in 0.2 M HEPES buffer (pH 7.4), and rapid and complete gelation was accomplished within roughly 200 seconds at 37 °C (Figure 2C). SEM observations showed that HA-BR nano-GEL@CLT possessed a densely cross-linked three-dimensional porous network structure. Such an interconnected porous network can construct a three-dimensional diffusion channel, which is conducive to the sustained and controlled release of CLT.

Subsequently, the gelation kinetics of HA-BR nano-GEL@CLT were investigated. According to time‑sweep measurements, the optimal gel formulation underwent a rapid sol‑to‑gel phase transition within 200 seconds (Figure 2D), which was consistent with our visual observation. Similar with earlier reports, adjusting the concentration of hydrogel precursors offers an effective strategy to regulate both the cross‑linking rate and the gelation kinetics (Figure 2D and E).47 Frequency sweep measurements indicated that the storage modulus (G′) was distinctly higher than the loss modulus (G″) over the entire range of frequencies examined. This behavior is characteristic of viscoelastic gel-like materials, verifying that HA-BR nano-GEL@CLT possesses good structural stability and elastic property. Such robust rheological properties guarantee that the hydrogel retains its structural and functional integrity under diverse physiological environments.51 Besides, the shear responsiveness of HA-BR nano-GEL@CLT was also explored (Figure 2F). It was found that the increasing strain led to an intersection of G′ and G″ at the critical point, corresponding to a gel-liquid state conversion. This reversible state transition under shear stress endows the hydrogel with injectable flowability and subsequent gel structural reversion. Importantly, when the strain was elevated to 1000%, the G′ of HA-BR nano-GEL@CLT dropped rapidly to less than 10 Pa, comparable to the modulus of natural synovial fluid (approximately 1 Pa),55,56 demonstrating its capability to provide lubrication and serve as an articular synovial fluid substitute. The coefficient of friction (COF) of HA-BR nano-GEL@CLT was also tested to assess its lubrication performance, and the hydrogel maintained a low COF value at roughly 0.13 (Figure S2), which directly verified the excellent lubrication behavior of the prepared hydrogel system.57

Additionally, the equilibrium swelling property of HA-BR nano-GEL@CLT was investigated. The swelling profiles presented in Figure 2G showed the hydrogel exhibited rapid water absorption and reached swelling equilibrium within 2 hours, which can be ascribed to the porous network structure of the hydrogel. Moreover, HA-BR nano-GEL@CLT underwent gradual degradation in PBS over 21 days without full breakdown (Figure 2H). This slow degradability reveals its great potential as a sustained-release drug depot.

The pH levels of most joint parts are altered under inflammatory conditions caused by rheumatoid arthritis.58 In this study, the in vitro CLT release behavior of the hydrogels was investigated in PBS (pH 7.4, 6.5 and 5.8) to mimic normal physiological and mildly acidic environments in RA, respectively. As displayed in Figure S3, both HA GEL@CLT and HA-BR nano-GEL@CLT displayed obvious sustained-release behavior of CLT over 15 days in PBS (pH 7.4), which could be explained by the slow and sustained degradation of the hydrogel network. HA GEL@CLT exhibited relatively rapid release kinetics compared to HA-BR nano-GEL@CLT. Within the first day, the cumulative release rates at pH 7.4, 6.5, and 5.8 were 25.66%, 31.23%, and 38.24%, respectively, indicating a clear pH-dependent drug release property. Notably, HA-BR nano-GEL@CLT exhibited a slower release rate than HA GEL@CLT, with cumulative release rates of 16.32%, 22.64%, and 32.56% at pH 7.4, 6.5, and 5.8 after 24 h, respectively. Similar results were observed for release profiles under conditions of various H2O2 concentrations. The release of CLT from HA GEL@CLT was accelerated as H2O2 concentration rose, verifying the ROS-responsive property of HA and PEG-based hydrogel.59 Besides, the CLT release behavior of HA-BR nano-GEL@CLT differed from that of HA GEL@CLT under the same conditions, accompanied by a marked reduction in CLT release rate. This discrepancy might be attributed to the stronger drug retention capacity derived from HA-BR nanostructures incorporated into the gel matrix.

In vitro Biocompatibility and Cell Uptake Behavior of HA-BR Nano-GEL@CLT

Given that hydrogel dressings are directly injected into the articular cavity, excellent biocompatibility is an essential characteristic of the hydrogels. The hemolytic properties of the hydrogels were evaluated by using rat blood. As depicted in Figure 2I and J, the hemolysis ratio of all groups was below 5%, indicating that the hydrogels are non-hemolytic and can be safely administered via intra-articular injection.60

Cell viability of the formulations was evaluated via CCK-8 assay in RAW264.7 cells. Experimental groups included blank hydrogel extracts, CLT solution, HA GEL@CLT, and HA-BR nano-GEL@CLT gel extracts. First, after RAW264.7 cells were incubated with a 0.2 g/mL blank hydrogel extract for 48 hours, the cell survival rate was above 80%, indicating the safety of the hydrogels (Figure 3A). Besides, as shown in Figure 3B, CLT exhibited potent cytotoxicity toward RAW264.7 macrophages at concentrations above 0.5 μg/mL. Both HA GEL@CLT and HA-BR nano-GEL@CLT inhibited the cytotoxicity of CLT at all the tested concentrations, and CLT at 0.125 µg/mL was ultimately selected for further cellular experiments from a cytocompatibility and biosafety perspective. Since macrophages serve as the primary cellular source of proinflammatory mediators and are highly enriched within inflamed synovial tissues, the internalization of therapeutic formulations by activated macrophages is essential for evaluating the therapeutic efficiency against RA.61 In this study, cellular uptake experiments were performed using resting macrophages and LPS‑stimulated macrophages and the hydrogels were labelled with DID, a fluorescent probe dye. Nevertheless, the growth rate of cellular uptake efficiency for HA‑BR nano‑GEL@DID group relative to HA GEL@DID group was markedly higher in LPS-stimulated macrophages than that observed in LPS-unstimulated cells. Specifically, the fluorescent intensity of HA‑BR nano‑GEL@DID was 1.05-fold, 1.10-fold and 0.98-fold higher than HA GEL@DID at 1 h, 2 h and 4 h in LPS-stimulated macrophages respectively, while the fluorescent intensity of HA‑BR nano‑GEL@DID was 0.33-fold, 0.36-fold and 0.33-fold higher than HA GEL@DID in resting macrophages. This was most likely attributed to the abundant CD44 expression on activated macrophages, and these receptors facilitated the internalization of HA‑BR nano‑GEL@DID. CLSM results also verified significant disparities between HA GEL@DID and HA‑BR nano‑GEL@DID groups at the designated time points, which was consistent with flow cytometric quantification results. As illustrated in Figure 3E and F, after 1, 2, and 4 h of co-incubation, CLSM images and their semi-quantitative results demonstrated that the fluorescent intensity of HA‑BR nano‑GEL@DID was remarkably stronger than that of HA GEL@DID in LPS-stimulated RAW264.7 cells.

Furthermore, a competitive inhibition assay was conducted to verify the CD44-targeting capability of HA for HA‑BR nano‑GEL@DID (Figure S5). The competitive blocking treatment using free HA markedly reduced the cellular uptake of HA‑BR nano‑GEL@DID, confirming the maintenance of the inherent CD44-targeting capacity of linear HA molecules.62 Collectively, these findings verify that the specific interaction between HA and CD44 receptors abundantly expressed on M1 macrophages contributes to the enhanced targeting efficiency of HA‑BR nano‑GEL.

In vitro Antioxidant, Anti-Inflammatory and Immunomodulatory Properties of HA-BR Nano-GEL@CLT

Within the pathological microenvironment of RA, excessive ROS trigger the phenotypic transition of macrophages from the anti‑inflammatory M2 phenotype toward the pro‑inflammatory M1 phenotype. Activated M1 macrophages subsequently release abundant pro‑inflammatory mediators, including TNF‑α and IL-6, and further lead to higher ROS levels, thereby establishing a self‑sustaining vicious cycle that drives chronic inflammation and progressive joint destruction.33 First, the antioxidant capacity of HA-BR nano-GEL@CLT was evaluated by determining its in vitro radical scavenging activity via a DPPH radical scavenging assay. As displayed in Figure 4A, DPPH radical scavenging ability of HA‑BR nano‑GEL group was 1.25-fold that of blank HA GEL (p < 0.001), indicating that HA-BR conjugate had strong ROS-scavenging and anti-oxidant properties due to bilirubin. Besides, compared to all treatment groups, HA‑BR nano‑GEL@CLT precursor displayed the highest DPPH scavenging ratio, indicating that this gel system possessed the strongest in vitro radical scavenging capacity. Consecutively, the ROS scavenging ability of different treatments was also assessed via DCFH-DA staining and a consistent tendency was observed between DCFH-DA staining and DPPH scavenging assay results. Flow cytometry results showed that HA-BR nano-GEL@CLT eliminated excess ROS produced by M1 macrophages most effectively compared to other treatment groups (Figure 4B and C). This finding was further confirmed by CLSM imaging, which showed that DCFH-DA fluorescence intensity significantly increased after LPS induction, indicating an increase in intracellular ROS levels of M1 macrophages. Furthermore, HA‑BR nano‑GEL group showed stronger DCFH-DA fluorescence intensity compared to blank HA GEL (p < 0.001) and HA‑BR nano‑GEL@CLT group exhibited stronger DCFH-DA fluorescence intensity compared to HA‑BR nano‑GEL (p < 0.001) (Figure 4D and E). Macrophages play a vital role in the pathogenesis of multiple chronic skeletal disorders, including rheumatoid arthritis. Macrophages are highly plastic cells whose polarization into M1 or M2 phenotypes is shaped dynamically by the surrounding microenvironment. Classically activated M1 macrophages are prominent producers of pro-inflammatory cytokines, such as IL‑6 and TNF‑α. On the other hand, M2 macrophages secrete large amounts of IL-10 and TGF-β2, mediating downstream anti-inflammatory and immunosuppressive activities.63 The cellular levels of inflammation-associated cytokines after treatment were determined using an ELISA kit. The results displayed that LPS stimulation significantly upregulated the secretion of IL-6 and TNF-α, following the downregulation of IL-10 in quiescent RAW264.7 cells, verifying their polarization into the pro-inflammatory M1 phenotype (Figure 4F, G and H). Compared to all treatment groups, HA-BR nano-GEL@CLT significantly reduced the levels of IL-6 and TNF-α, while elevating the expression level of IL-10, suggesting that HA-BR nano-GEL@CLT exerts a regulatory effect on the inflammatory microenvironment in rheumatoid arthritis (RA).

Based on the inflammatory cytokine profiles, macrophage polarization was studied by flow cytometry. As shown in Figure 4I, HA-BR nano-GEL@CLT significantly reduced the percentage of CD86-labeled M1 macrophages, while elevating the proportion of CD163-labeled M2 macrophages compared with all other treatment groups. Among all groups, HA-BR nano-GEL@CLT showed the lowest M1/M2 ratio, and the M1/M2 ratio in HA-BR nano-GEL@CLT group was only 0.19-fold that of model group (Figure 4J), suggesting that HA-BR nano-GEL@CLT most effectively drives macrophage phenotypic switching toward the anti-inflammatory M2 subtype in vitro, demonstrating its robust immunomodulatory activity. Besides, immunofluorescence staining was used to validate the macrophage polarization (Figure S6). The results confirmed that HA-BR nano-GEL@CLT induced macrophage polarization toward the anti-inflammatory M2 phenotype by reduced iNOS expression and enhanced Arg1 expression. Collectively, these results indicate that HA-BR nano-GEL@CLT exerts anti-inflammatory therapeutic effects by downregulating ROS levels, regulating the balance between pro‑ and anti‑inflammatory cytokine secretion and modulating macrophage polarization.

In vivo Persistence and Targeted Distribution of HA-BR Nano-GEL@DID in Articular Knee Joints

Injectable hydrogels represent a highly promising delivery platform due to their capability for site-specific delivery and prolonged retention within the target tissue.64 Following administration, HA-BR nano-GEL rapidly forms a stable gel structure in situ in response to the transition from room temperature to body temperature, thereby facilitating sustained drug release within the joint cavity. To further validate its in vivo performance, we evaluated the retention kinetics of HA-BR nano-GEL@DID via intra-articular injection in an adjuvant-induced arthritis (AIA) rat model. Following the establishment of AIA rats, DID solution, HA-GEL@DID, and HA-BR nano-GEL@DID were administered via intra-articular injection respectively, and dynamic observations were performed using an IVIS system at the designated time points after administration.

As shown in Figure 5A, a strong fluorescent signal derived from DID solution was observed on day 0.25 and 1, whereas no obvious fluorescence was detectable at subsequent time points. These observations indicate rapid clearance of DID solution from the knee joint following intra-articular administration. On the contrary, fluorescence signals originating from HA-GEL@DID hydrogel depot were detectable in vivo for at least 14 days and fluorescence signals of HA-BR nano-GEL@DID were sustained in vivo for at least 21 days. Semi-quantitative analysis revealed that the fluorescence intensity in HA-BR nano-GEL@DID group was significantly higher than that in the other two groups at all time points (Figure 5B). These observations demonstrate that HA-BR nano-GEL@DID hydrogel depot exhibits significantly prolonged retention in vivo compared with DID solution and HA-GEL@DID. Once injected into the articular cavity, free hyaluronic acid is rapidly degraded by hyaluronidase catalysis. Following the covalent crosslinking of HA with PEG to fabricate hydrogel scaffolds, a dense three-dimensional network is constructed. This interpenetrated crosslinked network acts as a physical steric barrier, hindering direct molecular contact between hyaluronidase and HA polymer backbones and consequently slowing enzymatic catabolism of the HA-derived matrix. Consistent findings were also observed in ex vivo imaging of the knee joints (Figure 5C). Fluorescence in the DID group was rapidly eliminated from the knee joints, whereas fluorescence in the hydrogel groups remained detectable on day 7. Notably, fluorescence in HA-BR nano-GEL@DID group persisted in the joint for up to 21 days, indicating that the HA-BR nano-GEL@DID formulation could prolong the retention time substantially by acting as a drug depot. Compared with the free DID solution and HA-GEL@DID groups, markedly stronger fluorescence accumulation in the limbs was observed in the HA-BR nano-GEL@DID at day 1, 7 and 21 (Figure 5D), suggesting the prolonged blood circulation and enhanced targeting efficiency to inflamed joints, which may be attributed to HA-BR modification. Meanwhile, ex vivo fluorescence imaging of major organs (Figure 5E) revealed that the fluorescent signals of the hydrogels were predominantly distributed in the livers even after 21 days, suggesting the prolonged circulation time of DID in hydrogels and that a portion of DID that entered systemic circulation may be recognized and eliminated by the hepatic reticuloendothelial system. In vivo Therapeutic Effects of HA-BR Nano-GEL@CLT on Rheumatoid Arthritis

The in vivo therapeutic efficacy of HA-BR nano-GEL@CLT was evaluated in AIA rat models. Following 14 days of arthritis induction, the rats exhibited severe swelling in their ankle joints and hind paws. Saline was administered intra-articularly in normal rats as a control group and AIA rats as a model group, while CLT solution, HA GEL@CLT and HA-BR nano-GEL@CLT were injected intra-articularly in AIA rats as treatment groups after 14 days of arthritis induction (Figure 6A). As depicted in Figure 6B, AIA rats in all experimental groups exhibited varying degrees of body weight loss compared with healthy rats, suggesting that rheumatoid arthritis severely compromised normal physiological activity. Furthermore, the model group showed more severe weight reduction, which may be attributed to the absence of drug administration compared with other treatment groups. Paw swelling degree serves as a key indicator for assessing therapeutic efficacy in AIA rats. As shown in Figure 6C, D and E, all the treatment groups exhibited reduced paw thickness relative to the saline group, whereas CLT solution group only showed a mild reduction in comparison. Among other treatment groups, the rats in the HA-BR nano-GEL@CLT group exhibited the lowest paw thickness (p < 0.001), indicating greater amelioration of inflamed limbs relative to CLT solution and HA GEL@CLT groups. Meanwhile, the clinical arthritis score was markedly reduced following HA-BR nano-GEL@CLT treatment, suggesting satisfactory therapeutic efficacy against arthritis (Figure 6F). Chronic Inflammation Amelioration and Macrophage Polarization Regulation by HA-BR Nano-GEL@CLT in RA

To explore the underlying mechanism responsible for the therapeutic efficacy of HA-BR nano-GEL@CLT in RA, the expression levels of inflammation-related factors in serum were firstly quantitatively analyzed. As shown in Figure 6G, H and I, the levels of pro-inflammatory cytokines including IL-6 and TNF-α in the model group were significantly upregulated and IL-10, the anti-inflammatory cytokine, was down-regulated compared to the normal group, indicating sustained and severe inflammatory responses in RA. Although all treatment groups exerted a certain regulatory effect on inflammation-related factors, HA-BR nano-GEL@CLT group exhibited a more pronounced reduction in the expression of IL-6 and TNF-α, and a more significant increase in the expression of IL-10 compared with CLT and HA GEL@CLT groups. Importantly, compared to HA GEL@CLT group, the expressions of IL-6 and TNF-α in HA-BR nano-GEL@CLT group were reduced by 27% and 43%, while the expression of IL-10 was increased by 47%, respectively.

Meanwhile, oxidative stress functions as a pivotal mediator in inflammatory and immune cellular reactions, thereby profoundly affecting joint injury in rheumatoid arthritis (RA). MDA is widely recognized as a classic indicator of oxidative stress and SOD serves as a critical antioxidant enzyme that neutralizes free radicals and alleviates the damage caused by oxidative stress. Therefore, the levels of malondialdehyde (MDA) and superoxide dismutase (SOD) were assessed to explore the regulatory effect of HA-BR nano-GEL@CLT on oxidative stress. Compared to the normal group, the model group markedly down-regulated the expression of SOD and upregulated the expression of MDA (Figure 6J and K), indicating enhanced lipid peroxidation and oxidative stress in inflamed joint tissues. Although there were increased levels of SOD and decreased levels of MDA observed after treatment with CLT and HA GEL@CLT, HA-BR nano-GEL@CLT exhibited a stronger suppression of MDA levels and a more potent enhancement of SOD expression (p < 0.01), reflecting a superior capability to alleviate oxidative damage.

Immunohistochemical staining of ankle joint tissues further validated that HA-BR nano-GEL@CLT notably suppressed inflammatory infiltration by reducing TNF-α and IL-6 levels, while elevating the expression of IL-10 (Figure 7A). Furthermore, immunohistochemical staining of HIF-1α and VEGFA in ankle joints was evaluated. HIF-1α and VEGFA act as critical mediators in the pathological progression of rheumatoid arthritis. Specifically, HIF-1α functions as a key mediator of hypoxic responses and plays a critical role in regulating cellular adaptation to low-oxygen environments, while VEGFA drives pathological angiogenesis and pannus formation, ultimately aggravating joint damage.66 The results showed that compared to CLT and HA GEL@CLT group, treatment with HA-BR nano-GEL@CLT resulted in markedly decreased expression levels of HIF-1α and VEGFA in joint tissues (Figure 7A). Collectively, these observations demonstrate that HA‑BR nano‑GEL@CLT exhibits potent inhibitory effects on inflammatory cascades, alleviates local hypoxic stress, and restrains pathological angiogenesis in arthritic joints. By modulating these key pathological factors, HA‑BR nano‑GEL@CLT contributes to the remodeling of the dysregulated inflammatory microenvironment and effectively impedes the progression of rheumatoid arthritis. To directly visualize the modulatory effects of HA-BR nano-GEL@CLT on macrophage polarization, immunofluorescent staining was performed to assess the expression and colocalization of M1 and M2 macrophage phenotypes. As illustrated in Figure 7B, treatment with HA-BR nano-GEL@CLT resulted in a prominent upregulation of CD163 expression representing M2 macrophages, accompanied by a marked reduction in CD86 levels representing M1 macrophages. Further semi-quantitative analysis of fluorescence intensity revealed that HA-BR nano-GEL@CLT treatment resulted in a marked phenotypic shift of macrophages from M1 to M2 subtype (Figure 7C). Immunohistochemical analysis of iNOS, a classical M1 marker, was also carried out to explore the inhibitory effect of HA-BR nano-GEL@CLT on M1 polarization. Significant upregulation of iNOS was observed in the model group, while treatment with CLT, HA GEL@CLT and HA-BR nano-GEL@CLT resulted in significant suppression of the elevated iNOS level (Figure 7A and C). Importantly, HA-BR nano-GEL@CLT group exhibited the lowest iNOS level among all the treatment groups, demonstrating the most potent capacity to suppress macrophage polarization toward the M1 phenotype. Macrophages with different polarization phenotypes are widely recognized as key contributors of secreting inflammatory mediators, and these inflammatory mediators such as TNF-α and IL-6 can regulate macrophage phenotypic polarization through multiple signaling cascades, including Notch, JAK/STAT, MAPK, NF-κB and Nrf2/HO-1.67,68 For example, TNF-α activates NF-κB signaling pathway, and NF-κB pathway activation promotes M1 macrophage polarization, which further triggers the secretion of abundant mature pro-inflammatory cytokines.69 Besides, pro-inflammatory cytokines TNF-α and IL-1β can trigger the activation of the MAPK signaling pathway, thereby promoting macrophage proliferation and survival.67 Collectively, these results reveal that HA-BR nano-GEL@CLT effectively drives the phenotypic transition of macrophages from a proinflammatory M1 phenotype toward an anti-inflammatory M2 phenotype in vivo, thereby remodeling the inflammatory microenvironment of arthritic lesions.

Biosafety Evaluation of HA-BR Nano-GEL@CLT in vivo

Celastrol is inherently associated with hepatotoxicity and nephrotoxicity, which markedly limits its clinical application.70 Therefore, the biosafety of HA-BR nano-GEL@CLT was evaluated by histopathological analysis. H&E staining showed hepatic cellular atrophy and destruction of renal tubular structure following treatment with free CLT (Figure 8A), whereas no obvious pathological abnormalities were observed in the hearts, lungs or spleens across all experimental groups. Furthermore, biochemical analysis of rat serum revealed that creatinine (Crea), blood urea nitrogen (BUN), alanine aminotransferase (ALT) and aspartate aminotransferase (AST) were significantly increased in CLT group (Figure 8B–E), indicating a certain degree of hepatic and renal damage. On the contrary, these biochemical parameters remained within the normal range in HA-BR nano-GEL@CLT group, implying significantly alleviated hepatic and renal toxicity. Limitations

Despite the promising anti-arthritic effects observed in the study, several inherent limitations should be acknowledged. Primarily, the AIA model is incapable of recapitulating the autoimmune pathological features of RA patients, since the immune system and in vivo microenvironment of human body are far more complex than those of rats.71 Large animal models such as rabbits and goats possess joint anatomical structures, synovial microenvironments and immune systems more similar to humans, but the main existing challenges include high experimental costs, long feeding cycles and stringent requirements for animal ethical approval. Significant individual variations among large animals may lead to fluctuating experimental data. Moreover, obvious interspecies differences in articular hyaluronidase activity and drug metabolism patterns compared with rats may alter the in vivo degradation and drug release profiles of the hydrogel. Although the core objective of the present study was to fabricate an injectable, long-acting, targeted therapeutic hydrogel platform and validate its therapeutic efficacy and underlying mechanisms, methotrexate and other routine first-line RA medications were not set as positive controls in animal experiments even though celastrol represents a promising anti-inflammatory and antioxidant agent. In addition, RAW264.7 cell line cannot fully recapitulate the biological characteristics of primary synovial macrophages isolated from RA patients or arthritic rats. Moreover, the biosafety risks related to long-term hydrogel retention inside joint cavities remain to be assessed.

Conclusion

This study successfully fabricated HA-BR nano-GEL@CLT, an innovative hydrogel platform by loading CLT into HA-BR nanoparticles, which were then integrated into HA hydrogel for RA treatment. Our investigations suggested that HA-BR nano-GEL@CLT possessed anti-inflammatory and antioxidant capacities, and could effectively modulate the polarization of macrophages. Meanwhile, HA-BR nano-GEL@CLT exhibited prolonged retention and CD44 targeting capacity in vivo, and exerted enhanced therapeutic effects compared to CLT and HA GEL@CLT. Furthermore, the hydrogel platform ameliorated the toxicity of CLT, strengthening its safety in RA therapy. Nevertheless, the research is still in the preclinical animal experiment stage, indicating that long-term joint cavity retention, degradation metabolism, repeated administration safety and other verifications are still needed to objectively define the clinical transformation potential of the delivery system. In summary, the fabricated composite hydrogel offers a promising candidate strategy for alleviating rheumatoid arthritis.
