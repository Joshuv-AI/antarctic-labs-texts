Effect of glucocorticoid production by the enteric nervous system on neuronal connectivity is blocked during inflammation in a GR-dependent pathway
Authors: Justine Blin, Valentine S. Moullé, Zoé Garel, Ulysse Bertholon, Thibauld Oullier, Tony Durand, Catherine Le Berre-Scoul, Damien Masson, Kalyane Bach-Ngohou, Michel Neunlist
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
SummaryGlucocorticoid (GC) extra-adrenal synthesis plays a central role in maintaining local homeostasis. However, whether GCs can be synthesized by the enteric nervous system (ENS), a key regulator of gut function, and how it might affect its function remain unexplored. In this study, we used primary rat ENS cultures to determine whether local GC production occurs within the ENS and to evaluate its functional impact under both basal and inflammatory conditions. First, we demonstrated that the primary ENS cultures, especially enteric glial cells, produce GCs from 11-dehydrocorticosterone (11DHC) through the regeneration pathway. Second, 11DHC treatment significantly increased the expression and density of key molecules associated with neuronal connectivity in a glucocorticoid receptor (GR)-dependent manner. Finally, inflammation blocked the 11DHC-induced enhancement of connectivity molecule expression and was associated with a reduced pGR-232/pGR-246 ratio. Our study demonstrates that GCs locally produced by the ENS enhance enteric connectivity via a GR-dependent mechanism, which is abolished under inflammatory conditions.

Introduction

Glucocorticoids (GCs) are essential, life-sustaining steroid hormones synthesized primarily in the zona fasciculata of the adrenal cortex.1 Their de novo synthesis begins with the mitochondrial import of cholesterol mediated by the steroidogenic acute regulatory protein (StAR), followed by a cascade of reactions catalyzed by steroidogenic enzymes. In rodents, four enzymes are required for the de novo production of corticosterone, whereas in humans, five enzymes mediate the synthesis of cortisol.1,2 The presence of a hydroxyl group at the 11β position is essential for GC biological activity. Accordingly, GCs can be inactivated into their inert forms—cortisone in humans and 11-dehydrocorticosterone (11DHC) in rodents—by the enzyme 11β-hydroxysteroid dehydrogenase type 2 (11βHsd2). These inactive metabolites can subsequently be reactivated, converting 11-oxo-GCs to their active 11-hydroxy counterparts (cortisol and corticosterone, respectively), through the reductase activity of 11β-hydroxysteroid dehydrogenase type 1 (11βHsd1) in the regeneration pathway.2 Expression of 11βHsd2 is relatively restricted, being mainly localized to the renal cortex, colon, salivary glands, and placenta, whereas 11βHsd1 is broadly expressed across multiple tissues.2

GCs exert their effects through the GC receptor (GR), which is ubiquitously expressed and regulates the transcription of numerous genes involved in a wide range of physiological processes, including energy metabolism and inflammation,3 as well as cell-specific functions such as epithelial repair and neuronal connectivity. Notably, contradictory findings have been reported regarding the effects of GCs on neuronal cell types in both the central and peripheral nervous systems, particularly concerning their influence on neurogenesis and synaptic plasticity.4

Although the adrenal glands represent the primary source of systemic GC production, local GC synthesis has been described in several peripheral tissues. Despite being produced in much smaller quantities than in the adrenal glands, locally synthesized GCs appear to play essential roles in maintaining tissue-specific homeostasis.5,6,7 This local production can occur either through de novo synthesis or via regeneration pathways. Locally synthesized GCs primarily act in an autocrine or paracrine manner and do not significantly contribute to circulating GC levels.8

In the gastrointestinal tract, de novo GC synthesis has been identified in several cell types, including immune cells and intestinal epithelial cells (IECs).2,9 Local GC synthesis within the gut can be upregulated in response to immune or inflammatory stress, contributing to the intestinal immune response and inflammatory regulation.2,10 For instance, TNF-α has been shown to stimulate GC synthesis in IECs, and GCs produced by IECs counteract the deleterious effects of TNF-α by enhancing tight junction protein expression and inhibiting TNF-α-induced apoptosis.11 In addition, these locally produced GCs attenuate immune cell recruitment and activation by suppressing pro-inflammatory cytokine expression and promoting apoptosis in activated T lymphocytes.2,9

Another major cellular component of the gut is the enteric nervous system (ENS), a key regulator of gastrointestinal homeostasis encompassing motility, epithelial barrier integrity, and immune responses. Recent evidence indicates that GCs can modulate proteins critical for enteric neurotransmission and gastrointestinal motility.12,13 Moreover, a GR-dependent enhancement of the cholinergic component within the ENS has been proposed to contribute, at least in part, to motility alterations observed in rodent models of repeated acute stress.14 However, whether the ENS possesses the capacity to synthesize GC—either de novo or through regeneration pathways—remains largely unexplored. Furthermore, while inflammation is known to alter enteric neuronal phenotype and function,15,16,17 the potential for inflammatory processes to influence GC production and the consequent effects on ENS activity have yet to be elucidated. Therefore, this study aims to determine whether local GC production occurs within the ENS and to evaluate its impact on ENS function under both basal and inflammatory conditions.

Results

To test the ability of ENS cells to produce GCs via the de novo or reactivation pathway, we measured GC levels in the culture supernatant of primary cultures of ENS using liquid chromatography-tandem mass spectrometry (LC-MS/MS). Under basal conditions, neither corticosterone nor 11DHC was detected in the medium of primary ENS cultures (Figure 1F). Then, we first aimed to confirm the inability of ENS to produce GCs from cholesterol via the de novo pathway. Since our culture medium did not contain cholesterol, we overloaded the culture medium with cholesterol, and we carried out the same experiment. Once again, neither corticosterone nor 11DHC was found in the culture supernatant (Figure S1). In a second step, we aimed to determine the ability of the ENS to produce GCs via regeneration of 11DHC. We added 11DHC in the culture medium at the concentration of 500 nmol/L and determined the concentration of 11DHC and corticosterone in the culture supernatants at 0, 14, 24, and 48 h after 11DHC addition (Figure 1F). We observed that 11DHC levels decreased over time, while corticosterone levels, initially undetectable, increased over time to reach a concentration close to 500 nmol/L after 48 h (p < 0.0001, two-way ANOVA). Thus, we demonstrated that the ENS can produce corticosterone from 11DHC by the reactivation pathway but not via the de novo synthesis pathway.

Enteric glial cells are major contributors to GC production by the ENS

As primary ENS cultures consist of two major cellular components—enteric neurons and enteric glial cells (EGCs) with a neuron-to-glia ratio of 1:1018—we next sought to determine the relative contribution of each cell type to GC production within the ENS. Using pure cultures of enteric neurons, we found that the mRNA expression of GC regeneration enzyme Hsd11b1 and inactivation enzyme Hsd11b2 was low in neurons (Figures 1C and 1D). In contrast, the expression of genes Bhsd3d1 and Cyp21a1 was markedly higher (Figure 1B). Consistently, the addition of 11DHC to pure neuronal cultures did not result in its conversion to corticosterone (p = 0.3964, two-way ANOVA) (Figure 1G).

Compared with neurons, mRNA expression of Hsd11b1 and Hsd11b2 (Figures 1C and 1D) was higher, while mRNA expression of Bhsd3d1 and Cyp21a1 was reduced in EGCs (Figure 1B). Furthermore, the ratio of Hsd11b1/Hsd11b2 was also increased in EGCs in comparison to enteric neurons and the ENS (Figure 1E). Moreover, incubation of EGCs with 11DHC led to a significant GC production, following a faster time course than that observed in primary ENS cultures (p < 0.0001, two-way ANOVA) (Figure 1H). Altogether, these findings identify EGCs as the main cellular source of GCs within the ENS.

11βHsd1 selective inhibitor reduces the conversion of 11DHC into corticosterone in the ENS

To confirm that corticosterone production by the ENS was mediated by 11βHsd1 activity, we tested the impact of a selective inhibitor of 11βHsd1 (10J, 1 μM) on 11DHC-induced corticosterone production. Treatment with 10J significantly reduced corticosterone production (p = 0.0173 for treatment effect, two-way ANOVA) (Figure 1J) and slowed down 11DHC consumption (p = 0.0681 for treatment effect, two-way ANOVA) (Figure 1I), reinforcing the role of 11βHsd1 as a contributor to GC production in the ENS.

11DHC/corticosterone ratio is higher in colonic tissue than in plasma

To determine whether 11DHC is locally present in the gut, we measured the concentration of 11DHC and corticosterone in both colon and plasma samples of control rats (Table 1). In colonic tissues, the 11DHC level was 144.2 nmol/mg of tissue (confidence interval [CI]: 73.8–214.6), and the corticosterone level was 82.6 nmol/mg of tissue (CI: 49.0–116.2), yielding an active/inactive hormone ratio of 0.57. Conversely, in the plasma, 11DHC was ten times less concentrated than corticosterone (116.3 ± 44.9 nmol/L vs. 1,263.0 ± 73.8 nmol/L, respectively). In contrast to the colon, in plasma, the active/inactive hormone ratio was 10.9 with concentrations of 1,263.0 (CI: 672.4–1853.5) and 116.3 nmol/L (CI: 80.4–152.2) for corticosterone and 11DHC, respectively. 11βHsd1-induced corticosterone production by ENS primary cultures activates GR and leads to upregulation of GR target genes

We next aimed to determine whether the corticosterone locally produced by primary ENS cultures was biologically active. We performed qPCR analyses on primary ENS to assess the expression of three GR target genes—Fkbp5, Sgk1, and Tsc22d3—known to be specifically upregulated by GR. We showed that the expression of these target genes was significantly increased (p value = 0.0022 for Fkbp5, p value = 0.0001 for Sgk1, and p value = 0.0007 for Tsc22d3) 48 h after the addition of 11DHC (Figures 2A–2C). We also quantified the protein expression of GR by western blot, since it is well established that activation of GR by GCs induces GR downregulation.19 Accordingly, we observed a decrease in GR expression following the addition of 11DHC to the culture medium (p value = 0.0006), suggesting that locally produced corticosterone activated GR and induced its own downregulation (Figures 2D and 2E). In addition, since phosphorylation at serine 232 (pGR-232) is induced by GC binding to GR and has been described as a biomarker of GR activity,20 we studied its expression following incubation of ENS with 11DHC. Endogenous GC induces neuritogenesis in ENS primary cultures, in a GR-dependent manner

Given that GCs have been reported to influence neuritogenesis and neuronal connectivity in the central nervous system (CNS) in a highly context-dependent manner, depending on the duration, the intensity of exposure, and the cellular state,21,22,23 we assessed the expression of key connectivity-related proteins in response to 11DHC treatment in primary ENS cultures. We first showed that the protein expression of β-III tubulin and synapsin-1 was significantly upregulated by GCs locally produced (p value = 0.0416 for β-III tubulin and p value = 0.0111 for synapsin-1) (Figures 3A and 3B), whereas PGP9.5 and synaptophysin levels remained unchanged (data not shown). Next, we aimed to determine whether the effects of 11DHC were mediated through GR-dependent pathways using a pharmacological approach. Pre-treatment with CORT108297, a specific GR antagonist, prevented the increase of β-III tubulin, synapsin-1, and nNOS expression compared to controls. In addition, CORT108297 significantly reduced synapsin-1 but not β-III tubulin and nNOS expression as compared to 11DHC alone. These results suggest that the effects of 11DHC on neuronal connectivity markers are mediated, at least in part, by GR signaling (Figure 3). To further confirm that the increased expression of β-III tubulin was associated with enhanced nerve fiber density, we performed immunohistochemistry to quantify the surface area of the ENS culture occupied by β-III tubulin-immunoreactive (IR) fibers. In the presence of 11DHC, the surface area occupied by β-III tubulin-IR fibers significantly increased to 11.4% (CI: 10.5–12.3) compared with 9.5% without 11DHC (CI: 8.7–10.3) (p value = 0.007) (Figures 3D and 3E). Interestingly, this increase in fiber density was not associated with any change in the total number of neurons, as identified by Hu staining (data not shown).

Inflammation induces an increase in 11βHsd1 expression and a decrease in 11BHsd2 expression, associated with an increase in the conversion of 11DHC to corticosterone

Since inflammation has been shown to modulate GC synthesis by gut-resident cells such as IECs,10,24 we next investigated the effects of inflammation on GC-regeneration-related enzymatic pathways (11β-Hsd1/2) and GC production by the ENS in vitro.

We first demonstrated that treatment of primary ENS cultures with IL1-β and TNF-α (at a concentration of 1 ng/mL) induced a significant increase in Hsd11b1 gene expression (p value = 0.0086) (Figure 4A), which was accompanied by a reciprocal decrease in Hsd11b2 expression (p value = 0.0005) (Figure 4B). We further showed that inflammation increased 11βHsd1 protein levels in ENS culture compared to controls (p value = 0.0056) (Figure 4C) and showed a non-significant trend toward decreased 11βHsd2 protein levels in ENS primary cultures (p value = 0.07) (Figure 4D). To determine whether inflammation-induced changes in the 11βHsd1/2 pathway led to increased corticosterone production via reactivation of 11DHC, we quantified corticosterone levels in ENS primary cultures at various time points following 11DHC addition. Corticosterone levels were significantly higher in supernatants from inflamed ENS cultures as compared with controls at 12 (p value = 0.0035) and 24 h (p value = 0.0058) but not at 48 h after 11DHC addition (Figure 4E). To identify putative mechanisms responsible for the lack of ENS response to increased GC production under inflammation, we quantified the expression of two main phosphorylated forms of GR, i.e., phosphorylation at Ser232 (pGR-232) and phosphorylation at Ser246 (pGR-246). Indeed, pGR-232 is associated with high transcriptional activity, whereas pGR-246 is associated with low transcriptional activity.3 Treatment of primary ENS cultures with 11DHC induced an increase in expression of pGR-232 (p value = 0.0006) (Figure 5F) and no changes in pGR-246 expression (p value = 0.1683) (Figure 5G). Under inflammatory conditions, 11DHC not only induced a significant increase in Ser232 phosphorylation (p value = 0.0002) (Figure 5F) but also induced a significant increase in Ser246 phosphorylation (p value = 0.0073) (Figure 5G). The pGR-232/pGR-246 ratio was increased by 11DHC alone (p value < 0.0001), whereas under inflammatory conditions, the pGR-232/pGR-246 ratio was decreased in the presence of 11DHC (p value = 0.0040) (Figure 5H). Together, these results suggest that inflammation could decrease GR transcriptional activity, leading to the absence of the 11DHC effect reported under inflammation as compared with control.

Discussion

Using primary cultures, this study demonstrates that among ENS cells, EGCs appear to be the primary cellular component responsible for producing corticosterone, predominantly through the reactivation of 11DHC rather than de novo steroidogenesis. Moreover, we show that this locally generated corticosterone facilitates neuritogenesis through GR-dependent signaling pathways. Finally, our findings reveal that although inflammatory stimuli enhance active GC synthesis by upregulating both Hsd11b1 mRNA and protein expression, inflammation also alters the phosphorylation profile of GR, leading to a loss of the pro-neuritogenic effects of GCs.

One of the first major findings of our work was the demonstration of the ENS capacity to produce active GCs via reactivation pathways but not by de novo steroidogenesis. Indeed, we demonstrated that the cellular components of primary ENS cultures are not equally involved in this local production. While enteric neurons did not display any capacity for GC production, EGCs exhibited the same ability as primary ENS cultures, suggesting that EGCs are the major cellular component responsible for local GC synthesis in the ENS. Although previous studies have identified the presence of 11βHsd1 in enteric neurons,13 the functional capacity of ENS to convert 11DHC into active GCs (i.e., corticosterone) had not been established. Our data add EGCs to the list of gut-resident cell types, such as IECs, capable of locally producing GCs.2,9,10,25 Moreover, EGCs may serve as a key contributor to local GC production, given that IECs display high 11βHsd2 activity.25 This activity could facilitate the local generation of 11DHC, which can subsequently be reactivated by EGCs to synthesize GCs in situ. Supporting this notion, we found significant levels of 11DHC in colon tissue homogenates. Interestingly, the ability of the ENS and EGCs to synthesize corticosterone in vitro is induced upon exposure to physiologically relevant concentrations of 11DHC, similar to those found in serum or colonic homogenates.

Furthermore, we discovered that GCs locally produced by the ENS, at physiological concentrations, exert biological activity on the ENS cells themselves. This is supported by 3 molecular indicators of GR activation: (1) the upregulation of Fkbp5, Sgk1, and Tsc22d3, three well-established target genes induced by GC-GR binding26; (2) the downregulation of GR itself, a well-established autoregulatory response to sustained GC signaling27; and (3) an increased ratio of GR phosphorylation at serine 232 relative to total GR, a widely recognized molecular marker of GR activation.20

A second key finding of this study is that inflammation modulates GC metabolism within the ENS by regulating the expression of GC-metabolizing enzymes. Specifically, inflammatory stimuli upregulate Hsd11b1 while downregulating Hsd11b2 mRNA levels, creating a putative enzymatic bias favoring GC production. This shift enhances the conversion of 11DHC to corticosterone within the ENS, a finding that we quantitatively validated by mass spectrometry, supporting increased local GC synthesis under inflammatory conditions. These observations are consistent with previous studies showing that proinflammatory cytokines upregulate 11β-HDS1 in immune cells,25 fibroblasts,28 and osteoblasts,29 while downregulating 11βHsd2 in renal epithelial cells.30 In the gut, similar alterations of the 11βHsd1/11βHsd2 balance to the one observed in our study were reported in colonic tissues during dextran sulfate sodium-induced colitis or in patients with inflammatory bowel diseases.31,32 However, regarding the ENS, only fasting has been reported as a stressor modulating 11β-HDS1 expression in enteric neurons,13 but its impact on GC production by the ENS remained unknown.

A third major finding of our study is that ENS-derived active GCs can influence neuronal functions not previously recognized as being GC regulated, most likely via an autocrine signaling pathway. Although the effects of GCs on neuronal structure and plasticity remain controversial, increasing evidence from the CNS indicates that GC actions on neuritogenesis and neuronal connectivity are highly context dependent, varying according to the duration, intensity, and cellular context of exposure.4,21,22,23 Within this framework, our data demonstrate that GC exposure at physiological concentrations promotes neuritogenesis in ENS primary cultures, as evidenced by increased nerve fiber density and elevated expression of key proteins involved in neuronal connectivity. Importantly, these effects were mediated through GR-dependent pathways, as they were abolished by a selective GR antagonist. Similar GC-induced neuritogenic effects have been reported in spinal afferent neurons,33 but to our knowledge, this has never been reported in the ENS. Although the specific GC-responsive genes mediating neuritogenesis in our study remain to be identified, increasing evidence suggests that FKBP5 and SGK1—both upregulated by 11DHC in our experiments—may contribute to this process. Indeed, FKBP5 (FKBP51) modulates GR signaling and has been implicated in neuronal plasticity,34 while SGK1 has been shown to enhance the number of primary neurites in hippocampal neurons.35 The observed increase in neuritogenesis may therefore enhance neuronal network connectivity and activity, potentially contributing to specific functional effects of GCs within the ENS. In addition, we found that GC exposure increased nNOS expression, suggesting that GCs may also modulate neurotransmitter systems in the ENS, beyond their previously described effect on choline acetyltransferase expression.12 While no study has yet addressed GC-mediated regulation of nNOS in the ENS, several reports in the CNS have shown that GCs can either downregulate or upregulate nNOS expression, depending on the cell type and brain region examined.36,37 Collectively, in addition to established neurotrophic factors that regulate enteric neuritogenesis—such as neurotrophin-3, nerve growth factor, and glial cell line-derived neurotrophic factor38,39—our study identifies GCs as a putative novel neurotrophic agent involved in enteric neuronal plasticity.

Importantly, our study further demonstrates that although inflammation enhances 11DHC-mediated GC production within the ENS, its promotive effects on neuritogenesis and nNOS regulation are abolished under inflammatory conditions. This loss of 11DHC responsiveness suggests the development of a selective GC resistance within the ENS, at least on neuritogenesis and nNOS regulation. Mechanistically, our data indicate that changes in the GR phosphorylation profile may contribute to this phenomenon. Specifically, combined exposure to inflammation and 11DHC resulted in a decreased pGR-232/pGR-246 ratio, a parameter closely associated with reduced GR transcriptional activity. This altered phosphorylation balance provides a plausible molecular explanation for the GC resistance observed under inflammatory conditions. While inflammation-induced changes in GR phosphorylation have been reported in other cell types, this phenomenon has not previously been described in ENS cells.

We also observed that 11DHC treatment under inflammatory conditions led to increased mRNA expression of some GC target genes compared with 11DHC alone (Figures S2A and S2B) and to significantly reduced total GR expression (Figure S2C). Whether inflammation-induced GC resistance—similar to that described in other cellular systems—also occurs in the ENS warrants further investigation. Interestingly, GR signaling regulation is an extremely complex process that involves several levels of control, such as phosphorylation, cellular localization, protein stability, or interaction with other transcription factors. In other contexts, inflammation has been shown to impair GR activity through multiple mechanisms, including limited nuclear translocation, altered phosphorylation, and NF-κB activation.40,41 GR is also known to functionally interact with NF-κB and AP-1, two transcription factors critically involved in inflammatory signaling and cellular stress responses.3 GR and NF-κB are known to engage in bidirectional antagonism; while GR represses NF-κB-dependent transcription, NF-κB can in turn inhibit GR function and contribute to GC resistance through diverse mechanisms.42,43,44 Here, we found that inflammatory conditions increased expression of COX-2 and ICAM-1, two known NF-κB target genes used as a proxy for modulation of NF-κB activity, and that this induction was attenuated in the presence of 11DHC (Figures S3A and S3B). Although a functional interference between GR and NF-κB is highlighted here, further experiments should be required to investigate the physical interaction between GR and NF-κB in this context. In fact, the lack of effects of GR activity under inflammatory conditions could result from an inflammation-induced increase in NF-κB, as assessed by the increase in its target genes (Icam1/Cox-2). This increase in NF-κB could then lead to NF-κB and GR interaction that could be (1) responsible for the lack of effects upon connectivity and (2) reduced expression of NF-κB induction of target genes. However, despite the loss of 11DHC responsiveness on neuritogenesis in inflammatory conditions, locally produced GCs by the ENS appear to maintain their anti-inflammatory properties, suggesting once more a selective GC resistance on neuritogenesis pathways.

From a functional perspective, inflammation-induced GC resistance in the ENS might be considered an adaptive response during acute inflammation. Acute inflammation is known to induce time-dependent remodeling of enteric neurons, initially causing neuronal loss and reduction of fiber innervation.45,46 In later stages, neuronal and axonal regeneration mechanisms are activated, supporting the restoration of gut function.47 Consequently, the absence of neuritogenesis observed during acute inflammation in our study aligns with previous reports indicating that regenerative processes are primarily engaged during the later phases of the inflammatory response.48,49 This impaired repair likely reflects a metabolic shift in enteric neurons, favoring survival and anti-inflammatory pathways over those promoting restoration of neuronal connectivity during early inflammation.

In conclusion, this study provides the first evidence that the ENS harbors the functional enzymatic machinery enabling local GC synthesis, which modulates enteric neuritogenesis through autocrine and/or paracrine mechanisms. EGCs represent the principal cellular source of this local steroidogenesis; however, the precise modes of communication between ENS cell types involved in GC signaling remain to be elucidated. In addition, as the present study was conducted using primary ENS cultures, future studies will be required to validate these findings in vivo. In particular, the use of cell type-specific genetic models, such as enteric glia-specific knockout mice targeting key enzymes of GC metabolism, would be essential to confirm the physiological relevance of ENS-derived GCs. Importantly, although inflammatory stimuli enhance local GC production within the ENS, they simultaneously abolish the neurotrophic effects observed under basal conditions. Our data suggest that this paradoxical effect may be explained, at least in part, by inflammation-induced changes in the GR phosphorylation profile. Nevertheless, additional mechanisms could be likely involved, including transrepression of pro-inflammatory transcription factors by GCs, and further studies will be required to fully elucidate the molecular basis of selective GC resistance in the ENS under inflammatory conditions.

Limitations of the study

This study has several limitations that should be acknowledged. First, all experiments were performed using primary ENS cultures derived from rodents, which may limit the direct translatability of our observations to the human ENS. In vivo experiments are required to evaluate the levels of 11DHC and corticosterone in the male colon and to confirm the physiological relevance of our findings within the intestinal environment. In addition, GC signaling is a highly complex pathway that was not explored exhaustively in the present study, and additional GC-regulated genes in the ENS remain to be identified. Finally, our work mainly focused on molecular and structural aspects of GC signaling and did not include direct functional analyses of ENS activity. Complementary approaches such as calcium imaging or electrophysiological recordings would therefore be valuable to better determine how locally produced GCs influence enteric neuronal and glial function.

Experimental model and study participant details

Animals

Rat colonic biopsies and plasma were recovered from 3-months-old Sprague-Dawley female rats (Janvier Labs, Le Genest-Saint-Isle, France). These were breeding rats, intended for sacrifice, which had not received any special treatment. They were individually housed under controlled temperature on a 12:12-h light-dark cycle with access to water and standard laboratory chow ad libitum. The rats were anesthetized with isoflurane and then euthanized between 9a.m. and 11a.m. All animal experimental protocols were approved by the Pays de la Loire Ethical Committee and were performed in accordance with the French law on animal welfare, EU Directive 2010/63/EU for animal experiments.

Primary cell cultures

Primary cultures of rat ENS (mixed culture with muscle, glia, and neuronal cells) were generated and cultured as previously described.50 Briefly, the intestines of male and female rat embryos at E15 (Sprague–Dawley rat, Janvier Labs, Le Genest-St-Isle, France) were finely dissected and digested at 37°C for 15 min in DMEM-F12 (ref. 31330-038, Gibco, Life Technologies) with 0.1% (w/v) trypsin (Ref. T1426, Sigma-Aldrich, Paris, France). The trypsin reaction was stopped by a medium containing 10% (v/v) fetal bovine serum (Ref. F2442, Sigma-Aldrich) and then treated with 0.01% (w/v) DNAse I (Ref. DN25, Sigma-Aldrich) for 10 min at 37°C. After trituration, cells were centrifuged at 750 rpm for 10 min, and seeded in 24-well plates previously coated for 30 min at 37°C with a solution of 0.5% (w/v) gelatin (Ref. G6144, Sigma-Aldrich). After 24 h, the medium was replaced with a serum-free DMEM + Ham’s F-12 Nutrient Mix medium (v/v) containing 1% of N2 supplement (ref. 17502048, Gibco) and 2 mmol L-1 glutamine (Gibco),100 IU/mL penicillin, and 100 μg/mL streptomycin (ref. 17502-048, Life Technologies).

Enteric neuron primary cultures of rat ENS were generated and cultured as previously described.51 Briefly, the intestines of male and female rat embryos at E15 (Sprague–Dawley rat, Janvier Laboratories SA, Le Genest-St-Isle, France) were individually placed in a drop of cold HBSS in a Petri dish and cut into several explants. Explants were placed in a plate previously coated with poly-L-lysine 0.1 mg/mL (Ref. P5899, Sigma-Aldrich) and covered it with a glia-conditioned culture medium with GDNF 50 ng/mL (ref. 512-GF-050/CF, R&D Systems). After 5 days of culture, many cells emerged from the explants and covered a large part of the well. We removed the explants using a P1000 pipette, and detached the cells with 300 μL of Accutase per well for 5 min. Accutase was inhibited by adding 4.7 mL of DMEM + FBS and cell suspensions were centrifuged at 1,500 rpm for 5 min at room temperature. The cell pellet was suspended in conditioned-glia medium + GDNF (50 ng/mL) and seeded either in a P24 plate, previously coated with poly-L-lysine 0.1 mg/mL. At day 6, an antimitotic (cytosine arabinoside: AraC, 5 μM) was added to the medium to eliminate the remaining glial and muscle cells. Purified neurons were used on day 14 and stopped on day 16.

Enteric glial cell (EGC) primary cultures were prepared from the colonic myenteric plexus of 12-week-old female Sprague-Dawley rats (Janvier Laboratories, Le Genest-St-Isle, France) as previously described.52 Cultures of glia were characterized by immunocytochemistry and only used if 95% of the cells were GFAP+ and/or S100β+.

ENS and neuron primary cultures were generated from a mix of male and female embryos. The potential sex influence in cultures was not investigated since we were not able to sex embryos before the beginning of the culture. Further, all culture supernatants were regularly tested for mycoplasma contamination.

Method details

Pharmacological treatment

After 14 days in vitro (DIV14), primary ENS cultures were exposed to inflammatory cytokines. TNF-α (Ref. T5944, Sigma-Aldrich) and interleukin-1β (ref. 400-01B-10UG, PeproTech) were dissolved in water, pre-diluted in culture medium, and added to the cells at a final concentration of 1 ng/mL each. After 11h30, cultures were treated with the GR antagonist CORT108297 (kindly provided by Dr. Azel Hunt, Corcept, Menlo Park, CA, USA) and 30 minutes later, with the 11DHC (Ref. TRC-D230115, LGC). Both CORT108297 and 11DHC were dissolved in ethanol to prepare concentrated stock solutions, then diluted in culture medium to obtain intermediate working solutions of 100 μmol/L (CORT108297) and 50 μmol/L (11DHC). For treatments, 10 μL of each working solution was added to 1 mL of culture medium per well, yielding final concentrations of 1 μmol/L for CORT108297 and 500 nmol/L for 11DHC. The use of TNF-α in combination with IL-1β was selected based on preliminary experiments testing different cytokines cocktails for their ability to modulate 11βHsd1 expression (Figure S4). The concentration used of CORT108297 was determined from a dose–response analysis evaluating FKBP5 (FK506 binding protein 5) and SGK1 (serum- and glucocorticoid-inducible kinase) expression 24 h after corticosterone exposure (Figure S5).

At 14 h, 24 h, and 48 h after 11DHC addition, 150 μL of culture supernatant was collected for steroid quantification. After 24h or 48 h of 11DHC treatment, cultures were harvested either with RIPA buffer for protein extraction or with RA1 buffer for RNA preservation. All samples were stored at −80°C. Water Soluble Cholesterol (Ref C4951, Sigma-Aldrich) was used, dissolved directly in the culture medium at the concentration of 17.5 μmol/L, according to bibliography.53

For 11βHsd1 inhibition, we used 10J (ref. 385581, Sigma-Aldrich) 12h before the 11DHC addition. 10J was dissolved in ethanol, then pre-diluted in culture medium, and added to the cells at a final concentration of 1 μM.

Quantitative PCR analysis

For colonic tissues stored in RA1 buffer, RNA extraction was performed with Nucleospin RNA/Protein Kit (ref. 740933, Macherey-Nagel, Hoerdt, France) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. One μg purified mRNA was denatured and processed for reverse transcription using Superscript III reverse transcriptase (ref. 18080044, Thermo Fisher Scientific, Saint-Herblain, France). PCR amplifications were performed using the Fast SYBR green master mix (ref. 4385614, Applied Biosystem) and run on a StepOnePlus system (ref. 4376600, Life Technologies). The primers used are listed in the key resources table.

The PCR signal was analyzed on StepOnePlus Software v2.3 and was normalized against RPS6 as reference gene to control for variability in the amount and quality of the RNA. The data are presented in arbitrary units and were calculated using the 2ˆ-ddCT method.

LC-MS/MS quantification

Corticosterone and 11DHC were measured simultaneously by LC-MS/MS. The analyses were carried out on a Waters Xevo TQ-XS with positive ion electrospray ionization, using a Waters Acquity UPLC system. Analytes were extracted from cell culture supernatant (150 μL) or from rat plasma (150 μL) via liquid-liquid extraction (n-Hexane/Ethyle acetate 80/20 (v/v)) with d8-corticosterone at 2.5 μM (LGC, Ref. TRC-C695702) included as internal standards. Analytes were separated at 40 °C on a C18 column (Waters, Acquity BEH C18 1.7 μm VanGuard) using a solvent gradient composed of two phases, an aqueous phase (H2O, 0.1% formic acid, 2 mM ammonium acetate) and an organic phase (methanol, 0.1% formic acid, 2 mM ammonium acetate) with a gradient run of 11 minutes and 0.3 mL/min of flow rate. Mass spectral conditions are detailed in Table S1. Pics were analyzed with Masslynx software (Waters SAS, Saint-Quentin-en-Yveline, France).

Western blot

Enteric nervous system and highly pure enteric neuron cultures were lysed in RIPA buffer (Millipore, 20–188), completed by phosphatase (Ref. P0044, Sigma-Aldrich, 1% v/v) and protease (ref. 11873580001, Roche, 1 tablet for 40 mL) inhibitors. Then the samples were sonicated (Bioruptor Plus sonication device, Hologic Diagenode) as follows: samples were thawed on ice and sonicated at 20–30% amplitude for 3 cycles of 5–10 s, with 20–30 s pauses at 4°C followed by centrifugation at 1,000 rpm. B0002, Thermo Fisher Scientific) running buffer, before electrophoretic transfer to nitrocellulose membranes using the iBlot2 Dry Blotting System (Thermo Fisher Scientific). Membranes were then blocked for 2h at room temperature in TBS with 0.1% (v/v) Tween 20 and 5% (w/v) non-fat dry milk and incubated overnight at 4°C with primary antibodies listed in the key resources table. All primary antibodies were diluted at 1:1,000 except for anti-β-III tubulin (1:5,000), anti-11βHsd1 (1:500), and anti-actin (1:10,000). As pGR-232 and pGR-246 migrate at the same molecular weight; there was revealed on two different blots. The immunoreactive bands were imaged using a Chemidoc imaging system (Bio-Rad Chemidoc XRS Gel Imaging System) and Image Lab software V6.1.0 (Bio-Rad).

Immunohistochemical staining protocol

At DIV5, ENS rat primary culture were treated with TNF-α and interleukin 1-β and 11DHC was added in the medium at DIV6. At DIV8, 48h after 11DHC addition, cells were fixed in 0.1M PBS containing 4% paraformaldehyde at room temperature for 15 min, permeabilized with PBS containing 0.25% Triton X-100 for 5 min at room temperature and saturated with PBS containing 10% BSA for 30 min. Cells were then incubated with the following primary antibodies: rabbit monoclonal anti-beta III tubulin [EP1569Y] (1:5,000, Ref. ab52623, Abcam), human anti-Hu (gift from the CHU of Nantes; 1:5,000) diluted in PBS containing 3% BSA over night at 4°C. After washing with PBS, cells were incubated for 2h at room temperature with the appropriate secondary antibodies, respectively, donkey anti-rabbit CY3 (1:500, Ref. JIR711-165-152, Jackson Immuno Research) and anti-human FP488 (1:500, Ref. FP-SA3110, Interchim). Nuclei were stained with 4′ 6-Diamidino-2- phenylindole dihydrochloride (DAPI, 1:10,000, D9542, Sigma-Aldrich).

Image analysis and quantification

Images from immunostained primary ENS culture were acquired using a digital camera (Axio Observer, Carl Zeiss, United States). For each well, images were collected from 10 randomly selected, non-overlapping microscopic fields of view distributed across the entire well. The images were analyzed using Fiji software (version 1.53c). The Threshold tool was applied to highlight all areas showing positive β-III tubulin staining, followed by the Measure function to quantify the surface area exhibiting positive staining.

Quantification and statistical analysis

GraphPad Prism software version 10.2 (GraphPad Software Inc., La Jolla, CA, USA) was used for statistical analysis. Data were represented as means ± SD and individual values. Group comparisons were made using the Mann–Whitney U test or by two-way ANOVA and Dunnett’s multiple comparisons test as indicated. Values of p < 0.05 were considered statistically significant.
