Early life high-fat diet exposure modulates adult metabolic phenotypes in Drosophila
Authors: Prachi Mishra, Gaurav Yadav, Shrishti Mitra, Raunak Gupta, Ranjan Kumar Sahu, Rogers Koboji, Kashyap Kumar Dubey, Ranjan Kumar Nanda, Vijendra Sharma, Pawel Michalak, Dau Dayal Aggarwal
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
SummaryEarly-life exposure to dietary excess of lipids increases lifelong vulnerability to metabolic dysfunction, but the determinants of this susceptibility remain unclear. Here, we combine paired-tissue transcriptomics (whole head and fat body), metabolomics, and gut microbiota profiling in Drosophila melanogaster to define how transient developmental exposure to a high-fat diet (HFD) remodels adult metabolism. Larval HFD exposure was associated with extensive transcriptional remodeling in the adult fat body, including coordinated repression of tricarboxylic acid (TCA) cycle and oxidative phosphorylation genes, accompanied by increased ROS accumulation and reduced mitochondrial bioenergetic function. In contrast, the whole-head transcriptome showed a weaker response to HFD than the fat body. Integrated host-microbiome profiling revealed persistent remodeling of the gut microbial community, with altered bacterial diversity and structure. Targeted adult interventions with the NAD+ precursor nicotinamide riboside, the TCA intermediate α-ketoglutarate, or commensal bacteria (Acetobacter and Lactobacillus spp.) restored mitochondrial redox balance, improved respiratory capacity, and extended lifespan. Together, these findings identify mitochondrial dysfunction and microbial remodeling as enduring features of developmental dietary lipid stress and suggest that this metabolic state remains partially reversible through mitochondrial and microbiota-directed interventions.

Introduction

Early-life nutrition is a critical determinant of lifelong metabolic health, establishing trajectories that predispose individuals to obesity, diabetes, and cardiometabolic disease.1 High-fat diets (HFDs) are potent metabolic stressors that induce lipid overload, insulin resistance,2 chronic inflammation, and impaired energy homeostasis across various species.3,4 While the detrimental effects of adult HFD exposure are well documented, the mechanisms by which transient lipid overload during development reprograms nutrient-sensing, bioenergetic, and redox pathways to produce lasting metabolic phenotypes remain poorly understood. Defining these persistent effects is essential for elucidating the developmental origins of metabolic vulnerability and identifying strategies to restore metabolic resilience.

Evidence from rodent models indicates that sustained HFD feeding induces adiposity and metabolic inflammation,5 coupled with gut microbial remodeling, altered short-chain fatty acid (SCFA) production,6 and accumulation of pro-inflammatory metabolites such as trimethylamine N-oxide.7 However, although rodent HFD models are physiologically informative, prolonged dietary exposure can make it difficult to separate primary developmental effects from later obesity-associated changes and ongoing dietary stress. Models that restrict lipid exposure to defined developmental windows and assess adult outcomes in a common dietary environment are therefore valuable for identifying persistent carryover effects of early nutritional stress. In line with the Developmental Origins of Health and Disease (DOHaD) paradigm,8,9 there is a pressing need for experimentally tractable model systems to decode the genetic and metabolic architecture of early nutritional imprinting.

The fruit fly Drosophila melanogaster provides a powerful platform for dissecting dietary programming due to its short lifespan, conserved metabolic pathways, tractable genetics, and well-established diet-manipulation protocols.10,11 Under HFD conditions, flies exhibit conserved features of metabolic syndrome, including lipid accumulation in the fat body, insulin resistance,1,11,12 cardiac dysfunction, and reduced lifespan.13 HFD-induced oxidative stress and mitochondrial dysfunction in flies parallel obesity-associated metabolic decline in mammals.14 Most prior studies, however, have focused on isolated traits or single tissues, leaving the systemic and cross-tissue consequences of developmental lipid exposure largely unexplored. Although a limited number of studies have examined how juvenile dietary conditions influence adult transcriptional and metabolomic profiles in Drosophila,15,16,17 the systemic and tissue-specific consequences of developmental lipid excess remain poorly defined, highlighting the need to determine how early-life diets shape long-term metabolic trajectories.

Given that microbiota-based interventions are under active exploration for the treatment of human metabolic disorders,18 understanding how developmental HFD reshapes host-microbe interactions is essential. In Drosophila, diet is a major determinant of gut microbial community composition,19 and recurrent commensal genera such as Acetobacter and Lactobacillus have been linked to host nutrient sensing, lipid metabolism, intestinal physiology, and immune signaling.20,21 Prior studies further indicate that gut-associated bacteria and microbial products can modulate host feeding behavior,22 stress responses, gut-brain signaling,23 and immune-metabolic pathways.24 Although Acetobacter and Lactobacillus have been linked to diet-dependent JAK/STAT and insulin signaling,25 and gut Acetobacter can exert systemic effects on Drosophila reproduction and embryonic development,26 whether developmental lipid excess persistently remodels the gut microbiota and whether targeted bacterial supplementation can improve HFD-associated host metabolic phenotypes remains unexplored.

Here, we integrate paired-tissue transcriptomics (whole head and fat body), fat-body metabolomics, host-microbiome profiling, and targeted supplementation to test how transient larval HFD exposure shapes adult metabolic physiology after return to a normal diet. Our findings show that developmental lipid stress produces a durable adult metabolic phenotype marked by altered fat-body transcriptional and bioenergetic readouts, microbial community remodeling, persistent redox and physiological alterations, and shortened lifespan, several of which are partially ameliorated by nicotinamide riboside, α-ketoglutarate, or bacterial supplementation.

Results

Larval high-fat diet exposure produces persistent tissue-specific transcriptional remodeling in adults

Higher triglyceride accumulation in adults derived from HFD-fed larvae (Figure S1) prompted transcriptomic profiling of whole head and fat body tissues. Using a paired-tissue design, whole-head and fat-body samples were obtained from the same adult flies within each biological replicate and processed separately for RNA-seq. Sequencing depth, quality-control metrics, and alignment summaries for each RNA-seq library are provided in Table S1. Principal component analysis (PCA) of RNA-seq data revealed diet-dependent clustering in both head (PC1 = 42.8%, PC2 = 26.1%; Figure 1A) and fat body (PC1 = 38%, PC2 = 23.4%; Figure 1B). The head dataset was interpreted as a whole-head profile rather than a pure brain-specific signal; therefore, non-neuronal cephalic tissues, including head-associated fat-body cells, may dilute tissue-specific HFD responses. Pathway enrichment showed that head DEGs were enriched in fatty-acid, amino acid, and beta-alanine metabolism (Figure 1E). In contrast, fat-body DEGs displayed pronounced repression of glycolysis, the tricarboxylic acid (TCA) cycle, and pyruvate metabolism (Figure 1F), indicating compromised mitochondrial function and reduced energy turnover. Importantly, the metabolic pathways most strongly repressed in the fat body were not comparably altered in the whole-head dataset, suggesting that head-associated fat-body cells alone are unlikely to account for the observed head expression profile. Network analysis identified hub genes (Acaa, Echs1) that mediate fatty acid β-oxidation and mitochondrial activity across taxa (fly and human), highlighting a conserved transcriptional architecture that underlies lipid-driven metabolic remodeling (Table S5).

Weighted gene co-expression network analysis (WGCNA) identified co-expression modules enriched for mitochondrial, immune, and lipid-associated gene programs (Figure 2A; Table S6). To assess whether module eigengenes were associated with diet rather than tissue identity alone, we correlated them across all 12 samples with dietary regime (ND versus HFD) and tissue identity (head versus fat body). This analysis showed that the blue module was significantly associated with dietary regime (r = −0.65, p = 0.02; Figure 2B), indicating that this module captures a diet-associated mitochondrial and bioenergetic co-expression program. By contrast, the turquoise module, which was enriched for immune- and lipid-associated genes, showed a strong association with tissue identity (r = 0.98, p = 2.5 × 10−8), suggesting that its co-expression structure primarily reflects a tissue-dependent expression pattern rather than a diet-specific transcriptional response. The brown module also showed a significant association with tissue identity (r = −0.69, p = 0.01). Module-module eigengene correlation analysis showed that WGCNA modules were organized into partially coordinated transcriptional blocks rather than a single uniform response (Figure 2C). The green, magenta, brown, and black modules showed broadly positive eigengene correlations with one another, whereas the green-yellow and red modules formed a smaller positively correlated group. To determine how tissue-specific HFD-responsive DEGs were represented within the co-expression network, we overlaid tissue-specific DEG lists onto WGCNA modules and tested DEG enrichment within each module using Fisher’s exact test, followed by BH correction for multiple testing (Figure 2D). Fat-body DEGs were strongly enriched within the blue module (1,074 of 1,501 matched DEGs; OR = 33.14, FDR-adjusted p < 0.001), inferring that this module captures a major component of the fat-body transcriptional response to HFD at the network level. Head DEGs were also significantly enriched in the turquoise module (OR = 2.32, p = 0.001). However, the effect size was substantially smaller than that observed for the blue module, consistent with the more limited number of HFD-responsive genes detected in the head.

Functional enrichment of WGCNA modules further resolved distinct biological programs within the network. The blue module was enriched for mitochondrial and bioenergetic processes, including the TCA cycle, oxidative phosphorylation, mitochondrial function, and ATP metabolism, whereas the red module was enriched for mitochondrial translation, protein modification, ubiquitination, chromatin organization, and signaling-related processes. In contrast, the turquoise module was enriched for immune and lipid-associated processes, including amino acid metabolism, immune responses, lipid transport, lipid storage, and ceramide metabolism (Figure 2E; Table S6). Overall, WGCNA identified the blue module as the principal diet-associated, fat-body DEG-enriched module, with functional enrichment for mitochondrial and bioenergetic genes (Figure 2F). However, the turquoise module primarily reflected tissue context and was enriched for immune-, lipid-, amino acid-, and stress-related functions. These network-level patterns support the interpretation that developmental HFD exposure is associated with persistent mitochondrial transcriptional repression in the adult fat body, accompanied by broader immune- and lipid-related transcriptional remodeling.

Larval high-fat diet represses mitochondrial energy-producing pathways in adult fat body

KEGG enrichment of fat body DEGs showed reduced expression of several energy-producing pathways, including the TCA cycle and oxidative phosphorylation, consistent with the mitochondria-enriched modules identified by co-expression analysis. In the fat body, key TCA enzymes (mAcon2, Fum2, Fum4, SdhCL) and oxidative phosphorylation components (Figures 3A and 3B), including NADH dehydrogenase (ND-20L, ND-51L2), cytochrome c oxidase (COX4L, COX6AL2, COX7CL), and ATP synthase subunits (ATPsynεL, ATPsynGL), were markedly downregulated, suggesting impaired electron transport and ATP generation. Untargeted LC-MS metabolomics revealed a clear metabolic separation between the ND and HFD groups (PC1 = 48.7%, PC2 = 21.5%; Figure S2A). Differential metabolite analysis identified altered metabolite abundances between diet groups (Figure S2B), including decreased isocitric acid, a TCA cycle intermediate, and elevated reduced glutathione, a central redox-buffering metabolite (Figure S2C). Functional assays further supported altered mitochondrial-associated physiology. HFD-derived adults showed an increased NADH/NAD+ ratio, elevated NBT reduction, higher mitochondrial swelling, and reduced respiratory output relative to ND ones, consistent with altered redox balance and mitochondrial membrane integrity (Figures 3C–3F). TMRE staining revealed lower mitochondrial membrane potential-dependent fluorescence in HFD-derived fat bodies, and quantification of corrected fluorescence intensity confirmed a significant reduction under HFD conditions (ND = 1.00, HFD = 0.687; Welch’s two-tailed t test, p = 0.0012; Figure 3G). ATP quantification also showed reduced ATP levels under HFD compared with ND controls (Figure 3H). qRT-PCR validation of mitochondrial and lipid-metabolism genes (Mtpβ, Mcad, Acox1, mAcon2, Fum2, and Vha16-5; Table S7; Figure S3) was performed using independently collected fat-body samples (separate from those used for RNA-seq), and confirmed the direction of the DEG responses observed in the transcriptomic data. These assays were performed in early adulthood (3 days post-eclosion) to assess the persistent effects of larval HFD exposure after return to a normal diet, i.e., before substantial age-related mitochondrial decline.

Cross-species comparison identifies limited gene-level concordance but recurring pathway-level overlap

We next compared the larval HFD-responsive adult fat-body transcriptome of Drosophila with published human transcriptomic datasets related to obesity and fatty liver disease27,28,29,30,31,32,33 using high-confidence fly-human orthologs (Figure S4A). Gene-level directional concordance was generally limited across datasets, with most comparisons falling near the 50% null expectation or showing broad confidence intervals (Figure S4B). This limited concordance is expected given the evolutionary distance between flies and humans, incomplete one-to-one orthology, differences in tissue composition, and the heterogeneity of human obesity and fatty-liver datasets compared with a controlled Drosophila dietary model. Although the Fisk et al. (2022) and Darci-Maher et al. (2023) datasets showed nominal directional concordance, these signals did not remain significant after a multiple-testing correction, indicating limited evidence for broad gene-by-gene conservation of HFD-responsive expression changes.

At the pathway level, GO-Slim comparison suggested recurrent representation of nutrient- and organelle-associated biological processes, including amino acid metabolism, vitamin metabolism, mitochondrial gene expression, and glycoprotein biosynthesis (Figure S4C). Thus, although directional concordance at the individual-gene level was limited, this analysis provides an exploratory pathway-level context for broad biological themes related to nutrient handling, mitochondrial activity, and protein-processing pathways across fly and human metabolic disease datasets.

Nicotinamide riboside and α-ketoglutarate partially improve redox balance, mitochondrial function, and longevity

To determine whether metabolic cofactor supplementation could mitigate HFD-associated mitochondrial and physiological defects, HFD-derived groups were supplemented with nicotinamide riboside (NR) or α-ketoglutarate (α-KG) either during larval HFD exposure followed by adult maintenance on ND (L→A) or only during adulthood for flies derived from HFD-fed larvae (A→A). Flies were then assessed across mitochondrial-associated and physiological outcomes.

NR supplementation partially improved mitochondrial-associated readouts, including partial normalization of the NADH/NAD+ ratio by ∼8–18%, improvement in mitochondrial membrane potential by ∼7–15%, reduction of ROS accumulation by ∼10–11%, increase in respiratory-associated output by ∼8–12% and increase in ATP levels by ∼6–13% relative to un-supplemented HFD-derived groups (nested ANOVA, p < 0.05; Figures 4A–4E). These effects were more pronounced after prolonged adult supplementation (Figures 4F–4J). α-KG supplementation produced complementary improvement, enhancing redox balance and improving mitochondrial-associated readouts (p < 0.05; Figures 4K–4T). qRT-PCR analysis of fat body tissue showed that NR, but not α-KG, partially normalized spargel expression in HFD-exposed adults, suggesting that NAD+ replenishment may influence mitochondrial regulatory gene expression. In contrast, α-KG more strongly induced Sdh expression, consistent with a possible effect on the α-KG-succinate-Complex II segment of the TCA cycle (Figure S5). These transcriptional changes paralleled the functional data, with both interventions improving the NADH/NAD+ ratio, reducing ROS, stabilizing mitochondrial integrity, and restoring CO2 output compared with HFD alone. Furthermore, phenotypic assays demonstrated that both cofactors improved locomotor capacity and extended lifespan (∼8–13%) under sustained supplementation (p < 0.05; Figures 4U–4X). Larval high-fat diet exposure is associated with persistent adult gut microbial remodeling and altered predicted metabolic potential

16S rRNA (V3–V4) sequencing of adult D. melanogaster revealed 269 high-quality amplicon sequence variants (ASVs), exposing substantial diet-associated remodeling of microbial community composition. HFD-fed flies exhibited reduced α-diversity (Shannon, Simpson) and clear β-diversity separation (Bray-Curtis, Jaccard), indicative of substantial community reorganization (Figures 5A–5D). Specifically, Shannon diversity decreased from 4.83 ± 0.38 in ND to 3.91 ± 0.21 in HFD, while Simpson diversity decreased from 0.93 ± 0.02 in ND to 0.84 ± 0.01 in HFD (Kruskal-Wallis test, H = 3.86, p = 0.0495 for both indices). In contrast, beta-diversity separation did not reach statistical significance by PERMANOVA. Although Proteobacteria remained dominant, their abundance declined under HFD, accompanied by increased representation of Firmicutes, Bacteroidetes, and Actinobacteria. At finer taxonomic resolution, Alphaproteobacteria and Gammaproteobacteria predominated in ND flies, whereas Bacilli (Firmicutes), Flavobacteriia, Cytophagia, and Sphingobacteria (Bacteroidetes) expanded under HFD (Figure S6), reflecting altered microbial community structure. Notably, the key commensals Lactobacillus and Acetobacter spp. displayed altered abundances between ND and HFD flies (Figure 5E). PICRUSt2 predicted enrichment of amino acid biosynthesis pathways, including L-serine, tryptophan, threonine, and lysine, and cofactor-related pathways, including thiamine, choline, and nitrogen metabolism, under the HFD regime (Figure 5F), suggesting plausible shifts in microbial metabolic potential related to anabolic and nutrient-processing pathways. Discussion

Our study investigates whether transient lipid excess during development leaves a lasting metabolic signature in adulthood and whether nutritional or microbial interventions can partially improve its adult consequences in Drosophila. We show that larval exposure to a high-fat diet is associated with an adult metabolic state characterized by reduced expression of TCA-cycle and oxidative phosphorylation genes in the fat body, altered fat-body lipid metabolism and β-oxidation gene expression, elevated oxidative stress, increased mitochondrial swelling, altered NADH/NAD+ balance, reduced respiratory-associated output, and changes in gut microbial community structure. Because HFD exposure was confined to larval development and adults were subsequently maintained on a normal diet, we interpret these adult effects as persistent carry-over consequences of early-life lipid exposure rather than as effects of ongoing HFD feeding. Together with the partial improvement of HFD-associated phenotypes by nicotinamide riboside, α-ketoglutarate, and bacterial supplementation, these findings support the concept that early-life lipid overload establishes a durable but partially reversible adult metabolic state.

Developmental lipid exposure remodels adult mitochondrial and metabolic physiology

We tested whether transient lipid excess during development durably alters mitochondrial-associated physiology and metabolic transcriptional architecture in adult Drosophila. Larval exposure to a high-fat diet triggered extensive transcriptional remodeling in the adult fat body, marked by altered expression of fatty acid degradation and β-oxidation genes, along with marked repression of the tricarboxylic acid (TCA) cycle and oxidative phosphorylation pathways. This reciprocal pattern suggests a shift toward lipid-associated metabolic remodeling, accompanied by reduced expression of core mitochondrial energy-producing programs, a feature reminiscent of metabolic inflexibility described in obese mammals.34,35 Key TCA enzymes (mAcon2, SdhAL, SdhCL) and electron transport chain components (ND-51L1, COX4L) were markedly downregulated, consistent with reduced mitochondrial bioenergetic gene expression.36 To determine whether these changes reflected network-level transcriptional organization, we applied weighted gene co-expression network analysis. WGCNA identified a principal diet-associated blue module that was strongly enriched for fat-body DEGs and mitochondrial/bioenergetic genes, including genes related to the TCA cycle, oxidative phosphorylation, mitochondrial function, and ATP metabolism. Beyond the diet-associated blue module, other modules captured immune-, lipid-, amino acid-, mitochondrial translation-, protein-modification-, and nuclear regulatory programs, reflecting the broader co-expression architecture across the head and fat-body transcriptomes. Notably, the turquoise and brown modules were more strongly associated with tissue identity than with diet and are therefore interpreted as tissue-contextual network programs rather than as direct diet-responsive modules. Together, these transcriptional networks support broad attenuation of bioenergetic programs, accompanied by immune- and lipid-associated remodeling, rather than the disruption of a single isolated pathway, consistent with multi-level metabolic suppression observed in higher organisms.37

We next asked whether functional metabolic alterations accompanied these transcriptional signatures. Metabolomic analysis revealed lower isocitric acid abundance and increased reduced glutathione abundance, consistent with altered availability of TCA cycle intermediates and engagement of redox-buffering pathways. In parallel, elevated ROS/NBT signals and increased mitochondrial swelling supported oxidative stress and altered mitochondrial membrane integrity.38 Upregulation of β-oxidation-associated genes in the fat body, including Mtpα, Mtpβ, and CROT, further suggests increased engagement of lipid catabolic pathways under HFD conditions.39 Altered expression of Atg8b and fzo points to transcriptional changes in mitochondrial quality-control-related pathways; however, whether these changes alter mitophagic flux or mitochondrial clearance remains to be experimentally determined. At the organismal level, reduced respiratory-associated output and impaired climbing ability indicated that developmental HFD exposure has persistent physiological consequences beyond the transcriptome, consistent with reduced energy availability and altered mitochondrial-associated function. The adult whole-head transcriptome showed a comparatively limited HFD response, with selective changes in β-oxidation and related metabolic genes, including Mtpα, Mtpβ, Acaa, and Echs1. Because whole-head RNA-seq includes neuronal and non-neuronal tissues, these changes should not be interpreted as a purely neuronal response. Nevertheless, the stronger repression of mitochondrial energy-producing pathways in the fat body than in the head supports tissue-specific sensitivity to developmental lipid excess.

Cross-species pathway overlaps in nutrient and metabolic remodeling

Cross-species analysis showed that the fly HFD response overlaps with human metabolic disease datasets at the pathway level, rather than at the level of individual orthologous genes. Limited concordance at the gene level suggests that HFD-responsive transcriptional changes are not broadly conserved gene by gene, likely reflecting differences in orthology, tissue context, disease heterogeneity, and species-specific regulation.40,41 However, the recurrent occurrence of terms related to nutrient- and organelle-associated processes, including amino acid metabolism, mitochondrial gene expression, glycoprotein biosynthesis, β-oxidation-related functions, and lipid homeostasis, suggests pathway-level convergence on broad themes of metabolic stress despite limited gene-level directional concordance. Thus, the fly model should be interpreted as a tractable system for examining pathway-level features of lipid-induced metabolic remodeling, rather than as a direct gene-level surrogate for human obesity.

Gut microbial remodeling is associated with nutritional stress and host metabolic physiology

Early-life HFD exposure was associated with persistent remodeling of the adult gut microbial community in flies derived from HFD-exposed larvae. Microbiota profiling revealed altered α-diversity and diet-associated shifts in community structure, including increased representation of Firmicutes, Bacteroidetes, and Actinobacteria, together with reduced Proteobacteria. These changes indicate that developmental lipid excess leaves a detectable microbial signature in adulthood, even after return to a normal diet, suggesting that early nutritional stress can shape host-associated microbial ecology beyond the period of direct dietary exposure. The reduction in Proteobacteria may reflect diet-dependent microbial filtering or competitive interactions among gut-associated taxa, whereas Firmicutes, largely represented by Lactobacillaceae in the dipteran gut, may influence host lipid metabolism through organic acid production, epithelial turnover, or modulation of nutrient availability.21,42 Such community-level restructuring is biologically relevant because gut bacteria are recognized to influence host redox balance, immune tone, nutrient processing, and energy metabolism,19,43 and several related host readouts were altered in HFD-derived adults in our study. Together, these findings support persistent, diet-associated restructuring of the adult gut microbial community that parallels host metabolic changes, suggesting a potential microbiota-linked layer of developmental lipid exposure-associated adult metabolic physiology that requires future resolution of the relevant microbial metabolites and host pathways.

Consistent with this taxonomic restructuring, predictive functional modeling indicated diet-associated shifts in microbial pathways involved in amino acid, lipid, cofactor, and energy metabolism, including pathways linked to oxidative-stress defense and redox processes. These predicted changes suggest that developmental HFD exposure is associated not only with altered microbial composition but also with altered functional potential of the adult gut microbial community. Notably, these microbial signatures occurred alongside host molecular and physiological changes, including immune-enriched WGCNA modules, altered NADH/NAD+ balance, increased ROS, and reduced expression of mitochondrial pathway genes. Given the established role of microbial immunogens in activating IMD/NF-κB-dependent immune pathways in Drosophila44,45 and the contribution of microbial metabolites to inflammatory and metabolic regulation,46,47 these findings suggest a potential link between HFD-associated microbial remodeling and gut-immune-metabolic crosstalk following developmental lipid exposure; however, the relevant microbial metabolites and host pathways were not directly examined in the present study. Collectively, early-life lipid excess is associated with changes in the microbial community that occur alongside several host metabolic alterations, while the specific microbial metabolites and host pathways linking these changes remain to be defined.

Dietary cofactors and commensal bacteria partially improve HFD-associated dysfunction

We next asked whether targeted metabolic supplementation could mitigate HFD-associated mitochondrial dysfunction, energy imbalance, and shortened lifespan. Nicotinamide riboside (NR) provides a relevant intervention because it enters NAD+ biosynthesis through nicotinamide riboside kinase-mediated conversion to nicotinamide mononucleotide and has been shown to increase intracellular and mitochondrial NAD+ availability.48,49 Increased NAD+ availability can support sirtuin-associated deacetylation programs, including SIRT1-and SIRT3-linked regulation of oxidative metabolism, mitochondrial gene expression, and stress resistance.50 Prior studies in HFD-fed mice and mitochondrial disease models have linked NR-mediated NAD+ repletion to improved energy expenditure, insulin sensitivity, mitochondrial gene and protein markers, oxidative phosphorylation-associated programs, and disease-related phenotypes.51,52,53,54 Complementing these mammalian studies, recent Drosophila work using the NADH-consuming enzyme LbNOX supports a role for NADH/NAD+ redox balance in stress resistance, muscle function, lifespan, and age-associated physiology, with NR feeding increasing NAD+ levels and reducing the NADH/NAD+ ratio.55,56 In line with this framework, NR supplementation in HFD-derived flies partially improved NADH/NAD+ redox balance, mitochondrial-associated functional readouts, locomotor performance, and survival. Partial normalization of spargel expression further suggests engagement of mitochondrial regulatory programs. Together, these data support functional improvement in redox- and mitochondrial-associated physiology, with SIRT-linked pathways providing a plausible mechanistic context50,51 rather than a directly tested mechanism in the present study. The stronger response to prolonged adult supplementation suggests that sustained availability of NAD+ precursor is important for maintaining bioenergetic improvement in adults with persistent effects of developmental HFD exposure.

We next examined whether α-ketoglutarate (α-KG), a TCA-cycle intermediate, could provide complementary metabolic improvement. α-KG supplementation improved redox balance, ATP levels, and respiratory-associated output, consistent with prior reports linking α-KG to metabolic and lifespan-related phenotypes.57 Beyond its role in the TCA cycle, α-KG has been linked to ATP synthase/TOR and AMPK/mTOR-associated nutrient-sensing pathways in longevity models, including C. elegans and Drosophila,57,58 providing a broader context for its effects in HFD-derived adults. In our data, α-KG supplementation was associated with increased Sdh expression. Because α-KG is metabolically connected to succinate production within the TCA cycle, this increase suggests a possible transcriptional association with the succinate/SDH-linked arm of mitochondrial metabolism. However, respiratory flux and Complex II activity were not directly measured. Together, the partial normalization of spargel by NR and the increased Sdh expression following α-KG supplementation suggest that these interventions improve HFD-associated mitochondrial physiology plausibly through overlapping yet distinct metabolic routes. Both interventions improved mitochondrial-associated readouts, performance, and survival after larval HFD exposure, with NR showing broader effects across several assays.

Microbiota supplementation further indicated that adult bacterial exposure can partially improve HFD-associated systemic dysfunction in adults derived from HFD-exposed larvae. Among the tested bacterial groups, Acetobacter spp. supplementation partially normalized the NADH/NAD+ ratio and improved respiratory-associated output, climbing performance, and survival, whereas Lactobacillus spp. alone produced no measurable improvement in these readouts. The co-association produced effects comparable to Acetobacter alone, suggesting that Acetobacter was the principal contributor to the observed physiological improvement. This pattern is consistent with prior Drosophila studies showing that Acetobacter species can influence host metabolic homeostasis through oxidative bacterial metabolism, including PQQ-ADH-dependent acetate production and insulin/IGF-associated signaling.20 In this context, Acetobacter may improve host redox and metabolic physiology by altering microbial metabolite availability and gut-host metabolic communication. As acetate/NAD+ metabolism, colonization dynamics, and insulin/IGF signaling were not directly assessed in this study, Acetobacter remains a putative modulator of HFD-associated physiology.

Finally, diet during early life influenced adult survival. Exposure to HFD during development shortened lifespan, likely due to ongoing metabolic stress even after switching back to a normal diet. Intervention results show this effect can be partially mitigated. NR supplementation increased the lifespan of adults derived from HFD-exposed larvae, aligning with its role in maintaining NAD+ redox balance and mitochondrial health.50 α-Ketoglutarate also provided a complementary benefit, consistent with its function as a TCA cycle intermediate and its known effects on metabolism and lifespan-related traits.57 Additionally, supplementation with Acetobacter improved survival, supporting prior research linking Acetobacter-driven metabolism to host metabolic stability.20 Overall, these results suggest that developmental HFD exposure shortens adult lifespan through persistent redox, mitochondrial, and microbial changes, and that targeted metabolic and microbial interventions can partly ameliorate these effects.

Limitations of the study

This study integrates transcriptomics, metabolomics, microbiota profiling, mitochondrial-associated assays, and intervention experiments to define persistent adult consequences of developmental HFD exposure. This multi-layered approach also identifies specific avenues for mechanistic resolution. First, although multiple readouts converged on altered mitochondrial-associated physiology, the causal links between TCA/OXPHOS repression, redox imbalance, and mitochondrial integrity remain to be resolved through respiratory flux analysis and targeted perturbation of key mitochondrial pathways. Second, 16S rRNA profiling and PICRUSt2 analysis identified diet-associated microbial restructuring and predicted functional shifts, providing a framework for future germ-free fly reconstitution and targeted metabolite studies to resolve the contributing taxa, microbial metabolites, and gut-substrate interactions. In addition, future inclusion of ND-derived supplemented controls will help determine whether NR, α-KG, and bacterial effects are specific to the HFD-derived state or reflect broader diet-independent benefits. Third, the durable adult metabolic state observed after developmental lipid exposure points to persistent regulatory mechanisms, motivating future studies of chromatin-level and other molecular intermediates that may maintain this response. Finally, although Drosophila offers genetic tractability, rapid generation time, and conserved nutrient-sensing and mitochondrial pathways, its distinct metabolic and microbial architecture warrants interpreting these findings as a tractable framework for studying developmental diet-associated metabolic remodeling, rather than as a surrogate for mammalian metabolic disease.

Experimental model and study participant details

Drosophila melanogaster maintenance and population setup

Wild-type Drosophila melanogaster (>5,000 individuals) were collected from the Delhi NCR region, India (28.61°N, 77.23°E; 216 m altitude) during October and November 2021. Field-collected females were pooled to establish a genetically diverse, outbred mass-bred population, which was maintained for eight generations under common-garden conditions on a standard yeast–cornmeal–agar diet at 20 ± 0.5°C under a 12 h light:12 h dark photoperiod and ∼70% relative humidity to minimize immediate collection-related environmental effects while preserving standing genetic variation. For the experimental setup, approximately 1,000 gravid females were distributed across six independent culture jars, with three jars assigned to the normal diet (ND) and three to the high-fat diet (HFD). Each jar represented an independent biological replicate. All experiments were performed on adult female flies.

Method details

Diet composition and feeding regimen

ND medium was prepared in water using maize powder (36 g/L), yeast (20 g/L), sugar (32 g/L), agar (7.5 g/L), sodium benzoate (0.5 g/L), and propionic acid (1.5 mL/L). For HFD treatment, the ND base medium was supplemented with 18% (v/v) coconut oil before solidification and mixed thoroughly to ensure uniform distribution. Larvae assigned to the HFD group were reared on this medium, whereas ND larvae were reared on unsupplemented ND medium. HFD exposure was restricted to larval development; after pupation, early pupae were transferred to ND vials for adult emergence and subsequent maintenance on ND medium, unless otherwise specified for rescue experiments. All assays were conducted at zeitgeber time (ZT) 4–5 h after lights-on to minimize circadian and environmental variability. Phenotypic and molecular analyses were performed on 3-, 7-, and 14-day-old post-eclosion adult females, as indicated for each assay.

Triacylglycerol (TAG) quantification

TAG levels were determined using the CheKine Micro Triglyceride Assay Kit (Abbkine; Cat# KTB2200) following the manufacturer’s protocol. TAG levels were measured separately in adult head and fat-body tissues. For each biological replicate (n = 3 per regime), tissues dissected from twenty adult flies were homogenized in 1 mL of extraction buffer on ice, and the lysates were clarified by centrifugation at 8,000 × g for 10 min at 4°C. The supernatant was collected, and the absorbance at 505 nm was measured. Total protein was measured separately using the Bradford method from parallel fly homogenates prepared from the same biological replicate cohort. TAG levels were normalized to total protein levels and expressed as TAG per unit protein.

RNA extraction, library preparation, and RNA-seq analysis

Total RNA was isolated from dissected whole head and fat body tissues of 3-day-old adult female flies using the TRIzol Plus RNA Purification Kit (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Cat. No. 12183555) following the manufacturer’s protocol. Head and fat body tissues were dissected from the same flies but processed as separate tissue-specific RNA-seq libraries. Each biological replicate corresponded to one independently maintained culture jar, with 20 flies pooled per replicate and three biological replicates per diet regime (n = 3 ND and n = 3 HFD per tissue).

RNA quantity and purity were assessed using a NanoDrop 2000 spectrophotometer, and RNA integrity was evaluated using a Bioanalyzer; RNA integrity number (RIN) values ranged from 7 to 10. Libraries were prepared using the TruSeq Stranded Total RNA Library Prep Kit with Ribo-Zero Plus for ribosomal RNA depletion. Sequencing was performed on the Illumina NovaSeq 6000 platform using paired-end 150 bp reads. Raw read quality was assessed using FastQC (v0.11.3), and reads were trimmed with Trimmomatic (v0.39) using LEADING:20, TRAILING:20, SLIDINGWINDOW:4:20, and MINLEN:36 to remove adapters and low-quality bases. Detailed sample-wise RNA-seq QC, trimming, and mapping statistics are provided in Table S1. High-quality reads were aligned to the D. melanogaster BDGP6 reference genome (Ensembl release 112, annotation BDGP6.46) using STAR (v2.7) with sorted BAM output. Gene and transcript abundances were estimated using RSEM (v1.3.3) with the parameters --alignments, --paired-end, and --strandedness reverse. RSEM expected counts were used as input for differential expression analysis in DESeq2 (v1.40.2) in R (v4.2.1), whereas STAR alignment files were retained for mapping-quality assessment and downstream alignment summaries. All libraries were prepared and sequenced together on the same platform; therefore, no batch correction was applied.

Weighted gene co-expression network analysis (WGCNA)

Normalized expression matrices were used to construct weighted gene co-expression networks in R using the WGCNA package (version 1.72). Pearson’s correlation coefficients were calculated across all expressed genes, and the resulting similarity matrix was raised to a soft-thresholding power β = 6 to approximate scale-free topology. The adjacency matrix was transformed into a topological overlap matrix (TOM), and hierarchical clustering of TOM dissimilarities defined network modules. To functionally characterize the identified gene modules, GO enrichment analysis was conducted using the clusterProfiler package in R. To connect co-expression modules to the dietary treatment, module eigengenes were correlated with tissue context (fat body = 0, head = 1) and dietary regime (ND = 0, HFD = 1) across all 12 samples using Pearson correlation, with p-values computed using the corPvalueStudent() function in the WGCNA package (v1.72). Results were visualized as a labeled heatmap, and modules with p < 0.05 were considered significantly trait-associated. To assess the enrichment of tissue-specific differentially expressed genes (DEGs) within WGCNA co-expression modules, a one-sided Fisher’s exact test was applied for each module–DEG list combination. Fly fat body DEGs and head DEGs (DESeq2, FDR <0.05) were first mapped to the WGCNA gene universe using FlyBase identifiers, retaining genes present in the expression matrix used for network construction. For each module, a 2 × 2 contingency table was constructed comprising: (a) DEGs within the module, (b) non-DEGs within the module, (c) DEGs outside the module, and (d) non-DEGs outside the module. The one-sided alternative hypothesis tested whether DEGs were overrepresented within the module relative to the chance expectation given the module size and the total DEG count. p-values were adjusted for multiple comparisons across all module–DEG list combinations using the Benjamini–Hochberg false discovery rate method. Odds ratios were computed as the ratio of within-module to outside-module DEG proportions. To visualize the functional landscape of co-expression modules, a binary membership matrix was constructed with modules as rows and five broad functional categories as columns: lipid-related processes, DNA processes, amino acid-related terms, immune response, and mitochondrial metabolism. GO Biological Process enrichment was performed for each module using clusterProfiler (FDR-adjusted p < 0.05), and significant terms were manually curated and assigned to one of the five functional categories. A binary matrix scoring module–category membership (1 = present, 0 = absent) was used to generate a chord diagram of functional annotations across modules.

Metabolite extraction and LC-MS analysis

Fat bodies from 50 adult flies per biological replicate (n = 3 ND and 3 HFD groups) were dissected in PBS, and each sample was homogenized for 2 min in 800 μL of precooled solvent (methanol: water: chloroform = 3:1:1, v/v/v) for metabolite profiling by LC-MS. The samples were centrifuged at 13,000 × g for 10 min at 4°C. Next, 20 μL of the supernatant was transferred to a new 1.5 mL polypropylene tube and diluted with 60 μL of precooled solvent to prepare the final sample for analysis.

All experiments were conducted using a Dionex UltiMate 3000 Rapid Separation LC system (Thermo Scientific, USA) coupled to a heated electrospray ionization (ESI)–Q Exactive mass spectrometer (Thermo Scientific) operating at 70,000 resolution. Sample analysis was performed in negative-ion mode over an m/z range of 100–1500. Negative ion mode was selected to enhance detection of acidic and polar metabolites, including organic acids, TCA-cycle intermediates, fatty-acid species, and redox-associated metabolites relevant to the metabolic pathways analyzed in this study. MS/MS fragmentation spectra were acquired in data-dependent acquisition mode for metabolite identification. The capillary temperature was set to 275°C, while the sheath and auxiliary gas flow rates were 30 and 10 arbitrary units, respectively. Solvent A consisted of 10 mM ammonium acetate (aq.) with the pH adjusted to 4.95 using glacial acetic acid, while solvent B was 100% methanol. Chromatographic separation was performed using a Hypersil Gold C18 column (2.1 mm × 100 mm, 1.9 μm particle size) at a flow rate of 0.400 mL/min using the gradient program provided in (Table S8). The injection volume was 15 μL. The instrument was calibrated according to the manufacturer’s instructions before sample analysis.

All LC-MS raw data were analyzed using ThermoFisher Scientific Compound Discoverer 3.3. Features were detected using a mass tolerance of ±5 ppm. Compound annotation was performed by searching mzCloud, ChemSpider, and Metabolika databases. To maximize annotation confidence, final reported metabolites were retained only when supported by mzCloud MS/MS spectral library matches and were reported at Metabolomics Standards Initiative (MSI) confidence level 2. Features annotated only by predicted composition or mass match alone were excluded from the final reported dataset.

Peak area data were normalized within Compound Discoverer 3.3 using the software’s default normalize areas workflow with constant median normalization. No external internal-standard or protein-content normalization was applied. Missing values were handled conservatively by retaining only features detected in all three biological replicates in each dietary group. Differential abundance between HFD and ND groups was assessed using Welch’s t test, and p-values were adjusted using the Benjamini–Hochberg false discovery rate correction. Transcriptomic and metabolomics integration analysis was done using MetaboAnalyst 5.0.

Gut microbiota profiling (16S rRNA sequencing and analysis)

In a matched multi-omics design, whole head, fat body, and gut tissues were dissected from the same 3-day-old adult female flies. Heads and fat bodies were processed separately for tissue-specific transcriptome analysis, whereas guts from the same flies were combined within each biological replicate for microbiome profiling. DNA was extracted from 20 pooled guts per biological replicate, with three independent biological replicates per diet group, using the phenol–chloroform method. The V3–V4 region of the bacterial 16S rRNA gene was amplified using V3V4-F 5′-CCTACGGNGGCWGCAG-3′ and V3V4-R 5′-GACTACHVGGGTATCTAATCC-3′ primers. 16S rRNA sequencing was performed on the Illumina NovaSeq (6000) platform.

The quality of the raw sequencing data was assessed using FastQC (v0.11.3), and adapter sequences and low-quality reads were trimmed using Trimmomatic (v0.39). Processed reads were analyzed using QIIME2 (v2024.2). For feature table generation and statistical analysis, DADA2 was implemented within QIIME2 to generate amplicon sequence variants (ASVs) and remove chimeric sequences. Taxonomic classification of ASVs was performed using the Greengenes 16S database (release 13–8). No separate extraction blank or no-template control samples were sequenced; therefore, potential background signals were addressed bioinformatically by removing non-bacterial and known host-associated features before downstream analysis. Samples were rarefied to 8,000 reads per sample for diversity analyses. Alpha diversity was assessed using Shannon and Simpson indices and compared between ND and HFD groups using the Kruskal–Wallis test with FDR correction. Beta diversity was visualized using Bray-Curtis and Jaccard distance matrices. Bray-Curtis differences between diet groups were tested using PERMANOVA with 999 permutations. Functional prediction was performed using PICRUSt2 on the filtered ASV representative sequences and feature table generated from the QIIME2 workflow. PICRUSt2 was used to infer enzyme commission profiles and MetaCyc pathway abundance from 16S rRNA marker-gene data. Pathway-level differences between ND and HFD groups were tested using the Wilcoxon rank-sum test, and p-values were corrected for multiple comparisons using the Benjamini–Hochberg false discovery rate (FDR) method. Prediction confidence was evaluated using nearest sequenced taxon index values from the marker_predicted_and_nsti output. PICRUSt2-derived results were interpreted as predicted microbial functional potential rather than direct measurements of microbial metabolic activity.

Quantitative real-time PCR (qRT-PCR)

For qRT-PCR validation of expression trends for selected genes (Mtpβ, Acox1, mAcon2, Mcad, Fum2, and Vha16-5), RNA was extracted from independently collected fat-body samples that were distinct from those used for RNA-seq library preparation. Each condition included three independent biological replicates, with five technical replicates per biological replicate; each technical replicate consisted of fat bodies dissected from 20 adult females. For supplementation-associated expression analysis, ND, HFD, HFD with NR, and HFD with α-ketoglutarate groups were used to assess the expression of selected intervention-related genes (sirt4, spargel, and SdhA). Complementary DNA (cDNA) was synthesized from total RNA using the G-Biosciences cDNA Synthesis Kit, and qPCR was performed on a Thermo Fisher QuantStudio 5 system using G-Biosciences 2× SYBR Green qPCR mix. GAPDH was used as the housekeeping gene for normalization. Relative gene expression was calculated using the 2ˆ-ΔΔCt method. Primer sequences are provided in Table S7.

Cross-species ortholog mapping and concordance analysis

To assess cross-species similarity, HFD-responsive genes in the adult fly fat body were mapped to human orthologs using the DRSC Integrative Ortholog Prediction Tool (DIOPT). Only high-confidence orthologs with a DIOPT score >7 and high-rank orthology support were retained for downstream analysis. These fly–human orthologs were then compared with published human obesity- and metabolic disease-associated transcriptomic datasets. Ortholog pairs were classified as concordant or discordant based on the direction of differential expression. Concordance was calculated as the fraction of overlapping orthologs changing in the same direction and tested against a null expectation of 50% concordance using a two-sided binomial test, followed by Benjamini–Hochberg FDR correction.

Pathway-level overlap was assessed using GO-Slim enrichment analysis of fly–human orthologs, with expressed orthologs used as the background set. Because gene-level concordance was not significant after FDR correction, cross-species similarities were interpreted primarily at the pathway level rather than as evidence of gene-by-gene conservation.

Functional assays

For functional mitochondrial and energy-related assays, as well as physiological and behavioral measurements, each biological replicate was subdivided into five technical replicates.

NAD+ and NADH quantification

NAD+ and NADH were quantified using the NAD/NADH-Glo Assay (Promega, G9071) with modifications for Drosophila. For each replicate, 30 fat bodies were homogenized in 600 μL ice-cold lysis buffer (1:1 PBS and 1% DTAB in 0.2 M NaOH), and clarified at 21,000 × g for 10 min at 4°C. Supernatants were divided for oxidized and reduced pools: NAD+ was stabilized by acid treatment (0.4 N HCl, 60°C, 15 min) and neutralized with Tris base; NADH by alkaline treatment (0.2 M NaOH, 60°C, 15 min) and neutralized with HCl. Samples were diluted 5-fold in stabilizing buffer containing 10 μM ascorbic acid, mixed with detection reagent (1:1, 50 μL each) in 96-well plates, and luminescence was recorded kinetically for up to 2 h. Standard curves (0.01–10 μM NAD+/NADH) were prepared in parallel, and concentrations were calculated from standard curves and reported after correction for dilution. Total protein content was determined from an untreated parallel aliquot using the Bradford protein assay, and dilution-corrected NAD+ and NADH values were normalized to total protein content. The NADH/NAD+ ratio was calculated from matched dilution-corrected NADH and NAD+ values from the same biological replicate.

Nitroblue tetrazolium (NBT) assay

Reactive oxygen species (ROS) formation was quantified using the nitroblue tetrazolium (NBT) reduction assay. Hemolymph was extracted from 50 flies per technical replicate. Briefly, adult flies were immobilized on ice, wings were removed, and the thorax was gently pierced with a fine needle. Punctured flies were placed in a perforated 0.5 mL tube nested inside a 1.5 mL collection tube and centrifuged at 5,000 rpm for 1 min at 4°C to collect hemolymph.67 NBT solution was added immediately to the extracted hemolymph. The reaction mixture was incubated in the dark at 30°C for 1 h and stopped by adding an equal volume of acetic acid. Samples were centrifuged at 12,000 rpm for 1 min, and the resulting pellet was resuspended in 150 μL of 50% acetic acid. Absorbance was measured at 595 nm on a BioTek Epoch 2 microplate spectrophotometer (Agilent Technologies). ROS levels were normalized to the number of flies used for hemolymph extraction and expressed as relative absorbance units per 50 flies.

Mitochondrial swelling assay

Mitochondria were isolated from 50 dissected fat bodies per replicate by differential centrifugation. Tissues were homogenized in ice-cold mitochondrial storage buffer containing 230 mM mannitol, 70 mM sucrose, 1 mM EGTA, 10 mM HEPES (pH 7.4), and a protease inhibitor cocktail. Homogenates were centrifuged at 800 × g for 30 min at 4°C to remove debris, and the supernatant was further centrifuged at 11,000 × g for 10 min to pellet mitochondria. Mitochondrial protein concentration was determined using the Bradford method, and the mitochondrial pellet was resuspended in hypotonic buffer (90 mM KCl, 20 mM MOPS-KOH, pH 7.2) at a final concentration of ∼100 μg protein/mL. Absorbance changes at 540 nm were monitored using a spectrophotometer for 5 min. The absorption rate (ΔA) and the initial swelling rate (ΔA/min for the first minute) were recorded.

CO2 production assay

CO2 production was measured following the method described elsewhere with slight modifications.68 Briefly, 10 flies per technical replicate were placed in a sealed pipette tip-capillary respirometer (without anesthesia) containing a small amount of soda lime to absorb CO2. The respirometers were positioned capillary-side down in dH2O containing 0.1% eosin dye inside a sealed TLC chamber, which was then placed in a 25°C incubator. Empty, sealed respirometers (without flies) were used as controls to account for background dye migration. Flies were tested for 1 h, and the CO2 production rate was indirectly quantified as the rate of liquid movement up the capillary per hour, after subtracting the background. Image data were analyzed using ImageJ.

TMRE staining and imaging

Mitochondrial membrane potential in adult fat body tissue was assessed using tetramethylrhodamine ethyl ester (TMRE) staining. Adult fat bodies from the experimental groups were dissected in cold Drosophila Schneider’s medium. Freshly dissected tissues were incubated with TMRE (Invitrogen; cat no: T669) working solution at a final concentration of 100 nM in Schneider’s medium for 20 min at room temperature in the dark. After staining, tissues were briefly washed with Schneider’s medium and mounted in Schneider’s medium containing 25 nM TMRE to maintain fluorescence during live imaging.

Samples were imaged immediately using a fluorescence microscope (Eclipse Ts2-FL, Nikon Corporation) under identical acquisition settings for all experimental groups, including laser intensity, exposure/gain, pinhole, detector settings, and magnification. DAPI was used to visualize nuclei where indicated. TMRE fluorescence intensity was quantified using ImageJ from equivalent regions of interest within the fat body after background subtraction. TMRE fluorescence was reported as background-subtracted mean fluorescence intensity from equivalent regions of interest and expressed in arbitrary units. Data are presented as mean ± SD from independent biological replicates.

ATP assay

ATP levels were quantified from whole adult females using the Elabscience ATP Colorimetric Assay Kit (E-BC-K157-S) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Adult female flies from the indicated dietary and rescue conditions were collected at the specified age post-eclosion. Each biological replicate consisted of 50 whole adult female flies, with 5 independent biological replicates per group. Samples were homogenized on ice in the extraction buffer supplied with the kit. Homogenates were centrifuged at 4°C to remove insoluble debris, and the clarified supernatant was used for ATP measurement. ATP reaction reagents were added to the sample supernatant, and absorbance was measured at 636 nm using a microplate reader. ATP concentrations were calculated from an ATP standard curve prepared in parallel. Total protein concentration was measured in the same extract using the Bradford method, and ATP levels were normalized to total protein. ATP values were expressed as ATP concentration per unit protein. Data are presented as mean ± SD from independent biological replicates.

Negative geotaxis (climbing) assay

The negative geotaxis assay evaluated locomotor performance. 10 flies from each technical replicate were gently introduced into a vertical cylinder using an aspirator. The number of flies crossing the 10-cm mark within 15 s was recorded. After each climbing session, flies were tapped back to the bottom and given a 30-s rest before the trial was repeated. The procedure was performed 3 times per vial, and the results were expressed as the percentage of flies that crossed the 10-cm mark within 15 s across 3 independent trials.

Lifespan assay

To evaluate lifespan, 10 newly eclosed (1–4 h old) virgin female flies were collected from each technical replicate and immediately transferred to vials (at 22°C) containing a normal diet. Flies were maintained under standard laboratory conditions throughout the experiment. Food vials were replaced every third day, and mortality was recorded daily.

Dietary supplementation with nicotinamide riboside (NR) and α-ketoglutarate (α-KG)

To evaluate metabolic rescue, nicotinamide riboside chloride (NR; 100 μM) and sodium α-ketoglutarate (α-KG; 10 mM) were incorporated into freshly prepared HFD medium before solidification. Supplement concentrations were selected based on prior Drosophila studies reporting beneficial metabolic or lifespan effects of NR56 and α-KG.57 They were further validated through preliminary tolerance trials in our experimental diet. Food was mixed thoroughly to ensure uniform distribution and dispensed at 5 mL per vial. Two supplementation paradigms were employed: (i) Larval supplementation, where larvae were reared on NR- or α-KG-enriched HFD until the early pupal stage, followed by transfer to un-supplemented ND for adult maintenance; and (ii) Adult supplementation, where newly eclosed HFD-fed flies were maintained on NR- or α-KG-enriched diets for 3- or 7-day post-eclosion. For behavioral and survival assays, supplementation was continued for 7 or 14 days as indicated.

Microbiota supplementation

For microbial supplementation, Lactobacillus spp. and Acetobacter spp. Stocks were used as exogenous bacterial supplements representing genera commonly examined in Drosophila host–microbiota and metabolic-intervention studies.25,26 Species- or strain-level identity was not independently resolved in the present study; therefore, the isolates are reported at the genus level. Genus-level nomenclature is commonly used in Drosophila host–microbiota studies when strain identity is not independently confirmed. Bacterial stocks were revived from glycerol stocks and grown overnight in selective media: de Man, Rogosa, and Sharpe (MRS) broth for Lactobacillus spp. and Acetobacter Glucose Medium (AGM) for Acetobacter spp. Cultures were incubated with shaking at 30°C for Lactobacillus spp. and 28°C for Acetobacter spp., at 180 rpm. Cells were harvested at OD600 ≈ 1.0, centrifuged at 4,000 rpm for 15 min, washed once in sterile phosphate-buffered saline (PBS), and resuspended in 1 mL PBS. For supplementation, 100 μL of the bacterial suspension was applied to the surface of fly food in each vial immediately before use, and flies were transferred to freshly supplemented food every other day.25 Bacterial supplementation was not lifelong; treatments were restricted to HFD-derived adults beginning at eclosion, and continued for 7 days for metabolic/behavioral readouts and up to 14 days for survival analyses, as indicated for each assay. Replicate vials were established for Lactobacillus spp., Acetobacter spp., and co-association experiments. Restorative effects were monitored using host physiological readouts, including NADH/NAD+ ratio, CO2 production, climbing performance, and lifespan.

Quantification and statistical analysis

RNA-seq and gene network analysis

Differential expression analysis was conducted using DESeq2 (v1.40.2) implemented in R (v4.2.1). Genes with a total read count <10 across all samples were removed before analysis. DESeq2 normalized the raw count data by estimating size factors to correct for differences in library size and composition. The design formula included diet regime as the main variable of interest (∼diet), and differential expression was tested using the HFD versus ND contrast separately for each tissue. Because all RNA-seq libraries were prepared and sequenced together, batch correction was not included in the model. DESeq2 calculated gene-wise dispersion estimates, and genes were considered differentially expressed at a Benjamini–Hochberg false discovery rate (FDR) < 0.05. Weighted gene co-expression network analysis (WGCNA), performed in R, was used to identify modules that correlated with one another. Pearson’s correlation coefficients were computed across all expressed genes, and module–module relationships were considered significant at p < 0.05. Functional enrichment of significant modules was performed using the clusterProfiler package (v4.8.3) for Gene Ontology (GO) and KEGG pathway analyses.

Permutation-based evaluation of RNA-seq differential expression

To assess the robustness of differential expression calls, we performed a label-permutation analysis separately for the head and fat-body datasets. For each tissue, the original 3:3 ND–HFD sample structure was preserved, but diet labels were reassigned across the six biological replicates to generate null 3-vs-3 comparisons. The true ND–HFD grouping and its reverse were excluded from the null set. Each permuted comparison was analyzed using the same filtering criteria, normalization procedure, DESeq2 model, and significance threshold as the observed comparison. The number of differentially expressed genes detected across the permuted label sets was summarized as the median null DEG count and presented alongside the DEG count obtained from the true ND-HFD grouping.

Physiological, mitochondrial, and behavioral assays

For physiological, mitochondrial, and behavioral datasets, each independent culture jar/cohort was treated as a biological replicate. Technical replicates were nested within the corresponding biological replicate. Data were analyzed using a nested ANOVA design with treatment as a fixed effect and biological replicate as the nested random structure, as appropriate for each assay. Data are expressed as mean ± standard deviation (SD), with n representing independent biological replicates. The assumptions of normality and homogeneity of variance were assessed using the Shapiro-Wilk and Levene’s tests, respectively, before selecting statistical tests. Post-hoc pairwise contrasts were adjusted using the Benjamini–Hochberg FDR correction. Survival data from lifespan assays were analyzed using the log rank test. All analyses and figure generation were performed using R (version 4.2.1), Python (version 3.11), and GraphPad Prism (version 9). Asterisks used in the figures denote statistical significance as follows: ∗p < 0.05, ∗∗p < 0.01 and ∗∗∗p < 0.001; ns indicates non-significance.
