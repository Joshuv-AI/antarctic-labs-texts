Impact of treatment modality on survival in FIGO stage IIB cervical cancer: a population-based propensity-score matched analysis
Authors: Hua Tu, Guochen Liu, Haifeng Gu, Zhimin Liu, He Huang, Jihong Liu
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
To investigate the impact of treatment modality on survival of International Federation of Gynecology and Obstetrics (FIGO) stage IIB cervical cancer. Patients with FIGO stage IIB cervical cancer registered in the Surveillance, Epidemiology, and End Results database between 1988 and 2015 were identified and grouped according to their treatment modalities. Propensity score matching (PSM) was performed to adjust the baseline characteristics. A total of 4718 stage IIB patients with eligible data were identified. Of them, 902 underwent surgery and 3816 received chemoradiotherapy alone. After PSM, 1208 patients were paired without baseline difference. Patients undergoing surgery (n = 604) had significantly improved overall survival (OS, 5-year rate: 68.0%, P = 0.007) and cancer-specific survival (CSS, 5-year rate: 70.4%, P = 0.007) compared with those receiving chemoradiotherapy alone (5-year rates: 59.9% for OS, 62.9% for CSS, n = 604). On multivariate analysis, treatment modality was an independent factor for both OS (hazard ratio [HR] = 1.334, P = 0.002) and CSS (HR = 1.369, P = 0.002). In subgroup analyses, surgery provided significantly better survival than chemoradiotherapy in subgroups with ≤ 4 cm squamous-cell carcinoma (P = 0.046 for OS, P = 0.020 for CSS, n = 143) and > 4 cm non-squamous-cell carcinoma (P = 0.010 for OS, P = 0.004 for CSS, n = 240). However, these differences were not observed in subgroups with > 4 cm squamous-cell carcinoma (P = 0.665 for OS, P = 0.816 for CSS, n = 523) or ≤ 4 cm non-squamous-cell carcinoma (P = 0.173 for OS, P = 0.293 for CSS, n = 30). In this population-based analysis, a surgery-based strategy was associated with improved survival compared to chemoradiation in patients with FIGO stage IIB cervical cancer. Exploratory subgroup analyses suggested that this benefit may vary by histology and tumor size.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-025-26555-3.

Introduction

In the International Federation of Gynecology and Obstetrics (FIGO) staging system for cervical cancer, IIB is an intermediate stage that separates early from advanced disease and presents in about 15% cases1. The diagnosis of stage IIB is primarily based on gynecological examination and sometimes on magnetic resonance imaging2. The presence of parametrial invasion implies a decreased chance for radical surgery, which is the preferred treatment for early-stage cases. Concurrent chemoradiotherapy was recognized as the standard of care for stage IIB patients, with a 5-year survival rate varying between 55 and 81%3.

Despite of this, there remains a rationality to apply surgical treatment for stage IIB patients4. With neoadjuvant chemotherapy, the tumor burden may be largely relieved before surgery and radiotherapy may be avoided due to reduced pathological risks5,6. Such a benefit is of particular importance for young patients with the desire to preserve ovarian and sexual functions7. Furthermore, surgical treatment provides an opportunity to assess the pathological status of retroperitoneal nodes, which allows for formulating more precise radiotherapy plans. On the other hand, about 15% cervical cancers have resistance to chemoradiotherapy, which is associated with poor prognosis8. Since there is no reliable method for predicting the sensitivity to chemoradiation, completion surgery may serve as a guarantee for therapeutic efficacy9.

The treatment strategy for locally advanced cervical cancer has long been debated by previous studies10–14. However, the proportions of stage IIB cases in these studies were relatively small and the results were inconsistent. Besides, most of the previous studies were retrospective, in which gynecologists might have biases when selecting treatments for stage IIB patients. It remains inconclusive whether neoadjuvant chemotherapy followed by radical surgery, or completion surgery after primary chemoradiation could improve the survival of stage IIB patients15. Due to the heterogeneity of stage IIB cervical cancers and selection bias in clinical practice, such a comparison should be carried out within large, well-matched cohorts.

With these considerations, we extracted the data from the Surveillance Epidemiology and End Results (SEER) database and compared the oncological outcomes of different treatment modalities in stage IIB patients. Propensity score matching (PSM) was conducted to adjust the baseline characteristics of each group. The aim of this study was to explore the survival impact of treatment modality on FIGO stage IIB cervical cancer and determine whether there exists an optimal therapeutic strategy for these patients.

Methods

Data from SEER database

The SEER Program is a population-based database supported and managed by the National Cancer Institute in the United States. With the approval of ethics committee of the Sun Yat-Sen University Cancer Center, we obtained access to the SEER database with the ID number 15,173-Nov2019. The SEER*Stat software (version 8.3.8) was used to extract clinicopathologic, therapeutic and survival information of target population. Data of patients with FIGO stage (2009) IIB cervical cancer diagnosed between 1988 and 2015 were extracted from the SEER databases. Patients were enrolled if they met the following criteria: (1) diagnosed with cervical cancer only; (2) their treatment modality was known; (3) their diagnostic confirmations were based on positive histology; (4) having definite information for FIGO or TNM stage and the stages were consistent (these stages were determined using the clinically derived staging variable within the SEER database, which reflects the original clinical assessment); (5) their cause of death was known; (6) their survival time were known and longer than 0 month; (7) no evidence of distant metastasis.

Eligible patients were grouped according to their treatment modalities (surgical or non-surgical). Surgical treatment was defined as hysterectomy (with or without neoadjuvant chemotherapy) followed by adjuvant therapy (chemotherapy and/or radiotherapy) based on pathological findings. Non-surgical treatment was defined as chemoradiotherapy without any type of hysterectomy. This design reflects the real-world clinical paradigm for stage IIB disease, where a primary surgical approach typically entails multimodal therapy. For patients identified as receiving surgical treatment, only those also receiving chemotherapy were enrolled. For the non-surgical group, inclusion was defined as having received chemotherapy concurrent with either external beam radiation alone or combined with brachytherapy. The following variables were collected for analyses: age, year of diagnosis, race/ethnicity, insurance and marital status, histological types and grades, stage, nodal status, tumor size, surgical approach, radiotherapy and chemotherapy status, follow-up and survival information.

Statistical analysis

PSM analysis was performed to adjust the potential confounding factors between the study groups. Propensity scores were calculated using a logistic regression model with covariates included age, year of diagnosis, race/ethnicity, insurance and marital status, histological types and grades, nodal status, and tumor size. Matching analysis was carried out using the nearest neighbor method with a ratio of 1:1 and a caliper of 0.1 times the standard deviation of the propensity score. Chi-squared tests were used to examine the baseline characteristics and group differences before and after PSM. The primary endpoint was cancer-specific survival (CSS), which was calculated as time from initial diagnosis to the date of death due to cervical cancer or last follow-up. The secondary endpoint was overall survival (OS), which was calculated as time from initial diagnosis to the date of death or last follow-up. Survival curves were plotted with the Kaplan-Meier method and compared by the log-rank test. Multivariate survival analysis, including subgroup analyses with formal tests for interaction, was performed using the Cox proportional hazards model. Hazard ratios (HR) were also calculated using this model. All statistical analysis was performed with SPSS software (version 26.0, SPSS Inc., Chicago, IL, USA) with a two-sided p-value of less than 0.05 considered statistically significant. We confirm that all methods were performed in accordance with the relevant guidelines and regulations.

Results

Clinicopathological characteristics of stage IIB patients before and after PSM

The schema of patient selection is shown in Fig. S1. A total of 4,718 eligible patients with FIGO stage IIB cervical cancer were identified between 1988 and 2015. The median age of patients at diagnosis was 49 years (range: 15–88 years). A total of 902 patients underwent hysterectomy (surgical group), of whom 848 (94.0%) also received radiotherapy and 366 (40.6%) received brachytherapy. The approaches for hysterectomy were radical in 460 (51.0%) patients and simple in 442 (49.0%) patients, respectively. A total of 3816 patients were treated by combination of chemotherapy and radiotherapy (non-surgical group). The approaches for radiotherapy were beam radiation with implants or isotopes in 2506 (65.7%) patients and beam radiation alone in 1310 (34.3%) patients. Table 1 summarizes the characteristics of the study population. As shown in Table 1, significant differences were found among the age, race/ethnicity, insurance recode, marital status, histological types, grade, N-stage, nodal status, and tumor size (all P < 0.05). Patients treated by surgery were younger and were more likely to be married, non-Black race, non-squamous cell carcinoma (including adenosquamous carcinoma), N1 stage, and have medical insurance, small tumor and definite histological grade compared to those receiving non-surgical treatment. These indicated clear biases in selection of treatment modalities. However, there was no significant difference between the years of diagnosis. PSM was then conducted and 1,208 patients were 1:1 matched, with 604 patients in each group. After PSM, none of the variables showed differences between the surgical and the non-surgical group (all P-value > 0.05, Table 1).

Survival analysis before and after PSM

The median follow-up time of the whole population was 46 months (range: 0–335 months). In the population before PSM, there were 1610 (42.2%) cervical cancer-specific deaths in total, with 299 and 1311 in the surgical and non-surgical group, respectively. Besides, 323 (8.5%) patients died from other causes, with 46 and 277 in the surgical and non-surgical group, respectively. The most common cause of non-cancer deaths was heart diseases (27.2%).

Before PSM, the 5-year CSS rate were 67.5% and 64.7%; 5-year OS rate were 65.3% and 60.9%, respectively, in the surgical and non-surgical group. Among patients who died from cervical cancer, the median survival time was 26 months in the surgical group and 21 in the non-surgical group. Kaplan-Meier analysis with log-rank test revealed that patients in the surgical group had significantly improved OS compared with those in the non-surgical group (P = 0.005). However, the difference in CSS between the two groups only approached significance (P = 0.084).

After PSM, the 5-year CSS rate were 70.4% and 62.9%; 5-year OS rate were 68.0% and 59.9%, respectively, in the matched surgical and non-surgical group. The median time to cancer-specific death was 25 months in the surgical group and 22 in the non-surgical group. Kaplan-Meier analysis with log-rank test revealed that the differences reached significant both in CSS (P = 0.007) and OS (P = 0.007) between the two groups. The survival curves for treatment modalities before and after PSM are shown in Fig. 1.

Univariate and multivariate analysis of survival after PSM

In the PSM matched groups, univariate analysis employing log-rank tests showed that histological type (P < 0.001 for OS; P < 0.001 for CSS), N-stage (P < 0.001 for OS; P < 0.001 for CSS) and tumor size (P = 0.001 for OS; P < 0.001 for CSS) were factors affecting both OS and CSS. Age (P = 0.001 for OS; P = 0.680 for CSS) and marital status (P = 0.020 for OS; P = 0.315 for CSS) were found to affect only OS but not CSS. The survival curves for these variables are shown in Fig. S2. In addition, neither the OS nor CSS differed significantly between patients with different year of diagnosis (P = 0.248 for OS; P = 0.346 for CSS), race/ethnicity (P = 0.184 for OS; P = 0.688 for CSS), insurance status (P = 0.171 for OS; P = 0.275 for CSS), or histological grades (P = 0.222 for OS; P = 0.272 for CSS). The results of univariate survival analyses after PSM are presented in Table 2.

To address concerns regarding the evolution of radiotherapy techniques, we performed a sensitivity analysis restricted to patients diagnosed in the more contemporary era (2004–2015), during which conformal radiotherapy became widespread. In this modern cohort, the surgery treatment remained significantly associated with improved survival compared to chemoradiation (P = 0.018 for OS, P = 0.039 for CSS, Fig. S3). To assess the potential impact of unmeasured confounding, a negative control analysis was performed by examining the effect of treatment modality on death from non-cancer causes. The analysis revealed no significant difference in the risk of non-cancer death between the surgical and non-surgical groups (HR = 1.15, 95% CI 0.722–1.83, P = 0.554, Fig. S3).

Subgroup analysis for OS and CSS after PSM

To supplement the primary analysis, we performed a series of subgroup analyses within the PSM-matched cohort using different stratification approaches. The aim was to explore the heterogeneity of treatment effects for surgery versus non-surgical therapy across specific patient populations.

First, to investigate the survival impact of different treatment approaches, we divided the matched groups into subgroups. The surgical group were divided into subgroup A (radical hysterectomy, n = 283) and subgroup B (simple hysterectomy, n = 321); the non-surgical group were divided into subgroup C (EBRT and vaginal brachytherapy, n = 401) and subgroup D (EBRT only, n = 203). Further survival analysis showed that patients in subgroup A had significantly improved OS and CSS compared with those in subgroup B (P = 0.017 for OS; P = 0.024 for CSS), C (P = 0.007 for OS; P = 0.005 for CSS) and D (P < 0.001 for OS; P < 0.001 for CSS). However, there was no significant difference in OS or CSS among the subgroup B, C and D. The 5-year OS rates were 71.0%, 65.4%, 61.0%, and 57.7%, respectively; the 5-year CSS rates were 74.0%, 67.3%, 63.6%, and 61.5%, respectively, in subgroup A, B, C and D.

Subsequently, we evaluated the influence of treatment sequence. According to the sequence of surgery and radiotherapy, we re-divided the same cohort of patients from the surgical group into subgroup E (radiotherapy prior to surgery, n = 150) and subgroup F (radiotherapy after surgery or no radiotherapy, n = 454) and then compared them with the non-surgical group. Survival analysis showed that both the subgroup E (P = 0.022 for OS; P = 0.009 for CSS) and F (P = 0.031 for OS; P = 0.044 for CSS) had significantly improved CSS and OS compared with the non-surgical group. However, no significant difference was observed in OS (P = 0.364) or CSS (P = 0.171) between the subgroup E and F. The 5-year OS rates were 71.6% and 66.9%, and the 5-year CSS rates were 75.1% and 69.0%, respectively, in subgroup E and F. The survival curves for these subgroup analyses are shown in Fig. S4.

Furthermore, we assessed the treatment effect stratified by key pathological and tumor characteristics. Survival analyses were performed in patients stratified by histological type and tumor size. Surgical treatment provided significantly better survival compared with non-surgical in patients with non-squamous-cell histology (P = 0.005 for OS; P = 0.002 for CSS), while they were comparable in patients with squamous-cell histology (P = 0.112 for OS; P = 0.177 for CSS). Similarly, surgical treatment provided significantly better survival compared with non-surgical in patients with ≤ 4 cm tumors (P = 0.017 for OS, P = 0.009 for CSS), while they were comparable in patients with > 4 cm tumors (P = 0.085 for OS, P = 0.076 for CSS). The survival curves for these subgroup analyses are presented in Fig. S5.

Based on the above findings, we conducted a further analysis stratified by both histology and tumor size, creating four subgroups (Table 4). Within these subgroups, point estimates for survival outcomes appeared more favorable for surgery in patients with ≤ 4 cm squamous-cell carcinoma (P = 0.046 for OS, P = 0.02 for CSS, n = 143) and > 4 cm non-squamous-cell carcinoma (P = 0.004 for OS, P = 0.010 for CSS, n = 240). However, a formal test for the three-way interaction between treatment, histology, and tumor size was not statistically significant (P = 0.088 for OS, P = 0.064 for CSS). The survival curves for these subgroup analyses are shown on Fig. 2.

Discussion

There are several important findings in this study. The treatment modality was found to independently affect survival of patients with FIGO stage IIB cervical cancer. However, gynecologists had clear selection biases when determining treatments for them. Survival analysis after PSM showed that surgical treatment significantly improved both OS and CSS when compared with chemoradiotherapy alone. However, such a benefit was restricted to patients with ≤ 4 cm squamous-cell carcinoma and > 4 cm non-squamous-cell carcinoma. Our findings conflict with traditional viewpoint that radical surgery and concurrent chemoradiotherapy were equivalent options for locally advanced cervical cancer10.

This study has several strengths. First, it was performed based on a large sample of patients, overcoming the statistical deficiencies in previous studies9,10. Second, we set strict criteria for patient selection. For patients identified as receiving surgical treatment, records of chemotherapy were required. For patients identified as receiving non-surgical treatment, only those with records of beam radiation and chemotherapy were included. These inclusion criteria accorded with the therapeutic principle for locally advanced cervical cancer and ensured the essential treatments in both groups. Third, we performed PSM analysis to adjust the baseline characteristics, which largely reduced the potential selection bias and made our results more reliable.

Concurrent chemoradiotherapy is currently the preferred treatment for stage IIB cervical cancer16. However, the therapeutic efficacy of concurrent chemoradiotherapy remains dissatisfactory. Although there is a favorable response rate for initial treatment, a considerable number of patients will experience local or distant relapse. Therefore, the presence of residual lesions following chemoradiotherapy remains a significant issue. For stage IIB patients, one feature separating them from advanced cases is that their diseases remain potentially resectable, which enables more aggressive treatments. Thus, radical surgery in addition to chemoradiotherapy may provide an opportunity to improve oncological control.

The surgical interventions included surgeries after neoadjuvant chemotherapy and concurrent chemoradiotherapy. Numerous studies had investigated the efficacy of neoadjuvant chemotherapy followed by radical surgery in locally advanced cervical cancer6,7,11. A multicenter randomized study showed a significant survival benefit of neoadjuvant chemotherapy plus radical surgery compared to radiotherapy alone17. However, the survival rate in this trial was relatively low, which might be attributed to the lack of concurrent chemotherapy. On the contrary, two other randomized trials failed to demonstrate the superiority of neoadjuvant chemotherapy over concurrent chemoradiotherapy18,19.

The treatment for cervical cancer is rapidly evolving with the integration of immunotherapy20. The KEYNOTE-A18 trial has established a new standard of care for locally advanced disease, demonstrating that adding pembrolizumab to definitive chemoradiotherapy improves outcomes21. It is noteworthy, however, that the magnitude of benefit was less pronounced in the stage IIB subgroup. In parallel, promising strategies involving neoadjuvant immunotherapy combined with chemotherapy, followed by radical surgery, have yielded high pathological response rates (pathological complete response rate: 32.6%-66.7%)22–24. While our study did not include immune checkpoint inhibitors, its findings remain highly relevant. The efficacy of a surgery-based strategy observed in our cohort provides a rationale for exploring its integration with modern immunotherapy regimens, suggesting a potential alternative pathway for optimizing outcomes in stage IIB patients.

In this study, the inclusion of patients who underwent simple hysterectomy, accounting for a substantial portion (49%) of the surgery-based cohort, was a deliberate choice to reflect real-world clinical scenarios. These cases largely represent two distinct situations: intraoperative conversion from a planned radical procedure due to technical challenges, or a planned simple hysterectomy following definitive chemoradiation as a consolidative approach. Although, as expected, patients receiving simple hysterectomy had inferior survival compared to those undergoing radical hysterectomy (P = 0.017 for OS; P = 0.024 for CSS), we maintained the inclusive cohort based on the intention-to-treat principle to provide an unbiased estimate of the overall effectiveness of initiating a surgery-based strategy. However, an unexpected observation was that, the 5-year CSS of patients treated by radical hysterectomy (subgroup A, n = 283) reached 74.0%, which was significantly higher than that by chemoradiotherapy alone (62.9%). In addition, we identified a subgroup of patients whose surgeries were performed after radiotherapy (subgroup E, n = 150). These patients also had significantly improved survival (5-year CSS: 75.1%) compared with those receiving chemoradiotherapy alone. This result coincided with the finding in the study by Yoshida et al.15. As population-based data, the 5-year CSS rates in subgroups A and E were very close to those reported by prospective trials3,12. Thus, we believed that the therapeutic efficacy of surgery was robust for stage IIB patients.

Previous studies had shown that adenocarcinoma/adenosquamous carcinoma was an independent prognostic factor for cervical cancer and more likely to have resistance to radiotherapy than squamous carcinoma25,26. In our study, histological type and tumor size were found to be independent factors for both OS and CSS. Further subgroup analyses revealed that the survival benefit of surgery was restricted to patients with ≤ 4 cm squamous-cell and > 4 cm non-squamous-cell tumors. These findings should be interpreted as exploratory and hypothesis-generating, indicating a potential pattern that warrants further investigation. For squamous cell carcinoma, which is more radiosensitive, surgery may offer superior local control for smaller tumors with limited parametrial invasion. For radioresistant non-squamous carcinomas, however, radical resection may be superior for bulky tumors due to better local control. These exploratory findings provide hypothesis-generating evidence for personalized treatment selection in stage IIB cervical cancer.

Furthermore, the records of brachytherapy were presented in only 40.6% patients in the surgery group before PSM. We speculated that there existed a percentage of patients who were exempted from brachytherapy due to reduced pathological risks after chemotherapy. This may contribute to decreasing the medical cost and radiotherapy-associated morbidities. Besides, this advantage is quite valuable in regions and hospitals where the brachytherapy devices and technicians are insufficient27,28.

The SEER database has inherent limitations with regard to completeness and accuracy29. Except for the variables adjusted in our PSM analysis, other variables that could not be evaluated in this study may also have impacted the treatment selection and survival, such as performance status, SCC-Ag level and chemotherapy agents. A key limitation of this study is the lack of detailed data on radiotherapy techniques in the SEER database, such as the use of total dose, completion status, and techniques such as intensity-modulated radiotherapy or image-guided brachytherapy. This prevents assessment of radiotherapy quality and means we cannot fully exclude the potential for bias due to technical variations. Furthermore, this analysis could not assess treatment-associated morbidities. As established in prior literature, the combined modality of surgery and radiotherapy is known to carry a higher burden of morbidity compared to definitive chemoradiotherapy30,31. This underscores the essential risk-benefit trade-off inherent in treatment selection for stage IIB cervical cancer. The central question our study raises is whether a potential survival benefit could justify the increased morbidity of a surgery-based approach in this specific population. To definitively address this question, we have initiated a multicenter randomized controlled trial. This prospective trial aims to directly compare neoadjuvant chemotherapy combined with radical surgery versus concurrent chemoradiotherapy in patients with FIGO stage IIB cervical cancer32. The hypothesis-generating findings from this population-based analysis provide a rationale for our ongoing trial, the results of which are awaited to offer new evidence for managing this disease.

In conclusion, our study revealed a significant impact of treatment modality on survival of patients with FIGO stage IIB cervical cancer. Neoadjuvant chemotherapy combined with radical surgery may be considered as the preferred choice for patients with ≤ 4 cm squamous-cell carcinomas and > 4 cm non-squamous-cell carcinomas. This finding challenges the traditional viewpoint and should be further verified by ongoing multicenter randomized trials.
