Val109 and Ile170 contribute to channel function in Guillardia theta-derived anion channelrhodopsins
Authors: Takanori Yokoyama, Eriko Sugano, Kitako Tabata, Taira Kakizaki, Shu Maruoka, Yuka Endo, Taku Ozaki, Tomokazu Fukuda, Lanlan Bai, Hiroshi Tomita
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractAnion channelrhodopsins (ACRs) are widely used for optical silencing of neurons. Photocurrents and kinetics are crucial for the precise control of cell hyperpolarization. In this study, we investigated the roles of amino acid residues in each transmembrane domain of the Guillardia theta-derived anion channelrhodopsins, GtACR1 and GtACR2, to develop high-performance ACRs. Several chimaera ACRs were generated by replacing the transmembrane (TM) domains of GtACR1 with homologous counterparts of GtACR2, and photocurrents in HEK cells transduced with each variant were recorded by patch-clamp. The relationship between photocurrent and protein localisation was determined. Chimaeras of TM4, TM5, and TM6 in GtACR1 replaced with the homologous counterparts of GtACR2 showed wavelength changes and fast kinetics(τon/off). V109 of TM3, the putative ion tunnel with I170, one of the key interactions for the peak photocurrent. Molecular dynamics simulations using data from the point mutation analysis in I170 showed that the distance from V109 to I170 correlated negatively with the peak photocurrents. The relationship between τ-off and the distance from E68 to N239, which formed the bottleneck of the ion tunnel, was positively correlated. These findings provide useful information for the development of ACRs with higher light sensitivities and faster kinetics(τon/off).Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-026-46487-w.Keywords: channelrhodopsin, anion channelrhodopsin, Guillaldia theta, GtACR1, GtACR2, V109I, kineticsSubject terms: Biochemistry, Biophysics

Introduction

Optogenetics, a technique that uses light to control the activity of genetically modified cells (typically neurons) in living organisms, has gained considerable traction in the field of neuroscience. Cation channelrhodopsins (CCRs), a light-sensitive cation channel protein, activates neuronal cells. Archaea-derived chloride ion pumps (NpHRs)1 and proton pumps, such as rhodopsin (Arch)2 are tools for suppressing neuronal activation. Pump-type rhodopsins, such as NpHR and Arch, actively transport chloride or proton ions out of the cell following light activation; the transport rate of chloride ions by NpHR is estimated at 219 chloride ions per 630 photons3,4. These rhodopsins can actively transport ions into the cytoplasm; however, rhodopsins require stimulation with high-intensity light to transport enough ions to inhibit nerves owing to their ineffective influx rate, bearing the risk of phototoxicity5.

Anion channelrhodopsins (ACRs), developed by introducing mutations into the amino acid sequence of cation channelrhodopsins (CCRs)6,7, show high conductance and can react with low-intensity light compared with pump-type rhodopsins1,2,5–7. Govorunva E.G et al. identified the first natural ACRs, GtACR1 and GtACR2, which were derived from the alga Guillardia theta8. Various ACRs from other organisms, such as Geminigera cryophila, Proteomonas sulcata, and Rhodomonas sp9–13, have been reported in addition to those developed by genetic modifications14–17. Patch-clamp recordings, in vitro experiments, X-ray crystallography, and molecular dynamics (MD) simulations have been employed to elucidate the protein structures18–21.

GtACR1 and GtACR2 are effective in controlling neurons in various species22–25. Given that ACRs function in the plasma membrane, their localization in the plasma membrane may be an important factor for photocurrent26–28. However, few studies have evaluated the membrane expression of ACRs. One reason is the lack of experimental methods for evaluating cell membrane localisation.

In this study, we designed chimaera ACRs by combining transmembrane regions of GtACR1 and GtACR2 to develop new ACRs with fast kinetics and photosensitivity. The developed ACRs were then analysed by patch-clamp recordings, MD simulation, and membrane localisation assays.

Results

Preparation and electrophysiological evaluation of chimaera ACRs

A previous study reported the successful generation of chimeric rhodopsins by recombining seven homologous transmembrane domains in ChR1 and ChR2 from green algae and provided functional insights into the transmembrane domains along with the development of improved chimeric ChRs29. We aimed to develop chimaera ACRs with improved function by recombination. Each transmembrane region of GtACR1 and GtACR2 is shown in Fig. 1A. First, the transmembrane regions of ACRs were divided into seven homologous segments from the N- to C-terminus. The seven transmembrane segments of GtACR1 were corresponding labelled “A”, “B”, “C”, “D”, “E”, “F”, and “G”. Similarly, those in GtACR2 were labelled “a”, “b”, “c”, “d”, “e”, “f”, and “g”. Thus, we generated six chimaera ACRs (AbcdefG, ABcdefG, ABCdefG, ABCDefG, ABCdEfG, and ABCdeFG) by recombination with the homologous transmembrane regions. The C-terminus of all chimaeras was fused with Venus, a green fluorescent protein, to visualise expression.

GtACR1/2 and chimaeras were transfected into HEK293 cells and their functions were characterised by patch-clamp recordings (Fig. 1B). These data were obtained under conditions of a holding potential of 0 mV, light intensity of 1 µW/mm2, and stimulus duration of one second with light of 455–505 nm wavelength. ABCDefG, ABCdEfG, and ABCdeFG did not respond to the light stimulation. Photocurrents were evoked at wavelengths ranging from 405 to 656 nm in GtCAR1. However, GtACR2 and the three chimeras (ABCdefG, ABcdefG, and AbcdefG) only showed a response from 405 to 560 (Fig. 1C, Supplementary Table 1). Comparing each chimaera with GtACR1, GtACR2, and ABcdefG showed a similar performance to GtACR2 over the entire wavelength range, whereas ABCdefG and AbcdefG showed significantly lower photocurrents at 405 nm. Disensitization, which represents the rate of attenuation of the photocurrent during light stimulation, was lower at 455 nm in ABcdefG and at 505 nm in ABCdefG and AbcdefG than in GtCAR1. Comparing ChimGt2 with the three chimeras, a significant difference was observed only at 405 nm in ABCdefG (Fig. 1D, Supplementary Table 2). The opening time (τon) constant of each chimerawere significantly accelerated, except for the 505 nm in ABcdefG and 560 nm in three chimeras compared to GtACR1, and for the 405 nm in three chimeras compared to GtACR2 (Fig. 1E, Supplementary Table 3). The closing time (τon) constant of each chimera were significantly accelerated compared to GtACR1, and at 455 nm in ABCdefG and AbcdefG compared to GtACR2(Fig. 1F, Supplementary Table 4).

Reasons underlying increasing photocurrents

Considering that the photocurrent of ABcdefG and ABCdefG, ABcdefG showed an increase compared to that of ABCdefG at all wavelengths due to the difference between “C” and “c” in transmembrane 3 (TM3), a comparison of the sequence of TM3 in two chimaeras showed that the “C” and “c” differed only at three sites, A93S, L108Q, and V109I (C’s residue―amino acid number conforming to GtACR1―c’s residue). This indicated that ABcdefG was synonymous with ABCdefG (A93S_L108Q_V109I) (Fig. 2A). Accordingly, to identify the mutation in A93S, L108Q, or V109I responsible for the increase in photocurrent in ABcdefG, we designed three additional variants: ABCdefG(A93S), ABCdefG(L108Q), and ABCdefG(V109I) (Fig. 2B). Representative photocurrent traces recorded at the wavelength evoked the maximal response (455 nm) are shown in Fig. 2C. The photocurrents of ABCdefG, ABcdefG {ABCdefG(A93S_L108Q_V109I)}, and the three mutants were measured by whole-cell patch-clamp recordings at different wavelengths (Fig. 2D). The results showed an increasing tendency in ABCdefG(V109I) compared to ABCdefG over the entire wavelength range. In contrast, ABCdefG(L108Q) did not differ from ABCdefG, and ABCdefG(A93S), showing a decreasing tendency in the photocurrent over the entire wavelength range. Subsequently, the disensitization was compared and found to be significantly increased at 405 nm in ABcdefG and ABCdefG(V109I) compared to that in ABCdefG. In contrast, there was a decrease in disensitization at 455 nm for ABCdefG(L108Q). No significant changes were observed in ABCdefG (A93S) over the entire wavelength range (Fig. 2E). At τon, ABcdefG was rapid compared with ABCdefG (Fig. 2F). However, no significant changes were observed in the three mutants, except for ABCdefG(V109I), at 560 nm. The τoff was significantly delayed in ABcdefG, ABCdefG(L108Q), and ABCdefG(V109I) compared with ABCdefG (Fig. 2G). No noticeable changes were observed in the expression of ABCdefG(A93S).

V109-I170 pair

Analysis of the differences between “C” and “c” suggested that the V109I mutation on TM3 was responsible for the increase in photocurrent. Subsequently, we performed homology modelling using GtACR1 (PDB ID:6EDQ19 as a template and predicted the structure of ABCdefG by calculating its molecular orbitals. V109 in ABCdefG was expected to interact with I70, and the V109I mutation caused a change in the interaction or distance between V109-I170, resulting in an altered photocurrent (Fig. 3A). We characterised the effect of the V109-I70 relationship on the photocurrent by mutating I170 to different amino acids (A, W, and Y). The typical waveforms for the three mutants {ABCdefG(I170A), ABCdefG(I170W), and ABCdefG(I170Y)} are shown in Fig. 3B. There were no significant changes in the photocurrent in mutant ABCdefG(I170A) compared to ABCdefG, except at 560 nm (Fig. 3C). However, the photocurrent was significantly attenuated in the ABCdefG(I170Y) and ABCdefG(I170W) variants, where larger aromatic amino acids were introduced. The disensitization data showed a significant increase only in ABCdefG(I170Y) compared with ABCdefG(Fig. 3D). The τon was significantly increased in ABCdefG(I170W) and ABCdefG(I170Y) compared with ABCdefG(Fig. 3E). The τoff increased significantly in ABCdefG(I170A) only at 455 nm, compared with ABCdefG(Fig. 3F). In contrast, ABCdefG(I170W) and ABCdefG(I170Y) did not show any significantly effect.

Membrane localisation and cytoplasmic accumulation

ABCdefG and its mutants were transfected into HEK293 cells, and green fluorescence from Venus was confirmed by fluorescence microscopy (Apotome.2, Zeiss, Germany) (Fig. 4A). The IN Cell Analyzer 2200 (GE Healthcare, USA) was used to evaluate membrane localisation against the total Venus intensity for each chimaera (Fig. 4B). The results showed no significant differences among the variants, with almost all variants showing a trend toward increased expression by 10–20% (Supplementary Tabel 5) compared with ABCdefG. Normalised photocurrents, calculated by dividing the photocurrent by the rate of membrane localisation, are shown in Fig. 4C. The normalised photocurrents could be evaluated as the ion transport ability of the ACRs themselves because of the ACR function at the site of the plasma membrane. There was no correlation between the photocurrent and the rate of membrane localisation (Fig. 4D); however, a weak negative correlation was found between the normalised photocurrents and rate of membrane localisation (Fig. 4E).

Cytoplasmic accumulation of ACRs was analysed using the same method as that for the rate of membrane localization (Fig. 4F). MD simulation

We simulated the ion permeation pathways in GtACR1, GtACR2, and ABCdefG using Caver 3.0 to determine the cause of the changes in photocurrents and kinetics observed in ABCdefG and each variant. The ion tunnel of GtACR1, GtACR2, and ABCdefG were predicted to consist of TM1, TM2, TM3, and TM7 (Fig. 5A, Supplementary Fig. 2B, C), similar to that of GtACR118,19. Focusing on the intracellular side of the predicted GtACR2 and ABCdefG ion tunnel, there were two exit points: one through V109(TM3)-I170(TM5) and the other in the A61(TM1)-L108Q(TM3)-T249(TM7) (Fig. 5A; Path 1, Path 2). Permeation pathway analysis was performed using two independent tools, MOLEonline and AlphaFold330,31, to gain further insight into the predicted tunnels. For the AlphaFold3 analysis, the AlphaFold3-derived structure was first equilibrated under the same conditions as the homology model and then used for tunnel prediction by utilizing Caver. MOLEonline analysis identified putative permeation pathways within the structures of GtACR1, GtACR2, and ABCdefG (Supplementary Results 3). However, in all channels, the predicted pathways were positioned between TM2, TM3, TM6, and TM7, differing from the previously reported pathway between TM1, TM2, TM3, and TM718,19. In ABCdefG, MOLEonline did not identify a pathway passing between V109 and I170, although such a pathway was detected in the homology model analyzed with Caver 3.0. In contrast, Caver 3.0 analysis based on the equilibrated AlphaFold3-derived structures revealed a tunnel formed by TM1, TM2, TM3, and TM7 in GtACR1 and GtACR2 (Supplementary Results 4). In ABCdefG, the results were largely consistent with those obtained from the homology model, and a tunnel extending toward the V109–I170 region was identified.

The replacement of amino acids constituting the V109-I70 pair had a large effect on the photocurrent, suggesting that changes in the two predicted intracellular exits were likely to affect the photocurrent. The distance between the V/I 109-I/A, W, Y 170 pairs of GtACR2 and ABCdefG variants, as in ABCdefG, was calculated by MD simulations. The correlations between distances and photocurrents were investigated (Fig. 5B). A strong negative correlation was detected between the distances and photocurrents of the V109-I170 pair in GtACR1, GtACR2, ABCdefG, and its variants. We examined the correlation between the photocurrent and the distance between each amino acid in A61-L/Q108-T249, considered as another intracellular exit point. We detected a weak negative correlation between the photocurrent and the distance between A61-L/Q108 and A61-T249. This indicated that the shorter the distance between A61-L/Q108 and A61-T249, the higher the photocurrent (Fig. 5C, D). However, no correlation was found between L/Q108-T249 and photocurrent (Fig. 5E).

ABCdefG and its variants showed better kinetics than GtACR1/2. To determine the underlying cause, we examined the correlation between the opening/closing rate and the distance/potential energy from E68 to N239 (Fig. 5A, Path 3). No correlation was found between τon and distance/potential energy (Fig. 5F,G). However, τoff and the E68-N239 distance or interactions were positively correlated (Fig. 5H). The distance and interaction in E68-N239 were strongly correlated (Fig. 5J).

Discussion

We generated six chimeric ACRs (AbcdefG, ABcdefG, ABCdefG, ABCDefG, ABCdEfG, and ABCdeFG) by replacing the seven homologous transmembrane regions of GtACR1 and GtACR2. We then investigated the effect of replacing the transmembrane regions on channel function by comparing them with each ACR. Chimeric ACR has superior kinetics to nature-derived ACR and identified the key residues, V109-I170 pairs, forming the putative ion tunnel contributing to the induced photocurrent by comparing the data obtained from a patch-clamp recording with MD simulation analysis.

Both GtACR1 and GtACR2 function as chloride channels, and swapping their transmembrane domains was therefore expected to have little impact on ion selectivity. In contrast, the two ACRs differ in kinetics and spectrum, suggesting that these features were more sensitive to transmembrane swapping. All chimaeras, except for those where photocurrent could not be recorded, showed faster kinetics (shoter τon/off) than GtACR1/2 (Fig. 1E,F). In particular, ABcdefG showed higher photocurrent than the other two, but tended to be slower in τoff. The negative correlation between the peak current and τ off has been previously reported32–35, and our results were consistent with previous findings32–35. Chimeras lacking proper combinations of the D/E/F segments (ABCDefG, ABCdEfG, and ABCdeFG) fail to generate photocurrents. Comparison of the amino acid sequences of GtACR1 and GtACR2 reveals differences in residues forming the retinal pocket near the β-ionone ring. These findings suggest that an appropriate combination of TM4–TM6 is essential for proper retinal pocket formation and the structural stability.

The difference between ABcdefG and ABCdefG was in three amino acid substitutions, A93S, L108Q, and V109I, in TM3 (Fig. 2A,B). Therefore, we investigated the contribution of each amino acid residue to the photocurrent by introducing each point mutation in ABCdefG. The V109I mutation may be responsible for the increase in photocurrent in ABcdefG, whereas A93S was responsible for the decrease in photocurrent (Fig. 2D). MD simulation analysis to calculate the ion tunnel indicated that V109I (TM3) interacted with I170 (TM5) and formed the ion tunnel (Fig. 5A). Mutations to amino acids located on the ion tunnel pathway often have a huge effect on the function of the channel14,18,36,37. Therefore, we replaced I170 in ABCdefG with A, W, and Y, each with a different amino acid size, to confirm that the V109-I170 pair affected the photocurrent. Replacement with I170A did not affect the photocurrent. The photocurrent was significantly reduced, and the τon and τoff rates were delayed by the replacement of larger aromatic amino acids, such as W and Y. Taken together with a strong negative correlation between the distance from I/V109 to I/A, W, Y 170 and the peaks (Fig. 5B), the large size of aromatic amino acid residue in the ion tunnel pathway may critically affect ion transport. The putative ion tunnel of ABCdefG suggested that the intracellular exit was not only at V109-I170, but also at A61-L108-T249, the reported ion tunnel of GtACR118,35(Fig. 5A). The peaks tended to decrease as the distance from A61 to L108 and T249 increased (Fig. 5C–E). Both V109 and I170 are hydrophobic residues, raising the question of whether they directly contribute to the formation of the ion pathway. In this study, homology modeling was performed based on the ground-state structure of GtACR1, and it should be noted that this model may not fully reflect the functional state of the protein. However, a strong correlation was observed between the distance at this position and the photocurrent, suggesting that these residues are likely to influence ion permeation either directly (V109-I170 pathway) or indirectly (A61-L108-T249 pathway).

All chimeras created in this study exhibited GtACR2-like absorbance characteristics. Since many chimeras, excluding the I170 mutation, share TM4-6 with GtACR2, the difference in absorbance between GtACR1 and GtACR2 likely originates from these regions. In particular, substitutions of key residues on TM4–TM6 that have been reported as major determinants of spectral tuning in GtACR1 (e.g., C133, S156, P208, and W211)38. C133 is thought to influence the absorption spectrum by modulating retinal binding geometry, whereas changes in the polarity of residues surrounding the β-ionone ring, including S156 and P208, alter the electrostatic environment of the retinal pocket and shift the absorption maximum. Our results are consistent with this view.

The chimaeras and their mutants were superior to GtACR1/2 in opening and closing speeds. To investigate the cause of these differences, we focused on the E68-N239-S43 amino acid interactions. Previous X-ray crystallography and simulations suggest that amino acids at their locations form the central gate of a putative ion tunnel in ChRs18,35,39. Mutations in the amino acids involved in the gate play a major role in the kinetics20,40,41. In our previous study, we successfully improved kinetics in the CCR ComV137 by H172A mutation (ComV1A), an amino acid forming the “inner gate”42. We hypothesised that the improvement in τon and τoff observed in the chimaeras is profoundly related to the changes in the hydrogen bonding network of E68-N239-S43. Analysis of MD simulation showed that the τoff accelerated as the distance decreased, and the attraction was stronger at E68-N239. In the CCR CrChR2, E90-N258 at the E68-N239 homologous site plays an important role in the opening and closing mechanisms of the channel based on the E90-Helix2 tilt model43. Therefore, E90-N258 was susceptible to channel kinetics. In GtACR1, the N239Q mutation accelerated channel closure, indicating that the E68-N239 was an important interaction determining the rate of channel closure20. Since ABCdefG is the chimaera of GtACR1 and GtACR2, changes in distance and interaction at E68-N239 may affect τoff. This result is important to consider when designing a faster closing rate (τoff) for the anion channel. In the present study, kinetics were measured using 1-s light pulses. Under this condition, activation of ACR molecules is not temporally synchronized, and multiple open states may accumulate during illumination. Therefore, a more precise evaluation of the relationship between the derived “on” and “off” time constants and intrinsic microscopic gating kinetics would require measurements using brief laser flash or other short-duration light stimulation protocols.

We established a method to objectively evaluate the rates of membrane localisation and cytoplasmic accumulation using the In Cell Analyzer 2200 (GE Healthcare (Molecular Device), USA). The photocurrent normalised by the rate of membrane localisation for each ACR showed a weak negative correlation (Fig. 4E), indicating that channels with lower membrane localisation tended to have a higher ion transport capacity. We found a weak positive correlation between cytoplasmic accumulation and normalised photocurrent (Fig. 4H) and a weak negative correlation between the rate of cytoplasmic accumulation and membrane localisation (Fig. 4I). The rate of cytoplasmic accumulation tended to increase the photocurrent, likely due to many contradictions in the consideration of ACRs that function in the plasma membrane. However, the channel function can explain this phenomenon.

The presence of a large percentage of negatively charged amino acids is expected to cause a repulsive force between the amino acids and chloride, thereby increasing the photocurrent20. However, an overabundance of the same charge in proximity to a charged amino acid can cause electrical repulsion and inhibit folding6. ChR have an abundance of cyclic amino acids residing around the retinal-binding pocket, and stacking interactions among these residues may contribute to protein aggregation; that is, ACRs with high ion permeability may have amino acid sequences and structures that easily induce cytoplasmic accumulation during folding and transport in the endoplasmic reticulum, suggesting a trade-off between ion permeability and aggregation or membrane localisation. This method is more reliable than visual inspection for evaluating membrane localization. However, fluorescence detected near the plasma membrane may include contributions from adjacent intracellular compartments, which may underlie the weak correlation between photocurrent and membrane localization.

In this study, we successfully produced a chimeric ACR with high kinetics by replacing the transmembrane regions of GtACR1 and GtACR2. By MD simulation, we found the 109th V, which forms an ion tunnel in the chimaera ACR, is important for the photocurrent. Chimeric ACRs with fast opening/closing rates and high light responsiveness are potential optogenetic tools that can induce hyperpolarisation more precisely at lower light levels than natural ACRs.

Methods

HEK293 Cell culture

HEK293 cells were maintained under 5% CO2 at 37 ℃ in minimum essential medium (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Tokyo, Japan) supplemented with 10% fatal bovine serum (Thermo Fisher Scientific), 1% Antibiotic-Antimycotic (Thermo Fisher Scientific), 1% GlutaMax™(Thermo Fisher Scientific) and 1% MEM non-essential amino acid (Thermo Fisher Scientific) in 60-mm dishes.

Gene transfection

The plasmid vectors used in this study were constructed following a previously described protocol37,42. The ABCdefG and variant plasmid vectors contained a CAG promoter44, woodchuck hepatitis virus post-transcriptional regulatory element45, and human poly A signal.

Each point mutation in ABCdefG was generated following the manufacturer’s protocol using the KOD-Plus-Mutagenesis Kit (Toyobo, Osaka, Japan). The sequence of each mutant was confirmed by Sanger sequencing (Genewiz, Kawaguchi, Japan).

Transfection of plasmid vectors into HEK293 cells was performed using a previously described calcium phosphate transfection protocol37,42. In brief, at 60–70% confuency, the medium in the 60-mm dish was replaced with a fresh medium. Calcium phosphorus-DNA particles containing the plasmid of 6.0 µg were prepared and added to the medium. After 6 h,

the medium was replaced with a fresh medium. HEK293 cells were cultured for 24–30 h and seeded onto circular glass slides for patch-clamp recordings.

Electrophysiology in HEK293 cells

Whole-cell patch-clamp recordings using HEK293 cells expressing each gene were performed 2 days after gene transfection. The recordings were performed using an EPC-10 amplifier and Patchmaster software (HEKA Electronic, Lambrecht, Germany). Light stimulation of the cells was performed using light-emitting diodes (Mightex Systems, Pleasanton, CA, USA) at the indicated wavelengths (405, 455, 505, 560, 617, and 656 nm). The light intensity was fixed at 1 µW/mm2 throughout the experiment. The holding potential was set to 0 mV. Data were recorded by filtering at 10 kHz and sampling at 5 kHz. The bath solution contained [in mM]: 138 NaCl, 1 NaHCO3, 0.3 Na2HPO4, 5 KCl, 0.3 KH2PO4, 1.25 CaCl2, 0.5 MgSO4・7H2O, 0.5 MgCl2, 5 HEPES・NaOH, and 22.2 D-glucose, adjusted to pH 7.2. The pipette solution contained [in mM]: 133 K-gluconate, 7 KCl, 4 MgCl2, 0.1 EGTA, 10 HEPES, 0.5 Na2GTP, and 2 Na2ATP, adjusted to pH 7.4.

Light-induced currents were analysed following a previously described protocol42. In summary, the photocurrent was the maximum value of the current induced by light. The opening/closing time constants (τon/off) were calculated by monoexponential fitting of the rise/disensitization of the photocurrent. τon was evaluated as the time it took for the current value to reach 1-e− 1 (63%) f the peak current from the point of light irradiation start. τoff was evaluated as the time it took to reach e− 1 (37%) f the steady-state current value. Disensitization was calculated as the rate of decrease from the peak current to the steady state.

Membrane staining in HEK293 cells

HEK293 cells, 24 h after gene transfection, were seeded onto poly-d-lysine-coated 8-well culture slides (354632, CORNING, USA). The concentration was 5.0 × 104 cells/300 µl per well. Following seeding, cells were incubated at 5% CO2 and 37 ℃ for 24 h.

The next day, the entire medium was gently aspirated, and 4% paraformaldehyde was added and incubated (5% CO2, 37 ℃) for 15 min to fix the cells. After 15 min, 4% paraformaldehyde was gently aspirated, and the wells were washed thrice with 1 × PBS (-). The staining solution was added to the wells and incubated at room temperature for 10 min. Rhodamine Wheat Germ Agglutinin (# RL-1022, Vector, USA) diluted in 1 µg/mL with HBSS was used for staining. Upon staining, the solution in the wells was aspirated and washed thrice with 1 × PBS (-). Subsequently, the washing buffer was discarded and sealed with a cover glass (#C024321, MATSUNAMI GLASS, Osaka, Japan) along with a DAPI-containing sealant (#0100 − 20, COSMO BIO COMPANY, Japan).

Imaging and analysis

Glass slides were imaged at wavelengths for DAPI (390 nm), FITC (475 nm), and Cy3 (542 nm) using IN Cell Analyzer 2200 [GE Healthcare, USA]. Thereafter, the obtained images were analysed using the IN Cell Developer Toolbox [GE Healthcare, USA]. First, cellular and plasma membrane regions were defined based on the location of the plasma membrane stained with Rhodamine Wheat Germ Agglutinin. Subsequently, cells with DAPI-stained nuclei within the cellular regions and expression of Venus fused to the C-terminus of the ChR were analysed. We calculated the amount of Venus fluorescence in each cellular and plasma membrane region of selected cells. Membrane expression was evaluated by dividing the fluorescence intensity in the membrane by that in the cell. The cytoplasmic region was defined as the cellular region subtracted from the plasma membrane region. Aggregation was defined as the fluorescence of Venus in granular form in the cytoplasmic region divided by the fluorescence of Venus in the cytoplasm as a whole.

MD simulation

The first structural models of ABCdefG and its mutants were built via homology modelling using the SWISS-MODEL server (https://swissmodel.expasy.org/)46–50. The crystal structure of GtACR1 in the dark (PDB ID:6EDQ19 was used as a template. NAMD 2.1451,52 was used for MD simulations, and the CHARMM force field53 was applied to all molecules. During the simulation, 2,000 steps of energy minimisation and 10,000 steps of MD calculation were performed under periodic boundary conditions. In this step, the main chains of the protein and all-trans-retinal were fixed. In the second step, the protein main chain was not fixed, and 2,000 steps of energy minimisation and 500,000 steps of MD calculations were performed using the same method. For the other conditions, the temperature during the simulation was maintained at 310 K using a Langevin thermostat. The pressure was maintained at 1.01325 bar using the Langevin piston method. Long-range electrostatic interactions were calculated using the particle-mesh Ewald method. Each step in the calculation was defined as 2 fs, and each atomic orbital was recorded every 500 steps. Molecular modelling and analysis were performed using VMD software54. The Caver software (version 3.0)55 was used to predict ion permeation pathways. MOLEonline(https://mole.upol.cz/) analysis, the homology modeling–derived structure was used. Calculations were performed in Pores mode with the Membrane Region option enabled30. Ion tunnel analysis based on AlphaFold3 was performed as follows. Structural modeling was performed using AlphaFold server (https://alphafoldserver.com/)31. The dimeric structure of GtACR1, GtACR2 and ABCdefG was generated based on its amino acid sequence (GtACR1: ipTM = 0.81, pTM = 0.8, GtACR2: ipTM = 0.7, pTM = 0.77, ABCdefG: ipTM = 0.81, pTM = 0.83). After model generation, all-trans retinal was manually docked into the retinal-binding pocket based on previously reported GtACR1(PDB ID: 6EDQ19. The model was then subjected to energy minimization and equilibration following the same protocol used for homology modeling. Subsequently, putative ion pathways were analyzed using Caver 3.0.

Statistics analysis

All figure data are expressed as ± standard error of mean. Statistical analyses were performed using the GraphPad Prism version 4.00 (GraphPad Software Inc., San Diego, CA, USA). For comparisons of absolute values, one-way ANOVA followed by Tukey’s or Dunnett’s multiple-comparison test was used, whereas relative values were analyzed using the Kruskal–Wallis test. The criterion for statistical significance was set at p < 0.05. The curves of photocurrent were generated by LOWESS fitting.
