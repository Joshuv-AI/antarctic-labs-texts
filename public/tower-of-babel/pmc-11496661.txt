Association between weight perception and perceived stress among Korean adults from the Korea National Health and Nutrition Examination Survey
Authors: Dong-uk Cha, Chang-Hwan Baek, Dongchan Lee, Hyun Soo Kim, Minkook Son
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractAlthough several studies showed a correlation between weight perception and depression, the association between weight perception and perceived stress is unclear. This study explored this association using the Korea National Health and Nutrition Examination Survey with 37,022 participants. Participants were grouped based on how they perceived their body versus real body mass index (BMI) and waist circumference: accurate, underperception, or overperception. Stress was assessed through a questionnaire. The logistic regression analysis was performed, and odds ratio (OR) with 95% confidence intervals (CIs) was presented. The results showed a correlation between overperception and higher perceived stress. This correlation was especially apparent in women with significant OR and 95% CIs of 1.15 (1.07–1.23) for BMI and 1.15 (1.07–1.23) for waist circumference. In the subgroup analysis according to the real obese status, all misperceptions were associated with higher perceived stress in normal participants. In comparison, underperception showed a correlation with lower perceived stress in obese participants. The results suggest weight perception as a potential marker for many stress-related diseases and an important factor in treating mental disorders. Introduction

In South Korea, the prevalence of obesity among adult males has surged from 25.7% in 1998 to an alarming 46.9% in 2020, while the figures for females have remained relatively stable, shifting from 25.9 to 29.9% within the same timeframe1. Such disparate trends between genders might be explained by varying perceptions and attitudes toward obesity2. Additionally, age-specific obesity prevalence shows intriguing patterns. Specifically, while younger females display declining obesity rates, the prevalence of obesity peaks within the 30–50-year age bracket for males, underscoring the critical state of obesity among middle-aged South Korean men1.

In addition to physiological ramifications, body composition can influence the psychosocial status of a person. With the increasing interest in psychosomatic medicine, emerging research underscores the stress-inducing nature of obesity, further elucidating the psychosocial ramifications of obesity stigma3. A comprehensive meta-analysis revealed a significant correlation between stress and various health indicators, including body mass index (BMI), waist circumference, and serum triglyceride levels4. Some research also suggests that underweight and obese people show increased risks of suicide attempts, highlighting the multifaceted relationships between psychosocial issues and body stature5.

However, considering stress implications, perceived body image might be pivotal. Considering metrics such as BMI and waist circumference, if individuals perceive themselves as underweight or obese, contrasting with their actual body weight, this can influence their stress levels. Although prior research investigated the relationship between weight perception and depressive symptoms in Korean adults, to our knowledge, no studies explored the relationship between weight perception and perceived stress among Korean adults6. Thus, our investigation fills this knowledge gap, examining the relationship across all BMI categories and waist circumference thresholds in the Korean adult population. Elucidating these associations could help develop tailored interventions targeting specific age, gender, or body composition categories, improving stress management. Therefore, we aimed to investigate the association between weight perception and perceived stress with nationally representative datasets, focusing on Korean adults aged 19–65 years without depression.

Results

Baseline characteristic of the study population

Baseline characteristics according to weight perception with BMI are described in Table 1. The underperception group predominantly comprised males (68.3%), while the overperception group largely included females (78.8%). The mean age for the underperception group was 46 years, while that of the overperception group was 43 years. Menopausal individuals were minimally represented in the underperception group (9.3%) but were more prevalent in the overperception group (14.6%). Smoking prevalence was notably higher in the underperception group (31.6%) compared to its counterpart (12.1%). Regular exercise engagement was more pronounced in the underperception group (41.1%) than in the overperception group (38.0%). Individuals who overestimated their body shape status showed increased perceived stress relative to those with accurate weight perception. In contrast, participants underestimating their weight status had decreased perceived stress compared to the accurate group. Association between weight perception and perceived stress

Weight perceptions about BMI and waist circumference were significantly linked to perceived stress among those who overperceived their physique (for BMI, adjusted OR = 1.11, 95% CI: 1.05–1.18, P < 0.001; for waist circumference, adjusted OR = 1.11, 95% CI: 1.05–1.18, P < 0.001). Overperceiving one’s body shape according to BMI and waist circumference was correlated with perceived stress in females. However, such an association was absent in men. For women, overperception about BMI showed a correlation with perceived stress (adjusted OR = 1.15, 95% CI: 1.07–1.23, P < 0.001; see Table 2). Conversely, this relationship was not observed in men.

Considering the COVID-19 pandemic, we performed the additional analysis using the KNHANES data from 2010 to 2019. The additional analysis yielded the same results as the main analysis. In the additional analysis, there was a significant association between weight perceptions and perceived stress (adjusted OR = 1.08, 95% CI: 1.01–1.16, P = 0.01; see Supplementary Table 2). In addition, we performed the sensitivity analysis the complex sampling design and sampling weights of KNHANES. The analysis yielded the same results as the main analysis (adjusted OR = 1.12, 95% CI: 1.04–1.21, P = 0.002; see Supplementary Table 3).

Subgroup analysis

Figure 1 describes the subgroup analysis for the association between weight perception and perceived stress. In age-related subgroup analysis, we found that overperception was positively correlated with higher perceived stress across different age groups. Specifically, for individuals aged 19–40 years, this was supported with adjusted OR values of 1.12 (95% CI: 1.02–1.22, P = 0.02) for BMI and 1.13 (95% CI: 1.05–1.23, P = 0.002) for waist circumference. Similarly, among those aged 41–65, the respective OR values were 1.11 (95% CI: 1.02–1.21, P = 0.02) and 1.10 (95% CI: 1.02–1.19, P = 0.02).

Subgroup analyses for the association between weight perception and perceived stress.

Subgroup analysis based on BMI classifications revealed distinct patterns across different BMI groups. Underperception with normal BMI showed increased perceived stress (adjusted OR = 1.16, 95% CI: 1.06–1.26, P = 0.001). In addition, overperception with normal BMI demonstrated even higher stress levels (adjusted OR = 1.28, 95% CI: 1.19–1.37, P < 0.001). Interestingly, underperception with obesity indicated reduced stress (adjusted OR = 0.75, 95% CI: 0.65–0.87, P < 0.001). Moreover, notable variations were apparent when observing the effects of waist circumference. Overperception with a normal waist circumference showed heightened stress (adjusted OR = 1.27, 95% CI: 1.19–1.35, P < 0.001). In contrast, underperception with central obesity demonstrated reduced stress (adjusted OR = 0.82, 95% CI: 0.70–0.97, P = 0.02).

Mediation and moderation analysis

For a more comprehensive understanding of the association between weight perception and perceived stress, we performed mediation and moderation analyses (see Supplementary Table 4). As a consequence, several mediational variables were identified, including sex, age, income level, education, menopause, smoking, alcohol consumption, and underlying diseases. Notably, a significant mediation effect was observed in the order of smoking, sex, and age (estimate; -0.018, 0.013, and 0.012, respectively). But, in moderation analyses, no variables demonstrated consistent results across all perceived stress variable types.

Discussion

This study used data from the KNHANES to investigate the relationship between weight perception and perceived stress. Overall, we found a significant association between weight overperception and perceived stress. Gender-specific analysis revealed that this relationship was particularly prominent in women, without such a relationship in men. Subgroup analysis revealed that both underperception and overperception of BMI were associated with higher perceived stress in the normal BMI group. Additionally, those who underperceived their weight showed a negative association with perceived stress compared to those with accurate perception in the obese group.

The observed relationship between weight perception and stress aligns with the findings of previous studies from other countries7–10. The societal evaluation of individuals based on weight is called weight stigma3,11,12. Societies sensitive to weight stigma exert strong social pressure on individuals who perceive their weight as not appropriate, leading to weight-controlling behaviors12. Particularly in modern societies, weight stigma is stronger for overweight individuals, often associated with perceiving a lack of self-control and laziness13. For these reasons, perceived stress is generally suggested to be higher in the overperception group.

Interestingly, we observed a strong association with perceived stress not only in groups that overperceive their weight but also in those that underperceive their weight despite having a normal BMI. This indicates that inaccurate subjective evaluations regarding one’s weight are related to stress. Individuals experiencing high stress often exhibit a lack of emotional regulation, interpersonal skills, and low self-efficacy14. They tend to distort self-perception and other external factors, including body shape, and such cognitive distortions can be rooted in these psychological factors15,16.

Gender-specific analysis revealed a significant relationship between weight perception and stress in women compared to men. Various previous studies based on gender reported similar findings7–10. Women experience greater pressure for weight control and a higher prevalence of weight stigma compared to men17,18. Additionally, eating disorders, such as anorexia nervosa and bulimia nervosa, are more prevalent in women than men19,20. Women engage in more weight-controlling behaviors, such as overeating, binge eating, and diet pill use, which can be perceived as stress-inducing21,22. Considering these factors, various psychological factors, including stress, might be more closely related to weight in women than in men17–23.

In groups classified as overweight based on BMI or waist circumference, the underperception group exhibited lower perceived stress. Therefore, individuals experiencing lower stress levels might be more likely to underperceive their weight. People with lower stress levels tend to have higher self-efficacy and are more tolerant of judgment from others14,24. Consequently, they might experience a lesser burden from societal weight stigma and perceive their weight and body positively. Furthermore, individuals who generally experience lower stress levels might neglect self-management, including weight control, leading to obesity21.

The limitation of this study is a simple cross-sectional design. Although we performed the mediation and moderation analyses and identified indirect mediating effects by several variables, further longitudinal studies are required to confirm the trajectory of stress responses according to weight perception. Additionally, selection bias might exist since the study comprised people who voluntarily participated in the KNHANES. Finally, a single self-report question was used to measure perceived stress. Despite these limitations, the strength of our study is that it is the first study investigating the association between weight perception and perceived stress within the Korean adult population using data from the KNHANES, a national statistical research program conducted by experienced medical experts. Furthermore, the sample size was large (37,022), with performed demographic correction with various demographic characteristics (e.g., sex, age, income level, education, residence, marriage status, menopause, smoking status, alcohol drinking, physical activity, hypertension, diabetes, and dyslipidemia status). These advantages offer notable advancements over prior research in terms of accuracy.

Moreover, the significance of this study lies in illuminating the subjective weight evaluation and its correlation with stress. Weight management is clinically crucial, especially for chronic diseases such as diabetes and hypertension. Accuracy in subjective weight perception is a prerequisite for effective weight management25. Stress is an adaptive behavior to internal and external threats and is related to an individual’s personality, which also affects lifestyle, such as diet and exercise26. Considering the relationship between weight, stress, and personality as shown above, if the objective BMI value expressed in numbers and the subjective perception of one’s weight are different, it is necessary to consider the individual’s personality related to stress. Therefore, in terms of weight management, clinical attention should be directed not only to the physical aspects of individuals but also to diverse psychological and social factors, including stress. Particularly, given the higher relevance of these factors in women compared to men, clinical and societal attention to weight and stress in women is needed.

For people complaining of stress and psychological distress in clinical settings, clinical attention to BMI and weight is also needed. Stress is an important risk factor for the development of depression, a major psychiatric disorder27. Additionally, depression and obesity are risk factors for high blood pressure and cardiovascular disease, and when these two are combined, there is a synergistic effect28. Additionally, if depressed patients are obese, their prognosis is poor, including an increased suicide rate29. Not only obesity but also underweight are related to various mental diseases, and in particular, depression and BMI are reported to show a U-shaped relationship30,31. Considering these points, for patients who complain of stress, it is important to understand the patient’s weight perception, provide accurate BMI, and manage weight.

To clinically control the relationship between stress and weight, weight stigma is an issue that must be addressed because it can lead to a variety of physical problems, including obesity17. Generally, stigma is created by members of society, not individuals. Therefore, in order to change weight stigma, individual efforts are important, but social and academic attention is also essential. Social media can have a negative impact on weight stigma, so social change is needed in this area, and governments should continue to look for legislative solutions to enact policies to prevent weight discrimination32. Public education is also needed based on accurate information that weight is caused by a combination of genetic factors and various social factors33.

In conclusion, in the misperception group, participants with normal BMI and waist circumference had high perceived stress, and obese participants in the underperception group showed low perceived stress. These connections were only found in women. Our finding suggests a potential role for weight perception as an effective and general marker for many diseases, which are highly related to stress, and as an important factor in the context of mental disorder treatment, especially in women. Further research is required to evaluate the relationship between weight perception and stress completely.

Methods

Study population

We used data from the Korea National Health and Nutrition Examination Survey (KNHANES) from 2010 to 2021, a survey run by the Korea Centers for Disease Control and Prevention (K-CDC)34. In recent years, there have been concerted efforts to construct an accurate, up-to-date, and comprehensive health database at the national level. These efforts have involved the establishment of a continuous annual survey system with full-time field staff, collaboration with relevant academic societies for quality control, and revision of the survey methods35. The survey collected detailed information on Koreans’ health habits, behaviors, and backgrounds through interviews, check-ups, and nutrition questionnaires. Further information regarding the KNHANES is summarized on the relevant website (https://knhanes.kdca.go.kr/knhanes/eng/index.do).

Of all the participants we aimed to include, 92.4% answered the question about their views on their body shape and daily stress (Fig. 2). Then, we excluded some participants: those aged < 19 years or > 65 years (n = 34,967), pregnant or breastfeeding participants (n = 8,640), and those who had depression, stroke, myocardial infarction, angina, or cancer (n = 5,368). After removing participants with incomplete data (n = 2,055), we included 37,022 participants (15,691 men and 21,331 women).

Selecting our study population from the 2010–2021 Korea National Health and Nutrition Examination Survey.

Weight perception pattern

We calculated BMI using the height and weight data. Using World Health Organization guidelines for the Asia-Pacific, we identified obesity and underweight at a BMI ≥ 25 and < 18.5, respectively36,37. Central obesity was defined as a waist size > 90 cm for men and > 85 cm for women38.

Participants shared their views on their body shape. They were asked: “How do you see your body shape?” They could choose from “very thin,” “slightly thin,” “normal,” “slightly obese,” or “very obese.” We grouped the answers into “very/slightly thin,” “normal,” and “slightly/very obese.” By matching what they thought with their actual measurements, including BMI and waist circumference, we sorted them into three groups: those who accurately perceived their body shape (accurate), those who thought they were thinner than their actual body shape (underperception), and those who thought they were more obese (overperception). Considering waist circumference, we excluded the “very/slightly thin” group from the analysis due to the absence of an underweight reference value for waist circumference. Figure 3 graphically describes the detailed group information.

Grouping of participants’ weight perception with actual body mass index (A) and waist circumference (B).

Perceived stress

Daily stress was evaluated using a simple questionnaire. The options for answers were “feel very much,” “feel much,” “feel a bit,” and “hardly feel.” The answers were extracted to measure stress. In this study, participants were regarded as stressed if they answered “feel very much” or “feel much.”

Covariates

We extracted further data that can influence stress. For females, we categorized their menstrual status as having menstrual cycles or being in menopause. Regarding marital status, we identified participants as “married,” “never married,” and “previously married (separated, divorced, or widowed).” Participants’ income levels were segmented into four quartiles. Education was determined based on the self-reported highest degree achieved: elementary school graduate, middle school graduate, high school graduate, and college graduate. We classified participants’ living areas as either urban or rural.

Prior research indicated a link between weight perception and certain behaviors, such as smoking39,40, alcohol consumption41, and physical activity42. For this reason, we assessed smoking status, alcohol drinking, and physical activity. We classified smoking status into current, former, and non-smokers. We categorized alcoholic drinking into consumers and non-consumers. Physical activity was evaluated using the Korean version of the Global Physical Activity Questionnaire, focusing on moderate and vigorous leisure-time activities (LTPA), defined as having ≥ 1occasions of either moderate LTPA (i.e., having at least 10 min of sport/exercise/recreation activities that slightly increased heart rate or breathing) or vigorous LTPA (i.e., having at least 10 min of sport/exercise/recreation activities that substantially increased heart rate or breathing) during the past week. Lastly, we identified participants with underlying diseases, such as hypertension, diabetes, and dyslipidemia, and included these as covariates.

Statistical analysis

The baseline characteristics of the study population were analyzed according to weight perception (accurate, underperception, or overperception). Continuous variables were presented as a mean with standard deviation, and categorical variables were presented as the number of cases with percentages. The logistic regression analysis was performed, and odds ratio (OR) with 95% confidence intervals (CIs) for perceived stress was calculated to investigate the association between weight perception and perceived stress. The multivariable-adjusted logistic regression analysis was adjusted for sex, age, income level, education, residence, marriage status, menopause, smoking status, alcohol drinking, physical activity, hypertension, diabetes, and dyslipidemia status. For further analyses, we performed three additional analyses. First, considering the COVID-19 pandemic, we performed the sensitivity analysis using the KNHANES data from 2010 to 2019. Second, we performed the sensitivity analysis with the complex sampling design and sampling weights of KNHANES using the complex sample procedures of SPSS. Third, we performed the mediation and moderation analyses to investigate mediator and moderator variables for perceived stress. For mediation and moderation analyses, the R lavaan package for structural equation modelling was employed, resulting in the presentation of estimates with standard error for each covariate43. Data processing and statistical analyses were performed using R version 4.3.0 (R Foundation for Statistical Computing, Vienna, Austria) and SPSS version 20 (IBM, SPSS Inc., NY, USA). A P-value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.
