Association between serum potassium and 28-day mortality in elderly patients with sepsis: a multicenter cohort study
Authors: Xiaoyan Liu, Qisheng Lin, Kaiwen Gao, Xinglin Chen, Shu Li, Xuying Zhu, Shan Mou, Zhaohui Ni, Haijiao Jin
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundWhile dyskalemia is known to affect outcomes in critically ill patients, its specific impact on elderly septic patients remains unclear. Previous studies in general intensive care unit (ICU) populations have shown a U-shaped relationship between serum potassium and mortality, but this pattern may differ in elderly patients with sepsis.MethodsThis retrospective cohort study analyzed 12,069 patients aged ≥ 65 years with sepsis from 208 U.S. hospitals using the eICU Collaborative Research Database (2014–2015). Patients were categorized based on their admission serum potassium levels: hypokalemia (< 3.5 mmol/L), normokalemia (3.5–5.0 mmol/L), and hyperkalemia (> 5.0 mmol/L). The primary outcome was 28-day mortality.ResultsAmong the cohort (mean age 77.0 [7.6] years; 50.6% women), 16.4% had hypokalemia, 73.2% had normokalemia, and 10.4% had hyperkalemia. In contrast to the U-shaped relationship previously reported in general ICU populations, we observed a significant linear association between serum potassium and 28-day mortality (P = .001). In the unadjusted model, each 1 mmol/L increase in serum potassium was associated with 65% higher odds of mortality (OR, 1.65 [95% CI, 1.54–1.77]). This association was particularly pronounced in patients aged ≥ 80 years (OR, 1.79 [95% CI, 1.59–2.03]). After comprehensive adjustment for confounders, each 1 mmol/L increase in serum potassium was associated with 23% higher odds of mortality (adjusted OR, 1.23 [95% CI, 1.09–1.39]). Compared to normokalemia, hyperkalemia carried significantly higher mortality risk (adjusted OR, 1.45 [95% CI, 1.11–1.88]).ConclusionsOur findings challenge the established U-shaped paradigm by demonstrating a linear relationship between serum potassium and mortality in elderly septic patients. This suggests that traditional approaches to potassium management may need modification in this vulnerable population, with greater emphasis on preventing hyperkalemia. These results provide a foundation for developing age-specific potassium management strategies in sepsis care.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12877-025-06574-5.Keywords: Sepsis, Elderly, Hyperkalemia, Mortality, Critical care

Introduction

Sepsis remains a leading cause of critical illness and mortality in intensive care units (ICUs), affecting approximately 49 million individuals annually worldwide and accounting for about 11 million deaths [1]. Despite advances in critical care medicine, the mortality rate of sepsis remains high, ranging from 25 to 30% for sepsis and reaching up to 40–50% for septic shock [1–3].

Electrolyte disorders, particularly dyskalemia, are common complications in critically ill patients with sepsis and are associated with increased mortality [4–7]. Several studies have demonstrated a U-shaped relationship between serum potassium levels and mortality in ICU patients, with both hypokalemia and hyperkalemia contributing to adverse outcomes [4, 8]. This relationship is particularly important as potassium disorders are potentially modifiable risk factors for mortality, as serum potassium levels can be readily monitored and corrected [8–10].

Elderly patients (aged ≥ 65 years) represent a growing proportion of ICU admissions [11, 12], accounting for approximately 65% of sepsis cases [13]. This population is particularly vulnerable to poor outcomes, with mortality rates more than twice that of younger adult sepsis [13]. Age-related changes in physiology, multiple comorbidities, and polypharmacy might make elderly patients more susceptible to electrolyte disturbances, especially hyperkalemia [14–20]. Notably, elderly adults demonstrate a significantly higher prevalence of hyperkalemia compared to younger populations [14]. This increased risk is attributed to multiple factors, including reduced renal function, comorbidities such as diabetes and chronic heart failure(CHF), and medications that affect potassium homeostasis [20–24]. However, the specific relationship between serum potassium levels and mortality in elderly septic patients in the ICU remains incompletely characterized, as most large-scale ICU studies have either focused on general populations without age-specific analyses, or have excluded elderly patients due to their complex comorbidities.

Therefore, we conducted this study to investigate the association between serum potassium levels and 28-day ICU mortality among elderly patients with sepsis, aiming to identify optimal potassium ranges for this vulnerable population. Tables 1, 2 and 3.

Methods

Data source and population

This study utilized data from the eICU Collaborative Research Database (version 2.0), a multicenter intensive care unit (ICU) database containing detailed clinical information from 200,859 ICU admissions across 208 hospitals in the United States during 2014–2015 [25]. The database includes comprehensive clinical data such as demographics, vital signs, laboratory measurements, severity scores, diagnoses, and therapeutic interventions.

We identified elderly patients (aged ≥ 65 years) with sepsis for inclusion.The diagnosis of sepsis in this study was based on the administrative coding recorded in the eICU Collaborative Research Database. Specifically, we identified sepsis cases using the information from the admissionDx table, which contains diagnoses recorded from the care plan or notes within the first 36 h of ICU admission. The most appropriate diagnosis code was selected by clinicians who were trained in APACHE methodology, following standardized rules. Patients with missing serum potassium values within 24 h of admission were excluded (Fig. 1). The final cohort comprised 12,069 patients who were categorized into three groups based on their admission serum potassium levels: hypokalemia (< 3.5 mmol/L, n = 1976), normokalemia (3.5–5.0 mmol/L, n = 8834), and hyperkalemia (> 5.0 mmol/L, n = 1259). For sensitivity analyses, we further refined the categorization into five groups: ≤ 3.5, > 3.5 to ≤ 4.0, > 4.0 to ≤ 4.5, > 4.5 to ≤ 5.0, and > 5.0 mmol/L.

Flow chart of participants selection

Variable extraction

We extracted baseline characteristics within 24 h of ICU admission, including: demographics: age, sex, and ethnicity; comorbidities: acute kidney injury (AKI), acute myocardial infarction (AMI), congestive heart failure (CHF), chronic obstructive pulmonary disease (COPD), diabetes, pneumonia, cardiac arrhythmia, cirrhosis, hepatic failure, lymphoma, leukemia, and metastatic cancer; clinical characteristics: body mass index (BMI) and Sequential Organ Failure Assessment (SOFA) score; laboratory parameters: serum potassium, serum creatinine, blood urea nitrogen(BUN), pH, serum sodium, serum calcium, serum albumin, low-density lipoprotein cholesterol (LDLc), total cholesterol; treatment measures: mechanical ventilation, vasopressor use and dialysis. We calculated estimated glomerular filtration rate (eGFR) using the Chronic Kidney Disease Epidemiology Collaboration (CKD-EPI) equation based on age, gender, race, and serum creatinine [26].

Primary outcome

The primary outcome was defined as all-cause 28-day ICU mortality.

Statistical analysis

Continuous variables were presented as mean (standard deviation), and categorical variables as frequencies (percentages). Between-group differences were assessed using the Kruskal–Wallis test for continuous variables and χ2 test or Fisher exact test for categorical variables. The association between serum potassium levels and 28-day ICU mortality was evaluated using multivariate logistic regression models, with sequential adjustment for potential confounders: Model 1: unadjusted; Model 2: adjusted for age and sex; Model 3: adjusted for age, sex, and serum creatinine; Model 4: fully adjusted for demographics, comorbidities (AKI, CHF, diabetes, pneumonia, metastatic cancer), clinical characteristics (BMI, SOFA score), laboratory values(BUN, serum sodium, serum calcium, serum albumin, pH), and treatment measures(mechanical ventilation, dialysis). Stratified analyses were performed to assess the consistency of associations across prespecified subgroups, including age categories, sex, comorbidities, clinical characteristics, laboratory parameters, and treatment interventions. Results were presented as odds ratios (ORs) with 95% confidence intervals (CIs). The potential non-linear relationship between serum potassium and 28-day ICU mortality was investigated using generalized additive models with smooth curve fitting (adjusted for the same covariates as in model 3). As a sensitivity analysis, we also conducted restricted cubic spline analyses with 3 and 4 knots to further assess potential non-linearity. Additionally, we constructed two additional models incorporating eGFR as an alternative measure of kidney function: Model 4 (adjusted for age, sex, and eGFR) and Model 5 (fully adjusted model with eGFR replacing serum creatinine, while maintaining all other covariates from Model 3).The statistical analysis was conducted using EmpowerStats (V4.2, www.empowerstats.com) and R (V3.4.3, http://www.R-project.org). A two-sided P-value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

Among 12,069 elderly patients with sepsis (mean [SD] age, 77.0 [7.6] years; 6105 [50.6%] women) admitted to the ICU, 1976 (16.4%) had hypokalemia (serum potassium levels < 3.5 mmol/L), 8834 (73.2%) had normokalemia (serum potassium levels of 3.5–5.0 mmol/L), and 1259 (10.4%) had hyperkalemia levels (serum potassium levels > 5.0 mmol/L). The majority of patients were Caucasian (81.1%−82.4%) across all groups (Table 1).

Significant differences were observed in baseline characteristics across potassium groups. Patients with hyperkalemia (> 5.0 mmol/L) were more likely to be female (55.28% vs 52.03% vs 41.22%; P < 0.001) and had higher prevalence of comorbidities including CHF (12.55% vs 9.97% vs 7.54%; P < 0.001), AKI (39.08% vs 22.33% vs 19.18%; P < 0.001), and diabetes (32.35% vs 26.18% vs 23.41%; P < 0.001). The hyperkalemic group also demonstrated greater disease severity, with higher mean (SD) SOFA scores (5.68 [2.99] vs 4.18 [2.83] vs 3.92 [2.77]; P < 0.001). Laboratory values showed significant differences, particularly in renal function parameters, with higher mean serum creatinine (3.14 vs 1.80 vs 1.46 mg/dL; P < 0.001), BUN (58.62 vs 36.26 vs 30.08 mg/dL; P < 0.001) and lower eGFR (26.74 vs 48.56 vs 60.90 mL/min/1.73m2; P < 0.001) in the hyperkalemic group. Regarding clinical interventions, patients with hyperkalemia required more intensive support, with higher rates of mechanical ventilation (33.50% vs 25.84% vs 26.48%, P < 0.001) and dialysis (7.33% vs 4.40% vs 3.28%, P < 0.001). The 28-day ICU mortality rate was 11.5% in elderly patients with sepsis, showing a significant association with serum potassium levels, being highest in the hyperkalemic group (23.43% vs 10.12% vs 9.77%; P < 0.001). In our sensitivity analysis with five refined potassium categories (Table S1), similar trends were observed. Across ascending potassium groups, we found progressive increases in serum creatinine, BUN, SOFA scores, and declining pH values. The proportion of patients with CHF, AKI, and diabetes consistently increased across higher potassium categories.

In the univariate analysis (Table 2), serum potassium was significantly associated with increased mortality risk (OR 1.65, 95% CI 1.54–1.77, P < 0.001). In the stratified analysis, we examined the association between serum potassium levels (per 1 mmol/L increase) and 28-day ICU mortality across various subgroups. The association remained significant across most predefined subgroups. The association between serum potassium levels and mortality was consistent across age strata, with slightly stronger associations observed in elderly patients (OR, 1.79 [95% CI, 1.59–2.03] for age > 80 years vs 1.54 [95% CI, 1.34–1.76] for age ≤ 70 years). The relationship was similar between female (OR, 1.70 [95% CI, 1.54–1.87]) and male patients (OR, 1.61 [95% CI, 1.46–1.78]). The association persisted across various comorbidity subgroups, including patients with and without CHF (OR, 1.53 [95% CI, 1.24–1.88] and 1.67 [95% CI, 1.55–1.79], respectively), AKI(OR, 1.40 [95% CI, 1.25–1.56] and 1.71 [95% CI, 1.56–1.87], respectively), and diabetes (OR, 1.54 [95% CI, 1.35–1.77] and 1.71 [95% CI, 1.56–1.85], respectively). The relationship between serum potassium and mortality was consistent across different SOFA score categories. The association persisted across most laboratory parameters, with varying strengths of association. Renal function demonstrated particularly notable differences: patients with normal serum creatinine (≤ 1 mg/dL) showed no significant association (OR, 0.96 [95% CI, 0.75–1.22]; P = 0.723), whereas those with moderate (1–2 mg/dL) and severe (> 2 mg/dL) renal dysfunction demonstrated significant associations (OR, 1.63 [95% CI, 1.43–1.86] and OR, 1.38 [95% CI, 1.25–1.51], respectively). Similarly, when stratified by eGFR categories, patients with eGFR ≥ 60 mL/min/1.73m2 showed no significant association (eGFR ≥ 60: OR, 1.13 [95% CI, 0.90–1.41], P = 0.301, while those with eGFR < 60 mL/min/1.73m2 demonstrated significant associations (eGFR < 60, ≥ 45: OR, 1.50 [95% CI, 1.13–1.97], P = 0.005); eGFR < 45, ≥ 30: OR, 1.69 [95% CI, 1.41–2.02], P < 0.001; eGFR < 30: OR, 1.39 [95% CI, 1.27–1.52], P < 0.001).The association remained significant regardless of mechanical ventilation status but was not statistically significant among patients receiving dialysis (OR, 1.32 [95% CI, 0.97–1.80]; P = 0.080), possibly due to the smaller sample size in this subgroup (n = 522).

In multivariable analyses, serum potassium demonstrated a significant association with 28-day ICU mortality (Table 3). When analyzed as a continuous variable, each 1 mmol/L increase in serum potassium was associated with higher odds of mortality in the unadjusted model (OR, 1.65 [95% CI, 1.54–1.77]; P < 0.001). This association persisted after adjusting for age and sex (OR, 1.66 [95% CI, 1.55–1.77]; P < 0.001) and further adjustment for serum creatinine (OR, 1.52 [95% CI, 1.41–1.63]; P < 0.001). In the fully adjusted model, which accounted for demographics, comorbidities, disease severity, and clinical interventions, the association remained significant although attenuated (OR, 1.23 [95% CI, 1.09–1.39]; P = 0.001). When categorizing serum potassium levels, patients with hyperkalemia (> 5.0 mmol/L) showed substantially higher mortality risk compared with those with normal potassium levels (3.5–5.0 mmol/L). This relationship was robust across all adjustment models, with OR of 2.72 (95% CI, 2.34–3.15) in the unadjusted model and 1.45 (95% CI, 1.11–1.88) in the fully adjusted model (P = 0.007). Notably, patients with hypokalemia (< 3.5 mmol/L) did not show significantly different mortality risk compared with the reference group across all models. The ordinal analysis of serum potassium categories demonstrated a significant trend, with each categorical increase in potassium level associated with higher mortality risk (fully adjusted OR, 1.25 [95% CI, 1.04–1.50]; P = 0.020). In our sensitivity analysis with refined potassium categories(≤ 3.5, > 3.5 to ≤ 4.0, > 4.0 to ≤ 4.5, > 4.5 to ≤ 5.0, and > 5.0 mmol/L), multivariable analysis shows that when analyzing these five categorized serum potassium groups as an ordinal variable, each incremental category increase was associated with a 12% higher risk of death in the fully adjusted model (OR 1.12, 95% CI 1.04–1.21, P = 0.004)(Table S2). We conducted additional sensitivity analyses incorporating eGFR as an alternative measure of kidney function in Models 4 and 5 (Table S3). In Model 4, adjusted for age, sex, and eGFR, each 1 mmol/L increase in serum potassium was associated with a 39% increased risk of 28-day ICU mortality (OR 1.39, 95% CI 1.29–1.50, P < 0.001). In the fully adjusted Model 5 (with eGFR replacing serum creatinine along with all other covariates), the association remained significant but attenuated, with each 1 mmol/L increase corresponding to a 21% increased mortality risk (OR 1.21, 95% CI 1.07–1.37, P = 0.003).

Analysis using a generalized additive model revealed a significant linear association between serum potassium and 28-day ICU mortality after multivariable adjustment (χ2 = 10.73; P = 0.001)(Fig. 2). This finding was confirmed by restricted cubic spline analyses with both 3 knots (overall: χ2 = 11.97, P = 0.003; non-linearity: χ2 = 0.32, P = 0.574)(Figure S1) and 4 knots (overall: χ2 = 12.42, P = 0.006; non-linearity: χ2 = 0.79, P = 0.675)(Figure S2), where the non-significant tests for non-linearity further supported a predominantly linear relationship. Additionally, the smooth curve fitting analysis using a fully adjusted model with eGFR replacing serum creatinine also demonstrated a statistically significant association between serum potassium levels and 28-day ICU mortality (χ2 = 9.013, P = 0.003) (FigureS3). Furthermore, to assess robustness, restricted cubic spline analyses with eGFR replacing serum creatinine confirmed significant associations between serum potassium and 28-day ICU mortality using both 3 knots (P-overall = 0.006, P-nonlinear = 0.562) (Figure S4) and 4 knots (P-overall = 0.015, P-nonlinear = 0.742) (Figure S5).

Discussion

In this large multicenter study of 12,069 elderly patients with sepsis, we found a significant linear association between serum potassium levels and 28-day ICU mortality, which differs notably from the U-shaped relationship previously reported in general ICU or septic ICU populations. This relationship persisted after comprehensive adjustment for potential confounders and demonstrated consistency across most patient subgroups.

Our study revealed several key findings. First, both hypokalemia (< 3.5 mmol/L) and hyperkalemia (> 5.0 mmol/L) were common among elderly septic patients, affecting 16.4% and 10.4% of the cohort, respectively. Second, and most importantly, we observed a linear relationship between serum potassium and mortality risk, contrasting with the U-shaped association consistently reported in previous studies of general ICU populations. This linear relationship was confirmed through generalized additive model analysis and restricted cubic spline models and remained robust after adjusting for multiple confounders. Third, the association between serum potassium levels and mortality was particularly pronounced in patients older than 80 years and those with impaired renal function.

Our findings both complement and contrast with existing literature. Hessels et al. [8] reported a U-shaped relationship between potassium and mortality in a general ICU population, with both hypo- and hyperkalemia contributing equally to mortality risk. Similarly, Tang et al. [27] and Zhao et al. [28] demonstrated a U-shaped association in septic patients across all age groups. The linear relationship we observed in elderly septic patients suggests that age-specific physiological changes may fundamentally alter the impact of dyskalemia on outcomes.

The stronger association between hyperkalemia and mortality in our elderly septic cohort aligns with previous findings [18, 29] in elderly patients with AKI, suggesting that older adults may be particularly vulnerable to the adverse effects of elevated serum potassium levels. Similarly, de Rooij et al. [30] demonstrated in their study of elderly CKD patients that both hypo- and hyperkalemia were associated with increased mortality risk, with hyperkalemia (> 5.5 mmol/L) showing a particularly strong association (HR: 1.8–2.2). Our study extends these findings specifically to the septic population, where inflammatory responses and organ dysfunction may further complicate potassium homeostasis [30].

Several mechanisms may explain the linear relationship observed in elderly septic patients. First, age-related decline in renal function and reduced potassium adaptive capacity may impair the ability to handle potassium loads effectively [15]. Second, the high prevalence of comorbidities in elderly patients, particularly chronic kidney disease (CKD), diabetes, and CHF, has been well-documented as independent risk factors for hyperkalemia [31]. Third, the high prevalence of polypharmacy in elderly septic patients, particularly medications affecting the renin–angiotensin–aldosterone system, may create a physiological environment more susceptible to potassium-related complications [24, 32–36].

Our findings have important implications for clinical practice. The linear relationship between serum potassium and mortality suggests that traditional approaches to serum potassium management, which often focus equally on preventing both hypo- and hyperkalemia, may need modification in elderly septic patients. While maintaining normal potassium levels remains important, our results suggest that preventing hyperkalemia should be a primary focus in this population. This may influence several aspects of care: more frequent monitoring of potassium levels in elderly septic patients; earlier intervention when potassium levels begin to rise; careful consideration of medications affecting potassium homeostasis and potentially different target ranges for potassium replacement therapy.

This study’s strengths include its large sample size, multicenter design, and comprehensive adjustment for confounders. However, several limitations should be acknowledged. First, as an observational study, we cannot establish causality. Second, our study analyzed only admission potassium levels rather than serial measurements. We recognize that potassium levels can fluctuate significantly during sepsis and ICU stay due to disease progression, therapeutic interventions, and metabolic changes. This approach may introduce misclassification bias, particularly in patients whose potassium levels changed substantially after admission. Future studies incorporating time-series analysis of potassium levels would provide valuable insights into the dynamic relationship between potassium fluctuations and mortality risk in elderly septic patients. Third, our cohort had relatively few patients with extreme potassium values-only 0.31% had levels below 2.5 mmol/L and just 0.34% had levels above 7.0 mmol/L-which may have limited our statistical power to detect non-linear effects in these ranges. While our generalized additive models and restricted cubic spline models offer flexibility, they may not fully capture complex relationships when observations at distribution extremes are sparse. Additionally, the database lacked direct eGFR measurements, requiring us to calculate values using the CKD-EPI equation, and these calculations may have reduced accuracy in sepsis settings where creatinine levels fluctuate rapidly and do not reflect steady-state conditions assumed by the formula. Our eGFR-stratified analyses should therefore be interpreted cautiously in the critical care context. Another key limitation of our study is the lack of information on medications that affect potassium homeostasis, particularly renin–angiotensin–aldosterone system inhibitors, potassium-sparing diuretics, and insulin. These medications are commonly prescribed in elderly patients with conditions such as heart failure, diabetes, and CKD, and can significantly influence serum potassium levels [37–39]. We also recognize that our data from 2014–2015 may not fully reflect current clinical practice, particularly given the introduction of newer medications for heart failure management, such as angiotensin receptor-neprilysin inhibitors (ARNI), sodium-glucose cotransporter-2 (SGLT-2) inhibitors, and third-generation mineralocorticoid receptor antagonists (MRAs), which have become more widely used since our study period [40–44]. These medications can impact potassium homeostasis and may influence the relationship between potassium levels and outcomes in contemporary elderly septic patients. Future studies should investigate whether serum potassium management strategies based on this linear relationship improve outcomes in elderly septic patients. Research is also needed to determine optimal potassium targets specifically for this population and to evaluate the impact of various management approaches on clinical outcomes.

Conclusions

In elderly patients with sepsis, serum potassium levels demonstrate a linear relationship with ICU mortality, contrasting with the U-shaped relationship observed in general ICU populations. This finding suggests the need for age-specific approaches to potassium management in sepsis, with particular attention to preventing hyperkalemia. These results provide a foundation for developing targeted management strategies for this vulnerable population.
