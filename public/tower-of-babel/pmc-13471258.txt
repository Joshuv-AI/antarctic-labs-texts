The association between triglyceride glucose-frailty index and cardiometabolic multimorbidity among Chinese middle-aged and older adults: a national prospective cohort study
Authors: Xinjiang Hou, Guaijuan Wang, Yan Gao
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundCardiometabolic multimorbidity (CMM) poses a growing global health burden, yet few studies have combined the Triglyceride–Glucose (TyG) index, which reflects metabolic dysfunction, with the Frailty Index (FI), which captures physiological reserve and aging-related vulnerability, to assess CMM risk. Given their complementary biological information, this study examines whether a composite TyG-FI index is associated with incident CMM and whether it improves risk stratification beyond established factors.MethodsThis prospective cohort study analyzed data from Chinese adults aged ≥ 45 years in the 2011–2020 waves of the China Health and Retirement Longitudinal Study (CHARLS). To assess the association between the TyG-FI index and incident CMM, we used Kaplan–Meier survival curves and multivariable Cox proportional-hazards models adjusted for potential confounders; restricted cubic spline analyses were employed to explore non-linear relationships. Predictive performance was evaluated using eight machine-learning algorithms: CatBoost, Extra Trees, Random Forest (RANGER), XGBoost, Recursive Partitioning (RPART), k-Nearest Neighbors (KKNN), Neural Network (NNET), and Support Vector Machine (SVM). Subgroup and sensitivity analyses were conducted to test the robustness of the results across population subgroups and modeling choices.ResultsThe analytic cohort comprised 2961 adults. Kaplan–Meier curves showed a graded, significant increase in cumulative CMM incidence across TyG‑FI quartiles (log‑rank P < 0.001). In multivariable Cox models, each unit increase in TyG‑FI was associated with a 1.80-fold higher CMM risk (HR = 1.80, 95% CI 1.57–2.05; P < 0.001); participants in the highest quartile had markedly elevated risk versus the lowest (Q4 vs. Q1 HR = 7.86, 95% CI 4.16–14.86). Restricted cubic spline analyses revealed significant non-linear relationships (P for non-linearity < 0.001), showing a J-shaped association between TyG-FI and CMM with threshold effects at TyG-FI ≈ 0.7 and cumulative TyG-FI ≈ 2.7. Subgroup analyses indicated stronger associations in participants < 60 years and in normotensive individuals. TyG-FI demonstrated better predictive performance for CMM than TyG index or FI alone, with improved C-statistic, Integrated Discrimination Improvement (IDI), and Net Reclassification Improvement (NRI). Among machine-learning models, RANGER performed best (AUC ≈ 0.81), and SHAP analysis identified cumulative and baseline TyG-FI as the primary predictors. Findings were robust in sensitivity analyses.ConclusionsTyG-FI exhibits non‑linear, threshold-defined associations with incident CMM and age‑dependent effect modification. Machine‑learning models incorporating TyG-FI show strong predictive performance. TyG-FI assessment may facilitate cost‑effective risk stratification for CMM and guide targeted prevention. Background

Cardiometabolic multimorbidity (CMM), defined as the co-occurrence of two or more cardiometabolic disorders including coronary heart disease, stroke, and type 2 diabetes, represents an escalating global public health challenge, particularly within aging populations [1]. Increasing epidemiological evidence demonstrates that CMM is strongly associated with elevated risks of all-cause mortality, impaired activities of daily living, and poor long-term prognosis, underscoring its clinical and societal significance [2]. China, recent population-based studies have documented a considerable prevalence of CMM, ranging from 5.94 to 16.9%, highlighting the urgent need for effective preventive strategies [3].

Traditionally, cardiometabolic diseases have been largely evaluated and managed as isolated conditions [4–6], despite their tendency to cluster and share common pathogenic mechanisms [7–10]. Consequently, there is a compelling need for integrated risk assessment approaches that simultaneously capture core metabolic disturbances and age-related physiological vulnerability [11–13]. Meanwhile,early and accurate identification of individuals at high risk of CMM remains critical for targeted intervention and alleviating the expanding healthcare burden [14].

As a robust surrogate marker of insulin resistance (IR), the triglyceride-glucose (TyG) index—derived from fasting triglycerides and glucose [15]—offers three distinct clinical advantages: simple computation enabling large-scale screening, broad predictive utility across cardiovascular, metabolic, pulmonary, and renal diseases [16–21], and enhanced prognostic stratification for personalized management [18, 22–24]. However, population-specific variability in its predictive thresholds and effect magnitudes across different ethnic, age, and clinical subgroups necessitates complementary use with other clinical indicators [25, 26].

The frailty index (FI), a well-established and validated measure of biological ageing, quantifies health status through the accumulation of deficits across physical function, comorbidities, cognition, and psychological condition [27]. It reflects diminished physiological reserve and heightened vulnerability to stressors, and is typically constructed using 10-step procedure, which ensures consistency in variable selection, coding, and index development [12, 28]. The FI is independently associated with diverse adverse outcomes, including cardiovascular disease (CVD), stroke, falls, cognitive decline, and all-cause mortality [29–31]. Owing to its high prevalence among older adults and predictive utility for adverse health outcomes, the FI constitutes an integral component of geriatric risk modelling [32]. However, the FI has demonstrated limited accuracy for predicting short-term outcomes and can be prone to inconsistent results due to variable-weighting methods; these limitations should be considered when applying the FI in clinical or research settings [33, 34].

Theoretically, frailty and cardiometabolic diseases are highly prevalent and frequently coexist in older populations, exacerbating each other’s adverse effects. Patients with CMM are more prone to developing frailty, and frailty in turn accelerates the progression of CMM [12, 35–38]. So, the TyG plus FI provides a comprehensive assessment of multisystem physiological reserve depletion. Clinically, these two indicators capture frailty and cardiovascular risk from the distinct perspectives of metabolic drivers and systemic vulnerability, respectively. The combined TyG-Frailty Index (TyG-FI) significantly improves predictive accuracy for chronic diseases by integrating metabolic insulin resistance (TyG index) and physiological decline (frailty). Compared to individual indicators, TyG-FI better identifies high-risk individuals, as TyG and frailty frequently coexist and synergistically elevate risks in middle-aged and older adults [17, 39]. For cardiovascular diseases and stroke, TyG-FI enhances discrimination and reclassification of incident events beyond traditional models, reducing heterogeneity in risk assessment [40, 41]. In metabolic disorders like Cardiovascular-Kidney-Metabolic syndrome, it predicts frailty progression and adverse outcomes more effectively, accounting for chronic inflammation and dysregulated metabolism [42]. These findings indicate that the combined TyG index-FI composite indicator exhibits clear advantages in predicting cardiovascular and metabolic diseases among middle-aged and older populations. Such merits effectively compensate for the limitations of traditional indicators used in prediction models and current clinical practice for CMM, a typical age-related disorder [38, 43, 44]. Conventional CMM risk models focus primarily on isolated metabolic or cardiovascular risk factors (e.g., glucose, lipids, blood pressure, and comorbidities), while neglecting physiological resilience and frailty-related deficits that are highly prevalent in the elderly and strongly modulate cardiometabolic risk [12, 37, 38, 45]. They also show insufficient sensitivity to subclinical metabolic dysfunction and mild frailty, leading to inadequate risk stratification [46–48].

Although the TyG index and FI each reflect insulin resistance and physiological vulnerability respectively, both have inherent limitations when used alone. By integrating these two complementary components, the TyG‑FI composite index achieves more accurate and reliable risk evaluation for CMM in clinical settings.

Despite theoretical progress, empirical evidence linking the TyG-FI index to CMM remains limited, especially in Chinese populations. Leveraging the China Health and Retirement Longitudinal Study (CHARLS), this prospective investigation examines the longitudinal associations between TyG-FI and incident CMM. By integrating metabolic and functional biomarkers, this study bridges a critical knowledge gap to enhance CMM risk stratification and guide precision prevention strategies.

Methods

Data source and study population

The participants of this study were middle-aged and older adults enrolled in the CHARLS, a prospective national cohort study conducted in China. The CHARLS initiated its national baseline survey (Wave 1) during June 2011–March 2012, enrolling 17,708 participants across 28 provinces. Participants underwent biennial follow-ups through 2013–2020: Wave 2 (2013–2014), Wave 3 (2015–2016), Wave 4 (2017–2018), and Wave 5 (2019–2020). A comprehensive account of the study’s design and the methodology of data collection for the CHARLS has been previously documented [49]. The data set is accessible for download at http://CHARLS.pku.edu.cn/en. The CHARLS survey was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki and had received approval from the BU Institutional Review Board (IRB 00001052–11015). Informed consent was obtained in writing from all participants prior to their enrollment in the study. CHARLS field staff received rigorous professional training and used a standardized questionnaire during face to-face interviews. This study defined the 2011–2012 cohort as baseline, with longitudinal follow-up at three timepoints: 2015, 2018, and 2020.

Assessment of TyG-FI

The TyG index was calculated using the formula: Ln [triglycerides (mg/dl) × glucose (mg/dl)/2] [50]. In the CHARLS cohort, a 30-item FI (Table S1) was constructed following the deficit accumulation model, incorporating variables across core domains chronic conditions, physical functioning, depressive symptoms, and cognitive performance, in line with established protocols and publications [51, 52].

The combined TyG and FI was constructed using a multiplicative model: TyG-FI = TyG × FI, as previously proposed in epidemiological analyses where product-based terms are used to reflect potential interaction effects between metabolic and functional deficits [39].

The cumulative TyG-IF, as an area under the curve estimate (mean TyG × time span), was calculated as (TyG-IF2012 + TyG-IF2015)/2* time (2015–2012) [53].

Assessment of CMM

The primary endpoint of this study was the occurrence of CMM events, characterized by the simultaneous presence of at least two CMDs, including heart disease, stroke, and diabetes [54]. The diagnosis of heart disease and stroke is based on self-reported data or related medication use collected at baseline and follow-up surveys. A diagnosis of diabetes was made if any of the following were met: (1) self-reported diagnosis of diabetes (“Have you been diagnosed with diabetes or hyperglycemia by your doctor?”); (2) current use of glucose-lowering medications; (3) FBG ≥ 126 mg/dL; or (4) hemoglobin A1c (HbA1c) ≥ 6.5%. Our CMM diagnostic criteria are consistent with those used in previous literature based on CHARLS data [53].

Covariates

In this study, the following five main components were included as covariates: (1) Demographic data: baseline age (years, continue variable), sex (female and male), marital status (married and others), educational level (illiteracy, middle school below and middle school and above), residence (rural village and urban community). (2) Lifestyle data: smoking status (never and others), Drinking status (current and others). (3) Disease history (yes or no): hypertension, dyslipidemia. (4) Body measures: systolic blood pressure (SBP), diastolic blood pressure (DBP). (5) Laboratory examination data: total cholesterol (TC), high-density lipoprotein cholesterol (HDL-C), and low-density lipoprotein cholesterol (LDL-C)). Hypertension was identified based on SBP ≥ 140 mmHg or DBP ≥ 90 mmHg or a history of hypertension, or the use of antihypertensive medications [53]. Dyslipidemia was determined by self-reported dyslipidemia or the use of lipid-lowering therapies.

Handling of missing data and data preprocessing

Because triglycerides, glucose, FI, and CMM were outcome and exposure variables, we performed listwise deletion of missing data for these variables.The degree of missing data in this study was detailed in Table S2. Before formal analyses, multicollinearity assessment was performed for all study variables. Variables with significant collinearity were defined as GVIF(1/2DF) ≥ 2, where GVIF denotes generalized variance inflation factor and DF indicates degrees of freedom (Table S3). Additionally, to reduce bias resulting from missing variables, we used multiple imputation by chained equations (MICE) to address the missing data. During data preprocessing, extreme values were detected in TyG and FI indices [54, 55].

Statistical analyses

Adjusted Kaplan–Meier curves estimated the cumulative incidence of CMM across TyG-FI quartiles (Q1–Q4) over a 9-year follow-up. Between-quartile differences were assessed using the log-rank test. Survival estimates were adjusted for potential baseline confounders by deriving model-based adjusted survival functions from multivariable Cox regression; numbers at risk are reported at 0, 3, 6and 9 years.

Cox proportional hazards models evaluated the association between TyG-FI (analyzed continuously and in quartiles, with Q1 as reference) and CMM, expressed as hazard ratios (HR) with 95% confidence intervals (CI). Trend tests assessed potential dose–response relationships. Four hierarchical models were constructed: Model 1: Unadjusted; Model 2: Adjusted for sociodemographic and lifestyle factors (age, gender, marital status, education, residence, smoking, drinking, BMI); Model 3: Model 2 + hypertension and dyslipidemia; Model 4: Model 3 + SBP, DBP, TC, HDL-C, LDL-C.Proportional hazards assumptions were verified. Restricted cubic splines (RCS) with four knots (placed at the 10th, 50th, and 90th percentiles) assessed potential non-linear relationships between TyG-FI, cumulative TyG-FI, and CMM risk, adjusting for Model 4 covariates. Schoenfeld residuals validated the proportional hazards assumption.

Threshold effect analysis using piecewise linear regression models identified potential inflection points in the TyG-FI/CMM association, with knot positions determined empirically via likelihood ratio tests. Non-linearity was formally assessed using quadratic terms and cubic splines, adjusting for all Model 4 covariates.

Stratified subgroup analyses were performed to evaluate the robustness of the associations between TyG-FI, cumulative TyG-FI, and incident cardiometabolic multimorbidity (CMM) across predefined population strata, including age (< 60 vs. ≥ 60 years), gender, BMI (< 25 vs. ≥ 25 kg/m2), marital status, smoking status, drinking status, hypertension, and dyslipidaemia. Multivariable Cox proportional hazards models, adjusted for the same set of covariates as the primary analysis, were applied to estimate adjusted hazard ratios (Adj HRs) with 95% confidence intervals (CIs) within each subgroup. Interaction terms between the exposure variables (TyG-FI/cumulative TyG-FI) and each stratifying factor were introduced into the models to test for effect modification.

To assess effect modification by age on the associations of TyG-FI and cumulative TyG-FI with CMM, penalized cubic spline models were constructed to estimate adjusted log-hazard ratios (logHR) across age strata. Interaction terms were incorporated into multivariable Cox regression to quantify statistical heterogeneity. All models were adjusted for sociodemographic, lifestyle, clinical, and biochemical covariates, with 95% confidence bands used to evaluate precision.

Incremental predictive performance for CMM was evaluated by comparing a prespecified baseline multivariable model (adjusted for age, sex, marital status, education, residence, smoking, alcohol consumption, hypertension, dyslipidemia, BMI, SBP, DBP, HDL, LDL, TC) with sequential models adding the FI, TyG index, their combination (TyG-FI), and cumulative TyG-FI, respectively. Discriminative ability was quantified via receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curve analysis and C-statistics (area under the curve, AUC) with 95% confidence intervals (CIs), and between-model differences were formally tested. Continuous net reclassification improvement (NRI) and integrated discrimination improvement (IDI) were calculated to assess reclassification and incremental discrimination, with 95% CIs derived via resampling.

E-values were computed to quantify the minimum strength of unmeasured confounding required to nullify observed associations between TyG-FI metrics and CMM risk, assuming balanced confounding effects,using fully adjusted Model 4 estimates. As an exploratory analysis, we performed mediation analyses using bootstrapping (1000 resamples) to test whether CRP or UA mediated the association between TyG-FI and CMM, adjusting for all covariates.

Continuous variable normality was assessed via visual inspection (histograms) and the Shapiro–Wilk test. Normally distributed data are expressed as mean ± SD and compared across quartiles using one-way ANOVA. Non-normally distributed continuous variables are expressed as median (IQR) and compared using the Kruskal–Wallis test. Categorical variables are expressed as n (%) and compared using the χ2 test.

Machine learning

The dataset was randomly partitioned into a training set (67%) and a test set (33%) using stratified sampling. Continuous variable normality was assessed using appropriate statistical tests. Normally distributed variables are expressed as mean ± standard deviation and compared using independent-samples t-tests. Non-normally distributed variables are presented as median (interquartile range) and compared using the Mann–Whitney U test. Categorical variables are reported as frequencies (percentages) and compared using either the chi-square test or Fisher's exact test, as appropriate. All statistical tests were two-tailed, with statistical significance defined as P < 0.05. Prior to modelling, candidate features underwent preprocessing to enhance model performance and stability. Missing values were imputed using the Random Forest algorithm. For categorical features with more than two levels, one-hot encoding was applied for numerical conversion. Continuous features exhibiting significant outliers were discretised to mitigate their influence. Feature preprocessing was integrated within pipeline functions alongside algorithms to ensure consistent processing of the validation set. Eight diverse machine learning algorithms were implemented for comprehensive comparative analysis: EXTRATREES, CATBOOST, RANGER (Random Forest), XGBOOST, RPART, KKNN SVM and NNET. This ensemble incorporated tree-based methods, ensemble techniques, linear models, and probabilistic approaches, enabling multi-dimensional analysis and robust performance evaluation. Model performance was assessed using multiple metrics: Classification Metrics: sensitivity, specificity, F1-score, and overall accuracy evaluated classification performance. Discriminative Capability: the area under the receiver operating characteristic curve (AUROC) assessed discrimination across thresholds. The area under the precision-recall curve (AUPRC) specifically evaluated positive case identification, crucial for imbalanced data. Model Calibration: calibration curves assessed the agreement between predicted probabilities and observed outcomes, ensuring reliable probability estimates. To ensure fairness in algorithm comparison, the RANGER model was selected as optimal for subsequent optimisation and implementation based on benchmark performance. A dual validation strategy was used to mitigate overfitting. Stratified five-fold cross-validation was first applied within the training set by dividing the data into five subsets and averaging performance metrics across folds where each fold served once as the test set and four times for training. Subsequently, independent test set validation was performed by evaluating the optimised model on the held-out test set to assess its generalisability. To investigate individual feature contributions to CMM risk prediction, Kernel SHAP was employed to compute SHAP values from the RANGER model [56, 57]. These values quantify feature contributions within the model's additive framework. Visualisation utilised the R package shapviz (v.0.9.6). SHAP facilitates both local and global interpretability; we leveraged it to explain the predictive model encompassing relevant CMM risk factors. Feature importance rankings within the optimal model were calculated to quantify each feature's contribution to prediction.

All analyses were performed using R Statistical Software (Version 4.2.2, http://www.R-project.org, The R Foundation) and Free Statistics analysis platform (Version 2.4 Beijing, China). A two-sided P value of < 0.05 was considered to represent statistical significance.

Results

This study initially enrolled 11,847 individuals undergoing blood collection. First, we excluded participants with missing data on triglycerides (TG), fasting blood glucose (FBG), FI at Wave 1 or Wave 3. Next, we excluded participants younger than 45 years old. Then, we excluded individuals who had CMM or lacked CMM-related information before Wave 3 (2015). Ultimately, the study included a total of 2961participants (Fig. 1).

Baseline characteristics of participants

Table 1 summarized the baseline characteristics of 3309 participants stratified by TyG-FI quartiles. Significant differences (P < 0.05) were observed across quartiles for most variables. Participants in higher TyG-FI quartiles (Q3–Q4) were older, more likely to be female, less educated, and resided in urban areas. Metabolic parameters including BMI, systolic/diastolic blood pressure, fasting glucose (Glu), triglycerides (TG), HbA1c, and TyG index exhibited progressively higher values from Q1 to Q4. Hypertension and dyslipidemia prevalence increased significantly with higher TyG-FI (P < 0.001). Notably, CMM incidence was elevated in Q4 (2.7%) compared to Q1 (0%). No significant differences were observed for HDL-C, LDL-C, or creatinine (P > 0.05). These findings suggest TyG-FI quartiles reflect distinct CMM risk profiles (Fig. 2).

Associations between TyG-FI and incident CMM

Adjusted Kaplan–Meier curves showed a clear, graded increase in cumulative incidence with higher TyG‑FI quartiles (log‑rank P < 0.001) as shown in Fig. 3. Separation of curves was apparent by year 3 and widened through year 9; participants in Q4 experienced the highest incidence throughout follow‑up. Each quartile began with ~ 827 participants, with numbers at risk declining to 630–768 by year 9, reflecting loss to follow‑up and events.

Dose–Response Relationships Between TyG-FI/Cumulative TyG-FI and Cardiometabolic Multimorbidity Risk (Restricted cubic spline curves depict adjusted hazard ratios (solid lines) and 95% confidence intervals (shaded areas) relative to reference values (TyG-FI: 0.7345; cumulative TyG-FI: 2.703; dashed vertical lines). Dashed horizontal lines indicate HR = 1.0. All models adjusted for sociodemographic, lifestyle, clinical and biochemical covariates)

As shown in Table 2, TyG-FI demonstrated a robust positive association with CMM risk across all models. Per unit increase in TyG-FI was associated with a 1.80-fold higher CMM risk in the fully adjusted Model 4 (HR = 1.80, 95% CI 1.57–2.05, p < 0.001). A significant graded relationship was observed across quartiles (P trend < 0.001), with Q4 exhibiting a 7.86-fold risk (HR = 7.86, 95% CI 4.16–14.86) versus Q1. Similarly, cumulative TyG-FI quartiles showed escalating risks (Q4 HR = 15.22, 95% CI 6.99–33.14). These associations persisted despite sequential adjustment for sociodemographic, clinical, and biochemical confounders, underscoring TyG-FI's independent predictive value. Figure 2 illustrated significant non-linear associations of TyG-FI (Panel A) and cumulative TyG-FI (Panel B) with CMM risk (P non-linearity < 0.001 for both). TyG-FI exhibited a J-shaped relationship with CMM, where risk increased modestly below the reference point (TyG-FI = 0.7345) but accelerated sharply above this threshold (HR > 1.5 at TyG-FI > 1.0). Similarly, cumulative TyG-FI demonstrated a supra-linear association, with risk escalation becoming markedly steeper beyond the reference (cumulative TyG-FI = 2.703). These patterns persisted after full adjustment, indicating complex dose–response relationships. Table 3 revealed significant threshold effects for both TyG-FI and cumulative TyG-FI. For TyG-FI, a critical inflection occurred at 0.7 (P < 0.001). Below this threshold, each unit increase conferred markedly higher CMM risk (OR = 5.14, 95% CI 2.88–9.15), while above 0.7, the association attenuated (OR = 1.39, 95% CI 1.01–91). Similarly, cumulative TyG-FI exhibited an inflection at 2.7 (P = 0.002), with stronger effects below (OR = 3.44, 95% CI 1.80–6.58) versus above (OR = 1.25, 95% CI 1.17–134) this point. Non-linearity was statistically confirmed (P < 0.001), supporting distinct risk patterns across biomarker ranges.

Mediation analysis

Mediation analyses using the average causal mediation effect (ACME) showed no significant mediating effects for CRP (P = 0.268 for TyG-FI; P = 0.532 for cumulative TyG-FI) (Table S6) or UA (P = 0.88 for TyG-FI; P = 0.952 for cumulative TyG-FI) (Table S7). Neither biomarker significantly mediated the association with CMM.

Sensitivity analyses

To assess the robustness of the key findings, we performed a series of sensitivity analyses. First, A comparison of data before and after multiple imputation indicated that sample attrition did not significantly distort the study's internal or external validity (as shown in Table S8). Second, after excluding individuals with hypertension, dyslipdemia at baseline, TyG-FI and cumu TyG-FI retained a significant association with CMM during follow-up (Table S9), Third, subgroup analyses revealed consistent dose–response relationships between TyG-FI quartiles, cumu TyG-FI quartiles and CMM across all strata (all P-trend < 0.001). No significant interaction effects were observed (all P-interaction > 0.05), indicating robust associations irrespective of age, BMI, gender, or cardiometabolic status (Tables S10, S11). Four, E-values were computed to quantify the minimum strength of unmeasured confounding required to nullify observed associations between TyG-FI metrics and CMM risk, assuming balanced confounding effects (as shown in Table S12). E-values for TyG-FI quartiles (Q4 vs. Q1: point estimate = 15.20) and cumulative exposure (Q4 vs. Q1: 29.93) exceeded 15, indicating robustness against substantial unmeasured confounding. Continuous TyG-FI associations (E-value = 3.00) also demonstrated moderate resistance.

Discussion

Our study comprehensively investigated the association between the TyG-FI index and CMM, with the following key findings. Results revealed a J-shaped relationship between TyG-FI indices and CMM incidence, wherein threshold effect analysis identified inflection points at approximately 0.7 for TyG-FI and 2.7 for cumulative TyG-FI, respectively. Furthermore, a significant age-dependent effect modification was observed for the association between both TyG-FI indexes and CMM. To improve predictive accuracy, eight machine learning algorithms were employed to develop a predictive model incorporating the TyG-FI indexes, which exhibited robust predictive performance. These findings offer novel insights for clinical practice, suggesting that integrating TyG-FI indexes into routine assessments provides a low-cost, accessible tool for evaluating CMM risk.

TyG-FI combines the TyG index, a validated surrogate for insulin resistance, with the frailty index, which reflects cumulative physiological decline. This integrated approach enhances predictive value for chronic diseases, particularly cardiovascular and metabolic disorders, by capturing synergistic effects not fully assessed by either index alone [39].

In CVD and stroke, TyG-FI demonstrates superior risk prediction. Studies show that high TyG and frailty independently elevate CVD risk, but their coexistence—common in aging populations—amplifies vulnerability. For instance, analyses from CHARLS reveal that TyG-FI associates strongly with increased CVD incidence, as frailty exacerbates metabolic dysregulation from insulin resistance, leading to accelerated atherosclerosis and adverse outcomes [58]. Compared to the TyG index alone, which predicts CVD through pathways like renal impairment or coronary severity [59, 60], or frailty alone linked to negative clinical events [39], TyG-FI provides a more holistic assessment. It accounts for interactions where insulin resistance promotes inflammation and lipid abnormalities, while frailty intensifies susceptibility to these metabolic stressors, improving accuracy in identifying high-risk individuals for early intervention [61].

For metabolic diseases, such as diabetes and metabolic syndrome, TyG-FI offers advantages over single indices by integrating metabolic and physiological decline markers. While TyG-related parameters (e.g., TyG-BMI) predict diabetes onset [40], and frailty correlates with metabolic dysfunction [18], TyG-FI better captures the bidirectional relationship: insulin resistance drives glucose-lipid dysregulation, and frailty progression accelerates under chronic inflammation and metabolic imbalance. This combined index thus enhances prognostic value for outcomes like new-onset CVD in metabolic syndrome populations, outperforming isolated metrics by reflecting multifactorial pathogenesis [61, 62]. Overall, TyG-FI's key advantage lies in its comprehensive nature, addressing limitations of single indices that overlook the interplay between metabolic health and physiological resilience. This makes it a robust tool for risk stratification in chronic disease management [39, 61].

Our research suggests TyG-FI and cumulative TyG-FI demonstrated strong, dose-dependent associations with CMM risk. Each unit increase in TyG-FI conferred a 1.80-fold higher risk. Quartile analysis revealed significant gradients: Q4 had 7.86-fold higher risk versus Q1, while cumulative TyG-FI showed even stronger effects (Q4 HR = 15.22, 95% CI 6.99–33.14). Subgroup analyses confirmed robust associations across strata, with significantly stronger effects in participants < 60 years (TyG-FI HR = 2.18 vs. 1.54 in ≥ 60 years). Incremental predictive value and predictive power demonstrated that the TyG-FI index significantly enhanced the predictive accuracy for cardiometabolic multimorbidity beyond conventional risk factors and single indicators (FI or TyG alone). Machine learning models identified TyG-FI indices as key predictors, with Random Forest performing best (AUC = 0.8094). Sensitivity analyses demonstrated robustness through multiple approaches: (1) Associations persisted after excluding baseline hypertension/dyslipidemia; (2) Consistent dose–response relationships across all subgroups (all P-trend < 0.001); (3) High E-values for quartile comparisons (15.20–29.93) and moderate resistance for continuous associations (E-value = 3.00) indicated substantial protection against unmeasured confounding. These findings establish TyG-FI indices as robust, clinically relevant predictors of CMM risk.

Mediation analyses revealed no significant mediating effects for CRP or UA in the TyG-FI-CMM association (all ACME P > 0.05). This suggests that the pathway from insulin resistance to CMM may be driven more directly by metabolic stress itself, rather than through systemic inflammation or urate-related pathways. Potential mechanisms linking the TyG-FI to incident CMM reflect a synergistic double-hit pathophysiological process involving intertwined metabolic stress and aging-related loss of physiological resilience. Molecularly, the TyG index promotes insulin resistance and resultant glucose–lipid dysregulation—reduced glucose uptake, elevated free fatty acids, and increased triglycerides via enhanced Very-low-density lipoprotein(VLDL)synthesis—which can directly impair endothelial function [25, 62]. This metabolic imbalance directly impairs endothelial function [63, 64], and stimulates the release of pro-inflammatory cytokines such as Tumor Necrosis Factor-alpha (TNF-α) and Interleukin-6 (IL-6) that accelerate atherogenesis, plaque vulnerability, and vascular damage [40]. Frailty, as a state of systemic dysregulation and inflammaging, is captured by the FI component of TyG-FI, so the composite index reflects simultaneous metabolic and inflammatory burden. The Remnant Cholesterol Inflammatory Index (RCII) may synergize with the TyG index across the CMM spectrum, promoting foam cell formation and inflammatory activation in endothelial cells and macrophages [40]. Together, metabolic abnormalities and frailty-associated chronic inflammation amplify systemic inflammation and oxidative stress, impair mitochondrial function, disrupt proteostasis, and compromise DNA repair, thereby accelerating cellular dysfunction and tissue decline [60]. Elevated TyG also associates with atherogenic lipid profiles and subintimal lipid deposition, while IR and inflammatory mediators can impair NO-mediated vasodilation, increase vascular permeability, and induce a pro-thrombotic state [20, 63, 64]. Clinically,these molecular processes create a vicious cycle: metabolic dysregulation can hasten frailty and reduce physiological reserve, and frailty can further worsen metabolic control and CVD risk [60, 62, 65]. Within the cardio–renal–metabolic axis, such reciprocal interactions substantially increase the likelihood of major adverse events (e.g., myocardial infarction, stroke, heart failure) and mortality [42, 65]. Recognizing these linked mechanisms helps explain how TyG-FI may predict CMM and highlights targets for integrated preventive strategies.

Of note, in CHARLS, we used a 30-item frailty index (FI) based on deficit-accumulation methods, balancing validity and feasibility. Although a full 30–40 item FI may be time-consuming in clinical settings, for translating TyG-FI into practice, we suggest validating abbreviated or Electronic Health Record-derived frailty measures against the full FI to confirm retained prognostic performance.

Study strengths and limitations

This study has several strengths. (1) It employed a prospective cohort design using data from CHARLS, with a large sample size and up to 9 years of follow-up. (2) The use of a composite indicator captured the core components of metabolic dysfunction within the comorbidity network, taking into account both disease interactions and shared pathological mechanisms. (3) By comparing baseline and cumulative data, the study comprehensively evaluated the performance of the index, enhancing the robustness and clinical applicability of the findings. (4) This study employed multiple approaches to validate the robustness of the analytical results.

Notwithstanding these strengths, several limitations warrant consideration. Primarily, the observational nature of this study precludes definitive causal inferences regarding the TyG-FI–CMM relationship, confining interpretations to associations. Second, while extensive covariate adjustments were implemented, residual confounding from unmeasured factors remains plausible. Third, CMM ascertainment relied predominantly on self-reported data from baseline and follow-up surveys, introducing potential misclassification risks. Four, the inability to obtain detailed information on the intensity of statin and antihypertensive medications may introduce potential bias in the relationship between exposure and outcomes. Finally, as CHARLS participants were recruited exclusively from China's middle-aged and older population, the ethnic and regional specificity may constrain extrapolation to other demographics or younger cohorts.

Conclusions

Our study thoroughly examined the TyG-FI index’s association with CMM. A J-shaped relationship between TyG-FI indices and CMM incidence was identified, with threshold effects and age-dependent effect modification observed. Machine learning predictive models incorporating TyG-FI indices demonstrated robust performance. These findings suggest TyG-FI assessment may serve as a cost-effective tool for CMM risk stratification in clinical settings, offering novel insights for preventive strategies tailored to demographic-specific risk profiles.
