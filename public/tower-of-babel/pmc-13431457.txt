Disparities in Working Conditions Among Maternal and Neonatal Health Personnel in a Crisis Context: The Case of the East of the Democratic Republic of Congo
Authors: Pacifique Mwene-Batu, Armand Bisimwa Mulume, Jean Corneille Zubulu Lembebu, Meighan Mary, Gaylord A Ngaboyeka, Emmanuel Ongezi Lurhangire, Rosine Bigirinama, Christine Chimanuka Murhima’alika, Christian Zalinga Chiribagula, Ghislain Bisimwa Balaluka, Hannah Tappis
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundMaternal and neonatal health (MNH) indicators remain poor in the Democratic Republic of Congo (DRC), despite the high rates of facility-based childbirth. Working conditions of MNH providers, particularly in conflict-affected eastern DRC, remain poorly documented. This study examines disparities in working conditions among MNH providers in North and South Kivu by sex, age, and professional category.MethodologyA cross-sectional survey was conducted among MNH providers in 28 health facilities across North Kivu (October 2023) and South Kivu (February 2023). A structured questionnaire collected information on working conditions, compensation, professional development and workplace violence. Data were entered into RedCap and analyzed using Stata 17. Differences by sex, age, and professional cadre (nurses/midwives vs medical doctors) were assessed through bivariate comparison analysis, with statistical significance set at p<0.05.ResultsA total of 220 MNH providers participated (116 in North Kivu, 104 in South Kivu), 76% were women, the median age was 38.5 years and 79% were nurses/midwives. Women experienced fewer promotion opportunities (p = 0.001), limited access to continuing professional development (p = 0.029), and higher levels of workplace (p = 0.038) and personal-life discrimination (p = 0.019) than men. Men experienced more verbal or physical aggression from patients or their relatives. Providers younger than 35 years reported more patient-related aggression (p = 0.021) and fewer salary supplements (p = 0.015). Doctors were more likely than nurses to receive non-monetary incentives, including leave and uniforms.ConclusionSignificant disparities in working conditions exist by sex, age, and professional cadre among MNH providers in eastern DRC. Equity-oriented human resource policies that strengthen professional development, workplace protection, and staff welfare are needed to improve provider well-being and quality of maternal and neonatal care in conflict-affected settings.Keywords: disparities, conflict affected zones, maternal and neonatal health, working conditions

Background

The health workforce is a fundamental pillar of health systems, yet persistent global shortages and inequitable distribution threaten progress towards universal health coverage.1,2 Health providers’ density, availability, skills, and working conditions are key determinants of health systems’ ability to deliver quality, safe, and equitable care, particularly in contexts marked by instability and scarce resources.3–5 As highlighted by The Lancet Global Health Commission on High-Quality Health Systems, access to care is not enough; quality of care is essential,6 and particularly crucial for maternal and neonatal health (MNH), where skilled health personnel presence during childbirth is key to providing interventions for reducing maternal and neonatal mortality and morbidity.7–10

However, in many countries, persistent health worker shortages are exacerbated by inequitable distribution across rural and urban areas, as well as between professional cadres.1,11 Structural imbalances in the availability of skilled health professionals (particularly midwives, nurses, and doctors) can hinder access to emergency obstetric and newborn care and compromise the quality of maternal health services.12 In addition, weak governance, poor working conditions, and workforce attrition due to migration, illness, and movement to the private or international sector further exacerbate these disparities, leading to increased workloads, staff dissatisfaction, and suboptimal healthcare delivery.13

Gender inequities compound systemic barriers within health systems. While women constitute the majority of the global nursing and midwifery workforce, they are significantly underrepresented in senior leadership and decision-making roles at institutional, national and global levels.14,15 Across diverse settings, studies consistently reveal gender-based disparities in opportunities for promotion, remuneration, access to professional development, and decision-making authority.16,17 In addition, gender imbalances within the workforce interact with client preferences for same-sex providers.18–20 In many settings, women’s preferences for female frontline health workers (particularly for maternal, newborn, and child health services) can influence service utilization, especially where sociocultural norms shape acceptability of care.18,19 When workforce composition does not align with these preferences, women’s access to respectful, client-centered care may be constrained.20

To ensure quality care, it is crucial that providers work in conditions free from discrimination based on age, gender, or professional category. However, in conflict-settings, these inequalities are often compounded by additional challenges faced by providers, including chronic insecurity, violence against health personnel, limited institutional support, and resource shortages, that directly affect providers’ ability to deliver safe and effective care.21,22 Although health workers in such contexts frequently demonstrate remarkable resilience, sustained exposure to insecurity and strain contributes to burnout, moral distress, compassion fatigue, and ultimately diminished quality of care.22–25

The provinces of North and South Kivu in eastern Democratic Republic of the Congo (DRC) have been plagued by armed conflicts for the past twenty years. The region has become a zone of lawlessness where civilians are looted, raped, and killed by identified or informal armed groups.26,27 These two provinces face clashes between the Congolese army and elements of different national and transnational armed groups. A recently published study in North and South Kivu demonstrated that maternity care is only moderately person-centered, with the lowest scores observed in women’s autonomy, communication, and privacy. Similarly, healthcare providers are also deeply affected, facing severe security risks driven by escalating regional violence and compromised facility safety. Only half of the surveyed maternal and neonatal health providers reported job satisfaction, with North Kivu workers experiencing significantly higher rates of workplace disrespect and verbal abuse from colleagues and patients than those in South Kivu.28 In eastern DRC, prolonged reliance on fragmented humanitarian financing has created parallel remuneration systems in which externally funded incentives often coexist with delayed or irregular government salaries. This dual financing structure contributes to disparities in compensation and working conditions among healthcare providers, particularly in conflict-affected settings. While the effects of conflict on health infrastructure, experiences of women giving birth at health facilities and community health outcomes have been documented, less attention has been paid to how insecurity shapes disparities based on age, gender, or professional category in the working conditions of health providers. Chronic insecurity, work overload, and gender inequalities contribute to demotivation, burnout, and a decline in performance, compromising both the quality and accessibility of care.29–31 In DRC, particularly in eastern conflict-affected areas, recurrent violence against health care workers and health facilities further intensifies occupational stress and undermines workforce stability.31 All of this is contrary to international conventions stipulating respect for health facilities, as well as for health providers and patients in times of war. Moreover, gender analyses of health systems remain marginal in the literature,32 even though it is crucial for developing inclusive policies.

Although previous studies have documented the impact of conflict on maternal health services, evidence describing disparities in the working conditions of maternal and newborn healthcare providers according to sex, age, and professional cadre remains scarce in eastern DRC. This study addresses this important knowledge gap.

Methodology

This work is a cross-sectional study on MNH providers, conducted as part of a larger assessment of the quality of maternal and neonatal care in health facilities in conflict-affected areas of North and South Kivu provinces in DRC.33 Data collection was conducted in South Kivu in February 2023 and in North Kivu in October 2023.28

Study Setting

Eastern DRC has been plagued by decades of sociopolitical instability and insecurity. North and South Kivu are two of the provinces most affected by the conflict in eastern DRC. In North Kivu, the humanitarian situation remains critical. At the time of data collection in 2023, the province was home to over 2.5 million internally displaced people, many of whom were living in precarious conditions with limited access to basic health services.34 Health infrastructure is regularly affected by conflict-related destruction or abandonment, and health personnel often work in unstable environments with limited resources. South Kivu, while similarly affected by the conflict, faces slightly different dynamics marked by localized insecurity, structural poverty, and weak governance. Armed groups remain active, particularly in rural territories where health infrastructure is sparse and health districts cover large, hard-to-reach populations. Armed conflicts in eastern DRC indirectly weaken the health system by disrupting economic, political, social, and infrastructural conditions. In addition, the crisis directly affects health system functioning through targeted attacks on health facilities, health professionals, and patients present in facilities at the time of the attacks.35

Study Population and Sampling

The parent study on quality of maternal and neonatal care conducted assessments in all facilities within the provinces with an average of five or more births per day, based on data reported in the national health management information system in 2022. Based on these criteria, 33 health facilities were selected (17 in South Kivu and 16 in North Kivu).28 These included 23 referral hospitals, 5 health centers, and 5 referral health centers. However, due to ongoing conflict, five facilities (4 health centers and 1 referral health center) in North Kivu were unreachable at the time of data collection.28 Health centers represent the first level of care in the DRC health system, where nurses are primarily responsible for providing primary health care, including basic emergency obstetric and neonatal care (BEmONC). More complex cases are referred to referral hospitals, which offer a wider range of services, including comprehensive emergency obstetric and neonatal care (CEmONC). In some areas without referral hospitals, some health centers have been upgraded to referral health centers to provide CEmONC.

At each selected health facility, up to 8 maternal healthcare providers were invited to participate in surveys.28 The maximum sample size was determined based on national staffing guidelines for primary healthcare facilities and hospitals in the DRC, as well as on national-level data and standards on staffing levels to account for potential variations in hospital size and workforce availability. These national guidelines specify the recommended number of health workers required in primary health facilities as well as in secondary-level hospitals, depending on the level of the facility and the range of services provided36 If more than eight providers were on duty at the time of the assessment, trained research assistants randomly selected eight participants (eligible nurses, midwives, and doctors) using a simple random sampling procedure based on the list of providers on duty. Facility managers only facilitated access to the facilities and were not involved in participant selection. Only healthcare providers who were actively providing maternal and newborn care were eligible. We excluded all healthcare providers a). trained in MNH but not directly involved in maternal healthcare delivery (e.g purely administrative roles, those working in other departments, etc), b). with less than three months of service delivery experience at that facility, and/or c). those who did not consent to participate in the study.28

Data Collection

Data collection was conducted by eight teams, each consisting of four trained public health professionals.28 One team member was responsible for administering the surveys to healthcare professionals, and the other three were responsible for other aspects of the larger quality of care assessment.28 The data collection teams underwent five days of training on study procedures, including recruitment, informed consent, survey administration, and data collection tools.28

The questionnaire was pretested in health facilities not included in the study, and minor revisions were made to improve the clarity and wording of selected questions. Surveys with healthcare professionals were conducted using a standardized questionnaire, initially developed in English and later translated into French by bilingual public health researchers and reviewed by the study team to ensure conceptual equivalence, and pretested before implementation.37 The survey lasted an average of 25 minutes and was conducted in French. Data collection was based on a pre-tested electronic questionnaire deployed through the Redcap platform.38 Because internet connectivity was unreliable in several study sites, questionnaires were administered face-to-face by trained research assistants using tablets, with offline data capture and subsequent synchronization to the Redcap server. Given the sensitive nature of questions related to violence and coercion, surveys were conducted in private settings to ensure confidentiality and minimize social desirability bias. Participants were also informed of available support and referral pathways, including psychosocial services and designated focal persons, in the event that distress or disclosure of abuse occured.

Variables of Interest

The quality of care assessment of the parent study draws on a series of maternal, newborn, and child health (MNCH) quality assessments initially conducted by the USAID-funded Maternal and Newborn Health (MNCH) Quality of Care Surveys from 2010 to 2012 in seven African countries,39 and later expanded through World Bank-UNICEF-USAID supported assessments in Central and South Asia.40,41 The assessment tools were refined using questionnaires adapted from the Service Provision Assessment (SPA) and the Demographic and Health Survey Service Provision Assessment, revised in 2022.42 Only the healthcare provider module and selected items relevant to working conditions were used. The questionnaire underwent contextual adaptation and French translation, with only minor wording modifications that did not alter the underlying constructs. This one was derived from standardized and previously validated MNCH quality of care assessment tools. Because the variables included independent indicators rather than multi-item latent constructs, internal consistency measures such as Cronbach’s alpha were not applicable.

For this study, data were collected using a structured questionnaire that included both sociodemographic and work-environment variables. Participant characteristics included sex, age, professional cadre (nurse/midwife, doctor, obstetrician), managerial role, and years of professional experience. Perceptions of the work environment were measured using categorical quantitative variables, asking providers to report their level of satisfaction with working at the facility, experiences of disrespect or mistreatment from colleagues or patients and their families, and perceived levels of mutual respect among healthcare workers and from the community. Additional variables captured experiences of workplace violence, including verbal, psychological, or physical abuse, as well as perceptions of safety while providing maternity and newborn care. In addition, specific items assessed challenges related to working in conflict-affected settings. All perception-related variables were collected using predefined response categories (eg, yes/no or multiple categorical options).

Data Analysis

Analysis was performed using Stata 17 software. All variables were described stratified by province (North and South Kivu), sex (male and female), age category (≤ 35 and over 35), and health profession cadre (doctor and nurse/midwife). Regarding age, we used 35 years as a pragmatic proxy to distinguish early-career from mid-career healthcare providers. Although career trajectories vary across settings and empirical data for the DRC remain limited, this threshold is consistent with previous health workforce studies in which mid-career generally corresponds to a period of greater professional experience and increased responsibilities.43 Variables with missing observations were analyzed using complete-case analysis. The proportion of missing data was low and no imputation was performed. Categorical variables are summarized as proportions, while continuous variables are presented as means with standard deviation or medians with minimum-maximum, depending on their distribution. Differences were assessed using Pearson’s chi-square test or Fisher’s exact test for categorical variables and Student’s t-test or Mann–Whitney test for dichotomous variables, as appropriate. The significance threshold was set at 5% (p value < 0.05).

Ethics Information

The study was reviewed and approved by the Institutional Review Board of the Johns Hopkins Bloomberg School of Public Health (IRB #21922) and the Ethics Committee of the Catholic University of Bukavu in the DRC (UCB/CIES/NC/011/2023) and complied with the 1964 Declaration of Helsinki and its later amendments.28

Results

A total of 220 health care providers were surveyed (116 in North Kivu and 104 in South Kivu), mostly women, with a median age of 38.5 years and an average professional experience of 10 years; 79% were nurses/midwives and 21% were doctors. Only one obstetrician was identified in the study regions (Table 1).The sociodemographic and professional characteristics of healthcare providers reveal some notable disparities between the provinces of North and South Kivu, particularly with regard to gender and professional cadre (Table 1). A statistically significant difference was observed in gender distribution (p = 0.007) and health professional cadre (p = 0.022), with women representing 76% of respondents, with a higher proportion in South Kivu (84%) compared to North Kivu (68%). Other variables analyzed (age, number of years in clinical practice, and time spent in the current position and status of clinical manager) did not show significant differences between provinces (Table 1).

Access to continuing professional development differed by sex also, with men more frequently reporting continuing professional development within the last six months compared to women (22% vs 14%, p=0.029), while women more often reported continuing professional development opportunities occurring more than 12 months prior (16% vs 6%; p = 0.029). Similarly, doctors were significantly more likely than nurses and midwives to report recent continuing professional development access over the last six months (24% vs 14%, p = 0.046), while age also appeared to be a factor influencing access to professional development, with differences approaching statistical significance (p = 0.053) (Table 2). Perceived discrimination compared to colleagues of the opposite sex was common, particularly regarding free time and work schedules, with women reporting higher levels of discrimination in free time allocation (87% vs 72%, p = 0.019) and work schedules (93% vs 82%, p = 0.038). Although perceptions of discrimination in training, promotion, and geographic assignments were high across all groups, no significant differences were observed by sex, age, or professional cadre (Table 2).

Compensation and Incentives

In terms of compensation and incentives, the majority of healthcare workers reported receiving a salary, with no significant differences by sex or professional cadre (Table 3); however, younger workers (≤35 years) were significantly less likely to receive a salary compared to those older than 35 years (92% vs 99%, p = 0.023). Most respondents indicated that salaries were usually paid on time, with no significant variation across sex, age, or cadre (Table 3). Receipt of salary top-ups or supplementary payments was generally limited, but older workers were significantly more likely than younger ones to report receiving such payments (33% vs 16%, p = 0.015), particularly in the form of monthly or daily salary supplements (20% vs 5%, p = 0.003). Non-monetary incentives were reported by a minority of respondents overall, yet clear cadre-based disparities were observed, with doctors more frequently reported having received time off or vacation periods (11% vs 1%, p = 0.012), uniforms or accessories (p = 0.055) although some of these differences were only marginally significant (Table 3). North Kivu shows significantly higher access to non-monetary incentives, particularly in professional equipment and uniforms, compared to South Kivu (Table 3).

Disrespect and Mistreatment

Workplace violence among healthcare workers is predominantly non-physical and occurs both between colleagues and from patients or their families (Table 4). Physical assaults such as slapping or being hit by colleagues were not reported, while verbal and psychological forms of violence were common. Shouting by colleagues was significantly more frequently reported by younger healthcare workers aged ≤35 years compared to those >35 years (48% vs 33%, p = 0.027). Being accused of acting in bad faith or intentionally causing damage by colleagues was reported more often by women than men (24% vs 15%, p = 0.030) and by nurses compared to doctors (23% vs 17%, p = 0.043). Violence perpetrated by patients or their families showed clearer gender, age, and cadre differences: men were significantly more likely than women to report having experienced slapping (7% vs 1%, p = 0.033), shouting (37% vs 22%, p = 0.024), threats (33% vs 20%, p = 0.042), and other forms of verbal or psychological abuse (35% vs 18%, p = 0.006). Younger workers were more likely to report slapping (p = 0.021) and being hit (p = 0.042) by patients or their families. Doctors were significantly more exposed than nurses to shouting (43% vs 21%, p = 0.002) and threats (38% vs 19%, p = 0.006) from patients or relatives. Finally, regardless of age, gender, or professional cadre, only half of the MNH providers surveyed reported that peers treated each other with mutual respect (Table 4). Significant provincial disparities were observed regarding workplace climate. South Kivu reported a notably higher prevalence of respectful interactions among health workers (p=0.004). In contrast, North Kivu faced higher levels of mistreatment, characterized by frequent shouting and verbal abuse among staff. Additionally, staff in North Kivu were significantly more exposed to threats, both from colleagues (p=0.014) and from patients or their families (p=0.004) (Table 4).

Discussion

This study was the first we are aware of to examine inequities in maternal and neonatal health workforce conditions in conflict-affected provinces in Eastern DRC. Our results highlight that structural disparities by gender, age, and professional cadre persist and negatively influence the working conditions of MNH providers in the conflict-affected provinces of Eastern DRC.

One of our major findings is that despite representing a large proportion of the healthcare workforce, women continue to face structural barriers rooted in sociocultural norms and institutional practices that hinder their professional progression. This observed gender disparities are consistent with findings from others studies.44,45 This could be explained by the fact that women are poorly represented in decision making positions leading to lower compensation and habitual scheduling of working hours by their male counterparts for similar roles and responsibilities.45–52 These persistent gender-based inequities highlight the urgent need for institutional reforms that actively promote women’s professional development, equitable access to training, and meaningful representation in leadership, particularly in fragile and resource-constrained settings such as Eastern DRC. Such reforms should also foster an institutional culture that challenges stereotypical views on women’s roles, encourages male leaders to actively support gender equity, and establishes clear, transparent, and merit-based promotion and hiring criteria to ensure that female staff are not overlooked.53,54

Turning to compensation and incentives, a positive finding is that most providers reported receiving a salary and it’s generally paid on time. In a conflict-affected setting where salary delays are common, that is notable But the picture becomes less equitable when we look beyond base salary to supplementary payments and non-monetary benefits. Because salary amounts were not collected, we were unable to assess whether reported remuneration was sufficient to ensure an adequate standard of living. Future studies should explore this dimension.

Younger providers under 35 were significantly less likely to receive salary top-ups. Only 5% of younger staff reported monthly supplements compared to 20% of their older colleagues. This goes beyond simple seniority gradient and suggests structural undervaluation of junior staff. Similarly, age-related disparities identified in other studies indicating that younger healthcare workers often experience less favorable working conditions.44,55,56 Systematic reviews analyses indicate that in conflict affected settings with weak governance, the lack of standardized salary frameworks, clear reporting systems, and protective policies deepens age-related inequalities among health workers while also eroding staff morale, perceived quality of care, and community health-seeking behaviors.57 In the DRC, irregular government salaries and dependence on performance-based and humanitarian incentives create fragmented and opaque compensation structures.58,59 These conditions tend to favor senior staff, while younger MNH providers; often on the frontline; remain under recognized and financially insecure, affecting their motivation, retention, and continuity of care. Addressing this requires harmonizing incentives under Provincial Health Divisions, ensuring transparent and workload-based compensation, and implementing targeted retention strategies for young professionals in conflict settings.60,61

Cadre hierarchy is another critical issue. Despite, 79% of the maternal and newborn workforce consists of the nurses/midwives’ group, this group has systematically less access to non-monetary incentives and professional support. Similar disparities in working conditions, access to protective resources, and professional recognition have been highlighted in global policy discussions on health workforce strengthening, underscoring their implications for staff retention, system resilience, and continuity of care in fragile and conflict-affected settings.62

The compound effect of professional category, age and gender is important. The majority of nurses/midwives are young women. So, the intersection of being young, female, and a nurse creates a form of potential triple disadvantage in compensation and benefits. In a conflict-affected context, where workforce retention is already fragile, these inequities have serious implications and deserves special attention in human resource planning policies.

In addition, the overall rates of perceived discrimination in training and promotion were high across all groups, indicating a broader systemic environment of inequity. Systemic discrimination in North and South Kivu creates a pervasive environment of inequity, undermining healthcare through professional “glass ceilings,” biased shift allocations, and arbitrary denial of leave. This environment fuels a significant brain drain and erodes patients’ and families’ trust in conflict-affected regions. To restore equity and retain essential personnel, human resource management must transition toward merit-based, anonymous promotion processes and transparently audited work schedules. Equitable access to protective equipment, annual leave, and psychosocial support is indispensable for staff facing trauma from regional insecurity. Furthermore, formal recognition of the skills of nurses working in isolated areas, combined with training on respectful workplace communication, conflict de-escalation, professional neutrality, and equitable interprofessional collaboration., is vital to restoring professional dignity.63,64 Improved coordination between the Ministry of Health and humanitarian partners would harmonize these benefits and prevent brain drain; also equitably valuing both professions is vital to ensuring the continuity and safety of healthcare in this volatile region.59,65

Another striking fact that requires special attention is that across all 28 facilities assessed, we only identified one obstetrician, a stark illustration of the severe shortage of specialists in this setting. The acute shortage of obstetricians in conflict zones causes high maternal mortality, necessitating the implementation of task-shifting to train general practitioners in emergency surgical procedures. To address this crisis, strategies such as telemedicine, mobile surgical units, and high-risk incentives are critical for providing essential care. These interventions prioritize immediate, life-saving obstetric services despite the lack of specialized personnel.

A paradoxical but positive finding is that, despite ongoing conflicts, healthcare providers in North Kivu report receiving significantly more non-monetary support, particularly in terms of professional equipment and uniforms. These results suggest that the concentration of emergency interventions and external funding in this province has led to a more structured resource allocation and better-equipped working environments.

Patterns of violence also varied according to gender, age, and professional role, highlighting the heterogeneous nature of workplace violence. Male providers and doctors were more likely to experience verbal abuse, threats, and physical assaults from patients and their families. Although this finding may initially appear counterintuitive, it likely reflects the tendency of patients and relatives to direct frustration toward healthcare professionals perceived as having greater authority and responsibility for clinical decision-making, particularly in resource-constrained and high-stress environments. Similar patterns have been reported in other healthcare settings, where unmet expectations, long waiting times, and dissatisfaction with care frequently trigger aggression toward physicians and other senior clinicians.66,67 These findings suggest that workplace violence is context- and role-specific, underscoring the need for prevention and protection strategies tailored to different categories of healthcare providers.

Turning to violence from colleagues, women were more exposed to slander and interpersonal mistreatment from colleagues, pointing to underlying gendered dynamics within the health system. Nurses were particularly affected, frequently facing accusations of wrongdoing, as well as persistent disrespect and undervaluation of their clinical contributions.68 Younger providers also appeared particularly vulnerable, possibly due to limited experience, lower income, and reduced access to professional development opportunities, which may contribute to dissatisfaction and a reduced sense of preparedness. Evidence from the DRC and other sub-Saharan African and crisis-affected settings confirms this trend.44,69 Overall, these findings underscore that workplace violence is shaped by intersecting factors—including gender, age, and professional hierarchy—and call for comprehensive, context-specific responses. Addressing these challenges requires not only strengthening institutional protection mechanisms but also implementing targeted, gender- and age-responsive interventions to improve workplace culture and safeguard healthcare workers.52,70

Another, more worrying finding is that only half of the service providers feel their colleagues treat each other with respect. This reveals a deeply tense work environment. In the conflict-ridden Kivu region, a lack of mutual respect among humanitarian service providers severely cripples the aid response. Additional interventions such as confidential counselling services, peer-support groups, psychological first aid, and staff wellbeing programs could further strengthen support for healthcare professionals exposed to violence and chronic stress. This toxicity causes high staff turnover, medical errors, and security vulnerabilities, requiring immediate, respectful workplace interventions.44,71,72 Effective solutions include implementing neutral mediation, Non-Violent Communication training, and strict, inclusive codes of conduct to rebuild trust and save lives.

Finally, structural inequalities affecting MNH professionals in Eastern DRC undermine the overall effectiveness of the health system. Most importantly, these are structural inequities. They are not about individual effort or performance. They reflect embedded organizational norms and policy failures that systematically disadvantage women, younger providers, and nurses/midwives. In addition, conflict acts as an amplifier. It may not create these inequities, but it intensifies them (In North Kivu, these dynamics are further exacerbated), weakening accountability mechanisms and reducing external oversight of workforce management practices. An inclusive approach, grounded in fair remuneration, meaningful community participation, workforce protection, and decentralized capacity-building, is essential to ensure quality care for all women and newborns, even in crises settings. In light of the findings from North and South Kivu, strengthening MNH workforce capacity will require locally adapted policies that address staffing shortages, inequitable distribution, and structural barriers affecting providers’ working conditions and career advancement.

This study has several limitations. First, while this study identifies key associations, its cross-sectional nature restricts our ability to establish causal relationships or observe temporal trends in working conditions, career advancement, and employee retention. In addition, Although not done in our work (sample size in subgroup prohibits this), it would be useful to conduct multivariate analyses in the future through longitudinal research taking into account the intersectionality of age, gender, occupational category and other confounding factors to better understand how these disparities shape career trajectories and attrition over time. The second limitation is the risk of selection bias. By excluding health facilities that offer fewer deliveries and may operate under different conditions, we may have inadvertently overlooked crucial information and variable health workforce conditions. Furthermore, data collection was limited to certain health districts in the two provinces due to the geographic inaccessibility and insecurity of some facilities at the time of data collection. Another limitation would be related to potential reporting biases, particularly on questions about topics like violence and discrimination. Finally, the near absence of specialist, just one obstetrician and no specialized neonatal nurses across 28 facilities, limited our ability to conduct meaningful specialist-level analysis. At the same time, that scarcity is itself an important finding. Despite limitations, our findings provide valuable insights that can inform advocacy and policy reform. Our study will serve as a basis for future research leading to more concrete recommendations.

Conclusion

This study highlights persistent inequalities in working conditions of MNH care providers in Eastern DRC, related to gender, age, and professional cadre. Women, although the majority, are less promoted and less exposed to professional development opportunities. Young providers are more vulnerable to violence, particularly from patients, and less valued in terms of salary. Doctors benefit from better conditions than nurses, both in terms of professional recognition and incentives. These findings reveal the urgent need for reforms in health human resources management policies adapted to gender, age, and professional cadre dynamics, especially in resource-limited and conflict-affected contexts. Addressing these structural inequities is not only essential for protecting the maternal and newborn health workforce but also represents a critical prerequisite for improving the quality, resilience, and equity of maternal and newborn healthcare delivery in conflict-affected settings.
