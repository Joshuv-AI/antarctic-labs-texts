Functional reorganisation of lingual gyrus mediates cognitive impairment in COPD: a multimodal neuroimaging study
Authors: Tianlu Teng, Yu Zhang, Jingting Sun, Ziyi Jing, Ling Wang, Xuejing Li, Xiangming Li, Lucas Becker, Huan Ye, Zhenghan Yang, Zhenchang Wang, Qian Chen
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundCOPD is a systemic disorder associated with cognitive impairment and affective dysfunction. However, the underlying neurobiological mechanisms remain unclear. This study examines brain reorganisation in patients with COPD and investigates potential neural mediators of cognitive and affective deficits.MethodsWe enrolled 51 COPD patients and 50 age- and sex-matched healthy controls. All participants underwent high-resolution structural magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) and resting-state functional MRI. Comprehensive neuropsychological assessments for cognitive function (Montreal Cognitive Assessment (MoCA)) and affective symptoms (Beck Depression Inventory (BDI) and State–Trait Anxiety Inventory (STAI)) were conducted. Voxel-wise analyses compared grey matter volume, fractional amplitude of low-frequency fluctuations (fALFF) and functional connectivity strength between groups. Mediation analysis was performed to explore causal pathways linking COPD, brain alterations and cognitive–affective dysfunction.ResultsCOPD patients exhibited significant cognitive deficits (lower MoCA scores) compared with healthy controls, and greater affective symptom burden (higher BDI and STAI scores). Neuroimaging revealed no grey matter volume differences, but distinct functional abnormalities: increased fALFF in bilateral lingual gyri and decreased fALFF in the right inferior orbitofrontal and left middle occipital regions, alongside enhanced long-range functional connectivity strength in the right frontal and fusiform areas (cluster-level p<0.05, family-wise error-corrected). Functional connectivity between bilateral lingual gyri was strengthened in those with COPD and correlated with anxiety symptoms. Critically, mediation analysis demonstrated that increased fALFF in the left lingual gyrus and enhanced interhemispheric connectivity in the lingual region mediated COPD-related cognitive decline, suggesting compensatory mechanisms.ConclusionsCOPD is associated with functional brain reorganisation, particularly in cognitive and emotional regions, without overt structural changes. The lingual gyrus emerges as a key compensatory hub, where heightened activity and connectivity may mediate cognitive impairment. These findings illuminate neuroprotective pathways in COPD and propose the lingual gyrus as a potential target for interventions aimed at preserving cognitive function.

Introduction

COPD is the third leading cause of death worldwide, characterised by persistent respiratory symptoms and progressive airflow limitation [1]. Although historically viewed as a pulmonary condition, COPD is now recognised as a complex systemic disorder with multi-organ involvement [2]. In particular, the neurocognitive manifestations are of clinical significance, as they substantially affect patients’ quality of life and disease management [3–5].

The prevalence of cognitive dysfunction in COPD patients is remarkably high. Studies report rates of 25–77%, depending on disease severity and assessment methods [6, 7]. These cognitive deficits primarily involve domains such as attention, executive function and memory processing, mirroring patterns observed in other conditions linked to chronic hypoxia and systemic inflammation [8]. Affective disturbances, including depression and anxiety, are also disproportionately common among COPD populations. Meta-analyses indicate prevalence rates approximately two to three times higher than in age-matched healthy controls [9–11]. This clustering of respiratory, cognitive and affective symptoms suggests shared or distinct underlying neuropathophysiological mechanisms that warrant systematic investigation.

Neuroimaging studies have begun to elucidate the neural correlates of these clinical observations [12–14]. Structural magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) revealed widespread grey matter atrophy in people with COPD, particularly affecting the prefrontal cortex, anterior cingulate, hippocampus and insular regions – areas intimately involved in cognitive and emotional processing [14–16]. Diffusion tensor imaging studies complement these findings by demonstrating white matter microstructural damage in the corpus callosum, major association tracts and even the thalamus [17, 18]. Functional MRI showed equally striking abnormalities, including disrupted connectivity within the default mode and frontoparietal control networks, altered regional homogeneity in temporal areas and aberrant amplitude of low-frequency fluctuations in key limbic regions [19–22]. These neuroimaging properties have been variously attributed to chronic intermittent hypoxia, systemic inflammation, oxidative stress, vascular endothelial dysfunction or some combination of these pathological processes [23]. Collectively, these findings substantiate the idea that COPD drives discernible structural and functional reorganisation of the cerebral architecture, whereby such neuroplastic alterations demonstrate an apparent pathophysiological association with clinically evident cognitive impairments in affected individuals [24, 25].

Despite these advances, two major knowledge gaps remain. First, many neural correlates of COPD have been identified, but few studies have systematically examined whether and how these brain changes mediate the relationship between respiratory pathology and cognitive–affective symptoms [17]. Second, there has been insufficient integration of multimodal neuroimaging data with comprehensive neuropsychological assessments in relatively adequately powered samples, and they have focused only on brain reorganisations [12, 17, 26]. Notably, studies have shown that cognitive impairment may correlate with heterogeneous multimodal abnormalities in cerebral architecture among affected patients [27, 28]. These limitations may seriously constrain our ability to develop mechanism-based interventions for cognitive or affective comorbidities in COPD patients.

Therefore, this study aims to address these critical gaps. By explaining how the neurobiological mechanisms underlying COPD pathology relate to cognitive–affective impairment, this study aims to catalyse a paradigm shift from viewing COPD as merely a respiratory disorder to understanding it as an integrated multi-system disease with profound central nervous system effects. Such reconceptualisation has the potential to transform clinical practice by emphasising the importance of cognitive and mental health screening in COPD management, while simultaneously opening new avenues for mechanism-based therapeutic development.

Methods

Participants

We enrolled 51 COPD patients from the outpatient department and 50 matched healthy controls from communities (figure 1). All participants signed an informed consent form, and this study received approval from the Ethics Committee of the Beijing Friendship Hospital, Capital Medical University (no. 2024-P2-255-01), Beijing, China.

Study inclusion criteria

All patients met the COPD diagnostic criteria established by the Global Initiative for Chronic Obstructive Lung Disease (GOLD). Exclusion criteria were as follows: 1) significant neurological disorders, 2) substantial physical diseases, 3) prior occurrences of craniocerebral trauma, surgical interventions or substance dependence, 4) current or historical psychiatric disorders and 5) participants with MRI contraindications, such as claustrophobia or metallic implants in the body. Data preprocessing for affective–cognitive assessments

Before the MRI scans, three standardised scales were employed to assess participants’ affective–cognitive states. Depression symptoms were assessed using the Beck Depression Inventory (BDI), a 21-item self-report questionnaire, where each item is rated on a 0–3 scale, with higher scores indicating increased levels of depression. Anxiety was assessed using the State–Trait Anxiety Inventory (STAI), comprising two subscales, each of 20 items, that measure state anxiety (SAI) and trait anxiety (TAI). Each item is scored on a four-point Likert scale, with higher scores reflecting greater anxiety levels. Cognitive function was evaluated using the Montreal Cognitive Assessment (MoCA), a 30-point screening tool that assesses several cognitive domains. Lower overall and sub-item scores indicate worse cognitive performance and associated subdomain effectiveness. In the study, we encompassed the total BDI score, as well as the scores of SAI and TAI from the STAI. The MoCA included the total score along with subdomain scores, which consist of visuospatial/executive, naming, memory recall, attention, language, abstraction and orientation, referred to as MoCA1 to MoCA7, respectively.

Considering the peculiarities of the individuals’ affective–cognitive deficits, this study initially conducted a manual quality check of the scales to ensure the accuracy of the data and subsequently extracted the multidimensional indicators for each scale. Further information is presented in table 1.

MRI protocols

Following the affective–cognitive assessments, subjects rested for at least 30 min before undergoing the MRI. We used a 3T MR scanner (MAGNETOM Vida, Siemens Healthineers, Erlangen, Germany), equipped with a 24-channel head coil, for image acquisition. Structural MRI data (T1-weighted high-resolution images) and resting-state functional magnetic resonance imaging (rs-fMRI) data were gathered for each subject. Details of the MRI preparation and sequence protocols are provided in the supplementary material.

Brain image data preprocessing

Structural data were processed with segmentation, normalisation, modulation and smoothing to obtain grey matter volume measures.

The preprocessing steps for rs-fMRI images were as follows: elimination of time points and slice timing; head movement correction; normalisation – images were aligned to Montreal Neurological Institute (MNI) space utilising the DARTEL deformation parameters derived from the preprocessing of the T1 images [29]; smoothing; regression of covariates; bandpass filtering; and indicator extraction – fractional amplitude of low-frequency fluctuations (fALFF) [30] and functional connectivity strength (FCS) [31] (including global (gFCS), short-range (srFCS) and long-range (lrFCS)) were initially calculated, and subsequent intergroup comparisons were performed to obtain significantly different clusters. This was followed by functional connectivity (FC) analyses of the significant clusters as the regions of interest (ROIs) to further investigate whether differences exist in the connections between these ROIs, referred to as ROI–ROI FC.

Further preprocessing details are provided in the supplementary material.

Statistical analysis

Evaluating brain differences between people with COPD and healthy controls

To investigate whether brain reorganisation occurs in people with COPD and which areas of the brain are affected, the grey matter volume from structural MRI and the fALFF, gFCS, srFCS and lrFCS from rs-fMRI were calculated. Then, voxel-wise two-sample t-tests were performed using Statistical Parametric Mapping 12 (SPM12) software to compare differences between COPD patients and healthy controls, with age and sex as covariates. The statistical threshold was conducted at a voxel level of p<0.0001 (uncorrected), with a cluster-level family-wise error (FWE) correction at p<0.05. To further explore the association between the seven significant clusters identified in the analysis above, this study conducted an analysis of ROI–ROI FC based on these clusters, correcting for multiple comparisons between groups using the network-based statistic [32].

Neurocausal pathways of altered affect–cognition in COPD

The analysis above revealed that COPD is associated with brain functional reorganisation, accompanied by affective–cognitive deficits. Based on the significant correlation between brain imaging and affect–cognition found in this study, we aimed to explore potential causal pathways between affective–cognitive alterations and brain remodelling in COPD, and to identify the primary brain regions involved in this process. Therefore, this study developed a mediating pathway model to examine the causal pathways between COPD, brain imaging and affect–cognition using the SPSS software PROCESS macro toolkit, version 4.2 (www.processmacro.org/download.html). We constructed the pathway for mediating analysis corresponding to model 4 of the PROCESS macro toolkit. Figure 2 illustrates the conceptual and statistical diagrams of the mediation analysis model. The corresponding modelling equation and the meaning of each pathway are provided in the supplementary material.

a) Conceptual and b) statistical diagrams for model 4 of the PROCESS macro toolkit. Path C (total effect): the total effect of the independent variable (X) on the dependent variable (Y) without considering the mediator (M). Path C′ (direct effect): the direct effect of the independent variable (X) on the dependent variable (Y) after excluding the mediator (M) in the model. Path A: the effect of the independent variable (X) on the mediator (M). Path B: the effect of the mediator (M) on the dependent variable (Y) after controlling for the independent variable. Path AB (indirect effect): the product of Path A and Path B, representing the extent to which the independent variable influences the dependent variable through the mediator. The statistical values (β) for each path were generated using the bias-corrected and accelerated bootstrap procedure implemented in PROCESS (10 000 resamplings), which estimates the effect value and its 95% confidence interval. The criterion for statistical significance is that the 95% confidence interval excludes zero.

Other statistical analyses

Nonparametric statistical methods were used for demographic and clinical data. The Mann–Whitney U test was applied to assess intergroup differences in continuous variables, such as age, body mass index and affective–cognitive scores. A chi-squared test was performed for the categorical variable of sex. Unless stated otherwise, the statistical threshold was set at p<0.05 and was two-tailed.

Results

Demographic and clinical characteristics

The demographic and clinical characteristics of participants with COPD (n=51; age: 57.25±6.35 years; male, n=48) and healthy controls (n=50; age: 55.92±6.30 years; male, n=47) are summarised in table 1. The two groups showed no significant differences in terms of age, sex distribution, years of education or body mass index (all p>0.05). Compared with the healthy control group, individuals with COPD scored significantly higher on the BDI, SAI and TAI (all p<0.001). Moreover, patients with COPD exhibited significantly reduced MoCA-Total scores, alongside lower subdomain scores in the executive/visuospatial, memory and abstraction domains (all p<0.05). However, no significant differences were noted between groups in the naming, attention, language and orientation domains. Disease-related indices in the COPD group showed moderate airflow limitation, with mean forced expiratory volume in 1 s (FEV1) of 61.07±18.11% predicted and FEV1/forced vital capacity ratio of 59.59±11.41. The average disease duration was 15.26±7.62 years, and the distribution across COPD severity stages was 10 mild, 21 moderate, 18 severe and 1 very severe case.

Brain reorganisation differences between the COPD patients and healthy controls

No significant differences were found in grey matter volume for the whole brain between the two groups (p>0.05, uncorrected). Compared with healthy controls, people with COPD showed increased fALFF in the bilateral lingual gyri and decreased fALFF in the right inferior orbitofrontal and left middle occipital regions (cluster-level p<0.05 with FWE correction) (table 2; figure 3a). Increased lrFCS was observed in the right fusiform, right middle frontal and right superior frontal gyri in the COPD group relative to the healthy control group (cluster-level p<0.05 with FWE correction) (table 2; figure 3b). ROI–ROI FC among the seven clusters identified from fALFF and lrFCS revealed significantly enhanced connectivity between the bilateral lingual gyri in COPD patients (with network-based statistic correction) (table 2; figure 3c).

Neural correlates of affective–cognitive deficits

The fALFF value in the right inferior orbitofrontal gyrus was negatively correlated with BDI (ρ=−0.296, p=0.003) (figure 4a) and TAI (ρ=−0.227, p=0.022) (figure 4b) scores, while fALFF in the left middle occipital gyrus was negatively correlated with BDI scores (ρ=−0.218, p=0.029) (figure 4c) and positively associated with the memory domain score in the MoCA (ρ=0.244, p=0.014) (figure 4d). The lrFCS value in the right fusiform gyrus showed positive associations with BDI (ρ=0.271, p=0.006) (figure 4e), SAI (ρ=0.229, p=0.021) (figure 4f) and TAI scores (ρ=0.282, p=0.004) (figure 4g) and negative associations with the executive and visuospatial domain score in the MoCA (ρ=−0.249, p=0.012) (figure 4h) and the memory domain score in the MoCA (ρ=−0.253, p=0.011) (figure 4i). The lrFCS value in the right middle frontal gyrus showed positive associations with SAI (ρ=0.199, p=0.046) (figure 4j). Moreover, the FC value between the bilateral lingual gyrus was positively associated with TAI scores (ρ=0.213, p=0.032) (figure 4l). The statistical threshold was set at p<0.05, uncorrected.

Neurofunctional mediation of cognitive deficits in COPD

In this study, we found that the increased fALFF in the left lingual gyrus plays a mediating role in the association between COPD and global cognitive impairment (β=0.9266, 95% CI 0.0179–2.0365) (figure 5a). Increased FC between the bilateral lingual gyri mediates the link between COPD and executive and visuospatial function (β=0.1810, 95% CI 0.0051–0.4575) (figure 5b). Increased fALFF in the left lingual gyrus mediates the relationship between COPD and abstract thinking ability (β=0.5960, 95% CI 0.1601–1.0843) (figure 5c).

Discussion

In this study, consistent with prior findings [3, 4, 10], we demonstrated that COPD patients exhibit significantly poorer cognitive performance and higher affective disturbance scores than healthy controls. Our study provides further evidence that COPD patients frequently exhibit cognitive and emotional deficits, accompanied by corresponding brain functional alterations. Most interestingly, we found that the functional alterations of the bilateral lingual gyri may mediate specific cognitive abnormalities in patients.

Brain reorganisation in COPD patients and neural correlations

We discovered distinct functional abnormalities in COPD patients, particularly in regions involved in visual processing, executive function and emotional regulation [33]. Importantly, these neural changes were significantly correlated with clinical measures of depression, anxiety and cognitive function, providing new insights into the neurobiological mechanisms underlying neuropsychiatric symptoms in COPD [10, 13]. The increased fALFF in the bilateral lingual gyri, coupled with decreased fALFF in the right inferior orbitofrontal cortex (OFC) and left middle occipital regions, suggests dysregulation in visual and higher-order cognitive processing in COPD [34, 35]. Notably, the reduced fALFF in the right inferior OFC was negatively correlated with both depression and anxiety scores, supporting the role of this region in emotional regulation [36]. Dysfunction in the OFC, a key hub for inhibitory control and affective processing, may contribute to the high prevalence of mood disorders in COPD patients [5]. Similarly, decreased fALFF in the left middle occipital gyrus was associated with higher depressive symptoms, while its positive correlation with MoCA scores suggests that preserved activity in this region may support better cognitive performance; one indirect line of evidence supporting this interpretation is a study of patients with dementia with Lewy bodies that showed that enhancing occipital α-band oscillations led to improvements in visuospatial and executive functions [37]. This aligns with a prior study linking occipital hypofunction to both visual processing deficits and cognitive decline in stable COPD [34].

The enhanced lrFCS in the right fusiform gyrus was particularly intriguing, as it exhibited positive associations with depression, state anxiety and trait anxiety, while showing negative correlations with MoCA scores. Given the fusiform gyrus's role in facial emotion recognition [38], its hyperconnectivity may reflect maladaptive neural responses to chronic emotional distress in COPD. In addition, increased lrFCS in the right middle frontal gyrus was positively correlated with the SAI scores, which may reflect prefrontal dysregulation in anxiety-related symptoms [39]. These findings suggest that excessive functional integration in these regions may not be compensatory but rather a marker of inefficient neural processing, contributing to both affective disturbances and cognitive impairment. The strengthened FC between the bilateral lingual gyri was positively associated with TAI scores, reinforcing the idea that altered visual network dynamics may be linked to anxiety in people with COPD. While the lingual gyrus is primarily involved in visual processing, emerging evidence also suggests its involvement in emotional arousal and threat detection [19]. Thus, increased connectivity in this network might reflect a neural substrate for hypervigilance or maladaptive coping mechanisms in anxious COPD patients. These findings align with previous studies reporting functional disruptions in COPD due to chronic hypoxia, oxidative stress or systemic inflammation [17, 26, 34]. Our study extends this literature by demonstrating direct associations between specific neural abnormalities and neuropsychiatric symptoms. Future longitudinal studies should investigate whether these neural patterns predict symptom progression or treatment response. In addition, interventions targeting emotional regulation or neuroprotective strategies could be explored to mitigate these neural and psychological disturbances [13, 14, 26]. However, based on whole-brain analysis, this study did not reveal any significant differences in grey matter changes between COPD patients and healthy controls. We speculate that the primary reasons for this may be as follows: 1) COPD with cognitive impairment or affective dysfunction may be more likely to cause functional changes while structural alterations remain relatively subtle and 2) the relatively small sample size in the current study might have been insufficient to detect such changes. This represents one of the key directions for our future research.

Neurofunctional mediation of cognitive deficits in COPD

To specifically investigate the mediating relationships between the brain alterations, COPD and cognitive/affective dysfunction – an aspect notably lacking in previous studies – we conducted this comprehensive mediation analysis. Our study suggests a potential compensatory neural mechanism in COPD-related cognitive impairment. Specifically, the observed increases in fALFF in the left lingual gyrus and enhanced FC between the bilateral lingual gyri may reflect adaptive responses rather than direct pathological alterations. This finding aligns with emerging evidence of neuroplastic adaptations in chronic respiratory diseases [40]. Specifically, the mediating role of lingual gyrus hyperactivity in the association between COPD and global cognitive function, along with its correlation with preserved abstract thinking, suggests that this region may be involved in mechanisms related to cognitive maintenance in the context of disease, consistent with reports of visual association cortex involvement in cognitive reserve mechanisms [41].

The strengthened FC between lingual gyri appears to mediate COPD's detrimental effects on executive and visuospatial functions, echoing findings of interhemispheric compensation in neurodegenerative conditions [42, 43]. These findings collectively propose a novel neuroprotective model where lingual gyrus reorganisation may represent the brain's adaptive response to preserve cognitive function in COPD, building upon prior work demonstrating functional reorganisation in hypoxic conditions [44, 45]. The directional nature of these mediation effects implies that targeted enhancement of lingual gyrus activity and interhemispheric connectivity could represent promising therapeutic avenues for preventing cognitive deterioration in COPD patients, as suggested by a recent neuromodulation study that thalamic deep brain stimulation may relieve breathlessness in those with COPD [46]. This differs from traditional views of neural alterations as predominantly degenerative [5], and points to possible endogenous mechanisms that could inform future clinical approaches. However, it is important to emphasise that these interpretations are speculative at this stage, being derived from the current data, and still require validation through larger-scale, longitudinal or even experimental studies.

Limitations

This study had several limitations. First, the relatively small sample size may affect the generalisability of our findings. Second, due to sample size constraints, we were unable to separately analyse patients with pure cognitive dysfunction and those with pure affective dysfunction, which might introduce confounding effects. Third, as a cross-sectional study, our results cannot capture the dynamic trajectory of brain remodelling in COPD; longitudinal investigations are currently under way to address this limitation. Fourth, while we focused on structural and resting-state functional alterations in this research, future studies should incorporate white matter analyses to better understand neural connectivity abnormalities. Fifth, our cohort was predominantly male (largely comprising silicosis-/pneumoconiosis-induced COPD cases), potentially limiting the extrapolation of findings for female populations. Future research should include more diverse aetiologies and balanced sex representation. Sixth, due to the limited sample size, multiple comparison correction was not applied in the correlation analyses conducted in this study. Finally, the findings from the mediation analysis in this study are based solely on currently data and should be interpreted as preliminary and largely speculative. Nonetheless, they represent a novel and meaningful discovery. Further validation through larger-sample studies, longitudinal designs or experimental approaches may be needed in the future.

Conclusions

This study demonstrates that COPD patients develop significant cognitive dysfunction and affective dysfunction. These neuropsychiatric manifestations are associated with substantial brain reorganisation, predominantly characterised by functional alterations rather than structural changes. Crucially, our mediation analysis reveals that the bilateral lingual gyri mediate COPD-related cognitive impairment through a compensatory mechanism – its increased brain regional activity and connectivity may appear to attenuate patients’ cognitive decline. These findings not only elucidate the possible neuropathological basis of neuropsychiatric manifestations in COPD, but also identify the lingual gyrus as a potential therapeutic target for preserving cognitive function in clinical practice. Future studies should explore neuromodulatory approaches to enhance this compensatory network while further investigating potential structural changes in advanced disease stages.
