Evaluation of hepatitis B vaccine effectiveness among fully vaccinated healthcare workers in Ethiopia
Authors: Adane Adugna, Gashaw Azanaw Amare, Yalew Muche, Mamaru Getinet, Abateneh Melkamu, Tebelay Dilnessa, Tabarak Malik, Habtamu Belew, Mohammed Jemal, Desalegn Abebaw
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundHepatitis B vaccination is administered for Healthcare workers (HCWs) in three doses at 0, 1, and 6 months to provide immunity. However, in the study area, the effectiveness of the HBV vaccine remains uncertain.ObjectiveThis study aimed to evaluate the effectiveness of the hepatitis B vaccine and identify factors influencing its effectiveness among fully vaccinated HCWs in all hospitals within East Gojjam Zone, Northwest Ethiopia.Materials and methodsA hospital-based cross-sectional study was conducted among 422 HCWs from March to November 2024. Simple random sampling technique was used to recruit the study participants. Total sample size was allocated proportionally to each hospital. Five to seven milliliters of venous blood samples were collected and serum samples were analyzed to detect the anti-hepatitis B surface antigen (anti-HBs), hepatitis B surface antigen (HBsAg), and anti-hepatitis B core antibody (anti-HBc) using a sandwich enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay (ELISA). Data were analyzed using SPSS software version 25.ResultsOf fully vaccinated HCWs, 346/422 (82.0%) (95% CI: 78.0–86.0%) of them were sero-protected (had anti-HBs titer ≥ 10 mIU/ml). The concentration of protective anti-HBs (≥ 10 mIU/mL) was observed in 337/401 (84.0%) of non-smokers. The overall sero-positivity of HBsAg was 23/422 (5.5%) (95% CI: 3.0–8.0). Blood transfusion (AOR: 16.5, 95% CI: 1.53, 29.24) (P < 0.011) and dental extraction (AOR: 13.5, 95% CI: 3.99, 45.57) (P < 0.000) were significantly associated with HBsAg positivity. Moreover, the paradoxical co-existence of HBsAg and anti-HBs was 4.6% (95% CI: 2.0–7.0%). In addition, the overall sero-positivity of anti-HBc was 30/422 (7.1%) (95% CI: 5.0–10.0). Injectable medication (AOR: 4.3, 95% CI: 1.73, 10.72) (P < 0.002) was significant determinant for anti-HBc sero-positivity.ConclusionsThe study revealed suboptimal hepatitis B vaccine effectiveness, leading to intermediate HBV infection rates in the study area. These findings highlight the importance of monitoring post-vaccination anti-HBs antibody levels to address gaps in protection.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12879-025-12348-6.

Introduction

Hepatitis B virus (HBV) is a significant global public health challenge, particularly in highly endemic regions such as sub-Saharan Africa, including Ethiopia [1]. HBV causes both acute and chronic disease, leading to severe complications like cirrhosis, liver failure, and hepatocellular carcinoma (HCC), which accounts for over 60% of liver cancer cases worldwide [2]. HBV is primarily transmitted through contact with infected blood and bodily fluids, with healthcare workers (HCWs) being at particularly high risk due to occupational exposure during medical, surgical, and diagnostic procedures [3].

On the other hand, according to the World Health Organization (WHO), nearly 2 million HCWs are exposed to the HBV infection annually, as part of approximately 3 million percutaneous exposures to blood borne pathogens each year [4]. This highlights the significant occupational burden faced by HCWs, particularly in regions with high or intermediate HBV endemicity [5]. In these areas, a substantial proportion of HCWs report needle-stick injuries, emphasizing the urgent need for comprehensive infection control measures and ensuring full vaccination coverage to prevent HBV infection. Globally, the incidence of acute HBV infection among HCWs is estimated at 5.3% [6, 7].

In Ethiopia, where HBV is highly endemic, the burden of infection among HCWs poses a major public health concern. Chronic HBV infections contribute to severe morbidity and mortality, further straining the healthcare system [2]. According to previous systematic reviews and meta-analysis, the estimated pooled prevalence of HBV among HCWs in Ethiopia is approximately 5.93%, with variations by region and occupation [8, 9].

The critical importance of preventive strategies for HBV infection lies in vaccination. The hepatitis B vaccine, available since the 1980s, is highly effective in preventing infection when administered as a three-dose series, providing over 90% protection [10]. The vaccine is given at 0, 1, and 6 months to induced protective anti-HBs (anti-HBs titer ≥ 10 mIU/ml) [11]. HCWs typically produce protective levels of anti-HBs 1–2 month after completing the full hepatitis B vaccine series [12]. Hence, anti-HBs levels are checked 1–2 month after the completion of the hepatitis B vaccine series to confirm immunity. If the anti-HBs titer is greater ≥ 10 mIU/mL, it is considered indicative of adequate immunity against HBV [13]. Regular monitoring of anti-HBs titers and timely administration of booster doses are essential strategies to maintain immunity and ensure comprehensive protection for HCWs against HBV infection [14].

While vaccination programs have been implemented, gaps in vaccine effectiveness persist. Factors such as incomplete vaccination schedules, the absence of post-vaccination serological testing for anti-HBs antibodies, and frequent occupational exposure may compromise immunity among HCWs and render them non-responders to the vaccine (anti-HBs titer < 10 mIU/ml). In addition, factors like age, gender, obesity, drinking, smoking and the host immune status may affect hepatitis B vaccine effectiveness [15, 16].

In Ethiopia the full dose (3 doses) of hepatitis B vaccination coverage among HCWs is 20–80% [8, 17]. However, there is no published data on the protective effectiveness of hepatitis B vaccine among HCWs in Ethiopia in general in the study area in particular. Hence, this study aimed to evaluate the effectiveness of the hepatitis B vaccine among fully vaccinated HCWs in Ethiopia and identify factors influencing its effectiveness. Understanding these factors is essential for improving vaccination strategies and protecting HCWs from HBV infection. By addressing these gaps, this study contributes to global efforts toward achieving the WHO’s goal of eliminating hepatitis B as a public health threat by 2030.

Materials and methods

Study design, setting, and period

A hospital based cross-sectional study design was conducted among hepatitis B fully vaccinated healthcare workers in all hospitals of East Gojjam Zone, North West Ethiopia, from March 25, 2024, to November 30, 2024. In East Gojjam Zone, there is one Comprehensive Specialized Hospital, one General Hospital, and nine Primary Hospitals.

Source and study population

All fully vaccinated healthcare workers employed in hospitals within the East Gojjam Zone were considered the source population and those meeting the eligibility criteria and present at their respective hospitals during the data collection period constituted the study population.

Inclusion and exclusion criteria

Healthcare providers currently working in hospitals within the northwestern region of Ethiopia were included in the study provided they were fully vaccinated against the hepatitis B virus, with documented proof of complete vaccination, and were aged 18 years or older. Additionally, participants needed to provide written informed consent and be employed during the data collection period from May to January 2025. Conversely, healthcare providers who were not fully vaccinated, declined to give informed consent, or were on leave or absent during the data collection timeframe were excluded. Furthermore, those with any medical conditions or circumstances that could interfere with participation or compromise data integrity, were also excluded from the study.

Operational definition

Fully vaccinated

Those healthcare workers who took 3 dose of HBV vaccine.

Hepatitis B vaccine non-responders

Those are healthcare worker her/ his protective antibody level is < 10 mUI/ml.

Hepatitis B vaccine responders

Those are healthcare workers their antibody level ≥ 10 mUI/ml.

Sample size = n (sample size) + (10% non-respondent). Therefore; final sample size (n) was calculated as n = (384 + 38) = 422. The total sample size was proportionally allocated to each hospital. Simple random sampling technique was used to recruit the study participants.

Socio-demographic and clinical data collection procedure

Socio-demographic and clinical data were collected using a pretested structured questionnaire. Information about healthcare workers’ vaccination status was obtained from themselves. Body mass index was measured (supplementary file one).

Laboratory procedures

Five up to seven (5–7) ml of vinous blood sample was collected by venipuncture from each study participant. All collected samples from different hospitals of East Gojjam Zone were transported to molecular lab of Debre Markos Comprehensive Specialized Hospital (DMCSH) using a cold box with ice packs for storage. Serum was separated from each blood sample by centrifugation, and then stored at -80 °C until laboratory analysis was done.

Using the sandwich enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay (ELISA) [BIO-RAD, Monolisa, France], anti-HBs, HBsAg, and anti-HBc were detected in Debre Markos Blood Bank Laboratory (DBBL). A qualitative and quantitative kind was employed to measure the anti-HBs level. A qualitative type of sandwich ELISA was used to detect HBsAg and anti-HBc.

Monolisa™ Anti-HBs PLUS was used for the qualitative and quantitative detection of anti-HBs and the absorbance values were read at 450/620–700 nm spectrophotometrically. In addition, HBsAg was detected by Monolisa™ HBs Ag ULTRA ELISA kit spectrophotometrically at 450/620–700 nm. Moreover, anti-HBcAb was detected by Monolisa™ Anti-HBc PLUS ELISA kit and the absorbance values were read using a spectrophotometer at 450/620–700 nm [18].

Data quality control

Training was given for data collector. Pretest was done before data collection to optimize the experimental set up. This was done in samples obtained from 5% of study participants. Standard operating procedures (SOPs) were strictly followed during sample collection, transportation, and processing steps. In addition, standards were checked for each ELISA kit with known positive and negative control samples.

Data processing and analysis

The data were entered into EpiData software version 3.1 and exported to SPSS version 25 for analysis. Results were presented in text and tables. Binary logistic regression was performed to assess associations between dependent and independent variables. In bivariate analysis, variables with a p-value < 0.25 were considered candidates for multivariate logistic regression. The multivariate model reported adjusted odds ratios (AORs) with 95% confidence intervals, and statistical significance was determined at p < 0.05.

Ethical consideration

The Institutional Research Ethics Review Committee (IRERC) of Debre Markos University, College of Health Sciences, approved the study in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki under protocol number [R/C/S/D/317/01/16]. Moreover, written informed consent was obtained from each study participants.

Results

A total of 422 healthcare workers participated in this study. The median age of the study participants was 36.00 ± 6.64 years. Approximately 78% of participants were over 30 years old. Among the study participants, 55% were male. Additionally, 56.4% held a bachelor’s degree (Table 1).

Sero-positivity of anti-HBs, HBsAg, HBsAg/anti-HBs, and anti-HBc

Of fully vaccinated HCWs, 346/422 (82.0%) (95% CI: 78.0–86.0%) of them had anti-HBs titer ≥ 10 mIU/ml (were vaccine responders). Conversely, 76 out of 422 (18.0%) vaccinated HCWs were non-responders, having anti-HBs titers below 10. Among fully vaccinated HCWs, 330 out of 422 (78.2%) were anti-HBs positive (anti-HBs titers ≥ 10 mIU/ml) without HBsAg. Among HCWs aged ≥ 30 years, 271 (82.4%) had an anti-HBs titer of ≥ 10 mIU/mL. Furthermore, the overall sero-positivity of HBsAg among all study participants was 23/422 (5.5%) (95% CI: 3.0–8.0). Among HBsAg-positive participants, 19/24 (79.2%) were over 30 years old. In other words, the co-existence of HBsAg/anti-HBs among fully vaccinated HCWs was 16(4.6%). In addition, the overall sero-positivity of anti-HBc was 30/422 (7.1%) (95% CI: 5.0–10.0). Moreover, 19(10.0%) of anti-HBc positive study participants were females. The number of vaccinated HCWs who were positive for both anti-HBc and anti-HBs (≥ 10 mIU/mL) was 24/ 422 (5.7%). Among vaccinated HCWs, 7 out of 422 (1.7%) were positive for both HBsAg and anti-HBc but negative for anti-HBs. Furthermore, 16 out of 422 HCWs (3.8%) tested positive for HBsAg, anti-HBc, and anti-HBs simultaneously (Table 2).

Discussion

Hepatitis B virus (HBV) poses a significant public health challenge among healthcare workers (HCWs), who face elevated occupational risks due to exposure to blood, bodily fluids, and needlestick injuries [19]. HCWs face a 10-fold higher risk of HBV infection compared to the general population. Key drivers include low vaccination uptake, unsafe practices like improper needle handling, and limited adherence to infection control protocols in resource-constrained healthcare systems. Chronic HBV infection among HCWs not only threatens their health but also risks transmission to patients, perpetuating cycles of infection [20]. Addressing this requires enhanced vaccination campaigns, improved occupational safety training, and routine HBV screening to mitigate risks and protect both HCWs and public health. Moreover, hepatitis B vaccine plays a critical role in protecting HCWs in Ethiopia from occupational HBV exposure [21]. According to the Ethiopian Ministry of Health, HCWs are recommended to receive a full three-dose hepatitis B vaccine series as part of the national Expanded Program on Immunization (EPI) to effectively protect against HBV infection. Prior to vaccination, testing such as screening for HBsAg and anti-HBc is encouraged to identify individuals with current or past infection. Moreover, the Ministry emphasizes free vaccination provision, infection prevention training, and enhancing awareness to improve vaccine coverage among HCWs, aiming to reduce the high risk of HBV infection in this population [22]. Strategic plans also include viral hepatitis prevention programs integrated into the health system to meet elimination goals by 2030 [23].

Anti-HBs (hepatitis B surface antibody) serve as a protective antibody that indicates immunity from HBV vaccination, with titers ≥ 10 mIU/mL confirming effective protection due to vaccination [24]. However, anti-HBs levels may decline over time, e.g., 30% of vaccinated HCWs develop titers < 10 mIU/mL years post-vaccination, particularly those immunized > 5 years, enabling rapid antibody resurgence upon HBV exposure [25]. The effectiveness of the hepatitis B vaccine in fully vaccinated HCWs can be diminished by several factors. Demographic factors, such as older age and male gender, are associated with lower immune responses to the vaccine [26, 27]. Lifestyle factors, including smoking, obesity, and alcohol consumption, negatively impact vaccine effectiveness by impairing immune function [28]. Additionally, medical conditions like chronic disease and other immune-compromising diseases reduce the body’s ability to induce a robust immune response to the vaccine. In addition, genetic factors, such as variations in immune regulation genes, also play a role in nonresponse [29].

In this study, the seroprotection levels of hepatitis B vaccine (anti-HBs titer ≥ 10 mIU/ml) among fully vaccinated HCWs was 82.0% (95% CI: 78.0–86.0%). This is comparable with the study conducted in Italy (79.2%) [30], and higher than the study in Mozambique (60%) [31], Kenya (37.5%) [6], and India (70%) [32], and lower than finding reported from Senegal (87%) [33]. The differences in hepatitis B vaccine seroprotection rates among fully vaccinated HCWs across studies can be attributed to various biological, demographic, and methodological factors. Biological factors like lower high-density lipoprotein cholesterol (HDL) levels and elevated lipid ratios (e.g., TG/HDL, LDL/HDL) are associated with reduced seroprotection, particularly in populations with metabolic risks like obesity or diabetes [34]. Age also plays a role, as older individuals experience diminished immune responses due to immunosenescence, which may explain lower protection rates in older individuals. Vaccine adherence and completion substantially influence outcomes; incomplete vaccination schedules lead to reduced immunity, as observed in regions with lower adherence rates. Demographic factors, including gender and regional HBV prevalence further contribute, with males and individuals in high-endemic areas often showing lower seroprotection [35]. Methodological differences, including variations in timing of anti-HBs titer measurement, vaccine schedules, and assay sensitivity, also impact findings. Furthermore, policy differences such as the presence or absence of booster dose recommendations and post-vaccination monitoring affect long-term immunity. These disparities highlight the need for standardized vaccination protocols, age-specific booster policies, and consistent post-vaccination evaluations to ensure consistent protection globally [36].

In the current study, participants with no history of cigarette smoking showed a relatively higher proportion of protective anti-HBs levels (≥ 10 mIU/mL) at 84.0% compared to smokers. This finding is consistent with previous research indicating that smoking harms immune function, notably impairing memory B-cell and antibody production, as well as weakening T-cell responses, all of which are crucial for vaccine effectiveness [37]. Cigarette smoke contains numerous toxic chemicals that can suppress the immune system, thereby reducing its capacity to mount a robust response to the hepatitis B vaccine. The observed association highlights the importance of smoking cessation to enhance vaccine-induced immunity and provide optimal protection against HBV infection [38].

Moreover, the overall sero-positivity of HBsAg among all study participants was 5.5% (95% CI: 3.0–8.0). This sero-positivity of HBsAg tells us the study area is found under the category of intermediate endemicity for HBV infection. This is comparable with the study done in Green land (3.3%) [39]. On the other hand, this finding is higher than the other studies done in Uzbekistan-Japan (0.8%) [40], Iran (0.6% [41], Italy (0.6%) [42], Egypt (0.6%) [43]. Moreover, the sero-positivity of HBsAg in the current study is lower than the findings reported from china (20.49%) [44]. These variations may be explained by difference in host genetic factors, ethnicity, cultural practice, lifestyles, socioeconomic status, and regional variations. Moreover, this variation also might be because of the difference infection control practice in the hospital setting, immune status, sample size, and sampling technique and type of laboratory method used for HBsAg detection.

Furthermore, in the current study, the sero-positivity of HBsAg among participants aged ≥ 30 years was higher (5.8% vs. 4.3%). Additionally, HBsAg sero-positivity was also elevated in male participants (6.0% vs. 4.7%). Older individuals are more affected by HBV due to cumulative exposure risks, waning immunity, and biological factors. Moreover, gender-specific immune and hormonal differences, behavioral disparities, and clinical outcomes collectively explain why males are disproportionately affected by HBV infection. Estrogen’s protective role mitigates HBV progression by reducing liver inflammation and promoting immune responses, delaying chronic liver disease development and lowering HBV-related cirrhosis and hepatocellular carcinoma (HCC) risk in females. Conversely, testosterone may enhance HBV replication or promote pro-inflammatory pathways, accelerating disease progression in males [45]. Behavioral factors, such as higher alcohol use, and unprotected sex, increase HBV transmission and liver damage risks in males. Genetic mechanisms, including higher HBV X gene prevalence in males and gender-specific cytokine responses, may further influence viral persistence and pathogenesis [46].

In this study, associated factors were also assessed for HBsAg positivity. Hence, participants with previous history of blood transfusion were 16.5 times more likely to be HBsAg sero-positive as compared to those who had no history of blood transfusion. Furthermore, participants who had previous history of dental extraction were 13.5 time more likely to be HBsAg sero-reactive as compare to their counterparts. This is due to exposure to contaminated blood or bodily fluids, iatrogenic transmission via unsterilized equipment, and viral persistence in occult infections. Dental extraction risks arise from contaminated instruments and blood-to-blood contact during procedures, particularly in settings with poor infection control or shared needles. These factors highlight the need for enhanced screening for blood donors and rigorous sterilization protocols to mitigate HBV transmission in high-risk settings.

In addition, the paradoxical co-existence of anti-HBs and HBsAg in fully vaccinated HCWs was 4.6% (95% CI: 2.0–7.0%). Protective HCWs (whose anti-HBs titer is ≥ 10 mIU/ml) will paradoxically be positive for HBsAg following chronic hepatitis B virus infection and mutations or deletions in the preS/S viral genomic region [47]. A potential explanation for the simultaneous HBsAg and anti-HBs serological profile has also occurred via alteration in the HBV ‘a’ determinant. The ‘a’ determinant of HBV consists of a serologically defined region that comprises the largest site of recognition of HBsAg by immune response cells and antibodies mapped to this region confer protection against all subtypes of HBV. Furthermore, host immunological state and genetic determinants may also be involved in the HBsAg/anti-HBs coexistence in individuals with chronic HBV infection [48]. Thus, concurrent HBsAg and anti-HBs constitute a peculiar serological pattern that may be observed in the context of persistent HBV infection.

In the present study the sero-positivity of anti-HBc in the fully vaccinated HCWs was 7.1% (95% CI: 5.0–10.0). This finding is relatively consist with the finding reported from Thailand (5.5%) [49]. On the other hand, this is higher than the study done in Brazil (4.4%) [50], Italy (3.3%) [42], and Iran (2.1%) [41]. However, it is lower than the findings reported from Gambian Villages (10.2%) [51]. The presence of anti-HBc typically indicates past or ongoing HBV infection, which does not happen in individuals whose immunity comes solely from vaccination. Hence, variations in anti-HBc positivity might reflect differences in exposure history or infection prevalence in the studied populations. Geographic and demographic factors, such as regional HBV endemicity and population mobility, could also impact the results [52]. In addition, methodological differences, such as variations in serological testing methods might contribute to discrepancies. Moreover, vaccine adherence and effectiveness could vary across regions, impacting the likelihood of breakthrough infections and subsequent anti-HBc positivity [53].

Additionally, anti-HBc positivity among HCWs may also be influenced by infection control practices and the immune status of study participants. Poor infection control measures, such as inadequate sterilization, improper disposal of medical waste, and lack of adherence to universal precautions, increase the risk of HBV exposure in hospital settings [54]. Additionally, the immune status of HCWs plays a critical role; individuals with compromised immunity or underlying conditions may be more susceptible to HBV infection despite vaccination, leading to higher anti-HBc sero-positivity [31].

Furthermore, participants who had previous history of injectable medications were 4.3 times more likely to be anti-HBc positive as compared to their counterparts. This might be due to blood born transmission risks from shared needles/syringes and contaminated equipment. Direct HBV exposure via blood-to-blood contact during drug use drives transmission of HBV infection.

Limitation of the study

Participants’ pre-vaccination HBV exposure history was not evaluated due to lack of information and resource constraints. In other words, for participants who were HBsAg positive, it was not possible to determine whether they were already infected prior to vaccination or if vaccine non-response contributed to acquisition of the infection. Data regarding the time elapsed since completion of vaccination was not collected, limiting the ability to better interpret vaccine response due to time and resource constraints. Moreover, as this study employed a cross-sectional design, it was difficult to establish causality (i.e., whether the exposure preceded the outcome or vice versa).

Conclusions and recommendations

The protective effectiveness of hepatitis B vaccine in the present study was intermediate. A high concentration of protective anti-HBs (≥ 10 mIU/mL) was observed among non-smokers. This study also revealed intermediate hepatitis B seroprevalence among fully vaccinated healthcare workers in the study area, suggesting suboptimal vaccine effectiveness. Moreover, blood transfusion and dental extraction were significantly associated with HBsAg sero-positivity. In addition, injectable medication was significant predicator for anti-HBc positivity.

Hence, we recommend implementing smoking cessation programs to facilitate quitting and enhance the immune response to hepatitis B vaccine. Additionally, personalized vaccination strategies for smokers should be considered, including booster vaccinations or higher immunogenicity vaccines, to improve vaccine effectiveness. Public awareness of the negative impact of smoking on vaccine effectiveness is crucial to encourage smoking cessation and enhance overall health outcomes.

Moreover, infection control measures must prioritize closed-system devices for blood transfusions, double-gloving and eye protection during dental procedures, and safety-engineered syringes for injectable medications to mitigate exposure risks. Occupational health interventions should enforce procedure-specific training on PEP protocols. Serological monitoring protocols should target high-risk HCWs (e.g., frequent blood/dental exposure) with biannual HBsAg/anti-HBc screening. Policy revisions must mandate HBV vaccination for high-exposure roles per Occupational Safety and Health Administration (OSHA) requirements. In addition, determination of levels of anti-HBs should be done, and then booster doses must be given for non-responders to increase the concentration of anti-HBs levels and prevent waning immunity. In other words, there should be routine anti-HBs titer testing post-vaccination to identify non-responders.
