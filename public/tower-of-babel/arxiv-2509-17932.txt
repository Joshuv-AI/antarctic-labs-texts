Training-free Truthfulness Detection via Value Vectors in LLMs
Runheng Liu, Heyan Huang, Xingchen Xiao, Zhijing Wu

Abstract
Large language models often generate factually incorrect outputs, motivating efforts to detect the truthfulness of their content. Most existing approaches rely on training probes over internal activations, but these methods suffer from scalability and generalization issues. A recent training-free method, NoVo, addresses this challenge by exploiting statistical patterns from the model itself. However, it focuses exclusively on attention mechanisms, potentially overlooking the MLP module—a core component of Transformer models known to support factual recall. In this paper, we show that certain value vectors within MLP modules exhibit truthfulness-related statistical patterns. Building on this insight, we propose TruthV, a simple and interpretable training-free method that detects content truthfulness by leveraging these value vectors. On the NoVo benchmark, TruthV significantly outperforms both NoVo and log-likelihood baselines, demonstrating that MLP modules—despite being neglected in prior training-free efforts—encode rich and useful signals for truthfulness detection. These findings offer new insights into how truthfulness is internally represented in LLMs and motivate further research on scalable and interpretable truthfulness detection.



1
Introduction

Large Language Models (LLMs) have achieved impressive performance across a broad range of tasks
Brown et al. 2020
;
OpenAI et al. 2024
. However, they often produce factually incorrect content—a phenomenon known as hallucination—which can mislead users and undermine trust in real-world applications. These risks have motivated growing interest in detecting the truthfulness of model-generated content as a means of mitigating hallucinations.

Most prior work approaches this goal by training probes on internal activations of LLMs to classify content as truthful or untruthful
Azaria and Mitchell 2023
;
Marks and Tegmark 2024
;
Li et al. 2023
;
Liu et al. 2024
;
Yuksekgonul et al. 2024
;
Chuang et al. 2024
. However, these methods require large amounts of labeled data and domain-specific training, limiting their scalability and generalization to out-of-distribution (OOD) data
Liu et al. 2024
;
Du et al. 2024
.

(a) illustrates the data flow through the MLP module when processing truthful content, which trigger certain key activations to be higher or lower. (b) shows an example of the argmax activation pattern where the correct answer results in a higher key activation compared to incorrect answers. The example is taken from the TruthfulQA (MC1) dataset.

To address these challenges,
Ho et al. 2025
recently proposed NoVo, a training-free method that detects truthfulness by measuring the norms of attention output—serving as statistical indicators derived directly from the model. While effective, NoVo focuses exclusively on attention mechanisms, implicitly assuming that other components, such as the MLP module, do not encode similarly informative signals. This assumption may overlook important opportunities. The MLP module is a core component of Transformer models and has been interpreted as key-value memories
Geva et al. 2021
. Prior work shows that its value vectors support factual recall and encode interpretable token-level concepts
Geva et al. 2022
;
Dai et al. 2022
, suggesting that they may also capture higher-level properties such as content truthfulness.

Motivated by this possibility, we investigate whether certain value vectors within MLP modules correlate with the truthfulness of content. Our analysis reveals that some value vectors exhibit consistent activation patterns when processing truthful versus untruthful contents—either increasing or decreasing in activation magnitude. We refer to these as argmax and argmin patterns. These value vectors are most prominent in the middle and final layers of the model. While they encode content-level truthfulness, they do not correspond to interpretable token-level concepts, suggesting that their signals are encoded in a more abstract and distributed manner.

Building on these findings, we propose TruthV, a simple and interpretable training-free method for truthfulness detection. TruthV treats each selected value vector as an individual predictor and ensembles their outputs. Specifically, for each input, it identifies the candidate answer that yields the highest (or lowest) activation under the argmax or argmin pattern. The final prediction is obtained by aggregating these individual predictions across all selected value vectors. On the NoVo benchmark, TruthV achieves an average accuracy of 70.33%, substantially outperforming both NoVo (61.67%) and log-likelihood baselines (49.25%). These results demonstrate that MLP modules—despite being previously overlooked—encode rich signals that support truthfulness detection without additional training.

2
Related Works

Truthfulness detection via internal probes.

A prominent line of work investigates the internal activations of LLMs for truthfulness detection by training probes
Alain and Bengio 2017
. These studies demonstrate that truthfulness-related signals can be extracted from layer outputs
Azaria and Mitchell 2023
;
Marks and Tegmark 2024
;
Li et al. 2023
, attention outputs
Li et al. 2023
;
Liu et al. 2024
, and attention weights
Yuksekgonul et al. 2024
;
Chuang et al. 2024
. However, probe-based approaches rely on labeled data and training for each target domain, making them less scalable and often vulnerable to poor generalization on OOD inputs
Liu et al. 2024
;
Du et al. 2024
. In contrast, our work aligns with recent efforts to detect truthfulness without training. NoVo
Ho et al. 2025
shows that the norms of attention head outputs can correlate with truthfulness and selects attention heads based on this correlation using simple statistics. Yet, such method focuses solely on the attention mechanism, overlooking the MLP module—a core component of Transformer architectures. We explore whether MLP modules also contain statistical patterns that support training-free detection.

Analysis of MLP modules.

The MLP modules of Transformer models have been increasingly studied as meaningful computation units beyond simple feedforward layers.
Geva et al. 2021
propose that MLPs can be viewed as key-value memories, where each column of the final projection matrix serves as a value vector, and the corresponding non-linear activation acts as its key. Building on this formulation,
Dai et al. 2022
find that key activations in BERT correlate with factual knowledge expression in cloze-style tasks.
Geva et al. 2022
further show that individual value vectors in GPT-2 encode interpretable concepts (e.g., food or drink) and influence specific token predictions. While these works suggest that value vectors encode meaningful and sometimes factual information, they primarily focus on token-level concept associations. In contrast, we explore whether value vectors encode not only token-level concepts but also content-level properties such as truthfulness. Our work extends this line of research by identifying truthfulness-related value vectors and leveraging them for training-free truthfulness detection.

3
Preliminaries

MLP module in LLMs.

The MLP module is applied after the multi-head attention module in each Transformer layer, serving to project representations to a higher-dimensional space and to introduce nonlinearity through activation functions. Recent LLMs commonly employ Gated Linear Units (GLUs) and their variants as activation functions
Shazeer 2020
.
For example, Llama-2
Touvron et al. 2023
, Llama-3
Grattafiori et al. 2024
and Qwen3
Yang et al. 2025
adopt SwiGLU, and Gemma-2
Team et al. 2024
employs GeGLU. With GLU-based activations, the output of the MLP module is given by

\displaystyle m=

\displaystyle W_{\text{down}}(f(W_{\text{gate}}h)\odot(W_{\text{up}}h)),

(1)

where
h\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
is the input to the MLP,
f
denotes an element-wise activation function, and
\odot
denotes the Hadamard product. Here,
W_{\text{gate}},W_{\text{up}}\in\mathbb{R}^{d^{\prime}\times d}
and
W_{\text{down}}\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times d^{\prime}}
, where typically
d^{\prime}>d
.

Memory interpretation for MLP.

Geva et al. 2021
suggests that the MLP module can be interpreted as a key-value memory, where each column of the final projection matrix
W_{\text{down}}
acts as a value vector, and the corresponding activation serves as a key. Under this view, Eq.
1
can be reformulated as

m=\sum_{i=1}^{d^{\prime}}k_{i}v_{i},

(2)

where
k_{i}=f(w_{\text{gate},i}^{T}h)(w_{\text{up},i}^{T}h)
and
v_{i}=w_{\text{down},i}
. Here,
w_{\text{gate},i},w_{\text{up},i}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
are the
i
th rows of
W_{\text{gate}}
and
W_{\text{up}}
, respectively, and
w_{\text{down},i}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
is the
i
th column of
W_{\text{down}}
.

4
Identifying Truthfulness-related Value Vectors

To investigate whether certain value vectors within MLP modules are associated with content truthfulness, we focus on the task of multiple-choice question (MCQ), where a model must select the correct answer from a set of candidates. For each question
q
with
M
candidate answers
\{a_{i}\}_{i=1}^{M}
, we construct input sequences
\{[q;a_{i}]\}_{i=1}^{M}
, where the prefix
q
is shared and variation in truthfulness is confined to the answer suffix. These sequences are then fed into the model to analyze how key activations vary with respect to the truthfulness of the input.

4.1
Settings

Dataset.

We utilize data from NoVo benchmark
Ho et al. 2025
, which includes various MCQ datasets covering diverse topics, formats, and reasoning abilities. These datasets comprise TruthfulQA MC1 (TQA)
Lin et al. 2022
for evaluating truthfulness, CommonsenseQA 2.0 (CSQA2)
Talmor et al. 2022
for commonsense reasoning, QASC
Khot et al. 2020
for scientific knowledge, SWAG
Zellers et al. 2018
and HellaSwag (HSwag)
Zellers et al. 2019
for challenging commonsense sentence completions, SIQA
Sap et al. 2019
for social reasoning, PIQA
Bisk et al. 2020
for physical reasoning, CosmosQA (Cosmos)
Huang et al. 2019
for causal reasoning in narratives, and CICERO V1 and V2 (CICv1, CICv2)
Ghosal et al. 2022
;
Shen et al. 2022
for multi-turn and strategic reasoning in dialogues. For each dataset, NoVo provides an optimal instruction along with 30 labeled samples for exploitation. These samples are drawn from the training set of each dataset, except for TQA, which lacks a training set; its samples are taken from the the ARC-Easy training set
Clark et al. 2018
, making TQA a zero-shot case.
Following the NoVo’ setting, we prepend the given instruction to
[q,a_{i}]
, ensuring a uniform content format across examples. We use the full training set of each dataset except for TQA during this experiment.

Models.

We utilize various modern instruction-tuned LLMs in experiment, including Llama-2-7B-Chat
Touvron et al. 2023
, Gemma-2-2B-it
Team et al. 2024
, Llama-3.2-3B-Instruct
Grattafiori et al. 2024
, and Qwen3-4B
Yang et al. 2025
.

Activation patterns triggered by truthful content.

We hypothesize that certain value vectors in the MLP modules encode information relevant to truthfulness, and that their corresponding key activations are strongly modulated depending on whether the content is truthful. Considering the formulation of the scalar key activation
k_{i}=f(w_{\text{gate},i}^{T}h)(w_{\text{up},i}^{T}h)
(see Eq.
2
), such modulation can result in either the highest or the lowest activation across candidate answers for a given question. We refer to these two cases as exhibiting
argmax
and
argmin
activation patterns, respectively.

Scoring and ranking value vectors.

Based on these activation patterns, we evaluate the ability of each value vector to distinguish correct answers, in order to identify those most aligned with truthfulness. Following prior work
Li et al. 2023
;
Marks and Tegmark 2024
;
Ho et al. 2025
, we focus on the final token of each input sequence. For the argmax pattern, we compute the accuracy of each value vector across the dataset
\mathcal{D}
as:

\frac{1}{|\mathcal{D}|}\sum_{i=1}^{|\mathcal{D}|}\left[(\arg\max_{j}k_{i,j})=l_{i}\right],

(3)

where
k_{i,j}
denotes the key activation for the
j
th candidate in the
i
th MCQ, and
l_{i}
is the index of the correct answer. We rank all value vectors by this score and select the top
p
as the most strongly aligned with the argmax activation pattern. A symmetric procedure is used to identify value vectors aligned with the argmin activation pattern, by selecting those whose key activations are minimized by the correct answer.

4.2
Results

Existence of truthfulness-related value vectors.

We compute accuracy rankings of value vectors and consistently identify a small subset of them that are strongly associated with truthfulness. As an example, Figure
2
illustrates the results of various model-dataset pair. While most value vectors perform near the random baseline, a small subset achieves significantly higher accuracy under the argmax pattern—suggesting a reliable connection to truthfulness. Conversely, some value vectors fall below the baseline, likely because they align better with the argmin activation pattern. To investigate this hypothesis, we plot the argmin accuracy using the same argmax-based ranking. Interestingly, we observe an opposite tend: as the argmax rank increases, argmin accuracy also tends to increase. This symmetry highlights the complementarity between the two patterns—value vectors that perform poorly under one may align well with the other. These results provide concrete evidence for the existence of truthfulness-related value vectors in LLMs.

Layer-wise distribution of truthfulness-related value vectors.

We next examine where in the model these value vectors are concentrated. For each dataset, we select the top 0.1% of value vectors by accuracy, assuming these are the most indicative of truthfulness, based on the above analysis. We then aggregate these value vectors and analyze their distribution across the layers of each model. As shown in Figure
3
, these value vectors are predominantly located in the middle and final layers, and this trend holds consistently across different models. The distributions for both argmax and argmin patterns are highly similar, again highlighting their underlying complementarity. Notably, we observe no such vectors in the early layers, indicating that truthfulness-related modulation is largely absent from early processing stages and begins to emerge in the middle layers. This observation aligns with prior work showing that internal activations from later layers are especially informative for truthfulness detection
Li et al. 2023
;
Marks and Tegmark 2024
;
Azaria and Mitchell 2023
;
Burns et al. 2023
;
Chuang et al. 2024
.

Semantic interpretability of truthfulness-related value vectors.

Previous work has shown that some value vectors in transformer models encode human-interpretable concepts, such as names of foods or drinks
Geva et al. 2022
. These vectors can be projected into the vocabulary space, enabling a rough approximation of their semantics by identifying the tokens with which are most closely aligned. Based on this perspective, we select the value vector with the highest accuracy for each dataset and assess whether it exhibits similar interpretability. Following
Geva et al. 2022
, the value vector
v
is projected into the vocabulary space via
r=Ev\in\mathbb{R}^{|\mathcal{V}|}
, where
E
is the output embedding matrix of the model and
|\mathcal{V}|
is the vocabulary size. We then analyze the top-10 tokens with the highest projection scores. Notably, we find no evidence of surface-level semantic alignment between the top-scoring tokens and the concept of truthfulness. This contrasts with findings in GPT-2 under similar projection-based analysis, where many value vectors were aligned with interpretable concepts
Geva et al. 2022
. However, even in that study, approximately 50% of value vectors could not be matched to any identifiable concept. It is therefore plausible that the selected truthfulness-related value vectors belong to this non-interpretable subset, which may encode more abstract or distributed properties. Moreover, differences in model architecture—such as the use of GLU-based MLPs in modern LLMs compared to the GELU-based ones in GPT-2—could further reduce the direct interpretability of individual value vectors. Furthermore, t-SNE visualizations of these selected value vectors reveal no clear clustering, further suggesting that they do not form a semantically coherent subspace.

Context-dependent key activations of truthfulness-related value vectors.

While key activations associated with the truthfulness-related value vectors systematically vary in magnitude—either increasing or decreasing—when processing truthful versus untruthful inputs, it remains unclear whether a consistent threshold can reliably separate truthful form untruthful inputs. We use the PIQA dataset, a two-choice QA dataset, which enables the construction of balanced sets of truthful and untruthful content for comparison. Specifically, we select the value vector with the highest accuracy on the training set and analyze its key activations on the same data. As shown in Figure
4
, although these value vectors achieve the highest accuracy, the distribution of their key activations under truthful and untruthful content largely overlap—a pattern observed consistently across different models. This implies that the question prefix determines the baseline range of the key activation, whereas the answer suffix—i.e., the truthful or untruthful choice—modulates its direction within that range.

5
TruthV: Training-free Truthfulness Detection via Value Vectors in LLM

Our previous analysis has demonstrated the existence of truthfulness-related value vectors in LLMs. Building on these findings, we next examine how these value vectors can be employed to detect the truthfulness of content.

5.1
Settings

Datasets.

We utilize data from NoVo benchmark
Ho et al. 2025
. In contrast to the previous section, which used the full training set to select truthfulness-related value vectors for analysis, we here adopt the 30 provided examples for fair comparison with baseline methods.

Method.

Previous studies have proposed ensembling various truthfulness predictors to improve prediction accuracy
Ho et al. 2025
;
CH-Wang et al. 2024
;
Himmi et al. 2024
. In our settings, each value vector acts as an individual predictor. We select the top-
p
value vectors based on their individual performance on the 30 provided samples and ensemble their predictions. Since these predictions are not expressed as normalized probability distributions, we follow the methodology of
Ho et al. 2025
, by computing the mode of the individual predictions. For the argmax pattern, the
c
-th value vector predicts an answer by choosing the candidate with the highest key activation:
a^{c}=\arg\max_{j}k^{c}_{j}
, where
k^{c}_{j}
is the key activation corresponding to the
c
-th value vector when applied to the
j
th input
[q;a_{j}]
. We aggregate the predictions
\{a^{c}\}
via majority voting:

\hat{a}=\arg\max_{a\in M}\text{Count}(a,\{a^{c}\}),

(4)

where
M
is the set of all candidate answer indices, and
\text{Count}(a,\cdot)
returns the number of times answer
a
appears among the predictions. The final predicted answer
\hat{a}
is the one that receives the most votes. A symmetric procedure is applied for the argmin pattern.

Baselines.

We compare our method against two baselines: NoVo and Log-Likelihood
Ho et al. 2025
. NoVo leverages the 30 provided samples to select a subset of attention heads. It uses the norm of their output to make predictions, and the predictions from the top-
p
heads are aggregated via voting to obtain the final prediction. In the Log-Likelihood method, the content with the highest log-likelihood score is chosen as the final prediction.

Metrics.

Following the setting of benchmark, we report accuracy as the evaluation metrics.

Implementation details.

All experiments are conducted on an NVIDIA A100-40G GPU.

Main Results

In Table

TruthV consistently achieves the best average accuracy across all four LLMs, outperforming existing baselines by a significant margin. Ablations

Effect of the number of selected value vectors.

We examine the impact of the hyper-parameter
p
, which controls the number of value vectors included in the ensemble, by varying it from 0.01% to 1%. As shown in Figure
5
, performance improves rapidly as
p
increases, reaching a peak at a relatively small
p
, and then declines more gradually as
p
continue to grow. However, the subsequent drop in accuracy is relatively small. We hypothesize that when
p
is too small, the selected value vectors may be overly specific to the 30 labeled samples used for selection, leading to poor generalization. In contrast, setting
p
too high may introduce unrelated value vectors into the voting pool, which degrades performance. We further observe that both activation patterns follow similar performance trajectories across different
p
values, indicating that their behavior with respect to ensemble size is broadly consistent. In addition, we observe that the optimal value of
p
varies across LLMs. To identify a unified setting, we compute the average accuracy across all four models and find that the performance peaks around
p=0.001
. Accordingly, we adopt
p=0.001
for all models in our experiments.

Effect of activation pattern.

Table
1
has shown that the value vectors selected under both argmax and argmin activation patterns can detect the truthfulness of content. Here, we examine whether combining their predictions can lead to further improvements. Specifically, we aggregate the predictions from both activation patterns and perform majority voting over the combined sets. As shown in Figure
5
, the optimal ensemble size for the combined pattern follows a similar trend to those observed for the individual argmax and argmin patterns. Moreover, the combined pattern slightly outperforms both individual patterns. This marginal improvement suggests that the value vectors selected by the two activation patterns largely encode overlapping information, but also contribute minor complementary signals.

5.4
Analysis

Efficiency of selection.

The results of the above experiments demonstrate that even with only 30 samples, the selected value vectors yield competitive performance. Intuitively, incorporating more samples into the selection process should improve the performance of TruthV. Conclusion

In this paper, we investigate whether the MLP modules of LLMs encode statistical patterns related to content truthfulness. Building on the key-value memory perspective of MLP modules, we identify specific value vectors whose key activations consistently vary between truthful and untruthful inputs. We propose TruthV, a simple and interpretable training-free method to detect content truthfulness. Experiments on the NoVo benchmark show that TruthV significantly outperforms both NoVo and log-likelihood baselines. Our findings demonstrate that MLP modules contain interpretable patterns that correlate with truthfulness and can be directly exploited without additional training. This work provides new insights into the internal structure of LLMs and highlights the potential of leveraging statistical patterns for scalable and interpretable truthfulness detection.
