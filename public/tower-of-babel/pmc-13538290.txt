Cognitive and motor profiles in biologically defined Parkinson's and Alzheimer's disease
Authors: Daniel Zarhin, Noa Bregman, Tamara Shiner, Anat Mirelman, Roy N Alcalay, Avner Thaler
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundParkinson's disease (PD) patients may harbor coexisting Alzheimer's disease (AD) pathology that accelerates cognitive and motor decline. Defining biomarker-defined AD in PD is important for prognosis, patient counseling, and trial stratification.ObjectiveTo determine the prevalence of AD biomarker positivity in α-synuclein seed amplification assay (αSyn-SAA) positive PD and assess its impact on cognitive and motor progression.MethodsWe analyzed data from the Parkinson's Progression Markers Initiative, a multinational prospective cohort of de novo PD patients. Baseline CSF biomarkers included αSyn-SAA, amyloid-β1-42 (Aβ1-42), and phosphorylated tau181 (p-tau181). AD biomarker positivity was defined by a low Aβ1-42/high p-tau181 profile using the CSF Aβ1-42/p-tau181 ratio (<39.2). αSyn-SAA-positive PD participants with and without AD biomarker positivity were compared. Outcomes included cognition, Montreal Cognitive Assessment (MoCA), neuropsychological testing, and MDS-UPDRS motor scores over follow-up.ResultsAmong 449 αSyn-SAA-positive PD patients, 42 (9.3%) met AD biomarker criteria (PD-AD). Baseline cognition and motor scores did not differ between PD-AD and PD without AD biomarkers (PD-nonAD). Over time, PD-AD patients showed greater cognitive decline, with lower MoCA scores and higher MCI prevalence at 5 years. At 8 years, PD-AD patients also demonstrated worse motor outcomes. MoCA <24 predicted AD biomarker positivity (PPV 15%; OR 6.1). APOE ε4 status was not associated with cognition.ConclusionsBiomarker-defined AD identifies a distinct PD subgroup with accelerated cognitive and motor decline, independent of APOE ε4. A ratio-based CSF Aβ1-42/p-tau181 framework offers a practical approach to detect AD copathology in PD and refine prognostic stratification.

Introduction

Parkinson's disease (PD) and Alzheimer's disease (AD) are the two most common neurodegenerative diseases. 1 Traditionally, both diseases were defined according to clinical criteria,2,3 and therefore regarded as separate conditions. While age is among the leading risk factors for both conditions, each disease has a distinct pathological basis. 1 AD is defined by extracellular Amyloid beta (Aβ) plaques and intracellular neurofibrillary tangles, containing hyperphosphorilated tau (ptau), whereas PD is distinguished by α-synuclein-containing Lewy bodies.

The majority of individuals with PD will ultimately experience dementia (PDD), characterized by progressive cognitive decline impacting daily function, as their condition progresses. 4 However, the onset and the pattern of dementia varies significantly, with some patients remaining cognitively intact for many years after their diagnosis. 4 Understanding its underlying pathology of PDD is important, 5 evidence suggests that the coexistence of Lewy body pathology and AD-related changes is the strongest pathological predictor of dementia in PD. 6

Growing evidence indicates that a substantial number of patients with synucleinopathies, including PD, exhibit AD-like pathology. 3 Accumulation of Aβ in parietal and occipital regions has been linked to cognitive decline in early PD, underscoring amyloid co-pathology's contribution to clinical variability. 7 Furthermore, postmortem studies in PD show that coexisting AD pathology is linked to a shorter interval between motor and cognitive symptom onset and reduced survival. 8

The 2024 revised AD diagnostic criteria introduced by the Alzheimer's Association emphasize a biomarker-driven framework for diagnosing AD. 9 This approach relies on Amyloid, Tau, and Neurodegeneration (ATN) biomarkers and suggests a diagnosis of AD even in asymptomatic individuals. Cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) levels of Aβ1-42, total tau (t-tau), and phosphorylated tau (p-tau) at threonine 181 (p-tau-181) have been established as highly reliable biomarkers for identifying individuals with early AD.

In PD, α-synuclein seed amplification assays (αSyn-SAAs) detect misfolded αSyn aggregates in CSF. Initially developed to aid the biochemical diagnosis of Lewy body disorders such as PD and dementia with Lewy bodies, they have more recently proven useful in identifying Lewy body co-pathology in clinically diagnosed AD. 10 Recently, PD research has adopted a biological classification framework integrating α-synuclein pathology, neurodegeneration, and genetic factors to refine diagnosis and classification.11,12 Applying these biological criteria for AD and PD enables systematic assessment of AD pathology prevalence among PD patients within recently proposed diagnostic frameworks.

Cross-sectional analyses of the Parkinson's Progression Markers Initiative (PPMI) cohort have shown that early, untreated PD patients exhibit subtle CSF alterations, including lower Aβ1-42 and p-tau181 levels compared with controls, suggesting partially overlapping but distinct pathology from AD. 13 Emerging evidence further suggests that CSF biomarker profiles may relate to cognitive, motor, and autonomic outcomes in PD, 14 supporting their potential role in disease stratification and in characterizing AD co-pathology within PD cohorts.

A recent study applied a modified ATN framework to the PPMI cohort to examine whether combined biofluid biomarkers could predict cognitive decline in early PD. 15 Using CSF Aβ1-42, p-tau181, and serum neurofilament light chain (NfL), and incorporating PD-optimized cutoffs, participants were classified along an AD-related pathological spectrum. The modified ATN-PD framework identified biologically meaningful subgroups and improved prediction of cognitive decline, but not motor progression.

While ATN-PD provided important insights into cognitive and motor outcomes over five years, further opportunities remain to extend longitudinal follow-up, particularly for motor trajectories, incorporate additional PD-specific pathology markers such as alpha-synuclein, and use simpler, more widely accessible biomarker metrics. Our study builds on this framework by employing the Aβ1-42/p-tau181 ratio, which may provide a practical alternative to PD-specific cutoffs, supported by evidence that p-tau181 retains prognostic utility even when using AD derived thresholds. 15 We also integrate αSyn-SAAs to improve patient stratification and extend follow-up time.

This dual, biomarker-based approach allows for a more comprehensive evaluation of AD copathology in PD and provides a practical means to examine its impact on long-term clinical trajectories.

We hypothesized that SAA-positive PD patients in the PPMI cohort with evidence of AD pathology, identified using combined biofluid biomarkers, would exhibit faster cognitive decline and greater disease progression. Detecting these coexisting pathologies is critical, as they may influence long-term trajectories in PD and could help guide patient stratification for targeted interventions, independent of conventional diagnostic categories. 16

Methods

Study population

This study utilized data from the Parkinson's Progression Markers Initiative (PPMI) database (www.ppmi-info.org/data). 17

We analyzed PPMI data for PD patients and healthy controls with available biomarker data, including αSyn-SAAs, CSF Aβ1-42, and P-tau181. For comparison, αSyn-SAA-positive PD cases with AD biomarker positivity were contrasted with αSyn-SAA-positive PD cases without. Baseline measures were taken from the CSF sample collected at the first clinical visit.

Participants classified as SWEDD (Scans Without Evidence of Dopaminergic Deficit) were excluded from this analysis because they lack confirmed dopaminergic deficits.

For Aβ1-42 and p-tau181 measurements in PPMI CSF samples, we used data generated by Shaw et al., 18 who analyzed the samples using Elecsys Aβ1-42, t-tau, and p-tau181 electrochemiluminescence immunoassays on a fully automated cobas e 601 analyzer (Roche Diagnostics). For CSF α-synuclein oligomers, we utilized available data generated by Amprion's 24-h Seed Amplification Assay. 19 Full methodological details for Aβ1-42, p-tau181, and αSyn-SAA measurements are provided in the PPMI Biospecimen Analysis Methods documentation. Data used in the preparation of this article were obtained on August 25, 2025, from the Parkinson's Progression Markers Initiative (PPMI) database (https://www.ppmi-info.org/access-data-specimens/download-data), RRID:SCR_006431. For up-to-date information on the study, visit http://www.ppmi-info.org.

Standard protocol approvals, registrations, and patient consents

The PPMI was established to identify biomarkers tracking PD progression and to define biologically and clinically distinct PD subgroups. This multinational, longitudinal study includes hundreds of newly diagnosed idiopathic PD patients and healthy controls. The inclusion and exclusion criteria for the PPMI are detailed in the study's Archive of Research Documents and Standard Operating Procedures (SOPs).

Enrollment for the de novo PD cohort began in June 2010, with longitudinal clinical, imaging, and biospecimen data collection ongoing. All PPMI sites received ethical approval and obtained written informed consent, adhering to the Declaration of Helsinki.

Clinical and cognitive evaluations

Demographics included sex, age at assessment, age at PD diagnosis, years since diagnosis, and education. Clinical evaluations included the total and Part III (motor) scores of the Movement Disorder Society-Unified Parkinson's Disease Rating Scale (MDS-UPDRS). 20 Global cognitive function was assessed using the Montreal Cognitive Assessment (MoCA), 21 the Investigator's Diagnosis of Cognitive State (categorized as 1 = Normal, 2 = Mild Cognitive Impairment [MCI], 3 = Dementia), and a test-based MCI definition. The latter classified individuals according to the criteria proposed by Litvan et al., 22 defining test-based MCI as having at least two cognitive test scores more than 1.5 standard deviations below the standardized mean (0 = Normal cognition, 1 = Test-based MCI).

Participants were classified as having AD biomarker positivity based on the 2024 AD diagnostic framework, 9 which defines AD biologically based on amyloid and tau pathology, if their CSF Aβ42/p-tau181 ratio was < 39.2 (Supplementary Fig. 1c). This threshold is grounded in the work of Leuzy et al., 23 who validated the ratio against amyloid PET imaging and clinical outcomes across multiple cohorts. Our application of this cutoff reflects both the published evidence and clinical expert consensus at our institution. Supporting CSF biomarker distributions for Aβ42 and ptau181 are shown in Supplementary Fig. 1a-(b).

Neuropsychological assessment

Cognition was assessed in four domains using standardized tests: memory (HVLT), processing speed and attention (SDMT), visuospatial ability (JLO), and semantic fluency (MSF). Scores were compared between PD patients with and without AD biomarker positivity.

Assessment of genetic contributions to cognitive decline

To investigate potential genetic influences on cognitive decline in PD patients with AD biomarker positivity, we assessed the role of the APOE ε4 allele. Within the PD-AD, we compared cognitive outcomes between APOE ε4 carriers and non-carriers. Due to the low sample size in each subgroup, homozygous and heterozygous carriers were combined for analysis.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analyses were performed using GraphPad Prism 9.0. Continuous variables were assessed for distributional characteristics and are presented as mean ± standard deviation. Categorical variables are presented as number and percentage. Comparisons between groups were performed using independent samples t-test or Mann-Whitney U test for continuous variables and chi-square test for categorical variables, as appropriate. To account for multiple comparisons, p-values were adjusted using the Bonferroni correction, when necessary, as described in the relevant figure legends. Multivariable analyses were performed to adjust for age. Continuous cognitive and clinical outcomes were analyzed using multiple linear regression models, with group status (PD-AD vs. PD-nonAD) and age included as independent variables. Results are presented as regression coefficients (β), and p-values, with β representing the adjusted mean difference in the outcome associated with PD-AD status after accounting for age. Categorical outcomes were analyzed using multiple logistic regression models with group status and age as predictors.

Levodopa equivalent daily dose (LEDD) was calculated at the 5-year and 8-year follow-up time points. 24 For each participant, LEDD was calculated as the sum of the LEDD values from all active antiparkinsonian medications at the respective follow-up time point, based on medication start and stop dates relative to the participant's baseline visit date.

All tests were two-sided, and statistical significance was defined as p < 0.05. The number of participants included in each comparison varied due to missing data for some measures. Sample sizes, mean scores, regression coefficients (β), odds ratios, 95% confidence intervals, and p-values are reported in the corresponding tables.

Forty-two individuals in the PD group (9.3%) and eighteen individuals in the healthy controls group (9.8%) were classified as having AD biomarker positivity (Table 1), based on pTau 181 and Aβ1-42 status (Supplementary Fig 1a-c). Although the PD group had a higher number of individuals meeting the AD biomarker positivity criterion, this difference did not reach statistical significance (p = 0.7, Table 1).

When the PD group was divided into PD without AD biomarker positivity (PD-nonAD) and PD with AD biomarker positivity (PD-AD), PD-AD patients were older than PD-nonAD (mean age 67 vs 60, p = 0.0001; Table 1), had a higher age at PD diagnosis (mean 67.1 vs 59.3, p = 0.0001; Table 1), and had slightly longer disease duration (3.7 vs 2.3 years, p = 0.01; Table 1). No significant differences were observed in sex or years of education between the groups (p = 0.3 and p = 0.1, respectively; Table 1).

To evaluate potential vascular confounders that could influence subsequent cognitive outcomes, we also compared the prevalence of common comorbidities, including hypertension and diabetes. Although these comorbidities were numerically more frequent in the PD-nonAD group, the differences were not statistically significant (p = 0.07 and 0.24, respectively; Table 1).

Cognitive and motor status in PD with and without biomarker-defined AD

We next examined the relationship between CSF biomarker-defined subgroups and cognitive outcomes. Given the significant age difference between the groups, all comparisons were performed using multivariable models adjusted for age. At baseline, MoCA scores did not differ significantly between PD-AD and PD-nonAD (mean 27.5 vs. 27.0, respectively, p = 0.81; Table 1). Similarly, categorical cognitive assessments showed no significant differences: 12.5% of PD-AD patients were classified as having MCI according to the Investigator Diagnosis of Cognitive State, compared with 8.9% of PD-nonAD patients (p = 0.81, Table 1). Likewise, 19.0% of PD-AD patients met Test-Based MCI criteria compared with 16.0% of PD-nonAD patients (p = 0.57, Table 1). Motor severity was also comparable between the groups. Total MDS-UPDRS scores were similar in PD-AD and PD-nonAD patients (35.3 vs. 32.5, respectively, p = 0.51; Table 1), as were MDS-UPDRS Part III (motor examination) scores (21.8 vs. 21.0, respectively, p = 0.79; Table 1).

Longitudinal analysis of cognitive and motor progression in PD with biomarker-defined AD

To examine whether differences in cognitive and motor status in the PD-AD group become pronounced over time, we repeated the analysis using data from assessments conducted after five and eight years from baseline. Since not all participants had follow-up data within this time frame, the sample size was smaller for these comparisons, and the available data varied across different parameters (see tables for details). Notably, attrition rates were significantly higher in the PD-AD group compared to PD-nonAD (43.9% vs. 15%, p = 0.0001; Supplementary Figure 1D), whereas attrition rates were not statistically different between healthy controls and healthy controls with AD biomarker positivity (17% vs 36%, p = 0.7; Supplementary Figure 1E).

After adjustment for age, PD-AD patients exhibited greater cognitive decline than PD-nonAD patients at the 5-year follow-up. Mean MoCA scores were significantly lower in the PD-AD group than in the PD-nonAD group (22.1 vs. 26.2, respectively, p = 0.0001; Table 2). Likewise, a higher proportion of PD-AD patients were classified as having MCI according to both the Investigator Diagnosis of Cognitive State (58.3% vs. 20.0%, p = 0.005; Table 2) and Test-Based MCI criteria (60.0% vs. 19.0%, p = 0.002; Table 2). Total MDS-UPDRS scores and MDS-UPDRS Part III motor examination scores were comparable between PD-AD and PD-nonAD patients (57.9 vs. 55.0 with p = 0.76 and 34.0 vs. 31.6 with p = 0.3, respectively; Table 2).

To assess whether differences in dopaminergic treatment may have contributed to these findings, we compared the summed levodopa equivalent daily dose (LEDD) of all active antiparkinsonian medications at the 5-year follow-up. No significant differences in summed LEDD were observed between PD-AD and PD-nonAD patients (757 vs. 636 mg/day, respectively, p = 0.44; Table 2).

Cognitive performance was assessed across four domains: memory (Hopkins Verbal Learning Test, HVLT), processing speed and attention (Symbol Digit Modalities Test, SDMT), visuospatial ability (Judgment of Line Orientation, JLO), and semantic fluency (Semantic Fluency Test, MSF). At baseline, cognitive performance was largely comparable between groups, with the exception of lower SDMT scores in PD-AD patients. At the 5-year follow-up, however, PD-AD patients consistently scored lower across all cognitive measures, indicating greater cognitive impairment (Supplementary Fig. 2).

At the eight-year follow-up, PD-AD patients demonstrated significantly worse motor outcomes, with higher total MDS-UPDRS scores compared with PD-nonAD patients (69.5 vs. 53.4, respectively, p = 0.008; Table 2) and higher MDS-UPDRS Part III motor examination scores (40.6 vs. 32.5, respectively, p = 0.04; Table 2). Similar to the five-year analysis, the summed LEDD of all active antiparkinsonian medications at the eight-year follow-up did not differ significantly between PD-AD and PD-nonAD patients (693 vs. 815 mg/day, respectively; p = 0.65; Table 2). To assess whether cognitive decline could serve as a marker of underlying AD biomarker positivity, participants were stratified according to MoCA score at five years from baseline (<24 vs. ≥ 24). A MoCA score <24 was associated with increased odds of PD-AD status (OR 6.1; 95% CI, 1.5–14.9), although the positive predictive value was limited (15%; Supplementary Fig. 1g).

Discussion

Pathological studies demonstrate that cognitive decline and dementia in PD can be of multifactorial biological etiologies. The most common causes of dementia are extension of PD-like pathology to the cortex (Lewy bodies) and existence of AD-pathology. 8 Until recently, PD and AD were defined clinically, and PD patients developing dementia were classified as PDD without specifying underlying pathology. With the shift to biomarker-based diagnoses, we tested whether a clinically distinguishable PD-AD entity exists and assessed the extent and relevance of AD pathology in PD. By focusing on α-synuclein SAA-positive PD patients, we aimed to isolate a pathologically homogeneous PD population and evaluate the clinical relevance of AD biomarker positivity using a practical, ratio-based CSF approach.

At baseline, after adjustment for age using multivariable regression analyses, AD biomarker positivity in PD was not linked to worse cognitive or motor scores (Table 1). Because PD-AD patients were significantly older than PD-nonAD patients at baseline, all subsequent group comparisons were adjusted for age. By five- and eight-year follow-ups, PD-AD patients showed significantly worse cognitive and motor outcomes than PD-nonAD, with differences in MoCA, MCI rates, and MDS-UPDRS scores persisting or increasing, suggesting AD pathology in PD drives faster clinical decline (Table 2).

Building on the ATN-PD framework, which demonstrated the value of combining CSF Aβ1-42, p-tau181, and serum NfL to predict cognitive decline over five years, 15 our findings highlight that the Aβ1-42/p-tau181 ratio provides a practical, biologically informed cutoff for identifying PD patients with AD pathology. In our α-synuclein SAA positive cohort, this ratio-based approach was associated with accelerated cognitive decline and over extended follow-up, worse motor outcomes, underscoring its utility for stratifying patients and tracking long-term disease trajectories.

Furthermore, the association with motor decline suggests that AD pathology may drive a more aggressive PD course, consistent with prior biomarker studies showing CSF Aβ and tau abnormalities predict worse cognitive and motor outcomes.13,14

The convergence of motor and cognitive decline in PD patients with AD biomarkers suggests shared or interacting mechanisms, as cerebral multi-pathology is common and “pure” pathology is rare on postmortem studies. 26 Mixed PD-AD pathology was present in 7.5% of dementia cases 27 with high proportions of dementia with Lewy bodies and PDD cases showing additional intermediate or high-level AD pathology. 28

Motor and cognitive function prior to death correlate with postmortem indices of AD pathology, 29 supporting the view that age-related decline across these domains may arise from common pathological processes. Furthermore, extrapyramidal signs (gait disturbance, bradykinesia, rigidity, tremor) are common in older adults, but their relationship to cognitive decline and AD development remains poorly understood. 30 Longitudinal studies suggest that the progression of parkinsonian signs in aging is associated with worsening cognition and an increased risk of AD. In one cohort of more than 700 older individuals followed annually for up to 8 years, baseline parkinsonian signs assessed with the MDS-UPDRS were linked to a higher likelihood of developing incident AD. 31 Notwithstanding, we did not identify any specific cognitive domain that was more affected among PD-AD, but rather a reduction in all domains assessed (Supplementary Figure 2).

In PD, amyloid-β and tau may amplify cortical and network dysfunction, especially in frontoparietal and hippocampal regions, accelerating cognitive decline. Elevated amyloid in non-demented PD predicted faster executive deterioration but was not associated with motor progression. 32 This overlap supports a motor-cognitive phenotype, where coexisting pathologies amplify deficits. Amyloid pathology was linked to MCI in early PD and correlated with reduced dopaminergic activity in the left ventral striatum. 33 This suggests that Aβ-positive PD patients show AD-related cognitive pathology within PD, with reduced ventral striatal dopamine potentially disrupting striatal and limbic circuits. Such disruption may contribute to both cognitive and motor impairments, highlighting the impact of coexisting amyloid pathology on PD.

Attrition was higher in the PD-AD group than in PD-nonAD (Supplementary Figure 1D), with no significant difference in healthy controls (Supplementary Figure 1E). This may reflect greater disease burden, frailty, or mortality in PD-AD, supporting the link between AD biomarker positivity and severe phenotype. Alternatively, selective retention of milder cases could underestimate the true clinical burden in PD-AD.

Interestingly, about 10% of the healthy controls harbored AD biomarker positivity, with intact cognitive functions at baseline, as the PPMI protocol recruited controls with MoCA scores above 26. At the 5-year follow-up, controls with AD biomarker positivity had slightly lower MoCA scores compared with those without AD biomarker positivity (26.1 vs 27.8), although the effect size was small (Supplementary Figure 1F).

Finally, when examining genetic risk factors such as APOE ε4, no significant associations were found with cognitive outcomes (Table 3). Although PD-AD patients had a higher proportion of APOE ε4 carriers compared with PD-nonAD (47% vs 22%; Table 1), these findings suggest that, in the context of AD pathology, cognitive decline in PD may be primarily driven by AD related mechanisms rather than by APOE ε4 status. However, these findings should be interpreted with caution given the limited sample size of the combined cohort, which may have reduced power to detect subtle genetic effects.

This study has several limitations. First, PD-AD biomarker burden was assessed only at baseline and may increase with age, raising co-pathology rates over time. Second, AD biomarker positivity was defined by research-based thresholds, which may not capture the complexity of mixed pathologies. Interactions between co-pathologies, as α-synuclein can influence AD spread in Lewy body disease, may affect individual thresholds. 34 Third, analysis of genetic factors in PD, such as LRRK2, GBA1 or PARK mutations, was not feasible due to the small number of patients with these variants. Fourth, although vascular comorbidities such as hypertension and diabetes may contribute to cognitive impairment in PD, their prevalence did not differ significantly between the PD-AD and PD-nonAD groups (Table 1). Likewise, differences in clinical outcomes were unlikely to be explained by differences in antiparkinsonian treatment, as the summed levodopa equivalent daily dose of all active antiparkinsonian medications at both the 5-year and 8-year follow-up did not differ significantly between the groups (Table 2). Together, these findings suggest that the accelerated cognitive and motor decline observed in PD-AD is more likely attributable to underlying AD pathology rather than differences in vascular comorbidity burden or dopaminergic treatment. Finally, as a retrospective observational analysis, unmeasured factors may affect the results, preventing causal conclusions. Additionally, some patients were lost to follow-up, and not all patients had complete biomarker or clinical measurements, which may introduce bias. Nonetheless, our findings are consistent with prior studies demonstrating worse cognitive and motor outcomes in PD patients with evidence of AD pathology.13–15 Prospective longitudinal studies are needed to validate these findings and establish causality.

Our findings show that a substantial subset of PD patients meet AD biomarker criteria and exhibit worse cognitive and motor outcomes, indicating AD-related mechanisms contribute to early decline and disease severity. MoCA screening was predictive: PD patients with scores <24 had a 15% PPV for AD biomarker positivity (OR = 6.1, Supplementary fig. 1g), suggesting cognitive tools can aid detection of AD pathology in PD. 35 Importantly, the Aβ1-42/p-tau181 ratio provided a practical, biologically informed cutoff to identify PD-AD patients without relying on PD-specific numerical thresholds, and this ratio predicted both cognitive and over extended follow-up, motor outcomes. Additional information such as imaging and in-depth cognitive phenotyping might assist in detecting PD patients at baseline who might be susceptible to co-pathologies.

We propose that PD patients with a biomarker-defined AD signature form a distinct PD-AD group, where the two pathologies converge to accelerate decline. A biomarker-based framework, including practical ratio-based measures, can improve patient stratification, guide prognosis, and potentially inform interventions targeting AD mechanisms in PD, underscoring the need for future studies to further refine and validate such frameworks.
