Sato-Tate Groups and Distributions of y ℓ = x ⁡ ( x ℓ − 1 ) y^{\ell}=x(x^{\ell}-1)
Heidi Goodson, Rezwan Hoque

Abstract
. Let C ℓ / ℚ C_{\ell}/\mathbb{Q} denote the curve with affine model y ℓ = x ⁡ ( x ℓ − 1 ) y^{\ell}=x(x^{\ell}-1) , where ℓ ≥ 3 \ell\geq 3 is prime. In this paper we study the limiting distributions of the normalized L L -polynomials of the curves by computing their Sato-Tate groups and distributions. We also provide results for the number of points on the curves over finite fields, including a formula in terms of Jacobi sums when the field 𝔽 q \mathbb{F}_{q} satisfies q ≡ 1 ( mod ℓ 2 ) q\equiv 1\pmod{\ell^{2}} .



1.
Introduction

Let
C_{\ell}/\mathbb{Q}
denote the curve with affine model
y^{\ell}=x(x^{\ell}-1)
, where
\ell\geq 3
is prime. The main goal of this paper is to study the limiting distributions of the normalized
L
-polynomials of curves in this family. These curves have complex multiplication by
\mathbb{Q}(\zeta_{\ell^{2}})
, and so work of Johansson
[
Joh17
]
implies that we can study these limiting distributions by computing the Sato-Tate groups of the Jacobians. Furthermore, work of Aoki in
[
Aok02
]
shows that the Jacobians of these curves are nondegenerate, which makes this family of curves a natural example to study in the context of Sato-Tate groups.

Recall that the Sato-Tate conjecture, posed in the 1960s by Mikio Sato and John Tate (independently), is a statistical conjecture regarding the distributions of the normalized traces of Frobenius on elliptic curves without complex multiplication (CM). The conjecture has been proven for non-CM elliptic curves defined over totally real fields (see, for example,
[
CHT08
,
Tay08
]
), and similar results are known for elliptic curves with CM. This conjecture was generalized to higher dimensional abelian varieties by Serre
[
Ser12
]
. The generalized Sato–Tate conjecture for an abelian variety predicts the existence of a compact Lie group, referred to in the literature as the
Sato-Tate group
, that determines the limiting distribution of coefficients of the normalized
L
-polynomials. While the conjecture is largely still open, it is known to be true in many cases, including for nongeneric abelian surfaces (see
[
FS14
,
Joh17
,
Tay20
]
) and for abelian varieties with complex multiplication (see
[
Joh17
, Proposition 16]
). Even in cases where the conjecture has not yet been proven, there has been a great amount of interest in computing Sato-Tate groups. For example, there are classification results in dimension 2
[
FKRS12
]
and dimension 3
[
FKS23
]
that determine all possible Sato-Tate groups that correspond to abelian varieties in these dimensions. We refer the reader to
[
FKS23
,
Goo23
,
Sut19
]
for more background information on Sato-Tate groups and the generalized Sato-Tate conjecture.

Many of the results in the literature in this area are for specific families of abelian varieties with CM since the generalized Sato-Tate conjecture is known to be true in these cases (see, for example,
[
ACFL+18
,
EG22
,
EG24
,
FGL16
,
FLGS18
,
GGL24
,
Goo23
,
Goo24
,
LS18
]
). Most of these results are for nondegenerate abelian varieties since, due to work of Banaszak and Kedlaya
[
BK15
]
, there are well-established techniques for computing Sato-Tate groups in these cases (see Section
3.1
for more details). However, progress was recently made in
[
GGL24
,
Goo24
]
on computing Sato-Tate groups for degenerate abelian varieties.

In this paper, we compute the Sato-Tate groups of Jacobians of the curves
C_{\ell}:y^{\ell}=x(x^{\ell}-1)
in order to gain information about the limiting distributions of the coefficients of their normalized
L
-polynomials

(1)

\overline{L}_{\mathfrak{p}}(C_{\ell},T):=L_{\mathfrak{p}}(C_{\ell},T/\sqrt{N(\mathfrak{p})})=T^{2g}+a_{1}T^{2g-1}+a_{2}T^{2g-2}+\cdots+a_{2}T^{2}+a_{1}T+1,

where
g
is the genus of the curve and
L_{\mathfrak{p}}(C_{\ell},T)
is the
L
-polynomial appearing in the numerator of the zeta function of the curve for primes
\mathfrak{p}
of good reduction. Our work generalizes the results of
[
LS18
]
which studies the case of the Picard curve with
\ell=3.
The Jacobians of the curves
C_{\ell}
are known to be simple and nondegenerate due to work of Aoki in
[
Aok02
]
. Furthermore, the curves and their Jacobians have complex multiplication, and so the generalized Sato-Tate conjecture is known to be true for this family. Moreover, the field of definition of the endomorphisms of
\Jac(C_{\ell})
is
K=\mathbb{Q}(\zeta_{\ell^{2}})
, and so both
\Gal(K/\mathbb{Q})
and the component group of the Sato-Tate group are cyclic. Thus, we obtain a relatively easy-to-state result for the Sato-Tate group in Theorem
3.4
. On the other hand, we encounter some interesting computational challenges, described in Section
4
, due to the high genii of the curves:
g=\ell(\ell-1)/2
, which is quite large even for small primes such as
\ell=5
and 7.

Organization of the paper

In Section
2
we present several important facts about the curves and their Jacobians. In Section
2.2
we prove new results for the number of points on the curves over finite fields, including a formula in terms of Jacobi sums when the field
\mathbb{F}_{q}
satisfies
q\equiv 1\pmod{\ell^{2}}
. We provide some background information on nondegeneracy in Section
3.1
. Section
3.2
contains the main result of the paper (Theorem
3.4
), where we give explicit generators of the Sato-Tate group. In Section
4
we describe our methods for computing moment statistics for the curves
C_{\ell}
and their Sato-Tate groups. Throughout the paper, we work through the example
\ell=5
to demonstrate our results.

2.
Preliminaries on the curve
C_{\ell}
and its Jacobian

The curves
C_{\ell}
that we work with in this paper are a special case of those appearing in
[
Aok02
]
:

y^{\ell}=x^{a}(x^{\ell^{e-1}}-1).

More specifically, we are considering the case where
a=1
and
e=2
. From the results of
[
Aok02
, Corollary 2.2]
and
[
KR78
]
, we know that the Jacobian variety
\Jac(C_{\ell})
is a simple, nondegenerate abelian variety of dimension
g={\phi(\ell^{2})}/{2}=\ell(\ell-1)/2
, where
\phi
denotes the Euler totient function. The curve
C_{\ell}
has CM by
K:=\mathbb{Q}(\zeta_{\ell^{2}})
and the endomorphism ring of
\Jac(C_{\ell})
satisfies
\End(\Jac(C_{\ell})_{\overline{\mathbb{Q}}})\otimes\mathbb{Q}\simeq K
.

For ease of notation, let
\zeta:=\zeta_{\ell^{2}}
. We define the automorphism
\alpha
of
C_{\ell}
by

(3)

\alpha:(x,y)\mapsto(\zeta^{\ell}x,\zeta^{\ell+1}y).

One can check that the order of
\alpha
is
\ell^{2}
.

Example 2.1
.

For
\ell=5
, we have the curve
C_{5}:y^{5}=x(x^{5}-1)
with curve automorphism

\alpha:(x,y)\mapsto(\zeta_{25}^{5}x,\zeta_{25}^{6}y),

whose order is 25.

2.1.
Endomorphisms of the Jacobian

As noted above, the dimension of
\Jac(C_{\ell})
is
g=\ell(\ell-1)/2
. It follows from
[
Mir95
, Problem IV.1.E]
that we can take the following basis for the space of regular differential 1-forms:

(4)

\mathcal{B}=\left\{\omega_{a,b}:=x^{a}\frac{dx}{y^{b}}\ \middle|\ \begin{array}[]{c}0\leq a\leq\ell-2,\\[2.84544pt]
a+1\leq b\leq\ell-1\end{array}\right\}.

Definition 2.2

(Ordering Scheme)
.

To order the list of 1-forms appearing in Equation (
4
), fix
a
and vary
b
until every
\omega_{a,b}
has been exhausted for the fixed
a
. Then, organize the list in increasing order (from left to right) of
a
first, then
b
.

Example 2.3
.

For
C_{5}
, the 1-forms in the basis
\mathcal{B}
, ordered as in Definition
2.2
, are

\displaystyle\omega_{0,1},\,\omega_{0,2},\,\omega_{0,3},\,\omega_{0,4},\,\omega_{1,2},\,\omega_{1,3},\,\omega_{1,4},\,\omega_{2,3},\,\omega_{2,4},\,\omega_{3,4}.

The pullback of the regular 1-form
\omega_{a,b}
associated to the map
\alpha
is given by

(5)

\alpha^{*}\omega_{a,b}=\zeta^{\ell(a+1-b)-b}\omega_{a,b}.

We obtain an endomorphism of
\Jac(C_{\ell})
by taking the symplectic basis of
H_{1}(\Jac(C_{\ell})_{\mathbb{C}},\mathbb{C})
(with respect to the skew-symmetric matrix
\diag(J,J,\ldots,J)
) corresponding the basis of regular 1-forms defined in Equation
4
(written according to the ordering scheme described in Definition
2.2
). The endomorphism
\alpha
is a diagonal matrix partitioned into
2\times 2
blocks such that the
j
th entry, denoted as
\alpha[j,j]
, is

(6)

\alpha[j,j]=Z^{\ell(a_{j}+1-b_{j})-b_{j}},

where
0\leq j\leq g-1
. For ease of notation, let

(7)

e_{j}:=\langle\ell(a_{j}+1-b_{j})-b_{j}\rangle_{\ell^{2}}.

Then the set of all exponents of
Z
appearing as entries in
\alpha
is

(8)

S:=\left\{e_{j}\ \middle|\ \begin{array}[]{c}0\leq a_{j}\leq\ell-2,\\[2.84544pt]
a_{j}+1\leq b_{j}\leq\ell-1\end{array}\right\}.

Remark 2.4
.

Simple arithmetic shows that
e_{i}\not=e_{j}
if
i\not=j
and that none of the
e_{i}
are divisible by
\ell
. Thus, the set
S
contains
g=\ell(\ell-1)/2
distinct elements, i.e.,
S
contains exactly half of the elements of
(\mathbb{Z}/\ell^{2}\mathbb{Z})^{*}
. Furthermore,
e_{i}+e_{j}\not=\ell^{2}
for any
i,j
satisfying the bounds given in the definition of the set
S
.

Example 2.5
.

We determine
\alpha[4,4]
for
C_{5}
. By Example
2.3
,
j=4
corresponds to the regular 1-form
\omega_{1,2}
, giving
a_{4}=1
and
b_{4}=2
. Using Equation (
6
),

\alpha[4,4]=Z^{5(1+1-2)-2}=Z^{-2}=Z^{23}.

Repeating this process for the nine remaining entries gives the endomorphism

\alpha=\diag(Z^{24},Z^{18},Z^{12},Z^{6},Z^{23},Z^{17},Z^{11},Z^{22},Z^{16},Z^{21}).

We conclude this subsection by demonstrating the action of the Galois group
\Gal(K/\mathbb{Q})
on the endomorphism
\alpha,
where
K=\mathbb{Q}(\zeta_{\ell^{2}}).
It is well-known that this Galois group is isomorphic to the cyclic multiplicative group
(\mathbb{Z}/\ell^{2}\mathbb{Z})^{*}
. Let
n\in(\mathbb{Z}/\ell^{2}\mathbb{Z})^{*}
, and let
\sigma_{n}
denote the element of
\Gal(K/\mathbb{Q})
mapping
\zeta\mapsto\zeta^{n}
. For any power
t
of the matrix
Z
, we have that

(9)

\prescript{\sigma_{n}}{}{Z^{t}}=\begin{pmatrix}\zeta^{nt}&0\\
0&\overline{\zeta^{nt}}\end{pmatrix}=Z^{nt}.

Let
0\leq i\leq g-1
and let
e_{i}
be an element of the set
S
defined in Equation (
8
). For ease of notation, let

(10)

g_{i}:=\langle\ell n(a_{i}+1-b_{i})-nb_{i}\rangle_{\ell^{2}}=\langle ne_{i}\rangle_{\ell^{2}}.

Then
g_{i}
is an element of
(\mathbb{Z}/\ell^{2}\mathbb{Z})^{*}
, and so, as noted in Remark
2.4
, we have either
g_{i}\in S
or
\ell^{2}-g_{i}\in S
. Thus, the action of the Galois automorphism
\sigma_{n}
on the endomorphism
\alpha
can be described by

(11)

\prescript{\sigma_{n}}{}{\alpha}[i,i]=\begin{cases}Z^{g_{i}}&\text{if $g_{i}\in S$}\\[7.22743pt]
\overline{Z}^{\ell^{2}-g_{i}}&\text{if $g_{i}\not\in S$},\end{cases}

so that all exponents that we obtain as a result of this action are in the set
S
.

Example 2.6
.

For
C_{5}
, we have that
\langle\sigma_{2}\rangle=\Gal(\mathbb{Q}(\zeta_{25})/\mathbb{Q})
. Recall from Example
2.5
that
Z^{23}
corresponds to
j=4
and
Z^{16}
corresponds to
j=8
. By the action defined in Equation (
9
),

g_{0}=\langle 2(e_{0})\rangle_{25}=\langle 2(24)\rangle_{25}=23\quad\text{and}\quad g_{5}=\langle 2(e_{5})\rangle_{25}=\langle 2(17)\rangle_{25}=9.

Since
23\in S
but
9\not\in S
, it follows that

\prescript{\sigma_{2}}{}{\alpha}[0,0]=Z^{23}\quad\text{and}\quad\prescript{\sigma_{2}}{}{\alpha}[5,5]=\overline{Z}^{16}

by Equation (
11
). Continuing this process for the eight remaining entries, we see that
\sigma_{2}
acts on the endomorphism
\alpha
as

\prescript{\sigma_{2}}{}{\alpha}=\diag(Z^{23},Z^{11},Z^{24},Z^{12},Z^{21},\overline{Z}^{16},Z^{22},\overline{Z}^{6},\overline{Z}^{18},Z^{17}).

2.2.
Point count

Let
q
be a prime distinct from the prime
\ell
and
\#C_{\ell}(\mathbb{F}_{q})
denote the number of
\mathbb{F}_{q}
-rational points on the curve
y^{\ell}=x(x^{\ell}-1)
. The first two lemmas of this section are generalizations of Propositions 1 and 2 of
[
HN02
]
for the Picard curves
y^{3}=x(x^{3}-1)
.

Lemma 2.7
.

If
q\not\equiv 1\pmod{\ell}
then
\#C_{\ell}(\mathbb{F}_{q})=q+1
.

Proof.

If
q\not\equiv 1\pmod{\ell}
, then the order of the multiplicative group
\mathbb{F}_{q}^{*}
is not divisible by
\ell
. Hence, the subgroup
\mathbb{F}_{q}^{*\ell}
of
\ell^{th}
powers in
\mathbb{F}_{q}^{*}
must be the full group. Thus, for each value of
x
in
\mathbb{F}_{q}^{*}
, there is exactly one value of
y
satisfying
y^{\ell}=x(x^{\ell}-1)
. Combining this set of solutions with the solution
(x,y)=(0,0)
, we get that there are
q
solutions
(x,y)
in
\mathbb{F}_{q}\times\mathbb{F}_{q}
. There is a single point at infinity, and so we obtain
\#C_{\ell}(\mathbb{F}_{q})=q+1
as desired.
∎

Lemma 2.8
.

If
q\equiv 1\pmod{\ell}
but
q\not\equiv 1\pmod{\ell^{2}}
then
\#C_{\ell}(\mathbb{F}_{q})=q+1
.

Proof.

We closely follow the proof of Proposition 2 of
[
HN02
]
. Let
\chi
be a character of
\mathbb{F}_{q}^{*}
of order
\ell
. Note that, since
\ell
is prime, all of the characters of order
\ell
for
\mathbb{F}_{q}^{*}
are of the form
\chi^{j}
, where
j\in\{1,\ldots,\ell-1\}
. We extend each
\chi^{j}
to all of
\mathbb{F}_{q}
by setting
\chi^{j}(0)=0
. Let
\varepsilon
denote the trivial multiplicative character for
\mathbb{F}_{q}^{*}
and set
\varepsilon(0)=1
.

It is well-known that the number of solutions to an equation
z^{n}=a
is given by

N(z^{n}=a)=\sum_{\lambda}\lambda(a),

where the sum is taken over all characters whose order divides
n
(see, for example,
[
IR90
, Proposition 8.1.5]
). Thus, we can compute the number of solutions to the equation
y^{\ell}=x(x^{\ell}-1)
by computing

\displaystyle N

\displaystyle=\sum_{c\in\mathbb{F}_{q}}N(y^{\ell}=c(c^{\ell}-1))

\displaystyle=\sum_{c\in\mathbb{F}_{q}}\left(\varepsilon(c(c^{\ell}-1))+\sum_{j=1}^{\ell-1}\chi^{j}(c(c^{\ell}-1))\right).

Note that
\varepsilon(c(c^{\ell}-1))=1
for all
c\in\mathbb{F}_{q}
, and so
\sum_{c\in\mathbb{F}_{q}}\varepsilon(c(c^{\ell}-1))=q
. We will show that
\sum_{c\in\mathbb{F}_{q}}\sum_{j=1}^{\ell-1}\chi^{j}(c(c^{\ell}-1))=0.

Since
\ell|(q-1)
but
\ell^{2}\nmid(q-1)
, the multiplicative group
\mathbb{F}_{q}^{*}
equals the internal direct product of its unique subgroup of order
\ell
, which we denote by
\langle g\rangle
, and of its subgroup of order
(q-1)/\ell
, denoted
U_{(q-1)/\ell}
. Thus, every element of
c\in\mathbb{F}_{q}^{*}
can be uniquely written in the form

c=dg^{i},

where
d\in U_{(q-1)/\ell}
and
i\in\{0,1,\ldots,\ell-1\}
. Thus, we obtain

\displaystyle\sum_{c\in\mathbb{F}_{q}^{*}}\sum_{j=1}^{\ell-1}\chi^{j}(c(c^{\ell}-1))

\displaystyle=\sum_{j=1}^{\ell-1}\sum_{i=1}^{\ell-1}\sum_{d\in U_{(q-1)/\ell}}\chi^{j}(dg^{i}((dg^{i})^{\ell}-1))

\displaystyle=\sum_{j=1}^{\ell-1}\sum_{i=1}^{\ell-1}\sum_{d\in U_{(q-1)/\ell}}\chi^{j}(g^{i})\chi^{j}(d^{\ell+1}g^{i\ell}-d)

\displaystyle=\sum_{j=1}^{\ell-1}\sum_{i=1}^{\ell-1}\sum_{d\in U_{(q-1)/\ell}}\chi^{j}(g^{i})\chi^{j}(d^{\ell+1}-d),

where the last equality holds since
g
is an element of order
\ell
, and so
g^{i\ell}=1
. Thus, we can split up the summand as

(12)

\displaystyle\sum_{j=1}^{\ell-1}\sum_{i=1}^{\ell-1}\sum_{d\in U_{(q-1)/\ell}}\chi^{j}(g^{i})\chi^{j}(d^{\ell+1}-d)

\displaystyle=\sum_{j=1}^{\ell-1}\sum_{d\in U_{(q-1)/\ell}}\chi^{j}(d^{\ell+1}-d)\sum_{i=1}^{\ell-1}\chi^{j}(g^{i}).

Recall that, for each
j
,
\chi^{j}
is a character of order
\ell
and so it is nontrivial on the subgroup
\langle g\rangle
of order
\ell
. Thus, by Proposition 8.1.2 of
[
IR90
]
,
\sum_{i=1}^{\ell-1}\chi^{j}(g^{i})=0
for each
j
. This implies that the entire expression on the right-hand-side of Equation (
12
) equals 0. Futhermore, when
c=0
we have
\chi^{j}(c(c^{\ell}-1))=\chi^{j}(0)=0
, for all
1\leq j\leq\ell-1
. Hence,

\sum_{c\in\mathbb{F}_{q}}\sum_{j=1}^{\ell-1}\chi^{j}(c(c^{\ell}-1))=0,

and so
N=q
. This yields the desired result since
\#C_{\ell}(\mathbb{F}_{q})=N+1=q+1
.
∎

The remaining case is
q\equiv 1\pmod{\ell^{2}}
, which we handle following the techniques used in
[
LS18
, Appendix]
for the Picard curve. Let
\zeta
be a primitive
\ell^{2}
root of unity, and let
\chi_{q}
denote the unique character
\chi_{q}:\mathbb{F}_{\ell}^{*}\to\mathbb{Q}(\zeta)^{*}
satisfying

\chi_{q}(x)=x^{(q-1)/\ell^{2}}\pmod{q}.

For
a,b\in\mathbb{Z}/\ell^{2}\mathbb{Z}
, let
J_{q}{(a,b)}
denote the Jacobi sum

(13)

J_{q}(a,b):=J_{q}(\chi^{a},\chi^{b})=\sum_{x\in\mathbb{F}_{q}}\chi^{a}(x)\chi^{b}(1-x)

and define

(14)

\Tr_{\mathbb{Q}(\zeta)/\mathbb{Q}}(J_{q}(a,b))=\sum_{s\in(\mathbb{Z}/\ell^{2}\mathbb{Z})^{*}}J_{q}(sa,sb).

Lemma 2.9
.

If
q\equiv 1\pmod{\ell^{2}}
then
\#C_{\ell}(\mathbb{F}_{q})=q+1+\Tr_{\mathbb{Q}(\zeta)/\mathbb{Q}}(J_{q}(\ell(\ell-1),1))
.

Proof.

Let
C_{\ell}^{\prime}:v^{\ell^{2}}=u(u+1)^{\ell(\ell-1)}.
There is an isomorphism
\phi:C_{\ell}\to C_{\ell}^{\prime}
given by

\phi(x,y)=\left(-\frac{1}{x^{\ell}},-\frac{y^{\ell-1}}{x^{\ell}}\right)

with inverse
\phi^{-1}:C_{\ell}^{\prime}\to C_{\ell}
given by

\phi^{-1}(u,v)=\left(-\frac{(u+1)^{\ell-1}}{v^{\ell}},-\frac{(u+1)^{\ell}}{v^{\ell+1}}\right).

We can easily determine the number of points on
C_{\ell}^{\prime}
using the same techniques as in the proof of Lemma
2.8
:

\displaystyle\#C_{\ell}^{\prime}(\mathbb{F}_{q})

\displaystyle=1+\sum_{c\in\mathbb{F}_{q}}\sum_{a\in\mathbb{Z}/\ell^{2}\mathbb{Z}}\chi^{a}(c)\chi^{a\ell(\ell-1)}(c+1)

\displaystyle=1+q+\sum_{c\in\mathbb{F}_{q}}\sum_{a\in(\mathbb{Z}/\ell^{2}\mathbb{Z})^{*}}\chi^{a}(c)\chi^{a\ell(\ell-1)}(c+1)

\displaystyle=q+1+\sum_{a\in(\mathbb{Z}/\ell^{2}\mathbb{Z})^{*}}\sum_{d\in\mathbb{F}_{q}}\chi^{a}(d-1)\chi^{a\ell(\ell-1)}(d),

where the second equality holds by
[
IR90
, §8.3, Thm. 1]
and the third equality holds by letting
d=c+1
. Since the order of
\chi
is odd, we have
\chi^{a}(-1)=1
for all
a
. Hence,

\displaystyle\#C_{\ell}^{\prime}(\mathbb{F}_{q})

\displaystyle=q+1+\sum_{a\in(\mathbb{Z}/\ell^{2}\mathbb{Z})^{*}}\sum_{d\in\mathbb{F}_{q}}\chi^{a}(-1)\chi^{a\ell(\ell-1)}(d)\chi^{a}(1-d)

\displaystyle=q+1+\sum_{a\in(\mathbb{Z}/\ell^{2}\mathbb{Z})^{*}}\sum_{d\in\mathbb{F}_{q}}\chi^{a\ell(\ell-1)}(d)\chi^{a}(1-d)

\displaystyle=q+1+\sum_{a\in(\mathbb{Z}/\ell^{2}\mathbb{Z})^{*}}J_{q}(a\ell(\ell-1),a)

\displaystyle=q+1+\Tr_{\mathbb{Q}(\zeta)/\mathbb{Q}}(J_{q}(\ell(\ell-1),1)),

where the last equality holds by Equation (
14
). Since
C_{\ell}^{\prime}(\mathbb{F}_{q})
is isomorphic to
C_{\ell}(\mathbb{F}_{q}),
this proves the desired result.
∎

These results will be referenced in Section
4.2
.

3.
The Sato-Tate group of
\Jac(C_{\ell})

In this section we give explicit generators for the Sato-Tate group of the Jacobian of the curve
C_{\ell}
for any prime
\ell\geq 3
. Our proof techniques rely on the nondegeneracy of the Jacobians.

3.1.
Preliminaries on nondegeneracy

We first recall some key facts about nondegeneracy that we depend on in the proofs of Proposition
3.2
and Theorem
3.4
.

Aoki proved in
[
Aok02
, Corollary 2.2]
that the Jacobians of the curves
C_{\ell}
are stably nondegenerate in the sense of Hazama
[
Haz89
]
, i.e., the Hodge ring of the Jacobian does not contain exceptional cycles and the Hodge group is maximal. By work of Banaszak and Kedlaya (see
[
BK15
, Theorem 6.6]
), this implies that we can describe the component group of the Sato-Tate group via the
twisted Lefschetz group
. For an abelian variety
A/F
of dimension
g
defined over a number field
F
, the twisted Lefschetz group, denoted
\TL(A)
, is a closed algebraic subgroup of
\Sp_{2g}
defined by

\displaystyle\TL(A):=\bigcup_{\tau\in\Gal(\overline{F}/F)}\LL(A)(\tau),

where
\LL(A)(\tau):=\{\gamma\in\Sp_{2g}\mid\gamma\alpha\gamma^{-1}=\tau(\alpha)\text{ for all }\alpha\in\End(A_{\overline{F}})\otimes\mathbb{Q}\}
. We then have the following result which connects the twisted Lefschetz group to the algebraic Sato-Tate group.

Proposition 3.1
.

[
BK15
]

The algebraic Sato-Tate Conjecture holds for nondegenerate abelian varieties
A
with
\AST(A)=\TL(A)
.

When the algebraic Sato-Tate conjecture holds, the Sato-Tate group of
A
is a maximal compact Lie subgroup of
\AST(A)\otimes_{\mathbb{Q}}\mathbb{C}
contained in
\USp(2g)
.

3.2.
Sato-Tate group results

We begin with a result for the identity component of the Sato-Tate group.

Proposition 3.2
.

The identity component of the Sato-Tate group of
\Jac(C_{\ell})
is

\ST^{0}(\Jac(C_{\ell}))\simeq\U(1)^{g}.

where
g=\ell(\ell-1)/2
is the genus of
C_{\ell}
.

Proof.

By definition,
\ST^{0}(\Jac(C_{\ell}))
is a maximal compact subgroup of
\AST^{0}(\Jac(C_{\ell}))\otimes_{\mathbb{Q}}\mathbb{C}.
Since the Jacobian variety
\Jac(C_{\ell})
is a simple nondegenerate abelian variety with CM, it follows from Proposition
3.1
and from techniques used in
[
EG22
, Proposition 3.3]
that we can take the maximal compact subgroup
\U(1)^{g}
.
∎

We now define the matrix
\gamma
that will appear in Theorem
3.4
.

Definition 3.3
.

Recall the definitions of
e_{j}
and
g_{i}
in Equations (
7
) and (
10
), respectively. Define
\gamma
to be the
2g\times 2g
block-signed permutation matrix whose
ij^{th}
block is

\gamma[i,j]=\begin{cases}I&\text{if $g_{i}=e_{j}$}\\
J&\text{if $g_{i}=\ell^{2}-e_{j}$}\\
0&\text{otherwise,}\\
\end{cases}

where
0\leq i,j\leq g-1
. The
ij^{th}
block of the inverse of
\gamma
is

\gamma^{-1}[i,j]=\begin{cases}I&\text{if $g_{j}=e_{i}$}\\
-J&\text{ if $g_{j}=\ell^{2}-e_{i}$}\\
0&\text{otherwise.}\end{cases}

Note the swap of
i
and
j
in the definitions of
\gamma
and its inverse.

The main result of this section is Theorem
3.4
where we give explicit generators of the Sato-Tate group.

Theorem 3.4
.

The Sato-Tate group, up to isomorphism in
\USp(2g)
, of
\Jac(C_{\ell})
is

(15)

\ST(\Jac(C_{\ell}))\simeq\langle\U(1)^{g},\gamma\rangle,

where
\gamma
is the matrix defined in Definition
3.3
.

Proof.

We proved that the identity component for the Sato-Tate group is
\U(1)^{g}
in Proposition
3.2
. We now show that the component group is generated by
\gamma
.

We start by proving that
\gamma
is an element of the twisted Lefschetz group by verifying that
\gamma\alpha\gamma^{-1}=\prescript{\sigma_{n}}{}{\alpha}
, where
\alpha
is defined in Equation (
6
). Note that the
ij^{th}
block entry of
\gamma\alpha\gamma^{-1}
can be expressed as

\displaystyle\gamma\alpha\gamma^{-1}[i,j]

\displaystyle=\sum_{k=0}^{n}(\gamma\alpha[i,k])\,\gamma^{-1}[k,j]

\displaystyle=\sum_{k=0}^{n}\left(\sum_{m=0}^{n}\gamma[i,m]\,\alpha[m,k]\right)\gamma^{-1}[k,j].

By Equation (
6
) we have
\alpha[m,k]=0
unless
m=k
, and so
\sum_{m=0}^{n}\gamma[i,m]\,\alpha[m,k]=\gamma[i,k]\,\alpha[k,k]
. Hence,

(16)

\gamma\alpha\gamma^{-1}[i,j]=\sum_{k=0}^{n}\gamma[i,k]\,\alpha[k,k]\,\gamma^{-1}[k,j].

Let
\sigma_{n}
be a generator of the cyclic group
\Gal(\mathbb{Q}(\zeta_{\ell^{2}})/\mathbb{Q}).
Both
\alpha
and
\prescript{\sigma_{n}}{}{\alpha}
are diagonal matrices, and so we first verify that
\gamma\alpha\gamma^{-1}
is also diagonal. By construction, each row and column of
\gamma
and of
\gamma^{-1}
contains exactly one nonzero block entry. Furthermore, the block entry
\gamma[i,k]
is nonzero if and only if
\gamma^{-1}[k,i]
is nonzero. Hence, if
i\not=j
then at least one of
\gamma[i,k]
or
\gamma^{-1}[k,j]
equals the zero matrix. Thus,
\gamma\alpha\gamma^{-1}[i,j]=0
if
i\not=j
and
\gamma\alpha\gamma^{-1}
is a diagonal block matrix.

We now consider the diagonal block entries of
\gamma\alpha\gamma^{-1}
. By Equation (
16
),

(17)

\displaystyle\gamma\alpha\gamma^{-1}[i,i]

\displaystyle=\sum_{k=0}^{n}\gamma[i,k]\,\alpha[k,k]\,\gamma^{-1}[k,i].

Note that
\gamma[i,k]
is nonzero for exactly one value of
k
, say
k=j
, and there are two possibilities: either
\gamma[i,j]=I
or
\gamma[i,j]=J
.

First suppose
\gamma[i,j]=I
. By Lemma 4.2 of
[
Goo23
]
and Equation (
17
), we have

\gamma\alpha\gamma^{-1}[i,i]=\alpha[j,j]=Z^{e_{j}},

with
e_{j}\in S
. Since
\gamma[i,j]=I
, Definition
3.3
tells us that
g_{i}=e_{j}
. Hence,
Z^{e_{j}}=Z^{g_{i}}
, which equals
\prescript{\sigma_{n}}{}{\alpha}[i,i]
by Equation (
11
).

Now suppose
\gamma[i,j]=J
. By Lemma 4.2 of
[
Goo23
]
and Equation (
17
), we have

\gamma\alpha\gamma^{-1}[i,i]=-J\alpha[j,j]J=-JZ^{e_{j}}J=\overline{Z}^{e_{j}}.

Since
\gamma[i,j]=J
, Definition
3.3
tells us that
e_{j}=\ell^{2}-g_{i}
. Hence,
\overline{Z}^{e_{j}}=\overline{Z}^{\ell^{2}-g_{i}}
. This equals
\prescript{\sigma_{n}}{}{\alpha}[i,i]
by Equation (
11
) since
g_{i}\not\in S
. Thus, we have shown that
\gamma\alpha\gamma^{-1}[i,j]=\prescript{\sigma_{n}}{}{\alpha}[i,j]
for all
i
and
j
. Hence,
\gamma
is an element of the twisted Lefschetz group.

We now show that
\gamma
generates the entire component group
\ST(\Jac(C_{\ell}))/\ST^{0}(\Jac(C_{\ell}))
. Since the Jacobian of the curve
C_{\ell}
is nondegenerate, we know by

[
BK15
, Section 6]
that the component group of the Sato-Tate group is isomorphic to
\Gal(K/\mathbb{Q})=\langle\sigma_{n}\rangle
, where
K=\mathbb{Q}(\zeta_{\ell^{2}})
is the field of definition of the endomorphisms. Thus, we need to prove that the order of
\gamma
in
\ST(\Jac(C_{\ell}))/\ST^{0}(\Jac(C_{\ell}))
equals the order of
\sigma_{n}
which equals
\phi(\ell^{2})=\ell(\ell-1)
.

We have seen that conjugating the diagonal matrix
\alpha
by
\gamma
permutes (and sometimes conjugates) its diagonal block entries consistent with the action of
\sigma_{n}
on
\alpha
. Since
\gamma\alpha\gamma^{-1}
is again a diagonal block matrix, conjugating by
\gamma
will again just permute (and sometimes conjugate) the diagonal block entries consistent with the action of
\sigma_{n}
. Hence,
\gamma^{d}\alpha\gamma^{-d}
is a diagonal block matrix for any
d
and we can write
\gamma^{d}\alpha\gamma^{-d}=\prescript{(\sigma_{n})^{d}}{}{\alpha}
.

By the construction in Definition
3.3
, the block entries of
\gamma^{d}
are of the form
\pm I
or
\pm J
. Let
d
be the smallest positive integer such that
\gamma^{d}\in\ST^{0}(\Jac(C_{\ell}))
. Then
\gamma^{d}
must be diagonal, and so
\gamma^{d}[i,i]=I
or
-I
for all
i
. In both cases, this implies that
\gamma^{d}\alpha\gamma^{-d}[i,i]=\alpha[i,i]
for all
i
, and so
\gamma^{d}\alpha\gamma^{-d}=\alpha
. However, we also know that
\gamma^{d}\alpha\gamma^{-d}=\prescript{(\sigma_{n})^{d}}{}{\alpha}
, and so we must have
\prescript{(\sigma_{n})^{d}}{}{\alpha}=\alpha
. Hence
d
is a multiple of the order of
\sigma_{n}
, which implies that the order of
\gamma
in
\ST(\Jac(C_{\ell}))/\ST^{0}(\Jac(C_{\ell}))
is
\ell(\ell-1)
.

Hence,
\gamma
generates the entire component group of the Sato-Tate group
\ST(\Jac(C_{\ell}))
, and so we have proved the statement of the theorem.

∎

4.
Moment statistics

Though the generalized Sato-Tate conjecture is known to be true for the Jacobians of the curves we are considering by work of Johansson
[
Joh17
, Proposition 16]
, it is still interesting to compare moment statistics coming from the Sato-Tate group and those computed numerically from the normalized
L
-polynomials of the curves. In particular, by computing moment statistics we are able to give a definitive solution to the problem of determining the limiting distribution of the normalized traces and see how much numerical estimates are limited by computational constraints.

Recall that the
n^{th}
moment of a probability density function is the expected value of the
n^{th}
powers of the values:
M_{n}[X]=E[X^{n}]
. For more background information on moment statistics see, for example,
[
EG22
, Section 6.1]
,
[
Sut19
, Sections 4.3, 4.4]
, and
[
LS18
, Section 3]
.

4.1.
Methods of computation

In this section we describe a variety of techniques for computing the numerical moments statistics associated to the coefficients of the normalized
L
-polynomials of the curves and the theoretical moment statistics coming from the traces of random matrices in the Sato-Tate group. We implemented these techniques in SageMath
[
Sag24
]
and used the Python libraries
SymPy
and
NumPy
(see
[
GH24
]
).

Let
C_{\ell}/\mathbb{Q}
denote the curve
y^{\ell}=x(x^{\ell}-1)
. Recall that for primes
p
of good reduction, the
a_{1}
-coefficient of the normalized
L
-polynomial in Equation (
1
) satisfies
a_{1}=\#C_{\ell}(\mathbb{F}_{p})
and, more generally,
a_{n}
encodes arithmetic information about the curve over the fields
\mathbb{F}_{p},\mathbb{F}_{p^{2}},\ldots,\mathbb{F}_{p^{n}}
. For the numerical moments, we consider the reduction of the curve modulo primes
p
of good reduction and use the built-in SageMath
[
Sag24
]
function
count_points(n)
, which computes the number of points over the finite fields
\mathbb{F}_{p},\mathbb{F}_{p^{2}},\ldots,\mathbb{F}_{p^{n}}
on schemes defined over
\mathbb{F}_{p}
. Using the results of Lemmas
2.7
and
2.8
, we are able to speed up our computations by restricting to primes
p\equiv 1\pmod{\ell^{2}}
. However, since the genii of our curves are so large, we are limited to a somewhat small bound for
p
. For example, when
\ell=5
the genus is 10 and we use
p<2^{22}
. Implementing the formula from Lemma
2.9
, unfortunately, did not meaningfully improve computation time.

The theoretical moments are computed from the coefficients of the characteristic polynomial of random conjugacy classes in the Sato–Tate group. By the isomorphism in Equation (
15
) of Theorem
3.4
, we can easily compute the moment sequence by working with matrices in the group
\langle\U(1)^{g},\gamma\rangle
. Throughout, let
U
be a matrix in
\U(1)^{g}
and let
\mu_{k}
denote the projection onto the interval
\left[-\binom{2g}{k},\binom{2g}{k}\right]
of the Haar measure obtained from the Sato-Tate group. We denote the restriction of
\mu_{k}
to the component
U\gamma^{i}
by
{}^{i}\mu_{k}
. In what follows, we describe two different techniques for working with the characteristic polynomials and computing moment statistics.

4.1.1.
The
a_{1}
-Coefficient

In this section we describe methods for computing the
a_{1}
-coefficients of the matrices of the form
\U\gamma^{i}
that appear in the isomorphism in Equation (
15
). First note that if
i>0
then the matrix
\gamma^{i}
has no nonzero entries along its diagonal. This can be seen from the definition of
\gamma
in Definition
3.3
and from the relationship between
\gamma
and the action of the Galois automorphism
\sigma_{n}
described in the proof Theorem
3.4
. Hence, when
i>0
, the
a_{1}
-coefficient of the characteristic polynomial of any matrix of the form
\U\gamma^{i}
equals 0 and

M_{n}[{}^{i}\mu_{1}]=0.

When
i=0
, we simply have the matrix
U\in\U(1)^{g}
, which is a diagonal matrix of the form

U=\diag(u_{0},\overline{u}_{0},u_{1},\overline{u}_{1},\ldots,u_{g-1},\overline{u}_{g-1}).

The
a_{1}
-coefficient is related to the trace of the matrix, and we have
a_{1}=-(\alpha_{0}+\alpha_{1}+\ldots+\alpha_{g-1}),
where
\alpha_{i}:=u_{i}+\overline{u}_{i}.
The moment sequence for each
\alpha_{i}
is that of the unitary group
\U(1)

M[\mu_{\U(1)}]=[1,0,2,0,6,0,20,\ldots].

Thus, the
n^{th}
moment of the
a_{1}
-coefficient for
\U\gamma^{0}
is the expected value of the
n^{th}
power of this sum, which is computed using properties of expected values as follows:

M_{n}[{}^{0}\mu_{1}]=\sum_{b_{0}+b_{1}+...+b_{g-1}=n}\binom{n}{b_{0},b_{1},...,b_{g-1}}M_{b_{0}}[\alpha_{0}]M_{b_{1}}[\alpha_{1}]...M_{b_{g-1}}[\alpha_{g-1}].

See
[
GH24
]
for an implementation of this.

4.1.2.
The
a_{2},a_{3},\ldots,a_{g}
-Coefficients

We now describe techniques for computing the remaining coefficients of the characteristic polynomials. Perhaps the most straightforward method to compute these higher traces is to use the built-in
charpoly()
function in SageMath. In our work, we found that this function sufficed for matrices of the form
\U\gamma^{i}
, where
i>0
, but it was prohibitively slow for the matrices
\U\gamma^{0}
.

An alternative method for computing the higher traces is to make use of the fact that the coefficients of the characteristic polynomial of a matrix can be expressed in terms of its eigenvalues (see, for example,
[
KK68
, Section 13.4-5]
). This works particularly well for the diagonal matrices
\U\gamma^{0}
since the eigenvalues are exactly the diagonal entries
u_{0},\overline{u}_{0},u_{1},\overline{u}_{1},\ldots,u_{g-1},\overline{u}_{g-1}
. See
[
GH24
]
for an implementation of this.
