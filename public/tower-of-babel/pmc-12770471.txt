Frailty and transitions across cardiometabolic disease states: evidence from multistate models in a 16-year Chinese cohort
Authors: Rui Hang Zhang, Jiao Wang, Ying Wang, Rui Qiang Li, Ting Yu Lu, Bai Jing Zhou, Lin Yang, Lei Su, Wei Sen Zhang, Tai Hing Lam, Kar Keung Cheng, Lin Xu
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Frailty is a prevalent geriatric syndrome, but its role in cardiometabolic disease (CMD) progression remains unclear. We examined whether frailty predicted CMD transitions. We included 22,754 participants aged 50+ from the Guangzhou Biobank Cohort Study without baseline CMD. Frailty was assessed using a 35-item frailty index (FI). Cardiometabolic multimorbidity (CMM) was defined as ≥2 of type 2 diabetes, ischemic heart disease, and stroke. Cox and multistate models estimated associations of frailty with first CMD (FCMD), CMM, and mortality. During a mean follow-up of 16.4 years, 8710 developed FCMD, 2332 progressed to CMM, and 5411 died. After adjustment, multistate analyses showed that frailty accelerated transitions from health to FCMD, from FCMD to CMM, and from CMM to death (HR per 0.1 increment in FI ranging from 1.14 to 1.48). Frailty was independently associated with CMD progression and mortality. Multistate analyses highlight its dynamic role in multimorbidity, supporting routine frailty assessment in cardiometabolic prevention.

Introduction

Cardiometabolic multimorbidity (CMM), defined as the coexistence of two or three cardiometabolic diseases (CMDs), typically including diabetes, ischemic heart disease (IHD) and stroke1–3, is one of the most common multimorbidity patterns4. The pooled global prevalence of multimorbidity was 42.4% till 20205. In 2019, there were 197 million (95% uncertainty interval (UI): 178–220 million) prevalent cases of IHD and 101 million (93.2–111 million) prevalent cases of stroke6. In 2021, the global age-standardised total diabetes prevalence was 6.1% (5.8–6.5%)7. CMM often coexists with frailty8, which may exacerbate its adverse outcomes9. The combination of frailty and CMM has been associated with a significantly higher mortality risk, more than doubling the risk compared to either condition alone9.

Frailty, a prevalent condition in aging populations10, predicts adverse outcomes comparable to chronic diseases3,11. The Frailty Index (FI) is based on the cumulative deficit model, quantifying frailty as a score that reflects the burden of accumulated health deficits across multiple domains12. A key advantage of the FI lies in its flexibility, as it can be calculated retrospectively using diverse data sources, including surveys, performance tests, and clinical records12. Previous research has demonstrated FI’s robust predictive validity for adverse health outcomes13. Although integrating frailty assessment into clinical practice could potentially improve risk stratification and guide precision management, its implementation remains limited, with frailty assessment rates below 5% in 50% of primary care practices14. Specifically, patients with both frailty and CMM may require more intensive monitoring, physical therapy and nutritional support than robust patients with the same diseases. However, frailty remains overlooked in clinical practice, possibly due to limited evidence on its role in disease progression and prognosis15. Given its dynamic and potentially reversible nature10, early identification of frailty presents an opportunity to mitigate disease burden before irreversible multimorbidity develops. Previous randomized controlled trials (RCTs) have shown that multicomponent interventions significantly improve frailty in middle-aged and older adults16,17. We conducted a PubMed search using the terms “frailty” and “cardiometabolic multimorbidity” and identified two cross-sectional studies (one from the US8 and another using data from the HRS, CHARLS, ELSA, and SHARE cohorts18), as well as two prospective cohort studies from the UK2,15 and one cohort study from China19, all of which reported positive associations between frailty and CMM. However, previous studies examining frailty and CMM have mostly been cross-sectional8,18 or overlooked the risks associated with transitions between different disease events19. Evidence from large prospective cohorts in Chinese populations applying multi-state models to characterize these longitudinal associations remains scarce. Moreover, it remains unclear whether inflammation acts as a mediator in the association between frailty and CMM.

Chronic low-grade inflammation was considered a biological mechanism linking aging, frailty, and chronic disease progression20. Investigating whether inflammation mediates the association between frailty and CMM could provide insight into potential therapeutic targets. For example, a 6-month RCT showed a significant reduction in plasma high-sensitivity C-reactive protein (hsCRP) concentrations during a lower-fat diet intervention21. Using data from the Guangzhou Biobank Cohort Study (GBCS), a large-scale prospective cohort with nearly 20 years of follow-up, we examined the associations between frailty and CMM progression, and whether inflammation was a mediator.

Results

Population characteristics

Of 30,430 participants attended baseline survey, those with more than 20% of FI items missing (N = 304), with a history of heart disease (N = 3538), diabetes (N = 3338), or stroke (N = 173), as well as those with unreasonable morbidity records (e.g., time of death occurs prior to time of morbidity) (N = 16) or lost to follow-up (N = 307) were excluded, leaving 22,754 participants in the main analysis (Supplementary Fig. 1). Of them, 7388 participants with inflammatory biomarker data were included in mediation analyses.

Characteristics of the FI

The FI showed a right skewed distribution (Supplementary Fig. 2). Inter-item correlation among the 35 items (Supplementary Table 1) was low, with Spearman’s rank correlation coefficients ranging from -0.06 to 0.26 (Supplementary Fig. 3). The prevalence of individual item, as well as the mean FI score and prevalence of frailty increased with older age (Supplementary Figs. 4, 5). Frailty was inversely associated with relative grip strength (RGS) and absolute grip strength (AGS) (Supplementary Table 2). The adjusted β (95% confidence interval (CI)) of RGSmax, RGSmean, RGSleft, and RGSright for per 0.1 increment in FI was −0.06 (−0.07, −0.05), −0.06 (−0.07, −0.05), −0.06 (−0.07, −0.05), and −0.06 (−0.07, −0.05), respectively (Supplementary Table 2). Similar results were found between frailty and AGS. Frailty was positively associated with time up-and-go test (TUGT) (adjusted β (95% CI) 0.19 (0.17, 0.22)) (Supplementary Table 2), and with higher odds of falls in the past 6 months (adjusted odds ratio (OR) (95% CI) 1.49 (1.33, 1.68)) (Supplementary Table 2). Higher FI scores were also associated with lower quality of life (QoL) across two composite- and eight domain-specific measures (Supplementary Table 3).

In addition, compared with robust participants, participants with frailty had significantly higher risks of all-cause (adjusted hazard ratio (HR) (95% CI) 2.04 (1.87, 2.23)) and cardiovascular disease (CVD) mortality (2.49 (2.11, 2.95)) (Supplementary Table 4). FI showed good discriminative ability for all-cause mortality, with an area under the curve (AUC) of 0.804 (Supplementary Fig. 6) and a C-statistic of 0.76 (Supplementary Table 5).

Per 0.1 increment in FI was associated with increased risks of transition from baseline to FCMD (adjusted HR (95% CI) 1.48 (1.43, 1.53)), baseline to death without CMD (1.32 (1.23, 1.41)), and FCMD to CMM (1.14 (1.07, 1.22)) (Fig. 1). Per 0.1 increment in FI was not associated with risk of death following FCMD (0.97 (0.90, 1.04)). However, a higher risk of death was observed following CMM (1.17 (1.05, 1.31)). Similar patterns were observed when frailty was analysed as a categorical variable (Supplementary Fig. 8). Stacked transition probability plots show that participants with frailty were at higher risks of progression to FCMD, CMM and death over time (Supplementary Fig. 9). The probability of developing FCMD was approximately 20% in participants with frailty, compared with around 10% in robust participants 5 years after baseline. At 15 years, the probability of CMM and death was 40% in the frail group, versus 20% in robust participants.

In disease-specific transition (Supplementary Fig. 10), frailty was most strongly associated with onset of T2D from baseline (adjusted HR (95% CI) per 0.1 increment in FI: 1.62 (1.49, 1.75)). Higher risks of progression from T2D (1.24 (1.08, 1.41)) and stroke (1.14 (1.00, 1.28)) to CMM were also observed. The association between frailty and progression from IHD to CMM was modest and non-significant (1.10 (1.00, 1.22)). No significant associations were observed between frailty and mortality following any single CMD.

Mediation analysis

The participants with and without inflammatory biomarkers were quite similar, with Cohen’s effect sizes ranging from 0.036 to 0.601 (Supplementary Table 6). Frailty (per 0.1 increment in FI) was positively associated with hsCRP (β (95% CI) 0.29 (0.19, 0.39)), white blood cell (WBC) count (0.10 (0.04, 0.16)), and lymphocyte count (0.05 (0.02, 0.07)) (Supplementary Table 7, model 2). The inflammatory biomarkers partially mediated the associations of frailty with CMD outcomes and all-cause mortality, with mediation proportions ranging from 1.0% to 3.9% (Table 3). Discussion

In this large, prospective cohort of middle-aged and older Chinese, frailty was prospectively associated with a higher risk of CMM progression, with inflammation as a potential mediator (mediation proportion from 1.0% to 3.9%). Frailty was associated with a higher risk of an initial CMD, and was also predictive of the transition from CMD to multimorbidity, and from multimorbidity to death. These findings highlight frailty as a dynamic and prognostically relevant condition, often overlooked, adverse health condition14, which should be managed proactively in clinical practice.

In clinical or public health practice, frailty is often underdiagnosed14, possibly due to its classification as a condition rather than a disease and the lack of a consensus on its definition and measurement. Given its potentially reversible nature10, early identification and integration of frailty assessment into routine clinical care may enhance risk stratification and enable timely interventions, such as targeted lifestyle modification, nutrition support and comprehensive geriatric management16,17, to mitigate CMM burden and premature mortality.

Our findings are consistent with previous studies on the association between frailty and the incidence of individual CMDs22–25. A meta-analysis of 6 prospective cohort studies from European populations reported a pooled HR of 1.70 for CVD onset in participants with frailty compared to robust counterparts24. Other cohort studies from European populations also showed higher risks of diabetes in participants with frailty than robust counterparts22,23. A Mendelian randomization (MR) study in European populations further supported a causal role, showing that genetically predicted higher FI score was associated with increased risks of coronary artery disease (OR (95% CI) 1.52 (1.17, 1.96)), T2D (1.80 (1.31, 2.47)), and stroke (1.36 (1.01, 1.84))25. Our results align with these studies and adds new evidence by showing that frailty assessed prior to CMD onset also predicted increased risk of subsequent CMM and death.

Our findings are consistent with previous studies linking frailty to CMM incidence2,15,19. Three cohort studies (one in China19 and two in the UK2,15) showed positive associations between frailty and CMM incidence. In our multistate modeling of the GBCS, which accounted for the dynamic progression of disease states, frailty was significantly associated with higher risks of transitions from baseline health to FCMD, from baseline to death, from FCMD to CMM, and from CMM to death. These patterns were consistent with those from UK Biobank, where HRs for similar transitions ranged from 1.09 to 1.47 per 0.1 increment in FI15. However, in contrast to previous studies2,15, we found no significant association between frailty and the transition from FCMD to death. This disparity may reflect the complex, time-varying nature of frailty, as well as behavioral adaption following FCMD diagnosis. Individuals diagnosed with CMD may adopt healthier lifestyles or receive medical interventions that can slow disease progression and reduce mortality risk26,27. Our results highlight that personalized management strategies for individuals with frailty and a single CMD may avert progression to multimorbidity and premature death, and intervention studies are warranted.

Our mediation analyses showed that the associations of frailty and CMM progression were potentially mediated through inflammation, including hsCRP, WBC count, and lymphocyte count. This finding aligns with results from previous meta-analyses of cross-sectional studies showing positive association between frailty and elevated CRP and WBC count28,29. In a longitudinal study, FI was positively associated with the expression of inflammation-related proteins 5–10 years later30. Several biological mechanisms may underline this mediation. First, frailty and ageing were associated with immune dysregulation and elevated pro-inflammatory immunogenic stimulations involving interleukin-1 (IL-1), IL-1 receptor antagonist protein, and other cytokines, contributing to chronic low-grade inflammation20. Chronic inflammation may impair the synthesis and activity of insulin-like growth factor I (IGF1), which is essential for muscle integrity and protective against plaque instability in atherosclerosis20. Additionally, pro-inflammatory cytokines, such as tumor necrosis factor-alpha (TNF-α), can activate intracellular pathways such as c-Jun amino-terminal kinase (JNK) and I kappa B kinase beta (IKKβ), leading to impaired insulin action31. These findings support the hypothesis that targeting inflammatory pathways may help to prevent or delay CMM development. Anti-inflammatory intervention trials to reduce frailty-associated cardiometabolic risk are warranted20.

In GBCS, the prevalence of frailty was 5.3%. A systematic review pooling data from 21 cohorts involving 61,500 participants reported a weighted frailty prevalence of 10.7%, with individual study estimates ranging from 4% to 59%32. This wide variation highlights the heterogeneity in frailty prevalence due to differences in operational definitions, demographic characteristics, assessment tools, and threshold criteria used across studies32.

We used the FI to assess frailty status in this study, following the deficit accumulation approach developed by Rockwood et al.12. This approach was chosen due to its flexibility and suitability for use in large-scale epidemiologic cohorts. Unlike phenotype-based definitions, the FI incorporates a broader spectrum of health deficits, spanning comorbidities, functional limitations and self-reported symptoms, allowing for a more comprehensive and sensitive measure of biological aging11. The FI has been widely validated and shown to predict adverse outcomes, including mortality, across different settings and populations13. Its use in previous studies on CMDs also facilitates cross-study comparisons and benchmarking18,19,33–35.

This study had several strengths, including a large and well-characterized cohort, extended follow-up duration, and the multistate models to dynamically assess the progression from baseline health through CMD onset to CMM and death. However, our study had some limitations. First, FI was derived primarily from self-reported questionnaire data. Previous studies showed that indices including 30–40 variables were sufficient for predicting adverse outcomes36, and the predictive accuracy did not depend strongly on the items included37. Second, frailty was assessed only once at baseline. Further studies on the association of frailty transitions over time with CMM are warranted. Third, exact dates of disease onset were unavailable, which might have led to the classification of some participants having simultaneous onset of multiple diseases. However, as we obtained as comprehensive information as possible from hospitalization data, record linkage with the Guangzhou Centre for Disease Control and Prevention (GZCDC), follow-up surveys, along with the use of 0.5-day intervals to differentiate disease states, the misclassification bias, if any, should not be a major concern. Fourthly, we were unable to include key inflammatory cytokines such as IL-6 and TNF-α. Future studies incorporating a more comprehensive panel of inflammatory markers are needed to better delineate the potential role of inflammation in the frailty–disease association. Fifthly, our participants were recruited from a social and welfare organization in Guangzhou, and hence, whether the results can be generalized to other populations needs to be examined. Finally, some associations, particularly for specific disease transitions, might have been missed due to limited sample size. Larger cohorts are needed to confirm these findings and to characterize more granular disease trajectories.

Methods

Study participants

GBCS is a three-way collaboration among the Guangzhou Twelfth People’s Hospital, the Universities of Hong Kong, China and Birmingham, UK. Details of the GBCS have been reported previously38. All participants were recruited from a social and welfare organization called “Guangzhou Health and Happiness Association for the Respectable Elders” (GHHARE). Membership is open to Guangzhou permanent residents aged 50 years or older. A total of 30,430 participants were included in baseline survey (2003–2008). Baseline information was collected by face-to-face interviews using computer-based standardized questionnaires. Anthropometric and laboratory parameters were measured using a standardized protocol. The study was approved by the Guangzhou Medical Ethics Committee of the Chinese Medical Association. All participants gave written, informed consent before participation. This study adhered to the principles of the Declaration of Helsinki.

Exposure

The exposure variable was frailty, which was assessed using a FI, constructed in accordance with the standard procedure established by Rockwood et al.12. FI incorporated 35 health-related items reflecting symptoms, signs, disabilities, and diseases. Each variable was coded on a scale from 0 (no deficit) to 1 (deficit present) (Supplementary Table 1)12, and the FI was calculated as the ratio of deficits present to the total number of items assessed, yielding a continuous score from 0 to 1. Items were selected based on four criteria: (1) less than 5% missing data; (2) prevalence between 0.5% and 80% at baseline39; (3) increasing prevalence with age; and (4) no high inter-item correlation (r > 0.95)12. Participants with more than 20% of the FI items missing were excluded12.

Construct validity of the FI was evaluated by examining inter-item correlations (Spearman rank correlation), and age-specific prevalence trends. Associations of FI with grip strength, TUGT score, QoL, and falls were examined. These indicators were selected as they reflected physical function, well-being, and fall risk, i.e., key domains affected by frailty, and are commonly used to assess frailty-related outcomes. Grip strength was assessed using a Jamar Hydraulic Hand Dynamometer with participants in a standing position. The assessment showed good test-retest reproducibility (r > 0.80) and excellent (r = 0.98) inter-rater reliability40. Measurements were performed twice for each hand, and the average value of both assessments for each hand was calculated. The maximal value of the grip strength in left and right hands was used as AGSmax, expressed as kilograms (kg). Maximum RGSmax was equal to AGSmax divided by body mass index (BMI). The average relative grip strength (RGSmean) was equal to the average value of the grip strength in both hands divided by BMI. Hand-specific relative grip strength (RGSleft/RGSright) was equal to the grip strength of the left- or right-hand divided by BMI, respectively41. All RGS parameters were expressed as kg per kg/m2, as described in our previous studies42–44. The TUGT was conducted by asking participants to stand up from a chair, walk 2.5 m around a marker, and return. Trained nurses recorded the time used to complete the test for each participant. The tests were assessed twice, and the scores (in seconds) were averaged. Details of the TUGT have been reported previously43. Self-reported fall history was collected by structured questionnaires, where participants were asked to recall the number of falls experienced in the past 6 months. Responses were categorized as “no falls” or “≥1 fall”. QoL was assessed using the Short-Form 12 Health Survey Version 2 (SF-12v2), an instrument comprising 12 items that cover eight domains: physical functioning (PF), role limitation due to physical problems (RP), bodily pain (BP), general health (GH), vitality (VT), social functioning (SF), role limitation due to emotional problems (RE), and mental health (MH)45. From these domains, two composite scores were calculated: the Physical Component Summary (PCS) and the Mental Component Summary (MCS)45. To facilitate straightforward interpretation, raw scores were standardized to US population norms (mean = 50, standard deviation = 10), with higher scores indicating better QoL45. FI status was categorized as robust (FI ≤ 0.10), prefrail (0.10 < FI < 0.25), or frail (FI ≥ 0.25), consistent with established thresholds10,34,39,46.

Study outcomes

Study outcomes included incident CMDs, CMM, and all-cause mortality. CMDs comprised T2D, IHD and stroke. CMM was defined as the coexistence of at least two of these conditions. The first occurrence of any CMD was designed as the FCMD, and the development of a second CMD marked the onset of CMM15,47. Incident CMDs were identified through three data sources: (1) hospitalization records from the Guangzhou Social Insurance Bureau and the GZCDC through December 2020; (2) death registration data from the GZCDC through November 2023; and (3) the first (March 2008 to December 2012) and second follow-up examinations (March 2013 to January 2020), both of which included self-reported disease, medication use, and fasting glucose measurements.

Disease diagnoses and causes of death were coded by trained hospital staff using the International Classification of Diseases, 10th Revision (ICD-10). IHD was based on code I20 to I25 or self-reported diagnoses of coronary heart disease, angina pectoris, and myocardial infarction48. Stroke was based on code I60 to I69 and self-reported history of stroke2,48. T2D was defined by fasting plasma glucose ≥7.0 mmol/L, self-reported diabetes, or use of hypoglycemic medications49,50. Unspecified diabetes (E14) was presumed to represent T2D27,47. The onset of the first FCMD and CMM was based on the date of first and second incidence of CMDs, respectively. Time to event was calculated from baseline to the first occurrence of the specific outcome, death, or the end of follow-up (November 2023), whichever occurred first.

Potential confounders and mediators

Multivariable models were adjusted for sociodemographic and behavioral factors known to influence frailty, CMDs, and mortality. These included sex, age (in years), education (primary or below, middle school, and college or above), occupation (manual, non-manual, and others), family income (<30,000 CNY/year, ≥30,000 CNY/year, and not known; US$1 ≈ 7 CNY), smoking status (never, former, and current), alcohol use (never, former, and current), and physical activity (inactive, moderate, and active).

To investigate potential biological mechanisms, systemic inflammatory biomarkers, including hsCRP, WBC count, lymphocyte count, platelets, and albumin were analysed as potential mediators of the association between frailty and study outcomes.

Statistical analysis

Participant characteristics were described using means (SDs) for continuous variables and proportions for categorical variables. Differences by frailty categories were assessed using one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) for continuous variables and chi-square tests for categorical variables. Cohen’s effect sizes were used to compare the baseline characteristics by participants with and without inflammatory biomarker data.

FI distribution, mean FI scores and the prevalence of frailty were plotted by sex and age. Predictive validity of FI was assessed using Cox proportional hazards regression for all-cause and CVD mortality. Discriminative capacity was assessed using receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curves to estimate AUCs, logistic regression-derived sensitivity and specificity, and C-statistics from Cox models.

Cox regression was used to analyse the associations of baseline frailty status with risks of individual CMDs (T2D, IHD, stroke), FCMD, CMM, and all-cause mortality, yielding crude and adjusted HRs with 95% CIs. Schoenfeld’s residuals were used to assess proportional hazards (PH) assumption, and time-varying covariates were considered when PH assumption was violated. Additionally, we used RCS plots with four knots to model the associations of frailty with major diseases and all-cause mortality.

Multistate models accounting for baseline covariates were used to assess transitions across five states: baseline to FCMD, baseline to death without CMD, FCMD to CMM, FCMD to death, and CMM to death, denoted as transition pattern A (Fig. 1)51. To accommodate instances of simultaneous disease onset, a correction of 0.5 days was subtracted from the recorded date of the earlier event27. In a secondary model (denoted as transition pattern B), FCMD was further disaggregated into T2D, IHD, and stroke, yielding 11 possible transitions (Fig. 1). When the temporal order of disease onset was uncertain, a theoretical order of T2D, IHD, and stroke was assumed2.

Linear regression was used to analyse associations of frailty with inflammatory biomarkers. Inflammatory biomarkers significantly associated with frailty were included in mediation analyses using “mediation” package in R. Sensitivity analyses excluded participants who died within the first two years of follow-up or had a history of cancer at baseline to mitigate reverse causation. Effect modification by sex and age was explored through stratified analyses and interaction testing. Statistical significance was defined as a two-sided P-value < 0.05. All analyses were conducted using Stata/MP 17.0 (Stata Corp LP) and R 4.4.1 (R Foundation for Statistical Computing).
