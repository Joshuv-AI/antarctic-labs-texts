Segmental Hair Analysis of Diphenhydramine and Cyclizine Following a Single Dose
Authors: Jan Bílek, Marie Katrine Klose Nielsen, Robert Kronstrand, Sys Stybe Johansen
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTDiphenhydramine and cyclizine are over‐the‐counter (in many countries) antihistamines that have considerable sedative effects. Therefore, they can be used in drug‐facilitated crimes to incapacitate victims. There is a knowledge gap regarding detection possibilities and concentration levels of these antihistamines in hair. This study aimed to investigate whether a single dose of diphenhydramine and cyclizine could be quantified in head hair, and for how long. A single‐dose study was conducted in which 12 adult volunteers ingested a single dose of diphenhydramine (25 mg) and cyclizine (44 mg). Hair samples were collected before drug intake and at 1, 3, 5, and if possible, 12 months after intake. To quantify a single dose of antihistamines in head hair, a validated LC–MS/MS method with a lower limit of quantification of 1 pg/mg was applied. Both antihistamines and their main metabolites, N‐demethyldiphenhydramine and norcyclizine, were detected in all hair samples collected up to 5 months after intake. Twelve months after intake, diphenhydramine was quantifiable in 25% and cyclizine in 62.5% of available hair samples. The concentrations found in hair within 1 year after intake ranged from 0 to 610 pg/mg for diphenhydramine and from 0 to 590 pg/mg for cyclizine. The median decrease in concentration over 2, 4, and 11 months was 43%, 70%, and 100% for diphenhydramine and 40%, 64%, and 98% for cyclizine. The data obtained provided indicative concentration ranges for a single oral dose of diphenhydramine and cyclizine in hair, thereby improving the interpretation of hair analyses in forensic casework.

Introduction

Drug‐facilitated crimes (DFCs) are crimes where the victims are under the influence of psychoactive drug(s), which are often ingested unknowingly [1]. DFCs mostly include sexual assault and robbery, and the drugs used usually cause sedation, sleepiness, loss of consciousness, and other accompanying effects, such as retrograde amnesia [2]. Consequently, the victim may not recall the incident and is confused about what happened. This confusion, along with fear, feelings of shame, or even mental breakdown, can lead to late reporting of the incident to the police and, hence, delayed collection of blood and urine for forensic investigation. In such cases, head hair is the biological material of interest as it provides a wider detection window compared with biofluids [3, 4]. The detection window of hair can range from weeks to years, depending on the hair length and other factors such as dose, type of drug, and sensitivity of the used analytical method [2].

After a drug is administered, it can be deposited in hair through the blood circulation, via sweat or sebum [4]. Under favorable conditions, the deposited drugs are stable in the keratinized hair matrix [3] and segmental analysis of head hair can be used as a tool for chronological investigation of an individual's drug exposure history [5]. Assuming that head hair grows with an average growth rate of 1 cm per month [6], every 1‐cm‐long segment corresponds to the hair grown during approximately 1 month and can reveal the presence of drugs consumed within this period [7]. Although hair analysis is not able to differentiate between voluntary and involuntary drug intake, it can be used to monitor relative changes in drug concentration in hair within an individual, prove exposure to the drug, and provide additional information depicting a single dose or repeated administrations [5, 8].

Drug analysis in hair and the interpretation of the findings can be challenging. The concentrations of drugs in hair can vary significantly depending on several factors, including the physicochemical properties and pharmacokinetics of the drug, dose, administration route and frequency, physiological characteristics of the hair, hair treatments, and the wash‐out effect from normal hair hygiene [3, 8]. Quantification of very low concentrations of drugs is often necessary, requiring high performance of the applied analytical method in terms of sensitivity and selectivity. Due to the character of hair, the sample preparation is usually more laborious and resource‐intensive compared with the analysis of biofluids. Despite these challenges, hair has become an important biological material that complements blood and urine analysis in forensic investigations. Although hair has been used for the analysis of drugs and illicit drugs in DFCs for several years, our knowledge about the concentration levels of different drugs in hair is still limited. Reference concentrations of drugs and metabolites in hair are crucial in forensic investigations of DFCs, enabling the interpretation of hair analysis results and enhancing the overall understanding of the criminal case. To date, only a limited number of controlled‐dose studies have been conducted on relevant types of drugs, such as sedatives–hypnotics–anxiolytics (diazepam [9], zopiclone [10], zolpidem [11], flunitrazepam [12], clonazepam [13], estazolam [14], GHB [15]), anesthetics (ketamine [16]), or opioid analgesics (tramadol [17], codeine [18, 19]).

The present study investigates two first‐generation H1‐antihistamines, diphenhydramine and cyclizine. H1‐antihistamines act as competitive inhibitors of histamine, binding to the H1 receptor and resulting in antihistamine and anticholinergic activities [20]. Diphenhydramine is indicated for the treatment of symptoms associated with allergic rhinitis, such as itchy eyes, runny nose, sneezing, or nausea associated with motion sickness. Cyclizine has antiemetic properties and is most often used to treat nausea, vomiting, and dizziness associated with motion sickness. Both diphenhydramine and cyclizine are over‐the‐counter drugs in many countries and can have considerable sedative properties, making them relevant in DFCs [20]. Although the use of diphenhydramine in DFCs has been reported [8, 21, 22, 23, 24], the data about reference concentrations in hair are limited. To date, only two studies with a focus on controlled intake of diphenhydramine and its concentration in hair have been conducted [25, 26]. Abuse of cyclizine has been reported due to its euphoric properties after administration of higher doses [27, 28, 29, 30]. Moreover, cases of transient paralysis [31] and fatal overdose [32] have been reported in association with cyclizine intake. Nevertheless, no data are available on cyclizine concentrations in hair, either from case reports or controlled dosing studies.

This study aimed to investigate concentration levels of diphenhydramine, cyclizine, and their main metabolites, N‐demethyldiphenhydramine and norcyclizine, in human head hair following a single dose. The study was designed to answer the following research questions: Can a single dose of diphenhydramine and cyclizine be measured in head hair, and how long?What are the concentration levels and temporal patterns of diphenhydramine and cyclizine in head hair at different times after intake of a single dose?Does thermal hair‐straightening treatment affect diphenhydramine and cyclizine concentrations in hair?

Materials and Methods

A Controlled Single‐Dose Study

To investigate the concentration levels of diphenhydramine and cyclizine in hair after a single dose and to answer the research questions, a controlled single‐dose study was designed. Twelve healthy adult volunteers (ID1–ID12) of different hair colors (black, dark brown, light brown, blond) participated in the study. The group of participants consisted of nine females and three males aged between 23 and 51 years old (mean age 41 years, median age 45 years). Hair growth rates of the participants were measured twice and ranged from 1.0 to 1.6 cm/month (median 1.2 cm/month). The participants reported all relevant medications taken before and during the study and were instructed not to cut or treat their hair during the study period. The participants ingested a single dose of the over‐the‐counter pharmaceutical products Benylan (28 mg of diphenhydramine hydrochloride, 10 mL of oral solution 2.8 mg/mL McNeil Denmark ApS, Soeborg, Denmark) and Marzine (50 mg of cyclizine hydrochloride, 1 tablet, Viatris ApS, Ballerup, Denmark). These doses correspond to 25 mg of diphenhydramine and 44 mg of cyclizine as pure drugs (drug base equivalent). The drugs were taken before bedtime, and the interval between consumption of the two drugs was approximately 24 h. The hair samples were collected before drug intake (T0), and 1 (T1), 3 (T2), 5 (T3), and 12 months (T4) post‐intake. The T3 hair sample was divided into two samples, where one was left untreated and the other (T3b) was treated three times with a thermal hair‐straightener at 200°C. When collecting the hair sample, the hair was cut from the vertex posterior region of the head as close to the scalp as possible. The collected hair samples were folded into a piece of aluminum foil and stored dark in a paper envelope at room temperature.

Ethical Considerations

All participants signed an informed consent form before participating in the study. The study was considered a methodology study by the Danish National Committee on Health Research Ethics (De Videnskabsetiske Komiteer, Region Hovedstaden, Denmark, F‐23039478). The University of Copenhagen approved the study protocol and registered the project under the number 514‐0893/23‐3000.

Chemicals and Reagents

Diphenhydramine was purchased from Fluka/Sigma Aldrich (Buchs, Switzerland) (99.9% purity), N‐demethyldiphenhydramine was purchased from TRC (Toronto, Ontario, Canada) (98% purity). Cyclizine was purchased from LGC (Teddington, England, UK) (100% purity). Norcyclizine was purchased from Alfa Aesar, Thermo Fisher Scientific (Haverhill, Massachusetts, USA) (99.4% purity). Diphenhydramine‐d5 was used as the internal standard (IS) for diphenhydramine and N‐demethyldiphenhydramine, and cyclizine‐d3 was used as the IS for cyclizine and norcyclizine, and they were both purchased from CDN Isotopes (Pointe‐Claire, Quebec, Canada) (99.0% purity). Propan‐2‐ol (HiPerSolv Chromanorm for HPLC) and methanol (HiPerSolv Chromanorm for HPLC, super gradient) were purchased from VWR (Darmstadt, Germany). Acetonitrile (Optima LC–MS Grade) and formic acid (Optima LC–MS Grade, 99.5% purity) were purchased from Fischer Scientific (Loughborough, England, UK). Ammonium formate (BioUltra, > 99.0% purity) was purchased from Fluka/Sigma Aldrich (Buchs, Switzerland). Deionized water for hair sample washing and mobile phase preparation was produced in‐house using an Elga Purelab Flex 3 system (Elga LabWater, Veolia Water, Paris, France).

Standard Solutions and Calibrators

A standard stock solution containing 25 mg/L of cyclizine, norcyclizine, diphenhydramine, and N‐demethyldiphenhydramine in methanol was prepared. A working standard solution was prepared freshly every time by diluting the standard stock solution to 2 mg/L with the extraction medium. The extraction medium was a 25:29:46 (v/v/v) mixture of methanol/acetonitrile/2 mM ammonium formate (pH 5.3). An IS stock solution containing 0.2 mg/L of diphenhydramine‐d5 and cyclizine‐d3 in methanol was prepared. A working IS solution was prepared freshly every time by diluting the IS stock solution to 0.01 mg/L with the extraction medium. Standard solutions from 0.04 to 40 μg/L were prepared by diluting the standard stock solution with the extraction medium. Nine calibrators ranging from 1 to 1000 pg/mg were prepared by adding 250 μL of the corresponding standard solution and 50 μL of working IS solution to 10 mg of drug‐free hair. The drug‐free hair used for the preparation of matrix‐matched calibrators, quality control (QC) samples, and blank samples was collected from drug‐abstinent donors.

QC Samples

Hair QC samples were prepared at up to four concentration levels by soaking drug‐free hair (around 1 g) in 10 mL of 0.001–1 mg/L soaking solutions containing cyclizine, norcyclizine, diphenhydramine, and N‐demethyldiphenhydramine diluted in a 1:1 mixture of methanol/water (v/v). The hair immersed in such a solution was left to soak for 2 h at room temperature. Afterwards, the soaking solution was decanted, and the hair was rinsed four times with 10 mL of methanol and dried overnight at room temperature. Further details are given in a previous publication [33]. The concentrations of the analytes in the prepared QC hair samples were in the range of 1–890 pg/mg, approximately covering the measuring range of the applied analytical method.

Hair Sample Preparation

Antihistamines were extracted from the hair using a previously published method [34] with minor modifications. Briefly, the hair tuft was aligned and cut into 2–16 segments of 1 cm length as demonstrated in the segmentation plan given in Table 1. Around 10 mg of the segmented hair were weighed into Precellys tubes (Bertin Technologies, Montigny‐le‐Bretonneux, France) using analytical balances Excellence Plus XP 105 Delta Range (Mettler Toledo, Greifensee, Switzerland) and washed. The washing procedure consisted of one isopropanol wash (1 mL) and two aqueous washes (0.5 mL each). During each wash, the samples were shaken for 1 min at 1500 RPM using an orbital shaker IKA VXR Basics VIBRAX (IKA, Staufen, Germany) and then centrifuged for 3 min at 6500 RPM (2840 ×g) using a high‐speed microcentrifuge MC‐24 Touch (Benchmark Scientific, New Jersey, USA). After washing, the excess liquid was removed. A volume of 115 μL of the last aqueous wash was pipetted into an Eppendorf tube, preserved by adding 135 μL of a 54:46 (v/v) mixture of ACN/MeOH, stored in the freezer at −20°C, and analyzed later together with the hair extracts to check for contamination. The washes of the expected drug‐positive segments and their neighboring segments were checked for contamination. The contamination was considered negligible if the analyte concentration measured in the wash was less than 10% of the concentration found in the corresponding segment, as recommended by Kintz [5]. The washed hair samples were dried overnight (approximately 18 h) at room temperature. The following day, six stainless steel beads (2.8 mm), Precellys Bulk Bead (Bertin Technologies, Montigny‐le‐Bretonneux, France), 250 μL of extraction medium, and 50 μL of 0.01 mg/L working IS solution were added. The samples were pulverized in two homogenization cycles at 6500 RPM for 4 × 30 s each cycle, using Precellys Evolution homogenizer (Bertin Technologies, Montigny‐le‐Bretonneux, France), and centrifuged for 3 min at 6500 RPM (2840 ×g). The samples were incubated overnight (approximately 18 h) at 37°C in a UN160 oven from Memmert (Schwabach, Germany). After incubation, the samples were centrifuged under the same conditions as previously, and the supernatant was filtered using filter vials Whatman Mini‐UniPrep, syringeless filter device with PTFE filter media, polypropylene housing, and pore size of 0.22 μm from Cytiva, Whatman (Marlborough, Massachusetts, USA), before analysis.

Analytical Method

Quantitative analysis was performed using an Acquity UHPLC system coupled with a Xevo TQ‐S triple quadrupole mass spectrometric detector (Waters, Milford, Massachusetts, USA) equipped with MassLynx 4.2 software (Waters, Milford, Massachusetts, USA). Further instrument details are listed in the publication by Wang et al. [34]. The samples were injected onto an Acquity UPLC HSS C18 chromatographic column (1.8 μm, 150 × 2.1 mm) (Waters, Milford, Massachusetts, USA) held at 50°C. The injection volume was 2 μL, and the mobile phase flow rate was 0.4 mL/min. The mobile phase consisted of 5 mM ammonium formate in 0.1% formic acid (pH 3) (mobile phase A) and 0.1% formic acid in acetonitrile (mobile phase B). The gradient elution program applied was at 20% B (0–0.2 min), 20%–30% B (0.2–1 min), 30%–65% B (1–6 min), 65%–100% B (6–6.5 min), 100% B (6.5–7.5 min), 100%–20% B (7.5–7.6 min), and 20% B (7.6–9 min). The autosampler was operated at a temperature of 10°C.

All analyses were conducted in multiple reaction monitoring (MRM) using electrospray ionization in positive mode. Ion source conditions were set as follows: capillary voltage 3 kV, source temperature 150°C, desolvation gas temperature 600°C, desolvation gas flow 1000 L/h, cone gas flow 150 L/h, collision gas flow 0.17 mL/min, and nebulizer gas flow 7 bar. Vacuum pressure in the collision cell was 3.8 × 10−3 mbar. Nitrogen (5.0) from the cryotank was used as the desolvation and cone gas, and argon (5.0) was used as the collision gas. Four MRM transitions were used for each analyte. 12C MRM transitions were applied for analyte quantification and confirmation over the range from the lower limit of quantification (LLOQ, 1 pg/mg) to 100 pg/mg. 13C MRM transitions were applied for analyte quantification and confirmation over the range from 100 pg/mg to the upper limit of quantification (ULOQ, 1000 pg/mg). The applied MRM transitions are summarized in Section S1 of the Supporting Information (Table S1). The cone voltage was set at 20 V for all analytes and ISs. Calibrators, two blank hair samples (one washed and one unwashed), four QC hair samples, and relevant wash samples were included in each batch of samples.

Method Validation

The method validation was performed according to the Society of Hair Testing guidelines [6, 35] and guidelines for method validation within the field of forensic toxicology [36, 37] with minor modifications. Validation details can be found in Section S2 of the Supporting Information—selectivity (S2.1.), calibration model (S2.2.), accuracy and precision (S2.3.), stability of processed samples (S2.4.), measuring range and method limits (S2.5.), matrix effects, process efficiency and extraction recovery (S2.6.), and carry over (S2.7. and Figure S1) were evaluated. Acceptance and tolerance criteria are listed in Table S2 of the same section of the Supporting Information.

Data Handling and Statistical Analysis

Chromatographic peaks were integrated using TargetLynx XS software by Waters Corporation (Milford, Massachusetts, USA). Descriptive statistical analyses of the maximum drug concentrations, change in cumulated drug concentration over time, and metabolite‐to‐parent drug ratios were performed using Microsoft Excel for Microsoft 365 (Microsoft Corporation, Redmond, Washington, USA). OriginPro2025 by OriginLab Corporation (Northampton, Massachusetts, USA) was used for data visualization and to perform a paired‐sample t‐test for statistical evaluation of the concentration differences between straightened and unstraightened hair.

Definitions

The term maximum concentration referred to the highest analyte concentration found in a specific segment within each sampling time point after drug intake. The maximum concentrations were used to establish indicative analyte concentration ranges and to calculate metabolite‐to‐parent drug ratios at each sampling time point. The term cumulated concentration referred to the sum of the analyte concentrations in all segments within each sampling time point. Cumulated concentrations were used to assess the effects of thermal hair straightening and the change in drug concentration in hair over time. The term temporal pattern of a drug in hair referred to its distribution along the hair shaft. Temporal patterns were used to assess drug distribution within the hair sample over time, depicting hair segments containing the drug and indicating possible contributions from sweat or sebum.

Results

In total, 454 segments of 68 hair samples collected from 12 participants at five different time points during 1 year were analyzed. A few relevant deviations from the study protocol were noted. The T0, T1, T2, and T3 hair samples were collected from all 12 participants, whereas the T4 hair sample was collected from eight participants who had not cut their hair during the study period. Participant ID7 reported having taken Benylan (diphenhydramine) months before the study was initiated. The T1 hair sample of one participant (ID12) was collected 8 days late, whereas the T3 hair sample of ID8 was collected 11 days late. The T3 hair sample of ID11 was collected 11 days earlier.

Diphenhydramine, cyclizine, and the main metabolites were found in all hair samples collected from all participants up to 5 months after the drug intake. Out of eight participants, from whom we a hair sample was available for collection 12 months after intake, diphenhydramine could be quantified in two (ID5, ID8) and cyclizine in five (ID3, ID5, ID6, ID8, ID10). Analysis of the final aqueous wash samples showed no contamination in the hair samples.

Drug Concentrations in Hair

Findings in Hair Samples Collected Before Drug Intake

For six participants (ID1, ID5, ID9, ID10, ID11, ID12), diphenhydramine was detected in T0 hair samples collected before drug intake (1.1–9.7 pg/mg). The cumulated concentrations found in hair before drug intake were 0.51%–19.9% of the cumulated concentration found 1 month after intake for the respective participant. N‐demethyldiphenhydramine was detected in one T0 hair sample at LLOQ (ID1, 1.1 pg/mg), and the determined concentration was 3.9% of the cumulated concentration found 1 month after intake for this specific participant. Cyclizine was detected in two T0 hair samples with concentrations near LLOQ (1.7–5.8 pg/mg, ID5 and ID12). The cumulated concentrations of cyclizine found in hair before drug intake were 0.58% and 13.2% of the cumulated concentration found 1 month after intake for the respective participants. Norcyclizine was not detected in any hair sample collected before drug intake.

Diphenhydramine

The highest maximum concentrations of diphenhydramine and its metabolite N‐demethyldiphenhydramine following a single dose of 25 mg of diphenhydramine were found at the sampling time point 1 month after intake for all 12 participants. The range of maximum concentrations in the proximal segment (0–1 cm) was 16–610 pg/mg (median 52 pg/mg) for diphenhydramine, and 7.1–310 pg/mg (median 20 pg/mg) for the metabolite N‐demethyldiphenhydramine. The means, medians, and ranges of maximum concentrations of diphenhydramine and N‐demethyldiphenhydramine in hair at all sampling time points are summarized in Table 2. At the first sampling point after intake, the highest measured maximum concentration of diphenhydramine and N‐demethyldiphenhydramine was observed in a participant with black hair ID5 (610 and 310 pg/mg), and the lowest measured maximum concentration was found in a participant with blond hair ID12 (16 and 7.1 pg/mg).

Metabolite‐to‐Parent Drug Ratios in Hair

The metabolite‐to‐parent drug ratio was calculated for each participant at sampling time points T1, T2, and T3, using values measured in the segment with the maximum parent drug concentration (n = 36 segments). The ratio of N‐demethyldiphenhydramine to diphenhydramine ranged from 0.16 to 0.91 with a median of 0.47 (mean 0.51). The intra‐individual RSD of the ratio across sampling time points ranged from 5% to 32% (mean 15%), whereas the total RSD across all sampling time points and participants was 41%. The ratio of norcyclizine to cyclizine ranged from 0.10 to 0.71 with a median of 0.38 (mean 0.36). The intra‐individual RSD of the ratio across sampling time points ranged from 5% to 29% (mean 15%), whereas the total RSD across all sampling time points and participants was 39%. The range, mean, median values, and RSDs for the metabolite‐to‐parent drug ratio for each participant and both drugs are shown in Section S3 of the Supporting Information (Table S3).

Temporal Drug Patterns

Diphenhydramine

In the hair samples collected 1 month after intake (T1), the proximal segment (0–1.0 cm) contained the highest levels of diphenhydramine and N‐demethyldiphenhydramine in all 12 participants. In addition to the proximal segment of T1 hair samples, diphenhydramine was also detected in Segment S2 of all participants and Segments S3 and S4 of 67% of participants. In the neighboring segment (S2), the determined concentrations of diphenhydramine were 3.8%–67% of the maximum concentration found for the individual participants. In the hair samples collected 3 months after intake (T2), the highest concentrations of diphenhydramine were found in Segment S3 for 75% and in Segment S4 for 25% of the participants. However, diphenhydramine was also found in lower quantities in other segments—the Segment S5 for 58%, Segments S2 and S6 for 50%, and Segment S1 for 17% of the participants. In the neighboring segments, the determined concentrations were 0%–89% of the maximum concentration found for the individual participants. In the hair samples collected 5 months after intake (T3), the highest concentrations of diphenhydramine were found in Segment S6 for 50%, Segment S5 for 33%, and in Segments S4 and S7 for 8% of the participants. Moreover, diphenhydramine was also detected in most of the remaining segments. In the neighboring segments, the determined concentrations were 0%–97% of the maximum concentration found for the individual participants. In the hair samples collected 12 months after intake (T4), diphenhydramine was detected in two out of eight participants, one having black hair (ID5) and the other dark brown hair (ID8). In the former, the drug was distributed from Segments S9 to S22, with the maximum concentration of diphenhydramine found in Segment S16. In the latter, the drug was spread from Segments S11 to S16, with the highest concentration of diphenhydramine found in Segment S13. In the neighboring segments, the determined concentrations were 73%–87% of the maximum concentration found for the individual participants. Two examples of the temporal patterns of diphenhydramine and N‐demethyldiphenhydramine in two participants (ID5—black hair and ID7—blond hair) are shown in Figure 1. Cyclizine

In the hair samples collected 1 month after intake (T1), the highest concentrations of cyclizine and norcyclizine were found in the proximal segment (0–1.0 cm) of all 12 participants. In addition to the proximal segment of T1 samples, cyclizine was also found in Segment S2 of all participants and in Segments S3 and S4 of 83% of the participants. In the neighboring segment (S2), the determined concentrations of cyclizine were 2.6%–21% of the maximum concentration found for the individual participants. In the hair samples collected 3 months after intake (T2), the highest concentrations of cyclizine were found in Segment S3 for 75% of the participants, Segment S4 for 17%, and Segment S2 for 8% of the participants. However, cyclizine was also found in lower quantities in the other analyzed segments—Segment S5 for 67%, Segment S6 for 50%, and Segment S1 for 25% of the participants. In the neighboring segments, the determined concentrations of cyclizine were 0%–67% of the maximum concentration found for the individual participants. In the hair samples collected 5 months after intake (T3), the highest concentrations of cyclizine were found in Segment S6 for 50% of the participants, in Segment S5 for 25%, in Segment S4 for 17%, and in Segment S7 for 8% of the participants. However, cyclizine was also found in most of the remaining segments (S8 for 33%, S9 for 25%, S10 and S3 for 17% and S2 for 8% of the participants). In the neighboring segments, the determined concentrations of cyclizine were 0%–98% of the maximum concentration found for the individual participants. In the hair samples collected 12 months after intake (T4), cyclizine was quantified in five out of eight participants (ID3, ID5, ID6, ID8, ID10). In most cases, the maximum concentrations found in these samples were near LLOQ (1 pg/mg), except for the black‐haired participant, in which the cyclizine concentration was found to be 33 pg/mg. Diphenhydramine and N‐demethyldiphenhydramine

Over the 2‐month period (1–3 months after intake), the cumulated concentration of diphenhydramine remained unchanged for one participant (ID5) and, for the rest of the participants, decreased by 12%–72%. Over the 4‐month period (1–5 months after intake), the cumulated concentration decreased by 24%–94% and over the 11‐month period (1–12 months after intake) by 87%–100%. The cumulated concentration of the metabolite N‐demethyldiphenhydramine slightly increased (by 2%) in the hair of one participant (ID2) over the 2‐month period, whereas in the hair of the rest of the participants, the concentration over the 2‐month period decreased by 6%–76%, over the 4‐month period by 33%–91%, and over the 11‐month period by 82%–100%. At the end of the study (12 months after intake), diphenhydramine and its metabolite could only be quantified in participants with dark brown hair (ID8) and black hair (ID5). The cumulated concentrations of diphenhydramine were 13% and 12% and the cumulated concentrations of the metabolite were 18% and 13% of the cumulated concentrations measured 1 month after intake for these participants.

Cyclizine and Norcyclizine

Over the 2‐month period, the cumulated concentration of cyclizine increased in the hair of three participants (ID5, ID6, ID8) by 29%, 7%, and 1%, respectively. For the rest of the participants, the cumulated concentration decreased by 23%–66%. Over the 4‐month period, the cumulated concentration of cyclizine increased by 17% in the hair of one participant (ID5), whereas in the hair of the rest of the participants, it decreased by 41%–90%. Over the 11‐month period, the cumulated concentration of cyclizine decreased by 80%–100%. The cumulated concentration of the metabolite norcyclizine increased in the hair of three participants (ID4, ID5, ID6) by 5%, 22%, and 2%, respectively, over the 2‐month period. For one participant, the cumulated concentration of norcyclizine remained unchanged (ID2), whereas for the rest of the participants, it decreased by 14%–55%. Over the 4‐month period, the cumulated concentration of norcyclizine increased by 15% in the hair of one participant (ID5), whereas in the hair of the remaining participants, it decreased by 26%–87%. Over the 11‐month period, the cumulated concentration of norcyclizine decreased in the hair of all participants by 66%–100%. At the end of the study (12 months after intake), the highest cumulated concentrations of cyclizine and its metabolite were measured in participants with dark brown hair (ID8) and black hair (ID5). The cumulated concentrations of cyclizine were 18% and 20%, and the cumulated concentrations of the metabolite were 21% and 34% of the cumulated concentrations measured 1 month after intake for these participants.

The Effects of Thermal Hair Straightening on Antihistamine Concentration in Hair

The effect of a single thermal hair straightening treatment on drug concentration was evaluated by comparing the cumulated concentrations determined in unstraightened and straightened hair samples. Using a paired‐sample t‐test at a 0.95 confidence level, the differences between the means of the populations (straightened and unstraightened hair) for diphenhydramine, cyclizine, and their main metabolites were insignificant. p‐values were 0.21 for diphenhydramine, 0.63 for N‐demethyldiphenhydramine, 0.31 for cyclizine, and 0.49 for norcyclizine.

Discussion

Drug Concentrations in Hair

The positive findings in the hair samples collected before drug intake (T0) do not originate from the controlled administration but indicate a possible previous intake of diphenhydramine and/or cyclizine by some of the study participants. Overall, higher drug concentrations were found in S2 segments compared with S1 segments of the positive T0 hair samples, indicating a more distant drug intake. A possible explanation for the positive T0 hair samples could be that the information about antihistamine intake history provided by the study participants was inaccurate. The participants may not have always fully recalled what specific medicines they had taken and when, thus reporting incomplete information. In our opinion, this did not negatively impact the study results and conclusions.

The overall variation of the concentrations of antihistamines found in hair at different time points following a single dose was substantial. The concentrations determined over the entire study period spanned two orders of magnitude, ranging from the LLOQ (1 pg/mg) to several hundred picograms per milligram. Numerous factors influencing drug concentration in hair should be taken into account when interpreting hair drug levels for forensic purposes. One of the important factors is the hair characteristics, especially its pigmentation [3]. It is generally accepted that basic and lipophilic drugs have a higher affinity for the black pigment eumelanin due to the chemistry of its molecule [4, 38]. Consequently, a higher amount of the drug tends to be deposited into black and brown hair compared with white, blond, or red hair [3, 4, 38]. In the present study, the hair colors of the participants were mostly light or dark brown (75% of the study participants); one participant was black‐haired, and two participants were blonde. The highest maximum concentrations of both antihistamines and their metabolites were found in the black‐haired participant at all sampling time points. The second‐highest maximum concentrations were observed in participants with dark brown hair (ID6). On the contrary, the lowest maximum concentrations of both antihistamines and their metabolites were found in the participants with either blond or light brown hair. Both diphenhydramine and cyclizine are lipophilic weak bases that are partially protonated at physiological pH, which promotes their affinity for melanin and likely drives their deposition into hair. Our results were mostly consistent with this.

Another factor influencing the incorporation rate of a drug in hair, and thus the total amount of drug deposited in hair, is the pharmacokinetics of the drug. Drugs with longer elimination half‐lives are more concentrated in the tissues and/or spend more time in the bloodstream, making them more available for deposition in the hair matrix [4, 39]. Consequently, the differences in elimination half‐life between individuals can result in different drug concentrations in hair. Following oral administration, the maximum plasma concentration of diphenhydramine is achieved approximately 2 h after administration, and the elimination half‐life is 4–13 h, depending, among other factors, on the age of the individual [40, 41, 42]. After oral administration of a 50 mg dose of cyclizine hydrochloride, the maximum plasma concentration was found to be about 4 h, and the elimination half‐life was 24 h [43].

The median maximum concentration of cyclizine found at sampling time points up to 5 months after intake was, on average, 95% higher compared with the median maximum concentration of diphenhydramine. Some possible explanations for this could be that the ingested dose of cyclizine was 76% higher than that of diphenhydramine, but also the fact that the elimination half‐life of cyclizine in adults is reported to be approximately 1–1.5× longer than that of diphenhydramine.

In addition, the wash‐out effect is one of the significant factors that influence the drug concentration in hair and its decline over time. The extent of the wash‐out effect depends on the individual's hair hygiene, such as washing frequency and use of hair products, as some may be more likely to extract the drug from the hair matrix than others. Moreover, common hair treatments, such as hair dyeing, bleaching, or straightening, have been proven to decrease the amount of some drugs in hair [3]. However, for the interpretation of the results of this dose study, a potential effect of such treatments could be excluded, as the study participants had been instructed to refrain from doing them. Furthermore, a possible chemical modification or degradation of the drug molecule can occur due to physicochemical factors, such as exposure to UV irradiation from the sun, depending on the physicochemical stability of the drug [4, 7].

Metabolite‐to‐Parent Drug Ratio

Orally administered diphenhydramine undergoes first‐pass metabolism, and the bioavailability is about 70% [41]. Both diphenhydramine and cyclizine are metabolized primarily by N‐demethylation to N‐demethyldiphenhydramine and norcyclizine (N‐demethylcyclizine), respectively. Only around 2% or less is excreted in urine as an unchanged drug [40, 41, 42, 43]. In forensic investigations, a drug's metabolite can be used as an additional biomarker to confirm drug intake. Moreover, the determination of the metabolite‐to‐parent drug ratio can be useful for distinguishing direct drug intake from external contamination and ruling out passive exposure [44]. In the present study, the median metabolite‐to‐parent drug ratio was 0.47 for diphenhydramine and 0.38 for cyclizine, with inter‐individual variation of about 40%. The intra‐individual variation of the metabolite‐to‐parent drug ratio for both antihistamines was below 20% for most of the participants. The metabolite‐to‐parent drug ratio can be influenced by the composition of the hair matrix (melanin content) or the diffusion of the parent drug and/or the metabolite across the hair. Our results indicate that diphenhydramine either underwent demethylation to a greater extent than cyclizine, or the metabolite was incorporated into hair more readily than that of cyclizine. Both metabolites were present and quantifiable in all relevant segments up to 5 months post‐intake and could therefore be used as additional markers of diphenhydramine and cyclizine consumption in DFC cases.

Comparison to Published Literature

Only a few DFC cases [8, 21, 22, 45] and controlled dose studies [25, 26, 46] involving diphenhydramine have been published up to this day. An overview of data available in the literature is given in Table 5. A case of repetitive sedation of a 9‐year‐old girl was reported by Pascal Kinz et al. [21], where a five‐centimeter proximal hair sample was collected approximately 7 weeks after the alleged incident. The hair was brown and was cut into three segments of 0–1, 1–3, and 3–5 cm, and the concentrations determined were 37, 39, and 33 pg/mg, respectively. These values of diphenhydramine are within the range of concentrations that we found in 1‐cm segments 1 and 3 months after intake. However, it is not trivial to compare the concentrations determined by different laboratories in this way. Different laboratories often use different sample preparation methodologies, which can be the source of significant inter‐laboratory variation [5]. In this case [21], the hair sample was cut, whereas in our study, the hair was pulverized, possibly yielding higher extraction recovery. Furthermore, the time delay between the incident and hair sample collection in the case was reported to be 7 weeks, whereas in our study, the hair samples were collected 4 and 12 weeks after diphenhydramine intake. In addition, as mentioned by the authors, the case represented repetitive administration, whereas in our controlled study, only a single dose was administered.

Another DFC case [22], in which an 81‐year‐old woman had been repetitively sedated, was reported by the same authors. The analysis of a 3‐cm‐long white hair sample revealed the presence of diphenhydramine at a concentration of 683 pg/mg. Considering the white hair of the victim, this concentration appears to be very high and likely corresponds to a high dose or repetitive intake of diphenhydramine, which was eventually concluded in this case. In our study, such high levels were only found in a black‐haired participant in the proximal segment 1 month after the intake of a single dose of 25 mg of diphenhydramine.

Another two DFC cases, in which diphenhydramine was detected in the victims' hair, were reported by Wang et al. [8]. The first case involved a girl with blond hair. Five segments of different lengths were analyzed (S1: 0–1.5 cm, S2: 1.5–3.5 cm, S3: 3.5–6.0 cm, S4: 6.0–8.0 cm, and S5: 8.0–10.0 cm), and diphenhydramine was detected in Segments S3 and S4, with concentrations being 25 and 10 pg/mg. However, according to the authors, S2 was the segment relevant for the period of the incident. The measured values seem comparable to the concentrations we found in 1‐cm segments 3 months after diphenhydramine intake, taking into account hair color and a possible dilution by drug‐free hair because they used 2‐cm‐ and 2.5‐cm‐long segments. The second reported case was classified as a drug‐facilitated sexual assault and concerned an early‐middle‐aged woman. The victim's hair was brown, reported to have been straightened daily, and dyed black every 2 or 3 weeks. The hair sample was cut into three segments (S1: 0–3.0 cm, S2: 3–5.5 cm, and S3: 5.5–7.5 cm), and diphenhydramine was solely detected in the relevant segment (S2) at 40 pg/mg. Considering the hair color and the longer segment, this concentration falls within the range of our results obtained 1 or 3 months after intake of a single dose of diphenhydramine.

In another report [45] of two fatalities, diphenhydramine intake was known from the case history. Concentrations determined after alkaline hydrolysis and head‐space–solid‐phase‐microextraction gas‐chromatography–mass‐spectrometry technique were 2000 and 3700 pg/mg. These concentrations are much higher compared with those obtained in our study. Both the hair sample preparation methodology and the analytical technique used were different from those used by us. Moreover, no further information about the circumstances of the case, time delay between the incident and hair sample collection, segment length or location, hair color, or frequency of diphenhydramine intake, was provided in the publication. It is therefore irrelevant to make a more detailed comparison of the determined concentrations.

A small study was conducted by Kuwayama et al. [26], where they investigated whether the day of diphenhydramine intake could be accurately determined using an internal temporal marker and micro‐segmental hair analysis. A single oral dose of 8.6 mg of diphenhydramine hydrochloride was ingested by three dark‐haired volunteers, and individual hair strands were collected 36–56.5 days after ingestion. A 3.84‐cm‐long hair strand sample was micro‐segmented into 96 segments of 0.4 mm length, using a tissue slicer. The maximum concentrations of diphenhydramine found in each hair strand were in the range of 2800–22,600 pg/mg. These concentrations are substantially higher than those measured in our single‐dose study, where a dose more than three times higher was administered. An explanation for such high concentrations could be that they used very short segments of a single black hair strand, where less dilution by the surrounding drug‐free hair usually occurs. A study from the same group, investigating the distribution profiles of diphenhydramine in different hair specimens after different administration routes, was published [46]. Similarly to the previously mentioned study, two volunteers ingested a single oral dose of 8.6 mg of diphenhydramine hydrochloride. Several weeks after drug ingestion, a few individual hair strands were collected, and a micro‐segmental hair analysis was performed using 0.4‐mm‐long segments. The maximum concentrations of diphenhydramine found in each hair strand ranged from 59 to 2534 pg/mg.

Chèze and Gaulier published internal reference data for a single dose of diphenhydramine used by their laboratory [25]. In a 2‐cm‐long hair segment, diphenhydramine concentrations of 34 and 396 pg/mg were found following a single dose of 90 and 180 mg of an unspecified medicine, respectively. In general, these values are within the concentration range determined in the present study by us. The authors, however, do not provide any further details, such as the subjects' hair color or the time delay between the drug intake and hair sample collection.

To our knowledge, no published data are available on cyclizine use in DFCs or on controlled‐dose studies involving oral administration followed by hair analysis of this sedative antihistamine.

Temporal Drug Patterns

Multiple sampling time points combined with extensive 1‐cm segmentation allowed tracking how drug‐positive hair segments grew outward from the scalp over time. Some of the factors that influence the temporal drug pattern in hair include the length of the analyzed segment, the frequency of drug intake, the time delay between drug intake and sample collection, the hair growth rate, the physiology of the hair growth cycle, hair sample misalignment, or contributions from sweat and sebum. In this study, a possible deposition of diphenhydramine or cyclizine into hair via sweat or sebum was observed in 83% of participants, especially at earlier sampling points. One month post‐intake, a probable deposition of diphenhydramine was seen in Segments S3 and S4 of all participants except for ID05, ID7, ID11, and ID12, as shown in Figures S2–S4 in Section S3 of the Supporting Information. Similarly, the possible deposition of cyclizine via sweat appeared in these segments of all participants except for ID3 and ID11. In Segments S3 and S4 of participants ID7 and ID12, this pattern was present for cyclizine but not for diphenhydramine. This may be due to generally lower hair concentrations of diphenhydramine compared with cyclizine measured in this study. Only participants ID3 and ID11 showed no signs of this contribution from sweat or sebum for either of the antihistamines in the hair samples collected at T1. Overall, the antihistamine concentrations in Segments S3 and S4 at sampling time point T1 ranged from 0.6%–10% of the maximum concentration among the involved participants, and similar temporal patterns were observed for both antihistamines, as shown for Participant ID4 in Figure 3.

In the case of single‐time drug exposure, the drug concentration determined in a hair segment can be significantly influenced by the length of the analyzed segment [10, 17]. The longer the segment, the lower the determined concentration, as the deposited drug gets diluted by the drug‐free hair [17]. Furthermore, the different growth phases of individual hair strands at different periods, as well as the radial and axial diffusion of the drug across the hair shaft, can result in the drug being distributed over several neighboring segments [47] and thus diluted. This effect is typically observed with increasing time since drug intake and was seen for both diphenhydramine and cyclizine at sampling time points T3 and T4 for Participant ID5 in Figures 1 and 2.

The measured hair growth rate of some participants was substantially different from the generally accepted average rate of 1 cm per month. Consequently, using 1‐cm‐long hair segments sometimes resulted in cutting in the middle of the period of drug ingestion, which was manifested by nearly equal distribution of the drug between the maximum‐concentration segment and one of its neighboring segments. This pattern can be observed for diphenhydramine in Figure 1 (Participant ID7, sampling time point T2, Segments S3 and S4) or for cyclizine in Figure 2 (Participant ID5, sampling time point T3, Segments S6 and S7).

In general, the maximum concentrations of antihistamines were found in the proximal segment (0–1.0 cm) of the T1 sample, despite the variations in the hair growth rate of the participants. However, at later sampling points, the influence of the hair growth rate became apparent, causing more variation in the location of the maximum‐concentration segment among participants. Moreover, discrepancies between the expected location of the maximum‐concentration hair segment and the actual location of the maximum‐concentration segment were observed. These discrepancies were likely caused by a combination of factors such as inaccurate measurement of the participants' hair growth rate, different stages of the hair growth cycle of the individual hair strands of the participants, misalignment of the hair sample, or, in some cases, by graying hair.

Three hair samples were collected outside the planned sampling schedule (ID8—T3 sample collected 11 days late, ID11—T3 sample collected 11 days earlier, and ID12—T1 sample collected 8 days late), and the observed temporal patterns of the antihistamines were consistent with these deviations.

Change in Drug Concentration in Hair Over Time

Changes in drug concentration over time varied significantly between participants, likely due to differing extents of the wash‐out effect associated with inter‐individual variabilities in hair hygiene, hair characteristics, and exposure to physicochemical factors from the environment. In the hair of some participants (ID5, ID6, ID8), no decrease in the cumulated concentration of cyclizine and norcyclizine was observed within the period from 1 to 3 months, and in the case of ID5, even from 1 to 5 months after intake. Instead, the cumulated concentration remained the same, or an increase was observed. This could be explained by the fact that, in these participants, even the more distal segments still contained analyte concentrations above the LLOQ at the concerned sampling time points. Therefore, if additional hair segments (e.g., S5 and S6 at T1) had been analyzed, the calculated cumulative concentration, and hence its change over time, might have been different. Another possible explanation could be that at the first sampling point, a part of the hair with the deposited drug had not yet emerged from the scalp, and therefore, it could not have been sampled. Consequently, the cumulated concentration 1 month after intake was lower than the cumulated concentration measured 3 and 5 months after intake.

For most participants, the cumulated concentration of diphenhydramine, cyclizine, and their main metabolites decreased throughout the entire study period from 1 to 5 (12) months following a single dose. Based on both the maximum concentrations and the cumulated concentrations, it could be concluded that the optimal time for sample collection in a DFC case would be 1–3 months after drug intake. Within this period, the hair levels of antihistamines following a single dose intake could be measured with adequate sensitivity in all participants. Nevertheless, hair samples collected at later time points may reveal a negative (or low‐concentration) segment adjacent to the segment corresponding to the incident period, thereby facilitating interpretation.

The Effects of One‐Time Thermal Hair Straightening on the Antihistamine Concentrations

It is generally accepted that hair treatments such as straightening, dyeing, and bleaching can significantly affect the amount of the drug deposited in the hair [6]. In contrast, the results of our experiment showed there was no significant decrease in the concentration of diphenhydramine, cyclizine, and their main metabolites after a single thermal hair straightening treatment. However, because we only investigated single‐time thermal hair straightening at one temperature, using one type of thermal hair straightener, the results of the experiment cannot be generalized to repeated thermal hair straightening treatments.

Conclusions

The applied LC–MS/MS method showed adequate sensitivity for measuring a single oral dose of diphenhydramine and cyclizine in head hair over multiple time points up to 1 year after controlled administration. Diphenhydramine, cyclizine, and their main metabolites, N‐demethyldiphenhydramine and norcyclizine, were quantifiable in the hair of all study participants for up to 5 months after intake. Twelve months after intake, diphenhydramine was quantified in 25%, whereas cyclizine was quantified in 62.5% of the available hair samples. One‐centimeter segmental hair analysis allowed the assessment of the temporal pattern of antihistamines and revealed possible contributions from sweat and/or sebum. A single thermal hair straightening treatment had no significant impact on the concentrations of the antihistamines.

The study results provided indicative concentration ranges for diphenhydramine and cyclizine in head hair following a single oral dose. The overall maximum concentration range determined within 5 months after intake was 1.7–610 pg/mg for diphenhydramine, 1.0–310 pg/mg for N‐demethyldiphenhydramine, 5.6–590 pg/mg for cyclizine, and 1.2–180 pg/mg for norcyclizine. The median decrease in concentration over 2, 4, and 11 months was 43%, 70%, and 100% for diphenhydramine, and 40%, 64%, and 98% for cyclizine.
