SemanticRail3D - A Mobile LiDAR Benchmark for Semantic and Instance Segmentation of Railway Corridors
Authors: Arshia Ghasemlou, Mario Soilán, Belén Riveiro
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractMonitoring and maintaining railway corridors requires accurate, high-resolution spatial data to ensure operational safety and efficiency. However, data-driven railway infrastructure assessment has been limited by the scarcity of large-scale, finely annotated 3D benchmarks. To address this gap we first introduce SemanticRail3D, a mobile-LiDAR dataset of 438 high-resolution point clouds (approximately 2.8 billion points) in 200 m segments, annotated via a heuristic rule-based segmentation method into 12 semantic classes and grouped into instance labels, with per-point intensity information. Building on this foundation, we present SemanticRail3D-V2, featuring a Machine Learning (ML)-ready preprocessing pipeline that aligns and sections raw scans into uniform blocks, and a novel evaluation protocol combining metric-based anomaly detection with a probabilistic validity analysis of class distributions and spatial relationships. Railway domain experts then reviewed each block to remove low-quality scans and divide the dataset into five training shards-selected for annotation accuracy and complexity-plus separate validation and held-out test sets. The SemanticRail3D dataset, together with the V2 enhancements, offers a rigorously curated, richly annotated benchmark for semantic and instance segmentation in railway environments, supporting research in condition monitoring and asset management.Subject terms: Civil engineering, Computational science

Background & Summary

Railway infrastructure is a vital backbone of the transportation system, enabling large-scale, continuous movement of people and goods. With 200,947 kilometers of track spanning the continent1, the European rail network facilitated a record 429 billion passenger-kilometres and served 8 billion passengers in 20232. Rail freight transport remains equally significant, with an estimated 403 billion tonne-kilometres moved across the EU in 20223. This sustained intensity of use, places enormous demands on trackside assets, making continuous monitoring and timely maintenance essential. In 2022 alone, the network recorded 1,567 significant accidents and 841 fatalities, pointing to the real-world risks posed by asset degradation, undetected failures, or inspection delays4. Maintaining and upgrading Europe’s railway infrastructure requires substantial public and private investment. Recent estimates indicate that infrastructure managers across Europe collectively spend between €15 billion and €25 billion annually on maintenance and renewal activities5. Beyond its logistical significance, rail transport offers considerable environmental advantages. According to the International Energy Agency, rail emissions per passenger-kilometre are approximately one-fifth those of air travel, and electrified railways powered by renewable energy sources can further reduce emissions. In the EU, rail accounts for only 0.4% of transport-related greenhouse gas emissions, despite handling a substantial share of passenger and freight transport6–8.

Economic analyses of risk-based and condition-based maintenance programs-often leveraging automated, LiDAR-enabled inspection workflows-show that performance-based maintenance can cut maintenance costs by up to 25%, risk-based maintenance yields around 20% savings, and fully integrated risk-based asset-management processes drive 20-30% overall cost reductions, a 30% drop in system failures and a 20-25% increase in asset availability9,10. Continuous, cloud-hosted monitoring and digital-twin integration then extend these gains by enabling real-time trend analysis, streamlined regulatory reporting and data-driven life-cycle optimization-letting infrastructure managers prioritize interventions, lengthen asset service life and further minimize downtime and total expenditure.

These realities highlight the need for accurate, high-resolution data about rail corridors-an area where LiDAR and 3D semantic analysis tools can offer transformative improvements in both efficiency and safety-driven decision making.

Point cloud representations of railway corridors provide rich, high-fidelity 3D data at millimeter-to-centimeter resolution, underpinning advanced semantic and instance segmentation techniques that automate the extraction and classification of key infrastructure components. Semantic segmentation assigns each point a class label-distinguishing items like rails, sleepers, ballast, catenary masts, vegetation and other track-side elements-while instance segmentation goes a step further, separating individual objects of the same class (for example isolating each sleeper or signal post). State-of-the-art deep-learning architectures-PointNet11, KPConv12, Superpoint Graphs13, Point Transformer14,15, and 3D Swin Transformers16-exploit varying receptive fields, dynamic graph convolutions, superpoint clustering, and self-attention across local and global contexts to achieve high classification accuracies on benchmark datasets17. Comparative studies have further emphasized the challenges of class imbalance and the influence of training pipeline design in railway-specific segmentation tasks18. By automating measurement of wear (e.g., rail profiles, sleeper spacing), early detection of defects (cracks, misalignment) and identification of encroaching vegetation or debris, these methods dramatically reduce reliance on labor-intensive manual inspections and time-consuming surveys, while enabling predictive maintenance strategies that preempt failures before they escalate into safety-critical incidents. Integrating segmentation-derived metrics into asset-management platforms facilitates continuous, large-scale monitoring-driving down inspection costs and network downtime while markedly enhancing overall reliability and safety19.

Embedding these segmentation outputs into maintenance and asset management systems drives a shift from calendar based to condition based and predictive maintenance. Methods such as FarNet20 have demonstrated long range track segmentation enabling analyses across continuous multi kilometer corridors, while specialized applications in catenary system detection21, automated rail track extraction22, and large scale corridor modeling from airborne and mobile scanning23 showcase the versatility of point cloud driven workflows. Case studies in data integration24 highlight the incorporation of heterogeneous data (IMU, GNSS, imagery) to enrich segmentation performance.

Over the past few years, publicly available railway point cloud datasets have proliferated, offering diverse geographic coverage, sensor configurations, and annotation schemes. Among them is the Railway Catenary Arches Dataset, released in 2022, which comprises fifteen high resolution static terrestrial laser scans covering approximately 0.8 km of Dutch railway catenary arches and totaling 55.4 million points. The point clouds were acquired with a Trimble TX8 laser scanner operating in Level 2 mode-providing a point spacing of 11.3 mm at 30 m range-and referenced in the national Rijksdriehoeksstelsel coordinate system. This dataset provides fourteen semantic classes-top bar, pole, drop post, top tie, bracket, pole foundation, steady arm, contact wire, stitch wire, wheel tension device, dropper, messenger wire support, insulator, and unlabelled-and is distributed under a CC BY 4.0 license via 4TU Research Data25.

Building on this foundation, the WHU Railway3D dataset was introduced in 2024 as a large scale benchmark covering approximately 30 km of railway (10 km each in urban, rural, and plateau environments) in China. Data were collected using three train mounted mobile mapping platforms-an Optech Lynx Mobile Mapper in urban scenes, a HiScan Z MLS in rural scenes, and a 32 line rMMS system in plateau scenes-recording multi return LiDAR, reflected intensity, scan angle, and per point return counts. WHU Railway3D contains roughly 4.6 billion annotated points across eleven semantic classes (Rails, Track bed, Masts, Support devices, Overhead lines, Fences, Poles, Vegetation, Buildings, Ground, and Others). Researchers can obtain the full dataset from the project’s GitHub repository26.

In 2024, Jiang et al. introduced RailPC, a large scale railway point cloud dataset covering a total of 25 km of track in China-10 km along an urban corridor in Nanjing and 15 km on a mountain line near Chengdu. Acquired in July 2019 by a HiScan C mobile laser scanner capable of one million points per second (360∘ FOV, 0.5-119 m range, 2 mm accuracy), the dataset comprises approximately 3 billion points with an average density of about 120 points/m2. Sixteen fine grained semantic classes-rail track; track bed; ground; roadside; catenary; pull wire; guardrail; vegetation; support structure; pole; transmission tower; stair; tunnel; building; concrete; and other-were annotated via a weakly supervised network (SQN) followed by manual refinement. RailPC is publicly available27.

The Rail3D dataset published in 2024 is a multi context 3D point cloud collection for railway semantic segmentation, comprising three subsets from Hungary, France, and Belgium. In total it covers 5 827 m of track-2 140 m in Hungary, 1 600 m in France, and 2 087 m in Belgium-amounting to 288 million points. The Hungarian portion was acquired with a Riegl VMX 450 mobile LiDAR (1.1 M pts/s, 3 mm average range accuracy, EPSG:23700), the Belgian with a Z+F 9012 push broom LiDAR (LAS, Lambert Belge 72), and the French with an SNCF rail borne LiDAR system (compressed LAZ, RGF93 CC44/NGF IGN69). Each point carries X, Y, Z, intensity and-where available-RGB attributes. All 288 M points have been manually annotated into nine universal classes: Ground, Vegetation, Rail, Pole, Wire, Signaling, Fence, Installation, and Building. Interested researchers can download the French and Hungarian subsets under open licenses via the authors’ GitHub repository; access to the Belgian data is by request to Infrabel under a confidentiality agreement28.

The OSDaR23 dataset (2023) supplements LiDAR point clouds with synchronized RGB and infrared imagery, 2D radar scans, IMU measurements, and GNSS (GPS) data-all recorded at 10 Hz on Hamburg’s mainline using a Velodyne HDL 64E LiDAR. It comprises 1,534 LiDAR frames and a total of 204 091 annotation instances, OSDaR23 defines twenty object classes across four categories. Dynamic objects include person, crowd, train, wagons, bicycle, group of bicycles, motorcycle, road vehicle, animal, group of animals, wheelchair, and drag shoe; railway specific assets comprise track, switch, and transition; static infrastructure covers catenary pole, signal pole, signal, signal bridge, and buffer stop; and special events are labeled as flame and smoke. All annotations-polylines, polygons, rectangles, and cuboids-adhere to the ASAM OpenLABEL format, and the full dataset is publicly accessible via the FID Move portal29.

Finally, RailCloud HdF dataset was introduced at VISAPP 2024 as a large open railway LiDAR collection to date, covering 267.5 km of eight lines in the Hauts de France region with roughly 8.06 billion points. Data were acquired using dual RIEGL VUX 1HA scanners, IGI MEMS INS, and Septentrio GNSS in round trip mode (7 mm spacing). Nine semantic labels-including eight specific infrastructure categories (rails, sleepers, catenary structures, vegetation, buildings, signs, level crossing gates) plus an unclassified catch all-are provided. RailCloud HdF is freely available under a CC BY NC ND 4.0 license30.

Collectively, these six public datasets-Catenary Arches (2022), WHU Railway3D (2024), RailPC (2024), Rail3D (2024), RailCloud HdF (2024), and OSDaR23 (2023)-provide a comprehensive foundation for advancing 3D perception, semantic segmentation, and autonomous operation technologies in railway environments. Complementary resources further expand the available modalities for rail perception: FRSign (2020) offers over 100,000 camera images of French railway traffic lights annotated for signal-state detection and classification, enabling research on vision-based train signalling recognition31, while UA_L-DoTT (2022) contributes a large-scale, multimodal dataset of synchronized LiDAR point clouds and camera imagery of trains and trucks collected across multiple sites, facilitating cross-domain learning between rail and road transport systems32. In addition, several proprietary datasets have been described in the literature, including a robotic MLS change detection collection gathered with a six wheeled inspection platform for rail safety and security33, a 3000 pts/m2 density, 193 km/h high speed MLS dataset from China for large scale track and catenary analysis34, a bolt point cloud segmentation and measurement set based on DBSCAN clustering in laboratory settings35, and also more railway specific point cloud collections have been identified in a systematic review19, although these remain unavailable for public use.

To address the need for richly detailed, railway-centric point-cloud benchmarks, we introduce the SemanticRail3D dataset, specifically tailored to the specialized equipment and structures found in rail environments. Spanning 438 georeferenced 200 m blocks over a 90 km survey (≈2.8 billion points), it delivers an average density of 980 pts/m2 and 5 mm range precision, enabling fine-grained assessment of rails, catenaries, supports, and ancillary hardware. Unlike more general-purpose collections, SemanticRail3D offers instance segmentation labels and per-track (rail-line encoding) annotations, making automated equipment identification and monitoring straightforward. Its public availability, vast spatial coverage, and focus on railway-specific infrastructure are rare among point-cloud datasets, providing a robust foundation for developing and benchmarking tools for equipment inspection, maintenance planning, and autonomous operation. The initial semantic labels in our pipeline are generated via the heuristic workflow of Lamas et al., which divides the survey into manageable blocks, voxelizes each, and applies geometry- and context-based rules to extract key elements-yielding a high-recall segmentation that anchors all subsequent refinement36.

In this Data Descriptor we introduce both the SemanticRail3D dataset and an enhanced version, SemanticRail3D V2, a large scale benchmark tailored for training deep learning models in both semantic and instance segmentation of railway environment point clouds. Compared to existing point-cloud repositories SemanticRail3D is explicitly curated to capture and annotate a wide variety of railway-specific equipment. In addition to its primary applications, this dataset is well suited for advanced tasks such as anomaly and defect detection on the rail itself, hazard and obstacle identification both on and beside the track, and environmental monitoring along the railway corridor. This emphasis on rail assets ensures that our dataset provides dense, fine-grained labels for objects that are critical to railway operation and maintenance, making it uniquely suited for tasks such as automated asset inspection, predictive maintenance scheduling, and safety-oriented monitoring along the rail corridor. Heuristic labeling

The dataset’s semantic and instance labels were generated by first partitioning each raw LiDAR sweep into locally aligned, low curvature sections of roughly 200 m. After slicing along the vehicle trajectory, each segment is re oriented via a modified PCA so its forward axis lies in the horizontal plane, preventing track straightening distortions. Points are then filtered to a fixed lateral window around the track, and each section is voxelized at 0.1 m spatial resolution. This voxel grid not only supports efficient neighborhood queries but also encodes local intensity and geometry statistics needed for subsequent feature extraction.

Semantic labeling proceeds by sequentially extracting classes from the most dominant (rails, ground) to more peripheral elements (masts, wires, droppers, signs). Rails and sleepers are identified by re voxelizing at coarser resolution, selecting voxels with no vertical neighbors, and clustering via DBSCAN (Density-Based Spatial Clustering of Applications with Noise) beneath the trajectory slice. Masts are detected next by local PCA on candidate voxels-retaining those whose principal direction is nearly vertical-and clustering to match expected mast geometry, with adjacent cantilevers attached. Wire instances (contact and catenary) are then traced by linear PCA across sections, region growing along seed cross sections, and classifying segments based on their height profiles. Droppers and isolated signs are finally extracted by spatial joins within wire-mast bounding boxes, flood fill clustering, and template matching against known CAD shapes.

Once all classes are identified, instance continuity is enforced by matching new segments to previous ones based on spatial proximity and voxel overlap (for continuous objects like wires and rails) or by ensuring non overlap (for point like objects such as masts and signs). Dependent objects (droppers under specific wires, rails paired to gauge distance) are linked hierarchically. Finally, every labeled voxel is mapped back to its original point index, yielding per point semantic class IDs and unique instance IDs for each physical object in the scene. • Stage 1 - Initial expert review

For every point cloud in the SemanticRail3D dataset, a 3D illustration was rendered in which each point cloud was colorized using the distinctive palette assigned to its semantic classes. A domain expert then reviewed each illustration, inspecting rails for continuity and correct segmentation, wires for proper separation of catenary, contact, droppers, and other wires, supports for accurately placed and aligned masts with their cantilevers, and ancillary objects such as traffic lights, signs, and marks for correct location and labeling, while also flagging any noise or spurious points.

• Stage 4 - Final Expert Review

Following Stage 1’s comprehensive review of entire point clouds and the subsequent extraction of detailed geometric and semantic features in Stage 3, a more precise evaluation was performed on segmented sections of each point cloud. This evaluation took into account insights and anomalies identified from the extracted features and prior assessments.

This refined analysis highlighted the relative nature of the extracted features, effectively identifying labeling errors, misclassifications, and noise within individual segments. Such insights provided valuable assistance to expert evaluators by pinpointing problematic areas, thereby enhancing the reliability and accuracy of their evaluations.

Observations from this stage confirmed that clouds labeled using heuristic methods often contained minor imperfections, particularly in complex environments. Removing these slightly flawed datasets could negatively impact the generalization capability of trained models. Consequently, point clouds with minor errors were retained within the training set to enhance the model’s ability to learn diverse and realistic features. Conversely, clean scans were reserved exclusively for validation and testing purposes, thereby ensuring the trained models’ performance and reliability in practical, real-world scenarios. Specifically, approximately 15% of all high-quality scans were allocated to the validation set and another 15% to the test set. The remaining 70% of the data-comprising both high-quality and minor-error segments-was used for training. To maintain balanced file sizes and consistent data loading during model training, the training subset was further divided into five non-overlapping shards of roughly equal volume.

We evaluated these features separately for each subset of the dataset (test, validation, and five training partitions). Statistical analyses and graphical representations of these metrics confirm the dataset’s overall high quality and consistency. Notably, the test and validation subsets, which were carefully selected to ensure robust and precise evaluation scenarios, demonstrate marginally superior results, reflecting the meticulous selection process aimed at enhancing dataset reliability. The slightly improved statistical outcomes observed in these subsets reinforce the effectiveness of our selection strategy. The detailed metrics presented in the subsequent sections provide clear evidence supporting the dataset’s suitability for reliable and accurate semantic segmentation applications, underscoring its value for rigorous machine learning experiments and real-world deployment in railway infrastructure monitoring and analysis.

By plotting the ECDFs of both linearity and gauge/wire height deviations, we not only validate the mean error summaries reported elsewhere, but also reveal subtle tails or outliers that could indicate data quality issues. This nonparametric, step-wise perspective provides highly intuitive benchmarks-across different data partitions-for demanding downstream tasks such as semantic segmentation and infrastructure monitoring.

Instance-Level Consistency Validation

In addition to the geometric and semantic analyses, a dedicated validation of the instance-level annotations was conducted to verify the coherence between the semantic and instance segmentation layers of the SemanticRail3D-V2 dataset. This analysis was designed to ensure that each object instance is correctly delineated, semantically unique, and spatially continuous.

For every instance (identified by a unique point_source_id), its semantic composition was examined to compute multiple diagnostic metrics. The semantic purity of an instance was defined as the fraction of its points belonging to the majority semantic label. A purity value of 1.0 indicates that all points within the instance belong to a single semantic category. The cross-class leakage rate quantified the percentage of instances containing points from more than one semantic class, thus identifying potential misassignments between semantic and instance labels. Across all evaluated instances, purity values of 1.0 were observed, resulting in a cross-class leakage rate of 0%, confirming perfect semantic consistency between the two annotation layers.

The label coverage metric was used to evaluate the completeness of the instance segmentation layer by measuring the proportion of points within each semantic class that were assigned valid instance identifiers. The results indicated 100% coverage for all annotated classes, except for the intentionally unclassified background (class 0), demonstrating that all semantic labels were properly represented at the instance level.

Spatial coherence was further assessed using a connected-component (CC) analysis, in which spatial clusters were identified within each semantic class through a radius-based neighborhood graph. Each connected component was then compared to the assigned instance identifiers to detect potential geometric fragmentation. The resulting over-segmentation rate, defined as the percentage of connected components divided across multiple instance IDs, was found to be 0% for all semantic categories. This confirms that the instance labels correspond to spatially continuous and geometrically coherent objects.

Overall, these results demonstrate that the instance annotations are semantically pure, spatially consistent, and fully aligned with the corresponding semantic labels. The instance segmentation layer of SemanticRail3D-V2 is therefore validated as a reliable and structurally coherent representation, ensuring that each object can be unambiguously identified and utilized in downstream instance- or panoptic-segmentation research.
