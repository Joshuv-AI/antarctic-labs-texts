Breaking voltage-controlled magnetic anisotropy–magnetoresistance trade-off for ultralow-energy switching in magnetic tunnel junctions
Authors: Yu Zhang, Meng Xu, Bowei Zhou, Carter Eckel, Supriya Ghosh, Hwanhui Yun, Ali Habiboglu, Deyuan Lyu, Daniel B. Gopman, Jian-Ping Wang, K. Andre Mkhoyan, Weigang Wang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Electric-field control of spin states offers a promising route to ultralow-power, ultrafast magnetization switching in spintronic devices such as magnetic tunnel junctions (MTJs). However, enhancing voltage-controlled magnetic anisotropy (VCMA) through interfacial engineering often disrupts coherent tunneling and suppresses tunnel magnetoresistance (TMR), limiting practical device performance. Here, we experimentally demonstrate highly energy-efficient, voltage-driven magnetization switching in MTJs enabled by a remote iridium (Ir) doping strategy that tailors the Ir concentration near the MgO/CoFeB interface in the free layer. By inserting an ultrathin Ir layer away from the tunnel barrier and leveraging controlled diffusion during annealing, we achieve sub-nanosecond switching with an energy of only 3.5 femtojoules per bit in nanoscale MTJs while maintaining a TMR ratio up to 160% after 400°C postannealing. These results resolve a long-standing VCMA-TMR trade-off and establish a scalable pathway toward ultralow-power nonvolatile spintronic devices under aggressive energy and scaling constraints.

INTRODUCTION

Magnetoresistance random-access memory (MRAM) offers key advantages over dynamic random-access memory, including nonvolatility, fast access speed, and high endurance (1–3). The magnetic tunnel junction (MTJ) (4–8) is the fundamental storage element in MRAM, consisting of two ferromagnetic (FM) layers separated by an ultrathin tunnel barrier, where information is encoded in the relative magnetization orientation through tunneling magnetoresistance (TMR), either parallel (P) as “0” or antiparallel (AP) as “1.” Early progress in MRAM relied on spin-transfer torque (STT) switching, which enables current-induced magnetization reversal (9, 10). However, in STT-MRAM, the write and read currents share the same tunnel barrier, leading to reliability concerns such as read disturbance and MgO degradation, particularly at sub–10-ns switching speeds (11). Spin-orbit torque (SOT) switching separates the write and read current paths in a three-terminal geometry (12–19), alleviating these issues; however, in conventional type-z and type-x SOT-MRAM geometries, the orthogonal configuration between spin polarization and equilibrium magnetization generally leads to higher switching current densities. As a result, despite extensive optimization, the switching energy is still rather high—the lowest switching energy obtained so far through current-induced switching mechanisms is 45 fJ, achieved in double spin-torque MTJs (20). To further reduce write energy, voltage-controlled magnetic anisotropy (VCMA) has emerged as a promising switching principle for MRAMs with perpendicular magnetic anisotropy (PMA) (21–43). In 3d transition-metal ferromagnets, magnetic anisotropy originates from the orbital occupation of itinerant d electrons near the Fermi level and can be modulated by an external electric field at the ferromagnet/dielectric interface, such as CoFeB/MgO, induced by either selective electron-hole doping in d-electron orbitals (22, 25, 33, 37) or electric-field–induced magnetic dipole moment (24). Owing to its purely electronic origin and the absence of ionic migration (44–46), the VCMA effect enables ultrafast voltage-driven magnetization switching using sub-nanosecond voltage pulses while substantially suppressing Joule heating compared with current-driven approaches. A write energy as low as 6 fJ has been reported in Ta/CoFeB/MgO-based VCMA-MTJs, albeit with a limited TMR ratio of up to 45% (42, 43). Achieving lower switching voltages requires further enhancement of the VCMA coefficient. First-principles calculations predict that a strong VCMA effect can be realized by introducing heavy-metal atoms at or near the ferromagnet/MgO interface (23, 33). Exceptionally large VCMA coefficients, up to 320 fJV−1 m−1, have been demonstrated in iridium (Ir)–doped Fe/MgO junctions, which have been attributed to enhanced modulation of the 5d majority spin states of interfacial and subinterfacial Ir atoms (26, 28). However, introducing impurity atoms directly at the tunnel barrier interface is well known to disrupt interfacial coherence and reduce TMR (47, 48), highlighting a fundamental trade-off between VCMA enhancement and readout performance. Whether such a large VCMA effect can be translated into genuinely energy-efficient, ultrafast switching in MTJ nanodevices has remained an open question, as dynamic switching in the sub-nanosecond regime has been largely unexplored.

In this work, we introduce a remote doping strategy in CoFeB/MgO junctions that enhances voltage-driven switching efficiency without degrading TMR, thereby overcoming the conventional VCMA-TMR trade-off at device level. Our approach is motivated by two key observations in VCMA-based MTJs: First, spin-dependent tunneling is a strictly interfacial phenomenon dominated by the first and second FM layers adjacent to the tunnel barrier (49) and therefore requires a well-preserved MgO/CoFeB interface. Second, the VCMA effect is more spatially nonlocal, as suggested by previous studies (26, 48, 50). Specifically, the role of the remotely doped Ir in enhancing VCMA is not simply associated with its large atomic number but rather with its strong spin-orbit coupling, spatially extended 5d orbitals, and induced magnetic moment when hybridized with neighboring Fe or CoFeB atoms. In this framework, the applied electric field modifies the orbital occupation and hybridization near the MgO/ferromagnet interface, while Ir atoms in interfacial and subinterfacial regions can further influence the anisotropy through their 5d majority spin states. First-principles calculations have shown that Ir atoms dispersed within the FM layer, including subinterfacial sites, can contribute substantially to the magnetic anisotropy energy and its electric-field modulation (26). This suggests that, unlike coherent tunneling—which is primarily determined by the atomic layers directly adjacent to the MgO barrier—the electric-field–induced modulation of magnetic anisotropy can involve hybridized electronic states extending over several atomic layers. This behavior is often referred to as a spatially nonlocal VCMA effect, where atomic species not strictly confined to the immediate interface can still influence voltage-driven anisotropy modulation. This distinction between the spatially extended nature of VCMA and the interfacial sensitivity of spin-dependent tunneling is crucial for understanding the challenges in device optimization. Therefore, by placing an ultrathin Ir layer away from the MgO interface and leveraging controlled diffusion during postgrowth thermal annealing (51), we achieve an optimal Ir distribution that strongly enhances voltage-driven magnetic anisotropy modulation while preserving coherent electron tunneling across the CoFeB/MgO interface. As a result, our MTJ nanopillars exhibit sub-nanosecond voltage-driven switching with an ultralow write energy of 3.5 fJ while maintaining a TMR ratio up to 160% after 400°C postannealing. Here rather than directly extracting the absolute VCMA coefficient in nanoscale MTJs, we focus on device-level switching behavior and its correlation with TMR and PMA.

RESULTS AND DISCUSSION

Device design and measurement configuration

The schematic structures of the four types of MTJs prepared in this study are shown in Fig. 1A. All MTJ films share the same buffer layers, Ta (5)/Ru(10)/Ta(5), and the same capping layers, Ta(7.5)/Ru(15), where the numbers in parentheses denote the layer thicknesses in nanometers. The core stack structures (from substrate to top) are as follows: Mo(1)/CoFeB(0.85)/MgO(2.5)/CoFeB(1.5)/Mo(1.5) (sample 1); Mo(1)/CoFeB(0.85)/MgO(2.5)/Ir(0.05/0.1/0.15)/CoFeB(1.5)/Mo(1.5) (sample 2); Mo(1)/CoFeB(0.85)/MgO(2.5)/CoFeB(1.5)/Ir(0.1)/Mo(1.5) (sample 3); and Mo(1)/CoFeB(0.85)/MgO(2.5)/CoFeB(0.75)/Ir(0.1)/CoFeB(0.75)/Mo(1.5) (sample 4). In all MTJs, the bottom CoFeB layer serves as the reference layer, whereas the top CoFeB layer serves as the free layer. The Mo layers in MTJ stacks were used to provide improved thermal robustness by suppressing detrimental diffusion from adjacent Ta/Ru-based layers during high-temperature annealing, thereby helping preserve PMA and TMR (52). The 2.5-nm MgO barrier was primarily chosen to increase the resistance-area (RA) product and suppress leakage current, while MgO thickness optimization remains an important consideration for practical VCMA-MTJ readout and circuit integration. After deposition, the MTJ films were patterned into 160-nm-diameter nanopillar devices using optical lithography, a nanosphere lithography (NSL) technique (53), and ion beam etching (IBE) (54, 55). The patterned MTJ nanopillars were then annealed in an Ar atmosphere at different temperatures ranging from 300° to 500°C. Additional details of the fabrication process can be found in our prior reports (52, 56, 57). More details of the measurement setup and device characterization are provided in Materials and Methods.

Effect of Ir insertion geometry and annealing on TMR and PMA

We first investigate the influence of Ir insertion on TMR and PMA of MTJs. All the magnetic fields applied in Fig. 2 were normal to the device plane. Figure 2A shows the TMR-H loops of MTJs with different positions of the Ir insertion layer after annealing at 300°C. Notably, there is no appreciable difference in TMR between sample 1 (without Ir insertion) and sample 3 (0.1-nm Ir inserted at the CoFeB/Mo interface). In contrast, the TMR decreases by more than 40% when a 0.1-nm Ir layer is inserted within the top CoFeB layer (sample 4). Figure 2B presents the TMR-H loops of MTJs with different Ir thicknesses inserted at the MgO/top-CoFeB interface (sample 2). The TMR monotonically decreases as the Ir thickness increases from 0 to 0.15 nm. The results in Fig. 2 (A and B) are summarized in the three-dimensional plot shown in Fig. 2C. On the one hand, the TMR decreases markedly with increasing Ir thickness when Ir is inserted at the MgO/CoFeB interface (samples 1 and 2), which can be attributed to the disruption of coherent tunneling channels (58). On the other hand, when the Ir insertion layer is gradually moved away from the MgO/CoFeB interface while keeping the Ir thickness fixed at 0.1 nm (samples 3 and 4), the TMR shows a clear recovery. A previous study reported that both VCMA effect and TMR can be enhanced by inserting a CoFe layer between an Ir-doped FM electrode and the MgO barrier (48). Although sample 4 has a similar multilayer configuration, it does not exhibit a comparably high TMR, as shown in Fig. 2 (A and C). Therefore, we focus on samples 1 to 3 in the following discussion.

An annealing temperature of 400°C is required for compatibility with the back-end-of-line (BEOL) process of complementary metal-oxide semiconductor (CMOS) technology (59). Accordingly, the effects of Ir insertion on coercivity (μ0Hc) (Fig. 2D) and TMR ratio (Fig. 2E) were examined as functions of annealing temperatures from 300° to 500°C. In the following analysis, only 0.1-nm Ir insertion is considered for sample 2, as this is the minimum thickness that can be reproducibly fabricated. As shown in Fig. 2D, the coercivity of all three samples decreases with increasing annealing temperature, indicating a progressive degradation of PMA due to the diffusion of heavy-metal atoms toward the MgO/CoFeB interface (60). At lower annealing temperatures (300° and 350°C), the coercivities of samples 1 and 3 are nearly identical and are approximately twice that of sample 2. This behavior indicates that the PMA of sample 2 is already noticeably degraded at relatively low annealing temperatures when Fe─O and/or Co─O bonds at the MgO/CoFeB interface are disrupted by Ir insertion (61, 62). The coercivity of sample 2 remains consistently smaller than those of samples 1 and 3 over the entire annealing temperature range. These results demonstrate that the PMA of sample 3 is substantially more thermally robust than that of sample 2 at 400°C, in agreement with previous reports (52). A more comprehensive evaluation of magnetic properties measured on blanket films by extracting the saturation magnetization (Ms), anisotropy field (μ0Hk), and effective PMA energy density (Keff) from M-H curves is provided in the Supplementary Materials (text S1, fig. S1 and table S1). Overall, although Ir insertion generally has a detrimental effect on PMA, this impact can be substantially mitigated when Ir is placed at the CoFeB/Mo interface (sample 3) rather than directly at the MgO/CoFeB interface (sample 2).

The evolution of the TMR ratio with annealing temperature is summarized in Fig. 2E. As the annealing temperature increases from 300° to 400°C, all three samples exhibit pronounced increases in TMR, which can be attributed to further crystallization of CoFeB at elevated temperatures (7, 63–66). Nevertheless, sample 2 consistently shows a lower TMR than samples 1 and 3 at all annealing temperatures. When the annealing temperature is further increased to 450° and 500°C, sample 2 rapidly loses its TMR owing to the collapse of PMA (65), consistent with the trend observed in Fig. 2D. In contrast, the TMR of sample 1 (sample 3) reaches a maximum value of 208% (165%) at 450°C (400°C). These TMR ratios are substantially higher than those reported previously for nanopillars in dynamic VCMA-induced switching studies (42, 43). The subsequent reduction in TMR for sample 1 (sample 3) after annealing at 500°C (450°C) is likely associated with the diffusion of Mo/Ta (Ir) atoms into the upper CoFeB and MgO layers (52). Together, these results indicate that the adverse impact of Ir on TMR can be largely alleviated by remote Ir doping at the CoFeB/Mo interface (sample 3), owing to the reduced Ir concentration near the MgO/CoFeB interface, particularly at annealing temperatures of 400°C and above.

Microstructural evidence for a graded Ir distribution

To elucidate the difference in Ir concentration near the MgO/CoFeB interface between sample 2 and sample 3, we carried out microstructural characterization and elemental composition analyses using scanning transmission electron microscopy (STEM). Figure 3A shows a high-angle annular dark-field (HAADF)–STEM cross-sectional image of sample 3 after postannealing at 400°C. A highly oriented polycrystalline MgO (001) layer is observed, which serves as a crystallographic template promoting the transformation of CoFe(B) into a bcc structure, a prerequisite for achieving high TMR (63, 67). Figure 3B presents the bright-field (BF)–STEM image together with the corresponding energy-dispersive x-ray (EDX) spectroscopy elemental maps. A well-defined interface between the Mo and Ir layers is observed, while the Ir layer partially overlaps with the adjacent CoFe(B) layer. This behavior is further quantified in the elemental composition line profile shown in Fig. 3C, where the peaks corresponding to Mo, Ir, Fe, and Mg are spatially well separated. More specifically, the distance between the Ir and Mg concentration peaks in sample 3 is ∼2.91 nm, and the Ir concentration decreases by about 52% from its peak value to the MgO/CoFeB interface. For comparison, the EDX spectroscopy elemental maps and composition profiles for sample 2 are provided in the Supplementary Materials (text S2 and fig. S2). In sample 2, the separation between the Ir and Mg concentration peaks is substantially narrower, ∼1.46 nm, and the Ir concentration decreases by only ∼33% from its peak value to the MgO/CoFeB interface. These results demonstrate that the Ir concentration in the vicinity of the MgO/CoFeB interface is substantially lower in sample 3, consistent with the remote Ir doping strategy in which Ir is inserted at the CoFeB/Mo interface. This graded Ir profile in sample 3 provides a structural basis for the coexistence of high TMR, preserved magnetic stability, and enhanced voltage-driven switching efficiency (shown later in Fig. 4) after 400°C annealing. An additional notable feature is that the Ir concentration peak in both sample 2 and sample 3 is shifted toward the CoFe(B) layer. This behavior is attributed to growth and annealing kinetics associated with differences in crystallinity between adjacent layers. Specifically, the sputtered CoFeB layers are amorphous in the as-deposited state, whereas both Mo (i.e., CoFeB/Ir/Mo in sample 3) and MgO (i.e., MgO/Ir/CoFeB in sample 2) are crystalline. During film growth and postannealing, Ir atoms therefore preferentially diffuse toward the metastable amorphous CoFeB layer, leading to the observed asymmetric Ir distribution.

Annealing-dependent voltage-driven switching efficiency

Next, we investigate the influence of Ir insertion on the switching probability of voltage-driven magnetization switching in MTJs under different annealing temperatures. All the applied magnetic fields here were optimized to maximize the voltage-driven switching efficiency (text S5 and fig. S4A). The product of switching probabilities, Psw, is defined asPsw=P01×P10(1)where P01 (P10) denotes the single switching probability from P (AP) to AP (P) state. Figure 4 (A to C) shows the evolution of Psw as a function of pulse voltage amplitude at annealing temperatures of 300°, 400°, and 500°C, respectively. The applied voltages are intentionally kept below the breakdown limit of the MTJ nanopillars. At 300°C (Fig. 4A), all three samples exhibit a clear threshold behavior: Psw remains negligible below a threshold voltage Vth and increases rapidly once the applied voltage exceeds Vth. Below Vth, the voltage-induced reduction of the energy barrier is insufficient to trigger precessional switching. When V≥Vth, the magnetization begins to precess around the effective magnetic field μ0Heff, which consists of the VCMA-modulated anisotropy field, the stray field from the reference layer, and the external magnetic field. In this sense, Vth reflects the combined effects of the intrinsic magnetic anisotropy and the efficiency of voltage-induced anisotropy modulation. Consistent with the PMA trends shown in Fig. 2D and the Supplementary Materials (text S1, fig. S1, and table S1), sample 2 exhibits the lowest Vth, followed by sample 3, whereas sample 1 shows the highest Vth. Moreover, sample 2 displays a larger Psw at higher pulse voltages at 300°C, indicating a stronger voltage response under this annealing condition.

After annealing at 400°C (Fig. 4B), sample 1 shows a switching behavior similar to that observed at 300°C, whereas samples 2 and 3 exhibit markedly different responses. The switching probability of sample 2 remains nearly zero due to the collapse of PMA, as evidenced in Fig. 2D and the Supplementary Materials (text S1, fig. S1, and table S1). In contrast, sample 3 shows a substantial enhancement of Psw, which can be attributed to a more optimal Ir concentration near the MgO/CoFeB interface after high-temperature annealing. As revealed by the compositional analysis in Fig. 3C, annealing at 400°C allows controlled Ir diffusion toward the MgO/CoFeB interface, resulting in a graded Ir distribution with a relatively low—but sufficient—interfacial Ir concentration. Compared with sample 2, this reduced interfacial Ir concentration prevents severe PMA degradation while still enabling a strong voltage-induced switching response. Consequently, remote Ir insertion at the CoFeB/Mo interface allows sample 3 to combine high TMR, robust magnetic stability, and efficient voltage-driven switching. To compare the efficiency of voltage-induced anisotropy modulation, we define a figure-of-merit parameter ξ∗=Keff/Vth, where Vth is defined above as the threshold voltage at which Psw begins to increase rapidly. Because the MgO thickness and nominal device geometry are identical across samples 1 to 3, ξ* serves here as a relative indicator of voltage-driven anisotropy efficiency rather than an absolute VCMA coefficient. As shown in table S2, at 400°C, sample 3 exhibits a larger ξ* than sample 1 while maintaining a comparable Keff, consistent with its higher switching probability. This analysis further supports that the improved switching behavior of sample 3 is not due simply to reduced magnetic stability but rather to a more efficient voltage-induced switching response.

At an annealing temperature of 500°C (Fig. 4C), further Ir diffusion toward the MgO/CoFeB interface results in a pronounced degradation of PMA in sample 3, leading to a vanishing switching probability. The switching probability of sample 1 is also substantially reduced, likely due to structural degradation of the MTJ stack at such elevated temperatures. Figure 4D summarizes the peak values of Psw for all three samples as a function of annealing temperature. Notably, sample 3 exhibits the highest Psw at 400°C, together with a high TMR ratio of 165% (Fig. 2E). These results in Fig. 4 demonstrate that an appropriately controlled Ir concentration near the MgO/CoFeB interface enables efficient voltage-driven switching while preserving PMA and TMR, thereby mitigating the conventional VCMA-TMR trade-off at the device level.

Ultrafast and ultralow-energy precessional switching dynamics

Last, the detailed voltage-driven switching dynamics of the MTJs are examined in Fig. 5. Sample 1 and sample 3 were annealed at 300° and 400°C, respectively, corresponding to their optimal annealing conditions identified above. The θH of the applied magnetic fields for sample 1 and sample 3 were fixed at 70° and 55°, respectively, which are the optimized configuration to maximize the switching probabilities (text S5 and fig. S4A). The resistance–magnetic field (R-H) loops shown here were measured under a tilted magnetic field configuration, and the field values correspond to the perpendicular component of the applied field. Figure 5A shows the R-H loops of sample 1 measured under two different dc bias voltages, 10 and 600 mV (see Fig. 1B and Materials and Methods). When the dc bias voltage is increased to 600 mV, the coercivity of sample 1 is markedly reduced to nearly zero, consistent with a strong VCMA effect (21, 22, 38, 40). In addition, the center of the minor TMR loop shifts markedly under high bias, which can be attributed to the voltage-controlled interlayer coupling effect (68). When voltage pulses with sufficient amplitude are applied, magnetization switching can be induced via the voltage-driven precessional mechanism discussed above, leading to an oscillatory dependence of the switching probability on pulse duration, as shown in Fig. 5B. This oscillatory behavior is sensitive to the effective magnetic field, damping, and possible local magnetic inhomogeneity, which can lead to variations in oscillation contrast and period between different samples. An example of deterministic back-and-forth precessional switching is shown in the inset of Fig. 5B, where the resistance alternates reproducibly between high- and low-resistance states upon successive identical voltage pulses (1.72 V), demonstrating reversible switching of the free layer. A more detailed discussion for VCMA effect and the voltage-driven precessional switching are provided in the Supplementary Materials (text S4 and fig. S3). As shown in Fig. 5B, a switching probability Psw exceeding 60% is achieved with a pulse duration of 100 ps at a voltage amplitude of 1.72 V for sample 1, highlighting the potential for ultrafast voltage-driven switching on the sub–100-ps timescale. We note that further improvement of the switching speed in voltage-driven MTJs is ultimately constrained by the magnitude of PMA; a detailed discussion is provided in text S5. We also note that the voltage-induced switching demonstrated in the present work occurs in the dynamic (precessional) regime, driven by sub-nanosecond voltage pulses, where switching is achieved through rapid modulation of magnetic anisotropy without relying on thermal activation. This is in sharp contrast to our previous study (40), where reversible switching was observed in the thermally activated regime under long voltage pulses (>100 ms), and the switching mechanism involved a combined contribution from STT and VCMA. In that regime, switching is inherently slower and can be influenced by Joule heating, nonuniform magnetization dynamics, and potentially voltage-controlled interlayer coupling, particularly in larger (∼400 nm) devices. The present work therefore represents a fundamentally different switching regime, characterized by ultrafast, VCMA-dominated dynamics with negligible STT contribution.

The switching dynamics of sample 3 are presented in Fig. 5C. As commonly observed in dynamic voltage-driven precessional switching, Psw exhibits an oscillatory dependence on pulse duration, with higher switching probabilities obtained at larger pulse amplitudes. Notably, a Psw close to 100% is achieved using a voltage pulse of 2.04 V with a pulse duration of ∼0.6 ns. The thermal stability factor of this device, defined as ∆=Eb/kBT (where Eb is the anisotropy energy barrier, kB is the Boltzmann constant, and T is the temperature), is estimated to be ∼32 from the dependence of coercivity on magnetic field sweep rate using a thermally activated switching model (text S6 and fig. S5). Although the extracted value carries some uncertainty due to the limited number of data points, it is close to the range (Δ ∼ 23 to 30) reported for similar VCMA-switched MTJ nanopillars (35, 39, 42). To determine the actual switching energy, the tunneling current at high bias must be taken into account, as the MTJ resistance decreases substantially with increasing voltage. The current at 2.04 V is estimated from the measured I-V characteristics using a method reported previously (43), as shown in Fig. 5D. On the basis of this analysis, the switching energy at the peak Psw of sample 3 is estimated to be 3.54 fJ. To the best of our knowledge, this value represents the lowest switching energy reported for MTJs to date. We note that the lateral dimension of the MTJ nanopillars in this work is ∼160 nm, limited by the NSL used in the fabrication process. In principle, the switching energy can be further reduced by scaling down the device size. A simple geometric extrapolation suggests that further size scaling to 10 nm could reduce the switching energy to the ∼0.01-fJ level, comparable to the switching energy of state-of-the-art CMOS transistors. Although such projection does not account for possible size-dependent material and structural nonuniformities, it still underscores the strong potential of the voltage-driven scheme demonstrated here for ultralow-energy spintronic devices.

In conclusion, by explicitly exploiting the interfacial nature of spin-dependent tunneling and the nonlocal character of VCMA, we demonstrate voltage-driven magnetization switching energies as low as 3.5 fJ per bit in nanoscale MTJs operating in the sub-nanosecond regime while maintaining a TMR ratio up to 160% after 400°C postannealing. Through a systematic correlation of PMA, TMR, switching probability, and microstructural/compositional analyses across multiple stack configurations, we identify interfacial Ir concentration as the key parameter governing the long-standing VCMA-TMR trade-off at device level. We further show that an optimal Ir distribution near the MgO/CoFeB interface can be achieved via remote Ir insertion at the CoFeB/Mo interface, enabling efficient voltage-driven switching while mitigating the degradation of PMA and TMR under BEOL-compatible thermal budgets, therefore without sacrificing readout performance or magnetic stability. Beyond advancing voltage-controlled spintronics, these results establish a scalable device and materials strategy for ultralow-energy, ultrafast, nonvolatile switching elements that directly address the stringent energy-latency constraints faced by data-centric and artificial intelligence–era computing systems, where memory access increasingly dominates power consumption. The demonstrated combination of femtojoule-level write energy, sub-nanosecond speed, and CMOS-compatible integration positions voltage-driven MTJs as promising building blocks for next-generation energy-efficient memory and computing architectures, extending well beyond conventional MRAM.

MATERIALS AND METHODS

Sample preparation

All samples were deposited on silicon wafers with a 1-μm thermally grown SiO2 layer using a combination of dc and radio-frequency (rf) magnetron sputtering in a commercial sputtering system with a base pressure of ∼1 × 10−9 torr. During deposition, the substrates were maintained at room temperature. Metallic layers were deposited using dc magnetron sputtering sources, while the MgO layer was deposited using an rf magnetron sputtering source.

Thin film characterization

Both in-plane and out-of-plane M-H loops were measured by vibrating sample magnetometry for postannealed thin films. From these loops, the saturation magnetization Ms, anisotropy field μ0Hk, and effective anisotropy energy density Keff were extracted. The effective magnetic thickness of CoFeB used in the analysis was calibrated against the literature benchmark (8).

Device nanofabrication and thermal annealing

The as-deposited films were patterned into MTJ nanopillars with a nominal diameter of 160 nm using a combination of conventional optical lithography, NSL, and IBE. Details of the NSL technique have been reported previously (53). After patterning, the MTJ nanodevices were subjected to rapid thermal annealing at temperatures ranging from 300° to 500°C for 10 min to optimize PMA and TMR. Post–deposition annealing was performed in a high-purity Ar atmosphere at a pressure of 300 to 500 Pa, with oxygen and moisture levels maintained below 0.3 and 0.1 parts per million, respectively.

Device characterization

The fabricated MTJ nanodevices were connected to a microwave measurement setup using rf probes, as shown in Fig. 1B. Sub-nanosecond voltage pulses with variable amplitudes and durations were generated by a pulse generator (Picosecond Pulse Labs 10070A) and delivered to the devices through the rf port of a bias tee. Owing to the impedance mismatch between the MTJ devices and the 50-Ω coaxial transmission line, the actual voltage applied across the MTJ nanopillars was approximately twice the nominal output voltage of the pulse generator (42). The dc port of the bias tee was connected to a dc current source (Keithley 220 Programmable Current Source) and a voltmeter (Keithley 2000 Multimeter) to monitor the MTJ resistance and determine the TMR ratio, defined as (RAP – RP)/RP, where RP and RAP are the resistance in the P and AP configurations, respectively. Tilted external magnetic fields with varying magnitudes and polar angles θH were applied during the measurements. The magnetization switching probability was determined by measuring the MTJ resistance 500 times following the application of identical voltage pulses. All the measurements were performed at room temperature. The thermal stability factor Δ was estimated from the ramp-rate dependence of coercivity using a thermally activated switching model. The switching energy quoted at high bias is an estimate based on measured I-V characteristics within the experimentally accessible voltage range and subsequent extrapolation.

Structural characterization and elemental compositional analysis

Cross-sectional specimens for STEM were prepared using a dual-beam focused ion beam (FIB) system. A protective amorphous carbon and platinum capping layer was deposited before FIB milling to minimize ion-beam–induced damage during lamella preparation. The samples were initially thinned using a 30-keV Ga+ ion beam, followed by low-energy milling at 2 keV to remove surface damage layers. HAADF-STEM imaging and EDX spectroscopy elemental mapping were performed using an aberration-corrected FEI (S)TEM equipped with a monochromator, a CEOS DCOR probe corrector, and an EDX spectrometer. The microscope was operated at an accelerating voltage of 200 keV with a probe convergence angle of 18.2 mrad and HAADF detector collection angles ranging from 55 to 200 mrad. HAADF images and elemental maps were acquired using a probe current of 100 pA.
