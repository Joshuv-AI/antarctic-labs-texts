Barriers and opportunities to health system climate change adaptation in the Eastern Caribbean
Authors: D E Greaves, W Gabriel, L Charles, A Thomas, L Telesford
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectiveTo assess the barriers and opportunities to strengthening climate-resilient health systems in the Eastern Caribbean.Design and methodsInformation was extracted from desk reviews of the National Health Adaptation Plans for Climate Change (2022–2023). SWOT (Strengths, Weaknesses, Opportunities, and Threats) analyses informed plan preparation.ResultsBarriers included inadequate enabling governance and weak multisectoral collaboration. Opportunities included strong political commitment, inter-ministerial committees, and access to climate and health financing.ConclusionCaribbean small island states are well positioned to apply adaptation and mitigation measures to build climate-resilient health systems.Keywords: Eastern Caribbean, Climate change, Climate change adaptation, Health systems, Small island developing states

Introduction

Climate change is an existential threat to the health and well-being of the global population [1,2]. Small Island Developing States (SIDS) in the Caribbean have experienced significant adverse direct and indirect impacts from climate change, including an increasing burden of climate-sensitive communicable and non-communicable diseases [[3], [4], [5], [6]]. The health systems across the Caribbean are increasingly strained to respond to the upward trajectory of endemic and emerging diseases in the region arising from global environmental changes. In the regional context, Caribbean countries are experiencing a higher frequency and more intense extreme weather events [6]. Countries have recorded persistent changes in temporal and seasonal temperature and precipitation patterns [6]. Sea level rise, hurricanes, flooding, drought, heat waves, and the inherent impacts on the terrestrial and marine environments, exacerbate the economic, social, health, and environmental vulnerabilities of SIDS [7]. The need for health systems to adapt to climate change is of critical importance for protecting the well-being of the Caribbean population.

The World Health Organization (WHO) identifies six building blocks of a health system: health service delivery, health workforce, health information system, access to essential medicines, health system financing, and leadership and governance [7]. The attention to the development of specific areas in health systems varies across the Caribbean; however, WHO emphasizes the need for strengthening the building blocks as the foundation of effective health care to protect population health and well-being in the changing environment. The small, resource-limited islands that characterize the Caribbean are disproportionately threatened by climate change. The Caribbean Action Plan on Health and Climate Change, developed by the Pan American Health Organization (PAHO) and WHO, proposes adaptation and mitigation actions to be taken at both regional and national levels to address the prioritization of investments in climate-resilient health systems [8]. The findings of the 2021 WHO Health and Climate Change Country Survey further emphasize the need for actions to strengthen health systems in SIDS [9]. The survey confirmed the fragility of the health systems, stemming largely from, among other things, the lack of warning and preparedness systems, and gaps in other strategic areas that need to be strengthened [9].

Despite resource and capacity limitations, Caribbean countries have taken several steps towards strengthening local health systems and mitigating the adverse impacts of climate change on the environment and population health and wellbeing. Projects have been undertaken by the Organization of Eastern Caribbean States (OECS) with support from the World Bank and the Pan American Health Organization (PAHO) to support health systems response to adverse events from global, regional, and local sources [10]. The PAHO Smart Hospital project continues to be relevant in upgrading facilities and services to be safe and efficient in the changing climate conditions and during adverse weather events as well as to reduce the carbon footprint of healthcare [11]. This SMARTing project is at an advanced stage in many countries. Despite the progress, there are still gaps in the understanding of the significance of the regional idiosyncrasies as barriers or enablers of health systems’ development. These gaps must be fully explored and addressed in sharing the development of the building blocks of the health systems across the region. This document-search and SWOT analysis research focuses on identifying and discussing barriers and opportunities to strengthen health systems in four Eastern Caribbean islands - Barbados, Grenada, Saint Lucia, and St. Vincent and the Grenadines - to improve adaptation and strengthen resilience to climate change in the health sector. Recommendations are provided to encourage the governments and policymakers in these islands and the wider Eastern Caribbean to accelerate actions to strengthen the health systems via the building blocks.

Design and methods

This review evaluates the political, economic, social, technological, legal, and environmental (PESTLE) factors of the six building blocks of a health system in Barbados, Grenada, Saint Lucia, and St. Vincent and the Grenadines. An internet search was conducted to derive information related to the climate change impacts on health systems and healthcare in the islands as well as adaptation and response measures. The scope of the document search included but was not limited to ministries of health strategic plans, national adaptation plans, national climate change policies, health-influencing sectors’ climate change policies and strategic plans, regional health and climate change program plans and reports, regional strategic and guiding documents for health and climate change action, and research on climate change and health in the Caribbean. From these documents, we extracted themes and information on the barriers and opportunities to health systems’ climate change resilience and adaptation. To conduct the assessment, we specifically examined the strengths, weaknesses, opportunities, and threats (SWOT) to climate change resilience and adaptation in the six building blocks of a health system as identified in the WHO Guidance to Protect Health from Climate Change through Health Adaption Planning [12] and the Quality Criteria for Health National Adaptation Plans [13]; i.e. health systems financing, leadership and governance, health information systems, health workforce, access to essential medicines, and service delivery. The findings from this study can be valuable to guide the wider Caribbean region in mainstreaming affirmative measures for health systems strengthening through climate change adaptation. Based on our findings, we provide practical recommendations for policy and program interventions in these countries.

Results

Our internet search produced several documents on ministries of health strategic plans, national adaptation plans, national climate change policies, health-influencing sectors' climate change policies and strategic plans, regional health and climate change program plans and reports, regional strategic and guiding documents for health and climate change action, and research on climate change and health in the Caribbean. The extracted themes and key findings are shown in Table 1. These results were also found to be generally consistent with the WHO Health and Climate Change Country Survey Report findings, published in 2019 [9], highlighting areas of strength and weaknesses in the healthcare systems of countries.

Key themes

systems financing

Decisive and equitable allocation of resources is paramount for the support of climate change adaptation [14,15]. In the context of the SIDS, health systems financing is largely dependent on government allocations to the health sector through the ministries of health. These allocations, as a proportion of total health expenditure, are primarily directed to medical care and can reach up to at least 75% in some countries [16]. Considering the increasing impacts of climate change, this approach to health financing is unsustainable. Nonetheless, climate change mitigation and adaptation in the islands have been financed through several mechanisms, including United National Framework Convention on Climate Change (UNFCCC) programs targeting vulnerable states, such as the small islands in the Caribbean [17].

Nationally Determined Contributions (NDC) reports to the UNFCCC identify strengths and weaknesses in achieving mitigation and adaptation targets across sectors [45]. The reports provide evidence to support proposals for financing health and other sectoral activities to better position countries to achieve goals under the Paris Agreement [45]. One such funding mechanism is the Green Climate Fund (GCF), a major multilateral financing arrangement to support developing countries in achieving a reduction in greenhouse gas emissions and strengthening climate change resilience and adaptation [18]. Through WHO, GCF financing is available to the health sector for, among other things, enabling and supporting adaptation and mitigation, technology development and transfer, capacity-building, and the preparation of national reports [18]. Caribbean countries may also access GCF funding through the Caribbean Development Bank (CDB) and the Caribbean Community Climate Change Center (5Cs) for special projects. Another financing mechanism of the UNFCCC is the Global Environment Facility (GEF) [19]. The GEF provides financial support to government agencies, civil society organizations, private sector companies, research institutions, and other partners to implement programs related to environmental conservation and adaptation, capacity building, and energy transformation, among other programs [19].

Despite the availability of these mechanisms, some countries do not access the resources owing to the absence of clearly defined climate and health programs in the corporate plans. The lack of financing has, therefore, emerged as one of the most common barriers to the implementation of climate change adaptation measures in the Caribbean countries [15]. In the absence of international and regional financing mechanisms, local contributions to health sector financing remain a concern, with the observed low uptake of private health insurance and with the absence of national health insurance programs. Governments are, therefore, tasked with the burden of subsidizing healthcare services costs and rely heavily on international and regional financial mechanisms for developing capital projects [16]. There is also the added challenge of limited capacity to attract large-scale financing for health sector development [20,21]. This challenge is partly due to limited project writing skills.

Water is vital for health and crucial in the delivery of health care services. Many communities and health institutions are increasingly water-stressed. At the same time, in some countries, there are limited funding programs for community adaptation, such as investments in rainwater harvesting for community-based facilities. Furthermore, climate change mitigation and adaptation financing are very often excluded or minimized in governments and institutions' annual budgets due to failure of programming or disregard for budget guiding documents such as the National Adaptation Plan (NAP) and the National Climate Change Policy. Additional challenges remain in the actual allocation of secured funds to implement national health strategies effectively. The misalignment of funding to program arises from information gaps and administrative challenges in accessing and deploying resources effectively. In each of the countries, there is a need for greater emphasis on the mobilization of adaptation program funding.

Leadership and Governance

Having ratified the Paris Agreement, Sendai Framework for Disaster Risk Management, the United Nations Sustainable Development Goals (SDGs), and other international Conventions related to climate change, several Caribbean islands have pledged their support for limiting the average increase in temperature to 1.50 C above pre-industrial levels. Countries have demonstrated strong political support for this international goal, with several having developed policies and implementation plans [15,22]. With the increasing frequency of extreme events, the islands also stand to benefit from a reversal in global trends. The OECS and CARICOM (political and economic union of the Caribbean Community) treaties provide some opportunities for countries to adopt affirmative climate change mitigation and adaptation policies and interventions. Health sector strategic plans also exist [16,26] and are updated periodically, allowing for the inclusion of strategies to address current and emerging threats from climate change. In three of the islands, there is a formally designated National Climate Change Focal Point, supported by a corresponding Focal Point within the Ministry of Health. Where such an arrangement exists, it provides appropriate support and leadership for program activities.

The lack of Health National Adaptation Plans (HNAPS) or delays in their implementation in the four countries leaves a void in climate change governance in the health sector [15]. In some cases, such as in Barbados, the health sector does not appear to hold a place of prominence in the island’s NDC report [31]. In St. Vincent, Grenada, and Saint Lucia, health sector adaptation is not mainstreamed in the NAP and there is no institutional framework and resource unit to implement climate change programs in the health sector. In St. Lucia and Grenada, the development and integration of early warning systems, climate-sensitive disease surveillance, and climate change budgeting through the ministries of health were deficient. Additionally, several pieces of legislation need to be updated [27] and in all of the islands, climate change mitigation and adaptation are largely absent from the strategic areas in health [9,26,29,30].

Health information systems

Syndromic data for notifiable communicable diseases is submitted routinely to surveillance departments in the ministries of health from hospitals and community clinics. This form of reporting has allowed for observed epidemiological thresholds. When there is excess morbidity, the ministries of health can detect and respond in a timely manner. CARPHA (Caribbean Public Health Agency) acts as an epidemiological data repository and hub and also provides technical support to countries to respond to epidemics [42]. PAHO provides technical support and assistance to countries in capacity building and outbreak response [8].

Despite the appointment of climate change focal points in ministries of health, there is still a lack of coordination between the various units of the ministries to specifically monitor and report on the direct and indirect climate-driven impacts on human health and the health system. In most cases, the archaic health information system that exists on the island is incapable of accommodating an efficient integration of a wide range of data from primary, secondary, and tertiary points of care [3,8]. Consequently, there is insufficient surveillance and alignment of climate-sensitive diseases to underscore the dire need for the protection of health. Other governance and enabling environment concerns include both weak health intra-ministerial communication and communication with other health-influencing sectors such as water, sanitation, energy, and transportation. In light of WHO’s assessment, there is a need for collaboration between health and meteorological organizations to facilitate weather data sharing, analysis, and the development of early warning systems [32]. In some instances, for example, there is difficulty in accessing data on sea-level rise and mean sea-level temperatures, which implicates marine-dependent livelihoods and the social and economic well-being of segments of the population. Sufficient data are not collected on non-communicable diseases, comparable to notifiable communicable diseases, and data are not disaggregated to give a deeper understanding of the relationship across demographic groups [20,21,33]. This results in under-reporting to the surveillance system.

Health workforce

The islands have registered progress in the development of policies and plans for health sector human resource development, with improvements in workforce availability and distribution across health services, especially with the utilization of multi-disciplinary teams at the primary care level [23]. Some opportunities are provided for training and capacity building of health workers and community members [40,43]. Nonetheless, insufficient provisions for capacity development, communication, and awareness of the linkages between climate change and health among the workforce remain areas of concern [20,21,33]. Both training and an increase in trained personnel are needed to scale up the services in the region. Apart from health workers, workers in health-influencing sectors such as agriculture, fisheries, community development, disaster management, and local government, where they exist in the region, need to be trained to understand the health issues associated with climate change, adaptation and mitigation measures, and to disseminate information to constituents as well as monitor behaviors. The co-benefits to be derived from the trained workforce in matters of climate change and health would be a reservoir of knowledge and leadership for climate change action [8,46].

The region is deficient in surveillance capacity and provision of specialized health services to adequately link climate-sensitive illnesses to environmental changes. While there has been some progress in developing healthcare facilities to address climate-related issues, challenges persist with limited knowledge and working resources as well as workforce exposures to climate events that decrease the physical and mental ability to deliver health services [25,28,29,47]. In one study, the region’s health professionals identified the lack of time and knowledge as key barriers to engagement, suggesting the need for support to engage with, advocate for, and act on climate change and health issues [48].

Access to essential medicines

There is awareness of the impacts of climate change on the storage, transportation, and safe handling of essential medicines. The need to improve facilities and technology for access to and rational use of medicines is well recognized in the islands [10,34]. PAHO’s resolution on “Access and Rational Use of Strategic and High-Cost Medicines and other Health Technologies” [44] offers health systems in the region policy proposals for improving access to essential medicines. There has been progress over the years; however, several multi-dimensional challenges need to be addressed through continuous action to improve access to essential medicines and related technologies [27].

Another challenge is the limited coordination between epidemiology units and pharmaceutical services [27]. Storage and transportation infrastructure need to be upgraded to adequately stock a range of required medicines to preserve the quality, including along cold chain, and those sensitive to heat. In general, specialized resources skilled in procurement and inventory management are in high demand in the region but are limited. The OECS pooled procurement services and Caribbean Disaster Emergency Management Agency (CDEMA) may be tapped to support increasing access to essential medicines at the most reasonable cost in circumstances of extreme climate events and to improve the overall health services in the islands.

Service delivery

Health services are provided by both privately and publicly owned facilities. Most of the Caribbean countries have a well-developed network of public health facilities, including hospitals and community health facilities. This improves access to health care for residents across all socioeconomic groups. Services are typically provided at minimal to no cost to residents. The private sector also contributes substantially to medical services in the region [30]. However, the contribution and impact of the private sector on health outcomes have not been measured.

There are barriers to timely, high-quality, and equitable access to preventive and curative care stemming from resource and capacity deficiencies [35]. The efficiency and effectiveness of service delivery are impacted by the resource constraints that characterize SIDS, exacerbating the challenges to invest in resilient healthcare infrastructure, technology, and workforce training [20,21]. The physical vulnerability of medical stations, health centers, and hospitals to extreme weather events is also a concern. For example, many of the health facilities across the region need upgrading and retrofitting with modern equipment to improve healthcare services, particularly in the rural areas [35]. The PAHO evaluation report on the SMART Hospital Program in the Eastern Caribbean corroborates this finding, stating that many health facilities are at high risk and in need of strengthening in the face of increasing climate threats [41].

The fragmented nature of the health systems poses a threat to achieving comprehensive and coordinated responses to climate-related health challenges. In addition, insufficient data and research on the health impacts of climate change impede decision-making [36,37]. These structural issues hinder the achievement of coordinated and comprehensive healthcare adaptation. In St. Vincent and the Grenadines, for example, social and environmental factors exacerbate the risk of climate-sensitive diseases within segments of the population. The health sector has limited capacity to effectively respond and protect vulnerable populations.

The need for community capacity development to respond to climate change and manage local resources is well recognized in the islands. In most cases, community social capital exists, represented by functioning local government and community and civil society organizations that can assume responsibility for community-led adaptation strategies. Implementation is more likely to be sustainable when it is rooted in local community needs and interests and tailored to the local context. Local government councils and community disaster preparedness committees can play a pivotal and effective role in mobilizing resources and supporting the implementation of sustainable programs. There is still a need for training community-based groups and civil society organizations and increasing participation and representation in decision-making for health systems strengthening [38]. Ministries of health can be effective in partnering with community organizations to initiate education and awareness campaigns and mobilize participation for policy development and action [39].

Recommendations

The significant vulnerability of the health sector to the impacts of climate change is widely acknowledged and has received much attention, given the heavy burden on SIDS. However, it is increasingly being recognized that climate change will present opportunities to strengthen climate change resilience in health systems and support economic growth, social cohesion, inclusion, and environmental sustainability in this region. We anticipate that the persistent negative consequences of climate change will demand a seismic shift in island systems’ health administration, policies, programs, and community engagement to explicitly consider climate change. The health system building blocks provide a systematic and comprehensive development of the health care system to respond to climate change. The following presents our recommendations:

•Mainstream adaptation considerations by health systems into existing health strategies and programs led by a ‘Health-in-all-Policies' strategy and ‘One-Health’ approach to incorporate the complex range of social, economic, cultural, and environmental factors.

In light of the escalating impacts of climate change, Eastern Caribbean islands must integrate climate resilience into their health systems comprehensively. The results presented highlight the necessity of embedding climate change considerations into all health-related strategies and enhancing cooperation between government and non-governmental organizations. Such an integrative approach will not only address the multifaceted influences on health but also will ensure health systems are adequately supported and funded to tackle the challenges posed by climate change. By systematically collecting and analyzing diverse data, strengthening regional and international partnerships, and enacting robust climate-related legislation, the Eastern Caribbean can safeguard and significantly enhance its public health infrastructure. Policymakers must act decisively to implement these strategies, recognizing that the health of their populations and the resilience of their communities depend on proactive and informed governance. The time for action is now as the health and environmental stability of future generations hangs in the balance.

The strategic policies proposed not only promise to fortify the health systems against the impacts of climate change locally but also set a precedent for global health security. By integrating climate resilience into health frameworks, these policies serve as a model for other regions facing similar environmental challenges. The successful implementation of such policies in the Eastern Caribbean could inspire and inform broader international efforts, demonstrating the effectiveness of proactive, integrated approaches to public health in the face of global climate challenges. Therefore, the actions taken here have the potential to resonate well beyond their immediate geographical boundaries, contributing to a more robust global response to climate-related risks.
