Generational differences in perceptions and acceptance of violence in emergency rooms: a cross-sectional study in Thailand
Authors: Laddawan Kiettikunwong, Pongmanut Deeod, Narong Kiettikunwong
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Background and aimViolence in hospital emergency rooms (ERs) presents serious risks to patient safety and healthcare workers. While prior research has focused on organizational risk factors, limited attention has been given to how the public interprets and accepts violent behavior, particularly across different generations. This study examined generational differences in perceptions and acceptance of violent behavior in hospital ERs in Thailand.MethodsA cross-sectional survey was conducted among 615 residents living in communities surrounding seven hospitals. Respondents were classified into four generational cohorts: Generation Z, Millennials, Generation X, and Baby Boomers. Data were collected using a structured questionnaire based on the Theory of Planned Behavior. Data analysis included descriptive statistics, Chi-square tests to examine severity classifications, and multiple regression analysis with dummy-coded generational variables (Baby Boomers as reference group) to examine the association between each generational cohort and acceptance of violence. Bias-corrected bootstrapping was employed to validate confidence intervals for all estimates.ResultsChi-square results indicated significant generational differences in how violence is defined; younger cohorts were more likely to label only physical assault as violence, while overlooking verbal aggression. Multiple regression analysis indicated that, relative to Baby Boomers (reference group), Generation Z (B = 0.263, β = 0.187, p = .019) and Millennials (B = 0.230, β = 0.155, p = .045) were significantly associated with higher acceptance of violent behavior in ERs, while Generation X did not differ significantly (B = 0.100, β = 0.062, p = .393). Overall model: F(3, 611) = 3.075, p = .027, R² = 0.015.ConclusionsGenerational background is significantly associated with differences in how individuals interpret and accept aggression in hospitals. Current Zero Tolerance policies may have limited effectiveness because younger demographics do not perceive verbal aggression as a violation. Prevention strategies may benefit from explicitly defining and prohibiting non-physical violence to align with the interpretive frameworks of younger generations.Clinical trial registrationNot applicable.

Introduction

Workplace violence is a critical public health and occupational-safety problem, particularly in hospital environments where high emotional intensity, urgent medical decision-making, and continuous interaction between staff, patients, and accompanying individuals create conditions that may lead to conflict and aggression. Healthcare workers face especially high risks because the nature of medical care requires close contact with distressed patients and relatives who may be experiencing pain, anxiety, fear, or grief, all of which can intensify emotional reactions and increase the likelihood of violent incidents [1, 2]. Violence in ERs has been shown to affect not only the physical safety of healthcare personnel but also their mental well-being, job satisfaction, and intention to remain in the profession, while also threatening the quality and continuity of patient care [3–6]. These consequences make hospital violence not only a matter of occupational risk but also a wider public-health concern with implications for service delivery and patient safety.

In Thailand, this issue has drawn increasing public attention due to a number of widely reported incidents occurring inside or near emergency room (ER) areas. Examples include the violent altercation involving rival youth groups at Kabin Buri Hospital in 2019 [7], the ER incident at Kaeng Khro Hospital in 2019 involving groups entering the treatment zone to confront one another [8], and the 2022 case in which juveniles detonated an explosive device outside the ER of Udon Thani Hospital [9]. These events illustrate a pattern in which violent conflicts originating in the community continue into hospital premises. This reflects a distinctive contextual feature of the Thai ER, in which the ER can become an extension of external disputes, leading to retaliatory confrontation, further injury, and in some cases loss of life within a public medical facility. Such situations create a dual burden for hospitals, which must simultaneously provide life-saving care and manage the risk of violent escalation within the treatment space.

ERs represent a unique high-risk environment because they are open, rapidly functioning spaces where emotions are heightened and medical staff are the primary authority figures. In the Thai context, ERs frequently receive victims of violent altercations who arrive together with opposing parties, creating opportunities for confrontation to continue inside the hospital [10]. However, while international studies have examined organizational risk factors and environmental conditions contributing to such behavior [1, 11], relatively few studies have explored how members of the public interpret or evaluate violent behavior occurring inside these environments. Existing research in Thailand has largely focused on describing incident patterns [12–15], leaving a limited understanding of the behavioral meanings and attitudes that shape how different groups perceive violence in hospitals.

Generational differences represent an important but underexplored dimension in this context. Prior research suggests that individuals from different generational cohorts are associated with distinct social environments and cultural norms, corresponding to differences in their thresholds for what constitutes acceptable behavior in confrontational situations [16–18]. These differences may help explain why some groups perceive certain forms of aggression in ERs as normal or situational, while others regard the same actions as unacceptable threats. This study investigates generational differences in perceptions toward violence and acceptance of violent behaviors in ERs in Thailand. By establishing behavioral evidence on how different age groups interpret violent conduct, this research aims to support the development of more precise, generation-responsive violence-prevention strategies that are better aligned with the value orientations of diverse groups, thereby strengthening the safety climate within healthcare settings.

Literature review and conceptual framework

Violence in hospital settings has been widely examined in international research, particularly in relation to occupational risk, patient safety, and organizational environments. The World Health Organization (WHO) defines violence against healthcare workers as behaviors ranging from verbal abuse and threats to physical assault, intimidation, and acts that create fear or psychological harm [19, 20]. Specifically, violence perpetrated by patients or visitors against staff is classified as “Type II violence” and is widely recognized as one of the most serious occupational hazards in healthcare sectors [21–23]. Much of the literature emphasizes that this violence does not arise only from individual behavior but is associated with situational contexts such as workload pressure, emotional distress, overcrowding, and communication difficulties. These factors interact within highly sensitive care environments, especially ERs, where time-critical treatment, uncertainty, and stress can increase the likelihood of aggressive reactions [1, 24]. Studies consistently demonstrate that ERs and high-demand service zones have the highest rates of Type II incidents, highlighting that hospital violence must be understood as a phenomenon embedded within high-pressure treatment environments [21, 22].

Research in the Thai context reflects similar trends, with scholars documenting risk factors such as communication breakdowns, emotional tension among relatives, and dissatisfaction with waiting times [12–14]. However, most of these studies focus on incident characteristics, environmental conditions, or institutional responses, while giving less attention to how members of the public perceive, interpret, or evaluate violent behavior within hospital environments. This indicates an important gap in the literature, as understanding public perceptions toward violence in hospitals is essential for explaining why some aggressive behaviors are normalized or tolerated, while others are rejected. In hospital contexts where violence can unfold rapidly, attitudes and values play a crucial role in shaping how individuals make sense of confrontational events and how they react when tension arises.

To understand these attitudinal differences, this study draws on generational cohort theory. Grounded in Mannheim’s sociology of knowledge, this perspective argues that people who grow up during the same historical and social period develop shared experiences, values, and worldviews that shape their behavioral orientations throughout life [16]. Studies in human development further show that exposure to social stress, peer-group norms, and changing communication cultures across generations may be associated with different emotional responses and tolerance thresholds toward aggression [17, 18]. From this perspective, generational identity does not merely reflect age but represents a socially patterned lens through which individuals evaluate actions and interpret the meaning of conflict. In the Thai context, this is particularly relevant as different cohorts have been socialized under distinct socio-political and economic conditions, potentially leading to divergent views on authority and public conduct.

Connecting these generational perspectives to behavior, the study utilizes the Theory of Planned Behavior (TPB). TPB proposes that human behavior is influenced by attitudes, subjective norms, and perceived behavioral control, meaning that individuals who hold permissive or accepting attitudes toward aggressive behavior are more likely to tolerate or engage in such actions when confronted with high-stress situations [25]. This theoretical position is supported by studies demonstrating strong links between attitudes toward interpersonal violence and the likelihood of violent or aggressive behavioral expression [26]. In the context of ERs, these insights suggest that public attitudes toward what counts as “acceptable” behavior may correspond to differences in how people respond when conflict emerges in treatment spaces, whether they perceive early aggression as harmless, and whether they recognize escalation risks.

Furthermore, insights from criminological research on fear of punishment reinforce the importance of moral evaluation. Findings indicate that the perception of legal sanction does not always operate as the primary factor discouraging harmful conduct; instead, moral evaluation and prior behavioral acceptance often play stronger roles [16, 27]. People may engage in confrontational actions even when punishment is possible if they do not view the behavior as morally wrong. When applied to hospital contexts, this perspective helps explain why some individuals may continue confrontational acts inside ERs despite legal prohibitions and the presence of medical authority. This highlights the importance of examining not only environmental risk factors but also underlying attitudinal patterns that correspond to how individuals define the boundaries of acceptable behavior.

Synthesizing these theories, the conceptual framework for this study (Fig. 1) positions generational perceptions toward violence (operationalized through generational cohort) as the primary independent variable theoretically associated with attitudes toward violence (conceptual psychological factor and proposed mediator), which is in turn theoretically associated with the level of acceptance of violent behavior in ERs (the outcome). Within this framework, the generational cohort functions as a social background factor reflecting shared life experiences and differences in how individuals perceive and classify violent behavior. Attitude toward violence is positioned as a psychological mechanism conceptually linked to interpretation but not directly tested in the empirical model. Acceptance of violence represents the behavioral outcome—the extent to which individuals tolerate or justify aggression in medical institutions. Hospital-based risk factors are incorporated as contextual modifiers that may interact with these processes, creating emotionally intense circumstances that may be associated with heightened acceptance of aggression.

This conceptual framing guides the empirical analysis, supporting an investigation of how different generational cohorts in Thailand perceive (i.e., classify severity) and accept violent behavior in hospital environments—particularly in ERs—and how these attitudinal differences may inform more precise and behaviorally responsive prevention strategies. It is important to note that while the conceptual model theoretically positions attitudes toward violence as a mediating variable, formal mediation analysis was not conducted in this study. The empirical analysis tests only the direct association between generational cohort (and associated perceptions) and acceptance of violent behavior, as well as differences in severity classification across cohorts. References to attitudes in this paper and its framing reflect the broader conceptual proposition; readers should distinguish between what is theoretically proposed and what is empirically tested.

Methodology

Research design

This study employed a quantitative survey research design to examine generational differences in attitudes toward violence and acceptance of violent behaviors occurring in hospital settings, with particular emphasis on ERs in Thailand. The objective of the research was to generate empirical evidence explaining how individuals from different generational cohorts perceive, interpret, and evaluate violent behavior when it occurs inside medical institutions rather than in everyday social contexts. The methodological design was therefore structured to align closely with the conceptual framework, in which generational cohort functions as a background factor, attitudes toward violence are positioned as a proposed mediator (note: formal mediation analysis for other factors, such as psychological or hospital-based risk factors, was not the focus of this study), and acceptance of violent behavior represents the outcome expressed in hospital-specific situations. The study population consisted of residents living within the community areas surrounding the seven hospitals. This specific sampling frame was chosen because violence in Thai ERs frequently originates from local inter-group conflicts that spill over into the hospital environment. Therefore, community members in the immediate vicinity represent the primary service-user base and the social context from which these conflicts emerge, making their attitudes most relevant to understanding the local safety climate.

Population and sample

The study was conducted across seven hospitals in Thailand that had previously reported violent or confrontational incidents in or around ER areas. These hospitals were purposively selected to reflect variation in institutional scale, patient-flow dynamics, and complexity of care across the national health-service delivery system. This multi-site selection strategy ensured that the study captured attitudinal patterns emerging across different types of service contexts rather than being limited to a single institutional setting. To enable international readers to clearly understand the structure of Thailand’s hospital system and the diversity of service environments included in the sample, the study sites are presented according to Ministry of Public Health service-level classifications. The characteristics of the seven study hospitals, including institutional level, capacity, and service context, are summarized in Table 1.

This site composition reflects urban, peri-urban, and rural contexts and allows the attitudinal findings to be interpreted across different service environments where violence may emerge in distinct forms. By explicitly situating the research within multiple tiers of the health-service system, the methodology strengthens both contextual relevance and the policy applicability of the results.

A stratified random sampling procedure was applied to ensure the sample accurately reflected the distribution of the population across the service areas. The stratification was based on hospital service level (Regional, General, and Community Hospitals) to ensure proportional representation. Within each stratum, respondents were randomly selected from households located within a 5-kilometer radius of the hospital. Sample-size determination followed quantitative research principles for comparative and explanatory analysis. Drawing on methodological guidance suggesting that attitudinal-structure models require sample sizes of at least ten to fifteen participants per indicator, a minimum target of approximately six hundred participants was established. An additional allowance was incorporated to account for potential non-response, resulting in a final sample of 615 respondents, which is sufficient for generational cohort comparison and multiple regression analysis while maintaining statistical robustness. Data collection took place between April 2023 and December 2023 through field-based, face-to-face administration to ensure clarity of interpretation and alignment with hospital-context experiences.

Research instrument

The research instrument consisted of a structured questionnaire developed in accordance with the study’s conceptual framework. The first section collected demographic and birth-year data used to classify respondents into generational cohorts: Generation Z (18–27 years), Millennials (28–43 years), Generation X (44–59 years), and Baby Boomers (60–78 years). The second section measured attitudes toward violence based on principles of the TPB. Respondents rated their agreement with statements regarding social norms and behavioral beliefs using a 5-point Likert scale ranging from “Strongly Disagree” to “Strongly Agree.” The third section assessed acceptance of violent behavior in ERs through hypothetical yet context-anchored scenarios reflecting forms of aggression commonly encountered in ER areas. These violence severity levels were constructed specifically for this study based on a review of Thai legal codes and hospital incident reports, and validated by content experts. To ensure clarity in measurement, the instrument explicitly defined “Minor Violence” (Level 1) as behaviors that violate social norms of respect but do not typically result in physical injury or immediate legal prosecution, such as impolite speech, shouting, verbal insults, inappropriate gesturing, or non-injurious physical contact. This section also covered escalation to confrontation between conflicting parties and retaliatory actions. The items were designed to ensure that respondents’ judgments were grounded in hospital-specific situations, thereby linking attitudinal responses directly to patient-safety and ER risk contexts rather than to general social behavior.

Data collection and ethics

Instrument quality was ensured through expert review and psychometric testing. Content validity was assessed by three subject-matter specialists in public health, hospital administration, and behavioral research, yielding a Content Validity Index ranging from 0.80 to 1.00. A pilot study involving thirty participants was conducted to examine reliability, and Cronbach’s alpha values exceeded 0.70 across all scales, confirming acceptable internal consistency. These validation processes were essential because the study aims to inform hospital-based prevention strategies, and therefore the measurement of attitudes and acceptance must accurately reflect how individuals interpret violence in real clinical environments.

Ethical procedures were strictly observed. The study protocol was reviewed and approved by the Human Research Ethics Committee of Khon Kaen University (Approval Number: HE663201). Written informed consent was obtained from all participants prior to data collection. Participation was voluntary, informed consent was obtained, anonymity was assured, and respondents retained the right to decline any item that they felt uncomfortable answering. The ethical approach was guided by the principle that research on hospital-based violence must protect the dignity and emotional well-being of participants who may have experienced or witnessed high-stress medical situations.

Data analysis

Data were analyzed using descriptive statistics to characterize the sample and Chi-square tests to assess associations between generational cohorts and acceptance levels. To examine whether generational cohort was differentially associated with acceptance of violent behavior, generation was dummy-coded with Baby Boomers as the reference group (the oldest cohort with the lowest mean acceptance score). Three dummy variables were created: Generation Z, Millennials, and Generation X. Multiple regression analysis (using SPSS v.26) was conducted to estimate the association between each generational cohort and acceptance scores, allowing the association to differ freely across groups. Chi-square assumptions were verified to ensure all expected cell counts exceeded 5. Sensitivity checks were conducted to address unequal sample sizes between cohorts, particularly the smaller Baby Boomer group (n = 44). The final sample size (N = 615) provided adequate statistical power. Multivariate normality was assessed using Mardia’s coefficient, and bootstrapped bias-corrected 95% Confidence Intervals (2,000 resamples) were computed for all regression weights. Notably, although the conceptual framework (Fig. 1) positions attitudes as a theoretically proposed mediator, formal mediation analysis was not conducted in this study. The empirical analysis focuses only on the direct association between generational cohort and acceptance. Formal mediation testing is therefore recommended for future research.

Findings / results

The analysis of the data is presented in two main components: first, the general demographic characteristics of the 615 respondents, and second, the generational differences associated with perceptions toward violence and the acceptance of violent behavior in ERs. The respondents were residents of communities surrounding the seven study hospitals and represented four generational cohorts, namely Generation Z, Millennials, Generation X, and the Baby Boomer Generation. The socio-demographic profile of the sample, including sex, age range, education level, occupation, marital status, and monthly income, is presented in Table 2. Based on this distribution, most respondents were female (56.7%), while males accounted for 43.3% of the sample. With respect to age, the largest group was Generation Z, aged approximately 18–27 years, comprising 38.2% of respondents, followed by Millennials (31.1%) and Generation X (23.6%), whereas the Baby Boomer Generation formed the smallest group at 7.2%. In terms of education, the largest proportion of respondents had completed upper secondary education or held a vocational certificate (37.0%), while the smallest group consisted of individuals with no formal education (0.5%). The most common occupation was trade or small-scale commerce (28.1%), followed by general laborers and monthly wage employees (17.2% each), whereas retired persons represented only 0.2% of the sample. Regarding marital status, nearly half of the respondents identified as single (48.9%), with a similar proportion reporting that they were married (46.2%); those who were widowed, divorced, or separated together accounted for a small minority of the sample. Monthly income was concentrated in the lower and lower-middle ranges, with the largest proportion earning 5,001–10,000 THB per month (28.3%) and the smallest proportion earning 20,001–25,000 THB (5.2%). This demographic profile summarized in Table 2 indicates that the study captures a broad cross-section of community members who interact with hospital environments in different social roles, thereby enhancing the relevance of the attitudinal findings for hospital-safety planning.

Analysis of general information

To investigate generational perspectives on the acceptability and interpretation of violent behavior in ERs, the analysis classified violence into four severity levels, ranging from very minor actions to serious criminal assault. Level 1 referred to very minor violence, including impolite speech or inappropriate behavior toward hospital staff that does not carry legal penalties. Level 2 referred to minor violence subject to non-severe legal penalties, such as insult or defamation. Level 3 covered severe violence carrying minor legal penalties, including pushing, shoving, or throwing objects. Level 4 represented very severe violence, comprising criminal offenses such as physical assault, with or without the use of a weapon. These levels were used to examine how different generational cohorts draw the boundary between behavior they consider merely confrontational and behavior they regard as true violence in hospital environments. A chi-square test of independence was conducted to explore the relationship between generational cohort and perceptions of these four severity levels. The chi-square results in Table 3 indicate a statistically significant association between generational cohort and perceived severity of violent conduct in ERs (χ² = 23.49, df = 9, p = .005, C.C. = 0.192). This finding supports the hypothesis that age-based generational groups differ in how they classify violent behaviors within healthcare environments. Across all four cohorts, most respondents tended to recognize violence primarily when physical aggression was involved. Actions such as assault, with or without weapons, were most frequently classified as Level 4, representing the highest severity category. In contrast, behaviors such as impolite speech, inappropriate conduct toward staff, verbal insults, defamation, pushing, shoving, or throwing objects—although categorized as forms of violence in theoretical models—were not generally regarded as violent by respondents from any generation. This pattern suggests that many community members perceive violence in hospitals as beginning only when actions cause direct physical harm or clearly constitute a criminal offense, thereby overlooking lower-level aggressive behaviors that may still have serious implications for staff well-being and patient safety.

To better understand the characteristics of individuals who perceive violence only at this highest severity level, further analysis was conducted specifically on the subgroup of respondents who classified violent behavior at Level 4. Their socio-demographic profiles are presented in Table 4. Among Generation Z, those who regarded violence as beginning when physical force or assault occurs were predominantly female, had completed upper secondary education or vocational certificates, were mainly students, and were single, with monthly incomes most commonly below 5,000 THB. For Millennials, respondents who perceived violence as beginning at the point of physical aggression were again mostly female, held a bachelor’s degree, worked in small-scale trade or retail, were married, and reported monthly incomes between 10,001 and 15,000 THB. Within Generation X, those who defined violence in terms of physical assault were primarily male, had completed upper secondary or vocational education, worked mainly in small-scale trade, were married, and earned between 5,001 and 10,000 THB per month. Among Baby Boomers, most respondents who classified violence based on physical aggression were female, had primary education, worked in trade or general wage labor, were married, and had monthly incomes between 10,001 and 15,000 THB. These patterns are summarized in Table 4 and are presented solely as descriptive profiles, without implying statistical associations between socio-demographic characteristics and severity classification.

With the four-level classification, this suggests a generational shift in how the most extreme behaviors are labeled, potentially reflecting differences in media exposure, social norms, and lived experience with public conflict.

An additional pattern observed in the data concerns how respondents assign meaning to violence in ER areas. Some younger participants distinguished between what they regarded as “serious violence,” such as physical assault, and what they saw as normal or emotionally understandable confrontation in moments of stress. Older respondents tended to classify both forms as unacceptable disturbances in a medical environment. This interpretive difference is particularly relevant for hospital-safety management because it suggests that conflict escalation in ERs may be partly driven by differing social expectations about what behavior is considered tolerable during crises. Where younger individuals underestimate the seriousness of early conflict behaviors, preventive intervention may be delayed, increasing the likelihood that incidents evolve into physical violence. Hence, the results provide behavioral evidence that can inform the design of violence-prevention strategies which are sensitive to generational patterns of interpretation, communication, and tolerance, especially in ERs where conflict can escalate rapidly.

Discussion

The findings of this study demonstrate that generational cohort is associated with differences in how violence is perceived and classified (Chi-square analysis of severity) and with the level of acceptance that individuals express toward violent behavior in hospital ERs (regression analysis). While the conceptual framework positioned attitudes toward violence as a proposed mediating variable, the empirical analysis tested only these direct associations. These results contribute to the broader body of research on hospital violence by showing that violence in medical institutions cannot be understood solely through environmental, organizational, or situational risk factors, but must also be examined through the behavioral meanings that different social groups assign to aggressive acts occurring in these spaces. The results suggest that younger generational cohorts, particularly late adolescents and young adults, tend to hold more permissive attitudes toward certain forms of aggression and are more likely to interpret emotionally charged confrontation as situational or understandable, whereas older cohorts are more likely to regard the same behaviors as unacceptable disturbances to safety and order in medical environments. This suggests that variation in the perception of violence in hospital ERs may be associated not only with the intensity of crisis situations but also with generationally patterned ways of evaluating acceptable behavior during moments of conflict. However, the effect size was very small (R² = 0.015), indicating that generational cohort alone explains only a very limited portion of this variation, and findings should be interpreted with appropriate caution.

These findings align with international literature indicating that ERs are high-risk environments characterized by emotional stress, uncertainty, and time-critical care processes that may trigger conflict between patients, relatives, and healthcare personnel [1, 24]. However, while previous studies have emphasized structural and organizational contributors to violence, this study adds a behavioral dimension by demonstrating that the public does not perceive or interpret aggression in ER areas in a uniform manner. The generational differences observed here suggest that individuals’ tolerance thresholds for confrontation may be associated with socialization experiences, communication norms, and value orientations that differ across age-defined cohorts. In practice, this means that two people witnessing the same event in an ER may evaluate its seriousness differently, with one perceiving it as an unacceptable threat and another perceiving it as a normal expression of stress, with these differences in interpretation potentially corresponding to variation in how conflict unfolds. Given the cross-sectional design of this study, however, causal conclusions cannot be drawn from these associations.

The conceptual framework proposed that attitudes toward violence may theoretically mediate the relationship between generational cohort and acceptance, consistent with the Theory of Planned Behavior [25]. However, formal mediation analysis was not conducted in this study; the empirical analysis tested only the direct association between generational cohort and acceptance of violence. The observed associations are therefore consistent with, but do not confirm, the proposed mediating role of attitudes. These findings align with prior research linking attitudinal orientations to behavioral intention in contexts of interpersonal confrontation [26], and extend this evidence base to ERs. Future research employing formal mediation testing with bootstrapped indirect effects would be required to empirically verify whether attitudes function as a mechanism in this relationship [28].

The findings also resonate with criminological research on fear of punishment and willingness to engage in harmful or rule-breaking behavior, which suggests that legal deterrence does not always function as the primary factor preventing aggression [29, 30]. Instead, moral evaluation and prior acceptance of behavior often play a stronger role in influencing whether individuals justify or participate in confrontational acts [31, 32]. When applied to ER settings, this perspective may help explain why some individuals continue retaliatory or confrontational actions inside hospitals despite the presence of legal authority, institutional rules, and visible risk. If individuals do not perceive early-stage aggression as morally wrong or socially unacceptable, they may underestimate the seriousness of their actions, thereby allowing escalation to continue within an environment where even minor conflict can endanger patients, staff, and bystanders.

A notable contribution of this study is the insight that younger generational cohorts tend to distinguish between what they perceive as “serious violence,” such as physical assault, and what they consider to be emotional confrontation or situational anger during moments of stress. While this distinction may reflect broader social changes in communication style and conflict expression, it carries important implications for hospital-safety management. In ER environments, early signs of confrontation — raised voices, hostile gestures, or group posturing — may serve as precursors to physical violence. However, if individuals within the environment interpret these acts as normal or non-threatening, opportunities for de-escalation may be missed. This gap between social interpretation and institutional risk perception highlights the potential value of prevention strategies that do not rely solely on security infrastructure but also address the underlying attitudes that normalize early-stage aggression in clinical spaces.

The Thai context further strengthens the relevance of these findings. It is important to interpret these generational differences within the specific socio-political trajectory of Thailand. Thai Baby Boomers and Generation X experienced periods of rapid economic industrialization and specific political shifts that emphasized social order and hierarchy, potentially reinforcing a lower tolerance for public disruption. In contrast, younger cohorts have been socialized during an era of digital connectivity and recent political reforms that prioritize individual expression and questioning of traditional authority. This historical divergence likely contributes to the distinct attitudinal profiles observed: older generations may view hospital disruption as a violation of established order, while younger groups may view it as a negotiated social interaction. As noted in earlier sections, ERs in Thailand sometimes function as extensions of external conflicts, particularly when injured individuals from violent altercations are brought to the hospital together with rival parties. In such situations, the ER space becomes a site where confrontation continues rather than resolves, creating heightened risk of retaliation, property damage, or fatal outcomes inside a public medical facility. The generational attitudes identified in this study may help contextualize why escalation is observed in these environments, as tolerance of confrontation among younger cohorts may interact with situational pressures to sustain conflict rather than disengage. Understanding these behavioral dynamics may provide useful insights for designing prevention measures that are sensitive not only to institutional procedures but also to the social expectations and value orientations of the populations who enter ER spaces.

From a policy and practice perspective, the findings suggest the importance of developing violence-prevention strategies that recognize generational diversity in attitudes toward aggression. Hospital-based security protocols and communication strategies may benefit from approaches that take into account how different age groups interpret confrontation. Given that the public may not consistently classify verbal abuse, threats, or intimidation (Levels 1–3) as violence, standard “Zero Tolerance” policies relying on general warning signage may have limited effectiveness. Individuals engaging in shouting or verbal hostility often do not believe they are violating safety rules because they do not equate their behavior with violence. Consequently, hospital communication strategies could consider moving beyond general warnings and clarifying expectations regarding non-physical behaviors—such as insults, shouting, and threats—to bridge the gap between public perception and institutional safety standards. Generation-responsive prevention strategies may include targeted public-communication campaigns, context-specific behavioral guidance in ER areas, and de-escalation approaches that acknowledge differing tolerance thresholds while reinforcing shared expectations of safety within medical institutions.

The findings of this study should be interpreted in light of certain limitations. First, the cross-sectional design prevents the establishment of causal relationships between generational cohorts and the acceptance of violence; directionality and causal mechanisms cannot be inferred from these data. Although the conceptual framework theoretically positioned attitudes as a mediator, formal mediation analysis was not conducted and the proposed indirect pathway remains to be tested. Second, the sampling frame focused specifically on residents living in close proximity to the target hospitals. While this approach effectively captures the primary service-user base, it may introduce selection bias. Residents living immediately next to ERs are frequently exposed to hospital-related disruptions—such as ambulance sirens, police presence, and visible conflicts—which may sensitize them to hospital incidents differently than the general population living further away. Future studies should consider a broader sampling frame to improve the generalizability of the results across the wider public. Third, the study relied on self-reported data, which may be subject to social desirability bias, although the use of hypothetical scenarios was intended to mitigate this by anchoring responses to specific situations rather than abstract moral judgments. Finally, the small effect size (R² = 0.015) indicates that generational cohort accounts for only 1.5% of variance in acceptance scores, with 98.5% remaining unexplained. Variables such as prior victimization experience, education level, income, and gender were not modeled and likely explain substantial additional variance. Future research should employ hierarchical regression or structural equation modeling to examine generational cohort alongside these factors. Additionally, the Baby Boomer subsample (n = 44) was considerably smaller than other cohorts, limiting statistical power; estimates for this group should be interpreted with particular caution.

Conclusion and policy implications

In the Thai context, with particular attention to ERs where confrontation and escalation are most likely to occur. Severity classifications of violent behavior were examined using Chi-square analysis, and acceptance of violence was assessed through regression analysis; attitudes toward violence were measured as part of the conceptual framework but were not the direct inferential outcome. While attitudes toward violence were conceptually proposed as a mediating mechanism, the empirical analysis focused on severity classification and acceptance outcomes, and mediation was not formally tested.

The findings indicate that generational cohort is associated with differences in how individuals interpret, tolerate, and evaluate aggression in hospital spaces, specifically, Generation Z and Millennials showed significantly higher acceptance of violence compared to Baby Boomers, while Generation X did not differ significantly. However, the effect size was very small (R² = 0.015), and these findings should be interpreted with appropriate caution. Although the conceptual framework theoretically positioned attitudes as a mediating variable, formal mediation analysis was not conducted in this study, and this pathway remains a proposition for future investigation. These results are consistent with the view that variation in violence acceptance in hospital environments may be associated with a combination of attitudinal, generational, and situational factors, rather than attributable to any single predictor.

The study contributes to the literature on hospital and workplace violence by extending the analytical focus beyond incident characteristics and organizational conditions to encompass the behavioral meanings that members of the public assign to aggressive actions in clinical environments. The evidence suggests that younger generational cohorts are more likely to interpret certain forms of confrontation as situational or emotionally driven rather than as unacceptable threats, while older cohorts tend to perceive the same behaviors as violations of order and safety within medical institutions. This distinction is particularly important in ER settings, where early-stage confrontation may be dismissed by some individuals as a normal emotional response, even though such behaviors may already represent critical warning signs from a risk-management perspective. Understanding these generationally patterned interpretations may provide a useful foundation for designing more responsive and socially attuned prevention strategies in healthcare systems.

The Thai ER context gives this study additional significance, as ERs in Thailand occasionally function as extensions of external conflicts in which injured individuals and rival groups enter the hospital space together, allowing confrontation to continue within a public medical facility. The findings suggest that tolerance of confrontation among younger cohorts may interact with these contextual dynamics in ways that sustain escalation rather than interrupt it, thereby increasing risk to patients, staff, and bystanders. Recognizing how social expectations and generational attitudes shape behavior in these situations may help hospitals move beyond reactive security responses and toward prevention approaches that address the underlying behavioral drivers of violence. This perspective supports the development of interventions that consider not only physical security measures but also the social and psychological conditions associated with how conflict unfolds inside ERs.

From a policy standpoint, the findings suggest that violence-prevention strategies in hospitals may benefit from incorporating a generation-responsive approach that acknowledges differences in tolerance thresholds, communication styles, and behavioral interpretations across age groups. Hospitals may benefit from designing public-facing communication and behavioral-guidance materials that clearly articulate expectations for conduct in ER areas, using language and formats appropriate for different generational audiences. Staff training in de-escalation and crisis communication can also be adapted to reflect the ways in which individuals from different age groups interpret confrontation and respond to authority during emotionally intense situations. Such measures may help reduce misalignment between public expectations and institutional safety requirements, thereby strengthening the capacity of hospitals to prevent escalation before incidents become severe.

In addition, the study underscores the importance of integrating behavioral evidence into hospital safety planning and national-level health-security policy. Policy frameworks addressing hospital violence typically focus on legal enforcement, security infrastructure, and institutional procedures, but the results of this study suggest that these measures must be complemented by strategies that address public attitudes and social meanings of violence in medical spaces. Incorporating attitudinal and generational insights into policy design can support the creation of prevention systems that are not only operationally effective but also socially informed, contextually relevant, and aligned with the lived realities of communities that interact with ER services. This alignment is especially valuable in contexts such as Thailand, where ERs operate as both clinical spaces and social environments that reflect broader patterns of community behavior and conflict.

In conclusion, this study provides empirical evidence that generational perceptions are associated with variation in public acceptance of violent behavior in hospital ERs. By linking generational cohort, attitudes toward violence, and behavioral acceptance within a hospital-specific framework, the study offers initial insight into how social and behavioral processes may to risk and safety in medical institutions. The findings suggest the potential value of more targeted, behaviorally informed, and generation-responsive approaches to violence prevention, contributing to ongoing efforts to build safer, more resilient, and more protective hospital environments for patients, healthcare workers, and the wider public.
