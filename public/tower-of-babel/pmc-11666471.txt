Cortical activations in cognitive task performance at multiple frequency bands
Authors: Zhu-Qing Gong, Xi-Nian Zuo
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractNeural oscillations are fundamental for brain function and govern various cognitive processes. Recent functional magnetic resonance imaging advances offer the opportunity to study frequency-specific properties of blood-oxygen-level-dependent oscillations at multiple frequency bands. However, most have focused on spontaneous brain activity in the resting state, leaving a gap in direct evidence regarding the specific activations of cognitive tasks across different frequency bands. We aim to address this gap by exploring the role of blood-oxygen-level-dependent oscillations across multiple frequency bands in cognitive processes. We used task-functional magnetic resonance imaging data of 339 healthy young adults from the Human Connectome Project to map the activation patterns of performing seven cognitive tasks at multiple frequency bands (ie slow-1 to slow-6). Our findings revealed that different frequency bands are associated with distinct task-activation patterns. Specifically, slow-1/2/3 oscillations primarily contribute to local sensory information processing, while slow-4 is crucial for various fundamental cognitive functions. Slow-5 is involved in cognitive processes that require greater memory load, integrated cognitive processing, and attention maintenance. This underscores the importance of analyzing a broad frequency range to capture the full spectrum of cognitive function, highlighting the diverse roles of different frequency bands in brain activity, shedding light on the underlying mechanism of brain–behavior associations.Keywords: task-fmri, frequency, neural oscillation, cognitive performance

Introduction

Brain function is based on various neural oscillations occurring at different frequencies. These oscillations govern different brain functions. Owing to their high temporal resolution, electrophysiological methods can record a wide range of high-frequency oscillations. During the past century, electrophysiological studies have characterized the frequency properties of neural oscillations. In general, oscillations in higher frequency bands predominate in local and bottom–up functions, whereas oscillations in lower frequency bands are more prevalent in long-range communications and top–down functions (Kopell et al. 2000; Buzsaki and Draguhn 2004). For example, sensory stimuli initially evoke higher-frequency gamma oscillations, followed by the observation of lower-frequency beta oscillations (Kopell et al. 2000). Similarly, in visual processing, local activities engage gamma oscillations, cross-cortex semantic processing engages beta oscillations, and long-range connections involve oscillations in a lower frequency range (theta or alpha) (von Stein and Sarnthein 2000). These phenomena reflect the temporal structure involved in the processing of external stimuli. Higher-frequency oscillations mediate stimuli detection and local primary processing, and information transitions to lower-frequency oscillations for further long-range integrative processing. The temporal structure of neural oscillations has significant implications for biological processes. A fast response is crucial for detecting external stimuli, especially dangerous signals. High-frequency oscillations that rapidly transmit information could satisfy this demand. In contrast, higher-order cognitive processing, such as learning, requires a longer processing window, facilitated by low-frequency oscillations. Additionally, the white matter structure provides the physiological basis for this frequency structure (Aboitiz 1992). The axons of the sensory and motor cortex are thick and well myelinated, allowing them to transfer neural activity very quickly. Associated regions and distant brain areas are connected by thin and sparsely myelinated axons that transfer information more slowly. This physiological basis ensures that the frequency characteristics of neural oscillations remain consistent regardless of measuring techniques. Therefore, neural oscillations recorded by other methods, such as functional magnetic resonance imaging (fMRI), are expected to exhibit frequency-specific properties and functions.

Although electrophysiological methods have high temporal resolution, their spatial resolution is not sufficient to map the distribution of neural oscillations across the brain. fMRI, which measures neural activity through high-spatial-resolution blood-oxygen-level-dependent (BOLD) signals, could fill this gap. Owing to the limited sampling rate and signal-to-noise ratio of early magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) devices, early resting-state fMRI studies focused only on a single frequency range (approximately 0.01 to 0.1 Hz), and most subsequent fMRI studies also used this conventional frequency range, thus ignoring the frequency information of the BOLD signal (Biswal et al. 1995). With the improved performance of MRI techniques, BOLD oscillations recorded with fast-sampling sequences can cover up to six frequency bands (eg slow-1, slow-2, … slow-6), making fMRI an excellent instrument for measuring the precise cortical distribution of neural oscillations across different frequencies. In the last decade, an increasing number of researchers have begun to investigate the frequency characteristics of BOLD oscillations. On one hand, the scale-free properties of BOLD signals have been extensively observed and analyzed (Klar et al. 2023a, Klar et al. 2023b). The power-law exponent (PLE) of BOLD signals exhibits a core-periphery pattern, being higher in higher-order association areas (core regions) compared to primary areas (periphery regions). Moreover, the PLE decreases with a reduction in consciousness levels and increases during task-related state compared to resting state. On the other hand, the activity characteristics of BOLD signals across different frequency bands have also been investigated. Both regional and network-level metrics commonly used in traditional single-frequency fMRI studies show frequency dependency (Xue et al. 2014; Jamadar et al. 2018; Li et al. 2018; Ries et al. 2018; Ma et al. 2021). Low-frequency BOLD oscillations are more active in high-order association areas, and high-frequency BOLD oscillations are more active in primary sensory and motor regions (Baria et al. 2011; Gong et al. 2021). At low frequencies, BOLD oscillations demonstrate more and stronger long-range connections, and networks exhibit greater global efficiency (Yan et al. 2009;Thompson and Fransson 2015; Park et al. 2019). However, at higher bands, connections are more localized and exhibit greater network efficiency (Thompson and Fransson 2015; Park et al. 2019). These findings indicate that BOLD oscillations have similar characteristics to those of electrophysiological oscillations, attributable to the same physiological entity of neural oscillations being recorded by different techniques. Besides these findings, recent theories have further clarified the crucial role of different timescales/frequencies in consciousness, underscoring the necessity of investigating BOLD oscillations across multiple frequency bands (Northoff 2017; Northoff and Zilio 2022a, Northoff and Zilio 2022b). The integrated information theory (IIT) and temporo-spatial theory of consciousness (TTC) demonstrate that short-term (high frequency) and long-term (low frequency) timescales are both vital for the formation of consciousness. IIT focuses on short-term integration, while TTC emphasizes the role of nonadditive integration of spontaneous brain activity and stimuli across longer timescales, maintaining the continuity of the stream of consciousness. This suggests that BOLD oscillations at different frequencies may play distinct roles in consciousness, making it essential to determine the cognitive functions of each BOLD frequency band.

There is still a missing link between the frequency characteristics of BOLD oscillations and the cognitive functions involved with each frequency band of BOLD oscillations. How BOLD oscillations are organized in the frequency domain and how BOLD oscillations in different frequency bands are activated during cognitive processes remain elusive. In an attempt to fill this gap, we recently investigated the frequency hierarchy of the six frequency bands of intrinsic BOLD oscillations (Gong and Zuo 2023). We observed that different brain networks demonstrate the highest integration level in different frequency bands, indicating that brain networks integrate information at different speeds. Generally, the primary sensory and motor regions integrate information more quickly than association regions. Furthermore, meta-analytic decoding revealed that from slow-1 to slow-6, cognitive functions associated with brain regions showing the highest level of integration transitioned from primary sensory and perception functions to more complex cognitive processing. These findings preliminarily reveal the brain functions embedded in the intrinsic BOLD oscillations. The next step is to provide concrete evidence to demonstrate the functions involved in each frequency band. Task-fMRI is a direct method for locating brain regions activated by cognitive tasks. By analyzing task activation across multiple frequency bands, it is also possible to locate the frequency bands activated by tasks. However, like most resting-state fMRI studies, previous task-fMRI studies were conducted in a single frequency range; thus, we cannot infer which frequency band brain regions are activated. In the present study, we plan to perform multiband task activation analysis on seven tasks to provide substantial evidence of the functions involved in different frequency bands. According to findings from multiband resting-state studies, we aim to test the following hypotheses. First, cognitive processes are coordinated by oscillations of multiple frequency bands; thus, each task will activate brain regions in more than one band. Second, each band governs distinct functions; thus, for a single task, the spatial activation patterns differ across frequency bands. The stimuli in each task may activate sensory regions in higher frequency bands, while further task processing may activate regions in lower frequency bands. Third, task processing that requires more cognitive load will activate lower bands than tasks that require less cognitive load.

Methods

Data acquisition and preprocessing

We used task-fMRI data from the Human Connectome Project (HCP) S1200 young adult dataset in this study because the HCP data were acquired with high temporal resolution and the task-fMRI data included seven tasks; thus, we could explore the distribution of multiple functions in multiple frequency bands. We included data from 339 unrelated participants in our analysis. All participants completed seven task-state scans, including working memory (N-back task), incentive processing (gambling task), motor mapping, language processing (story task), social cognition (theory of mind [ToM] task), relational processing (dimensional change detection task), and emotion processing (Hariri task). All task scans were block-designed, and each task scan comprised two runs. Details of the task paradigms and scan parameters are described in (Barch et al. 2013). The data were preprocessed according to the HCP minimal preprocessing pipeline with the Independent Component Analysis with FMRIB’s ICA-based X-noiseifier (ICA-FIX) denoising procedure (Glasser et al. 2013). The preprocessing procedures included (i) gradient distortion correction; (ii) motion correction; (iii) EPI image distortion correction; (iv) registration to the T1w image; (v) one-step spline resampling; (vi) intensity normalization and brain masking; (vii) transfer of volume-based time series into surface-based time series; and (vii) ICA-FIX denoising. Global regression and bandpass filtering were not included in the preprocessing procedures. After preprocessing, the cortical gray matter signals were projected onto the standard 32k-grayordinates surface space with about 30k vertices for each hemisphere for subsequent task-activation analysis.

Frequency decomposing

The frequency band distribution of neural oscillations adheres to the natural logarithm linear law (N3L) (Penttonen and Buzsáki 2003). The N3L was initially proposed based on findings from electrophysiological experiments. Since neural oscillations measured by different instruments are related to the same physical entity—the brain structure—and constitute a complex system that exhibits self-similarity, their frequency specificity is independent of the measurement instruments. Therefore, the N3L provides theoretical guidance for the band decomposition of BOLD oscillations. According to N3L, neural oscillations can be divided into distinct, nonoverlapping frequency bands that are continuously arranged along the frequency axis. Neural oscillations recorded by electrophysiological instruments primarily encompass frequency bands greater than 1 Hz on the frequency axis, such as gamma and beta waves. In contrast, neural oscillations measured by fMRI primarily cover slower bands on the frequency axis, adjacent to and slower than delta waves. On the natural logarithmic frequency axis, the center of each neural frequency band falls at an integer point, and the distance between adjacent band centers is equal to 1. This provides a computational method for the band decomposition of sampled neural signals. First, convert the highest- and lowest-frequency boundary values of the sampled signal to the natural logarithmic frequency axis. Each integer point within this frequency range represents the center of a frequency band. Adding and subtracting 0.5 from each integer provides the frequency boundaries for each band on the natural logarithmic axis. Converting the frequency boundary values back to the real number frequency axis yields the boundaries of the frequency bands for bandpass filtering.

The N3L provides the theoretical frequency boundaries for each neural oscillation frequency band. Neural oscillations collected by various instruments are discrete signals. Therefore, in practice, the specific number of segmented frequency bands and the actual values of band boundaries are determined by the scanning parameters. According to the Nyquist sampling theorem, the sampling time and scan duration determine the detectable frequency boundaries of the original signal, as well as the frequency resolution, which is related to the specific frequency boundaries of each decomposable frequency band. We then calculated the number of decomposable frequency bands and their frequency boundaries based on the N3L and the Nyquist sampling theorem. Specifically, the frequency boundary values of the original data obtained in the previous step were converted into natural logarithmic values. On the natural logarithm axis, each integer point covered by the upper and lower frequency boundaries of the original data represents the frequency center point of each decomposable subfrequency band, with each integer plus or minus 0.5 representing the frequency boundary values of each subfrequency band. In this way, we obtain the number of subfrequency bands and their theoretical frequency boundary values. By calculating the minimum frequency resolution of the original data based on the Nyquist sampling theorem, we found the points on the real number axis that are closest to these values to obtain the actual frequency boundary values of each subfrequency band. Finally, we filtered each frequency band from the preprocessed BOLD signal according to its frequency boundaries. Each piece of task-fMRI data was decomposed into several sub-band data. We also filtered the fMRI data of the traditional frequency band (0.01 to 0.08 Hz) and performed a task activation analysis to compare with the results of multiband task activation (Figs. S1–S7). We utilized the decoding rhythms of the brain system (DREAM) toolbox to perform calculations and bandpass filtering on the task fMRI data (Gong et al. 2021).

Task activation analysis

All tasks contained at least two task conditions, usually an experimental condition (eg the face matching condition in the emotion processing task) and a control condition (eg the shape matching condition in the emotion processing task). To fully characterize the activation patterns of each frequency band under different experimental conditions, we analyzed not only the contrasts between the experimental condition and the control condition but also those between all conditions and the baseline. For each run of each task, we modeled a predictor for every condition. The predictors were then convolved with the hemodynamic response function to produce model regressors used for activation estimates. Activation estimates were calculated using a general linear model (GLM) at the vertex-wise level. Specifically, for the working memory task, linear contrasts of activation effects were estimated for 2-back vs. baseline, 0-back vs. baseline, and 2-back vs. 0-back. For the language task, linear contrasts were estimated for story vs. baseline, math vs. baseline, and story vs. math. For the relational processing task, linear contrasts of relational processing vs. baseline, match vs. baseline, and relational processing vs. match were estimated. For the emotion processing task, linear contrasts of activation effects between story vs. baseline, math vs. baseline, and story vs. math were estimated. In the gambling task, linear contrasts between negative vs. baseline, positive vs. baseline, and negative vs. positive were estimated. In the motor task, activation effects for linear contrasts of tongue vs. baseline, left hand vs. baseline, left foot vs. baseline, right hand vs. baseline, right foot vs. baseline, and visual cue vs. baseline were estimated. For the social processing task, linear contrasts of ToM vs. baseline, random vs. baseline, and ToM vs. random were estimated. At the participant level, for each run of each task, the GLM analysis was performed for every frequency band using the FSL (Jenkinson et al. 2012). Then, on the two runs of each task, fixed-effects analyses were performed using FSL’s FMRI Expert Analysis Tool to estimate the average effects across runs for each frequency band within each participant. At the group level, for each frequency band of each task, mixed-effects analyses were performed using the FMRIB’s Local Analysis of Mixed Effects to estimate the average activation effects across participants. The t-test was performed for the activation effects at the vertex-wise level, followed by the t-to-P-to-z transformation. Finally, a group-level activation z-map was generated for every contrast of each task within each frequency band. We then performed a Bonferroni correction for multiple comparisons on each group map to generate activation clusters. Group maps were visualized using Connectome Workbench with a threshold of z = 4.873 (Bonferroni-corrected, P < 0.05, two-sided).

Task-specific multiband activation across brain networks

To investigate task-specific activation patterns across brain networks and frequency bands, we extracted the activation z-maps for group-level task contrasts from the previous task activation analysis, focusing on eight brain networks: auditory, visual, dorsal attention, ventral attention, somatomotor, control, language, and default mode networks. Then, we visualized the task-specific activation for each brain network and task to assess how the activation strength varied in each network of each frequency band in different tasks. For all tasks except the motor task, we used the contrast between experimental and control conditions (eg “2-back vs. 0-back” for the working memory task). In the motor task, due to the absence of a control condition, we used the contrast “tongue vs. baseline.”

Correlation between task activation and behavioral measures

To examine the relationship between task activation and behavioral performance, we performed vertex-wise Pearson correlations between the activation z-values for each task contrast (excluding the motor task, as no behavioral measures were collected) and the corresponding behavioral measures for each task in each frequency band. Specifically, for the working memory and relational processing tasks, we used reaction time (RT) and accuracy (ACC). For the language task, we included both math and story levels as well as RT. For emotion processing, gambling, and social cognition tasks, RT was used as a behavioral measure.

Results

Frequency decomposition

According to the scanning parameters, DREAM decomposed the BOLD oscillations of each task into bands ranging from slow-1 to slow-5. The sampling rate of all tasks was 0.72 s, so the highest frequency bands covered part of the slow-1 band. Since the scanning durations of all tasks differed, the lowest detectable frequencies varied among tasks. Notably, according to Nyquist’s theorem, the lowest frequency band of three tasks (the working memory task, language task, and motor task) could reach the slow-5 band. However, to guarantee the stationarity of the lowest frequency band, the DREAM toolbox discarded the four lowest frequency bins. After this adjustment, only the working memory task covered the slow-5 band. To reveal more comprehensive multiband activation characteristics, we still analyzed the slow-5 data for the language and motor tasks. Working memory

The working memory task data could be decomposed into five frequency bands: from slow-1 to slow-5. The working memory task contained two conditions: 0-back and 2-back. Both conditions’ activation patterns showed spatial separation across frequency bands (Fig. 1). In the slow-1 to slow-3 bands, the 0-back and 2-back conditions both activated the visual cortex. In slow-1, the activation region was constrained in the primary visual cortex. In slow-2, the activation area extended and covered the secondary visual cortex. In slow-3, the regions activated were concentrated in the ventral visual cortex. The activation of the visual cortex in these high-frequency bands is consistent with our hypothesis that the detection and local processing of sensory stimuli occurs in the high-frequency bands. In the slow-4 band, both conditions activated large areas in the visual cortex and the association cortices including the middle frontal gyrus (control network), the anterior insula (ventral attention network), the dorsal anterior cingulate cortex (ventral attention network), the superior parietal lobule (dorsal attention network), and the middle temporal gyrus (language network). The 0-back condition exhibited greater spatial extension than the 2-back condition in this band, especially in the occipital and temporal lobes. In the slow-5 band, the activation patterns of the two conditions were similar to those in the slow-4 band but with greater spatial extension in the frontal and parietal lobes and smaller activation areas in the occipital and temporal lobes. Although the activation patterns of the 0-back and 2-back conditions were similar across frequency bands, the contrast between conditions revealed that the higher memory-load function might be processed in the slow-5 band. The 2-back vs. 0-back contrast showed significant activation only in the slow-5 band. The activated areas were mainly localized in the control network, the ventral attention network, and the medial occipital cortex. Meanwhile, the 0-back vs. 2-back condition significantly activated the slow-3 to slow-5 bands. In particular, in the slow-4 band, the activation was the most robust and widespread across the cortex. The contrast in activation between the two conditions implied that slow-4 was more involved in basic cognitive processing with low memory load, while slow-5 was more involved with complex cognitive processing with high memory load.

Language

The language task contained two conditions: math and story. Auditory stimuli were used in both task conditions. The BOLD oscillations of the language task were decomposed into slow-1 to slow-5 frequency bands. The multiband activation patterns are shown in Fig. 2. In the slow-1 and slow-2 bands, both conditions elicited significant activation only in the superior temporal cortex. In slow-4, in addition to robust activation in the auditory cortex, both conditions also activated regions in the lateral frontal cortex (the control network), the dorsal anterior cingulate cortex (the ventral attention network), and regions in the visual network. In slow-5, the activation areas expanded for both conditions, indicating more brain regions participated in information processing. It can be inferred that the local processing of auditory stimuli occurred in high-frequency bands (slow-1 and slow-2), while further integration occurred in the lower band (slow-4 and slow-5). The story vs. math contrast activated the bilateral inferior parietal lobule (the default mode network), the superior frontal gyrus (the control network), and the medial prefrontal cortex (the default mode network) in the slow-3 band and regions in the default mode network, the language network, the somatomotor network, and the visual network in slow-4 and slow-5. Therefore, we infer that semantic processing mainly involves the slow-3 to slow-5 band. Particularly, the activation in multiple primary sensory cortices in slow-4 and slow-5 indicates that deep semantic comprehension emerges in lower frequency bands and triggers sensory representations (such as visual representations related to the content of the story) (Deniz et al. 2023). The math vs. story contrast only elicited significant activation in the slow-4 and slow-5 bands. In slow-4, the activation regions were localized in the control network and the dorsal attention network, especially the lateral side of the cortex. In slow-5, activation regions in the control network expanded, and large regions in the ventral attention network and medial visual network were activated. Therefore, we infer that arithmetic function is mainly associated with the slow-4 and slow-5 bands.

Relational processing

The relational processing task data were decomposed into four frequency bands (slow-1 to slow-4). The task contained two conditions: a relational processing condition and a control match condition. According to previous single-frequency range studies, the relational vs. match condition can reliably activate the anterior prefrontal cortex. The multiple-band activation results showed that the relational vs. match condition significantly activated large areas in the anterior prefrontal cortex only in the slow-4 band (Fig. 3). Thus, relational processing may occur in the slow-4 band. In addition, both conditions activated the visual cortex and the intraparietal sulcus in the slow-3 band. The match vs. relational condition activated sparse regions in the posterior parietal cortex (the control network) in slow-4.

Emotion processing

This task contained a face-matching condition and a control condition of shape-matching. The emotion processing task data were decomposed into four frequency bands (slow-1 to slow-4). The multiband activation patterns are shown in Fig. 4. The slow-1 band did not show any significant activation. In the slow-2 band, both the face condition and the face vs. shape condition activated small regions in the primary visual cortex. In slow-3, both conditions activated the occipital cortex (the visual network) and the superior parietal lobule (the dorsal attention network). The face vs. shape contrast activated the ventral lateral occipital cortex, especially the right side, in the slow-3 band. In the slow-4 band, the face vs. shape contrast elicited activation in the dorsolateral prefrontal cortex (the control network and dorsal attention network), the superior parietal lobule (the dorsal attention network), and the occipital cortex (the visual network). Therefore, we infer that the processing associated with emotional faces involves BOLD oscillations in the slow-4 band. Additionally, the shape vs. face contrast activated large regions in the frontal and parietal cortex in the slow-3 band.

Gambling

This task included a reward condition and a punishment condition. The frequency range of the data spans from slow-1 to slow-4. The multiband activation patterns are shown in Fig. 5. Neither the slow-1 nor the slow-2 showed significant activation. In the slow-3 band, the punishment and reward conditions both activated small regions in the primary visual cortex. In addition, the reward condition also activated the left angular gyrus and small regions in the right somatosensory cortex. The reward vs. punishment contrast activated small regions in the bilateral primary visual cortex and the right somatosensory cortex. In the slow-4 band, both the reward and punishment conditions activated the ventrolateral occipital lobe, especially the right hemisphere. In addition, the punishment vs. reward condition activated the bilateral anterior insula (the control network and the ventral attention network), middle frontal cortex (the control network), superior parietal lobule (the dorsal attention network), dorsal anterior cingulate cortex (the control network), dorsolateral and ventromedial occipital cortex (the visual network), the left premotor cortex (dorsal attention network), and the left sensorimotor cortex (somatomotor network). The reward vs. punishment contrast only activated a small region in the primary visual cortex in slow-4. Psychological research has shown that negative information and negative emotions tend to consume more cognitive resources and attention (Wegner 1994; Ito et al. 1998). Based on these findings, we propose that a possible explanation for our results is that negative emotions triggered by the punishment condition may lead to increased consumption of low-frequency cognitive and attentional resources during the performance of the same cognitive tasks.

Motor

This task contained five body-part movement conditions: tongue, left hand, right hand, left foot, and right foot. In addition to the motor conditions, we analyzed the visual cue period, which was a 3-s duration of cue words prior to each movement block. The motor task timeseries was decomposed into five frequency bands (slow-1 to slow-5). The multiband activation patterns are shown in Fig. 6. No significant activations were found for the slow-1 or slow-2 bands. The visual cue elicited significant activations in the occipital cortex (visual network), the dorsal cingulate cortex (ventral attention network), and the left premotor cortex (dorsal attention network) in the slow-3 band and in extensive brain regions other than the default mode network, the inferior and middle frontal gyrus, and parts of the motor cortex in the slow-4 band. In slow-3, the average of motor conditions activated the right angular gyrus (default mode network), bilateral medial prefrontal cortex (default mode network), and posterior medial occipital cortex (visual network). In slow-4, the motor conditions separately activated the motor cortex corresponding to the body parts in motion. The tongue condition activated the bilateral tongue regions in the sensorimotor cortex. The left- and right-foot conditions activated the contralateral foot regions in the sensorimotor cortex. The left- and right-hand conditions activated the contralateral hand regions in the sensorimotor cortex. In slow-5, the visual cue activated large areas outside the sensorimotor cortex, including the dorsal and ventral attention networks, the anterior insula (ventral attention network), the posterior temporal lobe (ventral and dorsal attention networks), the dorsal lateral prefrontal lobe (control network), and the medial visual network. In addition to activating the same regions as the visual cue, the motor conditions activated the sensorimotor cortex corresponding to the body parts in motion.

Social cognition

The social cognition task included a ToM condition and a random control condition. The data from this task were decomposed into four frequency bands: slow-1 to slow-4. The activation patterns across these frequency bands are depicted in Fig. 7. No significant activations were observed in the slow-1 or slow-2 bands for either condition. In the slow-3 band, both the ToM and random conditions resulted in activations of the lateral occipital cortex. In the slow-4 band, the ToM condition exhibited significant activations in the lateral occipital cortex (visual network), superior temporal sulcus (language network), superior parietal lobule (dorsal attention network), dorsal cingulate cortex (ventral attention network and control network), and anterior insula (ventral attention network), as well as regions in the somatomotor network. Additionally, the random condition and the comparison between the random and ToM conditions activated widespread regions, with the most robust activations observed in the visual network.

Task-specific multiband activation across brain networks

Figures 8 and 9 and Figs. S8–S10 illustrate the distribution of activation across brain networks for all tasks in each frequency band. For all tasks except the motor task, we plotted the contrast between the experimental and control conditions. Specifically, for the working memory task, the activation z-values were based on the “2-back vs. 0-back” contrast; for the language task, on the “story vs. math” contrast; for the relational processing task, on the “relation vs. match” contrast; for the emotion processing task, on the “face vs. shape” contrast; for the gambling task, on the “punish vs. reward” contrast; and for the social cognition task, on the “ToM vs. random” contrast. Since activation patterns for different body parts were similar in most networks, the “tongue vs. baseline” contrast was used for the motor task. Activation varied between both tasks and between frequency bands for each network. For example, in the language task, the activation levels for the story and math conditions were similar in the control, auditory, and language networks in slow-4. However, in slow-5, the math condition showed greater activation in the control network, while the story condition elicited more activation in the auditory and language networks. Correlation between behavioral performance

The results showed that the distribution of the correlation between the z-values of task activation in the high-frequency bands and behavioral measures was random, indicating that there was no simple linear relationship. In contrast, the spatial distribution of the correlation coefficients in the low-frequency bands (slow-4 and slow-5) exhibited a more systematic and regular pattern (Figs. 10–13, Figs. S11–S14). For the task of working memory and emotion processing task, activation levels showed a strong positive correlation with accuracy and task difficulty, while the correlation with reaction time was weaker. Furthermore, the correlation between activation in the slow-5 band and reaction time was stronger than that in the slow-4 band. For exploratory analysis, we did not apply thresholding or multiple comparison correction on the correlation maps to display the whole-brain distribution pattern of correlation coefficients.

Discussion

We examined the activation patterns of several cognitive tasks across distinct frequency bands. Each task elicited activations in multiple frequency bands, indicating the involvement of multiple BOLD oscillation frequencies during task processing. The spatial activation patterns for each task exhibited variations among frequency bands, particularly between higher frequencies (slow-1 to slow-3) and lower frequencies (slow-4 and slow-5). This observation suggests that different frequency bands contribute uniquely to information processing and provides preliminary evidence that BOLD oscillations in distinct frequency bands govern specific functions.

Slow-1 to slow-3: detection and preliminary processing of visual and auditory information

Six out of the seven task paradigms analyzed in this study (working memory, gambling, motor, social cognition, relational processing, and emotion processing) used visual stimuli, while the language task used auditory stimuli. Analysis of multiband activation revealed that brain activity triggered by these stimuli predominantly occurred in the slow-1 to slow-3 frequency bands. In these high-frequency bands, visual stimuli activated the visual cortex, whereas auditory stimuli activated the auditory cortex, indicating that activation in higher-frequency bands is largely determined by the physical properties of the stimuli. This is consistent with EEG findings showing that both visual and auditory stimuli can induce high-frequency activity in the sensory cortex (Tallon-Baudry and Bertrand 1999; Crone et al. 2001). The sensory cortex, distinguished by thicker and more heavily myelinated white matter fibers compared to association regions, supports faster transmission rates, facilitating higher frequency activity (Aboitiz 1992). This aligns with our observation that task stimuli predominantly activate sensory areas in higher frequency bands.

In the slow-1 and slow-2 bands, visual and auditory stimuli exclusively activated their respective sensory cortices, suggesting that initial sensory processing is confined to these areas. In the slow-3 band, in addition to activation in the sensory cortex, certain tasks also elicited activation in regions within the dorsal or ventral attention networks, indicating further integration of sensory information at this frequency. For example, in the relational processing task, both conditions activated the visual cortex and the intraparietal sulcus, which are involved in visual perception, attention, and visuospatial working memory (Grefkes and Fink 2005; Artemenko et al. 2020). In the motor task, visual cues triggered activation not only in the visual cortex but also in the anterior dorsal cingulate cortex and the left premotor cortex within the slow-3 band. The premotor cortex projects directly to the spinal cord and plays a role in motion-related functions and movement planning (Cisek and Kalaska 2005; Rizzolatti and Sinigaglia 2010), suggesting that visual cues undergo semantic processing and synchronize with motor areas for motor preparation in this frequency range. This perspective is supported by auditory perception research (Frühholz et al. 2020), which identified distinct components in the auditory cortex during a task. In the slow-1 and slow-2 bands, a component emerged immediately poststimulus, showing no preference for vocal or nonvocal stimuli, while in the slow-3 band, a later component demonstrated a significant preference for vocal stimuli. This timing may reflect the downward flow of information from higher frequencies, with the vocal preference in slow-3 suggesting more integrated auditory processing. Additionally, the language task revealed slow-3 band activation in the angular gyrus during the story vs. math condition, indicating that preliminary semantic processing may involve slow-3 oscillations. Resting-state BOLD oscillation patterns further highlight the role of higher frequencies in sensory detection and preliminary processing. The salience network exhibits the highest power in high-frequency bands, while auditory and visual cortices show elevated amplitude of low-frequency fluctuation (ALFF) values in these bands (Gohel and Biswal 2015; Gong et al. 2021). In addition, sensory cortices demonstrate the greatest integration within the slow-1 to slow-3 bands (Gong and Zuo 2023), suggesting that regions critical for salience detection and sensory processing are most active in higher frequencies, even at rest.

In summary, the slow-1 to slow-3 bands contribute to preliminary sensory processing with a hierarchical structure: The slow-1 and slow-2 bands are responsible for the detection and local processing of sensory stimuli, while the slow-3 band facilitates integrative sensory processing across brain regions.

Slow-4: motor function, basic cognition, and modular segregation

During the resting state, most networks, excluding the default mode network and the visual network, exhibit higher power values in the slow-4 band compared to other frequency bands (Gohel and Biswal 2015). The basal ganglia, which is critical for motor functions, also demonstrates the highest ALFF values in this frequency band (Zuo et al. 2010). Additionally, networks within the slow-4 band show increased segregation and efficiency alongside reduced global integration, suggesting that slow-4 oscillations facilitate modular organization for fundamental or localized functions (Thompson and Fransson 2015). Therefore, oscillations in the slow-4 band may be responsible for various fundamental functions, or functions with low demand for global integration, by facilitating modular segregation.

This perspective is supported by the activation patterns observed in multiple tasks within the slow-4 band. In the motor task, specific motor conditions exclusively activated the corresponding motor cortices within the slow-4 band, suggesting that individual motor control is governed by slow-4 oscillations. In the working memory task, refined activation patterns were observed in the slow-4 band, whereas broader and less specific activation appeared in the slow-5 band. Both 0-back and 2-back conditions engaged the occipital cortex, the posterior middle frontal gyrus (control network), the superior parietal lobule, the intraparietal sulcus (dorsal attention network), and the middle temporal gyrus (language network). The middle frontal cortex, superior parietal lobule, and intraparietal sulcus are brain regions associated with cognitive control (Ansari and Karmiloff-Smith 2002; Babiloni et al. 2005; Fox et al. 2006; Koenigs et al. 2009). The concurrent activation of these brain regions suggests that working memory processes are predominantly mediated in the slow-4 band. Moreover, the 0-back vs. 2-back condition showed widespread activation in the slow-4 band, indicating this frequency band is more involved in simple or basic cognitive processing.

In the language processing task, the contrast between math and story conditions showed significant activation in the slow-4 band. The math condition predominantly activated brain regions related to cognitive control, such as the dorsal lateral prefrontal cortex, posterior middle frontal gyrus, precentral gyrus, supramarginal gyrus, intraparietal sulcus, and regions in the temporal lobe. In contrast, the story condition primarily activated brain regions associated with syntax and semantics, the sensory cortex, and the default mode network. The activation of the sensory cortex and default mode network may indicate that during story comprehension, participants generated sensory representations and recalled self-related memories or experiences (Deniz et al. 2023; Fernandino and Binder 2024). The contrast activation between the math and story conditions was more refined in slow-4 than in slow-5, indicating that the elementary processes of arithmetic and language comprehension are mainly mediated by slow-4 oscillations.

In the social cognition task, the social interaction video elicited activation in the superior temporal sulcus, a region related to the theory of mind, suggesting that social cognition is associated with slow-4 oscillations. In the relational processing task, the relational versus match condition activated regions in the lateral prefrontal cortex, inferior parietal lobule, right inferior temporal gyrus, and occipital cortex. Overall, the activation patterns in the relational processing, n-back, and math conditions are similar within the slow-4 band. These cognitive tasks all elicited activation in the lateral prefrontal cortex and lateral parietal cortex, consistent with previous findings highlighting the crucial roles of these regions in cognition. There are also variations in the precise location of these activation areas across tasks in this band, reflecting the modular segregation of functional organization during different cognitive tasks.

Based on the activation patterns of performing these tasks, it can be inferred that slow-4 is the key to intelligence. The BOLD oscillations within this frequency band play a crucial role in various cognitive functions, somatic motor function, and social cognitive function. This inference is also evident from its role in white matter signal transmission. A study identified two frequency spectrum patterns in white matter voxels: one with a single peak near 0.01 Hz (slow-5/slow-6 boundary) and another with dual peaks, including a second peak near 0.06 Hz (slow-4 midpoint) (Li et al. 2021). The white matter complexity of voxels with dual peaks is higher, indicating the presence of different types of white matter fibers within these voxels: fibers with a signal transmission frequency of approximately 0.01 Hz and fibers with a frequency of approximately 0.06 Hz. The study further found that the power ratio of the two peaks (peak 2/peak 1) in dual-peak white matter voxels near the frontal lobe is positively correlated with cognitive task scores. The greater power in the slow-4 band supports its role in efficient information transmission via white matter fibers and highlights its significance for various cognitive and motor functions.

Slow-5: function integration, memory load, and attention

In the working memory task, the activation patterns of the two N-back conditions in the slow-5 band are similar to those in the slow-4 band. However, the frontal and parietal lobes show a more extensive activation range in the slow-5 band, indicating broader brain region involvement in task processing at this frequency. Similarly, in the language task, the spatial activation range in the slow-5 band surpasses that of the slow-4 band, suggesting a role in more generalized and integrated cognitive processing compared to the modular segregation observed in the slow-4 band. Intrinsic brain activity mirrors this pattern, with networks densely connected in the slow-5 band and segregated in the slow-4 band (Thompson and Fransson 2015).

The slow-5 oscillations are also important for cognitive processes that require a high memory load. In the working memory task, the 2-back vs. 0-back contrast showed significant activation only in the slow-5 band. The activated regions included the lateral prefrontal cortex, dorsal anterior cingulate cortex, precuneus, inferior parietal lobule, and medial occipital cortex. These activation regions, except for the precuneus, are similar to the activation regions observed in both conditions in the slow-4 band. This suggests that brain regions associated with working memory provide more computing resources for high-memory load tasks in the slow-5 band. The precuneus, associated with memory and inhibitory responses in cognitive control (Boruchow and Hutchins 1991; Lundstrom et al. 2005), was specifically activated in the slow-5 band during the 2-back condition and the 2-back vs. 0-back contrast. This finding, consistent with a study identifying the precuneus-centered parietal memory network specifically in the slow-5 band (Luo et al. 2021), supports the role of slow-5 oscillations in memory function. Overall, the activation patterns observed in the working memory task suggest that both the slow-4 and slow-5 bands play important roles in cognitive processing. The slow-4 band shows more modular segregation, while the slow-5 band exhibits more integrated and generalized activation patterns. We infer that slow-4 is more associated with elementary cognitive processing, while slow-5 oscillations are more involved in high memory load processing. The specific activation of the precuneus in the slow-5 band further highlights the involvement of this band in memory-related processes.

Attention maintenance may be another important function governed by slow-5 oscillations. Both the dorsal attention network and ventral attention network were prominently activated in the slow-5 band, particularly during the motor task. Unlike cognitive processing conditions, which exhibit similar spatial activation patterns in slow-4 and slow-5, all motor conditions showed distinct spatial activation patterns between the two bands. In slow-5, the motor conditions, as well as the visual cue, elicited large activations in the dorsal and ventral attention networks. This suggests that the attention-regulation function required to maintain task processes may be carried out through low-frequency oscillations. These findings align with resting-state meta-decoding results, showing that regions with the highest integration levels in the slow-5 band are linked to attention function (Gong and Zuo 2023).

Integration of multiband BOLD oscillations in cognitive processing

Different BOLD frequency bands have distinct functional roles in cognitive processing, yet they collaborate dynamically to achieve complex tasks. High-frequency bands (slow-1 to slow-3) primarily govern sensory detection and preliminary processing, while lower-frequency bands (slow-4 and slow-5) are involved in modular organization, integration, and higher-order functions. This interplay creates a hierarchical yet interconnected system for efficient cognitive functioning.

Taking working memory as an example, we observed a clear division of labor across frequency bands. In the slow-1 to slow-3 bands, both 0-back and 2-back conditions activated the visual cortex, reflecting rapid detection and localized processing of sensory stimuli. The transition from the primary visual cortex (slow-1) to the secondary visual cortex (slow-2) and ventral visual cortex (slow-3) illustrates a progression in sensory integration. In the slow-4 band, activation expanded to association cortices, including regions involved in attention and cognitive control such as the middle frontal gyrus and superior parietal lobule. This broader activation supports the integration of sensory and cognitive components, with the 0-back condition showing more extensive engagement of sensory regions, consistent with its lower memory load. The slow-5 band demonstrated a shift toward frontal and parietal regions critical for memory and high-level cognitive integration. Notably, the 2-back condition, with its higher memory demand, uniquely activated control and attention networks in this band. This highlights the role of slow-5 oscillations in managing complex tasks that require greater global integration and resource allocation.

In summary, the interplay of multiple BOLD frequency bands reflects a sophisticated system where distinct oscillatory patterns work in concert to support cognitive processing. Each band contributes uniquely, yet their coordination ensures seamless integration of sensory, attentional, and cognitive functions, highlighting the brain’s remarkable adaptability and complexity.

Limitations and future directions

This study demonstrated that BOLD oscillations across multiple frequency bands contribute to task processing and exhibit distinct functional roles. However, due to the short scanning durations (mostly less than 5 min), the slow-6 band could not be examined, and not all tasks covered the slow-5 band. Future studies should consider longer scanning times to include more low-frequency ranges. Multiband activation maps were generated using a conventional sustained response model, which may not capture transient high-frequency oscillations that do not persist throughout the block. Previous studies using model-free analysis have shown that simple tasks can activate the whole brain (Gonzalez-Castillo et al. 2012). Future studies should use more flexible methods to explore multiband oscillatory mechanisms, particularly at higher frequencies.

In this study, we focused solely on exploring the functions of multiband BOLD oscillations in the cortex. Recent research has also revealed meaningful BOLD signals in white matter, suggesting that exploring the functions of multiband BOLD oscillations in white matter could provide a more complete picture of multiband oscillatory functions in the brain (Ji et al. 2017; Ji et al. 2023, also see a recent review in Ji et al. 2024), a promising direction for future studies.

Previous studies have provided observations for understanding multiband activity in resting-state and clinical studies. For example, slow-4 showed greater alteration in Parkinson’s disease (PD) patients and improved discrimination between PD and healthy controls, as well as between PD patients with different symptoms (Hou et al. 2014; Hu et al. 2020; Tian et al. 2020). By harnessing the brain’s functional patterns of both spontaneous and task-induced activities as we documented (Gong and Zuo 2023; and this work) for mapping the human brain at multiple frequency bands, researchers could achieve a more comprehensive or systematic theory of functional organization in the human brain to foster future research toward a more integrative translation from bench to bedside (Liu et al. 2023; Zhou et al. 2023).
