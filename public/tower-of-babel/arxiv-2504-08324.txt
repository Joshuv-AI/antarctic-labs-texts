An Introduction to Double/Debiased Machine Learning
Achim Ahrens, Victor Chernozhukov, Christian Hansen, Damian Kozbur, Mark Schaffer, Thomas Wiemann

Abstract
This paper provides a practical introduction to Double/Debiased Machine Learning (DML). DML provides a general approach to performing inference about a target parameter in the presence of nuisance parameters. The aim of DML is to reduce the impact of nuisance parameter estimation on estimators of the parameter of interest. We describe DML and its two essential components: Neyman orthogonality and cross-fitting. We highlight that DML reduces functional form dependence and accommodates the use of complex data types, such as text data. We illustrate its application through three empirical examples that demonstrate DML’s applicability in cross-sectional and panel settings.

Introduction

Much of empirical research in economics aims to provide insights into the statistical relationship between two or multiple variables, such as understanding the causal impact of a policy on economic outcomes. Within such research, there is increasing interest in using machine learning (ML) methods to deal with complex data and/or to enhance the credibility of empirical conclusions. However, the (mis-)use of ML can lead to formally invalid and practically misleading inferential statements. Addressing this issue is the topic of a large and rapidly growing literature in statistics and econometrics that builds from classic ideas in semiparametric and nonparametric estimation. One method that provides a solution in a wide variety of empirical settings and that is the topic of this review is double/debiased machine learning
(henceforth DML;
Chernozhukov et al. 2018
)
.

Within this review, we focus on settings where there is a user-specified target parameter
\theta_{0}
defined as the solution to a moment condition

\displaystyle\mathrm{E}[m(W,\theta_{0},\eta_{0})]=0

(1.1)

where
m
is a user-specified moment (or score) function and
W
represents observable variables.
\eta_{0}
is a so-called nuisance (or auxiliary) parameter, which is not of direct interest but is needed for learning
\theta_{0}
. This setting applies to many practical empirical problems. For example, this structure encompasses settings where the target parameters are regression coefficients in cross-sectional or panel regressions, average treatment effects, parameters in linear instrumental variables models, local average treatment effects, dynamic treatment effects in staggered adoption designs, parameters in nonlinear structural models, and many others.

In many settings, the unknown parameter of interest
\theta_{0}
is low-dimensional and would be straightforward to estimate from the sample analogue of (
1.1
) if the nuisance parameter
\eta_{0}
were known. However, because
\eta_{0}
captures auxiliary objects such as the relation between the outcome and controls in a regression, it is often also unknown. Researchers must thus rely on auxiliary estimators
\widehat{\eta}
in place of
\eta_{0}
when estimating
\theta_{0}
. Using
\widehat{\eta}
in place of
\eta_{0}
complicates statistical learning of the target parameter,
\theta_{0}
, because estimation error in the nuisance parameter can propagate and have a large impact on the resulting plug-in estimator of
\theta_{0}
.

Complications with inference about the target parameter due to unknown nuisance parameters are exacerbated if
\eta_{0}
is
high-dimensional
(or complex)—i.e., when
\eta_{0}
itself is difficult to estimate.
1
1

1

We focus on settings where
\eta_{0}
is high-dimensional. The DML framework also applies in the presence of low-dimensional nuisance objects. The robustness to estimation of nuisance parameters carries over to this setting, though the impacts of estimation of low-dimensional nuisance parameters are generally less pronounced than in the high-dimensional case and usually can readily be accommodated through other approaches.
In applications, high-dimensional nuisance parameters can arise (1) with just a few covariates if there is no pre-specified, known parametric model for the nuisance parameter, (2) if there are many covariates even if researchers are willing to maintain known parametric functional forms, or (3) when there are many nuisance variables that may enter through unknown functions. High-dimensional nuisance parameters can thus occur both in seemingly simple applications with just a single continuous covariate as well as in applications where data may feature complicated structures such as text or images.

In settings with high-dimensional nuisance parameters, standard asymptotic approximations for statistical inference may fail due to two distinct sources of bias: regularization bias and overfitting bias. Both biases emerge when the moment equation (
1.1
) is sensitive to using an estimated nuisance parameter,
\widehat{\eta}
, instead of the true but unknown
\eta_{0}
.
Regularization bias refers to changes in plug-in estimator caused directly by estimation errors in
\widehat{\eta}
. Differences between the estimated
\widehat{\eta}
and the true
\eta_{0}
may propagate into substantial deviations in the moment equation, leading to significant changes in its solution.
Overfitting bias, on the other hand, arises more subtly. Because
\widehat{\eta}
itself depends on the data, it may be correlated with the sample
W
used to construct the moment condition. This correlation typically occurs when the same dataset is used both to estimate
\widehat{\eta}
and to evaluate the sample analog of equation (
1.1
). If this dependence becomes too strong, substituting
\widehat{\eta}
distorts the moment equation and thereby its solution.
Both regularization and overfitting biases are major concerns in high-dimensional contexts, as estimating
\eta_{0}
accurately is inherently challenging—leading to significant estimation errors—and typically requires flexible methods that increase the risk of dependence. Therefore, addressing these biases is essential for obtaining valid inferences regarding the target parameter
\theta_{0}
.

DML provides a blueprint for alleviating both regularization and overfitting bias. DML thus enables estimation and statistical inference about low-dimensional target parameters in the presence of potentially complicated nuisance parameters. At its core, DML combines two classical ideas from the rich literature on semiparametric inference—using
Neyman orthogonal scores
2
2

2

Such scores are also referred to as orthogonal scores, orthogonal moments, locally robust moments, debiased moments, influence functions, and pathwise derivatives. We follow
Chernozhukov et al. 2018
and use the jargon “Neyman orthogonal scores” in homage to Neyman’s early contributions, e.g.
Neyman 1959
;
Neyman 1979
.
to alleviate regularization bias and using
cross-fitting
to alleviate overfitting bias—in a common methodological framework.
Neyman orthogonality ensures that plugging in estimates that are close to, but not exactly equal to,
\eta_{0}
does not lead to large changes in the moment condition (
1.1
). Cross-fitting, a form of sample splitting, alleviates potential dependence between nuisance estimates
\widehat{\eta}
and parts of the data used for estimating the target parameter.

In conjunction, DML’s two essential components significantly reduce the impact of nuisance parameter estimation on estimates of the target parameter. However, high-quality estimation of the target parameter
\theta_{0}
still requires nuisance parameters to be estimated sufficiently well. Consequently, theoretical results for DML impose specific convergence rate conditions on nuisance parameter estimators. Many estimation methods, including modern machine learning (ML) approaches, can satisfy these conditions. DML thus enables researchers to leverage a wide range of ML tools, making it particularly valuable in complex data scenarios involving numerous variables, images, or textual data. Importantly, ML methods also benefit traditional research settings with fewer covariates and structured, tabular data, as they remove the need for researchers to commit beforehand to specific parametric (often linear) models. By offering practical solutions to the problem of statistical inference in the presence of high-dimensional nuisance parameters, the use of DML thus has the potential to enhance the credibility of research findings in a broad spectrum of settings.

This paper aims to motivate, ease, and guide the application of DML in empirical research. To this end, we divide our review into two parts. First, in Sections
2
and
3
, we introduce the DML blueprint at a high level. Section
2
discusses the practical implications of nuisance estimation and the role of DML’s two key components in their remedy. Section
3
contains algorithmic details on implementation of generic DML estimators. Throughout, our discussion is non-technical.

In the second part of the paper, we turn to three concrete economic applications, found in Section
4
through Section
6
, to illustrate DML and provide practical discussion of key issues that arise in its implementation.

In Section
4
, we compare the benefits of the DML average treatment effect estimator with inverse propensity weighted and regression-based estimators. Our analysis revisits
Poterba et al. 1995
, who analyze the effect of 401(k) eligibility on financial wealth. In a calibrated simulation, we show that the DML estimator—which uses both Neyman orthogonal scores and cross-fitting—is more robust to regularization and overfitting bias than these alternative approaches.

In Section
5
, we illustrate how DML can be leveraged to reduce the dependence on functional forms in staggered adoption designs. We revisit the analysis of
Dobkin et al. 2018
, who study the economic consequences of hospital admission. We estimate group-time average treatment effects on the treated under a conditional parallel trends assumption, and show how DML inference applies to dynamic average treatment effects. Because sample sizes used in staggered adoption designs can be moderate, the application also discusses how repeated DML estimators can be aggregated to improve their stability.

In Section
6

Dube et al. 2020

We also discuss important robustness checks in the application of DML in practice and suggest strategies for obtaining sensible and stable results.

Section
7

In complement to the present article, we provide additional resources on the website
dmlguide.github.io

In terms of scope, we emphasize that DML---or any other estimation framework---cannot replace careful reasoning about identifying assumptions. Rather, with a well-defined target parameter and corresponding identifying assumptions, DML can aid in obtaining estimates of the target parameter in the presence of complex data structures and without relying on pre-specified functional form assumptions.

2
Key Ingredients of DML

This section describes DML’s two essential components—Neyman orthogonality and cross-fitting—and discusses their role in admitting valid statistical inference about a low-dimensional parameter in the presence of a potentially high-dimensional nuisance parameter.

2.1
Setup

We consider an
i.i.d.
sample of random vectors
\{W_{i}\}
for
i\in\{1,\ldots,n\}
. A random vector
W_{i}
may capture, for example, outcomes
Y_{i}
, treatments
D_{i}
and controls
X_{i}
, of the
i
th individual.
4
4

4

The case with excluded instruments is also trivially accommodated by letting
W_{i}
consist of outcomes
Y_{i}
, treatments
D_{i}
, controls
X_{i}
, and excluded instruments
Z_{i}
, of the
i^{\textnormal{th}}
individual.
We emphasize that
W_{i}
may similarly capture outcomes, treatments and controls of the
i
th individual over multiple time periods—i.e.,
W_{i}\equiv(Y_{it},D_{it},X_{it})_{t=1}^{T}
. Maintaining independence across individuals throughout, the following discussions and results apply equally to cross-sectional and conventional panel settings with arbitrary temporal dependence and fixed
T
.

We focus our treatment of DML to a relatively general semiparametric framework with pre-specified, low-dimensional target parameter
\theta_{0}
that is strongly identified by moment conditions of the form

\displaystyle\mathrm{E}\left[m(W_{i};\theta_{0},\eta_{0})\right]=0

(2.2)

where
m(\cdot;\theta,\eta)
is a known (possibly vector-valued) score function indexed by
\theta\in\Theta
and nuisance parameter
\eta\in\mathcal{T}
with true value
\eta_{0}
.
\Theta
is a low-dimensional parameter space while
\mathcal{T}
is a possibly high-dimensional nuisance space.

This semiparametric framework captures a large range of common parameters of interest in empirical research, including regression coefficients and averages of treatment effects. In Example
2.1
, we discuss how the average treatment effect (ATE), the target parameter of our first application, fits into the DML framework. Sections
5
and
6
illustrate group-time average treatment effects on the treated and partially linear regression, respectively. Several additional examples are presented in Remark
2.3.1
and Appendix
A
.

{example}

[Average Treatment Effect; ATE]
Let
W_{i}\equiv(Y_{i},D_{i},X_{i})
where
Y_{i}
is an outcome,
D_{i}
is a binary treatment variable, and
X_{i}
is a vector of covariates. Consider the parameter

\displaystyle\theta^{ATE}_{0}\equiv\mathrm{E}\left[\mathrm{E}[Y_{i}|D_{i}=1,X_{i}]-\mathrm{E}[Y_{i}|D_{i}=0,X_{i}]\right].

(2.3)

Under a conditional unconfoundedness and an overlap assumption (e.g.
Imbens & Rubin 2015
), (
2.3
) is equivalent to the average treatment effect (ATE).

A commonly used approach to estimate the ATE uses the inverse propensity weighted (IPW) score function

\displaystyle m_{IPW}(W_{i};\theta,\alpha)\equiv\alpha(D_{i},X_{i})Y_{i}-\theta,

where the true value of the nuisance parameter
\alpha
is
\alpha_{0}(D_{i},X_{i})\equiv\frac{D_{i}}{r_{0}(X_{i})}-\frac{(1-D_{i})}{1-r_{0}(X_{i})}
and
r_{0}(X_{i})\equiv\mathrm{E}[D_{i}|X_{i}]
. Applying the law of iterated expectations, it is straightforward to confirm that
m_{IPW}(W_{i};\theta_{0},\alpha_{0})
satisfies the moment condition (
2.2
).

An alternative approach to estimate the ATE uses the doubly robust score, also called the augmented IPW score or the efficient influence function:

\displaystyle\psi(W_{i};\theta,\eta)\equiv\alpha(D_{i},X_{i})(Y_{i}-\ell(D_{i},X_{i}))+\ell(1,X_{i})-\ell(0,X_{i})-\theta,

where the nuisance parameter
\eta\equiv(\alpha,\ell)
takes true values
\alpha_{0}(X_{i})
and
\ell_{0}(D_{i},X_{i})\equiv\mathrm{E}[Y_{i}|D_{i},X_{i}]
; see, e.g.,
Newey 1994
and
Robins et al. 1994
. Applying the law of iterated expectations, we can confirm that
\psi(W_{i};\theta^{ATE}_{0},\eta_{0})
satisfies the moment condition (
2.2
).

Whether or not the IPW or doubly robust score are used for estimation can result in stark differences in the quality of inferential statements about the ATE, as we show below and in Section
4
.

In Example
2.1
, the nuisance parameters depend on conditional expectation functions. Outside of special cases such as randomized controlled trials where the propensity score
r_{0}(X_{i})
is known by design, these conditional expectation functions are generally unknown and thus need to be estimated. In the subsequent section, we discuss statistical issues stemming from the estimation of these nuisance parameters and outline how the combination of DML’s two essential components alleviates the impact of nuisance parameter estimation on the main inferential target. This reduction in dependence on the nuisance parameters then allows for a unified, simple approach to obtaining inferential statements that accommodates a wide range of estimators for the nuisance objects and importantly does not require deep understanding of the properties of these estimators. Fundamentally, it is this reduction in the dependence on the specific nuisance parameter estimator that allows DML to accommodate complex estimators, including popular machine learning approaches, for nuisance parameter estimation.

2.2
Regularization and Overfitting Bias

Plug-in estimators for the parameter of interest defined by moment conditions (
2.2
) can be constructed as roots to the sample average of the scores:

\displaystyle\check{\theta}:\quad\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}m(W_{i};\check{\theta},\check{\eta})=0

(2.4)

where
\check{\eta}
denotes a first-step estimator for the nuisance parameter
\eta_{0}
.
5
5

5

We focus exclusively on the case where we have as many score equations as parameters of interest and a sample root exists. The framework extends immediately to other settings such as GMM as discussed, e.g., in
Chernozhukov et al. 2018
.

These simple sample analog estimators are reliable and widely used when a parametric estimator is applied to a low-dimensional nuisance parameter (as in, e.g., linear regression or linear IV). Heuristically, inference about
\theta_{0}
in regular settings follows from a standard asymptotic Taylor expansion of (
2.4
) around the true parameters
(\theta_{0},\eta_{0})
:

\displaystyle\begin{split}\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}m(W_{i};\check{\theta},\check{\eta})&\approx\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}m(W_{i};\theta_{0},\eta_{0})+\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\frac{\partial}{\partial\theta}m(W_{i};\theta_{0},\eta_{0})(\check{\theta}-\theta_{0})\\
&\qquad+\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\frac{\partial}{\partial\eta}m(W_{i};\theta_{0},\eta_{0})(\check{\eta}-\eta_{0})\\
\Rightarrow\quad\sqrt{n}(\check{\theta}-\theta_{0})&\approx\left[\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\frac{\partial}{\partial\theta}m(W_{i};\theta_{0},\eta_{0})\right]^{-1}\frac{1}{\sqrt{n}}\sum_{i=1}^{n}m(W_{i};\theta_{0},\eta_{0})\\
&\qquad+\left[\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\frac{\partial}{\partial\theta}m(W_{i};\theta_{0},\eta_{0})\right]^{-1}\frac{1}{\sqrt{n}}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\frac{\partial}{\partial\eta}m(W_{i};\theta_{0},\eta_{0})(\check{\eta}-\eta_{0}).\end{split}

(2.5)

As shown by the presence of the final term of the approximation, the asymptotic distribution of
\check{\theta}
will generally depend on the asymptotic behavior of the nuisance estimator
\check{\eta}
. Thus, estimating
\eta_{0}
introduces additional uncertainty (estimation error), making the resulting distribution of
\check{\theta}
differ from the idealized case in which
\eta_{0}
is known exactly. In situations where the nuisance parameter is low-dimensional,
this additional uncertainty can be adequately characterized and managed through adjustments to the asymptotic variance, as discussed, e.g., in Section 6 of
Newey & McFadden 1994
.

However, the first-order dependence of the simple analog estimator,
\check{\theta}
, on
\check{\eta}
poses substantial complications in settings where the nuisance parameter is high-dimensional so that
\check{\eta}
corresponds to a flexible estimator. The complication results because flexible estimators are often associated with non-negligible bias and variance. As a consequence, the analog of (
2.5
) in the high-dimensional setting may be dominated by the final term that involves
\check{\eta}
. The dominance of the term involving estimation error in
\eta_{0}
then generally implies two distinct sources of bias in the target parameter estimator: regularization bias and overfitting bias. Roughly speaking, regularization bias arises because the estimation error
(\check{\eta}-\eta_{0})
is too large, while overfitting bias appears because
(\check{\eta}-\eta_{0})
is strongly correlated with
W_{i}
. Albeit for different reasons, both biases imply a failure of conventional inferential practice.

2.3
Two Essential Ingredients of DML

To resolve the problem of inference about
\theta_{0}
in the presence of a high-dimensional
\eta_{0}
without strong assumptions on the quality of nuisance parameter estimation, DML estimators rely crucially on two essential components—estimation based on Neyman orthogonal scores and cross-fitting—that mitigate regularization and overfitting bias. We now discuss each in turn.

2.3.1
Neyman Orthogonality

The chief difficulty in using the simple analog estimator
\check{\theta}
is its dependence on the value of the nuisance parameter estimator
\check{\eta}
. Neyman orthogonality is a local robustness property of the score function in (
2.2
) that decreases sensitivity of the score, and thus the resulting plug-in estimator, to the nuisance parameter.
Formally, a Neyman orthogonal score
\psi
satisfies

\displaystyle\begin{split}\mathrm{E}\left[\psi(W_{i};\theta_{0},\eta_{0})\right]&=0\\
\frac{\partial}{\partial\lambda}\big\{\mathrm{E}\left[\psi(W_{i};\theta_{0},\eta_{0}+\lambda(\eta-\eta_{0}))\right]\big\}\big|_{\lambda=0}&=0,\quad\forall\eta\in\mathcal{T},\end{split}

(2.6)

where
\eta
and
\lambda
can be interpreted as the direction and magnitude of a deviation away from the true nuisance parameter
\eta_{0}
, respectively. Intuitively, the second line of (
2.6
) can simply be thought of as the derivative of the moment condition with respect to the nuisance parameter.
6
6

6

The formal construction (
2.6
) allows us to consider settings where the nuisance parameter
\eta_{0}
is not just a vector of parameters but is an object such as a function belonging to a more abstract space
\mathcal{T}
.
This property then implies that, in sufficiently large samples, the plug-in estimator of
\theta_{0}

is less affected by its reliance on using an estimate of the nuisance parameter in place of the true
\eta_{0}
.

In addition to alleviating regularization bias, an important practical implication of Neyman orthogonality is that inferential statements about
\theta_{0}
do not depend on the precise statistical properties of the nuisance estimator
\check{\eta}
. While the ability to ignore uncertainty in nuisance estimation is always useful—avoiding cumbersome algebra or computationally costly resampling approaches—it is particularly important when modern flexible methods are employed. The value arises because the statistical properties of these methods are still under active development. For example, only coarse convergence guarantees are currently available for many promising flexible supervised machine learners, which precludes their use in settings where a precise understanding of their statistical properties is required.

Returning to the expansion in (
2.5
), consider the case where the estimator
\check{\theta}
is based on a Neyman orthogonal score. Neyman orthogonality implies that
\frac{\partial}{\partial\eta}\psi(W_{i};\theta_{0},\eta_{0})
is mean zero. If we knew that the first-step estimation error
(\check{\eta}-\eta_{0})
was independent of the data
W_{i}
, Neyman orthogonality would then imply that the
\sqrt{n}
-normalized final term in (
2.5
) vanishes,

\displaystyle\sqrt{n}\left(\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\frac{\partial}{\partial\eta}\psi(W_{i};\theta_{0},\eta_{0})(\check{\eta}-\eta_{0})\right)\approx 0,

(2.7)

under mild convergence requirements on
\check{\eta}
and additional mild regularity conditions.
7
7

7

Loosely,
\frac{1}{\sqrt{n}}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\frac{\partial}{\partial\eta}\psi(W_{i};\theta_{0},\eta_{0})
is a mean 0 normalized sum, so will converge by a central limit theorem, which yields (
2.5
) if
(\check{\eta}-\eta_{0})
converges to 0—i.e. if
\check{\eta}
is consistent.

Note that as a consequence, the asymptotic distribution of the plugin-estimator
\check{\theta}
does not depend on the nuisance estimator
\check{\eta}
, implying inference about
\theta_{0}
using
\check{\theta}
can proceed as if
\eta_{0}
were known and did not need to be estimated. This heuristic derivation can be formalized and underlies the crucial importance of Neyman orthogonality in providing general distributional approximations for estimators of target parameters in the presence of complex nuisance parameters.

Example
2.3.1
provides a heuristic analysis of the ATE scores from Example
2.1
to illustrate how to determine whether a score is Neyman orthogonal. The example shows that the commonly considered inverse propensity weighted (IPW) score is
not
Neyman orthogonal, but that the doubly robust score is Neyman orthogonal.

{example}

[Neyman Orthogonal Scores for the Average Treatment Effect]
We show that the IPW estimator, a popular estimator for identifying causal parameters under unconfoundedness, does not rely on a Neyman orthogonal score and is thus ill-suited as a basis for DML estimation. The doubly robust score does, in contrast, satisfy Neyman orthogonality.

Consider the IPW and doubly robust scores of Example
2.1
. It is convenient
to use
\eta(D,X)=(\alpha(D,X),\ell(D,X))
as the nuisance functions with true values
\alpha_{0}(D_{i},X_{i})\equiv\frac{D_{i}}{r_{0}(X_{i})}-\frac{(1-D_{i})}{1-r_{0}(X_{i})}
and
\ell_{0}(D_{i},X_{i})\equiv\mathrm{E}[Y_{i}|D_{i},X_{i}]
. Further, we use
\Delta\eta(D,X_{i})\equiv\eta(D,X_{i})-\eta_{0}(D,X_{i})
to denote a deviation away from the true value of the nuisance parameters.

First, consider how a small change in the nuisance parameter away from the true value impacts the IPW score:

\frac{\partial}{\partial\lambda}\mathrm{E}\left[m_{IPW}(W_{i};\theta_{0},\alpha_{0}+\lambda\Delta\alpha)\right]\big|_{\lambda=0}=\mathrm{E}[Y\Delta\alpha(D,X)]=\mathrm{E}[\ell_{0}(D,X)\Delta\alpha(D,X)].

\mathrm{E}[\ell_{0}(D,X)\Delta\alpha(D,X)]
is not generally zero unless
\ell_{0}(D,X)=0
. It follows that the IPW score
m_{IPW}
is not Neyman orthogonal.

In contrast, note that the doubly robust score evaluated at
\eta_{0}+\lambda\Delta\eta
, a small perturbation away from the true value of the nuisance parameter, can be written as

\displaystyle\psi(W_{i};\theta_{0},\eta_{0}+\lambda\Delta\eta)=

\displaystyle-\lambda^{2}\Delta\alpha(D_{i},X_{i})\Delta\ell(D_{i},X_{i})

\displaystyle\quad+(\alpha_{0}(D_{i},X_{i})+\lambda\Delta\alpha(D_{i},X_{i}))(Y_{i}-\ell_{0}(D_{i},X_{i}))

\displaystyle\quad+\lambda(\Delta\ell(1,X_{i})-\Delta\ell(0,X_{i})-\alpha_{0}(D_{i},X_{i})\Delta\ell(D_{i},X_{i}))

\displaystyle\quad+\ell_{0}(1,X_{i})-\ell_{0}(0,X_{i})-\theta_{0}.

Note that
\mathrm{E}[\ell_{0}(1,X_{i})-\ell_{0}(0,X_{i})-\theta_{0}]=0
by definition of the ATE. Further, the law of iterated expectations implies
\mathrm{E}[(\alpha_{0}(D_{i},X_{i})+\lambda\Delta\alpha(D_{i},X_{i}))(Y_{i}-\ell_{0}(D_{i},X_{i}))]=0
and
\mathrm{E}[\Delta\ell(1,X_{i})-\Delta\ell(0,X_{i})-\alpha_{0}(D_{i},X_{i})\Delta\ell(D_{i},X_{i})]=0
. Hence, the doubly robust score has a bilinear bias formula

\mathrm{E}\left[\psi(W_{i};\theta_{0},\eta_{0}+\lambda\Delta\eta)\right]=-\lambda^{2}\mathrm{E}\left[\Delta\alpha(D_{i},X_{i})\Delta\ell(D_{i},X_{i})\right],

(2.8)

which implies Neyman orthogonality:

\frac{\partial}{\partial\lambda}\mathrm{E}\left[\psi(W_{i};\theta_{0},\eta_{0}+\lambda\Delta\eta)\right]\big|_{\lambda=0}=\frac{\partial}{\partial\lambda}\lambda^{2}\big|_{\lambda=0}\mathrm{E}\left[\Delta\alpha(D_{i},X_{i})\Delta\ell(D_{i},X_{i})\right]=0.

We see that failing to use the Neyman orthogonal score in this example results in both larger bias and dispersion in the estimator of the average treatment effect.

2.3.2
Cross-fitting

Overfitting bias, also referred to as own-observation bias, arises if the error in the nuisance parameter estimator is strongly related to the data used in constructing the plug-in estimator. Again, the argument for being able to ignore the first-step estimation of
\check{\eta}
depends on the final term in the heuristic expansion (
2.5
) being asymptotically negligible. Above, we arrived at (
2.7
) by relying on Neyman orthogonality
and
assuming independence between the estimation error in the nuisance function,
\check{\eta}-\eta_{0}
, and
W_{i}
. More generally, Neyman orthogonality alone is not sufficient to guarantee that first-step estimation of nuisance parameters can be ignored in inference about low-dimensional target parameters.

The second key ingredient of DML, cross-fitting, is meant to address this issue. Cross-fitting is a form of repeated sample splitting intended to reduce dependence between first-step estimation error and the data, thus alleviating overfitting bias.
8
8

8

An alternative to using cross-fitting is to impose assumptions on the data generating process and use methods that are carefully tailored to avoid overfitting under those assumptions. This approach is taken in, for example,
Belloni et al. 2012
,
Belloni et al. 2014
,
Belloni et al. 2017
,
Javanmard & Montanari 2014
,
Geer et al. 2014
,
Zhang & Zhang 2014
, and
Farrell et al. 2021a
. Such results are available for relatively few ML tools and typically under estimation schemes that differ from the way these tools are implemented in practice. Simulation evidence also suggests that DML with flexible learners can perform similarly to these procedures when the assumptions underlying their validity hold and outperforms them when these assumptions do not hold; see, e.g.,
Ahrens et al. 2024
.

Intuitively, if we had access to two independent datasets, one dataset could be used to estimate the nuisance parameters
\check{\eta}
and the second dataset to estimate the target parameters
\theta_{0}
by plugging in the estimate
\check{\eta}
obtained from the first dataset. Because the datasets are independent, independence between
\check{\eta}-\eta_{0}
and
\frac{\partial}{\partial\eta}\psi(W_{i};\theta_{0},\eta_{0})
, which is a function of the data, follows. With a single sample of independent observations, we could split the sample at random into two partitions (often referred to as folds) to produce two independent samples—one for estimating
\eta_{0}
and the other for estimating
\theta_{0}
. Of course, such an approach inefficiently uses the available data because
\eta_{0}
or
\theta_{0}
are not estimated using every observations. Cross-fitting restores asymptotic efficiency by ensuring that all observations are used to estimate nuisance functions and the target parameters.

Cross-fitting itself is a generic sample-splitting procedure.
For example, the cross-fit version of the methods of moment estimator with generic score in (
2.4
) is

\displaystyle\tilde{\theta}:\quad\frac{1}{n}\sum_{k=1}^{K}\sum_{i\in I_{k}}m(W_{i};\tilde{\theta},\widehat{\eta}_{-k})=0.

(2.11)

Here,
\{I_{k}\}_{k=1}^{K}
is a random partition of the sample of units
\{1,\ldots,n\}
into
K
sub-samples of approximately equal size, and
\{\widehat{\eta}_{-k}\}_{k=1}^{K}
is a set of first-step nuisance parameter estimates, each calculated on the individuals
excluding
those in the sub-sample
I_{k}
. Because
\widehat{\eta}_{-k}
uses only observations
not
in sub-sample
k
, the estimation error in
\widehat{\eta}_{-k}
is independent of observations in sub-sample
k
, which mechanically alleviates overfitting bias as long as observations are independent across
i
. By swapping (“crossing”) samples for the estimation of nuisance parameters and the estimation of target parameters, cross-fitting asymptotically avoids losing efficiency relative to the hypothetical full-sample estimator that makes use of the true values of the nuisance parameters.
Note that, in a panel setting with serial dependence or cross-section setting with cluster-dependence (where
W_{i}
collects all observations of unit
i
), cross-fitting involves splitting the data by
i
to ensure that the folds are independent of each other (see Remark 2).

For example, overfitting bias is often viewed as a primary concern in instrumental variable analyses with many instruments
(e.g.,

.

3
Estimation and Inference with DML

We remain in the general semi-parametric setting of Section
2
where we focus on the estimation of a low-dimensional target parameter
\theta_{0}
, which is defined by moment conditions (
2.2
) and depends on an unknown (potentially high-dimensional) nuisance parameter
\eta_{0}
. We consider an
i.i.d.
sample of random vectors
\{W_{i}\}
for
i\in\{1,\ldots,n\}
where
W_{i}
may collect observations of unit
i
over time. The setting thus covers both conventional cross-sectional data, settings with cluster-dependence, and panel settings with arbitrary temporal dependence.

Within this general setting, a DML estimator is a plug-in estimator that leverages a first-step nuisance parameter estimator by relying on Neyman orthogonal scores and cross-fitting. The combination of these two key elements is designed to add robustness to first-step estimation errors of nuisance functions generically and is especially important when these nuisance functions are estimated with flexible ML tools. The DML estimator of a pre-specified, low-dimensional target parameter
\theta_{0}
is then

\displaystyle\widehat{\theta}:\quad\frac{1}{n}\sum_{k=1}^{K}\sum_{i\in I_{k}}\psi(W_{i};\widehat{\theta},\widehat{\eta}_{-k})=0,

(3.12)

where
\psi
is a Neyman orthogonal score,
\{I_{k}\}_{k=1}^{K}
is a random partition of the sample of individuals
\{1,\ldots,n\}
into
K
sub-samples of approximately equal size, and
\{\widehat{\eta}_{-k}\}_{k=1}^{K}
are cross-fitted nuisance parameter estimators.

Chernozhukov et al. 2018
provide conditions that allow researchers to make valid inferential statements about
\theta_{0}
using the DML estimator
\widehat{\theta}
. In addition to conventional sampling and regularity conditions, the central sufficient condition employed in the DML framework is that the nuisance parameter estimator
\widehat{\eta}
is of moderate quality in the sense that it satisfies certain rate of convergence requirements.
9
9

9

A crude sufficient condition for models in which the Neyman orthogonal moment is smooth in
\theta
and
\eta
is that nuisance functions have
n^{-1/4}

\ell_{2}
convergence rates; see, e.g., discussion on p. C25 of
Chernozhukov et al. 2018
. See also
Chernozhukov et al. 2022
and
Kennedy 2023
for further discussion.

Guarantees on the quality of nuisance estimation should be understood as a combination of an assumption on the structure of the underlying data generating process and a choice of estimator that successfully leverages that structure. A prominent example is the assumption of sparsity and the application of the lasso estimator that can successfully leverage sparsity. These structure-estimator combinations also apply to other modern ML methods, e.g., versions of random forests and neural networks, and thus motivate their use for nuisance estimation
(see, e.g.,
Bickel et al. 2009
;
Belloni et al. 2012
;
Wager & Walther 2015
;
Wager & Athey 2018
;
Oprescu et al. 2019
;
Athey et al. 2019a
;

;

;
Chetverikov et al. 2021
;
Chetverikov & Srensen 2022
;
Kueck et al. 2023

.
10
10

10

The asymptotic properties of ML methods remains an active research area and one expects many more results to be developed. Once their theoretical properties are sufficiently developed, new ML methods can readily be leveraged for nuisance parameter estimation within DML due to its blueprint approach.
In practice, we might, of course, not know which assumption is most plausible and which estimator is best suited for a given application. We will return to the topic of the choice of ML estimator in Section

Assuming that estimators of the nuisance parameters converge at appropriate rates, the sampling distribution of the DML estimator
\widehat{\theta}
can be well approximated by a normal distribution, i.e.,
\sqrt{n}\widehat{\Sigma}^{-1/2}(\widehat{\theta}-\theta_{0})\overset{d}{\to}\mathcal{N}(0,\mathrm{I})
, where

\displaystyle\begin{aligned} \widehat{\Sigma}\equiv&\widehat{J}^{-1}\left(\frac{1}{n}\sum_{k=1}^{K}\sum_{i\in I_{k}}\psi(W_{i};\widehat{\theta},\widehat{\eta}_{-k})\psi(W_{i};\widehat{\theta},\widehat{\eta}_{-k})^{\top}\right)\widehat{J}^{-1\top},\\
\widehat{J}\equiv&\frac{1}{n}\sum_{k=1}^{K}\sum_{i\in I_{k}}\frac{\partial}{\partial\theta}\left\{\psi(W_{i};\theta,\widehat{\eta}_{-k})\right\}\big|_{\theta=\widehat{\theta}}\>.\end{aligned}

(3.13)

Appropriate standard errors for
\widehat{\theta}
are thus given by square-roots of the diagonal values of
\widehat{\Sigma}/n
.
Corresponding
1-\alpha
confidence intervals about the target parameter
\theta_{0}
are then obviously constructed as

\displaystyle\left(\widehat{\theta}-c_{1-\frac{\alpha}{2}}\sqrt{\frac{\operatorname{diag}(\widehat{\Sigma})}{n}};\>\widehat{\theta}+c_{1-\frac{\alpha}{2}}\sqrt{\frac{\operatorname{diag}(\widehat{\Sigma})}{n}}\right),

(3.14)

where
c_{1-\frac{\alpha}{2}}
is the
(1-\frac{\alpha}{2})
th quantile of a standard normal.

As heuristically outlined in Section
2

The stated approximation result immediately applies in the standard cross-sectional setting as well as in the cross-section setting with cluster-dependence or the fixed-
T
panel setting. In the first case, the standard errors correspond to conventional heteroskedasticity-robust standard errors, and the standard errors are analogous to clustered standard errors with arbitrary dependence within unit
i
and across
t
in the latter cases.

4
Effect of 401k Eligibility on Financial Wealth

In this empirical application, we discuss estimation of the ATE with and without Neyman orthogonal scores and cross-fitting. Using a simple calibrated simulation, we highlight that it is DML’s combination of Neyman orthogonality and cross-fitting that allows for valid statistical inference.

We consider the setting of
Poterba et al. 1995
. Specifically, we use data from the 1991 wave of the Survey of Income and Program Participation (SIPP) and focus on studying the effect of eligibility to participate in a 401(k) plan on net financial assets. This application has frequently been revisited in the literature on nuisance parameter estimation with machine learning; see, for example,
Belloni et al. 2017

Chernozhukov et al. 2018

about the IPW and doubly robust score, the nuisance parameters are high-dimensional in the absence of strong functional form assumptions on these conditional expectations. For example, it would be practically impossible to saturate the model in indicators capturing all unique values of
X_{i}
in this example (e.g., income alone takes 7,334 unique values in the dataset of size 9,915).

Importantly, neither the IPW nor the RA score are Neyman orthogonal. In contrast, the doubly robust score is Neyman orthogonal as shown in Example
2.3.1
. The ATE estimators based on the IPW and RA score are thus sensitive to errors in the estimation of the nuisance parameters. Further, the standard error adjustment needed for these scores to account for nuisance estimation uncertainty is difficult or impossible for many modern ML methods given our current understanding of the properties of these methods. The DML estimator
\widehat{\theta}
based on the doubly robust score and cross-fitting is, in contrast, less sensitive to the estimation of nuisance parameters and does not require additional standard error adjustments to account for their estimation.

Panel A in Table

presents estimates of the ATE for estimators with and without Neyman orthogonal scores, and with and without cross-fitting. All standard errors are computed ignoring first step estimation of nuisance parameters. As discussed in Sections
2
and
3
, ignoring the first step in the computation of standard errors can be formally justified for DML under the assumption that the random forest performs sufficiently well in estimating nuisance functions. It cannot be easily justified for the full sample estimator as we do not know if the random forest suffers from overfitting.
Since neither the IPW nor the RA estimator uses Neyman orthogonal scores, theoretically valid inference for these objects would require accounting for first step estimation of nuisance parameters and thus the reported standard errors, which ignore this first step estimation, are not theoretically justified regardless of whether cross-fitting is employed. The lack of justification, of course, does not imply that standard errors will always be too small. In fact, we see in this example that the estimated IPW standard errors are too large, while the RA standard errors are strongly biased downwards.

The DML estimator is computed following the DML algorithm of Example
3
with the same choice of
K=10
cross-fitting folds and random forest nuisance estimators. For all approaches, we specifically use random forests with 1000 trees and a maximum depth of 8 and 4 for the outcome and treatment propensity functions, respectively.

Regardless of the ATE estimator considered, the results suggest a substantial positive effect of 401(k) eligibility on financial assets. However, we see stark numerical differences in the point estimates and standard errors for the six different estimators. The IPW estimators without and with cross-fitting result in the highest and lowest point estimates, respectively, while also exhibiting the largest standard errors. The standard errors of the RA estimators, in contrast, are an order of magnitude smaller than the IPW standard error estimates. Due to the high-dimensional nuisance parameter estimation, researchers should not place confidence in any of these estimates. The estimator based on the Neyman orthogonal score without cross-fitting has standard error between those of the IPW and RA approaches. Finally, the DML estimator, which is the only estimator considered with theoretical guarantees, has standard error roughly 40% lower than the IPW estimators and 5 times larger than the RA estimators.

Economic Consequences of Hospital Admission

A recent literature considers difference-in-differences analyses in the presence of heterogeneous treatment effects (see reviews in, e.g.,
de Chaisemartin & d’Haultfoeuille 2023
;
Roth et al. 2023
). To further demonstrate the general applicability of DML, we illustrate the DML estimation in staggered adoption designs for the estimation of group-time average treatment effects on the treated and dynamic average treatment effects as discussed in
Callaway & Sant’Anna 2021
. Outside of illustrating the application of DML in a canonical panel data setting, we further use this example to discuss the additional randomness introduced in DML due to the use of sample splitting. Specifically, we demonstrate that simply repeating the DML estimation can help us gauge the robustness of conclusions to particular sample splits. We then outline the median aggregation approach presented in
Chernozhukov et al. 2018
. Considering the impact of sample splitting is of practical importance, in particular, in settings with relatively few observations, as is often the case in staggered adoption designs.

Focusing on a panel of US households from the health and retirement survey (HRS),
Dobkin et al. 2018
analyze the causal effect of hospital admission on several economic outcomes, including out-of-pocket medical spending, using conventional two-way fixed effects estimation.
Sun & Abraham 2021
extend this analysis by re-estimating the dynamic effects of hospitalization using methods that are robust to heterogeneity in the dynamic effects of treatment. Following
Sun & Abraham 2021
, we consider a balanced sample of 656 households in waves 7-11 of the HRS.

14

14

We proceed in two steps. In Section

caused by hospitalization at time
g

5.1
Group-Time Average Treatment Effects of Hospitalization

To define the group-time average treatment effect on the treated (GT-ATT), let
G_{i}
denote the time of first hospital admission and
D_{it}\equiv\mathbbm{1}\{G_{i}\leq t\}
denote whether individual
i
has been hospitalized at time
t
. Further, let
(Y_{it}(1),Y_{it}(0))
denote the potential outcomes of out-of-pocket medical spending with and without hospitalization such that
Y_{it}=D_{it}Y_{it}(1)+(1-D_{it})Y_{it}(0)
. Then, the GT-ATT for group
g
and time
t
is defined as

\displaystyle\begin{aligned} \theta_{0}^{ATT(g,t)}\equiv\mathrm{E}[Y_{it}(1)-Y_{it}(0)|D_{it}=1,G_{i}=g].\end{aligned}

(5.17)

Following
Dobkin et al. 2018
and
Sun & Abraham 2021
, we leverage a parallel trends assumption on not-yet-hospitalized individuals to identify the GT-ATTs. Motivated by the robustness checks of
Dobkin et al. 2018
, we also consider relaxing the parallel trends assumption to only hold conditional on pre-treatment controls (
X_{i}
) including age, gender, race, and education. If we assume irreversibility of treatment, limited treatment anticipation, and overlap in addition to assuming that parallel trends holds conditional on pre-treatment characteristics
(see, e.g.,
Callaway & Sant’Anna 2021
)
, then the GT-ATT is equivalent to

\displaystyle\begin{aligned} \theta_{0}^{ATT(g,t)}&=\mathrm{E}[\Delta Y_{it}|D_{it}=1,G_{i}=g]-\mathrm{E}[\mathrm{E}[\Delta Y_{it}|G_{i}>t,X_{i}]|D_{it}=1,G_{i}=g],\end{aligned}

where
\Delta Y_{it}=Y_{it}-Y_{it-1}
, and the right-hand side is a known function of only reduced form objects.

A key insight about the estimation of the GT-ATT is that for each group-time pair, the target parameter is equivalent to a conventional ATT identified under conditional unconfoundedness where the outcome is replaced by a first-difference in outcomes. Building on the efficient ATT score of
Hahn 1998
,
Chang 2020
proposes a DML estimator based on the doubly robust score for the GT-ATT:
15
15

15

See also
Sant’Anna & Zhao 2020
.

\displaystyle\begin{aligned} \psi^{(g,t)}(W_{i};\theta,\eta)&\equiv\frac{\mathbbm{1}\{D_{it}=1,G_{i}=g\}(\Delta Y_{it}-\ell^{(0)}(X_{i}))}{p}\\
&\quad-\frac{h^{(1)}(X_{i})\mathbbm{1}\{G_{i}>t\}(\Delta Y_{it}-\ell^{(0)}(X_{i}))}{ph^{(0)}(X_{i})}-\frac{\mathbbm{1}\{D_{it}=1,G_{i}=g\}}{p}\theta\end{aligned}

(5.18)

where the nuisance parameter
\eta\equiv(h^{(0)},h^{(1)},\ell^{(0)},p)
takes true values
h_{0}^{(0)}(X_{i})\equiv{\mathrm{P}}(G_{i}>t|X_{i})
,
h_{0}^{(1)}(X_{i})\equiv{\mathrm{P}}(D_{it}=1,G_{i}=g|X_{i}),

\ell_{0}^{(0)}(X_{i})\equiv\mathrm{E}[\Delta Y_{it}|G_{i}>t,X_{i}],
and
p_{0}\equiv{\mathrm{P}}(D_{it}=1,G_{i}=g)
. Applying the law of iterated expectations, it is straightforward to confirm that (
5.18
) satisfies the moment condition (
2.2
). Following similar steps as in Example
2.3.1
, it is also straightforward to show that the doubly robust score is Neyman orthogonal. Using (
5.18
), a DML estimator can thus readily be constructed via Algorithm
1
.

The application of DML to estimation of the GT-ATTs provides a useful robustness check because it seems unlikely that a researcher has prior knowledge of the parametric form of the nuisance functions. In this application, it is also practically impossible to saturate the model in the control variables and thus eliminate the need for a functional form assumption or flexible dimension reduction as provided by flexible methods.
16
16

16

A fully interacted set of control variables results in 3,072 indicators, which exceeds the number of households in our sample.
For ease of exposition, we again focus on a single machine learner: a random forest estimator with 1000 trees and a minimum node size of 10 to estimate the nuisance parameters.

All of the DML estimators considered are asymptotically equivalent, yet, numerical differences can arise in finite samples due to differences in the cross-fitting partitions. These differences might be particularly pronounced in settings with relatively few observations, as is often the case in staggered adoption designs, where the DML estimation of GT-ATTs requires performing cross-fitting on different sub-samples for each group-time effect. For example, the estimated first-period treatment effect for group 9 is based on 176 treated and 404 untreated observations, respectively. To summarize results across multiple sample splits while accounting somewhat for variation across these splits, it is useful to consider median aggregation. Letting
\widehat{\theta}_{s}
and
\widehat{s.e.}_{s}
denote the
s
th DML estimator and its standard error among a total of
S
replications, the median-aggregated DML estimator and standard errors are given by

\displaystyle\widehat{\theta}^{\text{median}}\equiv\text{median}\left(\{\widehat{\theta}_{s}\}_{s=1}^{S}\right),\qquad\widehat{s.e.}^{\text{median}}\equiv\sqrt{\text{median}\left(\{\widehat{s.e.}_{s}^{2}+(\widehat{\theta}_{s}-\widehat{\theta}^{\text{median}})^{2}\}_{s=1}^{S}\right)}.

For vector-valued parameters, the median is applied element-wise. The median-aggregated DML estimator
\widehat{\theta}^{\text{median}}
is first-order equivalent to any
\widehat{\theta}_{s}
. We recommend reporting median aggregated DML estimator as a simple summary.
17

17

Chernozhukov et al. 2018
also consider mean aggregation. We are not aware of formal theoretical arguments that point to improved finite sample properties of mean or median aggregated DML estimators. Our recommendation should be taken as practical but heuristic.
In addition, the complete set of estimates and standard errors obtained in each different sample split should be provided or available for transparency.

We also compare the DML estimator of the GT-ATTs with estimates obtained using the parametric doubly robust estimators of
Sant’Anna & Zhao 2020
. These estimators use the same doubly robust score as the DML estimator. However, unlike the DML estimator,
Sant’Anna & Zhao 2020
make statistical convenience assumptions by imposing parametric functional form assumptions on the nuisance parameters: The nuisance parameters
{\mathrm{P}}(G_{i}>t|X_{i})
and
{\mathrm{P}}(D_{it}=1,G_{i}=g|X_{i})

\mathrm{E}[\Delta Y_{it}|G_{i}>t,X_{i}]

\mathbbm{1}\{G_{i}>t\}X_{i}
. If both parametric assumptions hold, the estimator of
Sant’Anna & Zhao 2020
is first-order equivalent to the DML estimator. When both parametric assumptions are false, however, the parametric doubly robust estimator is inconsistent.
18
18

18

If only one of the parametric assumptions is correct, statistical inference using the estimator of
Sant’Anna & Zhao 2020
remains valid but is less efficient than the corresponding DML estimator.
Given that the use of logit and linear parametric models is solely motivated by statistical convenience rather than economic theory, this makes the DML estimator for the GT-ATTs and the dynamic treatment effects an attractive alternative. The trade-off between the parametric doubly robust estimator of
Sant’Anna & Zhao 2020
and the DML estimator depends on the researcher’s confidence in the parametric models for the nuisance parameters and the availability of a flexible nuisance parameter of moderate quality. Regardless of the choice of main approach between the two approaches, considering the other as a robustness check thus appears sensible.

5.2
Dynamic Effects of Hospitalization

In many applications, the GT-ATTs themselves are not of primary interest. Rather, we are often interested in summaries of these effects. One leading example is the dynamic effects, which constitute the basis for event studies and can be used to assess the validity of the parallel trends assumptions.
19
19

19

Other summaries are readily available. See, for example,
Callaway 2023
.
The dynamic effects are the group-weighted ATTs measured
e
periods after the treatment (here, hospitalization) begins.
The dynamic effects are formally defined as

\displaystyle\tau_{0}^{DATT(e)}

\displaystyle\equiv\sum_{g\in\mathcal{G}}\mathbbm{1}\{g+e\leq T\}{\mathrm{P}}\left(G_{i}=g|G_{i}+e\leq T\right)\theta_{0}^{ATT(g,g+e)}

(5.19)

where
\mathcal{G}
is the set of all first-treatment times, and
\theta_{0}^{ATT(g,t)}
is the GT-ATT defined previously.
In the setting of
Dobkin et al. 2018
, the dynamic treatment effect
\tau_{0}^{DATT(e)}
at time
e
is thus the average of all identified GT-ATTs
e
periods after initial hospitalization. The group weights refer the size of the group relative to all groups observed
e
periods after their initial hospitalization.

Estimating the probabilities
{\mathrm{P}}\left(G_{i}=g|G_{i}+e\leq T\right)
is straightforward. For example, a natural consistent estimator for
{\mathrm{P}}\left(G_{i}=g|G_{i}+e\leq T\right)
is given by the binning estimator
\sum_{i=1}\mathbbm{1}\{G_{i}=g,G_{i}+e\leq T\})/(\sum_{i=1}\mathbbm{1}\{G_{i}+e\leq T\}
. Given an estimator for these probabilities and the DML estimators for the GT-ATTs, inference for
\tau_{0}^{DATT(e)}
then follows immediately from standard arguments.
20
20

20

Given a DML estimator
\widehat{\theta}
and corresponding estimated covariance matrix
\widehat{\Sigma}
, it is straightforward to make inferential statements about any linear combinations
\tau_{0}\equiv R^{\top}\theta_{0}
for some matrix
R
of appropriate dimension whenever a consistent estimator
\widehat{R}
for
R
exists. Let
\widehat{\tau}\equiv\widehat{R}^{\top}\widehat{\theta}
be the DML estimator for
\tau_{0}
. It follows by standard arguments that
\widehat{\tau}
will be approximately normal with approximate variance
\widehat{R}^{\top}\widehat{\Sigma}\widehat{R}/n
.

6
Monopsony in Online Markets

In this section, we revisit
Dube et al. 2020
who examine monopsony power on Amazon MTurk, an online platform for hiring workers to perform tasks.
22
22

22

The research falls into the wider literature on assessing monopsony power in the labor market by estimating the labor supply elasticity to the firm; see reviews in
Sokolova & Sorensen 2021
and
Langella & Manning 2021
.
The study serves as an excellent example for at least two reasons. First, the study allow us to illustrate how DML is able to incorporate complex, unstructured data such as text or images. Second, we discuss the question of how to select the machine learner (and its tuning parameters) for nuisance parameter estimation when performing DML analyses. In practice, nuisance parameter estimation using machine learning involves many choices—not only with text data. In this section, we discuss methods for validating the choice (and settings) of machine learners.

Dube et al. 2020
consider multiple identification strategies and datasets for assessing monopsony power. We focus here on the analysis of the cross-section sample compiled by
Ipeirotis 2010
. The parameter of interest is the partially linear regression (PLR) coefficient
\theta_{0}
defined as the solution to

\displaystyle(\theta_{0},\>\beta_{0})\>\equiv\>\argmin_{\theta\in\mathbbm{R},\beta\in\mathcal{T}}\>\mathrm{E}[(Y_{i}-D_{i}\theta-\beta(X_{i}))^{2}],

(6.20)

where
Y_{i}
is the logarithm of the time it takes for the posted job to be filled,
D_{i}
is the logarithm of the payment offered for the task,
X_{i}
denotes a vector of task characteristics, and
\beta_{0}(\cdot)
is a potentially high-dimensional function of the task characteristics.
23
23

23

Modern textbook treatments of linear regression emphasize its interpretation as the best linear approximation (in an MSE-sense) of the conditional expectation function (CEF) of
Y_{i}
given
D_{i}
and
X_{i}
in the absence of linear model assumptions
(e.g.,
Angrist & Pischke 2009
;
Hansen 2022
;
Chernozhukov et al. 2024a
)
. PLR can be motivated in similar manner as the best
partially
linear approximation to the CEF. We highlight here that arguments for economic interest in the best “fully” linear approximation to the CEF as outlined, e.g., in
Angrist & Pischke 2009
, also make PLR an attractive baseline choice in many economic analyses.

Dube et al. 2020
provide arguments under which
\theta_{0}
is equal to the negative labor supply elasticity to the firm and thus a measure of monopsony power. Since tasks vary in complexity, difficulty, and time commitment, it is important to adjust for characteristics that are likely correlated with duration and reward. Some of these task characteristics come in the form of unstructured text data (e.g., title, task description, keywords). Specifically, the controls in this application include hand-coded features (e.g., time allotted, common patterns identified through regular expressions, number of keywords, lengths of title and description), as well as the task title and descriptions vectorized using Doc2Vec embeddings, topic distributions, and n-grams.

To address the estimation challenge,
Dube et al. 2020
apply DML. The Neyman orthogonal score for the PLR coefficient is

\displaystyle\psi(W_{i};\theta,\eta)\equiv\left(D_{i}-r(X)\right)\left(Y_{i}-\ell(X_{i})\right)-\left(D_{i}-r(X_{i})\right)^{2}\theta,

(6.21)

where the nuisance parameter
\eta\equiv(r,\ell)
represents the conditional expectation functions
r_{0}(X_{i})\equiv\mathrm{E}[D_{i}|X_{i}]
and

The DML estimation results based on different machine learners highlight that DML estimates are only as credible as the choice of nuisance estimator. As illustrated, poorly tuned or ill-suited nuisance estimators can produce severely misleading point estimates. In practice, we recommend researchers to carefully choose and tune their nuisance function estimators and consider a rich set of estimation approaches. Fortunately, the selection of the best-performing nuisance estimators among a set of candidate learners is relatively straightforward using an appropriate cross-fitted performance metric such as
R^{2}
, by considering the
CVC
test, or by implementing model averaging approaches.

In this context, it is also noteworthy that the optimal choice of machine learner specification will often vary across conditional expectation functions. Indeed, there is no reason to assume that, for example, both
\mathrm{E}[D_{i}|X_{i}]
and
\mathrm{E}[Y_{i}|X_{i}]
follow the same data generating scheme—an assumption that is implicitly made by many estimation approaches. We emphasize that the application of DML with multiple nuisance estimators is important also outside of applications with textual controls. As illustrated, poorly tuned or ill-suited nuisance estimators can produce qualitatively different estimates. It is helpful when researchers not only document their final DML estimates but also the machine learners they consider (including their hyper-tuning parameters) and transparently justify their preferred choice based on suitable loss metrics or through model averaging approaches.

7
Conclusion

DML builds on ideas from the classic semiparametric estimation and inference literature to provide a framework for obtaining valid statistical inference about a target parameter in the presence of nuisance parameters. By combining Neyman orthogonal scores with cross-fitting, DML mitigates the impact of estimating nuisance parameters, allowing for theoretically justified inferential statements under relatively weak conditions on the quality of nuisance parameter estimators. These conditions can be met by a broad range of methods, including many modern machine learning techniques. As a result, DML provides a compelling statistical tool in settings where researchers seek to avoid imposing parametric assumptions based purely on convenience rather than substantive justification. Research on DML has expanded rapidly, reflecting the increasing interest in the topic. For example,
Chang 2020
;
Klosin & Vilgalys 2023
;
Abadie et al. 2024
;
Haddad et al. 2024
consider DML in canonical panel and difference-in-differences settings.
Jung et al. 2021
;
Deaner 2023
;
Singh & Sun 2024
consider DML in IV and proxy control settings.
Bonvini et al. 2021
;
Lewis & Syrgkanis 2021
;
Klosin 2021
;
Colangelo & Lee 2023
;
Sasaki & Ura 2023
;
Kallus et al. 2024
consider DML for policy relevant treatment parameters beyond traditional summaries such as average treatment effects for binary treatments.
Chernozhukov et al. 2021
;
Farrell et al. 2021
;
Chernozhukov et al. 2022
;
Chernozhukov et al. 2022a
;
Chernozhukov et al. 2022b
consider general frameworks for DML estimation and inference about complex parameters allowing for automatic construction of Neyman orthogonal moments.
Escanciano & Pérez-Izquierdo 2023
considers DML in a generated regressor setting.
Semenova 2023
considers DML within set-identified models.
Bia et al. 2024
provides a DML approach within a sample selection model. There is also a growing body of work applying the DML structure to estimating high-dimensional target parameters; see, e.g.,
Kennedy 2023a
;
Nie & Wager 2021
;
Semenova & Chernozhukov 2021
;
Foster & Syrgkanis 2023
.

Despite its flexibility, DML is not a panacea. It does not tell a researcher what interesting target parameters are or how to identify them. Rather it complements careful reasoning about objects of interest and their identification by allowing researchers to be more agnostic about auxiliary functional form assumptions and to leverage state-of-the-art prediction algorithms to construct estimates of reduced form quantities. In practice, its performance depends on the choice and tuning of nuisance function estimators, and poor choices can severely degrade estimation and inference. Ongoing advances in machine learning and AI will help clarify when particular learners are well-suited to DML, facilitating more refined understanding of its performance. In the meantime, researchers can readily employ DML with a variety of candidate learners and use available data-driven diagnostics to help select relatively good performers. At a minimum, this strategy represents a practical starting point and provides a valuable robustness check to results obtained using more standard, parametric methods. Further exploration of practical and theoretically grounded ways to pursue data driven tuning and choice of learner seems worth additional study. It also seems clear that there are tradeoffs to making use of sample splitting in finite samples that are not revealed by the current theory. More refined theoretical results that illuminate these tradeoffs may be an interesting area for further research.

While the conditions for nuisance function estimation in DML are compatible with many learners, they nonetheless impose meaningful restrictions. For example, these conditions may be violated in settings where nuisance functions depend on many variables and are relatively non-smooth.
26
26

26

See, for example, suggestive results in
Chi et al. 2022
.
Exploring estimation and inference in regimes where these conditions are not satisfied is an interesting area for further study. See, for example,
Robins et al. 2017
and
Bradic et al. 2022
for interesting work in this direction. Work on sensitivity analysis in DML settings as in
Chernozhukov et al. 2024
, while targeted at a different problem, may also offer practical inferential approaches in this challenging regime.

Overall, DML is a valuable complement to traditional methods, capable of both reinforcing empirical findings and identifying vulnerabilities in conventional approaches. Importantly, DML provides a viable path to using increasingly complex data sources—such as text or images—where conventional parametric models may be unsuitable in empirical economic research. As data rich environments become more commonplace and AI and ML methods advance, DML will only become more important as part of the empirical toolkit.

Appendix A
Neyman Orthogonal Scores for Common and New Target Parameters

This appendix discusses additional target parameters that fit the DML framework of Section
2.1
. To also facilitate the application of DML to target parameters outside of these examples, we begin by illustrating a construction of Neyman orthogonal scores when nuisance parameters are conditional expectation functions.

In many settings, the moment condition (
2.2
) is such that (a) the nuisance parameter
\eta_{0}
is a vector of conditional expectation functions, that is,
\eta_{0}\equiv(\gamma_{0}^{(k)})_{k=1}^{K}
where, for each
k
,
\gamma_{0}^{(k)}(B_{i}^{(k)})\equiv\mathrm{E}[A_{i}^{(k)}|B_{i}^{(k)}]
for some subvectors
A_{i}^{(k)}
and
B_{i}^{(k)}
of
W_{i}
, and (b) the score function
m
depends on
\eta_{0}
only through its value
\eta_{0}(W_{i})
.

Consider, for example, the average treatment effect defined in (
2.3
) with corresponding inverse-propensity weighted (IPW) score

\displaystyle m(W_{i};\theta,\eta)\equiv\frac{D_{i}Y_{i}}{\gamma^{(1)}(X_{i})}-\frac{(1-D_{i})Y_{i}}{1-\gamma^{(1)}(X_{i})}-\theta,

where the nuisance parameter
\eta\equiv\gamma^{(1)}
takes its true value at
\gamma_{0}^{(1)}(X_{i})\equiv\mathrm{E}[D_{i}|X_{i}]
, the propensity of treatment, with
A_{i}^{(1)}\equiv D_{i}
and
B_{i}^{(1)}\equiv X_{i}
. Clearly, the IPW score depends on the treatment propensity only through its value
\gamma_{0}^{(1)}(X_{i})
.

Newey 1994
shows that it is straightforward to compute the impact of nuisance estimation for this kind of moment condition. These calculations then directly assist in the construction of Neyman orthogonal scores; e.g.
Chernozhukov et al. 2022
;
Chernozhukov et al. 2021
. See also
Kennedy 2023
for further discussion and approaches to construct orthogonal scores.

Heuristically, the impact of bias in the
k^{\textnormal{th}}
nuisance parameter
\gamma^{(k)}
on the score is

\displaystyle\begin{split}\frac{\partial}{\partial\lambda}m(W_{i};\theta_{0},\gamma_{0}^{(1)},\ldots,\gamma_{0}^{(k)}+\lambda\Delta\gamma^{(k)},\ldots,\gamma_{0}^{(K)})\big|_{\lambda=0}=m_{k}(W_{i};\theta_{0},\gamma_{0})\Delta\gamma^{(k)}(B_{i}^{(k)})\end{split}

(A.22)

where the equality follows from the chain rule, and we use
\Delta\gamma^{(k)}\equiv\gamma_{0}^{(k)}-\gamma^{(k)}
to denote the direction of the bias and
m_{k}
to denote the partial derivative of
m
with respect to the value of
\gamma^{(k)}
.

To correct for this impact of bias in the
k^{\textnormal{th}}
nuisance parameter, define the adjustment factor

\displaystyle\alpha_{0}^{(k)}(B_{i}^{(k)})\equiv\mathrm{E}[m_{k}(W_{i};\theta_{0},\gamma_{0})|B_{i}^{(k)}].

(A.23)

A Neyman orthogonal score can then be constructed as

\displaystyle\psi(W_{i};\theta,\eta)\equiv m\left(W_{i};\theta,(\gamma^{(k)})_{k=1}^{K}\right)+\sum_{k=1}^{K}\alpha^{(k)}(B_{i}^{(k)})(A_{i}^{(k)}-\gamma^{(k)}(B_{i}^{(k)})),

(A.24)

where
\eta\equiv(\gamma^{(k)},\alpha^{(k)})_{k=1}^{K}
is the combined nuisance parameter with true value at
\eta_{0}\equiv(\gamma_{0}^{(k)},\alpha_{0}^{(k)})_{k=1}^{K}
.

To confirm that all scores constructed as in (
A.24
) are Neyman orthogonal, note first that the second term is mean-zero so that
\psi
and
m
identify the same target parameter. Then, by the chain rule

\displaystyle\frac{\partial}{\partial\lambda}\big\{\mathrm{E}\left[\psi(W_{i};\theta_{0},\eta_{0}+\lambda\Delta\eta)\right]\big\}\big|_{\lambda=0}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{k=1}^{K}\mathrm{E}[m_{k}(W_{i};\theta_{0},\gamma_{0})\Delta\gamma^{(k)}(B_{i}^{(k)})]-\sum_{k=1}^{K}\mathrm{E}[\alpha_{0}^{(k)}(B_{i}^{(k)})\Delta\gamma^{(k)}(B_{i}^{(k)})]

\displaystyle\quad+\sum_{k=1}^{K}\mathrm{E}[\Delta\alpha^{(k)}(B_{i}^{(k)})(A_{i}^{(k)}-\gamma_{k}(B_{i}^{(k)})]=0,

where the final equality follows from (
A.23
) and iterated expectations.

Returning to the example of the average treatment effect and its IPW score, we can thus compute the impact of propensity score estimation as

\displaystyle\frac{\partial}{\partial\lambda}m(W_{i};\theta_{0},\gamma_{0}^{(1)}+\lambda\Delta\gamma^{(1)})\big|_{\lambda=0}=m_{1}(W_{i};\theta_{0},\gamma_{0}^{(1)})\Delta\gamma^{(1)}(X_{i})

where

\displaystyle m_{1}(W_{i};\theta_{0},\gamma_{0}^{(1)})\equiv-\frac{D_{i}Y_{i}}{\gamma_{0}^{(1)}(X_{i})^{2}}-\frac{(1-D_{i})Y_{i}}{(1-\gamma_{0}^{(1)}(X_{i}))^{2}}.

We then construct the adjustment factor as

\displaystyle\alpha_{0}^{(1)}(X_{i})

\displaystyle\equiv\mathrm{E}[m_{1}(W_{i};\theta_{0},\gamma_{0}^{(1)})|X_{i}]=-\frac{\mathrm{E}[Y_{i}|D_{i}=1,X_{i}]}{\gamma_{0}^{(1)}(X_{i})}-\frac{\mathrm{E}[Y_{i}|D_{i}=0,X_{i}]}{1-\gamma_{0}^{(1)}(X_{i})},

where the final equality follows from the law of total probability. Combining provides a Neyman orthogonal score for the ATE:

\displaystyle\psi(W_{i};\theta,\eta)

\displaystyle\equiv\frac{D_{i}Y_{i}}{\gamma^{(1)}(X_{i})}-\frac{(1-D_{i})Y_{i}}{1-\gamma^{(1)}(X_{i})}-\theta+\alpha^{(1)}(X_{i})(D_{i}-\gamma^{(1)}(X_{i})).

Note that this score function differs from the alternative doubly robust score for the ATE in Example
2.1
in its parameterization of the nuisance parameter, yet, DML estimators leveraging either Neyman orthogonal score are first-order equivalent.
After replacing
\alpha^{(1)}
with its representation in terms of conditional expectations and some algebra, this expression reduces to the doubly robust score for the ATE in Example
2.1
.

A.1
), regression parameters (Section
A.2
), and fixed effect regression parameters (Section
A.3
).

A.1
Treatment Effect Parameters

Consider
W_{i}\equiv(Y_{i},D_{i},Z_{i},X_{i})
where
Y_{i}
is a scalar-valued outcome,
D_{i}
is a discrete treatment,
Z_{i}
is a binary instrument, and
X_{i}
is a vector of controls. Under standard assumptions as in
Imbens & Rubin 2015
, the following objects correspond to well-defined causal quantities.

A.1.1
Weighted Average Potential Outcome

The weighted average potential outcome corresponding to treatment level
d
is

\displaystyle\theta_{0}\equiv\mathrm{E}[\omega(X_{i})\mathrm{E}[Y_{i}|D_{i}=d,X_{i}]],

for some known weighting function
\omega
. The corresponding IPW score is

\displaystyle m(W_{i};\theta,\gamma^{(1)})\equiv\frac{\mathbbm{1}\{D_{i}=d\}Y_{i}}{\gamma^{(1)}(X_{i})}\omega(X_{i})-\theta,

where the nuisance parameter
\gamma^{(1)}
has true value
\gamma_{0}^{(1)}(X_{i})\equiv\mathrm{E}[\mathbbm{1}\{D_{i}=d\}|X_{i}]
. The corresponding correction term is given by

\displaystyle\alpha^{(1)}_{0}(X_{i})\equiv-\frac{\mathrm{E}[Y_{i}|D_{i}=d,X_{i}]\omega(X_{i})}{\mathrm{E}[\mathbbm{1}\{D_{i}=d\}|X_{i}]}.

A.1.2
Average Treatment Effect on the Treated

The average treatment effect on the treated for a binary treatment
D_{i}
is

\displaystyle\theta_{0}\equiv\mathrm{E}\left[\mathrm{E}[Y_{i}|D_{i}=1,X_{i}]-\mathrm{E}[Y_{i}|D_{i}=0,X_{i}]|D_{i}=1\right],

with corresponding IPW score

\displaystyle m(W_{i};\theta,\gamma^{(1)},\gamma^{(2)})\equiv\frac{D_{i}Y_{i}}{\gamma^{(2)}}-\frac{\gamma^{(1)}(X_{i})(1-D_{i})Y_{i}}{\gamma^{(2)}(1-\gamma^{(1)}(X_{i}))}-\frac{D_{i}}{\gamma^{(2)}}\theta,

where the nuisance parameters
\gamma^{(1)}
and
\gamma^{(2)}
take true values at
\gamma_{0}^{(1)}(X_{i})\equiv\mathrm{E}[D_{i}|X_{i}]
and
\gamma_{0}^{(2)}\equiv\mathrm{E}[D_{i}]
. The corresponding correction terms are given by

\displaystyle\alpha_{0}^{(1)}(X_{i})\equiv-\frac{1}{\mathrm{E}[D_{i}]}\left(\frac{\mathrm{E}[D_{i}|X_{i}]\mathrm{E}[Y_{i}|D_{i}=0,X_{i}]}{1-\mathrm{E}[D_{i}|X_{i}]}+\mathrm{E}[Y_{i}|D_{i}=0,X_{i}]\right),\quad\alpha_{0}^{(2)}\equiv 0.

A.1.3
Local Average Treatment Effect

The local average treatment effect (LATE) for a binary instrument
Z_{i}
is defined as

\displaystyle\theta_{0}\equiv\frac{\mathrm{E}\left[\mathrm{E}[Y_{i}|Z_{i}=1,X_{i}]-\mathrm{E}[Y_{i}|Z_{i}=0,X_{i}]\right]}{\mathrm{E}\left[\mathrm{E}[D_{i}|Z_{i}=1,X_{i}]-\mathrm{E}[D_{i}|Z_{i}=0,X_{i}]\right]},

with corresponding IPW score

\displaystyle m(W_{i};\theta,\gamma^{(1)})\equiv\frac{Z_{i}Y_{i}}{\gamma^{(1)}(X_{i})}-\frac{(1-Z_{i})Y_{i}}{1-\gamma^{(1)}(X_{i})}-\theta\left(\frac{Z_{i}D_{i}}{\gamma^{(1)}(X_{i})}-\frac{(1-Z_{i})D_{i}}{1-\gamma^{(1)}(X_{i})}\right),

where the nuisance parameter
\gamma^{(1)}
takes true value at
\gamma^{(1)}_{0}(X_{i})\equiv\mathrm{E}[Z_{i}|X_{i}]
. The corresponding correction term is given by

\displaystyle\alpha^{(1)}_{0}(X_{i})\equiv-\frac{\mathrm{E}[Y_{i}|Z_{i}=1,X_{i}]}{\mathrm{E}[Z_{i}|X_{i}]}-\frac{\mathrm{E}[Y_{i}|Z_{i}=0,X_{i}]}{1-\mathrm{E}[Z_{i}|X_{i}]}+\theta_{0}\left(\frac{\mathrm{E}[D_{i}|Z_{i}=1,X_{i}]}{\mathrm{E}[Z_{i}|X_{i}]}+\frac{\mathrm{E}[D_{i}|Z_{i}=0,X_{i}]}{1-\mathrm{E}[Z_{i}|X_{i}]}\right).

A.2
Regression Parameters

Consider
W_{i}\equiv(Y_{i},D_{i},Z_{i},X_{i})
where
Y_{i}
is a scalar-valued outcome,
D_{i}
is a vector of variables of interest,
Z_{i}
is a vector of instruments, and
X_{i}
is a vector of controls.

A.2.1
Partially Linear Regression and Partially Linear Instrumental Variables

Consider the instrumental variable (IV) regression

\displaystyle Y_{i}=D_{i}^{\top}\theta_{0}+\beta_{0}(X_{i})+\varepsilon_{i},

where the target parameter
\theta_{0}
and confounding function
\beta_{0}
are defined through the orthogonality restrictions
\mathrm{E}[Z_{i}\varepsilon_{i}]=0
and
\mathrm{E}[\varepsilon_{i}|X_{i}]=0
, and the IV relevance condition that
\mathrm{E}[\text{Cov}(Z_{i},D_{i}|X)]
has full column rank. This setting corresponds to a scenario where a researcher has a known set of instruments,
Z
, that are taken to satisfy the exclusion restriction only after conditioning on controls
X
and the researcher does not wish to impose the functional form in which confounds enter the model.
Further note that we recover partially linear regression by setting
Z_{i}=D_{i}
. Solving for
\beta_{0}
and substituting, we obtain the vector-valued score

\displaystyle m(W_{i};\theta,\gamma^{(1)},\gamma^{(2)})\equiv Z_{i}(Y_{i}-\gamma^{(1)}(X_{i})-\theta^{\top}(D_{i}-\gamma^{(2)}(X_{i}))),

where the nuisance parameters take true value at
\gamma^{(1)}_{0}(X_{i})\equiv\mathrm{E}[Y_{i}|X_{i}]
and
\gamma^{(2)}_{0}(X_{i})\equiv\mathrm{E}[D_{i}|X_{i}]
. The corresponding correction terms are given by

\displaystyle\alpha^{(1)}_{0}(X_{i})\equiv-\mathrm{E}[Z_{i}|X_{i}],\quad\alpha^{(2)}_{0}(X_{i})\equiv\mathrm{E}[Z_{i}|X_{i}]\theta^{\top}.

A.2.2
Flexible Partially Linear Instrumental Variables

Consider the IV regression

\displaystyle Y_{i}=D_{i}^{\top}\theta_{0}+\beta_{0}(X_{i})+\varepsilon_{i},

where the target parameter
\theta_{0}
and the confounding function
\beta_{0}
are defined through the orthogonality restrictions
\mathrm{E}[\varepsilon_{i}|Z_{i},X_{i}]=0
, and the IV relevance condition that
\mathrm{E}[\text{Var}(\mathrm{E}[D_{i}|Z_{i},X_{i}]|X_{i})]
is positive definite. Note that this setting differs from that considered in Section
A.2.1
in that we use the stronger orthogonality condition
\mathrm{E}[\varepsilon_{i}|Z_{i},X_{i}]=0
, which implies that any function of
(Z_{i},X_{i})
can be used as a valid instrument. Given this, we further suppose that the researcher would like to use the optimal instrument under homoskedasticity (
\mathrm{E}[D_{i}|Z_{i},X_{i}]
).
27
27

27

Other targets could also be considered.

Using that
\mathrm{E}[\varepsilon_{i}|Z_{i},X_{i}]=0
implies
\mathrm{E}[\varepsilon_{i}|X_{i}]=0
by the law of iterated expectations, we can solve for
\beta_{0}
as before. Further, note that
\mathrm{E}[\varepsilon_{i}|Z_{i},X_{i}]=0
implies
\mathrm{E}[\gamma^{(3)}_{0}(Z_{i},X_{i})\varepsilon_{i}]=0
for
\gamma^{(3)}_{0}(Z_{i},X_{i})\equiv\mathrm{E}[D_{i}|Z_{i},X_{i}]
. This motivates the vector-valued score

\displaystyle m(W_{i};\theta,\gamma^{(1)},\gamma^{(2)},\gamma^{(3)})\equiv\gamma^{(3)}(Z_{i},X_{i})(Y_{i}-\gamma^{(1)}(X_{i})-\theta^{\top}(D_{i}-\gamma^{(2)}(X_{i}))),

where the nuisance parameters
\gamma^{(1)}
,
\gamma^{(2)}
, and
\gamma^{(3)}
take true values, respectively, at
\gamma^{(1)}_{0}(X_{i})\equiv\mathrm{E}[Y_{i}|X_{i}]
,
\gamma^{(2)}_{0}(X_{i})\equiv\mathrm{E}[D_{i}|X_{i}]
, and
\gamma^{(3)}_{0}(Z_{i},X_{i})\equiv\mathrm{E}[D_{i}|Z_{i},X_{i}]
.
The corresponding correction terms are given by

\displaystyle\alpha^{(1)}_{0}(X_{i})\equiv-\mathrm{E}[D_{i}|X_{i}],\quad\alpha^{(2)}_{0}(X_{i})\equiv\mathrm{E}[D_{i}|X_{i}]\theta^{\top},\quad\alpha^{(3)}_{0}(Z_{i},X_{i})\equiv 0.

A.3
Fixed Effect Regression Parameters

Consider
W_{i}\equiv(Y_{it},D_{it},Z_{it},X_{it})^{T}_{t=0}
where
t
denotes a secondary dimension (e.g., time),
Y_{it}
is a scalar-valued outcome,
D_{it}
is a vector of variables of interest,
Z_{it}
is a vector of instruments, and
X_{it}
is a vector of controls. It is convenient to define the first difference operator
\Delta A_{it}\equiv A_{it}-A_{it-1}
for random variables
A_{it}
and
A_{it-1}
.

Consider the IV regression with fixed effects
\iota_{i}

\displaystyle Y_{it}=D_{it}^{\top}\theta_{0}+\beta^{(t)}_{0}(X_{it})+\iota_{i}+\varepsilon_{it},\quad\forall t=0,1,\ldots,T,

where the target parameter
\theta_{0}
and the differenced confounding functions
\{\Delta\beta^{(t)}_{0}\}_{t=0}^{T}
are defined through the orthogonality restrictions
\mathrm{E}[\sum_{t=1}^{T}\Delta Z_{it}\Delta\varepsilon_{it}]=0
,
\mathrm{E}[\Delta\varepsilon_{it}|X_{it},X_{it-1}]=0,\forall t\in\{1,\ldots,T\}
, and the IV relevance condition that
\mathrm{E}[\text{Cov}(\Delta Z_{it},\Delta D_{it}|X_{it},X_{it-1})]
has full column rank for at least some
t\in\{1,\ldots,T\}
. Note that we recover fixed effects partially linear regression by setting
(Z_{it})^{T}_{t=1}=(D_{it})^{T}_{t=1}
.
Solving for
\Delta\beta^{(t)}_{0}
and substituting, we obtain the vector-valued score

\displaystyle m(W_{i};\theta,\{\gamma^{(1t)},\gamma^{(2t)}\}_{t=1}^{T})\equiv\sum_{t=1}^{T}\Delta Z_{it}(\Delta Y_{it}-\gamma^{(1t)}(X_{it},X_{it-1})-\theta^{\top}(\Delta D_{it}-\gamma^{(2t)}(X_{it},X_{it-1}))),

where the nuisance parameters
\{\gamma^{(1t)},\gamma^{(2t)}\}_{t=1}^{T}
take true value at
\gamma^{(1t)}_{0}(X_{it},X_{it-1})\equiv\mathrm{E}[\Delta Y_{it}|X_{it},X_{it-1}]
and
\gamma^{(2t)}_{0}(X_{i})\equiv\mathrm{E}[\Delta D_{it}|X_{it},X_{it-1}]
, for all
t\in\{1,\ldots,T\}
. The corresponding correction terms are given by

\displaystyle\alpha^{(1t)}_{0}(X_{it},X_{it-1})\equiv-\mathrm{E}[\Delta Z_{it}|X_{it},X_{it-1}],\quad\alpha^{(2t)}_{0}(X_{it},X_{it-1})\equiv\mathrm{E}[\Delta Z_{it}|X_{it},X_{it-1}]\theta^{\top}.

Appendix B
Calibrated Simulation Setup

This appendix details the calibrated simulation considered in Section
4
.

Let
\{(y_{i},d_{i},x_{i})\}_{i=1}^{n}
denote the sample of
n=9,915
observations in the 1991 wave of the SIPP, where
y_{i}
is net-financial assets,
d_{i}
denotes 401(k) eligibility, and
x_{i}
is the vector of controls discussed in Section
4
. The calibrated simulation proceeds as follows:

1.

Using the full sample, estimate the reduced forms
{\mathrm{P}}(D_{i}=1|X_{i})
and
\mathrm{E}[Y_{i}|D_{i}=d,X_{i}],d\in\{0,1\}
, using random forest estimators, each with 1000 trees and a minimum node size of 10 observations. Call the corresponding estimates
\widehat{r}
and
\widehat{\ell}^{(d)},d\in\{0,1\},
respectively.

2.

Estimate the treatment-specific variances via
\widehat{\sigma}_{d}^{2}\equiv\frac{1}{\sum_{i=1}^{n}\mathbbm{1}\{d_{i}=d\}}\sum_{i:d_{i}=d}(y_{i}-\widehat{\ell}^{(d)}(x_{i}))^{2}
.

3.

For simulation
s\in\{1,\ldots,10000\}
, do:

(a)

Create a simulated dataset
\{(\tilde{y}_{i,s},\tilde{d}_{i,s},x_{i})\}_{i=1}^{n}
. Draw simulated treatments
\tilde{d}_{i,s}=1
with probability
\widehat{r}(x_{i})
and
\tilde{d}_{i,s}=0
otherwise. Draw simulated outcomes
\tilde{y}_{i,s}=\widehat{\ell}^{(\tilde{d}_{i,s})}(x_{i})+\tilde{\varepsilon}_{i,s}
where
\tilde{\varepsilon}_{i,s}\sim\mathcal{N}(0,\widehat{\sigma}_{\tilde{d}_{i,s}}^{2}).

(b)

Estimate the ATE estimators of Section
4
on the simulated dataset and store the results.

4.

Compute simulation summary statistics against the true ATE in the simulated datasets, which is given by
\sum_{i=1}^{n}(\widehat{\ell}^{(1)}(x_{i})-\widehat{\ell}^{(0)}(x_{i}))\approx 6889
.
