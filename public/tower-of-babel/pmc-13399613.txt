Perioperative Intravenous Dexamethasone Improves Pain and Functional Outcomes After Arthroscopic Rotator Cuff Repair
Authors: Trevor D Wolterink, Germain Craddock, Joshua P Castle, Michael A Gaudiani, Chimdindu Obinero, Alexander S Jurayj, Christian A Freitag, Nathan Y Li, Jared Mahylis, Stephanie J Muh
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractPurposeTo evaluate the effect of perioperative dexamethasone on patient‐reported pain, functional outcomes, and minimal clinically important difference (MCID) achievement following arthroscopic rotator cuff repair.MethodsPatients who underwent rotator cuff repair between 2013 and 2023 were identified and divided into groups. Those who received peri operative dexamethasone were compared with controls. Outcomes included visual analog scale (VAS) pain, patient‐reported outcomes measurement information system (PROMIS) upper extremity, PROMIS pain interference, and MCID. Multivariate logistic and linear regression models adjusted for age, sex, body mass index, diabetes, race/ethnicity, smoking, and preoperative opioid use. Subgroup analyses were performed by tear size.ResultsA total of 309 patients was included (187 dexamethasone and 122 control). Dexamethasone administration was associated with improved functional recovery and sustained pain reduction. PROMIS upper extremity and PROMIS pain interference scores favored Dexamethasone at 6 weeks, 3 months, and 6 months (all P < .01). VAS pain was significantly lower at 12 months (1.9 ± 2.9 vs 3.1 ± 3.5, P = .003) and 24 months (1.0 ± 2.5 vs 1.8 ± 3.1, P = .035). Only 37% of patients completed a 2‐year follow‐up, limiting the strength of these long‐term comparisons. In small/medium tears, MCID achievement for VAS was greater at 6 weeks (31% vs 21%, P = .041) and 3 months (34% vs 21%, P = .012). Logistic regression confirmed dexamethasone as an independent predictor of early MCID (OR 1.67, 95% CI 1.02‐2.73, P = .041). Large/massive tears showed transient VAS improvement without MCID benefit. Opioid consumption did not differ between groups (all P > .12). Dexamethasone reduced ondansetron use in large/massive tears at 0‐4 hour (β = −0.34, P = .04) and in small/medium tears at 4‐8 hour (β = −0.19, P = .04), with no differences for other antiemetics. Complication rates were similar (11.2% vs 4.9%, P = .055).ConclusionsPerioperative intravenous dexamethasone is a safe adjunct to arthroscopic rotator cuff repair, supporting early antiemetic benefits, durable pain relief, and selective improvements in patient‐reported outcomes. Benefits appear most pronounced in small/medium tears at early follow‐up and large/massive tears at later stages, suggesting tear size‐specific response.Level of EvidenceLevel III, retrospective comparative study.

The incidence of rotator cuff repair (RCR) continues to rise, with over 500,000 procedures performed annually in the United States. 1 Optimizing perioperative recovery is essential for controlling immediate postoperative pain, facilitating early mobilization, achieving clinically meaningful functional improvement, and sustaining pain relief over the long‐term. 2 , 3 , 4 Early postoperative pain can impede participation in rehabilitation and delay the restoration of shoulder function. In contrast, persistent pain is associated with reduced patient‐reported outcome scores and slower return to activity. 5 , 6 Targeted perioperative strategies that address both acute and sustained recovery, particularly in subgroups such as patients with small and medium tears who may have the greatest capacity for rapid improvement, have the potential to improve outcomes after RCR meaningfully. 7

Perioperative intravenous (IV) dexamethasone is a potent, long‐acting glucocorticoid with both anti‐inflammatory and antiemetic properties. 8 In the context of shoulder surgery, it has been shown to prolong the duration of regional nerve blocks, reduce postoperative pain intensity, and decrease postoperative nausea and vomiting. 9 , 10 , 11 The mechanism of analgesic benefit is thought to involve suppression of inflammatory mediators and modulation of nociceptive signaling, which may extend beyond the immediate postoperative period. 11 , 12 Randomized controlled trials and meta‐analyses have shown that dexamethasone, whether administered intravenously or perineurally, can meaningfully enhance the duration of analgesia and improve early recovery after arthroscopic shoulder procedures. 9 , 10 , 12 , 13 , 14 However, most studies have focused on early pain relief, with limited attention to patient‐centered outcomes such as achieving the minimum clinically important difference (MCID) in pain scores or exploring whether these benefits differ by rotator cuff tear size.

Although perioperative IV dexamethasone has shown consistent benefits in extending regional block duration, reducing pain, and decreasing postoperative nausea and vomiting in both arthroscopic and open shoulder procedures, 9 , 10 , 11 , 12 , 13 , 14 , 15 the literature is limited in evaluating its effect on longer‐term, patient‐centered outcomes following RCR. Specifically, few studies have examined whether perioperative dexamethasone increases the proportion of patients achieving a MCID in pain scores. This threshold reflects meaningful improvement from the patient's perspective, and is not clear whether these effects vary by rotator cuff tear size. Furthermore, there is a paucity of evidence assessing whether the potential benefits of dexamethasone persist beyond the immediate postoperative window to influence recovery trajectories in the early rehabilitation period. Addressing these questions may guide selective perioperative use of dexamethasone to optimize functional recovery after RCR. The purpose of this study was to evaluate the effect of perioperative dexamethasone on patient‐reported pain, functional outcomes according to tear size, and MCID achievement following arthroscopic RCR. We hypothesized that patients who receive dexamethasone would experience improved pain control and functional outcomes, which would differ according to tear size, after undergoing arthroscopic RCR compared with the control group.

METHODS

This retrospective cohort study included adult patients who underwent primary arthroscopic RCR at a single tertiary academic medical center between January 1, 2013, and December 31, 2023. Eligible cases were identified using institutional surgical logs and billing records (Current Procedural Terminology code 29827; International Classification of Diseases, Tenth Revision, codes M75.1 and S46.0) and cross‐referenced with anesthesia records to determine perioperative IV dexamethasone administration. The Institutional Review Board approved the study protocol and granted a waiver of informed consent due to the retrospective design.

Inclusion criteria were patients aged ≥18 years who received a preoperative interscalene regional anesthesia block and underwent arthroscopic RCR, with or without concomitant acromioplasty, biceps tenodesis or tenotomy, or distal clavicle excision. Exclusion criteria were subscapularis or unclassified rotator cuff tears, prior ipsilateral RCR or other shoulder surgery, end‐stage kidney or liver disease requiring transplantation, and a history of alcohol or drug abuse. Patients were also excluded if they did not receive a preoperative regional anesthesia block, received a block with an anesthetic other than bupivacaine or ropivacaine, received <10 mg of perioperative dexamethasone, or had a postoperative continuous indwelling catheter. All surgeries were performed in the beach chair position under general anesthesia with interscalene block by board‐certified sports medicine or shoulder and elbow orthopaedic surgeons.

A retrospective chart review was conducted to collect demographic, injury, surgical, pain, medication, and complication data. Pain scores and medication administration were recorded up to 3 months postoperatively to capture early recovery, and complications were tracked for up to 2 years. Demographic variables included age, sex, race, body mass index (BMI), tobacco use, and diabetes status. Pre‐ and postoperative hemoglobin A1C values (within 3 months of surgery) were recorded when available. History of opioid use within 3 months preoperatively was also documented. 16 Injury characteristics included mechanism (degenerative or traumatic) and chronicity, with acute tears defined as <6 weeks and chronic tears as >6 weeks from onset to surgery. Preoperative imaging was reviewed for the number of tendons torn, tear thickness (partial or full), coronal and sagittal tear length, and muscle atrophy or retraction. Full‐thickness tears were classified by size using the Cofield classification: small (0‐1 cm), medium (1‐3 cm), large (3‐5 cm), or massive (≥2 tendons or >5 cm). 17 Operative reports were reviewed for concomitant procedures, and for time in outpatient recovery unit (hours).

Visual analog scale (VAS) pain scores were recorded preoperatively, at 0‐4 hours and 4‐8 hours postoperatively, and during routine follow‐up visits up to 3 months. The highest score was used if multiple scores were available within a time interval.

Perioperative medication use was obtained from anesthesia and medication administration records, including regional anesthesia block agents (bupivacaine or ropivacaine), 18 acetaminophen, opioids, and antiemetics (diphenhydramine, dimenhydrinate, meclizine, metoclopramide, ondansetron, and scopolamine). Opioid use was recorded intraoperatively and during the 0‐4 and 4‐8 hour postoperative intervals, converted to milligram morphine equivalents, and summed for each period. 19

Postdischarge opioid prescriptions were tracked for up to 3 months, with the number and dosage of tablets converted to milligram morphine equivalents. Prescription refills were categorized into 0‐3.5, 3.5‐9, and 9‐12 weeks after surgery, corresponding to standard follow‐up visits at 2, 6, and 12 weeks. These intervals were selected to align with institutional follow‐up patterns and ensure consistency in postoperative data capture.

Statistical Analysis

Patients who met the inclusion criteria and received ≥10 mg of perioperative IV dexamethasone were classified into the dexamethasone group. Those who met the same criteria but did not receive perioperative dexamethasone comprised the control group. No matching was performed between groups. Both groups were stratified by tear size into partial, small/medium, and large/massive subgroups for analysis.

Statistical analyses were performed using SPSS Statistics 28.0.1.1 (IBM, Armonk, NY), Python (v3.13), and Microsoft Excel (Microsoft Corporation, Redmond, WA). Analyses were conducted using available data without imputation; patients were not excluded based on data completeness. Descriptive statistics were calculated for all variables. Continuous variables were reported as means with standard deviations or medians with interquartile ranges, depending on distribution, and categorical variables as frequencies with percentages. Between‐group comparisons (dexamethasone vs. no dexamethasone) were performed using independent‐samples t‐tests or Mann‐Whitney U tests for continuous variables and chi‐square tests for categorical variables (Fisher's exact test if any expected cell count was ≤5).

MCID thresholds were predefined based on published literature as a ≥2‐point reduction for VAS pain scores, ≥4.5‐point improvement for patient‐reported outcomes measurement information system (PROMIS) upper extremity (PROMIS‐UE), ≥4‐point reduction for PROMIS pain interference (PROMIS‐PI), and ≥3.5‐point reduction for PROMIS‐D. MCID thresholds were calculated using the ½ standard deviations method applied to change scores within each tear size subgroup. The percentage of patients achieving MCID was compared between groups using chi‐square or Fisher's exact tests. 20 , 21 , 22 , 23

Independent predictors of achieving MCID for each outcome measure (VAS pain score, PROMIS‐UE, PROMIS‐PI, and PROMIS‐D) were identified using multivariable logistic regression. Analyses included all available postoperative timepoints (1 week, 6 weeks, 3 months, 6 months, 1 year, and 2 years). Covariates were selected based on clinical relevance and included age, sex, BMI, tear size, preoperative VAS, and dexamethasone use. Categorical variables (sex, tear size) were treated as factors, and no interaction terms were included.

To account for multiple comparisons across timepoints and subgroups, a Holm‐Bonferroni correction was applied for the primary outcomes (VAS pain scores and opioid consumption). Statistical significance was set at P < .05 for both unadjusted and adjusted analyses.

RESULTS

Two thousand forty‐five patients were screened for eligibility, with 309 meeting inclusion criteria for analysis. The dexamethasone cohort comprised 187 patients: 25 with partial‐thickness tears, 114 with small/medium full‐thickness tears, and 48 with large/massive tears. The control cohort included 122 patients: 29 with partial‐thickness tears, 62 with small/medium full‐thickness tears, and 31 with large/massive tears (Tables 1 and 2).

Demographic, Injury, and Surgical Information

The dexamethasone and control groups were similar in age and sex distribution. Race distribution differed significantly between groups (P = .033), with the dexamethasone group having a higher proportion of Caucasian patients and the control group a higher proportion of African American patients. The dexamethasone group had a significantly lower mean BMI (30.7 ± 6.0 kg/m2 vs 33.5 ± 7.5 kg/m2; P < .001) and a lower prevalence of diabetes mellitus (20.3% vs 36.9%; P = .001) and preoperative opioid use (29.9% vs 42.6%; P = .023).

Injury, tear, and surgical characteristics are summarized in Table 2. magnetic resonance imaging was used more frequently in the control group (63.1% vs 50.3%; P = .026). Tear size distribution also differed significantly between groups (P = .034), and distal clavicle excision was more common in the control group (21.3% vs 5.9%; P < .001).

Perioperative Dexamethasone Administration Timing

Most patients in the dexamethasone group received the medication preoperatively (137/187, 73.3%), followed by intraoperative administration (47/187, 25.1%). A small proportion received dexamethasone postoperatively (3/187, 1.6%), and no patients received both pre‐ and postoperative doses (Table 3).

Postoperative Pain, Function Outcomes, and MCID Achievement

Across the whole cohort, mean VAS scores improved in both groups over time, with the dexamethasone group showing significantly lower pain at 12 months (1.9 ± 2.9 vs 3.1 ± 3.5, P = .003) and 24 months (1.0 ± 2.5 vs 1.8 ± 3.1, P = .035) compared with controls (Table 4). Only 37% of patients completed a 2‐year follow‐up, which limits the strength of long‐term conclusions. PROMIS‐UE scores were significantly higher in the dexamethasone group preoperatively (P = .007) and remained superior at 6 weeks (P = .001), 3 months (P < .001), and 6 months (P < .001). PROMIS‐PI scores were also higher preoperatively (P = .007) and at 6 weeks, 3 months, and 6 months (all P < .001). PROMIS‐D scores were higher preoperatively (P = .002) and at 6 weeks (P = .035) in the dexamethasone group, with no significant differences thereafter.

Subgroup and Multivariate Analysis

Multivariate logistic regression showed that dexamethasone use was independently associated with higher odds of achieving the MCID in VAS pain scores at 6 weeks in the total cohort (OR 1.67, 95% CI 1.02‐2.73, P = .043) and in small/medium tears at 3 months (OR 1.98, 95% CI 1.00‐3.90, P = .050) (Table 10). Significant treatment‐tear size interaction effects were significant at both 6 weeks (P = .044) and 3 months (P = .047), with large/massive tears demonstrating greater pain reduction at each time point (β = −0.93, 95% CI −1.82 to −0.30, P = .043 at 6 weeks; β = −1.33, 95% CI −2.66 to −0.01, P = .048 at 3 months) (Tables 11 and 12). Smoking status was also significant, with nonsmokers achieving greater pain reduction at 6 weeks compared with smokers (β = −0.71, 95% CI −1.34 to −0.08, P = .027) (Table 11).

DISCUSSION

The most important finding of the present study is that perioperative administration of 10 mg IV dexamethasone was associated with meaningful improvements in early functional recovery and perioperative symptom control in patients undergoing arthroscopic RCR. These benefits were observed without an increase in retear rates, infection, or wound‐healing complications, reinforcing the safety of a single perioperative dose in this setting.

These results align with prior randomized controlled trials and meta‐analyses showing that intravenous dexamethasone improves postoperative analgesia and functional recovery following shoulder arthroscopy and arthroplasty, 9 , 15 while also reducing the incidence of postoperative nausea and vomiting across orthopaedic procedures. 24 In the present study, patients receiving dexamethasone achieved higher rates of clinically important improvement in PROMIS‐UE at both early and late follow‐up and early gains in PROMIS‐PI and PROMIS‐D. Subgroup analysis revealed that small/medium tears had greater odds of achieving MCID in VAS pain scores at 6 weeks and 3 months. In contrast, large/massive tears experienced greater pain reduction at both time points in the interaction analysis. Additionally, dexamethasone use was associated with significantly reduced ondansetron requirements in the immediate postoperative period, within 0‐4 hours for large/massive tears and 4‐8 hours for small/medium tears, highlighting its potential role in mitigating postoperative nausea and vomiting (PONV), a common complication linked to delayed discharge, reduced patient satisfaction, and increased healthcare utilization. 9 , 15 , 24

These findings are clinically relevant because early pain reduction and functional improvement within the first few months after RCR are critical to optimizing rehabilitation adherence, minimizing opioid exposure, and facilitating earlier return to daily activities. 7 , 20 , 21 , 23 Previous randomized trials in orthopaedic surgery have shown perioperative dexamethasone to provide short‐term analgesic and antiemetic benefits, 9 , 15 , 24 but few have quantified patient‐centered outcomes such as MCID achievement. Our study shows that a single 10 mg IV dose can increase the likelihood of achieving clinically important PROMIS‐UE and PROMIS‐PI improvements, changes that patients perceive as meaningful, at early and sustained follow‐up. The tear size‐specific advantages we observed, with small/medium tears showing robust early MCID attainment and large/massive tears experiencing greater absolute VAS reductions, suggest that baseline pathology may influence steroid responsiveness. Additionally, our finding of reduced ondansetron requirements aligns with prior meta‐analyses on dexamethasone's antiemetic efficacy, 15 , 24 adding evidence for a tear size‐dependent effect in the shoulder arthroscopy setting. Finally, the absence of increased infection, retear, or wound complications in our cohort corroborates previous safety data from arthroplasty and arthroscopy studies, 25 , 26 supporting the integration of perioperative dexamethasone into multimodal recovery protocols.

Multiple randomized controlled trials and meta‐analyses in shoulder surgery have shown that perioperative IV dexamethasone, most commonly in the 4‐10 mg range, provides consistent short‐term analgesic benefit and reduces PONV without increasing surgical complications. 9 , 24 , 27 , 28 In RCR, prior studies have reported lower early pain scores and decreased rescue antiemetic use. 10 , 29 , 30 However, most did not stratify outcomes by tear size or evaluate patient‐centered thresholds such as the MCID. 7 , 20 , 21 , 22

Our findings confirm these established early analgesic and antiemetic effects while extending prior work in several ways. First, we show sustained functional improvements at late follow‐up in PROMIS‐UE and PROMIS‐PI, an outcome rarely reported in prior RCR dexamethasone trials. 23 Second, we identify a tear size‐specific benefit profile: small/medium tears had higher MCID attainment, whereas large/massive tears experienced greater absolute VAS reduction. 7 , 21 , 22 Third, our results align with meta‐analytic evidence across orthopaedic procedures showing a approximately 30%‐40% reduction in PONV incidence and prolonged time first to rescue analgesia, 9 , 24 , 27 , 28 while adding the observation that the timing of the antiemetic effect may vary by tear size (0‐4 hours vs 4‐8 hours).

Although opioid consumption trended lower in the dexamethasone group at all follow‐up intervals, these differences did not reach statistical significance. Although modest, the numerical reduction at 1 week may still be clinically relevant in the context of multimodal analgesia and warrants further investigation in adequately powered prospective studies. 15 , 19 , 28 , 29 , 30

Mechanistic Rationale

The observed benefits of perioperative dexamethasone are biologically plausible given its potent anti‐inflammatory and antiemetic properties. As a synthetic glucocorticoid, dexamethasone inhibits phospholipase A2 activity and downstream prostaglandin and leukotriene synthesis, reducing peripheral inflammation and central sensitization. 8 , 16 These effects likely underlie the early pain reduction and sustained functional improvement in PROMIS‐UE and PROMIS‐PI scores.

Its antiemetic activity is thought to involve inhibition of prostaglandin release, decreased serotonin turnover in the gastrointestinal tract, and modulation of the nucleus tractus solitarius, a central emetic center. 8 , 24 These mechanisms align with meta‐analytic evidence showing dexamethasone reduces PONV incidence by approximately 30%‐40% and prolongs time to first rescue analgesia. 24 , 27 , 28 Our tear size‐specific findings, antiemetic effects appearing earlier for large/massive tears (0‐4 hours) versus small/medium tears (4‐8 hours), may reflect differences in surgical duration, tissue trauma, and systemic inflammatory response. 29 , 30

Glucocorticoids also suppress postoperative edema and cytokine‐mediated hyperalgesia, 8 , 16 which may explain the greater absolute VAS reduction observed in large/massive tears, where soft tissue disruption is more extensive. Conversely, patients with smaller tears may experience less baseline inflammation, making early MCID attainment in PROMIS‐UE and PROMIS‐PI more likely. 21 , 22

Finally, the absence of increased infection, retear, or wound‐healing complications is consistent with previous arthroscopy and arthroplasty literature, where single‐dose perioperative dexamethasone has shown a favorable safety profile. 25 , 26

Future research should focus on prospective randomized controlled trials with standardized dexamethasone administration timing and dosage to validate these findings and clarify optimal protocols. Mechanistic studies evaluating inflammatory cytokine profiles, pain mediators, and perioperative stress responses by tear size may further elucidate the biological basis for differential responsiveness. Additionally, larger multicenter studies with diverse populations are warranted to enhance generalizability, assess rare adverse events, and evaluate long‐term functional and structural outcomes.

Limitations

This study has several limitations inherent to its retrospective design, including the inability to establish causality and the potential for unmeasured confounding variables. The lack of power analysis and small sample size increases the risk of a type II error. Despite multivariate adjustment for demographic and surgical covariates, baseline differences, such as BMI, diabetes prevalence, race distribution, and magnetic resonance imaging utilization, may have influenced outcomes. Tear size distribution also differed between groups, which could partially account for subgroup‐specific findings, although interaction analyses were performed.

The single‐center setting and reliance on electronic medical record documentation may limit generalizability and introduce selection or documentation bias. Administration timing of dexamethasone was not standardized and varied between pre‐, intra‐, and postoperative periods, potentially affecting the magnitude and onset of its effects.

PROMIS and VAS scores were collected at fixed follow‐up intervals; interim fluctuations in pain and function were not captured, and loss to follow‐up at later time points could bias results toward patients with better recovery. Only 37% of patients completed a 2‐year follow‐up, which substantially limits the strength and generalizability of long‐term conclusions. Post hoc power analysis indicated that many comparisons were underpowered (<80%), increasing the risk of type II error and limiting conclusions for outcomes without statistically significant differences (Tables 16 and 17). Additionally, postoperative opioid use was estimated from prescription refills, which may not directly reflect actual consumption patterns.

Finally, although complication rates, including infection and retear, were similar between groups, the relatively small sample size for rare adverse events limits our ability to rule out potential safety concerns associated with perioperative dexamethasone definitively. Additionally, retear rates were determined clinically, as routine imaging follow‐up was not performed, which may underestimate or overestimate actual structural failure.

CONCLUSIONS

Perioperative IV dexamethasone is a safe adjunct to arthroscopic RCR, supporting early antiemetic benefits, durable pain relief, and selective improvements in patient‐reported outcomes. Benefits appear most pronounced in small/medium tears at early follow‐up and in large/massive tears at later stages, suggesting a tear size‐specific response.
