Prospective ECUAging observational study: cohort design and preliminary results
Authors: Israel Vinueza-Fernández, Tamara Rodriguez Quintana, Isabel Masson, Alejandra Marroig, Fernando Massa, Mario J. Grijalva, Graciela Muniz-Terrera
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundThe global population is aging, including in historically youthful regions such as low- and middle-income countries in Latin America. Evidence on older adults living in rural areas of this region remains limited. The EcuAging study was designed to fill this gap by characterizing adults aged 55 years and older in a rural inland province of Ecuador.MethodsOver a two-week period in June-July 2023, mobile stations were set up in rural communities of Calvas and Gonzanama counties in the province of Loja, Ecuador. Adults aged 55 and above living in these regions were invited to attend health evaluations and to join our study. 269 individuals agreed to participate in the study. They completed questionnaires about their ociodemographic characteristics, health measures, lifestyle, physical and mental health, functional status, and quality of life. The majority self-identified as mestizo (93.7%) and reported low prevalence of smoking (4.1%) or alcohol consumption (7.4%). Background

The World Health Organization (WHO) has projected that between 2020 and 2030, the population of older adults will increase by 34% [1]. Critically, low- and middle-income countries, which traditionally had a large proportion of young individuals and a smaller proportion of older adults, will also experience substantial demographic changes with important increases in the segment of older adults [2]. These demographic changes will have multiple consequences, including higher demands for health and social care services, which, in low- and middle-income countries where resources are scarce, will lead to considerable challenges for individuals and health and social care providers.

Ecuador, a Latin American country of about 17 million people, also follows the trend of demographic changes [3]. Notably, the changes in Ecuador’s demographics are influenced by significant increases in life expectancy. In the 1950 s, life expectancy at birth was only 48.6 years, but by 2022, it had risen to 76.7 years. Projections suggest that in the next 30 years, life expectancy will increase to 82.3 years, surpassing that of more developed nations, which currently stands at around 80.9 years [4, 5]. At this growth rate, Ecuador is heading towards the category of an aging society by the year 2065, as individuals aged 60 years and older will become the largest demographic group in the country [4, 5]. Ecuador’s exhibits notable demographic diversity, with 71.9% identifying as mestizo, and approximately 7% identifying as motubia, Afro-Ecuadorian, or indigenous [6]. Loja province has the highest concentration of older adults relative to its own population size, at 11.9% (57,799) Out of the total number of older adults in the country, 2,738 are aged 100 years or older. The province of Loja is one of those with the highest concentration of centenarians relative to its own older adult population [7]. This rich ethnic composition underscores the importance of gaining of the factors associated the aging process among older Ecuadorians.

An emerging body of literature reports important differences between individuals aging in rural and urban settings [8]. Indeed, the literature discusses the imperative need to recognize and address rural aging populations as a distinct demographic group, given the distinctive cultural, socioeconomic, and health-related characteristics that are intrinsic to these communities [9]. In Ecuador, around 37% of the population lives in rural areas, where poverty surpassed 40% in 2022 [10]. Other social determinants of health known to be associated with poor health outcomes are also highly prevalent in the country and are most critical in rural areas. For example, access to health care is limited in rural regions, with research showing that in these areas, financial, structural, and cognitive health are major care access barriers [11].

Knowledge about aging in rural Ecuador is extremely limited. Although Ecuador participated in 2010 in a multinational effort to collect data on older adult’s health and well-being in various Latin American and Caribbean countries (SABE), this data is outdated and not focused on the country’s rural population. A more recent study collected data on older adults living in a coastal area but focused on cardiovascular risk factors and their impact on the burden of stroke and ischemic heart disease. Although informative, this study does not provide insights about older adults living inland in rural areas where life conditions are likely to be harsher [12].

To fill in the gap in knowledge, we present the EcuAging study, a prospective study of older adults living in the communities of Calvas and Gonzanamá Counties of Province of Loja, Ecuador, located in the southern highlands, near the border with Peru (Fig. 1). The aim of the EcuAging study is to characterize the population of older adults living in these two rural communities in the highlands of Ecuador.Fig. 1Map of of Calvas and Gonzanamá counties showing the sampling locations. Methods

Study design and participants

The EcuAging study, that aims to characterize the population of adults aged 55 years and older living in a rural region of Ecuador, follows a cross sectional design and is descriptive in nature (Fig. 2). We collected detailed data on various dimensions of participants’ including sociodemographic characteristics, physical function, mental health, quality of life, activities of daily living and blood markers. Data was collected over a two-week period in June-July 2023. Trained researchers, medical staff and qualified psychologists interviewed the participants and took the blood samples.Fig. 2STROBE flow chart. Individuals living in rural communities in Calvas and Gonzanamá Counties in the Province of Loja (Table 1) were informed through campaigns led by project personnel, Ministry of Public Health of Ecuador (MOH) staff, and community leaders about the opportunity to attend a health fair and participate in the study. A set of mobile stations were established at rotating locations for interested individuals to attend. At these stations, individuals were informed about the research and eligible individuals were invited to participate in it. Individuals who agreed were invited to sign informed consent. The subjects were eligible for the study if they were 55 years and older, able to sign a consent form, living in the Calvas and Gonzanamá cantons, local communities of Chaquizhca, Guara, and Bellamaría, and surrounding areas, Loja Province, Ecuador. We excluded individuals with physical and mental disabilities, individuals with visual and auditory acuity not adequate for neuropsychological tests, individuals with significant mobility limitations to attend a clinical center. Anthropometric measures and health

Trained personnel measured participants height (cm) without shoes, weight (kilograms), and their waist and hip ratio. Three blood pressure measurements were taken at 1-minute intervals between measurements using a calibrated and mobile sphygmomanometer with individuals in a seated and relaxed position after a minimum of a 15-minute prior rest. See Table 5. Heart rate measurements were conducted using an oximeter equipped with an LCD digital display. Prior to the readings, meticulous care was taken to ensure the cleanliness of the measurement sites, with fingers undergoing thorough cleaning using 70% isopropyl alcohol. The placement of probes was methodically executed, with the finger probe securely placed onto the right index finger. To further ascertain the reliability of the data, subjects were instructed to move their fingers before the commencement of the tests, thereby testing the robustness of the probe placements and mitigating potential motion artifacts. Blood samples

Blood sample extraction was conducted by trained personnel of a contracted local laboratory (certificate of operation No. ACESS-2022-Z07-0163037), who extracted the samples according to a pre-established protocol. Biometric analyses included creatinine, a complete lipid panel (total, HDL, and LDL cholesterol; Triglycerides), glycosylated hemoglobin (HbA1c), vitamin B12, C-reactive protein (CRP), and Interleukin 6 (In-6). For each participant, a 4 ml tube remained in storage in a freezer at a −80 C for future use.

Physical function

Trained physiotherapists asked participants to complete a series of tasks. These included the Short Physical Performance Battery (SPPB) [14], which measures the degree of physical performance that a person possesses to perform a series of daily life activities. The protocol consists of three timed components: a 4-meter round-trip walk at a normal pace, rising from a chair five times without using the arms, and a progressive standing balance test. The study personnel demonstrated each of the tasks before assessing the participants. The walking test was repeated twice, and the best attempt recorded. Times for each component were scored from 0 to 4, with higher scores indicating better performance. The final total SPPB score was derived adding the scores from each of the 4 components and ranges from 0 to 12, where 0 to 6 represent low performance, 7 to 9 indicate intermediate performance, and 10 to 12 reflect the best performance, based on previously established clinical cutoff points [14]. Participants grip strength, a proxy for upper body strength, was measured using a CAMRY Digital Hand Dynamometer. The maximal voluntary grip force contraction test was conducted in a seated position. The test position for the grip strength of each subject was established with the shoulder adducted and in neutral rotation, the elbow joint flexed at 90°, the forearm in a neutral position, and the wrist positioned between 0° and 30° of extension, following the recommendations of the American Society of Hand Therapists [15]. Participant’s were asked to hold the dynamometer vertically with one hand and instructed to close their hand as tightly as possible. The measurement was repeated three times, and then the other hand is assessed. See Table 5.

Mental health

Trained psychologists conducted mental health assessments of study participants asking them to complete the Generalized Anxiety Scale (GAD-7) [16], which was used to evaluate the anxiety or worries that the participant has in their life at present. GAD-7 scores were derived following recommendations, and the following categories were created: scores 0-4 indicate minimum anxiety, scores 5-9 indicate mild anxiety, 10-14 indicate moderate anxiety, and scores higher than 15 indicate severe anxiety. The Geriatric Depression Scale (GDS-15) [17] was used to determine the participants mood and whether the participant has depressive symptoms. GDS-15 scores were categorized as normal (0-4), mild (5-8), moderate (9-11), and severe (12-15). See Table 6.

Quality of life and functional status

Trained psychologists also assessed participants quality of life in relation to daily life asking participants to complete the Quality-of-Life Scale (WHOQOL-BREF) [18], which covers various domains including physical health, psychological well-being, social relationships, and environmental factors. This scale takes values between 0 and 100, with higher values indicating better quality of life. The Katz Index of Independence in Activities of Daily Living was used to assess individuals ability to perform basic activities of daily living independently [19].This scale takes integer values between 0 and 6, with higher values indicating more independence. See Table 6.

Analyses plan

Sample size calculations

The sample size was determined based on the feasibility of conducting a wide range of assessments with each participant, considering costs and logistics. Logistical restrictions considered included availability of trained staff, access to local facilities and funding available. In this study, we used snowball sampling, a non-probabilistic sampling technique, which was deemed to be the most appropriate approach considering the challenges of accessing and recruiting participants in the targeted rural community of older individuals. This method involved initially identifying a small number of participants who were easily accessible or willing to participate. These participants then facilitated the recruitment of additional participants from their own social networks or community connections, forming a ‘snowball effect’ in participant recruitment. This approach is particularly suitable for accessing hard-to-reach populations, as discussed in the study by Faugier and Sargeant (1997) [20]. who highlighted the methodological challenges inherent in researching hidden populations, as the targeted population of our study. For our study population, we assumed a prevalence of mental health impairment to be 2%. With a margin of error of 0.05, we calculated that a sample size of 246 individuals is needed. We chose to base our sample size calculation on mental health impairment, (which we considered to be highly relevant for our study aims and population) considering a relatively high prevalence to plan for a realistic, yet conservative, scenario.

Statistical analyses

Continuous variables are described in terms of mean and standard deviations (SD) and categorical variables using counts and percentages. We evaluated differences in means using t-tests and chi-squared tests to compare proportions. We report results from individuals with complete data. All statistical tests were two-tailed, and a significance level of 0.05 (alpha level) was considered to determine statistical significance.

Ethics

The study protocol was approved by the Committee of Human research ethics at Pontifical Catholic University of Ecuador (Protocol Number CEISH-494-2023). The informed consent process was conducted in Spanish by trained personnel. All individuals who attended the health evaluation fair were provided with a written copy of the Informed Consent form which was also read to them. After being given the opportunity to ask questions, participants were invited to sign the informed consent form. A copy of the form was provided to them for their records.

Data sharing

In order to augment reporting transparency, this study will adhere to the guidelines outlined in the Strengthening the Reporting of Observational Studies in Epidemiology Statement (STROBE): Guidelines for Reporting Observational Studies. The data and resources generated are accessible to other eligible investigators through established academic channels. Interested parties may obtain the datasets employed in this study by submitting a reasonable request to the senior author.

Results

The total number of individuals who agreed to participate in the study was 270. However, there was one individual for whom only basic sociodemographic data was recorded, and therefore, a final sample size of 269 individuals with complete data was obtained. The age distribution showed that most participants (82.5%) were aged 60 years and above, with only 17.5% falling within the 55–59 years subgroup. The overall mean age was 70.1 years (SD = 9.9). Discussion

In this paper, we present the characteristics of participants in the EcuAging study, which included adults aged 55 years and older. Although the inclusion criteria started at age 55, the majority of the cohort consisted of individuals aged 60 years and above. Consequently, our findings primarily reflect the characteristics and health status of an older adult population residing in the rural highlands of Ecuador. To the best of our knowledge, this is the first study to examine this population in this region.

Male and female participants differed in work status and family income. More female participants were unemployed or work informally than men, which partially conforms with the country’s labor force participation [22]. Interestingly, no sex differences were found in anxiety and depression, which is in contrast with previous literature that has found the females are disadvantaged in terms of mental health compared to men. Only one other study has studied a sample of older adults in Ecuador, and in their analysis of a sample of individuals living in coastal areas, they found that depression and anxiety were more common in women than in men [23]. However, the results were based on small numbers (33 individuals in total with depression and 41 with anxiety), hence further research would be needed to fully understand reasons for these discrepant findings. In terms of physical health, women were found to have higher systolic blood pressure than men and higher BMI. It is possible that the differences in systolic blood pressure are explained by women being in the menopausal stage, as the literature suggests that before the menopause, women tend to have lower systolic blood pressure than men, but this reverses during and after the menopause.

Most participants were mestizos, had completed primary education and had a level of income that is below the average monthly income for the country. They also had a healthy lifestyle with no or little alcohol, tobacco, and coffee consumption. The contrast in results between physical health and mental health is noteworthy. Whereas results from physical function tests and ADL scores indicate that individuals had good physical function and could perform activities of daily living independently, mental health of the study participants was impaired. Indeed, the proportion of individuals with poor mental health was very high (about 75%). However, the use of these cut-off points in Latin- American populations has been questioned and given the scarce evidence generated from rural low educated native populations, these categorizations may not be adequate [24]. It is possible, however, that differences in physical health and mental health outcomes a reflect a bias in the sample since participants had to travel on their own to attend the assessments, which require certain levels of physical function as most traveled by foot. The high prevalence of anxiety and depression may also be explained by having limited social and the limited access to mental health clinics in the area. In another remote area of Ecuador [25], social support after experiencing an earthquake was reported to be moderate, so it is possible that in Loja, social support is even more limited.

The analysis revealed distinct sociodemographic and lifestyle profiles between Calvas and Gonzanamá cantons in Ecuador, with Gonzanamá demonstrating lower education levels, higher proportions of indigenous and white individuals, and a greater prevalence of lower family income and informal work. However, no significant differences were found in lifestyle behaviors such as alcohol and coffee consumption, meal frequency, physical activity, or sleep duration. While both cantons exhibited similar mental health and quality of life scores, disparities emerged in physical function, with Calvas showing better performance in aspects such as functional status compared to Gonzanamá. These findings underscore the importance of targeted interventions to address socioeconomic disparities and promote overall health equity across different regions.

This dataset offers unique opportunities to better understand the population of older adults living in a remote area of Ecuador. Although Ecuador is classified as upper middle-income country, poverty in rural areas is highly prevalent and various social determinants of health affect the rural population of older Ecuadorians. For example, rural areas are severely medically undeserved, with more than 86% of public practice health care providers and more than 96% of private practice providers working in urban areas and hence, may not serve and cover the needs of rural populations as the one of the EcuAging study [26, 27]. In addition, the country’s mental health care system has been reported to have limited reach beyond urban settings and hence, may not serve and cover the needs of rural populations as the one of the EcuAging study. Additional barriers to access to health care, including financial, structural, and cognitive barriers [11] have also been reported in the country, which are also likely to impact the mental health of the population.

Collecting data in rural and remote locations like Loja and Cariamanga is not without challenges. For instance, participants were requested to fast to ensure the reliability of blood analyses. However, this proved inconvenient for study participants due to the lengthy distances they had to travel for medical evaluations, resulting in several hours of fasting. Consequently, there was a trade-off between our ability to complete data collection and the exhaustion/tiredness experienced by individuals. In addition, as many of the inhabitants of the region are farmers, participation in the study also resulted in them not attending their daily tasks, which added another challenge for participation. Considering these logistical and practical challenges, the two-week timeframe in June-July was chosen as it allowed for a balance between data collection feasibility and participant burden. The chosen timeframe aligns with seasonal factors and community dynamics, maximizing the potential for representative data collection while minimizing participant inconvenience.

Exploring the potential of leveraging technology to expedite the data collection process is a promising avenue. Nevertheless, it comes with its own set of challenges. Many individuals may lack familiarity with advanced devices, and some locations might face connectivity issues. Furthermore, the collection of biological samples presents additional hurdles, particularly due to the considerable distances between locations and the processing facilities in laboratories.

Limitations in our study encompass the utilization of a restricted range of free cognitive batteries for assessing cognitive function, as well as the narrow scope of domains covered. Additionally, our sample is not fully representative of the broader population, particularly due to the focus on rural samples, and the relatively small, cross-sectional nature of the sample further limits generalizability. While we acknowledge the limitations inherent in a short data collection period, we believe that our approach allows for meaningful insights into the health status and wellbeing of the older population living in these rural communities. Despite these constraints, we strive to navigate the logistical challenges inherent in collecting data within the EcuAging study population, ensuring that participants were not overwhelmed to minimize errors and enhance the reliability of the gathered data to the best of our ability. Future data collections in this population are already in advanced planning stages and will permit the analysis of change in key indicators of interest.

Conclusion

The EcuAging study is the first to characterize adults aged 55 years and older living in rural inland Ecuador, with more than 80% of participants aged 60 years or above, thus reflecting predominantly the health and well-being of older adults. The cohort, marked by limited education and below-average incomes, showed a paradoxical profile: while most participants maintained independence in daily activities and preserved physical function, nearly three-quarters reported symptoms of anxiety or depression. Socioeconomic inequalities were evident, as women achieved higher levels of education but faced lower income and employment rates, and participants from Gonzanamá displayed poorer socioeconomic indicators than those from Calvas. Despite these disparities, no significant differences emerged in mental health or quality of life by sex or canton. These findings highlight the dual reality of aging in rural Ecuador—adequate physical functioning coupled with compromised mental health—and emphasize the urgent need to expand access to health services, particularly mental health care, in underserved areas. Strengthening the provision of health care in rural communities is essential to address these disparities and to ensure healthy aging among older Ecuadorians.
