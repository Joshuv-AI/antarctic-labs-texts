C2–C3 anterior cervical discectomy: Anatomical considerations and outcomes
Authors: Zvipo Mutsa Chisango, Siddharth Jasti, Firas Batrash, Jacob Glueck, Nicolas L. Carayannopoulos, Puru Sadh, Jinseong Kim, Scott Mallozzi, Bassel G. Diebo, Bryce A. Basques, Alan H. Daniels
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTIntroduction:Anterior cervical discectomy and fusion (ACDF) at C2–C3 is technically demanding due to complex neurovascular anatomy and limited surgical access. Comprehensive anatomical guidance for this approach remains limited. We provide a detailed anatomical review of the anterior approach to C2–C3 and present institutional clinical outcomes for this procedure across a range of indications.Materials and Methods:A comprehensive literature review was synthesized with surgical experience to describe relevant anatomy and technical considerations for C2–C3 ACDF. In this retrospective series, patient demographics, operative details, complications, and outcomes were analyzed for patients undergoing ACDF, including C2–C3 at our institution.Results:Eight patients (75% female, mean age: 60.6) underwent ACDF, including C2–C3 between 2022 and 2025. Indications included proximal junctional failure, trauma, infection, rheumatoid arthritis, and degenerative myelopathy. Fusions ranged from single-level C2–C3 to multilevel C2-–C7 constructs. Mean operative time was 78.9 min with mean blood loss of 21.9 cc. Complications occurred in 37.5% of patients, including persistent dysphagia (n = 2) and failed extubation (n = 1). No direct neurovascular injuries occurred. Mean length of stay was 16.4 days.Conclusions:ACDF at C2–C3 requires mastery of anatomy, including positioning, sequential dissection, and identification of neurovascular structures. Careful attention to anatomical principles allows this procedure to be performed safely without producing specific neurovascular injury, although dysphagia and airway-related morbidity remain common complications. This work provides practical strategies for navigating this challenging level.

INTRODUCTION

Anterior cervical discectomy and fusion (ACDF) is a commonly utilized technique to address neural compression, deformity, and biomechanical instability in the cervical spine. The procedure involves an anterior incision, removal of the disc and osteophytes, followed by intervertebral bone graft or cage placement with or without anterior plate fixation.[1] Indications for ACDF include cervical radiculopathy, myelopathy, deformity, trauma, tumor, infection, and instability.[2] The procedure is most commonly performed for degenerative pathology in the lower cervical spine, particularly at C5–C6 and C6–C7.[3]

ACDF can also be performed at the C2–C3 level despite a more challenging surgical approach due to the narrow surgical corridor and proximity to complex critical neurovasculature. The anterior approach at this level requires careful protection of the hypoglossal nerve, branches of the superior laryngeal nerve (SLN), the superior thyroid and laryngeal vessels, as well as navigation around the mandible, submandibular gland, and hyoid bone.[4] Given these anatomical challenges, ACDF at the C2–C3 level has been reported to have higher rates of perioperative morbidity than ACDF at lower levels of the spine.[5]

Despite these challenges, the anterior approach is often favored over its lateral counterparts due to its similarity to the well-established Smith–Robinson technique used at more caudal levels of the cervical spine.[6] Although complication rates are higher at this cephalad level, ACDF at C2–C3 has demonstrated improvements in pain and neurological symptoms with acceptable rates of solid fusion.[7]

Comprehensive anatomical guidance for the anterior approach to C2–C3 remains limited, with existing reports focusing on a single indication or on operative technique alone. In this investigation, we provide a comprehensive anatomical review of the anterior approach to C2–C3 with an emphasis on surgical technique, critical neurovascular structures, strategies to minimize perioperative complications, and present our institutional experience with ACDF at the C2–C3 level in eight patients with differing surgical indications.

MATERIALS AND METHODS

Patient selection and data collection

A comprehensive review of the literature was synthesized with the authors’ surgical experience to describe the current understanding of relevant surgical anatomy and technical considerations for the anterior approach to C2–C3. The anatomical considerations for ACDF at these levels are described in detail below, followed by the clinical outcomes from our institutional case series.

A retrospective review of patients who underwent ACDF, including the C2–C3 level for various indications at our institution, was performed. Eight cases were selected purposively to demonstrate the range of pathologies and clinical presentations that may require anterior fusion at this level. The complication, length-of-stay, and outcome figures reported below are therefore descriptive of this selected series.

Data extracted from patient medical charts included patient age, sex, and presenting symptoms, indication for ACDF at C2–C3, preoperative imaging findings, levels fused, perioperative complications, length of stay, and final disposition.

Anatomical considerations

Successful execution of ACDF at C2–C3 requires a thorough understanding of the unique anatomy at this level. The following sections provide a detailed review of patient positioning, fascial dissection, identification of critical neurovascular structures, and strategies to avoid complications. Patient positioning and approach

A primary obstacle to anterior approaches to the cephalad cervical spine is the mandible, which obstructs the surgical field. Nasotracheal intubation can be considered for these procedures, as orotracheal intubation requires the mouth to remain open, displacing the mandible caudally into the operative field, although none of the patients in our case series required nasotracheal intubation.[8] Following intubation, the patient is positioned supine with the head rotated approximately 30° away from the operative side. The neck is placed in slight extension to maximize the distance between the mandible and the C2–C3 level.[59] In addition, a roll should be placed under the shoulder to facilitate cervical extension and cephalad displacement of the mandible.[10]

The incision is made over the C3 vertebral body, which can be approximated by palpating the hyoid bone in some patients. Once the C3 vertebral body is marked, fluoroscopic imaging with a C-arm may be used to verify positioning over the correct vertebral segment. Incision placement over the C3 vertebral body provides an appropriate trajectory to the C2–C3 disc space. The incision should be made more than 2 cm from the inferior margin of the mandible to prevent damage to the marginal mandibular branch (MMB) of the facial nerve. This safety margin is supported by a large cadaveric study demonstrating that the MMB is consistently located within 2 cm of the inferior border of the mandible.[11] The incision should span 5–6 cm across the midline to the anterior border of the sternocleidomastoid (SCM).[10]

Superficial cervical fascia

The superficial cervical fascia contains the platysma muscle, which extends from the chest to the lower face. Following the initial incision, the platysma is identified and divided transversely. Blunt dissection in the subplatysmal plane is performed in both the cephalad and caudal direction to achieve adequate exposure without excessive retraction.

During subplatysmal dissection, the inferior border of the submandibular gland and the associated facial vessels are encountered crossing the surgical field.[12] The MMB of the facial nerve originates from within the parotid gland and courses medially toward the mandibular angle, traveling deep to the platysma muscle.[13] The nerve then curves around the inferior margin of the mandible before traveling superiorly to innervate the circumoral muscles.[11] Injury to this nerve causes flattening of the lower lip, which is particularly evident when smiling and can significantly affect patients’ perception of their appearance.[14] Several studies have confirmed the consistent positioning of the MMB superficial to the facial artery; as such, palpating the facial artery can aid identification of the MMB.[1516] The facial vessels are typically retracted superolaterally toward the mandible to maintain the surgical corridor, though they may be ligated if needed.[10]

Deep cervical fascia-investing layer

After incising the platysma, the surgeon encounters structures enclosed in the deep cervical fascia. The investing layer is the most superficial of the deep cervical fascia, and it splits to surround the SCM laterally.[17] The SCM is an important anatomical landmark, serving to orient the surgeon within the surgical field.[18]

Deep cervical fascia-pretracheal layer

Medial to the SCM, the pretracheal fascia consists of a muscular layer surrounding the infrahyoid muscles and a visceral layer which surrounds the thyroid gland, trachea, and esophagus.[17] Among the infrahyoid muscles, the omohyoid and sternohyoid are more superficial than the sternothyroid and thyrohyoid.[18]

Traditional approaches to C2–C3 can result in limited laryngeal mobility due to the attachments of the infrahyoid muscles to the hyoid bone, particularly when the neck is extended. In a recent prospective study, Okamoto et al. described a modified technique involving temporary detachment of the omohyoid and sternohyoid muscles from their hyoid attachments with caudal reflection. This modification was found to improve laryngeal mobility, facilitate identification of the internal branch of the SLN (iSLN), and was associated with reduced rates of postoperative dysphagia.[10]

Following mobilization of the infrahyoid muscles, the fascial plane between the carotid sheath laterally and the laryngopharyngeal structures medially is carefully developed through blunt dissection. This step allows lateral retraction of the SCM and carotid sheath while the viscera are retracted medially, providing access to the prevertebral layer and anterior cervical spine. Adequate development of this plane is crucial to minimize stretch injury to neurovascular structures during retractor placement.

Deep cervical fascia-prevertebral layer

The prevertebral fascia, the deepest layer of the deep cervical fascia, surrounds the vertebral column and prevertebral muscles, including the longus colli and longus capitis.[9] It is incised in the midline to expose the underlying longus colli muscles that run longitudinally along the anterolateral aspects of the cervical vertebral bodies. As such, the longus colli muscles can be used as a landmark for defining the midline of the cervical spine. Once the longus colli muscles have been undermined in a subperiosteal fashion, bilateral self-retaining retractors are placed beneath the muscles to expose the anterior spine. Care is required at this step as the vertebral arteries and cervical sympathetic chain lie posterolateral to the longus colli muscles and can be injured with aggressive lateral dissection.[19] Once exposure is adequate, the anterior longitudinal ligament is incised sharply, allowing for subsequent discectomy and instrumentation.

Neurovascular structures

Carotid sheath

The carotid sheath is a fascial component derived from contributions of all three layers of the deep cervical fascia.[20] It contains the common carotid artery, which bifurcates into the internal carotid artery and external carotid artery (ECA) at approximately the C3–C4 level, the internal jugular vein, and the vagus nerve. During the high anterior cervical approach, the carotid sheath is mobilized carefully with blunt dissection and retracted laterally.

Hypoglossal nerve (HN)

The hypoglossal nerve (CN XII) descends from the skull base in close relationship to the carotid sheath and turns sharply toward the midline at approximately the C2–C3 level to course deep and parallel to the digastric muscle. The nerve is most commonly located at the C2 vertebral level, though there is some anatomical variation.[4] If additional exposure is required, division of the digastric muscle at the tendinous attachment facilitates retraction of the hypoglossal nerve superiorly.[521] Damage to the hypoglossal nerve may present with ipsilateral tongue deviation, dysarthria, and dysphagia, which increase the risk for aspiration pneumonia.[2223]

Laryngeal nerves and vessels

The SLN branches from the vagus nerve within the carotid sheath before bifurcating into internal and external branches (iSLN and eSLN). The iSLN is most commonly found at or within 1 cm of the C3–C4 level and is reliably thicker than the eSLN.[4] The iSLN pierces the thyrohyoid membrane to provide sensory innervation to the supraglottic larynx.[24] Injury to the iSLN may lead to the loss of laryngeal sensation and impairment of protective reflexes, predisposing patients to dysphagia and aspiration.[25]

The superior laryngeal artery branches from the superior thyroid artery (which arises from the ECA) and travels in close proximity to the superior laryngeal vein and iSLN toward the thyrohyoid membrane. These vessels are routinely divided to identify the iSLN.[510] Division of the superior laryngeal vessels is generally well tolerated due to extensive collateral circulation.[1026]

The recurrent laryngeal nerve (RLN) courses in the tracheoesophageal groove and supplies motor innervation to all intrinsic laryngeal muscles except the cricothyroid.[2728] During high anterior cervical approaches, proper retractor placement away from the tracheoesophageal groove is critical to avoid injury to the RLN. Injury to this structure results in ipsilateral vocal cord paralysis, which may manifest as hoarseness and potential airway compromise if bilateral injury occurs.[29] It is recommended to monitor and release endotracheal cuff pressure after retractor placement to reduce the risk of RLN injury during this approach.[30]

These anatomical relationships and surgical modifications guided our approach in the case series presented below.

RESULTS

Patient demographics and surgical indications

Eight patients underwent ACDF of C2–C3 at our institution between 2022 and 2025. 75.0% of patients were female, with a mean age of 60.6 years (range: 36–73 years). Indications for surgery included proximal junctional failure and infection following prior instrumented fusion (n = 1) [Figure 2], traumatic hangman’s fracture [n = 2; Figure 3], methicillin-resistant Staphylococcus aureus bacteremia with epidural abscess and rapidly evolving spinal cord injury (n = 1) [Figure 4], rheumatoid arthritis [n = 1; Figure 5], failure of previously placed spine hardware after a fall (n = 1), and chronic cervical myelopathy [n = 2; Figure 6].

Surgical details

Fusion levels varied according to pathology. One patient underwent single-level fusion at C2–C3, five patients underwent two-level fusion at C2–C4, one patient underwent ACDF at C3–C5 with anterior plating at C2–C7, and one patient underwent ACDF at C2–C7. Mean operative time was 78.9 min (range: 44–99 min) and mean estimated blood loss was 21.9 cc (range: 10–50 cc).

Complications and outcomes

Postoperative complications occurred in three of eight patients (37.5%). One patient (C3–C5 ACDF with anterior plating to C2–C7) had a persistently abnormal barium swallow requiring a percutaneous endoscopic gastrostomy tube, with worsened weakness on the discharge examination. A second patient (C2–C4 ACDF with C3 corpectomy) also had a persistently abnormal barium swallow but refused intervention. A third, treated for a hangman’s fracture, could not be extubated postoperatively and required tracheostomy; the neurological examination remained limited by multiple concurrent fractures. The patient treated with irrigation and debridement (I and D) for an epidural abscess had persistent epidural infection requiring subsequent posterior decompression and fusion, reflecting progression of the index infection rather than a procedural complication. The discharge examination was limited by altered mental status. The remaining four patients had no perioperative complications, two with a stable neurological examination at discharge and two with improved motor function.

Mean length of stay was 16.4 days (range: 1–33 days). One patient was discharged to hospice, two to a skilled nursing facility, two to acute rehab facilities, and three were discharged home.

DISCUSSION

ACDF is a commonly performed procedure for cervical spine pathology but is rarely performed at the C2-C3 level, where degenerative pathology is less frequent, and the anterior approach is more technically demanding.[31] In this series, eight patients underwent ACDF, including the C2–C3 level. The approach was completed without direct neurovascular injury, although dysphagia and airway morbidity were the most common complications.

Reports of the anterior approach to C2–C3 remain limited and have largely centered on a single indication or on operative technique alone.[32] Across these reports, dysphagia consistently emerges as the most frequent approach-related morbidity, and complication rates exceed those of lower cervical ACDF.[33] Our results were consistent with this pattern, with dysphagia being the most common complication in our cohort (25%). Operative time and blood loss were low (mean: 78.9 min and 21.9 cc) and comparable to lower cervical ACDF.[32] Despite the complication rate being elevated relative to the broader literature (37.5%), this likely reflects the severity of underlying pathology in patients requiring ACDF at C2–C3 rather than the surgical approach itself.[34] This cohort presented with a broader range of indications than prior reports, making the rationale for ACDF at C2–C3 particularly relevant at this level. Despite the breadth of surgical indications represented in this series, the surgical technique and thorough understanding of the anatomical principles described above facilitated the preservation of critical neurovascular structures in all cases.

Anterior surgery is preferred for ventral compression and when anterior column support or kyphosis correction is required.[35] Conversely, a posterior approach is often preferred for dorsal or multilevel compression, and when anterior access is limited.[36] In trauma, the approach depends on stability. Stable axis fractures are often managed with external immobilization, whereas unstable fractures usually require fixation. For the latter, anterior C2–C3 fusion is an accepted, minimally invasive option, particularly when a disrupted C2–C3 disc compresses the cord, or the C2 pedicles cannot accept screws.[37] For degenerative disease, approach selection follows the site of compression. Dorsal compression is treated posteriorly, and lower cervical degeneration can often be addressed without extending the fusion to C2–C3. These principles are reflected in the range of indications represented in this series.[36]

Limitations

This study has several limitations. It is a retrospective, single-institution series of only eight patients, selected to illustrate a range of pathologies rather than a consecutive sample. The reported complication and length-of-stay figures therefore describe this specific cohort and should not be read as generalizable rates for C2–C3 ACDF. Additional larger, consecutive, and prospectively followed cohorts are warranted to better define the morbidity and indications for anterior fusion at this level. The neurovascular anatomy described represents typical relationships and varies between individuals; therefore, the accompanying illustration is intended to serve as a schematic guide rather than a patient-specific map.

CONCLUSIONS

ACDF of the spine at C2–C3 is a technically demanding procedure requiring mastery of the anatomy of the cephalad cervical spine. While associated with higher complication rates than ACDF at more caudal levels of the spine, careful attention to patient positioning, systematic fascial dissection, and identification of critical neurovascular structures allows the approach to be performed without direct neurovascular injury, even in medically complex patients.
