Network-based single-cell analysis identifies a proliferative GZMB+ plasma cell subset linked to Hedgehog signaling and prognostic risk in lung squamous cell carcinoma
Authors: Wen Ma, Junjie Kuang, Xiaoye Hu, Shaohui Huang, Jie Zhou, Zhiwei Liao, Yan Yuan
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundLung squamous cell carcinoma (LUSC) is a heterogeneous cancer characterized by complex cellular interactions. Plasma cells, a critical immune population, exhibit functional diversity in LUSC. The aim of this study was to characterize plasma cell heterogeneity and their interactions with tumor cells using an integrative single-cell framework.MethodsWe constructed a high-resolution single-cell atlas of LUSC using single-cell RNA-sequencing data to identify plasma cell subsets. Plasma cell heterogeneity was assessed through clustering and differential expression analysis, and differentiation trajectories were plotted. Cell-cell communication was analyzed using the “CellChat” package. A prognostic nine-gene signature was derived using LASSO-Cox regression and further validated across independent GEO cohorts. Upstream regulatory mechanisms of prognostic genes were investigated through integrative transcriptomic analysis. The role of ZBTB20 in tumor progression was validated through functional assays.ResultsThree plasma cell subsets were identified, including a proliferative GZMB+ (C2) subset characterized by higher G2M/S activity and stemness-associated features. Trajectory analysis revealed a differentiation axis from IGLC2+ to IGKC+ to GZMB+ plasma cells. Network-based communication analysis demonstrated that C2 plasma cells interact with LUSC tumor cells via the SHH-PTCH1 signaling axis. A nine-gene prognostic signature robustly stratified patients, and was associated with immune signaling, cytokine interactions, apoptosis regulation, and tumor microenvironment remodeling. Integrative regulatory analysis identified UBB as a central prognostic gene, with ZBTB20 and PPARG as potential upstream transcriptional regulators. The prognostic significance of the C2-related gene signature was validated in independent GEO cohorts. ZBTB20 was further identified as a key regulator of tumor-related signaling and cellular proliferation, and its oncogenic role was verified through functional experiments.ConclusionsThe proliferative C2 plasma cell subset has prognostic and functional significance in LUSC. ZBTB20 regulates key tumor-related pathways in LUSC cells through UBB-centered transcriptional control. These findings provide a systems-level framework for understanding plasma cell dynamics and tumor-immune interactions in LUSC progression for future development of targeted therapies.Keywords: Lung squamous cell carcinoma, Tumor stem cells, Hedgehog signaling pathway, KCTD1, ZBTB20

Introduction

Lung squamous cell carcinoma (LUSC) is one of the most common subtypes of non-small cell lung cancer (NSCLC), and accounts for approximately 30% of all lung cancer cases [1]. According to the latest global cancer statistics report released by the International Agency for Research on Cancer, lung cancer remains the most prevalent malignancy worldwide and the leading cause of cancer-related deaths [2]. In 2018, approximately 2.1 million new cases of lung cancer were diagnosed worldwide, and the death toll reached 1.8 million [3, 4]. The disease burden of lung cancer is particularly heavy in China, where it has ranked first among malignant tumors in terms of incidence and mortality for many consecutive years, with approximately 730,000 new cases and over 600,000 deaths reported each year [5–7]. LUSC is a typical representative of smoking-related lung cancer, and its incidence rate is significantly correlated with the smoking index. i.e., years of smoking × number of cigarettes smoked per day [8, 9]. In addition, the incidence of LUSC presents significant gender differences and regional characteristics, with higher prevalence among male smokers and the Asian population [10]. LUSC is not only a serious public health issue, but also imposes significant social and economic burden. From a pathological perspective, LUSC originates from squamous epithelial cells of the bronchial mucosa and is typically located in the central region of the lungs, close to the main bronchi [1, 11]. The molecular characteristics of LUSC differ significantly from that of lung adenocarcinoma; for instance, the EGFR mutation rate is relatively low, whereas alterations in the PI3K pathway and SOX2 amplification are more common in LUSC [12–15]. Furthermore, LUSC can be classified into three histopathological subtypes, namely the keratinized type, non-keratinized type, and basal-like type [16], depending on the presence of intercellular bridges, keratinized bead formation and cancer nest structure [17]. The clinical manifestations of LUSC are often related to the central location of the tumor, and include irritating cough, hemoptysis, recurrent obstructive pneumonia, chest pain and breathing difficulties, etc. At present, the clinical treatment strategies for LUSC mainly rely on the tumor stage, physical condition of the patient, and molecular characteristics. For early-stage patients, surgical resection is the preferred and potentially curative approach [18, 19]. On the other hand, a comprehensive treatment approach based on surgery or a concurrent model of radiotherapy and chemotherapy is usually adopted for patients with locally advanced stages. In case of advanced or metastatic disease, the main treatment options are platinum-based dual-drug chemotherapy (such as gemcitabine + cisplatin) and immune checkpoint inhibitors (such as PD-1/PD-L1 monoclonal antibodies) [20, 21]. Despite advances in treatment methods, the clinical management of LUSC faces significant challenges, mainly differential treatment responses and the emergence of drug resistance due to high tumor heterogeneity [22]. Although immunotherapy may improve the long-term survival of some patients, its overall effective rate still needs to be improved, and there is a lack of stable and precise biomarkers for screening patients who may benefit from the treatment [23]. In addition, the scarcity of driver gene mutation targets in LUSC has impeded the development of targeted therapies [24]. However, the advent of high-throughput sequencing and functional genomics in recent years has helped identify multiple potential targetable signaling pathways in LUSC, thus offering hope for the development of new therapies. Therefore, in-depth exploration of the molecular pathogenesis of LUSC, especially the regulatory network of signaling pathways, is crucial for discovering new therapeutic targets and improving patient survival.

The Hedgehog (Hh) pathway is a conserved signaling pathway that plays a key role in embryonic development, stem cell maintenance, and tissue homeostasis [25, 26]. It is relatively downregulated in the adult tissues under physiological conditions, and activated in various malignant tumors [27, 28]. The Hh pathway consists of ligand SHH, receptor PTCH1, co-receptor SMO and the downstream GLI family of transcription factors (GLI1, GLI2, GLI3) [29, 30]. The binding of Hh ligands to PTCH1 activates SMO and triggers a cascade reaction, resulting in the nuclear translocation of GLI proteins, which then regulate the expression of target genes related to cell proliferation, survival, migration and stem cell characteristics [31, 32]. The Hh pathway plays a crucial role in maintaining the self-renewal capacity of cancer stem cells (CSCs) [33], which are the major drivers of tumorigenesis, metastasis, treatment resistance and recurrence [34, 35]. Studies show that the activation of the Hh pathway promotes the stemness of CSCs, inhibits their differentiation, and maintains them in a quiescent state to evade the damage caused by chemotherapy and radiotherapy [36, 37]. For instance, the continuous activation of the Hh pathway in basal cell carcinoma and medulloblastoma directly drives the expansion of tumor stem cells [38, 39].

In this study, we integrated single-cell transcriptomics, functional experiments and clinical data analysis to explore the functional heterogeneity of plasma cells in the LUSC microenvironment, and elucidate the molecular mechanism underlying their interaction with tumor cells. We have identified a highly proliferative C2 plasma cell subpopulation with stemness characteristics and a unique molecular phenotype, and constructed a prognostic model for LUSC based on the genetic characteristics of this subpopulation. Furthermore, we have established a key role of the ZBTB20–KCTD1-Hh signaling axis in the malignant progression of LUSC; ZBTB20 binds to and blocks transcription of the KCTD1 promoter, thereby relieving the inhibitory effect of KCTD1 on the Hh pathway, and activating downstream signals that enhance the proliferation, migration, invasion, stemness and in vivo tumorigenic ability of tumor cells. Our findings provide new molecular insights into the pathogenesis of LUSC, as well as potential intervention targets in the Hh signaling axis for the development of novel therapeutic strategies.

Material and methods

Data acquisition and processing

The RNA expression profiles and corresponding clinical data of LUSC (n = 493) were obtained from the National Cancer Institute (NCI) and the National Human Genome Research Institute (NHGRI). The bulk RNA-seq dataset GSE159857, comprising 17 LUSC tumor samples and 11 normal lung tissue samples, was retrieved from the National Center for Biotechnology Information (NCBI). In addition, the GSE157010 (n = 235 tumor samples), GSE30219 (n = 61 tumor samples), and GSE50081 (n = 43 tumor samples) datasets were obtained from the Gene Expression Omnibus (GEO) for the external validation of ZBTB20 expression and its prognostic relevance. Candidate survival-associated genes were further cross-referenced using the GEPIA database (http://gepia.cancer-pku.cn/index.html) to identify genes with significant survival stratification potential. Single-cell RNA sequencing (scRNA-seq) data of LUSC were obtained from the Lung Cancer Single-cell and Multi-omics Data Portal (https://lungcancer.chenlulab.com/#/), including 32 LUSC tumor samples and a total of 74,729 cells.

Data preprocessing and analysis were performed using the Seurat R package (v4.1.3). Quality control criteria were as follows: mitochondrial gene content < 10%, erythrocyte gene content < 3%, unique molecular identifier (UMI) counts 200–10,000 per cell, and gene counts 200–3000 per cell. Data normalization was performed using the NormalizeData function. Highly variable genes (n = 2000) were identified using the FindVariableFeatures function. Cell cycle effects were regressed out using the ScaleData function with parameters vars.to.regress = c(“S.Score”, “G2M.Score”). After batch effect correction using the Harmony algorithm, dimensionality reduction was performed using UMAP, followed by Louvain clustering for unsupervised cell clustering. The differentially expressed genes (DEGs) between clusters or cell types were screened using the FindAllMarkers function, with p-value < 0.05, log2 fold change > 0.25, and minimum expression proportion > 0.1 as the thresholds.

Cell annotation analysis

Cell clusters were annotated using the classic markers for epithelial cells (EPCAM, KRT18, KRT19, CDH1), fibroblasts (DCN, THY1, COL1A1, COL1A2), endothelial cells (PECAM1, CLDN5, FLT1, RAMP2), T cells (CD3D, CD3E, CD3G, TRAC), NK cells (NKG7, GNLY, NCAM1, KLRD1), B cells (CD79A, IGHM, IGHG3, IGHA2), plasma cells (JCHAIN), myeloid cells (LYZ, MARCO, CD68, FCGR3A), and mast cells (KIT, MS4A2, GATA2). In addition, the plasma cell subtypes were annotated using markers for ciliated cells (FOXJ1, DNAH5), alveolar type I epithelial cells (AGER), alveolar type II epithelial cells (SFTPC), and LUSC epithelial cells (SOX2, KRT5, KRT14, EPCAM). The subpopulation with the high expression of SPRR1B was considered the secretory subpopulation.

Establishment of risk model

Univariate Cox analysis was performed on the marker genes of C2 plasma cells using the “glmnet” R package to identify the genes significantly related to prognosis (p < 0.01). Eleven prognostic genes were further filtered using Lasso-Cox regression to obtain the model genes. To determine the predictive accuracy of the model, receiver operating characteristic (ROC) cures were plotted using the “timeROC” R package, and the area under the curve (AUC) values were calculated for 1-, 3-, and 5-year survival.

Cell-cell communication analysis

The “CellChat” R package was used to evaluate potential intercellular communication. The normalized gene expression matrix was imported to create the CellChat object, and the data was preprocessed using identifyOverExpressedGenes, identifyOverExpressedInteraction and ProjectData functions with default parameters. Potential ligand-receptor interactions were identified using the computeCommunProb, filterCommunication, and computeCommunProbPathway functions. Finally, the aggregateNet function was used to generate the cell communication network.

Supplementary experimental methods

Cell culture

The BEAS-2B, A549, HCC827, Calu-1, NCI-H2228 and NCI-H1975 cell lines were procured from the American Cell Bank (ATCC). The A549 and Calu-1 cells were cultured in DMEM (Hyclone, USA), the BEAS-2B cells were grown in bronchial epithelial cell growth medium (BEGM, Lonza, USA), and the HCC827, NCI-H2228 and NCI-H1975 cells were cultured in RPMI-1640 medium (Gibco, Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA). All culture media were supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS, Hyclone, USA) and 1% penicillin-streptomycin solution (Keygen, China). The cells were maintained in a standard incubator (37 °C, 5% CO₂, saturated humidity), and grown till the logarithmic phase prior to in vitro experiments.

Transfection

The NCI-H2228 and HCC827 cells were transfected with ZBTB20-specific siRNA (si- ZBTB20) and normal control siRNA (si-NC) sequences (Sangon Biotech, China). Briefly, the cells were cultured in 6-well plates till they reached 80% confluency, and the Lipo3000 transfection reagent and siRNAs were each diluted with Opti-MEM Reduced Serum Medium (Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA). The reagents were left undisturbed for 5 min, mixed, and incubated for 20 min. The transfection mixture was added to the cells, and medium was replaced 5 hours later with fresh culture medium.

Total RNA extraction and RT-qPCR

The cultured cells were harvested, pelleted, and lysed using 950 µL Trizol reagent (Takara, Japan) supplemented with RNase inhibitors for 5 min. The lysates were mixed with 150 µL chloroform (Sinopharm Chemical Reagent Co. LTD., China), and subjected to vigorous eddy current oscillation until a uniform milky white emulsion was formed. The samples were then centrifuged for 5 min, and the supernatant was aspirated and mix it with an equal volume of isopropyl alcohol (SINOPHARM, China)for RNA precipitation. After centrifuging for 5 min, the pelleted RNA was thoroughly washed with 1 mL of 75% ethanol or anhydrous ethanol, and then dried. RNA concentration and DNA/protein contamination were measured after extraction. Genomic DNA was eliminated using the PrimeScript RT kit (TaKaRa, Japan) according to the manufacturer’s protocol, and the purified RNA was quantified. Subsequently, cDNA was synthesized by the reverse transcription reaction, and real-time qPCR was performed using the SYBR GreenER Supermix (TaKaRa, Japan) on the Roche 480 PCR system (Roche, Switzerland). Three replicate wells were set up for each group, and β-actin was used as the internal reference.

Cell counting Kit-8 (CCK8) assay

Cells were harvested 24 h after transfection, and seeded into 96-well microplates at the density of 4000 cells per well. Triplicate wells were set up for each group. The CCK-8 reagent (KeyGEN Biotech, China) was diluted with the culture medium, and 100 μL of the reagent was added per well at pre-determined time points. The cells were incubated for 1.5 h at 37 °C in the dark, and the absorbance of the wells was measured at 450 nm using a microplate reader. Multiple repeated measurements were conducted at the designated time points, and the results were visualized.

Apoptosis staining

Apoptosis rates were analyzed by flow cytometry following Annexin V-FITC/PI double staining. NCI-H2228 cells transfected with si-NC or si-ZBTB20 were harvested, washed with PBS, and stained with Annexin V-FITC and PI for 15 minutes in the dark at room temperature. The stained cells were immediately analyzed in a flow cytometer (BD FACSCalibur), and the percentage of apoptotic cells (early: Annexin V+ /PI−; late: Annexin V+ /PI+) was calculated using FlowJo software. All experiments were performed in triplicate.

Transwell assay

Cell migration and invasion were assessed by the Transwell assay. NCI-H2228 cells transfected with si-NC or si-ZBTB20 were seeded into the upper chambers of transwell inserts in serum-free medium at the density of 1 × 105 cells per well. The lower chambers were filled with RPMI-1640 medium with 10% FBS as the chemoattractant. For the invasion assay, the membrane was pre-coated with Matrigel. After 24 h of incubation, non-migrated/invasive cells were removed, and migrated/invaded cells were fixed, stained with crystal violet, and counted under a microscope in five random fields per well.

Western blotting

Total protein fraction was isolated from the cultured cells using RIPA lysis buffer (containing protease and phosphatase inhibitors) on ice for 30 min. The lysates were centrifuged at 12,000 ×g for 15 min at 4 °C, and the protein content in the supernatants were determined by the BCA method (BCA Protein Assay Kit, ZJ101, Yamei Biotechnology). Equal amounts of protein (30–50 µg per lane) were separated by 10% SDS-polyacrylamide gel electrophoresis (SDS-PAGE) and transferred onto PVDF membranes (Millipore) using a semi-dry transfer system. After blocking with 5% non-fat milk in Tris-buffered saline with Tween-20 (TBST) for 1 h at room temperature, the membranes were incubated overnight at 4 °C with anti-c-caspase-3 (1:1000, #9661, Cell Signaling Technology), anti-Bcl-2 (1:2000, ab182858, Abcam), anti-E-cadherin (1:1000, ab1416, Abcam), anti-Vimentin (1:1000, ab92547, Abcam), and anti-β-actin (1:1000, ab179511, Abcam) antibodies. The membranes were washed with TBST, and then incubated with HRP-conjugated secondary antibody (1:2000, ab205718, Abcam) for 1 h at room temperature. The protein bands were visualized using enhanced chemiluminescence reagent (SQ201, Yamei Biotechnology) and imaged with a ChemiDoc XRS+ imaging system (Bio-Rad). Band intensity was quantified using ImageJ software (NIH, USA), and relative expression levels were normalized to β-actin. All experiments were performed in triplicate.

Bioinformatic prediction of transcription factor binding sites

Potential ZBTB20 binding motifs within the approximately 2.0 kb region upstream of the human KCTD1 gene transcription start site were predicted using the JASPAR (https://jaspar.genereg.net/) and PROMO (ALGGEN) algorithms. The search was conducted with a matrix similarity threshold of >0.85. The three sites with the highest prediction scores were selected for experimental validation.

Plasmid construction for luciferase reporter assays

DNA fragments encompassing different lengths of the KCTD1 promoter region were amplified from human genomic DNA template by PCR using specific primers (sequences available upon request). The PCR products were subsequently cloned into multiple cloning sites of the pGL4.10[luc2] basic vector (Promega, USA) upstream of the firefly luciferase gene to generate a series of promoter-reporter constructs: pGL4–KCTD1-full, pGL4–KCTD1-Δ488, pGL4–KCTD1-Δ300, and pGL4–KCTD1-Δ83. The full-length human ZBTB20 coding sequence was cloned into the pcDNA3.1(+) vector (Invitrogen, USA) to generate the ZBTB20 overexpression plasmid. All constructs were verified by Sanger sequencing.

Dual-luciferase reporter assay

Human embryonic kidney 293T cells were seeded in 24-well plates and co-transfected at 60–70% confluency using Lipofectamine 3000 (Invitrogen, USA). Each transfection mixture contained 450 ng of a KCTD1 promoter-firefly luciferase reporter plasmid, 50 ng of the Renilla luciferase control plasmid pRL-TK (Promega, USA), and 100 ng of either the pcDNA3.1-ZBTB20 plasmid or the empty pcDNA3.1 vector. Cells were harvested 48 h post-transfection, and luciferase activity was measured using the Dual-Luciferase Reporter Assay System (Promega, USA) according to the manufacturer’s protocol. Firefly luciferase activity was normalized to the Renilla luciferase activity for each well to control for transfection efficiency. Triplicate wells were set up for each experimental group, and the experiment was performed thrice.

Chromatin immunoprecipitation and quantitative PCR (ChIP-qPCR)

ChIP was performed using the SimpleChIP® Plus Enzymatic Chromatin IP Kit (Cell Signaling Technology, USA) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Briefly, approximately 4 × 106 NCI-H2228 cells per sample were cross-linked with 1% formaldehyde for 10 min at room temperature. Chromatin was digested with micrococcal nuclease to obtain DNA fragments of 150–900 bp. The digested chromatin was then immunoprecipitated overnight at 4 °C with 5 µg of a specific anti-ZBTB20 antibody (ProteinTech, Cat# 23987–1-AP) or normal rabbit IgG as a negative control. Protein G magnetic beads were used to capture the antibody-chromatin complexes. After cross-link reversal and proteinase K digestion, the immunoprecipitated DNA was purified, and subjected to qPCR using SYBR Green Master Mix (Roche, Switzerland) on a LightCycler® 480 II system. Primers were designed to specifically amplify the genomic regions surrounding the three predicted ZBTB20 binding sites in the KCTD1 promoter. A region in the GAPDH promoter lacking predicted sites served as a negative control. The enrichment of each target region was calculated as the percentage of input chromatin using the formula: % Input = 2^(Ct[Input] - Ct[IP]) * 100, where Ct[Input] is the cycle threshold for 1% of the starting chromatin sample. Each ChIP experiment was performed in triplicate.

Statistical analysis

All data processing, statistical analyses and plotting were performed using R software (4.1.3). The correlation between two continuous variables was evaluated through the Pearson correlation coefficient. Chi-square test was used to compare categorical variables, and Wilcoxon rank sum test was used to compare continuous variables. The “survminer” R package was used to determine the optimal cut-off value. Cox regression and Kaplan-Meier analyses were performed using the “survival” R package. A p value less than 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

Construction of a single-cell atlas of LUSC and identification of heterogeneous plasma cell subpopulations

To construct a single-cell atlas of LUSC, we analyzed the scRNA-seq data and annotated cell types using canonical marker genes. Several cell populations were identified, including alveolar epithelial cells (AT1 and AT2), ciliated cells, LUSC tumor cells, LUSC neuroendocrine (NE) cells, endothelial cells, fibroblasts, myeloid cells, mast cells, B cells, T/NK cells, and plasma cells. The distribution of the UMI counts, numbers of detected genes, and cell cycle-related scores (G2M and S phase) are shown in the UMAP embedding in Fig. 1A. Each cell population showed high expression of its corresponding marker genes, thereby supporting the reliability of cell-type annotation (Fig. 1B). The distribution of cell types across clinical stages (Stages I-III) and cell cycle phases (G1, S, and G2M) was also evaluated, and the proportion plots and relative observed/expected (Ro/e) heatmaps are shown in Fig. 1C–D. The LUSC tumor cells were significantly enriched in both the G2M phase and Stage II samples, indicating increased proliferative activity (Fig. 1E). Re-clustering analysis was performed to characterize the heterogeneity of plasma cells in the tumor microenvironment (TME), and three distinct subpopulations were identified – C1 (IGKC+ plasma cells), C2 (GZMB+ plasma cells), and C3 (IGLC2+ plasma cells) (Fig. 1F). The subgroup-specific marker genes were also identified by screening the DEGs (Fig. 1G). The proportion of the respective subpopulations varied across clinical stages and cell cycle phases, suggesting potential associations with disease progression and proliferative status (Fig. 1H–I). The C2 subpopulation exhibited higher G2M and S phase scores compared to the other subgroups, indicating a greater proliferative capacity (Fig. 1J–K). Furthermore, the C2 cells also expressed high levels of the GZMB and HLA-DRA genes, which is consistent with features related to cytotoxicity and immunomodulation (Fig. 1L). GO enrichment analysis of the subgroup-specific genes revealed that the C2 subpopulation was enriched in pathways associated with gene expression regulation, including cytoplasmic translation, mRNA splicing via spliceosome, and RNA splicing (Fig. 1M). Altogether, these results indicate that C2 plasma cells may possess elevated transcriptional and translational activity, potentially reflecting their adaptation to the metabolic demands of the TME.

Trajectory inference and intercellular communication of plasma cell subpopulations

The potential developmental relationships among the plasma cell subpopulations were explored by trajectory inference analysis using Slingshot. A continuous differentiation trajectory (lineage 1) was identified, originating from C3 (IGLC2+ plasma cells), transitioning through C1 (IGKC+ plasma cells), and ultimately converging on the C2 (GZMB+ plasma cells) state (Fig. 2A). The expression dynamics of key marker genes along the trajectory were further examined. The expression patterns of IGLC2, IGKC, and GZMB showed gradual and subgroup-specific changes along pseudotime, consistent with the distribution of the corresponding cell subpopulations (Fig. 2B). To further validate subgroup-specific transcriptional programs, module scores were calculated for each plasma cell subtype using the AddModuleScore function based on their respective marker genes. The resulting scores were visualized using both bar plots and UMAP projections, and showed clear separation and consistency with the defined subpopulations (Fig. 2C–D). Intercellular communication analysis was subsequently performed to explore the interactions between plasma cell subpopulations and other cell types within the TME. The results revealed significant receptor-ligand interactions between the C2 and C3 plasma cells and LUSC tumor cells (Fig. 2E–G). Furthermore, the key Hh pathway components, including SHH, PTCH1, PTCH2, and SMO, were highly expressed in the LUSC tumor cells, suggesting that the cells initiate Hh signaling within the LUSC microenvironment (Fig. 2H–I). Notably, communication between C2 plasma cells and LUSC cells was mediated through the SHH-PTCH1 ligand-receptor pair (Fig. 2J). These findings indicate that plasma cell subpopulations, particularly the C2 subset, may actively interact with tumor cells through Hh signaling, potentially contributing to the regulation of tumor-associated cellular processes.

C2 plasma cells in LUSC exhibit a proliferative and less differentiated state

The distribution of plasma cells across samples and cell cycle phases was determined using UMAP visualization (Fig. 3A), and the proportion of each subpopulation in the different samples and cell cycle stages were analyzed (Fig. 3B). Distinct distribution patterns were observed for the three subpopulations, indicating potential functional heterogeneity. The expression levels of subgroup-specific marker genes were also evaluated across plasma cell subpopulations and samples, and the results confirmed the robustness of subtype classification (Fig. 3C). To further characterize the biological properties of these subpopulations, we analyzed the expression of genes associated with progenitor-like or less differentiated states, including CD44, CTNNB1, KDM5B, and HIF1A. As shown in Fig. 3D, the above genes were relatively upregulated in the C2 subpopulation. Consistent with this, CytoTRACE analysis predicted a higher developmental potential of the C2 plasma cells compared to the other subgroups, suggesting a less differentiated cellular state (Fig. 3E). Furthermore, gene set enrichment analysis (GSEA) based on the genes differentially expressed in the C2 plasma cells revealed significant enrichment in biological processes related to protein synthesis, including cytoplasmic translation and ribosome biogenesis (Fig. 3F). Pathway activity was further quantified using the AddModuleScore function based on KEGG gene sets. The top 20 pathways with the highest activity in C2 plasma cells were identified, of which pathways related to ribosome and autoimmune thyroid disease were the most prominently enriched (Fig. 3G). The activity of these pathways was visualized at the single-cell level using UMAP (Fig. 3H), and consistently elevated scores in the C2 subgroup (Fig. 3I–J).

Hh signaling mediates the interaction between LUSC cells and plasma cell subpopulations

All inferred lineage trajectories of plasma cell subpopulations are shown in Fig. 4A. These results confirmed the continuity and overall structure of plasma cell state transitions. Furthermore, the expression patterns of IGLL5, IGHA2, GPR183, PTGDS, IGLC3, and IGHG3 exhibited dynamic changes along pseudotime, thus supporting the transcriptional heterogeneity of plasma cell subpopulations (Fig. 4B). Prognostic modeling based on C2 plasma cell-associated genes and functional characterization of risk groups

The prognostic significance of C2 plasma cell-associated genes was determined by univariate Cox proportional hazards regression analysis. A total of 11 genes were significantly associated with overall survival (Fig. 5A), and these candidate genes were subjected to least absolute shrinkage and selection operator (LASSO) Cox regression analysis to construct a prognostic model. Based on the optimal penalty parameter, a nine-gene signature was established (Fig. 5B–C), and the corresponding coefficients are shown in Fig. 5D. The expression patterns of the nine model genes were visualized using a heatmap, and a risk score for each patient was calculated based on the model. Patients in the TCGA cohort were stratified into high-risk and low-risk groups according to the median risk score. The distribution of risk scores and survival status indicated that patients in the high-risk group had worse clinical outcomes (Fig. 5E–F). Kaplan-Meier survival analysis further demonstrated that the overall survival of the high-risk group was significantly shorter than that of the low-risk group (Fig. 5G). Time-dependent ROC analysis was performed to assess the predictive performance of the model. The AUC values for 1-, 3-, and 5-year overall survival were 0.59, 0.68, and 0.66 respectively, indicating moderate predictive performance (Fig. 5H). To further explore the biological differences associated with the risk groups, single-sample GSEA (ssGSEA) was conducted using gene sets from the MsigDB database. The pathways with significant differences between the high- and low-risk groups are shown in Fig. 5I. Dimensionality reduction analysis demonstrated distinct distribution patterns of KEGG pathways such as leukocyte transendothelial migration, cytokine-cytokine receptor interaction, and hypertrophic cardiomyopathy (HCM), as well as GO biological processes including positive regulation of extrinsic apoptotic signaling pathway and positive regulation of cellular extravasation depending on the risk score (Fig. 5J). Furthermore, these pathways were significantly upregulated in the high-risk group (Fig. 5K), suggesting that high-risk tumors may exhibit enhanced biological features related to immune cell trafficking, cytokine signaling, apoptosis regulation, and cell migration.

Identification of key transcription factors regulating the core prognostic gene UBB

To investigate the upstream regulatory mechanisms of the core prognostic genes, we performed GO and KEGG enrichment analyses on the nine-gene prognostic signature. The results revealed significant enrichment in pathways associated with lipid metabolism (Fig. 6A). Subsequently, the DEGs between tumor and adjacent normal tissues were screened using bulk RNA-seq data (logFC < −0.5, adj. p < 0.05), and 6101 significantly downregulated genes were identified in the tumor samples (Fig. 6B). Among the nine prognostic genes, UBB exhibited the highest hazard ratio (HR). The putative transcription factors of UBB were retrieved from the GeneCards database (https://www.genecards.org/cgi-bin/carddisp.pl?gene=UBB), and those with the highest GeneCards scores (GH scores) were selected. The intersection of 1069 lipid metabolism‑related genes, 6101 downregulated genes in LUSC, and the 315 putative transcription factors of UBB revealed two overlapping genes – ZBTB20 and PPARG (Fig. 6C).

Clinical relevance and functional implications of ZBTB20 in LUSC

ZBTB20 was identified as a candidate transcriptional regulator associated with the C2-derived prognostic signature. Therefore, we next evaluated its clinical relevance in LUSC based on three independent GEO datasets. To this end, the patients in each cohort were stratified into the ZBTB20high and ZBTB20low groups based on the median expression level. As shown in Fig. 7A, patients with high ZBTB20 expression had significantly worse overall survival compared to the ZBTB20low patients across all three cohorts, thus supporting its potential role as a risk-associated factor. To further investigate the biological processes associated with ZBTB20, the DEGs between the ZBTB20high and ZBTB20low groups were screened using the LIMMA algorithm across the three datasets, and the genes upregulated in the ZBTB20high group were subjected to functional enrichment analysis. The results indicated that these genes were significantly enriched in pathways related to immune regulation and tumor microenvironment remodeling, including T cell-associated functions and extracellular matrix organization (Fig. 7B–D).

Expression and functional heterogeneity of ZBTB20 in LUSC cell subsets

The LUSC cell types were annotated from the single-cell data using canonical markers, and the distribution of UMI counts, number of detected genes, and cell cycle scores (G2M and S phase) were visualized on UMAP (Fig. 8A). Based on ZBTB20 expression levels, LUSC cells were divided into the ZBTB20low (n = 16,967) and ZBTB20high (n = 294) groups (Fig. 8B), and the proportion of each cell subpopulation in the two groups were compared. The C1 (UBE2C+) subpopulation was concentrated in the ZBTB20high group (Wilcoxon test, p = 0.044), suggesting that ZBTB20 may be associated with the proliferative characteristics of this subset (Fig. 8C–D). The expression levels of marker genes in each LUSC cell type are shown in the bubble plot in Fig. 8E. We also calculated the scores of the Hh pathway and five tumor-related pathways using the AddModuleScore function. The C1 and C5 subgroups exhibited high activity of these pathways, indicating strong proliferation and survival signals (Fig. 8F, H). The p53 and Hh pathway scores showed significant differences between the ZBTB20high and ZBTB20low groups as well, suggesting that ZBTB20 may be involved in regulating these signaling processes (Fig. 8G, I). Functional enrichment analysis of the DEGs revealed distinct the biological characteristics of each subgroup: C0 was primarily involved in detoxification and metabolic processes; C1 and C5 were significantly enriched in cell cycle, DNA replication, and chromosome segregation pathways; C3 was associated with histone expression and immune regulation; and C4 was linked to nuclear division and DNA metabolism (Fig. 8J–K). These results systematically elucidate the functional heterogeneity of LUSC cells and provide new insights into the role of ZBTB20 in distinct functional states of tumor cells.

ZBTB20 is a potential oncogene in LUSC and binds to the KCTD1 promoter

ZBTB20 mRNA expression was higher in the NCI-H2228 and HCC827 cells compared to that in the HEK293 and other cancer cell lines (Fig. 9A). To evaluate the biological relevance of ZBTB20, we knocked down the gene in NCI-H2228 and HCC827 cells using specific siRNA, and confirmed the reduction in ZBTB20 mRNA levels by qPCR (Fig. 9B). Cell proliferation was assessed by the CCK-8 assay, which showed reduced viability of the ZBTB20-silenced cells compared to the control cells, and the difference was more evident at later time points (Fig. 9C–D). In addition, the ZBTB20 knockdown group had a higher proportion of apoptotic cells compared to the control group (Fig. 9E). The migration and invasion rates of the LUSC cells also decreased significantly upon ZBTB20 silencing compared to that in the corresponding control groups (Fig. 9F). Consistent with the results of the functional assays, NCI-H2228 cells showed increased expression of cleaved caspase-3 and E-cadherin, and a decrease in Bcl-2 and vimentin levels following ZBTB20 knockdown (Fig. 9G). To explore potential transcriptional regulation by ZBTB20, we identified putative ZBTB20 binding motifs within the promoter region of KCTD1 through bioinformatics prediction. Three candidate binding sites were identified at approximately −488 bp, −300 bp, and −83 bp relative to the transcription start site (Fig. 9H). To functionally assess these regions, luciferase reporter constructs containing sequential truncations of the KCTD1 promoter were generated and co-transfected into 293T cells with ZBTB20 expression plasmid. Luciferase assays showed that promoter fragments containing the −300 bp and −83 bp regions exhibited higher transcriptional activity in the presence of ZBTB20, whereas no obvious change was observed for the fragment containing the −488 bp region. The −300 bp-containing fragment showed the highest luciferase activity (Fig. 9I). ChIP-qPCR further showed that compared to the IgG control, ZBTB20 was enriched at the −300 bp and −83 bp regions of the KCTD1 promoter in the NCI-H2228 cells, while no significant enrichment was observed at the −488 bp region (Fig. 9J). Altogether, these findings indicate that ZBTB20 binds to the promoter region of KCTD1, and may therefore regulate KCTD1 transcription in the LUSC cells.

Discussion

LUSC, one of the major subtypes of non-small cell lung cancer, is characterized by its unique location of onset, complex TME and poor clinical prognosis. Given the considerable heterogeneity of LUSC, it is crucial to explore its cellular landscape and the complex inter-cellular regulatory network for developing new diagnostic and treatment strategies.

Through dimensionality reduction and clustering analysis of scRNA-seq data, we successfully identified multiple cell types in LUSC, including alveolar epithelial cells, immune cells, stromal cells, and tumor cells. Each cell type showed distinct distribution patterns across different tumor stages and cell cycle phases. For instance, the LUSC tumor cells were significantly enriched in the G2M phase and Stage II. In addition, we identified three functionally distinct subpopulations of the plasma cells – C1 (IGKC+ plasma cells), C2 (GZMB+ plasma cells), and C3 (IGLC2+ plasma cells). The C2 subpopulation exhibited strong proliferative activity and cell cycle progression signals, and expressed high levels of GZMB and HLA-DRA genes, which correspond to cytotoxic and/or immunomodulatory functions. Furthermore, the C2 subpopulation was significantly enriched in the cytoplasmic translation and mRNA splicing pathways, indicating that these cells have high metabolic activity and protein synthesis rates, which allow them to adapt to the stressful conditions in the TME. Consistent with its high proliferative activity and metabolic characteristics, the C2 subpopulation also expressed high levels of multiple stemness-related genes. In addition, CytoTRACE analysis also predicted the highest stemness for C2 cells. Taken together, the C2 plasma cell subpopulation exhibits high proliferative potential, cytotoxic molecule expression, and stem cell-like characteristics, and may be involved in the long-term maintenance of immune responses, or play a specific role in tumor immune editing.

The developmental relationships among these plasma cell subpopulations were further explored through trajectory inference analysis, which demonstrated a path that differentiates from C3, through C1, and ultimately to the C2 state. Thus, the C2 subgroup may be a terminal or effector state of the plasma cell lineage, which is driven by tumor-specific signals. Intercellular communication further showed that the C2 cells interact with other cell types in the TME, especially tumor cells. The Hh signaling pathway was highly active in the LUSC tumor cells, and SHH-PTCH1 was identified as the key ligand-receptor pair mediating the interaction between the tumor cells and C2 cells. This functional link between the C2 cells – an immune subpopulation with proliferative and stemness potential – and the Hh signaling pathway, which is crucial in embryonic development and tumorigenesis, suggests that this pathway may play a key role serve in reshaping the TME.

We also constructed a prognostic model for LUSC by integrating nine genes that were differentially expressed in the C2 subgroup, namely UBB, CST3, MYADM, CLIC3, TBC1D1, ENPP2, SMC6, DHRS4L2, and MRPS21. This nine-gene signature could effectively distinguish between the high-risk and low-risk LUSC patients in multiple cohorts, and patients in the high-risk group exhibited significantly worse prognosis. Furthermore, the upregulated genes in the high-risk group were enriched in pathways such as leukocyte transendothelial migration, cytokine-cytokine receptor interaction, and apoptosis signal regulation. Thus, the high-risk state represents more active immune cell infiltration, a more complex cytokine network, and stronger anti-apoptotic and invasive capabilities in the TME, which also establishes the biological basis of the C2-based prognostic model.

The key tumor suppressor gene ZBTB20 was significantly upregulated in the C1 subpopulation, and its differential expression was related to the oncogenic Hh and P53 pathways. This suggests that ZBTB20 plays an important regulatory role within the LUSC microenvironment. Consistent with the bioinformatics results, ZBTB20 mRNA was highly expressed in the NSCLC cell lines. Furthermore, ZBTB20 knockdown significantly inhibited the proliferation, migration and invasion of the LUSC cells, and enhanced apoptosis rates. At the molecular level, ZBTB20 regulated the Bcl-2/c-caspase-3 pathway, and the epithelial-mesenchymal transition (EMT)-related proteins (E-cadherin and vimentin). These findings indicate that ZBTB20 acts as an oncogene in lung cancer by influencing the survival and metastatic potential of the tumor cells, and warrants further study as a prognostic biomarker and therapeutic target for lung cancer. Nevertheless, the function of ZBTB20 will have to be validated in vivo using animal models, and its upstream and downstream regulatory networks will need to be identified for developing ZBTB20-targeted interventional strategies in future.

Conclusion

We identified a highly proliferative and stem-like GZMB+ plasma cell subpopulation in LUSC, which may interact with the tumor cells via the SHH-PTCH1 signaling axis, and modulate the Hh pathway. A robust nine-gene prognostic model was developed based on this subpopulation for clinical risk stratification in LUSC. Functional assays further demonstrated that the transcription factor ZBTB20 plays a central oncogenic role in LUSC by promoting tumor cell proliferation, migration, and invasion, and suppressing apoptosis. Mechanistically, ZBTB20 may be linked to the regulation of the Bcl-2/c-caspase-3 apoptotic pathway and the EMT process. In summary, our study provides single-cell-level insights into the heterogeneity and cellular interactome of the LUSC immune microenvironment, uncovers an unconventional role of a specific plasma cell subpopulation, and elucidates the key tumor-promoting function of ZBTB20, thereby offering a new molecular basis for prognostic assessment and targeted interventions in LUSC.
