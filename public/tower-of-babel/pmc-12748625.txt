Effect of reinforcement type and viscoelasticity on static crack growth, fatigue, and creep resistance in acrylonitrile butadiene rubber (NBR) elastomers
Authors: Sahar Tavosi, Mohammad Alimardani, Mir Hamid Reza Ghoreishy, Mohammad Tavakol
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Various mechanisms contribute to the failure of engineering rubber products, including static crack growth, dynamic crack growth (fatigue), thermal accumulation, and creep failure. The viscoelasticity, which is greatly affected by the particulate or resin-based reinforcement, plays a controlling role in most of these failure phenomena. Here, the failure mechanism of the NBR (Nitrile-butadiene rubber) matrix filled with particulate carbon black (CB N660) or a high styrene resin (HSR) is investigated by performing various experimental tests. In addition, finite element analysis was adapted to calculate the J-integral as a crack propagation driving force numerically. Adding 30 phr HSR to a carbon black-filled rubber uniquely modified each failure mechanism. It increases the linear and non-linear viscoelastic loss, leading to a 3-fold increase in static crack growth resistance and a 46% increase in fatigue crack growth resistance. However, the creep resistance experienced a 100% reduction, and the propensity for the crack growth unfavorably underwent a 5-fold increase. The fracture pattern of samples with a high percentage of HSR was knotty. The resistance against creep and fatigue crack growth was remarkably reduced, where part of the particulate filler was substituted with the HSR resin.

Introduction

Rubbers are extensively employed in aerospace and mechanical engineering applications, including tires, shock absorbers, vibration mitigating devices, etc., because of unique entropic-based elasticity characterized by low stiffness, high elongation at break, and high recoverability1,2. The durability of an elastomer depends on various failure mechanisms that either separately or cooperatively limit the elastomer life. The primary failure mechanisms are static crack growth, dynamic crack growth (i.e., fatigue)3, rapid gas decompression4,5, thermal accumulation6, creep failure, stress relaxations, etc7. Without proper reinforcement by particulate fillers such as carbon black and silica, synthetic rubbers have low strength/toughness and are of little engineering value8. Particulate reinforcements tend to amplify non-linear viscoelastic loss and provides physical barriers against crack propagation7,10,11. It has been shown that when reinforcing fillers like carbon black are added to rubbers, it only increases the value of G0 (i.e., the energy to break covalent bonds) by about two times. However, under normal temperature and load application rate, the value of Gc (i.e., critical tearing energy) can increase by a factor of ten under the Influence of the filler. It is, therefore, realized that the dissipation mechanisms triggered by very high strains at the crack tip12–14 are responsible for the augmentation of crack growth resistance in the presence of reinforcing fillers. Viscoelastic loss originated from the filler-based reinforcement, though being the most effective toughening mechanism in rubbers, adversely intensifying the heat accumulation sources in rubbers, increasing the possibility for thermal failure, especially under high frequency-high strain dynamic applications15,16. Increasing viscoelastic loss is also unsuitable for creep resistance because it increases the time-dependent deformations in rubbers. Therefore, improving rubber toughness without adversely affecting the heat buildup and creep resistance is paramount for applications provoking multiple failure modes17,18.

Improving the filler state of dispersion and increasing filler-polymer interface strength is the pathway toward higher toughness rubbers with no expense enlarging the viscoelastic loss. The impact of covalent and non-covalent interface bonding on the crack growth deviation and resistance in rubber composites has been documented in the literature19. Elsewhere, it has been claimed that using hybrid nanoparticles improves filler dispersion (and enhances fracture resistance and heat buildup properties) because the small presence of a second filler enhances the dispersion of the main filler component20–23. Izadi et al. revealed that adding nanoclay to a carbon black-filled NBR rubber improves the tear, fatigue, and tensile properties24. Rooj et al. added modified organomontmorillonite (EOMt) to natural rubber along with carbon black reinforcing filler and found that adding 5 phr of EOMt significantly reduces crack growth25. Wei et al. investigated the effect of hybrid systems, including carbon black, graphene, and carbon nanotubes, on the durability of natural rubber. They found highly mechanical properties, high fatigue resistance, and low heat buildup. Efforts have been made elsewhere to combine an optimal percentage of graphene oxide and CNT to control the fatigue behavior on one hand and to prevent unnecessary viscoelastic loss on the other26.

There are other non-filler-based pathways to increase the rubber toughness. Incorporating sacrificial non-covalent interactions, blending rubbers with a high-loss polymer, and double network rubbers can be named essential approaches27. Each pathway has its benefits and weaknesses. These mechanisms have the merit that only intensifies linear viscoelastic loss, and therefore, the energy dissipation is strain-independent and is not amplified by increasing the dynamic strain. Han et al. studied the dynamic fatigue crack propagation properties of composites made of natural rubber (N.R.) and silicon rubber (VMQ) at a constant G input of 1000 J/m2. The results demonstrated that adding VMQ improved the crack propagation resistance of NR/VMQ composites. A correlation was established between the loss compliance modulus J” and crack growth rate dC/dN28.

As mentioned earlier, increasing viscoelastic loss adversely affects the physical creep rate. The physical creep depends on the molecular network’s reorientation and slippages and the elastomeric chains’ crosslinks18,29. Although physical creep deformation could be controlled by the type of elastomeric matrix or reinforcement particles29,30, modifying the surface of fillers has the advantage that it does not jeopardize other failure-related phenomena31. For instance, to enhance the creep behavior of rubber composites, Xue et al. found that adding graphene oxide and modified graphene oxide to carbon-black filler natural rubber composites positively increased creep resistance. The improved performance depends on the chemical bonds created between the graphene and polymer molecular chains32.

Efforts to balance failure-related phenomena in elastomers are still underway. Tunnicliffe compared the effect of structure and specific surface area of various carbon blacks on the natural rubber composites with a hardness level of 70 Shore A33.HSR, an emulsion-polymerized high-styrene (65%) butadiene resin, is preferred as a filler/resin-based additive to enhance the durability of rubbery parts. It improves tensile strength, hardness, adhesive modulus, and resilience, while its thermoplastic properties soften the rubber for better processing34. HSR is compatible with natural rubber and undergoes vulcanization for crosslinking, making it suitable for lightweight, high-performance tennis rackets. Pal et al.34–36 found that optimizing the NR: HSR ratio creates compatible rubber blends with enhanced properties. Their study showed that increasing HSR from 10% to 15% improved tensile and tear strength due to reduced chain mobility and higher cross-link density. Lower HSR percentages resulted in minimal heat buildup. Additionally, HSR blends well with NBR in various proportions. Nayak et al.37 studied NBR blends with 33% AN and HSR with 65% styrene content, finding that higher HSR content increased hardness and modulus but reduced tensile strength, elongation, abrasion loss, oil resistance, and the number of cycles to crack initiation, failure, and propagation. Furthermore, Kim et al.38 highlighted that vulcanized rubbers are environmentally problematic due to non-decomposability and harmful emissions. Blends like SBS/HSR, SBS/HIPS, and SBS/PS provide recyclable alternatives, enhancing abrasion resistance and tear strength up to a certain concentration before performance declines.

This article compares the effect of two types of reinforcement on balancing short-term mechanical and static crack growth parameters, as well as long-term fatigue and creep properties. A comparison in the failure mechanism of NBR composites having filler-based or filler/resin-based reinforcement is established by elaborating the role of linear and non-linear viscoelasticity.

Experimental section

Materials and compounding

The materials used in this study and the nomenclature of the samples are listed in Table 1. Further details of the formulations could be found elsewhere6. The first three samples had a systematic level of 0, 10, and 30 phr of HSR while having a constant amount of carbon black (60 phr). These samples are introduced by the names H0N60, H10N60, and H30N60. The final formulation (H30N20) contained 30 phr HSR, and the level of carbon black was lowered to 20 phr to make it similar in hardness to the hardness of the first formulation (H0N60). The desired hardness can be achieved before mixing, with each phr of CB N660 typically increasing hardness by approximately 0.3. Consequently, reducing N660 from 60 to 20 phr (while keeping the oil phr constant) decreases the hardness by 12 Shore A. The strategy of constant hardness formulations facilitated a comparison between the intrinsic reinforcing capacity in systems having the particulate filler and the HSR resin. The compounding ingredients were mixed on two-roll mills working out in a 1:1.2 friction ratio at 60 °C for 20 min. By compression molding at 160 °C, vulcanizates were finally obtained after a proper curing time.

The fatigue crack growth experiment for rubber compounds was conducted using pure shear geometry, in accordance with the ISO 27,727 standard. This approach benefits from the fact that the energy release rate remains constant, regardless of crack length. As a result, the rate of crack growth is not exacerbated by an increase in crack length, allowing for a consistent assessment of fatigue crack growth. The tests were performed on samples measuring 200 × 60 × 2 mm³ (which is the size of the rubber between the grips) under a “displacement-control” condition, utilizing simple sinusoidal cyclic excitations. Modeling background

Using the finite element analysis, a model of trouser geometry was constructed and subjected to static loading. By performing a fracture-mechanics analysis, the driving force of the crack to propagate inside the material was numerically evaluated using the J-integral parameter. This analysis and the tearing analysis introduced earlier complete our understanding of the material reinforcing effects on the crack driving and resisting forces. Experimental uniaxial tension data previously obtained for all the rubber compounds. The Yeoh hyperelastic model was chosen, and the stability of the model was confirmed for all compounds. An encaster type of constraint was applied to the end surface of the samples, and the legs on the opposite end were then pulled apart. Mechanical properties

As shown in Fig. 4, the uniaxial tension test was carried out on the rubber samples, and the important data of tensile strength, elongation at break, tensile modulus, and hardness are outlined separately for better quantitative representation. Comparing the samples H0N60, H10N60, and H30N60, it can be observed that the stepwise increase in the HSR loading is associated with an increase in tensile modulus (and equivalently hardness) and a continuous decrease in the tensile strength and elongation at break. The sample containing a high level of HSR and carbon black (H30N60) has the highest modulus among all the samples, and probably because of a higher propensity for crack initiation and growth, this composite revealed a more significant deviation in the tensile strength values. There is an abrupt change in the direction of mechanical properties when the HSR loading is kept constant and the carbon black loading is reduced to 20 phr (i.e., H30N20). The minimum level of tensile strength has been obtained for the H30N20 composite. This highlights the significant role of particulate filler in delaying crack initiation and propagation, that could be reflected in the tensile strength value. By following a judicial substitution in the type of reinforcement in H0N60 and H30N20, tensile modulus and the hardness remained constant, and the real intrinsic difference between these two reinforcement types could be well disclosed. Lower tensile strength and a sudden increase in the elongation at break are the outcomes of such a replacement in H0N60 and H30N20 composites.

Viscoelastic properties and thermal failure

The interactions between the rubber matrix, reinforcing filler, and HSR resin significantly impact the viscoelastic properties, including the storage and loss properties. Viscoelastic loss is the primary factor that defines many failure types, including quasi-static crack growth, fatigue, creep, and thermal degradation. The viscoelastic behavior of vulcanized rubber systems was analyzed by conducting a small-strain dynamic-mechanical test under a temperature sweep (DMTA) and performing a large deformation loading-unloading cycle in the tension mode to take the hysteresis loop. In Fig. 5, the storage modulus (G’) and the tanδ data have been plotted in various separate sets to mark the difference among all composites. For samples containing HSR, a curve with two glass-rubber transitions could be identified. The first glass transition temperature is related to the NBR chains, and it is located around − 3 ◦C for the H0N60, and it changes to − 1.9, − 4.3, and − 3.7 for the H10N60, H30N60, and H30N20, respectively. Comparing H0N60, H10N60, and H30N60 compounds, the NBR glass transition temperature increases and decreases with a rise in the HSR resin content. A small amount of HSR resin, which poses less of a challenge regarding mixing compatibility, increases the glass transition temperature due to its higher microstructural stiffness. However, a further increase in the resin content leads to the intensification of the phase separation and the appearance of two separate glass transitions. It is believed that, in such cases, the hard phase can even facilitate molecular movements in the areas close to the NBR chains, lowering the NBR glass transition temperature. The second visible transition peak is associated with the glass transition of the second polymer phase and is located at 54.1 to 54.5 ◦C for samples containing HSR reinforcement. This noticeable second transition apparently unveils the dissimilarity that exists between the polarity of HSR resin and the nitrile rubber. The intensity of the second peak is proportional to the HSR loading level.

The other point that must be considered is the intensity of the tanδ peak. For particulate reinforcement, the tanδ peak intensity is expected to be lowered by the filler addition46. This is because particulate filler increases the storage modulus and the loss modulus. Since the rise in the storage modulus is more severe than the increase in the loss modulus, the net change in the tanδ = G’’/G’ would be a reduction in the peak intensity. A similar effect also resulted from the HSR reinforcement, where a higher level of HSR steadily reduces the tanδ peak intensity for samples having identical C.B. content. It is interesting to note that the two samples (i.e., H0N60 and H30N20) having identical hardness show completely different intensities for the primary peak, where the peak intensity of 1 for the H0N60 is reduced to a value of almost 0.7 for H30N20 samples. This could be explained by the lower percentage of NBR in the H30N20 composite.

Analysis of quasi-static crack growth

For a precise analysis of the dependency of quasi-static crack growth on the reinforcement type, one must simultaneously monitor the forces of crack driving (i.e., G: energy release rate) and resist (i.e., Gc critical energy release rate). The former is the demand for crack growth, and the latter is the material capacity for crack growth49. The numerical method relying on calculating the J-integral in the trouser geometry was adapted to find the effect of particulate and resin-based reinforcement on the G value. This is while the Gc could be experimentally obtained. The higher value of G is an indication of a higher propensity for crack growth. In this study, the elastic crack growth modeling was performed where the G value in this approach is proportional to the hardness of the material10. The similarity in the trends of hardness and G values provided a suitable validation of the model’s performance. Figure 7 demonstrates the contour of maximum principal stress in the regions around the crack and the driving force of crack growth (G). G was calculated under two displacement levels (i.e., 10 and 20 mm). The driving force of crack is directly increased by increasing the HSR content in three samples of H0N60, H10N60, and H30N60. The increase for H10N60 and H30N60 is 1.5 and 2.58 times G in H0N60 at 10 mm displacement. Enhancement in G for these two systems also reached 1.75 and 5.1 times G in H0N60 at the quasi-static displacement of 20 mm. It seems that at a higher level of deformation, the significantly higher hardness and tensile modulus in HSR-based systems accelerate the propensity of crack growth. In numerical crack growth analysis using FEA, when the material properties are defined solely as hyperelastic, the model cannot account for rate-dependent effects arising from viscoelasticity. The numerically calculated G is merely correlated to the hardness/tensile modulus of samples. The H0N60 and H30N20 samples with the same hardness have similar driving forces for crack propagation. The G in H30N20 is about 80% of G in H0N60, and if this is combined with the previous findings on the higher viscoelastic loss of H30N20, a better quasi-static crack growth behavior could be anticipated for the H30N20. The physical interpretation could be given for the results obtained by the J-integral analysis. If the composites with a similar pre-existing crack length are loaded to the same strain level, the higher modulus and hardness sample will provide a higher propensity for crack growth. In other words, high HSR loading is not recommended for strain-controlled loading services(Fig. 8).

Having analyzed the material dependency of energy release rate, the effect of reinforcement type on the tearing pattern and the values of critical tearing energy have been demonstrated in Figs. 9 and 10, respectively. Analysis of tearing using the trouser geometry has the merit of obtaining further mechanistic data by analyzing the tearing pattern curve (Fig. 9). Tearing patterns are traditionally classified into “smooth,” “saw-toothed,” or “knotty” tearing50. The existence of repetitive microscopic inconsistencies in material uniformity can potentially alter a smooth tearing process into a more erratic, uneven fracture pattern known as “saw-toothed” tearing. When the tearing type is “knotty,” and strong fluctuations in the maximum and minimum levels of tearing force are observed, analysis of the tearing force is not trivial because a reproducible level of tearing force cannot be readily identified. In the H30N60 and H30N20 samples, where the maximum level of HSR resin is available in the samples, the shape of the crack growth changes from the typical saw-tooth (with relatively straight type crack growth) to the knotty pattern where the crack severely deviates from spreading out of the original trajectory. This might be caused by the droplet-matrix structure of the HSR resin in the NBR rubber matrix or the high viscoelastic loss generated by this resin13. Composites solely filled by the particulate reinforcement (H0N60) and having minor HSR (N10N60) follow a saw-toothed tearing pattern with minimum fluctuations in the tearing force over time.

Analysis of creep failure resistance

Creep and stress-relaxation experiments reflect the time-dependent microstructural changes that unfavorably lead to material failure either by excessive deformations (creep failure) or by releasing sealing force when applying rubber seals. Based on the Eq. (4), the creep strain indicates the normalized value of the deformation between t = 1 min (from the imposition of quasi-static load) and t = 10,000 min. In Fig. 12, creep strain over time was plotted for two replicates of H0N60 as a representative sample, and then the calculated creep rate for all the rubber composites was demonstrated. The net difference between the creep deformation at t = 10,000 min to the time = 1 min has also been plotted in the secondary axis of the graph. It should be noted that the difference in the creep behavior may be independent of the rubber modulus. Samples with higher modulus experience less initial deformation (L1) and may exhibit relatively small creep over time. However, if the creep deformation index (Lt-L1) is normalized to the deformation in one minute to calculate the creep strain, the Influence of the modulus on the creep behavior would almost have vanished. With increasing HSR in H0N60, H10N60, and H30N60 samples, the creep rate has unfavorably increased in the same direction as the viscoelastic loss. Active reinforcing fillers can affect creep behavior in two ways. One way is by increasing the viscoelastic loss that tends to augment the time-dependent creep deformation. On the other hand, increasing the amount of active reinforcing filler imparts restriction on the mobility of rubber chains that may retard chain relaxations and temporarily conceals time-dependent behavior51. Comparing the creep behavior of H30N60 and H30N20, it can be observed that despite the lower viscoelastic loss in H30N20, the creep resistance is remarkably lower for the H30N20 composite because reducing the level of carbon black provides a higher relaxation and mobility for the chains. Other samples benefit more from the retardation of molecular mobility by reinforcing fillers. Although they have a higher viscoelastic loss, the creep resistance is greater for samples having 60 phr of particulate filler (i.e., carbon black). In compounds with the same hardness, H0N60, and H30N20, the existence of rubber chains with higher mobility and increasing viscoelastic loss can be named as the primary and secondary factors elucidating the unfavorable creep resistance of the H30N20 composite.

Conclusion

In this research work, the effect of two reinforcing types, including a particulate reinforcement (carbon black) and a resin-based reinforcement (HSR), on the main failure mechanisms of NBR is compared. The rubber viscoelasticity seems to be the bridging factor relating the reinforcement type to the failure phenomena. It was demonstrated that simultaneous usage of resin-based and particulate reinforcement leads to a severe change in rubber properties, including rubber hardness, tensile modulus, tensile strength, etc. HSR significantly enhances crack growth resistance under both quasi-static and dynamic loading conditions by increasing viscoelastic energy dissipation, making it highly suitable for dynamic dampers, anti-vibration mounts, and other applications prioritizing energy absorption and fatigue life. However, this benefit comes at the severe expense of creep resistance, fundamentally disqualifying HSR-dominated compounds from use in quasi-static seals, gaskets, and any component requiring dimensional stability under sustained load. It was possible to fabricate composites with similar hardness levels, one reinforced by a combination of HSR and carbon black and the other solely by carbon black. Evaluating dynamic properties indicated that the presence of HSR resin in the compound revealed a secondary transition peak. This increases the viscoelastic loss and improves crack growth resistance, although it adversely affects creep properties. Also, due to the possible drop-matrix structure of HSR resin in the NBR rubber matrix or the high viscoelastic loss resulting from this resin, the fracture surface of samples containing a high percentage of HSR was rough and knotty. In two samples, H0N60 and H30N20, with the same hardness, although the amount of viscoelastic loss was slightly higher in the H30N20, the fatigue failure mechanism severely deteriorated in the sample, with a low level of reinforcing filler. This is because the physical presence of the filler has a remarkable role in creating a barrier against fatigue crack growth. Lowering the mobility-restricting mechanism in rubbers with low particulate filler levels led to augmented mobility and lower creep resistance.
