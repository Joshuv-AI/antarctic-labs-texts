Introduction

Why should we be concerned: the burden

Adolescence is a critical stage of human development, marked by significant physical, emotional and social transitions that shape long-term health and well-being. Globally, approximately one in seven adolescents aged 10–19 years experiences a mental health condition, with most cases remaining undiagnosed and untreated.1 The burden of adolescent mental health conditions is unevenly distributed, with low- and middle-income regions, including sub-Saharan Africa (SSA), disproportionately affected.

SSA is home to approximately 256 million adolescents (10–19 years), representing 23% of its total population.2 An estimated one in five adolescents in SSA experiences at least one mental health problem.3 A previous review reports prevalence rates of 26.9% for depression, 29.8% for anxiety, 40.8% for emotional and behavioural disorders, 21.5% for post-traumatic stress disorder (PTSD) and 20.8% for suicidal ideation.4 A recent meta-analysis indicates slightly lower but still substantial prevalence estimates: 15.27% for depression, 11.78% for anxiety, 12.53% for PTSD and 9.76% for conduct disorder.5 The prevalence of substance use is also concerning, with alcohol, 10%–20%, and cannabis, 5%–10% being the most frequently used substances.6 7 These estimates vary considerably across countries and contexts, such as conflict-affected versus stable settings, rural versus urban environments and between school-attending and out-of-school adolescents. Multiple overlapping factors, including infectious disease outbreaks (eg, Ebola), HIV/AIDS, exposure to violence, poverty and climate-related stressors, further influence these variations.4 5 7 Adolescents in conflict-affected and displacement settings are particularly vulnerable to conditions such as PTSD and depression. Those living in high HIV-burden settings frequently experience compounded psychosocial distress due to stigma, orphanhood and caregiving responsibilities. Out-of-school adolescents often face heightened risk profiles, including increased exposure to violence and child labour.8 In conflict-affected settings, the prevalence estimates of adolescent mental health conditions range from 25% to 83%.810 For example, in Sudan, Awad et al found that 67.7% of conflict-affected adolescents had major depressive disorder, with high levels of suicidal ideation.10 Dessauvagie et al recorded a 25% prevalence of psychiatric disorders among adolescents living with HIV, with 30%–50% experiencing emotional/behavioural distress.11 Following the Ebola outbreak in the Democratic Republic of Congo, Cénat et al recorded high prevalence rates of depression (87.32%) and PTSD (44.42%) among adolescent survivors.12

Despite recent efforts to quantify the burden of adolescent mental health conditions in SSA, significant gaps persist in the availability and representativeness of data across many settings. Most existing studies are geographically limited, lack representative samples or employ screening tools that are not fully validated for local contexts. Consequently, current estimates likely underestimate the true burden and may not accurately reflect context-specific determinants.13

Key challenges and barriers to progress

Adolescent mental health remains severely underprioritised across SSA. Estimates suggest that fewer than 10% of adolescents with diagnosable mental disorders receive any formal care.14 This treatment gap is sustained by multilevel barriers encompassing structural, institutional and interpersonal dimensions (figure 1).

Stigma remains one of the major barriers to the utilisation of mental health services. Adolescents may be discouraged from seeking care due to fear of judgement shaped by gender norms, concerns about confidentiality within clinical settings, punitive school environments and discrimination from peers or family. Persistent beliefs that mental illness reflects personal weakness, spiritual affliction or bewitchment further limit engagement with formal services.15 These challenges are compounded by pluralistic care pathways, in which adolescents and their families navigate multiple systems simultaneously, including traditional, faith-based and biomedical care.16 While often viewed as barriers, such informal care pathways, if constructively engaged, can complement formal services by improving trust, access, acceptability and continuity of healthcare.15 16

At the system level, underinvestment and policy implementation challenges further constrain progress. Mental health receives none to less than 2% of the national health budgets in most SSA countries.17 18 This limits financing for medications, specialised facilities, research and workforce development.18 19 For instance, there is a severe shortage of trained professionals, with fewer than 1.5 specialised mental health workers (either psychiatrists, psychologists or psychiatric nurses) per 100 000 population.19 20 Although this highlights the treatment gap, it also presents an opportunity for task-sharing and collaborative models with informal care providers. Moreover, fewer than 20% of countries have adolescent-specific mental health strategies, and where policies exist, implementation is often undermined primarily by governance challenges and weak intersectoral coordination.14 16 Mental health services are poorly integrated into primary healthcare, which limits opportunities for routine screening, referral and continuity of care.21 Schools and community-based platforms, which are key entry points for early detection and intervention, are also often under-resourced.

The cost of inaction

Failing to invest in adolescent mental health carries serious consequences for individuals, economies and societies. Untreated conditions such as anxiety and depression often progress into chronic, debilitating illnesses, increasing reliance on long-term and costly healthcare. In economic terms, the costs are substantial, as indicated in a review in low-income settings, where the annual economic loss per capita attributed to mental health disorders ranges from approximately US$5 to US$15, accounting for as much as 0.4%–1.0% of gross domestic product.22 The cost of delivering scaled-up, evidence-based interventions (such as therapies for depression or anxiety) is estimated at US$0.50 to US$2.50 per person; yet, these yield high returns, with benefit-cost ratios exceeding 2:1 and up to 9:1 in some cases.22 While these estimates apply to mental health disorders more broadly, disaggregated data specifically for adolescent mental health in SSA remain sparse, highlighting an important evidence gap. The educational and social consequences of unaddressed mental health disorders are also substantial. Poor mental health adversely affects academic performance, increases school dropout rates and diminishes future workforce productivity, thereby reinforcing cycles of socioeconomic disadvantage. Mental health disorders are associated with higher rates of substance abuse, crime and social instability. Adolescents experiencing multiple psychosocial adversities are 4–5 times more likely to report suicidal ideation.23 Neglecting adolescent mental health thus risks not only individual suffering, but also socioeconomic loss at a large scale. The up-front investments required for prevention, early intervention and scaling proven services are likely modest compared with the long-term returns in health, productivity and social resilience.

Call to action: what should be done?

Addressing the adolescent mental health crisis in SSA requires a coordinated, systems-based approach that begins with meaningful integration of primary healthcare, schools and community platforms into health system frameworks. A stepped-care model embedded within existing systems offers a more pragmatic solution. It emphasises early identification, tiered interventions based on severity and clear referral pathways.

Primary healthcare systems are a critical entry point for adolescent mental health services, particularly in settings where specialised care is scarce. Integrating mental health into primary care enables routine screening, early identification, timely intervention and continuity of care within existing service platforms. This can include the use of brief, validated screening tools adapted to local contexts to identify common mental health conditions.24 25 Given the shortage of specialised professionals in such facilities, task-sharing approaches that engage trained non-specialist providers, such as nurses, counsellors and community health workers, offer a scalable and cost-effective solution, especially when supported by supervision and standardised treatment protocols.26 27 Effective implementation requires clearly defined stepped-care pathways, including referral thresholds for moderate-to-severe cases. The integration efforts must also be context-sensitive, aligning with local workforce capacity and health system infrastructure available,26 28 while addressing potential barriers such as mistrust, workforce incentives and cultural perceptions of mental illness28 29

Schools are a critical platform for prevention and early intervention, given their ability to reach large adolescent populations during formative years. Integrating mental health literacy into school curricula can improve awareness, reduce stigma, strengthen coping and help-seeking skills.30 31 School-based programmes should also address key drivers of psychological distress, including bullying, violence and academic pressure.32 33 Teachers and school counsellors play a central role in early identification and support, and therefore require adequate training and institutional backing to ensure sustainability. Establishing clear referral pathways linking schools with primary healthcare and community services is essential to ensure continuity of care for students with identified needs. Available trial evidence suggests that school-based mental health programmes show promise; however, evidence remains uneven across countries, populations and intervention types.30 Moreover, given the high proportion of out-of-school adolescents in SSA, complementary community-based strategies are necessary to ensure equitable access to care.

Community and social protection platforms are essential for reaching adolescents, whether or not they are engaged in formal health or education systems, while also addressing broader social determinants of mental health.34 Community-based interventions can be delivered through youth-friendly spaces that promote peer support, psychosocial care and life-skills development.34 35 These platforms also offer opportunities to strengthen family environments by supporting caregivers and promoting positive parenting, especially for adolescents experiencing household-related stressors. Integrating mental health interventions with social protection mechanisms such as educational subsidies, cash transfers and livelihood programmes can help mitigate structural drivers of distress.35 Furthermore, engaging community actors, including faith-based organisations, traditional healers and youth organisations, as partners in awareness, referral and support pathways is crucial to enhance cultural relevance, acceptability and uptake of services.36

To support effective implementation across these platforms and ensure sustainable impact, several cross-cutting priorities must be addressed. As an aspirational policy benchmark, we urge governments to increase mental health investment towards at least 5% of national health budgets, consistent with WHO Mental Health Gap Action Programme guidelines.17 Expanding the workforce through specialist training and institutionalised task-sharing models is critical to improve service delivery. Strengthening data systems and research capacity is moreover, vital to generate context-specific evidence, monitor services and guide the adaptive and equitable implementation.

Conclusions

The burden of adolescent mental health in SSA is escalating. Urgent, sustained investment is essential to avert long-term health, social and economic consequences. A systems-integrated approach integrating primary care, schools and community platforms could offer a scalable pathway to impact. Strengthening financing, workforce and research is equally critical for sustainable outcomes.
