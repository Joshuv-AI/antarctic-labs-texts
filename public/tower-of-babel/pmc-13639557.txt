Associations between Finger-Dexterity Dual Task and Neuropsychological Tests in Middle-Aged and Older Adults
Authors: Ziheng Pan, Kenji Tsunoda, Jaehoon Seol, Taiki Inoue, Kyohei Shibuya, Koki Nagata, Iku Nishimura, Jieun Yoon, Miyuki Nemoto, Tetsuaki Arai, Tomohiro Okura
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundDespite the importance of early cognitive impairment detection, standard screening tools have limitations. Motor-cognitive dual tasks such as the Digital Trail Making Peg Test (DTMP) are feasible objective alternatives. Nonetheless, most studies have focused on older adults, and whether these tasks are associated with cognitive performance in midlife remains unclear. This study investigated associations between DTMP performance and neuropsychological test performance in middle-aged and older adults.MethodsWe analyzed 171 middle-aged (mean age, 54.5 years; female, 61.4%) and 173 older (mean age, 73.7 years; female, 41.6%) adults from the Tsukuba Happiness Life Study. Finger-dexterity dual-task performance was assessed using DTMP-A, requiring peg insertion in ascending numerical order, and DTMP-B, requiring alternating peg insertion between numbers and hiragana characters. Neuropsychological test performance was assessed using standardized measures of attention, executive function, memory, and language. Associations were examined using age-stratified multiple linear regression models after adjusting for covariates, with supportive domain-level models additionally adjusted for the Digit Symbol Substitution Test (DSST).ResultsIn age-stratified models, faster DTMP-B performance was significantly associated with better performance across attention, executive, memory, and language measures in both age groups, with standardized β coefficients ranging in absolute value from 0.22 to 0.64. Compared with DTMP-A, DTMP-B showed a broader and more consistent pattern of associations. In supportive DSST-adjusted models, DTMP-B remained associated with executive, memory, and language domains.ConclusionThe finger-dexterity dual task, particularly DTMP-B, showed consistent associations with neuropsychological test performance across cognitive domains, suggesting its potential as a practical performance-based indicator in middle-aged and older adults.

INTRODUCTION

Cognitive impairment is a major determinant of quality of life among older adults.1) Given that underlying pathological changes may progress for years before clinical symptoms appear, early identification of cognitive impairment is critical for timely intervention and dementia prevention.2) Dementia prevention is increasingly approached from a life-course perspective, with modifiable risks accumulating from midlife onward.3) Nevertheless, cognitive screening remains uncommon among middle-aged adults,4) and practical scalable tools suitable for this population are lacking.

Mild cognitive impairment (MCI), a prodromal stage of dementia, is typically assessed using neuropsychological batteries.1,5) Although early definitions of MCI emphasized memory deficits,6) impairments in attention, executive, language, and visuospatial domains are also evident in the early stages of MCI.7,8) Common tools such as the Montreal Cognitive Assessment9) and Mini-Mental State Examination10) require professional administration, elicit anxiety,11) and lack sensitivity to subtle changes in community settings.1) Motor-cognitive performance-based tasks have attracted increasing interest as complementary approaches to cognitive assessment.12-14) Among them, finger-dexterity tasks are feasible in community settings because they can be performed while seated, require minimal physical burden, and may be perceived as less test-like than conventional cognitive screening. The Trail Making Peg test, which combines pegboard manipulation with the sequencing demands of the Trail Making Test,15) has demonstrated acceptable validity and reliability in community-dwelling older adults.16) Accordingly, the Digital Trail Making Peg Test (DTMP) was developed to enable standardized timing and automated error detection.17) These features may allow DTMP to serve as a low-burden, performance-based complement to conventional cognitive assessments in community-based settings. Reportedly, DTMP performance is associated with cognitive performance, and DTMP-B (insert the pegs in an alternating sequence of numbers and “hiragana” [a Japanese syllabary]) imposes greater cognitive load than DTMP-A.17,18)

Collectively, previous studies conducted exclusively on older adults have supported the feasibility of DTMP-A and provided preliminary evidence on stronger cognitive associations with DTMP-B.16-18) However, it remains unknown whether similar associations exist in middle-aged adults, a group that has received comparatively less attention despite representing a key target for dementia prevention.3,4) Additionally, the associations between DTMP performance and cognitive domains evaluated using standardized neuropsychological assessments have not yet been systematically examined. Therefore, we aimed to describe age-stratified associations of DTMP-A and DTMP-B completion times with standardized neuropsychological test scores in middle-aged and older adults. Additionally, we compared the association of DTMP-A and DTMP-B and examined whether these associations persisted after additional adjustment for the Digit Symbol Substitution Test (DSST).

MATERIALS AND METHODS

Study Design and Participation

This cross-sectional study was conducted within the framework of the Tsukuba Happiness Life Study (THLS), an ongoing longitudinal prospective cohort study that began in 2023 and tracks the health of Tsukuba citizens to determine those who are more likely to suffer from dementia and require nursing care and to develop preventive methods.19) The participant selection process is presented in Fig. 1.

The THLS comprised two main phases: a mail survey and an in-person field assessment. In the mail survey phase, a questionnaire was mailed to 10,000 residents aged 45–89 years from six districts in Tsukuba City, who were randomly selected using stratified block randomization based on area, sex, and age distribution. Among 2,281 respondents, 1,208 individuals who met one or more of the following potential risk criteria were invited to participate in the field assessment: (1) cognitive decline risk20) (Everyday Cognition mean score ≥1.54, n=616); (2) sleep disorder risk21,22) (Athens Insomnia Scale total score ≥6, n=638); and (3) physical function risk (history of stroke, heart disease, or musculoskeletal disorders, or age ≥60 years, underweight, and inability to walk continuously for 15 minutes, n=471). These criteria were used to enrich the invited sample for potential health-related risk.

Overall, 395 individuals participated in the field assessment phase, which included DTMP, neuropsychological assessments, and structured interviews to collect demographic and health-related information.

Because the broader THLS project was designed to examine multiple health-related risks, including cognitive, sleep, and physical function-related risks, individuals with these risk profiles were included at the invitation stage. However, for the present analysis, participants with medical conditions that could directly affect neuropsychological test performance or interfere with task completion were excluded to reduce potential confounding. Accordingly, 27 participants were excluded for medical reasons: cerebrovascular disease (n=13), dementia diagnosis or dementia-related medication use (n=3), psychiatric disorders (n=3), Parkinson’s disease (n=3), and other systemic or neurological conditions (n=5). These medical conditions were identified using a self-administered medical history questionnaire completed at the in-person assessment and confirmed with participants by trained research staff. Additionally, 24 participants were excluded because of incomplete or invalid data: DTMP-related issues (DTMP not performed, n=3; DTMP-B completion time reached the predefined 300-second maximum, n=2; inability to understand the DTMP-B rules, n=4), TMT-B-related issues (TMT-B not performed, n=3; TMT-B completion time reached the predefined 300-second maximum, n=2), and missing Geriatric Depression Scale (GDS) or Clock Drawing Test (CDT) data (n=10). Finally, 344 participants were included in the final analysis and classified as middle-aged adults (<65 years) and older adults (≥65 years).

This study was approved by the Ethics Committee of the University of Tsukuba Hospital (R03-335) and was registered at the University Hospital Medical Information Network Center (UMIN000051941). Written informed consent was obtained from all participants at enrollment. Data were collected from February to August 2023.

Data Collection

Digital Trail Making Peg Test

Each participant performed both the DTMP-A and DTMP-B using the Peg-Amore device (Japan Patent 2019-024,707; NEWCOM Inc., Japan) (Fig. 2).

In the DTMP-A, the participants were instructed to move 25 pegs from a distal board to a proximal board in ascending numerical order (1–25), as displayed in the panel. Prior to the main trial, the participants completed a practice session using numbers 1–9 to familiarize themselves with the task procedure.

In the DTMP-B, the panel displayed both numbers (1–13) and hiragana characters (あ-し). The participants were instructed to insert the pegs in an alternating sequence of numbers and hiragana, following the order: 1 → あ → 2 → い → 3 … 12 → し → 13. The full DTMP-B task layout is shown in Supplementary Fig. S1. Prior to the main trial, the participants completed a brief practice session using numbers 1–5 and hiragana characters あ-え to familiarize themselves with the alternating sequence format.

In both modes, an auditory beep was automatically triggered when the peg was inserted at an incorrect position, and the participants had to remove the peg and insert it into the correct position. This automated procedure was intended to reduce examiner-dependent variability in administration and scoring. All participants performed both tasks with their dominant hand to standardize procedures, reduce participant burden, and minimize variability attributable to non-dominant-hand performance. The completion time was defined as the duration from the start signal to the insertion of the final (25th) peg and was recorded in seconds to the nearest 0.1 second. A shorter completion time indicated better performance.

Neuropsychological tests

Attentional function: Attentional function was evaluated using the Digit Symbol Substitution Test (DSST; 0–135)23) and the Japanese version of TMT-A (completion time in seconds).24,25)

Executive function: Executive function was assessed using the Japanese version of TMT-B (completion time in seconds), in which the participants alternately connected numbers and Japanese hiragana characters.24,25)

Memory function: To comprehensively capture the memory domain, memory function was assessed using the Japanese version of both the logical memory immediate recall (LM-IA; 0–25) and delayed recall (LM-IIA; 0–25) subtests of the Wechsler Memory Scale-Revised26,27) and the Japanese version of the free and cued selective reminding test-free recall (FCSRT-FR; 0–48).28,29)

Language function: Verbal fluency was assessed using the Japanese version of the Category Fluency Test (CFT; total number of correct responses across the animal, fruit, and vegetable categories).30,31)

Visuospatial function: Visuospatial function was evaluated using the CDT (0–10), which was scored according to the method proposed by Rouleau et al.32)

All neuropsychological tests were conducted face-to-face during the field assessment by trained research staff using a standardized protocol. The tests were administered in the following standardized order: LM-IA, DSST, CFT, TMT-A, TMT-B, CDT, LM-IIA, and FCSRT-FR. Each neuropsychological testing session lasted 50 minutes, and short breaks were provided between tasks as needed to reduce participant burden.

Covariates

The covariates were sex, age, body mass index (BMI) category, educational attainment, diabetes, heart disease, and depressive symptoms. BMI was calculated from objectively measured height and weight and categorized as <18.5, 18.5–24.9, and ≥25 kg/m² to distinguish participants who were underweight, normal weight, and overweight or obese, respectively. Educational attainment was dichotomized into ≤12 years (high school or lower) and ≥13 years (college, junior college, vocational school, or above). Heart disease and diabetes were identified based on self-reported medical history. Depressive symptoms were assessed using the 15-item GDS, with higher scores indicating more severe symptoms.

Statistical Analysis

DTMP and neuropsychological test scores were compared between age groups using Student’s t-test, with Cohen’s d calculated as the effect size. For the primary analyses, age-stratified multiple linear regression models were fitted separately for middle-aged and older adults. In each model, performance on one neuropsychological test (DSST, TMT-A, TMT-B, LM-IA, LM-IIA, FCSRT-FR, or CFT) was specified as the dependent variable, and either DTMP-A or DTMP-B completion time was entered as the independent variable. Three models were fitted: an unadjusted model, an age- and sex-adjusted model, and a multivariable-adjusted model further adjusted for BMI category, educational attainment, diabetes, heart disease, and GDS score. The standardized β coefficients indicated the direction and magnitude of the associations between DTMP completion time and performance on each neuropsychological test. For interpretive context, absolute standardized β values of approximately 0.10, 0.30, and 0.50 were regarded as small, moderate, and large standardized associations, respectively; these thresholds were used as heuristic references rather than clinical cutoffs.33) For supportive analysis, domain-level models were additionally adjusted for the DSST, a proxy for attentional control.34) Cognitive-domain scores were calculated by averaging the z-scores of tests within each domain—executive (TMT-B), memory (LM-IA, LM-IIA, and FCSRT-FR), and language (CFT). The TMT-B was reverse-scored prior to standardization so that higher scores indicated better performance for all variables. We did not model the CDT because the scores showed a ceiling effect (mean: 9.3/10). Benjamini–Hochberg false discovery rate (FDR) correction was applied to account for multiple association analyses. FDR correction was performed separately within each age group, DTMP type, and model across the seven neuropsychological outcomes for the test-level analyses and across the three cognitive-domain scores for the supportive domain-level analyses. FDR-adjusted p-values were used to determine statistical significance. Regression diagnostics included inspection of standardized residual plots, normal Q–Q plots, plots of residuals against fitted values, and assessment of multicollinearity. In the multivariable-adjusted models, the maximum variance inflation factor for the DTMP-A and DTMP-B was 2.1, indicating a low multicollinearity risk. All statistical analyses were performed using SPSS Statistics version 28 (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA).

RESULTS

Participant Characteristics

The final analysis included 171 middle-aged adults (mean age, 54.5±5.6 years; female, 61.4%) and 173 older adults (mean age, 73.7±6.0 years; female, 41.6%), totaling 344 participants.

The characteristics of the participants are summarized in Table 1. The age groups differed with respect to sex distribution, educational attainment, and health status.

The DTMP and neuropsychological test scores are presented in Table 2. Middle-aged adults generally performed better than older adults, consistent with expected age-related differences (Cohen's d=0.49–1.45; except for the CDT, d=0.10).

Associations between DTMP and Neuropsychological Tests

Longer DTMP-A and DTMP-B completion times were significantly associated with poorer performance across multiple neuropsychological tests in both middle-aged and older adults (Table 3). In the multivariable-adjusted models, the significant standardized coefficients ranged from small to large in magnitude (absolute β=0.19–0.64).

Attentional function

DTMP performance was significantly associated with performance on measures of attentional function in middle-aged and older adults. Longer DTMP-A completion times were associated with lower DSST scores (middle-aged, β=-0.38; older, β=-0.53) and longer TMT-A completion times (middle-aged, β=0.41; older, β=0.46). Similarly, longer DTMP-B completion times were associated with lower DSST scores (middle-aged, β=-0.34; older, β=-0.37) and longer TMT-A completion times (middle-aged, β=0.38; older, β=0.50).

Executive function

DTMP performance was significantly associated with performance on the TMT-B, a measure of executive function, in middle-aged and older adults. Longer DTMP-A completion times were consistently associated with longer TMT-B completion times (middle-aged: β=0.31, older: β=0.28), with a larger standardized coefficient observed for DTMP-B (middle-aged, β=0.64; older, β=0.41).

Memory function

Performance on measures of memory function was associated with DTMP performance in both age groups. Longer DTMP-B completion times were significantly associated with lower LM-IA scores (middle-aged, β=-0.23; older, β=-0.22), LM-IIA scores (middle-aged, β=-0.25; older, β=-0.22), and FCSRT-FR scores (middle-aged, β=-0.23; older, β=-0.40) in middle-aged and older adults. Although DTMP-A showed less consistent associations, DTMP-B showed significant associations across all memory measures in both age groups.

Language function

DTMP performance was associated with performance on the CFT, a measure of language function, in middle-aged and older adults. Lower CFT scores were significantly associated with longer DTMP-A (middle-aged, β=-0.24; older, β=-0.25) and DTMP-B (middle-aged, β=-0.45; older, β=-0.35) performance in both age groups.

Additional Model with DSST Adjustment (Domain-Level Results)

The associations between DTMP performance and standardized cognitive-domain scores in multivariable-adjusted and supportive models with additional adjustment for the DSST are presented in Table 4. The full test-level estimates from the DSST-adjusted models are provided in Supplementary Table S1.

For DTMP-A, only the association with the executive-function domain in middle-aged adults remained significant after additional adjustment for the DSST (β=-0.23), whereas the remaining domain-level associations were not significant.

For DTMP-B, associations with executive function (middle-aged, β=-0.62; older, β=-0.36), memory function (middle-aged, β=-0.27; older, β=-0.26), and language function (middle-aged, β=-0.40; older, β=-0.25) remained significant after additional adjustment for the DSST in both age groups. The magnitude of these DSST-adjusted associations was moderate-to-large for the executive-function domain and small-to-moderate for memory and language domains.

DISCUSSION

This study revealed that longer DTMP completion times were associated with poorer performance on neuropsychological measures spanning multiple cognitive domains in both age groups. Within this pattern, DTMP-B showed a broader and more consistent association profile than DTMP-A, particularly for executive, memory, and language-related measures, and these associations persisted after additional DSST adjustment. These findings suggest that DTMP-B is associated with aspects of neuropsychological test performance not solely explained by attentional demands or common demographic and health-related confounders. DTMP-B coefficients were generally moderate-to-large for executive-function measures and small-to-moderate for memory and language measures, suggesting that the associations were not negligible. The broadly similar association patterns across age groups support the relevance of DTMP-B as a finger-dexterity dual-task measure from midlife to later life.

Our results should be interpreted in the context of previous work on motor-cognitive dual-task assessment, which has focused primarily on older adults, with most protocols relying on gait-based paradigms.35) These findings extend previous work by showing that associations between finger-dexterity dual-task performance and neuropsychological test performance are observable not only in older adults but also in middle-aged adults. The inclusion of middle-aged adults is clinically relevant because midlife is increasingly recognized as a key period for dementia prevention, during which modifiable risk factors may accumulate and preventive strategies may still be implemented.3,4) A brief seated finger-dexterity dual task such as DTMP, which can be completed in approximately five minutes and is administered as a simple peg-placement task rather than a conventional question-and-answer cognitive test, could be incorporated into community-based health programs as a low-burden performance measure to identify individuals who may benefit from more comprehensive cognitive assessment or preventive support. However, DTMP should not be interpreted as a standalone screening or diagnostic tool at this stage, and longitudinal validation is needed before implementation in preventive programs.

Extending the exploratory work by Inoue et al.,17) who used the Five-Cog screening test36) in older adults, the present study used standardized neuropsychological measures and included both middle-aged and older adults. DTMP-B showed a broader association profile than DTMP-A, particularly for executive, memory, and language-related measures.

With respect to attentional function, both DTMP-A and DTMP-B were significantly associated with DSST and TMT-A in both age groups, and these links remained robust after covariate adjustment (Table 3). Because attentional resources are fundamental to a wide range of cognitive processes,37,38) we examined whether the associations with memory, executive function, and language domains persisted after DSST adjustment.

Regarding executive function, DTMP-B showed larger standardized coefficients than DTMP-A in both age groups. Because DTMP-B and TMT-B share similar task demands, these associations may partly reflect an overlap in task structure and underlying cognitive components. Even considering this aspect of test similarity, this pattern is consistent with neuroimaging evidence demonstrating increased prefrontal activation during DTMP-B execution.18) The combined demands of manual dexterity, sequencing, and cognitive flexibility may explain the relatively larger standardized coefficients observed for DTMP-B compared with the simpler DTMP-A.39,40)

We included both the LM and FCSRT to provide a broader assessment of the memory domain. Unlike DTMP-A, for which most associations with memory were attenuated to non-significance in the multivariable-adjusted models, DTMP-B maintained significant associations with memory across both age groups. These associations for DTMP-B were largely retained after domain-level DSST adjustment, whereas the association for DTMP-A with the memory domain was no longer significant (Table 4). This pattern is consistent with evidence that memory decline reflects contributions from both executive-attentional control processes and long-term declarative systems.41-45) These mechanisms may explain why DTMP-B showed broader and more consistent associations with memory than DTMP-A from midlife to late life.

A similar pattern was observed for language performance, with DTMP-B remaining associated with the language domain after DSST adjustment, whereas the association for DTMP-A was no longer significant. While the relatively high educational attainment of our sample may have influenced the overall CFT performance,46,47) the robustness of DTMP-B associations suggests that this dual task is associated with language-related cognitive performance beyond general attentional resources.

One methodological strength of the present study lies in the use of standardized neuropsychological assessments to examine DTMP performance across multiple cognitive domains and two distinct age groups. This design aligned DTMP with neuropsychological tests commonly evaluated in diagnostic workups for MCI and extended its potential applicability to middle-aged adults. Furthermore, by incorporating DSST-adjusted domain-level analyses, this study provided supportive evidence that the broader associations observed for DTMP-B are not solely driven by general attentional resources.

This study had several limitations. First, the study sample did not represent the general population of middle-aged and older adults. Participants were selected from mail survey respondents who met one or more predefined risk criteria for cognitive decline, sleep disorders, or physical function impairment, and the sample also had relatively high educational attainment, with 66.3% of participants having ≥13 years of education (Table 1). These sampling characteristics may have shifted the distribution of cognitive performance and introduced heterogeneity in cognitive reserve,41) thereby limiting the generalizability of the observed associations between DTMP performance and neuropsychological test performance. Accordingly, the present findings should be interpreted with caution when applying them to healthier, lower-risk, or less highly educated populations. Additionally, the restricted variability observed for visuospatial function assessed by the CDT may partly reflect educational influences48) and the limited sensitivity of conventional CDT scoring to subtle decline.32,49) The observed associations may also differ in populations with lower or more diverse educational backgrounds. Second, the generalizability of the hiragana-based DTMP-B task to populations with different writing systems and literacy levels may be limited. Because DTMP-B requires alternating between numbers and hiragana characters, its applicability to populations with different writing systems or limited Japanese literacy may be restricted. Culturally and linguistically adapted versions using locally familiar ordered symbols should be developed and validated before applying DTMP-B in non-Japanese populations. Third, because of the cross-sectional design, this study could not establish temporal sequence or causality between DTMP performance and neuropsychological test performance, nor determine the predictive value of DTMP. Although a comprehensive neuropsychological battery was employed, we did not perform formal MCI diagnoses. Future studies should evaluate the discriminative validity of DTMP in clinically diagnosed populations. As in previous longitudinal studies of other physical performance measures and cognitive outcomes,50) the predictive validity of DTMP should also be examined in prospective cohort studies with baseline DTMP assessment and follow-up for incident cognitive impairment, including MCI or dementia. Additionally, because DTMP performance was assessed using only the dominant hand, this study did not allow us to evaluate interlimb asymmetry or subtle motor impairment affecting the non-dominant hand. Potential practice or learning effects arising from the practice trials before the main DTMP tasks may also have influenced task performance. Finally, although our analyses were stratified by age, more fine-grained analyses may help clarify age-related differences in these associations.

In conclusion, finger-dexterity dual-task performance, as assessed by the DTMP, was associated with neuropsychological test performance across multiple cognitive domains in both middle-aged and older adults. Compared with DTMP-A, DTMP-B showed a broader and more consistent pattern of associations, with generally moderate-to-large associations for executive-function measures and small-to-moderate associations for memory and language measures. The present findings suggest that DTMP-B may have potential as a low-burden, performance-based indicator of cognitive performance across a broader age range. However, because this cross-sectional study relied on continuous neuropsychological test scores rather than formal diagnostic classification, DTMP-B should not be interpreted as a screening or prognostic tool at this stage. Future prospective cohort studies should assess baseline DTMP performance for incident MCI or dementia prediction and the generalizability of these findings to more diverse populations.
