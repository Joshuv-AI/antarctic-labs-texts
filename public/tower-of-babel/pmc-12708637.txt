Effects of feeding different baits on the growth of Scylla paramamosain megalopa and the bacterial community in a mariculture system
Authors: Zhiqiang Liu, Likun Xu, Guangde Qiao, Yuanyuan Li, Chunyan Ma, Fengying Zhang, Ming Zhao, Jiayuan Xu, Lingbo Ma, Keyi Ma, Wei Wang
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Bait feeding plays a pivotal role in the artificial nursery process, as different baits can significantly influence water quality and microbial community structure, thereby directly affecting the growth and development of cultured organisms. In this study, four types of baits, including live Artemia nauplii (AN), frozen copepods (FC), frozen Artemia nauplii (FA), and formulated diet (FD) were used to feed megalopa-stage mud crabs (Scylla paramamosain) until their metamorphosis into the first juveniles. The effects on water quality, microbial community structure, metamorphosis survival rate, and molting duration were statistically analyzed. Results indicated that the AN group had 88 unique bacteria OTUs, followed by 80, 64 and 27 unique OTUs in the FA, FD and FC groups, respectively. Proteobacteria predominated in both AN and FA groups, while Bacteroidetes demonstrated the highest percentage in the FC and FD groups. Actinobacteria maintained consistently low relative abundance in all treatments. The FA group exhibited the highest Chao1, ACE and observed richness indices (P > 0.05), suggesting the greatest bacterial community richness. The AN group displayed the highest Pielou’s evenness and Shannon index, along with the lowest Simpson′ index (D) (P < 0.05), indicating a more uniform species distribution and higher community diversity. In addition, the AN group exhibited the lowest concentrations of ammonia nitrogen and nitrite among the four treatments. Moreover, the AN group showed the highest abundance of the beneficial genus Marivita, whereas the FD, FC, and FA groups had a significant increase in the harmful genus Tenacibaculum. Compared with those in the FA, FC and FD groups, the megalopae in the AN group also demonstrated the highest metamorphosis survival rate and the shorter molting duration, underscoring the critical role of live Artemia nauplii in improving water quality, and stabilizing microbial communities. These findings suggested that live Artemia nauplii were the optimal feed choice for enhancing the success of S. paramamosain megalopa breeding. Future studies could explore the integration of appending probiotics and periodic removal of harmful bacteria in the aquaculture water to further optimize microbial structure, thereby improving the metamorphosis survival rate of S. paramamosain megalopa. Moreover, strategies such as partial substitution of live Artemia nauplii with frozen Artemia could be investigated to reduce seedling cultivation costs and advance S. paramamosain aquaculture technology in the further research.

Introduction

The mud crab (Scylla paramamosain) is a crucial species in marine aquaculture in China, prized for its flavorful and nutritious meat1. By 2022, the cultivated area of mud crabs in China has reached 24,170 hm2, with an annual output of 220,000 tons2. However, the artificial breeding technology for S. paramamosain has not yet fully matured, and there are still challenges in achieving large-scale and stable production. Environmental factors, such as bait composition, salinity, and water temperature, are critical determinants of the success of S. paramamosain artificial breeding. Studies have shown that bait composition, including protein and fat content, as well as the presence of additives like inositol affected the metamorphosis survival rate of megalopa3–5. Additionally, changes in temperature and salinity in the culture water also impacted their growth and development. For instance, the mud crab megalopa exhibited the highest survival rate at 24–28 °C, but the survival rate significantly declined below 20 °C or above 36 °C6. Similarly, a salinity of 25‰ was the optimal for megalopa, whereas extreme conditions (lower than 14‰ or higher than 39‰) could induce stress responses7.

The developmental stage of the mud crab megalopa is crucial for successful artificial breeding. After hatching, the mud crab larvae progress through five zoeal stages before metamorphosing into the megalopa, which undergo significant morphological changes5. Within approximately six to ten days, megalopae transition into juveniles, shifting from pelagic to benthic habit. During this stage, selecting appropriate bait and maintaining a stable environment are critical for ensuring successful metamorphosis. Although prior studies have examined the effects of temperature, salinity, and feeding regimes, the impact of different baits on microbial community composition in water during the stage of the megalopa culture remains unexplored. It is necessary to comprehend whether the microbial assemblages were affected with different baits.

Aquatic environment is a complex and delicate system, where various organisms interact to maintain ecological balance8,9. The bacterial community structure in water influences both water quality and the health of cultured organisms, making its study vital for the smooth progression of aquaculture10,11. Recent reports have highlighted the role of microbiota in aquaculture systems. For example, organic fertilization in rice-crayfish fields enhanced microbial diversity and ecosystem stability12. In addition, probiotics in California bass ponds improved water quality, and reduced the abundance of pathogenic bacteria13. Furthermore, the composition of the water microbiota significantly affected the gut microbiota of cultured organisms14, thereby modulating critical physiological processes such as growth, metabolism, and immunity15,16. In high-density larval aquaculture systems, infrequent water changes often lead to water quality deterioration, and result in large-scale larval mortality or even complete system failure. Therefore, selecting appropriate baits to maintain microbial diversity, abundance, and metabolic functions is essential for preserving ecological balance, preventing water quality deterioration, and reducing disease outbreaks.

In order to clarify the effects of baits on the growth of S. paramamosain megalopae and the structure of the microbial community in their mariculture systems, in this study, we collected water samples from megalopa stage of S. paramamosain fed with four types of baits, namely live Artemia nauplii (AN), frozen copepods (FC), frozen Artemia nauplii (FA), and formulated diet (FD). Using high-throughput 16 S rRNA sequencing technology, we analyzed the bacterial community structure after feeding with these different baits. Additionally, we monitored key water quality parameters and quantified the survival rate of mud crab megalopae during metamorphic molting. These data will provide new insights into optimizing bait selection during the megalopa breeding, and offer theoretical support for disease prevention and control as well as micro-ecological management strategies for S. paramamosain megalopa breeding.

Materials and methods

Experimental design and culture conditions

S. paramamosain megalopae used in this study were sourced from Ninghai Research Center of the East China Sea Fisheries Research Institute of the Chinese Academy of Fishery Sciences in Zhejiang Province. The experiment was conducted in the mariculture system using 400-liter hatchery buckets under controlled conditions in the research center. Four experimental groups were established and fed with live Artemia nauplii (AN), frozen copepod (FC), frozen Artemia nauplii (FA), and formulated diet (FD), respectively. Each treatment group consisted of three biological replicates, with 1,000 newly metamorphosed S. paramamosain megalopae stocked per replicate. The megalopa culture conditions were maintained as follows: natural lighting, water temperature of 26 (± 1) ℃, salinity of 25‰, continuous aeration throughout the culture period, and no water exchange during the experiment. Megalopae were fed twice daily at 06:00 and 13:00. The total wet mass of 1000 megalopae was approximately 6.6 g. Given that the active predation ability of megalopae is relatively limited, a sufficient amount of bait was supplied to all four experimental groups in this study. The amount of feed provided per feeding session was equivalent to 50% of the total biomass of megalopae (i.e., approximately 3.3 g). Accordingly, the baits for the AN, FC, and FA groups were prepared by removing excess water and then weighing to a wet mass of 3.3 g. While the formulated diet (FD group) was weighed directly to the same mass (3.3 g) without drainage.

Water quality analysis and sample collection

To assess water quality, ammonia nitrogen (NH3-N) and nitrite (NO2−) concentrations were measured in water samples collected daily from Day 1 to Day 12 (Fig. 1A). The concentration of NH3-N was determined using a commercial Water Ammonia Nitrogen Detection Kit (Shanxi Yirongdacheng Biotechnology Co., Ltd.) in accordance with the manufacturer’s protocol. Briefly, water samples were filtered through a 0.45 μm membrane filter to remove particulate matter prior to analysis. A 2 mL aliquot of the filtrate was then transferred to a 10 mm glass cuvette, followed by the sequential addition of kit reagents (e.g., sodium hypochlorite and salicylic acid) with thorough mixing after each step. The mixture was subsequent incubated at room temperature for 1 h to allow for complete color development. A calibration curve was prepared concurrently using ammonium chloride standard solutions at concentrations of 0, 0.25, 0.5, 0.75, 1.0, and 1.25 mg/L, which were treated identically to the samples. The absorbance of the resulting solution was measured at 697 nm using a spectrophotometer. Finally, the NH3-N concentration in the original water samples was determined by interpolation from the calibration curve.

The NO2− concentration in water samples was determined using a commercial Nitrite Content Detection Kit (Jiangsu Aidisheng Biotechnology Co., Ltd.) according to the manufacturer’s protocol. Briefly, water samples were centrifuged at 8000× g for 10 min at room temperature, and the resulting supernatant was collected for subsequent analysis. A 525 µL aliquot of the supernatant was transferred to a 1.5 mL centrifuge tube. Then, 175 µL each of kit reagents, namely sulfanilic acid and N-(1-naphthyl)ethylenediamine, were added sequentially. After thorough mixing, the mixture was incubated at room temperature for 15 min. The entire mixture was then transferred to a 10 mm glass cuvette, and its absorbance was measured at 540 nm. Blank and standard measurements were conducted concurrently. The blank was prepared using distilled water instead of the water sample, while the standard was prepared using a 1 mg/L sodium nitrite solution. Finally, the NO2− concentration (C) in the water sample was calculated according to the following formula: C = (Asample - Ablank) / (Astandard - Ablank) × Cstandard, where Cstandard is the concentration of the sodium nitrite standard (1 mg/L), and A represents the absorbance measured at 540 nm.

Due to the pronounced differences in morphology and size between the megalopa and the juvenile crab, as well as their distinct swimming behaviors, the metamorphosis from megalopa to juvenile crab is readily observable. In this study, the molting duration (days) was defined as the period from the initial emergence day of a juvenile crab metamorphosizing from the megalopa to the final day on which the last megalopa completed the molt within each experimental group. The survival rate was calculated as the final number of juvenile crabs / the initial number of megalopae (1000) × 100%. After metamorphosis, 2 L of water samples were collected from each replicate, filtered through sterile 0.22 μm membranes to capture and concentrate microbial biomass, and stored at -80 ℃ for subsequent genomic DNA isolation.

DNA extraction and PCR amplification

For genomic DNA extraction, bacterial cells were first released from the filtration membranes by vortexing in sterile phosphate-buffered saline (PBS) for 10 min. The resulting microbial suspension was then processed using the EZNA®soil Kit (Omega Bio-tek, USA) according to the manufacturer’s protocol. The quality of the isolated DNA was assessed with a NanoDrop 2000c spectrophotometer (Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA) based on absorption ratios of A260/280 and A260/230, and with 1% agarose gel electrophoresis. Subsequently, the V3-V4 hypervariable region of the 16 S rRNA gene was amplified using primer pair 341 F (5’-CCTAYGGGRBGCASCAG-3’) and 806R (5’-GGACTACNNGGGGTATCTAAT-3’). PCR was performed in a 20 µL reaction mixture containing 4 µL of 5× Fast Pfu Buffer, 2 µL of 2.5 mM dNTPs, 0.8 µL of each primer (5 µM), 0.4 µL of Fast Pfu Polymerase, and 10 ng of template DNA. Amplification was performed under the following thermocycling conditions: an initial denaturation at 94 °C for 10 min, followed by 30 cycles of 94 °C for 30 s, 55 °C for 30 s, and 72 °C for 50 s, and a final extension at 72 °C for 10 min. PCR products were purified using AxyPrep DNA Gel Extraction Kit (Axygen Biosciences, USA) following separation on 2% agarose gels.

Library construction and sequencing

To investigate the underlying mechanisms for water quality changes induced by different baits and their subsequent effects on megalopa development, we performed high-throughput 16 S rRNA sequencing of microbial community. Purified 16 S rRNA gene amplicons were accurately quantified using Qubit® 3.0 (Invitrogen, USA). Equimolar concentrations of all 12 barcoded amplicon samples were pooled to construct paired-end libraries following the Illumina Library Preparation Guide (Illumina, San Diego, USA). The final library was sequenced in PE250 mode using the Illumina platform according to the standard process.

Bioinformatic processing of sequencing data

Raw sequencing data were used to distinguish the samples according to the barcodes and primers at the beginning and end of the sequences. Briefly, data deblending was performed after data splitting with the following parameters: (1) Filtering of bases with a quality value of 20 or less at the end of the read, setting a window of 10 bp. (2) The maximum mismatch ratio allowed in the overlap region of the spliced sequence was 0.2. (3) According to the overlap relationship between PE reads, pairs of reads were spliced (merged) into one sequence with a minimum overlap length of 10 bp. Valid data were obtained after high-quality control and chimera removal. According to the different similarity levels, all the sequences in the valid data were categorized to obtain the operable taxonomic units (OTUs).

Chimeric sequences were identified and removed using UCHIME2 software (version 4.2 https://drive5.com/usearch/manual/uchime_algo.html), and sequences with 97% similarity were clustered into OTUs using UPARSE (version 7.1 http://drive5.com/uparse/). The RDP classifier (http://rdp.cme.msu.edu/) Bayesian algorithm was used. The representative sequences of OTUs were taxonomically analyzed against the Silva database with a confidence threshold of 0.7 to obtain species information for each OTU at each taxonomic level, from which the microbial community composition of each sample was counted at each taxonomic level.

Microbial community diversity analysis

Alpha diversity reflects the richness (species abundance) and evenness within a microbial community. In this study, the α diversity was assessed using six indices: Chao1, ACE, observed richness, Pielou′s evenness, Shannon, and Simpson′s (D)17. Specifically, Chao1 and ACE are nonparametric estimators of species richness, incorporating the number of rare species to predict true species richness. The formulas are Chao1 = Sobs + F12 / 2F2 and ACE = Sabund + Srare / Crare + (F1 / Crare)γ2, respectively (Sobs is the actual number of observed species; F1 and F2 represent the number of singleton and doubleton species; Srare and Sabund denote the number of rare and abundant species; Crare is the sample abundance coverage estimator; and γ² estimates the coefficient of variation for rare species.)18. Observed richness was the total number of species detected in each sample. Pielou′s evenness, Simpson′s index (D) and Shannon index are the indices of community evenness. Specifically, Pielou′s evenness (J) was measured using the formula J = H′ / ln(S), reflecting the balance of species distribution (H′ and S represent Shannon index and community species richness index, respectively)19. Simpson′s index (D) (D = ∑pi2) (a high value means low diversity and evenness) and Shannon index (H′ = – ∑pi ln pi) indices were used to assess community diversity (pi represents the relative abundance of species).

Principal coordinate analysis (PCoA) and non-metric multidimensional scaling (NMDS) were conducted to further investigate the β diversity of microorganisms. The PCoA was calculated by using three different distance matrices, namely Bray-Curtis distance (based on species abundance similarity), Unweighted UniFrac distance, and Weighted UniFrac distance (based on phylogenetic relationships and species abundance), to assess the differences in community composition between samples. The NMDS analysis employed the aforementioned three distance matrices was performed to evaluate the dissimilarities in species composition among samples. The goodness-of-fit for the NMDS ordination was assessed using Kruskal’s stress-1 value, with lower values (e.g., < 0.05) indicating a good representation of the distance matrix in the reduced dimensions. Usually, it generated axes by retaining the “distance” or “similarity” between samples, thus positioning similar samples in close proximity and disparate samples at a greater distance. Finally, the statistical significance of the observed grouping patterns in the β-diversity ordination was assessed using Analysis of Similarities (ANOSIM) based on the Bray-Curtis distance matrix with 999 permutations, to reveal the differences and dynamic changes in microbial community composition between the different groups20. Statistical significance was accepted at P < 0.05.

LEfSe analysis

LEfSe analysis (https://huttenhower.sph.harvard.edu/galaxy/) (Linear Discriminant Analysis Effect Size) was utilized to detect the major differential microbial taxa in water. Linear Discriminant Analysis (LDA) was applied to calculate the effect size (LDA score), with a size-effect threshold of 4.0 on the logarithmic LDA score to discriminate functional biomarkers, quantifying its contribution to the group differences. A higher LDA score indicated a stronger ability of the feature to discriminate between groups. The Kruskal-Wallis test of LEfSe was employed to identify features significantly associated with the different four groups.

Results

Effects of feeding different baits on Megalopa development

The developmental progression from megalopa to juvenile crab stage was systematically evaluated by monitoring three key parameters: (1) water quality dynamics, (2) molting duration, and (3) post-metamorphic survival rate (Fig. 1). The megalopa went through several days to metamorphose into the juvenile. Morphologically, the brachyurization was one of the notable features during this transition period (Fig. 1A). The results showed that juvenile crab was first observed in the AN group on Day 6 after feeding, while first juvenile appearance in the FA group initiated on Day 8. The FC and FD groups firstly exhibiting juvenile crab was on Day 9 and Day 10, respectively (Fig. 1B). Notably, the molting process in the AN group commenced earlier compared to both the FC and FD groups. Moreover, the AN and FA groups had the shortest molting duration, which only persisted for approximately two days. In contrast, the FC and FD groups exhibited significantly longer molting duration, which lasting for three days (P < 0.05). Additionally, the AN group had the highest survival rate compared to the other groups (P < 0.05), followed by the FA and FC groups, whereas the FD group showed the lowest survival rate (Fig. 1B). Figure 1C showed that the AN group exhibited the lowest ammonia nitrogen and nitrite levels, followed by the FC and FA groups, while the FD group had the highest levels of these compounds during the molting duration. Our data demonstrated that the utilization of different baits could result in variations in water quality, which in turn affected the molting duration and survival rate of the megalopa.

α/β diversity analysis

The sequencing yielded 444,838 high-quality sequences, resulting in 1,199 valid OTUs. These OTUs encompassed 23 phyla, 46 classes, 114 orders, 163 families, and 257 genera.

The results revealed that the bacterial communities exhibited variations among the groups, as evidenced by differences in indices reflecting community richness (Chao1, ACE, and observed richness), and community evenness (Pielou’s evenness, Shannon index, and Simpson’s index (D)) under different bait treatments (Fig. 2). The FA group exhibited the highest Chao1, ACE and observed richness indices (P > 0.05), suggesting the greatest bacterial community richness. The AN group displayed the highest Pielou’s evenness and Shannon index, along with the lowest Simpson′ index (D) (P < 0.05), indicating a more uniform species distribution and higher community diversity. The FC group showed intermediate Chao1 and ACE indices between the AN and FA groups, whereas the FD group exhibited the lowest values. Furthermore, the FC and FD groups had significantly lower richness, evenness, and Shannon indices, and a higher Simpson’s index (D) compared to the AN and FA groups. These findings demonstrated that feeding strategies significantly influence bacterial diversity in aquaculture water, with the FA group exhibiting the highest microbial richness, followed by the AN group, while the AN group exhibited microbial community diversity.

PCoA was employed to evaluate β-diversity patterns among the four groups’ water samples (Fig. 3A). The first principal coordinate (PC1) explained 66.36% of the total variance. Based on Bray-Curtis (36%), Unweighted UniFrac (35.81%), and Weighted UniFrac (89.51%) distance matrices, the AN group samples showed clear separation along both PC1 and PC2 axes compared to FA, FC, and FD groups. Conversely, FC and FD groups exhibited closer distances, indicating higher similarity in their microbial community structures. NMDS visualization in two-dimensional space further supported these patterns (Fig. 3B). The NMDS ordination showed excellent to good fit, with low stress values (Bray-Curtis: 0.000107; Unweighted UniFrac: 0.0806; and Weighted UniFrac: 0.0000696), indicating a reliable representation of the community dissimilarities. The results revealed that the AN group diverged markedly from FC and FD groups along the primary axis, while showing separation from FA group along the secondary axis of the variation. These NMDS results were consistent with the PCoA clustering patterns. Statistical testing using ANOSIM based on the Bray-Curtis distance matrix confirmed that the observed grouping pattern was significant (R = 1.000, P = 0.001). Collectively, both PCoA and NMDS analyses demonstrated notable divergence of AN group’s microbial community composition relative to the other three groups. Conversely, FC and FD groups maintained higher community similarity based on both analytical approaches.

Taxonomic composition analysis across phylogenetic levels

The relative abundances of the top 10 most represented taxa at phylum, family, and genus levels were analyzed separately (Fig. 4). At the phylum level (Fig. 4A), the four groups exhibited remarkably similar bacterial community structures, with Proteobacteria, Bacteroidota, and Actinobacteriota consistently emerging as the three most dominant phyla across all groups. However, there were differences in the proportion of the dominant bacterial communities. Proteobacteria predominated in both AN and FA groups, while Bacteroidetes demonstrated the highest percentage in the FC and FD groups. Actinobacteria maintained consistently low relative abundance in all groups. Family-level analysis revealed distinct distribution patterns (Fig. 4B). Rhodobacteraceae represented the most abundant family in AN and FA groups, followed by Flavobacteriaceae. It is noteworthy that Microbacteriaceae reached near-equivalent abundance to Flavobacteriaceae in the FA group. In contrast, FC and FD groups showed reversed patterns, with Flavobacteriaceae as the dominant family, followed by Rhodobacteraceae, Microbacteriaceae, and Saprospiraceae. Genus-level profiling demonstrated greater compositional complexity and group-specific variations in each group (Fig. 4C). The highest abundance genus in the AN group was Aestuariicoccus, followed by Rhodobacteraceae_Unclassified and Candidatus Aquiluna. In the FC group, the most prevalent taxa changed into Tenacibaculum, Nautella, and Roseibacterium. The FA group featured Tenacibaculum, Candidatus Aquiluna, and Roseibacterium as predominant genera. In the FD group, the higher prevalent genera were Tenacibaculum, Candidatus Aquiluna, and Phaeodactylibacter.

Identification of different bacterial taxa

A Venn diagram was constructed based on the OTU counts in the four groups (Fig. 5A). The results revealed that the AN group had 88 unique bacteria OTUs, followed by 80, 64 and 27 unique OTUs in the FA, FD and FC groups, respectively. Notably, the four groups shared 423 OTUs, accounting for 35.28% of total OTUs, indicating substantial inter-group community heterogeneity. To identify differentially abundant taxa, we performed LEfSe testing. The analysis revealed that there were 17, 3, 10 and 6 biomarkers in the AN, FC, FA and FD groups, respectively (Fig. 5B). As shown in Fig. 5B, the genus Tenacibaculum, class Bacteroidia and phylum Bacteroidota made a significant contribution to the FD group. While the top 3 featured bacterial taxa significantly contributed to the FA group were genus Roseibacterium, family Microbacteriaceae and order Mocrococcales. In the FC and AN group, the top significantly dominated bacteria was family Flavobacteriaceae and phylum Proteobacteria, respectively.

Discussion

Accumulating evidence has demonstrated the detrimental effects of ammonia nitrogen (NH3-N) and nitrite (NO2⁻) on larval stages of aquatic organisms, indicating these compounds exert substantial developmental toxicity during critical early life stages. As key aquatic pollutants, NH3-N and NO2⁻ have been documented to adversely affect multiple physiological parameters including growth performance, survival rates, and immune competence across diverse aquatic species21. In the Pacific white shrimp (Litopenaeus vannamei), exposure to NH3-N concentrations exceeding 0.5 mg/L resulted in significant growth retardation and markedly reduced survival rate in zoeal stages22. Zoeae of the blue crab (Callinectes sapidus) exhibited elevated oxidative stress marker and compromised survivability following combined NH3-N/NO2⁻ exposure23. Additionally, NH3-N/NO2⁻ exposure has been shown to impair growth, survival rate, behavioral response, and physiological adaptation in multiple amphibian species24. In light of these documented impacts, we systematically quantified temporal variations in NH3-N and NO2⁻ concentrations throughout the megalopa-to-juvenile transition in S. paramamosain under different baits. Our results revealed that the AN group maintained significantly lower NH3-N and NO2⁻ concentrations, concomitant with optimal performance of survival and molting rates. In contrast, the FD group displayed the highest levels of ammonia nitrogen and nitrite, correlating with poorest survival rate and significantly prolonged molting duration. It suggested that higher concentrations of ammonia nitrogen and nitrite in water negatively affected the development of the S. paramamosain megalopa.

Notably, our data revealed that diet-induced variations in NH3-N and NO2⁻ concentrations altered the microbial community structure in rearing water. Microbial community analysis demonstrated that the AN group had a more uniform species distribution and higher community diversity, with the dominant microbial categories being Proteobacteria, Bacteroidetes, and Actinobacteria, among which Proteobacteria abundance in AN group was significantly elevated compared to other groups. Proteobacteria is the dominant microbial group in the recirculating aquaculture system for the S. paramamosain25, as well as in the recirculating aquaculture system for many fish, such as turbot and grouper26,27. As a ubiquitous marine phylum, Proteobacteria contains numerous taxa involved in critical biogeochemical processes including water nitrogen removal, phosphorus removal, reduction of chemical oxygen demand (COD), and organic matter degradation28–31. This Proteobacteria-dominated consortium likely contributed to the observed nitrogen removal efficiency, thus explaining the lower NH3-N and NO2⁻ concentrations in the AN group. Additionally, the main dominant microbial taxa of all four groups were Proteobacteria and Bacteroidetes, which is consistent with the previous findings32. After feeding with the live Artemia nauplii, the megalopae exhibited higher survival rate, which could be related to the higher abundance of Proteobacteria in the cultured water, suggesting that Proteobacteria may contain more beneficial microorganisms33, thereby promoting the growth and development of the mud crab megalopae. Meanwhile, these findings further substantiated the significant impact of different feeding baits on the composition of microbial communities in aquaculture water.

Microbial community analysis identified Rhodobacteraceae as the predominant bacterial family in both AN and FA treatment groups. Rhodobacteraceae were known to perform crucial ecological functions, particularly in carbon fixation and sulfur oxidation processes. As the dominant intestinal microbiota component in L. vannamei, this family has been demonstrated to significantly correlate with shrimp health status34. Additionally, Flavobacteriaceae exhibited differential distribution patterns across treatment groups, showing moderate abundance in AN and FA groups, while reaching significantly higher levels in FC and FD groups. These two bacterial families represented key components of the Bacteroidetes phylum, which typically constituted 15–30% of total prokaryotic communities in marine ecosystems and demonstrated remarkable adaptability to various aquatic environments35. These microbial groups can decompose organic matter by producing extracellular enzymes, thereby removing excess nitrogen and other nutrients from aquatic systems36. Integrating the microbial community composition at the family level with measured NH3-N/NO2⁻ concentrations, our data suggested that Rhodobacteraceae likely plays a more significant role in nitrogenous compound degradation compared to Flavobacteriaceae in S. paramamosain megalopa rearing systems. Additionally, diversity analysis revealed significant associations between the microbial species richness and evenness and the observed variations in ammonia nitrogen and nitrite concentrations in this study. Consistent with previous findings, microbial diversity indices demonstrate strong positive correlations with both system productivity and environmental stability37. Consequently, the combination of enhanced microbial community diversity and elevated abundance of functionally important taxa in the AN group likely contributed to superior water quality maintenance and more efficient nitrogen removal compared to other groups.

Genus-level analysis revealed a more uniform microbial community structure in the AN group, where 12 bacterial genera exceeded 1% relative abundance. Marivita, an important genus within the Rhodobacteraceae family, was significantly more abundant in the AN group compared to other groups. This observation aligned with previous reports demonstrating Marivita’s probiotic potential in crustacean larviculture32,38. On the contrary, the genus Tenacibaculum exhibited significantly higher abundance in the FA, FC, and FD groups compared to the AN group. Tenacibaculum was a pathogenic bacterium that has been identified as one of the primary causative agents of Acanthaster planci skin rot disease39, and was highly harmful to fish and shellfish as well40. Recent studies confirmed Tenacibaculum as the etiological agent of black spot disease cases across penaeid shrimp, brachyuran crabs, and bivalve mollusks41. Therefore, the positive correlation between Tenacibaculum abundance and NH3-N/NO2⁻ concentrations suggested that elevated nitrogenous compounds may create favorable conditions for pathogen proliferation, potentially explaining the increased mortality rates of the S. paramamosain megalopa observed in FA, FC, and FD groups.

Aquatic microbial communities play a pivotal role in determining the quality and viability of aquaculture seedlings through multiple mechanisms42. Maintaining a stable and functionally diverse microbial consortium is essential for successful aquaculture operations, particularly during critical larval developmental stages42,43. Our results demonstrated significant diet-induced variations in both microbial community structure and nitrogenous compound concentrations during S. paramamosain’s megalopa-to-juvenile transition, which collectively explained the variance in metamorphic survival rate and molting duration. Therefore, a critical interdependence between microbial community structure, larval performance metrics, and nitrogenous compound concentrations in the cultured water could not be ignored during the S. paramamosain artificial seedling cultivation.

In addition, microbial community stability serves as a fundamental determinant of aquaculture system, with balanced consortia enhancing nutrient cycling and pathogen resistance13. In recent years, probiotics have been widely applied in aquaculture, and studies have shown that the addition of probiotics can alter the microbial community structure, thereby improving water quality and increasing farming success rate44. For example, adding Bacillus to shrimp ponds could significantly suppress pathogenic microorganism Vibrio, leading to improved shrimp yields45. Similarly, Probiotic amendment in Ctenopharyngodon idella aquaculture systems elevated α-diversity indices while enhancing organic matter decomposition efficiency44. Thereby, Marivita could be added as a potential probiotic in larval artificial cultivation of S. paramamosain.

Conclusions

In summary, this study systematically evaluated the tripartite relationship between water quality parameters, microbial community dynamics, and developmental performance during the critical transition from megalopa to first juvenile stage of S. paramamosain under four distinct baits. Live Artemia nauplii feeding established a more stable microecosystem compared to other treatments. The AN group demonstrated significantly higher microbial community diversity and Proteobacteria dominance, concomitant with the lowest recorded concentrations of NH3-N and NO2⁻ among all groups. The observed phenomenon could be explained by three sequential mechanisms: (1) the superior palatability of live Artemia nauplii minimized feed waste; (2) the consequent reduction in organic loading promoted microbial community diversity and stability; (3) these microbial improvements enhanced larval survival rate, creating a self-reinforcing cycle that maintained optimal rearing conditions. To improve the survival rate and shorten molt duration of the mud crab megalopa, future studies could explore the addition of probiotics such as Marivita or periodically remove harmful bacteria such as Tenacibaculum in cultured water. Furthermore, a cost-reduction strategy of partial replacement of Artemia nauplii with frozen Artemia could be investigated to maintain larval performance while improving economic viability of S. paramamosain artificial seedling cultivation.
