Survival as a source of confounding, selection bias, and effect modification
Authors: C Mary Schooling, Guoyi Yang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundSurvival is a long-standing source of confounding, selection bias, and effect modification that is often overlooked. Here, we describe how it can occur and when it can be averted.MethodsDirected acyclic graphs were used to illustrate how survival could cause confounding and selection bias. Selection diagrams were used to illustrate how survival could cause effect modification by factors such as age or health status.ResultsSurvival can be a confounder that biases towards favouring the exposure when exposure allocation starts or changes after recruitment. Survival can be a source of Type 1 selection bias that attenuates or reverses estimates when the exposure and outcome, or the exposure and a competing risk of the outcome, affect survival before recruitment. Confounding and selection bias by survival can largely be averted by ensuring exposure allocation coincides with recruitment. Given survival is usually most relevant to older or sicker people, confounding and selection bias due to survival are likely more evident with advancing age or poorer health status.ConclusionsConfounding by survival should be considered when exposure allocation starts or changes after recruitment. Type 1 selection bias from survival should be considered when the exposure starts before recruitment. Introduction

Increasing use of target trial emulation (hypothetical pragmatic randomized trial) [1] and quasi-experimental designs [2] has drawn attention to the importance of observational study designs that achieve exchangeability and thereby provide unbiased effect estimates. Correspondingly, operationalization of exchangeability as encompassing no confounding and no selection bias [3] has facilitated identification of sources of bias. Confounding typically arises from the existence of common causes of exposure and outcome [4]. Type 1 selection bias arises from restricting on a collider, and Type 2 selection bias arises from restricting on an effect modifier [5]. Sources of bias are usually identified from subject matter knowledge. Here, we consider the role of survival as a threat to internal validity from confounding or Type 1 selection bias and thereby as a source of apparent effect modification.

Survival has long been known as a potential source of bias in assessing the role of drugs or treatments [6], such as life-saving surgery. If recruitment occurs before surgery, those who do not survive to have surgery are inevitably unexposed and have a poor outcome, generating confounding by survival after recruitment, which typically exaggerates benefits. This bias is the classical immortal time bias [7]. Conversely, if recruitment occurs after surgery, those who died during surgery are missing from the study [6, 8], which generates Type 1 selection bias for the effect of the surgery on longer-term mortality [9, 10]. Correspondingly, survival has recently been identified as a source of bias arising from the misalignment of exposure allocation and recruitment in pharmaco-epidemiological studies [9, 10]. Specifically, survival after recruitment causes classical immortal time bias (described as confounding or misclassification), whereas survival to recruitment causes Type 1 selection bias [9, 10]. Here, we consider more generally where survival could be a source of bias (confounding or Type 1 selection bias) or of apparent effect modification in observational studies estimating the average treatment effect relative to the counterfactual selected sample [11].

Survival after recruitment as confounding

Confounding by survival after recruitment can occur when exposure allocation starts after recruitment or depends on events after recruitment, so differential survival after recruitment affects whether the exposure of interest is experienced or not and affects lifespan. We use two examples for illustration. First, consider an observational study to estimate the effect of initiating treatment on lifespan; then we consider another study to estimate the effect of smoking cessation on lifespan in smokers.

Figure 1.1a provides an illustration of confounding by survival after recruitment using a time-varying directed acyclic graph (DAG). Specifically, drug status at Time 1 (recruitment) affects drug status at Time 2 and death after Time 1 (Time 1+). Death after Time 1 (Time 1+) affects drug status at Time 2 because individuals have to remain alive to receive treatment at Time 2. Death after Time 1 (Time 1+) also affects death status after Time 2 (Time 2+) because individuals who die at Time 1+ are also dead at Time 2+. U (e.g. health status) is a common cause of death at Time 1+ and death at Time 2+, meaning that individuals who are alive at Time 1+ are more likely to remain alive at Time 2+. As such, the effect of drug allocation at Time 2 on death at Time 2+ is confounded by death at Time 1+, i.e. is confounded by survival between recruitment and potential allocation to the drug. Finally, additional sources of confounding could exist, which we have not shown to avoid distraction. Similar situations have also been described as misclassification of treatment allocation related to survival in the early follow-up period [10]. Specifically, ‘Immortal time arises for some treatment strategies because individuals can only be assigned to them if they survived the early follow-up period’ [10]. Correspondingly, a target trial abrogates this issue by requiring alignment of recruitment and exposure allocation [1]. For parsimony, we have described this situation as confounding by survival [9].

Similar pairs of time-varying DAGs and DAGs are given for the association of ex-smoking with death (Fig. 1.1b and 1.2b, respectively). Similar situations have also been described as misclassification of treatment allocation related to survival in the early follow-up period [10]. For example, years of life gained from smoking cessation could be a key motivator to quit smoking (Fig. 1.1b). However, the benefits might be overstated because ex-smokers had to survive their smoking to become ex-smokers, and those who have the ability to survive smoking are more likely to live longer, i.e. confounding by survival after recruitment (Fig. 1.2b).

Survival to recruitment as Type 1 selection bias

Survival to recruitment can generate selection bias when the exposure starts before recruitment, because both exposure and outcome can occur before recruitment, which creates a difference between the study sample and the target population, i.e. the counterfactual selected sample. To illustrate, Fig. 2.1a gives a time-varying DAG for selecting on survival of exposure and outcome to recruitment. Specifically, exposure at Time 0 can cause death at Time 0+ and exposure at Time 1. Similarly, outcome at Time 0+ can cause death at Time 0+ and outcome at Time 1+. U (e.g., health status) is a common cause of outcome at Time 0+ and outcome at Time 1+. Selection on death at Time 0+ creates an open backdoor path between exposure at Time 0 and outcome at Time 0+, and then between exposure at Time 1 and outcome at Time 1+. So, the association of the exposure at recruitment at Time 1 with the outcome at Time 1+ is biased by missing deaths from both exposure and outcome before recruitment. Figure 2.2a gives a simplified DAG showing that bias arises when exposure and outcome are selected on survival to recruitment. Selection bias from selection on exposure and outcome (Fig. 2.2a) is unrecoverable [12, 13] unless information sufficient to recover the association in the counterfactual selected sample from the factual selected sample is available [14]. Selection bias can also occur from selecting only on the outcome in the presence of other causes of the outcome [15].

Older people, by definition, are survivors of the birth cohorts who formed the relevant population. As a result, harmful long-term exposures in younger people, such as hypertension, adiposity, smoking, and alcohol use, may have unexpected associations in observational studies of older people, where apparently harmful exposures look neutral or protective [16–19]. In contrast, trials have increasingly shown benefits in older people of treating hypertension [20] and adiposity [21]. Ignoring this logical error means older people may be undertreated and under-advised.

Studies of exposures that affect survival to recruitment on outcomes open to competing risk before recruitment, due to common causes of outcome and survival to recruitment, such as socio-economic position (SEP), may also be biased towards the null or even reversed in observational studies [22]. To illustrate, Fig. 2.1b gives a time-varying DAG for selection on survival of exposure and of common causes (e.g. SEP) of outcome and survival to recruitment. Specifically, exposure at Time 0 can cause death at Time 0+, when SEP at Time 0 can also cause death at Time 0+. Such deaths prevent recruitment at Time 1 and hence observation of the outcome at Time 1+. So, the association of the exposure at Time 1 (recruitment) with the outcome at Time 1+ is biased by missing deaths from exposure and from SEP at Time 0+. Figure 2.2b gives a simplified DAG showing that given the exposure can cause death before recruitment, the existence of common causes of survival to recruitment and the outcome, here shown as SEP, creates selection bias due to a competing risk. For example, observationally lipids, proprotein convertase subtilisin/kexin type 9 inhibitors, and statins appear to have little association with stroke [23–25] when trials show they reduce stroke risk. The likely explanation is that heart disease tends to kill earlier in life than stroke [26], so observational studies of these drugs on stroke are missing the exposed who would have had a stroke but for their prior demise from heart disease, i.e. are biased by competing risk before recruitment due to exposure and due to common causes of survival and outcome, such as SEP. In contrast, trials allocate the intervention at recruitment, so they are only open to competing risk after recruitment. As a result of such competing risk due to exposure and common causes of survival and outcome, potential targets of intervention may be overlooked or not investigated in a timely fashion. Similar to studies in older people, studies in patients are also open to selection on survival to recruitment and thus may give attenuated or reversed estimates because of selecting on patient status [27, 28] and on surviving the exposure to recruitment.

Many other circumstances occur in which selection bias due to survival before recruitment can arise. For example, studies of birth weight on cancer recruited in adulthood are missing those who died of childhood cancer before recruitment, which is potentially related to birth weight [29]. Selection bias due to survival means that population representative samples can be biased most noticeably if death occurs before recruitment from exposure and outcome (Fig. 2.1a) or from exposure and common causes of survival to recruitment and outcome (Fig. 2.1b). These issues are compounded when studies are limited to patients because patients are survivors of their illness, hence the large biases seen in studies of patients [27]. Finally, we did not consider competing risk after recruitment because that has been considered in detail [30, 31].

Survival as a source of apparent effect modification by age or health status

Disease and death rates generally increase with age and ill-health. Confounding or selection bias due to survival before recruitment might be more evident in older and sicker populations because more people from the underlying birth cohorts who formed the population are missing due to prior death. Variables that differ in distribution between populations [32] can be depicted using a selection diagram [13, 33], i.e. DAGs with S-variables [13]. S-variables are indicated by a filled-in square atop the arrow shaft. ‘S-variables locate the mechanisms where structural discrepancies between the two domains [populations] are suspected to take place’ [34]. Selection diagrams illustrate why effect modification has occurred [33], whereas DAGs for Type 2 selection bias show selection on a specific instance of an effect modifier [5]. Specifically, DAGs usually depict causal structures in one population, whereas we are trying to explain why the magnitude of bias varies across populations. Here, different amounts of bias by survival might underlie different associations observed for older versus younger groups and for sicker versus healthier groups.

When exposure allocation occurs after recruitment, bias by survival after recruitment might be more evident when assessing effects on outcomes that affect survival in older or sicker populations than in young, healthy populations because fewer people survive to experience the exposure . As such, effect modification, where the estimates are more extreme with older age or ill-health, is a possible indicator of confounding by survival (Fig. 3a), although physiological reasons for effects varying by age could exist.

Survival as apparent effect modification by age and ill-health when the amount of bias from survival from (a) confounding and (b) selection bias varies with population age and ill health; a square box at the top of an arrow means that associations vary depending on the values of the selection factor, here population age and ill-health.

Selection into a study on survival to recruitment with respect to the exposure and outcome might also become more marked with increasing age and ill-health for long-term exposures that affect survival prior to recruitment, when the outcome also affects survival. Those remaining alive to be recruited represent more and more strongly selected survivors of their original birth cohorts, so estimates in older or sicker people can be attenuated or reversed by selection on survival to recruitment. As such, effect modification, where associations apparently become weaker or reverse with increasing age or with worsening health, may be a possible indicator of selection bias due to survival to recruitment (Fig. 3b). However, associations of harmless exposures with disease or death will be consistent with age [35] and likely health status, because selection only on outcome does not usually cause such substantial bias [36, 37].

Amelioration of bias from survival

Confounding due to survival

Randomized controlled trials, by definition, ensure that exposure allocation and recruitment co-occur and thereby avoid confounding by survival. By design, in a target trial, exposure allocation starts at recruitment; thus, an observational study emulating a target trial avoids confounding due to survival [1]; nevertheless, other sources of confounding would remain possible. It is important to specify Time 0 of follow-up (when outcome and intercurrent events start being measured) as the time of recruitment and exposure allocation and to capture all relevant events after recruitment. Use of instrumental variables is a key means of avoiding confounding by proxying the exposure with an event, such as a change in policy at a specific time or in a specific place or allocation to treatment within an area of randomness [38]. Mendelian randomization using genetic instruments should also abrogate confounding by survival after recruitment for the exposure because genetic attributes are randomly allocated at conception, i.e., before recruitment [38].

In observational studies where exposure allocation occurs after recruitment, adjustments need to be made for survival after recruitment, possibly using health status as a likely incomplete proxy of survival. Notably, in long-term cohorts, key exposures of interest varying over the lifetime will generate confounding by survival after recruitment because some exposures can only be experienced by survivors of the relevant prior exposure, such as becoming a long-term statin user after a heart attack, having an additional child, achieving intentional weight loss after being overweight, or becoming a widow(er) after being married. Study designs accounting for changes in exposure status during the study, such as marginal structure models [39] and G-estimation [40], assume no unmeasured confounding, so they need to measure and account for potential confounding by survival where relevant.

Selection bias due to survival

Randomized controlled trials, by definition, ensure that the intervention is unrelated to survival to recruitment. By design, a target trial with exposure allocation starting at recruitment excludes the possibility of selecting directly on exposure and outcome. However, an observational study emulating a target trial where a factor exists that affects exposure allocation and survival to recruitment when some potential recruits had died before recruitment would remain open to selection bias. Use of instrumental variables may or may not avoid selection bias depending on the instrument and outcome. Proxying the exposure with an event (change in policy by time or place) or location in an area of randomness should not induce selection on survival. However, genetically determined instruments, as used in Mendelian randomization, are lifelong, when people recruited in middle or old age are only the survivors of their genetic endowment and the outcome, or a competing risk of the outcome and thus are open to selection bias [41] due to selection on survival.

In observational studies, selecting on exposure and outcome occurring before recruitment (Fig. 2a) can be unrecoverable [42]. Such selection bias could be addressed by redesigning the study to ensure that the sample was representative of the underlying birth cohorts who formed the population. However, few such cohorts are available and are often limited in size and scope. Another approach is to use a study design where attributes of the long-lived are compared with those of young people [43], or by age at recruitment, because attributes only evident in young people may be those that preclude longevity [44]. However, when the only outcome is a measure of survival, effect sizes can vary by setting and over generations, recruitment needs to encompass the age range before many deaths from the exposure of interest have occurred to avoid selection bias, and bias from cohort effects is possible [44].

Apparent effect modification due to survival

In observational study designs, the amount of selection bias from survival of exposure and outcome or common causes of survival and outcome before recruitment, and the amount of confounding from surviving any waiting period before exposure allocation after recruitment, typically increases with increasing age or worsening health (Fig. 3a and b). Restricting the study to the strata least open to selection bias from survival, i.e. young and healthy people [45], might be helpful. Assessment of whether associations of exposures with major causes of death or all-cause mortality vary by age or health status might give an indication of whether estimates are open to selection bias from survival [45]. With increasing age or worsening health, associations being more extreme may indicate confounding by survival because fewer people survive after recruitment to receive the intervention, while associations being attenuated or reversed may indicate selection bias from selecting on survival, because more people have died of the exposure and of the outcome or a competing risk of the outcome before recruitment, i.e. a common cause of surviving to recruitment and the outcome. Nevertheless, we cannot exclude the possibility that any such variation is due to differences between study populations.

When selection on survival to recruitment is inevitable, estimates for a harmful or protective exposure on disease or death should attenuate or even reverse in older or sicker participants because they are survivors. Conversely, consistently observed associations by age or ill-heath at recruitment for long-term or life-long exposures with major causes of death or mortality suggest that the observed associations are more apparent than real.

Conclusions

Survival is an overlooked source of confounding, Type 1 selection bias, and apparent effect modification that can explain anomalies, such as classic immortal time bias, harmful exposures looking protective in older or sicker people, and associations changing with age or health status. In observational studies, confounding by survival after recruitment should be considered as a possible source of bias when exposure status is allocated or changed after recruitment. Selection bias due to survival to recruitment should be considered when an exposure affecting survival starts before recruitment, and the outcome also affects survival or is subject to a competing risk of survival. Effect estimates becoming more extreme with increasing age or with worsening health at recruitment are most parsimoniously explained as confounding by survival after recruitment. Effect estimates that attenuate or reverse with increasing age or with worsening health are most parsimoniously explained as selection bias from selecting on survival to recruitment. Consideration of the role of survival as an overlooked source of confounding, selection bias, and effect modification may inform the validity of findings from observational and quasi-experimental studies.
