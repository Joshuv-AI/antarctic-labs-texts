Global structural determinants of MASLD: Development-stratified pathways linking food systems, metabolic risk, and liver outcomes
Authors: James M. Paik, Markos Kalligeros, Shira Zelber-Sagi, Annette Paik, Saleh A. Alqahtani, Zobair M. Younossi
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Background & AimsStructural features of national food systems shape metabolic risk, yet their pathways to metabolic dysfunction-associated steatotic liver disease (MASLD) across development levels remain poorly characterized. We examined food-system pathways to MASLD prevalence and liver-related mortality across 204 countries.MethodsA cross-national ecological study harmonized data from the Global Burden of Disease study, NCD Risk Factor Collaboration, and FAOSTAT. Exposures were anchored to 2005–2011, metabolic mediators to 2013–2015, MASLD prevalence to 2019–2021, and liver mortality to 2023. Countries were stratified by Socio-Demographic Index (SDI; low <0.60 vs. high ≥0.60). Structural equation models estimated direct and indirect path associations among food-system characteristics, metabolic mediators, and liver outcomes.ResultsIn low-SDI countries (n = 101), urbanization was associated with higher ultra-processed food retail exposure (β = +0.41), which was associated with higher obesity prevalence (β = +0.17). Food price burden was inversely associated with caloric surplus (β = −0.44). Caloric surplus was associated with MASLD prevalence (β = +0.46). Political stability was inversely associated with MASLD prevalence (β = −0.31). In high-SDI countries (n = 103), ultra-processed food exposure was associated with higher sugar kilocalorie share (β = +0.35), which was associated with higher obesity (β = +0.54). Cereal kilocalorie share was independently associated with higher type 2 diabetes (T2D) prevalence (β = +0.17). T2D was the dominant structural correlate of MASLD (β = +0.80). Healthcare Access and Quality index was positively associated with MASLD prevalence (β = +0.28) and inversely associated with MASLD mortality (β = −0.33). Healthcare quality was inversely associated with hepatitis B and hepatitis C mortality in both strata, but not alcohol-related liver disease mortality.ConclusionsStructural pathways to MASLD differ by development level: ultra-processed food exposure and caloric surplus dominate in low-SDI settings, whereas T2D dominates in high-SDI settings. These findings identify development-specific intervention targets for reducing liver disease burden.Impact and implicationsFood-system structural characteristics including ultra-processed food retail exposure, dietary composition, and food price burden are differentially associated with MASLD prevalence through development-stratified pathways, providing a cross-national structural framework that extends beyond individual metabolic risk factors. These findings are relevant to global health researchers, epidemiologists, and policymakers working to understand and reduce rising liver disease burden in diverse economic settings. In low-SDI countries, efforts to monitor and moderate ultra-processed food retail expansion may warrant priority alongside caloric sufficiency programs; in high-SDI countries, structural findings reinforce the centrality of type 2 diabetes detection and management as a liver disease prevention strategy. Because this study is ecological and cross-sectional in design, associations reflect country-level structural patterns and should not be interpreted as individual-level causal effects; prospective and interventional research is needed to evaluate whether modifying these structural antecedents translates to reductions in MASLD burden.

Introduction

Metabolic dysfunction-associated steatotic liver disease (MASLD), formerly known as non-alcoholic fatty liver disease (NAFLD), affects approximately one in three adults and is closely intertwined with the global rise in obesity, diabetes, and metabolic risk factors.1,2 Although these direct metabolic drivers are well recognized, they arise within broader structural conditions, including food insecurity, food availability, affordability, and dietary environments.3,4 Over the past two decades, global dietary transitions have accelerated, driven by increased penetration of ultra-processed foods (UPFs), expanding commercial food systems, and growing dependence on imported processed ingredients.3,5 These shifts disproportionately affect lower-income populations and reshape dietary patterns in ways that may increase susceptibility to metabolic disorders.

Evidence from national surveys and multi-country analyses shows that UPF contributes to excess caloric intake, nutrient dilution, and metabolic imbalance across diverse settings.3,5,6 At the same time, food and nutrition insecurity remain persistent global challenges that influence diet quality and access to healthy foods, particularly in lower-resource contexts.7,8 Despite these patterns, little is known about how structural food-system characteristics such as food price burden, import dependency, and retail exposure to UPF relate to MASLD burden across countries at different levels of development.

To address this gap, we conducted a cross-national ecological study integrating harmonized data from 156 countries. Using structural equation modeling stratified by the Socio-Demographic Index (SDI), we identified context-specific pathways linking food affordability, food-system characteristics, metabolic mediators, and MASLD burden.

Materials and methods

Study design and data sources

This was a cross-national ecological study examining the relationship between national sociodemographic context, food environments, and the burden of MASLD. Country-level data were harmonized from the Global Burden of Disease (GBD) 2023 study,9 the Non-Communicable Diseases Risk Factor Collaboration (NCD-RisC),10 and the Food and Agriculture Organization Corporate Statistical Database (FAOSTAT) of the United Nations.11 FAOSTAT provides standardized global data on food balance, trade, supply, and food security indicators. Datasets were merged using ISO-3166-1 alpha-3 country codes to ensure consistent cross-country alignment.

Timing of measures

To maintain temporal ordering between structural exposures, metabolic mediators, and liver outcomes, variables were anchored to staggered measurement windows reflecting the hypothesized progression from upstream determinants to downstream disease burden (Table S1). Early structural determinants representing the broader development context, SDI and urbanization, were measured using baseline averages from 2005 to 2007. Food-system exposures, including food price indicators, food balance sheet variables, dietary adequacy indicators, and the UPF Availability and Exposure Index (UPAEI), were derived primarily from 2009 to 2011 to capture the structural food environment preceding changes in metabolic risk.

Intermediate metabolic mediators, including obesity prevalence, diabetes prevalence, and diabetes treatment prevalence, were measured for 2013–2015. MASLD prevalence was measured for 2019–2021, and MASLD-related liver mortality was measured for 2023 as the final downstream outcome. The Healthcare Access and Quality (HAQ) index was obtained for 2019.

Where possible, 3-year averages were used to stabilize estimates and reduce the influence of short-term fluctuations in national indicators. For indices available only for fixed years, the reported reference year was used. This staggered temporal structure allows structural food-system conditions to precede metabolic risk factors and liver disease outcomes by ∼10–18 years, consistent with the long latency between dietary exposures, metabolic disease development, and liver-related morbidity and mortality.

Outcomes

Primary outcomes of this analysis were age-standardized MASLD prevalence and MASLD-related liver mortality, derived from the GBD 2023 study.9 For comparison, age-standardized mortality rates from alcohol-related liver disease (ALD), HBV, and HCV were extracted from the same source. MASLD prevalence estimates represent 2019–2021 averages, and liver mortality estimates correspond to 2023.

Metabolic risk factors

National prevalences of obesity (BMI ≥30 kg/m2) and type 2 diabetes (T2D) from NCD-RisC, a pooled analysis of population-based measurements from health surveys and registries that provide harmonized national estimates over time.10 Estimates represent 2013–2015 averages. Where sex-specific estimates were available, sex-combined national values were computed by weighting male and female estimates by each country’s sex distribution.

Context and structural determinants

Socioeconomic development was measured using the GBD SDI (0–1), a composite of lag-distributed income per capita, mean educational attainment, and total fertility rate.9 Countries were stratified into low-SDI (SDI <0.60) and high-SDI (SDI ≥0.60) strata, consistent with GBD development classifications, to capture structural heterogeneity in food systems and health infrastructure. All SDI measures represent 2005–2007 averages to reflect baseline development context preceding food-system exposures. Health-system performance was characterized using the HAQ (0–100), obtained for 2019.11 Additional contextual indicators included urbanization (percent urban population) and political stability from the World Bank governance index, both measured as 2005–2007 averages.12

Affordability and food price environment

Food prices were described using the Consumer Food Price Index (CFPI), food price inflation, and the cost of a healthy diet (PPP-adjusted international dollars per person per day), all representing 2009–2011 averages.13 Affordability was proxied by the food price burden: Cost of a Healthy Diet divided by daily GDP per capita. The Processed Import-Dependency Index (PIDI) captured national reliance on imports of sugars, sweeteners, and vegetable oils, computed as calorie-weighted import shares (imports ÷ domestic supply) and rescaled to 0–100, representing the 2009–2011 average.14

Retail food environment

Exposure to ultra-processed food systems was summarized using the Ultra-Processed Agrifood Environment Index. The index was constructed by standardizing the ultra-processed share of dietary energy, agrifood greenhouse gas emissions, and urbanization, subtracting standardized protein supply as a protein-dilution correction, and rescaling the composite to 0–100, based on 2009–2011 data.15

Diet quantity and structure

Diet measures were derived from FAOSTAT food balance sheets, representing 2009–2011 3-year averages to reduce the influence of annual fluctuations.16 Dietary energy supply (kcal/person/day) and food adequacy (dietary supply ÷ requirement) were summarized as caloric surplus (adequacy - 100). Diet structure included cereal share of energy (% of total dietary energy [kcal/capita/day]) and sugar kilocalorie share (% of total of total dietary energy). Additional dietary indicators included protein and fat supply (g/person/day), palm oil share among vegetable oils (%), animal fat-to-vegetable oil ratio, and animal-source protein supply (g/person/day). The Unhealthy Diet Supply Index (UDSI) was constructed as the difference between the summed supply of obesogenic food groups (sugar, vegetable oils, animal fats, rice, and wheat) and the summed supply of protective food groups (fruits, vegetables, pulses, and fish), each expressed in kg/person/day. Higher UDSI values indicate a less healthy national food supply.

Food security

Prevalence of undernourishment and Food Insecurity Experience Scale (FIES) indicators, moderate or severe food insecurity and severe food insecurity, were obtained from FAO and represent 2009–2011 averages.17

Food-system environmental intensity

Agrifood-system greenhouse gas emissions were obtained from FAOSTAT and represent per capita CO2-equivalent estimates averaged over 2005–2007.18 Three indicators were included: pre- and post-production emissions (greenhouse gases from food processing, packaging, and transport beyond the farm gate); overall agrifood emissions (total per capita CO2-equivalent footprint across all food-system stages); and livestock-specific emissions (contributions from enteric fermentation, manure management, and feed production).

Fiscal policy indicators

Country-level fiscal policies were obtained from the WHO Global Health Observatory and the World Bank Global Sugar-Sweetened Beverage (SSB) Tax Database.19,20 The WHO database reports the proportion of retail price represented by excise taxes for beer, spirits, and SSBs (reference year 2023). Because policy data were not available for earlier periods, they were treated as indicators of recent fiscal environments. From the World Bank dataset,21 we identified whether an SSB tax had been implemented by 2021 and calculated policy duration as years between first implementation and 2021 (values after 2021 recoded as 0).

Statistical analysis

Country characteristics were summarized using means (SD) for continuous variables and frequencies (percentages) for categorical variables. Differences between SDI strata were assessed using Kruskal–Wallis tests for continuous variables and Pearson Χ2 tests for categorical variables. SDI strata were prespecified as low (SDI <0.60) and high (SDI ≥0.60), a threshold approximating the median of the 204-country analytic sample and corresponding to the boundary between low-middle and middle SDI development tiers in GBD classifications. MASLD prevalence tertiles were examined within each stratum using Kruskal–Wallis tests.

Structural equation models (SEMs) were estimated separately for each SDI stratum to evaluate associations linking upstream structural determinants, the food environment, dietary patterns, metabolic mediators, and MASLD prevalence. The conceptual framework was prespecified based on an a priori theoretical structure comprising five domains: upstream structural determinants (political stability, urbanization, and food price burden), the retail food environment (UPAEI and PIDI), dietary patterns (cereal kilocalorie share, sugar kilocalorie share, animal fat-to-vegetable oil ratio, and caloric surplus), metabolic mediators (obesity prevalence and T2D prevalence), and MASLD prevalence as the primary outcome (Table S1). The HAQ index entered the high-SDI model as a structural determinant given its hypothesized role in disease detection and metabolic risk management.

Model refinement was guided by evaluation of modification indices using Wald tests (removal criterion p >0.15) and Lagrange multiplier tests (addition criterion p <0.05), while maintaining theoretical consistency and avoiding redundant pathways. Final model specifications are provided in Tables S4 and S5.

Models were estimated using full-information maximum likelihood (FIML) under a missing-at-random assumption, allowing all available observations to contribute to estimation without listwise deletion. Standardized path coefficients (β) were obtained directly from the SEMs, representing associations scaled to each variable's standard deviation to facilitate comparison across pathways. Direct path coefficients reflect associations between two variables after accounting for other modeled pathways. Indirect associations represent the product of coefficients along mediated pathways, and total associations represent the sum of direct and indirect coefficients. Residual error covariances between outcome variables were specified to capture shared variance unexplained by modeled pathways, reflecting potential co-occurring or unmeasured etiological factors common to multiple liver disease outcomes.

Model fit was evaluated using the Comparative Fit Index (CFI), Standardized Root Mean Square Residual (SRMR), and Root Mean Square Error of Approximation (RMSEA). The low-SDI MASLD prevalence model demonstrated acceptable fit (CFI = 0.949, SRMR = 0.094, RMSEA = 0.091), whereas the high-SDI model showed poorer fit (CFI = 0.775, SRMR = 0.153, RMSEA = 0.191), reflecting greater heterogeneity in dietary patterns and food-system organization among high-income countries.

To contextualize MASLD mortality within the broader liver disease burden, a multi-outcome mortality model was estimated for each stratum, modeling MASLD, ALD, HCV, and HBV liver mortality as correlated outcomes. This reduced-form model included MASLD prevalence, HAQ index, political stability, diabetes treatment prevalence, and urbanization as predictors.

Dietary heterogeneity within high-SDI countries was explored using k-means clustering based on five dietary indicators (cereal kilocalorie share, sugar kilocalorie share, animal fat-to-vegetable oil ratio, UPAEI, and PIDI). Cluster characteristics were compared using Kruskal–Wallis tests; cluster-specific SEMs were not pursued because of limited sample size.

WHO excise-tax data were analyzed descriptively but excluded from SEMs owing to temporal misalignment with exposure windows.

Given the ecological cross-sectional design, associations reflect structural co-variation across countries rather than causal relationships at the individual level. All analyses were conducted in SAS Viya (SAS Institute Inc., Cary, NC, USA) using two-sided α = 0.05.

Results

Study sample and country characteristics

The analytic sample comprised 204 countries, stratified into low-SDI (n = 101) and high-SDI (n = 103) countries. High-SDI countries had markedly higher urbanization (70.4% vs. 42.1%), HAQ index (70.0 vs. 39.1), and political stability (0.43 vs. −0.55), alongside greater dietary energy availability (caloric surplus 28.2% vs. 12.2%), higher sugar kilocalorie share (12.2% vs. 8.2%), and lower cereal kilocalorie share (36.8% vs. 56.5%; all p <0.001). Despite these structural contrasts, MASLD prevalence (15.19% vs. 15.58%; p = 0.055) and T2D prevalence (12.4% vs. 11.9%; p = 0.473) was similar between strata. Diabetes treatment coverage differed substantially (49.3% vs. 27.8%; p <0.001). Viral hepatitis mortality was markedly higher in low-SDI countries (HBV: 12.75 vs. 4.29; HCV: 9.91 vs. 4.41 per 100,000; both p <0.001). Within each stratum, MASLD prevalence tertiles showed progressive gradients in obesity, diabetes, caloric surplus, and food-system exposures (Tables S2 and S3). In low-SDI countries, countries in the highest MASLD prevalence tertile had greater obesity (28.7% vs. 6.9%), higher diabetes prevalence (15.7% vs. 9.6%), higher sugar kilocalorie share (12.0% vs. 5.3%), and lower kilocalorie share (49.7% vs. 62.9%) than those in the lowest tertile (all p <0.001). In high-SDI countries, the highest MASLD prevalence tertile had markedly higher obesity (34.0% vs. 21.1%), higher diabetes prevalence (19.3% vs. 6.6%), higher food price burden, and greater palm oil dependence (25.7% vs. 5.3%) compared with the lowest tertile (all p <0.001). Notably, HAQ was inversely distributed across high-SDI MASLD prevalence tertiles in unadjusted comparisons (59.1 vs. 82.6; p <0.001), reflecting confounding by political stability and development level that the structural models address below.

Structural associations with MASLD prevalence in low-SDI countries

In low-SDI countries, a sequential food-system pathway linked upstream structural conditions to MASLD prevalence (Table 2 and Fig. 1). Food price burden was negatively associated with caloric surplus (β = −0.44; 95% CI, −0.63 to −0.26), which showed a positive direct association with MASLD prevalence (β = +0.46; 95% CI, 0.32–0.59). Caloric surplus also showed indirect associations through obesity (β = +0.14), yielding a total association of β = +0.60 (95% CI, 0.47–0.72). Structural associations with MASLD prevalence in high-SDI countries

The structural architecture differed markedly in high-SDI countries (Table 3 and Fig. 2). T2D prevalence was the dominant correlate of MASLD (direct β = +0.80; 95% CI, 0.68–0.92). Dietary exposures, including sugar kilocalorie share, cereal share, animal fat-to-vegetable oil ratio, and PIDI, were associated with MASLD primarily through indirect associations with obesity and diabetes rather than through direct pathways. The HAQ index showed a positive direct association with MASLD prevalence (β = +0.28; 95% CI, 0.11–0.46), consistent with a detection paradox in which countries with stronger health systems record higher MASLD prevalence through greater diagnostic capacity. A strong residual covariance between HAQ and T2D (r = −0.44; 95% CI, −0.60 to −0.28) indicated shared unmeasured structural determinants between health-system capacity and metabolic disease burden. Political stability remained inversely associated with MASLD (total β = −0.49; 95% CI, −0.64 to −0.34), with both direct and indirect components. Sensitivity analysis—dietary clustering within high-SDI countries

K-means cluster analysis identified two dietary clusters among high-SDI countries with complete dietary data (k = 2; cubic clustering criterion [CCC] = 3.74, Pseudo-F = 24.31; Table S6). Cluster 1 (n = 42; predominantly Middle Eastern, Central Asian, and Southern/Eastern European countries) was characterized by higher cereal share of energy than Cluster 2 (40.2% vs. 33.4% of total dietary energy) and lower sugar kilocalorie share (9.9% vs. 14.5%). Cluster 2 (n = 43; predominantly Nordic, Anglophone, and Latin American countries) was characterized by higher sugar kilocalorie share, animal fat-to-vegetable oil ratio, and ultra-processed food exposure. Despite the less overtly Western dietary profile of Cluster 1, MASLD prevalence was significantly higher in Cluster 1 than Cluster 2 (16,537 vs. 12,708 per 100,000; p = 0.010). Obesity and diabetes prevalences did not differ significantly between clusters (p = 0.093 and p = 0.137, respectively).

Discussion

In this global ecological analysis of 204 countries, we identified distinct structural food-system pathways to MASLD that differ systematically by development level. In low-SDI countries, urbanization was the dominant upstream driver, increasing UPF retail exposure, which was directly associated with higher obesity prevalence. Food price burden was inversely associated with caloric surplus, reflecting caloric constraint under food unaffordability. Both obesity and caloric surplus were associated with MASLD prevalence. In high-SDI countries, urbanization was no longer a significant upstream signal. UPF exposure increased sugar kilocalorie share, which drove obesity, while cereal kilocalorie share independently drove diabetes; T2D served as the dominant correlate of MASLD. These findings highlight that MASLD burden reflects structural conditions within national food systems operating over extended time horizons, and that different development contexts require different intervention approaches.

The inverse association between cereal share and obesity was the strongest individual structural path in the low-SDI model, consistent with evidence that staple-based dietary patterns characteristic of lower-income settings are associated with lower adiposity, and that displacement of cereals by energy-dense alternatives drives metabolic risk.3,22 Food price burden was inversely associated with caloric surplus in low-SDI countries, indicating that under conditions of food unaffordability, overall caloric intake is constrained rather than redirected toward cheaper energy-dense substitutes. This caloric constraint attenuates the obesity pathway but does not eliminate MASLD risk, as UPF exposure independently drove obesity through direct market penetration rather than through dietary quality markers.7,8 These structural food-system pathways are consistent with evidence linking food insecurity, food environment quality, and MASLD outcomes at the community level.4,7,8,17,23

UPF environment exposure, operationalized using the UPAEI, contributed to MASLD burden through distinct mechanisms in each stratum. In low-SDI countries, UPAEI showed a direct positive association with obesity, consistent with evidence that the rapid expansion of UPF markets in lower-income settings increases population exposure to energy-dense, nutrient-poor products that directly drive adiposity.3,5,6 In high-SDI countries, UPAEI operated upstream through dietary quality markers, particularly sugar kilocalorie share, rather than directly on obesity, consistent with a more mature UPF environment in which dietary composition has already been substantially reshaped and metabolic consequences are mediated through established disease pathways.3,15 This stratum contrast reflects the stage of nutrition transition: in lower-income settings, UPF penetration is still reshaping eating patterns and body weight directly, whereas in higher-income settings, dietary transition is more complete and the metabolic sequelae operate through downstream pathways to diabetes and liver disease.

In high-SDI countries, T2D was the dominant correlate of MASLD, with dietary exposures operating primarily through indirect metabolic pathways. The absence of a significant direct obesity-to-MASLD path in this stratum reflects mediation: once diabetes is specified in the model, it captures the obesity-to-MASLD relationship, consistent with mechanistic evidence that obesity promotes MASLD primarily through insulin resistance and hepatic glucose dysregulation rather than through adiposity alone.24 The total association of obesity with MASLD remained substantial, operating through diabetes rather than directly. This pattern is consistent with evidence that among individuals with T2D, MASLD prevalence and liver-related complications are markedly elevated relative to metabolically healthy populations.25 Notably, despite these divergent direct pathways, total associations between upstream food-system exposures and MASLD were broadly similar in magnitude across low- and high-SDI strata. This convergence indicates that structurally distinct mechanisms produce comparable aggregate MASLD burden at the population level, reinforcing the interpretation that these represent development-specific expressions of a shared global dietary transition.

In descriptive analyses within the high-SDI stratum, palm oil share of vegetable oils increased progressively across MASLD prevalence tertiles, a pattern not observed in low-SDI countries. Although palm oil share was not included in structural models because of incomplete data coverage, this signal is consistent with experimental evidence linking saturated and palm-derived fatty acids to hepatic lipid accumulation, increased de novo lipogenesis, and steatosis.26 Palm oil, used by the food industry, is the most widely consumed vegetable oil globally, and its share within high-SDI food supplies varies substantially across regions, being particularly prominent in Middle Eastern and Southeast Asian countries, which also carry disproportionate MASLD burden in our sample.11 These ecological findings cannot establish whether palm oil intake directly increases MASLD risk or whether the observed association is explained by its role in increasing total caloric intake, promoting obesity, and driving insulin resistance. Prospective studies with individual-level dietary data are needed to clarify this relationship.

Political stability showed independent inverse associations with MASLD prevalence in both strata, operating through multiple structural pathways including food price burden, UPF environment, and diabetes prevalence, as well as through a direct governance pathway. This finding is consistent with evidence that political instability disrupts food supply chains, reduces investment in public health infrastructure, and amplifies population vulnerability to food price volatility, all of which compound metabolic risk over time.7,27 The positive association between political stability and MASLD mortality in both strata reflects the same detection dynamic observed for HAQ: more stable countries with stronger vital registration systems more completely capture and attribute liver-related deaths. Overall, governance quality emerges as a structural determinant of both MASLD burden and its epidemiological visibility, an upstream factor that shapes not only population metabolic risk, but also the measurability of that risk.12,28

The positive association between HAQ index and MASLD prevalence in high-SDI countries may reflect a detection paradox: stronger health systems identify more MASLD through systematic metabolic screening and diagnostic capacity rather than representing a true adverse relationship. This interpretation is supported by the contrasting direction of HAQ's association with MASLD mortality in the same stratum, where healthcare quality was inversely associated with liver-related deaths. These findings together suggest that surveillance infrastructure simultaneously records more prevalent disease and more effectively prevents its progression to death. An important implication is that GBD prevalence estimates in low-SDI settings likely underestimate true MASLD burden because of limited diagnostic capacity, and the similar crude prevalence observed across strata of ∼15%, therefore likely reflects a detection gradient rather than true epidemiological equivalence.28 The young median age in low-SDI countries (22.3 vs. 37.4 years in high-SDI countries) reflects earlier demographic transition rather than a uniformly young adult population, and GBD age-standardized rates were used to enable valid cross-national comparison independent of age structure. Nonetheless, MASLD burden in younger low-SDI populations likely underestimates future disease trajectory, as these populations will age over coming decades in the context of ongoing dietary transition and rising obesity.

Across both strata, MASLD prevalence was the strongest correlate of MASLD mortality, confirming that higher disease burden translates proportionally into higher liver-related deaths. Healthcare quality modified this relationship in high-SDI settings but not in low-SDI settings, indicating that treatment capacity and metabolic management infrastructure must exist for HAQ to reduce mortality. These findings extend evidence linking healthcare access deficits and preventable hospitalizations to higher premature MASLD mortality at the community level.29 In contrast, HAQ was consistently and inversely associated with HBV and HCV mortality across both strata, reflecting the well-established responsiveness of viral hepatitis to vaccination, antiviral therapy, and acute care. HAQ showed no significant association with ALD mortality in either stratum, suggesting that ALD mortality is less responsive to general healthcare quality improvements and more influenced by structural and cultural determinants of alcohol consumption. In high-SDI countries, urbanization was inversely associated with ALD mortality, possibly reflecting harm-reduction infrastructure concentrated in urban settings.30

Substantial residual error covariances between MASLD and ALD mortality and between HCV and HBV mortality indicate shared variance unexplained by modeled pathways. GBD methodology assigns liver disease deaths to mutually exclusive etiological categories, meaning co-existing pathologies, including metabolic dysfunction and alcohol-associated liver disease (MetALD) and concurrent MASLD-HCV are not captured as such in our outcome data.9 These residual covariances statistically represent this shared burden without attributing it to specific co-pathologies. Future analyses using data systems that permit coding of co-existing liver conditions will be needed to quantify the extent of MetALD and MASLD-viral hepatitis overlap at the population level.

The k-means sensitivity analysis confirmed two empirically distinct dietary clusters within the high-SDI stratum. Cluster 1 (predominantly Middle Eastern, Central Asian, and Southern/Eastern European countries) was characterized by higher cereal kilocalorie and lower sugar kilocalorie share, reflecting a dietary supply dominated by refined cereal staples including white rice and refined wheat. Cluster 2 (predominantly Nordic, Anglophone, and Latin American countries) was characterized by higher sugar kilocalorie share, animal fat-to-vegetable oil ratio, and UPF exposure, reflecting a more overtly Western dietary profile. Despite the less Western dietary profile of Cluster 1, MASLD prevalence was significantly higher in Cluster 1 than Cluster 2. Obesity and diabetes prevalences did not differ significantly between, indicating that dietary pattern composition alone does not determine MASLD burden in high-SDI settings. The higher MASLD prevalence in Cluster 1 despite similar obesity and diabetes prevalences between clusters suggests that factors beyond dietary composition and measured metabolic mediators may contribute to between-country variation in MASLD burden within the high-SDI stratum, including population differences in genetic susceptibility or unmeasured dietary quality markers not captured by the clustering variables. These findings reinforce that T2D, regardless of its dietary antecedents, is the proximate structural correlate of MASLD in high-SDI settings, and that ecological dietary patterns alone are insufficient to explain the full distribution of MASLD burden across high-SDI countries.24,25 Population-level variation in genetic susceptibility, including PNPLA3 I148M and HSD17B13 variants that differ in frequency across ancestry groups and may modify the relationship between metabolic exposures and hepatic steatosis, represents an additional source of between-country heterogeneity that ecological analyses cannot account for.31,32

In both strata, T2D prevalence increased progressively across MASLD prevalence tertiles, consistent with the central role of diabetes in the structural models. In high-SDI countries, diabetes treatment coverage followed an inverse pattern, with the highest MASLD tertile showing lower treatment coverage than the lowest tertile, co-occurring with lower HAQ and lower SDI in these countries. This descriptive pattern reflects the development gradient within the high-SDI stratum rather than a causal protective effect of treatment, as countries with the lowest MASLD burden also had the lowest underlying diabetes prevalence. Nonetheless, the co-occurrence of high diabetes burden and low treatment coverage in countries with the greatest MASLD burden identifies inadequate metabolic management as a potentially modifiable structural feature of high-SDI countries at the lower end of the development spectrum, and supports prospective evaluation of diabetes treatment coverage as a lever for reducing MASLD burden at the population level.25

Although fiscal policies such as the SSB taxation were evaluated, available tax data were limited and post-dated our outcome year, with tax-share information available only for 2023. SSB tax duration showed no significant associations with dietary or metabolic pathways in structural models. Longitudinal evaluations with adequate follow-up are needed to assess the population impact and lag effects of fiscal interventions on metabolic and liver outcomes.19,20

Limitations

This study has several limitations. First, as an ecological cross-sectional analysis, associations reflect structural co-variation across countries and should not be interpreted as causal relationships at the individual level. Second, GBD prevalence and mortality estimates are modeled outputs subject to methodological assumptions and reporting heterogeneity, with likely systematic underestimation of MASLD burden in low-SDI settings owing to limited diagnostic capacity. Third, although the staggered temporal structure was designed to approximate biological progression from food-system conditions to liver outcomes, the exact latency is uncertain and our framework represents a structural approximation rather than a precisely calibrated causal lag. Fourth, the high-SDI model showed moderate fit, reflecting dietary pattern heterogeneity within this stratum, and parameter estimates should be interpreted as average pathway associations across diverse country contexts. Fifth, unmeasured confounders including population-level genetic susceptibility variants such as PNPLA3 I148M and HSD17B13, cultural determinants of alcohol consumption, and within-country dietary variation may influence observed associations. Sixth, GBD assigns liver disease deaths to mutually exclusive etiological categories, precluding analysis of co-existing pathologies including MetALD. Seventh, pandemic-related changes in food access, physical activity, alcohol consumption, and healthcare utilization may have influenced the 2019–2021 prevalence and 2023 mortality estimates in ways not captured by pre-pandemic exposure variables.

Conclusions

Structural pathways to MASLD differ by development level. In low-SDI settings, UPF exposure and caloric surplus were the dominant upstream correlates, operating through obesity. In high-SDI settings, T2D was the dominant structural correlate, with dietary exposures operating through metabolic mediation. Despite divergent pathways, total food-system associations with MASLD were comparable across strata. These findings extend evidence on structural determinants of MASLD and identify development-specific intervention targets for reducing population-level liver disease burden.

These findings identify development-specific structural targets for MASLD prevention. In low-SDI settings, limiting urbanization-driven UPF exposure represents the most modifiable upstream structural target for reducing obesity and MASLD burden. In high-SDI settings, diabetes detection and management represents a higher-yield population-level strategy than dietary modification alone.25 Across all settings, investment in health-system infrastructure for metabolic disease surveillance and treatment represents a modifiable structural target for reducing preventable liver-related mortality globally.12,27,28

Data availability

All data used in this study were publicly available, aggregated, and fully de-identified. Metabolic risk factor estimates were accessed through the NCD Risk Factor Collaboration (NCD-RisC).
