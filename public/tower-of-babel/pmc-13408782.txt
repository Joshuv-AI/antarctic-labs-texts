Rediscovery and epitypification of Resupinatus merulioides (Resupinataceae, Agaricales) from its type locality in Japan
Authors: Yuki Kurosaki, Kentaro Hosaka
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractResupinatus merulioides was originally described from Nikko, Tochigi Prefecture, Japan and characterized by its merulioid hymenophore. Since its description, no additional records have been reported from the type locality, and only a few records have been documented from other regions in Japan. Here, we report some new rediscoveries of R. merulioides from its type locality and other regions in Japan. Newly collected specimens were morphologically consistent with the original description. However, some fruiting bodies had different morphological characteristics such as variations in coloration. Molecular phylogenetic analyses revealed that Japanese specimens identified as R. merulioides formed a well-supported clade, and distinct from other species in this genus. Additionally, specimens labeled as R. merulioides from New Zealand formed a sister clade to R. merulioides s.s. and were genetically distinct. Because of newly observed morphological characters and DNA data, we designate an epitype based on the newly collected specimen from its type locality.Keywords: epitype, ITS, New Zealand, phylogeny, Tochigi

Resupinatus merulioides Redhead & Nagas. was described from Japan as an exceptional species within Resupinatus (Resupinataceae, Agaricales) characterized by a merulioid hymenophore (Redhead & Nagasawa, 1987; Vizzini et al., 2024), whereas most species in this genus have a lamellate hymenophore. However, in the original description, only the holotype was assessed, and no additional collection was recorded from type locality since then. Therefore, it remains unclear whether the original description adequately reflects the intraspecific variation of R. merulioides. More importantly, although specimens identified as R. merulioides have also been reported from New Zealand (Cooper, 2012), and several morphologically similar species have been described elsewhere, no Japanese specimen of R. merulioides was included in previous phylogenetic analyses (Bijeesh et al., 2020; Thorn et al., 2005) due to the lack of DNA sequence data from type specimens. The authors conducted fieldwork across Japan and collected specimens authentically identified as R. merulioides from its type locality and other sites. However, some fruiting bodies had different morphological characteristics from the original description. In this paper, we report newly observed characters of R. merulioides and infer its phylogenetic position in this genus.

Fresh materials were used to observe macroscopic characters and to establish cultures. Thereafter, they were dried using a food dehydrator at 35 °C with good air circulation, then specimens were deposited in the mycological herbarium of the National Museum of Nature and Science, Tsukuba, Japan (TNS). In addition, we examined one specimen identified as R. merulioides collected in Gunma Prefecture (Yamamoto & Ohmae, 2024) stored in the mycological herbarium of the Tochigi Prefectural Museum (TPM).

To establish cultures, one basidiocarp was attached under the lid of petri dish to facilitate basidiospore discharge on potato dextrose agar (PDA; Shimadzu, Tokyo, Japan) plate and stored at room temperature. After spore germination, germinated hyphae were transferred to PDA slants to establish isolates. The ex-epitype isolate was deposited in the NITE Biological Resource Center (NBRC), Kisarazu, Japan under the catalog number NBRC 00117583. Micromorphology was observed from dried specimen mounted in 3% (w/v) KOH with Congo red, using a BX53 microscope (Olympus, Tokyo, Japan) under Nomarski differential interference contrast, and microphotographs were captured with a TS-2500 camera (Sato Shouji, Kanagawa, Japan). To check the basidiospore's amyloid reaction, Melzer's reagent (MLZ) was used. Basidiospores were measured under 1000× magnification in Melzer's reagent and given as: range of length and width (mean ± standard deviation); variation of Q (mean ± standard deviation). Maximum and minimum values were also provided with numbers in parentheses. Q means length/width ratio of the basidiospore.

To extract DNA, the mycelia were cultivated on 2% (w/v) malt extract broth (BactoTM Malt Extract; Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA) at room temperature. Cultivated mycelia were frozen at -20 °C overnight, freeze-dried using a Bulk Tray Dryer (Labconco, Kansas, USA), and pulverized by bead beating with a TissueLyser (Qiagen, Hilden, Germany) according to the manufacturer's instructions. DNA was extracted by a modified CTAB extraction followed by glass milk purification methods as explained by Hosaka and Nam (2023). When isolates were not available, DNA was extracted from a piece of freeze-dried fruiting body using the same method as above.

To perform molecular phylogenetic analyses, the internal transcribed spacer (ITS) and large subunit (LSU) region of the nuclear ribosomal DNA were used. PCR reactions were performed following Hosaka et al. (2025). Primer pairs used were ITS5-ITS4 (White et al., 1990) for the ITS and LR0R-LR5 (Cubeta et al., 1991; Vilgalys & Hester, 1990) for the LSU region. When appropriate PCR products were not obtained, a newly designed primer, ITS2-res1 (5’ CCGAAGGGTATGCCTGTTTG 3’), located upstream of the ITS2 region and specific to the genus Resupinatus, was used with ITS4 to amplify a partial ITS region of ca. 250 bp.

PCR products were checked in 1.5% agarose gels stained with Midori Green (NIPPON Genetics EUROPE, Dueren, Germany) and visualized under UV light (Lti-ExLB; BioTools, Gunma, Japan). Purification of PCR products, cycle sequence reactions, and sequencing were performed according to Hosaka et al. (2025). The obtained raw sequences were assembled and edited using ATGC ver. 7.1.0 (GENETYX Corporation, Tokyo, Japan) and registered to International Nucleotide Sequence Databases through DNA Data Bank of Japan (DDBJ; https://www.ddbj.nig.ac.jp/ddbj/index-e.html).

In addition to the newly obtained sequences of R. merulioides, a total of 39 sequences from 23 specimens of Resupinatus (Table 1) were retrieved from INSD and added to the dataset. Pleurotus djamor (Rumph. ex Fr.) Boedijn and P. ostreatus (Jacq.) P. Kumm. were selected as the outgroup based on the phylogenetic analyses of the previous study (Consiglio & Setti, 2018). The obtained sequences were aligned using MAFFT ver. 7 (Katoh & Standley, 2013) with the L-INS-i strategy and ambiguous region were removed using trimAl ver.1.2 (Capella-Gutiérrez et al., 2009) under a gappyout option. Preliminary analyses were conducted for each dataset (ITS or LSU) using the neighbor-joining (NJ) method implemented in MEGA-X (Kumar et al., 2018) with 500 bootstrap replicates. Although some discordance was observed in interspecific relationships between the ITS and LSU trees, which may be attributed to long-branch attraction or differences in taxon sampling, no significant conflict was detected between the overall topologies of the ITS and LSU datasets. Consequently, the two datasets were combined for subsequent analyses.

Notes: According to the original description of R. merulioides, the marginal hyphae of cheilocystidia were aculeate or tubular (Redhead & Nagasawa, 1987). In the newly collected samples, they were also aculeate or tubular, but some showed swellings at the tip (Figs. 1J, K, 2E). However, these swellings tend to disappear depending on the condition of the specimen and cannot consistently be observed in all specimens. Other macro- and micromorphological characteristics matched those described in Redhead and Nagasawa (1987); thus, the new samples were clearly identified as R. merulioides.

In Japan, an undescribed species of Resupinatus with merulioid hymenophore was listed in Aoki (2008) under a tentative Japanese name “Tyatizimitake”. Although it is nearly identical to R. merulioides, its conspecificity remains unclear (Redhead & Nagasawa, 1987). “Tyatizimitake” differs by having fewer tibiiform ends at the terminal hyphae of the pileipellis and brownish fruiting bodies, in contrast to the grayish ones of R. merulioides (Aoki, 2008; Redhead & Nagasawa, 1987). However, in the present study, some newly collected fruiting bodies of R. merulioides were brown (Fig. 1E), and the number of tibiiform ends varied among specimens. Therefore, these differences appear to represent intraspecific variation, and the two taxa should be regarded as conspecific.

Resupinatus merulioides resembles R. odoratus C.K. Pradeep, C. Bijeesh & A.M. Kumar, recently described from India, in having a non-gilled hymenophore, but differs in having smaller basidiospores and smaller fruiting bodies (Bijeesh et al., 2020). Molecular phylogenetic analyses showed that Japanese specimens of R. merulioides formed a well-supported clade with very short internal branch lengths and were phylogenetically distant from R. odoratus (Fig. 3). R. merulioides also resembles R. porosus M.P. Martín, Lodge & Thorn from Puerto Rico and R. vinosolividus (Segedin) J.A. Cooper from New Zealand in having a non-lamellate hymenophore, but it can be distinguished from R. porosus by the latter's poroid hymenophore (Thorn et al., 2005) and from R. vinosolividus, which has a simple intervenose hymenophore and ellipsoid basidiospores (Segedin, 1993). Moreover, these species were not grouped with R. merulioides in the present phylogenetic analyses (Fig. 3).

The holotype of R. merulioides was reported from Zelkova (Redhead & Nagasawa, 1987), whereas the new samples were collected from Acer pictum and an unidentified hardwood (neither Zelkova nor Acer). These results suggest that the host range of R. merulioides is broader than previously recognized.

In the original description, the type locality of R. merulioides was given as Gunma Prefecture. However, the Kanaya Hotel garden at Lake Chuzenji, the actual collection site, is located in Tochigi Prefecture. The type locality should therefore be corrected to Tochigi Prefecture. In Japan, R. merulioides has also been reported from Gunma Prefecture (Yamamoto & Ohmae, 2024) and Ehime Prefecture of the Shikoku region (Okino, 1999; this study). Furthermore, molecular phylogenetic analyses demonstrated that sequences from China obtained from GenBank formed a well-supported clade with the Japanese sequences (Fig. 3), indicating that R. merulioides also occurs in China. These results therefore suggest that the geographic distribution of R. merulioides is broader than previously recognized.

Some specimens identified as R. merulioides have also been reported from New Zealand and their morphology matches the original description of the Japanese samples (Cooper, 2012). In the phylogenetic analyses, the New Zealand sequences formed a sister clade to the Japanese-Chinese clade but were genetically distinct (less than 98.2% ITS sequence similarity). In this study, we treated this clade as a species distinct from R. merulioides s.s. However, further studies are needed to determine whether the New Zealand specimens represent a species distinct from R. merulioides s.s.

Although the original description of R. merulioides is thorough and detailed, we argue that epitypification is necessary. The absence of DNA data from the original type material represents the lack of an essential character for the precise application of the name (Cruz et al., 2018). In the case of R. merulioides, DNA sequences from the holotype could not be successfully obtained (Thorn, personal communication) and the isotype specimen could not be located in the Tottori Mycological Institute, Tottori, Japan (TMI) (Ushijima, personal letter). Furthermore, the newly collected specimens exhibit morphological features not reported in the original description, resulting in an expansion of the diagnostic characters of the species. This complicates the confident assessment of conspecificity between the newly collected specimens and the original type material. Therefore, designating an epitype supported by newly observed morphological characters and DNA sequence data is necessary to avoid future taxonomic confusion.
