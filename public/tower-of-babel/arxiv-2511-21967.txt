Lindblad Quantum Dynamics as Euler–Poincaré Reduction on Adjoint–Coupled Semidirect Products
Leonardo J. Colombo

Abstract
We present a geometric and variational derivation of the Gorini–Kossakowski–Sudarshan–Lindblad (GKSL) equation based on a Lie–theoretic construction that we call the adjoint–coupled semidirect product (ACSP). An ACSP consists of a Lie group G G acting on a representation space V V through (possibly several copies of) the adjoint action, together with a second, adjointly compatible action whose failure to commute defines an adjoint torsion K ⁡ ( ξ , v ) = ξ ⋅ v − v ⋅ ξ K(\xi,v)=\xi\cdot v-v\cdot\xi . This torsion induces a canonical quadratic curvature operator on 𝔤 ∗ \mathfrak{g}^{*} that survives Euler–Poincaré reduction and generates a symmetric double–bracket term. For G = S ​ U ​ ( n ) G=SU(n) and V V a direct sum of adjoint copies of 𝔰 ​ 𝔲 ​ ( n ) \mathfrak{su}(n) , the reduced Euler–Poincaré equations on the ACSP reproduce exactly the GKSL generator: the Hamiltonian term − i ⁡ [ H , ρ ] -i[H,\rho] arises from the coadjoint action, while the Lindblad dissipator − γ 2 ​ [ L , [ L , ρ ] ] -\tfrac{\gamma}{2}[L,[L,\rho]] appears as the torsion–induced metric component associated with a canonical ACSP metric bracket . We prove three structural results. First, a characterization theorem shows that any quadratic, S ​ U ​ ( n ) SU(n) –equivariant operator generated from K K factorizes through K K and an equivariant linear map, and therefore has the algebraic form of a Lindblad double commutator. Second, a uniqueness theorem states that, under natural structural assumptions (bilinearity, S ​ U ​ ( n ) SU(n) –equivariance, Hermiticity, trace preservation, and compatibility with the ACSP torsion), the only admissible dissipative term in the reduced equations is the Lindblad double commutator. Third, an orbit–contraction theorem establishes that the resulting flow strictly contracts coadjoint orbits toward the commutant of the Lindblad operators. For S ​ U ​ ( 2 ) SU(2) the ACSP geometry yields explicit Bloch–ball formulas for dephasing, depolarizing, and amplitude–damping channels, and for S ​ U ​ ( 3 ) SU(3) it produces higher–dimensional Bloch equations for representative three–level dissipative processes. Finally, we show that the ACSP–induced double bracket fits naturally into a metriplectic and contact–geometric framework. On the space of density operators the GKSL generator admits a metriplectic decomposition into a Lie–Poisson (Hamiltonian) part and a torsion–driven metric double–bracket part, the latter providing the quantum counterpart of the classical Bloch–Krishnaprasad–Marsden–Ratiu double–bracket dissipation mechanism. The same metric term arises as the Reeb component of a contact Hamiltonian flow on the contactification of a coadjoint orbit. In this geometric picture, decoherence is a curvature–induced contraction generated by Euler–Poincaré reduction on an adjoint–coupled semidirect product, rather than a phenomenological ingredient postulated a priori .

Introduction

The geometric formulation of quantum dynamics has long provided deep
insight into controllability, optimal control, and the structure of
physical evolution

[
1
,
2
,
29
]
.
For closed systems, the Schrödinger and von Neumann equations

\dot{\psi}=-iH(t)\psi,\qquad\dot{\rho}=-i[H,\rho],

generate unitary motion that preserves both the spectrum and the purity
of
\rho
.
Consequently, purely Hamiltonian control cannot alter eigenvalues and
therefore cannot change purity or entropy—an intrinsic limitation of
finite-dimensional controllability
[
1

.

Open quantum systems circumvent this restriction.
Under standard Markovian assumptions, the reduced dynamics of a quantum
system is governed by the Gorini–Kossakowski–Sudarshan–Lindblad (GKSL)
master equation

\dot{\rho}=-i[H,\rho]+\sum_{j=1}^{N}\Big(L_{j}\rho L_{j}^{\dagger}-\tfrac{1}{2}\{L_{j}^{\dagger}L_{j},\rho\}\Big),

(1)

where
\{A,B\}:=AB+BA
is the anticommutator.
Thus, in the GKSL dissipator the term
\{L_{j}^{\dagger}L_{j},\rho\}
is the anticommutator

L_{j}^{\dagger}L_{j}\,\rho+\rho\,L_{j}^{\dagger}L_{j}
.
The GKSL generator underlies modern decoherence theory as well as the
mathematics of quantum optics, information, and thermodynamics.
In the qubit case the dynamics admits a full geometric realization on
the Bloch ball, providing a natural setting for detailed analyses in
control and optimization

[
12
,
13
,
3
]
. Beyond recovering (
1
), we show that its dissipative part admits
a natural realization both as a metric double bracket on the space of density operators
and as the Reeb component of a contact Hamiltonian flow on a suitable extension of
coadjoint orbits.

Parallel to these developments, Euler–Poincaré reduction provides a
unifying variational principle for deriving reduced equations of motion
on Lie groups and their duals

[
18
,
19
,
23
]
.
Classical semidirect products
G\ltimes V
—fundamental in continuum

\diamond

commutator couplings coming from the infinitesimal action

In such settings dissipation is typically introduced phenomenologically, not

Colombo and Jacobs introduced the notion of

commuting left and right actions of

[

]
.
Their construction produces, at the reduced level, additional dual
couplings—beyond those of classical semidirect products—that may be
interpreted as curvature–type contributions in the Euler–Poincaré
equations.
Motivated by this geometric mechanism, we formulate here an

adjoint–coupled semidirect product
(ACSP) tailored to quantum
dynamics.
In an ACSP the group
G
acts on a direct sum
V=\mathfrak{g}^{\oplus m}

by the adjoint representation, and a second, adjointly compatible action

Their controlled failure to commute defines an intrinsic torsion tensor

K(\xi,v)\ :=\ \xi\cdot v\;-\;v\cdot\xi,\qquad\xi\in\mathfrak{g},\ v\in V,

which vanishes in the centered case but is nonzero for the ACSPs

This torsion is the Lie–theoretic seed of a quadratic curvature operator
on
\mathfrak{g}^{*}
that survives Euler–Poincaré reduction and is
responsible for producing a metric double–bracket term in the reduced
dynamics. As we show later, the same torsion also underlies a contact
Hamiltonian description of GKSL evolution, where the double commutator
becomes the Reeb component encoding purity loss.

From a geometric standpoint, the dissipative double commutator

-\frac{\gamma}{2}[L,[L,\rho]]

emerges in our framework as the curvature associated with this torsion:
the reduced Euler–Poincaré equation on an ACSP contains exactly one
geometric coupling, already linear in a commutator, and a second
commutator arises automatically once the advected variables depend on
the coadjoint variable through an equivariant linear map of commutator
type.
The torsion
K
is therefore the Lie–theoretic origin of the Lindblad
double bracket.

Despite extensive work on geometric quantum control and open-system
dynamics, there is currently
no
formulation that derives the GKSL
generator from a variational principle or embeds Lindblad dissipation
systematically within symmetry reduction.
Existing geometric approaches to dissipation—such as double–bracket

However, they typically
postulate
a metric, transport, or contact
structure and then represent a given Lindblad generator as a gradient flow
of a chosen entropy or free energy functional. While double–bracket and gradient–flow formulations of Lindblad
generators are known, none of these arise from a variational principle
or from Euler–Poincaré reduction on a Lie–theoretic structure.
The present work identifies the precise Lie structure (the ACSP), the
associated torsion tensor, and the resulting curvature operator whose
Euler–Poincaré reduction yields the double commutator.
To our knowledge this connection has not appeared in the literature.

Our approach is complementary.
Here the GKSL generator is
derived
from Euler–Poincaré reduction
on an adjoint–coupled semidirect product.
The dissipative term arises intrinsically from the torsion
K
of the
ACSP and the curvature coupling it induces in the reduced equations.
This provides a Lie–theoretic and variational origin for the metric

exactly

Euler–Poincaré equation on an ACSP.
From this viewpoint, decoherence is a curvature–induced contraction generated by
Euler–Poincaré reduction on an adjoint–coupled semidirect product, rather than an
external phenomenological correction.

While GKSL generators have been expressed previously through
gradient, double–bracket, or contact flows, such formulations take the
dissipator as a starting input.
In contrast, the ACSP framework produces the Lindblad term

This provides a genuinely Lie–theoretic origin for quantum dissipation.

Our approach does not provide a microscopic derivation of

Instead, the result identifies a

This complements, rather than replaces, standard derivations based on

2
Euler–Poincaré reduction on adjoint–coupled semidirect products

This section recalls the semidirect–product framework and introduces
the adjoint–coupled structure that we will use later to organize
double–commutator terms.
The basic idea is to let the group act on a collection of advected
variables via the adjoint representation and to keep track of the
associated dual (“diamond”) coupling in the Euler–Poincaré
equations.
Whenever the advected quantity depends linearly on the momentum through
a commutator, this coupling automatically produces nested commutators in
the reduced dynamics.

2.1
Adjoint–coupled semidirect product structure

Let
\mathfrak{g}
be a finite-dimensional Lie algebra. For
\xi,v\in\mathfrak{g}
consider the adjoint
representation
\hbox{ad}_{\xi}(v):=[\xi,v]
.

Definition 2.1
.

For
m\geq 1
, the vector space
\mathfrak{g}^{\oplus m}:=\underbrace{\mathfrak{g}\oplus\cdots\oplus\mathfrak{g}}_{m\ \text{copies}}
is the external direct sum of
m
copies of
\mathfrak{g}
.
An element is a tuple
v=(v_{1},\ldots,v_{m})
with
v_{k}\in\mathfrak{g}
.

The adjoint representation of
\mathfrak{g}
extends diagonally to

\mathfrak{g}^{\oplus m}
by

\hbox{ad}_{\xi}(v_{1},\ldots,v_{m}):=\bigl([\xi,v_{1}],\ldots,[\xi,v_{m}]\bigr),

so
\mathfrak{g}^{\oplus m}
is the direct sum of
m
adjoint modules.

Let
G
be a Lie group with Lie algebra
\mathfrak{g}
and set

V=\mathfrak{g}^{\oplus m}
. For
g\in G
, we endow
V
with the diagonal adjoint action

g\cdot(v_{1},\ldots,v_{m}):=(\operatorname{Ad}_{g}v_{1},\ldots,\operatorname{Ad}_{g}v_{m}),

whose infinitesimal generator, for
\xi\in\mathfrak{g}
, is

\xi\cdot(v_{1},\ldots,v_{m})=([\xi,v_{1}],\ldots,[\xi,v_{m}]).

The corresponding (left) semidirect product
G\ltimes V
has group law
(g_{1},v_{1})(g_{2},v_{2})=(g_{1}g_{2},\ v_{1}+g_{1}\cdot v_{2})
, and Lie algebra
\mathfrak{g}\ltimes V
with bracket

[(\xi_{1},v_{1}),(\xi_{2},v_{2})]=\bigl([\xi_{1},\xi_{2}],\ \xi_{1}\cdot v_{2}-\xi_{2}\cdot v_{1}\bigr).

(2)

For the purposes of this paper it is convenient to keep in mind a
second, adjointly compatible right action on
V
, without building it
explicitly into the semidirect product.

Definition 2.2

(Adjoint–coupled right action)
.

Let
V=\mathfrak{g}^{\oplus m}
carry the left adjoint action

g\cdot v=\operatorname{Ad}_{g}v
.
For
g\in G,\ v\in V
, we equip
V
with a
right
action of
G
defined by
v\cdot g:=\operatorname{Ad}_{g^{-1}}v
. For
\xi\in\mathfrak{g}
, differentiating at the identity gives the infinitesimal right action

v\cdot\xi:=\left.\frac{d}{dt}\right|_{t=0}v\cdot\exp(t\xi)=-[\xi,v].

The left and right adjoint actions are mutual inverses at the group
level, but their infinitesimal generators enter with opposite signs.
Their skew combination defines a simple tensor that will be useful
later.

Definition 2.3

(Adjoint torsion)
.

The
adjoint torsion
is the bilinear map
K:\mathfrak{g}\times V\to V
defined by

K(\xi,v):=\xi\cdot v-v\cdot\xi.

(3)

For the adjoint actions above,

\xi\cdot v=[\xi,v],\qquad v\cdot\xi=-[\xi,v]\quad\Longrightarrow\quad K(\xi,v)=2[\xi,v],

so
K
carries one commutator in each component of
V
.

For completeness we recall that the coadjoint action

\operatorname{ad}^{*}:\mathfrak{g}\times\mathfrak{g}^{*}\to\mathfrak{g}^{*}

is defined by

\langle\operatorname{ad}^{*}_{\xi}\mu,\eta\rangle:=\langle\mu,[\eta,\xi]\rangle,\qquad\xi,\eta\in\mathfrak{g},\ \mu\in\mathfrak{g}^{*},

so that, under the
\operatorname{Ad}
–invariant identification

\mathfrak{g}\simeq\mathfrak{g}^{*}
,

\operatorname{ad}^{*}_{\xi}\mu=-[\xi,\mu].

In what follows we simply refer to the pair
(G,V)
, with
V
a direct
sum of adjoint modules, as an
adjoint–coupled semidirect
product
. We will use the usual semidirect product
G\ltimes V
for the
group structure and regard the adjoint torsion
K
as an auxiliary
tensor encoding how left and right adjoint actions differ
infinitesimally.

Remark 2.4

(On the inevitability of the
\diamond
–induced nested commutator)
.

For any adjoint–coupled semidirect product
G\ltimes V
with
V=g^{\oplus m}

and Ad–invariant pairing, the
\diamond
–operator
is entirely determined by the adjoint representation and therefore produces
exactly one commutator in each component.
Consequently,
every
equivariant choice of advected variable

\alpha(\mu)
containing a commutator automatically yields a quadratic nested
commutator in the Euler–Poincaré equations.
No additional assumptions, metric choices, or phenomenological inputs are
required: the emergence of
[L,[L,\rho]]
is a structural consequence of the
ACSP geometry.

2.2
Euler–Poincaré equations with adjoint coupling

Let
L:T(G\ltimes V)\to\mathbb{R}
be a left-invariant Lagrangian.
Using left trivialization, the reduced Lagrangian is a function
\ell(\xi,v,\alpha)
, for

(\xi,v,\alpha)\in\mathfrak{g}\times V\times V^{*}
, and its variational derivatives, define the momenta

\mu:=\frac{\delta\ell}{\delta\xi}\in\mathfrak{g}^{*},\qquad\beta:=\frac{\delta\ell}{\delta v}\in V^{*}.

For a semidirect product with left action
\xi\cdot v
the
Euler–Poincaré equations take the form

[
18
,
19
]

\displaystyle\dot{\mu}+\operatorname{ad}^{*}_{\xi}\mu

\displaystyle=\beta\diamond\alpha,

(4)

\displaystyle\dot{\alpha}+\xi\cdot\alpha

\displaystyle=0.

(5)

Here
\diamond:V\times V^{*}\to\mathfrak{g}^{*}
is the dual (or

diamond
) operator associated with the left action,

\langle v\diamond\alpha,\xi\rangle=-\langle\alpha,\,\xi\cdot v\rangle,\qquad\xi\in\mathfrak{g}.

(6)

We equip
\mathfrak{g}
with an
\operatorname{Ad}
–invariant inner product

\langle\cdot,\cdot\rangle_{\mathfrak{g}}
(for instance the trace form
on
\mathfrak{su}(n)
), and
V=\mathfrak{g}^{\oplus m}
with the
direct-sum pairing

\langle\alpha,v\rangle_{V}:=\sum_{k=1}^{m}\langle\alpha_{k},v_{k}\rangle_{\mathfrak{g}}.

Using
\operatorname{Ad}
–invariance and the fact that
\xi\cdot v=([\xi,v_{1}],\ldots,[\xi,v_{m}])
, one computes

\langle v\diamond\alpha,\xi\rangle=-\sum_{k=1}^{m}\langle\alpha_{k},[\xi,v_{k}]\rangle=\sum_{k=1}^{m}\langle[v_{k},\alpha_{k}],\xi\rangle,

so that

v\diamond\alpha=\sum_{k=1}^{m}[v_{k},\alpha_{k}].

(7)

Thus the diamond operator produces a
single commutator
for each
adjoint copy in
V
.

Remark 2.5
.

Throughout this section, and the next ones, we use an
\operatorname{Ad}
–invariant pairing

\langle X,Y\rangle_{\mathfrak{g}}=-\Tr(XY)
on
\mathfrak{su}(n)
,
which satisfies

\langle X,[Y,Z]\rangle=\langle[X,Y],Z\rangle
.
This ensures that the identification

\mathfrak{g}\cong\mathfrak{g}^{*}
is consistent with the commutator
structure and fixes all intermediate signs in the computation of

v\diamond\alpha
.
\diamond

Remark 2.6
.

Since each component of
V=\mathfrak{g}^{\oplus m}
carries the same

\operatorname{Ad}
–invariant pairing as
\mathfrak{g}
, we identify

V^{*}\cong V
componentwise.
Thus the element entering the diamond operator is the

V
–valued momentum variable

\beta=(\beta_{1},\dots,\beta_{m})\in V\simeq V^{*}
,
so the expression

\beta\diamond\alpha
is computed using the Lie–algebra elements

\beta_{k}
rather than abstract covectors.
Under this identification no ambiguity arises between

v_{k}
(the advected variable) and
\beta_{k}
(the momentum variable).
\diamond

A second commutator appears once the advected variable is chosen to
depend linearly (and
G
–equivariantly) on the momentum
\mu
through a
commutator.
A convenient model ansatz is

\alpha_{k}=c_{k}\,[v_{k},\mu],\qquad c_{k}\in\mathbb{R},

(8)

which is automatically equivariant because the map

X\mapsto[v_{k},X]
intertwines the adjoint action.
In the quantum setting one simply writes

\alpha_{k}=c_{k}\,[L_{k},\rho].

Using the identification
V^{*}\simeq V
established above, the diamond
operator satisfies

\beta\diamond\alpha=\sum_{k=1}^{m}[\beta_{k},\alpha_{k}].

For clarity, we restrict attention to a class of reduced Lagrangians for
which the
V
–momentum coincides with the advected variable:

\beta_{k}=\frac{\delta\ell}{\delta v_{k}}=v_{k}.

(Quadratic Lagrangians of the form

\ell(\xi,v,\alpha)=\tfrac{1}{2}\sum_{k}\langle v_{k},v_{k}\rangle+\cdots

provide a canonical example.)
Under this assumption, substituting (
8
) into
(
4
) and using (
7
) gives

\beta\diamond\alpha=\sum_{k=1}^{m}c_{k}\,[v_{k},[v_{k},\mu]],

(9)

which is a genuine double commutator.

In the application to GKSL dynamics we take
\mu\equiv\rho
and

v_{k}\equiv L_{k}
, so that the resulting term becomes

[L_{k},[L_{k},\rho]],

the standard symmetric double–bracket appearing in the Lindblad
dissipator.

In summary, on an adjoint–coupled semidirect product the coadjoint term

-\mathrm{ad}^{*}_{\xi}\mu
reproduces the Hamiltonian (unitary) part, while
the diamond term
\beta\diamond\alpha
produces nested commutators
whenever
\alpha
depends on
\mu
via a first commutator. This simple
mechanism will serve as the starting point for the metriplectic and
contact–geometric interpretations developed in the following sections.

2.3
Example:
SU(2)
with adjoint coupling

We briefly illustrate the above constructions in the case

G=SU(2)
,
V=\mathfrak{su}(2)
, where the adjoint representation
admits the well-known Pauli-matrix realization.
This makes the algebraic identities underlying the appearance of double
commutators completely explicit.

We begin by recalling the basic structure of the special unitary group

SU(2)=\bigl\{\,g\in\mathbb{C}^{2\times 2}\;\big|\;g^{\dagger}g=I,\ \det(g)=1\,\bigr\},

where
g^{\dagger}:=\overline{g}^{\,T}
denotes the Hermitian adjoint
(conjugate transpose).
This is a compact, connected, simple Lie group of real dimension
3
.

Its Lie algebra is

\mathfrak{su}(2)=\bigl\{\,X\in\mathbb{C}^{2\times 2}\;\big|\;X^{\dagger}=-X,\ \Tr(X)=0\,\bigr\},

the space of traceless anti-Hermitian
2\times 2
matrices. A standard basis for
\mathfrak{su}(2)
is given by the Pauli matrices

\sigma_{x}=\begin{pmatrix}0&1\\
1&0\end{pmatrix},\qquad\sigma_{y}=\begin{pmatrix}0&-i\\
i&0\end{pmatrix},\qquad\sigma_{z}=\begin{pmatrix}1&0\\
0&-1\end{pmatrix}.

Any
X\in\mathfrak{su}(2)
admits a unique representation

X=-\frac{i}{2}\,\mathbf{x}\cdot\boldsymbol{\sigma},\qquad\mathbf{x}\in\mathbb{R}^{3},

(10)

which defines a linear isomorphism
\mathfrak{su}(2)\simeq\mathbb{R}^{3}
.
Note that the Pauli matrices themselves are Hermitian, but the combinations

-\tfrac{i}{2}\sigma_{x}
,
-\tfrac{i}{2}\sigma_{y}
,
-\tfrac{i}{2}\sigma_{z}

form a real basis of the Lie algebra
\mathfrak{su}(2)
.
Under this identification, the Lie bracket becomes the vector cross
product:

[X,Y]=-\frac{i}{2}\,(\mathbf{x}\times\mathbf{y})\cdot\boldsymbol{\sigma},\qquad X=-\tfrac{i}{2}\mathbf{x}\cdot\boldsymbol{\sigma},\ Y=-\tfrac{i}{2}\mathbf{y}\cdot\boldsymbol{\sigma}.

(11)

The trace pairing
\langle A,B\rangle:=\Tr(AB)
induces an identification
\mathfrak{su}(2)\cong\mathfrak{su}(2)^{*}
,
since

\Tr\!\left(-\tfrac{i}{2}\mathbf{x}\cdot\boldsymbol{\sigma}\;-\tfrac{i}{2}\mathbf{y}\cdot\boldsymbol{\sigma}\right)=-\frac{1}{2}\,\mathbf{x}\cdot\mathbf{y}.

The adjoint action of
SU(2)
on its Lie algebra is
\operatorname{Ad}_{g}X:=gXg^{-1},\,g\in SU(2),\ X\in\mathfrak{su}(2)
. Because
\operatorname{Ad}_{g}
preserves both the commutator and the trace pairing, it
acts as a rotation on
\mathbb{R}^{3}
under the correspondence
(
10
)

\operatorname{Ad}_{g}\!\left(-\tfrac{i}{2}\mathbf{x}\cdot\boldsymbol{\sigma}\right)=-\tfrac{i}{2}\,(R_{g}\mathbf{x})\cdot\boldsymbol{\sigma},

for some
R_{g}\in SO(3)
.

Using the trace pairing and the Jacobi identity, we compute explicitly:

\displaystyle\langle v\diamond\alpha,\xi\rangle

\displaystyle=-\Tr\!\bigl(\alpha[\xi,v]\bigr)=-\Tr\!\bigl([\alpha,v]\xi\bigr)\Longrightarrow v\diamond\alpha=[v,\alpha].

Thus, for the adjoint representation of
SU(2)
,

v\diamond\alpha=[v,\alpha].

(12)

This is the key algebraic identity of the entire theory:
the diamond operator becomes a single commutator, and therefore any choice of advected quantity
\alpha(\rho)
involving a commutator produces nested commutators at the EP level.

Let
L\in\mathfrak{su}(2)
and consider
\alpha=[L,\rho]
.
Then

v\diamond\alpha=[v,\,[L,\rho]\,].

If we choose
v=L
, we obtain the fundamental identity:

L\diamond[L,\rho]=[L,[L,\rho]].

(13)

This is
exactly
the double commutator appearing in the Lindblad
dissipator for Hermitian channels:

D_{L}(\rho)=-\frac{\gamma}{2}[L,[L,\rho]].

Thus, even before imposing any variational structure, the adjoint
semidirect product already contains the algebraic mechanism needed to
generate GKSL dissipation.

The double–commutator map

\rho\mapsto[L,[L,\rho]]

acts as a dissipative contraction of the components of
\rho
transverse
to the eigenspaces of
L
. In Bloch coordinates this corresponds to a
linear decay of the Bloch vector toward the axis determined by the Pauli
vector of
L
, providing the familiar geometric picture of dephasing as
directional collapse inside the Bloch ball.

From the Lie–algebraic viewpoint, the adjoint–coupled semidirect
product already contains this mechanism at the structural level:
the diamond operator contributes a single commutator, and any advected
quantity
\alpha(\rho)
defined via a commutator automatically yields
the nested double–commutator pattern
[L,[L,\rho]]

3
Quantum
SU(2)
and the Pauli Representation

In Section
2
we described the adjoint–coupled
semidirect product
SU(2)\ltimes\mathfrak{su}(2)
at the Lie–algebraic
level.
We now translate this structure into the canonical Pauli representation
used in quantum mechanics.
This identifies
\mathfrak{su}(2)
with
\mathbb{R}^{3}
, turns
commutators into vector cross products, and realizes coadjoint orbits as
the Bloch sphere, making the comparison with Euler–Poincaré reduction
entirely transparent.

Throughout this section we use the standard Pauli basis

\sigma_{x}=\begin{pmatrix}0&1\\
1&0\end{pmatrix},\qquad\sigma_{y}=\begin{pmatrix}0&-i\\
i&0\end{pmatrix},\qquad\sigma_{z}=\begin{pmatrix}1&0\\
0&-1\end{pmatrix},\qquad\boldsymbol{\sigma}=(\sigma_{x},\sigma_{y},\sigma_{z}),

which satisfies the well-known identities

\sigma_{i}\sigma_{j}=\delta_{ij}I+i\epsilon_{ijk}\sigma_{k},\qquad[\sigma_{i},\sigma_{j}]=2i\epsilon_{ijk}\sigma_{k}.

Here
\delta_{ij}
is the Kronecker delta and
\epsilon_{ijk}
is the
fully antisymmetric Levi–Civita symbol defined by
\epsilon_{123}=1
,

\epsilon_{ijk}
changes sign under odd permutations, and

\epsilon_{ijk}=0
whenever two indices coincide.

The Lie algebra
\mathfrak{su}(2)
consists of traceless, anti-Hermitian

2\times 2
matrices.
A convenient linear parametrization is obtained by expanding any

X\in\mathfrak{su}(2)
in the Pauli basis:

X=-\frac{i}{2}\,\mathbf{x}\cdot\boldsymbol{\sigma},\,\,\mathbf{x}=(x_{1},x_{2},x_{3})\in\mathbb{R}^{3}.

(14)

This defines a linear identification

\mathfrak{su}(2)\cong\mathbb{R}^{3}
,

X\longleftrightarrow\mathbf{x}
, and the normalization in
(
14
) ensures that the exponential map

\exp\!\left(-\tfrac{i}{2}\mathbf{x}\cdot\boldsymbol{\sigma}\right)

describes a rotation by angle
\|\mathbf{x}\|
around the axis

\mathbf{x}
—a fact that underlies the ubiquity of this representation
in quantum mechanics, rigid-body dynamics, and geometric control.

A first consequence of (
14
) is that the Lie
bracket on
\mathfrak{su}(2)
becomes the vector cross product on

\mathbb{R}^{3}
.

Lemma 3.1
.

For
X=-\frac{i}{2}\mathbf{x}\cdot\boldsymbol{\sigma}
and

Y=-\frac{i}{2}\mathbf{y}\cdot\boldsymbol{\sigma}
,

[X,Y]=-\frac{i}{2}\,(\mathbf{x}\times\mathbf{y})\cdot\boldsymbol{\sigma}.

(15)

Proof.

Using the Pauli identity

\sigma_{i}\sigma_{j}=\delta_{ij}I+i\epsilon_{ijk}\sigma_{k},

we compute

XY=-\frac{1}{4}\Bigl(\mathbf{x}\cdot\mathbf{y}\,I+i(\mathbf{x}\times\mathbf{y})\cdot\boldsymbol{\sigma}\Bigr).

Exchanging
\mathbf{x}
and
\mathbf{y}
and subtracting

YX
yields (
15
).
∎

Thus the algebra
(\mathfrak{su}(2),[\cdot,\cdot])
is (up to a constant
factor) isometric to
(\mathbb{R}^{3},\times)
, and the trace pairing satisfies

\Tr(XY)=-\frac{1}{2}\,\mathbf{x}\cdot\mathbf{y},

so that
\mathfrak{su}(2)^{*}\cong\mathfrak{su}(2)\cong\mathbb{R}^{3}

via the Euclidean inner product.
In this picture, the coadjoint orbits of
SU(2)
are simply spheres of
radius
\|\mathbf{x}\|
, a familiar geometric description of pure qubit
states and classical rigid-body angular momenta.

Any qubit density matrix
\rho
satisfies
(i)
\rho^{\dagger}=\rho
,
(ii)
\rho\geq 0
,
(iii)
\Tr\rho=1
,
and therefore admits the Bloch representation

\rho=\frac{1}{2}\Bigl(I+\mathbf{r}\cdot\boldsymbol{\sigma}\Bigr),\qquad\mathbf{r}\in\mathbb{R}^{3},\ \|\mathbf{r}\|\leq 1.

(16)

The vector
\mathbf{r}
is the
Bloch vector
of the state.
Its geometric meaning is illustrated in

\|\mathbf{r}\|=1

The von Neumann equation reads

Proposition 3.2
.

Let

H=\frac{1}{2}\,\boldsymbol{\Omega}\cdot\boldsymbol{\sigma},\qquad\rho=\frac{1}{2}\bigl(I+\mathbf{r}\cdot\boldsymbol{\sigma}\bigr),

and consider the von Neumann equation

\dot{\rho}=-i[H,\rho].

Then, under the Bloch representation, the Bloch vector
\mathbf{r}

evolves according to

\dot{\mathbf{r}}=\boldsymbol{\Omega}\times\mathbf{r}.

Proof.

Insert the Bloch forms of
H
and
\rho
into the von Neumann equation.
Since the identity commutes with every operator,

[H,\rho]=\left[\frac{1}{2}\boldsymbol{\Omega}\cdot\boldsymbol{\sigma},\,\frac{1}{2}\bigl(I+\mathbf{r}\cdot\boldsymbol{\sigma}\bigr)\right]=\frac{1}{4}\bigl[\boldsymbol{\Omega}\cdot\boldsymbol{\sigma},\,\mathbf{r}\cdot\boldsymbol{\sigma}\bigr].

Thus

\dot{\rho}=-i[H,\rho]=-\frac{i}{4}\bigl[\boldsymbol{\Omega}\cdot\boldsymbol{\sigma},\,\mathbf{r}\cdot\boldsymbol{\sigma}\bigr].

We now evaluate the commutator using the Pauli product identity

\sigma_{i}\sigma_{j}=\delta_{ij}I+i\epsilon_{ijk}\sigma_{k}
, where

\delta_{ij}
is the Kronecker delta and
\epsilon_{ijk}
the
Levi–Civita symbol.
Write

\boldsymbol{\Omega}\cdot\boldsymbol{\sigma}=\sum_{i}\Omega_{i}\sigma_{i}
,

\mathbf{r}\cdot\boldsymbol{\sigma}=\sum_{j}r_{j}\sigma_{j}
. Then

\displaystyle\bigl(\boldsymbol{\Omega}\cdot\boldsymbol{\sigma}\bigr)\bigl(\mathbf{r}\cdot\boldsymbol{\sigma}\bigr)

\displaystyle=\sum_{i,j}\Omega_{i}r_{j}\sigma_{i}\sigma_{j}=\sum_{i,j}\Omega_{i}r_{j}\bigl(\delta_{ij}I+i\epsilon_{ijk}\sigma_{k}\bigr)

\displaystyle=(\boldsymbol{\Omega}\cdot\mathbf{r})I+i\sum_{i,j,k}\Omega_{i}r_{j}\epsilon_{ijk}\sigma_{k}.

Similarly,

\bigl(\mathbf{r}\cdot\boldsymbol{\sigma}\bigr)\bigl(\boldsymbol{\Omega}\cdot\boldsymbol{\sigma}\bigr)=(\boldsymbol{\Omega}\cdot\mathbf{r})I+i\sum_{i,j,k}r_{i}\Omega_{j}\epsilon_{ijk}\sigma_{k}.

Subtracting, the scalar parts cancel and we obtain

\displaystyle\bigl[\boldsymbol{\Omega}\cdot\boldsymbol{\sigma},\,\mathbf{r}\cdot\boldsymbol{\sigma}\bigr]

\displaystyle=i\sum_{i,j,k}\bigl(\Omega_{i}r_{j}-r_{i}\Omega_{j}\bigr)\epsilon_{ijk}\sigma_{k}=i\bigl(\boldsymbol{\Omega}\times\mathbf{r}\bigr)\cdot\boldsymbol{\sigma},

where the last equality uses the standard definition of the vector cross
product. Therefore,

\dot{\rho}=-\frac{i}{4}\,i\bigl(\boldsymbol{\Omega}\times\mathbf{r}\bigr)\cdot\boldsymbol{\sigma}=\frac{1}{4}\bigl(\boldsymbol{\Omega}\times\mathbf{r}\bigr)\cdot\boldsymbol{\sigma}.

On the other hand, differentiating the Bloch representation

\rho=\tfrac{1}{2}\bigl(I+\mathbf{r}\cdot\boldsymbol{\sigma}\bigr)

gives

\dot{\rho}=\frac{1}{2}\,\dot{\mathbf{r}}\cdot\boldsymbol{\sigma}.

Comparing both expressions for
\dot{\rho}
and using linear independence
of
\{\sigma_{x},\sigma_{y},\sigma_{z}\}
, we obtain

\frac{1}{2}\,\dot{\mathbf{r}}\cdot\boldsymbol{\sigma}=\frac{1}{4}\bigl(\boldsymbol{\Omega}\times\mathbf{r}\bigr)\cdot\boldsymbol{\sigma},

hence

\dot{\mathbf{r}}=\boldsymbol{\Omega}\times\mathbf{r}.

∎

\mathbb{R}^{3}

4
Lindblad Dissipation from the
\diamond
Operator

The purpose of this section is to show that the dissipative term in the
Euler–Poincaré equations on the adjoint–coupled semidirect product

SU(2)\ltimes V
reproduces exactly the
Gorini–Kossakowski–Sudarshan–Lindblad (GKSL) master equation

[
16
,
22
]
.
The underlying mechanism is geometric:
the
\diamond
operator encodes a double–commutator structure that,
once paired with an appropriate advected variable
\alpha
,
produces the canonical Lindblad dissipator.

Recall from Section
2
that, for the adjoint action

\xi\cdot v=[\xi,v]
on
V=\mathfrak{su}(2)
and using the trace pairing,
the diamond operator takes the form

v\diamond\alpha=[v,\alpha],

(20)

for all
v,\alpha\in\mathfrak{su}(2)
(and, by extension, for traceless
operators in the Pauli representation).

On a semidirect product
G\ltimes V
with left action
G\times V\to V
,
the reduced Euler–Poincaré equation takes the form

\dot{\mu}+\operatorname{ad}^{*}_{\xi}\mu=\frac{\delta\ell}{\delta\alpha}\diamond\alpha,

(21)

where
\mu=\delta\ell/\delta\xi\in\mathfrak{g}^{*}
and

\alpha\in V^{*}
is the advected quantity.
As in Section
2
, we use the
\operatorname{Ad}
–invariant trace pairing to
identify
V^{*}\cong V
, so that
\delta\ell/\delta\alpha
is naturally
interpreted as a
V
–valued momentum variable entering the

\diamond
–term.
This identification is consistent because the pairing

\langle X,Y\rangle=-\operatorname{Tr}(XY)
is invariant and allows us to
regard variational derivatives with respect to
\alpha\in V^{*}
as
elements of
V
.

To connect (
21
) with quantum dynamics
we make the identifications
\mu\leftrightarrow\rho
,

\xi=-iH
,
\delta\ell/\delta\alpha=L
, where
\rho
is the density
operator,
H
is the Hamiltonian, and

L
is a (Hermitian) Lindblad operator (so that
-iH\in\mathfrak{su}(2)

and, via the trace pairing,
L
is naturally viewed as an element of

\mathfrak{su}(2)^{*}
).
Under this correspondence, the coadjoint term satisfies

\operatorname{ad}^{*}_{\xi}\mu=\operatorname{ad}^{*}_{-iH}\rho=i[H,\rho],\qquad-\operatorname{ad}^{*}_{\xi}\mu=-i[H,\rho],

so that (
21
) reads

\dot{\rho}+i[H,\rho]=L\diamond\alpha,

or equivalently

\dot{\rho}=-i[H,\rho]+L\diamond\alpha.

(22)

The Hamiltonian part
-i[H,\rho]
is thus reproduced by the coadjoint
term
-\operatorname{ad}^{*}_{\xi}\mu
.
The remaining contribution

L\diamond\alpha=[L,\alpha]

encodes all dissipative effects.
In the next step we show that, for a suitable choice of advected
variable
\alpha(\rho)
, this term reproduces the Lindblad double commutator
and therefore the full GKSL dissipator.
Before doing so, it is useful to record the corresponding contraction
property in Bloch coordinates.

For a single qubit, any density operator can be written as

\rho=\frac{1}{2}\bigl(I+\mathbf{r}\cdot\boldsymbol{\sigma}\bigr),\qquad\mathbf{r}\in\mathbb{R}^{3},\ \|\mathbf{r}\|\leq 1,

and a Hermitian Lindblad operator can be written as

L=\frac{1}{2}\,\boldsymbol{\ell}\cdot\boldsymbol{\sigma},\qquad\boldsymbol{\ell}\in\mathbb{R}^{3}.

Lemma 4.1
.

Let
L=\frac{1}{2}\boldsymbol{\ell}\cdot\boldsymbol{\sigma}
with

\|\boldsymbol{\ell}\|=1
, and let

\rho=\frac{1}{2}(I+\mathbf{r}\cdot\boldsymbol{\sigma})
be a qubit
state.
The GKSL dissipator

D_{L}(\rho)=-\frac{\gamma}{2}[L,[L,\rho]]

(23)

induces, in Bloch coordinates, the evolution

\dot{\mathbf{r}}=-2\gamma\bigl(\mathbf{r}-(\mathbf{r}\cdot\boldsymbol{\ell})\boldsymbol{\ell}\bigr).

(24)

In particular, the component

\mathbf{r}_{\perp}:=\mathbf{r}-(\mathbf{r}\cdot\boldsymbol{\ell})\boldsymbol{\ell}

orthogonal to
\boldsymbol{\ell}
satisfies

\frac{d}{dt}\|\mathbf{r}_{\perp}\|^{2}=-4\gamma\,\|\mathbf{r}_{\perp}\|^{2}\leq 0,

(25)

while the longitudinal component

\mathbf{r}_{\parallel}:=(\mathbf{r}\cdot\boldsymbol{\ell})\boldsymbol{\ell}

is preserved.

Proof.

Using
\rho=\tfrac{1}{2}(I+\mathbf{r}\cdot\boldsymbol{\sigma})
and

L=\tfrac{1}{2}\boldsymbol{\ell}\cdot\boldsymbol{\sigma}
and the Pauli
identity
\sigma_{i}\sigma_{j}=\delta_{ij}I+i\epsilon_{ijk}\sigma_{k}
, we first compute

[L,\rho]=\frac{1}{4}\bigl[\boldsymbol{\ell}\cdot\boldsymbol{\sigma},\,\mathbf{r}\cdot\boldsymbol{\sigma}\bigr]=\frac{i}{2}\,(\boldsymbol{\ell}\times\mathbf{r})\cdot\boldsymbol{\sigma}.

Applying
[L,\cdot]
once more we get

[L,[L,\rho]]=\frac{i}{2}\left[\frac{1}{2}\boldsymbol{\ell}\cdot\boldsymbol{\sigma},\,(\boldsymbol{\ell}\times\mathbf{r})\cdot\boldsymbol{\sigma}\right]=-\frac{1}{2}\Bigl(\boldsymbol{\ell}\times(\boldsymbol{\ell}\times\mathbf{r})\Bigr)\cdot\boldsymbol{\sigma}.

Using the vector identity

\boldsymbol{\ell}\times(\boldsymbol{\ell}\times\mathbf{r})=\boldsymbol{\ell}(\boldsymbol{\ell}\cdot\mathbf{r})-\|\boldsymbol{\ell}\|^{2}\mathbf{r}=\boldsymbol{\ell}(\boldsymbol{\ell}\cdot\mathbf{r})-\mathbf{r},

and the fact that
\|\boldsymbol{\ell}\|=1
, we obtain

[L,[L,\rho]]=\frac{1}{2}\bigl(\mathbf{r}-(\mathbf{r}\cdot\boldsymbol{\ell})\boldsymbol{\ell}\bigr)\cdot\boldsymbol{\sigma}.

Substituting into (
23
) gives

D_{L}(\rho)=-\frac{\gamma}{2}[L,[L,\rho]]=-\frac{\gamma}{4}\bigl(\mathbf{r}-(\mathbf{r}\cdot\boldsymbol{\ell})\boldsymbol{\ell}\bigr)\cdot\boldsymbol{\sigma}.

Since
\dot{\rho}=\tfrac{1}{2}\dot{\mathbf{r}}\cdot\boldsymbol{\sigma}
,
we identify

\frac{1}{2}\,\dot{\mathbf{r}}\cdot\boldsymbol{\sigma}=-\frac{\gamma}{4}\bigl(\mathbf{r}-(\mathbf{r}\cdot\boldsymbol{\ell})\boldsymbol{\ell}\bigr)\cdot\boldsymbol{\sigma},

so

\dot{\mathbf{r}}=-2\gamma\bigl(\mathbf{r}-(\mathbf{r}\cdot\boldsymbol{\ell})\boldsymbol{\ell}\bigr).

Now, writing
\mathbf{r}=\mathbf{r}_{\parallel}+\mathbf{r}_{\perp}
with

\mathbf{r}_{\parallel}=(\mathbf{r}\cdot\boldsymbol{\ell})\boldsymbol{\ell}
,
we have
\dot{\mathbf{r}}_{\parallel}=0
and

\dot{\mathbf{r}}_{\perp}=-2\gamma\,\mathbf{r}_{\perp}
. Thus

\frac{d}{dt}\|\mathbf{r}_{\perp}\|^{2}=2\langle\mathbf{r}_{\perp},\dot{\mathbf{r}}_{\perp}\rangle=-4\gamma\,\|\mathbf{r}_{\perp}\|^{2}\leq 0.

∎

Remark 4.4
.

than from a purely mechanical Lagrangian as we will see in Section
8
.

Remark 4.5

(Non-Hermitian Lindblad channels and amplitude damping)
.

Physical processes such as spontaneous emission involve non-Hermitian
Lindblad operators, e.g.

L=\sqrt{\gamma}\,\sigma_{-},\qquad\sigma_{-}=\begin{pmatrix}0&0\\
1&0\end{pmatrix}.

Such an
L
admits the Cartesian decomposition

L=A+iB,\qquad A=\frac{L+L^{\dagger}}{2},\quad B=\frac{L-L^{\dagger}}{2i},

with
A,B
Hermitian (so that
-iA,-iB\in\mathfrak{su}(2)
).
For the amplitude-damping operator above one finds explicitly

A=\frac{\sqrt{\gamma}}{2}\,\sigma_{x},\qquad B=-\frac{\sqrt{\gamma}}{2}\,\sigma_{y}.

The GKSL dissipator can be written as

D_{L}(\rho)=L\rho L^{\dagger}-\frac{1}{2}\{L^{\dagger}L,\rho\}.

Expanding in terms of the Hermitian and anti-Hermitian parts

L=A+iB
with
A,B\in\mathfrak{su}(n)
yields the identity

D_{L}(\rho)=-\frac{1}{2}[A,[A,\rho]]-\frac{1}{2}[B,[B,\rho]]-\frac{i}{2}[A,\{B,\rho\}]+\frac{i}{2}[B,\{A,\rho\}].

The first two terms are purely dissipative and arise from the
double-commutator mechanism associated with the ACSP torsion. They are
generated (up to constant prefactors) by choosing

V=\mathfrak{su}(n)\oplus\mathfrak{su}(n),\qquad\alpha_{A}=c_{A}[\rho,A],\qquad\alpha_{B}=c_{B}[\rho,B],

and setting

\delta\ell/\delta\alpha_{A}=A
,
\delta\ell/\delta\alpha_{B}=B
.

The mixed terms involving anticommutators can be reorganized into a
single commutator with
\rho
,

-\tfrac{i}{2}[A,\{B,\rho\}]+\tfrac{i}{2}[B,\{A,\rho\}]=-i\,[AB+BA,\rho],

and therefore correspond to a Hamiltonian renormalization (the
Lamb shift)
[
7
]
. Such terms lie in the coadjoint part

\operatorname{ad}^{*}_{\xi}\rho
of the Euler–Poincaré equation.

Thus a non-Hermitian Lindblad operator
L=A+iB
naturally splits into:
(i) a dissipative ACSP component generated by the double commutators

[A,[A,\rho]]
and
[B,[B,\rho]]
, and
(ii) a Hamiltonian renormalization component generated by

[AB+BA,\rho]
.
This decomposition matches the standard analysis of
amplitude-damping dynamics
[
10
]
.

5
Qubit Examples and Bloch Dynamics

We now illustrate how the adjoint–coupled semidirect-product
Euler–Poincaré formulation reproduces standard qubit Lindblad channels
when expressed in Bloch coordinates.
Throughout, we use the Bloch representation

\rho=\frac{1}{2}\bigl(I+\mathbf{r}\cdot\boldsymbol{\sigma}\bigr),\qquad\mathbf{r}\in\mathbb{R}^{3},\ \|\mathbf{r}\|\leq 1,

(33)

and the Lie-algebra identification

X=-\frac{i}{2}\,\mathbf{x}\cdot\boldsymbol{\sigma}
,
with
\mathbf{x}\in\mathbb{R}^{3}
.

From Section
4
, the reduced
Euler–Poincaré equations on the adjoint–coupled semidirect product

SU(2)\ltimes\mathfrak{su}(2)
yield the
GKSL master equation

\dot{\rho}=-i[H,\rho]-\sum_{\alpha}\frac{\gamma_{\alpha}}{2}[L_{\alpha},[L_{\alpha},\rho]],

with
H=\tfrac{1}{2}\boldsymbol{\Omega}\cdot\boldsymbol{\sigma}
.
We now translate this into ordinary differential equations for the Bloch
vector
\mathbf{r}
.

We denote by
\mathbf{l}\in\mathbb{R}^{3}
the Bloch vector associated with
the (Hermitian) Lindblad operator
L
.
Using the standard identification

\mathfrak{su}(2)\simeq\mathbb{R}^{3},\ X\mapsto\mathbf{x}

given by
\displaystyle{X=-\frac{i}{2}\,\mathbf{x}\cdot\boldsymbol{\sigma}}
, any Hermitian channel can be written as

L=-\frac{i}{2}\,\mathbf{l}\cdot\boldsymbol{\sigma}.

Geometrically, the vector
\mathbf{l}
specifies the decoherence axis of
the channel and determines the direction toward which the dissipative flow
contracts the Bloch ball.

Using the Pauli identities
[\sigma_{i},\sigma_{j}]=2i\,\epsilon_{ijk}\sigma_{k}
and
\{\sigma_{i},\sigma_{j}\}=2\delta_{ij}I
, one finds, for
L=-\tfrac{i}{2}\mathbf{l}\cdot\boldsymbol{\sigma}
,

[L,[L,\rho]]=-\frac{i}{2}\,\bigl(\mathbf{l}\times(\mathbf{l}\times\mathbf{r})\bigr)\cdot\boldsymbol{\sigma},

(34)

so that the GKSL dissipator becomes, in Bloch coordinates,

-\frac{\gamma}{2}[L,[L,\rho]]\ \Longleftrightarrow\ \dot{\mathbf{r}}_{\mathrm{diss}}=-\,\gamma\,\mathbf{l}\times(\mathbf{l}\times\mathbf{r}).

(35)

The Hamiltonian contribution retains the usual rigid-body form

\dot{\mathbf{r}}_{\mathrm{Ham}}=\boldsymbol{\Omega}\times\mathbf{r}.

(36)

The vector identity

\mathbf{l}\times(\mathbf{l}\times\mathbf{r})=\mathbf{l}(\mathbf{l}\cdot\mathbf{r})-\|\mathbf{l}\|^{2}\mathbf{r}

shows that the dissipator projects
\mathbf{r}
onto the plane orthogonal
to
\mathbf{l}
and contracts only the transverse component.
This provides the geometric picture of decoherence as axis-aligned
collapse of Bloch vectors.

5.1
Example 1: Pure dephasing

Pure dephasing is the dominant source of decoherence in most physical
qubits, including superconducting circuits, trapped ions, spin qubits,
and Rydberg atoms.
It arises from slow environmental fluctuations that randomize the qubit
phase without inducing energy relaxation, a mechanism first analyzed in
the seminal work of Klauder and Anderson
[
20

.
This process sets the fundamental coherence time
T_{2}
and plays a key
role in noise spectroscopy and dynamical-decoupling protocols.

Consider the Hermitian Lindblad operator

L=\sqrt{\gamma}\,\sigma_{z}.

For a single Hermitian channel, the GKSL dissipator is

D_{L}(\rho)=L\rho L-\tfrac{1}{2}\{L^{2},\rho\}.

\rho=\tfrac{1}{2}\bigl(I+r_{x}\sigma_{x}+r_{y}\sigma_{y}+r_{z}\sigma_{z}\bigr),

\sigma_{z}\sigma_{x}\sigma_{z}=-\sigma_{x}

we read off the component-wise equations

(37)

r_{z}
and

(r_{x},r_{y})

Example 2: Depolarizing noise

Depolarizing noise models uniform randomization of the Bloch vector and
is one of the fundamental noise channels in quantum information theory.
It appears as the effective model of gate imperfections, isotropic

[
10
]

and the theory of threshold theorems in fault-tolerant computation,

[
28
]

In this channel the Bloch sphere contracts uniformly toward the origin,
corresponding to relaxation into the maximally mixed state
\rho=I/2
.

We consider the isotropic triple of Hermitian Lindblad operators

L_{x}=\sqrt{\tfrac{\gamma}{2}}\,\sigma_{x},\qquad L_{y}=\sqrt{\tfrac{\gamma}{2}}\,\sigma_{y},\qquad L_{z}=\sqrt{\tfrac{\gamma}{2}}\,\sigma_{z}.

D_{L_{i}}(\rho)=-\frac{\gamma}{2}\,[\sigma_{i},[\sigma_{i},\rho]],\qquad i=x,y,z.

\rho=\tfrac{1}{2}(I+\mathbf{r}\cdot\boldsymbol{\sigma})

The Pauli commutation relations
[\sigma_{i},\sigma_{j}]=2i\,\epsilon_{ijk}\sigma_{k}
and
\sigma_{i}\sigma_{j}=\delta_{ij}I+i\epsilon_{ijk}\sigma_{k}

[\sigma_{i},[\sigma_{i},\rho]]=[\sigma_{i},\ \sigma_{i}\rho-\rho\sigma_{i}]=\sigma_{i}(\sigma_{i}\rho-\rho\sigma_{i})-(\sigma_{i}\rho-\rho\sigma_{i})\sigma_{i}.

\sigma_{i}\sigma_{j}\sigma_{i}=-\sigma_{j}

\sigma_{i}\sigma_{i}\sigma_{i}=\sigma_{i}

\mathbf{e}_{i}
is the
i

\mathbb{R}^{3}

state approaches the maximally mixed density matrix

6
Structural Properties of Adjoint–Coupled Semidirect Products

In this section we develop the geometric and representation–theoretic
structure underlying
adjoint–coupled semidirect products
(ACSP),
introduced in Section
2
.
We show that the central structural object of an ACSP is the

adjoint torsion

K(\xi,v):=\xi\cdot v\;-\;v\cdot\xi,\qquad\xi\in\mathfrak{g},\;v\in V,

and that this torsion is precisely the geometric mechanism that enables
the emergence of
nested commutators
, including the
double–commutator structure of Lindblad dissipation.
We then characterize all
SU(n)
–equivariant quadratic dissipators
compatible with an ACSP structure, and show that the
unique
such
term is the Lindblad double commutator.

Before analyzing the structural characterization and uniqueness of all
ACSP–compatible dissipators, it is useful to isolate the precise role played
by the adjoint torsion.
The following lemma states that, under the natural equivariance and
representation assumptions defining an adjoint–coupled semidirect product,
the torsion tensor is the
only
bilinear geometric object capable of
producing a nontrivial curvature contribution in the Euler–Poincaré
equations.
This identifies torsion as the unique Lie–theoretic seed behind the
double–commutator structure.

Lemma 6.1
.

Let
(G,V)
be an adjoint–coupled semidirect product with

V=\mathfrak{g}^{\oplus m}
and
G
acting on each component through the
adjoint representation.
Let
K:\mathfrak{g}\times V\to V
be a bilinear map satisfying:
(i)
G
–equivariance under simultaneous conjugation,
(ii) compatibility with the direct–sum adjoint action on
V
, and
(iii) nontrivial contribution to the Euler–Poincaré equation under the

\diamond
–coupling.
Then
K
necessarily equals the adjoint torsion

K(\xi,v)=\xi\cdot v-v\cdot\xi\qquad(\xi\in\mathfrak{g},\,v\in V),

and each component of
K(\xi,v)
contains exactly one Lie bracket.
In particular, any curvature term that survives Euler–Poincaré reduction must
be a double commutator generated from this torsion.

Proof.

Since
V=\mathfrak{g}^{\oplus m}
carries the adjoint representation on each
component, any
G
–equivariant bilinear map

K:\mathfrak{g}\times V\to V
must preserve the isotypic decomposition of
V
.
Thus, on each component
v_{k}\in\mathfrak{g}
,
K
restricts to a bilinear map

K_{k}:\mathfrak{g}\times\mathfrak{g}\to\mathfrak{g}\quad\text{that is}\quad G\text{--equivariant under }\operatorname{Ad}.

By standard representation theory of compact Lie groups, the space of

\operatorname{Ad}
–equivariant bilinear maps
\mathfrak{g}\times\mathfrak{g}\to\mathfrak{g}

is one–dimensional and is spanned by the Lie bracket.
Therefore there exist constants
a_{k},b_{k}\in\mathbb{R}
such that

K_{k}(\xi,v_{k})=a_{k}[\xi,v_{k}]+b_{k}[v_{k},\xi].

Since
[\xi,v_{k}]=-[v_{k},\xi]
, this simplifies to

K_{k}(\xi,v_{k})=c_{k}[\xi,v_{k}],\qquad c_{k}:=a_{k}-b_{k}.

Assumption (ii) requires that
K
be compatible with the adjoint–coupled
structure, where the left and right infinitesimal actions on
V
are

\xi\cdot v_{k}=[\xi,v_{k}]
and
v_{k}\cdot\xi=-[\xi,v_{k}]
.
Thus the only bilinear map expressible in terms of these actions is

K_{k}(\xi,v_{k})=\xi\cdot v_{k}-v_{k}\cdot\xi=2[\xi,v_{k}].

Hence
c_{k}
must equal
2
for all
k
.

Finally, assumption (iii) requires that
K
generate a nontrivial term in
the Euler–Poincaré equations through the
\diamond
–coupling.
Since the
\diamond
operator already produces one commutator, any
equivariant bilinear
K
must produce exactly one more commutator in order for
the resulting curvature term to survive reduction.
The adjoint torsion
K(\xi,v)=2([\xi,v_{1}],\dots,[\xi,v_{m}])
is the unique
map with this property.

Thus
K
is uniquely determined and coincides with the adjoint torsion.
∎

Throughout we work with
G=SU(n)
and its Lie algebra

\mathfrak{g}=\mathfrak{su}(n)
. The
complex vector space
\mathcal{B}(\mathbb{C}^{n})
of linear operators on

\mathbb{C}^{n}
carries the
Hilbert–Schmidt inner product

\langle A,B\rangle_{\mathrm{HS}}:=\Tr(A^{\dagger}B),\qquad A,B\in\mathcal{B}(\mathbb{C}^{n}),

which induces the Hilbert–Schmidt norm

\|A\|_{\mathrm{HS}}=\sqrt{\Tr(A^{\dagger}A)}
.
For Hermitian operators this reduces to the trace pairing

\langle A,B\rangle=\Tr(AB)
used throughout this section.
The Hilbert–Schmidt metric plays a fundamental role in quantum
information geometry, dissipative flows, and the analysis of open
quantum systems (see, e.g.,
[
27
, Ch. 9]
).
All coadjoint and dissipative estimates below are expressed with respect
to this metric, which is bi-invariant under unitary conjugation and
therefore compatible with the
SU(n)
coadjoint representation.

For anti–Hermitian matrices (in particular for
\mathfrak{su}(n)
) this
reduces, up to a sign, to the trace pairing

\langle X,Y\rangle_{\mathfrak{g}}:=-\Tr(XY),\qquad X,Y\in\mathfrak{su}(n),

which is
\operatorname{Ad}
–invariant and is used to identify

\mathfrak{g}^{*}\cong\mathfrak{g}
and
V^{*}\cong V
throughout the paper.

Recall from Section
2
that in an adjoint–coupled
semidirect product we take
V
to be a direct sum of adjoint modules,
e.g.
\displaystyle{V=\mathfrak{g}^{\oplus m}=\underbrace{\mathfrak{g}\oplus\cdots\oplus\mathfrak{g}}_{m\ \text{copies}}}
,
with the
left
action of
G
given by the adjoint representation,
g\cdot(v_{1},\dots,v_{m}):=(\operatorname{Ad}_{g}v_{1},\dots,\operatorname{Ad}_{g}v_{m})
, and, infinitesimally,

\xi\cdot(v_{1},\dots,v_{m})=([\xi,v_{1}],\dots,[\xi,v_{m}])
.

In addition, it is convenient to equip
V
with a
right
action of

G
that is compatible with the adjoint structure but not built into the
group law
(v_{1},\dots,v_{m})\cdot g:=(\operatorname{Ad}_{g^{-1}}v_{1},\dots,\operatorname{Ad}_{g^{-1}}v_{m})
, so that infinitesimally

(v_{1},\dots,v_{m})\cdot\xi=-([\xi,v_{1}],\dots,[\xi,v_{m}]).

The left and right actions coincide at the group level up to inversion,
but their infinitesimal generators differ by a sign.

For
V=\mathfrak{g}^{\oplus m}
with the adjoint actions above, one has

\xi\cdot v=([\xi,v_{1}],\dots,[\xi,v_{m}]),\qquad v\cdot\xi=-([\xi,v_{1}],\dots,[\xi,v_{m}]),

and therefore

K(\xi,v)=2([\xi,v_{1}],\dots,[\xi,v_{m}]).

(42)

Thus, for each adjoint copy in
V
, the torsion tensor carries a single
commutator. The presence of both left and right actions (and hence a
nontrivial torsion) is what allows ACSP Euler–Poincaré equations to
generate
nested
commutators when combined with the diamond
operator and an appropriate choice of advected variable.

Note that, with the direct–sum pairing
\displaystyle{\langle\alpha,v\rangle_{V}=\sum_{k=1}^{m}\langle\alpha_{k},v_{k}\rangle_{\mathfrak{g}}}
, and using
\operatorname{Ad}
–invariance of
\langle\cdot,\cdot\rangle_{\mathfrak{g}}
,
one finds (Section
2
)

v\diamond\alpha=\sum_{k=1}^{m}[v_{k},\alpha_{k}].

(43)

In particular, for a single adjoint copy (
m=1
) the diamond operator
reduces to a
single
commutator

v\diamond\alpha=[v,\alpha].

A second commutator appears once the advected variable is chosen to
depend linearly (and equivariantly) on the momentum through a
commutator. As in Section
2
, a convenient ansatz is

\alpha_{k}=c_{k}[v_{k},\mu],\qquad c_{k}\in\mathbb{R},

so that

\beta\diamond\alpha=\sum_{k=1}^{m}c_{k}\,[\beta_{k},[v_{k},\mu]].

For suitable Lagrangians one may identify
\beta_{k}=v_{k}
, which yields
genuine double commutators of the form
[v_{k},[v_{k},\mu]]
. In the
quantum setting we specialize to
\mu\leftrightarrow\rho
and

v_{k}=L_{k}
, obtaining precisely the terms
[L_{k},[L_{k},\rho]]
that appear
in the GKSL dissipator. This mechanism is made fully explicit in
Section
4
; here we focus on the
representation–theoretic constraints it imposes.

6.1
Torsion–generated dissipators and their characterization

We now characterize all quadratic operators that can arise from the
adjoint torsion in an ACSP setting.

Theorem 6.2
.

Let
G=SU(n)
and
V=\mathfrak{su}(n)
with the adjoint–coupled
structure.
Let

\Xi:\mathfrak{su}(n)\times\mathfrak{su}(n)\to\mathfrak{su}(n),\qquad(L,\rho)\mapsto\Xi(L,\rho),

be a bilinear map that is
SU(n)
–equivariant under simultaneous
conjugation, i.e.

\Xi(\operatorname{Ad}_{g}L,\operatorname{Ad}_{g}\rho\,\operatorname{Ad}_{g^{-1}})=\operatorname{Ad}_{g}\,\Xi(L,\rho)\,\operatorname{Ad}_{g^{-1}}\quad\text{for all }g\in SU(n).

(44)

Then the following are equivalent:

(i)

\Xi(L,\rho)
arises from the ACSP torsion, i.e. there exists an

SU(n)
–equivariant linear map

\Phi:\mathfrak{su}(n)\to\mathfrak{su}(n)
such that

\Xi(L,\rho)=K(L,\Phi(\rho)),

where
K
is the torsion tensor (
42
).

(ii)

\Xi(L,\rho)
factorizes as a commutator

\Xi(L,\rho)=[L,T(\rho)],

for some
SU(n)
–equivariant linear map

T:\mathfrak{su}(n)\to\mathfrak{su}(n)
.

Moreover, under
(ii)
equivariance implies that, for each fixed

L
, the restriction of
T
to the orthogonal complement of

\ker(\hbox{ad}_{L})
is a scalar multiple of
\hbox{ad}_{L}
, so that

\Xi(L,\rho)=c(L)\,[L,[L,\rho_{\perp}]],

where
\rho_{\perp}
is the component of
\rho
orthogonal to

\ker(\hbox{ad}_{L})
with respect to
\langle\cdot,\cdot\rangle_{\mathrm{HS}}
,
and
c(L)\in\mathbb{R}
. In particular, if one requires Hermiticity
preservation, trace preservation and complete positivity, the only
admissible choice is

\Xi(L,\rho)=-\frac{\gamma}{2}[L,[L,\rho]],\qquad\gamma\geq 0,

the Lindblad double commutator.

Proof.

(i)
\Rightarrow
(ii).

For
V=\mathfrak{su}(n)
with the adjoint–coupled structure,
(
42
) gives

K(L,\Phi(\rho))=2[L,\Phi(\rho)].

Thus, if
\Xi(L,\rho)=K(L,\Phi(\rho))
, we may define

T:=2\Phi
and obtain

\Xi(L,\rho)=[L,T(\rho)].

(ii)
\Rightarrow
(i).

Conversely, assume
\Xi(L,\rho)=[L,T(\rho)]
with
T

SU(n)
–equivariant. Setting
\Phi:=\tfrac{1}{2}T
yields

K(L,\Phi(\rho))=2[L,\Phi(\rho)]=[L,T(\rho)]=\Xi(L,\rho).

We now analyze the structure of
T
under the equivariance
condition (
44
). Fix
L
and consider the linear
map

\Xi_{L}:\mathfrak{su}(n)\to\mathfrak{su}(n),\qquad\Xi_{L}(\rho):=\Xi(L,\rho)=[L,T(\rho)].

Equation (
44
) implies that, for any
g
commuting
with
L
(i.e.
\operatorname{Ad}_{g}L=L
), one has

\Xi_{L}(\operatorname{Ad}_{g}\rho\,\operatorname{Ad}_{g^{-1}})=\operatorname{Ad}_{g}\,\Xi_{L}(\rho)\,\operatorname{Ad}_{g^{-1}}.

Thus
\Xi_{L}
is an intertwiner of the adjoint representation restricted
to the isotropy subgroup

Z(L):=\{g\in SU(n)\mid\operatorname{Ad}_{g}L=L\}.

The image of
\Xi_{L}

\operatorname{Im}(\hbox{ad}_{L})

\Xi_{L}(\rho)=[L,T(\rho)]
is a commutator with
L

The subspace
\operatorname{Im}(\hbox{ad}_{L})

\mathbb{C}

Z(L)

On each such irreducible component, Schur’s lemma implies that any

Z(L)

In particular, the restriction of
T
to
\operatorname{Im}(\hbox{ad}_{L})

must act, on each irreducible block, as a scalar multiple of
\hbox{ad}_{L}
.
Therefore, for each fixed
L

such that

where
\rho_{\perp}
denotes the projection of
\rho

\operatorname{Im}(\hbox{ad}_{L})

[L,\rho_{\parallel}]=0

\Xi(L,\rho)=-\frac{\gamma}{2}[L,[L,\rho]],\qquad\gamma\geq 0,

which is precisely the Lindblad double–commutator dissipator for a
single Hermitian channel.
∎

6.2
Uniqueness of ACSP–compatible dissipators

We now show that, under the structural assumptions of Euler–Poincaré
reduction on an ACSP, the Lindblad double commutator is the
only

admissible dissipative term.

Theorem 6.3
.

Let
G=SU(n)
and let
V=\mathfrak{g}^{\oplus m}
carry the adjoint
action on each component. Let

\Xi(L,\rho)
be a bilinear map

\mathfrak{su}(n)\times\mathfrak{su}(n)\to\mathfrak{su}(n)
satisfying:

•

SU(n)
–equivariance under simultaneous conjugation;

•

trace preservation and Hermiticity preservation in
\rho
;

•

compatibility with the reduced Euler–Poincaré equation on the
ACSP
(G,V)
, i.e.
\Xi
arises from the

\delta\ell/\delta\alpha\diamond\alpha
term for some reduced
Lagrangian
\ell
.

Then

\Xi(L,\rho)=-\frac{\gamma}{2}\sum_{k=1}^{m}[L_{k},[L_{k},\rho]],\qquad\gamma\geq 0,

where
L=(L_{1},\dots,L_{m})\in V
collects the Hermitian Lindblad generators.
In particular, for a single channel
m=1
one has

\Xi(L,\rho)=-\frac{\gamma}{2}[L,[L,\rho]].

Proof.

For
V=\mathfrak{g}^{\oplus m}
, the diamond operator is given by
(
43
),
\displaystyle{v\diamond\alpha=\sum_{k=1}^{m}[v_{k},\alpha_{k}]}
. In the reduced Euler–Poincaré equation the dissipative contribution (after identifying
\mu\leftrightarrow\rho

and
\delta\ell/\delta\alpha\leftrightarrow v
) is

\Xi(L,\rho)=v(L)\diamond\alpha(\rho)=\sum_{k=1}^{m}[L_{k},\alpha_{k}(\rho)],

where each
\alpha_{k}(\rho)\in\mathfrak{su}(n)
is linear in
\rho
.

Bilinearity of
\Xi
in
(L,\rho)
and
SU(n)
–equivariance under
simultaneous conjugation imply that each
\alpha_{k}(\rho)
must transform
covariantly under the adjoint action and, for fixed
L_{k}
, must be a
linear combination of terms of the form
[L_{k},\rho]
and
\rho
.
However, the component proportional to
\rho
drops out when inserted
into a commutator with
L_{k}
that already appears as
[L_{k},\cdot]
in

\Xi
, up to terms commuting with
L_{k}
. Thus, without loss of
generality (and up to components in
\ker\hbox{ad}_{L_{k}}
which do not
contribute), we may write

\alpha_{k}(\rho)=K_{k}[L_{k},\rho],\qquad K_{k}\in\mathbb{R}.

Substituting into the ACSP expression for
\Xi
gives

\Xi(L,\rho)=\sum_{k=1}^{m}[L_{k},\alpha_{k}(\rho)]=\sum_{k=1}^{m}K_{k}[L_{k},[L_{k},\rho]].

Trace preservation is automatic by cyclicity of the trace. Hermiticity
preservation requires that each
L_{k}
be Hermitian and that

K_{k}\in\mathbb{R}
. Finally, complete positivity of the associated
generator forces
K_{k}=-\gamma_{k}/2
with
\gamma_{k}\geq 0
, yielding

\Xi(L,\rho)=-\sum_{k=1}^{m}\frac{\gamma_{k}}{2}[L_{k},[L_{k},\rho]],

which is exactly the multi–channel GKSL dissipator of
Section
4
. For a single channel

m=1
this reduces to

\Xi(L,\rho)=-\frac{\gamma}{2}[L,[L,\rho]].

∎

Remark 6.4

(Geometric necessity of the Lindblad form)
.

The uniqueness theorem shows that once the assumptions of
SU
(n)
–equivariance, bilinearity, trace preservation,
and compatibility with the adjoint torsion are imposed,
no freedom remains in the dissipative structure:
the only admissible curvature–driven operator on
\mathfrak{g}^{*}
is
the Lindblad double commutator.
Thus, within the ACSP framework, the algebraic form of
GKSL dissipation is not one possibility among many but
the
only
geometrically consistent contraction mechanism.

6.3
Metric contraction toward the isotropy of
L

We now describe the contraction properties of the double–commutator
dissipator in the Hilbert–Schmidt geometry.

Proposition 6.5
.

Let
G=SU(n)
, let
\rho
be a density operator on
\mathbb{C}^{n}
, and
let
L\in\mathfrak{su}(n)
be Hermitian. Consider the dissipative
vector field

\dot{\rho}_{\rm diss}=-\frac{\gamma}{2}[L,[L,\rho]].

Then:

(i)

\dot{\rho}_{\rm diss}
preserves trace and Hermiticity.

(ii)

Writing
\rho=\rho_{\parallel}+\rho_{\perp}
, where

\rho_{\parallel}
commutes with
L
and
\rho_{\perp}
is
orthogonal to
\ker\hbox{ad}_{L}
with respect to

\langle\cdot,\cdot\rangle_{\mathrm{HS}}
, one has the strict
contraction identity

\frac{d}{dt}\|\rho_{\perp}\|_{\mathrm{HS}}^{2}=-\gamma\,\|[L,\rho_{\perp}]\|_{\mathrm{HS}}^{2}\leq 0.

(iii)

Equality in
(ii)
holds if and only if
\rho
commutes with
L
.

Thus the dissipator generates a transverse contraction toward the fixed
manifold
\{\rho\mid[L,\rho]=0\}
, geometrically corresponding to
collapse onto the coadjoint isotropy of
L
.

Proof.

(i) Cyclicity of the trace implies

\Tr[L,[L,\rho]]=\Tr([L,L\rho-\rho L])=0.

Thus
\Tr\dot{\rho}_{\rm diss}=0
. If
\rho^{\dagger}=\rho
and

L^{\dagger}=L
, then

[L,[L,\rho]]^{\dagger}=[L,[L,\rho]],

so
\dot{\rho}_{\rm diss}
remains Hermitian.

(ii) Decompose
\rho
orthogonally as

\rho=\rho_{\parallel}+\rho_{\perp},

with

\rho_{\parallel}\in\ker\hbox{ad}_{L}

and

\rho_{\perp}\in(\ker\hbox{ad}_{L})^{\perp}

relative to
\langle\cdot,\cdot\rangle_{\mathrm{HS}}
. Then

[L,\rho_{\parallel}]=0
, so only
\rho_{\perp}
contributes to the
dissipator. Using linearity and the orthogonality of the decomposition,

\dot{\rho}_{\perp}=-\frac{\gamma}{2}[L,[L,\rho_{\perp}]].

We now use the basic identity

\langle A,[L,[L,A]]\rangle_{\mathrm{HS}}=\|[L,A]\|_{\mathrm{HS}}^{2},\qquad A\in\mathcal{B}(\mathbb{C}^{n}),

(45)

which follows from the
\operatorname{Ad}
–invariance of the Hilbert–Schmidt
pairing. Indeed, invariance gives

\langle A,[L,B]\rangle_{\mathrm{HS}}=\langle[A,L],B\rangle_{\mathrm{HS}}=-\langle[L,A],B\rangle_{\mathrm{HS}}

for all
A,B
. Taking
B=[L,A]
we obtain

\langle A,[L,[L,A]]\rangle_{\mathrm{HS}}=-\langle[L,A],[L,A]\rangle_{\mathrm{HS}}=\|[L,A]\|_{\mathrm{HS}}^{2},

since
[L,A]
is anti–Hermitian.

Differentiating the squared norm of
\rho_{\perp}
,

\frac{d}{dt}\|\rho_{\perp}\|_{\mathrm{HS}}^{2}=2\langle\rho_{\perp},\dot{\rho}_{\perp}\rangle_{\mathrm{HS}}=2\left\langle\rho_{\perp},-\frac{\gamma}{2}[L,[L,\rho_{\perp}]]\right\rangle_{\mathrm{HS}}=-\gamma\langle\rho_{\perp},[L,[L,\rho_{\perp}]]\rangle_{\mathrm{HS}},

and applying (
45
) with
A=\rho_{\perp}
yields

\frac{d}{dt}\|\rho_{\perp}\|_{\mathrm{HS}}^{2}=-\gamma\,\|[L,\rho_{\perp}]\|_{\mathrm{HS}}^{2}\leq 0.

(iii) The right–hand side vanishes if and only if
[L,\rho_{\perp}]=0
.
But
\rho_{\perp}
is orthogonal to
\ker\hbox{ad}_{L}
, so

[L,\rho_{\perp}]=0
implies
\rho_{\perp}=0
. Hence

\rho=\rho_{\parallel}
and
[L,\rho]=0
.
∎

Proposition
6.5
shows that the
double–commutator dissipator is a
metric gradient
–type
contraction transverse to the coadjoint orbits of
SU(n)
, while
preserving the orbit itself. This is exactly the dissipative part of
the metriplectic decomposition discussed in
Section
8
.

When does a classical semidirect product produce quantum
dissipation?

The results above highlight the key structural difference between a
classical semidirect product
G\ltimes V
and an adjoint–coupled
semidirect product.

In the classical setting,
V
carries only a
left
infinitesimal
action
\xi\cdot v
with
\xi\in\mathfrak{g},\ v\in V
, and the Euler–Poincaré equations on
G\ltimes V
involve only this
single action. There is no canonical
right
action
v\cdot\xi

available, so it is not possible to form the antisymmetric combination

\xi\cdot v-v\cdot\xi
. In other words, the torsion tensor
K
is

not defined
in the purely classical semidirect–product
framework.

By contrast, in an adjoint–coupled semidirect product we explicitly
equip
V
with both left and right infinitesimal actions, and define
K(\xi,v)=\xi\cdot v-v\cdot\xi
. For
V
built from adjoint modules this torsion is nonzero and, as we
have seen, it feeds into the
\diamond
–term of the reduced
Euler–Poincaré equation to produce quadratic (double–commutator)
dissipation. This leads to the following structural criterion.

Proposition 6.6
.

A classical semidirect product
G\ltimes V
can generate a quadratic
double–commutator dissipator of Lindblad type via Euler–Poincaré
reduction if and only if its action extends to an adjoint–coupled
structure
(G,V)
with a nontrivial torsion tensor
K\neq 0
.

Proof.

Necessity:

Suppose we only have the classical semidirect–product action, with
infinitesimal coupling given by a left action
\xi\cdot v
. In this
setting there is no canonical right action
v\cdot\xi
, and therefore no
well–defined torsion tensor
K(\xi,v)
. The reduced Euler–Poincaré
equations can only involve terms constructed from
\xi\cdot v
and its
dual
\diamond
coupling. These produce, at most,
single

commutators (or their classical analogues), but not quadratic curvature
terms of the form
[L,[L,\rho]]
. Hence a nontrivial torsion tensor is
necessary for the appearance of double–commutator dissipation.

Sufficiency:

Conversely, assume the classical action extends to an adjoint–coupled
structure, so that both left and right actions are defined and

K(\xi,v)=\xi\cdot v-v\cdot\xi
is nonzero. Then, for
V
a direct
sum of adjoint modules,
K(\xi,v)
contains a commutator in each
component (cf. (
42
)), and, as shown, the
\diamond
–term in the
reduced Euler–Poincaré equation produces operators of the form

\Xi(L,\rho)=[L,\Phi(\rho)]

for suitable equivariant linear maps
\Phi
. By
Theorem
6.2
and
Theorem
6.3
, any such bilinear, equivariant,
trace–preserving, Hermiticity–preserving operator arising from torsion
must be a (possibly multi–channel) Lindblad double commutator,

\Xi(L,\rho)=-\frac{\gamma}{2}[L,[L,\rho]],\qquad\gamma\geq 0.

Thus a nontrivial torsion tensor is also sufficient for the appearance
of Lindblad–type double–commutator dissipation.
∎

In summary, the adjoint–coupled structure is not a cosmetic extension
of the classical semidirect product: it is the
minimal

representation–theoretic enhancement required to encode quantum
dissipative curvature within the Euler–Poincaré framework. Classical
semidirect products become
quantum–compatible
precisely when
their actions extend to adjoint–coupled representations with
K\neq 0
.

Example 6.7

(The
SU(2)
case in Bloch coordinates)
.

Let
G=SU(2)
and
V=\mathfrak{su}(2)
with the adjoint–coupled
structure. Using the Pauli representation from
Section
3
, every element of
\mathfrak{su}(2)
can
be written as

X=-\frac{i}{2}\,\mathbf{x}\cdot\boldsymbol{\sigma},\qquad\mathbf{x}\in\mathbb{R}^{3},

and the Lie bracket corresponds to the vector cross product,

[X,Y]=-\frac{i}{2}(\mathbf{x}\times\mathbf{y})\cdot\boldsymbol{\sigma}.

The torsion tensor becomes

K(\xi,v)=2[\xi,v]\quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad 2\bigl(\boldsymbol{\xi}\times\mathbf{v}\bigr),

and the diamond operator is simply

v\diamond\alpha=[v,\alpha]\quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad\mathbf{v}\times\boldsymbol{\alpha}.

Let

L=\frac{1}{2}\boldsymbol{\ell}\cdot\boldsymbol{\sigma},\qquad\rho=\frac{1}{2}\bigl(I+\mathbf{r}\cdot\boldsymbol{\sigma}\bigr),

with
\|\boldsymbol{\ell}\|=1
. Then the GKSL dissipator

D_{L}(\rho)=-\frac{\gamma}{2}[L,[L,\rho]]

induces, in Bloch coordinates, the evolution

\dot{\mathbf{r}}=-2\gamma\bigl(\mathbf{r}-(\mathbf{r}\cdot\boldsymbol{\ell})\boldsymbol{\ell}\bigr)=2\gamma\,\boldsymbol{\ell}\times(\boldsymbol{\ell}\times\mathbf{r}),

where the second equality uses the vector identity

\boldsymbol{\ell}\times(\boldsymbol{\ell}\times\mathbf{r})=\boldsymbol{\ell}(\boldsymbol{\ell}\cdot\mathbf{r})-\mathbf{r}.

Thus the double commutator produces a rigid linear contraction of the
Bloch vector toward the axis determined by
\boldsymbol{\ell}
, while
preserving the longitudinal component.

From the ACSP viewpoint, this is precisely the effect of the torsion
and the diamond operator:
K(L,\cdot)
supplies one commutator (hence a
cross product), and the choice of advected variable

\alpha(\rho)\propto[\rho,L]
supplies the second. The resulting term

L\diamond\alpha
is the Lindblad double commutator, whose Bloch
representation is the directional contraction

\mathbf{r}\mapsto\boldsymbol{\ell}\times(\boldsymbol{\ell}\times\mathbf{r})
.
This example makes completely explicit how adjoint torsion, the diamond
operator, and Euler–Poincaré reduction combine to produce the standard
geometric picture of decoherence on the Bloch ball.

7
Reduction for Finite-Dimensional Quantum Systems

The adjoint–coupled semidirect product (ACSP) construction developed
for
SU(2)
extends directly to arbitrary finite-dimensional quantum
systems.
The key observation is that the algebraic ingredients responsible for
Lindblad dissipation—commutator torsion,
\operatorname{Ad}
–invariant pairings,
and the semidirect Euler–Poincaré coupling via the
\diamond

operator—are independent of the dimension of the Hilbert space.
Thus, the same ACSP mechanism that produced the GKSL generator for

SU(2)
yields, without modification, the full GKSL structure for

SU(n)
.

Let
G=SU(n)
with Lie algebra
\mathfrak{g}=\mathfrak{su}(n)
.
We equip
\mathfrak{g}
with the
\operatorname{Ad}
–invariant inner product
\langle X,Y\rangle_{\mathfrak{g}}:=-\Tr(XY)
, with
X,Y\in\mathfrak{su}(n)
, and identify
\mathfrak{g}\cong\mathfrak{g}^{*}
via this pairing.
For
m\geq 1
we take as representation space
V:=\mathfrak{g}^{\oplus m}=\mathfrak{g}\oplus\cdots\oplus\mathfrak{g}
, with elements
v=(v_{1},\ldots,v_{m})
,
v_{k}\in\mathfrak{g}
.
Each copy carries the adjoint representation of
G
,
g\cdot v_{k}:=\operatorname{Ad}_{g}v_{k}
with
g\in G
, and we use the diagonal action on
V
,

g\cdot(v_{1},\dots,v_{m}):=(\operatorname{Ad}_{g}v_{1},\dots,\operatorname{Ad}_{g}v_{m})
.

The infinitesimal left action for
\xi\in\mathfrak{g}
is
\xi\cdot(v_{1},\dots,v_{m})=([\xi,v_{1}],\dots,[\xi,v_{m}])
, and the corresponding right action is
(v_{1},\dots,v_{m})\cdot\xi=-([\xi,v_{1}],\dots,[\xi,v_{m}])
. The
adjoint torsion

K:\mathfrak{g}\times V\to V
is

K(\xi,v):=\xi\cdot v-v\cdot\xi=2\,([\xi,v_{1}],\dots,[\xi,v_{m}]).

The pairing on
V
is the direct sum of the pairings on each component,

\langle\alpha,v\rangle_{V}:=\sum_{k=1}^{m}\langle\alpha_{k},v_{k}\rangle_{\mathfrak{g}},\qquad\alpha=(\alpha_{1},\dots,\alpha_{m})\in V^{*}\cong V.

For the left adjoint action, the
\diamond
operator,

\diamond:V\times V^{*}\to\mathfrak{g}^{*}
, is defined by
\langle v\diamond\alpha,\xi\rangle_{\mathfrak{g}}=-\langle\alpha,\xi\cdot v\rangle_{V}
for
\xi\in\mathfrak{g}
, and, using the
\operatorname{Ad}
–invariance

\langle X,[Y,Z]\rangle_{\mathfrak{g}}=\langle[X,Y],Z\rangle_{\mathfrak{g}}
,
one obtains (cf. (
7
))

v\diamond\alpha=\sum_{k=1}^{m}[v_{k},\alpha_{k}],\qquad v=(v_{k}),\ \alpha=(\alpha_{k})\in V\cong V^{*}.

(46)

Thus, in
SU(n)
, the
\diamond
operator again produces a
single commutator in each adjoint component of
V
.

Let
L:T(G\ltimes V)\to\mathbb{R}
be a left-invariant Lagrangian. Using left trivialization, the reduced Lagrangian is a function
\ell(\xi,v,\alpha)
, with
(\xi,v,\alpha)\in\mathfrak{g}\times V\times V^{*}
, and we denote the variational derivatives

\mu:=\frac{\delta\ell}{\delta\xi}\in\mathfrak{g}^{*},\qquad\beta:=\frac{\delta\ell}{\delta v}\in V^{*}.

Writing
\beta=(\beta^{(1)},\dots,\beta^{(m)})
and

\alpha=(\alpha^{(1)},\dots,\alpha^{(m)})
with

\beta^{(k)},\alpha^{(k)}\in\mathfrak{g}^{*}
, the reduced
Euler–Poincaré equations on
G\ltimes V
are

\dot{\mu}+\operatorname{ad}^{*}_{\xi}\mu=\beta\diamond\alpha,\qquad\beta\diamond\alpha=\sum_{k=1}^{m}\beta^{(k)}\diamond\alpha^{(k)},

(47)

together with the advection equations

\dot{\alpha}^{(k)}+\xi\cdot\alpha^{(k)}=0,\qquad k=1,\dots,m.

Under the
\operatorname{Ad}
–invariant identification
\mathfrak{g}\cong\mathfrak{g}^{*}
,
the coadjoint action satisfies

\operatorname{ad}^{*}_{\xi}\mu=-[\xi,\mu]
, and the componentwise

\diamond
term reduces to
(cf. (
46
))

\beta\diamond\alpha=\sum_{k=1}^{m}[\beta^{(k)},\alpha^{(k)}].

(48)

To connect with quantum dynamics, we interpret
\mu
as a density
operator and
\xi
as the (skew-Hermitian) Hamiltonian generator:

\mu\ \leftrightarrow\ \rho,\qquad\xi=-iH,\quad H=H^{\dagger}.

Then, using
\operatorname{ad}^{*}_{\xi}\mu=-[\xi,\mu]
, the left-hand
side of (
47
) becomes

\dot{\rho}+\operatorname{ad}^{*}_{-iH}\rho=\dot{\rho}-[-iH,\rho]=\dot{\rho}+i[H,\rho],

so the Euler–Poincaré equation reads

\dot{\rho}=-i[H,\rho]+\beta\diamond\alpha.

(49)

We now choose the reduced Lagrangian so that the
V
–momentum
components
\beta^{(k)}
coincide with a prescribed family of Hermitian
Lindblad operators:

\beta^{(k)}=\frac{\delta\ell}{\delta v_{k}}=L_{k},\qquad L_{k}=L_{k}^{\dagger}\in\mathfrak{su}(n).

Since
V^{*}\cong V
, we can regard each
L_{k}
as an element of
V
and
insert it into the
\diamond
term.

To generate the GKSL dissipator we impose an ACSP–compatible ansatz
for the advected variables

\alpha^{(k)}(\rho)=\frac{\gamma_{k}}{2}\,[\rho,L_{k}],\qquad\gamma_{k}\geq 0.

By construction, each
\alpha^{(k)}
depends linearly and
equivariantly on
\rho
, and transforms in the adjoint representation.
Using
[\rho,L_{k}]=-[L_{k},\rho]
and
(
48
), we compute

\beta\diamond\alpha=\sum_{k=1}^{m}[L_{k},\alpha^{(k)}]=\sum_{k=1}^{m}\frac{\gamma_{k}}{2}\,[L_{k},[\rho,L_{k}]]=-\sum_{k=1}^{m}\frac{\gamma_{k}}{2}\,[L_{k},[L_{k},\rho]].

Substituting into (
49
) yields

\dot{\rho}=-i[H,\rho]-\sum_{k=1}^{m}\frac{\gamma_{k}}{2}[L_{k},[L_{k},\rho]],

(50)

which is exactly the GKSL generator for multiple Hermitian Lindblad
channels. So, we have shown the following result:

Theorem 7.1
.

Let
\mathcal{H}
be a finite dimensional Hilbet space,
\mathcal{H}\simeq\mathbb{C}^{n}
and
G=SU(n)
.
For any family of Hermitian Lindblad operators

L_{k}=L_{k}^{\dagger}\in\mathfrak{su}(n)
and rates
\gamma_{k}\geq 0
, the
GKSL master equation

\dot{\rho}=-i[H,\rho]-\sum_{k=1}^{m}\frac{\gamma_{k}}{2}[L_{k},[L_{k},\rho]]

arises from Euler–Poincaré reduction on the adjoint–coupled
semidirect product
SU(n)\ltimes\mathfrak{su}(n)^{\oplus m}
by choosing
the reduced Lagrangian such that

\delta\ell/\delta v_{k}=L_{k}
and the advected variables

\alpha^{(k)}=(\gamma_{k}/2)[\rho,L_{k}]
.
Conversely, any dissipative term generated by the ACSP

\diamond
–coupling under these structural assumptions is of
Lindblad double–commutator form.

Remark 7.2
.

The “converse” part follows from the structural classification
developed in Section
6
: under bilinearity,

SU(n)
–equivariance, trace and Hermiticity preservation, and ACSP
compatibility, Theorem
6.2
and
Theorem
6.3
imply that the only admissible
quadratic dissipator is the Lindblad double commutator.
\diamond

The general GKSL form for a finite-dimensional quantum system is

\dot{\rho}=-i[H,\rho]+\sum_{\alpha}\bigl(L_{\alpha}\rho L_{\alpha}^{\dagger}-\tfrac{1}{2}\{L_{\alpha}^{\dagger}L_{\alpha},\rho\}\bigr),

where the Lindblad operators
L_{\alpha}
need not be Hermitian or
traceless. Each non-Hermitian channel

L_{\alpha}\in\mathcal{B}(\mathcal{H})
admits the Cartesian decomposition

L_{\alpha}=A_{\alpha}+iB_{\alpha},\qquad A_{\alpha},B_{\alpha}=A_{\alpha}^{\dagger},B_{\alpha}^{\dagger}.

Projecting onto
\mathfrak{su}(n)
separates out the scalar (identity)
part, which contributes only to Hamiltonian renormalization and not to
dissipation.

As shown in Remark
4.5
on non-Hermitian channels, the GKSL dissipator can be
rewritten as

D_{L_{\alpha}}(\rho)=-\frac{1}{2}[A_{\alpha},[A_{\alpha},\rho]]-\frac{1}{2}[B_{\alpha},[B_{\alpha},\rho]]-\frac{i}{2}[A_{\alpha},\{B_{\alpha},\rho\}]+\frac{i}{2}[B_{\alpha},\{A_{\alpha},\rho\}].

The first two terms are of pure double–commutator type and are
therefore generated by the ACSP
\diamond
–coupling applied to the
Hermitian channels
A_{\alpha}
and
B_{\alpha}
(each regarded as a
component in
V
with an appropriate advected variable).
The mixed terms involving anticommutators can be reorganized as
commutators with
\rho
and absorbed into the Hamiltonian sector as
Lamb-shift corrections.

Thus, at the structural level, the adjoint–coupled semidirect product
encodes the genuinely dissipative part of the GKSL generator—the
double–commutator contributions—while Hamiltonian renormalizations
are carried by the coadjoint term in the Euler–Poincaré equation.
The ACSP mechanism therefore captures the full GKSL structure once both
Hermitian and non-Hermitian channels are decomposed into their
Hermitian components.

Example 7.3

(Qutrit dephasing in
SU(3)
)
.

We conclude with a simple but nontrivial example illustrating the
dimension–independent nature of the construction.

Let
\mathcal{H}=\mathbb{C}^{3}
and
G=SU(3)
, and consider pure
dephasing in the computational basis.
A convenient choice of commuting Hermitian Lindblad operators is

L_{1}=\lambda_{3},\qquad L_{2}=\lambda_{8},

where
\lambda_{3},\lambda_{8}
are the standard diagonal Gell–Mann
matrices.
Take rates
\gamma_{1},\gamma_{2}\geq 0
and consider the GKSL equation

\dot{\rho}=-i[H,\rho]-\frac{\gamma_{1}}{2}[L_{1},[L_{1},\rho]]-\frac{\gamma_{2}}{2}[L_{2},[L_{2},\rho]].

In the ACSP picture, we choose

V=\mathfrak{su}(3)\oplus\mathfrak{su}(3),\qquad\beta^{(1)}=\frac{\delta\ell}{\delta v_{1}}=L_{1},\quad\beta^{(2)}=\frac{\delta\ell}{\delta v_{2}}=L_{2},

and advected variables

\alpha^{(1)}=\frac{\gamma_{1}}{2}[\rho,L_{1}],\qquad\alpha^{(2)}=\frac{\gamma_{2}}{2}[\rho,L_{2}].

Then, by (
48
),

\beta\diamond\alpha=[L_{1},\alpha^{(1)}]+[L_{2},\alpha^{(2)}]=-\frac{\gamma_{1}}{2}[L_{1},[L_{1},\rho]]-\frac{\gamma_{2}}{2}[L_{2},[L_{2},\rho]],

so the Euler–Poincaré equation (
49
)
reproduces exactly the above GKSL dephasing master equation.

Geometrically, each double commutator
[L_{j},[L_{j},\rho]]
contracts the
components of
\rho
that do not commute with
L_{j}
(in particular, the
off-diagonal entries in the eigenbasis of
L_{j}
), while leaving the
joint eigenspaces of
L_{1}
and
L_{2}
invariant.
The fixed-point set of the dissipator is precisely the commutant of

\{L_{1},L_{2}\}
, a block-diagonal algebra reflecting the decoherence into
classical mixtures of the joint eigenstates.
From the ACSP viewpoint, this contraction is nothing but the metric
gradient effect of the
\diamond
–coupling, acting transversely to the

SU(3)
coadjoint orbits, in complete analogy with the qubit case
discussed earlier.

To quantify the strength of the ACSP–induced contraction, it is useful to
obtain explicit analytic bounds on the double commutator

[L,[L,\rho]]
in the Hilbert–Schmidt geometry.
The following lemma provides a sharp estimate that relates the curvature
operator to the norms of
L
and
\rho
and characterizes precisely when the
bound is saturated.
This establishes a clean geometric measure of maximal decoherence.

Lemma 7.4
.

Let
G=\mathrm{SU}(n)
and let
L\in\mathfrak{su}(n)
be Hermitian.
For any
\rho\in\mathfrak{su}(n)
, the double commutator satisfies the bound

\|[L,[L,\rho]]\|_{\mathrm{HS}}\;\leq\;2\,\|L\|_{\mathrm{HS}}^{2}\,\|\rho\|_{\mathrm{HS}},

and equality holds if and only if
L
and
\rho
have orthogonal
Hilbert–Schmidt support, i.e.
\Tr(L\rho)=0
and

[L,\rho]
attains maximal norm.

Proof.

Using
\operatorname{Ad}
–invariance of the Hilbert–Schmidt pairing,

\langle A,[L,B]\rangle_{\mathrm{HS}}=\langle[A,L],B\rangle_{\mathrm{HS}}\qquad\text{for all }A,B\in\mathfrak{su}(n),

we compute

\|[L,[L,\rho]]\|_{\mathrm{HS}}=\sup_{\|X\|_{\mathrm{HS}}=1}\langle X,[L,[L,\rho]]\rangle_{\mathrm{HS}}=\sup_{\|X\|=1}\langle[X,L],[L,\rho]\rangle_{\mathrm{HS}}.

Applying Cauchy–Schwarz,

|\langle[X,L],[L,\rho]\rangle_{\mathrm{HS}}|\leq\|[X,L]\|_{\mathrm{HS}}\;\|[L,\rho]\|_{\mathrm{HS}}.

Since
\mathrm{ad}_{L}
is a skew–Hermitian operator with

\|[X,L]\|_{\mathrm{HS}}\leq 2\|L\|_{\mathrm{HS}}\|X\|_{\mathrm{HS}}
, we obtain

\|[L,[L,\rho]]\|_{\mathrm{HS}}\leq 2\|L\|_{\mathrm{HS}}\;\|[L,\rho]\|_{\mathrm{HS}}.

Applying the same bound to
[L,\rho]
yields

\|[L,\rho]\|_{\mathrm{HS}}\leq 2\,\|L\|_{\mathrm{HS}}\,\|\rho\|_{\mathrm{HS}},

and combining the two inequalities proves

\|[L,[L,\rho]]\|_{\mathrm{HS}}\leq 2\,\|L\|_{\mathrm{HS}}^{2}\,\|\rho\|_{\mathrm{HS}}.

For equality to hold in both applications of Cauchy–Schwarz,

[X,L]
must be proportional to
[L,\rho]
, and
[L,\rho]
must attain its
maximal possible Hilbert–Schmidt norm.
The latter occurs exactly when
L
and
\rho
have orthogonal support in the
Hilbert–Schmidt sense (i.e.
\Tr(L\rho)=0
), which characterizes maximal
decoherence.
∎

In summary, the ACSP reduction on
SU(n)\ltimes\mathfrak{su}(n)^{\oplus m}

provides a unified geometric mechanism for finite-dimensional open
quantum systems: Hamiltonian dynamics is realized as coadjoint motion on

SU(n)
orbits, and GKSL dissipation is encoded by the adjoint torsion
via the
\diamond
operator, independently of the dimension
n
.

8
Metriplectic and Double–Bracket Structure of ACSP Dissipation

The Euler–Poincaré formulation on the adjoint–coupled semidirect
product (ACSP)
G\ltimes V
provides a natural geometric mechanism for
dissipative quantum dynamics.
In this section we show that the resulting structure is

metriplectic
in the sense of
Morrison–Grmela
[
25
,
17
,
26
]
, and that
it is the precise quantum analogue of the
double-bracket
dissipation
formalism of Bloch, Krishnaprasad, Marsden and
Ratiu
[
8
]
, familiar from plasma
physics, continuum mechanics, spin systems, and control theory.

The starting point is the ACSP uniqueness result of
Section
6
: under natural structural assumptions
(bilinearity,
SU(n)
–equivariance, trace and Hermiticity preservation,
and compatibility with the ACSP
\diamond
–operator), the dissipative
term in the reduced Euler–Poincaré equation must take the Lindblad
double–commutator form

\Xi(L,\rho)=-\frac{\gamma}{2}[L,[L,\rho]],\qquad\gamma\geq 0.

We now show that this term defines a symmetric, negative semidefinite
bracket, and that the full GKSL generator is naturally decomposed into a
Poisson (Hamiltonian) part plus this metric (double–bracket) part.

A
metriplectic system
on a phase space
\mathcal{M}
consists of
a Poisson bracket
\{\cdot,\cdot\}
and a symmetric, positive
semidefinite bracket
(\cdot,\cdot)
, together with two functionals:
the Hamiltonian
H
and the entropy
S
.
The evolution of any observable
F:\mathcal{M}\to\mathbb{R}
is given by

\dot{F}=\{F,H\}+(F,S),

(51)

with the compatibility conditions

\{F,S\}=0,\qquad(F,H)=0,

for all
F
.
These imply

\dot{H}=\{H,H\}+(H,S)=0,\qquad\dot{S}=\{S,H\}+(S,S)=(S,S)\geq 0,

so
H
is conserved while
S
increases monotonically (increasing in the
standard thermodynamic sign convention). Morrison and Grmela showed that many dissipative continuum systems
admit such a structure, with dissipation encoded by a
double
bracket
that contracts coadjoint orbits

[
25
,
17
,
26
]
.

In our quantum setting the role of
\mathcal{M}
is played by the space
of density operators on
\mathbb{C}^{n}
, endowed with the Hilbert–Schmidt
inner product

\langle A,B\rangle:=\Tr(A^{\dagger}B),\qquad A,B\in\mathcal{B}(\mathbb{C}^{n}),

and we identify
\mathfrak{su}(n)^{*}
with
\mathfrak{su}(n)
by

\langle A,B\rangle=-\Tr(AB)
for traceless Hermitian matrices.
The Lie–Poisson bracket on
\mathfrak{su}(n)^{*}
(identified with
density operators) is

\{F,H\}_{\rm LP}(\rho)=\Big\langle\rho,\big[\tfrac{\delta F}{\delta\rho},\tfrac{\delta H}{\delta\rho}\big]\Big\rangle,

(52)

which generates the Hamiltonian evolution
\dot{\rho}=-i[H,\rho]
when

H(\rho)=\Tr(H\rho)
and
\delta H/\delta\rho=-iH
.

Remark 8.1
.

There are by now several metriplectic or gradient-flow formulations of
Lindblad-type dynamics, often in the context of non-equilibrium
thermodynamics or noncommutative optimal transport; see, for example,
the entropy-based approach of Barbaresco
[
6
]

and the gradient-flow structure of symmetric quantum Markov semigroups
due to Carlen–Maas, Mittnenzweig–Mielke, and Wirth
(e.g.
[
11
,
24
,
30
]
).
In those works, a suitable metric or transport structure is

postulated
in the space of density operators to represent a given
Lindblad generator as a gradient flow of the von Neumann entropy or a relative entropy functional. The ACSP perspective is complementary: here, the metric bracket is not
assumed but rather
derived
variationally from Euler–Poincaré
reduction on an adjoint–coupled semidirect product
SU(n)\ltimes V
, and curvature encoded in the adjoint–coupled
construction. The torsion
K(\xi,v)=\xi\cdot v-v\cdot\xi
and the resulting double
commutator are exactly the Lie-theoretic ingredients that produce the
metric bracket
(\cdot,\cdot)_{\rm ACSP}
.
Thus the usual gradient-flow picture of Lindblad dynamics acquires a
concrete representation-theoretic origin.

\diamond

To encode the ACSP dissipative term, we introduce the
ACSP metric
bracket
associated with a fixed Hermitian Lindblad operator

L\in\mathfrak{su}(n)
:

(F,S)_{\mathrm{ACSP}}:=-\frac{\gamma}{2}\Big\langle[L,\tfrac{\delta F}{\delta\rho}],[L,\tfrac{\delta S}{\delta\rho}]\Big\rangle,

(53)

for sufficiently smooth functionals
F,S
of
\rho
.
Using the adjoint–invariance of the Hilbert–Schmidt pairing, this can
also be written as

(F,S)_{\mathrm{ACSP}}=-\frac{\gamma}{2}\Big\langle\frac{\delta F}{\delta\rho},[L,[L,\tfrac{\delta S}{\delta\rho}]]\Big\rangle.

(54)

The second form makes the double–commutator structure explicit, while
the first form is convenient for showing symmetry and negative
semidefiniteness.

Remark 8.2
.

The metric bracket induced by the ACSP torsion does not in general
annihilate the Hamiltonian functional; compatibility in the strict
Morrison–Grmela sense holds precisely when
[L,H]=0
.
Thus our use of the term “metriplec­tic” refers to the
generalized structure (Poisson + symmetric bracket) rather than the
fully compatible subclass.
We make this distinction explicit to avoid any confusion about the
strength of the claimed metriplectic properties.

Theorem 8.3

(ACSP metric bracket)
.

Let
G=SU(n)
and let
(\cdot,\cdot)_{\mathrm{ACSP}}
be given by

(F,S)_{\mathrm{ACSP}}:=-\frac{\gamma}{2}\Big\langle\frac{\delta F}{\delta\rho},[L,[L,\tfrac{\delta S}{\delta\rho}]]\Big\rangle,\qquad\langle A,B\rangle:=\Tr(A^{\dagger}B),

(55)

for real–valued functionals
F,S
on the space of density operators and a fixed
Hermitian
L=L^{\dagger}
. Then:

(i)

Symmetry:

(F,S)_{\mathrm{ACSP}}=(S,F)_{\mathrm{ACSP}}\quad\text{for all }F,S.

(ii)

Negative semidefiniteness:

(F,F)_{\mathrm{ACSP}}\leq 0,

with equality if and only if
[L,\delta F/\delta\rho]=0
.
Hence
-(\cdot,\cdot)_{\mathrm{ACSP}}
is a genuine metric bracket in the
sense of metriplectic theory (positive semidefinite symmetric bracket).

Proof.

For brevity, write

X:=\frac{\delta F}{\delta\rho},\qquad Y:=\frac{\delta S}{\delta\rho}.

Since
F
and
S
are real–valued functionals on the real vector space of
Hermitian matrices, their first variations satisfy

\delta F(\rho)[\delta\rho]=\langle X,\delta\rho\rangle=\Tr(X^{\dagger}\delta\rho)\in\mathbb{R}

for all Hermitian
\delta\rho
. This forces
X
itself to be Hermitian
(similarly for
Y
). Thus throughout the proof we may assume

X^{\dagger}=X
and
Y^{\dagger}=Y
.

Using the definition (
55
) and

L^{\dagger}=L
, we compute

(F,S)_{\mathrm{ACSP}}=-\frac{\gamma}{2}\,\Tr\big(X^{\dagger}[L,[L,Y]]\big).

The Hilbert–Schmidt pairing is invariant under the adjoint action:
for any
A,B
we have

\langle A,[L,B]\rangle=\Tr(A^{\dagger}[L,B])=\Tr([L,A]^{\dagger}B)=\langle[L,A],B\rangle.

Applying this identity twice, first with
(A,B)=(X,[L,Y])
, gives

\Tr\big(X^{\dagger}[L,[L,Y]]\big)=\langle X,[L,[L,Y]]\rangle=\langle[L,X],[L,Y]\rangle=\Tr\big([L,X]^{\dagger}[L,Y]\big).

Therefore

(F,S)_{\mathrm{ACSP}}=-\frac{\gamma}{2}\,\Tr\big(X^{\dagger}[L,[L,Y]]\big)=-\frac{\gamma}{2}\,\Tr\big([L,X]^{\dagger}[L,Y]\big).

(56)

Because
L,X,Y
are Hermitian,
[L,X]
and
[L,Y]
are anti–Hermitian,
so
[L,X]^{\dagger}=-[L,X]
and similarly for
Y
.

With the shorthand
A:=[L,X]
and
B:=[L,Y]
we have

(F,S)_{\mathrm{ACSP}}=-\frac{\gamma}{2}\,\Tr(A^{\dagger}B),\qquad(S,F)_{\mathrm{ACSP}}=-\frac{\gamma}{2}\,\Tr(B^{\dagger}A).

For any matrices
A,B
we have

\Tr(A^{\dagger}B)=\overline{\Tr(B^{\dagger}A)}.

In our setting
A
and
B
are anti–Hermitian (as commutators of
Hermitian matrices), so
iA
and
iB
are Hermitian and

\Tr(A^{\dagger}B)
is real. Thus

\Tr(A^{\dagger}B)=\Tr(B^{\dagger}A),

and hence

(F,S)_{\mathrm{ACSP}}=-\frac{\gamma}{2}\,\Tr(A^{\dagger}B)=-\frac{\gamma}{2}\,\Tr(B^{\dagger}A)=(S,F)_{\mathrm{ACSP}}.

This proves symmetry.

Setting
F=S
in (
56
) gives

(F,F)_{\mathrm{ACSP}}=-\frac{\gamma}{2}\,\Tr\big([L,X]^{\dagger}[L,X]\big)=-\frac{\gamma}{2}\,\|[L,X]\|_{\mathrm{HS}}^{2}\;\leq\;0,

since
\Tr(A^{\dagger}A)\geq 0
for all
A
and vanishes if and only if

A=0
.

Equality
(F,F)_{\mathrm{ACSP}}=0
holds iff

\|[L,X]\|_{\mathrm{HS}}^{2}=0\quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad[L,X]=0,

i.e., iff the functional gradient
X=\delta F/\delta\rho
commutes with

L
.

Putting these together,
(\cdot,\cdot)_{\mathrm{ACSP}}
is symmetric and
negative semidefinite, with
(F,F)_{\mathrm{ACSP}}=0
precisely on
gradients that lie in the commutant of
L
. Thus

-(\cdot,\cdot)_{\mathrm{ACSP}}
is a positive semidefinite symmetric
bracket, as required in metriplectic theory.
∎

Thus
(\cdot,\cdot)_{\mathrm{ACSP}}
satisfies the defining properties
of a metriplectic metric bracket up to an overall minus sign: the bracket is
symmetric and (after flipping the sign) positive semidefinite. It
remains to show that, for an appropriate choice of
S
, the bracket
reproduces the Lindblad double commutator.

Proposition 8.4

(GKSL dissipator as ACSP metric term)
.

Let

S(\rho):=\frac{1}{2}\,\Tr(\rho^{2}),

so that
\delta S/\delta\rho=\rho
with respect to the Hilbert–Schmidt
pairing.
Then the ACSP metric bracket (
54
) yields,
for any linear observable
F(\rho)=\Tr(A\rho)
,

(F,S)_{\mathrm{ACSP}}=\Tr\!\left(A\,\Big(-\frac{\gamma}{2}\,[L,[L,\rho]]\Big)\right).

Equivalently, the induced evolution of
\rho
is

\dot{\rho}_{\mathrm{diss}}=-\frac{\gamma}{2}[L,[L,\rho]].

Proof.

For
F(\rho)=\Tr(A\rho)
one has
\delta F/\delta\rho=A
, so by
(
54
)

(F,S)_{\mathrm{ACSP}}=-\frac{\gamma}{2}\,\Big\langle A,[L,[L,\rho]]\Big\rangle=-\frac{\gamma}{2}\,\Tr\big(A^{\dagger}[L,[L,\rho]]\big).

For Hermitian
A
this reduces to

(F,S)_{\mathrm{ACSP}}=\Tr\!\left(A\,\Big(-\frac{\gamma}{2}\,[L,[L,\rho]]\Big)\right).

Since this holds for all linear observables, the corresponding
dissipative vector field on the space of density operators is

\dot{\rho}_{\mathrm{diss}}=-\frac{\gamma}{2}[L,[L,\rho]].

∎

Note that
S(\rho)=\tfrac{1}{2}\Tr(\rho^{2})
is (up to sign) the purity; it

decreases
along the dissipative flow:

\dot{S}=(S,S)_{\mathrm{ACSP}}=-\frac{\gamma}{2}\,\|[L,\rho]\|^{2}\leq 0,

in agreement with Proposition
6.5
. Thus,
in our convention, the metric bracket drives the system down the
gradient of a quadratic Lyapunov functional rather than up an entropy;
this is only a choice of sign and is standard in double–bracket
mechanics.

Combining the Lie–Poisson bracket (
52
) with the
ACSP metric bracket (
53
) we obtain:

Theorem 8.5

(Metriplectic formulation of Hermitian GKSL)
.

Let
H(\rho)=\operatorname{Tr}(H\rho)
be a Hermitian Hamiltonian functional and let

S(\rho)=\tfrac{1}{2}\operatorname{Tr}(\rho^{2})

be the quadratic Lyapunov functional. Then the metriplectic evolution

\dot{F}(\rho)=\{F,H\}_{\mathrm{LP}}(\rho)+(F,S)_{\mathrm{ACSP}}(\rho)

generates the Lindblad master equation with Hermitian noise operator
L
,

\dot{\rho}=-i[H,\rho]-\tfrac{\gamma}{2}[L,[L,\rho]].

Moreover,

\dot{S}=(S,S)_{\mathrm{ACSP}}=-\tfrac{\gamma}{2}\,\|[L,\rho]\|^{2}\leq 0,

so that
S
is a strict Lyapunov function for the dissipative dynamics. In general one does not have

\dot{H}=0
; energy conservation holds only when the compatibility condition

[L,H]=0

is satisfied, in which case the standard metriplectic relation

(F,H)_{\mathrm{ACSP}}=0

follows.

Proof.

Let
F
be arbitrary. Then
\displaystyle{\dot{F}(\rho)=\Big\langle\frac{\delta F}{\delta\rho},\,\dot{\rho}\Big\rangle}
.

By definition of the Lie–Poisson bracket,

\{F,H\}_{\rm LP}=\left\langle\frac{\delta F}{\delta\rho},-i[H,\rho]\right\rangle.

Since
F
is arbitrary, the corresponding contribution to
\dot{\rho}
must be

\dot{\rho}_{\rm Ham}=-i[H,\rho],

which is the usual coadjoint (unitary) evolution.

By Theorem
8.3
,
the ACSP metric bracket satisfies

(F,S)_{\mathrm{ACSP}}=-\frac{\gamma}{2}\left\langle\frac{\delta F}{\delta\rho},[L,[L,\rho]]\right\rangle.

Again, since
F
is arbitrary, the only
\rho
–dynamics compatible with
this identity is

\dot{\rho}_{\rm diss}=-\frac{\gamma}{2}[L,[L,\rho]].

Combining the Hamiltonian and metric parts gives

\dot{\rho}=-i[H,\rho]-\frac{\gamma}{2}[L,[L,\rho]],

the GKSL equation.

Since
\{H,H\}_{\mathrm{LP}}=0
always holds, we obtain

\dot{H}=(H,S)_{\mathrm{ACSP}}.

In general this term does not vanish, because the symmetry of the metric
bracket does not imply
(H,S)_{\mathrm{ACSP}}=0
. The dissipative
contribution disappears exactly when
[L,H]=0
, which is the usual
compatibility condition in metriplectic systems.

Next,

\dot{S}=\{S,H\}_{\rm LP}+(S,S)_{\rm ACSP}=0+(S,S)_{\rm ACSP}.

By Theorem
8.3
,

(S,S)_{\rm ACSP}=-\frac{\gamma}{2}\,\|[L,\rho]\|^{2}\leq 0,

so that

\dot{S}=-\frac{\gamma}{2}\,\|[L,\rho]\|^{2}\leq 0,

which expresses monotonic decay of purity.

Thus, unless
[L,H]=0
, the Hamiltonian functional is not conserved by
the dissipative part of the dynamics. When
[L,H]=0
, the energy is
conserved and the system becomes fully metriplectic in the sense of
Morrison–Grmela (with the sign convention explained above).
∎

Thus ACSP reduction endows the GKSL equation with a natural metriplectic
structure: the commutator part is Lie–Poisson (Hamiltonian), while the
double commutator is a metric double bracket generated by the ACSP
torsion.

Relation to classical double–bracket dissipation

The double–bracket formalism of Bloch, Krishnaprasad, Marsden and Ratiu
(BKMR)
[
8
]
provides a geometric
mechanism for
selective decay
on Lie algebras and coadjoint
orbits.
Given a Lie algebra
\mathfrak{g}
with Lie–Poisson bracket

\{\cdot,\cdot\}_{\mathrm{LP}}
, a Hamiltonian
H:\mathfrak{g}^{*}\to\mathbb{R}

and a Casimir
C:\mathfrak{g}^{*}\to\mathbb{R}
, the BKMR equation reads

\dot{\mu}=\{\mu,H\}_{\mathrm{LP}}\;-\;\lambda\,\operatorname{ad}^{*}_{\frac{\delta C}{\delta\mu}}\!\left(\operatorname{ad}^{*}_{\frac{\delta C}{\delta\mu}}\mu\right),

(57)

with
\lambda>0
a dissipation parameter.
The first term is the Lie–Poisson Hamiltonian flow; the second is the

double–bracket
dissipation generated by
\delta C/\delta\mu
.

For matrix Lie algebras
\mathfrak{g}\subset\mathbb{C}^{n\times n}
,
identifying
\mu
with a matrix
M
and

\delta C/\delta\mu
with
\nabla C(M)
, and using

\operatorname{ad}^{*}_{X}M=[M,X]
, the dissipative part becomes

\operatorname{ad}^{*}_{\nabla C}\!\left(\operatorname{ad}^{*}_{\nabla C}M\right)=[M,[M,\nabla C(M)]],

so the BKMR evolution reduces to

\dot{M}=[M,\nabla H(M)]\;-\;\lambda\,[M,[M,\nabla C(M)]].

(58)

The double bracket
-\lambda[M,[M,\nabla C]]
is tangent to coadjoint
orbits (and therefore preserves the spectrum), decreases the Hamiltonian

H
monotonically, preserves the Casimir
C
exactly, and defines a
gradient flow on each coadjoint orbit with respect to a left-invariant
metric.
Consequently, the BKMR construction produces a strictly contractive,
entropy-increasing evolution on coadjoint orbits, the classical
mechanism known as
selective decay
.

The structural similarity with our GKSL dissipator

\dot{\rho}_{\rm diss}=-\frac{\gamma}{2}[L,[L,\rho]]

is immediate: both are generated by double commutators and induce
(coadjoint) contraction with respect to a natural invariant metric.
The crucial difference is conceptual: in the BKMR setting the
double bracket is
postulated
by choosing a Casimir
C
, whereas
in our setting, the double commutator emerges
necessarily
from the adjoint torsion and symmetry reduction.

In the qubit case, the Bloch representation makes this correspondence
completely explicit.
For
G=SU(2)
, a Hermitian channel

L=\tfrac{1}{2}\,\boldsymbol{\ell}\cdot\boldsymbol{\sigma}
induces
(in Bloch coordinates) the dissipative Bloch equation

\dot{\mathbf{r}}_{\mathrm{diss}}=-2\gamma\Big(\mathbf{r}-(\mathbf{r}\cdot\boldsymbol{\ell})\,\boldsymbol{\ell}\Big)=-2\gamma\,\mathbf{r}_{\perp},

where
\mathbf{r}_{\perp}
is the component orthogonal to
\boldsymbol{\ell}
.
This is precisely the BKMR double–bracket flow on

\mathfrak{su}(2)\simeq\mathbb{R}^{3}
,

\dot{\mathbf{m}}=-\lambda\,\mathbf{m}\times(\mathbf{m}\times\nabla C(\mathbf{m})),

under the identifications

\mathbf{m}\leftrightarrow\mathbf{r},\qquad\nabla C\leftrightarrow\boldsymbol{\ell},\qquad\lambda=2\gamma.

In particular, the ACSP Lindblad dissipator is the quantum counterpart
of the classical double–bracket mechanism, with the role of the
“preferred direction”
\nabla C
now played by the Lindblad operator

L
.

, as

\dot{\rho}=-i[H,\rho]\;+\;2[L,\alpha],

(59)

For Hermitian channels we choose

\alpha(\rho)=-\frac{\gamma}{4}[L,\rho],

which yields

2[L,\alpha(\rho)]=2\Big[L,-\frac{\gamma}{4}[L,\rho]\Big]=-\frac{\gamma}{2}[L,[L,\rho]],

and therefore

\dot{\rho}=-i[H,\rho]-\frac{\gamma}{2}[L,[L,\rho]],

the single–channel GKSL dissipator.

Let
F(\rho)
be any functional.
Using the Hilbert–Schmidt inner product,

\dot{F}(\rho)=\Big\langle\frac{\delta F}{\delta\rho},\dot{\rho}\Big\rangle=\{F,H\}_{\rm LP}(\rho)+(F,S)_{\mathrm{ACSP}}(\rho),

where the Lie–Poisson bracket on
\mathfrak{su}(n)^{*}
is

\{F,H\}_{\rm LP}(\rho)=\left\langle\frac{\delta F}{\delta\rho},-i[H,\rho]\right\rangle,

and the metric bracket is
(F,S)_{\mathrm{ACSP}}
as in
(
53
).
By Theorem
8.3
, this bracket is symmetric
and negative semidefinite (and
-(\cdot,\cdot)_{\mathrm{ACSP}}
is a
metric bracket in the usual sense). Thus, the GKSL equation admits the metriplectic decomposition

\dot{\rho}=\underbrace{-i[H,\rho]}_{\text{Poisson / Hamiltonian}}\;+\;\underbrace{-\frac{\gamma}{2}[L,[L,\rho]]}_{\text{metric / double bracket}}.

Remark 8.6
.

There are three closely related but distinct geometric structures at
play.

(1) Classical double bracket (BKMR):
a purely metric flow on
coadjoint orbits,
-\lambda[M,[M,\nabla C(M)]]
.

(2) Metriplectic systems (Morrison–Grmela):
the combination of a
Poisson bracket and a symmetric bracket, with entropy production.

(3) ACSP metric bracket (this work):
a metric bracket

derived from symmetry reduction
via the adjoint–coupled
semidirect product, which produces exactly the Lindblad double
commutator.

The ACSP bracket reduces to the BKMR double bracket on

\mathfrak{su}(2)
and fits naturally into the metriplectic
decomposition of GKSL dynamics (up to the overall sign convention for
the metric bracket), thereby linking the classical,
quantum, and geometric–mechanics perspectives in a unified structural
framework.
\diamond

Example 8.7
.

We illustrate the metriplectic structure and the ACSP metric bracket for
three–level quantum systems.
Let
\{\lambda_{a}\}_{a=1}^{8}
denote the Gell–Mann basis of

\mathfrak{su}(3)
, normalized by
\Tr(\lambda_{a}\lambda_{b})=2\delta_{ab}
.
Every density matrix admits the expansion

\rho=\frac{1}{3}I+\frac{1}{2}\sum_{a=1}^{8}r_{a}\lambda_{a},\qquad r_{a}\in\mathbb{R},

with the eight–dimensional Bloch vector
\mathbf{r}=(r_{1},\dots,r_{8})
.

Let
L=L^{\dagger}\in\mathfrak{su}(3)
be a fixed Lindblad operator.
The single–channel GKSL dissipator reads

\dot{\rho}_{\mathrm{diss}}=-\frac{\gamma}{2}[L,[L,\rho]].

We now analyze several explicit choices of
L
.

Population-dephasing in the
\lambda_{3}
direction

Take

L=\lambda_{3}=\begin{pmatrix}1&0&0\\
0&-1&0\\
0&0&0\end{pmatrix}.

Since
[\lambda_{3},\lambda_{a}]=2if_{3ab}\lambda_{b}
, the dissipator becomes

\dot{r}_{a}=-2\gamma\sum_{b,c}f_{3ab}f_{3bc}r_{c}.

Using the
SU(3)
structure constants

f_{312}=1,\ f_{345}=1/2,\ f_{367}=1/2,

one finds:

\displaystyle\dot{r}_{1}

\displaystyle=-2\gamma\,r_{1},

\displaystyle\dot{r}_{2}

\displaystyle=-2\gamma\,r_{2},

\displaystyle\dot{r}_{4}

\displaystyle=-\tfrac{\gamma}{2}r_{4},

\displaystyle\dot{r}_{5}

\displaystyle=-\tfrac{\gamma}{2}r_{5},

\displaystyle\dot{r}_{6}

\displaystyle=-\tfrac{\gamma}{2}r_{6},

\displaystyle\dot{r}_{7}

\displaystyle=-\tfrac{\gamma}{2}r_{7},

\displaystyle\dot{r}_{3}

\displaystyle=0,

\displaystyle\dot{r}_{8}

\displaystyle=0.

Thus
\lambda_{3}
dephasing produces: decay of coherences between levels
1
and
2
at rate
2\gamma
; decay of coherences involving level
3
at rate
\gamma/2
; invariance of populations (components along
\lambda_{3}
and
\lambda_{8}

SU(3)
analogue of qubit dephasing.

Dissipation generated by a diagonal
L

Let

L=\mathrm{diag}(1,-1,1).

Then
\mathrm{ad}_{L}
acts as

[L,\rho]_{ij}=(L_{i}-L_{j})\rho_{ij}.

Thus, off-diagonal entries with
(L_{i}-L_{j})\neq 0
decay quadratically; and entries within the degenerate eigenspaces of
L
remain invariant.

In the 8-dimensional Bloch representation, this corresponds to a
projection onto the subspace commuting with
L

\dot{\mathbf{r}}=-\gamma\,\Pi_{\perp}(\mathbf{r}),

where
\Pi_{\perp}
projects onto the orthogonal complement of the commutant
of
L

Non-diagonal Lindblad generator

Take the “ladder” operator

L=\lambda_{1}=\begin{pmatrix}0&1&0\\
1&0&0\\
0&0&0\end{pmatrix},

and

A straightforward calculation gives

\sigma_{12}

Thus the dissipator drives
\rho
toward the submanifold of states
invariant under exchange of levels
1
and
2

SU(3)
example of the metriplectic contraction:
the metric bracket forces relaxation toward the isotropy of
L
.

Metric bracket for
SU(3)

For any functional
F(\rho)
with gradient

,
the ACSP metric bracket takes the explicit form

(F,S)_{\rm ACSP}=-\frac{\gamma}{2}\sum_{a,b,c}f_{Lab}f_{Lbc}X_{a}r_{c},

where

9
Contact–Geometric Interpretation of ACSP Dissipation

The metriplectic formulation of Section
8
shows
that the dissipation generated by the adjoint–coupled semidirect
product (ACSP) splits naturally into Hamiltonian (coadjoint) motion and
a metric (double–bracket) component.
In this section we present a complementary viewpoint based on

contact geometry
: the Lindblad double commutator can be regarded
as the nonconservative (Reeb-type) part of a contact evolution on a
suitable extension of the space of density operators.
This connects ACSP–induced open quantum dynamics with
contact–mechanical approaches to dissipation and thermodynamics
(see, e.g.,

[
5
,
4
,
9
,
15
]
). Our goal here is not to rebuild the full machinery of contact geometry
on coadjoint orbits, but to highlight a natural contact structure in
which purity loss appears as motion along a Reeb direction and the
ACSP double bracket provides the “gradient” component of the contact
flow.

Given a symplectic manifold
(M,\omega)
and a symplectic potential

\theta
with
d\theta=\omega
, its
contactification
is

(M\times\mathbb{R},\ \lambda=\theta+dz)
, where
z
is an additional
coordinate.
Since

\lambda\wedge(d\lambda)^{n}=(dz+\theta)\wedge\omega^{n}\neq 0,

the pair
(M\times\mathbb{R},\lambda)
is a contact manifold.
The extra
z
–direction then provides an intrinsic degree of freedom for
encoding nonconservative effects or entropy production, as in geometric
thermodynamics and contact Hamiltonian mechanics

[
15
,
9
,
4
]
: motion along

z
measures departure from conservative dynamics. In our context, a natural choice for
M
is the coadjoint orbit

\mathcal{O}_{\rho}\subset\mathfrak{su}(n)^{*}
through a fixed density
operator
\rho
, equipped with the Kirillov–Kostant–Souriau (KKS)
symplectic form
\omega_{\mathrm{KKS}}
.

Before describing how the torsion–induced metric bracket integrates into a
contact Hamiltonian framework, it is useful to ensure that the geometric
reconstruction is well posed.
In particular, one must verify that the contactification of a coadjoint orbit
provides a genuine contact manifold and that the resulting contact Hamiltonian
flow can reproduce the GKSL dynamics on the
\rho
–component.
The following lemma records these facts.

Lemma 9.1
.

Let
(\mathcal{O}_{\rho},\theta_{\mathrm{KKS}})
be a coadjoint orbit equipped
with the Kirillov–Kostant–Souriau one–form.
For any smooth function
S(\rho)
with
dS(\rho)
nonvanishing on generic
orbits, the 1–form

\lambda=\theta_{\mathrm{KKS}}+dz

defines a contact structure on
\mathcal{O}_{\rho}\times\mathbb{R}
.
Moreover, given the torsion–induced metric bracket
(\cdot,\cdot)_{\mathrm{ACSP}}
,
there exists a contact Hamiltonian
K(\rho,z)
whose associated contact
Hamiltonian vector field
X_{K}
satisfies:

\bigl(X_{K}\bigr)_{\rho}\;=\;\{\,\rho,H\,\}_{\mathrm{LP}}\;+\;(\rho,S)_{\mathrm{ACSP}}\;=\;-i[H,\rho]-\frac{\gamma}{2}[L,[L,\rho]],

i.e. the
\rho
–component of
X_{K}
coincides with the GKSL generator.

Proof.

We first show that
\lambda=\theta_{\mathrm{KKS}}+dz
is a contact form on

\mathcal{O}_{\rho}\times\mathbb{R}
.
The Kirillov–Kostant–Souriau two–form

\omega_{\mathrm{KKS}}:=d\theta_{\mathrm{KKS}}
is symplectic on the
coadjoint orbit, hence nondegenerate of rank
\dim(\mathcal{O}_{\rho})
.
Since
dz
is exact and independent of the orbit variables,

d\lambda=d\theta_{\mathrm{KKS}}=\omega_{\mathrm{KKS}}.

Let
n=\frac{1}{2}\dim(\mathcal{O}_{\rho})
.
Then, using
\theta_{\mathrm{KKS}}\wedge\omega_{\mathrm{KKS}}^{\,n}\neq 0

on
\mathcal{O}_{\rho}
, we compute

\lambda\wedge(d\lambda)^{n}=(\theta_{\mathrm{KKS}}+dz)\wedge\omega_{\mathrm{KKS}}^{\,n}.

The term
dz\wedge\omega_{\mathrm{KKS}}^{\,n}
is nonvanishing because

\omega_{\mathrm{KKS}}^{\,n}
is a volume form on the orbit, and
dz

introduces the transversal direction along
\mathbb{R}
.
Thus
\lambda\wedge(d\lambda)^{n}
is a nowhere vanishing

(2n+1)
–form on
\mathcal{O}_{\rho}\times\mathbb{R}
, proving that
\lambda

defines a contact structure.

We now establish the existence of a contact Hamiltonian whose

\rho
–component reproduces the GKSL generator.
A vector field
X_{K}
on a contact manifold
(M,\lambda)
is determined by

\iota_{X_{K}}d\lambda=dK-(R_{\lambda}K)\,\lambda,\qquad\lambda(X_{K})=-K,

where
R_{\lambda}
is the Reeb vector field.
On
\mathcal{O}_{\rho}\times\mathbb{R}
we have

d\lambda=\omega_{\mathrm{KKS}}
and the Reeb vector field is

R_{\lambda}=\partial_{z}
.

Since
\omega_{\mathrm{KKS}}
restricts to a symplectic form on the orbit,
its contraction with any horizontal vector determines uniquely the
horizontal component of
X_{K}
.
To match the Hamiltonian part of the GKSL equation, choose
K
so that

\iota_{(X_{K})_{\rho}}\omega_{\mathrm{KKS}}=d\langle\rho,H\rangle,

which is the standard coadjoint–orbit formulation of Hamiltonian motion;
this yields
(X_{K})_{\rho}=\{\rho,H\}_{\mathrm{LP}}=-i[H,\rho]
.

To incorporate the torsion–induced metric bracket, note that the
ACSP symmetric bracket
(\cdot,\cdot)_{\mathrm{ACSP}}
is generated by a
dissipation potential
S(\rho)
such that

(\rho,S)_{\mathrm{ACSP}}=-\frac{\gamma}{2}[L,[L,\rho]].

Because
dS(\rho)
is nonvanishing on generic orbits by assumption, the
function
K(\rho,z):=\langle\rho,H\rangle+S(\rho)
satisfies

\iota_{(X_{K})_{\rho}}\omega_{\mathrm{KKS}}=d\langle\rho,H\rangle+dS\quad\Longrightarrow\quad(X_{K})_{\rho}=\{\rho,H\}_{\mathrm{LP}}+(\rho,S)_{\mathrm{ACSP}}.

Finally, the condition
\lambda(X_{K})=-K
determines the
z
–component of

X_{K}
uniquely along the Reeb direction and does not affect the

\rho
–component.
Putting the two parts together gives

(X_{K})_{\rho}\,=\,-i[H,\rho]-\frac{\gamma}{2}[L,[L,\rho]],

which is exactly the GKSL generator on the orbit variables.
∎

Now, let
\mathcal{O}_{\rho}\subset\mathfrak{su}(n)^{*}
be the coadjoint orbit
through
\rho
, equipped with the Kirillov–Kostant–Souriau (KKS)
symplectic form
\omega_{\mathrm{KKS}}
.
Its contactification is

\mathcal{C}=\mathcal{O}_{\rho}\times\mathbb{R},\qquad(\rho,z)\in\mathcal{C},

with contact form

\lambda=\theta_{\mathrm{KKS}}+dz,

(60)

where
\theta_{\mathrm{KKS}}
is any symplectic potential satisfying

d\theta_{\mathrm{KKS}}=\omega_{\mathrm{KKS}}
.

The coordinate
z
will play the role of a purity/entropy variable: along GKSL flows driven by a fixed channel
L
,
z
will track the loss of coherence measured by the double commutator
[L,[L,\rho]]
. Since
d\lambda=\omega_{\mathrm{KKS}}
has no
dz
–component, the Reeb
vector field
R_{\lambda}
is purely vertical:

\iota_{R_{\lambda}}d\lambda=0,\qquad\lambda(R_{\lambda})=1\quad\Longrightarrow\quad R_{\lambda}=\frac{\partial}{\partial z}.

Lemma 9.2
.

(\mathcal{C},\lambda)
is a contact manifold, i.e.
\lambda\wedge(d\lambda)^{n}\neq 0
and
R_{\lambda}=\partial_{z}
is well
defined as above.

Proof.

The nondegeneracy of
\omega_{\mathrm{KKS}}
on the
2n
–dimensional
orbit implies

\lambda\wedge(d\lambda)^{n}=(dz+\theta_{\mathrm{KKS}})\wedge\omega_{\mathrm{KKS}}^{n}\neq 0,

so
\lambda
is a contact form.
Since
d\lambda=\omega_{\mathrm{KKS}}
annihilates the vertical
direction and
\lambda(\partial_{z})=1
, the Reeb vector field is

R_{\lambda}=\partial_{z}
.
∎

Remark 9.3
.

In the contact formulation of thermodynamical systems developed in

[
4
]
, the nonconservative part of the
dynamics is generated by the so–called
evolution operator
,
which is closely related to the Reeb vector field of the underlying
contact manifold. More precisely, if
(M,\lambda)
is a contact manifold with
Reeb field
R_{\lambda}
characterized by
\iota_{R_{\lambda}}\lambda=1,\,\iota_{R_{\lambda}}\mathrm{d}\lambda=0
, and
h\colon M\to\mathbb{R}
is a contact Hamiltonian, the associated contact
Hamiltonian vector field
X_{h}
is defined by

\iota_{X_{h}}\mathrm{d}\lambda=\mathrm{d}h-(R_{\lambda}h)\lambda,\qquad\lambda(X_{h})=-h.

(61)

The term proportional to
R_{\lambda}h
singles out the component of

X_{h}
along the Reeb direction. In Darboux coordinates

(q^{i},p_{i},z)
with
\lambda=\mathrm{d}z-p_{i}\mathrm{d}q^{i},\,R_{\lambda}=\partial_{z}
the contact Hamiltonian equations read

\dot{q}^{i}=\frac{\partial h}{\partial p_{i}},\qquad\dot{p}_{i}=-\frac{\partial h}{\partial q^{i}}-p_{i}\frac{\partial h}{\partial z},\qquad\dot{z}=p_{i}\frac{\partial h}{\partial p_{i}}-h.

(62)

The last equation describes the evolution along the Reeb direction
(“vertical”
z
–direction), and in the thermodynamical
interpretation
z
is an entropy–like variable, so that the

evolution operator
—the Reeb component of
X_{h}
—measures
entropy production.

In our construction, the same geometric mechanism appears.
We work on a contact manifold
(\mathcal{M},\lambda)
whose points
encode quantum states together with an additional coordinate
z
.
The Reeb vector field is
R_{\lambda}=\partial_{z}
, and we equip
\mathcal{M}
with a contact Hamiltonian
H_{c}
such that
the induced contact Hamiltonian vector field
X_{H_{c}}
projects onto
the GKSL dynamics on density matrices,

\dot{\rho}=-\mathrm{i}[H,\rho]-\frac{\gamma}{2}[L,[L,\rho]].

(63)

Here
H
is the system Hamiltonian,
L
is a (for simplicity,
selfadjoint) Lindblad operator and
\gamma>0
is the dissipation rate.
The first term
-\mathrm{i}[H,\rho]
is the usual Hamiltonian
contribution, while the double–commutator
\mathcal{D}(\rho)=-\frac{\gamma}{2}[L,[L,\rho]]
is the GKSL dissipator generated by the ACSP.

To see that this term plays the role of an “entropy producing” Reeb component, consider the purity
\mathcal{P}(\rho):=\mathrm{Tr}(\rho^{2})
. Using (
63
) and the cyclicity of the trace one computes

\displaystyle\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}t}\mathcal{P}(\rho)

\displaystyle=2\,\mathrm{Tr}\!\bigl(\rho\,\dot{\rho}\bigr)=2\,\mathrm{Tr}\!\bigl(\rho\,\mathcal{D}(\rho)\bigr)

\displaystyle=-\gamma\,\mathrm{Tr}\!\bigl(\rho\,[L,[L,\rho]]\bigr).

(64)

If
L=L^{\dagger}
, one can rewrite the last term as

\mathrm{Tr}\!\bigl(\rho\,[L,[L,\rho]]\bigr)\;=\;\mathrm{Tr}\!\bigl([L,\rho]^{\dagger}[L,\rho]\bigr)\;\geq\;0,

(65)

so that

\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}t}\mathcal{P}(\rho)\;=\;-\,\gamma\,\mathrm{Tr}\!\bigl([L,\rho]^{\dagger}[L,\rho]\bigr)\;\leq\;0.

(66)

Equivalently, for the purity entropy
S_{\mathrm{p}}(\rho):=1-\mathcal{P}(\rho)
we obtain

\dot{S}_{\mathrm{p}}(\rho)=\gamma\mathrm{Tr}\bigl([L,\rho]^{\dagger}[L,\rho]\bigr)\geq 0,

(67)

which is precisely an entropy production inequality:
the GKSL dissipator
\mathcal{D}(\rho)
is responsible for the
monotonic loss of purity.

Now we turn back to the contact picture. On
(\mathcal{M},\lambda)
we
choose coordinates
(x^{a},z)
adapted to the contact structure, with

\lambda=\mathrm{d}z-\alpha
for a 1–form
\alpha
on the
“quantum” variables
x^{a}
. As in (
61
), the
contact Hamiltonian vector field
X_{H_{c}}
splits into a horizontal
part tangent to
\ker\lambda
and a Reeb component,
X_{H_{c}}=X_{H_{c}}^{\mathrm{hor}}+(R_{\lambda}H_{c})R_{\lambda}
. In our construction
X_{H_{c}}^{\mathrm{hor}}
reproduces the unitary
Hamiltonian evolution
-\mathrm{i}[H,\rho]
, while the Reeb component
encodes the dissipative GKSL term. More concretely, if we choose
z

to be (up to an affine rescaling) the purity–entropy
S_{\mathrm{p}}
,

z(\rho)=aS_{\mathrm{p}}(\rho)+b\qquad(a>0),

(68)

then along the flow of
X_{H_{c}}
we have

\dot{z}=R_{\lambda}(H_{c})=a\,\dot{S}_{\mathrm{p}}(\rho)=a\gamma\mathrm{Tr}\bigl([L,\rho]^{\dagger}[L,\rho]\bigr)\geq 0,

(69)

where we used (
67
). In other words, the Reeb vector field

R_{\lambda}=\partial_{z}
generates a strictly monotone flow along the

z
–direction, and the coefficient
R_{\lambda}(H_{c})
is determined
by the double–commutator term
-\frac{\gamma}{2}[L,[L,\rho]]
.

Therefore, the GKSL dissipator is geometrically realized as the Reeb
component of the contact Hamiltonian vector field
X_{H_{c}}
, exactly
in the same way as the evolution operator in the contact formulation
of nonequilibrium thermodynamics encodes entropy production along the
Reeb direction
[
4
]
. Here, the role of
thermodynamic entropy is played by purity loss, and the Reeb flow
drives the departure from purely Hamiltonian (unitary) motion.

\diamond

Let us recall the basic structure of a contact Hamiltonian system. On a
contact manifold
(\mathcal{C},\lambda)
with Reeb field
R_{\lambda}
, the
contact Hamiltonian vector field
X_{K}
associated with a Hamiltonian

K:\mathcal{C}\to\mathbb{R}
is defined by

\iota_{X_{K}}d\lambda=dK-(R_{\lambda}K)\,\lambda,\qquad\lambda(X_{K})=-K.

(70)

Its flow has a “horizontal” component tangent to the contact
distribution
\ker\lambda
and a “vertical” component along
R_{\lambda}
. In our setting, the conservative part
-i[H,\rho]
of GKSL dynamics is
the Hamiltonian flow of
H(\rho)=\Tr(H\rho)
with respect to the KKS
symplectic form on
\mathcal{O}_{\rho}
. The dissipative part,

-\frac{\gamma}{2}[L,[L,\rho]]
, is transverse to coadjoint orbits and
acts as a metric gradient with respect to the Hilbert–Schmidt norm (see
Proposition
6.5
). It is therefore natural
to interpret this double–commutator term as the nonconservative
component of a contact-type evolution on
\mathcal{C}
.

Theorem 9.4
.

Let
\mathcal{O}_{\rho}\subset\mathfrak{su}(n)^{*}
be a coadjoint orbit of

SU(n)
with KKS symplectic form
\omega_{\mathrm{KKS}}
, and consider
the contact manifold

\mathcal{C}=\mathcal{O}_{\rho}\times\mathbb{R},\qquad(\rho,z)\in\mathcal{C},

with contact form
\lambda=\theta_{\mathrm{KKS}}+dz
as in
(
60
).
Let
L\in\mathfrak{su}(n)
be Hermitian and define the contact Hamiltonian

K(\rho,z)=H(\rho)+\frac{\gamma}{4}\,\mathrm{Tr}\big([L,\rho]^{2}\big),

where
H(\rho)=\mathrm{Tr}(H\rho)
is the expectation value of the
system Hamiltonian and
\gamma\geq 0
.
Then the contact Hamiltonian vector field
X_{K}
satisfies

\dot{\rho}=-i[H,\rho]-\frac{\gamma}{2}[L,[L,\rho]],\qquad\dot{z}=-\frac{\gamma}{2}\,\|[L,\rho]\|^{2},

(71)

where
\|\cdot\|
is the Hilbert–Schmidt norm.
In particular, the Lindblad double commutator is realized as the
Reeb-driven dissipative component of the contact Hamiltonian flow.

Proof.

We write
X_{K}=(\dot{\rho},\dot{z})
and use the defining equation
(
70
) with
\lambda
given by
(
60
) and
R_{\lambda}=\partial_{z}
.

On
\mathcal{O}_{\rho}
, the restriction of
d\lambda
is the KKS form

\omega_{\mathrm{KKS}}
, and the horizontal component of
(
70
) reduces to

\iota_{\dot{\rho}}\omega_{\mathrm{KKS}}=d_{\rho}K\big|_{T\mathcal{O}_{\rho}}
. Writing
K(\rho,z)=H(\rho)+\Phi(\rho)
with

\Phi(\rho):=\frac{\gamma}{4}\,\mathrm{Tr}\big([L,\rho]^{2}\big),

we can decompose
\dot{\rho}=\dot{\rho}_{H}+\dot{\rho}_{\Phi}
, where
\dot{\rho}_{H}
and
\dot{\rho}_{\Phi}
satisfy

\iota_{\dot{\rho}_{H}}\omega_{\mathrm{KKS}}=dH,\qquad\iota_{\dot{\rho}_{\Phi}}\omega_{\mathrm{KKS}}=d\Phi.

The first equation is the usual Hamiltonian coadjoint motion:

\dot{\rho}_{H}=-\operatorname{ad}^{*}_{\delta H/\delta\rho}\rho=-i[H,\rho],

using the standard identification
\delta H/\delta\rho=-iH
on

\mathfrak{su}(n)
.

For the second equation we compute
d\Phi
.
Let
\delta\rho
be a variation tangent to
\mathcal{O}_{\rho}
.
Then

\delta\Phi=\frac{\gamma}{2}\,\mathrm{Tr}\big([L,\rho]\,[L,\delta\rho]\big).

Using cyclicity of the trace and the fact that
L^{\dagger}=L
, one
obtains the identity

\mathrm{Tr}\big([L,\rho]\,[L,\delta\rho]\big)=\mathrm{Tr}\big([L,[L,\rho]]\,\delta\rho\big),

so that
\delta\Phi=\frac{\gamma}{2}\,\mathrm{Tr}\big([L,[L,\rho]]\,\delta\rho\big)
.

Hence the functional derivative of
\Phi
with respect to
\rho
is

\frac{\delta\Phi}{\delta\rho}=\frac{\gamma}{2}[L,[L,\rho]].

On a coadjoint orbit, the Hamiltonian vector field associated to a
functional
f(\rho)
with gradient
\delta f/\delta\rho=A
is

X_{f}(\rho)=-[A,\rho]
.
Thus, the
Hamiltonian
contribution associated to
\Phi
is

\dot{\rho}_{\Phi}^{\text{(Ham)}}=-\Big[\frac{\gamma}{2}[L,[L,\rho]],\,\rho\Big],

which is tangent to the orbit.
However, the double commutator
[L,[L,\rho]]
itself provides a natural

normal
direction along which purity decreases, and plays the role
of a metric gradient in the metriplectic formulation
(Section
8
).

The contact construction encodes this normal direction in the Reeb
component.
Since
R_{\lambda}=\partial_{z}
and
K
is independent of
z
, we have

R_{\lambda}(K)=0
, and the Reeb contribution is not fixed by
(
70
) alone.
Instead, it is determined by the balance relation for the contact
Hamiltonian flow
\lambda(X_{K})=-K(\rho,z)
.

A standard gauge choice for
\theta_{\mathrm{KKS}}
on coadjoint orbits
allows one to arrange that

\theta_{\mathrm{KKS}}(\dot{\rho}_{H})=H(\rho),\qquad\theta_{\mathrm{KKS}}(\dot{\rho}_{\rm diss})=0,

where we now write

\dot{\rho}_{\rm diss}:=-\frac{\gamma}{2}[L,[L,\rho]]

for the dissipative (normal) component dictated by the ACSP
double–bracket structure.
With this choice, the condition
\lambda(X_{K})=-K(\rho,z)
yields

\theta_{\mathrm{KKS}}(\dot{\rho}_{H})+\theta_{\mathrm{KKS}}(\dot{\rho}_{\rm diss})+\dot{z}=-H(\rho)-\Phi(\rho),

so that

H(\rho)+0+\dot{z}=-H(\rho)-\Phi(\rho)\quad\Rightarrow\quad\dot{z}=-\Phi(\rho)=-\frac{\gamma}{4}\,\mathrm{Tr}\big([L,\rho]^{2}\big).

\dot{z}=-\frac{\gamma}{2}\,\|[L,\rho]\|^{2}\leq 0.

Collecting the Hamiltonian part and the prescribed dissipative part we
obtain

\dot{\rho}=-i[H,\rho]-\frac{\gamma}{2}[L,[L,\rho]],\qquad\dot{z}=-\frac{\gamma}{2}\,\|[L,\rho]\|^{2},

which is exactly the GKSL evolution for a single Hermitian channel,
augmented by a contact variable
z
measuring purity loss.

To place our geometric construction in a broader theoretical context, it is instructive to compare it with alternative formalisms designed to describe dissipative and entropy–producing dynamics in generalized Hamiltonian systems. A prominent example is the

framework, which supplements a Poisson bracket with a symmetric bracket generated by an entropy functional. However, the Lindblad–GKSL evolution possesses specific structural features—its linearity, its strictly contractive character on the state space, and the absence of a globally compatible entropy functional—that make metriplectic realizations intrinsically noncanonical. In contrast, contact geometry captures the separation between conservative and dissipative contributions in a natural and geometrically intrinsic way, with the irreversible part arising precisely along the Reeb direction. The proposition below articulates this contrast in a precise manner.

Proposition 9.7
.

The Lindblad–GKSL evolution of an open quantum system

\dot{\rho}\;=\;-\,\mathrm{i}[H,\rho]\;+\;\mathcal{D}(\rho),\qquad\mathcal{D}(\rho)=-\frac{\gamma}{2}[L,[L,\rho]],

(73)

admits a natural and geometrically consistent realization as a

contact Hamiltonian system
, whereas its realization as a

metriplectic system
(with a globally defined entropy functional
and positive semidefinite symmetric bracket in the sense of
(
51

incompatible with the linear and irreversible character of the GKSL flow.

Proof.

We compare the two formalisms at the structural level. Let
(M,\lambda)
be a contact manifold with Reeb vector field
R_{\lambda}

satisfying

\iota_{R_{\lambda}}\lambda=1,\qquad\iota_{R_{\lambda}}\mathrm{d}\lambda=0.

(74)

Given a contact Hamiltonian

contact Hamiltonian vector field
X_{h}
is defined by

\iota_{X_{h}}\mathrm{d}\lambda=\mathrm{d}h-(R_{\lambda}h)\,\lambda,\qquad\lambda(X_{h})=-h.

(75)

Writing
\lambda=\mathrm{d}z-\alpha

(x^{a},z)

X_{h}=X_{h}^{\mathrm{hor}}\;+\;(R_{\lambda}h)\,R_{\lambda},

(76)

where

is tangent to
\ker\lambda
(conservative
part) and
(R_{\lambda}h)R_{\lambda}
is the irreversible component.

Let the additional coordinate
z
encode (up to affine rescaling)

S_{\mathrm{p}}(\rho)\;:=\;1-\mathrm{Tr}(\rho^{2}).

Using the identity

\dot{S}_{\mathrm{p}}(\rho)=\gamma\,\mathrm{Tr}\!\bigl([L,\rho]^{\dagger}[L,\rho]\bigr)\;\geq\;0,

obtained from the GKSL dissipator, the contact Hamiltonian

R_{\lambda}(h)\;=\;\gamma\,\mathrm{Tr}\!\bigl([L,\rho]^{\dagger}[L,\rho]\bigr).

Conclusions and Future Directions

We have shown that Lindblad dissipation admits a fully geometric and
variational origin: the Gorini–Kossakowski–Sudarshan–Lindblad (GKSL)
generator arises
exactly
as the Euler–Poincaré equation on an
adjoint–coupled semidirect product (ACSP).
In this framework,
\mathfrak{g}
carries its usual adjoint action,
the representation space
V=\mathfrak{g}^{\oplus m}
carries an adjointly
compatible second action, and the failure of these actions to commute produces
an intrinsic torsion tensor

K(\xi,v)\;=\;\xi\cdot v\;-\;v\cdot\xi,\qquad\xi\in\mathfrak{g},\ v\in V.

Upon Euler–Poincaré reduction, this torsion induces a quadratic curvature
operator on
\mathfrak{g}^{*}
which manifests as a metric
double–bracket term.
For
G=SU(n)
, this curvature is precisely the Lindblad dissipator

\dot{\rho}_{\rm diss}=-\tfrac{\gamma}{2}[L,[L,\rho]],

while the Hamiltonian part arises from coadjoint motion.
This yields the central conclusion:

Decoherence is a curvature–induced contraction generated by
Euler–Poincaré reduction on an adjoint–coupled semidirect product.

In particular, projecting a Hamiltonian system on

T^{*}\!\left(G\ltimes\mathfrak{g}^{\oplus m}\right)
onto its
G
–variables

necessarily
produces an irreversible, contractive evolution.
Thus, the GKSL dissipator is not an external phenomenological input but the
unique curvature contribution compatible with ACSP symmetry.

Geometrically, the ACSP framework places Lindblad dynamics on the same
conceptual footing as rigid-body motion, plasma models, and
non-equilibrium continua.
It provides both a
Lie–theoretic origin
for dissipation and a

unified metriplectic structure
in which

\dot{\rho}=\underbrace{-i[H,\rho]}_{\text{Poisson / Hamiltonian}}\;+\;\underbrace{-\tfrac{\gamma}{2}[L,[L,\rho]]}_{\text{metric / ACSP curvature}},

so that decoherence is precisely the metric component of a
dimension-independent metriplectic system.

These results open several promising avenues for future research.

(1) Geometric reservoir engineering.

The torsion–induced dissipation

\dot{\rho}_{\rm diss}=2[L,\alpha(\rho)]\quad\text{with}\quad\alpha(\rho)=-\tfrac{\gamma}{4}[L,\rho]

suggests a
geometric design principle
for engineered noise.
By choosing the ACSP coupling appropriately, one may generate dissipative
channels with desired invariant subspaces or pointer bases.
Applications include dissipative preparation of target states, autonomous
quantum error correction, bath engineering, and reservoir-assisted
entanglement generation.

(2) Dissipation-assisted and hybrid feedback control.

The intrinsic decomposition

\dot{\rho}=-i[H(\rho),\rho]-\tfrac{\gamma}{2}[L,[L,\rho]]

provides a natural template for hybrid coherent/incoherent control.
Using torsion as a tunable contraction mechanism suggests new forms of
dissipation-assisted tracking, entropy pumping, and asymptotic stabilization
of pure or entangled states.
ACSP-based controllers could provide geometric alternatives to stochastic,
measurement-based feedback schemes.

(3) Simulation and modeling of structured quantum noise.

The torsion tensor
K(\xi,v)
offers a concrete parametrization of
structured dissipative channels.
Its representation-theoretic nature makes it suitable for hardware-oriented
noise modeling, benchmarking of quantum processors, and the design of
geometric noise families for quantum sensing.
In particular, noise processes that preserve symmetry subspaces or dynamical
group orbits can be encoded directly in ACSP data.

(4) Coarse-graining, emergent Markovianity, and renormalization.

Interpreting dissipation as curvature contraction suggests deep connections
with quantum coarse-graining and emergent Markovianity.
Since the torsion-generated curvature survives reduction while other terms do
not, ACSPs offer a geometric mechanism for understanding how Lindblad
generators arise as effective descriptions of underlying Hamiltonian dynamics.
This may help clarify renormalization flows of open systems and the emergence
of irreversible behavior from reversible microscopic models.

(5) Quantum–classical analogies and robotics.

The ACSP contraction mechanism resembles consensus dynamics,
alignment flows, and gradient methods on Lie groups.
This analogy suggests potential applications of ACSP ideas in distributed
control, multi-agent robotics, synchronization on manifolds,
and hybrid quantum–classical architectures.
Conversely, classical geometric control may benefit from
ACSP-inspired dissipative couplings, providing new primitives for stabilization
and consensus over nonlinear configuration spaces.

(6) Quantum thermodynamics and geometric dissipation.

The curvature–induced contraction of ACSP dynamics suggests a
geometric route to quantum thermodynamics.
Since the Lindblad double bracket determines a canonical direction of entropy
production, ACSPs provide a natural framework for studying the geometry of
irreversibility, entropy balance laws, and thermodynamic fluxes on
coadjoint orbits.
This opens the door to geometric formulations of quantum detailed balance,
thermal fixed points, fluctuation relations, and entropy production
inequalities, with potential applications in the design of thermodynamically
consistent quantum devices and in the analysis of work extraction,
efficiency bounds, and energy–information trade-offs.

(7) Analytic properties of ACSP–induced semigroups.

A natural direction is the rigorous analysis of the ACSP flow as a
quantum Markov semigroup: generation theorems, spectral properties,
hypocoercivity, and contractivity estimates.
The algebraic and geometric structure identified here provides a
candidate framework for such analytic developments.

The adjoint–coupled semidirect product incorporates Lindblad dynamics
into the core structure of geometric mechanics.
It explains decoherence in terms of torsion–induced curvature and places
quantum irreversibility within a unified framework that includes
Poisson geometry, metriplectic systems, and contact Hamiltonian flows.
We expect this unification to stimulate further interaction between geometric
control, open quantum systems, quantum thermodynamics, and
non-equilibrium statistical mechanics, and to provide new tools for
dissipation engineering, complexity reduction, and the geometric design
of quantum technologies.
