Effects of occupational therapy on quality of life, occupational performance, and occupational satisfaction in older adults with cancer: a multicentre, pragmatic, randomized, controlled trial protocol (OCEAN-OT)
Authors: Cynthia Engels, Sophie Marliac Schweitzer, Odile Blond, Rabia Boulahssass, Céleste David, Hélène Faure, Cloé Gaborit, Sylvie Kirscher, Kathleen Doyle Lyons, Lucia Machado Antunes, Laure Morisset, Mackenzi Pergolotti, Monica Pierro, Céline Pineau-Ribas, Sylvain Thamié, Florence Rollot-Trad, Florence Canouï-Poitrine

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundCancer can impact the way older adults and their care partners engage in their occupations. As an expert in the field of occupations, the occupational therapist is able to assess and support both a person with cancer and (if applicable) their care partners, with regard to occupational disruptions. The primary objective of this study is to assess the effect of occupational therapy (OT) on quality of life (QoL) in older adults with cancer. The study’s secondary objectives are to (i) describe changes over time in occupational performance and satisfaction scores in older adults with cancer receiving (or not) OT and (if applicable) for their care partners, (ii) assess the effect of OT sessions on QoL and adverse events in older adults with cancer and in their care partners, and (iii) describe the OT sessions’ content and procedures.MethodsOCEAN-OT is a multicentre, pragmatic, three-arm, parallel-group, open-label, assessor-blinded, superiority randomized controlled trial. The inclusion criteria are age 70 or over, a diagnosis of colorectal, breast, prostate or lung cancer, treatment with curative intent, and residence at-home. The first control group will receive usual care. A second control group will receive usual care and a priority-setting interview called the Canadian Occupation Performance Measure (COPM) at baseline and at 3 and 6 months. The intervention group will receive usual care, the COPM, and a three-month program of home OT sessions. QoL will be assessed by administering the 30-item European Organisation for Research and Treatment of Cancer Quality of Life Group Core Questionnaire at baseline and at 3 and 6 months (for patients) and the Zarit Burden Interview (for care partners).DiscussionThere are few published, evidence-based studies of rehabilitation in older adults with cancer, and the available data are related to exercise and physical health, rather than occupations. The future trial results might have implications for health policies and might be crucial for maintaining quality of life for older adults with cancer and for their care partners.Trial RegistrationThe study protocol has been approved by an investigational review board (reference: 2023-A00925-40) and registered at ClinicalTrials.gov (NCT05878782).Keywords: Occupational therapy, Cancer, Older adults, Randomized controlled trial, Healthcare professions, Occupational engagement, Innovative approaches, Client-centred, At-home rehabilitation, Occupations.

Introduction

Background and rationale

The incidence of cancer is increasing in the population as a whole and among adults aged 65 or over in particular; the latter age group will account for 60% of new cases by 2035 [1]. The World Health Organization defines health as “a state of complete physical, mental and social well-being”. However, today’s studies of rehabilitation in older adults with cancer tend to focus on exercise and physical health.

In the field of occupational science, occupations are defined as “the everyday activities that people do as individuals, in families and with communities to occupy time and bring meaning and purpose to life. Occupations include things people need to, want to and are expected to do.” [2]. Townsend and Polatajko [3] distinguish between self-care occupations, productive occupations (including home management and voluntary work), and leisure occupations. Satisfactory engagement in occupations is critical for individuals, as the results of previous studies of older adults have highlighted a link between occupational engagement on one hand and state of health and quality of life on the other [4].

Neo et al.’s systematic review and meta-analysis [5] notably showed that 37% of adults living with cancer experienced difficulties or needed assistance in basic activities of daily living (ADL), and the corresponding proportion for instrumental activities of daily living (IADL) was 55%. Furthermore, frail older adults are more at risk of toxic effects, which are associated with a decline in quality of life [6]. In view of the functional repercussions of cancer and cancer therapy [7], and given that nearly 70% of older people with cancer have at least one comorbidity [8] (which can reduce quality of life [9]), one can reasonably expect both occupational performance (the way one does an activity) and occupational satisfaction (how satisfied one is with the way they do this activity) are expected to be affected in all three occupational categories (i.e. self-care, productive and leisure occupations). Although there is a large body of literature data on the repercussions of cancer on older adults’ self-care occupations, few researchers have assessed leisure occupations and productive occupations in this setting [10]. However, in a pilot study of seven older adults with cancer (each of whom was asked to report up to five occupational problems), 12 of the 21 problems were related to productive or leisure occupations [11].

Furthermore, the literature data on the burden of care for older adults with cancer also raises questions about the impact on the caregiver’s occupations [12]. Indeed, the repercussions of caregiving (time spent, fatigue, worry, etc.) can affect both the caregiver’s own occupations and co-occupations shared with the care recipient, i.e. occupations that involve “aspects of shared physicality, shared emotionality, and shared intentionality, embedded in shared meaning” [13].

Occupational therapists are experts in the field of occupations and are able to assess and support people with cancer and (if appropriate) their care partner with regard to occupational disruptions. In Nielsen et al.’s study, 26% of older adult cancer survivors stated that they had difficulty in engaging in activities that contribute to life satisfaction [14]. This difficulty was related mainly to a physical symptom/impairment, a psychological issue (related to mood, attitudes, preferences, or mental state) or, to a lesser extent, the environment, time management, and social issues [14]. Other studies have evidenced the positive impact of intensive occupational therapy (OT) at home on the quality of life of older adults with chronic health issues [15], and some of these studies included caregivers [16, 17]. One other study showed that after OT sessions, 68% of the 24 older adults with cancer had attained the goals they had set at the start of the OT follow-up [18].

To the best of our knowledge, an occupation-based study of older adults with cancer in France has not been conducted. Our hypothesis is that OT during cancer treatment can improve the quality of life, occupational performance, and occupational satisfaction among older adults.

Objectives

The primary objective of the OCEAN-OT study is to assess the effect of OT on quality of life among older adults with cancer. The secondary objectives are to (i) describe changes over time in occupational performance and satisfaction scores for older adults with cancer receiving (or not) OT and (if applicable) for their care partners, (ii) assess the effect of OT sessions on quality of life and adverse events in older adults with cancer and in their care partner, and (iii) describe the OT sessions’ content and procedures.

Methods

Trial design

This is a multicentre, three-arm, parallel-group, open-label, assessor-blinded, randomized, controlled superiority trial.

Participants, interventions, and outcomes

The protocol was designed and reported in accordance with the Standard Protocol Items Recommendations for Interventional Trials [19]. The experimental design was inspired by a similar study of the effect of occupation-based, intensive, client-centred, home-based OT for older adults [15]. Nielsen et al. used the Canadian Occupational Performance Measure (COPM), an evidence-based, semi-structured interview in which a person identifies and prioritizes up to five occupations that they do not currently perform to their own satisfaction. However, the researchers pointed out that administration of the COPM per se might increase awareness and motivation and thus have a therapeutic effect [15]. We therefore decided to create two control groups (a “usual care” group and a “usual care + COPM” group) and an intervention group (usual care, the COPM, and OT at home for three months).

Study setting

Patients and caregivers will be recruited in three French hospitals (a university hospitals, and two cancer hospitals). The intervention will be home-based. The list of study sites can be obtained by e-mailing the first author.

Eligibility criteria

The inclusion criteria for patients are age 70 or over, colorectal, breast, prostate or lung cancer (the most frequent types of cancer in France and worldwide [20]), a time interval of one to three months after the start of cancer treatment (depending on the type of treatment: surgery and/or systemic treatment and/or radiotherapy); treatment with curative intent; affiliation with a social security scheme, living in one’s own home or in a relative’s home, and the voluntary provision of written, informed consent. The inclusion criteria for care partners were the provision of care to a patient meeting the above-listed criteria, living with the patient, and the voluntary provision of written, informed consent. The non-inclusion criteria for both patients and care partners are as follows: inability to speak French, provision of state medical aid; person under legal guardianship, or administrative supervision. The caregivers will be assigned to the same group as their corresponding older adults with cancer.

The occupational therapists delivering the OT intervention will have received formal training in administration of the COPM.

The intervention

The usual care control group will receive usual care only, i.e. no OT and no COPM. The usual care + COPM control group will receive usual care plus the COPM interview. The latter will be conducted by an occupational therapist at the patient’s home, in one or two one-hour sessions (depending on the interviewee’s state of fatigue) during the first week of the study. At three months and six months, the COPM will be administered at home by a different occupational therapist, who will be blinded to the previous scores (in order to decrease bias).

The intervention group will complete the COPM and receive the OT intervention, which will follow Fisher’s Occupational Therapy Intervention Process Model (OTIPM) [21]. The OTIPM is an intervention model that guides occupational therapists to use a top-down, client- and occupation-centred approach. In an initial evaluation and goal-setting phase, the occupational therapist seeks to understand the occupational problems faced by the patient, then uses a performance analysis to understand the cause(s) of these problems, and then determines occupation-focused goals in collaboration with the patient and (if appropriate) their care partner. The OT assessment and goal-setting will take place during weeks 1 to 3. Furthermore, the first COPM will be administered during week 1, in one or two sessions (depending on the interviewee’s state of fatigue). During week 2, the occupational therapist will then seek to “understand the complex relationship between the client’s occupations and situational contexts”, “implement performance analyses” and “finalize the assessment” [21], in one or two sessions. These sessions may notably include a task analysis (i.e. observation of the patient performing an occupation), and assessments of body functions and environmental and/or contextual factors or interactions that may influence the occupational context. In week 3, the occupational therapist and the patient will together define the objectives of the OT intervention and fix the intervention schedule [22]. Weeks 4 to 11 will be devoted to OT sessions (twice a week in weeks 4 to 7 and weekly in weeks 8 to 11); this frequency is a compromise between Nielsen et al.’s protocol [15], and the consensus opinion of five of the investigating occupational therapists. As mentioned above, the OT intervention will follow the top-down, client- and occupation-centred OTIPM approach [3, 21–23]. Each OT session will involve one or more of the four models developed by Fisher et al. (2019): a compensatory model, an education and teaching model, an acquisitional model for occupational skills training, and a restorative model for enhancing body functions and other client elements [21]. According to Fisher et al. (2019), the compensatory model is used to “compensate for decreased performance skill”, the education and teaching model is used to “share knowledge related to occupational performance, occupational experience, and participation, including strategies to support occupational skill and knowledge related to adaptation strategies”, the acquisitional model is used to “enhance (i.e., acquire, redevelop, maintain) performance skill” and the restorative model is used to “enhance (i.e., restore, develop, maintain) body functions and other clients elements”. In this pragmatic study, the occupational therapists will use their knowledge and experience to choose the most appropriate model(s) with regard to the person’s needs on the day of the OT session. Each session will last an hour at most but can be shortened as a function of the patient’s state of fatigue. Any concomitant care and interventions are permitted except those including occupational therapy.

Outcome measures

The primary outcome measure will be the “role functioning” subscore of the 30-item European Organisation for Research and Treatment of Cancer Quality of Life Group Core Questionnaire (EORTC QLQ-C30), version 3. Role functioning was chosen because it provides a more specific assessment of a person’s ability to perform daily activities, leisure time activities, and/or work; this is very similar to the definition and classification of occupations used in the COPM. The EORTC QLQ-C30 will first be administered (with the assistance of a clinical research associate, if needed) in hospital, on inclusion (i.e. before randomization). It will then be administered again (with the assistance of an occupational therapist or clinical research associate, if needed) at home, three months and six months (± one month) after the first measurement.

The overall EORT QLQ-C30 score will be a secondary outcome.

For care partners, the first outcome will be the Zarit Burden Interview score. This tool will be administered in hospital or (if the care partner has not planned to return to the hospital on the day when the patient is randomized) by telephone, at the time of inclusion (before randomization) and with the assistance of a clinical research associate, if needed. The Zarit Burden Interview will then be repeated at home 3 months and 6 months after inclusion, with (if needed) the assistance of an occupational therapist blinded to the previous scores.

The scores in the COPM (the nature of occupational problems described qualitatively, the median occupational performance scores, and the median occupational satisfaction scores) will be used as secondary outcomes for both patients and care partner (if applicable) in the two control groups. The COPM interview with the patient will be conducted by the occupational therapist during the first home visit (in two sessions, if needed, depending on the patient’s fatigue). If the care partner is also included, the COPM will be administered at home separately by the occupational therapist. The COPM will be administered again at three and six months by a different occupational therapist (Fig. 1).

Participant timeline

Each patient and (if applicable) their care partner will participate in the study for six to eight months:

Up to 1 month between the screening consultation (when the patient is told about his/her cancer diagnosis) and first visit by the occupational therapist.

Up to 1 month between the screening consultation (when the patient is told about his/her cancer diagnosis) and first visit by the occupational therapist.

12 ± 2 weeks of home-based assessments, OT sessions, and re-assessments.

12 ± 2 weeks of home-based assessments, OT sessions, and re-assessments.

12 ± 2 weeks between the final OT session (the first re-assessment) and the final re-assessment.

12 ± 2 weeks between the final OT session (the first re-assessment) and the final re-assessment.

Sample size

We postulate a difference of six points in the primary endpoint at three months between the intervention group and the “usual care” control group (with a standard deviation of 6), as that was the mean difference in role functioning observed for the subset of RCTs in Cock’s et al.’s evidence-based guidelines for determination of sample size of the EORTC QLQ-C30 [24]. Using PASS software (NCSS Statistical Software, Kaysville, UT, USA) and after taking account of multiple comparisons, an overall alpha risk of 5% and a power of 82%, we calculated that 38, 38 and 76 patients are respectively needed in the two control groups and the intervention group (i.e. 152 in total) to detect this difference. We estimate that 20% of the participants will be lost to follow-up or not evaluable [25], and we therefore intend to include 191 participants and 40 carers.

Recruitment

Each patient’s referring oncologist, oncologist-geriatrician or oncology advanced practice nurse will verbally present the study and hand over study information sheets and a simplified brochure. When the patient returns to hospital, they will be asked to say whether or not they agree to participate in the study. If the patient’s care partner also meets the inclusion criteria, the care partner will also be asked to say whether or not they agree to participate in the study.

Assignment of interventions

Allocation concealment mechanism

The randomization will be centralized and computerized. After the provision of written, informed consent, validation of the eligibility criteria, and completion of the initial EORTC QLQ-C30 by the centre’s clinical research associate, participants will be randomized in a 1:1:2 ratio to the two control groups and the intervention group. Having twice as many patients in the intervention group as in either of the two control groups will enable us to analyze the clinical, sociodemographic and environmental profiles of the patients who respond best to the full intervention. The randomization will be stratified by centre, in blocks of variable size.

Blinding

Neither patients nor investigators can be blinded to the use or not of OT sessions. However, in order to limit the evaluator’s potential measurement bias, the occupational therapist conducting out the re-assessments will be blinded to the patient’s randomization group and initial scores. The investigators collecting the data before randomization will also be blinded to the randomization group.

Data collection, management, and analysis

Data collection methods

Sociodemographic data (sex, age, educational level, socioprofessional category, place of residence, presence or absence of home help) and data generated during the treatment and patient care (type of cancer, cancer stage, treatments, nutritional status, pain levels, etc.) will also be reported. Memory complaints [26], and the adapted Perneczky criteria (combining the Mini Mental State Examination (MMSE), memory complaints and IADL scores, as a guide to the presence of neurocognitive disorders defined in the Diagnostic and Statistical Manual of Mental Disorders, fifth edition [26]) will also be reported.

In the intervention group, the occupational therapist will complete the patient’s records after each session. The following deidentified data will be recorded: the session duration (subsequently analyzed as the median), the type of activities performed, the type of model used, the occurrence of any adverse events during the session, and (if deemed necessary) a qualitative comment on the session).

All data from the study assessments will be noted on paper and then entered into an electronic case report form (e-CRF, CleanWeb, Telemedicine Technologies SA, Boulogne-Billancourt, France) (Table 1).

Data management

The investigators will enter data into the e-CRF via a secure Internet protocol. The data will be stored on a central server in the Paris Public Hospitals Group’s operational services department (Assistance Publique - Hôpitaux de Paris). Data not present in a patient’s medical records will be recorded on a paper case report form prior to entry into the CleanWeb e-CRF.

Statistical methods

The results of the statistical analysis will be reported in accordance with the Consolidated Standards of Reporting Trials statement and its extension for non-pharmacological trials [36]. The main intent-to-treat analysis will consider the participants alive at three months. Missing data will be replaced. A secondary, per-protocol analysis will be performed. The patients’ baseline characteristics will be described by group. In accordance with international guidelines, statistical tests will not be used to compare the groups at baseline. The primary outcome will be assessed through pairwise comparisons of the change in the EORTC QLQ-C30 role functioning subscore at three months vs. baseline, after Tukey’s correction for multiple comparisons. All three pairwise comparisons will be of interest because they are likely to provide information on the influence of each component of the intervention (the COPM and the OT sessions); this will help to establish specific guidelines on transposition into routine clinical practice. The other judgment criteria (the other QLQ-C30 scores at three months, the COPM score, and Zarit Burden Interview score will be analyzed in the same way. The repeated data for the analysis at six months (three measurements) will be taken into account in hierarchical mixed models. To test the effect of the intervention, we shall look for a time*arm interaction. A centre effect will also be sought. No interim analyses are planned. The statistical significance threshold will be set to p < 0.05 (after Tukey’s correction for multiple comparisons).

Monitoring

Data monitoring

The data monitoring committee (DMC) will be composed of the study’s principal investigator, the study’s scientific manager, and specially appointed representatives of the study sponsor. The DMC will define the general organization of the research, coordinate information exchange, determine the initial methodology, monitor the study’s progress, and suggest actions to be taken during research.

The DMC will decide on any protocol amendments required for continuation of the study. In particular, these include measures for facilitating recruitment into the trial, protocol amendments to before their presentation to the investigational review board, the discussion of the study results, and the strategy for publishing these results.

The DMC will meet before the start of the trial and then whenever is necessary. Decisions concerning a major protocol amendment or a budget modification must be approved by the study sponsor.

Harms

We do not expect participation in the study to be associated with particular risks. However, any emergent adverse events will be notified in the e-CRF by a clinical research associate and immediately brought to the attention of the investigating centre’s principal investigator, who may at any time decide to stop the trial.

Auditing

The investigators have agreed to quality control audits by the sponsor and inspections carried out by the competent authorities. All data, documents and reports may be subject to audits and regulatory inspections, and the confidential nature of personal medical data and the physician-patient relationship cannot be invoked as a reason for refusing the said audits and regulatory inspections. An audit can be carried out at any time by staff mandated by the study sponsor and who are not otherwise involved in the study. The audit’s objective is to assess the quality of the study, the validity of the study’s results, and the study’s compliance with the legislation and regulations in force. The individuals managing and monitoring the study will agree to comply with the sponsor’s requirements with regard to the audit. The audit can be applied to all stages of the study, from development of the protocol and publication of results to classification of data used or produced within the framework of the research.

Protocol amendments

Any substantial amendments to the study protocol will be submitted to the investigational review board for approval.

Consent

The inclusion visit for patients (and for their care partners, if applicable) will take place between two weeks to three months after the selection visit, when the patient returns for a consultation at the hospital. The voluntary provision of written, informed consent by the patient and/or care partner (as appropriate) will be documented by a clinical research associate.

Confidentiality

The people responsible for quality control in clinical studies (as defined in the French public health code) will take all necessary precautions to ensure that the study data (and notably the identities of study participants and the study results) remain confidential. These people and the investigators are subject to a professional duty of confidentiality (as defined in the French penal code).

Declaration of interests

The principal investigators have no financial or other competing interests with regard to the trial as a whole and its implementation at each study site.

Access to data

During and after the clinical study, the data on the participants sent to the study sponsor by the investigators or other study personnel will be de-identified. Only the participant’s initials will be recorded and given a study-specific, sequential study identifier. The study sponsor will ensure that each study participant has given their written consent to the access to their personal data required for the study’s quality control procedures.

Dissemination policy

Publications in international scientific journals and oral presentations at national and international conferences are planned during and after the study period.

Discussion

The OCEAN-OT study is an innovative, multicentre, randomized, controlled trial designed to assess the effect of OT on quality of life in older adults with cancer and in their care partners.

The results of the study may have implications in terms of health policy. There are indeed few occupational therapists working with people with cancer in France - notably because of the lack of knowledge of whom OT would benefit [7]- and there are even fewer of them working with people with cancer at home. However, a few articles highlight the benefit that OT could have for people of all ages with cancer [7, 39–42]. Recent special issues devoted to cancer in OT journals (e.g., British Journal of Occupational Therapy in 2021, Occupational Therapy in Health Care journal in 2022, ErgOThérapies journal in 2023), also demonstrate the growing interest of the profession in this population. If the results of our trial were positive, this would argue in favour of implement home OT sessions for this population and fund them. The results will also able to better define which populations may benefit from home OT according to demographics, type of cancer and baseline physical, psychological and socio-environmental characteristics. This paucity of literature is especially noticeable when regarding older adults. However, a growing body of evidence is starting to support the need to develop OT for older adults with cancer with a functional approach [7, 43]. Yet the literature concerning an occupation-based approach with this population is, to our knowledge, non-existent to date. Furthermore, among the few studies existing about OT in older adults with cancer, even fewer include care partners.

Choosing quality of life as the main outcome of our study was also prompted by the French government’s ten-year National Cancer Plan (published in 2021), the second objective of which is to “limit the after-effects and improve the quality of life”. The National Cancer Plan frequently highlights the importance of integrating supportive care into healthcare provision, although OT per se is not yet mentioned [44]. Quality of life has also been mentioned by the International Society of Geriatric Oncology as a major primary endpoint in studies of older people [45]. Some national OT associations have already published position statements or guidelines on OT for people with cancer, which are in line with certain aspects of the French National Cancer Plan [46, 47] . The present study might generate convincing data that link the ten-year National Cancer Plan to the OT associations’ position statements.

We anticipated some limitations to our study, that we tried to minimize. First, the patients' place of residence may be far from the hospital of inclusion, which can complicate the fact that the occupational therapist can visit their homes. For this reason, we sought to involve occupational therapists working in different areas around the hospitals that include the clients. We also anticipated a risk of attrition of 20% in our sample. Finally, we chose to intervene early in patients’ care pathway. Some may therefore not have experienced any occupational problems at the time of recruitment, which may limit the number of patients willing to join the study. For this reason, the investigating centers will try to present the study in the best possible conditions to patients and their caregivers (taking the time, in a quiet place, explaining the study with enough details, etc.)

Conclusion

This trial will address a significant gap in our knowledge of the potential benefits of OT for older adults with cancer. The study’s future results might help occupational therapists to develop practices better suited to this population and might provide arguments in favour of the implementation of OT in the care pathway for older patients with cancer. The study’s future results might prove to be crucial for maintaining quality of life for older adults with cancer and for their care partners.
