Cyclodextrins and Cyclodextrin‐Based Polymers and Their Supramolecular Host–Guest Interactions for Sustainable Water Purification and Perovskite Solar Cell Devices: A Review
Authors: Anny Leudjo Taka, Seyed Hesam‐Aldin Samaei, Jinkai Xue, Vivek Maheshwari
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractCyclodextrin‐based polymers are a versatile class of emerging materials with unique supramolecular host–guest interaction features. Their diverse functional groups, hydrophobic cavities, and hydrophilic exteriors make them intriguing materials. These structural advantages promote molecular recognition, the absorption of guest molecules in their cavities, and the selective adsorption of guest molecules by the functional groups on their external surface. Recent research highlights that cyclodextrin polymers can simultaneously leverage supramolecular host–guest interactions and chelating ability to adsorb inorganic, organic, and microbial contaminants from water. They can also enhance the stability and performance of perovskite solar cell devices. This paper discusses the multifaceted function of cyclodextrin polymers and outlines the broad applicability of cyclodextrin‐based materials in water purification and in metal halide perovskite solar cell devices.Keywords: absorption, adsorption, cyclodextrin polymers, host–guest interactions, perovskite solar cell, water purification

Introduction

Cyclodextrins (CDs), with their fascinating cyclic oligosaccharide structure (Figure 1) derived from the enzymatic reaction of starch, have sparked considerable interest owing to their unique supramolecular host–guest interaction features. Their versatile nature and rich functional groups make them a canvas for creativity [1, 2, 3]. Their properties enable grafting onto inorganic (or organic) materials or direct copolymerization with other monomers, resulting in the creation of CD polymers. CDs have a form of a truncated cone with hydrophilic exteriors and hydrophobic cavities, facilitating molecular recognition, the absorption of guest molecules in their cavity, and the selective adsorption of guest molecules by the functional groups on their external surface, which adds to their intrigue [1, 2, 4, 5]. These unique features of CDs and CD polymers (e.g., CD polymer‐based nanosponges) enable noncovalent interactions, host–guest complexation, as an effective choice for water purification and favorable, sustainable solutions to improve electronic devices’ stability, functionality, charge transport, and defect passivation [6]. Furthermore, terms such as cyclodextrin, cyclodextrin polymers, cyclodextrin derivatives, cyclodextrin nanosponge, and supramolecular complex are widely used throughout this review. It is important to note that these materials have different structures and functions. Therefore, based on their structure and function, they can be defined, respectively, as follows: CDs are natural sugar rings made of glucose units. They have a cavity center to hold other molecules [1, 2, 3]. A cyclodextrin polymer is obtained when many cyclodextrin rings are linked together to form large chains or networks [7]. A cyclodextrin derivative refers to a modified form of cyclodextrin obtained after addition of new chemical moieties (functional groups) to the original structure of cyclodextrin [8]. A cyclodextrin nanosponge is also a cyclodextrin‐based polymer obtained through crosslinking polymerization of cyclodextrin with a cross‐linker agent (e.g., isocyanate). A cyclodextrin nanosponge is very small, sponge‐like polymer network. In its limited spaces, it can carry a large amount of target molecules [2]. A supramolecular complex is the final bonded structure formed when a cyclodextrin host interacts with a guest molecule (safely locked inside the cyclodextrin cavity), known as a host–guest complex [9]. Despite the advantages, the practical application of CD polymers requires further structural modifications or integration with nanomaterials to overcome application‐specific limitations.

In water purification, the challenge is to demonstrate the capabilities of CD polymer‐based nanosponges modified with nanomaterials to remove various types of contaminants from polluted water [2, 10, 11]. In contrast, in electronic devices with particular focus on perovskite solar cells, the challenge is to achieve sustainable, green, and efficient electronic devices by showing that the introduction of either CDs, CD derivatives, or CD polymers in the fabrication of electronic devices can be considered as a strategy to passivate the defects (bulk and/or interfacial defects) present in the devices, i.e., to lower the impact of these defects on the device’s performance, to augment the performance and stability of electronic devices [12, 13, 14, 15, 16].

There are extensive research works on the use of CD polymers in drug delivery application [5, 17, 18, 19, 20, 21, 22, 23, 24, 25]. UpToDate, there are many ongoing research exploring how to optimize the properties of CD polymers through their modification with various chemical moieties or nanomaterials to develop CD‐based nanosponges for water treatment [7, 26, 27, 28, 29, 30, 31, 32]. However, the utilization of the CDs, CD derivatives, and CD‐based polymers in electronic devices (e.g., perovskite solar cell devices) is still in their infancy [13]. Therefore, this work highlights the multifaceted function of CDs and CD–basedpolymers, emphasizing how their supramolecular host–guest interactions can favor efficient water pollutants capture during water purification and enhance performance in next‐generation energy‐saving electronic devices with particular focus on perovskite solar cell devices.

Synthesis and Characterization of Cyclodextrin‐Based Polymers

Synthesis of CD Polymer‐Based Nanosponges

Among different types of CDs (Figure 1), beta‐cyclodextrin (β‐CD) stands out as the preferred choice for the synthesis of cyclodextrin‐based polymers (also called cyclodextrinnanosponges). Its cavity size not only presents superior complexation capability and stability but also provides high encapsulation capability due to its ability to form more stable complexes. Moreover, its lower cost and higher production rates make β‐CD the most practical and economically viable option for large‐scale applications [1, 13, 33, 34].

β‐CDs show high reactivity owing to their strong binding affinity and stability, which make them suitable in various chemical reactions [1]. They can be readily copolymerized with various monomers or grafted onto inorganic and organic substrates through diverse synthetic approaches [35, 36, 37, 38, 39]. In this regard, the synthesis of CD‐based polymers (or CD nanosponges) is a versatile process, with various methods (Table 1), such as the microwave method, solvent condensation method, melt method, and ultrasound‐assisted method [4]. Among these methods, the solvent condensation method stands out as the most widely used to obtain water‐insoluble CD‐based polymers or CD nanosponges, offering a range of possibilities for experimentation and innovation [2, 5]. The unique of advantages of using solvent condensation method in CD based polymer‐based nanosponges synthesis is mainly the ability to effectively control the degree of cross‐linking density to produce high yield and pure products with well‐defined structural integrity [40]. This approach also favor surfacefunctionalization of CD with other materials or chemical moieties to create specialized material for specific applications (e.g., water purification, drug delivery, and sensors).

In particular, β‐CD‐based nanosponges are formed via the cross‐linking polymerization of cyclodextrin polymer with a cross‐linking agent (Figure 2). A crosslinker agent is a molecule possessing bifunctional groups, which can either be diisocyanates, dianhydrides, epoxides, carbonyl, or carboxylic acids compounds (Figure 2) [5, 40, 41, 42]. Depending on the type of crosslinker agent, it is possible to obtain either a water‐insoluble or water‐soluble CD‐based nanosponges having supramolecular 3D‐hyperreticulated nanoporous structures which are highly stable over a broad temperature and pH ranges [2]. The potential of CD‐based nanosponges is vast, with various nanomaterials and functional groups being utilized to functionalize them. This not only enhances their properties but also increases their practicality for many applications, inspiring optimism for the future of this technology [8, 9, 40].

CD polymer nanosponges can be comprehensively characterized using a versatile range of techniques. These include thermal gravimetry analysis, various spectroscopy and electron microscopy techniques, as well as BET for the determination of surface area, pore volume, and size, as detailed in Table 3. These characterization techniques are useful because they ensure a thorough understanding of the nanosponges’ properties. Application of Cyclodextrin‐Based Polymers in Water Purification

CD‐based polymers (also called CD‐based nanosponges) have demonstrated the capability to absorb and adsorb a wide variety of pollutants: organic contaminants, dyes, heavy metals, dyes, drug molecules, pesticides, and microorganism pollutants present in contaminated water [4, 31, 33, 44, 50, 51, 52, 53, 54, 55, 56, 57, 58]. Leudjo Taka and coworkers employed a synergistic strategy involving phosphorylation, cross‐linking polymerization with hexamethylene diisocyanate (HMDI) as linker, and a sol–gel process to fabricate phosphorylated multiwalled carbon nanotube–cyclodextrin/Ag‐doped TiO2 (pMWCNT‐CD/TiO2‐Ag) nanosponges for removing TCE, CR dye, Pb2+, Co2+ metal ions, and microorganism pollutants (e.g., E. coli, B. substilis) from contaminated water (Figure 3). Leudjo Taka and coworkers have demonstrated that key factors affecting the adsorption efficiency and capacity of the synthesized pMWCNT‐CD/TiO2‐Ag were mainly the pH, adsorbent dosage, and contact time. The removal mechanism of TCE was facilitated by the host–guest interactions through a chemical sorption process, while a combined process of ionexchange‐electrostatic interactions favored the adsorption of CR dye. The sorption mechanism of metal ions (Pb2+, Co2+) was mainly a combined process of physical adsorption, ion exchange, and electrostatic interactions taking place through a multilayer heterogeneous adsorption. Leudjo taka and coworkers were able to demonstrate that pMWCNT‐CD/TiO2‐Agnanosponges can be regenerated and reuse up without any significant loss in the adsorption efficiency. From Figure 3D,E, the removal percentage of TCE, CR dye, Pb2+, and Co2+ was maintained to a maximum of 99% up to the 3rd cycle [28, 44, 59, 60].

A two‐step thermally induced nucleophilic substitution reaction was conducted by Jia et al. [4] to synthesize porous cyclodextrin‐based nanocube sponges for the adsorption of bisphenol A (BPA), methylene blue (MB), and Pb2+. The capacity of adsorption was the highest for the removal of BPA and Pb2+, and the equilibrium was established within 30s. The mechanism of adsorption of MB and BPA was via host–guest interaction and hydrogen bond interactions, whereas the electrostatic interactions and chelation controlled the uptake of Pb2+ [4].

Lingling Zhang and colleagues synthesized magnetic nanoparticles immobilized porouscyclodextrin (MN‐PCDP) nanosponges through a combined process of cross‐linking polymerization of β‐CD and tetrafluoroterephthalonitrile (TFT) linker agent and one‐step solvothermal reaction (to immobilized β‐CD‐cross‐linked TFT polymer onto magnetic Fe3O4 NPs) (Figure 5). The synthesized MN‐PCDP nanosponge showed a high surface area and small pore size [27, 61] while demonstrating higher removal capacity (Figure 6b,c). The equilibrium reached about 95% in 10s for the uptake of all organic pollutants, such as pesticides (parathion, carbendazim), plastic components (BPS, BPA), and aromatic organic compounds (1‐naphthyl amine (1‐NA), 2‐naphtol (2‐NO)) (Figure 6b). The mechanism of removal of these pollutants was mainly through host–guest inclusion complexes [61].

Xuejiao Hu and coworkers have developed a magnetic carboxymethylated β‐CD‐based porous polymer possessing superparamagnetic and fast adsorption capacity. They have shown that developed polymer has large pores and can adsorbed organic pollutants (BPA), anionic dyes (MB), and heavy metal ions pollutants (Pb2+, Cu2+, Zn2+, and Cr3+) from dyeing wastewater through negative and positive electric attraction. The developed magnetic carboxymethylated β‐CD‐based porous polymer can also be regenerated and reused at room temperature without losing its adsorption performance [62].

Khammar and coworkers have successfully grafted carboxymethyl‐β‐CD to the surface of core–shell TiO2 magnetic Fe3O4 nanoparticles to obtained carboxymethyl‐β‐CD‐ Fe3O4@TiO2 polymer nanocomposite. The developed carboxymethyl‐β‐‍CD‐ Fe3O4@TiO2 has shown to be efficient for the degradation of polychlorinated biphenyls (PCBs) and possess good magnetic, photocatalytic, and recyclable properties. Khammar and coworker demonstrated that carboxymethyl‐β‐CD‐ Fe3O4@TiO2 polymer nanocomposite can achieve 83% optimum degradation of PCBs in just 16 min at 25 °C [63].

Furthermore, CD‐based polymer membranes have been developed because CD can be produced at a large scale from starch and offer advantages such as low cost, nontoxicity [64], and sustainability [65]. CD‐based polymer membranes have also been demonstrated to be suitable for a variety of filtrations and separations purposes [66], and the process to produce these CD‐based polymer membranes is well known [7]. CD as also been used as filler in films as part of film‐forming polymer membrane. Literature has demonstrated that these modified polymer CDs membranes possess excellent potential for many applications and separations [7, 67].

Pangeniet et al. synthesized cross‐linked sulfonated polyvinyl alcohol membrane using titanium glycinate‐N,N‐dimethylphosphate as linker then modified the synthesized polymer membrane with sulfonated β‐CD. The modified polymer membrane obtained was made of 20% and 16% by mass of sulfonated β‐CD and demonstrated excellent proton conductivity and good range (1.40–2.55 meq/g) of ion exchange capacity [68]. In addition, Wang and coworkers have demonstrated that by blending ethylene alcohol membrane with macrocyclic hyperamphiphiles CD, it helps increase the antifouling and flux performance of the hybrid membrane obtained [69].

β‐CD‐based polymers have also been shown to be effective in the uptake of drug molecule such as ibuprofen [70], ethynyl‐estradiol [71], propranolol hydrochloride [71, 72], ciprofloxacin [52], testosterone [73], progesterone [74] from wastewater. Additionally, they have shown the removal capacity of above 60% toward the removal of pesticides, including imidacloprid [75, 76], butachlor [77], atrazine [77, 78], and benalaxyl [79].

In summary, β‐CD‐based polymers as nanosponges and or as membranes have played a significant, innovative role as a sustainable filter material for water purification. Their supramolecular host–guest complexation, structural tunability, recyclability and inherent biocompatibility and biodegradability properties make them to be consider as future sustainable polymer materials. These β‐CD‐based polymers can further be modified with other biopolymers or inorganic nanofillers to produce biobased hybrid polymer nanosponge composite or hybrid polymer nanocomposite membrane, which possess multifunctional properties with enhanced adsorption capacity, antifouling behavior, and long‐term reusability to target various classes of water pollutants from wastewater.

Cycloextrin and Cyclodextrin Polymers for Application in Perovskite Solar Cell Devices

Metal halide perovskites have opened new horizons for many optoelectronic applications, including chiral effects, solar cells, light‐emitting diodes, patterned displays, and photodetectors. Despite challenges posed by their environmental sustainability, unfavorable biocompatibility, and limited stability (due to moisture, ion migration, and defect formation present in the device), these materials continue to inspire optimism due to their high efficiency and low cost. The development of supramolecular host–guest interaction features in β‐CDs and β‐CD derivatives (including β‐CD polymers) is an innovative approach to address these challenges and instill hope for the future of these materials in optoelectronics [80, 81, 82, 83, 84].

Metal Halide Perovskite Solar Cell Devices: Overview of Device Design and Fabrication

Ongoing research on metal halide perovskites (PVKs) has increased significantly. PVK materials have attracted significant consideration as a result of their low processing costs, tunable band gaps, high defect tolerance, exceptional optoelectronic properties, and high power conversion efficiency (PCE) [85]. Despite the remarkable optoelectronic properties of metal halide PVKs, they face many challenges (summarized in Table 4), among which stability remains a key challenge that significantly affects performance. Additionally, improving the light absorption efficiency of metal halide PVKs will further enhance their performance [101, 102]. In this aspect, the stability and light absorption of PVK materials can be enhanced using various strategies, as reported in the literature [103, 104]. These strategies include the use of additives, surface passivation, compositional engineering, nanostructures [105, 106], antireflective coatings [107], tandem structures, plasmonic materials [108], and textured substrates [109, 110]. Among these strategies, this article will mainly discuss compositional engineering and use of cyclodextrin as additive for improving the stability and performance of metal halide PVKs.

The compositional engineering of PVK is a critical factor affecting their performance, primarily through improving the stability and their ability to absorb light. Therefore, there is a need to develop compositional engineering strategies to make three‐dimensional (3D) metal halide PVK materials that possess excellent stability and light absorption efficiency, benign or reduced defect states, long carrier diffusion lengths, low nonradiative rates of recombination, as well as tunable properties [111]. The 3D metal halide PVKs mainly have a ABX3 stoichiometry, with a BX6 octahedra corner‐sharing network, where A is a monovalent cation (e.g., formamidinium (FA+), cesium (Cs+), methylammonium (MA+); X is a halogen anion (e.g., Cl−, Br−, I−), and B (e.g., Pb2+) is a divalent metal cation (Figure 7). Additionally, to achieve the formation of a suitable metal halide perovskite phase, the tolerance factor is used (Equation (1)). The tolerance factor (t) is a critical parameter that predicts the stable crystal structure for the PVK material. PVKs with an ‘t’ value of close to ‘1’ have a stable cubic structure. However, when the tolerance deviates from 1 (i.e., 0.7 <t < 1.1), it indicates that there is a phase distortion to orthorhombic or tetragonal phase [111].

In this portion of the article, we mainly focus on 3D lead (Pb) halide PVKs. These 3D Pb halide PVK materials have been widely studied and have demonstrated remarkable performance, making them suitable for commercial applications [111]. Three major approaches have been used for preparing perovskite thin films on a substrate: vapor deposition, solution processing, and hybrid vapor‐solution processing. Among these methods, the cost‐effective and common method is solution processing. This solution‐processing approach can be implemented using a two‐step sequential method or a one‐step method. However, the widely used method is the one‐step method, which involves spin‐coating the PVK precursor solution onto a substrate and then annealing it to produce a crystalline PVK thin film. This method is very simple and enables effective control of grain growth, crystallinity, and morphology during PVK thin‐film formation [113].

During the fabrication of PVK solar cells, several parameters must be considered when engineering the composition of Pb halide PVKs. These parameters include the choice of A‐site cation, the type of halide, stoichiometric control of halide‐to‐Pb ratio, the use of mixed halides, and the integration of additives or dopants into the perovskite precursor solution. These compositional parameters determine the optoelectronic properties of Pb halide PVKs and hence must be considered based on the target application [112]. For example, they influence light absorption, stability, and performance in various functional device applications, including integrated perovskite solar cells. The optoelectronic properties primarily depend on the band‐edge electronic structure arising from antibonding coupling between the halogen (X) anion p orbital (e.g., I 5p) and Pb (divalent) metal cation 6s orbital [112].

In general, by selecting the appropriate halide and cation for the fabrication of Pb halide perovskite materials, one can achieve excellent properties such as a desirable bandgap, high defect tolerance, long charge‐carrier lifetimes, extended carrier diffusion length, great charge‐carrier mobility, ambipolar transport characteristics, and high absorption coefficient [112, 114]. Furthermore, multication approaches have been explored and found to enhance not only the optoelectronic properties and performance, but also the stability of PVK materials. For example, Zhao et al. [115] were able to fabricate a multi‐cation‐halide PVK (Cs0.10FA0.78MA0.12PbI2.55Br0.45) using PVK seed growth method. They achieved a planar perovskite with quasi‐single‐crystallographic orientation in a PVK film, low trap density, large grain size, and improved operational stability and efficiency [115]. Moreover, it is also essential to control the halide‐to‐cation stoichiometric ratio during the preparation of the perovskite precursor solution. This is because some defects and/or unwanted phases can form when an incorrect halide‐to‐cation ratio is used in the perovskite precursor solution. Even with optimal ratios, formation of defects is inevitable during the nucleation and growth of the PVK film. The formation of these defects negatively affects the performance and stability of Pb halide perovskite. Therefore, to reduce the defects in the Pb halide PVK and enhance device performance and stability, one strategy demonstrated in the literature is to passivate the metal halide perovskite films using additives or dopants [116, 117].

Cyclodextrin‐Enabled Defect Passivation and Interfacial Stabilization in Metal Halide Perovskites

One approach to passivate the metal halide perovskite films is to incorporate dopants or additives. The advantage of introducing an additive (e.g., β‐CDs) in perovskite precursor solution is that it helps enhance environmental stability, reduce the defects in PVK materials, improve both crystallinity and morphology of the film, increase the light absorption efficiency, and consequently the performance of the PVK [116, 117]. Moreover, incorporating additives can change the electronic structure of PVK materials, reduce the number of grain boundaries, which enhances charge‐carrier mobility, leading to reduced electron–hole recombination and thus improving PVK performance [116]. For instance, Yi‐Xin Luo and coworkers demonstrated that the optical and structural properties of metal halide PVK can be enhanced through phase transformation of metal halide PVK resulting from the introduction of CDs (as additives) in precursor perovskite solution. In fact, this phase transformation was possible due to the strong chemical interaction of perovskite metal ions and halide ions with macrocyclic cyclodextrin’s hydroxyl groups [84].

Ferdowsi et al. [13] assessed the effect of adding β‐CD to the triple‐cation perovskite Cs0.05(FA0.83MA0.17)0.95Pb(I0.83Br0.17)3. They demonstrated that β‐CD’s supramolecular host–guest characteristic can be introduced into a triple‐cation perovskite (Figure 8). This fabrication approach for perovskite solar cells enhanced stability under high thermal stress and humidity while also improving the passivation of undercoordinated lead cation defects in the material. This interaction between the β‐CD and the perovskite precursors led to uniform crystal growth and an increase in PCE [13]. Additionally, Ferdowsi and coworkers [13] showed that there is a strong positive interaction specifically between β‐CD and MAI and PbI2, which helps prevent the crystallization of residual PbI2, thereby further increasing the device’s stability, enhancing optoelectronic performance, and thus increasing the high PCE of the perovskite solar cell [13, 118].

Picture illustrating how to incorporate β‐CD in triple cation perovskite, Cs0.05(FA0.83MA0.17)0.95Pb(I0.83Br0.17)3 and fabricate triple cation perovskite solar cell by one‐step spin coating deposition technique. Reproduced with permission from [13]. Copyright 2024, Royal Society of Chemistry.

Liu et al. [119] fabricated a perovskite solar cell device by incorporating a functionalized β‐CD, i.e., 2‐O‐methyl‐β‐CD, into perovskite precursor solutions. They stated that the host–guest interactions between 2‐O‐methyl‐β‐CD and perovskite ions were beneficial to reduce the defects in perovskite films, which limits the ion migration phenomena. The 2‐O‐methyl‐β‐CD can effectively capture and interact with cations; as a result, it modulates the crystallization of the PVK films and improves its crystallinity. This enhancement will lead to better light absorption by the perovskite solar cell and also improve stability and PCE of solar cell device [119].

Furthermore, Tian et al. [120] proved that the inclusion of 2‐hydroxypropyl‐β‐cyclodextrin (HPβCD) and super‐hydrophobic perfluorooctyltriethoxysilane (PFOS) in CsPbBr3 perovskite, taking place via hydrogen bond formation and host–guest interaction, was able to induce the spatial confinement effect, reducing the size of perovskite nanocrystals. As a result, there was narrowing of the photoluminescence emission (full‐width at half‐maximum (FWHM) <17 nm) and overlapping of the absorption band edge with emission wavelength (Figure 9). This shows that the additives were effective in modulating the crystallization of the PVK, passivating the defects, and also enhanced the optical properties of the perovskite [12, 120].

The supramolecular host–guest approach can also facilitate halide ion compensation, reducing halide deficiencies, mitigating ion redistribution and migration. For instance, at the perovskite interface with charge‐transport layers, Zhang and coworkers employed a bifunctional buffer layer such as starch–polyiodide supermolecule, which promotes defect self‐healing and suppresses ion migration. They showed that perovskite solar cells modified in this way can maintain after 42 diurnal cycles 98% of their PCE. Additionally, these devices reached a PCE of 24.3% and exhibited strong electroluminescence with an external quantum efficiency exceeding 12% [121].

Yang et al. [99] worked on perovskite solar cells and demonstrated the passivation of perovskite with functionalized βCD. They have introduced a self‐crosslinking supramolecular complex comprising 1,2,3,4‐butane tetracarboxylic acid (BTCA) and 2‐hydroxypropyl‐β‐cyclodextrin (HPβCD) into perovskite precursors (Figure 10), which can effectively bind to I− ions and undercoordinated Pb2+ in PVKs via several chemical interactions (e.g., chelation, hydrogen bonding, and coordination bonding). These significant chemical interactions are advantageous to inhibit the deprotonation of the precursor material, such as formamidinium iodide (FAI), and to prevent unwanted side reactions that produce by products (e.g., s‐triazine). This prevents the aging of perovskite precursors and improves the quality of perovskite films and their device stability under harsh conditions such as heat, moisture, and stress. Yang and coworkers also demonstrated that incorporating the cross‐linking HPβCD‐BTCA supramolecular complex into a perovskite solar cell device can help minimize Pb leakage and lower Pb toxicity, which is detrimental to the environment and human health. The effectiveness of the HPβCD–BTCA supramolecular complex is due to its strong multidentate chelation with Pb2+ ions and chemical coordination [99].

(A) Picture showing the various chemical interactions involve through the passivation of perovskites with crosslinked HPβCD‐BTCA; (B) illustration of the fabrication process of HPβCD‐BTCA‐perovskite solar cell device. Reproduced with permission from [12]. Copyright 2024, OAE Publishing Inc.

Wang and coworkers [122] introduced a β‐cyclodextrin derivative (β‐CD‐(SH)7) in inverted Cs0.05FA0.95PbI3 perovskite solar cell, achieving 26.14% PCE by utilizing a trifunctional molecule for synergistic iodine confinement and redox‐mediated defect repair. This supramolecular passivation method of Cs0.05FA0.95PbI3 perovskite with (β‐CD‐(SH)7) also enhanced the device stability and helped to capture lead to avoid Pb leakage. The defects passivation and photothermal stability of PVK solar cell devices are improved through thiol chemistry and host–guest interactions. For instance, Wang et al. further demonstrated that the β‐CD cavity captured I2 via supramolecular interactions. On the other hand, the role of thiol functional groups (–SH) was to favor, via dynamic S─S bond formation, the oxidation of Pb0 to Pb2+ and the reduction of I2 to I−. This mechanism facilitated the self‐sustained regeneration of I−. Hence, the PVK precursor solution stability was enhanced by the reducing effect of the ─SH groups [122].

Thiol functional groups (─SH) have also been used by He et al. [123] to functionalize mercapto‐cyclodextrin (MCD), a multifunctional additive used to address two major challenges in Sn–‍Pb narrow‐bandgap perovskite solar cells: Sn2+ oxidation and non‐uniform Pb/Sn distribution. The incorporation of the 6–8 thiol groups (‐SH) in MCD helps coordinate Pb2+ and Sn2+ ions, regulate their migration and crystallization, and suppress Sn2+ oxidation while also passivating defects. The cyclodextrin framework further provides spatial confinement that reduces ion clustering and uncontrolled nucleation. This dynamic metal–thiol coordination and spatial confinement strategy produces more homogeneous Pb/Sn compositions, improves device stability, and enhances charge transport. As a result, He and coworkers achieved 22.3% efficiency for narrow‐bandgap PVK solar cells and 27.9% for all‐PVK tandem solar cells. Their approach offers a favorable approach for controlling crystallization and compositional segregation in multicomponent perovskite optoelectronic materials [123].

Furthermore, He et al. [124] developed an in situ self‐assembled amorphous grain‐boundary strategy using β‐cyclodextrin derivatives to improve the mechanical durability, stability, and efficiency of flexible PVK Cs0.05(FA0.9MA0.1)0.95Pb(I0.9Br0.1)3 solar cells (PSCs). The resulting flexible PSCs achieved a 24.52% PCE, retained 92.5% of their performance after 10,000 bending cycles, and maintained 95% stability after 300 days in air and 80% after 650 h under continuous 1‐sun operation. This outcome can be justified by supramolecular host–guest interactions between cyclodextrin (host) and the PVK (guest). In this host–guest interaction mechanism, the amorphous grain boundaries enhance charge/photovoltaic performance while reducing strain‐induced degradation. The strategy was also successfully extended to large‐area modules, achieving approved efficiencies of 17.38% for 0.5 m2 and 21.09 % for 21.07 m2 modules, culminating in a 1.4725 m2 flexible module delivering 226 W with a high specific power of 558 W kg−1. Overall, their research work has shown that cyclodextrin‐based supramolecular grain‐boundary engineering can enable efficient, durable, stable, and scalable flexible perovskite photovoltaics [124].

Besides that, Yang et al. [125] provided a comprehensive overview of latest advances in incorporating supramolecular hosts based on macrocyclic molecules (e.g., CDs) and porous crystalline frameworks (covalent organic frameworks (COFs) and metal‐organic frameworks (MOFs)) into perovskite optoelectronic systems. They have explored macrocyclic host–guest chemistry to stabilize metal halide PVKs. They discussed that CDs utilize a dual‐binding mechanism, in which a hydrophobic cavity anchors organic cations, and hydroxyl‐rich rims form coordinate bonds with Pb2+ ions and halide anions, thereby reducing defect densities and enhancing stability. Furthermore, they discussed how these hosts encapsulate lead complexes and chelate free lead ions, forming insoluble supramolecular complexes that effectively trap toxic lead within the perovskite structure and prevent environmental leakage [125].

In summary, research has shown that supramolecular host–guest inclusion, a strategy where one molecule (the guest) is absorbed into the structure of another molecule (the host), is a promising approach for overcoming the limitations of optoelectronic materials [80, 81, 82, 83, 84]. The strong interactions between metal halide PVKs (including their precursors) and the cyclodextrin molecules with cavity‐like structure and a variety of functional groups, offer a comprehensive foundation for future investigation and improvement of their optoelectronic properties [15, 16, 81, 126]. This host–guest interaction arising from their unique cavity‐like structure, encompassing compensation, ion immobilization, defect passivation, structural stabilization, spatial confinement, and chemical chelation, results in comprehensive enhancements in sustainability and optoelectronic performance [9, 13, 14, 127]. This strategy therefore not only improves the performance of optoelectronic devices with a focus on PVK solar cell devices but also ensures their sustainability, providing a strong reassurance about the future of optoelectronics and instilling confidence in the potential of these advancements [83, 128].

Perovskite Optoelectronic Materials for Fluorescence Sensing, and Degradation of Water Pollutants

The supramolecular host–guest chemistry of CDs has been considered a powerful and promising approach for water purification, defect passivation, and improving the stability and efficiency of PVK solar cell devices. However, an emerging and highly relevant area that bridges these fields is the integration of CDs with metal‐halide perovskite optoelectronic materials to develop multifunctional platforms for fluorescence sensing, detection, and removal of water pollutants. Recent work by Ying Tan and coworkers has demonstrated blue‐emitting perovskite membranes capable of both detecting and photocatalytically degrading perfluorooctanoic acid [129]. In another research work, metal‐halide perovskite quantum dots (PQDs), particularly CsPbBr3, have been developed, and related compositions exhibit high photoluminescence quantum yields, tunable emission wavelengths, strong optical absorption, and favorable charge‐carrier properties, making them attractive materials for fluorescence‐based chemical sensing [130]. For example, water‐stable perovskite quantum‐dot systems have been successfully developed for fluorescence detection of organic contaminants in aqueous media, demonstrating the potential of perovskite‐based nanomaterials for sensitive optical monitoring of pollutants [131, 132]. However, the intrinsic sensitivity of many halide PVKs toward moisture and polar solvents remains a major limitation for their direct application in water. Surface functionalization with CDs offers an attractive approach to address this challenge while introducing molecular‐recognition functionality [129, 130].

Therefore, integrating cyclodextrin molecular recognition with perovskite optoelectronic and photocatalytic properties could enable a “capture–sense–degrade” approach in which the cyclodextrin component selectively captures and removes pollutants, the perovskite provides a fluorescence‐based sensing signal, and photoinduced charge carriers contribute to pollutant degradation [129]. Such multifunctional materials could provide an attractive platform for simultaneous environmental monitoring and water treatment. However, practical implementation requires careful consideration of perovskite stability under aqueous conditions and the potential release of toxic Pb2+ from lead‐halide compositions. Strategies including cyclodextrin/polymer encapsulation, surface passivation, incorporation into robust porous matrices, and the development of lead‐free PVKs will therefore be important for improving aqueous stability, minimizing secondary contamination, and enabling the practical application of these materials in water‐treatment technologies.

Structure–Property Relationships Governing the Performance of Cyclodextrin‐Based Polymer Hybrid Systems

The functional performance of cyclodextrin‐based polymer hybrid systems results from the interaction between the supramolecular structure of cyclodextrin polymers and the properties of their inorganic components, including metal ions, metal oxides, and metal halide PVKs. Key characteristics such as adsorption capacity, catalytic activity, electronic behavior, stability, and selectivity are governed by this structure–property relationship [7, 133].

At the molecular level, CDs possess a truncated cone‐shaped structure featuring a hydrophilic outer surface and a hydrophobic inner cavity. This architecture facilitates host–guest inclusion interactions, enabling selective encapsulation of organic molecules or small inorganic species within the cavity [64]. Polymerization or cross‐linking of CDs into networks produces porous frameworks with numerous binding sites. These structural characteristics directly affect adsorption efficiency and selectivity for pollutants in water purification, including heavy metal ions, organic contaminants, and pharmaceutical residues [134].

In hybrid CD‐based polymers such as βCD polymer nanocomposites, the incorporation of inorganic nanoparticles or metal ions introduces additional functional properties. Coordination interactions between cyclodextrin hydroxyl groups or polymer chains and metal centers create multidentate binding sites that enhance metal ion capture and stabilization. The distribution, size, and surface chemistry of inorganic components significantly influence surface area, pore structure, and accessibility to active sites. These factors control mass transfer and adsorption kinetics [29, 44, 135].

Cross‐linking density and polymer architecture of cyclodextrin networks are critical factors. Highly cross‐linked structures provide mechanical stability and chemical durability while preserving sufficient porosity for guest molecule diffusion. Functionalization with groups such as carboxyl, amine, or sulfonate modifies surface charge, coordination capacity, and affinity for target species, thereby enhancing performance in environmental remediation and sensing applications [43, 136].

In electronic and optoelectronic devices, including perovskite solar cells, cyclodextrin‐based polymers improve performance via interfacial engineering and defect passivation. Their supramolecular cavities and functional groups interact with undercoordinated metal ions or halide vacancies in perovskite materials, reducing defect density and suppressing ion migration. These structural interactions enhance charge transport, interfacial stability, and device durability during operation [12, 99].

The performance of cyclodextrin‐based polymer hybrid systems depends on the synergy among the supramolecular architecture, polymer network, and inorganic component. By optimizing key parameters such as cavity accessibility, cross‐linking strategy, and inorganic integration, these materials can be tailored for advanced applications. Targeted fields include water purification, environmental remediation, catalysis, and energy devices [2, 13, 7, 84, 133].

Summary and Outlook

The research reviewed and discussed CD and CD‐based polymers, highlighting their multifaceted functions through supramolecular host–guest interactions and their ability to address challenges in water purification and perovskite solar cell devices. Supramolecular host–guest chemistry offers specificity, reusability, adaptability, sustainability, and an impressive strategy for monitoring molecular recognition and interfacial interactions in CD‐ and CD‐based polymer hybrid materials for water purification and electronic devices. In water purification, CD‐‍based nanosponges, with their hydrophobic inner cavity, facilitate inclusion complex formation (based on size and polarity) between the CD host cavity and hydrophobic guest molecules (apolar organic pollutants), forming stable inclusion host–guest complexes. Whereas CD‐based nanosponges, with their hydrophilic exterior surface, allow the adsorption of various polar organic, inorganic, and heavy‐metal pollutants through molecular recognition such as chelation and electrostatic interactions, and molecular recognition of various organic, microbial, inorganic, and heavy‐metal pollutants in contaminated water, enabling their effective removal. In electronic devices (e.g., solar cell devices), the host–guest complexation ability of CD polymers helps narrow the perovskite nanocrystal sizes, mitigate ion migration, and reduce defects, thereby boosting the durability and optical properties of PVK materials. The passivation of Pb‐based halide PVKs with CD polymers has also been shown to be a valuable strategy for lead capture via chelation, coordination bonds, and supramolecular interactions, helping resolve the issue of lead toxicity and unwanted lead leakage from broken perovskite solar cell devices, which currently pose a critical problem for the human health and environment. While fruitful results have been achieved using the supramolecular host–guest interactions of cyclodextrin polymers in metal halide perovskite solar cells, research on their application in electronic devices remains scarce. There is still a need to explore further the ability of CD polymers in solar cell devices and other optoelectronic devices (e.g., organic light‐emitting transistors, organic field‐effect transistors, X‐ray imaging, and photodetectors).
