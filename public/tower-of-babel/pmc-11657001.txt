Association of neutrophil percentage to albumin ratio with gallstones: a cross-sectional study from the United States NHANES
Authors: Jianjun Wang, Zhiwen Shen, Yuwen Liang, Chuan Qin, Sirui Chen, Ruizi Shi, Yu Huang, Xi Chen, Hua Luo, Pei Yang, Jianping Gong, Xintao Zeng, Decai Wang
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackground and objectiveThe neutrophil percentage to albumin ratio (NPAR) is an emerging, costimulatory indicator of inflammation that is associated with a variety of diseases, such as non-alcoholic fatty liver disease, liver fibrosis, stroke, and cardiovascular disease. However, the relationship between NPAR and gallstones (GS) has not yet been explored. Therefore, this study aimed to evaluate the association of the NPAR with the odds of GS and the age of patients at the time of their first GS surgery.MethodsParticipants were selected from the National Health and Nutrition Examination Survey (NHANES) in the United States, a nationally representative survey. Logistic regression analysis and dose-response curve were performed to analyze the relationship between NPAR and the prevalence of GS. Multiple linear regression analysis and dose-response curve were used to analyze the association between NPAR and the age of patients at the time of their first GS surgery. Subgroup analyses further explored the relationships between NPAR and age, sex, race, body mass index, hypertension, and diabetes.ResultsIn total, 7805 adults aged > 20 years were included in this study, of whom 838 had a history of GS. After adjusting for all potential confounders, each 1-unit increase in NPAR was found to be associated with a 4% increase in the prevalence of GS (odds ratio (OR): 1.04, 95% confidence interval (CI): 1.02, 1.07) and an advancement in the age of the patient at the time of the first GS surgery by 0.35 years (β = − 0.35, 95% CI: − 0.68, − 0.02). Dose-response curves further confirmed that NPAR was positively associated with the prevalence of GS and negatively associated with the age of patients at the time of their first GS surgery. The results of the subgroup analyses suggested that after adjusting for all potential confounders, the positive association of NPAR with the prevalence of GS was more pronounced in the 40–59-year-old (OR: 1.07, 95% CI: 1.02, 1.12), male (OR: 1.07, 95% CI: 1.02, 1.12), non-Hispanic Black (OR: 1.06, 95% CI: 1.01, 1.12), non-hypertensive (OR: 1.06, 95% CI: 1.02, 1.10), and non-diabetic populations (OR=: 1.05, 95% CI: 1.02, 1.08).ConclusionsThe higher the NPAR, the higher the prevalence of GS, and the earlier the age of the patient at the time of the first GS surgery. Due to the nature of cross-sectional study, it is not possible to determine a causal relationship between them.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12889-024-21071-x.Keywords: Gallstones, Neutrophil percentage to albumin ratio, Inflammation indicator, National health and nutrition examination survey, Cross-sectional study

Introduction

Gallstones (GS) is a globally prevalent digestive disease, especially in developed countries and regions such as the United States and Europe [1, 2]. The majority of patients with GS are asymptomatic, while a few may present with symptoms such as abdominal pain, nausea, and vomiting [3]. Despite being a benign disease, GS can cause serious consequences, such as gangrene and perforation of the gallbladder, severe cholangitis, pancreatitis, and even infectious shock [4]. In severe cases, GS can result in mortality. Therefore, most patients with GS must be cured by surgical treatment. The high prevalence of GS places a heavy burden on public health and healthcare worldwide [1]. In the United States alone, the annual healthcare costs associated with GS have been reported to reach $6 billion [5]. Therefore, identifying the risk factors for GS and finding reliable indicators to predict the risk of GS prevalence are important for reducing the burden of the disease.

The neutrophil percentage to albumin ratio (NPAR) is a recently developed indicator of inflammation that is strongly associated with various diseases. For example, high NPAR levels are an independent risk factor for all-cause mortality in patients with cardiovascular diseases [6, 7]. Similarly, high NPAR levels are independently associated with the severity of coronary atherosclerosis in patients with chronic kidney disease, which is more pronounced in women and elderly individuals [8]. Additionally, NPAR is associated with metabolic disorder-related diseases. For example, increased NPAR is significantly associated with an increased risk of developing non-alcoholic fatty liver disease (NAFLD) [9]. However, the relationship between the NPAR and GS has not yet been explored.

The pathogenesis of GS is complex, with numerous risk factors, including genetics, sex, age, diet, inflammatory response, oxidative stress, and metabolic disorders [10–12]. One of the main mechanisms of GS formation is bile supersaturation with cholesterol, facilitated by an inflammatory response [13, 14]. Oxidative stress can increase the formation of cholesterol crystals and stones through lipid peroxidation, which can affect the motor function of the gallbladder [13, 14]. In addition, various components of metabolic syndrome (such as obesity, diabetes mellitus, hypercholesterolemia, etc.) are known risk factors for the development of GS [15, 16]. As a novel indicator, the NPAR not only reflects the level of the inflammatory response and oxidative stress in vivo, but it is also closely associated with metabolic disorders [9]. Therefore, we hypothesized that there is a link between NPAR and GS.

The NPAR could help to deepen our understanding of the mechanisms involved in the formation of GS; thus, it is also expected to serve as a predictive tool for identifying individuals at a high risk of GS. Through an analysis of data from participants of the National Health and Nutrition Examination Survey (NHANES), this cross-sectional study explored the association of NPAR with the prevalence of GS and patient age at the time of the first GS surgery.

Methods

Study population

Participants were selected from the NHANES, a nationally representative survey project conducted in the United States and administered by the National Center for Health Statistics (NCHS). This project is a valuable resource for understanding and tracking the health trends and needs of the American population [17]. The NHANES plays a very important role in helping to identify health priorities, developing disease prevention strategies and public health policies, preventing chronic disease, improving nutrition, and promoting healthy lifestyles. It uses stratified multistage probability sampling (mainly consisting of the following steps: stratification, primary sampling units, secondary sampling units, household sampling, oversampling) to ensure that the sample is nationally representative. NHANES updated every 2 years for populations of approximately 10,000 people. The project was suspended in March 2020 due to the impact of COVID-19. Starting in 2017, the project collected information about GS in the population through questionnaires; therefore, we selected participants from that period (2017–2020.03) as the study population. NHANES consists of 4 main components, including demographic data, examination data, laboratory data and questionnaire data. A total of 15,560 participants were enrolled during the study period, of whom 6328 were aged < 20 years; these participants were excluded. Participants lacking information about GS (n = 22) and NPAR (n = 1341) were excluded. In addition, 64 individuals were excluded for lack of information on covariates such as hypertension (n = 10), diabetes (n = 3), coronary heart disease (n = 25), asthma (n = 5), cancer (n = 4), education level (n = 9), marital status (n = 5), and smoking status (n = 3). Ultimately, 7805 individuals were included in the study, and Fig. 1 shows details of the screening criteria for the study population.

Flowchart for participants from NHANES (2017–2020)

Data collection

Patients with GS were evaluated based on the questionnaire results. The questionnaire was administered only to adults aged 20 years and older; therefore, individuals < 20 years of age were not included in this study. Multiple covariates were included in this study, and detailed information is provided in Supplementary Table 1. All participants conducted two 24-hour dietary recalls (these recalls were conducted on non-consecutive days during the same survey cycle rather than across different years); therefore, the average consumption of the two recalls was used in the analysis. The measurement procedures for all covariates are available on the NHANES website (www.cdc.gov/nchs/nhanes/). NPAR was calculated as the neutrophil ratio (%)/albumin level (g/dL).

Statement of ethics

All NHANES participants signed an informed consent form; therefore, no additional permission or ethical review was required for this study.

Statistical methods

Categorical variables were presented as numbers and percentages (%), and comparisons between groups were made using Pearson’s χ2 test or Fisher’s exact test as appropriate. Continuous variables were expressed as medians with interquartile ranges (IQRs) and comparisons of continuous variables between groups were conducted using the t-test or one-way ANOVA. We constructed multivariable logistic regression models to examine the association between the NPAR and the prevalence of GS. Multiple linear regression analysis was used to analyze the association between NPAR and the age of patients at the time of their first GS surgery. Model 1 was unadjusted, Model 2 was adjusted for age, sex, race, and marital status, and Model 3 was additionally adjusted for education level, physical activity, smoked status, alcohol consumption, diabetes, hypertension, asthma, cancers, coronary heart disease, total cholesterol, triglyceride, high-density lipoprotein cholesterol, low-density lipoprotein cholesterol, alanine aminotransferase, aspartate aminotransferase, haemoglobin A1c, ferritin, poverty income ratio, total kcal, total fat, and total sugar. In each regression model, we ensured the normality of residuals by performing diagnostic tests to confirm the validity and robustness of the models. To further investigate the relationship between NPAR, the prevalence of GS, and age at first GS surgery, we employed generalized additive models (GAM) with smoothed curve fitting (penalized spline method). For nonlinear relationships, we calculated the inflection points using a likelihood ratio test. Notably, the analysis between NPAR and age at first surgery was only performed in those who had undergone GS surgery. Subgroup analyses were conducted to evaluate these relationships across different strata, including age, sex, race, body mass index (BMI), hypertension, and diabetes. Statistical significance was defined as P < 0.05. All analyses were conducted using R software version 4.0.2 (The R Foundation for Statistical Computing, Vienna, Austria) and Empower software (X&Y Solutions Inc., Boston, MA, USA).

Results

Baseline characteristics

Ultimately, 7805 participants were included in the analysis, including 3778 males and 4027 females (Table 1). Based on the questionnaire results, 838 participants had GS. The NPAR was significantly higher in the GS group compared to the non-GS group [14.07 (12.31–15.86) vs. 14.86 (13.15–16.69), P < 0.001]. In addition, the GS group was older, had a higher BMI, and had a higher probability of hypertension, diabetes, asthma, coronary heart disease, and cancer than the non-GS group (all P < 0.001). Moreover, there were significant differences between the two groups in other aspects such as sex, race, physical activity, marital status, alcohol consumption, total kcal intake, total fat intake, triglyceride, and hemoglobin A1c (all P < 0.001).

A higher NPAR is associated with a higher prevalence of GS

The results of multivariable logistic regression showed that the NPAR was positively associated with the prevalence of GS; this relationship was maintained even after adjusting for all potential confounders (odds ratio (OR): 1.04, 95% confidence interval (CI): 1.02, 1.07, Table 2). Therefore, every 1-unit increase in NPAR is associated with a 4% increase in the odds of GS. When we converted the NPAR from a continuous variable to a categorical variable (tertiles) for the sensitivity analyses, the results suggested a significant 1.41-fold increase in GS prevalence at tertile 3 (OR: 1.41, 95% CI: 1.16, 1.72) compared with the lowest NPAR tertile (tertile 1). The relationship between NPAR and the prevalence of GS was further analyzed using additive generalized models and smoothed curve fitting. These results suggest that NPAR is positively correlated with the prevalence of GS (Fig. 2). The results of the piecewise linear regression (PLR) test suggested that when the NPAR was < 18.91, the OR of NPAR to GS prevalence was 1.05 (95% CI: 1.01, 1.08), implying that for every unit increase in NPAR, there was a 5% increase in the prevalence of GS; this result was statistically significant. When the NPAR was ≥ 18.91, the OR of NPAR to GS prevalence was 1.02 (95% CI: 0.92, 1.13), implying a 2% increase in GS prevalence for each unit increase in NPAR; however, this result was not statistically significant. The results of the univariate linear regression (ULR) test suggested that the NPAR remained positively associated with the overall context (OR: 1.04, 95% CI: 1.02, 1.07). Furthermore, the logarithmic likelihood ratio (LLR) test results suggested that there was no significant threshold effect at NPAR = 18.91 (P = 0.70).

Subgroup analysis

Subgroup analyses further assessed the robustness of the association between the NPAR and the prevalence of GS (Table 3). The results suggested that after adjusting for all potential confounders, the positive association of the NPAR with the prevalence of GS was more pronounced in 40–59-year-olds (OR: 1.07, 95% CI: 1.02, 1.12), males (OR: 1.07, 95% CI: 1.02, 1.12), non-Hispanic Black population (OR: 1.06, 95% CI: 1.01, 1.12), the non-hypertensive population (OR: 1.06, 95% CI: 1.02, 1.10), and the non-diabetic population (OR: 1.05, 95% CI: 1.02, 1.08).

A higher NPAR may be associated with an earlier age of the patient at the time of first GS surgery

The analysis between NPAR and age at first surgery was only performed in those who had undergone GS surgery. 838 patients with a self-reported history of GS were included in this study, and 618 underwent surgery. The results of the multiple linear regression analyses suggested that increased NPAR was associated with earlier age at the time of the patient’s first gallstone surgery in Model 1 (β = − 0.14, 95% CI: − 0.51, 0.23) and Model 2 (β = − 0.20, 95% CI: − 0.56, 0.14), but this relationship was not yet statistically significant. However, in Model 3, this relationship was statistically significant when adjusted for all potential confounders. Specifically, for each 1-unit increase in NPAR, the age at which patients underwent their first GS procedure might be 0.35 years earlier (β = − 0.35, 95% CI: − 0.68, − 0.02). The results of the generalized additive model and smoothed curve fitting further confirmed that the NPAR was negatively correlated with patient age at the time of the first GS surgery (Fig. 3). The PLR test showed that when NPAR < 20.06, the OR between NPAR and the patient’s age at the time of first GS surgery was − 0.19 (95% CI: − 0.56, 0.19), which implies that for each unit increase in NPAR, the patient’s age at the time of first GS surgery was 0.19 years earlier; however, this result was not statistically significant. When NPAR was ≥ 20.06, the OR for the age of patients at the time of first GS surgery was − 1.86 (95% CI: − 3.60, − 0.11), which meant that for each unit increase in NPAR, the age of patients at the time of first GS surgery was 1.86 years earlier; this result was statistically significant. The results of ULR test showed that NPAR was still negatively correlated with patients’ age at first GS surgery (OR: − 0.35, 95% CI: − 0.68, − 0.02). In addition, the LLR test showed no significant threshold effect at NPAR = 20.06 (P = 0.08). Overall, the higher the NPAR, the earlier the patient’s age at the time of the first GS surgery was likely to be.

Discussion

To the best of our knowledge, this is the first comprehensive analysis of the relationship between the NPAR and GS. By analyzing data from a nationally representative sample of 7805 adult participants, we found a positive correlation between the NPAR and the prevalence of GS, and a negative correlation with the age of the patient at the time of the first GS surgery. Specifically, for every 1-unit increase in the NPAR, the prevalence of GS would increase by 4%, and the age of patients at first GS surgery would advance by 0.35 years. Furthermore, we found that the positive association between the NPAR and GS prevalence was more pronounced in 40–59-year-old, male, non-Hispanic Black, non-hypertensive, and non-diabetic populations.

Despite being a benign disease, GS has a complex pathogenesis influenced by various risk factors [12, 18]. GS tends to cluster in families, and mutations in genes such as ABCG5 and ABCG8 elevate GS risk [19]. Sex, age, and race also play significant roles. GS is more prevalent in women due to hormonal influences, especially estrogen and progesterone [2, 20]. Estrogen increases cholesterol synthesis while inhibiting bile acid production, leading to cholesterol stone formation [21–23]. Progesterone, particularly during pregnancy, slows gallbladder emptying, promoting stone formation [24]. Women’s lipid metabolism, characterized by higher fat storage and cholesterol levels, further elevates their risk [25–27]. Age is another key factor; as people age, cholesterol production increases while bile acid synthesis decreases, promoting cholesterol stones [28]. Changes in bile composition and reduced gallbladder function in older adults also contribute to GS [29, 30]. Race differences are notable, with GS more prevalent in Caucasians than Black populations, likely due to differences in diet, lifestyle, and healthcare [29, 31]. Everhart et al. [31] reported that GS prevalence was 16.6% in White females and 13.9% in Black females, while it was 8.6% in White males compared to 5.3% in Black males. In our study, non-Hispanic Whites had the highest GS prevalence, followed by Mexican Americans.

To clarify the relationship between NPAR and GS, it is important to delve further into the roles of inflammation and oxidative stress, which are key factors in GS formation. Inflammation and oxidative stress not only alter bile composition, increasing cholesterol saturation and crystal formation, but they also contribute to the development of gallbladder dyskinesia [32, 33]. Chronic inflammation can impair gallbladder motility by disrupting the contractile function of gallbladder smooth muscle cells, which is exacerbated by the production of cytokines such as IL-1, IL-6, and TNF-α during inflammation [34, 35]. These cytokines interfere with the neuromuscular transmission that coordinates gallbladder contraction and bile release, promoting bile stasis, which is a known precursor to GS formation.

Furthermore, oxidative stress leads to the generation of reactive oxygen species (ROS) that directly interact with lipids and cholesterol in the bile, forming oxidized cholesterol derivatives. These oxidized compounds are less soluble in bile and are more prone to precipitate, thereby facilitating the nucleation and growth of cholesterol crystals [36, 37]. Over time, this process increases the likelihood of cholesterol stone formation, especially when coupled with impaired bile flow due to gallbladder dysfunction.

Additionally, systemic inflammation, as reflected by elevated NPAR, indicates a heightened state of immune activation and oxidative stress, which is central to the pathogenesis of GS. Neutrophils, a key component of the NPAR, contribute to the generation of ROS and release enzymes such as myeloperoxidase and elastase that exacerbate tissue damage and promote chronic inflammation. These enzymes can impair the structural integrity of the gallbladder epithelium and smooth muscle, further aggravating cholestasis and predisposing individuals to stone formation [13, 14].

Given that NPAR reflects both neutrophil-driven inflammation and hypoalbuminemia, which is often associated with chronic disease and malnutrition, it serves as a comprehensive indicator of a patient’s overall inflammatory and oxidative state [38]. As inflammation and oxidative stress are core mechanisms in GS pathogenesis, the positive association between higher NPAR and increased GS prevalence observed in our study aligns with these underlying biological processes. The fact that a higher NPAR is also associated with an earlier age of GS surgery suggests that patients with elevated systemic inflammation and oxidative stress may experience more rapid progression of gallstone disease, necessitating earlier surgical intervention.

Moreover, considering the growing body of evidence linking NPAR with other diseases characterized by chronic inflammation and oxidative stress, such as cardiovascular disease, ischemic stroke, and NAFLD [8, 9, 38–42], the NPAR may represent a broader marker of susceptibility to inflammatory conditions, including GS. The predictive power of the NPAR, as shown in studies like that of Xu et al. [43] in peritoneal dialysis patients and Zawiah et al. [41] in stroke-associated pneumonia, further underscores its clinical utility in identifying patients at higher risk for complications associated with inflammation.

In conclusion, our study is the first to establish a link between NPAR and GS, emphasizing the role of systemic inflammation and oxidative stress in GS pathogenesis. The NPAR, being an accessible and cost-effective measure, offers potential as a risk stratification tool in clinical practice, allowing for early identification and intervention in individuals at elevated risk of developing gallstones and requiring surgical treatment. This novel application of NPAR could provide clinicians with a simple yet effective method for monitoring and managing patients with gallstone disease, particularly those with other underlying inflammatory conditions.

In clinical practice, surgical intervention is not always necessary unless GS cause complications. For example, acute cholecystitis is usually due to obstruction of the gallbladder duct by GS, leading to increased pressure in the gallbladder and subsequent infection [2, 4]. In clinical practice, the standard treatment for acute cholecystitis includes the use of antibiotics and surgical intervention. The most common surgical procedure is laparoscopic cholecystectomy, which is considered the gold standard for the treatment of acute cholecystitis [2, 4]. Laparoscopic surgery is widely used to treat this condition because of its less invasive nature, quicker recovery, and shorter hospitalization [4]. For patients with mild symptoms, conservative treatments such as fasting, intravenous fluids, antibiotics and pain relief can be used. However, if the patient’s condition is severe or there is no improvement after taking conservative treatment measures, early surgical treatment is recommended [2, 20].

Our study has several strengths. First, this study was the first to assess the association of the NPAR with the prevalence of GS and patient age at the time of first GS surgery. Second, our analyses were based on a nationally representative sample of American adults. Therefore, our results provide a good representation of the health status of the entire population of the United States. Finally, our analyses considered all confounding factors that could have influenced the results; therefore, our findings are most applicable to adult populations in the U.S. and similar settings where the distribution of key demographic and health factors (such as age, sex, race, BMI, and comorbid conditions) are comparable to those represented in NHANES.

However, this study has some limitations. First, this was a cross-sectional study, and we were unable to determine a causal relationship between the NPAR and the prevalence of GS. And the cross-sectional nature of the study limits the ability to generalize the findings to other countries or populations with different health profiles. Second, the diagnosis of GS was based on a questionnaire; thus, recall (recollection) bias may be present. In addition, due to the preliminary design of NHANES, we were only able to measure neutrophil percentage and albumin levels at a single time point, and these blood metrics may change over time, so we were unable to determine the relationship between the dynamics of NPAR and GS. Finally, although we adjusted for many potential confounders, we were unable to eliminate the effects of other unmeasured confounders.

Conclusion

In conclusion, this study explored, for the first time, the association between the NPAR and GS prevalence and patient age at the time of first GS surgery. High NPAR levels were associated with increased GS prevalence and earlier patient age at the time of the first GS surgery. Despite the inability to clarify causality, our results are interesting.
