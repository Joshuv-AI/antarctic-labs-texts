Validity of the T-MoCA for Screening MCI and Dementia and Its Equivalence to the K-MoCA-22
Authors: Haeyoon Kim, Yujin Jeong, Hansol Ji, Jong-Sik Park, In-Uk Song, Jong-Hee Sohn, Juhee Chin, Yeonwook Kang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundWith rapid population aging, the prevalence of cognitive impairment and dementia continues to rise, underscoring the need for accessible and reliable screening tools. The Telephone-Montreal Cognitive Assessment (T-MoCA) enables remote cognitive evaluation but has been rarely validated in non-Western populations or in patients with dementia. This study aimed to evaluate the diagnostic validity of the T-MoCA and its score equivalence to the corresponding face-to-face version, the Korean version of the MoCA (K-MoCA)-22, across the full cognitive spectrum in older Korean adults.MethodsThe participants included 52 cognitively normal (CN) adults, 51 with mild cognitive impairment (MCI), and 50 with dementia. All participants completed the T-MoCA, the K-MoCA-30, and Korean-Mini Mental State Examination, 2nd Edition (K-MMSE-2). The K-MoCA-22 scores were derived from the K-MoCA-30 by excluding visuomotor items. The T-MoCA and K-MoCA-30 were administered approximately one month apart.ResultsThe T-MoCA showed moderate to strong correlations with the K-MoCA-30 and moderate correlations with the K-MMSE-2. T-MoCA scores declined progressively from CN to MCI to dementia, with significant between-group differences consistent with those observed for the K-MoCA-30. No significant differences in the areas under the curve were found between the T-MoCA and K-MoCA-30, or between the T-MoCA and K-MMSE-2, indicating comparable discriminability. Lin’s concordance correlation coefficients showed good overall agreement, and Bradley–Blackwood tests revealed no significant differences in means or variances between the T-MoCA and K-MoCA-22. Bland–Altman plots showed no systematic bias, with differences symmetrically distributed around zero.ConclusionThese findings support the T-MoCA as a valid, reliable, and clinically equivalent alternative to in-person MoCA formats for older Korean adults, encompassing normal cognition, MCI and dementia. As a practical and accessible tool for remote cognitive screening, the T-MoCA is expected to facilitate early detection and longitudinal monitoring of cognitive decline, particularly in community and telehealth settings.Keywords: Telephone-Montreal Cognitive Assessment, Dementia, Mild Cognitive Impairment, Cognitive Screening, Validity, Telehealth

INTRODUCTION

With the global population aging rapidly, the prevalence of cognitive impairment and dementia is also increasing, placing a growing burden on individuals, families, and healthcare systems.1,2 Early detection of cognitive decline has therefore become a key priority in clinical and public health efforts aimed at preventing or delaying dementia.3,4 Cognitive screening tests serve a critical role in both clinical and community settings by enabling the early detection of cognitive impairment, facilitating referral to comprehensive diagnostic evaluation, and supporting long-term monitoring.5,6 Among the most widely used instruments are the Mini Mental State Examination (MMSE)7,8 and the Montreal Cognitive Assessment (MoCA).9 The MoCA was developed to overcome the limitations of the MMSE by incorporating more rigorous assessments of executive function, attention, and memory, thereby improving sensitivity for detecting mild cognitive impairment (MCI) and early dementia.9,10,11 Consequently, the MoCA is recommended as a screening tool across various types of cognitive disorders, including Alzheimer’s disease (AD),11,12,13 vascular disorder,13,14,15,16 Parkinson’s disease,17,18,19 and frontotemporal dementia.20

To address the practical challenges of administering the MoCA in diverse settings, several modified versions have been developed. Among these, the MoCA-Blind (MoCA-22)21 and the Telephone-MoCA (T-MoCA)22 have drawn particular attention because they are especially useful for assessing cognition in individuals with visual impairment, stroke, or movement disorders, where standard MoCA administration may not be feasible. Both versions exclude four visuospatial and motor tasks—Alternating Trail Making, Cube Copy, Clock Drawing, and Naming—yielding a 22-point scale with identical cognitive items. The MoCA-22 is administered face-to-face, whereas the T-MoCA is conducted by telephone, making it particularly useful for large-scale community screening or when in-person testing is not feasible. Previous studies have demonstrated that both the MoCA-22 and the T-MoCA have acceptable utility across various clinical and community populations, including individuals with AD,21,23 transient ischemic attack,22 stroke,24,25 Parkinson’s disease,26 atrial fibrillation,27 and community-dwelling older adults.28,29,30 During the COVID-19 pandemic, the T-MoCA in particular gained recognition as a standardized tool for remote cognitive screening. Even beyond the pandemic, remote assessment continues to play a critical role in improving accessibility for aging populations, especially among individuals living in rural areas or with mobility limitations.31,32

Despite its growing clinical use, empirical evidence regarding the validity of T-MoCA and its score equivalence to MoCA-22 in non-Western older adults remains limited. Most existing studies have examined highly educated samples (average 14–16 years of education),23,28 and have rarely included individuals with lower educational attainment. Cultural and linguistic factors may further affect test performance, underscoring the need to validate the T-MoCA in specific populations such as older Korean adults—of whom only 38.2% in 2023 had completed high school or higher.33

Given the increasing reliance on remote cognitive assessment, establishing score equivalence between the T-MoCA and MoCA-22 is essential. Although the two versions share identical test items, they differ in their mode of administration (telephone vs. face-to-face). Demonstrating such equivalence ensures that results obtained via remote testing are directly comparable to those from in-person assessments, thereby enabling clinicians and researchers to use both formats interchangeably for initial screening and longitudinal follow-up and to maintain continuity of cognitive data across remote and in-person assessments.

Although the Korean version of the MoCA-22 (K-MoCA-22) has recently been validated in older Korean adults34 and equivalence between the K-MoCA-22 and the T-MoCA has been examined in community-dwelling cognitively normal (CN) elderly,29 the diagnostic validity of the T-MoCA and its score equivalence to the K-MoCA-22 have not been investigated in clinical populations. Moreover, prior studies often compared only CN individuals with those who have MCI,28 or combined MCI and dementia groups,23 leaving the test’s ability to distinguish MCI from dementia unclear. To date, no study has examined the discriminative validity of the T-MoCA or its score equivalence to the MoCA-22 in patients with dementia as a distinct diagnostic group.

Extending previous work, the present study broadens validation efforts to include patients with dementia—a population underrepresented in earlier T-MoCA research. By assessing both the discriminative validity of the T-MoCA and its score equivalence to the face-to-face K-MoCA-22, this study aims to determine whether the T-MoCA can serve as a clinically interchangeable alternative to in-person cognitive screening across the full spectrum of older Korean adults.

METHODS

Participants

Participants included 52 CN older adults, 51 patients with MCI, and 50 patients with mild to moderate dementia. Healthy older adults were recruited through community outreach. Those who met Christensen’s health screening criteria35 and demonstrated normal performance on the Korean-Mini Mental State Examination, 2nd Edition (K-MMSE-2),36 defined using an age- and education-adjusted norm-based cut-off score (≥ −1 standard deviation [SD], corresponding to the 16th percentile or higher), were included in the CN group.

Patients with MCI or dementia were recruited from the neurology departments of university hospitals. All patients underwent a standardized clinical evaluation for dementia, including a comprehensive neuropsychological test battery and brain imaging studies. MCI was diagnosed based on the modified Petersen’s criteria,37 and included amnestic, non-amnestic, single-domain, and multiple-domain subtypes. Dementia was diagnosed based on the fifth edition of the Diagnostic and Statistical Manual of Mental Disorders, 5th Edition.38 Cognitive impairment was defined as performance at least 1.0 SD below normative data on the comprehensive neuropsychological battery, and functional status was assessed using the Korean Instrumental Activities of Daily Living (IADL).39 Final diagnoses of MCI and dementia were made by dementia specialists (neurologists). The MCI group included individuals with a Clinical Dementia Rating (CDR)40 global score (CDR-GS) of 0 or 0.5 (4 non-amnestic patients with CDR-GS = 0 and 47 with CDR-GS = 0.5; all had CDR-sum of boxes [CDR-SB] ≥ 0.5), whereas the dementia group included individuals with CDR-GS scores of 1.0 or 2.0 (46 patients with CDR-GS = 1.0 and 4 with CDR-GS = 2.0).

Among the MCI group, 44 (86.27%) had amnestic multiple-domain MCI, 4 (7.84%) had non-amnestic multiple-domain MCI, and 3 (5.88%) had non-amnestic single-domain MCI. The dementia group included patients with major neurocognitive disorder due to AD (n = 24, 48.00%), multiple etiologies (n = 14, 28.00%), vascular disease (n = 7, 14.00%), and Parkinson’s disease (n = 5, 10.00%).

Measures

All participants completed the T-MoCA, the Korean version of the MoCA (hereafter referred to as K-MoCA-30 to distinguish it from the 22-item version), and the K-MMSE-2. In accordance with the standard 22-point protocol, the K-MoCA-22 score was derived from the K-MoCA-30 by excluding visuomotor items. In the CN group, the T-MoCA (telephone) and K-MoCA-30 (in-person) were administered approximately one month apart, and the order of administration was counterbalanced across participants. In the patient groups (MCI and dementia), the order was not counterbalanced. The K-MoCA-30 was always administered first during routine outpatient visits, and those who provided consent completed the T-MoCA by telephone approximately one month later, consistent with procedures used in prior studies.22,28

The T-MoCA was administered by telephone following standardized procedure. Participants were contacted in advance and instructed to remain in a quiet environment free of background noise and without individuals present during the assessment. When a caregiver could be reached beforehand, the caregiver was asked to assist by moving the patient to a quiet room and ensuring that the participant could remain alone through the test. To minimize the possibility of receiving assistance or writing down information, participants were instructed to keep all pens and paper out of reach and to hold the phone with their dominant hand so that it remained occupied during the assessment.

Statistical analysis

Group differences in baseline demographic and clinical characteristics were examined using analyses of variance (ANOVAs) and Pearson’s χ2 tests, with Bonferroni post hoc comparisons following significant ANOVA results. Associations between five cognitive test measures (T-MoCA, K-MoCA-22, K-MoCA-30, K-MMSE-2, and the difference score between the T-MoCA and K-MoCA-22) and demographic variables were examined using multivariate regression.

Convergent validity was assessed with partial correlations among the T-MoCA, K-MoCA-30, and K-MMSE-2, controlling for age and education. To compare performance across diagnostic groups, analyses of covariance (ANCOVAs) were conducted for total scores, with age and education as covariates. Multivariate ANCOVA (MANCOVA) was used for six T-MoCA domains index scores. Receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curve analyses evaluated each test’s discriminative ability, and areas under the curve (AUCs) were compared using the Hanley and McNeil method.41

Agreement between the T-MoCA and K-MoCA-22 was examined through paired t-tests for total and subtest scores, Lin’s concordance correlation coefficient (CCC),42,43 the Bradley–Blackwood test44 for equality of means and variances, and Bland–Altman plots45 to visualize score difference against their means.

All analyses were conducted using SPSS 25.0 (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA) for ANOVA, correlations, χ2 tests, and MANCOVA; R 4.3.2 for equivalence analyses; and SAS 9.2 (SAS Institute Inc., Cary, NC, USA) for ROC curve analyses.

Ethics statement

The present study protocol was reviewed and approved by the Institutional Review Board of Hallym University (approval No. HIRB-2024-103), Chuncheon Sacred Heart Hospital (approval No. 2025-05-005-001), and Incheon St. Mary’s Hospital (approval No. OC25QISI0024). Informed consent was waived because of the retrospective nature of the study.

Validity of the T-MoCA

Partial correlations controlling for age and education (Table 3) showed strong correlations between the T-MoCA and K-MoCA-30 in the CN (r = 0.74, P < 0.001) and dementia (r = 0.71, P < 0.001), and moderate in MCI (r = 0.55, P < 0.001). Correlations with K-MMSE-2 were moderate across all groups (CN= 0.50, MCI = 0.45, dementia = 0.69; all P < 0.001). In addition, correlations between the T-MoCA and K-MoCA-22 were strong in the CN (r = 0.70, P < 0.001) and the dementia groups (r = 0.71, P < 0.001), and moderate in MCI group (r = 0.61, P < 0.001).

Equivalence between T-MoCA and K-MoCA-22

ANCOVA showed no group effect on the mean difference between the T-MoCA and K-MoCA-22 scores (F[2, 148] = 0.49, P = 0.612). Paired t-tests confirmed non-significant mean difference in CN (t[51] = 1.18, P = 0.243), MCI (t[50] = 1.41. P = 0.166) and dementia (t[49] = 0.26, P = 0.798). At the subtest level, CN showed higher repetition on the K-MoCA-22 (t[51] = −2.47, P = 0.017), and higher delayed recall on T-MoCA (t[51] = 2.42, P = 0.019). MCI also showed higher delayed recall on T-MoCA (t[50] = 2.59, P = 0.013). No subtest differences were observed in dementia (Table 7).

DISCUSSION

This study evaluated the diagnostic validity of the T-MoCA and its equivalence to the face-to-face K-MoCA-22 in Korean older adults with normal cognition, MCI, and dementia. To our knowledge, this is the first study to demonstrate both the clinical utility and the equivalence of the T-MoCA in a sample that includes individuals with MCI and dementia.

We examined convergent validity by relating T-MoCA scores to established cognitive screening measures (K-MoCA-30 and K-MMSE-2). Across groups, the T-MoCA showed moderate-to-strong correlations with K-MoCA-30 (r = 0.55–0.74) and K-MoCA-22 (r = 0.61–0.71), whereas correlations with the K-MMSE-2 were moderate (r = 0.45–0.69). The slightly lower correlations with the K-MMSE-2 likely reflect its limited coverage of frontal-executive and attentional domains,46 reducing its conceptual overlap with MoCA-based instruments. Notably, the strong correlations with the K-MoCA-22 indicate good convergence between the telephone and face-to-face MoCA formats, which assess identical cognitive content.

To evaluate discriminability, we examined whether the T-MoCA differentiated among CN, MCI, and dementia groups. Consistent with previous findings,23,34 total and MIS index scores declined with increasing cognitive severity. ROC curve analyses demonstrated excellent diagnostic accuracy across all pairwise group comparisons (AUCs = 0.96–1.00). The discriminability of the T-MoCA was statistically comparable to that of the K-MoCA-30 and K-MMSE-2, supporting its validity as a brief, telephone-based tool of identifying both early and more advanced cognitive impairment.

To assess equivalence, we compared the T-MoCA and K-MoCA-22 scores across diagnostic groups. Mean differences did not vary by group, suggesting stable comparability across the cognitive spectrum. At the subtest level, consistent with prior findings in community-dwelling older Koreans,29 CN participants showed higher repetition scores on the K-MoCA-22 and higher delayed recall scores on the T-MoCA; in MCI, only delayed recall was higher on the T-MoCA; and in dementia, no subtest differences emerged. These patterns may reflect the absence of examiner-provided visual cues during telephone administration, which may influence repetition,47,48 as well as reduced test anxiety49,50 or the use of memory aids (i.e., “cheating”)51,52 that can enhance delayed recall during remote testing. Supporting this interpretation, MCI participants with higher delayed recall on the T-MoCA were younger (72.55 vs. 77.58 years) and had lower CDR-SB scores (0.95 vs. 1.57) than those who did not, suggesting that relatively preserved cognition and emotional regulation facilitated better performance under telephone condition. As cognitive impairment progresses, these advantages diminish, leading to parallel declines across formats. Previous studies also indicate that audiovisual integration and lip-reading abilities decline by the MCI stage,53 while performance anxiety decreases in dementia due to reduced insight or awareness of performance expectations.54 Clinicians should be aware that telephone administration may slightly elevate delayed-recall performance in individuals with milder impairment, such as those with MCI.

Lin’s CCC indicated good overall agreement between the T-MoCA and K-MoCA-22 in the entire sample, with moderate agreement within CN, MCI, and dementia groups, consistent with values previously reported in CN adults.29 The relatively lower CCC in MCI likely reflects range restriction and the transitional, heterogeneous nature of this group, in which performance is less stable across testing conditions than in individuals who are clearly normal or demented. Bias-correction coefficients were close to 1.0 across groups, indicating minimal systematic bias between formats. Across diagnostic strata, the Bradley–Blackwood test detected no discordance in means or variances, and Bland–Altman plots showed differences symmetrically distributed around zero, supporting consistent score interpretation under standardized administration. Taken together, these converging results indicate that the two formats are well aligned across the score range.

Demonstrating such equivalence not only validates the T-MoCA as a reliable remote cognitive screening tool but also establishes its clinical interchangeability with the face-to-face K-MoCA-22. This finding has particular relevance for longitudinal and multicenter studies where mixed administration formats are common. The ability to integrate telephone-based and in-person assessments enhances continuity of cognitive data, facilitates flexible scheduling for individuals with mobility or geographical barriers, and supports large-scale cognitive surveillance in aging populations.

Several practical considerations emerged regarding dementia. Among dementia participants who initially consented (N = 67), 25.37% did not complete the second assessment due to nursing-home placement limiting telephone access, clinical deterioration or hospitalization, withdrawal of consent, significant hearing loss, or limited mobile-phone literacy. Although CDR-GS or CDR-SB score did not differ significantly between completers (n = 50) and non-completers (n = 17), the latter had lower education (9.22 vs. 7.29 years; Table 8). Among dementia completers, 60.00% required caregiver assistance to initiate calls or handle devices, compared with 15.67% in MCI, highlighting the practical challenges of remote testing in this population. Although caregiver assistance was limited to logistical support and standardized procedures required caregivers to leave the room before testing began—with examiners confirming that patients were alone in a quiet environment—the possibility of subtle or unobserved caregiver influence cannot be fully excluded. Such influences may be more likely among individuals with greater functional dependence, and future studies may benefit from more structured documentation or monitoring of caregiver involvement during remote assessments.

Beyond caregiver-related considerations, additional procedural challenges emerged during telephone administration. Compared with CN, many MCI and most dementia participants required additional time and examiner support (e.g., repeated instructions), reflecting the increased cognitive demands of processing auditory-only instructions and the reduced availability of contextual cues in remote formats. These challenges align with evidence that individuals with cognitive impairment experience greater difficulty sustaining attention, interpreting verbal instructions, and adapting to unfamiliar testing contexts. Such demands place an increased burden on examiners to maintain standardization while ensuring comprehension and engagement. Therefore, careful examiner training, structured administration protocols, and sufficient time allocation are essential to preserve reliability and reduce variability in telephone-based cognitive assessments.

Taken together, these findings demonstrate that the T-MoCA is a valid and reliable tool for evaluating cognitive function and that its results are equivalent to those of the face-to-face K-MoCA-22. This equivalence supports interchangeable use across remote and in-person formats, facilitating broader access to cognitive screening for older adults who face mobility or geographical challenges and reinforcing a continuous care pathway spanning community screening, primary care, and specialized clinical services. To ensure consistency and quality, institutional standardization of remote testing—covering environmental conditions, administration protocols, and documentation—is critical. Ongoing examiner training and quality control are also essential to reduce variability across settings. Finally, integrating telephone-based assessments into routine clinical workflows, while addressing technology access and the digital divide, may further improve the accessibility and long-term sustainability of cognitive health services.

Despite these strengths, several limitations should be acknowledged. Although we were not able to counterbalance test order in the patient groups, the pattern of subtest scores suggests that modality-related influences are more important than pure order or practice effects. In the CN group, where both test order and mode of administration (face-to-face vs. telephone) were counterbalanced, the T-MoCA still yielded higher delayed recall scores and lower repetition scores than the K-MoCA-22, indicating that telephone and in-person administration can differentially affect these items independent of order. Therefore, although the fixed test order in the MCI and dementia groups represents a methodological limitation, it is unlikely to have fundamentally distorted the equivalence estimates.

This study included only patients with mild to moderate dementia. Future research should incorporate individuals across the full dementia spectrum, from very mild to severe stages, to further clarify the applicability of the T-MoCA in more advanced disease. Given prior evidence that T-MoCA performance is influenced by education,28,29 further analyses using interaction terms or stratified models are warranted. Combined with existing data on education effects in the K-MoCA-22,34 this will enable more accurate interpretation in clinical contexts. In addition, large-scale normative studies stratified by age and education are needed so that T-MoCA performance can be interpreted using age- and education-adjusted reference values. Finally, because etiologic diagnoses were not considered in the present study, future studies should examine T-MoCA validity across specific dementia subtypes (e.g., AD, vascular cognitive impairment) and determine whether T-MoCA–derived cognitive index scores capture subtype-specific cognitive profiles.
