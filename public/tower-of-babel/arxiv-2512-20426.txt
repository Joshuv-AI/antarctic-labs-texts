Metrologically advantageous states: long-range entanglement and asymmetric error correction Preprint: APS/123-QED
Junjie Chen, Rui Luo, Yuxuan Yan, You Zhou, Xiongfeng Ma

Abstract
Quantum metrology aims to exploit many-body quantum states to achieve parameter-estimation precision beyond the standard quantum limit. For unitary parameter encoding generated by local Hamiltonians, such enhancement is characterized by superlinear scaling of the quantum Fisher information (QFI) with system size. Despite extensive progress, a systematic understanding of which many-body quantum states can exhibit this scaling has remained elusive. Here, we develop a general framework that connects metrological performance to long-range entanglement, state-preparation complexity, and quantum error-correction properties. We prove that super-linear QFI scaling necessarily requires long-range entanglement by deriving rigorous complexity-dependent upper bounds on the QFI. We further show that, for two broad classes of quantum error-correcting codes, nondegenerate codes and Calderbank–Shor–Steane quantum low-density parity-check codes, a nonconstant code distance precludes super-linear QFI scaling for a wide class of local Hamiltonians, revealing a fundamental incompatibility between metrological sensitivity and protection against local noise. Finally, we identify constructive routes that evade this obstruction by exploiting asymmetric code structures. In particular, we show that states associated with classical low-density parity-check codes, as well as asymmetric toric code states, both having asymmetric logical distances, can achieve Heisenberg-limited scaling. Together, our results establish long-range entanglement and asymmetric error correction as the essential resource underlying quantum metrology and clarify the interplay among state complexity, error correction, and metrological power.

Introduction

Quantum metrology, exploiting many-body quantum states to estimate unknown parameters with a precision surpassing what is achievable classically

Focusing on local Hamiltonians, which are of primary physical interest, it is well established that entanglement is a necessary ingredient for surpassing the standard quantum limit
34
;
26

30

4
;
26

30

In this work, we address the problem of characterizing states with metrological advantage from three complementary perspectives. First, we establish a direct connection between long-range entanglement and metrological advantage. Specifically, we derive a rigorous upper bound on the quantum Fisher information based on state-preparation complexity, showing that super-linear scaling of the QFI necessarily requires long-range entanglement. Conversely, we also obtain a lower bound on the required state complexity for achieving a given scaling of the QFI. Furthermore, these bounds reveal intrinsic obstacles to preparing metrologically advantageous states with noisy quantum circuits
27
;
37

Having established that long-range entanglement is a necessary ingredient for metrological advantage, we turn to quantum error-correcting (QEC) code states, which constitute canonical examples of long-range entangled and noise-resilient many-body resources. Despite their robustness and widespread use, we surprisingly find that many important QEC constructions are fundamentally incompatible with metrological advantage. Specifically, for two broad classes of QEC codes, nondegenerate codes
3

8
;
32
;
20
;
24

This result naturally leads to the conjecture that the same limitation holds for general QEC code states with nonconstant distance. Physically, it reveals a fundamental tension between metrological sensitivity and the ability to protect against local noise, thereby ruling out a broad class of intuitive proposals that aim to combine error correction and metrology by directly employing self-protected or self-correcting physical code states as metrological probes.

Importantly, this obstruction is not universal and can be circumvented by relaxing the requirement of strong error-correction performance against all error types. We identify constructive routes toward metrologically advantageous states by exploiting asymmetry in error-correction structure

More generally, metrologically advantageous asymmetric code states are characterized by the presence of two mutually anticommuting logical operators, one with constant weight and the other with nonconstant weight, reflecting a fundamental asymmetry in error protection. II
Preliminaries

In this section, we provide the foundational concepts of quantum metrology and quantum state complexity (related to long-range entanglement). Consider the scenario where we aim to determine an unknown parameter
\theta
encoded in an
n
-qubit quantum state
\rho(\theta)
. To achieve this, we can prepare multiple copies of the quantum state and perform measurements on them. From the measurement outcomes, we can construct an estimator for
\theta
, denoted as
\hat{\theta}
. The estimator
\hat{\theta}
is considered unbiased if
\mathbb{E}(\hat{\theta}-\theta)=0
, where the expectation is taken over the different measurement outcomes.

Given that the estimator
\hat{\theta}
is unbiased, we also want to minimize its variance, defined as
(\Delta\hat{\theta})^{2}\equiv\mathbb{E}(\hat{\theta}-\theta)^{2}
. When measurements are applied identically, the quantum Cramér-Rao bound
6
provides a fundamental limit on the variance:

(\Delta\hat{\theta})^{2}\geq\frac{1}{N\mathcal{F}(\rho(\theta))}

(1)

where
N
is the number of copies of the quantum state that we measure, and
\mathcal{F}(\rho(\theta))
is the quantum Fisher information (QFI) of the state. It is important to note that Eq. (
1
) is independent of the specific choice of measurements or the construction of
\hat{\theta}
. Therefore, the QFI
\mathcal{F}(\rho(\theta))
represents the best achievable precision for estimating
\theta
. This bound also implies that to achieve a sufficiently accurate estimator for
\theta
, the sample size
N
must scale inversely with the QFI, highlighting the connection between sample complexity and the precision of the estimator.

In this work, we focus on the canonical case of phase estimation, a fundamental problem in quantum metrology
6
;
14
;
25
;
15
. In this setting, an initial probe state
\rho
evolves under a Hamiltonian
\hat{H}
according to the unitary transformation
\rho(\theta)=e^{-i\theta\hat{H}/2}\rho e^{i\theta\hat{H}/2}
, where the goal is to estimate the parameter
\theta
from the evolved state
\rho(\theta)
with the highest possible precision. The QFI for the state
\rho=\sum_{i}\lambda_{i}\ket{\psi_i}\bra{\psi_i}
, where
\lambda_{i}
are the eigenvalues of
\rho
and
\ket{\psi_i}
are its eigenvectors, is given by:

\mathcal{F}(\rho(\theta))=\mathcal{F}(\rho,\hat{H})=\frac{1}{2}\sum_{\lambda_{i}+\lambda_{j}\neq 0}\frac{(\lambda_{i}-\lambda_{j})^{2}}{\lambda_{i}+\lambda_{j}}\left|\bra{\psi_i}\hat{H}\ket{\psi_j}\right|^{2}.

(2)

When the initial state is pure, this expression simplifies to:

\mathcal{F}(\ket{\psi},\hat{H})=\bra{\psi}\hat{H}^{2}\ket{\psi}-\left|\bra{\psi}\hat{H}\ket{\psi}\right|^{2}.

(3)

The phase estimation problem is a central task in quantum metrology, as it determines the ultimate precision limits of quantum-enhanced measurements. It underlies a wide range of applications, including timekeeping, magnetic-field sensing, and interferometry. The QFI characterizes the fundamental bound on the achievable estimation precision and serves as a benchmark for comparing different quantum states and measurement strategies. Consequently, understanding and optimizing phase estimation protocols is essential for realizing the advantage of quantum metrology.

For a given Hamiltonian, different initial states
\rho
have varying impacts on the precision of quantum metrology, making the choice of initial state
\rho
crucial for minimizing the sample complexity. When the Hamiltonian is not specified, the corresponding state with optimal QFI can be various. In this work, we focus on physical Hamiltonians, which are local in nature. Specifically, we consider a
K
-local Hamiltonian of the form:

\hat{H}=\sum_{j=1}^{m}\hat{H}_{j}

(4)

where each
\hat{H}_{j}
acts on at most
K
qubits, and each qubit is acted upon by no more than
K
of these terms
\hat{H}_{j}
. To define this more formally, suppose an
n
-qubit operator
O
be decomposed as
O=O_{A}\otimes\mathbb{I}_{\bar{A}}
, with the subsystem
A\subseteq[n]
, where
O_{A}
cannot be further decomposed. We define
\mathrm{supp}(O)=A
as the support of
O
, i.e., the set of qubits on which
O
acts non-trivially. This definition of operators can be extended to quantum channels in a similar manner. Furthermore, we define the degree of qubit
i
for a set of operators
\mathcal{O}
as
\mathrm{deg}(i,\mathcal{O})=|\{O\in\mathcal{O}\mid i\in\mathrm{supp}(O)\}|
.
Based on these definitions, the decomposition of
\hat{H}
must satisfy the following two conditions:
\left|\mathrm{supp}(\hat{H}_{j})\right|\leq K
for all
j\in[m]
, and
\mathrm{deg}(i,\{\hat{H}_{j}\}_{j=1}^{m})\leq K
for all
i\in[n]
. Additionally, we require the norm of each term to be bounded,
\norm{\hat{H}_j}\leq 1
, and the number of terms to scale as
m=\Theta(n)
.

As we transition from quantum metrology to state complexity, we focus on the role of quantum circuits in preparing resource states for metrological tasks
28
;
22
;
5
. For quantum state preparation, unless stated otherwise, we assume the initial state is
\ket{\mathbf{0}}=\ket{0^n}
. A layer of
\kappa
-local channels, denoted as
\mathcal{C}
, can be decomposed as

\mathcal{C}=\prod_{\begin{subarray}{c}\sum_{j}B_{j}=[n],\left|B_{j}\right|\leq\kappa\\
\mathrm{supp}(\mathcal{C}_{B_{j}})=B_{j}\end{subarray}}\mathcal{C}_{B_{j}}

(5)

where
\sum_{j}B_{j}=[n]
means that
\bigcup_{j}B_{j}=[n]
and
B_{j}\cap B_{j^{\prime}}=\emptyset
for any
j\neq j^{\prime}
. Thus, each gate in
\mathcal{C}
acts non-trivially on at most
\kappa

\mathcal{C}_{B_{j}}
should keep the dimension of subsystem

unchanged. In this context, a quantum circuit prepares a state by applying a sequence of layers of
\kappa
-local channels
\mathcal{C}_{1},\mathcal{C}_{2},\ldots,\mathcal{C}_{t}

\ket{\mathbf{0}}
. The full channel

is given by the composition of these layers,
\mathscr{C}=\mathcal{C}_{t}\circ\cdots\circ\mathcal{C}_{2}\circ\mathcal{C}_{1}
. The circuit depth of

is denoted by

, and the circuit complexity of a state is defined as the minimum circuit depth required to prepare it

III
Long-range entanglement in quantum metrology

Long-range entangled states, typically defined as states that cannot be prepared by shallow circuits from product states, constitute important resources in quantum information processing
9
. Their presence is inherently tied to high state-preparation complexity, as shallow circuits are incapable of generating such entanglement efficiently. While long-range entanglement is known to be essential for tasks such as quantum error correction
35
;
7
and quantum simulation
18
, its role in quantum metrology remains unclear. The following theorem provides a rigorous result addressing this fundamental problem.

Theorem 1
.

Given
\hat{H}=\sum_{j=1}^{m}\hat{H}_{j}
as a
K
-local Hamiltonian, suppose
\rho
can be prepared by a depth-
t

\kappa
-local quantum circuit, i.e., there exists a quantum circuit
\mathscr{C}=\mathcal{C}_{t}\circ\cdots\circ\mathcal{C}_{2}\circ\mathcal{C}_{1}
such that each
\mathcal{C}_{j}
is
\kappa
-local for all
j\in[t]
, and
\rho=\mathscr{C}(\outerproduct{\mathbf{0}}{\mathbf{0}})
. Then, the quantum Fisher information is bounded by

\mathcal{F}(\rho,\hat{H})\leq mK^{2}\cdot g(\kappa,2t)

(6)

where
g(\kappa,t)
is a function related to the connectivity of the system, with its formal definition provided in Appendix
A
. Specifically,
g(\kappa,t)=\kappa^{t}
for an all-to-all connected system, and
g(\kappa,t)=(2(\kappa-1)t+1)^{r}
for an
r
-dimensional system.

The proof of Theorem
1
relies on a bound for the correlation function derived from the lightcone argument. For states prepared by such quantum circuits, two qubits exhibit a nonzero correlation function only if their forward lightcones intersect. Since the growth of the lightcones is constrained by a function of the circuit depth, the number of qubit pairs with nonzero correlations is also limited. Given that the quantum Fisher information can be bounded by the sum of correlation functions, the resulting quantum Fisher information is similarly bounded by a function of the circuit depth. The detailed proof is provided in Appendix
A
.

Eq. (
6
) shows that states prepared by shallow circuits, often referred to as trivial states, cannot achieve a quantum metrological advantage, as their quantum Fisher information is limited to
O(n)
scaling. This establishes long-range entanglement as a necessary ingredient for surpassing the classical precision limit in quantum metrology.

Conversely, Theorem
1
implies a lower bound on the circuit depth required to prepare metrologically advantageous states. As an illustration, the following corollary provides an explicit depth lower bound for preparing states that achieve optimal quantum Fisher information.

Corollary 1
.

Given
\hat{H}=\sum_{j=1}^{m}\hat{H}_{j}
as a
K
-local Hamiltonian, the state with optimal quantum Fisher information, denoted as
\ket{\psi^*}
, has a circuit complexity of
\Omega(\log n)
in all-to-all connected systems and of
\Omega(n^{1/r})
in
r
-dimensional systems.

Since long-range entangled states require relatively deep state-preparation circuits, they are inherently vulnerable to noise on present-day quantum devices. As a result, although the precise scaling of the quantum Fisher information with circuit depth in the regime from
\omega(1)
to
o(\log n)
remains unclear, it is plausible that noisy quantum circuits cannot generate states that exhibit a genuine metrological advantage.

We note that a recent work
13
investigates a problem related to ours. While both studies explore the connection between state-preparation complexity and quantum Fisher information, the physical settings and technical approaches differ substantially.
These differences lead to complementary perspectives on the role of complexity in quantum metrology.

IV
Large code distance implies bad metrological performance

In the previous section, we show that long-range entanglement is essential for surpassing the
O(n)
-scaling QFI, also known as the standard quantum limit. Quantum error-correcting code states with good code parameters are known to possess robust long-range entanglement, which suggests they may be promising candidates for quantum metrology. This raises the question of whether these states can achieve QFI scaling beyond
O(n)
, thereby offering an advantage in metrological tasks.

To address this, we first present the following theorem, which demonstrates that non-degenerate code states with good distance cannot exceed the shot-noise limit.

Theorem 2
.

Given
\hat{H}=\sum_{j=1}^{m}\hat{H}_{j}
as a
K
-local Hamiltonian, suppose
\ket{\psi}
is a code state of a non-degenerate code with code distance
d
, its quantum Fisher information is bounded by

\mathcal{F}(\ket{\psi},\hat{H})=mK^{2}

(7)

as long as
d\geq 2k+1
.

Proof sketch.

When
d\geq 2k+1
, the local Hamiltonian can be interpreted as errors that map the code subspace onto distinct orthogonal subspaces, as defined by non-degenerate codes. Intuitively, in this scenario, the effects of different local Hamiltonians
\hat{H}_{j}
do not coherently add up, which can be viewed as independent sensing of the parameter
\theta
. This leads to a linear QFI scaling,
\mathcal{F}(\ket{\psi},\hat{H})=O(m)
. Mathematically, when we express the QFI as a sum of correlation functions, the Knill-Laflamme conditions for error correction ensure that most of these correlations vanish. Nonzero contributions only arise when the supports of two local Hamiltonians intersect, and the number of such terms is bounded by
O(m)
. Thus, the QFI is also bounded by
O(m)
. Rigorous proof is presented in Appendix
B
.
∎

For degenerate codes, the absence of the orthogonal subspace property makes it difficult to provide a general theorem that excludes all code states with non-constant distance from good metrological performance. However, we can generalize the no-go theorem to CSS QLDPC code states. Before presenting the theorem, we first introduce some necessary notations.

Consider a CSS QLDPC code with
Z
-type stabilizer generators
\mathcal{Z}=\{S_{z,j}\}_{j=1}^{n_{z}}
and
X
-type stabilizer generators
\mathcal{X}=\{S_{x,j}\}_{j=1}^{n_{x}}
. Each stabilizer generator has constant weight, i.e.,
\mathrm{wt}(S_{z,j})\leq s
for all
j
and
\mathrm{wt}(S_{x,j})\leq s
for all
j
. Any
Z
-type stabilizer can be written as a product of
Z
-type stabilizer generators,
S_{z}(\vec{z})=\prod_{j=1}^{n_{z}}S_{z,j}^{z_{j}}
, where
\vec{z}=(z_{1},z_{2},\dots,z_{n_{z}})^{T}
is a binary vector. Similarly, an
X
-type stabilizer is written as
S_{x}(\vec{x})=\prod_{j=1}^{n_{x}}S_{x,j}^{x_{j}}
.

Next, we give an informal definition of the expansion properties used in the construction of QLDPC codes.

Definition 1

(Weak Systolic and Cosystolic Expansion (Informal))
.

A CSS QLDPC code has a weak systolic expansion property if any
Z
-type stabilizer
S_{z}(\vec{z})
that cannot be decomposed as a product of a constant number of
Z
-type stabilizer generators should have a non-constant weight. Specifically, if
\mathrm{wt}(\vec{z})=\omega(1)
, then
\mathrm{wt}(S_{z}(\vec{z}))=\omega(1)
.

The weak cosystolic expansion property can be similarly defined by replacing all the
z
-related terms by
x
-related terms.

We remark that the definition is informal since a rigorous definition needs to generalize the stabilizer generators to be not always independent, with a formal definition given in Appendix
C
. Equipped with this definition, we demonstrate that any CSS QLDPC code with weak systolic and cosystolic expansion properties cannot exhibit good metrology performance.

Theorem 3
.

Given
\hat{H}=\sum_{j=1}^{m}\hat{H}_{j}
as a
K
-local Hamiltonian, suppose
\ket{\psi}
is a code state of a QLDPC code with
n
physical qubits and distance
d
, satisfying both the weak cosystolic expansion and the weak systolic expansion properties, then its quantum Fisher information is bounded by
\mathcal{F}(\ket{\psi},\hat{H})=O(m)
as long as
K=O(1)
and
d=\omega(1)
, with respect to
n
.

The proof of Theorem
3
is provided in Appendix
C
. Roughly speaking, since QLDPC codes are stabilizer codes, bounding QFI reduces to bounding the number of elements in the same local error pattern. To be specific, we decompose the local Hamiltonians into Pauli operators, reducing the problem to considering only the correlation function of
K
-local Pauli operators. If two Pauli operators exhibit the same error pattern on the QLDPC code, their product must be a stabilizer of the code, contributing a nonzero term to the QFI. Otherwise, they map the code subspace onto distinct subspaces, contributing nothing to the QFI.

To show that the number of elements in the same local error pattern is always bounded, we observe that the nonzero contributions can only scale linearly with the number of terms
m
. The approach involves starting with a specific
K
-local error and identifying stabilizers that, when multiplied with it, result in another
K
-local error. By applying the weak systolic and cosystolic expansion properties, we can ultimately demonstrate that the number of distinct local errors corresponding to the same error pattern is bounded by a constant.

We note that, to the best of our knowledge, all constructed CSS QLDPC codes with non-constant distance satisfy the weak systolic and cosystolic expansion properties, including typical topological codes such as toric codes
20
, two-dimensional hyperbolic codes
1
, hypergraph product codes
33
, and good QLDPC codes
24
. Thus, we believe these properties hold for any CSS QLDPC code with a non-constant distance. Furthermore, for topological codes with geometric local stabilizers in finite dimensions, we rigorously prove that they always satisfy the weak systolic and cosystolic expansion properties.

Based on Theorem
3
, we conclude that QLDPC code states with non-constant distance and the weak systolic and cosystolic expansion properties cannot provide a metrological advantage over the classical limit. Extending this result to general code states, we conjecture that the same holds for any code state with non-constant distance.

Our results imply a fundamental difficulty of engineering resource states that are simultaneously highly noise-robust and metrologically powerful. We emphasize, however, that this limitation does not apply to metrological schemes that operate directly at the logical level, where logical qubits themselves serve as the fundamental sensing units
19
;
2
;
11
;
36
. Our results constrain only the metrological performance achievable at the level of physical qubits and do not preclude quantum-enhanced metrology based on encoded logical degrees of freedom.

V
Construction of metrologically advantageous states via asymmetric quantum error correction

We have shown that non-degenerate code states and CSS QLDPC code states with non-constant distance do not exhibit metrological advantage. This naturally raises the question of which classes of states can serve as useful resources for quantum metrology. In this section, we present two explicit constructions of metrologically advantageous states, which can reach the Heisenberg scaling of QFI.

The first construction is motivated by classical LDPC codes. Consider an
[n,k,d]
classical LDPC code with parity-check matrix
H_{z}=(\vec{z}_{1},\vec{z}_{2},\ldots,\vec{z}_{n-k})^{T}
and generator matrix
H_{x}=(\vec{x}_{1},\vec{x}_{2},\ldots,\vec{x}_{k})^{T}
, which satisfy the orthogonality condition
\vec{z}_{j}\cdot\vec{x}_{l}=0
for all
j
and
l
. We denote by
w
the row weight of
H_{z}
, which characterizes the maximum number of bits involved in a single parity check, and assume that the column weight of
H_{z}
is
2
. Such a code can be described by a graph
G
, whose vertices represent parity checks and edges represent qubits.

From such a classical code, we can construct a corresponding quantum error-correcting code with only
Z
-checks, whose stabilizer generators are given by
\{Z^{\vec{z}_{j}}\}_{j=1}^{n-k}
, where
Z^{\vec{z}_{j}}\equiv\prod_{i=1}^{n}Z_{i}^{z_{j}(i)}
. The logical
X
operators of this code can be chosen as
\bar{X}_{l}=X^{\vec{x}_{l}}
, which consist solely of Pauli-
X
operators. Correspondingly, the logical
Z
operators can be defined using only Pauli-
Z
operators. Therefore, the

-distance equals the distance of the original classical code, while the
z
-distance is simply
1

Z
operators are computational basis states, which are trivial from the perspectives of state complexity and quantum metrology. To obtain a nontrivial code state, we therefore require the state to be an eigenstate of at least one logical
X
operator.

With this notion, we show that when the underlying graph
G
possesses appropriate structure, the corresponding nontrivial classical LDPC code states can exhibit genuine metrological advantage for suitably chosen Hamiltonians. In particular, we consider the case where
G
has girth (the length of the shortest cycle)

and vertex degree at least
2
, examples of which are shown in Fig.

\ket{\psi}
associated with a nontrivial classical LDPC and further with such a graph, one can construct a local Hamiltonian
\hat{H}=\sum_{j=1}^{m}\hat{H}_{j}
for which the quantum Fisher information achieves the optimal scaling

\mathcal{F}(\ket{\psi},\hat{H})=m^{2}
. The detailed construction and proof are presented in Appendix
D
.

VI
Discussion

In this work, we established a unified framework connecting quantum metrology, long-range entanglement, and quantum error correction. We showed that surpassing the standard quantum limit under local Hamiltonians necessarily requires long-range entanglement, or equivalently, nonconstant state complexity. At the same time, we demonstrated that strong forms of error protection—such as non-degenerate codes and CSS QLDPC codes with non-constant distance—fundamentally constrain metrological performance under local Hamiltonians. These results reveal an intrinsic tension between metrological sensitivity and noise robustness. We further showed that this limitation can be circumvented by introducing asymmetry in code distance, and illustrated this mechanism through two explicit constructions of metrologically useful code states.

Several directions remain open. First, it is natural to ask whether our no-go results for non-degenerate codes and CSS QLDPC codes extend to more general quantum error-correcting codes. More broadly, can one formulate a quantitative and model-independent trade-off relating metrological usefulness (e.g., achievable QFI scaling under local Hamiltonians) to error-correction capability (e.g., distance, correctable error sets, or locality/expansion properties)? Establishing such a relation would sharpen the dilemma between sensitivity amplification and protection against local noise.

Second, our constructive results indicate that introducing asymmetry in code distance is an effective way to obtain metrologically useful states, while the approaches provided in Sec.
V
are unlikely to be unique. It would be interesting to explore other families of metrological advantageous states based on this idea, as well as to identify alternative construction principles that do not rely on distance asymmetry. Developing a broader set of explicit examples could help clarify which structural features of many-body states are most relevant for achieving enhanced metrological performance.

Third, our code-based constructions are naturally tailored to commuting Hamiltonians, where the metrological Hamiltonian aligns with structured logical operators. Extending these ideas to noncommuting local Hamiltonians is an important challenge. In particular, it would be valuable to understand how to engineer states with large QFI when the local terms do not mutually commute, and whether analogous error-pattern or expansion-based arguments can be adapted to this setting.

For each operator

The following lemma shows that the cardinality of an equivalence class is bounded by a constant, provided that the number of possible expanded connected components is bounded by a constant.

Lemma 1
.

If
|CC_{A}(\vec{x}^{*})|=O(1)
and
|CC_{A}(\vec{z}^{*})|=O(1)
, then
|A|=O(1)
.

Proof.

Distinct operators
\hat{H}_{j}
with
j\in A
correspond to distinct pairs
(\vec{x}_{j},\vec{z}_{j})=(\vec{x}_{j,1}+\vec{x}_{j,2},\vec{z}_{j,1}+\vec{z}_{j,2})
. If
|CC_{A}(\vec{x}^{*})|=O(1)
and
|CC_{A}(\vec{z}^{*})|=O(1)
, then the number of possible choices of
\vec{x}_{j,1}\in CC_{A}(\vec{x}^{*})
and
\vec{z}_{j,1}\in CC_{A}(\vec{z}^{*})
is bounded by a constant. Moreover, since Pauli
X
and Pauli
Z
operators do not cancel each other, and the supports of
\vec{x}_{j,1}
(respectively
\vec{z}_{j,1}
) and
\vec{x}_{j,2}
(respectively
\vec{z}_{j,2}
) are disjoint, any operator
\hat{H}_{j}
with fixed
\vec{x}_{j,1}
and
\vec{z}_{j,1}
must act nontrivially on the support of these vectors, regardless of the choice of
\vec{x}_{j,2}
and
\vec{z}_{j,2}
. By the assumption that the Hamiltonian is
K
-local with constant
K
, each qubit can be acted on by at most
O(1)
terms
\hat{H}_{j}
. Consequently, for fixed
\vec{x}_{j,1}
and
\vec{z}_{j,1}
, the number of possible choices of
\vec{x}_{j,2}
and
\vec{z}_{j,2}
is also bounded by a constant
K
. Combining these observations, we conclude that the numbers of allowed choices for
\vec{x}_{j,1}
,
\vec{z}_{j,1}
,
\vec{x}_{j,2}
, and
\vec{z}_{j,2}
are all
O(1)
. Therefore, the total number of distinct pairs
(\vec{x}_{j},\vec{z}_{j})
, and hence the cardinality of the equivalence class
A
, is bounded by a constant.
∎

Lemma
1
reduces the problem to bounding the sizes of
CC_{A}(\vec{x}^{*})
and
CC_{A}(\vec{z}^{*})
. The following lemma further shows that
|CC_{A}(\vec{x}^{*})|
is bounded provided that the number of possible choices of
\sigma
in Eq.

Lemma 2
.

For every
\hat{H}_{j}
with
j\in A
, consider its corresponding
\vec{x}_{j,1}=\vec{x}^{*}+\sum_{\sigma\in\Sigma_{j}}\delta^{k-1}\sigma
where
\Sigma_{j}
denotes a minimal set of
\sigma
required to express
\vec{x}_{j,1}\in CC(\vec{x}^{*})
. Then if

t_{A}\equiv\max_{j\in A}|\Sigma_{j}|=O(1)

(36)

we have
|CC_{A}(\vec{x}^{*})|=O(1)
.

Proof.

Define the set

\Sigma(A)\equiv\bigcup_{j\in A}\Sigma_{j},

(37)

which collects all
X
-checks that appear in the expansion of
CC(\vec{x}^{*})
. We now bound the size of this set.

To do so, consider the following expansion process:

\text{qubits (in $\mathrm{supp}(\vec{x}^{*})$)}\xrightarrow[\phantom{aaaaaa}]{\alpha_{1}}\text{checks}\xrightarrow[\phantom{aaaaaa}]{\beta_{1}}\text{qubits}\xrightarrow[\phantom{aaaaaa}]{\alpha_{2}}\cdots

(38)

More explicitly, to generate elements of
CC(\vec{x}^{*})
, we start from the qubits in the support of
\vec{x}^{*}
and identify all
X
-checks directly connected to them. The number of such checks is at most
\alpha_{1}|\mathrm{supp}(\vec{x}^{*})|
, where
\alpha_{1}
is bounded by the density
s
of the QLDPC code and hence is a constant. From these checks, we then identify all qubits directly connected to them, increasing the total number of qubits by at most a factor
\beta_{1}
, which is also bounded by the density
s
. Iterating this procedure alternately between checks and qubits for
t_{A}

X
-checks that may appear in
\Sigma(A)
.

As a result, we obtain the bound

|\Sigma(A)|\leq|\mathrm{supp}(\vec{x}^{*})|\cdot\prod_{i=1}^{t_{A}}\alpha_{i}\cdot\prod_{i=1}^{t_{A}-1}\beta_{i}\leq s^{2t_{A}-1}\cdot\mathrm{wt}(\vec{x}^{*})=O(1)

(39)

since
\alpha_{i},\beta_{i}\leq s=O(1)

|\mathrm{supp}(\vec{x}^{*})|=O(1)
.

Finally, when forming elements of

, each check in

may either be included or excluded. This yields the bound

|CC_{A}(\vec{x}^{*})|\leq 2^{|\Sigma(A)|}=O(1),

(40)

which completes the proof.

∎

Based on the former two lemmas, we finally reduce the problem to bounding the size

in Eq. (

For any operator
\hat{H}_{j}
with

Recall that the norm defined in Definition

\norm{f}=O(1)\;\Longleftrightarrow\;|\Sigma(f)|=O(1).

Once the chain complex is fixed and the spatial dimension of the physical qubits is specified, the constant prefactors associated with individual simplices can be absorbed into the norm definition. Since

It is quite straightforward to promote the assertion for the toric code into two-dimensional hyperbolic codes, as shown in the following proposition:

Appendix D
Detailed construction of metrologically advantageous states

As discussed in Sec.
V
, constructing metrologically advantageous states from classical LDPC codes with column weight
2
can be reduced to designing an appropriate underlying graph
G
. One such construction was presented in Sec.
V
without proof. In this section, we first introduce a general sufficient condition for constructing graphs
G
that yield metrologically advantageous states, and we prove that this condition indeed guarantees metrological usefulness. We then show that this framework encompasses the construction given in Sec.
V
.

We first present a sufficient, though not necessary, condition on the graph
G
for generating metrologically advantageous states. The graph
G
is required to satisfy the following properties:

1.

Every vertex in
G
has degree at least
2
and at most
w
where
w
is a constant.

2.

There exist two adjacent vertices
v_{s}
and
v_{t}
such that, after removing the edge
(v_{s},v_{t})
, the graph distance between
v_{s}
and
v_{t}
becomes
\Delta=\Theta(n)
.

3.

In the graph with the edge
(v_{s},v_{t})
removed, the breadth-first search tree
\mathcal{T}=(V_{\mathcal{T}},E_{\mathcal{T}})
rooted at
v_{s}
has depth
d_{\mathcal{T}}=\Theta(n)
and width
W_{\mathcal{T}}=O(1)
.

We denote by
Z_{e}
the Pauli-
Z
operator acting on the qubit associated with an edge
e
. For each vertex
v
, we denote by
Z_{v}
the corresponding
Z
-type stabilizer, defined as
Z_{v}=\prod_{e\ni v}Z_{e}
. More generally, for an edge set
E
, we write
Z_{E}
to denote the product of Pauli-
Z
operators acting on all qubits associated with edges in
E
; for a vertex set
V
, we write
Z_{V}
to denote the product of
Z
-type stabilizer corresponding to the vertices in
V
.

For the code state associated with such a graph
G
, we construct a local Hamiltonian as follows. We first define
\hat{H}_{1}=Z_{(v_{s},v_{t})}
, where
(v_{s},v_{t})
denotes the edge connecting the two distinguished vertices. At the
j
-th step, we define

\hat{H}_{j+1}
recursively by multiplying
\hat{H}_{j}
with
Z_{L_{j-1}}
, where
L_{j-1}
denotes the
(j-1)
-th level of the breadth-first search tree
\mathcal{T}
. Here, the zeroth level consists of the root vertex
v_{s}
, and the first level consists of its immediate neighbors. Repeating this procedure for
\Delta
steps yields the sequence

\hat{H}_{1},\hat{H}_{2},\ldots,\hat{H}_{\Delta+1}
. Since
\Delta\leq d_{\mathcal{T}}
by definition, (which also implies that
d_{\mathcal{T}}=\Theta(n)
can be naturally derived from
\Delta=\Theta(n)
,) this construction traverses successive levels of the tree and terminates just before reaching the level containing
v_{t}
. In conclusion, the final Hamiltonian is defined as

\hat{H}=\sum_{j=1}^{\Delta+1}\hat{H}_{j}=\sum_{j=1}^{\Delta+1}\left(Z_{(v_{s},v_{t})}\prod_{l=0}^{j-1}Z_{L_{j-1}}\right)

(47)

where we let
L_{-1}=\emptyset
for consistence.

Let
E_{j-1,j}
denote the set of edges connecting vertices in the
(j-1)
-th and
j
-th levels of the breadth-first search tree
\mathcal{T}
, namely,
E_{j-1,j}\equiv\left\{(v_{1},v_{2})\in E_{\mathcal{T}}|v_{1}\in L_{j-1},v_{2}\in L_{j}\right\}
. We can prove that
\hat{H}_{j+1}=Z_{E_{j-1,j}}
by induction. For the base case
j=1
, note that
Z_{v_{s}}=Z_{(v_{s},v_{t})}Z_{E_{0,1}}
, which implies
\hat{H}_{2}=Z_{E_{0,1}}
. Now, assume inductively that
\hat{H}_{j+1}=Z_{E_{j-1,j}}
holds for some
j
. By construction, we have

\hat{H}_{j+2}=\hat{H}_{j+1}Z_{L_{j}}
. The operator
Z_{L_{j}}
can be decomposed into contributions from edges connecting vertices in
L_{j}
to those in
L_{j-1}
and
L_{j+1}
. Edges with both endpoints in
L_{j}
appear twice in the product and therefore cancel out. As a result, we obtain
Z_{L_{j}}=Z_{E_{j-1,j}}Z_{E_{j,j+1}}
. Substituting this expression yields
\hat{H}_{j+2}=Z_{E_{j-1,j}}Z_{E_{j-1,j}}Z_{E_{j,j+1}}=Z_{E_{j,j+1}}
, which completes the inductive step and thus proves the claim.

Given the condition that the width of the tree
W_{\mathcal{T}}=O(1)
, we can bound the size of
E_{j-1,j}
by
|E_{j-1,j}|\leq w|L_{j-1}|\leq w\cdot W_{\mathcal{T}}
. Moreover, each qubit is acted on by at most one of
\hat{H}_{j}
. Thus, the Hamiltonian is
K
-local with
K\leq w\cdot W_{\mathcal{T}}=O(1)
.

Consider the logical
X
operators of the corresponding quantum error-correcting code, or equivalently, the generator matrix
H_{x}
. One can always choose a codeword
\vec{x}
whose support includes the qubit corresponding to the edge
(v_{s},v_{t})
. Otherwise, the operator
Z_{(v_{s},v_{t})}
would commute with all logical
X
operators and hence constitute an additional independent stabilizer, which is impossible.

Let
\ket{\psi}
be the logical eigenstate of
\bar{X}=X^{\vec{x}}
. Then we have
\bra{\psi}Z_{(v_{s},v_{t})}\ket{\psi}=\bra{\psi}X^{\vec{x}}Z_{(v_{s},v_{t})}X^{\vec{x}}\ket{\psi}=-\bra{\psi}Z_{(v_{s},v_{t})}\ket{\psi}=0
. By construction, each
\hat{H}_{j}
contains
Z_{(v_{s},v_{t})}
as a factor and the other part is a stabilizer, implying
\bra{\psi}\hat{H}_{j}\ket{\psi}=0
for all
j
. Moreover, for any
j
and
j^{\prime}
, the product
\hat{H}_{j}\hat{H}_{j^{\prime}}
can be written as a product of
Z
-checks,
\hat{H}_{j}\hat{H}_{j^{\prime}}=\prod_{l=0}^{j-1}Z_{L_{j-1}}\prod_{l^{\prime}=0}^{j^{\prime}-1}Z_{L_{j^{\prime}-1}}
, which is a stabilizer of the code. Consequently,
\bra{\psi}\hat{H}_{j}\hat{H}_{j^{\prime}}\ket{\psi}=1
for all
j
and
j^{\prime}
. Combining these observations, the quantum Fisher information of the state
\ket{\psi}
with respect to the Hamiltonian
\hat{H}=\sum_{j}\hat{H}_{j}
is given by

\mathcal{F}(\ket{\psi},\hat{H})=\sum_{j,j^{\prime}}\left(\bra{\psi}\hat{H}_{j}\hat{H}_{j^{\prime}}\ket{\psi}-\bra{\psi}\hat{H}_{j}\ket{\psi}\bra{\psi}\hat{H}_{j^{\prime}}\ket{\psi}\right)=(\Delta+1)^{2},

(48)

whose scaling is
\Theta(n^{2})
since
\Delta=\Theta(n)
in the former conditions.

Then, we prove that a graph with girth
g=\Theta(n)
and vertex degree at least
2
(and at most
w
) satisfies the former sufficient conditions.

Condition 1 is satisfied by definition. For condition 2, denote the distance between
v_{s}
and
v_{t}
after cutting the edge
(v_{s},v_{t})
as
\mathrm{dis}(v_{s},v_{t})
. One can find a path of length
\mathrm{dis}(v_{s},v_{t})
from
v_{s}
to
v_{t}
. Combining it with the edge
(v_{s},v_{t})
, we have a cycle of length
\mathrm{dis}(v_{s},v_{t})+1
in
G
. By definition, we have
\mathrm{dis}(v_{s},v_{t})+1\geq g=\Theta(n)
and
\mathrm{dis}(v_{s},v_{t})=\Theta(n)
therefore.

For condition 3, the depth requirement
d_{\mathcal{T}}=\Theta(n)
follows directly from the former condition
\mathrm{dis}(v_{s},v_{t})=\Theta(n)
. It therefore suffices to show that the tree width satisfies
W_{\mathcal{T}}=O(1)
. To this end, we consider the non-tree edges (“chords”) of
\mathcal{T}
, denoted by
e_{1}^{*},e_{2}^{*},\ldots,e_{k}^{*}
. The total number of such chords is
k=|E_{\mathcal{T}}|-|V_{\mathcal{T}}|=n-(n-k)=k
. Each chord
e^{*}=(v_{1}^{*},v_{2}^{*})
uniquely defines a cycle given by
e^{*}\cup\mathrm{Path}(v_{1}^{*},v_{2}^{*})
, where
\mathrm{Path}(v_{1}^{*},v_{2}^{*})
is the unique path between
v_{1}^{*}
and
v_{2}^{*}
in the tree. Assume, for the sake of contradiction, that
W_{\mathcal{T}}=\omega(1)
. Since every vertex has degree at least
2
, the number of chords must be at least the number of leaves in the tree part of
\mathcal{T}
, which is itself lower bounded by
W_{\mathcal{T}}
. Consequently, we must have
k=\omega(1)
. Moreover, each such cycle has length at least the girth
g=\Theta(n)
. Although these cycles may overlap, for any cycle whose overlap with others has length
o(n)
, its remaining unique part must have length
\Theta(n)
. On the other hand, since the total number of vertices is
\Theta(n)
, there can be at most
O(1)
cycles whose overlap with other cycles has length
\Theta(n)
while their unique parts have length
o(n)
. Otherwise, one could find two cycles whose overlap is extensive, and whose respective unique parts form a new cycle of length
o(n)
, contradicting the assumption that all cycles have length
\Theta(n)
. Therefore, at least
\omega(1)
cycles must have disjoint unique segments of length
\Theta(n)
. This implies that the total number of vertices would scale as
\omega(1)\cdot\Theta(n)=\omega(n)
, contradicting the fact that the graph contains only
n
vertices. This contradiction shows that the assumption
W_{\mathcal{T}}=\omega(1)
is false, and hence
W_{\mathcal{T}}=O(1)
.
