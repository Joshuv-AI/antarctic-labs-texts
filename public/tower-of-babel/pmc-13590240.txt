Definitive Histologic Diagnosis of Bone Marrow Carcinomatosis Followed by Dose‐Adjusted Chemotherapy in Metastatic Urothelial Carcinoma: A Case Report
Authors: Shohei Yamaki, Yuki Endo, Jun Akatsuka, Hikaru Mikami, Shunsuke Ikuma, Kotaro Obayashi, Masato Yanagi, Yuka Toyama, Go Kimura, Yukihiro Kondo
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTIntroductionBone marrow carcinomatosis (BMC) is a rare manifestation of metastatic urothelial carcinoma (UC) that may mimic hematologic disease and cause severe cytopenia, diagnostic difficulty, and rapid clinical deterioration.Case PresentationA 65‐year‐old man presented with back pain, thrombocytopenia, and leukoerythroblastosis. Bone marrow aspiration resulted in a dry tap, but biopsy revealed diffuse carcinoma‐cell infiltration consistent with UC, establishing BMC as the cause of marrow failure. Introduction

Bone marrow carcinomatosis (BMC), which overlaps with disseminated carcinomatosis of the bone marrow (DCBM), is characterized by diffuse marrow infiltration by carcinoma cells and hematologic abnormalities such as cytopenia and leukoerythroblastosis [1, 2]. In this report, we use BMC to describe biopsy‐proven diffuse marrow infiltration by urothelial carcinoma (UC) associated with severe cytopenia. Although BMC/DCBM from UC is rare, related cases have been reported as bone marrow metastasis, marrow infiltration, or myelophthisis [3, 4, 5, 6, 7]. Severe marrow failure may preclude systemic therapy, even when the underlying malignancy is potentially chemosensitive. We report a case of metastatic UC presenting with BMC in which histologic confirmation of UC‐related marrow failure supported carefully selected dose‐adjusted gemcitabine and cisplatin chemotherapy.

Case Presentation

A 65‐year‐old man presented with progressive lower back pain lasting several weeks. Laboratory examination revealed anemia (hemoglobin, 10.8 g/dL), severe thrombocytopenia (platelet count, 9000/μL), and elevated lactate dehydrogenase (836 U/L) and alkaline phosphatase (966 U/L) levels. A peripheral blood smear showed leukoerythroblastosis, raising suspicion for marrow involvement by malignancy.

Bone marrow aspiration was attempted but resulted in a dry tap. Subsequent bone marrow biopsy demonstrated diffuse infiltration of carcinoma cells positive for cytokeratin 7, cytokeratin 20, and GATA‐3, an immunophenotype supporting urothelial origin (Figure 1a–c). Urine cytology was classified as Class V, and cystoscopy revealed a broad‐based bladder tumor. Transurethral biopsy confirmed high‐grade papillary urothelial carcinoma (Figure 1d).

Positron emission tomography–computed tomography (PET/CT) revealed widespread bone metastases involving the spine and pelvis, with para‐aortic lymph node involvement (Figure 2a). Magnetic resonance imaging further demonstrated diffuse bone marrow involvement. Based on the pathological findings, imaging results, and clinical presentation, the patient was diagnosed with metastatic UC complicated by BMC, which was considered the main cause of the severe cytopenia.

(a) Positron emission tomography/computed tomography (PET/CT) at diagnosis showing widespread bone metastases involving the spine and pelvis, with para‐aortic lymph node involvement. (b) PET/CT after two cycles of reduced‐dose gemcitabine and cisplatin showing decreased fluorodeoxyglucose uptake in multiple bone lesions. (c) Spinal magnetic resonance imaging after seven cycles of avelumab showing progression of bone metastases. (d) Fluid‐attenuated inversion recovery magnetic resonance imaging showing high signal intensity in the pons and cerebellar vermis.

At chemotherapy initiation, the patient had an ECOG performance status of 1, no clinically significant active bleeding, and no DIC, with elevated rather than decreased fibrinogen. Because marrow failure was considered to be driven mainly by UC infiltration, the first two cycles of reduced‐dose gemcitabine at 840 mg/body (50%) and cisplatin at 30 mg/body (25%) were administered in the inpatient setting under close monitoring, without platelet or red blood cell transfusion. After two cycles, PET/CT showed stable disease with decreased uptake in multiple bone lesions (Figure 2b), and the platelet count increased from 9000/μL to 238 000/μL. Treatment was then escalated to standard‐dose gemcitabine and cisplatin from the third cycle onward in the outpatient setting.

Despite initial disease control, magnetic resonance imaging after seven cycles of avelumab showed progression of bone metastases (Figure 2c). Gemcitabine and cisplatin rechallenge was attempted but discontinued because of severe adverse effects, including anorexia, and pembrolizumab was initiated.

Approximately six weeks after pembrolizumab initiation, the patient developed headache, neck pain, and gait disturbance. Fluid‐attenuated inversion recovery magnetic resonance imaging showed high signal intensity in the pons and cerebellar vermis (Figure 2d). Cerebrospinal fluid cytology revealed malignant cells, leading to a diagnosis of carcinomatous meningitis. The patient died 12.4 months after diagnosis. Discussion

This case describes a patient with metastatic UC complicated by BMC who maintained access to systemic therapy despite severe bone marrow dysfunction. Although the initial presentation with thrombocytopenia and leukoerythroblastosis could mimic hematologic disease, bone marrow biopsy confirmed diffuse marrow infiltration by carcinoma cells consistent with UC. This definitive histologic diagnosis informed an individualized decision to initiate reduced‐dose gemcitabine and cisplatin, after which hematologic recovery, radiologic improvement, subsequent standard‐dose chemotherapy, avelumab maintenance therapy, and survival for 12.4 months after diagnosis were observed.

BMC from UC is extremely rare and appears to progress rapidly. However, related cases have been reported under broader terms, including DCBM, bone marrow metastasis, marrow infiltration, and myelophthisis [3, 4, 5, 6, 7]. The clinical characteristics and outcomes of these cases are summarized in Table 1. Therefore, the novelty of the present case does not lie in being the first report of marrow involvement by UC. Rather, its clinical significance lies in the histologic confirmation of UC‐related marrow failure and the subsequent carefully selected use of dose‐adjusted systemic chemotherapy. In the present case, thrombocytopenia, leukoerythroblastosis, elevated lactate dehydrogenase, and bone pain raised suspicion of marrow involvement [8]. However, these findings are not specific to BMC and may be misinterpreted as primary hematologic disease.

The second important point is the cautious use of dose‐adjusted chemotherapy. Severe thrombocytopenia and anemia usually discourage cytotoxic chemotherapy because of concerns regarding bleeding and further hematologic toxicity [2]. In the present case, however, treatment was considered because the patient had preserved performance status, no clinically significant active bleeding, no DIC with elevated rather than decreased fibrinogen, and biopsy‐proven UC‐related marrow failure. Reduced‐dose gemcitabine and cisplatin were initiated under close monitoring to achieve early disease control while minimizing hematologic risk. This approach should not be generalized, but it may be considered in highly selected patients as an individualized attempt to restore marrow function and preserve treatment opportunity.

This approach is clinically relevant in the current therapeutic era of metastatic UC. Avelumab maintenance therapy, enfortumab vedotin, and more recently, enfortumab vedotin plus pembrolizumab have expanded treatment opportunities, including in Japanese real‐world practice [9, 10, 11, 12, 13, 14, 15]. However, patients with extensive bone marrow involvement may lose access to these therapies if diagnosis or initial treatment is delayed until disseminated intravascular coagulation or irreversible marrow failure develops. In this context, the goal of initial dose‐adjusted chemotherapy is not only short‐term disease control but also preservation of eligibility for subsequent systemic therapy. Therefore, definitive histologic diagnosis and carefully dose‐adjusted initial chemotherapy may help preserve access to subsequent systemic treatment.

This case highlights the diagnostic importance of considering UC‐related BMC in patients with cytopenia, leukoerythroblastosis, elevated LDH, and bone pain. Histologic confirmation is essential to distinguish marrow failure caused by diffuse UC infiltration from primary hematologic disease or other causes of cytopenia. This single case does not establish dose‐adjusted gemcitabine and cisplatin as a general treatment strategy for BMC‐related marrow failure. However, in carefully selected patients without active bleeding or DIC, histologic confirmation of UC‐related marrow failure may support individualized systemic therapy. Timely diagnosis may prevent loss of opportunity while systemic treatment remains feasible.
