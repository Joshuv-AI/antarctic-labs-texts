Matrix metalloproteinase-guided targeting of vesicular stomatitis virus to tumors following systemic administration
Authors: Eileen Victoria Theune, William C. Theune, Elham Ahmadi, Conner C. Chiari, Neha Singh, Rory Geyer, Jason Tonne, Muriel Metko, Olamide T. Olaoba, Isabella China Wang, Dylan Dai, Jianbin Ruan, Robert Clark, Guangfu Li, Penghua Wang, Evan R. Jellison, Anthony T. Vella, Richard Vile, Kepeng Wang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
The lack of tumor-specific targeting by oncolytic viruses (OVs) restricts their therapeutic potential. Here, we report a “cap-linker” strategy to modify the vesicular stomatitis virus (VSV) glycoprotein (G protein, VSV-GIN). The modified VSV (Pro-VSVIN) loses its ability to infect cells until present in the tumor microenvironment, where the linker is cleaved by tumor-enriched matrix metalloproteinases (MMPs). When Pro-VSVIN is armed with a single-chain IL-12 (Pro-VSVIN-IL-12) and systemically injected into MC38 tumor-bearing mice, Pro-VSVIN-IL-12 eradicates established tumors while avoiding systemic toxicity, and elicits long-term anti-tumor immune memory. Systemic administration of Pro-VSVIN-IL-12 also markedly inhibits the growth of metastatic cancers in the lung. The efficacy of Pro-VSVIN-IL-12 was further improved when combined with PD-1 checkpoint blockade. Compared to WT VSVIN that carries IL-12, intravenously injected Pro-VSVIN-IL-12 leads to strong Th1 immunity, near abolishment of regulatory T cells, and potent CD8+ T cell activation and clonal expansion in tumors. This preclinical work demonstrates a new tumor-specific targeting strategy of VSV for enhanced immunotherapy safety with superior efficacy.

Introduction

Oncolytic viruses (OVs) are emerging immunotherapies under active clinical investigation.1 These agents, which can be used in their wild type or engineered forms, preferentially infect, replicate in, and lyse cancer cells, exposing tumor antigens to the host immune system to help facilitate an anti-cancer immune response. Currently, four OVs, and one non-replicating OV, are approved for use clinically, each of which require intratumoral administration.2–6 Additional agents under preclinical investigation are engineered to harbor immune-enhancing and costimulatory payloads, or even suicide cassettes to bolster anti-tumor immunity.7–9 In many cases, clinical applications of these agents are limited by their current requirement for local injection. This precludes tumors that are not surgically accessible, or are metastatic. For OVs that are injected systemically, the lack of a “tumor-specific” viral delivery platform limits the choice of immune activating payloads to avoid severe toxicities.8–10 Further clinical development of novel viral vectors that have refined specificity for targeting tumors is needed.

Vesicular stomatitis virus (VSV) belongs to the Rhabdoviridae family of viruses, having a single-stranded, negative-sense RNA genome of around 11 kb.11,12 VSV mainly infects livestock and causes blister-like lesions in these animals.11,12 While VSV infection in humans is rare, it can cause flu-like symptoms.11 VSV replicates strictly in the host cell’s cytoplasm, does not enter the nucleus, and does not interact with host DNA. This feature, combined with a lack of a DNA phase for VSV, makes it safer compared to DNA viruses (such as Adenovirus) due to the absence of potential insertional mutagenesis to the host.11,12 Owing to its low prevalence (and thus less prior existing immunity against the vector) and mild symptoms in humans, VSV has been studied as a potential vector for cancer immunotherapy.11,13 For instance, the human IFN-β transgene has been cloned into the VSV backbone to further enhance its viral safety profile.14–16 Others incorporated interleukin-12 (IL-12) into VSV, and showed that intratumoral injection of these viruses control tumor burden.17,18 VSV harbors its G protein (VSV-G) on the surface of the membrane, which binds to the low-density lipoprotein receptor (LDL-R) on host cells to begin the cell entry process. A recent publication further narrows down the interaction between VSV-G to the CR-2 and CR-3 domains of the LDL-R.19 Due to the wide expression pattern of the LDL-R across different tissues and cell types, the VSV-G protein was used to replace the spike protein of HIV to generate lentivirus that is now commonly used in laboratories.20 However, the broad-tropism of the VSV-G protein also determines the lack of tumor specificity of VSV, limiting its route of administration to intratumoral injection when potent payloads, such as IL-12, are incorporated.

Although current immunotherapies have demonstrated clinical success, response rates to these therapies vary dramatically between cancer type, and person.21–24 For instance, colorectal cancer (CRC) is the third leading cause of cancer-related deaths in the United States,25 and only a small fraction (15%) of CRC that harbor microsatellite instability (MSI) mutations are responsive to immune checkpoint blockade (ICB) therapies such as anti-PD-1.26 Additionally, for advanced CRCs that are microsatellite stable (MSS), immunotherapeutic options are limited and there is a critical need for better treatments. The success of ICB is limited by factors including tumor immunoediting, mutational burden, and the presence and activation state of infiltrating lymphocytes.27 To overcome therapeutic limitations observed with ICB, OVs are used to inflame the tumor microenvironment (TME) and promote T cell responses directed against tumor antigens, as well as increase the expression of checkpoint molecules on immune and tumor cells.28–34 Currently, various clinical trials (NCT03017820, NCT03647163, NCT03120624, NCT06581406, NCT04735978, NCT03767348, NCT04725331) are actively investigating the efficacy of these therapeutic combinations, as preclinical trials have pointed towards a synergy from dual treatments.29–32 However, the efficacy of the combination therapy is heavily dependent on several factors not limited to the type of tumor, strain of OV, transgene payload, as well as the dosing scheme, underscoring the importance of understanding how each unique OV construct impacts the efficacy of the combination therapy.

Here, we demonstrate the development of a cancer-specific oncolytic VSV by engineering the endogenous G protein with a “cap-linker” strategy. This modification allows for the safe systemic delivery of potent, immune-stimulating IL-12 directly to the site of the tumor resulting in tumor eradication. We also interrogated the efficacy of the engineered virus in treating metastatic cancers and its ability to mount long-term immune memory. Importantly, we combined tumor-targeted delivery of IL-12 with ICB for enhanced efficacy against established tumors. These efforts pave a new way of applying our novel Pro-VSV technology to safely deliver potent immune activating payloads to human cancers that are not surgically reachable. Further, we generalized our study to include G proteins from two serotypes of VSV and the Maraba virus, to show that the “cap-linker” strategy may be generally applicable for targeted tumor delivery of oncolytic viruses.

Results

Rational design of a cancer-specific viral platform using lentivirus

VSV-G binds to LDL-R on target cells, resulting in broad tropism in humans and experimental animals.19,35 Specifically, VSV’s clinical use is limited by its potential for neurotoxicity and infectivity of the heart and kidney, which is observed in mice and non-human primates, as well as hepatotoxicity.36–38 Lack of tumor specificity by VSV thus limits the choice of payload that the virus may carry, with immune-related adverse events as a concern if potent immune activating payloads (such as IL-12) are used.

To improve the cancer-specific targeting of systemically injected VSV, we engineered VSV-GIN (Indiana serotype) to limit its ability to bind its cognate receptor unless present in the tumor microenvironment (TME). We developed a ‘cap-linker’ cancer-specific targeting system where the ‘cap’ acts as an inhibitory domain to VSV-GIN, but is released when the linker connecting the cap to VSV-GIN is cleaved by proteases in the TME. We first utilized the lentiviral platform to determine the optimal cap and linker length to mediate viral inhibition. We used WT VSV-GIN (Fig. 1b) and Pro-VSV-G,IN which contains the cap-linker modification (Fig. 1c). For the Pro-VSV-GIN constructs, we selected the cysteine-rich 2 (CR-2) domain (~ 4.5 kDa) from the LDL-R as an inhibitory domain (cap), and cloned it to the N’-terminus of the mature VSV-GIN after the endogenous signal peptide and connected it to VSV-GIN by a 12- or 20-amino acid (aa) protease cleavable linker (Fig. 1c).19 We also generated a CR-2 cap which harbors four different point mutations at the amino acids that have been shown to be critical for mediating VSV-G’s binding to the CR-2 domain (Fig. 1c).19 Additionally, we investigated the use of a mannose binding protein (MBP) (~43 kDa) cap, in place of the CR-2 domain, and connected it to VSV-GIN with a 20 aa linker (Fig. 1c). Finally, we used a 3C protease cleavable linker to avoid inadvertent activation of Pro-VSV-GIN pseudotyped viruses by proteases endogenous to the HEK-293T cells used for viral production and screening.39

To probe the infectious activity of Pro-VSV-GIN pseudotyped viruses, we transduced HEK-293T cells with WT VSV-GIN or Pro-VSV-GIN pseudotyped, GFP-encoding lentiviruses prior to or directly following 3C protease digest and analyzed the number of infected cells by GFP signal (Fig. 1d). Compared to lentivirus harboring WT VSV-GIN, the infectious activity of Pro-VSV-GIN pseudotyped viruses, prior to 3C protease digest, was completely abolished with all constructs, except Pro-VSV-GIN harboring the mutated CR-2 cap (Fig. 1e, upper panels). We next treated viruses with 3C protease and examined the degree of restoration of lentivirus infectivity (Fig. 1e, lower panels). While only a marginal rescue of the viral titer was observed with the removal of the MBP or mutant CR-2 caps, the removal of the CR-2 cap resulted in complete restoration of lentivirus infection, with the 20 aa linker length proving to be robust beyond that of the WT VSV-GIN control (Fig. 1e–g). The Pro-VSV-GIN pseudotyped lentivirus with a CR-2 cap and 20 aa linker showed a near 700-fold activation compared to its undigested counterpart, demonstrating the versatility of the cap-linker strategy in VSV-G inhibition and protease-dependent activation. The higher signal of the Pro-VSV-GIN pseudotyped lentivirus (CR-2 cap, 20 aa linker) may be attributed to the inability of the virus to infect host cells during viral production, resulting in enhanced viral accumulation in the supernatant.

Optimization of a cancer-specific virus using viral spike protein cognate binding domains

With the success of the CR-2 domain as an effective cap in our engineered Pro-VSV-GIN (Fig. 1), we next compared the efficacy of the additional six CR domains of the LDL-R for their ability to inhibit VSV-GIN function. We included the New Jersey (NJ) serotype of VSV-G (VSV-GNJ), which is more pathogenic than the Indiana serotype,40,41 and investigated the Maraba virus glycoprotein which uses the LDL-R for cell entry and is more resistant to serum neutralization.42

We covalently linked the CR-1 through CR-7 domains of the LDL-R to the N’-terminus of VSV-GIN, VSV-GNJ, or Maraba-G, using the 20 aa 3 C protease cleavable linker (Fig. 2a). We transduced HEK-293T cells and quantified viral titer by assessing GFP+ cells 3 days post infection. The CR-2 domain provided the greatest fold of activation for Pro-VSV-GIN and Pro-VSV-GNJ (Fig. 2b–e; supplementary Figs. 1 and 2); however, the higher basal level of activity of Pro-VSV-GNJ in its native form reduced the extent of activation of the virus compared to the Indiana serotype (Fig. 2c, e). Engineering of Pro-VSV-GIN pseudotyped lentivirus for tumor-specific protease activation

Among different proteases, the matrix metalloproteinase (MMP) family has been shown to be upregulated across different human cancers.39,43–47 Additionally, MMP activity is known to promote tumor development by aiding angiogenesis and metastasis.48 The abundance of MMPs across multiple tumor types makes them ideal for use as an activating signal for the liberation of Pro-VSV-GIN.39,43–47 Therefore, we engineered our 20-aa linker to contain a universal MMP cleavage site (Pro-VSV-GIN-MMP, Fig. 3a).Fig. 3MMP-dependent activation of Pro-VSV-GIN-MMP. a Illustration of the design for Pro-VSV-GIN-MMP which harbors a CR-2 inhibitory domain and a 20 aa flexible, pan-MMP cleavable linker fused to the N’-terminus of VSV-GIN. b–e WT VSV-GIN and Pro-VSV-GIN-MMP containing plasmids were co-transfected with lentiviral helper plasmids to HEK-293T cells to generate lentiviruses coated with WT or Pro-VSV-GIN-MMP spike proteins, respectively. The incorporation of an MMP-cleavable linker resulted in a viral activity that was only marginally lower than the WT virus (Fig. 3b–e), which was due to HEK-293T cells producing MMPs. Addition of marimastat, a broad-spectrum MMP inhibitor that blocks MMPs 1, 2, 3, 7, 8, 9, and 14, resulted in a dose-dependent decrease in the viral titer of Pro-VSV-GIN-MMP lentivirus (Fig. 3b–e).49 Pro-VSV-GIN-MMP lentivirus pre-digested with recombinant MMP-7 prior to infection, resulted in a significant increase in the percent of virally infected cells compared to the marimastat-treated counterparts (Fig. 3b–e). Taken together, these data demonstrate the MMP-dependent activity of Pro-VSV-GIN-MMP in guiding viral entry into the cell.

CRISPR knockout of MMPs in HEK-293T cells allows for efficient production of Pro-VSV-GIN pseudotyped lentivirus in its native form

We suspected that the remnant activity of Pro-VSV-GIN-MMP pseudotyped lentivirus was due to the expression of MMPs in HEK-293T cells not targeted by marimastat. RT-qPCR analysis of all MMP mRNAs show that MMPs 2, 11, 15, 16, and 17 are expressed at relatively high levels in HEK-293T cells (supplementary Fig. 4a), which prompted us to engineer HEK-293T cells using CRISPR/Cas9 technology to knockout all five MMPs (supplementary Fig. 4b, MMP-KO HEK-293T cells). Successful knockout of these genes was confirmed by Sanger sequencing of genomic DNA from single MMP-KO HEK-293T cell clones, and we selected the best clone C2-11 for subsequent study (supplementary Fig. 4b, c). We next performed a functional test to investigate the ability of our MMP-KO HEK-293 T (clone C2-11) to produce lentivirus (supplementary Fig. 4d) and showed that the MMP-KO HEK-293T cells were as efficient as WT HEK-293T cells in producing lentiviruses (supplementary Fig. 4e). The activity of Pro-VSV-GIN-MMP lentivirus produced in MMP-KO HEK-293T cells is abolished when used to infect MMP-KO HEK-293T cells and is completely rescued when recombinant MMP-7 is used to pre-digest the virus prior to infection of these cells (supplementary Fig. 4f, g). Thus, the MMP-KO HEK-293T cell line serves as an ideal platform to produce MMP-dependent viruses by preventing premature MMP cleavage on the viral particles.

Systemic injection of Pro-VSV-GIN-MMP pseudotyped vesicular stomatitis virus exhibits a tumor-restricted tropism

We examined the safety profile of the Pro-VSVIN-GFP compared to WT VSVIN-GFP. Using non-tumor bearing, immune competent C57BL/6J mice, we probed the safety profile by dose escalation to determine the maximum tolerated dose (MTD) of each virus. Pro-VSVIN-GFP proved to have a nearly 4-times higher MTD than WT VSVIN-GFP (Fig. 4h).

We then performed a biodistribution analysis over eight days using CACO2 human colon tumor bearing NSG-c-KIT mice intravenously (i.v.) injected with 1 × 107 pfu of Pro-VSVIN-GFP. Pro-VSVIN-GFP preferentially expanded in the tumor environment (Fig. 4i–k, supplementary Fig. 5a). Compared to all normal tissues examined, 500 times the amount of GFP RNA was detected in the tumor of mice treated with Pro-VSVIN-GFP (Fig. 4i–k). The level of Pro-VSVIN-GFP quickly elevated in the tumor through the 8-day period, suggesting robust replication of the virus in the tumor environment (supplementary Fig. 5a). Additionally, when compared to systemic injection of WT VSVIN-GFP into CACO2 bearing NSG-c-Kit mice, Pro-VSVIN-GFP showed much greater accumulation in the tumor (Fig. 4j, k). VSV infects liver cells,50 and H/E staining of liver showed that WT VSVIN-GFP, but not Pro-VSVIN-GFP, caused marked damage (supplementary Fig. 5b). Pro-VSVIN-GFP readily infects HEK-293T cells, but not primary human brain microvascular endothelial cells (pBEC) (Fig. 4l). Pre-digestion of Pro-VSVIN-GFP with MMP rescued its defective infection in the non-cancerous endothelial cells (Fig. 4l). In addition, Pro-VSVIN-GFP also infects two mouse cancer cell lines, MC38 and 4T1, and its viral activity is further enhanced when the virus is pre-digested with MMP (Fig. 4m, n). Collectively, our data demonstrate that Pro-VSVIN-GFP is tumor-specific and possess an excellent safety profile.

To interrogate the safety profile of the IL-12 containing viruses, a dose escalation study in C57BL/6J hosts was performed. Pro-VSVIN-IL-12 is safe at a dose 10-fold higher than WT VSVIN-IL-12, since no treatment related paralysis, or death was observed following systemic injection of Pro-VSVIN-IL-12 at titers up to 2 × 108 pfu per injection, compared to 2 × 107 pfu for WT VSVIN-IL-12 (Fig. 5f). A biodistribution study over eight days using CACO2 tumor bearing NSG-c-KIT mice showed the preference of Pro-VSVIN-IL-12 to accumulate in tumor, compared to that of WT VSVIN-IL-12, which also accumulated in liver (Fig. 5g, h). When injected i.v. to immune competent C57BL/6J mice, WT VSVIN-IL-12 led to significant elevation in IL-12 and IFN-γ protein levels in the serum (Fig. 5i, j). This effect was largely abolished in the case of Pro-VSVIN-IL-12 (Fig. 5i, j). It is known that IL-12 is toxic when systemically exposed, and the reduction in IL-12 and IFN-γ exposure in the serum by Pro-VSVIN-IL-12 agrees with its enhanced safety profile.

Next, to explore the potential therapeutic benefit of Pro-VSVIN-IL-12 as a cancer treatment, we established a syngeneic, ectopic model of colon adenocarcinoma by grafting MC38 cells subcutaneously to the flanks of C57BL/6J mice (Fig. 5k–r). The tumors were palpable 7 days following the tumor graft, at which time the mice were treated with four doses every three days by i.v. injection of PBS or one-quarter of the determined MTD of the transgenic viruses. While little or no tumor control was observed for mice treated with PBS (Fig. 5l, supplementary Fig. 6e), WT VSVIN-GFP (Fig. 5m, supplementary Fig. 6f), as well as Pro-VSVIN-GFP (Fig. 5n, supplementary Fig. 6g), the addition of the potent immune-stimulating scIL-12 in both WT VSVIN-IL-12 (Fig. 5o, supplementary Fig. 6h) and Pro-VSVIN-IL-12 (Fig. 5p, supplementary Fig. 6i) resulted in delayed tumor growth. However, unlike WT VSVIN-IL-12, Pro-VSVIN-IL-12 proved to be the superior treatment, eradicating tumors in nine of ten mice, and significantly increasing the survival of mice compared to all other treatment groups (Fig. 5p–r). Importantly, no treatment-related deaths or signs of toxicity were observed in the Pro-VSVIN-IL-12 treated cohort. Additionally, to test if treatment with Pro-VSVIN-IL-12 conferred long-term immune memory, Pro-VSVIN-IL-12 treated mice were rechallenged with MC38 tumor cells 40–60 days after the initial tumor graft. None of the 9 mice grew tumors following MC38 subcutaneous rechallenge (Fig. 5s). Immune memory was further confirmed by isolating splenocytes from mice with tumors eradicated following treatment with Pro-VSVIN-IL-12, co-culturing with MC38 tumor cells, and analyzing for the expression of IFN-γ (Fig. 5t).

To explore the scope of anti-tumor efficacy of Pro-VSVIN-IL-12, we treated tumor-bearing mice with four doses of PBS or Pro-VSVIN-IL-12 by intraperitoneal (i.p.), instead of i.v., injection every three days (supplementary Fig. 6j–o). Treatment with Pro-VSVIN-IL-12 resulted in a significant decrease in the growth rate of tumors compared to PBS treated controls, and eradicated tumors in seven of ten mice (supplementary Fig. 6j–l). Likewise, Pro-VSVIN-IL-12 significantly extended the lives of mice compared to PBS treated controls (supplementary Fig. 6m). As observed with i.v. administration of Pro-VSVIN-IL-12, i.p. administration likewise conferred immune memory (supplementary Fig. 6p).

One day after the last injection, TILs were isolated by Ficoll-Paque density centrifugation and analyzed by single-cell RNA and TCR sequencing, as well as flow cytometry (Fig. 7b–j, supplementary Fig. 7). To find the cell population that mediates Pro-VSVIN-IL-12 efficacy, CD4, CD8, and NK cells were depleted with antibodies in MC38 tumor-bearing mice treated with Pro-VSVIN-IL-12. This may be due to the depletion of both CD4+ Th1 cells and Treg cells by the anti-CD4 antibody. It is known that these two T cell populations play contradicting roles in cancer immunotherapy. Taken together, we show that systemic injection of Pro-VSVIN-IL-12 activates Th1 and CD8+ T cells, and reduces Treg cells in the tumor environment.

Combination therapy of single dose Pro-VSVIN-IL-12 with checkpoint blockade improves anti-tumor potency

Immune checkpoint blockade has become a standard of care for several cancer types. To test if treatment with the combination of Pro-VSVIN-IL-12 and αPD-1 would further the therapeutic benefit of checkpoint blockade, we grafted MC38 cells to the flanks of mice, and when tumors reached an average volume of 40–50 mm3, treated mice with a single dose of Pro-VSVIN-IL-12 with αPD-1 (Fig. 8a–g, supplementary Fig. 11). The combination of Pro-VSVIN-IL-12 with checkpoint blockade outperformed isotype treated controls (Fig. 8b), a single dose of Pro-VSVIN-IL-12 with isotype control treated mice (Fig. 8c), as well as αPD-1 as a monotherapy (Fig. 8d), eradicating tumors in all but one treated mouse (Fig. 8e). Additionally, combination of Pro-VSVIN-IL-12 and αPD-1 led to significantly improved long-term survival of treated mice, with no tumor regrowth following the cure of these established colon tumors (Fig. 8f, g). Overall, our data suggest that the cap-linker strategy specifically targets Pro-VSVIN to the tumor environment (Fig. 8h). The tumor-restricted delivery of therapeutic payloads, such as IL-12, contributes to the greatly enhanced safety and efficacy for the Pro-VSVIN platform. Discussion

To date, two virotherapies have been FDA approved in the United States. One is T-Vec, an oncolytic virus which was approved in 2015 for unresectable melanoma and the second is Adstiladrin, a non-oncolytic virus, approved in 2022 for non-muscle invasive bladder cancer. Currently, no oncolytic viruses have been approved for systemic injection, but multiple therapies are under clinical evaluation including a modified VSV that carries IFN-β.75 Development of systemically injected oncolytic viral agents requires careful evaluation of their payloads to avoid intolerable immune-related adverse events. For instance, IL-12 was incorporated into several viruses including HSV,76 adenovirus,77 and vaccinia virus (clinical trial NCT04301011). Clinical trials with these viruses used intratumoral injection as a delivery method. In our study, we show that the cap-linker modification on the VSV-GIN protein significantly enhanced the safety margin of IL-12 armed VSV. Compared to WT VSVIN that carries IL-12, Pro-VSVIN-IL-12 has a 10-times higher MTD (Fig. 5f). Further, when injected at the same dose, Pro-VSVIN showed undetectable distribution to normal organs such as the liver and the brain, and enhanced replication in grafted tumors in mice (Figs. 4k, 5h). These promising results demonstrate the enhanced tumor-specificity of the Pro-VSV platform, allowing i.v. administration for the treatment of surgically unreachable tumors.

We systematically tested the feasibility of different CR domains from human LDL-R as the inhibitory domain (the cap), which allowed for the robust rescue of viral activity following cleavage of the linker to VSV-GIN (Fig. 2). Both CR-2 and CR-3 domains physically interact with VSV-GIN,19 and indeed, when the CR-2 domain was mutated on four amino acids that were reported to mediate its binding to VSV-G,IN the signal-to-noise ratio of the mutant cap-VSV-GIN protein in guiding viral infection was diminished (Fig. 1e, f). To maximize these findings, we propose that our cap-linker strategy may be applied to other viruses and their cognate receptor domains, whereby the binding activity between the cap and viral spike protein serves as a key for effective inhibition of viral entry until the linker between them is cleaved.

We chose IL-12 as a payload to be tested in this study due to its potency in inducing anti-tumor immunity. Recombinant IL-12 has been tested in human cancers, but failed a phase II clinical trial due to the loss of two patients,55,78 indicating the need for more targeted delivery methods of IL-12. Pro-VSVIN-IL-12 addresses this problem due to its enhanced tumor-specificity compared to WT VSVIN. Selective replication of Pro-VSVIN-IL-12 provides sustained local secretion of the potent Th1 inducing cytokine. In addition to IL-12, Pro-VSV may encode other cytokines, chemokines, antibodies, and tumoricidal proteins. For instance, bispecific antibodies against tumor-associated antigens and CD3 serve as T cell engagers (TCEs) to “pull” human T cells into contact with tumor cells, resulting in potent tumor killing. One major issue with systemic delivery of recombinant TCEs is the expression of the selected tumor antigens in normal organs, leading to “on target, off tumor” activation of T cells and severe toxicity in some cases.79 Specific delivery of T cell engagers by Pro-VSV may allow enhanced therapeutic tolerance and efficacy for human cancers, which remains to be interrogated.

Our Pro-VSV design relies on the enzymatic activity of MMPs in the tumor environment to cleave the linker and release the inhibition to VSV-GIN. The MMP family contains more than 20 members and are widely upregulated and overexpressed in human cancers.47,80 Therefore, the potential application of the Pro-VSV platform, and Pro-VSVIN-IL-12 in particular, should not be limited to colorectal and breast cancers as tested in this study, but to all solid tumors with elevated MMP activity. On the other hand, it is possible that heterogeneity in MMP expression level/activation exists among cancer patients. Therefore, it may be feasible to start future clinical evaluation of Pro-VSVIN-IL-12 in patients whose tumor biopsies show upregulated MMP expression. In addition to MMPs, human tumors also overexpress other proteases, and often exhibit reduced pH levels compared to normal tissues.81,82 Exploiting these characteristics of human tumors may aid further modification of the Pro-VSV by varying the linker region of the Pro-VSVIN-G protein to respond to acidic environments or other proteases such as matriptase and hepsin.83

In addition to limited tumor specificity, systemic injection of oncolytic viral therapies also faces induction of host immunity against viral proteins, and varying efficiency of viruses reaching the tumor site. It is unclear if the masking of VSV-GIN by the human LDL-R CR-2 domain alleviates immunogenicity of Pro-VSV compared to its WT counterpart. In mice, repeated Pro-VSVIN-IL-12 was done in multiple cancer models, and was effective in controlling tumor growth (Figs. 5 and 6). The treatment scheme spans around two weeks of time, and within this period we did not observe obvious loss of therapeutic activity against tumors. The impact of host immunity, such as antibodies against VSV-GIN protein, remains to be tested in a human clinical setting. Another approach to enhance viral entry into the tumor site is the delivery of viruses along other vehicles, such as T cells.84 Previously, we tested the co-delivery of VSV-IFN-β with CAR-T cells and showed marked increase in tumor viral titer compared to i.v. injected VSV alone.84 A similar approach may be employed to further enhance the tumor accumulation of Pro-VSV to the tumor sites.

In this study, we show that Pro-VSVIN-IL-12 has much higher MTD compared to WT VSVIN-IL-12. Thanks to this enhanced safety profile, we were able to treat mice with a much higher dose of Pro-VSVIN-IL-12 in our therapeutic efficacy study, which resulted in almost complete eradication of established tumors. However, this treatment scheme makes it difficult to distinguish improved intrinsic anti-tumor potency from dose-dependent effects of Pro-VSVIN-IL-12. A future study comparing equal dose of Pro-VSVIN-IL-12 with WT VSVIN-IL-12 will help clarify this point.

To best imitate a clinical setting, we combined Pro-VSVIN-IL-12 with immune checkpoint inhibitors to test for therapeutic efficacy. Combination therapy eradicated the majority of grafted MC38 tumors, surpassing both monotherapies using αPD-1 or Pro-VSVIN-IL-12 (Fig. 8). The single injection of Pro-VSVIN-IL-12 in this treatment scheme, when translated to human clinical practice, would also circumvent the potential caveat of virus-induced immunity. Mechanistically, we show that Pro-VSVIN-IL-12 potently activates Th1 cells and CD8+ T cells in the tumor environment. Several T cell activation/exhaustion markers were upregulated in T cells following Pro-VSVIN-IL-12 i.v. treatment, including PD-1. Significantly increased proportion of Th1 cells in the tumor also contributes to the secretion of IFN-γ, which is known to induce PD-L1 expression in tumor cells.85 These data support our observed synergy between Pro-VSVIN-IL-12 and αPD-1. Other combination schemes are also under investigation, and our understanding on how Pro-VSVIN-IL-12 remodels tumor immunity may inform their chance of success. For example, we also observed that Pro-VSVIN-IL-12 potently reduced the population of Tregs in the tumor. Inhibition and depletion of Tregs was thought to be one of the major mechanisms of action by anti-CTLA4 blocking antibody. On the other hand, since Pro-VSVIN-IL-12 fosters an immunologically “hot” environment in the tumor, combining this i.v. oncolytic viral therapy with other immune checkpoints, T cell co-stimulation agonists, and other immunotherapeutic regimens that require T cell infiltration as a prerequisite, may further enhance the potential of developing effective therapies against advanced human cancers that fail checkpoint blockade or chemotherapy.

Virus construction and production

The second-generation lentiviral system was utilized to investigate VSV-GIN, VSV-GNJ and Maraba-G spike proteins and modifications made to the spike proteins.86 VSV-GIN (Addgene plasmid #138479) was cloned into the pCDNA3-TOPO vector (a gift from Dr. Jianbin Ruan, Department of Immunology, UConn Health) for purposes of lentiviral production, in conjunction with psPAX2 (Addgene #12260), and pLenti-eGFP (Addgene plasmid #141395). The pcDNA3-TOPO vector was used as the parental vector for cloning.

For the generation of lentivirus, adherent HEK-293T or C2-11 MMP-KO HEK-293T cells were used and seeded to be ~70% confluent at the time of transfection, typically 3 × 105 cells per well of a 6-well plate, on plates coated with Poly-D-Lysine (Gibco, Cat # A3890401). Manufacturer’s protocol was used for transfection. Briefly, at the time of transfection, LipofectamineTM 3000 (Invitrogen, Cat # L30000015) was diluted in 125 μl of Opti-MEMTM I Reduced Serum Medium (Gibco, Cat # 31985070), and combined with 2.5 μg of total DNA of psPAX2, pLenti-GFP and spike protein containing pCDNA3-TOPO plasmids, diluted in 125 μl Opti-MEMTM, and combined with P3000 reagent, then mixed and incubated at room temperature for 15 min before being added to the cells in a drop-wise manner. Cells containing the transfection complex were incubated for 24 h at 37 °C in 5% CO2 prior to medium change to Opti-MEMTM. Viral supernatant was harvested 48 h post transfection and clarified and filtered prior to use.

WT HEK-293T cells or C2-11 MMP-KO HEK-293T cells were transduced with GFP-expressing lentivirus. Cells were harvested 72 h post transduction and stained for viability using Live/Dead fixable exclusion dye and then analyzed for the presence of GFP. Flow cytometry analyses were performed on BD FACSymphony A5 SE flow cytometer at the UConn Health Flow Cytometry Core Facility. Data were analyzed with FlowJo software (FlowJo, LLC).

All VSV constructs were generated from the recombinant pVSV eGFP ΔG plasmid (Addgene plasmid # 31842).87 VSV-GIN or Pro-VSV-GIN were cloned into the fifth (ΔG) position using MluI and NotI restriction enzyme cut sites. A single-chain (sc) Interleukin (IL)-12 (scIL-12),88 harboring mutated MscI restriction enzyme cut sites (Twist Biosciences) was cloned into the first (GFP) position using XhoI and MscI restriction enzyme cut sites. WT or Pro-VSVIN expressing GFP or scIL-12 were rescued from the VSV cDNA plasmid using preestablished reverse genetics systems in BHK-21 cells, as previously described.89–92 In brief, BHK-21 cells were infected with MVA-T7 (a gift from Dr. Richard Vile) at an MOI of 1, incubated at 37 °C and 5% CO2 for 1 h, then transfected with pVSV genomic plasmid (10 μg), pBluescript (pBS)-encoding VSV-N (3 μg), pBS-encoding VSV-P (5 μg), and pBS-encoding VSV-L (1 μg) proteins (pBS plasmids were a gift from Dr. Richard Vile) using Fugene6 (Promega, Cat # E2691). After 48–72 h, supernatant was collected then clarified through a 0.2 μm filter. At least two rounds of plaque purification were performed.

Amplification of rescued VSV was achieved by a single round of infection of BHK-21 cells. Viral supernatant was collected after 24–96 h, clarified using a 0.2 μm filter and centrifuged at 20,000 × g for 3.5 h. The pellet was resuspended in an appropriate volume of phosphate-buffered saline (PBS) (Corning, Cat # 21-040-CM). Viral titers were determined by standard plaque assay of serially diluted samples using BHK-21 cells. For titering of Pro-VSVIN viruses, an additional step of digesting Pro-VSVIN with active MMP-7 protease (MilliporeSigma, Cat # 444270) was performed for 1 h at 4 °C followed by the standard plaque assay procedure.

Animal models

C57BL/6J, NOD.Cg-KitW-41J Tyr+ Prkdcscid Il2rgtm1Wjl/ThomJ (JAX stock # 026622),93–95 and BALB/c mice were obtained from the Jackson Laboratory. All mice were maintained in specific-pathogen-free conditions with filter-topped cages on autoclaved food and water at UConn Health, and all experiments were approved by the IACUC of UConn Health and performed in accordance with the university and NIH guidelines and regulations. All experiments used co-housed, gender-matched littermates.

Determination of maximum tolerated dose

The MTD for this study is defined as the highest dose of virus administered not resulting in paralysis or death. Male and female C57BL/6J mice were given a single intravenous (retro-orbital) dose of WT VSVIN-GFP, Pro-VSVIN-GFP, WT VSVIN-IL-12, or Pro-VSVIN-IL-12 ranging from 2 × 106 pfu to 3 × 108 pfu, and monitored up to two months post injection for any clinical signs. Dose levels of each virus were increased incrementally until any clinical signs were observed in injected mice, at which point dose escalation was ceased and MTD set.

Biodistribution studies

NSG-c-Kit mice were grafted with 5 × 106 CACO2 human colon cancer cells subcutaneously on one flank. When tumors reached a volume of 200 to 300 mm3, mice were treated with a single i.v. (retro-orbital) dose of 1 × 107 plaque forming units (pfu) of each transgenic virus suspended in 200 μl of PBS. Mice were sacrificed at indicated timepoints of 0, 1, 2, 4, and 8 days post injection. Major organs, tissues, and tumors were harvested, weighed, and analyzed for the presence of viral RNA by RT-qPCR.

Tumor immunotherapy models

MC38, or 4T1 cells were cultured in DMEM with 5 or 10% FBS, respectively, penicillin, and streptomycin. Cultured MC38 cells were digested with trypsin, washed, and resuspended at a concentration of 5 × 105 cells per 50 μl in 1x PBS. To establish subcutaneous tumors, cells were injected into the right flanks of 2-to-3-month-old C57BL/6J (for MC38) mice. For metastatic models, 4T1 at a concentration of 3 × 105 cells per 200 μl in PBS were grafted by tail vein injection to the lungs of BALB/c mice. Equal numbers of male and female mice were used for the tests, and mice were assigned randomly into different treatment groups. Viral injections (5 × 106–5 × 107 pfu suspended in 200 μl of PBS) were performed intravenously (by retro-orbital injection) or intraperitoneally at indicated timepoints after tumor grafting. Tumor length and width were measured every three days with a caliper. Tumor volume was calculated as V = (Length × Width2)/2. Mice were euthanized when tumors reached a volume of 2 × 103 mm3.

For checkpoint blockade experiments, mice were given 100 μg αPD-1 antibody (Bio X Cell, Cat # BP0146) or isotype control (Bio X Cell, Cat # BP0089) which were injected intraperitoneally at indicated timepoints.

For in vivo immune memory experiments, mice treated with Pro-VSVIN-IL-12 whose tumors were eradicated, were rechallenged by a subcutaneous graft of 5 × 105 MC38 cells into their right flank. In some cases, a second round rechallenge was performed by injecting the same number of MC38 cells to the opposite flank of mice 3 months after the first rechallenge. Naïve C57BL/6J mice were used as controls.

For in vitro immune memory experiments, splenocytes were isolated from mice treated with Pro-VSVIN-IL-12 whose tumors were eradicated, or from naïve mice, and resuspended at a concentration of 1 × 106 cells/mL in DMEM containing 5% FBS and 1% penicillin-streptomycin. These splenocytes were co-cultured with 5 × 104 MC38 tumor cells for 48 h, and supernatants were analyzed for IFN-γ by an enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay (ELISA) per manufacturer’s instructions (Mabtech, Cat # 3321-1H-6 and # 3652-F10).

Antibody depletion

Depleting antibodies against CD4 (Bio X Cell, Cat # BE0003), CD8α (Bio X Cell, Cat # ΒΕ0061), and NK1.1 (Bio X Cell # BE0036) were injected intraperitoneally at 200 μg at indicated timepoints. The control antibodies (Bio X Cell # BE0083 and BP0089) were administered to the other groups. Flow analysis of splenocytes was used to confirm subset-specific depletions.

Tumor infiltrating immune cell isolation and flow cytometry analysis

Tumor infiltrating immune cells were isolated as previously described.96,97 In brief, tumors were isolated from the flanks of mice, mechanically dissociated using scissors and crushed through a 70 μm cell strainer in a single well of a 6-well plate to generate a single cell suspension. The cell suspension was then passed through a second 70 μm cell strainer into a 50 ml conical tube and diluted up to a total volume of 30 ml with RPMI-1640 media. Next, 10 ml of Ficoll-PaqueTM PLUS (GE Healthcare, Cat # 17-1440-03) was slowly added to the bottom of the 50 ml conical tube, and then centrifuged at 1025 × g for 20 min at 20 °C. Following centrifugation, the upper 20 ml layer of media was discarded, and the remaining 10 ml of media and layer of mononuclear cells were isolated and transferred to a clean 50 ml conical tube. Complete RPMI-1640 medium was then used to wash the mononuclear cells twice at 650 × g for 10 min. Upon completion of the washes, cells were stimulated using a Cell Stimulation Cocktail (eBioscience, Cat # 00-4970-03) for 3 h, stained with Live/Dead fixable exclusion dye (Tonbo Bioscience, Cat# 13-0868), followed by fluorochrome-conjugated antibodies in PBS with 2% fetal bovine serum and 1 mM EDTA for 1 h at 4 °C for anti-CD45.2 (eBioscience, Cat # 17-0454-82), anti-CD45 (Biolegend, Cat # 103138), anti-CD3 (Tonbo Bioscience, Cat # 75-0032), anti-CD4 (Biolegend, Cat # 100453), and anti-CD8α antibody (Biolegend Cat # 100751 and Tonbo Bioscience, Cat # 20-0081-U025). Upon completion of surface marker staining, cells were washed twice followed by fixation with a Foxp3/transcription factor staining buffer set (eBioscience, Cat# 00-5523-00) and intracellular staining for anti-IFN-γ (Biolegend, Cat #505806), and anti-Foxp3 antibody (Invitrogen, Cat # 12-5773-82). Flow cytometry analyses were performed on BD FACSymphony A5 SE flow cytometer at the UConn Health Flow Cytometry Core Facility. Data were analyzed with FlowJo software (FlowJo, LLC).

Serum cytokine analysis

C57BL/6J mice were treated with 1 × 107 pfu of WT VSVIN-IL-12 or Pro-VSVIN-IL-12 every three days for a total of two treatments. One day following the last treatment, blood was collected from mice, left at room temperature to clot for 30 min, and centrifuged at 1000 × g for 15 min followed by serum collection. Serum cytokine analysis was run targeting IFN-γ and IL-12p70 according to manufacturer’s specification (BioLegend, LEGENDplex Cat # 740824).

Histology of lung tissue

Lungs were harvested and fixed in 10% formalin in PBS for 48 h at 4 °C, washed with PBS, then dehydrated in 70% ethanol in water followed by paraffin-embedding, and sectioning. H&E-stained sections were prepared for analysis of metastatic nodules at the UConn Health Histology Core Facility.

Transmission electron microscopy (TEM) imaging of the viral particles

Concentrated virus particles were fixed using 1% glutaraldehyde solution for 10 min on ice. 10 μl of the fixed viral particles were placed on glow discharged 400 mesh carbon-coated nickel grid (Electron Microscopy Sciences, #CF400NI50) for 1 min. Grids were then rinsed with PBS (3 times, 5 min each) and further with DI water (3 times, 5 min each). Negative stain with freshly prepared 1% uranyl acetate in DI water was applied to the grids for 45 s. The grids were let to dry and examined under Hitachi H7650 Transmission Electron Microscopy at the UConn Health Central Electron Microscopy Facility.

Western blotting

Antibodies used for Western blotting include: Anti-VSV-GIN antibody (Sigma-Aldrich, Cat # V4888), Anti-VSV-GNJ (a gift from CaroGen Corporation), Anti-FLAG M2 antibody (Millipore Sigma, Cat # F1804), Anti-VSV antibody (Imanis Life Sciences, Cat # REA005), IL-12p35 antibody/IL-12A (NSJ Bioreagents, Cat # RQ4058), Anti-rabbit IgG, HRP-linked Antibody (Cell Signaling Technology, Cat # 7074), Anti-mouse IgG, HRP-linked Antibody (Cell Signaling Technology, Cat # 7076).

Single-cell RNA-Seq data processing and analysis

Tumors from 5 mice were dissected and tumor infiltrating lymphocytes (TILs) isolated and pooled. TILs were stained for CD45, CD3, CD4, CD8 and viability by propidium iodide (Invitrogen, Cat # P3566). After FACs, cells were washed and resuspended in PBS containing 0.04% BSA and immediately processed for sequencing as follows. Cells were counted and viability was assessed using the LUNA-FX7 (Logos Biosystems) with Acridine Orange/Propodium Iodide Stain. Up to 29,000 cells were then loaded for capture onto one lane of a 10x Genomics Chromium GEM-X Single Cell 5’ Chip v3. Capture, barcoding and llumina sequencing library preparation were performed according to manufacturer protocols using the 10x Genomics Chromium X platform 5’ version 3 GEM-X chemistry (protocol CG000733, revA). From each sample, cDNA was used for construction of both gene expression and TCR (VDJ) libraries. cDNA and libraries were assessed for quality via Tapestation 4200 (Agilent) and Qubit Fluorometer (ThermoFisher) and quantified by KAPA qPCR. Libraries were sequenced on an Illumina NovaSeq X+ (10B 100 cycle) flow cell with a 28-10-10-90 (R1-I1-I2-R2) asymmetric read configuration, targeting 20,000 barcoded cells with an average sequencing depth of 50,000 reads per cell and 15,000 reads per cell for the gene expression and TCR libraries, respectively. Sequencing was performed by the Jackson Laboratory Single-Cell Genomics Lab and University of Connecticut Center for Genome Innovation.

Illumina basecall (.bcl) files were converted to FASTQ using bcl2fastq v2.20.0.422 (Illumina) and FASTQ files were aligned to the GRCm39 reference assembly with vM33 annotations from GENCODE (10x Genomics GRCm39 reference version 2024-A) using 10X Genomics Cell Ranger ‘multi‘ pipeline (version 9.0.1) with default parameters. Single-cell RNA sequencing cell-counts matrices were analyzed using Seurat v5.3.0 with R version 4.5.1. A merged Seurat object containing each of the 5 conditions was generated, and QC was performed; retaining cells with ≥500 genes and ≤10% reads mapping to mitochondrial genes. Doublets were filtered using DoubletFinder version 2.0.6. A total of 88,297 single cells were retained after QC. Gene expression was normalized, scaled, and dimensionality reduction was performed. UMAP and heatmap plots were generated using Seurat, pheatmap v1.0.13 and ggplot2 v3.5.2. Single-cell TCR data was integrated with 5’ GEX data and analyzed using Seurat and scRepertoire v2.4.0. Clonal expansion was determined based on CDR3 nucleotide sequences using scRepertoire. Differential gene expression was determined using Seurat’s FindMarkers function (adjusted p-value < 0.05, log2 Fold Change ≥0.25, min.pct ≥0.1).

Statistical analysis

Statistical analysis was performed with GraphPad Prism version 10.3.1 (GraphPad Software, San Diego, USA), unless stated otherwise. Multi-group comparison was analyzed by one-way ANOVA. Multi-group and multi-variable comparison was analyzed by two-way ANOVA. Pairwise comparison was analyzed by Student’s t-test. In all statistical tests, p < 0.05 was considered significant. In all experiments, *p < 0.05; **p < 0.01; ***p < 0.001; and ****p < 0.0001. Error bars denote SEM unless otherwise stated in the figure legends. Survival data from the animal studies were analyzed by log-rank test. Unless stated otherwise, n is the number of biological replicates (individual mice, organs, or tissue samples, etc.) with all experiments being repeated at least two times. Representative results are shown.
