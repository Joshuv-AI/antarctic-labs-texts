Lyman-alpha emission at the end of reionization: line strengths and profiles from MMT and JWST observations at z ∼ 5 − 6 z\sim 5-6
Gonzalo Prieto-Lyon ,, Charlotte A. Mason, Victoria Strait, Gabriel Brammer, Rohan P. Naidu, Romain A. Meyer, Pascal Oesch, Sandro Tacchella, Alba Covelo-Paz, Emma Giovinazzo, Mengyuan Xiao

Abstract
Aims. With JWST, it is now possible to use Lyman-Alpha (Ly α \mathrm{\alpha} ) emission from galaxies beyond z > 8 z>8 to trace neutral hydrogen in the intergalactic medium (IGM) as the Universe became reionized. However, observed Ly α \mathrm{\alpha} emission lines are affected by scattering by neutral hydrogen in the IGM and the interstellar and circum-galactic medium, necessitating ‘baseline’ models of Ly α \mathrm{\alpha} properties in the ionized IGM to disentangle their impacts. In this work, we characterize Ly α \mathrm{\alpha} properties at the end of reionization, z ∼ 5 − 6 z\sim 5-6 , providing a baseline that can be applied to z > 6 z>6 observations. Methods. We targeted GOODS-N with MMT/Binospec, obtaining R ∼ \sim 4360 rest-frame UV spectra of 236 galaxies at z ∼ 5 − 6 z\sim 5-6 , selected primarily from HST/CANDELS, finding 62 Ly α \mathrm{\alpha} detections. We use JWST observations from JADES and FRESCO for a subset of our sources to characterize Ly α \mathrm{\alpha} properties as a function of UV continuum and H α \alpha emission. We present the first statistical measurements of the Ly α \mathrm{\alpha} FWHM distribution at z ∼ 5 − 6 z\sim 5-6 , and produce empirical baseline models of Ly α \mathrm{\alpha} equivalent width (EW Lyα ) and escape fraction ( f esc Ly ​ α f_{\textrm{esc}}^{\textrm{Ly}\alpha} ) conditional on UV magnitude and slope. Results. We find our EW Lyα and f esc Ly ​ α f_{\textrm{esc}}^{\textrm{Ly}\alpha} models strongly depend on UV magnitude, and infer 45 ± \pm 5% and < 62 ± 8 % <62\pm 8\% of M uv {}_{\textsc{uv}} = -19.5 galaxies have EW Lyα > > 25Å and f esc Ly ​ α > 0.2 {f_{\textrm{esc}}^{\textrm{Ly}\alpha}}>0.2 , respectively. We find a mean FWHM Lyα of 245 km s -1 and median Ly α \mathrm{\alpha} velocity offset of 258 km s -1 , and that both correlate with increasing UV luminosity. Our median observed Ly α \mathrm{\alpha} line profile is broader and has higher velocity offset compared to pre-JWST models based on z ∼ 2 z\sim 2 lines, which may reflect both resonant scattering by residual neutral hydrogen in the IGM at z ∼ 5 − 6 z\sim 5-6 and increasing ISM/CGM densities. Our median line profile thus predicts higher Ly α \mathrm{\alpha} transmission in a fully neutral IGM, providing insight into recent detections of Ly α \mathrm{\alpha} at z > 10 z>10 .

Introduction

The epoch of reionization (EoR) was the last phase transition of the Universe, when the majority of the neutral hydrogen in the intergalactic medium (IGM) became ionized due to the injection of ionizing photons from the first galaxies
(
Stark 2016
, e.g.,)
. The rate and morphology of this transition is determined by the galaxies’ ionizing properties and thus constraining the reionization process offers unique insights into early galaxies, even those below our direct detection limits. Multiple independent studies have reached consensus that the Universe was fully reionized at z
\sim
5 - 6
(
Fan et al. 2006
;
Becker et al. 2015
;
Eilers et al. 2018
;
Yang et al. 2020
;
Qin et al. 2021
;
Bosman et al. 2022
;
Gaikwad et al. 2023
, e.g.)
, but likely predominantly neutral at
z\sim 7-8

(
Stark et al. 2010
;
Treu et al. 2012
;
Pentericci et al. 2014
;
Schenker et al. 2014
;
Mason et al. 2018b
;
Davies et al. 2018
;
Yang et al. 2020
;
Jung et al. 2020
;
Bolan et al. 2022
;
Umeda et al. 2024
;
Nakane et al. 2024
;
Tang et al. 2024b
;
Jones et al. 2024
;
Mason et al. 2025
, e.g.,)
.
The earliest stages of reionization at
z>9
are still very poorly constrained, but will be crucial for understanding the build up of the first galaxies and the higher than expected UV luminosity density detected by JWST
(
Gelli et al. 2024
;
Muñoz et al. 2024
;
Whitler et al. 2025
)
.

Lyman-alpha (Ly
\mathrm{\alpha}
) emission from galaxies is one of our best probes of the early stages of the EoR. Its high cross-section for damping wing scattering by neutral hydrogen means Ly
\mathrm{\alpha}
flux traces the IGM opacity
(
Miralda-Escude 1998
;
Mesinger & Furlanetto 2008
, e.g.,)
and we are now detecting 1000s of
z>8
galaxies with JWST
(
Adams et al. 2022
;
Donnan et al. 2024
;
Whitler et al. 2025
, e.g.)
, providing large samples to comprehensively trace the IGM at high redshift.
In contrast, the number density of quasars – the typical IGM probes at lower redshifts –
(
Jiang et al. 2016
;
Matsuoka et al. 2018
;
Wang et al. 2020
;
Schindler et al. 2023
, e.g.,)
, decreases significantly at z
>
7
(
Euclid Collaboration et al. 2019
)
.
The visibility of Ly
\mathrm{\alpha}
from galaxies drops considerably at
z>6
, implying an increasing neutral IGM
(
Stark et al. 2010
;
Pentericci et al. 2014
;
De Barros et al. 2017
;
Jung et al. 2019
;
Fuller et al. 2020
;
Laporte et al. 2021
;
Tang et al. 2024b
, Seen in the declining fraction of galaxies detected with strong (EW
>
25 Å) Ly
\mathrm{\alpha}
,)
.
JWST has opened a new window on reionization studies – enabling Ly
\mathrm{\alpha}
observations in faint galaxies at
z\,\hbox to0.0pt{\lower 3.5pt\hbox{$\mathchar 0\sim$}\hss}\raise 1.0pt\hbox{$>$}\,6
without the atmospheric and sensitivity limitations of ground-based telescopes.
Early JWST results have confirmed the downturn in Ly
\mathrm{\alpha}
emission at
z\,\hbox to0.0pt{\lower 3.5pt\hbox{$\mathchar 0\sim$}\hss}\raise 1.0pt\hbox{$>$}\,6
seen from the ground and demonstrated strong Ly
\mathrm{\alpha}
is extremely rare at
z>8
in current samples
(
Nakane et al. 2024
;
Chen et al. 2024
;
Tang et al. 2024b
;
Jones et al. 2024
;
Kageura et al. 2025
)
, and have also provided the first detections of Ly
\mathrm{\alpha}
at
z>10

(
Bunker et al. 2023
;
Witstok et al. 2025a
)
. These observations demonstrate JWST will be a key tool to understand the earliest stages of reionization.

However, the interpretation of Ly
\mathrm{\alpha}
observations during the Epoch of Reionization rests on understanding the transmission of Ly
\mathrm{\alpha}
on multiple spatial scales. Ly
\mathrm{\alpha}
photons are subject to scattering not only by neutral hydrogen in the IGM, but also in the interstellar and circumgalactic media (ISM and CGM).
To use Ly
\mathrm{\alpha}
to track the reionization history, an understanding of Ly
\mathrm{\alpha}
emerging from the ISM/CGM is necessary.
In particular, because of the strong wavelength dependence of the IGM Ly
\mathrm{\alpha}
damping wing
(
Miralda-Escude 1998
)
, both an understanding of the Ly
\mathrm{\alpha}
line strength and
spectral shape
emerging from the ISM/CGM is important to disentangle the impact of the IGM
(
Dijkstra et al. 2011
;
Mason et al. 2018a
;
Endsley et al. 2022
)
.

To this end, samples of galaxies at the end of the EoR, z
\sim
5 - 6, have been commonly used as a baseline for interpreting higher redshift observations
(
Stark et al. 2011
;
De Barros et al. 2017
)
. These galaxies are expected to be physically similar to those at earlier epochs, but suffer minimal damping wing attenuation by the neutral IGM. Therefore, these galaxies provide a window into the emergent Ly
\mathrm{\alpha}
emission from the ISM and CGM, and have been used to infer the additional impact of the IGM on
z>6
galaxies’ Ly
\mathrm{\alpha}

(
Mason et al. 2018b
;
Whitler et al. 2020
;
Jung et al. 2020
;
Bolan et al. 2022
, e.g.,)
.
However, these baseline samples had been limited to the most readily available observables pre-JWST – Ly
\mathrm{\alpha}
EW and UV photometry, and, with only a handful of systemic redshifts known from strong rest-UV or FIR lines
(
Stark et al. 2017
;
Bradač et al. 2017
, e.g.,)
: our knowledge of the emerging Ly
\mathrm{\alpha}
line profile had been severely limited. These and other limitations, such as the unknown Ly
\mathrm{\alpha}
reionization analyses, have led previous models to rely on empirical relations based on
z\sim 0-2
galaxy samples
(
Mason et al. 2018b
;
Mason et al. 2019
;
Hayes et al. 2023
, e.g.,)
, where the rest-frame optical is accessible from the ground. However, it is unclear whether these samples are good analogs of reionization-era sources. For example, recent works have shown the Ly
\mathrm{\alpha}
velocity offsets of z
\sim
6 galaxies appear systematically higher than models derived from
z\sim 2
galaxies
(
Prieto-Lyon et al. 2023b
;
Tang et al. 2024a
)
.

JWST’s ability to observe the rest-frame optical is transforming our ability to understand galaxies in this era.
Thanks to
R\,\hbox to0.0pt{\lower 3.5pt\hbox{$\mathchar 0\sim$}\hss}\raise 1.0pt\hbox{$>$}\,1000
-resolution observations, such as NIRCam grism data, we now have accurate systemic redshifts for 1000s of
z>5
galaxies
(
Oesch et al. 2023
;
Covelo-Paz et al. 2025
;
Meyer et al. 2024
, e.g.,)
and measurements of Ly
\mathrm{\alpha}
escape fractions (
f_{\textrm{esc}}^{\textrm{Ly}\alpha}
), relative to Balmer lines, and velocity offsets (
\Delta v_{\mathrm{Ly}\alpha}
) are now routinely being measured for
z\sim 5-9
galaxies
(
Lin et al. 2024
;
Chen et al. 2024
;
Tang et al. 2023
;
Tang et al. 2024a
;
Saxena et al. 2024
;
Chen et al. 2025
, e.g.,)
.

Tang et al. 2024a
recently presented constraints on the emergent Ly
\mathrm{\alpha}
EW and escape fraction distributions in bins of M
{}_{\textsc{uv}}
, UV
\beta
slope and [OIII]+H
\beta
EW, and velocity offset distributions in
z\sim 5-6
galaxies.
These observations have provided a more robust baseline for predicting emergent Ly
\mathrm{\alpha}
at
z>6
and thus interpreting observed Ly
\mathrm{\alpha}
in the context of reionization
(
Tang et al. 2024b
)
.

However, these works have mostly focused on measuring Ly
\mathrm{\alpha}
emission distributions in bins of galaxies properties, limiting the information used in the conditional distributions, and less attention has focused on the shape of the Ly
\mathrm{\alpha}
line profile at
z\sim 5-6
.
Accurately measuring the emergent lineshape is critical for understanding the transmission of Ly
\mathrm{\alpha}
through the IGM
(
Dijkstra & Wyithe 2010
;
Mason et al. 2018b
;
Mason et al. 2019
;
Endsley et al. 2022
;
Tang et al. 2024c
;
Mukherjee et al. 2024
)
.
High resolution spectroscopy is required to accurately measure both the asymmetric shape of Ly
\mathrm{\alpha}
, and its offset from the systemic redshift (
\Delta v_{\mathrm{Ly}\alpha}
). Resolution below
\sim
100 km s
-1
(
R\,\hbox to0.0pt{\lower 3.5pt\hbox{$\mathchar 0\sim$}\hss}\raise 1.0pt\hbox{$>$}\,4000
) is optimal, as typical full-width half-max (FWHM) in Ly
\mathrm{\alpha}
-emitters in the ionized IGM (
z<5
) range from
\sim 100-400
km s
-1

(
Verhamme et al. 2018
;
Tang et al. 2024c
)
and values of
\Delta v_{\mathrm{Ly}\alpha}
range from 150 km s
-1
to 500 km s
-1

(
Prieto-Lyon et al. 2023b
;
Tang et al. 2024c
, e.g.,)
.
Tang et al. 2024a
demonstrated that
z\sim 5-6
Ly
\mathrm{\alpha}
line profiles appear more offset from systemic than profiles for sources with comparable EW
Lyα
at
z\sim 2-3

(
Tang et al. 2024c
)
, which they discuss may be due to residual neutral gas in the ionized IGM resonantly scattering Ly
\mathrm{\alpha}
close to line center and/or increased scattering in (relatively dust-poor) ISM and CGM.
But we currently have no statistical information about the broadness and asymmetry of Ly
\mathrm{\alpha}
line profiles at these redshifts, which provides additional information to understand the evolution in line profiles, primarily due to the moderate to low resolution of Ly
\mathrm{\alpha}
spectroscopy in fields overlapping with deep JWST spectroscopy (i.e. VLT/MUSE
R\sim 3000
). Additionally, JWST spectroscopic observations using NIRSpec/PRISM have insufficient resolution (R
\sim
100) to accurately measure Ly
\mathrm{\alpha}
velocity offsets. By contrast, NIRCam grism spectra, with a higher resolution (R
\sim
1600), is better suited for studies such as this one.

To that end, we have carried out a deep, high-resolution (
R\sim 4400
) near-infrared spectroscopic survey of 236
z\sim 5-6.5
Lyman-break galaxies in the GOODS North field
(
Giavalisco et al. 2004
)
, selected from deep HST imaging, with MMT/Binospec
(
Fabricant et al. 2019
)
, with the aim of constraining the Ly
\mathrm{\alpha}
strength, and line profiles emerging from the ISM/CGM of galaxies in the post-reionization era. MMT/Binospec, with its high resolution (R
\sim
4360), high throughput, and wide on-sky coverage (two 8
′
x 15
′
fields of view), offers great advantage for Ly
\mathrm{\alpha}
studies, surpassing the resolution of most z
\sim
5-6 detections in the literature (e.g., LRIS, MUSE, VIMOS, JWST, R
<
3500). Our sample includes 62 high-resolution Ly
\mathrm{\alpha}
detections.
Thanks to the excellent ancillary datasets in GOODS-N, we supplement our Ly
\mathrm{\alpha}
spectroscopy with JWST/NIRCam slitless spectra from FRESCO + CONGRESS
(
Oesch et al. 2023
;
Egami et al. 2023
)
, and deep HST and JWST photometry from the CANDELS
(
Giavalisco et al. 2004
;
Grogin et al. 2011
;
Koekemoer et al. 2011
)
and JADES surveys
(
Eisenstein et al. 2023
)
to obtain precise measurements of galaxy properties (UV magnitudes, UV beta slopes, systemic velocities, Ly
\mathrm{\alpha}
escape fractions:
f_{\textrm{esc}}^{\textrm{Ly}\alpha}
).
With these data we build two new empirical models for EW
Lyα
and
f_{\textrm{esc}}^{\textrm{Ly}\alpha}
conditional on UV observables, M
{}_{\textsc{uv}}
and
\mathrm{\beta}
, which can be used as a baseline for constraining the reionization history. We also explore the properties of Ly
\mathrm{\alpha}
lineprofiles (
\Delta v_{\mathrm{Ly}\alpha}
, FWHM, asymmetry) in our sample.

2.5
Binospec Data Reduction

In the following section, we describe how we reduced our Binospec data applying a telluric calibration, a flux calibration and additional sky subtraction. The initial reduction and sky subtraction was done by the Binospec Pipeline
6
6

6

http://mingus.mmto.arizona.edu/ bjw/mmt/binospec_info.html
. To optimize the reductions for our faint emission line targets we further reduced each individual exposure. None of our targets showed evidence of a UV continuum detection.

To reduce features due to atmospheric emission such as the O2 A& B bands, we perform a telluric calibration of our sources. We follow a similar method to
Vacca et al. 2003
. On each mask we observe two standard F-type stars. We compare the observed star in each exposure to F-star synthetic models using the
SEDPy
7
7

7

https://github.com/bd-j/sedpy
library.
We use the synthetic models that best fits the spectrum of our standard stars. We extract and normalize the spectra to obtain the telluric transmission
T(\lambda)
by dividing the data by the model (both continuum normalized).
We apply the transmission function to all spectra in each exposure:

\displaystyle f_{corr}(\lambda)=\frac{f_{obs}(\lambda)}{T(\lambda-\alpha)^{\beta}}

(1)

The constant
\alpha
accounts for any wavelength shifts between the spectra and transmission and
\beta
is used as a scaling factor of the transmission. To calculate
\alpha
and
\beta
we minimize the RMS in the two most important telluric regions, O2 A& B bands in f
{}_{corr}(\lambda)
for every exposure for every slit containing a high redshift target.

We perform flux calibration for each exposure by calibrating our standard stars with magnitudes obtained in the
SDSS

(
Abdurro’uf et al. 2022
)
. We use the
i
filter of
Sloan
since it covers a similar wavelength range as the MMT/Binospec data. We convolve our standard star spectra with the corresponding
i
sdss
filter using SEDPy.

We perform additional sky subtraction of the exposures for each mask, to improve on the pipeline sky subtraction. We make a global 1D sky spectrum for each individual exposure,
S(\lambda)
, using the standard deviation of flux in all slits containing
z>5
targets with no clear emission lines (as our targets are faint, we do not expect to detect the continuum, so the ‘empty’ slits should measure the sky), using sigma clipping to remove any cosmic rays. We perform additional sky subtraction to each spectrum per exposure as:

f_{\mathrm{skysub}}(\lambda)=f_{\mathrm{obs}}(\lambda)-A\cdot S(\lambda)

(2)

We find
A
by minimizing the RMS of
f_{\mathrm{skysub}}(\lambda)
per slit’s exposure given the S(
\lambda
) measured in each mask, so that the distribution of signal in an empty region of the slit is centered around
\frac{S}{N}\sim 0
. For this we use
LMFIT
(non-linear least-squares minimization)
8
8

8

https://lmfit.github.io/lmfit-py/
.

Lyman-Alpha and rest-frame optical line identification

In the following section we describe our search for Ly
\mathrm{\alpha}
emission in our MMT/Binospec spectra (Section
3.1
), and H
\alpha
and/ or [O
iii
] emission in the FRESCO JWST/NIRCam slitless spectra (Section
3.2
).

3.1
Lyman-alpha emission line detection

For the case of spectroscopically confirmed galaxies (either from rest-optical lines or existing Ly
\mathrm{\alpha}
spectroscopy), extracting the Ly
\mathrm{\alpha}
profile is straightforward since the redshift is already known. For sources without spectroscopic confirmation we perform a systematic search for the emission line given the photometric redshift.

We first collapse the 2D spectra into 1D spectra for each target by extracting within the median spatial extension of our Ly
\mathrm{\alpha}
detections (= 1.7
′′
= 7 pixels). We maximize the signal captured in the extraction, with more extended extractions the SNR plateaus adding mostly noise. Starting at the position of the UV continuum, we repeat this process for every spatial position of the slit within 2.4
′′
of the UV coordinates to account for any spatial offset between the UV continuum and Ly
\mathrm{\alpha}
emission
(
Hoag et al. 2019
;
Lemaux et al. 2021
)
. We then scan through the 1D spectra to search for high SNR peaks, scanning all wavelengths. We flag line candidates where SNR
\geq
3 is found in 3 consecutive wavelength pixels, essentially an integrated SNR
\geq 5
detection. Finally, we manually inspect the candidate lines in the 1D and 2D spectra to remove any false positives near the sky lines or from randomly high peaked background. For galaxies where we have a systemic redshift from the rest-frame optical lines, we do a manual search of Ly
\mathrm{\alpha}
within
\sim
2000 km s
-1
of the systemic redshift. For detected emission lines we measure Ly
\mathrm{\alpha}
fluxes from a fitted asymmetric Gaussian (Section
3.1.3
) as this allows for more accurate measurements in the case of strong skylines near the emission line than obtaining the flux directly from the 1D spectra.

3.1.1
Completeness

To evaluate the completeness of our emission line search we perform Monte Carlo simulations. We add simulated Gaussian Ly
\mathrm{\alpha}
profiles at random wavelengths into the slits of non-detected targets and then attempt to recover them with the method described above. We vary the line flux and FWHM as these are the most important factors in determining the detection of a line, and show the resulting completeness in Figure
2
. The asymmetry of the profile is sub-dominant to the flux and FWHM so we use a Gaussian profile for simplicity. The presence of sky lines plays an important role, retrieval is unlikely if an emission line falls at the same wavelength, leading to high redshift line recovery to be even harder due to the increased sky line emission at longer wavelengths.

completeness for lines with FWHM=100 km s
-1
and flux
\,\hbox to0.0pt{\lower 3.5pt\hbox{$\mathchar 0\sim$}\hss}\raise 1.0pt\hbox{$>$}\,10^{-17.2}

3.1.2
Slit Losses

As Ly
\mathrm{\alpha}
can be spatially extended beyond the width of our extraction window (1.7”) we correct our Ly
\mathrm{\alpha}
fluxes for slit losses following the approach of
Tang et al. 2024a
. We first produce a 2D Ly
\mathrm{\alpha}
surface brightness profile following for typical z
\sim
5-6 galaxies as measured by MUSE
(
Leclercq et al. 2017
)
: consisting of two declining exponential functions to describe a Ly
\mathrm{\alpha}
core and an extended Ly
\mathrm{\alpha}
halo, with 0.3 kpc and 3.8 kpc exponential decay constants respectively. We allocate 35% and 65% of the total flux to the core and halo respectively. We convolve the resulting model with the PSF of each mask, and quantify the flux that would be obtained following the Ly
\mathrm{\alpha}
extraction (7pix
\sim
1.7
′′
).

For all sources, we estimate 65-70% of the total Ly
\mathrm{\alpha}
flux is captured by our extractions in the Binospec slits. We take this into account for any calculations involving these quantities. We calculate the slit loss directly for individual sources, and use the median of 67% slit loss for our non-detection upper limits. We note that we assume each galaxy has the same surface brightness profile, therefore the only difference between galaxies will be the redshift and following angular size conversion.

3.1.3
Ly
\mathrm{\alpha}
fluxes and upper limits

For galaxies with Ly
\mathrm{\alpha}
detections we obtain line fluxes by fitting the lines with an asymmetric Gaussian profile, this enables us to account for the presence of sky lines. Thanks to the
R\sim 4000
resolution of our spectra, we are able to observe detailed Ly
\mathrm{\alpha}
line profiles for our detections. We fit a skewed Gaussian model to our Ly
\mathrm{\alpha}
lines:

f(x;A,\xi,\omega,\alpha)=\frac{A}{\omega\sqrt{2\pi}}e^{[{-{(x-\xi)^{2}}/{{2\omega}^{2}}}]}\Bigl\{1+{\operatorname{erf}}\bigl[\frac{{\alpha}(x-\xi)}{\omega\sqrt{2}}\bigr]\Bigr\}

(3)

The location is represented by
\xi
. The skewness is parameterised by
\alpha
. The scale is
\omega
and it describes the standard deviation with a factor of skewness. For example, a symmetrical Gaussian profile will have
\alpha=0
, while for a completely red-sided asymmetric profile
\alpha\rightarrow+\infty
. When fitting we fix
\alpha\geq 0
as we expect only profiles which are truncated on the blue side due to the resonant scattering of Ly
\mathrm{\alpha}
photons
(
Laursen et al. 2011
)
. The flux of the line is then obtained by integrating the resulting line profile. To be consistent with the literature, we extract the FWHM by its definition, by measuring the width of the profile at half its maximum and subtract the instrumental resolution in quadrature.

We fit the line profiles using an MCMC with the python library
emcee
9
9

9

https://emcee.readthedocs.io/

(
Foreman-Mackey et al. 2013
)
.
As the skewness, is most sensitive to
\alpha\sim 0-3
we set a log uniform prior on the skewness between
\alpha\in[0,15]
, allowing perfectly Gaussian to completely asymmetric profiles. For all other parameters of the fit, amplitude,location and scale, we use uniform priors with positive values. By setting 30 walkers with 2000 steps, with a burn-in of 750 steps, we obtain converged distributions for our parameters. We use the chains of the MCMC to measure the FWHM posterior distribution. In Appendix
B
we show some examples of the spectra and the resulting fits.

For the galaxies which do not show a Ly
\mathrm{\alpha}
emission feature, we calculate flux upper limits using the following procedure. We add simulated emission lines to the 2D spectra of our sources, considering a grid of flux, FWHM and redshift. We take uniformly distributed values of flux between
0.1-3.2\times 10^{-17}
erg/s/cm
2
, and FWHM values between 100 - 400 km/s, which span the range we find most of our detections (see Section
4
). To account for the presence of skylines and the photometric redshift we take uniformly distributed values of redshift within
z_{phot}\pm 0.5
. We generate fully asymmetric Gaussian profiles and run the line selection code (Section
3.1
) to detect SNR
>
5 simulated lines.

We simulated 500,000 lines for each galaxy across the above grid of parameters. We calculate the minimum detectable line flux for every sampled FWHM and redshift. We take the median of the resulting flux distribution as the Ly
\mathrm{\alpha}
flux limit for the galaxy.

4
Lyman Alpha and Galaxy Properties

The combination of high resolution Ly
\mathrm{\alpha}
spectroscopy, deep HST and JWST photometry, and rest-frame optical JWST spectroscopy allows us to characterize Ly
\mathrm{\alpha}
emission in our
z\sim 5-6
sample via measurements of Ly
\mathrm{\alpha}
EW, line profiles and velocity offsets, and escape fractions. In the following section we describe these properties in our sample and investigate trends between Ly
\mathrm{\alpha}
properties and galaxy properties to better understand Ly
\mathrm{\alpha}
transmission in the ISM and CGM in our sample.

4.1
UV Continuum properties

We calculate the UV absolute magnitude (M
{}_{\textsc{uv}}
) and UV slope (
\beta
) for our targets using HST and JWST photometry as described in Section
2.4
.
We include only filters that have their effective width (W
eff
) within the rest-frame UV between 1250-2600 Å so as to not include bands containing Ly
\mathrm{\alpha}
flux. We obtain all filter information from
Rodrigo & Solano 2020
.
For the redshift, we use z
sys
when available. If z
sys
is unavailable but Ly
\mathrm{\alpha}
is detected, we use z
Lyα
. If neither are available, we use z
phot
.
We note the choice of redshift has negligible effect on our M
{}_{\textsc{uv}}
and
\beta

We fit a power law,
f_{\lambda}\propto\lambda^{\beta}
, to the selected photometric filters that capture the UV continuum. We retrieve M
{}_{\textsc{uv}}
by evaluating the fit at 1500 Å. For sources with JWST NIRCam data, the M
{}_{\textsc{uv}}
and
\beta
estimates are consistent with the HST estimates, but we obtain lower uncertainties. We fit the continuum using an MCMC method assuming a Gaussian likelihood for the flux densities.

We plot the resulting M

Similarly to previously reported trends in the literature

Bouwens et al. 2012

Bouwens et al. 2014

4.2
Ly
\mathrm{\alpha}
Equivalent Widths

To calculate Ly
\mathrm{\alpha}
rest-frame equivalent widths (EW), we compare the Ly
\mathrm{\alpha}
flux, or upper limit, with the flux density of the UV continuum. We estimate the continuum flux density,
f_{\lambda,UV}
, at the Ly
\mathrm{\alpha}
wavelength, 1215.6 Å, using our power-law fit to the UV continuum (Section
4.1
). The rest-frame EW is:

\mathrm{EW_{rest}=\frac{F_{Ly\alpha}}{f_{\lambda,UV}(1+z)}}

(4)

Where F
Lyα
is the total flux of the Ly
\mathrm{\alpha}
line in erg s
-1
cm
-2
(or upper limit) and f
λ,UV
the flux density [erg s
-1
cm
-2

\AA^{-1}
] of the UV continuum at 1215.6
\AA
. As our sources are faint and the continuum is not detected in the spectra, we do not perform continuum subtraction. For sources with only photometric redshifts we use z
phot
as the redshift.

In Figure
5
we show our EW
Lyα
measurements against the 2 UV observables. We mainly find enhanced EW
Lyα
in UV faint galaxies, while a trend with UV slope is not evidently present. We also show the average 5
\sigma
flux limit of our observations. Since the line flux limit is related to Ly
\mathrm{\alpha}
broadness, it is possible to observe lines below this limit if they are narrower than the median FWHM
Lyα
of our sample.

4.3
Ly
\mathrm{\alpha}
line profiles

Thanks to the
R\sim 4000
(
\approx 70
km s
-1
) resolution of the Binospec spectra and high S/N of many of our detections, we are able to observe detailed Ly
\mathrm{\alpha}
line profile shapes, which offer insights into the scattering of Ly
\mathrm{\alpha}
in the ISM and CGM
(
Neufeld 1991
;
Verhamme et al. 2006
;
Gronke et al. 2015
, e.g.,)
. At
z\sim 0.2-2
, the strongest Ly
\mathrm{\alpha}
emitters tend to show narrow Ly
\mathrm{\alpha}
emission lines with flux close to the systemic velocity, implying Ly
\mathrm{\alpha}
is scattering in low column density neutral gas and thus not diffusing significantly in frequency space
(
Erb et al. 2014
;
Hashimoto et al. 2015
;
Henry et al. 2015
;
Yang et al. 2017
;
Matthee et al. 2021
;
Naidu et al. 2022
;
Tang et al. 2024c
, e.g.,)
.
At low redshifts (
z=0.05-0.44
), Ly
\mathrm{\alpha}
line widths and velocity offsets are seen to correlate with properties, e.g. M
{}_{\textsc{uv}}
, FWHM of rest-frame optical lines, which likely trace gas mass
(
Hayes et al. 2023
)
.
The sample of published spectra with sufficient S/N and resolution to measure Ly
\mathrm{\alpha}
lineshapes at
z\sim 5-6
is small (e.g. MUSE resolution is
\sim
100 km s
-1
). Our sample thus provides new insights into the lineprofiles at
z\sim 5-6
. We are particularly interested in understanding if Ly
\mathrm{\alpha}
lineshapes at
z\sim 5-6
are significantly different from lower-
z
samples in ways which may impact the transmission of Ly
\mathrm{\alpha}
through the IGM.

As discussed in Section
3.1.3
we fit our 62 detected Ly
\mathrm{\alpha}
lines with an asymmetric Gaussian profile, enabling us to measure the broadness and skewness of the lines, which we discuss in more detail below.
A strong asymmetric Ly
\mathrm{\alpha}
lineshape is expected at
z\,\hbox to0.0pt{\lower 3.5pt\hbox{$\mathchar 0\sim$}\hss}\raise 1.0pt\hbox{$>$}\,5
due to resonant scattering by residual neutral gas in the ionized IGM
(
Laursen et al. 2011
;
Mason & Gronke 2020
, e.g.,)
. To explore the asymmetry in our data, we choose a binary classification of asymmetric and symmetric. We set profiles with
\alpha>3
in our asymmetric Gaussian model (see Equation
3
) as asymmetric profiles and sample from our posteriors in our error analysis. We find that 69
\pm
7
\%
of our Ly
\mathrm{\alpha}
sample have asymmetric line profiles, suggesting the IGM is already playing a significant role in shaping Ly
\mathrm{\alpha}
emission at
z\sim 5-6
, consistent with previous analyses of stacked lines without and with systemic redshifts by
Pentericci et al. 2018
;
Hayes et al. 2021
and
Tang et al. 2024a
respectively.

The shape of the profile emerging from the ISM/CGM affects the fraction of Ly
\mathrm{\alpha}
transmitted through the IGM
(
Dijkstra et al. 2007
;
Dijkstra et al. 2011
;
Mason et al. 2018a
;
Yuan et al. 2024
, e.g.,)
, but can also reveal the impact of the IGM at
z\,\hbox to0.0pt{\lower 3.5pt\hbox{$\mathchar 0\sim$}\hss}\raise 1.0pt\hbox{$>$}\,6
as the IGM damping wing should produce smooth attenuation as a function of wavelength
(
Miralda-Escude 1998
)
, making profiles more symmetric at higher redshifts. To test this, we explore the redshift evolution of asymmetry splitting our samples in two bins. We find the asymmetric fraction is 72
\pm
6
\%
between
z=5.2-5.8
, and 60
\pm
9
\%
at
z=5.8-6.4
. With the large uncertainties, we cannot conclude if profiles become more symmetric at
z>5.8
, with a stronger impact of the IGM damping wing. Larger samples of Ly
\mathrm{\alpha}
line profiles would provide more insight into this effect.

4.5
Ly
\mathrm{\alpha}
escape fraction

Ly
\mathrm{\alpha}
and H
\alpha
are both emitted predominantly in photoionized nebulae in a series of hydrogen line transitions, or recombination cascades, therefore linking the production of both types of photons. The ratio of products of the recombination cascade depends on the electron temperature (T
e
) and electron number density (n
e
), but most importantly the optical depth of the medium
(
Dijkstra 2014
)
. In this work, we assume a Case-B recombination scenario, where the surrounding medium is optical thick to the Lyman series, as expected of HII regions, with n
e
= 250cm
-3
, T
e
= 10
4

(
Dijkstra 2014
)
. This yields an intrinsic Ly
\mathrm{\alpha}
flux of
8.7\times
the dust-corrected H
\alpha
flux.

The Ly
\mathrm{\alpha}
escape fraction is the ratio of the observed to intrinsic Ly
\mathrm{\alpha}
flux:

\quad\quad\quad\quad\quad\quad f_{\mathrm{esc,ly\alpha}}=\frac{F_{\mathrm{Ly}\alpha}}{8.7F_{\text{dust corrected,}{\mathrm{H}\alpha}}}

(7)

With both terms referring to the total flux of the emission lines (observed in the case of Ly
\mathrm{\alpha}
, dust-corrected in the case of H
\alpha
), and the 8.7 is the factor derived from case-B recombination. Recovering
{f_{\textrm{esc}}^{\textrm{Ly}\alpha}}<1
implies not all Ly
\mathrm{\alpha}
escapes from the galaxy, due to dust absorption or scattering by high column densities of neutral hydrogen, effectively removing Ly
\mathrm{\alpha}
from the line-of-sight.

We correct all H
\alpha
fluxes for dust attenuation following
Lam et al. 2019
. We assume an SMC (Small Magellanic Cloud) dust curve
(
Prevot et al. 1984
)
, which is expected to be similar to that in
z\sim 4-6
galaxies based on the infrared excess (IRx) -
\beta
slope relationship
(
Bouwens et al. 2016
)
. The dust correction increases H
\alpha
fluxes by
\sim 1-10\%
. We propagate the uncertainty of
\beta
into the dust correction, though we find the error bars of
f_{\textrm{esc}}^{\textrm{Ly}\alpha}
are dominated by the uncertainty of the line fluxes in all cases.

5
An empirical model for emergent Ly
\mathrm{\alpha}

We now seek to build an empirical model to predict emergent Ly
\mathrm{\alpha}
properties based on easily observable galaxy properties, M
{}_{\textsc{uv}}
and UV slope
\beta
. In the following section we describe the approach we used to produce model distributions for both Ly
\mathrm{\alpha}
EW and
f_{\textrm{esc}}^{\textrm{Ly}\alpha}
.

To estimate the Ly
\mathrm{\alpha}
EW and
f_{\textrm{esc}}^{\textrm{Ly}\alpha}
distributions we use a Bayesian approach
(
Treu et al. 2012
;
Schenker et al. 2014
;
Oyarzún et al. 2017
;
Mason et al. 2018b
;
Tang et al. 2024a
, e.g.,)
which allows to obtain the posterior distribution of the parameters that describe the EW
Lyα
and
f_{\textrm{esc}}^{\textrm{Ly}\alpha}
distributions.
We follow
Mason et al. 2018b
and assume the distributions for EW and
f_{\textrm{esc}}^{\textrm{Ly}\alpha}
are described by an exponential with a potential peak at zero for non-emitters, with two parameters
\theta=(A,X_{0})
. We note that previous work has shown the exact form of the distributions (exponential, log-normal etc.) does not significantly impact results
(
Schenker et al. 2014
;
Oyarzún et al. 2017
;
Tang et al. 2024a
)
. In the following we use
X
to denote the ‘data’ EW or
f_{\textrm{esc}}^{\textrm{Ly}\alpha}
as we model both distributions in the same way. The model distribution is:

p(X|\theta=\{A,X_{0}\})=B\frac{A}{X_{0}}e^{-X/X_{0}}\mathrm{H}(X)+(1-A)\delta(X)

(8)

The parameter
X_{0}
represents the scale length of the exponential, and
A
is a normalization term which represents the fraction of Ly
\mathrm{\alpha}
emitters (i.e. the fraction of galaxies which have EW or
f_{\textrm{esc}}^{\textrm{Ly}\alpha}

>0
).
B
is a normalization term for the distribution given the limits of EW and
f_{\textrm{esc}}^{\textrm{Ly}\alpha}
(
B=1
for EW, and
B=1/(1-\exp(-1/X_{0}))
for
f_{\textrm{esc}}^{\textrm{Ly}\alpha}
).
H(X)
is the Heaviside function and
\delta(X)
is the Dirac-delta function.

We assume Gaussian measurement uncertainties with standard deviation
\sigma
on our data
X_{\mathrm{obs}}
for each galaxy such that:

p(X_{\mathrm{obs}}|X)=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi}\sigma}\exp\left[{-\frac{(X-X_{\mathrm{obs}})^{2}}{2\sigma^{2}}}\right]

(9)

Thus the likelihood for an individual observation is:

\quad\quad\quad p(X_{\mathrm{obs}}|\theta)=\int dX\,p(X_{\mathrm{obs}}|X)p(X|\theta)

(10)

Where the limits of integration are [
0,\infty
] for EW
Lyα
and [0,1] for
f_{\textrm{esc}}^{\textrm{Ly}\alpha}
.

Convolving our likelihood with the Gaussian distribution and solving the integral, we obtain the likelihood for our observations:

\begin{split}p(X_{\mathrm{obs}}|\theta)&=B\frac{A}{2X_{0}}e^{\frac{\sigma^{2}-2X_{0}X_{\mathrm{obs}}}{2X_{0}^{2}}}\left[\mathrm{erf}\left(Z+\frac{X_{\mathrm{max}}}{\sqrt{2}\sigma}\right)-\mathrm{erf}(Z)\right]\\
&+\frac{(1-A)}{\sqrt{2\pi}\sigma}e^{-\frac{X_{\mathrm{obs}}^{2}}{2\sigma^{2}}}.\\
\mathrm{Where}\;Z&=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}\sigma}\left(\frac{\sigma^{2}}{X_{0}}-X_{\mathrm{obs}}\right)\end{split}

(11)

Where
erf
is the error function, and
X_{\mathrm{max}}=\infty
for EW and 1 for
f_{\textrm{esc}}^{\textrm{Ly}\alpha}
.

For galaxies with Ly
\mathrm{\alpha}
non-detections (i.e. upper limits in EW
Lyα
and
f_{\textrm{esc}}^{\textrm{Ly}\alpha}
) the likelihood is:

p(X_{i,\mathrm{obs}}|\theta)=p(X_{i,\mathrm{obs}}<X_{\mathrm{uplim}}|\theta)+p(X_{i,\mathrm{obs}}>X_{\mathrm{uplim}}|\theta)\cdot(1-C)

(12)

Where the cumulative distribution function is obtained by integrating
p(X_{i,\mathrm{obs}}|\theta)
(Equation
10
) from
x_{i,\mathrm{obs}}=-\infty
to
x_{\mathrm{uplim}}
.We incorporate the second term using the methods from
Schenker et al. 2014
, accounting for the probability of an emission line above the 5
\sigma
flux limit being undetected due to a skyline. The completeness (
C
) is calculated for individual slits over
\Delta\mathrm{z_{phot}}
= 0.5. If H
\alpha
or [O
iii
] are available, we determine completeness for z
sys
within a range of typical
\Delta v_{\mathrm{Ly}\alpha}
from 0 to 500  km s
-1
.

For galaxies with no H
\alpha
detection we have only a lower limit on
f_{\textrm{esc}}^{\textrm{Ly}\alpha}
, thus in those cases the likelihood is:

p(X_{i,\mathrm{obs}}|\theta)=p(X_{i,\mathrm{obs}}>X_{\mathrm{lowlim}}|\theta)

(13)

Where we integrate
p(X_{i,\mathrm{obs}}|\theta)
from
X_{\mathrm{lowlim}}
to
1
.

The final posterior is the product of all individual galaxy posteriors,
p(\theta|\{X_{\mathrm{obs}}\}\propto p(\theta)\prod_{i}p(X_{i,\mathrm{obs}}|\theta)
. We assume flat priors on A and X
0
:
A\in[0,1]
,
W_{0}\in[0,500]
and
f^{\mathrm{Ly}\alpha}_{\mathrm{esc,0}}\in[0,1]
.

5.1
Dependence of the distributions on galaxy properties

To better understand the dependence of Ly
\mathrm{\alpha}
emission on galaxy properties we parameterise our model as a function of M
{}_{\textsc{uv}}
and
\beta
. Previous work, and our results in Section
4
, have demonstrated that Ly
\mathrm{\alpha}
EW and escape may be enhanced in UV-faint, bluer, galaxies
(
Tang et al. 2024a
;
Oyarzún et al. 2017
, e.g.,)
.

We follow the approach done by
Oyarzún et al. 2017
and parameterise our model parameters
A
and
X
0
as a linear combination of M
{}_{\textsc{uv}}
and
\beta
.

\begin{split}\qquad\qquad\qquad A&=A_{{M_{\textsc{uv}}}}{M_{\textsc{uv}}}+A_{\beta}\beta+A_{c}\\
\qquad\qquad\qquad X_{0}&=X_{{M_{\textsc{uv}}}}{M_{\textsc{uv}}}+X_{\beta}\beta+X_{c}\end{split}

(14)

To compute the posterior distributions, we follow the same method as before, assuming the same priors on
A
and
X_{0}
.

We perform tests to mock datasets to assess whether our sample size is sufficient to robustly recover these parameters. We find that our Ly
\mathrm{\alpha}
EW sample (236 galaxies, including 62 Ly
\mathrm{\alpha}
detections) is large enough that we expect to recover the parameters in Equation
14
within 5-30% of the
true
parameters.
We find our
f_{\textrm{esc}}^{\textrm{Ly}\alpha}
sample (72 galaxies, including 10 Ly
\mathrm{\alpha}
+H
\alpha
detections) is not large enough to fit the linear model robustly, so we assume constant
A
and
X_{0}
, and find we can recover these robustly when splitting our sample into two bins. Both
A
and
X_{0}
are recovered with similar accuracy and precision in our tests.

f_{\textrm{esc}}^{\textrm{Ly}\alpha}
is much higher in our fainter M
{}_{\textsc{uv}}
bin. We find median
{f_{\textrm{esc}}^{\textrm{Ly}\alpha}}=0.13\pm 0.05
in our UV-bright bin compared to 0.28
\pm 0.08
in our UV-faint bin.
From our UV-faint bin we obtain that 62
\pm 8\%
of M
{}_{\textsc{uv}}
¿-20.1 galaxies should have
f_{\textrm{esc}}^{\textrm{Ly}\alpha}

>0.2
. This is considerably higher than the 30
\pm 6\%
found by
Tang et al. 2024a
for M
{}_{\textsc{uv}}

\sim-19.5
galaxies. We attribute this difference to our high upper limits on
f_{\textrm{esc}}^{\textrm{Ly}\alpha}
which do not significantly constrain our models and the use of a uniform prior on
f_{\textrm{esc}}^{\textrm{Ly}\alpha}
. If we decrease our Ly
\mathrm{\alpha}
flux limits by a factor 2(4), we obtain 50
\pm 12\%
(35
\pm 10\%
) of M
{}_{\textsc{uv}}
¿-20.1 galaxies have
f_{\textrm{esc}}^{\textrm{Ly}\alpha}

>0.2
, demonstrating the sensitivity of the inference to upper limits. Thus we consider our reported distribution an upper limit on the underlying distribution.

We do not find significant difference between the
f_{\textrm{esc}}^{\textrm{Ly}\alpha}
distributions binned by UV slope. Previous works at z
\sim
5-6
(
Tang et al. 2024a
;
Chen et al. 2024
)
have shown that
f_{\textrm{esc}}^{\textrm{Ly}\alpha}
strongly correlates with UV slope. However, similar to z
\sim
2 Ly
\mathrm{\alpha}
surveys
(
Naidu et al. 2022
, i.e.)
, we do not see this correlation in our sample (see also Figure
8
). The lack of trends with UV slope might also be driven by our target selection which is incomplete for blue (
\beta\,\hbox to0.0pt{\lower 3.5pt\hbox{$\mathchar 0\sim$}\hss}\raise 1.0pt\hbox{$<$}\,-2
) M
{}_{\textsc{uv}}

<
-19.5 galaxies (see discussion in Section
6.1
).

6
Discussion

In this paper we have presented new measurements and models of Ly
\mathrm{\alpha}
for Lyman-Break selected galaxies at
z\sim 5-6
. In Sections
4
and
5
we have connected the shape and strength of the Ly
\mathrm{\alpha}
line with other physical properties, such as M
{}_{\textsc{uv}}
. In the following discussion, we explore the implications of our findings for predicting Ly
\mathrm{\alpha}
emission emerging from the ISM/CGM (Section
6.1
), as well as the impact of this transmission on its observability during the epoch of reionization (Section
6.2
),.

6.1
Predicting Ly
\mathrm{\alpha}
transmission from the ISM/CGM at
z\sim 5-6

The main goal of this paper was to build a predictive model for Ly
\mathrm{\alpha}
at the end of reionization, providing a basis to better interpret Ly
\mathrm{\alpha}
observations at
z>6
. These baseline observations are important due to the degeneracy between scattering of Ly
\mathrm{\alpha}
by neutral hydrogen in the IGM and ISM/CGM during the EoR. In particular, we focused on Ly
\mathrm{\alpha}
line shape and strength properties, including FWHM
Lyα
,
\Delta v_{\mathrm{Ly}\alpha}
, EW
Lyα
and
f_{\textrm{esc}}^{\textrm{Ly}\alpha}
. In this section we discuss their trends with physical galaxy properties, M
{}_{\textsc{uv}}
, UV slope. Finally, we explore the possible physical drivers behind these trends and how they can be used to better predict Ly
\mathrm{\alpha}
at
z>6
.

In Section
4.3
we showed that Ly
\mathrm{\alpha}
lineshape properties:
\Delta v_{\mathrm{Ly}\alpha}
and FWHM
Lyα
, have tentative correlations with M
{}_{\textsc{uv}}
. In Figure
7
, combining our
\Delta v_{\mathrm{Ly}\alpha}
results with other samples from the literature works at
z>3
, we see a strong correlation of increasing
\Delta v_{\mathrm{Ly}\alpha}
with higher UV luminosity.
We also found a mild correlation of increased FWHM
Lyα
in UV bright galaxies. It has been shown in theoretical works
(
Neufeld 1991
;
Verhamme et al. 2006
, e.g.)
that broader Ly
\mathrm{\alpha}
profiles and higher
\Delta v_{\mathrm{Ly}\alpha}
are byproducts of scattering in high column density HI gas, increasing Ly
\mathrm{\alpha}
resonant scattering events. The trends we find of increasing
\Delta v_{\mathrm{Ly}\alpha}
and FWHM
Lyα
with UV luminosity are thus consistent with the physical picture that UV bright galaxies generally trace more massive and spatially extended galaxies, favoring higher HI column densities.
This scenario is supported by observations showing a strong correlation between galaxy size and UV luminosity
(
Shibuya et al. 2015
;
Morishita et al. 2024
, e.g.,)
. Furthermore, results from hydrodynamical simulations predict high HI column densities are more prevalent in UV bright galaxies, where supernova feedback is less efficient in disrupting the neutral ISM and opening channels of low HI column density
(e.g.,
Rosdahl et al. 2022
, Gelli et al. in prep, albeit with large sightline variance)
.

A key goal of this work was to better understand what shapes Ly
\mathrm{\alpha}
line profiles at
z\sim 5-6
, to help predict emergent Ly
\mathrm{\alpha}
during the EoR at
z>6
, as pre-JWST reionization inferences have primarily been based on line profiles from
z\sim 2
galaxies
(
Mason et al. 2018b
, e.g.,)
.
Recently,
Tang et al. 2024a
demonstrated the median Ly
\mathrm{\alpha}
line profile at
z\sim 5-6
is shifted to higher velocity offsets relative to a
z\sim 2
comparison sample with similar Ly
\mathrm{\alpha}
EW
(
Tang et al. 2024c
)
, and discussed this may reflect enhanced scattering in the ISM/CGM at these redshifts and/or resonant scattering by infalling residual neutral gas in the IGM with mean neutral fraction
\overline{x}_{\mathrm{HI}}\,\hbox to0.0pt{\lower 3.5pt\hbox{$\mathchar 0\sim$}\hss}\raise 1.0pt\hbox{$>$}\,10^{-4}

(
Dijkstra et al. 2007
;
Laursen et al. 2011
)
.
Resonant scattering by infalling IGM gas should produce a sharp cut-off in the line profile, at the velocity of the infalling gas, resulting in observed Ly
\mathrm{\alpha}
lines which are narrower and with more redshifted peaks compared to lines transmitted through the highly ionized IGM
(
Santos 2004
;
Mason et al. 2018b
;
Park et al. 2021
;
Tang et al. 2024c
)
.
We find that
\approx 70\%
of Ly
\mathrm{\alpha}
lines in our
z\sim 5-6
sample are highly asymmetric
(
Pentericci et al. 2018
;
Hayes et al. 2021
, see also,)
, and our median FWHM
Lyα
=245  km s
-1
is lower than that of the
z\sim 2-3
sample presented by
Tang et al. 2024c
(=290 km/s), which were selected as reionization-era analogs, with [O
iii
] +H
\beta
EW comparable to
z\,\hbox to0.0pt{\lower 3.5pt\hbox{$\mathchar 0\sim$}\hss}\raise 1.0pt\hbox{$>$}\,6
galaxies. These observations are qualitatively consistent with the predicted sharp flux cut-off due to resonant scattering by the IGM.

However, in Section
4.3
we also demonstrated our
z\sim 5-6
FWHM
Lyα
distribution peaks at higher values than the model predictions by
Mason et al. 2018b
;
Mason et al. 2019
, which were based on
z\sim 2
Lyman-break galaxies
(
Erb et al. 2014
;
Steidel et al. 2014
)
. We also found the majority of our velocity offset measurements exceed this model (also seen among other
z>5
sources in the literature).
In the
Mason et al. 2018b
model, Ly
\mathrm{\alpha}
velocity offsets and FWHM
Lyα
are empirically estimated to scale with halo mass,
\sim M_{h}^{1/3}
, with redshift evolution incorporated by a shift in star formation to lower mass halos at fixed M
{}_{\textsc{uv}}
at higher redshifts
(
Behroozi et al. 2013
;
Mason et al. 2015
, due to more rapidly rising star formation histories, e.g.,)
.
One possible factor missing from the
Mason et al. 2018b
model is cosmological density evolution, which would increase the HI column density in the ISM/CGM at fixed halo mass with increasing redshift.
This would be expected to produce a redshift scaling of both
\Delta v_{\mathrm{Ly}\alpha}
and FWHM
Lyα

\sim(1+z)^{2/3}
at fixed halo mass, implying
\sim 1.6\times
higher
\Delta v_{\mathrm{Ly}\alpha}
and FWHM
Lyα
at
z\sim 5
compared to the original model.
Such a scaling appears in good agreement with the ratio between our median observed line profile and the original model predictions: median observed
\Delta v_{\mathrm{Ly}\alpha}
=258(FWHM
Lyα
=245)  km s
-1
, compared to
\Delta v_{\mathrm{Ly}\alpha}
=150(FWHM
Lyα
=160) km s
-1
predicted by the
Mason et al. 2018b
model.
Current samples of sources with both Ly
\mathrm{\alpha}
velocity offsets and FWHM
Lyα
measurements at
z\sim 5-6
are still small, but larger samples will be important for better determining the impact of IGM infall relative to ISM/CGM density evolution on Ly
\mathrm{\alpha}
line profiles at these redshifts.

In Section
5.2
, we showed that EW
Lyα
and
f_{\textrm{esc}}^{\textrm{Ly}\alpha}
generally increase with decreasing UV luminosity. Additionally, we identified a strong correlation between
f_{\textrm{esc}}^{\textrm{Ly}\alpha}
and EW
Lyα
. We also showed find that
f_{\textrm{esc}}^{\textrm{Ly}\alpha}
generally increases as Ly
\mathrm{\alpha}
lines become narrower. These findings are consistent with previous studies, where EW
Lyα
and
f_{\textrm{esc}}^{\textrm{Ly}\alpha}
have an enhanced probability of being stronger in galaxies that are UV faint and bluer
(
Oyarzún et al. 2017
;
Tang et al. 2024a
)
. However we find no strong trends with UV slope in our sample, as we discuss below.
As discussed by
Tang et al. 2024a
, the strong correlation of EW
Lyα
and
f_{\textrm{esc}}^{\textrm{Ly}\alpha}
suggests that strong EW
Lyα
requires an environment capable of high
f_{\textrm{esc}}^{\textrm{Ly}\alpha}
. This is consistent with the scenario discussed above, where reduced resonant scattering events are more common in UV faint galaxies, leading to lower probability of Ly
\mathrm{\alpha}
being destroyed by dust or scattered out the line of sight
(
Dijkstra 2014
)
, and therefore increased
f_{\textrm{esc}}^{\textrm{Ly}\alpha}
. The link between Ly
\mathrm{\alpha}
strength and the properties of the ISM/CGM suggest that EW
Lyα
and
f_{\textrm{esc}}^{\textrm{Ly}\alpha}
can be predicted statistically using non-Ly
\mathrm{\alpha}
observables. This is supported by previous work at low redshifts linking Ly
\mathrm{\alpha}
emission to rest-frame optical properties
(
Trainor et al. 2019
;
Hayes et al. 2023
, e.g.,)
, and at
z>6
by our results, which, consistent with other recent studies using JWST observations of Ly
\mathrm{\alpha}
-emitting galaxies at
z\sim 5-6

(
Tang et al. 2024a
;
Chen et al. 2024
;
Lin et al. 2024
)
, show that Ly
\mathrm{\alpha}
strength can be predicted statistically from UV observables at these redshifts. Empirical baselines mapping from UV observables to Ly
\mathrm{\alpha}
are crucial for interpreting Ly
\mathrm{\alpha}
detections in
z>10
spectra, where we lose rest-frame optical wavelength coverage with NIRSpec.

Our measurements in Figures
5
and
8
show no clear trends of EW
Lyα
and
f_{\textrm{esc}}^{\textrm{Ly}\alpha}
with UV slope. The same is true of our EW
Lyα
and
f_{\textrm{esc}}^{\textrm{Ly}\alpha}
empirical models in Section
5.2
and
5.3
: we find no significant dependencies of the two models on UV slope. Nonetheless, previous works have found that Ly
\mathrm{\alpha}
is strongly enhanced in galaxies with bluer UV continuum
(
Oyarzún et al. 2017
;
Tang et al. 2024a
;
Lin et al. 2024
)
. We attribute this missing trend in our sample to our target selection, magF160W
<
27.5 (see Section
2.1
) which results in a lack of M
{}_{\textsc{uv}}

>-19.5
, blue (
\beta<
-2) galaxies. We tested this by comparing our sample in M
{}_{\textsc{uv}}
and UV slope against the JADES-DJA photometric catalogs. We find that imposing a magF160W
<
27.5 cut for galaxies within
5<z_{\mathrm{phot}}<6.5
in the JADES-DJA catalog biases against blue (
\beta<
-2) UV slopes for M
{}_{\textsc{uv}}

\gtrsim-19.5
galaxies. At M
{}_{\textsc{uv}}

>-19.5
we find median
\beta
=-1.6 in our data, while the full JADES-DJA sample finds
\beta
=-2.2. If we apply the magF160W ¡ 27.5 cut to JADES-DJA, the median UV slope also becomes
\beta
=-1.6. As the faintest, bluest galaxies typically have strongest Ly
\mathrm{\alpha}
emission
(
Oyarzún et al. 2017
;
Tang et al. 2024a
;
Lin et al. 2024
)
we attribute our lack of trends of EW
Lyα
and
f_{\textrm{esc}}^{\textrm{Ly}\alpha}
with UV slope to our sample incompleteness. However, omitting sources with M
{}_{\textsc{uv}}

>-19.5
from our EW
Lyα
and
f_{\textrm{esc}}^{\textrm{Ly}\alpha}
models does not significantly impact our conclusions.

6.2
Implications for Ly
\mathrm{\alpha}
observability during reionization

Our primary goal was to better understand the typical emergent Ly
\mathrm{\alpha}
emission as it leaves the ISM/CGM in high-redshift galaxies. As the effects of HI in the IGM and ISM/CGM on Ly
\mathrm{\alpha}
are entangled during the EoR, we must separate the emergence of Ly
\mathrm{\alpha}
from galaxies and its absorption caused by the damping wing of the IGM
(
Miralda-Escude 1998
;
Dijkstra et al. 2007
;
Dijkstra et al. 2011
;
Mason et al. 2018b
)
. With this goal, we have produced baseline measurements with observations at
z\sim 5-6
. Our observations at the end of the EoR, allow us to isolate the effects of the ISM/CGM over Ly
\mathrm{\alpha}
, and to better infer the reionization timeline in the future. In particular, with
R\sim 4000
resolution and observations of up to 15 hours, we have made the first measurement of the FWHM
Lyα
distribution at
z\sim 5-6
, previously limited to bright sources and stacked profiles with lower resolution
(
Pentericci et al. 2018
;
Hayes et al. 2021
, e.g.,)
.

The Ly
\mathrm{\alpha}
lineshape plays a key role in the Ly
\mathrm{\alpha}
detection rate once the IGM becomes increasingly neutral at
z\,\hbox to0.0pt{\lower 3.5pt\hbox{$\mathchar 0\sim$}\hss}\raise 1.0pt\hbox{$>$}\,6
, as the damping wing profile of the IGM is strongly wavelength dependent
(
Miralda-Escude 1998
;
Mesinger et al. 2015
)
. Therefore, we must understand to what extent the Ly
\mathrm{\alpha}
transmission drop past
z\,\hbox to0.0pt{\lower 3.5pt\hbox{$\mathchar 0\sim$}\hss}\raise 1.0pt\hbox{$>$}\,6
is purely due to the IGM damping wing, or to the emergent line properties of Ly
\mathrm{\alpha}
. The FWHM
Lyα
has two opposing effects on the observability of Ly
\mathrm{\alpha}
: First, a broader line profile will more easily transmit through the IGM as it increases the fraction of flux emitted at redder wavelengths, where it experiences lower damping wing optical depths
(
Prieto-Lyon et al. 2023b
;
Mukherjee et al. 2024
;
Yuan et al. 2024
)
. However, a broader profile will lead to the same flux being spread over a wider wavelength range, hindering its detection in flux-limited observations. Increased
\Delta v_{\mathrm{Ly}\alpha}
will always boost Ly
\mathrm{\alpha}
transmission through the IGM by shifting Ly
\mathrm{\alpha}
to redder wavelengths with lower damping wing optical depths.

To assess the impact of the lineshape on reionization analyses we compare our lineshape with the assumptions by
Mason et al. 2018b
;
Mason et al. 2019
in their neutral fraction inference.
We compare the IGM Ly
\mathrm{\alpha}
transmission for an
{M_{\textsc{uv}}}=-20
galaxy at
z=9
: comparing our median line profile and the model by
Mason et al. 2018b
.
We assume a completely neutral IGM, in a region without ionized bubbles
(
Dijkstra 2014
, using the damping wing approximation by)
.
The transmitted Ly
\mathrm{\alpha}
using our line shape median values (Gaussian, with FWHM
Lyα
= 245 km s
-1
,
\Delta v_{\mathrm{Ly}\alpha}
= 258 km s
-1
) is
9\times
higher than that predicted by
Mason et al. 2019
(Gaussian FWHM
Lyα
= 160 km s
-1

\Delta v_{\mathrm{Ly}\alpha}
= 150 km s
-1
, and truncated at 80 km/s due to infalling IGM) – finding 0.9
\%
vs 0.1
\%
of Ly
\mathrm{\alpha}
flux is transmitted through the IGM respectively. We note that for bubbles
R\,\hbox to0.0pt{\lower 3.5pt\hbox{$\mathchar 0\sim$}\hss}\raise 1.0pt\hbox{$>$}\,1
pMpc
(
Lu et al. 2023
, roughly the expected mean bubble size when the IGM is
\sim 70
% neutral,)
the difference in transmission becomes negligible, implying the lineshape plays an important role in Ly
\mathrm{\alpha}
visibility in the earliest stages of reionization.

We also examine how the strongly asymmetric shape of Ly
\mathrm{\alpha}
should facilitate Ly
\mathrm{\alpha}
transmission in a mostly neutral IGM. For the 14 sources in our sample with measured velocity offsets we calculate the fraction of flux at
{\Delta v_{\mathrm{Ly}\alpha}}>400
km/s, which can be transmitted even in a neutral IGM at
z\sim 13

(
Dijkstra et al. 2011
;
Mason et al. 2018a
;
Yuan et al. 2024
)
. We obtain a median and standard deviation of 22
\pm 18\%
from this sample, agreeing closely with recent results from hydrodynamical radiative transfer simulations by
Yuan et al. 2024
. This suggests the red tail of Ly
\mathrm{\alpha}
emission be important for allowing Ly
\mathrm{\alpha}
to be visible even at extremely high redshifts
(
Witstok et al. 2025a
, e.g.,)
.

The higher transmission for Ly
\mathrm{\alpha}
which is both more offset from systemic and has extended red wings implies Ly
\mathrm{\alpha}
can be visible even in a very neutral IGM
(
Dijkstra et al. 2011
;
Witstok et al. 2025b
)
. Our results suggest we may require a higher neutral fraction at
z\,\hbox to0.0pt{\lower 3.5pt\hbox{$\mathchar 0\sim$}\hss}\raise 1.0pt\hbox{$>$}\,8
to explain the Ly
\mathrm{\alpha}
decline compared to analyses which use
z\sim 2
lineshapes as a baseline. This highlights the importance of including
z\sim 5-6
samples, such as those used here, when making reionization inferences
(
Yuan et al. 2024
, see also,)
. For instance,
Tang et al. 2024b
recently adopted the
z\sim 5-6
Ly
\mathrm{\alpha}
line profile from
Tang et al. 2024a
as a baseline when constraining the IGM neutral fraction at
z\sim 6-13
from JWST observations.

7
Conclusions

A detailed understanding of Ly
\mathrm{\alpha}
as it emerges from galaxies in the first billion years is critical for interpreting Ly
\mathrm{\alpha}
observations during the reionization era. Our main goal was to produce empirical baseline models for Ly
\mathrm{\alpha}
to enable better constraints on the reionization history. We have presented an analysis of Lyman-break galaxies at
z\sim 5-6
, when the IGM is expected to be mostly ionized. With our new ground-based Ly
\mathrm{\alpha}
spectra, supplemented by JWST imaging and spectra, we have studied the correlations between Ly
\mathrm{\alpha}
shape and strength with UV luminosity. We conclude the following:

1.

We present high-resolution MMT/Binospec restframe-UV spectroscopy for 236 Lyman-break galaxies. We detect Ly
\mathrm{\alpha}
at S/N
>5
for 62 of these, with a median
5\sigma
flux limit of 1.34
\times 10^{-17}
erg s
-1
cm
-2
. Overlapping ancilliary JWST/NIRCam spectra from FRESCO allows us to measure z
sys
for 56 of our targets, including 14 galaxies detected with Ly
\mathrm{\alpha}
.

2.

With
R\sim 4600
spectroscopy we measure Ly
\mathrm{\alpha}
line profiles with a high-resolution of
\sim 70
km s
-1
. We measured for the first time the FWHM
Lyα
distribution at
z\sim 5-6
, a key unknown quantity in interpreting the decline of Ly
\mathrm{\alpha}
at
z>6
. We find mean FWHM
Lyα
=245 km s
-1
, higher than models based on
z\sim 2
Ly
\mathrm{\alpha}
observations
(
Mason et al. 2018b
;
Mason et al. 2019
)
, but lower than
z\sim 2-3
observations of reionization-era analogs by
Tang et al. 2024c
. We discussed that this may be due to resonant scattering by infalling IGM with
\overline{x}_{\mathrm{HI}}\,\hbox to0.0pt{\lower 3.5pt\hbox{$\mathchar 0\sim$}\hss}\raise 1.0pt\hbox{$>$}\,10^{-4}
at
z\sim 5-6
, as well as increasing ISM/CGM densities at higher redshifts at fixed halo mass – the latter an effect which was not included in the
Mason et al. 2018b
model.

3.

We obtain 14 new measurements of Ly
\mathrm{\alpha}
velocity offsets at
z>5
through systemic redshifts from H
\alpha
or [O
iii
] from FRESCO NIRCam slitless spectra. We reported correlations between physical properties and Ly
\mathrm{\alpha}
lineshape:
\Delta v_{\mathrm{Ly}\alpha}
and FWHM
Lyα
. We find that the majority of galaxies show
{\Delta v_{\mathrm{Ly}\alpha}}\,\hbox to0.0pt{\lower 3.5pt\hbox{$\mathchar 0\sim$}\hss}\raise 1.0pt\hbox{$>$}\,100
km s
-1
, with a median 258 km s
-1
. Combined with
z>3
data from the literature, we find a strong correlation for increased
\Delta v_{\mathrm{Ly}\alpha}
in UV bright galaxies. We found mild correlations of higher FWHM
Lyα
in galaxies that are UV-bright. Our lineshape trends are consistent with a scenario of higher Ly
\mathrm{\alpha}
optical depth, with numerous scattering events in the ISM/CGM, for increasingly UV bright galaxies.

4.

We measured the line strength properties EW
Lyα
and
f_{\textrm{esc}}^{\textrm{Ly}\alpha}
. We found strong correlations of EW
Lyα
and
f_{\textrm{esc}}^{\textrm{Ly}\alpha}
with increasing UV luminosity, and mild correlations of increasing
f_{\textrm{esc}}^{\textrm{Ly}\alpha}
with lower FWHM
Lyα
. The decline of
f_{\textrm{esc}}^{\textrm{Ly}\alpha}
with line broadness and UV luminosity is consistent with a scenario of higher Ly
\mathrm{\alpha}
optical depth in UV bright galaxies.

5.

We created an empirical model for the probability distribution of EW
Lyα
and
f_{\textrm{esc}}^{\textrm{Ly}\alpha}
at the end of the EoR, as a function of M
{}_{\textsc{uv}}
and UV slope. Our EW
Lyα
and
f_{\textrm{esc}}^{\textrm{Ly}\alpha}
models strongly depend on M
{}_{\textsc{uv}}
. We find strong EW
Lyα
(
>25
Å) and
f_{\textrm{esc}}^{\textrm{Ly}\alpha}
(¿0.2) are common at
z\sim 5-6
: Our models predict that 45
\pm
5% and
<62\pm 8
% of M
{}_{\textsc{uv}}
=-19.5 galaxies have EW
Lyα

>25
Å and
f_{\textrm{esc}}^{\textrm{Ly}\alpha}

>0.2
.

The NIR capabilities of JWST are now allowing us to unlock new information about emergent Ly
\mathrm{\alpha}
line profiles and escape fractions through rest-frame optical emission lines. As
R\,\hbox to0.0pt{\lower 3.5pt\hbox{$\mathchar 0\sim$}\hss}\raise 1.0pt\hbox{$>$}\,1000
optical line samples at
z\sim 5-9
grow steadily by the thousands, in large part thanks to NIRCam grism surveys
(
Kashino et al. 2023
;
Naidu et al. 2024
;
Covelo-Paz et al. 2025
;
Meyer et al. 2024
, e.g.)
, we can increasingly refine our understanding of emergent Ly
\mathrm{\alpha}
at
z\sim 5-6
. The unprecedented detections of Ly
\mathrm{\alpha}
at
z\sim 10-13

(
Bunker et al. 2023
;
Witstok et al. 2025b
)
have opened a new window on studying the earliest stages of reionization. Baseline Ly
\mathrm{\alpha}
empirical correlations and models, such as those we have presented
(
Chen et al. 2024
;
Tang et al. 2024a
, see also)
, are crucial for interpreting the growing JWST observations of Ly
\mathrm{\alpha}
at
z\sim 6-13
to constrain the reionization process
(
Tang et al. 2024b
;
Mason et al. 2025
)
.
