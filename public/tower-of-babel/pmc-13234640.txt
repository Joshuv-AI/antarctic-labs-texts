Metal-Free Induced Tandem Cyclization of 2‑Aryl‑N‑Methacryloyl Indoles via 1,2-Hydrogen Atom Transfer
Authors: Peng Chen, Wanting Peng, Xingqin Tian, Shuying Li, Fang Ye, Guanghui Lv
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
This article reports a photoredox-catalyzed tandem cyclization of 2-aryl-N-methacryloyl indoles via 1,2-hydrogen atom transfer (1,2-HAT) to rapidly synthesize indolo­[2,1-a]­isoquinoline derivatives under mild conditions. This method affords a variety of indolo­[2,1-a]­isoquinoline compounds in good to excellent yields. In contrast to previous reports that relied on transition-metal catalysis or harsh reaction conditions, this strategy offers multiple advantages, including mild and metal-free reaction conditions, a radical tandem reaction mechanism involving a 1,2-HAT process, a wide range of compatible substrates, and excellent tolerance toward functional groups.

Introduction

Nitrogen-containing heterocycles, particularly fused-indole derivatives, serve as key structural motifs in a wide array of pharmaceuticals, organic functional materials, and bioactive natural products. Among them, indolo­[2,1-a]­isoquinoline derivatives possess a distinctive tetracyclic core architecture (Figure ) and exhibit a wide range of biological activities. Given the great potential of indolo­[2,1-a]­isoquinoline derivatives in drug and material chemistry, considerable effort has been devoted to synthesizing this unique heterocyclic framework through various methods. Traditional synthetic methods for constructing indole/benzimidazole-isoquinoline fused polycyclic compounds have relied on condensation reactions. However, all these classic methods have limitations such as the difficulty in obtaining starting materials, harsh reaction conditions and inconvenient practical operation. Over the past few decades, researchers have established several methods to construct the core structure of indole/benzimidazole [2,1-a]­isoquinoline, such as rhodium-catalyzed oxidative coupling and C–H activation, − palladium-catalyzed cross-coupling reactions involving C–H and C-X bonds, and dehydration coupling of tertiary amines and ketones, etc. In 2018, Song reported a novel [4 + 2] cyclization reaction, in the presence of a rhodium catalyst, to construct benzimidazole [2,1-a]­isoquinoline compounds from 2-arylimidazole and α -diazone esters. Unfortunately, this reaction employed potentially explosive diazo compounds and expensive rhodium catalysts. Over the past decade, radical tandem reactions have been extensively employed in the synthesis of polycyclic compounds with diverse structures. Free radical tandem reactions have significant atomic/step economy and can effectively reduce the work and time required for synthesis. Meanwhile, the transition metal-mediated C–H functionalization and intramolecular tandem annulations of heterocycles have emerged as the most effective method for constructing fused polycyclic scaffolds. , Until now, various radicals, sulfonyl radicals, alkyl radicals, acyl radicals, selenyl/sulfenyl radicals, silyl radicals, CO2 radical anion, and other radicals have been well employed in the tandem cyclization of 2-aryl-N-methacryloyl indoles (Scheme a). Among the various functionalizations of indolo­[2,1-a]­isoquinoline, alkylation stands out as the most prevalent.

Strategies for C–H Functionalization of 2-Aryl-N-methacryloyl Indoles

Hydrogen atom transfer (HAT) is one of the key strategies for achieving selective C–H functionalization. , In recent years, nitrogen/oxygen-centered radicals generated via 1,5-hydrogen atom transfer (1,5-HAT) have been widely used in photoredox catalysis, owing to their low energy barrier and the stability of the six-membered chair transition state. In contrast, 1,2-HAT processes, characterized by high barriers and constrained three-centered transition states, are less common (Scheme b). − Recent studies − indicated that 1,2-HAT processes often compete with 1,5-HAT processes, making the formation of products via net 1,2-HAT challenging in organic synthesis. In a study by Yang’s group, a new route to 1,2-diamine derivatives was established through a net 1,2-HAT process, wherein amidyl radicals exhibit a marked preference for 1,2-HAT over 1,5-HAT.To date, only limited examples exist in which N-centered radicals derived from 1,2-HAT have been applied to the functionalization of biologically active compounds or pharmacophores. Wu’s group developed a distinctivephotoredox-catalyzed net 1,2-HAT of amidyl radicals for alkylation of glycine derivatives and peptides, along with a photoexcited copper-catalyzed alkene difunctionalization to access 2,5-diamino acid derivatives via an atypical 1,2-HAT process. Despite these advancements, net 1,2-HAT processes remain valuable for exploring rapid molecular complexity construction. In particular, the synthesis of indolo­[2,1-a]­isoquinoline derivatives via a cascade cyclization of 2-aryl-N-methacryloyl indoles involving a 1,2-HAT has not yet been reported.

Therefore, we attempted to achieve the alkylative cyclization of 2-aryl-N-methacryloyl indoles using hydroxamic acid derivatives as radical donors under photocatalytic conditions via a rare 1,2-HAT process (Scheme c).

Results and Discussion

To evaluate the above hypothesis, we initially selected 2-methyl-1-(2-phenyl-1H-indol-1-yl)­prop-2-en-1-one (1a) and N-methyl-N-((4-(trifluoromethyl)­benzoyl)­oxy)­benzamide (2a) as model substrates to explore the optimal reaction conditions. Under an argon atmosphere, using PH–PTZ (phenyl-phthalazine) as the photocatalyst, DABCO (1,4-diazabicyclo[2.2.2]­octane) as the base, and DMSO as the solvent, the reaction was carried out at room temperature for 24 h in a photoreactor equipped with 450–455 nm light source, affording the desired product 3aa in 92% yield (Table , entry (1). It is known from our group’s previous reports that the photosensitizer plays a paramount role in the single-electron transfer to substrate 2 when it acts as a radical acceptor in 1,2-HAT reactions. ,− Consequently, the involvement of a photosensitizer was inferred to be necessary for the present system. Substitution of PH–PTZ with other common photosensitizers, including 4CzIPN (2, 4, 5, 6-Tetrakis­(carbazol-9-yl)-1,3-dicyanobenzene), Eosin Y, and fac-Ir­(ppy)3 (fac-Ir­(2-phenylpyridyl)­3), resulted in inferior yields (Table , entries 2–4). Meanwhile, the base was found to facilitate the efficiency of the aforementioned reactions. We propose that it promotes the deprotonation step in the current reaction system. A screen of alternative bases revealed that substitutions for DABCO, including NaHCO3, K2CO3, NaOH, and Et3N, afforded product 3aa in markedly inferior yields (Table , entries 5–8). Besides the photosensitizer, the light source significantly influences the occurrence of photochemical reactions. Previous studies have shown that blue light promotes the involvement of substrate 2 in the reaction. − Building on this, we examined light sources of different wavelengths and confirmed that blue LEDs at 450–455 nm was the most effective (Table , entry 9). Furthermore, previous studies indicated that the polar aprotic solvent DMSO is optimal for the participation of substrate 2 in the reaction. Consistent with this finding, other solvents, such as DMA, THF, DMF, and CH3CN, failed to improve the yield compared to DMSO (Table , entries 10–13). As evidenced by control experiments, the photocatalyst, visible light, and argon were each found to be crucial for the success of this reaction (Table , entries 14–17). After establishing the optimal reaction conditions, the substrate scope of the reaction was investigated, as summarized in Scheme . First, the influence of variously substituted 2-arylindoles 1 on the reaction was examined. The results showed that 2-aryl-N-methacryloylindoles bearing Ph, Et, or Me substituents at the R1 position exhibited good reaction tolerance, affording the target products 3ba-3fa in excellent yields of 86%–93%. Furthermore, N-methacryloyl-2-phenylbenzimidazole was also confirmed to be a suitable substrate for this cyclization reaction, providing the corresponding products 3ga-3ha in yields of 85%–88%. Subsequently, the scope of hydroxamic acid derivatives 2 was investigated. Various substituted derivatives, whether bearing electron-donating groups (−OCH3, -t-Bu, –CH3) or electron-withdrawing groups (-F, -Cl, -Br), all proceeded smoothly to afford products 4aa-4al in 80–89% yields, with no significant electronic effect observed. Pleasingly, replacement of the aromatic ring in substrate 2 with cyclohexyl, cyclopentyl, 2-furyl, or 2-thienyl groups also furnished the target products 4am-4ap in good yields (76–80%). Additionally, replacing the N-methyl group in compound 2a with an isopropyl group successfully delivered the desired product 4aq in 85% yield.

Based on this group’s previous experience with reactions involving the 1,2-HAT process of substrate 2, mechanistic studies indicated that such reactions follow a single-electron transfer mechanism involving radical intermediates. − Combined with the control experimental results from Table , we hypothesized that the current reaction likely also proceeds via a single-electron transfer mechanism involving radical intermediates. To test this hypothesis and to elucidate similarities to and distinctions from known reaction paradigms, we designed and performed a series of mechanistic validation experiments. Initially, the reaction mechanism was thoroughly investigated using radical trapping experiments under standard conditions. Upon the addition of 3.0 equiv of the radical trapping agent 2,2,6,6-tetramethyl-1-piperidinyloxy (TEMPO), no formation of the target product 3aa was observed. Subsequent HRMS (ESI-TOF) analysis of the reaction mixture revealed the presence of the corresponding radical adduct 5 (Scheme a). When 3.0 equiv of another radical trapping agent, 1,1-diphenylethylene, were employed, the reaction was significantly inhibited, and the generation of 3aa was almost completely suppressed, whereas the corresponding radical adduct 6 was detected via HRMS (Scheme b). These experimental findings confirmed that the reaction proceeds through a radical pathway. Subsequently, to further investigate the mechanism of the photocatalytic reaction, Stern–Volmer fluorescence quenching experiments were conducted. The results showed that 2a effectively quenched the excited state of the photocatalyst PH–PTZ* in the system, whereas 1a exhibited negligible quenching effect on the excited state of PH–PTZ* (Figures –). Finally, cyclic voltammetry experiments were conducted to supplement the mechanistic investigation and confirm that this redox reaction is thermodynamically favorable. The research findings indicated that the photocatalyst PH–PTZ absorbs visible light to generate the corresponding excited state PH–PTZ* (E 1/2 [PH–PTZ/*PH–PTZ] = −2.1 V vs SCE) [40], which is capable of reducing 2a (Figures –).Finally, the practical utility of this transformation was evaluated. When substrate 1a was scaled up to 6.0 mmol under standard conditions, the yield decreased slightly, but the target compound 3aa could still be isolated in a high yield of 88% (Scheme c).

On the basis of the mechanistic experimental results and related literature reports, − we proposed a plausible reaction mechanism, as illustrated in Scheme . Under visible light irradiation, the photocatalyst PH–PTZ absorbs light energy and is excited to PH–PTZ. Subsequently, the excited-state PH–PTZ undergoes single electron transfer (SET)­with 2a, generating the radical cation Ph-PTZ•+ and a nitrogen-centered radical intermediate A. Intermediate A rapidly undergoes an intramolecular 1,2-hydrogen atom transfer process to afford a more stable carbon-centered radical intermediate B. Radical intermediate B then combines with 1a to form radical intermediate C, followed by intramolecular cyclization to afford radical D. Radical D undergoes a second SET with Ph-PTZ•+, thereby being oxidized to intermediate E, while the photocatalyst PH–PTZ is regenerated and returns to the ground state. Finally, intermediate E undergoes deprotonation in the presence of a base to yield the desired product 3aa.

Conclusion

In conclusion, we have developed a photoredox-catalyzed radical cascade cyclization that efficiently constructs the target product from 2-aryl-N-methacryloyl indoles at room temperature through a rare 1,2-HAT process. This reaction successfully affords a series of indolo­[2,1-a]­isoquinoline derivatives with broad substrate scope. A range of indolo­[2,1-a]­isoquinoline derivatives were synthesized using this protocol, which exhibited good substrate scope. This method provides a metal-free, convenient, scalable, and sustainable way to achieve structural and functional diversity in indolo­[2,1-a]­isoquinoline derivatives. It is worth noting that the scale–up reaction further highlights the application potential of this method in the field of drug development.

Experimental Section

General Information

All reagents used in the experiments were commercially available unless otherwise noted and were used without further purification. All manipulations were performed under an argon atmosphere in dried tubes equipped with magnetic stir bars. Unless specifically indicated as dry solvents, all solvents were treated by conventional methods. All reactions were monitored by thin-layer chromatography (TLC) and visualized under UV light. Products were purified by silica gel column chromatography. TLC was performed using precoated silica gel GF254 (0.2 mm) plates, and column chromatography was carried out using silica gel (100–200 mesh). 1H, 13C, and 19F NMR spectra were recorded on a Varian spectrometer operating at 400 MHz (Varian), 100 MHz (Varian), and 376 MHz (Varian), respectively, using tetramethylsilane (TMS, δ = 0.00 ppm) as the internal standard. 1H NMR chemical shifts are reported relative to CDCl3 (δ = 7.26 ppm), and 13C NMR chemical shifts are reported relative to the central peak of CDCl3 (δ = 77.0 ppm). Coupling constants (J) are reported in Hz. The following abbreviations are used to denote signal multiplicities: br = broad, s = singlet, d = doublet, t = triplet, q = quartet, m = multiplet, dd = doublet of doublets, dt = doublet of triplets. High-resolution mass spectrometry (HRMS) was recorded on a micrOTOF-QIII mass spectrometer (Bruker Daltonics). Melting points were measured on a YRT-3 melting point apparatus (Shantou Keyi Instrument Co., Ltd., Shantou, China). Photochemical reactions were performed in an SSSTECH-LAL1CV 1.0 parallel reactor (Shanghai 3S Technology Development Co., Ltd.) under visible light irradiation from a blue LED, which featured an emission peak at 451 nm with a half-peak width of 21 nm and delivered an irradiance of 49.2 mW/cm2 at 12 W power. Reactions were carried out in borosilicate glass test tubes placed 0.5 cm from the light source without the use of a filter. 2-aryl-N-methacryloyl indoles and hydroxamic acid derivatives were synthesized following literature procedures. ,,, ,

General Synthetic Procedure for products 3 and 4

A dried 10 mL reaction tube equipped with a stir bar was charged with 2-aryl-N-methacryloyl indole 1 (0.10 mmol, 1.0 equiv), hydroxamic acid derivative 2 (0.15 mmol, 1.5 equiv), DABCO (2 equiv), and photocatalyst PH–PTZ (5 mol %). The tube was sealed with a rubber septum and Parafilm, then evacuated and backfilled with argon (three times). Anhydrous DMSO (1.0 mL) was then added via syringe, and the reaction mixture was stirred under irradiation in a 450–455 nm blue LED photoreactor (12 W) for 24 h. Upon completion, the reaction mixture was diluted with water (5.0 mL) and extracted with ethyl acetate. The combined organic layers were washed with saturated brine (5.0 mL), dried over anhydrous Na2SO4, filtered, and concentrated under reduced pressure to give the crude product. The residue was purified by flash column chromatography using hexane/ethyl acetate or hexane/dichloromethane as the eluent to afford the target product in the indicated yield.

Scale-Up Reaction of 3aa

To a dried 50 mL Schlenk tube fitted with a stir bar were sequentially added 1a (1.57 g, 6 mmol), 2a (2.91 g, 9 mmol), DABCO (2 equiv), photocatalyst PH–PTZ (5 mol %), and anhydrous DMSO (20 mL). The tube was then sealed with a rubber septum and Parafilm, evacuated, and backfilled with argon three times. The reaction mixture was irradiated with a 450–455 nm blue LED photoreactor (12 W) at room temperature for 24 h. Upon completion of the reaction, distilled water (15 mL) was added, and the mixture was extracted with ethyl acetate (3 × 15 mL). The combined organic layers were washed with saturated brine, dried over anhydrous Na2SO4, and filtered. The filtrate was concentrated under reduced pressure using a rotary evaporator to afford the crude product, which was purified directly by silica gel column chromatography using ethyl acetate/petroleum ether as the eluent, yielding 2.08 g of product 3aa in 88% yield.

Control Experiments

To a dried 10 mL reaction tube equipped with a stir bar were sequentially added 2-aryl-N-methacryloyl indole 1a (0.10 mmol, 1.0 equiv), hydroxamic acid derivative 2a (0.15 mmol, 1.5 equiv), DABCO (0.20 mmol, 2.0 equiv), photocatalyst PH–PTZ (5 mol %), and the radical trapping agent TEMPO (0.30 mmol, 3.0 equiv). The tube was sealed with a rubber septum and Parafilm, evacuated, and backfilled with argon three times. Anhydrous DMSO (1.0 mL) was then added via syringe, and the tube was transferred to a blue LED photoreactor (12 W) and irradiated for 24 h. Reaction monitoring revealed that no target compound 3aa was formed upon the addition of the radical trapping agent TEMPO, while the TEMPO-radical adduct 5 was detected by HRMS (Figure S3).

Stern-Volmer Fluorescence Quenching Experiments

Argon was bubbled through DMSO for 30 minutes to remove oxygen. A 4.0×10-5 M stock solution of PH-PTZ was then prepared by dissolving it in the degassed DMSO. From this stock, 100 μL aliquots were transferred into each of six 10 mL volumetric flasks. Afterwards, a DMSO solution containing quencher 1a or 2a (0.125 M, 1.0 mL) was added to the flasks in progressively larger volumes (0, 100, 200, 300, 400, and 500 μL). Each flask was then brought to a final volume of 10 mL with DMSO. The emission intensities at 447 nm in the absence and presence of the quencher are denoted as I0 and I, respectively. All fluorescence measurements were performed using an F-98 FL spectrophotometer.

Cyclic Voltammetry Experiments

Cyclic voltammetry experiments were conducted on a “computer-controlled CH Instrument Electrochemical Analyzer [AUTOLAB PGSTAT128N]” in a three-electrode cell (beaker-type cell) at room temperature (25 ± 2 °C) using a glassy carbon working electrode, a saturated calomel electrode (SCE) as reference electrode, a Pt wire as auxiliary electrode, and 0.1 M nBu4NBF4 as electrolyte with a 10 mM solution of the sample in DMSO. The surface area of the glassy carbon electrode is 7.06 mm2 and the electrode is round-shaped. The electrode is polished with figure-eight motions on a cloth polishing pad in a wateralumina slurry. For the cyclic voltammetry measurement, IUPAC convention was followed. The starting point for the CV curve was at 0.0 V and measured in the positive direction. For the CV experiments, the initial potential was 0.0 V, the switching potential was +2.5 V, and the scan rate was 0.1 V/s. All solutions used for the voltammetric experiments were deoxygenated by purging with highpurity argon gas for up to 5 minutes, and measurements were performed in open air at room temperature (25 ± 2 °C).
