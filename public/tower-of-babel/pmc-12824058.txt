Comparison of surgical and non surgical treatment strategies for non metastatic oropharyngeal cancer using inverse probability weighting
Authors: Antoine Dubray-vautrin, Olivier Choussy, Laurence Bozec, Christophe Le Tourneau, Valentin Calugaru, Pierre Graff, Rabah Taouachi, Maria Lesnik, Nathalie Badois, Jimmy Mullaert
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
ObjectiveSurgical (SS) and non-surgical strategies (NSS) are both considered as valid options in the therapeutic management of oropharyngeal squamous cell carcinoma (OPSCC) because of the absence of randomized control trial.MethodThe objective of this retrospective cohort study was to compare the efficacy of SS versus NSS on overall survival (OS) and disease-free survival (DFS) in non-metastatic OPSCC. The study included patients treated in two centers between 2012 and 2020 treated by upfront surgery in SS group and other therapies in NSS. A Cox model allowed to estimate hazard ratios and 95%CI., Weighting by the inverse probability of a propensity score was applied due to the absence of randomization.ResultsWe analyzed 432 patients with a majority (71%) of male patients and a mean age of 62.6 (SD10.1), HPV prevalence of 70%, 61% were smokers. The 5-year OS and DFS were 59% (95%CI 54–64) and 61% (95% CI 56–66), respectively. The sample was split into SS (n = 254) and NSS (n = 178). Patients exposed to SS were statistically younger (p < 0.001), with a better performance status (PS) (p < 0.001), and less advanced TNM stage (p < 0.001). After weighting, the two groups were comparable. The weighted HR of SS versus NSS were 0.65 (95%CI 0.45–0.95, p = 0.02) and 0.67 (95%CI 0.47–0.94, p = 0.02) for OS and DFS respectively. Further sensitivity analysis confirmed these results.ConclusionsWhile our findings suggest potential benefits of SS, ongoing controlled randomized trials are essential to conform these observations.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1007/s12672-025-04272-7.

Introduction

Oropharyngeal squamous cell carcinomas (OPSCC) are among the most common head and neck carcinomas, with a global incidence of approximately 100,000 cases per year [1]. This incidence is rising, largely due to the increasing prevalence of human papillomavirus (HPV) infections [2]. HPV-related OPSCC accounts for 50% to 74% of all OPSCC in Europe [3] and North America [4]. The prognosis varies significantly between HPV-positive and HPV-negative OPSCC, with a 3-year overall survival (OS) of 82% for HPV-positive OPSCC and 57% for HPV-negative OPSCC [5]. An intermediate prognosis is observed in HPV-positive OPSCC patients with a history of smoking.

Importantly, HPV status significantly treatment approaches. HPV-associated OPSCC represents a distinct disease entity characterized by better prognosis and increased treatment responsiveness, prompting investigation into treatment de-escalation strategies to minimize long-term toxicities. In contrast, HPV-independent OPSCC, often linked to traditional risk factors like smoking and alcohol, tends to have a poorer prognosis and may require more intensive treatment. Despite these differences, current clinical guidelines continue to present overlapping therapeutic dilemmas for both HPV-positive and HPV-negative patients [6, 7], emphasizing individualized treatment decisions integrating multidisciplinary input and patient preferences. This complexity underscores ongoing debates about the optimal integration of surgery [8] and chemoradiation in the management of OPSCC tailored to HPV status. Surgery is often considered an alternative to chemoradiation, though its role remains debated due to its associated morbidity [9]. The heterogeneity of surgical techniques further complicates the evaluation of morbidity. In early-stage OPSCC, surgical approaches, such as transoral robot-assisted surgery, have been evaluated in a randomized phase II clinical trial [10], which assessed their associated morbidity. For more extensive cases, advancements in surgical techniques, including free flap reconstruction can significantly impact the quality of life [11]. Recent innovations in both surgical and radiotherapy techniques may warrant a reevaluation of their efficacy in improving survival outcomes. However, determining the optimal therapeutic strategy remains challenging due to a lack of robust scientific evidence. The efficacy, toxicity, and quality-of-life outcomes of surgical strategies, particularly when incorporated into multimodal therapy, need further clarification to enable personalized treatment for OPSCC patients [12]. Furthermore, a de-escalation approach is under investigation [9, 13] aiming to refine both surgical and radiotherapy strategies. This includes evaluating the potential role of surgery as part of a comprehensive multimodal treatment plan. Notably, the ECOG-ACRIN 3311 trial has demonstrated the favorable impact of deintensified postoperative radiation on patient outcomes, supporting efforts to minimize treatment-related morbidity while maintaining oncologic efficacy [14].

The objective of this study is to compare the prognostic impact of surgical strategy (SS) versus non-surgical strategy (NSS) on overall survival (OS) and disease-free survival (DFS) in non-metastatic OPSCC, using data from an observational database taking into consideration clinical confounders to guide therapeutic decision.

Patients and methods

Study design

This study was a retrospective, bi-centric cohort study of OPSCC patients treated between January 1 st, 2012, and December 31 st, 2020, in two French tertiary oncologic centers. Patients were informed about the use of their health data for research purposes and given the opportunity to express non-opposition in accordance with the French MR004 methodology and waived the requirement for written informed consent due to its retrospective design. In accordance ethical approval was obtained from the local hospital committee before collecting data (Institut Curie institutional review board, Paris, France, approval number: DATA230001). We reported this study in accordance with STROBE guidelines [15].

The screening period was chosen after the introduction of IMRT radiotherapy at both centers, allowing for a longitudinal follow-up of at least 3 years.

Study population

The inclusion criteria in the cohort were adult patients (> 18 years) with histologically or cytologically proven non-metastatic OPSCC with extirpable disease. Exclusion criteria were: synchronous cancer, metastatic disease, opposition of participant and non-sufficient data quality (absence of information about therapy or vital status), absolute surgical contra-indication (Skull base involvement, carotid artery involvement, vertebral involvement).

Data collection

Data were extracted from medical files using the ICD-10 classification (C09.0, C10.0, C14.0, C14.2). All locations of OPSCC were extracted (tongue base, tonsil, soft palate, and posterior wall of the oropharyngeal space). We collected demographics data (age, gender, tobacco consumption, alcohol consumption, weight, BMI), patients general condition (Malnutrition, performance status (PS) score, ASA Physical Status Classification system (ASA)).

Oncologic status was assessed using the clinical AJCC and UICC 7th edition TNM classification for oropharyngeal carcinomas, considering T as tumor stage, N as nodal stage, M as metastatic stage, and overall staging from I to IV. This edition provides detailed anatomical criteria for tumor and nodal involvement, including extranodal extension, which is crucial for accurately characterizing disease extent and guiding therapeutic decisions, particularly in HPV-associated and HPV-independent cases. Patients diagnosed before 2017 were staged exclusively with the 7th edition. From 2017 onward, with the introduction of the 8th edition, patients were staged using both the 7th and 8th editions to capture important prognostic factors especially extranodal extension and to align with contemporary staging practices, the 7th editions was conserved for statistical analyses and interaction testing.

The initial therapeutic sequences included induction chemotherapy, surgery, (chemo)radiation therapy, or adjuvant chemoradiation. HPV tumor status was determined as positive based on strong and intense p16INK4a expression in immunohistochemistry in at least 70% of tumor cells and/or HPV viral typing using PCR. HPV association was defined by HPV PCR when available (62% of patients) or by p16 status when PCR was not available. Vital and recurrence status were assessed up to 5 years post-inclusion, with patients lost to follow-up censored at the date of last contact in survival analysis. Surgical contraindications under debate included internal or common carotid artery involvement of ± 180°, tongue base involvement exceeding 50%, and invasion of the prevertebral fascia. Tobacco status and alcohol status were considered as binary variables [16]. We considered a regular smoker to be an individual with a cumulative smoking exposure exceeding 10 pack-years, who has not ceased smoking for more than 10 years. For alcohol status, the limit of 20 g per day for men and 10 g per day for women were considered as positive. Performance status score was classified from 0 to 5 [17], ASA score was classified 1 to 5 [18]. For better consideration of anatomical involvement and comparison of HPV-associated and independent carcinomas we used the 7th TNM staging and included in the models an interaction between TNM and HPV.

Exposure

The therapeutic modalities were classified into two strategies [19]: the surgical strategy (SS), where curative surgery was initially performed including transoral, hybrid oral/neck approach and mandibulotomy approach with or without flap reconstruction. We performed bilateral neck dissection in cases of bilateral nodal involvement or when the primary tumor involved the midline. For patients with unilateral nodal involvement classified as N0, N1, or N2a, we performed homolateral neck dissection. We administered adjuvant radiotherapy with doses ranging from 60 Gy (30 fractions) to the primary site and dissected neck areas, or 66 Gy (33 fractions) to high-risk areas in patients with extracapsular extension or positive/close surgical margins. In cases without contralateral neck dissection, a dose of 54–56 Gy (30–33 fractions) was delivered to the contralateral neck.

The non-surgical strategy (NSS), where (chemo) radiation with or without neoadjuvant chemotherapy was the initial treatment (eTable 1). All patients treated with radiation therapy received Intensity-Modulated Radiation Therapy (IMRT). We conducted definitive radiotherapy involving a total dose of 70 Gy in 35 fractions over 7 weeks. For T1 OPSCC, exclusive radiotherapy was performed. For other stages concurrent chemotherapy with Cisplatin 100 mg/m² at day 1, 22 and 43 or weekly with Cisplatin 40 mg/m². We performed induction chemotherapy with Docetaxel (75 mg/m²), Cisplatin (75 mg/m²), 5-Flurouracil (750 mg/m²/day) in 5 days repeated every 3 weeks for 3 cycles.

Outcomes

The outcomes assessed were overall survival (OS) and disease-free survival (DFS). Recurrence status was determined based on one or more of the following criteria: (i) histologic or cytologic confirmation, (ii) documentation through 18 F-FDG PET-CT showing locoregional involvement or distant metastases, or (iii) documentation via CT scan. The date of initial treatment was defined as the date of inclusion, with a follow-up horizon of 5 years.

Statistical analysis

We performed statistical analyses with R-studio (4.2.0) with descriptive analyses using mean with standard deviation and percentages. We carried-out Comparative analyses with Z-test for quantitative variable and Chi² for qualitative variable with a p-value cut-off of 0.05 considered as significant. We estimated OS and DFS with Kaplan Meier survival analysis and log rank for comparative estimation.

Missing data were imputed with a multiple imputation by chained aquations using “MICE package” [20] using covariables with missing data and variables of outcome (Nelson Aalen estimator and survival status). Univariable Cox proportional hazard models were fitted to report hazard ratios (HR), 95% confidence interval and the p-value of likelihood ratio test for each variable. We assessed the hypothesis of proportional risks with Schoenfeld residuals (eFigure 1) and Martingale residuals to validate the quantitative coding for age.

We calculated a propensity score using covariables associated with therapeutic decision (age, HPV status, tobacco consumption, malnutrition, TNM staging, ASA score, PS). We compared SS and NSS groups before and after weighting by the inverse probability of treatment with Wilcoxon rank-sum test for complex survey samples and chi-squared test with Rao & Scott’s second-order correction.

We then performed three main statistical analyses:

i.A Cox model using inverse probability of therapeutic weighting considered as main analysis with a sensitivity analysis excluding patients with surgical contraindication and/or induction chemotherapy.ii.Cox proportional hazard model using full matching on propensity score considered as sensitivity analysis with representation of the balance of standardized error (eFigure 2).iii.A Cox model adjusted on variables selected in univariable analysis, also considered as sensitivity analysis. We kept Significant variables in univariable analysis with a p-value < 0.05 in the multivariable model.

We conducted interaction tests between treatment strategies and other covariates to identify patient subgroups with stronger or weaker effects. We performed interaction analyses using the following variables: age (< 50 years, 50–70 years, and > 70 years), HPV status (positive or negative), smoking status (current or never smoker), and TNM staging (Stages I to IV). We assessed independently each interaction between the treatment strategy and the tested covariates using a multivariable Cox model adjusted for the following variables: age, HPV status, tobacco consumption, malnutrition, TNM staging, ASA score, and performance status. We represented the interaction hazard ratios (HR) of treatment strategies across subgroups. We considered a p-value less than 0.05 as statistically significant.

Results

We identified 758 eligible patients and ultimately included 432 consecutive patients in the cohort, as depicted in the flowchart (Fig. 1). The patients were categorized into two groups: SS (n = 254) and NSS (n = 178). Ten distinct treatment modalities were identified and are detailed in the supplementary data (eTable 1). The mean age of the cohort was 62.6 years (SD ± 10.1), with the majority being male (71%) and 70% of patients having HPV-positive disease. The demographic and oncologic characteristics of the cohort are summarized in Table 1. Tumor status (T) and nodal status (N), ranging from N0 to N3, differed significantly between groups (p < 0.001). Furthermore, age (p < 0.001), malnutrition status (p < 0.001), TNM stage (p < 0.001), and performance status (PS) (p < 0.001) were more advanced in the NSS group.

Flow chart of the cohort

After applying inverse probability of treatment weighting (IPTW), the SS and NSS groups were comparable in terms of age, gender, tobacco consumption, alcohol consumption, HPV status, and TNM staging (Table 1). However, surgical contraindications remained significantly different (p < 0.001).

The 5-year overall survival (OS) and disease-free survival (DFS), estimated using Kaplan-Meier analysis, were 59.1% (95% CI 54.3–64.3) and 60.7% (95% CI 55.8–66.1), respectively, for the entire cohort. Subgroup analysis of 5-year OS revealed significant differences based on HPV and tobacco status: 75.2% (95% CI 68.3–82.8) for HPV-positive non-tobacco users, 56.2% (95% CI 48.2–65.6) for HPV-associated tobacco users, and 39.8% (95% CI 31.1–51.0) for HPV-negative patients (log-rank test, p < 0.001).

The SS group was associated with significantly better OS and DFS compared to the NSS group. Log-rank tests showed statistical significance for OS (p < 0.001) and DFS (p < 0.001), with patients in the SS group demonstrating a more favorable prognosis (Fig. 2).

In the Cox univariable analysis (Table 2), several factors were significantly associated with shorter overall survival (OS), including surgical strategy (p < 0.001), age (p < 0.001), tobacco consumption (p < 0.001), alcohol consumption (p < 0.001), HPV positivity (p < 0.001), higher TNM stage (p < 0.001), malnutrition (p < 0.001), higher performance status (PS) (p < 0.001), and higher ASA score (p < 0.001). For disease-free survival (DFS), the surgical strategy (p < 0.001), age (p < 0.001), tobacco consumption (p = 0.04), alcohol consumption (p = 0.03), HPV positivity (p < 0.001), higher TNM stage (p < 0.001), malnutrition (p < 0.001), higher PS (p < 0.001), and higher ASA score (p < 0.001) were also statistically significant. These variables were subsequently included in the multivariable Cox model analysis for both OS and DFS.

The first weighted Cox model yielded hazard ratios (HRs) for the therapeutic strategy of 0.65 (95% CI 0.45–0.95, p = 0.02) for overall survival (OS) and 0.67 (95% CI 0.47–0.94, p = 0.02) for disease-free survival (DFS). In a sensitivity analysis excluding patients with surgical contraindications and induction chemotherapy (n = 395), the HRs were 0.72 (95% CI 0.49–0.98, p = 0.04) for OS and 0.72 (95% CI 0.49–0.96, p = 0.02) for DFS.

In the second Cox model, which used a multivariable approach (including therapeutic strategy, age, tobacco consumption, alcohol consumption, HPV status, TNM stage, malnutrition, PS, and ASA score), the HRs were 0.70 (95% CI 0.49–0.98, p = 0.04) for OS and 0.60 (95% CI 0.42–0.86, p = 0.005) for DFS (eFigure 4).

The third model, using full matching of the propensity score, estimated HRs of 0.42 (95% CI 0.30–0.57, p < 0.001) for OS and 0.46 (95% CI 0.35–0.61, p < 0.001) for DFS. The 5-year OS weighted estimates were 66.3% (95% CI 59.8%–73.4%) for the SS group and 58.6% (95% CI 48.4%–66.6%) for the NSS group. The 5-year DFS weighted estimates were 58.9% (95% CI 52.5%–66.2%) for the SS group and 43.1% (95% CI 34.4%–54.1%) for the NSS group.

An interaction plot depicting the relationships between exposure, tobacco status, HPV status, and TNM staging is shown in Fig. 3. Interactions between exposure and current smoker status, TNM stage, HPV status, and age were not statistically significant. We tested separately the subgroups HPV-independent and HPV-associate, the HRs for OS were 0.61 (95% CI 0.40–1.00, p = 0.12) and 0.82 (95% CI 0.40–1.10, p = 0.24), respectively and non-statistically significant.

In subgroup analyses by age (< 45 years, 45–70 years, and > 70 years), the HRs for OS were 0.91 (95% CI 0.20–2.70, p = 0.58), 0.82 (95% CI 0.50–1.20, p = 0.32), and 0.50 (95% CI 0.20–0.90, p = 0.02), respectively, between the SS and NSS groups. No significant differences between SS and NSS were found in subgroup analyses by TNM stage (stage I to IV).

Discussion

In this study, we evaluated the prognosis of surgical strategy compared to a non-surgical strategy for overall survival (OS) and disease-free survival (DFS) in patients with non-metastatic oropharyngeal squamous cell carcinoma (OPSCC) taking account indication bias and confounders. Clinical data, HPV status, and TNM staging were considered to guide therapeutic decisions. To address potential indication bias inherent in observational data, we applied multiple robust analytical methods incorporating relevant clinical confounders. All approaches consistently demonstrated a survival benefit associated with the surgical strategy (SS) compared to the non-surgical strategy (NSS) in terms of overall survival (OS) and disease-free survival (DFS). Subgroup analyses further identified that the favorable effect of SS was particularly pronounced in patients aged over 70 years and/or current smokers. These findings support the prognostic impact of SS versus NSS in non-metastatic oropharyngeal squamous cell carcinoma (OPSCC) within a real-world clinical setting and provide guidance for therapeutic decision-making.

The demographic characteristics of the cohort were consistent with those reported in the literature in terms of age and gender [21, 22]. The prevalence of HPV was approximately 70%, comparable to other European studies [23], but higher than in the French prospective cohort Papillophar [24]. This difference is likely attributable to the increasing incidence of HPV infection over the last decade. Both centers involved in the study utilized Intensity-Modulated Radiation Therapy (IMRT) between 2012 and 2020, which is the standard of care for head and neck radiation therapy [25], ensuring a consistent therapeutic approach over time and across centers. The choice of surgical approach whether transoral surgery, hybrid transoral and neck dissection, oropharyngectomy with mandibulotomy, with or without flap reconstruction did not have a measurable influence on outcomes in our study. The effect measured in this study corresponds to the impact of the primary therapeutic decision. Histopathological prognostic factors resulting from surgery were not included in the statistical models, in order to accurately reflect the dilemma between choosing surgery or radiotherapy.

The role of a surgical strategy in HPV-associated oropharyngeal carcinoma warrants further investigation, particularly in cases where the tumor board does not initially recommend a triple-modality approach involving surgery, radiotherapy, and chemotherapy. This consideration is especially important in scenarios favoring less intensive treatments or when specific patient factors call for a more individualized therapeutic approach. Larger observational studies specifically focused on HPV-associated oropharyngeal squamous cell carcinoma (OPSCC) are needed to better elucidate the prognostic impact of surgical strategy, particularly in patients with locally advanced disease who are not initially recommended for a trimodal approach (surgery, radiotherapy, and chemotherapy). Such studies could provide valuable real-world evidence to guide personalized therapeutic decision-making in this evolving treatment landscape.

The 5 years OS and 5 years DFS observed in our study were consistent with those reported in other studies, with a 5-year OS of approximately 60% [26]. HPV-positive OPSCC was associated with a better prognosis, similar to previous findings reporting 5-year OS rates ranging from 75% to 82% [3, 5, 23, 24, 27]. In univariable analysis, the sublocation of OPSCC was not significantly associated with prognosis, likely due to technical advances such as endoscopic and transoral robotic surgery (TORS) approaches, which reduce the morbidity associated with traditional external surgical techniques.

Additionally, using the 7th edition TNM staging, no significant interaction was observed in our cohort, although the hazard ratio (HR) tended to be lower for early-stage OPSCC. The lack of statistical significance may be due to limited power in the study. Furthermore, the surgical strategy showed a favorable effect in the subgroup of patients over 70 years of age. Since age over 70 can be a contraindication for systemic therapy, reducing the likelihood of chemoradiation, surgical management remains a viable and often preferred option in this population [28].

A favorable effect of surgical strategy was also reported by Evers et al. in 2020 [29], in an observational study of early-stage OPSCC. However, some residual bias regarding the surgical strategy should be acknowledged, given the diversity of surgical techniques and recent advances such as the introduction of transoral robotic surgery (TORS) since 2012 [30], which have reduced toxicity and improved surgical control [31, 32]. Retrospective or prospective cohorts of OPSCC that simultaneously consider surgical strategies, HPV status, and both early-stage and locally advanced cases to quantify treatment effect sizes remain rare. Two recent studies from the French retrospective GETTEC cohort [19, 33] have confirmed the prognostic value of surgical strategy in OPSCC for both p16-negative and p16-positive carcinomas. A larger Brazilian cohort study reported better survival outcomes in the surgical treatment group; however, this analysis did not include HPV status, which is a key prognostic factor in oropharyngeal squamous cell carcinoma, and thus may limit the interpretation of treatment effects [34]. A recent systematic review published in 2025 concluded that surgical treatment followed by adjuvant chemoradiotherapy may be associated with improved overall survival compared to non-surgical treatment in patients with locally advanced oropharyngeal squamous cell carcinoma, reinforcing the potential prognostic benefit of incorporating surgery within a multimodal therapeutic approach [35].

It remains important to quantify the effect size of a surgical strategy versus a non-surgical strategy in OPSCC to inform the design of randomized controlled trials and accurately calculate the required sample size. Further investigation into clinical interactions with TNM staging is essential to identify patient subgroups best suited for such trials. Interaction analyses by age category revealed a statistically significant positive prognostic impact of the surgical strategy in patients over 70 years old. One possible explanation is that cisplatin-based chemotherapy is relatively contraindicated after this age, rendering non-surgical strategies less optimal. In these interaction analyses, tobacco consumption also remains a key factor influencing the decision-making process for surgical management of OPSCC. Additionally, given the favorable effect of surgical strategy in early-stage tumors, an equivalence trial focusing on treatment de-escalation such as omitting adjuvant radiation could be considered to reduce reliance on multimodal approaches involving surgery, radiotherapy, and chemotherapy.

Limitations and methodology

The primary limitation of this study is its retrospective data collection. Although we made efforts to control for classification bias, retrospective studies inherently carry risks of misclassification and unmeasured confounding factors. To minimize classification bias, all data were verified, and tumor and nodal staging were classified according to the 7th edition of the clinical TNM staging system, including an interaction term with HPV status. Tumors were staged from T1 to T4 and nodes from N0 to N3, ensuring reliable assessment of disease extent while considering the major prognostic impact of HPV status in OPSCC. Regardless of TNM stage, tumors with vessel infiltration greater than 180°, tongue base involvement exceeding 50%, and other factors were classified as surgically contraindicated. Alcohol consumption was categorized using cut-offs of 10 g per day for women and 20 g per day for men, as recommended by clinical guidelines [16, 36]. However, this cut-off is imperfect, as the risk of cancer increases gradually with alcohol consumption [37]. For tobacco use, we applied a cut-off of 20 years after last consumption to classify smokers and non-smokers. Nonetheless, it may be difficult to determine the exact cessation date, and the relationship between time since quitting smoking and current risk remains unclear [16].

Due to the non-randomized design, the observed benefits of the surgical strategy require confirmation and further clarification. The study included both monomodal and multimodal treatment approaches but did not specifically assess the impact of different surgical techniques or types. The reported effect size encompasses all strategies involving surgery and (chemo)radiation therapy, without distinguishing the direct effects of various radiation techniques or chemotherapy agents. The focus was on overall therapeutic management based on initial tumor board decisions, where the choice between strategies is critical. Other approaches, such as immunotherapy and surgery combined with neck dissection plus (chemo)radiation, were not included due to limited sample size and should be addressed in future research. Target trial emulation studies with larger cohorts are needed to better estimate the impact of therapeutic decisions on survival, with subgroup analyses to clarify the effect sizes in HPV-associated versus HPV-independent carcinomas.

In this study, there was no evaluation of the quality of life, although it is important to take into consideration [38] to evaluate the benefit risk balance and to have a complete evaluation of the surgical strategy. Quality of life in strongly impacted by therapeutic management of oropharyngeal cancer [39] and taking it to account allowed the development of oncologic therapeutic personalized therapies [40]. Recent studies have reported that surgery combined with radiotherapy (SRT) and definitive chemoradiotherapy (CRT) yield comparable long-term outcomes across multiple domains, including quality of life, speech, swallowing function, sleep quality, psychological distress, and nutritional status [41]. Amin et al. [42] described reported an increased risk of dysphagia following transoral robotic surgery (TORS) in patients with cT1/T2 oropharyngeal squamous cell carcinoma (OPSCC), which underscores the need to carefully weigh the relative survival benefits of surgical intervention against potential long-term functional impairments. This consideration is particularly relevant given the comparable oncologic outcomes observed between TORS and non-surgical treatments in early-stage OPSCC, highlighting the importance of individualized treatment decisions that account for both survival and quality-of-life outcomes. When the therapeutic strategy involves a triple modality the cumulative toxicity with a less significant survival benefit is likely not favorable.

Conclusions

In this cohort study, the surgical strategy was associated with better outcomes, even after accounting for indication bias. Ongoing randomized clinical trials will definitively answer this question by comparing surgical and non-surgical approaches directly. The surgical strategy still needs to be debated, especially when a triple modality treatment involving surgery, radiotherapy, and chemotherapy is not proposed and when chemotherapy is contraindicated. The selection of therapy in non-metastatic oropharyngeal cancer remains a challenge due to the lack of predictive models and biomarkers to predict the therapeutic response to the initial treatment strategy. Future research should focus on developing these tools to personalize treatment decisions and improve patient outcomes.
