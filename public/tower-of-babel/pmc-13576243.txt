Pyrogallol aggravates arsenic toxicity in Triticum aestivum L. through oxidative stress and mineral imbalance
Authors: Cansu Altuntaş
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundPyrogallol (PG), a plant-derived phenolic compound, exhibits dual redox behaviour as either an antioxidant or pro-oxidant depending on cellular context; however, its interaction with metal and metalloid stress remains poorly understood. This study investigated whether exogenous PG amplifies arsenic-induced oxidative stress and disrupts mineral balance in detached shoot tissues of fifteen-day-old wheat (Triticum aestivum L.).MethodsDetached wheat shoots were exposed to PG (5 mM), arsenic (As; 100 µM), or their combination under acute controlled conditions for 18 h. Oxidative stress markers (O₂•−, H₂O₂, TBARS), antioxidant enzyme activities (SOD, CAT, APX), proline content, mineral composition (macro- and micro-elements by ICP-MS), and elemental composition (CHNS) were quantified. UV–Vis spectrophotometric titration and in silico molecular docking were used to assess potential PG–As⁵⁺ and PG–APX interactions, respectively. Two-way ANOVA,pearson correlation, and principal component analysis were performed to evaluate PG–As⁵⁺ interaction effects.ResultsCombined PG + As treatment increased tissue As accumulation by 21% relative to As alone and markedly intensified oxidative stress, as evidenced by elevated lipid peroxidation and hydrogen peroxide levels. Antioxidant enzyme activities (SOD, CAT, and APX) were substantially reduced, and proline accumulation was not associated with attenuation of oxidative damage. Elemental analysis revealed significant disruptions in mineral composition alongside a decreased C: N ratio, indicative of impaired carbon-nitrogen metabolism. Two-way ANOVA confirmed a significant PG–As⁵⁺ interaction effect for 16 of 19 measured parameters, providing quantitative support for interaction rather than additive effects.ConclusionsThese findings suggest that PG may act as a redox-active pro-oxidant under combined stress, contributing to suppression of antioxidant defenses and disruption of mineral balance in detached wheat shoot tissues, with implications for future investigations into phenolic–metal/metalloid stress responses in cereals.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s12870-026-09722-0.Keywords: Antioxidant enzymes, Arsenic, Oxidative stress, Pyrogallol, Triticum aestivum

Background

Wheat (Triticum aestivum L.) is a major staple crop that feeds more than three billion people worldwide, providing approximately 20% of daily caloric and protein intake [1]. However, this essential crop is increasingly threatened by arsenic (As) contamination. In South and Southeast Asia, millions of hectares of farmland are irrigated with As-contaminated water, exposing over 230 million people to chronic health risks [2, 3]. Arsenic accumulation disrupts photosynthesis, reduces vegetative growth, and decreases grain yield by 10–50% [4, 5]. It also lowers grain zinc (Zn) and iron (Fe) contents by 20–40%, with direct implications for human nutritional quality and food security in vulnerable populations [6, 7].

Although As phytotoxicity has been widely investigated, one dimension has remained largely unaddressed: the biochemical consequences of co-occurring plant-derived phenolic compounds on the tissue-level response to As in cereal shoots. Polyphenolic compounds are continuously deposited in agricultural soils through root exudates, crop residues, and microbial decomposition of lignin [8], and their rhizospheric concentrations can reach biologically meaningful levels. Despite this co-occurrence, whether such phenolics amplify or attenuate As-induced damage in wheat shoot tissues remains directly unexamined. This is not a trivial gap, because phenolic–metalloid interactions are mechanistically ambiguous: many phenolics chelate metal and metalloid ions and scavenge reactive oxygen species (ROS) [9], yet the same molecules can undergo metal-catalyzed auto-oxidation to generate ROS and act as pro-oxidants [10, 11]. Whether protective or destructive behavior dominates under combined phenolic–As stress is therefore a context-dependent question that single-stress studies cannot resolve. Renewed interest in the environmental and biological behavior of simple polyphenols such as pyrogallol, driven by expanding applications in materials chemistry, drug delivery, and nanotechnology [12], has further emphasized the need to resolve how this compound class behaves in realistic agroecological contexts.

To address this question, pyrogallol (PG; 1,2,3-trihydroxybenzene) was selected as a tractable chemical model rather than as a proposed endogenous wheat metabolite. Four considerations guided this choice. First, the pyrogallyl (1,2,3-trihydroxyphenyl) moiety represents a fundamental structural unit of widely distributed plant polyphenols, occurring as the defining subunit of gallic acid, gallotannins, ellagitannins, epigallocatechin gallate, and myricetin [8]; such compounds are continuously deposited into agricultural soils through root exudation, crop residue decomposition, and lignin turnover [9]. Biochemical responses to PG therefore inform the behavior of this broader compound class at the minimal-structure level. Second, the reduced structural complexity of PG, lacking the carboxyl, glycosidic, or polymeric substitutions present in more elaborate polyphenols, enables cleaner discrimination between antioxidant and pro-oxidant behavior without confounding contributions from additional functional groups [12]. Third, among polyphenolic structures, PG exhibits the most pronounced dual redox behavior: its vicinal trihydroxyl arrangement drives rapid auto-oxidation and superoxide generation under aerobic conditions, a property so reproducible that it forms the mechanistic basis of Marklund’s superoxide dismutase assay [13], while simultaneously enabling strong coordination with trivalent metal and metalloid ions [9, 12]. Fourth, 5 mM PG combined with 100 µM As⁵⁺ produced reproducible and biologically interpretable responses in a previous detached-shoot investigation in maize (Zea mays L.) seedlings [14], providing a directly comparable reference system for examining species-level differences in wheat.

Building on this rationale, the present study addresses a single focused question: does exogenous pyrogallol amplify or alleviate arsenic-induced biochemical damage in wheat shoot tissues, and if amplification occurs, through which redox and mineral-homeostasis routes? It was hypothesized that, owing to the pronounced auto-oxidation capacity of PG under aerobic and metal-rich conditions, exogenous PG would intensify rather than alleviate As-induced oxidative stress and mineral disruption in detached wheat shoot tissues, with the magnitude of amplification greatest under combined PG + As exposure, manifested as a statistically significant PG–As⁵⁺ interaction effect rather than simple additive effects of the two stressors.

To test this hypothesis, an acute detached-shoot model was employed in fifteen-day-old wheat, an experimental framework that deliberately isolates tissue-level biochemical responses from root-mediated regulatory mechanisms to achieve precise chemical control over the stress agents reaching the aerial tissues [14–16]. Tissues were exposed to PG (5 mM), As⁵⁺ (100 µM), or their combination under controlled conditions. Within this unified framework, (i) oxidative stress markers and antioxidant enzyme activities were quantified, (ii) mineral and elemental composition was assessed to detect stress-induced homeostatic disruption, and (iii) possible direct PG–As⁵⁺ chemical interactions were examined through UV–Vis spectrophotometric titration and in silico molecular docking as preliminary, exploratory evidence consistent with the observed tissue-level responses.

Methods

Plant growth conditions

Wheat seeds (Triticum aestivum L.) were surface-sterilized and germinated on moistened filter paper under controlled conditions. Seedlings were raised hydroponically in half-strength Hoagland nutrient solution in a controlled-environment growth chamber set to 25 ± 2 °C (day) / 22 ± 2 °C (night), 60–65% relative humidity, a 16/8 h light/dark photoperiod, and 400 µmol m⁻² s⁻¹ photosynthetic photon flux density (PPFD). After germination, seedlings were maintained in half-strength Hoagland solution during early development and subsequently transferred to full-strength Hoagland solution. Fifteen-day-old seedlings were used, as this stage shows reproducible biochemical responses to acute As stress in cereals [14, 17].

Cutting protocol and treatments

After 15 days of hydroponic cultivation, wheat seedlings were detached 2 cm above the substrate surface using a single sterile razor cut. The aerial tissues were left intact. Shoots were transferred to 20 mL glass tubes containing distilled water and maintained under the same growth-chamber conditions for 1 h to stabilize tissue water balance before treatment. The detached-shoot protocol followed previously established procedures for tissue-level analyses in cereals [14–16].

Pyrogallol (PG; 1,2,3-trihydroxybenzene; Sigma-Aldrich, St. Louis, MO, USA; purity ≥ 99%) was freshly dissolved in deionized water immediately before each experiment to prevent auto-oxidation [13]. Arsenic was applied as pentavalent arsenate As⁵⁺ from a certified 1000 mg L⁻¹ H₃AsO₄ standard in 2% HNO₃ (Merck KGaA, Darmstadt, Germany), diluted to 100 µM. Tissue arsenic was quantified by ICP-MS as total arsenic, without species discrimination.

PG (5 mM) and As⁵⁺ (100 µM) concentrations and exposure durations (6 h PG pre-treatment followed by 12 h As⁵⁺ exposure) were selected based on preliminary dose-response screening (PG range: 0.5–10 mM; n = 3) and previously published cereal stress protocols [14, 18].

Four treatment groups were established in five biological replicates (n = 5): Control (18 h deionized water); PG (6 h 5 mM PG → 12 h water); As (6 h water → 12 h 100 µM As⁵⁺); and PG + As (6 h 5 mM PG → 12 h 100 µM As⁵⁺). The sequential design separates the PG pre-conditioning phase from the subsequent PG–As⁵⁺ co-exposure phase. Shoots were then harvested, flash frozen in liquid nitrogen, and stored at − 20 °C until analysis.

Determination of oxidative stress parameters

The content of superoxide radicals (O₂•⁻) was estimated by monitoring the reduction of XTT [2,3-bis(2-methoxy-4-nitro-5-sulfophenyl)-5-(phenylamino)carbonyl-2 H-tetrazolium hydroxide], following the general procedure of [19] with slight modifications. Fresh leaf tissues (0.5 g) were cut into small fragments and vacuum-infiltrated for 2 h in darkness using 10 mM sodium citrate buffer (pH 7.0) containing 500 µM XTT. Parallel incubations were conducted in the presence or absence of SOD (3.5 U mL⁻¹) to distinguish the specific contribution of O₂•⁻. After incubation, the infiltrated solution was collected and its absorbance was measured spectrophotometrically at 470 nm. The difference in absorbance between SOD-treated and untreated samples was considered to represent the XTT reduction specifically attributable to superoxide. The corresponding content of O₂•⁻ was calculated using the molar extinction coefficient of 2.16 × 10⁴ M⁻¹ cm⁻¹ and expressed as nmol O₂•⁻ g⁻¹ FW.

For H₂O₂ determination, 1 mL of supernatant was mixed with 10 mM potassium phosphate buffer (pH 7.0) and 1 M potassium iodide (KI). The reaction mixture was incubated in darkness for 10 min at room temperature, and absorbance was measured at 390 nm against a blank without KI. H₂O₂ content was calculated using a standard curve prepared with known concentrations of H₂O₂ (0–100 µM) and expressed as µmol g⁻¹ fresh weight [20].

Lipid peroxidation was assessed by quantifying TBARS following the method of [21]. Fresh leaf tissue (0.1 g) was homogenized in 0.1% (w/v) trichloroacetic acid (TCA) and centrifuged at 10 000 g for 15 min at 4 °C. A 1 mL of the supernatant was mixed with 4 mL of 0.5% (w/v) thiobarbituric acid (TBA) prepared in 20% (w/v) TCA. The reaction mixture was heated at 95 °C for 30 min, then cooled on ice. Absorbance was measured at 532 nm with a 600 nm correction for non-specific turbidity. TBARS content was calculated using the following formula: ΔAbs (Abs532 - Abs600) = Ɛ×C×L (Ɛ = 155 mM⁻¹ cm⁻¹, L = 1 cm). Results were expressed as nmol g⁻¹ fresh weight.

As and mineral contents analysis by ICP-MS

Instrumentation

The concentrations of selected macro- and microelements, including Mg, K, Ca, Fe, Zn, Cu, Mn, Na, and As, were determined using an Agilent 7900 ICP-MS system (Agilent Technologies, USA). Instrumental parameters and operating conditions are provided in Table A1. Sample digestion was conducted using an Anton Paar microwave reaction system (Anton Paar GmbH, Austria) equipped with PTFE digestion vessels.

Reagents and solutions

All reagents were of analytical grade. Concentrated nitric acid (65%, Merck KGaA, Germany) and H2O2 (35%, Merck KGaA, Germany) were used for sample digestion. Multi-element calibration standards were prepared from certified reference materials to encompass the full concentration range of the target analytes.

Sample preparation and digestion

Seedling samples were oven-dried at 60 °C to constant weight and finely ground using a stainless-steel mill. Approximately 0.1 g of dried and homogenized plant material was accurately weighed into PTFE digestion vessels. Subsequently, 8 mL of concentrated HNO₃ and 1 mL of H₂O₂ were added to each vessel. Microwave-assisted digestion was carried out following a three-step program: ramp to 185 °C over 20 min, hold at 185 °C for 15 min, then cool to 60 °C. After digestion, samples were quantitatively transferred to volumetric flasks and diluted to 50 mL with ultrapure water.

Analytical determination

Elemental analyses were performed using an inductively coupled plasma mass spectrometer to quantify As and essential macro- and micronutrients in the digested samples. The instrument was calibrated with multi-element standard solutions prepared in the same acid matrix as the samples to minimize matrix mismatch and signal suppression. Daily performance verification included automated tuning and optimization according to the manufacturer’s protocol, with particular attention given to correction of matrix effects, double-charged ions, and oxide interferences. Analytical quality assurance and control were rigorously maintained by simultaneous analysis of certified reference materials (plant tissue standards; NIST SRM series) and procedural blanks with each analytical batch. All measurements were conducted in triplicate to ensure reproducibility. The elements determined included As, sodium (Na), magnesium (Mg), potassium (K), calcium (Ca), manganese (Mn), iron (Fe), zinc (Zn), and copper (Cu). Blank-corrected contents were calculated on a dry-weight basis and expressed as µg g⁻¹ of dry tissue.

Tissue PG quantification by HPLC-DAD

Chemical materials

HPLC-grade acetonitrile was obtained from Sigma-Aldrich (St. Louis, MO, USA) and HPLC-grade methanol from Merck KGaA (Darmstadt, Germany). All phenolic reference standards, including PG, were purchased from Sigma-Aldrich.

HPLC instrumentation and conditions

Chromatographic separation was performed using an ACE 5 C18 column (250 × 4.6 mm, 5 μm particle size; Advanced Chromatography Technologies, Aberdeen, UK). The mobile phase consisted of (A) methanol and (B) 1.5% (v/v) acetic acid in water. The gradient elution program was as follows: 0–29 min, 10% A / 90% B; 29–40 min, linear gradient to 60% A / 40% B; 40–53 min, linear gradient to 90% A / 10% B. The HPLC system (Agilent 1260 series) was equipped with a diode array detector (DAD) set to monitor wavelengths at 280, 290, 320, 370, and 535 nm, a quaternary pump operating at 0.7 mL min⁻¹, an autosampler with 10 µL injection volume, and a column oven maintained at 35 °C.

Standard solution preparation

Calibration curves for PG quantification were established using six standard concentrations: 25, 50, 75, 100, 200, and 300 µg mL⁻¹. Each standard solution was prepared in the same solvent system used for sample extraction and analyzed by HPLC-DAD under identical conditions.

Sample preparation and extraction

Sample extraction was performed using a two-step solvent system. First, acetonitrile and 1% (v/v) acetic acid were mixed in a 1:9 ratio (solution A). Then solution A was combined with methanol in a 1:1 ratio to produce solution B. For each sample, 5 g of dried plant material was mixed with 50 mL of solution B. The mixture was subjected to ultrasonic extraction for 30 min to enhance compound recovery, followed by dark incubation at room temperature for 24 h to ensure complete extraction. After incubation, extracts were filtered through Whatman No. 1 filter paper and stored at 4 °C until HPLC analysis. All extractions were performed in triplicate.

UV–visible spectrophotometric analysis of PG–As5+ interaction

A series of solutions with varying metal ion/ligand molar ratios (M/L) were prepared and absorption spectra were recorded to characterize PG–As⁵⁺ interaction and determine its stoichiometry using UV–Vis spectrophotometric titration. A stock As5+ solution at a concentration ten times that of the ligand was prepared. For each measurement, 2 mL of PG solution was added to individual tubes, followed by appropriate volumes of the stock As5⁺ solution to achieve increasing molar ratios in the range M/L = 0.0–10.0. The final volume of each solution was adjusted to 4 mL with deionised water.

All measurements were performed in quartz cuvettes with a 1 cm optical path length at 25 ± 1 °C. PG concentration was kept constant throughout, while As⁵⁺ concentration was incrementally increased. At each titration point, full UV–Vis absorption spectra were recorded over the 220–400 nm range and inspected for the appearance of new absorption bands, isosbestic points, or shifts in λmax that would be indicative of discrete coordination complex formation. Absorbance at 265 nm, the characteristic absorption maximum of PG, was recorded after each addition [22].

CHNS analysis

Total carbon (C), hydrogen (H), nitrogen (N), and sulfur (S) contents of wheat seedlings were determined using a Flash 2000 CHNS/O Elemental Analyzer (Thermo Scientific, USA). Dried plant samples were finely ground to ensure homogeneity, and approximately 10 mg of each sample was weighed into tin capsules using an analytical microbalance. The sealed capsules were loaded into the autosampler of the elemental analyzer.

Combustion was performed in an oxygen-rich environment at 950 °C, resulting in complete oxidation of organic matter. The combustion gases (CO₂, H₂O, N₂, and SO₂, corresponding to C, H, N, and S content, respectively) were carried by helium carrier gas through a reduction furnace to convert nitrogen oxides to N₂, and subsequently separated using a packed chromatographic column maintained at 65 °C. Detection was achieved using a thermal conductivity detector (TCD).

The instrument was calibrated using certified reference standards, including acetanilide (C₈H₉NO) for C, H, and N determination, and additional sulfur-containing standards for S analysis. Daily performance checks were conducted with known reference materials, and blank corrections were applied using empty tin capsules. Results were calculated against calibration curves and expressed as percentage of dry weight (% DW) for each element [23].

Determination of proline content

Proline content was quantified spectrophotometrically using a modified acid ninhydrin method as described by [24]. Fresh leaf tissue (0.1 g) was homogenized in 1.8 mL of 40% (v/v) ethanol and incubated at 4 °C overnight to allow complete extraction. The homogenate was centrifuged at 15 000 g for 15 min at 4 °C. A 1 mL aliquot of the clear supernatant was mixed with 2.0 mL of acidic ninhydrin reagent, which was freshly prepared by mixing glacial acetic acid and 6 M phosphoric acid in a 3:1 (v/v) ratio, followed by the addition of ninhydrin. The reaction mixture was incubated in a boiling water bath at 95 °C for 20 min to develop the characteristic red chromophore, then rapidly cooled on ice to terminate the reaction. Absorbance was measured at 520 nm against a reagent blank. Proline content was determined using a standard curve constructed with known L-proline concentrations ranging from 0 to 100 µM, and results were expressed as µmol g⁻¹ fresh weight.

Determination of antioxidant enzyme activities

Enzyme extraction

Fresh leaf tissue (0.5 g) was ground into fine powder in liquid nitrogen and homogenized in 5 mL of ice-cold extraction buffer containing 50 mM potassium phosphate buffer (pH 7.0), 1 mM ethylenediaminetetraacetic acid (EDTA), and 0.1% (w/v) polyvinylpolypyrrolidone (PVPP). The homogenate was centrifuged at 20 000 g for 20 min at 4 °C, and the supernatant was collected as the crude enzyme extract. All enzyme activities were normalized to total soluble protein content determined by the Bradford method.

Superoxide dismutase (SOD) activity

SOD activity (EC 1.15.1.1) was determined by monitoring the photochemical reduction of nitroblue tetrazolium (NBT) according to [25]. The reaction mixture (3 mL) contained 50 mM potassium phosphate buffer (pH 7.8), 0.1 mM EDTA, 13 mM L-methionine, 75 µM NBT, 2 µM riboflavin, and 50 µL enzyme extract. The reaction was initiated by exposing the mixture to white light at 375 µmol m⁻² s⁻¹ for 10 min at room temperature. Under illumination, riboflavin-generated superoxide radicals reduced NBT to formazan, producing a blue color. SOD activity was measured as the amount of enzyme required to inhibit NBT reduction by 50%. Absorbance was recorded at 560 nm against a dark control. SOD activity was calculated using the following formula: (Absorbance of blank - Absorbance of sample) ÷ Absorbance of blank ÷ (50% × reaction mixture volume) ÷ protein content in the enzyme extract.

Catalase (CAT) activity

CAT activity (EC 1.11.1.6) was measured following [26] by monitoring the decomposition of H₂O₂. The reaction mixture (3 mL) contained 50 mM potassium phosphate buffer (pH 7.0) and 30 mM H₂O₂, and 50 µL enzyme extract. The reaction was initiated by adding the enzyme extract, and the decrease in absorbance at 240 nm was monitored for 1 min at 25 °C. CAT activity was calculated using the extinction coefficient of 39.4 mM⁻¹ cm⁻¹ and expressed as µmol H₂O₂ decomposed min⁻¹ mg⁻¹ protein.

Ascorbate peroxidase (APX) activity

APX activity (EC 1.11.1.11) was determined according to [27]. The reaction mixture (1 mL) contained 50 mM potassium phosphate buffer (pH 7.0), 0.25 mM ascorbate, 5 mM H₂O₂, and 20 µL enzyme extract. The reaction was initiated by adding H₂O₂, and the decrease in absorbance at 290 nm due to ascorbate oxidation was recorded for 1 min at 25 °C. APX activity was calculated using the extinction coefficient of 2.8 mM⁻¹ cm⁻¹ and expressed as µmol ascorbate oxidized min⁻¹ mg⁻¹ protein.

Protein content

Protein content in enzyme extracts was determined according to [28]. Enzyme extracts were mixed with Coomassie Brilliant Blue G-250 dye reagent, and absorbance was measured at 595 nm. Protein levels were calculated using a standard curve prepared with bovine serum albumin (BSA) (0–100 µg mL⁻¹) and expressed as mg mL⁻¹.

In silico molecular docking analysis

The three-dimensional structures of selected compounds were obtained from the PubChem database, and energy minimization was performed to stabilize each conformation before docking. The crystal structure of recombinant APX (PDB ID: 1APX) was retrieved from the RCSB Protein Data Bank. Prior to docking, the protein structure was refined by removing crystallographic water molecules and non-relevant ligands, while polar hydrogens were added and Gasteiger partial charges were assigned using AutoDock Tools (v1.5.7).

Docking experiments were carried out using AutoDock Vina [29]. The search grid was defined to encompass the enzyme’s active site. For each ligand, multiple binding poses were generated, and the conformation with the lowest binding energy (kcal mol⁻¹) was selected for further analysis. The top-ranked docking poses were further analyzed with Molegro Virtual Docker (MVD) for hydrogen bonding, electrostatic contributions, and cavity-fitting parameters [30].

Statistical analysis

All experiments used five independent biological replicates (n = 5), with each biochemical assay performed in three technical replicates per biological replicate; HPLC-DAD measurements were performed as single injections with reproducibility ensured by a multi-point calibration curve (R² ≥ 0.99). Results are expressed as means ± standard deviation (SD) of the five biological replicates.

The experimental design is a 2 × 2 factorial (PG × As), and data were therefore analyzed by two-way ANOVA with PG (± 5 mM) and As⁵⁺ (± 100 µM) as fixed factors. This model estimates the main effect of PG, the main effect of As⁵⁺, and the PG–As⁵⁺ interaction effect simultaneously, which is essential for testing the study’s central hypothesis of interaction rather than additive action. Where the interaction term was significant (p < 0.05), group means were compared post hoc using Duncan’s multiple range test.

Pearson correlation analysis between tissue PG content and biochemical parameters was performed on individual biological replicate values (n = 5 per group; total n = 20). Principal component analysis (PCA) was conducted on all biochemical parameters with variables scaled to unit variance. All analyses were performed in IBM SPSS Statistics v27 and R v4.5.3.

Results

Oxidative stress parameters

All treatments resulted in elevated oxidative stress markers relative to the control (Fig. 1). Superoxide radical (O₂•⁻) production increased progressively across all treatment groups, reaching 2.13-fold above control under PG alone and 3-fold under As alone. The combined PG + As treatment produced the greatest elevation, with O₂•⁻ levels reaching 5.10-fold above control (17.70 ± 0.78), representing a 1.7-fold increase over As alone. H₂O₂ levels increased significantly under all three stress conditions relative to control (1.46–1.52-fold; p < 0.05) but did not differ significantly among PG, As, and PG + As treatments (p > 0.05). The two-way ANOVA, however, revealed a significant PG–As⁵⁺ interaction effect (F = 18.3, p < 0.001), reflecting a saturating rather than additive response, in which combined exposure produced an H₂O₂ level (0.533 ± 0.027) markedly below the value expected under simple additivity (~ 0.675). TBARS levels showed a treatment-dependent increase, with PG alone producing only a marginal elevation (1.04-fold), As alone a moderate increase (1.20-fold), and the combined PG + As treatment the greatest increase at 1.72-fold above control (6.42 ± 0.09), representing a 1.43-fold increase over As alone (Fig. 1 ).

Arsenic accumulation

As shown in Table 1, arsenic accumulation differed markedly among treatments. Control and PG-treated tissues contained only background levels of As (0.012 ± 0.002 and 0.015 ± 0.007 mg kg⁻¹ DW, respectively), consistent with the absence of arsenic in the exposure medium. As treatment caused a pronounced rise in tissue As content to 39.29 ± 0.5 mg kg⁻¹ DW, while the combined PG + As treatment produced the highest accumulation at 47.50 ± 0.9 mg kg⁻¹ DW, corresponding to an approximately 21% increase over As alone (p < 0.05).

Tissue PG content

Compared with the control, exogenous PG treatment significantly increased tissue PG content, corresponding to a 1.65-fold increase (from 1.97 to 3.25 ppm). In contrast, As treatment alone markedly reduced tissue PG content to 0.81 ppm, representing a 59% reduction relative to the control (Table 2). The combined PG + As treatment resulted in the highest tissue PG level (7.25 ppm), corresponding to an 8.95-fold increase over As alone (p < 0.05).

UV–vis titration and PG–As5+ interaction

The interaction between PG and As⁵⁺ was investigated by UV–Vis spectrophotometric titration. All experiments were conducted using a certified H₃AsO₄ standard prepared in 0.5 mol L⁻¹ nitric acid (Merck KGaA, Darmstadt, Germany), ensuring that arsenic was maintained in a chemically well-defined and stable form throughout all experimental conditions. The absorption spectrum of pure PG exhibited a characteristic maximum at 265 nm, which decreased progressively upon incremental addition of As⁵⁺ (Fig. 2A).

UV–Vis spectrophotometric titration of pyrogallol with As5+. A Absorption spectra of PG at increasing As5+/PG molar ratios. B Molar ratio plot showing absorbance at 265 nm as a function of [As5+]/ [PG]

Absorbance values at 265 nm were plotted as a function of the [As⁵⁺]/[PG] molar ratio (Fig. 2B). The absorbance decreased markedly at low molar ratios and approached a plateau near equimolar conditions (M/L ≈ 1.0).

Mineral balance

Detached wheat shoot tissues exposed to PG, As, or their combination exhibited pronounced, treatment-specific disruptions in mineral nutrient balance (Tables 3 and 4). Among the macro-elements, Ca content decreased slightly under PG and As compared with the control but increased markedly under the combined PG + As treatment, reaching approximately 10% above the control and 17% above As alone. K content declined to approximately 77% and 81% of control under PG and As, respectively, and further to 71% under PG + As. Mg declined under all treatments, reaching its lowest level under PG + As at approximately 70% of control.

Discussion

The present study provides biochemical evidence suggesting that pyrogallol (PG), commonly regarded as an antioxidant plant phenolic, may operate as a redox-active pro-oxidant under combined arsenic stress in detached wheat shoot tissues, amplifying rather than alleviating As-induced biochemical damage. This interpretation is supported by three converging lines of evidence: (i) a 21% increase in tissue As accumulation under PG + As compared with As alone, consistent with UV–Vis spectrophotometric evidence of a PG–As5+ interaction; (ii) a coordinated suppression of all three antioxidant enzymes (SOD, CAT, APX) under combined exposure, despite their substantial upregulation under either stressor alone; and (iii) a significant PG–As5+ interaction effect detected by two-way ANOVA for 16 of 19 measured parameters, providing quantitative support for interaction rather than additive action.

UV–Vis spectrophotometric titration provided preliminary spectroscopic evidence of PG–As5+ interaction under defined aqueous conditions, with the molar ratio plot showing a saturation-like pattern near equimolar conditions. However, the absence of isosbestic points or λmax shifts precludes definitive conclusions regarding complex stoichiometry or formation in planta. The convergence between this chemical-interaction evidence and elevated tissue As levels nonetheless suggests that PG may enhance As tissue-level accumulation specifically when external arsenic is present, rather than functioning as a generalized uptake stimulant [14]. A closely related study in maize seedlings using an analogous detached-shoot protocol reported a substantially greater (5-fold) increase in As accumulation under combined PG + As treatment [14], suggesting that the magnitude of PG-associated As mobilization may differ between cereal species.

The antioxidant enzyme response showed a clear biphasic pattern. Both PG and As individually stimulated SOD, CAT, and APX activities by approximately three- to fourfold, reflecting a classical upregulation of ROS-detoxifying enzymes in response to moderate oxidative challenge [31, 32]. Under PG + As co-treatment, all three enzymes underwent simultaneous and substantial suppression, with SOD declining 6.5-fold relative to As alone, and CAT and APX decreasing 2.7- and 4.1-fold, respectively. This coordinated decline suggests that a critical redox threshold had been exceeded, beyond which the antioxidant machinery was no longer capable of adaptive compensation. Three possible contributing factors may underlie this response. First, Zn depletion may impair Cu/Zn-SOD by reducing cofactor availability [33], though isoform-specific SOD activity was not measured in this study. Second, elevated H2O2 levels can inactivate catalase through compound II formation [34], and lipid peroxidation products can covalently modify critical active-site residues in antioxidant enzymes [35]. Third, an in silico docking analysis examined whether PG could interact with the APX catalytic pocket, APX was selected because pyrogallol’s polyhydroxylated aromatic core resembles ascorbate, its natural substrate. The analysis generated predicted poses with a binding energy of − 5.5 kcal/mol and an estimated Ki of 136 µM against the Pisum sativum APX crystal structure (PDB 1APX; used as a structural proxy given high APX conservation across plants, including wheat). The predicted pose included hydrogen bonds and non-covalent contacts with key active-site residues (Table 7; Fig. 5), resembling binding modes reported for related polyhydroxylated phenolics [36, 37]; however, these predictions reflect plausible binding geometry rather than direct evidence of enzyme inhibition, and require kinetic validation with purified wheat APX. This enzyme-suppression pattern is directly consistent with findings in detached maize shoot tissues, where PG or As alone elevated all three enzymes whereas their combination caused marked suppression (SOD 4.1-fold, CAT 1.9-fold, APX 4.3-fold relative to PG or As alone) [14]. Collectively, the critical antioxidant-defense threshold appears to be reached only under combined PG + As exposure.

The oxidative stress profile that emerged from combined PG + As exposure was non-classical in its ROS distribution. Rather than showing proportional accumulation of all ROS species, the treatment induced a 5.1-fold increase in O2•⁻ generation above control while H2O2 accumulated only moderately (1.52-fold) and TBARS levels increased by a similarly restrained 1.72-fold. This decoupling suggests that H2O2 accumulation may be constrained by alternative consumption pathways beyond classical enzymatic dismutation. This pattern of saturating H2O2 accumulation is reinforced by the post-hoc results: although PG, As, and combined PG + As treatments all elevated H2O2 significantly above control (p < 0.05), they were statistically indistinguishable from one another, indicating that H2O2 levels reached a common upper limit irrespective of stress identity. At the same time, the two-way ANOVA detected a significant PG–As5+ interaction effect (F = 18.3, p < 0.001), but the direction of this interaction was sub-additive: under simple additivity of the two main effects, H2O2 in PG + As tissues would be expected to reach approximately 0.68 nmol g⁻¹ FW, whereas the observed value (0.533 ± 0.027) was substantially lower. This dissociation between the H2O2 ceiling and the progressive increases in upstream O2•⁻ and downstream TBARS is consistent with rapid consumption of H2O2 at its site of generation rather than a passive plateau. Plausible consumption routes include Fenton-type chemistry or peroxidase-mediated turnover [38–41]. The progressive TBARS elevation further supports active downstream radical generation rather than steady-state H2O2 pool expansion.

Proline accumulation increased progressively across treatments, peaking at 1.96-fold of control under PG + As. Under single stress, such accumulation likely reflects adaptive osmotic and redox buffering [42, 43]; however, under combined exposure, given concurrent enzyme suppression and persistently elevated oxidative damage, proline is more consistent with a stress marker than effective defense. This pattern parallels earlier observations in maize, where progressive proline accumulation similarly accompanied elevated TBARS and H2O2 under PG + As [14].

The tissue PG content data further illustrate the complexity of phenolic behavior under combined exposure. While As alone was associated with reduced tissue PG content (0.81 ppm), likely reflecting arsenic-induced suppression of phenolic metabolism [44], the combined PG + As treatment was associated with markedly elevated tissue PG levels (7.25 ppm). Because exogenous PG was applied directly to shoot tissues in this study, the measured tissue PG content likely includes both absorbed exogenous and endogenously produced phenolics; these pools were not separated analytically. Pearson correlation analysis (n = 20) revealed significant positive associations between tissue PG content and TBARS (r = 0.84, p < 0.01) and superoxide production (r = 0.76, p < 0.05), and a significant negative association with SOD activity (r = − 0.65, p < 0.05). The positive association between tissue PG content and TBARS is consistent with evidence that polyhydroxylated phenolics exhibit pro-oxidant activity in the presence of transition metals [45, 46], consistent with PG-mediated amplification of As stress.

The mineral and elemental data collectively reinforce this picture of tissue-level disruption. The pronounced depletion of Fe, Zn, and Mn under PG + As treatment is consistent with chelation-mediated competition for essential transition metals by phenolic ligands [47–49], with the PG–As5+ interaction for Zn highly significant (F = 53,828; p < 0.001). The severe Zn depletion, falling to approximately 58% of control under PG + As, plausibly contributes to the observed decline in total SOD activity given Zn’s established role as an essential cofactor for Cu/Zn-SOD [50]; however, as total rather than isoform-specific SOD activity was measured, a direct causal relationship cannot be confirmed from the present data alone. The antagonistic Ca–K pattern, elevated Ca coinciding with K depletion, is consistent with ROS-mediated activation of Ca2+-permeable channels alongside stimulation of outward-rectifying K+ channels [51–53], with the approximately 25% K+ loss aligning with earlier reports in stress-sensitive wheat cultivars [54]. The concurrent depletion of Na under PG + As, given Na’s partial osmotic compensation role in C3 cereals [55, 56], suggests that simultaneous loss of both K and Na may impair cation-retention capacity and contribute to osmotic instability. The disproportionate decline of Fe relative to Mn shifted the Mn/Fe ratio, consistent with preferential As-mediated impairment of Fe acquisition while Mn uptake via NRAMP transporters remains comparatively less affected [54, 57].

At the elemental level, the C: N ratio decreased progressively (0.92-, 0.90-, 0.82-fold of control under PG, As, PG + As). The concurrent C and H decline alongside a relative N increase is consistent with stress-induced shifts in carbon- and hydrogen-rich biomolecules, particularly membrane lipids. The PG–As5+ interaction reached significance for H content (F = 9.7, p = 0.007), directionally consistent with peroxidation-mediated loss of hydrogen-rich membrane components, but was non-significant for C, N, and the C: N ratio, indicating primarily additive effects on carbon–nitrogen metabolism [58]. Sulfur was detected only in PG-containing treatments, while remaining below quantification in control and As-only tissues; the biological significance of this signal remains unclear and warrants dedicated sulfur-metabolite analysis.

Principal component analysis captured the multivariate signature of treatment groups, with the first two principal components collectively explaining 90.8% of total variance (PC1: 52.9%; PC2: 37.9%). The PG + As group separated distinctly from all others along PC1, driven by stress-amplifying variables (tissue PG, TBARS, O2•⁻, As accumulation) and concurrent antioxidant enzyme suppression (SOD, CAT, APX). This converges with the univariate ANOVA: the 16 parameters showing significant PG–As5+ interaction carry the strongest PC1 loadings, supporting a qualitatively distinct biochemical state under combined exposure. The As and PG single-stress groups showed partial overlap along PC1, consistent with broadly similar single-stress biochemical profiles.

Several limitations frame these findings. First, the detached-shoot protocol isolates tissue-level PG–As interactions but excludes root-based regulatory barriers (phytochelatin sequestration, xylem-loading dynamics) [59–63]; tissue viability across the 18-h window is supported by previously reported Fv/Fm values of 0.80–0.85 under comparable conditions [14]. Second, single concentrations of PG (5 mM) and As5+ (100 µM) preclude dose–response characterization, and total ICP-MS quantification of As does not resolve As5+/As3+ partitioning, which would require speciation methods (HPLC-ICP-MS, XANES). Third, the PG–As5+ and PG–APX interactions were characterized under simplified chemical and in silico conditions; in planta validation through enzyme kinetics with purified wheat APX, and direct detection of hydroxyl radicals and redox-active intermediates, which were not measured in this study, are required to confirm the proposed mechanistic interpretations. Fourth, only total SOD activity and biochemical endpoints were measured; isoform-specific SOD activity and transcriptomic/proteomic validation, alongside correlation analyses (n = 20) presented here as exploratory, represent priorities for future work.

Despite these constraints, a consistent pattern emerges across the integrated dataset: combined PG + As treatment produced only a modest (~ 21%) increase in tissue As accumulation yet resulted in a 6.5-fold suppression of SOD activity, a 5.1-fold elevation of superoxide radical production, depletion of Zn to approximately 58% of control, and a 0.82-fold C: N ratio (18% decrease), a constellation of disruptions not observed under either stressor alone. Whereas pro-oxidant phenolics such as cinnamic acid [64] and caffeic acid [65] applied as single stressors induce moderate upregulation of antioxidant enzymes as an initial adaptive response, and protective phenolics such as hesperidin and chlorogenic acid actively alleviate arsenic-induced oxidative damage [66], PG appears to occupy a distinct position, in which co-application with arsenic not only fails to protect but amplifies metalloid toxicity through concurrent enzyme suppression, arsenic mobilization, and disruption of mineral balance. Notably, the significant PG–As⁵⁺ interaction was restricted to parameters involving redox and mineral-homeostasis pathways rather than affecting plant metabolism globally, indicating that PG’s pro-oxidant action converges with As⁵⁺ toxicity specifically at the redox interface. Collectively, these observations are consistent with a model in which PG functions as a chemical and redox sensitizer that amplifies arsenic phytotoxicity through converging mechanisms, including chelation-associated arsenic mobilization, the observed suppression of antioxidant enzyme function, and the putative contribution of autoxidation-generated ROS.

Conclusion

In conclusion, the present study provides evidence suggesting that pyrogallol (PG) may amplify arsenic phytotoxicity in detached shoot tissues of Triticum aestivum under acute controlled exposure conditions. The combined PG + As treatment produced the highest tissue As accumulation, increasing accumulation by approximately 21% relative to As alone, accompanied by substantially greater biochemical disruption: antioxidant enzyme activities (SOD, CAT, APX) declined substantially, oxidative damage markers were elevated, and mineral balance was disrupted with marked depletion of Zn, Fe, K, and Na. Two-way ANOVA confirmed a significant PG–As⁵⁺ interaction effect (p < 0.05) for 16 of the 19 measured parameters, providing quantitative support for interaction rather than additive effects. Pearson correlation analysis identified significant positive associations between tissue PG content and oxidative damage markers (TBARS, O₂•⁻) and a negative association with SOD activity, while principal component analysis confirmed the biochemical distinctiveness of the PG + As treatment group, collectively supporting a pro-oxidant role for PG under combined arsenic stress.

UV–Vis spectrophotometric titration provided preliminary chemical evidence for a direct PG–As⁵⁺ interaction under simplified aqueous conditions, supporting the chemical plausibility of PG–As⁵⁺ interaction at the tissue level, although direct confirmation under in planta conditions remains required. Molecular docking analysis using the Pisum sativum APX structure provided preliminary in silico evidence for a possible PG–APX interaction, though confirmation through enzyme kinetics assays using purified wheat APX is required. The observed Zn depletion suggests a possible association with the decline in SOD activity, although a direct causal relationship was not established.

These findings are consistent with the possibility that redox-active phenolics may intensify metalloid toxicity under co-exposure conditions in detached aerial plant tissues, and suggest that the assumption of uniformly protective antioxidant behavior for plant-derived phenolics warrants further examination. Validation in intact wheat seedlings under hydroponic and soil-based conditions, alongside dose-dependent investigations across growth stages, soil types, and cultivars with varying arsenic tolerance, is identified as an immediate priority. It is emphasized that these findings represent acute, tissue-level observations obtained from a detached shoot model, and that broader agronomic or ecotoxicological implications cannot be established without further validation in intact biological systems.
