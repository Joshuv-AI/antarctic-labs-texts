Facile construction of ZnMn2O4 nanosheets via alcohol solvothermal zinc ion-exchange reaction for high-performance lithium-ion batteries
Authors: Wenming Liao, Qingbo Xiao, Wanfei Li, Hongzhen Lin
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Ternary metal oxides are acknowledged as potential electrode materials for lithium-ion batteries on account of their conspicuous electrochemical properties. Regulating internal morphology structure of materials is conducive to achieving the best electrochemical performance. Among them, two-dimensional nano-metal oxides with high specific surface areas and excellent electronic conductivity have been actively developed. In this work, utilizing pleated manganese-based nanosheets as templates, ternary spinel-structured ZnMn2O4 nanosheets were successfully synthesized using a straightforward alcohol solvothermal zinc ion-exchange routine. When cycled at 500 mA g− 1, ZnMn2O4 nanosheets, about 1.1 mg cm− 2 loading weight in electrode, have a specific capacity of 321.48 mAh g− 1 at the 100th cycle, along with an impressive Coulombic efficiency of about 100%, which obviously exceeds that of MnO2. This is a great improvement on the electrochemical properties when zinc ions are introduced into manganese-based materials. Furthermore, ZnMn2O4 nanosheets, only 0.5 mg cm− 2 loading weight in electrode, own a superior discharge capacity of 1047 mAh g− 1 despite having undergone 200 cycles. Based on our researches, ZnMn2O4 nanosheets are identified as a high-specific-energy active material with exceptional lithium-storage performance and have potential practical value in LIBs.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-025-26226-3.

Introduction

Various electrochemical energy storage devices such as sodium-ion batteries1,2, ammonium-ion batteries3, aluminum-ion batteries4, zinc-ion batteries5–7, lithium-ion batteries8,9 are constantly developed to meet the ever-growing demands of social development. In recent decades, rechargeable lithium-ion batteries (LIBs) are generally exploited in many types of electrical equipment due to their superior specific energy and portability8–11. However, commercial LIBs encounter substantial constraints imposed by a low theoretical capacity (372 mAh g− 1) of traditional graphitized carbon materials. Many studies have exploited metal oxides having high specific capacities as alternatives12–14. Among them, spinel-type ZnMn2O4 crystals are recognized as potential alternative anode materials because of their eco-friendliness, good reversible Li-insertion ability and high specific capacity12,15–22. Zinc and manganese ions possess different potential positions21,22 and thus serve as a buffer matrix for each other, creating very good mutual synergy to buffer volume expansion, while improving electrical conductivity and Li+ ions transport capability12,23. Fan et al.24 successfully synthesized hierarchical porous ZnMn2O4 microspheres by using an ultrasonic spray pyrolysis technique. When cycled 200 times at 500 mA g− 1 in LIBs, ZnMn2O4 microspheres exhibited a superior capacity of 1044 mAh g− 1. The nanosheets, consisting of nanoparticles, were interconnected to build porous microspheres. This unique structure could not only be conducive to efficient electrolyte penetration to shorten the diffusion distance of Li+ ions, but also effectively tolerate volume variation. Additionally, several reports25–27 have shown that ternary metal oxide nanosheets have remarkable surface-to-volume ratios and efficient electron transport pathways, which have emerged as highly promising anode materials for LIBs.

Given the research results and reaction mechanism, we anticipated a facile approach used in the synthesis of ZnMn2O4 nanosheets as anodes in LIBs. However, no report has described the synthesis of ZnMn2O4 nanosheets for application in LIBs. In the past twenty years, metal ion-exchange methods have been regarded as good choices for preparing Zn-based ternary metal oxides by utilizing easily controlled metal compounds as metal ion reactive templates21,22,28–31. Yuan et al.21 exploited a convenient way of preparing ZnMn2O4 nanotubes by an alkaline aqueous zinc ion-exchange reaction at room-temperature. In this procedure, MnO2@CNTs served dual roles as structural templates and manganese sources, enabling Zn2+ ions to partially replace Mn4+ ions within the crystal lattice or to occupy Mn4+ ion vacancies while preserving the original morphology, which was explained as a reduction/ion-exchange/recrystallization reaction process21,22. We discussed the process of alcohol solvothermal zinc ion-exchange reaction in our previous work and revealed the lithium-storage electrochemical reaction mechanism of Zn2Ti3O8 crystals30,31. An acidic environment drove a complete zinc ion-exchange reaction to synthesize Zn2Ti3O812,31.

Herein, spinel structure ZnMn2O4 nanosheets were successfully fabricated by an alcohol solvothermal zinc ion-exchange way for the first time. First, pleated manganese compounds (MnOOH and manganese tartrate) nanosheets were synthesized in a polyvinylpyrrolidone (PVP) aqueous solution under the alkali condition. Subsequently, manganese compounds nanosheets were used as morphological structure template and manganese source, and zinc acetate served as the exchanged zinc ion sources to synthesize the spinel phase ZnMn2O4 nanosheets during the alcohol solvothermal reaction process. At 500 mA g− 1, the reversible capacity of ZnMn2O4 nanosheets in LIBs is achieved at 321.48 mAh g− 1 at the 100th cycle, which is obviously superior to that of MnO2 crystals. After the mass loading was controlled to 0.5 mg cm− 2 in electrode, ZnMn2O4 nanosheets could possess a more impressive capacity of 1047 mAh g− 1 through continuous 200 cycles. As such, a rational strategy is proposed to synthesize ZnMn2O4 nanosheets as high delivered specific-capacity anodes for use in LIBs.

Experimental

Materials synthesis

Synthesis of manganese compounds nanosheets

Manganese(II) acetate tetrahydrate (2.451 g, 0.01 mol) was dissolved in deionized water (250 mL). Following this, 1 g of polyvinyl pyrrolidone (PVP, K30) surfactant was introduced while vigorously stirring using magnetic or mechanical methods with a long agitator (approximately 5.0 cm in length) for 18 h. Subsequently, a sodium hydroxide aqueous solution (100 mL, 0.4 M) was poured into the above solution, resulting in the formation of dark-green manganese compounds precipitates under ambient conditions. The precipitates were gathered through repetitive centrifugation and washing with deionized water until the pH value became neutral. Ultimately, through simple dry treatment at 60 °C for 12 h, sticky brownish-black manganese compounds (containing a certain amount of moisture) were obtained.

Synthesis of ZnMn2O4 nanosheets

ZnMn2O4 nanosheets were synthesized by the alcohol solvothermal zinc ion-exchange way. This procedure was performed according to our previous reports30,31. In a typical procedure, 0.001 mol of manganese compounds (containing a certain amount of moisture) as a reactive template was uniformly dispersed into 30 mL of ethanol by vigorous stirring for 20 h with the assistance of intermittent ultrasonic treatment. Zinc acetate (0.0005 mol) in 30 mL of ethanol solution was uniformly mixed with the above suspension by high-speed and continuous stirring at 60 ℃ for 22 h. Whereafter, the resulting mixture was placed into a Teflon-lined autoclave (100 mL) to complete the solvothermal zinc ion-exchange reaction at a set oven temperature of 100 ℃ for 12 h. After that, the temperature was cooled to 40–50 ℃, and then the Teflon-lined autoclave was slowly opened. The liquid supernatant was removed, and then the brownish precipitates (Zn–Mn–O nanosheets) were collected using simple physical centrifugation. Furthermore, the sample was heated to 500 ℃ for keeping 2 h in air. Finally, highly crystalline spinel-phase ZnMn2O4 nanocrystals were obtained.

Materials characterization

Field Emission Scan Electron Microscopy (FESEM; Quanta FEG 250) was applied to observe the nanostructure of main samples, including their morphologies and sizes. For a more in-depth analysis, the morphology and lattice fringes of ZnMn2O4 nanosheets were closely examined using Transmission Electron Microscopy (TEM; Tecnai G2 F20). The elemental composition in ZnMn2O4 nanosheets was determined by Energy-Dispersive X-ray Spectroscopy (EDS) coupled with the FESEM. Crystal structures were identified through X-ray diffraction (XRD; D8-Focus) using Cu Kα radiation and scanned over a 2θ range of 15º to 80º. The chemical states of the elements were explored by X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy (XPS; Escalab 250Xi) with a detected surface depth of several nm (less than 10 nm). Nitrogen adsorption–desorption isotherms of ZnMn2O4 nanosheets were collected at 77 K by a BSD surface area and porosity analyzer. Through Brunauer-Emmett-Teller (BET) method and Barrett-Joyner-Halenda (BJH) model, the specific surface area, size distribution and pore volume of ZnMn2O4 nanosheets was calculated.

Electrochemical tests

ZnMn2O4 nanosheets, carbon black, and polyvinylidene fluoride were evenly ground at a mass ratio of 7:2:1. After dropping N-methyl-2-pyrrolidinone (NMP), the mixture was constantly ground to obtain a uniform slurry. Then, using a scraper with an even gap, the slurry was spread on a thin battery-grade copper film. After vacuum drying at 80 ℃ for 12 h, the NMP solvent was removed to obtain the electrode, which was cut into 1.5 cm discs in diameter. The disc working electrodes and Celgard 2400 separator were infiltrated by commercial electrolyte (1.0 M LiPF6 in ethylene carbonate/diethyl carbonate, 1:1 v/v), and battery-grade lithium foil was placed on another side of the separator. Note that all the coin-type LIBs were fabricated in a vacuum glove box that was filled with pure argon. After being shelved for several hours, battery testers were used to measure the electrochemical performance of the samples using a galvanostatic test way (voltage range: 0.01–3.0 V). Cyclic voltammograms (CVs, scan rate: 0.10 mV s−1, potential range: 0.01–3.0 V) and electrochemical impedance spectroscopy (EIS, frequency range: 100 kHz–10 mHz, amplitude: 5 mV) of the assembled cells were performed on an electrochemical station (CHI).

Results and discussion

A schematic diagram of the possible formation procedure of ZnMn2O4 nanosheets is illustrated in Fig. 1a. Anisotropic growth leads to the development of two-dimensional nanosheets of manganese compounds. Sodium hydroxide aqueous solution was poured into the above solution to quickly transform into a highly alkaline solution, which inhibited a stable formation process from nanoparticles to agglomerates of manganese compounds. NaOH not only acts as a reaction agent to form manganese compounds but also boosts the assembly of nanosheet structures because the growth of different crystal facets is influenced by different ligand-surface binding energies, i.e. it drives a 3D nanocrystal to transform into a 2D structure32. Because manganese ions coordinated with -N and/or C = O functional groups17,18,33,34, PVP surfactant might drive stable morphological evolution by a microscopic interface reaction. Notably, under rapid stirring using a long agitator, a small amount of dissolved oxygen promotes the formation of MnOOH and manganese tartrate nanosheets rather than simple Mn3O4 particles (see Figure S1 and Figure S2 in the Supplementary Material). Moreover, instantaneous burst air bubbles may also drive the formation of nanosheets. Consequently, there are three main technical factors leading to the formation of nanosheets of precursors: a long agitator, high-speed stirring, and rapid pouring of a sodium hydroxide solution. A representative SEM image revealing the distinct morphological characteristics of the as-synthesized manganese-based nanosheets (ultrathin thickness) is presented Fig. 1b, revealing potential two-dimensional material properties for use in electrochemical energy storage. EDS analysis confirms that the precursor nanosheets mainly contain two elements—manganese and oxygen (Figure S3a and Table S1).

SEM image in Fig. 1c reveals the well-defined two-dimensional morphology of the as-synthesized Zn–Mn–O with several micrometers in length and several nanometers in thickness after a zinc ion-exchange solvothermal reaction. EDS analysis in Figure S3b and Table S1 shows that the Zn/Mn mole ratio in Zn–Mn–O nanosheets is close to 1:2, which is consistent with the stoichiometry of ZnMn2O4. The higher outer surface facilitates the adsorption of Zn2+ ions on the pleated manganese-based nanosheets. Dissolved Zn2+ ions in ethanol could efficiently penetrate into manganese-based nanosheets due to the lower surface tension of the ethanol solvent (22.3 mN, 20 ℃) and little tendency of the hydrated metal ions35. During the zinc ion-exchange reaction process, zinc acetate can react with manganese compounds to form trace amounts of acids, thereby leading to the synthesis of Zn‒Mn‒O nanosheets by a complete dissolution‒regeneration/recrystallization process12,31. Through a simple high-temperature calcination process, high-purity spinel I41/amd ZnMn2O4 nanosheets are obtained, but the morphology collapses slightly (Fig. 1d). Note that the sintered ZnMn2O4 nanosheets easily appear an agglomerated state because of their high specific surface area and surface energy. Elemental mapping images shows that Zn, Mn and O elements are determined as uniform distribution in the ZnMn2O4 nanosheets (Figure S4). TEM image (Fig. 1e) reveals that ZnMn2O4 has a two-dimensional nanosheet structure that is approximately a few nanometers in thickness. High-resolution TEM (HR-TEM) image (Fig. 1f) displays 0.25 nm and 0.49 nm lattice fringes, which correspond to the (211) and (101) planes of tetragonal ZnMn2O4 (I41/amd, No. 141) crystals, respectively.

When the temperature of solvothermal zinc ion-exchange reaction was further increased to 150 ℃, the nanosheets are transformed into many particles (Figure S5a). Therefore, a higher solvothermal reaction temperature results in the deformation of morphology. EDS analysis (Figure S5b, c) reveals that the particles contain three elements of zinc, manganese and oxygen, and the Zn/Mn mole ratio is approximately 1:2.

XRD patterns were recorded to analyze the crystal structure evolution induced by the zinc ion-exchange reaction. For manganese compounds nanosheets, as depicted in Figure 2a(I), the 2θ diffraction peaks at 18.3°, 19.18°, 24.79°, 25.22°, 34.18°, 36.71°, 49.69°, 52.97°, and 66.81° can be indexed to the (200), (011), (121), (130), (231), (330), (440), (402) and (631) crystal planes of manganese tartrate crystal (JCPDS card No. 38–0910). Additionally, a dominant 2θ diffraction peak at 19.195° is ascribed to the (002) crystal plane of MnOOH crystal (JCPDS card No. 18–0804). The sharp and well-defined diffraction peaks indicate good crystallinity of the as-synthesized precursor. After alcohol solvothermal zinc ion-exchange treatment and then calcination, an obvious shift of 2θ diffraction peaks is observed (Figure 2a(II)), which demonstrates the formation of tetragonal I41/amd(141)-type ZnMn2O4 crystals (JCPDS card No. 24–1133). This implies that zinc ions are successfully embedded into the crystal lattice of manganese compounds to form a new and perfect crystal structure. The crystal model of tetragonal I41/amd(141)-type ZnMn2O4 (a=b=5.8183 Å, c=9.4071 Å) is illustrated in Figure 2b. The manganese and zinc atoms are situated in the cores of [MnO6] octahedra and [ZnO4] tetrahedra in ZnMn2O4 crystal, respectively, whereas the oxygen atoms are located at the corners of the octahedra and tetrahedra. In comparison, when the manganese compounds nanosheets were calcined in air, the obtained material is tetragonal MnO2 nanocrystals. As evidenced by the XRD curve (Figure S6), the 2θ diffraction peaks well coincide with the tetragonal I4/m(87)-type MnO2 crystal planes (JCPDS card No. 44–0141).

XPS survey spectrum (Fig. 2c) confirms that zinc, manganese and oxygen are presented in the material (ZnMn2O4 nanosheets). The carbon species in the XPS devices result in a distinct carbon peak. For the Zn 2p spectra (Fig. 2 d), two distinct peaks at 1044.7 eV and 1021.6 eV are attributed to Zn 2p1/2 and Zn 2p3/2, respectively, and a 23.1 eV difference of peak energy reveals Zn2+ in the lattice of ZnMn2O4 crystals21,22. Similarly, two characteristic peaks at 653.4 eV (Mn2p1/2) and 641.3 eV (Mn2p3/2) are observed in the Mn 2p spectrum (Fig. 2e), which is well consistent with ZnMn2O4 crystals20,21.

The nitrogen adsorption‒desorption isotherms of ternary ZnMn2O4 nanosheets exhibit characteristic IV isotherms (Fig. 3a). A distinct H3-type hysteresis loop is detected at a relative pressure (P/P₀) range of 0.8‒1.0, which indicates the presence of slit-like pores formed by the aggregation of plate-like particles. The BET specific surface area is calculated to be 46.89 m2 g− 1, and the cumulative pore volume for BJH desorption is determined to be 0.2794 cm3 g− 1. In Fig. 3b, the BJH desorption pore size distribution curve reflects a sharp primary peak of 23.38 nm and some satellite peaks. Furthermore, the average pore size of 18.81 nm calculated by the BJH method, confirms a mesoporous characteristic in ZnMn2O4 nanosheets.

The lithium-storage electrochemical properties of ZnMn2O4 and MnO2 (approximately 1.1 mg cm− 2 mass loading in electrode) were compared by using Neware battery tests. To activate the coin-type LIBs, the current densities were fixed at 500 mA g− 1 for discharge and 50 mA g− 1 for charge during the initial ten cycles. As shown in Fig. 4a, during the 1 st cycle, the capacities of ZnMn2O4 are clearly greater than those of MnO2, and a pair of discharge‒charge voltage plateaus appear at approximately 0.3 V and 1.25 V, respectively. After 10 cycles, all the current densities are switched at 500 mA g− 1 (Fig. 4b). Through continuous 100 cycles, ZnMn2O4 nanosheets have a higher capacity of 321.48 mAh g− 1. However, MnO2 crystals only release a low capacity of 33.36 mAh g− 1. Therefore, zinc and manganese ions with different Li-insertion potential positions21,22 could act as cushions for one another to alleviate volume expansion and increase the electrical conductivity and Li+ ion transport capability, synergistically improving their delivered specific capacity12,23. The first Coulombic efficiency of ZnMn2O4 is 42.86%, which is higher than that of the MnO2 electrode (30.79%) (Fig. 4c). Both solid electrolyte interface (SEI) formation and irreversible lithium intercalation into the framework result in big capacity loss14,21,22,24. To the 11th cycle, the Coulombic efficiency has a significant cliff descent when the charge current density is adjusted to 500 mA g− 1. Through many cycles, all the Coulombic efficiencies approach 100%. The porous 2D nanostructure could provide a large interface to increase electron transfer and Li-ion transportation capability to improve their Li-insertion electrochemical performance. EIS analyses (Fig. 4d) show that the correlation diameter of the semicircle in the Nyquist plots of ZnMn2O4 electrodes after 100 cycles is significantly lower than that at the initial open-circuit voltage, manifesting improved electrode‒electrolyte contact and faster charge transfer kinetics through electrochemical milling36.

The electrochemical dynamics performance of ZnMn2O4 nanosheets electrodes was further evaluated by using Land battery tests when the loading weight was only 0.5 mg cm− 2 (Fig. 5a). The initial ten cycles are mainly used for electrochemical activation, and the current densities are set at 500 mA g− 1 for discharge and 100 mA g− 1 for charge. The 1 st discharge specific capacity could reach 880.6 mAh g− 1. The current densities are subsequently controlled at 500 mA g− 1 for charge and discharge. When the number of cycles reaches 67 times, the specific capacity gradually decreases to a low value of 443.1 mAh g− 1. During the following cycles, the capacity constantly increases. To the 200th cycle, the specific capacity of ZnMn2O4 nanosheets reaches 1047 mAh g− 1. It is mainly ascribed to the improved surface area caused by electrochemical milling and the possible reversible reaction of the polymeric gel-like film through the kinetically activated electrolyte degradation12,21,22. The test results are consistent with many literature reports in Table S2, far exceeding those of commercial graphite carbon materials, demonstrating that it has potential practical value in LIBs24. Such good electrochemical performance of the 2D ZnMn2O4 nanosheets is attributed to it having large surface-to-volume ratios, high electron transfer and good crystal structure. In addition, compared with the high loading mass in electrode, ZnMn2O4 nanosheets with a lower loading amount in electrode have longer cycling stability and better dynamic performance37. After 300 cycles, ZnMn2O4 nanosheets can still release a relatively high discharge specific capacity of 823.1 mAh g− 1. Note that except for the first few cycles, nearly 100% of the Coulombic efficiencies in the other cycles demonstrates its superior reversibility. Furthermore, the first and second charge‒discharge curves are depicted in Fig. 5b. According to the observation of the voltage curve at the first cycle, Mn3+ is reduced to Mn2+ at 1.25 V, whereas Mn0, Zn0 and Li‒Zn alloy should be generated at another discharge voltage platform of 0.41 V. However, at the second cycle, the discharge plateau of 1.25 V disappears, and only the discharge plateau of approximately 0.6 V can be observed, as described in previous reports18–21.

After the cells underwent 304 cycles at 500 mA g−1, rate tests were conducted to investigate the electrochemical kinetics by adjusting the test current densities. By changing the current density, ZnMn2O4 nanosheets can release approximately 1200 mAh g−1 (100 mA g−1), 1000 mAh g−1 (200 mA g−1), 600 mAh g−1 (500 mA g−1), 350 mAh g−1 (1000 mA g−1), 115 mAh g−1 (2000 mA g−1) and 38 mAh g−1 (5000 mA g−1), respectively, indicating a stepwise decrease in capacity (Fig. 5c). Notably, when the current density returns to 100 mA g−1, the ZnMn2O4 nanosheets can still release a superior specific capacity of 1260 mAh g−1, exhibiting a good cycle reversibility. Therefore, ZnMn2O4 nanosheets have high practical value for use in LIBs.

After multiple rate tests were performed, the current density was controlled at 500 mA g−1 for charge and discharge again. The following cyclic specific capacity reaches a steady state of approximately 650 mAh g−1 (Fig. 5c). The inherent electrochemical characteristics of ternary ZnMn2O4 nanosheets contribute to forming good electrochemical performance. Furthermore, a stable and tough SEI film containing a LiF protective layer on the surface of the electrode is formed after many cycles, which also contributes to improving the cycle stability and reversibility37–39. Figure 5d depicts the rate charge‒discharge voltage curves of ZnMn2O4 nanosheets. With increasing current density, the reversible capacities drop gradually, and the charge − discharge voltage platform space increases gradually, indicating increased voltage polarization. In particular, during high-rate cycles, the Li+ ion insertion/extraction electrochemical reaction may not have enough time to reach reaction equilibrium or complete Li+ ion diffusion to form a large concentration polarization and ohmic impedance, which only occurs at the interface of electrodes, thereby resulting in poor capacity.

TEM was used to further analyze the morphological and structural evolution process of ZnMn2O4 nanosheets electrodes during repeated discharge-charge processes. After 69 cycles, although the morphology structure of ZnMn2O4 have changed obviously, there are some nanosheet-like substances observed by TEM (Fig. 6a). When the electrode is further cycled to 105 times, all the nanosheets have obvious expansion, but the sheet-like substances are still linked together (Fig. 6b). Notably, better electrolyte wettability caused by electrochemical milling, while maintaining a stable nanosheet-like structure, may boost better electrochemical performance, as discussed in the above EIS analyses36. Therefore, the 2D ZnMn2O4 nanosheets, having remarkable surface-to-volume ratios, can effectively restrain the volume expansion, increase electrolyte wettability and slow down the pulverization process during the repeated cycles, thereby improving the lithium-storage electrochemical performance.

Conclusions

In summary, the spinel structure I41/amd ZnMn2O4 nanosheets were successfully synthesized through a solvothermal zinc ion-exchange reaction based on pleated manganese compounds nanosheets as a template and then calcination. Remarkably, the formation of manganese compounds nanosheets precursor was ascribed to the use of a long agitator for high-speed stirring, and the rapid pouring of sodium hydroxide solution. The morphological structure of the precursor laid the foundation for the subsequent generation of ZnMn2O4 nanosheets. Note that it still has a nanosheet structure after high-temperature calcination. When evaluated at 500 mA g− 1, the specific capacity of ZnMn2O4 nanosheets achieved 321.48 mAh g− 1, exceeding that of MnO2. It was a great influence in promoting the Li-insertion electrochemical performance in the LIBs because of the introduction of zinc ions. When the loading weight of ZnMn2O4 nanosheets was controlled at 0.5 mg cm− 2, the specific capacity reached 1047 mAh g− 1 after 200 cycles. TEM analyses indicated that 2D nanosheet structure was conducive to promoting the electrochemical performance of materials. Building on these fundamental studies and insights, we successfully develop a novel routine to synthesize ZnMn2O4 nanosheets, which have a greater practical value in high-performance LIBs.
