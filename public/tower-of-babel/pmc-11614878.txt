Binary composite crossover genetic algorithm for locating critical slip surface
Authors: Wei Qin, Jiancheng Zhao
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractSolving slope stability problems requires determining the critical slip surface (CSS) of a slope and its corresponding minimum factor of safety (Min. F), and determining the CSS is a complex optimisation problem. In this paper, we propose a real-coded binary composite crossover genetic algorithm (RGA-BCC) to locate the CSSs of slopes. A reasonable combination of parameters for the binary composite crossover (BCC) operator in the RGA-BCC is determined through six benchmark function experiments. On this basis, the stability of five soil slopes from the literature was analysed using the RGA-BCC in combination with the Morgenstern and Price method, and the results obtained were compared with those in the published literature to demonstrate that the RGA-BCC is able to efficiently determine the CSSs of slopes and their minimum factors of safety. The results of the sensitivity analyses show that having a reasonable setting for the number of control variables is the key to effectively determining the CSS. Moreover, the effects of slope structural characteristics should be considered when setting the number of control variables and population size. Algorithm comparison results show that RGA-BCC has better performance in solving the slope CSS problem compared with differential evolution (DE) and sparrow search algorithm (SSA).Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-024-81688-1.Keywords: Slope stability analysis, Genetic algorithm, Critical slip surface, Factor of safetySubject terms: Natural hazards, Mathematics and computing, Civil engineering

Introduction

Stability analysis of slopes is a classical problem that has long received the attention of many engineers and researchers. Commonly used methods in slope stability analysis include limit equilibrium methods (e.g., the simplified Bishop’s method1, Morgenstern and Price’s method2, Spencer’s method3, Janbu’s method3, Sarma’s method4, the generalised method of slices5) and numerical simulation methods (e.g., finite element method6,7, finite difference method, discrete element method, discontinuous deformation analysis8). In the field of geotechnical engineering, numerical simulation methods can give an intuitive and comprehensive simulation of the entire process of soil and rock mass deformation and failure9,10. However, numerical simulation methods are computationally intensive, time-consuming, require more input parameters, and are costly to use compared to limit equilibrium methods. Therefore, the simple and reliable limit equilibrium method is widely used by engineers and researchers.

Determining the minimum factor of safety (Min. F) of a slope and its corresponding CSS is the main task of slope stability analysis. The search for CSSs on slopes is essentially an optimisation problem and is characterised by many variables, complex constraints and possible discontinuities over the entire solution domain. In the past, many researchers have used traditional deterministic optimisation methods to determine the CSSs of slopes and their corresponding factors of safety. For example, Baker11 used the dynamic programming method, Arai and Tagyo12 used the conjugate gradient method, Nguyen13 used the simplex reflection technique, Li and White14 used the alternating-variable technique, and Chen and Shao15 used the simplex, steepest descent, and Davidon-Fletcher-Powell (DFP) methods. Despite the good results achieved by these traditional deterministic optimisation methods, the initial values have an impact on the traditional deterministic optimisation methods, leading to trapped local extreme points. The Monte Carlo method is a stochastic search technique with a very simple structure and has also been used by some scholars to determine the CSSs of slopes and their corresponding factors of safety. For example, Chen16 searched for the approximate location of the CSS by random search and used this as an initial estimate for a conventional optimisation method. Greco17 and Malkawi et al.18 proposed different Monte Carlo-based techniques of the random walk type to locate the CSSs of slopes and their corresponding factors of safety. However, these methods still do not guarantee that the global minimum can be found.

Metaheuristic algorithms are a class of stochastic search algorithms (e.g., genetic algorithms, particle swarm optimisation algorithms, and ant colony optimisation algorithms) that search for a solution through guided experimentation, which provides a certain trade-off between randomisation and local search and is suitable for most global optimisation problems19. Various metaheuristic algorithms have been devised and used to solve various optimisation problems in different domains due to their global search feature, which reduces the probability of falling into a local extreme point20–22. In the field of geotechnical engineering, many researchers have applied various metaheuristic algorithms to the search for CSSs on slopes. Goh et al.23 applied a genetic algorithm based on binary coding to the stability analysis of multiple-wedge slopes and verified the effectiveness of the genetic algorithm in searching for CSSs of slopes. Zolfaghari et al.24 proposed a genetic algorithm based on binary coding to search for noncircular CSSs of slopes and to calculate the factor of safety of slopes using the Morgenstern-Price method. Sun et al.25 used a spline curve combined with a genetic algorithm to search for CSSs and used Spencer’s method to calculate the factor of safety; the reliability and efficiency of the method were illustrated through examples. Li et al.26 used a real-coded genetic algorithm to search for the CSSs of slopes and proposed a dynamic bound technique to achieve a more efficient search within the solution domain. Zhu et al.27 introduced a local improvement procedure based on a tabu search into the basic real-coded genetic algorithm and searched for the CSSs of slopes. Cheng et al.28 proposed an improved particle swarm optimisation algorithm with a termination criterion for determining the CSSs of slopes. Himanshu et al.29 proposed a simplified segmented noncircular slip surface generation method and searched for the CSSs of slopes using the unified particle swarm optimisation algorithm. Kahatadeniya et al.30 proposed an ant colony optimisation method for determining the CSS of a slope. Gao31 proposed a premium-penalty ant colony optimisation algorithm and combined it with Spencer’s method to search for the CSSs of slopes. Kang et al.32 proposed an artificial bee colony algorithm with a multislice adjustment method for locating the CSS of a soil slope and calculated the factor of safety by using Spencer’s method. Gao33 determined the CSS of a soil slope based on an improved black hole algorithm. Li et al.34 combined nonlinear failure criteria with the Morgenstern and Price method to calculate the safety factors of slopes and proposed an improved whale optimisation algorithm to determine the CSSs of soil slopes. Himanshu et al.35 used the grey wolf optimisation (GWO) algorithm to search for CSSs of slopes and discussed the applicability and efficiency of the GWO algorithm in solving slope stability problems. Kashani et al.36 used the imperialistic competitive algorithm to determine the CSSs of slopes. Mishra et al.37 used a teaching-learning-based optimisation algorithm to locate the CSS of a soil slope. Xiao et al.38 proposed an enhanced fireworks algorithm to locate the CSSs of slopes.

Although these metaheuristic algorithms have achieved good results, no single optimisation algorithm is able to solve all problems, as there is no free lunch39. Therefore, improving existing metaheuristic algorithms and exploring new metaheuristic optimisation algorithms are important for solving optimisation problems. Among many metaheuristic algorithms, the genetic algorithm is an optimisation algorithm proposed by Professor Holland in the 1970s to simulate the biological evolutionary process of Darwinian genetic variation and natural selection40. This algorithm is the most famous and commonly used among many metaheuristic algorithms. However, the current standard genetic algorithm and its improved algorithms still have defects in certain respects, such as slow convergence and premature convergence, so they need to be further improved and perfected41–44.

The crossover operator is widely regarded as the most important and efficient search scheme in genetic algorithms, which generates potential offspring chromosomes by fusing individuals from different parents43. Many existing crossover operators often have one crossover scheme with a single search mode. Therefore, in order to solve the previously mentioned difficulties, we developed a BCC operator to improve the genetic algorithm. Unlike many existing crossover operators with only one search scheme, the BCC operator consists of two sub-crossover operators with distinctly different search modes. During the evolutionary process, the algorithm will periodically determine which sub-crossover operator to use in subsequent evolutions based on changes in the fitness value so that the algorithm can efficiently search for the optimal solution.

In this paper, the proposed real-coded genetic algorithm with a BCC operator (RGA-BCC) is combined with the Morgenstern and Price method based on the limit equilibrium method. A Python program is written to analyse the stability of five slope problems selected from the literature. The examples of slopes selected for this study include (1) homogeneous soil slopes; (2) nonhomogeneous soil slopes with three soil layers; (3) nonhomogeneous soil slopes with a weak soil layer sandwiched between two strong soil layers; (4) nonhomogeneous soil slopes with horizontal layering; and (5) nonhomogeneous soil slopes containing a weak soil layer affected by groundwater and seismic loading. The numerical study of the aforementioned examples indicates that the RGA-BCC proposed in this paper is effective for identifying CSSs on many complex and difficult slopes.

The paper is organised as follows: Section “Generation of the trial slip surface and objective function” presents the generation of a trial slip surface and the objective function for calculating the safety factor. Section “The proposed RGA-BCC” describes the proposed RGA-BCC. Section “Results and discussion” experimentally determines the parameters of the BCC operator and presents and discusses the computational results for five slope problems. Finally, Section “Conclusions” provides the conclusion.

Generation of the trial slip surface and objective function

Generation of the trial slip surface

Method of generating a trial slip surface

To evaluate the stability of a slope using the limit equilibrium method, it is essential to determine the location of the trial slip surface. The trial slip surface can be generated by a set of control variables. Searching for the CSSs of slopes involves finding a set of control variables that minimise the factor of safety.

The entire potential landslide body is divided equally into n vertical slices. The slices are numbered from the trailing edge of the potential landslide towards the leading edge. The base of slice i is approximated as a straight line segment ViVi+1 with an inclination of αi. If the slip surface dip is the same as the sliding direction, αi is positive; otherwise, αi is negative. The bases of neighbouring slices are connected in turn to form a noncircular trial slip surface.

Further subdivision of slices

This paper aims to enhance the calculation accuracy and programming efficiency by subdividing the slices under two conditions.

When the slope is nonhomogeneous, it is necessary to ensure that the base of a slice lies within a single soil layer to be able to calculate the stability of the slope more accurately. Therefore, if the base of a slice crosses different soil layers, the slice is divided into two slices by the intersection of the base of the slice with the stratification line.

To make programming simpler, when a slice contains a slope node, the slice is divided into two slices bounded by that node.

Objective function for evaluating the factor of safety

Locating the CSSs of slopes is an optimisation problem: determining the slip surface that minimises the factor of safety. The Morgenstern and Price2 method is a technique for analysing slope stability that considers the balance of forces and moments. It can be used to calculate the factor of safety for slopes with noncircular slip surfaces. The method accounts for the interaction of forces between slices and has a small error, but the calculation process is more complicated. Zhu et al.46 proposed a simple algorithm for calculating the factor of safety based on the Morgenstern and Price method, and this algorithm is used in this paper.

Selection operator

Once the fitness values of the parents are determined, the individuals are selected according to the fitness values. In this paper, the tournament selection47 operation is used. The principle of tournament selection is based on randomly selecting nt individuals from the population to participate in a tournament. The winner is the individual with the largest fitness value. The larger nt is, the greater the probability of selecting an individual with a larger fitness value, but overly favouring individuals with larger fitness values will limit the exploration capability of the algorithm. The dimension D of the variable affects the difficulty of search in solving the problem; typically, the larger D is, the more difficult the search. Therefore, in this paper, we set nt according to the range D falls within. When D ≤ 10, nt = 3; otherwise, nt = 2.

Binary composite crossover operator

A search algorithm must balance the exploration and exploitation of the search space. Exploration involves visiting new regions in the search space, and exploitation involves visiting regions in the search space within the neighbourhood of previously visited points19,48. The crossover operator plays a crucial role in the genetic algorithm. A well-designed crossover operator can help the algorithm achieve a balance between exploration and exploitation in the process of searching for a solution and avoid premature convergence.

This paper presents a BCC operator, which comprises an N-time single-gene crossover (NSGC) operator and a group-centred directed crossover (GDC) operator. The NSGC operator increases the diversity of the population and enhances the algorithm’s exploration ability. The group-centred directed crossover operator can better guide the search direction, enhance the algorithm’s exploitation ability, and promote the generation of excellent individuals.

(3)Binary composite crossover operator.

Binary composite crossover operator.

The BCC operation alternates between using GDC operators and NSGC operators based on certain conditions. This approach achieves a balance between exploration and exploitation capabilities, improving the algorithm’s performance.

When k ≥ 2, if αk/αk−1 is larger, the crossover operator used is more effective, so this crossover operator should continue to be applied in the next cycle; if αk/αk−1 is smaller, the crossover operator used is less effective, so it needs to be replaced by another crossover operator in the next cycle.

Therefore, the strategy for implementing the binary composite crossover operator is as follows: after T generations of evolution using one crossover operator, the operator is replaced if αk ≤ 0.5αk−1. Otherwise, the same crossover operator is used again.

Population replacement strategies

The population replacement strategies of the RGA-BCC comprises three parts: parent population update, offspring population generation, and the elite strategy47,51.

Parent population renewal and offspring population generation occur simultaneously. After the crossover and mutation operations are performed on the selected individuals, the two newly generated offspring are placed in the offspring population. Simultaneously, the newly generated copies of the offspring are added to the parent population to replace the corresponding parents and participate in the next selection. If two individuals are selected from the same parent, the offspring with the larger fitness value replaces the parent in the parent population.

When the number of individuals in the offspring population reaches the population size, the elite strategy is implemented. The population is updated by replacing the least fit individual in the offspring population with the most fit individual in the parent population.

Results and discussion

In this section, we first analyse the effects on the RGA-BCC of the initial crossover operator type, the parameter N in the NSGC, and the number of segments ns in the maximum number of evolutionary generations; the analysis is based on six benchmark function values, which are obtained through experiments to determine reasonable parameter values. Then, the Morgenstern-Price method is incorporated to search for the CSSs of five soil slopes selected from examples in the literature, and the results are compared with the minimum factors of safety calculated by other optimisation methods in the literature. The sensitivity of the number of control variables D and the population size NP are then analysed. Finally, the performance of RGA-BCC, differential evolution (DE)52, and sparrow search algorithm (SSA)53,54 for searching CSS is compared.

All experiments were based on the Windows 10 Home Chinese Edition operating system and were implemented through Python programs written using the Spyder IDE 5.1.5 (Python 3.9.7 64-bit) development platform.

Numerical examples of slope stability analysis

This section uses the RGA-BCC in conjunction with the Morgenstern-Price method to analyse five problems for soil slopes (both homogeneous and nonhomogeneous) selected from the literature.

In genetic algorithms, both the population size and the number of control variables have a significant impact on the performance of the algorithm. Larger population sizes, in general, are good for enhancing population diversity, but this often requires more computational time for the algorithm to converge. A larger number of control variables will make the problem more difficult to solve. For the CSS search problem, the number of control variables is related to the number of slices. When the number of control variables is small, a smaller number of slices may make it difficult to effectively reflect the true shape of the CSS, even though the search is less difficult. When the number of control variables is set reasonably, the trial slip surface can not only approximate the shape of the CSS, but also the calculation amount is relatively low. If the number of control variables is too large, theoretically, although the trial slip surface can more accurately approximate the shape of the CSS, it will increase the difficulty of solving the problem and the amount of computation, and the improvement of the computational accuracy is not obvious. Especially for some structurally complex slopes, a larger number of slices will most likely make the search difficulty significantly increase, leading the algorithm to fall into the local extreme points, thus making it difficult to effectively search for the CSS. Therefore, the reasonable setting of the population size and the number of control variables is crucial for the search of the CSS.

To analyse the effectiveness of the RGA-BCC in searching for CSSs on slopes, as well as the effect of the number of control variables D and the population size NP on the algorithm, for each slope case, D is set to 5, 15, and 25, and NP is set to 20, 40, and 60. Therefore, a total of 9 sets of experiments are designed. Moreover, when NP is 20, 40, and 60, the maximum number of evolutionary generations Tmax is set to 300, 150, and 100 for slope problems 1 and 2, respectively. For slope problems 3 and 5, Tmax is set to 450, 225, and 150, respectively. For slope problem 4, Tmax is set to 1,500, 750, and 500, respectively. Thus, the total numbers of function evaluations (NFEs) for all slope problems are essentially the same. To fairly compare the effects of different parameter combinations on the RGA-BCC, each set of experiments is run 30 times individually.

The following parameter settings are used for all experiments: the crossover probability is pc = 0.9, the mutation probability is pm = 0.1, the parameter for polynomial variation is ηm = 20, the number of single gene crossovers in NSGC is N = 10, the number of segments in the maximum number of evolutionary generations is ns = 10, and the initial crossover operator is the NSGC operator. Of these parameters, the settings of N, ns and the initial crossover operator are based on experimental results in section “ Parametric studies of binary composite crossover operator ”. The remaining parameters are determined based on experience combined with attempted calculations prior to formal experiments.

Statistical data analysis

Choosing reasonable parameter settings affects whether the metaheuristic algorithm can effectively search for the optimal solution. To enable the RGA-BBC algorithm to efficiently search for CSSs on slopes, we conducted sensitivity analyses by varying the number of control variables D and the population size NP.

The computational statistics in Tables 5, 7, 9, 11, 13, 14, 15 and 16 show that for different problems, the number of control variables D affects the search results differently. For Problems 1, 2, and 5, the calculated slope Min. F gradually decreases as D increases, and an optimal solution is achieved at D = 25. This is because D is related to the number of slices. For these three problems, the limited number of slices makes it difficult to effectively approximate the actual CSS when D is small, so the CSS of slopes cannot be identified when D is small. For Problem 3 and Problem 4, the optimal solutions were achieved for D = 15 and D = 5, respectively. The reason for this is that both slopes contain relatively thin weak layers, and the shape of the CSS will be strictly controlled by the weak layers, which will make the search significantly more difficult if D is large. In particular, for Problem 4, since there is a relatively thin horizontal weak layer with no ground outcrop, the CSS will inevitably cross the horizontal weak layer twice, and the problem will be further complicated by an excessive number of control variables. It should be noted, however, that although the search difficulty of Problem 4 increases when D = 25 and the RGA-BCC fails to achieve the search results for D = 5, it still outperforms the search results of algorithms such as GWO, PSO, MPSO, and IW-PSO.

It can also be observed from Tables 5, 7, 9, 11, 13, 14, 15 and 16 that NP affects the solution of the problem. For Problems 1, 2, 3, and 5, when D is the optimal parameter value, Mean (Min. F) gradually decreases and converges to the global optimum as NP increases from 20 to 60 if the NFE is approximately the same. This indicates that for slope problems 1, 2, 3, and 5, NP should be set to 60, which is sufficient to accurately evaluate the objective function to determine Min. F and the corresponding CSSs of slopes. For Problem 4, when D is the optimal parameter value, that is, D = 5, Mean (Min. F) decreases and then increases as NP increases from 20 to 60 and converges to the global optimum at NP = 40, if the NFE is in the range of 30,020 to 30,060. This indicates that for slope problem 4, NP should be set to 40, which is sufficient to accurately evaluate the objective function and determine Min. F for the slope and the corresponding CSS.

Through the parameter sensitivity analysis of the above five slopes, it can be found that the structural characteristics of the slopes have an influence on the reasonable setting of the number of control variables D and the population size NP, which in turn summarizes the scheme of parameter setting as follows: (1) For homogeneous slopes (e.g., Problem 1), or slopes with relatively thick monolayers (e.g., Problem 2), it can be made that D = 25 and NP = 60. (2) For slopes with relatively thin weak soil layer and outcrop of weak soil layer on the free surface (e.g., Problem 3 and Problem 5), the number of control variables should be set according to the relative thickness of the weak soil layer. If the thickness is slightly larger, D = 25, and if the thickness is relatively small, D = 15. NP can be set to 60. (3) For slopes containing a relatively thin layer of weak soil, if there is no outcrop of the weak soil layer (e.g., Problem 4) and it can be predicted that the CSS will pass through this weak soil layer, D = 5 and NP = 40 can be set.

Conclusions

In this paper, a real-coded genetic algorithm with a BCC operator is proposed to determine the CSSs of slopes and their minimum factor of safety by incorporating the Morgenstern–Price method. The following conclusions are drawn from the research in this paper:

Benchmark function tests show that when using the RGA-BCC algorithm, the parameters of the BCC operator should be set as follows in order to achieve good optimization results: the NSGC operator is used as the initial crossover operator and ns=10, N = 10.

Benchmark function tests show that when using the RGA-BCC algorithm, the parameters of the BCC operator should be set as follows in order to achieve good optimization results: the NSGC operator is used as the initial crossover operator and ns=10, N = 10.

Experiments on slope problems show that the proposed RGA-BCC, combined with the Morgenstern and Price method, can effectively locate the CSSs of homogeneous and non-homogeneous slopes. It also shows that the improvement of trial slip surface generation method in this paper is effective.

Experiments on slope problems show that the proposed RGA-BCC, combined with the Morgenstern and Price method, can effectively locate the CSSs of homogeneous and non-homogeneous slopes. It also shows that the improvement of trial slip surface generation method in this paper is effective.

The results of the sensitivity analysis show that both the number of control variables and the population size have an effect on the search results of slope CSS, and the effect of the number of control variables is more significant. In addition, the structural characteristics of the slope affect the difficulty of solving the problem, and the type of slope structure should be considered when setting the number of control variables and population size.
