Adaptation of mesenchymal stromal cells to culture on collagen-based bioscaffold
Authors: Huan Ting Ong, Dulce B. Vargas-Landin, Sharon L. Redmond, Leon Neethling, David I. Rhodes, Rodney J. Dilley
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Decellularized tissue scaffolds may provide a substrate for cell delivery, yet their impact on stem cell function remains unclear. We evaluated a decellularized pericardium bioscaffold (CardioCel®) as a platform for mesenchymal stromal cells (MSC), testing whether it maintains multipotency without enforcing lineage commitment. Human adipose- and bone marrow-derived MSC adhered, proliferated, and differentiated under appropriate stimuli when cultured on the scaffold. Transcriptome analysis showed most genes retained their expression levels, with only 57 differentially expressed, mainly in pathways related to adhesion, oxygen response, and endogenous stimuli. Genes encoding regenerative paracrine factors were preserved, indicating that the scaffold provides a supportive but non-directive environment. These findings suggest MSC assays can serve as a screening approach to predict scaffold compatibility with endogenous progenitor cells, supporting the broader application of pericardium scaffolds in regenerative medicine.

Introduction

In regenerative medicine, clinical tissue engineering has processed across several indications, from cartilage repair to bone regeneration, cardiac repair, musculoskeletal interface reconstruction, and the use of biomacromolecules with biofabrication [1–5]. Despite their diversity, these strategies share a practical goal in providing an adaptable microenvironment that supports transplanted cells during the critical period of adhesion, survival, and integration. Such support increases the chance of replacing, repairing, or regenerating injured tissues in a way that supports physiological structure and function. In this context, defining how living cells adapt to the scaffold surface and architecture is central to predicting performance beyond simple biocompatibility. Establishing that a scaffold sustains cell function without unintentionally biasing fate decisions is therefore a meaningful benchmark for translation.

CardioCel® is a collagen-based biomaterial derived from bovine pericardium and developed for cardiovascular surgery because of its non-immunogenic, bio-stable nature and resistance to calcification [6]. The scaffold is processed using the proprietary ADAPT® (Antigen Deletion and Anti-Calcification Treatment; Admedus Pty Ltd) technology, which combines decellularization, low-toxicity glutaraldehyde crosslinking, and an anti-calcification treatment designed to enhance durability and reduce cytotoxicity [6]. It retains the three anatomical layers of pericardium – a fibrous (rough) external layer, a middle parietal layer, and a visceral (smooth) layer facing the heart, thereby maintaining structural integrity and providing different surface topographies for potential cell attachment.

Clinically, CardioCel® has been widely used in congenital and adult cardiac surgery, including valve reconstruction, closure of atrial and ventricular septal defects, and complex procedures such as repair of Aortic-to-Right Ventricular Tunnel (ARVT) in d-Transposition of the great arteries with aortic valve atresia [7–10]. Long-term outcomes have demonstrated durable function for at least 24 months in paediatric patients undergoing repair of congenital heart defects [11]. Comparative analyses also show that CardioCel® performs favourably against other commonly used bioscaffolds, offering both functional physical properties and biocompatibility [12]. Beyond clinical use, in vitro studies have reported its ability to support mesenchymal stromal cell (MSC) retention and new tissue formation over extended culture periods [13].

Transcriptomic approaches have increasingly been applied to characterise how MSC respond to biomaterial scaffolds at the molecular level. Studies of MSC cultured on 3D synthetic scaffolds have reported consistent enrichment of cell adhesion, ECM organisation, and mechanosensing pathways [14], suggesting that scaffold-induced transcriptional responses share common features across material types. However, published RNA-seq studies have predominantly used single MSC sources, homogeneous synthetic materials, or native decellularized ECMs without proprietary crosslinking or anti-calcification treatment. CardioCel® is processed using the ADAPT® technology, which combines decellularization with low-toxicity glutaraldehyde crosslinking and anti-calcification treatment [6], producing a material with distinct chemical and structural properties from those previously characterised. Whether this processing preserves a non-directive transcriptional environment for MSC, and whether such a profile is consistent across both adipose- and bone marrow-derived sources, has not previously been established.

CardioCel® is derived from bovine pericardium, and extracellular matrix composition, fibre architecture, and signalling cues differ between species and across tissue types, which can influence cell behaviour [15]. The ADAPT® processing reduces this concern by depleting cellular antigens and using low-toxicity crosslinking to minimize xenogeneic immune responses [6]. However, the biomechanical properties of CardioCel® reflect its pericardial origin, which is a dense, fibrous collagen structure suited for cardiovascular loading and may not match the mechanical demands of softer tissues. Whether these properties support or limit cell function in non-cardiovascular settings depends on how well cells adapt to this environment without losing their functional identity, which is what this study set out to address.

Despite its established cardiovascular applications, the broader use of decellularized scaffolds in regenerative medicine depends on understanding how implanted or resident cells adapt to the biomaterial environment. A major hurdle in biomaterial development remains the limited knowledge of cell adaptability and the interactions between cells and scaffolds at the cellular and molecular level [16, 17]. This is particularly important when scaffolds are implanted into tissues containing endogenous stem or progenitor cells, where unintended differentiation could compromise repair or integration.

We therefore hypothesized that CardioCel® provides a permissive but non-directive environment that supports MSC adhesion and growth while preserving multipotency without enforcing lineage commitment. To test this, we evaluated adhesion, proliferation, trilineage differentiation, and transcriptomic adaptation of human MSC cultured on the scaffold. Our aim was to determine both its cellular compatibility and the potential of MSC assays as a predictive tool for scaffold safety. Framing MSC assays as a predictive screening tool for scaffold compatibility with endogenous progenitors represents a translational approach not yet prominent in the biomaterial literature, and one that could be applied more broadly to the preclinical evaluation of cell delivery platforms. In this context, MSC represent a clinically relevant model due to their established capacity to differentiate along mesenchymal lineages [18, 19]. They also secrete paracrine factors that contribute to wound healing [20–22], angiogenesis [23–26], tissue formation [24], anti-inflammatory responses [23, 27], and antimicrobial activity [28]. While previous studies showed that decellularized pericardium supports engraftment and survival of human bone marrow MSC in vitro [13], little is known about how MSC cellular processes and molecular mechanisms adapt when cultured directly on this material. This study, therefore, combines transcriptomic analysis and cell function assessments to evaluate whether human adipose-derived and bone marrow-derived MSCs (hADSC & hBMSC, or collectively as hMSC) maintain their stem cell properties and reparative potential on a decellularized pericardium scaffold in vitro.

Materials and methods

Cell sourcing and culture maintenance

Human ADSC and BMSC were purchased from Lonza (Lonza, Walkersville, Maryland), which were characterised by the company as negative for virus and microbial infections, positive for CD29, CD44, CD73, CD90, CD105, and CD166, and negative for CD14, CD31, CD34, and CD45. hMSC were selected from donors who are 20–45 years old, with cell viability assessed to be at least 70%, cryopreserved at minimum of 1 × 106 cells, with a doubling time of maximum 25 h. Cell cultures were established, expanded and maintained on tissue culture plastic (TCP) in growth medium (Dulbecco’s Modified Eagle Medium (DMEM), with 1 g/L D-glucose, L-Glutamine, 110 mg/L sodium pyruvate, 1% Penicillin-Streptomycin (Gibco, Carlsbad, California)), supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS; Bovogen, Keilor East, Australia). Cultures were grown in a humidified incubator at 37 °C, with 5% carbon dioxide (CO2) until confluent, with spent medium replaced with fresh every 2–3 days. Passages 5–8 were used in this study, and hMSC from two donors were used for each hADSC and hBMSC.

Trilineage differentiation

Cultures were tested for their ability to differentiate into adipose, bone and cartilage-like cells using protocols previously described [29, 30].

Adipogenesis

Cells were seeded at 50,000 cells/well in a 24-well plate in standard growth medium and grown to confluence before the spent medium was replaced with adipogenic medium, containing growth medium supplied with 10 µM Insulin, 200 µM Indomethacin, 0.5 mM 3-isobutyl-1-methylxanthine and 1 µM dexamethasone (Dex) (Sigma-Aldrich, St Louis, Missouri), or fresh growth medium (control cultures). Media was changed twice a week over 14 days. After 14 days, rinsed cells were fixed in 10% neutral-buffered formalin (NBF), rinsed again, before being stained with 3% Oil Red O stain solution (Sigma-Aldrich, St Louis, Missouri) to visualize cytoplasmic lipid droplets. Cells were stored in PBS for imaging.

Osteogenesis

Cells were seeded at 50,000 cells/well in a 24-well plate in standard growth medium and grown to confluence before the spent medium was replaced with osteogenic medium made up with growth medium supplied with 0.1 µM Dex, 10 mM Sodium beta-glycerophosphate and 50 µM Ascorbic acid (Sigma-Aldrich, St Louis, Missouri), or fresh growth medium (control cultures). Media were changed twice a week over a course of 24 days. Cells were rinsed in ddH2O then fixed in 10% NBF. Subsequently, rinsed cells were stained with 2.5% Silver Nitrate (Sigma-Aldrich, St Louis, Missouri) solution and exposed under direct UV light for 30 min. Cells were then washed again and stored in ddH2O for imaging.

Chondrogenesis

Cells were seeded at high densities of ~120,000 cells in a 10 µL droplet containing standard growth medium, and cell attachment was allowed over a 2-h period before the wells were topped up with chondrogenic medium containing growth medium supplied with 1 µmol/L Insulin, 50 nM Ascorbic acid and 10 ng/mL TGF-ß1 (Sigma-Aldrich, St Louis, Missouri), or growth medium in controls. Media were changed twice a week. After the 14-day incubation, cells were gently rinsed in PBS and fixed in 10% NBF for 15 min. After rinsing, cells were stained with 1% Alcian Blue solution (Sigma-Aldrich, St Louis, Missouri) for 30 min at room temperature. Cells were then washed again and stored in PBS for imaging.

Preparation of scaffold materials

Decellularized pericardium (CardioCel®, Admedus, Malaga, Australia) scaffolds were first rinsed in sterile PBS before incubating in excess growth medium overnight for complete adsorption prior to experiments (300 µL growth medium for 96-well plates, or 2 mL growth medium in 24-well plates). Pieces of scaffold were then cut with either a sterile scalpel or biopsy punch to various sizes to accommodate different tissue culture plastic (TCP) vessel sizes. Sterile instruments were used to handle the scaffold throughout the procedure.

hMSC culture on decellularized pericardium scaffold – cell morphology

The scaffold was opaque, preventing the monitoring of cell growth and attachment by transmitted light microscopy. To observe the cell morphology of MSC seeded on scaffold, scanning electron microscopy (SEM) was performed. Scaffolds (7-mm diameter discs of 0.32 cm2) seeded with MSC at 25,000 cells/well in a 96-well plate were fixed in 2% glutaraldehyde in 0.1% phosphate buffer overnight, followed by rinsing several times with 1x PBS pH 7.4. Scaffolds were then dehydrated in a series of increasing ethanol solutions (50, 70, 95, 100%, ethanol) (Sigma-Aldrich, St Louis, Missouri), followed by critical point drying using an Emitech K850 (Quorum Technologies Ltd, East Sussex, United Kingdom). Scaffolds were then mounted onto aluminium stubs (Ted Pella Inc, Redding, California) before being sputter coated at 0.07 Torr in argon gas for 2 min with gold (2–5 nm), (E5100 coating unit; Polaron Equipment Ltd, East Sussex, United Kingdom). Scaffolds were viewed using a Philips XL30 scanning electron microscope (Phillips, Amsterdam, Netherlands) at a voltage of 5 kV. The surface morphology and cell interactions were imaged at 50×, 200× (not shown) and 500× magnification. SEM micrographs were false coloured in GIMP software (v 2.8.18) to aid visualisation of hMSC adhered on scaffold.

For immunofluorescence microscopy, scaffold seeded with hMSC was first fixed in 10% NBF for 15 min and rinsed in PBS pH 7.4. Cells were then immunostained in 4’,6-Diamidino-2-Phenylindole, Dihydrochloride (DAPI; Molecular Probes, Waltham, Massachusetts) and in Alexa FluorTM 488 Phalloidin for F-actin (Invitrogen, Carlsbad, California), before being mounted on a microscope slide with 0.17 mm coverslips in prepared 1X PBS:Glycerol 1:1. Images were taken on Nikon Ti-E inverted microscope (Nikon, Tokyo, Japan) with a Nikon A1Si confocal laser-scanning system (Nikon, Tokyo, Japan), a 20X objective (Nikon Pan Fluor, NA 0.75), and the pinhole set to 1.0 Airy Unit. A violet laser (405 nm) was used for DAPI excitation and the fluorescence emission was detected through a 450/50 nm bandpass filter. An argon laser (488 nm) was used for Alexa FluorTM 488 Phalloidin excitation and the fluorescence emission was detected through a 525/50 nm bandpass filter. Images were captured using the NIS Elements software and saved as 12-bit grayscale images before merging using ImageJ/FIJI (v 2.8) [31].

hMSC on decellularized pericardium scaffold – trilineage differentiation

hMSC were seeded on 1 cm by 1 cm square samples (1 cm2) for each well for adipogenesis and osteogenesis. For chondrogenesis, scaffold was cut into 2-mm diameter discs (0.03 cm2) using a sterile 2-mm biopsy punch for each droplet per well. All differentiation and staining protocols were performed as described above. To observe cell differentiation on scaffold, microscopic images were taken on Nikon Eclipse Ti2 inverted fluorescence microscope equipped with motorised stage (Nikon, Tokyo, Japan). The Nikon DS-Fi3 light camera was used for light and phase contrast images. Objectives used were 4x (Nikon CFI Plan Fluor DL4x F, NA 0.13, WD 16.4 mm), 10x (Nikon CFI Plan Fluor 10x, NA 0.30, WD 16.0 mm), and 20x (Nikon CFI Plan Fluor 20x, NA 0.75, WD 2.1 mm). Images were captured using the NIS Elements HC software (v5.11.01 (Build 1367), 64-bit) coupled with High Content Analysis (Nikon, Tokyo, Japan), and saved as 12-bit grayscale images before merging using ImageJ/FIJI (v 2.8) [31]. Images of differentiated cells on scaffold were possible due to the contrast provided by strong stained colours.

hMSC on decellularized pericardium scaffold – cell adherence and proliferation

Scaffold 7 mm diameter discs (0.32 cm2) were used for adherence and proliferation tests in 96-well plates. The optimal cell density was determined for each of the hMSC cultures. hMSC were seeded on either scaffold or TCP (positive control) at 0 h. For each time point, scaffold-containing wells with no cells were included as blanks to account for background absorbance contributed by the scaffold material.

The analysis of cell adherence was performed at 2, 7, 24 and 48 h (24 and 48 h data not shown). At the end of each time point, wells were rinsed 3 times with sterile PBS to remove any non-adherent cells, prior to incubation with 3-[4,5-dimethylthiazol-2yl]-5-[3-carboxymethyl phenyl]-2-[4-sulfophenyl]-2H-tetrazolium inner salt (MTS) (CellTiter 96® AQueousOne) assay solution (1:5 ratio; Promega Corporation, Fitchburg, Wisconsin) and incubated for a further 2 h.

Cell proliferation was measured at 24, 48 and 72 h to determine the kinetics of proliferation. MTS solution (1:5 ratio) was added directly into each well after each time point without rinsing, and incubated for a further 2 h.

Following a 2-h incubation with MTS solution, 120 µL of the cell culture supernatant from each well was transferred to a new plate and the absorbance measured using the Epoch™ Spectrophotometer (BioTek, Winooski, Vermont).

Absorbance values were normalized to the corresponding blanks of scaffold containing no cells. All experiments were performed alongside positive controls (data not shown) of both hMSC cultured on normal TCP the same way.

RNA extraction, quantification and analysis of RNA integrity

hMSC were seeded on either 1 cm by 1 cm square scaffold (1 cm2) or TCP and grown in a growth medium containing 10% FBS for 48 h. Total RNA was extracted from ~1 ×106 cells per culture condition in replicates using the RNeasy Plus Micro kit (Qiagen, Hilden, Germany) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Briefly, cells were lysed, and genomic DNA was removed via the gDNA Eliminator spin column. Total RNA was then bound, collected and washed on the RNeasy MinElute spin column before being eluted at 14 µL per sample. Samples for this section were performed in duplicates.

All RNA samples were submitted to the Australia Genome Research Facility (AGRF, Melbourne, Australia) for quality control, library preparation and RNA sequencing. Briefly, RNA samples were quantified using a 2100 Bioanalyzer (Agilent Technologies, Santa Clara, California) where samples of at least an RNA Integrity Number (RIN) of ≥8.0 and an A260/280 ratio of 1.8–2.0 were accepted.

Library preparation and RNA sequencing

For all samples, library preparation was performed using the Illumina TruSeq® Stranded mRNA kit according to the manufacturer’s protocol (Illumina, San Diego, California). Briefly, mRNA was purified via PolyA RNA and fragmented for double-stranded cDNA synthesized using specific methods to improve strand specificity. Adapters were ligated to DNA fragments to prepare for hybridization onto a flow cell during RNA sequencing and enriched by amplification.

RNA sequencing was performed on the Illumina HiSeq 2500 platform High Output Mode using the Hiseq Control Software (HCS) v2.2.68 and Real Time Analysis (RTA) v1.18.66.3 running on the Illumina sequencing computer for single-end reads. The Illumina bcl2fastq 2.18.0.12 pipeline was used next to generate the sequence data. For the purpose of expression profiling, samples were single-end reads at 50 bp, in 10–20 million clusters per sample with about 20 samples multiplexed in one lane (sequencing details and processing steps are attached in Supplementary File 1). RNAseq files were uploaded onto ArrayExpress accession E-MTAB-9976.

Bioinformatics analysis

RNAseq raw fastq files were checked using the FastQC (v 0.11.9) tool [32] to evaluate the quality of the sequenced samples. Reads were trimmed with Trimmomatic (v 0.36) [33] with parameters recommended in the Trimmomatic tool manual for single-end reads. Trimmed reads were then aligned to the GrCh38 human reference genome assembly obtained from Ensembl using the HISAT2 (v 2.1.0) tool [34], with the default parameters in the HISAT2 tool manual. Mapped reads were processed using SamBamba (v 0.6.6) [35] to generate Bam files. Multi Mappers were filtered out using a mapping quality threshold of ≥5. Sorted and filtered Bam files were used as input for the featureCounts function [36].

Differential expression analyses were performed using DESeq2 (v 1.26.0) [37] on R studio to compare differential gene expressions from hMSC cultured on scaffold against TCP. Low count reads (<10) were removed and DESeq2 was used with the multi-factor design including both “conditions” (scaffold vs TCP) and “cell types” (hADSC and hBMSC) as variables. This design accounts for the transcriptional differences between cell types as a covariate, such that the differential expression results reflect scaffold-driven changes independently of cell-type effects, rather than a naïve pooled comparison of the two MSC populations. Count data were transformed using regularized logarithmic transformation (rlog function) in DESeq2 to compare between datasets in the clustering analyses. Differential expression analysis was performed on raw count data and results were shrunk using the Apeglm function [38] for better estimation of distribution and exported as Log2 Fold Changes (L2FC). Transcripts were considered significantly differentially expressed (DE; adjusted P < 0.05) if L2FC was more than or equal to 2. Statistically significant DE transcripts in scaffold were considered for gene enrichment ontology analysis.

Gene ontology analysis

Firstly, specific gene ontology (GO) terms from QuickGO annotations (EMBI-EBI) [39] were extracted to generate lists of genes involved in cell adherence (n = 233), angiogenesis (n = 462), extracellular matrix (n = 340) and wound healing (n = 156) pathways. hMSC genes expressed on scaffold or TCP were expressed as percentages of the total number of genes from each pathway. Next, the proportion of genes DE and not DE on scaffold were represented as percentages out of all genes expressed in each pathway of interest. Lastly, all DE transcripts generated from DESeq2 were analysed using Gorilla [40, 41] by uploading all expressed transcripts as the background set and DE transcripts as the target set to enrich for GO terms. Only GO terms that had a false discovery rate of ≤0.05 (Benjamini and Hochberg) were considered to be significantly enriched, except for GO terms identified for Molecular Functions.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analyses were performed using GraphPad Prism (v.8.4.1) and presented as mean ± standard deviation. Two-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) was performed followed by post-hoc test with Dunnett’s multiple comparisons test. Statistical values of P < 0.05 were considered statistically significant.

hMSC adaptation on decellularized pericardium scaffold – cell morphology

After two days of culture, SEM confirmed that both hADSC and hBMSC were able to adhere and spread across the distinct surfaces of the decellularized pericardium scaffold (Fig. 2). On the rough fibrous surface, cells were observed attaching directly to collagen fibres, extending processes that engaged the scaffold microarchitecture and bridged pores between fibres. By contrast, on the smooth visceral surface, cells spread more evenly, forming a flatter monolayer that reflected the relatively uniform underlying structure. Confocal imaging of DAPI-stained nuclei further supported these findings, showing that on the fibrous surface, cells were distributed at varying depths within the scaffold matrix, while on the smooth surface they aligned in a more uniform plane. hMSC adaptation on decellularized pericardium scaffold – cell differentiation

To determine if cells retained their differentiation capacity on decellularized pericardium scaffold, both hMSC were cultured on decellularized pericardium scaffold and were incubated with appropriate differentiating media to direct differentiation toward adipogenic, chondrogenic or osteogenic phenotype (Fig. 3). MSC adhered to and remained viable on decellularized pericardium scaffold throughout the differentiation period (14–21 days). Furthermore, cells in adipogenic media differentiated into adipocytes containing cytoplasmic lipid droplets which stained red with oil red O. MSC cultured in chondrogenic media on and around decellularized pericardium scaffold also formed rounded nodules of cartilage which stained positively for extracellular matrix (ECM) using Alcian Blue. There was also some blue staining developed in control samples (Fig. 3), suggestive of spontaneous differentiation. The apparent extent of chondrogenic nodule formation appeared greater in hBMSC than hADSC on both scaffold and TCP controls, consistent with published reports of stronger chondrogenic capacity in bone marrow-derived compared to adipose-derived MSC [42, 43]. However, as the staining intensity was not quantified, formal comparison between cell types was not made. Cell proliferation analysed using the MTS assay demonstrated significant increase in metabolic activity for both cell types on decellularized pericardium scaffold over 72 h (P < 0.001), and across all cell densities (Fig. 4B, P < 0.001). When comparing increasing cell number over time, two-way ANOVA shows increasing significance with higher densities for both cell types (P = 0.019 for hADSC, P < 0.001for hBMSC). Dunnett’s multiple comparisons test then showed significance increases in absorbance readings starting from medium cell densities against the lowest cell densities for both hMSC at all time points. At the lowest cell densities, hADSC and hBMSC cell growth was up to 7.7% and 5.3% increase in absorbance over 72 h, respectively, and at highest cell densities an increase of 75.7% and 87.3% respectively was observed. The MTS assay reflects cellular metabolic activity, which under these conditions correlates with cell accumulation on scaffold. Direct fluorescence-based quantification of cell number was not feasible due to the high autofluorescence of the glutaraldehyde-crosslinked scaffold material, as evident in Supplementary File 2. The progressive increase in MTS signal over 72 h, together with the DAPI nuclear staining confirming cell presence across time points, provides consistent evidence of cell accumulation on scaffold.

Gene ontology enrichment analysis of the differentially expressed set (Fig. 6B) suggested that most products localized to the plasma membrane (GO:0044459) and extracellular region (GO:0005576), consistent with modifications in cell–matrix interactions. Enriched biological processes included response to endogenous stimulus (GO:0009719), response to lipid (GO:0033993), biological adhesion (GO:0022610), and cell adhesion (GO:0007155), indicating an adaptive adhesion program in MSC responding to the collagenous scaffold. Additional enrichments in response to oxygen levels (GO:0070482) and decreased oxygen levels (GO:0036293) further suggest that cells sensed and adjusted to the altered microenvironment. In contrast, molecular function categories did not reach significance after false discovery rate correction (FDR > 0.05) and are therefore interpreted cautiously. A comprehensive list of GO terms is available in Supplementary File 5.

Given that only 57 transcripts were differentially expressed out of the 13,810 mapped, we next examined whether these shifts impacted pathways directly relevant to tissue repair. Four functional categories were analysed: cell adherence, angiogenesis, ECM organisation, and wound healing. Across these pathways, global expression differences between scaffold and TCP conditions remained minimal, with less than 1.7% of associated genes showing altered expression (Fig. 6C). At the level of individual genes expressed in each pathway (Fig. 6D), fewer than 1.6% were differentially expressed, again underscoring transcriptomic stability. Notable exceptions included increases in integrin subunit alpha 2 (ITGA2, 2.2-fold) and angiopoietin-like 4 (ANGPTL4, 2.4-fold), which are associated with enhanced adhesion and vascular support. In contrast, down-regulation was observed in several extracellular regulators including thrombospondin 1 (THBS1, −2.1-fold), connective tissue growth factor (CTGF, −2.1-fold), annexin A3 (ANXA3, −2.3-fold), pentraxin 3 (PTX3, −3.0-fold), and hyaluronan and proteoglycan link protein 1 (HAPLN1, −3.6-fold). Normalized read counts and fold changes for each pathway gene are provided in Supplementary File 6.

Discussion

To characterize how mesenchymal stem cells adapt functionally to a decellularized collagen scaffold, we established tissue engineering models from human MSC of two sources, adipose and bone marrow tissues, on decellularized pericardium scaffold. These models were assessed for adhesion, proliferation, and trilineage differentiation potential. Both hMSC types readily attached and proliferated, and with appropriate cues retained their ability to form adipose-, bone-, and cartilage-like tissues, confirming that stemness was preserved. Beyond these phenotypic outcomes, transcriptomic profiling provided additional insight into how cells adapt at the molecular level to the scaffold environment. This is important because a clinically relevant scaffold must not only support MSC growth and differentiation under controlled stimuli, but also avoid driving unscheduled lineage specification, which would be undesirable when implanted into tissues that contain endogenous progenitors.

Cellular adaptation of MSC in adherence, proliferation, and differentiation

Practical considerations such as seeding density and incubation time are critical for scaffold applications. Our data showed that MSC adhered to decellularized pericardium within 2 h, and that sufficiently high seeding densities supported robust proliferation over 72 h. These results suggest that clinically relevant workflows may not require extended incubation, and that higher seeding densities could compensate for shorter attachment times. For example, densities of at least 13,000 cells/cm2 (medium) were sufficient to ensure significant adherence within 2 h.

The capacity of MSC to differentiate on scaffold is equally important. Most tissue engineering approaches involve multiple cell types with limited capacity for self-repair. Delivering differentiated cells alone may not recapitulate tissue complexity, but stem cells offer the flexibility to grow, integrate, and differentiate in situ. In this study, MSC cultured on scaffold retained adipogenic, chondrogenic, and osteogenic potential when stimulated, demonstrating that the scaffold is permissive to directed differentiation and does not impair lineage responsiveness.

Functional genomics to characterise hMSC adaptation on scaffold

Transcriptome analysis revealed that hMSC cultured on decellularized pericardium scaffold remained transcriptionally stable, with only 57 genes significantly differentially expressed out of 13,810 transcripts mapped. This modest shift suggests that the scaffold provides a supportive yet non-directive environment. Importantly, regenerative paracrine programs were preserved: angiogenesis- and wound-healing–associated factors including vascular endothelial growth factor A (VEGFA), transforming growth factor alpha (TGFA), transforming growth factor beta 1 (TGFB1), transforming growth factor beta 2 (TGFB2), interleukin 1 beta (IL1B), interleukin 6 (IL6), angiopoietin 1 (ANGPT1), thrombospondin 2 (THBS2), platelet-derived growth factor subunit A (PDGFA), heparin-binding EGF-like growth factor (HBEGF), and multiple matrix metallopeptidases (MMP1, MMP2, MMP3, MMP9, and MMP13) were robustly expressed on both scaffold and TCP. Other secreted factors such as fibroblast growth factor 2 (FGF2; −1.1-fold) and hepatocyte growth factor (HGF; 1.1-fold) were also maintained with only minimal changes. Collectively, these results indicate that hMSC delivered on scaffold would be expected to retain regenerative signalling properties, provided the implant environment does not impose additional directional cues.

Of the 22 upregulated genes, many encoded transmembrane proteins and receptors with roles in mechanosensing, ion transport, adhesion, or communication. These included solute carrier family 16 member 6 (SLC16A6; 4.3-fold), carbonic anhydrase 9 (CA9; 3.7-fold), gap junction protein beta 2 (GJB2; 2.9-fold), C–C motif chemokine receptor 1 (CCR1; 2.5-fold), integrin subunit alpha 2 (ITGA2; 2.2-fold), and cadherin related 23 (CDH23; 2.1-fold). Notably, ITGA2 protein is involved in adhesion to collagen; its induction is consistent with the collagen-rich scaffold and suggests reinforced MSC-matrix interactions. Another aspect of the cellular response to the decellularized pericardium scaffold environment was the upregulation of genes in cell-cell signalling, which included bone morphogenetic protein 2 (BMP2, 2.5-fold), triggering receptor expressed on myeloid cells 1 (TREM1, 2.4-fold), ANGPTL4 (2.4-fold), regulator of G protein signalling 17 (RGS17, 2.1-fold) and others. ANGPTL4 protein acts as an apoptosis survival factor for vascular endothelial cells and may also bind to ECM [44]. Three Wnt signalling-related transcripts were also upregulated: notum, palmitoleoyl-protein carboxylesterase (NOTUM; 3.2-fold), NKD inhibitor of Wnt signalling pathway 2 (NKD2; 2.7-fold), and Wnt family member 7B (WNT7B; 2.5-fold). Other upregulated genes were involved in regulating cellular growth and cell cycle such as the forkhead box Q1 (FOXQ1, 3.5-fold) [45] and protein tyrosine phosphatase receptor type N (PTPRN, 2.9-fold) [46]. Gene ontology confirmed enrichment in cell adhesion (GO:0007155) and response to organic cyclic compounds (GO:0014070), supporting the interpretation that scaffold contact triggers active adhesion and environmental sensing programs.

The 35 downregulated genes included several associated with ECM production and adhesion, such as beta-1,3-galactosyltransferase 2 (B3GALT2, −4-fold) [47], HAPLN1 (-3.6-fold) [48], adhesion G protein-coupled receptor G6 (ADGRG6, −2.6-fold) [49], neural precursor cell expressed, developmentally down-regulated 9 (NEDD9, −2.3-fold) [50], cadherin-4 (CDH4, −2.2-fold) [51], and sarcoglycan gamma (SGCG, −2.1-fold) [52]. This reduction may reflect decreased need for new ECM production since the scaffold already provides a collagen-rich matrix. Other downregulated genes included scrapie-responsive gene 1 (SCRG1, −3.6-fold), PTX3 (−3-fold), peptidase inhibitor 16 (PI16, −2.7-fold), endothelin 1 (EDN1, −2.6-fold), pappalysin 2 (PAPPA2, −2.5-fold), interleukin 26 (IL26, −2.3-fold), CTGF (−2.1-fold), which contributed to other signalling pathways and regenerative functions. Of note, PAPPA2 specifically cleaves insulin-like growth factor binding protein 5 (IGFBP-5) [53]. Recently, it was demonstrated that IGFBP5 promotes pulmonary fibrosis by increasing production of ECM genes and pro-fibrotic growth factor CTGF in primary human lung fibroblasts [54, 55]. A reduction of PAPPA2 expression in hMSC suggests a reduced cleavage of IGFBP5, and hence an increasing production of ECM and tissue remodelling. Down-regulation of IL26, a pro-inflammatory factor [56], may indicate a decrease in proinflammatory response in hMSC, further supporting decellularized pericardium scaffold as a suitable biomaterial for cell transplantation. Taken together, these findings suggest that MSC adapt to scaffold culture by reinforcing adhesion and communication pathways while down-regulating fibrotic and inflammatory responses.

The enrichment of cell adhesion and ECM-related GO terms observed here is consistent with transcriptomic studies of MSC cultured on other biomaterial scaffolds. Testa et al. reported that rat bone marrow MSCs cultured in 3D micro-scaffolds showed GO enrichment predominantly in cell adhesion molecules, ECM organisation, and actin cytoskeleton remodelling compared to 2D controls [14]. That study interpreted these responses as reflecting a conserved mechanosensing adaptation, in which scaffold contact triggers a mechano-transduction cascade from the ECM through cell adhesions to the nucleus. Our findings support this interpretation: the upregulation of ITGA2, which encodes an integrin subunit that mediates collagen binding, alongside enrichment in cell adhesion GO terms, is consistent with integrin-mediated mechanosensing of the collagen-rich pericardial scaffold surface. Notably, Testa et al. observed 654 differentially expressed genes in their system, substantially more than the 57 identified here, which may reflect differences in scaffold dimensionality, material stiffness, or species-specific responses. The comparatively modest transcriptional shift in our study further supports the conclusion that CardioCel® provides a permissive rather than directive environment for MSC.

Calcification of cardiac tissue is a problem in cardiovascular post-engraftment surgeries, so anti-calcification measures were taken in developing decellularized pericardium scaffold [6]. These transcriptomic findings align with the clinical rationale for CardioCel®, where the downregulation of ANXA3, a calcium-dependent phospholipid binding protein known to regulate cell growth and apoptosis [57, 58], could indicate reduced calcium-dependent activity or calcium content on the biomaterial, further supporting the current use of decellularized pericardium scaffold as a heart valve replacement and cardiac repair biomaterial.

In this approach, we were able to evaluate how hMSC adapt to a biomaterial scaffold at the transcriptome level, providing a predictive view of their regenerative performance. These findings also highlight that hMSC exhibit sensitive molecular changes to scaffold culture, which could be further investigated through proteomic validation in future studies.

Limitations

Differentiation assays confirmed lineage potential, but quantification was limited by imaging challenges across the scaffold surface. Future studies using single-cell transcriptomics or quantitative morphometrics could strengthen this assessment. The MTS assay was used as a measure of metabolic activity as a proxy for cell accumulation; direct cell counting was precluded by the high autofluorescence of the crosslinked scaffold material.

Transcriptome analysis combined hADSC and hBMSC, providing an overview of common responses but obscuring source-specific differences. As PCA indicated distinct baseline transcriptomes, future work should parse hADSC and hBMSC responses separately. Validation of specific genes (e.g., ITGA2, ANGPTL4, PAPPA2, IL26) by qRT-PCR or proteomics, and correlation with in vivo outcomes, will further define the scaffold’s performance as a delivery platform.

Overall suitability of decellularized pericardium scaffold as a platform

While decellularized pericardium scaffold is clinically established for valve replacement and other cardiac repairs, this study expands its evaluation as a potential cell delivery platform. Human MSC adhered, proliferated, and differentiated on scaffold without loss of stemness. Transcriptomic profiling revealed a stable secretome and regenerative program, with only modest, adaptive changes in adhesion and signalling genes. These features position decellularized pericardium as a versatile biomaterial that could integrate into tissues containing endogenous progenitors, without imposing unscheduled lineage commitment. CardioCel® is mechanically suited to cardiovascular loading conditions, and its dense, crosslinked collagen architecture may not match the stiffness requirements of softer target tissues. Translating this scaffold beyond cardiac repair would require consideration of whether its biomechanical properties are compatible with the intended implant site, particularly in applications where matrix stiffness influences lineage commitment or tissue integration. Contexts where a structurally rigid scaffold is appropriate, such as bone or fibrocartilage repair, may therefore be more suitable candidates than soft tissue applications requiring higher compliance. Similarly, as a bovine-derived xenogeneic material, the ECM composition of CardioCel® reflects its pericardial origin and may not recapitulate the tissue-specific biochemical cues required for non-cardiovascular applications, a consideration that warrants evaluation in tissue-specific preclinical models.

Conclusions

This study used complementary functional and transcriptomic approaches to characterise MSC adaptation to decellularized pericardium scaffold. Cell functionality assays confirmed that the scaffold supports MSC adhesion, proliferation, and trilineage differentiation, while transcriptomics showed preservation of regenerative signalling programs with limited changes. Together, these findings support the scaffold’s safety and compatibility as a platform for MSC delivery. Importantly, MSC assays themselves can serve as a sensitive bioassay for scaffold evaluation, providing an early readout of whether biomaterials preserve multipotency and paracrine activity. This reinforces the broader application of decellularized pericardium scaffold beyond cardiovascular repair and establishes MSC-based assays as a practical screening tool in the preclinical development of biomaterials.
