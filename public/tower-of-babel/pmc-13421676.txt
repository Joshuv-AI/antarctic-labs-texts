Knowledge distillation and pseudo-labeling for lightweight YOLOv11-based structural crack detection
Authors: Kaan Arik, Burak Ağgül
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractStructural cracks threaten the safety and long-term durability of civil infrastructure, yet manual inspection remains slow, subjective, and unreliable on complex surfaces. This study presents a training-centric strategy to improve a lightweight YOLOv11-N detector (2.6 M parameters) by transferring knowledge from a high-capacity YOLOv11-L teacher, without modifying the student architecture. Two semi-supervised mechanisms are investigated: pseudo-labeling and knowledge distillation. Using a crack dataset enlarged via systematic data augmentation, pseudo-labeling combines high-confidence teacher predictions with available ground-truth annotations through IoU and NMS filtering, while the distillation approach guides the student using both hard labels and teacher-derived soft signals to strengthen localization behavior. Experimental results show that both strategies enhance the baseline student model, with pseudo-labeling providing more stable training dynamics and stronger overall gains, whereas distillation primarily improves convergence behavior and sample efficiency. Ablation analyses highlight that the benefit of pseudo-labeling is data-dependent and requires a minimum pseudo-label density to achieve consistent improvements. Finally, edge-device (Raspberry 3B + and Nvidia Jetson Nano Kit) benchmarks validate that the resulting lightweight detector is suitable for deployment on resource-constrained platforms, enabling practical UAV- and mobile-oriented crack inspection across diverse edge computing tiers.Keywords: Crack detection, Pseudo labeling, Knowledge distillation, Object detection, Yolov11Subject terms: Engineering, Materials science, Mathematics and computing

Introduction

Today, construction industry is undergoing a multidimensional transformation that goes beyond the construction of physical structures, integrating digitalization and automation processes1. This transformation plays a critical role, particularly in terms of ensuring structural safety and increasing sustainability2. While manual inspections in the past were time-consuming and prone to error, today advanced imaging technologies, sensor systems, and remote access solutions enable the condition of structures to be analyzed more quickly and accurately3,4. At the heart of this transformation lies the integration of Artificial Intelligence (AI) technologies with structural engineering5. Developments in deep learning and computer vision are minimizing human error by automating critical processes such as structural damage detection, maintenance planning, and risk analysis6,7.

For example, processes such as crack detection, which previously relied on expert opinion, can now be performed in seconds using machine learning-based models, providing a significant advantage for sustainable monitoring of large-scale structures8,9. These technological developments not only increase efficiency but also enhance safety10. Micro-damage that goes undetected in early stages can lead to major destruction over time11. Therefore, regular monitoring of structures and the creation of early warning systems are of vital importance12. AI-supported analysis tools offer proactive solutions to such scenarios by operating with high accuracy even under different environmental conditions and structural materials13,14. In this direction, future of building technology relies not only on stronger materials or architectural innovations but also on intelligent and predictive systems15. AI-based applications developed in this direction have become an inevitable necessity not only for academic research but also for practical engineering solutions16. These approaches, which can be applied in many areas from infrastructure to high-rise buildings, are opening the doors to a new era in monitoring building health17,18.

Related works

This section provides a summary of some studies that have used deep learning-based object detection methods for wall crack detection in the context of structural engineering over past 5 years.

Recent studies have focused on optimizing YOLO-based models for structural crack detection. Wang et al.19 developed a YOLOv5s model using BotNet, GhostNetV2, and ODConv modules, achieving high accuracy and a significant reduction in parameters by using rotated bounding boxes. Similarly, Li et al.20 proposed CL-YOLOv8, which enhanced the YOLOv8n infrastructure with a ConvNeXt V2 body and an LSKA attention module, providing a moderate improvement in mAP over the baseline. In another study, Dong et al.21 developed YOLOv8-CD, a lightened adaptation using LSKA, the Ghost module, and GSConv, which yielded high improvements in mAP values and enabled real-time operation. For practical application, Bigakenar and Pai22 presented a drone-based inspection system where a YOLO-v3 model transfers real-time damage data to a ground station, which can then control the drone, demonstrating good mAP performance and efficient operation. These approaches highlight a trend of moving beyond standard horizontal bounding boxes, which can cause position errors on non-horizontal cracks. The use of rotated boxes, or alternatively, two-stage detectors like Faster R-CNN, is often preferred for achieving high accuracy and capturing fine details, even with limited data, thanks to comprehensive region proposal mechanisms23.

Single-stage detection algorithms are easy to use in real-time applications because they can produce results very quickly since they perform classification and positioning tasks over a single network. For example, improved YOLOv5s model can reduce computational costs while maintaining a similar level of accuracy with fewer parameters. In this regard, YOLOv8 offers a suitable alternative for real-time systems with a lower number of parameters and a high FPS (Frames Per Second) value. However, it has been reported that single-stage models sometimes fall short in detecting detailed structures such as narrow cracks or complex surfaces. Indeed, it has been noted that YOLOv3 model, trained with a small dataset, struggles to detect fine cracks; in contrast, two-stage approach of Faster R-CNN is more successful in detecting such details.

However, high accuracy of these complex models often necessitates high-performance GPUs. Access to such hardware is not universally available to all researchers due to significant costs, and virtual cloud GPUs also present considerable expense. This computational requirement is a notable disadvantage, as two-stage approaches generally run slower and demand higher resources compared to single-stage models like YOLO or SSD, making them difficult to optimize for real-time applications24,25. Our study primarily addresses this challenge. While two-stage methods are typically preferred in applications where accuracy is paramount, our solution aims to mitigate their high computational demands; on the other hand, single-stage models remain frequently used in areas where a balance between speed and computational cost is the primary consideration26,27.

Recent advances in lightweight neural network architectures have significantly expanded the feasibility of deploying crack detection systems on resource-constrained devices. A lightweight encoder-decoder network for automatic pavement crack detection introduced an efficient encoder-decoder design tailored for pavement crack analysis, demonstrating that compact segmentation-oriented architectures can preserve competitive performance while reducing computational burden28. Building on this line of research, Automatic lightweight networks for real-time road crack detection with DPSO further emphasized the importance of balancing model complexity and inference speed for real-time deployment, showing that lightweight optimization strategies can improve practical usability in field conditions29. In a similar direction, OUR-Net: A multi-frequency network with octave max unpooling and octave convolution residual block for pavement crack segmentation highlighted how multi-frequency feature learning and octave-based operations can enhance crack segmentation accuracy while maintaining a lightweight design philosophy30. These studies collectively confirm that lightweight architectural design is a promising pathway for improving deployability without severely compromising detection quality.

CrackU-net31 introduced a novel encoder-decoder architecture specifically designed for pixelwise pavement crack detection, demonstrating that carefully designed lightweight networks can achieve competitive segmentation performance while maintaining computational efficiency. Building upon this foundation, automated road crack detection using deep convolutional neural networks optimized for mobile deployment32 emphasized the critical balance between model complexity and inference speed in real-world scenarios. Further advancements33 incorporated residual connections and attention gating mechanisms into a lightweight convolutional neural network for pavement surface crack segmentation, demonstrating that strategic architectural modifications can enhance feature extraction capabilities without substantially increasing computational overhead. A pragmatic approach to road damage detection and classification using smartphone images34 proved that deep neural networks can be effectively deployed on consumer-grade mobile devices when properly optimized. This work underscores the practical viability of edge-based structural health monitoring systems, which aligns closely with the deployment objectives of resource-efficient crack detection methodologies.

The integration of YOLO-based detection paradigms with lightweight design principles has emerged as a particularly promising research direction for real-time structural inspection applications. CrackGAN35 leverages generative adversarial learning with partially accurate ground truths, offering an alternative semi-supervised approach that complements traditional pseudo-labeling strategies by addressing the challenge of incomplete or noisy annotations. Automatic pavement crack detection through structured prediction combined with convolutional neural networks36 provided valuable insights into the trade-offs between bounding-box-based detection and pixel-level segmentation approaches. The application of YOLO models in conjunction with 3D ground-penetrating radar images for road detection and maintenance37 illustrated the potential of multi-modal sensor fusion to enhance detection robustness under challenging field conditions. Foundational groundwork for deep learning-based crack damage detection using convolutional neural networks38 established early benchmarks that have guided subsequent research in balancing accuracy with computational efficiency. Automatic bridge crack detection through transfer learning strategies39 demonstrated that pre-trained models can be effectively adapted to structural health monitoring tasks with limited labeled data. A comprehensive comparison between pixel-level and object detection approaches for concrete structure evaluation40 offered empirical evidence that bounding-box-based methods can achieve satisfactory performance in many practical scenarios while maintaining superior inference speed. In addition to segmentation-oriented lightweight designs, bounding-box-based crack detection has also remained highly relevant for real-time structural inspection. For example, Automatic pavement crack detection based on YOLOv5-AH adopted a YOLOv5-based detection framework and demonstrated that lightweight one-stage detectors can provide an effective balance between localization accuracy and deployment efficiency for pavement crack analysis41. These studies collectively highlight that the choice between architectural innovation, training methodology enhancement, and multi-modal integration depends critically on the specific constraints and objectives of the deployment environment, reinforcing the value of flexible, training-centric approaches that can elevate existing lightweight architectures without necessitating structural redesign.

A parallel line of research focuses on deploying crack detection models on resource-constrained edge devices, especially for UAV-based inspection. In this context, studies have emphasized not only accuracy but also model size, energy consumption, and inference speed for real-time operation42–44. Existing work mainly follows two directions: developing lightweight architectures and improving established detectors with specialized modules. Although effective, these approaches often require architectural modification, which may complicate implementation and limit flexibility across model versions. Alongside deployment-oriented studies, architectural approaches for crack detection have also continued to evolve. These include CNN-based frameworks for detailed crack analysis as well as hybrid methods that combine classical image processing with deep learning45,46. Such studies show that both architectural design and pre-processing strategies can improve crack detection performance.

Overall, recent studies indicate that YOLO-based detectors remain a relevant and widely adopted choice for crack detection, particularly when real-time performance and deployment efficiency are important.

Dataset and method

Dataset description and augmentation

Our dataset contains images of cracks on various surfaces, such as asphalt, concrete, and painted walls. The images include crack samples captured in different colors, textures and lighting conditions. This provides an important advantage when testing the model’s ability to generalize in different environmental conditions. In each image, the cracks are marked with green bounding boxes for use in training object detection algorithms. The dataset includes examples where cracks can be observed in fine detail, enabling different types of cracks to be identified. Before the introduction of the data and method, we note that the dataset and the code related to the methodology are available on authors’ GitHub47.

Another notable feature of the dataset is the wide variety of building materials covered by images in Fig. 1. The variety of cracks observed on different types of surfaces, such as asphalt, concrete, stone and plaster, provides machine learning models with ample opportunity to perform material-independent crack detection. Additionally, the presence of cracks that are very small or thin in some images is beneficial for developing more sensitive detection models capable of handling low-contrast and challenging examples. This diversity provides a robust testing environment for evaluating and comparing the performance of deep learning–based object detection models, such as YOLO.

Table 1 provides a detailed overview of the image data augmentation techniques used to diversify training data and their functions. These techniques aim to improve generalization ability of deep learning models and reduce risk of overfitting48. Horizontal and vertical flip operations reduce sensitivity to object orientation, whereas fixed 90° rotations enable learning from alternative viewpoints. Crop augmentation (0–30% zoom) introduces samples at different apparent distances, and random rotations within the range of −15° to + 15° improve recognition under oblique perspectives. To provide a clearer understanding of dataset’s composition, images are distributed across primary surface types as follows: approximately 45% depict cracks on concrete surfaces, 35% on asphalt, and remaining 20% on painted walls, plaster, or stone. This distribution reflects a focus on common civil infrastructure materials while maintaining diversity for model generalization.

Experimental environment and training

Training session was carried out on NVIDIA A100-SXM4-80GB GPU in an environment configured with CUDA 12.6, PyTorch 2.8.0, and Ultralytics YOLOv11 (v8.3.185). YOLO11-L model used in this study contains 190 layers, approximately 25.3 million parameters, and 86.6 GFLOPs of computational complexity. All input images were processed at a resolution of 640 × 640 pixels, and the model was trained on a custom Roboflow dataset comprising 624 samples. This configuration ensured stable training and strong computational performance.

Table 3 summarizes the experimental settings and training configurations for both Pseudo Labeling (PL) and Knowledge Distillation (KD). In PL stage, model was trained for a total of 60 epochs, requiring approximately 2.043 h, with GPU memory usage ranging between 14.5 GB and 18.9 GB. During the KD stage, training also continued for 60 epochs, lasting approximately 2.109 h, while GPU memory utilization increased slightly, reaching between 15.5 GB and 20.9 GB. These values demonstrate that although both stages maintained similar training durations, KD phase demanded more memory due to the additional feature imitation and teacher–student interaction mechanisms. Overall, the adopted hardware configuration ensured efficient computation and stable training performance across both stages.

YOLOv11

YOLOv11 introduces significant architectural improvements over its predecessors for enhanced performance. Its modular structure consists of a Backbone, Neck, and Head. The Backbone is optimized with C2f (Concatenate-to-Fuse) blocks, which replace C3 modules of earlier versions to provide more-efficient feature extraction and information merging. The model’s learning is enhanced by CBS (Convolution + BatchNorm + SiLU) structures. The Neck section uses a Feature Pyramid Network (FPN) and Path Aggregation Network (PAN) to effectively combine feature maps at different scales, improving the detection of both small and large objects50,51.

As illustrated in Fig. 4, head generates detection masks from multi-scale feature maps. In contrast to earlier YOLO versions, YOLOv11 adopts an anchor-free design, where predictions are produced from point-based representations instead of predefined anchor boxes. This formulation streamlines the detection process, accelerates training, and improves the localization of small objects. Moreover, YOLOv11 provides a scalable multi-task framework that supports tasks such as classification and detection. With variants ranging from n to x, architecture can be adapted to both edge devices and high-performance computing environments.

Detailed architecture diagram for YOLOv11 architecture.

From YOLOv11n to YOLOv11x, scaling primarily affects the Backbone and Neck, with progressive increases in network depth, channel width, and number of C3K2 blocks. Smaller variants prioritize efficiency for real-time deployment, while larger variants strengthen representational capacity through deeper feature extraction and richer multi-scale fusion. Although the Head remains structurally consistent, the improved feature maps produced by larger models enhance localization and mask quality, especially for fine crack patterns. Accordingly, each variant provides a practical balance between computational demand and detection performance.

Figure 5 illustrates overall workflow of proposed teacher-student-based semi-supervised learning framework. In first stage, both labeled and unlabeled images are fed into Teacher network. Using labeled samples, Teacher is trained under direct supervision, while PL are generated for unlabeled images. To improve robustness, controlled noise is introduced during PL generation, preventing student from overfitting to overly confident teacher predictions. It repairs module then refines these predictions to reduce noise-induced and teacher-generated errors. The repaired pseudo-labels, together with the mixed image-label pairs, are subsequently used to train the student network. During this process, Student model is optimized through multiple loss components, while teacher-guided supervision provides additional regularization. This iterative framework reduces the need for costly manual annotation and improves detection performance by stabilizing training and mitigating the effect of low-quality pseudo-labels.

The feature imitation process, illustrated in Fig. 6, is designed to guide the student’s attention towards discriminative crack regions identified by the teacher. The key steps and parameters are detailed below:

Imitation Mask Generation: The imitation mask is generated by thresholding the teacher’s intermediate feature maps at a specific convolutional layer (taken from the Neck module, after the first FPN-PAN fusion). For each spatial location in the feature map, we compute the L2-norm across channels. Regions where this norm exceeds a threshold ϕ = 0.7 are considered “high-activation” and likely to correspond to meaningful crack features. These regions are binarized to form the initial imitation mask.

Region Proposals from the Mask: The binary mask is processed using a connected-components algorithm to identify contiguous high-activation regions. Each connected component whose area is greater than 10 pixels (at the feature map resolution) is considered a valid region proposal. This typically yields between 5 and 20 region proposals per image, depending on crack complexity.

Forming Imitation Targets: These region proposals are combined with the available ground-truth bounding boxes. For each proposal that has an Intersection-over-Union (IoU) greater than 0.3 with any ground-truth box, it is retained as a fine-grained imitation target. Proposals with lower IoU are considered background or noise and are discarded. This step ensures the student focuses on teacher-activated regions that are relevant to actual crack locations.

Feature Adaptation and Loss: Within these target regions, the student’s corresponding feature maps (from the same hierarchical level) are aligned with teacher model via a 1 × 1 convolutional projection layer that matches the student’s channel dimension to teacher model. The imitation loss ₎ is computed as the Mean Squared Error (MSE) between the projected student features and the teacher features, averaged over all pixels within the target regions. This loss is added to the overall KD objective (Eq. 15) with a weighting factor ₎ = 0.1.

This structured imitation strategy provides the student with spatially grounded, feature-level guidance beyond simple label matching, encouraging it to internalize the teacher’s representation of crack morphology.

Discussion

The comparative analysis of Pseudo-Labeling (PL) and Knowledge Distillation (KD) reveals distinct profiles for each knowledge-transfer strategy, offering valuable guidance for practitioners. The PL method’s primary strength lies in its ability to deliver exceptional stability and reliable performance uplift. By learning from the teacher’s high-confidence pseudo-labels, the student model not only achieves accuracy metrics closely mirroring the teacher but also does so through a smooth, predictable optimization process with balanced loss components. This makes PL a robust choice for applications where deployment stability and consistent performance are paramount, effectively transferring the teacher’s localization strategy alongside its classification knowledge.

In contrast, the KD method excels in convergence speed and sample efficiency, forcing the student to focus on and master the most challenging aspect of the task—precise localization, as evidenced by its dominant focus on minimizing the Distribution Focal Loss (DFL). However, this comes at the cost of training volatility and sensitivity to hyperparameters (e.g., temperature ), as seen in the fluctuations of mAP50-95 and precision-recall metrics. This suggests that KD acts as a powerful but less stable optimizer, potentially guiding the student to better local minima but requiring careful tuning and validation.

The proposed approach inherits two main practical limitations. First, it necessitates a two-stage training pipeline: a high-capacity teacher must be trained first, which itself requires significant computational resources, before knowledge transfer to the student can occur. This adds complexity compared to single-stage end-to-end training of a lightweight model. Second, the performance of the KD variant, while fast-converging, may be less predictable out-of-the-box due to its volatility, potentially demanding additional stabilization techniques or extensive hyperparameter searches for reliable production use.

Our methodology addresses a notable gap in the current literature, which often polarizes into (a) developing novel, lightweight architectures or (b) applying post-training compression to existing models. We offer a third, complementary pathway that enhances a standard, deployment-friendly architecture through its training regimen. This allows the lightweight student model (YOLOv11-N) to approach the accuracy of larger models or specialized architectures without any structural modification, preserving its inherent efficiency advantages. This training-centric enhancement is orthogonal to and can be combined with subsequent architectural tweaks or compression techniques for compounded gains.

A detailed comparison of our approach with recent and relevant crack detection algorithms highlights its distinct positioning. Unlike studies that focus on quantization and compression for deployment (e.g., Zhang et al.44 who optimized MobileNets via QAT/PTQ for microcontrollers), our work operates at an earlier stage, aiming to maximize the inherent accuracy of a lightweight model before such compression is applied. This makes the two approaches complementary; a model enhanced by our PL/KD method could subsequently be quantized for even greater efficiency.

Compared to research proposing novel lightweight architectures—such as the encoder-decoder networks with depthwise convolutions42 or optimized U-Nets for segmentation44—our methodology offers architectural flexibility. We demonstrate that significant performance gains can be achieved without designing a new network, using instead a standard, well-supported model (YOLOv11-N). This reduces development complexity and leverages ongoing community optimization for the base architecture.

When contrasted with other studies that enhance YOLO architectures through added modules, for example, attention mechanisms or adaptive fusion45, our training-centric strategy provides a non-invasive alternative. It avoids increasing model complexity or inference time, which is a critical advantage for real-time edge deployment. Furthermore, compared to hybrid methods combining classical image processing with CNNs46, our approach is fully data-driven and requires no hand-crafted feature engineering, potentially offering better generalization across diverse crack appearances and surface conditions.

In essence, our contribution is orthogonal to many recent trends. We do not introduce a new model, a compression technique, or a hybrid pipeline. Instead, we provide a powerful training framework that elevates existing, efficient models to be more competitive with specialized alternatives, effectively narrowing the performance gap between lightweight and heavyweight detectors for the specific task of crack detection. This positions our work as a foundational step that can be integrated into various pipelines seeking to combine high accuracy with operational efficiency.

The observed performance gap between the Pseudo-Labeling and Knowledge Distillation strategies can be further attributed to specific architectural asymmetries between the YOLOv11-L teacher and YOLOv11-N student. The mismatch is most pronounced in the deeper stages of the backbone and the neck’s feature pyramid layers. The teacher’s larger model possesses significantly more channels and a deeper stack of C2f (or C3) modules in these stages, allowing it to learn richer, high-dimensional feature representations for multi-scale crack patterns. When distilling knowledge via feature or logit imitation, the student’s limited capacity in these layers—fewer channels and shallower structures—creates a representational bottleneck. It cannot fully accommodate or replicate the teacher’s complex feature maps, leading to a loss of fine-grained spatial and contextual information during transfer, which is crucial for precise crack localization (reflected in the lower mAP50-95).

A promising direction to mitigate this issue, suggested by the reviewer, involves employing feature-alignment modules. Introducing lightweight projection layers (e.g., 1 × 1 convolutions or small MLPs) at the distillation points could map the student’s intermediate features into a space that is dimensionally aligned with the teacher’s. Alternatively, attention-based adaptation modules could help the student focus on the most transferable regions of the teacher’s feature maps. These adaptations would not significantly burden the student’s lean architecture but could facilitate a more effective “translation” of knowledge, potentially closing the performance gap between KD and PL. Exploring such adaptive distillation mechanisms remains an interesting avenue for future work, as noted in the following section.

Conclusion

This study successfully demonstrated that knowledge transfer strategies can substantially elevate the performance of lightweight object detection models for structural crack detection without requiring architectural modifications. Two distinct approaches were systematically evaluated: Pseudo-Labeling and Knowledge Distillation, both utilizing a high-capacity YOLOv11-L teacher model to enhance a compact YOLOv11-N student model with only 2.6 million parameters.

Pseudo-Labeling method achieved exceptional results, enabling the student model to attain a mAP50 of 0.81 and mAP50-95 of 0.59, approaching the teacher model’s performance (mAP50: 0.84, mAP50-95: 0.60) while maintaining inference speeds exceeding 600 FPS. This approach exhibited remarkable training stability with smooth convergence across all loss components and balanced optimization. The Knowledge Distillation method demonstrated superior convergence speed and sample efficiency, particularly excelling in precise localization tasks through focused optimization of Distribution Focal Loss, though it introduced greater metric volatility during training.

Comparative benchmarking against state-of-the-art lightweight detectors (MobileNetV3-SSD, YOLOv8n, EfficientDet-D0) confirmed that the proposed Pseudo-Labeling approach achieves an optimal balance between accuracy and computational efficiency, making it highly suitable for edge deployment scenarios such as drone-based structural inspections. The results validate that training methodology innovation presents a viable and flexible pathway for developing high-performance, resource-efficient crack detection systems, offering a complementary alternative to architectural redesign or post-training compression techniques. Future research will explore hybrid training regimes combining both strategies, semi-supervised learning with larger unlabeled datasets, and real-world deployment validation on edge computing platforms.
