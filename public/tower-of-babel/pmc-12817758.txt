CDCP1 allosterically regulates the AMPK α1 subunit to enhance fatty acid oxidation in osteoblasts
Authors: Xiang Li, Weichun Zhu, Jinlong Ma, Zhenqian Sun, Limin Wang, Guangjun Jiao, Yunzhen Chen
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundLipid metabolism disorders in osteoblasts may lead to osteoporosis. CUB domain-containing protein 1 (CDCP1) is associated with various intracellular signaling pathways. We investigated how CDCP1 regulates lipid metabolism and osteoblast function.MethodsThis study utilized gene overexpression (via lentivirus) and loss-of-function (CRISPR/Cas9-mediated knockout) techniques to investigate the involvement of CDCP1 in lipid metabolism and osteogenesis. Transcriptomic and metabolomic analyses were performed to examine the mechanism of action of CDCP1. Furthermore, proximity ligation assays, GST pull-down, and molecular docking were employed to identify the interaction between CDCP1 and AMP-activated protein kinase (AMPK).ResultsCDCP1 alleviated bone loss in mice. In vitro, CDCP1 promoted the phosphorylation of AMPK. Phosphorylated AMPK can enhance the activity of carnitine palmitoyltransferase, leading to increased fatty acid oxidation and promoting osteogenesis. Mechanistically, CDCP1 prevents the formation of the autoinhibitory conformation of the autoinhibitory domain by binding to the α3 helix, thereby protecting AMPK phosphorylation from self-inhibition.ConclusionsOur research revealed a new molecular mechanism linking CDCP1 and allosteric control of AMPK. These findings reveal for the first time the mechanism by which CDCP1 affects osteogenesis through lipid metabolism regulation, suggesting its potential as a therapeutic target for osteoporosis.Graphical abstract Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s11658-025-00832-5.

Introduction

Bone homeostasis relies on the equilibrium between osteoblast-mediated bone formation and osteoclast-mediated bone resorption. Osteoporosis and other conditions can emerge from osteoblast dysfunction [1]. Osteoblasts, originating from mesenchymal progenitor cells, play a vital role in depositing new bone matrix and mineralization, necessitating substantial energy. Fatty acid oxidation (FAO) stands out as a crucial energy source in cells. Disrupting FAO with certain drugs can hinder osteoblast function [2]. Enhancing FAO not only supports collagen synthesis but also boosts alkaline phosphatase (ALP) activity [3]. An elevation in FAO as osteoblasts mature leads to heightened mineralizing activity [2]. In addition to the energy supply, lipids also influence the bone microenvironment. As individuals age, adipocytes within the bone marrow produce copious amounts of fatty acids [4]. Studies have indicated that lipotoxicity resulting from an over-accumulation of fatty acids harms osteoblasts, hindering their function [5, 6]. Lipid metabolism within bones may have systemic implications as well. Research by Niemeier et al. showed that bone contributes to postprandial lipid metabolism, with osteoblasts utilizing fatty acids to help clear circulating lipids and maintain systemic lipid balance [7]. Therefore, enhancing FAO in osteoblasts may mitigate lipotoxicity and promote bone formation.

CUB domain-containing protein 1 (CDCP1), also known as SIMA135, has garnered increasing attention as a potential biomarker and therapeutic target for various tumors [8, 9]. It plays functional roles in human diseases and serves as a significant mediator of diverse molecular cascade reactions. CDCP1 has been reported to be associated with the PI3K/AKT, WNT, and RAS/ERK axes, among others [10]. Moreover, it is involved in the metabolic regulation of cells. Wright et al. reported that CDCP1 promotes FAO and reduces lipid droplet abundance in breast cancer cells by regulating acyl coenzyme A synthetase [11]. Nevertheless, the potential regulation of lipid metabolism by CDCP1 in osteoblasts remains unclear.

Our study identified CDCP1 as a novel factor that regulates osteoblast function through FAO. Experimental data indicate that CDCP1 enhances osteogenesis and mitigates in vivo bone loss. Specifically, CDCP1 hinders the formation of the autoinhibitory conformation of the autoinhibitory domain (AID) through its interaction with the AID of the AMP-activated protein kinase (AMPK) α1 subunit. This interaction protects AMPK phosphorylation from self-inhibition, consequently promoting FAO. These findings imply that CDCP1 may represent a novel therapeutic target for osteoporosis in the future.

Materials and methods

The Supplementary Methods section contains further experimental details regarding cell culture, transfection, microquantitative computed tomography (micro‐CT), histological analysis, immunoblot, immunoprecipitation, immunofluorescence, cellular metabolism analysis, RNA extraction, qRT-PCR, omic assays, bioinformatics analysis, osteoblastic induction, and cell viability and apoptosis assays.

Animals

C57BL/6 J mice were obtained from Vital River Co., Ltd. (Beijing, China) and were housed in a specific pathogen-free environment at a temperature of 22–24 °C under a 12-h light/dark cycle. Upon acclimatization, the mice were randomly assigned to groups (n = 7).

In a previous study, a model of postmenopausal osteoporosis was induced in 10-week-old female mice [12]. Four weeks post-ovariectomy, the female mice received lentiviral intramedullary injections at a concentration of 1 × 107/mL [12]. Similarly, a model of senile osteoporosis was established using 72-week-old male mice, which were immediately administered with lentiviral injections at the same concentration [13]. Furthermore, certain mice were subjected to intraperitoneal injections of 1 mg/kg etomoxir twice per week after lentiviral injection [14].

Proximity ligation assay (PLA)

The Duolink-PLA assay was conducted to detect the interaction between CDCP1 and AMPK utilizing the Duolink In Situ Kit according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Specifically, CDCP1 and AMPK were introduced into cell lysis solutions overnight. Subsequently, following antibody removal, the cells were incubated with PLUS and MINUS PLA probes for 1 h at 37 °C. The PLA signals were visualized using a Zeiss laser confocal microscope 900.

Molecular docking

The sequences of human AMPK and CDCP1 were retrieved from the UniProt database and modeled through AlphaFold. Ramachandran diagrams were utilized to evaluate the conformational plausibility (the number of residues in the disallowed regions was < 5%).

HDOCK was used to perform molecular docking [15, 16]. AMPK was designated as the receptor, and CDCP1 was designated as the ligand. The AID of AMPK, spanning residues 302–381, was specifically designated as the docking site.

The preliminary conformation of AMPK/CDCP1 was utilized as the input structure for a molecular dynamic simulation using Discovery Studio 2019 to determine the stable binding mode between AMPK and CDCP1. To pinpoint a stable conformation for the dynamic simulation of AMPK/CDCP1, a virtual saturation mutation technique was employed by individually replacing the interaction interface residues of AMPK with all essential amino acids, followed by an analysis of the mutation energy. In addition, the interaction interface residues of AMPK-AID underwent virtual alanine scanning following the Protein Design module’s “build mutation protocol” in Discovery Studio 2019, and the binding energy was computed using the “calculate binding energy protocol” in the same software. PyMOL (version 2.5) and Ligplot (version 2.2) were used for visualization.

Ethics statement and statistical analysis

All animal experiments were approved by the Animal Ethics Committee of Qilu Hospital of Shandong University. The animals received humane care according to the criteria outlined in the Guide for the Care and Use of Laboratory Animals prepared by the National Academy of Sciences and published by the National Institutes of Health.

The experimental plan received approval from the Medical Ethics Committee of Shandong University Qilu Hospital. The human bone samples utilized in this study were obtained from surgically removed bone tissue, and their collection did not affect the treatment. The demographic and clinical characteristics of the participants are listed in Supplementary Table 1.

All analyses were conducted using R (version 4.2.3). The data are presented as the means ± standard deviations from three or more experiments. Group differences (n < 3) were evaluated using either a two-tailed Student’s t test or a Mann‒Whitney U test (both two-tailed). For group comparisons when n ≥ 3, one-way ANOVA followed by Bonferroni post hoc analysis was employed to assess pairwise differences between groups. P < 0.05 indicated statistical significance. The primers, antibodies, and commercial kits utilized in this study are listed in Supplementary Tables 2–4.

Results

CDCP1 positively correlates with FAO in osteogenesis

To investigate the role of CDCP1 in bone formation, we initially analyzed data from public databases. The data from GSE148203 indicated that the expression of CDCP1 increased during osteogenesis. On the ninth day of osteogenesis, its expression level was tens of times higher than the initial state (Supplementary Fig. 1A). Moreover, we observed a significant positive correlation (R = 0.552) between CDCP1 expression and FAO scores (Supplementary Fig. 1B).

Transcriptomic analysis of primary osteoblasts from women with osteoporotic fractures revealed a substantial decrease in CDCP1 expression (Supplementary Fig. 1C, D). We validated this finding using clinical samples (n = 18) from our institution, which showed a significant decrease in the expression of CDCP1 in the bones of patients with osteoporosis, both at the mRNA and protein levels (Supplementary Fig. 1E, F). Thus, these findings suggest that CDCP1 may play a positive role in osteogenesis by regulating FAO.

CDCP1 alleviates lipotoxicity and promotes osteogenesis by promoting FAO

First, we stably overexpressed and knocked out CDCP1 in mouse osteoblasts (MC3T3-E1) (Supplementary Fig. 2A–C). The data indicated that altered expression of CDCP1 and treatment with etomoxir (an inhibitor of FAO) did not significantly impact osteoblast proliferation or apoptosis (Supplementary Fig. 3A, B). These ruled out the possibility of CDCP1 and etomoxir regulating osteogenesis by affecting the number of osteoblasts.

Palmitate is the most prevalent fatty acid in the bone marrow environment in postmenopausal patients with osteoporosis [8]. Lipotoxicity was induced by exposing the cells to palmitate (250 µM) in this study [17]. We found that CDCP1 overexpression markedly reduced the presence of lipid droplets (Fig. 1A, B) and the accumulation of lipids (including free fatty acids, triglycerides, and cholesterol) (Fig. 1C). Osteoblasts that overexpressed CDCP1 exhibited improved cellular viability and lower levels of apoptosis (Fig. 1D, E). The overexpression of CDCP1 led to enhanced expression of osteogenesis-related proteins (COL1A1, SPP1, and osteocalcin/OCN) (Fig. 1F) and an increase in ALP activity (Fig. 1G, H). Furthermore, extracellular matrix mineralization was significantly enhanced in osteoblasts overexpressing CDCP1 (Fig. 1I). Conversely, all of these effects of CDCP1 were attenuated to varying degrees after the addition of etomoxir. The results revealed a reduction in the levels of several long-chain fatty acids compared with those in the control group (Fig. 4A). We further performed transcriptomic analysis (n = 6) and assessed the reliability of the obtained data. As illustrated in Fig. 4B, there was no notable disparity in the mRNA expression of the crucial enzymes implicated in FAO. This suggested that CDCP1 was more likely to impact the activity of enzymes through specific pathways rather than directly stimulating enzyme expression, thereby promoting FAO. Consequently, a pathway enrichment analysis was carried out. And the AMPK signaling pathway caught our attention (Fig. 4C).

AMPK, a crucial regulator of energy homeostasis, is ubiquitously expressed in eukaryotic cells [18]. The main event required for complete activation of AMPK is the phosphorylation of Thr172 in the α-subunit [19]. Activated AMPK controls lipid metabolism by directly phosphorylating two key enzymes, i.e., acetyl-CoA carboxylase 1 (ACC1) (FA synthesis) and ACC2 (FA oxidation). Simultaneously, AMPK promotes FAO by relieving the inhibition of carnitine palmitoyltransferase (CPT) by malonyl-CoA, which is locally generated by ACC at the outer mitochondrial membrane [20]. Consequently, changes in the levels of AMPK and a range of its downstream molecules were examined.

The data revealed a significant increase in the phosphorylation level of AMPK in cells overexpressing CDCP1 compared with that in control cells. Knockout of CDCP1 also led to a decrease in the level of phosphorylated AMPK (Fig. 4D). Similar results were found in mouse femurs, where the phosphorylation level of AMPK was significantly elevated in femurs supplemented with CDCP1, regardless of the administration of etomoxir (Fig. 4E). In addition, the activities of ACC, CPT, and malonyl-CoA changed as anticipated. In both cells and femurs, increased AMPK phosphorylation inhibited ACC activity, reduced malonyl coenzyme A levels, and enhanced CPT activity (Fig. 4F, G). Further data indicate that phosphorylated AMPK promoted the phosphorylation of ACC2 and inhibited the phosphorylation of ACC1 both in vivo and in vitro (Fig. 4H, I).

We sought to investigate the impact of CDCP1 on FAO and osteoblasts by inhibiting the phosphorylation of AMPK using compound C (dorsomorphin). Immunoblots demonstrated that compound C effectively inhibited the phosphorylation of AMPK (Supplementary Fig. 6). The further findings revealed that inhibiting the phosphorylation of AMPK led to an increase in ACC activity and malonyl-CoA content, while CPT activity decreased (Fig. 5A). The stimulatory effect of CDCP1 on FAO was reversed (Fig. 5B), resulting in lipid accumulation within the cells (Fig. 5C–E). Furthermore, the expression of osteogenesis-related proteins influenced by CDCP1 decreased upon the inhibition of AMPK phosphorylation (Fig. 5F). Upon examining interspecies differences, we conducted a comparative analysis of the sequence and structure of human and mouse CDCP1. CDCP1 interacts with the autoinhibitory domain of the AMPK α1 subunit

To decipher the potential mechanism through which CDCP1 modulates AMPK phosphorylation, we initially conducted co-immunoprecipitation (Co-IP) assays. The Co-IP experiments conducted on MC3T3-E1 and HEK293 cells confirmed a binding association between CDCP1 and AMPK, as shown in Fig. 6B. In addition, immunofluorescence analysis revealed the colocalization of CDCP1 and AMPK within MC3T3-E1 cells (Fig. 6C). Through proximity ligation assays, we observed red fluorescence resulting from the intracellular interaction between CDCP1 and AMPK (Fig. 6D). These data demonstrated an intracellular interaction between CDCP1 and AMPK.

AMPK exists as a trimeric complex consisting of a catalytic subunit (α-subunit) and two regulatory subunits (β-subunit and γ-subunit). In mammals, the α-subunit has two isoforms, while the β-subunit and γ-subunit have two and three isoforms, respectively. The expression levels of these AMPK isoforms vary across tissues, resulting in diverse subunit compositions in different cell types, with the most prevalent isoforms being α1, β1, and γ1 [20]. We generated various vectors encoding the sequence of AMPK. Co-IP and GST (Glutathione-S-Transferase) pull-down was utilized to identify which isomers could interact with CDCP1. The data demonstrated that intact AMPK could pull down CDCP1. However, this interaction vanished in the absence of the α1 subunit (Fig. 6E, F), indicating that CDCP1 binds to AMPK through the α1 subunit. In addition, CDCP1 could promote the phosphorylation level of AMPK only when the α1 subunit was present (Fig. 6G).

The α-subunit of AMPK is composed of four structural domains, with the N-terminal kinase domain (KD) being the most crucial. When threonine 172 is phosphorylated, KD becomes active, making it a commonly used indicator of AMPK activation [21]. Adjacent to the KD on the carboxyl side are the autoinhibitory domain (AID), the linker domain (LD), and the C-terminal domain (CTD). Subsequent GST pull-down experiments confirmed that CDCP1 only co-precipitated with the α1 subunit of AMPK when AID was present (Fig. 6H). These results signify the essential role of AID in regulating CDCP1-mediated AMPK phosphorylation.

In addition, In western blot analyses of CDCP1, we consistently detected two predominant bands: one at approximately 130 kDa and the other at ~70 kDa. According to previous studies, the 130 kDa band represents the full-length CDCP1 protein. The ~70 kDa band is recognized as a proteolytically cleaved fragment of CDCP1 [10]. This cleavage event is a common post-translational modification of CDCP1 and may be implicated in the regulation of its function. The consistent presence of this ~70 kDa fragment across various cell lines and femur samples supports its identity as a specific, biologically relevant cleavage product rather than nonspecific degradation.

CDCP1 maintains AMPK phosphorylation by inhibiting AID

Our attempts to investigate the binding of CDCP1 to AMPK-AID through co-crystallization experiments were unsuccessful in obtaining diffractable crystals. Subsequently, computational modeling was employed to predict the potential binding modes between CDCP1 and AMPK-AID. AlphaFold was utilized to generate plausible protein structures for conducting molecular docking simulations (Supplementary Fig. 8). Among the generated models, the complex with the most negative docking score was chosen for further analysis (Fig. 7A). The elucidation of the interaction details between CDCP1 and AMPK-AID was carried out using Ligplot (Fig. 7B). Specifically, Phe351 in AMPK-AID engaged in hydrogen bonding and ligand bonding with CDCP1, while Glu373 formed a salt bridge and ligand bond with CDCP1. In addition, multiple hydrophobic interactions were observed between CDCP1 and AMPK-AID. Subsequent molecular dynamics simulations confirmed the stable binding of CDCP1 to AMPK-AID (Fig. 7C, D). Analysis revealed that eight residues within AMPK-AID were involved in interactions with CDCP1 (Fig. 7E); further comprehensive information is provided in Supplementary Table 6. Examination of the interactions formed by these residues with CDCP1 indicated that all residues, barring phenylalanine, predominantly localized in and around the α3 helix. In order to strengthen this result, we subsequently conducted virtual alanine scanning by mutating the interaction interface residues of AMPK-AID to alanine, and the results showed that the mutation of Phe377 to alanine resulted in the highest mutation energy. Moreover, mutations of Phe377, Glu373, Pro372, His371, and Phe351 were found to destabilize the AMPK-AID/CDCP1 complex (Supplementary Fig. 9A). Next, we performed virtual saturation mutagenesis by mutating Phe377, Glu373, Pro372, His371, and Phe351 to all essential amino acids, and then analyzed the mutation energy. The results indicated that mutating His371 and Glu373 to Tyr would lead to a more stable complex. This suggests that His371 and Glu373 may not be essential for the binding of AMPK-AID and CDCP1. However, regardless of the essential amino acid they were mutated to, mutations of Phe351, Pro372, and Phe377 were more likely to destabilize the AMPK-AID/CDCP1 complex, indicating that these three residues are indispensable for the interaction between AMPK-AID and CDCP1 (Supplementary Fig. 9B).

Subsequent to the targeted mutagenesis of the three residues, a GST pull-down assay was performed to illustrate that the mutated AID failed to interact with CDCP1 in vitro (Fig. 7F). Considering that the presence of fusion partners may affect the natural conformation of proteins, thereby impacting the detection of interactions, we employed various fusion protein tags, such as HA and Flag tags, to minimize the impact on protein conformation.

Subsequently, endogenous AMPK was depleted in MC3T3-E1 cells and both wild-type (WT) and mutant (Mut) AMPK plasmids were transfected for Co-IP analysis to evaluate the impact of the mutation on the interplay between CDCP1 and AMPK-AID. Results indicated that the mutated AID still did not form a complex with CDCP1 in vitro (Fig. 7G). Furthermore, the phosphorylation level of mutant AMPK remained unchanged compared with the WT counterpart (Fig. 7H). Nevertheless, the phosphorylation level of AMPK in cells overexpressing CDCP1 and transfected with mutant AMPK was lower than in cells transfected with the WT control (Fig. 7I). These results support our hypothesis that CDCP1 prevents the formation of the autoinhibitory conformation of AID by binding to the α3 helix, thereby protecting AMPK phosphorylation from self-inhibition.

Discussion

In the present study, our aim was to investigate the role of CDCP1 in osteoblasts. Initially, we assessed the expression of CDCP1 in osteoporosis and established that cytoplasmic CDCP1 enhances osteogenesis by activating AMPK-mediated FAO. A previous study suggested a transcription-independent function of CDCP1 in lipid metabolism. We found a similar phenomenon. Our findings indicate that CDCP1 does not influence the transcription of enzymes but rather promotes FAO by regulating their activity. In addition, we used two animal models of osteoporosis in our study. We observed that CDCP1 mitigated bone loss in both aged male mice and ovariectomized female mice, suggesting that the CDCP1-dependent mechanism is not specific to age or sex.

Lipids are one of the basic components of the human body and have various biological functions. Evidence suggests that lipids from food, especially essential fatty acids, polyunsaturated fatty acids, and fat-soluble vitamins, play an important role in the functioning of osteoblasts. Adamek et al. demonstrated that parathyroid hormone and 1,25-dihydroxyvitamin D3 can promote bone formation by enhancing FAO [23]. However, with increasing age, especially when osteoporosis occurs, adipocytes occupy a large portion of the bone marrow environment, which may lead to lipotoxicity [4]. The use of statin drugs to lower blood lipids effectively reduces the risk of fractures and increases bone density [24]. Preventing lipotoxicity in the skeleton has been proposed as a new approach for treating osteoporosis [25]. Osteoblasts are the primary bone producers, generating the extracellular protein composition that forms the main component of the skeleton. Understanding the detailed mechanisms that regulate osteoblast differentiation and function is important for clinical applications, such as treating osteoporosis. FAO is one of the main ways in which cells consume lipids. CDCP1 enhances osteoblast FAO, which can both prevent lipid overload and meet substantial energy demands during bone remodeling. Therefore, CDCP1 may be a target that can regulate both fat metabolism and bone mass.

Our research revealed that the interaction with AMPK is an important pathway through which CDCP1 participates in metabolic regulation. As a balancer of energy in eukaryotic cells, AMPK is a highly conserved protein that has evolved. In addition to regulating metabolism, AMPK can also respond to DNA damage, lysosomal damage, and autophagy [26]. In this study, we focused on the role of AMPK in osteoblasts. The data show that the interaction between AMPK α1 and CDCP1 promotes the function of osteoblasts. However, the subunit composition of AMPK is different in different cell types. For example, in skeletal muscle and cardiac muscle, the main subunit of AMPK is α2 [27]. Considering the selectivity of CDCP1-mediated collaboration with AMPK α1, this collaboration cannot be fully generalized to other types of cells. Further research is necessary to expand the subunit range of interactions between CDCP1 and AMPK.

The key to AMPK activation is the phosphorylation of conserved serine residues in the KD activation loop. In addition to AMP and ADP, there are also several noncanonical activation mechanisms, such as Ca2 + /calmodulin-dependent kinase kinases [26]. In addition to these external regulatory mechanisms, the internal regulation of AMPK phosphorylation mainly depends on the AID of the α subunit [20]. Conformational changes in AID play a crucial role in regulating AMPK activity [28, 29]. AID comprises three α-helices, with the α3 helix capable of binding to the N-terminal leaflet of the KD and inhibiting its activity. The AID can rotate away from the KD via the LD and restore KD activity [30]. In this study, we found that the binding of CDCP1 to the AID can maintain the phosphorylation conformation of AMPK. In this case, we consider CDCP1 to be a natural ligand for AMPK that promotes FAO through the regulation of AMPK phosphorylation.

Conclusions

These collective findings offer insights into the role of CDCP1 in the regulation of lipid metabolism during osteoblast differentiation. CDCP1 promotes FAO by preventing the self-repression of AMPK phosphorylation through binding to the AID of the AMPK α1 subunit. The AID of AMPK is fundamental for CDCP1-mediated lipid metabolism regulation. Moreover, we present a proof-of-concept that targeting osteoblast differentiation through lipid metabolism could be a potential strategy for treating osteoporosis.
