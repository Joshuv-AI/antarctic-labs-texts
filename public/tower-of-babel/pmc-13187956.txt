Advancing HIV drug resistance testing: A leap forward with DNA-based genotyping
Authors: Yan Jiang
Published: 2026

HIV drug resistance (HIVDR) has emerged as a critical threat to global efforts to end the acquired immune deficiency syndrome (AIDS) epidemic by 2030. With approximately 39 million people living with human immunodeficiency virus (HIV) worldwide and nearly 30 million on antiretroviral therapy (ART), the rise of resistant strains jeopardizes decades of progress in treatment and prevention. According to a 2023 cross-sectional survey covering all 31 provincial-level divisions in China, the prevalence of transmitted drug resistance (TDR) among newly diagnosed individuals has reached 11.4%, with resistance to non-nucleoside reverse transcriptase inhibitors (NNRTIs) specifically at 7.9% [1]. Such a situation mirrors the alarming trajectory of tuberculosis drug resistance, where delayed interventions led to entrenched, hard-to-treat strains.

The current cornerstone of HIVDR monitoring is the drug resistance testing (DRT) with plasma HIV-1 RNA (RNA DRT), typically using Sanger sequencing (SS). However, RNA DRT has a significant limitation: it often fails in individuals with low-level viremia (LLV), defined as a viral load (VL) between 50 and 1000 copies/mL, or in those with residual viremia (<50 copies/mL). This failure creates a critical gap in clinical management, leaving physicians without guidance for a substantial portion of patients who may be experiencing treatment failure or are at risk of developing complications. While international research increasingly validates HIV-1 DNA DRT (DNA DRT) as a feasible tool for managing LLV, yet it remains cautiously positioned in major guidelines due to concerns regarding the clinical interpretation of archived proviral resistance. The study by Wu et al. [2] in the Journal of Pharmaceutical Analysis systematically addresses this challenge, presenting a large-scale, nationwide evaluation that powerfully argues for the integration of DNA DRT with whole blood into standard clinical practice [2].

In my opinion, Wu et al. [2] propose a dual-pronged, innovative strategy that enhances existing RNA DRT and allows comprehensive comparison of RNA and DNA DRTs among different VL strata. Specifically, in addition to the optimization of amplification primers, the authors developed a novel pre-analytical viral enrichment strategy of extracellular particle (EP)-HIV-1 co-isolation, which demonstrated high proficiency in enriching and recovering HIV-1 viral particles. With such a strategy, they showed that the RNA amplification success rate could be significantly boosted for LLV samples to over 74.8%.

Second, by analyzing 10,903 samples from 9428 people living with HIV-1 across 25 Chinese provinces, including 1475 with paired RNA-DNA results, the study provides an unprecedented dataset. The study confirms that drug resistance mutations (DRMs) are prevalent across all VL strata, including those with suppressed viremia (<50 copies/mL). Crucially, it demonstrates that DNA DRT itself is highly feasible, with amplification success rates exceeding 93.5% for samples above the limit of quantification and remaining above 80.4% even for samples with VL < 50 copies/mL. The core of the validation lies in the reproducibility analysis. Results were categorized as concordant (identical DRMs), degenerate (DNA results include all RNA DRMs plus others), or discordant (completely different DRMs). The combined concordance/degeneracy (C/D) rates between RNA and DNA DRTs were remarkably high, ranging from 90.4% to 100% across different gene regions. Importantly, discordance was associated with significantly lower plasma VLs, suggesting stochastic sampling effects during SS at low template concentrations.

Third, perhaps even more critical for clinical translation is the high agreement (nearly 90%) in the final drug susceptibility interpretation, which is the ultimate metric guiding therapeutic decisions. Based on Stanford penalty scores for 25 ART drugs, the agreement rate for drug resistance interpretations between DNA and RNA DRTs was 98.3% ± 1.7% in the degeneracy group. Even within the discordance group, the mean agreement remained high at 89.5% ± 5.0%, with only four NNRTIs (doravirine, efavirenz, nevirapine, rilpivirine) showing agreement below 85%. This indicates that while minor sequence-level variations may occur, the core clinical message regarding drug susceptibility remains robust when using DNA DRT. There is favorable evidence from clinical practice linking ART regimen adjustments based on DNA DRT to subsequent virologic suppression [3]. Furthermore, a particularly interesting contribution of this work is the elucidation of the mathematical basis for this high reproducibility. Using single-molecule real-time (SMRT) sequencing, the team experimentally determined that the analytical sensitivity limit for a viral quasispecies to be reliably detected as a dominant sequence in SS-based DNA DRT is 24.6%. This quantification provides an evidence-based standard for interpreting DNA DRT results.

The practical advantages of DNA DRT are equally significant for public health planning. The test uses more stable whole blood samples, simplifying logistics by eliminating the need for immediate plasma separation and complex cold-chain transportation. Furthermore, the simplified workflow reportedly reduces reagent costs by 47%, making scalable HIVDR monitoring more feasible.

It is important to recognize that DNA DRT is inherently biased toward detecting dominantly archived DRMs within the proviral reservoir. For patients experiencing recent treatment failure, the plasma HIV-1 RNA may originate from minority quasispecies within the proviral DNA pool that fall below the detection threshold of DNA DRT. The transition of these minority variants into dominant proviral populations requires a period of viral evolution and selection, which explains the higher frequency of discordant results between RNA and DNA DRT observed in LLV samples. When DRMs associated with the four NNRTIs exhibiting the most discordant resistance profiles are detected by DNA DRT, particularly in LLV samples, clinicians should be aware that these results may differ from those obtained through RNA DRT and should interpret them in the context of the patient's treatment history and current viral dynamics.

In summary, the large-scale, real-world validation provided by Wu et al. [2] establishes DNA DRT as an essential component of the clinical toolkit for managing HIV. The research argues compellingly for the integration of DNA DRT into clinical guidelines. For settings that prefer RNA DRT, the research offers optimized primers and EP-HIV co-isolation methods to enhance RNA amplification sensitivity. In contrast, for settings where conditions permit, the evidence presented by Wu et al. [2] supports DNA DRT as a first-line option. Overall, this work provides a well-validated and practical strategy for China's healthcare system and for global efforts to proactively address HIVDR. Future research should focus on establishing standardized international thresholds for DNA-based resistance reporting, and longitudinal studies are warranted to evaluate if DNA-guided therapy can prevent virologic failure in patients with LLV.
