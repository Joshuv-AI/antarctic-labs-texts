When orcas hit the rudder: what Iberian orca–boat interactions reveal about action explanations in animal cognition
Authors: Christian Frings
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractSince 2020, Iberian orcas have repeatedly interacted with sailing vessels, often targeting rudders. These well-documented interactions have been discussed primarily as play, exploration, maladaptation, or a product of cultural transmission. Here, I argue for a complementary interpretation. This phenomenon also exposes a broader explanatory issue in animal cognition: complex animal behavior in the wild is often described without an explicit theory of action. Drawing on cognitive action–effect approaches, I suggest that rudder-directed interactions may also be understood as actions organized around anticipated environmental consequences. The orca case thus highlights the need for action models that can travel across species, ecological contexts, and disciplinary boundaries.

Since 2020, a small subpopulation of Iberian orcas has repeatedly interacted with sailing vessels in Iberian waters, often targeting rudders and in some cases rendering boats unnavigable. These interactions are now well documented and have attracted public and scientific attention (Esteban et al. 2022; Díaz López and Methion 2024). They are striking not only because they involve repeated contact with human artifacts, but because they appear structured, recurrent, and selective. Current explanations typically invoke play, exploration, maladaptive responses to anthropogenic stimuli, or cultural transmission (Esteban et al. 2022; Díaz López and Methion 2024). These explanations may all be relevant, but they primarily address the classification, elicitation, or social propagation of the behavior rather than its online organization as action. Here, action refers to behavior whose selection and execution are systematically related to its anticipated or currently sampled environmental consequences. In this article, I argue (i) that applying action-effect approaches to the orca case can add another layer of understanding that might even suggest specific interventions and (ii) that because of the complexity of the behavior, the public attention it has attracted, and the potential social and cognitive complexity of the species, orca-boat interactions are a particular informative test-case for attempting to apply newer cognitive frameworks to wild animal behavior.

The distinction between behavior and action matters for animal cognition. In many areas of cognitive science, action is not understood as a mere response to proximal stimuli (e.g., touching the light switch). Instead, action is often modeled as being organized around anticipated distal effects (e.g., turning the light on) that is actions are goal-oriented. In the Theory of Event Coding (TEC), perception and action are linked in a common representational medium with distal reference (Hommel et al. 2001). In the Binding and Retrieval in Action Control (BRAC) framework, this picture is sharpened by emphasizing how feature binding and retrieval dynamically structure action control across contexts (Frings et al. 2020). On such accounts, organisms do not simply react. They selectively re-enact action–effect couplings, that is, they use previous episodes of action-effect coupling to predict the sensory outcome of an action. TEC and BRAC are contemporary approaches in the tradition of ideomotor theorizing, yet there are many other contemporary approaches that push in a similar direction by treating action as the control of expected sensory consequences rather than as an output appended to perception, including predictive coding or active inference approaches (Friston 2010; Pezzulo et al. 2024), or ecological and embodied approaches inspired by Gibson, as well as related enactive theories (Gibson 1979; Barrett 2011). Thus, the action-effect perspective I suggest applying to the orca-boat interactions in this article is not bound to one particular theory but follows from several cognitive frameworks.

My claim is not that Iberian orca–boat interactions are already explained by action-effect approaches. Rather, it is that the current explanations can be enhanced by adding another layer in terms of action-effect couplings. The Iberian orca case is particularly interesting because the observed complex pattern seems readily describable in action-theoretic terms. Reports consistently describe orcas approaching vessels from the stern, interacting disproportionately with rudders rather than hulls, and persisting in ways that alter resistance, vibration, and boat motion (Esteban et al. 2022). These are not just properties of the stimulus. They are salient effects produced by intervening on a particular functional part of an artifact. From the perspective of cognitive action frameworks, the central question is therefore not only what elicited the behavior, but what environmental changes the animal may be controlling, probing, or re-instantiating through repeated rudder-directed acts.

A stimulus–response account can say that a moving vessel constitutes a salient stimulus, perhaps especially under certain ecological conditions (indeed most reported interactions concern the rudder (Esteban et al. 2022) and the rudder might be a perceptually distinct stimulus that triggers a response). This may help explain why interactions begin. Once the behavior shows reliable target selectivity, such an account becomes less informative unless it is supplemented by a theory of why that target matters for the organism. A rudder is not merely one region among many. It is a functionally distinctive part of the boat, and contact with it produces characteristic distal effects on motion and resistance. If those effects are behaviorally relevant, then a purely trigger-based explanation seems incomplete.

Cultural-transmission accounts primarily explain how a behavioral pattern spreads through a group (Esteban et al. 2022); by themselves, they do not necessarily specify how the behavior is organized online. Saying that a behavioral pattern is socially learned does not yet say what is learned: a movement routine, a target object, a local regularity, or a controllable effect. This distinction is important. If the relevant unit is an action–effect relation, then the spread of the phenomenon may itself be better understood as the spread of an effect-oriented intervention on a manipulable object. In other words, cultural transmission may be part of the story without being the whole story. Social learning could explain how individuals acquire a pattern whose internal organization is characterized at the level of action-effect coupling.

The action–effect perspective therefore adds another layer to orca-boat interactions that complements stimulus-response and cultural transmission approaches. It asks whether the behavior can be understood as an action organized around anticipated environmental consequences of rudder contact, such as altered movement, increased resistance, or characteristic vibrations. This does not commit us to a strong claim about mental representation. It merely takes seriously the possibility that the observed selectivity of the behavior reflects not just what the animal encounters, but what the animal is doing to the environment. That is a modest claim, but an important one. It shifts the explanatory focus from “what class of behavior is this?” to “what kind of environmental control does this behavior instantiate?” This reframing also has empirical consequences. It shifts attention away from broad classificatory labels and toward more precise questions. Which distal effects are most behaviorally diagnostic? Are the interactions better predicted by rudder shape, vessel speed, water flow, vibration, or other effect-relevant properties? How stable are rudder-focused action–effect couplings across contexts? How flexibly do individuals switch targets when expected effects fail? What exactly is transmitted when the pattern spreads socially: a target preference, a sequence of movements, or sensitivity to a class of consequences? These questions are not opposed to biological explanation. They are biological questions asked against a richer cognitive conception of action that follow from modern cognitive frameworks of action (Hommel et al. 2001; Frings et al. 2020; Friston 2010; Pezzulo et al. 2024). It should also be noted that this way of thinking is not alien to comparative cognition (Barrett 2011). Recent work shows that monkeys can use visual simulation to solve complex physical problems, suggesting prospective control over action in relation to predicted environmental dynamics (Ahuja et al. 2024). Other comparative work has explored future-oriented planning, prospective information seeking, rational monitoring, and insight-like problem solving in non-human animals (Barrett 2011; Hope et al. 2025). Across taxa, animal behavior is increasingly interpreted through concepts such as expectation, simulation, planning, monitoring, and prospective control. Nevertheless, contemporary cognitive action frameworks have been applied less systematically to large-scale, naturally occurring interactions with human-made artifacts than to controlled laboratory behavior.

A likely objection especially relevant for action-effect approaches is that such language risks anthropomorphism as action are described as goal-oriented. However, the present proposal does not require attributing human-like cognition, symbolic reasoning, or propositional intentions to orcas. Action–effect approaches provide one mechanistic way of characterizing goal-directed organization without assuming propositional reasoning or human-like intentions. For instance, in BRAC and related action–effect frameworks, intentional organization can arise from learned couplings between movements and perceivable consequences, without requiring explicit reasoning about means and ends (Hommel et al. 2001; Frings et al. 2020). On this view, to describe an action as organized around anticipated effects is not to say that the actor has a verbally articulable goal. It is to say that the behavior is structured in relation to consequences that matter for control.

The broader implication is methodological as well as theoretical. Animal cognition has often advanced by exporting explanatory tools across domains: from laboratory paradigms to field behavior, from primate work to broader comparative questions, and from human cognitive theory to non-human cases when the concepts travel well. The Iberian orca case suggests that action theory should be part of that export. If contemporary frameworks such as TEC, BRAC, and related action–effect approaches are genuinely cross-species in ambition, they should help us think about behavior outside the laboratory, not only about button presses, reach trajectories, and highly controlled response tasks in humans. Conversely, if they cannot illuminate structured, repeated, target-selective interactions with artifacts in the wild, their claim to generality is weaker than often assumed. Iberian orca–boat interactions have become a vivid puzzle because they sit at the boundary between animal behavior, artifact engagement, and human interpretation. That boundary is exactly where animal cognition should have something useful to say and contemporary cognitive frameworks might add a valuable layer of understanding. At the same time, the theorizing that is stimulated by the orca case is not by any means restricted to orca behavior itself – the Iberian orca case is just an example of complex animal behavior in the wild that might be interpreted by modern cognitive frameworks.

Beyond their general theoretical implications, action-effect interpretations also generate concrete, testable predictions. If rudder-directed interactions are governed mainly by the rudder as a salient stimulus, then contact should remain relatively stable even when the sensory consequences of that contact change. By contrast, an action–effect account predicts that the persistence, form, and termination of the interaction should depend on the feedback produced by the orca’s own actions, including changes in resistance, vibration, water flow, and vessel movement. Orcas should therefore adjust their behavior when these consequences are altered, abandon targets that no longer produce the expected effects, or transfer the interaction to novel structures that generate similar consequences. These predictions also suggest cautious practical implications. If the behavior is maintained partly by the effects produced through rudder contact, attempts to reduce interactions may be more successful when they alter or decouple those effects rather than merely changing the visual appearance of the rudder. Such interventions would, however, need to be evaluated carefully, because modifying sensory feedback could increase exploration, persistence, or risk rather than reduce it.

Taken together, the Iberian orca case should be treated not only as a mystery of cetacean behavior, but also as a stress test for how we explain behavior across species. The same observed event can be described as stimulus-driven, socially transmitted, and action-effect-organized. Those descriptions are not equivalent. They answer different questions and complement each other. Recognizing that difference may help move the discussion of these interactions beyond competing labels and toward a more explicit theory of what it means for animals to act on the world.
