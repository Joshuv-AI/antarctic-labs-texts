Abstract




Generalized relativistic second order magnetohydrodynamics:

A correlation function approach using Zubarev’s nonequilibrium statistical operator

Abstract

We use total energy-momentum conservation and the Bianchi identity (magnetic-flux conservation) to construct second-order relativistic magnetohydrodynamics in a Zubarev’s non-equilibrium statistical operator (NESO) framework. We obtain all dissipative tensors in the medium by focusing on a relativistic magnetized plasma that preserves parity and is symmetric to charge-conjugation. We also provide Kubo formulas for all transport coefficients that arise at second order. Moreover, we extend the NESO formalism to systematically take into account for nonlocal contributions.

The standard derivation of RMHD starts with two fundamental equations: (i) the conservation of fluid energy-momentum influenced by electromagnetic fields and (ii) Maxwell’s equations with sources
[

,

This method involves breaking down the total energy-momentum tensor into distinct fluid and electromagnetic components, a nuanced process due to the need for consistent specification of interaction terms. Additionally, these equations do not appear solely in a conservative form and inherently include non-hydrodynamic (gapped) modes that diminish over time. This challenge stems from considering the electric field as a hydrodynamic variable, despite electric fields not being hydrodynamic degrees of freedom due to Debye screening. The typical solution is to assume infinite electrical conductivity, thus excluding the electric field from the hydrodynamic sector. While this approach is adequate for many ideal and resistive MHD applications, it creates a conceptual conflict: a framework based on the assumption of infinite conductivity cannot consistently incorporate dissipative corrections.

Recent research has developed a formulation that relies exclusively on conserved densities and their fluxes by considering the magnetic field as a hydrodynamic variable. Unlike electric fields, magnetic fields are not subject to static screening. The Bianchi identity, which reflects the absence of magnetic monopoles, prevents the existence of a magnetic equivalent to Debye screening. This has led to a new approach to RMHD that is grounded in the conservation of total energy and momentum, along with the Bianchi identity (conservation of magnetic flux) as the core dynamic equations
[

,

,
36
,

[
36
,
37
]
. However, it is well recognized that first-order relativistic dissipative theories are generally acausal; these challenges become even more complex when electromagnetic fields are involved. Therefore, it is crucial to develop a second-order RMHD theory based on this new formulation.

Starting from this new foundational basis, where total energy-momentum conservation together with the Bianchi identity (magnetic-flux conservation) serve as the fundamental dynamical equations, we extend RMHD to second order in the gradient expansion using Zubarev’s nonequilibrium statistical operator (NESO) formalism. The NESO provides a microscopic basis for relativistic hydrodynamics by constructing a density operator that satisfies local constraints, such as the energy-momentum tensor and conserved charges, and by solving the Liouville equation with a retarded prescription
[
38
,
39
]
. Through this framework, one obtains hydrodynamic constitutive relations that naturally incorporate quantum and statistical effects. The NESO formalism generalizes the Gibbs ensemble in a manner that preserves the local validity of the Gibbs relation. Additionally, the causal “switch-on” procedure for the irreversibility parameter selects the retarded response, fixes the arrow of time by incorporating irreversibility, and ensures consistency with the second law of thermodynamics. Moreover, linearization of the NESO leads to transport coefficients expressed in terms of equilibrium correlation functions. Systematic higher-order corrections follow from expanding the NESO beyond first order, generating relaxation times and nonlinear couplings, Israel-Stewart-type terms, encoded in memory kernels and multi-point correlators
[
40
]
.

are parity odd. These parity assignments will play an important role in determining the allowed correlation functions in the subsequent sections.

With these parity assignments established, the next step in refining Eq. (

h_{1}^{\mu}
and
g_{1}^{\mu}
. Using the relations (

\displaystyle 2h_{1}^{\mu}\,\mathbb{U}^{\alpha\beta}_{\hskip 4.55244pt\hskip 4.55244pt\mu}\partial_{\alpha}\beta_{\beta}

\displaystyle=\frac{-2B}{\varepsilon+p_{\perp}}\,h_{1}^{\mu}\,\text{\sout{$\mathbb{B}$}}^{\alpha\beta}_{\hskip 4.55244pt\hskip 4.55244pt\mu}\partial_{\alpha}\mathcal{H}_{\beta},

(34)

\displaystyle 2g_{1}^{\mu}\,\text{\sout{$\mathbb{U}$}}^{\alpha\beta}_{\hskip 4.55244pt\hskip 4.55244pt\mu}\partial_{\alpha}\mathcal{H}_{\beta}

\displaystyle=-2Hg_{1}^{\mu}\,\mathbb{B}^{\alpha\beta}_{\hskip 4.55244pt\hskip 4.55244pt\mu}\partial_{\alpha}\beta_{\beta}.

(35)

Using (
34

35

\displaystyle\partial_{\mu}S^{\mu}

\displaystyle=-\,\Pi_{1\parallel}\,\bar{\theta}_{\parallel}-\,\Pi_{1\perp}\,\bar{\theta}_{\perp}+\pi_{1\perp}^{\mu\nu}\,\bar{\sigma}_{\perp\mu\nu}+m_{1}^{\mu\nu}\bar{\chi}_{\mu\nu}+\,\mathscr{K}_{1}^{\mu}\,\bar{\mathscr{Y}}_{\mu}+\mathscr{J}_{1}^{\mu}\,\bar{\mathscr{X}}_{\mu}.

(36)

Here,
\mathscr{K}_{1}^{\mu}
and
\mathscr{J}_{1}^{\mu}
are the first order correction to the currents
\mathscr{K}^{\mu}
and
\mathscr{J}^{\mu}
, respectively and they are given by

\displaystyle\mathscr{K}^{\mu}

\displaystyle=f^{\mu}+Hg^{\mu},

(37)

\displaystyle\mathscr{J}^{\mu}

\displaystyle=l^{\mu}-\frac{B}{\varepsilon+p_{\perp}}h^{\mu},

(38)

respectively. Also, we use
\bar{\theta}_{\parallel}=\beta\theta_{\parallel}
,
\bar{\theta}_{\perp}=\beta\theta_{\perp}
, and define the thermodynamic forces:
\bar{\sigma}_{\perp\mu\nu}\equiv\mathcal{G}^{\alpha\beta}_{\hskip 4.55244pt\hskip 4.55244pt\mu\nu}\,\partial_{\alpha}\beta_{\beta}
,
\bar{\chi}_{\mu\nu}\equiv\text{\sout{$\mathcal{G}$}}^{\alpha\beta}_{\hskip 4.55244pt\hskip 4.55244pt\mu\nu}\,\partial_{\alpha}\mathcal{H}_{\beta}
,
\bar{\mathscr{X}}_{\mu}\equiv 2\,\text{\sout{$\mathbb{B}$}}^{\alpha\beta}_{\hskip 4.55244pt\hskip 4.55244pt\mu}\partial_{\alpha}\mathcal{H}_{\beta}
, and
\bar{\mathscr{Y}}_{\mu}\equiv 2\,\mathbb{B}^{\alpha\beta}_{\hskip 4.55244pt\hskip 4.55244pt\mu}\partial_{\alpha}\beta_{\beta}
.

To ensure the semi-positivity of Eq. (
36
), we impose linear constitutive relations that express the dissipative tensors in terms of the thermodynamic forces.
Guided by Curie’s symmetry principle that an effect cannot possess more symmetries than its cause. Accordingly, the dissipative tensors can be expressed only as linear combinations of thermodynamic forces possessing the same tensorial rank and parity. Thus, we obtain

\displaystyle\Pi_{1\parallel}

\displaystyle=-\bar{\zeta}_{\parallel}\bar{\theta}_{\parallel}-\bar{\zeta}_{\times}\bar{\theta}_{\perp},

(39)

\displaystyle\Pi_{1\perp}

\displaystyle=-\bar{\zeta}_{\perp}\bar{\theta}_{\perp}-\bar{\zeta}_{\times}^{\prime}\bar{\theta}_{\parallel},

(40)

\displaystyle\pi_{1\perp}^{\mu\nu}

\displaystyle=2\bar{\eta}_{\perp}\bar{\sigma}_{\perp}^{\mu\nu},

(41)

\displaystyle m_{1}^{\mu\nu}

\displaystyle=2\bar{\rho}_{\perp}\bar{\chi}^{\mu\nu},

(42)

\displaystyle\mathscr{K}_{1}^{\mu}

\displaystyle=\bar{\eta}_{\parallel}\bar{\mathscr{Y}}^{\mu},

(43)

\displaystyle\mathscr{J}_{1}^{\mu}

\displaystyle=\bar{\rho}_{\parallel}\bar{\mathscr{X}}^{\mu}.

(44)

As we can see there is no cross term between
\mathscr{K}_{1}^{\mu}
and
\mathscr{J}_{1}^{\mu}
because they are of different parity.
This construction yields the associated transport coefficients
\bar{\zeta}_{\parallel}
,
\bar{\zeta}_{\perp}
,
\bar{\eta}_{\parallel}
,
\bar{\eta}_{\perp}
,
\bar{\rho}_{\parallel}
, and
\bar{\rho}_{\perp}
, which are constrained to be nonnegative. However, for a charge conjugate symmetric plasma
\bar{\zeta}_{\times}=\bar{\zeta}^{\prime}_{\times}
and follow the inequality
\bar{\zeta}_{\parallel}\bar{\zeta}_{\perp}-\bar{\zeta}^{2}_{\times}\geq 0

[
37
]
.

4
Nonequilibrium statistical operator

We consider the system in hydrodynamic regime, wherein thermodynamic parameters can be defined locally (local thermodynamic equilibrium).
For a system in which the magnetic flux is treated as a conserved quantity, the nonequilibrium statistical operator
\hat{\rho}(t)
is defined as
[
36
]

\hat{\rho}(t)=Q^{-1}\exp\Big[-\int d^{3}x\hat{Z}(\vec{x},t)\Big],~\text{with}~~Q={\rm Tr}\exp\Big[-\int d^{3}x\hat{Z}(\vec{x},t)\Big],

(45)

where

\hat{Z}(\vec{x},t)=\epsilon\int_{-\infty}^{t}dt_{1}e^{\epsilon(t_{1}-t)}\Big[\beta_{\nu}(\vec{x},t_{1})\,\hat{T}^{0\nu}(\vec{x},t_{1})+\mathcal{H}_{\nu}(\vec{x},t_{1})\,\hat{\tilde{F}}^{0\nu}(\vec{x},t_{1})\Big].

(46)

Here,
\epsilon
denotes a small infinitesimal parameter that enforces irreversibility throughout the calculation, and it is set to zero at the end.
The tensors
\hat{T}^{\mu\nu}
and
\hat{\tilde{F}}^{\mu\nu}
are operator-valued function. Zubarev’s
prescription for taking the statistical average of any operator
\hat{O}(\vec{x},t)
is given by
[
38
]

\left<\hat{O}(\vec{x},t)\right>=\lim_{\epsilon\to 0^{+}}\lim_{V\to\infty}{\rm Tr}\left[\hat{\rho}(t)\hat{O}(\vec{x},t)\right].

(47)

The statistical averages of the operators
\hat{T}^{\mu\nu}
and
\hat{\tilde{F}}^{\mu\nu}
reproduce the corresponding hydrodynamic
values in Eqs. (
11
) and (
12
), i.e.
\big<\hat{T}^{\mu\nu}\big>=T^{\mu\nu}
and
\big<\hat{\tilde{F}}^{\mu\nu}\big>=\tilde{F}^{\mu\nu}
.
A principal advantage of the NESO formalism is that it gives a clean separation into equilibrium and nonequilibrium parts.
This follows by integrating
\hat{Z}(\vec{x},t)
by-parts, which yields the density operator

\hat{\rho}(t)=Q^{-1}\exp{(-\hat{\mathcal{A}}+\hat{\mathcal{B}})},

(48)

where

\displaystyle\hat{\mathcal{A}}(t)

\displaystyle=\int d^{3}x\left[\beta_{\nu}(\vec{x},t)\,\hat{T}^{0\nu}(\vec{x},t)+\mathcal{H}_{\nu}(\vec{x},t)\,\hat{\tilde{F}}^{0\nu}(\vec{x},t)\right],

(49)

\displaystyle\hat{\mathcal{B}}(t)

\displaystyle=\int d^{3}x\int_{-\infty}^{t}dt_{1}\;e^{\epsilon(t_{1}-t)}\,\hat{\mathcal{C}}(\vec{x},t_{1}),

(50)

\displaystyle\hat{\mathcal{C}}(\vec{x},t)

\displaystyle=\hat{T}^{\mu\nu}(\vec{x},t)\,\partial_{\mu}\beta_{\nu}(\vec{x},t)+\hat{\tilde{F}}^{\mu\nu}(\vec{x},t)\,\partial_{\mu}\mathcal{H}_{\nu}(\vec{x},t).

(51)

Here,
\hat{\mathcal{A}}(t)
denotes the equilibrium contribution and
\hat{\mathcal{B}}(t)
the nonequilibrium part. We used the conservation laws to write the nonequilibrium part
\hat{\mathcal{B}}(t)
in a covariant form.
Treating
\hat{\mathcal{B}}
as a perturbation, the
\hat{\rho}(t)
can be expanded around local equilibrium, thereby
systematically incorporating deviations around local equilibrium.
Consequently, the statistical average of any operator, expanded up-to second order about the local equilibrium, can be written as
[
41
,
40
]
:

\left<\hat{O}(x)\right>=\left<\hat{O}(x)\right>_{l}+\int d^{4}x_{1}\Big(\hat{O}(x),\hat{\mathcal{C}}(x_{1})\Big)+\int d^{4}x_{1}d^{4}x_{2}\Big(\hat{O}(x),\hat{\mathcal{C}}(x_{1}),\hat{\mathcal{C}}(x_{2})\Big)+\cdots,

(52)

here,
\int d^{4}x_{1}=\int d^{3}x_{1}\int_{-\infty}^{t}dt_{1}e^{\epsilon(t_{1}-t)}
and the statistical averaging in local equilibrium is denoted
by the subscript
l
. The two-point and three-point
correlation functions are defined by

\displaystyle\Big(\hat{O}(x),\hat{X}(x_{1})\Big)

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{1}d\tau\Big<O(x)\left[X_{\tau}(x_{1})-\left<X_{\tau}(x_{1})\right>_{l}\right]\Big>_{l},

(53)

\displaystyle\Big(\hat{O}(x),\hat{X}(x_{1}),\hat{Y}(x_{2})\Big)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\int_{0}^{1}d\tau\int_{0}^{1}d\lambda\Big<\tilde{T}\Big\{\hat{O}(x)\Big[\hat{X}_{\lambda}(x_{1})\hat{Y}_{\tau}(x_{2})-\langle\hat{X}_{\lambda}(x_{1})\rangle_{l}\hat{Y}_{\tau}(x_{2})

\displaystyle~~~-\hat{X}_{\lambda}(x_{1})\langle\hat{Y}_{\tau}(x_{2})\rangle_{l}-\langle\tilde{T}\hat{X}_{\lambda}(x_{1})\hat{Y}_{\tau}(x_{2})\rangle_{l}+2\langle\hat{X}_{\lambda}(x_{1})\rangle_{l}\langle\hat{Y}_{\tau}(x_{2})\rangle_{l}\Big]\Big\}\Big>_{l},

(54)

respectively. Here,
X_{\tau}=e^{-\tau A}Xe^{\tau A}
and

\tilde{T}\{X_{\lambda}Y_{\tau}\}
is the anti-chronological
time-ordering operator with respect to variable
\tau
and

\lambda
. Also, observing (
54
), we infer that the three-point correlation function follows the symmetry relation:

\displaystyle\int d^{4}x_{1}d^{4}x_{2}\Big(\hat{O}(x),\hat{X}(x_{1}),\hat{Y}(x_{2})\Big)=\int d^{4}x_{1}d^{4}x_{2}\Big(\hat{O}(x),\hat{Y}(x_{1}),\hat{X}(x_{2})\Big).

(55)

Furthermore, to develop a second-order theory and to obtain the evolution equations for the dissipative tensors,
\Pi_{\parallel},\,\Pi_{\perp},\,\mathscr{K}^{\mu},\,\mathscr{J}^{\mu},\,\pi_{\perp}^{\mu\nu},
and
m^{\mu\nu}
up to second order in the gradient expansion within the NESO framework, it is necessary to express the statistical average in Eq. (
52
) explicitly in terms of hydrodynamic gradients. To this end, we must systematically extract all first- and second-order contributions arising from the two-point and three-point correlation functions. For this purpose, consider an integral of the form

\displaystyle\int d^{4}x_{1}\Big(\hat{O}(x),\hat{\mathcal{C}}(x_{1})\Big).

(56)

We may factorize each term in
\hat{\mathcal{C}}
into two components, writing
\hat{\mathcal{C}}=\hat{\mathcal{C}}_{oper}\mathcal{C}_{hydro}

where
\hat{\mathcal{C}}_{oper}
denotes the operator-valued part and
\mathcal{C}_{hydro}
represents the hydrodynamic fields. Performing a Taylor expansion of the hydrodynamic fields appearing in
\hat{\mathcal{C}}(x_{1})
about the point
x_{1}=x
, while keeping all operator-valued quantities in
\hat{\mathcal{C}}(x_{1})
evaluated at
x_{1}
, we may write

\displaystyle\hat{\mathcal{C}}(x_{1})

\displaystyle=\hat{\mathcal{C}}_{oper}(x_{1})\mathcal{C}_{hydro}(x_{1})

\displaystyle=\hat{\mathcal{C}}_{oper}(x_{1})\left[\hat{\mathcal{C}}(x_{1})\right]_{x}+\hat{\mathcal{C}}_{oper}(x_{1})(x_{1}-x)^{\lambda}\left[\frac{\partial}{\partial x_{1}^{\lambda}}\mathcal{C}_{hydro}(x_{1})\right]_{x}+\cdots.

(57)

Here, the notation
[...]_{x}
indicates that values inside are evaluated at
x
.
This expansion allows us to factor out all
x
-dependent hydrodynamic quantities from the integral in (
56
). Apart from that, the dominant contribution to the integral (
56
) comes from
|\vec{x}_{1}-\vec{x}|
which encodes the correlation length, of order the microscopic mean free path
\lambda
. Consequently,
(x_{1}-x)^{\lambda}\,\partial/\partial x_{1}^{\lambda}\sim\lambda/L\equiv\mathrm{Kn}
, with
L
being the macroscopic scale (
\mathrm{Kn}
is the Knudsen number). Therefore, the second term in (
57
) is one order high in gradient ordering compared to the first term. The first term in (
57
), in which the hydrodymamic fields are evaluated at
x_{1}=x
, is called “local” contribution. All the higher-order terms are correspondingly “nonlocal” contributions.

Thus, we first decompose
\hat{\mathcal{C}}
as
\hat{\mathcal{C}}=\hat{\mathcal{C}}_{F}+\hat{\mathcal{C}}_{S}
and then perform a Taylor expansion of the hydrodynamic fields to extract the local and nonlocal contributions, as prescribed in Eq. (
57
). Here,
\hat{\mathcal{C}}_{F}
denotes the part of
\hat{\mathcal{C}}
that contains hydrodynamic fields which are first order in hydrodynamic gradients, while
\hat{\mathcal{C}}_{S}
denotes the part of
\hat{\mathcal{C}}
that contains hydrodynamic fields which are second order in hydrodynamic gradients. In this way, the statistical average of any operator is systematically organized order-by-order in derivatives of the hydrodynamic fields.

\left<\hat{O}(x)\right>=\left<\hat{O}(x)\right>_{l}+\left<\hat{O}(x)\right>_{1}+\left<\hat{O}(x)\right>_{2},

(58)

where
\left<\hat{O}(x)\right>_{1}
and
\left<\hat{O}(x)\right>_{2}
are the first-order and second-order
gradient terms, respectively. The first-order contribution to the statistical average emerges by extracting the local contribution from two-point correlation function. The
resulting expression is given by

\left<\hat{O}(x)\right>_{1}=\int d^{4}x_{1}\Big(\hat{O}(x),\hat{\mathcal{C}}_{F}(x_{1})\Big)\Big|_{local}.

(59)

Whereas, the second-order contribution to the statistical average
can originate from three possible ways, given by

\left<\hat{O}(x)\right>_{2}=\left<\hat{O}(x)\right>_{2}^{(2),NL}+\left<\hat{O}(x)\right>_{2}^{(2),ET}+\left<\hat{O}(x)\right>_{2}^{(3)},

(60)

where,

\displaystyle\left<\hat{O}(x)\right>_{2}^{(2),NL}

\displaystyle=\int d^{4}x_{1}\Big(\hat{O}(x),[\partial_{\lambda}\hat{\mathcal{C}}(x_{1})]_{x_{1}=x}\Big)(x_{1}-x)^{\lambda},

(61)

\displaystyle\left<\hat{O}(x)\right>_{2}^{(2),ET}

\displaystyle=\int d^{4}x_{1}\Big(\hat{O}(x),\hat{\mathcal{C}}_{S}(x_{1})\Big)\Big|_{local},

(62)

\displaystyle\left<\hat{O}(x)\right>_{2}^{(3)}

\displaystyle=\int d^{4}x_{1}d^{4}x_{2}\Big(\hat{O}(x),\hat{\mathcal{C}}_{F}(x_{1}),\hat{\mathcal{C}}_{F}(x_{2})\Big)\Big|_{local}.

(63)

Here, the term
\big<\hat{O}(x)\big>_{2}^{(2),NL}
contains the nonlocal corrections arising from two-point correlation functions and
[\partial_{\lambda}\hat{\mathcal{C}}(x_{1})]_{x_{1}=x}
signifies that we differentiate all hydrodynamic variables (not the operators) appearing in
\hat{\mathcal{C}}
with respect to
x_{1}
, and then set
x_{1}=x
, while the operators remain evaluated at
x_{1}
.
\big<\hat{O}(x)\big>_{2}^{(2),ET}
contains the local corrections from two-point correlators driven by extended thermodynamic forces (
\hat{\mathcal{C}}_{S}
),
and
\big<\hat{O}(x)\big>_{2}^{(3)}
comprises the local contributions from three-point correlation functions.

In order to evaluate the second-order correction, we observe from Eqs. (
61
)-(
63
) that both
\hat{\mathcal{C}}
and
\partial_{\lambda}\hat{\mathcal{C}}
are required. We therefore compute these quantities one by one. To obtain
\hat{\mathcal{C}}
in terms of gradients of hydrodynamic quantities, our strategy is to begin with a tensor decomposition of the operators
\hat{T}^{\mu\nu}
and
\hat{\tilde{F}}^{\mu\nu}
:

\displaystyle\hat{T}^{\mu\nu}

\displaystyle=\hat{\varepsilon}\,u^{\mu}u^{\nu}+\hat{p}_{\parallel}\,b^{\mu}b^{\nu}-\hat{p}_{\perp}\,\mathcal{G}^{\mu\nu}+\hat{h}^{\mu}u^{\nu}+\hat{h}^{\nu}u^{\mu}+\hat{f}^{\mu}b^{\nu}+\hat{f}^{\nu}b^{\mu}+\hat{\pi}^{\mu\nu}_{\perp},

(64)

\displaystyle\hat{\tilde{F}}^{\mu\nu}

\displaystyle=\hat{B}\,(b^{\mu}u^{\nu}-b^{\nu}u^{\mu})\;-\;(\hat{l}^{\mu}b^{\nu}-\hat{l}^{\nu}b^{\mu})\;+\;\hat{g}^{\mu}u^{\nu}-\hat{g}^{\nu}u^{\mu}\;+\;\hat{m}^{\mu\nu}.

(65)

All operators, except the pressures
\hat{p}_{\parallel}
and
\hat{p}_{\perp}
, can be directly matched to their hydrodynamic counterparts by taking statistical averages with the full nonequilibrium statistical operator:

\displaystyle\varepsilon\equiv\big\langle\hat{\varepsilon}\big\rangle,\quad B\equiv\big\langle\hat{B}\big\rangle,\quad h^{\mu}\equiv\big\langle\hat{h}^{\mu}\big\rangle,\quad f^{\mu}\equiv\big\langle\hat{f}^{\mu}\big\rangle,\quad l^{\mu}\equiv\big\langle\hat{l}^{\mu}\big\rangle,\quad g^{\mu}\equiv\big\langle\hat{g}^{\mu}\big\rangle,\quad\pi^{\mu\nu}\equiv\big\langle\hat{\pi}^{\mu\nu}\big\rangle,\quad m^{\mu\nu}\equiv\big\langle\hat{m}^{\mu\nu}\big\rangle.

(66)

In contrast, the statistical average of the total longitudinal and transverse pressure operators yield the equilibrium pressure plus the corresponding bulk viscous corrections:

\displaystyle\big\langle\hat{p}_{\parallel}\big\rangle\equiv p_{\parallel}+\Pi_{\parallel},\qquad\big\langle\hat{p}_{\perp}\big\rangle\equiv p_{\perp}+\Pi_{\perp}.

(67)

All above operators present in (
64
) and (
65
) can be expressed as suitable projections of
\hat{T}^{\mu\nu}
and
\hat{\tilde{F}}^{\mu\nu}
.

\displaystyle\hat{\varepsilon}

\displaystyle=u_{\alpha}u_{\beta}\,\hat{T}^{\alpha\beta},~~\hat{p}_{\perp}=-\frac{1}{2}\,\mathcal{G}_{\alpha\beta}\,\hat{T}^{\alpha\beta},~~\hat{p}_{\parallel}=b_{\alpha}b_{\beta}\,\hat{T}^{\alpha\beta},

(68)

\displaystyle\hat{h}^{\mu}

\displaystyle=\mathbb{U}_{\alpha\beta}^{\hskip 4.55244pt\hskip 4.55244pt\mu}\,\hat{T}^{\alpha\beta},~~\hat{f}^{\mu}=-\,\mathbb{B}_{\alpha\beta}^{\hskip 4.55244pt\hskip 4.55244pt\mu}\,\hat{T}^{\alpha\beta},~~\hat{\pi}_{\perp}^{\mu\nu}=\mathcal{G}^{\mu\nu}_{\hskip 4.55244pt\hskip 4.55244pt\alpha\beta}\,\hat{T}^{\alpha\beta},

(69)

\displaystyle\hat{B}

\displaystyle=u_{\alpha}b_{\beta}\,\hat{\tilde{F}}^{\alpha\beta},~~\hat{g}^{\mu}=-\,\text{\sout{$\mathbb{U}$}}_{\alpha\beta}^{\hskip 4.55244pt\hskip 4.55244pt\mu}\,\hat{\tilde{F}}^{\alpha\beta},~~\hat{l}^{\mu}=-\,\text{\sout{$\mathbb{B}$}}_{\alpha\beta}^{\hskip 4.55244pt\hskip 4.55244pt\mu}\,\hat{\tilde{F}}^{\alpha\beta},~~\hat{m}^{\mu\nu}=\text{\sout{$\mathcal{G}$}}^{\mu\nu}_{\hskip 4.55244pt\hskip 4.55244pt\alpha\beta}\,\hat{\tilde{F}}^{\alpha\beta}.

(70)

where the projectors are defined in (
1
)-(

). In order to write
\hat{\mathcal{C}}
in first- and second-order hydrodynamic gradients, we substitute the decomposition of
\hat{T}^{\mu\nu}
and
\hat{\tilde{F}}^{\mu\nu}

\displaystyle\hat{\mathcal{C}}

\displaystyle=\hat{\varepsilon}\,D\beta-\hat{B}\,D\mathcal{H}-\beta\,\hat{p}_{\parallel}\,\theta_{\parallel}-\beta\,\hat{p}_{\perp}\,\theta_{\perp}-\hat{B}\,\mathcal{H}\,\theta_{\parallel}

\displaystyle\quad+2\hat{h}^{\mu}\,\mathbb{U}^{\alpha\beta}_{\hskip 4.55244pt\hskip 4.55244pt\mu}\partial_{\alpha}\beta_{\beta}+2\hat{f}^{\mu}\,\mathbb{B}^{\alpha\beta}_{\hskip 4.55244pt\hskip 4.55244pt\mu}\partial_{\alpha}\beta_{\beta}-2\hat{g}^{\mu}\,\text{\sout{$\mathbb{U}$}}^{\alpha\beta}_{\hskip 4.55244pt\hskip 4.55244pt\mu}\partial_{\alpha}\mathcal{H}_{\beta}+2\hat{l}^{\mu}\,\text{\sout{$\mathbb{B}$}}^{\alpha\beta}_{\hskip 4.55244pt\hskip 4.55244pt\mu}\partial_{\alpha}\mathcal{H}_{\beta}

Here, the first five terms in

5
First order magnetohydrodynamics

First-order relativistic hydrodynamics derived using Zubarev’s nonequilibrium statistical operator (NESO) method was previously developed in Ref.
[
20
]
, where Kubo formulas for the transport coefficients of first-order magnetohydrodynamics were obtained. However, that derivation followed the conventional formulation in which the energy-momentum tensors of the fluid and the electromagnetic field are treated separately, rather than starting from a fully conserved set of equations. More recently, first-order relativistic magnetohydrodynamics has been formulated entirely in terms of conserved quantities by employing the conservation of the total energy-momentum tensor together with magnetic-flux conservation (the Bianchi identity)
[
36
,
37
]
. In this section, we provide a brief review of first-order magnetohydrodynamics within this modern framework.

We begin by evaluating the bulk viscous pressure parallel to the magnetic field,
\Pi_{\parallel}
.
According to Eq. (
362
), the first-order correction to
\Pi_{\parallel}
is given by

\displaystyle\Pi_{1\parallel}(x)

\displaystyle=\big<\hat{P}_{\parallel}(x)\big>_{1}=\int d^{4}x_{1}\,\big(\hat{P}_{\parallel}(x),\,\hat{\mathcal{C}}_{F}(x_{1})\big)\big|_{local}.

Here, we employ Eq. (
58
) and (
59
) to express
\big<\hat{P}_{\parallel}\big>_{1}
in terms of two-point correlation function.
By Curie’s symmetry principle, the scalar operator
\hat{P}_{\parallel}
couples only to the operators of the same rank and parity; consequently, within
\hat{\mathcal{C}}_{F}
(Eq. (
77
)) it can couple only to
\hat{P}_{\parallel}
and
\hat{P}_{\perp}
. Hence,

\displaystyle\Pi_{1\parallel}(x)

\displaystyle=\int d^{4}x_{1}\,\big(\hat{P}_{\parallel}(x),\,\hat{P}_{\parallel}(x_{1})\big)\,[-\bar{\theta}_{\parallel}(x_{1})]\big|_{local}+\int d^{4}x_{1}\,\big(\hat{P}_{\parallel}(x),\,\hat{P}_{\perp}(x_{1})\big)\,[-\bar{\theta}_{\perp}(x_{1})]\big|_{local}.

(86)

We next perform a Taylor expansion of
\bar{\theta}_{\parallel}(x_{1})
about
x_{1}=x
:

\bar{\theta}_{\parallel}(x_{1})=\bar{\theta}_{\parallel}(x)+(x_{1}-x)^{\lambda}\frac{\partial}{\partial x_{1}^{\lambda}}\left[\bar{\theta}_{\parallel}(x_{1})\right]\bigg|_{x}+\cdots.

(87)

As discussed in Sec
4
,
(x_{1}-x)^{\lambda}\,\partial/\partial x_{1}^{\lambda}\sim\lambda/L\equiv\mathrm{Kn}
(
\mathrm{Kn}
is the Knudsen number). Since
\theta_{\parallel}
is already first order in gradients, this term scales as
\mathcal{O}(\mathrm{Kn}^{2})
(second order in the hydrodynamic gradients) and is neglected in first-order hydrodynamics. Hence, extracting the local terms in (
86
), we obtain

\displaystyle\Pi_{1\parallel}(x)

\displaystyle=-\bar{\theta}_{\parallel}(x)\,\bar{\zeta}_{\parallel}(x)-\bar{\theta}_{\perp}(x)\,\bar{\zeta}_{\times}(x),

(88)

where, we defined

\displaystyle\bar{\zeta}_{\parallel}(x)

\displaystyle=\int d^{4}x_{1}\,\big(\hat{P}_{\parallel}(x),\,\hat{P}_{\parallel}(x_{1})\big),

(89)

\displaystyle\bar{\zeta}_{\times}(x)

\displaystyle=\int d^{4}x_{1}\,\big(\hat{P}_{\parallel}(x),\,\hat{P}_{\perp}(x_{1})\big),

(90)

by comparing (
88
) to (
39
). Similarly, we can obtain

\displaystyle\Pi_{1\perp}(x)

\displaystyle=-\bar{\theta}_{\perp}(x)\,\bar{\zeta}_{\perp}(x)-\bar{\theta}_{\parallel}(x)\,\bar{\zeta}_{\times}^{\prime}(x),

(91)

where the transport coefficients are defined by

\displaystyle\bar{\zeta}_{\perp}(x)

\displaystyle=\int d^{4}x_{1}\,\big(\hat{P}_{\perp}(x),\,\hat{P}_{\perp}(x_{1})\big),

(92)

\displaystyle\bar{\zeta}_{\times}^{\prime}(x)

\displaystyle=\int d^{4}x_{1}\,\big(\hat{P}_{\perp}(x),\,\hat{P}_{\parallel}(x_{1})\big).

(93)

For a charge-conjugate-symmetric plasma the cross bulk transport coefficients
\bar{\zeta}_{\times}=\bar{\zeta}_{\times}^{\prime}

[
36
]
.

Furthermore, in order to obtain the first-order corrections to the statistical averages of the remaining dissipative tensors, we use Eqs. (
58
) and (
59
) to express these corrections in terms of two-point correlation functions.
Invoking Eq. (
77
) together with Curie’s principle, only correlators of same rank and parity survive, so the first-order corrections are given by

\displaystyle\mathscr{K}_{1}^{\mu}(x)=\left<\hat{\mathscr{K}}^{\mu}(x)\right>_{1}

\displaystyle=\int d^{4}x_{1}\,\left(\hat{\mathscr{K}}^{\mu}(x),\,\hat{\mathscr{K}}^{\alpha}(x_{1})\right)\bar{\mathscr{Y}}_{\alpha}(x_{1}),

(94)

\displaystyle\mathscr{J}_{1}^{\mu}(x)=\left<\hat{\mathscr{J}}^{\mu}(x)\right>_{1}

\displaystyle=\int d^{4}x_{1}\,\left(\hat{\mathscr{J}}^{\mu}(x),\,\hat{\mathscr{J}}^{\alpha}(x_{1})\right)\bar{\mathscr{X}}_{\alpha}(x_{1}),

(95)

\displaystyle\pi_{1\perp}^{\mu\nu}(x)=\left<\hat{\pi}_{\perp}^{\mu\nu}(x)\right>_{1}

\displaystyle=\int d^{4}x_{1}\,\left(\hat{\pi}_{\perp}^{\mu\nu}(x),\,\hat{\pi}_{\perp}^{\alpha\beta}(x_{1})\right)\bar{\sigma}_{\perp\alpha\beta}(x_{1}),

(96)

\displaystyle m_{1}^{\mu\nu}(x)=\left<\hat{m}^{\mu\nu}(x)\right>_{1}

\displaystyle=\int d^{4}x_{1}\,\left(\hat{m}^{\mu\nu}(x),\,m^{\alpha\beta}(x_{1})\right)\bar{\chi}_{\alpha\beta}(x_{1}).

(97)

Here we have used the fact that the statistical average of dissipative tensors vanishes in local equilibrium,

\langle\hat{\mathscr{K}}^{\mu}\rangle_{l}=\langle\hat{\mathscr{J}}^{\mu}\rangle_{l}=0
,
\left<\hat{\pi}_{\perp}^{\mu\nu}(x)\right>_{l}=0
, and
\left<\hat{m}^{\mu\nu}(x)\right>_{l}=0
. Similar to the calculation of bulk viscous pressure, we expand the thermodynamic forces in (
94
)-(
97
) around
x_{1}=x
and keep the leading order terms, also we write the correlation functions in terms of traces and the corresponding projection operators, given by

\displaystyle\left(\hat{\mathscr{K}}^{\mu}(x),\,\hat{\mathscr{K}}^{\alpha}(x_{1})\right)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\,\mathcal{G}^{\mu\alpha}\left(\hat{\mathscr{K}}^{\nu}(x),\,\hat{\mathscr{K}}_{\nu}(x_{1})\right),

(98)

\displaystyle\left(\hat{\mathscr{J}}^{\mu}(x),\,\hat{\mathscr{J}}^{\alpha}(x_{1})\right)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\,\mathcal{G}^{\mu\alpha}\left(\hat{\mathscr{J}}^{\nu}(x),\,\hat{\mathscr{J}}_{\nu}(x_{1})\right),

(99)

\displaystyle\left(\hat{\pi}_{\perp}^{\mu\nu}(x),\,\hat{\pi}_{\perp}^{\alpha\beta}(x_{1})\right)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\,\mathcal{G}^{\mu\nu\alpha\beta}\Big(\hat{\pi}_{\perp}^{\rho\sigma}(x),\,\hat{\pi}_{\perp\rho\sigma}(x_{1})\Big),

(100)

\displaystyle\left(\hat{m}^{\mu\nu}(x),\,m^{\alpha\beta}(x_{1})\right)

\displaystyle=\text{\sout{$\mathcal{G}$}}^{\mu\nu\alpha\beta}\Big(\hat{m}^{\rho\sigma}(x),\,\hat{m}_{\rho\sigma}(x_{1})\Big).

(101)

Thus we obtain,

\displaystyle\mathscr{K}_{1}^{\mu}(x)=\left<\hat{\mathscr{K}}^{\mu}(x)\right>_{1}

\displaystyle=\bar{\eta}_{\parallel}(x)\,\bar{\mathscr{Y}}^{\mu}(x),

(102)

\displaystyle\mathscr{J}_{1}^{\mu}(x)=\left<\hat{\mathscr{J}}^{\mu}(x)\right>_{1}

\displaystyle=\bar{\rho}_{\parallel}(x)\,\bar{\mathscr{X}}^{\mu}(x),

(103)

\displaystyle\pi_{1\perp}^{\mu\nu}(x)=\left<\hat{\pi}_{\perp}^{\mu\nu}(x)\right>_{1}

\displaystyle=2\,\bar{\eta}_{\perp}(x)\,\bar{\sigma}_{\perp}^{\mu\nu}(x),

(104)

\displaystyle m_{1}^{\mu\nu}(x)=\left<\hat{m}^{\mu\nu}(x)\right>_{1}

\displaystyle=2\,\bar{\rho}_{\perp}(x)\,\bar{\chi}^{\mu\nu}(x),

(105)

where the transport coefficients are defined by

\displaystyle\bar{\eta}_{\parallel}(x)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\int d^{4}x_{1}\,\left(\hat{\mathscr{K}}^{\nu}(x),\,\hat{\mathscr{K}}_{\nu}(x_{1})\right),

(106)

\displaystyle\bar{\rho}_{\parallel}(x)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\int d^{4}x_{1}\,\left(\hat{\mathscr{J}}^{\nu}(x),\,\hat{\mathscr{J}}_{\nu}(x_{1})\right),

(107)

\displaystyle\bar{\eta}_{\perp}(x)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{4}\int d^{4}x_{1}\,\Big(\hat{\pi}_{\perp}^{\rho\sigma}(x),\,\hat{\pi}_{\perp\rho\sigma}(x_{1})\Big),

(108)

\displaystyle\bar{\rho}_{\perp}(x)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\int d^{4}x_{1}\,\Big(\hat{m}^{\rho\sigma}(x),\,\hat{m}_{\rho\sigma}(x_{1})\Big).

(109)

Here, we like to emphasize that the relations (
98
)-(
101
) are valid only to leading order in the gradient expansion.
In principle,
u^{\mu}
and
b^{\mu}
vary in spacetime, so the projectors at
x
and
x_{1}
need not coincide.
However, within linear response, when evaluating Kubo correlators (
98
)-(
101
) in first order hydrodynamic theory, we can observe that the thermodynamic forces in (
94
)-(
97
) are already first order in gradients.
Therefore, we may approximate
u^{\mu}(x)\simeq u^{\mu}(x_{1})
and
b^{\mu}(x)\simeq b^{\mu}(x_{1})
in the leading order approximations, and the projectors in (
98
)-(
101
) need not be tied to a specific point.
However, if one goes beyond first order, e.g., to compute second order hydrodynamic corrections, one must keep distinct projectors
\mathcal{G}^{\mu\nu}(x)
and
\mathcal{G}^{\mu\nu}(x_{1})
and Taylor-expand
\mathcal{G}^{\mu\nu}(x_{1})
around
x_{1}=x
.
The resulting higher-order terms generate nonlocal contributions and new couplings between thermodynamic forces and dissipative tensors.
These effects are naturally incorporated in the nonlocal correction, Eq. (
61
), via the evaluation of
\big[\partial_{\lambda}\hat{\mathcal{C}}(x_{1})\big]_{x_{1}=x}
.

6
Second order magnetohydrodynamics

In the conventional way, second-order magnetohydrodynamics has been derived in both the non-resistive
[
23
]
and resistive
[
24
]
limits from the Boltzmann-Vlasov equation using the method of moments. Second-order formulations in the non-resistive and resistive limits have also been obtained within the relaxation-time approximation in Refs.
[
26
,
27
]
. In this section, we employ Zubarev’s nonequilibrium statistical operator formalism to derive second-order relativistic magnetohydrodynamics based entirely on the underlying conserved quantities and symmetries.

6.1
Second-order correction and evolution equation for shear stress tensor (
\hat{\pi}^{\mu\nu}_{\perp}
)

6.1.1
Nonlocal correction from the two-point correlation function:
\big<\hat{\pi}_{\perp}^{\mu\nu}(x)\big>^{\text{(2),NL}}_{2}

Using (
61
), the nonlocal correction is given by

\displaystyle\Big<\hat{\pi}_{\perp}^{\mu\nu}(x)\Big>^{\text{(2),NL}}_{2}

\displaystyle=\int d^{4}x_{1}\Big(\hat{\pi}_{\perp\,\mu\nu}(x),[\partial_{\lambda}\hat{\mathcal{C}}(x_{1})]_{x_{1}=x}\Big)(x_{1}-x)^{\lambda}.

(110)

We now employ Eq. (
85
) to evaluate
\big[\partial_{\lambda}\hat{\mathcal{C}}(x_{1})\big]_{x_{1}=x}
by setting all hydrodynamic variables to
x_{1}=x
while leaving the operators at
x_{1}
. Invoking Curie’s symmetry principle, that correlations can exist only between operators of the same rank and parity, we find that only the term containing
\hat{\pi}_{\perp}^{\mu\nu}
in

\big[\partial_{\lambda}\hat{\mathcal{C}}(x_{1})\big]_{x_{1}=x}
contributes to the integral in Eq. (
110
). This yields

\displaystyle\Big<\hat{\pi}_{\perp}^{\mu\nu}(x)\Big>^{\text{(2),NL}}_{2}

\displaystyle=\int d^{4}x_{1}\Big(\hat{\pi}_{\perp}^{\mu\nu}(x),\hat{\pi}_{\perp}^{\alpha\beta}(x_{1})[\partial_{\lambda}\bar{\sigma}_{\perp\alpha\beta}-B^{\prime}\bar{\mathscr{X}}_{\alpha}\partial_{\lambda}u_{\beta}-\bar{\mathscr{Y}}_{\alpha}\partial_{\lambda}b_{\beta}]_{x}\Big)(x_{1}-x)^{\lambda}

\displaystyle=\big[\partial_{\lambda}\bar{\sigma}_{\perp\alpha\beta}(x)-B^{\prime}(x)\bar{\mathscr{X}}_{\alpha}(x)\partial_{\lambda}u_{\beta}(x)-\bar{\mathscr{Y}}_{\alpha}(x)\partial_{\lambda}b_{\beta}(x)\big]\int d^{4}x_{1}\Big(\hat{\pi}_{\perp}^{\mu\nu}(x),\hat{\pi}_{\perp}^{\alpha\beta}(x_{1})\Big)(x_{1}-x)^{\lambda}.

(111)

Since the integral in Eq. (
111
) already contains a factor that is second order in hydrodynamics gradients, we may safely approximate the Kubo correlators using Eq. (
100
).
Accordingly, Eq. (
111
) simplifies to

\displaystyle\Big<\hat{\pi}_{\perp}^{\mu\nu}(x)\Big>^{\text{(2),NL}}_{2}

\displaystyle=2\,\mathcal{G}^{\mu\nu\alpha\beta}\big[\partial_{\lambda}\bar{\sigma}_{\perp\alpha\beta}(x)-B^{\prime}(x)\bar{\mathscr{X}}_{\alpha}(x)\partial_{\lambda}u_{\beta}(x)-\bar{\mathscr{Y}}_{\alpha}(x)\partial_{\lambda}b_{\beta}(x)\big]\,\mathfrak{a}_{\pi}^{\lambda}(x),

(112)

where

\displaystyle\mathfrak{a}_{\pi}^{\lambda}(x)=\frac{1}{4}\int d^{4}x_{1}\Big(\hat{\pi}_{\perp}^{\rho\sigma}(x),\,\hat{\pi}_{\perp\rho\sigma}(x_{1})\Big)(x_{1}-x)^{\lambda}.

(113)

Here,
\mathfrak{a}_{\pi}^{\lambda}
can be further simplified with the help of (
387
) in Appendix
F
. We get

\displaystyle\mathfrak{a}_{\pi}^{\lambda}

\displaystyle=iu^{\lambda}\lim_{\omega\to 0}\frac{d}{d\omega}\bar{\eta}_{{}_{\pi\pi}}(\omega),

(114)

where
\bar{\eta}_{{}_{\pi\pi}}(\omega)
is the frequency dependent transport coefficient, given by

\displaystyle\bar{\eta}_{{}_{\pi\pi}}(\omega)=\frac{1}{4}\int d^{4}x_{1}e^{i\omega(t-t_{1})}\Big(\hat{\pi}_{\perp}^{\rho\sigma}(x),\,\hat{\pi}_{\perp\rho\sigma}(x_{1})\Big).

(115)

With this, Eq. (
112
) takes the simplified form,

\displaystyle\big<\hat{\pi}_{\perp}^{\mu\nu}\big>^{\text{(2),NL}}_{2}

\displaystyle=2\big[{}^{<}D\bar{\sigma}_{\perp}^{\mu\nu>}-B^{\prime}\bar{\mathscr{X}}^{<\mu}Du^{\nu>}-\bar{\mathscr{Y}}^{<\mu}Db^{\nu>}\big]\mathscr{W}_{\pi\pi}.

(116)

Here, for brevity, we omitted the explicit arguments. Also, we have defined

\displaystyle\mathscr{W}_{\pi\pi}=i\lim_{\omega\to 0}\frac{d}{d\omega}\bar{\eta}_{{}_{\pi\pi}}(\omega).

(117)

6.1.2
Local correction from the two-point correlation function using extended thermodynamic forces:
\big<\hat{\pi}_{\perp}^{\mu\nu}(x)\big>^{\text{(2),ET}}_{2}

Since the extended thermodynamic forces,
\hat{\mathcal{C}}_{S}
, contains no rank-two operators, Curie’s symmetry principle implies that it cannot couple to the rank-two tensor operator
\hat{\pi}_{\perp}^{\mu\nu}
.
Consequently, the corresponding two-point correlation vanishes:

\displaystyle\Big<\hat{\pi}_{\perp}^{\mu\nu}(x)\Big>^{\text{(2),ET}}_{2}=\int d^{4}x_{1}\,\big(\hat{\pi}_{\perp}^{\mu\nu}(x),\,\hat{\mathcal{C}}_{S}(x_{1})\big)=0.

(118)

6.1.3
Local correction from the three-point correlation function:
\big<\hat{\pi}_{\perp}^{\mu\nu}(x)\big>^{\text{(3)}}_{2}

Unlike the two-point correlators—where, by Curie’s principle, only operators of the same tensorial rank couple—three-point correlation functions can admit couplings among tensors of different ranks.
Such mixed-rank couplings are allowed provided the correlator can be decomposed into irreducible tensors that reproduce the symmetry, parity, and exchange properties of the operators entering the correlator.

With these symmetry considerations in hand, we now turn to the evaluation of the three-point correlators. Using (
63
) and (
77
), we get

\displaystyle\Big<\hat{\pi}_{\perp}^{\mu\nu}(x)\Big>^{\text{(3)}}_{2}=\int d^{4}x_{1}d^{4}x_{2}\,\big(\hat{\pi}_{\perp}^{\mu\nu}(x),\,\hat{\mathcal{C}}_{F}(x_{1}),\,\hat{\mathcal{C}}_{F}(x_{2})\big)\big|_{local}.

(119)

To avoid confusion, we use

\displaystyle\hat{\mathcal{C}}_{F}(x_{1})

\displaystyle=-\bar{\theta}_{\perp}\,\hat{P}_{\perp}-\bar{\theta}_{\parallel}\,\hat{P}_{\parallel}+\hat{\mathscr{K}}^{\rho}\,\bar{\mathscr{Y}}_{\rho}+\hat{\mathscr{J}}^{\rho}\,\bar{\mathscr{X}}_{\rho}+\hat{\pi}_{\perp}^{\rho\sigma}\bar{\sigma}_{\perp\rho\sigma}+\hat{m}^{\rho\sigma}\bar{\chi}_{\rho\sigma},

(120)

\displaystyle\hat{\mathcal{C}}_{F}(x_{2})

\displaystyle=-\bar{\theta}_{\perp}\,\hat{P}_{\perp}-\bar{\theta}_{\parallel}\,\hat{P}_{\parallel}+\hat{\mathscr{K}}^{\alpha}\,\bar{\mathscr{Y}}_{\alpha}+\hat{\mathscr{J}}^{\alpha}\,\bar{\mathscr{X}}_{\alpha}+\hat{\pi}_{\perp}^{\alpha\beta}\bar{\sigma}_{\perp\alpha\beta}+\hat{m}^{\alpha\beta}\bar{\chi}_{\alpha\beta}.

(121)

As discussed in the Section
5
, we extract the local terms by evaluating the hydrodynamic quantities present in
\hat{\mathcal{C}}_{F}(x_{1})
and
\hat{\mathcal{C}}_{F}(x_{2})
at
x_{1}=x
and
x_{2}=x
, respectively. As a result, each term in Eq. (
119
) carries a second-order gradient term extracted from the hydrodynamic variables in
\hat{\mathcal{C}}_{F}
.
Consequently, all Kubo correlators can be evaluated to leading order as mention in section
5
, and we can use the projectors (irreducible tensors) to write a tensor decomposition without specifying a particular spacetime point.
In this approximation, a general covariant tensor decomposition for a sixth-rank Kubo correlator, transverse to both fluid velocity and magnetic field, takes the form

\displaystyle\Big(\hat{X}^{\mu\nu}(x),\hat{Y}^{\rho\sigma}(x_{1}),\hat{Z}^{\alpha\beta}(x_{2})\Big)

\displaystyle=a_{1}\,\mathcal{G}^{\mu\nu}\mathcal{G}^{\rho\alpha}\mathcal{G}^{\sigma\beta}+a_{2}\,\mathcal{G}^{\mu\nu}\mathcal{G}^{\rho\beta}\mathcal{G}^{\sigma\alpha}+b_{1}\,\mathcal{G}^{\rho\sigma}\mathcal{G}^{\mu\alpha}\mathcal{G}^{\nu\beta}+b_{2}\,\mathcal{G}^{\rho\sigma}\mathcal{G}^{\mu\beta}\mathcal{G}^{\nu\alpha}

\displaystyle~~~+c_{1}\,\mathcal{G}^{\alpha\beta}\mathcal{G}^{\mu\rho}\mathcal{G}^{\nu\sigma}+c_{2}\,\mathcal{G}^{\alpha\beta}\mathcal{G}^{\mu\sigma}\mathcal{G}^{\nu\rho}+d\,\mathcal{G}^{\mu\nu}\mathcal{G}^{\rho\sigma}\mathcal{G}^{\alpha\beta}

\displaystyle~~~+e_{1}\,\mathcal{G}^{\mu\rho}\mathcal{G}^{\nu\alpha}\mathcal{G}^{\sigma\beta}+e_{2}\,\mathcal{G}^{\mu\rho}\mathcal{G}^{\nu\beta}\mathcal{G}^{\sigma\alpha}+e_{3}\,\mathcal{G}^{\mu\sigma}\mathcal{G}^{\nu\alpha}\mathcal{G}^{\rho\beta}+e_{4}\,\mathcal{G}^{\mu\sigma}\mathcal{G}^{\nu\beta}\mathcal{G}^{\rho\alpha}

\displaystyle~~~+e_{5}\,\mathcal{G}^{\mu\alpha}\mathcal{G}^{\nu\rho}\mathcal{G}^{\sigma\beta}+e_{6}\,\mathcal{G}^{\mu\alpha}\mathcal{G}^{\nu\sigma}\mathcal{G}^{\rho\beta}+e_{7}\,\mathcal{G}^{\mu\beta}\mathcal{G}^{\nu\rho}\mathcal{G}^{\sigma\alpha}+e_{8}\,\mathcal{G}^{\mu\beta}\mathcal{G}^{\nu\sigma}\mathcal{G}^{\rho\alpha},

(122)

This work develops a complete second-order formulation of relativistic magnetohydrodynamics (RMHD) within the framework of Zubarev’s nonequilibrium statistical operator (NESO), based on the modern, symmetry-driven foundation in which the total energy-momentum conservation and the Bianchi identity (magnetic-flux conservation) serve as the fundamental dynamical equations. This approach avoids the conceptual limitations of the conventional RMHD formulation, which separates the fluid and electromagnetic energy-momentum tensors and relies on the assumption of infinite conductivity to remove the electric field from the hydrodynamic sector. By contrast, in the present formalism the magnetic field is treated as a true hydrodynamic variable at
\mathcal{O}(\partial^{0})
, whereas the electric field appears only as a higher-order induced effect encoded in the dissipative corrections.

We include a full extraction of nonlocal contributions by expanding not only the thermodynamic forces coupled to
\hat{T}^{\mu\nu}
and
\hat{\tilde{F}}^{\mu\nu}
, but also the hydrodynamic fields entering the tensor structures of these operators themselves. This improves upon previous treatments, which expanded only selected thermodynamic forces and omitted the hydrodynamic fields inside the operators. This refinement leads to the elimination of several terms from the evolution equations for both the bulk viscous pressure and the dissipative vectors. By fully expanding all hydrodynamic quantities contained in
\hat{\mathcal{C}}
, we find that the evolution equation for the bulk viscous pressure no longer contains terms of the form
\dot{u}_{\mu}\bar{\mathscr{X}}^{\mu}
,
\dot{u}_{\mu}\bar{\mathscr{Y}}^{\mu}
. Likewise, the evolution equations for the dissipative vectors
\mathscr{J}^{\mu}
and
\mathscr{K}^{\mu}
no longer include contributions of the type
\dot{u}_{\nu}\bar{\sigma}_{\perp}^{\mu\nu}
and
\dot{u}_{\nu}\bar{\chi}^{\mu\nu}
.

Furthermore, we find that the nonlinear terms involving three-point correlations between the second-rank tensors operators
\hat{\pi}_{\perp}^{\mu\nu}
and
\hat{m}^{\mu\nu}
are absent in the evolution equations for
\pi_{\perp}^{\mu\nu}
and
m^{\mu\nu}
. This can be traced to two reasons.
First, certain three-point correlators—such as
\big(\hat{\pi}_{\perp}^{\mu\nu}(x),\,\hat{m}^{\rho\sigma}(x_{1}),\,\hat{\pi}_{\perp}^{\alpha\beta}(x_{2})\big)
—do not preserve the parity of the equilibrium state. By Curie’s principle, such terms must vanish and are therefore discarded. Second, even among the correlators that do preserve parity—such as
\big(\hat{\pi}_{\perp}^{\mu\nu}(x),\,\hat{\pi}_{\perp}^{\rho\sigma}(x_{1}),\,\hat{\pi}_{\perp}^{\alpha\beta}(x_{2})\big)
and
\big(\hat{\pi}_{\perp}^{\mu\nu}(x),\,\hat{m}^{\rho\sigma}(x_{1}),\,\hat{m}^{\alpha\beta}(x_{2})\big)
—the corresponding transport coefficients are found to vanish due to the specific tensor structures involved.

In this work, we focus on a parity-conserving, charge-conjugation-symmetric plasma and therefore do not include Hall transport. An important direction for future research is to extend the present formulation to systems exhibiting Hall responses within the fully conserved and symmetry-based framework developed here, and to derive the corresponding evolution equations for Hall transport. It would also be of considerable interest to incorporate spin degrees of freedom together with magnetic fields. Although first-order theories combining spin and magnetohydrodynamics have been formulated—both within kinetic theory
[
45
]
and nonequilibrium statistical approaches
[
46
]
—a complete second-order relativistic hydrodynamic theory that consistently includes both spin and magnetic fields has not yet been established.

Appendix E
Bulk viscous pressure

The bulk viscous pressure is defined as the difference between the statistical average of the total pressure operator and the equilibrium pressure (Eq. (
67
)).
For the bulk viscous pressure, parallel to the magnetic field, this yields

\displaystyle\Pi_{\parallel}

\displaystyle=\langle\hat{p}_{\parallel}\rangle-p_{\parallel}(\varepsilon_{eq},B_{eq}).

(354)

Here,
p_{\parallel}
denotes the equilibrium pressure parallel to the magnetic field.
It is a state function of the two independent conserved quantities—the energy density
\varepsilon
and the magnetic flux density
B
—whose dynamics are governed by the conservation equations (
9
) and (
10
).

Using (
58
), we can write

\displaystyle\big<\hat{p}_{\parallel}\big>=\big<\hat{p}_{\parallel}\big>_{l}+\big<\hat{p}_{\parallel}\big>_{1}+\big<\hat{p}_{\parallel}\big>_{2}+\cdots.

(355)

The local-equilibrium average of the total pressure operator is a function of the equilibrium energy density and equilibrium magnetic flux density.
Accordingly, using

\big<\hat{\varepsilon}\big>_{l}=\big<\hat{\varepsilon}\big>-\Delta\varepsilon
and
\big<\hat{B}\big>_{l}=\big<\hat{B}\big>-\Delta B
,
we obtain

\displaystyle\big<\hat{p}_{\parallel}\big>_{l}

\displaystyle=\left\langle\hat{p}_{\parallel}\big(\big<\hat{\varepsilon}\big>_{l},\ \big<\hat{B}\big>_{l}\big)\right\rangle_{l}

(356)

\displaystyle=p_{\parallel}(\varepsilon_{eq},B_{eq})-\frac{\partial p_{\parallel}}{\partial\varepsilon}\,\Delta\varepsilon-\frac{\partial p_{\parallel}}{\partial B}\,\Delta B+\frac{1}{2}\,(\Delta\varepsilon)^{2}\,\frac{\partial^{2}p_{\parallel}}{\partial\varepsilon^{2}}+\frac{1}{2}\,(\Delta B)^{2}\,\frac{\partial^{2}p_{\parallel}}{\partial B^{2}}+(\Delta\varepsilon)(\Delta B)\,\frac{\partial^{2}p_{\parallel}}{\partial\varepsilon\,\partial B},

(357)

where
\Delta\varepsilon
and
\Delta B
denote the higher-order corrections to the statistical averages of the energy density and the magnetic flux density, respectively, given by

\displaystyle\Delta\varepsilon

\displaystyle=\big<\hat{\varepsilon}\big>_{1}+\big<\hat{\varepsilon}\big>_{2},\qquad\Delta B=\big<\hat{B}\big>_{1}+\big<\hat{B}\big>_{2}.

(358)

Hence, retaining terms up to second order in the gradient expansion, we can write

\displaystyle\big<\hat{p}_{\parallel}\big>_{l}

\displaystyle=p_{\parallel}(\varepsilon_{eq},B_{eq})-\frac{\partial p_{\parallel}}{\partial\varepsilon}\,\big<\hat{\varepsilon}\big>_{1}-\frac{\partial p_{\parallel}}{\partial B}\,\big<\hat{B}\big>_{1}-\frac{\partial p_{\parallel}}{\partial\varepsilon}\,\big<\hat{\varepsilon}\big>_{2}-\frac{\partial p_{\parallel}}{\partial B}\,\big<\hat{B}\big>_{2}

\displaystyle\quad+\frac{1}{2}\,\big<\hat{\varepsilon}\big>_{1}^{2}\,\frac{\partial^{2}p_{\parallel}}{\partial\varepsilon^{2}}+\frac{1}{2}\,\big<\hat{B}\big>_{1}^{2}\,\frac{\partial^{2}p_{\parallel}}{\partial B^{2}}+\big<\hat{\varepsilon}\big>_{1}\,\big<\hat{B}\big>_{1}\,\frac{\partial^{2}p_{\parallel}}{\partial\varepsilon\,\partial B}.

(359)

Using (
355
) in (
354
), we obtain

\displaystyle\Pi_{\parallel}

\displaystyle=\big<\hat{p}_{\parallel}\big>-p_{\parallel}(\varepsilon_{eq},B_{eq})

(360)

\displaystyle=\big<\hat{p}_{\parallel}\big>_{l}+\big<\hat{p}_{\parallel}\big>_{1}+\big<\hat{p}_{\parallel}\big>_{2}-p_{\parallel}(\varepsilon_{eq},B_{eq})

(361)

Now, substituting (
359
) in (
361
), we obtain

\displaystyle\Pi_{\parallel}=\big<\hat{P}_{\parallel}\big>_{1}+\big<\hat{P}_{\parallel}\big>_{2}+\frac{1}{2}\,\big<\hat{\varepsilon}\big>_{1}^{2}\,\frac{\partial^{2}p_{\parallel}}{\partial\varepsilon^{2}}+\frac{1}{2}\,\big<\hat{B}\big>_{1}^{2}\,\frac{\partial^{2}p_{\parallel}}{\partial B^{2}}+\big<\hat{\varepsilon}\big>_{1}\big<\hat{B}\big>_{1}\,\frac{\partial^{2}p_{\parallel}}{\partial\varepsilon\,\partial B}.

(362)

Here, we defined

\displaystyle\hat{P}_{\parallel}=\hat{p}_{\parallel}-\hat{\varepsilon}\,\frac{\partial p_{\parallel}}{\partial\varepsilon}-\hat{B}\,\frac{\partial p_{\parallel}}{\partial B},

(363)

Similarly, by analogy with Eq. (
362
), the second-order expression for
\Pi_{\perp}
can be written as

\displaystyle\Pi_{\perp}=\big<\hat{P}_{\perp}\big>_{1}+\big<\hat{P}_{\perp}\big>_{2}+\frac{1}{2}\,\big<\hat{\varepsilon}\big>_{1}^{2}\,\frac{\partial^{2}p_{\perp}}{\partial\varepsilon^{2}}+\frac{1}{2}\,\big<\hat{B}\big>_{1}^{2}\,\frac{\partial^{2}p_{\perp}}{\partial B^{2}}+\big<\hat{\varepsilon}\big>_{1}\big<\hat{B}\big>_{1}\,\frac{\partial^{2}p_{\perp}}{\partial\varepsilon\,\partial B}.

Appendix F
Correlation Function

The derivation presented in this section follows closely the analysis given in Ref.
[
41
,
20
,
40
]
.
Starting from the (
53
), we can write

\Big(\hat{X}(\vec{x},t),\hat{Y}(\vec{x}_{1},t_{1})\Big)=\int_{0}^{1}d\tau\Big<\hat{X}(\vec{x},t)\left[e^{-\hat{A}\tau}\hat{Y}(\vec{x}_{1},t_{1})e^{\hat{A}\tau}-\left<e^{-\hat{A}\tau}\hat{Y}(\vec{x}_{1},t_{1})e^{\hat{A}\tau}\right>_{l}\right]\Big>_{l}.

(366)

Referring to Eq. (
49
), we infer that
\hat{A}=\beta\hat{\mathscr{H}}
, where
\hat{\mathscr{H}}
denotes the effective Hamiltonian of the system in the presence of an external magnetic field. Consequently, the imaginary-time evolution of an operator in the Heisenberg picture is given by

\hat{Y}(\vec{x},t+i\tau^{\prime})=e^{-\hat{\mathscr{H}}\tau^{\prime}}\hat{Y}(\vec{x},t)e^{\hat{\mathscr{H}}\tau^{\prime}}.

(367)

This relation immediately implies the Kubo-Martin-Schwinger (KMS) conditions:

\displaystyle\left<\hat{Y}(\vec{x},t+i\tau^{\prime})\right>_{l}

\displaystyle=\left<\hat{Y}(\vec{x},t)\right>_{l},

(368)

\displaystyle\left<\hat{X}(\vec{x},t)\hat{Y}(\vec{x}_{1},t_{1}+i\beta)\right>_{l}

\displaystyle=\left<\hat{Y}(\vec{x}_{1},t_{1})\hat{X}(\vec{x},t)\right>_{l}.

(369)

Using (
368
) and (
369
), in (
366
) and
assuming that the correlations vanish at
t\to\infty
, we can
establish the relation

\Big(\hat{X}(\vec{x},t),\hat{Y}(\vec{x}_{1},t_{1})\Big)=-\frac{1}{\beta}\int_{-\infty}^{t_{1}}dt^{\prime}G^{R}_{\hat{X}\hat{Y}}(\vec{x}-\vec{x}_{1},t-t^{\prime}),

(370)

where

\displaystyle G^{R}_{\hat{X}\hat{Y}}(\vec{x}-\vec{x}_{1},t-t^{\prime})=-i\theta(t-t^{\prime})\left<\left[\hat{X}(\vec{x},t),\hat{Y}(\vec{x}_{1},t^{\prime})\right]\right>_{l}.

(371)

We now consider the general structure of a frequency-dependent transport coefficient expressed in terms of a two-point correlation function. It is defined as

\displaystyle\mathcal{I}_{\hat{X}\hat{Y}}(\omega)=\beta\int d^{3}x_{1}\int_{-\infty}^{t}dt_{1}e^{\epsilon(t_{1}-t)}e^{i\omega(t-t_{1})}\Big(\hat{X}(\vec{x},t),\hat{Y}(\vec{x}_{1},t_{1})\Big).

(372)

The retarded Green’s function obtained in (
371
) is
transitionally invariant both in space as well as in time. We exploit this property to rewrite the integral (
372
)
by setting
(\vec{x},t)=(0,0)
.
Substituting (
370
) in (
372
), we obtain

\mathcal{I}_{\hat{X}\hat{Y}}(\omega)=-\int^{0}_{-\infty}dt_{1}e^{(\epsilon-i\omega)t_{1}}\int_{-\infty}^{t_{1}}dt^{\prime}\int d^{3}x_{1}G_{\hat{X}\hat{Y}}^{R}(-\vec{x}_{1},-t^{\prime}).

(373)

Considering the Fourier transformation

G^{R}_{\hat{X}\hat{Y}}(\vec{x}_{1},t^{\prime})=\int\frac{d^{3}k}{(2\pi)^{3}}\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\frac{d\omega^{\prime}}{2\pi}e^{-i(\omega^{\prime}t^{\prime}-\vec{k}\cdot\vec{x})}G_{\hat{X}\hat{Y}}^{R}(\vec{k},\omega^{\prime}),

(374)

and using the definition of Dirac delta function, we can write

\int d^{3}xG_{\hat{X}\hat{Y}}^{R}(-\vec{x},-t^{\prime})=\lim_{\vec{k}\to 0}\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\frac{d\omega^{\prime}}{2\pi}e^{i\omega^{\prime}t^{\prime}}G_{\hat{X}\hat{Y}}^{R}(\vec{k},\omega^{\prime}).

(375)

Substituting (
375
) in (
373
) and
solving the integral over
t^{\prime}
by shifting
\omega^{\prime}\to\omega^{\prime}-i\delta
, where
\delta\to 0^{+}
, we obtain

\mathcal{I}_{\hat{X}\hat{Y}}(\omega)=\lim_{\delta\to 0^{+}}\frac{i}{\omega+i\epsilon}\oint\frac{d\omega^{\prime}}{2\pi i}\left[\frac{1}{\omega^{\prime}-(\omega+i\epsilon+i\delta)}-\frac{1}{\omega^{\prime}-i\delta}\right]G^{R}_{\hat{X}\hat{Y}}(\omega^{\prime}).

(376)

Here,
G^{R}_{\hat{X}\hat{Y}}(\omega^{\prime})\equiv\lim_{\vec{k}\to 0}G^{R}_{\hat{X}\hat{Y}}(\vec{k},\omega^{\prime})
.

To evaluate (
376
), we first extend
\omega^{\prime}
to the complex plane and apply Cauchy’s integral formula together with the residue theorem. From (
376
), we identify two poles at
\omega^{\prime}=\omega+i\epsilon+i\delta
and
\omega^{\prime}=i\delta
. We close the contour in the upper half-plane and assume that
G^{R}_{\hat{X}\hat{Y}}(\omega^{\prime})
decays sufficiently rapidly at infinity compared with (
\frac{1}{\omega^{\prime}}
). Consequently, we obtain

\mathcal{I}_{\hat{X}\hat{Y}}(\omega)=\lim_{\delta\to 0^{+}}\frac{i}{\omega+i\epsilon}\left[G^{R}_{\hat{X}\hat{Y}}(\omega+i\epsilon+i\delta)-G^{R}_{\hat{X}\hat{Y}}(i\delta)\right].

(377)

We now take the limits
\delta\to 0^{+}
and
\epsilon\to 0^{+}
,
where
\epsilon
denotes the irreversibility parameter that must be
set to zero at the end of the calculations. In this limit, we obtain

\mathcal{I}_{\hat{X}\hat{Y}}(\omega)=\frac{i}{\omega}\left[G^{R}_{\hat{X}\hat{Y}}(\omega)-G^{R}_{\hat{X}\hat{Y}}(0)\right].

(378)

For
\omega\to 0
, (
378
) can be simplified to

\mathcal{I}_{\hat{X}\hat{Y}}(0)=i\lim_{\omega\to 0}\frac{d}{d\omega}G^{R}_{\hat{X}\hat{Y}}(\omega)=i\lim_{\omega\to 0}\lim_{\vec{k}\to 0}\frac{\partial}{\partial\omega}G^{R}_{\hat{X}\hat{Y}}(\vec{k},\omega).

(379)

Moreover, since the operators
\hat{X}
and
\hat{Y}
are Hermitian,
we can write

\left<\left[\hat{X}(\vec{x},t),\hat{Y}(\vec{x}_{1},t_{1})\right]\right>_{l}^{*}=-\left<\left[\hat{X}(\vec{x},t),\hat{Y}(\vec{x}_{1},t_{1})\right]\right>_{l},

(380)

which indicates that
\left<\left[\hat{X}(\vec{x},t),\hat{Y}(\vec{x}_{1},t_{1})\right]\right>_{l}
is a purely imaginary
quantity.

Using Eq. (
371
), it follows that the retarded Green’s function

G^{R}_{\hat{X}\hat{Y}}
and the canonical correlation

\Big(\hat{X}(\vec{x},t),\hat{Y}(\vec{x}_{1},t_{1})\Big)

\mathcal{I}_{\hat{X}\hat{Y}}(0)

(381)

The second type of correlation function that emerges in our analysis is given by

Here, the temporal component is given by

The spatial component of

\mathcal{J}^{\mu}_{\hat{X}\hat{Y}}(\omega)
vanishes in the local
rest frame, since the correlator
\Big(\hat{X}(x),\hat{Y}(x_{1})\Big)
evaluated in local rest frame depends on

|\vec{x}-\vec{x}_{1}|
, which make

\mathcal{J}^{i}_{\hat{X}\hat{Y}}(\omega)
an odd function of

\vec{x}-\vec{x}_{1}
.

Furthermore, considering a Taylor expansion
of
G^{R}_{\hat{X}\hat{Y}}(\omega)
around
\omega=0
, we have

G^{R}_{\hat{X}\hat{Y}}(\omega)=G^{R}_{\hat{X}\hat{Y}}(0)+\omega\frac{d}{d\omega}G^{R}_{\hat{X}\hat{Y}}(\omega)\bigg|_{\omega=0}+\frac{\omega^{2}}{2!}\frac{d^{2}}{d\omega^{2}}G^{R}_{\hat{X}\hat{Y}}(\omega)\bigg|_{\omega=0}+\cdots,

(384)

Substituting (

), we obtain

\mathcal{I}_{\hat{X}\hat{Y}}(\omega)=i\frac{d}{d\omega}G^{R}_{\hat{X}\hat{Y}}(\omega)\bigg|_{\omega=0}+\frac{i\omega}{2!}\frac{d^{2}}{d\omega^{2}}G^{R}_{\hat{X}\hat{Y}}(\omega)\bigg|_{\omega=0}+\cdots.

Next, upon inserting Eq. (
385

\omega\to 0
, we find

\displaystyle\mathcal{J}^{0}_{\hat{X}\hat{Y}}(0)

\displaystyle=-\frac{1}{2}\lim_{\omega\to 0}\frac{d^{2}}{d\omega^{2}}G^{R}_{\hat{X}\hat{Y}}(\omega)=-\frac{1}{2}\lim_{\omega\to 0}\frac{d^{2}}{d\omega^{2}}{\rm Re}G^{R}_{\hat{X}\hat{Y}}(\omega)=-\frac{1}{2}\lim_{\omega\to 0}\lim_{\vec{k}\to 0}\frac{\partial^{2}}{\partial\omega^{2}}{\rm Re}G^{R}_{\hat{X}\hat{Y}}(\vec{k},\omega).

(386)

Finally, we can write

\beta\int d^{4}x_{1}\Big(\hat{X}(x),\hat{Y}(x_{1})\Big)(x_{1}-x)^{\mu}=\mathcal{J}^{\mu}_{\hat{X}\hat{Y}}(0)=u^{\mu}\mathcal{J}^{0}_{\hat{X}\hat{Y}}(0)=u^{\mu}\lim_{\omega\to 0}i\frac{d}{d\omega}\mathcal{I}_{\hat{X}\hat{Y}}(\omega).
