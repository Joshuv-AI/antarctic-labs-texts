‘Pushing the change’: barriers, facilitators, and implementation plan for integrating individually tailored physical activity guidance in rheumatology practice
Authors: Tanja Thomsen, Jeanette Wassar Kirk, Mette Aadahl, Charlotte Schøn Frengler, Henrik Røgind, Anette Rosager Poulsen, Bente Appel Esbensen
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ObjectivesThe objective of this paper was to identify determinants to implementing individually tailored physical activity (PA) counselling in rheumatology outpatient care, and secondarily, to develop a context-specific implementation plan. Together, these activities constituted the initial phase of a larger implementation research project guided by the Quality Implementation Framework (QIF).MethodsThis qualitative study was conducted in 4 rheumatology outpatient clinics in the Capital Region of Denmark. Data were collected through nonparticipant observations of clinical consultations and semistructured interviews with nurses, physicians, and managers. Data were analysed using a 2-step approach: a deductive thematic analysis guided by the Theoretical Domains Framework (TDF), followed by an inductive content analysis of all TDF-coded data. Identified determinants were mapped to implementation strategies in collaboration with local working groups.ResultsTwelve health professionals and managers were interviewed (mean age of 44 years and an average of 11 years of experience in rheumatology care). The most prominent TDF domains were Environmental context and resources, Knowledge, and Beliefs about consequences. Four interrelated categories were identified: (i) Knowledge gaps and uncertainty in PA counselling; (ii) Role ambiguity in PA counselling; (iii) Organisational context shapes capacity and motivation; and (iv) Patient feedback as a driver of PA counselling. Barriers included knowledge gaps, limited confidence in tailoring advice, unclear role responsibilities, and insufficient structural support. Facilitators included strong professional values, leadership support for lifestyle counselling, and reinforcement through positive patient feedback. Based on these determinants, a theory-informed implementation plan was developed using Expert Recommendations for Implementing Change strategies.ConclusionsIntegrating PA counselling into rheumatology care requires addressing determinants at individual, professional, and organisational levels. Combining the QIF and TDF enabled a systematic and context-specific approach to early-phase implementation planning.

INTRODUCTION

Physical activity (PA) and exercise are now widely recognised as core components in the management of inflammatory arthritis (IA) [[1], [2], [3], [4], [5], [6], [7]]. Interventions across a spectrum of intensities from light PA to moderate-to-vigorous PA or replacement of sedentary behaviour with increased daily movement have shown improvements in pain, fatigue, physical function, quality of life, cardiometabolic biomarkers, and cardiorespiratory fitness outcomes [2,4,8,9]. Reflecting this, the European Alliance of Associations for Rheumatology recommends tailored PA promotion as standard in rheumatology care [10]. In particular, individually adapted PA counselling, respecting patient preferences, capacities, and contexts, has shown promising effects across various health outcomes in patients with IA [9,[11], [12], [13]]. Our own studies within the ‘Joint Resources’ research programme have demonstrated that a 16-week tailored behavioural intervention combining motivational interviewing and text messages effectively reduced sitting time and improved symptoms and metabolic risk factors in patients with RA [9,11].

Given the strength of evidence and professional endorsement, there is now a broad consensus that the key challenge is no longer if PA should be promoted in rheumatology, but how it can be consistently and meaningfully integrated into routine care [14]. However, despite these developments, implementation remains limited. Research shows that PA is often addressed inconsistently, with patients describing the advice as vague and nonspecific [15], and only about half of respondents in a large Swedish survey [16] recalled having discussions about PA with rheumatology clinical staff.

These gaps reflect a broader challenge in shifting rheumatology practice towards inclusion of a more preventive, health-promoting care. To address this, the field of implementation science offers useful methods for translating research evidence into practice [17]. Frameworks such as the Quality Implementation Framework (QIF) [18] provide structured guidance, beginning with an assessment of local conditions and moving through active implementation and sustainability. QIF emphasises the need for context-fit implementation strategies, developed in collaboration with stakeholders, and tailored to local barriers and facilitators (determinants) [18].

In this study, we applied the QIF to explore individual, professional, and organisational determinants for implementing tailored PA counselling in 4 outpatient rheumatology clinics in the Capital Region of Denmark. Our primary aim was to identify barriers and facilitators for integrating such counselling into routine practice. A secondary aim was to use these insights to inform the development of a context-specific implementation plan.

METHODS

Study design and epistemological orientation

This qualitative study formed part of a larger hybrid type III [19] implementation study (IMPAIA—Implementation of Physical Activity guidance in patients with Inflammatory Arthritis) aiming to promote and strengthen individually tailored PA counselling in rheumatology care. The current study focused on the preparatory phase of QIF (Phases 1 and 2) [18], ie, exploring determinants for PA counselling in the clinics. Figure 1 provides an overview of the phases in the overall IMPAIA project. Guided by a pragmatic epistemology [20] and a social constructivist view of knowledge [21], the study prioritised context-sensitive and coproduced insights through nonparticipant observations and semistructured interviews. This approach emphasises the generation of contextually relevant and applicable knowledge, while recognising that understanding of implementation processes is socially constructed and coproduced between researchers and participants. The theoretical domains framework

To systematically identify determinants of PA counselling, the Theoretical Domains Framework (TDF) [23] was applied due to its well-established ability to capture a broad range of individual, interpersonal, and contextual influences on professional behaviour [23,24]. The TDF consists of 14 theoretical domains, including, eg, Knowledge, Skills, Social/professional role and identity, Beliefs about capabilities, Environmental context and resources, and Social influences, and has been applied across numerous settings and behaviours [23]. In the current study, the TDF was used both to structure the interview guide and to support the analysis of determinants (see interview guide in Supplementary Material 2). This approach enabled structured identification of perceived determinants of PA counselling across clinical roles, and provided a structured approach to link this knowledge to strategies needed to support implementation of PA counselling into routine practise.

Setting

The study was conducted in 4 rheumatology outpatient clinics within a large, publicly funded hospital department in the Capital Region of Denmark. The clinics serve a high-volume patient population with IA and are staffed by multidisciplinary teams including rheumatologists, nurses, and administrative personnel. The sites vary in scale in terms of patient flow and staff size but operate under a shared leadership structure. Core outpatient activities include assessment, treatment, and long-term management of IA, with nurses holding primary responsibility for patient education, self-management support, and lifestyle counselling, including PA. Although PA is discussed in routine care, the extent and depth of counselling currently vary across clinics.

Participants

A purposive sampling strategy was adopted to recruit participants with direct experience of outpatient rheumatology care in order to capture perspectives relevant to the implementation of PA counselling in the clinics. The sampling aimed to ensure variation across professional roles and organisational levels by including physicians, nurses, and clinical managers from each of the 4 participating outpatient clinics. Managers were included to provide perspectives on organisational priorities, leadership support, and structural conditions relevant to the implementation of PA counselling. The research group defined these inclusion criteria and the intended professional representation. Clinical managers assisted with the practical recruitment of participants by identifying staff members who fulfilled the criteria and were available to participate, given clinical workload and scheduling constraints. All potential participants received written study information outlining the purpose and scope of the observational and interview components. Observed and interviewed participants were not the same. Participation was voluntary, and informed consent was obtained.

Data collection

Data were collected in 2 consecutive steps from February to October 2022: first, nonparticipant observations of clinical rheumatology practice, and second, individual semistructured interviews.

1) Nonparticipant observations

Across the 4 clinics, 12 days of nonparticipant observations were conducted by the first author (TT), 7 days of nurse-led and 5 days of physician-led consultations. Observations occurred during regular hours, with each session lasting 20-30 minutes. The aim was to explore real-time clinical interactions and how, when, and by whom PA was addressed. An open, exploratory approach was applied without a predefined checklist [25]. Field notes captured verbal exchanges (eg, phrasing, questions, and hesitations) and contextual factors (eg, time pressure, workflow interruptions, and body language), offering insight into routines and the organisational setting for PA counselling.

The observations played a direct formative role in shaping the interview guide. Accordingly, observation data were not formally analysed because their primary purpose was to provide contextual and practice-based grounding for the later interviews rather than to generate independent findings. However, they were subject to simple, informal thematisation, eg, noting recurring patterns such as who initiated PA, how symptoms shaped the dialogue, or when PA was deprioritised. Specifically, field notes from the observations were reviewed shortly after each observation session, and notes were discussed in the research group. These observations informed the refinement of the interview guide by identifying specific themes and situations that warranted further exploration in interviews. For instance, repeated observations showed that health professionals who personally valued PA were more likely to initiate PA conversations. This led to specific interview questions about the perceived role of PA, personal beliefs, and how these influenced clinical behaviour. Similarly, observed variation in how PA was addressed (or omitted) informed questions about the participants’ beliefs about PA, their perceived role responsibilities, and contextual and organisational constraints within consultations.

2) Semistructured interviews

Following the observations, individual semistructured interviews were conducted by TT with physicians, nurses, and clinical managers. Interviews took place face to face in private clinic rooms to ensure confidentiality and minimise interruptions. All interviews were digitally recorded. A TDF-based interview guide was developed and tailored to each professional role (Supplementary 2). While the same core topics were covered, emphasis differed: clinicians were asked about routines, skills, and patient interactions, whereas managers were asked about organisational priorities, resources, and decision-making. Each interview began with brief background information (eg, role and years of experience) to contextualise responses. A total of 12 interviews were conducted—1 nurse, 1 physician, and 1 manager from each of the 4 outpatient clinics to ensure both professional and site-level representation. This sample size was prespecified and considered sufficient based on the concept of information power, as the study had a clear aim, a relatively homogenous participant group, and strong interview specificity.

Ethical considerations

The study adhered to the Declaration of Helsinki [26] and was approved by the data protection agency in the Capital Region of Denmark (journal no. P-2021-726). All participants provided written informed consent. No participants received compensation, as observations and interviews were conducted during regular working hours. The clinical department managers gave prior approval. All data were pseudonymised during interview transcription and handled confidentially.

Reflexive attention to relational and situational ethics [27] and sensitivity to participants’ perceived comfort were maintained throughout observations, including awareness of the researcher’s influence and withdrawal during emotionally sensitive situations.

Data analysis

All interviews were transcribed verbatim using participant pseudonyms, and transcripts were imported into NVivo (Version 14, QSR International Ltd). Data were analysed using a deductive thematic analysis approach [28], based on the 14 TDF domains, allowing for systematic identification of implementation-relevant determinants delivering PA counselling. All transcripts were coded by TT and selected transcripts were double coded by coauthors (JWK and BAE) (examples in Table 1). Following this, an inductive content analysis [29] of the full coded dataset was conducted to group barriers and facilitators into overarching categories. This allowed less frequent yet meaningful insights to be included. The inductive analysis followed the procedures described by Graneheim and Lundman [29], focusing on identifying patterns across meaning units to develop manifest-level categories grounded in the participants’ own expressions. This was appropriate given the study’s aim to describe actual barriers and facilitators as articulated by participants, rather than exploring underlying assumptions. Rigour and trustworthiness of the analysis was ensured through several strategies. Transcript verification involved reviewing the transcripts against the audio recordings to ensure accuracy of transcription. Analytic consistency was supported through iterative discussions within the research team and through double coding of selected transcripts by coauthors (JWK and BAE). Reflexive awareness of the researchers’ roles and potential influence on data interpretation was maintained throughout the analytic process. Illustrative quotes are presented to support transparency and grounding of the findings in the participants’ statements.

Development of an implementation plan

To support the development of a context-specific implementation plan, we conducted a structured, multistep process following the identification of behavioural and contextual determinants. First, TDF-based determinants were mapped to specific implementation needs and subsequently to implementation strategies. An implementation strategy was defined as a ‘method or technique used to enhance the adoption, implementation, and sustainability of a clinical program or practice’ [30], ie, methods to support integration of PA counselling into routine care.

This mapping and selection of strategies were carried out collaboratively between TT and small working groups at each outpatient clinic, consisting of a nurse, a physiotherapist, and a department manager, ensuring representation of both clinical and managerial perspectives when prioritising and assessing the feasibility of strategies. These health professionals had also contributed to earlier study phases and provided essential insights into feasibility and contextual relevance. At the first meeting, TT presented synthesised findings from the TDF-guided analysis. Secondly, this was followed by structured discussions on practical needs and implementation opportunities, guided by printed summaries and matrices linking determinants to potential strategies. Detailed notes were taken to capture feedback and key suggestions. Based on these discussions, the working groups prioritised strategies guided by the Expert Recommendations for Implementing Change (ERIC) taxonomy [31] to ensure consistency between identified determinants (eg, skills, role clarity, and environmental resources) and practical feasibility. In this selection of strategies, the distribution of meaning units across TDF domains was used descriptively to illustrate how determinants were represented in the dataset. However, the frequency of coding was not interpreted as a direct indicator of practical importance. The prioritisation of implementation strategies was instead informed by a combined assessment of the deductive TDF findings, cross-cutting categories identified in the inductive analysis, and structured discussions with the clinical working groups. Finally, strategies were then refined in discussions between TT and the last author (BAE) and further validated in a second meeting with the working groups.

To strengthen clinical relevance, the working groups also drew on the implementation model by Metsios and Kitas [32], which outlines how structured PA counselling in rheumatology care can be delivered by different professionals at varying levels of intensity. This model helped situate the implementation strategies within existing rheumatology workflows and informed practical decisions about roles, delivery formats, and integration into daily practice, for example, the emphasis on nurse-led delivery and graded PA advice.

Category 1. Knowledge gaps and uncertainty in PA counselling

Most participants agreed that PA is important in managing rheumatic conditions, but differences in knowledge and confidence influenced how and whether they addressed it in consultations. Many expressed uncertainties about safe recommendations when symptoms fluctuate, particularly regarding pain and fatigue, leading to hesitation or vagueness:‘I know it's good, of course, but I’m not always sure what to say when the patient has pain or fatigue. What is safe then?’ (Nurse, >5 years of rheumatology experience).

‘I know it's good, of course, but I’m not always sure what to say when the patient has pain or fatigue. What is safe then?’ (Nurse, >5 years of rheumatology experience).

Several participants highlighted a lack of up-to-date knowledge or training in PA recommendations, especially for patients with active disease:‘The problem is that we don’t always know what type of activity is recommended when the patient has, say, swollen joints or low energy. We haven’t really been taught that’ (Physician, >10 years of rheumatology experience).

‘The problem is that we don’t always know what type of activity is recommended when the patient has, say, swollen joints or low energy. We haven’t really been taught that’ (Physician, >10 years of rheumatology experience).

Uncertainty also extended to how to approach PA counselling in a way that supports behaviour change, rather than just providing general advice:‘It’s one thing to say that PA is good, but how do you help someone who feels completely fatigued or scared to move? I think I would feel a bit lost in those conversations.’ (Nurse, <5 years of rheumatology experience). And expressed by this physician:‘I think I miss a more structured approach… like, how do you guide someone from knowing it is important to actually doing it? It is not just about giving information, it is about helping them take the next step.’ (Physician, >5 years of rheumatology experience)

In contrast, participants with strong personal convictions about the benefits of PA were more likely to raise the topic, despite lacking formal tools or guidelines. This was also supported by data from the clinical field observations, where participants who seemed to personally endorse PA more frequently initiated discussions with patients about PA:‘I’ve always been physically active myself, and I try to encourage that mindset in my patients. But yes, I mostly speak from personal conviction - not necessarily clinical evidence.’ (Physician, >5 years of rheumatology experience)‘It makes a difference when you believe in it yourself. These findings highlight how varying knowledge levels, lack of formal guidance, and personal beliefs influence confidence and consistency in PA counselling across clinics.

Category 2. Role ambiguity in PA counselling

There was a clear variation in how PA counselling was perceived as part of professional responsibilities. Nurses often saw it aligned with their supportive role:‘We have the time to go into these lifestyle aspects, and patients expect us to, so of course we need to’ (Nurse, >5 years of rheumatology experience).

‘We have the time to go into these lifestyle aspects, and patients expect us to, so of course we need to’ (Nurse, >5 years of rheumatology experience).

However, role boundaries were sometimes unclear, leading to doubts about who should initiate PA conversations:‘Sometimes I’m unsure if I should take it up or if it is physiotherapists who really should offer this.’ (Nurse, >5 years of rheumatology experience).

‘Sometimes I’m unsure if I should take it up or if it is physiotherapists who really should offer this.’ (Nurse, >5 years of rheumatology experience).

Physicians echoed similar uncertainty, with some seeing PA counselling as outside their primary remit:‘It’s not that I don’t care about PA, but I see it more as part of what the nurse or physiotherapist would bring up.’ (Physician, >15 years of rheumatology experience).

‘It’s not that I don’t care about PA, but I see it more as part of what the nurse or physiotherapist would bring up.’ (Physician, >15 years of rheumatology experience).

This ambiguity was also reflected in observations, where some nurses confidently integrated PA counselling, while others redirected patients to physiotherapists. These differences appeared linked less to time and more to perceived role clarity. From the clinical managers’ perspective, this ambiguity was acknowledged:‘We don’t have a common understanding of who says what and when. So sometimes we all think someone else will do it.’ (Manager, >10 years of management experience).‘If we want this (PA counselling) to be standard, we need to define who does what and when.’ (Manager, <5 years of management experience).

‘We don’t have a common understanding of who says what and when. So sometimes we all think someone else will do it.’ (Manager, >10 years of management experience).

‘If we want this (PA counselling) to be standard, we need to define who does what and when.’ (Manager, <5 years of management experience).

Thus, the lack of shared expectations and clear routines appeared to contribute to inconsistent practice and missed opportunities for systematic PA counselling.

Category 3. Organisational context shapes capacity and motivation

Participants frequently emphasised how the broader work environment influenced their ability to provide PA counselling. Structural factors such as lacking time, lack of routines, and absence of prompts in the electronic patient record were seen as key barriers:‘ If it doesn’t appear anywhere in the record or isn’t part of the agenda, it easily slips away, especially when you are pressed for time.’ (Nurse, >5 years of rheumatology experience).

‘ If it doesn’t appear anywhere in the record or isn’t part of the agenda, it easily slips away, especially when you are pressed for time.’ (Nurse, >5 years of rheumatology experience).

Several participants pointed to system-level constraints, including missing templates or materials:‘It would be helpful if we had something built into the system, like a prompt or a template. Right now, you have to remember it yourself. And maybe also a simple brochure or a short video to give patients - something concrete to support the conversations.’ (Physician, >5 years of rheumatology experience).

‘It would be helpful if we had something built into the system, like a prompt or a template. Right now, you have to remember it yourself. And maybe also a simple brochure or a short video to give patients - something concrete to support the conversations.’ (Physician, >5 years of rheumatology experience).

A clinical manager elaborated on how staffing and prioritisation affected practice:‘There are consultations where there would be time to address physical activity, but if we are short-staffed or the clinic is busy, it often gets deprioritized. It really depends on the individual (health professional) and the situation, but also on how they prioritize it themselves…and whether they feel adequately equipped to provide and communicate meaningful advice about physical activity’ (Manager, >5 years of management experience).

‘There are consultations where there would be time to address physical activity, but if we are short-staffed or the clinic is busy, it often gets deprioritized. It really depends on the individual (health professional) and the situation, but also on how they prioritize it themselves…and whether they feel adequately equipped to provide and communicate meaningful advice about physical activity’ (Manager, >5 years of management experience).

Despite these barriers, many expressed a strong intrinsic motivation to improve PA dialogue, but also that this was often in conflict with organisational structures:‘It’s frustrating, because I believe in it and want to help, but there’s not much support and time to actually do it.’ (Nurse, >5 years of rheumatology experience).

‘It’s frustrating, because I believe in it and want to help, but there’s not much support and time to actually do it.’ (Nurse, >5 years of rheumatology experience).

This tension between motivation and practical constraints was a recurring theme. Several participants highlighted the importance of shared responsibility between leadership and frontline staff to legitimise and enable PA counselling:‘I think we could do much more if we had a structure or something to lean on. Such experiences reinforced the importance of raising the topic:‘I had a patient coming back after our talk and she had now joined a walking group. It really motivates me to bring it up again together with another patient". Later, the same nurse continued: "It’s rewarding when you hear that our guiding actually is helping the patients to act, to do something. It makes you feel like it matters’ (Nurse, <5 years of rheumatology experience).

Together, the 4 categories highlight how the implementation of structured PA counselling is shaped by the interplay between knowledge, beliefs, roles, organisational context, and reinforcement through patient interaction. These elements function as both barriers and facilitators.

Strategies were prioritised in collaboration with the clinical working groups and designed to address the identified determinants. For instance, to address gaps in knowledge, confidence, and skills related to initiating and tailoring PA conversations (linked to the domains Skills, Knowledge, and Beliefs about capabilities), the plan included several core strategies, such as a 3-day motivational interviewing course for nurses delivered by an external consultant, supplemented by quarterly in-house training on PA evidence, recommendations, and behaviour change techniques. DISCUSSION

This study examined barriers and facilitators (determinant s) to implementing individually tailored PA counselling in rheumatology outpatient care and used these insights to inform the development of a context-specific implementation plan. The most frequently coded domains—Environmental context and resources, Knowledge, and Beliefs about consequences—highlight how both individual and structural factors shape implementation opportunities. Four interrelated categories were inductively derived from the data: (i) Knowledge gaps and uncertainty in PA counselling, (ii) Role ambiguity in PA counselling, (iii) Organisational context shapes capacity and motivation, and (iv) Patient feedback as a driver of PA counselling.

Key behavioural barriers at the individual level included limited up-to-date knowledge about PA recommendations for patients with symptoms such as pain and fatigue, and a perceived lack of capability to tailor advice—particularly when precision and evidence-based guidance were expected. Facilitators included strong personal beliefs in the value of PA, high intrinsic motivation, and alignment with preventive care values. Organisational barriers included time constraints, competing priorities, unclear professional roles, lack of prompts in the electronic record, absence of written support materials, and limited follow-up opportunities. In contrast, facilitators included visible leadership support, departmental prioritisation of lifestyle counselling, and the motivating impact of positive patient feedback, especially when patients reported symptom improvement or behavioural changes.

The identified implementation determinants—spanning knowledge, confidence, tailored guidance, role clarity, structural resources, and motivational reinforcement—formed a nuanced basis for the development of targeted implementation strategies. These determinants may interact dynamically. For example, strong individual motivation may be undermined by a lack of structural support, while organisational support and clear role expectations can activate existing capabilities. This interplay aligns with the COM-B model (Capability, Opportunity, Motivation – Behaviour) [33], which conceptualises behaviour (here PA counselling) as a function of capabilities, opportunities, and motivation, offering a useful lens to understand how various determinants shape clinical behaviour and can be influenced through specific strategies.

Expanding on this, a consistent theme across interviews was variation in knowledge and beliefs about PA in relation to IA management. Participants expressed uncertainty about how to tailor advice for patients with fatigue, pain, or disease flares. This reflects previous findings showing that limited knowledge, confidence, and training can hinder PA counselling, despite clinicians’ recognition of its importance [34,35], highlighting the connection between perceived competencies and clinical actions. In our study, motivation to promote PA was consistently high across professional groups, but this was often counteracted by doubts about their capabilities, particularly psychological aspects such as confidence, up-to-date knowledge, and ability to tailor advice. Many participants felt a responsibility to provide precise and safe guidance, and hesitated when they felt underprepared. This tension between motivation and perceived competence echoes findings from oncology care, where health professionals feared discussing PA without having concrete solutions [36].

Our findings also highlight the role of automatic motivation in PA counselling, as described in the COM-B model [33]. When patients shared improvements after prior consultations, health professionals expressed emotional reward and increased commitment to PA guidance. These small ‘success stories’ functioned as meaningful reinforcers, strengthening the behaviour over time. This is supported by findings from a prospective cohort study [37], which showed that health professionals’ PA counselling is not only shaped by intention and perceived control but also by goal-directed behaviours facilitated by positive patient outcomes.

Our findings further highlight the importance of opportunity—both physical (eg, time, clinical resources, and educational materials) and social (eg, leadership support and role clarity)—in enabling PA counselling (cf. COM-B). Even when motivation and/or capability were present, a lack of structural support or organisational expectations often hindered action. This was evident when counselling was not embedded in routines, such as electronic prompts or patient materials, or when health professionals were uncertain whether PA discussions were within their responsibility. This reflects how PA counselling is shaped not only by internal factors but also by external conditions, including infrastructure, time, and professional norms [23,33,38]. A central barrier in our study was a lack of role clarity, with participants questioning whether PA counselling was their responsibility or that of physiotherapists or colleagues. Clarifying roles and expectations is essential in multidisciplinary care [39], as also illustrated by Alsop et al [40], who described how ‘everyone’s job is no one’s job’ undermines PA promotion efforts in hospitals. In this context, PA counselling by nurses and physicians may serve as an initial and integrated part of routine care, while referral to physiotherapists or occupational therapists may be appropriate when more specialised or intensive support is needed. Similarly, Parchment et al [41] found Environmental Context and Resources to be the most prominent TDF domain impeding behavioural counselling delivery in the UK healthcare system, due to workload pressures, limited time, and lack of leadership endorsement or embedded routines. Related to this, it is also important to consider that the role and emphasis of PA may differ across rheumatic diagnoses. While exercise is often considered a core nonpharmacologic treatment in osteoarthritis [42], in IA (eg, rheumatoid arthritis), it is typically recommended as a supportive component of comprehensive disease management alongside pharmacologic treatment. This may partly explain why organisational barriers emerged prominently in our study, as clinical priorities in IA care often focus on pharmacologic management and disease monitoring, leaving limited time and structural support for lifestyle counselling.

Taken together, these findings suggest that strengthening PA counselling in rheumatology care requires more than addressing gaps in knowledge and skills. Efforts must also enhance professionals’ confidence and motivation, provide structured opportunities, and clearly define roles to support sustained behaviour change in clinical practice.

Methodological and theoretical considerations

This study aimed to inform early implementation efforts by identifying local barriers and facilitators to PA counselling in rheumatology care. A qualitative design combining observations and interviews enabled insights into both clinical behaviours and underlying professional perspectives. This triangulation of methods strengthened the analysis and supported the development of a theory-informed and context-relevant implementation plan. Observations also helped shape the interview guide to reflect real-world practices. However, some limitations must be acknowledged. Observations were limited in number, scope, and duration, and participants were not involved in both data types. This separation was intentional to reduce bias but limited the ability to directly link observed practices with reported experiences. Additionally, participant recruitment was shaped by clinical workload, potentially introducing selection bias.

The study was positioned in the initial phase of the QIF, which emphasises assessing local conditions before active implementation begins [18]. This was crucial for developing strategies grounded in everyday clinical practice. However, perspectives from patients and cross-sectoral collaborators (eg, primary care or rehabilitation staff) were not included at this stage, which may limit the broader applicability of findings across rheumatology care. To partly address this, the study draws on previous qualitative research led by TT exploring patients’ experiences with PA counselling in Danish rheumatology care [15].

Implementation science offers a wide array of frameworks and models to guide research and intervention design, with the choice depending on the aim and stage of the implementation effort [17]. In this study, the TDF was selected to explore contextual, individual, and professional determinants of PA counselling. The TDF was considered suitable due to its integration of constructs from multiple behavioural theories into a practical framework for developing targeted strategies [24,43]. An alternative approach could have been the Consolidated Framework for Implementation Research [44], which offers a broader, multilevel perspective. However, we found that it places less emphasis on individual behavioural mechanisms, making the TDF more appropriate for our focus.

Our findings highlighted the need for implementation strategies that would address the dynamic interplay between individual, professional, and organisational determinants [45]. To achieve this, we systematically linked TDF-identified determinants to strategies from the ERIC taxonomy, ensuring both theoretical grounding and contextual relevance. This approach aligns with calls for more structured, theory-informed implementation planning in healthcare [30,31]. Selected strategies—such as ongoing training, role clarification, development of educational materials, and identification of PA champions—were selected to directly address key barriers and facilitators and reflect recommendations for implementation in chronic care contexts [46].

To conclude, this study identified key behavioural and contextual determinants for PA counselling in rheumatology care, including knowledge gaps, role ambiguity, and structural barriers. Guided by the TDF, we codesigned a targeted implementation plan in collaboration with clinical staff, addressing both individual and organisational factors. The project now moves into its active phase, where the strategies are being implemented and evaluated for fidelity [47] and acceptability [48]. This marks a transition from identifying barriers to embedding PA counselling into routine care—an essential step towards realising the ambitions of international recommendations and strengthening the role of implementation in rheumatology practice. We are now pushing the change.
