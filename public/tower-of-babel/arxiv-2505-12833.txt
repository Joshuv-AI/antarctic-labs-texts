Reasoning BO: Enhancing Bayesian Optimization with the Long-Context Reasoning Power of LLMs
Zhuo Yang, Lingli Ge, Dong Han, Tianfan Fu, Yuqiang Li

Abstract
Many real-world scientific and industrial applications require the optimization of expensive black-box functions. Bayesian Optimization (BO) provides an effective framework for such problems. However, traditional BO methods are prone to get trapped in local optima and often lack interpretable insights. To address this issue, this paper designs Reasoning BO, a novel framework that leverages reasoning models to guide the sampling process in BO while incorporating multi-agent systems and knowledge graphs for online knowledge accumulation. By integrating the reasoning and contextual understanding capabilities of Large Language Models (LLMs), we can provide strong guidance to enhance the BO process. As the optimization progresses, Reasoning BO provides real-time sampling recommendations along with critical insights grounded in plausible scientific theories, aiding in the discovery of superior solutions within the search space. We systematically evaluate our approach across 10 diverse tasks encompassing synthetic mathematical functions and complex real-world applications. The framework demonstrates its capability to progressively refine sampling strategies through real-time insights and hypothesis evolution, effectively identifying higher-performing regions of the search space for focused exploration. This process highlights the powerful reasoning and context-learning abilities of LLMs in optimization scenarios. For example, in the Direct Arylation task (a chemical reaction yield optimization problem), our method increased the yield to 60.7%, whereas traditional BO achieved only a 25.2% yield. Furthermore, our investigation reveals that smaller LLMs, when fine-tuned through reinforcement learning, can attain comparable performance to their larger counterparts. This enhanced reasoning capability paves the way for more efficient automated scientific experimentation while maintaining computational feasibility.

Introduction

Real-world scientific optimization problems often involve complex, expensive-to-evaluate
functions across diverse domains, from chemical synthesis
[
1
]

to hyperparameter tuning
[
2
,
3
]
.
Bayesian Optimization (BO) provides an efficient framework for such black-box
problems by constructing probabilistic surrogate models and intelligently guiding
experiments through acquisition functions
[
4
]
. While BO outperforms
traditional methods in sample efficiency, it faces key limitations: susceptibility
to local optima
[
5
]
, sensitivity to initial sampling,
and lack of interpretable scientific insights. These challenges motivate our
integration of reasoning capabilities with BO.

Recent research has explored integrating LLMs to create hybrid intelligent optimization
frameworks that overcome Bayesian Optimization’s inherent limitations. These
frameworks leverage three key LLM capabilities: First, their cross-domain
knowledge from pre-training provides global heuristics to avoid local optima
[
6
]
.
Second, domain priors like chemical reaction rules can be naturally injected
through language to reduce sensitivity to initial sampling. Most importantly,
LLMs generate scientifically plausible mechanistic hypotheses and iteratively
refine them through experiments
[
5
]
. However,
current LLM-BO integration approaches suffer from two critical limitations: (1)
hallucinated optimization suggestions that violate domain constraints,
potentially compromising safety, and (2) inability to dynamically incorporate
experimental discoveries into subsequent optimization cycles. These
shortcomings necessitate the development of memory-augmented closed-loop
optimization systems.

To address these challenges, we present the Reasoning BO framework (illustrated
in Figure
2
), which incorporates three novel mechanisms
for trustworthy intelligent optimization. First, we embed a Reasoning Model
within the traditional BO loop, operating through: (a) Experiment Compass
interaction where users describe experiments via natural language for
automatic configuration generation, (b) LLM-generated hypotheses with confidence
scores based on experimental history, knowledge graphs, and BO candidate points,
and (c) confidence-based filtering of hypotheses against historical data to
select the most scientifically plausible candidates. Second, we construct a
dynamic knowledge management system containing structured domain rules in
knowledge graphs and encoded unstructured literature in vector databases. This
system enables bidirectional interaction—allowing both pre-optimization expert
knowledge injection and real-time assimilation of new discoveries. Third, we
fine-tune lightweight models using reinforcement learning, with data sourced
from previous reasoning trajectories. These components synergistically achieve
a complete "hypothesis generation-experimental validation-knowledge
accumulation" closed loop.

For ease of exposition, we summarize our main contributions as

•

Incorporation of a reasoning model that leverages large language models’
inference abilities to automatically generate and evolve scientific
hypotheses, reducing time costs through automated validation while ensuring
scientific plausibility via confidence-based filtering, with
reinforcement learning-based knowledge distillation for stable
optimization.

•

Development of a full-cycle knowledge management system enabling seamless
encoding and dynamic updating of domain expertise throughout optimization,
featuring flexible knowledge architectures with customizable storage
formats and open agent interfaces for extensibility.

•

Demonstrated superior performance in complex scientific optimization
tasks requiring extensive domain knowledge, particularly in chemical
yield prediction with intricate objective functions. Our framework rapidly
identifies promising regions while achieving better convergence speed
and final accuracy, substantially reducing research costs by
minimizing validation trials and providing a trustworthy
optimization solution for high-cost experiments. In our Direct Arylation
benchmark, Reasoning BO demonstrates significant improvements over
Vanilla BO, achieving a 23.3% higher final yield (94.39% vs 76.60%)
and showing 44.6% higher initial performance (66.08% vs 21.62%),
showcasing both superior initialization and continuous optimization
capabilities.

Related Work

2.1
Bayesian Optimization

Bayesian Optimization serves as a fundamental paradigm for black-box function
optimization, with its core idea being the probabilistic modeling of the
objective function through surrogate models that are iteratively updated
based on historical observations
[
7
]
. A typical BO
workflow consists of two key: (1) A probabilistic surrogate model, typically
implemented as a Gaussian Process (GP) that approximates the objective
function

f(\mathbf{x})\sim\mathcal{N}(\mu(\mathbf{x}),\sigma^{2}(\mathbf{x}))

[
7
]
, where
\mu(\mathbf{x})
and
\sigma(\mathbf{x})

represent the predicted mean and uncertainty respectively; and (2) an acquisition
function that guides the selection of evaluation points by balancing exploration
and exploitation. Among various acquisition strategies, Expected Improvement
(EI) remains widely adopted due to its theoretical foundations and practical
effectiveness
[
8
,
9
]
.

In our work, we employ a robust variant of Expected Improvement called qLogEI
that effectively handles batch evaluations while maintaining numerical stability

[
10
]
. This acquisition function demonstrates particular
advantages when combined with Monte Carlo sampling methods, offering
improved performance in high-dimensional optimization scenarios. See Supplementary
Material Section
D.1
for detailed mathematical formulation
and derivation of qLogEI.

Despite BO’s theoretical sample efficiency, it faces fundamental challenges
in high-cost evaluation scenarios: (1) the computational complexity of surrogate
model training grows substantially with the number of evaluations, limiting
its feasibility for longer optimization runs
[
11
]
, and (2)
standard acquisition functions struggle to dynamically adjust the
exploration-exploitation trade-off, often leading to sub-optimal local optima

[
12
]
. In contrast, our approach partially
alleviates these limitations by leveraging LLMs’ global heuristic perspective
and reasoning capabilities.

3
Methodology

In this section, we present Reasoning BO, a novel framework that integrates reasoning
models with Bayesian optimization. Classical Bayesian optimization defines
search spaces as rigid parametric constructs, targeting either single-objective
optima
[
38
]
or Pareto fronts
[
39
,
40
]

in multi-objective scenarios. Such formulations are inadequate for domains requiring
flexible constraints
[
41
]
. Reasoning BO overcomes
this limitation by enabling natural language specification of experimental goals
and parameter constraints during initialization, allowing for highly
customizable reformulation of the search space. This approach facilitates the
injection of expert domain knowledge into the optimization process,
effectively ruling out infeasible regions early in the search. To manage accumulated
knowledge, Reasoning BO employs a dual-channel system combining vector database
retrieval and knowledge graph-driven causal reasoning. This hybrid architecture
supports continuous online updates, where reasoning trajectories are parsed
into verified <entity, relation, entity> triples by agents, which are then validated
through multi-agent collaboration and incrementally incorporated into the knowledge
base. Our work aims to design a user-friendly, highly customizable Bayesian optimization
framework adaptable to diverse downstream applications. Reasoning BO operates as
an end-to-end system: users provide an experiment compass outlining their objectives,
and the framework executes the experiment, delivering optimization reports and
hypotheses. Additionally, we explore the potential of smaller models enhanced
through reinforcement learning to achieve performance comparable to large-scale
reasoning models when supported by our comprehensive knowledge system,
holding significant implications for resource-constrained environments.

3.1
Motivation

The integration of LLMs with traditional Bayesian optimization stems from
three fundamental limitations in conventional approaches: (1) ineffective utilization
of domain-specific prior knowledge, (2) opaque mathematical black-box
optimization lacking interpretability, and (3) weak cross-domain adaptability.
These constraints become particularly pronounced in domains like chemistry
and biology where substantial domain knowledge is required, motivating our exploration
of a novel paradigm combining language models’ semantic understanding with
Bayesian optimization.

Current reasoning models typically neglect systematic storage of Chain-of-Thought
(CoT) data, leading to knowledge dissipation. To address this, we innovatively
employ intelligent agents to extract structured knowledge notes from reasoning
trajectories and implement persistent storage through vector databases and knowledge
graphs.

As illustrated in Figure
1
, traditional approaches do
not retain Chain-of-Thought (CoT) data within the context window. In subsequent
rounds of interaction, CoT data from previous rounds is not referenced, a design
choice intended to prevent lengthy reasoning processes from consuming valuable
context space and introducing noise. However, in experimental sciences, such
reasoning trails may contain critical insights, even if they are not immediately
salient in task performance. To address this, our framework extracts structured
notes from each round of CoT data and stores them in a database. During
subsequent queries to the language model, relevant information is retrieved from
the database using keywords extracted from the query. This mechanism ensures
more effective utilization of CoT data compared to conventional methods, enhancing
both the depth and relevance of the optimization process.

To provide a concise overview of Reasoning BO’s algorithmic framework, as illustrated
in Figure
2
, we outline the main process starting with an
Experiment Compass that initializes the search space using natural language
constraints and reformulates it into a structured format compatible with
Bayesian optimization. Dual-channel knowledge management dynamically updates
domain-specific information via vector databases and knowledge graphs during
iterative optimization rounds, while leveraging cumulative knowledge to
achieve performance comparable to large language models through smaller
reinforcement learning-enhanced models. This sets the stage for the three key
stages detailed in the following sections.

3.2
Reasoning-enhanced BO

Our Reasoning BO framework integrates reasoning models with Bayesian optimization
through a knowledge-guided optimization process. First, users provide an Experiment
Compass that leverages the language model’s semantic understanding to initialize
the search space. The model then generates an experimental overview and warm-starts
the initial data points. During each optimization round, a dual-channel
knowledge management system (vector database and knowledge graph)
dynamically updates domain knowledge.

The Reasoning BO algorithm (Algorithm
1
) implements
reasoning-enhanced Bayesian optimization through three key phases: (1) Initialization
where the LLM generates initial samples and constructs the Gaussian Process surrogate
model; (2) Iterative optimization rounds that generate five candidate points,
from which the LLM selects three optimal candidates based on current knowledge
and experimental data; (3) Continuous updates to both the knowledge base and
surrogate model parameters, ensuring the optimization balances domain knowledge
with data-driven principles.

Our sampling strategy avoids direct generation of candidate points by the LLM
to prevent test data contamination from its pretraining corpus and potential
hallucinations that may introduce noise. Instead, we adopt a BO-guided approach
where the optimization process directs the LLM’s hypothesis generation and reasoning,
enhancing robustness and stability. This design leverages the LLM’s strengths
in knowledge integration and interpretable reasoning while delegating numerical
optimization to the Bayesian framework. For a more concrete understanding of
the Reasoning BO framework, we provide an end-to-end algorithm example in Section
E.8

in supplementary material, which demonstrates the entire process with a
simplified case on Buchwald-Hartwig benchmark.

3.3
Dynamic Knowledge Precipitation

Our dynamic knowledge precipitation mechanism uses a multi-agent framework
for efficient extraction, verification, and integration of knowledge. A Verifier
agent extracts variable relationships and domain knowledge from LLM-generated
reasoning data, validating it via tool calls like web searches, SQL queries,
and code execution. A Formatter agent then structures the verified knowledge
for storage, ensuring consistency. Finally, a Notes Agent integrates this
structured knowledge into the vector database and knowledge graph. Additionally,
the system incorporates expert and prior knowledge to enhance domain-specific
comprehensiveness.

Verified knowledge is systematically stored in a dual-channel knowledge
management system: vector databases store high-dimensional semantic features
for efficient similarity searches, while knowledge graphs maintain
interpretable networks of conceptual relationships. This hybrid architecture
balances retrieval efficiency with semantic preservation.

During optimization, the system triggers knowledge retrieval through keyword
queries: first traversing the knowledge graph for structured knowledge paths,
then querying the vector database for contextual information. This dual retrieval
mechanism ensures access to both precise domain knowledge and potential cross-domain
associations.

The system continuously feeds high-quality reasoning insights back into the knowledge
base. New knowledge undergoes rigorous multi-agent verification: generation agents
gather relevant information and expand on it, while verification agents
ensure logical consistency, and domain expert agents assess professional reliability.
Only knowledge passing all verification stages enters the knowledge base, effectively
ensuring quality control and preventing error propagation. The management
system employs vector databases for high-dimensional semantic features and
knowledge graphs to represent interpretable conceptual relationships.

3.4
Post Training Strategies

To enhance the reasoning capabilities of smaller language models, we
implement a two-stage post-training strategy followed by a knowledge
integration phase:

1. Cold Start with Supervised Fine-Tuning:
In this stage, we
leverage Low-Rank Adaptation (LoRA)
[
42
]
to fine-tune
the model using domain-specific reasoning data. LoRA enhances efficiency by
freezing the pre-trained model weights to prevent catastrophic forgetting, while
introducing trainable rank decomposition matrices
B
and
A
into the multi-head
self-attention layers of the transformer architecture. These matrices are
sensitive to task-specific adaptations and allow the model to learn domain-specific
terminology and relationships effectively. During forward propagation, the
input
x
is processed as follows:

\varDelta W=BA,\hskip 10.00002pth=W_{0}x+\varDelta Wx=W_{0}x+BAx,

(1)

where
W_{0}
represents the original weight matrix of the pre-trained model,
and
\varDelta W
denotes the incremental adjustment introduced by LoRA. This
approach ensures that the model’s general linguistic capabilities remain intact
while adapting to the target domain.

2. Reinforcement Learning from Human Feedback (RLHF):
We employ
Group Relative Policy Optimization (GRPO) on the GSM8K mathematical
reasoning dataset to develop robust reasoning patterns. This stage strengthens
the model’s ability to: (1) decompose complex problems into logical steps, (2)
maintain coherent reasoning chains, and (3) verify intermediate conclusions.

Beyond these two stages, during the knowledge integration phase, the model
dynamically integrates information from the knowledge graph and vector
database (established in Sections 3.2-3.3). Each reasoning step triggers a dual
retrieval process: first querying the knowledge graph for structured
relationships, then searching the vector space for contextual patterns.

Empirical Insights

(1)
Performance:
As shown in Figure
3
,
Reasoning BO demonstrate the superior performance across a diverse set of
benchmarks, including real-world chemical optimization tasks such as Suzuki,
Direct Arylation, and Lunar, as well as mathematical synthetic functions
like Levy, Rosenbrock, and Ackley. Notably, Reasoning BO exhibits rapid
convergence even in high-dimensional and complex tasks, such as the 12-dimensional
Lunar benchmark, where it consistently identifies near-optimal solutions
within the early stages of optimization. This capability underscores the algorithm’s
robustness and efficiency in navigating intricate search spaces with significant
multi-factor interactions.

(2)
Initialization Strategy:
A distinguishing feature of Reasoning BO is
its advanced initialization leveraging expert priors and domain-specific
knowledge. For mathematical functions, this approach systematically selects
critical points within the search space, leading to highly effective initial
sampling. For instance, in the Levy function, whose global minimum lies at [1,
…, 1], Reasoning BO successfully initializes close to the optimal point,
facilitating rapid convergence. Similarly, for the Rosenbrock function, the algorithm
demonstrates the ability to locate favorable starting points that significantly
accelerate optimization progress. Even in cases with asymmetric bounds, such
as the Ackley function, Reasoning BO’s adaptive learning mechanism mitigates
suboptimal initializations by reflecting on and refining subsequent samples
based on accumulated insights.

Conclusions

This paper has presented Reasoning BO, a novel framework that systematically
integrates knowledge graphs, multi-agent systems, and reinforcement learning
into the Bayesian optimization paradigm, establishing an intelligent optimization
system for scientific discovery with superior cost-effectiveness. The
framework innovatively combines the reasoning capabilities of large language
models with the knowledge storage properties of knowledge graphs and vector
databases, creating a dynamic knowledge fusion mechanism that effectively injects
domain knowledge into the optimization process. The multi-agent architecture
ensures flexibility and adaptability, providing a unified and customizable solution
for user-friendly interactions. Coupled with a dynamic knowledge
accumulation mechanism that continuously aggregates scientific insights, it serves
as an extensible intelligent core for automated experimental platforms.
Future research directions include developing global attention mechanisms to
overcome context length constraints, creating novel knowledge distillation paradigms
to enhance knowledge extraction efficiency, and exploring synergistic
optimization paths between reinforcement learning algorithms and generated
data, further unlocking the reasoning potential of large language models in Bayesian
optimization.

Appendix D
Baselines

In our Reasoning BO approach, we primarily focus on the acquisition function
design by leveraging prior knowledge and LLMs’ reasoning capabilities.
Therefore, our baseline selection mainly considers variations of acquisition
functions, particularly the LogEI family. Additionally, we include
Covariance Matrix Adaptation Evolution Strategy (CMA-ES) as a special
numerical optimization baseline. CMA-ES is a stochastic, derivative-free
optimization method for nonlinear or non-convex continuous problems, belonging
to the class of evolutionary algorithms that mimic biological evolution principles
through mutation and selection mechanisms.

D.1
LogEI Family

The LogEI family addresses numerical stability issues in traditional
Expected Improvement (EI) by operating in log-space. While standard EI implementations
can suffer from numerical underflow when improvement probabilities are small,
LogEI transformations enable stable computation across the full range of
possible inputs
[
10
]
. The key transformation
is given by:

\text{LogEI}_{y^{*}}(\mathbf{x})=\texttt{log\_h}((\mu(\mathbf{x})-y^{*})/\sigma(\mathbf{x}))+\texttt{log}(\sigma(\mathbf{x}))

where
y^{*}
is the current best observation value,
\mu(\mathbf{x})
is
the predicted mean, and
\sigma(\mathbf{x})
is the predicted standard deviation.

D.1.1
Analytic LogEI

Analytic LogEI provides a numerically robust implementation through piecewise
decomposition:

\texttt{log\_h}(z)=\begin{cases}\texttt{log}(\phi(z)+z\Phi(z))&z>-1\\
-z^{2}/2-c_{1}+\texttt{log1mexp}(\log(\texttt{erfcx}(-z/\sqrt{2})|z|)+c_{2})&-1/\sqrt{\epsilon}<z\leq-1\\
-z^{2}/2-c_{1}-2\log(|z|)&z\leq-1/\sqrt{\epsilon}\end{cases}

where
c_{1}=\log(2\pi)/2
,
c_{2}=\log(\pi/2)/2
,
\epsilon
is the
numerical precision,
\phi
is the standard normal PDF,
\Phi
is the
standard normal CDF,
log1mexp
is a numerically stable
implementation of
\log(1-\exp(z))
, and
erfcx
is a numerically
stable implementation of
\exp(z^{2})\text{erfc}(z)
.

D.1.2
Monte Carlo Parallel LogEI

For parallel batch optimization, Monte Carlo LogEI extends the stability benefits
through:

\text{qLogEI}_{y^{*}}(\mathbf{X})\approx\log\text{sumexp}_{i}\left(\tau_{\max}\log\text{sumexp}_{j}\left(\log\text{softplus}_{\tau_{0}}(\xi^{i}(\mathbf{x}_{j})-y^{*})\right)/\tau_{\max}\right)

where
i
indexes Monte Carlo draws from the GP posterior,
j=1,\dotsc,q
indexes
candidates in the batch,
\tau_{0}
and
\tau_{\max}
are temperature
parameters controlling the approximation quality, and
logsoftplus

is a numerically stable implementation of
\log(\log(1+\exp(z)))
.

D.2
CMA-ES

CMA-ES (Covariance Matrix Adaptation Evolution Strategy) is an evolutionary
algorithm for continuous nonlinear optimization that adapts both the mean
and covariance matrix of its search distribution. The method combines maximum-likelihood
principles with evolution path tracking, making it particularly effective
for complex, non-convex optimization problems
[
51
]
.

The algorithm updates its parameters through likelihood maximization similar
to expectation-maximization. The mean vector update maximizes:

m_{k+1}=\underset{m}{\arg\max}\sum_{i=1}^{\mu}w_{i}\log p_{\mathcal{N}}(x_{i:\lambda}\mid m)

where
\log p_{\mathcal{N}}(x)
is the log-likelihood from a multivariate
normal distribution:

\log p_{\mathcal{N}}(x)=-\frac{1}{2}\log\det(2\pi C)-\frac{1}{2}(x-m)^{T}C^{-1}(x-m)

The covariance matrix update follows:

\sum_{i=1}^{\mu}w_{i}\frac{x_{i:\lambda}-m_{k}}{\sigma_{k}}\left(\frac{x_{i:\lambda}-m_{k}}{\sigma_{k}}\right)^{T}=\underset{C}{\arg\max}\sum_{i=1}^{\mu}w_{i}\log p_{\mathcal{N}}\left(\frac{x_{i:\lambda}-m_{k}}{\sigma_{k}}\mid C\right)

These updates enable CMA-ES to learn a second-order model of the objective
function while requiring only solution rankings rather than derivatives. The
method has demonstrated superior performance to Bayesian optimization in certain
hyperparameter tuning tasks
[
57
]
. The algorithm’s
effectiveness stems from several distinctive characteristics: its adaptive
step-size control mechanism prevents premature convergence, while the updates
can be interpreted as natural gradient descent. Furthermore, CMA-ES performs
principal component analysis on successful search steps to guide the optimization
trajectory, and exhibits notable robustness when handling ill-conditioned
problems.

Appendix F
Limitations and Future Work

F.1
Context Window Constraints

The context window limitation has been a fundamental constraint since the
initial adoption of large language models (LLMs), remaining a critical factor
in assessing model capabilities. While our algorithm employs a single-turn
interaction paradigm that avoids the memory burden of traditional multi-turn
dialogues, the accumulation of historical insights and experimental data across
optimization iterations may eventually approach context window limits.
Although our current experiments have not encountered this boundary, more complex
or prolonged optimization campaigns could face increased computational costs
due to context expansion. Future developments should investigate more
efficient memory-sharing architectures and attention mechanisms to enable
sustainable long-horizon optimization workflows. Additionally, multi-agent
framework designs could offer more flexible sampling strategies, while early
stopping mechanisms may help mitigate context inflation.

F.2
Instruction-Following Capability Dependencies

While fine-tuning and reinforcement learning can enhance a model’s zero-shot
warmstartting and in-context learning (ICL) capabilities, single-task
optimization may inadvertently reduce the model’s generalization and
instruction-following abilities
[
58
]
.
Excessive task-specific adaptation risks overfitting the model to particular
formats, potentially compromising its adaptability to novel tasks. In our
framework, the Insight Object serves as the critical interface between LLMs and
Bayesian optimization, with strict formatting requirements enforced through
prompt engineering and regular expression parsing. A valid Insight Object must
satisfy three criteria:

•

Strict adherence to the predefined Insight format specification

•

Generation of valid hypotheses and candidate points that comply with
all defined constraints

•

Absence of extraneous annotations or markers

Empirical observations reveal that smaller models (3B-7B parameters) frequently
fail to maintain consistent format compliance. This limitation persists
during reinforcement learning, where we observe significant degradation in foundational
instruction comprehension—even when employing anti-catastrophic forgetting techniques
like LoRA. For instance, our experiments with Qwen-2.5 (7B)
[
48
]

demonstrate substantial deterioration in instruction adherence post-adaptation,
necessitating the use of larger 14B models. Although real-world applications
may tolerate minor output deviations compared to benchmark conditions, the
observed declines in base capabilities and generalization remain concerning.
Developing adaptation paradigms that preserve model versatility represents a
crucial research direction for automated Bayesian experimentation, with
potential to significantly lower the adoption barrier for LLM-driven
optimization in science discovery.

F.3
Multi-Objective Optimization Potential

Our framework currently handles single-objective optimization, but LLMs show
promise for multi-objective scenarios. Their natural language capabilities
could help navigate trade-offs between competing objectives like yield and
cost
[
52
]
. Future work should explore how LLMs can
model these relationships and explain optimization decisions.
