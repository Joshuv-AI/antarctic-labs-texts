Stabilizing Federated Learning under Extreme Heterogeneity with HeteRo-Select
Md. Akmol Masud, Md Abrar Jahin, Mahmud Hasan

Abstract
Federated Learning (FL) is a machine learning technique that often suffers from training instability due to the diverse nature of client data. Although utility-based client selection methods like Oort are used to converge by prioritizing high-loss clients, they frequently experience significant drops in accuracy during later stages of training. We propose a theoretical HeteRo-Select framework designed to maintain high performance and ensure long-term training stability. We provide a theoretical analysis showing that when client data is very different (high heterogeneity), choosing a smart subset of client participation can reduce communication more effectively compared to full participation. Our HeteRo-Select method uses a clear, step-by-step scoring system that considers client usefulness, fairness, update speed, and data variety. It also shows convergence guarantees under strong regularization. Our experimental results on the CIFAR-10 dataset under significant label skew ( α = 0.1 \alpha=0.1 ) support the theoretical findings. The HeteRo-Select method performs better than existing approaches in terms of peak accuracy, final accuracy, and training stability. Specifically, HeteRo-Select achieves a peak accuracy of 74.75%, a final accuracy of 72.76%, and a minimal stability drop of 1.99%. In contrast, Oort records a lower peak accuracy of 73.98%, a final accuracy of 71.25%, and a larger stability drop of 2.73%. The theoretical foundations and empirical performance in our study make HeteRo-Select a reliable solution for real-world heterogeneous FL problems.



I
Introduction

Federated Learning (FL) is an important method in machine learning that enables model training on decentralized datasets while preserving data privacy. It allows multiple clients to collaboratively build a shared model without sharing their local data. However, a major limitation in FL is statistical heterogeneity, where non-independent and identically distributed (non-IID) data across clients leads to conflicting updates and model divergence. This often results in unstable training and presents challenges in achieving consistent performance. Intelligent client selection addresses this issue by choosing a subset of clients in each round who are most likely to contribute positively to the global model.

The present study on client selection methods often focuses on immediate utility to accelerate convergence. The authors in
[
1
]
and Oort
[
2
]
studied techniques like Power-of-Choice that prioritize clients with high local loss for their informative updates. Although these methods are effective in the early stages of training, they often suffer from performance instability and struggle to maintain strong performance in later rounds. Such instability shows a critical challenge for real-world applications that require reliable and consistent model performance.

To address this, we present the
HeteRo-Select
framework, designed for long-term stability over short-term gains. Unlike utility-focused methods, HeteRo-Select employs a multi-phase scoring system that evaluates clients on various dimensions. It balances immediate utility with factors like historical performance trends (momentum), participation equity (fairness), and data diversity to ensure a robust selection strategy. This approach prevents overfitting to a narrow subset of high-loss clients, mitigating performance degradation in later training stages and promoting consistent, dependable outcomes.

II
Related Works

The challenge of communication efficiency in FL, particularly under statistical heterogeneity, has been addressed primarily through two approaches: (1) reducing the size of the updates sent by clients, and (2) intelligently selecting which clients should send updates. Our work focuses on the latter, proposing a novel client selection framework that complements existing methods for managing heterogeneity.

II-A

Client Selection Strategies

The foundational FedAvg algorithm
[
3
]
proposed random client selection, which remains a common baseline. However, subsequent research has demonstrated that more intelligent client selection strategies can lead to significant performance improvements. Early studies have focused on utility-based selection. For instance, the Power-of-Choice approach proposed by the authors in
[
1
]
selects clients with a probability proportional to their local loss, thereby prioritizing those with more to learn. More recently, Oort in
[
2
]
introduced a sophisticated client selection method that balances statistical utility (based on local loss) with system efficiency (e.g., training time), aiming to maximize progress per unit of time. While Oort serves as a strong baseline, our experiments highlight a critical limitation: its emphasis on high-utility clients results in performance instability, failing to match the peak accuracy and long-term stability. This limitation represents a significant practical concern, one that our HeteRo-Select framework is specifically designed to overcome. Recent advances since 2021 have further diversified client selection strategies in Federated Learning. Clustered client selection methods, such as those proposed in
[
4
]
, group clients based on data similarity before selection. Reinforcement learning-based approaches in
[
5
]
learn optimal selection policies through trial-and-error interactions. Additionally, gradient diversity methods aim to select clients with diverse gradient directions to enrich learning dynamics. Other recent studies explore multi-objective optimization techniques that balance competing goals such as model accuracy and fairness during client selection.

Key Distinctions of HeteRo-Select
: Unlike these approaches, our HeteRo-Select framework uniquely combines: (1)
momentum tracking
that considers client improvement trends over time, (2)
explicit fairness and staleness factors
that ensure long-term participation equity, directly mitigating the stability issues observed in utility-focused methods like Oort, (3)
dynamic temperature adjustment
for a principled exploration-to-exploitation transition, and (4)
update norm penalties
to prevent destabilizing contributions. Most importantly, our work demonstrates the critical synergy between this sophisticated selection mechanism and strong regularization (
\mu=0.1
), a factor not explicitly optimized in prior selection strategies.

II-B

Update Compression and Heterogeneity

Many methods have been proposed to reduce the size of model updates, serving as a complementary approach to client selection in Federated Learning. These include techniques like quantization in
[
6
]
, sparsification, and knowledge distillation in
[
7
]
. These approaches focus on
how
an update is transmitted, whereas our work focuses on
who
should transmit it. Integrating update compression with intelligent selection could further improve efficiency, an area we leave for future exploration.

A significant body of work has analyzed the impact of heterogeneity on FL convergence in
[
8
,
9
]
. Within this context, FedProx in
[
10
]
emerged as a critical method for mitigating client drift by adding a proximal term to the local objective. In our work, FedProx is leveraged not merely as a baseline but as an integral component of our proposed solution. Our key contribution is demonstrating that when FedProx is used with a sufficiently strong regularization parameter (
\mu=0.1
), it has a powerful synergistic effect with our HeteRo-Select framework, enabling stable convergence in scenarios where other methods fail. This interplay between intelligent selection and strong regularization is the central theme of our paper. As our baseline experiments confirm, other advanced methods like SCAFFOLD and FedNova can be highly sensitive to hyperparameters, leading to divergence if not carefully tuned. This highlights the practical appeal and robustness of the FedProx-based approach, especially for real-world deployments.

Motivated by the need to overcome the instability of utility-focused selection methods, our proposed method centers on the
HeteRo-Select
mechanism. HeteRo-Select is designed to implement a controlled-bias, bounded-variance selection policy by balancing multiple client characteristics, moving beyond a singular focus on loss. This is combined with a standard, strongly regularized local client training process to manage update variance and promote stable convergence.

Definition III.1

(Client Update Information Value)
.

A central concept in our framework is the
information value
of a client’s update, denoted
V_{k}(t)
for client
k
at round
t
. This value quantifies the expected utility of client
k
’s participation in round
t
, serving as a proxy for how much their update might improve the global model. Intuitively, if a client exhibits high local loss on the current global model, this suggests that its data is under-represented or “surprising” relative to the global distribution, meaning its update may offer valuable correction. Therefore, we define
V_{k}(t)
to be increasing in the local loss
\ell_{k}(w_{t})
.

Assumption 1

(Learning Setting and Selection Conditions)
.

We operate under the following standard conditions for convergence and selection analysis:

A1

L-Smoothness:
Each local objective
f_{k}
and the global objective
f
are
L
-smooth, i.e.,
\|\nabla f_{k}(x)-\nabla f_{k}(y)\|\leq L\|x-y\|
for all
x,y
.

A2

Unbiased Gradients:
The local stochastic gradient is an unbiased estimator with bounded variance:
\mathbb{E}_{\xi_{k}}[g_{k}(w)]=\nabla f_{k}(w)
and
\mathbb{E}[\|g_{k}(w)-\nabla f_{k}(w)\|^{2}]\leq\sigma^{2}
.

A3

Bounded Gradients:
The squared norm of both stochastic and full-batch gradients is uniformly bounded: there exists
G^{2}<\infty
such that
\|g_{k}(w)\|^{2}\leq G^{2}
and
\|\nabla f_{k}(w)\|^{2}\leq G^{2}
for all clients
k
and models
w
. This assumption, standard in FL theory, is stated explicitly for completeness and is used later in Theorem
III.2
.

A4

Bounded Heterogeneity:
The average gradient dissimilarity across clients is finite:
B^{2}=\tfrac{1}{K}\sum_{k=1}^{K}\|\nabla f_{k}(w)-\nabla f(w)\|^{2}<\infty
.

A5

Client Availability:
All clients are assumed to be available for selection in each round. (Relaxations to partial availability follow
[
2
]
.)

A6

Score Component Bounds:
All individual scoring components
a_{ki}
are normalized to the range
[0,1]
, ensuring that both
S_{\min}
and
S_{\max}
are finite, where
S_{\min}
and
S_{\max}
denote the minimum and maximum possible value of the non-staleness part of the client score
S_{k}(t)
.

III-B

HeteRo-Select

The HeteRo-Select mechanism is a multi-phase scoring and selection process designed to comprehensively select clients by balancing immediate data utility with long-term objectives of fairness and diversity. At each round
t
, for each available client
k
, a composite score
S_{k}(t)
is computed. While we explored both multiplicative and additive approaches for combining scoring components, our experiments (detailed in Table
III
) confirmed that an additive model provides superior stability and performance. Therefore, the primary HeteRo-Select framework presented in this paper uses the following additive scoring function:

\displaystyle S_{k}(t)

\displaystyle=w_{v}V^{\prime}_{k}(t)+w_{d}D_{k}(t)+w_{m}M_{k}(t)

\displaystyle\quad+w_{f}F^{\prime}_{\text{add}}(t)+w_{st}St^{\prime}_{\text{add}}(t)+w_{n}N^{\prime}_{\text{add}}(t)

(1)

where
w_{v},w_{d},w_{m},w_{f},w_{st},w_{n}
are weights for the six components, with the last three representing additive transformations of fairness, staleness, and update norm factors. In our champion configuration, we set
w_{v}=w_{d}=w_{m}=1.0
and
w_{f}=w_{st}=w_{n}=1.0
. Hereafter, “HeteRo-Select” will refer to this additive configuration.

A multiplicative variant, used for comparison in our ablation studies, is defined as:

S_{k,\text{mult}}(t)=(V^{\prime}_{k}(t)\cdot D_{k}(t))\cdot M_{k}(t)\cdot F_{k}(t)\cdot St_{k}(t)\cdot N_{k}(t)

(2)

The scoring components are defined below.

III-B
1
Information Value and Diversity (
V^{\prime}_{k}(t),D_{k}(t)
)

The base score combines the information value proxy with a diversity metric. We use the client’s local loss as the primary value proxy, normalized across all available clients:

V^{\prime}_{k}(t)=\frac{\mathcal{L}_{k}(w_{t-1})-\min_{j}\mathcal{L}_{j}(w_{t-1})}{\max_{j}\mathcal{L}_{j}(w_{t-1})-\min_{j}\mathcal{L}_{j}(w_{t-1})+\epsilon}

(3)

The diversity score
D_{k}(t)
is based on the Jensen-Shannon (JS) divergence between the client’s label distribution
P_{k}
and the average distribution
P_{\text{avg}}
, weighted dynamically to favor diversity more in early rounds:

D_{k}(t)=\text{JS}(P_{k}\|P_{\text{avg}})\cdot(2\cdot(1-0.5\cdot\min(\frac{t}{100},1)))

(4)

III-B
2
Momentum Factor (
M_{k}(t)
)

To reward clients showing consistent improvement, we introduce a novel momentum tracking component that captures improvement trends between rounds. The momentum factor is based on relative loss change, bounded by a sigmoid-like transformation to prevent extreme values:

\displaystyle M_{k}(t)

\displaystyle=\frac{2}{1+e^{-5\cdot m_{k}(t)}}-0.5,

\displaystyle\text{where}\quad m_{k}(t)

\displaystyle=\frac{\mathcal{L}_{k}(w_{t-2})-\mathcal{L}_{k}(w_{t-1})}{\mathcal{L}_{k}(w_{t-2})}

(5)

Intuition
: The relative loss change
m_{k}(t)
measures how much a client’s performance improved between consecutive rounds. The sigmoid transformation
\frac{2}{1+e^{-5x}}-0.5
maps this to the range [-0.5, 1.5], ensuring that clients with moderate improvements get positive momentum scores while preventing clients with erratic or extreme loss patterns from dominating the selection process. This component is unique to our framework and addresses a key limitation of static utility-based selection methods. By tracking whether a client’s performance is improving or degrading over time, we can identify clients that are actively benefiting from the global model updates and are likely to provide valuable reciprocal contributions. The sigmoid transformation ensures that the momentum factor remains bounded between -0.5 and 1.5, preventing clients with erratic loss patterns from dominating the selection process.

III-B
3
Fairness and Staleness Factors (
F_{k}(t),St_{k}(t)
)

To ensure broad participation, two modulating factors are applied. The fairness factor penalizes frequently selected clients, where
h_{k}
is the participation count for client
k
:

F_{k}(t)=\left(1+\eta\cdot\frac{h_{k}^{t-1}}{\max_{j}h_{j}^{t-1}}\right)^{-2}

(6)

The staleness factor boosts clients not recently selected, where
l_{k}
is the last round client
k
was selected:

St_{k}(t)=1+\gamma\cdot\log(1+\min(t-l_{k},20))

(7)

III-B
4
Additive Transformations for Combined Scoring

For the additive scoring function in Equation (8), the multiplicative factors are transformed to additive components as follows:

Additive Fairness Factor:
The multiplicative fairness factor
F_{k}(t)\in[0,1]
is converted to an additive penalty term in the range
[-1,0]
:

F^{\prime}_{\text{add}}(t)=F_{k}(t)-1=\left(1+\eta\cdot\frac{h_{k}^{t-1}}{\max_{j}h_{j}^{t-1}}\right)^{-2}-1

(8)

Additive Staleness Factor:
The multiplicative staleness factor
St_{k}(t)\in[1,\infty)
is converted to a non-negative additive bonus:

St^{\prime}_{\text{add}}(t)=St_{k}(t)-1=\gamma\cdot\log(1+\min(t-l_{k},20))

(9)

Additive Update Norm Penalty:
The multiplicative norm factor
N_{k}(t)\in[1-\alpha,1]
(defined in the next section) is converted to an additive penalty in the range
[-\alpha,0]
:

N^{\prime}_{\text{add}}(t)=N_{k}(t)-1

(10)

These transformations ensure that the additive model maintains the same relative influence of each component while providing the flexibility that proves superior in our experiments.

III-B
5
Update Norm Penalty and Final Selection

A penalty factor
N_{k}(t)
discounts clients with excessively large updates from the previous round to mitigate instability. This is defined as:

N_{k}(t)=1-\alpha\cdot\left(\frac{2}{1+e^{\left\{-3\cdot\frac{\|w_{k}^{t^{\prime}}-w_{t^{\prime}-1}\|^{2}}{\text{avg}_{j}\,\|w_{j}^{t_{j}^{\prime}}-w_{t_{j}^{\prime}-1}\|^{2}}\right\}}}-1\right)

(11)

where
\alpha
is the norm penalty weight and
t^{\prime}
is the last round client
k
participated.

Intuition
: This formula maps the squared norm of a client’s update, normalized by the average across all clients, to a penalty term between 0 and
\alpha
using a shifted sigmoid-like function. Clients with update norms significantly larger than average receive higher penalties, reducing their selection probability to prevent destabilizing contributions to the global model.

Final scores are used to compute selection probabilities via a softmax function with dynamic temperature
\tau(t)=\tau_{0}\cdot(1-0.5\cdot\min(t/100,1))
, encouraging exploration early and exploitation later:

p_{k}(t)=\frac{\exp(S_{k}(t)/\tau(t))}{\sum_{j\in\mathcal{A}_{t}}\exp(S_{j}(t)/\tau(t))}

(12)

Clients are then selected based on these probabilities. The integrated process is detailed in Algorithm
1
.

III-B
6
Dynamic Temperature Adjustment

The dynamic adjustment of the temperature parameter
\tau(t)
is a critical feature of HeteRo-Select. In early training rounds, a higher temperature (e.g.,
\tau_{0}
) results in a more uniform selection probability distribution, promoting exploration by giving lower-scoring clients a higher chance of selection. As training progresses, the temperature decreases according to
\tau(t)=\tau_{0}\cdot(1.0-0.5\cdot\min(t/100,1.0))
, making the selection more deterministic and favoring high-scoring clients for exploitation. This transition ensures that the system initially explores a wide range of client data distributions to build a robust model foundation, then focuses on refining the model with the most valuable contributors. Parameter impacts are significant: a low base temperature (e.g., 0.1) leads to near-deterministic selection, while a high temperature (e.g., 1.0) increases randomness. Similarly, the staleness weight
\gamma
influences selection fairness, with higher values (e.g., 0.7) strongly prioritizing long-ignored clients, and lower values (e.g., 0.05) focusing on other metrics.

III-B
7
Scoring Function Justification

The choice between an additive and multiplicative combination for the components of the HeteRo-Select score
S_{k}(t)
represents a critical design decision. A multiplicative model enforces that all components must be reasonably balanced, as a low value in any single component significantly reduces the overall score. This can be beneficial for ensuring a holistic evaluation. However, an additive model provides greater flexibility, allowing a very high score in one area (e.g., high utility) to compensate for a lower score in another (e.g., low staleness). Our experiments in Section
V
show that this flexibility is crucial in heterogeneous environments. The additive approach consistently leads to better long-term stability and higher final accuracy, as it prevents the premature exclusion of valuable clients that might temporarily score low on one metric but are otherwise strong contributors. This data-driven validation is why we selected the additive model as our champion configuration.

III-C

The Critical Role of Regularization in Client Selection

A key finding is that intelligent client selection is most effective with strong local regularization. While HeteRo-Select selects diverse clients, their local gradients might conflict, risking instability during aggregation. To mitigate this, we employ the FedProx
[
10
]
objective during local training. The local objective for client
k
is:

\min_{w}\mathcal{L}_{k}(w)+\frac{\mu}{2}\|w-w_{t-1}\|^{2}

(13)

where
\mathcal{L}_{k}(w)
is the local loss,
w_{t-1}
is the previous global model, and
\mu
is the proximal coefficient. Our experiments show that strong regularization (e.g.,
\mu=0.1
) is essential for controlling the variance introduced by adaptive selection, ensuring stable integration of diverse information.

III-D

Theoretical Analysis

In this subsection, we provide a theoretical analysis for our HeteRo-Select framework. Our analysis demonstrates that under conditions of significant data heterogeneity, intelligent partial client participation can achieve better stability and faster convergence compared to full or random participation. Our analysis is based on the following standard assumptions for non-convex FL:

1.

L-Smoothness:
Each local objective
f_{k}
and the global objective
f
are L-smooth.

2.

Unbiased and Bounded Variance Gradients:
Local stochastic gradients
g_{k}(w)
are unbiased,
\mathbb{E}[g_{k}(w)]=\nabla f_{k}(w)
, and have bounded variance,
\mathbb{E}[\|g_{k}(w)-\nabla f_{k}(w)\|^{2}]\leq\sigma^{2}
.

3.

Bounded Heterogeneity:
The expected squared norm of the gradients is bounded by
G^{2}
, and the client drift is bounded by
B^{2}
, where
B^{2}\geq\frac{1}{K}\sum_{k=1}^{K}\|\nabla f_{k}(w)-\nabla f(w)\|^{2}
.

Theorem III.2

(Advantage of Intelligent Client Selection)
.

Let clients perform
E
local steps with learning rate
\eta_{l}
. The convergence of FL is fundamentally limited by a client drift term proportional to the heterogeneity of the selected clients.

1.

Random Sampling:
Selecting
m
clients uniformly at random results in a drift error term of
O(E\eta_{l}B^{2})
.

2.

Intelligent Selection:
An intelligent selection algorithm like HeteRo-Select, which prioritizes clients based on metrics such as momentum and diversity, can select a subset
C_{t}
with a lower effective heterogeneity
B_{sel}^{2}<B^{2}
. This reduces the drift error term to
O(E\eta_{l}B_{sel}^{2})
, leading to faster convergence to a more accurate model.

Justification:
The key insight is that the aggregated update of the
selected subset
of clients provides a (generally
biased
) estimate of the true global gradient, an estimate whose bias depends on how heterogeneous the chosen clients are and how they are sampled. The error in this estimate is driven by the diversity of gradients among the
selected
clients. Random sampling averages over the entire population’s heterogeneity
B^{2}
. HeteRo-Select aims to find a biased sample with more aligned gradients, thus constructing a better estimate of the global gradient and reducing the error that slows down convergence.

Theorem III.3

(Provable Exploration in HeteRo-Select)
.

Let the HeteRo-Select scoring function be
S_{k}(t)
, which includes a staleness bonus
\gamma\log(1+\min(t-l_{k},T_{\max}))
where
\gamma>0
and
l_{k}
is the last round client
k
was selected. Assume all other score components are bounded within
[S_{\min},S_{\max}]
. The selection probability
p_{k}(t)
for client
k
is strictly positive and lower-bounded by:

p_{k}(t)\geq\epsilon_{k}(t)=\frac{e^{\frac{S_{\min}+\gamma\log(1+t-l_{k})}{\tau(t)}}}{e^{\frac{S_{\min}+\gamma\log(1+t-l_{k})}{\tau(t)}}+(m-1)\,e^{\frac{S_{\max}}{\tau(t)}}}

(14)

This guarantees that as a client’s staleness
(t-l_{k})
increases, its probability of being selected also increases, preventing client starvation.

Justification:
This theorem provides a formal guarantee of fairness. The logarithmic staleness bonus ensures that a client’s score cannot remain indefinitely low. As other clients are chosen, a neglected client’s staleness bonus will grow, eventually making its total score high enough to be selected. The formula provides a precise, non-zero lower bound on this probability, proving the explorative nature of the algorithm.

Theorem III.4

(Role of FedProx in Stabilizing Local Updates, following
[
10
]
)
.

When clients perform
E>1
local steps, FedProx with regularization
\mu>0

from the global model

\mathbb{E}[\|w_{k}^{t,E}-w_{t}\|^{2}]\leq\frac{2E^{2}\eta_{l}^{2}}{1+E\eta_{l}\mu}(G^{2}+B^{2})

This bound is significantly tighter than the

\mu

Justification:
The proximal term
\frac{\mu}{2}\|w-w_{t}\|^{2}
acts as a gravitational pull, keeping local updates from straying too far from the consensus global model. This is critical under high heterogeneity, where unconstrained local training would cause local models to overfit to their skewed data, leading to conflicting updates that harm the global model upon aggregation.

(Non-Convex Convergence of the HeteRo-Select Framework)
.

\eta_{l}

reduces the stochastic gradient variance, as expected.

Justification:
This unified theorem demonstrates how the different parts of the proposed method work together. The convergence rate is limited by three main sources of error: initial distance to optimum (vanishes with

The combination leads to a more stable and faster learning process than methods that address these issues in isolation.

VI
Conclusion

We present the HeteRo-Select framework, a theoretical and empirical approach for addressing model instability in Federated Learning under statistical heterogeneity. Our key contributions include: (1) a rigorous theoretical framework demonstrating the superiority of selective communication under heterogeneity, with formal convergence guarantees; (2) a novel multi-phase additive scoring mechanism that provably balances utility, fairness, momentum, and diversity; (3) theoretical analysis showing the critical synergy between adaptive selection and strong regularization; and (4) comprehensive empirical study on CIFAR-10 demonstrating better long-term stability compared to utility-focused methods like Oort. Unlike purely utility-driven methods that suffer from instability, our HeteRo-Select framework achieves better peak accuracy of 74.75% and a final accuracy of 72.76% with only a 1.99% stability drop. This outperforms Oort, which achieves a lower peak (73.98%) and suffers a greater stability drop (2.73%) to finish at 71.25%. The theoretical framework provides formal justification for why intelligent partial participation can outperform full participation under extreme heterogeneity, while the additive scoring formulation ensures mathematical clarity and reproducibility.

Future work will focus on extending the theoretical analysis to non-convex objectives, developing adaptive hyperparameter tuning mechanisms, and evaluating computational overhead empirically across larger federated networks. The combination of strong theoretical foundations and superior empirical performance makes HeteRo-Select a promising solution for real-world FL deployments.

\bar{g}_{t}=\tfrac{1}{m}\sum_{k\in C_{t}}g_{k}^{t}

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[\|\bar{g}_{t}-\nabla f(w_{t})\|^{2}\right]

\displaystyle=\underbrace{\mathbb{E}\left[\left\|\bar{g}_{t}-\tfrac{1}{m}\textstyle\sum_{k\in C_{t}}\nabla f_{k}(w_{t})\right\|^{2}\right]}_{\text{local SGD variance}}

\displaystyle+\underbrace{\mathbb{E}\left[\left\|\tfrac{1}{m}\textstyle\sum_{k\in C_{t}}\nabla f_{k}(w_{t})-\nabla f(w_{t})\right\|^{2}\right]}_{\text{client heterogeneity}}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{\sigma^{2}}{m}+B_{\mathrm{sel}}^{2}

where
B_{\mathrm{sel}}^{2}=\tfrac{1}{m}\sum_{k\in C_{t}}\|\nabla f_{k}(w_{t})-\nabla f(w_{t})\|^{2}
denotes the
effective heterogeneity
of the selected subset. For uniformly random sampling
C_{t}^{\text{rand}}
of size

we have
\mathbb{E}[B_{\mathrm{sel}}^{2}]=B^{2}
by definition.

Let
\pi_{t}
be the probability distribution induced by HeteRo-Select. Because the score

is monotone in both momentum and diversity, clients with gradients further from the global direction receive smaller selection probabilities (they either progressed little — low momentum — or have outlier data — high JS distance, pushing the diversity term negative after centering). Formally, define the client‐specific heterogeneity
b_{k}^{2}=\|\nabla f_{k}(w_{t})-\nabla f(w_{t})\|^{2}
. Then

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{C_{t}\sim\pi_{t}}\bigl[B_{\mathrm{sel}}^{2}\bigr]

\displaystyle=\sum_{k=1}^{K}\pi_{t}(k)\,b_{k}^{2}\;\leq\;\sum_{k=1}^{K}\tfrac{1}{K}\,b_{k}^{2}\;=\;B^{2},

(18)

where the inequality follows because
\pi_{t}
puts larger mass on small

values (a consequence of the anti‐correlation between

and

proven in Lemma

, and the standard descent-lemma argument yields the improved convergence rate.

.

Under the scoring rules of Section

The diversity sub-score is proportional to

, which is monotonically increasing in

b_{k}

A-D

Additive
vs.
Multiplicative Scoring

Let
S_{k}^{\text{add}}\!=\!\sum_{i=1}^{p}a_{ki}
and
S_{k}^{\text{mult}}\!=\!\prod_{i=1}^{p}a_{ki}
, where
a_{ki}\in[0,1]
are normalized component scores. Define the coefficient of variation (CV) of the resulting softmax probabilities across clients as a proxy for selection concentration.

Proposition A.5
.

If the component scores are independent and
\operatorname{Var}[a_{ki}]>0
, then
\operatorname{CV}(\text{softmax}(S^{\text{mult}}))\geq\operatorname{CV}(\text{softmax}(S^{\text{add}}))
. Hence, multiplicative scoring yields more peaked (less fair) selections.

Sketch.

Using the delta-method for softmax outputs and the log transform for
S^{\text{mult}}
, one obtains

\operatorname{Var}[S^{\text{mult}}]\approx\bigl(\sum_{i}\operatorname{E}[\log a_{ki}]\bigr)^{2}\!\operatorname{Var}[a_{ki}]
, which compounds variability multiplicatively, whereas
\operatorname{Var}[S^{\text{add}}]=p\operatorname{Var}[a_{ki}]
grows only linearly. The larger input variance to the softmax translates into a higher CV of the resulting probabilities.
∎

This analytical result explains the empirical observation that the multiplicative variant exhibits higher selection concentration and larger stability drops (Table
I
). While the independence assumption simplifies the variance comparison, in practice, the component scores can be correlated. Our experiments (Section
V
) show that the additive model still yields superior stability under such correlations, so Proposition A.5 should be viewed as a guiding heuristic rather than a strict guarantee.
