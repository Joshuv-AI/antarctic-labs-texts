Background

Diabetes mellitus encompasses a group of metabolic disorders marked by abnormal metabolism of glucose, fat, and protein, with hyperglycemia as a defining characteristic. This condition can lead to acute complications such as diabetic ketoacidosis (DKA) and hyperosmolar hyperglycaemic state (HHS), and chronic issues including nephropathy, neuropathy, retinopathy, and cardiovascular diseases. Diabetes arises due to either an absolute or relative deficiency of insulin, combined with varying degrees of insulin resistance [1–4].

According to the WHO’s 2019 classification, diabetes is categorized into Type 1 (immune-mediated β-cell destruction), Type 2 (impaired insulin secretion with peripheral resistance), hybrid types, other specific types, unclassified diabetes, and gestational diabetes [1]. Type 2 diabetes is the most prevalent, accounting for approximately 90% of cases [5, 6]. The proportion of undiagnosed Type 2 diabetes varies globally, and about 40% of patients are asymptomatic at diagnosis, with 32% of these already experiencing microvascular complications [7, 8].

Achieving good glycemic control in Type 2 diabetes patients is crucial, as demonstrated by several large clinical trials. The UKPDS trial revealed significant benefits of intensive glycemic control, including a 12% reduction in diabetes-related endpoints and a 25% reduction in microvascular complications [9, 10]. The Kumamoto study also highlighted the benefits of intensive glycemic control, showing lower incidence and progression of retinopathy and nephropathy [11]. The ADVANCE trial indicated a 10% reduction in macrovascular and microvascular events with intensive control, primarily due to a 21% reduction in nephropathy. However, the ACCORD trial reported increased cardiovascular mortality in the intensive treatment arm, leading to its early termination [12–14]. The VADT trial found no significant difference in cardiovascular events between intensive and conventional treatment groups among patients with poorly controlled long-standing Type 2 diabetes [15].

Treatment for Type 2 diabetes should be individualized, considering factors such as patient life expectancy, existing complications, and comorbidities. Generally, a target HbA1c level of < 7% is recommended, but less stringent targets (< 8%) may be appropriate for patients with long-standing diabetes, complications, or those at risk of hypoglycemia [16]. Initial treatment includes diabetes education, lifestyle modifications, and the prevention of complications. Metformin is the first-line pharmacologic treatment unless contraindicated. For patients with established cardiovascular disease or chronic kidney disease, GLP-1 receptor agonists or SGLT2 inhibitors are preferred. Insulin therapy is considered for patients with severe hyperglycemia, symptoms of catabolism, or when other therapies fail to achieve glycemic control [16–18].

Despite evidence supporting early and intensive glycemic control, clinical inertia remains a significant barrier to initiating and intensifying treatment, including insulin therapy [19]. Various studies have identified reasons for delaying insulin therapy, such as fear of hypoglycemia, perceived inconvenience, and patient resistance due to concerns about pain and side effects [20–29]. In Ethiopia, a study at Tikur Anbessa Specialized Hospital (TASH) found that longer diabetes duration and insulin therapy were associated with poor glycemic control [30, 31].

Given the increasing burden of Type 2 diabetes globally and in Ethiopia, early treatment and prevention of complications are critical. Understanding the attitudes, practices, and knowledge of both patients and physicians regarding insulin therapy can help improve diabetes management. This study involves healthcare providers and patients from TASH Hospital, aiming to gather data on perceptions of insulin therapy and identify potential areas for intervention to enhance diabetes care.

Methods

Study area

The study was conducted at Tikur Anbessa Specialized Hospital (TASH). TASH is located in Addis Ababa, the capital of Ethiopia. It is one of the most highly specialized Hospitals in town that accepts referrals from all over the country.

Study period

The study was conducted from July 1 to September- 30, 2020.

Study design

A cross-sectional, quantitative study design was used.

Source population

For the data collected from T2DM patients, the source population was all T2DM patients who were having follow-up at the Diabetic clinic of Tikur Anbessa Specialized Hospital (TASH).

For the data collected from health care providers, the source population was all General Internists, residents, and General practitioners who are working in the Diabetic clinic.

Study population

For the data collected from the patients with T2DM, the study population was all selected patients with type 2 DM who came for follow-up at the TASH Diabetic clinic during the data collection period.

For the data collected from health care providers, the source population was all General Internists, residents, and General practitioners who were working in the Diabetic clinic during the data collection period were included.

Inclusion and exclusion criteria

Inclusion criteria

For the data to be collected from health care providers, the inclusion criteria was being either an Internist, internal medicine resident, or a general practitioner and working at the Diabetes clinic in TASH and consenting to be included in the study.

For the data to be collected from the patients with T2DM, the inclusion criteria was being a T2 DM patient on follow-up at the TASH diabetes clinic, age ≥ 18, and willing to participate in the study.

Exclusion criteria

Those patients with no record of HgA1C in the past 03–06 month were excluded from participating in the study conducted on patients with T2DM.

Data collection and quality assurance

Data collection from patients was conducted using a structured questionnaire. Which was adopted from Kenyan study and modified for this study [see supplementary] Data was collected by using trained nurses with continuous supervision from the main investigator. Translation of questionnaire from English to Amharic and then back to English was performed using 2 different experts of English language and the differences were settled via agreement between the two. Content validation of the questionnaire was performed by analyzing reviews from three different experts of the subject matter.

The questionnaires were pretested on 10 patients and 10 physicians who were later excluded.

The questionnaire prepared for the physicians was sent to them individually via e-mail using Google Forms.

The quality of the data was checked daily for completeness and inconsistency with on-site visits by the main investigator and the online survey was directly sent by the investigator.

Data entry and statistical analysis

After checking the data quality by the principal investigator, the data entry was done to MS. Excel 2019 for data cleaning and coding then exported to Statistical Package for Social Sciences (SPSS) Version 26 for analysis.

Discussion

In this study, we tried to show differences in perception between patients and physicians about insulin therapy. There is no significant difference between those taking insulin and those not on insulin therapy on the HgA1C level which shows treatment is not being intensified timely in study patients. Although more than half of physicians think preparing injection is difficult for patients but only 15.3% of patients agree with them and this is comparable to The GAAP study in which only 10.3% of patients reported difficulty. Only 21.6% of patients said it’s difficult to adjust the insulin dose; on the GAAP survey, 16.8% of them reported difficulty which will make it easier to recommend adjusting insulin dose according to their requirement. [30].

The majority (88.5%) of our physicians feel they have to choose between efficacy and safety when they treat diabetes with insulin and which is higher compared to Japanese physicians involved in the GAAP study which was 62%. This is probably due to the lesser availability of self-blood glucose monitoring in our setup. [32].

Most of our patients and physicians agree on a wish for possible insulin regimen that would cover if patients forget to take a dose or fits daily life which was 96.7% of physicians and 83.8% patients which are comparable to the GAAP study 92.5% and 91.2% for patient and physicians respectively. [30].

Compared to the patients on the GAAP study more patients in our setting said it is hard to live a normal life while managing diabetes 66.6% Vs 54.4%.[30] this could be 96.3% of them said it’s expensive for them to buy insulin. In the Kenyan study, 46.1% of patients feel insulin management will make life less flexible. [33].

19% of patients admitted they missed insulin doses sometimes which is lesser than the GAAP study participants which were 33.2%, and 62.3% of physicians think their typical patient may have missed insulin injections which is still lesser than the physicians in the GAAP study 72.5%. The top reason mentioned by patients for the omission of insulin dose is being too busy both on the GAAP and our patients. Traveling was the second most common reason reported by both patients and physicians in our setting as well as in the GAAP study. [30].

When we see our patients not yet started on insulin therapy compared to the Trinidad study more than half of the 56.4% Vs 38% of patients admitted know nothing about insulin and 22.7% Vs 34.1% know a little about insulin therapy [34] which shows a great knowledge gap in our patients compared to our physicians reported only 54.7% of them to mention about insulin therapy at the time of diagnosis of T2DM.

More than half of our patients not on insulin therapy want more communication about insulin therapy from their physicians and 43.6% of them reported their physicians have recommended them insulin therapy to better control their diabetes which is greater than the patients in Trinidad which were 15%. When our patients were asked if they would take insulin if recommended by their doctor, 68.2% of them said they will take it which is better than the Trinidad study which was 54.6%, and even if it’s not directly comparable 82.7% of Kenyan patients has Psychological resistance to insulin therapy. [33, 34].

The two top reasons for refusing insulin therapy by patients, not on insulin therapy is fear of insulin needles (79.6%) and possible pain during injection 46.1% of patients which is higher than Trinidad patients reported 54% and 56% respectively.[34] However, half of our insulin-treated patients reported they have never felt pain during injection.

There is no statistically significant difference between Internists, internal medicine residents, and GPs on the target HgA1C, delay to initiate insulin if HgA1C is off-target, intensification of therapy, and agreement on patients may fail to take insulin as prescribed. But there was a statically significant difference in the frequency of HgA1C follow-up in which 50% of internists and 46.7% of GPs want to follow HgA1C every 6 months while 43.2% of residents want to follow every 03–06 months which is in line with the ADA 2020 recommendation. [22] The other area which shows a significant discrepancy between physicians is the significant number of patients has off-target HgA1C where 33.3% of GPs tend not to agree with this opinion but 90% of our internists and 90.5% of our Residents agree on this idea which is in line with the Japanese patients on GAAP study. [32].

Most of our physicians mention insulin therapy at the time of diagnosis or during the intensification of oral therapy. Despite ADA 2020 guideline recommendation to start patients on insulin therapy for patients with evidence of catabolism only 50.8% of our physicians start these patients on insulin therapy, for those with HgA1C > 10% at diagnosis only 55.7% of our physicians start these patients on insulin therapy, for patients with difficult to differentiate between type 1 and type 2 DM 81.2% of our physicians start these patients on insulin therapy and for patients with HgA1C above target despite dual or triple oral therapy 90.9% of our physicians start these patients on insulin therapy. [22].

Limitation of the study

The study participant patients are from single-center which may not be representative of the whole diabetic population in our country. The number of physicians involved in the study is small and we need a survey involving more internists and General practitioners in the future. Construct validity of the questionnaires was not assessed in this study.

Conclusion

Most of our patients both in the insulin group and those not on insulin have poor glycemic control. Physicians tend to overestimate the difficulty of insulin injection preparation and dose adjustment fears the risk of hypoglycemia when starting insulin therapy. More than half of our patients not on insulin therapy know nothing about insulin therapy which we have to improve. Even if patients not on insulin therapy overestimate insulin needle phobia and injection pain those who are already on insulin disagree with them.

Recommendation

We need to educate our patients more about insulin therapy and possibly create a forum for patient-to-patient education on our diabetes follow up clinics.

We need a continuous medical education forum on updated guidelines and treatment recommendations possibly online with GPs and Internists working out of teaching centers.
