Efficient Dynamic Test Case Generation for Path-Based Coverage Criteria
Jakub Zelek, Jakub Ruszil, Adam Roman, Artur Polański

Abstract
We present a novel approach to test-case generation that satisfies four white-box, path-based coverage criteria: Prime Path, Simple Cycle, Simple Path, and Edge-Acyclic Path. Our method builds on a modified version of Johnson’s algorithm and enables test cases to be generated incrementally and on demand, rather than requiring the entire test suite to be computed upfront. This streaming capability represents a substantial advancement over existing approaches, as it allows testers to begin executing and refining tests immediately, thereby significantly improving the efficiency of test design. Our solution is inherently memory efficient, as it does not store all discovered coverage items; instead, it retains only the minimal set of paths required to generate subsequent coverage items on the fly. As a result, the approach scales to arbitrarily large graphs. In addition, the algorithm gives testers explicit control over the size of the generated test suite by allowing them to restrict the number of cycles permitted in a test path. The approach is grounded in new theoretical insights, most notably a novel characterization of prime paths in terms of the strongly connected components of control-flow graphs. We complement these theoretical contributions with a practical implementation and a comprehensive empirical evaluation. The results demonstrate that our method not only outperforms existing techniques in terms of execution time and memory consumption, but also provides testers with a more flexible and efficient tool for achieving high coverage while substantially reducing test design overhead.



I
Introduction

Path-based coverage criteria, such as Prime Path Coverage (PPC), play an important role in white-box testing for several compelling reasons. First, they are strong criteria in the sense that they subsume many other well-established and rigorous coverage criteria. For instance, PPC subsumes All-DU-Paths Coverage, Edge-Pair Coverage, and Complete Round-Trip Coverage
[
16
,
1
]
. As a result, satisfying PPC implicitly ensures satisfaction of these weaker criteria. Second, path-based criteria capture long dependency chains that are often missed by more localized coverage measures. Because prime paths are non-extendable, they tend to represent meaningful execution flows, including business-critical sequences and extended control dependencies. This makes PPC particularly effective at revealing faults associated with complex interactions and long-range program behavior. Third, criteria such as PPC strike a practical balance between thoroughness and feasibility. While exhaustive path testing is infeasible for most real-world programs due to the potentially infinite number of execution paths, PPC focuses on a finite yet critical subset of paths, thereby providing strong fault-detection capability without incurring prohibitive testing costs. Finally, prime paths can be viewed as the fundamental building blocks of program execution. Analogous to the Prime Factorization Theorem (which states that every natural number greater than one can be uniquely expressed as a product of prime numbers), every execution path in a program can be seen as a composition of prime paths. In this sense, prime paths form the essential elements from which all program control-flow behaviors are constructed.

Prime paths, intuitively, are simple cycles (cycles without repeating vertices but the first) or simple paths (paths with no repeating vertices) in a digraph that cannot be extended without losing the property of being simple (i.e., adding any vertex to the start or to the end would repeat some vertex in the path). Fazli
[
8
]
notes that most existing methods for generating prime/test paths have limited success in producing sets of all prime/test paths for structurally complex programs (e.g., those with high NPATH complexity). He proposes a time-efficient, vertex-based, compositional approach in which prime paths are calculated for the strongly connected components (SCCs) of the CFG, and then composed with prime paths for the CFG with vertices that represent the SCCs. In
[
9

, Fazli presents a novel scalable GPU-based method that outperforms existing methods for prime path and test path generation by several orders of magnitude, both in time and space efficiency.

Existing solutions calculate test paths only after prime paths are generated. In this paper, we characterize prime paths in terms of SCCs, dividing prime paths into simple cycles and acyclic paths. Based on this approach, we propose a solution that calculates simple cycles and simple paths, enabling the simultaneous, dynamic generation of test cases that satisfy four path-based coverage criteria: Prime Path Coverage, Simple Cycle Coverage, Simple Path Coverage, and edge-Acyclic Path Coverage. This allows for more efficient testing, as there is no need to wait for all test items to be generated, which can take a very long time for large CFGs. The proposed algorithms are parameterized to strike a balance between the length of test paths and the number of test cases.

Prime Paths Characterization

A classical algorithm for prime paths generation was proposed in
[
1

Next, it removes paths that are subpaths of other paths. The remaining part is the set of all prime paths. Apart from the fact that this algorithm is not very effective, the user must wait till the end to receive the whole set of prime paths.

V
Coverage Criteria

The above results enable us to develop further algorithms for generating test suites that meet the specified coverage criteria. The advantage of our solution is that individual test cases are returned immediately after they are found. This is particularly useful when the coverage criterion is demanding in terms of the number of objects to be covered, or when there are time constraints, because test coverage items can be generated independently of the test cases that exercise them. In this section, we show how to design test cases that satisfy four types of path-related test coverage criteria defined in Section
II
. All the algorithms introduced in this section have a similar structure – they are based on accumulating the test coverage items that are connected by the shortest path between the last and first coverage items yielded by the algorithm. If that is not possible, the algorithm finds the shortest path to the final vertex and yields a test case based on a parameter (
k
) that controls the maximum number of coverage items accumulated per test case. The implementation of the mentioned algorithms is available in our ANONYMIZED package.

This criterion requires test cases to cover all simple cycles in a graph. For some graphs, the number of simple cycles can be relatively large, e.g., when a graph is a complete digraph. The final experiment was designed to provide a more detailed evaluation of the memory (RAM) consumption of our algorithm. In approaches that store all intermediate or final results in memory, such as those proposed by Ammann&Offutt and Fazli et al., testers must ensure that sufficient memory is available throughout execution. Given that the number of prime paths can grow exponentially with the size of the control-flow graph, the required memory is not only difficult to predict but may also become prohibitively large in practice.

In contrast, our algorithm employs a streaming-based strategy that generates and processes results incrementally, thereby avoiding the need to retain large numbers of prime paths (or their parts) in memory. Consequently, RAM usage is not a limiting factor for our approach. In this experiment, the limits were set to 10,000,000 simple paths and 10,000,000 simple cycles, consistent with the previous experiment. VII
Conclusions

We presented a novel, streaming-based approach to dynamic test-case generation for path-based white-box coverage criteria, including Prime Path, Simple Cycle, Simple Path, and Edge-Acyclic Path coverage. Our method fundamentally departs from existing techniques that require exhaustive, upfront enumeration of all coverage items.

The key advantage of the proposed solution lies in its ability to generate coverage items incrementally and on demand. Unlike traditional algorithms, which store all intermediate and final paths in memory and therefore suffer from poor scalability, our approach produces test coverage items one at a time, enabling immediate execution and refinement of tests. This streaming capability makes memory consumption largely independent of the total number of prime paths, a critical improvement given that this number can grow exponentially even for moderately sized control-flow graphs.

Our empirical evaluation on real-world CFGs demonstrates that the proposed algorithm consistently outperforms state-of-the-art approaches in both execution time and memory usage. Notably, these improvements hold even when our method is applied to substantially larger graphs than those considered in prior work. The results confirm that the streaming mechanism is not only theoretically sound but also practically effective, making our approach well suited for large-scale and resource-constrained testing environments.

Several promising directions for future research emerge from this work. First, the streaming framework could be extended to support additional coverage criteria such as data-flow–based. Second, integrating the algorithm into industrial-grade testing tools and continuous integration pipelines would enable further validation of its practical impact in real development workflows. Third, future work could explore adaptive strategies that dynamically adjust path-generation limits based on observed fault detection effectiveness or resource constraints. Finally, parallel and distributed variants of the streaming approach may further improve performance on very large or highly complex control-flow graphs.

Overall, this work lays a strong foundation for scalable, flexible, and efficient test-case generation and opens new opportunities for advancing path-based software testing techniques.
