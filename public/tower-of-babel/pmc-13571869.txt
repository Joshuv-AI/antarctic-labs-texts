Mapping the Symptom Networks of Depression, Anxiety, and Their Comorbidity Among Chinese Individuals Under Community Correction
Authors: Yichao Lv, Ruyong Li, Zhe Hou, Xinze Liu, Xinyuan Zou, Minmin Cai, Xiangping Liu, Shanshan Chen, Yanqiang Tao
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Objective Individuals under community correction are a high-risk population for anxiety and depression, which frequently co-occur. However, the symptom network structures of anxiety, depression, and their comorbidity in this population remained unexplored. This study addresses this gap using a network analysis approach. Methods A total of 818 Chinese individuals under community correction (134 females, 684 males; over 95% aged 18–60 years) completed the Self-Rating Anxiety Scale (SAS) and Self-Rating Depression Scale (SDS). Separate symptom networks for anxiety and depression were estimated to identify central symptoms using expected influence (EI), while a comorbidity network was estimated to identify bridge symptoms using bridge EI and bridge betweenness. Directed acyclic graph (DAG) analyses were then conducted to identify potential trigger symptoms and further clarify the roles of central and bridge symptoms. Results A total of 36.7% and 13.1% of participants reported depression and anxiety symptoms, respectively, with 11.0% exhibiting comorbidity. In the anxiety network, “panic” (SAS-03) was the central symptom (highest EI) and the key trigger symptom in the DAG. In the depression network, “emptiness” (SDS-18) was the central symptom, while “fatigue” (SDS-10) was the key trigger. In the comorbidity network, “insomnia” (SAS-19), “sleep disturbance” (SDS-04), and “fatigue” were identified as bridge symptoms. In the comorbidity DAG, “fatigue” acted as a key trigger, facilitating the transmission from depression to anxiety via “sleep disturbance” and “insomnia.” Conclusion The study preliminarily examined the phenomenon and mechanisms of anxiety, depression, and their comorbidity among individuals under community correction, providing new insights for intervention planning.

INTRODUCTION

As China’s community correction system has become increasingly institutionalized, the population under communieISSN 1976-3026 OPEN ACCESS ty correction has steadily increased [1]. Community correction refers to non-custodial sanctions for offenders sentenced to control, probation, parole, or temporary service of sentences outside prison [2]. Individuals under community correction are supervised and reformed as “offenders” while simultaneously living as “members” of the community. This dual status is associated with identity disturbance, social exclusion and discrimination, low social support, impaired social adaptation, and an elevated risk of recidivism [3-5]. Collectively, these challenges render this population particularly vulnerable to psychiatric distress, especially depression and anxiety [6-8]. Epidemiological studies among Chinese individuals under community correction estimate anxiety prevalence at approximately 18% and depression prevalence ranging from 18% to 72% [6,9]. Notably, these conditions are strongly correlated, suggesting a substantial tendency toward comorbidity [6].

However, most extant research has focused on categoricallevel manifestations, neglecting the underlying dynamics of symptom interactions [6,9]. Network psychopathology offers a robust framework to address this gap. From this perspective, mental disorders (e.g., depression) are conceptualized as systems of mutually interacting symptoms (e.g., depressed mood and guilt), rather than as the expression of a single latent cause [10]. Accordingly, comorbidity between depression and anxiety may arise from direct interactions among their constituent symptoms [11,12]. To date, few studies have applied this framework to examine symptom-level architectures in individuals under community correction. To address this gap, the present study employs network analysis (NA) and Bayesian NA to identify interaction patterns, detect central symptoms, and elucidate bridge mechanisms underlying comorbidity in this population.

Within the context of community correction, various psychosocial stressors may jointly drive anxiety and depression, which frequently co-occur. Prior criminal or unlawful behavior may first evoke guilt, shame, emptiness, and reduced selfacceptance— core features of depression—while also heightening vigilance and worry about social evaluation [13,14]. These internal conflicts may be further exacerbated by restricted access to key resources (e.g., housing, employment, and mobility), increasing uncertainty and helplessness [15,16]. Moreover, perceived stigma and discrimination may reinforce an internalized “offender” identity, leading to reduced self-esteem [17,18]. Stigma may also promote maladaptive emotion regulation strategies. It can heighten hypervigilance toward the social environment—sometimes manifesting as defensiveness or hostility—while simultaneously promoting emotional suppression and social withdrawal [4,19]. In interactions with family members or correctional staff, individuals may remain skeptical of supportive intentions and suppress the outward expression of distress [15,20,21].

Driven by these shared stressors, anxiety and depression may co-occur through tightly interconnected symptom processes. Persistent worry may amplify negative affect, such as sadness and hopelessness, while low mood may, in turn, intensify rumination and future-oriented concern [22]. These bidirectional interactions are often sustained by shared or closely related symptoms that act as bridges across domains [11]. For instance, insomnia, a common somatic symptom of both anxiety and depression, may function as a “biological bridge” linking these conditions [23]. Sleep disturbance can diminish the energy required for daily functioning, thereby deepening a sense of emptiness and amplifying future-oriented worry [24]. It may also impair emotion regulation capacity, further exacerbating emotional irritability, low mood, and feelings of helplessness, and even increasing suicide risk [25-27]. Ultimately, such cross-disorder spillover creates a vicious cycle that solidifies comorbidity, making psychological distress increasingly difficult to disentangle within the unique constraints of the community correction environment. Therefore, it is imperative to clarify symptom-level interactions of anxiety and depression to inform more effective psychological support.

The network psychopathology model provides a promising framework for examining symptom-level interactions and elucidating the nature of comorbidity [12]. NA and directed acyclic graph (DAG) analysis are two complementary approaches [10,28]. NA conceptualizes mental disorders (e.g., depression) as systems of interacting symptoms, in which nodes represent symptoms (e.g., depressed mood and guilt) and edges represent their associations. Importantly, this approach utilizes centrality indices to identify central symptoms that exert the greatest influence within a disorder, and bridge centrality to identify bridge symptoms that link different disorders. Identifying these symptoms helps characterize symptom-level interactions within and across disorders, uncover mechanisms of comorbidity, and inform targeted interventions [10]. However, cross-sectional networks primarily capture associations and cannot determine how symptoms influence or trigger one another. Bayesian DAG analysis complements NA by exploring directed relationships, thereby distinguishing potential triggers from downstream outcomes [28,29]. Prior research has shown that central or bridge symptoms in NAs may also act as triggers in DAG networks [29,30]. Together, NA and DAG are used in this study to examine both undirected and directed symptom relationships and to identify the distinct roles of symptoms.

Previous research has applied NA and DAG analyses to examine symptom networks of depression and anxiety across diverse populations, yet evidence in Chinese individuals under community correction remains scarce [31-35]. For depression, symptoms such as “depressed mood,” “fatigue,” and “emptiness” are commonly identified as central in NA, while “depressed mood” and “fatigue” often emerge as triggers in DAG structures [31,36-38]. In contrast, studies focusing on anxiety as a single disorder are relatively limited, with most examining its comorbidity with depression. Nevertheless, available evidence indicates that “uncontrollable worry” is a central anxiety symptom and often serves as a key trigger [39]. In comorbidity networks, symptoms such as “crying spells,” “fatigue/easy fatiguability,” “sleep disturbance,” and “tachycardia/palpitation” have consistently emerged as bridge symptoms, with “depressed mood” and “fear” identified as key triggers [31-35]. However, whether these patterns generalize to Chinese individuals under community correction remains unclear, given the unique psychosocial context of this population. The present study therefore applies NA and DAG approaches to examine the symptom networks of depression, anxiety, and their comorbidity in this group.

The current study

The present study aims to apply NA and DAG analysis to map the networks of anxiety and depression among Chinese individuals under community correction. Specifically, we sought to 1) estimate the prevalence of anxiety, depression, and their comorbidity; 2) construct separate symptom networks for anxiety and depression to examine symptom interactions and identify central symptoms; 3) build a comorbidity network to detect bridge symptoms and key edges linking the two disorders; and 4) develop DAGs for anxiety, depression, and their comorbidity to explore potential triggering roles and directional pathways within the networks.

METHODS

Participants

This study was approved by the Institutional Review Board of Shanxi Normal University. According to the institutional ethics review system, approval numbers are not assigned to research studies; therefore, no reference number is available. With the assistance of community staff, the survey was administered to individuals under community correction during routine follow-up visits. All participants provided informed consent prior to participation. Before data collection, we estimated the required sample size using a common rule of thumb in multivariate analysis, which recommends at least 10 participants per variable [40]. As the Self-Rating Anxiety Scale (SAS) and Self-Rating Depression Scale (SDS) included 40 items in total, we aimed for a minimum of 400 participants. A total of 862 individuals under community correction from Linfen, China, voluntarily participated in this study. Data were collected via Wenjuanxing, a widely used online survey platform (https://www.wjx.cn). In this study, 44 participants were excluded for not providing informed consent or not completing the required surveys. A total of 818 participants (134 females, 16.4%) were included in the final analysis. Detailed demographic characteristics (age, marriage, education, and annual income) are presented in Table 1. Additionally, a post hoc statistical power test was conducted using the powerly package (version 1.8.6) based on our current sample [41].

Procedures and measures

Prior to participation, all participants were informed about the purpose and procedures of the study, assured of their right to withdraw at any time, and provided written informed consent. Later, participants completed the SAS and SDS.

The SAS and SDS were used to measure anxiety and depression over the past week, respectively [42,43]. Both scales consist of 20 items, each rated on a 4-point Likert scale (1=a little of the time, 4=most or all of the time). The scores of the twenty items are summed to obtain a raw score, ranging from 20 to 80. This raw score is then multiplied by 1.25 and rounded to obtain the standard score, ranging from 25 to 100. The higher the standard score, the more severe the anxiety or depression. The standard scores of the SAS and SDS are classified as follows: less than 50, no anxiety or depression; 50–59, mild anxiety or depression; 60–69, moderate anxiety or depression; above 70, severe anxiety or depression [42-44]. In this study, the Cronbach’s α values for the SAS and SDS were 0.81 and 0.89, respectively.

Statistical analysis

All analyses were conducted in R 4.3.2 [45]. First, descriptive statistics were calculated for the overall sample and demographic subgroups, and analyses of variance or independent samples t-tests were used to examine group differences. Prevalence estimates were computed to assess the distribution of anxiety, depression, and their comorbidity. In addition, we compared our sample with recent normative SAS (36.92±7.33) and SDS (40.50±11.31) scores from Chinese adults to evaluate symptom severity [46].

Second, symptom network analyses were conducted. Using the R packages bootnet (1.4.3) and qgraph (1.6.9), we estimated and visualized separate anxiety and depression networks, as well as a comorbidity network [47,48]. Networks were estimated using the estimateNetwork function with the Extended Bayesian Information Criterion graphical least absolute shrinkage and selection operator. To account for potential demographic confounding, three additional networks controlling for demographic variables were estimated. Node centrality was assessed using expected influence (EI) for the separate anxiety and depression networks via the centralityPlot function in qgraph (1.6.9) [49]. Nodes with the highest Z-scored EI values were identified as central symptoms [50]. For the comorbidity network, bridge EI and bridge betweenness were estimated using the bridge function in the networktools package (1.1) to identify bridge symptoms [51]. Nodes with both Z-scored bridge EI and bridge betweenness exceeding 1 were considered bridge symptoms. Edge-weight accuracy and centrality stability were evaluated through a series of bootstrap analyses (1,000 samples) using bootnet (1.4.3) [48].

Third, Bayesian networks were estimated using a hill-climbing algorithm implemented in the bnlearn package (4.9.3), with 10,000 bootstrap samples, to identify potential directional relationships among symptoms, including triggers and outcomes [28]. The DAG structure was determined by optimizing the Bayesian Information Criterion (BIC), a goodness-of-fit criterion. In the visualized DAG, nodes positioned at the top and bottom were considered triggers and outcomes, respectively. Arrow thickness reflects the change in BIC (ΔBIC) when an arrow is removed, with larger absolute values indicating greater importance for model fit. Negative and positive ΔBIC values indicate decreases and increases in model fit following edge removal, respectively. In other words, negative ΔBIC values suggest that the presence of an arrow improves model fit [28]. In addition, the directional probabilities (Dir. Prob.) of each arrow were computed.

Finally, post hoc power analyses were conducted using the powerly package (1.8.6) to examine whether the sample size provided sufficient power to recover the true network structure [41]. Detailed analytical procedures are provided in the Supplementary Materials.

RESULTS

Descriptive statistics, comparisons with norm, and prevalence

Table 1 displayed the descriptive statistics of SAS and SDS scores for the overall sample and compares them with the Chinese normative data. Individuals under community correction in China exhibited significantly higher SAS and SDS scores than the normative sample (p<0.001) (Table 1). As shown in Figure 1, 61.2% of participants reported no anxiety or depression. Among the remaining 38.8% who exhibited symptoms, the prevalence of at least mild depression and anxiety was 36.7% (25.7%+11.0%) and 13.1% (2.1%+11.0%), respectively, with 11.0% experiencing both (i.e., comorbidity). Notably, a high overlap was observed between the two syndromes: 30% of individuals with depressive symptoms also had anxiety symptoms, whereas 84% of those with anxiety symptoms also reported depressive symptoms. Table 1 also presents the differences in SAS and SDS scores across demographic groups (i.e., sex, age, education, employment, and income), while Table 2 presents descriptive statistics for each symptom.

Separate networks of anxiety and depression

In the anxiety network (Figure 2A and Supplementary Figure 1A), the strongest edge was between “anxiousness” (SAS-01) and “panic” (SAS-03; r=0.313), followed by “dyspnea” (SAS-13) and “sweating” (SAS-17; r=0.299) (Supplementary Table 1 for full edge weights). In the depression network (Figure 2C and Supplementary Figure 1B), the strongest edge was between “hopelessness” (SDS-14) and “emptiness” (SDS-18; r=0.339), followed by “depressed affect” (SDS-01) and “sleep disturbance” (SDS-04; r=0.273) (Supplementary Table 2 for full edge weights). Regarding centrality (Table 2, Figure 2B and D), “panic” (SAS-03) and “emptiness” (SDS-18) had the highest EI centrality and were identified as the most central symptoms in the anxiety and depression networks, respectively.

Given the observed differences in SAS and SDS scores across demographic groups (Table 1), we additionally estimated networks controlling for these variables (see edge-weight matrices in Supplementary Tables 3 and 4). Mantel’s test further suggested that the original networks were highly correlated with those adjusted for demographics (anxiety: r=0.637, p=0.001; depression: r=0.719, p=0.001), suggesting minimal in-fluence of demographic factors on the network structures.

Comorbidity network of anxiety and depression

In the comorbidity network (Figure 2E, Supplementary Figure 1C, and Supplementary Table 5), the strongest edges between anxiety and depression were observed between “palpitation” (SAS-10) and “tachycardia” (SDS-09; r=0.258), as well as between “easy fatiguability, weakness” (SAS-08) and “fatigue” (SDS-10; r=0.190). Three symptoms were identified as the strongest bridge symptoms, as they demonstrated high values in both bridge EI and bridge betweenness (all >1): “insomnia” (SAS-19; bridge EI=3.17, bridge betweenness=3.11), “sleep disturbance” (SDS-04; bridge EI=1.40, bridge betweenness=2.30), and “fatigue” (SDS-10; bridge EI=1.39, bridge betweenness=2.34). In contrast, several other symptoms showed elevated values in only one of these indices (Table 2).

These bridge symptoms connected anxiety and depression through strong edges and the shortest paths in the network (Supplementary Table 6). “Insomnia” (SAS-19) acted as the strongest bridge, linking depression symptoms such as “sleep disturbance” (SDS-04) and “diurnal variation” (SDS-02) with anxiety symptoms such as “dyspnea” (SAS-13) and “sweating” (SAS-17). In turn, “sleep disturbance” (SDS-04) connected SAS-19 with other depression symptoms such as “depressed affect” (SDS-01) and “fatigue” (SDS-10), and further linked “fatigue” (SDS-10) to “nightmares” (SAS-20). Moreover, “fatigue” (SDS-10) acted as a bridge between “easy fatiguability, weakness” (SAS-08) with other depression symptoms including “psychomotor agitation” (SDS-13), “sleep disturbance” (SDS-04), and “tachycardia” (SDS-09). Additionally, the network structure remained nearly unchanged after controlling for demographic variables (r=0.996, p=0.001; see edge-weight matrix in Supplementary Table 7), suggesting minimal influence of these factors.

Network accuracy and stability

For separate anxiety and depression networks, as well as the comorbidity network, bootstrap analyses showed that the 95% confidence intervals (CIs) for edge weights were sufficiently narrow (Supplementary Figure 2), indicating high estimation accuracies. Furthermore, case-dropping bootstrap analyses (Supplementary Figure 3) revealed high CS-C values for EI in the anxiety (0.75) and depression (0.75) networks, as well as for bridge EI (0.67) and bridge betweenness (0.28) in the comorbidity network. These results indicate that the centrality metrics across all networks remained stable even when a substantial proportion of the sample was removed. Additionally, bootstrapped difference tests for edge weights (Supplementary Figure 4) and centrality (Supplementary Figure 5) indicated that most comparisons of edge weights, EIs, bridge EIs, and bridge betweenness of nodes were statistically significant, indicating good discriminability of the estimated edges and centrality indices across the three networks.

Separate DAGs of anxiety and depression

Figure 3 shows the DAGs for anxiety and depression, and Supplementary Tables 8 and 9 show the detailed BIC and Dir. Prob values in DAGs. In the anxiety DAG (Figure 3A), “panic” (SAS-03) emerged as the primary parent node. It could trigger other psychological symptoms including “anxiousness” (SAS-01) and “fear” (SAS-02), as well as physical symptoms including “easy fatiguability, weakness” (SAS-08), “palpitation” (SAS-10), “dizziness” (SAS-11), and “nightmares” (SAS-20). The strongest arrow was from “panic” (SAS-03) to “anxiousness” (SAS-01). Subordinate parent nodes included “body aches & pains” (SAS-07) and “dyspnea” (SAS-13), whereas “nausea & vomiting” (SAS-15) and “urinary frequency” (SAS-16) appeared as downstream symptoms.

In the depression DAG (Figure 3B and Supplementary Table 9), “fatigue” (SDS-10) emerged as a parent symptom of eight other symptoms, most notably “depressed affect” (SDS-01). The central symptom “emptiness” (SDS-18) was also notable, as it strongly triggered several symptoms located in the half bottom of DAG, especially “hopelessness” (SDS-14). In addition, “emptiness” triggered symptoms such as “dissatisfaction” (SDS-20), “diurnal variation” (SDS-02), and “personal devaluation” (SDS-17). Finally, “diurnal variation” (SDS-02) and “indecisiveness” (SDS-16) appeared as downstream symptoms.

Comorbidity DAGs of anxiety and depression

In the anxiety-depression comorbidity DAG (Figure 4), anxiety and depression symptoms first clustered by disorder but later showed considerable overlap. Supplementary Tables 10 and 11 present the detailed values of ΔBIC and Dir. Prob of each arrow. Here, we focused on the bridge symptoms, i.e., “fatigue” (SDS-10), “sleep disturbance” (SDS-04), and “insomnia” (SAS-19), which were located at the top, middle, and bottom of the DAG, respectively.

In the upper part of the DAG, “fatigue” (SDS-10) functioned as an initial trigger, facilitating the spread from depression to anxiety by activating individual depressive and anxiety symptoms. Specifically, “fatigue” first triggered eight depression symptoms, particularly “depressed affect” (SDS-01), “crying spells” (SDS-03), “tachycardia” (SDS-09), and “sleep disturbance” (SDS-04). In turn, “depressed affect” activated upstream anxiety symptoms such as “panic” (SAS-03), “anxiousness” (SAS-01), and “nightmares” (SAS-20), whereas “tachycardia” activated “palpitation” (SAS-10). Moreover, “fatigue” also directly triggered three anxiety symptoms, particularly “easy fatiguability, weakness” (SAS-08), which further propagated activation to “palpitation” (SAS-10).

Next, “sleep disturbance” (SDS-04)—triggered by “fatigue” (SDS-10), “depressed affect” (SDS-01), and “panic” (SAS-03)— further activated anxiety symptoms such as “nightmares” (SAS-20) and “insomnia” (SAS-19). “Insomnia” was also strongly triggered by “emptiness” (SDS-18), and then triggered “diurnal variation” (SDS-02), a downstream symptom in the network. In sum, the pathway from “fatigue” (SDS-10) to “sleep disturbance” (SDS-04), then to “insomnia” (SAS-19), and finally to “diurnal variation” (SDS-02) highlighted a potential activation route across the symptom network.

Post hoc power analysis

The post hoc power analysis (power=0.8) indicated that the required sample sizes to reliably recover the structures of the anxiety, depression, and comorbidity networks were 829 participants (95% CI=[784, 833]), 515 participants (95% CI=[482, 526]), and 663 participants (95% CI=[653, 683]), respectively, ensuring an 80% probability of accurately reconstructing these network structures. Overall, the current sample size of 818 participants was basically enough to complete the network estimations.

DISCUSSION

Individuals under community correction have been recognized as a vulnerable population in terms of mental health outcomes. In the present study, 36.7% and 13.1% of participants reported depression and anxiety symptoms, respective-ly, with 11.0% exhibiting comorbidity. This pattern of a relatively higher burden of depressive symptoms is similar to that reported in previous studies [6]. Furthermore, by applying NA and DAG analyses, this study is the first to examine the network structures of anxiety, depression, and their comorbidity in this population. These findings provide new insights into symptom-level mechanisms and warrant further discussion.

Panic as a central and triggering symptom of anxiety

“Panic” (SAS-03) emerged as both a central and a triggering symptom in the anxiety network. It exhibited the strongest connections with other symptoms and occupied a key upstream position. Specifically, panic activated both psychological symptoms such as “anxiousness” (SAS-01) and “fear” (SAS-02), and somatic symptoms, including “easy fatiguability, weakness” (SAS-08), “palpitation” (SAS-10), “dizziness” (SAS-11), and “nightmares” (SAS-20). In short, panic appears to promote the development and amplification of anxiety symptoms [52]. Although direct evidence in individuals under community correction remains limited, our findings parallel those observed in other high-risk populations. “Panic” has also been identified as a central symptom among teachers and clinically diagnosed patients with comorbid anxiety and depression [32-34]. Moreover, although panic was not the initial trigger among teachers, it still activated symptoms such as “anxiousness,” “fear,” and “easy fatiguability, weakness,” showing a pattern consistent with the present study [32]. The similarity in symptom networks suggests that individuals under community correction may share comparable psychological burdens with other high-risk groups. In this population, prior criminal involvement may increase vulnerability to trauma and panicb [7,53]. In addition, the possibility of recidivism may further exacerbate these symptoms, as violations of supervision conditions can result in the revocation of community correction status and re-incarceration [54]. If unaddressed, such acute distress may precipitate chronic panic disorder, which has been found to affect up to 20% of correctional samples [7]. Accordingly, we suggest integrating evidence-based interventions, such as graded exposure therapy or panic control treatment, into community mental health programs to reduce panic sensitivity and prevent further deterioration [55,56].

Fatigue as a trigger and emptiness as a central symptom of depression

“Fatigue” (SDS-10) emerged as a trigger symptom within the depression network. As a somatic symptom, fatigue often manifests as reduced activity, low energy, and sleep disturbances [52]. Importantly, it may also impair attention and contribute to negative mood states (e.g., apathy, reduced interest, and low mood) [57]. This may explain why “fatigue” strongly triggered “depressed affect” (SDS-01) in our network. In contrast, prior studies in teachers found that “depressed affect” tends to trigger “fatigue [32].” This pattern suggests that somatic fatigue may be more prominent than depressed mood in community correction populations. For this population, limited employment opportunities often lead to greater engagement in manual labor, which may contribute to physical fatigue [54,58]. More importantly, fatigue often persists as a residual symptom even after depression treatment [59]. Early screening and targeted interventions are therefore crucial. Interventions such as physical exercise, cognitive behavioral therapy (CBT), and mindfulness meditation have shown promise in alleviating fatigue [60-62].

“Emptiness” (SDS-18) was identified as the central symptom in the depression network. Despite not being the initial driver, it played a pivotal role in cascading distress throughout the network. Specifically, emptiness strongly triggered “hopelessness” (SDS-14), “dissatisfaction” (SDS-20), and “diurnal variation” (SDS-02). This central role of “emptiness” and its downstream effects align with previous findings in both clinically diagnosed patients and teachers [32,37]. This consistency further indicates that individuals under community correction may experience comparable psychological distress. Feelings of emptiness may manifest as loneliness, boredom, hopelessness, and impaired daily functioning [16,63]. These findings likely reflect the systemic and socioeconomic marginalization of this population, characterized by restricted rights, financial instability, and diminished social or family support [14,17,54]. In response, these individuals may resort to maladaptive coping strategies, such as smoking or alcohol use and, in more severe cases, may be at increased risk of non-suicidal self-injury and suicidality [63-65]. To address these issues, incorporating meaningful and manageable daily activities into routine community correction programs may help alleviate feelings of emptiness and enhance emotional vitality [66,67].

Bridge symptoms and directed pathways underlying anxiety-depression comorbidity

In the comorbidity network, “insomnia” (SAS-19), “sleep disturbance” (SDS-04), and “fatigue” (SDS-10) were identified as bridge symptoms. These symptoms showed strong crossdisorder edges (bridge EI) and acted as key intermediaries in the shortest cross-disorder paths (bridge betweenness). Findings based on bridge EI align with previous research across diverse populations, including college students during the COVID-19 pandemic and clinically diagnosed patients [33,35]. This study further confirms their structural bridging roles in key pathways underlying anxiety-depression comorbidity. More crucially, their consistent emergence across populations suggests that sleep- and fatigue-related symptoms serve as universal conduits linking anxiety and depression, and may be foundational to emotion regulation processes [23]. For individuals under community correction, the dual burden of social stigma and restricted autonomy may contribute to sustained autonomic nervous system hyperarousal, resulting in persistent disruptions of the sleep–wake cycle [4,19,58]. Within this context, other anxiety and depression symptoms may become interconnected through these “biological bridges,” thereby forming a disorganized comorbidity cascade [23,24].

The DAG analysis further revealed directed pathways underlying anxiety-depression comorbidity and clarified the role of these bridge symptoms in symptom transmission within the network. “Fatigue” (SDS-10) acted as the global trigger of the comorbidity DAG, initiating cross-cluster propagation from depression to anxiety. Specifically, “fatigue” may serve as an upstream driver that first increases “depressed affect” (SDS-01), which subsequently facilitates the emergence of anxiety symptoms such as “panic” (SAS-03), “anxiousness” (SAS-01), and “nightmares” (SAS-20). Although prior research has not consistently positioned “fatigue” at the top of this cascade, the predictive links from “depressed affect” to “panic” and “anxiousness” are well supported [32]. These findings reinforce the tight coupling of core emotional symptoms—such as persistent sadness, worry, fear, and panic—which also persist under the unique stressors of the community correction context. Once activated, these symptoms not only reinforce intra-disorder dynamics but also facilitate cross-disorder propagation, thereby sustaining comorbidity [22].

Furthermore, “fatigue” exhibits somatic pathways linking to the anxiety symptom cluster. Specifically, it predicts its anxiety counterpart “easy fatiguability,” which in turn predicts “palpitation” (SDS-10 → SAS-08 → SAS-10). In parallel, “fatigue” activates “tachycardia,” which subsequently activates “palpitation” (SDS-10 → SDS-09 → SAS-10). These pathways suggest that biologically similar symptoms act as key links, allowing distress to spread between depression and anxiety. Importantly, our findings highlight fatigue as a critical driver of palpitations, which aligns with previous studies [32,68,69]. Since persistent palpitations are known to increase the risk of adverse cardiovascular outcomes, this is particularly concerning for the community correction population [70]. Notably, about onefifth of individuals in this group reported prior diagnoses of cardiovascular conditions or hypertension, both of which are closely associated with tachycardia [64,71]. Routine cardiovascular screening and timely interventions targeting fatigue may therefore help reduce underlying ANS dysregulation.

“Sleep disturbance” (SDS-04) and “insomnia” (SAS-19) emerged as another set of key bridge symptoms, positioned at the midstream and downstream levels in the network, respectively. “Sleep disturbance” (SDS-04) was triggered by “fatigue” (SDS-10), both directly and indirectly via “depressed affect” (SDS-01). In turn, it activated downstream sleep-related anxiety symptoms, particularly “nightmares” (SAS-20) and “insomnia” (SAS-19). Through “insomnia” (SAS-19), “sleep disturbance” (SDS-04) further activated “diurnal variation” (SDS-02), the only downstream symptom in the network. This pathway mirrors those identified in high-stress occupational groups, such as teachers (“fatigue” → “sleep disturbance” → “insomnia/nightmares” → “diurnal variation”) and firefighters (“fatigue” → “sleep disturbance”; “insomnia” → “diurnal variation”) [31,32]. Among individuals under community correction, chronic fatigue may stem from life pressures and ongoing challenges, contributing to persistent sleep problems and impaired daytime functioning [64,72]. This cascade may accelerate the cooccurrence of anxiety and depression, thereby increasing treatment complexity [23]. Therefore, effective sleep interventions are essential for this population. Cognitive behavioral therapy for insomnia (CBT-I) has shown strong efficacy across diverse populations [73]. In addition, community public health organizations could offer sleep hygiene workshops to provide structured education on healthy sleep habits and practical strategies such as relaxation training and mindfulness exercises.

Implications

This study investigated the symptom network structures of anxiety, depression, and their comorbidity among Chinese individuals under community correction. Our findings elucidate the symptom-level mechanisms underlying these disorders and offer evidence-based insights for targeted interventions. Targeting the identified central and bridge symptoms may help disrupt maladaptive symptom cascades and alleviate psychological distress [74]. Although the sample was recruited from Linfen, a fourth-tier city, its characteristics likely reflect regions with comparable socioeconomic and judicial contexts. Consequently, these findings may be partially generalizable to similar settings across China, where they may inform psychological interventions and policy development. Specifically, incorporating mental health screening and tailored interventions into the community correction system may enhance prevention and care. Moreover, addressing broader social determinants may be necessary to improve long-term outcomes. By providing vocational empowerment and safeguarding legal rights, policymakers can reduce the stigma associated with community correction, ultimately lowering recidivism and supporting the holistic reintegration of these individuals [75].

Limitations

Some limitations of this study should be noted. First, the cross-sectional design hindered causal inference between symptoms; thus, findings—especially those from DAG analyses— should be interpreted with caution. Future research should employ cross-lagged panel network analysis (CLPN) to track the comorbid dynamics of anxiety and depression over time [74]. Second, although our sample is somewhat representative of underdeveloped regions in China, broader multicenter studies across diverse regions are needed to enhance generalizability. Larger samples would also allow examination of network differences across demographic subgroups (e.g., age, sex, employment status), which was not possible in the current study, where demographic variables were only included as covariates [76,77]. Third, due to privacy concerns and potential impacts on participation, this study did not collect offense-related demographic information (e.g., number and length of imprisonment, or offense type). Beyond this limitation, personality traits (e.g., the Big Five and dark triad traits) and emotion regulation strategies (e.g., cognitive reappraisal and expressive suppression) can influence network estimation of anxiety and depression networks [78-80]. Future studies may consider including these variables to better capture individual differences in mental health networks among individuals under community correction [78,79,81]. Fourth, although the SAS and SDS cover a range of symptoms, they may have limitations in precision and efficiency compared with other commonly used instruments. Measures such as the Hospital Anxiety and Depression Scale (HADS) and the Beck Anxiety Inventory (BAI) may provide more efficient and precise assessments [82,83]. Future research may consider using these instruments to refine mental health assessment in this population.

Conclusions

Focusing on Chinese individuals under community correction, this study revealed a substantial burden of depression (36.7%) and anxiety (13.1%) symptoms, with a notable comorbidity rate (11.0%). NA identified distinct central and bridging symptoms across disorders, while the DAG further clarified their functional roles within directed pathways. “Panic” (SAS-03) emerged as the most central symptom and a key trigger in the anxiety network. In the depression network, “emptiness” (SDS-18) functioned as the central symptom and “fatigue” (SDS-10) as the key triggering symptom, respectively. Importantly, “insomnia” (SAS-19), “sleep disturbance” (SDS-04), and “fatigue” served as key bridge symptoms linking depression and anxiety. The comorbidity DAG further suggested that “fatigue” functioned as a primary upstream trigger, facilitating symptom transmission from depression to anxiety through sleep-related pathways. Collectively, these findings elucidate the symptom-level mechanisms underlying anxiety, depression, and their comorbidity in this population, highlighting key symptoms as potential targets for intervention to improve mental health outcomes.
