Strong coupling structure of 𝒩 = 4 \mathcal{N}=4 SYM observables with matrix Bessel kernel
Bercel Boldis a,b

Abstract
In this paper I continue the program of studying the strong coupling expansion of certain observables in 𝒩 = 4 \mathcal{N}=4 supersymmetric Yang–Mills theory, which are given by a determinant with a matrix Bessel kernel. I show that, by reorganizing the transseries of the determinant at large values of the ’t Hooft coupling, a simple underlying structure emerges, in which each exponentially suppressed correction is related to the perturbative series in a simple way. This new approach provides an efficient method to generate the full transseries for 𝒩 = 4 \mathcal{N}=4 SYM observables, such as the cusp anomalous dimension, multi-gluon scattering amplitudes, and the octagon form factor. Using high-precision numerical analysis, I verify the results and provide a complete description of the resurgence structure of the strong coupling expansion.

Introduction and summary

Computing observables in four-dimensional superconformal Yang–Mills theories at finite ’t Hooft coupling
\lambda=g_{\mathrm{YM}}^{2}N
is central to the study of gauge/gravity duality. Within the AdS/CFT correspondence
1
, observables in the planar limit of the gauge theory admit different descriptions depending on the value of
\lambda
. At weak coupling, they can be computed directly in the gauge theory using standard perturbative techniques. In this case, the expansion in the coupling constant is well controlled, and calculations can be systematically organized in terms of Feynman diagrams. However, for larger values of
\lambda
, the series typically become divergent and the weak coupling expansion loses its validity.

In contrast, at strong ’t Hooft coupling, the same observables are described by a dual string theory. In this regime, the leading contribution is typically captured by a classical or semiclassical string configuration, whereas subleading terms arise from quantum fluctuations of the string worldsheet. Determining these corrections from direct string theory calculations is extremely difficult; thus, obtaining a finite
\lambda
answer for a given observable from the strong coupling expansion is a challenging task.

Since accessing observables at finite ’t Hooft coupling lies beyond the reach of both perturbative gauge theory and semiclassical string theory, therefore, a complete description of observables requires alternative methods, such as those based on integrability or localization
1
;
28
;
48

In this paper, I investigate a special class of these observables. In the planar limit, at arbitrary values of the coupling constant, they admit the representation as a determinant:

\displaystyle Z_{\ell}(g)=\det\Big(\delta_{nm}+\mathbb{K}_{nm}(\alpha)\Big)\Big|_{1+\ell\leq n,m<\infty}\,.

(1)

Summary and conclusions

The paper is organized as follows: In Section 2, I summarize the results obtained in
9
for the strong coupling expansion (
8

related to the moments of
\partial_{x}\log\chi_{\alpha}(x)

In the first part of Section 3, I rearrange the transseries and show that the strong coupling expansion in (

\displaystyle Z_{\ell}(g)=A(g)

\displaystyle\sum_{\delta^{+},\delta^{-}}(8\pi g)^{-\Delta(\Delta-2a)}e^{-8\pi g\left(\sum_{l\in\delta^{+}}x_{l}^{+}+\sum_{j\in\delta^{-}}x_{j}^{-}\right)}e^{i\pi a\Delta}S^{(\delta^{+},\delta^{-})}\mathcal{D}^{(\delta^{+},\delta^{-})}(g)\,,

\delta^{-}

. For a fixed pair of

In Section 3.1, I show that it is convenient to introduce the notation for the perturbative

\mathcal{I}_{n}
as:

\mathcal{I}_{2}\to\mathcal{I}_{2}+\frac{2}{\left(\frac{1}{2}+a\right)}\,,\qquad\mathcal{I}_{3}\to\mathcal{I}_{3}+\frac{2}{\left(\frac{1}{2}+a\right)^{2}}\,,\qquad\mathcal{I}_{4}\to\mathcal{I}_{4}+\frac{2}{\left(\frac{1}{2}+a\right)^{3}}\,,\qquad\dots\,,

(28)

or:

\mathcal{I}_{n}\to\mathcal{I}_{n}+\frac{2}{\left(\frac{1}{2}+a\right)^{n-1}}\,.

(29)

The series again starts with a
\mathcal{O}(1/g)
term.

(1,1)
correction

The next subleading contribution appears at order
\Lambda_{-}^{2}\Lambda_{+}^{2}
. At this order we find the
1/g
expansion:

\mathcal{Z}^{(1,1)}(g)=P^{(1,1)}\left[1+(a^{2}-\ell^{2})\sum_{k\geq 1}\frac{(-1)^{k}}{k!}\frac{f_{k}^{(1,1)}}{(8\pi g)^{k}}\right]\,.

(30)

The functions
f^{(1,1)}_{n}
are similar to (
2
):

\displaystyle f^{(1,1)}_{1}

\displaystyle=\mathcal{I}_{2}+2(1/2-a)^{-1}+2(1/2+a)^{-1}\,,

\displaystyle f^{(1,1)}_{2}

\displaystyle=(a^{2}-\ell^{2}+1)\left(\mathcal{I}_{2}+2(1/2-a)^{-1}+2(1/2+a)^{-1}\right)^{2}+

\displaystyle+2a\left(\mathcal{I}_{3}-2(1/2-a)^{-2}+2(1/2+a)^{-2}\right)\,,

\displaystyle f^{(1,1)}_{3}

\displaystyle=\left(a^{2}-\ell^{2}+1\right)\left(a^{2}-\ell^{2}+2\right)\left(\mathcal{I}_{2}+2(1/2-a)^{-1}+2(1/2+a)^{-1}\right)^{3}+

\displaystyle+6a\left(a^{2}-\ell^{2}+2\right)\left(\mathcal{I}_{2}+2(1/2-a)^{-1}+2(1/2+a)^{-1}\right)\times

\displaystyle\times\left(\mathcal{I}_{3}-2(1/2-a)^{-2}+2(1/2+a)^{-2}\right)+

\displaystyle+2(5a^{2}-\ell^{2}+1)\left(\mathcal{I}_{4}+2(1/2-a)^{-3}+2(1/2+a)^{-3}\right)\,.

(31)

The overall prefactor
P^{(1,1)}
is given by:

P^{(1,1)}=-\left(\frac{1}{2}-a\right)^{1+2a}\left(\frac{1}{2}+a\right)^{1-2a}

(32)

In this case
\mathcal{Z}^{(1,1)}(g)
is purely real.

It can be easily seen, that
f_{n}^{(1,1)}
can be directly obtained from the perturbative coefficients
f_{n}^{(0,0)}
by first applying the shift (
24
) then (
28
) subsequently on the integrals
\mathcal{I}_{n}
, or vica versa.

General observations

From the definition of
\mathbb{K}(\alpha)
it follows that the determinant is symmetric under the exchange
\alpha\to-\alpha
, therefore the strong-coupling expansion should reflect this symmetry as well. This leads to relation between the non-perturbative sectors:

\mathcal{Z}^{(n,m)}(g)=\left.\mathcal{Z}^{(m,n)}(g)\right|_{a\to-a}\,.

(33)

The exponential scales in (
9
) also respect this symmetry.

By generating several large-
g
contributions both analytically and numerically for the logarithm of the determinant
\mathcal{F}(g)
and taking its exponential to compute the determinant, we observed the cancellation of several non-perturbative corrections in
Z_{\ell}(g)
. We found that
Z^{(n,m)}
is only non-zero, if
(n,m)
satisfies the following constraint:

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}p(p-1)\leq n<\frac{1}{2}p(p+1)\,,\qquad 0\leq m-n\leq p\,,

(34)

with
p
being an integer such that
p\geq 0
. In Figure 1, I show at which orders of
\Lambda_{-}^{2}
and
\Lambda_{+}^{2}
there is a non-zero series
\mathcal{Z}^{(n,m)}
.

Figure 1:
The horizontal axis denotes the powers of
\Lambda_{-}^{2}
, while the vertical one represents the powers of
\Lambda_{+}^{2}
. The colored squares correspond to corrections that satisfy (
34
), hence give non-zero contribution to the strong coupling expansion. Different colors represent how many different contributions they contain from the
(\delta^{+},\delta^{-})
representation.

For the non-zero contributions we also found that at order
\Lambda_{-}^{2n}\Lambda_{+}^{2m}
, the leading term in
1/g
is of order:

\mathcal{Z}^{(n,m)}(g)=\mathcal{O}(1/g^{(n-m)^{2}})\,.

(35)

Furthermore, if
n\neq m
, the functions
\mathcal{Z}^{(n,m)}(g)
are complex valued. Each of these contributions gain an overall prefactor of
e^{i\pi(n-m)a}
. As it can be seen, the
1/g
powers of the leading order contribution in
\mathcal{Z}^{(n,m)}(g)
depends on the combination
n-m
. Also the scale
\Lambda_{-}^{2n}\Lambda_{+}^{2m}
gives an additional factor of
1/g^{2a(n-m)}
. Later we will see, that the difference
n-m
appears in several other ways in describing the large-
g
expansion of the determinant, therefore it is convenient to introduce the difference:

\Delta=n-m\,,

(36)

that is different for different non-perturbative sectors. Collecting the leading power of
g
in each non-perturbative contribution, we find:

\Lambda_{-}^{2n}\Lambda_{+}^{2m}\mathcal{Z}^{(n,m)}(g)\sim\mathcal{O}\left(g^{-\Delta(\Delta-2a)}\right)\,.

(37)

As mentioned previously, in addition to the
\alpha\to-\alpha
symmetry of the determinant, it is also periodic in
\alpha\to\alpha+\pi
. Therefore, it is enough to restrict the values of
\alpha
to the interval
0<\alpha<\pi/2
. However, for further reasons, it is worth commenting on the results if
\alpha
is outside of this branch.

The above results are, strictly speaking, only valid in the branch
-\pi/2\leq\alpha\leq\pi/2
. Outside this regime a more careful analysis is necessary. First, consider the modified symbol defined in (
10
). This function has the following properties:

\chi_{\alpha}(x)=\chi^{*}_{-\alpha}(x)=-\chi_{\alpha+\pi}(x)=\chi_{\pi-\alpha}^{*}(x)\,,

(38)

where ”
∗
” means complex conjugation. The moments (
18
) have to reflect these symmetries, namely
\mathcal{I}_{n}
has to satisfy:

\mathcal{I}_{n}=(-1)^{n}\left.\mathcal{I}_{n}\right|_{-a}=\left.\mathcal{I}_{n}\right|_{a+1}=(-1)^{n}\left.\mathcal{I}_{n}\right|_{1-a}\,.

(39)

Although the first property is automatically satisfied by the evaluated form (
19
) of
\mathcal{I}_{n}
, the function does not admit the rest of the properties. Since (
19
) has singularities at
a=\pm 1/2
, this problem can be solved by defining
\mathcal{I}_{n}
outside the branch
-1/2<a<1/2
by:

\left.\mathcal{I}_{n}\right|_{a\to a+m}\equiv\mathcal{I}_{n}\,,

(40)

with some integer
m
. This ensures that the periodicity in (
39
) holds.

Since the strong coupling expansion of
Z_{\ell}(g)
does not depend on periodic functions of
a
, the remaining explicit dependence on
a
in the transseries should be shifted according to the branch in which
a
lies. This, together with the periodicity (
40
) can be formulated as follows: suppose that for
-1/2<a<1/2
, the strong coupling expansion of
Z_{\ell}(g)
is a function of the integrals
\mathcal{I}_{n}
:

Z_{\ell}(g)\equiv Z_{\ell}^{0}[\mathcal{I}_{n}](g)\,,

(41)

and it also has an explicit dependence on the parameter
a
. Then for
m-1/2<a<m+1/2
, the strong coupling expansion is given by:

Z_{\ell}(g)=\left.Z_{\ell}^{0}[\mathcal{I}_{n}]\right|_{a\to a-m}(g)\,.

(42)

That is, the large-
g
expansion is the same as in the principal branch with the same moments given in (
19
), but the explicit dependence on
a
is shifted according to the branch of
a
. Later we will see, that the same property appears in the relation connecting the perturbative series with the non-perturbative corrections.

One final remark is that although it is formally possible to determine the transseries (
8
) up to arbitrary order in
1/g
and
\Lambda_{\pm}^{2}
with our previous method established in
9
, however, due to the scaling (
35
), beyond a certain exponential order, it becomes difficult to gain analytical results for the strong coupling coefficients. Following the idea of
11
, in the next section I will show, that (
8
) can be recast into a new form, in which a natural connection between the perturbative and non-perturbative contributions emerges, making the computation of non-perturbative corrections extremely efficient.

3
The transseries structure from a different angle

The reason for the cancelation of terms that do not satisfy the condition in (
34
) remained unknown, as it does not follow trivially from the method presented in
9
. It can also be seen that, by applying certain transformations to the parameter
a
and the integrals
\mathcal{I}_{n}
, the non-perturbative functions
\mathcal{Z}^{(n,m)}(g)
with
(n,m)\neq(0,0)
can easily be obtained from the perturbative part
\mathcal{Z}^{(0,0)}(g)
. In the following I will show that the procedure of
11
can be generalized to the matrix Bessel kernel (
1
) with symbol
(
6
)
, and these properties follow automatically from the analytic structure of the modified symbol (
10
) and its successive redefinition at each exponential level.

In
24
it was shown that for
\alpha=0
and arbitrary symbol function
\chi(x)
, the non-perturbative coefficients for (
1
) appear at exponential orders
e^{-8\pi gx_{n}}
, where
x_{n}
denote the zeros of the function
1-\chi(x)
. As follows from our method in
9
, in case of the matrix Bessel kernel with mixing angle
\alpha
, this function is replaced by the modified symbol (
10
). The solution of the underlying integro-differential equations involves the Wiener-Hopf decomposition of
\chi_{\alpha}(x)
, which can be written as:

\displaystyle\chi_{\alpha}(x)={2\cos\pi a\over x}\Phi_{+}(x)\Phi_{-}(-x)\,,

(43)

with
\Phi_{\pm}(x)
are given by:

\displaystyle\Phi_{+}(x)=\frac{\Gamma\left(\frac{1}{2}-a\right)\Gamma\left(1+\frac{ix}{2\pi}\right)}{\Gamma\left(\frac{1}{2}-a+{ix\over 2\pi}\right)}=\prod_{j\geq 0}\frac{1+\frac{ix}{2\pi x_{j}^{+}}}{1+\frac{ix}{2\pi y_{j}}}\,,

\displaystyle\Phi_{-}(x)=\frac{\Gamma\left(\frac{1}{2}+a\right)\Gamma\left(1+\frac{ix}{2\pi}\right)}{\Gamma\left(\frac{1}{2}+a+{ix\over 2\pi}\right)}=\prod_{j\geq 0}\frac{1+\frac{ix}{2\pi x_{j}^{-}}}{1+\frac{ix}{2\pi y_{j}}}\,.

(44)

These functions are both analytic in the lower half-plane and have infinitely many zeros and poles in the upper half-plane. They vanish at
x=2\pi ix_{j}^{+}
and
x=2\pi ix_{j}^{-}
respectively, with
x_{j}^{\pm}
given by:

x_{j}^{\pm}=j+\frac{1}{2}\mp a,\qquad j\in\mathbb{N}_{0}\,,

(45)

while both of the functions have poles at
x=2\pi iy_{j}
, where
y_{j}=j+1
and
j
is a non-negative integer.

The functions
\Phi_{\pm}(x)
and the zeros
x_{j}^{\pm}
depend on the parameter
a
and
\Phi_{+}
and
x_{n}^{+}
are related to
\Phi_{-}
and
x_{n}^{-}
via the exchange
a\to-a
. However, for reasons explained later, I suppress their
a
dependence and treat these quantities as if they were
a
independent.

From the method of
9
and the structure studied in
11
, we expect the appearance of non-perturbative contributions at orders of
e^{-8\pi gx^{\pm}_{j}}
. If we look at the exponential scales
\Lambda_{-}^{2n}\Lambda_{+}^{2m}
multiplying
\mathcal{Z}^{(n,m)}(g)
in the transseries (
8
), we find that the exponential prefactors are:

\Lambda_{-}^{2n}\Lambda_{+}^{2m}\sim\exp\left\{-8\pi g\left[n\left(\frac{1}{2}-a\right)+m\left(\frac{1}{2}+a\right)\right]\right\}\,.

(46)

For different values of
n
and
m
, the exponent contains both
+a
and
-a
terms and can be written as finite linear combinations of the zeros
x_{j}^{\pm}
, with each
x_{j}^{\pm}
appearing with a multiplicity of one. Therefore, together with the findings of
11
, this suggests that in the case of matrix Bessel kernel, non-perturbative contributions contain exponential factors that are at most first order in each parameter
e^{-8\pi gx_{j}^{+}}
and
e^{-8\pi gx_{j}^{-}}
. Therefore, the exponential orders can be parametrized as:

e^{-8\pi g\sum_{l\in\delta^{+}}x_{l}^{+}}e^{-8\pi g\sum_{j\in\delta^{-}}x_{j}^{-}}\,,

(47)

where
\delta^{+}
and
\delta^{-}
are finite sets of non-negative integers, both containing any integer at most once.

To be more precise, by equating:

\sum_{l\in\delta^{+}}x_{l}^{+}+\sum_{j\in\delta^{-}}x_{j}^{-}=n(1/2-a)+m(1/2+a)\,,

(48)

and using (
45
), it can be shown that to a specific combination of the sets
\delta^{+}
and
\delta^{-}
, a pair of integers
(n,m)
can be uniquely assigned, so that (
48
) is satisfied and (
47
) contains the same exponential factor as
\Lambda^{2n}_{-}\Lambda^{2m}_{+}
. For example:

\displaystyle\delta^{+}=\{0\},\,\delta^{-}=\{\}\

\displaystyle\rightarrow

\displaystyle n=1,m=0\,,

\displaystyle\delta^{+}=\{0\},\,\delta^{-}=\{0\}\

\displaystyle\rightarrow

\displaystyle n=1,m=1\,,

\displaystyle\delta^{+}=\{1\},\,\delta^{-}=\{\}\

\displaystyle\rightarrow

\displaystyle n=2,m=1\,,

\displaystyle\delta^{+}=\{0\},\,\delta^{-}=\{1\}\

\displaystyle\rightarrow

\displaystyle n=2,m=2\,,

\displaystyle\delta^{+}=\{1\},\,\delta^{-}=\{0\}\

\displaystyle\rightarrow

\displaystyle n=2,m=2\,,

\displaystyle\delta^{+}=\{0,1\},\,\delta^{-}=\{\}\

\displaystyle\rightarrow

\displaystyle n=3,m=1\,,

\displaystyle\delta^{+}=\{0,1\},\,\delta^{-}=\{0\}\

\displaystyle\rightarrow

\displaystyle n=3,m=2\,,

\displaystyle\delta^{+}=\{2\},\,\delta^{-}=\{\}\

\displaystyle\rightarrow

\displaystyle n=3,m=2\,,

\displaystyle\delta^{+}=\{1\},\,\delta^{-}=\{1\}\

\displaystyle\rightarrow

\displaystyle n=3,m=3\,.

(49)

By investigating further combinations of
\delta^{+}
and
\delta^{-}
and assigning pairs of integers
(n,m)
to them via (
48
), we find that not every pair of
(n,m)
occurs (eg.
(n,m)\neq(0,2)
). In fact, we only find a contribution at order
\Lambda_{-}^{2n},\Lambda_{+}^{2m}
, if
n
and
m
satisfy the condition (
34
), so at the same orders as indicated in Figure 1. This confirms that instead of (
8
), it is more convenient to parametrize the strong coupling expansion of the determinant in terms of (
47
).

From the examples in (
3
), it can also be seen that different combinations of
\delta^{+}
and
\delta^{-}
could produce the same pair of
(n,m)
. Indeed, by investigating higher order contributions, the number of exponential corrections in (
47
) correspond to the same order
\Lambda_{-}^{2n}\Lambda_{+}^{2m}
rapidly increases. In Figure 1 I indicated the order of this degeneracy using different colors.

It is easy to see that for different sets
(\delta^{+},\delta^{-})
that correspond to the same pair of
(n,m)
, the number
\Delta
defined in (
36
) is invariant and given by:

\Delta=n-m=|\delta^{+}|-|\delta^{-}|\,.

(50)

Here,
|\delta|
denotes the number of elements in the set
\delta
. Therefore, the value of
\Delta
represents the difference between the number of exponential corrections of type
e^{-8\pi gx^{+}_{l}}
and
e^{-8\pi gx^{-}_{j}}
.

Finally, collecting all the properties discussed above and in Section
2
, I conjecture that the strong coupling expansion of
Z_{\ell}(g)
is given by:

\displaystyle Z_{\ell}(g)=A_{\ell}(g)

\displaystyle\sum_{\delta^{+},\delta^{-}}(8\pi g)^{-\Delta(\Delta-2a)}e^{-8\pi g\left(\sum_{l\in\delta^{+}}x_{l}^{+}+\sum_{j\in\delta^{-}}x_{j}^{-}\right)}e^{i\pi a\Delta}S^{(\delta^{+},\delta^{-})}\mathcal{D}^{(\delta^{+},\delta^{-})}(g)\,.

(51)

This series is an alternative form of the strong coupling expansion in (
8
). Here, the exponential weights are precisely given by the zeros of the relevant symbol
\chi_{\alpha}(x)
and each exponential correction is at most first order in every
e^{-8\pi gx_{l}^{+}}
and
e^{-8\pi gx_{j}^{-}}
factor.

The functions
\mathcal{D}^{(\delta^{+},\delta^{-})}(g)
are given by series in
1/g
:

\mathcal{D}^{(\delta^{+},\delta^{-})}(g)=\sum_{k\geq 0}\frac{d_{k}^{(\delta^{+},\delta^{-})}}{(8\pi g)^{k}}\,,

(52)

and each term in the series (
51
) is normalized in such a way that
d_{0}^{(\delta^{+},\delta^{-})}=1
and
S^{(\{\},\{\})}=1
.
S^{(\delta^{+},\delta^{-})}
are the Stokes constants of the transseries. Notice that in each term, I have pulled out the complex factors
e^{i\pi a\Delta}
, so that
S^{(\delta^{+},\delta^{-})}
and
\mathcal{D}^{(\delta^{+},\delta^{-})}(g)
are real.

The
a\to-a
symmetry of the determinant is reflected in the Stokes constants and the non-perturbative functions as:

\displaystyle S^{(\delta^{+},\delta^{-})}

\displaystyle=S^{(\delta^{-},\delta^{+})}\left.\right|_{a\to-a}\,,

(53)

\displaystyle\mathcal{D}^{(\delta^{+},\delta^{-})}(g)

\displaystyle=\mathcal{D}^{(\delta^{-},\delta^{+})}(g)\left.\right|_{a\to-a}\,.

(54)

In the perturbative sector
\delta^{\pm}=\{\}
the coefficients
d_{k}^{(\{\},\{\})}
with
k\geq 1
are related to the functions
f_{k}^{(0,0)}
given in (
2
) via:

d_{k}^{(\{\},\{\})}=(a^{2}-\ell^{2})\frac{(-1)^{k}}{k!}f_{k}^{(0,0)}\,.

(55)

For the leading order non-perturbative contribution
\delta^{+}=\{0\}
and
\delta^{-}=\{\}
we have:

d_{k}^{(\{0\},\{\})}=((a-1)^{2}-\ell^{2})\frac{(-1)^{k}}{k!}f_{k}^{(1,0)}\,,

(56)

with the first few
f_{k}^{(1,0)}
are given in (
2
). At this exponential level the Stokes constant coincides with
P^{(1,0)}
and by (
23
) its explicity value is:

S^{(\{0\},\{\})}=-(-1)^{\ell}\left(\frac{1}{2}-a\right)^{2a-1}\frac{\Gamma(1+\ell-a)}{\Gamma(\ell+a)}\,.

(57)

The next subleading coefficients
d_{k}^{(\{\},\{0\})}
and the corresponding Stokes constant
S^{(\{\},\{0\})}
can be obtained from the
{(\{0\},\{\})}
sector by changing
a\to-a
.

For subleading corrections, the situation becomes more complicated. As can be seen in (
3
), for example, the
\Lambda_{-}^{4}\Lambda_{+}^{4}
term receives a contribution from two different sources in the sum (
51
): from the corrections with
\delta^{+}=\{0\}
,
\delta^{-}=\{1\}
and
\delta^{+}=\{1\}
,
\delta^{-}=\{0\}
. Therefore, at this order
\mathcal{Z}^{(2,2)}(g)
is expected to be written as a sum:

\mathcal{Z}^{(2,2)}(g)=S^{(\{1\},\{0\})}\mathcal{D}^{(\{1\},\{0\})}(g)+S^{(\{0\},\{1\})}\mathcal{D}^{(\{0\},\{1\})}(g)\,.

(58)

In the following I present a method to compute the non-perturbative functions
\mathcal{D}^{(\delta^{+},\delta^{-})}(g)
from the perturbative one
\mathcal{D}^{(\{\},\{\})}
. By comparing numerically the results with the series (
8
) obtained in
9
, I verify the degeneracy of higher order contributions
\mathcal{Z}^{(n,m)}(g)
in the
\delta^{\pm}
prescription. I also show a recursive way to effectively generate the Stokes constants
\mathcal{S}^{(\delta^{+},\delta^{-})}
. This fully describes the strong coupling expansion of the determinant (
1
).

3.1
1/g
expansion

First, I describe the structure of the series
\mathcal{D}^{(\delta^{+},\delta^{-})}(g)
. As was discussed in Section
2
, the
1/g
expansion of these functions is governed by the integrals
\mathcal{I}_{n}
. Their values are given in (
18
). Using the series representation of the polygamma function, these integrals can be directly expressed in terms of the zeros and poles of the the symbol
\chi_{\alpha}(x)
(or
\Phi_{\pm}(x)
):

\mathcal{I}_{n}=\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\left[(-1)^{n-1}\left(\frac{1}{(x_{k}^{+})^{n-1}}-\frac{1}{(y_{k})^{n-1}}\right)-\left(\frac{1}{(x_{k}^{-})^{n-1}}-\frac{1}{(y_{k})^{n-1}}\right)\right]

(59)

the locations of the zeros
x_{k}^{\pm}
are given in (
45
).

In Section
2
, I have shown, that while the perturbative series
\mathcal{D}^{(\{\},\{\})}
is given in terms of (
59
), the
1/g
expansions of the leading order non-perturbative sectors
\mathcal{Z}^{(1,0)}(g)
,
\mathcal{Z}^{(0,1)}(g)
and
\mathcal{Z}^{(1,1)}(g)
– therefore
\mathcal{D}^{(\{0\},\{\})}(g)
,
\mathcal{D}^{(\{\},\{0\})}(g)
and
\mathcal{D}^{(\{0\},\{0\})}(g)
– can be easily obtained from the perturbative series by applying the transformations (
25
) and (
29
) and shifting the explicit dependence on
a
by
a\to a-1
and
a\to a+1
and
a\to a
respectively. In terms of the zeros of
\Phi_{\pm}(x)
, for example, the transformation (
25
) is simply written as:

\mathcal{I}_{n}\to\mathcal{I}^{(\{0\},\{\})}_{n}\equiv\mathcal{I}_{n}-(-1)^{n-1}\frac{2}{\left(x_{0}^{+}\right)^{n-1}}

(60)

From the point of view of expansion (
59
), this rule corresponds to changing the sign before the terms containing
x_{0}^{+}
to contribute to
\mathcal{I}_{n}
in the same way as the locations of the poles
y_{k}
do. In other words, the ”new” integral
\mathcal{I}^{(\{0\},\{\})}_{n}
entering the non-perturbative series
\mathcal{Z}^{(1,0)}(g)
is given by the same integral as in (
18
), but with a new symbol,
\chi^{(\{0\},\{\})}_{\alpha}(x)
:

\chi^{(\{0\},\{\})}_{\alpha}(x)={2\cos\pi a\over x}\Phi^{\{0\}}_{+}(x)\Phi_{-}(-x)\,,

(61)

where the function
\Phi^{\{0\}}_{+}(x)
is:

\Phi^{\{0\}}_{+}(x)=\frac{\Phi_{+}(x)}{\left(1+\frac{ix}{2\pi x_{0}^{+}}\right)^{2}}\,.

(62)

This function has zeros at
x=2i\pi x_{j}^{+}
with
j\geq 1
and poles at
x=2i\pi x^{+}_{0}
and
x=2i\pi y_{j}
with
j\geq 0
.

We can interpret the transformation (
29
) in the same way, but with another symbol:

\chi^{(\{\},\{0\})}_{\alpha}(x)={2\cos\pi a\over x}\Phi_{+}(x)\Phi^{\{0\}}_{-}(-x)\,,

(63)

with the function
\Phi^{\{0\}}_{-}(x)
being:

\Phi^{\{0\}}_{-}(x)=\frac{\Phi_{-}(x)}{\left(1+\frac{ix}{2\pi x_{0}^{-}}\right)^{2}}\,.

(64)

Now, this function has zeros at
x=2i\pi x_{j}^{-}
with
j\geq 1
and poles at
x=2i\pi x^{-}_{0}
and
x=2i\pi y_{j}
with
j\geq 0
. These transformation rules are analogous to what we have found in
11
.

This suggests that all the subleading non-perturbative corrections can be obtained in a similar way, by promoting the corresponding zeros to be poles of the functions
\chi_{\alpha}(x)
, namely at every exponential level introducing a new symbol function
\chi^{(\delta^{+},\delta^{-})}
:

\chi_{\alpha}^{(\delta^{+},\delta^{-})}(x)={2\cos\pi a\over x}\Phi^{\delta^{+}}_{+}(x)\Phi^{\delta^{-}}_{-}(-x)\,,

(65)

with:

\displaystyle\Phi_{+}^{\delta^{+}}(x)

\displaystyle=\frac{\Phi_{+}(x)}{\prod_{j\in\delta^{+}}\left(1+\frac{ix}{2\pi x_{j}^{+}}\right)^{2}}\,,

\displaystyle\Phi_{-}^{\delta^{-}}(x)=\frac{\Phi_{-}(x)}{\prod_{j\in\delta^{-}}\left(1+\frac{ix}{2\pi x_{j}^{-}}\right)^{2}}\,.

(66)

This new symbol enters the
1/g
expansion of
\mathcal{D}^{(\delta^{+},\delta^{-})}
through the integrals of the form (
18
) but with
\chi_{\alpha}(x)
replaced by
\chi^{(\delta^{+},\delta^{-})}_{\alpha}(x)
. This results in the modified moments:

\displaystyle\mathcal{I}_{n}^{(\delta^{+},\delta^{-})}

\displaystyle=\mathcal{I}_{n}-(-1)^{n-1}\sum_{l\in\delta^{+}}\frac{2}{(x_{l}^{+})^{n-1}}+\sum_{j\in\delta^{-}}\frac{2}{(x_{j}^{-})^{n-1}}=

\displaystyle=\mathcal{I}_{n}-(-1)^{n-1}\sum_{l\in\delta^{+}}\frac{2}{\left(l+\frac{1}{2}-a\right)^{n-1}}+\sum_{j\in\delta^{-}}\frac{2}{(j+\frac{1}{2}+a)^{n-1}}

(67)

where in the second relation, I restated the exact form of
x^{\pm}_{l}
. Taking into account all the properties discussed above, the function
\mathcal{D}^{(\delta^{+},\delta^{-})}(g)
is given by the perturbative function by replacing the integrals
\mathcal{I}_{n}
by (
3.1
) and shifting the explicit
a
dependence by
a\to a-\Delta
, with
\Delta
defined in (
50
)

In other words, if we indicate the functional dependence of the perturbative part on
\mathcal{I}_{n}
:

\mathcal{D}^{(\{\},\{\})}(g)\equiv\mathcal{D}\left[\mathcal{I}_{n}\right](g)\,,

(68)

then the non-perturbative contribution
\mathcal{D}^{(\delta^{+},\delta^{-})}(\tilde{g})
is given by:

\mathcal{D}^{(\delta^{+},\delta^{-})}(g)=\left.\mathcal{D}\left[\mathcal{I}^{(\delta^{+},\delta^{-})}_{n}\right](g)\right|_{a\to a-\Delta}\,.

(69)

To put this in an explicit form, using (
16
) an (
55
), we can parameterize any non-perturbative function
\mathcal{D}^{(\delta^{+},\delta^{-})}(g)
as:

\mathcal{D}^{(\delta^{+},\delta^{-})}(g)=1+\left((a-\Delta)^{2}-\ell^{2}\right)\sum_{k\geq 1}\frac{(-1)^{k}}{k!}\frac{f_{k}^{(\delta^{+},\delta^{-})}}{(8\pi g)^{k}}\,,

(70)

with the coefficients
f^{(\delta^{+},\delta^{-})}_{k}
given by:

\displaystyle f^{(\delta^{+},\delta^{-})}_{1}

\displaystyle=\mathcal{I}^{(\delta^{+},\delta^{-})}_{2}\,,

\displaystyle f^{(\delta^{+},\delta^{-})}_{2}

\displaystyle=((a-\Delta)^{2}-\ell^{2}+1)\left[\mathcal{I}^{(\delta^{+},\delta^{-})}_{2}\right]^{2}+2(a-\Delta)\mathcal{I}^{(\delta^{+},\delta^{-})}_{3}\,,

\displaystyle f^{(\delta^{+},\delta^{-})}_{3}

\displaystyle=\left((a-\Delta)^{2}-\ell^{2}+1\right)\left[\mathcal{I}^{(\delta^{+},\delta^{-})}_{2}\right]^{3}+

\displaystyle+6(a-\Delta)\left((a-\Delta)^{2}-\ell^{2}+2\right)\mathcal{I}^{(\delta^{+},\delta^{-})}_{2}\mathcal{I}^{(\delta^{+},\delta^{-})}_{3}+

\displaystyle+2(5(a-\Delta)^{2}-\ell^{2}+1)\mathcal{I}^{(\delta^{+},\delta^{-})}_{4}\,,

(71)

etc.

With this simple rule, up to the overall factor
S^{(\delta^{+},\delta^{-})}
, it is possible to generate any non-perturbative contributions to the determinant (
1
), by computing the perturbative series of a ”new” determinant with a suitably modified symbol function.

At the end of Section 2, I discussed the effect of the periodicity of
Z_{\ell}(g)
in
\alpha\to\alpha+\pi
on the strong coupling expansion. According to (
41
) and (
42
), the right-hand side of (
69
) is the large-
g
perturbative series of the determinant evaluated in the branch
\Delta-1/2<a<\Delta+1/2
with the original symbol
\chi_{\alpha}(x)
replaced by
\chi^{(\delta^{+},\delta^{-})}_{\alpha}(x)
.

It is important to emphasize that although the zeros
x_{j}^{\pm}
and hence the integrals
\mathcal{I}_{n}
are
a
dependent, the shift
a\to a-\Delta
in (
69
) only stands for the explicit
a
dependence in the coefficients
f_{k}^{(0,0)}
. This is the reason why I have suppressed the
a
dependence in the notation
x^{\pm}_{j}
and
\mathcal{I}_{n}
.

For the parameter values of
(a,\ell)=(1/4,0)
,
(a,\ell)=(1/4,1)
, and
(a,\ell)=(1/(2\sqrt{2}),2)
, I numerically generated 50
1/g
terms in the expansions of
\mathcal{D}^{(\{0\},\{\})}(g)
,
\mathcal{D}^{(\{\},\{0\})}(g)
and
\mathcal{D}^{(\{0\},\{0\})}(g)
with 50 digit precision. By normalizing the functions
\mathcal{Z}^{(n,m)}(g)
with their leading
1/g
coefficients, I was able to verify (
69
) for these non-degenerate contributions. For the next few degenerate non-perturbative sectors, from the analytic values of their leading
1/g
coefficients, I checked that there exist certain overall constant factors
S^{(\delta^{+},\delta^{-})}
, such that relations like (
58
) hold, with
\mathcal{D}^{(\delta^{+},\delta^{-})}(g)
determined via (
69
).

3.2
Stokes constants

The only remaining task to completely describe the large-
g
expansion of the determinant is to give a closed formula for the Stokes constants
S^{(\delta^{+},\delta^{-})}
. In the following I show that there is a consistent prescription to determine these constants with the argument given for
\mathcal{D}^{(\delta^{+},\delta^{-})}(g)
above, which allows to derive a pair of recurrence relations for the Stokes constants.

I start by rewriting the leading order constants
S^{(\{0\},\{\})}
,
S^{(\{\},\{0\})}
and
S^{(\{0\},\{0\})}
– which, according to (
3
), are essentially the same as
P^{(1,0)}
,
P^{(0,1)}
and
P^{(1,1)}
– in terms of the functions
\Phi_{\pm}(x)
and the locations of their zeros and poles
x_{j}^{\pm}
and
y_{j}
. From the method presented in
9
it follows that the factors
S^{(\{0\},\{\})}
and
S^{(\{\},\{0\})}
can be written as:

\displaystyle S^{(\{0\},\{\})}

\displaystyle=\frac{(-1)^{\ell}}{2\pi i}\,\frac{\Gamma(1+\ell-a)}{\Gamma(\ell+a)}\frac{\Phi_{-}(-2i\pi x_{0}^{+})}{\partial_{x}\Phi_{+}(2i\pi x_{0}^{+})}(x_{0}^{+})^{2a-2}

\displaystyle S^{(\{\},\{0\})}

\displaystyle=\frac{(-1)^{\ell}}{2\pi i}\,\frac{\Gamma(1+\ell+a)}{\Gamma(\ell-a)}\frac{\Phi_{+}(-2i\pi x_{0}^{-})}{\partial_{x}\Phi_{-}(2i\pi x_{0}^{-})}(x_{0}^{-})^{-2a-2}

(72)

By investigating higher order contributions, it turns out that similar combinations appear in the Stokes constants at each exponential level, but with
x_{0}^{\pm}
replaced by
x_{j}^{\pm}
. Therefore, it is convenient to introduce the functions:

\displaystyle S_{+}^{\{j\}}

\displaystyle=\frac{(-1)^{\ell}}{2\pi i}\,\frac{\Gamma(\ell-a)}{\Gamma(1+\ell+a)}\frac{\Phi_{-}(-2i\pi x_{j}^{+})}{\partial_{x}\Phi_{+}(2i\pi x_{j}^{+})}(x_{j}^{+})^{2a}

\displaystyle S_{-}^{\{j\}}

\displaystyle=\frac{(-1)^{\ell}}{2\pi i}\,\frac{\Gamma(\ell+a)}{\Gamma(1+\ell-a)}\frac{\Phi_{+}(-2i\pi x_{j}^{-})}{\partial_{x}\Phi_{-}(2i\pi x_{j}^{-})}(x_{j}^{-})^{-2a}

(73)

Then the leading order coefficients are given by:

\displaystyle S^{(\{0\},\{\})}

\displaystyle=\left.S^{\{0\}}_{+}\right|_{a\to a-1}\,,

\displaystyle S^{(\{\},\{0\})}

\displaystyle=\left.S^{\{0\}}_{-}\right|_{a\to a+1}\,.

(74)

Notice that the shift in
a
is again understood only for the explicit dependence on
a
and the functions
\Phi_{\pm}(x)
and their zeros
x^{\pm}_{j}
are treated as independent objects.
S_{\pm}^{\{j\}}
was chosen in such a way that this shift is consistent with
a\to a-\Delta
appearing in (
69
).

By generating exact analytical expressions for subleading corrections up to
\Lambda_{-}^{6}\Lambda_{+}^{6}
, I found that at orders
\mathcal{O}\left(e^{-8\pi gx_{j}^{\pm}}\right)
, similar relations to (
3.2
) still hold, namely at orders
(\delta^{+},\delta^{-})=(\{j\},\{\})
, the Stokes constants are:

\displaystyle S^{(\{j\},\{\})}=\left.S^{\{j\}}_{+}\right|_{a\to a-1}\,,

(75)

whereas if
\delta^{+}=\{\}
and
\delta^{-}=\{j\}
we have:

\displaystyle S^{(\{\},\{j\})}=\left.S^{\{j\}}_{-}\right|_{a\to a+1}\,.

(76)

The problem arises for mixed terms, that is, when
\delta^{+}
and
\delta^{-}
together contain more than one element. By generating subleading corrections
\mathcal{D}^{(\delta^{+},\delta^{-})}(g)
with (
69
) and, with their proper degeneracy, comparing their linear combinations with
\mathcal{Z}^{(\delta^{+},\delta^{-})}
, for the first few subleading Stokes constants, I found:

\displaystyle S^{(\{0\},\{0\})}=-\left(\frac{1}{2}-a\right)^{1+2a}\left(\frac{1}{2}+a\right)^{1-2a}

\displaystyle S^{(\{0,1\},\{\})}=-\left(\frac{1}{2}-a\right)^{-1+2a}\left(\frac{3}{2}-a\right)^{-3+2a}\frac{\Gamma(\ell-a+1)\Gamma(\ell-a+2)}{\Gamma(\ell+a)\Gamma(\ell+a-1)}

\displaystyle S^{(\{0,1\},\{0\})}=\frac{(-1)^{\ell}}{4}\left(\frac{1}{2}+a\right)^{3-2a}\left(\frac{1}{2}-a\right)^{1+2a}\left(\frac{3}{2}-a\right)^{-1+2a}\frac{\Gamma(\ell-a+1)}{\Gamma(\ell+a)}

\displaystyle S^{(\{0,1,2\},\{\})}=\left(\frac{1}{2}-a\right)^{-1+2a}\left(\frac{3}{2}-a\right)^{-3+2a}\left(\frac{5}{2}-a\right)^{-5+2a}\frac{\Gamma(\ell-a+1)\Gamma(\ell-a+2)\Gamma(\ell-a+3)}{\Gamma(\ell+a)\Gamma(\ell+a-1)\Gamma(\ell+a-2)}

\displaystyle S^{(\{0,1\},\{0,1\})}=\frac{1}{144}\left(\frac{1}{2}-a\right)^{3+2a}\left(\frac{3}{2}-a\right)^{1+2a}\left(\frac{1}{2}+a\right)^{3-2a}\left(\frac{3}{2}+a\right)^{1-2a}

(77)

Further can be obtained by using the symmetry property in (
53
).

The first two coefficients above (together with their
a\to-a
counterparts) can also be expressed in terms of the functions in (
3.2
):

\displaystyle S^{(\{0,1\},\{\})}

\displaystyle=-\left(1-\frac{x_{1}^{+}}{x_{0}^{+}}\right)^{2}\left(\left.S_{+}^{\{0\}}\right|_{a\to a-1}\right)\left(\left.S_{+}^{\{1\}}\right|_{a\to a-2}\right)\,,

\displaystyle S^{(\{\},\{0,1\})}

\displaystyle=-\left(1-\frac{x_{1}^{-}}{x_{0}^{-}}\right)^{2}\left(\left.S_{-}^{\{0\}}\right|_{a\to a+1}\right)\left(\left.S_{-}^{\{1\}}\right|_{a\to a+2}\right)\,,

\displaystyle S^{(\{0\},\{0\})}

\displaystyle=-\left(1+\frac{x_{0}^{-}}{x_{0}^{+}}\right)^{-2}\left(\left.S_{+}^{\{0\}}\right|_{a\to a-1}\right)S_{-}^{\{0\}}\,.

(78)

Therefore, in addition to the products of
S^{\{j\}}_{\pm}
with certain shifts in parameter
a
, we have additional factors containing the zeros
x_{j}^{\pm}
. Moreover, for the mixed terms, there is more than one way to express them in terms of (
3.2
). For example, beside (
3.2
), we can also write:

\displaystyle S^{(\{0,1\},\{\})}

\displaystyle=-\left(1-\frac{x_{0}^{+}}{x_{1}^{+}}\right)^{2}\left(\left.S_{+}^{\{1\}}\right|_{a\to a-1}\right)\left(\left.S_{+}^{\{0\}}\right|_{a\to a-2}\right)\,,

\displaystyle S^{(\{\},\{0,1\})}

\displaystyle=-\left(1-\frac{x_{0}^{-}}{x_{1}^{-}}\right)^{2}\left(\left.S_{-}^{\{1\}}\right|_{a\to a+1}\right)\left(\left.S_{-}^{\{0\}}\right|_{a\to a+2}\right)\,,

\displaystyle S^{(\{0\},\{0\})}

\displaystyle=-\left(1+\frac{x_{0}^{+}}{x_{0}^{-}}\right)^{-2}\left(\left.S_{-}^{\{0\}}\right|_{a\to a+1}\right)S_{+}^{\{0\}}\,.

(79)

In this case, there are different shifts in
a
and different prefactors, but surprisingly the results are the same as (
3.2
).

Following the same argument that I presented for the functions
\mathcal{D}^{(\delta^{+},\delta^{-})}(g)
, these properties can be understood in a simple way. Assume that we start with the exponential correction
(\delta^{+},\delta^{-})=(\{j\},\{\})
whose Stokes constant is given in (
75
). Now append an element
k\neq j
to
\delta^{+}
, so we have the exponential correction at level
(\delta^{{}^{\prime}+},\delta^{-})=(\{j,k\},\{\})
. With this step,
\Delta
increased and became
\Delta^{\prime}=|\delta^{{}^{\prime}+}|-|\delta^{-}|=2
. Then motivated by the structure of
\mathcal{D}^{(\delta^{+},\delta^{-})}(g)
, it is reasonable to guess that the new Stokes constant is (
75
) multiplied by
S_{+}^{\{k\}}
with its explicit
a
dependence shifted by
a\to a-\Delta^{\prime}=a-2
. However, in addition to the shift, we saw that at every exponential level, to generate the functions
\mathcal{D}^{(\delta^{+},\delta^{-})}(g)
, we have to remove the corresponding zeros of
\Phi_{\pm}(x)
and promote them to be poles. Therefore, we have to use the new functions in (
66
) to describe the
1/g
expansion. Since at the level
(\delta^{+},\delta^{-})=(\{j\},\{\})
we already ”removed” the
x_{j}^{+}
zero, this suggests that to obtain
S^{(\{j,k\},\{\})}
from
S^{(\{j\},\{\})}
, we have to multiply (
75
) with:

\left.\frac{(-1)^{\ell}}{2\pi i}\,\frac{\Gamma(\ell-a)}{\Gamma(1+\ell+a)}\frac{\Phi_{-}(-2i\pi x_{k}^{+})}{\partial_{x}\Phi^{\{j\}}_{+}(2i\pi x_{k}^{+})}(x_{k}^{+})^{2a}\right|_{a\to a-2}=\left(1-\frac{x_{k}^{+}}{x_{j}^{-}}\right)^{2}\left(\left.S^{\{k\}}_{+}\right|_{a\to a-2}\right)\,.

(80)

In case of
j=0
,
k=1
and
j=1
,
k=0
, up to an overall minus sign, this reproduces the first lines of (
3.2
) and (
3.2
).

Similarly if we start with
(\delta^{+},\delta^{-})=(\{j\},\{\})
and go to level
(\delta^{+},\delta^{{}^{\prime}-})=(\{j\},\{k\})
then we get the same number of ”
+
” type contributions as ”
-
” ones, therefore at this level we have
\Delta^{\prime}=|\delta^{+}|-|\delta^{{}^{\prime}-}|=0
. In the first step
x_{j}^{+}
was removed from the symbol. Therefore, it suggests that the new Stokes constant is given by the product of (
76
) and:

\displaystyle\frac{(-1)^{\ell}}{2\pi i}\,\frac{\Gamma(\ell+a)}{\Gamma(1+\ell-a)}\frac{\Phi^{\{j\}}_{+}(-2i\pi x_{k}^{-})}{\partial_{x}\Phi_{-}(2i\pi x_{k}^{-})}(x_{k}^{-})^{-2a}=\left(1+\frac{x_{k}^{-}}{x_{j}^{+}}\right)^{-2}S_{-}^{\{k\}}\,.

(81)

With
j=0
and
k=0
, up to an overall minus sign, this reproduces the result in the last line of (
3.2
).

The similar procedure can be done by starting from
(\delta^{+},\delta^{-})=(\{\},\{j\})
to reproduce the rest of (
3.2
) and (
3.2
). The simultaneous shifts of
a
and removal of zeros guarantees that the two prescriptions give the same answer independently of the path of adding elements to
\delta^{\pm}
.

This procedure can be generalized to arbitrary exponentially small corrections. By appending more and more elements to
\delta^{+}
and
\delta^{-}
, at each step we have to multiply the previous Stokes constant with (
3.2
), but with
\Phi_{\pm}(x)
replaced by
\Phi_{\pm}^{\delta^{\pm}}(x)
and the explicit
a
dependence shifted by the new
\Delta
.

The procedure can be summarized in the following way: the Stokes constant for the perturbative part is:

S^{(\{\},\{\})}=1\,.

(82)

Now assume that we know the value of
S^{(\delta^{+},\delta^{-})}
at a specific level
(\delta^{+},\delta^{-})
. Then the Stokes constant at level
(\delta^{{}^{\prime}+},\delta^{-})=(\delta^{+}\cup\{k\},\delta^{-})
, which is obtained from
(\delta^{+},\delta^{-})
by adding the integer
k\notin\delta^{+}
to the set
\delta^{+}
is given by:

S^{(\delta^{{}^{\prime}+},\delta^{-})}=(-1)^{\Delta}\frac{\prod_{j\in\delta^{+}}\left(1-\frac{x_{k}^{+}}{x_{j}^{+}}\right)^{2}}{\prod_{j\in\delta^{-}}\left(1+\frac{x_{k}^{+}}{x_{j}^{-}}\right)^{2}}\left(\left.S_{+}^{\{k\}}\right|_{a\to a-\Delta^{\prime}}\right)S^{(\delta^{+},\delta^{-})}\,,

(83)

where
\Delta=|\delta^{+}|-|\delta^{-}|
and
\Delta^{\prime}=|\delta^{{}^{\prime}+}|-|\delta^{-}|=\Delta+1
.

Similarly, the Stokes constant at level
(\delta^{+},\delta^{{}^{\prime}-})=(\delta^{+},\delta^{-}\cup\{k\})
, which differs from the original
(\delta^{+},\delta^{-})
by adding the element
k\notin\delta^{-}
to
\delta^{-}
, is given by:

\displaystyle S^{(\delta^{+},\delta^{{}^{\prime}-})}=(-1)^{\Delta}\frac{\prod_{j\in\delta^{-}}\left(1-\frac{x_{k}^{-}}{x_{j}^{-}}\right)^{2}}{\prod_{j\in\delta^{+}}\left(1+\frac{x_{k}^{-}}{x_{j}^{+}}\right)^{2}}\left(\left.S_{-}^{\{k\}}\right|_{a\to a-\Delta^{\prime}}\right)S^{(\delta^{+},\delta^{-})}\,,

(84)

where in this case
\Delta^{\prime}=|\delta^{+}|-|\delta^{{}^{\prime}-}|=\Delta-1
. Using these recurrence relations, the Stokes constants can be determined up to arbitrary non-perturbative orders.

The prefactors
(-1)^{\Delta}
in (
84
) and (
83
) were introduced by investigating numerical results with different values of
a
and
\ell
up to
\Lambda_{-}^{5}\Lambda_{+}^{5}
. The origin of these factors can be understood by absorbing the
e^{i\pi a\Delta}
factors from (
51
) as
e^{\pm i\pi a}
phases in (
3.2
). Then the Stokes constants will no longer be real, but the
a\to a-\Delta
shifts in the recurrence relations (
83
) and (
84
) will produce exactly the same factors
(-1)^{\Delta}
as in (
83
) and (
84
) and will produce the same complex phases
e^{i\pi a\Delta}
as in (
51
). We will see later that the prescription that I used above is more convenient regarding the resurgence analysis of the transseries (
51
).

To put the recurrence relations (
83
) and (
84
) into a more practical form, I recover the explicit forms of the functions
\Phi_{\pm}(x)
and the zeros
x^{\pm}_{l}
. With this, the first recurrence relation becomes

\displaystyle S^{(\delta^{+}\cup\{k\},\delta^{-})}=

\displaystyle-(-1)^{\ell+\Delta}\frac{\Gamma\left(\ell-a+\Delta+1\right)}{\Gamma\left(\ell+a-\Delta\right)}\frac{\Gamma^{2}\left(k+\frac{1}{2}-a\right)}{\Gamma^{2}\left(\frac{1}{2}-a\right)\Gamma^{2}\left(k+1\right)}\times

\displaystyle\times\frac{\prod_{j\in\delta^{+}}\left(\frac{j-k}{j+\frac{1}{2}-a}\right)^{2}}{\prod_{j\in\delta^{-}}\left(\frac{j+k+1}{j+\frac{1}{2}+a}\right)^{2}}\left(k+\frac{1}{2}-a\right)^{2a-1-2\Delta}S^{(\delta^{+},\delta^{-})}\,,

(85)

while the second one is:

\displaystyle S^{(\delta^{+},\delta^{-}\cup\{k\})}=

\displaystyle-(-1)^{\ell-\Delta}\frac{\Gamma\left(\ell+a-\Delta+1\right)}{\Gamma\left(\ell-a+\Delta\right)}\frac{\Gamma^{2}\left(k+\frac{1}{2}+a\right)}{\Gamma^{2}\left(\frac{1}{2}+a\right)\Gamma^{2}\left(k+1\right)}\times

\displaystyle\times\frac{\prod_{j\in\delta_{j}^{-}}\left(\frac{j-k}{j+\frac{1}{2}+a}\right)^{2}}{\prod_{j\in\delta_{j}^{+}}\left(\frac{j+k+1}{j+\frac{1}{2}-a}\right)^{2}}\left(k+\frac{1}{2}+a\right)^{-2a-1+2\Delta}S^{(\delta^{+},\delta^{-})}\,.

(86)

These relations reproduce the examples given in (
3.2
).

The recurrence relations (
83
) and (
84
) together with (
69
) completely describe the large-
g
expansion of the determinant
\mathcal{Z}(g)
and allow us to effectively determine the physical observables discussed in Section 1 up to arbitrary non-perturbative and
1/g
orders by only knowing the perturbative contributions of the determinant. The relations (
69
), (
3.2
) and (
3.2
) were numerically verified for the parameter values of
(a,\ell)=(1/4,0)
,
(a,\ell)=(1/4,1)
and
(a,\ell)=(1/(2\sqrt{2}),2)
by comparing different corrections in (
51
) with the corresponding terms (
8
) up to order
\Lambda_{-}^{8}\Lambda_{+}^{8}
.

3.3
A practical example

In this subsection, I specify the parameter
a
as
a=1/4
and generate exact strong-coupling results for the determinant. As was discussed in the Introduction, this is a relevant example for the
\mathcal{N}=4
SYM, since the ratio of the determinants evaluated at
\ell=1
and
\ell=0
gives the cusp anomalous dimension (see equation (
7
)).

For
a=1/4
, the zeros of
\Phi_{\pm}(x)
are located at:

x^{+}_{j}=j+\frac{1}{4}\,,\qquad x_{j}^{-}=j+\frac{3}{4}\,,

(87)

with
j\geq 0
an integer. Therefore, in this case, the exponential corrections scale as powers of
e^{-2\pi g}
and we can parametrize the strong coupling expansion for
a=1/4
as:

Z_{\ell}(g)=A_{\ell}(g)\sum_{n\geq 0}e^{-2\pi ng}\mathcal{C}_{\ell}^{(n)}(g)\,.

(88)

Using (
69
), the first few coefficient functions
\mathcal{C}_{\ell}^{(n)}(g)
are:

\displaystyle\mathcal{C}_{\ell}^{(0)}(g)=\left.\mathcal{D}^{(\{\},\{\})}(g)\right|_{a=1/4}\,,

\displaystyle\mathcal{C}_{\ell}^{(1)}(g)=e^{\frac{i\pi}{4}}(8\pi g)^{-\frac{1}{2}}\left.S^{(\{0\},\{\})}\mathcal{D}^{(\{0\},\{\})}(g)\right|_{a=1/4}\,,

\displaystyle\mathcal{C}_{\ell}^{(2)}(g)=0\,,

\displaystyle\mathcal{C}_{\ell}^{(3)}(g)=e^{-\frac{i\pi}{4}}(8\pi g)^{-\frac{3}{2}}\left.S^{(\{\},\{0\})}\mathcal{D}^{(\{\},\{0\})}(g)\right|_{a=1/4}\,,

\displaystyle\mathcal{C}_{\ell}^{(4)}(g)=\left.S^{(\{0\},\{0\})}\mathcal{D}^{(\{0\},\{0\})}(g)\right|_{a=1/4}\,,

\displaystyle\mathcal{C}_{\ell}^{(5)}(g)=e^{\frac{i\pi}{4}}(8\pi g)^{-\frac{1}{2}}\left.S^{(\{1\},\{\})}\mathcal{D}^{(\{1\},\{\})}(g)\right|_{a=1/4}

(89)

In the examples above, for a given
\mathcal{C}_{\ell}^{(n)}(g)
, only one non-perturbative function
\mathcal{D}^{(\delta^{+},\delta^{-})}(g)
contributes. However, for
a=1/4
, at levels with
n\geq 8
, certain combinations of
(\delta^{+},\delta^{-})
produce an exponential contribution to (
88
) at the same level, therefore, in general
\mathcal{C}^{(n)}(g)
is a linear combination of multiple
\mathcal{D}^{(\delta^{+},\delta^{-})}(g)
. At higher orders, to compute
\mathcal{C}^{(n)}(g)
, one should sum up all the
(\delta^{+},\delta^{-})
corrections in (
51
), for which:

\sum_{l\in\delta^{+}}\left(4l+1\right)+\sum_{j\in\delta^{-}}\left(4j+3\right)=n

(90)

Since
\mathcal{I}_{n}
are independent of the parameter
\ell
, before giving explicit expressions for the different coefficients in (
88
) with
\ell=0,1
, it is convenient to rewrite the moments in a more familiar form. By substituting
x^{\pm}_{k}
with
a=1/4
into (
59
), the moments
\mathcal{I}_{n}
with odd
n
can be written as:

\mathcal{I}_{2k+1}=2^{2k}\beta(2k)\,,

(91)

while for even
n
:

\mathcal{I}_{2k}=-(2^{4k-2}-2^{2k-1}-2)\zeta(2k-1)\,,

(92)

with
\zeta(x)
is the Riemann zeta function and
\beta(x)
is the Dirichlet beta function. The same special functions already appeared in the analysis of the strong coupling expansion of the cusp anomalous dimension. For example, in equations (28) and (29) of
16
the perturbative part of the cusp anomalous dimension were given in terms of some coefficients
c_{n}
proportional to
\zeta(x)
and
\beta(x)
with odd and even integer arguments respectively. Hence these coefficients are in one-to-one correspondence with moments
\mathcal{I}_{n}
, namely:

\displaystyle\mathcal{I}_{2}=-8\pi c_{1}\,,

\displaystyle\mathcal{I}_{3}=256\pi^{2}c_{2}\,,

\displaystyle\mathcal{I}_{4}=-4096\pi^{3}c_{3}\,,

\displaystyle\mathcal{I}_{5}=\frac{262144}{21}\pi^{4}c_{4}\,,

\displaystyle\mathcal{I}_{6}=-\frac{4194304}{87}\pi^{5}c_{5}\,,

\displaystyle\mathcal{I}_{7}=\frac{134217728}{1605}\pi^{6}c_{6}\,,

(93)

etc. To avoid cumbersome expressions, in the following I will express everything in terms of
\mathcal{I}_{n}
.

According to (
3.1
) the moments
\mathcal{I}_{n}^{(\delta^{+},\delta^{-})}
entering the higher order contributions are obtained from (
91
) and (
92
) via:

\mathcal{I}_{n}^{(\delta^{+},\delta^{-})}=\mathcal{I}_{n}-(-1)^{n-1}\sum_{l\in\delta^{+}}\frac{2}{\left(l+\frac{1}{4}\right)^{n-1}}+\sum_{j\in\delta^{-}}\frac{2}{(j+\frac{3}{4})^{n-1}}

(94)

Then using rule (
69
), all subleading
\mathcal{D}^{(\delta^{+},\delta^{-})}(g)
evaluated at
a=1/4
can be easily obtained from the perturbative part:

\left.\mathcal{D}^{(\delta^{+},\delta^{-})}(g)\right|_{a=1/4}=\left.\mathcal{D}\left[\mathcal{I}_{n}^{(\delta^{+},\delta^{-})}\right](g)\right|_{a=1/4-\Delta}\,,

(95)

where
\mathcal{D}\left[{\mathcal{I}_{n}}\right](g)
coincides with the perturbative function in (
16
), and the integrals
\mathcal{I}_{n}^{(\delta^{+},\delta^{-})}
are given by (
94
), with
\mathcal{I}_{n}
expressed in (
91
) and (
92
).

Substituting these integrals into the perturbative function (
16
), evaluating them at
a=1/4-\Delta
with the appropriate shift, and summing them up according to (
90
), arbitrary non-perturbative correction can be determined to (
88
).

Now I turn to the special cases of

4
Resurgence relations

We see that (
69
) together with the recurrence relations (
3.2
) and (
3.2
) is extremely efficient to gain information about the large-
g
expansion of the determinant with matrix Bessel kernel, hence to compute the strong coupling expansion of several physical observables in supersymmetric gauge theories. Although the method presented above is powerful to obtain analytic results, by choosing specific values of
a
and
\ell
, higher order contributions with hundreds of
1/g
terms can be achieved numerically up to arbitrary exponential orders.

Using relation (
69
) and numerically evaluating
1/g
coefficients for the functions
\mathcal{D}^{(\delta^{+},\delta^{-})}(g)
with specific values of
a
and
\ell
, one finds that these coefficients are asymptotic or, in other words,
d_{k}^{(\delta^{+},\delta^{-})}
grow factorially for large values of
k
. This means that the transseries in (
51
) is only formal and a resurgence analysis
(
47
;
36
;
4
)
is essential to be able to resum the transeries and obtain a reasonable physical answer.

In
9
we have already investigated the resurgence properties of the determinant in representation (
8
). There we have studied the asymptotic behavior of the leading non-perturbative contributions with high precision numerical analysis. Although we found that certain contributions are connected via direct resurgence relations, it turned out that not all of them are related, and the reason of the absence of certain relations remained unknown. The structure of direct connections is illustrated in Figure 3. of
9
. In this section I will follow the ideas discussed in Appendix A to derive some basic resurgence properties for the restructured strong coupling expansion (
51
). I show that the asymptotic behavior of the non-perturbative functions and the analytic structure of their Borel transforms immediately follow from the transseries structure and that the new form of the expansion shed light to the resurgence structure found in
9
. With high precision numerical analysis, I verify these resurgence relations and by that, I give another strong evidence on the validity of (
51
).

Since in the transseries (
51
), the coupling constant
g
only appears together with a factor of
8\pi
, to avoid lengthy expressions, in the following I will use the notation:

\tilde{g}=8\pi g=2\sqrt{\lambda}\,.

(103)

In terms of the rescaled coupling constant
\tilde{g}
, with a slight abuse of notation, the strong coupling expansion takes the form:

\displaystyle Z_{\ell}(g)=A_{\ell}(g)

\displaystyle\sum_{\delta^{+},\delta^{-}}\tilde{g}^{-\Delta(\Delta-2a)}e^{-\tilde{g}\left(\sum_{l\in\delta^{+}}x_{l}^{+}+\sum_{j\in\delta^{-}}x_{j}^{-}\right)}e^{i\pi a\Delta}S^{(\delta^{+},\delta^{-})}\mathcal{D}^{(\delta^{+},\delta^{-})}(\tilde{g})\,.

(104)

\mathcal{D}^{(\delta^{+},\delta^{-})}(g)
given by the series:

\mathcal{D}^{(\delta^{+},\delta^{-})}(\tilde{g})=\sum_{k\geq 0}\frac{d_{k}^{(\delta^{+},\delta^{-})}}{\tilde{g}^{k}}\,,

(105)

with the same expansion coefficients
d_{k}^{(\delta^{+},\delta^{-})}
as before.

4.1
Stokes automorphism

The asymptotic behavior of the perturbative function
\mathcal{D}^{(\{\},\{\})}
is captured by the analytic properties of its Borel transform:

\mathcal{B}\left[\mathcal{D}^{\left(\{\},\{\}\right)}\right](s)=\sum_{k\geq 0}\frac{d_{k}^{(\{\},\{\})}s^{k}}{\Gamma(k+1)}\,.

(106)

For a convergent series of the form (
105
), the function
\mathcal{B}\left[\mathcal{D}^{\left(\{\},\{\}\right)}\right](s)
can be resummed, and its inverse, given by the integral (
132
) over the positive real line, gives back the original function.

However, since the coefficients
d_{k}^{(\{\},\{\})}
grow factorially with
k
, the function
\mathcal{B}\left[\mathcal{D}^{\left(\{\},\{\}\right)}\right](s)
produces singularities and cuts on the complex
s
plane, called the Borel plane. These singularities should be avoided while taking the invers transformation either below, or above the real axis. Therefore, one has to define the lateral Borel resummations:

\mathcal{S}_{\pm}\left[\mathcal{D}^{(\{\},\{\})}\right](\tilde{g})=\tilde{g}\int_{0}^{\infty e^{i\epsilon}}dse^{-\tilde{g}s}\mathcal{B}\left[\mathcal{D}^{(\{\},\{\})}\right](s)\,.

(107)

This means that there is an ambiguity in the resummation of
\mathcal{D}^{(\{\},\{\})}(\tilde{g})
. This ambiguity is the reason of the appearance of non-perturbative contributions in the transseries (
51
). The same argument holds for the higher order, exponentially small corrections as well.

The two lateral Borel resummations
\mathcal{S}_{+}
and
\mathcal{S}_{-}
are related by the Stokes automorphism
\mathfrak{S}
:

\mathcal{S}_{+}=\mathcal{S}_{-}\circ\mathfrak{S}\,.

(108)

Its logarithm is defined by the so called Alien derivatives
\Delta_{\omega}
:

\ln\mathfrak{S}=\sum_{\omega}e^{-\tilde{g}\omega}\Delta_{\omega}

(109)

where
\omega
denotes the singular points of (
106
) on the real line. The Alien derivatives describe the discontinouities of the Borel transform across the singular points
\omega
.

For a given transseries, such as (
51
), obtained from some system of integro-differential equations, the Alien derivatives can be computed via the Bridge equations
(
47
;
36
;
4
)
. If we assign a parameter
\sigma_{l}^{\pm}
to every distinct exponential weight
x_{l}^{\pm}
, we can define:

\displaystyle Z_{\ell}\left(g,\{\sigma^{\pm}\}\right)=A_{\ell}(\tilde{g})

\displaystyle\sum_{\delta^{+},\delta^{-}}\left(\prod_{l\in\delta^{+}}\sigma_{l}^{+}e^{-\tilde{g}x_{l}^{+}}\right)\left(\prod_{j\in\delta^{-}}\sigma_{j}^{-}e^{-\tilde{g}x_{j}^{-}}\right)\tilde{g}^{-\Delta(\Delta-2a)}e^{i\pi a\Delta}S^{(\delta^{+},\delta^{-})}\mathcal{D}^{(\delta^{+},\delta^{-})}(\tilde{g})

(110)

Then the Bridge equations connect the Alien derivatives of
Z_{\ell}\left(g,\{\sigma^{\pm}\}\right)
to ordinary differentials with respect to the parameters
\sigma_{l}^{\pm}
. In Appendix A, using the Bridge equations, I derive the Alien derivatives for a similar, but somewhat less complicated transseries than (
51
). It is simpler in the sense, that it contains only one set of exponential weights
\{x_{l}\}_{l\geq 0}
instead of having two sectors
\{x^{\pm}_{l}\}_{l\geq 0}
, and it does not contain the complex phases
e^{i\pi a\Delta}
. However, for the relevant values
0<a<1/2
, the physical weights can be ordered as
x_{0}^{+}<x_{0}^{-}<x_{1}^{+}<x_{1}^{-}<\dots
, therefore, we can assign
x_{2l}\to x^{+}_{l}
,
x_{2l+1}\to x^{-}_{l}
, and redefine
\mathcal{D}^{(\delta^{+},\delta^{-})}(\tilde{g})
by collecting its prefactors, so we arrive at the same transseries as in (
129
). The power
\lambda(\delta)
of the leading
1/g
terms is given by
-\Delta(\Delta-2a)
in this case.

Repeating the same argument as in Appendix A, one finds that the singular points of
\mathcal{B}\left[\mathcal{D}^{(\delta^{+},\delta^{-})}\right](s)
appear on the positive real line at
x^{\pm}_{j}
with
j\notin\delta^{+},\delta^{-}
and the corresponding Alien derivatives are:

\displaystyle\Delta^{+}_{j}\mathcal{D}^{(\delta^{+},\delta^{-})}(\tilde{g})=\tilde{g}^{-1-2\Delta+2a}A_{j}^{+}\frac{S^{(\delta^{+}\cup\{j\},\delta^{-})}}{S^{(\delta^{+},\delta^{-})}}\mathcal{D}^{(\delta^{+}\cup\{j\},\delta^{-})}(\tilde{g})\,,

\displaystyle\Delta^{-}_{j}\mathcal{D}^{(\delta^{+},\delta^{-})}(\tilde{g})=\tilde{g}^{-1+2\Delta-2a}A_{j}^{-}\frac{S^{(\delta^{+},\delta^{-}\cup\{j\})}}{S^{(\delta^{+},\delta^{-})}}\mathcal{D}^{(\delta^{+},\delta^{-}\cup\{j\})}(\tilde{g})\,.

(111)

The factors
A_{j}^{\pm}
are yet undetermined numbers, and for simplicity, I have introduced the notation
\Delta_{j}^{\pm}\equiv\Delta_{x^{\pm}_{j}}
. For
j\in\delta^{+}
or
j\in\delta^{-}
the Alien derivatives give zero.

From the Bridge equations it also follows that (
4.1
) is supplemented with the relations:

\left(\Delta^{\pm}_{j}\right)^{2}=0,\qquad\left[\Delta^{+}_{l},\Delta^{+}_{j}\right]=\left[\Delta^{-}_{l},\Delta^{-}_{j}\right]=\left[\Delta^{+}_{l},\Delta^{-}_{j}\right]=0\,,

(112)

for all
j,l\geq 0
integers.

Equations (
4.1
) and (
112
) mean that
\mathcal{D}^{(\delta^{+},\delta^{-})}(\tilde{g})
resurges only with the subleading corrections
\mathcal{D}^{(\delta^{+}\cup\{j\},\delta^{-})}(\tilde{g})
and
\mathcal{D}^{(\delta^{+},\delta^{-}\cup\{j\})}(\tilde{g})
(so that their exponential orders differ only by a factor of
e^{-\tilde{g}x^{\pm}_{j}}
). This is in accordance with the findings of
9
. There we found a direct resurgence relation, for example, between the
(n,m)=(0,0)
and
(n,m)=(2,1)
terms of (
8
), but not between the
(n,m)=(0,0)
and
(n,m)=(2,2)
contributions. The
(n,m)=(0,0)
perturbative part belongs to
(\delta^{+},\delta^{-})=(\{\},\{\})
in the (

) prescription. Due to (

The Alien algebra in (

4.2
Asymptotic analysis

In the following I illustrate how the Stokes automorphism (

\displaystyle d_{k}^{(\{\},\{\})}=\frac{A_{0}^{+}}{2\pi i}S^{(\{0\},\{\})}\sum_{j\geq 0}d_{k}^{(\{0\},\{\})}\frac{\Gamma(k-j-1+2a)}{(x_{0}^{+})^{k-j-1+2a}}+\dots\,,

4.3

The appearance of trigonometric factors

Appendix A
Bridge equations

In this appendix, based on
(
47
;
36
;
4
)
I derive the Alien algebra for a transseries of the form:

F(g)=\sum_{\delta}e^{-g\sum_{l\in\delta}x_{l}}g^{\lambda(\delta)}\Phi^{(\delta)}(g)\,,

(129)

with an infinite set of distinct exponential weights
x_{0}<x_{1}<\dots
. The summation goes over all possible unordered sets
\delta
of distinct non-negative integers. This means that the sum contains terms that are at most first order in each factor of
e^{-gx_{l}}
. The functions
\Phi^{(\delta)}
are given by the expansions:

\Phi^{(\delta)}(g)=\sum_{k\geq 0}\frac{\phi_{k}^{(\delta)}}{g^{k}}\,,

(130)

and
\lambda(\delta)
is a function of
\delta
that governs the leading
1/g
order at exponential level
\delta
. For simplicity in this Appendix I considered only one sector of exponential weights. Although the large-
g
expansion of the determinant
Z(g)
in (
51
) contains two sets of weights
x^{+}_{j}
and
x^{-}_{j}
, the argument below remains the same for
Z(g)
as well. The function
F(g)
with
\lambda(\delta)=0
and
g\to 8\pi g
coincides with the strong coupling expansions investigated in
11
.

The non-perturbative structure is deeply connected to the large order behavior of the coefficients
\phi_{k}^{(\delta)}
. This property is captured by the Borel transformation:

\mathcal{B}\left[\Phi^{(\delta)}\right](s)=\sum_{k\geq 0}\frac{\phi_{k}^{(\delta)}}{\Gamma(k+1)}s^{k}

(131)

If the series (
130
) is convergent, then its inverse is simply a Laplace transform:

\mathcal{S}\left[\Phi^{(\delta)}\right](g)=g\int_{0}^{\infty}dse^{-gs}\mathcal{B}\left[\Phi^{(\delta)}\right](s)\,.

(132)

and it gives back the original function
\Phi^{(\delta)}(g)
.

The problem arises if the coefficients
\phi_{k}^{(\delta)}
grow factorially, for example if they have the asymptotic form:

\phi_{k}^{(\delta)}=\frac{1}{\pi}\sum_{j\geq 0}c_{j}\frac{\Gamma(k+\lambda-j)}{A^{k+\lambda-j}}\,,

(133)

where
A
,
\lambda
and
c_{j}
(with
j\geq 0
) are different parameters of the expansion. In general, the expansion of
\phi_{k}^{(\delta)}
is a linear combination of terms similar to (
133
) with various sets of parameters.

In this case, the series in (
130
) cannot be resummed directly, only through its Borel transform. After the resummation of (
131
), its inverse (
132
) should be taken to compute the function
\Phi^{(\delta)}
. However, for an asymptotic series, the function (
131
) produces poles and cuts on the Borel plane, and the integration contour in (
132
) crosses these singularities. To avoid them, one has to shift the integration contour in (
132
) slightly below or above the real axis and define the lateral Borel summations:

\mathcal{S}_{\pm}\left[\Phi^{(\delta)}\right](g)=g\int_{0}^{\infty e^{i\epsilon}}dse^{-gs}\mathcal{B}\left[\Phi^{(\delta)}\right](s)\,.

(134)

For an asymptotic series, it produces an ambiguity in calculating the resummation of (
130
) and hence in computing the function
F(g)
. Due to this ambiguity, different terms in the expansion depend on the choice of regularization, and one has to choose either from:

F_{+}(g)=\sum_{\delta}e^{-g\sum_{l\in\delta}x_{l}}g^{\lambda(\delta)}\mathcal{S}_{+}\left[\Phi^{(\delta)}\right](g)\,,

(135)

or:

F_{-}(g)=\sum_{\delta}e^{-g\sum_{l\in\delta}x_{l}}g^{\lambda(\delta)}\mathcal{S}_{-}\left[\Phi^{(\delta)}\right](g)

(136)

to compute
F(g)
. We will see that due to the presence of exponentially small corrections, the ambiguities kill each other, and the resummation of
F(g)
becomes independent of the regularization, namely:
F_{+}(g)=F_{-}(g)
.

The difference between the two prescriptions in (
134
) contains all the information about the full discontinuity of
\mathcal{B}\left[\Phi^{(\delta)}\right](s)
along the real axis:

\mathcal{S}_{+}-\mathcal{S}_{-}=\mathcal{S}_{-}\circ\text{Disc}\,.

(137)

Therefore, it is convenient to introduce the so called Stokes automorphism
\mathfrak{S}
, which connects the two lateral resummations, and tells us how
\mathcal{B}\left[\Phi^{(\delta)}\right](s)
jumps across the real line:

\mathcal{S}_{+}=\mathcal{S}_{-}\circ\left(\mathbb{I}-\text{Disc}\right)\equiv\mathcal{S}_{-}\circ\mathfrak{S}\,.

(138)

For the simplest example, one can take the Borel transform (
131
) of
\Phi^{(\delta)}
, with its large
g
coefficients given in (
133
). Then it is straightforward to show that the function exhibits a singularity at
s=A
. The discontinouity across this singularity is:

\lim_{\epsilon\to 0}\left(\mathcal{B}[\Phi^{(\delta)}](s+i\epsilon)-\mathcal{B}[\Phi^{(\delta)}](s-i\epsilon)\right)=2i\theta(s-A)\sum_{j\geq 0}c_{j}\frac{(s-A)^{j-\lambda}}{\Gamma(1+j-\lambda)}\,.

(139)

Therefore, the difference between the lateral Borel resummations is:

\mathcal{S}_{+}[\Phi^{(\delta)}](g)-\mathcal{S}_{-}[\Phi^{(\delta)}](g)=2ig^{\lambda}e^{-gA}\sum_{j\geq 0}\frac{c_{j}}{g^{j}}\,.

(140)

From this simple example, we immediately see that for an asymptotic series, the exponential corrections are related to the ambiguity in the resummation. Since the discontinouity is directly related to the Stokes automorphism, if one can compute
\mathfrak{S}
for a transseries of the form (
129
), then the asymptotic expansions (namely the parameters
A
,
\lambda
and
c_{j}
) of the coefficients
\phi_{k}^{(\delta)}
can be determined.

As was mentioned previously, the asymptotic series in (
133
) could contain several similar terms, producing different singularities and cuts on the Borel plane. To separate the jumps along different cuts on the real line, it is useful to write the Stokes automorphism in terms of the Alien derivatives. By definition, they are related to
\mathfrak{S}
via:

\mathfrak{S}=\exp\left(\sum_{\omega}e^{-\omega g}\Delta_{\omega}\right)\,

(141)

where the summation runs along all the singular points
\omega
of the Borel transform.
\Delta_{\omega}
called a derivative, since it satisfies the Leibnitz rule.

The calculation of the Alien derivatives can be done via the so called Bridge equations. The first step is to assign a parameter
\sigma_{j}
to each exponential weight
x_{j}
in the transseries (
129
) and define:

F(g,\{\sigma\})=\sum_{\delta}\left(\prod_{l\in\delta}\sigma_{l}\right)e^{-g\sum_{l\in\delta}x_{l}}g^{\lambda(\delta)}\Phi^{(\delta)}(g)\,,

(142)

so every exponential factor
e^{-gx_{l}}
comes with a factor of
\sigma_{l}
. Now it can be shown (see
(
47
;
36
;
4
)

\dot{\Delta}_{\omega}=e^{-\omega g}\Delta_{\omega}

(143)

commutes with differentiation with respect to the variable

\sigma_{j}
, so that:

\left[\partial_{g},\partial_{\sigma_{j}}\right]=\left[\partial_{g},\dot{\Delta}_{\omega}\right]=0\,.

(144)

If we suppose that
F(g)
is a solution of any non-linear problem, it follows that
\partial_{\sigma_{j}}F(g,\{\sigma\})
(with every parameter
\sigma_{j}
) and
\dot{\Delta}_{\omega}F(g,\{\sigma\})
solve the same linear, homogeneous equation whose exact form depends on the original problem. As a consequence,
\dot{\Delta}_{\omega}
can be written as a linear combination of differentiations with respect to the parameters
\sigma_{j}
:

\dot{\Delta}_{\omega}=\sum_{l\geq 0}A_{\omega,l}\partial_{\sigma_{l}}\,,

(145)

where

are related to the Stoke constants.
This is called the Bridge equation and it relates the partial derivatives with respect to the resurgence parameters
\sigma_{j}
and the Alien derivatives
\Delta_{j}

Acting on

with (
143

\omega
