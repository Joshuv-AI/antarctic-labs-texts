De novo coassembly of functional chlorophyll- and carotenoid-protein complexes in Saccharomyces cerevisiae
Authors: Chenhao Li, Guangyu E. Chen
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Chlorophylls are essential for photosynthesis, yet their heterologous production is a major challenge in synthetic biology due to pathway complexity and the diversion of resources away from essential cofactor biosynthesis. Here, we reconstruct the complete chlorophyll biosynthetic pathway in Saccharomyces cerevisiae. By integrating 13 heterologous genes and deleting 3 endogenous genes, we engineered a yeast chassis capable of producing chlorophyll a at titers of 26 micrograms per gram of dry cell weight. This platform was further expanded with a ketocarotenoid biosynthetic module to produce echinenone and canthaxanthin, enabling the in vivo coassembly of two distinct pigment-protein complexes: the plant-derived water-soluble chlorophyll protein and the cyanobacterial orange carotenoid protein (Syn_OCP). Spectroscopic and biochemical analyses confirm that these yeast-assembled complexes retain native spectral and functional signatures, including Syn_OCP photoactivity, demonstrating high-fidelity pigment binding. Our work establishes a versatile eukaryotic platform for producing photosynthetic complexes, which could help open avenues for studying their assembly outside plants and for engineering light-driven metabolism in nonphotosynthetic eukaryotes.

INTRODUCTION

Photosynthesis powers the biosphere on Earth by using specialized pigments. At the heart of this process are chlorophyll (Chl) molecules, modified cyclic tetrapyrroles characterized by a centrally chelated magnesium ion and a distinctive isocyclic fifth ring. These structural features allow Chls to perform the essential tasks of light harvesting and charge separation (1). Given their central role, the biosynthesis of Chl remains a key aspect of photosynthesis research (2) and has become an important focus for metabolic engineering and synthetic biology applications (3–5). While we previously assembled a functional Chl biosynthetic pathway in a prokaryotic system using Escherichia coli (3, 4), establishing a eukaryotic Chl-producing platform is an essential next step. The baker’s yeast Saccharomyces cerevisiae offers a robust, versatile eukaryotic chassis, capable of supporting the high-level heterologous production of complex compounds. Its subcellular compartmentalization facilitates the tolerance to toxic metabolites, while its advanced protein-folding machinery and complex posttranslational modifications offer critical capabilities absent in E. coli. These features are essential for studying intricate, eukaryote-derived photosynthetic apparatuses.

The engineering of Chl biosynthesis in yeast must be meticulously integrated with the native tetrapyrrole pathway, which naturally terminates in heme (Fig. 1A) (6). This process begins with the synthesis of the universal precursor, 5-aminolevulinic acid (ALA), via the Shemin pathway, where ALA synthase (Hem1p) catalyzes the condensation of glycine and succinyl–coenzyme A (Suc-CoA). In a sequence of tightly regulated steps, ALA is converted into porphobilinogen (PBG) by PBG synthase (Hem2p), followed by polymerization into hydroxymethylbilane (HMB) via PBG deaminase (Hem3p). HMB is then cyclized to uroporphyrinogen III (UPGIII) by UPGIII synthase (Hem4p) and subsequently decarboxylated to coprophyrinogen III (CPGIII) by UPGIII decarboxylase (Hem12p). CPGIII undergoes oxidative decarboxylation by CPGIII oxidase (Hem13p) to produce protoporphyrinogen IX (PPGIX), which is then oxidized to protoporphyrin IX (PPIX) by PPGIX oxidase (Hem14p). Last, ferrochelatase (Hem15p) inserts Fe2+ into the macrocycle of PPIX to complete heme biosynthesis. Critically, this pathway is spatially partitioned (Fig. 1B): ALA synthesis, PPGIX oxidation, and iron insertion are localized in mitochondria, whereas all intervening steps take place in the cytosol.

Because PPIX serves as the common precursor for both heme and Chl biosynthesis, establishing a Chl biosynthesis pathway requires the installation of a branchpoint at PPIX in yeast to divert metabolic flux toward magnesium chelation (Fig. 1A) (2). The magnesium chelatase complex (CHLI/D/H and GUN4) inserts Mg2+ into PPIX to produce Mg-PPIX (MgP), the first committed Chl intermediate. Following MgP formation, a six-step enzymatic cascade completes the synthesis of mature Chl a. This begins with MgP methyltransferase (CHLM), which generates the substrate for MgP monomethyl ester (MgPME) cyclase. This cyclase forms the characteristic isocyclic fifth ring to yield 3,8-divinyl protochlorophyllide a (DV PChlide a), the first intermediate to exhibit a distinct green pigmentation. Subsequently, protochlorophyllide oxidoreductase (POR) reduces the C17═C18 double bond, followed by the reduction of the 8-vinyl group by divinyl reductase (DVR). The produced chlorophyllide a (Chlide a) is esterified with geranylgeranyl pyrophosphate (GGPP) by Chl synthase (CHLG) to form GG-Chl a. Last, GG reductase (CHLP) catalyzes the stepwise hydrogenation of the GG moiety into a phytyl chain, producing mature Chl a.

Beyond the primary role of Chls, all natural photosynthetic systems depend on carotenoids to serve as auxiliary light-harvesting enhancers, critical photoprotective agents, and “molecular glue” that provides structural stability to larger complexes (7–10). Derived from the universal isoprenoid precursor GGPP, carotenoids are diverse, with more than 1100 variants identified in nature to date (11). Many of these pigments have already been heterologously produced in yeast with high yields, especially for β-carotene and its derivatives (12). To truly mimic natural photosynthetic systems, it is necessary to cosynthesize carotenoids and Chls. Furthermore, because these pigments are inherently hydrophobic, they do not function in isolation; instead, they operate exclusively within the specialized microenvironments of pigment-protein complexes (13–15). Consequently, a robust synthetic biology platform for photosynthesis must transcend simple pigment production to incorporate the specific apoproteins necessary for pigment sequestration and functionality. Despite the challenge of installing light-dependent metabolism into nonphotosynthetic organisms, recent progress has been made by Tong and coauthors, who built a photosystem incorporating the Chl precursor MgP, enabling E. coli to harness light energy for ATP and NADH regeneration (16).

In this study, we have successfully reconstructed the complex, multistep biosynthetic pathways for both Chl a and two ketocarotenoids, echinenone (ECN) and canthaxanthin (CAN), within S. cerevisiae. We demonstrate that these pigments can be assembled in vivo into two distinct model architectures: a plant-derived Chl–containing complex and a cyanobacterial carotenoid-protein complex. Biochemical and spectroscopic analyses verify that these yeast-assembled complexes retain their native spectral and functional signatures. Our work establishes yeast as a versatile eukaryotic platform for investigating the assembly of photosynthetic apparatuses and may help inform future efforts to engineer light-dependent metabolism in nonphotosynthetic eukaryotic organisms.

RESULTS AND DISCUSSION

Engineering the Chl biosynthesis pathway in yeast

We selected the S. cerevisiae CEN.PK2-1C strain (17), a well-established chassis for natural product biosynthesis, as the host for engineering the Chl biosynthesis pathway. To avoid plasmid-associated limitations including constrained gene capacity, dependence of selection markers, and expression heterogeneity, we opted for a chromosomal integration strategy. Individual gene expression cassettes were designed by flanking the gene of interest with a constitutive promoter and terminator sourced from a characterized yeast toolkit (18). By synergizing the DNA assembler (19) and CRISPRm (20) methods, we achieved the simultaneous in vivo assembly of donor DNA and its seamless integration into specific genomic loci in a single step.

PPIX serves as a common precursor for both heme and Chl biosynthesis (Fig. 1A). Although PPIX is natively produced in yeast mitochondria by PPGIX oxidase (Hem14p) (Fig. 1B), we chose to localize Chl biosynthesis in the cytosol due to four critical considerations: (i) multiple Chl biosynthetic steps depend on NADPH, which is predominantly cytosolic rather than mitochondrial; (ii) ATP produced in mitochondria is rapidly exported to the cytosol (21), maintaining a high cytosolic ATP:ADP ratio that can ensure ample energy supply for Chl biosynthesis; (iii) Chl intermediates are highly hydrophobic and could destabilize the densely packed protein complexes of the mitochondrial inner membrane; and (iv) Chl intermediates are phototoxic, posing a severe risk to the delicate respiratory complexes and membrane lipids of mitochondria. We sourced genes primarily from Arabidopsis thaliana (hereafter Arabidopsis), a well-established model plant, to engineer the Chl biosynthesis pathway in yeast. To enable cytosolic PPIX synthesis, we heterologously expressed the Arabidopsis gene encoding PPGIX oxidase (At_PPO) in yeast without its chloroplast transit peptide (cTP) coding sequence (see table S1 for information of genes used in this study). We also introduced genes encoding the Arabidopsis Mg-chelatase complex (At_CHLI/D/H and At_GUN4) and MgP methyltransferase (At_CHLM) omitting the cTP into the At_PPO-expressing strain to create strain GY1 (see Fig. 2A for construction of yeast strains GY1–27; see table S2 for genotypes of all yeast strains used in this study). With the first two committed steps of Chl biosynthesis installed, GY1 was expected to accumulate the Chl intermediate MgPME. However, analysis of pigments by high-performance liquid chromatography (HPLC) showed that no MgPME was detected in GY1 (Fig. 2B).

The absence of MgPME production in strain GY1 may be attributed to the sequestration of the common precursor, PPIX, by the endogenous heme biosynthesis pathway. The transcription factor Rox1p is a repressor of hypoxic genes and also inhibits the expression of HEM13 (22), which encodes CPGIII oxidase converting CPGIII to PPGIX. It has been shown that disruption of ROX1 induces high levels of CPGIII oxidase activity (23), resulting in elevated cellular heme levels (24). We deleted ROX1 in GY1 to yield strain GY2 but still could not detect any MgPME (Fig. 2B). Kachroo and coauthors showed that each yeast heme biosynthesis enzyme can be replaced by its E. coli equivalent, irrespective of orthology or localization (25). They also found that replacing yeast mitochondrial ferrochelatase (Hem15p) with the E. coli counterpart (Ec_HemH) without adding a mitochondrial targeting sequence (MTS) causes cytosolic PPIX accumulation and a pink phenotype due to secreted PPIX (25). We adopted this strategy to construct strain GY3, which accumulated MgPME (Fig. 2B). To assess the necessity of At_PPO and the rate-limiting role of PBG deaminase (26, 27), we modified GY3 either by deleting the At_PPO gene (strain GY4) or replacing it with one of the two PBG deaminase encoding genes: yeast HEM3 (strain GY5) or E. coli hemC (strain GY6). Pigment analysis revealed that GY5 and GY6 performed similarly with respect to MgPME production, and both were superior to GY3 and GY4 (Fig. 2B and fig. S1A). We chose GY5 as the MgPME-producing platform strain.

To convert MgPME into DV PChlide a, we constructed strain GY7 by introducing the O2-dependent MgPME cyclase from the purple bacterium Rubrivivax gelatinosus (Rg_AcsF), which is the simplest type of the enzyme (28) and was successfully used for assembly of the Chl biosynthesis pathway in E. coli (3, 4). DV PChlide a was detected in GY7 but at limited levels (Fig. 2C). To enhance the Rg_AcsF activity, we coexpressed the electron transfer components from the cyanobacterium Anabaena sp. PCC 7119 (hereafter Anabaena), ferredoxin (Fd; Ana_PetF) and Fd:NADP+ reductase (FNR; Ana_PetH), to ensure effective reduction of the AcsF di-iron center. The resulting strain, GY8, exhibited a slight but not significant increase in DV PChlide a levels (Fig. 2C and fig. S1B). To meet the iron demand for cofactor assembly of Rg_AcsF and Ana_Fd, we deleted the CCC1 gene (strain GY9) to prevent vacuolar iron sequestration and increase the availability of cytosolic ferrous ions (29). This modification substantially boosted the production (Fig. 2C and fig. S1B), establishing GY9 as the optimized synthesis platform for DV PChlide a.

DV PChlide a can be reduced to form DV Chlide a by a light-dependent POR enzyme, LPOR, or by a mechanistically distinct dark-operative enzyme, DPOR. We first evaluated the light-independent route by expressing the Rhodobacter sphaeroides DPOR complex (Rs_BchN/B/L), which was successfully used for engineering the Chl biosynthesis pathway in E. coli (4). The resulting strain, GY10, accumulated only DV PChlide a, with no detectable conversion to DV Chlide a (Fig. 2D). This lack of DPOR activity is likely due to its complex multisubunit architecture that requires precise assembly (30) and the inherent O2-sensitivity of its essential iron-sulfur clusters (31). We next explored the light-dependent route by introducing the Arabidopsis LPOR (At_LPOR) to generate strain GY11. We also made strain GY12 to test the Synechocystis sp. PCC 6803 (hereafter Synechocystis) LPOR (Syn_Lpor), which proved effective in installing the Chl biosynthesis pathway in E. coli (3). Both strains were exposed to dim white light during incubation to activate LPOR. Pigment analysis confirmed DV Chlide a production in both strains (Fig. 2D), demonstrating functional LPOR in yeast. Due to its superior production of DV Chlide a compared with GY11, the Syn_lpor-expressing GY12 strain was selected as the platform in which to complete the Chl biosynthesis pathway (Fig. 2D and fig. S1C).

Next, we introduced cTP-free versions of the final three Chl biosynthetic enzymes from Arabidopsis—DVR (At_DVR), Chl synthase (At_CHLG), and GG reductase (At_CHLP)—into GY12 to create strain GY13. The strain successfully produced GG-Chl a; however, no mature Chl a was detected (Fig. 2E). This result indicated a lack of functional GG reductase, the enzyme responsible for the three successive hydrogenation steps required to reduce the GG moiety to phytol (Fig. 1A). To resolve this bottleneck, we screened various GG reductases, including the Rba. sphaeroides BchP (Rs_BchP), Rvi. gelatinosus BchP (Rg_BchP), Synechocystis ChlP (Syn_ChlP), all of which were available for gene cloning from our laboratory strain stocks, as well as a kinetically enhanced variant (I206F/L377H) of the GG reductase from the archaeon Sulfolobus acidocaldarius (Sa_Ggr*) (32). We used the native genes for Rs_BchP, Rg_BchP, and Syn_ChlP and a yeast codon-optimized sequence for Sa_Ggr* to construct strains GY14, GY15, GY16, and GY17, respectively. Pigment analysis by HPLC revealed that only Syn_ChlP exhibited detectable activity, whereas all other enzymes failed to reduce the double bonds of the GG moiety (Fig. 2E). Because complete reduction of the GG moiety to phytol requires three molecules of NADPH, we hypothesized that the cytosolic NADPH might be limiting. In yeast, NADPH is regenerated primarily via the cytosolic glucose-6-phosphate dehydrogenase (Zwf1p) (33) and the mitochondrial NADH kinase (Pos5p) (34, 35). In addition, the transcription factor Stb5p is known to upregulate NADPH production by activating ZWF1 and other genes involved in NADPH production (36). It has been shown that moderate STB5 overexpression using a weak promoter can boost heterologous lycopene production in yeast (37). We introduced STB5 under control of the ADH1 promoter (PADH1) in GY16, generating strain GY18. Contrary to expectations, this modification severely impaired growth, preventing analysis of the pigment content in GY18. This growth inhibition may result from a redox imbalance caused by excess NADPH (38, 39). To enhance the cytosolic NADPH supply through alternative approaches, we modified GY16 by overexpressing wild-type (WT) ZWF1 (strain GY19), a hyperactive variant (N403D) of ZWF1 (ZWF1*; strain GY20) (40), or a truncated POS5 (lacking its MTS coding sequence; tPOS5) to redirect this NADH kinase to the cytosol (41) (strain GY21). All three modifications improved Syn_ChlP activity, as evidenced by higher levels of the first hydrogenation intermediate, dihydrogeranylgeranyl (DHGG)–Chl a, relative to GG-Chl a (Fig. 2E). However, all strains continued to accumulate a vast excess of GG-Chl a (Fig. 2E), with no significant increase in Chl a levels (fig. S1D). These results indicate that, while increasing NADPH regeneration partially alleviated the bottleneck, it was insufficient to drive the complete three-step reduction to synthesize mature Chl a.

In Arabidopsis, the stability and activity of GG reductase (At_CHLP) depend on its interaction with the light-harvesting-like protein 3 (LIL3) (42), which is thought to anchor the enzyme to the thylakoid membrane. Loss of LIL3 results in massive GG-Chl a accumulation, which can be partially rescued by fusing the LIL3 transmembrane (TM) domain to the C terminus of At_CHLP (43). To replicate this architecture in yeast, we first introduced the Arabidopsis LIL3:1 and LIL3:2 (At_LIL3:1/2) genes into our At_CHLP- and Syn_chlP-expressing strains, resulting in strains GY22 and GY23, respectively. These two strains showed some improvement: GY22 produced trace amounts of DHGG-Chl a, whereas GY23 accumulated higher levels of DHGG-Chl a relative to GG-Chl a but with no significant increase in Chl a levels (Fig. 2E and fig. S1D). The marginal improvements were likely constrained by the poor functional expression of plant membrane proteins in yeast. To bypass the need for LIL3, we engineered direct membrane localization of GG reductases by C-terminal fusion of TM domains. Specifically, we adopted the TM domain from the Arabidopsis l-ascorbate peroxidase T (At_APXT) (44), previously shown to effectively anchor At_CHLP to membranes (43), to construct strains GY24 and GY25, expressing At_CHLP-TMAt_APXT and Syn_ChlP-TMAt_APXT, respectively. Pigment analysis showed that Chl a became the predominant product in GY25, with GG-Chl a and its hydrogenation intermediates reduced to negligible levels (Fig. 2E). The membrane tethering also slightly improved the activity of At_CHLP as trace DHGG-Chl a can be detected in GY24, at levels similar to that in GY22 (Fig. 2E). We further evaluated the versatility of our membrane-targeting strategy by using the TM domain from the endogenous yeast SNARE (soluble N-ethylmaleimide–sensitive factor attachment protein receptor) protein, Snc1p (45). We constructed strains GY26 and GY27, expressing At_CHLP-TMSnc1p and Syn_ChlP-TMSnc1p, respectively. Both strains exhibited similar performance comparable to their At_APXT-tethered counterparts (Fig. 2E and fig. S1D). Western blot analysis revealed that At_CHLP was not detectable in the engineered yeast strains (fig. S2A), whereas Syn_ChlP was readily detected (fig. S2A), exclusively localized to membranes, and markedly enhanced upon fusion with a TM domain derived from either At_APXT or Snc1p (fig. S2B). These results suggest that achieving membrane localization, rather than the specific TM sequence, is the primary requirement for GG reductase activity in yeast. We selected GY27, using the Synechocystis ChlP fused to the TM domain of native yeast Snc1p, as our final Chl a–producing platform.

Our engineered GY27 strain incorporates a total of 14 additional genes, including 13 genes sourced from Arabidopsis (7 genes), E. coli (1 gene), Rvi. gelatinosus (1 gene), Anabaena (2 genes), and Synechocystis (2 genes), and 3 deletions of yeast regulatory and biosynthetic genes to achieve de novo Chl a biosynthesis in yeast. We calculated the productivity of Chl a in GY27 under the tested conditions to be 25.9 ± 3.8 μg g−1 dry cell weight (DCW), which corresponds to 2.53 ± 0.37 × 105 molecules per cell. We used fluorescence microscopy to determine the spatial distribution of Chl a in yeast cells and confirmed that the Chl a molecules were predominantly accumulated in the cytosolic lipid droplets (Fig. 3), likely driven by the pigment’s inherent hydrophobicity. Compared to native hosts, such as Synechocystis that contains 1.4 × 107 Chl a molecules per cell (46) and a plant chloroplast that contains ∼4.8 × 108 Chl a molecules per chloroplast (47) (see table S3 for a comparison across species), the yield of our engineered yeast strain is low. To increase Chl yield in yeast, future efforts should focus on three aspects: (i) increasing PPIX flux toward Chl biosynthesis, given competition from the native heme pathway; (ii) enhancing the activity of rate-limiting enzymes (e.g., the cyclase) via enzyme variants from other sources and improved cofactor supply; and (iii) boosting yeast lipid biosynthesis or engineering phototroph-derived lipid pathway to accommodate newly synthesized Chl molecules.

Combination of the Chl and carotenoid biosynthesis pathways in yeast

Following the successful engineering of the Chl biosynthesis pathway in yeast, we sought to determine whether Chl could be cosynthesized with carotenoids in this heterotrophic host, which would mimic natural photosynthetic organisms. We introduced the biosynthesis pathway for the carotenoid β-carotene (Fig. 4) into the Chl a–producing strain, GY27, by overexpressing carotenogenic genes from the bacterium Pantoea. agglomerans, encoding phytoene synthase (Pa_CrtB), phytoene desaturase (Pa_CrtI), and lycopene β-cyclase (Pa_CrtY) (see Fig. 5A for construction of yeast strains GY28–31). The resulting strain, GY28, was able to synthesize β-carotene but exhibited low productivity (Fig. 5B), likely due to competition for GGPP; this metabolic intermediate is essential for endogenous yeast processes and serves as a critical substrate for both of our engineered Chl and carotenoid biosynthesis pathways. To alleviate this constraint and enhance GGPP availability, we constructed strain GY29 by overexpressing the P. agglomerans crtE gene (Pa_crtE) encoding GGPP synthase alongside a truncated variant of the yeast HMG1 (tHMG1) encoding 3-hydroxy-3-methylglutaryl–coenzyme A (HMG-CoA) reductase (48). By removing the N-terminal regulatory domain (residues 1 to 552) of HMG-CoA reductase while retaining its soluble catalytic domain, we bypassed the native feedback inhibition of this enzyme (49). This modification was intended to increase metabolic flux toward mevalonate (50), the key precursor for yeast isoprenoids, thereby supporting the high demand of our dual-pigment biosynthetic systems. Pigment analysis revealed that GY29 achieved substantially enhanced β-carotene production (Fig. 5B) and increased levels of Chl a biosynthesis (Fig. 5C). GY29 accumulated Chl a at titers of 35.1 ± 5.3 μg g−1 DCW (equivalent to 3.42 ± 0.51 × 105 molecules per cell), a 35% increase compared with that in GY27.

Building upon this β-carotene-producing platform, we extended the carotenoid pathway to synthesize CAN, a diketocarotenoid. The conversion of β-carotene to CAN proceeds via the successive addition of two keto groups at the 4 and 4′ positions of the β-ionone rings (Fig. 4). This reaction is catalyzed by either CrtW or CrtO β-carotene ketolases and occurs through the mono-ketone intermediate, ECN (51, 52). We used a yeast codon-optimized sequence encoding the CrtW ketolase from the bacterium Brevundimonas vesicularis (Bv_CrtW) (53). This gene was integrated into GY29 to generate strain GY30 to enable β-carotene ketolation. Pigment analysis showed that GY30 produced ECN and CAN (Fig. 5B). We calculated the productivity of ECN and CAN in GY30 under the tested conditions to be 3.6 ± 0.2 μg g−1 DCW (0.57 ± 0.04 × 105 molecules per cell) and 24.6 ± 0.7 μg g−1 DCW (3.80 ± 0.11 × 105 molecules per cell), respectively. Notably, the introduction of Bv_CrtW did not alter Chl a productivity substantially, which remained stable at 38.5 ± 6.2 μg g−1 DCW (3.76 ± 0.60 × 105 molecules per cell), demonstrating the robustness of our dual-pigment biosynthesis yeast platform. The pigment yields of our engineered yeast strain are compared with those of several native and engineered organisms, as shown in table S3.

We further validated the performance of GY27 and GY30 by performing triplicate fermentation experiments under dark and illuminated conditions, alongside the control strain CEN.PK2-1C. Cell growth [optical density at 600 nm (OD600)], glucose consumption, and pigment production were monitored at multiple time points throughout the fermentation. Cell growth of GY27 and GY30 was similar and slightly slower than that of CEN.PK2-1C (fig. S3, A and B). Both GY27 and GY30 reached a plateau at an OD600 of ∼8 by 24 hours, whereas CEN.PK2-1C continued to grow, reaching an OD600 of ∼12 by the end of fermentation (72 hours). The growth profiles of all three strains were comparable under both dark and illuminated conditions. Glucose consumption patterns were also similar across strains, with glucose being fully exhausted by 24 hours (fig. S3, A and B). Chl a was detected at low levels in GY27 and GY30 under dark conditions, which is likely due to unavoidable light exposure during experiments (fig. S3C). Under illumination, Chl a levels rose rapidly between 8 and 24 hours and then decreased due to degradation (fig. S3D). GY30 consistently accumulated more Chl a than GY27, reaching a maximum of 52.4 ± 1.0 μg liter−1 compared to 35.2 ± 1.7 μg liter−1 (fig. S3D). Both ECN and CAN levels increased throughout the fermentation under dark and illuminated conditions, with the rate of accumulation diminishing after 40 hours (fig. S3, E and F). Levels were modestly lower under illumination: ECN reached 32.8 ± 0.8 μg liter−1 compared to 39.7 ± 2.0 μg liter−1 in the dark, whereas CAN reached 126.5 ± 4.2 μg liter−1 compared to 132.9 ± 5.6 μg liter−1 in the dark (fig. S3, E and F).

Coassembly of functional Chl- and carotenoid-protein complexes in yeast

Having establishing GY30 as a platform for the cosynthesis of Chl a and carotenoids, we next investigated whether these pigments could be incorporated into apoproteins to form functional pigment-protein complexes. Although native photosynthetic apparatuses typically harbor a heterogeneous mixture of Chls and carotenoids, we adopted a simplified strategy by focusing on complexes that bind Chl a or a single carotenoid species exclusively. This approach enabled independent analysis of the binding and functional states of each pigment in yeast. Furthermore, we prioritized benchmark complexes for these initial trials, allowing us to evaluate the assembly fidelity in yeast using established biochemical and spectroscopic criteria. We selected two well-characterized pigment-protein complexes to assemble in yeast, including the water-soluble Chl protein (WSCP) of higher plants and the cyanobacterial orange carotenoid protein (OCP) (see Fig. 6, A and B, for structures of the cauliflower WSCP and Synechocystis OCP, respectively). WSCP represents a unique class of hydrophilic, carotenoid-free pigment-protein complexes, functioning as a molecular “sponge” to bind free Chl and to prevent phototoxicity during stress-induced cellular disruption (54, 55). WSCP is an ideal Chl-binding scaffold due to its structural simplicity and high affinity for Chl a. OCP serves as a specialized photoreceptor central to cyanobacterial photoprotection (56) and binds a single ketocarotenoid—such as ECN, 3′-OH-ECN, or CAN—where the 4-keto group of the β-ionone ring is indispensable for its photoactivity (57). The spectroscopic signatures and light-activation mechanism of OCP are well-documented (58–60), making it a perfect benchmark for carotenoid incorporation.

We synthesized yeast codon-optimized sequences encoding the Brassica oleracea WSCP (Bo_WSCP) and the Synechocystis OCP (Syn_OCP). The construct for Bo_WSCP was engineered for cytosolic expression by removing the native N-terminal 19-residue signal peptide (responsible for targeting to the endoplasmic reticulum body) (61) and the C-terminal 21-residue extension peptide (62). An N-terminal Strep-tag II was also added to enable Western blot detection and affinity purification of the holo-Bo_WSCP complex. For Syn_OCP, we inserted a nine-residue sequence (GSSHHHHHH) immediately following the start methionine residue. This modification has been previously demonstrated to enhance protein solubility, overall yield, and the carotenoid loading efficiency of Syn_OCP (63). The integrated His6 tag also facilitates the detection and affinity purification of the holo-Syn_OCP complex. These constructs were integrated into the pigment production platform GY30 to generate strain GY31. Preliminary characterization confirmed the production of both Bo_WSCP and Syn_OCP polypeptides. Pigment analysis indicated that the heterologous expression of these two apoproteins did not perturb the carotenoid and Chl a biosynthesis (Fig. 5, B and C), ensuring an ample supply of pigments for in vivo complex assembly. GY31 strain accumulated Chl a, ECN, and CAN at levels of 49.1 ± 3.7 μg g−1 DCW (4.79 ± 0.36 × 105 molecules per cell), 3.5 ± 0.1 μg g−1 DCW (0.56 ± 0.02 × 105 molecules per cell), and 29.3 ± 1.4 μg g−1 DCW (4.52 ± 0.22 × 105 molecules per cell), respectively. The incorporation of pigment-binding proteins appears to exert a strong metabolic pull, enhancing the biosynthesis of Chl a and CAN compared with the pigment-only strain GY30 by 27 and 19%, respectively. Fermentation experiments revealed that GY31 and the pigment-only strain GY30 were indistinguishable in cell growth and glucose consumption under both dark and illuminated conditions (fig. S3, A and B), and their ECN production was similar as well (fig. S3, E and F). However, GY31 accumulated more CAN than GY30 at all time points in the dark (fig. S3E). Under illuminated conditions, CAN levels in GY31 tracked almost identically to GY30 until 32 hours, after which slight degradation occurred (fig. S3F). Notably, GY31 accumulated more Chl a than GY30 at all time points and showed substantially less degradation, retaining a level of 46.7 ± 10.6 μg liter−1 by the end of fermentation (72 hours) (fig. S3D). This is likely attributable to Bo_WSCP-mediated binding and protection of the pigment.

We next sought to determine whether these pigments were correctly incorporated into their respective protein scaffolds through a coordinated purification strategy using a single batch of GY31 cells. Syn_OCP was captured from the clarified cell-free lysate of GY31 using Ni–iminodiacetic acid (IDA) affinity chromatography with the flow-through reserved for purification of Bo_WSCP. The resulting orange fractions were pooled and purified via anion exchange and size exclusion chromatography (SEC). SDS–polyacrylamide gel electrophoresis (PAGE) and Western blot analysis confirmed a prominent Coomassie-stained band at the predicted molecular weight (MW) of Syn_OCP (35.7 kDa), with identity verified by anti-His antibody reactivity (Fig. 6C). Stoichiometric pigment analysis revealed that the purified Syn_OCP was nearly 100% loaded, containing one carotenoid molecule per protein monomer (table S4). To verify the photoactivity of the yeast-assembled complex, we monitored its spectral response to intense white light. Upon illumination, the complex underwent a characteristic bathochromic shift, with its absorption maxima shifting from 476 and 501 to 527 nm (Fig. 6D), corresponding to the conversion of Syn_OCP from its inactive orange state (Syn_OCPO) to the active red state (Syn_OCPR) (64). This transition was fully reversible; the complex spontaneously reverted to the orange state after a 10-min dark incubation at 37°C (Fig. 6D). These spectra are in excellent agreement with those reported for Syn_OCP purified from both native cyanobacterial and heterologous hosts (63, 64). Furthermore, SEC analysis showed that Syn_OCPO eluted as a single monomeric peak with an apparent MW (MWapp) of 39 kDa (Fig. 6E). In contrast, light-activated Syn_OCPR exhibited a major peak at 96 kDa and a minor peak at 31 kDa; the profile reverted to a single 38-kDa peak upon dark recovery (Fig. 6E). These light-induced changes in oligomeric state match established benchmarks for OCP conformational transitions (65). Collectively, these data demonstrate that the yeast-derived Syn_OCP correctly binds ketocarotenoid and maintains full photochemical functionality.

We used Strep-Tactin affinity chromatography to capture Bo_WSCP from the flow-through reserved during Syn_OCP purification. The resulting green fractions were pooled and subjected to heat treatment (95°C for 15 min); whereas contaminating proteins precipitated, Bo_WSCP remained soluble due to its exceptional thermostability (66). This step enhanced purity substantially, as evidenced by a prominent Coomassie-stained band at the predicted MW of Bo_WSCP (20.5 kDa) on SDS-PAGE, which cross-reacted with an anti–Strep-tag II antibody (Fig. 6F). The absorption spectrum of purified Bo_WSCP displayed characteristic maxima at 341, 383, 421, 435, 628, and 672 nm (Fig. 6G). These values align closely with previously reported spectra for both native and reconstituted Bo_WSCP (61, 66, 67). The functional state of the bound pigment was further validated by fluorescence spectroscopy; excitation at 430 nm produced a sharp emission peak at 675 nm (Fig. 6G), a hallmark of Chl a within a correctly folded protein environment. SEC analysis revealed a single peak with an MWapp of 65 kDa (Fig. 6H). Although the MWapp is slightly below the theoretical value for a Bo_WSCP tetramer, the elution profile remains consistent with the observation that Chl binding is exclusive to the tetrameric state (68). Together, the exceptional thermostability and precise spectral signatures confirm the successful assembly of functional, Chl-containing Bo_WSCP complexes within the yeast heterologous host.

Concluding remarks

In this study, we have reconstructed a Chl biosynthesis pathway in the yeast S. cerevisiae, extending the production of this fundamental photosynthetic pigment into a model heterotrophic eukaryote. This represents a landmark in metabolic engineering as it necessitated overcoming bottlenecks including competition for the PPIX precursor, the management of cytosolic iron availability, and the strategic membrane-anchoring of the GG reductase. By integrating a complete carotenoid pathway, we have developed a versatile pigment-production platform capable of stable cosynthesis of Chl a and the ketocarotenoids ECN and CAN. We further demonstrated the utility of this platform through the in vivo coassembly of two distinct pigment-protein complexes: the plant-derived Chl-containing Bo_WSCP and the cyanobacterial carotenoid-containing Syn_OCP. Biochemical purification and spectroscopic characterization confirmed that both yeast-assembled complexes retain their native spectral and functional signatures. Notably, the purified OCP exhibits its characteristic orange-to-red photoactivation cycle, proving the high-fidelity assembly of the complex. Together, these results establish yeast as a robust and powerful platform for the heterologous synthesis of photosynthetic pigments and their associated pigment-protein complexes. This work provides a basis for future assembly of more sophisticated, membrane-associated light-harvesting apparatuses and reaction centers and may help advance efforts to engineer solar energy utilization in heterotrophic hosts.

MATERIALS AND METHODS

Bacterial strains

Bacterial strains used in this study are listed in table S5. E. coli strains were grown at 37°C in LB medium. If required, antibiotics were added at 30 μg ml−1 for kanamycin and 100 μg ml−1 for ampicillin. Rvi. gelatinosus strains were grown in polypeptone–yeast extract–sodium succinate medium (69) at 30°C. Synechocystis strains were grown in BG-11 medium (70) buffered with 10 mM Tes (pH 8.2, adjusted by KOH) at 30°C with continuous illumination.

DNA manipulation

DNA oligonucleotides used in this study are listed in table S6. The nucleotide sequences of synthesized genes are listed in table S7. S. cerevisiae promoters and terminators used in this study are listed in table S8. The S. cerevisiae CEN.PK2-1C (17) cells were used as template for polymerase chain reaction (PCR) amplification of the endogenous yeast genes, constitutive promoters, terminators, and homologous arms for genomic loci. The Arabidopsis PPO, CHLI/D/H/M/P/G, GUN4, LPOR, DVR, and LIL3:1/2 genes without the cTP coding sequence and the APXT TM domain encoding sequence were PCR amplified from Arabidopsis cDNA. The E. coli hemH and hemC genes were PCR amplified using the E. coli DH5α cells as template. The acsF and bchP genes from Rvi. gelatinosus, the bchN/B/L/P genes from Rba. sphaeroides, and the lpor and chlP genes from Synechocystis were PCR amplified from their respective genomic DNA templates. The Anabaena petF and petH genes were PCR amplified from the pET28a-Fd and pET28a-FNR plasmids (71), respectively. The P. agglomerans crtB/I/Y/E genes were PCR amplified from the pAC-BETA plasmid (72). Gene sequences encoding the Sulfolobus acidocaldarius GG reductase variant (I206F/L377H), Brevundimonas vesicularis CrtW, Brassica oleracea WSCP (lacking the N-terminal 19 and C-terminal 21 residues; with an N-terminal Strep-tag II), and Synechocystis OCP (with a GSSHHHHHH insertion) were synthesized by GENEWIZ or Sangon Biotech with codons optimized for expression in S. cerevisiae and used as template for PCR to generate gene expression cassettes. The hyperactive variant (N403D) of ZWF1 was generated via overlap extension PCR (OE-PCR) using primers incorporated the mutation. OE-PCR was used to fuse the Arabidopsis CHLP and Synechocystis chlP genes with a sequence encoding the Arabidopsis APXT or S. cerevisiae Snc1p TM domain. A sequence encoding a GGGGS linker was included at the junction in the TMSnc1p fusion constructs.

The Cas9–single guide RNA expression plasmid pWS175 was a gift from T. Ellis (Addgene, plasmid no. 90962) and was used for CRISPR-Cas9–mediated genomic editing in yeast. Gene expression cassettes were targeted for integration at several auxotrophic loci (ura3, leu2, his3, his2, and trp1), and a suite of characterized neutral sites, including the nonessential genes pdc6 (73) and ho (74), and the intergenic regions int11 (75), s2, and s6 (76). For a genomic locus, guide sequences targeting 5′-NGG-3′ PAMs were selected using the CRISPOR tool (https://crispor.gi.ucsc.edu/) (77). Overlapping oligonucleotides containing the 20–base pair (bp) guide sequence and Bsm BI–compatible overhangs were synthesized and annealed to form dsDNA and ligated into Bsm BI–linearized pWS175, yielding the pWS175-locus plasmids.

We used the DNA assembler method to exploit the yeast homologous recombination machinery for in vivo assembly of multiple DNA fragments (19). For each targeted genomic locus, one to three expression cassettes were cotransformed alongside flanking homologous arms to generate a single, linear donor DNA molecule directly within the yeast cell. For integration of gene expression cassettes, the high-copy pUC18 vector was used to house intermediate fragments for subsequent in vivo yeast assembly. Individual gene expression cassettes—comprising a constitutive promoter, a gene, and a terminator—were generated via OE-PCR with ∼60-bp flanking homology regions. These cassettes, along with ∼500-bp upstream and downstream genomic targeting regions, were individually cloned into the Eco RI/Hind III sites of pUC18 by seamless assembly, resulting in the pUC18-gene, pUC18-locusUP, and pUC18-locusDOWN plasmids. To prepare the donor DNA for yeast electroporation, the respective fragments were excised from the pUC-based plasmids via digestion. Following electrophoresis, the target DNA fragments were excised from the agarose gel and purified using the QIAquick Gel Extraction Kit (QIAGEN). For gene deletions, donor DNA was prepared by annealing overlapping oligonucleotides containing ∼40-bp regions flanking the target gene. The resulting dsDNA was purified via standard ethanol precipitation, washed with ice-cold 70% ethanol, and resuspended in a minimal volume of ddH2O. For gene replacements, the replacement sequence was PCR amplified with primers that incorporated ∼65-bp homology arms. These PCR products were purified using the same ethanol precipitation protocol.

Yeast strain construction

The S. cerevisiae CEN.PK2-1C strain (17) served as the parental background for all strains generated in this study. Strains were grown at 30°C in yeast extract–peptone–dextrose (YPD) medium containing yeast extract (10 g liter−1), peptone (20 g liter−1), and dextrose (20 g liter−1). If required, Hygromycin B (Beyotime) was added at 200 μg ml−1. Strains were constructed using CRISPR-Cas9–mediated genomic editing. Yeast transformations were conducted using electroporation.

To prepare electrocompetent cells, a single yeast colony from a fresh plate was inoculated into 10 ml of YPD medium and grown overnight at 30°C and 250 rpm as start culture. The start culture was used to inoculate 50 ml of YPD medium in a 250-ml Erlenmeyer flask to have a starting OD600 of ∼0.2 and grown at 30°C and 250 rpm until the OD600 reached 0.6 to 0.8. The culture was chilled on ice for 10 min before harvested by centrifugation (751g, 4°C, 5 min). Cells were washed first with sterile ice-cold QH2O and then with sterile ice-cold 1 M sorbitol. Cells were resuspended in 10 ml of room-temperature conditioning buffer (100 mM lithium acetate and 10 mM dithiothreitol) and incubated at 30°C and 250 rpm for 30 min. The resulting culture was chilled, harvested, and washed with 1 M sorbitol as above. Cells were lastly resuspended in 0.4 ml of sterile ice-cold 1 M sorbitol and ready for electroporation.

The prepared yeast competent cells (75 μl) were mixed with purified donor DNA fragments (each 0.5 to 1 μg for cassette integration, 3 μg for gene deletion, and 1 μg for gene replacement) and 3 μg of the pWS175-locus plasmid in a prechilled Bio-Rad electroporation cuvette (0.2-cm gap). Electroporation was performed using a Bio-Rad MicroPulser Electroporator with the Sc2 program (1.5 kV, 5 ms). Immediately after the electroporation, 1 ml of YPD medium was added, and the cell suspension was incubated at 30°C and 250 rpm for 1 hour, followed by plating out onto YPD agar with Hygromycin B (200 μg ml−1). Plates were incubated at 30°C for 3 days, and the obtained colonies were streaked out onto YPD agar. Positive mutants were identified via colony PCR using primers flanking the integration site and then streaked twice on YPD agar to cure the pWS175-locus plasmid.

Pigment production and extraction

A single yeast colony from a fresh plate was inoculated into 10 ml of YPD medium and grown at 30°C and 250 rpm for 24 hours as start culture. The start culture was inoculated at 1:20 to 20 ml of YPD medium supplemented with 10 mM Mg2+ (equimolar mixture of MgCl2 and MgSO4) (for all strains) and ferric ammonium citrate (0.28 g liter−1; for GY7–31 strains) and grown in a 100-ml Erlenmeyer flask at 24°C and 250 rpm for 24 hours. Cultures requiring light activation were illuminated with a white light-emitting diode (LED; correlated color temperature, 6500 K) at an intensity of 5 μmol photons m−2 s−1 throughout the cultivation. Cells corresponding to 60 OD600 units or from 10 ml of fermentation culture were harvested by centrifugation (751g, 4°C, 5 min) and washed once in 25 mM Hepes buffer (pH 7.5). Pigments were extracted with 1 ml of ice-cold solvent [0.2% (w/v) ammonia in methanol for GY1–12 strains; acetone/methanol (7/2, v/v) for GY13–31 strains] in the presence of 0.6 g of 0.5-mm glass beads by vigorous shaking (4 cycles, 1 min) using a tissue grinder (Tiss-32, Jingxin, Shanghai) and clarified by centrifugation (17,000g, 5 min). The resulting supernatant containing extracted pigments was either directly analyzed by HPLC or dried using a vacuum centrifuge (MC-2, Jiaimu, Beijing). Dried pigments were reconstituted with 200 μl of 0.2% (w/v) ammonia in methanol and 50 μl of ddH2O (for GY1–12 strains) or 250 μl of 0.2% (w/v) ammonia in methanol (for GY13–31 strains). The clarified (17,000g, 5 min) sample (75 μl) was analyzed by HPLC.

HPLC analysis of pigments

Pigment samples were analyzed on an Agilent 1260 Infinity II HPLC system equipped with a Phenomenex Luna C18(2) reversed-phase LC column (5-μm particle size, 100-Å pore size, 250 mm by 4.6 mm). Pigment samples extracted from GY1–12 and GY13–27 strains were analyzed using HPLC methods described in (78) and (3), respectively. Pigment samples extracted from GY27–31 strains were analyzed using HPLC methods described in (4). The elution of pigment species was monitored by absorbance at 416, 440, 480, and 665 nm. Pigment standards were prepared from photosynthetic organisms, E. coli strains expressing Chl biosynthesis genes, in vitro enzymatic assays, or purchased from a commercial supplier. MgPME was extracted from a Rvi. gelatinosus ΔbchEΔacsF RifR mutant (78) using methods described previously (3). DV PChlide a was prepared from the E. coli C43(DE3)/IA strain (3). DV Chlide a was prepared via a two-step enzymatic assay: MgPME was converted to DV PChlide a using Rvi. gelatinosus AcsF (71), followed by reduction to DV Chlide a using Synechocystis LPOR (79). GG-Chl a was isolated from a Synechocystis ΔchlP mutant (80), and Chl a was isolated from the WT. ECN and CAN were purchased from Toronto Research Chemicals and CATO Research Chemicals Inc., respectively. Pigment levels were estimated on the basis of their respective HPLC peak areas using the following molar extinction coefficients: 71,430 M−1 cm−1 at 665 nm for Chl a (81), and 122,600 and 105,300 M−1 cm−1 at 480 nm for ECN and CAN, respectively.

Fluorescence microscopy

Yeast cells were pelleted and resuspended in QH2O to an appropriate density for imaging. Cells were stained with Nile Red (10 μg ml−1; dissolved in acetone; Yeasen Biotechnology) for 5 min in the dark. Imaging was performed on a Leica STELLARIS 5 inverted laser scanning confocal microscope equipped with a 100× oil-immersion objective (numerical aperture 1.518, Leica Microsystems Type F Immersion Liquid, ISO 8036). Nile Red was excited at 530 nm with fluorescence emission captured between 570 and 590 nm. Chl a autofluorescence was visualized via excitation at 665 nm and emission collection at 675 to 700 nm. All image acquisition and postprocessing were conducted using the Leica LAS X software suite (v.4.7.0.28176).

Yeast fermentation

For fermentation experiments, sample size was set at n = 3 independent biological replicates, which is standard for microbial studies. A single yeast colony from a fresh plate was inoculated into 100 ml of YPD medium in a 250-ml Erlenmeyer flask and grown at 30°C and 250 rpm for 24 hours as start culture. This was used to inoculate 1 liter of YPD medium supplemented with 10 mM Mg2+ (equimolar mixture of MgCl2 and MgSO4) and ferric ammonium citrate (0.28 g liter−1) to a starting OD600 of 0.25. Cultures were grown in 2-liter Erlenmeyer flasks at 24°C and 250 rpm for 72 hours. Light-activated cultures were illuminated throughout with a white LED (correlated color temperature, 6500 K) at an intensity of 5 μmol photons m−2 s−1. Samples were collected at 8, 16, 24, 32, 40, 48, and 72 hours. OD600, residual glucose, and pigment levels were measured. Residual glucose was quantified using a commercial glucose colorimetric assay kit (Shanghai Yuanye Bio-Technology), and pigments were analyzed by HPLC. No data points were excluded from the analysis.

Yeast fractionation and detection of GG reductase

Yeast strains were grown as for pigment production. To prepare whole-cell lysate, cells corresponding to 6 OD600 units were harvested by centrifugation (751g, 4°C, 5 min) and washed once in 25 mM Hepes buffer (pH 7.5). Cells were then washed sequentially with ice-cold 2 M lithium acetate and ice-cold 0.4 mM NaOH, with a 5-min incubation on ice before centrifugation (17,000g, 1 min). The pellet was resuspended in 100 μl of 1× Laemmli buffer and incubated at 37°C for 5 min. A 10-μl aliquot of the clarified sample was analyzed by SDS-PAGE, and GG reductase was detected by Western blot using an antibody raised against Syn_ChlP (82). This antibody also cross-reacted with At_CHLP. For fractionation analysis, cells corresponding to 140 OD600 units were harvested by centrifugation (751g, 4°C, 5 min) and washed once in 25 mM Hepes buffer (pH 7.5). Cells were resuspended in 1 ml of fractionation buffer [50 mM Hepes (pH 7.5), 150 mM NaCl, 1 mM phenylmethylsulfonyl fluoride, and protease inhibitor cocktail for fungal and yeast extracts (Adamas-Life)] and disrupted with 0.6 g of 0.5-mm glass beads by vigorous shaking (10 cycles, 1 min) using a tissue grinder (Tiss-32, Jingxin, Shanghai). The lysate (500 μl) was centrifuged (1000g, 4°C, 5 min) to remove unbroken cells. The resulting supernatant (350 μl) was centrifuged (21,100g, 4°C, 1 hour) to separate soluble and membrane fractions. The membrane fraction was washed once with fractionation buffer and resuspended in 100 μl of 1× Laemmli buffer. The soluble fraction was mixed with an equal volume of 2× Laemmli buffer. All samples were incubated at 37°C for 5 min, and 10 μl of each clarified sample was analyzed by SDS-PAGE and Western blot for GG reductase detection.

Purification of pigment-protein complexes

A single colony from a fresh GY31 plate was inoculated into 50 ml of YPD medium and grown in a 250-ml Erlenmeyer flask at 30°C and 250 rpm for 24 hours as start culture. The start culture was inoculated at 1:20 to 1 liter of YPD medium supplemented with 10 mM Mg2+ (equimolar mixture of MgCl2 and MgSO4) and ferric ammonium citrate (0.28 g liter−1) and grown in a 2-liter Erlenmeyer flask at 24°C and 250 rpm, illuminated with a white LED (correlated color temperature, 6500 K) at an intensity of 5 μmol photons m−2 s−1, for 24 hours. Cells from 3-liter cultures were harvested by centrifugation (4000g, 4°C, 30 min) and resuspended to 120 ml in buffer A [50 mM tris-HCl (pH 8.0), 500 mM NaCl, and 5 mM imidazole]. Cells were disrupted using a high-pressure homogenizer (Union-Biotech, UH-06) at 1000 to 1200 bar. The process consisted of two rounds of 5 cycles each, with a 10-min cooling period on ice between rounds to minimize thermal degradation. The resulting lysate was diluted to 180 ml in buffer A and cleared by centrifugation (63,282g, 10°C, 30 min). The supernatant was clarified via a 0.88-μm filter and loaded onto Ni-IDA beads 6FF (Smart-Lifesciences) [column volume (CV) of 1.5 ml] to capture Syn_OCP. The flow-through was reserved for subsequent Bo_WSCP purification. After washing the beads with 10 CV of buffer A, Syn_OCP was eluted using buffer B [50 mM tris-HCl (pH 8.0), 200 mM NaCl, and 200 mM imidazole]. The orange fractions were pooled, buffer exchanged into 20 mM tris-HCl (pH 8.0) using an Amicon Ultra Centrifugal Filter (10 kDa MWCO; Millipore), and loaded onto a 5-ml HiTrap Capto Q column (Cytiva). The column was washed sequentially with 50 ml of 20 mM tris-HCl (pH 8.0) and 50 ml of the same buffer containing 50 mM NaCl. Proteins were eluted over a 50-ml linear NaCl gradient (50 to 120 mM), followed by an additional 50-ml elution at 120 mM NaCl. The resulting orange fractions were pooled, concentrated, and further refined by SEC on a Superdex 200 Increase 10/300 GL column (Cytiva) (0.5 ml min−1) in buffer C [50 mM tris-HCl (pH 8.0) and 200 mM NaCl]. Purification process was monitored by SDS-PAGE, and Syn_OCP was confirmed by Western blot using an anti-His antibody (70796-M; Sigma-Aldrich). Syn_OCP concentration was determined by absorbance at 280 nm using an extinction coefficient of 34,950 M−1 cm−1, calculated by ProtParam (83) in the ExPASy portal.

For Bo_WSCP purification, flow-through from the Ni-IDA beads was loaded onto Strep-Tactin XT 4Flow high-capacity beads (IBA-Lifesciences) (CV of 0.5 ml), washed with 40 CV of buffer D [50 mM tris-HCl (pH 8.0) and 500 mM NaCl], and eluted with buffer E [50 mM tris-HCl (pH 8.0), 500 mM NaCl, and 50 mM biotin (Sangon Biotech)]. The green fractions were pooled, buffer exchanged into buffer C, subjected to a heat treatment at 95°C for 15 min, and centrifuged (21,000g, 4°C, 5 min) to remove precipitants. Purification process was monitored by SDS-PAGE, and Bo_WSCP was confirmed by Western blot using an anti–Strep-tag II antibody (MCA2489P; Bio-Rad). Bo_WSCP concentration was determined by absorbance at 280 nm using an extinction coefficient of 30,940 M−1 cm−1, calculated by ProtParam (83) in the ExPASy portal.

The oligomeric state of purified pigment-protein complexes was determined by SEC on a Superdex 200 Increase 10/300 GL column (Cytiva) (0.5 ml min−1) in buffer C. MWs were estimated using a Bio-Rad Gel Filtration Standard Kit (1.35 to 670 kDa) to generate a calibration curve. Syn_OCP was light-activated by illumination with a white LED (correlated color temperature, 6500 K) at an intensity of ∼500 μmol photons m−2 s−1 for 15 min at 4°C, and dark recovery was performed by incubating the protein at 37°C in the dark for 10 min.

Absorbance and fluorescence spectroscopy

Absorbance spectra were recorded for Syn_OCP and Bo_WSCP in their as-isolated states, as well as for Syn_OCP in its light-activated and dark-recovered states, on a Cary 60 UV-Vis spectrophotometer (Agilent). For quantitative analysis, the molar extinction coefficients of ECN and CAN in a methanol/acetone mixture (8/2, v/v) were empirically determined. First, the concentration of a carotenoid stock solution was determined spectroscopically in petroleum ether, using reported specific absorption coefficients (A1 cm1%) of 2158 at 458 nm for ECN and 2200 at 466 nm for CAN (84). These stocks were then diluted into the methanol/acetone solvent to measure their absorbance, and the molar extinction coefficients for ECN and CAN were derived to be 122,600 and 105,300 M−1 cm−1 at 480 nm, respectively, and 87,500 M−1 cm−1 for both species at 501 nm. Carotenoids were extracted from isolated Syn_OCP by mixing 100 μl of protein solution with 900 μl ice-cold acetone, followed by a 30-min incubation on ice. After centrifugation (21,000g, 4°C, 5 min), the pellet underwent a second extraction with an acetone/H2O mixture (9/1, v/v). The supernatants from both steps were pooled and vacuum-dried. The resulting pigments were reconstituted in 500 μl of methanol/acetone (8/2, v/v), and the absorbance at 501 nm was recorded. By calculating the ratio of carotenoid to protein concentration, the isolated Syn_OCP was determined to contain one carotenoid molecule per monomer. The fluorescence spectrum of Bo_WSCP was recorded on a FluoroMax Plus fluorimeter (HORIBA Jobin Yvon) with excitation at 430 nm (5-nm slit width) and emission collected between 450 and 800 nm (5-nm slit width).
