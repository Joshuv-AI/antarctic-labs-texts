Determinants of uptake of childhood immunization in Gboko, Benue State, Nigeria
Authors: Bemhemba Iba, Vershima Daniel Zar, Joseph Oteng
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundImmunization is among the most impactful, effective, and cost-effective public health interventions for preventing deaths and illnesses associated with several childhood infectious diseases worldwide. Given the protective effect of immunization and the observed low immunization coverage in Nigeria, it is essential to evaluate immunization coverage and identify factors influencing its uptake in this region. This study examined the determinants of childhood immunization in Gboko, Benue State, Nigeria.MethodsThis community-based cross-sectional survey was conducted in Gboko between June and August 2024 among 446 mothers and caregivers of children aged 12–23 months. Participants were recruited using a multistage sampling technique, and data were collected through an interviewer-administered questionnaire. The data were analysed using descriptive and inferential statistics (binary and multivariate logistic regression) via SPSS version 25.ResultsOf the children surveyed, 68.4% were fully immunized, 19.7% partially immunized, and 11.9% unimmunized for age. Uptake rates for all five antigens assessed were higher than national averages but lower than those found in similar sub-national studies. A significant drop-off in vaccine uptake was observed between the initial and later doses. Key determinants of immunization completion included caregiver wealth status, availability of vaccines, and informal payment requirements at health facilities.ConclusionAlthough immunization uptake in Gboko LGA was relatively high, full coverage remains below the WHO-recommended targets. Major barriers, such as vaccine stockouts, informal charges, and caregiver misconceptions, continue to hinder completion. Background

Immunization is among the most impactful, effective, and cost-effective public health interventions for preventing deaths and illnesses associated with several childhood infectious diseases worldwide [1, 2]. Globally, an estimated 4.4 million deaths averted in 2021 were attributed to immunization [3]. Besides the protection offered against vaccine-preventable diseases through immunization, it strategically introduces families to healthcare, and provides a platform for which other healthcare services can be accessed [4]. Immunization offers additional advantages by reducing the effects of disease on persons, households, and communities, in addition to the immediate impact on the health of the population, and therefore reduces healthcare costs and enhances productivity and academic benefits [5, 6].

Despite the tremendous benefits and gains of immunization, its uptake has remained a challenge. In 2022, globally, 14.3 million children missed out on any vaccination (zero-dose children), and 6.2 million were partially vaccinated, highlighting the dearth of access to immunization and other essential medical services [7]. In many low- and middle-income countries, vaccine coverage remains below the World Health Organization’s (WHO) target of 90% national coverage for the three recommended doses of diphtheria-tetanus-pertussis (DTP)-containing vaccines [8]. In sub-Saharan Africa, it was estimated that 56.5% of children are fully vaccinated, 35.1% are partially vaccinated, and 8.4% are unvaccinated [5]. Studies across 25 sub-Saharan African countries have shown that full vaccination coverage ranges from 24% in Guinea to 93% in Rwanda [5]. Among the countries in the sub-Saharan African region with low immunization coverage, Nigeria ranks 4th (only above Chad, Angola, and Guinea) with 32% of children fully vaccinated, 49% of children partially vaccinated, and 19% of children who received no vaccination [5].

The 2021 Multiple Indicator Cluster Survey (MICS) study indicated that immunization coverage in Nigeria was still low, with at least 6.2 million children having never received or missed all their vaccines in the past five years [9]. Available data on childhood immunization revealed variations across the sub-national zones. Among the sub-regions, South West (23.6%) had the highest proportion of children aged 12 to 23 months who were fully vaccinated, followed by South East (19.6%), South-South (18.8%), North Central (15.2%), North West (13.7%), and North East (9.1%) with the lowest proportion of children who benefitted fully from immunization [10]. In 2021, the available data from Nigeria Multiple Indicators Cluster Survey (MICS) and National Immunization Coverage Survey (NICS) reported that Benue State had an immunization coverage of 63% for children aged 12–23 months [11] However, the most recent 2023–2024 National Demographic and Health Survey (NDHS) shows a substantial decline, with coverage dropping to 44.3% in the state [12].

Several factors contribute to the low immunization uptake in Nigeria and other low- and medium-income countries. Some of these factors are socio-demographic, such as maternal age, parents’ level of education, occupation of parents, marital status of mother, family income, wealth index of household, urban/rural residence, and ethnicity [2, 13, 14]. Other factors are obstetric factors such as antenatal care follow-up, postnatal care follow-up, the interval between births, and the place of delivery [13]. In addition, studies have attributed the low vaccine uptake and coverage to unfair access to immunization services, insufficient access to vaccines and consumables, financial challenges, cold-chain maintenance, manpower, and operational challenges [10, 15]. Other studies have highlighted challenges within Nigeria’s primary healthcare system, including limited government commitment and political will toward the Expanded Programme on Immunization (EPI) policy, over-centralization of EPI administration at the federal level, and vaccine hesitancy influenced by religious sentiments, particularly in northern Nigeria [16–19].

Although immunization is largely free in Nigeria, coverage remains suboptimal. Marked disparities exist between rural and urban areas; in 2021, complete immunization was 73% in urban settings compared with 47% in rural areas. In the same year, Benue State recorded 63% coverage, while Gboko LGA reported 68.8%, both figures still below the WHO district target of 80% [11, 20]. As a semi-urban area with both rural and urban characteristics, Gboko provides a unique opportunity to examine the diverse socio-demographic and systemic factors influencing immunization uptake. Its population size, mixed setting, and ongoing health interventions make it a relevant and representative site for understanding barriers to immunization and informing context-specific strategies to improve coverage in similar settings across Nigeria. The objective of this study was to assess immunization uptake in Gboko LGA and to determine the factors associated with it.

Methodology

Study setting

This study was conducted in Gboko Local Government Area (LGA) of Benue State, located in the North-Central geopolitical zone of Nigeria. Gboko LGA was established on May 11, 1970, and its administrative headquarters is situated in Gboko town. Covering a landmass of approximately 2,264 square kilometres, Gboko LGA had an estimated population of 593,267 as of 2023 [21]. Gboko is predominantly inhabited by the Tiv ethnic group, although Hausa and English are also commonly spoken. The area shares borders with Tarka LGA to the northwest, Gwer LGA to the south, Guma LGA to the west, Buruku LGA to the north, and Ushongo LGA to the east. The climate is tropical, with an average temperature of 28 °C and a relative humidity of about 49%. The primary economic activities in Gboko include subsistence and commercial farming, trading, and civil service employment. The LGA is rich in limestone, which supports local mining and cement industries. Notable landmarks in Gboko include the Gboko Hills, Mkar Hills, and the tomb of Senator J.S. Tarka, a notable political figure in Nigeria’s history. Gboko hosts several educational institutions, including tertiary institutions like the University of Mkar and the NKST College of Health Technology, as well as numerous secondary and primary schools. The LGA also has a growing healthcare infrastructure comprising primary health centres (PHCs), secondary health facilities, and a few private clinics and mission hospitals. However, healthcare access remains uneven, particularly in rural communities.

Study design

A community-based cross-sectional survey was employed to assess the determinants of uptake of childhood immunization in Gboko, Benue State, Nigeria.

Study participants

The study participants included children aged 12–23 months and their mothers or caregivers. This age group was purposively selected because, by 12 months, a child is expected to have completed the recommended routine immunization schedule according to Nigeria’s Expanded Programme on Immunization (EPI), allowing for a reliable assessment of full immunization status [12].

Inclusion criteria

Children aged 12–23 months who were resident in the selected council ward of the Local Government Area (LGA) for at least six months; and mothers or caregivers who had at least one child within the specified age range who were willing to participate in the study were all included in the study.

Exclusion criteria

Children and caregivers who were not available during the data collection period were excluded from the study.

Sample size

The minimum sample size for this study was 440 and it was determined via the Cochrane formula with a full immunization coverage rate of 22% obtained for Benue State in the most recent 2021 Nigeria Multiple Indicator Cluster Survey (MICS) & National Immunization Coverage Survey (NICS), at a 95% confidence level, 5.0% margin of error, design effect of 1.5 and an inflation of 10% to account for non-response.

Sampling technique

In this study, the 17 council wards of Gboko LGA were stratified into six major districts, reflecting the traditional administrative groupings within the LGA, to capture the geographic and socio-demographic diversity of the area. From each district, one ward was randomly selected, giving a total of six wards. Five communities were then randomly selected from each ward. Although the number of communities varied across wards, the fixed number of five was chosen to ensure uniformity, representativeness, and logistical feasibility. This resulted in 30 sampled communities across Gboko LGA. The detailed ward-level sampling distribution is presented in Appendix Table A1.

Variables and operational definitions

Outcome variable

The primary outcome variable was childhood immunization status, operationally defined as follows:

Fully immunized: A child aged 12–23 months who had received all basic vaccines per the National Programme on Immunization (NPI) schedule, including BCG, three doses of oral polio vaccine (OPV), three doses of pentavalent vaccine (Penta), three doses of pneumococcal conjugate vaccine (PCV), and one dose of measles vaccine.

Fully immunized: A child aged 12–23 months who had received all basic vaccines per the National Programme on Immunization (NPI) schedule, including BCG, three doses of oral polio vaccine (OPV), three doses of pentavalent vaccine (Penta), three doses of pneumococcal conjugate vaccine (PCV), and one dose of measles vaccine.

Partially immunized: A child who had received some but not all the required vaccines by 12 months of age.

Partially immunized: A child who had received some but not all the required vaccines by 12 months of age.

Unimmunized: A child who had received none of the vaccines listed above.

Unimmunized: A child who had received none of the vaccines listed above.

Data collection

Data for this study were collected using a pretested, structured, interviewer-administered questionnaire designed by the researcher based on a review of the available literature on immunization surveys in similar contexts. The questionnaire featured the socio-demographic characteristics of mothers/caregivers and their children in Part A; knowledge and attitude of mothers/caregivers towards immunization in Parts B and C respectively; uptake of immunization services in Part D; and factors associated with the uptake of immunization services in Part E.

Three trained interviewers, all of whom were graduates in health-related disciplines (Higher National Diploma in Public/Environmental Health), in addition to the principal researcher, were involved in collecting the data. Before the survey commenced, the interviewers underwent a two-day training session facilitated by the principal researcher. The training covered the objectives of the study, review of the questionnaire, ethical considerations, and interview techniques. In each selected household with a child between 12 and 23 months of age, the primary caregiver (mother) or a representative in her absence was interviewed. The fieldwork activities of the study were carried out between June and July 2024.

For every household visited, an immunization card was requested, and where it was present, information on the immunization history of the child was recorded. In cases where the card was not issued or has been misplaced, the information on the child’s immunization was based on the mother’s recall. The data for this study were obtained electronically via Kobocollect, which was installed on the mobile phones of the research assistants.

Data management and analysis

The data obtained from this study were first entered into Microsoft Excel and then exported to SPSS version 25 (IBM, incorporated, Chicago, USA) for cleaning, coding, and analysis. The knowledge of respondents about childhood immunization was assessed using a set of ten structured questions. Respondents who correctly answered seven or more questions (≥ 70%) were categorized as having good knowledge. Those who answered five to six questions correctly (50–69%) were categorized as having average knowledge, while respondents who answered fewer than five questions (< 50%) correctly were considered to have poor knowledge. This method of categorization was adopted from similar research [1].

The attitude of respondents toward childhood immunization was measured using five statements on a three-point Likert scale. Each response was scored as follows: +1 for agreement with a positive statement, 0 for neutrality, and − 1 for disagreement. Negative statements were reverse-coded. The cumulative score for each respondent, therefore, ranged from − 5 to + 5. Respondents with scores above 0 were classified as having a positive attitude, while those with scores of 0 or below were classified as having a poor attitude. Descriptive statistics, including frequencies and proportions, were used to summarize the immunization uptake rates. A chi-square test was used to assess the statistically significant difference in immunization uptake among children 12–23 months of age. Binary logistic regression was used to establish the relationships between immunization uptake and independent variables such as sociodemographic variables, knowledge, attitudes, healthcare-seeking behaviour, and facility–related variables. Multivariate logistic regression was used to assess the independent effect of each variable on the dependent variable. Results were presented in percentages, odds ratios (ORs) with 95% confidence intervals (CIs), and a P value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Validity/Reliability of study tools

The questionnaire was initially developed in English, translated into Tiv, and then back-translated into English to ensure accuracy. It underwent expert review by specialists in epidemiology and public health to establish content validity in terms of clarity, relevance, and appropriateness. The tool was pre-tested among 45 mothers/caregivers with children aged 12–23 months in Mbatyu, a council ward not selected for the main study, to assess comprehensibility, sequence, and acceptability. Feedback from the experts and pre-test was incorporated, and necessary adjustments were made before deployment. Reliability of the instrument was assessed using Cronbach’s Alpha, with a test–retest coefficient of 0.816, indicating good internal consistency.

Discussion

The immunization uptake rates for the various antigens in this study were generally higher than those reported by NDHS 2023–2024 [12] and Nigeria MICS 2021 Statistical Snapshots [22] for Benue State, but relatively lower than those reported by Ariyibi et al. [23] in Ilorin, Kwara State. BCG uptake in this study was higher, followed by PCV 1, OPV 0, and Penta 1. All five antigens were relatively higher than the Nigerian MICS 2021 Statistical Snapshots [22] report, but the Ariyibi et al. [23] study had a higher uptake, as previously stated. Notably, there was a significant dropout in vaccine uptake, with a steady decline observed from the initial doses to the subsequent ones. This could be traced to missed opportunities arising from the unavailability of vaccines at the health facilities or outreach centres and other reasons that might have caused mothers/caregivers to fail to present their children for immunization. The proportion of children who were immunized in this study was lower than that of Eze et al. [18]. in Enugu and Ariyibi et al. [23] in Ilorin, Kwara State, but better than the findings of Tadesse [24] in Ethiopia and Bobo et al. [5] in sub-Saharan Africa. However, it is noteworthy that the studies conducted in Ethiopia and sub-Saharan Africa had larger sample sizes and were implemented at the national level, offering broader generalizability. In contrast, the study in Gboko was more localized and context-specific, serving as a sub-national comparison to the findings of Inyamuwa [25]. This contextual difference may reflect evolving trends such as increased community awareness of immunization, reduced misconceptions, and improved attention to primary healthcare services.

The reasons for non-vaccination identified in this study were similar to those reported in Edo State, Nigeria, in 2023 [1]. Other reported barriers, such as forgetfulness, lack of time due to work, and assumptions that immunization was unnecessary, point to potential gaps in awareness or motivation. Spousal disapproval was another notable factor, often linked to concerns about vaccine side effects or cultural beliefs [13, 26].

Caregivers also mentioned that child’s illness or travel at the time of scheduled immunization contributed to missed doses. This finding is consistent with reports from other contexts where child sickness, even when mild, was perceived as a contraindication to vaccination, leading to delays or missed appointments [13, 18]. Furthermore, stockouts of vaccines, especially in rural outreach centres, were frequently reported. This challenge may reflect broader systemic issues such as cold chain logistics, limited storage infrastructure, and staffing constraints in primary healthcare facilities.

The wealth index of caregivers was found to be significantly associated with full immunization. Mothers in the middle-class wealth index category were four times more likely to complete immunization schedules than those in the poor wealth index group. This is consistent with findings from other studies in Nigeria [27], Ethiopia [28], and Liberia [19], which reported that children from wealthier households were more likely to be fully immunized. This may be attributed to the fact that immunization services in many centres were not entirely free, and caregivers with higher socio-economic status were better positioned to afford transportation costs and service charges.

The finding that payment before immunization was significantly associated with full immunization is both noteworthy and concerning. This finding is critical as it contradicts the national immunization policy, which mandates free vaccination services under the Expanded Programme on Immunization (EPI). The fact that caregivers who paid approximately ₦200 ($0.13 USD) were significantly more likely to complete immunization suggests the presence of informal or unauthorized fees. While the amount may seem minimal, it could still pose a barrier for the poorest households. Similar to findings by Abdullahi [26], caregivers who perceived immunization as affordable had higher completion rates. However, this practice undermines equity and access, especially in rural settings, and calls for stricter enforcement of the free immunization policy and improved accountability within the health system.

The availability of vaccines at health facilities was another factor associated with higher uptake. Missed opportunities were frequently attributed to vaccine stockouts, especially in outreach locations, as reported by Gooding et al. and Sato et al. [29, 30]. Contributing factors included stock depletion at district cold chain levels, logistical challenges, and loss of vaccine potency due to exposure or failure to meet threshold numbers for multi-dose vials.

Conclusions

This study found relatively higher immunization uptake rates in Gboko LGA compared to national averages, but highlighted significant dropouts between initial and subsequent doses. Key factors associated with immunization completion included caregiver wealth status, perceived affordability, and availability of vaccines. Barriers such as informal service charges, vaccine stock-outs, misconceptions, and spousal disapproval hindered full immunization. These findings underscore the need for stricter enforcement of the free immunization policy, improved vaccine logistics, and targeted community education to enhance equitable and sustained immunization coverage.

Limitations of the study

The findings of this study agree with those of other researchers both within and outside Nigeria, but should be interpreted with caution due to the following limitations: First, causal relationships could not be determined because the study employed a cross-sectional design.

Secondly, in households where a child’s immunization card was not available, the child’s immunization history was obtained through maternal recall, which is prone to recall bias. However, to minimize this, the study prioritized the use of immunization cards where available and only relied on recall when cards were missing. Thirdly, there is a possibility that mothers/caregivers over-reported immunization uptake due to social desirability bias, which may have influenced the findings.
