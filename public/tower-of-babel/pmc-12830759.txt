Cryo-EM structure of TMEM164 reveals distinct phospholipid remodeling mechanisms with anti-ferroptotic potential
Authors: Minjing Ke, Yuanyue Shan, Ziwei Zhai, Guoqiang Yao, Xinyi Guo, Xiaoxi Li, Zichao Wu, Huifeng Chen, Mengmeng Zhang, Meiyu Chen, Ying Li, Chengchen Zhao, Bo Wang, Micky D Tortorella, Xiaodong Shu, Mingfeng Zhang, Junqi Kuang, Duanqing Pei
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractPhospholipids in cell membrane provide both regulatory and structural function of a cell. How lipid remodeling regulates cell fate remains less explored. Here we report the cryo-electron microscopy structure of TMEM164 identified by genome-wide CRISPR screen as an anti-ferroptotic factor. The overall architecture reveals a dimer of two 7 transmembrane domain monomers and a metal ion catalytic center with phospholipid substrate in a distinct polyunsaturated fatty acyl (PUFA)-C123 intermediate state. Both loss and gain of its function result in the decline of PUFA-ePE and elevation of C16/18:1-ePE, consequently confer resistance to GPX4 inhibitor RSL3 induced ferroptosis. Mutagenesis studies further validate critical residues for the catalytic center (C123) and the chelates center (E106, Y177 and H181). Through virtual screen and rational design, we identify and test candidate inhibitors for TMEM164, including activity for Montelukast S-enantiomer with 4 order of magnitude higher affinity. Our works not only demonstrates TMEM164 as a membrane lipid remodeler that controls the ferroptotic fate, but also highlights the power of integrating multi-scale platforms to unravel distinct mechanisms and functions.Subject terms: Target identification, Cryoelectron microscopy, Lipidomics, Membrane structure and assembly

Introduction

Cells from all kingdoms of life are invariably enveloped by a lipid bilayer not only as a protective barrier against a hostile environment, but also as a hub of communications for signals and materials. The bilayer is made of phospholipids (PLs) as the basic building blocks, but more critically as regulators for energy, biosynthesis, and signaling pathways1. As such, elaborate mechanisms have been evolved to maintain lipid homeostasis for cells to function properly2. Perturbation of lipid metabolic homeostasis are known to cause metabolic diseases, such as obesity, fatty liver, tumorigeneses, and atherosclerosis3.

The apparent robustness of the lipid bilayer relies on the chemical diversity of the sn−3 headgroup and the sn−1/2 fatty chains to confer high degrees of flexibility and complexity for the membrane to deal with challenges a cell faces4. Among the mechanisms to generate diversity, the Lands cycle is known to mediate fatty chain de-acylation and re-acylation by phospholipase (PLA)1/2s and lysophospholipid acyltransferases (LPLATs), respectively5,6. In mammalian cells, 14 LPLATs have been reported, which can be divided into two families, the 1-acylglycerol-3-phosphate O-acyltransferase (AGPAT) and the membrane bound O-acyltransferase (MBOAT)6. In MBOATs families, in addition to the acyltransferases with lysophospholipid (LPL) as substrate, like LPCAT3 and LPEAT1, there are PORCN, HHAT and GOAT that can also acylate protein/peptide7. Despite the distinct primary structures between AGPAT and MBOAT families, they all utilize acyl-coenzyme A (acyl-CoA) as a donor for acyl group to catalyze the acyl transfer reaction6–10.

Damages to lipid bilayer often lead to cell death, such as ferroptosis driven by iron-dependent lipid peroxidation11. Paradoxically, both the deficiency of LPCAT3 and the ectopic expression of MBOAT1/2 could protect cells from ferroptosis by reducing the polyunsaturated phospholipids (PUFA-PLs)12–14. How cells integrate various signals to die or survive remains largely unknown.

We performed a CRISPR-Cas9 screen for key regulators of ferroptosis and identified TMEM164 as a hit (Supplementary Fig. 1). Consistently, TMEM164 was previously identified as a hit in a similar screen, but not characterized nor validated11. Recently, TMEM164 has been reported to function as an acyltransferase with C20:4-PC as a substrate directly, instead of acyl-CoA, to generate ferroptotic C20:4-ePLs in several tumor cell lines, implying that TMEM164 is pro-ferroptotic15. In addition, TMEM164 has been implicated in diverse physiological and pathological conditions, such as neurodegeneration16, autophagy17 and tumorigenesis18. It remains unclear how TMEM164 confers such diverse function mechanistically.

Here we report the cryo-EM structure of human TMEM164. With a dimeric architecture, TMEM164 contains a metal ion coordinated catalytic center. We show an intermediate state containing thioester bond of fatty acyl-Cys123, apparently revealing a distinct catalytic mechanism in comparison to all other known acyltransferases of MBOATs superfamily. By virtual screening, we identify the S-enantiomer of Montelukast (S-ena M), a leukotriene receptor antagonist clinically used to treat asthma, bronchospasm, allergic rhinitis and urticaria, as an inhibitor of TMEM164.

Results

The cryo-EM structure of human TMEM164 as an acyltransferase

Our genome-wide CRISPR-Cas9 knockout library suppressor screens identify TMEM164 as a pro-ferroptotic regulator (Supplementary Fig. 1a, b). While our work was in progress, a recent report suggests that TMEM164 is an acyltransferase15. To gain mechanistic insight into TMEM164, we then determined, by cryo-EM, a 2.59 Å structure for this enzyme (Supplementary Figs. 2a, b, 3) based on protocols established in our lab19. We re-confirmed its acyltransferase activity by both membrane extracts from TMEM164-overexpressing cells (Supplementary Fig. 2c) and the purified protein (Supplementary Fig. 2d), showing that it catalyzes the conversion of PC(18:0_20:4) and a lyso-ether phospholipid (eLPE) into PE(P-18:0_20:4) as expected15. By the 3D architecture, we show that TMEM164 protomers assume a dimeric configuration (Fig. 1a). The top view of the cross-section of the transmembrane layer at the position indicated by the dashed line in Fig. 1a depicts seven transmembrane helices of each subunit marked by transmembrane helices (TM) 1–7 (Fig. 1b, d, Supplementary Fig. 4). The dimeric interface of TMEM164 is made of two TM2 from each protomer by hydrophobic interaction and Salt bridges of residues from their side chains, where there is a pore allowing lipid biosynthesis reactions (Fig. 1b, c, Supplementary Fig. 5).

TMEM164 exhibits high substrate selectivity, specifically catalyzing PLs with polyunsaturated fatty acids (PUFA) at the sn−2 position, while showing no catalytic activity toward PLs containing saturated or monounsaturated fatty acids at the corresponding position15. Our structural analysis further reveals that this substrate selectivity arises from a pocket of limited depth, which can accommodate precisely the kinked conformation of the PUFA chain (Fig. 2a). Specifically, we show that residues Phe217, Phe221, Met238, Pro241 and Ala256 are responsible for accommodating the sn−2 PUFA moiety (Supplementary Fig. 6a). At the entrance of the reactive pocket, the presence of glycine 155 (Gly155), which lacks a side chain, provides the necessary physical space for substrate access (Fig. 2b).

We also observed a metal ion within the catalytic pocket, which was identified as iron, copper or zinc by inductively coupled plasma-Mass Spectrometry (ICP-MS) (Supplementary Fig. 7a), coordinated by residues of His181, His124, Glu106, and Cys123 (Fig. 2b). Specifically, the side chains of His181 and His124 chelate the metal ion through direct coordination, while those of Glu106 and Cys123 interact with it via water-mediated interactions. Furthermore, our in vitro biochemical assays demonstrate that TMEM164 exhibits the highest enzymatic activity in the presence of Zn²⁺ (Supplementary Fig. 7b), suggesting that zinc ion likely serves as its preferred metallocofactor. Thus, to the best of our knowledge, the structure of TMEM164 suggests a distinct mechanism of ion mediated lipid acylation.

To confirm structurally that TMEM164 is an acyltransferase capable of generating ferroptotic C20:4 ether PLs15, we first attempted to identify a critical histidine based on previous structural studies on lipid acyltransferases of membrane-bound O-acyltransferase (MBOAT) ACAT1(His460), DGAT1(His415), LPCAT3(His388), PORCN(His336) and HHAT(His379)8,9,20–22 (Supplementary Fig. 8) and show that TMEM164 has a His181 in the catalytic pocket with the substrate phosphatidylethanolamine (PE) (Fig. 2b). As accurate positioning of the phospholipid substrate is critical for catalysis, we show that Tyr177 and His181 are likely involved in this process. Their side chains are positioned at distances of approximately 2.487 Å and 2.504 Å, respectively, from the nearest oxygen atom of the phosphate headgroup (Fig. 2b). Additionally, Asn236 may interact with the amino group in the phosphate headgroup of the phospholipid substrate via hydrogen bonding, potentially influencing TMEM164’s preference for specific substrates or products (Fig. 2b).

Interestingly, we identify a distinct intermediate state with a thioester bond of polyunsaturated fatty acyl-Cys123 (Fig. 2c) where the cysteine residue serves the role of coenzyme A as shown for members of the MBOAT superfamily (Supplementary Fig. 8). Similarly, the absence of a side chain in Gly155 reduces steric hindrance around PUFA–Cys123 (Fig. 2d), which is likely critical for facilitating the enzymatic reaction. Residues Pro122, Met152, Met216, Phe217, Met238, Phe221, Pro241, and Ala256 are responsible for accommodating the PUFA-Cys123 moiety (Supplementary Fig. 6b) and under this condition, given that the imidazole group of His181 is protonated, the metal ion is likely stabilized within the catalytic pocket primarily by residues Glu106 and His124.

TMEM164 remodels cellular phospholipid composition

Glycerophospholipids can be classified not only by their phosphate headgroups, such as PE, phosphatidylserine (PS), or phosphatidylcholine (PC), but also by the substituent at the sn−1 position of the glycerol backbone, giving rise to diacyl PLs and ether PLs (Supplementary Fig. 9a)23,24. Ether PLs are further subdivided into alkyl-acylphospholipid and alkenyl-acylphospholipid (i.e., plasmalogen) subtypes based on the chemical bond at the ether-linked oxygen (Supplementary Fig. 9a)23. However, regardless of whether the sn−1 position is esterified to an acyl, alkyl, or alkenyl group, the saturation state of its attached fatty acid chain does not influence cellular sensitivity to ferroptosis. In contrast, the presence of UFA, such as arachidonic acid (ArA, C22:6) and docosahexaenoic acid (DHA, C20:4), at the sn−2 position enhances cellular susceptibility to ferroptosis, whereas the saturated fatty acid, like stearic acid (C18:0), at this site promotes ferroptosis resistance25–27.

To further understand the role on TMEM164 in ferroptosis, we show that loss of TMEM164 (Supplementary Fig. 9b) led to the imbalance of the phospholipid composition (Supplementary Fig. 9c) and a significant decrease of polyunsaturated fatty acid ether phosphatidylethanolamines (PUFA-ePEs) C20:4-ePEs and slight raising of saturated/monounsaturated fatty acid (SFA/MUFA) phospholipid (Supplementary Fig. 9c–f), consistent with findings from a previous report15. Intriguingly, the overexpression of TMEM164 also reveals a phospholipid remodeling of significant reduction of PUFA-ePEs (C22:6) and the increase of C18:1/2-ePEs and free fatty acids (FFA), such as Docosahexaenoic acid (DHA) (C22:6) and Arachidonic acid (ArA) (C20:4) (Fig. 3a–d). These seemingly contradictory results further suggest a complex role for TMEM164 at the cellular context.

TMEM164 confers anti-ferroptoptic potential to cells

The apparent reduction of ferroptotic PUFA-ePEs observed with TMEM164 overexpression (Fig. 3a–d), further suggest that TMEM164 may confer resistance to ferroptosis, a property highly relevant to tumorigenesis11. To test this hypothesis, we utilized two ferroptosis-inducing agents, RSL3 (GPX4 inhibitor) and Erastin (VDAC inhibitor) to induce ferroptosis in HT1080 cells with or without TMEM164 overexpression, and performed morphological analysis, cell viability determination and lipid peroxidation detection experiments. We show that the cells with overexpression of TMEM164 could significantly resist ferroptosis triggered by RSL3 and Erastin (Fig. 3e, Supplementary Fig. 9g), with no effect on the other cell death processes induced by H2O2, Ampilimod or STS (Supplementary Fig. 9h–j). Time course studies also suggest that the wild type cells treated with RSL3 exhibit the typical ferroptosis morphology after 6 h treatment of RSL3, whereas TMEM164 overexpressing cells can be maintained in a normal state within 24 h (Fig. 3f). Moreover, the accumulation of lipid peroxidation is not detected in TMEM164 overexpressed cells after treated with RSL3(Fig. 3g). Together, these results suggest that TMEM164 has the potential to confer resistance to ferroptosis.

Anti-ferroptotic potential of TMEM164 is Ion coordination dependent

The ferroptosis resistance conferred by TMEM164 provides an opportunity to test the structural features uncovered by cryo-EM (Fig. 1). First, we wish to test the role of hydrophobic interaction and salt bridge within dimeric interface on the ability of TMEM164 to mediate ferroptotic resistance. We show that mutations in hydrophobic residues of the dimeric interface render TMEM164 ineffective to confer ferroptotic resistance (Supplementary Fig. 5, 10). We then performed mutational analysis of residues coordinating the metal ion. As the ion chelate center is enclosed by 16 presented residues in ion chelate chamber of TM2, TM3 and TM5 (Fig. 2), we selected nine residues to perform experiments to verify their roles in ferroptosis resistance. When introduced to HT1080 by lentiviral vectors carrying E106A, H124A, C123A, N121A, H181A, G155A, N236A, H220A and Y177A mutations, TMEM164E106A, TMEM164G155A, TMEM164H181A, TMEM164C123A and TMEM164Y177A expressing cells are no longer resistant to RSL3 induced ferroptosis, while the rest have not effect (Fig. 4a, b). Consistently, BODIPY-C11, an indicator for lipid peroxide, accumulates significantly in the cells of TMEM164 E106A, G155A, H181A, C123A and Y177A mutants after the treatment of RSL3(Fig. 4c).

We further verified the significance of these mutations for ion chelated center by performing time course study(0-24 h) of cell morphology and show that cells with expression of H124A, N236A, H220A and N121A mutation still reached 80% confluence by 24 h, while the E106A, G155A, H181A, C123A and Y177A expressing cells are sensitive to ferroptosis (Fig. 4d). Consistently, cells expressing TMEM164 mutants C123A, E106A, G155A and Y177A lose the capability to remodel phospholipid composition (Supplementary Fig. 11). To rule out cell line bias, we also performed the ferroptosis assay in MDA-MB-231 cells, obtained similar results (Supplementary Fig. 12a–c). These results suggest that E106, G155, H181, C123 and Y177 residues are vital for ion coordinated center as well as the physiology function of TMEM164 and the absence of side chain of G155 might provide space to accommodate substrate phospholipid.

Additionally, we observed that while both overexpression and knockout of TMEM164 confer resistance to ferroptosis, with the degree of protection correlating in a dose-dependent manner with TMEM164 expression levels (Supplementary Fig. 13), lipidomic profiling revealed that overexpression leads to a more pronounced accumulation of free PUFAs. Given the distinct catalytic mechanism of TMEM164, we hypothesize that these free PUFAs may be byproducts of its enzymatic activity, suggesting that the enzyme may also exhibit phospholipase-like properties. To test this, we incubated purified TMEM164 protein with isotopically labeled substrate PC(18:0_20:4-d8) and detected the release of deuterated arachidonic acid (ArA-d8) even in the absence of exogenous LPL acceptors (Supplementary Fig. 12d). Consistent with a functional role of this activity, supplementation with ether-linked lysophosphatidylethanolamine (eLPE) partially restored the ferroptosis phenotype in TMEM164-overexpressing cells (Supplementary Fig. 12e).

Based on these results, we further propose a working model for TMEM164 as an acyltransferase with a distinct intermediate state (Fig. 4e). In the substrate-free state, the metal ion is chelated by residues His181, His124, Cys123, and Glu106, thereby stabilizing the conformation of the catalytic pocket. Upon entry of a PUFA-containing phospholipid into the reactive pocket, Tyr177 guides the phospholipid into the optimal position via hydrogen bonding. The imidazole group of His181 facilitates deprotonation of the Sulfhydryl group, conferring a positive charge, and together with Tyr177, stabilizes the phosphate headgroup. The deprotonated thiolate of Cys123 then nucleophilically attacks the ester bond at the sn−2 position of the phospholipid substrate, resulting in the formation of a thioether-linked intermediate, PUFA-Cys123, and the concomitant release of the accessory substance LPL. Then, another LPL substrate enters the pocket and the His181 deprotonates the hydroxyl group at sn−2 of LPL substrate. Consequently, the hydroxyl group could directly attack the thioester bond of PUFA-Cys123 formed in the intermediate state to generate the final product (Fig. 4e). By a similar mechanism, at heightened level, the reaction mediated by TMEM164 may be shifted to favor the generation of free PUFA (Fig. 3a), due to abundant unstable intermediate fatty acyl-Cys123 complexes (Supplementary Fig. 12f).

Structure based screen and design for TMEM164 inhibitors

The ion coordinated catalytic pocket within TMEM164 provides an ideal target to screen for potential TMEM164 inhibitors. Accordingly, we performed a virtual ligand screen based on compound docking. Over 23,900 small-molecule compounds with known activity and well-defined targets from the MCE Bioactive Compounds Library were tested in silico (details in the methods) and 56 candidate compounds were selected and tested for the TMEM164 inhibitor screening via ferroptotic assay. Except the three compounds with severe cytotoxicity at working concentrations (Supplementary Fig. 14a), we show that four chemicals, TH1834, Montelukast, ZK824190 and Zofenopril, have potential inhibitory effects on TMEM164 activity (Fig. 5a, b). Next, we conducted several independent experiments, such as Surface Plasmon Resonance (SPR), Biolayer Interferometry (BLI) and Isothermal Titration Calorimetry (ITC), and show that TH1834 and Montelukast both have affinity with TMEM164 as analyzed by all three tests (Supplementary Fig. 14b–d). By molecular docking, we show that both compounds have three branches, and one branch both have a carboxyl that form salt bridges and polar contacts with positively charged residues and metal ion, respectively (Supplementary Fig. 14e). The other two branches all contain aromatic rings forming pi-pi or pi-cation stacking with adjacent residues (Supplementary Fig. 14e).

We also designed a series of molecules based on the structural skeletons of the above two screening hits and analyzed the binding affinity of these compounds as well as the chiral isomer (Fig. 5c) with TMEM164 protein by SPR. Intriguingly, we found that the S-enantiomer of Montelukast (S-ena M) binds TMEM164 protein potently and has an improvement of four orders of magnitudes over Montelukast (Fig. 5d). We show that both molecules have comparable cytotoxicity profiles (Supplementary Fig. 14f), but the S-enantiomer is a much stronger inhibitor than Montelukast in cell-based assay (Fig. 5e). As expected, we show that S-ena M exhibits a biphasic effect on ferroptosis in TMEM164-overexpressing cells: at concentrations below 27 μM, cells display progressively increased sensitivity to ferroptosis with escalating concentration, whereas at 50 μM, they reverted to a ferroptosis-resistant phenotype (Supplementary Fig. 14g). We also confirmed that S-ena M significantly inhibits the enzymatic activity of purified TMEM164 in in vitro biochemical assays (Fig. 5f). In ferroptosis induction experiments, S-ena M reduces sensitivity in wild-type cells but had no effect on TMEM164-knockout cells (Fig. 5g). Furthermore, we measured the impact of S-ena M on PUFA-ePLs levels and show a consistent correlation with ferroptosis sensitivity. Treatment with 9 μM S-ena M reduces PUFA-ePLs, like PE(O-18:1_22:6), in wild-type cells, while upregulating PUFA-ePLs, including PE(P-16:0_20:4), in TMEM164-overexpressing cells (Supplementary Fig. 14h).

Next, we sought to determine the structural basis of this interaction. All-atom molecular dynamics (MD) simulations were carried out in AMBER 24 with a system prepared in CHARMM-GUI28,29. Structural visualization was completed in UCSF Chimera30. We proposed that S-ena M engages its target via distinct interaction motifs, including a polar domain and two hydrophobic domains (designated hydrophobic region A and B) (Fig. 5c). Based on the structural information, MD simulations were subsequently conducted to assess the stability of the predicted pose (Fig. 5h, Supplementary Fig. 15a, b), demonstrating that S-ena M maintains low deviation within the pocket during simulation period for three independent replicas (Supplementary Fig. 15c, d). Three highly recognized key residues of the predicted pose were chosen from three different regions of the binding pocket: Asn236 mediates polar interactions, while Phe162 and His220 are involved in hydrophobic interactions (Supplementary Fig. 15e). Isothermal titration calorimetry (ITC) assays demonstrate that mutagenesis in these residues significantly reduces binding affinity compared to the wild-type protein (Supplementary Fig. 15f), supporting the feasibility of predicted binding mode. Taken together, we used LIGPLOT31 to generate a protein-ligand interaction map illustrating S-ena M binding to the key residues of TMEM164 pocket (Fig. 5i). These results demonstrate the feasibility to generate candidate compounds for targets in a timely fashion by integrating multi-platform approaches.

Discussion

We report here the cryo-EM structure of human TMEM164 and candidate compounds as its inhibitors. We validated the catalytic activity of TMEM164 using in vitro enzymatic assays and demonstrated its functional roles in lipid metabolism and ferroptosis through cellular assays. By integrating additional assays, we were able to demonstrate many similar features shared with known members of the LPL acyltransferase MBOAT superfamily8,9,20–22, and more importantly several features. First, all these enzymes including TMEM164 rely on a conserved histidine residue in the catalytic pocket to serve as the acceptor of proton. Second, the acylation modification occurs on the hydroxyl group at the sn−2 position of LPL or the serine residue of the substrate protein/peptide. On the other hand, the most striking difference for TMEM164 is the absence of coenzyme A, thus revealing a distinct catalytic process, as opposed to the one-step reaction seen in other MBOAT enzymes. Another surprising difference is the ion in the catalytic pocket of TMEM164, compared to the zinc in other MBAOTs, such as PORCN (Supplementary Fig. 8)22,32.

The thioether-linked PUFA–Cys123 intermediate serves as a distinctive signature of TMEM164 enzymatic activity, revealing a distinct mechanism not seen in other members of the MBOATs family. The accumulation of free PUFA observed in in vitro enzymatic assays and lipidomic profiles of overexpression cell experiments suggest that this intermediate state is relatively unstable. When LPL fails to enter the catalytic pocket in a timely manner, hydrolysis of the thioether bond occurs, resulting in the release of free fatty acids. Thus, the transfer of the acyl group from PUFA–Cys123 to the sn−2 position of a LPL is likely the rate-limiting step in TMEM164 catalysis. Our results indicate that mutation of the His124 residue has a minor impact on TMEM164 function (Fig. 4a). Furthermore, the imidazole group of His181—which normally coordinates the metal ion—acquires a proton and subsequently interacts with the phosphate headgroup of the phospholipid. In the intermediate state, the metal ion is likely stabilized within the catalytic pocket primarily through coordination with Glu106. This may explain why Glu106 is critically important for TMEM164 function (Fig. 4a). Lipidomic analysis revealed that PE is the most significantly affected phospholipid species upon TMEM164 modulation. This substrate preference is likely established during the catalytic intermediate state, where Asn236 selectively coordinates the subsequent entry of LPL via hydrogen-bond interactions. However, mutagenesis experiments showed that mutation of this residue has only a minor effect on TMEM164-mediated ferroptosis. This suggests that the sensitivity to ferroptosis is predominantly determined by the unsaturation level of the sn−2 fatty acyl chain rather than the chemical properties of the phosphate headgroup.

Thus, TMEM164 seems to possess a distinct catalytic mechanism with a distinct catalytic core and a distinct intermediate state for acylation in cellular lipid metabolism. Given these features, we further suggest or speculate that the distinct intermediate fatty acyl-TMEM164 state may account for the apparent contradiction that both TMEM164 deficiency and overexpression could decrease PUFA-PL and counter ferroptosis. At low or moderate level, TMEM164 utilizes the polyunsaturated fatty acyl of intermediate state to generate PUFA-PLs (Supplementary Fig. 16) (i.e., behaves as a classic acyltransferase), whereas at elevated level, such as observed in pathological conditions it behaves as a phospholipase based on the abundant unstable fatty acyl-TMEM164 complexes and limited amount of endogenous LPL (Supplementary Fig. 16). Since TMEM164 possesses both acyltransferase and phospholipase activities, its overexpression leads to aberrant accumulation of free PUFAs (Fig. 3a) that would otherwise be used in the Lands cycle to produce PUFA-ePLs, thereby limiting their incorporation into PLs.

The fact that TMEM164 has been implicated in various pathological conditions makes it a potential drug target. We took advantage of the high-resolution structure we obtained and embarked on screening and designing candidate inhibitors. Among inhibitors, we show that S-enantiomer of Montelukast33 is a potent inhibitor of TMEM164, far more potent than Montelukast. We are not aware of similar cases that both the S and R forms can be inhibitors to two different protein targets. The chemical basis of this observation remains unknown and future studies may illuminate the structural mechanisms for this selectivity and their therapeutical potentials may also be further explored and developed in the near future.

Methods

Cell Lines

Spodoptera frugiperda Sf9 cells and Expi-HEK293F suspension cells were acquired from Thermo Fisher Scientific. HEK293T was purchased from American Type Culture Collection (ATCC). HT1080 and MDA-231 cell lines were supplied gratis by Xiaodong Shu’s lab. All cell lines were routinely tested and confirmed to be free of mycoplasma contamination. Sf9 cells and Expi293 cells were used for protein purification. Both Sf9 and Expi293F cells were cultured in Sf-900 II SFM medium (Gibco) at 27 °C and HEK293 medium (yocon) supplemented with 1% FBS at 37 °C with 6% CO2 and 70% humidity, respectively. MDA-MB-231 and HT1080 were cultured in Dulbecco’s Modified Eagle Medium (GE Hyclone, #SH30022.01) supplemented with 10% FBS (Natocor, #SFBE), 1% Glutamax (GibcoTM, #35050061) and 1% MEM NEAA (GibcoTM, # 11140050) and maintained at 37 °C with 5% CO2. For regularly culture, the medium was changed every other day. While the cells cultured to the desired confluence, cells were detached with Trypsin-EDTA 0.25% (GibcoTM, 25200056) for propagation or other cell-based assay. Morphological studies were conducted with images and captured as required. All imaging experiments were independently repeated a minimum of three times, and consistent results were observed throughout. Representative images are presented in the corresponding figures.

Protein Expression and purification

DNA fragment encoding hTMEM164 (UniProtKB Q5U3C3) were amplified by PCR from human cDNA libarary and cloned into the plasmid Eric Gouaux (pEG) BacMam vector using EcoRI and XhoI restriction sites, where hTMEM164 gene is placed before a PreScission protease (Ppase) cleavage site, followed by a C-terminal enhanced green fluorescent protein (EGFP) and FLAG tag. The construct was identified by fluorescence detection size-exclusion chromatography (FSEC). The hTMEM164 was expressed in expi-HEK293F cells using the BacMam method. Briefly, a bacmid carrying hTMEM164 was generated by transforming E. coli DH10Bac cells with the hTMEM164 construct according to the manufacturer’s instructions (Bac-to-Bac; Invitrogen). Baculoviruses were produced by transfecting sf9 cells with the bacmid using Cellfectin II (Invitrogen). After three rounds of amplification, baculoviruses were used for cell transduction. Suspension cultures of expi-HEK293F cells were grown at 37 °C to a density of ~3×106 cells/ml and viruses were added (6% v/v) to initiate the transduction. After 12 h, 10 mM sodium butyrate was supplemented, and the temperature was shifted to 30 °C. Cells were harvested at 48 h post-transduction.

The cell pellet from 0.5 L culture was collected by centrifugation at 4000 g. and resuspended with 500 ml of lysis buffer (20 mM Tris-HCl, pH 8.0, 150 mM NaCl), supplied with protease inhibitor cocktail containing 1.3 μg/ml aprotinin, 0.7 μg/ml pepstatin, and 5 μg/ml leupeptin and 2 mM phenylmethylsulfonyl fluoride (PMSF). Unbroken cells and cell debris were removed by centrifugation at 8000 g. for 10 min. Supernatant was centrifuged at 36,000 g. for 30 min in a Ti45 rotor (Beckman). The membrane pellet was mechanically homogenized and solubilized in extraction buffer containing 20 mM Tris-HCl, pH 8.0, 150 mM NaCl, 1% (w/v) lauryl maltose neopentyl glycol (LMNG) and 0.1% (w/v) cholesteryl hemisuccinate (CHS). Unsolubilized materials were removed by centrifugation at 36,000 g. for 1 h in a Ti45 rotor. Supernatant was load onto anti-FLAG G1 affinity resin (Genscript) by gravity flow. Resin was further washed with 10 column volumes of wash buffer (lysis buffer with 0.02% LMNG), and protein was eluted with an elution buffer (lysis buffer with 0.02% LMNG and 230 μg/ml FLAG peptide). The C-terminal GFP tag of eluted protein was removed by human rhinovirus 3 C (HRV 3 C) protease cleavage overnight. The protein was further concentrated by a 50-kDa cutoff concentrator (Milipore) and loaded onto a Superose 6 increase 10/300 column (GE Healthcare) running in lysis buffer with 0.03% digitonin. Peak fractions were pooled and concentrated to around 5 mg/ml for cryo-EM sample preparation. All steps were performed at 4 °C.

Cryo-EM sample preparation

For cryo-EM sample preparation, aliquots (3.5 μl) of the protein sample were loaded onto glow-discharged (20 s, 15 mA; Pelco easiGlow, Ted Pella) Au grids (Quantifoil, R1.2/1.3, 300 mesh). The grids were blotted for 6.0 s with 2 force after waiting for 20 s and immersed in liquid ethane using Vitrobot (Mark IV, Thermo Fisher Scientific/FEI) in condition of 100% humidity and 8 °C.

Cryo-EM image acquisition

Cryo-EM data for hTMEM164 were collected on a Titan Krios microscope (FEI) equipped with a cesium corrector operated at 300 kV. Movie stacks were automatically acquired with EPU software on a Gatan K3 Summit detector in super-resolution mode (215,000× magnification) with pixel size 0.285 Å at the object plane and with defocus ranging from −1.5 to −2.0 μm and GIF Quantum energy filter with a 20 eV slit width. Each stack was 2.02 s long and does-fractioned into 32 frames with 63 ms for each frame. Total exposure was 40 e-Å−2.

Image processing

A flowchart of the Cryo-EM data processing process can be found in Supplementary Fig. 3. Data processing was carried out with the cryoSPARC v4 suite. Super-resolution image stacks were gain-normalized, binned by 2 with Fourier cropping, and patch-based CTF parameters of the dose-weighted micrographs (0.57 Å per pixel) were determined by cryoSPARC. Around 543 K particles were picked by blob picking from 1000 micrographs and 2D classification was performed to select 2D classes as templates. Template picker was used to pick around 383,913 K particles. Particles were extracted using a 160-pixel box with a pixel size of 0.849 Å. The dataset was cleared using several rounds of 2D classification, followed by Ab initio reconstruction using C1 symmetry. One class with 22.7 K particles (16.8%) was further refined using non-uniform refinement and local refinement for reconstructing the density map while imposing a C2 symmetry. The final map was low-pass filtered to the map-model FSC value. The reported resolutions were based on the FSC = 0.143 criterion.

Site-Directed Mutagenesis for Plasmid Construction

Mutagenic primers were designed using Software SnapGene® Primer Designer V1.1.3 to introduce the specify mutation E106A, N121A, C123A, H124A, G155A, Y177A, H181A, H220A, N236A and dimeric interface: V97A-L99A-L101A-V107A-F111A into the TMEM164 gene within the PKD-TMEM164-IRES2-puro vector. The primers (oligo sequences are provided in Supplementary Data 2) were synthesized by Tsingske Biotechnology Co., Ltd. Briefly, rolling circle PCR amplification was carried out with 25 cycles using 2×Phanta Max Master Mix (Vazyme) to generate amplified product containing the desired mutation. The parental (non-mutated) plasmid template was subsequently digested by incubation with DpnI for 1 h at 37 °C to selectively cleave the methylated template DNA.

Determination of Lipid Peroxide Production by flow cytometer

Virtual screening for TMEM164 inhibitors

Docking screening was conducted using AutoDock Vina. The protein model was prepared in AutoDock Tools by removing ligands, waters, and Chain B from the 164-dimer structure. Subsequently, missing side chains were added, bond orders corrected, and hydrogen bond orientations optimized. Constrained energy minimization was then employed to relax the structure. We identified Cys123 as the focal point of the binding pocket and defined the docking area using a 20 × 20 × 20 Å box centered on Cys123, Arg168, His220 and Asn236. Over 23,900 small-molecule compounds with known activity and well-defined targets from the MCE Bioactive Compounds Library were isomerized using MayaChem Tools (www.mayachemtools.org/index.html) and subsequently subjected to energy optimization and 3D generation by OpenBabel. These prepared molecules underwent docking screening. Each molecule was screened in Vina and Smina with the exhaustiveness parameter set at 8. The top 300 molecules from each docking workflow were extracted and clustered, with duplicates and molecules exhibiting inappropriate torsion angles removed. Considering structural diversity, binding mode rationality, compound stability, and synthetic feasibility, 56 candidate molecules were selected for further experimental verification.

Surface Plasmon Resonance (SPR)

The affinity of small molecules and TMEM164 protein were assessed by Surface Plasmon Resonance (SPR; Cytiva, PCCF-BMC006). Briefly, the experiment was performed by the CM5 chip amino coupling method. 400 mM EDC and 100 mM NHS in running buffer (10 mM HEPES, pH7.4, 137 mM NaCl, 2.7 mM KCl, 0.05% P20, 1% DMSO) at a flow rate of 10 μl/min for 600 s to activate CM5 chip. To couple the target protein, TMEM164 was diluted to 10 μg/ml with 10 mM sodium acetate (pH 4.0) and coupled for 300 s at a flow rate of 10 μl/min. Then blocked the coupled CM5-TMEM164 with 1 M ethanolamine with a flow rate of 10 μl/min for 600 s. The small molecule candidates were diluted to 0.156 μM in a half-way gradient from 20 μM by running buffer (10 mM HEPES, pH7.4, 137 mM NaCl, 2.7 mM KCl, 0.05% P20, 1% DMSO). A multi-cycle kinetic assay was performed with a flow rate of 30 μl/min for 180 s each cycle, dissociating for 180 s, and then proceeding to the next cycle to obtain kinetic affinity data for the TMEM164 protein. The obtained data were analyzed using Biacore Insight Evaluation Software (V 2.0.15.12933) in a 1:1 model for kinetics fitting.

Dynamics Simulations

Molecular dynamics (MD) simulations of TMEM164 were carried out under a wild-type, non-fusion background. The starting conformation was derived from Cryo-EM structures, into which the S-ena M docking pose was incorporated. The processed model was aligned through the OPM server34 and subsequently embedded in a pre-equilibrated POPC (1-palmitoyl-2-oleoyl-sn-glycero-3-phosphocholine) bilayer. Water molecules were introduced, and Na+ and Cl- ions were added to neutralize the system, reaching a final salt concentration of 0.15 M. All system assemblies were generated using the CHARMM-GUI Membrane Builder35.

For all simulations, the CHARMM36m force field36 was applied together with the TIP3P water model, while S-ena M parameters were derived from the CHARMM General Force Field (CGenFF)37. Energy minimization was performed in two stages: 7000 steps of steepest descent followed by 5000 steps of conjugate gradient minimization. The systems were then gradually heated from 0 K to 300 K over 125 ps under an NVT ensemble, employing a Langevin thermostat and applying harmonic positional restraints of 10.0 kcal·mol−1·Å−2 on proteins and ligands. Equilibration was first carried out under NVT conditions and subsequently in NPT, with restraints progressively decreased from 5.0 to 0.5 kcal·mol−1·Å−2 in 4 ns intervals over 16 ns, and maintained at 0.1 kcal·mol−1·Å−2 for an additional 10 ns. Production simulations were then run without restraints under NPT at 300 K and 1 bar, using a Langevin thermostat in combination with a Monte Carlo barostat. Hydrogen mass repartitioning38 enabled a 2 fs timestep. Short-range nonbonded interactions were truncated at 9.0 Å, while electrostatics were handled by the particle mesh Ewald method. SHAKE39 was used to constrain bonds involving hydrogen.

For each prepared system, three independent production runs of 300 ns were conducted, with snapshots saved every 0.5 ns. Simulations were executed using the CUDA-accelerated PMEMD engine in Amber 2440 with periodic boundary conditions. Trajectory analysis was performed using CPPTRAJ from AmberTools 2541, and structural visualization was carried out in VMD, and UCSF Chimera42.
