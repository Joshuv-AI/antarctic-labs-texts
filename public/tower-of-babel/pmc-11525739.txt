Self-Adaptive Layering Structure of Nanoconfined Fe–Ni Liquids: Origin of the Liquid–Liquid Phase Transition
Authors: Qingshui Liu, Jian Huang, Mengshuang Fu, Zhichao Li, Ruopu Zhao, Jifeng Tang, Yanyan Jiang, Weikang Wu, Hui Li
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractMetallic liquids under confinement exhibit different properties compared to those of their corresponding bulk phases, such as miscibility, diffusion, and phase transitions. Unfortunately, the challenges in experimentally characterizing Fe–Ni liquids at the nanoscale and the high cost of first-principles simulations hindered the atom-level understanding that is necessary for controlling Fe–Ni liquids. Here, we report a comprehensive molecular dynamics study of the liquid Fe–Ni alloy confined within nanoslits. Driven by the slit size, the confined Fe–Ni liquid experiences the liquid–liquid phase transition (LLPT) that is characterized by layering transitions. Interestingly, during the LLPT, a transition liquid phase appears, separating two layering phases, which is accompanied by abnormal variations in density, potential energy, and pressure perpendicular to the wall. The Voronoi cluster analysis reveals a self-adaptive local structural evolution in confined Fe–Ni liquids during the LLPT. The effect of temperature, pressure, and composition on the LLPT is investigated. Based on the pressure-confinement phase diagram, the LLPT is mainly induced by confinement under high pressure, while under low pressure, the LLPT is mainly pressure-driven. Our research will stimulate more interest in the phase transition under confinement in a metallic system.

Introduction

Two or more liquid states may exist, even for single-component substances, a phenomenon known as liquid polymorphism. The transition between these states is referred to as a liquid–liquid phase transition (LLPT).1−4 LLPT is one of the most mysterious phenomena in liquid science, and its presence and absence have often been debated for various systems. Since this problem is of fundamental importance in our understanding of the liquid state, LLPT has continued to attract considerable attention. Conventionally, phase transitions are predominantly induced by the temperature and pressure. Confinement, in which rich and peculiar phase behaviors occur that deviate substantially from their three-dimensional counterparts, has also been reported to cause phase transitions. For example, the spontaneous freezing of 2D liquid water in hydrophobic nanoslits transforms into 2D ice.5 Monolayer water exhibits remarkably diverse and intricate phase behavior, which is highly influenced by the van der Waals pressure exerted within the nanochannel. In addition, carbon nanotubes and graphene sheets have been experimentally utilized as confined spaces to investigate the diverse and unique phase transition behaviors of various materials. Therefore, confined spaces provide new opportunities for studying phase transition behaviors.

Phase transitions under nanoconfinement are also crucial in industrial adsorption and catalytic processes. Micro- and mesoporous adsorbents, with pore widths under 2 nm and 2–50 nm, respectively, are extensively utilized in the chemical, petrochemical, gas processing, and pharmaceutical industries for separations and pollution control and as catalysts and catalyst supports. Additional application areas (e.g., in geology, oil recovery, and nanofabrication, including nanotemplating through nanoconfinement) are described in the excellent review article by Gelb et al.6 Therefore, understanding the phase behavior under nanoconfinement is crucial for the rational design and control of many processes and devices, both in the processing industries and in the emerging field of nanotechnology.

A nonuniformity of density is an intriguing indication of layering that occurs under confinement. Numerous researchers have conducted comprehensive studies of the structural origins of stratification phenomena. For instance, Ruiz-Barragan7 examined the properties of two-dimensional confined water between graphene sheets, which unveiled pronounced layering phenomena perpendicular to the graphene surface. Furthermore, Federico Sebastiani8 elucidates the nanoconfinement effects on the H-bond network of stratified water lamellae hosted within graphene-based two-dimensional pores. Cicero9 believes that the occurrence of stratification is due to the interference effect of two surfaces on water, and this effect is amplified under extreme constraints. The phenomenon of layering under confinement has garnered significant interest among researchers. However, a comprehensive and clear explanation of the structural basis for layering behavior remains elusive, and there is a lack of reported information about the structural changes of clusters under confinement. A thorough understanding of the layering transition behavior is crucial for comprehending the phenomenon of LLPT under confinement.

In the present study, molecular dynamics (MD) was employed to examine the phase transition in a confined Fe–Ni liquid by manipulating the slit sizes (SS) and pressure. Our findings reveal the underlying structural mechanism behind the delamination transition, which involves the adaptive evolution of clusters. Fe–Ni liquid has a wide range of applications in aerospace, the catalytic field,10,11 and electronic devices12−15 due to its advantages, such as a thermal expansion coefficient that is well-matched with ceramic and glass materials. The Fe–Ni liquid, whether in the form of 2D nanosheets or 1D nanorods, displays unsaturated metal active sites that enhance electron transfer and mass transport.16 Consequently, it is extensively utilized in the field of electrocatalysis.17,18 Understanding the phase transition behavior of the Fe–Ni liquid under nanoconfined constraints is crucial for the synthesis and design of low-dimensional materials.

Models and Methods

The simulation process is performed using the LAMMPS19 software package. The initial structure of the Fe50Ni50 alloy is constructed in a 20 × 20 × 2 face-centered cubic lattice unit box, which consists of 1600 atoms. The elements are randomly replaced to obtain the desired components. The Fe50Ni50 alloy is confined by virtual carbon walls.

The interaction potential between iron and nickel atoms is modeled using the second nearest-neighbor modified embedded-atom method (2NN MEAM) potential proposed by Wu et al.,20 which is accurate in calculating the physical properties of metals and alloys, such as elastic constants, diffusivities, and melting points.21−23 The interactions between the carbon wall and Fe–Ni liquid are described by 12–6 Lennard-Jones (LJ) potentials, which generally take the form

where ε and rm, respectively, represent the depth of the potential well and its location. εFe–C = 0.043 eV, σFe–C = 2.221 Å,24 εNi–C = 0.023049 eV, σNi–C = 2.852 Å.25 The wall is perpendicular to the z-axis, and periodic boundary conditions are applied along the x and y directions.

The MD process is solved using the velocity Verlet algorithm with a time step of 1 fs in an NPT system, where the temperature and pressure are controlled by a Nose-Hoover thermostat and a pressure controller, respectively. A constant pressure P∥ is applied parallel to the wall. The SS ranges from 7.0 to 14.0 Å along the z-axis.

The simulation process consisted of the following steps: (a) the solid with a selected composition of Fe50Ni50 is heated to 3000 K at a rate of 1 K/ps using the virtual wall. (b) The system is then allowed to relax for 1 ns to reach equilibrium. (c) The temperature gradually decreases from 3000 to 2000 K at a cooling rate of 1 K/ps. (d) The system is again allowed to relax at 2000 K for 2 ns to achieve a stable structure.

The above steps are repeated while varying the pressure P∥ = (0–30 GPa) and the Fe–Ni liquids to explore their effects on the system.

Results and Discussion

LLPT in Nanoconfined Fe–Ni Liquids

Layering Phenomenon and LLPT

We investigate the density distribution ρ(z) of nanoconfined Fe–Ni liquids along the z-axis at various SS values in Figure 1. This is achieved by calculating the atomic number density within a thin slice with a thickness of 0.05 Å in the z-direction. (We also examined the density distribution and snapshots at various SS and pressures and compositions, as illustrated in the Supporting Information: Figures S1 and S2.)

Density distribution ρ(z) and snapshots of the nanoconfined Fe–Ni liquids at Fe50Ni50, P∥ = 10 GPa: (a) SS = 7.6 Å, (b) SS = 8.0 Å, and (c) SS = 8.8 Å. (d) Pair distribution functions (PDFs) at different SS. (e) Local bond orientational order parameters Q6 at different SS.

As shown in Figure 1a, at Fe50Ni50, P∥ = 10 GPa, SS = 7.6 Å, the Fe–Ni liquid shows a clear bilayer phase. As shown in Figure 1b, when SS = 8.0 Å, a “virtual” layer appears between the two layers near the wall, and a shoulder peak is observed. As shown in Figure 1c, when SS = 8.8 Å, the Fe–Ni liquid exhibits a trilayer phase, with the peak being more pronounced compared to SS = 8.2 Å. We demonstrate that there is a transition phase separating two layering phases. The characteristic of the transition phase is the appearance of shoulder peaks in the density distribution function.

At an SS of 8.0 Å, the emergence of shoulder peaks is attributed to surface separation. We argue that surface separation is closely related to the SS, which is not only influenced by the interaction strength between metals and carbon (εFe–C and εNi–C) but also involves metal–metal interactions. Our results indicated that given that εFe–C > εNi–C, Fe atoms in the confined space are always found to be near the C wall, while Ni atoms move toward the slit center. As the SS expands, the trend of surface separation increases; however, metal–metal interactions counteract this trend. The competition between the confinement effect of the confined space and the metal–metal interactions results in a periodic process of surface separation which results in the emergence of the shoulder peak followed by the remixing of Fe and Ni atoms, which makes the shoulder peak gradually disappear, and finally increases the number of liquid layers (forming new main peaks).

To further characterize the ordering degree of the layered structures, Figure 1d,e shows the corresponding pair distribution functions (PDFs) and local bond orientational order (BOO) parameters for the different SS, respectively. For all layering phases, the intensity of the first peak is relatively strong, indicating the presence of short-range order; however, the second and third peaks of the transition phase are much weaker, indicating the disappearance of long-range order. As for the layering phase, their second and third peaks are much higher and more clear than those of the transition phase, meaning that the ordering degree of the layering phase is much higher than that of the transition phase.

The PDF is not sufficient to effectively distinguish the ordering degrees of the layered structures. Therefore, we introduced BOO, which is widely applied in the study of phase transitions.1 BOO, first proposed by Steinhardt,26 describes the geometries of atomic clusters and has been frequently used for classifying the structures of solid-like clusters in liquids and glasses. The BOO parameters are defined as

where Nb is the number of bonds with lengths shorter than rc, and θi and ϕi are the polar and azimuthal angles of the i-th bond, respectively. These angles are defined with reference to bond vectors r, and a rotationally invariant order parameter can be constructed using spherical harmonics Ylm(θ,ϕ) for a set of bonds.

We evaluate one of the BOO parameters, Q6, to characterize the LLPT. For isotropic liquids, Q6 = 1, and for anisotropic liquids, Q6 = 0. Figure 1e presents the variation of Q6 with respect to the SS. The cutoff radius is chosen as the distance between the first and second peaks of the PDF. Clearly, Q6 exhibits distinct values for the layering phase (0.025–0.1) and the transition phase (0.325–0.4). This indicates that there are transition phases separating two layering phases. When the layering phase transitions to the transition phase, the order is abruptly lost from the layering phase to the transition phase. Therefore, we believe that the layering phase and transition phase are two different liquid phases.

Overall, we distinguish between the layering phase and the transition layer by employing the PDFs and the Q6 order parameter. Based on the number of layers, we categorize the layering phase into bilayer phase, trilayer phase, tetralayer phase, etc. The corresponding SS for the transition phase are (7.8–8.6 Å, 9.8–10.6 Å, 11.6–14 Å), while the corresponding SS for the layering phase are (7.0–7.6 Å, 8.8–9.6 Å, 10.8–11.4 Å). As for the confined Fe–Ni liquids, the total density of layering phases and transition phase exhibits a distinct decreasing trend as the slit widens. This indicates that the layering transition is an LLPT.

Potential Energy and Volume

Restricted metallic liquids have significant differences compared to bulk liquids. Figure 2 shows the variation curve of Ep and V of the confined liquids under P∥ = 10 GPa, where the SS ranges from 7 to 14 Å. From the graph, it can be seen that as the SS increases, Ep and V fluctuate.

Average potential energy (Ep) and volume (V) as a function of SS.

Generally, if no layering phenomenon occurs, the potential energy of the confined system should decrease as the width of the slit widens. This is because the shrinking contacting area between Fe–Ni liquids and the wall reduces the surface energy, while the bulk free energy remains unchanged. Considering the constant temperature condition (constant kinetic energy), the potential energy of the system would decrease, just as the reduction of surface energy. This explains the decreasing feature in the potential energy for the transition phase. However, if the layering phase appears, then the ordered layering feature would make its potential energy much lower than that of transition phases. Thus, the system experiences an elevation of potential energy during the transition from a transition phase to a layering phase.

Regulating Layering Transition

The occurrence of LLPT is attributed to changes in SS, which, in turn, leads to the phenomenon of surface separation. To elucidate the mechanisms of surface separation, we computed the variations in the distance (D) between two layers adjacent to atomic walls. This distance δ = (DFe– DNi) is depicted in Figure 1b, corresponding to the distance between the peaks of the density distribution curves near the walls.

As illustrated in Figure 3a, the distances of both the Fe and Ni layers from the walls increase with increasing SS. It is particularly noteworthy that during the transition from the layering phase to the transition phase, DFe increases significantly and abruptly, whereas DNi increases gradually with SS. The observed increase in the distance between the Fe and Ni layers, as shown in Figure 3b, is a precursor to surface separation.

(a) Total interlayer spacing of Fe and Ni atoms changes with the variation of SS. (b) Difference in interlayer spacing between Fe and Ni changes with the variation of SS.

The difference in equilibrium distances (σFe–C–σNi–C) is 0.631 Å, indicating that the Fe–C distance is longer by this amount compared with the Ni–C distance. This variation in equilibrium distances between Fe and C atoms and Ni and C atoms, due to changes in SS, results in fluctuations in δ with increasing. When the difference in distances δ exceeds 0.631 Å, surface separation occurs, resulting in the formation of a transition phase. In contrast, when this difference is less than 0.631 Å, a layering phase is observed.

Self-Adaptive Structural Evolution during LLPT

Important structural information beyond pair distribution can be extracted from the bond-angle distribution function (BADF), which defines the probability distribution P(h) for the angle h between a chosen particle and its two nearest neighbors.27

As shown in Figure 4a, when SS = 8.0 Å, the calculated distribution exhibits a prominent peak near 58°, aligning closely with the internal angle of an equilateral triangle (60°). The broad peak’s position is in close proximity to the internal angle of a regular pentagon (108°), indicating the existence of pentagonal bipyramidal structures in the liquid. These structures are primarily attributed to defective icosahedral rather than the more prevalent defective body-centered cubic structures.28 For SS = 7.6 Å, peaks correspond to 58, 90, 118, and 168°. Similarly, for SS = 8.8 Å, peaks at 58, 91, 119, and 171° closely resemble the peak values of the BADFs for face-centered cubic or hexagonal close-packed (fcc/hcp) structures: 90, 120, and 180°.29 This suggests the presence of a significant number of crystalline-like clusters in the layering phase.

(a) Bond-angle distribution functions (BADFs) for three different SS. (b) Evolution of representative Voronoi polyhedra (VPs) with increasing SS at Fe50Ni50, P∥ = 10 GPa. (c) Total fractions of icosahedron-like and crystalline-like VPs are compared; the inset shows the variation ∂Fraction/∂SS with respect to SS. (d) Configurations of seven typical VPs and the competing relationship between icosahedron-like VPs and crystalline-like VPs.

The Voronoi tessellation is commonly used to describe the arrangement and symmetry of the nearest-neighbor atoms around the central atom. Each Voronoi polyhedron (VP) is labeled by the Voronoi index <i3, i4, i5, i6>,30−33 where each i represents the number of faces with j edges. <0,0,12,0> is generally called a full icosahedron, where the central atom is surrounded by 12 neighboring atoms, and all 12 bonds are 5-fold.29,34−36 As shown in Figure 4b, ≪0,2,8,2> is a deformed icosahedron, while <0,2,8,1> and <0,1,10,2> are considered defective icosahedra according to previous publications.37 Therefore, ≪0,2,8,2>, <0,2,8,1>, and <0,1,10,2> are classified as icosahedron-like VP here. According to the work of Guder et al.,38 <0,4,4,2> and <0,3,6,2> are crystalline-like VPs, <0,3,6,3> is a mixed cluster, and <0,4,4,3> is a crystal cluster. They are distorted fcc/hcp VPs.38 Therefore, <0,4,4,2>, <0,3,6,2>, <0,3,6,3>, and <0,4,4,3> can be regarded as crystalline-like VPs.

Figure 4c displays a comparison of the total fraction between the icosahedron-like VP and crystalline-like VP. We can divide the fraction curve into two regions: SS = 7.6–8.0 Å and SS = 8.0–8.8 Å. At SS = 7.6–8.0 Å, the crystalline-like VP decreases rapidly, while the icosahedron-like VP increases rapidly. Conversely, at SS = 8.0–8.8 Å, the situation reverses compared to SS = 7.6–8.0 Å. Regardless of whether it is present in the layering phase or transition phase, the proportion of crystalline-like VP is higher than that of icosahedron-like VP. In comparison to the transition phase, the layering phase exhibits a higher abundance of crystalline-like VP, while the transition phase contains a greater proportion of icosahedron-like VP. The inset of Figure 4c depicts the changes in the ∂Fraction/∂SS with respect to the SS. The intersection of the two curves at 8.0 Å signifies a layering transition, consistent with Figure 1. As the SS varies, the system undergoes a transition from the layering phase to the transition phase and back to the layering phase, which is the result of the adaptive structural evolution of clusters within the confined liquids.

Figure 4d shows that competitions occur between icosahedron-like VPs (including ≪0,2,8,2>, <0,2,8,1>, and <0,1,10,2>) and crystalline-like VPs (including <0,4,4,2>, <0,3,6,2>, <0,3,6,3>, and <0,4,4,3>) to varying degrees. Consequently, experiments were conducted to investigate the impact of SS on these two types of VPs. The findings revealed that crystalline-like VPs facilitate liquid layering, while icosahedron-like VPs hinder liquid layering.

Effect of Pressure and Composition on LLPT

As shown in Figure 5a, with an increase in the SS from 7.0 to 14.0 Å, the ρ of the confined system fluctuates and decreases for all three components: Fe65Ni35, Fe50Ni50, and Fe35Ni65. The density profiles of the three fractions show similar fluctuations. Specifically, the density profiles indicate higher values with increasing Fe content, suggesting that the Fe65Ni35 liquids form a denser structure. As shown in Figure 5c, the potential energy curve of Fe65Ni35 is the lowest, indicating that the denser the atomic arrangement, the more stable the system.

(a) Density (ρ) of the system, (b) pressure perpendicular to the walls (P⊥), and (c) Ep as a function of SS at P∥ = 0 GPa, under three different compositions: Fe65Ni35 (red), Fe50Ni50 (blue), and Fe35Ni65 (purple). (d–f), ρ, P⊥, and Ep are functions of the SS for Fe50Ni50 under three different pressures: 0 GPa (red), 5 GPa (blue), and 10 GPa (purple).

The layering phase transition of Fe65Ni35 occurs at SS = 8.2 Å, which is earlier than the transition of Fe35Ni65 (SS = 8.6 Å). The results indicate that the higher the iron content in the restricted Fe–Ni liquids, the more likely LLPT will occur. As depicted in Figure 5b, the pressure perpendicular to the wall decreases in the layering phase regions. However, it increases during the transitions from the layering phase to the transition phase. This is due to an increase in the number of layers, resulting in an increase in the pressure perpendicular to the wall. Additionally, there is a rapid decrease in the average potential energy of the system, as shown in Figure 5c, indicating that the system becomes unstable during the transitions from the layering phase to the transition phase.

Next, we studied the LLPT under different pressures and with varying SS. From Figure 5d–f, it can be observed that as the pressure increases, the atomic density also increases. The pressure variation perpendicular to the wall becomes more intense, resulting in lower overall potential energy and greater stability.

Phase Diagram

Based on the aforementioned analysis, the LLPT of Fe–Ni liquids is influenced by factors such as restricted distance, pressure, and composition. To gain a deeper understanding of this phase transition, we have constructed a phase diagram for restricted Fe–Ni liquids, as depicted in Figure 6. Figure 6a–c illustrates the pressure-confinement phase diagrams for Fe65Ni35, Fe50Ni50, and Fe35Ni65, respectively. The various colored regions on the diagram correspond to the bilayer, trilayer, tetralayer, pentalayer, hexalayer, and transition phases. As the nickel content increases, the bilayer phase region expands gradually, while the hexalayer region diminishes.

Pressure-confinement phase diagram of restricted Fe–Ni liquids: (a) Fe65Ni35, (b) Fe50Ni50, and (c) Fe35Ni65.

The multilayered liquid phase is affected by pressure in the low-pressure zone, making layer transitions more susceptible to pressure changes. Conversely, the lower layers of the liquid phase in the low-pressure zone are significantly influenced by the fissure size, making layer transitions more susceptible to SS induction. At a constant pressure, with increasing Fe content, the critical SS for LLPT reduces. As for the reason, we believe it is because of the strong interaction between Fe atoms and the wall, which makes Fe atoms adhere to the wall compared to Ni atoms. When more Fe atoms are introduced to the confined liquids, the near-wall layer would be closer to the wall, leaving more space for accommodating atoms in the middle layers. This leads to a decrease in the critical SS of LLPT.

Conclusions

In this work, we conducted MD simulations to investigate the behavior of Fe–Ni liquids under confinement. The LLPT is influenced by lateral pressure, composition, and SS, leading to a variation in the number of liquid layers. Based on our results, the following conclusion can be made:

1.The Fe–Ni liquids undergo LLPT within confined spaces, characterized by distinct density stratification and fluctuations in potential energy and volume. Based on the PDF and BOO, layered liquids are divided into a layering phase and a transition phase. When δ < 0.631 Å, it is considered as the layering phase; otherwise, it is considered as the transition phase.

The Fe–Ni liquids undergo LLPT within confined spaces, characterized by distinct density stratification and fluctuations in potential energy and volume. Based on the PDF and BOO, layered liquids are divided into a layering phase and a transition phase. When δ < 0.631 Å, it is considered as the layering phase; otherwise, it is considered as the transition phase.

2.During the process of LLPT, clusters adapt their structural evolution to the changing SS. In the layering phase, the crystalline-like VPs dominate, while in the transition phase, the icosahedron-like VPs are more advantageous. The competition between the two types of VPs leads to a transition from the layering phase to the transition phase.

During the process of LLPT, clusters adapt their structural evolution to the changing SS. In the layering phase, the crystalline-like VPs dominate, while in the transition phase, the icosahedron-like VPs are more advantageous. The competition between the two types of VPs leads to a transition from the layering phase to the transition phase.

3.Pressure and composition can also regulate the LLPT of confined liquids. Higher Fe content or increased pressure leads to a denser atomic structure and a more stable configuration. The pressure-confined phase diagrams for different compositions offer a more intuitive and comprehensive reflection: in the low-pressure region, the stratification transition of multilayered liquid phases is mainly influenced by pressure, while the stratification transition of fewer-layered liquid phases is primarily affected by SS. Conversely, in the high-pressure region, the size of the slit plays a dominant role in regulating the LLPT.

Pressure and composition can also regulate the LLPT of confined liquids. Higher Fe content or increased pressure leads to a denser atomic structure and a more stable configuration. The pressure-confined phase diagrams for different compositions offer a more intuitive and comprehensive reflection: in the low-pressure region, the stratification transition of multilayered liquid phases is mainly influenced by pressure, while the stratification transition of fewer-layered liquid phases is primarily affected by SS. Conversely, in the high-pressure region, the size of the slit plays a dominant role in regulating the LLPT.

In experiments, high-temperature X-ray diffraction, volume variation measurements, and flash DSC are employed to detect the characteristics of confined liquids, including PDFs, density changes, pressure anomalies, and heat absorption peaks indicative of LLPT.

Our present study explicitly and systematically elucidated the structural characteristics of LLPT under constrained circumstances, offering insights into the potential regulation of such phase transitions.
