Sharp quantitative Talenti’s inequality in particular cases
P. Acampora, J. Lamboley

Abstract
In this paper, we focus on the famous Talenti’s symmetrization inequality, more precisely its L p L^{p} corollary asserting that the L p L^{p} -norm of the solution to − Δ ​ v = f ♯ -\Delta v=f^{\sharp} is higher than the L p L^{p} -norm of the solution to − Δ ​ u = f -\Delta u=f (we are considering Dirichlet boundary conditions, and f ♯ f^{\sharp} denotes the Schwarz symmetrization of f : Ω → ℝ + f:\Omega\to\mathbb{R}_{+} ). We focus on the particular case where functions f f are defined on the unit ball, and are characteristic functions of a subset of this unit ball. We show in this case that stability occurs for the L p L^{p} -Talenti inequality with the sharp exponent 2.

Introduction

In this paper, we will investigate some stability versions of Talenti’s inequality in particular cases. Given
\Omega\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}
a bounded open set and
f\in L^{2}(\Omega)
, we will denote by
u_{f}
the unique weak solution to

\left\{\begin{array}[]{ccccc}-\Delta u_{f}&=&f&\textrm{ in }&\Omega,\\
u_{f}&=&0&\textrm{ on }&\partial\Omega.\end{array}\right.

(1.1)

The aim of Talenti’s inequality is to compare
u_{f}
and
u_{f^{\sharp}}
where
f^{\sharp}:\Omega^{\sharp}\to\mathbb{R}
is the Schwarz symmetrization of
f
, defined on
\Omega^{\sharp}
the centered ball of same volume as
\Omega
(see Definition
2.1
): more precisely Talenti’s inequality (Theorem
2.4
) states that if
f\geq 0
, then

\forall x\in\Omega^{\sharp},\;\;u_{f}^{\sharp}(x)\leq v(x),

where
v=u_{f^{\sharp}}
solves

\left\{\begin{array}[]{ccccc}-\Delta v&=&f^{\sharp}&\textrm{ in }&\Omega^{\sharp}\\
v&=&0&\textrm{ on }&\partial\Omega^{\sharp}.\end{array}\right.

(1.2)

and
u_{f}^{\sharp}
is the Schwarz symmetrization of
u_{f}
. This ponctual comparison implies in particular the following: for every
p\in[1,+\infty]
,

\|u_{f}\|_{L^{p}(\Omega)}\leq\|v\|_{L^{p}(\Omega^{\sharp})}.

(1.3)

Moreover, as shown in
[
2
]
(see Theorem
2.5
), equality is realized in (
1.3
) for some
p\in[1,+\infty]
only if
\Omega
is a ball and
f=f^{\sharp}
up to translations, see also
[
16
]
.

In this paper we are interested in quantitative versions of (
1.3
). Notice that there are two parameters that are symmetrized in these inequalities:
\Omega
and
f
. Therefore the stability inequalities we may be seeking for should take into account both the distance from
f
to
f^{\sharp}
and from
\Omega
to
\Omega^{\sharp}
: to evaluate the asymmetry of
\Omega
for example, we denote

\alpha(\Omega)=\min_{x_{0}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}}\left\{\frac{|\Omega\Delta B_{r}(x_{0})|}{|B_{r}|},\;|B_{r}|=|\Omega|\right\}

the Fraenkel asymmetry of
\Omega
(
B_{r}(x)
denotes the ball of radius
r>0
and centered at
x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
; we also denote
B_{r}=B_{r}(0)
the centered ball of radius
r
).

As far as we know, there are only two partial results in this direction:

1.

in
[
9
]
, the author focuses on the case
f\equiv 1
and shows that for every
p\in[1,+\infty]
, there exists
c=c(n,p)
such that for every
\Omega
bounded open set in
\mathbb{R}^{n}
,

\begin{split}&\|v\|^{p}_{L^{p}(\Omega^{\sharp})}-\|u_{1}\|^{p}_{L^{p}(\Omega)}\geq c\alpha(\Omega)^{2+p}\qquad\textrm{ if }p\in[1,+\infty),\\
&\|v\|_{L^{\infty}(\Omega^{\sharp})}-\|u_{1}\|_{L^{\infty}(\Omega)}\geq c\alpha(\Omega)^{3}.\end{split}

2.

in
[
3
]
the authors focus on the case
p=\infty
: it is shown that for any
\Omega
bounded open set of
\mathbb{R}^{n}
and any nonnegative
f\in L^{2}(\Omega)
, there exists
c=c(n,|\Omega|,f^{\sharp})
and
\theta=\theta(n)
such that

\|v-u^{\sharp}\|_{L^{\infty}(\Omega^{\sharp})}\geq c\left(\alpha(\Omega)^{3}+\inf_{x_{0}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}}\Big\|f-f^{\sharp}(\cdot+x_{0})\Big\|_{L^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{n})}^{\theta}\right).

Note that the proof of Talenti’s inequality shows that
v-u^{\sharp}
is radially decreasing, so that
\lVert v-u^{\sharp}\rVert_{L^{\infty}(\Omega^{\sharp})}=\lVert v\rVert_{L^{\infty}(\Omega^{\sharp})}-\lVert u\rVert_{L^{\infty}(\Omega)}
.

In the same paper, the authors also obtain a partial result in the case
p=2
, namely:

\|v\|_{L^{2}(\Omega^{\sharp})}^{2}-\|u\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}^{2}\geq c\alpha(\Omega)^{4}.

In both of these results, the obtained exponents are not expected to be sharp (also in the second result,
\theta
does not seem to be explicit).

The goal of this paper is to provide a first quantitative result for (
1.3
) with a sharp exponent. In order to start this investigation with only one parameter (similarly to
[
9
]
where the author assumed
f\equiv 1
), we will assume that
\Omega=B_{1}
is a centered ball of radius 1, so that
\Omega=\Omega^{\sharp}
and the only parameter is
f
. Moreover, we will assume
f=\chi_{E}
to be the characteristic function of a set
E\subset B_{1}
: in this case we denote
u_{E}
for
u_{\chi_{E}}
, and
f^{\sharp}=\chi_{B_{*}}
where
B_{*}
is the centered ball of same volume as
E
. This will allow us to use a geometric approach and the framework of shape derivatives. In this setting, we obtain the following stability result:

Theorem 1.1
.

Let
n\geq 1
,
p\in[1,+\infty]
, and
m\in(0,|B_{1}|)
. Then there exists
c=c(p,m,n)>0
such that for every measurable set
E\subset B_{1}
with
\lvert E\rvert=m
we have

\lVert u_{B_{*}}\rVert_{L^{p}(B_{1})}-\lVert u_{E}\rVert_{L^{p}(B_{1})}\geq{c}\lvert E\Delta B_{*}\rvert^{2},

(1.4)

where
B_{*}
is the centered ball of volume
m
.

Moreover, we show in Propositions
2.2
,
2.4
and
2.4
that the exponent 2 obtained in Theorem
1.1
is sharp. In a forthcoming work
[
1
]
we will generalize the strategy introduced in the present paper to investigate the more general case where
f
is not assumed to be a characteristic function.

The proof of
Theorem 1.1
will be divided in three cases depending on the value of
p
. In the special case
p=1
, the proof is quite straightforward and relies only on Talenti’s inequality: we provide the proof in Section
2.2
, which is based on the study of the following shape optimization problem:

\max\Set{\lVert u_{E}\rVert_{1}}{\begin{gathered}|E|=m,\\
|E\Delta B_{*}|=\delta\end{gathered}}

for
m\in(0,|B_{1}|)
and
\delta>0
, which can be seen as looking for the worst set of given asymmetry, with regard of the Talenti deficit
\|u_{B_{*}}\|_{1}-\|u_{E}\|_{1}
. We identify explicitely the solution of this problem as a target-shaped set (it is the union of a ball and an annulus, see
Section

2.2
).

In the case
p>1
, we were not able to solve explicitly
\max\{\|u_{E}\|_{p},|E|=m,|E\Delta B_{*}|=\delta\}
, we therefore follow a strategy developed in
[
18
,
19
,
5
,
6
]
to prove (
1.4
). In fact the case
1<p<\infty
falls into the following framework: given a function
j:\mathbb{R}_{+}\to\mathbb{R}
, we study the stability for the following shape optimization problem:

\max\Set{\mathcal{J}(E)}{\lvert E\rvert=m},\qquad\qquad\mathcal{J}(E):=\int_{B_{1}}j(u_{E}(x))dx

(1.5)

which is solved by the centered ball
B_{*}
if
j
is non-decreasing.
Classically, under convexity assumption on
j
, such problem can be relaxed in the class

\mathcal{M}_{m}:=\Set{V\in L^{\infty}(B_{1})}{\begin{gathered}0\leq V\leq 1,\\
\int_{B_{1}}V=m\end{gathered}}

where
m\in(0,|B_{1}|)
, and we define
\mathcal{J}(V):=\int_{B_{1}}j(u_{V})
for
V\in L^{\infty}(B_{1})
non-negative. More precisely we prove:

Theorem 1.2
.

Let
n\geq 1
,
m\in(0,\lvert B_{1}\rvert)
, and
j\in C^{1}(\mathbb{R}_{+})\cap C^{2}((0,+\infty))
be such that
j^{\prime}(0)\geq 0
and
j^{\prime\prime}(s)>0
for every
s>0
, and such that

\limsup_{s\to 0^{+}}s^{\alpha}j^{\prime\prime}(s)<+\infty

(1.6)

for some
\alpha<1
. Then there exists a positive constant
c=c(j,m,n)
such that for every
V\in\mathcal{M}_{m}
we have

\mathcal{J}(B_{*})-\mathcal{J}(V)\geq c\lVert V-\chi_{B_{*}}\rVert_{1}^{2}.

where
B_{*}
is the centered ball of volume
m
.

Remark 1.3
.

Note that assumption (
1.6
) implies that
j^{\prime}
is locally
\beta:=(1-\alpha)
-Hölder continuous. This follows easily from the equality
j^{\prime}(y)-j^{\prime}(x)=\int_{x}^{y}j^{\prime\prime}(t)dt
.

Proof of
1.1
from
1.2
when
p\in(1,\infty)
:

Let
p\in(1,\infty)
: we consider
j:s\in[0,\infty)\mapsto s^{p}
, which is
C^{2}
in
(0,\infty)
and
C^{1}
up to 0, with
j^{\prime}(0)=0
and
j^{\prime\prime}>0
on
(0,\infty)
, and satisfies (
1.6
).

Therefore,
1.2
applies, and there exists
c=c(p,m,n)
such that

\lVert u_{B_{*}}\rVert_{p}^{p}\left(1-\left(\frac{\lVert u_{E}\rVert_{p}}{\lVert u_{B_{*}}\rVert_{p}}\right)^{p}\right)\geq c\lvert E\Delta B_{*}\rvert^{2}.

Then the result follows by using the inequality

1-x^{p}\leq p(1-x)\qquad\qquad\forall x\geq 0.

∎

Finally, we give a seperate proof of

when

Outline of the paper

In the following section, we recall basic definitions on Schwarz symmetrization, and then produce a proof of
1.1
in the particular case
p=1
. We then provide useful results to prepare the proof of
Theorem 1.2
: in particular we prove that under suitable assumptions on

,

\mathcal{J}(B_{*})=\max\Set{\mathcal{J}(V):=\int_{B_{1}}j(u_{V}(x))dx}{V\in\mathcal{M}_{m}},

that
B_{*}
is the unique maximizer, and that for any small
\delta>0
there exists
E_{\delta}
such that
|E_{\delta}|=m
,
|E_{\delta}\Delta B_{*}|=\delta
and

\mathcal{J}(E_{\delta})=\max\Set{\mathcal{J}(V)}{\begin{gathered}V\in\mathcal{M}_{m},\\
\lVert V-\chi_{B_{*}}\rVert_{1}=\delta\end{gathered}}.

We also show that the exponent 2 in the stability result of
1.1
and
1.2
is sharp.

We then focus on the proof of
1.2
, which is split in the next two sections:
in
Section

3
, we show that the proof of
1.2
reduces to a Fuglede type of result (
3.1
) asserting that stability occurs for smooth deformations of
B_{*}
. This strategy falls into the strategy of “selection principle” first introduced in the setting of shape optimization by Cicalese and Leonardi
[
8
]
to prove the quantitative isoperimetric inequality: the main tool here is the quantitative baththub principle proved in
[
19
]
.

Section

4
, the most technical part of the paper, is devoted to the proof of
3.1
. It requires a computation of first and second order shape derivative of the energy (
Section

4.1
), a proof of the coercivity of the second order optimality condition (
Section

4.2
), and a continuity property of the second order derivative to control the Taylor expansion (
Section

4.3
).

In
Section

5
we provide the proof of
1.1
for
p=+\infty
. The proof uses a representation of the solution with the Green function of the ball, and relies on adapting the shape derivative approach of
Section

3
and

to the

norm. This requires to compute a second order shape derivative of such norm, which up to our knowledge, is new (see
[
14
]
for a computation of a first order shape derivative in a similar context).

Finally, we detailed in the appendix some results about the way convergence of functions may imply the convergence of level sets, that did not seem completely new but for which the literature felt a bit elliptic, so we provided precise proofs for these statements used in Section 3.

Tools and first results

2.1
Symmetrization and Talenti’s inequality

We refer the reader to
[
22
]
or
[
16
]
for an overview about definitions and properties of rearrangements.

Definition 2.1
.

Let
\Omega\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}
be a bounded open set, and let
u:\Omega\to\mathbb{R}
be a measurable function. We define the
distribution function

\mu_{u}:[0,+\infty)\,\to[0,+\infty)
of
u
as the function

\mu_{u}(t)=\lvert\Set{x\in\Omega\,,\,\lvert u(x)\rvert>t}\rvert.

Definition 2.2
.

Let
u:\Omega\to\mathbb{R}
be a measurable function. We define the
decreasing rearrangement

u^{*}
of
u
as

u^{*}(s)=\inf\Set{t>0\,,\;\mu_{u}(t)\leq s}.

Definition 2.3
.

Let
u:\Omega\to\mathbb{R}
be a measurable function. We define the
Schwarz rearrangement

u^{\sharp}
of
u
as

u^{\sharp}(x)=u^{*}(\omega_{n}\lvert x\rvert^{n})\qquad\qquad x\in\Omega^{\sharp},

where
\Omega^{\sharp}
denotes the centered ball having the same volume as
\Omega
.

The next result is the famous symmetrization result obtained by G. Talenti, see
[
21
, Theorem I]
.

Theorem 2.4

(Talenti’s comparison)
.

Let
\Omega\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}
be a bounded open set, let
f\in L^{2}(\Omega)

and let
v_{f}\in{H^{1}_{0}}(\Omega)
and
v_{f^{\sharp}}\in{H^{1}_{0}}(\Omega^{\sharp})
be the unique solutions to

\left\{\begin{array}[]{cccc}-\Delta u_{f}&=&f&\text{ in }\Omega,\\
u_{f}&=&0&\text{ on }\partial\Omega,\end{array}\right.\qquad\qquad\left\{\begin{array}[]{cccc}-\Delta u_{f^{\sharp}}&=&f^{\sharp}&\text{ in }\Omega^{\sharp},\\
u_{f^{\sharp}}&=&0&\text{ on }\partial\Omega^{\sharp}.\end{array}\right.

Then

u_{f}^{\sharp}\leq u_{f^{\sharp}}.

In particular, for every set
E
, letting
u_{E}=u_{\chi_{E}}
and
u_{E^{\sharp}}=u_{\chi_{E^{\sharp}}}
, we have

u_{E}^{\sharp}\leq u_{E^{\sharp}}.

The following result solves the case of equality in Talenti’s inequality and was first proved in
[
2
, Theorem 1]
.

Theorem 2.5
.

Let
\Omega\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}
be a bounded and open set, and let
f\in L^{2}(\Omega)
a non-negative function such that
f\not\equiv 0
. If
u_{f}^{\sharp}=u_{f^{\sharp}}
almost everywhere, then there exists
x_{0}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
such that up to negligible sets
\Omega=x_{0}+\Omega^{\sharp}
,
f(\cdot)=f^{\sharp}(\cdot+x_{0})
, and
u_{f}(\cdot)=u_{f^{\sharp}}(\cdot+x_{0})
almost everywhere.

2.2
Proof of Theorem
1.1
for
p=1

In the case
p=1
, the proof of Theorem
1.1
is quick and relies only of Talenti’s symmetrization result. It also shows the importance of the following annulus.

Definition 2.6
.

Let
m\in(0,\lvert B_{1}\rvert)
. For every
\delta\in(0,\min\{2m,2(|B_{1}|-m)\})
we define the
optimal
\delta
-asymmetric radial open set

A_{\delta}
as

A_{\delta}=B_{r_{1}(\delta)}\cup\Set{x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}}{r_{*}<\lvert x\rvert<r_{2}(\delta)},

where
r_{*}
is the radius of
B_{*}
the ball of volume
m
, and
r_{1}(\delta)
and
r_{2}(\delta)
are chosen in such a way that

\lvert A_{\delta}\Delta B_{*}\rvert=\delta.

Namely, we have

r_{1}(\delta)=\frac{1}{\lvert B_{1}\rvert^{1/n}}\left(m-\frac{\delta}{2}\right)^{\frac{1}{n}}\qquad\textrm{ and }\qquad r_{2}(\delta)=\frac{1}{\lvert B_{1}\rvert^{1/n}}\left(m+\frac{\delta}{2}\right)^{\frac{1}{n}}.

The next result shows that among sets
E
of given volume and such that
|E\Delta B_{*}|=\delta
,
A_{\delta}
is the worst set with regard to the deficit
\|u_{B_{*}}\|_{1}-\|u_{E}\|_{1}
.

Proposition 2.7
.

Let
j:s\in\mathbb{R}_{+}\mapsto s
, i.e. for every measurable set
E\subset B_{1}
,

\mathcal{J}(E)=\int_{B_{1}}u_{E}\,dx.

Let
m\in(0,|B_{1}|)
and
\delta\in(0,\inf\{2m,2(|B_{1}|-m)\})
. Let
E\subset B_{1}
be a measurable set such that
|E|=m
and
|E\Delta B_{*}|=\delta
where
B_{*}
is the centered ball of volume
m
. Then

\mathcal{J}(E)\leq\mathcal{J}(A_{\delta}),\;\;i.e.\;\;\;\int_{B_{1}}u_{E}\,dx\leq\int_{B_{1}}u_{A_{\delta}}\,dx.

where
A_{\delta}
is defined in Definition
2.2
.

Proof.

By linearity we may write

u_{E}=u_{E\cap B_{*}}+u_{E\cup B_{*}}-u_{B_{*}}.

Using Talenti’s comparison result (see
2.4
), we know that

u_{E\cap B_{*}}^{\sharp}\leq u_{B_{r_{1}(\delta)}},\qquad\qquad u_{E\cup B_{*}}^{\sharp}\leq u_{B_{r_{2}(\delta)}}.

Integrating over
B_{1}
the two inequalities, and using the equi-measurability of the Schwarz rearrangement, we get:

\begin{split}\int_{B_{1}}u_{E}\,dx&=\int_{B_{1}}u_{E\cap B_{*}}^{\sharp}\,dx+\int_{B_{1}}u_{E\cup B_{*}}^{\sharp}\,dx-\int_{B_{1}}u_{B_{*}}\,dx\\
&\leq\int_{B_{1}}u_{B_{r_{1}(\delta)}}\,dx+\int_{B_{1}}u_{B_{r_{2}(\delta)}}\,dx-\int_{B_{1}}u_{B_{*}}\,dx=\int_{B_{1}}u_{A_{\delta}}\,dx.\end{split}

∎

Proposition 2.8
.

With the same notations as in
Section

2.2
, there exists a positive constant
c=c(m,n)
such that

\mathcal{J}(B_{*})-\mathcal{J}(E)\geq c\lvert E\Delta B_{*}\rvert^{2},

for every measurable set
E\subseteq B_{1}
such that
\lvert E\rvert=m
. Moreover, the exponent 2 is optimal, in the sense that the inequality cannot be valid for any lower exponent.

Proof.

Let
E\subset B_{1}
of volume
m
. Then
\delta=|E\Delta B_{*}|\in[0,\min\{2m,2(|B_{1}|-m)\})
. If
\delta=0
then
E=B_{*}
a.e. and
\mathcal{J}(B_{*})=\mathcal{J}(E)
.
If however
\delta>0
, then we have by Proposition
2.2
:

\mathcal{J}(B_{*})-\mathcal{J}(E)\geq\mathcal{J}(B_{*})-\mathcal{J}(A_{\delta}).

We introduce
w\in H^{1}_{0}(B_{1})
the unique solution to
-\Delta w=1
in
B_{1}
. Classical computations lead to
w(x)=w(\lvert x\rvert)=\frac{1-|x|^{2}}{2n}
. Moreover

\displaystyle\mathcal{J}(B_{*})-\mathcal{J}(A_{\delta})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\int_{B_{1}}(-\Delta w)[u_{B_{*}}-u_{A_{\delta}}]=\int_{B_{1}}w[\chi_{B_{*}}-\chi_{A_{\delta}}]

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle|\mathbb{S}^{n-1}|\left(\int_{r_{1}(\delta)}^{r_{*}}w(r)r^{n-1}dr-\int_{r_{*}}^{r_{2}(\delta)}w(r)r^{n-1}dr\right)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{|\mathbb{S}^{n-1}|}{2n(n+2)}\Big(r_{1}(\delta)^{n+2}+r_{2}(\delta)^{n+2}-2r_{*}^{n+2}\Big)

When
n=1
we compute explicitely
r_{1}(\delta)^{3}+r_{2}(\delta)^{3}-2r_{*}^{3}=\frac{3}{2^{4}}m\delta^{2}
so that
c=m/2^{4}
works. In the case
n\geq 2
, we use that the function
\varphi:x\in(0,1)\mapsto x^{(n+2)/n}
is strongly convex (
\varphi^{\prime\prime}\geq\frac{2(n+2)}{n^{2}}
), so that

\begin{split}r_{1}(\delta)^{n+2}+r_{2}(\delta)^{n+2}-2r_{*}^{n+2}&=\varphi\left(\frac{m-\frac{\delta}{2}}{|B_{1}|}\right)+\varphi\left(\frac{m+\frac{\delta}{2}}{|B_{1}|}\right)-2\varphi\left(\frac{m}{|B_{1}|}\right)\\[7.0pt]
&\geq\frac{2(n+2)}{n^{2}}\left(\frac{\delta}{2|B_{1}|}\right)^{2}\end{split}

hence the result with
c=\frac{1}{4n^{2}\omega_{n}}
.
Finally, to show that the exponent is optimal, we use a Taylor expansion in the previous computation to obtain:

\mathcal{J}(B_{*})-\mathcal{J}(A_{\delta})=\frac{m^{2/n-1}}{4n^{2}\omega_{n}^{2/n}}\delta^{2}+o(\delta^{2}).

Therefore, if
\alpha\in(0,2)
then

\frac{\mathcal{J}(B_{*})-\mathcal{J}(A_{\delta})}{|B_{*}\Delta A_{\delta}|^{\alpha}}\xrightarrow[\;\delta\to 0\;]{\;}0.

∎

Remark 2.9
.

We notice that the case

implies

is in

, convex and

2.3
Optimization among densities

In this section, we prove the following:

Proposition 2.10
.

Let
j\in C^{0}(\mathbb{R}_{+})
be a convex non-decreasing function and
m\in(0,|B_{1}|)
. Then the functional
\mathcal{J}:V\in\mathcal{M}_{m}\mapsto\int_{B_{1}}j(u_{V})
is well-defined, convex, and

\mathcal{J}(B_{*})=\max_{V\in\mathcal{M}_{m}}\mathcal{J}(V).

(2.1)

Moreover, the following properties hold:

1.

for any
\delta\in(0,\min\{2m,2(|B_{1}|-m)\})
, we define the set of
fixed asymmetry
weights

\mathcal{\mathcal{M}}_{m}^{\delta}:=\Set{V\in\mathcal{M}_{m}\;,\;\lVert V-\chi_{B_{*}}\rVert_{1}=\delta}.

Then the optimization problem

\sup_{V\in\mathcal{M}_{m}^{\delta}}\mathcal{J}(V)

(2.2)

admits a bang-bang solution, which means there exists a set
E_{\delta}
such that

\mathcal{J}(E_{\delta})=\max_{V\in{\mathcal{M}_{m}^{\delta}}}\mathcal{J}(V).

2.

if
j
is strictly convex and strictly increasing in
(0,\infty)
, then
\chi_{B_{*}}
is the unique maximizer to (
2.1
).

Before proving this result, we state first a classical elliptic regularity theorem that will be used often throughout the paper. We refer for instance to
[
13
, Theorem 9.13, Theorem 8.16]
.

Theorem 2.11
.

Let
q>\max\{1,n/2\}
. Then there exists
C=C(n,q)
such that for every
f\in L^{q}(B_{1})
,

\lVert u_{f}\rVert_{2,q}\leq C\lVert f\rVert_{q},

where
u_{f}{\in H^{1}_{0}(B_{1})}
is the unique solution to (
1.1
).

Also, we need a few technical results:
classically, weak-
*

L^{\infty}
convergence does not imply convergence of the
L^{1}
-norm, but if one has some control on the sign of the involved functions, then one can retrieve such convergence:

Lemma 2.12
.

Let
h\in L^{\infty}(B_{1})
and
(h_{k})
be a sequence of functions in

such that

We assume that there exists

It is sufficient to notice that under these assumptions,

\begin{split}h_{k}^{+}\xrightharpoonup{*-L^{\infty}({B_{1}})}h^{+}=h\chi_{E},\\[7.0pt]

Lemma 2.13
.

Let
m\in(0,|B_{1}|)
and
\delta\in(0,\min\{2m,2(|B_{1}|-m)\})
. Then the sets
\mathcal{M}_{m},\mathcal{M}_{m}^{\delta}
are convex and compact with respect to the weak-
*

L^{\infty}
topology, and their extremals are characteristic functions.

Proof.

•

The convexity for
\mathcal{M}_{m}
is immediate by definition, while for
\mathcal{M}_{m}^{\delta}
, we need to show that if
W_{0},W_{1}\in\mathcal{M}_{m}^{\delta}
and

W_{\alpha}=\alpha W_{1}+(1-\alpha)W_{0},

for
\alpha\in(0,1)
, then
\lVert W_{\alpha}-V_{0}\rVert_{1}=\delta
. This is true since
0\leq W_{\alpha}\leq 1
, and by definition of

we have

W_{\alpha}\leq V_{0}\text{ in }B_{*},\qquad\qquad W_{\alpha}\geq V_{0}\text{ in }B_{1}\setminus B_{*},

so that explicitly computing the

norms,

\lVert W_{\alpha}-V_{0}\rVert_{1}=\alpha\lVert W_{1}-V_{0}\rVert_{1}+(1-\alpha)\lVert W_{0}-V_{0}\rVert_{1}=\delta.

•

\mathcal{M}_{m}
is compact because if
W_{k}
weakly-
*
converges in
L^{\infty}
to some

, then

m=\lim_{k}\int_{B_{1}}W_{k}\,dx=\int_{B_{1}}W\,dx.

For what regards
\mathcal{M}_{m}^{\delta}
, if we take a sequence
W_{k}\in\mathcal{M}_{m}^{\delta}
converging to
W
weakly-
*
in
L^{\infty}
, then the functions

satisfy the assumptions of

2.3

\delta=\lim_{k}\lVert W_{k}-\chi_{B_{*}}\rVert_{1}=\lVert W-\chi_{B_{*}}\rVert.

•

The fact that extremal points of
\mathcal{M}_{m}
are characteristic functions is classical (see for instance
[
15
, Prop. 7.2.17]
). Let us detail the same result for
\mathcal{M}_{m}^{\delta}
: let

, and assume that

we must show that

is not extremal. Since

, we have

0<\delta=\lVert W-\chi_{B_{*}}\rVert_{1}=2\int_{B_{*}}(1-W)\,dx\leq 2\lvert\set{0<W<1}\cap B_{*}\rvert.

Analogously we prove

such that

Under these assumptions we can write

2.4
Sharpness of the exponent

We show that the exponent 2 is sharp in
1.2
, in the sense that for
\alpha\in(0,2)
, one can find a sequence of sets
E_{k}\in\mathcal{M}_{m}\setminus\{B_{*}\}
such that

\frac{\mathcal{J}(B_{*})-\mathcal{J}(E_{k})}{|B_{*}\Delta E_{k}|^{\alpha}}\xrightarrow[\;k\to\infty\;]{\;}0.

We proceed as in
Section

2.2
by using the annulus
A_{\delta}
(who is a candidate as being the worst asymmetric set with regard to Talenti’s deficit, though we are not in position to prove it except in the case of the
L^{1}
-norm). More precisely, we show the following result:

Proposition 2.14
.

Let
m\in(0,\lvert B_{1}\rvert)
, and let
j\in C^{1}(\mathbb{R}_{+})
be a non-constant, non-decreasing, convex function. Then there exists
\bar{\delta}>0
and a positive constant
C=C(j,m,n)
such that for every
\delta\in(0,\bar{\delta})
,

\frac{1}{C}\delta^{2}\leq\mathcal{J}(B_{*})-\mathcal{J}(A_{\delta})\leq C\delta^{2}.

(2.5)

For the proof of
Section

2.4
and in several other places in this paper, we need to define the notion of adjoint state (see also
[
15
, Section 5.8]
):

Definition 2.15

(Adjoint State)
.

Let
j\in C^{1}(\mathbb{R}_{+})
. For every
V\in L^{2}(B_{1})
nonnegative, we define the
adjoint state

w_{V}
of
u_{V}
as the unique function solving in the weak sense the
adjoint problem

\begin{cases}-\Delta w_{V}=j^{\prime}(u_{V})&\text{in }{B_{1}},\\[5.0pt]
w=0&\text{on }\partial{B_{1}},\end{cases}

namely,

\int_{{B_{1}}}\nabla w_{V}\cdot\nabla\varphi\,dx=\int_{B_{1}}j^{\prime}(u_{V})\varphi\qquad\qquad\forall\varphi\in H^{1}_{0}({B_{1}}).

(2.6)

When
V=\chi_{E}
we will write
w_{E}:=w_{\chi_{E}}
.

Proof of
Section

2.4
.

For every
\delta\in[\,0,\min\{2m,\,2|B_{1}|-2m\}\,)
, let us define
u_{\delta}=u_{A_{\delta}}
,
u_{0}=u_{B_{*}}
, and
w_{\delta}=w_{A_{\delta}}
,
w_{0}=w_{B_{*}}
. Moreover, since these functions are all radial, we will identify, with a slight abuse of notation

u_{\delta}(x)=u_{\delta}(\lvert x\rvert),\qquad\qquad w_{\delta}(x)=w_{\delta}(\lvert x\rvert).

By convexity of
j
, we have

\int_{B_{1}}j^{\prime}(u_{\delta})(u_{0}-u_{\delta})\,dx\leq\mathcal{J}(B_{*})-\mathcal{J}(A_{\delta})\leq\int_{B_{1}}j^{\prime}(u_{0})(u_{0}-u_{\delta})\,dx.

After two integration by parts, we may rewrite

\int_{B_{1}}w_{\delta}(\chi_{B_{*}}-\chi_{A_{\delta}})\,dx\leq\mathcal{J}(B_{*})-\mathcal{J}(A_{\delta})\leq\int_{B_{1}}w_{0}(\chi_{B_{*}}-\chi_{A_{\delta}})\,dx.

(2.7)

Noticing that
\chi_{B_{*}}-\chi_{A_{\delta}}
is non-zero only between the radii
r_{1}(\delta)
and
r_{2}(\delta)
, we focus our attention on the values of
w_{\delta}
and
w_{0}
near
\lvert x\rvert=r_{*}
, where we recall that
B_{*}=B_{r_{*}}
.

•

Estimates for
w_{0}
:

by Taylor expansion,

w_{0}(r)=w_{0}(r_{*})+\partial_{r}w_{0}(r_{*})(r-r_{*})+R_{1}(r),

(2.8)

with

R_{1}(r)=\int_{r_{*}}^{r}\left(\partial_{r}w_{0}(s)-\partial_{r}w_{0}(r_{*})\right)\,dr.

Since
w_{0}
solves the equation

, then by classical elliptic regularity (
2.11
) we have that

for some
\beta\in(0,1)
, so that

\lvert\partial_{r}w_{0}(r_{*})-\partial_{r}w_{0}(s)\rvert\leq C\lvert s-r_{*}\rvert^{\beta},

and

\lvert R_{1}(r)\rvert\leq C\lvert r-r_{*}\rvert^{1+\beta}.

Therefore, noticing that (see
Section

2.2
for the definition of

\int_{B_{1}}(\chi_{B_{*}}-\chi_{A_{\delta}})\,dx=0\qquad\qquad\chi_{B_{*}}(r)-\chi_{A_{\delta}}(r)=\sgn(r_{*}-r)\chi_{(r_{1},r_{2})}(r),

we obtain by (
2.8
) and (
2.9
)

\int_{B_{1}}w_{0}(\chi_{B_{*}}-\chi_{A_{\delta}})\,dx=\partial_{r}w_{0}(r_{*})|\mathbb{S}^{n-1}|\int_{r_{1}}^{r_{2}}r^{n-1}\lvert r-r_{*}\rvert\,dr+R_{2}(\delta)

with

\lvert R_{2}(\delta)\rvert\leq C\lvert r_{2}-r_{1}\rvert^{2+\beta}.

In particular, since

, we have

\int_{B_{1}}w_{0}(\chi_{B_{*}}-\chi_{A_{\delta}})\,dx\leq C\delta^{2}+o(\delta^{2}),

(2.10)

so that, with (
2.7
), the upper bound in (

) is proven for a suitable choice of

.

•

Estimates for
w_{\delta}
:
we now need to adapt the previous estimate with
w_{\delta}
instead of
w_{0}
: to that end, we first notice that
w_{\delta}
smoothly converges to
w_{0}
for
\delta
that goes to 0. Indeed, since

, we have from classical elliptic regularity (see
2.11
) that

In particular, the continuity of

ensures that

converges in

to

and, for any

This convergence, joint with (

) and (

) ensures that for some uniform positive constant

we have

(2.11)

with

\lvert R_{3}(r)\rvert\leq C\lvert r-r_{*}\rvert^{1+\beta}.

(2.12)

If

is small enough, since

and

we know by Hopf’s lemma that

B_{1}
, we obtain
|\{w_{\delta}=t\}|=0
. This shows the existence of
t_{\delta}
, and also that
|\{w_{\delta}>t_{\delta}\}|=|\{w_{\delta}\geq t_{\delta}\}|
. Finally, we show that
t_{\delta}
is unique: let
\widehat{t}
be such that
|\{w>\widehat{t}\}|
is also equal to
m
, and assume for example
t_{\delta}\leq\widehat{t}
(the other case being similar). Then the set
\{t_{\delta}<w<\widehat{t}\}
is open with zero measure, so it is empty. But as
m\in(0,|B_{1}|)
,
\min w_{\delta}<t_{\delta}\leq\widehat{t}<\max w_{\delta}
, so by connectedness of
B_{1}
and continuity of
w_{\delta}
, the set
\{t_{\delta}<w<\widehat{t}\}
can be empty only if
t_{\delta}=\widehat{t}
.

•

Second, we show that
\widetilde{E}_{\delta}
is a
W^{2,q}
deformation of
B_{*}
. First, by classical elliptic estimates (
2.11
)
u_{\delta}
converges to
u_{0}
in
W^{2,q}(B_{1})
for every
q<\infty
. As
j
is locally
\beta:=(1-\alpha)
-Hölder continuous (see
section 1
) this implies that
w_{\delta}
converges to
w_{0}
in
W^{2,q}
for every
q<\infty
. On the other hand,
j^{\prime}(u_{0})
is
C^{1}
far from
\partial B_{1}
, so that
w_{0}\in W^{3,q}(B_{1-\varepsilon};\mathbb{R}^{n})
for every small
\varepsilon
.
Moreover, since
0<m<\lvert B_{1}\rvert
, there exists a positive constant
c
such that
\lvert\nabla w_{0}\rvert\geq c
on
\partial B_{*}
.

Therefore, we are in position to apply
Section

A.1
to
w_{\delta}
and
Section

A.1
to
w_{\delta}-t_{\delta}
, so that the following holds: for every fixed
\varepsilon>0
and for
\delta<\delta_{0}(\varepsilon)
we can find deformations
\Phi_{\delta}\in W^{2,q}(B_{1};\mathbb{R}^{n})
such that
\lVert\Phi_{\delta}\rVert_{W^{2,q}}\leq\varepsilon
,
\Phi_{\delta}
are orthogonal to
\partial B_{*}
, and

\widetilde{E}_{\delta}=B_{*}^{\Phi_{\delta}}.

•

Third, by the convergence of
w_{\delta}
and the regularity of
\Phi_{\delta}
, we find constants
c,C>0
such that for
\delta
small enough,

\lvert\nabla w_{\delta}\rvert\geq c\qquad\textrm{ on }\partial B_{*}\quad\textrm{ and }\quad\mathcal{H}^{n-1}(\{w_{\delta}=t_{\delta}\})=\int_{\partial B_{*}}\jac^{\partial B_{*}}(\id+\Phi_{\delta})\,d\mathcal{H}^{n-1}\leq C.

(3.3)

We now compute, using the convexity of
j
,

\begin{split}\mathcal{J}(E_{\delta})&=\int_{B_{1}}j(u_{\delta})\,dx\leq\mathcal{J}(\widetilde{E}_{\delta})-\int_{B_{1}}j^{\prime}(u_{\delta})(\widetilde{u}_{\delta}-u_{\delta}),\end{split}

where
\widetilde{u}_{\delta}=u_{\widetilde{E}_{\delta}}
. We integrate by parts two times using
j^{\prime}(u_{\delta})=-\Delta w_{\delta}
, so that

\mathcal{J}(E_{\delta})\leq\mathcal{J}(\widetilde{E}_{\delta})-\int_{B_{1}}w_{\delta}(\chi_{\widetilde{E}_{\delta}}-\chi_{E_{\delta}}).

We now notice that thanks to the uniform estimates (
3.3
) and the quantitative bathtub principle (
3.3
) we get the existence of a uniform constant
c
such that

\mathcal{J}(\widetilde{E}_{\delta})-\mathcal{J}(E_{\delta})\geq c\lvert\widetilde{E}_{\delta}\Delta E_{\delta}\rvert^{2}.

(3.4)

On the other hand, since
\lVert\Phi\rVert_{W^{2,q}}<\varepsilon
, we can apply
3.1
when
\varepsilon
is small enough, which gives

\mathcal{J}(B_{*})-\mathcal{J}(\widetilde{E}_{\delta})\geq c\lvert\widetilde{E}_{\delta}\Delta B_{*}\rvert^{2},

(3.5)

also for a positive constant
c
. Finally, noticing that

\delta=\lvert E_{\delta}\Delta B_{*}\rvert\leq\lvert E_{\delta}\Delta\widetilde{E}_{\delta}\rvert+\lvert\widetilde{E}_{\delta}\Delta B_{*}\rvert,

we join (
3.4
) and (
3.5
), and we get the existence of a constant
c
such that

c\delta^{2}\leq 2c\left(\lvert E_{\delta}\Delta\widetilde{E}_{\delta}\rvert^{2}+\lvert\widetilde{E}_{\delta}\Delta B_{*}\rvert^{2}\right)\\
\leq\mathcal{J}(B_{*})-\mathcal{J}(E_{\delta}),

which is a contradiction and concludes the proof.
∎

Proof of
3.1

So far, we have proved

in the special case

, and we have reduced the proof of

(which contains

for

) to the proof of

asserting stability of

among smooth deformations. This section is dedicated to the proof of this last result, which is based on a shape derivative approach (see for example

1.

first, the computation of first and second order shape derivatives, that leads to the expression of the Lagrangian for problem (

),

2.

4.1
Computation of shape derivatives

In this section we compute the shape derivatives of the shape functional

E\longmapsto\mathcal{J}(E)=\int_{B_{1}}j(u_{E}).

We start by recalling some basic facts about shape derivatives.

Definition 4.1

(Shape derivative)
.

Let
s\in(0,1)
, let
\mathcal{O}
be a family of
C^{1,s}
sets compactly supported in
B_{1}
, and let

\mathcal{F}:\mathcal{O}\to X

with
X
a Banach space. Let
q>n
large enough to have
W^{2,q}(B_{1};\mathbb{R}^{n})\hookrightarrow C^{1,s}(B_{1};\mathbb{R}^{n})
. For every set
E\in\mathcal{O}
, we say that
\mathcal{F}
is shape differentiable at first or second order if the functional

\Phi\in W^{2,q}(B_{1};\mathbb{R}^{n})\longmapsto\mathcal{F}((\id+\Phi)(E))\in X.

(4.1)

admits Fréchet derivatives (of first or second order) at 0, and in this case, we
define the
shape derivatives
(denoted
\mathcal{F}^{\prime}(E)[\Phi]
and
\mathcal{F}^{\prime\prime}(E)[\Phi,\Psi]
) as the Fréchet derivatives at 0 of (
4.1
).

We will follow the classical approach (see for instance
[
15
, Theorem 5.3.2]
) to prove the existence and compute the shape derivative of
u_{E}
,
w_{E}
and finally
\mathcal{J}(E)
, in the next three results respectively. In the whole section,
E
will denote a set of class
C^{1,s}
such that
\overline{E}\subset B_{1}
, and
p
large enough so that
W^{1,q}(B_{1})\subset C^{0,s}(\overline{B_{1}})
. Then for every
\Phi\in W^{2,q}(B_{1};\mathbb{R}^{n})

E^{\Phi}:=(\id+\Phi)(E)

is also of class
C^{1,s}
, and
W^{1,q}(B_{1},\mathbb{R}^{n})
is an algebra.

We then define the functions

\displaystyle u_{\Phi}:=u_{E^{\Phi}}

\displaystyle w_{\Phi}:=w_{E^{\Phi}}

\displaystyle\widehat{u}_{\Phi}:=u_{E^{\Phi}}\circ(\id+\Phi)\qquad\qquad

\displaystyle\widehat{w}_{\Phi}:=w_{E^{\Phi}}\circ(\id+\Phi),

where
w_{E}
is the adjoint state defined in
Section

2.4
.
Let us recall the classical Hadamard formula that can be found for instance in
[
15
, Corollary 5.2.8]
.

Lemma 4.2

(Hadamard formula)
.

Let
T>0
and
F\in C^{1}([0,T);W^{1,\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{n};\mathbb{R}^{n}))
and we denote
F_{t}(x)=F(t,x)
. We assume
F_{t}
invertible for every
t\in[0,T)
. Then for every function
f\in C^{1}([0,T);L^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{n}))\cap C^{0}([0,T);W^{1,1}(\mathbb{R}^{n}))

\frac{d}{dt}\left(\int_{F_{t}(E)}f(t,x)dx\right)=\int_{F_{t}(E)}\left(\partial_{t}f(t,x)+\divv(f(t,x)\,{V_{t}}(x))\right)\,dx,

(4.2)

where
{V_{t}}(x)=\partial_{t}F_{t}(F_{t}^{-1}(x))
.

Remark 4.3
.

In particular, when
F_{t}=\id+t\Phi
with
\Phi\in W^{1,\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{n};\mathbb{R}^{n})
, we define

{\widetilde{\Phi}}_{t}(x):=V_{t}(x)=\Phi\circ F_{t}^{-1}(x).

Proposition 4.4

(Shape differentiability of
u_{\Phi}
)
.

The application

\Phi\in W^{2,q}(B_{1};\mathbb{R}^{n})\longmapsto u_{\Phi}\in W^{1,q}_{0}(B_{1})

is of class
C^{1}
in a neighborhood of 0. In particular, if
\Phi\in W^{2,q}(B_{1};\mathbb{R}^{n})
is small enough and we denote by
u_{t}:=u_{t\Phi}
, then for every
t\in[0,1]
we have that
u^{\prime}_{t}\in W^{1,q}_{0}(B_{1})
is the unique solution in
H^{1}_{0}(B_{1})
to

-\Delta u^{\prime}_{t}=({\widetilde{\Phi}}_{t}\cdot\nu_{t})\,d\mathcal{H}^{n-1}\,\raisebox{-1.07639pt}{$\mathbin{\vrule height=8.61108pt,depth=0.0pt,width=0.55974pt\vrule height=0.55974pt,depth=0.0pt,width=3.01385pt}$}_{\,\partial E^{t\Phi}},

(4.3)

(in the distributional sense), where
\widetilde{\Phi}_{t}=\Phi\circ(\id+t\Phi)^{-1}
and
\nu_{t}
is the exterior unit normal to
E^{t\Phi}
.

Proof.

•

If
\lVert\Phi\rVert_{2,q}<1
, we can invert the matrix
I_{n}+D\Phi
, so that, defining

J_{\Phi}:=\det(I_{n}+D\Phi)\qquad\qquad A_{\Phi}:=J_{\Phi}\,(I_{n}+D\Phi)^{-1}(I_{n}+D\Phi)^{-T},

we have in the distributional sense

-\divv(A_{\Phi}\nabla\widehat{u}_{\Phi})=\chi_{E}\,J_{\Phi}.

•

We introduce

X=H^{1}_{0}(B_{1})\cap W^{2,q}(B_{1})
and

\begin{array}[]{cccl}\mathcal{F}:&W^{2,q}(B_{1};\mathbb{R}^{n})\times X&\longrightarrow&L^{q}(B_{1})\\[7.0pt]
&(\Phi,\widehat{u})&\longmapsto&-\divv(A_{\Phi}\nabla\widehat{u})-\chi_{E}\,J_{\Phi}.\end{array}

Then
\mathcal{F}
is of class
C^{\infty}
in a neighbourhood of
0
.
Indeed,
we first notice that the application

(A,\widehat{u})\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times n}\times X\longmapsto-\divv(A\nabla\widehat{u})\in L^{p}(B_{1})

is of class
C^{\infty}
because it is linear and continuous in both variables. Analogously, using that
W^{1,q}
is an algebra, multilinearity and continuity in the origin imply that the applications

\displaystyle\Phi\in W^{2,q}(B_{1})\longmapsto(I_{n}+D\Phi)^{-1}=\sum_{k=1}^{+\infty}(D\Phi)^{k}\in W^{1,q}(B_{1})

\displaystyle\Phi\in W^{2,q}(B_{1})\longmapsto J_{\Phi}=\det(I_{n}+D\Phi)\in W^{1,q}(B_{1})

are of class
C^{\infty}
in a neighborhood of
0
. Therefore, the application

\Phi\in W^{2,q}(B_{1})\longmapsto A_{\Phi}\in W^{1,q}(B_{1})

is
C^{\infty}
in a neighborhood of
0
, as well as
\mathcal{F}
.

•

We are now in position to use the implicit function theorem to deduce from the previous point that the application

\Phi\in W^{2,q}(B_{1};\mathbb{R}^{n})\longmapsto\widehat{u}_{\Phi}\in X,

that satisfies
\mathcal{F}(\Phi,\widehat{u}_{\Phi})=0
is of class
C^{\infty}
in a neighborhood of 0.
Indeed,
\widehat{u}_{0}=u_{E}
and the map

\xi\in X\longmapsto D_{\widehat{u}}\mathcal{F}(0,u_{E})[\xi]=-\Delta\xi-\chi_{E},

is a diffeomorphism from
X
onto
L^{p}(B_{1})
thanks to the classical elliptic regularity (see for instance
[
13
, Theorem 9.14]
).

•

Then, by noticing that
u_{\Phi}=\widehat{u}_{\Phi}\circ(\id+\Phi)^{-1}
, we know that the application

\Phi\in W^{2,q}(B_{1};\mathbb{R}^{n})\longmapsto u_{\Phi}\in W^{1,q}_{0}(B_{1})

is
C^{1}
in a neighborhood of
0
(for instance, we can apply
[
15
, Lemma 5.3.3]
to
\widehat{u}_{\Phi}\circ(\id+\Phi)^{-1}
and
\nabla\widehat{u}_{\Phi}\circ(\id+\Phi)^{-1}
).
Let us also recall that
u_{t}
solves the weak equation:

\int_{B_{1}}\nabla u_{t}\cdot\nabla\varphi\,dx=\int_{E^{t\Phi}}\varphi\,dx\qquad\qquad\forall\varphi\in H^{1}_{0}({B_{1}}).

(4.4)

Using
Section

4.1
to differentiate (
4.4
), we get that
u^{\prime}_{t}
solves

\int_{B_{1}}\nabla u^{\prime}_{t}\cdot\nabla\varphi\,dx=\int_{\partial{E^{t\Phi}}}\varphi\,({\widetilde{\Phi}}_{t}\cdot\nu_{t})\,d\mathcal{H}^{n-1}\qquad\qquad\forall\varphi\in H^{1}_{0}({B_{1}}),

which is (
4.3
).

∎

Proposition 4.5

(Shape differentiability of
w_{\Phi}
)
.

Let
j\in C^{1}(\mathbb{R}_{+})\cap C^{2}((0,\infty))
such that

\limsup_{s\to 0^{+}}s^{\alpha}j^{\prime\prime}(s)<+\infty

for some
\alpha\in(0,1)
, and let
\varepsilon\in(0,1)
. Then the application

\Phi\in W^{2,q}(B_{1};\mathbb{R}^{n})\longmapsto w_{\Phi}\in W^{1,q}_{0}(B_{1})\cap W^{2,q}(B_{1-\varepsilon})

is of class
C^{1}
in a neighborhood of 0. In particular, if
\Phi\in W^{2,q}(B_{1};\mathbb{R}^{n})
is small enough and we denote by
w_{t}:=w_{t\Phi}
, then for
t\in[0,1]
we have that
w^{\prime}_{t}\in W^{1,q}_{0}(B_{1})
is the unique solution in
H^{1}_{0}(B_{1})
to

-\Delta w^{\prime}_{t}=j^{\prime\prime}(u_{t})u^{\prime}_{t},

(4.5)

where

is defined in
Section

4.1
.

Proof.

We proceed as in the proof of
Section

4.1
, with the extra difficulty that

is not
C^{2}
up to 0.

•

First we claim that the map

\mathcal{G}:\Phi\in W^{2,q}(B_{1};\mathbb{R}^{n})\longmapsto j^{\prime}(\widehat{u}_{\Phi})\in L^{q}(B_{1})\cap W^{1,q}(B_{1-\varepsilon/2})

is of class
C^{1}
near 0. Since
j^{\prime}
is
C^{1}
far from 0, we immediately have by
Section

4.1
that
\Phi\mapsto j^{\prime}(\widehat{u}_{\Phi})\in W^{1,q}(B_{1-\varepsilon/2})
is of class
C^{1}
. It remains to show that
\Phi\mapsto j^{\prime}(\widehat{u}_{\Phi})\in L^{q}(B_{1})
is
C^{1}
. As
j^{\prime}
is not assumed to be
C^{1}
up to 0, as it is done for instance in
[
12
, Lemma 2.5]
, we approximate the functional
\mathcal{G}
: for small

we define

\mathcal{G}_{\varepsilon}:\Phi\in W^{2,q}(B_{1};\mathbb{R}^{n})\longmapsto j^{\prime}(\varepsilon+\widehat{u}_{\Phi})\in L^{q}(B_{1}).

From the proof of
Section

4.1
, recalling

X=H^{1}_{0}(B_{1})\cap W^{2,q}(B_{1})\subset C^{1}(\overline{B_{1}}),

there exists
\mathcal{U}
neighborhood of 0 in
W^{2,q}(B_{1};\mathbb{R}^{n})
such that the map
\Phi\in\mathcal{U}\longmapsto\widehat{u}_{\Phi}\in X
is

. In particular
\lVert\widehat{u}_{\Phi}\rVert_{\infty}
is equibounded for
\Phi\in\mathcal{U}
, and since

, we get that
\mathcal{G}_{\varepsilon}
is
C^{1}
in
\mathcal{U}
and that
\mathcal{G}_{\varepsilon}(0)
converges to
\mathcal{G}(0)
in
L^{q}(B_{1})
. Once we prove that

\begin{array}[]{cccl}\mathcal{G}_{\varepsilon}^{\prime}:&{\mathcal{U}}&\to&\mathcal{L}(W^{2,q}(B_{1};\mathbb{R}^{n}),L^{q}(B_{1}))\\[5.69054pt]
&\Phi&\mapsto&\left(\eta\mapsto{j^{\prime\prime}(\varepsilon+\widehat{u}_{\Phi})\,\widehat{u}_{\Phi}^{\prime}[\eta]}\right)\end{array}

converges uniformly in
\Phi
, from
[
4

we can conclude that
\mathcal{G}
is
C^{1}
near 0. If we let
h(t)=tj^{\prime\prime}(t)
, then by assumption
h\in C^{0}({\mathbb{R}_{+}})
and by uniform continuity,
h(\varepsilon+\widehat{u}_{\Phi})
converges to

uniformly in
\Phi\in\mathcal{U}
. Therefore, it remains to check that

\lim_{\varepsilon\to 0^{+}}\sup_{\begin{subarray}{c}\Phi\in\mathcal{U}\\
\lVert\eta\rVert_{2,q}\leq 1\end{subarray}}\left\lVert\widehat{u}^{\prime}_{\Phi}[\eta]\left(\frac{1}{\varepsilon+\widehat{u}_{\Phi}}-\frac{1}{\widehat{u}_{\Phi}}\right)\right\rVert_{p}=0.

(4.6)

Up to choosing a smaller
\mathcal{U}
, there exist uniform positive constants

such that
\lVert\nabla\widehat{u}^{\prime}_{\Phi}[\eta]\rVert_{\infty}\leq C
, and
\lvert\nabla\widehat{u}_{\Phi}(y)\rvert\geq c
for every

(here we used that
\lvert\nabla u_{0}\rvert>0
near

). This leads to the following uniform estimate: for every

, and for every

,

\left\lvert\frac{\varepsilon\,\widehat{u}^{\prime}_{\Phi}[\eta](x)}{(\varepsilon+\widehat{u}_{\Phi}(x))\,\widehat{u}_{\Phi}(x)}\right\rvert\leq\frac{C\varepsilon(1-\lvert x\rvert)}{\varepsilon+c\,\max\{1-\lvert x\rvert,\,c\}}\leq\frac{C}{1+c\,\max\{1-\lvert x\rvert,\,c\}},

which, by dominated convergence, ensures (
4.6
).

•

As in the proof of
Section

4.1
, we obtain

\begin{array}[]{cccl}\mathcal{F}:&W^{2,q}(B_{1};\mathbb{R}^{n})\times X&\longrightarrow&L^{q}(B_{1})\\[7.0pt]
&(\Phi,\widehat{u})&\longmapsto&-\divv(A_{\Phi}\nabla\widehat{u})-j^{\prime}(\widehat{u}_{\Phi})\,J_{\Phi}.\end{array}

is
C^{1}
.
As

D_{\widehat{w}}\mathcal{F}(0,w_{E})[\xi]=-\Delta\xi-j^{\prime}(u_{E})

is a diffeomorphism of
X
onto

, the implicit function theorem applies, and
\Phi\in W^{2,q}(B_{1};\mathbb{R}^{n})\mapsto\widehat{w}_{\Phi}\in X
is of class

in a neighborhood of 0, and

\Phi\in W^{2,q}(B_{1};\mathbb{R}^{n})\longmapsto w_{\Phi}\in W^{1,q}_{0}(B_{1})

is
C^{1}
in a neighborhood of 0.
Moreover, the function

solves the weak equation (

), namely

\int_{B_{1}}\nabla w_{t}\cdot\nabla\varphi\,dx=\int_{B_{1}}j^{\prime}(u_{t})\varphi\,dx\qquad\qquad\forall\varphi\in H^{1}_{0}({B_{1}}).

(4.7)

Using
Section

4.1
, we can differentiate (

) to obtain that

solves

\int_{B_{1}}\nabla w^{\prime}_{t}\cdot\nabla\varphi\,dx=\int_{B_{1}}j^{\prime\prime}(u_{t})u^{\prime}_{t}\,\varphi\,dx\qquad\qquad\forall\varphi\in H^{1}_{0}({B_{1}}),

∎

Proposition 4.6

(Shape derivative of
\mathcal{J}
)
.

Let
j\in C^{1}(\mathbb{R}_{+})\cap C^{2}((0,+\infty))
be such that

\limsup_{s\to 0^{+}}s^{\alpha}j^{\prime\prime}(s)<+\infty

for some
\alpha\in(0,1)
. Then the application

\Phi\in W^{2,q}(B_{1};{\mathbb{R}^{n}})\longmapsto\mathcal{J}(E^{\Phi})\in\mathbb{R}

is of class
C^{2}
in a neighborhood of 0.
Moreover, if
\Phi\in W^{2,q}(B_{1};\mathbb{R}^{n})
is small enough, then
J(t)=\mathcal{J}(E^{t\Phi})
satisfies for
t\in[0,1]
,

\begin{split}J^{\prime}(t)=\int_{\partial E^{t\Phi}}w_{t}\,({\widetilde{\Phi}}_{t}\cdot\nu_{t})\,d\mathcal{H}^{n-1},\end{split}

(4.8)

where
w_{t}=w_{E^{t\Phi}}
is the adjoint state defined in
Section

2.4
,
\nu_{t}=\nu_{E^{t\Phi}}
is the outer unit normal to
E^{t\Phi}
, and
{\widetilde{\Phi}}_{t}=\Phi\circ(\id+t\Phi)^{-1}
. Moreover, letting
g_{t}={\widetilde{\Phi}}_{t}\cdot\nu_{t}
,

\begin{split}J^{\prime\prime}(t)=\int_{\partial E^{t\Phi}}\left(w^{\prime}_{t}g_{t}+g_{t}(\nabla w_{t}\cdot{\widetilde{\Phi}}_{t})\right)\,d\mathcal{H}^{n-1}+a_{t}(\Phi,\Phi),\end{split}

(4.9)

where

a_{t}(\Phi,\Phi)=\int_{\partial E^{t\Phi}}w_{t}\left(g_{t}\divv({\widetilde{\Phi}}_{t})-{((D{\widetilde{\Phi}}_{t}){\widetilde{\Phi}}_{t})}\cdot\nu_{t}\right)d\mathcal{H}^{n-1}.

Proof.

For every
\Phi\in W^{2,q}(B_{1};\mathbb{R}^{n})

\mathcal{J}(E^{\Phi})=\int_{B_{1}}j(u_{\Phi})\,dx,

so
Section

4.1
, joint with
j\in C^{1}(\mathbb{R}_{+})
, gives that
\Phi\mapsto\mathcal{J}(E^{\Phi})
is
C^{1}
.
Let us denote by
u_{t}:=u_{t\Phi}
. We begin by noticing that

J^{\prime}(t)=\int_{B_{1}}j^{\prime}(u_{t})u^{\prime}_{t}\,dx=\int_{B_{1}}\nabla w_{t}\cdot\nabla u^{\prime}_{t}\,dx=\int_{\partial E^{t\Phi}}w_{t}\,g_{t}\,d\mathcal{H}^{n-1}

where we used (
2.6
) and (
4.3
). Therefore, we have the following shape derivative

\mathcal{J}^{\prime}(E_{\Phi})[\Psi]=\int_{\partial E^{\Phi}}w_{E^{\Phi}}(\Psi\cdot\nu_{E^{\Phi}})\,d\mathcal{H}^{n-1}=\int_{E^{\Phi}}\divv(w_{E^{\Phi}}\Psi)\,dx.

By
Section

4.1
we know that the map
\Phi\mapsto w_{E^{\Phi}}\in W^{2,q}(B_{1-\varepsilon})
is of class
C^{1}
in a neighbourhood of 0. In particular, if
\Phi
and
\varepsilon
are small, we have
E^{\Phi}\subset B_{1-\varepsilon}
. Finally, since for every fixed

the map
\Phi\mapsto\int_{E^{\Phi}}f\,dx
is of class

in a neighbourhood of 0 (see for instance
[

, Theorem 5.2.2]
), then
\mathcal{J}
is of class
C^{2}
.
We now compute again

as

J^{\prime}(t)=\int_{E^{t\Phi}}\divv(w_{t}{\widetilde{\Phi}}_{t})\,dx,

so that by the Hadamard’s formula

4.1
we get

J^{\prime\prime}(t)=\int_{\partial E^{t\Phi}}\divv(w_{t}{\widetilde{\Phi}}_{t}){\widetilde{\Phi}}_{t}\cdot\nu_{t}\,d\mathcal{H}^{n-1}+\int_{\partial E^{t\Phi}}(w^{\prime}_{t}{\widetilde{\Phi}}_{t}+w_{t}\,\partial_{t}{\widetilde{\Phi}}_{t})\cdot\nu_{t}\,d\mathcal{H}^{n-1}.

Formula (
4.9
) finally follows by differentiating in

the equation
{\widetilde{\Phi}}_{t}\circ(\id+t\Phi)=\Phi
, leading to

\partial_{t}{\widetilde{\Phi}}_{t}=-(D{\widetilde{\Phi}}_{t}){\widetilde{\Phi}}_{t}.

∎

Remark 4.7
.

) in

When

Proof.

We define
m=|\{u>t_{*}\}|=|\{u_{k}>t_{k}\}|
. Since functions
u_{k}
converge uniformly, they are equi-bounded. In particular, this ensures that the sequence
t_{k}
is bounded in
\mathbb{R}
, otherwise we would either get for large
k
that
t_{k}>\sup_{j}\lVert u_{j}\rVert_{\infty}
, and
\lvert\set{u_{k}>t_{k}}\rvert=0
, or
t_{k}\leq\inf_{j}\essinf u_{j}
and
\lvert\set{u_{k}>t_{k}}\rvert=|\Omega|
. Therefore, up to passing to a subsequence, we can define
\bar{t}=\lim_{k}t_{k}
, and let
\varepsilon>0
. By
L^{\infty}
convergence we get that for large
k

\set{u>\bar{t}+2\varepsilon}\subseteq\set{u_{k}>\bar{t}+\varepsilon}\subseteq\set{u_{k}>t_{k}},

and therefore defining
\mu(t)=|\{u>t\}|
(this differs from
Section

2.1
as we did not assume
u
to be non-negative), we get

\mu(\bar{t}+2\varepsilon)\leq m.

Analogously

\set{u>\bar{t}-2\varepsilon}\supseteq\set{u_{k}>\bar{t}-\varepsilon}\supseteq\set{u_{k}>t_{k}},

and so

\mu(\bar{t}+2\varepsilon)\leq m\leq\mu(\bar{t}-2\varepsilon).

(A.1)

We now observe that
\mu
is strictly decreasing in
(\essinf_{\Omega}u,\|u\|_{\infty})
. Indeed, if
\mu
is constant in
(t_{1},t_{2})
with
\essinf u<t_{1}<t_{2}<\|u\|_{\infty}
, then
\lvert\Set{t_{1}<u\leq t_{2}}\rvert=0
. On the other hand, since
\Omega
is connected and
u
is continuous,
u^{-1}((t_{1},t_{2}))
is an open non-empty set, which has positive measure, thus a contradiction. Therefore, as by assumption
t_{*}\in(\essinf u,\|u\|_{\infty})
,
(
A.1
) implies

\bar{t}-2\varepsilon\leq t_{*}\leq\bar{t}+2\varepsilon,

and since
\varepsilon
is arbitrary,
\bar{t}=t_{*}
. The argument does not depend on the choice of the subsequence, and the conclusion follows.
∎

Proposition A.2
.

Let
q>n
, let
\Omega\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}
be an open bounded connected set, and let
u\in W^{3,q}(\Omega)
be a function such that for some positive constant
k

\lvert\nabla u\rvert(x)\geq k,\qquad\qquad\forall x\in u^{-1}(0).

Assume in addition that
d(u^{-1}(0),\partial\Omega)>0
. Let
u_{j}\in C^{2,\alpha}(\Omega)
be a sequence of functions such that

u_{j}\xrightarrow{W^{2,q}(\Omega)}u.

Then for every
j
large enough
u_{j}^{-1}(0)
is a
C^{1,s}
hypersurface, with
s=1-n/q
, and there exist deformations
\Phi_{j}\in W^{2,q}(\Omega;\mathbb{R}^{n})
such that

(i)

\Phi_{j}
are orthogonal to
u^{-1}(0)
;

(ii)

\displaystyle u_{j}^{-1}(0)=(\id+\Phi_{j})(u^{-1}(0));

(iii)

\displaystyle\lim_{j}\lVert\Phi_{j}\rVert_{2,q}=0

Proof.

•

Step 1:
we first notice that for every
\varepsilon>0
there exists
t_{0}
such that if
\lvert t\rvert\leq t_{0}
then

u^{-1}(t)\subseteq(u^{-1}(0))^{\varepsilon},

where we use the notation

(K)^{t}=\Set{p\in\mathbb{R}^{n}\;}{\;d(p,K)\leq t}
,
for the
outer parallel
set.

Indeed, let us assume by contradiction that there exist
{\varepsilon_{0}}>0
and points
y_{k}\in u^{-1}(t_{k})
with
\lim_{k}t_{k}=0
such that

\forall k\in\mathbb{N},\qquad d(y_{k},u^{-1}(0))>\varepsilon_{0}.

As
\Omega
is bounded, up to a subsequence, we may assume that
y_{k}
converge to some point
\bar{y}\in\overline{\Omega}
. The continuity of
u
gives
u(\bar{y})=0
, so that
\bar{y}\in u^{-1}(0)
. On the other hand,

d(\bar{y},u^{-1}(0))\geq\varepsilon_{0},

which is a contradiction.

•

Step 2:

We now want to extend the non-degeneracy property of the gradient of
u
to the functions
u_{j}
on their level sets
u_{j}^{-1}(0)
. We notice that by uniform convergence and by the previous step, for every
\varepsilon>0
there exist
t_{0}>0
and
j_{0}
such that

\forall j\geq j_{0},\qquad u_{j}^{-1}(0)\subset u^{-1}\left((-t_{0},t_{0})\right)\subset(u^{-1}(0))^{\varepsilon}.

(A.2)

On the other hand,
\nabla u
is uniformly continuous in
\Omega

so there exists
\alpha_{0}>0
such that

\forall(x,z)\in\Omega\textrm{ s.t. }|z-x|\leq\alpha_{0},\qquad\lvert\nabla u(z)-\nabla u(x)\rvert\leq\frac{k}{4}.

In particular,

\forall x\in(u^{-1}(0))^{\alpha_{0}},\qquad|\nabla u(x)|\geq\frac{k}{2}.

Also,
\nabla u_{j}
uniformly converge to
\nabla u
, so for
j
large enough

\lvert\nabla u_{j}(x)-\nabla u(x)\rvert\leq\frac{k}{8}\qquad\forall x\in\Omega.

From the previous estimates, we get
\lvert\nabla u_{j}\rvert(x)>0
for every
x\in u_{j}^{-1}(0)
and
j
large enough, which implies that the sets
u_{j}^{-1}(0)
are
C^{1,s}
hypersurfaces.

•

Step 3:

Let us denote
\Omega_{1}=(u^{-1}(0))^{\alpha_{0}}
and

\forall x\in\Omega_{1},\qquad\nu(x)=-\frac{\nabla u}{\lvert\nabla u\rvert}(x).

We show that for every
x\in\Omega_{1}
, the line

t\in[-\alpha_{0},\alpha_{0}]\mapsto x+t\nu(x)

intersects
u^{-1}(0)
. Let
x\in\Omega_{1}
. Up to choosing a smaller
\alpha_{0}
, using that
u^{-1}(0)
is far from
\partial\Omega
, we may assume that
(\Omega_{1})^{\alpha_{0}}\subset\Omega
. By Lagrange theorem applied to the function
\alpha\mapsto u(x+\alpha\nu(x))-u(x)-\alpha\nabla u(x)\cdot\nu(x)
, there exists
z
on the segment between
x
and
x+\alpha_{0}\nu(x)
such that

u(x+\alpha_{0}\nu(x))=u(x)-\alpha_{0}\lvert\nabla u\rvert(x)+\alpha_{0}\left(\nabla u(z)-\nabla u(x)\right)\cdot\nu(x)\leq u(x)-\frac{\alpha_{0}k}{4}.

Similarly we get

u(x-\alpha_{0}\nu(x))\geq u(x)+\frac{\alpha_{0}k}{4}.

•

Step 4:

Now we construct the diffeomorphisms
\Phi_{j}
. For every
x\in\Omega_{1}
, let us define

F_{j}(x,t)=u_{j}(x+t\nu(x))-u(x).

We show that
F_{j}
is strictly monotone in
t
and that it always admits a zero. First we notice that

\partial_{t}F_{j}(x,t)=\nabla u_{j}(x+t\nu(x))\cdot\nu(x).

and from Step 2 we deduce

\forall t\in[-\alpha_{0},\alpha_{0}],\quad\forall j\geq j_{0},\qquad\partial_{t}F_{j}(x,t)\leq-k/4.

By uniform convergence and the previous step, for
j
large enough we have

F_{j}(x,\alpha_{0})<0<F_{j}(x,-\alpha_{0})
. Therefore, there exists a unique
t_{j}(x)\in[-\alpha_{0},\alpha_{0}]
such that
F_{j}(x,t_{j}(x))=0
, or, equivalently

u_{j}(x+t_{j}(x)\nu(x))=u(x).

(A.3)

•

Step 5:

We claim that
\Phi_{j}(x):=t_{j}(x)\nu(x)
works (up to multiplying it by a cutoff function).

Property
(i)
follows by noticing that by construction
\nu
is orthogonal to
u^{-1}(0)
.

Let us now prove
(ii)
. First (
A.3
) for
x\in u^{-1}(0)
implies

(\id+\Phi_{j})(u^{-1}(0))\subset u_{j}^{-1}(0).

For the converse inclusion, let
y\in u_{j}^{-1}(0)
. The point
y
can be written as

y=x+d(y,u^{-1}(0))\bar{\nu}(x)

with
x\in u^{-1}(0)
and either
\bar{\nu}=\nu(x)
or
\bar{\nu}=-\nu(x)
. On the other hand, by (
A.2
), we have for large
j

u_{j}^{-1}(0)\subset(u^{-1}(0))^{\alpha_{0}},

so that
d(y,u^{-1}(0))<\alpha_{0}
. As a consequence,
d(y,u^{-1}(0))=\lvert t_{j}(x)\rvert
and we have proved that

(\id+\Phi_{j})(u^{-1}(0))=u_{j}^{-1}(0).

Finally,
(iii)
follows by noticing that (
A.2
) implies that
t_{j}
converges uniformly to
0
and by computing

\begin{split}\nabla t_{j}(x)&=-\frac{\partial_{x}F_{j}(x,t_{j}(x))}{\partial_{t}F_{j}(x,t_{j}(x))}\\
&=\lvert\nabla u\rvert(x)\frac{\nabla u_{j}(x+\Phi_{j}(x))-t_{j}(x)D\nu(x)\nabla u_{j}(x+\Phi_{j}(x))-\nabla u(x)}{\nabla u_{j}(x+\Phi_{j}(x))\cdot\nabla u(x)}.\end{split}

Since
W^{2,q}
is an algebra, computing the derivatives of
t_{j}
we iteratively get the convergence of higher order derivatives.

∎
