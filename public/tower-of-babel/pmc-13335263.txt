Longitudinal dynamics of harsh parenting and non-suicidal self-injury in Chinese adolescents: a three-wave RI-CLPM with basic psychological needs frustration as a mediator
Authors: Qingqi Zhang, Bingtao Zhou, Da Yi, Yixuan Dong, Ai Ma, Ke Qi
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectivesNon-suicidal self-injury (NSSI) is highly prevalent during adolescence and has been linked to adverse family environments. However, little is known about the dynamic mechanisms through which harsh parenting contributes to NSSI over time. This study examined the longitudinal associations among harsh parenting, basic psychological needs frustration (BPNF), and adolescent NSSI, and tested whether BPNF mediates this relationship at both the between-person and within-person levels.MethodsA three-wave longitudinal study was conducted among 1014 Chinese adolescents recruited from 10 middle schools in Beijing. Data were collected at three time points: February 2025 (T1), June 2025 (T2), and October 2025 (T3). Harsh parenting, BPNF, and NSSI were assessed using validated self-report measures. Cross-lagged panel models (CLPM) and random-intercept cross-lagged panel models (RI-CLPM) were estimated to distinguish stable between-person differences from within-person fluctuations over time, controlling for key demographic variables.ResultsThe CLPM indicated reciprocal associations between harsh parenting and NSSI and revealed a significant indirect pathway from harsh parenting to NSSI via BPNF. After accounting for stable between-person differences, the RI-CLPM showed that within-person increases in harsh parenting predicted subsequent increases in BPNF, which in turn predicted later increases in NSSI. The longitudinal indirect effect remained significant at the within-person level.ConclusionThese findings suggest that fluctuations in harsh parenting contribute to adolescent NSSI through increased frustration of basic psychological needs. Targeting family interactions and adolescents’ psychological need satisfaction may represent important avenues for preventing NSSI.Keywords: Adolescent, Non-suicidal self-injury, RI-CLPM, Harsh parenting, Basic psychological needs frustration

Introduction

NSSI is the direct, deliberate destruction of one’s own body tissue in the absence of suicidal intent [1]. Numbers show that in community samples, lifetime prevalence of NSSI was from 17% to 46%, while the overall prevalence among Chinese middle school students was 22.37% [2]. Teenage years are a common time for NSSI to start, and it gets worse during this time [3]. Adolescents who do NSSI not only hurt themselves physically, but they also have psychological problems and social difficulties, which makes more likely that they may hurt themselves again or think about suicide later [4].

Extensive research suggests that family environment, especially parenting practices, is a proximal factor in adolescent NSSI [5, 6]. In the Chinese cultural context, where obedience and respect for parents has always been emphasized [5], harsh parenting, which includes physical punishment, verbal abuse, hostility, and psychological manipulation [7], may be more usual. Prior studies have linked harsh parenting to adolescents’ maladaptive adjustment [8], and recent work has further connected it to self-injurious behaviors [9]. Although both cross-sectional and longitudinal studies have associated harsh parenting with adolescent NSSI [10, 11], prior longitudinal research has generally not distinguished between-person differences from within-person changes over time. As a result, it remains unclear whether increases in harsh parenting relative to an adolescent’s usual level predict later increases in NSSI. To address this gap, this study uses a three-wave RI-CLPM to examine the temporal ordering between harsh parenting and NSSI, as well as the mediating mechanism underlying this association among Chinese adolescents.

Harsh parenting and non-suicidal self-injury

A developmental psychopathology perspective may help explain the association between harsh parenting and adolescent NSSI. Harsh parenting is a chronic family-based risk factor that may undermine adolescents’ adaptive development over time. Repeated exposure to hostility, rejection, and coercive parenting may heighten emotional dysregulation, negative self-evaluation, and maladaptive coping, thereby increasing vulnerability to NSSI during adolescence [12]. Consistent with this perspective, cross-sectional studies have shown that harsh parenting is positively associated with adolescent NSSI [9, 13]. Longitudinal findings further suggest that harsh parenting predicts later emotional and behavioral maladjustment, including self-harm risk [14]. At the same time, reciprocal associations are also theoretically plausible. Harsh parenting may increase adolescents’ risk for later NSSI, whereas adolescent NSSI may in turn place strain on family interactions and evoke harsher parental responses. A cross-lagged framework is therefore needed to clarify the temporal ordering and potential bidirectionality of these associations.

However, less is known about whether these associations reflect stable between-person differences or within-person processes within the same adolescent, especially in Chinese samples. This distinction is important because perceived harsh parenting may vary within the same adolescent over time in response to changing family interactions and situational stressors. If such fluctuations are meaningful, they may help explain later within-person changes in BPNF and NSSI. Both the traditional cross-lagged panel model (CLPM) and the random-intercept cross-lagged panel model (RI-CLPM) are used to examine longitudinal associations across repeated measurements. The CLPM does not separate stable between-person differences from within-person fluctuations over time [15]. In contrast, the RI-CLPM decomposes each construct into a trait-like between-person component and a time-specific within-person component. This allows researchers to test whether deviations from an adolescent’s usual level on one variable predict later deviations on another variable [16]. Therefore, compared with the traditional CLPM, the RI-CLPM provides a more rigorous framework for examining within-person developmental processes.

Basic psychological needs frustration as a mediator

Although harsh parenting has been connected to teenager NSSI in past research, less is understood about how this link develop. Self-determination theory (SDT) highlights that frustration of the needs for autonomy, competence, and relatedness as a core marker of maladaptive functioning in adverse interpersonal contexts [17, 18]. Harsh parenting is inherently need-thwarting. Parent angry and refuse can make weak connection by reduce feeling of warm and safe [19]; punish and mind control parenting style can maybe stop autonomy by reduce teenager sense of control [20]; and always criticize and shame can damage feeling of ability, make a sense of not good enough in adolescents [21]. When such experiences recur over time, adolescents may come to feel chronically controlled, devalued, and emotionally disconnected, resulting in stronger frustration of their basic psychological needs. Research indicates that harsh and controlling parenting has been linked to more experience of psychological need frustration [22]. In this sense, BPNF may serve as a proximal and context-sensitive mechanism linking harsh parenting to adolescent NSSI, because it directly reflects adolescents’ experiences within ongoing parent–child interactions.

Furthermore, BPNF may increase vulnerability to NSSI. When basic psychological needs are always not met, it connects with stronger negative affect, less control, social disconnection, and lower self-worth [23]. NSSI may serve as a maladaptive coping response. For adolescents whose autonomy is frustrated, NSSI may provide a temporary sense of control over the body and pain [24]. When relatedness is frustrated, self-injury may communicate distress or compensate for unavailable social connection [25]. When competence is frustrated, NSSI may function as self-punishment in response to feelings of inadequacy [26]. Empirical research has further demonstrated a positive association between psychological needs frustration and NSSI, supporting to this theoretical framework [27]. Gu et al. reported that harsh parenting prospectively predicted adolescent NSSI and that this association was mediated in part by basic psychological needs frustration and self-concept clarity [28]. However, this longitudinal evidence did not distinguish stable between-person differences from within-person changes over time. At the same time, reciprocal associations among harsh parenting, BPNF, and NSSI are also possible, making a cross-lagged framework necessary to clarify their temporal ordering over time. Accordingly, the present study used RI-CLPM to test whether within-person increases in harsh parenting are followed by increases in BPNF and subsequent NSSI over time.

The current study

The present study used a three-wave longitudinal design to examine the associations among harsh parenting, BPNF, and NSSI in Chinese adolescents. It extends prior longitudinal research in three ways. First, it distinguishes stable between-person differences from within-person fluctuations by using RI-CLPM, thereby testing whether harsher-than-usual parenting predicts later increases in adolescent NSSI within the same individual. Second, it examines whether BPNF functions as a dynamic mediator at the within-person level rather than only as a general correlate or between-person mechanism. Third, by estimating both the traditional CLPM and the RI-CLPM, the study evaluates whether conclusions about temporal ordering and mediation differ across modeling approaches. On this basis, we proposed the following hypotheses:

Hypothesis 1 (Between-person). Harder parenting leads to greater average BPNF and NSSI, and higher BPNF is positively related with higher NSSI.

Hypothesis 1 (Between-person). Harder parenting leads to greater average BPNF and NSSI, and higher BPNF is positively related with higher NSSI.

Hypothesis 2 (Within-person). Within individuals, rises in BPNF will predict later rises in NSSI, and increases in harsh parenting will predict subsequent increases in both NSSI and BPNF.

Hypothesis 2 (Within-person). Within individuals, rises in BPNF will predict later rises in NSSI, and increases in harsh parenting will predict subsequent increases in both NSSI and BPNF.

Hypothesis 3 (Mediation). Both within-person and between-person relationships between harsh parenting and NSSI will be mediated by BPNF.

Hypothesis 3 (Mediation). Both within-person and between-person relationships between harsh parenting and NSSI will be mediated by BPNF.

Methods

Participants and procedure

Participants were recruited from ten middle schools in Beijing, China. Data were collected at three time points: February 2025 (T1), June 2025 (T2), and October 2025 (T3). The interval between each wave was four months. At T1, 1308 students completed the survey (52.52% boys; Mage=14.31, SDage=0.86), including 943 junior high school students (72%) and 365 senior high school students (28%). At T2, 1183 students completed the assessment (50.80% boys; Mage=13.75, SDage=0.72). At T3, 1014 students provided valid responses (49.21% boys; Mage=14.24, SDage=0.74). The main reasons for attrition were student absence and school transfer. Data were collected with paper-and-pencil questionnaires during regular school hours in classrooms. Parents gave written informed consent before participation. Adolescents also gave assent before taking part in the study.

We used Little’s Missing Completely at Random (MCAR) test to examine whether the missing data were systematic. The result was not significant, χ² = 137.62, df = 120, p = 0.12. the missing data pattern was consistent with randomness.

Measures

Harsh parenting

Harsh Parenting was measured with the Harsh Parenting Scale originally developed by Simons, Whitbeck [7] and later adapted into Chinese version [29]. The scale has 4 items. It measures children’s exposure to punitive and coercive parenting behaviors (e.g., When I do something wrong, my parent hits me with their hand or kicks me). Each item was rated on a 5-point Likert scale (1 = never to 5 = always). Higher total scores indicate higher level of harsh parenting. In this study, Cronbach’s α was 0.863 at T1, 0.850 at T2, and 0.842 at T3.

Non-suicidal self-injury

Non-suicidal self-injury was assessed using the 12-item behavior section of the Functional Assessment of Self-Mutilation (FASM) [30]. The validated Chinese version was used in this study [31]. Participants reported how often they had engaged in each self-injurious behavior in the past 3 months, such as cutting, burning, scratching, hitting themselves, and biting (e.g., Cut or carved on your skin). Each item was rated on a 5-point Likert scale from 0 (never) to 4 (more than 11 times). Higher total scores mean a higher frequency of non-suicidal self-injurious behaviors. In this study, Cronbach’s α was 0.825 at T1, 0.847 at T2, and 0.814 at T3.

Basic psychological need frustration

Basic psychological need frustration was measured using the 12-item frustration subscale of the Basic Psychological Need Satisfaction and Frustration Scale (BPNSFS) [32]. This subscale measures frustration of the needs for autonomy, competence, and relatedness (e.g., I feel forced to do many things that I actually do not want to do). Participants rated each item on a 5-point Likert scale ranging from 1 (strongly disagree) to 5 (strongly agree). Higher scores indicate greater basic psychological need frustration. In this study, Cronbach’s α was 0.795 at T1, 0.804 at T2, and 0.826 at T3.

Demographic and control variable

Participants reported their gender, age, parental educational level, family socioeconomic status (SES), only-child status, and family structure. Gender was coded as 0 = male and 1 = female. Only-child status was coded as 0 = non-only child and 1 = only child. Family structure was coded as 0 = two-parent family and 1 = single-parent family. Parental education was defined as the highest educational level of either parent. Family SES was calculated as a composite index based on parents’ educational level, occupational status, and family possessions [33]. These demographic variables were controlled in data analyses. Earlier studies have shown their associations with parenting practices, basic psychological need experiences, and adolescent adjustment outcomes [34–38].

Data analysis

Data analyses were conducted using SPSS 26.0 and Mplus 8.3. Preliminary analyses included descriptive statistics, Pearson correlations, and intraclass correlation coefficients (ICCs) to estimate the proportion of between-person versus within-person variance. Longitudinal measurement invariance was tested in Mplus by confirmatory factor analysis. Configural, metric, and scalar invariance models were tested step by step. Measurement invariance was supported when ΔCFI was no greater than 0.01 and ΔRMSEA was no greater than 0.015.

Longitudinal associations were examined with the CLPM and RI-CLPM by using the maximum likelihood estimator with robust standard errors (MLR). Although the NSSI total score showed some non-normality, the proportion of zero scores was limited (6.3%). Given the 12-item, 5-point format of the validated Chinese FASM, severe zero inflation at the summed-score level was unlikely; thus, MLR was considered appropriate. In the CLPM, gender, age, parental education, family socioeconomic status, only-child status, and family structure were included as covariates predicting the observed study variables at each wave. In the RI-CLPM, the same covariates were included as predictors of the random intercepts. The RI-CLPM further decomposed each construct into stable between-person (random intercept) and within-person components to estimate within-person cross-lagged effects while accounting for trait-like stability.

We also tested whether the structural paths were equal over time by using nested models based on Orth, Clark [39]. An unconstrained baseline model (M1) was compared with models constraining autoregressive paths (M2), cross-lagged paths (M3), and both autoregressive and cross-lagged paths simultaneously (M4). The model’s fit was assessed using several indices: χ², CFI, SRMR, RMSEA, and BIC. To compare nested models, we used χ² difference tests and looked at changes in CFI and RMSEA. The model that was the simplest, but still fit the data well compared to more complex models, was chosen [32, 39, 40].

Results of RI-CLPM analysis

Random-intercept cross-lagged panel models (RI-CLPM) were then estimated to account for stable between-person differences in harsh parenting, BPNF, and NSSI. All four RI-CLPM variants showed excellent fit (CFI = 0.999–1.000, SRMR = 0.008–0.012, RMSEA = 0.012–0.027; see Table 3). Minimal changes in ΔCFI (≤ 0.001) and ΔRMSEA (≤ 0.009) indicated stable autoregressive and cross-lagged associations. Compared with the CLPM, the RI-CLPM showed substantially better fit, particularly in RMSEA and SRMR, reflecting its ability to separate stable between-person differences from within-person dynamics. Considering fit and parsimony, M3 was selected as the final model.

The RI-CLPM results indicated that, at the between-person level, the random intercepts were positively correlated (see Fig. 2), suggesting stable positive associations among the trait-like components of the three constructs. At the within-person level, autoregressive effects were generally weak. Specifically, the autoregressive paths for harsh parenting and BPNF were not statistically significant, whereas NSSI demonstrated a significant stability effect across waves. Regarding cross-lagged effects, within-person increases in harsh parenting predicted subsequent increases in BPNF (β = 0.142, p < 0.001), and increases in BPNF predicted subsequent increases in NSSI (β = 0.147, p < 0.001). All other cross-lagged paths were not statistically significant. Importantly, the longitudinal indirect effect at the within-person level was significant (indirect effect = 0.021, p = 0.013, 95%CI [0.004, 0.038]), indicating that increases in harsh parenting were prospectively associated with increases in NSSI via BPNF.

Discussion

The present study investigated associations among harsh parenting, basic psychological needs frustration (BPNF), and non-suicidal self-injury (NSSI) over time using both CLPM and RI-CLPM. The CLPM supported reciprocal relationships and a mediation path where harsh parenting leads to later NSSI through BPNF. When we separate between-person stability from within-person change, the RI-CLPM found that within-person increases in harsh parenting predicted later increases in BPNF, and then that predicts more NSSI later. The mediation effect remained significant at the within-person level. These findings can help make prevention programs that focus on family relationships and psychological need processes.

Longitudinal associations between harsh parenting and non-suicidal self-injury

The CLPM revealed that harsh parenting and NSSI had reciprocal connection across three waves. This kind of pattern fits with stress diathesis perspectives that ongoing exposure to bad family environments can make emotional control worse and lead to poor coping ways [42, 43]. Repeated self-injurious behavior may strain family interactions and elicit harsher parenting responses. These associations are consistent with Hypothesis 1 regarding between person differences.

When accounting for stable individual differences using RI CLPM, a more detailed within person dynamic emerged: increases in harsh parenting predicted later increases in NSSI, whereas the reverse pathway was not significant, supporting Hypothesis 2. This directional finding at the within-person level gives stronger causal inference about the role of harsh parenting in precipitating adolescent NSSI. An emotion regulation framework thinks that harsh parenting acts as an interpersonal stressor that makes regulatory capacities too much, making NSSI a not good way to make negative affect less [44, 45]. Adding to this view, developmental psychopathology further says that harsh parenting during sensitive times changes the growing up of prefrontal-limbic circuitry, making long neurobiological scars that make susceptibility to NSSI higher across development [46].

The nonsignificant within-person effect from NSSI to harsh parenting suggests that the reciprocal links in the CLPM could mostly show stable between-person factors, such as genetic weaknesses, temperament, or long family problems [47], which make adolescents more likely to get harsh parenting and do NSSI at the same time. The time between waves might have limited finding of NSSI-to-parenting effects, which could show up over shorter or longer times.

The longitudinal mediating role of basic psychological needs frustration

The present findings give evidence for the longitudinal mediating role of BPNF in the association between harsh parenting and NSSI. In the CLPM, harsh parenting predicted later increases in BPNF, which then predicted later NSSI, showing a significant indirect effect. The RI-CLPM confirmed this pathway at the within-person level: within-person increases in harsh parenting were followed by increases in BPNF, which then predicted increases in NSSI. The significant within-person indirect effect supports Hypothesis 3 and suggests that BPNF works not merely as a stable correlate, but as a dynamic psychological mechanism linking fluctuations in parenting to changes in self-injurious behavior over time. This result is also consistent with recent longitudinal evidence. Zhao et al. found that parent–child conflict predicted later NSSI among Chinese vocational school students through need frustration and related internalizing processes [48]. Compared with this two-wave study, the present research provides a more fine-grained test of whether increases in harsh parenting relative to an adolescent’s usual level are followed by subsequent increases in BPNF and NSSI.

At the between-person level, adolescents who get higher levels of harsh parenting also report they have long time high BPNF and NSSI. It is consistent with self-determination theory [17], which posits that persistent need frustration may undermine adaptive functioning [49]. Prolonged exposure to harsh parenting may undermine autonomy, competence, and relatedness, fostering a stable vulnerability marked by more use of bad ways to deal with problems like NSSI [50].

Crucially, the within-person mediation found in the RI-CLPM makes clear the changing character of this process. When adolescents got parenting that was more harsh than normal, they subsequently reported increased BPNF, which then led to higher NSSI. This pattern suggests that needs frustration works as a close, time-changing way that family environment changes turn into behavior risk [51]. Harsh parenting may signal relational threat, and when it makes relatedness and competence needs not satisfied, adolescents may experience heightened social disconnection and reduced self-worth, both of which have been found to predict NSSI [52]. Also, this dynamic pattern can be understood through interpersonal and cognitive weakness perspectives. Experiences of need frustration may foster perceptions of rejection, incompetence, and less self-worth in adolescents. Such cognitive-affective schemas increase vulnerability to NSSI by making self-critical rumination and negative self-evaluation, both of which are proximal predictors of NSSI [53, 54].

Interpreting the nonsignificant within-person paths

Another noteworthy finding was that the within-person autoregressive paths for harsh parenting and BPNF were not statistically significant. This does not mean that the cross-lagged effects lack meaning. Rather, once stable between-person differences were accounted for, fluctuations in perceived harsh parenting and need frustration may have been relatively state-like and context-dependent across four-month intervals. In adolescence, family interactions and psychological need experiences may shift in response to situational stressors, parent–child conflicts, school demands, and developmental transitions. Thus, elevations in harsh parenting or need frustration at one wave may not persist to the next, even though they may still have meaningful downstream effects on later adjustment. This pattern may reflect the relatively transient and reactive nature of family experiences and psychological need states during this developmental period.

Furthermore, the nonsignificant NSSI-driven paths suggest that, in the present study, within-person increases in NSSI were not followed by subsequent increases in harsh parenting or need frustration. This pattern is noteworthy because the traditional CLPM suggested reciprocal associations, whereas the RI-CLPM did not. One possible interpretation is that the reciprocal association in the CLPM may have reflected stable between-person differences rather than genuine within-person reciprocity. At the same time, the RI-CLPM finding should be interpreted with caution rather than as evidence of a definitive null effect. Adolescents may conceal NSSI from parents, especially when self-injury is experienced as shameful or stigmatizing, which could reduce parents’ immediate awareness and weaken short-term child-driven effects [44, 52]. In addition, any parental or psychological response to NSSI may unfold over shorter or longer timescales than the four-month interval used in the present study. Thus, the current results provide stronger support for the pathway from harsh parenting to need frustration and later NSSI, while not ruling out the possibility of reciprocal effects under different temporal conditions. This also suggests that the four-month interval, although useful for examining medium-term associations, may not have been the optimal timescale for capturing the stability of these more state-like experiences.

Theoretical and practical implications

Theoretically, the present study extends prior longitudinal research on harsh parenting and adolescent NSSI in three ways. First, it distinguishes stable between-person differences from within-person processes, thereby providing a more precise account of how harsh parenting is linked to adolescent NSSI over time. Second, it suggests that BPNF may function as a dynamic within-person mechanism rather than merely a general correlate. More broadly, these findings extend self-determination theory by showing that fluctuations in need frustration may help explain how changes in the family environment translate into maladaptive behavioral risk over time. Third, by comparing the CLPM and the RI-CLPM, the study highlights the importance of model choice in interpreting longitudinal associations.

Practically, interventions targeting adolescent NSSI may benefit from a dual focus on reducing harsh parenting practices and restoring basic psychological need satisfaction. Family-based prevention programs that help autonomy-supportive, competence-affirming, and relationship-enhancing parenting may stop the chain from need frustration to self-injury. In addition, clinical interventions for adolescents could put in strategies aimed at strengthening perceived autonomy, competence, and relatedness, so making less the effect of short-time increases in family stress.

Limitation and future direction

First, although the three-wave design strengthens causal inference, the four-month interval may not have been optimal for capturing all within-person dynamics. Shorter intervals may better detect state-like continuity, whereas reciprocal family effects may emerge over different timescales. Future research should use more fine-grained longitudinal designs to clarify these processes. Second, using only adolescent self-report can bring possible report bias. Adolescents’ perceptions of parenting may be influenced by mood, and NSSI may be underreported due to shame. Future work should add reports from parents or doctor interviews. Third, the sample was recruited from Beijing, China, so the results may not apply to other places or cultures. We need do same studies in different cultures to see if the within-person changes found here are same elsewhere. In addition, the sample decreased from 1,308 at T1 to 1,014 at T3 (attrition rate = 22.5%). Although Little’s MCAR test was not significant, attrition may still have been related to school transitions or other factors relevant to the study variables. This may have affected parameter estimates and generalizability. Fourth, other potential mediators or moderators, such as emotion regulation strategies, peer relationships, or genetic vulnerabilities, were not examined. Future research should try more complete models that integrate cognitive, emotional, social, and biological factors to identify additional intervention targets. Fifth, although sex was included as a control variable in the present study, we did not formally test whether the longitudinal pathways differed between boys and girls. Future research should examine whether the between-person and within-person associations among harsh parenting, BPNF, and NSSI operate differently across sex.

Conclusion

The present study employed CLPM and RI-CLPM to examine longitudinal associations among harsh parenting, basic psychological needs frustration (BPNF), and non-suicidal self-injury (NSSI) in Chinese adolescents. The CLPM revealed reciprocal associations among harsh parenting, BPNF, and NSSI, whereas the RI-CLPM identified more refined within-person dynamics: increases in harsh parenting predicted subsequent increases in BPNF, which in turn predicted later increases in NSSI. BPNF mediated the association between harsh parenting and NSSI at both the between-person and within-person levels. These findings suggest that reducing adolescent NSSI may require attention to both family functioning and adolescents’ basic psychological needs.
