The association between weight misperception and weight-related behaviors among U.S. adults with overweight and obesity
Authors: Richard R Rosenkranz, Amin Mokari-Yamchi
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundWeight misperception arises when there is a difference between an individual’s actual weight and their perceived weight status. This study aimed to determine associations between weight misperception and weight-related behaviors in individuals with overweight and obesity.MethodsThe study utilized 2017–2020 data from the National Health and Nutrition Examination Survey (NHANES). Adult participants who answered the “How do you consider your weight?” question were included in the analysis. Adjusted odds ratios (aORs) with 95% confidence intervals (CIs) were calculated using weighted logistic regression models to estimate associations between weight-related behaviors and weight misperception.ResultsThe study included 4,608 participants, of whom 1,155 (25.1%) misperceived their weight status. Weight misperception was highest among males (65.7%), young adults (33.9%), and non-Hispanic white individuals (56.3%). Multivariable analysis revealed that females were less likely than males to misperceive their weight (aOR: 0.32 [95% CI: 0.27–0.38]). Participants with higher incomes (aOR: 0.73 [95% CI: 0.56–0.95]) and obesity (aOR: 0.11 [95% CI: 0.09–0.13]) had lower odds of misperception. Conversely, Hispanic (aOR: 1.47 [95% CI: 1.16–1.86]), non-Hispanic Black individuals (aOR: 2.4 [95% CI: 1.86–3.11]), and single participants (aOR: 1.37 [95% CI: 1.14–1.63]) had higher odds. Weight misperceivers were significantly less likely to have attempted weight loss (aOR: 0.28 [95% CI: 0.23–0.33]) and engaged in weight-related behaviors, as compared to those with accurate weight perception.ConclusionsLower engagement in weight-related behaviors was associated with weight misperception, highlighting the importance of awareness of actual weight for effective obesity interventions.Keywords: Weight misperception, Obesity, Weight-related behaviors, American adults, NHANES

Introduction

Obesity, along with its related physical and mental health issues, continues to be a significant public health challenge. Currently, more than two-thirds of adults in the U.S. are classified as overweight or obese, and these rates are still on the rise [1]. Overweight and obesity are often defined by body mass index (BMI) of 25 or higher indicating overweight and 30 or higher indicating obesity. A recent report from the Centers for Disease Control and Prevention (CDC) indicates that the obesity prevalence increased from 30.5% in 1999 to 41.9% in 2020 [2]. Additionally, the prevalence of severe obesity (BMI ≥ 40 kg/m²) in the United States (U.S.) rose from 4.7% to 9.2% during the same period [3]. Time trend analyses suggest that by 2030, around 51% of adults in the U.S. are expected to be obese, resulting in an additional $549.5 billion in medical expenses compared to keeping obesity rates at 2010 levels [4].

Lifestyle modifications, including dietary changes and increased physical activity, are effective in promoting successful weight management behaviors [5, 6]. Recognizing the true weight status among individuals with overweight and obesity has been suggested to be crucial for motivating participation in lifestyle modification activities. There are subset types of weight misperceivers that can subsequently influence their weight related behaviors [7, 8]. Weight misperception, defined to be the over- or underestimation of one’s weight, can itself be a barrier to health related improvements [9]. Various theoretical frameworks, including the Transtheoretical Model (TTM) [10] and the Health Belief Model (HBM) [11], are commonly used to explain and predict health behaviors. Each model has distinct constructs related to individuals’ likelihood of undertaking behavior change. The initial stage of both TTM and HBM focuses on contemplation and the perception of the issue [12]. These models emphasize how awareness of a problem and perceptions of potential threats can significantly influence an individual’s willingness to adopt healthy behaviors [12, 13].

Several studies have investigated the relationship between weight status perception and health behaviors across different groups [8, 14], however, conflicting evidence has been reported. Some findings indicate that individuals who accurately perceive their weight status are more likely to engage in weight loss behaviors [8]. Mbarushimana et al. reported that U.S. adults who correctly identified their weight were 1.7 times (CI: 1.20–2.55) more likely to report dieting, compared to those who underestimated their weight [15]. Similarly, Duncan et al.’s study based on 2003–2006 National Health and Nutrition Examination Survey (NHANES) data revealed that overweight American adults who were weight misperceivers were less likely to pursue weight-loss strategies [16]. Conversely, weight misperceivers were significantly less likely to engage in disordered weight control behaviors like meal skipping and vomiting or taking diet pills [17]. A study by Althumiria et al. in 2021 on Saudi Arabian adults, however, found no significant association between weight misperception and physical activity or nutritional knowledge [18].

Understanding how weight-related behaviors differ between overweight and obese individuals with and without weight misperception is a vital step in clarifying these mechanisms within behavior change models, particularly given the conflicting evidence. Additionally, identifying factors that influence the likelihood of weight misperception can inform future efforts to develop tailored approaches to help individuals achieve a healthy weight. Therefore, this study aimed to determine associations between weight misperception and weight-related health behaviors, including dietary, physical activity, label use and health monitoring behaviors, within a nationally representative sample of American adults with overweight and obesity.

Materials and methods

This is a cross-sectional study, using data from the National Health and Nutrition Examination Survey (NHANES) collected between 2017 and 2020, prior to the COVID-19 pandemic (official NHANES website: https://www.cdc.gov/nchs/nhanes/index.htm), a robust survey conducted by Centers for Disease Control and Prevention (CDC). NHANES employs a multistage probability sampling method to ensure that the non-institutionalized U.S. civilian population is adequately represented. Sample weights were applied in each analysis following the NHANES guidelines to account for the complex survey design. The analysis included non-pregnant adults aged 20 years and older who had answered the main study question regarding weight perception. All participants were carefully weighed on a calibrated scale, measured for height on a stadiometer, and surveyed with the question: “How do you consider your weight?” Individuals with overweight and obesity who responded “Underweight” or “About the right weight” were considered as weight misperceivers. Participants with missing data (including “missing” or “refused” or “don’t know” responses) were excluded. Due to limited data availability regarding marital and pregnancy status for individuals under the age of 20, as well as different educational categories for those under 20 years old, they were excluded from our final sample. Participants with missing data in weight, height, or answers to weight-related behaviors (dietary, physical activity and health monitoring behaviors) were also excluded. Finally, the total sample size was consisted of 4,608 participants. The original NHANES study data were collected with ethical approval from the Institutional review board of National Center for Health Statistics (NCHS) with informed consent from all participants.

Sociodemographic variables

Various demographic characteristics, including age, sex, race/ethnicity, and socioeconomic indicators such as the family-income-to-poverty-guidelines ratio (FIPR), education level, marital status and health insurance coverage were considered. Marital status was categorized into two groups: married/living with a partner and widowed/divorced/separated. Race/ethnicity of participants was categorized as follows: Hispanic, Non-Hispanic White, Black and Asian. FIPR was divided into three groups: lower income (0 to 1.3) (equivalent to eligibility for participation in the Supplemental Nutrition Assistance Program [19]), middle income (1.3 to 3.5), and higher income (≥ 3.5). Participants’ educational levels were classified into four groups: Less than high school, high school, some college, college graduate and above. Body Mass Index (BMI) was calculated by dividing participants’ weight in kilograms by the square of their height in meters, with BMI values classified as overweight (25.0–29.9) and obesity (≥ 30).

Weight-related behaviors

The explanatory variables of interest for this study were various weight-related behaviors, specifically related to dietary, physical activity, label use and health monitoring behaviors. NHANES collected detailed information on these behaviors by asking participants a series of questions. For weight-related dietary behaviors participants were asked: During the past 12 months, have you tried to lose weight? They were also queried about specific weight loss strategies, including: “Ate less fat to lose weight”, " Ate more fruits, vegetables, salads”, " Changed eating habits”, " Ate less sugar, candy, sweets”, " Ate less junk food or fast food” and " Followed unhealthy weight loss strategies (e.g., skipped meals, started to smoke, took laxatives or vomited, or took prescription or non-prescription diet pills)”.

Physical activity behaviors were assessed using the survey question " Exercised to lose weight in past year?” and in a typical week, how much time do you spend doing: “moderate and vigorous work activity?“, " Walk/bike for travel?” and “moderate and vigorous recreational activities?”

The label use behaviors were evaluated by asking each participant the following questions: Do you use calorie information at fast food/pizza, resturant and grocery store?; Do you use health claims on food packages?:, Do you use nutrition facts panel, ingredients list, serving size info and calorie information on food label?

For health monitoring behaviors, the following questions were used: “Have you ever had your blood cholesterol checked?” and “Have you had a blood test for high blood sugar or diabetes within the past three years?“.

Statistical analysis

All analyses were conducted using SPSS version 22 and took into account the weighted methods, specifically full sample interview weights, in accordance with NHANES analysis guidelines [20]. The distributions of participants identified as weight misperceivers and accurate perceivers were described across various demographic variables, including sex, age, ethnicity, household income, health insurance coverage, education, marital status, and weight status. Weighted logistic regression was conducted to model the association between weight misperception with demographic factors. Additionally, weighted logistic regression was used to compare weight-related behaviors between weight misperceivers and accurate perceivers, adjusting for age, sex, ethnicity, education, income status, health insurance coverage, marital status, and weight status. Results were reported as crude odds ratios (OR) and adjusted odds ratios (AOR), accompanied by 95% confidence intervals (CIs), with a p-value of < 0.05 indicating statistical significance.

Results

The characteristics of participants

The study included 4,608 participants, with 2,243 (48.7%) identifying as male and 2,365 (51.3%) as female. Out of the total sample, 1,155 participants misperceived their weight status, while 3,453 accurately perceived their weight. The majority of weight misperceivers were male (65.7%), young adults (33.9%), non-Hispanic white (56.3%), with health insurance (84.3%), overweight (75.6%), married (62.2%), and with a high income status (43.7%) (Table 1).

Association between the weight misperception and demographic characteristics

As shown in Table 2, weighted logistic regression analysis revealed that females were less likely to misperceive their weights than males (adjusted Odds Ratio (aOR: 0.32) [95% CI: 0.27–0.38. Compared to individuals with less than a high school education, those with a high school education (aOR: 0.66 [95% CI: 0.48–0.91]), some college education (aOR: 0.58 [95% CI: 0.42–0.8]), and college graduates (aOR: 0.34 [95% CI: 0.24–0.47]) were less likely to misperceive their weight. Association between weight-related behaviors and weight misperception

The associations between weight-related behaviors and weight misperception are presented in Table 3. Weight misperceivers were less likely to report using calorie information at restaurants (aOR: 0.55 [95% CI: 0.4–0.77]), and on food labels (aOR: 0.72 [95% CI: 0.59–0.87]) compared to individuals with correct weight perceptions. Moreover, individuals who misperceived their weight status were less likely to have attempted to lose weight in the past year (aOR: 0.28 [95% CI: 0.23–0.33]) compared to individuals with correct weight perceptions. Furthermore, weight misperception was associated with dieting strategies including: eating less fat (aOR: 0.39 [95% CI: 0.31–0.5]), less sugar and sweets (aOR: 0.4 [95% CI: 0.33–0.5]), less junk food or fast foods (aOR: 0.4 [95% CI: 0.33–0.49]) as well as more fruits and vegetables (aOR: 0.39 [95% CI: 0.32–0.47]). Additionally, unhealthy weight loss strategies such as taking laxatives or vomiting were less likely to be followed by weight misperceivers (aOR: 0.42 [95% CI: 0.32–0.55]) compared to individuals with accurate weight perceptions. Likelihood of engaging in exercise to lose weight during past year was significantly lower among weight misperceivers compared to those with accurate weight perceptions (aOR: 0.43 [95% CI: 0.35–0.52]). However, weight misperceivers had significantly higher odds of engaging in vigorous work (aOR: 1.26 [95% CI: 1.04–1.52]) and recreational activities (aOR: 1.36 [95% CI: 1.12–1.65]) compared to those with accurate weight perceptions. However, compared to individuals with accurate weight perceptions, those who misperceived their weight were significantly less likely to have had a blood test for high blood sugar or diabetes in the past three years (aOR = 0.80, 95% CI: 0.67–0.96). They were also less likely to have had their blood cholesterol checked (OR = 0.68, 95% CI: 0.56–0.83), although this difference was not statistically significant after adjustment (aOR = 0.84, 95% CI: 0.64–1.11).

Discussion

The intent of the present study was to examine the relationship between weight-related behaviors and weight status misperception in individuals with overweight and obesity. The results of the study clearly highlight significant demographic differences in weight perception, with a notable prevalence of misperception among males. This aligns with prior findings by Weng et al. indicating that gender is an influential factor in body image and weight perception [21]. In particular, younger women’s misperceptions, particularly among those with eating disorders, have been extensively studied [22, 23]. Sociocultural and psychological factors likely contribute to these differences. Women often experience greater societal pressure to conform to thinness ideals, which may lead to heightened body dissatisfaction and distorted perceptions of weight, even among those with normal or low BMI [24]. In contrast, men may be influenced by cultural norms that associate masculinity with muscularity and size, contributing to underestimation of weight status, especially among those who are overweight or obese. These differences have important implications for health behavior: Women who overestimate their weight may be more likely to engage in unhealthy weight control practices such as extreme dieting or disordered eating, while men who underestimate their weight may be less likely to engage in weight loss efforts or seek medical advice [25–27]. However, further research is needed to explore male misperceptions regarding obesity. Perhaps images of strength, health, or vitality influence these misperceptions in men [28]. Furthermore, our finding that Hispanic and non-Hispanic black individuals had higher likelihood of weight misperception compared to non-Hispanic whites is consistent with other published reports [29, 30], suggesting that cultural factors may influence body image perceptions and the internalization of societal weight norms [31]. Additionally, higher income and education were associated with lower odds of weight misperception. This indicates that individuals with greater levels of education and financial resources likely to have better health literacy and improved access to facilities, which in turn enhances their awareness and understanding of healthy weight standards [32]. Interestingly, participants with health insurance coverage exhibited markedly higher odds of misperception. Individuals lacking insurance may experience barriers to accessing healthcare resources and education. In a related study, Gopalan et al. discovered that participants with insurance coverage were significantly more aware of their prediabetes status than those without such awareness [33]. Furthermore, in the present study, being single (including widowed, divorced, or separated) was linked to weight misperception. These findings align with a recent study conducted in Iraq, which concluded that body weight misperception was significantly more prevalent among unmarried women compared to married women [34].

Our findings suggest that weight misperceivers were less likely to engage in all weight loss practices, such as eating less fat, sugar and junk foods. This aligns with the notion that weight misperception was associated with lower engagement in weight loss behaviors in adolescents, regardless of gender [16]. Similarly, research conducted by Nazar et al. on Chileans aged 15 and older yielded comparable results [8]. Additionally, Rahman et al. reported that both healthy and unhealthy weight-reduction behaviors were more common among weight misperceivers [29]. Furthermore, our study found that individuals with weight misperception were less likely to utilize calorie information on menus and food labels. To our knowledge, no study has directly evaluated the relationship between weight perception and the use of food labels. However, findings by Nianogo et al. suggest that participants whose desired weight was lower than their current weight had higher odds of using calorie information on food packages, highlighting the potential influence of weight perception on dietary decision-making [35]. This suggests that a misperception of weight may diminish the motivation to make informed dietary choices, which is crucial for effective weight management.

According to TTM, individuals progress through stages of behavioral change, from precontemplation to action [36]. Misperception of weight status among individuals with obesity may hinder this progression, particularly in adopting weight-related behaviors. For example, weight misperceivers may remain in the precontemplation stage, lacking awareness of the need for change, which could explain their lower engagement in weight-loss practices and reduced use of food labels. Similarly, based on HBM model, weight misperception may reduce perceived susceptibility to weight-related health risks [37], thereby diminishing the motivation to adopt healthier behaviors, such as dietary changes or physical activity.

The lower odds of engaging in exercise with the purpose of weight loss among misperceivers is consistent with our findings on other weight-loss practices and aligns with similar studies [8, 27, 38]. In contrast, the finding that weight misperception was associated with increased odds of engaging in vigorous work and recreational activities raises important questions about the motivations underlying these behaviors. Similar to our findings Hahn et al. also found that weight misperception among american adolescents was associated with increased odds of being physically active and participating in sports teams [14]. It is possible that weight misperceivers participate in physical activities for reasons unrelated to weight management, such as personal enjoyment, occupational requirements, or other non-weight-related goals. Alternatively, this relationship could be interpreted in reverse: engaging in routine vigorous activities may contribute to weight misperception, as individuals might underestimate their weight status or deny any limitations associated with it due to their active lifestyles. To our knowledge, no other group has examined the relationship between health monitoring behaviors and weight misperception. Our findings suggest that weight misperceivers may be less likely to monitor their lipid profiles and blood sugar levels, possibly due to a reduced sense of urgency stemming from the mistaken belief that their weight-related health is adequate. However, it should be considered that increasing awareness of weight status may encourage healthier behaviors, it also risks heightening stigma, which can have negative psychological and behavioral effects [39]. Weight stigma has been linked to increased stress, lower self-esteem, and even weight gain due to maladaptive coping strategies such as emotional eating and exercise avoidance [40, 41]. Striking a balance between raising awareness of weight status and avoiding stigma is crucial. Efforts should focus on promoting holistic health behaviors—such as balanced nutrition, physical activity, and regular health monitoring—without reinforcing harmful stereotypes or discouraging individuals from seeking care. Future research should explore strategies for effectively communicating weight-related health risks in ways that support both physical and mental well-being.

This study has several strengths. It utilized a large sample drawn from a nationally representative dataset, enhancing the generalizability of the findings. Additionally, objective weight status was determined using rigorously measured height and weight, rather than relying on self-reported data, which strengthens the accuracy of the weight classification. However, the study has some limitations. First, the use of cross-sectional data limits the ability to draw causal inferences from the findings. Second, outcomes were measured using a single question, which may not fully capture the participants’ characteristics or behaviors. Additionally, most variables, including weight perception and weight-related behaviors, were self-reported, which introduces the possibility of response bias and social desirability effects. The small sample size of participants who perceived themselves as underweight led to the exclusion of this group from analysis. Future studies focusing on this population could yield interesting insights. Additionally, while BMI is commonly used as a screening tool for obesity, it has limitations. BMI does not differentiate between muscle and fat mass and does not account for fat distribution, which can be a more critical determinant of metabolic health. Therefore, while BMI can be a useful first step, it should be followed up with more accurate assessments of adiposity. Based on our findings regarding disparities in demographic variables associated with weight misperception, future investigations may benefit from focusing on specific population groups.

In conclusion, high prevalence of weight misperception, particularly among specific demographic groups, emphasizes the need for targeted interventions aimed at promoting accurate weight perceptions. Our study highlights that individuals with weight misperception are less likely to adopt weight loss strategies, use food labels and engage in health monitoring behaviors, suggesting that accurate weight perceptions serve as a critical point for initiating effective weight-related behaviors in individuals with overweight and obesity.
