The Machine Learning Approach to Moment Closure Relations for Plasma: A Review
Samuel Burles \aff 1 \corresp, Enrico Camporeale \aff 1, \aff 2

Abstract
The requirement for large-scale global simulations of plasma is an ongoing challenge in both space and laboratory plasma physics. Any simulation based on a fluid model inherently requires a closure relation for the high order plasma moments. This review compiles and analyses the recent surge of machine learning approaches developing improved plasma closure models capable of capturing kinetic phenomena within plasma fluid models. The purpose of this review is both to collect and analyse the various methods employed on the plasma closure problem, including both equation discovery methods and neural network surrogate approaches, as well as to provide a general overview of the state of the problem. In particular, we highlight the challenges of developing a data-driven closure as well as the direction future work should take toward addressing these challenges, in the pursuit of a computationally viable large-scale global simulation.



1
Introduction

Space and laboratory plasmas exhibit complex dynamics across many spatio-temporal scales. For many phenomena observed to occur in plasmas, it is often the case that small-scale wave-particle interactions play a crucial role in the large-scale dynamics. Therefore, we must have accurate, multi-scale models to describe and predict these phenomena
(
21
;
10
;
23
)
. Historically, the numerical modelling of plasma has been broadly divided between kinetic and fluid approaches.

Kinetic models of collisionless plasmas, typically found in space environments, are described by the time evolution of a distribution function, governed by the collisionless Vlasov equation. The so-called one-particle distribution function describes the probability distribution of particles in velocity-position phase space. Fully kinetic simulations can capture self-consistently the interactions between particles and electromagnetic fields. However, they are computationally prohibitive at large scales because of the dimensionality of the phase space, and the stringent requirements on time and grid resolution, for numerical stability.

The fluid description of collisionless plasma assumes the particles to have an approximately Maxwellian distribution, allowing the plasma to be described by a series of observable macroscopic quantities. Fluid descriptions of plasmas are low-dimensional and therefore better suited to model large-scale systems while maintaining a low computational cost. Fluid simulations, however, are ill-suited to resolve small-scale kinetic effects due to the simplifying approximations required to construct the models.

The fluid models of plasma can be rigorously derived by taking velocity moments of the Vlasov equation, resulting in an infinite hierarchy of equations, where the evolution of each moment is dependent on the next higher-order moment, leaving the set of equations open. Hence, to obtain a self-consistent fluid model, one must truncate the system of equations by specifying a closure. A closure equation specifies a chosen highest-order moment in terms of only lower-order moments. The small-scale kinetic effects will typically be lost when the physical assumptions necessary to obtain an analytic closure model are made. It is therefore a key problem to find new closure models that can include important small-scale kinetic effects in fluid models without incurring a steep computational cost in simulations.

One aspect of plasma modelling that has always posed a computational challenge is the different scales associated with various species in the plasma. Electrons typically behave on much shorter time scales than ions because of the significant difference in the charge-mass ratio. To model both ion and electron species, a hybrid model can be employed where one species is treated kinetically and one as a fluid, with the ion species often chosen to be kinetic and the electron species as a fluid. Another option is to lower the simulated difference in charge-mass ratio between species to speed up the computation
(
50
;
41
)
.

Yet another approach to model large-scale systems and accounting, to some extent, for kinetic effects is to embed a small kinetic domain into a larger fluid simulation. For example, in the context of a planetary magnetosphere, the magnetotail is typically treated kinetically because it is the region where magnetic reconnection is expected to occur
(
48
;
42
;
23
)
.

Recent advances in parallel computing and GPU capabilities have driven rapid progress in machine learning, resulting in new techniques applied in multiple scientific fields. Machine learning approaches are becoming increasingly popular in neutral fluid dynamics, with much focus on improving turbulent simulations and the long-lasting closure problem
(
4
;
38
;
25
;
43
)
. We believe that a data-driven approach to finding improved closure models for space and astrophysical plasmas is now a path worthy of investigation.

This review paper aims to collect and analyse various works in scientific machine learning and plasma simulation that focus on developing improved closure models that include small-scale kinetic physics. The review will begin with a brief overview of the background physics involved in defining the plasma moment closure. In Section 2, an overview of the relevant machine learning techniques and methodology will be given to provide context. Section 3 presents a review of the research that has been conducted in applying a data-driven approach to solving the plasma closure problem. Finally, Section 4 concludes and presents future ideas.

1.1
Background

In the kinetic description of plasma, one can, in principle, describe the system of particles with a function containing all particle positions and velocities at a given time,
N_{\alpha}(\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{v},t)
. This detailed description of a plasma, while containing all necessary information, is, however, difficult to work with, and so we seek a reduced description of the plasma. This is achieved by performing a statistical average over particles. We restrict our model to the one-particle distribution function, neglecting particle interactions. The one-particle distribution function
f_{\alpha}(\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{v},t)
describes the probability density that a particle of species
\alpha
will have a position
\boldsymbol{x}
and velocity
\boldsymbol{v}
within an infinitesimal region of phase-space
d\boldsymbol{x}d\boldsymbol{v}
, at time
t
.

To describe the plasma dynamics, we then require an evolution equation of the distribution function, self-consistently coupled with the Maxwell equations. This evolution equation is the Vlasov equation and can be derived through the so-called BBGKY procedure, starting from the Liouville equation for the one-particle distribution function, or from taking statistical averages of the Klimontovich equation for the density function
(
19
;
40
)
. We further restrict ourselves to the collisionless Vlasov equation
(
44
)
:

\frac{\partial f_{\alpha}}{\partial t}+\boldsymbol{v}\bcdot\bnabla f_{\alpha}+\frac{q_{\alpha}}{m_{\alpha}}\left(\boldsymbol{E}+\boldsymbol{v}\times\boldsymbol{B}\right)\bcdot\frac{\partial f_{\alpha}}{\partial\boldsymbol{v}}=0

(1)

coupled to the Maxwell equations:

\displaystyle\bnabla\bcdot\boldsymbol{E}=\frac{1}{\epsilon_{0}}\sum_{\alpha}q_{\alpha}\int d\boldsymbol{v}f_{\alpha}(\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{v},t)

(2)

\displaystyle\bnabla\bcdot\boldsymbol{B}=0

(3)

\displaystyle\bnabla\times\boldsymbol{E}+\frac{\partial\boldsymbol{B}}{\partial t}=0

(4)

\displaystyle\bnabla\times\boldsymbol{B}-\mu_{0}\epsilon_{0}\frac{\partial\boldsymbol{E}}{\partial t}=\mu_{0}\sum_{\alpha}q_{\alpha}\int d\boldsymbol{v}\;\boldsymbol{v}f_{\alpha}(\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{v},t)

(5)

where
\boldsymbol{E}
and
\boldsymbol{B}
are the electric and magnetic fields, respectively,
q_{\alpha}
and
m_{\alpha}
are the charge and mass of a particle of species
\alpha
,
\epsilon_{0}
is the electric permittivity of the vacuum and
\mu_{0}
is the magnetic permeability of the vacuum.

The kinetic description of a collisionless plasma, therefore, requires a 6-dimensional phase space. This high dimensionality often makes the computational cost of large-scale 3D simulations using the kinetic description prohibitive, even on the most advanced HPC architecture.

Techniques such as the particle-in-cell method for computing fully kinetic simulations can mitigate some of the computational cost by utilising ’mean-particles’ to discretise the distribution function. However, these methods still suffer from the high-dimensional phase space, and are not suited for long-time, global simulations
(
32
;
8
)
.

To reduce the computational cost required to simulate a plasma, it makes sense to seek reduced models. As we mentioned, fluid equations can be constructed by taking velocity moments of the particle distribution function.

Following the procedure set out in
40
, we can write the collisionless Vlasov equation as

\frac{\partial f}{\partial t}+\bnabla\bcdot(\boldsymbol{v}f)+\bnabla_{\boldsymbol{v}}\bcdot(\boldsymbol{A}f)=0

(6)

Where
\boldsymbol{A}
is the acceleration due to the electric and magnetic fields, and the subscripts
\alpha
have been dropped for conciseness. We can proceed to introduce a scalar function of velocity,
Q_{m}(\boldsymbol{v})=\boldsymbol{v}^{m}
. Then, defining the moment taking process as averaging over the velocity:

\left<Q_{m}(\boldsymbol{v})\right>\;=\;\int Q_{m}fd\boldsymbol{v}

(7)

We can then define the zeroth order moment
n(\boldsymbol{x})=\int f(\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{v})d\boldsymbol{v}
as the density in configuration space, corresponding to
\left<Q_{0}\right>
. We can then go on to multiply the Vlasov equation by
Q_{m}(\boldsymbol{v})
and integrate in velocity:

\int Q_{m}\frac{\partial f}{\partial t}d\boldsymbol{v}+\int Q_{m}\bnabla\bcdot\boldsymbol{v}fd\boldsymbol{v}+\int Q_{m}\bnabla_{\boldsymbol{v}}\bcdot\boldsymbol{A}fd\boldsymbol{v}=0

(8)

As
Q_{m}(\boldsymbol{v})
is a function only of the velocity, we can rearrange as:

\frac{\partial}{\partial t}\int Q_{m}fd\boldsymbol{v}+\bnabla\bcdot\int Q_{m}\boldsymbol{v}fd\boldsymbol{v}+\int Q_{m}\bnabla_{\boldsymbol{v}}\bcdot\boldsymbol{A}fd\boldsymbol{v}=0

(9)

It is then clear that the first term is simply
\frac{\partial}{\partial t}\left<Q_{m}\right>
from the definition in Eq. (
7
). The second term can be written similarly as
\bnabla\bcdot\left<Q_{m}\boldsymbol{v}\right>
as
Q_{m}
is a scalar function of only the velocity. The third term can be simplified by using the vector identity
\bnabla\bcdot(a\boldsymbol{A})=\boldsymbol{A}\bcdot\bnabla a+a\bnabla\bcdot\boldsymbol{A}
and the divergence theorem. Then, assuming that the distribution vanishes for
v\rightarrow\infty
, causing the surface integral to vanish, the third term becomes
\left<\boldsymbol{A}\bcdot\bnabla_{\boldsymbol{v}}Q_{m}\right>
. Writing all terms together gives:

\frac{\partial}{\partial t}\left<Q_{m}\right>+\bnabla\bcdot\left<Q_{m}\boldsymbol{v}\right>-\left<\boldsymbol{A}\bcdot\bnabla_{\boldsymbol{v}}Q_{m}\right>=0

(10)

This is a general formula for the evolution of the velocity moments. By setting the scalar function
Q_{m}(\boldsymbol{v})
to different powers of the velocity, we can obtain the evolution equation for any-order moment. The closure problem arises because the second term of this general equation will always contain the next-higher order moment in
\left<Q_{m}\boldsymbol{v}\right>
. The hierarchy of equations can only be closed by defining the last-order moment as a function of only lower-order moments: that constitutes our closure. As easy as it might sound, different choices of closure dramatically affect the accuracy of a model and how well (or not) kinetic effects are captured.

Finally, it is customary to define macroscopic fluid quantities in terms of the first four velocity moments
(
45
;
7
)

\displaystyle n

\displaystyle=\int fd\boldsymbol{v}

(11)

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{u}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{n}\int\boldsymbol{v}fd\boldsymbol{v}

(12)

\displaystyle\mathsfbi{P}

\displaystyle=m\int(\boldsymbol{v}-\boldsymbol{u})^{2}fd\boldsymbol{v}

(13)

\displaystyle\mathsfbi{Q}

\displaystyle=m\int(\boldsymbol{v}-\boldsymbol{u})^{3}fd\boldsymbol{v}

(14)

The zeroth-order moment is the bulk density, the first-order moment is the bulk velocity, the second-order moment is the electron-pressure tensor, and the third-order moment is the heat flux tensor.
\mathsfbi{P}
and
\mathsfbi{Q}
have been defined as the centred-difference moments of the distribution function to define the observable fluid properties.

The plasma fluid model for the first three moments becomes:

\displaystyle\frac{\partial n}{\partial t}+\bnabla\bcdot(n\boldsymbol{u})=0

(15)

\displaystyle m\frac{\partial}{\partial t}(n\boldsymbol{u})+\bnabla\bcdot\mathsfbi{P}-nq\left(\boldsymbol{E}+\frac{\boldsymbol{u}\times\boldsymbol{B}}{c}\right)=0

(16)

\displaystyle\frac{\partial\mathsfbi{P}}{\partial t}+\bnabla\bcdot\mathsfbi{Q}-q\left(n\boldsymbol{u}\boldsymbol{E}^{\intercal}+\frac{\mathsfbi{P}\times\boldsymbol{B}}{c}\right)=0

(17)

Once again, it is evident how the evolution of each moment depends on the next higher-order moment, which keeps the set of equations open. To form a closed set of equations that we can use, one must therefore truncate the system with a closure relation. This closure must relate the highest-order moment we choose to include in the model to only the lower-order moments, a task that typically requires some simplifying physical approximations to be made. This approximation can cause the model to become unable to include small-scale kinetic effects.

As an example, truncating the system of moment equations at the third order requires an expression that relates the heat flux to the lower-order moments.

\mathsfbi{Q}=\mathcal{N}(n,\boldsymbol{u},\mathsfbi{P},\boldsymbol{E},\boldsymbol{B})

(18)

Where
\mathcal{N}
is the moment closure model. An example of a closure of this form is the Hammett-Perkins closure, which expresses the heat flux as a function of the temperature in Fourier space to capture Landau damping.
(
10
)

\tilde{q}_{k}=-n_{0}\sqrt{\frac{8}{\upi}}v_{t}\frac{\mathrm{i}k\tilde{T}_{k}}{|k|}

(19)

The Hammett-Perkins closure is an example of a so-called Landau fluid closure. Other analytic closure models include the 5 and 10-moment models.
(
20
;
39
)
Truncating the system of equations at the second order gives the 10-moment model, which describes the evolution of the density, the momentum and the six components of the symmetric pressure tensor. The 5-moment model arises from the assumption that the pressure is a scalar and so is only described by a single moment equation (an approximation that assumes an isotropic temperature). Many more examples of closure models exist, with each typically able to model different plasma regimes and different phenomena.
(

2
Machine Learning

The purpose of applying ML techniques to the plasma closure problem is twofold: Including higher-order effects into large-scale simulations and improving the computational cost and speed of simulations. Similar work has been ongoing in computational fluid mechanics for some time, where the aim is primarily to emulate fluid models using ML to obtain fast-running models that can be employed in near-real time.
(
43
)

Broadly, ML problems can be divided into three categories:

•

Supervised learning: The ML model is trained on labelled data where each (multidimensional) input data point is paired with an output data point (‘label’ or ‘ground truth’). Supervised learning tasks typically consist of regression (continuous labels) and classification (discrete labels).

•

Unsupervised learning: The ML model is trained on unlabelled data, where the goal is to extract a pattern or underlying behaviour from the data without any information about a specific solution. Examples of unsupervised learning tasks typically include clustering and dimensionality reduction.

•

Semi-Supervised learning: The ML model is trained with a mixture of labelled and unlabelled data. Examples of semi-supervised learning include reinforcement learning and physics-informed neural networks.

The plasma closure problem has so far been approached as a supervised learning problem, where labelled training data is generated through high-resolution kinetic simulations directly, or from a known analytic closure equation. A semi-supervised approach is also applicable, however, utilising techniques such as physics-informed neural networks, where the ML model is trained on both labelled data from simulations and through the values of the governing equations evaluated at ‘collocation points’.
(
36
)

The goal, then, is to choose the most appropriate techniques from the field of machine learning to train a model for the plasma moment closure able to capture kinetic effects and to be integrated into large-scale plasma simulations without incurring a steep computational cost. Although the plasma community has only recently started to appreciate the potential of using machine learning in the closure problem, in this review, we aim to collate several preliminary works. Hopefully, the review will inform future efforts and highlight possible research directions.

2.1
Why ML?

A pertinent question to ask is why a data-driven approach to improving plasma closure models is appropriate and useful. Some of the rationale is related to the relative success of employing machine learning in the neighbouring field of neutral fluid dynamics (including turbulence)
(
25
;
5
;
4
)
.

Inherently, by truncating the system of equations, we are making an approximation to describe a reduced model of the plasma. In performing this approximation, the model loses detail, causing simple plasma fluid models to be incapable of capturing kinetic effects. An approximation of this kind is inevitably a trade-off between accuracy and computational cost. By utilising a neural network, we can take advantage of its ’universal approximator’ property to learn an approximate closure that could remain close to the fully kinetic model, while reducing the computational cost of modelling
(
11
)
. In other words, if we accept the fundamental assumption of any fluid model that a closure exists and that it preserves, to some level of accuracy, the dynamics of low-order moments, then a neural network should be able to approximate such a closure.

The main benefit of the neural network approach is in the speed at which neural networks can make predictions, as even for a neural network that takes a significant time to train, the forward propagation of the network to make predictions once trained is typically much faster than traditional numerical methods, making neural network approaches also more suitable for real-time applications.

In contrast to the ’black-box’ approach of neural networks, multiple attempts are being made at using equation discovery methods, such as sparse and symbolic regressions, to find governing equations of systems. By utilising these equation discovery methods, an analytic form of the closure relation could be found, purely from a data-driven approach. Such analytic closures could aid in the development of theoretical work, as well as provide more stable models for forecasting and simulation.

2.2
ML Models

2.2.1
Neural Networks

Many recent studies aimed at improving plasma closure are utilising neural networks. The subject of neural networks and their applications (or deep learning) is large, and due to the current popularity of the field, it is expanding rapidly. For this review, an outline of the relevant neural network architectures will be given to provide extra context for some of the techniques discussed.

A neural network is a machine learning model that can approximate any continuous function to any desired level of accuracy, according to the universal approximation theorem.
(
11
)
Neural networks, initially inspired by the human brain, are made up of ’neurons’, arranged in layers, with neurons in subsequent layers connected via tunable parameters known as ’weights’. The individual neurons in neural networks can be represented as a simple linear combination of connected inputs and a nonlinear ’activation function’.

z\>=\sigma(\mathsfbi{W}\boldsymbol{x}+\boldsymbol{b})

(20)

Where
z
is the output of the neuron,
\boldsymbol{x}
is the input vector of the neuron,
\mathsfbi{W}
is the matrix of weights representing the neural network layer,
\boldsymbol{b}
is the bias vector, and
\sigma
is the non-linear activation function. Typically, only
\mathsfbi{W}
and
\boldsymbol{b}
are trainable parameters of the neural network. The optimal weights and biases are estimated by solving an optimization problem that minimizes a loss function, defined as the error between the neural network output and the corresponding ground truth. Networks are often trained using the backpropagation algorithm, which enables a version of gradient descent to be applied efficiently to each trainable parameter of the network.

A significant advantage of the neural network approach is the ability for the model to be trained on fully kinetic simulation data, without any prior knowledge of the governing models. Neural networks are ’universal approximators’, meaning that theoretically, a neural network with enough layers and neurons, and with enough available training data, can approximate the input data to an arbitrary level of accuracy
(
11
)
. This characteristic of neural networks allows them to be applied to situations where little is known about the underlying model, which could be the case for some high-dimensional kinetic simulations, where an analytic closure model that can capture the dynamics is not available in closed form.

A different type of neural networks, Physics-informed neural networks (PINNs), were developed by
36
as a way to integrate physical knowledge of the system into the architecture and training of neural networks. This is done by including the governing equations of the system into the loss function used for training the neural network. By including the governing equations, the PDE of the system is treated as a regularisation term in the loss function
(
17
)
. A more recent variation of the PINN has been developed by
51
, the gradient-enhanced PINN (gPINN), which includes the gradients of the physics loss terms as additional constraints during the training of the network.

Neural operators, developed by
18
, are networks that, instead of approximating a function, seek to approximate an operator representing the mapping between the input and output function spaces. Many variations of the neural operator have been developed, such as the Deep Operator Network (DeepONet) from
30
and the Fourier Neural Operator (FNO) from
26
A variation of the neural operator, including the principles of PINNs, has also been developed by
27
, the physics-informed neural operator (PINO). In principle, neural operators improve upon neural networks by being better adapted to including multiple initial conditions in their training, whereas a traditional neural network would need to be re-trained for each set of different initial and boundary conditions when approximating a system. Neural operators have also been shown to have a ’discretisation invariance’ allowing training to be conducted on solutions obtained on a coarser discretisation to be later generalized on finer resolutions.

Another benefit, particularly applicable to neural operators and physics-informed neural networks, is the potential for greater adaptability and generalisation, especially for the newer approaches of While well-generalisable models can be difficult to train, once trained, a neural network model can generalise well to multiple different conditions of the system, a contrast to the analytic closure approach, which typically requires a different closure model for each regime and phenomena being investigated. The adaptability of these methods to multiple different plasma phenomena, however, has not been fully investigated as of yet, with only a few studies, such as the work by
49
, attempting to investigate the ability for neural operators to generalise to multiple different initial conditions.

Neural networks, while promising, do suffer from some significant disadvantages. A primary concern for physics is the lack of interpretability of the neural network models. Extracting meaning from the models is increasingly difficult if the entire system of fluid equations is being approximated by a model, as is the case for some studies. Neural networks can also be subject to problems of accumulating errors and numerical instability when results are propagated in time, either through numerical schemes when the closure is included in a plasma model, or if using a time-dependent neural network, such as a recurrent neural network or a transformer. This is due to the small errors that will be present in the neural network approximation after training, which will eventually grow over time into large errors and possibly cause the growth of instabilities in the simulation.

The rapid progress in neural network research has also resulted in a large set of possible architectures and, therefore, choices that a researcher must make when finding the best solution to their problem. It is often not obvious which type of neural network will work best for the plasma closure problem, and the secondary issue of choosing the optimal hyperparameters for the neural network could also present some issues for future work in modelling the plasma closure relation. Much of the research in applying neural networks to the plasma closure has been concerned with finding which neural network architecture will be most suitable for the given regime and conditions. The work from
31
and
33
represents early investigations into how the different neural network types perform for the given closure model.

2.2.2
Sparse Regression

Sparse regression methods can infer the governing partial differential equations (PDEs) of dynamical systems by identifying the coefficients of a large library of candidate terms that best represent the training data
(
37
)
. This is accomplished by using time series data of system variables and their derivatives to estimate a set of sparse coefficient vectors corresponding to different non-linear terms in the library. The library itself is constructed by generating all possible non-linear combinations of the input variables and their derivatives up to a specified order. In many practical cases, derivatives are not directly available from the data and must be approximated numerically, for example, using finite-difference schemes. The chosen numerical method and its order of accuracy can significantly influence the convergence and stability of the sparse regression process. The choice of how many terms to include in the library is crucial and can critically affect the performance of the sparse regression technique.

Where sparse regression differs from symbolic regression is in the principle of Pareto optimisation. The sparse regression process will seek to use the fewest number of terms possible while minimising the cost function. Sparse regression in this way is utilising an ’Occam’s razor’ approach, that the simplest model to match the training data is most probably the correct one.

Recently, some packages have been developed for implementing sparse regression more quickly and easily for the discovery of underlying partial differential equations, namely PySINDy, based on the SINDy (sparse identification of non-linear dynamics) method
(
16
;
3
)
.

Of relevance to this review is also the PDE-net method from
29
, which is notable for the ability to remove terms from the library during the training process, cutting down on required computations.

The main advantage of the sparse regression technique, and equation discovery more generally, is the highly interpretable models that it produces, a stark contrast to the neural network approach. Due to this interpretability, it is also possible to use sparse regression as a means of informing theoretical work on the plasma closure, using the machine learning approach as a means of extracting some physical meaning out of complex nonlinear dynamics. An example of this process is present in the work of Alves and Fiuza, where they utilise the sparse regression method to obtain a hierarchy of models of different complexities, and can link these models to different physical approximations required to derive them.
(
1
)

Sparse regression, while able to produce highly interpretable models, does require some amount of prior knowledge of the system. The library of terms to be used by the method needs to be specifically defined, and can quickly become very large for high-dimensional problems, causing the method to require significant computation to complete. Due to the expanding term library, the number of terms must be reduced in many cases, necessitating a level of knowledge about which terms are most likely to be relevant to the problem.

Noise in the training data can also become a problem for the sparse regression technique. For the case of learning governing fluid equations from kinetic data, however,
1
demonstrated that learning the equations in integral form allows systemic noise to be reduced by performing spatial integrals over the data. See also
34
for a relevant technique, applied to terrestrial weather modelling.

3
Plasma Closure Review

A surrogate model is a simplified model used to capture complex dynamics in a computationally efficient way. For the plasma closure problem, our surrogate model can either be an approximation for the entire system of plasma fluid equations, including a closure relation implicitly, or an approximation of the closure relation only, which is then integrated into the full plasma fluid model.

3.1
Analytic Closure Training Data

Early work on constructing surrogate models for the plasma closure relation using neural networks was chiefly concerned with presenting proof-of-concept studies, which used already known analytic models to train neural networks to see if the neural networks were capable of capturing the dynamics of the analytic closures to a suitable degree of accuracy. These early studies also typically use the classical neural network architectures, such as the multi-layer perceptron and the convolutional neural network. The studies applying the classical architectures were important to show that even the simplest implementation of the neural network approach can capture the kinetic effects described by the analytic closure models.

One of the earliest efforts in this regard was by
31
. This proof-of-principle study represents an early investigation into the viability of neural networks as surrogate models for analytic closure models. The authors chose to investigate Landau damping via the Hammett-Perkins closure model in configuration space to test different neural network architectures:

\tilde{q}(x)=-n_{0}\sqrt{\frac{8}{\upi^{3}}}v_{t}\int_{0}^{\infty}dx^{\prime}\frac{\left[\tilde{T}\left(x+x^{\prime}\right)-\tilde{T}\left(x-x^{\prime}\right)\right]}{x^{\prime}}

(21)

Where the authors have performed an inverse Fourier transform on the Hammett-Perkins closure of equation
19
. The authors chose to learn the closure in configuration space to investigate non-local behaviour, which is not present in the original Hammett-Perkins closure in Fourier space, due to the dependence on temperature at locations close to
x
, from the
T(x+x^{\prime})
terms only present in the closure in configuration space.

The authors constructed three different neural network architectures: a multi-layer perceptron (MLP), a convolutional neural network (CNN) and a two-layer discrete Fourier transform (DFT) network. Training data was generated directly from equation
21
. The space was discretised into 128 grid points, and the inputs to each neural network model were the temperature values at each of these grid points, with the outputs being the value of the heat flux at each grid point. Each grid point was treated as a separate input feature, resulting in each neural network model having an input layer and output layer of width 128.

The authors found that a minimum volume of training data was required for the deep learning methods to perform well. More interestingly, the authors found that the MLP required also a minimum number of neurons in the hidden layers, corresponding to the number of degrees of freedom of the closure model in Fourier space, despite the training of the models being conducted in configuration space.

The authors also investigated the effects of adding noise to the training data. The noise was found to have no additional effect when the magnitude of the noise was less than the overall prediction error of the neural network itself.

Finally, the authors identified key characteristics of the model architectures, such as the locality of the closure model, any systemic noise in the data, and the scale and dimensionality of the experiment, that will need to be considered and related to the choice of the deep learning model.

The work from
31
, being the earliest example covered in this review, represents an important first step in applying a data-driven approach to plasma closure.

33
built upon the work of
31
, exploring the potential of neural networks to approximate analytic closure relations and then integrate the models into a plasma fluid model. The primary objective of this work was to examine the applicability of neural networks for approximating general closure relations within magnetised plasmas, investigating both collisional and collisionless plasma regimes.

The study considered three analytic closure models, each representing different physical assumptions and regimes: The Braginskii closure
(
2
)
, representing the collisional regime, a fully local closure; The Hammett-Perkins closure
(
10
)
, representing the collisionless limit with periodic boundary conditions, and global non-locality; The Guo-Tang closure
(
9
)
represents the collisionless regime with an upstream Maxwellian plasma source and a downstream absorbing wall. This closure has non-locality that is contained over a finite region.

For the Braginskii closure, the authors used a supervised learning method, using the lower-order moments of the density, velocity, the velocity gradients and the temperature at different points as input features; the target variables were the diagonal components of the viscosity tensor. For the Hammett-Perkins closure, the authors used the temperature at points in the domain as the input and mapped these to values of the heat flux for the outputs. For the Guo-Tang closure, the authors generate training profiles of the magnetic field and the ambipolar potential for use as inputs, and train the network to match the three components of the heat flux.

The authors investigated three different neural network architectures in the study: A fully connected neural network, a locally connected neural network and a convolutional neural network. The authors found a link between the performance of the different network architectures and the inherent spatial locality of the target closure model, indicating a link between the connectedness of the network and the spatial locality of the target model. In particular, it was found that for spatially local closures, such as the Braginskii closure, networks with less connectivity perform best, and for closures that are spatially non-local, such as the Guo-Tang closure, a fully connected network is required.

The work by
33

This review is chiefly interested in collisionless astrophysical plasmas, but the findings of the closure locality and network connectedness will be of relevance to any future work seeking to use neural networks to learn closure relations from kinetic data.

3.2
Kinetic Simulation Training Data

Building further on the work to approximate analytic closure relations was the study by
22
that explored the use of neural networks to derive local closure relations for the electron pressure tensor and heat flux vector directly from fully kinetic simulation data. This represents one of the earliest works to apply neural networks to learn directly from kinetic simulations, and one of the only studies to look at learning a model approximating the full pressure tensor.

The data used to train the machine learning models was extracted from fully kinetic simulations of a double-Harris sheet experiment of magnetic reconnection. The simulation data were produced using the implicit Particle-in-Cell code iPiC3D
(
32
)
. The magnetic field gradients were computed using second-order finite differences. The authors generated 4 different simulations with identical initial conditions except for the background guiding magnetic field, which was increased for each simulation.

The authors trained two different machine learning models to learn the closure relations: a multilayer perceptron (MLP) and a gradient-boosting regressor (GBR). A baseline linear regressor was also included for comparison.

Training, validation and test sets were generated from the simulation data by assigning different time snapshots from each of the four simulations to each dataset and choosing cells in the simulation based on the agyrotropy to preferentially choose regions of reconnection. From these cell snapshots, the inputs are generated by calculating the velocity moments from the PIC particle data, namely, the magnetic field vector, its gradients and magnitude, the velocity vector, the charge density and the value
\alpha
taken from the closure of
24
, where the parallel and perpendicular components of the pressure tensor can be approximated as,

\displaystyle p_{*\parallel}=n_{*}\frac{2}{2+\alpha}+\frac{\upi^{3}n_{*}^{3}}{6B_{*}^{2}}\frac{2\alpha}{2\alpha+1}

(24)

\displaystyle p_{*\perp}=n_{*}\frac{1}{1+\alpha}+n_{*}B_{*}\frac{\alpha}{\alpha+1}

(25)

Where
\alpha=n_{*}^{3}/B_{*}^{2}

To avoid confusion, the machine learning models were not trained on data generated by the closure of
24

For both the diagonal and off-diagonal components of the electron pressure tensor, the machine learning models demonstrated comparable performance to the empirical closure model and outperformed the baseline linear regression model. The MLP model outperformed the GBR model for the diagonal components of the pressure tensor across the entire reconnection region; however, the GBR model was more accurate away from the reconnection site.

The machine learning models struggled particularly in the prediction of fluctuations in the off-diagonal components of the electron pressure tensor and the heat flux vector. The authors identified their sampling strategy as a source of error. The study also highlighted limitations in the amount and diversity of data used to train the models. The authors noted that the numerical noise in the data, inherent to PIC simulations, constrained the models’ ability to generalise. The authors suggested that a broader and more varied dataset, including different initial conditions, would help create more robust machine learning models.

The paper from
22
demonstrates that machine learning models can successfully learn spatially local closure relations for complex plasma systems from fully kinetic simulations. The models consistently outperformed a baseline linear regressor and performed comparably to empirical closure models for both the electron pressure tensor and heat flux vector. While the models showed promising results, the study also identified several areas for improvement, with the off-diagonal components of the electron pressure tensor presenting a particular challenge.

Notable also is the fact that this study used 2-dimensional PIC simulation data to train machine learning models. Primarily, studies thus far have been restricted to one-dimensional problems, so the work from
22
provides an insight into the challenges and benefits of extending the methods to more dimensions.

The study by
35
applies the PINN and gPINN model architectures to learn from one-dimensional kinetic simulation data of Landau damping. This work is significant in being one of the earliest approaches to the plasma closure problem that applies physics-informed neural networks to the learning of kinetic simulation data. The closure relation was trained implicitly within the model, relating the heat flux to the lower-order moments.

The authors developed a new variation of gPINN, the gPINNp, which uses only the gradient of the pressure residual, as opposed to the gradients of all residuals. Only using one gradient significantly reduces the computational cost of gPINN.

The authors calculated the first 3 moments from the kinetic simulation data and compared these with the outputs of the networks to judge the performance. The authors find that all 3 models tested were able to predict the plasma moments well, even into late times.

The authors find that of the 3 models tested, the new gPINNp is the most accurate, especially at later times. This implies that the heat flux is most closely correlated with the pressure and its gradients, consistent with the expectations for a Landau fluid closure.

From Figure

The network takes the time and position as inputs and outputs the values of the density, bulk velocity, pressure, heat flux, and electric field at that point and time. However, learning the entire system of equations in this way does limit the scope of including the closure in different fluid model simulations, as the closure model cannot be simply inserted into a fluid solver. The physics-informed loss function includes the fluid equations up to the pressure equation, to be minimised as residuals.

The kinetic simulation was sampled sparsely at early times, forcing the models to extrapolate for later simulation times. This allowed the authors to demonstrate the potential for generalisation that the PINN method allows. The gPINNp model agrees well with the kinetic simulation at late times, despite the lack of training data for late times. This is an example of the physics-informed neural network approach being better able to extrapolate beyond the training data, which a traditional neural network is not typically able to do.

Very interestingly, the authors were able to show that the gPINNp method took advantage of the high dependence expected of the pressure on the closure relation. Insights such as these, using knowledge and intuition of the target system, in conjunction with the data-driven approach, could lead to better-performing models with a reduced computational cost.

The study is an example of a successful proof-of-concept of the PINN method. Taking the findings of this work into future research will be important, especially in the application of PINNs to higher-dimensional problems, where the additional computational costs would increase training time if a full gradient-enhanced PINN model is used. More investigation into the predictive capability of these models is also required, as this study shows that the PINNs can make predictions at times outside of those which it has been trained on, it will be important to fully study how this aspect of PINNs will operate within a full application to a fluid simulation, specifically if the trained PINNs will be able to continue to make accurate predictions beyond training regimes, or with different initial/boundary conditions. Further investigation into PINNs for higher-dimensional problems is also needed, as so far, only applications in one dimension have been attempted.

A later study from
49
gives a first application of neural operators to the plasma closure, giving an insight into the performance capabilities of the Fourier neural operator architecture, as well as a proof-of-concept investigation into the ability of neural operators to learn from multiple different initial conditions, without the need for retraining.

The study from
49
seeks to improve the generalisability of surrogate machine learning models to different initial conditions by using the Fourier neural operator (FNO) introduced by
26
. The authors train an FNO network on kinetic simulation data in one dimension to learn a multi-moment fluid model with a closure that can capture Landau damping from multiple sets of kinetic simulation data with different initial conditions. The authors compare the results of the FNO to those of a multilayer perceptron as a baseline.

The authors find that the FNO can achieve a
10.8\%
relative error on the test set, while the MLP was only able to achieve a
66.7\%
relative error. They attribute this to the FNO’s ability to describe mapping between function spaces, as opposed to the MLP, which can only approximate single functions. The two networks are both trained on the same set of data, highlighting the neural operator’s advantage over the classical MLP network in being able to learn from multiple sets of data with different initial conditions.

Further study into the performance of neural operators on the plasma closure model will be needed, especially to investigate into the difference between a classical neural network architecture trained on a dataset with only one set of initial conditions and a neural operator trained on datasets with multiple different initial conditions, to gain a more complete picture of the difference in performance between the two model approaches. For future work, it will also be important to see this principle of multiple initial conditions in comparison to some more extensive studies in higher dimensions, to find if there will be a significant improvement over more typical neural networks.

There is also a possibility that the Fourier network employed in the FNO was simply more suited to the problem than the MLP in a similar situation to what was observed by
31
when using a discrete Fourier transform network to learn the Hammett-Perkins closure. The kinetic model used by the authors was defined as a set of randomly generated perturbation modes, perhaps causing the kinetic data to be well suited to a Fourier transform approach, irrespective of the wider neural network architecture. Further application of the neural operators on different closure models and kinetic simulations will be useful to gain a more robust understanding of the difference between neural operators and classical neural network architectures.

A more recent application of the FNO technique to the plasma closure problem comes from
12
. This study employs an FNO model to learn a closure model from simulation data of both linear and non-linear Landau damping.

The authors train the FNO to map the lower-order fluid moments to the gradient of the heat flux. Training data is generated from a Vlasov solver code for both cases of linear and non-linear Landau damping. The authors were inspired by the Hammett-Perkins closure of equation
19
to use a Fourier neural operator, as the Hammett-Perkins closure is local in Fourier space.

The authors generated the training dataset by taking snapshots of the Vlasov solver data at an increased time step compared to the original data and then used the entire dataset for testing.

The work goes on to implement the trained FNO into a fluid solver, which can recover Landau damping. The authors find that the FNO can capture both linear and non-linear Landau damping well in the fluid model, and can better reproduce the results of the kinetic simulation than the fluid solver using the Hammett-Perkins closure model, indicating that the data-driven closure can better capture the kinetic physics and reproduce the desired phenomenon in the fluid model.

It is unclear from the study how the specific choice of the FNO architecture affected the performance of the model. Going forward, a full comparison of different architectures will be required to investigate exactly what is required for successfully learning the plasma closure. Considerations for the best choices of architecture will be more important when applying the method to higher dimensions, where the increased volume of data and the requirement for more complex closures will increase the computational demand of training the models.

The final test of these surrogate models will be in their integration into large-scale fluid simulations, with the final goal of the process being to be able to simulate large-scale systems such as the Earth’s magnetosphere. It is important, then, in future work to focus on developing robust surrogate models that can accurately capture the kinetic physics, all while decreasing the computational cost over fully kinetic simulations and hybrid simulations. It will be important to investigate how the neural network surrogate models can deal with fully 3-dimensional problems and more complicated dynamics beyond the toy models that have been used so far to test the techniques.

3.3
Data Driven Equation Discovery

In addition to the research into data-driven surrogates of the plasma closure, there are investigations into finding explicit analytic closure models directly from the kinetic simulation data. Equation discovery consists of many different machine learning methods of extracting models from data; the studies discussed here will predominantly be concerned with sparse regression, with one study by
28
utilising physics-informed neural networks in an inverse problem to find the governing system of equations from a kinetic simulation.

Conversely to the surrogate model approach, equation discovery results in directly interpretable models, and can involve using the data-driven results as a means of informing theoretical development of further closure models. The analytic closure equations produced from equation discovery methods will be more straightforward to implement into fluid solvers than the previously discussed neural network models, although the data-driven equations can still be subject to the approximation errors inherent to all machine learning approaches, which will need to be addressed and investigated when implementing them into fluid simulations.

Equation discovery techniques typically rely on a library of terms from which an algorithm can choose the most suitable combination. The technique quickly becomes limited by the requirement for knowledge of which terms are expected to be present in the closure. The need to reduce the scope of the library of terms may not be a hindrance for low-dimensional problems or simple phenomena, but when taking to high dimensions and very non-linear behaviours, the number of possible terms can increase significantly, resulting in a much greater computational requirement for training. Prior knowledge of expected terms is then needed to reduce the size of the library before the implementation of the machine learning method.

3.3.1
SINDy Approach

An early example of using equation discovery to find a plasma closure relation is the study by
1
, which utilises sparse regression to find reduced models of plasma dynamics from fully kinetic plasma simulations. The study aimed to seek interpretable and generalizable models from which links to theoretical work in plasma physics could be found.

The authors found that the sparse regression algorithm is sensitive to noise in the data, which can be significant in PIC simulations, due to the projection of particles onto the grid. This study addresses these challenges by reformulating the sparse regression method to identify the partial differential equations in their integral form, helping to reduce the effects of noise and improve the accuracy of the identified models. By reformulating, the authors can utilise a centred-finite-difference method to integrate the target variables over compact volumes in the data, reducing the noise and significantly reducing the error compared to the identification of the equations in differential form.

The authors first applied sparse regression to recover the kinetic Vlasov equation and both the one-fluid MHD and multi-fluid equations directly from the kinetic simulation data. By utilising the method of finding the underlying partial differential equations in their integral form, the authors were able to recover the Vlasov equation to within a
2\%
error. The multi-fluid equations were able to be found within a
1-2\%
error, while the one-fluid MHD equations were found within a
1-4\%
error, demonstrating the ability of the sparse regression algorithm to identify the governing equations of the system.

Through the analysis of the accuracy-complexity graphs of the plasma fluid sparse regression task, the authors can identify a hierarchy of reduced models with increasing complexity (see Figure
2
). The authors identify the models of this hierarchy as different closure models resulting from various physical approximations. The authors are therefore able to identify the terms for which their inclusion results in the largest increase in model accuracy, implying these terms are the most relevant to the dynamics of the system. The authors then propose the analysis of this hierarchy of models as a method for which new closure models could be informed.

The paper concludes that sparse regression is a powerful tool for extracting interpretable and generalisable reduced models from complex plasma simulations. The approach provides a bridge between computational data and theoretical plasma physics, facilitating the development of more accurate, data-driven models. Future work on improving the closure relations will focus on utilising the hierarchy of models as a guiding framework for choosing terms that are most important to the dynamics of the plasma when constructing new closure relations.

This early application of the sparse regression technique to the plasma closure serves as a great example of how the technique can be used to inform future work on developing closure models, as seen by the model hierarchy, which the authors were able to produce and link directly to well-known closure models resulting from different physical approximations. Alves and Fiuza were also very influential in their solution to the problem of the systemic noise from kinetic particle-in-cell simulations, with many of the future uses of sparse regression also learning the governing equations in integral form to mitigate the effect of noise in the training data.

Building off of the study from
1
is the paper by
7
, which constructs a ten-moment fluid model of a plasma by taking moments of the kinetic Vlasov equation. The authors then utilise the SINDy method for sparse regression to discover an appropriate closure model that includes the kinetic effects relevant to the dynamics. The authors use data from magnetic reconnection events of a kinetic PIC simulation.

The authors used the particle simulation code (PSC) from
8
, a fully kinetic PIC simulation code, to generate the training data. PIC simulations are subject to systemic noise introduced due to the sampling of macro-particles to construct the plasma distribution function. This study uses the technique of learning the integral form of the underlying equations from
1
to reduce the effect of the noise. The simulation used was that of a collisionless Harris sheet reconnection problem.

The authors utilise their modified SINDy method for verifying the ten-moment model of plasma, finding the local Hammett-Perkins closure, then finally applying the method in search of an improved closure model. Their methods involve loading the moment and field data from the PSC simulation, then calculating the spatial and temporal derivatives through numerical methods.

The authors have shown that fully kinetic simulation data may be used to verify the 10-moment multi-fluid model as demonstrated up to and including the second-order conservation laws. This was achieved by satisfying the 0th, 1st and 2nd order moment equations with numerical error
\leq 0.28
. (calculated from a normalised
L_{2}
metric). Only the on-diagonal terms were verified up to the 2nd-order terms. Difficulties were found in the discovery of off-diagonal terms, as the discovered equations had large coefficient errors, a result that is also shared by the study from
22
, highlighting the difficulty in accurately approximating the off-diagonal pressure terms for magnetic reconnection events.

The authors found that their discovered solutions narrowly deviated from conservation laws, finding more sparse solutions with lower numerical error. This was due to the excluded terms having a lower order of magnitude, leading to elimination by SINDy. The authors argue that this is not, however, evidence that the discovered model violates conservation laws, but is a consequence of the choice of bounds to eliminate terms. The use of feature scaling instead of a pre-specified range of coefficients could help to alleviate this.

When comparing the discovered closure model to the Hammett-Perkins model baseline, the on-diagonal terms significantly reduced numerical error over the baseline, while the off-diagonal terms were unsuccessfully modelled, failing to reduce numerical error. The discovered closure was found to hold well in the non-linear phase, but could not be guaranteed for the linear phase, as the data for the linear phase did not have a large enough signal-to-noise ratio for the linear phase to be included in training.

The authors were unable to demonstrate the performance of their discovered closure relation in the linear regime due to the low signal-to-noise ratio. Future studies will need to investigate these regimes of significant noise to ensure the closure models can capture all the relevant dynamics. In this case, the kinetic simulation data has inherent systemic noise that must be mitigated for the models to be properly trained and tested; however, reducing the noise levels in the simulations will require new numerical techniques or a non-trivial amount of additional computational resources to increase the number of particles.

Interestingly, then, this study from Donaghy and Germaschewski encountered similar difficulties in modelling the off-diagonal components of the pressure tensor as the study from
22
. Future work must be concerned with mitigating these difficulties, which have arisen even between two different approaches. More research needs to be conducted to investigate the factors limiting the performance of the learned closure models on the off-diagonal components, whether it is down to a need to include 3-dimensional simulation data, a requirement for larger training datasets or an inherent difficulty of the specific machine learning methods being used.

A more recent example of equation discovery, utilising sparse regression, is from
15
. In this study, the authors use sparse regression on one-dimensional kinetic PIC simulations of Landau damping and the two-stream instability to construct a closure relation for the plasma heat flux. The authors go on to identify the relative importance of terms in the trained closure relation.

The authors find the most accurate expressions for the heat flux in a range of model complexities. Model complexity is controlled by varying the target number of terms which are to be included in the final output of the sparse regression method. Investigating models with different complexities allows the authors to find the optimal complexity for maximising model accuracy. The study utilises the spatial integration technique from Alves and Fiuza to reduce the effect of simulation noise on the sparse regression technique.

The authors investigated both a case of Landau damping and a case of Landau growth from the two-stream instability. The sparse regression technique gives similar models for each of the two cases, with the optimal model for each application including 6 terms. By selecting terms in the closure relations to remove, the study can quantify the relative importance of each term in the discovered closure by measuring the change in relative error of the approximation. Interestingly, it was found that removing some of the terms had more impact on certain regimes, with one term being found to have a higher relative importance during the growth/decay phase, inferring that the term is at least partially responsible for describing this behaviour, while the other terms do not affect behaviour in that regime.

Steps to be taken to improve the model in the future are outlined by the authors. The study did not include any terms coupled with the
E
and
B
fields in the term library, which would significantly increase the size of the term library and therefore increase the computational cost. The authors indicate the need to also investigate a non-local closure, as this study was only considering a local approximation of the closure. There are also problems in implementing fluid closures into fluid simulations and solvers due to the rise of numerical instabilities, representing a highly non-trivial problem to overcome.

3.3.2
Neural Network Approach

As a different approach to the equation discovery methodology, the study by
28
uses a PINN implementation to learn a plasma closure relation of the heat flux in terms of the pressure, as well as recovering the 10-moment fluid model equations. The authors use training data from a kinetic simulation of Landau damping of a collisionless plasma in one dimension. This study is significant for being one of the earliest applications of PINNs to the plasma closure problem.

The authors utilise two PINNs, one learning the fluid model coefficients from the kinetic data, and the other forming a surrogate model, trained to predict the output.

The method results in the following closure relation for the heat flux, relating the heat flux in terms of the pressure, density and their gradients:

q=-4.1231\sqrt{\frac{p}{n}}\left(\frac{\partial p}{\partial x}-\frac{p}{n}\frac{\partial n}{\partial x}\right)

(26)

The prediction, equation
26
, found by the trained PINN agrees well with theoretical predictions for both the density and the electric field. The coefficients found by the PINN are also found to be within less than
1\%
numerical error from the coefficients of the theoretical 10-moment fluid model. The authors verify the accuracy of the learned coefficient for the heat flux closure by comparing predictions using the closure and the simulated data, finding good agreement between the two.

The study represents an early implementation of the PINN architecture to find a plasma fluid closure. The work is restricted to a one-dimensional problem and only used to find coefficients of a pre-defined closure, restricting the technique of equation discovery still to prior known forms of the closure relation. Significantly, this study shows that a PINN approach to solving the inverse problem to extract governing equations from kinetic simulation data is also viable and comparable in performance to that of sparse regression, which has seen much more use in subsequent studies.

Another study from
6
goes a step further by investigating the predictive capabilities of their discovered models by making predictions through time with the closure model. The authors derive the governing equations of the plasma fluid model from kinetic simulation data using PDE-net. They can derive the set of governing equations that can recreate the kinetic effects.

The PDE-net method from
29
is a method combining sparse regression with neural networks that can reject terms completely from the training process as the terms are found to be unimportant to the target model.
6
use the PDE-net method to learn the multi-moment fluid model from the kinetic plasma simulation.

The PDE-net method is capable of learning from time-series data. The authors used spatio-temporal data from the plasma kinetic simulation. They also use time-prediction as a final step after the PDE-net learns the multi-moment fluid model. The time differentiation adopts the forward Euler method, and the space differentiation adopts the central difference method with periodic boundary conditions.

The discovered closure relation from
6
is as follows:

q=-4.2665n\sqrt{T}\frac{\partial T}{\partial x}

(27)

Which, a relation to the temperature, is identical in form to the discovered relation from the study by
28
when replacing the Temperature,
T=p/n
.

Within the time interval investigated, the authors found that the physical quantities predicted agree with the kinetic data. The authors found that errors gradually increase as time is propagated; this is potentially due to small errors in the learned coefficients causing small deviations to build over time, but there could also be potential for small numerical errors due to the prediction scheme to build over time.

6
were able to recover the multi-moment fluid model and a closure model for the heat flux. The PDE-net method, while being able to rely more on the predictive capabilities of neural networks, still requires expected terms to be included in the library. The learned multi-moment model was able to recreate the Landau damping rates observed in the kinetic simulation data.

This study made a good demonstration of the PDE-net technique for sparse regression, as well as a great demonstration of taking the learned multi-moment fluid model, with closure, and propagating this through time to make predictions that can be compared with the kinetic simulation data. The authors were able to demonstrate the ability of the PDE-net technique to capture Landau damping in one dimension, showing agreement with both linear theory and the kinetic simulation data.

The PDE-net technique has therefore been demonstrated as a viable approach to learning the closure relation in one dimension; further work will need to extend the technique into higher dimensions. This study only considered a simple case of Landau damping, allowing for a high level of prior knowledge of the expected model. A future test of the PDE-net technique will be required to consider more complex phenomena with an unknown closure relation. The authors also highlight the growth of errors at late times in their predictions, possibly due to the numerical error in the learned coefficients of the model. Discovering the exact source of these errors and finding ways to mitigate their impact will be important for future work and the integration of the models into large-scale simulations.

Much progress has been made on applying equation discovery techniques to the plasma closure problem. Sparse regression has been the most popular technique, but there have also been examples of neural networks (either combined with sparse regression, like PDE-net or used in an inverse problem with PINNs). Most of the current research has been proof-of-principle studies, with the majority of applications being in one dimension; only the work from
7
has attempted to do sparse regression for a 2-dimensional problem so far.

There has been interesting work done by
1
, as well as
15
, to use sparse regression as a method of inferring physics from the simulation data, with the model complexity hierarchy from
1
, to the principle of removing terms from the model to investigate their relative importance that
15
demonstrated. These investigations into inferring physical meaning from the data-driven approaches are well-suited to the equation discovery methodology, especially as compared to the much less interpretable neural network surrogate approach.

Despite the advancement of equation discovery methods in the application to the plasma closure model, numerous challenges remain to be overcome. There is a need to investigate further how the models perform when integrated into large-scale models to overcome issues of numerical stability when making predictions over time. There has been some discussion from
15
as well as
6
on the integration of the discovered closure models into larger plasma fluid models, but investigation into how these models perform in high-dimensional problems is important for the goal of integrating the data-driven models into large-scale plasma fluid simulations.

Most of the techniques used so far make use of a term library, which can explode in size for complex models and in higher dimensions. Investigation into how to mitigate the computational costs at high dimensions and how to reduce the size of the term library is therefore required to extend these techniques into higher dimensions and to more complex dynamics without the computational cost of learning the closure becoming exponentially large.

4
Discussion

4.1
Overview of Prior Work

Work on applying the novel machine learning techniques to the plasma closure problem is ongoing, with the majority of the studies outlined here being benchmark and proof-of-concept tests rather than full-fledged applications of the technique. It is clear from the work outlined here that the techniques are viable for test cases and worthy of investigation into more complex problems.

The first notable study in applying neural networks to the plasma closure problem was by
31
, which acted as an initial proof-of-concept study to see if neural networks were capable of capturing the behaviour found in the analytic closure model of
10
.
31
demonstrated the viability of this method, showing that the neural network models were capable of approximating well the non-linear behaviour expected from the plasma closure models.

The work of
31
was extended by
33
by applying neural network models to learn multiple different analytic closure relations for different regimes of collisionality in the plasma. The study was able to identify a link between the connectedness of the neural network architecture employed and the spatial locality of the target closure model, which must be an important consideration for future work when choosing a machine learning architecture to use.

46
, while focusing more on collisional regimes, went on to train a neural network on data generated from a plasma closure relation and implement the model into a plasma fluid simulation, demonstrating the ability of the machine learning approach to include the kinetic effects in future plasma fluid simulations.

The study by
22
was significant for its use of Particle-in-Cell simulation data to train neural networks, this being the first example presented here of a neural network being trained on fully kinetic plasma simulation data in two spatial dimensions. This study also informed important considerations for future work, namely, the difficulties that the neural networks had in approximating the off-diagonal components of the pressure tensor. Solving this could require larger/more complex network architectures, more available training data, or a different approach to learning the closure relation.

1
made the first effort in applying sparse regression to fully kinetic plasma simulation data.
1
were able to address the problem of the systemic noise inherent to the particle-in-cell simulations when attempting to apply sparse regression, a method sensitive to noise in the training data. Their solution of learning the PDEs in integral form will be useful in any future work applying a similar approach. This work also introduced the ’hierarchy of reduced models’ from their Pareto-optimisation process, which could be useful as a future tool to inform parallel theoretical work on finding new analytic closure relations.

The work from
7

4.2
Comparison of Methods

As outlined here, the progress of work on applying scientific machine learning principles can be roughly separated into two groups: that of finding an explicit closure relation, either as a machine learning surrogate model or as an analytic model discovered through a regression algorithm. The latter approach, while allowing for more immediately understandable models, requires some prior knowledge of the closure being modelled, at the very least requiring a library of terms expected to be found in the relation. Finding a ’black box’ model for the closure, conversely, does not require any prior knowledge and can be found from only kinetic simulation data, allowing for more expressive models, but is difficult to interpret or gain much physical understanding from.

Data-driven methods allow us to capture non-local and non-linear effects in closure models that are challenging to describe using traditional analytic closures. For data-driven closures to progress into useable models for simulation and forecasting, a clear view of the different methods and their strengths and weaknesses must be considered. We must evaluate these methods in the context of the challenges presented by the development of closure relations capable of capturing kinetic effects. Problems such as cross-scale coupling, high-dimensional phase spaces and data availability will pose significant challenges.

•

Neural Networks:

Advantages:

–

Standard neural networks remain a practical starting point for closure modelling due to their flexibility and strong capacity for function approximation.

–

Neural networks are able to learn non-local and non-linear functions, which analytic closures struggle to capture.

–

Forward propagation of the network is typically very fast and efficient once training is complete.

Disadvantages:

–

The ‘black box’ nature of the models limits any physical interpretation, a key consideration when predictive modelling must be consistent with known plasma theory.

–

Standard neural networks have a tendency to overfit and their lack of generalization beyond the training distribution limit their reliability in untested regimes.

•

PINNs:

Advantages:

–

Addresses the shortcoming of standard neural networks in dealing with sparse data regimes and extrapolating beyond training sets by constraining the model with conservation laws and symmetries of the system.
(
36
)

Disadvantages:

–

Training PINNs requires computation of higher-order derivatives at each epoch when performing the automatic differentiation for the backpropagation algorithm. This can dramatically increase the computational cost of training PINNs.

–

PINNs frequently fail to capture highly varying or oscillatory structure, such as those associated with the wave-particle interactions found in plasma. This is due to requiring a resolution at which to compute the physics loss terms.

•

Neural Operators:

Advantages:

–

Neural operators take a different approach to neural networks by learning the mapping between functions spaces. This allows a neural operator, in theory, to capture the underlying physics over a range of initial and boundary conditions.
(
18
)

–

Neural operators could allow for a closure model capable of generalising over a range of plasma states and geometries.

Disadvantages:

–

Neural operators typically have a higher data requirement and can be much slower to converge during training, as opposed to standard neural networks.

–

While neural operators reduce dependence on the exact discretization, accuracy at finer resolutions than those used in training is not guaranteed, placing practical limits on their ability to extrapolate beyond training data.

•

Equation Discovery:

Advantages:

–

The symbolic closure relations that result from symbolic regression allow a transparency that enables direct physical insight, making sparse and symbolic regression valuable for hypothesis generation or model reduction.

Disadvantages:

–

The main drawback is computational scalability. As the number of possible terms in the candidate library grows rapidly with the dimensionality and non-linearity of the system, sparse regression becomes computationally expensive and may require prior knowledge to restrict the search space meaningfully.

Overall, these methods can be viewed as complementary rather than competing strategies. Neural networks and neural operators excel in expressivity and predictive performance, making them well suited for data-rich, strongly non-linear regimes. Physics-informed networks strike a balance between data-driven flexibility and physical consistency, while sparse regression provides transparent models that can be related to the underlying physics of the system. For collisionless plasma modelling, an optimal closure strategy may involve hybrid approaches that blend these methodologies, for instance, combining operator learning with symbolic regression or embedding physical constraints within neural operator frameworks—thereby capturing the interpretability of analytic closures while retaining the descriptive power of machine learning models.

4.3
Ongoing Challenges

The goal of improving the plasma closure model is to attain a data-driven closure model that can be integrated into fluid simulations to include small-scale kinetic physics in large-scale plasma fluid simulations, leading to improved predictions and forecasting ability.

The challenges currently faced in learning closure models from kinetic data are, in essence, the challenges of most machine learning applications. In addition to the common difficulties of machine learning approaches, there are the challenges presented by the requirement for our data-driven closure models to be integrated into large-scale plasma models. Our learned model must be numerically stable within the large-scale plasma fluid and be able to predict forward in time without minimal growth of the numerical error. The issue of including multiple different initial conditions must also be addressed by future research; for the learned model to be useful when implemented into the large-scale fluid model, our learned model must be able to adapt and maintain accuracy for the different phenomena and conditions that would be present in a large-scale 3-dimensional plasma fluid.

4.3.1
Accurate models

The investigations conducted to date have been able to report high levels of accuracy. Previous work has been chiefly concerned with the development of proof-of-concept models that have consistently achieved higher accuracy than the baseline and analytic closure models they were compared to. However, previous studies rarely account for any higher-dimensional cases, where, for the few investigations that did attempt higher-dimensional cases (such as
22
and
7
), difficulties in modelling the off-diagonal components of the pressure and heat flux tensors were found. Future work, therefore, must be concerned with the improvement of modelling accuracy for these off-diagonal components, for higher-dimensional cases, as well as more complex dynamics, with the majority of research thus far being concerned with simple 1D linear Landau damping.

4.3.2
Generalisable models

Some investigation has been conducted into the ability of physics-informed neural networks to allow for extrapolation beyond the training data used. Work from
35
was able to show that a PINN trained on only simulation data sampled from early times was able to accurately predict Landau damping at late times. There are, however, still difficulties in achieving this kind of extrapolation for more complex dynamics, especially for high-frequency behaviour that the PINN would struggle to model. The example from
35
was also only for a 1-dimensional problem; there has been no investigation into the accuracy of models beyond their training data for higher-dimensional problems. Going forward, then, it will be important to keep exploring the ability of physics-informed machine learning to supplement training data to allow for the models to accurately predict behaviour outside of the region covered by the training data, especially for more complex phenomena.

4.3.3
Inclusion of multiple different phenomena/conditions

For the implementation of data-driven models into large-scale simulations, a model will need to be able to respond to varying conditions at its boundaries, and different plasma regimes. The majority of research has been utilising neural networks that require re-training for the inclusion of different initial/boundary conditions. Early work on applying neural operators, which could potentially include different conditions, has been undertaken by
49
. Future work should be undertaken to investigate whether neural operators can achieve good accuracy across different initial/boundary conditions for more complex phenomena found in higher-dimensional cases; this is only one option, however, and investigation into other alternatives (such as multiple different closures deployed accordingly) will be required for comparison.

4.3.4
Implementation into large-scale plasma fluid simulations

The work from
46
was one of the first to implement the data-driven closure model into a fluid simulation; it was found that the numerical error did not grow significantly, and the data-driven model did perform better at late times than the traditional numerical model. Further, also was the work from
12
which integrated an FNO closure model of Landau damping into a fluid solver, showing the data-driven closure to outperform the Hammett-Perkins closure for both linear and non-linear Landau damping. Both investigations were restricted to a 1-dimensional Landau damping example. Future work will need to investigate the ability for these data-driven models to be integrated into plasma fluid simulations in higher dimensions and larger-scale systems to discover how numerically stable they can remain, and to give an estimate of the time frames over which these models could be implemented while retaining the correct kinetic physics.

4.3.5
Kinetic simulation data for training

The different studies considered thus far have generated their kinetic simulation data, whether from a PIC code, Vlasov solver, or other. Generating new simulation data, while useful for obtaining simulation data for the specific case being investigated, does require large computational resources and for future research into 2 and 3-dimensional cases, as well as for the possible inclusion of multiple different phenomena/conditions, this will represent a steep computational cost. One possibility to combat the computational cost would be to aggregate kinetic simulation data from multiple sources, for example, prior simulation data from different research groups, as opposed to generating all the data individually, to leverage the community to get a larger volume of training data, therefore improving future models. It will also be important in future research to include observational data from satellite missions in the training of the closure models. However, this will present many challenges; the exact phenomenon will not be as clearly defined as is possible in simulation.

Appendix A
Machine Learning Glossary

Provided here is a brief glossary of machine learning terms used throughout this review paper for plasma physicists who may be unfamiliar with the field.

A.1
General Concepts

•

Machine Learning (ML):
A subset of artificial intelligence (AI) focused on building models that learn from data to make predictions or decisions without being explicitly programmed.

•

Data-Driven Approach:
Using data (often from simulations or observations) to inform or construct models, rather than relying solely on theoretical or analytic formulations.

•

Model:
A mathematical or computational representation trained to map inputs to outputs, used for prediction or analysis.

•

Dataset:
A structured collection of data used for training, validating, or testing ML models.

•

Deep Learning:
The process of training neural network models with more than one hidden layer. It can also refer to the general field of neural networks with multiple hidden layers.

A.2
Types of ML

•

Supervised Learning:
Training ML models on data with paired sets of inputs (features) and outputs (labels), typically used for regression and classification tasks.

•

Unsupervised Learning:
Models are trained on unlabelled data, aiming to find underlying patterns (e.g., clustering, dimensionality reduction).

•

Semi-Supervised Learning:
Combines labelled and unlabelled data for training, often used when labelled data is scarce.

•

Reinforcement Learning:
A type of ML where an agent learns to make decisions by receiving rewards or penalties for its actions.

A.3
Neural Network Concepts and Architectures

•

Neural Network:
A computational model inspired by the human brain, composed of layers of interconnected nodes (neurons) capable of learning complex patterns.

•

Input Layer:
The first layer of a neural network, which receives the feature vector.

•

Hidden Layer:
The layers between the input and output layers of a neural network.

•

Output Layer:
The final layer of a neural network, producing the model’s predictions.

•

Multi-Layer Perceptron (MLP):
A type of feedforward neural network with multiple layers of neurons, typically used for regression or classification.

•

Convolutional Neural Network (CNN):
A neural network architecture particularly suited for processing data with grid-like topology (e.g., images, multidimensional simulation data).

•

Physics-Informed Neural Network (PINN):
A neural network that incorporates physical laws (e.g., differential equations) into its training process, often by including them in the loss function.
36

•

Gradient-Enhanced PINN (gPINN):
A variation of PINN that includes gradients of the physics loss terms as additional constraints during training.
(
51
)

•

Neural Operator:
A neural network designed to approximate mappings between function spaces (operators), rather than just functions. Useful for learning solutions to partial differential equations across varying conditions.
(
18
)

•

Fourier Neural Operator (FNO):
A neural operator that leverages Fourier transforms to learn mappings in problems with periodicity or spatial structure efficiently.
26

•

Deep Operator Network (DeepONet):
A neural operator architecture designed to learn nonlinear operators for differential equations.
(
30
)

•

Physics-Informed Neural Operator (PINO):
Combines neural operators with physics-informed constraints for learning PDE solutions.
(
27
)

A.4
ML Techniques

•

Regression:
Predicting a continuous output variable from input data. In the document, this often refers to learning closure relations.

•

Classification:
Assigning input data to discrete categories or classes.

•

Sparse Regression:
A regression technique that selects a minimal set of terms from a large library of candidate functions to best fit the data, promoting interpretability and parsimony.

•

Symbolic Regression:
The process of finding mathematical expressions that best fit a dataset, often via evolutionary algorithms or sparse regression.

•

Pareto Optimisation:
A principle where models are selected based on a trade-off between complexity and accuracy, seeking the simplest model that achieves acceptable error.

•

Equation Discovery:
Using machine learning (often sparse regression or PINNs) to infer governing equations from data.

•

Surrogate Model:
A simplified model (often ML-based) that approximates a more complex or computationally expensive model, used to speed up simulations or enable real-time predictions.

A.5
Training and Evaluation

•

Training Data:
The portion of a dataset used to fit model parameters.

•

Validation Data:
Used during training to tune model hyperparameters and prevent overfitting.

•

Test Data:
Data not seen during training, used to evaluate model performance and generalisation.

•

Label:
The target output variable in supervised learning.

•

Feature:
An individual measurable property or characteristic used as input to a model.

•

Feature Scaling:
Adjusting the range of input variables to improve model training and convergence.

•

Loss Function:
A mathematical function quantifying the difference between predicted and true values, used to guide model training.

•

Model Parameter:
The numerical values that make up the computations of the model. These are iteratively altered during training to fit the model to the training data. For neural networks, the parameters are the respective weights and biases of each neuron.

•

Early Stopping:
Stopping training when performance on the validation set stops improving to avoid overfitting.

•

Batch Size:
The number of training examples used in one iteration of model parameter updates.

•

Generalisation:
The ability of a model to perform well on data unseen during training, not just the training set.

•

Overfitting:
When a model is too complex and learns the training data too well, including any present noise, and performs poorly on new data, leading to poor generalisability.

•

Underfitting:
When a model is too simple to capture the underlying patterns in the data, resulting in poor performance on both the training and test data.
