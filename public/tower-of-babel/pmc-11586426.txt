Trends and annual percentage changes in tuberculosis mortality estimated by Joinpoint regression and age-period-cohort analyses, Taiwan, 1978–2022
Authors: Shu-Yuan Chen
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractTuberculosis (TB) was a public health problem in the past and remained a threat worldwide, including in Taiwan. We aimed to estimate annual percentage changes (APC) in TB mortality and the age-period-cohort effects on TB mortality. Taiwan TB mortality data from 1978 to 2022 was used to estimate the APC through the Joinpoint regression analysis and age-period-cohort models. Crude and age-standardized mortality rates (ASMR) reduced from 23.46 to 3.02 and 41.70 to 1.59 per 100,000 population in males, while 8.84 to 1.09 and 14.58 to 0.45, respectively, in females. During the COVID-19 pandemic, the crude rates still significantly declined in males from 2020 to 2022 but not in females. APC in ASMR significantly decreased in males and females (-7.17% and -7.64%), while after adjusted age-period-cohort effects, the estimated APC were -8.83% and -9.77%. Age-period-cohort effects were observed, with the highest peaks in the youngest (aged 0–4) and the period 1978–1982 in both sexes and peaks in the male cohort 1898–1902 and female cohort 1903– 1907. TB mortality in Taiwan has declined in all ages, sexes, periods, and cohorts. However, the COVID-19 pandemic stopped TB mortality from decreasing.Keywords: Annual percentage changes, Crude mortality rates, Age-standardized mortality rates, Tuberculosis, Joinpoint regression, Age-period-cohort model, TaiwanSubject terms: Tuberculosis, Epidemiology

Introduction

Tuberculosis (TB), an infectious disease caused by the bacterium Mycobacterium tuberculosis, has devastated the world for centuries; with the efforts in advancing public health infrastructure, diagnostics, vaccines, and treatments over the past century, making TB both preventable and curable and reducing the incidence and mortality, but the COVID-19 pandemic, war, drug-resistant, and global aging retarded the downward trend of TB and let it remain a significant global public health concern, especially in low-income regions1,2. Before the coronavirus (COVID-19) pandemic, TB was the top cause of death from a single infectious agent, even with a global decline between 2005 and 2019. During the COVID-19 pandemic, TB deaths increased globally. TB claimed an estimated 1.30 million lives in 2022, which declined from the estimates in 2020 and 20213. TB was a significant burden in Taiwan in the last century. TB ranked the eighth cause of death with 2,809 deaths (16.55 per 100,000 death rate) and 3.56% of proportionate mortality in 1978; the twenty-fourth cause of death with 477 deaths (2.05 per 100,000 death rate) and 0.23% of proportionate mortality in 20224. When the global downward trends in TB incidence and mortality in the twenty-first century were interrupted and rebounded during the COVID-19 pandemic in contrast to the global rebound, continuing decreasing trends in TB incidence and mortality 2010–2021 were observed in Taiwan1,2,5. However, when the COVID-19 pandemic slammed Taiwan overwhelmingly and ranked the top third cause of death in 2022, Taiwan TB mortality rebounded in both sexes in line with the global rebound in TB at the beginning of the pandemic worldwide3,4. In 2022, 399 of the 477 deaths (83.65%) were aged 65 years or older. In May 2024, 18.70% of the Taiwanese population were 65 or older6. Taiwan was projected to become a super-aged society, defined as having more than 21% of the population aged 65 or older by 2026. Therefore, TB remains a health burden in the aged society of Taiwan.

Taiwan has a comprehensive system for collecting vital statistics and yielding complete and accurate mortality data. By law, a doctor’s confirmed death certificate must be registered within 30 days in Taiwan. These vital statistics data were released by the government annually. Through the solid health infrastructure in Taiwan, the death and household registration system worked as usual during the COVID-19 pandemic, enabling this study to compare the crude TB mortality before and during the COVID-19 pandemic. In former studies, statistical methods such as the Joinpoint regression analysis and age-period-cohort model were applied to TB’s notification, incidence, and mortality data7–17. To analyze trends in TB mortality in Taiwan before and during the COVID-19 pandemic, we utilized statistical methods, including Joinpoint regression and age-period-cohort (A-P–C) models. Joinpoint regression identifies significant changes (‘joinpoints’) in trends over time and calculates the annual percentage change (APC), providing insights into the direction and magnitude of these trends. APC refers to the percentage change in a rate over a year. Meanwhile, A-P–C models enable us to disentangle the influences of age, period, and birth cohort on mortality rates, thereby enhancing our understanding of the underlying dynamics. It is the first exploration to use two analysis methods simultaneously applied to Taiwan’s TB mortality data from 1978 to 2022 and describe the mortality difference before and during the COVID-19 pandemic.

Methods

Data

Health Statistics issued by the Ministry of Health and Welfare in Taiwan provided the annual 5-year-age-grouped age-sex-specific TB deaths (ICD8 010–019, 1978–1980; ICD9 010–018, 1981–2007; ICD10 A15-A19, 2008–2022)4. The annual population data was from the Demographic Statistics reported by the Department of Household Registration, Ministry of the Interior, Taiwan6. To illustrate the trends of sex-specific TB mortality from 1978 to 2022 in Taiwan, the age-standardized mortality rates (ASMR) were calculated using the WHO World Standard Population 2000.

The crude and age-standardized TB mortality rates were calculated year-wise from 1978–2022. All the calculations were done on Excel spreadsheets (version 2019; Microsoft Corporation, Redmond, WA, USA). By sex, the TB crude mortality from 2020–2022 (during COVID-19) was compared with mortality in 2019 (before COVID-19) respectively.

The data for Joinpoint regression analyses were yearly crude and age-standardized TB mortality rates. The rate calculation function in Joinpoint regression software was used in this study. When doing data management, annual sex-specific TB deaths, populations, and rates were available in Excel spreadsheets.

The data for A-P–C analysis were prepared as follows. We calculate age-sex-specific mortality rates of TB according to 9 calendar periods of 5 years each (1978–1982, 1983–1987, 1988–1992, and so forth until 2018–2022), 17 age groups (0–4, 5–9, 10–14, and so forth until 80–84). In the A-P–C analysis, the age group of 85 or older was excluded due to the 5-year interval requirement. Therefore, the population was classified into 25 birth cohorts (1898–1902, 1903–1907, 1908–1912, 1913–1917, and so forth until 2018–2022). By sex, the age-group-specific TB mortality rates were calculated for each period with a 5-year interval. The age-group-specific rate was an average annual rate within a 5-year interval period. All the calculations were performed using Excel.

Ethical approval was not required using aggregate data in government publications.

Statistical analysis

Poisson regression was used to estimate rate ratios when comparing TB mortality rates before and during COVID-19 in the study. Poisson regression, a log-linear model, was commonly used for analyzing counts or mortality rates. The Poisson distribution modeled the variance as a function of the mean, the mean equal to the variance. The dispersion was checked in models. In this study, there was no overdispersion in data for modeling. The analyses were executed using SAS software (version 9.4; SAS Institute, Cary, NC, USA). The 95% CI and Wald χ2 test were used to test significance with two-sided tests and set 0.05 as the significance level.

We applied the Joinpoint regression model to sex-specific crude and age-standardized TB mortality rates to estimate APC18. The maximum number of Joinpoints in the regression model was seven. Models were selected using the Weighted Bayesian Information Criterion (WBIC), and the Empirical Quantile Method was used to calculate the CIs for the APC and Average APC (AAPC). This study’s upgraded software for the Joinpoint regression analysis was the Joinpoint Regression Program, Version 5.0.2–May 2023; Statistical Methodology and Applications Branch, Surveillance Research Program, NCI.

Regarding A-P–C analysis, we fit log-linear models of sex-specific TB mortality rates to assess APC for all ages in the model and each age group and broke up A-P–C effects on mortality. The model results showed the overall trend (net drift as an APC analog of the estimated APC of the age-standardized rates) and the trend by age group (local drift as an estimated APC over time specific to each age group). The longitudinal age curve illustrated TB mortality rates in successive 5-year age groups from 0 −4 to 80–84 for the reference cohort 1958–1962. Period rate ratios (RRs) were estimated by the rate in each period compared to that in reference period 1998–2002. The cohort RRs were the rates in 25 consecutive birth cohorts compared to that in the reference birth cohort 1958–1962. The Wald χ2test was used to examine the significance of estimated parameters with two-sided tests and the α level set at 0.05. The estimates were obtained through the Web Tool for the A-P–C Analysis19.

Results

Long-term trends of TB mortality in Taiwan

In the study period 1978–2022, 47,432 and 13,982 TB deaths were reported, and 492,517,028 and 474,526,542 person-years were observed in males and females, respectively. The long-term trends of sex-specific crude mortality rates and the age-standardized mortality rates (ASMR) for TB declined in Taiwan from 1978 to 2022(Fig. 1). The ASMR for TB was reduced from 41.70 per 100,000 in 1978 to 1.59 per 100,000 in 2022 among males, while 14.58 per 100,000 to 0.45 per 100,000 among females. The downward trends of ASMR in both sexes were steep during 1978–2022, with the rates decreasing 96.19% in males and 96.91% in females, respectively, and the rate ratios of ASMR in 1978 vs. ASMR in 2022 were 26.23 in males and 32.40 in females. The crude mortality rate for TB was reduced from 23.46 per 100,000 in 1978 to 3.02 per 100,000 in 2022 among males, while 8.84 per 100,000 to 1.09 per 100,000 among females. Compared to the downward trends of ASMR, the trends of crude rate in both sexes were more gentle during 1978–2022, with the rates decreasing 87.13% in males and 87.67% in females, respectively, and the rate ratios of crude rate in 1978 vs. crude rate in 2022 were 7.77 in males and 8.11 in females. During the COVID-19 pandemic, TB crude mortality rates significantly declined in males when compared with the rate in 2019. The TB crude mortality rates rebounded in 2022 in both sexes. In Fig. 1, besides the long-term trends of TB crude rate and ASMR, the rate ratios (RRs) during the COVID-19 pandemic compared with before the pandemic were illustrated, and so were the joinpoints for crude mortality and ASMR trends.

Table 1 showed sex-specific APC in TB crude mortality rate and ASMR from 1978 to 2022 in Taiwan using Joinpoint regression analysis. For crude rates, the average APC (AAPC) was −4.68% and −4.99% in males and females. In males, all the segments but segment 1987–1990 significantly decreased with p-value < 0.05 but with different APCs; in females, all segments decreased with different APCs but not significantly in segments 1987–2001 and 2007–2022. In males, the most decrease was found in 2003–2006 with −15.89% of APC, contrasting with an insignificant increase in 1987–1990 with + 1.47% of APC. In females, the most decrease was in 2001–2007 with −8.21% of APC, while the insignificant APC was −3.15% in 1987–2001. In females, the most decrease was in 1999–2020 with −9.81% of APC, contrasting with the increase of 11.44% in 2020–2022. The age-period-cohort analysis

After adjusting the A-P–C effect, the overall estimated APCs (EAPCs) and all the age-specific EAPCs in TB mortality from 1978 to 2022 were below zero. The average decrease was −8.83% per year among males and −9.77% per year among females (Fig. 2). In males, the most decline occurred at ages 25–29 with EAPC (95% CI) of −10.19% (−11. 53%, −8.82%), while in females, at ages 0–4 with EAPC (95% CI) of −11.92% (−21.36%, −1.35%). The TB mortality decreases were significantly different between males and females between the ages of 45 and 74, with a greater decrease in females. Over 65, the mortality decreases less in both sexes.

Discussion

This study illustrated a long-term downward trend of sex-specific TB mortality in Taiwan from 1978 to 2022, except during 2020–2022 in females (Fig. 1 and Table 1). According to Joinpoints analysis, the crude mortality rates had an AAPC of −4.68% in males and −4.99% in females. The overall APC was a 9% decrease in males, while 10% in females after adjusting the A-P–C effects (Fig. 2) compared to a 7% decline in males and 8% in females, with only age-adjusted (Table 1). The age-specific APC, estimated by A-P–C model, was negative and differed, with significantly more decreases in TB mortality between 45 and 74 years old in females than in males (Fig. 2). The longitudinal age-specific TB mortality rates for both sexes in the reference cohort 1958–1962 showed tri-peak curves with the highest peak at ages 0–4 (Fig. 3). The downward curves of period and cohort RRs of TB mortality were more pronounced in females than males (Fig. 4).

The difference in APC estimated by Joinpoints regression and the A-P–C model might be attributed to the number of factors adjusted and the different number of age groups included. In Joinpoint regression, the APC was estimated with no factor adjusted in crude rate and age-adjusted in ASMR. In the A-P–C model, the APC was estimated with A-P–C adjusted. When calculating ASMR uploaded for Joinpoints analysis, we used 18 age groups, the oldest being 85 + . In contrast, in the A-P–C analysis, we only uploaded 17 age-specific rates for each period excluding the oldest of 85 + due to the 5-year age interval required. The older the age, the TB mortality rate was higher, and the decrease in the mortality rate was slighter. Excluding the aged 85 + group might contribute to the bigger APC estimated by the A-P–C model.

Taiwan TB mortality rates decreased over 45 years, except in 2020–2022 among females. Retrieving from the same period in previous studies, TB mortality in this study was higher than in Japan10. The trends of age-standardized TB mortality in this study looked like those in the high-middle socio-demographic index (SDI)20. The TB mortality decreased more after 1999 and 2000 in males and females, respectively (Table 1). It was a coincidence that the Taiwan Centers for Disease Control (Taiwan CDC) was established in July 1999. More details of correspondence between TB mortality and anti-TB resources and policies in Taiwan were referred to in Supplementary Tables S1, S2, and S3. The Severe Acute Respiratory Syndrome (SARS) pandemic occurred in 2002. The SARS pandemic triggered the Communicable Disease Control Act amendment, reforming and adjusting organizational structure for anti-infectious disease, raising professional manpower, and strengthening Taiwan’s overall epidemic prevention and response system. This improvement also benefited the prevention of TB and resulted in more decreases in TB mortality. The 2009 H1N1 pandemic attacked. Co-infection with influenza and TB was related to a high risk of mortality21. The COVID-19 pandemic after 2019 slowed down the downward trend of TB mortality or even increased TB deaths worldwide3. These facts might explain the slowing TB mortality decline after 2010 in males and a slight increase in females in 2020–2022 in this study. Another reason might be that besides working, females inevitably had to shop for groceries and pick up and drop off their school children during the COVID-19 pandemic. That unavoidable daily routine increased the infection risk of COVID-19 among females in Taiwan. However, the effect of co-infection on TB mortality was different between sexes, which needs further studies. In females, TB crude mortality rate and ASMR trends increased during the COVID-19 pandemic, while in males, TB mortality decreased and then rebounded in 2022. The discrepancy might be partly explained by the co-infection of COVID-19 and TB, co-morbidity, and competing causes of death. Among the TB and COVID-19 co-infection cohort, male and co-morbidity were risk factors for COVID-19 death; the survival was worst in those whose deaths were attributed to TB and COVID-19, then followed in those to COVID-19 alone, and the highest in those to TB alone22. The co-infection TB/COVID-19 had a higher proportion of diabetes than TB patients before the pandemic23. In-hospital mortality in older patients (> = 65 years) with COVID-19 was higher in males than females24. The proportionate mortality from TB was similar between the patients diagnosed with TB alone and those with TB/COVID-1923,25. In Taiwan, males had significantly higher mortality from COVID-19 than females, with rate ratios (RR) of 3.73, 1.89, and 1.36 in 2020, 2021, and 2022, respectively; so was the TB mortality with RR of 3.57, 2.79, and 2.784. In Taiwan, there were few COVID-19 deaths in 2020, but COVID-19 ranked the 19th cause of death in 2021 and ranked the top third cause of death in 2022. In 2020, TB mortality decreased in both sexes when COVID-19 deaths were few, while in 2022, it increased in both sexes when the COVID-19 pandemic overwhelmed the healthcare system in Taiwan with a COVID-19 mortality sex ratio of 1.36, getting close to one. Therefore, co-infection and competing causes of death might partly explain the gender discrepancy in TB decline during the pandemic. During the COVID-19 pandemic, cervical and breast cancer screening utilization declined in Taiwan26. Did females have a higher COVID-19 pandemic-related disruptions to TB healthcare access and screening than males? Was there a higher proportion of deaths from COVID-19 in males than in females among co-infection TB/COVID-19 in Taiwan? It’s worth exploring these issues in depth.

This study showed that TB mortality decreased more for females than males in Taiwan, and TB mortality decreased less with age in this study (Fig. 2), and the age-specific TB rates were increased with age in males but not obvious in females in cohort 1958–1962 (Fig. 3). The sex difference might be attributed to biological differences, such as hormonal and genetic27, exposure to risk factors such as smoking and alcohol20,28, treatment compliance and effects29,30, and improved health inequality between males and females. In Taiwan, education and health policies such as the 9-year compulsory education started in 1968, the vaccination program from 1949, the national health insurance project launched in 1995, and the directly observed treatment, short course (DOTS) program did improve the health equality between males and females. When people got older, TB mortality decreased less in this study. Bacillus Calmette-Guerin (BCG) vaccination protects from TB deaths, especially those under 15 years old31. The waning immune responses to BCG, increased exposure to multiple risk factors in adulthood, and compromised immune systems in older adults might contribute to less EAPC with age32,33.

Period and cohort effects on TB mortality were significant, with downward trends (Fig. 4). Taiwan was transformed from an agricultural to an industrially affluent society, with USD1,606 and USD 32,756 of GDP per capita in 1978 and 2022, respectively34. The national nutrition status not only had improved but also surplus35. The anti-TB organization and authority changed from a single TB ward built in 1915 for treatment to the CDC for prevention and was raised from local to central government. The anti-TB programs expanded from treatment to prevention, as referred to in Supplementary Table S1. The BCG vaccination was launched in 1949, and it has had a coverage rate of about 98% in the past decade36. Free treatment for TB and the DOTS program contributed to a complete treatment. This study’s period and cohort effects might be attributed to affluent societal transition, nutritional improvement, healthcare infrastructure advancement, high BCG vaccination coverage, and free treatment.

The strength of this study is that the household and death certificate registration system ensured our complete data on population size and TB deaths. The notification system and excellent diagnosis and treatment quality enhanced TB death data accuracy. The age-adjusted and A-P–C-adjusted APCs were estimated, and A-P–C effects on TB mortality were illustrated. However, there were several limitations in the study. First, since 2019, the Ministry of Health and Welfare in Taiwan has adopted the 2016 ICD-10 cause of death selection criteria and used IRIS Institute’s automated coding system for causes of death. For data before 2019, there were conversion ratios for tuberculosis, but it was not suggested to apply the ratios to data many years ago, according to the Department of Statistics, Ministry of Health and Welfare (MHW), Taiwan4. The conversion ratios for crude and standardized TB mortality rates were 1.1005 and 1.0930 in males and 1.1102 and 1.0958 in females, respectively. The change in 2019 might explain the decreasing trends in TB mortality, which encountered a rebound in 2019 and then went downward again in 2020. The change might cause an underestimated declining trend from 2018–2020. Second, another limitation is our lack of long-term TB-related racial and immigration information in Taiwan. Therefore, analyzing the long-term trend of TB by race and immigrants was impossible. Third, we don’t have individual information about exposure and TB risk. The aggregate data from government publications were used to illustrate the trends of TB mortality in Taiwan in this study. Ecological fallacy might emerge if the associations from population-level results were inferred at the individual level. However, this study might contribute to generating hypotheses for further studies involving individual-level age-sex differences in risk factors, healthcare access, and treatment adherence.

Global aging and labor movement might hinder the WHO End TB Strategy: 2025 milestones and antagonizing the prevention of TB. Despite the constantly decreasing trends in TB mortality, Taiwan faces the burdens of elderly TB and immigrant workers with TB in line with that in Japan37. To achieve the End TB goal, we need to embrace any state-of-the-art anti-TB strategies with open arms. This study’s results might enable public health authorities to identify successful strategies against TB and adjust prevention programs in the future.
