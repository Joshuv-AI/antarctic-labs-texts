Antisymmetric Raman response
Mattia Udina 1,2 and Indranil Paul 1

Abstract
We develop the theory of antisymmetric Raman response, defined as the difference between the Raman signals of two scattering geometries related by an exchange of mutually perpendicular incoming and the outgoing photon polarizations. Such responses, finite in orthorhombic or lower symmetry systems, are related to cross-susceptibilities of two Raman operators and are characterized by the absence of intraband terms. This is in contrast to standard Raman responses which measure auto-susceptibilities where both intra- and interband processes contribute. We illustrate the theory with examples from the charge density wave rare-earth tritellurides and the excitonic insulator Ta 2 NiSe 5 . Our theory establishes antisymmetric Raman response as a unique tool to probe microscopic features such as interband energy scales and to detect reflection symmetry breaking.

Formulation.
—
We start from the standard description of nonresonant Raman response.
Ignoring geometric pre-factors, the scattering intensity is given by
Devereaux07

\displaystyle I(\Omega)=(1/Z)\sum_{I,F}e^{-\beta E_{I}}\left|M_{F,I}\right|^{2}\delta(E_{I}-E_{F}+\Omega),

where
Z
is the partition function,
\beta
is inverse temperature,

\Omega=\omega_{i}-\omega_{f}
is the Raman shift,
\omega_{i,f}
are the in and out photon frequencies with polarizations

\hat{e}_{i,f}
, and
E_{I,F}
are the energies of the initial and final states.
We assume that the low-energy effective theory describing the system has bandwidth

D\ll\bar{\omega}
, where
\bar{\omega}=(\omega_{i}+\omega_{f})/2
. In most cases of interest
D/\bar{\omega}\sim

0.1 or less. Then, the matrix element
M_{F,I}
can be approximated by
[for details see the Supplementary Material (SM)]

\displaystyle M_{F,I}\approx\sum_{\alpha,\beta}(e_{i,\alpha}e^{\ast}_{f,\beta})\langle F|\left(\hat{v}_{\alpha\beta}-\left[\hat{v}_{\alpha}\,,\,\hat{v}_{\beta}\right]/\bar{\omega}\right)|I\rangle.

(1)

Here
\hat{v}_{\alpha}\equiv\partial\mathcal{H}/\partial k_{\alpha}
and

\hat{v}_{\alpha\beta}\equiv\partial^{2}\mathcal{H}/(\partial k_{\alpha}\partial k_{\beta})

are the paramagnetic and diamagnetic currents, respectively,
\mathcal{H}
is the Hamiltonian and
{\bf k}
the
wavevector.

We consider a quasi two-dimensional system for which
I_{x_{\theta}y_{\theta}}(\Omega)
is the Raman intensity
for in and out polarizations along
\hat{x}_{\theta}
and
\hat{y}_{\theta}
, respectively, with

\hat{x}\cdot\hat{x}_{\theta}=\cos\theta
, as shown in Fig.
1
(a). The angle dependent antisymmetric Raman intensity
can be expressed as (see SM for details)

\displaystyle I_{A}(\Omega,\theta)=2\cos(2\theta)I_{A_{2g}}^{B_{2g}}(\Omega)-2\sin(2\theta)I_{A_{2g}}^{B_{1g}}(\Omega),

(2)

where

\displaystyle I_{A_{2g}}^{i}(\Omega)\equiv(1/Z)\sum_{I,F}e^{-\beta E_{I}}\delta(E_{I}-E_{F}+\Omega)

\displaystyle\times\left[\langle I|\hat{\Gamma}_{A_{2g}}|F\rangle\langle F|\hat{\Gamma}_{i}|I\rangle-\langle I|\hat{\Gamma}_{i}|F\rangle\langle F|\hat{\Gamma}_{A_{2g}}|I\rangle\right],

(3)

with
i=(B_{1g},B_{2g})
. The Raman operators
\hat{\Gamma}_{A_{2g}}\equiv\left[\hat{v}_{x},\hat{v}_{y}\right]/\bar{\omega}

Burdin20
;
Riccardi19
,

\hat{\Gamma}_{B_{1g}}\equiv(\hat{v}_{xx}-\hat{v}_{yy})/2
and
\hat{\Gamma}_{B_{2g}}\equiv\hat{v}_{xy}
.
Note,
\hat{\Gamma}_{A_{2g}}
is an antisymmetric rank two tensor and is
an antihermitian operator, while
\hat{\Gamma}_{i}
are symmetric rank two tensors and Hermitian operators.

Two comments are in order. First, the commutator
\hat{\Gamma}_{A_{2g}}
is non-zero only for
multi-orbital systems, and it cannot be constructed within the widely-used effective mass approximation
that neglects the paramagnetic current
Devereaux07
. Moreover,
antihermiticity enforces that
\hat{\Gamma}_{A_{2g}}
has only interband terms of the type

(c^{\dagger}_{{\bf k},1}c_{{\bf k},2}-c^{\dagger}_{{\bf k},2}c_{{\bf k},1})
between bands 1, 2. This ensures that
antisymmetric responses have no intraband contribution.
Second, since
A_{2g}\otimes B_{2g}=B_{1g}
, finite
I_{A_{2g}}^{B_{2g}}(\Omega)

implies broken reflection symmetries

M_{x^{\prime}}:(x,y)\rightarrow(y,x)
and
M_{y^{\prime}}:(x,y)\rightarrow(-y,-x)
,
where
(x^{\prime},y^{\prime})
are

\pi/4
rotated counterclockwise with respect to
(x,y)
. Likewise, finite

I_{A_{2g}}^{B_{1g}}(\Omega)
implies broken reflections

M_{x}:(x,y)\rightarrow(x,-y)
and
M_{y}:(x,y)\rightarrow(-x,y)
. Thus,
even though we use tetragonal notation,
antisymmetric Raman response is finite only for orthorhombic or lower symmetry systems.

Fluctuation dissipation relation.
—
The next step is to identify the susceptibilities that can be extracted from
I_{A}(\Omega,\theta)
. For this we define the
imaginary time ordered function

\chi_{A_{2g}}^{i}(\tau)\equiv-\langle T_{\tau}\hat{\Gamma}_{A_{2g}}(\tau)\hat{\Gamma}_{i}(0)\rangle,

over the interval
\tau=[-\beta,\beta]
, and its Fourier transform by

\chi_{A_{2g}}^{i}(\tau)=(1/\beta)\sum_{\Omega_{n}}\chi_{A_{2g}}^{i}(i\Omega_{n})e^{-i\Omega_{n}\tau}

in terms of bosonic Matsubara frequencies
\Omega_{n}
. Here
\langle\cdots\rangle
implies thermodynamic average.
We also define the related cross-susceptibility

\phi_{A_{2g}}^{i}(i\Omega_{n})\equiv\chi_{A_{2g}}^{i}(i\Omega_{n})-\chi_{A_{2g}}^{i}(-i\Omega_{n})
.
Note, by definition,
\chi_{A_{2g}}^{i}(-i\Omega_{n})=\chi_{i}^{A_{2g}}(i\Omega_{n})
.
Using Lehmann representation
we get (see SM for details)

\displaystyle\phi_{A_{2g}}^{i}(i\Omega_{n})

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{Z}\sum_{n,m}\left[(\hat{\Gamma}_{A_{2g}})_{nm}(\hat{\Gamma}_{i})_{mn}-(\hat{\Gamma}_{i})_{nm}(\hat{\Gamma}_{A_{2g}})_{mn}\right]

\displaystyle\times(e^{-\beta E_{n}}-e^{\beta E_{m}})/(i\Omega_{n}+E_{n}-E_{m}),

(4)

where
(\hat{O})_{nm}\equiv\langle n|\hat{O}|m\rangle
. Comparing Eqs. (
Antisymmetric Raman response
) and (
Antisymmetric Raman response
)
we obtain the fluctuation-dissipation relation

\displaystyle I_{A_{2g}}^{i}(\Omega)=-(1/\pi)[1+n_{B}(\Omega)]{\rm Im}\phi_{A_{2g}}^{i}(\Omega+i\eta),

(5)

where
n_{B}(\Omega)\equiv 1/(e^{\beta\Omega}-1)
is the Bose function.
By contrast, once symmetry resolved, standard Raman response gives the auto-susceptibility

{\rm Im}\chi_{j}^{j}(\Omega+i\eta)
, where

\chi_{j}^{j}(\tau)\equiv-\langle T_{\tau}\hat{\Gamma}_{j}(\tau)\hat{\Gamma}_{j}(0)\rangle,

and
j=(A_{1g},B_{1g},B_{2g},A_{2g})
. Consequently, it cannot detect reflection symmetry breaking.

General properties.
—
We now compare the properties of
\phi_{A_{2g}}^{i}(i\Omega_{n})
extracted from antisymmetric Raman response
with those of
\chi_{j}^{j}(i\Omega_{n})
.

(i) Eq. (
Antisymmetric Raman response
) implies
\phi_{A_{2g}}^{i}(\Omega+i\eta)^{\ast}=\phi_{A_{2g}}^{i}(\Omega-i\eta)
,
using the antihermiticity of
\hat{\Gamma}_{A_{2g}}
. Moreover,
\phi_{A_{2g}}^{i}(i\Omega_{n})
is odd in
\Omega_{n}
.
Thus, the real and the imaginary parts of
\phi_{A_{2g}}^{i}(\Omega+i\eta)
are
odd and even functions of
\Omega
, respectively. It is opposite in case of
\chi_{j}^{j}(\Omega+i\eta)
, as shown in
Fig.
1
(c, d).

(ii) From the detailed balance relation
Loudon78

I_{y_{\theta}x_{\theta}}(-\Omega)=e^{-\beta\Omega}I_{x_{\theta}y_{\theta}}(\Omega)
(see SM for details),
we get
I_{A}(\Omega\rightarrow 0,\theta)\rightarrow 0
. Since
n_{B}(\Omega)\propto 1/\Omega
in this limit,
we have

\lim_{\Omega\rightarrow 0}{\rm Im}\phi_{A_{2g}}^{i}(\Omega+i\eta)\sim|\Omega|^{\gamma}
with
\gamma>1
.
As discussed later, in practice
{\rm Im}\phi_{A_{2g}}^{i}(\Omega+i\eta)
from electronic excitations
[one loop graph in Fig.
1
(b)] is zero below a threshold, as shown in Fig.
1
(c). Under suitable
conditions a collective mode [two loop graph in Fig.
1
(b)] can appear inside this spectroscopic “gap”.

(iii) From Eq. (
Antisymmetric Raman response
) we obtain

\displaystyle\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\frac{d\Omega}{2\pi}\phi_{A_{2g}}^{i}(\Omega+i\eta)=\langle[\hat{\Gamma}_{i}\,,\,\hat{\Gamma}_{A_{2g}}]\rangle.

(6)

Since the right hand side is zero for a tetragonal system, it acts
as an order parameter for reflection symmetry breaking which can be extracted from spectroscopy.
By contrast, in standard Raman the frequency integral of

{\rm Im}\chi_{j}^{j}(\Omega+i\eta)/\Omega
is related to a static susceptibility by Kramers-Kronig
relation
Gallais16
;
Gallais13
.

(iv) Reciprocity relation: We define the correlation function

C_{A_{2g}}^{i}(t)\equiv\langle\hat{\Gamma}_{A_{2g}}(t)\hat{\Gamma}_{i}(0)\rangle
and

C_{A_{2g}}^{i}(\Omega)\equiv\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}dt\exp(i\Omega t)C_{A_{2g}}^{i}(t)
, and likewise

C_{i}^{A_{2g}}(t)
and
C_{i}^{A_{2g}}(\Omega)
.
The anitsymmetric Raman intensities are given by

I_{A_{2g}}^{i}(\Omega)=C_{A_{2g}}^{i}(\Omega)-C_{i}^{A_{2g}}(\Omega)
.
We consider a time reversal symmetric system in an external magnetic field
{\bf h}
.
As the Raman operators are even under time reversal, and
\hat{\Gamma}_{A_{2g}}
is antihermitian,
one can show that

C_{A_{2g}}^{i}(t,h)=-C_{i}^{A_{2g}}(t,-h)
. This gives the
reciprocity relation
(see SM for details)

I_{A_{2g}}^{i}(\Omega,h)=I_{A_{2g}}^{i}(\Omega,-h).

(7)

A violation of the above was reported recently for the CDW phase of GdTe
3

Wulferding24
.

Raman cross-susceptibilities
B_{1g}\otimes B_{2g}
or
E_{g}^{1}\otimes E_{g}^{2}

are obtained by subtracting right-left
circular polarizations signal from the left-right one in reference tetragonal or hexagonal systems,
respectively
Liu22
;
Perfetti24
.
However, they do not involve the
A_{2g}
-operator, and hence they do not have the features discussed in this work.

cross-susceptibilities of two Raman operators, of which one is
\hat{\Gamma}_{A_{2g}}

that filters out intraband excitations. The spectra have “gaps” below a threshold which
measures minimum interband excitation energy in metals and the charge gap in insulators. It can detect
subtle mirror symmetry breaking such as CDW transitions in tritellurides and excitonic insulator
transition in Ta
2
NiSe
5
. In symmetry broken phases a
collective mode can appear inside the gap which has
microscopic information such as the orbital character of the order parameter.
Thus, antisymmetric Raman response
is a powerful tool to probe low symmetry materials, both metallic and insulating.

Supplementary Information

.1
Derivation of Eq. (1)

In general, the Raman matrix element is given by

\displaystyle M_{F,I}

\displaystyle=\sum_{\alpha,\beta}(e_{i,\alpha}e^{\ast}_{f,\beta})\left[\langle F|\hat{v}_{\alpha\beta}|I\rangle+\sum_{M}\frac{\langle F|\hat{v}_{\alpha}|M\rangle\langle M|\hat{v}_{\beta}|I\rangle}{E_{I}-E_{M}-\omega_{f}}\right.

\displaystyle+\left.\frac{\langle F|\hat{v}_{\beta}|M\rangle\langle M|\hat{v}_{\alpha}|I\rangle}{E_{I}-E_{M}+\omega_{i}}\right],

(S1)

where
\Omega=\omega_{i}-\omega_{f}
is the Raman shift,
\omega_{i,f}
are the in and out photon frequencies with polarizations

\hat{e}_{i,f}
.

We assume that the bandwidth of the effective theory
D\ll\omega_{i},\omega_{f}
. Since
(E_{I}-E_{M})<D
, we can neglect it in
the denominators, and the intermediate states
M
can be summed to unity. Writing

\omega_{i,f}=\bar{\omega}\pm\Omega/2
, and neglecting
\Omega/\bar{\omega}
we get Eq. (1) of the main text.

.2
Derivation of Eqs. (2) and (3)

We consider the scattering geometry where the in-coming photon polarization is

\hat{e}_{i}=\hat{x}_{\theta}=\cos(\theta)\hat{x}+\sin(\theta)\hat{y}
, and the out-going photon polarization is

\hat{e}_{f}=\hat{y}_{\theta}=-\sin(\theta)\hat{x}+\cos(\theta)\hat{y}
. The associated Raman matrix element
can be written

M_{F,I}(\theta)=\langle F|\hat{D}(\theta)+\hat{P}(\theta)|I\rangle,

(S2)

where the diamagnetic term is

\hat{D}(\theta)=\cos(2\theta)\hat{\Gamma}_{B_{2g}}-\sin(2\theta)\hat{\Gamma}_{B_{1g}},

and the paramagnetic term is

\hat{P}(\theta)=-\hat{\Gamma}_{A_{2g}},

with
\hat{\Gamma}_{A_{2g}}\equiv\left[\hat{v}_{x},\hat{v}_{y}\right]/\bar{\omega}
,

\hat{\Gamma}_{B_{1g}}\equiv(\hat{v}_{xx}-\hat{v}_{yy})/2
and
\hat{\Gamma}_{B_{2g}}\equiv\hat{v}_{xy}
.
We need to compare this scattering geometry with the one obtained by interchanging the
in and out polarization directions. Denoting the Raman matrix element of the latter by

M_{F,I}(\theta_{\perp})
, we get

M_{F,I}(\theta_{\perp})=\langle F|\hat{D}(\theta)-\hat{P}(\theta)|I\rangle.

(S3)

Thus, the antisymmetric Raman response is

\displaystyle I_{A}(\Omega,\theta)=

\displaystyle I_{x_{\theta}y_{\theta}}(\Omega)-I_{y_{\theta}y_{\theta}}(\Omega)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{Z}\sum_{I,F}e^{-\beta E_{I}}\delta(E_{I}-E_{F}+\Omega)

\displaystyle\times

\displaystyle\left[|M_{F,I}(\theta)|^{2}-|M_{F,I}(\theta_{\perp})|^{2}\right].

(S4)

Noting that
\hat{P}(\theta)
is antihermitian, from the above we get Eqs. (2) and (3)
of the main text.

.3
Derivation of Eq. (4)

The imaginary time ordered function

\chi_{A_{2g}}^{i}(\tau)\equiv-\langle T_{\tau}\hat{\Gamma}_{A_{2g}}(\tau)\hat{\Gamma}_{i}(0)\rangle,

can be expressed in the Lehmann basis as

\displaystyle\chi_{A_{2g}}^{i}(\tau)

\displaystyle=-\theta(\tau)\frac{1}{Z}\sum_{n,m}e^{-\beta E_{n}}(\hat{\Gamma}_{A_{2g}})_{nm}(\hat{\Gamma}_{i})_{mn}e^{\tau(E_{n}-E_{m})}

\displaystyle-\theta(-\tau)\frac{1}{Z}\sum_{n,m}e^{-\beta E_{n}}(\hat{\Gamma}_{i})_{nm}(\hat{\Gamma}_{A_{2g}})_{mn}e^{\tau(E_{m}-E_{n})},

(S5)

where
\tau
is defined over the interval
[-\beta,\beta]
. From the above we deduce that

\chi_{A_{2g}}^{i}(\tau>0)=\chi_{A_{2g}}^{i}(\tau-\beta<0)
and, in particular,
\chi_{A_{2g}}^{i}(\beta)=\chi_{A_{2g}}^{i}(0)
.
The periodic boundary condition implies that
\chi_{A_{2g}}^{i}(\tau)
can be expanded in bosonic Matsubara frequency.
We get

\displaystyle\chi_{A_{2g}}^{i}(i\Omega_{n})

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{\beta}d\tau e^{i\Omega_{n}\tau}\chi_{A_{2g}}^{i}(\tau)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{Z}\sum_{n,m}(\hat{\Gamma}_{A_{2g}})_{nm}(\hat{\Gamma}_{i})_{mn}\,\frac{e^{-\beta E_{n}}-e^{-\beta E_{m}}}{i\Omega_{n}+E_{n}-E_{m}}.

(S6)

This implies that

\displaystyle\phi_{A_{2g}}^{i}(i\Omega_{n})

\displaystyle=\chi_{A_{2g}}^{i}(i\Omega_{n})-\chi_{A_{2g}}^{i}(-i\Omega_{n})

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{Z}\sum_{n,m}\left[(\hat{\Gamma}_{A_{2g}})_{nm}(\hat{\Gamma}_{i})_{mn}-(\hat{\Gamma}_{i})_{nm}(\hat{\Gamma}_{A_{2g}})_{mn}\right]

\displaystyle\times\frac{e^{-\beta E_{n}}-e^{\beta E_{m}}}{i\Omega_{n}+E_{n}-E_{m}},

(S7)

which is Eq. (4) of the main text.

.4
Comparing Stokes and anti-Stokes processes

We consider a Raman scattering process where the in-photon has frequency and polarization

\omega_{1}
and
\hat{\alpha}
, respectively, while for the out-photon they are
\omega_{2}
and
\hat{\beta}
, respectively.
We take
\Omega=\omega_{1}-\omega_{2}>0
, i.e., this is a Stokes process. The scattering intensity for this process is
given by

\displaystyle I_{\alpha\beta}(\omega_{1},\omega_{2})=\frac{1}{Z}\sum_{i,f,m}e^{-\beta E_{i}}\delta(E_{i}-E_{f}+\Omega)\left|M_{fi}^{\alpha\beta}(\omega_{1},\omega_{2})\right|^{2},

where

\displaystyle M_{fi}^{\alpha\beta}(\omega_{1},\omega_{2})=(\hat{v}_{\alpha\beta})_{fi}+\frac{(\hat{v}_{\alpha})_{fm}(\hat{v}_{\beta})_{mi}}{E_{im}-\omega_{2}}+\frac{(\hat{v}_{\beta})_{fm}(\hat{v}_{\alpha})_{mi}}{E_{im}+\omega_{1}},

(S8)

with
E_{im}\equiv E_{i}-E_{m}
. This intensity is to be compared with an anti-Stokes process
where the in-photon has frequency and polarization
\omega_{2}
and
\hat{\beta}
, respectively, while
those for the out-photon are
\omega_{1}
and
\hat{\alpha}
, respectively. The intensity of this process
is

\displaystyle I_{\beta\alpha}(\omega_{2},\omega_{1})=\frac{1}{Z}\sum_{i,f,m}e^{-\beta E_{i}}\delta(E_{i}-E_{f}-\Omega)\left|M_{fi}^{\beta\alpha}(\omega_{2},\omega_{1})\right|^{2},

where

\displaystyle M_{fi}^{\beta\alpha}(\omega_{2},\omega_{1})

\displaystyle=(\hat{v}_{\beta\alpha})_{fi}+\frac{(\hat{v}_{\beta})_{fm}(\hat{v}_{\alpha})_{mi}}{E_{im}-\omega_{1}}+\frac{(\hat{v}_{\alpha})_{fm}(\hat{v}_{\beta})_{mi}}{E_{im}+\omega_{2}}

(S9)

Using energy conservation relation
E_{i}+\omega_{2}=E_{f}+\omega_{1}
the anti-Stokes matrix element can be re-written as

\displaystyle M_{fi}^{\beta\alpha}(\omega_{2},\omega_{1})=(\hat{v}_{\beta\alpha})_{fi}+\frac{(\hat{v}_{\beta})_{fm}(\hat{v}_{\alpha})_{mi}}{E_{fm}-\omega_{2}}+\frac{(\hat{v}_{\alpha})_{fm}(\hat{v}_{\beta})_{mi}}{E_{fm}+\omega_{1}}

\displaystyle=\left[(\hat{v}_{\alpha\beta})_{if}+\frac{(\hat{v}_{\alpha})_{im}(\hat{v}_{\beta})_{mf}}{E_{fm}-\omega_{2}}+\frac{(\hat{v}_{\beta})_{im}(\hat{v}_{\alpha})_{mf}}{E_{fm}+\omega_{1}}\right]^{\ast}

\displaystyle=\left[M_{if}^{\alpha\beta}(\omega_{1},\omega_{2})\right]^{\ast}

(S10)

Using the above relation we get

\displaystyle I_{\beta\alpha}(\omega_{2},\omega_{1})

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{Z}\sum_{i,f,m}e^{-\beta E_{i}}\delta(E_{i}-E_{f}-\Omega)\left|M_{if}^{\alpha\beta}(\omega_{1},\omega_{2})\right|^{2}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{Z}\sum_{i,f,m}e^{-\beta E_{f}}\delta(E_{f}-E_{i}-\Omega)\left|M_{fi}^{\alpha\beta}(\omega_{1},\omega_{2})\right|^{2}

\displaystyle=e^{-\beta\Omega}I_{\alpha\beta}(\omega_{1},\omega_{2}).

(S11)

The above relation implies that for
\Omega\rightarrow 0
, the antisymmetric Raman intensity
I_{A}(\Omega,\theta)\rightarrow 0
.

.5
Reciprocity relation

From the definition of the correlation functions
C_{A_{2g}}^{i}(t)
,
C_{i}^{A_{2g}}(t)
and their Fourier transforms, we have

\displaystyle C_{A_{2g}}^{i}(\Omega)-

\displaystyle C_{i}^{A_{2g}}(\Omega)=\frac{1}{Z}\sum_{n,m}e^{-\beta E_{n}}\left[(\hat{\Gamma}_{A_{2g}})_{nm}(\hat{\Gamma}_{i})_{mn}\right.

\displaystyle-\left.(\hat{\Gamma}_{i})_{nm}(\hat{\Gamma}_{A_{2g}})_{mn}\right]\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}dte^{i(E_{n}-E_{m}+\Omega)t}

\displaystyle=I_{A_{2g}}^{i}(\Omega)

(S12)

Next, we establish how the correlation functions vary under time reversal operator
\hat{K}
. The antilinearity of
\hat{K}
implies
the relation

\langle n|\hat{A}|n\rangle=\langle\bar{n}|\hat{K}\hat{A}^{\dagger}\hat{K}^{-1}|\bar{n}\rangle,

(S13)

where
\hat{A}
is any operator and
|\bar{n}\rangle=\hat{K}|n\rangle
. Since a complete set of states
\{|n\rangle\}
can be
replaced by another complete set
\{|\bar{n}\rangle\}
, we have

{\rm Tr}(\hat{A})={\rm Tr}(\hat{K}\hat{A}^{\dagger}\hat{K}^{-1}).

(S14)

We consider a system which is intrinsically time reversal symmetric such that the Hamiltonian
\hat{H}
satisfies

\hat{K}\hat{H}(h)\hat{K}^{-1}=\hat{H}(-h)
, where
h
is the external magnetic field. In the above equation we set

\hat{A}=e^{-\beta\hat{H}(h)}e^{i\hat{H}(h)t}\hat{\Gamma}_{A_{2g}}e^{-i\hat{H}(h)t}\hat{\Gamma}_{i}
. This gives

\displaystyle C_{A_{2g}}^{i}(t,h)=\frac{1}{Z}{\rm Tr}\left(e^{-\beta\hat{H}(h)}e^{i\hat{H}(h)t}\hat{\Gamma}_{A_{2g}}e^{-i\hat{H}(h)t}\hat{\Gamma}_{i}\right)

\displaystyle=-\frac{1}{Z}{\rm Tr}\left(\hat{K}\left[\hat{\Gamma}_{i}e^{i\hat{H}(h)t}\hat{\Gamma}_{A_{2g}}e^{-i\hat{H}(h)t}e^{-\beta\hat{H}(h)}\right]\hat{K}^{-1}\right)

\displaystyle=-\frac{1}{Z}{\rm Tr}\left(\hat{\Gamma}_{i}e^{-i\hat{H}(-h)t}\hat{\Gamma}_{A_{2g}}e^{i\hat{H}(-h)t}e^{-\beta\hat{H}(-h)}\right)

\displaystyle=-\frac{1}{Z}{\rm Tr}\left(e^{-\beta\hat{H}(-h)}e^{i\hat{H}(-h)t}\hat{\Gamma}_{i}e^{-i\hat{H}(-h)t}\hat{\Gamma}_{A_{2g}}\right)

\displaystyle=-C_{i}^{A_{2g}}(t,-h).

(S15)

In the above we used the fact that the Raman operators are time reversal invariant and that
\hat{\Gamma}_{A_{2g}}
is
antihermitian.
Thus, the Fourier transforms satisfy the relation
C_{A_{2g}}^{i}(\Omega,h)=-C_{i}^{A_{2g}}(\Omega,-h)
, from which
Eq. (7) of the main text follows.

.6
Example (
1
), antisymmetric response from electronic continuum

We start from the single layer, two orbital model described in example (
1
) of the main text.
Writing
\mathcal{H}_{0}=\sum_{{\bf k}}\hat{h}_{{\bf k}}
, the velocity operators are defined by

\hat{v}_{\nu}\equiv\sum_{{\bf k}}\partial\hat{h}_{{\bf k}}/\partial k_{\nu}
with
\nu=(x,y)
.
This leads to

\hat{\Gamma}_{A_{2g}}=\frac{2tV_{0}}{\bar{\omega}}\sum_{{\bf k}}q_{{\bf k}}\left(c^{\dagger}_{{\bf k},x}c_{{\bf k},y}-{\rm h.c.}\right),

(S16)

q_{{\bf k}}\equiv\epsilon_{m}/V_{0}\sin k_{x}\sin k_{y}(\cos k_{x}+\cos k_{y})+\sin^{2}k_{x}\cos k_{y}+\cos k_{x}\sin^{2}k_{y}
.
Likewise, computing

\partial^{2}\hat{h}_{{\bf k}}/\partial k_{x}^{2}-\partial^{2}\hat{h}_{{\bf k}}/\partial k_{y}^{2}
we get

\hat{\Gamma}_{B_{1g}}=2t\sum_{{\bf k}}\left(\cos k_{x}c^{\dagger}_{{\bf k},x}c_{{\bf k},x}-\cos k_{y}c^{\dagger}_{{\bf k},y}c_{{\bf k},y}\right).

(S17)

The next step is to write the above operators in the band basis for which we need the eigensystem of
the matrix associated with
\hat{h}_{{\bf k}}
. The eigenvalues are

\epsilon_{{\bf k},a/b}=m_{{\bf k}}\pm\sqrt{n_{{\bf k}}^{2}+V_{{\bf k}}^{2}},

(S18)

where
m_{{\bf k}}\equiv(\epsilon_{{\bf k},x}+\epsilon_{{\bf k},y})/2
,

n_{{\bf k}}\equiv(\epsilon_{{\bf k},x}-\epsilon_{{\bf k},y})/2
,
V_{{\bf k}}
is the hybridization, and
(a,b)
are the band
indices. The associated
eigenvectors are

\begin{pmatrix}u_{{\bf k},a}\\
v_{{\bf k},a}\end{pmatrix}=\frac{1}{L_{{\bf k},a}}\begin{pmatrix}1\\
s_{{\bf k},a}\end{pmatrix},\quad\begin{pmatrix}u_{{\bf k},b}\\
v_{{\bf k},b}\end{pmatrix}=\frac{1}{L_{{\bf k},b}}\begin{pmatrix}1\\
s_{{\bf k},b}\end{pmatrix},

(S19)

respectively,
where
s_{{\bf k},a}=V_{{\bf k}}/(\sqrt{n_{{\bf k}}^{2}+V_{{\bf k}}^{2}}+n_{{\bf k}})
,

L_{{\bf k},a}^{2}=1+s_{{\bf k},a}^{2}
,

s_{{\bf k},b}=V_{{\bf k}}/(\sqrt{n_{{\bf k}}^{2}+V_{{\bf k}}^{2}}-n_{{\bf k}})
, and

L_{{\bf k},b}^{2}=1+s_{{\bf k},b}^{2}
.
The antihermitian property of
\hat{\Gamma}_{A_{2g}}
ensures that it is an interband operator with

\hat{\Gamma}_{A_{2g}}=-\frac{2tV_{0}}{\bar{\omega}}\sum_{{\bf k}}q_{{\bf k}}{\rm Sgn}(V_{{\bf k}})\left(c^{\dagger}_{{\bf k},a}c_{{\bf k},b}-{\rm h.c.}\right),

(S20)

where
q_{{\bf k}}=(\sin^{2}k_{x}\cos k_{y}+\cos k_{x}\sin^{2}k_{y})
and
{\rm Sgn}
is the sign function.
Likewise, for the Hermitian operator
\hat{\Gamma}_{B_{1g}}
we get

\hat{\Gamma}_{B_{1g}}=\frac{t|V_{{\bf k}}|}{\sqrt{n_{{\bf k}}^{2}+V_{{\bf k}}^{2}}}\left(\cos k_{x}+\cos k_{y}\right)\left(c^{\dagger}_{{\bf k},a}c_{{\bf k},b}+{\rm h.c.}\right)+\cdots,

(S21)

where the ellipsis imply intraband operators that do not enter the calculation.
The evaluation of the cross-susceptibility follows standard steps, and we get

\displaystyle\chi_{A_{2g}}^{B_{1g}}(i\Omega_{n})

\displaystyle=-\frac{2t^{2}V_{0}}{\bar{\omega}}\frac{1}{\Omega_{0}\beta}\sum_{{\bf k},\omega_{n}}g_{{\bf k}}

\displaystyle\times\left\{G_{b}({\bf k},i\omega_{n}+i\Omega_{n})G_{a}({\bf k},i\omega_{n})-a\leftrightarrow b\right\}.

(S22)

In the above

g_{{\bf k}}=(\cos k_{x}+\cos k_{y})V_{{\bf k}}q_{{\bf k}}/p_{{\bf k}}
,
with
p_{{\bf k}}=((\epsilon_{{\bf k},x}-\epsilon_{{\bf k},y})^{2}/4+V_{{\bf k}}^{2})^{1/2}
,

G_{a/b}({\bf k},i\omega_{n})=1/(i\omega_{n}-\epsilon_{{\bf k},a/b})
. From the above we also have

\chi_{A_{2g}}^{B_{1g}}(-i\Omega_{n})=-\chi_{A_{2g}}^{B_{1g}}(i\Omega_{n})
, which implies that

\phi_{A_{2g}}^{B_{1g}}(i\Omega_{n})=2\chi_{A_{2g}}^{B_{1g}}(i\Omega_{n})
.

If we assume the electronic excitations to be undamped, the frequency sum can be performed analytically. We get

\displaystyle L_{ab}({\bf k},i\Omega_{n})

\displaystyle\equiv\frac{1}{\beta}\sum_{\omega_{n}}G_{a}({\bf k},i\omega_{n})G_{b}({\bf k},i\omega_{n}+i\Omega_{n})

\displaystyle=\frac{n_{F}(\epsilon_{{\bf k},a})-n_{F}(\epsilon_{{\bf k},b})}{i\Omega_{n}+\epsilon_{{\bf k},a}-\epsilon_{{\bf k},b}},

(S23)

where
n_{F}
is the Fermi function. This gives,

\displaystyle\phi_{A_{2g}}^{B_{1g}}(i\Omega_{n})

\displaystyle=-\frac{4t^{2}V_{0}}{\bar{\omega}}\frac{1}{\Omega_{0}}\sum_{{\bf k}}g_{{\bf k}}\left(n_{F}(\epsilon_{{\bf k},a})-n_{F}(\epsilon_{{\bf k},b})\right)

\displaystyle\times\left\{\frac{1}{i\Omega_{n}+\epsilon_{{\bf k},a}-\epsilon_{{\bf k},b}}+\frac{1}{i\Omega_{n}+\epsilon_{{\bf k},b}-\epsilon_{{\bf k},a}}\right\}.

The other limit where the frequency sum can be performed analytically
is with finite electron lifetime but at
T=0
. In this limit

\displaystyle L_{ab}({\bf k},\Omega+i\eta)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2\pi i}\left[\frac{J_{1}({\bf k},\omega)}{\Omega+\epsilon_{{\bf k},a}-\epsilon_{{\bf k},b}}\right.

\displaystyle+\left.\frac{J_{2}({\bf k},\omega)}{\Omega+2i\gamma+\epsilon_{{\bf k},a}-\epsilon_{{\bf k},b}}\right],

(S24)

where

\displaystyle J_{1}({\bf k},\omega)

\displaystyle=\ln(\Omega-\epsilon_{{\bf k},b}+i\gamma)+\ln(-\Omega-\epsilon_{{\bf k},a}-i\gamma)

\displaystyle-\ln(-\epsilon_{{\bf k},a}+i\gamma)-\ln(-\epsilon_{{\bf k},b}-i\gamma),

(S25)

\displaystyle J_{2}({\bf k},\omega)

\displaystyle=-\ln(\Omega-\epsilon_{{\bf k},b}+i\gamma)-\ln(-\Omega-\epsilon_{{\bf k},a}-i\gamma)

\displaystyle+\ln(-\epsilon_{{\bf k},a}-i\gamma)+\ln(-\epsilon_{{\bf k},b}+i\gamma).

(S26)

The computations for example (
1
) were performed using this limit.

For the numerical evaluation we set
t_{1}=2
eV,
t_{2}=0.37
eV,
\mu=1.5
eV,
V_{0}=0.32
eV
and
\epsilon_{m}=0.35
eV. The resulting Fermi surface is shown in Fig. 2(a) of the main text.
The threshold for interband transitions is given by
\Omega_{ph}=2V_{0}

in the
\gamma\simeq 0
limit, while it develops a tail at lower frequencies when the
electron broadening is increased. In Fig. 1(c)-(d) of the main text we set
\gamma=0.05
eV.

Example (
2
), amplitude mode of the CDW phase

Starting from the two-orbital model discussed in example (
1
), the CDW
phase can be effectively described by adding the mean-field inter-orbital interaction

\mathcal{H}_{int}=\Delta\sum_{\mathbf{k}}[c_{\mathbf{k+Q},x}^{{\dagger}}c_{\mathbf{k-Q},y}+c_{\mathbf{k+Q},y}^{{\dagger}}c_{\mathbf{k-Q},x}+\text{h.c.}]

to the total Hamiltonian
\mathcal{H}=\mathcal{H}_{0}+\mathcal{H}_{int}
.
The amplitude mode is associated with amplitude fluctuations of the CDW order
parameter
\Delta
around its equilibrium position. Its contribution to the Raman
response is obtained as the vertex correction in the amplitude channel of the bare
electronic response, which corresponds, from a diagrammatic perspective,
to the two-loop contribution shown in Fig. 1(b) of the main text.
The two fermionic susceptibilities in Eq.(̃8) of the main text explicitly read

\displaystyle\chi_{A_{2g}}^{\Delta}(i\Omega_{n})

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\Omega_{0}\beta}\sum_{\mathbf{k},\omega_{n}}\text{Tr}\left[\hat{\Gamma}_{A_{2g}}\hat{G}(\mathbf{k},i\omega_{n}+i\Omega_{n})\hat{\Delta}\hat{G}(\mathbf{k},i\omega_{n})\right],

(S27)

\displaystyle\chi_{\Delta}^{i}(i\Omega_{n})

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\Omega_{0}\beta}\sum_{\mathbf{k},\omega_{n}}\text{Tr}\left[\hat{\Delta}\hat{G}(\mathbf{k},i\omega_{n}+i\Omega_{n})\hat{\Gamma}_{i}\hat{G}(\mathbf{k},i\omega_{n})\right],

where
i=(B_{1g},B_{2g})
,
\hat{\Delta}\equiv\partial\mathcal{\hat{H}}_{int}/\partial\Delta

is the scattering vertex due to the amplitude mode and
\hat{G}(\mathbf{k},i\omega)=(i\omega-\mathcal{\hat{H}}_{\mathbf{k}})^{-1}
is the electronic Green’s function,
with
\mathcal{H}_{\mathbf{k}}
written in the extended orbital basis defined by the

\Psi_{\mathbf{k}}=\left(c_{\mathbf{k-Q},x},c_{\mathbf{k+Q},x},c_{\mathbf{k-Q},y},c_{\mathbf{k+Q},y}\right)^{T}
spinor, such that
\mathcal{H}=\sum_{\mathbf{k}}\Psi_{\mathbf{k}}^{{\dagger}}\mathcal{\hat{H}}_{\mathbf{k}}\Psi_{\mathbf{k}}
.
Following the same procedure detailed in the previous Section, it is convenient to write

\hat{G}
in the band basis and perform the frequency sum at
T=0
. One then introduces
a finite electron lifetime
\gamma
, set to
\gamma=0.1\Delta
with
\Delta=0.1
eV in
Fig. 2 of the main text. The tight-binding parameters are the same detailed in example (
1
),
while the ordering wave-vector is set to
\mathbf{Q}=\sqrt{2}Q_{0}\{\cos[(1+r)\pi/4],\sin[(1+r)\pi/4]\}
, with
Q_{0}=5\pi/8
. The amplitude mode propagator is well described
by a Lorentzian function
D(\Omega)=2E_{0}/[(\Omega+i\gamma_{0})^{2}-E_{0}^{2}]
,
with
E_{0}=0.005
eV and
\gamma_{0}=0.2E_{0}
.
