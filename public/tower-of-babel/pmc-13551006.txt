Presenteeism and Its Associated Factors Among Rotating Nurse Managers: A Cross‐Sectional Study
Authors: Li Wan, Chao Huang, Tianlan Li, Yanfei Ma
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTAimTo investigate the current status of presenteeism and its associated factors among rotating nurse managers in Western China and to provide insights for nursing practice.DesignA cross‐sectional study.MethodsA convenience sampling method was used. The questionnaire was distributed through nursing management departments and professional contacts across 15 secondary and tertiary general hospitals in Western China, and 97 eligible nurse managers with recent rotation experience completed the survey. Data were collected using a general information questionnaire, the Stanford Presenteeism Scale‐6 (SPS‐6), and the Work Limitations Questionnaire (WLQ). Associations with SPS‐6 scores were examined using univariate analysis, correlation analysis, and multiple linear regression. The number of rotations was treated as a categorical predictor, and a sensitivity analysis was additionally performed by adjusting for years as a nurse manager as a theoretically relevant covariate.Data SourcesThe questionnaire was distributed across 15 secondary and tertiary general hospitals in Western China in 2025, and data from 97 eligible rotating nurse managers were included in the analysis.ResultsThe mean SPS‐6 score among the 97 rotating nurse managers was 16.22 ± 5.22, with higher scores indicating greater health‐related productivity impairment. Univariate analysis showed significant differences in SPS‐6 scores according to hospital level, rotation frequency, health status, perception of the rotation system, and informed consent prior to rotation (all p < 0.05). Correlation analysis showed a weak positive correlation between physical demands and SPS‐6 scores (r = 0.228, p < 0.05). In the primary multiple linear regression model, health status (B = 2.907, 95% CI: 0.725–5.089, p = 0.010), perception of the rotation system (B = 2.189, 95% CI: 0.197–4.181, p = 0.032), and informed consent prior to rotation (B = 2.977, 95% CI: 1.057–4.897, p = 0.003) were significantly associated with SPS‐6 scores. Hospital level, physical demands, and the individual rotation‐frequency categories were not significantly associated with SPS‐6 scores in the multivariable model. The model explained 38.0% of the variance (R 2 = 0.431, adjusted R 2 = 0.380; F = 8.345, p < 0.001). The main findings remained consistent in the sensitivity analysis after additional adjustment for years as a nurse manager.ConclusionHealth status, perception of the rotation system, and informed consent prior to rotation were independently associated with SPS‐6 scores among rotating nurse managers. Given that higher SPS‐6 scores indicate greater health‐related productivity impairment, these findings suggest that both health‐related and organizational factors are relevant to presenteeism in this population. Given the cross‐sectional design, the observed associations should not be interpreted as causal relationships.Implications for the Profession and/or Patient CareThe findings highlight the importance of considering nurse managers' health status and organizational factors related to the rotation process. Healthcare institutions may consider strengthening communication, participation, and organizational support during nurse manager rotation.ImpactThis study provides evidence on presenteeism and its associated factors among rotating nurse managers. The findings may inform the development and refinement of organizational support and rotation‐management practices for nurse managers.Reporting MethodThis study was reported in accordance with the Strengthening the Reporting of Observational Studies in Epidemiology (STROBE) guidelines.No Patient or Public ContributionNo patients or members of the public were involved in the design, conduct, reporting, or dissemination of this study.

Introduction

Nurse managers, as core forces in hospital management, play a crucial role in improving nursing quality, optimizing service levels, and promoting hospital development (Yu et al. 2024). Currently, many hospitals have implemented a nurse manager rotation system, which involves planned, periodic transfers of nurse managers between different departments. This aims to enhance nurse managers' work motivation, increase their managerial competitiveness, and foster innovation awareness (Sorour et al. 2024). However, rotation may disrupt nurse managers' personal work and life (Chatterjee et al. 2023), leading to a series of negative effects (Wang et al. 2024; Li et al. 2025), such as emotional exhaustion, disengagement, and burnout, which may ultimately be associated with reduced work efficiency.

From a theoretical perspective, nurse manager rotation may influence presenteeism through several potential pathways. First, rotation often involves changes in clinical departments, team structures, and work environments, which may increase role ambiguity and role conflict (Zhang et al. 2025; Flatau‐Harrison et al. 2022). These uncertainties can elevate psychological stress and reduce clarity in work expectations. Second, during the transition process, nurse managers are required to adapt to new clinical settings, acquire new professional knowledge, and rebuild interpersonal relationships, which may increase job demands and cognitive load. Third, according to the job demands–resources framework, increased job demands combined with potentially insufficient organizational support during the early stage of rotation may result in resource depletion, emotional exhaustion, and reduced work capacity (Bakker and Demerouti 2017). Taken together, these factors may impair individuals' ability to maintain work functioning (i.e., work performance under occupational constraints).

Presenteeism refers to a condition in which an individual continues working despite health‐related or psychological problems, resulting in impaired work functioning even when physically present at work (Rainbow 2019; Mohammadi et al. 2021). It is commonly manifested as reduced work engagement, decreased concentration, and compromised task performance, leading to suboptimal work outcomes.

In the 1990s, presenteeism was increasingly discussed in occupational health research. Early studies described two conceptual forms of presenteeism: illness‐related presenteeism, in which individuals work while ill and experience reduced efficiency, and work‐function impairment, which emphasizes reduced work performance due to health‐related or psychological conditions (Lerner et al. 2000). In the nursing context, this perspective emphasizes the decline in work functioning (i.e., work performance under occupational constraints) while remaining on duty despite health‐related constraints (Rainbow and Steege 2017).

Existing studies indicate that presenteeism is more pronounced among nurses compared to other industries (McGregor et al. 2018; Kinman 2019; Rainbow et al. 2020). Presenteeism not only affects the quality of nursing services but can also lead to nurse burnout, increased work stress, and teamwork disruptions, thus negatively impacting the overall operation of hospitals and patient care (Zhang et al. 2021; Abd et al. 2025). Existing studies on presenteeism have predominantly focused on specific groups of nurses, such as those in emergency, intensive care, anaesthesia, geriatrics, and general clinical settings (Zhao et al. 2025; Mosteiro‐Díaz et al. 2020; Song et al. 2021; Kang et al. 2022; Liao et al. 2023; Jianlan et al. 2024; Wu et al. 2024), providing valuable evidence on prevalence and influencing factors in frontline nursing populations. However, evidence regarding managerial‐level nursing staff, particularly rotating nurse managers, remains limited. A review of the literature indicates that most existing studies are cross‐sectional investigations of clinical nurses, while quantitative studies specifically targeting rotating nurse managers are scarce. Moreover, the potential mechanisms through which rotation systems may influence presenteeism among nurse managers remain underexplored. This study aims to fill this research gap by investigating presenteeism among rotating nurse managers in Western China, as measured by the Stanford Presenteeism Scale‐6 (SPS‐6), and examining its associations with factors including health status, perceptions of the rotation system, informed consent prior to rotation, and work limitations, with the goal of providing scientific evidence for optimizing clinical nursing management and addressing presenteeism among nurse managers. Additionally, it intends to offer insights for hospital management decisions and policy formulation.

Methods

Design

This study adopted a cross‐sectional design and was conducted after ethical approval in February 2025. The reporting of this study followed the STROBE guideline for cross‐sectional studies.

Sample Size

The sample size was estimated based on the widely used rule of thumb for regression analysis, which recommends a minimum of 5–10 participants per independent variable to ensure model stability (Hayat 2013). In this study, 13 independent variables were included; therefore, the initial required sample size was calculated as 13 × (5–10) = 65–130 participants.

Considering a potential 20% non‐response or invalid questionnaire rate, the minimum required sample size was adjusted to at least 82 participants (65÷[1–0.20]). A total of 102 questionnaires were distributed, and 97 valid responses were obtained, yielding an effective response rate of 95.10%.

Participants

In February 2025, a convenience sampling method was used to recruit eligible rotating nurse managers. The questionnaire was distributed through professional collaboration networks and nursing administrative contacts across 15 hospitals in Western China. Eligible rotating nurse managers were invited to participate with the assistance of the nursing management departments of these hospitals. A total of 97 eligible participants completed the survey and were included in the analysis. Individual hospital identifiers were not collected; therefore, the distribution of respondents across the 15 hospitals could not be determined.

Inclusion criteria (1) Serving as a nurse manager for at least 1 year; (2) Having rotation experience within the past 3 months (defined based on clinical practice patterns to ensure homogeneity of recent rotation experiences); (3) Voluntary participation in the study.

Exclusion criteria: Nurse managers on medium‐ or long‐term leave (≥ 7 days) during the survey period.

Data exclusion criteria: Questionnaires with any missing responses or with identical answers for all items were excluded.

Instruments

The general information questionnaire consisted of two parts. The first part included demographic and professional characteristics (e.g., sex, age, marital status, number of children, professional title, hospital level, years as a nurse manager, and number of rotations). The second part included rotation‐related subjective variables (e.g., perceived health status, perception of the rotation system, and informed consent prior to rotation). The questionnaire was developed based on a literature review, and the content validity was ensured through expert consultation. As the first part consisted of objective demographic variables, no reliability or validity testing was required.

The Stanford Presenteeism Scale‐6 (SPS‐6) was developed by Koopman et al. (2002). Presenteeism can be conceptualized from different perspectives depending on the theoretical framework and measurement tools used. In the present study, presenteeism was assessed using the validated Chinese version of the SPS‐6 translated and adapted by Zhao et al. (2010)., which evaluates health‐related productivity impairment at work. The scale consists of 6 items and is rated on a 5‐point Likert scale ranging from ‘strongly disagree’ to ‘strongly agree.’ Items 1–4 are scored from 1 to 5, whereas Items 5 and 6 are reverse‐scored from 5 to 1. The item scores are summed to obtain a total score ranging from 6 to 30, with higher total scores indicating greater health‐related productivity impairment (i.e., more severe presenteeism). The Cronbach's α coefficients of the Chinese version reported in previous studies range from 0.76 to 0.90, exceeding the acceptable threshold of 0.70, indicating good reliability27. In the present study, the Cronbach's α coefficient for the SPS‐6 was 0.860.

The Work Limitations Questionnaire (WLQ) was developed by Lerner et al. (2002). A validated Chinese version of the WLQ, translated and adapted by Dong et al. (2013), was used in this study. The questionnaire consists of 25 items across four dimensions: time management, physical demands, cognitive/interpersonal demands, and output demands. Items are rated on a 5‐point Likert scale ranging from 0 to 4, representing the proportion of time affected by work limitations, from ‘all of the time (100%)’ to ‘none of the time (0%)’. The total score ranges from 0 to 100, with higher scores indicating greater work limitations. In the Chinese validation study, the overall Cronbach's α coefficient was 0.922, and the test–retest reliability was 0.893, indicating good reliability and stability (Dong et al. 2013). In the present study, the Cronbach's α coefficient for the WLQ was 0.918.

Data Collection

Data collection for this study was conducted using the Wenjuanxing (Questionnaire Star) online survey platform. Participants accessed the questionnaire through a link or QR code distributed by the research team via cooperating hospital contacts and completed the survey anonymously online. Before data collection, the project leader communicated with relevant personnel from participating hospitals to explain the study objectives and procedures. With their assistance, eligible nurse managers were invited to participate. All members of the research team and data management staff received standardized training to ensure consistent understanding of the questionnaire and data collection procedures. To ensure data quality, two independent researchers were responsible for data export, verification, and cross‐checking. The dataset was reviewed in two rounds to minimize errors and missing entries. Incomplete questionnaires were excluded from the final analysis. Only fully completed responses were included in the dataset used for statistical analysis.

Data Analysis

Data analysis was performed using SPSS version 23.0 (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA). Continuous variables were tested for normality prior to analysis. Normally distributed continuous variables were presented as mean ± standard deviation (SD), while non‐normally distributed variables were described using median and interquartile range (IQR). Categorical variables were summarized as frequencies and percentages. For group comparisons, independent‐samples t‐test or one‐way analysis of variance (ANOVA) was used for normally distributed variables, while chi‐square (χ (Sorour et al. 2024)) tests were applied for categorical variables. Correlation analysis was conducted using Pearson correlation for normally distributed variables and Spearman correlation for non‐normally distributed variables.

Multiple linear regression analysis was performed to examine factors associated with SPS‐6 scores. Variables showing statistically significant associations in the univariate or correlation analyses were included in the primary regression model. The number of rotations was treated as a categorical predictor, with one rotation as the reference category, to avoid assuming a linear ordinal effect across rotation‐frequency categories. Prior to regression analysis, multicollinearity was assessed using the variance inflation factor (VIF), and model assumptions were evaluated. The Durbin–Watson (DW) statistic was used to assess autocorrelation of residuals. To assess the robustness of the primary findings, a sensitivity analysis was additionally performed by including years as a nurse manager as a theoretically relevant covariate, irrespective of its statistical significance in the univariate analysis, given its potential relevance to presenteeism among nurse managers reported in previous research. Regression results were reported as unstandardized coefficients (B), standardized coefficients (β), 95% confidence intervals (95% CIs), and p‐values. All statistical tests were two‐tailed, and p < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Ethical Considerations

The study was approved by the Ethics Review Committee of Mianyang Central Hospital (Approval No. S20250317‐01; approval date: February 18, 2025). Before proceeding to the questionnaire, potential participants were first asked whether they voluntarily agreed to participate in the study. Only those who selected ‘Yes’ were able to proceed with the questionnaire, whereas those who selected ‘No’ exited the survey without completing any further items. Although questionnaire distribution was facilitated by nursing management departments and professional contacts, the decision to participate was made individually by each potential participant through this consent procedure. Research informed consent was distinct from the questionnaire variable concerning informed consent before job rotation. The latter was a retrospective item asking participants to report whether they had been informed of and had agreed to the rotation before their previous job rotation was implemented.

Results

Univariate Analysis of SPS‐6 Scores Among Rotating Nurse Managers

The mean SPS‐6 score among the 97 rotating nurse managers was 16.22 ± 5.22. Normality of SPS‐6 scores was assessed using histogram and Q–Q plot inspection, and the distribution was approximately normal; therefore, parametric tests were applied. Univariate analysis showed statistically significant differences in SPS‐6 scores according to hospital level, number of rotations, health status, perception of the rotation system, and informed consent prior to rotation (all p < 0.05). No statistically significant differences were observed according to sex, age, marital status, number of children, job title, or years of experience as a nurse manager.

Specifically, nurse managers from secondary hospitals had higher SPS‐6 scores than those from tertiary hospitals (18.93 ± 5.26 vs. 15.76 ± 5.10, t = −2.143, p = 0.035). SPS‐6 scores also differed significantly across the four rotation‐frequency groups (F = 2.725, p = 0.049), with mean scores of 15.40 ± 4.73, 17.25 ± 5.43, 13.85 ± 6.11, and 18.75 ± 3.84 among nurse managers who had experienced one, two, three, and more than three rotations, respectively. Nurse managers who perceived that the disadvantages of rotation outweighed the advantages had higher SPS‐6 scores than those who believed that the advantages outweighed the disadvantages (18.60 ± 4.51 vs. 14.87 ± 5.13, t = −3.585, p = 0.001). Similarly, participants who had not provided informed consent prior to rotation had higher SPS‐6 scores than those who had provided informed consent (17.89 ± 5.32 vs. 14.20 ± 4.35, t = −3.682, p < 0.001). In addition, nurse managers with poorer self‐reported health status reported higher SPS‐6 scores than those with good health status (18.94 ± 4.17 vs. 15.68 ± 5.25, t = −2.337, p = 0.022). Discussion

The Current Status and Contributing Factors of Presenteeism Among Rotating Nurse Managers

Presenteeism among nursing staff varies significantly across different countries, hospitals, departments, positions, time periods, and environments. The present study reveals that the presenteeism score of 97 rotating nurse managers was (16.22 ± 5.22). When compared to similar studies: in 2020, nurses in Portugal had a score of (20.37 ± 4.37) (Mosteiro‐Díaz et al. 2020) in Spain (20.11 ± 4.34) (Mosteiro‐Díaz et al. 2020) in Brazil (19.90 ± 4.88) (Mosteiro‐Díaz et al. 2020) in China (14.18 ± 4.33) for nurses in 2021 (Song et al. 2021) (15.30 ± 4.88) for clinical nurses in 2022 (Kang et al. 2022) (15.13 ± 4.20) for emergency medical service personnel in China in 2023 (Liao et al. 2023) (14.67 ± 3.92) for anesthesiology nurses in China in 2024 (Jianlan et al. 2024) (16.83 ± 4.24) for ICU nurses in China in 2024 (Wu et al. 2024) and (16.63 ± 3.91) for community elderly care nurses in China in 2025 (Zhao et al. 2025). According to the scoring direction of the Chinese SPS‐6 used in this study, higher scores indicate greater health‐related productivity impairment and more severe presenteeism. These comparisons should be interpreted with caution because differences in healthcare systems, organizational structures, working environments, and measurement contexts may affect score comparability across studies and countries.

Previous studies have shown that nursing staff's presenteeism is associated with staffing levels, workload, work pressure, and shift patterns (Zhao et al. 2025; Mosteiro‐Díaz et al. 2020; Song et al. 2021; Kang et al. 2022; Liao et al. 2023; Jianlan et al. 2024; Wu et al. 2024; Dirgar et al. 2024). Leadership support and perceived colleague burden may also influence presenteeism behaviors (Shan et al. 2022; Lui et al. 2024). In this study, SPS‐6 scores among rotating nurse managers were significantly associated with health status, perception of the rotation system, and informed consent prior to rotation. These associations reflect differences in self‐reported health‐related productivity impairment rather than objective performance differences. Compared with frontline nurses, rotating nurse managers may face additional responsibilities related to team coordination, quality control, and administrative decision‐making while simultaneously adapting to unfamiliar clinical environments. These role transitions may increase psychological demands and reduce available coping resources, which may be associated with greater health‐related productivity impairment during the rotation process.

Health status, encompassing both physical and mental health, is considered an important factor associated with individual presenteeism. Rotation may be a major source of stress in a nurse manager's career, as they may face role conflicts, limitations in professional familiarity, interpersonal relationship rebuilding, and a temporary reduction in sense of belonging. Additionally, this study found that perceptions of the rotation system and the level of informed consent prior to rotation were statistically significant factors. Nurse managers reporting poorer health status had higher SPS‐6 scores than those reporting good health status, indicating greater health‐related productivity impairment. Similarly, participants with less favourable perceptions of the rotation system or without informed consent prior to rotation had higher SPS‐6 scores, indicating greater health‐related productivity impairment. However, these observed associations should not be interpreted as evidence of causal relationships.

The reasons underlying these observed associations remain unclear based on the current data. One possible explanation is that factors such as individual perceptions, prior experiences, or organizational communication may contribute to differences in health‐related productivity impairment; however, these potential mechanisms were not directly assessed in this study and require further investigation.

Similarly, insufficient communication before rotation may be associated with increased uncertainty or psychological strain, but the specific pathways linking communication processes and presenteeism cannot be determined from this cross‐sectional study. According to the job demands–resources framework, job resources such as autonomy, information, and organizational support may buffer the impact of increased job demands on employees' work functioning. However, in this study, the observed associations should be interpreted as reflecting differences in self‐reported health‐related productivity impairment rather than direct causal effects of organizational conditions on performance. Because this study used a cross‐sectional design, these findings should be interpreted as associations rather than causal relationships.

Strategies and Recommendations to Address Presenteeism Among Rotating Nurse Managers

Presenteeism not only affects the quality of nursing services but may also lead to nurse burnout, increased work stress, and obstacles to teamwork, ultimately having a negative impact on hospital operations and patient care (Zhang et al. 2021; Abd et al. 2025). In this study, the SPS‐6 reflects self‐reported health‐related productivity impairment rather than objectively measured productivity; therefore, differences in SPS‐6 scores should be interpreted as differences in the severity of self‐reported presenteeism rather than direct differences in actual work performance.

Implementing a Bidirectional Job Selection System With Full Informed Consent

The attitude of rotating head nurses and their perceptions of the rotation process may be associated with their rotation experience and presenteeism. Ensuring adequate communication and informed consent before rotation is an important consideration, and a bidirectional job selection mechanism may represent one potential approach to enhancing participation and communication during the rotation process. In this study, 45.36% of nurse managers reported having been informed of and having agreed to the rotation before it was implemented, whereas 54.64% reported that informed consent had not been obtained prior to rotation. Based on these findings, healthcare institutions may consider strengthening communication and participation mechanisms before rotation to improve nurse managers' understanding and acceptance of the rotation process.

Within the context of this study, differences in perceived autonomy and participation may be associated with variations in self‐reported health‐related productivity impairment during the transition period. However, the specific psychological or organizational pathways underlying these associations were not directly assessed.

Further studies are needed to explore whether and how factors such as autonomy, organizational communication, and psychological responses influence presenteeism among rotating nurse managers.

Providing Professional Guidance to Reduce Presenteeism

The challenges associated with rotation‐related transitions are complex and may involve multiple factors (Lenssen et al. 2025). Research shows that sufficient human resources and supportive leadership may help reduce presenteeism and improve work quality (Haoyan et al. 2023; Brewer et al. 2016). Organizational empowerment, attention, and support are the prerequisites and guarantees for head nurses' rotation (de Vries et al. 2023; Marufu et al. 2021). These measures may help rotating head nurses familiarize themselves with the new work environment, rebuild interpersonal relationships, acquire specialized knowledge and skills, and adapt to new management models. They may also strengthen trust in the organization. Examples include monitoring physical and mental health (Dirgar et al. 2024), providing targeted training, establishing communication mechanisms, ensuring comprehensive handovers, and implementing regular feedback procedures.

Scientifically Implementing the Head Nurse Rotation Mechanism

Establishing a planned, periodic, scientific, and fair rotation mechanism may help optimize the implementation of nurse manager rotation. It is essential to identify head nurse positions that require rotation and those that cannot be rotated, and to design rotation routes and cycles based on the hospital's size, department complexity, and the head nurse's experience level (Haoyan et al. 2023). The departments and specialties suitable for cross‐department or intra‐department rotation should be clearly defined. During the rotation period, the head nurse's work should be evaluated from multiple dimensions and feedback should be provided regularly. Evaluation dimensions should include nursing quality, team management, resource management, emergency response, and other aspects. The evaluation may involve multiple perspectives, including senior leadership, medical and nursing staff from the department, patients, and their families, which may provide a more comprehensive perspective for assessing nurse managers' performance.

Limitations

This study has several limitations. First, although the minimum required sample size was estimated to be 82 participants, the final sample included 97 rotating nurse managers, which may still be relatively small for a regression model including multiple independent variables. This may affect the stability and precision of the regression estimates. In addition, due to limitations in the study population and inclusion/exclusion criteria, the representativeness of the sample may be limited. Second, convenience sampling was used, and the questionnaire was distributed across 15 hospitals in Western China through professional collaboration networks and nursing administrative contacts. Because individual hospital identifiers were not collected, the distribution of respondents across the hospitals could not be determined, and potential hospital‐level clustering could not be accounted for in the regression analysis. These factors may limit the generalizability of the findings to other regions and healthcare settings. Third, both presenteeism and work limitations were assessed using self‐reported questionnaires, which may be subject to recall bias and social desirability bias. Fourth, the sex distribution was highly imbalanced, with only three male participants, and several other subgroups also had small sample sizes. These factors may have reduced the stability of subgroup comparisons and limited the ability to examine differences across demographic groups. Fifth, this study adopted a cross‐sectional survey design and collected data at a single time point, which does not allow for dynamic tracking of changes in presenteeism and work limitations over time. Moreover, cross‐sectional studies primarily identify associations and cannot establish causal relationships. Future studies with larger and more diverse samples, longitudinal designs, and mixed‐method approaches are needed to further explore the mechanisms underlying presenteeism among rotating nurse managers and to identify potential influencing factors at the individual, organizational, and institutional levels, thereby providing stronger evidence for optimizing nurse manager rotation systems. Finally, the SPS‐6 is a self‐reported measure and therefore reflects perceived health‐related productivity impairment rather than objective productivity or actual performance outcomes.

Conclusion

This study found that SPS‐6 scores among rotating nurse managers were significantly associated with health status, perceptions of the rotation system, and informed consent prior to rotation. Given that higher SPS‐6 scores indicate greater health‐related productivity impairment, these findings suggest that both individual health and organizational factors related to the rotation process may be relevant to presenteeism among rotating nurse managers. However, because this study employed a cross‐sectional design, the observed relationships should be interpreted as associations rather than causal effects. Based on these findings, healthcare institutions may consider strengthening communication, participation, and organizational support within the nurse manager rotation system. Rotation routes and cycles may be designed more rationally according to health status, professional experience, and actual hospital and departmental conditions. Prior to rotation, mechanisms that promote communication and participation may be considered, and post‐rotation support strategies may be strengthened to support nurse managers' transition and address health‐related productivity impairment during the rotation process. Future multicenter and longitudinal studies are needed to further validate these findings and to provide stronger evidence for optimizing nurse manager rotation systems.

Implications for Nursing Practice

In nursing management, maintaining the physical and psychological well‐being of nurse managers is important for supporting their work functioning and professional development. Given the high job demands they face, healthcare institutions may consider implementing targeted support strategies to help nurse managers manage occupational stress and navigate different stages of their managerial roles.

First, the health status of nurse managers should be prioritized. In this study, health status was significantly associated with SPS‐6 scores among rotating nurse managers, with poorer self‐reported health status associated with greater health‐related productivity impairment. Therefore, healthcare institutions may consider providing health promotion programs, psychological support services, and regular health assessments to support nurse managers' well‐being and reduce health‐related productivity impairment.

Second, optimizing the rotation system is important for supporting nurse managers during role transitions. Healthcare institutions may consider designing rotation schedules and task allocations based on health status, professional experience, and competency levels to better align rotation arrangements with individual and organizational needs. Clear communication prior to rotation is also important to improve nurse managers' understanding of rotation arrangements and support the transition process.

Finally, ensuring informed consent prior to rotation is an important consideration in rotation management. In this study, informed consent prior to rotation was associated with SPS‐6 scores, with the absence of informed consent associated with greater health‐related productivity impairment; therefore, healthcare institutions may consider strengthening communication and participation mechanisms before rotation. Nurse managers should receive adequate information regarding rotation responsibilities and expectations. Such approaches may support engagement and acceptance during the rotation process, although their potential effects require further evaluation in future longitudinal studies.

Overall, organizational support and well‐designed rotation processes may provide a supportive environment for addressing presenteeism among nurse managers during rotation. Future intervention studies are needed to determine whether specific management strategies can improve work outcomes and influence presenteeism‐related outcomes.
