Ocular adnexal plasmacytoma presenting as a lesion of the plica semilunaris and caruncle in a 21-year-old male
Authors: Hend M Al-Kaylani, Guadalupe Torres, Carol J Holman, Elaine Binkley, Mark A Greiner, Kenneth M Goins, Mark C Smith, Nasreen A Syed, Kanwal S Matharu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractPurposeSolitary extramedullary plasmacytoma (SEMP) makes up 2-5% of plasma cell dyscrasias and is defined as a plasma cell neoplasm in the absence of systemic multiple myeloma or other dyscrasia. A rare subset of SEMP is plasmacytoma located in the ocular adnexa, most commonly in the conjunctiva and orbital soft tissues. We present and discuss the case of an isolated mass of the caruncle and plica semilunaris in an otherwise healthy 21-year-old male.ObservationsA 21-year-old male with no past medical or ocular history presented with an enlarging, irritating mass in the left medial canthus. He had 20/15 visual acuity bilaterally and no deficits in extraocular motility. Magnetic resonance imaging revealed a T2-hyperintense, well-circumscribed lesion with no infiltration into the sinuses or other tissues. After excision, histopathological analysis confirmed plasmacytoma. The patient underwent a systemic workup for multiple myeloma, including bone marrow biopsy and serum immunofixation electrophoresis, that was unremarkable. After confirmation of no other sites of involvement, a diagnosis of ocular adnexal plasmacytoma was made and the patient underwent localized radiation.Conclusions and importanceWhile ocular adnexal plasmacytoma has been reviewed in previous case studies, it is rare to identify this neoplasm in a healthy young adult. Biopsy is indicated to establish the diagnosis and guide treatment. No clinical trials exist on the treatment of ocular adnexal plasmacytoma; previous reports have demonstrated that cases respond well to localized radiation therapy or, if fully excisable, excision alone with close monitoring for recurrence. While ocular adnexal plasmacytoma has a lower risk for progression to multiple myeloma than bone marrow-derived plasmacytomas, patients should be monitored regularly after treatment by their ophthalmologist, primary care physician, or oncologist.Keywords: Plasmacytoma, Caruncle, Neoplasm of caruncle, Extramedullary plasmacytoma

Introduction

Plasmacytoma of the ocular adnexa is a rare neoplasm in which plasma cells are identified on histopathology of the conjunctiva or orbital soft tissues in the absence of systemic involvement.1 Histopathology shows sheets of plasma cells, characterized by eccentric nuclei and abundant cytoplasm.1,2 Because biopsy-confirmed plasmacytoma is associated with multiple myeloma in over 50% of cases, diagnosis requires a thorough systemic evaluation to exclude other sites of involvement, including lymph node assessment, PET CT, skeletal survey, and bone marrow biopsy.3,4 We describe a case of a solitary extramedullary plasmacytoma presenting as a growth of the caruncle and plica semilunaris in an otherwise healthy 21-year-old male.

Case presentation

Patient consent for this report was obtained verbally in clinic. This study adhered to the tenets of the Declaration of Helsinki and the Health Insurance Portability and Accountability Act.

A healthy 21-year-old man of Latinx origin was referred to our clinic for a growing lesion of the bulbar conjunctiva in the left eye with associated tearing, dryness and irritation. He first noticed the lesion seven months prior. He had no ocular history; family history was significant for “blood cancer” in a maternal cousin.

Visual acuity was 20/15 bilaterally, intraocular pressure was within normal limits, and pupils were equal, round, and reactive to light. On exam, a thick, fleshy lesion of the bulbar conjunctiva was present near the plica semilunaris (Fig. 1). The patient chose to defer biopsy at that time. He had concurrent findings of allergic and vernal conjunctivitis, for which he was started on tacrolimus 0.3% ointment once daily, topical prednisolone acetate four times daily, and topical olopatadine twice daily. One month later, there was no change in examination findings; prednisolone was ceased at that visit to avoid confounding of biopsy results. Magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) of the orbit with and without contrast was obtained (Fig. 2). Patient again deferred biopsy at that time.

Slit lamp examination of the left eye at initial presentation demonstrated a fleshy, well-circumscribed lesion of the left caruncle and plica semilunaris (white arrows).

MRI of the orbit without contrast showed a T2 hyperintense 1.2 × 0.7 cm lesion within the medial aspect of the left globe (yellow arrow) with circumscribed margins and no infiltration to adjacent structures.

One month later, the patient agreed to undergo biopsy. The tumor was unable to be completely excised. Histopathology is presented in Fig. 3. Flow cytometry showed a 21% population of plasma cells with the following immunophenotype: dim CD45+, bright CD38+, CD319+, cytoplasmic kappa +, cytoplasmic lambda-, CD117-, CD27−, CD56−, CD81+, dim CD19+, CD20−. The immunophenotype was consistent with a kappa-restricted plasma cell neoplasm which is aberrantly CD27 negative.

(A) Photomicrograph illustrating round cells with perinuclear halos (arrows) containing immunoglobulin characteristic of plasma cells (H&E, original magnification = 300x). (B) Immunohistochemical staining for CD20 was negative, (C) CD3 staining was negative, (D) CD138 was positive, indicating plasma cell origin, (E) Kappa light chain was positive throughout most of the lesion, and (F) Lamda light chain was largely negative, in keeping with a kappa restricted proliferation as seen on flow cytometry. (DAB with hematoxylin counterstain; original magnification = 100x for images B-F).

Serum infectious workup was positive for Epstein-Barr virus (EBV) antibodies and negative for human immunodeficiency virus (HIV) antibody testing. Bone marrow analysis, karyotype, fluorescence in situ hybridization, and serum immunofixation electrophoresis were unremarkable; there was no evidence for active multiple myeloma. Lastly, full body positron emission tomography (PET) showed no other sites of involvement.

Three months after biopsy, the lesion demonstrated stability in size and characteristics (Fig. 4). The patient was referred to radiation oncology for localized external beam radiotherapy (40 Gy, 20 fractions) given confirmation of the lesion as a solitary plasmacytoma without active multiple myeloma or other sites of growth.

Slit lamp examination of the left eye three months after biopsy and before radiation (Panels A and B) and at the end of external beam radiation (Panels C and D). The full tumor was unable to be removed through an anterior approach. At 3 months, the lesion extended 8 mm from the medial canthus, with a height of 20 mm. After radiation, treatment sequelae include conjunctival hyperemesis, madarosis, and focal scleral thinning (yellow arrow).

After completion of radiotherapy, the lesion significantly decreased in size. He had 2+ punctate epithelial erosions on slit lamp exam and symptoms of ocular surface dryness related to radiation which were managed with artificial tears and ointment. Visual acuity was 20/20-1 with pinhole. He continues to follow in our clinic for surveillance.

Discussion

Ocular adnexal plasmacytoma is a subset of solitary extramedullary plasmacytoma, defined as a plasma cell neoplasm in the absence of systemic disease or medullary lesions.4,5

Cases of plasmacytoma have been reported in the bulbar1,5 and palpebral conjunctiva2,6 as well as the posterior orbit.1 One reported case of conjunctival plasmacytoma had corneal involvement at the site of a main incision for cataract surgery seven months prior.7 In patients with previously diagnosed multiple myeloma, ocular plasmacytoma can signify insufficiently treated or recurrent disease; previous cases include a mass beneath subconjunctival hemorrhage.8 Other manifestations of multiple myeloma in the eye include corneal crystals, ciliary body/pars plana cysts, and hyperviscosity of the retinal vasculature; thus, posterior segment evaluation is crucial to a thorough physical examination.5

Our patient presented with ocular adnexal plasmacytoma involving the caruncle and plica semilunaris. Neoplasms in this region have a wide differential; imaging and biopsy are crucial to guide management. Benign neoplasms of the ocular adnexa include nevus (most common), sebaceous gland adenoma, squamous papilloma, pyogenic granuloma, capillary hemangioma, and reactive lymphoid hyperplasia; malignant and pre-malignant lesions include conjunctival keratoacanthoma, oncocytoma, epithelial dysplasia, follicular lymphoma, ocular surface squamous neoplasia, and basal cell carcinoma9, 10, 11, 12. For our patient, biopsy was essential to establish the diagnosis and pursue systemic workup for plasma cell dyscrasia.

Given its rarity, risk factors for ocular adnexal plasmacytoma have not been clearly established. In recent work, EBV has been theorized as a causal factor in the development of multiple myeloma13 but its association with ocular adnexal plasmacytoma has not been investigated to date. Our patient had serum anti-EBV antibodies. Hower, given greater than 90% of the population also has serum anti-EBV antibodies, an association between EBV exposure and plasmacytoma in our patient is very unlikely.

No clinical trials have been conducted to date to establish guidelines for plasmacytomas of the orbit. Previous cases have responded well to external beam radiation.6,14,15 Excision can help alleviate space-occupying symptoms or proptosis, and chemotherapy can be considered if the patient has risk factors for transformation to multiple myeloma.16 Periodic monitoring after radiotherapy is important to ensure the absence of systemic multiple myeloma, especially in the six months after initial biopsy.2,5,17 Alternatively, if complete excision is successful, the area can be monitored closely for recurrence without radiation treatment.5,7 Our patient was young and otherwise healthy with no risk factors for systemic disease, but the lesion was unable to be excised completely. So, he was offered 40 Gy of localized radiation therapy. While highly effective at controlling the tumor, there was also radiation-associated exacerbation of ocular surface dryness in our patient. Patients undergoing radiation should be counseled on the risks for ocular surface dryness and radiation retinopathy/optic neuropathy.

Although ocular adnexal plasmacytomas are rare, ophthalmologists should be aware that plasma cell dyscrasias can present in the eye and orbit, even in patients with no risk factors. In patients with multiple myeloma, regular ophthalmologic exams are a method of surveillance for disease progression or recurrence.8 Strong relationships with oncology and pathology colleagues allow accurate diagnosis, management, and monitoring of patients with plasmacytomas of the ocular adnexa.
