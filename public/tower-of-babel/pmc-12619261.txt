Integration of gamma irradiation breeding from doubled haploid and systematic screening to develop adapted rice mutants under salinity stress
Authors: Muhammad Fuad Anshori, Bambang Sapta Purwoko, Nono Carsono, Iswari Saraswati Dewi, Abd Haris Bahrun, Achmad Kautsar Baharuddin, Reskiana Rahman, Wijaya Murti Indriatama, Majed Alotaibi, Nawab Ali, Mahmoud F. Seleiman
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Gamma radiation breeding is a significant approach for developing salt-adapted rice varieties that are crucial for food security, particularly in archipelagic nations such as Indonesia. The generated mutation diversity is inherently random and requires a large population. Optimizing mutations requires a systematic methodology that encompasses the induction potential and selection processes. This can be accomplished by optimizing mutations in double haploid lines and screening for salt tolerance within extensive mutant populations. This study aimed to optimize the mutagenic radiation dose in double haploid rice lines and develop a method for screening salt tolerance from germination to the seedling stage. The M1 generation underwent gamma ray irradiation at doses of 0, 200, 400, 600, 800, and 1000 Gy on the double haploid line HS4-15-1-63. The optimized M1 results were advanced to the M2 generation. This process was performed through salinity screening in three stages: germination under salt stress, seedling phase screening in saline soil, and evaluation of the growth and production of adaptive mutants. Research findings show that the optimal radiation dose for inducing diversity in Generation M1 ranged from 200 to 400 Gy, with 200 Gy yielding the highest diversity in Generations M1 and M2. A dose of 200 Gy enhanced the adaptability of HS4-15-1-63 to salt stress. The screening method that integrates the germination and seedling phases with saline soil in trays proved to be efficient. Therefore, a 200 Gy dose and this systematic selection concept are recommended for optimizing mutant adaptability from double haploid lines to salinity stress.

Introduction

Rice is an essential element of economic sustainability and food security for a substantial portion of the global population. The leading rice-producing countries include China, India, Indonesia, Bangladesh, and Vietnam [1], Fluctuations in rice production are a significant threat to food security in these regions. Although rice production has demonstrated a relative annual increase, growth has been gradual. Conversely, the population has surged, correlating with an increased demand for rice [2]. This dynamic is crucial for maintaining food security in many countries. Furthermore, this instability is intensified by the effects of global warming [3]. Global warming exacerbates suboptimal conditions for rice cultivation, including drought, salinity, and flooding. Salinity stress, in particular, adversely affects island nations such as Indonesia. Global warming can result in rising sea levels, which are associated with seawater intrusion into terrestrial areas [4]. This intrusion can elevate soil salinity levels, leading to significant production declines or even crop failure in rice-growing areas near coastal regions. This issue is critical for Indonesia, where 15% of the total rice production is situated near coastal areas [5]. Therefore, addressing rice-related challenges in coastal regions is imperative to maintain the resilience and stability of rice production in Indonesia.

The issue of salinity in rice cultivation can be effectively addressed using plant breeding strategies. This approach is a fundamental component in resolving production challenges in agriculture, including salinity stress. Generally, plant breeding focuses on the genetic modification of plants to develop new varieties with optimal potential based on specific objectives [6]. The Indonesian government has released several rice varieties that exhibit tolerance to salinity stress. However, the dynamics of environmental change continue to evolve, necessitating the ongoing development and enhancement of adaptive varieties in line with technological advancements. Several studies have reported progress in the development of salt-tolerant and adaptive rice varieties [7–10], including the development of a haploid rice variety, HS4-15-1-63 line (F37), that is adaptive to salt stress. Nonetheless [11], demonstrated a reduction in the salinity stress tolerance of the F37 line when screened in saline soil in a tray. This finding indicates that this line requires enhanced adaptability to effectively address the challenges posed by ongoing climate change. Consequently, genetic modification of this line is necessary, which can be achieved through mutation breeding.

Mutagenesis is a straightforward and efficacious technique for generating a diverse base population of mutants. This method is employed to develop additional varieties with specific novel traits derived from the genetic composition of existing varieties [12]. Numerous studies have substantiated the efficacy of variety development through the application of mutations. This concept can be realized through the use of chemical and physical mutagens. However, most studies employ physical mutation or irradiation to create populations exhibiting a wide to extreme level of diversity. According to a bibliometric analysis related to rice irradiation mutation, gamma-ray mutagens are the most prevalent method (Fig. 1). This mutagen is considered relatively cost-effective, with commendable efficacy in establishing a mutation-based population. Nevertheless, the mutation concept is highly contingent upon the wild-type genetic construct or M0, as the mutations that occur are concentrated solely on the genetic base of the wild type, without any contribution from other genetic constructs. The haploid F37 rice line, which possesses a genetic construct sufficiently adaptive to salinity stress, is deemed suitable for enhancement using mutation. However, the process of inducing mutation diversity, which is highly stochastic, renders the direction of selection challenging to predict. This suggests that the success and efficacy of the process are heavily reliant on the size of the selected population. The larger the population, the greater is the probability of identifying the desired traits. However, a large population necessitates substantial costs and systematic methods for the selection process. The development of a salinity screening concept for mutants can be accomplished by optimizing mutation diversity in M1 and systematically selecting salinity screening in M2. The optimization of the mutation diversity in M1 represents the initial stage of the mutation process. The determination of radiosensitivity through LD50 is frequently employed in mutation breeding, including in rice. LD50 serves as an indicator of the effectiveness of mutation breeding [13–16]. A higher LD50 corresponds to greater diversity, although the number of surviving individuals decreases. Conversely, a lower LD50 results in fewer surviving individuals, making LD50 the optimal diversity point with a sufficient percentage of surviving individuals for selection [17–20]. Based on data from several reports (Table 1), the LD50 of rice in gamma radiation mutation ranges from 200 to 600 Gy. This indicates that each genotype exhibits a relatively diverse pattern in response to the administered mutagen dose. Furthermore, the lines used originated from double-haploid technology, suggesting that the mutation response pattern for the F37 line may differ from existing patterns. The development of a systematic selection framework for salinity tolerance screening can be executed in three distinct phases: germination, seedling, and reproductive. The seedling phase is extensively utilized in salinity screening, as it is considered a critical juncture for rice in response to salinity stress and is conducted during the vegetative phase [29]. This phase allows for the rapid and straightforward determination of potential tolerance [9, 30, 31]. However, selection during this phase frequently employs hydroponic methods, which have been criticized for inadequately reflecting the interactions between soil and salinity [8, 9]. Moreover, the tolerance traits identified using this method do not strongly correlate with adaptability during the reproductive phase. A salinity selection method utilizing small containers has been developed, which aligns with the general screening of saline soil in pots and can be adapted for the seedling phase [11]. Such adaptations can be applied to seedling trays, facilitating a large-scale selection capacity at M2. Furthermore, integrating this method with the germination phase could enhance the stability of tolerance traits during the vegetative phase. This approach has not been previously implemented, particularly in mutant rice; thus, its application could represent a novel advancement in the selection of mutant rice with a large population. Consequently, optimizing irradiation on the double haploid line F37 and conducting systematic selection based on the germination-seedling phase in seedling trays for M2 mutants is promising. Therefore, the objectives of this study were (1) to determine the optimal mutagenic irradiation dose for double haploid lines and (2) to assess the effectiveness of developing a new method for screening salt tolerance in mutant rice during the germination-seedling phase.

Materials and methods

Experimental design

The research was conducted at the Breeding Laboratory and Greenhouse Center of Excellence (CoE) Experimental Garden, Faculty of Agriculture, Hasanuddin University, located in the Tamalanrea District of Makassar City, South Sulawesi. The site was situated at an altitude of 0–25 m above sea level, with greenhouse temperatures averaging between 21.4 °C and 41.4 °C. The study was conducted from March to June 2024 for the M1 generation and from July to December 2024 for the M2 generation. The M1 generation was investigated by applying gamma-ray mutagenic irradiation to the haploid line HS4-15-1-63 (F37) from Anshori et al. [7]. The mutation process was carried out at the National Nuclear Energy Agency (BATAN) of Indonesia. The irradiator used was the “Gamma Cell 220,” with the source being Cobalt-60. The dose rate when used was 2.13 kGy/hour, the water content of the rice seeds was 11%, and the procedure was Alanine dosimetry. The mutagenic doses were administered at six levels: 0 (wild type), 200, 400, 600, 800, and 1000 Gy. All irradiated mutants were cultivated until the reproductive phase was reached. The seeds produced in the M1 generation were used in the subsequent M2 generation study. The M2 mutant rice generation study comprised three stages: (1) screening for salinity stress during the germination phase, (2) screening for salinity stress during the seedling phase, and (3) evaluation of the growth and production of adaptive mutants. Research procedure

Optimization of gamma ray irradiation mutagen dose in M1 generation mutants

This phase employed an exploratory design to investigate morphological and physiological variations in M2 mutant rice plants. The objective of this study was to identify individuals exhibiting superior traits before conducting further experimental trials. A total of 50 mutant seeds (M1) of rice were planted for each radiation dose of 0 Gy (control), 200 Gy, 400 Gy, 600 Gy, 800 Gy, and 1000 Gy. The mutated seeds (M1) were planted in a 108-hole seedling tray. The tray contained a growing medium produced by combining soil and compost at a ratio of 3:1 (v/v). The seeds were first soaked and then planted evenly in each hole of the tray according to the mutagenic radiation dose. After two weeks, the seedlings were transferred to pots containing the same growing medium as that in the trays. The pots used had a volume of 10 L, and the growing medium filled the pots to a volume of 8 L. Rice seedlings were planted in the pots at a rate of one seedling per pot. Each pot was fertilized with NPK 15:15:15 at a dose of 5 g/pot at 7 days after sowing (DAS). Subsequently, additional fertilizer was applied at 30 DAS, with 3 g of urea per pot. At this stage, maintenance included irrigation, weeding, and pest and disease control. Irrigation was performed regularly when the water in the pots began to decrease. Weeding was performed by pulling out the weeds growing in each pot. Disease control was performed chemically by applying the fungicide Antracol at a concentration of 3 g per liter of water, and pest control was performed mechanically by manually removing and killing the pests. Rice plants were harvested when the grains had entered the physiological ripening phase, with the criteria of 80% of the panicles appearing yellow and the rice grains at the base of the panicles hardening. Rice harvesting was performed by cutting the lower part of the panicle base using scissors. The harvested rice was then placed in the sample envelopes.

Selection of the germination phase in M2 mutant rice lines

The initial phase of research on the M2 generation was undertaken during the germination stage under salinity stress conditions. The genotypes included the F37 mutant at doses of 200 Gy (F37_200) and F37 at a dose of 400 Gy (F37_400). Each mutation dose group included 500 seeds for salinity germination screening. Screening was conducted using a paper test method. The screening was performed in plastic trays measuring 17 cm × 10 cm × 6 cm with a concentration of 100 mmol/L (5.84 g/L) NaCl, equivalent to 10 dS/m at the EC level of the solution. Each plastic tray was filled with three layers of tissue paper, and each tray contained 100 seeds, depending on the treatment, with 50 seeds per treatment. Specifically, the wild-type genotypes F37 and IR29 were each sown with 100 seeds of good seed quality and divided into two groups: 50 seeds were subjected to salt stress treatment, and 50 seeds were not subjected to salt stress treatment. Wild-type F37 was used as a positive control, whereas IR29, which is known for its potential tolerance to salinity stress, was used as a negative control. All small container seeds were covered to maintain seed moisture, and a germination test was conducted for 7 days. Seed maintenance in plastic trays was performed by placing the trays in a dark location for three days and in a lighted location for four days.

Selection of the seedling phase of selected M2 mutant rice varieties through saline soil in seedling trays

In the second stage of the M2 generation, all seedlings that survived the salinity screening, including the wild type and IR29, were evaluated. This stage follows Anshori et al. [9], which has been modified. This method used trays with specifications identical to those used in the M1 generation study. The test method involved using trays with a NaCl concentration of 60 mM/L (3.50 g/L) or equivalent to 6 dS/m on the EC scale. As in the first stage, the wild type and IR29 under normal germination conditions were also planted in trays without salinity treatment or normal conditions. Wild type and IR29 under salinity conditions were placed in each tray as a control to compare tolerance between trays. Before the normally germinated seeds were transplanted, the transplanting medium was prepared using soil and compost in a 3:1 (v/v) ratio and placed in pots. The seeds that had been transferred to the germination trays were allowed to grow normally for 7 DAS. After this period, salinity stress was initiated by pouring 3 L of NaCl solution, equivalent to 6.34 dS/m, onto the tray base. Each tray was placed on a tray base to induce salinity stress in the seedlings. The maintenance of the trays after NaCl application focused on maintaining water availability; therefore, water was added periodically according to the solution limit at intervals of 3 to 15 days after treatment. After 15 days, the seedlings were observed, and the saline solution was replaced with normal water. Salinity adjustment and recovery were conducted over five days. Seedlings that survived and recovered at this stage were subjected to the third stage to evaluate the potential of mutant lines against the effects of salinity stress.

Evaluation of the response and impact of M2 mutant rice growth and production on salinity stress during the seedling nursery phase

The third stage of the M2 generation study was the evaluation of the adaptability potential of the mutant rice lines that had been selected in the second stage. This step was carried out in reference to our initial study by [8], which identified that seedling selection techniques in saline soil in small containers correlated well with selection during the reproductive phase in saline soil in pots. In addition, the resulting generation is still relatively early (the M2 generation) and has a high level of diversity. This diversity still needs to be maintained by requiring a large number of seeds to pass on to the M3 generation. Furthermore, this stage can also determine the yield potential or optimal recovery after experiencing germination and seedling stress simultaneously. Therefore, direct selection during the reproductive phase was not performed at this stage.

The method used in this stage involves seeds that were adaptively recovered from salinity stress (score 1–5) in pots and is carried out under non-saline conditions. In this stage, several comparison varieties were added, namely Inpari 13, Padjajaran, and M70D, which were germinated and sown alongside the lines selected in stages 1 and 2 of the M2 generation. The selected varieties were included because of their prevalence among farmers and their collective representation of early-to moderate-maturing cultivars, thereby offering a practical reference for assessing the adaptability of the mutant lines. All these varieties were not subjected to salinity stress during the germination and sowing processes or were independent. Data analysis in this study was conducted in distinct stages for both the M1 and M2 generations. In the M1 generation, the analysis focused on radiosensitivity was analyzed using regression analysis and determination of LD50. The LD50 was calculated using Curve Expert 1.3 and Minitab v. 17 for the graph software [25]. Additionally, a biplot diversity analysis based on Principal Component Analysis was performed for this generation to identify patterns of diversity distribution among each mutant dose group, including the wild type [32]. This analysis was performed using the RStudio program ver. 4.5.1 and the factoextra package [33].

In the analysis of salinity screening during the M2 generation’s germination and seedling phases, the focus was on fitness analysis and the relative decline compared to the wild type. Discussion

Potential diversity and assessment of the effectiveness of F37 rice line mutation to various doses of gamma irradiation in Generation M1

According to this study, the pattern of lethal dose reduction in relation to germination percentage was linear, with an LD50 identified at a dose of 502 Gy. These findings align with the LD50 range presented in Table 1 and the review report by [41]. However, during the adaptation phase following transplantation, the reduction in lethal dose assumes a quadratic form, with LD50 occurring at a dose of 273 Gy. This suggests that the LD50 observed in this study was relatively high compared to the general LD50 range. Nonetheless, when evaluating the percentage of surviving plants post-transplantation, the LD50 in this study also fell within the general LD50 range. The discrepancy in LD50 between the germination percentage in trays and the percentage of surviving plants in pots indicates an accumulation of stress between the mutagen treatment and transplantation, as reported by [42]. Generally, mutagen treatment results in a significant increase in reactive oxygen species (ROS) [43–47]. These ROS can damage membranes, nucleic acids, and various cellular components, leading to plant tissue death [48–52]. This effect is further intensified by the transplantation process, which contributes to increased ROS production. As noted by [51], environmental changes in plants can trigger ROS accumulation, resulting in an extremely high accumulation that shifts the stress status from elastic or linear to plastic or quadratic. Consequently, a dose of 273 Gy at the 8-week post-sowing or 6-week post-planting stage was deemed optimal for estimating the LD50 in the F37 double haploid mutant line.

Analysis of the performance of mutant rice lines across various mutation doses revealed that mutation induction generally resulted in a reduction in the mean values of five critical growth traits, except for plant height. This suggests that mutation induction significantly affects generative traits, as corroborated by previous studies [53–56]. Typically, mutations induce physiological and genetic imbalances within plant tissues [57, 58], which in turn alter the performance of rice plants, particularly in terms of tiller formation. This results in a reduced number of tillers compared to M0 or wild type [42, 45, 57–59]. The decrease in tiller number is directly correlated with the number of panicles produced; thus, the observed reduction in panicles in this study can be attributed to the mutation [60, 61]. Notably, the M1 mutant line exhibited a pronounced reduction in the percentage of filled grains, characterized by an exponential decline in the percentage. This observation is consistent with previous studies [45, 56, 62]. The significant decrease in filled grains suggests that the mutation process induces genetic imbalances in the reproductive tissues of rice plants, thereby impairing optimal meiosis [45, 63, 64]. Similar sterility due to mutations has been documented in other plant species, including black gram [65], wheat [66], Pachyrhizus erosus [67], and adlay [68]. Such disruptions in cell division can lead to genetic imbalances, culminating in seed sterility [45]. Consequently, the biological yield in mutant plants showed a similarly extreme decline in grain percentage. However, this pronounced decline also necessitates consideration of the potential genetic diversity generated by this decline.

The PCA biplot analysis results indicated that most mutant lines exhibited distinct diversity compared to their wild-type counterparts. This suggests that the mutation process exerts differential effects on each seed, leading to a broad diversity that diverges from the potential of the wild-type plant. This phenomenon has been similarly reported by other studies [67, 69–71]. Furthermore, the 200 Gy dose demonstrated the greatest diversity, despite the number of surviving plants not exceeding that of the 400 Gy dose. This suggests that the damage and induction of diversity at 400 Gy were relatively more targeted than at 200 Gy, resulting in the diversity produced by the 200 Gy dose being more random and varied. The overall findings reinforce the assessment of performance across the mutation dose groups. The mutation process examined in this study resulted in substantial genetic modifications in the M2 generation. These results suggest that this variety may facilitate the development of mutant lines with enhanced tolerance to saline conditions in the future, pending further evaluations.

Potential tolerance of M2 mutant rice to systemic salinity stress during the seedling phase

The findings from the seedling-phase tolerance screening indicated that the majority of lines exhibited resilience to this stage of stress. This is demonstrated by the variations in percentage and fitness between the mutant lines and wild types under both normal and saline conditions. Generally, during the seedling phase, rice demonstrates a relatively high tolerance to salinity stress [30, 72–74]. This resilience enables rice seeds to endure relatively high salinity levels during the seedling phase of development. Nonetheless, the impact of salinity stress is apparent in the reduction of plumule height, with potential decreases of up to 50% compared to the wild type under normal conditions, as reported by [75, 76]. Salinity stress continues to impede cell division, allowing for a provisional estimation of the tolerance potential based on the plumule height potential [76, 77]. However, this estimation remains imprecise and complex, further complicated by the moderate heritability potential of plumules. Consequently, the assessment of tolerance potential must be extended to the seedling phase.

Analysis of the seedling phase screening results revealed a significant increase in mortality due to salinity stress, exceeding 50% across all mutant dose groups. Notably, IR29 and wild-type F37 exhibited the highest mortality rates, surpassing 90%. Conversely, the 200 Gy mutant dose group demonstrated superior fitness and a negative relative decline compared to the other groups. This observation suggests that stress during the seedling phase results in substantial stress accumulation, thereby diminishing the potential for salinity tolerance at this stage of development. As noted in [30–79], the seedling phase is a critical period for rice under salinity stress. Typically, hydroponic screening is employed during this phase [30, 73]. However, this study utilized soil screening in confined seedling trays, aligning with the findings of [11], where screening in saline soil within small containers produced stress levels comparable to those in pot containers. This approach does not incorporate systematic stress during the seedling stage. Furthermore, the high potential of the seedlings indicates that the mutant lines outperform their wild-type counterparts under both normal and saline conditions, supported by the high heritability of this trait. Collectively, these findings suggest that the selection methodology employed in this study is effective for selecting salt tolerance, particularly during the vegetative phase of growth. Consequently, the selection approach presented here offers a novel method for extensively estimating salt tolerance, particularly in large mutant populations.

According to salinity tolerance scores, the wild-type F37 exhibited a markedly low level of tolerance compared to the mutant dose groups. Notably, the F37_200 Gy mutant group contained the highest number of lines that demonstrated adaptability to salinity stress, as indicated by scores 1, 3, and 5. Mutants in this group also showed vigorous performance, with the potential for a negative relative decline in seedling height. Low-dose mutations can genetically engineer plants in a positive direction, especially for wild-type genotypes with a fairly good tolerance potential [80–83]. This was also demonstrated in this study. In addition, these findings align with the previously observed potential of M1, wherein mutants subjected to a 200 Gy mutation dose displayed greater diversity relative to other mutant dose groups, including the wild type. This suggests a synergistic interaction between the diversity potential of M1 and the diversity potential of M2 tolerance in the systematic screening for salinity stress. Consequently, an irradiation dose of 200 Gy was deemed optimal for enhancing rice tolerance to salinity stress.

Growth and production performance in pots of M2 mutant lines assessed as adaptive in seedling screening

A systematic evaluation of crop performance is essential, with a focus on production potential [84]. This approach aims to enhance the robustness of the evaluation process by establishing evaluation criteria [ 85, 86]. These criteria can be determined through the application of multivariate analysis, incorporating both factor and path analyses. This methodological combination has been documented in previous studies [8, 87, 88]. The integration of these analyses indicated that the number of productive tillers and grains per panicle are significant determinants of productivity. These findings align with the observations in M1, where both traits were correlated with productivity. Consequently, these two traits, along with productivity, can serve as evaluation criteria for assessing the growth and production of adaptive mutant rice lines during salinity screening at the seedling stage.

Upon evaluating the obtained criteria, it was evident that the number of productive tillers significantly impacted the differentiation of potential among the tolerance groups. Generally, in rice cultivation, the concept of clumping positions productive tillers is a key indicator of productivity. An increase in the number of productive tillers correlates with enhanced productivity [58, 84]– [89]. Furthermore, several studies have identified the number of productive tillers as a selection criterion for assessing rice tolerance and adaptability to salinity stress [8, 9, 57, 90–94]. According to [95], rice subjected to salinity stress accumulates reactive oxygen species (ROS) in its leaves and main stems. Genotypes that are sensitive or less adaptive exhibit suboptimal ROS-scavenging capacity, leading to increased ROS accumulation [96, 97]. This accumulation disrupts cell division and enzymatic processes in sensitive genotypes, compelling them to prioritize the stability of enzymatic processes in the main stem to avoid mortality [98]. Consequently, this phenomenon inhibits tiller formation, which is associated with panicle development, resulting in fewer productive tillers in less adaptive sensitive genotypes than in tolerant or adaptive genotypes. Therefore, potential differences among salinity tolerance classes can be discerned through the number of productive tillers or panicles. As demonstrated in Table 7, the total number of tillers (NTT) exhibited a strong positive correlation with biological yield (r = 0.83), although its direct effect on yield was negative (−0.71). Similar patterns have been observed in rice, where total tillers exert a direct negative effect on yield but a positive effect through productive tillers [99, 100]. This suggests that merely increasing the total number of tillers may not enhance the yield, possibly because of competition for assimilates. Nevertheless, the positive indirect effect mediated by the number of productive tillers (NPT) compensates for this, resulting in an overall positive correlation.

When evaluating the potential of each tolerance group, the 200 Gy mutant group demonstrated commendable adaptability following exposure to salinity stress during the seedling phase. This was evident from the overall potential of the 200 Gy mutant tolerance group (score 1–3), which consistently surpassed that of the 400 Gy group. This enhancement was significantly attributed to the increase in the number of productive tillers in the 200 Gy mutant. This observation underscores the pattern that rice tolerance to salinity stress is highly contingent upon a genotype’s capacity to produce tillers. The potential of the 200 Gy mutant group was further corroborated by the diversity pattern observed in the PCA biplot analysis, where this group exhibited greater diversity than the other groups, rendering selection within this mutant dose group effective. The comprehensive results are presented in Table 8; Fig. 5. suggests that the diversity of salinity tolerance in mutation breeding is influenced by the diversity pattern observed in the M1 generation. This phenomenon is consistent with the findings in sorghum [20] and cotton [101]. Compared to the potential of the wild type, mutations with a dose of 200 Gy are considered to enhance adaptability potential relative to its parent F37. The F37 line, as noted in [8, 9], exhibits sufficient adaptive potential to salinity stress. However, this new screening concept with elevated stress levels resulted in moderate potential for the haploid F37 line. This indicates that a 200 Gy mutation dose can augment the adaptability potential of a rice genotype that is already sufficiently adaptive. Conversely, a sufficiently high dose (400 Gy) is suspected to significantly alter the potential of the wild type. This is consistent with the findings of [6], where the higher the mutation dose, the greater the resulting diversity. However, this diversity may not be related to salinity stress adaptability. Therefore, the salinity tolerance potential of the 400 Gy group was not as favorable as that of the 200 Gy group. Recent analyses have demonstrated that a gamma irradiation dose of 200 Gy successfully enhanced significant diversity and adaptability in the F37 line under the conditions tested. Although these findings are promising, further assessments in more advanced generations and trials across various locations are required to determine the broader applicability of this technology for breeding salt-tolerant varieties.

Conclusion

The findings indicate that a 200 Gy irradiation dose is optimal for the HS4-15-1-63 line under the tested conditions, as it yielded the highest genetic diversity and consistent seedling performance across both the M1 and M2 generations. Although irradiation doses ranging from 200 to 400 Gy generally enhanced genetic diversity in rice populations, the 200 Gy dose consistently resulted in the greatest diversity and adaptability, particularly in the HS4-15-1-63 line. Conversely, the 400 Gy dose offered similar or reduced adaptability compared to the wild type, suggesting potential adverse effects at higher doses. This study introduces an innovative and effective method for selecting salt-tolerant rice mutants from large populations by integrating vegetative screening with the seedling-to-seedling selection phase. Seedlings that consistently exhibited strong performance were identified as the most reliable criterion for determining salt tolerance, highlighting the significance of selection based on seedling performance in identifying resilience to salinity stress. Although the results strongly support the use of 200 Gy to enhance adaptability and tolerance in this mutant line, further validation is required.

This study has several limitations, notably its focus on the M1 and M2 generations, leaving the performance of later generations (M3, M4, and beyond) unexamined. Furthermore, the research was conducted in greenhouse settings, which may not accurately represent the outcomes in diverse field conditions. Consequently, future investigations should expand to include M3 and M4 generations and involve multi-location trials. This approach is crucial before adopting a systematic shuttle breeding strategy to improve the salinity stress tolerance of mutants. Although this study primarily assessed salinity tolerance during the early vegetative stages, further exploration of tolerance during the reproductive stage is warranted. Addressing these limitations in subsequent trials is vital for validating the broader applicability of the results.
