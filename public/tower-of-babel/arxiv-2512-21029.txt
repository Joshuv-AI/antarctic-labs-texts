Critical Points of Degenerate Metrics on Algebraic Varieties: A Tale of Overparametrization
Giovanni Luca Marchetti *, Erin Connelly *, Paul Breiding *, Kathlén Kohn *

Abstract
We study the critical points over an algebraic variety of an optimization problem defined by a quadratic objective that is degenerate. This scenario arises in machine learning when the dataset size is small with respect to the model, and is typically referred to as overparametrization. Our main result relates the degenerate optimization problem to a nondegenerate one via a projection. In the highly-degenerate regime, we find that a central role is played by the ramification locus of the projection. Additionally, we provide tools for counting the number of critical points over projective varieties, and discuss specific cases arising from deep learning. Our work bridges tools from algebraic geometry with ideas from machine learning, and it extends the line of literature around the Euclidean distance degree to the degenerate setting.



Keywords:
Euclidean distance degree,
neuromanifolds,
least-squares regression,
overparametrization.

Optimization of a quadratic objective over a constrained space is central in machine learning, and many other branches of the sciences and engineering.
Often, the constraints are polynomial equalities or inequalities, making the underlying space a (semi-)algebraic variety.
This has motivated the development of tools from algebraic geometry for analyzing quadratic optimization problems over algebraic varieties.

A typical regression objective of this form in machine learning and statistical inference is the mean-squared error, consisting in minimizing the squared distance between the model and the data. A fundamental assumption underlying the above-mentioned literature is that the quadratic form is nondegenerate, i.e., it coincides with a squared distance from a target point in the ambient space.
In deep learning, this holds when the dataset size is large enough, but fails in scenarios of data scarcity.
In the latter case, the optimization problem is underspecified: the number of available data points is insufficient to determine the target unambiguously, and the model optimizes an objective with, potentially, a continuum of global minima. From an algebraic perspective, the quadratic form defined by the objective is degenerate, with rank proportional to the dataset size. The behavior of this optimization problem differs drastically from the nondegenerate case when the dataset size is significantly lower than the dimension of the neuromanifold or, more roughly, than the number of parameters, which is typical in contemporary large-scale models. This scenario is often referred to as

2.1
Algebraic sets

A real or complex
algebraic variety

X
is a subset of
\mathbb{K}^{n}
that is the solution set of finitely many polynomials in
\mathbb{K}[x_{1},\ldots,x_{n}]
, where
\mathbb{K}
is either
\mathbb{R}
or
\mathbb{C}
.
In this article, we assume that
X
is
irreducible
, meaning that it cannot be written as the union of two proper non-empty subvarieties. If a variety is not irreducible, it is a finite union of irreducible varieties, so we can always consider these irreducible components individually.

To define the singular locus of
X
, we consider its
prime ideal

I_{X}
that consists of all polynomials in
\mathbb{K}[x_{1},\ldots,x_{n}]
that vanish along
X
and fix a generating set
\langle f_{1},\ldots,f_{s}\rangle=I_{X}
.
We then compute the
s\times n
Jacobian matrix
J
whose
(i,j)
-th entry is
\frac{\partial f_{i}}{\partial x_{j}}
.
For almost every point
p
on
X
(i.e., except for
p
on some proper subvariety) the rank of
J(p)
is the same.
That rank is the
codimension

c
of
X
. The dimension is then

d=\dim X=n-c.

(5)

The points
p
on
X
where the Jacobian
J(p)
attains that rank are called
smooth
or
regular
. The remaining points on
X
(where the rank of the Jacobian is less than the codimension of
X
) are the
singular
ones.
They form a subvariety of
X
, defined by the prime ideal
I_{X}
and the
c\times c
minors of the Jacobian. We denote by
X_{\mathrm{reg}}
the set of smooth points of
X
.

A
semialgebraic set
in
\mathbb{R}^{n}
is a finite union of subsets of
\mathbb{R}^{n}
, each defined by polynomial equalities and inequalities.
The Tarski–Seidenberg theorem
[
18
, Theorem 4.17]
implies that the image of every polynomial map between real algebraic varieties is a semialgebraic set. We refer to
[
5
, Section 4]

for a detailed discussion on the structure of images of polynomial maps.
In particular, when a machine learning model parametrizes functions that are polynomial both in their input and in the parameters, then the set of parametrized functions (i.e., the neuromanifold) is a semialgebraic subset of a finite-dimensional vector space.

\displaystyle X:=\{W\in\mathbb{R}^{n_{L}\times n_{0}}\mid\textnormal{all $(r+1)\times(r+1)$-minors of $W$ vanish}\},

For a semialgebraic set

2.2
Quadratic optimization

In this article, we study the optimization of a quadratic form over a real algebraic variety
X\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}
, focusing on the critical points. Since optimization is often performed via gradient descent, the critical points are the most interesting ones, corresponding to the equilibria of the gradient flow. As customary in the EDD literature, we consider only the smooth locus
X_{\mathrm{reg}}
. While singular and (Zariski) boundary points play an important role in some machine learning scenarios
[
20
]
(cf. Example
2.1
), we leave the consideration of singularities and semialgebraic sets for future work. Thus, we wish to analyze the critical points of the map

\displaystyle X_{\mathrm{reg}}\to\mathbb{R},\quad x\mapsto Q(x-u),

(9)

where
u\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
and
Q
is a quadratic form on
\mathbb{R}^{n}
.
In other words, we are interested in the smooth points
x\in X_{\mathrm{reg}}
such that
Q(v,x-u)=0
for all
v
in the tangent space
\mathrm{T}_{x}{X}
. We point out that
X_{\mathrm{reg}}
is an open set in both the Euclidean or the Zariski topology on
X
.

If one wants to find all real critical points, it is in general hard to work only over the real numbers. This is because the number of critical points changes with varying
u
or
Q
, and so one typically does not have a certificate for when all critical points have been found.
However, over the complex numbers, we have a globally defined count of critical points, which enables us to find all real critical points by first finding all complex ones and then disregarding the non-real solutions.

To explain this formally, we consider the Zariski closure
X(\mathbb{C})\subseteq\mathbb{C}^{n}
of the real variety
X
when viewed as a subset of
\mathbb{C}^{n}
(i.e.,
X(\mathbb{C})
is the set of all complex points satisfying the same polynomial equations as
X
). Similarly, we extend
Q
to a quadratic form on
\mathbb{C}^{n}
(without complex conjugation).
Our main object of interest is therefore the set of complex critical points, denoted by

\mathrm{Crit}_{X,Q}(u)=\{x\in X_{\mathrm{reg}}(\mathbb{C})\mid\forall v\in\mathrm{T}_{x}{X}:Q(v,x-u)=0\}.

(10)

Geometrically,
x\in\mathrm{Crit}_{X,Q}(u)
if, and only if,
x-u
is orthogonal w.r.t.
Q
to the tangent space
\mathrm{T}_{x}{X}

(e.g., see the normal lines in Figure
3(b)
). We denote this relation

(x-u)\perp_{Q}\mathrm{T}_{x}{X}\quad:\Longleftrightarrow\quad\forall v\in\mathrm{T}_{x}{X}:Q(v,x-u)=0.

(11)

If the quadric
Q
is nondegenerate, then, for generic
u\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
, the sets
\mathrm{Crit}_{X,Q}(u)
have the same finite (and positive) cardinality
[
7
]
. This invariant is known as the
Euclidean distance degree
(EDD) of
X
w.r.t.
Q
, denoted by
\textnormal{EDD}_{Q}(X)
.
For all
u\in\mathbb{C}^{n}
, we either have that
|\mathrm{Crit}_{X,Q}(u)|\leq\textnormal{EDD}_{Q}(X)
(with equality when counted with multiplicity) or
|\mathrm{Crit}_{X,Q}(u)|=\infty
.
Hence, the EDD provides, for generic
u\in\mathbb{C}^{n}
, a certificate for having found all complex critical points.

Moreover, for generic quadrics
Q
, the degrees
\textnormal{EDD}_{Q}(X)
are the same.
This invariant is called the
generic Euclidean distance degree
of
X
, which we denote by
\textnormal{gEDD}(X)
.
All nondegenerate quadrics
Q
satisfy

\textnormal{EDD}_{Q}(X)\leq\textnormal{gEDD}(X)

(12)

due to semicontinuity. The following standard example shows that this relation is in general indeed an inequality.

((b))

Our main result Theorem

from nondegenerate quadrics to degenerate ones.
As mentioned in Section

it does not hold for the

2.3
Machine Learning Perspective

In this section, we explain how quadratic optimization arises in machine learning. To this end, we focus on polynomial models, meaning that the neuromanifold
X
consists of polynomial functions
\mathbb{R}^{n_{\mathrm{in}}}\to\mathbb{R}^{n_{\mathrm{out}}}
of degree at most
D
. In other words,
X
is contained in the vector space of all multivariate polynomials of degree at most
D
:

X\subset(\mathbb{R}[x_{1},\ldots,x_{n_{\mathrm{in}}}]_{\leq D})^{n_{\mathrm{out}}}

(13)

(compare Examples
1.1
and
2.2
).
Given a finite dataset
\mathcal{S}\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{n_{\mathrm{in}}}\times\mathbb{R}^{n_{\mathrm{out}}}
, a typical regression problem is to minimize, over
f\in X
, the mean-squared error loss

\mathcal{L}(f)=\sum_{(a,b)\in\mathcal{S}}\|f(a)-b\|^{2}.

(14)

The optimization problem in (
14
) is equivalent to minimizing a (potentially degenerate) distance from some given point in the ambient vector space to
X
.
To see this,
we use the standard trick of turning polynomial regression into a linear one by considering the Veronese embedding
\nu_{D}
that sends
(x_{1},\ldots,x_{n_{\mathrm{in}}})
to a tuple of all monomials in
(x_{1},\ldots,x_{n_{\mathrm{in}}})
of degree at most
D
.
Then, we can identify
f
with its coefficient matrix
W
whose
i
-th row contains the coefficients of the
i
-th coordinate function such that
f(a)=W\nu_{D}(a)
.
Let us denote by
A
and
B
the matrices whose columns are the
\nu_{D}(a)
and
b
, respectively, where
(a,b)
runs over the whole dataset
\mathcal{S}
.

Example 2.5
.

For a single input
n_{\mathrm{in}}=1
, the Veronese embedding is
\nu_{D}(x)=(1,x,x^{2},\ldots,x^{D})
. Therefore,
A
is a Vandermonde matrix.
\diamondsuit

With the notation above, the mean-squared error loss (
14
) can be written in the concise form

\mathcal{L}(f)=\mathcal{L}(W)=\|WA-B\|_{\textnormal{Frob}}^{2},

(15)

where the norm here is the
Frobenius norm

\|M\|_{\textnormal{Frob}}=\sqrt{\mathrm{tr}(MM^{\top})}
.

Proposition 2.6
.

Let
X\subseteq(\mathbb{R}[x_{1},\ldots,x_{n_{\mathrm{in}}}]_{\leq D})^{n_{\mathrm{out}}}
. Then,

\arg\min_{W\in X}\mathcal{L}(W)=\arg\min_{W\in X}\|W-BA^{\dagger}\|^{2}_{AA^{\top}},

(16)

where

A^{\dagger}
is the Moore–Penrose inverse of
A
and

\|M\|_{AA^{\top}}:=\sqrt{\textnormal{tr}(MAA^{\top}M^{\top})}=\|MA\|_{\textnormal{Frob}}.

(17)

Proof.

This follows from the property
A^{\dagger}AA^{\top}=A^{\top}
of the Moore–Penrose inverse. Writing ‘const.’ for terms that do not depend on
W
, we see that

\displaystyle\|W-BA^{\dagger}\|^{2}_{AA^{\top}}

\displaystyle=\textnormal{tr}(WAA^{\top}W^{\top})-2\,\textnormal{tr}(BA^{\dagger}AA^{\top}W^{\top})+\textnormal{const.}

\displaystyle=\textnormal{tr}(WAA^{\top}W^{\top})-2\,\textnormal{tr}(BA^{\top}W^{\top})+\textnormal{const.}=\|WA-B\|_{\textnormal{Frob}}^{2}+\textnormal{const.}

Now the claim follows using (
15
).
∎

Proposition
2.6
says that minimizing the mean-squared error loss over a polynomial model
X
amounts to finding the closest point on
X
to a given point
u:=BA^{\dagger}
, where closeness is measured by the Frobenius-type seminorm induced by the covariance
AA^{\top}
of the input data. The quadratic form over
(\mathbb{R}[x_{1},\ldots,x_{n_{\mathrm{in}}}]_{\leq D})^{n_{\mathrm{out}}}
corresponding to this seminorm is the Kronecker product

Q:=I_{n_{\mathrm{out}}}\otimes AA^{\top}.

(18)

Both
AA^{\top}
and
Q=I_{n_{\mathrm{out}}}\otimes AA^{\top}
are rank-deficient whenever the dataset size
|\mathcal{S}|
is less than the dimension of
\mathbb{R}[x_{1},\ldots,x_{n_{\mathrm{in}}}]_{\leq D}
. As mentioned in Section
1
, this degenerate scenario is common in modern large-scale machine learning models, and is referred to as overparametrization
[
4
]
. This is the core motivation behind our work.

We note that, even in the setting of noisy and big data
|\mathcal{S}|\geq\dim\mathbb{R}[x_{1},\ldots,x_{n_{\mathrm{in}}}]_{\leq D}
, the quadric in (
18
) is not generic, but of a special form: 1) it has tensor structure, and 2) the matrix
AA^{\top}
is structured, as seen in Example
2.7
below. This means that our main result (Theorem
1.2
), which assumes a generic quadric, does not immediately hold in this setting.

Example 2.7
.

Consider quadratic polynomial functions in two variables, i.e.,
n_{\mathrm{in}}=2
and
D=2
.
Choosing the monomial ordering

\nu_{2}(x_{1},x_{2})=(1,x_{1},x_{2},x_{1}^{2},x_{1}x_{2},x_{2}^{2})
, we obtain

\displaystyle AA^{\top}=\sum_{((a_{1},a_{2}),b)\in\mathcal{S}}\begin{pmatrix}1&a_{1}&a_{2}&a_{1}^{2}&a_{1}a_{2}&a_{2}^{2}\\
a_{1}&a_{1}^{2}&a_{1}a_{2}&a_{1}^{3}&a_{1}^{2}a_{2}&a_{1}a_{2}^{2}\\
a_{2}&a_{1}a_{2}&a_{2}^{2}&a_{1}^{2}a_{2}&a_{1}a_{2}^{2}&a_{2}^{3}\\
a_{1}^{2}&a_{1}^{3}&a_{1}^{2}a_{2}&a_{1}^{4}&a_{1}^{3}a_{2}&a_{1}^{2}a_{2}^{2}\\
a_{1}a_{2}&a_{1}^{2}a_{2}&a_{1}a_{2}^{2}&a_{1}^{3}a_{2}&a_{1}^{2}a_{2}^{2}&a_{1}a_{2}^{3}\\
a_{2}^{2}&a_{1}a_{2}^{2}&a_{2}^{3}&a_{1}^{2}a_{2}^{2}&a_{1}a_{2}^{3}&a_{2}^{4}\end{pmatrix}.

In particular, even when
|\mathcal{S}|>>6
, the matrix
AA^{\top}
is
not
an arbitrary symmetric positive definite matrix, as it has repeated entries.

\diamondsuit

Question 2.8
.

What is the semialgebraic description of the set of all matrices
AA^{\top}
for varying data
\mathcal{S}
and fixed
n_{\mathrm{in}},D,|\mathcal{S}|
?

Despite the special structure of the quadric
Q
in (
18
), we can still apply Theorem
1.2
using the following trick, provided the variety
X
that we minimize the loss over is general. Consider a general affine linear transformation
\varphi(x)=Mx+\beta
, and define the quadric
Q^{\prime}:=M^{-\top}QM^{-1}
and the variety
X^{\prime}:=\varphi(X)=\{\varphi(x)\mid x\in X\}
. Since
\varphi
is affine linear,
\mathrm{T}_{x^{\prime}}{X^{\prime}}=M\cdot\mathrm{T}_{x}{X},
where
x^{\prime}=\varphi(x)
. Therefore, the criticality condition (
11
) for
X
and
X^{\prime}
are related by

\forall v\in\mathrm{T}_{x}{X}:Q(v,x-u)=0\quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad\forall v^{\prime}\in\mathrm{T}_{x^{\prime}}{X^{\prime}}:Q^{\prime}(v^{\prime},x^{\prime}-u^{\prime})=0,

(19)

where
u\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
and
u^{\prime}=\varphi(u)
. This means that
x\in X
is a critical point for
u
if, and only if,
x^{\prime}
is a critical point for
u^{\prime}
. In particular, the number of critical points of
X
w.r.t. to
u
and of
X^{\prime}
w.r.t.
u^{\prime}
is the same. However, since
X
is assumed to be general,
X^{\prime}
is general and therefore
Q^{\prime}
and
u^{\prime}
can be assumed to be general for
X^{\prime}
, although we did not define them independently. Consequently, Theorem
1.2
applies to
X^{\prime},Q^{\prime}
and
u^{\prime}
. This gives the number of critical points also for the original setting with
X,Q
and
u
. The reason why this works is that given a quadric, a general variety always has generic EDD for that quadric. The article
[
17
]
calls, in the nondegenerate case, the difference between generic EDD and actual EDD the
ED defect
. It would be interesting to study the ED defect also in the setting of degenerate quadrics.

We close this section with discussing the point
u=BA^{\dagger}
to which we aim to find a closest point on
X
. Note that, while the quadric
Q
in (
18
) only depends on the input data in
\mathcal{S}
, the point
u
depends on both the input and output data. The following result says that, for sufficiently noisy data
\mathcal{S}
, we can assume the point
u
to be generic, at least after projecting away the kernel of the quadric
Q
(which is also the only genericity needed for Theorem
1.2
).

Proposition 2.9
.

For fixed dataset size
s
, consider the set of all possible data points
u
, that is,

U_{s}:=\{BA^{\dagger}\mid\mathcal{S}\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{n_{\mathrm{in}}}\times\mathbb{R}^{n_{\mathrm{out}}},|\mathcal{S}|=s\}.

(20)

1.

If
s\geq\dim\mathbb{R}[x_{1},\ldots,x_{n_{\mathrm{in}}}]_{\leq D}
, then
U_{s}
equals the ambient space
(\mathbb{R}[x_{1},\ldots,x_{n_{\mathrm{in}}}]_{\leq D})^{n_{\mathrm{out}}}
.

2.

If
s<\dim\mathbb{R}[x_{1},\ldots,x_{n_{\mathrm{in}}}]_{\leq D}
, then
U_{s}
is the orthogonal complement (w.r.t. to the standard dot product) of the kernel of the matrix
Q
.

Proof.

The linear span of the Veronese variety
\mathrm{im}(\nu_{D})
is its whole ambient space of dimension equal to
\dim\mathbb{R}[x_{1},\ldots,x_{n_{\mathrm{in}}}]_{\leq D}
.
Hence, for generic
\mathcal{S}
with size at least
\dim\mathbb{R}[x_{1},\ldots,x_{n_{\mathrm{in}}}]_{\leq D}
, the matrix
A
is of full column rank, and so
A^{\dagger}
is of full row rank. Therefore, for varying
B
, we have that
BA^{\dagger}
is an arbitrary matrix.

Now, we assume that
|\mathcal{S}|
is less than
\dim\mathbb{R}[x_{1},\ldots,x_{n_{\mathrm{in}}}]_{\leq D}
.
In this case, for varying
B
,
the
n_{\mathrm{out}}
many rows of the matrix
BA^{\dagger}
are arbitrary vectors in the row space of
A^{\dagger}
, which equals the column space of
A
.
The kernel of the quadric
Q=I_{n_{\mathrm{out}}}\otimes AA^{\top}
is
\mathbb{R}^{n_{\mathrm{out}}}\otimes\ker(A^{\top})\cong\ker(A^{\top})^{n_{\mathrm{out}}}
, so its orthogonal complement is the
n_{\mathrm{out}}
-fold direct sum of the column space of
A
. Therefore,
BA^{\dagger}
is an arbitrary point in the orthogonal complement of
\ker Q
.
∎

3
General Varieties

In this section, we prove the main results of this work. We first show Theorem
1.2
, and then count the occurring finite sets of critical points in Section
3.3
.

Recall that we wish to describe the critical point set
\mathrm{Crit}_{X,Q}(u)
in (
10
), and from (
11
) that the criticality condition is
(x-u)\perp_{Q}\mathrm{T}_{x}{X}
. Analogously to the case of positive definite quadrics studied in
[
7
]
, a central object in our proofs is the
ED correspondence

E_{X,Q}:=\{(x,u)\in X_{\mathrm{reg}}(\mathbb{C})\times\mathbb{C}^{n}\mid(x-u)\perp_{Q}\mathrm{T}_{x}{X}\}.

(21)

By construction, the set of critical points is the fiber of
u
under the projection
E_{X,Q}\rightarrow\mathbb{C}^{n}
onto the second factor.

The main difference when compared to the nondegenerate case is that taking orthogonals w.r.t. a degenerate quadric
Q
is more subtle, and exhibits a non-uniform behavior.
Denoting again by
K
the kernel of the bilinear form associated with
Q
,
for a linear subspace
L\subseteq\mathbb{C}^{n}
, it holds that

\dim L^{\perp_{Q}}=n-\dim L+\dim L\cap K.

(22)

This means that the projection
E_{X,Q}\rightarrow X_{\mathrm{reg}}
onto the first factor is not a vector bundle over
X_{\mathrm{reg}}
; its fibers are affine subspaces of
\mathbb{C}^{n}
whose dimension depends on how the tangent space
\mathrm{T}_{x}{X}
intersects
K
. To partition the ED correspondence accordingly, we define the following subsets of
X
. Recall from (
3
) and (
4
) that we denote
d=\dim X
and
k=\dim K
. Then, we define

P_{i,K}(X):=\{x\in X_{\mathrm{reg}}(\mathbb{C})\mid\dim\mathrm{T}_{x}{X}\cap K=i\},\quad 0\leq i\leq\textnormal{min}\{d,k\}.

(23)

Setting

E_{i,Q}(X):=\{(x,u)\in E_{X,Q}\mid x\in P_{i,K}(X)\},

(24)

we obtain the partition

E_{X,Q}=\bigcup_{i}E_{i,Q}(X).

(25)

The key to proving Theorem
1.2
are the following two lemmata. The first lemma implies that each component
E_{i,Q}(X)
in (
25
) contributes to the critical points of some generic data
u
only if its dimension is at least
n
. The second lemma then computes the dimension of
E_{i,Q}(X)
(and also of
P_{i,K}(X)
). Therefore, these lemmata show us which
E_{i,Q}(X)
must be studied for determining the number of critical points.

Lemma 3.1
.

If
\dim E_{i,Q}(X)<n
, then, for generic
u\in\mathbb{C}^{n}
,
\mathrm{Crit}_{X,Q}(u)\cap P_{i,K}(X)=\emptyset
.

Proof.

As explained above, the critical points form the fiber of
u
under the projection
E_{X,Q}\rightarrow\mathbb{C}^{n}
onto the second factor.
Hence, if
\dim E_{i,Q}(X)<n
, then the fiber over a generic
u\in\mathbb{C}^{n}
misses the component
E_{i,Q}(X)
.
∎

To compute the dimensions of the components
E_{i,Q}(X)
, we make use of dimension counts in Grassmannians.
Given a finite-dimensional complex vector space
V
and
0\leq m\leq\dim V
, we denote by
\mathrm{Gr}(m,V)
the
Grassmannian
of
m
-dimensional linear subspaces of
V
. Moreover, we consider the
Gauss map

\tau\colon X\dashrightarrow\mathrm{Gr}(d,\mathbb{C}^{n}),x\mapsto\mathrm{T}_{x}{X};

(26)

i.e., the rational map induced by the tangent bundle of
X_{\mathrm{reg}}
.

For the next lemma recall again that
d=\dim X
and
k=\dim K
(see (
3
) and (
4
)).

Lemma 3.2
.

Let
Q
be a generic quadric with
k
-dimensional kernel.
If both
i\geq d+k-n
and
\dim\tau(X)\geq i(i+n-d-k)
, we have

\displaystyle\dim P_{i,K}(X)

\displaystyle=d-i(i+n-d-k)\quad\text{ and}

(27)

\displaystyle\dim E_{i,Q}(X)

\displaystyle=n-i(i+n-d-k-1).

(28)

In all other cases, we have
P_{i,K}(X)=\emptyset
and
E_{i,Q}(X)=\emptyset
.

Proof.

For all
x\in X_{\mathrm{reg}}
, we have that
\dim\mathrm{T}_{x}{X}\cap K\geq d+k-n
. Thus,
P_{i,K}(X)=\emptyset
whenever
i<d+k-n
.
From now on, we fix
i\geq d+k-n
.
Consider the subspace of the Grassmannian

Z_{i,K}:=\{T\in\mathrm{Gr}(d,\mathbb{C}^{n})\mid\dim T\cap K=i\}.

(29)

Note that
P_{i,K}(X)=\tau^{-1}(Z_{i,K})
.
We compute the dimension of
P_{i,K}(X)
via the intersection of
Z_{i,K}
with the tangent bundle
\tau(X)
, as follows.

We begin by computing the dimension of
Z_{i,K}
. Its Zariski closure is

\displaystyle\{T\in\mathrm{Gr}(d,\mathbb{C}^{n})\mid\dim T\cap K\geq i\}

\displaystyle=\displaystyle\bigcup_{L\in\mathrm{Gr}(i,K)}\underbrace{\left\{T\in\mathrm{Gr}(d,\mathbb{C}^{n})\mid L\subseteq T\right\}}_{\simeq\mathrm{Gr}(d-i,\mathbb{C}^{n-i})}.

(30)

Therefore,
\dim Z_{i,K}=\dim\mathrm{Gr}(i,K)+\dim\mathrm{Gr}(d-i,\mathbb{C}^{n-i})=i(k-i)+(d-i)(n-d)
. As
K
varies,
Z_{i,K}
gets translated via the action by
\textnormal{GL}(\mathbb{C}^{n})
on
\mathrm{Gr}(d,\mathbb{C}^{n})
. Hence, by Kleiman’s transversality theorem
[
13
]
, for generic
K
, we have that the intersection of
Z_{i,K}
with
\tau(X)
has the expected dimension:

\dim\tau(X)\cap Z_{i,K}=\dim Z_{i,K}+\dim\tau(X)-\dim\mathrm{Gr}(d,\mathbb{C}^{n})=\dim\tau(X)-i(i+n-d-k).

(31)

This number being negative means that the intersection is empty, in which case also
P_{i,K}(X)
is empty. From now on, we assume that (
31
) is non-negative so that the intersection is not empty.
To compute
the dimension of
P_{i,K}(X)
, we consider the linear spaces that are tangent to
X
at more points than expected:

\Delta:=\{T\in\tau(X)\mid\dim\tau^{-1}(T)>d-\dim\tau(X)\}.

(32)

The space
\Delta
is a lower-dimensional Zariski closed subset of
\tau(X)
.
Hence, again by Kleiman’s transversality, we have
\dim\Delta\cap Z_{i,K}<\dim\tau(X)\cap Z_{i,K}
for generic
K
.
The latter means that a generic linear space in the intersection
\tau(X)\cap Z_{i,K}
is tangent to
X
at a sublocus of dimension
d-\dim\tau(X)
, showing that

\dim P_{i,K}(X)=\dim(\tau(X)\cap Z_{i,K})+(d-\dim\tau(X))
.
Together with (
31
), this implies (
27
).

Lastly, for all
x\in P_{i,K}(X)
, we have that
\dim\mathrm{T}_{x}{X}^{\perp_{Q}}=n-d+i
. This implies that we have
\dim E_{i,Q}(X)=\dim P_{i,K}(X)+n-d+i
and with this (
28
) follows.
∎

We now prove Theorem
1.2
by investigating the components
E_{i}(X,Q)
of dimension at least
n
and their behavior under the orthogonal projection
\pi:\mathbb{R}^{n}\to K^{\perp}
onto the orthogonal complement of
K
.
We treat the two cases in Theorem
1.2
separately.

3.1
Mildly degenerate quadrics (
k+d<n
)

Throughout this section, we assume that
k+d<n
, meaning that
\dim K+\dim X<n
.

We denote the Zariski closure of the projection of
X
by

Y:=\textnormal{cl}\,\pi(X).

(33)

In the setting of this section,
Y
is a proper subvariety of
K^{\perp}
. Moreover, for generic
K
, we have that
\mathrm{T}_{x}{X}\cap K=\{0\}
for generic
x\in X_{\mathrm{reg}}
. In other words,
P_{0,K}(X)
is Zariski dense in
X
.
In fact, none of the other loci
P_{i,K}(X)
for
i>0
is expected to contain critical points, which follows from Lemma
3.1
in combination with the next statement.

Lemma 3.3
.

For a generic quadric
Q
with
k
-dimensional kernel, it holds that:

\dim E_{i,Q}(X)\;\begin{cases}=n&\textnormal{ if }i=0,\\
<n&\textnormal{ if }i>0.\end{cases}

(34)

Proof.

Since
n-d-k>0
, (
34
) follows from Lemma
3.2
.
∎

Now, we finally prove the first case of our Main Theorem
1.2
.
For that, recall that
\pi
turns the degenerate quadric
Q
into a nondegenerate quadratic form
\pi(Q)
on
K^{\perp}
.

Theorem 3.4
.

As in (
33
)
denote
Y=\textnormal{cl}\,\pi(X)
.
For a generic quadric
Q
with a
k
-dimensional kernel and a generic
u\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
, the projection
\pi
induces a bijection between the critical point sets

\mathrm{Crit}_{X,Q}(u)\overset{1:1}{\longleftrightarrow}\mathrm{Crit}_{Y,\pi(Q)}(\pi(u)).

(35)

In particular,
|\mathrm{Crit}_{X,Q}(u)|=\textnormal{gEDD}(Y)
.

Proof.

Recall that
\mathrm{Crit}_{X,Q}(u)
is the fiber over
u
of the projection
E_{X,Q}\to\mathbb{C}^{n}
onto the second factor.
By Lemmata
3.1
and
3.3
,
that fiber is the same as the fiber of the restricted projection

E_{0,Q}(X)\to\mathbb{C}^{n}
, and

\mathrm{Crit}_{X,Q}(u)\subseteq P_{0,K}(X)
, where
K
is the kernel of
Q
.

Now, we consider the orthogonal projection
\pi\colon\mathbb{R}^{n}\to K^{\perp}
.
Due to the genericity of
K
and the assumption in this section that
\dim K=k<n-d
,
the restricted projection
\pi|_{X}
is birational onto its image, i.e., for almost
x\in X
, we have that
\pi^{-1}(\pi(x))=\{x\}
.
Hence,

\Delta:=\{x\in X\mid|\pi^{-1}(\pi(x))\cap X|>1\}

(36)

is a lower-dimensional subset of
X
.
Therefore,

\dim\{(x,v)\in E_{0,Q}(X)\mid x\in\Delta\}=\dim(\Delta\cap P_{0,K}(X))+n-d<n,

(37)

and so the genericity of
u
implies that
\mathrm{Crit}_{X,Q}(u)\cap\Delta=\emptyset
.

All in all,
\mathrm{Crit}_{X,Q}(u)
is contained in the locus
P_{0,K}(X)\setminus\Delta
.
That locus contains only smooth points of
X
, and its Zariski closure is all of
X
.
The projection
\pi
restricted to that locus is injective with injective differentials. Thus, for all
x
in that locus, we have that
\pi(x)
is a smooth point of
Y
and
\pi(\mathrm{T}_{x}{X})=\mathrm{T}_{\pi(x)}{Y}
.
Therefore, every
x\in\mathrm{Crit}_{X,Q}(u)
(which satisfies
(x-u)\perp_{Q}\mathrm{T}_{x}{X}
) also satisfies
(\pi(x)-\pi(u))\perp_{\pi(Q)}\mathrm{T}_{\pi(x)}{Y}
, i.e.,
\pi(x)\in\mathrm{Crit}_{Y,\pi(Q)}(\pi(u))
.

For the converse direction of the proof, we make a similar argument for the ED correspondence of
Y
by considering the set

\Delta^{\prime}:=\{y\in Y_{\mathrm{reg}}\mid y\notin\pi(X)\quad\text{ or }\quad|\pi^{-1}(y)\cap X|>1\quad\text{ or }\quad\pi^{-1}(y)\cap X\not\subseteq P_{0,K}(X)\}.

(38)

Then,
\Delta^{\prime}
is a lower-dimensional subset of
Y
. As with (
37
), we conclude that the dimension of

\{(y,v)\in E_{Y,\pi(Q)}\mid y\in\Delta^{\prime}\}
is less than
\dim K^{\perp}
. Therefore, since
\pi(u)
is a generic point in
K^{\perp}
, we have

\mathrm{Crit}_{Y,\pi(Q)}(\pi(u))\cap\Delta^{\prime}=\emptyset
.
Hence, for every
y\in\mathrm{Crit}_{Y,\pi(Q)}(\pi(u))
, there is a unique
x\in X
with
\pi(x)=y
; and moreover,
x\in P_{0,K}(X)
. This implies again
\mathrm{T}_{y}{Y}=\pi(\mathrm{T}_{x}{X})
.
Thus,
(y-\pi(u))\perp_{\pi(Q)}\mathrm{T}_{y}{Y}
implies
(x-u)\perp_{Q}\mathrm{T}_{x}{X}
. Consequently, for every
y\in\mathrm{Crit}_{Y,\pi(Q)}(\pi(u))
there is a unique
x\in\mathrm{Crit}_{X,Q}(u)
.
∎

3.2
Significantly degenerate quadrics (
k+d\geq n
)

In this section, we assume
k+d\geq n
, which means
\dim K+\dim X\geq n
.

In that case,
Y=\textnormal{cl}\,\pi(X)
for generic
K
is all of
K^{\perp}
.
Moreover, a generic tangent space of
X
and the kernel
K
span the whole ambient space
\mathbb{C}^{n}
.
The expected dimension of the intersection between a tangent space and
K
is
d+k-n
, meaning that
P_{d+k-n,K}(X)
is Zariski dense in
X
. The next statement, together with Lemma
3.1
, shows that we also need to consider points where the intersection between
\mathrm{T}_{x}{X}
and
K
is
d+k-n+1
; i.e., one higher than expected. This means that, in general, also
P_{d+k-n+1,K}(X)
contributes to the critical points for a generic data point
u
.

Recall from (
3
) and (
4
) that
d=\dim X
and
k=\dim K
.

Lemma 3.5
.

For a generic quadric
Q
with
k
-dimensional kernel, it holds that:

\dim E_{i,Q}(X)\;\begin{cases}=d+k&\textnormal{ if }i=d+k-n,\\
=n&\textnormal{ if }i=d+k-n+1\textnormal{ and }d-\dim\tau(X)<n-k,\\
<n&\textnormal{ otherwise. }\end{cases}

(39)

Proof.

Since
n-d-k\leq 0
, (
39
) follows from Lemma
3.2
.
∎

A general variety
X
of dimension
d=\dim X
satisfies that
\dim\tau(X)=d
. In that case, the second condition from (
39
) is always satisfied (as long as the quadric
Q
is non-zero). Hence, we typically expect both loci
P_{d+k-n,K}(X)
and
P_{d+k-n+1,K}(X)
to contribute to the critical point set.

The Zariski closure of
P_{d+k-n+1,K}(X)
is the
ramification locus
of the orthogonal projection
\pi
restricted to the variety
X
:

\displaystyle\mathrm{Ram}(\pi|_{X}):=

\displaystyle\textnormal{cl}\ P_{d+k-n+1,K}(X)

(40)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\textnormal{cl}\ \{x\in X_{\mathrm{reg}}\mid K+x\textnormal{ intersects }X\textnormal{ at }x\textnormal{ non-transversely}\}.

In other words,
\mathrm{Ram}(\pi|_{X})
is the Zariski closure of the set of critical points of
\pi
over
X
(this has been informally introduced in Section
1.1
without taking the Zariski closure).
The
branch locus

\mathrm{Br}(\pi|_{X}):=\textnormal{cl}\,\pi(\mathrm{Ram}(\pi|_{X}))

(41)

is the Zariski closure of the corresponding set of critical values.
By Lemma
3.2
,

\dim\mathrm{Ram}(\pi|_{X})=n-k-1,

(42)

and so the branch locus is expected to be a hypersurface in
K^{\perp}
.
In fact, we will prove that the finitely many critical points on that hypersurface with respect to the nondegenerate quadric
\pi(Q)
are expected to be in bijection with the finitely many critical points described in the second case of our Main Theorem
1.2
.
For that, we need the following statement.

Lemma 3.6
.

For a sufficiently general variety
X
, the projection
\pi
is birational over the ramification locus
\mathrm{Ram}(\pi|_{X})
, i.e., it is injective almost everywhere.

We postpone the proof of that lemma to the end of this section, where we also specify concrete assumptions on
X
that make it sufficiently general.
Now, we provide the remaining parts of the proof of our Main Theorem
1.2
.

Theorem 3.7
.

For a generic quadric
Q
with a
k
-dimensional kernel and a generic
u\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
, the critical point set

\mathrm{Crit}_{X,Q}(u)
consists of the zero-loss solutions
(K+u)\cap X_{\mathrm{reg}}
, plus at most finitely many points on the ramification locus
\mathrm{Ram}(\pi|_{X})
.
If
X
is sufficiently general,
those finitely many points are in bijection, via
\pi
, with

\mathrm{Crit}_{\mathrm{Br}(\pi|_{X}),\pi(Q)}(\pi(u))
;
in particular, their cardinality is
\textnormal{gEDD}(\mathrm{Br}(\pi|_{X}))
.

Proof.

Let
\delta:=d+k-n
.
By Lemmata
3.5
and
3.1
, we have that
\mathrm{Crit}_{X,Q}(u)
is the union of the fibers over
u
of the projections

E_{\delta,Q}(X)\to\mathbb{C}^{n}

and
E_{\delta+1,Q}(X)\to\mathbb{C}^{n}
onto the second factor.

First, we consider
E_{\delta,Q}(X)\to\mathbb{C}^{n}
. We show that its fiber
\mathcal{F}_{\delta}(u)
over
u
equals
(K+u)\cap X_{\mathrm{reg}}
. We begin by showing that the first set is a subset of the latter.
By definition,
\mathcal{F}_{\delta}(u)
is contained in
P_{\delta,K}(X)\subseteq X_{\mathrm{reg}}
.
For all
x\in P_{\delta,K}(X)
, we have that
(\mathrm{T}_{x}{X})^{\perp_{Q}}=K
.
Therefore,
\mathcal{F}_{\delta}(u)
is indeed contained in
X_{\mathrm{reg}}\cap(K+u)
.
For the converse direction, we recall from Lemma
3.2
that the dimension of the ramification locus
\mathrm{Ram}(\pi|_{X})=\textnormal{cl}(P_{\delta+1,K}(X))
is
n-k-1
and thus the genericity of
u
implies that
K+u
does not intersect
\mathrm{Ram}(\pi|_{X})
.
In other words,
(K+u)\cap X_{\mathrm{reg}}\subseteq P_{\delta,K}(X)
.
As argued above, all points
x
in that intersection satisfy
(x-u)\perp_{Q}\mathrm{T}_{x}{X}
, and so we conclude that
(K+u)\cap X_{\mathrm{reg}}\subseteq\mathcal{F}_{\delta}(u)
.

Second, we consider
E_{\delta+1,Q}(X)\to\mathbb{C}^{n}
and its fiber
\mathcal{F}_{\delta+1}(u)
over a generic
u
.
Since
\dim E_{\delta+1,Q}(X)\leq n
by Lemma
3.5
, the fiber
\mathcal{F}_{\delta+1}(u)
consist of at most finitely many points. As argued in (
40
), those lie on the ramification locus
\mathrm{Ram}(\pi|_{X})
.

For the rest of this proof, we assume that
X
is a sufficiently general variety.
In particular,
\dim\tau(X)=d
and so
\dim E_{\delta+1,Q}(X)=n
by Lemma
3.5
.
We show that the fiber
\mathcal{F}_{\delta+1}(u)

is in bijection with
\mathrm{Crit}_{\mathrm{Br}(\pi|_{X}),\pi(Q)}(\pi(u))
via the projection
\pi
.
For that, we proceed analogously to the proof of Theorem
3.4
.

By Lemma
3.6
, the projection
\pi
induces a birational map
\mathrm{Ram}(\pi|_{X})\to\mathrm{Br}(\pi|_{X})
.
To simplify notation, we write
R:=\mathrm{Ram}(\pi|_{X})
and
B:=\mathrm{Br}(\pi|_{X})
. We will first show that this map takes critical points in
P_{\delta+1,K}(X)
to critical points in
\mathrm{Br}(\pi|_{X})
.
Define the subset

\Delta:=\Big\{x\in P_{\delta+1,K}(X)\;\Big|\;|\pi^{-1}(\pi(x))\cap R|>1\quad\text{ or }\quad x\notin R_{\mathrm{reg}}\Big\},

(43)

which we need to show our critical points generically avoid. This is a lower-dimensional subset of
P_{\delta+1,K}(X)
.
Therefore,

\{(x,v)\in E_{\delta+1,Q}(X)\mid x\in\Delta\}

has dimension

\dim(\Delta)+n-d+\delta+1<n
, and so the genericity of
u
implies that
\mathcal{F}_{\delta+1}(u)\subseteq P_{\delta+1,K}(X)\setminus\Delta
.
The latter locus contains only smooth points of
R
, and its Zariski closure is all of
R
.
The projection
\pi
restricted to that locus is injective with injective differentials.
Thus, for all
x
in that locus,
\pi(x)
is a smooth point of
B
and
\pi(\mathrm{T}_{x}{R})=\mathrm{T}_{\pi(x)}{B}
.
Moreover, since

\dim\pi(\mathrm{T}_{x}{X})=d-(\delta+1)=n-k-1=\dim B
, we even have that
\pi(\mathrm{T}_{x}{X})=\mathrm{T}_{\pi(x)}{B}
.
Therefore, every
x\in\mathcal{F}_{\delta+1}(u)
(which satisfies
(x-u)\perp_{Q}\mathrm{T}_{x}{X}
) also satisfies
(\pi(x)-\pi(u))\perp_{\pi(Q)}\mathrm{T}_{\pi(x)}{B}
, i.e.,
\pi(x)\in\mathrm{Crit}_{B,\pi(Q)}(\pi(u))
.

For the converse direction, we consider

\Delta^{\prime}:=\{y\in B_{\mathrm{reg}}\mid y\notin\pi(R)\quad\text{ or }\quad|\pi^{-1}(y)\cap R|>1\quad\text{ or }\quad\pi^{-1}(y)\cap R\not\subseteq P_{\delta+1,K}(X)\}.

(44)

This is a lower-dimensional subset of
B
, and so

\dim\{(y,v)\in E_{B,\pi(Q)}\mid y\in\Delta^{\prime}\}<\dim K^{\perp}
.
Since
\pi(u)
is a generic point in
K^{\perp}
,

\mathrm{Crit}_{B,\pi(Q)}(\pi(u))\cap\Delta^{\prime}=\emptyset
.
Hence, for every
y\in\mathrm{Crit}_{B,\pi(Q)}(\pi(u))
, there is a unique
x\in R
with
\pi(x)=y
. Moreover, we have
x\in P_{\delta+1,K}(X)
, which implies again
\mathrm{T}_{y}{B}=\pi(\mathrm{T}_{x}{X})
.
Thus,
(y-\pi(u))\perp_{\pi(Q)}\mathrm{T}_{y}{B}
implies
(x-u)\perp_{Q}\mathrm{T}_{x}{X}
, i.e.,
x\in\mathcal{F}_{\delta+1}(u)
.
∎

Proof of Lemma
3.6

We provide a proof based on projective geometry and, in particular, projective duality.

The
n
-dimensional complex projective space
\mathbb{P}^{n}
is

\mathbb{P}^{n}:=(\mathbb{C}^{n+1}\setminus\{0\})/\sim,

(45)

where two non-zero vectors
v,w
are identified (i.e.,
v\sim w
) if, and only if, they are proportional (i.e.,
w=\lambda v
for some
\lambda\in\mathbb{C}
).
The
dual projective space

(\mathbb{P}^{n})^{\ast}
is constructed in the same way from the dual vector space
(\mathbb{C}^{n+1})^{\ast}
. In other words,
(\mathbb{P}^{n})^{\ast}
is the space of all hyperplanes in
\mathbb{P}^{n}
.
A
projective variety

X\subseteq\mathbb{P}^{n}
is the solution set of a system of homogeneous polynomial equations in
n+1
variables.
The
dual variety

X^{\vee}\subseteq(\mathbb{P}^{n})^{\ast}
is the Zariski closure of the set of all hyperplanes tangent to
X_{\mathrm{reg}}
.
For instance, if
X
is a point, then
X^{\vee}
is a hyperplane in
(\mathbb{P}^{n})^{\ast}
.
Slightly more generally, if
X
is a projective subspace of dimension
d
, then
X^{\vee}
is a projective subspace of
(\mathbb{P}^{n})^{\ast}
of dimension
n-d-1
, which satisfies the biduality
(X^{\vee})^{\vee}=X
.
Over the complex numbers, also nonlinear projective varieties enjoy the property of biduality.

Fact 3.8

(
[
9
, Ch. 1, Thm. 1.1]
)
.

Let
X\subseteq\mathbb{P}^{n}
be a projective variety. Moreover,
let
x\in X_{\mathrm{reg}}
and
H\in X^{\vee}_{\mathrm{reg}}
.
The hyperplane
H
is tangent to
X
at
x
if, and only if, the hyperplane
x^{\vee}
is tangent to
X^{\vee}
at the point
H^{\vee}
.
In particular, we have
(X^{\vee})^{\vee}=X
.

To make use of projective duality, we proceed as follows.
We consider
\mathbb{C}^{n}
as an affine chart of
\mathbb{P}^{n}
, by identifying each vector
(x_{1},\ldots,x_{n})
with the equivalence class of
(x_{1},\ldots,x_{n},1)
under
\sim
.
Then, starting from a real variety
X\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{n}
, we consider the Zariski closure
\bar{X}
of
X(\mathbb{C})\subseteq\mathbb{C}^{n}\subseteq\mathbb{P}^{n}
inside the complex projective space
\mathbb{P}^{n}
.
The dimension of
X
inside
\mathbb{P}^{n}
is the same as the real dimension of
X
, which we denote by
d
, as before.
The orthogonal projection
\pi:\mathbb{R}^{n}\to K^{\perp}
with kernel
K
of dimension
k
corresponds in the projective setting to a projection

\bar{\pi}\colon\mathbb{P}^{n}\dashrightarrow\mathbb{P}^{n-k}
that is undefined precisely at the
(k-1)
-dimensional subspace
\bar{K}
that is the intersection of the Zariski closure of
K
inside
\mathbb{P}^{n}
with the hyperplane
H
at infinity (i.e., the hyperplane
H\subseteq\mathbb{P}^{n}
where the last coordinate is zero).
We will now prove, for sufficiently general
X
, that the ramification and branch loci of projecting
\bar{X}
via
\bar{\pi}
are indeed birational to each other via
\bar{\pi}
.
Since a general variety
X
satisfies the following three properties: 1) it is smooth, 2) its closure
\bar{X}
meets the hyperplane
H
at infinity transversely, and 3) the dual variety
\bar{X}^{\vee}
is a hypersurface
[
9
, Ch. 1, Cor. 1.2]
, it is sufficient to prove the following statement.

Lemma 3.9
.

Suppose that
H
intersects
\bar{X}
only at smooth points, and that the intersection is transversal therein.
Then, for a generic subspace
\bar{K}\subseteq H
of dimension
k-1
,
\bar{\pi}
has finite fibers over its ramification locus
\mathrm{Ram}(\bar{\pi}|_{\bar{X}})
. Suppose moreover that
\bar{X}^{\vee}
is a hypersurface. Then, for a generic subspace
\bar{K}\subseteq H
of dimension
k-1
,
\bar{\pi}
is birational over its ramification locus
\mathrm{Ram}(\bar{\pi}|_{\bar{X}})
, i.e., it is bijective to the branch locus
\mathrm{Br}(\bar{\pi}|_{\bar{X}})
almost everywhere.

Proof.

To simplify notation, we write
X,\pi,K
instead of
\bar{X},\bar{\pi},\bar{K}
.

Since
H
intersects
X
transversally (at smooth points), the classical theorem of Bertini
[
14
]
implies that a generic
K\subset H
will intersect
X
only at smooth points and transversally as well. Thus, for generic
K
, we have that
K\cap\mathrm{Ram}(\pi|_{X})=\emptyset
. This implies that the projection
\pi
restricted to
\mathrm{Ram}(\pi|_{X})
has finite fibers (indeed, if a fiber
\pi^{-1}(y)\cap\mathrm{Ram}(\pi|_{X})
would contain a curve, then – since
K
is a hyperplane in the projective space
\pi^{-1}(y)
– that curve would intersect
K
, which contradicts that
K\cap\mathrm{Ram}(\pi|_{X})=\emptyset
).

We now show the second claim. For
x\in P_{d+k-n+1,K}(X)
, we have that
H_{x}:=K+\mathrm{T}_{x}{X}
is a hyperplane in
\mathbb{P}^{n}
, since
\dim H_{x}=\dim\mathrm{T}_{x}{X}+\dim K-(d+k-n+1)=n-1
. In particular,
H_{x}^{\vee}
belongs to
X^{\vee}\cap K^{\vee}
. Hence, we obtain a rational map

\alpha\colon\mathrm{Ram}(\pi|_{X})=\textnormal{cl}\ P_{d+k-n+1,K}(X)\dashrightarrow X^{\vee}\cap K^{\vee}

(46)

defined over
P_{d+k-n+1,K}(X)
as
x\mapsto H_{x}^{\vee}
. Since
X^{\vee}
is a hypersurface, this map is birational. Indeed, by biduality, a rational inverse is provided by the Gauss map (
26
)
X^{\vee}\dashrightarrow(X^{\vee})^{\vee}=X
restricted to
X^{\vee}\cap K^{\vee}
. That restriction is a well-defined rational map, since – due to the genericity of
K
–
X^{\vee}\cap K^{\vee}
is not contained in the singular locus of
X^{\vee}
.

Note that for a generic
x\in\mathrm{Ram}(\pi|_{X})
, we have by biduality that

\mathrm{T}_{H_{x}^{\vee}}{(X^{\vee}\cap K^{\vee})}=\mathrm{T}_{H_{x}^{\vee}}{X^{\vee}\cap K^{\vee}}=x^{\vee}\cap K^{\vee}.

(47)

The intersection
x^{\vee}\cap K^{\vee}
depends only on
\pi(x)
(i.e., not on
x
itself).
Hence, the following is a well-defined map over the projectivized quotient
\mathbb{P}^{n-k}
:
\beta(y):=x^{\vee}\cap K^{\vee}
for some
x\in\pi^{-1}(y)
. Putting everything together, we obtain the following commutative diagram:

{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces{\mathrm{Ram}(\pi|_{X})}}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces{X^{\vee}\cap K^{\vee}}}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces{\mathrm{Gr}(n-k-1,(\mathbb{P}^{n})^{\ast})}}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces{\mathbb{P}^{n-k}}}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\alpha}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\pi}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\delta}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\beta}

Here,
\delta
is the Gauss map (
26
) of
X^{\vee}\cap K^{\vee}
. Now,
x^{\vee}\cap K^{\vee}={\tilde{x}}^{\vee}\cap K^{\vee}
if, and only if,
\pi(x)=\pi({\tilde{x}})
. In other words,
\beta
is injective.
Since
\pi
has finite fibers by the first part of the proof and
\beta
is injective, the generic fibers of
\delta
must be finite as well. By
[
25
, Theorem 2.3]
, the generic fibers of projective Gauss maps are projective subspaces, and so we conclude that the generic fiber of the Gauss map
\delta
must be a point. In other words,
\delta
is birational onto its image. Since
\alpha
is also birational, we have

\pi^{-1}=\alpha^{-1}\circ\delta^{-1}\circ\beta.

(48)

That means
\pi
is birational as well.
∎

3.3
Counting over Projective Varieties

In this section, we consider varieties
X\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{n}
that are
affine cones
over projective varieties; that is,
X
is the solution set in
\mathbb{R}^{n}
of
homogeneous
polynomials in
n
variables. For this, we denote real projective space by
\mathbb{P}_{\mathbb{R}}^{n}
(defined by replacing
\mathbb{C}
by
\mathbb{R}
in (
45
)).

Many neuromanifolds are such affine cones.
For instance, if the activation
\sigma
of the multilayer perceptron in Example
2.2
is a monomial
\sigma(x)=x^{r}
, then its neuromanifold is an affine cone.
The same holds for similar architectures with weight-sharing restrictions, such as convolutional neural networks
[
21
]
.
Also, the neuromanifolds of deep networks where each layer is a lightning self-attention mechanism (
1
) are affine cones
[
11
]
.

All results from the sections above apply to affine cones over projective varieties.
In particular, for a sufficiently general projective variety, its affine cone is general enough for Lemma
3.6
and the full version of Theorem
3.7
to hold.
Indeed, if
X\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{n}
is the affine cone over a general projective variety
Z\subset\mathbb{P}^{n-1}_{\mathbb{R}}
and
\bar{X}
is the Zariski closure of
X
in complex projective space
\mathbb{P}^{n}
, then Lemma
3.9
applies due to the following reasons: for a general
Z
, we have that 1)
Z
is smooth and so is
\bar{X}
, 2) the hyperplane
H
at infinity intersects
\bar{X}
transversely, and 3)
Z^{\vee}
is a hypersurface and thus
\bar{X}^{\vee}
is a hypersurface as well.

A crucial advantage of the projective scenario is that it enables an elegant counting of critical points via characteristic classes.
To this end, we recall the concept of
polar varieties
that is classical in projective geometry (see e.g.
[
19
]
):
Given an affine cone
X\subseteq\mathbb{C}^{n}
over a projective variety and a linear subspace
V\subseteq\mathbb{C}^{n}
, the corresponding polar variety is

\displaystyle\mathcal{P}(X,V):=\textnormal{cl}\ \{x\in X_{\mathrm{reg}}\setminus V\mid V+x\text{ intersects }X\text{ at }x\text{ non-transversely}\}.

(49)

Note that, in the setting of Section
3.2
, if
X
is an affine cone, then
\mathrm{Ram}(\pi|_{X})=\mathcal{P}(X,K)
; see (
40
).
More generally, the non-transversality condition in (
49
) (which means that
\dim(V+\mathrm{T}_{x}{X})<n
) is equivalent to
\dim(V\cap\mathrm{T}_{x}{X})\geq\dim V+d-n+1
, where
d
is the dimension of the affine cone
X
.
Thus, for
\dim V\geq n-d-1
, we have that

\displaystyle\mathcal{P}(X,V)=\textnormal{cl}\ P_{\dim V+d-n+1,V}(X);

(50)

cf. (
23
).
For generic linear spaces
V
of fixed dimension
j
, the degrees of the polar varieties
\mathcal{P}(X,V)
are the same. It is thus possible to define the
j
-th polar degree of
X
as

\delta_{j}(X):=\degg\mathcal{P}(X,V),

(51)

where
V\subseteq\mathbb{C}^{n}
is a generic linear subspace of dimension
j
.
When
X
is smooth, its polar degrees are equivalent to the Chern classes of its tangent bundle; in fact, they can be computed from one another via a recursive formula
[
12
]
.

A fundamental result for projective varieties states that the generic Euclidean distance degree of the associated affine cone coincides with the sum of all the polar classes
[
7
, Theorem 5.4]
:

\displaystyle\textnormal{gEDD}(X)=\sum_{j=n-d-1}^{n-1}\delta_{j}(X).

(52)

Since polar degrees stay invariant under generic projections
[
19
]
,
an interesting consequence of (
52
) is the following:

Fact 3.10

(
[
7
, Cor. 6.1]
)
.

Let
X\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{n}
be the affine cone over a projective variety,
K\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{n}
be a generic linear subspace of dimension
\dim K=k<n-d=n-\dim X
, and as in (
33
) denote
Y=\textnormal{cl}\ \pi(X)
. Then, the orthogonal projection
\pi:\mathbb{R}^{n}\to K^{\perp}
satisfies that

\displaystyle\textnormal{gEDD}(Y)=\textnormal{gEDD}(X).

(53)

In particular, for an affine cone
X
over a projective variety, the number of critical points in Theorem
3.4
is simply the generic Euclidean distance degree of
X
itself.

We believe that this fact holds true for all varieties (i.e., not just for affine cones), but we are not aware of a proof at the moment. Proving the following would be a fundamental contribution to the theory of Euclidean distance degrees.

Conjecture 3.11
.

Fact
3.10
holds for arbitrary varieties.

Now, we turn our attention to the setting of Section
3.2
and the computation of
\textnormal{gEDD}(\mathrm{Br}(\pi|_{X}))
.
Recall that the finitely many critical points on the ramification locus in Theorem
3.7
appear as the generic fiber of the projection

E_{d+k-n+1,Q}(X)\to\mathbb{C}^{n}
onto the second factor.
If
X
is the affine cone over a general projective variety and has dimension
d
, then
\dim\tau(X)=d-1
.
Thus, in the case that
K
is a hyperplane (i.e.,
k=n-1
), we have that
E_{d+k-n+1,Q}(X)=\emptyset
by Lemma
3.2
, meaning that there are no critical points on the ramification locus in addition to the zero-loss solutions; see Example
3.12
below.
Whenever
k<n-1
(while still assuming
k\geq n-d
), we have that
\dim E_{d+k-n+1,Q}(X)=n
by Lemma
3.5
, and so – by Theorem
3.7
– we expect finitely many critical points on
\mathrm{Ram}(\pi|_{X})

of cardinality
\textnormal{gEDD}(\mathrm{Br}(\pi|_{X}))
.

Example 3.12
.

[
n=3,d=2,k=2
.]
For a general surface
X
in
\mathbb{R}^{3}
, its set of tangent planes
\tau(X)
exhausts (almost) all origin-passing planes in
\mathbb{R}^{3}
. Hence, we expect a given plane
K
to coincide with the tangent plane at finitely many points; see also Figure
2(b)
and Lemma
3.5
, which yields that
\dim P_{2,K}(X)=0
.
However, for an affine cone
X
over a projective plane curve, the set of tangent planes
\tau(X)
is one-dimensional and so we expect none of those planes to coincide with
K
, meaning that
P_{2,K}(X)=\emptyset
.

\diamondsuit

For affine cones over projective varieties, we can generalize the formula (
52
) to a count of
\textnormal{gEDD}(\mathrm{Br}(\pi|_{X}))
by truncating the sum of polar classes:

Theorem 3.13
.

Let
X\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{n}
be the affine cone over a projective variety. Suppose that
X
satisfies the assumptions of Lemma
3.9
. Then, a generic subspace
K\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{n}
of dimension
k\geq n-\dim X
satisfies

\displaystyle\textnormal{gEDD}(\mathrm{Br}(\pi|_{X}))=\sum_{j=k}^{n-1}\delta_{j}(X).

(54)

To prove this claim, we first show the following technical result on polar varieties.

Lemma 3.14
.

Let
X\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{n}
be the affine cone over a projective variety, and let
K\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{n}

be a linear subspace of dimension
k\geq n-\dim X
. Suppose that the orthogonal projection
\pi\colon\mathbb{R}^{n}\rightarrow K^{\perp}
satisfies
\dim\pi(\mathcal{P}(X,K))=\dim\mathcal{P}(X,K)
.
For a generic linear subspace
L\subseteq K^{\perp}
, we have that

\mathcal{P}(\pi(\mathcal{P}(X,K)),L)=\textnormal{cl}\,\pi(\mathcal{P}(X,\pi^{-1}(L))).

(55)

In particular, if
X
satisfies the assumptions in Lemma
3.9
, then we have for generic
K
that

\delta_{j}(\pi(\mathcal{P}(X,K)))=\delta_{j+k}(X).

(56)

Proof.

We begin by proving (
55
).
If
\mathcal{P}(X,K)=\emptyset
, then we also have that
\mathcal{P}(X,\pi^{-1}(L))=\emptyset
due to
K\subseteq\pi^{-1}(L)
, and so (
55
) holds trivially.
Hence, from now on we assume that
\mathcal{P}(X,K)\neq\emptyset
.
Consider a generic
x\in\mathcal{P}(X,\pi^{-1}(L))
. Then,
\mathrm{T}_{x}{X}+\pi^{-1}(L)
is a proper subspace of
\mathbb{R}^{n}
. Since
K\subseteq\pi^{-1}(L)
, we conclude that
x\in\mathcal{P}(X,K)
. Due to the genericity of
L
, we have that
x
is a generic point in
\mathcal{P}(X,K)
. Thus,
\mathrm{T}_{\pi(x)}{\pi(\mathcal{P}(X,K))}=\pi(\mathrm{T}_{x}{\mathcal{P}(X,K)})
, which implies that

\mathrm{T}_{\pi(x)}{\pi(\mathcal{P}(X,K))}+L=\pi(\mathrm{T}_{x}{\mathcal{P}(X,K)}+\pi^{-1}(L))

(57)

is a proper subspace of
K^{\perp}
. In conclusion,
\pi(x)\in\mathcal{P}(\pi(\mathcal{P}(X,K)),L)
.

Now, consider a generic
y\in\mathcal{P}(\pi(\mathcal{P}(X,K)),L)
, and a generic preimage
x\in\mathcal{P}(X,K)
under
\pi
.
Due to the genericity of
L
, we have again that
x
is a generic point on
\mathcal{P}(X,K)
, and so (
57
) holds. Hence,
x\in\mathcal{P}(\mathcal{P}(X,K),\pi^{-1}(L))
, which implies that

\dim\mathrm{T}_{x}{\mathcal{P}(X,K)}\cap\pi^{-1}(L)\geq\dim\mathrm{T}_{x}{\mathcal{P}(X,K)}+\dim\pi^{-1}(L)-(n-1)=\dim L.

(58)

The latter equality holds, since we have by (
50
) and Lemma
3.2
that
\dim\mathcal{P}(X,K)=n-k-1
.
Moreover,
\dim\mathrm{T}_{x}{X}\cap K\geq d+k-n+1
, where
d:=\dim X
.
Furthermore, the assumption
\dim\pi(\mathcal{P}(X,K))=\dim\mathcal{P}(X,K)
and the fact that

is a generic point on
\mathcal{P}(X,K)
yield that
\mathrm{T}_{x}{\mathcal{P}(X,K)}\cap K=\{0\}
. Putting everything together,

\displaystyle\dim\mathrm{T}_{x}{X}\cap\pi^{-1}(L)

\displaystyle\geq\dim\mathrm{T}_{x}{\mathcal{P}(X,K)}\cap\pi^{-1}(L)+\dim\mathrm{T}_{x}{X}\cap K

(59)

\displaystyle\geq\dim L+d+k-n+1=\dim\pi^{-1}(L)+d-n+1.

We conclude that
x\in\mathcal{P}(X,\pi^{-1}(L))
, as desired.

Finally, as we have now proven (
55
), we observe for
j:=\dim L
that

\delta_{j}(\pi(\mathcal{P}(X,K)))=\degg\mathcal{P}(\pi(\mathcal{P}(X,K)),L)=\degg\pi(\mathcal{P}(X,\pi^{-1}(L)))
.
Since
\pi
restricted to
\mathcal{P}(X,K)
is birational and its kernel

intersects
\mathcal{P}(X,K)
only at the origin (by Lemma
3.9
and its proof), the map
\pi
does not change the degree of
\mathcal{P}(X,K)
. Due to the genericity of

, the same holds for
\mathcal{P}(X,\pi^{-1}(L))
, i.e.,

\degg\pi(\mathcal{P}(X,\pi^{-1}(L)))=\degg\mathcal{P}(X,\pi^{-1}(L))
.
The latter equals
\delta_{j+k}(X)
due to the genericity of
K
.
∎

Proof of Theorem
3.13
.

Recall that in the setting of affine cones where

we have that
\pi(\mathcal{P}(X,K))=\mathrm{Br}(\pi|_{X})
. Hence, by (
52
) and Lemma
3.14
, we conclude

\displaystyle\textnormal{gEDD}(\mathrm{Br}(\pi|_{X}))=\sum_{j=0}^{n-k-1}\delta_{j}(\mathrm{Br}(\pi|_{X}))=\sum_{j=k}^{n-1}\delta_{j}(X).

(60)

This is the stated formula for the generic EDD in terms of polar classes.

4.1
Linear Neural Networks

Motivated by Example

, we consider the case of the
determinantal variety
, which is the neuromanifold of a multilayer perceptron with identity activation function

and without bias vectors. Given

n_{\mathrm{out}}\times n_{\mathrm{in}}

In the following, we use the notation of Section

Since the networks we consider in this section are linear, the Veronese embedding

is not needed.
For the purpose of optimizing the loss (
14
), given the dataset

, we first prove that the matrix

Lemma 4.1
.

Let
A=U\Sigma V^{\top}
be a singular value decomposition of

, where
U
and
V
are orthogonal matrices and the entries of the diagonal matrix
\Sigma
are in non-ascending order.
Write
\Sigma=DP
, where

is an invertible diagonal matrix and
P\in\mathbb{R}^{n_{\mathrm{in}}\times|\mathcal{S}|}
is a (generally non-square) diagonal matrix whose first
\rank(A)
entries are
1
and all others
0

Then, we have
\mathcal{L}(W)=\|WUD-R\|_{PP^{\top}}^{2}+\textnormal{const},
where
R:=BV\Sigma^{\dagger}D\in\mathbb{R}^{n_{\mathrm{out}}\times n_{\mathrm{in}}}
.
In particular, the optimization problem (
16
) is equivalent to solving

\arg\min_{\hat{W}\in X}\|\hat{W}-R\|_{PP^{\top}}^{2}.

(61)

Proof.

For a matrix
M
, we have

\|M\|_{AA^{\top}}=\|MUDPV^{\top}\|=\|MUDP\|=\|MUD\|_{PP^{\top}}.

Moreover, the Moore–Penrose inverse of

is
A^{\dagger}=V\Sigma^{\dagger}U^{\top}
and so, using (
16
), we conclude that

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}(W)=\|W-BA^{\dagger}\|_{AA^{\top}}^{2}+\textnormal{const}

\displaystyle=\|W-BV\Sigma^{\dagger}U^{\top}\|_{AA^{\top}}^{2}+\textnormal{const}

\displaystyle=\|(W-BV\Sigma^{\dagger}U^{\top})UD\|_{PP^{\top}}^{2}+\textnormal{const}

\displaystyle=\|WUD-BV\Sigma^{\dagger}D\|_{PP^{\top}}^{2}+\textnormal{const}.

Since the map
X_{\mathrm{reg}}\to X_{\mathrm{reg}},W\mapsto WUD
is a diffeomorphism, it yields a one-to-one correspondence between the critical points of
\min_{W\in X}\|WUD-R\|_{PP^{\top}}^{2}
and
\min_{\hat{W}\in X}\|\hat{W}-R\|_{PP^{\top}}^{2}
.
∎

A subtlety is that the matrix

in Lemma
4.1
has rank bounded as

\rank(R)\leq p:=\min\{n_{\mathrm{in}},n_{\mathrm{out}},n_{\mathrm{data}}\},\quad\text{ where }n_{\mathrm{data}}:=|\mathcal{S}|,

(62)

since

is the product of a
(n_{\mathrm{out}}\times n_{\mathrm{data}})
-matrix and a
(n_{\mathrm{data}}\times n_{\mathrm{in}})
-matrix. Thus, if the dataset size satisfies

, we cannot assume that

is a generic matrix, even if we assume that

and

are generic.
Hence, three ranks are involved in the optimization problem: the rank of
r
of the architecture, the rank
\rank(A)=\rank(P)
of the seminorm, and the rank

of the data matrix

. When the dataset
\mathcal{S}
is generic and

, we have that

, which we will assume from now on. We study several cases depending on the specific ordering of these ranks. We first recall a result describing the tangent space of

– for a proof, see, e.g.,
[
2
]

Fact 4.2
.

Let
W\in X
of rank

. Then,
W
is a smooth point in

and

\displaystyle\mathrm{T}_{W}{X}

\displaystyle=\spann\{uv^{\top}\in\mathbb{R}^{n_{\mathrm{out}}\times n_{\mathrm{in}}}\mid u\in\cspan(W)\ \textnormal{ or }\ v\in\rspan(W)\}

(63)

\displaystyle=\mathbb{R}^{n_{\mathrm{out}}}\otimes\rspan(W)+\cspan(W)\otimes\mathbb{R}^{n_{\mathrm{in}}},

where
\cspan
and
\rspan
denote the span of the columns and of the rows, respectively.

Given
W,R\in\mathbb{R}^{n_{\mathrm{out}}\times n_{\mathrm{in}}}
, let
W^{\prime}
and
R^{\prime}
denote their left
n_{\mathrm{out}}\times n_{\mathrm{data}}
blocks. The quadric function in the optimization problem (
61
) can then be written as

\|W-R\|_{PP^{\top}}=\|W^{\prime}-R^{\prime}\|_{\textnormal{Frob}}.

(64)

Note that the genericity of
\mathcal{S}
implies that
R^{\prime}
is a generic
n_{\mathrm{out}}\times n_{\mathrm{data}}
matrix and that
R=(R^{\prime}\mid 0)
; cf. Proposition
2.9
.

Given
k\leq r
, let
C_{k}
be the set of all critical points on the determinantal variety of matrices of rank at most
r-k
with respect to the Euclidean distance to
R^{\prime}
.
Due to the Eckart–Young Theorem (see, e.g.,
[
7
, Example 2.3]
), if
r-k\leq p=\min\{n_{\mathrm{data}},n_{\mathrm{out}}\}
, then
C_{k}
consists of

|C_{k}|={p\choose r-k}

(65)

many points obtained from choosing
r-k
of the
p
singular values of
R^{\prime}
.
We then define for all
\max\{0,r-p\}\leq k\leq r
:

\displaystyle\begin{split}H_{k}:=\big\{\begin{pmatrix}W^{\prime}&W^{\prime\prime}\end{pmatrix}\in X\,\mid\,W^{\prime}&\in C_{k},\ \cspan(W^{\prime\prime})\subset\cspan(R^{\prime})^{\perp}\oplus\cspan(W^{\prime}),\\
&\textnormal{ and the }\cspan(R^{\prime})^{\perp}\textnormal{ part of }W^{\prime\prime}\textnormal{ has rank }k\big\}.\end{split}

(66)

The definition of
H_{k}
has the following motivation. Suppose that
W\in X
is a critical point whose left block is
W^{\prime}
, and thus
W^{\prime}
is a low-rank matrix approximation of
R^{\prime}
. Then, if
W^{\prime}
has rank
r-k
, the right block
W^{\prime\prime}
of
W
must be chosen in such a way that
W=\begin{pmatrix}W^{\prime}&W^{\prime\prime}\end{pmatrix}
has rank
r
. The degrees of freedom for this are choosing the columns of
W^{\prime\prime}
from
\cspan(R^{\prime})^{\perp}\oplus\cspan(W^{\prime})
. Here, directions in
\cspan(R^{\prime})^{\perp}
increase the rank, while directions in
\cspan(W^{\prime})
do not change the rank. Directions outside of
\cspan(W^{\prime})
contribute to the local perturbations of
W^{\prime}
in the tangent space and the directions orthogonal to
R^{\prime}
are the only ones which do not threaten criticality. If
p<r
, then we must additionally define for
k<r-p
:

H_{k}:=\big\{\begin{pmatrix}R^{\prime}&W^{\prime\prime}\end{pmatrix}\in X\big\}.

(67)

The definition of these sets is independent of
k
; the indexing is exclusively for compatibility with the upcoming Theorem
4.3
. This

is equivalent to the global minimum component seen in Theorem
3.7
. Note that
H_{k}
in (
66
) is not generally closed for

; its closure will allow
W^{\prime\prime}
to have less than maximal rank.
Moreover,
H_{k}
in (
66
) is empty whenever
k>\dim\cspan(R^{\prime})^{\top}=n_{\mathrm{out}}-p
or
k>n_{\mathrm{in}}-n_{\mathrm{data}}.

If non-empty,
H_{k}
consists of

|C_{k}|

connected components (cf. (

. Each component of

has dimension

\begin{split}\dim(H_{k})&=k\cdot(\dim\cspan(R^{\prime})^{\perp}\oplus\cspan(W^{\prime}))+r\cdot(n_{\mathrm{in}}-n_{\mathrm{data}}-k)\\
&=k\cdot(n_{\mathrm{out}}-p+r-k)+r\cdot(n_{\mathrm{in}}-n_{\mathrm{data}}-k)\quad\quad\textnormal{ if }k\geq r-p\end{split}

(68)

and

\begin{split}\dim(H_{k})&=n_{\mathrm{out}}\cdot(r-p)+r\cdot(n_{\mathrm{in}}-n_{\mathrm{data}}-r+p)\quad\quad\quad\quad\textnormal{ if }k<r-p.\end{split}

(69)

This can be seen by counting the degrees of freedom when choosing the columns of
W^{\prime\prime}
; there are
\dim(\cspan(R^{\prime})^{\perp}\oplus\cspan(W^{\prime}))
degrees of freedom when choosing each of the first
k
columns, at which point

achieves its maximum rank

, and then there are
r
degrees of freedom when choosing each of the remaining columns.

Theorem 4.3
.

Given a generic
n_{\mathrm{out}}\times n_{\mathrm{data}}
matrix
R^{\prime}
and
R=(R^{\prime}\mid 0)
, the critical points of (
61

\bigcup\limits_{k=\max\{0,r-n_{\mathrm{data}}\}}^{r}H_{k}.

(70)

This result stands in contrast to Theorems
3.4
and
3.7
, which assume that the kernel of the quadric

intersects the variety generically. In this scenario,

has a specific, non-generic structure. To evaluate this theorem through this lens, we note that the setting of Section
3.1
corresponds to the case where the rank

of a generic

is strictly larger than

, while the setting of Section
3.2
corresponds to the case

, in which case we also have the critical component

in (

Proof.

Keep notation as above. We wish to show that
W=\begin{pmatrix}W^{\prime}&W^{\prime\prime}\end{pmatrix}\in X
is a critical point if, and only if,
W\in H_{k}
, where
\rank(W^{\prime})=r-k
.
From (
64
) it follows that

is a critical point if, and only if,

is orthogonal (with respect to the standard Frobenius scalar product) to the projected tangent space

(with slight abuse of notation as the matrices in the tangent space contain redundant vanishing entries). From (
63
) we deduce that

\displaystyle\mathrm{T}_{W}{X}\cdot P

\displaystyle=\mathbb{R}^{n_{\mathrm{out}}}\otimes(\rspan(W)\cdot P)+\cspan(W)\otimes(\mathbb{R}^{n_{\mathrm{in}}}\cdot P)

(71)

\displaystyle=\mathbb{R}^{n_{\mathrm{out}}}\otimes\rspan(W^{\prime})+\cspan(W)\otimes\mathbb{R}^{n_{\mathrm{data}}}.

The orthogonal complement of the above expression is:

\displaystyle(\mathrm{T}_{W}{X}\cdot P)^{\perp}

\displaystyle=\left(\mathbb{R}^{n_{\mathrm{out}}}\otimes\rspan(W^{\prime})\right)^{\perp}\cap\left(\cspan(W)\otimes\mathbb{R}^{n_{\mathrm{data}}}\right)^{\perp}

(72)

\displaystyle=\cspan(W)^{\perp}\otimes\rspan(W^{\prime})^{\perp}.

Again by (
63
), the tangent space at
W^{\prime}
to the determinantal variety of matrices of rank at most

coincides with
\mathbb{R}^{n_{\mathrm{out}}}\otimes\rspan(W^{\prime})+\cspan(W^{\prime})\otimes\mathbb{R}^{n_{\mathrm{data}}}
, and is therefore contained in
\mathrm{T}_{W}{X}
. Together with (

), this implies that

is critical if, and only if, we have

, and
\cspan(W^{\prime}-R^{\prime})\subseteq\cspan(W)^{\perp}
. The second of these two conditions reduces to
\cspan(W^{\prime}-R^{\prime})\subseteq\cspan(W^{\prime\prime})^{\perp}
since, when

, we already have

. Taking orthogonal complements, we can rephrase the condition for criticality as follows:

W=\begin{pmatrix}W^{\prime}&W^{\prime\prime}\end{pmatrix}\in X\text{ is critical }\;\Longleftrightarrow\;W^{\prime}\in C_{k}\text{ \& }\cspan(W^{\prime\prime})\subseteq\cspan(W^{\prime}-R^{\prime})^{\perp}.

(73)

The rest of the proof is based on this equivalence.
We now distinguish two cases:

The first case is when
p<r
and
k<r-p
. Since the generic rank of
W^{\prime}
is
r-k>p=\textrm{rank}(R^{\prime})
, we necessarily have

in this case. So, the second condition on the right hand side of (

is critical if, and only if,

(cf. (

For the second case we assume that

.
We will show below that

\cspan(W^{\prime}-R^{\prime})^{\perp}=\cspan(R^{\prime})^{\perp}\oplus\cspan(W^{\prime}).

(74)

From (
74
) we conclude the proof as follows:
Since we force
\rank(W^{\prime})=r-k
, as explained after (
66
), the
\cspan(R^{\prime})^{\perp}
part of

is forced to have rank

.
Therefore, (

) is equivalent to the condition for

in (

).

Now, it remains to show (
74
).
From the Eckart–Young Theorem it follows that, if
R^{\prime}=T_{1}\Sigma T_{2}
is a singular value decomposition, then we must have
W^{\prime}=T_{1}\Sigma^{\prime}T_{2}
where the singular values satisfy
\sigma^{\prime}_{i}=\sigma_{i}
for
r-k
indices

and all other
\sigma_{i}^{\prime}=0

[
7
, Ex. 2.3]
. In particular,
W^{\prime}-R^{\prime}=T_{1}(\Sigma^{\prime}-\Sigma)T_{2}
. By the genericity of
R^{\prime}
, there are
p
non-vanishing and distinct singular values
\sigma_{i}
. Without loss of generality, we assume that the first

\sigma_{i}
are non-zero, and that the first

\sigma^{\prime}_{i}
are nonzero. Since

has full rank, multiplying by it on the right preserves the column span. Moreover, since

is orthogonal, it commutes with taking the column span, and with taking orthogonal complements:

\displaystyle\cspan(W^{\prime}-R^{\prime})^{\perp}

\displaystyle=(T_{1}\cdot\cspan(\Sigma-\Sigma^{\prime}))^{\perp}

(75)

\displaystyle=T_{1}\cdot\cspan(\Sigma-\Sigma^{\prime})^{\perp}.

Note that

is the space of vectors with vanishing entries from index

to

, and can be written as
\cspan(\Sigma)^{\perp}\oplus\cspan(\Sigma^{\prime})
. The right-hand side of (

\displaystyle T_{1}\cdot(\cspan(\Sigma)^{\perp}\oplus\cspan(\Sigma^{\prime}))

\displaystyle=(T_{1}\cdot\cspan(\Sigma))^{\perp}\oplus(T_{1}\cdot\cspan(\Sigma^{\prime}))

(76)

\displaystyle=\cspan(R^{\prime})^{\perp}\oplus\cspan(W^{\prime}),

as desired.
∎

– except that, instead of isolated critical points, there are two lines of critical points on the ramification locus. The loss is constant along each of those lines.

5
Conclusion

This work is motivated by deep learning, where a mean-squared error loss with fewer data points than parameters results in a degenerate quadratic loss function. We count the (complex) critical points of a degenerate quadratic loss
Q
defined on an algebraic variety
X\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}
. When the quadric is not degenerate, this count is known as the Euclidean distance degree
[
7
]
(EDD). We generalize the EDD to the degenerate setting.

Theorems
3.4
and
3.7
prove that, for a generic degenerate quadric
Q
, counting critical points falls into two regimes depending on the dimension
k=\dim K
of the kernel
K
of
Q
relative to the dimension
d=\dim X
. When the quadric is mildly degenerate (
k<n-d
), then critical points are in bijection with critical points on
Y=\textnormal{cl}\,\pi(X)
, where
\pi
is the orthogonal projection onto orthogonal complement of
K
. On the other hand, when the quadric is significantly degenerate (
k\geq n-d
), there are two classes of critical points. One class comes from points with zero loss, and the other comes from the branch locus of
\pi
.
Furthermore, Theorem
3.13
gives a formula for the number of critical points arising from the branch locus, expressed in terms of polar degrees, that extends the classical polar-degree sum formula for the EDD.

However, in deep learning the quadric has the form (
18
) and is hence not generic. We exemplified this in Section
4
, where we computed the critical points when
minimizing the mean-squared error loss over deep linear fully-connected neural networks or a lightning self-attention mechanism.
We hope that this sparks interest in both the applied algebraic geometry and deep learning community to further study neuromanifolds and Euclidean distance degrees for quadrics of the form (
18
).
