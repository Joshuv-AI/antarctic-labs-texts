Evaluation of readmissions following elective colorectal surgery: a quality improvement initiative

Introduction

Hospital readmission rates are increasingly recognised as a key indicator of healthcare quality and surgical outcomes. In the context of elective colorectal surgery, unplanned readmissions can result in increased healthcare costs, patient dissatisfaction and potential medicolegal consequences.1 The UK’s Getting It Right First Time (GIRFT) programme reports national 30-day readmission benchmarks of 6.1% for elective colonic resections and 8.8% for elective rectal cancer resections, emphasising the importance of monitoring postoperative outcomes and embedding continuous quality improvement within surgical practice.2

At Whiston Hospital, a tertiary National Health Service (NHS) Trust, elective colorectal resections represent a significant component of the surgical workload. During the 2024 surgical governance review, internal performance dashboards and NHS Model Health System (MHS) data suggested that the Trust’s readmission rates might be higher than national averages. In addition, feedback from ward teams and post-discharge reviews highlighted recurrent unplanned readmissions for potentially preventable complications such as urinary retention and wound-related issues. These findings raised concerns about perioperative optimisation, discharge processes and follow-up strategies, prompting a structured quality improvement initiative.3 4

This quality improvement project (QIP) aimed to evaluate the 30-day readmission rate following elective colorectal resections at our centre and to compare local outcomes with national benchmarks. Secondary aims included identifying common causes for readmission and suggesting system-level interventions to enhance perioperative care, patient safety and surgical outcomes.

Methods

Study design and setting

This was a retrospective observational QIP conducted in the Department of General Surgery at Whiston Hospital, a tertiary care NHS Trust in the UK. The study aimed to evaluate 30-day readmissions following elective colorectal resections and benchmark local outcomes against national GIRFT metrics.

Study period and population

The study included all patients who underwent elective colorectal resections between July and September 2024. Patients were identified using operative theatre records and electronic admission logs. A total of 28 consecutive patients were included in the analysis.

Adult patients undergoing elective colorectal resections, including right hemicolectomy, left hemicolectomy, high anterior resection and low anterior resection.

Readmission was defined as any unplanned hospital admission within 30 days of discharge following the index operation. Each readmission was reviewed in detail, with relevant clinical notes, imaging, microbiology and operative findings assessed to determine the underlying cause.

Benchmarking and outcome measures

The primary outcome was the 30-day readmission rate following elective colorectal resections. This was compared against the GIRFT national benchmarks of 6.1% for elective colonic resections and 8.8% for elective rectal cancer resections.2 Secondary outcomes included the identification of common causes for readmission and potential system-level interventions to reduce preventable postoperative readmissions.

Patient and public involvement

Patients and members of the public were not involved in the design, conduct, reporting or dissemination of this QIP. The project involved retrospective analysis of anonymised institutional data and was undertaken as part of ongoing service evaluation within the Trust.

Results

Study cohort

Between July and September 2024 (Quarter 2, 2024–2025), a total of 28 patients underwent elective colorectal resections at Whiston Hospital. The mean age of the cohort was 61±14 years, with a male-to-female ratio of 17:11 (61% male). All procedures were performed electively under consultant supervision and included high anterior resections, low anterior resections and other segmental colorectal resections (Figure 1). This audit included all patients undergoing elective colorectal resections during the study period, regardless of underlying pathology. While national GIRFT and MHS benchmarks report readmissions following elective cancer resections only, local data encompassed both malignant and benign indications. This may account for minor differences when comparing local and national figures. This inclusion of both benign and malignant resections provides a realistic overview of local elective colorectal activity and serves as a baseline for future cancer-specific benchmarking.

Length of stay

The median length of stay (LOS) during the index admission was 6 days (range: 3–12 days). There was no significant variation in LOS by gender or procedure type.

Readmission rate

Out of 28 patients, 4 were readmitted within 30 days of discharge, giving a 30-day readmission rate of 14.2%. This exceeded the GIRFT national benchmarks of 6.1% for elective colonic resections and 8.8% for rectal cancer resections. These trends suggest that postoperative complications, comorbid status and possibly insufficient early recovery contribute to higher-than-expected readmission rates.

MHS data for July–September 2024 reported 30-day emergency readmission rates of 12.6% for elective colonic cancer resections (benchmark 6.1%) and 17.5% for elective rectal cancer resections (benchmark 8.8%). These figures are broadly consistent with the locally audited 14.2% readmission rate across all elective colorectal resections. The variation between local audit and MHS data likely reflects differences in case definition and coding methodology. The MHS uses Hospital Episode Statistics (HES) data grouped under Healthcare Resource Group codes, which capture only elective cancer resections for national reporting, whereas the local audit included both benign and malignant indications.

Subsequent monitoring through the MHS during October–December 2024 demonstrated readmission rates of 13.1% for colonic cancer and 17.6% for rectal cancer resections. While these rates remain above national benchmarks, the stability across consecutive quarters indicates a consistent baseline from which to evaluate the impact of ongoing quality improvement measures such as enhanced recovery pathway compliance, strengthened discharge planning and early post-discharge review. Continued use of the MHS as a monitoring tool will support sustainability and data-driven refinement of perioperative processes.

Discussion

This QIP identified a 30-day readmission rate of 14.2% following elective colorectal resections at our institution—higher than both the national GIRFT benchmarks (6.1% for colonic and 8.8% for rectal resections) and the optimal performance deciles for colon and rectal resections.2 This positions our Trust in the third to fourth quartile nationally, according to the current GIRFT distribution metrics (Figures2 3), reinforcing the need for targeted improvements in perioperative care.

The most common causes of readmission in our cohort were urinary catheter complications, port-site hernia, abdominal collections suggestive of anastomotic leak, postoperative gastrointestinal symptoms without evidence of leak. These findings align with previous literature where infectious complications, anastomosis related issues and surgical site concerns remain leading contributors to early readmission.5 6 Importantly, all readmitted patients had a length of stay ≤6 days, highlighting a potential risk associated with early discharge protocols, particularly in higher-risk patients.7 Similar observations have been described in studies evaluating the balance between enhanced recovery targets and safe discharge criteria. The predominance of robotic anterior resections among readmitted cases may reflect the greater technical complexity of rectal cancer surgery, although the small cohort limits procedural inference.

Impact of ERAS and prehabilitation

Multiple studies have demonstrated the effectiveness of Enhanced Recovery After Surgery (ERAS) protocols in reducing complications and readmission.8 Our current ERAS adherence was variable, and standardised documentation (eg, an ERAS booklet) was not uniformly in place. Likewise, prehabilitation—encompassing nutritional, physical and psychological optimisation—has been shown to improve outcomes in colorectal surgery6 but was not routinely available during the study period. Addressing these gaps formed a key focus for subsequent improvement work.9

Benchmarking and quality monitoring

GIRFT benchmarking provides a transparent and powerful tool for comparing institutional performance. Unlike GIRFT datasets, which include cancer resections only, our audit cohort included all elective colorectal resections. This broader inclusion may partially explain the higher readmission rate observed locally. As our rate surpasses peer and regional medians (15.0% for rectal cancer, 10.6% for colon), our findings warrant institutional reflection and action. MHS data for consecutive quarters (July–December 2024) demonstrated stable readmission rates—12.6% to 13.1% for colonic and 17.5% to 17.6% for rectal resections—indicating consistent baseline performance and providing a platform for ongoing quality monitoring. These trends are illustrated in figures2 3, which show our Trust’s comparative national performance across both quarters, demonstrating sustained oversight through the MHS framework.

Impact and sustainability

Although a formal re-audit has not yet been completed, the interventions introduced following this QIP have been embedded into routine colorectal practice. ERAS compliance monitoring and discharge checklist audits are now performed quarterly as part of the Trust’s surgical governance programme. Preliminary MHS data for the subsequent quarter (October–December 2024) demonstrated stable readmission rates for both colonic and rectal resections, suggesting early consistency in performance. As shown in onlinesupplemental figures 4 5, readmission rates remained stable in Quarter 3 (October–December 2024), supporting early signs of sustained improvement and validating the impact of ongoing quality initiatives. The continuation of virtual post-discharge follow-up and prehabilitation pilot schemes will support sustainable improvement, with a full re-audit planned to assess long-term outcomes and effectiveness of these measures.

Conclusion

This QIP identified a 30-day readmission rate of 14.2% following elective colorectal resections at our institution—substantially higher than national GIRFT benchmarks (6.1% for colonic and 8.8% for rectal resections). Readmissions were primarily driven by postoperative complications including catheter-related problems, port-site hernias and intra-abdominal collections. Early discharge in high-risk patients may also have contributed to unplanned readmissions.

In response, we introduced targeted interventions including enhanced ERAS adherence, prehabilitation pilot programmes and virtual post-discharge follow-up, alongside revised discharge protocols. These changes were low-cost and easily integrated into existing workflows, offering sustainable avenues for improvement.

While limited by a small sample size and retrospective design, this QIP provides actionable insight into readmission drivers and reinforces the value of benchmarking against national datasets. Ongoing efforts will focus on expanding data collection, incorporating formal risk stratification and completing the audit cycle to assess the impact of implemented changes.

Ultimately, this project demonstrates that even small-scale, local quality initiatives—when grounded in national data and focused on practical interventions—can serve as meaningful catalysts for improving surgical outcomes.
