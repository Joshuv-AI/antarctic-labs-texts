Gender and power dynamics in three types of injection partnerships among young women in rural Appalachia
Authors: Cheyenne R Wagi, Jaelyn Murphy, Madison N Enderle, Hannah Piscalko, Kathleen L Egan, Christina S Meade, Bridget Freisthler, April M Young, Angela Estadt, Kathryn E Lancaster
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundYoung women who inject drugs (YWID) face heightened risks for HIV, hepatitis C, and overdose that are shaped by the relational contexts in which they inject. Injection partnerships, defined as dyadic relationships between people who inject together, remain understudied beyond male–female intimate pairs.MethodsWe conducted 26 semi-structured interviews with YWID (ages 18–35) in rural Appalachian Ohio to explore dynamics within three partnerships types: dual partnerships with male intimate partners, family partnerships, and friend partnerships, with attention to female peer partnerships as a distinct subset. Guided by the Theory of Gender and Power and Bourdieu’s concept of cultural capital, we analysed how access to resources, technical skills, and emotional bonds shaped power and risk across these relationships.ResultsDual partnerships concentrated men’s control over money, equipment, and injecting skills, limiting women’s ability to negotiate safer practices and at times exposed them to violence. Family partnerships were pragmatic and offered little emotional or harm reduction support. Friend partnerships, particularly between female peers, redistributed injecting skills as care rather than control, fostering mutual protection but also normalizing distributive syringe sharing.ConclusionsFindings highlight female peer partnerships as a promising but underutilized context for harm reduction interventions tailored to YWID in rural settings.Keywords: Young women, Injection drug use, Gender and power, Rural Appalachia, Harm reduction

Introduction

The number of young women who inject drugs (YWID) is increasing; however, harm reduction and treatment strategies remain largely designed for men or delivered through male networks [1, 2]. Women are often reached secondarily, through outreach to their male partners, which can leave them feeling unwelcome or unsafe in drug treatment and harm reduction programs [3–5]. YWID also encounter barriers tied to caregiving responsibilities, stigma, and fear of child custody loss, limiting both their availability and willingness to engage in services [1, 3, 5]. Compared to men, women report greater difficulties in accessing sterile equipment and are more likely to share injecting equipment [6], often in the context of intimate partner violence or transactional sex [7–11]. These structural and relational inequities heighten women’s risks for HIV infection, hepatitis C, and overdose [12].

Injection partnerships, dyadic relationships between people who inject together, are a key context in which these risks unfold. Prior research has primarily examined dual partnerships between intimate or sexual partners [13], usually women with male partners. Studies show that women in dual partnerships are more likely to use a syringe or needle after their partner, rely on their partner for injection, and engage in daily shared injecting routines [14]. They also have elevated odds of both receptive and distributive needle sharing compared to men [15]. Yet, while dual partnerships have received attention, much less is known about other forms of injection partnerships. Limited evidence suggests women also inject with family members (e.g., siblings, cousins, parents) and with friends, including other women, where they may exchange assistance with injecting or safeguard one another against harms such as abscesses or overdose [16]. Comparative evidence across these partnership types, particularly among young women, remains scarce.

To interpret how risks and safety are negotiated within partnerships, we draw on the Theory of Gender and Power (TGP) [17] and Bourdieu’s concept of cultural capital [18]. TGP highlights how the sexual division of labor (who secures drugs and equipment), the sexual division of power (control and coercion), and the structure of cathexis (norms and emotional attachments) shape unequal relationships between women and men. Comparatively, cultural capital consists of resources that confer social advantage and, for this population, includes objective capital (possession of injection equipment and drugs), embodied capital (the ability to obtain drugs as influenced by social network, gender, sex worker status), and technical capital (injecting skills). In injection partnerships, the distribution and exchange of these forms of capital determine who holds authority, who provides care, and whose needs are prioritized [19, 20]. For example, a male partner’s greater access to equipment and technical injecting skills may reinforce his control, while among female friends these same skills may reduce harm. Integrating TGP with cultural capital allows us to illuminate not only gendered inequities, but also how different relational contexts redistribute or reinforce power.

This study addresses the gap in understanding of injection partnerships beyond male–female dual relationships. Through semi-structured interviews with YWID in rural Appalachia, we examine the dynamics of three types of partnerships: dual partnerships with male intimate partners, family partnerships, and friend partnerships, with attention to female peer partnerships as a distinct subset. Our analysis explores how access to resources, technical skills, and emotional bonds shape power dynamics and injection practices across these partnership types. By comparing these contexts, we aim to advance scholarship on gender, power, and cultural capital in drug use, and to inform harm reduction strategies that recognize relational contexts as critical sites for intervention to improve outcomes in YWID.

Materials and methods

Aim, design, setting

The aim of the study was to learn more about the experiences of YWID related to injection partnerships through semi-structured interviews in rural Appalachia Ohio. Of note, due to factors such as increased overdose deaths and unemployment, this geographic setting was estimated to be in the top 5% of counties most vulnerable to HIV and hepatitis C outbreaks [21].

Sample

Participants were recruited between June 2021 and June 2022 using multiple strategies, including emails, phone calls, text messages, and in‑person outreach at a university‑based research office and a local syringe services program (SSP). Recruitment initially focused on recontacting eligible participants from the ongoing National Rural Opioid Initiative (NROI) cohort studies, with rurality defined using the Health Resources and Services Administration “Am I Rural” classification [22, 23]. Interviews were conducted in person and over the phone between June 2021 and June 2022. Each participant received a $35 gift card for completing the interview as reimbursement for their time dedicated to this study.

Data collection

Semi-structured interviews were conducted with 26 YWID in rural Appalachian Ohio. Participants were asked about the first time they used injection drugs and their current injection drug use, focusing on the people with whom they use regularly. Questions sought to understand the participants’ history with injection drugs, relationships with other people who inject drugs, and the power dynamics in those relationships.

The interviews were deeply personal accounts in which the participants wove together a history of their experiences related to drug use, highlighting the important people in their stories Participant responses were often characterized by detailed contextualization, with participants offering comprehensive narratives beyond the scope of the initial questions. Responses often intertwined present day and past, requiring disentangling the timelines presented by the participants. All participants were asked to share experiences of initiating injection drug use, their relationship(s) with those present when they injected the first time, current drug use, and current injection partnerships. This study was approved by the Ohio State University IRB (#2019B0073, approved 2/25/2019). Prior to data collection, research staff obtained informed consent from participants.

Analysis

The codebook was developed using extant codes from the Theory of Gender and Power Framework [17, 24], as well as inductive codes developed using grounded theory [25]. Three coders (CW, AW, RM) coded the interviews using ATLAS.ti 24. CW, an experienced qualitative researcher, trained AW and RM on coding methods. The coders co-coded interviews until consensus was reached and no new codes emerged, which took five interviews to achieve. The coders then coded independently, meeting as needed to discuss questions and findings until all interviews were coded. CW led the analysis using Template Analysis [26], reviewing the results with KL to identify patterns and themes.

We analysed gender and power dynamics, including differences in needle sharing and HIV/Hepatitis C Virus (HCV) status discussions among partnership types (friends, family members, and dual partners), power dynamics within these different partnerships, and overdose as a case example to demonstrate gender and power dynamics across injection drug use partnerships. Dual partnerships are defined as partnerships between a participant and a male with whom she injects regularly and has a sexual/romantic relationship. Family partnerships are defined as partnerships between the participant and a family member with whom she injects regularly. Friend partnerships are defined as partnerships between the participant and a person who is both non-family and non-significant other with whom she injects regularly. A subcategory of friend partnerships was identified as female peer partnerships, and these are defined as a partnership between the participant and a female friend with whom she injects regularly.

Results

Partnership types

Participants described three main injection partnership types: dual partners (male sexual and injection partners), family partners (siblings, cousins, or other relatives), and friend partners (non-family, often female peers) (Table 1). These partnership types emerged organically from the data and were not predefined. Several described more than one current partner. Of the 26 YWID interviewed, 12 reported dual partners, six had friend partners, and five reported family partners. Friendships between women emerged as an important but underexplored partnership type. In general, family ties reflected convenience, friendships reflected trust and mutual support, and dual partnerships were the most complex, marked by both support and control (Table 2). Male–female dual partnerships

In dual partnerships YWID often described relationships in which men had greater objective (equipment, money) and technical capital (injecting skills), reinforcing women’s dependence on them. Division of labor added another layer of inequality: men often controlled its use even when women generated income. This dynamic mirrored women’s constrained ability to negotiate safer sex, when reliant on men for injecting or equipment, they had limited power to insist on sterile syringes or safer practices. Women who built embodied or technical capital (e.g., injecting themselves) gained partial agency, but broader gendered power structures constrained their autonomy.

Relational context

Dual partnerships were characterized by overlapping intimacy, support, dependence, and abuse. Women described these relationships as both supportive and fraught with conflict:

‘I don't even know where to start with that one. We started using pain pills together first. And then like down the road, we used heroin and ice together. He would - like that he'd always say - we'd fight because he would always say, ‘Oh, you got more than me.’ Or like, I don't know. It was stupid. Like just stupid stuff. He would fight over stupid stuff. Yes, we used after each other. We only had one needle at the time, so we both used it.’

Needle sharing and injection support

Needle sharing was normalized, often tied to women’s reliance on male partners to inject them. Men’s technical capital often positioned them as gatekeepers of when and how women injected. Some sought independence by teaching themselves to inject:

‘I: How often would you say that you end up sharing needles? P: Probably Well, basically, all the time. Because if I don't have one, he has one. If he doesn't have one, I have one. So, it's usually just one needle.’ (P3).

‘He injected me until I figured out how to do it myself… Because I felt like I wasn't getting what I was supposed to be getting, and he was being greedy. And I didn't want to rely on someone else to do what I was doing.’ (P25).

Further, some women had limited access to syringe services as a result of low embodied capital due to their status as mothers. Women with children held fear that being seen accessing harm reduction services may result in loss of child custody, resulting in going without these services or relying on others to provide clean needles.

‘When the Coronavirus started getting big, they started doing the [syringe] exchange outside. So for me, I went through a lot with losing my kids and getting them back…I would have other people go for me because it was outside literally broad daylight outside…I: You were scared of other people seeing you? P: Yeah. It’s literally like walking distance from our courthouse. I: And then was your biggest fear then people seeing you and then it having a negative impact on you getting your children back? P: Yep.’ (P13).

HCV and HIV risk

Partners sometimes discussed HIV or HCV, but conversations rarely translated into testing or reduced risk:

‘P: We need to be careful and be clean about it… of course there’s a risk, it’s a major concern. I: And you have been tested for hepatitis C, correct? P12: Not smart, no. I: You haven't, okay. Has your boyfriend? P12: I'm not sure.”’ (P12).

Division of labor

How money and drugs were obtained shaped women’s power within dual partnerships. When both partners contributed financially, relationships were sometimes described as ‘teamwork’:

‘We don’t look at it as, like, splitting it…we just contribute where we can contribute.’ (P22).

If men primarily provided money and secured drugs, women often perceived the relationship as balanced despite their limited role:

‘I: When it came down to get meth, who would get it? P: Mainly him… I: Did you guys just split the meth when you got it? P21: Yes. He would give me some and if I wanted more, he would give me more I: Did you guys both work to get money to pay for meth or was it was it mostly you? P21: Mostly him, actually because I was staying here with my daughter.’ (P21).

By contrast, when women were the main earners, they often described having the least control, with men dictating drug purchases or responding with violence:

‘If he wasn’t able to get high and I didn’t give him the money, I got my brains beat out.’ (P24).

‘P: Well, I worked, I was a server. So, he would come get my tips for me halfway through my shift and bring me back something or whatever. But he didn't really have like a job, job, but he hustled… I: So, would you say that he controlled the money, even though you made it? P: Yeah…When I got with my boyfriend, he was on heroin, I was on ice. And he, instead of coming back with what I wanted, what he wanted to come back with was what he wanted.’ (P25).

Taken together, these accounts show how men’s control over both economic resources and objective and technical capital reinforced gendered power imbalances, while women’s efforts to claim agency (earning income, learning to inject) were often undermined by abuse, manipulation, and their embodied status as mothers.

Family partners

Family partnerships reflected convenience rather than solidarity. Women generally avoided sharing needles with relatives, suggesting protective caution, and thin trust and limited communication restricted emotional support or harm reduction.

Relational context

Family injecting partnerships, most often with sisters, cousins, or uncles, were described as pragmatic rather than supportive. These relationships were shaped by proximity and convenience rather than emotional connection:

‘I: Who would you say you inject with the most? P: Uh well my uncle and my brother’s girlfriend. I: Is there a reason you inject with them the most? P: Uh, they are right next door’ (P20).

Emotional or financial support was rarely noted, and several women described preferring not to inject with family because of judgment or lack of trust:

‘I: Is there anyone you prefer to inject with? P20: Not family. I: Is there a reason for that? P20: They talk shit even though they do it too... I: So, then do you feel in general, do you trust that they would take care of you like if you were to overdose? P20: Nope. I: What do you think they would do? P20: I don't know. They wouldn't help though. I know that.’

Some who did use regularly with relatives described the relationship as limited to injecting itself:

‘My [female] cousin… twice a day. It’s okay.’ (P26).

These accounts suggest that while family ties offered convenience, they lacked the emotional investment or reciprocity in peer partnerships.

Needle sharing & injecting support

Women were cautious about sharing needles with family members, often citing mistrust or hygiene concerns:

‘No, like we keep everything separate… I just don’t feel comfortable.’ (P13).

‘No, God, no, I supplied her with a fresh one, made sure of that… not everybody’s as cautious as I am.’ (P9).

HCV and HIV risk

Discussions of HCV or HIV testing were rare in family contexts, even when occasional sharing occurred:

‘I: Have you guys ever talked about so, when you guys inject, do you guys share needles, correct? P: Occasional. I: Have you guys ever talked about infections like Hepatitis C or HIV? P: No. I: Is that something you're, like, worried about at all? P. No.’ (P26).

Friend partners

Friend partnerships (defined by a participant referring to their injection partner as their friend) were primarily partnerships between two female friends and reflected trust and care, with women sharing resources, emotional support, and injection techniques. Unlike dual partnerships, technical capital was exchanged between female partners as a form of protection rather than control. This redistribution of capital and resources framed female friendships as safer and more equitable, though frequent distributive sharing and assumptions of shared risk heightened vulnerability to HCV infection. Friend partners that were male were often described as previous intimate partners or friends who became intimate partners at a later date.

Relational context

Friend injection partners, often female peers, were described as the most supportive and reciprocal relationships. Women emphasized emotional closeness and mutual aid:

‘She’s kind of honestly my support system… we get together when we’re emotional, we get food together.’ (P16).

Friends also provided practical help with injecting, reframing technical capital as care rather than control:

‘She’ll miss and get abscesses… so I help her do it, because I’m not gonna let her go through that. You’re gonna lose your job. Your family’s gonna know… So, I help her do it.’ (P2).

Needle sharing & injecting support

Distributive needle sharing was common among friends, though women resisted receptive sharing.

‘She [female friend] will use after me. I’m always using a new needle mainly because I’ve already known I’ve got hep C. And she’s already been tested, so now she’s got hep C.’ (P2).

Some emphasized that using a needle after a friend only happened in emergencies:

‘I’ll give them if I have it and they don’t… Will I use a needle after somebody? No… if I was dope sick I would.’ (P16).

HCV and HIV risk

Discussions of HCV or HIV were rare in friend partnerships. One participant disclosed sharing status with a close friend:

‘When I first found out that I had it, I called him,’cause he was my best friend at the time… he told me that he had it too.’ (P16).

Others assumed shared risk, reducing concern about protective practices:

‘Yeah, but if they already have it, then I already have it.’ (P23).

Discussion

In our study of YWID in rural Appalachia, injection partnerships were patterned into three relational forms: male–female dual partnerships, family partnerships, and friend partnerships, with distinct distributions of resources, care, and control. By integrating the Theory of Gender and Power (TGP) with cultural capital (objective, embodied, technical), we were able to clarify how power circulated within these ties and with what consequences for HIV and HCV risk, overdose, and women’s capacity to enact safer practices.

We found that women in our sample often did not have their own injecting equipment and reported greater difficulty acquiring sterile syringes compared to men, consistent with prior studies [5, 6]. Many relied on their male partners to provide equipment, placing them at the subordinate end of unequal relationships where reduced access to sterile supplies heightened risks for HIV and HCV [15]. Similar to women’s limited ability to negotiate condom use in heterosexual relationships [17], we observed that women in dual injection partnerships often lacked the power to negotiate access to sterile needles when dependent on their male partner for injecting, as they were often injected with their partner’s used needle.

Our findings also highlight how embodied capital intersected with the sexual division of labor. Women in dual partnerships reported the least equality when they were primary income earners, but their male partners retained control over drug purchasing. As prior work suggests, when women rely on others to obtain drugs [27], they may risk withdrawal, expectation of sexual services in return, or pressure to use substances they do not typically consume. Our findings that male partners often retained control over purchasing drugs suggest these same dynamics may apply to YWID in our sample.

Technical capital further reinforced inequalities in our sample. Women in dual partnerships commonly relied on men to inject them, a trend consistent with prior literature that this reliance begins at initiation and persists over time [6]. Dependence on a male injector positioned women at risk of overdose or injury if refused, while granting men leverage over when and how women injected. Male partners also controlled dosing and technique, creating opportunities for coercion. In contrast, among female peer partnerships in our study, injection skills were exchanged as care rather than control, with women helping one another to prevent abscesses or reduce overdose risk. These findings demonstrate how women's exchange of cultural capital moves toward harm reduction within peer networks.

Relational contexts shaped the structure of cathexis in important ways. Women in family partnerships generally described these relationships as pragmatic rather than supportive, with little emotional investment or reciprocity. By contrast, peer partnerships in our study were characterized by trust, closeness, and mutual support, often providing both emotional care and safer injecting practices. Dual partnerships were more complex, marked by intimacy, abuse, and manipulation, reflecting prior findings on male partners’ control in both sexual and drug-related practices [1].

Few harm reduction interventions currently leverage peer-to-peer injection partnerships [28, 29], despite evidence of their potential. Existing peer-led programs with people who inject drugs (PWID) have improved knowledge of safer practices, enhanced empowerment, and reduced barriers to services [4, 30]. Our findings suggest that female peer partnerships could be an underutilized resource for safer injecting environments. Women in our sample described helping one another with safe injecting, responding to overdose, and providing emotional support, activities that could be formalized through peer-to-peer models. Peer-driven strategies may be especially relevant for YWID, who constitute a unique culture within PWID shaped by age, gender, and experiences of violence and caregiving responsibilities [1, 31].

In our study, women also reported fear of repercussions for publicly accessing harm reduction services, preferring private or women-centered contexts for learning and support, which aligns with prior findings that women are more likely to engage in educational activities in private settings and with other women, while men are more easily reached in public outreach [4, 5]. Peer-led interventions have been effective in sensitive domains such as sex work and HIV prevention, improving uptake of HIV testing and linkage to care [32–35]. Extending such models to YWID could address structural barriers to harm reduction, including stigma, childcare responsibilities, and fear of child custody loss [36–38].

Although these data were collected in 2021, subsequent expansions in harm reduction services (e.g., naloxone distribution and syringe services) and shifts in drug supply may have influenced current patterns of risk and service engagement [39, 40]. However, persistent structural barriers in rural settings suggest that the gendered dynamics within injection partnerships identified here likely remain relevant [6, 7, 12, 14, 15].

Our study was limited to one rural Appalachian region, which may affect transferability. Within Ohio, the rural region included in this study is marked lower educational attainment, poor housing affordability, higher rates of hepatitis C infection, and higher rates of overdose death than some other regions within the state [41]. While the Appalachian region is often spoken about as a homogenous group, Ohio’s comparatively high rate of drug overdose deaths [42] and their aggressive harm reduction and overdose prevention response [39, 40] may limit generalizability to other states. Recruitment at a syringe exchange increases bias toward women who are accessing harm reduction services; these women may inherently have more agency in their injection partnerships. Data were collected during the COVID-19 pandemic, which slowed recruitment and may have influenced willingness to participate. Nevertheless, our comparative perspective across partnership types provides novel insights into how gender and power dynamics unfold in relational contexts of injection. Future research should seek to identify and test implementation strategies preferred by women, as well as strategies that reduce gender-specific barriers, to increase access to harm reduction and health-related social needs resources within peer-to-peer interventions.

Conclusion

We found that YWID in rural Appalachia engaged in three main types of injection partnerships: dual partnerships with male intimate partners, family partnerships, and peer partnerships. Dual partnerships concentrated men’s control over economic and technical resources, reinforcing women’s subordination and limiting their ability to negotiate safer practices. Family partnerships were largely pragmatic and offered little beyond avoiding the risks of injecting alone. Peer partnerships, especially those between female friends in our study, provided mutual support, shared skills, and protection, though distributive sharing still carried HCV and HIV transmission risk.

Our findings highlight female peer injection partnerships as a promising but underutilized avenue for harm reduction. Leveraging these relationships through peer-led interventions and increasing YWID’s autonomy may increase access to sterile equipment, enhance safer injection practices, and reduce overdose risk. Integrating peer strategies into existing harm reduction infrastructures could align interventions with women’s lived experiences, strengthen feasibility and acceptability of harm reduction education and resources, and expand equitable access to services in rural contexts.
