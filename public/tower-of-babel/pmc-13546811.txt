Gender and Perceived Racial Diversity in Academic Otolaryngology
Authors: Rachel A. Haji, Robin Powszok, Grant Primer, Joyce Jeong, Dina Simon, Krupa R. Patel, Vanessa Stubbs
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTObjectivesTo characterize gender and perceived racial representation among academic otolaryngology faculty in the United States and evaluate associations with academic rank, leadership roles, and research productivity.MethodsThis cross‐sectional study evaluated faculty with professorial titles across 133 Accreditation Council for Graduate Medical Education‐accredited otolaryngology residency programs in the United States. Faculty rosters were collected from program websites between October 2024 and March 2025 and supplemented with publicly available sources. Variables included perceived gender, perceived race, academic rank, leadership roles, years in practice, subspecialty training, and h‐index. Associations were evaluated using chi‐square tests and multivariable ordinal logistic regression. Because demographic variables were assigned from publicly available information rather than self‐identification, perceived race was analyzed only as White versus non‐White and is interpreted as a limited, socially constructed variable.ResultsAmong 1697 faculty, 69.1% were male and 68.8% were perceived as White. Women were more represented in pediatric otolaryngology and laryngology, whereas men were more represented in head and neck surgery, neurotology, and comprehensive otolaryngology (p < 0.001). Women were more likely to be assistant professors and men professors (p < 0.001). Women more often held residency leadership roles (9.7% vs. 5.6%; p = 0.002), whereas men more often held fellowship leadership roles (8.4% vs. 5.3%; p = 0.028) and chair positions (5.0% vs. 2.7%; p = 0.026). Men had more years in practice and higher h‐indices (both p < 0.001). No significant differences by perceived race were observed in academic rank, leadership, or research productivity. In multivariable analysis, years in practice and h‐index independently predicted higher academic rank, whereas gender and perceived race did not.ConclusionAcademic rank was more strongly associated with years in practice and h‐index than with gender or perceived race. Perceived‐race findings should be interpreted cautiously because binary externally assigned categories may obscure within‐group differences.Level of Evidence4

Introduction

The US physician workforce has become more diverse, with increasing numbers of women and individuals from underrepresented racial and ethnic groups entering medicine, but subspecialties like otolaryngology have not kept pace [1]. Prior research links physician diversity to improved healthcare outcomes, cultural competency, and patient satisfaction, particularly among underserved populations [2, 3, 4]. These findings underscore the importance of promoting diversity within academic otolaryngology.

Academic departments help shape the future otolaryngology workforce. Programs that feature diverse faculty and support diversity, equity, and inclusion (DEI) initiatives have been associated with increased gender and racial diversity among residency applicants [5, 6]. Conversely, a lack of visible representation may limit otolaryngology's appeal to underrepresented groups, weakening diversity in the future workforce.

Subspecialty‐level analyses suggest persistent gaps in representation. In 2020, 76.6% of academic laryngologists were non‐Hispanic White and 66% were male [7]. In pediatric otolaryngology, female surgeons have had lower h‐indices, fewer publications, and fewer citations than their male counterparts [8]. In rhinology, the proportion of female trainees pursuing fellowship has plateaued since 2002, and as of 2020, only 13% of members of the American Academy of Facial Plastic and Reconstructive Surgery were women [9, 10].

Leadership roles within otolaryngology mirror these disparities. Across United States and Canadian programs from 2010 to 2020, nearly 80% of residency program directors were male and non‐Hispanic White [11]. Women also remain underrepresented among department chairs and fellowship directors and are less likely to attain full professorships or to match the research productivity of their male colleagues. Some studies suggest that these patterns persist even when accounting for years in practice [12, 13].

While several studies have examined diversity within specific subspecialties and leadership roles, few have assessed diversity at the specialty‐wide level. Limited data show that female representation among otolaryngology faculty increased from 19.7% in 2012 to 23.6% in 2019, yet this growth has not been mirrored in senior ranks or research output [13, 14, 15]. Prior studies have largely focused on gender representation, with perceived race at the specialty‐wide level lagging behind.

To inform ongoing equity efforts, a broader understanding of faculty demographics is needed. This study aims to characterize gender and perceived racial diversity across all academic otolaryngology departments in the United States, with a focus on differences in research productivity, academic advancement, and leadership representation.

Materials and Methods

Data Collection

This cross‐sectional study evaluated faculty demographics across all 133 Accreditation Council for Graduate Medical Education (ACGME)‐accredited otolaryngology residency programs in the US. Eligible faculty were academic otolaryngologists with professorial titles (assistant, associate, full, or emeritus professor) listed on residency program websites. Faculty with alternative titles, such as clinical instructor, part‐time lecturer, or volunteer faculty, were excluded.

Data were collected between October 2024 and March 2025 through systematic review of publicly available program websites. When information was incomplete, secondary sources, including LinkedIn, Doximity, and Castle Connolly, were used to corroborate or supplement faculty details. Collected variables included institutional affiliation, perceived gender, perceived race, academic rank, subspecialty fellowship training, residency and fellowship leadership roles, chair status, years in practice, and h‐index. Perceived gender was categorized as male or female, and perceived race as White or non‐White based on publicly available photographs and names. Two independent reviewers performed these assessments, with disagreements resolved by a third reviewer.

Race was treated as a social construct rather than a biological category. Because publicly available data did not permit determination of self‐identified race, ethnicity, multiracial identity, or within‐group heterogeneity, we use the term “perceived race” to emphasize that classifications reflect external perception rather than self‐identification. To avoid overinterpretation of photographs and names, we did not assign more granular racial or ethnic categories. This approach may introduce misclassification and obscure meaningful subgroup differences; therefore, analyses of perceived race should be considered exploratory and hypothesis‐generating.

Faculty academic characteristics included subspecialty fellowship training (categorized into head and neck surgery, rhinology and skull base surgery, neurotology, laryngology, sleep surgery, facial plastic and reconstructive surgery, pediatric otolaryngology, or comprehensive otolaryngology), departmental chair status, and involvement in residency or fellowship leadership (defined as holding titles such as program director, associate/assistant director, or codirector).

Years in practice were calculated from the year of residency graduation or, when applicable, the year of fellowship completion. Because data collection occurred from October 2024 to March 2025, years in practice were referenced to 2025. Research productivity was quantified using the h‐index, obtained through Scopus by querying each faculty member's name and institutional affiliation. Faculty were excluded from the analysis if no Scopus profile could be identified, if residency or fellowship dates were indeterminable, or if multiple discrepancies in publicly available data precluded confident classification.

Because the study used publicly available data and did not involve patient information, the Rush Research Regulatory Operations Department determined that it was exempt from institutional review board review.

Statistical Analysis

Descriptive statistics were used to summarize faculty characteristics. Categorical variables were reported as counts and percentages, and continuous variables were reported as means with standard deviations or medians with interquartile ranges, as appropriate.

Faculty characteristics were compared by perceived gender and perceived race using χ 2 tests for categorical variables and appropriate parametric or nonparametric tests for continuous variables. All tests were two‐sided, and statistical significance was set at p < 0.05.

A multivariable ordinal logistic regression model was used to identify factors independently associated with higher academic rank. Academic rank was modeled as an ordinal outcome (assistant professor, associate professor, and professor). Perceived gender and perceived race were included as primary explanatory variables, and years in practice and h‐index were included as covariates. Adjusted odds ratios with 95% CIs were calculated. Emeritus faculty were excluded from regression analyses because their rank may not reflect current promotion processes. All statistical analyses were performed using SPSS Statistics, Version 29.02.02 (IBM Corp).

Results

A total of 1697 academic otolaryngologists from 133 Accreditation Council for Graduate Medical Education–accredited otolaryngology residency programs met inclusion criteria. Of these, 1172 (69.1%) were male and 525 (30.9%) were female. Most faculty were perceived as White (1167 [68.8%]), and 530 (31.2%) were perceived as non‐White. The most common subspecialties were head and neck surgery (351 [20.7%]), comprehensive otolaryngology (309 [18.2%]), pediatric otolaryngology (247 [14.6%]), neurotology (246 [14.5%]), and facial plastic and reconstructive surgery (230 [13.6%]). Overall, 689 (40.6%) were assistant professors, 476 (28.0%) were associate professors, and 532 (31.3%) were professors. A total of 117 (6.9%) faculty held residency leadership roles, 126 (7.4%) held fellowship leadership roles, and 73 (4.3%) served as department chairs. The median years in practice were 13 years, and the median h‐index was 11 (Table 1).

Comparison by Perceived Gender

Subspecialty distribution differed by gender (p < 0.001). Men were more highly represented in head and neck surgery (251 [71.5%] vs. 100 [28.5%]), neurotology (177 [71.9%] vs. 69 [28.1%]), and comprehensive otolaryngology (231 [74.8%] vs. 78 [25.2%]). Men also comprised most faculty in rhinology (121 [75.2%] vs. 40 [24.8%]) and facial plastic and reconstructive surgery (165 [71.7%] vs. 65 [28.3%]). Women accounted for a larger share of faculty in pediatric otolaryngology (107 [43.3%]) and laryngology (58 [43.3%]) than in several other subspecialties, although men remained the majority in both groups. Sleep surgery was the least represented subspecialty overall (19 [1.1%]) (Table 2).

Comparison by Perceived Race

Differences by perceived race were limited. Faculty perceived as White had more years in practice than faculty perceived as non‐White (median, 14 vs. 12 years; p < 0.001). However, h‐index did not differ between groups (median, 11 vs. 11; p = 0.991). No statistically significant differences by perceived race were observed in subspecialty training (p = 0.055), academic rank (p = 0.094), or residency leadership (79/117 [67.5%] White vs. 38/117 [32.5%] non‐White; p = 0.763), fellowship leadership (78/126 [61.9%] White vs. 48/126 [38.1%] non‐White; p = 0.084), or chair status (50/73 [68.5%] White vs. 23/73 [31.5%] non‐White; p = 0.959) (Table 3).

Discussion

In this cross‐sectional study, gender differences were observed in academic rank and leadership representation in unadjusted analyses. After adjustment, gender was not independently associated with academic rank, with years in practice and research productivity being the most prominent predictors. These findings should not be interpreted as evidence that gender is unrelated to promotion. Rather, years in practice and the h‐index may themselves reflect cumulative differences in mentorship, sponsorship, protected research time, funding, publication opportunities, and institutional support. Adjustment for these variables may account for downstream manifestations of structural differences rather than eliminating their influence.

These concepts build on existing literature demonstrating that gender disparities remain evident across surgical disciplines, including otolaryngology [2, 5, 6, 7]. DeSisto et al. further emphasized that women remain underrepresented in senior academic and leadership roles in otolaryngology, noting that visibility of role models is essential to sustaining progress toward equity [16]. One potential contributor is disproportionate participation by women in roles that are essential to departmental operations but variably valued in promotion. This pattern is consistent with broader literature showing that women in academic medicine are more likely to take on “non‐promotable” tasks—activities that are essential to institutional function but variably recognized in promotion criteria. Women are not only more likely to volunteer for such tasks but are also more often asked to take them on, reinforcing a cycle of undervalued labor that may siphon time away from scholarship [17]. Ladlie et al. corroborated these patterns in otolaryngology specifically, demonstrating persistent gender disparities in scholarly productivity, funding, and promotion, despite increases in the number of women entering the specialty [18]. Numerous other studies have shown that women in academic medicine spend significantly more time on administrative tasks such as electronic medical record (EMR) documentation, even after adjusting for clinical volume [19, 20]. This administrative load, coupled with a greater likelihood of service‐oriented roles, can leave less protected time for research and scholarly activity [21, 22].

While nontraditional measures of scholarship such as teaching activity and educational leadership are increasingly being used in promotion review, their implementation is institution‐dependent and may not fully offset cumulative differences in research productivity or national visibility. Outside the hospital, female physicians are also more likely to shoulder the primary responsibility for domestic tasks, including childcare, meal preparation, and household management, especially when partnered with another full‐time professional [23]. Together, these factors should be understood as structural barriers that disproportionately divert women from promotable activities, rather than as individual explanations for differences in academic advancement.

Increasing recruitment of women may partly explain current senior‐rank disparities through a generational cohort effect, but this explanation is incomplete if promotion systems continue to differentially reward traditional scholarly productivity. The 2025 Otolaryngology Workforce Report indeed supports the interpretation that current senior‐rank differences may partly reflect the specialty's generational workforce composition. Among practicing otolaryngologists, 64% of those aged 30–39 were male and 36% were female, compared with 87% male and 13% female among those aged 50–59 and 100% male among those aged 60–69 [24]. These data suggest that representation at senior academic ranks is likely to improve as younger, more gender‐diverse cohorts progress through the workforce; however, such progress should not be assumed to occur automatically. If promotion systems continue to prioritize traditional productivity metrics while service, education, mentorship, and caregiving burdens remain unevenly distributed, disparities may persist despite a more diverse pipeline.

In contrast to the gender findings, we observed no significant differences in promotion, leadership, or research productivity by perceived race in either unadjusted or adjusted analyses. This finding diverges from prior studies on racial disparities in academic otolaryngology, which have revealed severe underrepresentation in clinical research leaders [17]. It also differs from recent AAMC‐linked analyses of academic medicine demonstrating that promotion patterns vary substantially by specific race, ethnicity, and gender intersections, with some groups experiencing lower promotion rates than others [25]. Possible explanations include cohort composition, limited racial categorization, limited power to detect subgroup differences, and the use of perceived rather than self‐identified race. The White versus non‐White categorization was used because self‐identified race and ethnicity were not available from public sources for individual faculty members, but this approach may mask meaningful heterogeneity among Asian, Black, Hispanic or Latino, Middle Eastern or North African, multiracial, and other faculty groups. For example, Black individuals remain underrepresented among otolaryngology residents relative to their applicant pool, with no significant changes in the racial or ethnic composition of applicants from 2016 to 2021 [26].

While perceived race was not independently associated with academic rank, this finding may reflect the relatively early career stage of many non‐White faculty in our cohort. Increased attention to DEI efforts in recent years may have led to improved racial representation in junior academic ranks, but these individuals have not yet reached the point in their careers where promotion disparities would become apparent. Therefore, more recent diversification of junior faculty may not have yet translated into differences at senior ranks [25, 26, 27, 28]. Longitudinal data using self‐identified demographic categories are therefore needed to evaluate whether racial equity is sustained as these cohorts advance through the academic hierarchy.

This analysis does not capture barriers to entry or attrition, both of which disproportionately affect groups underrepresented in medicine [25, 29]. As a result, our sample may not reflect individuals who left academia before promotion or who were excluded from academic pathways altogether. Unmeasured factors such as implicit bias in authorship, funding decisions, and access to collaborative networks may influence academic advancement in ways not accounted for in this model. Kim et al. highlighted that such systemic limitations disproportionately hinder faculty advancement for underrepresented groups in otolaryngology, with promotion and leadership inequities persisting despite increased diversity at the entry level [30]. Finally, limited subgroup power and the use of perceived rather than self‐reported race may have reduced our ability to detect meaningful disparities.

Prior studies have shown that access to mentorship is strongly associated with academic advancement, leadership attainment, and retention, particularly for women and underrepresented groups [11, 12, 15]. Yet despite their importance, these support systems remain inconsistently available, informally distributed, and susceptible to the same systemic biases that affect promotion [25]. Establishing structured, equitable mentorship pathways may help mitigate the cumulative disadvantages that women and faculty from groups underrepresented in medicine face and provide a clearer, more supported route to advancement.

To our knowledge, this is among the largest national analyses of academic otolaryngologists across faculty ranks, institutions, and subspecialties. By incorporating both gender and perceived race as variables alongside traditional metrics of academic productivity, we provide a broad, reproducible assessment of equity in faculty advancement. The inclusion of all ACGME‐accredited otolaryngology programs enhances the generalizability of our findings.

These findings may inform departmental efforts to improve transparency in promotion, expand access to mentorship and sponsorship, and support inclusive advancement pathways. These efforts should include regular assessment of promotion criteria and outcomes to ensure accountability and fairness across all stages of career development. Policies that support part‐time or non‐traditional FTE arrangements, as well as family‐friendly institutional practices, may also help mitigate barriers that disproportionately affect women and underrepresented faculty. Such strategies are essential not only for advancing equity within the profession, but also for strengthening the specialty's capacity to serve an increasingly diverse patient population. These priorities align with the stated objectives of the AAO‐HNS Diversity, Equity, and Inclusion Task Force [31].

These findings also raise the question of how quickly change should be expected. If the observed differences are primarily due to a generational gap in the otolaryngology workforce, increasing representation among residents and early‐career faculty should gradually narrow senior‐rank disparities over future promotion cycles. However, if structural and cultural factors influencing service, sponsorship, research productivity, and caregiving remain unchanged, improvements in recruitment alone may be insufficient to achieve equity at senior academic ranks. Future studies should therefore evaluate not only representation by rank, but also promotion timelines, retention, access to sponsorship, distribution of educational and service roles, and institutional promotion criteria.

Limitations

This study has several limitations. The use of publicly available data introduces potential inaccuracies in faculty rank, fellowship status, and publication attribution. In addition, perceived rather than self‐identified gender and race may have introduced misclassification. Race was analyzed only as White versus non‐White because more granular self‐identified race and ethnicity data were unavailable; this binary classification lacks biological validity, reflects social perception rather than identity, and may mask important differences among racial and ethnic groups. The cross‐sectional design restricts our ability to assess promotion trajectories over time or determine whether current junior faculty diversity will translate into senior leadership representation. In addition, h‐index does not capture other dimensions of academic contribution, including mentorship, educational leadership, grant funding, clinical excellence, sponsorship, or service that may be valued differently across institutions.

Conclusion

In this cross‐sectional study of academic otolaryngology faculty, years in practice and h‐index were more strongly associated with academic rank than gender or perceived race. Although gender differences in senior rank were evident in unadjusted analyses, these differences were attenuated after adjustment for career duration and scholarly productivity. No significant differences by perceived race were observed; however, these findings should be interpreted cautiously because perceived binary racial categorization may obscure subgroup‐specific disparities. Longitudinal studies using self‐identified demographic data are needed to clarify promotion trajectories and identify institutional practices that support equitable advancement.
