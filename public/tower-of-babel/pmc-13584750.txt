Vestibular Paroxysmia Coexisting With Meige Syndrome: A Case Report
Authors: Yanan Ding, Gendi Yin, Yongqiang Dai, Feng Qin, Xiangli Zeng, Qiling Huang, Yongqi Li
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTVP and MS are distinct clinical entities arising from the peripheral vestibular system and central extrapyramidal pathways, respectively. We report a rare case of a 69‐year‐old woman with coexistence of VP and MS. Individualized pharmacotherapy resolved vertigo and markedly improved blepharospasm. MRI and multidisciplinary collaboration were key.

Key Clinical MessageThis case highlights that vestibular paroxysmia (VP) and Meige syndrome (MS), though pathophysiologically distinct, can coexist. High‐resolution MRI (Magnetic Resonance Imaging) is essential for diagnosing neurovascular conflict in brief recurrent vertigo. Multidisciplinary collaboration enables accurate dual diagnosis and effective tailored pharmacotherapy.

Introduction

Vestibular paroxysmia (VP) is a neurovascular compression syndrome characterized by brief, recurrent dizziness caused by vascular cross‐compression of the eighth cranial nerve [1, 2]. The diagnostic criteria, formalized by the Bárány Society, emphasize short‐lasting, stereotypical episodes that may respond to anticonvulsants [3].

Meige syndrome (MS) is a segmental craniocervical dystonia, typically combining blepharospasm (involuntary eyelid closure) with oromandibular dystonia [4]. It is considered a disorder of the basal ganglia, and its treatment primarily involves botulinum toxin injections, oral medications, or deep brain stimulation [5].

The pathophysiological mechanisms of VP (peripheral) and MS (central) are distinct. While neurovascular conflict is a recognized cause of hemifacial spasm and trigeminal neuralgia, its co‐occurrence with movement disorders such as MS is scarcely reported. We present a rare case of VP coexisting with MS, successfully treated this case through a structured diagnostic workup and multidisciplinary collaboration.

Case History/Examination

A 69‐year‐old female was admitted in May 2024 due to “recurrent vertigo for over 10 days.” The vertigo was spontaneous, rotational, lasting about 5 min per episode, and occurred 7–8 times daily, accompanied by bilateral “rumbling” tinnitus.

Her history revealed similar but infrequent vertigo spells (1–2 times annually) for the past 10 years. Notably, for the same duration, she had developed persistent, involuntary bilateral blepharospasm with dry eye sensation, which disappeared during sleep. Previous treatments for blepharospasm at local hospitals were unsuccessful. She had a history of right‐eye cataract surgery in 2011.

We first conducted a preliminary physical examination on the patient. The patient was alert and oriented. The cranial nerve examination revealed no abnormalities, including symmetrical facial wrinkles and nasolabial folds. Motor and sensory examinations revealed no deficits. Coordination tests (finger‐to‐nose and rapid alternating movements) were normal. No pathological reflexes were elicited. Vestibular Examination: No spontaneous or positional nystagmus was observed. The Romberg test and Unterberger stepping test were negative. Ophthalmological Examination: Frequent and forceful blinking was observed. The right eye had an intraocular lens in situ, and the left lens showed mild opacity. Funduscopy revealed thin retinal vessels without hemorrhages or exudates.

Investigations

MRI revealed a neurovascular conflict between the right labyrinthine artery and the cisternal segment of the right vestibulocochlear nerve on a 3 T MRI of the brain and internal auditory canals (Figure 1A,B). Regarding audiometry, pure‐tone audiometry showed bilateral high‐frequency sensorineural hearing loss (Figure 2A). As for vestibular function tests, the Auditory Brainstem Response (ABR) was within normal limits (Figure 2B), and the video Head Impulse Test (vHIT) showed normal gains for all semicircular canals without central signs (Figure 2C). Likewise, the Videonystagmography (VNG) examination was unremarkable, as detailed in Figure S1. Furthermore, the electrocochleography (ECochG) results showed fair bilateral waveform differentiation and repeatability, with notable interaural asymmetry in—SP/AP ratios (right ear area ratio 4.664 vs. left 1.424; right ear amplitude ratio 0.406 vs. left 0.341) (Figure 2D). Finally, cervical vestibular evoked myogenic potentials (cVEMP) were present bilaterally, but ocular vestibular evoked myogenic potentials (oVEMP) were absent at 500 and 1000 Hz in both ears (Figure 2E,F).

Outcome and Follow‐Up

Initial empiric therapy for peripheral vertigo (including betahistine, mecobalamin, and postauricular injections of lidocaine with methylprednisolone) provided minimal relief. A neurology consultation suspected VP. Treatment was switched to flunarizine 5 mg nightly and topiramate 25 mg twice daily, which led to the complete cessation of vertigo attacks during hospitalization.

To address the debilitating blepharospasm, a multidisciplinary team (MDT) involving Neurology, Ophthalmology, and Neurosurgery was convened. After excluding primary ocular causes, a diagnosis of Meige Syndrome (blepharospasm‐predominant) was confirmed. The patient was started on clonazepam 0.5 mg nightly and haloperidol 1 mg twice daily. Within 1 week, the blepharospasm significantly improved. At the 3‐month follow‐up, the patient remained free of vertigo, and the blepharospasm was well controlled, and quality of life had improved significantly. At the 6‐month follow‐up (by phone), the patient confirmed that the blepharospasm remained well controlled and there had been no recurrence of vertigo episodes.

Discussion

We report a rare case of the coincidental occurrence of VP and MS in a single patient. This presentation underscores the necessity for a broad differential diagnosis when patients exhibit symptoms spanning multiple neurological domains. This case requires differential diagnosis from Meniere's disease. The patient's cochleogram shows an elevated summating potential‐to‐action potential amplitude ratio (0.406) on the right side. However, the patient does not exhibit symptoms of fluctuating hearing loss, which rules out a diagnosis of Meniere's disease. Based on the symmetrical, persistent, and spasmodic nature of the patient's frequent bilateral involuntary blepharospasm, the absence of a history of drug exposure, and normal neurological and imaging findings, conditions such as hemifacial spasm, tic disorder, tardive dyskinesia, and Parkinson's disease were ruled out. Following a multidisciplinary evaluation by specialists in ophthalmology, neurology, and neurosurgery, the patient was ultimately diagnosed with Meige syndrome.

The diagnosis of VP in our patient was strongly supported by the classic clinical picture of brief, frequent, and stereotyped vertigo spells and the radiological evidence of neurovascular conflict on MRI [3]. The absence of oVEMP responses, potentially indicating selective vestibular nerve involvement, and the excellent response to low‐dose topiramate (a sodium channel blocker with antiepileptic properties) further corroborated the diagnosis [1, 2]. This case aligns with the growing recognition that VP is a treatable cause of episodic vertigo, often overlooked in its early stages.

The patient's long‐standing blepharospasm is a classic presentation of a focal dystonia. Its progression in isolation over 10 years and the marked response to a combination of a benzodiazepine (clonazepam) and a dopamine receptor antagonist (haloperidol) are consistent with the diagnosis of MS [5, 6]. The successful management with oral medication alone is a noteworthy aspect of this case, providing a viable alternative for patients who may be hesitant about or have limited access to botulinum toxin therapy.

From a pathophysiological perspective, VP and MS are believed to originate from different neural substrates: VP from peripheral ephaptic transmission at the root entry zone of CN VIII, and MS from central dysfunction within the basal ganglia‐thalamo‐cortical circuits [1, 5]. There is no established common pathway linking the two. Therefore, their coexistence in our patient is most plausibly explained by chance, albeit a rare one. However, this coincidence holds significant clinical value. It teaches us that the presence of a clear explanation for one set of symptoms (e.g., vertigo) should not preclude a thorough investigation for a second, independent neurological disorder, especially when the symptomatology is atypical or refractory to initial treatment.

The pivotal role of an MDT in this case cannot be overstated. The collaboration between otologists, neurologists, and ophthalmologists was instrumental in deconstructing the complex clinical picture, avoiding diagnostic anchoring, and formulating a comprehensive and effective treatment plan. This case report still has some limitations; for example, the Burke‐Fahn‐Marsden Dystonia Rating Scale (BFMDRS) and disability scores could be used to quantitatively assess symptoms in patients with Meige syndrome. The significant improvement in the patient's clinical symptoms and the stable results observed during the 6‐month follow‐up period demonstrate the effectiveness of our treatment regimen.

Conclusion

This case illustrates that VP and MS, though pathophysiologically distinct, can co‐occur. It highlights several key lessons for clinical practice: The diagnosis of VP should be considered in patients with brief, recurrent vertigo, and high‐resolution MRI is crucial for identifying neurovascular conflict.A multidisciplinary approach is essential for the accurate diagnosis and management of patients with overlapping symptoms from different neurological specialties.Tailored oral pharmacotherapy can be highly effective for both VP and MS, as demonstrated by the successful use of topiramate/flunarizine and clonazepam/haloperidol, respectively. This report enriches the literature on rare neuro‐otological and movement disorder coincidences and emphasizes the importance of comprehensive neurological evaluation.
