Osteoporosis exacerbates perioperative complications in total knee arthroplasty: a 10-Year nationwide analysis
Authors: Yiran Feng, Junhao Lin, Chong Zheng, Jian Wang, Qinfeng Yang, Jianping Wang
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Abstract​​BackgroundOsteoporosis, a prevalent comorbidity in patients undergoing total knee arthroplasty (TKA), is associated with compromised bone quality and increased susceptibility to postoperative complications. However, its independent impact on perioperative outcomes remains poorly quantified. This study aimed to elucidate the association between preoperative osteoporosis and adverse outcomes following TKA.​​Methods Using the National Inpatient Sample (NIS) database (2010–2019), we identified 1,330,099 TKA patients, of whom 56,888 (4.28%) had preoperative osteoporosis. Propensity score matching (1:1) was performed to balance baseline covariates. Outcomes included perioperative complications, length of stay (LOS), hospital costs, and resource utilization. Multivariable logistic regression models were used to estimate adjusted odds ratios (aOR) and 95% confidence intervals (CI).​​ResultsOsteoporotic patients exhibited significantly higher risks for major complications compared to non-osteoporotic counterparts: periprosthetic fracture (aOR = 2.13; 95% CI = 1.31–3.45; P < 0.001), prosthetic loosening (aOR = 1.26; 95% CI = 1.11–1.43; P < 0.001), and postoperative hemorrhage (aOR = 1.41; 95% CI = 1.15–1.72; P < 0.001). Additionally, osteoporosis was independently associated with prolonged LOS (aOR = 1.11, 95% CI: 1.07–1.14, P < 0.001) and increased hospitalization costs (aOR = 1.07, 95% CI: 1.04–1.10, P < 0.001). Notably, complications such as lower limb peripheral nerve injuries (aOR = 1.30), muscle atrophy (aOR = 2.77), blood transfusion (aOR = 1.23), stroke (aOR = 1.26), urinary tract infection (aOR = 1.10), and pressure ulcers (aOR = 1.53) were also more frequent in osteoporotic patients.​​ConclusionsPreoperative osteoporosis independently increases the risk of major perioperative complications, including periprosthetic fracture (aOR = 2.13), prosthetic loosening (aOR = 1.26), and postoperative bleeding (aOR = 1.41), while increasing hospital resource utilization (prolonged LOS: aOR = 1.11; excess costs: aOR = 1.07). These findings advocate for routine osteoporosis screening, optimization of bone health preoperatively, and enhanced postoperative surveillance to mitigate risks and improve long-term implant durability. Integrating osteoporosis management into TKA care pathways may reduce hospital-associated infections and enhance patient outcomes.Keywords: Osteoporosis, Total knee arthroplasty, Resource utilization

Introduction

Total knee arthroplasty (TKA) is a common surgical procedure for the treatment of end-stage knee joint diseases [1]. Over one million TKAs are performed globally each year [2, 3]. By correcting the mechanical axis and reconstructing functional joints, TKA effectively relieves pain, corrects joint deformities, and significantly improves patients’ quality of life2. However, several key challenges still hinder the long-term success of TKA. Complications such as prosthesis loosening (with an incidence of 5%–10% within 15 years) and periprosthetic fractures (occurring in 3%–6% of cases within five years postoperatively) have become common adverse surgical outcomes [5]. Notably, these complications may be predominantly influenced by osteoporosis-induced bone loss rather than surgical technique alone [6–8].

Osteoporosis, as a preoperative comorbidity in TKA patients, is characterized by systemic bone loss and microarchitectural deterioration, which directly compromise the biomechanical integrity of the bone-implant interface [9, 10]. A limited number of studies have identified osteoporosis as a risk factor for periprosthetic fractures following TKA [11]. However, few have conducted statistical analyses to evaluate the relationship between osteoporosis and postoperative complications following TKA [12]. Consequently, the current body of literature offers limited evidence regarding the impact of osteoporosis on TKA outcomes.

Given these considerations, the aim of this study is to investigate the impact of osteoporosis on patients undergoing TKA using data from the National Inpatient Sample (NIS). By examining the characteristics and postoperative outcomes of TKA patients with preexisting osteoporosis, this study seeks to assess the prevalence of osteoporosis in the TKA population, Charlson Comorbidity Index (CCI) scores, payer types, total hospital charges, length of stay (LOS), patient demographics, associated comorbidities, and perioperative complications following TKA in patients with osteoporosis. Unlike prior studies, which were limited to small sample sizes, lacked adjustment for comorbidities, or focused narrowly on prosthesis-related outcomes, our study is novel in leveraging a decade of nationwide data and rigorous propensity score matching to provide the first comprehensive evaluation of osteoporosis on a wide spectrum of perioperative complications and healthcare resource utilization.

Materials and methods

Data source

The NIS database served as the primary data source for this study. Recognized as the largest all-payer inpatient database in the United States, the NIS has been in operation since 1998, annually, NIS encompasses around 8 million acute inpatient stays from 1,050 hospitals across 44 states, representing approximately 90% of nonprofit academic medical centers nationwide. Managed by the Healthcare Cost and Utilization Project (HCUP) and sponsored by the Agency for Healthcare Research and Quality (AHRQ), the NIS collects detailed information, including patient demographics, hospital characteristics, length of stay, total charges, type of payer, in-hospital mortality, and diagnostic and procedural codes from the International Classification of Diseases Clinical Modification (ICD-CM) [13]. The data utilized in this research were deidentified to protect privacy and confidentiality, thereby qualifying the study for exempt status.

Study cohort

We identified 1,330,099 patients who underwent TKA with ICD-10 and Clinical Modification/Procedure Coding System (CMP) codes from 2010 to 2019 in NIS database. Cases with missing data were excluded (Fig. 1). To address potential selection bias, we performed a 1:1 propensity score matching (PSM) on baseline characteristics and preoperative comorbidities (caliper value: 0.01; abandoned rate: 0.36%). After PSM, 204 osteoporosis patients were excluded due to unmet eligibility criteria. The final cohort consisted of 113,686 patients, divided into two groups: the osteoporosis group (n = 56,684) and the non - osteoporosis group (n = 56,684).

Outcomes

Perioperative complications following TKA were extracted from the database. We used ICD-9 and ICD-10 codes to identify perioperative complications in diverse organ systems including stroke, hemorrhage, blood transfusion, dvt, postoperative shock, acute heart failure, acute myocardial infarction, pneumonia, urinary tract infection, urinary retention, gastrointestinal bleeding, lower limb peripheral nerve injuries, postoperative delirium, pressure ulcer, muscle atrophy, wound infection, wound dehiscence, sepsis, prosthetic loosening, periprosthetic fracture, joint dislocation, and prosthetic infection. To quantify resource consumption, we analyzed total cost and LOS during hospitalization. Prolonged LOS and higher total costs were defined as values exceeding the 75th percentile for LOS and total cost, respectively [14].

Statistical analysis

To assess statistical differences between osteoporosis and matched non-osteoporosis cohorts, Wilcoxon rank tests were performed for continuous variables and Pearson Chi-square tests for categorical variables. To evaluate the association between osteoporosis and postoperative adverse outcomes, we employed univariate and multivariate logistic regression models. Odds ratios (OR) and 95% confidence intervals (CI) were calculated using the non- osteoporosis group as reference. Both unadjusted odds ratios (uOR) and adjusted odds ratios (aOR) were reported. Statistical significance was defined as a two-sided α level of 0.05 (P < 0.05), consistent with other NIS-based studies [15].

Results

A total of 1,330,099 patients who underwent TKA between 2010 and 2019 were included in the study, with 56,888 (4.28%) diagnosed with osteoporosis. Before PSM, significant demographic and clinical differences were observed between osteoporotic and non-osteoporotic patients. Osteoporotic patients were older, with a median age of 72 years compared to 66 years in the non-osteoporotic cohort. The majority of osteoporotic patients were female (92.68%) compared to 60.61% in the non-osteoporotic group. After matching, the age and gender distributions between the two groups were balanced, with no significant differences (P = 0.449 for age and P = 0.234 for gender). Similarly, while osteoporotic patients were more likely to have Medicare coverage before matching (76.60% vs. 54.98%, P < 0.001), this difference was no longer significant after matching.

The incidence of comorbidities in osteoporosis patients was significantly higher than that in whole non-osteoporosis patients apart from alcohol abuse, congestive heart failure, diabetes, drug abuse, hypertension, liver disease, lymphoma, metastatic cancer, obesity, psychoses, renal failure, solid tumor without metastasis, and peptic ulcer disease(P < 0.001) (Table 1).

Discussion

In this study, we investigated the impact of osteoporosis on adverse outcomes after TKA by comparing and analyzing the clinical outcomes of osteoporotic and non-osteoporotic patients after TKA. Osteoporotic patients face greater challenges in postoperative rehabilitation and complication management, with longer postoperative recovery times and significantly higher complication rates. We examined 1,330,099 TKA patients from the NIS database between 2010 and 2019, of whom 4.28% were registered as having osteoporosis, a prevalence comparable to studies using the same database. The results of this study are consistent with previous studies, in which the duration of postoperative hospitalization, hospitalization costs, postoperative complications, and postoperative functional recovery of patients with osteoporosis were significantly affected [16].

In this study, we found that osteoporosis patients were generally older (72 vs. 66 years). It has been shown that aging is strongly associated with osteoporosis, with the incidence of osteoporosis increasing with age, and that older patients have weaker postoperative recovery [17, 18]. The postoperative recovery period is usually longer in elderly patients, especially if they are comorbid with other chronic diseases, and the risk of postoperative complications is also higher [19]. In addition, this study also found that women had a higher proportion of osteoporosis patients (92.68%). The decrease in estrogen levels in women after menopause leads to accelerated bone conversion and faster bone loss, a physiological characteristic that makes women more susceptible to complications after TKA, such as problems with loosening of the prosthesis and postoperative fractures [20]. Relevant literature also points out that female patients have a stronger perception of postoperative pain and lower adherence to rehabilitation, which may further affect their postoperative functional recovery [21].

In addition, osteoporosis patients were more likely to utilize Medicare coverage (76.60% vs. 54.98%), which was closely related to the age and health status of the patients. Studies have shown that Medicare-covered patients typically have multiple comorbid chronic conditions and greater resource needs for postoperative rehabilitation, resulting in longer hospital stays and significantly higher hospitalization costs [22].

Osteoporotic patients are often accompanied by multiple comorbidities prior to TKA, which not only affects the patient’s postoperative recovery, but may also exacerbate the occurrence of postoperative complications [23]. This study showed that osteoporosis patients often have multiple comorbidities prior to TKA, and these comorbidities significantly increase the risk of postoperative adverse outcomes. Comorbidities such as iron-deficiency anemia, rheumatoid arthritis (RA), coagulation disorders, chronic lung disease, depression, hypothyroidism, electrolyte disorders, and peripheral vascular disease may affect postoperative recovery through different mechanisms [24]. For example, anemia can lead to decreased oxygen delivery capacity and increase the risk of postoperative infections; RA exacerbates the inflammatory response and delays postoperative recovery; coagulation dysfunction increases the risk of bleeding; chronic lung disease increases the rate of postoperative pulmonary complications; patients with depression often have delayed recovery due to enhanced pain perception; hypothyroidism affects bone metabolism and increases the severity of osteoporosis; electrolyte disorders may lead to cardiac and neurologic functional problems; and peripheral vascular disease may affect circulation and wound healing, increasing the risk of complications. Joint diseases may adversely affect postoperative outcomes in patients undergoing TKA [25]. Therefore, management of these comorbidities to optimize the postoperative prognosis is crucial for osteoporotic patients [26].

Osteoporosis is a common comorbidity in patients undergoing TKA, and its impact is not limited to the skeletal system, but also involves the postoperative recovery of multiple physiologic systems [27]. This study investigated the potential impact of osteoporosis on adverse postoperative outcomes in TKA patients. Specifically, osteoporosis was significantly associated with longer LOS, higher total cost, and increased risk of various complications, including cardiovascular complications (stroke, postoperative hemorrhage), urinary complications (urinary retention, urinary tract infections), postoperative gastrointestinal bleeding, pressure ulcer, lower extremity nerve injury, wound infection, muscle atrophy, prosthesis-related complications (prosthesis loosening and periprosthesis fractures) and blood transfusions.

In the cardiovascular system, both postoperative bleeding (0.41% vs. 0.29%, aOR = 1.41) and stroke (aOR = 1.26) were more common, potentially related to hemodynamic instability, coagulation abnormalities, and vascular dysfunction linked to abnormal bone and vitamin D metabolism [16, 20, 21]. Respiratory complications, including pneumonia and acute respiratory failure, were also significantly increased, likely due to prolonged immobilization, reduced respiratory muscle function, and skeletal fragility that limits early mobilization [22, 23]. In the urinary system, both urinary tract infection (2.81% vs. 2.61%, aOR = 1.10) and urinary retention (2.06% vs. 1.84%, aOR = 1.23) occurred more frequently, which may be attributed to prolonged bed rest, catheter-associated infection, impaired bladder function, and neurologic deficits secondary to osteoporosis-related spinal or pelvic fragility [24, 25]. Furthermore, osteoporotic patients faced an elevated risk of lower extremity nerve injury (aOR = 1.30), potentially caused by intraoperative fractures, unstable implant fixation, and traction-related injury in the setting of reduced bone density [31, 32]. Furthermore, osteoporotic patients faced an elevated risk of lower extremity nerve injury (aOR = 1.30), potentially caused by intraoperative fractures, unstable implant fixation, and traction-related injury in the setting of reduced bone density [8, 28, 29]. These findings highlight the need for tailored perioperative management—including careful hemodynamic monitoring, early mobilization, respiratory and urinary rehabilitation, judicious anticoagulant and gastrointestinal protective strategies, as well as meticulous intraoperative nerve protection—to mitigate the heightened complication risk in this vulnerable population.

Osteoporotic patients were more likely to develop pressure sores after TKA (aOR = 1.53). The main reasons may be postoperative activity restriction, prolonged bed rest and decreased local tissue tolerance. In addition, osteoporotic patients often suffer from malnutrition, which affects skin healing ability and further increases the risk of pressure ulcers [34, 35]. Therefore, postoperative positional management, regular turning, use of pressure-reducing aids, and optimized nutritional support should be enhanced to reduce the incidence of pressure ulcers.

Muscle atrophy was more common in osteoporotic patients after TKA (aOR = 2.77). The higher risk of postoperative muscle atrophy in osteoporotic patients may be related to delayed postoperative rehabilitation, prolonged bed rest and lower muscle mass [36]. Muscle atrophy not only affects gait recovery, but may also increase the risk of falls and prosthetic complications [37]. Therefore, early postoperative rehabilitation combined with nutritional support, such as protein and vitamin D supplementation, should be performed to improve muscle function and promote recovery.

Postoperative implantation of prostheses may be associated with serious complications, usually related to prosthesis-related complications, such as prosthesis loosening and periprosthesis fractures. This study showed that the incidence of prosthesis loosening was significantly higher in osteoporotic patients than in non-osteoporotic patients (aOR = 1.26). Due to decreased bone density and bone microarchitectural disruption caused by osteoporosis, the stability of the bone-prosthesis interface may be impaired postoperatively, which in turn increases the risk of prosthesis loosening [11]. In addition, the incidence of postoperative periprosthetic fractures was also higher in osteoporotic patients (aOR = 2.13), which may be related to decreased bone healing capacity, increased bone fragility, and abnormal weight bearing after surgery [38]. Therefore, before TKA, patients’ bone quality should be assessed and intensive BMD management strategies, such as preoperative supplementation with vitamin D, calcium, and anti-osteoporosis medications, should be used to reduce the incidence of prosthesis-related complications.

In this study, we found that osteoporotic patients had a significant increase in resource consumption after TKA, as evidenced by prolonged hospitalization (19.22% vs. 17.71%, aOR = 1.11) and elevated healthcare costs (24.96% vs. 23.67%, aOR = 1.07). Postoperative complications, such as infections, bleeding, and nerve damage, may result in additional treatment and monitoring needs, thereby prolonging hospitalization [39]. In addition, advanced age, poorer health status, and the need for postoperative blood transfusions and ICU monitoring further add to the burden of hospitalization. Osteoporosis patients’ prolonged bed rest increases the risk of complications due to decreased bone density and limited mobility after surgery, creating a vicious cycle of prolonged hospitalization [40]. Meanwhile, postoperative medical costs for TKA mainly cover surgery, inpatient care, rehabilitation and medication. Osteoporosis patients often require more medical resources, such as additional imaging tests, anti-infection treatment, rehabilitation care and secondary surgery due to the higher risk of complications, which pushes up the overall medical costs [41]. Due to slower functional recovery after surgery, osteoporosis patients have increased rehabilitation needs, further adding to the financial burden. Therefore, optimizing preoperative bone management, strengthening postoperative rehabilitation guidance, and reducing complications will help reduce healthcare resource consumption and improve patients’ postoperative quality of life and the overall efficiency of the healthcare system. Recent studies on robotic-assisted TKA suggest that it may improve surgical accuracy and help reduce complications in patients with osteoporosis [42].

It is noteworthy that although osteoporosis was significantly associated with rare complications such as periprosthetic fracture and wound infection, their absolute incidences remained very low (< 0.1%). Given the very large sample size in our study (> 1.3 million TKA cases), even rare events could be captured and reached statistical significance. Moreover, as the NIS database only records inpatient encounters, complications that develop after discharge may not be fully captured, which could partly explain the low observed incidences. Therefore, these rare findings should be interpreted primarily as epidemiological signals rather than strong clinical directives.

There are inherent limitations associated with utilizing the NIS database that must be considered. Firstly, as a retrospective analysis based on a national dataset, it poses challenges in assessing specific patient-level characteristics and the nuanced factors that influence clinical decision-making. Secondly, because the NIS relies solely on administrative billing codes, there is a potential for coding errors or inconsistencies, which may undermine the accuracy and reliability of multivariate analyses when evaluating trends in complication development. Despite these limitations, the NIS remains a valuable and widely accepted resource for identifying national patterns and demographic distributions, given its large sample size and representativeness.

Conclusion

This large-scale study demonstrates that preoperative osteoporosis independently increases the risk of major perioperative complications, including periprosthetic fracture (aOR = 2.13), prosthetic loosening (aOR = 1.26), and postoperative bleeding (aOR = 1.41), while increasing hospital resource utilization (prolonged LOS: aOR = 1.11; excess costs: aOR = 1.07). These findings underscore the potential importance of preoperative bone health optimization and postoperative surveillance. While our study was not designed to evaluate the efficacy of specific interventions, the results suggest that incorporating osteoporosis assessment and management may be beneficial in reducing perioperative risks and improving long-term implant outcomes. Prospective interventional studies are warranted to establish definitive guidelines.
