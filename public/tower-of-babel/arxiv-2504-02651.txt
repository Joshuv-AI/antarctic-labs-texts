Quantized Markov Chain Couplings that Prepare Qsamples
Kristan Temme, Pawel Wocjan

Abstract
We present a novel approach to quantizing Markov chains. The approach is based on the Markov chain coupling method, which is frequently used to prove fast mixing. Given a particular coupling, e.g., a grand coupling, we construct a completely positive and trace preserving map. This quantum map has a unique fixed point, which corresponds to the quantum sample (qsample) of the classical Markov chain’s stationary distribution. We show that the convergence time of the quantum map is directly related to the coupling time of the Markov chain coupling.

Central to our construction is that many important classical Markov chains in the literature are shown to be fast mixing by constructing suitable Markov chain couplings

We use such couplings to construct our novel tcp-maps from classical Markov chains and prove that the convergence time to reach the qsample fixed point is closely related to the coupling time of the classical chain. In particular we show that

(main results (informal))
.

Assume we are given a Markov chain coupling for an ergodic Markov chain
P
with stationary distribution
\pi=(\pi_{x}:x\in\Omega)
. We construct a corresponding trace preserving map
\mathcal{T}
called quantized coupling.

For independent and grand couplings, the resulting quantized coupling

is a valid tcp-map that prepares the qsample
|\sqrt{\pi}\rangle\langle\sqrt{\pi}|
. Its convergence is bounded by

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}\big\|\mathcal{T}^{m}(\rho)-|\sqrt{\pi}\rangle\langle\sqrt{\pi}\big\|_{\mathrm{tr}}

\displaystyle\leq\sqrt{\varepsilon},

(4)

whenever

\displaystyle m\geq\frac{1}{2}\log_{2}\left(\frac{1}{\varepsilon\;\pi_{*}}\right)\;t_{\mathrm{couple}},

(5)

where
\rho
is any initial state,
\pi_{*}=\min\{\pi_{x}:x\in\Omega\}
.

The quantized coupling
\mathcal{T}
can be implemented efficiently for sparse grand couplings.

Note that this result is different from Szegedy walks
[
2
]
, which are unitaries that permit the reflection about the qsample when combined with phase estimation. The ability to reflect about a state does not necessarily imply that the state can be prepared efficiently, cf. e.g.,
[
13
]
. The result here appears to be close to the statement that we can prepare qsamples if we have rapidly mixing classical Markov chains that sample from the distribution. However, we want to point out that the fact that a Markov chain is rapidly mixing does not immediately imply that a corresponding coupling coalesces quickly. Indeed, the former statement seems too powerful for quantum algorithms as such a result would imply that a quantum computer could efficiently solve complete problems for statistical zero knowledge proofs
[
11
]
. Relating the preparation of qsamples to coupling times is therefore a meaningful relaxation.

We now briefly highlight some of the key areas where qsamples are used. They play a central role in algorithms for estimating partition functions and Bayesian inference
[
14
,
15
]
. Qsamples are also essential in algorithms for estimating volumes of convex bodies
[

]
, sampling log-concave and non-log-concave distributions
[

,
18
]
, and operator-level quantum acceleration
[
19
]
.

In the algorithms for partition function estimation, Bayesian inference, and volume estimation, the following situation arises: a sequence of slowly varying Markov chains is used, where the stationary distributions of adjacent chains are close in total variation distance. This implies that the corresponding qsamples have large overlap, and the qsample for the first Markov chain in the sequence can be efficiently prepared. Both methods
[
11
,
20
]
require traversing intermediate steps, akin to climbing a ladder one rung at a time. One approach constructs a sequence of Hamiltonians whose ground states are the qsamples, transforming adiabatically the initial qsample to the final one through these intermediate states
[
11
]
. The other uses Szegedy walks and fixed-point search to achieve the same transformation step-by-step
[
20
]
.

The algorithms for sampling log-concave, non-log-concave distributions, and operator-level quantum acceleration assume the existence of a “warm start” state with sufficiently large overlap with the desired qsample.

Our new method differs by preparing qsamples directly for a single Markov chain starting in an arbitrary initial state. While our focus in this work is on introducing this new mechanism—rather than optimizing all parameters or deriving the tightest running times—we emphasize that improving its efficiency remains an important open question. Such advancements could enable broader applications in these and other emerging quantum algorithms.

The paper is organized as follows.
In Section
2
, we introduce Markov chain couplings and describe how they are used to bound the mixing time. We present two types of couplings: (a) independent couplings and (b) grand couplings.
In Section
3
, we present our quantization method that transforms Markov chain couplings into linear maps that act on quantum states. We prove that our method converts independent and grand couplings into valid tcp-maps. In the appendix, we also present a counterexample of a certain coupling that does lead to a valid tcp-map (because the resulting linear map is not completely positive).
In Section
4
, we analyze the convergence rate of quantized couplings, that is, how fast they prepare the qsamples. We establish a close connection to the coalescence time of the Markov chain coupling.
In Section
5
, we describe how the quantized couplings can be efficiently implemented. We discuss the implementation for three concrete examples: (i) random walk on hypercube, (ii) random colorings, and (iii) hardcore model with fugacity. These three examples are all grand couplings. We also formulate a general theorem that shows when quantized couplings can be efficiently implemented for sparse grand couplings. The latter include many important examples in the literature.

2
Markov chain couplings

Markov chain coupling is a powerful tool for bounding the mixing time. We give only a brief overview and refer the reader to
[
10
]
for an in-depth introduction.

Recall that the column stochastic matrix
P=(p_{x^{\prime},x})
denotes the transition matrix of an ergodic Markov chain on the finite state space
\Omega
with limiting distribution
\pi
. Define

\displaystyle d(m)

\displaystyle=\max_{x\in\Omega}\frac{1}{2}\sum_{x^{\prime}\in\Omega}\big|[P^{m}]_{x^{\prime},x}-\pi_{x^{\prime}}\big|,

(6)

where
([P^{m}]_{x^{\prime},x}:x^{\prime}\in\Omega)
is the probability distribution that we obtain by starting the Markov chain in the initial state
x
and making
m
transitions. The total variation distance
d(m)
measures how close we are to the desired stationary distribution
\pi
as the number of steps
m
increases.
The mixing time
t_{\mathrm{mix}}(\varepsilon)
is defined by

\displaystyle t_{\mathrm{mix}}(\varepsilon)

\displaystyle=\min\{m:d(m)\leq\varepsilon\}

(7)

for
\varepsilon\in(0,1)
.
It satisfies the bound

\displaystyle t_{\mathrm{mix}}(\varepsilon)

\displaystyle\leq\big\lceil\log_{2}\varepsilon^{-1}\big\rceil t_{\mathrm{mix}},

(8)

where
t_{\mathrm{mix}}=t_{\mathrm{mix}}(1/4)
.

A coupling is a Markov chain on state space
\Omega\times\Omega
with transition matrix
C=(c_{(x^{\prime},y^{\prime}),(x,y)})
so that the following
coupling conditions
are satisfied:

1.

Each component viewed separately undergoes transitions according to the original transition matrix
P
:

\displaystyle\sum_{y^{\prime}}c_{(x^{\prime},y^{\prime}),(x,y)}

\displaystyle=p_{x^{\prime},x}

(9)

\displaystyle\sum_{x^{\prime}}c_{(x^{\prime},y^{\prime}),(x,y)}

\displaystyle=p_{y^{\prime},y}

(10)

2.

The two components remain together forever once they have met, that is,
x^{\prime}=y^{\prime}
if
x=y
:

\displaystyle c_{(x^{\prime},x^{\prime}),(x,x)}

\displaystyle=p_{x^{\prime}x}

(11)

\displaystyle c_{(x^{\prime},y^{\prime}),(x,x)}

\displaystyle=0\mbox{ for }x^{\prime}\neq y^{\prime}

(12)

3.

The two components satisfy the symmetry condition:

\displaystyle c_{(x^{\prime},y^{\prime}),(x,y)}=c_{(y^{\prime},x^{\prime}),(y,x)}

(13)

Let
(X_{m},Y_{m})
denote the random state of the coupling that starts in the initial state
(x,y)
and makes
m
transitions according to the transition matrix
C
. Let
\tau_{\mathrm{coal}}
denote the random variable

\displaystyle\tau_{\mathrm{coal}}

\displaystyle=\min\{m:X_{\ell}=Y_{\ell}\mbox{ for all }\ell\geq m\},

(14)

which is called the coalescence time for the initial state
(x,y)
. To keep the notation simple, we omit
(x,y)
when writing
\tau_{\mathrm{coal}}
.

We need to make an important remark at this point. Observe that the transition matrix
C
could depend on the initial state
(x,y)
. It could be constructed in such a way that the corresponding coalescence time is reduced for this particular initial state. However, we will not consider such specialized transition matrices. We assume that the transition matrix
C
is the same when defining the coalescence time for all initial states
(x,y)
.

The most important result of Markov chain coupling is summarized in the following theorem
[
10
]
:

Theorem 2

(Connection between coalescence and mixing times)
.

The total variation distance
d(m)
and the mixing time
t_{\mathrm{mix}}
are bounded from above by

\displaystyle d(m)

\displaystyle\leq\max_{x,y}\Pr_{x,y}\big(\tau_{\mathrm{coal}}>m\big)

(15)

\displaystyle t_{\mathrm{mix}}

\displaystyle\leq 4\max_{x,y}\mathbb{E}_{x,y}(\tau_{\mathrm{coal}}),

(16)

respectively, where

•

\Pr_{x,y}(\tau_{\mathrm{coal}}>m)
denotes the probability that the random variable
\tau_{\mathrm{coal}}
is greater than
m
and

•

\mathbb{E}_{x,y}(\tau_{\mathrm{coal}})
denotes the expected value of the random variable
\tau_{\mathrm{coal}}

for the initial state
(x,y)
.

Thus, the problem of showing that a particular Markov chain with transition matrix
P
mixes fast reduces to the problem of constructing a suitable coupling with transition matrix
C
that coalesces quickly when starting in any initial state
(x,y)
.

In an analogy to the mixing time
t_{\mathrm{mix}}
, it is convenient to introduce the coupling time
t_{\mathrm{couple}}
for our analysis. Given the probability
\Pr_{x,y}(\tau_{\mathrm{coal}}>m)=\Pr_{x,y}(X_{m}\neq Y_{m})
, we define the coupling time as

\displaystyle t_{\mathrm{couple}}

\displaystyle=\min\left\{m:\max_{x,y}\Pr_{x,y}(\tau_{\mathrm{coal}}>m)\leq\frac{1}{4}\right\}.

(17)

Using the upper bound on the total variation distance
d(m)
in (
15
) and the definitions of
t_{\mathrm{mix}}
and
t_{\mathrm{couple}}
, it follows directly that
t_{\mathrm{mix}}\leq t_{\mathrm{couple}}
.

Furthermore, we can relate the probability that we have not coalesced after
m
to the transition matrix as follows:

Remark 3
.

For the analysis of our quantized coupling, it is very helpful to note that
\Pr_{x,y}(\tau_{\mathrm{coal}})
can be equivalently written as

\displaystyle\Pr_{x,y}(\tau_{\mathrm{coal}}>m)

\displaystyle=\sum_{x^{\prime}\neq y^{\prime}}[C^{m}]_{(x^{\prime},y^{\prime}),(x,y)},

(18)

where
([C^{m}]_{(x^{\prime},y^{\prime}),(x,y)}:(x^{\prime},y^{\prime})\in\Omega\times\Omega)
is the probability distribution that we obtain by starting the coupling in the state
(x,y)
and running it for
m
times steps.

2.1
Examples of couplings

We present independent and grand couplings.

Definition 4

(Independent coupling)
.

The coupling
C_{\mathrm{ind}}=(c_{(x^{\prime},y^{\prime}),(x,y)})
is defined by

\displaystyle c_{(x^{\prime},y^{\prime}),(x,y)}

\displaystyle=\left\{\begin{array}[]{cl}p_{x^{\prime}x}\cdot p_{y^{\prime}y}&\mbox{ if }x\neq y\\
p_{x^{\prime}x}&\mbox{ if }x^{\prime}=y^{\prime}\mbox{ and }x=y\\
0&\mbox{ if }x^{\prime}\neq y^{\prime}\mbox{ and }x=y\end{array}\right.

In some sense, an independent coupling is the most basic coupling possible. If the two components are different, apply the Markov chain to both components independently. If they are equal, apply the Markov chain only to the first component and copy the resulting successor state to the second component. Independent couplings are not very useful for proving fast mixing.

We need to introduce some terminology before we can define grand couplings, which are very useful for establishing fast mixing.

A
random mapping representation
of the transition matrix
P=(p_{x^{\prime},x})
on state space
\Omega
is a function
f:\Omega\times\mathcal{R}\rightarrow\Omega
, along with a
\mathcal{R}
-valued random variable
R
, satisfying

\displaystyle\Pr\big(f(x,R)=x^{\prime}\big)

\displaystyle=p_{x^{\prime},x}

(22)

for all possible pairs of current state
x
and successor state
x^{\prime}
.

It is known that every transition matrix
P
on a finite state space has a random mapping representation. This is proved in
[
10
]
with the following choices: the set
\mathcal{R}
is equal to the interval
[0,1]
and the random variable
R
is uniformly chosen in
\mathcal{R}
. For our purposes, we assume that
\mathcal{R}
is a finite set; this occurs naturally for many important Markov chains.

For
r\in\mathcal{R}
, we write
\Pr(r)
to denote the probability that the random variable
R
takes on the value
r
. The transition matrix
P
can be expressed as

\displaystyle P

\displaystyle=\sum_{r\in\mathcal{R}}\sum_{x\in\Omega}\Pr(r)\,|f(x,r)\rangle\langle x|.

(23)

Definition 5

(Grand coupling)
.

We use the same randomness in both components to define
the transition matrix
C_{\mathrm{grand}}=(c_{(x^{\prime},y^{\prime}),(x,y)})
of a grand coupling.
We set the transition probabilities to

\displaystyle c_{(x^{\prime},y^{\prime}),(x,y)}

\displaystyle=\Pr(f(x,R)=x^{\prime}\mbox{ and }f(y,R)=y^{\prime}).

(24)

Observe that the transition matrix
C_{\mathrm{grand}}
can be expressed as

\displaystyle C_{\mathrm{grand}}

\displaystyle=\sum_{r\in\mathcal{R}}\sum_{(x,y)\in\Omega\times\Omega}\Pr(r)\,|f(x,r),f(y,r)\rangle\langle x,y|.

(25)

It plays a central role in our quantization method, which is introduced in the next section.

3
Quantized Markov chain couplings

We define a linear map in terms of the transition matrix
C
of an
arbitrary
coupling. We prove that this linear map leads to valid tcp-maps for independent and grand couplings.

Definition 6

(Quantized coupling)
.

Let
C=(c_{(x^{\prime},y^{\prime}),(x,y)})
be the transition matrix of an arbitrary coupling of an ergodic Markov chain with transition matrix
P=(p_{x^{\prime},x})
and stationary distribution
\pi=(\pi_{x})
.

Define

\displaystyle\mathcal{T}^{*}(\bullet)

\displaystyle=D^{-1/2}\,\mathcal{C}^{*}(D^{1/2}\bullet D^{1/2})\,D^{-1/2}

(26)

where

\displaystyle\mathcal{C}^{*}(\bullet)

\displaystyle=\sum_{x,y,x^{\prime},y^{\prime}\in\Omega}c_{(x^{\prime},y^{\prime}),(x,y)}|x^{\prime}\rangle\langle x|\bullet|y\rangle\langle y^{\prime}|

(27)

and
D=\mathrm{diag}(\pi_{x}:x\in\Omega)
.

The quantized coupling
\mathcal{T}
is defined as the dual of the linear map
\mathcal{T}^{*}
with respect to the Hilbert-Schmidt inner product.

Later, it will become apparent why we define the quantized coupling
\mathcal{T}
with the help of the dual map
\mathcal{T}^{*}
. The latter appears naturally in the proof establishing that the qsample
|\sqrt{\pi}\rangle
is the unique fixed point and also in the derivation of the bound on the convergence rate, which relates it to the coalescence time of the coupling.

Let us now highlight some key properties of quantized couplings. First, observe that the action of the linear map
\mathcal{C}^{*}
in the vectorized representation is described by the transition matrix
C

\displaystyle C

\displaystyle=\sum_{x,y,x^{\prime},y^{\prime}\in\Omega}c_{(x^{\prime},y^{\prime}),(x,y)}|x^{\prime}\rangle\langle x|\otimes|y^{\prime}\rangle\langle y|

(28)

\displaystyle=\sum_{x,y,x^{\prime},y^{\prime}\in\Omega}c_{(x^{\prime},y^{\prime}),(x,y)}|x^{\prime}y^{\prime}\rangle\langle xy|,

(29)

where we abbreviate
|x^{\prime}y^{\prime}\rangle\equiv|x^{\prime}\rangle\otimes|y^{\prime}\rangle
and
|xy\rangle\equiv|x\rangle\otimes|y\rangle
. This is seen using a fundamental property of the vectorization map and the property that
C
is symmetric under exchanging both components (which is the third coupling condition).
Let
M
be any matrix acting on
\Omega
. We use the convention that
\mathrm{vec}(M)
is the column vector obtained by stacking the column vectors of
M
. Matrix multiplication under the vectorization behaves according to formula

\displaystyle\mathrm{vec}(AMB)

\displaystyle=(B^{T}\otimes A)\mathrm{vec}(M),

(30)

which holds for any triplet of square matrices
A
,
M
, and
B
acting on
\Omega
.

Second, observe that the application of the linear map
D^{1/2}\bullet D^{1/2}
inside
\mathcal{C}^{*}
and the linear map
D^{-1/2}\bullet D^{-1/2}
outside
\mathcal{C}^{*}
in (
26
) corresponds to the similarity transformation

\displaystyle(D^{-1/2}\otimes D^{-1/2})\,C\,(D^{1/2}\otimes D^{1/2})

(31)

in the vectorized representation.

Finally, note that it is not necessary for the mathematical definition of a quantized Markov chain coupling that the underlying chain is detailed-balanced (also called reversible). That said, detailed-balanced maps have the convenient property that the ratios of the probabilities in the stationary distribution can be expressed in terms of the transition probabilities. This can be used to simplify the algorithmic implementation.

Lemma 7

(Trace preserving)
.

The map
\mathcal{T}^{*}
satisfies

\displaystyle\mathcal{T}^{*}(I)

\displaystyle=I,

(32)

that is,
\mathcal{T}
is trace preserving.

Proof.

We have

\displaystyle\mathcal{T}^{*}(I)

\displaystyle=D^{-1/2}\,\mathcal{C}^{*}(D^{1/2}ID^{1/2})\,D^{-1/2}

(33)

\displaystyle=D^{-1/2}\,\mathcal{C}^{*}(D)\,D^{-1/2}

(34)

\displaystyle=D^{-1/2}\,D\,D^{-1/2}=I.

(35)

In the above derivation, we used the identity
\mathcal{C}^{*}(D)=D
. This identity follows from the definition
D=\mathrm{diag}(\pi_{x}:x\in\Omega)=\sum_{x}\pi_{x}|x\rangle\langle x|
and the coupling properties. We have

\displaystyle\mathcal{C}^{*}(D)

\displaystyle=\sum_{x,y,x^{\prime},y^{\prime}\in\Omega}c_{(x^{\prime},y^{\prime}),(x,y)}\,|x^{\prime}\rangle\langle x|D|y\rangle\langle y^{\prime}|

(36)

\displaystyle=\sum_{x,x^{\prime},y^{\prime}\in\Omega}c_{(x^{\prime},y^{\prime}),(x,x)}\,\pi_{x}\,|x^{\prime}\rangle\langle y^{\prime}|

(37)

\displaystyle=\sum_{x,x^{\prime}\in\Omega}c_{(x^{\prime},x^{\prime}),(x,x)}\,\pi_{x}\,|x^{\prime}\rangle\langle x^{\prime}|

(38)

\displaystyle=\sum_{x^{\prime}\in\Omega}\left(\sum_{x\in\Omega}p_{x^{\prime},x}\,\pi_{x}\,\right)\,|x^{\prime}\rangle\langle x^{\prime}|

(39)

\displaystyle=\sum_{x^{\prime}\in\Omega}\pi_{x^{\prime}}\,|x^{\prime}\rangle\langle x^{\prime}|=D.

(40)

The proof shows that the similarity transformation converts the identity
\mathcal{C}^{*}(D)=D
into the identity
\mathcal{T}^{*}(I)=I
.
∎

Lemma 8

(Fixed point)
.

The qsample
\left|\sqrt{\pi}\right\rangle
is a fixed point of the map
\mathcal{T}
so that

\displaystyle\mathcal{T}(\left|\sqrt{\pi}\right\rangle\!\left\langle\sqrt{\pi}\right|)

\displaystyle=\left|\sqrt{\pi}\right\rangle\!\left\langle\sqrt{\pi}\right|.

(41)

Proof.

We have
\left|\sqrt{\pi}\right\rangle=D^{1/2}\left|e\right\rangle
with
\left|e\right\rangle=(1,1,\ldots,1)^{T}=\sum_{x\in\Omega}\left|x\right\rangle
. Hence, we can write

\displaystyle\mathcal{T}(\left|\sqrt{\pi}\right\rangle\!\left\langle\sqrt{\pi}\right|)

\displaystyle=D^{1/2}\,\mathcal{C}(D^{-1/2}|\sqrt{\pi}\rangle\langle\sqrt{\pi}|D^{-1/2})\,D^{1/2}

(42)

\displaystyle=D^{1/2}\,\mathcal{C}(\left|e\right\rangle\!\left\langle e\right|)\,D^{1/2}.

(43)

Note that we have
D^{-1/2}\bullet D^{-1/2}
inside
\mathcal{C}
and
D^{1/2}\bullet D^{1/2}
outside
\mathcal{C}
because we consider
\mathcal{T}
and instead of the dual map
\mathcal{T}^{*}
.

We now use
\sum_{x^{\prime},y^{\prime}\in\Omega}c_{(x^{\prime},y^{\prime}),(x,y)}=1
for all
x,y\in\Omega
since the transition matrix
C
is column stochastic. Thus, we have

\displaystyle\mathcal{C}(\left|e\right\rangle\!\left\langle e\right|)

\displaystyle=\sum_{x,y,x^{\prime},y^{\prime}}c_{(x^{\prime},y^{\prime}),(x,y)}|y\rangle\langle y^{\prime}|e\rangle\langle e|x^{\prime}\rangle\langle x|

(44)

\displaystyle=\sum_{x,y}|y\rangle\langle x|

(45)

\displaystyle=\left|e\right\rangle\!\left\langle e\right|.

(46)

Again, note that we consider
\mathcal{C}
instead of
\mathcal{C}^{*}
, which explains why the outer products
|x^{\prime}\rangle\langle x|
and
|y\rangle\langle y^{\prime}|
appear on different sides.

Combining everything together, we obtain

\displaystyle\mathcal{T}(\left|\sqrt{\pi}\right\rangle\!\left\langle\sqrt{\pi}\right|)

\displaystyle=D^{1/2}\,\mathcal{C}(\left|e\right\rangle\!\left\langle e\right|)\,D^{1/2}.

(47)

\displaystyle=D^{1/2}\,\left|e\right\rangle\!\left\langle e\right|\,D^{1/2}=\left|\sqrt{\pi}\right\rangle\!\left\langle\sqrt{\pi}\right|

(48)

establishing that
\left|\sqrt{\pi}\right\rangle
is a fixed point of
\mathcal{T}
.
∎

3.1
Completely positive quantized couplings

We showed that the map
\mathcal{T}
is trace-preserving and has the qsample
|\sqrt{\pi}\rangle
as a fixed point. To establish that this map is a physical quantum operation, we need to prove that it is also completely positive. Unfortunately, this is not true for all couplings
C
. We provide a counterexample (c.f. Appendix), which shows that there exist classical couplings whose corresponding quantized couplings are not completely positive. However, product couplings and grand couplings (c.f. Subsection
2.1
) always give rise to completely positive maps.

Lemma 9

(Quantized independent coupling is completely positive)
.

The map
\mathcal{T}
corresponding to an independent coupling is completely positive.

Proof.

It suffices to check that the map
\mathcal{C}^{*}
is completely positive since:

(i)

\mathcal{T}
is completely positive if and only its dual
\mathcal{T}^{*}
is completely positive

(ii)

the similarity transformation between
\mathcal{C}^{*}
to
\mathcal{T}^{*}
preserves complete positiveness.

Let us provide more details on the second statement.
\mathcal{C}^{*}
is completely positive if and only if it can be expressed with some (unnormalized) Kraus operators
C_{j}
. These transform into the Kraus operators
T_{j}=D^{1/2}C_{j}D^{-1/2}
of
\mathcal{T}^{*}
.

We prove that the Choi-Jamiołkowski state
J(\mathcal{C}^{*})
is positive semidefinite, which means that the linear map
\mathcal{C}^{*}
is completely positive. For each
x
, let
|p_{x}\rangle
denote the column vector of the transition matrix
P
corresponding to the state
x
. We can write
J(\mathcal{C}^{*})
as follows:

\displaystyle J(\mathcal{C}^{*})

\displaystyle=\sum_{x,y}\mathcal{C}^{*}(|x\rangle\langle y|)\otimes|x\rangle\langle y|

(49)

\displaystyle=\sum_{x}\sum_{x^{\prime}}p_{x^{\prime},x}|x^{\prime}\rangle\langle x^{\prime}|\otimes|x\rangle\langle x|+\sum_{x,y:x\neq y}|p_{x}\rangle\langle p_{y}|\otimes|x\rangle\langle y|

(50)

\displaystyle=\sum_{x,y}|p_{x}\rangle\langle p_{y}|\otimes|x\rangle\langle y|+\sum_{x}\big(\mathrm{diag}(|p_{x}\rangle)-|p_{x}\rangle\langle p_{x}|\big)\otimes|x\rangle\langle x|.

(51)

It suffices to show that the matrices
\mathrm{diag}(|p_{x}\rangle)-|p_{x}\rangle\langle p_{x}|
are positive semidefinite for all
x
since the first sum defines a positive semidefinite matrix. This is the case because these matrices are diagonally dominant.

Consider an arbitrary
x
and the corresponding matrix
\mathrm{diag}(|p_{x}\rangle)-|p_{x}\rangle\langle p_{x}|
. Pick any column
x^{\prime}
. Its diagonal entry is

\displaystyle p_{x^{\prime},x}-p_{x^{\prime},x}^{2}

\displaystyle=p_{x^{\prime},x}(1-p_{x^{\prime},x})

(52)

and its off-diagonal entries are

\displaystyle p_{x^{\prime},x}\cdot p_{x^{\prime\prime},x}

(53)

where
x^{\prime\prime}\neq x^{\prime}
. We now see that the matrix is diagonally column dominant because the off-diagonal entries (which are all nonnegative) in each row sum up to the corresponding diagonal entry.
∎

Lemma 10

(Quantized grand coupling is completely positive)
.

The map
\mathcal{T}
corresponding to a grand coupling is completely positive.

Proof.

We apply the matricization map (the inverse of the vectorization map) to the transition matrix of the grand coupling in (
25
) and obtain

\displaystyle\mathcal{C}^{*}(\bullet)

\displaystyle=\sum_{r\in\mathcal{R}}\sum_{x,y}\Pr(r)\,|f(x,r)\rangle\langle x|\bullet|y\rangle\langle f(y,r)|.

(54)

After applying the similarity transformation, we obtain

\displaystyle\mathcal{T}^{*}(\bullet)

\displaystyle=\sum_{r\in\mathcal{R}}\sum_{x,y}\Pr(r)\,D^{-1/2}|f(x,r)\rangle\langle x|D^{1/2}\bullet D^{1/2}|y\rangle\langle f(y,r)|D^{-1/2}.

(55)

Thus, the quantized grand coupling
\mathcal{T}
can be written as

\displaystyle\mathcal{T}(\bullet)

\displaystyle=\sum_{r\in\mathcal{R}}T_{r}\bullet T_{r}^{\dagger}

(56)

with Kraus operators

\displaystyle T_{r}

\displaystyle=\sqrt{\Pr(r)}\sum_{x\in\Omega}D^{1/2}|x\rangle\langle f(x,r)|D^{-1/2}.

(57)

The existence of the Kraus representation implies that
\mathcal{T}
is completely positive.
∎

It is beneficial to explicitly verify that the quantized grand coupling has
|\sqrt{\pi}\rangle
as a fixed point. Recall that this already follows from Lemma
8
. We have

\displaystyle\mathcal{T}(|\sqrt{\pi}\rangle\langle\sqrt{\pi}|)

\displaystyle=\sum_{r\in\mathcal{R}}\Pr(r)\sum_{x,y\in\Omega}D^{1/2}|x\rangle\langle f(x,r)|D^{-1/2}|\sqrt{\pi}\rangle\langle\sqrt{\pi}|D^{-1/2}|f(y,r)\rangle\langle y|D^{1/2}

(58)

\displaystyle=\sum_{r\in\mathcal{R}}\Pr(r)\sum_{x,y\in\Omega}D^{1/2}|x\rangle\langle f(x,r)|e\rangle\langle e|f(y,r)\rangle\langle y|D^{1/2}

(59)

\displaystyle=\sum_{x,y\in\Omega}D^{1/2}|x\rangle\langle y|D^{1/2}

(60)

\displaystyle=|\sqrt{\pi}\rangle\langle\sqrt{\pi}|,

(61)

where
|e\rangle=\sum_{x\in\Omega}|x\rangle=(1,1,\ldots,1)^{T}
denotes the all-ones column vector.

4
Convergence rate of quantized Markov chain couplings

We establish here that the channel
{\mathcal{T}}
converges to the pure fixed point
\left|\sqrt{\pi}\right\rangle
and provide a bound on the rate of convergence from an arbitrary initial state in the trace norm distance
\|\cdot\|_{1}
. Conventional approaches to bounding mixing times
[
21
,
22
,
23
,
24
,
23
,
25
]
for quantum Markov chains typically rely on the assumption that the fixed point is a mixed state of full rank. When the fixed points are pure states, we need to analyze the absorption probability into invariant subspaces
[
26
,
27
]
. We will show that the correct projector
Q
onto the subspace of invariant states under
\mathcal{T}
and its complement
Q^{\perp}
are
given by

\displaystyle Q

\displaystyle=\left|\sqrt{\pi}\right\rangle\!\left\langle\sqrt{\pi}\right|

(62)

\displaystyle Q^{\perp}

\displaystyle=\mathbb{I}-Q,

(63)

respectively. To identify the absorbing subspace
[
26
]
and prove convergence to this subspace, cf. Lemma 3.1 and Theorem 3.2 in
[
27
]
, we need to prove the following properties.

Lemma 11

(Fixed point projector)
.

Let
\mathcal{T}
be a completely positive quantized Markov chain coupling of the Markov chain
P
with fixed point
\pi
and coupling
C
. Then,
Q=\left|\sqrt{\pi}\right\rangle\!\left\langle\sqrt{\pi}\right|
, is the maximal projector onto the absorbing subspace that satisfies the following properties:

1.

The projection
Q
is said to
reduce

\mathcal{T}
if it is globally invariant under
\mathcal{T}^{m}
, which means

\displaystyle Q\left[\mathcal{T}^{*}\right]^{m}(A)Q=Q\left[\mathcal{T}^{*}\right]^{m}(QAQ)Q\;\;\;\;\mbox{for all}\;\;\;\;A\in\mathbb{M}_{|\Omega|\times|\Omega|}(\mathbb{C}).

(64)

2.

The projection
Q
is
expanding
under the dynamics of
\mathcal{T}
,
which means

\displaystyle\lim_{m\rightarrow\infty}\left[\mathcal{T}^{*}\right]^{m}(Q)=\mathbb{I}.

(65)

Proof.

For (1), note that equality holds if for all states
\rho
we have

\displaystyle\mathrm{tr}\left[\rho Q\left[\mathcal{T}^{*}\right]^{m}(A)Q\right]=\mathrm{tr}\left[\rho Q\left[\mathcal{T}^{*}\right]^{m}(QAQ)Q\right].

(66)

We can work in the dual picture now and write using the definition of
Q
that

\displaystyle\mathrm{tr}\left[\mathcal{T}^{m}(Q\rho Q)A\right]=\left\langle\sqrt{\pi}\right|\rho\left|\sqrt{\pi}\right\rangle\mathrm{tr}\left[\mathcal{T}^{m}(\left|\sqrt{\pi}\right\rangle\!\left\langle\sqrt{\pi}\right|)A\right].

(67)

Recall that, cf. Lemma
8
, we have that
\mathcal{T}(\left|\sqrt{\pi}\right\rangle\!\left\langle\sqrt{\pi}\right|)=\left|\sqrt{\pi}\right\rangle\!\left\langle\sqrt{\pi}\right|
, so that

\displaystyle\mathrm{tr}\left[\mathcal{T}^{m}(Q\rho Q)A\right]=\left\langle\sqrt{\pi}\right|\rho\left|\sqrt{\pi}\right\rangle\left\langle\sqrt{\pi}\right|A\left|\sqrt{\pi}\right\rangle.

(68)

The same set of arguments gives rise to

\displaystyle\mathrm{tr}\left[\rho Q\left[\mathcal{T}^{*}\right]^{m}(QAQ)Q\right]=\left\langle\sqrt{\pi}\right|\rho\left|\sqrt{\pi}\right\rangle\left\langle\sqrt{\pi}\right|A\left|\sqrt{\pi}\right\rangle,

(69)

showing that both are equal. For (2), we have

\displaystyle(\mathcal{T}^{*})^{m}(Q)

\displaystyle=D^{-1/2}(\mathcal{C}^{*})^{m}(D^{1/2}QD^{1/2})D^{-1/2}

(70)

\displaystyle=D^{-1/2}(\mathcal{C}^{*})^{m}(D^{1/2}|\sqrt{\pi}\rangle\langle\sqrt{\pi}|D^{1/2})D^{-1/2}

(71)

\displaystyle=D^{-1/2}(\mathcal{C}^{*})^{m}(|\pi\rangle\langle\pi|)D^{-1/2}.

(72)

We now use that

\displaystyle\lim_{m\rightarrow\infty}(\mathcal{C}^{*})^{m}(|\pi\rangle\langle\pi|)

\displaystyle=\sum_{x\in\Omega}\pi_{x}|x\rangle\langle x|=D.

(73)

To derive this limes, observe that
(\mathcal{C}^{*})^{m}(|\pi\rangle\langle\pi|)
is equal to

\displaystyle C^{m}(|\pi\rangle\otimes|\pi\rangle)

(74)

in the vectorized representation. The product probability distribution
|\pi\rangle\otimes|\pi\rangle
has
|\pi\rangle
as marginal in both components. The transition matrix
C
preserves
|\pi\rangle
as marginal due to the first coupling condition and
P|\pi\rangle=|\pi\rangle
. But
C
also coalesces the components as
m
increases meaning that the probability of having unequal states in both components goes to
0
. The unique probability distribution that has
|\pi\rangle
as marginal and does not contain unequal terms coincides with
\sum_{x}\pi_{x}|x\rangle\otimes|x\rangle
, which is the vectorization of
D
.

Combining the above, we obtain

\displaystyle\lim_{m\rightarrow\infty}(\mathcal{T}^{*})^{m}(Q)

\displaystyle=\mathbb{I}.

(75)

This is equivalent to

\displaystyle\lim_{m\rightarrow\infty}(\mathcal{T}^{*})^{m}(Q^{\perp})

\displaystyle=0,

(76)

which implies that
Q
is the fixed point of
\mathcal{T}
.
∎

Having established these properties of
Q
and
\mathcal{T}
, we can now rely on the results of
[
27
]
that prove that
\mathcal{T}^{m}
converges to the invariant subspace with the projector
Q
. In particular, these results imply that
\left|\sqrt{\pi}\right\rangle
is the unique fixed point of the quantized Markov chain coupling
\mathcal{T}
.
However, rather than referring to
[
27
]
, we take a more direct approach and follow
[
28
]
to derive a quantitative bound on the convergence rate, which also immediately implies that
Q
is the correct absorbing projector for
\mathcal{T}
.

Lemma 12

(Convergence bounds)
.

Let
\mathcal{T}
and
Q
be given as above. If we can find an integer
m
for some
\varepsilon>0
so that
\mathrm{tr}\left[Q^{\perp}\mathcal{T}^{m}(\rho_{0})\right]<\varepsilon
for all density matrices
\rho_{0}
, then

\displaystyle\left\|\mathcal{T}^{k}(\rho_{0})-\left|\sqrt{\pi}\right\rangle\!\left\langle\sqrt{\pi}\right|\right\|_{1}\leq 2\sqrt{\varepsilon},

(77)

for all
k\geq m
.

Proof.

We set
\rho_{m}=\mathcal{T}^{m}(\rho)
and write
\mathrm{tr}[Q^{\perp}\rho_{m}]=1-\mathrm{tr}[Q\rho_{m}]<\varepsilon
. Now, following the argument in
[
28
]
we apply the gentle measurement lemma
[
29
]
for the projective measurement
Q
so that we have the trace norm bound

\displaystyle\left\|\rho_{m}-\frac{Q\rho_{m}Q}{\mathrm{tr}[Q\rho_{m}]}\right\|_{1}\leq 2\sqrt{\varepsilon}.

(78)

Given our definition of
Q
, we immediately have that
(Q\rho_{m}Q)(\mathrm{tr}[Q\rho_{m}])^{-1}=\left|\sqrt{\pi}\right\rangle\!\left\langle\sqrt{\pi}\right|
, which gives us the bound in the lemma for all
k\geq m
by applying the data processing inequality
[
29
]
and observing that the projector reduces
\mathcal{T}
. ∎

Using these results, we therefore need to show that
\mathrm{tr}\left[Q^{\perp}\mathcal{T}^{m}(\rho_{0})\right]<\varepsilon
for pairs of
m
and
\varepsilon
. We will establish a connection between the quantized Markov coupling
\mathcal{T}
and the coalescence time of the classical Markov chain coupling
C
. We need to introduce some terminology. We define the edge states for
x,y\in\Omega
as

\displaystyle|-_{xy}\rangle

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}\big(|x\rangle-|y\rangle\big).

(79)

We refer to the matrices

\displaystyle|-_{xy}\rangle\langle-_{xy}|

(80)

as elementary Laplacians and call any linear combination of them a graph Laplacian. Note that
Q^{\perp}
is related to the
\pi
-weighted graph Laplacian of the complete graph on
\Omega
.

Proposition 13
.

The rescaled projector
D^{1/2}Q^{\perp}D^{1/2}
can be expressed as convex combination of elementary Laplacians. More precisely, we have

\displaystyle D^{1/2}Q^{\perp}D^{1/2}

\displaystyle=\sum_{x,y}\pi_{x}\pi_{y}|-_{xy}\rangle\langle-_{xy}|.

(81)

Proof.

The lemma statement is established by the following elementary calculations:

\displaystyle D^{1/2}Q^{\perp}D^{1/2}

\displaystyle=D^{1/2}\big(I-|\sqrt{\pi}\rangle\langle\sqrt{\pi}|\big)D^{1/2}

(82)

\displaystyle=D-|\pi\rangle\langle\pi|

(83)

\displaystyle=\sum_{x}\pi_{x}|x\rangle\langle x|-\sum_{x,y}\pi_{x}\pi_{y}|x\rangle\langle y|

(84)

\displaystyle=\sum_{x,y}\pi_{x}\pi_{y}|x\rangle\langle x|-\sum_{x,y}\pi_{x}\pi_{y}|x\rangle\langle y|

(85)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\sum_{x,y}\pi_{x}\pi_{y}\big(|x\rangle\langle x|-|x\rangle\langle y|-|y\rangle\langle x|+|y\rangle\langle y|\big)

(86)

\displaystyle=\sum_{x,y}\pi_{x}\pi_{y}|-_{xy}\rangle\langle-_{xy}|.

(87)

∎

We introduced these decompositions into graph Laplacians because the quantum map
\mathcal{C}^{*}
preserves this structure as shown in the lemma below.

Lemma 14

(Preservation of graph Laplacians)
.

The map
\mathcal{C}^{*}
preserves graph Laplacians. For
x\neq y
, we have

\displaystyle\mathcal{C}^{*}(|-_{x,y}\rangle\langle-_{x,y}|)

\displaystyle=\sum_{x^{\prime},y^{\prime}}c_{(x^{\prime},y^{\prime}),(x,y)}|-_{x^{\prime},y^{\prime}}\rangle\langle-_{x^{\prime},y^{\prime}}|.

(88)

Proof.

We have

\displaystyle|-_{x,y}\rangle\langle-_{x,y}|

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\big(|x\rangle\langle x|-|x\rangle\langle y|-|y\rangle\langle x|+|y\rangle\langle y|\big).

(89)

Let us derive how
\mathcal{C}^{*}
acts on these terms. We will make use of the coupling conditions stated in Section
2
.

We have

\displaystyle\mathcal{C}^{*}(|x\rangle\langle x|)

\displaystyle=\sum_{x^{\prime}}c_{(x^{\prime},x^{\prime}),(x,x)}|x^{\prime}\rangle\langle x^{\prime}|

(90)

\displaystyle=\sum_{x^{\prime}}p_{x^{\prime},x}|x^{\prime}\rangle\langle x^{\prime}|

(91)

\displaystyle=\sum_{x^{\prime}}\sum_{y^{\prime}}c_{(x^{\prime},y^{\prime}),(x,y)}|x^{\prime}\rangle\langle x^{\prime}|,

(92)

which holds for any
y\neq x
. To obtain this identity, we used the first coupling condition and the second coupling condition.

Analogously, we have

\displaystyle\mathcal{C}^{*}(|y\rangle\langle y|)

\displaystyle=\sum_{x^{\prime}}\sum_{y^{\prime}}c_{(x^{\prime},y^{\prime}),(x,y)}|y^{\prime}\rangle\langle y^{\prime}|,

(93)

which holds for any
x\neq y
.

For
x\neq y
, we have

\displaystyle\mathcal{C}^{*}(|x\rangle\langle y|)

\displaystyle=\sum_{x^{\prime}}\sum_{y^{\prime}}c_{(x^{\prime},y^{\prime}),(x,y)}|x^{\prime}\rangle\langle y^{\prime}|

(94)

\displaystyle\mathcal{C}^{*}(|y\rangle\langle x|)

\displaystyle=\sum_{x^{\prime}}\sum_{y^{\prime}}c_{(y^{\prime},x^{\prime}),(y,x)}|y^{\prime}\rangle\langle x^{\prime}|

(95)

\displaystyle=\sum_{x^{\prime}}\sum_{y^{\prime}}c_{(x^{\prime},y^{\prime}),(x,y)}|y^{\prime}\rangle\langle x^{\prime}|

(96)

We used the third coupling condition to establish the last inequality.

Putting everything together using linearity, we arrive at the lemma statement.
∎

Lemma 15
.

The probability
P_{x,y}\{\tau_{\mathrm{coal}}>m\}
that the two components have not coalesced after making
m
transitions according to the transition matrix
C
when starting in the initial state
(x,y)
can be written as

\displaystyle\Pr_{x,y}\{\tau_{\mathrm{coal}}>m\}

\displaystyle=\mathrm{tr}\Big(\big[\mathcal{C}^{*}\big]^{m}\big(|-_{x,y}\rangle\langle-_{x,y}|\big)\Big).

(97)

Proof.

We apply the previous lemma to evaluate
[\mathcal{C}^{*}]^{m}(|-_{x,y}\rangle\langle-_{x,y}|)
. After taking the trace, the resulting expression simplifies further because the trace of each edge Laplacian (with
x^{\prime}\neq y^{\prime}
) is equal to
1
. This yields

\displaystyle\mathrm{tr}\Big(\big[\mathcal{C}^{*}\big]^{m}\big(|-_{x,y}\rangle\langle-_{x,y}|\big)\Big)

\displaystyle=\sum_{x^{\prime},y^{\prime}}[C^{m}]_{(x^{\prime},y^{\prime}),(x,y)}\mathrm{tr}\big(|-_{x^{\prime},y^{\prime}}\rangle\langle-_{x^{\prime},y^{\prime}}|\big)

(98)

\displaystyle=\sum_{x^{\prime}\neq y^{\prime}}[C^{m}]_{(x^{\prime},y^{\prime}),(x,y)}

(99)

\displaystyle=\Pr_{x,y}\{\tau_{\mathrm{coal}}>m\},

(100)

where we used the simplification introduced in Remark
3
for the final step.
∎

Lemma 16

(Classical coalescence implies rapid quantum convergence)
.

Consider an ergodic Markov chain with transition matrix
P=(p_{x^{\prime},x})
and stationary distribution
\pi=(\pi_{x})
.
Assume that we are given a Markov chain coupling with transition matrix
C
such that the corresponding quantized coupling
\mathcal{T}
is completely positive.

Then, we have

\displaystyle\mathrm{tr}\Big(Q^{\perp}\mathcal{T}^{m}(\rho_{0})\Big)

\displaystyle\leq\frac{1}{\pi_{*}}\Pr_{\mathrm{max}}\{\tau_{\mathrm{coal}}>m\},

(101)

where
\pi_{*}=\min\{\pi_{x}:x\in\Omega\}
.

Proof.

We have

\displaystyle\mathrm{tr}\Big(Q^{\perp}\mathcal{T}^{m}(\rho_{0})\Big)

(102)

\displaystyle=\mathrm{tr}\Big(\big(\mathcal{T}^{*}\big)^{m}\big(Q^{\perp}\big)\rho_{0}\Big)

(103)

\displaystyle=\mathrm{tr}\Big(D^{-1/2}\big(\mathcal{C}^{*}\big)^{m}\big(D^{1/2}Q^{\perp}D^{1/2}\big)D^{-1/2}\rho_{0}\Big)

(104)

\displaystyle\leq\frac{1}{\pi_{*}}\mathrm{tr}\Big(\big(\mathcal{C}^{*}\big)^{m}\big(D^{1/2}Q^{\perp}D^{1/2}\big)\Big)

(105)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\pi_{*}}\sum_{x,y}\pi_{x}\pi_{y}\,\mathrm{tr}\left(\big(\mathcal{C}^{*}\big)^{m}\big(|-_{x,y}\rangle\langle-_{x,y}|\big)\right)

(106)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\pi_{*}}\sum_{x,y}\pi_{x}\pi_{y}\,\Pr_{x,y}\{\tau_{\mathrm{coal}}>m\}

(107)

\displaystyle\leq\frac{1}{\pi_{*}}\Pr_{\mathrm{max}}\{\tau_{\mathrm{coal}}>m\}

(108)

First, we switch from the Schrödinger picture
\mathcal{T}
to the Heisenberg picture
\mathcal{T}^{*}
.
Second, we appeal to the fact that
\mathcal{T}^{*}
is obtained from
\mathcal{C}^{*}
by the similarity transformation
\mathcal{T}^{*}(\bullet)=D^{-1/2}\mathcal{C}^{*}(D^{1/2}\bullet D^{1/2})D^{-1/2}
, which facilitates the computation of the power
(\mathcal{T}^{*})^{m}
.
Third, we rely on the bound
\mathrm{tr}(AB)\leq\lambda_{\mathrm{max}}(B)\mathrm{tr}(A)
that holds for arbitrary positive semidefinite matrices
A
and
B
. We apply it with
A=(\mathcal{C}^{*})^{m}(|-_{x,y}\rangle\langle-_{x,y}|)
and
B=D^{-1/2}\rho_{0}D^{-1/2}
and use
\lambda_{\mathrm{max}}(B)\leq\frac{1}{\pi_{*}}
.
Fourth, we invoke Lemma
13
to write
D^{1/2}Q^{\perp}D^{1/2}
as a convex combination of elementary Laplacians.
Fifth, we invoke Lemma
15
to write the traces of
[\mathcal{C}^{*}]^{m}(|-_{x,y}\rangle\langle-_{x,y}|)
as
\Pr_{x,y}\{\tau_{\mathrm{coal}}>m\}
.
Finally, we use that the convex combination of
\Pr_{x,y}\{\tau_{\mathrm{coal}}>m\}
is upper bounded by
\Pr_{\mathrm{max}}\{\tau_{\mathrm{coal}}>m\}
.
∎

Lemma 17
.

Consider an arbitrary Markov chain with transition matrix
P
. For any Markov chain coupling with transition matrix
C
, we have

\displaystyle\Pr_{\mathrm{max}}\{\tau_{\mathrm{coal}}\geq\ell\cdot m\}

\displaystyle\leq\left(\Pr_{\mathrm{max}}\{\tau_{\mathrm{coal}}\geq m\}\right)^{\ell}.

(109)

Proof.

The coupling transition matrix
C
acts on state space
\Omega\times\Omega
, which we decompose into the disjoint union of
\Omega_{\mathrm{diag}}=\{(x,x):x\in\Omega\}
and its complement denoted by
\bar{\Omega}_{\mathrm{diag}}
.

The matrix
C
has the following block structure

\displaystyle\left(\begin{array}[]{cc}P&A\\
0&B\end{array}\right)

with respect to this decomposition. Observe that
P\in\mathbb{R}^{N\times N}
is the transition matrix of the Markov chain. The matrices
A\in\mathbb{R}^{N\times\bar{N}}
and
B\in\mathbb{R}^{\bar{N}\times\bar{N}}
are some matrices. The reason for this particular block structure is that once the components meet, they stay together forever. In addition,
C
restricted to
\Omega_{\mathrm{diag}}
acts as
P
.

The power
C^{m}
has the block structure

\displaystyle\left(\begin{array}[]{cc}P^{m}&A^{(m)}\\
0&B^{m}\end{array}\right).

Observe that

\displaystyle\|B^{m}\|_{1}

\displaystyle=\max_{x\neq y}\sum_{x^{\prime}\neq y^{\prime}}[C^{m}]_{(x^{\prime},y^{\prime}),(x,y)}

(114)

\displaystyle=\max_{x\neq y}\Pr_{x,y}\{\tau_{\mathrm{coal}}>m\}

(115)

\displaystyle=\Pr_{\mathrm{max}}\{\tau_{\mathrm{coal}}>m\}.

(116)

We assume that
m
is chosen to be large enough so that

\displaystyle\Pr_{\mathrm{max}}\{\tau_{\mathrm{coal}}>m\}\leq\frac{1}{2}.

(117)

The power
C^{\ell m}
has the block structure

\displaystyle C^{\ell m}

\displaystyle=\left(\begin{array}[]{cc}P^{\ell m}&A^{(\ell m)}\\
0&B^{\ell m}\end{array}\right).

We have

\displaystyle\Pr_{\mathrm{max}}\{\tau_{\mathrm{coal}}>\ell m\}

\displaystyle=\|B^{\ell m}\|_{1}

(120)

\displaystyle\leq\|B^{m}\|_{1}^{\ell}

(121)

\displaystyle=\left(\Pr_{\mathrm{max}}\{\tau_{\mathrm{coal}}>m\}\right)^{\ell}

(122)

since the matrix norm
\|\bullet\|_{1}
is sub-multiplicative.
∎

We can now collect the previously established results and prove the main convergence result.

Theorem 18

(main)
.

Let
\mathcal{T}
denote a quantized coupling of the Markov chain
P
with stationary distribution
\pi=(\pi_{x}:x\in\Omega)
. Given any initial state
\rho
, this map converges to the qsample
|\sqrt{\pi}\rangle\langle\sqrt{\pi}|
in trace norm distance as

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}\big\|\mathcal{T}^{m}(\rho)-|\sqrt{\pi}\rangle\langle\sqrt{\pi}|\big\|_{\mathrm{tr}}

\displaystyle\leq\sqrt{\varepsilon},

(123)

after

\displaystyle m\geq\frac{1}{2}\log_{2}\left(\frac{1}{\varepsilon\;\pi_{*}}\right)\;t_{\mathrm{couple}}

(124)

steps, where
\pi_{*}=\min\{\pi_{x}:x\in\Omega\}
.

Proof.

The result follows from a combination of the results above, when using the reducing and expanding projector
Q=\left|\sqrt{\pi}\right\rangle\!\left\langle\sqrt{\pi}\right|
for which we know that due to Lemma
16
that

\displaystyle\mathrm{tr}\Big(Q^{\perp}\mathcal{T}^{m}(\rho_{0})\Big)

\displaystyle\leq\frac{1}{\pi_{*}}\Pr_{\mathrm{max}}\{\tau_{\mathrm{coal}}>m\}.

(125)

Setting m =
\ell\,t_{\mathrm{couple}}
, we can bound
\Pr_{\mathrm{max}}\{\tau_{\mathrm{coal}}>m\}\leq 4^{-\ell}
due to Lemma
17
and the definition of
t_{\mathrm{couple}}
. Setting
\ell\geq 2^{-1}\log_{2}\left(\frac{1}{\varepsilon\;\pi_{*}}\right)
, we have that
\mathrm{tr}(Q^{\perp}\mathcal{T}^{m}(\rho_{0}))\leq\varepsilon
so that we can apply Lemma
12
and read off the bound as stated in the theorem after
m
steps.
∎

5
Implementation of quantized grand couplings

We now show how to efficiently implement quantized grand couplings. The key insight is that the Kraus operators
T_{r}
in (
57
) are sparse for many interesting Markov chains studied in the literature.

5.1
Simulation of sparse tcp-maps

For the sake of completeness, we briefly describe how to simulate tcp-maps given access to block-encodings of their Kraus operators. To keep the presentation simple, we assume that the block-encodings perfectly encode the Kraus operators. We refer the reader to
[
30
, Lemma 2]

Lemma 19

(Simulation of tcp-maps)
.

Let

\displaystyle\mathcal{N}(\bullet)

\displaystyle=\sum_{k=1}^{\kappa}A_{k}\bullet A_{k}^{\dagger}

(126)

be an arbitrary tcp-map acting on
\mathbb{C}^{d}
with Kraus operators
A_{k}
. Assume we are given perfect block-encodings of the Kraus operators
A_{k}
by the unitaries

\displaystyle U_{k}

\displaystyle=\left(\begin{array}[]{cc}A_{k}&C_{k}\\
B_{k}&D_{k}\end{array}\right),

acting on
\mathbb{C}^{2}\otimes\mathbb{C}^{d}
(where
B_{k}
,
C_{k}
, and
D_{k}
can be any matrices acting on
\mathbb{C}^{d}
as long as
U_{k}
is unitary). Then, there exists an efficient quantum circuit that simulates the tcp-map
\mathcal{N}
.

Proof.

Adjoin a new control register
\mathbb{C}^{m}
. Define the controlled unitary

\displaystyle W

\displaystyle=\sum_{j=1}|j\rangle\langle j|\otimes U_{j}

(129)

acting on
\mathbb{C}^{\kappa}\otimes\mathbb{C}^{2}\otimes\mathbb{C}^{d}
and the state

\displaystyle|\mu\rangle

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\sqrt{\kappa}}\sum_{k=1}^{\kappa}|k\rangle.

(130)

Let
|\xi\rangle\in\mathbb{C}^{d}
be an arbitrary initial state. Applying
W
to the state

\displaystyle|\Psi\rangle

\displaystyle=|\mu\rangle\otimes|0\rangle\otimes|\xi\rangle.

(131)

results in the state

\displaystyle|\Phi\rangle

\displaystyle=W|\Psi\rangle

(132)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\sqrt{\kappa}}\sum_{k=1}^{\kappa}|k\rangle\otimes U_{k}\big(|0\rangle\otimes|\xi\rangle\big)

(133)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\sqrt{\kappa}}\sum_{k=1}^{\kappa}|k\rangle\otimes|0\rangle\otimes A_{k}|\xi\rangle+\frac{1}{\sqrt{\kappa}}\sum_{k=1}^{\kappa}|k\rangle\otimes|1\rangle\otimes B_{k}|\xi\rangle

(134)

It will be helpful to exchange the positions of the
\mathbb{C}^{\kappa}
register and the
\mathbb{C}^{2}
register. After this rearrangement, we can express everything as

\displaystyle|\Psi\rangle

\displaystyle=|0\rangle\otimes|\psi\rangle

(135)

\displaystyle|\Phi\rangle

\displaystyle=|0\rangle\otimes\frac{1}{\sqrt{\kappa}}\,|\phi_{0}\rangle+|1\rangle\otimes\sqrt{1-\frac{1}{\kappa}}\,|\phi_{1}\rangle,

(136)

where

\displaystyle|\psi\rangle

\displaystyle=|\mu\rangle\otimes|\xi\rangle

(137)

\displaystyle|\phi_{0}\rangle

\displaystyle=\sum_{k=1}^{\kappa}|k\rangle\otimes A_{k}|\psi\rangle

(138)

\displaystyle|\phi_{1}\rangle

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\sqrt{\kappa-1}}\sum_{k=1}^{\kappa}|k\rangle\otimes B_{k}|\psi\rangle.

(139)

Observe that
|\phi_{0}\rangle
and
|\phi_{1}\rangle
have the norm
1
. For
|\phi_{0}\rangle
, we have

\displaystyle\langle\phi_{0}|\phi_{0}\rangle

\displaystyle=\sum_{k=1}^{\kappa}\langle\psi|A_{k}^{\dagger}A_{k}|\psi\rangle=1

(140)

since the Kraus operators satisfy the condition
\sum_{k}A_{k}^{\dagger}A_{k}=I_{d}
. For
\phi_{1}
, we have

\displaystyle\langle\phi_{1}|\phi_{1}\rangle

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\kappa-1}\sum_{k=1}^{\kappa}\langle\psi|B_{k}^{\dagger}B_{k}|\psi\rangle=1

(141)

since
\sum_{k=1}^{\kappa}B_{k}^{\dagger}B_{k}=(\kappa-1)I_{d}
. The latter is seen as follows. For each
\kappa
, the top-left block of
U_{k}^{\dagger}U_{k}
is given by the formula
A_{k}^{\dagger}A_{k}+B_{k}^{\dagger}B_{k}
, which must be equal to
I_{d}
since
U_{k}
is unitary. Summing over
k=1,\ldots,\kappa
yields
\kappa\,I_{d}=\sum_{k}A_{k}^{\dagger}A_{k}+B_{k}^{\dagger}B_{k}=I_{d}+\sum_{k}B_{k}^{\dagger}B_{k}
, where we used the Kraus condition.

Let us emphasize that the amplitudes
1/\sqrt{\kappa}
and
\sqrt{1-1/\kappa}
of
|\phi_{0}\rangle
and
|\phi_{1}\rangle
, respectively, in the decomposition of
|\Phi\rangle
in (
136
) are constants (that is, they are completely independent of
|\xi\rangle
even though
|\Phi\rangle
depends on
|\xi\rangle
). This makes it possible to apply oblivious amplitude amplification to map the initial state
|\Psi\rangle
to
|\Phi\rangle
. The adjective “oblivious” in this context means that reflecting around the initial state
|\Psi\rangle
is not necessary. Define the reflection
R=2P-I
, where
P=|0\rangle\langle 0|\otimes I_{\kappa}\otimes I_{d}
, and the Grover iteration
G=-WRW^{\dagger}R
. We refer the reader to
[
31
, Lemma 3.6]
, which shows that
O(\sqrt{\kappa})
Grover iterations suffice to map
|\Psi\rangle
to
|\Phi\rangle
.

To summarize, the discussion above shows that we can prepare the state

\displaystyle|0\rangle\otimes\sum_{k=1}^{\kappa}|k\rangle\otimes A_{k}|\xi\rangle

(142)

on
\mathbb{C}^{2}\otimes\mathbb{C}^{\kappa}\otimes\mathbb{C}^{d}
starting from an arbitrary state
|\xi\rangle
in the third register.

It now suffices to trace out the
\mathbb{C}^{2}
and
\mathbb{C}^{\kappa}
registers to implement the tcp-map
\mathcal{N}
. Discarding the two register prepares the state

\displaystyle\sum_{k=1}^{\kappa}A_{k}|\xi\rangle\langle\xi|A_{k}^{\dagger}.

(143)

It is important that this works for any initial state
|\xi\rangle
on
\mathbb{C}^{d}
.

5.2
Random walk on the hypercube

This is based on
[
10
, Example 5.3.1]
.
Consider a lazy walk on the hypercube
\{0,1\}^{n}
: at each step the walker remains at the current position with probability
1/2
and with probability
1/2
moves to a position chosen uniformly at random among the neighboring vertices (bit-strings at Hamming distance
1
).

A convenient way to generate the lazy walk is as follows: pick one of the
n
coordinates uniformly at random, and refresh the bit at this coordinate with a random bit (one which equals
0
or
1
with probability
1/2
).

This method for running the walk leads to the following coupling of two walks: first,
pick among the
n
coordinates uniformly at random; suppose that the coordinate
i
is selected. In both walks, replace the bit at coordinate
i
with the same random fair bit.

A moment’s thought reveals that this is a coupling that satisfies all the conditions required to define a valid tcp-map.
For
i\in\{1,\ldots,n\}
and
b\in\{0,1\}
define the Kraus operator
T_{i,b}
acting on the computational basis of
(\mathbb{C}^{2})^{\otimes n}
as follows

\displaystyle T_{i,b}|x_{1},\ldots,x_{i},\ldots x_{n}\rangle

\displaystyle=|x_{1},\ldots,b,\ldots x_{n}\rangle.

(144)

We now have

\displaystyle\mathcal{T}(\bullet)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\sum_{b\in\{0,1\}}T_{i,b}\bullet T_{i,b}^{\dagger}.

(145)

We do not have to apply the similarity transformation because the limiting distribution is the uniform distribution. We can efficiently implement the tcp-map because the Kraus operators are
n
-sparse.

If
\tau
is the first time when all of the coordinates have been selected at least once, then the two walkers agree with each other from time
\tau
onwards. The distribution of
\tau
is exactly the same as the coupon’s random variable. One can show that

\displaystyle\Pr\{\tau>n\log n+cn\}\leq e^{-c}.

(146)

5.3
Random colorings

In this and the next subsection, we consider two chains whose state spaces are contained in a set of a form
S^{V}
, where
V
is the vertex set of a graph and
S
is a finite set. The elements of
S^{V}
called configurations, are the functions from
V
to
S
. We visualize a configuration as a labeling of vertices with elements of
S
.

A proper
q
-coloring of a graph
G=(V,E)
with vertex set
V
and edge set
E
is an element
x
of
\{1,2,\ldots,q\}^{V}
, the set of functions from
V
to
\{1,2,\ldots,q\}
, such that
x(v)\neq x(w)
for all edges
\{v,w\}\in E
.

For a given configuration
x
and a vertex
v
, call a color
k
allowable at
v
if
k
is different from all colors assigned to neighbors of
v
. That is, a color is allowable at
v
if it does not belong to the set
\{x(w):w\sim v\}
.

Let
\mathcal{X}
denote the collection of all proper
q
-colorings of
G
and
\pi
denote the uniform distribution on
\mathcal{X}
.
In
[
10
, Example 3.5]
a
Metropolis
chain for
\pi
is constructed. A transition for this chain is made by first selecting a vertex
v
uniformly from
V
and then selecting a color
k
uniformly from
\{1,\ldots,q\}
. If color
k
is allowable at
v
, then vertex
v
is assigned color
k
. Otherwise, no transition is made.

Analogously to a single Metropolis chain on colorings
\mathcal{X}
, the grand coupling at each move generates a single vertex and color pair
(v,k)
, uniformly at random from
V\times\{1,\ldots,q\}
and independently of the choices made in previous time steps. For each
x\in\mathcal{X}
, the coloring is updated by attempting to re-color vertex
v
with color
k
, accepting the update if and only if the proposed new color
k
is allowable at
v
. (If a re-coloring is not accepted, then chain remain in its current state).

For two different colorings
x,y\in\mathcal{X}
, let
X_{m}^{x}
and
X_{m}^{y}
denote the state of the grand coupling after
m
steps when starting the two components in
x
and
y
. One can show that

\displaystyle\Pr\{X_{m}^{x}\neq X_{m}^{y}\}

\displaystyle\leq n\;\exp\big(-m\;c_{\mathrm{met}}(\Delta,q)/n\big),

(147)

where
\Delta
denotes the maximum degree in the graph and
c_{\mathrm{met}}=1-(3\Delta)/q
.
That is, as long as
q>3\Delta
, the probability that the grand coupling has not coalesced decays exponentially in the number of time steps
m
.

We can now write the quantized coupling acting on
(\mathbb{C}^{q})^{\otimes n}
by

\displaystyle\mathcal{T}(\bullet)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{qn}\sum_{v\in V}\sum_{k=1}^{q}T_{(v,k)}\bullet T_{(v,k)}^{\dagger}.

(148)

The (unnormalized) Kraus
T_{(v,k)}
are defined below.
For a coloring
x\in\{1,\ldots,q\}^{V}
(that does not need to be proper), define the operator
T_{(v,k)}
by its action on the computational basis states according to the three cases:

•

T_{(v,k)}|x\rangle=|x\rangle
if
x
is not a proper colorings

•

T_{(v,k)}|x\rangle=|x\rangle
if
x
is a proper coloring, but color
k
is not allowable at
v

•

T_{(v,k)}|x\rangle=|x^{\prime}\rangle
if
x
is a proper coloring and re-coloring vertex
v
with color
k
yields the proper coloring
x^{\prime}

We do not have to apply a similarity transformation because the limiting distribution
\pi
is uniform.
The resulting tcp-map can be implemented efficiently because the Kraus operators are
(qn)
-sparse.

5.4
Hardcore model with fugacity

A hardcore configuration on the graph
G=(V,E)
is a function

x\in\{0,1\}^{V}
satisfying
x(v)x(w)=0
whenever
\{v,w\}\in E
; that is,
no two adjacent vertices are simultaneously occupied. A vertex
v
with

x(v)=1
is called occupied, and a vertex
v
with
x(v)=0
is called vacant. Let
\mathcal{X}

denote the set of all hardcore configurations on
G
.

The hardcore model with fugacity
\lambda
is the probability distribution
\pi
on hardcore configurations
x\in\mathcal{X}
defined by

\displaystyle\pi(x)

\displaystyle=\frac{\lambda^{\sum_{v\in V}x(v)}}{Z(\lambda)}.

(149)

The partition function
Z(\lambda)=\sum_{x\in\mathcal{X}}\lambda^{\sum_{v\in V}x(v)}

normalizes
\pi
to have unit total mass.

In
[
10
, 3.3.4]
a Glauber chain for
\pi
is constructed. It updates a configuration
X_{m}
to a new configuration
X_{m+1}
as follows:

If there is a neighbor
w^{\prime}
of
w
such that
X_{m}(w^{\prime})=1
, then set
X_{m+1}(w)=0
; otherwise, let

\displaystyle X_{m+1}(w)=\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}1&\mbox{with probability $\lambda/(1+\lambda)$,}\\
0&\mbox{with probability $1/(1+\lambda)$.}\end{array}\right.

Furthermore, set
X_{m+1}(v)=X_{m}(v)
for all
v\neq w
.

We use the grand coupling which is run as follows: a vertex
v
is selected uniformly at random, and a coin with probability
\lambda/(1+\lambda)
of heads is tossed, independently of the choice of
v
. Each hardcore configuration
x
is updated using
v
and the result of the coin toss. If the coin lands tails, any particle present at
v
is removed. If the coin lands heads and all neighbors of
v
are unoccupied in the configuration
x
, then a particle is placed at
v
.

The arguments in the proof of
[
10
, Theorem 5.9]
show that

\displaystyle\Pr\{X_{m}^{x}\neq X_{m}^{y}\}

\displaystyle\leq n\exp\big(-m\;c_{H}(\lambda)/n\big),

(152)

where
c_{H}(\lambda)=[1+\lambda(1-\Delta)]/(1+\lambda)
.

Similarly to the previous example, we see that the resulting tcp-map can be realized with
n
-sparse Kraus operators. However, in the present example we need to perform the similarity transformation because the stationary distribution
\pi
is not uniform. This can be done efficiently because it is easy to compute the ratios
\pi(x)/\pi(y)
for two hardcore configurations. The hard-to-compute partition function
Z(\lambda)
cancels out, and we are left with

\displaystyle\lambda^{\sum_{v\in V}x(v)-y(v)}.

(153)

5.5
General theorem

It is now straightforward to see that the following general result holds.

Theorem 20

(Efficient implementation of quantized grand couplings)
.

Assume that we have a random mapping representation such that

•

we can efficiently compute the image and pre-images of the function
f:\Omega\times\mathcal{R}\rightarrow\Omega

•

we can efficiently compute the probabilities
\Pr(r)

•

we can efficiently compute the ratios
\pi(x)/\pi(y)
for the limiting distribution.

Then, the Kraus operators

\displaystyle T_{r}

\displaystyle=\sqrt{\Pr(r)}\sum_{x\in\Omega}D^{1/2}|x\rangle\langle f(x,r)|D^{-1/2}.

(154)

of the quantized coupling
\mathcal{T}
are
|\mathcal{R}|
-sparse. Thus,
\mathcal{T}
can be implemented efficiently.

6
Conclusions and open problems

We have presented a novel method for efficiently preparing qsamples of stationary distributions of certain ergodic Markov chains. Our method can be turned in to an efficient quantum algorithm when there exists a grand coupling that is sparse and fast coalescing.

Our quantization serves a purpose different from Szegedy’s Markov chain quantization
[
2
]
, which establishes a connection between the classical mixing time (through the spectral gap) of reversible ergodic Markov chains and the complexity of block-encoding a reflection around the qsamples (or equivalently, a projection onto the qsample).

Let us briefly discuss how Szegedy method can be used to prepare qsamples. In general, it will be difficult to find good initial state that has sufficiently large over the qsample. Therefore, a Grover-type algorithm will take long to prepare the desired state. However, there are situations when we have a sequence of slowly varying Markov chains. Here slowly varying means that the stationary distributions of adjacent chains are close to each other in total variation distance. The latter implies that the corresponding qsamples have large overlap. This observation has been leveraged in
[
20
]
to prepare qsamples.

We propose a few open research problem to explore in the context of the approach presented here.

1.

We have seen that not all couplings of Markov chains give rise to completely positive maps, cf. Appendix. However, the more common ones, such as the trivial product coupling, cf. Definition
4
, and the Grand canonical coupling in Definition
5
do. There are considerably more general couplings that can be constructed and a very natural question to ask is, what other coupling gives rise to a tcp-map following our construction. Moreover, what couplings give rise to maps that can efficiently be implemented as a quantum circuit?

2.

The contraction bound in Lemma
16
reads
\mathrm{tr}\left(Q^{\perp}\mathcal{T}^{m}(\rho_{0})\right)\leq\pi_{*}^{-1}

\Pr_{\mathrm{max}}\{\tau_{\mathrm{coal}}>m\}
. Note that since
Q^{\perp}
is projector the value of
\mathrm{tr}\left(Q^{\perp}\mathcal{T}^{m}(\rho_{0})\right)\leq 1
is actually always bounded by
1
. The prefactor
\pi_{*}^{-1}
, therefore seems like a substantial overestimation, that ultimately leads to an increase in the convergence time by
\log_{2}(\pi_{*}^{-1})
when compared to the classical coupling time. The immediate question is whether this prefactor is only owed to our proof techniques and can be dropped, or whether it is indeed fundamental.

3.

We have discussed the relationship and differences between our construction and the qsample reflection by using Szegedy walks. The work
[
34
]
introduced a generalization of Szegedy walk unitaries that apply to quantum detailed balanced Lindbladians, such as the Davies generator
[
35
]
. Here, one reflects about a purification of thermal state fixed point instead of a qsample of a stationary distribution. In some sense such a purification can be seen as a generalization of qsamples. One can now ask, whether it is possible to construct a tcp-map that converges to this purification instead of the mixed state by making similar arguments as done in this work.

n
