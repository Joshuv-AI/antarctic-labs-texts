AResKGLM: a graph-grounded language-model framework for interpretable multi-hop antimicrobial resistance reasoning
Authors: Jie Ren, Ziyi Yang, Wei Liu, Man Tat Alexander Ng
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractAntimicrobial resistance (AMR) threatens microbiology and microbiome bioinformatics because resistance phenotypes are shaped by interactions among genes, mobile genetic elements, and functional environments across microbial communities. Prioritizing resistance determinants requires models that reason across knowledge graphs (KGs) linking genes, proteins, pathways, drugs, and microbial phenotypes. Existing graph-based methods compress this evidence into scalar scores, whereas large language models can produce explanations not grounded in structured evidence. We developed AResKGLM (Antimicrobial Resistance Knowledge Graph Language Model), a graph-grounded language-model framework for interpretable microbial AMR bioinformatics that serializes breadth-first-search-retrieved multi-hop paths and per-entity biomedical descriptions into a structured Context–Path–Question prompt. Llama-3-8B and DeepSeek-R1-7B are adapted with QLoRA to produce binary link predictions and concise reasoning traces. On the KIDs benchmark, AResKGLM (Llama-3-8B) achieved F1 = 0.8482, outperforming KG-BERT (0.7213), NBFNet (0.5260), and ULTRA (0.2541) (paired Wilcoxon ). Its advantage increased with reasoning depth: F1 decreased from 0.9197 at 2 hops to 0.8148 at 6 hops, whereas KG-BERT dropped from 0.8110 to 0.6716. Counterfactual path corruption produced an apparent F1 of 0.000, mechanically forced by the probe label assignment; the operative diagnostic is the per-sample flip rate (0.04–0.16), consistent with sensitivity to supplied biological evidence rather than reliance on pretrained priors alone. Cross-species evaluation yielded F1 = 0.81–0.88 with Matthews correlation coefficient (MCC) = 0.35–0.54 on Mycobacterium tuberculosis, Pseudomonas aeruginosa, and Staphylococcus aureus. Temporal ranking of 81 post-2022 gene–drug associations achieved Precision@20 = 100% and AUC-PR = 0.855. AResKGLM offers an interpretable, reproducible framework for multi-hop AMR reasoning, linking candidate prioritization with mechanism-oriented hypothesis generation.Keywords: antimicrobial resistance, microbiome bioinformatics, knowledge graph, large language model, AMR surveillance, graph-grounded reasoning

Introduction

Antimicrobial resistance (AMR) is a major challenge in global health, microbiology, and microbiome research [1, 2]. Resistance genes and mobile genetic elements disseminate through clinical, animal, food-chain, and environmental microbial communities, making AMR a resistome-level problem rather than a property of isolated genes. Clinically relevant resistance often reflects interactions among resistance genes, mobile elements, genomic context, and functional environments. These interactions span gene regulation, protein function, metabolic pathways, and drug–target relationships, so resistance phenotypes are difficult to explain from local or pairwise observations alone.

For microbiome and meta-omics studies, this creates a practical bioinformatics problem. Resistome surveys and pathogen surveillance can identify candidate genes or variants [3, 4], but deciding which gene–drug associations are mechanistically plausible requires interpretable reasoning over distributed biological evidence. Models that rely mainly on local context can miss long-range dependencies that connect genotype to phenotype through regulatory, functional, and pathway-level intermediates.

Biological knowledge graphs (KGs) are well suited to this setting because they organize heterogeneous biomedical entities and relations into an explicit relational structure [5, 6]. In AMR, KGs can connect genomic annotations, protein functions, regulatory interactions, drug–target associations, resistome resources, and phenotypic observations [7]. Their topology can expose indirect regulatory cascades and mechanistic bridges that are sparse or weakly represented in local neighborhoods, but recovering such links requires explicit multi-hop reasoning over graph structure.

Current KG reasoning approaches still have important limitations for AMR modeling. Early embedding-based methods are largely transductive and struggle to generalize to unseen entities or novel combinations of biological factors [8]. More recent inductive approaches, including GraIL [9], NBFNet [10], ULTRA [11], and subgraph-aware or ontology-enhanced methods [12–14], reason over enclosing subgraphs or learned relational representations [15], but typically compress rich relational evidence into scalar scores. Although useful for ranking candidate links, these representations do not expose the mechanistic chains behind a prediction. This limits their use in AMR studies, where experimental validation and mechanistic interpretation are important for prioritizing candidate resistance determinants.

Large language models (LLMs) can complement KG methods by expressing intermediate reasoning steps in natural language [16, 17]. Recent studies have integrated LLMs with KG structure to improve grounding and reasoning fidelity [18–20]. However, many approaches use simplified sequence formulations, such as linearized triples, that do not preserve the topology of long-range reasoning paths and can weaken the link between generated explanations and graph evidence [21, 22]. LLMs are also prone to fluent but weakly supported explanations when not constrained by structured evidence [23]. A key open problem is therefore how to combine KG topology with LLM-generated reasoning while keeping predictions grounded in the supplied biological evidence.

We address this problem with AResKGLM (Antimicrobial Resistance Knowledge Graph Language Model), a graph-grounded language-model framework for interpretable multi-hop reasoning over AMR biological KGs. AResKGLM is intended as a reusable microbiome bioinformatics resource for prioritizing microbial gene–drug associations and generating inspectable hypotheses for AMR surveillance and follow-up validation. The framework makes three methodological contributions. First, it uses a topology-preserving serialization that converts BFS-retrieved multi-hop paths and per-entity biomedical semantics into a structured Context–Path–Question prompt, preserving relational topology as part of the model input. Second, it introduces a counterfactual path-faithfulness evaluation protocol combining path perturbation, symbolic entity anonymization, and a frozen-backbone 2× 2 factorial to separate path-evidence use from prompt-surface shortcuts and pretrained biomedical priors. Third, it provides a reproducible evaluation across entity-holdout benchmarks on two datasets, four cross-species extensions to WHO priority pathogens, drug-family stratification across antibiotic classes, and temporal ranking of post-2022 gene–drug associations. The complete framework—KG data, evaluation splits, training and inference scripts, and analysis code—is available for peer review and will be released as an open bioinformatics resource upon publication.

These contributions distinguish AResKGLM from the closest prior frameworks. KG-LLM-style approaches serialize each retrieved path into a single linear triple chain of opaque node/relation identifiers (e.g. “Node_47405 has relation_179 with Node_46497”) and instruction-tune the LLM on it [18–20], committing to one DFS walk per query and providing no per-entity semantic grounding. AResKGLM instead keeps a topology-preserving multi-path prompt in which each BFS path is retained as a directed, ordered chain, and multiple paths around the query are exposed jointly, with every entity in the Context block enriched by curated descriptions from UniProt, NCBI Gene, RefSeq, and Wikipedia [24, 25] (Methods, Prompt construction; Supplementary Table S1); KG-BERT-style triple classifiers [21] consume the same textual content without path topology, and pure-structural GNNs such as NBFNet [10] and ULTRA [11] consume the topology without biomedical semantics, and the symbolic-entity ablation isolates the contribution of each. On the evaluation side, KG-LLM balances classes by random undersampling under a random 80/20 node split on generic KG benchmarks (WN18RR, FB15k-237, YAGO3-10, and NELL-995); AResKGLM uses prefix-based cluster GroupKFold entity-holdout with structurally hard negatives, adds a matched RAG-only fine-tuned control and a fine-tuned × frozen-backbone factorial that separately isolate the graph-path signal from retrieved entity text and from pretrained biomedical priors, and reports paired Wilcoxon signed-rank tests with bootstrap 95% CIs across matched fold–hop slices. Faithfulness in prior KG-LLM work is illustrated by a chain-of-thought gloss shown as an ICL demonstration and never tested at inference time; AResKGLM’s reasoning trace is instead a generated per-query output subjected to a counterfactual-path battery and an automatic consistency check that traces rationale entities and relations back to input KG edges. The evaluation is also domain-grounded on antibiotic resistance mechanisms across five organisms (Escherichia coli plus four WHO priority pathogens), stratified by drug family, and augmented with a post-2022 temporal stress test, making the cross-distribution generalization explicit.

Results

Framework overview

AResKGLM converts multi-hop evidence in a biological KG into an explicit reasoning input for an LLM (Fig. 1). The workflow has three stages: (i) constrained BFS extraction of 2–6-hop paths connecting query entities; (ii) serialization into a structured prompt containing entity descriptions from UniProt and NCBI [24, 25] (Context block), the explicit graph path (Reasoning Path block), and the target query (Question block); and (iii) parameter-efficient fine-tuning (QLoRA) of foundation LLMs to generate a concise reasoning trace followed by a binary decision grounded in the supplied evidence.

(b) Prefix-based cluster splitting for group-based entity-holdout evaluation followed by BFS extraction of 2–6-hop paths. (c) Three-block prompt serialization of subgraph topology and entity semantics. (d) Final prediction with an explicit textual rationale.

AResKGLM outperforms graph baselines under entity-holdout evaluation

We benchmarked AResKGLM under a group-based entity-holdout split in which approximately one-fifth of triple-active entities were withheld per fold. Both AResKGLM backbones exceeded all baselines on the primary KIDs benchmark (Fig. 2a–c and Table 1). AResKGLM (Llama-3-8B) achieved F1 = 0.8482, outperforming KG-BERT (F1 = 0.7213 ± 0.043), NBFNet (F1 = 0.5260 ± 0.054), and ULTRA (F1 = 0.2541 ± 0.068). Exact paired Wilcoxon signed-rank tests across 25 matched fold–hop slices confirmed statistical significance (mean ΔF1 = 0.1257, ). On the complementary Ares benchmark [26], the qualitative model ranking remained unchanged (Supplementary Fig. S1 and Table S2), indicating that the advantage is not an artifact of a particular hop distribution.

Robust generalization across reasoning chains of 2–6 hops

AMR mechanisms often involve cascades spanning multiple molecular entities [1, 2]. To evaluate long-range reasoning, we stratified the KIDs test set by path length (2–6 hops). Because the benchmark is approximately balanced across hop lengths, these curves mainly reflect reasoning difficulty rather than shifts in sample composition. In the hop-wise analysis (Fig. 2b and c), both AResKGLM variants outperformed the graph baselines across most hop lengths. AResKGLM (Llama-3-8B) maintained high five-fold mean F1 across all hop regimes, decreasing from 0.9197 at 2 hops to 0.8148 at 6 hops. KG-BERT dropped from 0.8110 to 0.6716 over the same range. DeepSeek-R1-7B remained competitive at shorter chains, peaking at 3 hops (F1 = 0.9337), but declined more sharply by 6 hops (F1 = 0.6337). Pure-structural methods were most affected by longer paths; e.g. ULTRA F1 was 0.056, 0.012, and 0.013 on 4-, 5-, and 6-hop cases. These results indicate that AResKGLM does not eliminate hop-dependent difficulty, but it improves robustness on longer reasoning chains, with the Llama-3-8B variant providing the most stable performance.

Topology and biomedical semantics jointly support performance

We used ablation studies to separate the contributions of graph structure and biological semantics (Supplementary Table S3). Under Instruction Masking, where the loss was computed only on generated completion tokens, Llama-3-8B F1 decreased from 0.8470 to 0.6675, indicating that the model benefits from training over the structured prompt context. In the symbolic-entity ablation, biomedical entity, and relation names were replaced with anonymous placeholders while the multi-hop graph topology was preserved. This reduced Llama-3-8B F1 from 0.8470 to 0.7312, with a stronger effect at longer path lengths: at 2–3 hops the reduction was modest, whereas at 4–6 hops the reduction was larger (e.g. from 0.8260 to 0.6469 at 5 hops). Paired Wilcoxon signed-rank tests confirmed that the natural-entity setting outperformed both the instruction-masked (ΔF1 = 0.1794, ) and symbolic (ΔF1 = 0.1158, ) settings (Supplementary Table S4). These results suggest that AResKGLM uses both explicit topology and biomedical semantics, especially on longer reasoning paths.

Explicit reasoning paths improve precision and calibration over text-only retrieval

To test whether multi-hop graph paths add information beyond text retrieval, we constructed a RAG-only control using the same Llama-3-8B backbone, LoRA hyperparameters, data splits, and answer format, but replacing the explicit Reasoning Path block with BM25-retrieved entity descriptions (Methods). The head and tail entities were always included in the retrieved context, so the baseline was not penalized for missing the queried entities. RAG-only (fine-tuned) achieved F1 = 0.8227 (±0.020), close to AResKGLM in aggregate F1 (Δ = +0.001, p = 0.14). The models nevertheless showed different precision–recall profiles: AResKGLM was significantly more precise (Precision Δ = +0.101, ) and accurate (Accuracy Δ = +0.022, ), whereas RAG-only had higher recall (Δ = −0.133, ). MCC, which penalizes class-imbalanced shortcuts, also favored AResKGLM directionally (Δ = +0.016, p = 0.075). RAG-only (frozen) performed worse (F1 = 0.5712, MCC = 0.4213; all ; Supplementary Fig. S2c and d), indicating that BM25-retrieved text alone, without graph paths or fine-tuning, is not sufficient for this multi-hop AMR task. Thus, entity descriptions can recover part of the signal after fine-tuning, but explicit graph paths improve prediction precision and calibration (Supplementary Fig. S2c and d).

Counterfactual probes suggest sensitivity to supplied path evidence

To test sensitivity to the supplied path evidence, we constructed a six-condition logical-probe battery over the full five-fold KIDs benchmark (32 910 probe instances). The aggregate results (Fig. 2e) showed three consistent patterns. First, template rewriting preserved performance (F1 from 0.654 ± 0.055 to 0.706 ± 0.022), suggesting robustness to prompt surface form. Second, counterfactual path corruption, where the multi-hop chain was replaced with a corrupted path connecting different entities, reduced F1 to 0.000 across all hops (this value is mechanically forced by the probe label assignment; see the per-sample flip-rate analysis below and Supplementary Table S5 for the operative diagnostic). Third, removing biological context or reducing the input to symbolic paths lowered F1 to 0.524 ± 0.018 and 0.498 ± 0.024, respectively. Hop-stratified curves (Fig. 2d) further showed that the no-context and symbolic-path-only settings were most affected on 4–6-hop cases, consistent with a growing role for biomedical semantics as path length increases. A post-hoc constrained-instruction decoding analysis indicated that high-hop false positives in the natural-control condition could be substantially reduced (Supplementary Table S6).

The original “F1 = 0.000” value is mechanical: the probe sets probe_expected_label to 0 for every sample (positives flipped yes→no by relation corruption, negatives kept at no), so any yes prediction becomes a false positive and F1 collapses regardless of behavior. The substantively informative diagnostic is the per-sample flip rate. Across all 5485 corrupted prompts in the five-folds (2051 originally positive), the legacy relation-shuffling probe flips only 4.8% of positive predictions from yes to no (Supplementary Table S5). To dissect which aspects of the path the model actually uses, we evaluated four additional controlled counterfactual variants ( prompts) from the same KIDs evaluation triples with the same fine-tuned checkpoints (Supplementary Table S5 and Methods). The model is most sensitive to edge-direction reversal (flip rate 0.164, rising to 0.331 at 6 hops) and to label-preserving relation paraphrasing (0.110), substantially less sensitive to partial-path masking (0.067), and only marginally sensitive to degree-matched intermediate-entity substitution (0.040). Specificity-style “no→yes” spurious flip rates on the held-out negatives ranged from 0.29 (direction reversal) to 0.41 (degree-matched substitution). The full per-hop breakdown of these four flip-rate profiles is reported in Supplementary Table S7, which shows the direction-reversal flip rate rising monotonically from 0.122 at 2 hops to 0.331 at 6 hops. Together these flip-rate profiles indicate that AResKGLM’s predictions are sensitive to path direction and relation surface form but are largely indifferent to the identity of intermediate non-endpoint nodes; we, therefore, softened the original phrasing from “confirm dependence” to “suggest sensitivity to supplied path evidence”.

A frozen-backbone control, using the unmodified Llama-3-8B-Instruct checkpoint without any LoRA adapter and score-based decoding, reached only F1 = 0.3596 on natural inputs, 48.7 F1 points below the fine-tuned counterpart (paired Wilcoxon ; Supplementary Fig. S2a and b). Frozen inference on symbolic prompts yielded higher F1 (0.4609) than on natural prompts, suggesting that familiarity with biomedical surface forms can introduce a yes-bias rather than relational reasoning. The natural–symbolic gap observed after fine-tuning (ΔF1 = +0.1158), therefore, reflects learned integration of topology and biomedical semantics rather than simple recall from pretraining. Taken together, these experiments indicate that AResKGLM’s performance depends on fine-tuning over graph-derived path evidence, not on pretrained biomedical priors alone.

Cross-species generalization across WHO priority pathogens

To assess extensibility beyond E. coli, we constructed independent AMR KGs for four additional WHO priority pathogens: Klebsiella pneumoniae (14 290 entities; 20 627 triples), Mycobacterium tuberculosis (15 182 entities; 18 371 triples), Pseudomonas aeruginosa (14 926 entities; 18 698 triples) and Staphylococcus aureus (17 452 entities; 18 610 triples), each with 28 relation types (Supplementary Table S8). Gene–drug–mechanism associations were assembled from CARD [7], UniProt, NCBI Gene, and published literature. Using the same five-fold entity-holdout pipeline and Llama-3-8B backbone, AResKGLM achieved five-fold pooled F1 scores of 0.8811 (Mycobacterium), 0.8141 (Staphylococcus), 0.8097 (Pseudomonas), and 0.7167 (Klebsiella), with MCC values of 0.4170, 0.3512, 0.5381, and 0.0392, respectively (Supplementary Fig. S3). These results cover species with distinct resistance mechanisms, from carbapenemase production in K. pneumoniae to intrinsic efflux-mediated resistance in P. aeruginosa, and suggest that the topology-preserving serialization strategy generalizes beyond a single organism. F1 alone is an unreliable summary metric on cross-species transfer because class prevalence differs from KIDs (0.55–0.74 versus 0.37) and specificity varies widely across species; MCC and Specificity are the operative metrics. In particular, the K. pneumoniae F1 = 0.72 is a positivity artifact—PPR = 0.999, TN = 1, Specificity = 0.003, and MCC = 0.039 (Supplementary Table S9)—so F1 reflects the base rate of positives rather than relational discrimination and should not be read as “useful F1 across four pathogens” in isolation. The lower MCC for Klebsiella is consistent with the greater sparsity of its KG (density 1.44 triples per entity versus 3.19 for E. coli), suggesting that cross-species calibration may improve as organism-specific KGs become denser and more completely curated.

For a more granular cross-species analysis, we report precision, recall, specificity, F1, and Matthews correlation coefficient with 95% percentile bootstrap confidence intervals for all 12 865 cached predictions across the five-folds (1000 resamples; Supplementary Tables S9 and S10). On KIDs, AResKGLM (Llama-3-8B) reached F1 = 0.8935 [0.8836, 0.9031] with specificity = 0.9420 [0.9343, 0.9499] and MCC = 0.8308 [0.8163, 0.8457] across all hops; the DeepSeek-R1-7B backbone reached F1 = 0.8622 [0.8508, 0.8734] and MCC = 0.7800 [0.7625, 0.7967]. Out-of-distribution F1 (95% CI) was 0.8811 [0.8574, 0.9025] for Mycobacterium, 0.8141 [0.7704, 0.8514] for Staphylococcus, 0.8097 [0.7598, 0.8543] for Pseudomonas, and 0.7167 [0.6890, 0.7433] for Klebsiella. The Klebsiella drop is driven by near-zero specificity (0.0028 [0.0000, 0.0086]) under the imbalanced cross-species evaluation, again consistent with sparser KG coverage. Per-organism confusion matrices and pooled counts are provided in Supplementary Table S9, and per-hop precision/recall/specificity tables in Supplementary Table S10. We did not compute calibrated AUC-PR or PR curves at this stage because the cached AResKGLM inference pipeline persisted only the binary yes/no decision and not yes/no token logits; score-capture analyses are reported separately and the score-based panels of Fig. 3f–h, therefore, remain limited to the temporal stress-test subset.

The full KIDs natural-control benchmark was evaluated with two complementary teacher-forced score-capture passes. The first pass records, for every prompt, the next-token log-probability of “ yes” and “ no” at the position immediately after the suffix The answer is, giving a single-token score_margin = – . The second pass computes the mean per-token log-probability over a 14-token answer completion of the form The answer is yes/no. The relation holds/does not hold based on the provided path., integrating the model’s confidence across multiple positions where the yes/no completions differ semantically. Together they form a two-channel calibration / shortcut diagnostic against the binary chain-of-thought decision (Supplementary Table S12 and Figs S4 and S5). Three findings: (i) the single-token margin yields a pooled AUC-PR of 0.428 [0.409, 0.449] against a class prevalence of 0.374, lifting ranking accuracy by only +0.054; thresholded at zero, it agrees with the chain-of-thought-derived y_pred on only 51.8% of rows. (ii) The mean-completion margin instead yields a pooled AUC-PR of 0.702 [0.681, 0.722], a substantively meaningful lift of +0.328 over prevalence; the lift is largest on the deepest reasoning chains (5 hop +0.31, 6 hop +0.35), and the negative- and positive-class score distributions separate by approximately one pooled standard deviation. Spearman rank correlation between the mean-completion margin and the binary label is ρ = 0.491 (95% CI [0.470, 0.512], ), versus ρ = 0.097 ([0.068, 0.123]) for the single-token margin—a five-fold separation that mirrors the AUC-PR comparison and confirms that the relationship between margin and label is largely monotonic rather than non-linear (Pearson r = 0.481). (iii) Together the two channels indicate that AResKGLM’s confidence about “yes ↔ supports” / “no ↔ does not support” does not appear at the immediate answer-position logit, where prompt-level shortcuts would be readable, but does emerge over the longer answer template, with the discriminative signal growing with reasoning depth. Combined with the counterfactual flip-rate sensitivity (Supplementary Table S5), the F1 cost of description sanitization and gene-family-prefix holdout (Supplementary Tables S13 and S14), and the trace-faithfulness check (Supplementary Table S15), this gives four independent and mutually consistent lines of evidence for a chain-of-thought-routed, path-grounded decision rather than an answer-position shortcut. Designing a single-shot ranking head that better integrates the chain-of-thought signal at deployment time remains future work.

We also added two stricter inductive-generalization probes that re-use the same five-fold KIDs benchmark without retraining (Supplementary Tables S13 and S14). (i) A gene-family holdout restricts evaluation to triples whose head or tail share a four-character prefix that does not occur anywhere in the corresponding fold’s training partition. The four-character prefix is used as a tractable proxy for ARO/CARD-style gene families because the KIDs vocabulary is not curated against either ARO families or COG functional categories; the prefix matches the existing GroupKFold convention used elsewhere in the pipeline and corresponds in practice to gene-symbol stems, such as bla, tet, aac, aph, erm, etc. Across 1002 such held-out triples, F1 dropped from 0.8465 (natural baseline, all hops pooled) to 0.5480 (), with MCC retreating from 0.7517 to 0.4166 and specificity falling from 0.9019 to 0.6037. The drug-family analog yielded only two qualifying triples on KIDs because of antibiotic vocabulary sparsity and is reported as a placeholder; the cross-species evaluation above provides the operative out-of-distribution drug-family signal. (ii) A description-sanitization probe redacts antibiotic-family keywords (e.g. aminoglycoside, cephalosporin), drug names, and the literals antibiotic/resistance/antimicrobial from the Context Information section of each prompt while leaving the Reasoning Path and Question untouched ( rows across all five-folds, 39%–46% of which contain at least one redacted token). Across all five-folds, F1 fell from 0.8465 to a fold-pooled mean of 0.6559 (, range -0.14 to -0.26) and MCC fell from 0.7517 to 0.4754. Recall stayed near-saturated (0.92–0.98) while precision and specificity dropped sharply, indicating that AResKGLM’s positive predictions are partly supported by surface-level AMR keywords in the entity descriptions, in addition to the relational-path evidence. The order of magnitude of the drop is comparable across the gene-family-prefix and description-keyword probes, suggesting two roughly co-equal sources of inductive shortcut that future work should disentangle.

Drug-family stratification across antibiotic classes

As an additional generalization analysis, we regrouped KIDs predictions by antibiotic family and retained the four families with reproducible multi-fold coverage: Aminoglycosides (n = 197), Cephalosporins (n = 77), Penicillins (n = 42), and Rifamycins (n = 25). Llama-3-8B showed its clearest advantage on Aminoglycosides (F1 = 0.9480 versus 0.8862 for DeepSeek-R1-7B), with a smaller edge on Penicillins, whereas DeepSeek-R1-7B was marginally stronger on Cephalosporins (Fig. 3a and b and Supplementary Table S16). Both backbones reached near-ceiling performance on Rifamycins. This analysis suggests that the backbone gap is not confined to a single antibiotic regime and that AResKGLM maintains robust performance across diverse drug classes (Fig. 3a–e and Supplementary Table S16).

To characterize specific outlier strata—in particular Aminoglycosides 2 hop () and Cephalosporins 5 hop () in the previous family × hop breakdown (Supplementary Table S11)—we built a per-stratum failure-mode dossier covering every (dataset, model, drug-family, and hop) cell with N ≤ 15 or (53 strata; Supplementary Table S17). The two reviewer-cited strata both reflect single-sample edge cases: the KIDs Aminoglycosides 2-hop stratum contains exactly one held-out triple per backbone (a negative crp–“activates”–Kanamycin link in fold 1 that both models classify as positive after seeing the regulatory cascade crp–[activates]→pspB–[upregulated by antibiotic]→Kanamycin), and the KIDs Cephalosporins 5-hop slot has zero qualifying samples for either backbone. Across all 53 underpowered strata, the median sample count is 2 (versus 70 in passing strata, , and N ≥ 20), indicating that low-N variance, rather than family-specific failure, dominates the F1 = 0 cases. As a complementary diagnostic we estimated, for every stratum, the fraction of reasoning-path entities sitting in the top decile of node degree in the corresponding training graph (Supplementary Table S18). Failing strata showed a higher mean hub fraction (median 0.90, mean 0.86) than passing strata (median 0.77, mean 0.77), indicating that the residual errors that survive the small-N regime are disproportionately associated with hub-mediated paths through high-degree resistance loci (e.g. blaKPC-2, blaTEM-1, and blaCTX-M-15 in Klebsiella Cephalosporins 5-hop). Both observations support our reading of these outliers as statistically underpowered or hub-confounded, rather than as evidence of systematic class-level failure.

To probe how AResKGLM behaves under harder negatives than the original tail-corruption regime, we evaluated four families of controlled negatives on a fold-0 pilot (–165 per hop; Supplementary Table S19) with the same fine-tuned checkpoint. Across all four regimes AResKGLM’s recall stayed near-saturated at 0.97–0.98, indicating that the model continues to identify true gene–drug associations even when negatives are constructed to maximize surface similarity to the positives. AResKGLM’s specificity did degrade with regime strictness (0.902 on natural negatives, 0.431 on degree-matched, 0.159 on gene-family-matched, and 0.094 on relation-type-matched; F1 = 0.847, 0.770, 0.804, 0.690, respectively). To determine whether this specificity ladder is AResKGLM-specific or a shared property of matched-negative evaluation, we evaluated the two closest baselines (KG-BERT, five-folds; RAG-only fine-tuned, fold-0) on the same negative pools (Supplementary Table S20). All three models degrade under matched negatives, but along distinct failure modes: KG-BERT’s yes-rate is roughly flat (recall ≈ 0.79, specificity 0.34–0.37 across regimes), consistent with a triple classifier that is largely insensitive to the negative distribution; RAG-only occupies the opposite extreme, defaulting to “no” whenever the retrieved text does not directly support the query, so its specificity is high (0.72–0.94) but recall drops to 0.48–0.58; AResKGLM alone maintains high recall across regimes and produces the best F1 in every regime (0.770, 0.804, 0.690 versus KG-BERT 0.645, 0.745, 0.661 and RAG-only 0.711, 0.587, 0.605). We interpret the AResKGLM specificity drop as a discrimination–reliance trade-off: high natural specificity (0.902) buys sharper decisions on the natural distribution but exposes more of the model’s implicit relation-type prior to matched-negative probing. In the spirit of type-constrained negative sampling from the KG-embedding literature, we adopt relation-type-matched negatives as a supplementary control axis and recommend it as a routine control for future AMR/biomedical KG-prompted LLM work. A descriptive comparison of positive and natural-baseline negative distributions on six surface descriptors (node degree, BFS path length, BFS path count, description length, drug/gene-family bucket, and AMR-keyword frequency) shows that four of the six descriptors overlap closely (|d| ≤ 0.23), while node degree (|d| = 0.67) and BFS path length (|d| = 0.84) are not matched by the tail-corruption construction (Supplementary Table S21); we therefore additionally control these axes with the four matched-negative regimes below, so that the residual specificity drop cannot be attributed to these surface features alone; the specificity ladder across all four negative regimes is visualized in Supplementary Fig. S6. Drug-family-matched negatives yielded only one valid sample on KIDs because of vocabulary sparsity (seven family-tagged drug entities) and are therefore reported only as a placeholder; cross-species evaluation (Supplementary Table S9) provides the complementary out-of-distribution drug-family signal.

Temporal ranking of post-2022 gene–drug associations

To examine whether AResKGLM can prioritize associations beyond the KG snapshot, we constructed an independent temporal evaluation set from PubMed literature published between January 2023 and April 2026. From 711 abstracts, we identified 81 “new-pair” gene–drug resistance associations involving entities already present in the KG but whose specific pairing was absent from training. These associations span 16 genes and 11 drugs. We further verified that none of the 81 positive (h, r, t) triples, in either direction, appeared in the KG training set of 25 668 triples. On a balanced set of 81 positives and 81 cross-functional negatives, confidence-margin scoring yielded Precision@20 = 100%, Precision@81 = 75.3% and AUC-PR = 0.855 (Fig. 3f–h). Bootstrap resampling (10 000 replicates) supported the stability of these estimates: AUC-PR 95% CI [0.785, 0.916], P@20 95% CI [0.850, 1.000]; only 0.5% of bootstrap replicates yielded P@20 below 0.80. Because entity descriptions were compiled from public resources rather than a prospectively frozen corpus, we treat this analysis as a ranking-based temporal stress test rather than a strict leakage-free benchmark.

Human-readable path-based rationales

Each AResKGLM prediction includes a graph-grounded reasoning trace that can be inspected biologically. In the benchmark evaluation, the model outputs a short reasoning trace followed by a binary answer. To illustrate its use for more detailed mechanism-oriented analysis, we present representative case studies (Fig. 4). In Fig. 4a, the model inferred that dsbA is associated with a cellular response to antibiotic through a 4-hop chain involving the envelope-stress regulator CpxR and the multidrug-response regulators MarA and MarR. This chain is biologically plausible, connecting envelope homeostasis to regulatory-cascade activation and an antibiotic-response phenotype [27–29]. In Fig. 4b, the model reorganizes fragmented KG evidence into a mechanism-oriented hypothesis linking cefsulodin-induced cell-wall stress [30], DacA-mediated peptidoglycan homeostasis, and TatC-dependent compensatory protein transport [31, 32]. These examples show how AResKGLM can recover known multi-step pathways and generate inspectable hypotheses while retaining more mechanistic detail than score-only KG models.

Interpretable pathway recovery and hypothesis generation (case studies). (a) Recovery of a validated 4-hop regulatory cascade connecting dsbA to a cellular response to antibiotic; all nodes and edges in panel (a) are KG-resident facts. (b) A mechanism-oriented hypothesis under cefsulodin exposure linking DacA-mediated peptidoglycan homeostasis and TatC-dependent compensatory protein transport. Nodes and edges are categorized by evidence source using explicit textual tags and line styles: [KG fact] denotes entities and relations present in the KIDs/CARD KG; [Model rationale] denotes mechanism-level concepts introduced by the AResKGLM reasoning trace (not a KG edge); [Lit] denotes external literature support with the cited PMID ([30], PMID 391106); and dashed elements labeled [Hyp] denote testable conjectures not present in the KG and not directly cited, presented as hypotheses rather than validated mechanisms. Panel (b) therefore mixes KG facts with model-generated rationales and hypotheses; the overall cefsulodin-to-Tat rescue chain culminating in a stress-resilient phenotype is a hypothesis and was not experimentally validated in this work.

To quantify the faithfulness of the cached short-format reasoning traces, we ran an automatic consistency check on all 5485 KIDs natural-control predictions across the five-folds (Supplementary Table S15). Trace text was tokenized against the KIDs entity and relation vocabularies (longest-match alternation, common stop tokens removed) and intersected with the corresponding sets extracted from each prompt’s Context Information block, Reasoning Path block, and parsed path edges. Because inference was capped at max_new_tokens = 64, 44.8% of traces are terse “The answer is yes/no.” responses with no recognized entity (we mark these vacuous and exclude them from the means below). Across the remaining 3028 traces we observe entity-in-context = 0.66, entity-in-path = 0.63, relation-in-path = 0.70, and an entity-hallucination rate of 0.34, with the hallucination rate falling monotonically from 0.45 at 2 hops to 0.30 at 6 hops as longer paths give the model more anchors to cite. We therefore present the case-study traces as human-readable path-based rationales rather than as wet-lab-validated mechanisms; an LLM-assisted dual-protocol agreement study on a 30-trace stratified sample (rater A: Claude Opus 4.7; rater B: GPT-5.5) yielded a linear-weighted Cohen’s κ = 0.917 (cell-level agreement = 93.3%, n = 30) on biological plausibility scoring under a matched 0/1/2 ordinal rubric (the full rubric is reproduced in Supplementary Table S22). We additionally report a stricter automatic dimension, relation-direction consistency (C5), from a max_new_tokens = 256 re-inference (Supplementary Table S23): 289 of the 5485 traces (5.3%) cite at least one directed relation, and among the 1003 directed pairs cited 478 (47.7%) match a directed edge in the prompt’s path block (per-trace C5 mean = 0.356, increasing monotonically from 0.29 at 2 hops to 0.62 at 6 hops). We additionally cannot rule out post-hoc rationalization for any individual trace, and have softened the corresponding wording throughout the manuscript.

Discussion

Our results demonstrate that graph-grounded language models can improve AMR link prediction while also exposing the evidence used for multi-hop inference. On the primary KIDs benchmark, AResKGLM achieved the strongest mean performance among the evaluated methods, including KG-BERT, NBFNet, and ULTRA, and retained inspectable textual reasoning traces. The gap between AResKGLM and pure-structural GNN baselines (F1 0.8482 versus 0.5260 and 0.2541), together with the stronger performance on longer chains, suggests that explicit graph paths and biomedical semantics are especially useful when AMR inference depends on extended mechanistic dependencies rather than local graph patterns alone. The baselines also differ in information budget and parameter count (Methods section Information parity across baselines and Supplementary Table S24). The observed F1 gaps therefore reflect modeling-pipeline differences as well as algorithmic differences, and should be read as a comparison between practical pipelines rather than as a clean test of LLM scale alone.

A consistent finding across the control experiments is that both topology and semantics are required for strong performance. Symbolic and instruction-masking ablations reduced performance, especially on 4–6-hop cases. Pure-structural baselines performed worse, and the gap widened at longer hops. Counterfactual path corruption produced an apparent F1 of zero, but this number is mechanically forced by the probe’s label assignment. The more informative diagnostic is the per-sample flip rate, which ranges from 0.04 (degree-matched intermediate-entity substitution) to 0.16 (edge-direction reversal). This profile is consistent with the model being most sensitive to relation surface form and direction. Removing context caused a smaller but still measurable drop. The frozen backbone reached only F1 = 0.3596 on natural inputs and performed slightly better on symbolic inputs (F1 = 0.4609), consistent with a pretrained yes-bias rather than relational reasoning. The RAG-only baseline matched aggregate F1 after fine-tuning but was less precise and less calibrated. Overall, these results indicate that AResKGLM’s performance comes from learning to combine graph-derived path evidence with biomedical semantics, rather than from pretrained priors or text retrieval alone.

The cross-species results suggest that the framework is not limited to the primary E. coli setting. AResKGLM maintained useful F1 scores on M. tuberculosis, S. aureus, and P. aeruginosa (F1 = 0.81–0.88, MCC = 0.35–0.54; the K. pneumoniae row of Supplementary Table S9 should be read jointly with its Specificity and MCC, as detailed in Results). A held-out threshold-recalibration diagnostic (leave-one-fold-out on the mean-completion score-margin channel; Supplementary Table S25) recovers substantial MCC on Mycobacterium (0.10 → 0.23), Pseudomonas (0.15 → 0.37), and Staphylococcus (0.08 → 0.13), with all three 95% CIs excluding zero, indicating that the cross-species positive-prediction bias reflects an out-of-distribution threshold shift rather than an absence of relational signal on those three species. A separate cross-species score-capture analysis (12 865 pooled rows; Supplementary Table S26 and Fig. S7) confirms that the mean-completion margin lifts AUC-PR above class prevalence on every species: Klebsiella 0.629 [0.585, 0.677] (lift +0.071), Mycobacterium 0.883 [0.848, 0.913] (+0.141), Pseudomonas 0.695 [0.612, 0.803] (+0.149), and Staphylococcus 0.772 [0.699, 0.836] (+0.097). Mean-completion lift exceeds single-token lift on all four species. The absolute lifts are smaller than on KIDs (+0.328, Supplementary Table S12) because the cross-species class prevalences are correspondingly higher (0.55–0.74 versus 0.374 on KIDs), compressing the headroom; the consistent direction of the mean-completion advantage—and not its magnitude—is the operative cross-distribution signal. The temporal ranking experiment further supports its use for hypothesis prioritization, with Precision@20 = 100% on post-2022 gene–drug associations.

The reasoning traces also make the predictions easier to inspect than score-only KG outputs. In the case studies, the model recovered regulatory cascades and organized fragmented KG evidence into mechanism-oriented hypotheses. For computational studies where immediate wet-lab validation is not available, such traces can help researchers judge whether a predicted association is biologically plausible and worth prioritizing for follow-up [33].

This use case is relevant to microbiome and meta-omics research. In resistome profiling and metagenomic AMR surveillance [3, 34], candidate resistance genes are often recovered from complex microbial communities, but their links to specific drugs or phenotypes can remain unclear. AResKGLM provides a complementary prioritization layer: curated genes, proteins, drugs, and biological processes can be organized as a microbial AMR KG, and graph-grounded reasoning traces can show which multi-hop paths support a predicted gene–drug association. The approach is compatible with resources such as CARD [7] and with One Health settings that integrate AMR evidence across clinical, animal, and environmental microbiomes. It is not intended to replace experimental validation, but to help rank interpretable hypotheses for follow-up testing and community curation.

Several limitations remain. AResKGLM depends on the completeness of the underlying KG and the quality of retrieved paths. This study uses curated isolate- and pathogen-level AMR KGs rather than raw metagenomic cohorts. Applying the framework directly to metagenomic assemblies, MAGs or longitudinal microbiome cohorts will require additional entity normalization and evidence curation. Entity descriptions were compiled from public resources rather than a strictly date-frozen corpus. The temporal ranking experiment, although stable under bootstrap resampling (AUC-PR 95% CI [0.785, 0.916]), is moderate in size (81 positives and 81 negatives) and restricted to entities already present in the KG. Larger multi-cohort and multi-species temporal sets will be needed to assess prospective utility. The primary five-fold split uses prefix-based cluster GroupKFold entity-holdout; a strict ARO/CARD or GO ontology holdout is not feasible because the KIDs vocabulary is not curated against an external ontology and all GO-like branches appear in every fold’s training partition. As the closest implementable analog, we provide a GO-branch ontology-stratified evaluation across eight functional branches ( test rows; Supplementary Table S27), showing that model capacity is not uniformly distributed: performance is near or above the natural baseline on the most AMR-proximal branches (biosynthesis, regulation/response, and binding) and drops on catabolism and general metabolism. A strict ontology holdout requiring curated ARO/CARD-to-KIDs entity mapping is deferred as future work. Drug-family × hop strata with N ≤ 9 qualifying triples are statistically underpowered and the / values reported in Supplementary Table S17 for those cells should be read as single-sample variance rather than as a class-level failure of the model. A complementary per-row mean-completion score-margin diagnostic for these underpowered cells (Supplementary Table S28) shows that individual predictions are scattered across the passing-strata score distribution rather than collapsed to a single extreme value (Supplementary Fig. S8), corroborating the small-N-variance interpretation. Reasoning paths in the failing strata are dominated by high-degree resistance gene and strain hubs (Supplementary Table S18), so the per-row predictions are also susceptible to hub-mediated shortcuts that family-level F1 cannot detect. Calibrated AUC-PR, PR curves, and decision-threshold sensitivity were not computed for the original fine-tuned predictions because the inference pipeline cached only the binary yes/no decision. Two score-capture analyses (Supplementary Table S12 and Figs S4 and S5) close this gap on the natural-control benchmark: the mean-completion margin lifts pooled AUC-PR by +0.328 over class prevalence (most strongly on 5–6-hop chains), whereas the answer-position single-token margin lifts only +0.054. Together these results are consistent with a chain-of-thought-routed decision (see Discussion). Two further limitations are quantified by the new probes. First, AResKGLM’s high natural specificity comes with a discrimination–reliance trade-off under matched negatives: harder, relation-type-matched negatives lower specificity from 0.902 to 0.094 while recall stays at 0.97–0.98 (Supplementary Table S19). Evaluation of the baselines on the same negative pools (Supplementary Table S20) shows that KG-BERT is roughly flat (recall ≈ 0.79, specificity 0.34–0.37) and RAG-only defaults to “no” (specificity 0.72–0.94, recall 0.48–0.58); AResKGLM retains the highest F1 across all matched regimes but exposes the largest specificity swing. This diagnostic suggests that standard tail-corruption training can leave residual relation-domain priors when negatives are made semantically compatible with positives. Training-time matched hard negatives are, therefore, a natural future extension for future checkpoints, but are outside the scope of the present study; we now report specificity under matched negatives as a routine diagnostic axis. Second, AMR-keyword sanitization of entity descriptions and four-character gene-family-prefix holdout each cost ∼0.2 F1 (Supplementary Tables S13 and S14), indicating that part of the model’s signal comes from surface-level keyword and prefix-level lexical cues in the Context Information block in addition to the supplied path. Separately, using the same fine-tuned checkpoint without retraining, we profiled sensitivity to the path budget K and to path presentation order: F1 peaks at K = 3 (Δ = +0.015 relative to the production K = 1) and degrades at K = 10 (Δ = -0.031), while three random permutations of path order at K = 3 change F1 by <0.005 (Supplementary Tables S29 and S30); the production K = 1 therefore trades a small F1 gain for guaranteed output parseability. We treat all three signals as evidence that AResKGLM’s interpretability is real but bounded, not as grounds for retracting the path-grounded interpretation. No wet-lab validation was performed, so the framework should be viewed as a computational prioritization tool for generating testable hypotheses. Although PEFT reduces adaptation costs [35–37], efficient LLM deployment in resource-limited settings also remains a practical challenge [38]. Future work will focus on enriching cross-species KGs, integrating transcriptomic, proteomic, and metagenomic evidence, building strictly date-frozen corpora for temporal evaluation, and developing continual knowledge-update strategies.

Conclusion

AResKGLM presents a practical and interpretable framework for multi-hop AMR reasoning in microbial and resistome bioinformatics. By combining explicit KG-path topology, biomedical entity semantics and parameter-efficient LLM fine-tuning, the framework outperforms graph baselines, shows partial cross-species transfer with species-specific calibration limits, supports antibiotic-family analyses, and produces biologically inspectable reasoning traces. The complete framework will be released as an open bioinformatics resource to support mechanism-oriented AMR discovery, metagenomic AMR surveillance, and community curation of microbial-resistance mechanisms.

Materials and methods

Knowledge graph construction

The biological KG was built on the knowledge-integration graph from the KIDs study [39]. The KG integrates heterogeneous biomedical knowledge spanning microbial genes, proteins, antibiotics, resistance phenotypes, molecular mechanisms and biological processes. Core gene annotations were anchored to the E. coli K-12 substr. MG1655 reference genome (RefSeq GCF_000005845.2), supplemented with gene-symbol mappings from UniProt, RegulonDB, and DrugBank [24, 40, 41]. The processed KG comprises 8047 entities, 12 relation types and 25 668 positive triples. Entities were linked to textual descriptions from RefSeq, UniProtKB, NCBI Gene, and Wikipedia; 6542 entities (81.3%) have descriptions averaging 41.6 words (Supplementary Table S31).

Entity-holdout splitting and path extraction

We used GroupKFold over 7504 triple-active entities, assigning each to a prefix-based cluster (first four characters of normalized name). This yielded ∼6003 training entities and ∼1501 held-out entities per fold (20% holdout; Supplementary Table S32). For each query triple (h, r, t), constrained BFS extracted paths of 2–6 hops. Up to K ranked BFS paths (ordered by path length, then head-incidence count, then a deterministic ID order) may be retained per query; the production KIDs benchmark uses K = 1 to keep the prompt compact and fully parseable (sensitivity to K and to path presentation order is quantified in Supplementary Tables S29 and S30; see Discussion). Hard negatives were constructed by corrupting the tail entity such that and a BFS path from h to of length between 2 and 6 hops exists. At training time is sampled from the pool of training entities in that fold, whereas at dev/test time is sampled only from the fold’s held-out target entities, so every evaluation negative involves a tail unseen in the corresponding fold’s training triples.

Prompt construction

Extracted paths were serialized into a three-block prompt: (i) Context Information: entity names and retrieved descriptions; (ii) Reasoning Path: explicit multi-hop chain with relation labels; (iii) Question: whether the target relation holds (Supplementary Table S33). The supervised output was a short reasoning trace ending with “The answer is yes/no.” For symbolic robustness experiments, a deterministic mapping replaced entity/relation names with anonymous tokens while preserving topology.

Let denote the KG with entity set , relation set and triple set . For a query triple q = (h, r, t) and a set of constrained-BFS paths , where each has , and , the topology-preserving serialization produces a string with three blocks: (i) lists the union in deterministic order with each entity rendered as “name: description” truncated to 41 words on average (Supplementary Table S31); (ii) emits each verbatim as “ --[]--> --[]--> ⋯ --[]--> .” with one path per line, preserving relation direction and intermediate identities; (iii) states the binary question “Does h [r] t?”. Two design properties distinguish from a flattened triple list: relation labels are placed in their original positional order rather than concatenated as bag-of-edges, and intermediate entity identities are preserved so the LLM can attend to the same node across multiple paths. The symbolic ablation is the variant in which a deterministic injection replaces names while leaving the path topology and the entity-multiplicity structure intact. Pseudocode is provided in Supplementary Table S1.

Model fine-tuning

We fine-tuned Llama-3-8B-Instruct and DeepSeek-R1-7B [42, 43] using QLoRA (4-bit NF4) [35, 36]. Training used autoregressive cross-entropy over the full sequence (max 2048 tokens), bf16 precision, cosine decay, early stopping on validation loss, effective batch size 192 (6 GPUs × 8 per-device × 4 accumulation steps), and LoRA rank r = 16 / α = 32 for Llama-3 and rank r = 32 / α = 64 for DeepSeek (Supplementary Fig. S9). Full hyperparameters are in Supplementary Table S34.

Baselines

We compared against: (i) KG-BERT [21], triple classification via sequence classification; (ii) NBFNet [10], neural Bellman–Ford message passing; (iii) ULTRA [11], a pretrainable graph foundation model; (iv) GraIL [9], inductive subgraph-based GNN (Ares only); and (v) RAG-only, replacing paths with BM25-retrieved descriptions while keeping all other pipeline components identical (Llama-3-8B, same LoRA settings, same splits).

The RAG-only baseline was constructed for like-for-like comparison with AResKGLM. The retrieval corpus consisted of the same 6542 entity-description records used by AResKGLM (, 81.3% coverage of triple-active entities, mean 41.6 words per record; Supplementary Table S31), tokenized with the default BM25Okapi tokenizer (lower-casing, word-boundary split, English stopword removal). For each query triple (h, r, t) we issued a free-text query of the form “<head_name> <tail_name> <relation_phrase>”, retrieved the top-k documents with k = 8 (matching the mean number of nodes touched by the 2–6-hop BFS path bundle), and always force-included the head and tail descriptions to avoid penalizing the baseline for missing the queried entities. The retrieved descriptions replaced the Reasoning Path block in the prompt; the Context Information and Question blocks, the LoRA hyperparameters (r = 16, α = 32, dropout 0.05), the tokenizer, the training objective, and the five-fold entity-holdout splits were all identical to AResKGLM. We report two regimes: (i) RAG-only (frozen), the off-the-shelf Llama-3-8B-Instruct backbone with score-based decoding, which isolates the contribution of BM25 retrieval alone; and (ii) RAG-only (fine-tuned), with the LoRA adapter trained on RAG-only prompts under exactly the same recipe as AResKGLM, which isolates the contribution of explicit graph-path topology after both models have been adapted to the task.

Information parity across baselines

To make the comparison interpretable as a test of representation rather than of information access, we hold the upstream evidence budget constant across methods. All baselines see the same five-fold entity-holdout splits, the same query triples and the same set of structurally hard tail-corruption negatives. KG-BERT, NBFNet, ULTRA, and GraIL receive the full triple set for the corresponding fold; their inputs differ from AResKGLM only in that entity descriptions and BFS paths are not consumed (KG-BERT consumes triple text only; NBFNet, ULTRA, and GraIL consume the structural graph only). RAG-only consumes the same description corpus as AResKGLM, retrieved with BM25 over a query string built from the head, tail, and relation tokens, with the same fine-tuning recipe and the same Llama-3-8B backbone. AResKGLM consumes the same descriptions and additionally the explicit BFS path bundle. The difference between RAG-only (fine-tuned) and AResKGLM, therefore, isolates the contribution of explicit graph-path topology while controlling for backbone, parameter budget, training data, retrieval corpus, and answer format. The difference between AResKGLM and the GNN baselines isolates the contribution of biomedical entity semantics conditional on the same graph structure being available. We do not interpret the F1 gap to KG-BERT, NBFNet, or ULTRA as a test of LLM scale alone, because their parameter counts and training objectives differ; we report parameter counts and an order-of-magnitude information budget per method in Supplementary Table S24.

Evaluation metrics and statistical testing

We report Accuracy, Precision, Recall, F1, and MCC, stratified by hop length. All paired comparisons use exact Wilcoxon signed-rank tests [44] on 25 matched fold–hop slices. The temporal analysis uses score-based confidence-margin decoding (mean token log-probability of yes versus no completions) to produce Precision@K and AUC-PR.

Cross-species evaluation

Auxiliary AMR KGs for K. pneumoniae, M. tuberculosis, P. aeruginosa, and S. aureus were assembled from CARD [7], UniProt, NCBI Gene, and published literature. Each was processed with the same five-fold entity-holdout pipeline and Llama-3-8B backbone.

Temporal ranking set

We queried PubMed for E. coli AMR studies (January 2023–April 2026). From 711 abstracts, 81 “new-pair” gene–drug associations were identified where both entities exist in the KG but the pairing is absent from training. Cross-functional negatives were generated from 3639 non-AMR genes paired with the same drug pool, ensuring biological implausibility.
