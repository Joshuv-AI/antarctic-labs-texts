Clinical decision-making in neonatal gastrointestinal surgical emergencies: comparison between ChatGPT and human clinicians
Authors: Seyoon Kim, Dongho Choi, Joonhyuk Son
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundNeonatal gastrointestinal surgical emergencies (NGSEs) require rapid decisions to prevent morbidity and mortality. This study assessed the potential use of ChatGPT in supporting clinical decision-making for NGSEs.MethodsThe challenging NGSE cases (ileal atresia, midgut volvulus, Hirschsprung disease, meconium ileus, and pseudo-obstruction) were converted into structured short-answer questions including histories and radiologic images. Questions covered differential diagnosis, diagnostic plan, management plan, final diagnosis, and surgical plan. Each case was scored out of 10 (maximum 50). Scenarios were presented to 10 general surgery (GS) residents, 10 GS attendings, and 10 pediatric surgery (PS) attendings. GPT-4o was tested with 10 iterations per case. Group scores were compared using appropriate statistical tests.ResultsA total of five cases were involved. GPT-4o achieved a mean score of 44.95 (89.9%), higher than GS residents (27.05, p<0.001) and GS attendings (28.35, p<0.001), but lower than PS attendings (47.70, p=0.021). Subgroup analysis showed GPT-4o matched PS attendings in management, final diagnosis, and surgical planning, but scored lower in differential diagnosis (87.8% vs. 92.8%, p=0.0479) and diagnostic plan (75.0% vs. 93.8%, p<0.001). Compared with GS residents and attendings, GPT-4o performed significantly better across all categories except diagnostic plan.ConclusionsGPT-4o demonstrated performance comparable to PS attendings in key management domains, while clearly surpassing GS residents and attendings overall. These findings suggest that GPT-4o may have potential as a supplementary decision-support tool for NGSEs, although clinical use requires further validation in real-world settings.Keywords: pediatrics; education, medical

Introduction

Artificial intelligence (AI) technology has advanced rapidly in recent years, demonstrating its potential to augment clinical decision-making processes across various medical specialties.1–10 Among AI applications, large language models (LLMs) such as ChatGPT can generate human-like responses and provide context-specific information.11 12 Notably, models such as ChatGPT have demonstrated promising results on medical licensing exams and other standardized objective assessments, achieving performance levels comparable to those of human doctors.13–17 In additional, recent findings suggest that ChatGPT is capable of providing treatment recommendations for pediatric patients.18–20 However, the direct application of AI in clinical settings, particularly in managing complex cases in pediatric medicine, remains underexplored and requires careful evaluation.

One such high-risk area in pediatric surgery (PS) is neonatal gastrointestinal surgical emergencies (NGSEs), which include conditions such as ileal atresia, midgut volvulus, Hirschsprung disease, pseudo-obstruction, and meconium ileus.21–23 These conditions require timely and precise clinical decision-making to prevent severe complications. However, their presentation can often be atypical, making diagnosis and management particularly challenging, even for experienced surgeons. Delays in diagnosis or inappropriate initial management can lead to catastrophic outcomes, including bowel necrosis, sepsis, and mortality. The complexity of NGSE underscores the importance of specialized pediatric surgical expertise.23 24 However, in many hospitals, particularly in low- and middle-income countries, a shortage of pediatric surgeons has led to situations in which general surgeons or trainees must manage these cases without optimal subspecialty support.25–27

This study addresses these challenges by assessing the ability of ChatGPT to support clinical decision-making in NGSE cases and directly comparing its performance with that of general surgery (GS) attendings, GS residents, and subspecialty-trained PS attendings in simulated pediatric surgical scenarios. We aimed to explore the accuracy, reliability, and practical applicability of ChatGPT as a clinical decision-making support tool.

Materials and methods

Scenario development

To evaluate clinical decision-making in NGSEs, five clinical scenarios were developed by two board-certified pediatric surgeons. These cases were carefully selected to represent diagnostically challenging conditions frequently encountered in clinical practice, including ileal atresia, midgut volvulus, Hirschsprung disease, pseudo-obstruction, and meconium ileus. Each scenario was structured based on actual patient data to ensure a high degree of clinical relevance. However, all identifiable information was either removed or modified to maintain patient confidentiality. The cases were designed to closely simulate real-world decision-making, incorporating patient history, radiologic images (e.g., X-rays and contrast studies), and relevant clinical findings to create a comprehensive assessment tool. All radiologic images were provided as original DICOM-derived JPEG files with standard diagnostic resolution appropriate for clinical interpretation. No annotations or text labels were added. All personally identifiable information, including patient names and registration numbers, was completely removed during the DICOM export process.

Participant recruitment and question distribution

To mitigate inter-institutional bias and incorporate a broader range of clinical perspectives, the survey was distributed across multiple hospitals rather than being limited to a single institution. The study included GS residents with at least 1 year of clinical experience, GS attendings with a minimum of 3 years experience in non-pediatric surgical subspecialties, and PS attendings with a minimum of 3 years experience specifically in PS. Each group consisted of 10 participants who were presented with structured short-answer questions derived from clinical scenarios. Their responses were collected anonymously to eliminate potential bias and ensure that all participants provided independent assessments.

For comparison, ChatGPT-4o was tested under identical conditions, with each scenario evaluated 10 times independently. For each iteration, all prior conversation history was cleared, and the model’s memory function was disabled, ensuring that no information was carried over between runs. This iterative testing process was implemented to assess response variability and determine an average score that could be compared with that of the human participants. By using multiple iterations, this study aimed to evaluate the model’s consistency in clinical decision-making rather than relying on a single response, which might not reflect its overall performance.

Outcome measurement

A standardized evaluation system was used to maintain objectivity in scoring. Prior to the assessment, two board-certified pediatric surgeons jointly developed a unified scoring rubric through discussion and consensus. Using this rubric, a board-certified PS attending performed the initial scoring. This assessment encompassed five key categories of questions: differential diagnosis, diagnostic plan, management plan, final diagnosis, and surgical plan. Each scenario was given a maximum score of 10 points, with a maximum total score of 50 for all the scenarios.

Data collection and statistical analysis

Shapiro-Wilk test was used to confirm normal distribution. However, Levene’s test for homogeneity of variances indicated a violation of equal variances (p<0.05), necessitating the application of Welch’s analysis of variance (ANOVA) to make group comparisons. To further investigate the differences between individual groups, Games-Howell post hoc tests were applied.

The assumption of normality was met for differential diagnosis and total score. The assumption of homogeneity of variances was not met. Consequently, Welch’s ANOVA was used, followed by Games-Howell post hoc test. In contrast, the assumption of normality was not satisfied for the diagnostic plan, final diagnosis, management plan, and surgical plan, necessitating the use of Kruskal-Wallis tests for group comparisons, followed by Mann-Whitney U tests for post hoc pairwise analyses. In addition, independent statistical analyses were conducted for selected pairwise comparisons between the GPT and PS groups. The Shapiro-Wilk test was used to assess normality within each group for individual question categories. Depending on the distribution, either independent t-tests or Wilcoxon rank-sum tests were used to compare the means of the two groups. Statistical significance in all analyses was set at p<0.05.

Results

Table 1 presents the average total scores of the four groups: GPT-4o, PS attendings, GS attendings, and GS residents for the five NGSE cases, while table 2 describes the intergroup comparisons of the total scores. The PS attendings group scored the highest average score (mean±standard deviation (SD) 47.70±1.32), which was significantly higher than that of the GPT-4o group (44.95±2.23; p=0.021). Meanwhile, GPT-4o significantly outperformed both GS attendings (28.35±3.75; p<0.001) and GS residents (27.05±5.60; p<0.001) groups. However, the difference between the mean total scores of the GS attendings and GS residents groups was not statistically significant (figure 1).

Question-type-specific performance was analyzed. Figure 2 and table 3 present the average percentage scores across the five clinical question categories for each group. GPT-4o demonstrated significantly higher scores than GS attending and GS residents groups in differential diagnosis (p<0.001 and p<0.001, respectively), management plan (p<0.001 and p=0.007, respectively), final diagnosis (p=0.005 and p=0.003, respectively), and surgical plan (p<0.001 and p<0.001, respectively) (table 4). In the comparative analysis between GPT-4o and PS attendings, no statistically significant differences were observed in the management plan, final diagnosis, or surgical plan categories (p=0.763, p=0.0989, and p=0.925, respectively), reflecting comparable performance in these critical decision-making areas. Discussion

In this study, GPT-4o demonstrated promising clinical decision-making performance in evaluating NGSE cases, achieving scores comparable to those of PS attendings across most categories. Notably, GPT-4o consistently outperformed both GS residents and attendings, with a particularly strong performance in all question categories, except for the diagnostic plan. These findings underscore the potential of GPT-4o as a promising clinical decision-making support tool for NGSE, especially in settings where pediatric surgical expertise is limited. However, there are several limitations and risks associated with direct application in clinical settings. First, GPT-4o often provides textbook-based responses without fully integrating complex clinical contexts. Second, GPT-4o showed limitations in interpreting radiologic findings that are critical for decision-making in NGSE. Finally, the issue of AI-generated hallucinations, including logic, information, and interpretative errors, remains a concern. These challenges highlight the need for further refinement and cautious validation before GPT-4o can be safely integrated into the real-world clinical workflow.

A notable further limitation of this study is the tendency of GPT-4o to provide textbook-based diagnostic recommendations without adequately considering the nuanced clinical contexts often encountered in NGSE. Although GPT-4o successfully identified appropriate differential diagnoses based on initial clinical presentations, errors were frequently observed when determining the initial diagnostic step. For instance, in a clinical scenario involving a neonate with bile-stained vomiting and radiographic evidence of small bowel obstruction, GPT-4o accurately included atresia, meconium ileus, and midgut volvulus in the differential diagnosis. However, when asked to suggest the most appropriate initial diagnostic test, the GPT-4o frequently recommended an upper gastrointestinal series (UGIS), citing it as the gold standard for evaluating midgut volvulus. Although textbooks do consider the UGIS the definitive diagnostic modality for confirming midgut volvulus, it is not typically the first-line investigation in real-world clinical practice. In cases where gas distension occurs immediately after feeding in a 2-day-old neonate, experienced pediatric surgeons often prioritize the evaluation of more common conditions, such as intestinal atresia or meconium ileus. Consequently, they tend to opt for abdominal ultrasonography or contrast enema (eg, barium enema) as the initial diagnostic step, which is faster to perform and more informative for likely pathologies based on the clinical context. The tendency of GPT-4o to recommend UGIS in such scenarios reflects a textbook-oriented reasoning pattern that may not align with the dynamic, experience-based decision-making process required in NGSEs. Similarly, in cases of suspected Hirschsprung disease, GPT-4o frequently recommended rectal biopsy as the initial diagnostic test. Whilst this recommendation aligns with the gold standard definitive textbook diagnostic method, it overlooks the widely accepted clinical practice of initially performing a barium enema. Of note, this issue persisted even after we modified the prompts to explicitly highlight the need for an ‘initial diagnostic test’.

These patterns likely contributed to the relatively lower score of GPT-4o in the diagnostic plan category, where it scored significantly lower than PS attendings. However, there were no significant differences between the GPT-4o scores and those of the GS attendings or residents. This outcome further illustrates the tendency of the model to prioritize textbook-based recommendations that are often context-insensitive, highlighting the gap between theoretical knowledge and real-life clinical reasoning. This discrepancy underscores a key limitation of AI-based decision-making support systems: their difficulty in handling the complexity of real-world clinical decisions. Unlike human clinicians, LLMs cannot interact with patients, read nonverbal cues, or respond to real-time changes. Their static text-based operations limit their ability to manage dynamic clinical situations. These constraints raise concerns about their safe use in critical care and highlight the need for further improvement.

Beyond these behavior-level limitations, the knowledge base of GPT-4o is also constrained by its training data. The exact composition of GPT-4o’s training dataset has not been publicly disclosed. OpenAI reports that the model is trained on licensed data, human-generated content, and publicly available text, without real-time access to paywalled scientific journals. Accordingly, its medical knowledge likely reflects information derived from open-access or licensed sources, underscoring an inherent limitation of general-purpose LLMs. This gap underscores the fundamental limitation imposed by GPT-4o’s general-domain knowledge sources. Future medical AI models trained on domain-specific, expert-curated clinical datasets may ultimately overcome these constraints and provide more reliable decision support.

A further limitation of GPT identified in this study was poor ability to interpret radiologic data. As OpenAI’s latest GPT-4o model is equipped with image recognition functions. We tested its performance by providing only X-ray and contrast study images without additional textual explanations, thereby allowing the model to interpret images autonomously. Several notable interpretation errors were identified through this process. For instance, GPT-4o occasionally described small bowel obstruction radiographs with air distension as showing a ‘double bubble’ sign, even when the actual radiograph did not present such a finding. In another case, a contrast enema image that showed a well-dilated rectum with narrowing at the descending colon and a microcolon appearance was misinterpreted by the model as an abnormal transition zone. This was not present because an abnormal transition zone typically refers to a narrowed rectum followed by proximal colonic dilatation. These errors indicate that the current radiologic interpretation capability of GPT-4o is still limited and may lead to confusion or even misdiagnosis if applied directly in clinical settings without careful consideration. Additional testing revealed that providing explicit radiologic findings by inputting descriptive interpretations of the imaging results into the prompts did not significantly alter the diagnostic accuracy of the model. This suggests that although GPT-4o can process radiologic information in textual form, its ability to effectively interpret and apply such information in clinical reasoning remains limited. Given the complex and subtle nature of neonatal imaging, this is a critical limitation of the model. Nonetheless, there is optimism, as many independent efforts are underway to enhance deep learning models, specifically for radiologic tasks.28–30 Integration of these advanced imaging models with LLMs such as GPT-4o may substantially improve future diagnostic accuracy. However, until such advances are made, relying on raw imaging inputs for AI-assisted clinical decision-making remains risky.

In addition to evaluating the overall clinical decision-making performance of GPT-4o, this study systematically assessed the presence of different types of hallucinations observed in its responses. In this context, we defined hallucinations as AI-generated errors classified into four categories: (1) logic errors, representing flawed reasoning or premature conclusions unsupported by clinical data; (2) information errors, involving outdated, insufficient, or incorrect medical knowledge; (3) interpretation errors, which occur when GPT-4o misinterprets the intent of the question, distorts meaning, or overlooks the clinical context and (4) linguistic errors, such as imprecise word choices or vocabulary limitations. Through qualitative analysis, we frequently identified instances of all four categories across scenarios (see online supplemental table S1 for definitions and examples).

Although these hallucinations did not lead to substantial changes in the final quantitative scores of GPT-4o, as each hallucination was penalized by one point regardless of the correctness of the final answer, the potential clinical consequences of such errors cannot be overlooked. Inappropriate reasoning, misinterpretation of the clinical context or outdated information can result in critical diagnostic or management errors when applied to real-world scenarios. Moreover, despite attempts to mitigate these limitations, including provision of radiologic interpretations directly through prompts, the diagnostic performance of GPT-4o was not significantly altered. This highlights the persistent nature of hallucinations in LLM-based clinical decision-making support systems. Therefore, further research is necessary to develop systematic methods to detect, minimize, and correct these, especially before considering their integration into complex clinical environments such as neonatal surgery.

It is noteworthy that GPT-4o has remarkable strengths, particularly in aspects of clinical decision-making beyond diagnostic planning. As shown in figure 2, GPT-4o performed exceptionally well in the categories of management plan, final diagnosis, and surgical plan, with performance levels comparable to those of PS attendings. For example, GPT-4o appropriately recommended surgical intervention in cases where a patient’s distal small bowel obstruction progressively worsened over time. Furthermore, when midgut volvulus was strongly suspected based on the ultrasound findings, GPT-4o correctly emphasized the need for prompt emergency surgery. In additional, the model accurately identified final diagnoses after reviewing intraoperative findings and provided detailed and appropriate surgical plans. These results suggest that, from a clinician’s perspective, GPT-4o has the potential to offer meaningful guidance and support in decision-making, particularly in surgical management; this may be highly valuable in clinical practice, especially when pediatric surgical expertise is limited. However, these findings should not be interpreted as evidence that ChatGPT is ready to guide real-time clinical decisions, particularly in determining surgical intervention or the need for transfer to a higher level of care. Given the simulated nature of this study, further prospective validation in actual clinical environments will be essential before any clinical implementation can be considered.

This study has several limitations. First, fully organized clinical scenarios may introduce a methodological bias, as such well-structured information would not typically be available to surgeons in real clinical settings. This ‘scenario-preparation bias’ should be considered when interpreting its relative performance. Second, the data collection process relied on an online survey platform, which may have introduced environmental variability as responses were obtained under non-standardized conditions without controlled supervision. This limitation may have affected the consistency of the human participant responses. Third, participants within the same group likely had varying levels of clinical exposure to NGSE cases depending on their training institutions. This heterogeneity may have influenced individual performance and could have contributed to within-group variability. In addition, although the scoring rubric was standardized, it was developed by PS specialists and may therefore contain inherent bias in how clinical reasoning or management steps were weighed.

Nevertheless, this study had several strengths. Most importantly, all the clinical scenarios were derived from real patient cases, reflecting a high degree of clinical relevance and authenticity. Unlike previous studies that used artificial case vignettes, this approach allowed for a realistic evaluation of AI-assisted decision-making in NGSE. Additionally, the question structure was designed to replicate real-world diagnostic workflows, facilitating the assessment of the model’s applicability to actual clinical settings. Another major strength is the multicenter design, which involved participants from multiple independent institutions, thereby reducing the risk of institutional bias and improving the generalizability of the findings. These methodological advantages reinforce the robustness of this study and provide meaningful insights into the potential utility of GPT-4o in pediatric surgical decision-making.

In conclusion, GPT-4o demonstrated performance comparable to PS attendings in management planning and final diagnosis for NGSE cases, while outperforming GS residents and attendings in all aspects of clinical decision-making. These findings suggest that ChatGPT may have potential as a decision-support tool in managing neonatal gastrointestinal emergencies, particularly in resource-limited settings. However, further validation through real-world clinical trials is necessary to ensure its safety, reliability, and integration into clinical workflows.
