Hiding in plain sight: uncovering the genetic basis of complex phenotypes through low-risk groups
Authors: Ana I Vazquez, Yifei Li, Guanqi Lu, Harish Neelam, Molly S Bray, Sadeep Shrestha, Richard J Reynolds, Hongsheng Gui, Davorka Gulisija
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractMedical genetic studies focus on high-risk groups to enrich for targeted phenotypes. Although genetic effects may vary across the lifespan, age-related enrichment of target phenotypes can also arise from cumulative environmental exposures, without requiring changes in genetic architecture. Accumulating environmental effects over the lifetime can inflate nongenetic variance, creating the illusion of greater genetic signal while in fact masking underlying genetic effects. We propose investigating the genetic architecture of complex phenotypes in lower-risk, younger individuals, which may reduce residual variance and improve power to isolate the genetic underpinnings of complex disease-related traits. To illustrate this, we analyzed 9 complex traits across age-stratified White European cohorts in the UK Biobank. Genome-wide association analyses reveal a greater number of genome-wide significant SNPs in younger cohorts. Complementing these results, heritability analyses show a higher relative genetic contribution in younger cohorts. Together, these patterns demonstrate that genetic signals are more readily detected in lower-risk, younger individuals. This perspective suggests that temporal dynamics in genetic architecture should guide participant selection, challenging the traditional focus on high-risk older groups and highlighting the value of targeting higher-heritability groups for genetic discovery.

Polygenic nature of complex phenotypes

Many physiological traits, such as fasting glucose (Dupuis et al. 2010), blood pressure (Kurniansyah et al. 2022), or lipids in blood (Cupido et al. 2021; Choi et al. 2025), are highly polygenic. These so-called complex traits are influenced by thousands of genetic variants, most with very small effects (Hill et al. 2008), while environmental/lifestyle factors contribute to the nongenetic variance and obscure genetic signals. Together, these environmental factors reduce statistical power and hinder replication, making the identification of genetic mechanisms underlying disease challenging.

Meeting this challenge has motivated large-scale genetic and genomic initiatives, starting with the Human Genome Project (Lander et al. 2001; International Human Genome Sequencing Consortium 2004). At its inception, Francis S. Collins, then director of the National Institute of Health, testified before the United States Senate that the sequencing of the human genome represented “an awesome step toward a more profound understanding of the biological basis of disease and [would help us] develop more effective ways to diagnose, treat, and prevent illness.” (Collins 2001) Since the human genome was sequenced, sequencing technologies (Sanger et al. 1977; Mullis and Faloona 1987; Klein et al. 2005; Jinek et al. 2012) and analytical methods for studying complex traits (Gulisija et al. 2025) have advanced substantially and have become more affordable.

Despite substantial progress in identifying genetic contributors to common diseases, a comprehensive understanding of the genetic basis of rare and complex conditions remains elusive (100,000 Genomes Project Pilot Investigators 2021). Genome-wide studies have uncovered thousands of associations, yet reproducibility remains a persistent challenge (Li and Meyre 2013), with many reported signals failing to replicate and others reflecting false positives. At the same time, limited statistical power restricts the detection of variants with small effect sizes and rare alleles (Manolio et al. 2009; Rietveld et al. 2014; Marigorta et al. 2018), even though both contribute to complex phenotypes (Aslibekyan et al. 2014). Identified variants typically explain only a modest fraction of the heritability estimated from genome-wide data, a discrepancy known as missing heritability (Lynch and Walsh 1998; Hunter 2005).

In addition, complex gene–environment interactions remain underexplored (Lynch and Walsh 1998; Hunter 2005), further limiting predictive power (Manolio et al. 2009; Golan et al. 2014). Together, these limitations reflect fundamental constraints in statistical power, which is determined not only by sample size but also by key features of genetic architecture—allele frequencies, effect sizes, and the proportion of phenotypic variance attributable to genetic factors. Because these components of genetic architecture can vary across contexts and over time, improving discovery will require rethinking study design and participant selection to better account for such dynamics.

Prioritizing sample size and high-prevalence groups

To overcome limited statistical power, studies have focused on both increasing sample sizes (Manolio et al. 2009; Aslibekyan et al. 2014; Marigorta et al. 2018) and recruiting from high-risk groups enriched for target phenotypes, such as older cohorts (Dupuis et al. 2010). The assumption was that enrichment for target phenotypes is driven by enrichment for underlying genetic effects. Thus, jointly scaling sample size and strategically targeting high-risk groups has been used as a route to increased power for genetic discovery. Consequently, because many health conditions emerge in middle age, younger participants were often underrepresented in recruitment efforts.

In 2007, the Wellcome Trust Case–Control Consortium (WTCCC) published one of the first large-scale genome-wide association studies (GWAS) (The Wellcome Trust Case Control Consortium 2007). Examining nearly 17,000 individuals and 7 common diseases, the study identified 2 dozen genetic signals associated with disease risk. More valuable than its findings, however, was the perspective gained from this endeavor: with sufficiently large data, the genetic architecture of complex diseases could be uncovered. Since then, genetic studies have steadily expanded in scale. Early GWAS moved from 1,000 to 10,000 participants (e.g. Atherosclerosis Risk In Communities (ARIC) Study; The Atherosclerosis Risk in Communities Investigators 1989; and Framingham Heart Study; Mahmood et al. 2014), to consortia with tens of thousands of participants (Genetic Investigation of ANthropometric Traits; Yengo et al. 2018; DIAbetes Genetics Replication And Meta-analysis consortium; Dupuis et al. 2010), and now biobanks with hundreds of thousands of genotyped participants (UK-Biobank (UKB); Sudlow et al. 2015; All Of Us; All of Us Research Program Investigators 2019), all while enriching for older adults. These initiatives continue to grow sample sizes in an effort to address the still-present lack of statistical power to detect small effects (Manolio et al. 2009; Makowsky et al. 2011; de los Campos et al. 2018; Wainschtein et al. 2022). However, further increases in cohort size may be impractical or infeasible. For example, large clinical studies are costly, and sufficiently large patient populations may not exist. This limitation motivates the need for alternative strategies to enhance power.

Can statistical power be improved by reducing nongenetic variance without introducing ascertainment bias? Phenotypes associated with disease risk are influenced by environmental exposures. An enriched sample of subjects with increased prevalence of disease can be an effective way to study the environmental risk of developing disease, but it can obscure the genetic signal. Notably, sampling older adults to target a group with a higher prevalence of diseases, thus, the selection of more extreme risk phenotype (e.g. more subjects with high fasting glucose) does not imply a stronger genetic signal, a point we develop below.

The sources of variation in complex phenotypes

Complex phenotypes (P) emerge from the combined effects of numerous alleles (G), environmental influences (E), and their interactions (G×E), also referred to as phenotypic plasticity. Phenotypes, however, may change over time, driven by both preset temporal changes in G or changing environments over the lifetime of an individual:

with phenotypic variation consisting of time-dependent components of variance, with (.)t indicating time. For instance, gene expression can vary across the lifespan due to endogenous developmental programming (de Magalhães and Church 2005) or age-related epigenetic modification, altering genetic contributions to phenotypes independently of external environmental exposures. Such intrinsic temporal regulation can change the effects of genotypes underlying traits such as hypothalamic–pituitary–gonadal (HPG) axis activation (Acevedo-Rodriguez et al. 2018), age-dependent shifts in insulin signaling (Tatar et al. 2003), or immunosenescence (Mittelbrunn and Kroemer 2021).

At the same time, complex physiological phenotypes are increasingly shaped by accumulating environmental influences that interact with genetic effects. When such environmentally induced changes act on genetic contributions to the trait (Gt), they manifest as Gt×Et interactions, implying that phenotype etiology depends jointly on genetic architecture and its environmental modulation. Together, developmental programming and gene–environment interactions can cause different genes to influence the same phenotype at different life stages, producing age-dependent associations (Brouwer et al. 2022) and dynamic components of phenotypic variation across lifespan (Bergen et al. 2007; Jiang et al. 2021).

Furthermore, environmental contributions to phenotypes may vary across the lifespan. Yet, statistical genetics studies typically overlook this temporal dimension and instead focus on cohorts with the highest cross-sectional prevalence of targeted phenotypes.

The genetic architecture differs across traits: some traits exhibit stable genetic effects across the lifespan, such as largely nonplastic morphological traits (Fig. 1, G trait). Some traits have a variable Gt relative to the contribution of environmental factors accumulated over time, such as physical activity, nutrition, smoking, stress, medication use, or changes in socioeconomic status. Here, age captures the cumulative effects of multiple exposures, many of which are difficult to measure, making it a practical proxy for cumulative environmental influences. Accumulation of environmental effects over a lifespan not only modifies the risk of the complex condition in high-risk groups but may also obscure the genetic signal (G+E traits, Fig. 1). Here, we argue that focusing on younger subjects, whose traits are less shaped by accumulated environmental effects, reduces confounding effects and reveals the genetic basis of complex conditions. Evidence for a stronger genetic signal in younger age groups

To gain resolution into the temporal dynamics of human phenotypes, we leveraged the UKB European-White participant cohort (Supplementary Table 1) to compare genetic associations and heritability across 9 complex traits: body mass index (BMI, kg/m2), cholesterol (mmol/L), body fat percentage(%), high-density (HDL, mmol/L) and low-density (LDL, mmol/L) lipoprotein cholesterol, creatinine (umol/L), triglycerides (mmol/L), serum urate (umol/L), and systolic blood pressure (SBP, mmHg). The phenotypes were preadjusted for the fixed effects of sex, age, batch, center, and 5 SNP-derived principal components to account for potential confounding due to demographic differences, population stratification, and study-specific effects. The preadjusted phenotypes were then stratified by age and analyzed using standard GWAS and variance component methods (see Supplementary Information for further details), while the robustness of age treatment in the stratified model was examined under LDL by comparing no preadjustment for age with preadjustment including both linear and quadratic age terms (Supplementary Table 2).

First, we performed GWAS in equally sized groups partitioned at the mean age of 58, creating a group equal to or younger than 58 yr (younger age group), and another group with participants older than 58 yr (older age group). Remarkably, for all traits except triglyceride concentration, more SNPs surpassed genome-wide significance thresholds, and with stronger signals, in the younger-age group (Fig. 2). The number of inferred associations increased dramatically for some traits: SBP, cholesterol, and LDL more than doubled, whereas others, such as HDL, showed little difference in the number of inferred associations in the 2 groups. For all traits except body fat percentage, the younger group recapitulated most associations seen in the older group and identified many additional ones. Numerous age-specific associations suggest that different genes may contribute to body fat percentage across life stages. Age-related shifts in gene expression relevant to adiposity may indicate distinct developmental programming or gene–environment interactions underlying this trait over time, and are consistent with recent findings (Venkatesh et al. 2024).

In contrast, triglyceride concentration showed a greater number of significant associations in the older group, compared to the younger group. Given that this trait is sensitive to environmental effects such as diet (Parks 2001), the results could indicate genotype-by-environment interactions, or developmental plasticity, which may statistically contribute to the genetic effect.

Next, we estimated the heritability, the proportion of phenotypic variance attributable to genetic factors, across 5-yr sliding age windows for the same 6 traits (Fig. 3). The heritability tended to decrease with age, indicating an increase in the proportion of nongenetic/environmental variance. These results were in perfect agreement with the GWAS findings, showcasing a larger decrease in heritability across age for the traits where a greater number of SNPs surpassed the significance threshold in the younger group (Fig. 2).

Heritability analyses confirmed that traits with relatively stable G, such as HDL, where the number of associations is similar in younger and older groups, tend to display stable heritability estimates across age. On the other hand, the environmental effect is notably more influential as subjects age in many traits, resulting in lower heritability for several of the traits, such as BMI, cholesterol, LDL, serum urate, SBP, creatinine, and percent of body fat. For the traits for which the number of inferred associations halves (SBP, cholesterol, and LDL concentrations), the heritability sharply decreases with age.

The heritability of triglyceride levels showed a pronounced nonlinear pattern, peaking in midlife and stabilizing thereafter, consistent with the lower number of associations observed in the younger group. Indeed, in a twin study using a dichotomous age division in which middle-aged individuals were classified as the younger group, heritability in triglycerides was found to be notably lower than in the relatively older group (Heller et al. 1993). This comparison, however, should be interpreted cautiously, as twins, even when reared apart, still share the same prenatal environment and other nonadditive genetic effects, potentially reducing noise in the data.

Taken together, heritability and GWAS results indicate that genetic association discovery may be more effective in younger age groups for most traits: GWAS peaks are largely consistent across age, while statistical power declines in older groups due to increased residual variance.

To extend this conclusion to the full spectrum of genetic effects, including those undetected in association studies, we estimated genetic correlations between the youngest group and other age groups across 8 strata. We observe consistently strong genetic overlap across ages (ranging from 0.85 to 0.97 across traits), indicating that the same genetic factors largely underlie these traits throughout life. Traits with more shared GWAS peaks across age groups tend to exhibit slightly higher heritability. In particular, height, a trait with a highly stable GWAS landscape, shows the highest and most consistent genetic correlations (expected ≈0.95), reflecting a stable genetic architecture across the lifespan. HDL (0.94) and total cholesterol (0.93) also appear stable with minimal drift. LDL (0.92) and triglycerides (0.92) show slightly greater variability, particularly in intermediate age groups. Fat percentage (0.90) exhibits the most noticeable fluctuation, consistent with a somewhat more variable genetic architecture observed in GWAS, though correlations remain high overall.

While heritability declines with age as expected (Jiang et al. 2021), concordant GWAS peaks and high genetic correlations across age groups indicate a largely stable genetic architecture for most traits examined. Together, these results suggest that reduced heritability across the lifespan primarily reflects increased residual variance and not a change in genetic effects. Increased residual variance reduces power to detect genetic effects, supporting the use of younger cohorts to study the genetic basis of disease-related phenotypes without loss of generality.

Implications and future directions

These results highlight the untapped potential of studying low-risk groups, such as younger individuals, to uncover genetic effects often obscured in traditional designs (The Wellcome Trust Case Control Consortium 2007; Pairo-Castineira et al. 2023; Adams et al. 2024) that focus on age-enriched, high-risk cohorts (The Wellcome Trust Case Control Consortium 2007; Adams et al. 2024). For most traits we discussed here, the rise in target phenotypes with age appears disproportionately environmentally driven. For these traits, the same SNPs exhibit stronger associations in relatively younger individuals, and simultaneously, higher heritabilities for the traits in relatively younger individuals, suggesting a largely stable genetic architecture but elevated noise in older cohorts. This implies that simple variance partitioning can guide more efficient study designs by identifying high-resolution groups from which to recruit—those with higher heritability. We propose that broader age sampling in future studies would help to illuminate the dynamics of phenotypic change with age.

This study also uncovers an interesting trend: age-related changes in human continuous, physiological traits arise predominantly from growing environmental variance, not from a change in genetic effects. While studies reported genotype-by-environment interactions, for example, for obesity-related traits (Winkler et al. 2024), blood pressure (Shi et al. 2009; Simino et al. 2014), and blood lipid concentrations (Dumitrescu et al. 2011), few studies examined changes in cumulative genetic effect across age. Yet, genetic risk appears to decline with age for several complex diseases (Jiang et al. 2021), as does overall heritability in triglyceride levels (Heller et al. 1993) and cognitive performance (Finkel and Reynolds 2014). In particular, Jiang et al. (2021) analyzed genetic risk across 24 common diseases in the UK Biobank, identifying 21 to 116 associated variants across diseases, and found that, in 9 diseases, genetic risk decreased with age. This decrease is likely relative to other factors influencing disease risk, with environmental effects becoming more prominent in older individuals.

We, on the other hand, examined 9 continuous traits that change over the lifespan, rather than the incidence of disease. While many diseases appear later in life, the physiological traits that underlie risk, such as blood glucose or blood pressure, change continuously across the lifespan, offering a window to detect genetic effects before they are masked by environmental variation (Tabák et al. 2009). We argue that by studying these traits earlier and strategically selecting cohorts to limit environmental noise, standard statistical genetics analyses can achieve higher resolution, revealing underlying genetic architecture and enhancing discovery beyond what post hoc statistical adjustments provide.

The implications of this study rest on the assumption that additional associations reflect novel genes and provide meaningful insight into trait biology. As this work is intended as an introduction rather than an exhaustive analysis, we do not present genome-wide annotations for all traits. Instead, we provide an illustrative example focusing on LDL, a trait with a relatively stable association landscape but increasing environmental influence across the lifespan, with heritability declining from 0.35 to 0.10. Notably, the newly detected associations map to genes not detected in the older group. Indeed, the majority of genes identified for LDL are found in the younger group (42 unique genes), with a subset shared across age groups (28 genes) and only a subset unique to the older cohort (11 genes). Therefore, not only do more SNPs exhibit higher statistical significance in younger groups but also this shift toward lower P-values leads to the identification of more genes (Supplementary Tables 3–6); and, moreover, genes detected in both groups show consistently lower P-values in the younger group (Supplementary Table 4).

Several caveats merit consideration. Our study lacks a wide adult age spectrum, and we examined only 9 complex traits, leaving open the possibility of additional patterns in unexplored traits. We assumed that relatively older and younger cohorts are unbiased samples from the same population, allowing us to use cross-sectional rather than longitudinal data, but introducing potential cohort effects. Lastly, our analyses were restricted to White European participants. Nonetheless, our findings reveal an intriguing trend that could be further explored in broader, age-diverse cohorts such as All of Us (All of Us Research Program Investigators 2019).
