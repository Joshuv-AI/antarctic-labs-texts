Dried blood spots capture a wide range of metabolic pathways and biological changes associated with exercise
Authors: Karen L. DeBalsi, Kelli D. Goodman, Laura J. Sommerville, Matthew W. Mitchell, Blair A. Lane, Anne M. Evans, Adam D. Kennedy
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundMetabolomics is poised to significantly advance our ability to diagnose and characterize disease, identify diagnostic and prognostic biomarkers, and monitor a patient’s response to treatment. Despite these benefits, metabolomics-based diagnostic tests have not been adapted into clinical practice primarily because metabolomics technologies, which include high performance liquid chromatography and mass spectrometry, are not conducive to point-of-care. Expanding the reach of metabolomics-based testing requires centralized technology to be more accessible.MethodsOne way to achieve this is by utilizing dried blood spots (DBS) as a diagnostic testing sample type. Their easy collection, lack of reliance on cold chain, and cost-effective storage and shipment make them the ideal sample type for diagnostic testing that requires a centralized technology. To date, few studies have investigated the ability of DBS to capture the global metabolome and track biological changes in individual subjects.ResultsIn this feasibilty study, we tested these factors by performing untargeted metabolic profiling on DBS collected from study volunteers before and after exercising. We compared our results in DBS to results in serum and plasma that have been reported in the literature.ConclusionsOverall, our findings closely agreed with published studies, showing that DBS may be a viable sample type for metabolomics-based diagnostic and wellness testing.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1007/s11306-025-02390-5.

Introduction

Metabolites are small molecule reactants, intermediates, and products of metabolism upon which inputs from the genome, microbiome, environment, and lifestyle factors converge (Qiu et al., 2023). Given their unique position in the central dogma of biology, metabolites are thought to be the closest reflection of an individual’s real-time health status, which makes them an abundant source of biomarkers that can reveal deep phenotypic insight into disease (Cheng et al., 2025; Holmes et al., 2008; Li et al., 2025; Naeem et al., 2025; Tian et al., 2025; Vasilopoulou et al., 2016; Wishart, 2016). Metabolites reflect disease activity through changes in their abundance, which can be quantified using ultra-high performance liquid chromatography and tandem mass spectrometry (UHPLC-MS/MS) technologies. High level statistical analyses are then used to interpret measured metabolic changes in context with other health status indicators. When used in an untargeted manner, UHPLC-MS/MS can detect and measure a wide collection of metabolites (i.e., the metabolome) in a biological matrix to identify disease-causing metabolic perturbations. Indeed, untargeted metabolic profiling has proven to be a powerful tool for expanding our mechanistic understanding of disease progression (Bhargava & Anthony, 2020; LeWitt et al., 2017; Wendt et al., 2021; Wilkins & Trushina, 2017), identifying novel biomarkers for diagnosis and follow-up treatment monitoring (Balog et al., 2010; Han et al., 2017; Inglese et al., 2017), and characterizing undifferentiated phenotypes of inborn errors of metabolism (Burrage et al., 2019; Cappuccio et al., 2017; Ford et al., 2022; Glinton et al., 2018; Kennedy et al., 2016, 2017; Liu et al., 2021; Shayota et al., 2020; Wangler et al., 2018, Kennedy et al., 2016, Mutie et al., 2017).

Despite these contributions to medical research the value of using untargeted metabolomics-based testing in clinical practice has not been widely recognized, mainly due to logistical challenges associated with utilizing the technology at the point of care. The cost of purchasing and operating a UHPLC-MS/MS platform are quite prohibitive to private practices and clinical laboratories, and developing regulated procedures and standards for such a highly complex system go beyond the resources typically available for point of care testing. This underscores a critical need to establish and validate methods that enable simple and cost-effective access to metabolomic samples for diagnosing and monitoring disease within the boundaries of centralized technology.

Protocols for profiling the metabolome have been validated for various sample types including plasma, serum, urine, feces, and tissue (Chamberlain et al., 2019, Ly et al., 2016, Zierer et al., 2018, Meister et al., 2021, Bi et al., 2020, Chen & Yu, 2019, Dudka et al., 2020, James & Parkinson, 2015, Khamis et al., 2017, Shastry et al., 2025, Sun et al., 2024, Yang et al., 2024). Yet, all of these matrices require cold chain storage and most are not amenable to self-collection. Dried blood spots (DBS) are widely seen as the ideal solution for simple, inexpensive, and non-invasive collection of a blood sample that can be easily shipped to a central laboratory for diagnostic analysis. DBS only requires a fingerstick-sized volume of blood that patients can usually collect themselves and can be shipped and stored at ambient temperature. To date, studies that utilized untargeted metabolomics in DBS have focused primarily on newborn screening (Courraud et al., 2021; Mak et al., 2023; Petrick et al., 2020; Sebaa et al., 2023; Xiao et al., 2022, Li et al., 2013, Manicke et al., 2011, Han et al., 2014).

The goal of this feasibilty study was to further evaluate untargeted metabolomics as a suitable method for capturing broad metabolic pathways and biological characteristics using a DBS-based sample collection protocol. We evaluated the analytical performance and biological characteristics of metabolomic profiles in DBS collected from individuals before and after exercising. We then compared the metabolic changes captured in DBS to those found in plasma/serum/whole blood as reported in the literature. Herein, we show that DBS captures a broad range of metabolic pathways along with many exercise-induced changes that have been extensively reported in plasma and serum. Overall, this pilot study supports the idea that DBS is a suitable matrix for capturing the blood metabolome and justifies performing additional studies to validate these findings, as well as investigate the use of DBS in diagnostic applications, such as those performed in newborn screening, and in a variety of disease indications and wellness assessments.

Study design and sample collection

All DBS were self-collected by study subjects onto Whatman 903 Protein Saver Cards (Cytiva #10534612) using a lancet to prick the finger. Subjects were not instructed to collect samples at any particular time of the day or in relation to mealtimes. Diet, sleep, and other activities other than the 30-minute bout of exercise were not tracked. The study lasted 14 days. On days 1-8 days each subject collected one DBS sample (i.e., metabolic baseline sample), exercised for at least 30 minutes, then collected a second DBS sample immediately following the exercise bout. Participants were not monitored during exercise, nor were vitals recorded before or after exercise. On days 9-14 each subject collected a metabolic baseline sample, performed a sedentary (i.e., “rest”) activity for at least 30 minutes, then collected a second DBS sample immediately following the rest bout. A sedentary activity was any task that did not raise the subject’s heart rate above basal levels such as reading, performing desk work, or cooking. A daily log of the time exercised or rested and the activity that each subject performed are shown in Supplemental Table 1. All blood spots were dried at room temperature for 4 to 24 hours, sealed in gas-impermeable bags with desiccant, and left at room temperature for two weeks to simulate transit time from a clinic to a central laboratory. Samples were then stored at -80°C until analysis (less than one week) to simulate how DBS are handled when they arrive at Metabolon from an outside institution.

Sample processing

Samples were processed according to validated methods with certain modifications made for DBS (Evans et al., 2009, 2014; Ford et al., 2020). Briefly, industry standard 2 x 6 mm punches were taken from each DBS and rehydrated by vigorous shaking with 150μl of water. Protein was precipitated by shaking [4 minutes at 750 rpm (0.5× rate) the water/punch mixture with 500μl of methanol on a SPEXC 2000 Geno/Grinder and centrifuging (10 minutes at 2,800 rpm at 15°C). For quality control (QC) purposes, several isotopically labeled recovery standards were added to each sample before extraction as described in Supplemental Table 1 of (Ford et al., 2022). The resultant supernatants were divided into 4 aliquots and then placed on a sample evaporator (SPE-Dry 96) to remove solvents. Dried extracts were stored overnight under nitrogen. Dry Whatman card punches (DBS blanks) were extracted using an identical method in every set to ensure detected biochemicals met a 3:1 experimental-to-background level ratio. A DBS QC sample was extracted with 4 technical replicates in every set to monitor reproducibility.

Ultrahigh performance liquid chromatography-tandem mass spectrometry (UPLC/MS-MS)

Untargeted UHPLC-MS/MS was performed on samples extracted from DBS as described (Evans et al., 2009, 2014; Ford et al., 2020; Sumner et al., 2007). All samples were subjected to four different chromatography methods. Each of the 4 aliquots of dried extracts was reconstituted in a solvent optimized for each method. Aliquot #1 was analyzed using acidic positive ion conditions optimized for hydrophilic compounds. The extract was gradient eluted from a C18 column (Waters UPLC BEH C18; 100mm x 2.1 mm i.d.;, 1.7um particle size) using water and methanol containing 0.05% perfluoropentanoic acid (PFPA) and 0.1% formic acid (FA). Aliquot #2 was analyzed using acidic positive ion conditions optimized for hydrophobic compounds. The extract was gradient eluted from the same C18 column using methanol, acetonitrile, water, 0.5% PFPA, and 0.01% FA. Aliquot #3 was analyzed using basic negative ion-optimized conditions on a dedicated C18 column. The extract was eluted from the column with methanol, water, and 6.5 mM ammonium bicarbonate (pH 8.0). Aliquot #4 was analyzed using negative ionization after eluting from an HILIC column (Waters UPLC BEH Amide; 150 mm x 2.1 mm i.d., 1.7 um particle size) using a gradient consisting of water and acetonitrile with 10mM ammonium formate (pH 10.8). All aliquots were injected into an LC/MS platform consisting of Waters Acquity UHPLCs coupled to Thermo Fisher Q-exactive mass spectrometers.

Compound annotation and data analysis

Compounds were annotated by comparing the mass-to-charge (m/z), retention time, and associated fragmentation spectra in each sample to Metabolon’s proprietary reference library containing 7,000+ standard chemical entities as described (Dehaven et al., 2010; Evans et al., 2009, 2014; Ford et al., 2020; Sumner et al., 2007). Raw scan data was processed using proprietary tools developed in-house (Dehaven et al., 2010). Annotations conformed to Level 1 as described in (Sumner et al., 2007). The methods used to develop the proprietary reference library are described in (Dehaven et al., 2010). The analytical conditions of this study are described in detail in (Dehaven et al., 2010). Representative chromatographic runs are shown in Supplemental Figure 1. Technical replicates of DBS QC samples were extracted in each 48 well plate and interspersed throughout the run. Data from the donor DBS were normalized as follows: the raw peak areas for the metabolite for the sample were divided by the median of the raw peak areas of the DBS QC samples that were run in the same instrument batch. This computation was performed if the metabolite was detected in at least 50% of the samples in its instrument batch. Those metabolites that would be normalized in all of their batches were retained for the statistical analyses. After normalization, the missing values for a given metabolite were imputed with the observed minimum. Since the data had multiple nesting, after performing the log-transformation, the values of each state (exercise or rest) and time (pre or post) combination were averaged per subject. For this averaged data, the experimental design is split block where the donor is the blocking factor. This analysis was performed in R with the package ImerTest (Kuznetsova & Christensen, 2017). Contrasts were performed from these using the emmeans package to compute the least square means (R L. emmeans: Estimated Marginal Means & aka Least-Squares Means., 2023). Due to multiple testing, false discovery rates were computed with the q-value method of Storey and Tibshirani (Storey et al., 2022).

Results

Confirmation of analytical precision

To confirm acceptable precision and detection of metabolites we analyzed the DBS QC samples, which were extracted with 4 technical replicates per extraction plate for a total of 56 replicates. The median relative standard deviation (RSD) of this sample pool was 10%, which met our acceptance criteria of ≤ 15% for standard deviation.

Glycolysis

Exercise enhances glucose metabolism (i.e., glycolysis) in skeletal and cardiac muscle and in red blood cells (RBC) (Evans et al., 2019; Richter & Hargreaves, 2013). During glycolysis glucose is converted to lactate via a series of reactions that each results in the generation of a different glycolytic intermediate (Figure 1A). During exercise muscles increase their uptake of glucose from the blood to meet the heightened energy demand. Increased glucose uptake coupled with increased glycolysis in RBC subsequently leads to an overall decrease in glucose and increase in glycolytic intermediates in the blood, which has been extensively reported (Lewis et al., 2010; Messier et al., 2017; Ohno, 1978; Peake et al., 2014). In DBS we found a significant decrease in glucose and a significant increase in lactate and most glycolytic intermediates post-exercise relative to pre-exercise, pre-rest, and post-rest (Figure 1B-G). Interestingly, we did not see a significant change in glucose-6-phsophate (G-6-P) following exercise (Figure 1H). This stands in contrast to one study, which reported increased G-6-P in plasma following either 60 minutes of high intensity training or a marathon run (Lewis et al., 2010). Strenuous physical activity stimulates the release of adrenaline, which in turn signals the release of glucose from the liver (Howlett et al., 1999), enabling glycolysis to continue at an elevated rate. Thus, G-6-P is likely to be elevated during strenuous exercise. Given that the majority of participants in our study engaged in mild to moderate physical activity such as walking, jogging, and hiking the lack of change in circulating G-6-P may be due to either lower release of glucose from the liver or its retention in the muscle to either replete glycogen stores or metabolize to lactate. Despite this discrepancy, our data show that the post-exercise glycolytic profile captured by DBS closely matches the profile reported in plasma and serum.

Lipolysis and fatty acid oxidation

In addition to glucose, cardiac and skeletal muscle each derive energy from lipids during exercise through lipolysis and β-oxidation of free fatty acids (FFA) (Horowitz & Klein, 2000; Muscella et al., 2020). Briefly, during lipolysis triacylglycerols are broken down into FFA and glycerol. FFA are then taken up by muscle cells through fatty acid transporters on the cell surface. Once inside, the FFA is converted to fatty acyl CoA, which itself is converted to a long-chain acylcarnitine. Acylcarnitine is then transported across the inner mitochondrial membrane and converted back to fatty acetyl CoA. It enters the fatty acid β-oxidation pathway to produce many acetyl-CoAs, which enter The Citric Acid (TCA) cycle to generate ATP (Figure 2A).Fig. 2A Schematic of lipolysis and transfer of fatty acids into the mitochondria for β-oxidation. Lipolysis increases during exercise (Jeukendrup, 2002; Muscella et al., 2020), which increases the amount of circulating FFA, sometimes as much as 5-fold (Klein et al., 1994; Wolfe et al., 1990). DBS captured this exercise-induced change. We found significantly increased levels of several FFA including monounsaturated and polyunsaturated medium and long chain types (Figure 2B-E].

Carnitine is essential for the transport of long-chain fatty acids into the mitochondria for energy production. Thus, the increased demand for FFA during exercise decreases free carnitine in the plasma (Nuesch et al., 1999; Xu et al., 2016). Acylcarnitines are formed in the mitochondrial matrix from acyl-CoA and carnitine then exported from the mitochondria and released into plasma (Hove et al., 1993), which accounts for the reported increase in acylcarnitine during exercise (Friolet et al., 1994; Hiatt et al., 1989; Lennon et al., 1983; Palo et al., 1993; Soop et al., 1988). The heart primarily uses FFA to generate ATP during exercise and thereby contributes to the rise in acylcarnitine (Khairallah et al., 2004). Simultaneously, glomerular filtration rate decreases, which lowers excretion of acylcarnitines to help maintain their levels in circulation (Freund et al., 1991). We observed this same increase in acetylcarnitine (C2) and decrease in free carnitine in post-exercise DBS (Figure 2F,G).

We note that two studies have reported a rise in short chain acylcarnitines during exercise followed by a rapid return to baseline during recovery, and a sustained elevation of long-chain acylcarnitines well into the recovery period (Hansen et al., 2015; Lehmann et al., 2010). In contrast to these findings, we saw an increase in all classes of acylcarnitines in DBS (Figure 3A-F). This may reflect the inherent variability of sample self-collection or, more likely, variability in the number of minutes each sample was taken after exercise. The metabolome captured by DBS demonstrated increased concentrations of each of these oxylipids post-exercise relative to pre-exercise and post-rest (Figure 4B-G).

TCA Cycle

The TCA cycle is the final common pathway for the catabolism of carbohydrates, lipids, and certain amino acids to acetyl-CoA, which can enter the TCA cycle for oxidation to produce two CO2 molecules, NADH and FADH2, which become oxidized to form ATP (Figure 5A). In response to the enhanced TCA cycle flux in skeletal muscle, the concentrations of nearly all TCA cycle intermediates increase in plasma during and following exercise (Brugnara et al., 2012; Gibala et al., 1997; McKinnon et al., 2008; Nieman et al., 2012, 2017; Schranner et al., 2020; Stander et al., 2018; Zhang et al., 2019). In DBS we saw a significant increase in citrate, aconitate, alpha-ketoglutarate, fumarate and malate (Figure 5B-F). Post-exercise levels of succinate trended upward from pre-exercise and resting states but the differences were not statistically significant (p = 0.06) (Figure 5G). This may be explained by succinate’s rapid return to baseline levels during recovery, and its variability in elevation during exercise, which depends on the intensity and duration of activity (Hochachka & Dressendorfer, 1976; Hui et al., 2017; Lewis et al., 2010; Reddy et al., 2020). Overall, the exercise-induced changes to TCA cycle intermediates seen in DBS closely match those reported in plasma.Fig. 5A Schematic of the TCA cycle. Amino acids

The rate of amino acid catabolism significantly increases during exercise due to increased hepatic gluconeogenesis and upregulated use of amino acid carbon to power the TCA cycle (Henriksson, 1991; Mann et al., 2021). To meet this demand the branched-chain amino acids (BCAA)—leucine, isoleucine, and valine—are released from the liver, which contribute to the pool of free amino acids available for catabolism in the skeletal muscle (Ahlborg et al., 1974). Catabolism of BCAA releases degradation products into the plasma (Figure 6A). In DBS, leucine wase significantly elevated post-exercise compared to pre-exercise (Figure 6B). Even though a post-exercise increase in valine and isoleucine did not reach significance (p = 0.10) (Figure 6 C, D), downstream degradation products of all three branched chain amino acids were significantly increased (Figure 6E-L), which generally agrees with findings reported in the literature. Creatine

Creatine is a nitrogenous organic acid that is generated endogenously from glycine, L-arginine, guanidinoacetate (GAA), and S-adenosyl-L-methionine (SAM) and can also be consumed from meat (Figure 8A). Creatine is phosphorylated by creatine kinase (CK) to yield phosphocreatine and ADP, making phosphocreatine a crucial molecule in maintaining energy supply to working tissues (Guimaraes-Ferreira, 2014). Phosphocreatine is converted non-enzymatically to the chemical waste molecule creatinine, which is transported through circulation to the kidneys where most of it is filtered out and excreted in the urine (Ao, 1990). Approximately two-thirds of the creatine found in muscle is stored as phosphocreatine (Balsom et al., 1994; Bogdanis et al., 1996). During exercise enhanced creatine metabolism increases the levels of creatinine in plasma (Chapman & Schlader, 1985). As expected, elevated GAA and creatinine were observed in post-exercise DBS (Figure 8B,C). Creatine levels increased, but not significantly (p = 0.274) post-exercise as compared to pre-exercise, but it was significantly higher post-exercise as compared to post-rest (Figure 8D). This is not surprising given that blood creatine levels would be expected to rise more in response to exercise than in response to rest . Nucleotide metabolism

During vigorous exercise the total adenine nucleotide pool decreases due to the heightened consumption of ATP by skeletal muscle. This leads to an increase in the concentraiton of IMP (Stathis et al., 1994; Tullson et al., 1995). IMP can either be reaminated to AMP to replenish the total adenine pool (Meyer & Terjung, 1979, 1980), or be broken down to inosine. Inosine is metabolized into hypoxanthine, which is then either converted back to IMP or degraded into xanthine and then uric acid (urate) (Figure 9A). The products of IMP catabolism that are not recovered intramuscularly by purine salvage are released from the muscle and accumulate in the plasma (Bangsbo et al., 1992; Green & Fraser, 1988; Harkness et al., 1983; Hellsten-Westing et al., 1994; Patterson et al., 1982; Sahlin et al., 1999; Zhao et al., 2000). We observed this trend in post-exercise DBS as many products of purine catabolism were significantly elevated including adenine, inosine, hypoxanthine, xanthine, and urate (Figure 9B-F). The observed decrease in adenosine may be due to its rapid breakdown to inosine and adenine. Inosine significantly increased after rest, which was unexpected. Bile acids

Bile acids, which are synthesized in the liver from cholesterol, contribute to the solubilization and absorption of lipids. Cholic acid and chenodeoxycholic acid are the two main bile acids produced by hepatocytes, which can later be metabolized by gut bacteria into secondary bile acids deoxycholic acid and lithocholic acid, respectively (Ridlon et al., 2016). In both rodent models and in humans exercise has been shown to increase cholesterol turnover, biliary flow, and fecal bile acid excretion, changes that are associated with a reduction in the circulating bile acid pool and greater bile acid turnover (Meissner et al., 2011; Simko & Kelley, 1979). In agreement with these findings, several studies have reported decreased plasma concentrations of several bile acids during exercise (Danese et al., 2017; Mercer et al., 2021; Morville et al., 2018). Membrane lipids

Phosphatidylcholine (PC) is an essential lipid component in cell membranes that has been linked to insulin sensitivity (Chang et al., 2019). Several studies have suggested that PC may serve as a biomarker of cardiovascular fitness since individuals with high VO2 max have lower plasma levels of choline and higher plasma levels of phosphatidylcholine relative to individuals with low VO2 max (Bye et al., 2012; Floegel et al., 2014). This difference is thought to be accounted for by phospholipase D (PLD), an enzyme in the plasma membrane of mammalian cells. PLD catalyzed the hydrolysis of phosphatidylcholine to phosphatidic acid to subsequently release choline (Park et al., 2014). While plasma levels of PC seem to be affected by one’s overall physical fitness, there is a lack of data to suggest a cause-and-effect relationship between single bouts of exercise and altered levels of PC or other phospholipids. Vitamins and cofactors

Certain vitamins and cofactors are relevant to exercise-induced changes in metabolism because they are involved in metabolic pathways that supply energy to the working muscles. Pantothenate, also known as vitamin B5, is a water-soluble vitamin that plays an active role in fatty acid metabolism and facilitates acetyl CoA entry into the TCA cycle (Leonardi & Jackowski, 2007). Pantothenate has been shown to increase in plasma after exercise to an extent that correlates with the individual’s level of fitness (Lewis et al., 2010). In DBS, we saw a significant increase in pantothenate following exercise, which agrees with findings reported in the literature (Figure 12A). Pyridoxate, an end product of vitamin B6 metabolism increased with exercise. Pyridoxate has been shown to steadily increase in plasma during exercise (Crozier et al., 1994), which we also observed in DBS (Figure 12B). Ascorbic acid (vitamin C) is involved in a number of biochemical pathways that are important to exercise metabolism (Padh, 1990). Circulating ascorbate transiently increases during exercise and may remain elevated for hours after the exercise bout (Peake, 2003; Yimcharoen et al., 2019). These changes are thought to occur from increased exercise-induced oxidative stress (Yimcharoen et al., 2019). Discussion

Untargeted metabolomics represents a promising tool for broad and comprehensive screening, diagnosis, and follow-up treatment monitoring of various diseases. At present, the performance of analytical platforms and bioinformatics approaches are able to provide deep insight into pathophysiological mechanisms that result in the onset and progression of disease (Bhargava & Anthony, 2020; LeWitt et al., 2017; Wendt et al., 2021; Wilkins & Trushina, 2017), identify novel disease biomarkers (Balog et al., 2010; Han et al., 2017; Inglese et al., 2017), and link pathogenic variants to undifferentiated phenotypes of rare diseases to thereby aid diagnosis (Burrage et al., 2019; Cappuccio et al., 2017; Ford et al., 2022; Glinton et al., 2018; Kennedy et al., 2016, 2017; Liu et al., 2021; Shayota et al., 2020; Wangler et al., 2018). Despite the technological advancements that have afforded these capabilities to metabolomics, adapting routine metabolomics-based testing to clinical practice is currently hindered by its incompatibility with point-of-care.

DBS represents an ideal solution for expanding access to metabolomics owing to their lack of reliance on cold chain, phlebotomy services, and bench top equipment for plasma separation. Several studies have demonstrated the utility of untargeted metabolomics on DBS in newborn screening assay , and the goal of this study was to further evaluate the performance of this sample matrix in global, untargeted metabolomic profiling. Here, we performed a feasibilty study to evaluate DBS as a sample type for broad metabolic screening by performing untargeted profiling on DBS collected before and after exercise. We chose to evaluate exercise-induced changes in our analysis because they have been extensively studied in plasma, serum, and whole blood, and thus, would allow the comparison of our findings in DBS to findings that have been reported in other blood sample types.

We examined all major biochemical pathways known to change in response to exercise. Overall, the metabolic changes seen in DBS largely agreed with changes that have been reported in plasma, serum, and whole blood. In DBS, we saw a characteristic exercise-induced decrease in glucose and increase in intermediates of glucose metabolism (Figure 1), which has been extensively reported in the literature (Lewis et al., 2010; Messier et al., 2017; Ohno, 1978; Peake et al., 2014). We also saw a characteristic increase in circulating FFA and acylcarnitine in response to exercise that coincided with decreased free carnitine (Figures 2 and 3). This metabolic signature has also been extensively reported in the literature (Friolet et al., 1994; Hiatt et al., 1989; Klein et al., 1994; Lennon et al., 1983; Palo et al., 1993; Soop et al., 1988; Wolfe et al., 1990). Oxylipins also increased in post-exercise DBS (Figure 4) along with intermediates of the TCA cycle (Figure 5) and intermediates of BCAA catabolism (Figures 6 and 7), in agreement with established findings in plasma (Ahlborg et al., 1974; Brugnara et al., 2012; Gibala et al., 1997; Markworth et al., 2013; McKinnon et al., 2008; Nieman et al., 2012, 2013, 2014, 2017, 2019; Schranner et al., 2020; Stander et al., 2018; Zhang et al., 2019). Changes in metabolism of creatine (Figure 8), nucleotides (Figure 9), bile acids (Figure 10), and certain vitamins (Figure 12) found in DBS also widely agreed with changes reported in plasma (Bangsbo et al., 1992; Chapman & Schlader, 1985; Crozier et al., 1994; Danese et al., 2017; Frosig et al., 2007; Green & Fraser, 1988; Harkness et al., 1983; Hellsten-Westing et al., 1994; Leonardi & Jackowski, 2007; Mercer et al., 2021; Morville et al., 2018; Patterson et al., 1982; Peake, 2003; Sahlin et al., 1999; Yimcharoen et al., 2019; Zhao et al., 2000). In our study, DBS did not reveal significant changes in any of the membrane lipids detected (Figure 11). Given the scarcity of reports showing how single bouts of exercise affect the plasma levels of these lipids we cannot categorically say whether our finding in DBS agrees or disagrees with the occurrence in plasma. However, given that membrane lipids are not a major source of energy during exercise, it would reasonably follow that their circulating levels would not be significantly altered.

A few of our findings disagreed with published reports. For example, we did not see a change in G-6-P in DBS following exercise, yet this molecule has been reported to increase in plasma following high intensity training (Lewis et al., 2010). The difference in the intensity of exercise that our study subjects performed comapred to the subjects in the other report likely accounts for this discrepancy. In DBS we saw an increase in all classes of acylcarnitines following exercise rather than an increase in short-chain acylcarnitines only. Additionally, there was no change in succinate in DBS following exercise, which disagrees with findings reported in plasma (Schranner et al., 2020). Given that short-chain acylcarnitine and succinate rapidly return to baseline during the recovery period (Hochachka & Dressendorfer, 1976; Hui et al., 2017; Lewis et al., 2010; Reddy et al., 2020) these discrepancies may simply reflect slight variability in the time-to-collection of a DBS following exercise.

Altogether, the findings of this study show that exercise-induced metabolic changes can be identified from DBS using untargeted metabolomics, and that these changes widely agree with those that have been previously reported in plasma and serum. This feasibility study provides evidence that DBS is a suitable sample matrix for profiling the metabolome and could potentially be used for broad metabolomics-based diagnostic testing.

Although this study’s findings are intriguing, we acknowledge its limitations. There was a lack of matched plasma and serum samples taken from donors, and the variability in the type and intensity of exercise performed by the study subjects was high. Yet, despite these limitations, our findings in DBS closely mirror findings reported in plasma and serum, showing that our study design did not hinder the capture and analysis of metabolic changes associated with exercise. The exact blood volume and hematocrit of the DBS used in our study were unknown. Inconsistent sample volumes may lead to errors and bias in analyte quantitation (R1’s refs). “Milking” a puncture site causes an increase in the ratio of interstitial fluid to red blood cells, which contributes to hematocrit variability. Since study participants were not supervised when self-collecting samples, some DBS may have been subjected to “milking” and potentially introduced bias. The small study size was another weakness as this precluded the evaluation of inter-biological variability among study participants and the ability to adjust the data for demographic variables such as age, sex, BMI, etc., in a statistically meaningful way. Although this study’s design was adequate for a feasibility study, certainly, firm conclusions cannot be drawn from these findings alone. Better controlled studies with larger cohorts and targeted quantitation must be done to confirm and validate the findings presented herein. We also note that until further studies can be performed, plasma will remain the gold standard for clinical diagnostics. However, our data show that in cases where one’s sample collection environment is not conducive to cold storage or phlebotomy, DBS may serve as a suitable alternative. Overall, the findings of this study demonstrate suitability of DBS for metabolomics-based testing and justify further studies aimed at validating their use in diagnostic and wellness screening.
