Lung microbiome features associated with ICU delirium in mechanically ventilated patients: a secondary cohort analysis
Authors: Shiji Xiao, Fang Lin, Yinglin Li, Qiufeng Zhuang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundDelirium in mechanically ventilated intensive care unit (ICU) patients is linked to inflammatory dysregulation, but whether the pulmonary microbiome contributes to delirium risk remains unclear.MethodsWe performed a secondary analysis of 132 mechanically ventilated adults from the MicroNAV cohort. Baseline bronchoalveolar lavage (BAL) samples collected within 12 h of intubation were profiled by 16S rRNA gene sequencing; total bacterial burden was quantified by droplet digital PCR (ddPCR; n = 125), and BAL cytokines were measured using a multiplex assay (n = 70). Concordant genus-level signals identified by four differential-abundance methods were aggregated into ecological scores and tested using logistic regression adjusted for age and Glasgow Coma Scale at intubation. Absolute abundance was estimated by multiplying relative abundance by ddPCR-derived bacterial burden.ResultsDelirium was recorded in 45 patients (34.1%). Total bacterial DNA concentration was lower in patients with delirium (median 7419 vs 98,326 copies/μL, P = 0.001). A putative short-chain fatty acid (SCFA)-producing commensal score (Phocaeicola, Selenomonas, and Fretibacterium) was inversely associated with delirium in compositional analysis (OR 0.83, 95% CI 0.75–0.92, P < 0.001) and after ddPCR-anchored absolute quantification (OR 0.84, 95% CI 0.75–0.94, P = 0.002). This depletion extended to oral-core commensals (absolute OR 0.80, P < 0.001) and a pre-specified oral anaerobe score (absolute OR 0.82, P = 0.002). Opportunistic colonizers were enriched compositionally but not in absolute abundance (OR 1.10, P = 0.187). Commensal scores were positively associated with BAL IL-1β and TNF-α, whereas opportunistic-colonizer scores showed inverse associations.ConclusionsEarly pulmonary depletion of oral-derived commensals, particularly putative SCFA producers, was associated with ICU delirium. Prospective studies with standardized delirium assessment and functional metabolite measurements are needed.

Background

Delirium is an acute disorder of attention and cognition that frequently occurs in mechanically ventilated patients in the intensive care unit (ICU) and is associated with prolonged hospitalization, increased mortality, and long-term cognitive impairment (Girard et al., 2010; Salluh et al., 2015; Wilson et al., 2020). Increasing evidence implicates immune dysregulation, cytokine signaling, blood–brain barrier vulnerability, and altered neuroimmune communication in delirium pathophysiology (Wilson et al., 2020). Growing evidence linking intestinal dysbiosis to delirium has broadened this framework by implicating host–microbiome interactions in acute brain dysfunction (Garcez et al., 2023; Zhang et al., 2023). Whether microbial communities at other mucosal sites contribute to delirium susceptibility, however, remains unclear.

Pulmonary commensal communities may shape host immune tone through multiple effector pathways, including production of short-chain fatty acids (SCFAs) that regulate microglial maturation through free fatty acid receptor 2 (FFAR2) signaling (Erny et al., 2015, 2021). The lung–brain axis has recently been identified as an additional route of peripheral-to-central immune communication: perturbation of the murine lung microbiome altered microglial activation states and modulated brain autoimmune susceptibility (Hosang et al., 2022). Whether lung microbial composition influences neurological outcomes in critically ill humans remains unknown.

The lower respiratory tract harbors oral-derived anaerobes seeded by micro-aspiration (Bassis et al., 2015; Segal et al., 2016), and mechanical ventilation can disrupt this ecosystem by impairing airway clearance, bypassing upper-airway defenses, and altering lower-airway microbial dynamics (Zakharkina et al., 2017). Longitudinal studies of intubated patients have documented declining respiratory microbial diversity and increasing community instability during mechanical ventilation, with some patients developing progressive dominance by potential pathogens (Kelly et al., 2016; Zakharkina et al., 2017). More recent multicompartment analyses have similarly suggested loss of obligate anaerobes during critical illness, accompanied by increasing abundance of potential pathogens (Kitsios et al., 2024). These changes have mainly been studied in relation to ventilator-associated pneumonia and pulmonary inflammatory dysregulation (Bustos et al., 2025; Dickson et al., 2016), whereas their relevance to non-infectious neurological outcomes remains unclear. Because mechanical ventilation can directly perturb the lower-airway microbial ecosystem while promoting pulmonary inflammation and systemic immune signaling, the lung microbiome may represent a biologically plausible but underexplored compartment in delirium susceptibility.

We hypothesized that depletion of oral-derived pulmonary commensals, including putative SCFA producers, early during mechanical ventilation would be associated with ICU delirium and distinct bronchoalveolar lavage (BAL) inflammatory profiles. We tested this hypothesis in a secondary analysis of a prospective cohort of mechanically ventilated ICU patients with paired BAL microbiome, droplet digital PCR (ddPCR) quantification, and baseline BAL cytokine measurements. We aimed to (1) identify baseline microbial taxa associated with delirium using both compositional and absolute quantification, (2) assess within-patient microbiome dynamics during mechanical ventilation, and (3) compare microbial co-occurrence network structure between groups as a marker of community-level dysbiosis.

Methods

Study design and data source

This study is a secondary analysis of the MicroNAV prospective observational cohort (Bustos et al., 2025; Serrano-Mayorga et al., 2025). The parent study enrolled adults aged ≥18 years who were admitted to the intensive care unit at Clínica Universidad de la Sabana, Chía, Colombia, between January 2020 and July 2022 and were expected to require invasive mechanical ventilation for ≥48 h because of acute non-infectious respiratory failure. Exclusion criteria included pregnancy, prior mechanical ventilation for >24 h, antibiotic use within 48 h before intubation, clinical pulmonary infection at enrollment, chronic lung disease, and COVID-19. The study was approved by the institutional review board (IRB, 20190903, MED-468), and written informed consent was obtained from legally authorized representatives. After quality control, 132 patients with evaluable baseline bronchoalveolar lavage (BAL) data constituted the analytic cohort (Fig. 1).Fig. 1Study flow and analytical framework.

Study flow and analytical framework.

The primary outcome was ICU delirium, recorded as a binary variable indicating its presence or absence during the ICU stay. The specific assessment instrument, evaluation frequency, and timing of delirium onset were not available in the parent dataset.

Sample collection and laboratory measurements

BAL was performed within 12 h of intubation at baseline and repeated at approximately 72 h or day 5, depending on the duration of mechanical ventilation and the timing of follow-up sampling. Using a standardized bronchoscopic procedure, 30 mL of sterile saline was instilled into the right middle lobe. The recovered fluid was immediately aliquoted and stored at −80°C within 1 h of collection.

DNA was extracted from 500 μL of BAL fluid using the DNeasy Blood & Tissue Kit (QIAGEN). Total bacterial DNA burden was quantified using a QX200 droplet digital PCR system (ddPCR; Bio-Rad) targeting the bacterial 16S rRNA gene.

BAL concentrations of interleukin-1β (IL-1β), interleukin-6 (IL-6), and tumor necrosis factor-α (TNF-α) were measured using the MILLIPLEX High Sensitivity Human Cytokine kit (HCYTA-60K; Millipore). Before analysis, 100 μL of BAL fluid was mixed with 100 μL of sputolysin, and the assay was performed according to the manufacturer's instructions, as previously described (Bustos et al., 2025).

16S rRNA gene sequencing and bioinformatic processing

The V4 region of the bacterial 16S rRNA gene was amplified using the 515F/806R primer pair and AccuPrime High-Fidelity Taq polymerase, followed by paired-end sequencing on the Illumina MiSeq platform (2 × 250 bp). For the present secondary analysis, raw reads from BioProject PRJNA1212098 were independently reprocessed using DADA2 v1.38.0 (Callahan et al., 2016). Forward and reverse reads were truncated at 220 and 200 bp, respectively, with maximum expected-error thresholds of 2 and 5. Reads containing ambiguous bases were removed, error models were learned from the data, and paired reads were denoised, merged, and screened for chimeras using the consensus method.

Across the full MicroNAV sequencing dataset, including all specimen types and controls, 1044 of 1045 libraries yielded useable reads. After denoising, paired-read merging, and chimera removal, 13,533 amplicon sequence variants (ASVs) and 21,420,763 reads were retained. Taxonomy was assigned using the SILVA nr99 v138.2 reference database (Quast et al., 2013). Contaminant ASVs were identified using the combined frequency–prevalence method implemented in decontam (Davis et al., 2018), with a threshold of 0.10 and incorporating 38 negative controls and ddPCR-derived DNA concentrations. Mitochondrial and chloroplast sequences were removed, and experimental libraries with fewer than 1300 reads were excluded. ASVs were aggregated at the genus level, and genera present in at least 10% of baseline BAL samples were retained for downstream analyses.

Statistical analysis

The analytical framework comprised following layers, each addressing a distinct question.

Differential abundance screening. Age and Glasgow Coma Scale (GCS) at intubation were selected a priori as covariates for all adjusted analyses. Age is an established delirium risk factor, and GCS was included because baseline consciousness level may influence aspiration risk, lung microbiome composition, and delirium ascertainment (Hosang et al., 2022; Wilson et al., 2020). To identify genera associated with delirium, we applied four methods with complementary assumptions: Wilcoxon rank-sum test on relative abundances, DESeq2 (Love et al., 2014) on raw counts (adjusted for age and GCS), ANCOM-BC2 (Lin and Peddada, 2024) with the same covariates, and MaAsLin3 (Nickols et al., 2026) with total sum scaling and log transformation. A genus was considered concordant if significant in ≥2 methods (Wilcoxon/DESeq2/ANCOM-BC2: FDR < 0.10; MaAsLin3: q < 0.25). This multi-method concordance strategy was used to reduce method-specific findings in small-sample settings, as no single method is uniformly optimal (Nearing et al., 2022).

Functional group analysis. Concordant genera were grouped post hoc into ecological categories based on published evidence of fermentative metabolism, oral/lower-airway ecology, and opportunistic pathogenic potential (see Results). For each group, a centered log-ratio (CLR)-based score was computed as the sum of centered log-ratio–transformed genus abundances (pseudocount 0.5). CLR transformation was used because 16S data are compositional: taxon abundances are mutually constrained by sequencing depth, and CLR maps them into unconstrained space suitable for regression (Gloor et al., 2017). Associations with delirium were tested by logistic regression adjusted for age and GCS. Primary analyses used CLR scores; sensitivity analyses used log-transformed relative abundance scores. To assess whether the association was specific to the data-derived subgroup, an independent a priori oral anaerobe score comprising 13 literature-defined oral genera (Dewhirst et al., 2010) was tested in the same framework. Because compositional data cannot distinguish true depletion from proportional redistribution, absolute abundance was estimated by multiplying each genus's relative abundance by ddPCR-derived total bacterial DNA concentration. Group-level absolute scores were tested in the same logistic model, allowing identification of signals attributable to the compositional sum constraint (Gloor et al., 2017).

Longitudinal microbiome dynamics. Within-patient changes in functional group scores from baseline to follow-up were assessed by paired Wilcoxon signed-rank tests, using both CLR scores (n = 68) and ddPCR-anchored absolute abundance scores (n = 58). To test whether microbiome changes independently predicted delirium beyond baseline composition, logistic regression models were fitted: delirium ∼ Δscore + baseline score + age + GCS, where Δscore represents the within-patient score change from baseline to follow-up.

Exploratory analyses. Baseline associations between functional group scores and log-transformed BAL cytokine concentrations (IL-1β, IL-6, TNF-α) were assessed by linear regression adjusted for age and GCS (n = 70 with available data). To complete the evidence chain, baseline cytokine concentrations were tested for direct association with delirium using logistic regression with the same covariates. Genus-level co-occurrence networks were constructed separately for the delirium and non-delirium groups using SPIEC-EASI with the Meinshausen–Bühlmann method, StARS selection, and 50 subsamples (Kurtz et al., 2015). Differences in network topology were assessed using 100 label permutations.

Sensitivity analyses. The primary SCFA-producing commensal score was re-evaluated under alternative covariate specifications, including additional adjustment for sex, past or current smoking, antibiotic exposure within 24 h, total bacterial burden, and stroke history. Because early ICU death could truncate delirium ascertainment and delirium onset times were unavailable, formal competing-risk analyses were not feasible. We therefore performed three mortality-related sensitivity analyses: restricting the cohort to patients who survived to ICU discharge, reclassifying all non-delirium ICU decedents as delirium, and sequentially excluding ICU deaths before days 2, 4, and 11, corresponding to the quartiles of ICU length of stay among ICU decedents. These analyses assessed robustness to mortality-related truncation rather than constituting formal competing-risk analyses. Models were also refitted after excluding neurosurgical and subarachnoid hemorrhage admissions.

All analyses were performed in R v4.3.1. Unless otherwise specified, two-sided P < 0.05 was considered nominally significant; false-discovery rate correction was applied for genus-level differential-abundance screening as described above.

Functional group associations with delirium

Among the depleted genera, three with documented SCFA-producing capacity — Phocaeicola (acetate and propionate via polysaccharide fermentation (Clausen et al., 2024)), Selenomonas (propionate-producing capacity documented in representative species (Nguyen et al., 2025)), and Fretibacterium (acetate-producing oral anaerobe (Vartoukian et al., 2013)) —were designated SCFA-producing commensals. Four oral anaerobes without primary SCFA function -- Treponema, Leptotrichia, Fusobacterium, and Candidatus Saccharimonas -- were grouped as oral core commensals (Bor et al., 2019; Dewhirst et al., 2010). Three enriched genera with established opportunistic potential -- Fannyhessea, Enterococcus, and Anaerococcus -- were grouped as opportunistic colonizers (Miller et al., 2016; Murphy and Frick, 2013).

CLR-based group scores were independently associated with delirium risk after adjustment for age and GCS (Table 3). The SCFA-producing commensal score was inversely associated with delirium (OR 0.83, 95% CI 0.75–0.92, P < 0.001), the oral core commensal score showed a concordant inverse association (OR 0.86, 95% CI 0.77-0.95, P = 0.004), and the opportunistic colonizer score was positively associated (OR 1.31, 95% CI 1.15-1.50, P < 0.001). Absolute quantification confirmed baseline commensal depletion but not opportunistic expansion

Total bacterial DNA concentration was substantially lower in delirium patients (median 7419 vs 98,326 copies/μL, P = 0.001; n = 125). ddPCR-anchored absolute quantification confirmed absolute depletion of SCFA-producing commensals (OR 0.84, 95% CI 0.75-0.94, P = 0.002), oral core commensals (OR 0.80, 95% CI 0.71-0.91, P < 0.001), and the a priori oral anaerobe score (OR 0.82, 95% CI 0.72-0.93, P = 0.002). Opportunistic colonizers were not enriched in absolute terms (OR 1.10, 95% CI 0.95-1.28, P = 0.187), indicating proportional redistribution within a depleted community rather than genuine expansion.

Longitudinal changes showed limited additional association with delirium

In multivariable models adjusting for baseline score, age, and GCS, within-patient Δscores for SCFA-producing and oral core commensal groups were not independently associated with delirium (all P > 0.3; Fig. 4C–Supplementary Table S2). The absolute Δscore for opportunistic colonizers reached nominal significance in the inverse direction (OR 0.84, 95% CI 0.71–0.99, P = 0.038); however, given the borderline significance, reduced paired-sample size (n = 58), and the group-level expansion documented above, this isolated finding should be interpreted with caution. Overall, the delirium-associated microbiome signal was most evident at intubation and was not substantively augmented by within-patient changes during subsequent mechanical ventilation.

Exploratory BAL cytokine associations and reduced microbial network connectivity

Among 70 patients with baseline BAL cytokine data (Supplementary Table S3), SCFA-producing commensal scores were positively associated with IL-1β (β = 0.25, P = 0.007) and TNF-α (β = 0.13, P = 0.035), while opportunistic colonizer scores were inversely associated with both (β = −0.49 and −0.29, both P < 0.01). Neither IL-1β, IL-6, nor TNF-α was independently associated with delirium (all P > 0.05; Supplementary Table S4). Co-occurrence networks were sparser in the delirium group (34 vs 142 edges, mean degree 0.73 vs 3.05), with topological differences exceeding chance expectation (permutation P < 0.01; Supplementary Table S5).

Sensitivity analyses

The inverse association between the SCFA-producing commensal score and delirium remained robust across all eight covariate specifications (ORs 0.82–0.84, all P ≤ 0.002; Supplementary Table S6), including adjustment for stroke history (OR 0.84, P < 0.001). Mortality-related sensitivity analyses also supported the robustness of the primary association. Restricting the analysis to patients who survived to ICU discharge, including those who subsequently died in hospital (n = 98), yielded a similar estimate (OR 0.84, P = 0.001; Supplementary Table S7). In an extreme-case analysis reclassifying all 31 ICU decedents without recorded delirium as having delirium, the associations for SCFA-producing commensals (OR 0.90, P = 0.011) and all depleted commensals (OR 0.95, P = 0.019) remained significant, whereas the oral-core commensal association was attenuated and no longer significant (OR 0.93, P = 0.102; Supplementary Table S8). Sequential exclusion of ICU deaths occurring before 2, 4, and 11 days of ICU stay did not materially alter the SCFA-producing commensal association (ORs 0.83–0.84, all P < 0.001; Supplementary Table S9). The association was also preserved after excluding neurosurgical and subarachnoid hemorrhage admissions (OR 0.81, P < 0.001; Supplementary Table S10).

Discussion

In this secondary analysis of mechanically ventilated ICU patients without overt pulmonary infection at enrollment, early BAL microbiome composition was associated with ICU delirium. This association was not driven by alpha diversity or by simple pathogen enrichment. Rather, patients classified as having delirium showed depletion of oral-derived anaerobic commensals, including taxa with putative SCFA-producing capacity, a markedly lower total bacterial DNA burden, and a sparser microbial co-occurrence network. Importantly, ddPCR-anchored quantification showed that the apparent enrichment of opportunistic genera in relative-abundance analyses did not represent a parallel increase in baseline absolute abundance. Thus, the dominant microbial pattern was not expansion of a pathogenic consortium, but contraction of a commensal lower-airway community. The available longitudinal analyses further suggested that this microbial state was most evident at baseline, although reduced paired-sample availability limits definitive inference regarding subsequent microbial trajectories.

These observations extend current concepts of delirium pathobiology beyond circulating inflammation alone. Delirium during critical illness is commonly understood as a multiple-hit syndrome in which systemic inflammation, endothelial and blood–brain barrier vulnerability, altered neurotransmission, metabolic stress, and microglial activation converge on susceptible neural networks (Brummel et al., 2024; O'Keeffe et al., 2025). Prior studies linking gut microbiome dysbiosis to delirium have broadened this framework toward host–microbiome interactions (Garcez et al., 2023; Zhang et al., 2023). Our findings suggest that the lower airway may represent an additional, clinically relevant microbial compartment in this vulnerability landscape, particularly in mechanically ventilated patients, whose airway ecology is directly exposed to intubation, aspiration, altered clearance, oxygen shifts, antibiotic pressure, and local immune perturbation (Bustos et al., 2025; Fromentin et al., 2021).

The biological meaning of oral-derived anaerobe depletion in BAL should be interpreted within the ecology of the lower respiratory tract. The lower airway is continuously seeded by oral microbiota through microaspiration, and its composition is shaped by the balance of microbial immigration, clearance, and local growth conditions (Dickson and Huffnagle, 2015). Oral taxa in BAL are therefore not necessarily contaminants, nor are they always markers of infection. In selected contexts, oral-derived taxa have been associated with distinct mucosal immune phenotypes, and oral commensals may contribute to airway immune homeostasis rather than merely indicate aspiration or colonization (Huffnagle et al., 2017; Segal et al., 2016). In this context, the finding that delirium was associated with loss, rather than enrichment, of oral-derived anaerobic commensals suggests a different airway phenotype: not microbial invasion, but depletion of a commensal ecological niche.

The use of absolute quantification strengthens this interpretation. In compositional microbiome data, proportional enrichment of one taxonomic group can arise simply because other organisms have declined. In the present study, opportunistic colonizers were positively associated with delirium in relative or CLR-based analyses, but this signal was not preserved after anchoring to total bacterial DNA. By contrast, putative SCFA-producing commensals, oral core commensals, and an independent a priori oral anaerobe score remained depleted in absolute-abundance models. Thus, the dominant delirium-associated signal was genuine loss of commensal bacterial burden rather than baseline absolute expansion of opportunistic taxa. This distinction has clinical implications. It suggests that airway microbiome signatures related to delirium should not be interpreted exclusively through an infection-centered lens; they may instead reflect loss of mucosal ecological resilience and altered host–microbial immune communication.

The depletion of putative SCFA-producing commensals offers a biologically plausible but provisional mechanistic bridge between lower-airway ecology and acute brain dysfunction. Microbial SCFAs can shape immune signaling and microglial function, and experimental perturbation of the pulmonary microbiome has been shown to alter central nervous system immune reactivity, supporting the biological plausibility of a lung–brain axis (Erny et al., 2015; O'Riordan et al., 2022). However, our study did not measure BAL SCFA concentrations, and genus-level 16S rRNA sequencing cannot establish functional metabolite production. Therefore, this finding should be interpreted as loss of taxa with putative immunometabolic capacity, rather than direct evidence of impaired SCFA signaling. Even with this caution, the preferential depletion of fermentative oral anaerobes is consistent with the hypothesis that loss of commensal airway ecology may weaken local immune calibration and increase susceptibility to neuroimmune stress during critical illness.

The exploratory cytokine, longitudinal, and network analyses offer supportive context for this model without proving it. In the cytokine subset, commensal scores were positively associated with BAL IL-1β and TNF-α, whereas opportunistic-colonizer scores showed inverse associations; yet none of the measured BAL cytokines independently predicted delirium. These findings argue against a simple pulmonary hyperinflammation model. The positive associations of oral-derived commensals with IL-1β and TNF-α may reflect active mucosal immune engagement rather than a uniformly protective immunoregulatory effect, and the cross-sectional data cannot distinguish between these interpretations. Similarly, the available longitudinal data suggest that the delirium-associated microbial signal was strongest at baseline and was not explained by within-patient score changes during mechanical ventilation, although the reduced paired-sample size limits inference regarding later microbial trajectories. The sparser co-occurrence network in patients with delirium provides convergent ecological support for community-level destabilization, but network edges inferred from 16S data cannot be interpreted as direct microbial interactions. Taken together, these secondary analyses support the concept of early airway ecological vulnerability while remaining hypothesis-generating rather than causal.

Several limitations should be acknowledged. This was a secondary observational analysis, and causal inference is not possible. Delirium was recorded as a binary ICU outcome, while the assessment instrument, evaluation frequency, delirium duration, severity, and coma transitions were not available; misclassification is therefore possible. Important ICU exposures, particularly sedative and analgesic dosing, anticholinergic burden, antibiotic spectrum and timing, sleep disruption, spontaneous awakening practices, and organ-support trajectories, could not be fully modeled. Estimates were similar after additional adjustment for sex and smoking history, although the sample size limited more detailed assessment of sex-specific differences and smoking-related effects. Mortality was lower among patients classified as delirious, raising the possibility of survivorship or competing-risk bias despite sensitivity analyses. Cytokine and longitudinal microbiome analyses were limited by reduced sample size, and cytokine profiling was restricted to IL-1β, IL-6, and TNF-α. SCFA-producing capacity was inferred from genus-level taxonomy rather than targeted metabolomics, metagenomics, or direct functional assays. Finally, although negative controls, decontamination procedures, and ddPCR anchoring strengthen confidence in this low-biomass BAL dataset, 16S rRNA sequencing cannot reliably resolve strain-level function, microbial viability, or causal host–microbe interactions.

In conclusion, early lower-airway commensal depletion was associated with subsequent ICU delirium in mechanically ventilated patients, with the strongest signal at intubation rather than during subsequent ventilation. These findings identify the lung microbiome as a plausible and previously underexplored component of delirium vulnerability and support future work integrating quantitative microbiome profiling, airway and systemic metabolomics, and neuroimmune biomarkers to test whether this association is causal and potentially modifiable.
