Application of a new lavage-suction technique in managing chronic periprosthetic joint infection
Authors: Ahmad Alhaskawi, Rongtai Sun, Tiantian Ying, Xiangwei Jiang, Xiaobo Zhu, Fengchao Zhao
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundChronic periprosthetic joint infection (PJI) remains one of the most complex and burdensome complications in orthopaedic surgery. Although two-stage revision arthroplasty is the standard of care, limitations such as persistent biofilm-related infection, prolonged systemic antibiotic use, and elevated reinfection risk remain unresolved. This study investigates the efficacy of a novel lavage-suction (LS) protocol designed to improve interim infection control following first-stage revision for chronic PJI.MethodsIn this single-centre retrospective cohort study conducted between October 2019 and September 2023, 71 patients with confirmed chronic PJI undergoing two-stage revision were included. Patients were assigned to either the LS group (n = 33) or the conventional management (CM) group (n = 38). The LS group received continuous intra-articular lavage beginning on postoperative day one, consisting of 3,000 mL of sterile normal saline mixed with 300 mL of 5% povidone-iodine, administered twice daily for 7 days via a dual inflow-outflow catheter system. This was followed by low-pressure suction drainage (60–80 mmHg) for an additional 7 days. The CM group received standard postoperative care without lavage or suction. Primary endpoints included symptom resolution time, duration of systemic antibiotic therapy, re-debridement rate, inflammatory marker trajectories (CRP, ESR, WBC), interval to reimplantation, and reinfection rates at two-year follow-up.ResultsCompared with the CM group, LS-treated patients showed significantly faster resolution of local symptoms (5.94 ± 1.48 vs. 6.87 ± 1.47 days; p = 0.010), shorter antibiotic duration (44.39 ± 5.45 vs. 64.21 ± 19.57 days; p < 0.001), and a lower rate of re-debridement (9.1% vs. 31.6%; p = 0.043). Postoperative inflammatory markers declined more rapidly in the LS group, with significant differences observed from day 3 through week 12. The average interval to second-stage reimplantation was significantly shorter in the LS group (78 ± 19 vs. 110 ± 22 days; p < 0.001). At two years, no reinfections were observed in the LS group, whereas three cases occurred in the CM group (7.89%).ConclusionThe implementation of a continuous LS protocol using high-volume antiseptic irrigation and low-pressure drainage significantly improves interim infection control following first-stage revision for chronic PJI. This approach reduces systemic antibiotic exposure, lowers surgical burden, accelerates inflammatory resolution, and may reduce the risk of reinfection. Prospective, multicenter trials are warranted to validate these findings and to optimize irrigation parameters and antiseptic protocols.Keywords: Periprosthetic joint infection, Two-stage revision, Intra-articular irrigation, Biofilm eradication, Antibiotic duration, Reinfection prevention.

Introduction

Periprosthetic joint infection (PJI) is one of the most severe and challenging complications following total joint arthroplasty, with a reported incidence ranging from 1% to 2% for primary procedures and higher for revisions [1]. Chronic PJI, characterized by persistent or late-onset infections, is associated with substantial morbidity, high treatment costs, and considerable patient burden, including prolonged hospitalization, immobility, and impaired quality of life [2]. PJI is primarily caused by the infiltration of pathogenic microorganisms, including fungi and bacteria. The diagnosis is typically established through persistent wound drainage or the identification of a sinus tract at the site of the prosthesis, along with a positive microbial culture [3]. Complementary diagnostic methods, such as the analysis of blood biomarkers and synovial fluid, metagenomic sequencing, and imaging studies, further contribute to accurate diagnosis [3, 4]. Eradication of infection often necessitates aggressive surgical management in combination with systemic antimicrobial therapy. The current gold conventional for managing chronic PJI is the two-stage revision protocol [5, 6]. This method involves the removal of the infected prosthesis, extensive debridement of infected and necrotic tissue, placement of an antibiotic-loaded polymethylmethacrylate (PMMA) cement spacer, administration of systemic antibiotics, and delayed reimplantation after infection control is confirmed [7]. Despite being widely accepted, two-stage revision carries limitations, including prolonged treatment duration, risk of reinfection, and complications related to antibiotic toxicity or surgical burden. Reinfection rates have been reported to range from 10% to 20%, especially in cases complicated by resistant organisms or biofilm-forming pathogens [8, 9]. One of the critical challenges during the interval period between ex-implantation and reimplantation is ensuring effective eradication of residual bacteria, particularly those embedded within biofilms on necrotic tissue or adjacent to spacer surfaces [10, 11]. Biofilm-associated bacteria are notoriously difficult to eliminate with systemic antibiotics alone due to their reduced metabolic activity and protective matrix. As such, adjunctive strategies to enhance local infection control are urgently needed [12, 13]. Intra-articular lavage has been explored in recent years as a promising adjunctive method in infection management. Lavage was performed using normal saline, and with ongoing research and advancements in infection control and management, various irrigation/lavage protocols have been developed. These include the use of antibiotics, surfactants, and antiseptics to enhance the effectiveness of infection treatment [14, 15]. They enable mechanical flushing of purulent material, reduce local microorganism load, and may disrupt early biofilm formation [16]. Studies have shown that irrigation using antiseptic solutions such as povidone-iodine or chlorhexidine can further enhance microbial clearance and reduce inflammation [13, 14]. However, data supporting the routine use of lavage in the setting of chronic PJI, particularly after first revision surgery, remain limited and largely anecdotal.

This study introduces a novel lavage-suction (LS) protocol, to the best of our knowledge, not previously reported in the literature, to improve infection control in chronic PJI. By comparing this new protocol with the conventional management (CM) method, we seek to validate its efficacy in enhancing clinical outcomes. We hypothesize that the LS protocol, which involves early postoperative intra-articular lavage followed by low-pressure suction drainage, will result in faster improvements in clinical signs and symptoms, more rapid elimination of biofilms and microorganisms, reduced levels of inflammatory markers, shorter antibiotic treatment durations, fewer debridement surgeries, and a reduced time between revision surgeries. By analyzing these outcomes within a comparative cohort framework, this research aims to provide compelling evidence supporting the potential role of LS strategies in chronic PJI management.

Method

Ethical considerations

This study was designed as a single-center retrospective comparative cohort study conducted at our institution between October 1, 2019, and September 30, 2023. The study protocol was approved by the Institutional Ethics Committee (Approval No: 20191382), and all patients provided informed consent before participation. The research adhered to the principles outlined in the Declaration of Helsinki and complied with other guidelines and regulations for observational research. The primary objective was to evaluate the effectiveness of a novel continuous joint lavage-suction technique following the first-stage revision surgery for chronic PJI.

Inclusion and exclusion criteria

Eligible patients were aged between 18 and 85 years and had chronic infection involving a hip, knee, or shoulder prosthesis, necessitating a two-stage revision. Exclusion criteria included acute PJI (within 4 weeks of the index procedure), systemic autoimmune diseases, ongoing immunosuppressive therapy, a history of failed prior two-stage revision, or inability to comply with follow-up requirements.

Study design and patient management

A total of 71 patients diagnosed with chronic PJI were included, all of whom underwent two-stage revision surgery between October 1, 2019 and September 30, 2023 at our hospital. Diagnosis was established according to the 2018 Musculoskeletal Infection Society (MSIS) criteria. Comprehensive assessments, including patient history, physical examination, and preoperative evaluation, were conducted. The preoperative evaluation involved radiological imaging and laboratory tests. Surgery was performed with careful management of glucose level and blood pressure. Rifampicin and vancomycin were administered as the primary antibiotics, with adjustments made based on microbiological culture (joint fluid and tissue) results. All surgeries were carried out by the same orthopedic surgeon specializing in arthroplasty. Long-term follow-up (two years) data were collected for all patients included in the study.

Patients were assigned to groups based on the postoperative management protocol. The LS group (n = 33) received continuous intra-articular irrigation with iodine-saline solution for 7 days, followed by suction drainage for 7 days. The CM group (n = 38) received standard postoperative care without irrigation or suction.

Surgical intervention and LS protocol

All patients underwent first-stage revision surgery, which involved the complete explantation of the prosthesis, extensive debridement of infected and necrotic tissue, and placement of an antibiotic-loaded polymethylmethacrylate (PMMA) cement spacer. In the LS group, four silicone tubes were inserted during spacer placement; two as inflow lines and two as outflow lines. Continuous intra-articular lavage commenced on postoperative day one using 3000 mL of normal saline mixed with 300 mL of 5% povidone-iodine for 7 days. During this phase, patients remained ambulatory, with the irrigation system secured to allow assisted walking and bed-to-chair transfers under nursing supervision. Following the lavage phase, all tubes were connected to a low-pressure vacuum suction drainage system (60–80 mmHg) for an additional 7 days (Fig. 1). During this suction phase, patients were allowed to ambulate independently within the ward, including using the bathroom, by temporarily clamping the suction tubes during short periods of mobility. The lavage-suction system was monitored daily to ensure flow patency and prevent contamination. During the two-stage revision protocol, all patients initially received empirical intravenous antibiotic therapy, which was subsequently modified according to the results of intraoperative culture and antimicrobial susceptibility testing.

The lavage-suction technique after PJI debridement (first-stage revision surgery)

Intravenous antibiotics were administered for two weeks postoperatively, followed by oral antibiotics, adjusted based on culture sensitivity and individual patient response. Patients were scheduled for outpatient follow-up visits every two weeks to monitor clinical progress and ensure effective infection control. Reimplantation (second revision surgery) was performed only after sustained normalization of inflammatory markers and absence of clinical signs of infection. In the CM group, patients underwent the same first revision surgery and spacer placement, but postoperatively did not receive irrigation or suction therapy. Postoperative care included systemic antibiotics adjusted according to culture results, conventional wound management, and monitoring for signs of persistent infection. Repeat surgical debridement was performed only when clinically indicated in both groups, such as persistent or recurrent infection, and non-healing wounds characterized by fistula formation, persistent drainage, or severe pain.

Outcome measures

Preoperative clinical characteristics and demographic data were collected for all patients, including age, sex, body mass index (BMI, kg/m²), and the affected joint (hip, knee, or shoulder). Following the first revision surgery, outcomes such as resolution of symptoms (swelling, erythema, and discomfort), duration of intravenous and oral antibiotic therapy, need for additional debridement before the second-stage revision, and postoperative complications were recorded. Inflammatory markers, including C-reactive protein (CRP), erythrocyte sedimentation rate (ESR), and white blood cell count (WBC), were retrieved from medical records at standardized postoperative intervals; day 1, day 3, weeks 1, 2, and monthly. Follow-up assessments focused on identifying recurrence of infection and the presence of purulent discharge from the surgical incision.

Types of microorganisms in LS and CM

In the LS group, culture-negative infections were identified in 21 of 33 cases (63.6%), while in the CM group, 29 of 38 cases (76.3%) showed no detectable pathogens. Among culture-positive cases, Staphylococcus aureus and Staphylococcus epidermidis were the most frequently isolated organisms in both groups. Gram-negative bacteria (E. coli, P. aeruginosa, Klebsiella pneumoniae) and filamentous fungi were identified in a minority of cases (Fig. 2).

Discussion

Effective management of chronic PJI remains a critical challenge in orthopedic surgery due to the risk of persistent infection and extended recovery periods. Two-stage revision arthroplasty is the preferred surgical strategy for managing chronic PJI, offering superior infection eradication rates compared to alternative approaches. The procedure involves two distinct stages; the first includes removal of the infected prosthesis, aggressive debridement, and insertion of an antibiotic-loaded cement spacer; the second entails reimplantation of a new prosthesis once infection control is confirmed [17, 18]. In addition, systemic antibiotic therapy, usually pathogen-directed and administered intravenously, is typically continued for 4 to 6 weeks following the first stage. After completing antibiotics, an antibiotic-free interval of 2 to 4 weeks (a “drug holiday”) is often employed to reassess infection status using clinical evaluation, inflammatory markers (CRP, ESR), and joint aspiration [19]. The total interval between stages ranges from 8 to 12 weeks, though it may be extended in patients with ongoing signs of infection. Despite its effectiveness, with infection control rates frequently exceeding 85%, two-stage revision is associated with notable interstage complications. These include reinfection or persistent infection, spacer-related issues (such as dislocation, breakage, or soft tissue irritation), systemic antibiotic toxicity, and delayed or failed progression to reimplantation, which occurs in up to 24% of patients due to medical instability or inadequate infection resolution [6–21].

In this study, we evaluated the outcomes of a new LS protocol, involving early postoperative intra-articular irrigation followed by low-pressure suction drainage after the first stage revision surgery. Notably, the continuous lavage employed in our protocol provides sustained antimicrobial exposure and mechanical flushing, which may help to eradicate slow-growing or metabolically dormant bacterial phenotypes commonly associated with chronic biofilm infections. Furthermore, the gravity-driven infusion of lavage fluid generates a strong hydraulic effect. This hydrostatic pressure, when delivered in a controlled yet forceful stream, produces localized shear stress sufficient to dislodge adherent biofilms and pseudomembranous matrices from metallic implant surfaces and periarticular tissue. Unlike passive or low-volume antiseptic application methods, this high-flow dynamic lavage ensures both chemical and mechanical disruption of bacterial colonies. The resulting force vector and flow velocity exceed what can be achieved by conventional syringe irrigation or surface spraying, thereby enhancing decontamination efficiency [22–24].

Clinical efficacy and symptom resolution

A key finding of this study is the significantly improved clinical recovery trajectory observed in the LS group. Patients treated with the LS protocol experienced more rapid resolution of local symptoms, including joint swelling, erythema, and discomfort, with a mean reduction of nearly one day compared to the CM group. Furthermore, recent evidence suggests that reducing the duration of systemic antibiotic therapy in chronic PJI can be both effective and clinically beneficial. A two-stage exchange arthroplasty using only locally-delivered depot antibiotics without prolonged systemic antibiotic therapy achieved a high infection eradication rate (88–92%) in chronically infected total hip replacements, demonstrating that extensive surgical debridement and localized antibiotic delivery alone can be an effective and cost-saving treatment strategy [25]. In addition, studies have shown that intravenous antibiotic courses shorter than 4 weeks may achieve comparable or improved infection control compared to prolonged regimens, while reducing adverse effects and hospital stays [26, 27]. The LS protocol significantly shortened the duration of systemic antibiotic therapy at the interstage revision period. Patients in the LS group required significantly fewer days of antibiotic therapy compared to the CM group, suggesting that localized antimicrobial activity through mechanical lavage may reduce dependence on systemic therapy. This has critical implications in minimizing adverse effects such as nephrotoxicity and gastrointestinal disturbances, and in lowering the risk of antimicrobial resistance and improving patient compliance [27, 28].

In addition, surgical debridement is a critical component of infection control, aimed at removing necrotic tissue, biofilm-contaminated surfaces, and purulent material from the joint space [29]. However, 10% to 30% of patients may require additional interstage debridement surgeries, often due to persistent clinical signs of infection, elevated inflammatory markers, spacer-related complications, or positive cultures on aspiration. Repeat debridement may also involve spacer exchange, especially if biofilm colonization, mechanical failure, or soft tissue irritation occurs. Studies have shown that multiple interstage procedures are associated with increased morbidity, prolonged treatment timelines, and a higher risk of failure to reach reimplantation [30, 31]. Therefore, early and thorough debridement at the initial stage is critical to minimize the need for further surgical intervention and optimize infection control outcomes [32, 33]. The LS protocol is associated with a significantly lower rate of re-debridement procedures prior to second-stage reimplantation (9.1% vs. 31.6%). This is a particularly noteworthy outcome, as repeat debridement not only increases surgical morbidity and healthcare costs but may also delay reimplantation, compromise soft tissue quality, and increase the risk of secondary infections. The findings suggest that the LS system effectively assists in clearing residual microbial and necrotic material postoperatively, thereby reducing the need for further invasive intervention.

Inflammatory biomarker dynamics and implications for reimplantation

Another major strength of this study lies in its detailed longitudinal analysis of systemic inflammatory markers. In clinical practice, biomarkers such as CRP, ESR, and WBC counts are routinely used to guide decisions regarding the timing of reimplantation and to detect subclinical infection persistence. Previous studies have proposed that early normalization of CRP and ESR correlates with successful eradication of infection and readiness for second-stage reimplantation [34, 35]. In our cohort, patients in the LS group exhibited significantly faster normalization of all three markers. Differences between groups emerged as early as postoperative day 3 for CRP and ESR, and week 2 for WBC, with sustained statistical significance through week 12. This trend is biologically plausible and aligns with the mechanism of the LS system; continuous lavage physically dilutes and removes proinflammatory cytokines, cell debris, and bacterial byproducts from the joint space, while low-pressure suction prevents their reaccumulation. These results not only validate the LS protocol as a means of accelerating infection resolution but also present a practical clinical advantage, including earlier and safer decision-making regarding the timing of second-stage reimplantation. This is critical, as delays in reimplantation beyond 10–12 weeks have been associated with increased risk of reinfection, fibrotic changes in the joint capsule, and compromised implant fixation [36].

Microbiological findings and biofilm considerations

The high percentage of culture-negative PJI observed in both groups (63.6% in LS and 76.3% in CM) is consistent with the literature, especially in chronic or low-grade infections where biofilms impair accurate microbial detection [37]. Despite this diagnostic limitation, the LS group achieved markedly better clinical outcomes, suggesting that the benefits of the LS protocol are not contingent upon pathogen identification or targeted antimicrobial therapy. This observation supports the hypothesis that mechanical lavage and suction may serve as valuable adjuncts in managing biofilm-laden environments, which are notoriously resistant to systemic antibiotics due to their reduced metabolic activity and extracellular polymeric matrix. Furthermore, the inclusion of povidone-iodine in the lavage fluid may have contributed to enhanced microbial clearance. Prior studies have demonstrated the efficacy of antiseptic irrigation in reducing intra-articular bacterial load and preventing biofilm establishment on implant surfaces. Although antiseptic use remains controversial due to concerns over chondrotoxicity or systemic absorption, our results indicate that short-term intra-articular use of diluted povidone-iodine was safe, well tolerated, and effective in this cohort [38].

Long-Term outcomes and reinfection risk

The efficacy of the LS protocol is further highlighted by the improved second-stage revision outcomes. A higher proportion of LS-treated patients successfully proceeded to reimplantation, and the average interval between stages was significantly shorter compared to the CM group. At two years post-second-stage revision, the CM group experienced three cases of reinfection (7.89%), while no reinfections were observed in the LS group. Although the sample size limits definitive statistical conclusions regarding long-term recurrence, the complete absence of reinfection in the LS group is clinically significant and suggests a sustained benefit of enhanced local infection control during the interim phase.

Limitation

Despite its promising findings, this study has several limitations that must be acknowledged. First, the retrospective design may introduce selection bias, although both groups were well-matched in terms of baseline demographics and surgical procedures. Second, the single-center nature of the study may limit generalizability, and the relatively small sample size, particularly for long-term outcomes, necessitates cautious interpretation. Third, while inflammatory markers were used to guide reimplantation timing, the study did not include advanced synovial fluid analysis (e.g., alpha-defensin, leukocyte esterase, or PCR), which could provide more sensitive infection monitoring. Additionally, intra-articular pressure during the lavage phase was not measured, and variations in inflow and outflow dynamics may influence the efficacy of mechanical decontamination. Lastly, the antiseptic concentration of povidone-iodine was not optimized or compared; future studies should explore the impact of different irrigation pressures and antiseptic concentrations on infection clearance and tissue response.

Conclusion

The lavage-suction protocol offers a mechanistically distinct adjunct to standard two-stage revision, targeting the persistent challenges of biofilm and inflammatory burden. Its integration into surgical practice may enhance treatment precision during the prosthesis-free interval. These results provide a rationale for rethinking interim infection control in chronic PJI and lay the groundwork for future protocol-driven trials.
