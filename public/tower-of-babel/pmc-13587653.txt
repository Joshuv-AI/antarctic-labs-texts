To Ask or Not to Ask: Does Inquiring About Participants' Expectations Influence the Placebo Effect? A Placebo Mechanism Study in Healthy Volunteers
Authors: Sigrid Juhl Lunde, Christina Emborg, Simple Futarmal Kothari, Leslie Foldager, Andrew L Geers, Lene Vase
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTBackgroundExpectations are considered a key mechanism in placebo effects, yet the impact of assessing expectations on placebo responding remains unknown. This placebo mechanism study examined whether assessing participants' expectations influenced placebo effect magnitude.MethodsSixty‐four healthy participants were exposed to individually calibrated heat pain during conditioning with verbal suggestions and during two test conditions: application of an inert cream described as analgesic (placebo) and no cream (no‐treatment). Participants were randomised to rate expected pain before stimulation (Group 1: expectations assessed) or not (Group 2: expectations not assessed). All participants rated perceived pain during testing and completed a brief interview on expectations at study end.ResultsOverall, pain intensity and unpleasantness were significantly lower in the placebo than in the no‐treatment condition (both p < 0.001, d = 0.42–0.45). Group 1 showed significant medium‐sized placebo effects for both outcomes (both p < 0.001, d = 0.51–0.57), whereas Group 2 showed small effects (intensity, p = 0.052, d = 0.32; unpleasantness, p = 0.017, d = 0.35). However, no significant between‐group differences were observed in placebo effect magnitude. Expectations significantly predicted placebo effects in Group 1 (p ≤ 0.010). Retrospective reports indicated predominantly positive expectations, with no or negative expectations slightly more prevalent in Group 1.ConclusionsThese findings call for further investigation of the extent to which inquiring about participants' expectations influences the magnitude of placebo effects. They also highlight the importance of bridging placebo mechanism research and clinical trial methodology to better understand how psychological factors influence trial outcomes.Significance StatementThis study addresses an unresolved methodological question in placebo research: whether assessing participants' expectations may itself influence placebo responding. Using an experimental placebo analgesia paradigm, it shows that assessing expectations may, to some extent, influence placebo effect magnitude, although no significant differences were observed between participants assessing expectations and those not assessing expectations. Further research is needed to determine the extent of this influence, with potential methodological implications for the design and interpretation of placebo mechanism studies and clinical trials.

Introduction

Expert consensus points to expectations as a key mechanism in placebo effects (Evers et al. 2018) contributing to their magnitude (Montgomery and Kirsch 1996, 1997). In line with this understanding, many placebo mechanism studies use so‐called expectation interventions—such as verbal suggestions or conditioning—to induce or manipulate participants' expectations (Peerdeman et al. 2016). However, not all studies assess expectations (Lunde et al. 2024), and it remains to be determined whether explicitly assessing participants' expectations after expectation interventions and prior to pain ratings influences placebo effect magnitude.

Focusing on placebo mechanism studies that do assess expectations, a recent meta‐analysis found that participants' expectations contributed moderately to pain intensity, although with considerable heterogeneity across studies (Lunde et al. 2024). Part of this heterogeneity was suggested to relate to methodological differences in how expectations were assessed. Specifically, individual participant data demonstrated that asking about expected pain intensity versus expected pain relief led to different conclusions regarding the contribution of expectations.

Among studies that do not assess expectations, some assume that expectations are induced (Amanzio and Benedetti 1999; Benedetti et al. 2003; Elsenbruch et al. 2012; Roelofs et al. 2000), while others cite the absence of such measures as a limitation (Colloca and Benedetti 2009; Hunter et al. 2014) or explicitly refrain from assessment to avoid demand characteristics (Friehs et al. 2022). To address similar concerns, some studies assess expectations retrospectively at study end, aiming to avoid heightened sensitivity to expectation manipulations or contamination of pain ratings (Geers et al. 2006; Rose et al. 2012).

Despite these various methodological considerations and approaches, it remains unresolved whether asking participants about their expectations can shape or reinforce those very expectations or inadvertently draw attention to the placebo effect, potentially undermining it. Clarifying this issue has important implications for how placebo effects and expectations are empirically investigated.

In this placebo mechanism study, healthy participants were exposed to individually calibrated heat pain during conditioning procedures accompanied by verbal suggestions and during two subsequent test conditions: application of a pharmacologically inactive cream described as analgesic (placebo condition) and no cream applied (no‐treatment condition). All participants rated pain intensity and unpleasantness in each condition. Half of the participants (Group 1) also rated their expected pain intensity and unpleasantness immediately prior to pain stimulation, whereas the other half (Group 2) did not. We hypothesised lower pain ratings in the placebo than in the no‐treatment condition (i.e., a placebo effect) across the full sample and within each group. In addition, although placebo effects are commonly observed in studies assessing expectations (Lunde et al. 2024), empirical evidence on whether expectation assessment itself influences placebo responding is lacking. We therefore formulated a conservative hypothesis that assessing participants' expectations would not significantly influence placebo effect magnitude compared with not assessing expectations.

At study end, all participants completed a brief interview in which they retrospectively reported their expectations, factors contributing to these expectations and their understanding of expectations. These qualitative interview data served an exploratory purpose, providing context and nuance to the quantitative findings.

Materials and Methods

Participants

The study included sixty‐four healthy participants (23 men, 40 women and 1 other), aged 18–62 years (mean = 26.5, standard deviation (SD) = 10.8). Power calculations were informed by two previous experimental studies of placebo analgesia in healthy participants (Colloca et al. 2019; Kube et al. 2020) and indicated that a sample size of 60 participants (n = 30 per group) would provide a power of 80% to detect a placebo effect within each group and for the two groups combined. Calculations were based on a paired t‐test with Bonferroni adjustment for 3 tests, Cohen's d = 0.63 as effect size, and assuming a correlation of 0.7 between the two within‐subject measurements (placebo and no‐treatment). To account for potential data exclusions, 64 participants were enrolled. Inclusion criteria were normal general health, age 18–65 years, and fluent in Danish. Exclusion criteria were chronic pain, other medical or psychiatric conditions, use of antidepressants, daily use of analgesic medication or 24 h prior to the testing, substance abuse and pregnancy. Furthermore, individuals with a background in psychology, medicine or journalism, whether current or former students, were not enrolled to minimise prior exposure to knowledge about placebo.

Participants were recruited through advertisements at and around Aarhus University, via webpages and social media, and the study was conducted at the Department of Psychology, Aarhus University, Denmark. All participants gave written informed consent prior to their participation, and they were informed that they could withdraw from the study at any time without providing a reason. The study was approved by The Central Denmark Region Committees on Health Research Ethics (1‐10‐72‐111‐22), conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki and registered at ClinicalTrials.gov (Identifier: NCT06611267).

Study Design and Randomisation

To examine whether explicitly assessing participants' expectations would affect the magnitude of the placebo effect, participants were randomised to one of two groups (Figure 1): Group 1 (n = 32), in which expectations were assessed, and Group 2 (n = 32), in which expectations were not assessed. Both groups were exposed to experimental heat pain during pain calibration, a conditioning phase and a test phase. The test phase included two conditions, a placebo condition and a no‐treatment condition, presented in randomised order. Randomisation to group allocation and test condition order was performed by an independent researcher using an electronic randomisation program (https://www.random.org/). Furthermore, participants were randomised to receive calibration and conditioning on either the dominant or non‐dominant arm, with the test phase performed on the opposite arm. At the end of the study, all participants completed a brief interview. Thus, the only difference between the groups was whether they were asked to rate their expectations during the test phase.

Study design. Participants in both groups followed identical study procedures and all provided pain intensity and unpleasantness ratings after each pain stimulus. Only participants in Group 1 were asked to rate their expected pain intensity and unpleasantness immediately before the pain stimuli in the two test conditions (in the placebo condition, after application of the cream). The placebo effect was calculated as the difference in pain between the no‐treatment condition and the placebo condition.

Induction of a Placebo Effect

A placebo effect (i.e., the difference in pain ratings between the no‐treatment condition and the placebo condition (Fields and Levine 1984; Kirsch 2013)) was induced by using a combination of conditioning procedures (see Section 2.6.2.) and verbal suggestions (Peerdeman et al. 2016). During the conditioning phase and in the placebo condition of the test phase, a pharmacologically inactive cream (fragrance‐free hand lotion) was applied to the anterior forearm prior to the induction of experimental pain. The cream was dispensed from a tube labelled ‘Analgesic’, and participants were informed that ‘the agent you have just been given is known to significantly reduce pain in some participants’ (Vase et al. 2003). In the no‐treatment condition of the test phase, experimental pain was applied to the anterior forearm without application of the cream and without verbal suggestions regarding pain relief.

Experimental Heat Pain

Experimental heat pain was induced by applying an individually calibrated painful stimulus (see Section 2.6.1.) using a contact thermal stimulator (TCS model 2.1.b, QST.Lab, Strasbourg, France) with a 6.6 cm × 4 cm probe (T11) on the anterior surface of the participant's forearm.

Measures of Pain and Expectations

Pain Intensity and Unpleasantness

Pain intensity and unpleasantness evoked by the experimental heat stimuli were measured using mechanical visual analogue scales (M‐VAS) ranging from 0 to 100 mm. Participants were instructed on how to rate pain intensity and unpleasantness based on standardised written statements (Price et al. 1994), which explained that the pain experience comprises both a sensory (intensity) and an affective (unpleasantness) dimension that may be perceived differently (Price et al. 1987, 1983). Written anchors were used to clarify and distinguish these two dimensions. The M‐VAS for pain intensity was anchored at the left end by ‘no pain’ and at the right end by ‘worst imaginable pain,’ corresponding to scores of 0 and 100, respectively. Similarly, the M‐VAS for unpleasantness was anchored by ‘no unpleasantness’ and ‘worst imaginable unpleasantness,’ corresponding to 0 and 100, respectively (Price et al. 1983).

Expected Pain Intensity and Unpleasantness

Expectations were measured using the same M‐VAS used to assess pain, in line with its common use in placebo analgesia studies (De Pascalis et al. 2002; Locher et al. 2017; Montgomery and Kirsch 1997; Price et al. 1999). Expected pain intensity and unpleasantness were measured only in Group 1, immediately prior to the application of heat stimuli. As planned and described in the study protocol, participants in Group 1 (expectations assessed) were to be asked: ‘How would you rate your expectations regarding the pain?’, while participants in Group 2 (expectations not assessed) were to be asked: ‘Are you ready for the next pain stimulation?’ However, following pilot testing, the phrasing of these questions was slightly revised to sound more natural and contextually appropriate. In the final study, participants in Group 1 (expectations assessed) were asked: ‘Now that the analgesic cream has been applied, what do you expect your pain to be in the next round?’ (placebo condition) and ‘Now that no cream has been applied, what do you expect your pain to be in the next round?’ (no‐treatment condition). In both test conditions, participants in Group 2 (expectations not assessed) were asked: ‘Are you ready for the next round?’

Study Procedure

Participants were seated comfortably and introduced to the study procedures and pain rating scales to ensure familiarisation.

Pain Calibration Phase

First, a pain calibration phase was conducted to determine individually calibrated heat stimuli for use in the subsequent phases. Calibration was performed by applying heat stimuli to the anterior surface of the participant's forearm (see Section 2.4). Starting at 42°C, the temperature was increased in 1°C increments up to a maximum of 48°C (Lunde et al. 2022; Schafer et al. 2015). Each temperature was presented for 15 s, with interstimulus rest periods of 15 s at a baseline temperature of 32°C. Participants were asked to rate their pain using the M‐VAS (0–100) immediately after each heat stimulus. The individually calibrated temperature corresponding to a pain intensity of approximately 60 mm on the M‐VAS was selected for each participant (Jensen et al. 2012). This level ensured that the heat stimuli were perceived as painful but tolerable. The same temperature was used for that participant throughout the remainder of the study.

Conditioning Phase

Following the calibration phase, a conditioning procedure was conducted to induce placebo analgesia in the subsequent test phase (Montgomery and Kirsch 1997; Wrobel et al. 2016). Conditioning involved applying the individually calibrated heat stimuli to the same forearm used during calibration, but at slightly different sites to avoid habituation. Three heat stimuli (15 s each) were first applied without the cream, followed by three stimuli (15 s each) applied after the inactive cream had been administered. Participants were informed that the cream required 2 min to absorb and take effect before testing could proceed. However, when the inactive cream was applied, the temperature was reduced by 2°C without the participant's knowledge (Laverdure‐Dupont et al. 2018). In addition, verbal suggestions were provided, stating that the cream had previously been shown to significantly reduce pain in some participants. Immediately after each heat stimulus, participants rated their pain intensity and unpleasantness on the M‐VAS. These ratings were collected to support the conditioning procedure but were not included in the statistical analyses.

Test Phase

The test phase consisted of two conditions: a placebo condition and a no‐treatment condition. In both conditions, the individually calibrated heat stimuli were applied to the anterior surface of the participant's forearm three times, each lasting 15 s, with 15‐s rest intervals at 32°C between stimuli. The test phase was conducted on the arm opposite to that used during calibration and conditioning (see Section 2.2.), and the position of the thermal probe was slightly repositioned between conditions to avoid habituation. Immediately after each heat stimulus, participants rated their pain intensity and unpleasantness on the M‐VAS. In the placebo condition, the cream was reintroduced along with verbal suggestions of pain relief. As in the conditioning phase, participants were informed that the cream required 2 min to absorb and take effect before testing could proceed. Following the two‐minute waiting period, participants in Group 1 rated their expected pain intensity and unpleasantness on the M‐VAS prior to heat stimulation, while no expectation ratings were obtained from Group 2.

Interview

After the test phase, participants from both groups completed brief semi‐structured interviews. They were asked about their expectations regarding the cream (‘What were your expectations of the cream?’) and the factors that influenced these expectations (‘What influenced your expectations of the cream?’). Finally, participants were asked how they understood the concept of ‘expectations’ in this context (‘How do you understand the concept of “expectations” when asked about your expectations of the cream?’).

Statistical Analysis of Quantitative Data

Statistical analyses were carried out using the software R v. 4.3.0 [R Core Team, 2023] with a significance level of 0.05. Linear mixed effects models (LMEs) were fitted using the function lme from the nlme package v. 3.1–162. The assumption of normality was assessed by residual plots, assisted by Shapiro–Wilk's test. Variance heterogeneity was tested using anova (base package stats) after restricted maximum likelihood (REML) estimation, but variance homogeneity was not rejected.

Means and SDs were calculated for each condition separately, for each group, and for the combined sample. Mean differences between the no‐treatment (NT) and placebo (P) conditions were also calculated, and effect sizes were quantified as Cohen's d for repeated measures with 95% confidence intervals (CIs), using the cohen.d function from the effsize package v. 0.8.1.

For both pain intensity and pain unpleasantness, separate but otherwise identical models were fitted. Each model included Condition (NT, P), Group (1, 2), and their two‐way interaction as fixed effects, and subject (ID) as a random effect. Interaction and main effects were tested with F‐tests using the function joint_tests from the emmeans package v.1.8.5. Four post hoc contrasts with 95% confidence intervals (CIs) were obtained using the glht function from the multcomp package v.1.4–23 and adjusted for multiple comparisons with the default single‐step method (using a multivariate t distribution).

For Group 1 (expectations assessed), the relationship between expectations and the placebo effect was analysed using linear regression, with the observed difference in pain (NT − P) predicted by the corresponding difference in expectations (NT − P). Analyses were conducted separately for pain intensity and pain unpleasantness.

As there is currently no consensus on whether expectations should be modelled as a difference score predicting the placebo effect (i.e., the difference in pain) or as raw scores predicting pain ratings within each condition—a distinction that may also depend on whether analyses are conducted within a single study or across studies in meta‐analyses (see Lunde et al. 2024, including Supplemental Digital Content)—we performed additional post hoc analyses to reflect the latter approach. Here, raw expectation and pain scores were modelled within each condition (placebo, no‐treatment) using LME with an interaction between condition and expectation. This approach allowed estimation of separate slopes for each condition and enabled a direct test of whether associations differed between conditions. For pain unpleasantness, one participant was excluded due to convergence problems caused by an outlier.

Estimated marginal means (EM‐means; least squares means) with standard errors (SEs) and 95% CIs were calculated from the models using the functions emmeans and emtrends in the emmeans package.

Qualitative Data Analysis

All interviews were transcribed verbatim. Responses to the first two questions (expectations of the cream and factors influencing these) were openly coded in NVivo, a qualitative data software, and analysed thematically to identify patterns and recurring themes (Braun and Clarke 2006). For example, codes such as ‘eliminating the pain’, ‘reducing the pain’, ‘reducing unpleasantness’, ‘working effectively’, ‘feeling a difference’ and ‘helping’ were grouped under the theme positive expectations. Four themes were identified (positive expectations, no expectations, scepticism and negative expectations), and their distribution was compared between groups (Group 1: expectations assessed; Group 2: expectations not assessed) to examine whether assessment of expectations was associated with the prevalence of each theme. The same procedure was applied to both interview questions.

Responses to the third question (understanding of the concept) were analysed using a linguistic approach, focusing on the semantic properties of the lexical expressions participants used to describe ‘expectations’ in relation to the perceived effect of the cream (Evans and Green 2006; Fillmore 1982).

Interview data were not collected to achieve data saturation, as the qualitative analyses served an exploratory purpose, providing context and nuance to the quantitative findings.

Expectations as a Predictor of Pain Intensity

Looking at raw expectation and pain intensity scores within each condition in Group 1 (expectations assessed), the association between expected and reported pain intensity differed significantly between the placebo and no‐treatment conditions (interaction: F 1,29 = 4.99, p = 0.033). In the placebo condition, the model explained 80% of the variance (r 2 = 0.80), with an estimated slope of 1.02 (SE = 0.100, 95% CI: 0.82–1.23). In the no‐treatment condition, the model explained 73% of the variance (r 2 = 0.73), with an estimated slope of 0.75 (SE = 0.082, 95% CI: 0.58–0.92). Accordingly, expectations showed a stronger association with reported pain in the placebo condition (Figure S1).

Expectations as a Predictor of Pain Unpleasantness

Looking at raw expectation and pain unpleasantness scores within each condition in Group 1 (expectations assessed), the association between expected and reported pain unpleasantness differed significantly between the placebo and no‐treatment conditions (interaction: F 1,28 = 5.12, p = 0.032). In the placebo condition, the model explained 80% of the variance (r 2 = 0.80), with an estimated slope of 1.08 (SE = 0.097, 95% CI: 0.88–1.28). In the no‐treatment condition, the model explained 83% of the variance (r 2 = 0.83), with an estimated slope of 0.81 (SE = 0.072, 95% CI: 0.66–0.96). Accordingly, expectations showed a stronger association with reported unpleasantness in the placebo condition (Figure S1).

Qualitative Data

Interview data provided insights into participants' post‐test characterisations of their pre‐test expectations regarding the cream as well as their conceptualisations of ‘expectations’ within the study context. Although comparisons between groups are descriptive only and not based on statistical testing, the interview responses revealed noteworthy patterns: Participants in the two groups differed in both the valence of their reported expectations and the factors they identified as shaping them.

Participants' Expectations Regarding the Cream

Analysis of participants' responses during the final interview identified four categories of initial expectations regarding the cream: (1) positive expectations (expecting pain relief), (2) no expectations, (3) scepticism about whether the cream would have any effect and (4) negative expectations (expecting no pain relief). Figure 4 shows the distribution of these expectation categories across the two groups (Group 1: expectations assessed; Group 2: expectations not assessed). Most participants in both groups reported positive expectations, with more in Group 2 (n = 23) than in Group 1 (n = 17). On the contrary, participants in Group 1 were slightly more represented in the ‘no expectations’ and ‘negative expectations’ categories. Among those expressing scepticism, equal numbers in both groups (n = 3 each) suspected that the cream might be a placebo.

Categories of initial expectations by group. At the end of the study, participants retrospectively reported their initial expectations regarding the cream's analgesic effects. Most participants reported positive expectations, although no or negative expectations were slightly more prevalent in Group 1.

Thus, while positive expectations predominated in both groups, participants in Group 2 (expectations not assessed) more often described their initial expectations in positive terms, whereas participants in Group 1 (expectations assessed) more frequently reported either no expectations or negative expectations.

Factors Influencing the Expectations

To further explore participants' reported expectations, they were also asked which factors they believed had influenced their expectations regarding the cream. Across groups (Group 1: expectations assessed; Group 2: expectations not assessed), the most frequently reported factor was the information provided by the experimenter through verbal suggestions and written material (Figure S2). This factor was reported more often in Group 2 (n = 19) than in Group 1 (n = 6). In contrast, prior personal experience was mentioned more often in Group 1 (n = 12) than in Group 2 (n = 9). Observing or hearing about others' experiences with similar treatments was also reported as a contributor to expectations. Finally, 11 participants stated that nothing influenced their expectations. Overall, the two groups differed not only in their reported initial expectations but also in the factors they identified as contributing to them.

Conceptualisation of Expectations

After describing their expectations regarding the cream, participants were asked how they understood the concept of ‘expectations’ in the study context. Across groups, most participants (n = 53) described the concept of expectations as future‐oriented. The greater part of these descriptions were positively valenced (n = 26), typically focusing on beneficial effects of the cream. Neutral descriptions were almost as frequent (n = 23), reflecting on what would happen without assigning the future event a positive or negative quality. A small subset (n = 4) was negatively valenced, focusing on the pain participants expected to experience. Table S4 shows the distribution of valence categories across the two groups. Again, positively valenced descriptions of ‘expectations’ occurred more frequently in Group 2 (expectations not assessed) (n = 16 versus n = 10), and notably, all negatively valenced descriptions (n = 4) were provided by participants in Group 1 (expectations assessed). This pattern mirrors the findings regarding participants' reported expectations of the cream, where positive expectations were also more common in Group 2 than in Group 1.

In addition, participants used various terms to explain how they understood the concept of expectations (Figure S3). Participants typically conceptualised expectations in cognitive‐evaluative terms: Across groups, expectations were most often framed as ‘thoughts’ about what would happen (n = 18). Others described them as ‘assumptions’ about future events (n = 11), ‘ideas of’ what the experience would be like (n = 8), what they ‘reckoned on’ (n = 7), or what they ‘believed’ would happen (n = 7). Regardless of group allocation, participants generally conceptualised expectations as cognitive appraisals of future outcomes rather than affective states.

Taken together, the qualitative findings revealed differences between groups in the valence of reported expectations and in the factors participants identified as shaping them, while participants across groups largely shared a cognitive‐evaluative understanding of the concept of expectations.

Discussion

The present study demonstrated robust placebo effects in both pain intensity and unpleasantness across the full sample (Groups 1 and 2 combined). Group 1 (expectations assessed) showed significant placebo effects in pain intensity and unpleasantness, whereas Group 2 (expectations not assessed) showed a significant placebo effect only in unpleasantness. Placebo effects were numerically larger in Group 1, but no significant between‐group differences in placebo effect magnitude were observed. Expectation ratings in Group 1 significantly predicted the magnitude of the placebo effects and were also significant predictors of pain levels in both the placebo and no‐treatment conditions. Qualitative analyses further revealed subtle but potentially meaningful differences between groups in how participants retrospectively described their expectations and the factors they reported as influencing them.

By combining experimentally induced pain, conditioning, and verbal suggestions in healthy participants, the study applied standard procedures to induce placebo analgesia (Montgomery and Kirsch 1997; Price et al. 1999; Wrobel et al. 2016), and consistent with previous meta‐analyses (Lunde et al. 2024; Peerdeman et al. 2016), small‐to‐medium‐sized placebo effects were observed across the two groups combined. However, a key methodological difference—both from standard placebo paradigms and between the two groups in the present study—was that only one group was asked to rate their expectations throughout the study. Importantly, effect sizes indicated medium placebo effects in the group where expectations were assessed and only small effects in the group where expectations were not assessed (Table 1). These findings suggest that inquiring about participants' expectations may have some influence on the magnitude of placebo effects, contrary to our hypothesis.

Two contrasting psychological perspectives highlight why assessing expectations could potentially influence the magnitude of placebo effects—either increasing or decreasing their magnitude. From a construct accessibility perspective (Higgins 1996, 2012), asking participants to reflect on their expectations may prime these mental representations, increasing their accessibility and, in turn, their influence on subsequent pain evaluations to increase the magnitude of placebo effects. Prior work has shown that prompting participants to reflect on their attitudes or beliefs can activate this knowledge, increasing the likelihood that it will influence later responses (Kallgren and Wood 1986; Snyder and Kendzierski 1982; Snyder and Swann 1976). In contrast, psychological reactance theory (Brehm 1966; Rosenberg and Siegel 2018) suggests that making expectations explicit could elicit resistance if perceived as an attempt to guide or constrain responses. Such perceived threats to autonomy may trigger counterarguing or behavioural opposition (Dillard and Shen 2005; Steindl et al. 2015; Wellman and Geers 2009), and could potentially attenuate placebo effects.

While the present study was not designed to directly investigate the specific mechanisms through which expectation assessment might influence placebo responding, the quantitative findings may appear more consistent with the construct accessibility perspective. Specifically, the numerically larger placebo effects in Group 1 (expectations assessed) are compatible with repeated assessment increasing the salience and influence of participants' expectations. The qualitative findings, however, provide important nuance. Participants in Group 2 (expectations not assessed) were slightly more likely to report initially positive expectations about the cream's effect in retrospect (Figure 4). Moreover, participants in Group 2 characterised the concept of expectations solely in positively or neutrally valenced terms, whereas participants in Group 1 described them in positively, neutrally and negatively valenced terms (Table S4). This pattern could be interpreted in line with a reactance perspective but may also suggest that repeated prompting to reflect on expectations during testing encouraged a more nuanced engagement with the concept of expectations, resulting in more heterogeneous verbalisations.

Another possible interpretation of the quantitative findings is that the numerically larger placebo effects and effect sizes in Group 1 (expectations assessed) reflected demand characteristics (Friehs et al. 2022). However, the qualitative data on factors influencing participants' expectations do not fully support this account. If the results were due to demand characteristics, participants in Group 1 would have been more likely to attribute their expectations to the influence of the experimenter and to other study‐specific factors, which was not observed (Figure S2). Instead, participants in Group 2 (expectations not assessed) most frequently cited such factors—specifically verbal suggestions and written material—whereas Group 1 more often referred to prior personal experiences. A possible explanation for this finding might be that the continuous assessment of expectations can have reduced the relative salience of external suggestions presented by study staff, and instead steered the participants' attention towards their own thought processes and experiences.

It is important to note that the present findings—like much of the empirical evidence underpinning psychological theory (Henrich et al. 2010; Sears 1986)—are based on healthy individuals. However, it remains unclear to what extent the psychological and neurobiological processes underlying placebo effects operate similarly in healthy participants and patients (Colloca et al. 2020; Forsberg et al. 2017; Kaptchuk et al. 2020; Skyt et al. 2020). A study in patients undergoing chemotherapy demonstrated that assessing expectations for side effects did not influence the occurrence of side effects (Colagiuri et al. 2013), which could argue against a construct accessibility perspective. Furthermore, expectations in clinical populations may be more closely intertwined with affective dimensions such as hope and desire (Kaptchuk et al. 2009; Vase et al. 2003), whereas participants in the present study predominantly described expectations in cognitive‐evaluative terms, as thoughts or assumptions about what would happen. These considerations suggest that the present findings should not be assumed to generalise directly to clinical populations or clinical contexts. Nevertheless, asking about and managing patients' expectations may be important for optimising clinical outcomes and minimising adverse effects (Laferton et al. 2025). Extending this perspective, if inquiring about expectations—and thereby increasing their salience—enhances treatment outcomes, this could have significant implications for clinical practice.

The question of measuring expectations has also been raised in the context of clinical trials, with some proposing that assessing and accounting for patients' expectations could yield more precise estimates of drug and placebo effects (Frisaldi et al. 2017; Langford et al. 2023; Lunde et al. 2025). At the same time, methodological concerns could be raised that focusing too strongly on placebo mechanisms might influence how participants perceive the trial. Importantly, the qualitative findings from the present study indicated that similar proportions of participants in both groups suspected that the cream might be a placebo. This observation suggests that expectation assessment did not heighten suspicion. However, drawing methodological implications for clinical trials would be premature. Replication in experimental settings and, critically, in patient populations is needed. Such efforts could inform the design and conduct of future trials by clarifying the impact of what is communicated and assessed.

In future studies, addressing this issue with greater certainty will largely depend on sample size. The present study represents an initial step towards examining whether assessing expectations influences placebo effects. Importantly, the study was powered to detect placebo effects across and within each group, but not to detect between‐group differences in placebo effect magnitude or to formally test equivalence between groups. The absence of a statistically significant interaction should therefore not be interpreted as evidence that assessing expectations has no influence on placebo responding. Instead, the present findings indicate that no statistically significant between‐group differences were detected within the current sample and study design. The observed effect sizes suggest that assessing expectations may, to some extent, influence placebo effect magnitude. Yet, post hoc estimates based on the present data indicate that substantially larger samples would be required to detect between‐group differences (approximately 75 participants per group), and even larger samples to test equivalence (approximately 1250 participants per group, see Data S1, Supporting Information, section ‘Post hoc simulation‐based estimation of sample sizes’ for details).

These considerations highlight several important limitations that should be taken into account when interpreting the study findings. First, although no statistically significant between‐group differences were detected, the observed effect sizes and the interaction p‐value for pain intensity (p = 0.059) suggest that potentially meaningful differences cannot be ruled out. Consistent with the sample size estimates presented above, a post hoc sensitivity analysis by two‐sample t‐tests indicated that, given the achieved sample size, a Cohen's d of 0.72 was the minimum interaction effect size detectable with 80% power at a 5% significance level. Smaller interaction effects may therefore have remained undetected. Second, the conceptualisation of the placebo effect in the present study requires consideration of the distinction between placebo response and placebo effect. A placebo response encompasses the overall change observed following placebo treatment, including natural history and regression to the mean, whereas a placebo effect reflects the change specifically attributable to placebo mechanisms and therefore requires comparison with a no‐treatment condition (Benedetti and Shaibani 2026; Evers et al. 2018). In the present study, the randomised within‐subject comparison between the placebo and no‐treatment conditions is consistent with established approaches in placebo mechanism research, in which placebo effects are examined by comparing placebo and control conditions (Lyby et al. 2010; Price et al. 1999; Vambheim et al. 2021; Wrobel et al. 2016). Within this design, both conditions were embedded within the same experimental context involving conditioning, repeated stimulation, manipulation of expectations, and, in one group, repeated assessment of expectations. This raises the broader question of the extent to which the no‐treatment condition adequately reflects the natural history of pain responding and thus allows placebo‐specific mechanisms to be fully disentangled from confounding factors such as habituation and demand characteristics. Third, although the present study addressed a methodological question within placebo mechanism research, it was not designed to directly investigate the specific mechanisms through which expectation assessment might influence placebo responding. Because expectations were not assessed prior to conditioning, it was not possible to determine whether expectation assessment itself influenced participants' expectations or whether the groups differed in their initial expectations. Assessing expectations in both groups prior to conditioning would have enabled evaluation of baseline group differences and changes in expectations over time, but would also have precluded maintaining a group that had not been asked about expectations. Although the retrospective qualitative interviews provided some insight into participants' reported initial expectations regarding the cream, these data cannot establish whether expectation assessment altered expectations during the experimental procedures or influenced how participants later recalled and described their expectations. Future studies should carefully consider these methodological trade‐offs and further explore potential mechanisms and mediating factors, including how expectation assessment may influence expectations over time and interact with conditioning and learning processes.

Taken together, this study should be regarded as an initial exploratory examination of a largely unexplored methodological question rather than a definitive test of whether assessing expectations influences placebo responding. Results showed no significant between‐group differences in placebo effect magnitude. However, the observed effect sizes and qualitative findings suggest that assessing expectations in an experimental setting may have some influence on both the magnitude of placebo effects and how participants engage with the concept of expectations. The nature and implications of this potential influence, and whether similar processes extend to patient populations and clinical contexts, warrant further systematic investigation in large‐scale studies and coordinated research efforts.
