Association between gut microbiota composition and physical functioning in patients with knee osteoarthritis: a machine learning study
Authors: Alessandro de Sire, Elettra Mancuso, Nicola Marotta, Mattia Massimino, Roberta Zito, Carolina Averta, Isabella Bartalotta, Angela Palummo, Annamaria Cerantonio, Luigi Citrigno, Matteo Soverini, Andrea Castagnetti, Gaia Chiara Mannino, Antonio Ammendolia, Francesco Andreozzi
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Gut dysbiosis and systemic inflammation are increasingly recognized in the pathogenesis of knee osteoarthritis (OA). While altered gut microbiota profiles have been reported, the link between specific microbial taxa and physical functioning in OA patients remains understudied. This study investigated the association between gut microbiota composition and clinical/functional outcomes in mild to moderate knee OA. We analysed faecal samples and clinical data from 18 OA patients and 20 age-matched controls, and we used a machine learning approach to assay associations with functional tests. OA patients showed altered gut microbiota diversity. Machine learning identified Blautia luti, Collinsella aerofaciens, and Butyricicoccus faecihominis as discriminating taxa. Correlation analysis revealed distinct associations between genera of Bacteroidetes and Firmicutes with clinical and functional parameters. Species-level analysis identified species like as F. prausnitzii and Blautia luti as relevant for physical performance and pain. For the first time, this study provides novel evidence linking specific gut bacterial taxa to physical functioning and OA symptoms in mild to moderate knee OA. These findings highlight the potential role of the gut-joint axis and warrant further research into underlying mechanisms and microbiota-targeted interventions for OA management.

Introduction

Knee osteoarthritis, or osteoarthritis (OA), is a chronic degenerative disease caused by multiple factors that may lead to disability1. It affects about 3.3 to 3.6% of the global population and results in moderate to severe disability in 43 million people, making it the 11th most debilitating disease worldwide2.

Although traditionally viewed as a purely degenerative condition, recent evidence has highlighted a significant chronic inflammatory component in OA3. This supports the idea that OA presents with a wide range of symptoms, with systemic and local inflammation playing crucial roles in the disease’s development and progression4. However, the clinical diagnosis of OA still relies heavily on symptomatic presentations (e.g., pain, swelling, stiffness) and confirmatory radiographic findings5.

The precise OA etiology is still unidentified, but several risk factors, including age, estrogens, obesity, metabolic syndrome, genetics, muscle weakness, and joint injury, contribute to the development of this condition6. A growing body of evidence points to a link between the gut microbiota and the onset of inflammatory states7, and dysbiosis is considered a potential risk factor for OA. Specific alterations in the microbiota have been observed in OA patients, such as an increase in Actinobacteria and Proteobacteria and a decrease in Firmicutes, which fuel low-grade inflammation, a key factor in OA pathogenesis8. The hypothesis linking gut dysbiosis to the development of osteoarthritis is based on a series of interconnected factors: low-grade intestinal inflammation, elevated lipopolysaccharide (LPS) levels, metabolic endotoxemia, meta-inflammation, and metabolic syndrome9,10.

Research has demonstrated a significant correlation between elevated levels of inflammatory biomarkers in the blood, including LPS, and the severity of OA11. This suggests that metabolites produced by the gut microbiome may act as pathogenic mediators, contributing to the development of inflammatory musculoskeletal disorders12. For instance, an animal study showed that mice fed a high-fat, high-sugar diet for 28 weeks developed obesity and increased cartilage damage, accompanied by significant increases in Lactobacillus and Methanobrevibacter in their gut microbiota12. Supporting this, Ulici et al. found reduced post-traumatic OA severity in germ-free mice, further indicating a causal role of the gut-bone axis in musculoskeletal pathogenesis13.

The association between dysbiosis and arthritis is further strengthened by the presence of microbial DNA in articular cartilage and the identification of specific fecal microbiome changes in OA patients, such as reduced bacterial diversity and disease-associated clusters14. In humans, a similar pathogenic process occurs when gut microbiome dysbiosis compromises the integrity of the epithelial barrier, promoting the translocation of bacteria and their metabolites into the bloodstream8,15,16. This phenomenon, known as “leaky gut” or intestinal permeability syndrome, is exacerbated by stress and pain associated with metabolic syndrome and OA. Notably, stress and pain can modulate the composition of the gut microbiota through the release of neurotransmitters, further contributing to increased intestinal permeability. This vicious cycle can fuel systemic inflammation and contribute to OA progression16. While significant insights have emerged regarding the connection between the gut microbiota and OA, important gaps persist in our understanding, especially concerning OA. Currently, there is a lack of observational studies on gut microbiota alterations in humans with OA, particularly regarding the relationship between gut dysbiosis and physical functioning.

The hypothesis of our study was built on prior evidence linking gut dysbiosis with systemic low-grade inflammation, a recognized driver of OA pathogenesis7,11. Pre-clinical studies have shown that germ-free mice or those with altered gut microbiota exhibit reduced cartilage degeneration and inflammation12, and human studies have reported distinct microbiota alterations in OA patients compared to healthy individuals7,13. Since systemic inflammation and metabolic endotoxemia are also known to negatively influence muscle strength, mobility, and functional performance, we hypothesized that gut microbiota composition could be associated not only with OA pathophysiology but also with clinical and functional outcomes in these patients.

Therefore, in the present study, by using a statistical and scalar machine learning approach based on Random Forest17, we aimed to evaluate the correlation between the phylogenetic gut microbiota profile components (focusing on genera of the Firmicutes and Bacteroidetes phyla) with physical functioning in patients with mild to moderate knee OA.

Materials and methods

Population study

Participants were recruited from the Physical and Rehabilitation Medicine Unit at the “Renato Dulbecco” University Hospital in Catanzaro, Italy. Inclusion criteria were: (1) individuals aged 50 to 85 years; and (2) a diagnosis of mild to moderate knee OA, defined as Grade II or III on the Kellgren and Lawrence radiographic classification. Exclusion criteria were: (1) knee ligament injuries; (2) knee arthroplasty; (3) prior knee surgery; (4) tibial and/or fibular fractures; (5) intra-articular corticosteroid or hyaluronic acid therapy within the past year; (6) antibiotic therapy within the past six months; (7) genetic predispositions to chronic inflammatory diseases (e.g., ulcerative colitis, Crohn’s disease); and (8) chronic gastric pathologies requiring treatment and dietary modification. All the individuals were assessed in terms of gut microbiota and biochemical determinations at the Internal Medicine Unit of the “Renato Dulbecco” University Hospital in Catanzaro, Italy. The collected samples were labelled with progressive numbering and without any reference to the patients’ sensitive data; the first and last names of the patients were expressed only by initials to ensure privacy protection.

The study was conducted in accordance with the principles of the Declaration of Helsinki. Written informed consent was obtained from all participants. The study was approved by the Institutional Ethics Committee of the Calabria Region (approval code: 428/21; approval date: 16/12/2021).

Functioning assessment

Western Ontario and Mcmaster Universities Osteoarthritis Index

The Western Ontario and McMaster Universities Osteoarthritis Index (WOMAC) is a self-administered, disease-specific questionnaire comprising 24 items across three dimensions: pain (5 questions), stiffness (2 questions), and physical function (17 questions). A five-point Likert scale (0 = none to 4 = extreme) was used to assess the intensity of each item. Scores for each dimension at the latest follow-up were calculated by summing the item responses and then standardized to a range of 0 (worst condition) to 100 (best condition)17.

Pain visual analogue scale

The visual analogue scale (VAS) is a self-reported scale consisting of a horizontal or vertical line, usually 10 cm long (100 mm) anchored at the extremes by 2 verbal descriptors referring to the pain status18. An introductory question (with or without a time recall period) asks the patient to tick the line on the point that best refers to his or her pain. The introductory question, the recall period, and the content of the external verbal descriptors vary in the literature18.

Short physical performance battery

The short physical performance battery (SPPB) assesses lower extremity function in older adults through three tests performed in a specific order: balance, 4-m gait speed (repeated twice), and a repeated (5 times) chair stand without using arms. Standardized instructions and demonstrations are provided for each test. The balance test includes side-by-side, semi-tandem, and tandem stances held for 10 s each, with a total score ranging from 0 (no balance) to 4 (no impairment)19. The 4-m gait speed test records the fastest of two usual-paced walks, with scores from 0 (unable) to 4 (fastest time indicating no impairment). The repeated chair stand test measures the time to complete five stands from an armless chair; scores range from 0 (unable or > 60 s) to 4 (fastest time indicating no impairment). The sum of the three test scores yields a total SPPB score (0–12), where lower scores indicate poorer lower body function and scores below 10 suggest mobility limitations. The SPPB is a standardized instrument to measure lower extremity function in populations, including those with chronic low back pain19.

Timed up and go test

Timed Up and Go (TUG) test was administered in both Study 1 and 2 by instructing participant to stand up from an armchair, walk 3 m, turn, walk back to the chair, and sit down20. Participants were told to use a comfortable and safe walking speed, and used regular footwear and customary walking aids. For safety, a research team member maintained close proximity to the participant without interfering with the assessment. After a practice trial, the mean of 2 actual trials was recorded.

Berg balance scale

Berg balance scale (BBS) employed to evaluate the balance and fall risk in elderly individuals. It consists of 14 tasks, each rated on a scale of 0 to 4 points, resulting in a total score of 56 points21.

Mini-nutritional assessment score

The Mini-Nutritional Assessment (MNA) is a quick (< 10 min) test to assess nutritional status in older adults. It includes anthropometric measurements, a global assessment, and a dietary/subjective assessment. Total scores categorize individuals as having no malnutrition (> 23.5), risk of malnutrition (17–23.5), or malnutrition (< 17)22. The full MNA was administered to all participants, even those with high scores on the MNA-short form23.

Fecal samples and DNA extraction

Fecal samples were collected in sterile containers and, within four hours, transferred using the OMNIgene GUT collection kit (Voden, Meda, Italy) and stored at − 20 °C. The fecal samples were then thawed for DNA extraction. Bacterial DNA was extracted using the QIAamp DNA Stool Mini Kit (Qiagen, Hilden, Germany) following the manufacturer’s instructions, then DNA purity and concentration were assessed using a Qubit Fluorometer (Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA). The V3-V4 region of the 16S rRNA gene was amplified by PCR (Thermal Cycler: Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA) using universal primers. The amplified products were pooled and purified using Agencourt AMPure XP magnetic beads (Beckman Coulter, USA).

Bioinformatics and biostatistics analyses

The study included 18 patients diagnosed with gonarthrosis and 20 age-matched healthy controls [EU FP7 NU-AGE project PRJNA661289]. Microbiota composition was assessed using 16S rRNA sequencing, with raw sequencing reads subjected to quality filtering, denoising, and the generation of amplicon sequence variants (ASVs) using the Qiime2 platform24 and DADA225. The obtained ASVs were assigned using an internally curated, high-precision taxonomic reference database based on both SILVA26 and Greengenes27. Downstream analyses included alpha diversity estimation (Observed Species, Shannon Index) and beta diversity assessment using Bray–Curtis dissimilarity, followed by principal coordinate analysis (PCoA). Group differences were statistically evaluated using permutational multivariate analysis of variance (PERMANOVA, Adonis test, p-value threshold). In addition to microbiota data, clinical variables such as comorbidities were used in the modelling process to assess their influence on predictive performance. A machine learning-based feature selection approach was employed, using an iterative random forest model trained and validated on the dataset [https://sourceforge.net/projects/vpr1-0/]. The model was built in a stepwise manner, initially including only microbial features and then integrating clinical metadata to determine their contribution to classification accuracy. To evaluate the associations between the relative abundances of bacterial species and clinical parameters, Spearman’s rank correlation coefficient was used. The clinical variables analysed included MNA score, WOMAC pain score, VAS pain score, TUG test, and SPPB balance score. Microbiome abundance data were derived from species-level taxonomic classification, and only taxa detected in at least one sample were considered. Correlations were computed individually for each species-parameter pair without adjusting for covariates, as Spearman’s correlation is a non-parametric measure that captures monotonic relationships and is robust to non-linearity and outliers28. P-values were adjusted for multiple testing using the Benjamini–Hochberg false discovery rate (FDR) correction to control for false positives29. The results were visualized using a volcano plot, where significant associations (FDR-adjusted p < 0.05) were color-coded based on the associated clinical parameter, and point sizes were scaled according to the mean relative abundance of each species in samples. Data processing and statistical analyses were conducted in R (v4.2.0) using the tidy verse, ggplot2, and grepel packages.

Results

Microbiome profiling

The gut microbiota of 18 patients (6 male and 12 female) with OA (whose anthropometrics and functional test results are reported in Table 1) was compared to that of 20 age-matched healthy controls [EU FP7 NU-AGE project PRJNA661289]. The observed trends in alpha diversity reduction (Fig. 1) and the distinct microbial clustering in beta diversity (Fig. 2), as revealed by Multidimensional Scaling (MDS) and confirmed by PERMANOVA (p < 0.001) analysis, are consistent with existing scientific literature that connects microbiota alterations to chronic inflammatory states30,31. Figure 3 displays a heatmap visualising the relative abundance of the main bacterial genera identified. The data were logarithmically transformed (Log10 scale) to highlight less abundant taxa. Fisher’s exact test on the clustering showed a statistically significant separation between the two groups (p < 0.001). The application of machine learning identified Blautia luti (p = 0.0003), Longibaculum muris (p = 3.6 e−05), Collinsella aerofaciens (p = 6.1 e−06), and Butyricicoccus faecihominis (p < 0.0008) as taxa that distinguished OA patients from controls (Fig. 4). Within the Bacteroidetes phylum, a significant positive correlation was found between both Prevotella and Odoribacter abundance and the WOMAC score (p < 0.05), while Phocaeicola abundance showed a significant negative correlation with WOMAC (p < 0.01). Additionally, a negative correlation was observed between Odoribacter and Paraprevotella abundance (p < 0.05). Finally, Odoribacter abundance was negatively associated with the BB score and positively correlated with the TUG test (p < 0.05). In contrast to the Bacteroidetes phylum, analysis of genera within the Firmicutes phylum revealed associations with a greater number of clinical parameters. The abundance of Lachnoclostridium and Kineothrix was negatively associated with the WOMAC score (p < 0.01 and p < 0.05, respectively), suggesting that lower levels of these genera might correlate with more severe OA symptoms. Conversely, Mediterraneibacter and Pseudoflavonifractor abundance showed a positive association with the WOMAC score (p < 0.05). An inverse relationship was observed between the SPPB score and the abundance of Dorea, Oscillibacter, Christensenella, Aminipila, Phascolarctobacterium, and Dialister genera (p < 0.05). Only a high abundance of the Phocaeicola genus was positively associated with the SPPB score (p < 0.05). Regarding pain, as measured by VAS, positive associations were found with Succiniclastium and Holdemania (p < 0.05). In contrast, negative associations with VAS were observed for Anaerobutyricum and Lacrimispora (p < 0.05). The TUG test, which assesses functional mobility, was positively associated with Anaerotruncus and Breznakia (p < 0.05), but negatively with Faecalibacterium and Lactobacillus (p < 0.05). Anaerotruncus and Oscillibacter abundance were negatively associated with the BB score, indicating that patients with lower levels of these genera tended to report lower pain grades. Regarding the MNA score, Lachnoclostridium abundance showed a positive association with this nutritional assessment test (p < 0.05).

Subsequently, we applied a machine learning method to evaluate the associations identified at the bacterial species level. Microbiota abundance data, derived from species-level taxonomic classification, included only taxa detected in at least one sample. Correlations were calculated individually for each species-parameter pair without adjusting for covariates, leveraging the non-parametric nature of Spearman’s correlation, which is robust to non-linearity and outliers. P-values were corrected for multiple testing using the Benjamini–Hochberg false discovery rate (FDR) to control for false positives. The results are visualised in a volcano plot (Fig. 7), where significant associations (FDR-adjusted p < 0.05) are colour-coded based on the associated clinical parameter, and point sizes are scaled according to the mean relative abundance of each species across the samples. An inverse association was observed between WOMAC and Anaerotruncus, Anaeromassilibacillus, Oscillibacter, Lactococcus, Selenomonas, Megamonas, Caloranaerobacter, and Pelotomaculum (p < 0.05). The VAS score showed negative associations with Anaerobutyricum, Lacrimispora, and Clostridium (p < 0.05), and positive associations with Selenomonas and Cryptaerobacter (p < 0.05). The TUG test was positively associated with Anaerotruncus and Pseudoflavonifractor but negatively with Faecalibacterium (p < 0.05).

The abundance of several species, including Prevotella copri, Cronobacter muytjensii, Faecalibacterium prausnitzii, Blautia luti, and Bacteroides stercoris, was correlated with the SPPB score (FDR-adjusted p < 0.05). Conversely, a significant negative correlation was observed between the abundance of Faecalibacterium prausnitzii (a member of the phylum Firmicutes) and the VAS parameter. The VAS parameter was also negatively associated with Bacteroides mediterraneensis and Blautia luti. Kineothrix alysoides, Paraprevotella clara, and Anaerobutyricum hallii were inversely correlated with the clinical nutritional parameter, MNA. The results of the WOMAC test were positively associated with the species Phocaeicola plebeius. Finally, assessment of functional mobility using the TUG test revealed a positive association with Roseburia faecis, an anaerobic, Gram-negative or Gram-variable microorganism.

Discussion

Over the past few years, an increasing number of studies have investigated the intestinal microbiome and the potential involvement of the cartilage-gut-microbiome axis in OA pathogenesis16. This research has established a connection between OA and the intestinal microbiota, suggesting a link between joint disorders and metabolites derived from the gut15,15,16.

Recent research highlights the critical role of post-microbial metabolites, such as short-chain fatty acids (SCFAs) and lipopolysaccharide (LPS), in mediating the "gut-joint axis" and influencing the pathogenesis of osteoarthritis (OA)12,32,33. SCFAs can mitigate joint degeneration both directly and indirectly34. These metabolites are known to maintain the gut mucosal barrier, which in turn reduces systemic inflammation. Additionally, they have direct protective effects on key joint tissues. For example, SCFAs have been linked to preserving cartilage integrity by reducing inflammation and matrix degradation34. Butyrate, a specific SCFA, shows particular promise in protecting OA cartilage. Studies demonstrate its ability to directly reduce inflammation in chondrocytes by inhibiting key signalling pathways and decreasing inflammatory mediators35,36. Butyrate also helps preserve cartilage integrity by suppressing matrix-degrading enzymes (MMPs), promoting the expression of Type II collagen, and mitigating oxidative damage. Beyond cartilage, SCFAs also regulate the synovium in OA; propionate, another SCFA, has been shown to induce senescence in synovial fibroblasts, alleviating inflammatory arthritis37. Conversely, the gut microbiota’s involvement in OA is also linked to elevated levels of the pro-inflammatory bacterial metabolite, LPS. Since LPS is associated with obesity and metabolic syndrome—both major OA risk factors—it’s speculated that LPS-induced low-grade inflammation, metabolic endotoxemia, and macrophage activation contribute to joint damage32. Research by Huang et al. supports this, finding that increased levels of LPS and LPS-binding protein (LBP) are directly associated with knee osteophyte severity and a higher number of activated macrophages in the synovium12. The authors suggest that monitoring circulating LPS could even serve as a new method to diagnose and treat specific OA phenotypes32. This growing body of evidence underscores the crucial link between both beneficial and detrimental microbial metabolites and the overall health of the joint.

However, the relationship between the intestinal microbiota and clinical tests of physical functionality remains unexplored.

First, we explored differences in the gut microbial composition between people with OA disease and healthy subjects. Consistent with previous studies, we noted a decrease in richness assessed by α-diversity using two complementary approaches: observed species analysis, a more quantitative method that directly counts the number of unique taxa present, and Shannon diversity, which provides a measure of both the richness (number of species) and evenness (relative abundance) of each bacterial species within the community. This dual approach allowed for a more comprehensive assessment of the microbial diversity in our samples.

Likewise, the results expressed in terms of β-diversity support the evidence that OA induces a more pronounced alteration in the gut microbiome composition, indicating a significant dissimilarity in bacterial species composition between the two groups, which bolsters the hypothesis of gut dysbiosis in this population. These results are consistent with a substantial body of scientific literature linking gut microbiota alterations to chronic inflammatory states29,30.

The analysis of taxonomic abundances at the genus level appears to confirm the established roles of certain species in inflammation-related pathogenic mechanisms. For instance, Butyricicoccus, a butyrate-producing genus that is reduced in individuals with advanced chronic kidney disease38, was also less abundant in our OA subjects compared to controls. Similarly, the gut commensal Parabacteroides distasonis, known to alleviate inflammatory arthritis39, at the level of genus was reduced in our OA groups. Longibaculum, a gut microbe linked to glycemic control and intestinal inflammation40, also warrants consideration. Interestingly, our study observed a reduced abundance of Collinsella in individuals with OA, in contrast to the literature describing Collinsella as a pathobiont often elevated in metabolic disorders type 2 diabetes, autoimmune polyendocrine syndrome type 1, and psoriatic arthritis41–44, and its role in exacerbating arthritis in animal models via increased gut permeability and pro-inflammatory cytokines45.

The application of machine learning identified Blautia luti, Collinsella aerofaciens, and Butyricicoccus faecihominis as taxa that distinguished OA patients from controls. While this observations support the notion that specific microbial features may be associated with the disease, the limited sample size suggests that these findings should be validated in independent cohorts. Additionally, as this is an observational study, it remains unclear whether these microbial differences contribute to gonarthrosis pathophysiology or arise as a consequence of the disease process. Among the taxa with significantly lower relative abundances in OA patients, Blautia luti has been associated with anti-inflammatory properties, and its depletion has been reported in other inflammatory disorders46,47. If confirmed in larger studies, it might be assumed that a reduction in Blautia luti could contribute to an altered gut microbial environment that promotes systemic inflammation. A decrease in Collinsella aerofaciens was also observed, but its role in inflammation remains unclear. While some studies suggest that C. aerofaciens may support gut homeostasis30, others indicate that it could increase intestinal permeability and promote systemic inflammation48. The functional implications of its reduction in gonarthrosis, therefore, remain uncertain and require further investigation. A lower abundance of Butyricicoccus faecihominis, a known butyrate producer, was also detected. Since butyrate has been implicated in gut health and immune modulation47, its depletion in OA patients could have consequences for inflammation. Similarly, Longibaculum muris was significantly less abundant, but the lack of information on its role in the human gut microbiome prevents any clear interpretation of this finding. Overall, while our study highlights potential microbiota differences in OA patients, further research is needed to confirm these associations and explore their functional relevance.

Next, we aimed to investigate the potential role of Bacteroidetes and Firmicutes about functional tests. Given that physical exercise plays a beneficial role in both the prevention and treatment of numerous chronic illnesses, including OA49, and is recommended by multiple guidelines as the primary management strategy for this condition50,51, understanding the interplay between gut microbiota and physical function is crucial. In this scenario, it is mandatory to understand the correlation between gut dysbiosis and musculoskeletal disorders, already widely discussed in the scientific literature52–57, such as the potential influence of physical activity on gut microbiota composition58–60

Kendall’s Tau correlation analysis revealed distinct associations between gut bacterial genera from the Bacteroidetes and Firmicutes phyla and clinical manifestations of OA. Within Bacteroidetes, increased abundance of Prevotella and Odoribacter correlated positively with WOMAC pain scores, suggesting a potential link to heightened pain, consistent with Prevotella’s pro-inflammatory associations61. Conversely, higher Phocaeicola abundance correlated negatively with WOMAC scores, hinting at a possible protective role against OA pain. The negative correlation between Odoribacter and Paraprevotella may indicate an ecological interaction, the clinical relevance of which requires further study. Odoribacter abundance was also negatively associated with BB score and positively with TUG, suggesting a link to functional limitations.

Analysis of Firmicutes genera showed broader clinical associations. Lower abundance of Lachnoclostridium and Kineothrix correlated with higher WOMAC pain scores, suggesting a possible link to more severe pain; notably, Lachnoclostridium also correlated positively with MNA score, potentially linking it to nutritional status. Conversely, higher Mediterraneibacter and Pseudoflavonifractor abundance correlated with higher WOMAC pain scores, suggesting a possible contribution to pain. Lower abundance of Dorea, Oscillibacter, Christensenella, Aminipila, Phascolarctobacterium, and Dialister correlated with lower SPPB scores (poorer lower extremity function), while only Phocaeicola abundance correlated positively with SPPB. Higher Succiniclastium and Holdemania abundance correlated with higher VAS pain scores, while higher Anaerobutyricum and Lacrimispora abundance correlated with lower VAS pain scores. Higher Anaerotruncus and Breznakia abundance correlated with poorer TUG performance, while higher Faecalibacterium and Lactobacillus abundance correlated with better TUG performance. Finally, lower Anaerotruncus and Oscillibacter abundance correlated with higher BB scores, and higher Lachnoclostridium abundance correlated with higher MNA scores.

Species-level analysis, facilitated by machine learning and visualized in the volcano plot, provided further granularity. The positive correlation of Prevotella copri, Cronobacter muytjensii, Faecalibacterium prausnitzii, Blautia luti, and Bacteroides stercoris with SPPB suggests a potential positive influence of these species on physical function. The significant negative correlation between Faecalibacterium prausnitzii and VAS reinforces its potential role in pain modulation. Similarly, Bacteroides mediterraneensis and Blautia luti were negatively associated with VAS. The inverse correlations of Kineothrix alysoides, Paraprevotella clara, and Anaerobutyricum hallii with the MNA score point towards a possible link between these species and nutritional status in OA patients. Finally, the positive association of Phocaeicola plebeius with WOMAC and of Roseburia faecis with the TUG test further underscores the intricate relationship between specific gut bacteria and OA manifestations.

Overall, taken together, our results show an association between gut microbiota and functional outcomes specifically for mild-moderate knee OA patients. We are aware that the observed associations could only suggest a complex interplay between gut microbiota composition and the clinical presentation including contrasting directions of correlations, with some taxa positively and others negatively associated with measures of pain, function, and mobility, thus, our findings highlighted the intricate role of the gut microbiome in systemic inflammation and potentially in modulating the local environment of the joint.

In this scenario, it should be underlined a positive association of Faecalibacterium and Faecalibacterium prausnitzii with SPPB and their negative association with VAS align with their known anti-inflammatory properties. Conversely, the positive correlation of Prevotella with WOMAC echoes findings in other inflammatory conditions, suggesting a potential pro-inflammatory role in the context of OA as well. The negative association of several Firmicutes genera (Anaerotruncus, Anaeromassilibacillus, Oscillibacter, Lactococcus, Megamonas, Caloranaerobacter, and Pelotomaculum) with WOMAC, suggesting that higher abundance might be protective against OA symptoms, warrants further investigation into the specific species within these genera and their metabolic functions. The correlation between specific bacterial species and functional tests (SPPB and TUG) represents a novel finding of this study, suggesting a potential link between gut microbial composition and the physical capabilities of individuals with knee OA. This observation opens new avenues for research into the mechanisms by which gut dysbiosis might contribute to functional decline in OA. Suggesting a protective effect on functional performance and pain perception. Prevotella species have been implicated in both rheumatoid arthritis and OA; more in detail, an increased abundance of P. copri in early-stage of rheumatoid arthritis patients and even in individuals at risk has been documented, with murine gnotobiotic models showing that such dysbiosis can exacerbate Th17-mediated arthritis. On the contrary, in OA, Prevotella has not been consistently linked to functional impairment in healthy individuals, suggesting that its pathological relevance may be at least partially disease-specific62,63.

The emerging evidence in OA supports the concept of a unique gut–joint axis: fecal microbiota transplantation from metabolically compromised OA patients into germ-free mice resulted in increased gut permeability, elevated systemic inflammatory biomarkers, and significantly worsened OA pathology following joint injury10,64,65. This underscores a mechanistic link between an adverse gut microbiome and joint degeneration, further reinforcing disease specificity.

From a translational perspective, these insights open promising avenues for microbiota-targeted interventions. Probiotics and prebiotics have demonstrated potential to modulate host immunity and suppress inflammatory pathways (including OA and rheumatoid diseases) through beneficial shifts in gut microbial composition and increased production of short-chain fatty acids53.

Moreover, select probiotics such as Streptococcus thermophilus and Lactobacillus pentosus have shown chondro-protective and anti-catabolic effects in preclinical OA models, reducing cartilage degradation and inflammatory markers52,66.Albeit interventions like probiotics, prebiotics, dietary modulation, or even fecal transplantation may hold therapeutic promise for attenuating systemic inflammation and improving OA-related function, it should be taken into consideration that current data are still preliminar55,67.

Our study has several inherent limitations. The small sample size may restrict the generalizability of our conclusions and limit our ability to observe variations across different stages of mild to moderate knee OA. The cross-sectional design prevents the establishment of causal relationships and the analysis of longitudinal changes, and while an age-matched control group was used, we could not investigate the impact of therapeutic-rehabilitative interventions or the reduction of subclinical inflammation. Additionally, we did not adjust for potential confounding variables and lacked data on genetic predispositions and metabolomics.

Despite these constraints, to the best of our knowledge, this is the first report that has investigated the relationship between knee OA and the effects of gut dysbiosis on physical function, and importantly, the first to explore correlations between the Firmicutes and Bacteroidetes and clinical and functional outcomes in humans with mild to moderate knee OA.

Our results can be relevant in two main clinical areas. First, the observed differences in gut microbiota composition suggest their potential as a non-invasive diagnostic and prognostic biomarker. Distinct microbial profiles could help identify specific patient phenotypes, enabling earlier and more accurate diagnosis of OA. Additionally, monitoring changes in the gut microbiome over time could serve as a prognostic tool to predict disease progression or a patient’s response to treatment. Second, our findings provide a foundation for therapeutic modulation of the gut environment. By promoting the growth of beneficial bacteria or introducing favorable metabolites, interventions could aim to alleviate the inflammatory component of OA. This can be achieved through dietary changes, prebiotics, probiotics, or even fecal microbiota transplantation.

We believe the gut-joint axis offers a promising new target for personalized treatments that seek to restore gut homeostasis, thereby reducing joint inflammation and damage.

Our tool is both diagnostic and clinically translational. Considering the high variability of the disease phenotype, it could be used to stratify participants in clinical trials, ensuring that patients enrolled in studies on microbiome-targeted therapies have a microbial profile that makes them suitable to respond to treatment. This approach, which aligns perfectly with the principles of precision medicine, would increase the effectiveness of trials, reduce response variability, and significantly improve clinical outcomes. Our research provides a solid foundation for the development of a non-invasive biomarker that supports more targeted recruitment for future studies.

To validate these initial findings and further explore the underlying mechanisms, future longitudinal studies are essential. These studies should involve larger cohorts, multivariate models that adjust for clinical or demographic covariates (e.g., age, sex, BMI), and multi-omics approaches, such as metabolomics, proteomics, and genomics, to link specific metabolites with clinical endpoints. Additionally, interventions that target the gut microbiota should be investigated.

Conclusion

For the first time, the present study provides valuable insights into the complex associations between gut microbiota composition and clinical and functional outcomes in individuals with mild to moderate knee OA. The identification of specific bacterial taxa correlated with pain, physical function, and mobility underscores the potential role of the gut-joint axis in this debilitating condition and highlights promising avenues for future research and potential therapeutic interventions. Further prospective studies investigating the role of physical exercise on the gut microbiota composition might provide crucial findings that could also change the perspective in the management of OA patients.
