The Intrinsic Riemannian Proximal Gradient Method for Convex Optimization
Ronny Bergmann, Hajg Jasa, Paula John, Max Pfeffer

Abstract
. We consider a class of (possibly strongly) geodesically convex optimization problems on Hadamard manifolds, where the objective function splits into the sum of a smooth and a possibly nonsmooth function. We introduce an intrinsic convex Riemannian proximal gradient (CRPG) method that employs the manifold proximal map for the nonsmooth step, without operating in the embedding or tangent space. A sublinear convergence rate for convex problems and a linear convergence rate for strongly convex problems is established, and we derive fundamental proximal gradient inequalities that generalize the Euclidean case. Our numerical experiments on hyperbolic spaces and manifolds of symmetric positive definite matrices demonstrate substantial computational advantages over existing methods.

1.
Introduction

A prominent approach to nonsmooth optimization are splitting methods, where the objective function
can be split, for example

(1)

\argmin_{p\in\mathcal{M}}f(p),\qquad\text{where}\quad f(p)=g(p)+h(p),

where
\mathcal{M}
is a Riemannian manifold,
and both
g
and
h
have certain properties,
for example both are convex, lower-semicontinuous, and proper.

These splitting methods have been studied extensively in the Euclidean case,
and several methods have been generalized to the Riemannian setting.
Starting with an even more general splitting into
n
functions instead of
2
,
the cyclic proximal point algorithm (CPPA)
[
2
]
is
one of the first such splitting methods on manifolds.
Another approach was introduced in
[
12
]
,
where the Douglas-Rachford algorithm (DRA)
[
21
]
was generalized to Hadamard manifolds.
An algorithm that is known to be equivalent to DRA is the Chambolle-Pock algorithm
[
15
]
,
which is very popular especially in signal and image processing.
This was generalized to Riemannian manifolds in
[
11
]
,
which further led to investigations on duality on manifolds
[
30
,
29
]
.

Another popular method that is based on splitting is the proximal gradient method.
In the Euclidean case,
[
4
, Chapter 10]
provides a thorough overview.
Here,
g
is assumed to be (
L

g

-)smooth and
h
to be convex and potentially nonsmooth.
In this case, the Euclidean proximal gradient method can be shown to have a convergence rate of
\mathcal{O}(1/k)
whenever
g
is convex.

To the best of our knowledge, the authors in
[
16
]
first introduced a partially convex
proximal gradient version for the Stiefel manifold, phrasing the nonsmooth part as a constrained problem in the embedding, and requiring the nonsmooth function to be the restriction of a convex function in the embedding.
This was generalized to general Riemannian manifolds in
[
24
]
,
where the authors phrase the nonsmooth subproblem as a problem in the tangent space at the current iterate, and do not consider convexity assumptions in their general analysis.
The linear convergence of this algorithm under strong retraction convexity has been studied in
[
17
]
.

All these works are based on a “generalized notion of the proximal map”
[
24
, (3.1)]
, and solving the corresponding subproblem either in the embedding or the tangent space of the current iterate.
This general notion phrases the proximal map as a retraction-based subproblem in a tangent space, which is then solved with Euclidean optimization tools, sometimes rather advanced ones such as nonsmooth quasi-Newton methods. It is also often restricted to specific manifolds, usually embedded manifolds or quotients thereof.
The convexity considerations are either retraction-based or coming from the embedding, and they usually require some kind of Lipschitz property.

Most of these works assume that
g
and
h
fulfil some convexity property, either along geodesics or along curves generated by retractions when using a retraction-based generalization of the proximal subproblem.
In the work of
[
24
]
, a “retraction smoothness” of
g
is required, but
h
can be nonsmooth and nonconvex for their convergence investigations.
However, their algorithm employs the embedding in a way that
h
is required to have certain properties thereon in order to solve their approximation to the proximal map.
In
[
22
]
the authors do employ a proximal map, but restrict their investigation to Hadamard manifolds and employing the Kurdyka-Łojasiewicz property on manifolds.

In a related work,
[
26
]
consider gradient schemes that combine approximate solutions to proximal maps defined on the manifold, leading to a globally accelerated gradient descent algorithm on Hadamard manifolds.
Furthermore,
[
27
]
introduce a composite Riemannian descent method for geodesically convex functions that solves a proximal-type linearized subproblem at each iteration, which is also defined on the manifold.
Their convergence rates are in line with their Euclidean counterparts, albeit accounting for the curvature of the manifold.

In this work, we introduce a formulation of the Riemannian proximal gradient method for (strongly) geodesically convex functions that actually employs a proximal map defined on the manifold for the nonsmooth step.
This continues our work on that method that was started in
[
8
]
, where we have investigated the nonconvex case.
It also relates the new proximal gradient method directly back to both the PPA
[
23
]
as well as gradient descent, e. g.
[
34
]
.

This new formulation allows to investigate several properties from
[
4
, Chapter 10]
.
Most prominently, a sublinear convergence rate in the function values is established when the objective is assumed to be geodesically (not strongly) convex.
In this case, a conditional convergence statement can be formulated when the stepsize is kept constant and chosen to satisfy a particular geometric condition.
We also generalize the fundamental prox-grad inequality, which is extensively used in Beck’s analysis, and showcase how this approach does not directly provide convergence properties in the general Hadamard case.
Finally, when the objective is assumed to be strongly geodesically convex, a linear convergence result can be established.

The remainder of this paper is organized as follows.
After introducing the necessary notation and some preliminary properties in
Section

2
, we derive the new formulation of the Convex Riemannian Proximal Gradient (CRPG) method in
Section

3
.
In
Section

4
, we present convergence results for the geodesically convex and strongly convex cases on Hadamard manifolds.
In
Section

5
, we derive Riemannian variants of the fundamental prox-grad inequality that generalize
[
4
, Theorem 10.16]
and discuss their usefulness.

Section

6
illustrates the performance of the algorithm and its convergence, and
Section

7
concludes the paper.

2.
Preliminaries

In this section, we recall some notions from Riemannian geometry.
For more details, the reader may wish to consult
[
19
,
13
]
.
Let
\mathcal{M}
be a smooth manifold with Riemannian metric
(\cdot\,,\,\cdot)
and let
D
be its Levi-Civita connection.
Given
p,q\in\mathcal{M}
, we denote by
C_{pq}
the set of all piecewise smooth curves
\gamma\colon[0,1]\to\mathcal{M}
from
p=\gamma(0)
to
q=\gamma(1)
.
The
arc length
of
\gamma\in C_{pq}
,
L\colon C_{pq}\to\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}
is defined as

L(\gamma)=\int_{0}^{1}\lVert\dot{\gamma}\rVert_{\gamma(t)}\,\mathrm{d}t,

where
\lVert\cdot\rVert_{p}
is the norm induced by the Riemannian metric in the tangent space
\mathcal{T}_{p}\mathcal{M}
at the point
p
.
The inner product given by the Riemannian metric at
p
is
(\cdot\,,\,\cdot)_{p}\colon\mathcal{T}_{p}\mathcal{M}\times\mathcal{T}_{p}\mathcal{M}\to\mathbb{R}
.
The explicit reference to the base point will be omitted whenever the base point is clear from context.
The sectional curvature at
p\in\mathcal{M}
is

K_{p}(X_{p},Y_{p})=\frac{(\mathrm{R}_{p}(X_{p},Y_{p})Y_{p}\,,\,X_{p})}{\lVert X_{p}\rVert^{2}\,\lVert Y_{p}\rVert^{2}-(X_{p}\,,\,Y_{p})^{2}},

where
\mathrm{R}
is the Riemann curvature tensor, and
X_{p},Y_{p}\in\mathcal{T}_{p}\mathcal{M}
.

The Riemannian distance between two points,
d\colon\mathcal{M}\times\mathcal{M}\to\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}\cup\{+\infty\}
, is given by

d(p,q)=\inf_{\gamma\in C_{pq}}L(\gamma).

A
geodesic arc
from
p\in\mathcal{M}
to
q\in\mathcal{M}
is a smooth curve
\gamma\in C_{pq}
that is parallel along itself, i. e.,
\textup{D}_{\dot{\gamma}(t)}\dot{\gamma}(t)=0
for all
t\in(0,1)
.
A finite-length geodesic arc is
minimal
if its arc length coincides with the Riemannian distance between its extreme points.
Given two points
p,q\in\mathcal{M}
, we denote with
{\gamma}_{p,q}
a minimal geodesic arc that connects
p={\gamma}_{p,q}(0)
to
q={\gamma}_{p,q}(1)
.
Given a point
p\in\mathcal{M}
and a tangent vector
X_{p}\in\mathcal{T}_{p}\mathcal{M}
, a geodesic can also be specified
as the curve
{\gamma}_{p,X_{p}}
such that
{\gamma}_{p,X_{p}}(0)=p
and
{\gamma}_{p,X_{p}}^{\prime}(0)=X_{p}
.
The
exponential map
at
p\in\mathcal{M}
maps a tangent vector
X_{p}\in\mathcal{T}_{p}\mathcal{M}
to the endpoint of the corresponding geodesic,
\expOp_{p}(X_{p})={\gamma}_{p,X_{p}}(1)
.
If it exists, the inverse map is called the
logarithmic map

\logOp_{p}\colon\mathcal{M}\rightarrow\mathcal{T}_{p}\mathcal{M}
.
We call a space
uniquely geodesic
if every two points in that space are connected by one and only one geodesic in that set.
In this case the logarithmic map exists everywhere.
A set
\mathcal{U}\subseteq\mathcal{M}
is said to be
(uniquely) geodesically convex
if for any
p,q\in\mathcal{U}
, there exists a (unique) minimal geodesic arc
{\gamma}_{p,q}
which lies entirely in
\mathcal{U}
.
Observe that if a manifold
\mathcal{M}
has positive sectional curvature, the set
\mathcal{U}
may not be allowed to have arbitrarily large diameter.
For example, if
{\hyperlink{def:k-min}{\kappa_{\mathrm{l}}}}>0
, for
\mathcal{U}
to be uniquely geodesically convex, its diameter must be bounded by
\diam(\mathcal{U})<\pi/\sqrt{{\hyperlink{def:k-min}{\kappa_{\mathrm{l}}}}}
.
See, e. g.,
[
32
, Proposition 4.1]
or
[
14
, Proposition II.1.4]
.

We denote the
parallel transport
from
p
to
q
along
{\gamma}_{p,q}
with respect to the connection
D
by
\textup{P}_{q\leftarrow p}\colon\mathcal{T}_{p}\mathcal{M}\to\mathcal{T}_{q}\mathcal{M}
.
This is defined by

\textup{P}_{q\leftarrow p}Y_{p}=X_{q}

for
Y_{p}\in\mathcal{T}_{p}\mathcal{M}
, where
X
is the unique smooth vector field along
{\gamma}_{p,q}
which satisfies

X_{p}=Y_{p}\text{ and }\textup{D}_{\dot{\gamma}}{X}=0.

Notice that
\textup{P}_{q\leftarrow p}
depends on the choice of
{\gamma}_{p,q}
.

Given a differentiable function
f\colon\mathcal{M}\to\mathbb{R}
, we denote the
Riemannian gradient
of
f
at
p\in\mathcal{M}
by
\grad f(p)\in\mathcal{T}_{p}\mathcal{M}
.
See
[
13
, Definition 8.57]
for more details.
A function
f\colon\mathcal{M}\to\mathbb{R}
is said to be Lipschitz at
p\in\mathcal{M}
if there exists an open neighborhood
\mathcal{U}
of
p
and a constant
L_{p}>0
such that

\lvert f(y)-f(z)\rvert\leq L_{p}d(y,z)\quad\text{for all }y,z\in\mathcal{U}.

Furthermore,
f
is called locally Lipschitz if it is Lipschitz at every point
p\in\mathcal{M}
.

For a geodesically convex set
\mathcal{U}\subseteq\mathcal{M}
, a proper function
f\colon\mathcal{U}\to\overline{\mathbb{R}}
and a point
p\in\dom(f)
, a tangent vector
X_{p}\in\mathcal{T}_{p}\mathcal{M}
is said to be a
subgradient
of
f
at
p
if
f(q)\geq f(p)+(X_{p}\,,\,\logOp_{p}{q})
for all
q\in\mathcal{U}
.
The set of all subgradients of
f
at
p
is the
subdifferential
of
f
at
p
, and it is denoted by
\partial f(p)
.
This is a nonempty, convex, and compact set at any point in
\mathcal{M}
.
Given a real number
{\hyperlink{def:strong-convex-constant}{\mu_{f}}}>0
, a function
f\colon\mathcal{U}\to\overline{\mathbb{R}}
is said to be
(
μ

f

-strongly) geodesically convex
over
\mathcal{U}
if, for any geodesic arc
{\gamma}\colon[0,1]\to\mathcal{M}
contained in
\mathcal{U}
, the composition
f\circ{\gamma}
is (
\mu
-strongly with
\mu={\hyperlink{def:strong-convex-constant}{\mu_{f}}}L({\gamma})^{2}
) convex in the Euclidean sense; cf.
[
13
, Definition 11.5]
.
This is equivalent to the following inequality holding for all
p,q\in\mathcal{U}
and all
X_{p}\in\partial f(p)

(2)

f(q)\geq f(p)+(X_{p}\,,\,\logOp_{p}{q})+\frac{{\hyperlink{def:strong-convex-constant}{\mu_{f}}}}{2}d^{2}(p,q).

If the previous inequality is satisfied with
{\hyperlink{def:strong-convex-constant}{\mu_{f}}}=0
, the function
f
is equivalently geodesically convex over
\mathcal{U}
.
Furthermore, if
f
is subdifferentiable and geodesically convex on an open geodesically convex set,
0_{p}\in\partial f(p)
if and only if
p
is a global minimizer of
f
in
\mathcal{M}
.
See, e. g.,
[
31
]
for a thorough analysis of convex functions on Riemannian manifolds.
Given a real number
L_{f}>0
, a differentiable function
f
is said to be
L_{f}
-smooth
over
\mathcal{U}
if

f(q)\leq f(p)+(\grad f(p)\,,\,\logOp_{p}{q})+\frac{L_{f}}{2}d^{2}(p,q),

for any
p,q\in\mathcal{U}
.
For a geodesically convex function
f
and a real number
\lambda>0
, define

\proxOp_{\lambda f}(p)\coloneq\argmin_{q\in\mathcal{M}}f(q)+\frac{1}{2\lambda}d^{2}(p,q),

if the minimizer exists.
If now
f=g+h
with
g
of class
\mathcal{C}^{1}(\mathcal{U})
and
h
locally Lipschitz over
\mathcal{U}
, then
[
6
, Lemma 3.1]
gives

(3)

\partial(g+h)(p)=\grad g(p)+\partial h(p),

for all
p\in\mathcal{U}
.
Therefore, the condition
0_{p}\in\partial(g+h)(p)=\grad g(p)+\partial h(p)
is equivalent to the stationarity condition

(4)

-\grad g(p)\in\partial h(p).

In the analysis of the proximal gradient method, we will also need the following curvature-dependent quantities.
Given real numbers
\kappa_{1},\kappa_{2}
, which will typically be lower and upper bounds to the sectional curvature of

For a given
\lambda>0
and
p\in\mathcal{M}
, we will also often use the following notation for the gradient step throughout the manuscript

\Hy@SaveSpaceFactor\hbox to0.0pt{\hss\smash{\HyperRaiseLinkHook\raise 0.0pt\hbox{\Hy@RestoreSpaceFactor\hypertarget{def:gradient-step}{}\Hy@SaveSpaceFactor}}}\Hy@RestoreSpaceFactor\penalty\hskip 0.0pt{\hyperlink{def:gradient-step}{z(p)}}\coloneq\expOp_{p}(-\lambda\grad g(p)).

Furthermore, when referring to specific iterations of the algorithm, we will always assume
z(p

(
n
)

)
to be prescribed by the stepsize at the
n
-th step, that is, we write
{\hyperlink{def:gradient-step}{z(p^{(n)})}}=\expOp_{p^{(n)}}(-\lambda^{(n)}\grad g(p^{(n)}))
for a given
n\in\mathbb{N}

3.
The Riemannian Proximal Gradient method

The main idea for the derivation of the proximal gradient method is to replace
g
by a proximal regularization of its linearized function
[
5
, Sec. 2.2]
.
For
\lambda>0
and a point
p
we compute a new candidate as

\argmin_{q\in\mathcal{M}}g(p)+(\grad g(p)\,,\,\logOp_{p}q)+\frac{1}{2\lambda}d^{2}(p,q)+h(q).

We introduce
r(p)\coloneq\frac{1}{2\lambda}d^{2}(p,q)
and Taylor expand this function, cf.
[
13
, Sec. 4.8]
,

\begin{split}r(\expOp_{p}(X_{p}))&=r(p)+(\grad r(p)\,,\,X_{p})+\mathcal{O}(\lVert X_{p}\rVert^{2}),\\
&=r(p)+-\frac{1}{\lambda}\mathopen{}\left(\logOp_{p}q\,,\,X_{p}\mathclose{}\right)+\mathcal{O}(\lVert X_{p}\rVert^{2}),\end{split}

where
X_{p}\in\mathcal{T}_{p}\mathcal{M}
.
For the case
X_{p}=-\lambda\grad g(p)
this reads

\frac{1}{2\lambda}d^{2}(\expOp_{p}(-\lambda\grad g(p)),q)=\frac{1}{2\lambda}d^{2}(p,q)+(\logOp_{p}q\,,\,\grad g(p))+\mathcal{O}(\lambda^{2}),

using this as a second quadratic approximation we obtain the new candidate as

\argmin_{q\in\mathcal{M}}g(p)+\frac{1}{2\lambda}d^{2}(\expOp_{p}(-\lambda\grad g(p)),q)+h(q).

Since the first term
g(p)
does not change the minimizer, the resulting expression is equal to the proximal map.

\proxOp_{\lambda h}\bigl(\expOp_{p}(-\lambda\grad g(p))\bigr)=\argmin_{q\in\mathcal{M}}\frac{1}{2\lambda}d^{2}(\expOp_{p}(-\lambda\grad g(p)),q)+h(q).

4.
Convergence

In this section, we analyze the convergence behavior of the Convex Riemannian Proximal Gradient (CRPG) method.
When
g
is assumed to be geodesically convex, we obtain a sublinear convergence of the cost function.
In case
g
is strongly geodesically convex, we can show a linear convergence rate.

Let us introduce the standing set of assumptions that we will use in the rest of this section.

Assumption 4.1

(Standing assumptions)
.

(1)

\mathcal{M}
is a Hadamard manifold, i. e. complete, simply connected and with nonnegative sectional curvatures everywhere.

(2)

There exists a number
{\hyperlink{def:k-min}{\kappa_{\mathrm{l}}}}\in\mathbb{R}

\Hy@SaveSpaceFactor\hbox to0.0pt{\hss\smash{\HyperRaiseLinkHook\raise 0.0pt\hbox{\Hy@RestoreSpaceFactor\hypertarget{def:k-min}{}\Hy@SaveSpaceFactor}}}\Hy@RestoreSpaceFactor\penalty\hskip 0.0pt{\hyperlink{def:k-min}{\kappa_{\mathrm{l}}}}\leq K_{p}(X_{p},X_{p})

.

We can improve our previous result from
[

, Lemma 4.4]
on Hadamard manifolds whenever the (possibly) nonsmooth function

This upper bound exactly recovers the Euclidean one in
[

Lemma 1
.

If
4.1
holds, then for any
p\in\interior(\dom(g))
and
\lambda\in\bigl(0,\frac{2}{{\hyperlink{def:gradg-lipschitz-constant}{L_{g}}}}\bigr)
, the following sufficient decrease inequality holds

f(p)-f({\mathrm{T}}_{{\lambda}}{(p)})\geq\frac{2-\lambda{\hyperlink{def:gradg-lipschitz-constant}{L_{g}}}}{2\lambda}d^{2}(p,{\mathrm{T}}_{{\lambda}}{(p)}).

Proof.

By the
L

g

-smoothness of
g
and the convexity of
h
, we have

\displaystyle f(p)

\displaystyle\geq g({\mathrm{T}}_{{\lambda}}{(p)})+\frac{1}{\lambda}\mathopen{}\left(\logOp_{p}{{\hyperlink{def:gradient-step}{z(p)}}}\,,\,\logOp_{p}{{\mathrm{T}}_{{\lambda}}{(p)}}\mathclose{}\right)-\frac{{\hyperlink{def:gradg-lipschitz-constant}{L_{g}}}}{2}d^{2}(p,{\mathrm{T}}_{{\lambda}}{(p)})

\displaystyle\quad+h({\mathrm{T}}_{{\lambda}}{(p)})+\frac{1}{\lambda}\mathopen{}\left(\logOp_{{\mathrm{T}}_{{\lambda}}{(p)}}{{\hyperlink{def:gradient-step}{z(p)}}}\,,\,\logOp_{{\mathrm{T}}_{{\lambda}}{(p)}}{p}\mathclose{}\right),

where we also used the definition of
z(p)
and the fact that
\frac{1}{\lambda}\logOp_{{\mathrm{T}}_{{\lambda}}{(p)}}{{\hyperlink{def:gradient-step}{z(p)}}}\in\partial h({\mathrm{T}}_{{\lambda}}{(p)})
.
Applying the Riemannian cosine inequality in
[
26
, Corollary 15]
to both inner product terms we get

\frac{1}{\lambda}\mathopen{}\left(\logOp_{p}{{\hyperlink{def:gradient-step}{z(p)}}}\,,\,\logOp_{p}{{\mathrm{T}}_{{\lambda}}{(p)}}\mathclose{}\right)\geq\frac{1}{2\lambda}[d^{2}(p,{\hyperlink{def:gradient-step}{z(p)}})+d^{2}(p,{\mathrm{T}}_{{\lambda}}{(p)})-d^{2}({\hyperlink{def:gradient-step}{z(p)}},{\mathrm{T}}_{{\lambda}}{(p)})]

and

\frac{1}{\lambda}(\logOp_{{\mathrm{T}}_{{\lambda}}{(p)}}{{\hyperlink{def:gradient-step}{z(p)}}}\,,\,\logOp_{{\mathrm{T}}_{{\lambda}}{(p)}}{p})\geq\frac{1}{2\lambda}[d^{2}({\mathrm{T}}_{{\lambda}}{(p)},{\hyperlink{def:gradient-step}{z(p)}})+d^{2}({\mathrm{T}}_{{\lambda}}{(p)},p)-d^{2}({\hyperlink{def:gradient-step}{z(p)}},p)],

Remark
.

The previous theorem shows that the shrinkage of the function values is characterized by two regimes, depending on the ratio

-\zeta_{1,{\hyperlink{def:k-min}{\kappa_{\mathrm{l}}}}}(R)\Delta^{(0)}+\zeta_{1,{\hyperlink{def:k-min}{\kappa_{\mathrm{l}}}}}(R)\Delta^{(k)}+\sum_{n=1}^{k}\Delta^{(n)}\leq\frac{R^{2}}{2\lambda},

where we used the fact that the function
\zeta_{1,{\hyperlink{def:k-min}{\kappa_{\mathrm{l}}}}}(r)
is monotonically increasing for
r\geq 0
.
Since the sequence
\Delta^{(k)}
is nonincreasing, we have again

\sum_{n=1}^{k}\Delta^{(n)}=\sum_{n=0}^{k-1}\Delta^{(n+1)}\geq k\Delta^{(k)},

which combined with the previous inequality yields

\Delta^{(k)}\leq\frac{R^{2}+2\lambda\zeta_{1,{\hyperlink{def:k-min}{\kappa_{\mathrm{l}}}}}(R)\Delta^{(0)}}{2\lambda(\zeta_{1,{\hyperlink{def:k-min}{\kappa_{\mathrm{l}}}}}(R)+k)}.

Compare, e. g.,
[
34
, Theorem 13]
, for the convergence rate of the RGD method on Hadamard manifolds.

In general,
g,h\neq 0
, and the term involving
d^{2}(q,{\hyperlink{def:gradient-step}{z(q)}})
in
Equation

34
cannot be canceled out using the sufficient decrease condition.
Therefore, the fundamental proximal gradient inequality is not sufficient to directly provide a convergence rate of the function values for
Algorithm

1
, as no telescoping can be easily achieved following this path.

Furthermore, in the Euclidean setting, the proximal gradient inequality is exploited in
[
4
, Theorem 10.23]
to establish the Fejér monotonicity of the sequence
(p^{(n)})
in the convex case, which is then used in
[
4
, Theorem 10.24]
to prove convergence of such sequence to an optimal solution of the problem
Equation

1
.
The same cannot be achieved here.
The reason is that none of the fundamental proximal gradient inequalities imply the Fejér monotonicity of the sequence
(p^{(n)})
in the geodesically convex case.
Indeed, taking
Equation

39
and dropping the positive terms gives

\displaystyle d^{2}(p^{(n+1)},p^{\ast})

\displaystyle\leq\frac{{\hyperlink{def:zeta-first}{\zeta_{1,{\hyperlink{def:k-min}{\kappa_{\mathrm{l}}}}}(d(p^{(n)},z_{p^{(n)}}))}}+1}{2}d^{2}(p^{(n)},p^{\ast})

\displaystyle\quad+\frac{{\hyperlink{def:zeta-first}{\zeta_{1,{\hyperlink{def:k-min}{\kappa_{\mathrm{l}}}}}(d(p^{\ast},p^{(n)}))}}-1}{2}d^{2}(p^{(n)},z_{p^{(n)}}),

which does not imply any Fejér monotonicity.

6.2.
Sparse Mean on
\mathcal{H}^{n}

In the next experiment, we want to compute the mean of
N
points on the hyperbolic space
\mathcal{H}^{n}
with additional sparsity constraints.
That is, we solve the optimization problem in
Equation

1
on
\mathcal{M}=\mathcal{H}^{n}
, the hyperbolic space equipped with the Minkowski metric of signature
(+,\ldots,+,-)
.
Then
\mathcal{H}^{n}
is a Hadamard manifold with constant sectional curvature
{\hyperlink{def:k-min}{\kappa_{\mathrm{l}}}}=-1
.

For the computation of the sparse mean, we set

g(p)=\frac{1}{2N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}d^{2}(p,q_{i})

for
p\in\mathcal{M}
and
q_{i}\in\mathcal{M}
for
i=1,\ldots,N
, where
N=1000
and
q_{i}
are randomly sampled points on
\mathcal{M}
.
Furthermore, we choose
h(p)=\|p\|_{1}
, which is the
\ell_{1}
-norm on the hyperbolic space.

Soft Thresholding on
\mathcal{H}^{n}

We now set out to derive a method for computing the proximal operator of the
\ell_{1}
-norm on the hyperbolic space
\mathcal{H}^{n}
that is easy to implement, while arguing that the function is geodesically convex.

We represent the hyperbolic space
\mathcal{H}^{n}
as a subset of
\mathbb{R}^{n+1}
using the Minkowski pseudo inner product
\langle{x},{y}\rangle_{M}=x^{\top}Jy
with
J=\diag(1,\ldots,1,-1)
as

\mathcal{H}^{n}=\{x\in\mathbb{R}^{n+1}\,|\,\langle x,x\rangle_{M}=-1,\,x_{n+1}>0\},

with the metric
g_{M}=\langle\cdot,\cdot\rangle_{M}
.
Then the tangent space at
x\in\mathcal{H}^{n}
and the projection of a vector
z\in\mathbb{R}^{n+1}
onto it are given by

\mathcal{T}_{x}\mathcal{H}^{n}=\{v\in\mathbb{R}^{n+1}\,|\,\langle x,v\rangle_{M}=0\},
and

\projOp_{x}(z)=z+\langle x,z\rangle_{M}\,x,

see, e. g.,
[
13
, Chapter 7.6]
.
The geodesic distance is defined as

d(x,y)=\text{arccosh}\left(-\langle x,y\rangle_{M}\right),

and the exponential and logarithm maps are

\displaystyle\expOp_{x}(v)

\displaystyle=\cosh{\left(\sqrt{\langle v,v\rangle_{M}}\right)}x+\sinh{\left(\sqrt{\langle v,v\rangle_{M}}\right)}\frac{v}{\sqrt{\langle v,v\rangle_{M}}},

\displaystyle\logOp_{x}(y)

\displaystyle=\frac{\text{arcosh}\left(-\langle x,y\rangle_{M}\right)}{\sqrt{\langle x,y\rangle_{M}^{2}-1}}\left(y+\langle x,y\rangle_{M}x\right),

for any
x,y\in\mathcal{H}^{n}
and
v\in\mathcal{T}_{x}\mathcal{H}^{n}
; for more details see
[
12
, Appendix C]
.
With this we can calculate the geodesic arc from
x
to
y
as

\displaystyle\gamma_{x,y}(t)

\displaystyle=\left(\cosh(t\,\text{arcosh}\left(-\langle x,y\rangle_{M}\right))+\langle x,y\rangle_{M}\frac{\sinh(t\,\text{arcosh}\left(-\langle x,y\rangle_{M}\right))}{\sqrt{\langle x,y\rangle_{M}^{2}-1}}\right)x

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad\qquad+\left(\frac{\sinh(t\,\text{arcosh}\left(-\langle x,y\rangle_{M}\right))}{\sqrt{\langle x,y\rangle_{M}^{2}-1}}\right)y.

Using this representation of geodesic arcs and the triangle inequality, one can show that the
\ell_{1}
-norm is geodesically convex on
(\mathcal{H}^{n},g_{M})
, e. g. by Taylor expanding the coefficients of
x
and
y
in the expression for
\gamma_{x,y}(t)
around
t=0
and
t=1
.

The rest of this section is devoted to show that the proximal operator of the
\ell_{1}
-norm, defined by

(42)

\proxOp_{\mu\|\cdot\|_{1}}(x)\coloneq\argmin_{y\in\mathcal{H}^{n}}\|y\|_{1}+\frac{1}{2\mu}d^{2}(x,y),

can be computed as
p_{x}(t^{*})
, with
t^{*}
a fixed point of
\sigma_{x}\circ p_{x}
, where

\displaystyle\sigma_{x}(y)\coloneq\mu\frac{\sqrt{\langle x,y\rangle_{M}^{2}-1}}{\text{arcosh}\left(-\langle x,y\rangle_{M}\right)},

\displaystyle\quad p_{x}(t)\coloneq\frac{\bar{p}_{x}(t)}{\sqrt{-\langle\bar{p}_{x}(t),\bar{p}_{x}(t)\rangle_{M}}}\in\mathcal{H}^{n},

and
\bar{p}_{x}(t)
is defined elementwise as

\displaystyle(\bar{p}_{x}(t))_{i}\coloneq\begin{cases}x_{i}+t&\text{if }i=n+1,\\
\max\{0,x_{i}-\text{sign}(x_{i})t\}&\text{otherwise}.\end{cases}

With this result, we can compute the solution of the prox-subproblem
Equation

42
using a fixed point iteration that in our experience only takes a few steps to converge to the desired accuracy.

First, we show that such fixed points exist and can be easily computed via a fixed point iteration.

Theorem 6.1
.

Let
x\in\mathcal{H}^{n}
and
\mu\geq 0
.
Then there exists a fixed point
t^{*}
of
T\coloneq\sigma_{x}\circ p_{x}
and every sequence
t_{k}=T(t_{k-1})
with
t_{0}\geq 0
converges to
t^{*}
.

Proof.

Define

x_{\max}=\max_{i=1,\dots,n}\lvert x_{i}\rvert
.
First, we observe that for
t\geq x_{\max}
, we have
p_{x}(t)=(0,\dots,0,1)
and thus
T(t)=\mu\frac{\sqrt{x_{n+1}^{2}-1}}{\text{arcosh}(x_{n+1})}=:t_{\max}\leq t
.
Note that
\lim_{s\to-1}\frac{\sqrt{s^{2}-1}}{\text{arcosh}(-s)}=1
, hence
T(0)=\mu\geq 0
.

We will now show that
T
is strictly increasing on the interval
[0,x_{\max}]
.
Together with
T
being continuous, this implies that
T
has exactly one fixed point
t^{*}\in[0,t_{\max}]
.
We can assume w.l.o.g. that
\lvert x_{1}\rvert\leq\dots\leq\lvert x_{n}\rvert
.
Notice that
p_{x}(t)
is smooth on the smaller intervals
I_{k}=\left(\lvert x_{k-1}\rvert,\lvert x_{k}\rvert\right)
for
k=1,\dots n
and
x_{0}=0
.
Consider the function
\phi_{k}(t)=-\langle p_{x}(t),x\rangle_{M}
on the interval
I_{k}
, then

\phi_{k}(t)=\frac{x_{n+1}(x_{n+1}+t)-\sum_{i=k}^{n}x_{i}^{2}-t\lvert x_{i}\rvert}{\sqrt{(x_{n+1}+t)^{2}-\sum_{i=k}^{n}(\lvert x_{i}\rvert-t)^{2}}}

and

\displaystyle\phi_{k}^{\prime}(t)

\displaystyle=\frac{\left(\sum_{i=k}^{n+1}\lvert x_{i}\rvert\right)\sqrt{(x_{n+1}+t)^{2}-\sum_{i=k}^{n}(\lvert x_{i}\rvert-t)^{2}}}{(x_{n+1}+t)^{2}-\sum_{i=k}^{n}(\lvert x_{i}\rvert-t)^{2}}

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad-\frac{\left(\left(\sum_{i=k}^{n+1}\lvert x_{i}\rvert\right)-(n-k)t\right)\left(x_{n+1}(x_{n+1}+t)-\sum_{i=k}^{n}x_{i}^{2}-t\lvert x_{i}\rvert\right)}{\sqrt{(x_{n+1}+t)^{2}-\sum_{i=k}^{n}(\lvert x_{i}\rvert-t)^{2}}\left((x_{n+1}+t)^{2}-\sum_{i=k}^{n}(\lvert x_{i}\rvert-t)^{2}\right)}

\displaystyle\geq\sum_{i=k}^{n+1}\lvert x_{i}\rvert\frac{(x_{n+1}+t)^{2}-\sum_{i=k}^{n}(\lvert x_{i}\rvert-t)^{2}-x_{n+1}(x_{n+1}+t)+\sum_{i=k}^{n}x_{i}^{2}-t\lvert x_{i}\rvert}{\sqrt{(x_{n+1}+t)^{2}-\sum_{i=k}^{n}(\lvert x_{i}\rvert-t)^{2}}\left((x_{n+1}+t)^{2}-\sum_{i=k}^{n}(\lvert x_{i}\rvert-t)^{2}\right)}

\displaystyle>0.

Thus,
\phi_{k}
is strictly increasing on
[0,x_{\max}]
.
Since
\frac{\sqrt{s^{2}-1}}{\text{arcosh}(s)}
is also strictly increasing for
s\geq 1
, it follows that
T
is strictly increasing on
[0,x_{\max}]
.

Now, consider the sequence defined by
t_{k}=T(t_{k-1})
for some initial value
t_{0}
.
If
t_{k}<t^{*}
, then
t_{k}<T(t_{k})<t^{*}
, so
\lvert T(t_{k})-t^{*}\rvert<\lvert t_{k}-t^{*}\rvert
.
If
t_{k}>t^{*}
then
T(t_{k})<t_{k}
and
T(t_{k})\geq t^{*}
, with equality if
t^{*}=t_{\max}
. Thus, in this case as well,
\lvert T(t_{k})-t^{*}\rvert<\lvert t_{k}-t^{*}\rvert
.
Therefore,
t_{k}
converges to
t^{*}
.
∎

Now that we have established the existence of the fixed point of
T
and shown how to compute it, we can use this result to find the solution of the proximal operator.

Theorem 6.2
.

Let
x\in\mathcal{H}^{n}
and
\mu\geq 0
.
Then
\proxOp_{\mu\|\cdot\|_{1}}(x)=p_{x}(t^{*})
, with
t^{*}
the unique fixed point of
T=\sigma_{x}\circ p_{x}
.

Proof.

As discussed in
Section

2
,

y^{*}=\proxOp_{\mu\|\cdot\|_{1}}(x)

is uniquely characterized by

0_{y^{*}}\in-\logOp_{y^{*}}(x)+\mu\partial^{\mathcal{H}^{n}}\|y^{*}\|_{1}
.
First, we show
\projOp_{y}(J\partial^{\mathbb{R}^{n+1}}\lVert y\rVert_{1})\subseteq\partial^{\mathcal{H}^{n}}\lVert y\rVert_{1}
, where

\partial^{\mathbb{R}^{n+1}}\lVert y\rVert_{1}=\left\{v\in\mathbb{R}^{n+1}\,\middle|\,v_{i}\in\begin{cases}\{\text{sign}(y_{i})\}&\text{ if }y_{i}\neq 0\\
[-1,1]&\text{ if }y_{i}=0\end{cases},\;i=1,\dots,n+1\right\},

is the Euclidean subgradient of
\lVert y\rVert_{1}
.
First, note that for any
v\in\partial^{\mathbb{R}^{n+1}}\lVert y\rVert_{1}
, we have

\langle v,y\rangle=\lVert y\rVert_{1}
.
Thus,
\lVert y\rVert_{1}+\langle v,z-y\rangle=\langle v,z\rangle
.
Hence,
v\in\partial^{\mathbb{R}^{n+1}}\lVert y\rVert_{1}
if and only if

\lVert z\rVert_{1}\geq\langle v,z\rangle

for all
z\in\mathbb{R}^{n+1}
.
Let
z\in\mathcal{H}^{n}
and write

\phi(s)=\frac{\text{arcosh}\left(s\right)}{\sqrt{s^{2}-1}}
, then

\displaystyle\lVert z\rVert_{1}

\displaystyle\geq\lVert\phi(-\langle y,z\rangle_{M})z\rVert_{1}

\displaystyle\geq\langle Jv,\phi(-\langle y,z\rangle_{M})z\rangle_{M}

\displaystyle=\langle Jv,\phi(-\langle y,z\rangle_{M})\projOp_{y}(z)\rangle_{M}-\langle Jv,\phi(-\langle y,z\rangle_{M})\langle y,z\rangle_{M}y\rangle_{M}

\displaystyle=\langle\projOp_{y}(Jv),\logOp_{y}{z}\rangle_{M}+(-\langle y,z\rangle_{M})\phi(-\langle y,z\rangle_{M})\langle v,y\rangle

\displaystyle\geq\langle\projOp_{y}(Jv),\logOp_{y}{z}\rangle_{M}+\lVert y\rVert_{1},

where we used that for
s\geq 1
,
\phi(s)\leq 1
and
s\phi(s)\geq 1
.
Thus,
\projOp_{y}(Jv)\in\partial^{\mathcal{H}^{n}}\lVert y\rVert_{1}
.

Let now
y^{*}=p_{x}(t^{*})
, where
t^{*}
the fixed point of
\sigma_{x}\circ p_{x}
and
v^{*}\in\partial^{\mathbb{R}^{n+1}}\lVert y^{*}\rVert_{1}
defined elementwise by
v^{*}_{i}=\text{sign}(y^{*}_{i})
if
y^{*}_{i}\neq 0
and
v^{*}_{i}=\frac{x_{i}}{t^{*}}
if
y^{*}_{i}=0
.
By rewriting the logarithmic map as a scaled projection onto the tangent space and using that
\sigma(y^{*})=t^{*}
, we get that
\logOp_{y^{*}}(x)=\mu\projOp_{y}(Jv^{*})
is equivalent to

x-t^{*}Jv^{*}=-\langle y^{*},x-t^{*}Jv^{*}\rangle_{M}y^{*}.

This equality holds because, by construction,
x-t^{*}Jv^{*}=y^{*}
and
\langle y^{*},y^{*}\rangle_{M}=-1
.
Hence,
\logOp_{y^{*}}(x)\in\mu\projOp_{y}(J\partial^{\mathbb{R}^{n+1}}\lVert y^{*}\rVert_{1})\subseteq\mu\partial^{\mathcal{H}^{n}}\|y^{*}\|_{1}
.
∎

Discussion

The results of the numerical experiment are shown in
Figure

2
.
The left plot shows the CPU runtime in seconds as a function of the manifold dimension, while the right plot shows the number of iterations to convergence.
As can be seen from the plots, CRPG with constant stepsize converges faster in time than the PGA except for one case.
The PGA in turn converges faster than CRPG with backtracking except a handful of cases.
The number of iterations is similar for CRPG in both variants, with the CRPG with constant stepsize requiring slightly fewer iterations than the backtracked case.
Interestingly, CRPG requires more iterations than the PGA, especially when the dimension is low, but still converges faster in terms of CPU runtime.

7.
Summary & Conclusion

In this article, we introduced a Riemannian proximal gradient method that works intrinsically on a Riemannian manifold for solving (possibly strongly) geodesically convex problems.
We established state-of-the-art convergence results on Hadamard manifolds with standard geodesic convexity assumptions.
We have demonstrated the efficiency of our method with several numerical experiments.
We also generalized the proximal gradient inequalities to Riemannian manifolds, which allowed us to derive known convergence rates for the proximal point algorithm and the gradient descent method on Hadamard manifolds.

Further research directions include investigating theoretical aspects of accelerated variants of CRPG, and studying other splitting methods based on proximal maps for solving optimization problems intrinsically on Riemannian manifolds.
