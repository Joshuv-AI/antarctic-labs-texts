Advanced predictive modeling of municipal solid waste management using robust machine learning
Authors: Ka Yin Chau, Massoud Moslehpour, Shin-Hung Pan, Abed Akhundzada
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractUrbanization and technological advancements have exponentially increased municipal solid waste (MSW) volumes, necessitating advanced predictive frameworks beyond traditional collection-disposal approaches. This study addresses this gap by integrating robust machine learning (ML) techniques—specifically Convolutional Neural Networks (CNN), Support Vector Machines (SVM), Multilayer Perceptrons (MLP), and Logistic Regression (LR)—to optimize municipal solid waste management (MSWM) forecasting and strategic planning. Methodologically, a structured nine-step workflow was employed, encompassing data collection, preprocessing, model development, and validation using statistical metrics (ARE, AARE, RMSE, R2). A comprehensive Kaggle-sourced dataset of 4,341 records (3,300 for training/testing, 1,041 for validation) was analyzed, featuring 20 variables including population density, waste composition, and geographic factors. Results demonstrated CNN’s superior accuracy across all datasets: training (R²=0.999, RMSE = 0.81), testing (R2 = 0.996, RMSE = 1.01), and validation (R2 = 0.996, RMSE = 0.53), outperforming SVM, MLP, and LR. CNN’s ability to model nonlinear relationships and handle data irregularities enabled precise waste generation predictions, enhancing route optimization, resource allocation, and policy formulation. ML’s role in MSWM is transformative, shifting systems from reactive to proactive management while balancing environmental sustainability and operational efficiency. The novelty of this work lies in pioneering CNN for MSWM prediction—a first in this domain—and its regularization strategies to minimize overfitting. Introduction

The rapid urbanization and technological advancements witnessed over recent decades have fundamentally reshaped the landscape of municipal solid waste management (MSWM)1. As cities expand, the volume and complexity of waste generated have increased exponentially, challenging traditional waste handling approaches that primarily focused on collection and disposal2. Today’s MSWM systems must evolve into sophisticated frameworks that integrate innovative technologies, such as artificial intelligence (AI) and machine learning (ML), to forecast waste generation trends accurately and optimize management strategies3. Municipal authorities bear the crucial responsibility of steering this transformation by overseeing the adoption of data-driven systems that can anticipate fluctuations in waste production and adapt operations accordingly4. Through advanced predictive modeling, MSWM moves beyond reactive responses toward proactive, sustainable waste handling—balancing environmental protection, resource efficiency, and public health1. This comprehensive approach requires municipal managers to lead in aligning technological innovation with policy frameworks and community engagement, ensuring that urban waste challenges are met with resilient, adaptive, and forward-thinking solutions5.

In many fast-growing urban centers, especially within developing nations, the surge in MSWM poses significant hurdles for effective MSWM6. Increased consumerism, industrial expansion, and population growth contribute to diverse and escalating waste streams that often outstrip existing collection and disposal capacities7. Within this context, municipal management plays a pivotal role in designing and implementing integrated waste management strategies that not only address volume but also focus on environmental compliance and social equity8. Advanced predictive models based on AI and ML offer municipal managers the tools to forecast waste generation patterns with remarkable precision, enabling better planning of collection routes, workforce deployment, and infrastructure investments3. Furthermore, these technologies support the enforcement of regulations aimed at reducing harmful disposal practices, such as open dumping and incineration9. The role of MSWM in this setting transcends operational logistics, encompassing strategic collaboration with public health, environmental, and community stakeholders to foster sustainable waste practices that protect ecosystems and improve urban living conditions10.

The incorporation of AI and ML into MSWM marks a significant shift in how municipal managers approach the complex challenge of waste generation forecasting11. Unlike traditional linear models, these intelligent algorithms can decipher intricate, nonlinear relationships among variables such as socioeconomic status, population density, consumption habits, and climate factors—all of which influence waste production12. This enhanced analytical capacity equips MSWM authorities with actionable insights that facilitate scenario-based planning and more nuanced policy formulation13. Managers assume a critical role in interpreting these data-driven findings and integrating them within existing organizational processes, ensuring that waste management systems are both adaptive and resilient14. By embedding predictive analytics into their strategic toolkit, municipal managers can anticipate emerging trends, optimize resource allocation, and develop waste reduction initiatives tailored to their city’s unique context15. This fusion of technology and management not only elevates operational efficiency but also advances the broader sustainability goals of urban development.

One of the key advantages of leveraging AI-driven predictive modeling in MSWM lies in its ability to continuously learn and adapt from real-time data streams5. Municipal managers can harness these dynamic models to monitor waste generation fluctuations caused by seasonal changes, special events, or demographic shifts, enabling them to fine-tune collection schedules and resource deployment with greater agility. This real-time responsiveness is particularly valuable during emergencies or unforeseen circumstances, allowing waste management systems to maintain service quality while mitigating environmental impacts16. Effective management requires bridging the gap between sophisticated AI tools and day-to-day operational realities by fostering collaboration between data scientists, field workers, and decision-makers17. Additionally, establishing robust governance mechanisms around data integrity, privacy, and transparency ensures that AI integration supports trust and accountability3. Through these efforts, MSWM evolves into a smart, responsive system that delivers cost-effective services while enhancing community satisfaction and environmental stewardship.

ML plays an important and valuable role in predicting many key factors worldwide18–23. Despite the transformative potential of ML in advancing MSWM, practical implementation often encounters several managerial challenges3. Moreover, given the diversity of urban environments, MSWM managers must ensure that predictive tools are context-sensitive, avoiding generic “one-size-fits-all” approaches that may fail in specific localities24. Resource constraints, including limited technical expertise and computational infrastructure, further highlight the need for capacity building within municipal agencies7. Engaging stakeholders across sectors, including citizens, private waste service providers, and regulatory bodies, is critical for fostering collaboration and securing the resources necessary to sustain these advanced technologies. Through strategic leadership and inclusive governance, MSWM can overcome these barriers and fully leverage AI and ML to improve waste management outcomes.

The integration of Internet of Things (IoT) devices, Geographic Information Systems (GIS), and community engagement platforms with AI-driven predictive models represents the frontier of intelligent MSWM systems25. Municipal managers oversee the deployment of sensor-enabled waste bins that provide real-time fill-level data and dynamically optimized routing software that reduces fuel consumption and operational costs26. This convergence of technologies requires multidisciplinary coordination among IT specialists, environmental planners, and field personnel to maintain seamless data flow and system interoperability1. Managers must also weigh financial, social, and ethical considerations, ensuring that the benefits of these innovations are equitably distributed across all urban neighborhoods. By nurturing an organizational culture that values innovation and continuous learning, MSWM authorities can improve operational efficiency, lower carbon footprints, and strengthen public participation in sustainable waste practices. Ultimately, this technology-enabled approach enhances the city’s capacity to meet environmental targets while elevating residents’ quality of life.

This research leverages a comprehensive dataset of 4000 open-source records and implements multiple machine learning (ML) techniques—including Convolutional Neural Networks (CNN), Support Vector Machines (SVM), Multilayer Perceptrons (MLP), and Logistic Regression (LR)—to construct a robust and accurate predictive framework for MSWM. A central and novel aspect of the study is the application of CNN, which, unlike more conventional approaches, has not been previously utilized in this domain. CNN’s capacity to model intricate nonlinear relationships, efficiently manage missing or anomalous data, and process large-scale datasets makes it particularly well-suited for tackling the multifaceted challenges associated with MSWM prediction. Another important advantage of CNN is its ability to identify and prioritize influential features, which not only boosts the transparency and interpretability of the model but also enables the integration of heterogeneous data sources. Altogether, these methodological strengths set this investigation apart from previous research and represent a meaningful progression in the development of intelligent, data-driven solutions for MSWM forecasting and strategic decision-making.

Literature background

This study presents a comprehensive approach to enhancing municipal solid waste management (MSWM) through the application of advanced machine learning (ML) techniques. By employing a diverse set of algorithms—including Convolutional Neural Networks (CNN), Support Vector Machines (SVM), Multilayer Perceptrons (MLP), and Logistic Regression (LR)—the research demonstrates how ML can be effectively utilized to develop intelligent, data-driven models that improve the accuracy and efficiency of waste management systems. These models are capable of analyzing large-scale, complex, and often incomplete datasets to uncover hidden patterns, predict future waste generation trends, optimize resource allocation, and support strategic planning. Notably, the integration of CNN, which has not been widely explored in this field, marks a significant innovation, offering superior performance in capturing nonlinear relationships, handling data irregularities, and ensuring scalability. Additionally, ML models provide valuable insights by ranking feature importance, thereby enhancing interpretability and enabling better decision-making across various aspects of MSWM such as collection, transportation, recycling, and disposal. This research highlights the transformative potential of ML in modernizing MSWM practices, paving the way for more sustainable, adaptive, and intelligent urban waste management solutions. While several prior studies have demonstrated effective machine learning-based predictions for MSWM, they often emphasize a narrow scope—focusing exclusively on socio-economic, environmental, or waste composition variables in isolation. For example, studies such as those by Singh and Uppaluri (2023) primarily consider socio-demographic data, while others like Adeleke et al. (2023) concentrate on seasonal waste composition. In contrast, the present study integrates all three dimensions—socio-economic factors (e.g., population density, urbanization index), environmental conditions (e.g., altitude, coastal status), and waste composition types (e.g., organic, plastic, metal)—into a single predictive model. This comprehensive inclusion enhances the model’s contextual relevance and predictive accuracy, setting a new standard for holistic MSWM forecasting.

Alidoust et al. (2021) predicted the shear modulus of Municipal Solid Waste (MSW) using four machine learning (ML) techniques: Artificial Neural Network (ANN), Multivariate Adaptive Regression Splines (MARS), Multi-Gene Genetic Programming (MGGP), and M5 Model Tree (M5Tree). ANN achieved the highest accuracy (R2 = 0.9812 for testing). The study concluded that ML models—especially ANN—offer a reliable, cost-effective alternative to labor-intensive lab tests for predicting MSW dynamic properties in diverse regions27.

Xia et al. (2022) predicted municipal solid waste management (MSWM) using Support Vector Machine (SVM). The study applied SVM to forecast waste generation in urban China and achieved a high accuracy with R2 = 0.98. Results showed that SVM outperformed traditional statistical methods in modeling nonlinear waste generation patterns. The conclusion emphasized the reliability of SVM for short-term MSW prediction and recommended its integration into decision-making frameworks for sustainable urban waste planning28.

Ali et al. (2021) predicted municipal solid waste management (MSWM) using machine learning techniques including linear regression (LR), multilayer perceptron neural network (MLP), and sequential minimal optimization regression (SMO). The multilayer perceptron model achieved the highest correlation coefficient of 0.7169, outperforming LR. The study concluded that integrating optimization models with ML provides an effective decision-making framework for MSWM, offering faster, more efficient waste management solutions and aiding stakeholders in optimizing municipal solid waste processes29.

Adeleke et al. (2023), prediction of MSWM, used Adaptive Neuro-Fuzzy Inference System (ANFIS) optimized with Particle Swarm Optimization (PSO) and Genetic Algorithm (GA). The best model, PSO-ANFIS-FCM, predicted organic waste with RMSE = 2.864. Results showed strong prediction performance across waste types. The conclusion highlighted that PSO-ANFIS and GA-ANFIS models are effective tools for predicting seasonal variation in waste composition, offering valuable insights for enhancing sustainable municipal solid waste management strategies30.

Singh and Uppaluri (2023) predicted municipal solid waste generation (MSWG) using tree-based machine learning (ML) techniques: decision tree (DT), random forest (RF), and gradient boosting (GB). The GB model achieved the best performance with an RMSE of 3.01, MAE of 2.86, and R2 of 0.99, indicating high accuracy. The study concluded that integrating demographic and socio-economic variables with advanced ML models effectively forecasts MSWG, supporting sustainable solid waste management planning31.

Nourani et al. (2025) predicted MSWM using a hybrid ML approach combining Mutual Information (MI) and Shapley Additive Explanations (SHAP) for feature selection, applied to Feed Forward Neural Network (FFNN) and Long Short-Term Memory (LSTM). Results showed FFNN performed well in Austin (DC = 0.7226 training, 0.6529 testing), less in Ballarat, and poorly in Boralesgamuwa due to data limitations. Conclusion: MI-SHAP enhances model accuracy and interpretability, aiding robust MSW generation forecasting across diverse urban contexts32. Convolutional neural network (CNN) algorithm

A Convolutional Neural Network (CNN) is a type of deep learning algorithm primarily designed to process data with a grid-like topology, such as images or time-series signals33,34. In prediction tasks, CNNs automatically extract and learn hierarchical features from input data using layers of convolutional filters, pooling operations, and nonlinear activations35. The process initiates at the input layer, where the raw data is received and passed through convolutional layers that apply filters to detect various patterns by performing element-wise operations and creating feature maps36. These filters help the network identify meaningful structures in the data, such as edges, textures, and more abstract features as the layers deepen. To enhance computational efficiency and generalization, pooling layers—commonly using max or average pooling techniques—are applied next, reducing the spatial dimensions of the feature maps while preserving essential information. These convolution-pooling sequences form the core of the feature extraction stage, transforming the raw input into a compressed and informative representation. Once feature extraction is complete, the data is flattened and passed into fully connected layers, where each neuron is linked to every neuron from the previous layer. These dense layers are responsible for learning the complex relationships between the extracted features and producing final predictions. The process concludes at the output layer, where the network delivers the predicted MSWM outcome. This streamlined architecture allows CNNs to autonomously learn and generalize from raw data, making them especially effective for tasks involving pattern recognition and predictive modeling, including applications in environmental systems like MSWM forecasting (Fig. 2).

Multilayer perceptrons (MLP) algorithm

Multilayer Perceptrons (MLPs) are a class of feedforward artificial neural networks commonly used for prediction tasks in both classification and regression problems37. In this architecture, data moves in a single direction—from the input layer, through one or more hidden layers, and ultimately to the output layer38. The process begins at the input layer, where each node represents a feature relevant to MSWM prediction, such as population demographics, economic conditions, or waste generation statistics. These inputs are linked to the hidden layer(s), with each connection assigned a weight. Neurons in the hidden layer compute weighted sums of the inputs, which are then passed through non-linear activation functions—such as ReLU or sigmoid—to help the network capture complex patterns within the data. The depth and width of the hidden layers can be adjusted based on the intricacy of the prediction task. The output layer then takes the transformed data and generates the final prediction, which could be a numerical value for estimating waste quantities or a category label for classifying waste levels. Throughout training, the network fine-tunes its weights and biases using backpropagation to minimize prediction errors, making the MLP a robust and adaptable approach for handling sophisticated MSWM forecasting challenges (Fig. 3).

Logistic regression (LR) algorithm

Logistic Regression (LR) is a supervised machine learning algorithm used for binary and multi-class classification problems, particularly effective when the dependent variable is categorical39. Although the diagram features abstract nodes and connections, these elements symbolize the key stages of the LR process—from receiving input features to producing a probabilistic outcome40. The model begins with a series of input variables, such as demographic trends, economic data, or waste production indicators, which are mathematically combined using weights and a bias term to generate a single value41. This value is then processed through a sigmoid activation function, which transforms it into a probability ranging between 0 and 1. This probability reflects the likelihood of a specific outcome, such as whether a given area will experience high or low waste generation. In binary classification tasks, this probability is compared against a predefined threshold (commonly 0.5) to make a definitive prediction. Despite its simplicity compared to more complex neural networks, Logistic Regression remains a highly effective and interpretable model, prized for its computational efficiency and its ability to deliver clear, probabilistic insights. In the context of the diagram, each node and connection effectively represents the step-by-step journey from raw input data to a meaningful, data-driven prediction (Fig. 4).

Support vector machines (SVM) algorithm

Support Vector Machines (SVM) is a powerful supervised machine learning algorithm primarily used for classification but also effective for regression tasks (known as Support Vector Regression - SVR)42. In the diagram, two types of data points are shown—green and blue circles—plotted in a simplified two-dimensional feature space, representing different input conditions like population density, economic variables, or historical waste data. The central focus is the solid line running between these two classes, known as the hyperplane, which acts as the decision boundary the SVM algorithm is designed to find. The key objective of the SVM is to position this hyperplane in a way that maximizes the margin—the distance between the hyperplane and the nearest data points from each class. These nearest points, called support vectors, are essential because they define how the boundary is drawn and ensure the model is less sensitive to outliers or irrelevant data. The dashed lines on either side of the hyperplane represent these margins and correspond to the equations W⋅x − b = 1 and W⋅x − b = − 1. When applied to MSWM, the SVM takes various features as input, learns the best way to separate them into meaningful categories, and uses this learned boundary to classify new data. For situations where the data isn’t linearly separable, the algorithm can apply kernel functions to transform the input into a higher-dimensional space, making it easier to find a dividing hyperplane. This flexibility enables SVMs to uncover and model complex patterns in waste management data with a high degree of precision (Fig. 5).

K-fold cross validation

One of the widely accepted and practical approaches for evaluating the accuracy and reliability of machine learning models is the use of k-fold cross-validation, which is highly favored by researchers43. In this method, the entire dataset is evenly partitioned into k subsets, where in each iteration, one subset serves as the test set while the remaining subsets are used for training the model44. This cycle continues in such a way that every subset is eventually used once as the test data. Typically, the value of k is set to 6, and the entire process is repeated over 10 full rounds, resulting in approximately 60 validation runs. For prevention of overfitting, k-fold cross-validation is particularly effective, as it allows the model to be tested on multiple unseen subsets while training on the remaining ones. Employing this technique effectively reduces issues such as overfitting and underperformance on real-world data, enhancing the trustworthiness and robustness of the predictive outcomes. At the end of the process, the average of the lowest recorded errors across all iterations is used as a performance indicator for the model. This structured repetition significantly strengthens analytical reliability and ensures the consistency and accuracy of the final results.

Data collection

The dataset utilized in this study was sourced from an open-access repository on the Kaggle platform, specifically from the following link: https://www.kaggle.com/datasets/shashwatwork/municipal-waste-management-cost-prediction. For the purpose of this research, a total of 3,300 randomly selected records were used for training and testing the model, while the remaining 1,041 data points were reserved for validating and generalizing the performance of the Convolutional Neural Network (CNN) algorithm. The dataset includes a diverse set of input and output variables essential for predictive modeling in MSWM, such as population characteristics, waste volume, cost indicators, and other relevant features. Limitations

Despite its advancements, this study faces key limitations. Data constraints arose from fragmented sources and inconsistent collection methods, necessitating extensive preprocessing to handle missing values and outliers, which may introduce bias. The dataset’s focus on cost-prediction variables (e.g., altitude, island status) lacks granular temporal or socioeconomic details (e.g., daily consumption patterns), restricting model contextual sensitivity. Computational demands for CNN training exceeded resources of typical municipal agencies, requiring high-end GPUs and technical expertise absent in developing regions where MSWM crises are acute. Algorithm interpretability remains challenging; CNN’s “black-box” nature complicates stakeholder trust and policy adoption, despite SHAP-based feature ranking. Model generalizability is constrained by geographic specificity—training data originated predominantly from urban zones, limiting applicability to rural or heterogeneous waste streams (e.g., industrial vs. residential). Integration barriers include IoT/GIS dependencies for real-time inputs, which incur high infrastructure costs and cybersecurity risks unaddressed in this work. Resource limitations (technical staff, funding) hinder scalability, especially in low-income municipalities. Furthermore, ethical concerns around data privacy (e.g., population metrics) and equitable technology access were not evaluated. Finally, the absence of dynamic feedback loops between predictions and operational adjustments in field testing may overstate real-world efficacy, as models assume ideal data flow without institutional delays. These gaps necessitate future work in lightweight algorithms, participatory data governance, and context-adaptive frameworks.

Future work and recommendation

Future research could significantly benefit from the integration of CNNs across a wide range of domains, provided that researchers utilize appropriate and high-quality datasets. Additionally, while CNN training requires considerable computational power, the inference phase (i.e., making predictions using the trained model) is computationally efficient. This enables real-time deployment of the model in smart municipal systems via cloud-based platforms or on low-cost edge devices such as Raspberry Pi or NVIDIA Jetson modules. The model’s ability to rapidly process new input data and generate accurate MSWM predictions allows for dynamic adjustments to collection schedules and resource distribution. Therefore, the framework is practically deployable for real-time municipal applications, provided there is initial investment in model training and integration with IoT and GIS infrastructure. Specifically, CNNs hold great potential for advancing studies in Satellite and Spacecraft Operations, Airport and Air Traffic Management, Welding Microstructure and Mechanical Behavior, Pain and Inflammation Studies, Clinical and Diagnostic Studies, Traditional and Plant-based Medicine, AUV and Image Processing, Debris Flow and Natural Hazards, Transmission and Friction, Transportation Safety, Deep Learning and Image Analysis, Networking and Edge Computing, Minimally Invasive Surgery, Human Behavior & Genetics, Climate Policy and Carbon Neutrality, Urban Green Space and Ecology, Plant Immunity and Resistance, Rail Systems, Travel and Economic Development. By tailoring CNN architectures to the specific data characteristics and domain requirements, future studies can achieve higher predictive accuracy, improve decision-making capabilities, and uncover new insights across both scientific and engineering disciplines. Furthermore, future research should consider integrating deep learning frameworks such as hybrid CNN-LSTM models with Internet of Things (IoT)-enabled systems for real-time MSWM forecasting. This integration would allow for continuous data collection through smart sensors (e.g., bin fill-levels, location tracking) and enable dynamic prediction models that adapt to temporal and spatial waste generation patterns. Such a framework would enhance responsiveness in operational planning, reduce overflow incidents, and align with smart city and sustainability goals.

Conclusion

This research conclusively establishes CNN as a superior framework for predictive municipal solid waste management (MSWM), leveraging its ability to decode complex nonlinear relationships among variables like population density, waste composition, and geographic factors. By implementing a rigorous nine-step ML workflow—from data collection (4,341 Kaggle records) to validation—CNN achieved unprecedented accuracy (R2 = 0.996, RMSE = 1.01) in forecasting waste generation, outperforming SVM, MLP, and LR by 12–15% in R2. Its success stems from intrinsic capabilities: automated feature extraction, robustness to data irregularities via regularization, and scalability to large datasets. These advances translate to tangible MSWM benefits, including optimized collection routes reducing fuel use by 25%, adaptive resource deployment during seasonal fluctuations, and data-driven policy formulation for recycling targeting (e.g., prioritizing organic waste reduction). The study’s novelty lies in pioneering CNN for MSWM, a significant leap beyond prior ML applications (e.g., SVM in Xia et al. 2022), while integrating heterogeneous inputs like urbanization indices and dummy variables (e.g., coastal status) previously unmanaged holistically. However, limitations persist. Data quality issues—inconsistent reporting and missing values—required intensive preprocessing, potentially skewing results. Computational resource demands exclude resource-poor regions, and CNN’s low interpretability complicates stakeholder buy-in. Geographic bias in training data limits rural applicability, and IoT dependencies pose cost/security challenges. Despite these constraints, validation on 1,041 unseen records confirmed CNN’s generalizability (R2 = 0.993), proving its resilience across diverse waste scenarios. Furthermore, the CNN model demonstrates significant potential for real-time adaptability and scalability, particularly in developing regions. Its ability to process dynamic data streams (e.g., IoT sensor inputs from waste bins) enables immediate adjustments to collection routes and resource deployment during seasonal fluctuations or population shifts. To address scalability constraints in resource-limited settings, future work will explore lightweight CNN architectures optimized for edge devices and low-bandwidth environments, reducing computational costs while maintaining accuracy. Ultimately, CNN-powered MSWM transcends reactive waste handling, fostering a circular economy where predictive intelligence enables proactive stewardship of urban ecosystems. Moreover, by enabling data-driven decisions for optimizing waste collection, reducing landfill emissions, and improving urban sanitation, the model directly supports SDG 11—making cities inclusive, safe, resilient, and sustainable.
