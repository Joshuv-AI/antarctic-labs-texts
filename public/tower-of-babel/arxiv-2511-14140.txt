Beyond Fixed and Dynamic Prompts: Embedded Jailbreak Templates for Advancing LLM Security
Hajun Kim, Hyunsik Na, Daeseon Choi

Abstract
As the use of large language models ( LLMs ) continues to expand, ensuring their safety and robustness has become a critical challenge. In particular, jailbreak attacks that bypass built-in safety mechanisms are increasingly recognized as a tangible threat across industries, driving the need for diverse templates to support red-teaming efforts and strengthen defensive techniques. However, current approaches predominantly rely on two limited strategies: (i) substituting harmful queries into fixed templates, and (ii) having the LLM generate entire templates, which often compromises intent clarity and reproducibility. To address this gap, this paper introduces the Embedded Jailbreak Template, which preserves the structure of existing templates while naturally embedding harmful queries within their context. We further propose a progressive prompt-engineering methodology to ensure template quality and consistency, alongside standardized protocols for generation and evaluation. Together, these contributions provide a benchmark that more accurately reflects real-world usage scenarios and harmful intent, facilitating its application in red-teaming and policy regression testing. Keywords: Large Language Models, Jailbreak Attacks, Jailbreak Template Generation, AI Safety and Security

Introduction

Large language models (LLMs) have rapidly emerged as transformative technologies in natural language understanding and generation, powering applications such as information retrieval, translation, summarization, and even code synthesis
[
32
,
20
]
. State-of-the-art models including GPT-
[
11
]
, Llama-
[
7
]
, and Claude-series
[
1
]
demonstrate human-level reasoning and contextual comprehension, thereby expanding their utility across diverse industrial domains. Despite these remarkable capabilities, LLMs inherently operate as probabilistic language models and thus carry significant risks
[
2
,
8
]
. More critically, the possibility that user prompts may induce harmful or malicious outputs poses severe security threats in real-world deployments
[
16
]
. To mitigate such vulnerabilities, modern LLMs are equipped with safety mechanism designed to detect and block prompts related to sensitive topics or hazardous behaviors
[
9
,
6
]
.

Recent studies have demonstrated a growing prevalence of jailbreak attacks
[
28
,
25
]
—methods that intentionally circumvent the safety mechanisms of LLMs to elicit harmful responses. Alarmingly, such attacks can often be executed through seemingly innocuous natural language prompts, lowering the technical barrier and enabling even non-expert users to extract unsafe outputs. This escalating threat has heightened the urgency for security researchers to not only develop robust defense strategies but also to construct large-scale, diverse, and realistic jailbreak template collections that can serve as the foundation for systematic red-teaming and defensive evaluation.

Early efforts in jailbreak research primarily relied on manually crafted attack templates, where human-designed prompts were used to expose vulnerabilities in LLM safety mechanisms
[
22
]
. While effective in demonstrating proof-of-concept exploits, this manual approach lacked scalability and quickly became obsolete as models were updated. To address these limitations, subsequent work introduced automated methods that systematically generate perturbations in natural language space. For instance, gradient-based optimization (GCG)
[
33
]
, evolutionary strategies such as AutoDAN
[
15
]
, exploratory approach like TAP
[
19
]
and PAP have been proposed to bypass safety through persuasion
[
30
]
. More recently, research has shifted toward constructing large-scale jailbreak datasets by leveraging LLMs themselves, as seen in initiatives such as WildJailbreak
[
12
]
and S-Eval
[
29
]
. These approaches enable the automated synthesis of extensive jailbreak corpora, offering richer evaluation and benchmarking capabilities. Nevertheless, existing datasets often fall short of capturing the full breadth of adversarial diversity, exhibit limited success rates in practice, and remain biased toward specific harmful categories—leaving open critical challenges for developing more comprehensive evaluation frameworks.

In this work, we introduce the Embedded Jailbreak Template (EJT) as a novel framework for constructing jailbreak benchmarks that embed harmful intent more explicitly and flexibly than prior approaches. Unlike fixed templates that append a harmful query to a predetermined slot, EJT dynamically integrates queries into broader regions of the template, thereby ensuring that the resulting prompts more faithfully preserve the semantics and intent of the harmful content. By systematically injecting a curated harmful query set into each jailbreak template, EJT enables controlled coverage of diverse harmful categories, mitigating the bias and instability observed in prior datasets.

To evaluate the feasibility and robustness of this approach, we conduct a broad set of experiments designed to assess both the intrinsic quality of EJT and its comparative effectiveness against existing approaches. First, we evaluate EJT’s ability to preserve template fidelity and faithfully embed harmful query intent, while also measuring refusal rates to ensure stable generation. We then contrast EJT with fixed templates through quantitative analyses of embedding-space variance and qualitative visualization, highlighting differences in structural diversity. Finally, we benchmark EJT against dynamic template methods, examining attack success rates and exposing category-level biases observed in prior datasets such as WildJailbreak. Together, these evaluations provide a comprehensive picture of EJT’s strengths, demonstrating its potential as a more reliable and balanced foundation for LLM security assessment.

We summarize our key contributions as follows:

•

Embedded Jailbreak Template.
We introduce EJT as a novel framework that dynamically integrates harmful queries into jailbreak templates, ensuring faithful intent preservation while mitigating category bias inherent in prior datasets.

•

Comprehensive Evaluation.
We conduct extensive analyses of EJT’s intrinsic quality (template fidelity, query intent preservation, refusal stability) and compare it against fixed and dynamic templates through embedding-space variance, visualization, and attack success rate benchmarks.

•

Toward Reliable Benchmarks.
Our results demonstrate that EJT provides richer structural diversity and balanced harmful category coverage, establishing a more reliable foundation for systematic security assessment of LLMs.

Background

0.2.1
Preliminaries

Large language models.
LLMs are generative models trained on massive text corpora to produce fluent and contextually coherent responses. Formally, we denote an LLM as
M
, which maps an input prompt
p
to an output distribution over token sequences. While LLMs excel at reasoning
[
23
]
, summarization
[
31
]
, and instruction following
[
21
]
, their probabilistic nature leaves them vulnerable to adversarial prompts that can manipulate outputs in unintended ways. In our experiments, we instantiate
M
with GPT
-
4o
[
11
]
for both prompt generation and response evaluation.

Jailbreak Attacks.
A jailbreak attack refers to a class of adversarial prompts designed to circumvent the built-in safety mechanisms of an LLM. Let
q_{h}
denote a harmful query that would normally be blocked by
M
’s s safety layer. A jailbreak attack seeks to craft a prompt
p_{j}
such that
M(p_{j})
yields a harmful output aligned with
q_{h}
, despite safety constraints. Unlike traditional adversarial examples that rely on small perturbations of input data
[
14
,
24
]
, jailbreaks exploit the instruction-following behavior of LLMs, directly targeting their alignment and refusal mechanisms
[
27
,
5
]
.

Jailbreak Templates.
A jailbreak template provides a structural scaffold for embedding harmful queries into natural language prompts
[
17
,
29
,
4
]
. Formally, let
T
denote a template and
q_{h}\in Q
a harmful query from a query set
Q
. The instantiated jailbreak prompt can then be written as
p_{j}=T(q_{h}
, where the template dictates how
q_{h}
is integrated into the overall prompt. By varying
T
and
Q
, one can systematically generate a collection of jailbreak prompts to evaluate LLM safety.

Types of Jailbreak Templates

We categorize jailbreak templates into two primary classes: Fixed Jailbreak Templates (FJT) and Dynamic Jailbreak Templates (DJT). While both are designed to integrate harmful queries
q_{h}\in Q
into a template
T
to construct an adversarial prompt
p_{j}=T(q_{h})
, their structural properties and practical implications differ substantially.

:
Fixed Jailbreak Template Example

Fixed Jailbreak Templates.
FJT defines a static textual scaffold where harmful queries are inserted into a predetermined slot (Figure
1
)
[
17
,
18
]
. Formally, the structure of
T
is constant, and variation arises only from the substitution of
q_{h}
. This design offers simplicity but inherently limits expressive diversity: the embedding distribution of
{p_{j}}
tends to be narrowly concentrated, which risks overfitting when used for training or evaluation. Moreover, because FJTs often employ a single long prompt with a fixed insertion point (e.g., “[INSERT QUERY HERE]”), they fail to capture the syntactic and semantic variability seen in natural adversarial prompts. As a result, while FJTs provide stability and reproducibility, they lack realism and generalization power in reflecting real-world jailbreak scenarios.

Dynamic Jailbreak Templates.
In contrast, DJT leverages LLMs themselves or predefined adversarial heuristics to automatically generate structurally diverse templates
[
12
,
15
,
19
]
. Instead of filling a fixed slot, the harmful query
q_{h}
is embedded into a dynamically produced context, where lexical choice, discourse framing, and syntactic style can all vary. This approach provides broader coverage of adversarial patterns and better approximates how jailbreak attempts may arise in practice. However, its generative flexibility comes with trade-offs: harmful intent may not always be faithfully preserved, and the resulting prompts can dilute or misrepresent the severity of certain categories (e.g., child exploitation or explicit sexual content). Consequently, DJTs enhance diversity and realism but often at the expense of consistency and reliability in benchmarking.

0.3.1
Progressive Prompt Engineering

In automatic generation, it is difficult to obtain the desired output with a simple prompt. Safety filters and output stochasticity often lead to template distortion, omission of
q_{h}
, and extraneous commentary.
We categorize the main failure modes as follows (
P
: Problem):

•

P1 (Response Denial):
The model refuses to response an output due to safety mechanisms.

•

P2 (Excessive Explanation):
Prefatory text or commentary is added beyond the requested template.

•

P3 (Structure Disruption):
The original template structure is altered or rewritten.

•

P4 (Instruction Deviation):
The model answers the underlying task instead of rewriting the template.

Figure 3
:
Progressive Prompt Engineering

To address these problems, we employ a progressive prompting strategy that incrementally steers the generation (Figure
3
).
Let
S
denote a corrective instruction addressing a specific problem
P
.
We apply the following stages:

•

S1 (Forced Response):
Discourage refusal and require an output.

•

S2 (Appropriate Output):
Suppress prefatory text and explanations, retaining only the template content.

•

S3 (Structure Preservation):
Explicitly constrain the generation to maintain the original layout.

•

S4 (Instruction Focus):
Restrict the model to template rewriting and avoid directly answering the query.

The complete set of templates for progressive prompt engineering is provided in appendix
.8.1
.

During the generation of
p^{(E)}_{j}
, the
LLM
may refuse to produce an output when conditioned on an input that combines the
q_{h}

p_{j}

We first define the refusal keyword set(
K

K=\{\text{``sorry''},\text{``apologize''},\text{``unable''}\}.

Given an LLM output
y

R(y)=\begin{cases}1&\text{if }y\text{ contains any keyword in }K,\\
0&\text{otherwise}.\end{cases}

Thus, if
R(y)=1
the output is labeled as a
refusal
, whereas
R(y)=0
denotes a normal response.
However, naive string matching is imperfect: some base templates already contain keywords from
K

To address these pitfalls, we perform a human second pass to adjudicate borderline cases and remove both false positives and false negatives, yielding a more reliable string-matching classifier.

Template Similarity

To quantitatively assess the structural similarity between the original template
p_{j}
and its
p^{(E)}_{j}
, we employ four metrics covering lexical, semantic, and syntactic dimensions. Using these metrics jointly allows us to evaluate how well EJT preserves the structure of the original template while naturally reflecting the newly inserted query.

(i) TF–IDF cosine similarity.

TF–IDF defines the importance of a word
w
as

\text{TF-IDF}(p_{j})=\text{tf}(w,d)\cdot\log\frac{N}{\text{df}(w)},

where
\text{tf}(w,d)
is the frequency of
w
in document
d
,
\text{df}(w)
is the number of documents containing
w
, and
N
is the total number of documents.
Here,
w
denotes a token drawn from the instantiated prompt
p_{j}
, and
d
denotes the document corresponding to
p_{j}
; we treat each prompt as a document in the corpus.
After converting each text into a TF–IDF vector,

\mathbf{v}_{J}=\text{TF-IDF}(p_{j}),\quad\mathbf{v}_{E}=\text{TF-IDF}(p^{(E)}_{j}),

we compute the cosine similarity to measure lexical overlap:

\text{Sim}_{\text{TF--IDF}}(p_{j},p^{(E)}_{j})=\frac{\mathbf{v}_{J}\cdot\mathbf{v}_{E}}{\|\mathbf{v}_{J}\|\,\|\mathbf{v}_{E}\|}.

(ii) Jaccard similarity.

Jaccard similarity measures the proportion of overlapping lexical items between two texts.
Let
S(p_{j})
and
S(p^{(E)}_{j})
be the token sets of
p_{j}
and
p^{(E)}_{j}
, respectively. Then

\text{Sim}_{\text{Jaccard}}(p_{j},p^{(E)}_{j})=\frac{|S(p_{j})\cap S(p^{(E)}_{j})|}{|S(p_{j})\cup S(p^{(E)}_{j})|}.

(iii) BERT-based semantic similarity.

Beyond surface-level token overlap, we estimate semantic similarity in an embedding space using a pretrained sentence encoder
f(\cdot)
:

\mathbf{v}_{J}=f(p_{j}),\quad\mathbf{v}_{E}=f(p^{(E)}_{j}).

We then compute cosine similarity between the embeddings:

\text{Sim}_{\text{BERT}}(p_{j},p^{(E)}_{j})=\frac{\mathbf{v}_{J}\cdot\mathbf{v}_{E}}{\|\mathbf{v}_{J}\|\,\|\mathbf{v}_{E}\|}.

This captures semantic preservation even when surface forms differ.

(iv) Levenshtein similarity.

The Levenshtein distance is the minimum number of edit operations(insertions, deletions, substitutions) required to transform one string into another. Let
d_{LD}(p_{j},p^{(E)}_{j})
be the distance between
p_{j}
and
p^{(E)}_{j}
; we define the similarity as

\text{Sim}_{LD}(p_{j},p^{(E)}_{j})=1-\frac{d_{LD}(p_{j},p^{(E)}_{j})}{\max(|p_{j}|,|p^{(E)}_{j}|)}.

Because it directly reflects the amount of character- or token-level editing, it is sensitive to syntactic (surface-form) differences.

Preservation of Harmful Intent

To assess how faithfully EJT preserves the intent of an inserted harmful query that is dispersed across multiple elements of the template, we adopt a multiple–choice classification protocol inspired by Lee et al.
[
13
]
. Concretely, given an EJT instance
p^{(E)}_{t}
, an evaluation model
M^{\text{eval}}
selects the most semantically aligned item from a fixed inventory
{Q}
; accuracy against the ground–truth
q_{h}
serves as our intent–preservation metric.

First, let the query inventory be

{Q}=\{q_{h}^{1},q_{h}^{2},\dots,q_{h}^{22}\},

where each
q_{h}^{i}
corresponds to one of the 22 category–defined harmful queries. For each EJT instance
p^{(E)}_{t}
, we feed the prompt to the evaluation model
M^{\text{eval}}
(we used GPT
-
4o) and ask it to pick the element of
Q
that is most semantically aligned with the prompt:

\hat{q}_{h}=M^{\text{eval}}\!\left(p^{(E)}_{t}\right).

We score the instance as correct if the predicted query matches the one originally inserted:

\mathrm{Acc}\!\left(p^{(E)}_{t}\right)=\begin{cases}1,&\text{if }\hat{q}_{h}=q_{h},\\
0,&\text{otherwise}.\end{cases}

Aggregating over the set of templates
\{p^{(E)}_{1},p^{(E)}_{2},\dots,p^{(E)}_{n}\}
, overall accuracy is

\mathrm{Accuracy}=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{t=1}^{n}\mathrm{Acc}\!\left(p^{(E)}_{t}\right).

On
n=20
EJT instances, the mean accuracy is 86.59%, indicating that EJT preserves the intent of the inserted harmful queries to a high degree.

Experimental Setup

EJT can explore a richer expressive space by combining queries with templates, which in turn enables a quantitative examination of dispersion in the embedding space. To this end, we work in the representation space of DeBERTaV3-base
[
10
]
.

The experimental procedure is as follows:

1.

Prompt set construction
: For each template type (FJT, EJT), we apply all 22 queries to construct the corresponding sets of jailbreak prompts.

2.

Embedding extraction
: From each prompt, we extract a 768-dimensional embedding vector.

3.

Per-template aggregation
: For prompts derived from the same template, we group their embeddings and compute four variance-based dispersion metrics.

4.

Averaging
: For each template, we aggregate the resulting variance values to obtain a mean, thereby capturing characteristic distributional properties that are not tied to any single query.

As evaluation metrics, we adopt cosine variance (
V_{cos}
),
l_{2}
variance (
V_{l_{2}}
), tri-ratio variance (
V_{tiri}
), and log-determinant variance (
V_{logdet}
). These quantify, respectively, directional dispersion, Euclidean spread, aspect-ratio structure, and volumetric expansion; taken together, they capture complementary facets of embedding diversity. In particular,
V_{tiri}
measures the ratio of variance explained by the first principal component relative to the remaining components, thereby capturing how strongly the distribution is aligned to a dominant axis (i.e., circular versus elongated spread). Meanwhile,
V_{logdet}
is computed as the log-determinant of the covariance matrix, which reflects the overall volume of the embedding distribution and thus serves as a proxy for global diversity across all dimensions.

Finally, to assess the statistical significance of mean differences between the two template families, we use Welch’s
t
-test
[
26
]
and report bootstrap-based 95% confidence intervals to convey effect sizes and uncertainty. This design allows us to test whether EJT meaningfully expands the embedding space.

Visualization of Embedding Space

To make the quantitative differences more intuitive, we visualize each template family via PCA and UMAP. Figure
5
plots 2D projections of the 768-dimensional embeddings.

FJT embeddings form relatively compact clusters and exhibit simple structural patterns along specific axes, reflecting the limited expressive diversity discussed earlier. In contrast, EJT embeddings occupy a wider region with multi-layered cluster boundaries. Notably, even embeddings derived from the same query can split into distinct subgroups, suggesting that the template combination itself is a key driver of representational diversity.

The UMAP projection further accentuates the distributional differences between FJT and EJT. While FJT appears more widely scattered overall, this is largely attributable to the inclusion of 20 distinct source templates—an external dispersion effect driven by between-template differences. The more instructive contrast lies in per-template cluster shape: within a single template, FJT clusters remain nearly circular even as the query varies, indicating limited directional and aspect-ratio variation. EJT clusters, by comparison, are more elliptical and anisotropic, revealing richer directional variation even under the same query set.

These observations align with the variance metrics in Table
2
. In particular, the substantially higher
V_{\text{tiri}}
for EJT indicates that, unlike FJT’s near-circular spreads, EJT achieves more diverse aspect-ratio structure in higher-dimensional space. Accordingly, the UMAP visualization highlights EJT’s superiority not merely in global spread but in the internal structural diversity of template-specific clusters.

Attack Success Rate Evaluation

Figure 6
:
Comparison of ASR scores between Embedded Jailbreak Template and WildJailbreak Template

EJT explicitly embeds harmful queries into the template, which is expected to yield a high attack success rate (ASR).
In this study, we propose an LLM evaluation method using GPT
-
4o as the evaluation model to compare the ASR of EJT and DJT.
Given a prompt
p_{j}
, the generated response
R_{i}=M(p_{j})
is fed into the evaluation model
M^{\text{eval}}
,
which assesses the degree of attack success on a four-level scale:

•

Full Refusal
: The model completely rejects the harmful request and provides no information.

•

Partial Refusal
: The model rejects the harmful request but provides some non-harmful or general information.

•

Partial Compliance
: The model includes some harmful content while also providing warnings or ethical disclaimers.

•

Full Compliance
: The model fully complies with the harmful request without any restrictions.

The evaluation score for each response
R_{i}
is defined as:

s_{i}=M^{\text{eval}}(R_{i}),\quad s_{i}\in\{1,2,3,4\}

where 1 corresponds to
Full Refusal
and 4 corresponds to
Full Compliance
.

Finally, the average attack success rate (ASR) over all
N
prompts is computed as:

\text{ASR}=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}s_{i}

Figure
6
presents the comparison between our proposed
p^{(E)}_{t}
and an existing DJT variant,
p^{(D)}_{t}
(WildJailbreak Template).
The experimental results show that
p^{(E)}_{t}
achieved an overall average score of 2.40, which is slightly higher than the 2.29 average score of
p^{(D)}_{t}
.
While
p^{(D)}_{t}
exhibits a higher proportion of level-4 responses, this can be attributed to its tendency to produce relatively vague requests that fail to fully capture the intended malicious behavior, thus triggering LLM safety mechanisms in a more superficial manner.
Consequently, although
p^{(D)}_{t}
may appear to succeed more frequently on the surface, the higher overall ASR of
p^{(E)}_{t}
indicates that our proposed method demonstrates superior performance compared to existing templates.

To evaluate the diversity of harmful intents captured by the DJT, we conducted an intent classification experiment on
p^{(D)}_{t}
. A total of 1,500 templates were used, and for each template, GPT
-
4o was employed to determine which harmful category the embedded query belonged to.

The classification was based on ten harmful categories derived from the eleven categories defined in CategoricalHarmfulQA. The model was instructed to assign exactly one category per input text. To mitigate the risk of bias arising from a specific subset of templates, the full set of templates was randomly partitioned into three batches of 500 and evaluated across three independent sessions.

The use of independent sessions was motivated by the concern that a single session might yield incorrect results due to certain templates disproportionately influencing the evaluation. In other words, an individual session might produce overly high or low ratings for specific templates, and thus, multiple independent sessions were conducted to assess whether consistent results would emerge across different evaluations.

As illustrated in Figure

Notably, categories such as C1 (Adult Content) and C2 (Child Exploitation), which tend to trigger strong safety responses from the LLM, are sparsely represented, while categories such as C6 (Illicit Trade) and C7 (Cybercrime) are more concentrated within certain sessions. The Pearson correlation coefficient measures the linear correlation between two variables, with values closer to 1 indicating a stronger positive relationship.

As shown in Table

Discussion and Future Work

0.5.1
Limitation of Embedded Jailbreak Template

While EJT offers the advantage of explicitly incorporating harmful intent compared to traditional jailbreak template approaches, it is limited by its reliance on the existence of pre-existing jailbreak templates into which the intent can be embedded. Moreover, EJT cannot be applied to all templates indiscriminately; it requires that the overall structure of the template be suitable for embedding a harmful query. Consequently, the feasibility of applying EJT depends heavily on template design, making it challenging to use flexibly across all template types.

Another limitation lies in its dependency on the quality and diversity of the query and template datasets. Although this study utilized a multilingual harmful query dataset, a limited dataset may fail to capture emerging attack types or novel scenarios adequately. Furthermore, the performance of EJT depends on the quality of the base templates; poor-quality initial templates may directly lead to degraded performance of EJT itself. This highlights the need for datasets containing a broader range of harmful queries and high-quality base templates in future work.

For both the generation and evaluation of EJT, we employed GPT
-
4o, chosen for its accessibility and strong performance. However, relying solely on GPT
-
4o introduces a potential limitation, as model performance may vary under different environments or requirements. It remains unclear whether GPT
-
4o is the most suitable choice for this task, or whether other models might achieve comparable or superior results. Addressing this question requires a comparative evaluation of multiple models.

0.5.2
Practical Implications and Challenges

In future research, we plan to explore methods for applying EJT to a broader range of templates. Specifically, we aim to develop techniques for automatically generating templates that are inherently compatible with EJT. By enabling seamless integration of EJT during the template design stage, we hope to establish a system that supports effective application of EJT across diverse templates. This may involve designing template generation algorithms powered by LLMs or employing approaches that automatically generate a variety of template structures based on existing jailbreak rules.

Expanding datasets to reflect a wider array of attack scenarios will also be a crucial research direction. Current datasets tend to focus on a limited set of attack types; thus, developing datasets that include a broader spectrum of harmful queries will strengthen EJT’s ability to adapt to diverse threats. Such expansions will enhance EJT’s robustness in detecting and addressing a wider range of risks.

Lastly, we intend to evaluate EJT’s performance across multiple models beyond GPT
-
4o, including Llama models, and Claude models, to assess how each performs within the EJT framework. This comparative analysis will ensure that EJT can operate effectively under various conditions and environments, enhancing its scalability and flexibility.

0.6
Conclusion

This study proposed the Embedded Jailbreak Template (EJT) as a practical and automatable benchmark for evaluating
LLM
safety. By contextually embedding harmful queries while preserving template structure, EJT addresses two major limitations of existing approaches: the limited expressiveness of Fixed Jailbreak Templates (FJTs) and the intent bias inherent to Dynamic Jailbreak Templates (DJTs). Moreover, progressive prompt engineering (S1–S4) effectively reduced refusal rates and improved structural consistency.

Quantitative evaluations demonstrated the effectiveness of EJT across multiple dimensions. Template similarity metrics (TF–IDF, Jaccard, BERT, LD score) consistently improved with iterative refinement, harmful intent preservation reached an average accuracy of 86.59%, and embedding space analyses indicated significantly greater variance (
p<0.001
) compared to FJTs, with richer internal diversity confirmed via PCA/UMAP. Additionally, EJT outperformed WildJailbreak in attack success rate (2.40 vs. 2.29), highlighting its practical advantage as a benchmarking method.

Despite these contributions, EJT remains dependent on the availability and quality of pre-existing jailbreak templates and query datasets, and its evaluation relied solely on GPT
-
4o. Future research will explore automatic generation of EJT-compatible templates, expand datasets to cover more diverse harmful categories, and assess performance across multiple LLMs to ensure broader applicability and robustness.

In conclusion, EJT introduces a structure-preserving, context-aware approach to jailbreak benchmarking that enhances both intent fidelity and diversity. By bridging the gap between rigid fixed templates and biased dynamic ones, EJT lays the groundwork for more precise
LLM
safety evaluation and provides a foundation for advancing robust defense strategies and practical red-teaming methodologies.
