Associations of long-term vitamin K antagonist and direct oral anticoagulant therapies with progression of coronary artery calcification: A Danish population-based study
Authors: Selma Hasific, Oke Gerke, Kristian Altern Øvrehus, Jesper Hallas, Jes S Lindholt, Jess Lambrechtsen, Flemming Hald Steffensen, Lars Frost, Marek Karon, Axel Diederichsen
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractAimsVitamin K antagonists (VKAs) may promote vascular calcification through inhibition of vitamin K-dependent proteins. We aimed to examine the association between long-term anticoagulant therapy with VKA or direct oral anticoagulants (DOACs) and progression of coronary artery calcification (CAC) in a large, population-based cohort.MethodsWe included men and women (<75 years) with two non-contrast CT scans for CAC ≥4 years apart, categorized into VKA, DOAC, mixed anticoagulant, and control groups. Cumulative treatment duration was calculated from prescription data. CAC progression was analysed using zero-inflated negative binomial and multinomial logistic regression, adjusting for traditional cardiovascular risk factors, baseline CAC and follow-up time. Sensitivity analyses excluded participants predisposed to vascular mineralization (statin use, mechanical valve, renal impairment).Results7365 participants with similar risk profile were included. VKA therapy was significantly associated with CAC progression (incidence rate ratio (IRR) = 1.004 per month of treatment (95% CI: 1.000-1.007)), showing a duration–response relationship. Long-term VKA therapy (>48months) was independently associated with higher CAC progression (IRR = 1.43 (95% CI: 1.076-1.908)), and an increased risk of having severe CAC (CAC score ≥400) at follow-up (relative risk ratio (RRR) = 1.012 per month of treatment (95% CI: 1.001-1.023)). In contrast, DOAC therapy was not associated with CAC progression. Sensitivity analyses confirmed that the association with VKA was not driven by high-risk subgroups.ConclusionIn this large, real-world cohort, VKA - but not DOAC - therapy was associated with accelerated, duration-dependent CAC progression. These findings suggest that choice of anticoagulant may influence CAC, yet the clinical implications remain to be determined.Keywords: Computed tomography, Coronary artery calcification, Vitamin K antagonists, Direct oral anticoagulants

Introduction

Vitamin K antagonists (VKAs; e.g., warfarin), have been fundamental in oral anticoagulation therapy for decades, primarily used to prevent thromboembolic events in patients with atrial fibrillation, venous thromboembolism and mechanical heart valves [1]. VKAs exert their anticoagulant effect by inhibiting the vitamin K dependent carboxylation and activation of clotting factors. However, this mechanism also affects other vitamin K-dependent proteins, notably matrix Gla protein (MGP), which plays a crucial role in inhibiting vascular calcification. Inhibition of MGP activation by VKAs has been associated with increased vascular and valvular calcification, potentially accelerating the progression of atherosclerosis and increasing the risk of coronary artery disease (CAD) and cardiovascular events [2,3].

Direct oral anticoagulants (DOACs; e.g., apixaban, dabigatran, edoxaban, rivaroxaban) have emerged as effective alternatives to VKAs, offering comparable or superior efficacy and safety profiles without interfering with vitamin K metabolism [4]. Unlike VKAs, DOACs do not inhibit MGP activation, suggesting a potentially more favorable impact on vascular health. Observational studies have indicated that patients on DOACs may have a lower risk of vascular calcification and related cardiovascular events compared to those on VKAs.​ [5,6].

Despite these findings, direct evidence comparing vascular calcification progression among VKA and DOAC users, and the general population remains limited. The existing randomised controlled trials often have restrictive inclusion criteria, modest sample sizes and short follow-up periods, which may limit their ability to capture the full spectrum of vascular effects associated with long-term anticoagulant use [[7], [8], [9], [10]]. Therefore, large-scale, real-world data may provide important insights to the impact of VKAs and DOACs on CAD progression in diverse patient populations.

In this registry-based study, we aim to examine the association between VKA, DOAC or no use of anticoagulation and progression of coronary artery calcification (CAC). We hypothesize that patients receiving VKA therapy will exhibit greater CAC progression than both DOAC-treated patients and the background population, consistent with inhibition of vitamin K-dependent anti-calcific mechanisms.

Methods

This study followed the Reporting of studies Conducted using Observational Routinely-collected health Data (RECORD) guidelines [11].

Study design, population and data sources

The study is designed as a longitudinal, observational cohort study aiming to evaluate the progression of CAC among individuals exposed to either VKAs or DOACs compared to a background population. We included men and women below the age of 75 years who had undergone two non-contrast computed tomography (CT) scans for CAC assessment with a minimum interval of four years between scans. This time frame was chosen to ensure sufficient follow-up for meaningful evaluation of CAC progression. Subjects with missing examination dates, baseline or follow-up CAC scores, or incomplete baseline cardiovascular risk factor information (sex, age, body mass index (BMI), family history of cardiovascular disease (CVD), diabetes, hypercholesterolemia, hypertension, renal impairment, smoking status) were excluded. If more than two CT scans were performed, the two with maximum inter-scan interval were included. Participants with prior coronary revascularization, including stent implantation or coronary artery bypass grafting (CABG), were excluded to ensure accurate and unbiased CT-based CAC assessment and to evaluate the effect of VKAs and DOACs.

The study population was identified through multiple complementary data sources in Western Denmark. The primary source was the Western Denmark Heart Registry (WDHR), which captures detailed clinical and imaging data from three out of the five Danish regions and includes all participants undergoing clinically indicated coronary CT scans since 2007 [12]. In addition, data were obtained from two population-based research cohorts; DanRisk (DANish Risk score study) – initiated in 2009 to investigate cardiovascular risk factors and subclinical atherosclerosis, including standardized CAC measurements, [13]; and DANCAVAS (DANish CArdioVAscular Screening trial) – a large randomised cardiovascular screening trial initiated in 2014 including primarily men aged 60-74 years and incorporated CAC scoring as part of its protocol [14,15]. Within the DANCAVAS trial, a subset of participants had two CT scans with a follow-up period of ≥ 4 years, while the remaining with only one available scan were attempted matched to a corresponding single scan from the WDHR; however, complete matching for all cases was not possible. sLastly, to enhance the representation of anticoagulant users, we identified individuals in anticoagulation therapy through WDHR who had undergone a baseline cardiac CT at least four years earlier and invited them for a follow-up non-contrast CT scan and medical questionnaire conducted in 2020–2021.

Variables

From WDHR, DANCAVAS, DanRisk and our re-invited anticoagulant users, we obtained baseline and follow-up CAC scores along with key cardiovascular risk factor information, including BMI, smoking status, and family history of CVD.

To supplement these datasets, we used the Danish Health Data Authority to retrieve comprehensive longitudinal information on medical history including laboratory measurements and prescription drug use from the Danish Civil Registration System [16], the Danish National Patient Registry [17], the Danish National Prescription Registry [18], and the Danish National Laboratory Database. Prescription data were specifically used to determine anticoagulant exposure status, differentiating between VKAs and DOACs, as well as to identify other relevant cardiovascular medications.

Baseline characteristics were defined as follows. Family history of CVD was defined as a first degree relative (men age <55 years and women age <65 years) with history of CVD. Diabetes mellitus was defined as registered diagnosis of diabetes prior to the baseline cardiac CT, two blood tests of HbA1c ≥ 48 mmol/mol or two redeemed prescriptions of diabetes medication (ATC code A10) within 240 days prior to baseline. Hypercholesterolemia was defined as registered hypercholesterolemia, one measured LDL-cholesterol ≥ 3.0 mmol/L or two redeemed prescriptions of lipid-lowering treatment (ATC codes C10AA and C10AX) within 240 days prior to baseline. Hypertension was defined as registered diagnosis or at least two different classes of antihypertensive treatment (ATC codes C03, C07, C08 and C09) with two redeemed prescriptions within 240 days prior to baseline. The CKD-EPI Creatinine Equation was used to estimate the glomerular filtration rate (eGFR) [19]. Renal impairment was defined as eGFR less than 60 mL/min.

Study groups and definitions

Based on prescription data, participants were categorized into four groups of anticoagulation use:

1.VKA group: Individuals with prescriptions of VKAs only during the follow-up period.

2.DOAC group: Individuals with prescriptions of DOACs only during the follow-up period.

DOAC group: Individuals with prescriptions of DOACs only during the follow-up period.

3.Mixed exposure group: Individuals with prescriptions of both VKAs and DOACs during the follow-up period.

Mixed exposure group: Individuals with prescriptions of both VKAs and DOACs during the follow-up period.

4.Control group: Individuals without prescriptions of VKAs or DOACs during the follow-up period, serving as the reference group.

Control group: Individuals without prescriptions of VKAs or DOACs during the follow-up period, serving as the reference group.

The VKA group included warfarin (ATC code B01AA03) and phenprocoumon (B01AA04). The DOAC group comprised apixaban (B01AF02), dabigatran (B01AE07), edoxaban (B01AF03) and rivaroxaban (B01AF01). The mixed exposure group included at least one substance from each treatment group.

Given the mechanistic hypothesis that VKAs may promote CAC through chronic inhibition of vitamin K–dependent pathways, we aimed to capture long-term exposure to anticoagulant therapy in a way suitable for dose–response evaluation. The primary exposure metric was defined as cumulative treatment duration, reflecting the total time a patient was exposed to VKA or DOAC therapy, rather than simply current use at the time of CT scanning or a single cumulative dose measure. The underlying rationale for using the cumulative treatment duration instead of the cumulative dose as our main exposure measure was that daily warfarin dose is titrated according to effect. A large part of the variation in effect, and thus in dose requirements, is explained by individual differences in pharmacokinetics. We can therefore reasonably assume that even though patients receive widely different doses, their blood warfarin levels vary much less. In other words, their cumulative exposure is better reflected by their cumulative duration than by their cumulative dose. Because our data source did not include information on the prescribed days’ supply, we defined treatment episodes per prescription to approximate continuous use over time. For VKA prescriptions, each comprising 100 tablets, a fixed treatment period of 100 days from the dispensing date was assumed, thereby avoiding the need to account for individual dosage variations. For DOAC prescriptions, the treatment period was calculated based on the total number of dispensed tablets, assuming a dosing regimen of one tablet per day for edoxaban and rivaroxaban, and two tablets per day for apixaban and dabigatran. To account for irregular dispensing patterns, non-perfect adherence, or stockpiling, a 30-day grace period was added to each DOAC prescription. If the calculated treatment period of one prescription, whether VKA or DOAC, overlapped with the dispensing date of the subsequent prescription of the same drug, these were considered part of a single uninterrupted treatment episode. The final prescription in an episode was assigned a treatment period using the same rules. The cumulative anticoagulant treatment duration of VKA or DOAC for each individual was then calculated by summing the total duration of all treatment episodes within the follow-up period. This approach allowed for a consistent and comparable estimation of anticoagulant exposure across both VKA and DOAC users.

Statistical analyses

Descriptive statistics were used to summarize baseline characteristics. Continuous variables were presented as mean (standard deviation) or median (25th percentile; 75th percentile), depending on the appropriateness of an assumed normal distribution, and categorical variables as counts and percentages. To account for potential measurement variability, cases where the CAC score at follow-up was lower than at baseline were corrected by substituting both values with their mean. Baseline differences between VKA and DOAC users were explored using t-tests, Wilcoxon's rank-sum tests and Chi-squared tests, as appropriate.

Confounding was addressed by using multinomial propensity score matching (PSM) to balance baseline covariates across the four treatment groups, mimicking a randomised trial design. Propensity scores were estimated using a multinomial logistic regression model with treatment group (VKA, DOAC, mixed exposure group or control) as the dependent variable and including the following covariates: sex, age, BMI, family history of CVD, diabetes, hypercholesterolemia, hypertension, renal impairment, smoking status, baseline CAC score and follow-up time. Weighted analyses were performed using the generated propensity score weights.

The primary outcome was the follow-up CAC score, analysed as a count variable. Given the large proportion of zero CAC scores, we applied a zero-inflated negative binomial regression (ZINB) model [20]. Two separate ZINB models were fitted: one including VKA and DOAC treatment duration as continuous variables (months of treatment), and one including treatment duration grouped into four categories (0 months, <24 months, 24-48 months, >48 months). All models included baseline CAC score, follow-up time between scans, and standard cardiovascular risk factors (sex, age, BMI, family history of CVD, diabetes, hypercholesterolemia, hypertension, renal impairment, and smoking status) as covariates. VKA and DOAC exposure durations were pooled for the pure and mixed groups. Results are presented as incidence rate ratios (IRRs) with 95% confidence intervals (CIs), where an IRR greater than 1 indicates a higher expected follow-up CAC score associated with the variable of interest after adjustment for all other covariates.

To assess whether anticoagulant treatment was associated with CAC severity, follow-up CAC was also analysed as a categorical outcome (0, 1–99, 100–399, ≥400 Agatston units (AU)) using a multinominal logistic regression. Baseline CAC category was included as a covariate. Results are presented as relative risk ratio (RRR) with 95% CI, where a RRR greater than 1 indicates a higher likelihood of belonging to a given follow-up CAC category compared with the reference category after adjustment for all other covariates.

As a supplemental approach, treatment group (VKA, DOAC, mixed exposure, or control) was included directly as a categorical covariate instead of treatment duration in the ZINB model. Predicted follow-up CAC scores for each treatment group, adjusted for all risk factors, were then estimated and graphically displayed using a margins plot. Sensitivity analyses were performed by excluding participants with mixed anticoagulant exposure, statin use, mechanical heart valves or renal impairment to assess the robustness of the findings.

All statistical tests were two-sided, and a P-value <0.05 was considered statistically significant. Analyses were performed using StataNow/MP (version 19.5, StataCorp, College Station, TX, USA).

Ethical considerations

The study was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki and data processing was approved by the Regional Data Protection Agency of the Region of Southern Denmark (J.nr.: 24/28491) and handled in compliance with the General Data Protection Regulation (GDPR) and Danish national data protection laws. All data were pseudo-anonymized before analysis and stored on secure, access-restricted servers. As this was primarily a retrospective observational study based on existing registry and clinical data, individual informed consent was waived in accordance with Danish legislation and ethical guidelines. For the re-invited anticoagulant users, written and oral informed consents were obtained from each participant, and the investigations were approved by the Regional Scientific Ethical Committee for Southern Denmark (S-20180039). Only aggregated results are reported, and no personal identifiable information is disclosed.

Baseline characteristics differed slightly between treatment groups. Primary results

In the ZINB model including treatment duration as a continuous covariate (Table 2), male sex, age, diabetes, hypercholesterolemia, hypertension, renal impairment, active smoking, baseline CAC score and follow-up time were all significantly associated with a higher expected CAC score at follow-up compared to the controls. BMI, family history of CVD and former smoking were not significantly associated with the expected follow-up CAC score. Longer VKA treatment duration was significantly associated with a higher expected follow-up CAC score (IRR 1.004 per month, 95% CI 1.000–1.007). Thus, each additional month of VKA exposure was associated with a 0.4% higher expected follow-up CAC score, corresponding to approximately 5% per additional year of exposure, after adjustment for covariates. No significant interaction was observed between VKA treatment duration and either sex or age (both P for interaction >0.05). In contrast, DOAC duration showed no significant association (IRR 1.003, 95% CI 0.999–1.008).

Supplemental analyses

In a simplified ZINB model including the treatment groups rather than treatment duration (Table S3), VKA-treated participants had 32% higher expected CAC scores at follow-up compared with controls (IRR 1.32, 95% CI 1.05–1.67). No significant association was observed for DOAC users (IRR 1.13, 95% CI 0.93–1.37) or participants with mixed anticoagulant exposure (IRR 1.05, 95% CI 0.82-1.13). In a sensitivity analysis excluding participants with mixed anticoagulant exposure group, longer VKA treatment duration remained significantly associated with a higher expected CAC score at follow-up (IRR 1.005 per month, 95% CI 1.001-1.010) (Table S4). Similarly, VKA treatment exceeding 48 months remained associated with a higher expected follow-up CAC score (Table S5). Excluding statin users yielded similar results, with longer VKA treatment duration remaining associated with a higher expected follow-up CAC score (IRR 1.004 per month of VKA, 95% CI 1.000–1.007) (Table S6). Likewise, VKA treatment exceeding 48 months remained associated with a higher expected follow-up CAC score (IRR 1.43, 95% CI 1.08-1.91), and longer VKA treatment duration remained associated with a higher likelihood of belonging to the severe CAC category (CAC score ≥ 400 AU; RRR 1.01, 95% CI 1.00-1.02) (Table S7–S8). Finally, exclusion of participants with mechanical heart valves (Table S9) or renal impairment (Table S10) yielded consistent findings, with longer VKA treatment duration remaining associated with a higher expected follow-up CAC score, whereas DOAC treatment showed no significant association in any model.

Discussion

In this large, population-based longitudinal cohort including over 7000 participants from Western Denmark, we investigated the association between anticoagulant treatment and CAC progression. Across all models, traditional cardiovascular risk factors such as male sex, higher age, diabetes, hypertension, renal impairment, and active smoking were consistently associated with greater CAC progression, in line with prior evidence [21]. With respect to anticoagulant exposure, we found that VKA therapy was independently associated with higher expected CAC scores at follow-up, displaying a duration–response pattern, with significant progression observed among individuals with long-term VKA exposure. In contrast, DOAC treatment showed no significant association regardless of exposure length. These findings support the mechanistic hypothesis that VKAs inhibit vitamin K–dependent γ-carboxylation of MGP, thereby promoting arterial calcification, whereas DOACs appear biologically neutral in this respect.

Our findings align closely with prior mechanistic and imaging studies examining the impact of VKAs on vascular calcification. Schurgers et al. [22] provided early evidence of the pro-calcific effects of warfarin in both animal and human studies, linking warfarin therapy to elevated concentrations of inactive MGP and increased coronary calcifications. Other human observational studies, characterized by smaller sample sizes compared to the current investigation, have similarly reported a higher risk of coronary calcification among VKA users compared with DOAC users and untreated controls [[5], [6], [7]]. In addition, Koos et al. [23] demonstrated an increased risk of valvular calcification associated with VKA therapy. Subsequent randomised trials comparing the progression of CAC among patients treated with various DOAC subtypes versus VKA therapy have yielded consistent findings [[7], [8], [9], [10]], even with follow-up durations as short as one year. Despite the relatively limited observation period, these studies were nonetheless able to demonstrate differences in calcification progress between treatment groups, underscoring the potential impact of warfarin on vascular mineralization.

Our study suggested a gradual progression of CAC, corresponding to a 0.4% monthly, or approximately 5% annual, higher increase in the expected follow-up CAC score than controls. Patients treated with VKAs for more than four years exhibited substantially higher expected CAC scores at follow-up compared with the control group, indicating a pronounced cumulative effect of prolonged exposure. Moreover, VKA treatment was linked to greater disease severity, as reflected by an increased likelihood of belonging to the high-risk CAC category (CAC ≥ 400 AU) at follow-up. Across all baseline CAC levels (0 to 1000 AU), VKA users exhibited the highest predicted follow-up CAC scores when adjusted for other risk factors, indicating a consistent pattern of greater calcification progression compared with DOAC or no anticoagulant use (Fig. 2).

Because certain participants were more predisposed to vascular mineralization, we conducted supplemental analyses excluding statin users, patients with mechanical prosthetic heart valves and those with renal impairment. The results remained robust across all groups, suggesting that the observed association between VKA therapy and CAC progression was not driven by these high-risk subgroups. Holden et al. [24] was able to demonstrate that even among patients with renal impairment at hemodialysis - who already exhibit a strong predisposition - warfarin use was independently associated with greater aortic valve calcification. These results further support a direct pro-calcific effect of VKA therapy, beyond the influence of underlying comorbidities.

Data from the present study further extend the existing evidence base and build upon our previous cross-sectional investigation indicating an association between anticoagulant therapy and CAC burden [25]. In contrast to the earlier study, the longitudinal design of the current investigation enables direct evaluation of CAC progression over time, thereby overcoming an important limitation inherent to cross-sectional analyses. From both clinical and biological perspectives, the progression of calcification constitutes a more informative and relevant endpoint than calcification burden assessed at a single time point. Importantly, we identified a duration-dependent association between VKA exposure and CAC progression, further strengthening the biological plausibility of a causal relationship. To the best of our knowledge, this study comprises the largest cohort and the longest follow-up duration reported to date. By integration of several complementary Danish cardiovascular cohorts and national registries, providing comprehensive longitudinal data on medication use and imaging outcomes, a detailed reconstruction of cumulative anticoagulant exposure and control of key confounders were enabled. The inclusion of participants undergoing repeated non-contrast CT scans over a minimum four-year interval - both as part of clinical evaluation and participants from general population screening initiatives - allowed direct quantification of CAC progression in a real-world setting. Exclusion of participants with established coronary artery disease and prior revascularization enabled evaluation of the effects of VKAs and DOACs on coronary artery calcification in a cohort free from clinically manifest coronary artery disease and prior revascularization. Importantly, the association between VKA use and CAC was independent of traditional cardiovascular risk factors, suggesting a pharmacologic effect rather than simple confounding by indication. In contrast, DOAC therapy, which directly inhibits thrombin or factor Xa without affecting the vitamin K cycle, was not associated with CAC progression in our study or previous investigations, ruling out confounding by indication given that it shares similar treatment indications with VKA therapy. This suggests that the pro-calcific effect is specific to vitamin K antagonism rather than an inherent feature of anticoagulation. Our results suggest that the ability to preserve vitamin K–dependent vascular protection may be one of the added benefits of DOAC therapy.

From a preventive clinical perspective, our findings may have important implications. While chronic VKA therapy remains essential for certain indications - most notably mechanical heart valves - DOACs provide equivalent or superior anticoagulant efficacy in conditions such as atrial fibrillation/flutter or venous thromboembolism [4]. This may be particularly relevant for patients with pre-existing coronary calcium, diabetes, or chronic kidney disease, in whom accelerated calcification contributes to high cardiovascular risk.

The interpretation of CAC progression, however, is complex. Although increased calcium burden generally reflects greater atherosclerotic burden, the biological significance of VKA-associated calcification remains uncertain. Andrews et al. [26] found that VKA therapy was associated with increased atheroma calcification without a corresponding change in total atheroma volume, suggesting a possible shift in plaque phenotype. Whether VKA-associated CAC progression reflects plaque stabilization or translates into an increased risk of clinical cardiovascular events remains to be established.

Future research should clarify both the mechanistic and clinical implications of anticoagulant-related vascular calcification. Incorporating circulating biomarkers such as dephosphorylated-uncarboxylated MGP and other vitamin K–dependent proteins may distinguish impaired vitamin K metabolism from broader vascular remodeling. Randomised clinical trials evaluating DOACs versus VKAs with imaging and clinical endpoints, including plaque measurements and coronary events, are needed to determine if the apparent vascular advantage of DOACs translates into improved long-term outcomes.

Study limitations

This present study adds important new evidence through its longitudinal design, large population-based cohort, prolonged follow-up, and detailed assessment of cumulative anticoagulant exposure, thereby allowing direct evaluation of CAC progression over time in a real-world setting. However, the study has several limitations that should be considered when interpreting the findings. First, as an observational registry-based study, residual confounding cannot be fully excluded despite the propensity weighting and adjustment for multiple cardiovascular risk factors. Important variables such as dietary vitamin K intake, over-the-counter drug use, and patient adherence to prescribed treatments were not available, which may have influenced both anticoagulant exposure and CAC progression. Hence, causality cannot be inferred from these observational data. Furthermore, the study included only participants with no prior revascularization who underwent repeated CAC scanning, potentially introducing survivorship and selection bias.

Second, although we attempted to capture cumulative anticoagulant exposure through treatment episodes, our approach required assumptions regarding treatment duration and dosing because prescription records did not include the prescribed days’ supply or actual ingestion. This limitation may have led to some degree of exposure misclassification, particularly among patients with irregular dispensing patterns or poor adherence. This misclassification, however, is likely non-differential and would tend to obscure differences rather than create some.

Third, CAC was assessed using two CT scans separated by at least four years, and for participants with more than two scans, only the two scans with the greatest temporal separation were included. While this approach allowed us to capture long-term progression, the exact timing of calcification onset between scans remains unknown, and measurement variability between scans could have influenced our progression estimates. Our correction method for apparent CAC regression may have slightly attenuated associations but was chosen to ensure conservative estimates. Ideally, risk assessment would involve serial, lifelong follow-up and imaging to more accurately evaluate the cumulative effect of VKA exposure over a lifetime.

Conclusion

In this large, population-based cohort, long-term VKA therapy was independently associated with higher follow-up CAC in a duration-dependent matter consistent with a dose-response relationship, whereas DOAC therapy showed no significant association. These findings are consistent with a potential role of vitamin K-dependent pathways in coronary artery calcification and suggest that DOACs may provide a more favorable vascular profile than VKAs. However, given the observational design of the study, causality cannot be inferred, and whether these differences translate into clinically meaningful cardiovascular outcomes remains to be determined.
