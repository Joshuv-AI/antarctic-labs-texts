Common gastrointestinal symptoms in healthy infants receiving goat milk-based formula or cow’s milk-based formula: a double-blind, randomized, controlled trial
Authors: P Maximino, L van Lee, Yvonne N Meijer-Krommenhoek, L van der Zee, H da Costa Ribeiro Junior
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectiveTo assess common gastrointestinal symptoms in healthy Brazilian infants receiving goat milk-based formula (GMF) compared to cow’s milk-based formula (CMF).MethodsWe performed a 24-weeks double-blind, randomized, controlled study in Brazil, enrolling healthy infants from 3 to 12 months of age. Primary outcome were the gastrointestinal (GI) symptoms stool consistency, regurgitation frequency and crying duration. Secondary outcomes were growth trajectories and hemoglobin levels. Repeated mixed models were used to compare outcomes variables between GMF and CMF groups, while adjusting for age at baseline.ResultsFifty-six infants were recruited and randomly allocated in the GMF (n = 26) and the CMF (n = 30) group. Scores on all measured GI symptoms were low and similar among the groups throughout intervention period and improved over time. Average age- and sex-adjusted WHO z-scores of weight, length, head circumference, and weight-for-length were all within +/-1 SD and similar between groups, indicating adequate growth. Serum hemoglobin was 11.1 (SD 0.7) g/dL in infants fed GMF and 11.0 (SD 0.8) g/dL in infants fed CMF after the intervention and was similar between groups.ConclusionGMF was well tolerated, safe and supported adequate growth in infants. This was shown by the low occurrence of GI symptoms, adequate blood hemoglobin levels and adequate growth within WHO standards.Trial registrationThe clinical trial was approved by the ethics committee of the Federal University of Bahia under number CAAE06923319.5.0000.5577. Introduction

Adequate early life nutrition is essential for optimal health and growth of infants as well as for their health later in life [1, 2]. Exclusive breastfeeding in the first 6 months of life is considered the most optimal source of nutrition for infants. Despite the World Health Organization (WHO) recommendations, more than 50% of infants globally are not exclusively breastfed during their first 6 months of life, and are therefore dependent on infant formula for their nutrition from early infancy [3]. Cow’s milk-based infant formulas (CMF) are commonly used, goat milk-based infant formulas (GMF) are a relatively new alternative. Goat milk is an approved safe and suitable source of protein for healthy growth and development in infants according to the European Food Safety Authority (EFSA) and Food and Drug Administration (FDA) [4, 5]. Goat milk protein has various indications for health benefits over cow’s milk protein with regards to digestion. To date, goat milk protein has been associated with faster [6] and more efficient digestibility [7], as compared to cow’s milk protein in in vitro experiments.

In the first year of life, it is common that infants experience some gastro-intestinal (GI) discomfort, such as hard stools, regurgitation, gassiness, and colic. The prevalence of these symptoms in infants has been reported between 25–55% [8–10]. GI discomfort in infants can lead to parental stress, concerns and a lack of sleep. Many parents may switch formula in their search for relief of their infant’s GI symptoms. Although most infants will naturally outgrow their GI symptoms as they get older [11], an infant formula that reduces GI discomfort might be helpful when GI symptoms peak in infancy.

GMF might beneficially affect the presence and intensity of GI symptoms due to the easier digestibility [7]. To date, a case study including three 11-months old infants showed that introduction of GMF declined GI symptoms rapidly [12], which was supported by another case report [13]. Nevertheless, high quality evidence is required to study the potential benefits of GMF on the intensity of GI symptoms in infants. Therefore, a double-blind randomized controlled trial was conducted to assess common GI symptoms in healthy Brazilian infants receiving either GMF or CMF over a 24-weeks period.

Methods

Study design and participants

This was a double-blind, randomized, controlled, parallel prospective design, comparing GMF and CMF over 24 weeks. Primary outcome were GI symptoms, measured through questions on stool consistency, regurgitation frequency and crying duration. Secondary outcomes focused on safety and included growth patterns, serum hemoglobin levels and days missed at daycare center (DCC) due to illness. The clinical trial was approved by the ethics committee of the Federal University of Bahia under number CAAE 06923319.5.0000.5577 and the trial was conducted according to the guidelines laid down in the Declaration of Helsinki. Written informed consent was obtained from the parent(s) or legal guardian(s) prior to start of the trial. This article was retrospectively registered in clinicaltrials.gov under identifier NCT06395571 on 02/05/2024.

All infants were recruited from a non-private DCC located in Salvador, Bahia. In this DCC, all infants’ parents have a very low socioeconomic status and live in similar environmental conditions. Eligible infants were born term (gestational weeks 37–42), 3–12 months old at enrollment, and should have consumed cow’s milk-based products during the 48 h prior to the baseline visit to exclude cow’s milk allergic infants.

The exclusion criteria were metabolic or chronic disease or congenital malformation, serious concurrent illness that would interfere with study treatment, (i.e. sepsis, pneumonia with respiratory failure, cardiac failure), infant’s parent(s) or primary caregiver(s) diagnosed with major depression, infant’s weight for length at baseline >|3| z-score indexes according to WHO criteria [14], infants enrolled in another interventional clinical research study, or infants diagnosed with cow’s milk allergies.

Study products

The GMF (Kabrita®, Ausnutria BV) and the CMF (Nestogeno, Nestlé) were both commercially available in Brazil and the tins were blinded for purpose of the clinical trial. The nutrient composition was similar in relation to energy content, macro- and micronutrients (Supplemental Table 1), except for prebiotics galacto-oligosaccharides (GOS) and fructo-oligosaccharides (FOS) added to the CMF (ratio GOS: FOS = 9:1; GOS 2.6 and FOS 0.29 g/100 g) but not in the GMF.

Formula was prepared according to the standardized instructions by the research assistants. Volume of formula feeding was standardized according to age appropriateness. Treatment compliance was measured by recording the volume in ml distributed to the infants and left-over formula for each feeding. For feedings taking place at home, the parent or caregiver measured the amount of study formula that wasn’t consumed with the use of the provided plastic measuring device with precision of 10 ml. Breastfeeding at home and complementary feeding were allowed, excluding vitamins and supplements. Occurrence of feeding in addition to formula was recorded without details about quantity.

Sample size and randomization

This trial used convenience sampling and invited all infants attending the DCC who were between 3 and 12 months old. Based on previous case studies [12, 13], a moderate effect of feeding was expected between the groups. Assuming a 2-sided test at an alpha level of 0.05, 30 participants would be needed per feeding group to detect an effect size of 0.4 with 80% power. The enrolment period lasted two weeks.

Eligible infants were randomly assigned to either GMF or CMF based upon the randomization list number provided by an independent statistician. Randomization was stratified by age below or above 6 months old. Emergency envelopes containing the unblinding code remained at the DCC. Investigators and participants remained blinded until statistical analyses were completed.

Study measurements

All research assistants at the DCC were trained according to standardized protocols to perform the measurements in infants. Anthropometric and physical measurements were performed monthly, whereas GI symptoms were assessed weekly and volumes of formula intake daily. Body length without shoes was measured while lying down to the nearest centimeter with a neonatometer. Weight was measured to the nearest 5.0 g with clothes and diaper removed using a calibrated weighing scale. Head circumference was measured with flexible non-stretching tape to the nearest cm. The GI symptoms crying duration, regurgitation frequency and stool consistency were recorded weekly for 24 weeks using an adapted version of the Cow’s Milk-related Symptom Score (CoMiSS®) where only the items for these scores were used [15]. The CoMiSS® is a validated questionnaire measuring the presence and severity of symptoms possibly related to milk consumption (sensitivity 20-77% and specificity 54-92%) and suitable for parents to self-report without training [15]. The CoMiSS® items skin and respiratory symptoms were not recorded in this study because these are more indirectly related to GI symptoms. When infants did not attend the DCC, parents reported information on the GI symptoms and adverse events (AE) through a diary.

At baseline and at the end of the study, a venipuncture was performed to collect 3 ml whole blood from each infant. Hemoglobin was measured at the hospital laboratory as part of a complete blood count. Hemoglobin levels were categorized as ‘normal’ for values ≥ 11 g/dL and as anemia for lower values according to WHO recommendations [16].

Statistical analysis

To evaluate baseline characteristics between groups, Mann–Whitney U test was used for the non-normally distributed continuous variables and the Fisher’s Exact Test for categorical factors. Due to low variability within the occurrence of GI symptoms, scores were dichotomized by using the median (crying ≤ 1 h/day; regurgitation ≤ 2 times/day). For stool consistency, stool type 2, 3 and 4 were compared to type 1 and 5. To analyze the time course of the GI symptoms separately between the 2 interventions, linear mixed models were generated. Here, the main effect factors treatment and age, the repeated measures factor time, and the interaction term treatment*time were included. The GMF treatment served as the reference in the model. The anthropometric measures; weight, length, and head circumference were transformed using the WHO child growth standards [14], and compared between intervention groups using mixed models.

In a subsample of infants aged < 6 months, the analyses for the GI symptoms were repeated in sensitivity analyses, since younger infants are more likely to have a high prevalence of experiencing GI discomfort [10].

Data analyses were performed using the RStudio version 3.6.1 and p-value for significance was set at < 0.05.

Results

Discussion

In this 24-week double-blind, randomized controlled trial the effect of feeding infants GMF on GI symptoms, growth and hemoglobin status was compared to those in infants fed CMF. This study showed that the infants tolerated both infant formulas well, as presented by the low occurrence of GI symptoms. Furthermore, growth was adequate according to the international WHO growth standards [14] and similar between the two groups throughout the intervention. In addition, the hemoglobin, an indicator for iron deficiency, showed adequate levels in both groups.

Previous case studies in infants reported improvement of GI symptoms after consuming GMF for three weeks [12, 13]. The present study found that GI symptoms improved over time, in both CMF-fed infants and GMF-fed infants. Several explanations are possible, the first being that the infants in the case studies were selected based on having GI issues, while this was not a criterium in the present RCT. In fact, the occurrence of GI symptoms in our study was quite low (6–13%) compared to previous literature reporting prevalence between 25–55% [8–10]. It should be taken into account that infants in these published studies were much younger, ranging from 10.1 (2.2) days to 4.1 (2.1–7.5) months old at baseline, whereas the median age of 6 months at baseline in the present study was considerably higher. This could be a second explanation for the lack of effect observed. As often seen in literature, aging of the infant and thereby the maturation of the GI tract reduces the prevalence of GI symptoms [17, 18]. Likewise, a cross-sectional study in 609 infants aged 6–12 months showed lower CoMiSS® scores with higher age [19]. This was also observed in the present study, since after each week of intervention the occurrences of GI symptoms decreased by 22%. Moreover, GI symptoms were associated with the infant’s age and time of intervention. Thirdly, infants were around the age of consuming weaning foods in addition to infant formula, which may have confounded the possible effects of the infant formulas. Additionally, the CMF contained added prebiotics GOS/FOS, while the GMF did not. The prebiotics GOF/FOS are known to promote the growth of bacteria beneficial for gut health [20, 21], therefore possible differences in effect size between the investigational and control product might have become too small to detect.

The growth trajectories in both treatment groups were similar and considered adequate according to the WHO growth standards, showing that GMF, as well as CMF, support healthy growth in the studied infants [14]. These results align with a recent meta-analyses that reported adequate growth and good safety of infants consuming GMF [22]. The meta-analysis was based on 4 double-blind randomized controlled studies comparing infants fed GMF with CMF in several European countries, Australia, New Zealand and China [23–26]. Additionally, these studies reported that GMF was safe for use in infant populations, as indicated by the low frequency of (serious) adverse events [23–26]. GMF has been available on the market in Latin America and specifically Brazil, but this is the first study confirming adequate growth and safety of GMF in Brazilian infants.

Infants in the present study were from a low socio-economic status, which has been associated with a higher risk for anemia [27]. This is confirmed by the results, as the prevalence of infants diagnosed with anemia at the beginning of the intervention was 46%. This is comparable to anemia prevalence observed in previous studies in infants in Brazil [28].

Additionally, considering that half of the infants were over 6 months old and that the average formula intake in our study was lower than would be expected in infants exclusively formula fed, it is assumed that many infants received solid foods in addition to the infant formula. Studies have found low iron intakes in individuals from a low socio-economic status [3], indicating that the anemia in this sample is caused by factors other than infant formula type, such as the quality of weaning foods and additional breastfeeding in which iron levels are expected to be low as they will reflect those of the mother.

Finally, some caregivers reported at baseline that infants in the sample (n = 19) were introduced to unmodified cow’s milk in the first months of life. Unmodified cow’s milk inhibits iron absorption and affects hemoglobin levels [28], and authorities recommend to not feed this to children before they are 12 months of age [29].

Strengths and limitations

Strength of the present study was that it was the first double-blind, randomized, controlled study in Latin America assessing GI symptoms in infants. Additional strengths include the highly-controlled environment within the DCC, enabling objective and daily monitoring of the infants. Moreover, the precise assessment of infant formula intake and the long period of intervention of 24 weeks added to the strength of this study. During the intervention, crying, regurgitation and stool scores were measured weekly, which gave detailed insight into the changes in GI symptoms over time.

The limitations of our study need attention as well. Over half of the mothers continued breastfeeding the infants throughout the intervention period. To eliminate the effect of human milk on GI symptoms, it would be preferable to select infants that are exclusively formula-fed. Simultaneously, we argue that the combination of human milk and infant formula make our results more realistic to the general population, where globally an estimated 23–32% of infants receive this so-called mixed milk feeding [30]. Furthermore, in our sample infants of both intervention groups received human milk, so we assume that it did not affect our results. Another limitation was that half of the infants were older than 6 months at baseline. This may have been the reason for the low prevalence of GI symptoms at the start of intervention and consequently low power to detect differences between the intervention groups. Another disadvantage of the infants being > 6 months of age, was the consumption of weaning foods. The weaning period may pair with symptoms of discomfort and formula intake diminishes.

Conclusion and future research

GMF was well tolerated, safe and supported adequate growth in the studied Brazilian infants. This was shown by the low occurrence of GI symptoms, adequate blood hemoglobin levels and adequate growth within WHO standards. All infants showed a significant decrease in GI symptoms over time, indicating that, in this population with few complaints at baseline, aging played the most important role in the reduction of GI symptoms. Ultimately, our results contribute to previous findings, indicating that the GMF is a safe and suitable alternative to CMF for infants unable to receive (exclusive) breastfeeding. Future studies on the impact of GMF on GI symptoms should include infants preferably below the age of 3 months before introduction of complementary feeding and already experiencing (mild) GI problems.
