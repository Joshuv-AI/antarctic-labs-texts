Enhancing the selection of drought tolerance varieties of rice (Oryza sativa) using germination and early plant growth stress indices
Authors: Charles O Joseph, Faraja Samwel Makwinja
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundRice (Oryza sativa L.) is a critical staple crop whose productivity is increasingly threatened by drought. However, breeding approaches that identify stable stress tolerance remain limited, particularly regarding the relationship between early-stage performance and subsequent plant growth. This study addressed this gap by evaluating six rice varieties under drought stress at both germination and seedling stages.MethodsSix rice varieties (SARO, IRRI 18, IRRI 17, SADASHAAME, AHMADA, and SUPA BC) were evaluated for their tolerance under four induced drought stress levels (4, 6, 8, and 12 g/L D-mannitol). A factorial experiment at the germination stage was conducted in a complete block design, while a randomized complete block design was used at the seedling stage. Growth data, relative performance index (RPI), and tolerance index (TOL) were recorded and analyzed using MultCompView, and ggplot2 packages in RStudio.ResultsSignificant effects were observed between stress level and variety (p < 0.05). The sensitivity of physiological traits to stress across varieties was stage-specific. At the germination stage, SARO (97%) and IRRI 18 (86.67%) recorded the highest germination percentages, while SUPA BC recorded the lowest (22.33%). At the seedling stage, SADASHAAME had the highest plant height (36.20 cm) and dry weight (0.052 g), while SARO had the highest TOL (0.92). Physiological trait performance was reduced significantly under increasing stress, with the highest stress level (12 g/L) resulting in the lowest RPI (72.20) and TOL (0.40). Principal component analysis revealed that PC1 explained 36.0% (germination) and 34.7% (seedling) of variance, with TOL consistently loading positively under high stress across both stages. Cluster analysis showed stable grouping for varieties across stages. Variety tolerance followed the order SADASHAAME ≥ SARO ≥ IRRI 18 ≥ IRRI 17 ≥ AHMADA ≥ SUPA BC.ConclusionThe study highlights the complex interactions among rice variety, stress level, and plant growth stage. Variety response to osmotic stress depends on plant growth stage and variety type. Screening at germination alone does not reliably predict seedling performance. Primary selection should be conducted under high stress level with stage-specific performance traits. Molecular characterization and multi-stage plant assessment (including reproductive stages) are recommended for future research.Keywords: Drought, Stress indices, Oryza sativa, Variety, Tolerance index, Relative performance index

Introduction

Drought is one of the most significant abiotic stresses threatening global food security. It reduces crop yield by disrupting plant morphological and physiological processes from germination through anthesis to maturity [1–3]. Among environmental stresses, drought is particularly devastating, causing greater yield losses than many other stresses combined [4–6]. Although commonly associated with arid and semi-arid regions, drought also occurs in other parts of Africa due to irregular rainfall distribution [7]. Projections indicate that by 2050, more than 50% of the world’s arable land may be affected by drought [8–10]. The severity of drought effects depends on stress intensity, plant genotype, and developmental stage, all of which interact to determine final crop yield [11–13].

Rice is a major staple crop, feeding more than one-third of the global population and ranking second after maize in Tanzania, where it contributes approximately 50–80% of daily caloric intake [14]. Despite its importance, rice is highly susceptible to drought due to its relatively shallow root system, thin cuticle wax layer, and rapid stomata closure [14, 15]. Approximately 23 million hectares of rain-fed rice are affected by drought stress [15], and prolonged drought conditions may reduce yields by up to 50%, posing a serious risk to food security. To mitigate these impacts, strategies such as breeding drought-tolerant varieties, improving water management, and adopting efficient irrigation systems have been widely recommended [16, 17].

Among these strategies, the characterization and selection of drought-tolerant genotypes remain central to crop improvement efforts. These approaches primarily aim to enhance biomass production under water-limited conditions and improve tolerance at critical growth stages. However, most drought evaluations focus on later developmental stages, particularly reproduction and maturity, which are time-, labor-, and resource-intensive.

Consequently, limited attention has been given to drought tolerance assessment at early growth stages, representing a critical gap in breeding programs. It remains unclear whether tolerance observed at early stages reliably predicts plant performance at later developmental stages.

Drought indices evaluated at early growth stages may offer a useful framework for assessing plant responses by integrating yield-related and physiological traits across developmental phases [17–20]. Therefore, a comprehensive evaluation that incorporates multiple stress intensities, growth stages, and traits is essential for improving selection efficiency. This study addresses these gaps by applying a multi-stage, multi-intensity, and multi-trait screening approach to evaluate rice genotypes under drought stress at germination and seedling stages under controlled environments. Under controlled environment screening, osmotic agents such as mannitol, sodium chloride, and polyethylene glycol (PEG-6000) are widely employed to simulate water deficit conditions and assess early plant responses [21–25]. The objective is to identify genotypes with stable phenotypic performance, thereby contributing to breeding and management strategies aimed at enhancing rice productivity and resilience under changing environmental conditions.

Materials and methods

Plant materials

The response of rice varieties to drought stress was evaluated at two early growth stages: seed germination and seedling development [15]. The study was conducted at the University of Dar es Salaam (UDSM), College of Agriculture and Food Technology (COAF), in the period of December 2023 to July 2024. Two factors, rice varieties and drought stress levels were evaluated. Seedling experiment was arranged in randomized complete block design (RCBD), while germination experiment was arranged in completely randomized design (CRD). In this study, 6 rice varieties (4 improved varieties SARO, SUPA BC, IRRI 17 and IRRI 18, and two (2) local varieties SADASHAAME and AHMADA), collected from Zanzibar Agricultural Research Institute (ZARI), were evaluated in 4 stress levels, and non-stress control.

Drought stress treatments at seed germination stage

Drought stress was induced using D-mannitol (C₆H₁₄O₆; Sigma-Aldrich, CAS No. 69-65-8) at five concentrations: 4, 6, 8, 10, and 12 g L⁻¹, corresponding to 21.96, 32.94, 43.91, 54.88, and 65.87 mM, respectively. Distilled water (DSTL) was used as the non-stress control. D-mannitol was selected as an osmotic agent because it is relatively non-ionic and slowly taken up by plant tissues, thereby inducing water deficit conditions primarily through osmotic effects rather than toxicity [26].

Seeds were surface-sterilized using a 1% sodium hypochlorite solution for 2 min with gentle agitation, followed by rinsing with sterile distilled water. The sterilized seeds were then primed in a gibberellic acid (GA₃) solution (1.5 mM) for 24 h and subsequently rinsed again with sterile distilled water.

A total of 50 seeds per variety were placed in Petri dishes (90 mm diameter) lined with two layers of filter paper moistened with the respective D-mannitol solutions. Each treatment was replicated three times (Fig. 1). The Petri dishes were arranged in a Completely Randomized Design (CRD) and placed in a conditioned germination chamber maintained at 28 ± 2 °C, with a 16 h light/8 h dark photoperiod and 50 ± 5% relative humidity.

Seeds were considered germinated when the radicle protruded at least 2 mm through the seed coat, and germination was recorded at 24 h intervals. Treatments were maintained for 14 days, during which seedlings were monitored daily. Germination percentage was calculated using Eq. (1). In addition, shoot length, root length, and fresh and dry weights were measured.

Drought stress treatment at germination stage

Drought stress evaluation at the seedling stage was conducted in a semi-covered screen house under ambient conditions (25–30 °C). Seeds were pre-soaked in distilled water for 24 h and then sown in planting trays containing soil as the growth medium. Seedlings were allowed to germinate and grow for 10 days.

Uniform seedlings were then transplanted into 4 L plastic buckets, with three seedlings per bucket, each containing 4 kg of forest soil. The experiment was arranged in a Randomized Complete Block Design (RCBD) to account for spatial variability within the screen house environment.

After transplanting, all seedlings were irrigated with distilled water for 4 days to allow establishment and recovery from transplanting shock. Thereafter, drought stress treatments were imposed by applying 5 mL of either distilled water as the control or D-mannitol solutions at the specified concentrations on a daily basis. Treatments were maintained until the seedlings reached the true leaf stage, after which drought stress responses were evaluated.

Data collection

Drought tolerance was evaluated based on parameters related to seed germination and seedling growth performance under the imposed drought stress levels. At the germination stage, the recorded parameters included germination percentage (Germ), root length (RL, cm), fresh weight (FrW, g), and dry weight (DrW, g). At the seedling stage, shoot length (ShL, cm), number of leaves (NL), fresh weight (FrW, g), and dry weight (DrW, g) were measured. All data were collected following the methodology described in [16]. In addition, two drought tolerance indices, namely Relative Performance Index (RPI) and Tolerance Index (TOL), were used for a comprehensive assessment of varietal response to drought stress, as they capture different aspects of plant performance under stress conditions. Statistical analysis

A two-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) was performed using the Standard Least Squares procedure to evaluate the effects of rice varieties and drought stress levels and their interaction on all measured traits. When significant interaction effects were detected (P ≤ 0.05), mean separation was conducted using Tukey’s Honestly Significant Difference (HSD) test on Least Squares Means (LSMeans) to compare treatment combinations. Hierarchical cluster analysis was conducted using Ward’s minimum variance method to group varieties based on agronomic traits and drought tolerance indices, allowing classification of genotypes according to their response patterns under stress conditions. Principal component analysis (PCA) was conducted on standardized trait data using a correlation matrix to assess multivariate variation under drought stress. The first two principal components were retained based on eigenvalues and scree plot evaluation, and trait contributions were interpreted from loading scores and biplots. All statistical analyses and visualizations were performed in R software, using the packages ggplot2, ggbiplot, and multcompView.

Results

The results of the effect of rice varieties on germination growth traits under drought stress conditions presented in Table 1. The six rice varieties exhibited significant variation in germination performance under drought stress. SARO and IRRI 18 recorded the highest germination percentages (97.00% and 86.67%, respectively), while SUPA BC showed the lowest germination (22.33%), suggesting high sensitivity to drought conditions. AHMADA and SADASHAAME had moderate germination rates, whereas IRRI 17 also performed relatively well. Two-way ANOVA (standard least squares) revealed a significant genotype × stress interaction for fresh weight (FrW) only, while all other measured traits showed no significant interaction effects (Fig. 2). Fresh weight declined progressively with increasing drought stress across all genotypes, although the magnitude of reduction differed among varieties. The non-parallel response patterns among genotypes confirm a significant interaction between genotype and stress level for fresh weight.

Cluster analysis based on physiological traits and stress tolerance indices

Hierarchical clustering based on standardized physiological traits Germ, ShL RL, Ph, FrW, DrW and NL) and stress tolerance indices (RPI and TOL) revealed clear stage-dependent variation in genotype relationships using Euclidean distance and Ward’s method (Fig. 3). At the germination stage, linkage distances ranged from 1.31 to 4.30, indicating moderate genetic divergence among genotypes. Cluster I (AHMADA, IRRI 17, SARO) showed close similarity in early physiological responses; Cluster II (IRRI 18, SADASHAME) grouped at intermediate distances and Cluster III (SUPA BC) remained highly distinct. At the seedling stage, linkage distances ranged from 0.00 to 4.20, indicating both stronger similarity among some genotypes and continued divergence among others. Cluster I (AHMADA and SUPA BC), merged at near-zero distance, indicating highly similar seedling-stage trait expression. Cluster II (IRRI 17, IRRI 18, and SARO), remained consistently grouped at low distances across stages. Cluster III (SADASHAME) indicated greater physiological divergence at the seedling stage.

Hierarchical cluster analysis of rice genotypes based on physiological traits and stress tolerance indices at germination and seedling stages using Euclidean distance and Ward’s method

Comparison across stages showed that the clustering of IRRI 17, IRRI 18, and SARO was stable across stages, suggesting consistent physiological responses and stress tolerance. In contrast, SUPA BC shifted from a highly divergent genotype at germination to close association with AHMADA at the seedling stage, indicating strong developmental plasticity. SADASHAME showed the opposite trend, moving from association with IRRI 18 at germination to isolation at the seedling stage, reflecting increased differentiation during development. Overall, clustering patterns demonstrate that genotype relationships are strongly stage-dependent, with early germination traits reflecting moderate separation, while seedling traits reveal both convergence and enhanced differentiation. Genotypes with stable clustering across stages (IRRI 17, IRRI 18, SARO) likely possess consistent stress response mechanisms, thus are good donors for developing broadly adapted varieties. Genotypes showing cluster shifts (SUPA BC and SADASHAME) exhibit stage-specific adaptive responses relevant for targeted breeding.

Principal component analysis of rice traits at germination and seedling stages under drought stress

To investigate how trait relationships change during early development, principal component analysis (PCA) was performed separately for the germination and vegetative (seedling) stages (Fig. 4). For each stage, the first two principal components (PCs) were retained based on scree plot eigenvalues. At the germination stage, the first seven PCs explained 99.95% of the variance, with PC1 and PC2 cumulatively explaining 55.6% (Fig. 4). PC1 (36.0%) was defined by opposing contributions from tolerance index (TOL) on the positive side and germination rate (Germ) on the negative side. Additional negative contributors to PC1 included root length (RL), fresh weight (FrWg), relative performance index (RPI), and shoot length (ShL). PC2 (19.0%) separated TOL, dry weight (DrWg), RL, FrWg, and ShL from Germ and RPI. At the vegetative stage, the first seven PCs explained 100% of the variance. PC1 and PC2 cumulatively explained 59.5% of the variation. PC1 was again defined by TOL on the positive side and dry weight (DrWv), RPI, plant height (Ph), fresh weight (FrWv), RL, and number of leaves (NL) on the negative side. PC2 separated TOL, DrWv, Ph, FrWv, and RL from NL and RPI. Trait vectors with high cos2 (> 0.7) were considered well-represented in the two-dimensional space. These include TOL and Germ at germination, and TOL, DrWv, and RPI at the vegetative stage.

Multivariate analysis of rice physiological traits using PCA at germination and seedling stages under drought stress conditions

The PCA results highlight that selection for drought tolerance should be stage-specific. At the germination stage, emphasis should be placed on germination percentage and early vigor traits to ensure successful establishment under stress. At the seedling stage, selection should prioritize root length, biomass accumulation, and tolerance index, as these traits better reflect sustained performance under drought conditions. Result further indicated that Tolerance index (TOL) is stable across stages and positively loads on PC1 in both. Breeders can use TOL as a reliable early-stage screening criterion for stress tolerance, particularly in direct-seeded or rainfed systems where early stress is common. In addition, clear shift in trait importance between stages suggests that combining early establishment efficiency with strong root and biomass performance will be critical for developing drought-resilient rice genotypes.

Principal component analysis of rice traits at different drought stress levels

Result for principal component analysis of rice traits at different drought stress levels is presented in Fig. 5. At both germination and seedling stages, trait associations were strongly influenced by stress concentration. Under low stress (4 g/L), performance-related traits (germ, FrW, RPI, and NL) dominated the negative side of PC1. Under high stress (12 g/L), tolerance-related traits (TOL and DrW) dominated the positive side of PC1. Intermediate stress levels (6–8 g/L) produced mixed trait associations, with genotypes showing transitional behaviour between performance and stress tolerance.

PCA biplots of TOL, tolerance index; Germ, germination rate; RL, root length; FrWg/FrWv, fresh weight (germination/seedling); DrWg/DrWv, dry weight (germination/seedling); ShL, shoot length; RPI, relative performance index; NL, number of leaves at germination and seedling stages at four stress levels (4,6 8 and 12 g/L)

Tolerance index (TOL) remained consistently associated with high stress (positive PC1) at both germination and seedling stages, confirming its stability across growth stages. Dry weight (DrW) sat germination clusters positively with TOL under high stress, but clusters negatively with TOL at seedling stage indicating growth stage stress specific. The stage-dependent shifts suggest that the physiological roles of traits change during development, and selection should therefore be conducted at the growth stage matching the target environment.

The spread of genotype scores along PC1 and PC2 was notably larger under high stress (12 g/L) compared to low stress (4 g/L). This pattern indicates high stress intensity amplify genotypic differences that remain hidden under favorable conditions, thereby increasing the power to discriminate superior tolerant genotypes. Therefore, screening under high stress (12 g/L) is recommended for primary selection of stress-tolerant lines, while low-stress screening may be more appropriate for selecting performance under non-stress environments.

Discussion

This study demonstrates that the response of physiological traits to drought stress depends on variety, stress level, and developmental stage, with significant implications for breeding strategies. At the germination stage, significant varietal differences were observed for germination percentage, with SARO and IRRI 18 recording the highest values (97.00% and 86.67%, respectively), while SUPA BC showed the lowest (22.33%), suggesting high sensitivity to drought conditions. However, shoot length, root length, fresh weight, dry weight, and tolerance index (TOL) did not differ significantly among varieties at germination, indicating that early seedling growth was relatively uniform. The relative performance index (RPI) showed some variation, with AHMADA, IRRI 18, and SADASHAAME exhibiting relatively higher values, while SUPA BC recorded the lowest, supporting its poor drought tolerance. Stress levels and the interaction between variety and stress were not significantly different for the measured traits at germination, indicating that varietal response remained consistent across the imposed drought conditions.

At the seedling stage, varietal differences became pronounced for plant height, fresh weight, dry weight, RPI, and TOL. SADASHAAME had the highest plant height (36.20 cm) and dry weight (0.052 g), while SARO had the highest TOL (0.92). SUPA BC consistently recorded the lowest values, particularly in fresh weight (0.25 g) and dry weight (0.031 g). Stress levels significantly affected most traits except plant height and dry weight. The control (DSTL) showed the best growth (e.g., root length 4.71 cm, fresh weight 0.48 g), while increasing stress levels reduced performance. The highest stress (12 g/L) resulted in the lowest RPI (72.20) and TOL (0.40). These findings confirm that stress tolerance is not uniformly expressed across traits or stages and that higher stress levels negatively impact seedling growth [27].

PCA results further explained these patterns. At the germination stage, PC1 (36.60%) separated TOL, Germ), RL), FrWg), RPI, and ShL). Additional negative contributors to PC1 included RL, FrWg, RPI, and ShL. PC2 (19.0%) separated TOL, DrWg, RL, FrWg, and ShL from Germ and RPI. At the seedling stage, PC1 (34.7%) again separated TOL from a broader cluster including DrWv, RPI, Ph), FrWv RL, and NL). PC2 separated TOL, DrWv, Ph, FrWv, and RL from NL and RPI. Trait vectors with high cos2 (> 0.7) including TOL and Germ at germination, and TOL, DrWv, and RPI at the seedling stage represented well the two-dimensional space. This stage-dependent expansion of the performance cluster indicates that as plants develop, tolerance becomes opposed by an increasingly complex suite of growth-related traits.

Although early-stage screening can serve as a preliminary filter to speed up breeding for drought stress resistance [27, 28], our results clearly demonstrate that germination-stage performance does not reliably predict seedling-stage performance. For example, SARO and IRRI 18 were most stress tolerance at germination stage, while SADASHAAME was most tolerance at seedling stage. Furthermore, hierarchical clustering analysis revealed that genotype relationships were strongly stage-dependent. At the germination stage, linkage distances ranged from 1.31 to 4.30, with Cluster I (AHMADA, IRRI 17, SARO) showing close similarity, Cluster II (IRRI 18, SADASHAAME) grouped at intermediate distances, and Cluster III (SUPA BC) remaining highly distinct. At the seedling stage, linkage distances ranged from 0.00 to 4.20, with Cluster I (AHMADA and SUPA BC) merged at near-zero distance, Cluster II (IRRI 17, IRRI 18, and SARO) consistently grouped, and Cluster III (SADASHAAME) indicating greater physiological divergence. IRRI 17, IRRI 18, and SARO formed a stable cluster across stages, suggesting consistent physiological responses and stress tolerance. In contrast, SUPA BC shifted from a highly divergent genotype at germination (Cluster III, distance 4.30) to close association with AHMADA at the seedling stage (Cluster I, merged at near-zero distance), indicating strong developmental plasticity. SADASHAAME showed the opposite trend, moving from association with IRRI 18 at germination to isolation at the seedling stage (Cluster III), reflecting increased differentiation during development.

These findings align with previous reports that drought stress impacts physiological features differently depending on the plant’s developmental stage [27]. The underlying mechanisms may involve stage-specific expression of genes responsible for carbohydrate mobilization, lipid metabolism, oxidative stress responses, enzymatic activation, respiration, and cell division during germination [27, 28]. Varieties with stable clustering across stages (IRRI 17, IRRI 18, SARO) likely possess consistent stress response mechanisms and are therefore good donors for developing broadly adapted varieties. Genotypes showing cluster shifts (SUPA BC and SADASHAAME) exhibit stage-specific adaptive responses relevant for targeted breeding rather than general-purpose tolerance.

This study also showed that multi-trait screening captures complementary tolerance mechanisms, no single trait is sufficient to assess drought tolerance across stages. Instead, a multi-trait approach is required. At the germination stage, PCA revealed a clear antagonistic axis between TOL and Germ. Genotypes with higher tolerance tended to show reduced germination percentage due to osmotic stress conditions [1, 2]. In contrast, at the seedling stage, TOL showed an opposite pattern to a wider set of growth and biomass-related traits (DrWv, RPI, Ph, FrWv, RL, NL), indicating that drought tolerance is associated with reduced overall seedling growth and biomass accumulation under continued stress exposure. Collectively, these patterns indicate that the physiological basis of drought response becomes increasingly multivariate as growth progresses [3].

The two-way ANOVA revealed a significant genotype × stress interaction for fresh weight (FrWv) only, while all other measured traits showed no significant interaction effects. This indicates that most traits respond consistently to increasing stress across varieties. Fresh weight declined progressively with increasing drought stress across all genotypes. Under control conditions, SARO (0.6389 g) and IRRI 18 (0.5773 g) exhibited the highest fresh weight. With increasing stress intensity, SARO, IRRI 18, and SADASHAAME maintained relatively higher values at moderate stress levels (4–6 g/L), while SUPA BC consistently recorded the lowest fresh weight across all stress treatments. The non-uniform response patterns among genotypes confirm that fresh weight is a valuable trait for detecting differential stress sensitivity.

Relative performance index (RPI) transitioned from a minor contributor at germination (negative PC2) to a major negative contributor on both PC1 and PC2 at the seedling stage. This implies that relative performance becomes increasingly integrated with vegetative traits as plants mature. Similarly, dry weight (DrW) showed a stage-dependent reversal, at germination, DrWg clustered positively with TOL under high stress, while at the seedling stage, DrWv loaded negatively with TOL. These stage-dependent shifts suggest that the physiological roles of traits change during development, and selection should therefore be conducted at the growth stage matching the target environment.

However, TOL remained stable across stages, it consistently cloaded positively on PC1 under high stress at both germination and seedling stages (cos2 > 0.7). Breeders can therefore use TOL as a reliable early-stage screening criterion for stress tolerance, particularly in direct-seeded or rainfed systems where early stress is common [4]. However, TOL alone is insufficient; it must be combined with stage-specific performance traits (Germ and ShL at germination; NL and RPI at seedling) to achieve balanced selection.

The other finding of this study is that trait associations are strongly influenced by stress concentration (Fig. 5). Under low stress (4 g/L), performance-related traits (Germ, FrW, RPI, and NL) dominated the negative side of PC1. Under high stress (12 g/L), tolerance-related traits (TOL and DrW) dominated the positive side of PC1. Intermediate stress levels (6–8 g/L) produced mixed trait associations, with genotypes showing transitional behaviour between performance and stress tolerance. These results confirm that trait correlations are not fixed; they change along the stress gradient [5, 6].

The spread of genotype scores along PC1 and PC2 was markedly larger under high stress (12 g/L) compared to low stress (4 g/L). This pattern indicates that high stress intensity amplifies genotypic differences that remain hidden under favorable conditions, thereby increasing the power to discriminate superior tolerant genotypes [7, 8]. This finding has direct implications for breeding, screening under high stress is recommended for primary selection of stress-tolerant lines, while low-stress screening may be more appropriate for selecting high-performance genotypes under non-stress environments. Varieties that tolerate high stress levels likely possess enhanced mechanisms of antioxidant enzyme activity and net photosynthetic rate, which delay stress-induced damage [29–31]. These mechanisms enable the plant to modify morphological features; including root and shoot architecture to cope with induced stress [12, 32, 33].

Integrating the findings from germination and seedling stage evaluations, cluster analysis, PCA, and stress-level comparisons, a clear framework emerges for developing rice varieties with stable drought tolerance. The results demonstrate that drought tolerance is not a fixed property of a genotype but rather a dynamic characteristic that depends on developmental stage, the specific traits being measured, and the intensity of the stress applied. Successful breeding for stable drought tolerance therefore requires a three-dimensional approach that simultaneously considers multiple stages, multiple traits, and multiple stress levels. Multi-stage screening is essential because varietal rankings and trait associations shift between germination and seedling stages. Multi-trait screening is necessary because no single trait captures tolerance across stages and multi-stress-level screening is critical because trait associations change along the stress gradient.

Even though the study has provided useful information, limitations and future directions exist. For the limitations, lack of multi-stage evaluation across reproductive and grain-filling stages, field validation to confirm these controlled-condition results under real-world conditions and lack of molecular characterization of the identified tolerant varieties are evident. Future research should therefore extend evaluation to all plant growth stages, including reproductive and grain-filling phases. Physiological and biochemical assays, such as antioxidant enzyme activity, proline accumulation, photosynthetic efficiency, and ABA quantification should be incorporated to elucidate mechanistic bases of tolerance [29–31]. Furthermore, field evaluations across multiple seasons and locations are needed to validate controlled-environment findings and assess genotype × environment interactions [9]. Molecular marker screening, including QTL mapping, GWAS, transcriptomics, or gene expression analysis, should be performed to identify genetic loci underlying stage-specific and stress-level-specific tolerance [10, 11]. Finally, multi-trait, multi-stage selection indices incorporating PCA-derived weights for TOL, RPI, Germ, NL, and RL should be developed to enable genomic prediction of stable drought tolerance [12].

Conclusion

This study demonstrated that drought stress resistance screening can be initiated at early plant stages, but such screening is a function of variety, trait, and specific plant stage. Screening at the germination stage alone does not reliably reflect seedling performance, as varietal rankings and trait associations shift significantly between stages. Therefore, drought stress evaluation should be conducted at both germination and seedling stages to capture stage-specific trait responses. The relative performance index (RPI) proved to be a valuable stage-dependent indicator of drought response. Varieties such as AHMADA, IRRI 18, and SADASHAAME exhibited higher RPI values, while SUPA BC consistently recorded the lowest, supporting its poor drought tolerance. The significant genotype × stress interaction observed for fresh weight (FrW) further indicates that certain traits are uniquely sensitive to differential genotypic responses and should be prioritized in multi-trait selection indices. Based on integrated PCA, cluster analysis, and stress-level comparisons, SARO, IRRI 18, and SADASHAAME emerged as the most optimal drought stress-resilient varieties. SARO and IRRI 18 demonstrated superior germination performance and stable clustering across stages, while SADASHAAME was resistant at the seedling stage. Primary screening should be conducted under high stress (12 g/L), as this condition amplifies genotypic differences and enhances selection power. Tolerance index (TOL) emerged as the only stable trait across both stages and stress levels, making it a reliable early-stage screening criterion. However, TOL must be combined with Germ, ShL at germination stage, and NL, RPI and RL at the seedling stage, to achieve stable selection. Future research should extend evaluation to reproductive and grain-filling stages, incorporate biochemical assays, conduct field validation across multiple seasons, and perform molecular marker screening to identify genetic loci underlying stage-specific and stress-level-specific tolerance.
