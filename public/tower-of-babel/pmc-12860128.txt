Correlation between questionnaire on air pollution-related factors and chronic respiratory diseases: a case study of Minhang District, Shanghai
Authors: Qingqing Liu, Xiaohua Zhang, Liang Li, Tianyun Wang, Xiaoying Hu, Pengyu Huang, Yimei Cao, Yanan Zhang, Yan Ge, Jing Gao, Juan Xie
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundAir pollution seriously threatens residents’ respiratory health, the objective of this study is to explore the relationship between air pollution-related factors and chronic respiratory diseases (CRDs), analyze the association between air pollution-related factors and chronic obstructive pulmonary disease (COPD) prevalence, and evaluate the current status of residents’ awareness of air pollution through questionnaire analysis.MethodsA cross-sectional was conducted in Minhang District, Shanghai. Personal information, air pollution-related risk factors, and residents’ cognitive level were collected through questionnaire investigations. Descriptive statistics, chi-square tests, univariate and multivariate logistic regression and receiver operating characteristic curve (ROC) were used.ResultsThe questionnaire was completed by 3136 respondents. The prevalence of CRDs was 24.6% and COPD was 12.1%. Among the air pollution-related variables, exposure to dust or fumes at work, used to cook with firewood or coal, frequent home cooking, and living proximity to industrial areas or other pollution sources were significantly associated with CRDs. The area under the curves (AUC) of COPD was 0.821 for assessing the prevalence of COPD with basic variables combined with air pollution-related variables. In terms of air pollution cognition, CRDs patients were generally lower than no-CRDs.ConclusionAir pollution-related factors are significantly related to the prevalence of CRDs, considering the significant harm of air pollution to COPD, it is recommended to add air pollution-related factors to the screening questionnaire of COPD to improve the screening accuracy. In addition, residents’ cognition levels of air pollution are associated with CRDs prevalence.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12889-025-26062-0.Keywords: Air pollution-related factors, Chronic respiratory disease, COPD, Public perception, Questionnaire study, Air pollution awareness

Introduction

Chronic respiratory diseases (CRDs) are a class of non-communicable diseases that seriously affect the health and lives of residents, with the prevalence of CRDs increasing by 39.8% and mortality by 28.5% from 1990 to 2019, making it the third leading cause of death in the world [1–3]. There are many factors influencing CRDs, among which air pollution is one of the important risk factors for CRDs morbidity and mortality [4].

There is growing evidence that air pollution is associated with chronic respiratory diseases [5]. Common air pollutants (such as PM, ozone, and oxidizing gases) can irritate respiratory tract inflammation, lead to cilia dysfunction, cause airway remodeling, and respiratory symptoms (such as cough) [6, 7]. Studies have shown that individuals exposed to high levels of pollution suffer from significantly higher odds ratio (OR) values for chronic respiratory diseases [8]. In addition, our country has a vast territory, and there are great differences in socio-economic levels and population structure in various places, so there are obvious differences in the exposure-response relationship between air pollution exposure and health in various places [9]. From the perspective of the overall environment, the southern area of Minhang in Shanghai is mostly an old industrial area, with relatively heavy air pollution, an aging population, and a high risk of chronic respiratory diseases [10]. Therefore, this study designed a questionnaire survey related to air pollution from the perspective of relevant environmental exposure levels to evaluate its impact on the prevalence of CRDs among residents in Minhang District.

Studies have shown that both objective and subjective exposure to air pollution increases the risk of dying from respiratory diseases [11]. Most cohort studies advocate improving the impact of the objective environment on morbidity and mortality [12]. However, the subjective level of residents’ perception of air pollution should not be ignored. Residents with low levels of awareness of air pollution and unreasonable measures can have more serious consequences than the disease itself [13]. Just as the knowledge-attitude-behavior model [14, 15], higher levels of knowledge are associated with greater concern for relevant issues and a higher likelihood of adopting protective measures to reduce exposure to air pollution-related factors, alleviate respiratory symptoms and avoiding exacerbations. Therefore, this study aims to further explore the impact of air pollution awareness of residents on the prevalence of CRDs in Minhang District.

The common CRDs currently studied are COPD, asthma, chronic bronchitis, allergic rhinitis, etc., among which COPD is the most common chronic respiratory disease [16]. Studies have shown that long-term exposure to air pollution increases the prevalence of COPD [17]. An epidemiological study in Italy showed that mean annual concentrations of particulate matters (PM) are the main drivers of COPD prevalence, with air pollution likely attributable to 50% of COPD [18, 19]. The results of a systematic review and meta-analysis of 436 studies worldwide showed that each 10 µg/m3 increase in PM2.5 levels was associated with an increased incidence of COPD, with a pooled risk ratio (HR) of 1.18 [20]. Therefore, we believe that air pollution has some research value in screening for COPD. In addition, the commonly used COPD screening questionnaires mainly involve age, smoking, respiratory symptoms, etc [13]. , and these symptom-based screening questionnaires mainly reflect physiological responses caused by internal exposure, and rarely include external exposure-related indicators such as air pollution. In disease progression, internal exposure is unavoidable, but external risk factors related to air pollution have intervenable implications for health promotion. Therefore, this study intends to include air pollution-related factors from the perspective of environmental exposure to evaluate its screening efficacy for COPD.

This study explored the relationship between air pollution-related factors and CRDs through questionnaire analysis, analyzed the association between air pollution-related factors and the prevalence of COPD, and evaluated the current status of residents’ perception of air pollution. In order to scientifically and accurately evaluate the level of individual environmental exposure, which is a necessary basis for subsequent environmental health research.

Methods

Study participants

This cross-sectional study was carried out from January 2024 to December 2024, and participants, in Minhang District (Jiangchuan, Gumei, Wujing, Zhuanqiao, Maqiao, Pujiang subdistrict), Shanghai, China, were selected based on the following inclusion criteria: (1) aged ≥ 40 years old; (2) resided in Minhang District for more than 10 years. Individuals were excluded if they: (1) could not complete the questionnaire independently or with the assistance of others (2). refuse to participate in this study.

To make the study more representative, stratified random cluster sampling was performed. Six subdistricts (Jiangchuan, Gumei, Wujing, Zhuanqiao, Maqiao, Pujiang) were selected respectively from Minhang District. We selected the sample size of each neighborhood community is according to the proportion of the population. Formula for estimating sample size as follows:

According to previous researches, the prevalence of CRDs in the Shanghai and similar urban typically among 20%-30% [21, 22]. Therefore, Through the calculations altered as follows: p = 25%, d = ± 2%, andα = 0.05 and the required sample size was deemed to be at least 1800. Considering the design effect of stratified cluster sampling and the possibility of 15% invalid questionnaires, a total of 3,800 questionnaires were collected for this study.

The study was evaluated and approved by the Ethics Committee of Shanghai Fifth people’s Hospital (2024 Ethics Approval No. 178). All participants signed informed consent, and all personal information was de-identified before further analysis.

Questionnaire investigation

This study adopts the unified design of the “Air Pollution and Residents’ Chronic Respiratory Health and Environmental Cognitive Behavior Questionnaire”, which was formulated by the research team with reference to relevant domestic and foreign research (Supplementary material 1). The following information was collected: (1) demographic characteristics, including sex, age, education, occupation, smoking, etc.; (2) Health status: definitively diagnosed chronic bronchitis, asthma, COPD, etc.; (3) Air pollution exposure: occupational contact (such as dust, smoke), outdoor exercise habits, cooking at home, straight-line distance of the house to traffic artery, etc.; (4) Residents’ cognition and attitude towards air pollution: awareness level of PM2.5 and air quality index (AQI), awareness of the harmfulness of air pollution, etc.

Interviewers were general practitioners from Shanghai Fifth People’s Hospital or general practitioners of Minhang Community Health Service Center, who received unified training and passed an assessment before the investigation. All participants had pulmonary function tests before completing the questionnaire and pulmonary function was assessed using a COSMED Pony FX spirometer (Italy). Other CRDs diagnoses are based on medical records and self-reported, etc.

Statistical analysis

Data analyses were performed using SPSS v.25.0 (IBM) and MedCalc v.15.0. Continuous variables are classified and reported as frequency (n) or ratio (%) along with other categorical variables, and they were compared by the chi-square test. Logistic regression analysis was used to evaluate the correlations between CRDs or COPD and other variables, with estimated odds ratios (ORs) and 95% confidence intervals (95% CI). Receiver-operating characteristic (ROC) curve was plotted and area under curve (AUC) was calculated to evaluate predictive ability of variables on the prevalence of CRDs or COPD. The DeLong test is used to compare the differences between distinct AUCs and statistical significance was set at p < 0.05.

Awareness of air pollution

Table 2 shows residents’ awareness in respect of air pollution and chronic respiratory diseases. The majority of residents paid attention to air pollution, accounting for 89.64%. 77.55% of residents thought that air pollution has a more impact on the elderly and CRDs patients, while 22.67% of residents have taken protective measures. There were statistically significant differences in cognition (e.g. awareness of PM2.5 and AQI, improving air quality to reduce the incidence of CRDs) and attitudes (e.g. paying attention to AQI and using air quality-related apps) between CRDs patients and no-CRDs residents. Overall, the awareness of air pollution among no-CRDs residents is higher than that among CRDs patients.

Association between air pollution-related factors and COPD

Logistic regression analysis conducted among COPD before and after adjusting for confounders (Table 4), identified several associations. In the unadjusted model, frequently exposure to dust or smoke at work, outdoor exercise habits, frequently cooking at home, have used firewood or coal when cooking and living near major industrial areas were significantly associated with prevalence of COPD. After adjusting for confounding factors, frequently exposure to dust or smoke at work (OR = 1.26; CI = 1.03–1.63), frequently cooking at home (OR = 1.42; CI = 1.03–1.81) and have used firewood or coal when cooking (OR = 1.46; CI = 1.10–1.94) were still found to be high-risk factors for COPD. In addition, outdoor exercise habits was still associated with COPD.

Discussion

This is a survey of air pollution-related factors and CRDs based on permanent residents in Minhang District, Shanghai, in which we showed that the prevalence of CRDs increased with exposure to air pollution-related factors (especially in COPD). Furthermore, residents have a high awareness rate of air pollution hazards, but lack practical action.

CRDs is a chronic disease typically developing over prolonged exposure to factors like smoking and long-term air pollution. In this study, it was found that exposure to dust or smoke at work, have used firewood or coal when cooking, frequently cooking at home, straight-line distance of the house to traffic artery < 200 m, and living near major traffic road were significantly associated with CRDs. Long-term exposure to these factors, persistent irritant particulate matter can trigger chronic inflammation of the airways, leading to a significant increase in the prevalence of chronic respiratory diseases [23, 24]. This study also found that outdoor exercise has a protective effect, which may be because regular outdoor physical activity is thought to help improve lung ventilation function, enhance cardiorespiratory endurance and immune levels, and reduce the incidence of CRDs [25, 26]. However, outdoor exercise is a complex model, despite partly counteracted by the positive effects of habitual exercise, the negative effects of exposure to air pollutants during exercise are hard to assess [27]. previous studies have shown that during periods of high air pollution concentrations, especially in those living in urban areas or near roads, physical activity can have harmful effects on lung function [28]. In addition, Consistent with other similar reports [29, 30], we found that after adjustment for confounding factors, proximity to straight-line distance of the house to traffic artery and living near major traffic road as the surrogate of greater air pollution from traffic were not major determinant of COPD. Suggesting that these factors had less impact on respiratory health in this sample or may be masked by other confounding variables.

This study has highlighted a knowledge gap in participants. Although 89.64% of residents agreed that air pollution poses serious health risks, there was a lack of specific practice, with only 22.67% of residents took protective measures. As numerous studies have often shown, knowledge and attitudes are not directly observable as practices [15], and perceived barriers has a direct impact on the adoption of protective measures [31]. Studies from USA have reported that risk perception and health information acquisition ability also affect the implementation of actions [32, 33]. A survey from Beijing, China indicated that perception showed a strong relationship with intentions to take protective behaviors, such that risk perception significantly predicted intentions related to measures such as wearing masks outdoors [34]. Given the gaps in knowledge identified, it is not surprising that almost two-thirds (66.97%) of people had made no changes to reduce air pollution. Furthermore, we also found that CRDs prevalence is related to air quality awareness [35, 36]. In addition, air pollution awareness not only affects the prevalence of CRDs, but also has an impact on cardiovascular and cerebrovascular diseases [37, 38]. In order to improve the health of residents, we should strive to improve the public’s risk perception of air pollution hazards and reduce disease hazards.

In this study, 24.6% of participants were diagnosed with at least one CRDs. COPD account for 12.1%, which was similar to the results of a 2023 study (12.9%) from Shanghai [39], and higher than the results reported in the 2018 China Pulmonary Health (CPH) study (8.6%) [40]. COPD is an important part of the burden of disease affecting public health in China, and early detection, early diagnosis and early treatment should be our focus [41, 42]. Currently, screening questionnaires widely used internationally include COPD-PS, COPD-SQ, and COPD-CDQ [43–45], It mainly involves age, smoking, BMI and respiratory symptoms, and rarely involves factors related to air pollutants. In this study, the AUC of the combined assessment of COPD prevalence was 0.668 (95%CI 0.651–0.684, p < 0.01) for outdoor exercise habits, frequent home cooking, and history of firewood or coal use. On this basis, the AUC could reach 0.821 (95% CI 0.807–0.834, p < 0.01) after adding sex, age, smoking, and family history of respiratory diseases, which was statistically significant. However, this assessment ability is significantly reduced in CRDs, with an AUC of only 0.703 after combination. Due to the limited variables included in this study, it cannot be compared with questionnaires such as COPD-PS, and it is planned to add more variables to improve the screening accuracy of COPD in future studies.

Several recent studies reported that socioeconomic status (SES) affect awareness and play crucial roles in determining the risk of CRDs, lower SES is associated with a higher risk [46–48]. Generally, the assessment of SES using total household income level, occupation (employment status), and educational level [49, 50]. Considering the sensitivity of income, education and occupation were included in the analysis of this study. In our study, workplace exposures remain an important risk factor for CRDs [51], but there was no difference in education levels which was obviously different compared to another population-based study in UK and USA [47]. After analysis, we considering possible response bias or the weakening effect of factors such as age and lifestyle risk factors [52, 53].

Based on a large-sample population survey, the relationship between air pollution exposure-related factors and the risk of CRDs was systematically evaluated. Different from previous studies that mainly relied on surveillance data and disease registration systems, this study combines individual-level cognitive behavior with objective disease symptoms from a community perspective, combined with epidemiological investigation methods, and proposes that early screening of COPD should pay attention to individual environmental exposure, which provides a reference for constructing a research path of “air pollution-cognition and behavior-health outcomes”.

There are some limitations for this survey. Firstly, the study was designed as a questionnaire survey, some CRDs diagnoses rely on self-report and medical history, which may have information bias. Secondly, SES was not included in the analysis, and there is no specific classification of occupations. Thirdly, questionnaire was designed by team experts and was not tested for reliability and conduct a sensitivity analysis to verify the robustness of the findings. Finally, to further verify the conclusions of this study, we intend to conduct an intervention study on patient behavior through a prospective cohort study combining objective air quality monitoring, wearable device recordings and biomarkers.

Conclusion

Air pollution-related factors are significantly related to the prevalence of CRDs, considering the significant harm of air pollution to COPD, it is recommended to add air pollution-related factors to the screening questionnaire of COPD patients to improve the screening accuracy. Secondly, residents’ awareness of air pollution is related to CRDs disease, which reveals the complexity of residents’ respiratory health problems in the context of urban air pollution and provides a practical basis for regional public health intervention.
