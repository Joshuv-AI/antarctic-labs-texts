The exceptional set of Goldbach problem
Genheng Zhao

Abstract
. Let E ⁡ ( X ) E(X) be the number of even integers below X X which are not a sum of two primes. We prove that E ⁡ ( X ) = O ⁡ ( X 0.709 ) E(X)=O(X^{0.709}) .



1.
Introduction

The famous Goldbach conjecture asserts that every large even integer is a sum of two odd primes, and every large odd integer is a sum of three odd primes. The ternary problem has been successfully solved by Vinogradov
[
1
]
in the 1930s, which is now called Vinogradov’s three primes theorem. As for the binary one, writing
E(X)
the number of even integers below
X
which are not a sum of two odd primes, his method is able to reach the estimate
E(X)=O_{A}(X(\log X)^{-A})
for any
A>0
. This result, compared to Hardy-Littlewood’s
[
2
]
conditional bound
E(X)=O_{\operatorname{\varepsilon}}(X^{1/2+\operatorname{\varepsilon}})
for any
\operatorname{\varepsilon}>0
, has large room for improvement.

In 1975, Montgomery and Vaughan
[
3
]
showed that
E(X)=O(X^{1-\delta})
holds for some unspecified
\delta>0
, where the implicit constant is ineffective due to the necessary application of Siegel-Walfisz theorem. Verifying the explicit value of this
\delta
needs an intricate analysis on the distribution of zeroes of Dirichlet
L
-functions. In 1989, J. R. Chen and J. M. Liu
[
4
]
confirmed that
\delta=0.05
is admissible. This was later improved to
\delta=0.086
by H. Z. Li
[
5
]
and
\delta=0.121
by W. C. Lu
[
6
]
. The best numerical result up to date is due to Pintz
[
10
]
, where
\delta=0.28
is available.

In this paper, with a refinement of Pintz’s method, we can show
\delta=0.291
. In other words, we have the following.

Theorem 1.1
.

E(X)=O(X^{0.709})
, where the implicit constant is ineffective.

The main advantage of Pintz’s method is that we only need to consider the influence of zeroes of Dirichlet
L
-functions for a single moduli and in a highly restricted area. This feature has been shared with Linnik’s problem on the least prime in an arithmetic progression. The best numerical result in this topic is due to Xylouris
[
7
]
, based on a refinement of the method of Heath-Brown
[
8
]
. In Pintz’s argument, the proof of Theorem 1.1 can be reduced to showing the following result, which can be regarded as a two-dimensional version of Linnik’s problem.

Theorem 1.2
.

Let
q,T,H,M\geq 2
,
c_{0}>0
, and
\{\mathcal{K}_{i}\}_{i\geq 1}
be pairwise disjoint sets of characters
\chi(\operatorname{mod}q)
. For each
i
, define the the multi-set

\mathcal{L}_{i}=\{\lambda=(1-\beta)\log q:L(\beta+i\gamma,\chi)=0,\lambda\leq H,|\gamma|\leq T,\chi\in\mathcal{K}_{i}\},

(1.1)

and assume that

\max_{(\chi,\chi^{\prime})\in\mathcal{K}_{i}^{2}}\operatorname{cond}(\bar{\chi}\chi^{\prime})\leq M,\quad\min_{\lambda\in\mathcal{L}_{i}}\lambda\geq c_{0}.

(1.2)

Then for
q\geq q(T,H,M,c_{0})
we have

\sum_{i\geq 1}\left(\sum_{\lambda\in\mathcal{L}_{i}}e^{-\delta^{-1}\lambda}\right)^{2}\leq 1-c_{1},

(1.3)

where
\delta=0.291
and
c_{1}>0
depends only on
c_{0}
.

We will briefly introduce this reduction in the next section and then give a proof to the inequality (
1.3
) in the rest sections. We achieve this improvement by introducing a dichotomy argument which can reduce nearly half of the number of zeroes under consideration. This will be shown in the end of Section 3.

2.
Preliminary

Let
r(m)=\sum_{m=p_{1}+p_{2}}(\log p_{1})(\log p_{2})
be the weighted version of the number of ways writing
m
as a sum of two primes
p_{1}
and
p_{2}
. For
X,T,H,P\geq 2
we use the notation
\mathcal{E}=\mathcal{E}(X,T,H,P)
to denote the multi-set of singularities of

-\frac{L^{\prime}}{L}(s,\chi),\quad\chi\text{ primitive, }\operatorname{cond}(\chi)\leq P,

(2.1)

in the region

\operatorname{Re}(s)\geq 1-\frac{H}{\log X},\quad|\operatorname{Im}(s)|\leq T.

(2.2)

Let
A(\operatorname{\varrho})\in\{-1,1\}
indicate the residue at each singularity
\operatorname{\varrho}\in\mathcal{E}
. Obviously, there is exactly one singularity
\operatorname{\varrho}_{0}=1
such that
A(\operatorname{\varrho}_{0})=1
, corresponding to the trivial character
\chi_{0}\equiv 1
. We may thus write
\mathcal{E}=\mathcal{Z}\cup\{\operatorname{\varrho}_{0}\}
, where, of course,
\mathcal{Z}
consists of all zeroes of the aforementioned
L
-functions in the region (
2.2
).

We now suppose
0<\operatorname{\varepsilon}<\operatorname{\varepsilon}_{0}
,
\operatorname{\vartheta}=\frac{2}{5}-\operatorname{\varepsilon}_{0}
and
P\leq X^{\operatorname{\vartheta}}
. By
[
9
, Theorem 2]
, there exist
X_{0}>0
and
h_{0}>0
, which depend only on
\operatorname{\varepsilon}_{0}
, such that when
X\geq X_{0}
we have either
E(X)=O(X^{1-\operatorname{\vartheta}})
, or

(1-\beta)\log X\geq h_{0},\quad\operatorname{\varrho}=\beta+i\gamma\in\mathcal{Z}.

(2.3)

Meanwhile, by
[
9
, Theorem 1]
, there exist
P\in[X^{\operatorname{\vartheta}-\operatorname{\varepsilon}},X^{\operatorname{\vartheta}}]
,
T=T(\operatorname{\varepsilon})
and
H=H(\operatorname{\varepsilon})
such that

\begin{split}r(m)=&\sum_{(\operatorname{\varrho}_{i},\operatorname{\varrho}_{j})\in\mathcal{E}^{2}}A(\operatorname{\varrho}_{i})A(\operatorname{\varrho}_{j})\mathfrak{S}(\chi_{i},\chi_{j},m)\frac{\Gamma(\operatorname{\varrho}_{i})\Gamma(\operatorname{\varrho}_{j})}{\Gamma(\operatorname{\varrho}_{i}+\operatorname{\varrho}_{j})}n^{\operatorname{\varrho}_{i}+\operatorname{\varrho}_{j}-1}+O(\operatorname{\varepsilon}X)\end{split}

(2.4)

holds for all but at most
O(X^{1-\operatorname{\vartheta}+\operatorname{\varepsilon}})
even numbers
n\in[\frac{X}{2},X]
. The generalized singular series
\mathfrak{S}(\chi_{i},\chi_{j},m)
satisfy a crude bound

|\mathfrak{S}(\chi_{i},\chi_{j},m)|\leq\mathfrak{S}(m),

(2.5)

where

\mathfrak{S}(m)=\mathfrak{S}(\chi_{0},\chi_{0},m)=\prod_{\begin{subarray}{c}p\mid m\end{subarray}}\left(1+\frac{1}{p-1}\right)\prod_{p\nmid m}\left(1-\frac{1}{(p-1)^{2}}\right)

(2.6)

is the usual singular series in binary Goldbach problem. Futhermore, for any
\eta>0
, there is a suitable integer
c(\eta)
such that, unless

[\operatorname{cond}(\chi_{i}),\operatorname{cond}(\chi_{i})]\mid c(\eta)m\text{ and }\operatorname{cond}(\chi_{i}\chi_{j})\leq\eta^{-3},

(2.7)

we have the finer bound

|\mathfrak{S}(\chi_{i},\chi_{j},m)|<\eta.

(2.8)

Observe that by the log-free zero density estimate of Jutila
[
11
]
, we have

|\mathcal{Z}|=e^{O(H)}=O_{\operatorname{\varepsilon}}(1).

(2.9)

Hence there exists
\eta=\eta(\operatorname{\varepsilon})
such that pairs
(\operatorname{\varrho}_{i},\operatorname{\varrho}_{j})
not obeying (
2.7
) can be removed in the summation at the right hand side of (
2.4
), with an error term which can be absorbed into
O(\operatorname{\varepsilon}X)
.
Meanwhile, when
X\geq X(\operatorname{\varepsilon})
, Siegel-Walfisz theorem implies
\operatorname{\varrho}_{0}=1
is the only singularity in
\mathcal{E}
with
\operatorname{cond}(\chi)\leq\eta^{-3}
. Thus the influence of
\{\operatorname{\varrho}_{0}\}
and
\mathcal{Z}
can be well separated in this step. To conclude, for
X\geq X(\operatorname{\varepsilon})
, we now arrive at

\begin{split}r(m)\geq\mathfrak{S}(m)m\left(1-\underset{\begin{subarray}{c}(\varrho_{i},\varrho_{j})\in\mathcal{Z}^{2}\end{subarray}}{\sum\nolimits^{*}}X^{\beta_{i}+\beta_{j}-2}\right)+O(\operatorname{\varepsilon}X)\end{split}

(2.10)

for all but at most
O(X^{1-\operatorname{\vartheta}+\operatorname{\varepsilon}})
even numbers
n\in[\frac{X}{2},X]
, where
*
abbreviates the condition (
2.7
).

Consider the new condition

[\operatorname{cond}(\chi_{i}),\operatorname{cond}(\chi_{i})]\mid c(\eta)m\text{ and }\operatorname{cond}(\bar{\chi}_{i}\chi_{j})\leq\eta^{-3}.

(2.11)

It is clear that

\underset{\begin{subarray}{c}(\varrho_{i},\varrho_{j})\in\mathcal{Z}^{2}\end{subarray}}{\sum\nolimits^{*}}X^{\beta_{i}+\beta_{j}-2}=\underset{\begin{subarray}{c}(\varrho_{i},\varrho_{j})\in\mathcal{Z}^{2}\end{subarray}}{\sum\nolimits^{**}}X^{\beta_{i}+\beta_{j}-2},

(2.12)

where ** abbreviates the new condition (
2.11
).

Let
\mathcal{K}(m)
be the set of all corresponding characters of the zeroes appearing in the above sum and
q(m)
be the least common multiple of their conductors. By (
2.11
) we have

q(m)\mid c(\eta)m
. Since
|\mathcal{Z}|=O_{\operatorname{\varepsilon}}(1)
,
q(m)
takes only
O_{\operatorname{\varepsilon}}(1)
possible values. Hence

q(m)\leq X^{\operatorname{\vartheta}}
holds for all but
O_{\operatorname{\varepsilon}}(X^{1-\operatorname{\vartheta}})
values of
m\in[\frac{X}{2},X]
.
Multiplied by a prime of suitable size if necessary, we can regard
\mathcal{K}(m)
as a set of characters
\chi(\operatorname{mod}q(m))
, with
q(m)\in[X^{\frac{\operatorname{\vartheta}}{2}},X^{\operatorname{\vartheta}}]
. After partitioning these characters into
O_{\operatorname{\varepsilon}}(1)
sets
\{\mathcal{K}_{i}(m)\}_{i\geq 1}
, which satisfy

\operatorname{cond}(\bar{\chi}\chi^{\prime})>\eta^{-3}
if and only if
\chi
and
\chi^{\prime}
come from two distinct sets, we obtain

\underset{\begin{subarray}{c}(\varrho_{i},\varrho_{j})\in\mathcal{Z}^{2}\end{subarray}}{\sum\nolimits^{**}}X^{\beta_{i}+\beta_{j}-2}\leq\sum_{i\geq 1}\left(\sum_{\chi\in\mathcal{K}_{i}(m)}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}L(\operatorname{\varrho},\chi)=0\\
\beta\geq 1-\operatorname{\vartheta}H(\log q(m))^{-1}\\
|\gamma|\leq T\end{subarray}\ }e^{-\operatorname{\vartheta}^{-1}(1-\beta)\log q(m)}\right)^{2}

(2.13)

It is easy to check that

\max_{(\chi,\chi^{\prime})^{2}\in\mathcal{K}_{i}(m)}\operatorname{cond}(\bar{\chi}\chi^{\prime})=O_{\operatorname{\varepsilon}}(1),\quad(i\geq 1)

(2.14)

and by (
2.3
),

(1-\beta)\log q(m)\geq\frac{\operatorname{\vartheta}}{2}h_{0},\quad\operatorname{\varrho}=\beta+i\gamma\in\mathcal{Z}.

(2.15)

Hence the conditions of Theorem 1.2 are satisfied, and Theorem 1.1 follows easily from Theorem 1.2 by choosing
\operatorname{\vartheta}
slightly larger than
\delta
and
\operatorname{\varepsilon}
sufficiently small.

The remaining part of this paper is devoted to the proof of (
1.3
). For convenience we will label
\lambda\in\mathcal{L}_{i}
by

\lambda_{i,1}\leq\lambda_{i,2}\leq\cdots,

(2.16)

and
\{\mathcal{L}_{i}\}_{i\geq 1}
by

\lambda_{1,1}\leq\lambda_{2,1}\leq\cdots.

(2.17)

The dependence on the parameters
T,H,M,c_{0}
will be ignored since they are harmless constants independent from
q
. For
i\geq 1
, let

S_{i}=\sum_{i\geq 1}e^{-\delta^{-1}\lambda_{i,j}}.

(2.18)

Then (
1.3
) amounts to

\sum_{i\geq 1}S_{i}^{2}\leq 1-c_{1}.

(2.19)

According to the possible values of
\lambda_{1,1}
, we further distinguish six cases as follows

(1)

c_{0}\leq\lambda_{1,1}\leq 0.08
,

(2)

0.08\leq\lambda_{1,1}\leq 0.3
,

(3)

0.3\leq\lambda_{1,1}\leq 0.4
,

(4)

0.4\leq\lambda_{1,1}\leq 0.5
,

(5)

0.5\leq\lambda_{1,1}\leq 0.6
,

(6)

\lambda_{1,1}\geq 0.6
.

Let
\mathcal{L}=\bigcup_{i\geq 1}\mathcal{L}_{i}
. The classical zero free region for
L
-functions implies that there is at most one term in
\mathcal{L}
in a certain range
\lambda\leq c
. If this term does exist and is very small, other terms in
\mathcal{L}
are expected to take large values due to the Deuling-Heilbronn phenomenon. Explicit versions of this phenomenon and their generalizations have already been established in
[
8
]
and
[
7
]
. We end up this section with the following lemma.

Lemma 2.1
.

Let
q
be sufficiently large.

(1)

In case (1),
\mathcal{L}
has at most one term in the range

\lambda\leq\max\{3.08,\log\lambda_{1,1}^{-1}\}.

(2)

In case (2),
\mathcal{L}
has at most one term in the range
\lambda\leq 1.58.

(3)

In case (3),
\mathcal{L}
has at most one term in the range
\lambda\leq 1.29.

(4)

In case (4),
\mathcal{L}
has either at most one term in the range

\lambda\leq 1.08,

or at most two terms in the range

\lambda\leq 1.36.

(5)

In case (5),
\mathcal{L}
has either at most one term in the range

\lambda\leq 0.92,

or at most two terms in the range

\lambda\leq 0.97.

(6)

In case (6),
\mathcal{L}
has at most two terms in the range

\lambda\leq 0.702.

Proof.

The range
\lambda\leq\log\lambda_{1,1}^{-1}
in case (1) is a simple consequence of
[
8
, Lemma 8.8]
.
The remaining inequalities are generated by a different method.

In cases (1)-(3), we have
\lambda_{1,1}\leq 0.4
. By
[
7
, Theorem 1]
this means that
\lambda_{1,1}
corresponds a real zero and a real character. While in cases (4)-(6),
\lambda_{1,1}
may correspond to a complex zero or a complex character.
In any case, if
\lambda_{1,1}
corresponds to a real zero and a real character, then the range for which
L
have at most one term is taken from Table 2,3,4,5,7 of
[
8
]
. Otherwise, when
\lambda_{1,1}
corresponds to a complex zero or a complex character, the range for which
L
have at most two terms is taken from Tabellen 2,3,7 of
[
7
]
.

∎

3.
Weighted sums over zeroes

In this section we confine our attention to the general sum

S=\sum_{j\geq 1}e^{-\delta^{-1}\lambda_{j}},

(3.1)

with
c_{0}\leq\lambda_{1}\leq\lambda_{2}\leq\cdots\leq H
. The underlying set
\mathcal{K}
may be a union of several
\mathcal{K}_{i}
or simply a single one. Let

M(\mathcal{K})=\max_{(\chi,\chi^{\prime})\in K^{2}}\operatorname{cond}(\bar{\chi}\chi^{\prime}).

(3.2)

Clearly,
M(\mathcal{K})=O(1)
if
\mathcal{K}=\mathcal{K}_{i}
,
i\geq 1
. This would be an advantage when estimating the value of
S
, as we can see below.

Lemma 3.1
.

Let
x,y,z,\operatorname{\varepsilon},\Lambda
be given. Let
k=2(\frac{2}{3}+3x+y+z)
in general and
k=2(\frac{1}{3}+3x+y+z)
if
M(\mathcal{K})=O(1)
. Then for
q\geq q(x,y,z,\operatorname{\varepsilon})
we have

\sum_{j\geq 1}e^{-k\max\{\lambda_{j},\Lambda\}}\leq(1+\operatorname{\varepsilon})B(x,y,z,\Lambda),

(3.3)

where

B(x,y,z,\Lambda)=\frac{1}{xz}\left(\frac{1}{2}+\frac{1/3+x}{y}\right)\sqrt{B_{0}(1/3+x+y,\Lambda)B_{0}(z,\Lambda)}

(3.4)

and

B_{0}(u,\lambda)=\frac{1-e^{-2u\lambda}}{6\lambda}+\frac{(1-e^{-u\lambda})^{2}}{\lambda^{2}}.

(3.5)

Proof.

This is a consequence of
[
10
, Theorem C]
, with a change on the notations of parameters. In fact, we have a even sharper bound

\sum_{j\geq 1}e^{-k\lambda_{j}-\frac{1/3+x+y+z}{2}\max\{\Lambda-\lambda_{j},0\}}\leq(1+\operatorname{\varepsilon})B(x,y,z,\Lambda).

Since in any case
\frac{1}{2}(1/3+x+y+z)\leq k
, we see (
3.3
) follows readily. ∎

To apply this lemma, for any
\Lambda\in(0,H]
we separate
S
into two parts, that is,

T=T(\Lambda)=\sum_{j\geq 1}e^{-\delta^{-1}\max\{\lambda_{j},\Lambda\}},\quad R=R(\Lambda)=S-T(\Lambda).

(3.6)

For
q\geq q(x_{1},y_{1},z_{1},x_{1},y_{2},z_{2},\operatorname{\varepsilon})
and
k_{1},k_{2}\leq\delta^{-1}
, we have by Lemma
3.1
that

T(\Lambda)\leq(1+\operatorname{\varepsilon})e^{-(\delta^{-1}-k_{1})\Lambda}B(x_{1},y_{1},z_{1},\Lambda)

(3.7)

and

T(\Lambda)\leq(1+\operatorname{\varepsilon})e^{-(\delta^{-1}-k_{2})\Lambda}B(x_{2},y_{2},z_{2},\Lambda)

(3.8)

if
M(\mathcal{K})=O(1)
. Choosing optimal parameters for each given
\Lambda
we obtain the following bounds of
T(\Lambda)
.

Corollary 3.2
.

Let
q
be sufficiently large. We have

T(\Lambda)\leq\begin{cases}0.675,&\Lambda=3.08,\\
5.899,&\Lambda=1.58,\\
7.715,&\Lambda=1.348,\\
8.030,&\Lambda=1.311,\\
8.211,&\Lambda=1.29,\\

(3.9)

and

T(\Lambda)\leq\begin{cases}50e^{-2\Lambda},&\Lambda\geq 3.08,\\

We only need to explain the estimate

(3.11)

\frac{\psi_{j}-\psi}{e^{\delta^{-1}(\Lambda-\lambda_{j})}-1}=\frac{1}{F_{x}(-\lambda_{0})}\int_{0}^{2/x}F_{x}(u)e^{-u(\Lambda-\lambda_{0})}\frac{e^{u(\Lambda-\lambda_{j})}-1}{e^{\delta^{-1}(\Lambda-\lambda_{j})}-1}du

(3.27)

increases as
\lambda_{j}
increases. Hence by our labelling of
\lambda_{j}
,

\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}j\geq 3\\
\lambda_{j}\leq\Lambda\end{subarray}}(e^{-\delta{-1}\lambda_{j}}-e^{-\delta^{-1}\Lambda})\leq\frac{e^{-\delta^{-1}\lambda^{*}}-e^{-\delta^{-1}\Lambda}}{\psi^{*}-\psi}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}j\geq 3\\
\lambda_{j}\leq\Lambda\end{subarray}}(\psi_{j}-\psi),

(3.28)

where

\psi^{*}=\frac{F_{x}(\lambda^{*}-\lambda_{0})}{F_{x}(-\lambda_{0})}.

(3.29)

Recall that by Lemma
3.3
we have

N(\Delta^{2}-\xi-\operatorname{\varepsilon})+2\Delta\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}j\geq 1\\
\lambda_{j}\leq\Lambda\end{subarray}}(\psi_{j}-\psi)\leq 1-\xi.

(3.30)

To make full use of this inequality,
for any given
\lambda_{0}
we will choose
\Lambda
to be as large as possible such that there exists
x\geq\max\{0.8\lambda_{0},2\delta\}
for which
\Delta^{2}>\xi
. Hence
\Delta^{2}\approx\xi
and with minor loss we obtain

\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}j\geq 1\\
\lambda_{j}<\Lambda\end{subarray}}(\psi_{j}-\psi)\leq\frac{1-\xi}{2\Delta},

(3.31)

which is exactly
[
10
, Theorem J]
.
When
\lambda_{0}=0.4
, the best choice is
\Lambda=1.273
, similarly we have
\Lambda=1.311
for
\lambda_{0}=0.5
and
\Lambda=1.348
for
\lambda_{0}=0.6
. Note that by Lemma
2.1
,
\lambda_{1}\leq 0.4
will imply
\lambda_{2}\geq 1.29
which is larger than
1.273
. This presents the main difference between cases (1)-(3) and (4)-(6).

Gathering these inequalities, we finally arrive at

R(\Lambda)\leq\sum_{j=1}^{2}(e^{-\delta^{-1}\lambda_{j}}-e^{-\delta^{-1}\Lambda})+\frac{e^{-\delta^{-1}\lambda^{*}}-e^{-\delta^{-1}\Lambda}}{\psi^{*}-\psi}\left(\frac{1-\xi}{2\Delta}-\sum_{j=1}^{2}(\psi_{j}-\psi)\right),

(3.32)

provided
x\geq\max\{0.8\lambda_{0},2\delta\}
,
\Delta>\sqrt{\xi}
and
q
sufficiently large. Choosing optimal
x
in each case gives the following estimates.

Corollary 3.5
.

Let
q
be sufficiently large, then we have

R(\Lambda)\leq\begin{cases}0.2668,&\Lambda=1.273,\quad\lambda_{1}=1.08,\quad\lambda_{2}=1.08,\quad\lambda^{*}=1.08,\\
0.3175,&\Lambda=1.311,\quad\lambda_{1}=0.97,\quad\lambda_{2}=0.97,\quad\lambda^{*}=0.97,\par\\
0.3487,&\Lambda=1.311,\quad\lambda_{1}=0.92,\quad\lambda_{2}=0.92,\quad\lambda^{*}=0.92,\\
0.6720,&\Lambda=1.348,\quad\lambda_{1}=0.60,\quad\lambda_{2}=0.60,\quad\lambda^{*}=0.702.\end{cases}

Since we require
x\geq 2\delta
, by
[
10
, Theorem K]
the above bounds are still valid when
\lambda_{1}
and
\lambda_{2}
get larger than the preassigned values.

The main innovation of our argument is an estimate of
R(\Lambda)
when
M(\mathcal{K})=O(1)
, which provides the crucial saving compared to Pintz’s argument. Recall that when
M(\mathcal{K})=O(1)
, we have

N(\Delta^{2}-\operatorname{\varepsilon})+2\Delta\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}j\geq 1\\
\lambda_{j}\leq\Lambda\end{subarray}}(\psi_{j}-\psi)\leq 1.

(3.33)

If we argue as before, it would follow

\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}j\geq 1\\
\lambda_{j}\leq\Lambda\end{subarray}}(\psi_{j}-\psi)\leq\frac{1}{2\Delta},

(3.34)

which is actually worse than (
3.31
) since we lose a
-\xi
in the numerator. The key observation is that, by our setting of
\Lambda
, we have
\Delta^{2}\approx\xi
and thus an additional assumption
N\geq N_{0}\geq 2
will imply, approximately, a better bound

\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}j\geq 1\\
\lambda_{j}\leq\Lambda\end{subarray}}(\psi_{j}-\psi)\leq\frac{1-N_{0}\xi}{2\Delta}.

(3.35)

In our situation we always have
1/\Delta^{2}>6
and
R(\Lambda)
contains at least three terms. So the value of
N_{0}
will be chosen from {4,5,6}. In this way, we have either
N\leq N_{0}-1
, or

R(\Lambda)\leq\sum_{j=1}^{2}(e^{-\delta^{-1}\lambda_{j}}-e^{-\Delta^{-1}\Lambda})+\frac{e^{-\delta^{-1}\lambda^{*}}-e^{-\delta^{-1}\Lambda}}{\psi^{*}-\psi}\left(\frac{1-N_{0}\Delta^{2}}{2\Delta}-\sum_{j=1}^{2}(\psi_{j}-\psi)\right).

(3.36)

Experimental results show that the optimal choice of
N_{0}
is
N_{0}=4
or
N_{0}=5
. For comparison, we can deduce from (
3.20
) the bound
N\leq 6
. This saving is of most importance in cases (5) and (6), where all terms in

have almost equal sizes.
This leads to the following estimate of

0.0985,&\Lambda=1.311,\quad\lambda_{1}=0.97,\quad\lambda_{2}=0.97,\quad\lambda^{*}=0.97\\

Cases (4)-(6)

In cases (4)-(6) the structure of
R_{i}
can be complicated. We will divide the sum into two parts, that is,

\sum_{i\leq i_{0}}S_{i}^{2}+\sum_{i>i_{0}}S_{i}^{2}.

Expanding the squares, the second part can be bounded by

(\max_{i>i_{0}}T_{i})(\sum_{i\geq 1}T_{i}+2\sum_{i>i_{0}}R_{i})+(\max_{i>i_{0}}R_{i})\sum_{i>i_{0}}R_{i}-(\max_{i>i_{0}}T_{i})\sum_{i\leq i_{0}}T_{i}.

In practice, the estimate of
T_{i}
for
i\leq i_{0}

i>i_{0}

\min_{i\leq i_{0}}T_{i}\geq\max_{i>i_{0}}T_{i}

\sum_{i\leq i_{0}}S_{i}^{2}-(\max_{i>i_{0}}T_{i})\sum_{i\leq i_{0}}T_{i}=\sum_{i\leq i_{0}}(R_{i}^{2}+2R_{i}T_{i})+\sum_{i\leq i_{0}}(T_{i}-\max_{i>i_{0}}T_{i})T_{i}

T_{i}
