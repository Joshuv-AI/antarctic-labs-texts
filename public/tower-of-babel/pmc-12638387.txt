LETM2 regulates mitochondrial function and autophagy in diabetic foot ulcers: implications for oxidative stress and therapeutic targeting
Authors: Nan Su, Jiwei Wang, Zhiwei Xue, Shujie Peng, Wenchen Xing, Haoyong Jin, Jiangli Zhao, Yanya He, Jiaqi Shi, Wenbo Li, Qijian Wei, Bin Huang, Chao Li, Xinyu Wang, Pei Liu, Ning Yang
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Abstract Background A major complication of diabetes, diabetic foot ulcers (DFU), has been linked to mitochondrial dysfunction and oxidative stress. Here, we performed analysis of RNA sequencing data from publicly available databases, including single-cell assays, to identify dysregulated genes associated with these processes in DFU and the specific cell types involved. Methods Analysis of transcriptome sequencing was performed on GSE134431 and GSE80178 datasets from the GEO database to identify differentially expressed mitochondrial-related genes (DE-MRGs) in DFU samples relative to controls. LASSO and SVM-RFE algorithms were used to identify critical genes, and functional enrichment analyses were conducted to identify associated pathways. Results were validated in a cohort of DFU samples and controls processed at our institution, and single-cell RNA sequencing data from GSE245703 was used to determine expression of candidate genes in endothelial cells from DFU relative to non-DFU samples. SiRNA knockdown of one candidate gene, LETM2, was performed in human umbilical vein endothelial cells (HUVECs) exposed to H2O2 as a model system for oxidative stress. Results LETM2 emerged as key gene significantly associated with DFU from our integrated analysis of the DFU RNA sequencing datasets. Single-cell RNA sequencing data revealed increased expression of LETM2 in endothelial cells from DFU samples compared to controls. Transcriptome sequencing of RNA from HUVECs with LETM2 knockdown under oxidative stress conditions revealed corelated expression with other genes involved in mitochondrial function. HUVECs with LETM2 knockdown furthermore showed impaired mitochondrial fission and mitophagy, as well as decreased cell viability and increased apoptosis in vitro. Conclusions These findings indicate a role for LETM2 in preserving mitochondrial function in HUVECs under oxidative stress and underscore its potential as a therapeutic target for mitigating mitochondrial dysfunction in diabetic complications such as DFU.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1007/s10142-025-01748-1.Keywords: Diabetic foot ulcers, Transcriptome sequencing, Machine learning, LETM2, Mitochondrial function

Introduction

Diabetic foot ulcers (DFU) represent one of the most severe complications of diabetes, with a global prevalence of approximately 6.3% (Wang et al. 2024). Epidemiological studies have demonstrated that the mortality rate in DFU patients is significantly higher compared to diabetic patients without foot ulcers, with rates of 231/1000 person-years and 182/1000 person-years, respectively (Armstrong et al. 2023). DFU not only significantly impacts patient quality of life but also imposes a substantial burden on healthcare costs and public health systems. While current treatment strategies, including surgical debridement, pressure offloading, treatment of lower limb ischemia, and management of foot infections are employed, the overall therapeutic outcomes remain suboptimal (An et al. 2021).

The healing of DFU is a critical aspect of diabetic foot management. Successful wound healing can effectively prevent the progression of inflammation and avert the development of gangrene. Vascularization plays a pivotal role in this process. Newly formed blood vessels not only supply the ulcer site with essential nutrients, oxygen, and growth factors, but also promote wound repair (Flournoy et al. 2022; Yuan et al. 2025). However, studies have shown that hyperglycemic environments can impair endothelial cell function, leading to compromised angiogenesis and reduced capillary density (Okonkwo and DiPietro 2017; Jiang et al. 2023a, b). Seitz et al. reported that the levels of VEGF-A, a key regulator of endothelial homeostasis, are significantly lower in diabetic ulcers than in normal wounds (Seitz et al. 2010), while Chakraborty et al. demonstrated that endothelial cells in diabetic ulcers are subjected to oxidative stress, resulting in endothelial cell dysfunction and induction of apoptosis (Chakraborty et al. 2022). Such mechanisms lead to endothelial cell dysfunction and impaired angiogenic capacity, which are major contributors to poor DFU healing.

Mitochondria are central organelles involved in cellular energy metabolism and the regulation of cell death (Tait and Green 2012). Mitophagy, a selective process for the removal of damaged mitochondria, plays a critical role in maintaining mitochondrial homeostasis, thereby ensuring the proper functioning of the cell. Mitochondrial dynamics, particularly the processes of mitochondrial fusion and fission, are essential for maintaining cellular energy balance and physiological processes (Yazdankhah et al. 2021; Noguchi and Kasahara 2018). Despite the relatively low mitochondrial content in endothelial cells, mitochondria act as key sensors for cellular responses to environmental changes and stress, directly influencing endothelial cell viability, structure, and function. Mitochondrial dynamics and mitophagy participate in coordinating cellular responses to various stressors by regulating the morphology, localization, and degradation of mitochondria (Zhang et al. 2023). Numerous studies have shown that maintaining normal mitochondrial dynamics and activating mitophagy can improve mitochondrial function and enhance endothelial cell tolerance to oxidative stress induced by hyperglycemia (Zhang et al. 2023; Li et al. 2022). However, the precise regulation of mitophagy and its impact on endothelial cell function in the specific environment of DFU remains insufficiently explored.

In this study, we systematically identified differentially expressed mitochondrial-related gene clusters in the DFU lesions by analyzing publicly available data from the GEO database. Through functional enrichment analysis, LASSO regression and Support Vector Machine algorithms, we explored the potential roles of candidate genes in the pathological process of DFU. Through analysis of publicly available single-cell expression data, we investigated candidate gene expression in endothelial cells in DFU samples. The response of human umbilical vein endothelial cells (HUVECs) with siRNA knockdown of one of these candidate genes, LETM2, was assessed in a simulated diabetic microenvironment, established through hydrogen peroxide-induced oxidative damage. The results support LETM2 as a potential prognostic marker of DFU and a therapeutic molecular target for counteracting diabetic endothelial cell injury.

Materials and methods

Ethics statement

The study protocol was approved by the ethics committee of the ethics committee of Qilu Hospital of Shandong University (ethics number 20210701, approved on May 13, 2021), and the trial procedures were conducted in accordance with the principles of the Declaration of Helsinki, with each participant providing informed consent after being fully informed about the study.

Data acquisition

The series matrix data files for GSE134431 (https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/geo/query/acc.cgi?acc=GSE134431) and GSE80178 (https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/geo/query/acc.cgi?acc=GSE80178) were downloaded from the GEO database (https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/geo/) with the GPL18573 and GPL16686 platforms, respectively. The ‘SVA’ package (v3.46.0) was employed for dataset merging. A total of 30 transcriptome datasets were included as the training set, comprising 11 diabetic skin samples and 19 DFU samples. The validation cohort consisted of independent sequencing data, which included 10 DFU and 10 diabetic control samples generated through an independent sequencing project conducted in collaboration with Novogene (Beijing, China). While the genes were initially identified in tissue samples, blood samples were used for validation to assess the broader applicability and potential systemic relevance of the identified genes. This approach allows us to explore whether these biomarkers, originally linked to tissue-specific expression in DFUs, also play a role in peripheral blood, which may have implications for non-invasive diagnostics. Detailed sample information and the corresponding gene expression matrix are provided in the Supplementary Materials. Single cell sequencing data from GSE245703 (https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/geo/query/acc.cgi?acc=GSE245703) were downloaded from the GEO database for further analysis. The dataset includes 7 non-diabetic foot ulcer samples and 5 diabetic foot ulcer samples. Mitochondrial-related genes (MRGs) were obtained from the MitoCarta 3.0 database (https://www.broadinstitute.org/mitocarta/mitocarta30-inventory-mammalian-mitochondrial-proteins-and-pathways).

Differential expression analysis of mitochondrial-related genes

The raw expression matrix was preprocessed with quantile normalization. Differential expression analysis was performed with the ‘Limma’ package, which constructs a linear model. Specifically, the empirical Bayes method was applied to estimate the standardized mean difference (SMD) of gene expression values, and multiple hypothesis testing was corrected with the Benjamini-Hochberg procedure. Differentially expressed genes (DEGs) were selected based on a significance threshold with a p-value of < 0.05. To visually present the differential expression profile, a volcano plot was performed using the OmicStudio tools at https://www.omicstudio.cn/tool.

Functional enrichment analysis of mitochondrial-related differentially expressed genes

Functional annotation was performed on the mitochondrial-related DEGs identified with the ‘clusterProfiler’ package. Gene Ontology (GO) analysis covered three main functional domains: (Wang et al. 2024) Biological Process (BP) (Armstrong et al. 2023), Molecular Function (MF), and (An et al. 2021) Cellular Component (CC). Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) pathway analysis focused on the disturbance patterns in metabolic pathways and signaling networks. Significant enrichment was defined by a p-value of < 0.05. Visualization of the results was performed with the ‘enrichplot’ package, which included bubble plots depicting the enrichment factor and gene count. All analyses were performed with the human genome annotation library ‘org.Hs.eg.db’ for gene ID conversion.

Machine learning-based biomarker identification and validation

Feature gene selection

A dual-model ensemble strategy was employed to identify biomarkers associated with DFU. The Least Absolute Shrinkage and Selection Operator (LASSO) algorithm was implemented with the ‘glmnet’ package, with 10-fold cross-validation to determine the optimal penalty coefficient λ (lambda.min standard was used for gene selection). The model was optimized by minimizing the binomial deviance. The Support Vector Machine-Recursive Feature Elimination (SVM-RFE) model was constructed with the ‘e1071’ package, with a radial basis function (RBF) kernel. Feature ranking was performed through 10 repetitions of 10-fold cross-validation. The intersection of the feature genes identified by both algorithms was generated with the ‘VennDiagram’ package, and the final core biomarker set was determined. Given the limited sample size (n = 30), the machine learning analysis was designed as an exploratory feature selection approach rather than as a predictive modeling exercise, with the goal of narrowing down biologically plausible candidates for further validation.

Model construction and validation

To evaluate prediction performance, the receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curve was plotted with the ‘pROC’ package, and the area under the curve (AUC) along with its 95% confidence interval were calculated. External validation was conducted with the RNA-seq data previously generated by our group.

Single-cell transcriptome analysis of the LETM2 expression profile

Raw single-cell RNA-seq data underwent the following quality control (QC) steps. Cells with fewer than 200 detected genes and genes expressed in fewer than three cells were removed. Potential doublets were identified and excluded using Scrublet (default threshold = 0.24, validated by manual inspection). Cells with mitochondrial gene proportion > 20% were excluded. Library size normalization was performed using Scanpy (scanpy.pp.normalize_total, target_sum = 1e4), followed by log1p transformation. The top 4,000 highly variable genes were selected based on the mean–variance relationship (scanpy.pp.highly_variable_genes, flavor=’seurat’). The effects of total UMI count, mitochondrial gene proportion, and ribosomal gene proportion were regressed out (scanpy.pp.regress_out).

Dimensionality reduction was performed via principal component analysis (PCA). Batch effects across datasets were corrected using Harmony (scanpy.external.pp.harmony_integrate) with 37 principal components, (n_pcs = 37), with the number of PCs selected based on the elbow plot of explained variance. UMAP embedding was computed on the integrated data (scanpy.tl.umap), and Leiden clustering was performed on the neighborhood graph. The expression distribution of LETM2 across DFU and control cells was visualized on UMAP plots (scanpy.pl.umap), and its differential expression across defined cell subpopulations was shown using dot plots (scanpy.pl.dotplot).

Cell culture and treatment

Human umbilical vein endothelial cells (HUVECs) were generously provided by the Department of Cardiology, Qilu Hospital of Shandong University. Cells were cultured at 37 °C in a humidified atmosphere with 5% CO2 in endothelial cell medium (Catalog No. 1001, ScienCell; Carlsbad, CA, USA) supplemented with 15% fetal bovine serum (FBS, Catalog No. 0025, ScienCell), 1% endothelial cell growth supplement (ECGS, Catalog No. 1052, ScienCell), and 1% penicillin/streptomycin solution (P/S, Catalog No. 0503, ScienCell). Oxidative stress was induced by incubating the cells in 300 µM hydrogen peroxide (H2O2, MM0707-500ML, Shanghai Maokang Biotechnology Co., Ltd.; Shanghai, China) as previously described (Huang et al. 2019).

Knockdown with small interfering RNA (siRNA)

When HUVECs reached 60–70% confluence, the culture medium was replaced with serum-free medium. Transfection complexes were prepared according to the manufacturer’s instructions for Lipofectamine 3000 (L3000150, Thermo Fisher Scientific; Waltham, MA, USA). The complexes were incubated in serum-free medium for 20 min at 25 °C, and cells were transfected for 6 h. After transfection, the complete culture medium was replaced, and cells were treated with 300 µM hydrogen peroxide (H2O2) for 48 h. Cells were then collected for subsequent analysis, and mRNA levels were assessed with qRT-PCR, while protein levels were quantitated on western blot. The custom-designed siRNA sequences are provided in Supplementary Table 1.

Quantitative real-time PCR

Total RNA was extracted with the RNA-Quick Purification Kit (ES-RN001, Yishan Biotechnology; Shanghai, China) following the manufacturer’s instructions. cDNA was synthesized from total RNA with the Hifair III 1 st Strand cDNA Synthesis SuperMix Kit for qPCR (gDNA digester plus; 11141ES60, Yeasen Biotechnology; Gaithersburg, MD, USA). qRT-PCR was performed with the Hieff qPCR SYBR Green Master Mix (without Rox; 11201ES08, Yeasen Biotechnology) on the Roche 480II Real-Time PCR Detection System (Roche, Basel, Switzerland). The expression of target mRNA was normalized to β-actin mRNA expression, which served as the reference gene. The primer sequences used are provided in Supplementary Table 2.

Cell counting Kit-8 (CCK-8) assay

HUVECs were seeded at a density of 5 × 10³ cells per well in a 96-well plate, and cell viability was assessed at 0, 24, 48, and 72 h post-treatment. A volume of 10 µL of CCK-8 reagent (CCK-8; CK04-500, Dojindo; Kumamoto, Japan) was added to each well, and the plates were incubated for 1 h at 37 °C in the dark. Absorbance was measured at 450 nm on a microplate reader (model 680, Bio-Rad; Hercules, CA, USA).

Apoptosis assay

Apoptosis was assessed following the manufacturer’s instructions (Yeasen Biotechnology, #40302ES50). Cells were first seeded in 6-well plates and treated for 48 h. After centrifugation, the cells were collected and resuspended in annexin binding buffer. Subsequently, 5 µl of Annexin V-FITC and 10µl of PI were added to the cell suspension. After a 15-minute incubation, apoptosis was analyzed using flow cytometry.

Immunofluorescence

HUVECs were seeded at a density of 4 × 10³ cells per well in a confocal-compatible dish (µ-Dish 35 mm, ibidi). After 12 h of attachment at 37 °C, cells were treated as per experimental conditions. After 48 h of treatment, cells were fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde for 15 min at room temperature, permeabilized with 0.1% Triton X-100 for 10 min, and blocked with 5% bovine serum albumin (BSA) for 1 h. Cells were incubated overnight at 4 °C with appropriately diluted primary antibodies. After washing, species-specific fluorescently labeled secondary antibodies were applied at a 1:2000 dilution and incubated for 1 h at room temperature. TRITC-phalloidin (40734ES75, Yeasen) was used to label F-actin, while DAPI (C1002, Beyotime Biotechnology; Haimen, Jiangsu, China) was used for nuclear counterstaining (30 min). Images were captured with a Leica TCS SP8 confocal microscope (Leica Microsystems; Wetzlar, Germany).

Mitochondria imaging

HUVECs were seeded at a density of 4 × 10³ cells per well in 35 mm confocal dishes (µ-Dish 35 mm, ibidi GmbH, Gräfelfing, Germany) and cultured at 37 °C for 12 h to allow attachment. After 48 h of experimental treatment, cells were washed three times with PBS, followed by incubation with 25 nM MitoTracker Red (Invitrogen, Molecular Probes; Waltham, MA, USA) at 37 °C for 30 min. The cells were then washed three times with PBS, permeabilized with 3% BSA and Triton X-100 at room temperature for 30 min, and washed again with PBS. The cells were incubated with anti-MAP1LC3B (Microtubule-associated protein 1 light chain 3 beta) antibody (12741, Cell Signaling Technology; Danvers, MA, USA) to visualize LC3B expression, for 2 h at room temperature. Afterwards, cells were incubated with appropriate species-specific fluorescently labeled secondary antibodies at a dilution of 1:2000 for 1 h at room temperature. Nuclei were counterstained with DAPI (C1002, Beyotime Biotechnology) for 30 min. Images were acquired with a Leica TCS SP8 confocal microscope (Leica Microsystems).

Western blotting

Cells were washed with PBS and lysed with RIPA buffer (containing a protease/phosphatase inhibitor mixture, P0013B, Beyotime Biotechnology) on ice. The lysates were sonicated (3 × 10 s, 20% power), and the debris was removed by centrifugation at 12,000 × g for 15 min at 4 °C. The protein concentration was determined with the BCA assay (P0009, Beyotime Biotechnology), with bovine serum albumin as the standard. Equal amounts of protein (30 µg) were mixed with 5× loading buffer and heated at 100 °C for 10 min. Proteins were separated by 10% SDS-PAGE (at a constant voltage of 80–120 V) and transferred to a 0.45 μm PVDF membrane (BSP0161, PALL; Port Washington, NY, USA) through wet transfer (230 mA, 90 min). The membranes were blocked with 5% non-fat milk at room temperature for 1 h, and then incubated with primary antibodies followed by incubation with HRP-conjugated secondary antibodies (ZB-2301/ZB-2305, 1:5000, Beijing ZhongShan Golden Bridge Biotechnology; Beijing, China). Signals were detected with ECL detection reagents (36208ES76, Yeasen). After the initial exposure, the membrane was stripped in a rapid stripping buffer (PS107, Yeasen) to remove the primary and secondary antibodies. The membrane was then re-blocked and re-probed with additional antibodies as needed. Primary antibodies used were the following: DRP1 (8570), phospho616-DRP1 (4494), phospho637-DRP1 (6319), PINK1 (6946), BNIP3L/NIX(12396), BNIP3 (44060), MAP1LC3B (12741), and NDP52 (60732) from Cell Signaling Technology; and LETM2 (17180-1-AP), PARKIN (14060-1-AP) and β-actin (66009-1-Ig) from Proteintech (Rosemont, IL, USA).

Transcriptome sequencing analysis

Three biological replicates of the siRNA negative control group (si-NC) and LETM2 knockdown group (si-LETM2#2) were sent for Illumina NovaSeq 6000 sequencing (PE150) at Novogene (Beijing, China). Each group (si-LETM2#2 and si-NC) included 3 biological replicates. A total of 40.16 Gb of clean bases were obtained, with each sample exceeding 6.55 Gb of clean bases. The percentage of Q20 bases was > 98.65%, and the percentage of Q30 bases was > 96.02%. Data were aligned with the Homo sapiens reference genome version hg38 (available at: https://ftp.ensembl.org/pub/release-94/fasta/homo_sapiens/dna/Homo_sapiens.GRCh38.dna.primary_assembly.fa.gz; https://ftp.ensembl.org/pub/release-94/gtf/homo_sapiens/Homo_sapiens.GRCh38.94.gtf.gz). Clean reads from each sample were aligned to the reference genome using fast and accurate alignment methods, and the mapping rates for each sample ranged from 93.81% to 95.23%. A total of 20,579 expressed genes were identified. DEGs were selected with DESeq2 (|log2FC| >0, P < 0.05). Gene Ontology (GO) and Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) pathway enrichment analyses were performed with the clusterProfiler package. GSEA was performed to identify LETM2-related regulatory gene sets in HUVECs with LETM2 knockdown. Genes were ranked based on log2 fold change (logFC) between low and high LETM2 expression groups. GSEA was conducted using gene sets from the c5.go.symbols.gmt (GO) and c2.cp.kegg.symbols.gmt (KEGG) databases. Significant pathways (P < 0.05) were visualized using GSEA plots.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analyses were conducted with GraphPad Prism (version 10; GraphPad Software; Boston, MA, USA). Results are presented as mean ± standard deviation (SD). For comparisons between two independent groups with equal variance, a two-tailed Student’s t-test was applied. One-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) was used to assess differences among multiple groups with equal variance. For quantitative analyses, each experiment was performed in triplicate to ensure reliability. A p-value of < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

Identification and functional analysis of differentially expressed mitochondrial-related genes (DE-MRGs)

Figure 1A depicts the flowchart of our study. We first performed differential gene expression analysis on the GSE134431 and GSE80178 datasets, which together included 30 diabetic foot samples with 19 from ulcer tissue and 11 from adjacent skin, followed by functional enrichment analysis (Supplementary Fig. 1 A). The enrichment results indicated that the DEGs were significantly associated with mitophagy pathways (Supplementary Fig. 1B-E). Given that diabetic foot ulcer (DFU) is closely linked to hyperglycemia-induced mitochondrial damage—leading to excessive reactive oxygen species (ROS) production, inflammation, and apoptosis, which together impair wound healing (Fang et al. 2025)—and that mitochondrial injury has been reported as essential in non-healing diabetic wounds (Jiang et al. 2023a, b), we aimed to further investigate mitochondrial-related mechanisms in DFU. To ensure a robust and functionally relevant mitochondrial gene subset, we intersected the genes from GSE134431 and GSE80178 datasets with a curated mitochondrial-related gene set from the MitoCarta 3.0 database (Fig. 1B). Differential expression analysis was performed between DFU and diabetic skin tissue using the mitochondrial-related gene set. A total of 240 differentially expressed mitochondrial-related genes (DE-MRGs) were identified with a p-value threshold of < 0.05. In this set, 114 genes were upregulated (47.5%) and 126 were downregulated (52.5%) (Fig. 1C). Functional annotation of these 240 DE-MRGs was performed with the ‘clusterProfiler’ package in R, including Gene Ontology (GO) functional analysis and Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) pathway enrichment analysis. The results were visualized for clarity. KEGG pathway enrichment analysis revealed 51 significantly enriched pathways, primarily associated with mitophagy in animals, chemical carcinogenesis involving reactive oxygen species, thermogenesis, ribosomes, diabetic cardiomyopathy, and glycine, serine, and threonine metabolism, among others (Fig. 1D). GO enrichment analysis identified 514 functional terms, including 377 biological processes, 47 cellular components, and 90 molecular functions. The top 30 significantly enriched terms for each category were visualized (Figs. 1E-G). Biological process analysis showed significant enrichment in mitochondrial gene expression, mitochondrial translation, cellular respiration, energy derivation by oxidation of organic compounds, mitochondrial transport, and organic acid catabolic processes. Cellular component analysis revealed significant enrichment in the mitochondrial matrix, mitochondrial inner membrane, mitochondrial protein-containing complexes, organelle outer membrane, outer membrane, and mitochondrial outer membrane. Molecular function analysis highlighted significant enrichment in the structural constituents of ribosomes, electron transfer activity, oxidoreductase activity (acting on CH − OH group donors), lyase activity, iron-sulfur cluster binding, and metal cluster binding.

Identification of DFU-related characteristic differentially expressed mitochondrial-related genes (DE-MRGs)

A dual-algorithm strategy combining LASSO regression and SVM-RFE was employed to identify characteristic genes related to DFU from the GSE134431 and GSE80178 datasets. Meanwhile, our transcriptome-sequencing dataset, which included 10 DFU and 10 diabetic control samples, was used as the validation cohort. The specific results of the transcriptome sequencing can be found in Supplementary Fig. 2. Initially, the LASSO regression algorithm was used to select DE-MRGs. By choosing the ‘lambda.min’ parameter, we identified eight characteristic genes: TRAP1, MTHFD1L, DCAKD, LETM2, PUSL1, MAOB, NOCT, and CPT1C (Fig. 2A and B). Subsequently, SVM-RFE analysis identified the optimal feature subset size as 6 genes, corresponding to the peak cross-validation accuracy (Fig. 2C) and minimal classification error (Fig. 2D). The top 6 ranked genes were: LETM2, STARD7, SAMM50, YRDC, RFK, and ACSL1. Intersection analysis of the results from both algorithms identified LETM2 as a consensus biomarker (Fig. 2E). ROC curve analysis based on the training set revealed an AUC value of 0.895 (95% CI: 0.812–0.978) for LETM2 (Fig. 2F), with a sensitivity of 84.21% (95% CI: 68.75%–94.00%) and a specificity of 81.82% (95% CI: 65.67%–92.83%), suggestive of a high diagnostic potential for DFU (Supplementary Table 3).

To further validate the accuracy and reliability of these results, we analyzed the expression levels of the characteristic genes, using our transcriptome-sequencing dataset which included 10 DFU and 10 diabetic control samples. The results showed that LETM2 expression was significantly higher in the DFU group compared to the control group (p = 0.0117, Fig. 3A). We assessed the differential expression between the two groups (Supplementary Fig. 2 A) and have performed ROC analysis on this validation cohort to evaluate the diagnostic potential of LETM2 (Supplementary Fig. 2B). The results showed an AUC of 0.800 (95% CI: 0.600–1.000), with a sensitivity of 90.00% (95% CI: 55.50%–99.70%) and a specificity of 50.00% (95% CI: 18.70%–81.30%) (Supplementary Table 4). Based on these findings, LETM2 may play a crucial role in the development of diabetic foot ulcers and could serve as a potential biomarker.

Single-cell sequencing to determine the expression pattern of LETM2 in endothelial cells from DFU samples

A comprehensive analysis of single-cell RNA sequencing data from 5 DFU and 7 non-diabetic ulcer samples in the GSE245703 dataset was performed. The UMAPs of non-DFU and DFU samples are presented side by side, allowing direct comparison of overall clustering patterns and cell type distributions between groups (Fig. 3B).

Cross-group comparison revealed that LETM2 expression was significantly elevated in endothelial cells from DFU samples compared with non-diabetic ulcer samples (P = 3.74 × 10⁻⁴, Fig. 3C and D). In addition, LETM2 upregulation was also observed in other cell types, including keratinocytes (P = 1.68 × 10⁻²⁴), neutrophils (P = 2.20 × 10⁻⁵), pericytes (P = 1.38 × 10⁻³), and T cells (P = 4.18 × 10⁻³). Despite these widespread changes, our study focused specifically on endothelial cells, as impaired angiogenesis and endothelial dysfunction are well-recognized hallmarks of DFU pathology (Guo et al. 2024). The detailed statistical results of the differences are provided in Supplementary Table 5.

To further investigate the role of LETM2 in the diabetic pathological microenvironment, an oxidative stress model was established by treating HUVECs with 300 µM hydrogen peroxide for 48 h. Immunofluorescence and cytoskeletal staining revealed significant morphological changes in the cells and a marked increase in LETM2 expression (Fig. 3E). Western blot and qPCR analyses confirmed that LETM2 expression was significantly upregulated at both the protein and mRNA levels under oxidative stress conditions (Fig. 3F and G). These findings corroborate the bulk and single-cell sequencing data, strengthening the evidence that LETM2 expression is enhanced under diabetic oxidative stress conditions in HUVECs. Together, these results suggest that LETM2 may play a role in regulating endothelial cell function and contribute to the pathological process of DFU.

Transcriptomic profiling of HUVECs with LETM2 knockdown under oxidative stress conditions

First, to investigate the role of LETM2 in endothelial cell function under diabetic oxidative stress conditions, we knocked down LETM2 in HUVECs with siRNA. Three biological replicates each of siRNA control (si-NC) and LETM2-knockdown (si-LETM2#2) groups were sequenced by Novogene (Beijing, China). The gene silencing efficiency of the two siRNAs was assessed with qRT-PCR and western blot (Fig. 4A and B). Transcriptome profiling in HUVECs under oxidative stress revealed 3,009 differentially expressed genes following LETM2 knockdown. Of these genes, 1,750 were upregulated and 1,259 downregulated in DFU relative to control samples (Fig. 4C). GO functional annotation analysis revealed that the DEGs were primarily enriched in the following biological processes: mitochondrial inner membrane, mitochondrial protein-containing complexes, and energy derivation by oxidation of organic compounds, all of which are associated with energy metabolism (Fig. 4D). KEGG pathway enrichment analysis further showed that the DEGs were significantly concentrated in key signaling pathways, including oxidative phosphorylation, the cell cycle, and apoptosis (Fig. 4E).

LETM2 knockdown inhibits mitochondrial fission-dependent mitophagy in H2O2-induced HUVECs in vitro

Transcriptome analysis indicated that LETM2 knockdown significantly alters expression of genes involved in oxidative phosphorylation pathways and mitochondrial inner membrane associated functions. We therefore next performed a series of functional experiments to determine how LETM2 affected endothelial cell function. First, LETM2 knockdown led to decreased HUVEC cell viability over time, as assessed with the CCK-8 assay (Fig. 5A). Second, flow cytometric analysis of Annexin V/PI-stained cells demonstrated a marked increase in apoptosis in HUVECs with LETM2 knockdown relative to controls (Fig. 5C). Third, MitoTracker staining showed that HUVECs with LETM2 knockdown exhibited a pronounced elongation of the mitochondrial network relative to controls (Fig. 5B). These results suggested a shift in the mitochondrial fusion/fission balance toward fusion. Finally, western blot analysis demonstrated that LETM2 knockdown in HUVECs specifically led to a decrease in the phosphorylation levels of a critical protein involved in mitochondrial fission, dynamin-related protein 1 (DRP1). Phosphorylation of Ser616 in DRP1 was decreased in HUVECs with LETM2 knockdown, while total DRP1 and DRP1 Ser637 phosphorylation levels remained unchanged (Fig. 5E). These results indicated that LETM2 might enhance mitochondrial fission by promoting the phosphorylation of DRP1 at Ser616.

LC3B colocalization with mitochondria indicates mitophagy, mediating the formation of autophagosomes for mitochondrial degradation (Lu et al. 2023; Zhang et al. 2022). To investigate the relationship between LETM2 and mitophagy, we performed LC3B/mitochondria colocalization analysis. The results showed that LETM2 knockdown led to a reduction in autophagosome-mitochondria colocalization points (Fig. 5D), suggesting impaired autophagic flux. In addition, the protein levels of key mitophagy regulatory molecules, including PINK1, PARKIN, NDP52, BNIP3L/NIX, BNIP3 and LC3B-II/LC3B-I, were significantly downregulated (Fig. 5E and F). These data all together suggest that loss of LETM2 impairs mitophagic clearance through a shift in the mitochondrial fusion/fission balance, leading to HUVEC cell death (Fig. 6). Thus, increased expression of LETM2 may promote cell viability of HUVECs under oxidative stress conditions.

Discussion

In healthy individuals, wound healing typically progresses through four overlapping stages: hemostasis, inflammation, proliferation, and remodeling (Tan et al. 2020). However, the healing process in DFU is often significantly delayed due to multiple pathological factors, including impaired angiogenesis, hypoxia, oxidative stress, and neuropathy. Oxidative stress, a key contributor to endothelial cell damage in diabetes, disrupts the intercellular connections between endothelial cells, increases vascular permeability, and exacerbates the progression of both macrovascular and microvascular complications (An et al. 2023; Sun et al. 2025). Therefore, further exploration of the molecular mechanisms underlying endothelial cell injury in DFU is crucial for improving clinical therapeutic outcomes.

In the pathogenesis of diabetes, chronic hyperglycemia leads to the accumulation of advanced glycation end products which significantly upregulates the generation of reactive oxygen species, thereby creating an oxidative stress microenvironment (Liu et al. 2023). Excessive ROS damage mitochondrial structure through various mechanisms, including lipid peroxidation, protein carbonylation, and mitochondrial DNA mutations, ultimately resulting in the uncoupling of the electron transport chain and ATP synthesis dysfunction (Kowalczyk et al. 2021). This collapse of energy metabolism not only directly impairs endothelial cell function but also disrupts essential processes during wound repair, such as cell migration, proliferation, and extracellular matrix remodeling (Qaed et al. 2025). Oxidative stress and mitochondrial dysfunction are considered central mechanisms in the delayed wound healing observed in diabetes, underscoring the importance of mitochondrial dysfunction in the progression of diabetic complications (Xu et al. 2022).

LETM2 was identified as a candidate gene through bioinformatics analysis of publicly available transcriptome datasets, the GSE134431 and GSE80178 datasets, followed by further validation using LASSO and SVM-RFE algorithms. However, given the relatively small sample size (n < 100), the results should be interpreted with caution, as smaller cohorts may lead to more cohort-specific findings. The analysis of single-cell RNA sequencing data revealed a significant upregulation of LETM2 in endothelial cells from DFU tissues (P = 3.74 × 10⁻⁴). This finding was corroborated in vitro, where LETM2 was markedly upregulated in human umbilical vein endothelial cells subjected to oxidative stress, supporting its role as a stress-responsive regulator of endothelial fate in DFU. Although LETM2 upregulation was also observed in keratinocytes, neutrophils, pericytes, and T cells in the single-cell dataset, we focused our investigation on endothelial cells because angiogenesis and endothelial dysfunction are central to DFU pathology and directly determine wound healing outcomes. Functional experiments in LETM2 knockdown cells further associated the upregulation of the gene with a role in mitochondrial energy metabolism and oxidative phosphorylation pathways. These results suggest that enhanced LETM2 expression may be critical in maintaining viability of HUVECs, whereas loss of LETM2 may contribute to the onset and progression of DFU. Therefore, LETM2 may not only serve as an important biomarker for oxidative stress and mitochondrial damage but also holds potential as a therapeutic target in DFU.

LETM2, a member of the SLC55 solute carrier family, is located in the mitochondrial inner membrane (IMM) and functions as a cation/proton antiporter, playing a crucial role in maintaining mitochondrial ion homeostasis (Gyimesi and Hediger 2020). This conserved function is similar to the function of its homolog, LETM1, which has been identified as a prognostic marker for poor outcome in various malignancies. Although the role of LETM2 in oncology has gained increasing attention, its function in non-neoplastic diseases remains underexplored (Dutt et al. 2011; Cheng et al. 2016; Zhou et al. 2022). In metabolic diseases such as DFU, LETM2 may play a dual role in coordinating mitochondrial function, cellular metabolism, and oxidative stress responses, potentially exacerbating disease pathology while also contributing to compensatory adaptive responses. This study is the first to investigate the role of LETM2 under diabetic oxidative stress conditions. We found that LETM2 knockdown significantly impacted oxidative phosphorylation pathways and mitochondrial inner membrane associated functions, leading to increased apoptosis in HUVECs, suggesting that LETM2 plays a protective role in endothelial cells under oxidative stress.

Mitochondrial dynamics, regulated through fission and fusion, control the structure and function of mitochondria, thereby maintaining cellular energy homeostasis (Zacharioudakis and Gavathiotis 2023). Our study revealed that the mitochondrial network in LETM2 knockdown HUVECs exhibited a pronounced elongation phenotype, suggesting that mitochondrial fission is inhibited while fusion is enhanced. This result indicates that the absence of LETM2 may disrupt the balance between mitochondrial fission and fusion, thus affecting mitochondrial quality control mechanisms in HUVECs. Mitochondrial fission not only aids in the separation of damaged mitochondria but also facilitates the clearance of dysfunctional mitochondria through mitophagy (Xian and Liou 2021). Further studies have shown that increased mitochondrial fission promotes autophagy and survival in ovarian and liver cancer cells (Hu et al. 2017; Huang et al. 2016). In addition, in the aggressive brain tumor type glioblastoma multiforme, a protein involved in apoptosis, BCL2L13, through DNM1L-mediated mitochondrial fission, plays a critical role in promoting mitochondrial autophagy (Wang et al. 2023). Mitochondrial fusion is generally associated with improved cell survival due to its role in enhancing content exchange and metabolic efficiency (Bao et al. 2025). However, our data indicate that LETM2 silencing induces an excessive mitochondrial fusion, which disrupts mitochondrial quality control mechanisms. The study pointed out, when fusion is excessive, it can lead to an imbalance in mitochondrial dynamics, affecting their ability to undergo proper fission. This imbalance may hinder the formation of smaller, healthier mitochondria, which are essential for efficient energy production and cellular function. Moreover, excessive fusion can cause mitochondrial DNA (mtDNA) to become exposed to the cytosol, triggering immune responses as the cell perceives this “foreign” DNA as an intruder. This immune reaction can lead to inflammation and, ultimately, contribute to cell death (Yamada et al. 2025).

To further elucidate the relationship between LETM2 and mitophagy, we showed that LETM2 knockdown significantly reduced the colocalization of LC3B with mitochondria, suggesting impaired mitophagic flux. Furthermore, the protein levels of key mitophagy regulators, including PINK1, PARKIN, NDP52, BNIP3L/NIX, BNIP3 and LC3B-II/LC3B-I, were significantly downregulated. These findings suggest that LETM2 depletion may disrupt mitochondrial fission, thereby impairing the mitophagic clearance mechanism.

Although this study provides significant evidence for the protective role of LETM2 under diabetic oxidative stress conditions, there are several limitations. First, while our findings are promising, the relatively small sample size (n < 100) and cohort-specific characteristics warrant caution in generalizing these results. The limited statistical power may lead to findings that are highly specific to this cohort. Future studies with larger, more diverse cohorts are needed to validate these findings and assess the robustness of LETM2 as a biomarker for DFU across different populations. Furthermore, we relied solely on in vitro models, specifically HUVECs, to investigate the function of LETM2. While these models offer valuable insights into cellular mechanisms, they do not fully replicate the complexity of the in vivo environment, where multiple factors influence LETM2 expression and function. Future studies in animal models, such as endothelial-specific LETM2 knockout or overexpression in diabetic mouse models, are needed to further define the role of LETM2 and assess its therapeutic potential in DFU and other diseases associated with mitochondrial dysfunction. These in vivo studies will provide more robust data on the physiological relevance of LETM2 in endothelial cells and its impact on wound healing in diabetic conditions. In addition, while we have demonstrated a protective effect of LETM2 on endothelial cells, the specific molecular pathways and genes mediating its function in mitochondrial autophagy and oxidative stress responses in HUVECs require further investigation. Our current findings provide a strong rationale for targeting LETM2, but additional research is needed to identify the precise mechanisms by which LETM2 regulates mitochondrial dynamics and modulates cellular responses to oxidative stress. Finally, pharmacological modulators of LETM2 are not yet well-established in the literature. Identifying specific inhibitors or activators of LETM2 that can effectively modulate its activity will require extensive preclinical studies and optimization. These efforts will be crucial to assess the safety, efficacy, and translational potential of LETM2-targeted therapies for DFU and other mitochondrial dysfunction-related diseases.

An important limitation of our study is the lack of detailed phenotypic and treatment information for the discovery datasets (GSE134431 and GSE80178), which precluded a thorough analysis of how variables such as age, sex, comorbidities, ulcer severity, and treatment regimens may influence gene expression profiles. Nonetheless, we acknowledge that phenotypic heterogeneity may affect mitochondrial gene expression and could influence the generalizability of our findings. Future multi-center studies with larger sample sizes and harmonized clinical datasets will be essential to evaluate the robustness of LETM2 as a biomarker across diverse DFU patient populations.

In conclusion, LETM2 plays a crucial role in maintaining mitochondrial dynamics and autophagy, particularly in protecting endothelial cells under diabetic oxidative stress conditions. Our study provides new insights into the potential therapeutic role of LETM2 in diseases associated with mitochondrial dysfunction, such as DFU. Future research should further investigate the role of LETM2 in diabetic complications and validate its therapeutic potential as a molecular target, with the aim of developing more precise treatment strategies for diabetic patients.

Conclusions

In conclusion, our study highlights a role for LETM2 in regulating mitochondrial function and the pathogenesis of DFU. We observed that LETM2 knockdown in HUVECs under oxidative stress disrupted mitochondrial dynamics by inducing excessive mitochondrial fusion, impaired mitophagic clearance through downregulation of key regulators, and led to mitochondrial dysfunction and increased apoptosis. The upregulation of LETM2 in DFU tissues highlights the gene as part of an adaptive response to oxidative stress to preserve mitochondrial function and thus mitigate DFU disease progression. These findings furthermore emphasize LETM2 as a biomarker for diagnostic purposes and as a potential therapeutic target for alleviating mitochondrial-related complications in diabetic patients. Further studies are needed to identify the pathways mediating the LETM2 regulatory mechanisms of mitochondrial function and to evaluate its therapeutic potential in clinical settings for managing DFU and other diabetes-related complications.
