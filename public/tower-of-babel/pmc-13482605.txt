High intrapancreatic fat deposition accelerates pancreatic ductal adenocarcinoma growth and metastasis with enrollment of FABP5 upregulation
Authors: Kaori Hamamoto, Shuhei Shinoda, Shigeyuki Suenaga, Toshihiko Matsumoto, Naoki Yamamoto, Naohiko Nakamura, Shin Hamada, Atsushi Masamune, Masato Yamamoto, Taro Takami
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractA prospective observational study has shown that intrapancreatic fat deposition (IPFD) increases pancreatic ductal adenocarcinoma (PDAC) risk; however, the underlying mechanisms remain unclear. In this study, we established a C57BL/6J mouse model of IPFD by feeding them Gubra Amylin NASH (GAN), a high-fat diet, to evaluate the effect of IPFD on PDAC. Mice in the GAN 30-week group exhibited significantly higher IPFD than controls. RNA sequencing of pancreatic tissue from this group revealed increased fatty acid-binding protein (FABP) 5 and MKI67 expression, along with an upregulation of the JAK/STAT, PI3K/AKT/mTOR, and Ras/Raf/MEK/ERK signaling pathways. In a syngeneic orthotopic PDAC mouse model using KPC cells, mice fed a GAN diet showed rapid tumor growth, a high incidence of liver metastasis, and intraperitoneal dissemination. In vitro, FABP5 promoted PDAC cell proliferation and migration by upregulating RAC1, a Rho family small GTPase associated with cellular movement. Inhibition of FABP5 using BMS-309,403 suppressed syngeneic KPC subcutaneous tumor growth without serious side effects. Conclusively, this study demonstrates that high IPFD enhances PDAC carcinogenesis, growth, and metastasis with enrollment of FABP5 upregulation, indicating that FABP5 inhibition may represent a potential therapeutic strategy for PDAC.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-026-56838-2.Keywords: Intrapancreatic fat deposition, IPFD, PDAC, Pancreatic cancer, MetastasisSubject terms: Cancer, Cell biology, Gastroenterology, Oncology

Introduction

Pancreatic ductal adenocarcinoma (PDAC) is the third leading cause of cancer-related death in Japan, with a 5-year survival rate of 13% 1. Known risk factors for PDAC include age, sex, smoking, diabetes, chronic pancreatitis1,2, and obesity, which is also a common risk factor for several other obesity-related cancers2,3. As the prevalence of obesity increases, identifying therapeutic targets for PDAC has become urgent. Adipose tissue (AT) plays a crucial role in PDAC development3,4. Adipokines, inflammation, lipid metabolism, and intrapancreatic fat deposition (IPFD) are closely linked to AT and may contribute to PDAC initiation and progression5–10. Recent studies have further implicated IPFD as a risk factor for PDAC8,9. However, the mechanisms by which IPFD promotes PDAC remain poorly understood. Identifying the key factors associated with IPFD may reveal potential biomarkers and therapeutic targets for PDAC.

Fatty acid-binding proteins (FABPs) are essential in binding and transporting fatty acids in cells and are crucial in signaling, inflammation, and metabolism11,12. FABP5, also known as epidermal FABP, is secreted by adipocytes, macrophages, and dendritic cells 13,14. Previous studies revealed that FABP5 is associated with the development of insulin resistance and chronic inflammation12,15. Furthermore, a meta-analysis showed that FABP5 expression is related to advanced cancer stage, vascular invasion, and lymph node metastasis16. Based on these findings, we hypothesized that FABP5 released from AT in patients with high IPFD could promote tumor progression and distant metastasis. To test this hypothesis, we assessed the effects of FABP5 and IPFD on PDAC progression using a novel C57BL/6J mouse model of IPFD, established by feeding mice a high-fat Gubra Amylin NASH (GAN) diet.

Results

BMS, an FABP inhibitor, suppressed syngeneic KPC subcutaneous tumor growth without serious side effects in mice

To evaluate whether FABP5 inhibition suppresses PDAC growth in vivo, we treated C57BL/6J mice with BMS-309,403(BMS: FABP inhibitor) after subcutaneous tumor establishment and monitored their survival (Fig. 1a). Mice in the BMS group showed a higher survival rate than controls (Fig. 1b). Compared with the control, BMS significantly suppressed tumor growth on day 11 (Fig. 1d-f, Supplemental Table 1). No serious side effects such as weight loss or altered serum biochemistry were observed in BMS-treated mice (Table 1). These results demonstrate that FABP inhibition effectively prevented syngeneic subcutaneous tumor growth without inducing adverse effects.

Mice fed with the GAN diet showed high IPFD

To establish a high IPFD mouse model, the pancreatic tissues of mice fed with a control diet and GAN for 10 weeks were assessed. The pancreatic tissue of mice in the GAN 10-week group showed intralobular and extralobular fatty infiltration (Fig. 2a and b). Moreover, mice in the GAN 30-week group exhibited non-uniform advanced fatty changes in parenchyma and higher percentage of IPFD than those in the control group (Fig. 2c-e). RNA-sequencing analysis of the pancreatic tissue of mice in the GAN 30-week group showed increased FABP5 and MKI67 expression (Fig. 2f and g). Notably, the GAN group also showed upregulation of JAK/STAT, PI3K/AKT/mTOR, and Ras/Raf/MEK/ERK signaling (Fig. 2h-j), suggesting that high IPFD induced by a high-fat diet may promote carcinogenesis through these signaling pathways.

GAN diet accelerated tumor growth and metastasis in syngeneic orthotopic PDAC mouse model

To analyze the effect of high IPFD on tumor growth and metastasis, C57BL/6J mice bearing a syngeneic orthotopic PDAC model were subjected to a 30-week GAN diet (Fig. 3a). Compared with controls, GAN-fed mice developed significantly larger tumor volumes, increased liver metastasis, and greater intraperitoneal dissemination (Fig. 3b-i, Supplemental Table 2).

FABP5 upregulated PDAC cell proliferation and migration in vitro

Cell proliferation was assessed using an MTS assay following treatment with FABP5 or BMS. In both KPC and MIA PaCa-2 cells, FABP5 significantly increased absorbance at 490 nm (Fig. 4a and b), whereas BMS treatment suppressed absorbance in a dose-dependent manner (Fig. 4c and d). Subsequently, the mRNA expression of RAC1, a Rho family small GTPase involved in cellular movement and migration, was assessed17,18. FABP5 significantly upregulated RAC1 expression, which was inhibited by BMS in PDAC cells (Fig. 4e and f). RAC1 inhibition (NSC23766) suppressed cell growth and RAC1 inhibition did not exhibit any additive effect when combined with BMS treatment (Fig. 4g and h). Furthermore, a wound-healing assay was conducted to analyze cell migratory capacity. FABP5 promoted PDAC cell migration, whereas BMS inhibited this effect (Fig. 5a-d). RAC1 inhibition suppressed migratory activity, showing an effect comparable to BMS. RAC1 inhibition did not exhibit any additive effect when combined with BMS treatment. Taken together, the absence of an additive effect suggests that RAC1 likely acts downstream of FABP5 signaling (Fig. 5e and f). Collectively, these results indicate that FABP5 enhances PDAC cell proliferation and migration, effects that are suppressed by BMS treatment.

Discussion

High IPFD is associated with various diseases, including PDAC9,19. In clinical settings, high IPFD is an overlooked physical condition affecting approximately 20% of adults20,21. An observational study by Yamazaki revealed that individuals with high IPFD (> 10%) were associated with pancreatic carcinogenesis8. Clinical evidence has demonstrated the importance of IPFD in PDAC; however, studies elucidating the mechanisms linking IPFD to PDAC remain limited. To our knowledge, previous studies have reported that a high-fat diet induces high IPFD in mouse models; nevertheless, how IPFD influences PDAC remains underexplored20,21. This study revealed that a high-fat diet–induced increase in IPFD elevated MKI67 expression in the pancreas, likely through upregulation of the JAK/STAT, PI3K/AKT/mTOR, and Ras/Raf/MEK/ERK signaling pathways. Notably, high IPFD increased pancreatic FABP5 expression, which accelerated PDAC cell proliferation and migration in vitro. Even though protein-level phosphorylation is not analyzed yet, our results suggested that FABP5 may play an important role in PDAC metastasis. Moreover, our findings align with a recent meta-analysis that reported that FABP5 expression in digestive system malignancies is associated with advanced-stage disease, vascular invasion, and lymph node metastasis16. Recent studies have also reported that FABP5 promotes carcinogenesis, tumor growth, and metastasis in various cancers16,22–24, suggesting that FABP5 may be a therapeutic target25,26.

One limitation of our study is that we used a high-fat diet to establish the high IPFD model. Consequently, the effects of metabolic changes, including elevated blood sugar levels and dyslipidemia, could not be completely excluded in the orthotopic mouse tumor model. To minimize this influence, we focused our analysis on pancreatic tissue. Establishing a high IPFD mouse model through pancreas-specific genetic manipulation, independent of diabetes and dyslipidemia, could lead to a better understanding of IPFD pathophysiology and represents an important future direction. Second limitation is that there is no known FABP5 super-specific inhibitor yet and BMS also inhibit FABP4 activity. Future analysis using the FABP5 knock out mice may help to overcome this limitation and we think this as next challenge.

In conclusion, the C57BL/6J high IPFD mouse model fed the GAN diet showed a higher percentage of IPFD and increased FABP5 and MKI67 expression. Additionally, the GAN group showed upregulation of the JAK/STAT, PI3K/AKT/mTOR, and Ras/Raf/MEK/ERK signaling pathways. These findings indicate that high IPFD may enhance the carcinogenesis, growth, and metastasis of PDAC through elevated FABP5 expression. Inhibition of FABP5 may represent a potential therapeutic strategy for patients with PDAC.

Methods

Cell lines

We used the MIA PaCa-2 (human pancreatic cancer cell line: CRL-1420, ATCC, Manassas, Virginia, USA) and KPC (murine pancreatic cancer cell line). KPC cells were isolated from a genetically modified C57BL/6J mouse model that showed pancreatic cancer with KRASG12D and p53 mutations (KPC mice). These KPC cells were provided by Atsushi Masamune (Tohoku University, Japan)27. MIA PaCa-2 and KPC cells were cultured in Dulbecco’s modified Eagle’s medium (DMEM) with 10% fetal bovine serum. All cells were cultured at 37 °C in a humidified incubator with 5% CO2.

Reagents

Recombinant mouse FABP5 protein (#10007433) was purchased from Cayman Chemicals (Cayman; Ann Arbor, MI, U.S). Human recombinant FABP5 protein (#RP-75555) was purchased from Invitrogen (Waltham, MA, U.S.).

BMS309403 (#HY-101903: BMS) was purchased from MedChemExpress (Monmouth Junction, NJ, U.S.). RAC1 inhibitor (#13196: NSC23766) was purchased from Cayman chemical (Ann Arbor, MI, U.S.)

Animal studies

All experimental procedures using animals were approved by the Yamaguchi University Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee (IACUC, # 21 − 004). All procedures related to animal handling, care, and treatment were performed according to guidelines approved by the IACUC. C57BL/6J mice were bred and maintained in the animal facility of the Yamaguchi University with authorized protocol from the IACUC. Mice were anesthetized with 2% isoflurane following IACUC guidelines. The mice were euthanized in their home cage by using compressed CO2 gas in cylinders following IACUC guidelines. In brief, representative criteria for euthanasia include: (1) tumor size exceeding 20 mm; (2) ulceration of the tumor; (3) impairment of movement or vital organ function caused by the tumor; and (4) observable signs of distress in the animal, such as labored breathing. The studies are reported in accordance with ARRIVE guidelines.

Mouse syngeneic subcutaneous tumor model

For survival analysis, KPC cells (2 × 106 cells) were injected into the flanks of C57BL/6J mice. Sixteen days later, the mice were categorized into two groups: control (n = 5) and BMS 4 mg/kg (n = 4). The control group received only PBS, while the BMS group received BMS through intraperitoneal (i.p.) administration on day 0.

To assess the antitumor effect of BMS, KPC cells (0.5 × 106 cells) were injected into the flanks of C57BL/6J mice. When the average estimated tumor volume reached 120 mm³, mice were divided into two groups: control (n = 10) and BMS 8 mg/kg (n = 10). The control group of mice received only PBS alone, and the BMS group received BMS by i.p. administration on days 0, 3, 7, and 10. Tumor growth was observed until day 14. Blood samples were collected on day 14, and the mice were euthanized following IACUC guidelines.Serum biochemical levels, including total protein, aspartate aminotransferase, lactate dehydrogenase, and creatinine, were analyzed using a Hitachi 7180 automatic analyzer (Hitachi High-Tech Corporation, Tokyo, Japan).

High IPFD mouse model

C57BL/6J mice were fed chow (control: n = 3) or the GAN diet (D09100310, EP Trading, Tokyo, Japan) rich in saturated fat (40%), fructose (22%), and cholesterol (2%) for 10 weeks (n = 3) or 30 weeks (n = 8). Mice were euthanized following IACUC guidelines after the experiments. Images of hematoxylin and eosin (HE)-stained mouse pancreatic samples were obtained using a Keyence BZ-X810 digital microscope (Keyence, Higashiyodokawa, Osaka, Japan). The percentage of IPFD was analyzed using BZ-H4M software (Keyence).

RNA sequencing

The pancreatic tissues of control (n = 3) and GAN (n = 4) mice were collected, and total RNA was extracted using the RNeasy Plus Universal kit (#73404, QIAGEN, Hulsterweg 82, Netherlands). After the RNA extraction, the quality of the RNA (RNA integrity number, RIN) was determined using Tapestation 4200 High Sensitivity RNA (#5067–5579, Agilent Technologies, Santa Clara, CA, U.S.). For library constructions, 7 nanograms of high-quality total RNA samples were processed using the NEBNext Ultra II Directional RNA Library Prep Kit for Illumina (#E7760, NEB. Ipswich, MA, U.S.) with a c1000 Touch thermal cycler (BioRad laboratories. Hercules, CA, U.S.) and NEBNext Multiplex Oligos for Illumina (#E6444, NEB), following the manufacturer’s instructions. The libraries were sequenced using the NovaSeq 6000 S1 Reagent Kit V1.5 (Illumina, San Diego, CA, USA). Data analysis was conducted using CLC Genomics Workbench 23.0.2(Qiagen).

Mouse orthotopic syngeneic tumor model

Orthotopic tumors were created by injecting 3 × 105 KPC cells in 20 µL PBS into the tail of the pancreas of C57BL/6J mice of control (n = 7) and GAN 30 weeks (n = 7). The mice were euthanized 12 days after the injection. The orthotopic tumor volume was calculated using the following formula: estimated tumor volume = (shortest diameter2 × longest diameter)/2. The incidence of liver metastasis and intraperitoneal dissemination for each group was assessed.

Cell proliferation

Cell proliferation was evaluated using the 3-(4,5-dimethylthiazol-2-yl)-5-(3-carboxymethoxyphenyl)-2-(4-sulfophenyl)-2 H-tetrazolium inner salt (MTS) assay. Cell suspensions (5000 cells/100 µL) were seeded into wells in a 96-well plate and incubated at 37 °C for 24 h. FABP5 0, 20 ng/mL (1.0 nM), 40 ng/mL (2.0 nM), 100 ng/mL (5.1 nM), and BMS (1.0, 2.0, 3.9, 7.8, 15.6, 31.3, 62.5, 125, 250, 500, 1000 µM) were added to each well. In addition, to clarify the relationship between RAC1 and FABP5, RAC1 inhibition was evaluated. NSC23766 (200µM) and/or BMS (60µM) were added each well. Control group is treated with vehicle control. The cells were then incubated for 48 h. Thereafter, 10 µL of CellTiter-96®Aqueous One-Solution Cell Proliferation Assay MTS reagent (Promega) was added to each 100 µL of culture media, which were incubated for 2 h. The absorbance at 490 nm was measured using a multimode reader (infinite M200PRO, TECAN, Männedorf, Switzerland). The results were expressed as the percentage of viable cells compared with the vehicle control.

RNA isolation and quantitative RT-PCR

Total RNA was isolated using the RNeasy Mini Kit (Qiagen, 74106) and reverse-transcribed using PrimeScript RT Master Mix (RR036A, Takara, Kusatsu, Japan). Quantitative RT-PCR was conducted using a Roche LightCycler 480 II and PowerUp SYBR Green Master Mix (A25742; Thermo Fisher). Gene expression was calculated using LightCycler Software (Roche No.124; Grenzacherstrasse, Switzerland) and delta-delta Ct value was calculated. The primer sequences used are described below.

RAC1 F: TCAGTTACACAACCAATGCATTTCC

RAC1 R: CAGCTGTATCCCATAAGCCCAGA

Beta actin F: CATTGCTGACAGGATGCAGAAGG

Beta actin R: TGCTGGAAGGTGGACAGTGAGG

Wound healing assay

PDAC cells were seeded in 6-well plates at approximately 80% confluence. Wound was scratched with 200 µL tips, and PDAC cells were cultured with DMEM containing recombinant FABP5 (0, 100 ng/mL) and BMS (0, 60 µM). In addition, to clarify the relationship between RAC1 and FABP5, RAC1 inhibition was also evaluated. NSC23766 (200µM) and/or BMS (60µM) were added. The area of the wound was evaluated under a microscope every 4 h and calculated using ImageJ software (National Institute of Health, MD, USA). The motility of cells was measured using the formula percentage of wound area, which was calculated as the ratio of the wound area at 8–24 h to the area at 0 h in each group.

Statistical analysis

Data are shown as mean ± standard deviation (SD). Statistical analysis was conducted using Student’s t-test or the Cochran–Armitage test for trend or the log-rank test. For all tests, differences were considered significant at P < 0.05. All statistical analyses were conducted using the JMP Pro software (version 18.0; SAS Institute, Cary, NC, USA).
