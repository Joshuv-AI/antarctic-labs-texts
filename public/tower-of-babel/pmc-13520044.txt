Protective Effect of the NHE1 Inhibitor Buthutin A on Oxidative Stress-Induced Apoptosis in HUVECs
Authors: Chenyue Shao, Xinru Zhao, Zhiling Cheng, Yunsheng Xu, Yanan Li, Yuming Liu, Xiangbo Gou, Congxin Li
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Objective: Na+/H+-exchanger 1 (NHE1) inhibitors are considered promising drugs for cardiovascular diseases. Buthutin A (Buth A) is a novel NHE1 inhibitor that was reported by our team recently. In this research, we explored the protective effect of Buth A against angiotensin II (AngII)-induced injury in vitro and in vivo. Methods: H9c2 cells and human umbilical vein endothelial cells (HUVECs) were divided into eight groups to investigate whether NHE1, cytosolic calcium ([Ca2+]c), conventional protein kinase C (cPKC), and PKCβ are associated with the protective effect of Buth A in vitro. Meanwhile, myocardial hypertrophy was induced by AngII in adult male C57BL/6 mice to verify the protective effect of Buth A in vivo. Results: Buth A significantly increased cell viability in AngII-treated H9c2 and HUVEC cells, as well as the superoxide dismutase (SOD) activity of AngII-treated H9c2 cells. Moreover, Buth A reduced the lactate dehydrogenase (LDH) and reactive oxygen species (ROS) levels, number of TUNEL-positive cells, and Bax/Bcl-2 protein expression ratio in AngII-treated H9c2 and HUVEC cells. It also significantly decreased the malondialdehyde (MDA) levels, cell size, and mitochondrial membrane potential in H9c2 cells, all of which were attenuated by the NHE1 activator (LiCl). We further found that the calcium chelator (BAPTA-AM), Na+/Ca2+-exchanger (NCX) inhibitor (SN6), cPKC inhibitor (Go6976), and PKCβ inhibitor (LY317615) had no effect on the protective effect of Buth A in HUVECs, including the inhibition of oxidative stress and apoptosis. Further results indicated that Buth A alleviated oxidative stress and apoptosis caused by myocardial hypertrophy via NHE1 inhibition in AngII-induced mice. Conclusions: Buth A reduced oxidative stress-induced apoptosis and exerted protective effects against AngII-induced cardiac injury in vivo and in vitro via NHE1 inhibition, which might be associated with [Ca2+]c and PKCβ activities.

Introduction

Vascular endothelial dysfunction is one of the elements causing cardiovascular diseases, including myocardial hypertrophy. Reactive oxygen species (ROS) are vital factors causing vascular endothelial dysfunction, and the imbalance between ROS generation and elimination plays an important role in the treatment of endothelial dysfunction. , There are several factors involved in ROS generation, such as Na+/H+-exchanger (NHE). NHE isoforms are ubiquitously expressed in mammalian cells, with the NHE1 isoform being expressed mainly in cardiac tissue. NHE1 is an important transmembrane protein that regulates intracellular pH (pHi) and intracellular sodium ([Na+]i) homeostasis under physiological conditions by exchanging intracellular H+ ([H+]i) and extracellular Na+ at a ratio of 1:1. NHE1 activation triggers [H+]i extrusion, nicotinamide adenine dinucleotide phosphate oxidase (NOX) activation, as well as further ROS production. Ribosome damage caused by ROS generation is key to several diseases, including obesity, aging-related metabolic diseases, and hypertension. In particular, excessive ROS generation could also aggravate cardiac hypertrophy and heart failure development.

In addition, NHE1 is also present in several endothelial cells (ECs) and plays an essential role in endothelial function by regulating pHi. Moreover, NHE1 activity is upregulated by hypoxia-inducible factor-1α (HIF-1α), tumor necrosis factor-α (TNF-α), and cyclic stretch in human umbilical vein endothelial cells (HUVECs). ,, Moreover, the upregulated NHE1 activity is a key factor in the development of cardiovascular diseases, such as myocardial hypertrophy and heart failure. , Especially, cardiomyocyte hypertrophy and ischemia/reperfusion injury models are accompanied by elevated NHE1 activity. , It has been reported that NHE1 inhibition can regress myocardial hypertrophy both in vitro and in vivo. , However, the underlying mechanism remains unknown. It was demonstrated that several NHE1 inhibitors have been shown to prevent myocardial hypertrophy in models of myocardial infarction, spontaneously hypertensive rats, and so on. At present, although common NHE1 inhibitors, such as amiloride, eniporide, and cariporide, can improve cardiac contractility and heart failure conditions after myocardial ischemia, calcium overload cannot be improved. , Clinical research has indicated that cariporide application might increase the occurrence of thromboembolic cerebrovascular diseases during coronary artery bypass grafting. Therefore, it is vital to develop a new NHE1 inhibitor for myocardial hypertrophy. In this research, we selected H9c2 and HUVEC cells to establish a cell injury model. Hypertrophy of myocardial cells is a direct pathological feature of myocardial hypertrophy, and H9c2 cells are commonly used for studying myocardial cell injury and hypertrophy. However, the vascular endothelial dysfunction indirectly affects the process of myocardial hypertrophy through inflammatory responses, oxidative stress, and vascular permeability. HUVECs are classic models for studying endothelial dysfunction. NHE1 was highly expressed in H9c2 cells and HUVECs, and its dysfunction was associated with oxidative stress and apoptosis. , Therefore, we selected these two cell lines to investigate the protective effect of Buth A.

Angiotensin II (AngII) plays a critical role in endothelial damage progression, causing ROS generation and cell apoptosis. Meanwhile, AngII, as a hormone, can cause myocardial hypertrophy injury, and NHE1 protein expression is elevated in H9c2 cells. Previous studies have reported that empagliflozin can mitigate ROS levels by inhibiting NHE1 in ECs. It is speculated that the enhanced [Na+]i caused by NHE1 activation leads to elevated cytosolic Ca2+ ([Ca2+]c) levels through the Na+/Ca2+-exchanger (NCX). , Elevated [Ca2+]c levels activate conventional PKC (cPKC), especially PKCβ, which plays a vital role in ROS generation and cell apoptosis.

Buthutin A (Buth A), a novel NHE1 inhibitor isolated fromButhus martensii and successfully synthesized in our laboratory, exhibited a high NHE1 activity inhibition and protection against doxorubicin-induced H9c2 cell injury. However, whether Buth A can exert a protective effect against AngII-induced ECs and cardiomyocyte injury through NHE1 inhibition remains unknown. AngII-induced injury is a well-known model for studying cardiac hypertrophy. Herein, we systematically investigated the protective effect of Buth A on AngII-induced damage both in vitro and in vivo and further explored the underlying signaling pathway. We finally confirmed that Buth A alleviated AngII-induced ROS accumulation and cell apoptosis via NHE1 inhibition, which might be associated with [Ca2+]c and PKCβ activity. The present work provides new evidence for the pharmacological value of Buth A as a promising candidate for the treatment of cardiac hypertrophy and related cardiovascular diseases.

Materials and Methods

Reagents

Buthutin A was isolated and synthesized in our laboratory, as reported previously. Cariporide, LiCl, BAPTA-AM, and SN6 were obtained from MedChemExpress (New Jersey, USA). Go6976 and LY317615 were obtained from Sigma (St. Louis, MO) and Target Mol (Boston, MA), respectively. AngII was bought from Enzo Biochem (New York, USA).

Cell Culture and Treatment

H9c2 cells (Procell, China) were cultured in DMEM (Gibco, USA) with 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS, Gibco, USA) and 1% penicillin-streptomycin (Solarbio, China). HUVECs (Procell, China) were cultured in complete medium (Procell, China). H9c2 cells and HUVECs were divided into the following groups for subsequent research.

Control group: The cells were treated with DMEM.

AngII group: The cells were treated with AngII (1 μM) for 24 h.

AngII + Buth A group: The cells were treated with AngII (1 μM) and Buth A (1 μM) for 24 h.

AngII + Buth A + LiCl group: The cells were treated with AngII (1 μM), Buth A (1 μM), and LiCl (5 mM) for 24 h.

AngII + Buth A + BAPTA-AM group: The cells were treated with AngII (1 μM) and Buth A (1 μM) for 24 h, as well as treated with BAPTA (5 μM) for 3 h.

AngII + Buth A + SN6 group: The cells were treated with AngII (1 μM) and Buth A (1 μM) for 24 h, as well as treated with SN6 (10 μM) for 3 h.

AngII + Buth A + Go6976 group: The cells were treated with AngII (1 μM), Buth A (1 μM), and Go6976 (1 μM) for 24 h.

AngII + Buth A + LY317615 group: The cells were treated with AngII (1 μM), Buth A (1 μM), and LY317615 (0.3 μM) for 24 h.

Cell Viability Assay

H9c2 cells and HUVECs (3 × 103 cells/well) in a 96-well plate were treated with Buth A (0, 0.1, 0.5, 1, 5, 10, and 20 μM) for 24 h to determine the effect of Buth A on cell viability. The cells were treated with 0.1, 1, 10, and 100 μM AngII for 24 h to determine the optimal concentration of AngII to establish an injury model. The cells were treated with 1 μM AngII and various concentrations of Buth A (0.1, 1, and 10 μM) for 24 h to evaluate the effect of Buth A on cell viability in the presence of AngII. After treatment, 0.5 mg/mL MTT solution (Solarbio, China) was added and incubated in the dark for 3 h. The absorbance was measured using an Epoch microplate reader (BioTek, USA), and cell viability was calculated as previously reported.

NHE1 Activity Measurement

H9c2 cells and HUVECs were divided into the control group, NH4Cl group, and different concentrations of Buth A groups to detect the inhibitory effect of Buth A on the NHE1 activity. The cells in the control group and NH4Cl group were treated with normal culture medium, while those in the other groups were treated with 0.1, 0.5, 1, 5, 10, and 20 μM Buth A for 30 min before measurement. In addition, the cells were divided into the NH4Cl group, AngII group, and various concentrations of Buth A groups, which were treated with the normal culture medium, 1 μM AngII, and concentrations of Buth A (0.1, 1, and 10 μM) in the presence of 1 μM AngII for 30 min before the measurement. The NHE1 activity was first measured using a fluorescence microplate reader (SpectraMax ID3, Molecular Devices, USA), with a fluorescence probe, BCECF-AM (Beyotime, China). The cells were then incubated with Krebs solution containing 1 μM BCECF-AM at 37 °C for 30 min, and the fluorescence intensity was measured at excitation wavelengths of 488 and 440 nm. The fluorescence intensity ratios of 488 and 440 nm were identified as FIR. Calibration was performed using a high-potassium solution containing Nigericin, and the obtained FIRs were plotted against the corresponding pH values. Cell pH recovery was assessed as follows. After the cells were acidified with 20 mM NH4Cl, FIR was recorded immediately. Subsequently, sodium-containing Krebs solution was added to replace the sodium-free Krebs solution to activate NHE1. The pH recovery rate after Na+ addition (ΔpHi/min) was calculated as an indicator of NHE1 activity. The effect of Buth A on NHE1 activity was further verified using a fluorescence microscope (Nikon, Japan). H9c2 cells and HUVECs were divided into the control group, AngII group (1 μM), and Buth A (0.1, 1, and 10 μM) added AngII (1 μM) groups. Cells were treated with the corresponding drugs for 24 h. The cells were washed and incubated with BCECF-AM solution at 37 °C for 30 min. Finally, the images were observed using a fluorescence microscope (Nikon A1HD25, Nikon, Japan, ex:em = 488 nm/535 nm), and the fluorescence intensity was analyzed using ImageJ.

Molecular Docking −

Docking between Buth A and NHE1 was performed as follows. The crystal structure of NHE1 (PDB: 7dsx) was obtained from the UniProt database (https://www.uniprot.org/). The species “Human” was selected, and the protein NHE1 with the smallest X-ray value was accurately chosen. The PDB structure of NHE1 was downloaded from the Protein Data Bank (https://www.rcsb.org). Crystallographic water molecules and redundant heteroatoms were removed in advance for protein structure pretreatment. The structure of Buth A was drawn using ChemDraw 20.0 and saved as an SDF file. The above file was converted to the mol2 format using Open Babel 3.1.1 to obtain the ligand. Buth A was geometrically optimized, and gasteiger partial charges were assigned to complete ligand parametrization. Molecular docking between NHE1 and Buth A was performed using AutoDockTools 1.5.7 software. The grid box was centered on the predicted ligand-binding pocket, with fixed spatial dimensions and 1.0 Å grid spacing set for docking calculations. The two-dimensional planar structures of NHE1 and Buth A were obtained using LigPlot 2.2.8. Visual analysis of the NHE1–Buth A complex with a strong binding ability was conducted with PyMOL 3.1 software.

Protein docking between PKC isoforms and NHE1 was performed as follows. The crystal structures of the NHE1 and PKC isoforms were obtained from the Protein Data Bank. The PDB formats of PKC isoforms and NHE1 were imported into the HDOCK website (http://hdock.phys.hust.edu.cn/) for protein docking. The docking result with the highest score was selected and visually analyzed by using PyMOL 3.1 software.

Molecular Dynamics Simulation

Molecular dynamics simulations were performed using GROMACS 2020.6. The AMBER99SB force field and GAFF force field were employed for NHE1 and Buth A, respectively, and the SPC water model was also applied during the simulation. Energy minimization was achieved by utilizing a 50,000-step steepest descent algorithm in the system. The NHE1–Buth A complex was embedded in a POPC lipid bilayer to mimic the native transmembrane environment and solvated with a physiological saline solution. The whole system was maintained at a temperature of 300 K and a pressure of 1 bar for 100 ns. Three independent MD replicates were performed using different initial velocities to verify the reproducibility of the simulation trajectories. Trajectory analysis of the NHE1–Buth A complex was performed by calculating the root-mean-square deviation (RMSD), root-mean-square fluctuation (RMSF), radius of gyration (R g), hydrogen bond (H-bond) number, and solvent-accessible surface area (SASA) distribution. The simulation results were visualized using QtGrace v0.2.7 and PyMOL 3.1.

Cellular Thermal Shift Assay (CETSA)

After culturing for 24 h, HUVECs were lysed to extract the proteins. After being placed at −80 °C for 30 min, the mixture was centrifuged at 12,000 rpm for 10 min at 4 °C. The supernatant was obtained, and the concentration was determined using a BCA protein assay kit (GlbBio, America). The supernatant was divided into two groups, which were treated with Buth A (5 μM) and DMSO, respectively. After incubating at 37 °C for 30 min, the sediment was subjected to heat at a temperature gradient (25, 37, 42, 47, 52, 57, 62, and 67 °C) for 5 min at each temperature. After centrifugation, the supernatant was collected for western blotting.

LDH Activity and Oxidative Stress Measurement

The culture medium supernatant of the cells was collected, and lactate dehydrogenase (LDH) activity was detected using an LDH assay kit (Nanjing Jiancheng, China) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Meanwhile, cells were collected to measure superoxide dismutase (SOD) activity and malondialdehyde (MDA) content using kits (Nanjing Jiancheng, China). ROS fluorescence intensity was determined using the DCFH-DA fluorescence probe (Solarbio, China, ex:em = 495 nm/518 nm). Finally, images were obtained using a fluorescence microscope (Nikon A1HD25, Nikon, Japan), and the mean fluorescence intensity (MFI) of DCFH-DA was calculated.

[Ca2+]c, Actin-Tracker Green, Mito-Tracker Red CMXRos, and TUNEL Staining

The cells were stained with a Fluo-4 AM fluorescent probe (Beyotime, China, ex:em = 488 nm/525 nm), Actin-Tracker Green-488 (Beyotime, China, ex:em = 488 nm/525 nm), TUNEL Apoptosis Assay working solution (Abbkine, China, ex:em = 555 nm/565 nm), and Mito-Tracker Red CMXRos working solution (Solarbio, China, ex:em = 579 nm/599 nm) to measure the [Ca2+]c, cell size, TUNELl-positive cells, as well as mitochondria and mitochondrial membrane potential. Fluorescence intensity was measured using a Nikon A1HD25 (Nikon, Japan) and analyzed using ImageJ.

[Ca2+]c and p-PKC Activity Measurement

After treatment with the corresponding drugs, HUVECs were collected and lysed to extract proteins. The calcium concentration was measured using a Calcium Assay Kit (Beyotime, China). The culture medium was collected, and the p-PKC activity was determined using a Human phosphorylated-PKC ELISA KIT (Mlbio, China) according to the manufacturer’s instructions.

All animal experiments were approved by the Animal Care and Use Committee of the Institute of Radiation Medicine (Approval number. IRM/2-IACUC-2511-010) and conducted in accordance with the National Institutes of Health Guide for the Care and Use of Laboratory Animals. Mice with severe infections, excessive weight loss, and accidental death were excluded. Then, the mice received osmotic pumps containing AngII (2000 ng/kg/min) with low-dose Buth A administration for 3 weeks.(4)AngII + high-dose Buth A group (AngII + H-Buth A) (n = 8): One week before the implantation of osmotic pumps, the mice received Buth A (10 mg/kg/d) by oral gavage once a day. Then, the mice received osmotic pumps containing AngII (2000 ng/kg/min) with low-dose Buth A and LiCl administration for 3 weeks. The mice were anesthetized by intraperitoneal injection of sodium pentobarbital to ameliorate suffering during surgery. After the last administration, the mice were subjected to echocardiography and electrocardiogram evaluations to estimate cardiac function. After cardiac function estimation, the mice were euthanized immediately at their predetermined endpoint by cervical dislocation, and blood and cardiac tissue were obtained. The time elapsed before euthanasia was minimized as much as possible to ameliorate suffering and avoid unnecessary stress. During the animal experiments, the diet, activity, body weight, as well as general health and behavior of all mice were monitored every day. In addition, all animal welfare considerations were taken into account in order to minimize the distress of the mice. All animal experiments were performed by trained personnel to ensure the accuracy of experiments, as well as to alleviate the distress of the mice. A double-blinded scheme was used for the experiments.

Echocardiography and Electrocardiogram Evaluation ,

After being anesthetized with isoflurane, all mice underwent echocardiography and an electrocardiogram. Echocardiography was performed using a Vevo 2100 system (FUJIFILM, Japan). Echocardiography data, including left ventricular internal diameter in end-systole (LVIDs) and end-diastole (LVIDd), left ventricular posterior wall in end-systole (LVPWs) and end-diastole (LVPWd), ejection fraction (EF), and fractional shortening (FS), were recorded. The following electrocardiogram data were recorded to estimate cardiac function: heart rate (HR), RR interval, QRS interval, and QT interval. The corrected QT interval (QTc) was calculated as previously reported (QTc‐Binterval=QT interval/RR interval) .

Biochemical Analysis

Blood was collected from the eyes of mice for further biochemical analysis. Cardiac troponin T (cTnT, mlbio, China), creatine kinase-MB (CK-MB, mlbio, China), LDH (Mlbio, China), atrial natriuretic peptide (ANP, mlbio, China), and N-terminal pro-B-type natriuretic peptide (NT-proBNP, mlbio, China) levels in serum were measured using ELISA kits according to the manufacturer’s instructions. SOD activity and MDA content in the serum were determined using kits (Nanjing Jiancheng, China) according to the manufacturer’s instructions, respectively. Meanwhile, cardiac tissues were collected and homogenized in PBS. ANP (mlbio, China) and NT-proBNP (mlbio, China) levels in the cardiac tissue were also detected using ELISA kits according to the manufacturer’s instructions.

Hematoxylin and Eosin (HE) and Masson Staining

The left ventricular tissue was fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde for 24 h. Then, the tissue was dehydrated using gradient concentrations of ethanol. After being immersed in xylene for 1 h and embedded in paraffin, the tissue was cut into 4 μm thickness for HE and Masson staining. Histological examination was performed using a light microscope.

Western Blotting

After being separated by SDS-PAGE electrophoresis, the protein in the PVDF membrane (Millipore, USA) was incubated with the corresponding primary antibodies overnight at 4 °C. The primary antibodies used were NHE1 (1:5000, 67363-1-lg, Proteintech, China), Bcl-2 (1:3000, 12789-1-AP, Proteintech, China), Bax (1:3000, 50599-2-Ig, Proteintech, China), GAPDH (1:4000, 10494-1-AP, Proteintech, China), and β-actin (1:2000, 20536-1-AP, Proteintech, China). The protein was subsequently incubated with horseradish peroxidase (HRP)-labeled goat antirabbit/mouse IgG (H + L) secondary antibodies (Beyotime, China). GAPDH or β-actin expression was used as the internal reference protein for normalization. Finally, the protein expression was detected using the Ultra High Sensitivity ECL kits (Glpbio, USA), and the results were analyzed using ImageJ.

Statistical Analysis

Data analysis was conducted using OriginPro 9.1 and expressed as mean ± SEM. Group comparisons were performed using Student’s t-test and ANOVA with Dunnett’s post hoc tests. Differences were considered statistically significant at p < 0.05.

Results

Buth A Protected the Injury Caused by AngII on H9c2 Cells and HUVECs

The Buth A structure is presented in Figure A. The viability of H9c2 cells and HUVECs was examined using the MTT method. The results are presented in Figure B, which illustrates that the viability of H9c2 cells and HUVECs was not affected by Buth A at concentrations ranging from 0 to 20 μM. We next investigated the protective effect of Buth A on cell injury caused by AngII. At first, the viability of H9c2 cells and HUVECs at different concentrations of AngII was measured. The results illustrate that AngII inhibited the cell viability at concentrations ranging from 0.1 to 100 μM (Figure S1, p < 0.01). Therefore, we chose 1 μM AngII to establish the injury models of H9c2 cells and HUVECs, which is consistent with previous research. , The results in Figure C,D show that Buth A significantly increased the viability of H9c2 cells and HUVECs in the AngII-treated group (p < 0.05). We used an NHE1 activator (LiCl) to investigate whether Buth A exerted a protective effect via NHE1 inhibition. The inhibitory effect of Buth A on LDH levels in H9c2 cells and HUVECs was attenuated by LiCl (Figure E, p < 0.05). In conclusion, we confirmed that the protective effect of Buth A on H9c2 cells and HUVEC injury caused by AngII may be mediated via NHE1 inhibition. Therefore, we further investigated the underlying mechanisms of this protective effect.

Molecular Docking and Molecular Dynamics Stimulation Analyses

The molecular docking results showed that Buth A had a high affinity for NHE1, with the binding sites located at the amino acid residues TYR342, GLU346, and HIS275 (Figures A and S2). Moreover, the average binding energy of molecular docking was −7.34 ± 0.11 kcal/mol. The CETSA experimental results indicated that in the presence of Buth A (5 μM), the stability of the NHE1 protein was enhanced at different time points (Figure B, p < 0.05).

Molecular dynamics simulations were performed to evaluate the stability of the NHE1–Buth A complex. Figure C displays the superposition of the simulated conformations. Figure D shows that the RMSD curve of the NHE1–Buth A complex fluctuated slightly and remained stable. The RMSF curve illustrates the residue-level flexibility of NHE1 (Figure E). The R g curve remained stable at around 3.3 nm, and Buth A did not significantly affect the conformational changes in NHE1 (Figure F). The NHE1–Buth A complex could also form hydrogen bonds (Figure G). The SASA of the NHE1–Buth A complex indicated that Buth A could reduce the surface area of NHE1 exposed to the solvent (Figure H). Molecular docking and molecular dynamics simulations preliminarily predicted the binding conformation between Buth A and NHE1. These analyses provided structural evidence supporting the potential inhibitory interaction, while cellular NHE1 activity assays were performed to validate the functional effects experimentally.

Buth A Inhibited NHE1 Activity and Protein Expression

In the following experiments, the effects of Buth A on NHE1 activity and protein expression in vitro were further investigated. The results showed that 0.1 to 20 μM Buth A significantly decreased NHE1 activity in H9c2 cells and HUVECs (Figure S3, p < 0.05). Therefore, we investigated whether Buth A affected NHE1 activity in the presence of AngII. We found that 1 μM AngII could significantly activate NHE1, which was decreased by Buth A in H9c2 cells and HUVECs (Figure A–D, p < 0.05). NHE1 activity was quantified by monitoring the recovery rate of intracellular pH using the BCECF-AM fluorescent probe after acute acid loading. Compared to the AngII-treated group, Buth A significantly slowed the pH restoration process and demonstrated an inhibitory effect on the NHE1 exchange function. Furthermore, Buth A showed a higher NHE1 activity inhibition compared with cariporide, a classical NHE1 inhibitor, at the same concentration (Figure S4, p < 0.05). It has been reported that NHE1 activity could affect NHE1 protein expression; therefore, we further investigated the effect of Buth A on the NHE1 protein. The results in Figure E–G show that AngII increased NHE1 protein expression, which was suppressed by Buth A treatment (p < 0.05).

Buth A Suppressed Oxidative Stress and Apoptosis Caused by AngII via NHE1 Inhibition

NHE1 activation is involved in several cardiac diseases, such as cardiac hypertrophy, cardiac infarction, and cardiac failure, as previously reported. , In addition, NHE1 activation is associated with oxidative stress and apoptosis, which are involved in several cardiac diseases. It was reported that AngII could activate NHE1 activity, as well as induce oxidative stress and apoptosis, which are the causes of myocardial hypertrophy. , Therefore, we investigated whether Buth A could alleviate oxidative stress and apoptosis caused by AngII in vitro through NHE1 inhibition. The results in Figure A-D indicate that 1 μM Buth A significantly decreased the ROS levels caused by AngII in H9c2 cells and HUVECs, which were partially reversed by LiCl (p < 0.001). The ROS are associated with cell apoptosis; therefore, we further estimated the apoptosis levels of H9c2 cells and HUVECs. The TUNEL staining results in Figure E–G show that Buth A could suppress the increase in TUNEL-positive H9c2 cells and HUVECs caused by AngII (p < 0.001). Moreover, Buth A could also decrease the Bax/Bcl-2 ratio in AngII-treated H9c2 cells and HUVECs (Figure H–J, p < 0.05). We further explored whether Buth A could alleviate other myocardial cell injuries caused by AngII. The results showed that Buth A could reduce the cell size and MDA content in AngII-treated H9c2 cells, as well as elevate SOD activity (Figures S5 and S6, p < 0.05). In addition, Buth A also alleviated the mitochondrial damage caused by AngII (Figure S7, p < 0.001).

[Ca2+]c May Participate in the Inhibition of Buth A on Apoptosis Induced by AngII in HUVECs

It has been reported that [Ca2+]c levels are affected by NHE1 activity through NCX regulation. However, HUVECs have always been used in research investigating NHE1 activity and related mechanisms. The results in Figure A,C showed that Buth A could decrease the elevated [Ca2+]c levels caused by AngII, which was relieved by LiCl (p < 0.001). The above results illustrated that the effect of Buth A on inhibiting [Ca2+]c levels may be via NHE1 inhibition. It has been reported that the altered [Na+]i caused by NHE1 enhanced [Ca2+]c levels through NCX, which finally activated cPKC activity. In the following experiments, we used SN6, an NCX inhibitor, to determine whether the decreased effect of Buth A on [Ca2+]c levels was associated with NCX. The results shown in Figure B,D revealed that SN6 had no effect on [Ca2+]c levels in the Buth A-treated group. Therefore, these results indicated that Buth A inhibited NHE1 activity and restrained the [Ca2+]c levels.

It has been reported that elevated [Ca2+]c levels can activate cPKC activity, which is associated with apoptosis induced by AngII. PKCβ Activity May Participate in the Inhibition of Buth A on Apoptosis Induced by AngII in HUVECs

We explored the cPKC isoforms involved in the above mechanism. PKCα, PKCβ, and PKCγ are the main cPKC isoforms expressed in cardiac tissue. The docking scores for PKCα, PKCβ, and PKCγ with NHE1 were −280.54, −335.69, and −322.55, respectively (Figure A). The results indicated that PKCβ had the highest affinity for NHE1 among cPKC isoforms. We also analyzed the docking scores for PKCε and PKCη with NHE1, which also presented a lower affinity for NHE1 than PKCβ (Figure S8). The results in Figure S9 showed that AngII significantly increased the levels of both phosphorylated PKCα and PKCβ proteins, whereas Buth A markedly reduced the level of phosphorylated PKCβ protein, but had no effect on phosphorylated PKCα (p < 0.001). Therefore, we speculated that the inhibition of Buth A on NHE1 may be associated with the PKCβ isoform. To further verify the molecular docking results, we used the PKCβ inhibitor, LY317615, to investigate whether PKCβ was involved in the effect of Buth A. The results in Figure B,C illustrated that LY317615 did not further reduce ROS levels following Buth A treatment (p < 0.001). The results in Figure D,E showed that LY317615 did not further decrease the TUNEL-positive HUVEC levels in the Buth A group (p < 0.001). Moreover, LY317615 did not further decrease the Bax/Bcl-2 ratio in the Buth A group (Figure F,G, p < 0.001). The above results illustrated that the alleviating effect of Buth A on AngII-induced ROS elevation and apoptosis may be associated with PKCβ. In the above results, the molecular docking score only reflected the binding affinity and was not equated with functional inhibition. Therefore, we considered PKCβ as a potential candidate isoform through which Buth A alleviates AngII-induced ROS elevation and apoptosis. In future experiments, gene knockdown or overexpression was applied to evaluate the contribution of PKCβ to the effects of Buth A.

We further investigated the effect of Buth A on myocardial injury caused by AngII in vivo. The experimental procedure is illustrated in Figure A. As shown in Figure B, AngII treatment increased the heart weight/body weight ratio, which was significantly decreased by Buth A treatment (p < 0.05). Furthermore, the levels of myocardial injury biomarkers in myocardial tissue, such as ANP and NT-proBNP, were both significantly reduced by Buth A (Figure C, p < 0.01). The echocardiography results in Figure D,E indicated that Buth A could decrease LVPWs and LVPWd levels, as well as increase LVIDd, LVIDs, EF, and FS levels in the AngII group (p < 0.01). However, all the regulatory effects of Buth A on echocardiographic parameters were attenuated by LiCl (Figure D,E, p < 0.01). HE staining images indicated that the fragmentation of the cardiac tissue caused by AngII was alleviated by Buth A (Figure H). Meanwhile, Masson staining showed that cardiac fibrosis in mice treated with Buth A was obviously reduced compared with that in AngII-treated mice (Figure I). Therefore, the above results indicated that Buth A could attenuate cardiac function and pathology impairment caused by AngII via NHE1 inhibition.

Discussion

In this research, we investigated the protective effects of Buth A against AngII-induced injury in vivo and in vitro, as well as the underlying mechanisms. Buth A is a novel NHE1 inhibitor synthesized in our laboratory recently, which exhibits an excellent inhibitory effect on NHE1 activity. To date, we have drawn the following conclusions. (1) Buth A exhibited a strong inhibitory effect on NHE1 activity in both H9c2 cells and HUVECs under physiological and pathological conditions. (2) Buth A decreased the upregulated NHE1 protein expression caused by AngII in H9c2 cells and HUVECs. (3) Buth A protected HUVECs against AngII-induced oxidative stress and apoptosis by NHE1 inhibition, and it also affected [Ca2+]c and PKCβ activity under AngII treatment. (4) Buth A had a protective effect against oxidative stress-induced apoptosis on myocardial injury mice caused by AngII via NHE1 inhibition.

AngII is a well-known hormone that regulates blood pressure and plays a vital role in cardiac hypertrophy and cardiac failure. It has been demonstrated previously that AngII upregulates NHE1 activity via the AngII type 1 receptor (AT1). Further research has reported that AngII could increase ROS generation by inducing NHE1 hyperactivity. Moreover, studies have reported that upregulated NHE1 activity and [Na+]i levels caused by inflammation are closely associated with oxidative stress in endothelial cells. For instance, a short-term addition of TNF-α also activates NHE1 in human hepatocytes. Therefore, NHE1 activity is closely related to oxidative stress and inflammation. Although NHE1 inhibitors can improve cardiac function, their clinical application still faces challenges. Clinical research has indicated that cariporide application might increase the occurrence of thromboembolic cerebrovascular diseases during coronary artery bypass grafting. Therefore, developing novel NHE1 inhibitors for conditions such as myocardial hypertrophy is of great significance. Our research suggests that Buth A is a novel NHE1 inhibitor that decreases ROS generation in H9c2 cells and HUVECs via NHE1 inhibition. It is well-known that apoptosis and oxidative stress are two important elements in cardiac disease development, especially cardiac hypertrophy. Therefore, we hypothesized that Buth A exerts an antihypertrophy effect by inhibiting ROS generation and apoptosis. In the following results, we confirmed that Buth A significantly reduced apoptosis caused by AngII by decreasing TUNEL-positive cells, regulating apoptosis-related markers such as Bax and Bcl-2 in vitro and in vivo. Therefore, Buth A exerted an inhibitory effect on apoptosis and oxidative stress via NHE1 inhibition, but the related mechanisms remain unknown. Endothelial dysfunction is a hallmark of diabetic cardiomyopathy and heart failure, and plays a key role in cardiac hypertrophy. Therefore, we assumed that the alleviation of endothelial dysfunction by Buth A had a certain influence on cardiac hypertrophy treatment.

It has been reported that several factors increase [Na+]i via NHE1 activation and enhance [Ca2+]c levels via NCX. As a result, the increased [Ca2+]c levels consequently stimulated cPKC and triggered ROS generation. , Our previous studies indicated that AngII activated cPKC and PKCε, which presented as an activated form and translocated from the cytosol to the nucleus or cell membranes. Therefore, we hypothesized that the effect of Buth A on PKC activity might be associated with NHE1 and [Ca2+]c levels. In the following experiments, our results verified that Buth A had an inhibitory effect on [Ca2+]c levels and PKC activity in AngII-treated HUVECs. The results indicated that [Ca2+]c and cPKC were affected by the inhibitory effect of Buth A on apoptosis and ROS generation. Because previous reports have shown that elevated PKCβ activity plays a dominant role in NOX activation and ROS production in human ECs, we performed molecular docking on NHE1 and the PKC isoforms, including PKCα, PKCβ, PKCγ, PKCε, and PKCη. The results showed that NHE1 had a higher binding affinity for PKCβ compared to other PKC isoforms. We further applied the PKCβ inhibitor, LY317615, to verify whether PKCβ was involved in the protective effect of Buth A. These results suggested that the inhibitory effect of Buth A on AngII-induced ROS elevation and apoptosis may be associated with PKCβ. This study has some limitations. As an NHE1 activator, the application of LiCl limits the interpretation of results in vivo. We must emphasize that LiCl was only applied for preliminary phenotypic screening rather than precise pathway verification. We could not attribute the cardioprotective effect of Buth A solely to NHE1 activation due to the off-target effects of LiCl; therefore, we plan to use specific NHE1 activators in future investigations to explore the cardioprotective effect of Buth A. Furthermore, our research did not clarify the specific mechanism by which Buth A affected [Ca2+]c fully, which might be the result of several regulatory mechanisms, such as Ca2+ release mediated by IP3 receptor, extracellular Ca2+ influx mediated by the TRP channel, as well as uptake and buffering capacity of Ca2+ by mitochondria. At present, we are unable to distinguish between the mechanisms involved. Therefore, the association of [Ca2+]c with the downstream mechanism of Buth A still requires further exploration. In future research, we will use more specific pharmacological tools to clarify the specific source of Ca2+ changes, as well as the relationship with Buth A.

Conclusions

We concluded that Buth A exerted a protective effect on HUVECs against oxidative stress-induced apoptosis caused by AngII via NHE1, which might be associated with [Ca2+]c and PKCβ activity. Buth A also exerted a protective effect on hypertrophic H9c2 cells and mice with cardiac hypertrophy against oxidative stress-induced apoptosis caused by AngII via NHE1 inhibition.
