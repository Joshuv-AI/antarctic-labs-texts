This article reviews the epidemiology, pathophysiology, diagnosis and risk of perioperative complications of children with congenital heart disease (CHD) undergoing non-cardiac procedures. We provide guidance on the perioperative management of both routine and complex paediatric cases with a focus on information important to general anaesthetists. We highlight key aspects required for the safe care of these patients while emphasising communication and collaboration with our colleagues in cardiac anaesthesia and cardiology.

Epidemiology

Congenital heart disease includes a spectrum of structural cardiac abnormalities that arise before birth because of abnormal fetal cardiac development; it does not include hereditary disorders with cardiac manifestations such as Marfan's or hypertrophic cardiomyopathy.1 Congenital heart disease affects ∼8% of the population worldwide with the most common lesions being atrial and ventricular septal defects (ASD and VSD, respectively).2 Risk factors for CHD are multifactorial, including genetic disorders, maternal disease and medication/chemical exposure in utero. With improvements in medical and surgical care, >90% of patients with CHD will survive into adulthood.3 In addition to cardiac procedures, children with CHD may need anaesthesia for non-cardiac diagnostic or therapeutic procedures (most commonly gastrointestinal or otolaryngologic).4 With increased rates of turnover of paediatric cardiac anaesthetists and slow growth in paediatric fellowship-trained paediatric anaesthetists, many patients with CHD are cared for by general paediatric anaesthetists and adult cardiac anaesthetists.5,6

Pathophysiology

Morphology

The segmental approach to CHD was developed as a multistep schema in order to standardise the description of cardiac anatomy.7 The first step is to determine visceroatrial situs which refers to the position of atria in relation to abdominal anatomy. Figure 1 demonstrates situs solitus (normal), inversus (inverted), or ambiguus (unclear also known as heterotaxy). The second step defines the orientation and position of the ventricular loop based on the morphology of each ventricle. The third step examines the atrioventricular and ventriculoarterial connections. The final step determines any other abnormalities of coronaries, chambers, valves and inflow/outflow tracts. Segmental analysis may often be performed using transthoracic echocardiography.8 However, paediatric cardiac catheterisation reports also provide anatomical information in addition to measured data such as cardiac output (CO), resistance, pressure and saturations (see Fig. 2).

Series vs parallel circulation

Because of the variation in CHD, it is often more logical for the paediatric anaesthetist to focus on how blood flows to the systemic and pulmonary circulation. In conventional/series circulation, blood flows from the systemic circulation to pulmonary circulation in series resulting in a pulmonary blood flow (Q˙ p) to systemic blood flow (Q˙ s) ratio of 1:1 (see Table 1).9 In contrast, parallel circulation is characterised by blood flow being balanced between the pulmonary and systemic circulation. Blood flowing differentially to the pulmonary or systemic circulation may result in an imbalanced Q˙ p: Q˙ s ratio. For example, in a patient with a large VSD, a mix of deoxygenated and oxygenated blood is delivered to the lungs and body. If this patient has a Q˙ p: Q˙ s of 1:2, this will result in twice as much blood flowing to the systemic circulation compared with the pulmonary circulation. Too much Q˙ p would result in decreased arterial pressure and systemic perfusion, whereas too much Q˙ s would result in significant oxygen desaturation. Q˙ p and Q˙ s are inversely correlated with pulmonary vascular resistance (PVR) and systemic vascular resistance (SVR), respectively. Factors that decrease PVR include hypocapnia or pulmonary vasodilators, while factors that increase PVR include hypoxaemia, hypercapnia, high haematocrit, positive pressure ventilation, cold, metabolic acidosis and alpha-adrenergic stimulation. Similarly, factors that increase SVR include sympathetic stimulation, vasoconstrictor drugs and hypothermia, while factors that decrease SVR include vasodilators and hyperthermia.10

Single ventricle physiology

Single ventricle physiology is a broad term encompassing any condition that relies on one ventricle for both systemic and pulmonary circulation. Many conditions with single ventricle physiology are not amenable to a two-ventricle repair and subsequent series circulation. In these cases, the eventual goal is to connect the systemic veins to the pulmonary arteries to restore series circulation. However, neonates have PVR that is too high to maintain circulation in this manner. Therefore, children will require palliative procedures to adapt the single ventricle to a series circulation. The palliation of single ventricles typically occurs with staged correction.

In early-stage palliative procedures, the treatment approach is primarily dictated by Q˙ p, obstruction to Q˙ s and any other cardiac abnormalities.11 In neonates with decreased Q˙ p (cyanotic), prostaglandin E1 may be used to maintain ductal patency until a more definitive treatment (e.g. systemic-to-pulmonary shunt, usually modified Blalock-Taussig Shunt) may be performed. Patients with increased Q˙ p may benefit from banding of the pulmonary arteries while patients with obstructed Q˙ s may require a procedure to create a new aorta such as the Norwood procedure. Once an infant's pulmonary artery (PA) pressures are adequate, they may then undergo the next stage of palliative correction, the superior vena cava to pulmonary artery anastomosis (also known as bidirectional Glenn) in which the SVC is anastomosed to the PA. Slightly increased PVR (via hypercarbia or acidosis) improves oxygen transport at this stage as a result of the increase in cerebral blood flow and subsequent drainage through the SVC to the PA. The introduction of the bidirectional Glenn after the first stage and before final repair has significantly improved survival over the past few decades. However, interstage mortality still remains high.12,13 Around the age of 4 yrs, the final stage, the total cavopulmonary connection (or Fontan procedure) is completed in which the single ventricle pumps blood to the body, returning to the lungs through the pulmonary arteries and finally back to the heart. As there is no ‘right’ ventricle to pump blood through the pulmonary circulation, pulmonary blood flow (PBF) depends on transpulmonary gradient. Therefore, decreases in central venous pressure and increases in PVR (via hypercarbia or acidosis) may severely limit CO.

Patients with single ventricle physiology are at high risk for perioperative complications including cardiac arrest, especially in the immediate postoperative period.14 Early palliative patients require more careful perioperative management/vigilance because changes in pulmonary and systemic pressures will affect Q˙ p and hence oxygenation. This combined with low diastolic pressure may compromise coronary circulation as opposed to more advanced palliation with more stable blood flow patterns (Norwood, Bidirectional Glenn, Fontan procedures).4 Given their high risk, perioperative optimisation is paramount with general goals for haemodynamic management including: maintenance of oxygenation, spontaneous ventilation (whenever possible), sinus rhythm, preload, contractility, afterload and decreased PVR.

High-risk cardiac disease

Patients with a history of CHD are at risk for a wide variety of sequelae including pulmonary hypertension (PH), heart failure, arrhythmia, cyanosis and left ventricular outflow tract obstruction (LVOTO). In this section we briefly discuss these high-risk conditions and their potential impact in the perioperative period.

Pulmonary hypertension

Pulmonary hypertension is defined as a mean arterial pressure of >20 mmHg at rest. Pulmonary hypertension increases perioperative risk significantly.15 Pulmonary hypertension may be divided into precapillary, postcapillary, or combined categories. Precapillary PH (arterial) may be treated with vasodilator drugs whereas postcapillary PH (venous) does not respond. Postcapillary PH is often caused by left-sided cardiac lesions.

Patients with long standing PH and unrestricted atrial or ventricular left-to-right shunts are at risk of developing Eisenmenger syndrome with subsequent right ventricular failure and reversal of shunt flow to right-to-left shunt. It is associated with significant morbidity and mortality with one international cohort demonstrating a 5-yr mortality rate of 25.3%.16

Heart failure

Heart failure is an independent predictor of increased perioperative risk. It is often caused in patients with CHD by volume/pressure overload from specific cardiac lesions, monogenic factors that cause both CHD and heart failure (IE Alagille syndrome), or some combination of the two. Depending on the combination of factors leading to heart failure, careful management of inotropy, afterload, preload, heart rate and rhythm is warranted to optimise CO.17

Arrhythmias

These may arise from abnormal cardiac anatomy, surgical repair, deviations from euvolemia, or abnormal sympathetic activity. If the patient has a pacemaker in place, the anaesthetist should determine pacemaker settings, last interrogation, pacemaker dependence and the effect of magnet placement. If cautery is needed, short bursts of bipolar cautery are recommended, and the dispersive pad (also known as the patient return electrode) should be away from the pacemaker device. Of note, young children and some complex (i.e. single ventricle corrected with Fontan surgery) will often have pacemakers placed in the abdominal wall because of the use of epicardial electrodes instead of trans-venous which potentially complicates abdominal surgery.

Cyanosis

Cyanosis is common in parallel and single ventricle physiology. However, one must distinguish between whether the cyanosis is a result of poor systemic circulation, or ventilation-perfusion mismatch, right-to-left shunt, or both. From a systemic standpoint, cyanotic patients develop polycythaemia as compensation for chronic hypoxia which may result in secondary thrombocytopenia and coagulation disorders.18 Adequate hydration and early mobilisation are important to decrease the risk of thrombosis.

Left ventricular outflow tract obstruction

Left ventricular outflow tract obstruction commonly occurs with conditions such as Williams syndrome and hypertrophic obstructive cardiomyopathy. It may lead to left ventricular hypertrophy, which places children at risk of myocardial ischaemia during haemodynamic disturbances. These factors lead to children with Williams syndrome having a risk of sudden death 25–100 times that of the general population.19 Maintaining preload/afterload and minimising tachycardia are critical to avoid the downward spiral of decreased CO leading to decreased myocardial oxygen delivery.

Perioperative issues

Perioperative outcomes

Although overall mortality from CHD has declined in the past few decades by as much as 39.4% as a result of improvements in medical and surgical care, patients with CHD are at increased perioperative risks of complications.20,21 However, recognition of disease severity in children with complex CHD can be challenging. Decreased socialisation and reduced exercise capacity at presentation may help mask the severity of disease. As such, the American College of Surgeons' National Surgical Quality Improvement Program (NSQIP Paediatric) divides CHD patients among three categories of severity:

(i)Minor: cardiac condition with or without medication and maintenance (e.g. ASD, small to moderate VSD without symptoms). Repair of CHD with normal cardiovascular function and no medication.

Minor: cardiac condition with or without medication and maintenance (e.g. ASD, small to moderate VSD without symptoms). Repair of CHD with normal cardiovascular function and no medication.

(ii)Major: repair of CHD with residual haemodynamic abnormality with or without medications (e.g. tetralogy of Fallot with free pulmonary regurgitation, hypoplastic left heart syndrome including stage 1 palliation).

Major: repair of CHD with residual haemodynamic abnormality with or without medications (e.g. tetralogy of Fallot with free pulmonary regurgitation, hypoplastic left heart syndrome including stage 1 palliation).

(iii)Severe: uncorrected cyanotic congenital HD. Patients with documented PH, with ventricular dysfunction requiring medication or listed for heart transplantation.20

In an analysis of 131,164 children, Miller and colleagues found a complication rate of 7% in children without CHD including cardiac arrest, reintubation, infection, renal failure, neurologic, thromboembolic, reoperation, 30-day readmission, mortality and prolonged stay.22 Alarmingly, overall complication rates in patients with minor, major and severe CHD were 22%, 31% and 36%, respectively. Children under 1 yr old had a significantly elevated risk of complications compared with older children. After an analysis of 37,658 children with CHD undergoing non-cardiac surgery, Faraoni and colleagues determined eight factors associated with increased risk of 30-day mortality which are summarized in Table 2.23 These factors were each then assigned a score and the total composite score delineated the patient's risk category with <3 factors considered low risk, 4–6 medium risk, and score >7 high risk.23 High intrinsic surgical risk did not appear to significantly increase 30-day mortality.4

Airway complications

Management of CHD is often complicated by airway abnormalities which may be primary or secondary. Trisomy 21, 22q11.2 microdeletion, and CHARGE (coloboma, heart defects, atresia of choanae, retardation of growth, genital and ear anomalies) are some of the most common syndromes associated with both CHD and airway abnormalities. The most frequent airway abnormalities in patients with CHD are laryngomalacia and bronchomalacia, while the most frequent acquired airway abnormalities are vocal cord paralysis, potentially from damage to the recurrent laryngeal nerve during dissection around the aorta.24 Airway abnormalities may also be caused by direct tracheobronchial compression from abnormal vasculature which may require surgeries such as aortopexy to correct.25 From a practical standpoint, patients with CHD also have a higher likelihood of difficult intubation and worse Cormack Lehane views on laryngoscopy.26,27 In children with CHD, videolaryngoscopy compared with direct laryngoscopy may decrease the number of intubation attempts, although increase time to intubation.28

Systemic and extracardiac manifestations

Patients with CHD are at risk for significant congenital and acquired extracardiac manifestations of their disease process. While CHD may be part of a syndrome with non-cardiac manifestations, CHD may also cause systemic effects on other organ systems (i.e. protein-losing enteropathy or congestive hepatopathy). In 2018, Neidenbach and colleagues found that 95.2% of 821 patients with CHD have additional systemic manifestations with the most prevalent systems affected being metabolic disorders (44%) followed by gastrointestinal (30.8%), vascular (28.9%) and musculoskeletal (28.3%). Same-day surgery

Despite the complexity of CHD, many patients can still be safely managed on a day-case basis. In fact, the number of patients with CHD presenting for same-day surgery continues to increase with one study reporting an increase from 19,719 in 2015 to 25,890 in 2019.30 Patients with CHD have a low risk of mortality for day-case surgery (0.02%), similar to that of the general population. This is likely to result from selection bias, as the most common cardiac comorbidities in the outpatient setting are lower risk patients with ASD, VSD or patent ductus arteriosus (PDA). Even so, this study has critically identified patients with CHD who may successfully receive day-case procedures care. Anaesthesia-related factors associated with admission were inadequate pain control, postoperative nausea and vomiting (PONV), hypoxia, headache, or fever. The decision on whether to have day-case surgery should be tailored to the patient's condition and the capability of the site whether a hospital, satellite hospital, or freestanding ambulatory surgery centre.31

Non-operative procedures

Patients with congenital defects often need numerous procedures that occur outside the operating room. Extensive imaging is often necessary to fully understand the complex cardiac anatomy of many of these patients, usually using computed tomography or magnetic resonance imaging. These procedures often require sedation or general anaesthesia in order to obtain optimal imaging. These studies are performed in the radiology suite where usual monitoring and anaesthesia equipment may not be as readily accessible. Extra care should be maintained during these procedures, with adequate access to immediate resuscitation equipment, should it be required. I.V. access is often challenging in patients with CHD. Patients often require frequent and long-standing access, all increasing the likelihood of difficulty as time passes. Patients often need longer indwelling access catheters that are placed in interventional radiology. Out of operating room sites should have equipment, medications, oxygen and scavenging capabilities as close to operating room conditions as possible. Suction and capnometry are also mandatory.32

Cardiac catheterisation

The cardiac catheterisation laboratory is integral to management of various cardiac lesions and procedures performed in this area comprise most non-operative procedures for patients with CHD. Procedures performed are ever evolving including younger patients with more severe diseases. It was shown that most complications occurred in patients <1 yr of age.33 Another study demonstrated that a weight <2 kg was an even greater risk of poor outcome.34 It is important that the cardiac catheterisation laboratory has protocols in place to safely care for the most complex patients. For instance, if interventions such as valvuloplasty or stent placement are planned for the patient, it may be prudent to ensure that cross-matched blood be set up. A cardiac surgeon should be available if needed, and the ability to provide extracorporeal membrane oxygenation (ECMO) is ideal.

Given the importance of haemodynamic data in the cardiac catheterisation laboratory, careful planning is required regarding ventilation strategy. Tracheal intubation allows for precise control of Paco2; but, it can result in increased intrathoracic pressure and decreased venous return particularly in preload-dependent patients (e.g. Fontan circulation). Spontaneous ventilation whether through a natural airway, supraglottic airway, or tracheal tube, has minimal effect on haemodynamics. However, increased Paco2 from oversedation can increase PVR. Given the advantages and disadvantages of either technique, the Congenital Cardiac Anesthesia Society does not specifically recommend controlled or spontaneous ventilation.35 Where the patient is to recover is another important consideration. A recovery team experienced in caring for patients after heart catheterisation is also important. It is often preferable to have patients completely emerge after anaesthesia to ensure adequate ventilation and airway protection. However, another important factor is the need to lay flat and still after the procedure often for extended periods of time. This can be challenging for infants, children and patients with cognitive delays. Hence, a dexmedetomidine infusion may be used after surgery to help manage patients who are moving. Whether the tracheal tube is removed while the patient is still anaesthetised should be based on the patient's cardiac status, airway, ventilation difficulty, and their cognitive status before surgery.36

Requirement for a paediatric cardiac anaesthetist

Children with CHD who require sedation or general anaesthesia may arrive at facilities that do not have specialists trained to care for paediatric cardiac patients. It is important to be able to quickly assess and triage patients with appropriate therapies and to the proper location if time permits. For emergency cases, a paediatric cardiac anaesthetist must be consulted, if available. For low-risk situations, outpatient surgeries such as circumcisions may be performed. For high-risk situations, a paediatric centre with ICU bed availability and consultation with a paediatric cardiac anaesthetist are required. Discussion with the patient's care team including the surgeon and cardiologist is warranted. These discussions can significantly impact medical and surgical optimisation such as safe management of perioperative anticoagulation and minimising abdominal insufflation pressure in patients with preload dependent lesions.

Practical aspects of management

Preoperative optimisation

Managing patients with CHD is a multidisciplinary task. Review of information from the patient's primary cardiology team and potential consultation is imperative to ensure patients are optimised for their procedures. A detailed history and physical examination should be performed including history of weight gain, cyanosis, swelling, easy fatigability, baseline saturation, cardiac examination, pulmonary auscultation and distal perfusion. Questions such as ‘Can your child keep up and play with children their own age without getting tired?’ can help determine the child's functional status. For patients with previous cardiac intervention, one must determine whether the patient's condition has been totally corrected, partially corrected with residual defects, or palliated. Review of echocardiography and catheterisation laboratory reports (Fig. 2) can provide additional information regarding cardiac function, perfusion and potential haemodynamic significance of changes in SVR or PVR. Catheterisation diagrams can often help elucidate a patient's anatomy after a complicated repair. Cardiovascular medications such as antihypertensives and pulmonary vasodilators may be maintained in the perioperative period.37 Although angiotensin-converting enzyme (ACE) inhibitors and angiotensin receptor blockers are frequently associated with perioperative hypotension in adult patients, there are fewer data in paediatrics. Regardless, many centres recommend discontinuation the day before surgery.4 Patients may be taking anticoagulation or antiplatelet medications for conditions such as shunt, valve replacement, or atrial fibrillation. Anticoagulant and antiplatelet medications should not be held for simple or superficial surgery, as thrombosis is a common complication of CHD. Discussion is warranted with the patient's primary cardiologist/haematologist regarding continuation of anticoagulation or antiplatelet medications.38 Requirement of spontaneous bacterial endocarditis should be determined before the procedure using international guidelines such as the European Endocarditis Guidelines.39 Patients with severe complex cardiac lesions may benefit from preoperative hydration before induction of anaesthesia to maintain adequate preload. It is also important to delineate the risks associated with anaesthesia with the parents in the preoperative visit if possible. Explaining the possible need for multiple lines, blood products and escalation of care is helpful.

Numerous syndromes are associated with various cardiac lesions, and it is important to be aware of common cardiac lesions associated with different syndromes. For instance, Trisomy 21 is often associated with atrioventricular canal defects, commonly repaired early in the first year of life. William Beuren syndrome is associated with supravalvar aortic stenosis and are at high risk for anaesthesia as previously mentioned. These patients may benefit from early admission with preoperative hydration and slow, careful induction aimed at minimising oxygen consumption and maintaining afterload. A facility with ECMO capabilities is prudent.19 DiGeorge syndrome or 22q11.2 microdeletion can also be associated with a number of cardiac defects (Table 3). These can range from mild conditions such as a VSD, or more severe defects including truncus arteriosus, aortic arch defects, or tetralogy of Fallot.

Intraoperative management

Intraoperative management is diverse, and most techniques have been used. In some patients, such as those with ventricular dysfunction, a slow, carefully titrated induction with cardiac stable medications is beneficial. Propofol and volatile anaesthetic agents should be used judiciously given their potential for decreases in SVR (Table 4). Opioids, dexmedetomidine, midazolam, ketamine and etomidate minimally affect vascular resistance and may be used synergistically to reduce overall propofol or volatile anaesthetic concentration.40 Opioids and benzodiazepines should be used judiciously in patients sensitive to increases in PVR from hypercarbia such as those with PH or Fontan physiology. Synthetic opioids such as fentanyl and hydromorphone may obviate the histamine release associated with morphine which may also exacerbate PH. Multimodal analgesia and enhanced recovery after surgery protocols using regional anaesthesia have continued to increase in popularity.41 Resuscitation equipment and emergency cardiac medications to treat rhythm and haemodynamic disturbances must be immediately available as boluses and infusions, depending on the case and type of procedure.

Regional anaesthesia including neuraxial, truncal and peripheral blocks have all been used successfully in patients with CHD. While both spinal and epidural blocks can be associated with significant haemodynamic derangements, they are often well tolerated from a haemodynamic standpoint in both younger and older patients with various forms of CHD.42,43 Neuraxial anaesthesia may be precluded by factors related to CHD such as anticoagulation medication or vertebral anomalies associated with syndromes such as VACTERL (Vertebra, Anus, Cardiac, Trachea, Esophagus, Renal, Limb).44 Truncal blocks including erector spinae have all been shown to be safe and effective in paediatric cardiac surgery.45 For patients with coagulation abnormalities precluding neuraxial blockade, these blocks can be particularly useful. If postcardiac surgery diaphragmatic dysfunction is suspected, caution should be used with placement of supraclavicular or interscalene blocks that could inadvertently block the phrenic nerve.

Suspected undiagnosed paediatric CHD and minor elective procedures

For elective cases it is best to postpone if there is a suspicion of a significant cardiac lesion. By identifying cardiac lesions before anaesthesia, one can adequately prepare for optimal management of the patient during the procedure. Deciding if the lesion is in fact significant may be more challenging. The history and physical examination should lead the management in these cases. Symptoms that should raise suspicion for underlying cardiac disease include poor weight gain, cyanosis, swelling, easily fatigued, or frequent sweating. A loud murmur or one heard during diastole is also concerning. An echocardiogram should be obtained and consultation with a paediatric cardiologist is highly recommended.

Suspected undiagnosed paediatric CHD in emergency cases

Emergency cases for patients with suspected cardiac lesions can be very challenging. If possible, the patient should be transferred quickly to a centre equipped to handle patients with CHD. If transfer is not possible, consultation with a paediatric cardiac anaesthetist is necessary for recommendations for management. Induction of anaesthesia should be carefully titrated with medications that maintain SVR and CO, such as ketamine and midazolam. Vasoactive medications such as adrenaline (epinephrine) and dopamine should be readily available. Airway management should be performed with minimal effects on SVR and CO. Controlled ventilation should be accomplished with careful attention to venous return and CO. A plan should be in place for transfer as soon as possible to a more specialised centre if needed after surgery.

Conclusions

Patients with CHD may require a multitude of anaesthetics. With increasing survival rates in patients with CHD, many non-paediatric trained anaesthetists will take care of these children at some point in their careers. Understanding patients' anatomic and physiologic classification allows for optimisation of their anaesthetic. In children with more complex disease, surgery, or both, early consultation with the patients' primary cardiac team and a fellowship-trained paediatric anaesthetist is crucial to determine treatment options and if transfer to a specialised care centre is necessary.
