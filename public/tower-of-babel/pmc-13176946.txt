A Robust Cost‐Effective Spectrophotometric Method for Nb Determination in Refractory Nb‐doped Gadolinium Zirconate: Overcoming Matrix Dissolution Challenges
Authors: Poonam Verma, Annapurna Chandane
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTA simple, robust and cost‐effective analytical methodology was developed for the determination of niobium in highly refractory materials: Nb‐doped gadolinium zirconate (Gd2Zr2O7:Nb) and niobium pentoxide (Nb2O5). The challenge of digesting these matrices was successfully accomplished by establishing two independent dissolution procedures: (i) Fluoride‐free digestion under reflux conditions and (ii) microwave‐assisted digestion using a minimal volume of hydrofluoric acid (0.1 mL of 40% hydrofluoric acid). This dual digestion approach provides reliability, flexibility and robustness to the method. Subsequent to dissolution of highly refractory solid matrices, a one‐step spectrophotometric method for Nb determination based on the formation of peroxoniobium complex in sulfuric acid medium was optimised and developed. The proposed method is easy and straightforward, with fewer procedural steps and requires a lower chemical inventory. The developed method has a wide linear dynamic range of 2–225 ppm Nb spanning two orders of magnitude with a regression coefficient of 0.99945 and a detection limit of 0.4 ppm. The precision obtained is 2.13% (method relative standard deviation [RSD], n = 5) and 0.17% (instrumental RSD, n = 9). The recovery of Nb from spiked samples at a 50 ppm level is greater than 98% and, thereby validating the proposed method. Furthermore, the proposed method is independently authenticated using total reflection X‐ray fluorescence/energy‐dispersive X‐ray fluorescence techniques, with the results showing good agreement within the 95% confidence interval.

Introduction

Nuclear energy is crucial in addressing global energy demand while ensuring a clean and sustainable future [1, 2, 3]. In nuclear reactors, excess reactivity is incorporated intentionally at the beginning of the operational cycle. The purpose of initial excess reactivity in the reactor core is to ensure that the reactor remains critical over time, compensating for the loss of reactivity which happens due to a decrease in the fissile content and accumulation of neutron‐absorbing fission products [4]. The initial excess reactivity at the beginning of reactor operation is controlled by burnable absorbers. The burnable absorber, also called burnable neutron poison, are materials with a large neutron absorption cross section (σa) which are converted into a material of relatively low absorption cross section after neutron absorption [4, 5, 6]. Thus, burnable poisons gradually deplete as the reactor operates, and ideally, these poisons should decrease their negative reactivity at the same rate that the fuel's excess positive reactivity is depleted. Apart from controlling the initial excess reactivity, burnable absorbers also contribute to higher burnup levels, enhanced fuel utilisation, and longer fuel cycles [7, 8]. Burnable absorber helps to balance neutron flux across the reactor core, reduces control rod adjustments and prevents excessive power spikes or instability. Additionally, Burnable absorber is also used for tritium production [9], and burning of long‐lived radionuclides from spent nuclear fuel [5].

Among the burnable absorbers like 10B, 113Cd, 151Eu and 153Eu, 155Gd and 157Gd, 167Er, etc. [4], Gd is widely used due to the high neutron absorption cross section and natural abundance of 155Gd and 157Gd [6, 10]. Their absorption cross‐sections are the highest among all stable isotopes. Thermal neutron absorption cross‐section values for 155Gd and 157Gd are 60,900 and 2,54,000 barns with natural abundance of 14.8% and 15.7%, respectively. Gd as Gd2Zr2O7 (gadolinium zirconate) exhibits exceptional radiation tolerance, chemical and thermal stability, high melting point, large thermal expansion coefficient, low thermal conductivity and low swelling properties [6, 10, 11, 12], making it an excellent control rod material for compact high temperature reactor (CHTR, a light water reactor). Additionally, actinides can be incorporated into the lattice of gadolinium zirconate, making it a suitable candidate for the immobilisation of nuclear waste [13, 14, 15, 16, 17, 18, 19, 20].

Gd2Zr2O7 is an A2B2O7 type of oxide, and depending on the radius ratio of the cations (rA/rB), A2B2O7 forms either pyrochlore (for ratios 1.46–1.80) or fluorite (for lower ratios) structure [21, 22]. The pyrochlore structure is better ordered and more stable. The cation radius ratio for Gd2Zr2O7 is 1.46, which is at the lower stability limit for the ordered pyrochlore structure. Doping Gd2Zr2O7 with Nb increases the cation radius ratio and enhances the formation of a more ordered and stable pyrochlore phase; thereby further increasing its radiation tolerance [23, 24, 25]. Additionally, Nb doping suppresses the disorder–order transitions between pyrochlore and defect‐fluorite phases. Nevertheless, Nb doping increases the “cation disorder” in the matrix [26]. Thus, Nb content in gadolinium zirconate is usually kept between 1% to 6% depending on the specific application. Consequently, precise and accurate quantification of niobium content is essential for characterising Nb‐doped gadolinium zirconate.

In literature, Nb determination by inductively coupled plasma mass spectroscopy (ICP‐MS) [27], inductively coupled plasma atomic emission spectroscopy (ICP‐AES) [28], inductively coupled plasma optical emission spectroscopy (ICP‐OES) [29], laser‐induced breakdown spectroscopy (LIBS) [30], neutron activation analysis (NAA) [31], voltammetry [32], ion‐exchange chromatography [33], and spectrometric methods [34, 35, 36, 37] in diverse matrices is reported. However, most of the reported methods require sophisticated instrumentation, high operation cost and are applicable for trace level analysis conditions in the reported relatively simple matrices. Conversely, the sample matrices are the highly refractory ceramic gadolinium zirconate doped with 1% to 6% Nb. As the analyte Nb is a minor constituent of the sample matrix rather than a trace impurity, the required precision, along with the accuracy, is not met by the reported analytical methods. The spectrophotometric determination of Nb, based on the formation of a peroxoniobium complex with H2O2 in the presence of H2SO4 [35], was systematically investigated, optimised, and refined for application to the Nb‐doped gadolinium zirconate matrix. Accordingly, a simple and cost‐effective analytical methodology with improved precision and accuracy was successfully developed for the determination of Nb in gadolinium zirconate doped with Nb.

Dissolution of Nb‐doped gadolinium zirconate is very challenging as it is extremely refractory in nature. Various dissolution methods for Nb‐bearing compounds/ores, comprising leaching/digestion/fusion, reported in the literature are laborious and cumbersome [38, 39]. Dissolution procedures employing significant amounts of fluoride reagents are not only tedious but are also highly undesirable due to fluoride toxicity, its corrosive nature and environmental hazard [35, 40, 41]. Therefore, in the present work, studies were carried out to dissolve refractory Nb‐doped gadolinium zirconate in a heating mantle set‐up with a reflux condenser arranged inside the fume‐hood with a mixture comprising sulfuric acid and ammonium sulphate. Further, microwave‐assisted dissolution in pressurised closed vessels was studied and optimised as an independent alternate digestion method to enhance the flexibility and robustness of the overall analytical method.

The present paper thus describes the development of a simple, fast, cost‐effective and precise method for Nb determination in refractory gadolinium‐zirconate doped with Nb, subsequent to optimisation of dissolution procedures (heating mantle with reflux condenser and microwave technique).

Experimental

Instrumentation

Jasco V670 from Hitachi (Japan), a double‐beam spectrophotometer, was used for obtaining the normal and derivative spectrophotometric data. This instrument works in the wavelength range of 190–1100 nm and uses a photomultiplier tube as a detector.

An Anton Paar Multiwave‐5000 microwave digestion system with an 8× rotor was used for dissolution of Nb‐doped Gd2Zr2O7 and Nb2O5.

Reagents and Chemicals

Unless otherwise stated, all reagents used were of analytical grade quality and were used without further purification. AAS standard of Nb (V) stock solution (985 µg/mL in water) (Fluka, USA) was used for preparing fresh working standards by appropriate dilution. Suprapur sulfuric acid (96% assay), hydrochloric acid (30% assay), nitric acid (65% assay) and hydrofluoric acid (40% assay) of Merck and ammonium sulphate (AR) of Thomas Baker were used in the experiments. High‐purity deionised (DI) water of resistivity 18.2MΩ cm from the Millipore system was used throughout.

Procedure for Dissolution of Nuclear Matrices

Fluoride‐free Reflux Digestion

For dissolution using a heating mantle with a reflux condenser, about 500 mg of Nb‐doped Gd2Zr2O7 or 50 mg of Nb2O5 is taken in a round‐bottom flask. 25 mL of sulfuric acid (96% assay) and 10 g of ammonium sulphate is added to the flask. A condenser of 50 cm length was attached to the mouth of the round‐bottom flask. The heating mantle with a round‐bottom flask and condenser was kept at 200°C for 8 h for dissolution of the matrix. The dissolved solution is carefully transferred from the round‐bottom flask along with the washings. An appropriate aliquot of the dissolved solution is taken for the spectrophotometric measurement.

Microwave‐assisted Digestion With Minimum HF

In microwave‐assisted dissolution, about 50 mg of Nb‐doped Gd2Zr2O7 or 10 mg of Nb2O5 is taken inside the NXF100 Teflon vessels. An acid mixture of 6 mL of sulphuric acid (96% assay), 3 mL of hydrochloric acid (30% assay), 0.1 mL of hydrofluoric acid (40% assay) and 0.9 mL of water is added to the solid sample in the NXF100 Teflon vessel. The microwave system was then closed and run according to the program given in Table 1. After cooling, the dissolved solution is transferred along with the washings and a suitable aliquot is taken for spectrophotometric measurement.

Procedure for Spectrometric Measurement of Nb

Suitable volume of sample/standard solution of Nb is taken in a 10 mL volumetric flask, and 0.3 mL of hydrogen peroxide (40% assay) and 8.5 mL of sulphuric acid (96% assay) are added. The solution is made to 10 mL with DI water. The absorbance spectrum of the solution is measured in the wavelength range from 280 to 500 nm against a reagent blank using a quartz cell of 1 cm path length. The absorbance measurements were done with a Jasco V670 from Hitachi (Japan) double‐beam spectrophotometer, in the concentration range of 2–225 ppm of Nb.

Results and Discussion

Optimisation of Dissolution Parameters

Fluoride‐free Reflux Digestion

Dissolution of refractory Nb‐doped Gd2Zr2O7 is quite challenging, and studies were done to dissolve the matrix in a heating mantle with a reflux condenser. Different acid compositions along with complexing agents were studied at varying temperatures and at different heating times. It was observed that dissolution was appreciable in a mixture of ammonium sulphate along with sulfuric acid, and thus, further studies were done in the mentioned mixture (Table 2). Complete dissolution of about 500 mg of Nb‐doped Gd2Zr2O7 or 50 mg of Nb2O5 was done in a mixture of 25 mL of sulfuric acid and 10 g of ammonium sulphate with a condenser of 50 cm length when heated for 8 h at 200°C.

Microwave‐assisted Digestion With Minimum HF

Microwave technique with pressurised closed vessels was alternatively studied to dissolve refractory materials. Acid mixture composition and microwave parameters were varied systematically for the dissolution studies. Different acid compositions like HCl+HF, HNO3+HF, (H2SO4+HCl), (H2SO4+HF), (H2SO4+HCl+HF) and (HCl+HNO3) were investigated.

As seen in Figure 1, dissolution was better in acid mixtures with HF and predominantly in the acid mixture of (H2SO4+HCl+HF). Concentrated H2SO4 is non‐volatile and has a high boiling point (∼337°C) that helps to elevate the temperature inside the Teflon vessels. HF acid has a unique role in dissolution because the high electronegativity of the fluoride ion can break the strong metal‐oxygen bond of the refractory material, and also due to the strong complexing capacity of the fluoride ion. Thus, further investigations were done in an acid mixture of (H2SO4+HCl+HF) with varying amounts of HF. Studies were done to dissolve the highly refractive materials in the minimum possible HF. In microwave‐assisted dissolution, the hold‐up time of the maximum wattage used is a significant parameter. Figure 2 shows Nb obtained from the dissolved solution of the refractory matrix with varying hold time of 900 W in a microwave system. It is seen from Figure 2 that complete dissolution of Nb‐doped gadolinium zirconate is achieved at a hold‐up time of 80 min at 900 W, and thus, the hold‐up time of 80 min at 900 W was fixed, and the total program time was further optimised to three hours. The optimised microwave digestion conditions are presented in Table 1. Using these conditions, approximately 50 mg of Nb‐doped gadolinium zirconate or 10 mg of Nb2O5 was completely dissolved using an acid mixture comprising 6 mL H2SO4, 3 mL HCl, 0.1 mL HF, and 0.9 mL water.

Thus, both the digestion approaches, namely (i) fluoride‐free reflux digestion using an optimised mixture of sulphuric acid and ammonium sulphate and (ii) microwave‐assisted digestion employing sulphuric acid, hydrochloric acid and hydrofluoric acid, can be effectively employed for the dissolution of refractory matrices. The microwave‐assisted method offers the advantage of shorter digestion time (3 h) and reduced chemical consumption (cf. Table 3), whereas reflux digestion requires a longer duration (8 h). Notably, the reflux method eliminates the use of highly corrosive fluoride reagents and allows processing of larger sample sizes. In contrast, the microwave‐assisted technique requires a minimal volume of HF (0.1 mL, 40% assay). Further, Nb obtained by both the digestion methods agrees well within the 95% confidence interval (Table 4) and corroborates that both the digestion methods are equally applicable.

The use of two independent digestion methods enhanced the method's robustness and reliability. The results obtained by both the digestion methods were compared using Student's t‐test at the 95% confidence level. As shown in Table 4, the calculated t values were lower than the critical t value, indicating no significant difference between the two methods. The agreement of results obtained for Nb from two different chemical approaches confirms quantitative dissolution of the refractory matrix and rules out method‐specific biases, analyte loss, or matrix‐induced interferences. This dual‐digestion strategy therefore strengthens confidence in the developed digestion procedures.

Optimisation of Spectrophotometric Parameters, Interference Studies and Analytical Figures of Merit

Niobium forms a yellow‐coloured peroxoniobium complex with hydrogen peroxide in an acidic medium. The formation of this coloured complex from initially transparent Nb solutions enables the determination of Nb by UV–Visible spectrophotometry [32]. Compared to other analytical methods, spectrophotometric determination of Nb as peroxoniobium offers advantages such as low chemical consumption, shorter analysis time, operational simplicity, and fewer procedural steps. Accordingly, studies were undertaken to optimise the spectrophotometric parameters for Nb determination.

The effect of sulfuric acid concentration was investigated using sulfuric acid–water mixtures as the reaction medium. An increase in sulfuric acid concentration resulted in a corresponding increase in the absorbance of the Nb peak, accompanied by a bathochromic shift toward longer wavelengths, as shown in Figure 3. The increase in the absorbance and the bathochromic shift is due to a decrease in the coordinated water with the peroxoniobium complex with the increase in sulfuric acid concentration. Maximum absorbance for the Nb peak was observed in 80% sulfuric acid at a λmax of 362 nm, and therefore, further studies were carried out in 80% sulfuric acid to ensure improved reaction conditions and signal stability.

Effect of sulfuric acid concentration on peroxoniobium absorbance. [Nb] = 100 ppm.

Further, the effect of the reagent hydrogen peroxide was studied, and it was found that 0.3 % hydrogen peroxide gave maximum absorbance for 50 ppm of Nb, while 0.9 % hydrogen peroxide was required to obtain maximum absorbance for Nb concentrations higher than 100 ppm. Excess ligand peroxide is required for optimal complex formation with metal Nb. Figure 4A,B illustrates the effect of hydrogen peroxide concentration on 50 ppm and 120 ppm of Nb, respectively. A slight excess of hydrogen peroxide (1.2%) was therefore selected for subsequent studies to ensure complete complex formation over a wide concentration range. With optimised parameters, the Nb peak is observed with maximum absorbance soon after the reagents are mixed in the cuvette (within 30 s) and is stable at least till 300 min, as shown in Figure 5, indicating rapid kinetics of the reaction and stability of the solution within the day of analysis.

Since spectrophotometric determination of Nb was carried out in the presence of matrix, interference studies of matrix elements Gd and Zr were done over a range of weight ratios. The Nb content in gadolinium zirconate is kept between 1% to 6%, and for 1% Nb, the ratio of matrix elements Gd and Zr would be the highest. For 1% Nb in the matrix, Zr is 30 times, and Gd is 50 times that of Nb on a weight basis. Therefore, interference studies were done upto these maximum weight ratio of matrix elements to evaluate their maximum effect on Nb determination.

It was observed that Gd does not have significant absorbance with hydrogen peroxide in the wavelength region of interest and therefore does not interfere with Nb determination. However, Zr shows slight absorbance at the wavelength region of Nb and may cause spectral interference, as shown in Figure 6. Therefore, Nb was analysed by the standard addition method to eliminate matrix and medium effects.

The calibration plot was linear over the concentration range of 2–225 ppm, with a regression coefficient of 0.99945 at a hydrogen peroxide concentration of 1.2% (cf. inset of Figure 7). Method Validation and Application to Nuclear Matrices

Method validation was carried out by studying the recovery of Nb from the spiked samples of Nb‐doped Gd2Zr2O7, and the recovery obtained at the 50 ppm level was better than 98%.

The developed spectrophotometric method was successfully applied to determine niobium in actual samples of Nb‐doped Gd2Zr2O7 and the precursor raw material, niobium pentoxide, using the standard addition technique. The developed method was further authenticated by analysing selected actual samples by total reflection X‐ray fluorescence (TXRF) and energy‐dispersive X‐ray fluorescence (EDXRF). Student's t‐test was performed at the 95% confidence level, and the calculated t values were lower than the corresponding critical value (Table 6), indicating no significant difference between the results obtained by the developed spectrophotometric method and the alternate TXRF/EDXRF methods.

Conclusions

An efficient and robust analytical method was successfully developed and applied for the determination of niobium in highly refractory nuclear materials: niobium‐doped gadolinium zirconate (Gd2Zr2O7:Nb) and niobium pentoxide (Nb2O5). The primary challenge of digesting these refractory matrices was overcome through the establishment of two independent dissolution procedures: (i) A fluoride‐free digestion employing optimized mixture of sulfuric acid and ammonium sulphate under reflux conditions, thereby reducing corrosion, waste handling issues and analyst exposure and (ii) By microwave‐assisted digestion technique employing a minimal volume of HF (0.1 mL of 40%, which is substantially lower than typically reported values in the conventional methods). The microwave technique is quicker and requires a lower chemical inventory. The dual digestion approach provided flexibility, redundancy, and demonstrated method robustness across different digestion chemistries. Following digestion, niobium is quantified with a one‐step spectrophotometric peroxoniobium method, which significantly reduces analytical complexity compared to existing multi‐step or extraction‐based methods. Therefore, the proposed developed method is flexible and simple, reducing both procedural steps and chemical use while maintaining high analytical performance, as validated by good precision, quantitative spike recoveries (>98%), high linear dynamic range spanning two orders of magnitude and agreement of results within the 95% confidence interval with independent TXRF/EDXRF analysis.
