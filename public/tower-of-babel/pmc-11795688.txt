Use of machine learning algorithms to predict outcomes among frail patients undergoing percutaneous coronary intervention: Are we there yet?
Authors: Josip Andelo Borovac, Mihajlo Kovacic, Stefan Harb
Published: 2024

Frailty is an ambiguous phenomenon that is often overlooked in clinical practice and is difficult to measure. Clegg and colleagues defined frailty as a state of increased vulnerability in which body's functional reserve to respond to various stressors is significantly decreased [1]. Frailty is much more prevalent in patients with advanced age, however, frailty must not be misunderstood as a condition exclusive to elderly because it may equally affect young patients with severe comorbidities such as those with chronic renal failure or autoimmune diseases [2]. This process is marked by the cumulative decline in function of multiple physiological systems and might predispose individual to major disturbances and changes in their health status, regardless of the magnitude of initial stressor. Since frailty is associated with adverse clinical outcomes and increased mortality and morbidity risks across virtually every medical discipline, it becomes increasingly important to identify these patients in clinical practice [3].

Of note, the presence of frailty negatively impacts outcomes in several cardiovascular conditions [4]. For example, Marinus et al. showed that frailty was present in up to 74 % to 80 % of patients with heart failure (HF) or aortic stenosis and even up to 30 % of patients with coronary artery disease (CAD) [5]. Same study reported the overall 2.5- to 3.5-fold increase in the risk of mortality among frail vs. non-frail patients. A recent systematic review and meta-analysis showed that, among elderly patients undergoing percutaneous coronary intervention (PCI), frailty was associated with an increased likelihood of in-hospital (OR 3.59), short-term (OR 6.61) and long-term (OR 3.24) mortality, compared with non-frail patients while the prevalence of frailty was up to 27.8 % [6]. A prospective study from a tertiary care hospital revealed that frailty was present in nearly 20 % of elderly patients undergoing PCI [7]. In the acute setting, it has been consistently demonstrated that frail patients with ST-elevation myocardial infarction (STEMI) and non-ST-elevation myocardial infarction (NSTEMI) undergoing primary PCI experience worse short-term, mid-term, and long-term outcomes [8], [9], [10], [11]. Due to perceived procedural risks and associated comorbidities, these patients are much less treated with PCI in the real world, although they might gain robust short-term survival benefits with revascularization as opposed to conservative treatment only [9], [10], [12]. In the setting of non-ST-elevation acute coronary syndrome (NSTE-ACS), elderly patients with frailty were less likely to receive invasive treatment while such treatment appeared to improve outcomes compared to non-invasive treatment [13], [14], although there are studies that failed to show survival benefit of PCI in this population [15].

For the reasons stated above, it becomes evident that clinical and paraclinical tools that might help us identify patients with frailty undergoing invasive percutaneous procedures are urgently needed and further research efforts in this arena should be encouraged. This problem was examined in the work by Soong and colleagues in the original paper published in IJC Heart & Vasculature [16]. In this work, authors examined data of slightly over 1,700 patients that underwent PCI in elective and semi-urgent setting at the tertiary-level care center. Patients with STEMI were excluded from the analysis while the study was conducted between 2014 and 2015. Authors recreated frailty index (FI) [17] by using logistic regression model aided by Extreme Gradient Boosting (XGBoost) machine learning (ML) methodology to determine most important predictors of adverse clinical outcomes following PCI. Patients were followed for outcomes during 33 ± 37 months while primary endpoint consisted of cardiac and in-hospital death while secondary outcome was an investigator-selected composite of repeat coronary angiogram, subsequent congestive heart failure (CHF) and myocardial infarction or cerebrovascular events. Enrolled patients were predominantly male (80.6 %) with the mean age of 61 ± 11 years, 70.9 % had arterial hypertension, 42.1 % had diabetes mellitus, 12.7 % had congestive HF while nearly 7 % had atrial fibrillation.

As their main finding, authors report that FI had greatest predicting power of adverse events following PCI, especially when added to chronological age and sex and they found this to be the case regardless of clinical presentation (elective PCI vs. ACS). Based on multiple statistical analyses, it seems that FI, aided with additional information on age and sex, provided consistent and robust prediction of cardiac death and in-hospital death, but less so of composite adverse events. This, in our opinion, is not a major problem since the composite outcome of adverse events consisted of arbitrarily chosen variables and rather “soft” endpoints, compared to “hard” endpoints such as cardiac and all-cause in-hospital death.

This study presents an important research effort from a perspective of a busy clinician since this informatical approximation of clinical frailty is calculated automatically based on routinely collected patient data from electronic health record (EHR) system. Variables that were taken into account by the ML algorithm encompassed demographic data, patient associated-diagnoses, comorbidities, angiography data, cardiac imaging, and laboratory data. The 30 variables that are used to generate FI should be available in the vast majority of institutions with electronically linked data, therefore, this might theoretically be implemented as a module within various EHR systems. This could be the elegant way to gain objectivization of frailty based on routinely available data since we know that screening for the frailty is poorly and unfrequently done in clinical practice. This could further reduce physician workload burden and might aid in the assessment of periprocedural risks, procedure planning, and prognosis estimation.

Another opportunity that remains unexplored is the lack of quality of life (QoL) measurements during the hospitalization. As we well know, QoL is an important metric from the patient perspective and might matter more than mortality among frail patients. Perhaps contemporary tools such as artificial intelligence (AI) might in the future inform us about the optimal treatment for frail patients with significant coronary disease that would maximize patient QoL down the line. Similar was shown among patients with peripheral artery disease that were evaluated for vascular intervention on lower limbs by the AI [18]. Finally, wearable devices that measure various physiological signals or smartphone apps that capture data and responses from patients in real-time are a future way to go since they continuously generate huge amounts of real-world prospective and fluctuating data. Such an approach would likely help us in the true assessment of impact of PCI on QoL among frail patients. For example, similar approach was cleverly implemented by Rajkumar & Al-Lamee et al. in their ORBITA II trial in which patients used a dedicated smartphone app to report the presence or absence of angina or daily number of anginal episodes to gauge impact of percutaneous revascularization on daily living and symptoms [19]. This is important because, quality-wise, such data are superior to retrospectively collected data that are post-hoc boosted by extreme statistic multiplication routines. In fact, one of the limitations of the current study is that it was derived from retrospective data and there is a lack of external validation. Therefore, it remains uncertain how would this model fare in the prospective external validation studies at other clinical sites. As previously hinted, there is also an issue of imputation of missing variables from the dataset and while the authors attempted to solve this with classification and regression tree (CART) imputation, we need to be aware that no “statistical wizardry” can act as a perfect substitution for real data.

Nevertheless, authors should be commended for their innovative approach towards frailty assessment among patients undergoing PCI since we know that this is linked with hard endpoints. The model showed a good predictive power for these outcomes and is a promising tool that might be easily automated within hospital EHR systems. However, future studies should externally validate this model before it is considered for widespread implementation in clinical practice. Finally, we also need to learn what to do with this information. How will this inform practice and help us in selecting the best candidates for PCI among frail patients? This is another piece of the puzzle added to the complex process of decision-making in this vulnerable patient population but we need to be aware that we are still far away from solving the puzzle. Let this paragraph serve as an answer to a question posed in the title.
