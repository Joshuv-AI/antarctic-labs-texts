Nationwide enzooticity of ovine toxoplasmosis and neosporosis in Türkiye
Authors: Onur Ceylan, Ferda Sevinc, Abdoul Bhassit Bonkoungou, Ceylan Ceylan, Harun Yonar, Malak Najmalden Ghaibullah, Shinuo Cao, Xuenan Xuan
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundToxoplasmosis and neosporosis are important protozoan infections affecting sheep production in Türkiye, with implications for both animal health and zoonotic risk. In light of their epidemiological relevance, the present study aimed to investigate the seroprevalence and endemic status of Toxoplasma gondii and Neospora caninum infections in the sheep population across Türkiye.MethodsA total of 2,688 sheep serum samples randomly collected from the seven geographical regions of the country were analyzed using an indirect ELISA based on recombinant TgSAG2 and NcSAG1 proteins.ResultsAccording to the results, the overall seroprevalence was 30.51% for T. gondii and 5.21% for N. caninum. The highest T. gondii seropositivity was in the Black Sea Region (38.54%), and the lowest in Central Anatolia (19.27%) and the Mediterranean Region (21.35%). For N. caninum, the highest seroprevalence was also in the Black Sea Region (11.46%), and the lowest in the Aegean Region (2.60%). Mono-infections with T. gondii and N. caninum were 27.01% and 1.71%, respectively, while 3.50% of the animals were co-infected. T. gondii seropositivity was significantly higher in sheep over 12 months of age (33.92%) than in younger animals (18.41%) (P < 0.001), whereas no age-related association was found for N. caninum (P = 0.485). Regional differences were significant for both pathogens (P < 0.001). Although higher seropositivity rates were observed in rural areas for both infections, the differences were not statistically significant (T. gondii: P = 0.318; N. caninum: P = 0.0777). Inoculation rates revealed endemic instability for both toxoplasmosis and neosporosis across all age groups, indicating a risk of uncontrolled spread and potential for periodic outbreaks.ConclusionIn conclusion, T. gondii was found to be widespread among sheep in Türkiye, whereas N. caninum had a more limited yet notable distribution. Given the zoonotic importance of toxoplasmosis, it is crucial to strengthen biosecurity and implement region-specific, risk-based control strategies. This study lays a scientific foundation for future preventive measures by enabling nationwide epidemiological mapping of ovine toxoplasmosis and neosporosis. Introduction

Sheep farming plays a vital role in Türkiye’s rural economies, contributing substantially to household income, food security, and the sustainability of small ruminant production systems. Sheep meat, which constitutes nearly a quarter of the country’s red meat production, and nutrient-rich sheep milk, widely used in traditional dairy products, are both integral to local consumption and agricultural livelihoods [1, 2]. The strategic economic and nutritional value of sheep-derived products highlights the need to address infectious diseases that threaten flock productivity and rural resilience. Among these, protozoan infections pose a significant barrier to sustainable sheep production. Notably, ovine toxoplasmosis and neosporosis are two economically significant protozoan diseases that adversely affect small ruminant productivity [3]. Beyond their direct impact on animal health and productivity, these diseases are also of public health concern due to their zoonotic potential. In Türkiye, public health reports and antenatal screening data indicate substantial human exposure to Toxoplasma gondii, including documented congenital cases, underscoring its ongoing relevance for maternal–fetal health and the broader population. This human health burden reinforces the need to evaluate animal reservoirs and transmission pathways in sheep and to align ovine surveillance with public-health priorities [4–6].

Toxoplasma gondii, the causative agent of toxoplasmosis, is known to cause abortion, stillbirth, and weak offspring in pregnant ewes, leading to reduced flock productivity and profitability through reproductive losses. In addition to these direct impacts, the economic burden associated with toxoplasmosis includes the costs of diagnosis, treatment, and the implementation of control measures [7]. On the other hand, N. caninum, although primarily known as an abortifacient agent in cattle, has also been implicated in reproductive losses in sheep. While its economic impact in sheep is less well defined, increasing evidence supports N. caninum’s role in ovine abortion and neonatal mortality [8–10]. Both pathogens are capable of establishing persistent infections through tissue cyst formation and can be transmitted transplacentally, with environmental contamination playing a major role in horizontal transmission [3, 7].

The intricate pathogenesis and overlapping transmission pathways of T. gondii and N. caninum highlight the potential for co-infections within the same host. Such co-infections may exacerbate clinical manifestations and pose additional challenges for accurate diagnosis [11, 12]. Multiple studies have reported co-infections in small ruminants, particularly among animals with a history of abortion, suggesting shared environmental exposure and common risk factors. For instance, Ceylan et al. [10] reported co-infection rates of 82.76% (48/58) for N. caninum and 63.16% (48/76) for T. gondii in aborted ewes, underscoring the need for improved surveillance and control strategies.

Given their predominantly subclinical nature, diagnosing these infections typically relies on serological methods. Among these, indirect enzyme-linked immunosorbent assays (iELISA) using recombinant surface antigens such as TgSAG2 and NcSAG1 have been widely employed for their high specificity and sensitivity, enabling reliable detection of specific antibodies and large-scale seroepidemiological surveys [13–15]. These assays also help reduce false-positive results due to cross-reactivity, thereby improving diagnostic accuracy [11].

In Türkiye, several regional studies have explored the seroprevalence of toxoplasmosis and neosporosis in small ruminants, with rates varying by geography, climate, and management practices [16, 17]. Kolören & Dubey [6] reported toxoplasmosis seroprevalence ranging from 9.5% to 98.92%, with higher rates observed in warm and humid regions conducive to oocyst survival. Neosporosis appears less prevalent, with seropositivity rates ranging from 2.1% to 12.4% across provinces [17–19]. However, a recent study from Central Anatolia reported a remarkably high seroprevalence of 50.4% in sheep with abortion history [10], indicating a potential underestimation of the disease burden in previous studies. Despite these findings, a nationwide survey assessing the seroprevalence and endemic status of these infections in Türkiye is lacking. Comprehensive data are urgently needed to guide evidence-based control strategies. Understanding the endemic stability or instability of T. gondii and N. caninum infections, especially through inoculation rate analyses, is crucial for designing targeted interventions and for prioritizing vaccine development and implementation [20–22]. Therefore, this study aims to determine the seroprevalence and endemic status of ovine toxoplasmosis and neosporosis across the seven geographical regions of Türkiye, while also evaluating variations based on region, age, and settlement type.

Materials and methods

Study area and serum samples

The material used in this study consisted of sheep serum samples previously collected from across Türkiye within the scope of a different study and currently available in our laboratory stocks, under the ethical approval number 2017/36 granted by the Experimental Animal Production and Research Center Ethics Committee of the Veterinary Faculty of Selcuk University. In this study, the required sample size to determine the seroprevalence of T. gondii and N. caninum in each region of the target sheep population was calculated using the formula proposed by Thrusfield [23]: n = (1.96)² × Pexp × (1 − Pexp)/d², where n is the minimum required sample size for each animal group, Pexp is the expected prevalence of the disease in the population (assumed to be 50% in this study), and d is the desired level of precision (set at 5%). The expected prevalence (Pexp) was set at 50% based on a review of prior data on the seroprevalence of T. gondii and N. caninum in the study population. This is a conservative estimate that maximizes the required sample size, as a prevalence near 50% demands the largest sample size to maintain a specific level of precision. A 5% precision level (d = 0.05) was chosen, indicating that the study aimed to estimate prevalence with a ± 5% margin of error. Based on these parameters (Pexp = 0.50, d = 0.05), the minimum required sample size was calculated to be 384 sheep per region. This value ensures adequate statistical power and precision at a 95% confidence level. In total, 2,688 sheep serum samples from seven regions of Türkiye were included in the study. Procurement of proteins to be used as antigens in the indirect ELISA

Recombinant TgSAG2, NcSAG1, and GST proteins were available in sufficient quantities in our laboratory stocks to conduct the planned analyses. These proteins were originally obtained from the National Research Center for Protozoan Diseases, Obihiro University of Agriculture and Veterinary Medicine, Japan [13]. In the iELISA assays conducted in this study, the recombinant TgSAG2 and NcSAG1 proteins from our stocks were used as antigens, while the purified GST protein was used as a control antigen to account for potential non-specific reactivity associated with the GST fusion system.

Recombinant TgSAG2 and NcSAG1 based indirect ELISA

To detect the presence of anti-T. gondii and anti-N. caninum specific antibodies in sheep serum samples, an iELISA technique described by Zhou et al. [13] was used, employing recombinant TgSAG2 and NcSAG1 proteins as antigens. In the iELISA assay, TgSAG2, NcSAG1, and GST proteins were diluted in carbonate-bicarbonate buffer (pH 9.6, 0.05 M) at a concentration of 2 µg/ml. ELISA microplate wells were coated with 100 µl of antigen solution and incubated overnight at 4 °C. Because both recombinant TgSAG2 and NcSAG1 proteins were expressed using the GST fusion system, purified GST protein was included in the assay as a control antigen to account for potential non-specific binding. After discarding the coating solution and washing once with PBST (containing Tween 20), the plates were blocked with 1X PBS containing 3% non-fat dry milk at 37 °C for 1 h. The wells were then washed once with PBST and incubated with serum samples diluted 1:100 in 3% non-fat dry milk at 37 °C for 1 h. Following this step, the plates were washed six times with PBST. To detect antigen-antibody binding, horseradish peroxidase-conjugated anti-sheep IgG secondary antibody (produced in rabbit; Bethyl, Montgomery, AL, USA) at a 1:4000 dilution and ABTS [2,2’-azinobis (3-ethylbenzothiazoline-6-sulfonic acid)] substrate (Sigma, St. Louis, MO, USA) were used. Color development was observed at room temperature, and the reaction was stopped by adding 2 M sulfuric acid as a stop solution to inactivate the peroxidase enzyme. Optical density (OD) was measured at 415 nm using an ELISA microplate reader (Rayto Microplate Reader, Model: RT-2100 C).

Evaluation of iELISA

In this study, a cut-off value was calculated to determine positive and negative samples for anti-T. gondii and anti-N. caninum antibodies. For this purpose, 20 sheep serum samples previously identified as negative in our earlier studies [10, 13] were analyzed. The cut-off value was determined by adding three times the standard deviation to the mean OD415 value obtained from these negative samples. In addition, two positive and two negative control sera were included in each microplate to ensure the accuracy and consistency of the assay results. Samples with OD415 values above the cut-off were considered positive, while those below the cut-off were considered negative.

Statistical analysis

Various chi-square tests were applied to evaluate the statistical significance of differences between categorical variables. For comparisons involving two groups, the Yates’ corrected chi-square test was used to assess the association between groups. In cases involving more than two groups, the Pearson chi-square test was preferred. When a statistically significant overall difference was observed among the groups, Bonferroni-adjusted pairwise proportion comparisons were conducted to identify which groups differed from each other. In these comparisons, groups labeled with the same letter were considered not significantly different, while those marked with different letters were considered to differ significantly. Data were summarized as frequencies and percentages (%), and for each comparison, the chi-square value (χ²), degrees of freedom, and P-value were provided below the tables. A P-value of less than 0.05 was considered statistically significant. All analyses were performed using R software (version 4.4.1).

Results

A total of 32.18% (865/2,688) of the serum samples analyzed using iELISA tests based on recombinant TgSAG2 and NcSAG1 proteins were found to be positive for at least one of the anti-T. gondii or anti-N. caninum antibodies. When infections with either of the two species were considered collectively, the overall seropositivity rates by region were as follows: Black Sea Region 40.89%, Aegean Region 39.58%, Marmara Region 40.1%, Eastern Anatolia 34.38%, Southeastern Anatolia 26.31%, Mediterranean Region 23.18%, and Central Anatolia 20.31%.

Using the iELISA based on recombinant TgSAG2 protein as antigen, anti-T. gondii-specific antibodies were detected in 30.51% (820/2,688) of the serum samples. Regionally, the highest T. gondii seroprevalence was observed in the Black Sea Region (38.54%), followed by the Aegean Region (38.28%), Marmara Region (37.76%), Eastern Anatolia Region (32.81%), Southeastern Anatolia Region (25.52%), Mediterranean Region (21.35%), and Central Anatolia Region (19.27%). On the other hand, the iELISA based on recombinant NcSAG1 protein detected anti-N. caninum-specific antibodies in 5.21% (140/2,688) of the samples. The regional distribution of N. caninum seropositivity showed a pattern similar to that of toxoplasmosis, with the highest seroprevalence again found in the Black Sea Region (11.46%). This was followed by the Eastern Anatolia Region (6.25%), Marmara Region (5.99%), Mediterranean Region (3.65%), Central Anatolia Region (3.39%), Southeastern Anatolia Region (3.13%), and the Aegean Region (2.6%).

When mono-infections and dual co-infections with T. gondii and N. caninum were assessed, the overall seroprevalence of T. gondii mono-infection was 27.01% (726/2,688), while N. caninum mono-infection was detected in 1.71% (46/2,688) of the samples. Although N. caninum infections were mostly observed concurrently with T. gondii, the dual co-infection seroprevalence was determined to be 3.50% (94/2,688). According to the letter-based groupings from the post hoc test, statistically significant differences were observed between regions bearing different letters. For example, the Black Sea, Aegean, and Marmara regions (group b) stood out with higher seroprevalence rates, while the Mediterranean and Central Anatolia regions (group a) displayed lower rates. In this study, the seroprevalence of Neospora caninum in sheep was evaluated using iELISA across the seven geographical regions of Türkiye, and the obtained data were statistically compared (Table 2). Chi-square analysis revealed that the differences in seropositivity rates among regions were statistically significant (χ² = 44.835, df = 6, P < 0.001). According to pairwise comparisons with Bonferroni correction, the Black Sea Region had the highest seropositivity rate at 11.46% and was found to differ significantly from all other regions (group b). The results indicate a statistically significant association between age group and T. gondii seropositivity (Table 3). The seropositivity rate in the 6–12-month age group (86.7%) was significantly higher compared to the 0–6-month group. This finding suggests that exposure to T. gondii increases with age and that the infection is more likely to be detected through the antibody response of the immune system as animals grow older.

No statistically significant difference was observed in N. caninum seroprevalence among age groups (Table 3). Although the seropositivity rate was numerically higher in the 6–12-month group compared to the 0–6-month group (80.7% vs. 19.3%), this difference was not statistically significant (P > 0.05). This finding suggests that N. caninum infection does not markedly increase with age and that exposure may occur at similar rates even in early life stages.

In the study, T. gondii seropositivity was compared between urban (district/city) and rural (village/hamlet/highland) settlements (Table 3). The seropositivity rate was 57.4% in rural areas and 42.6% in urban areas. Although the positivity rate was numerically higher in rural regions, the chi-square test (χ² = 0.995, P = 0.3185) indicated that this difference was not statistically significant (P > 0.05). This finding suggests that there is no significant association between settlement type and T. gondii infection, implying that the seroprevalence remains relatively similar in sheep raised in both rural and urban environments.

In Table 3, N. caninum seropositivity rates were compared between urban (district/city) and rural (village/hamlet/highland) settlements. The seropositivity rate was 48.6% in urban centers and 51.4% in rural areas. Although the rate was numerically higher in rural regions, the chi-square test (χ² = 3.113, P = 0.0777) indicated that this difference was not statistically significant (P > 0.05). However, since the P-value is close to the threshold of significance (0.05), this finding may be interpreted as a trend approaching statistical significance. It suggests that in studies with larger sample sizes, settlement type could potentially have an effect on the prevalence of N. caninum.

Discussion

Despite their typically subclinical course in sheep, T. gondii and N. caninum infections can result in significant reproductive losses, leading to considerable economic burdens in the livestock industry. Moreover, due to their zoonotic potential, they also pose a risk to public health [3, 8, 24]. Toxoplasma gondii is a major pathogen transmitted via oocysts shed into the environment by cats, leading to abortion, stillbirth, and weak lambs during gestation. The risk of congenital toxoplasmosis in pregnant women and severe clinical presentations in immunosuppressed individuals highlight the zoonotic importance of this parasite [25, 26]. While N. caninum is less frequently reported in sheep, recent evidence supports its involvement in abortion and perinatal losses in small ruminants [17, 18, 27]. Given these concerns, there is a pressing need to understand the current epidemiological landscape of these infections across Türkiye. This study represents the most comprehensive investigation to date in Türkiye, evaluating the seroprevalence and endemic status of T. gondii and N. caninum infections using 2,688 sheep serum samples equally collected from each of the country’s seven geographical regions, and the findings reveal substantial regional variation, underscoring the need for tailored disease control strategies that account for local risk factors and transmission dynamics.

Although numerous studies have explored the seroprevalence of toxoplasmosis in sheep across different parts of Türkiye [10, 13, 28, 29], data from many provinces remain scarce, and a comprehensive dataset representing the nationwide epidemiological status is still lacking. Given the zoonotic nature and complex transmission dynamics of T. gondii, toxoplasmosis continues to pose a public health concern. Therefore, accurate risk assessment and the design of effective control strategies require updated, large-scale epidemiological data [30]. In this context, both host-related factors and the diagnostic tools employed play a critical role in determining seroprevalence [31]. Li et al. [32] demonstrated that iELISA based on recombinant TgSAG2 protein offers high specificity and practicality for detecting anti-T. gondii antibodies in large populations. Building on this, the current study utilized the TgSAG2-based iELISA to assess the seroprevalence of T. gondii in sheep and provide the first comprehensive, regionally balanced mapping of the disease across Türkiye, a major small ruminant-producing country in Europe.

The seroprevalence of ovine toxoplasmosis has been extensively studied in Türkiye, with results consistently revealing marked regional differences. In a comprehensive review, Kolören and Dubey [6] compiled previous findings based on various serological techniques, such as SFDT, LAT, IHA, IFAT, and ELISA, and reported a seroprevalence range of 9.5% to 98.92%. More recent studies largely support this variation; however, Aktaş and Aydın [29] reported a considerably lower rate of 4.58% in a study involving 960 pre-slaughter sheep serum samples from Erzurum province. This outlier suggests that the lower boundary of the national prevalence range should be revised. In light of current data, the updated seroprevalence range for T. gondii in Turkish sheep now spans from 4.58% to 98.92% [6, 10, 17, 29]. In recent years, iELISA utilizing recombinant TgSAG2 antigen has been increasingly adopted for its superior sensitivity and specificity. For instance, Ceylan et al. [17] reported a 32.6% seropositivity rate using this method in samples collected from Southeastern Türkiye. In another study involving sheep with abortion history, the same group found a notably high seroprevalence of 66.09% [10], reinforcing the association between T. gondii infection and reproductive disorders in small ruminants. The substantial variation in seroprevalence across different studies and regions may be attributed to differences in climatic conditions, management practices, environmental contamination, and feline population density. These findings underscore the heterogeneous distribution of toxoplasmosis in Türkiye and highlight the urgent need for standardized, large-scale epidemiological surveys. The development of comprehensive, harmonized seroprevalence maps would significantly improve our understanding of the disease’s geographical spread. In this context, the present study addresses a critical gap in the literature by providing nationally representative data based on a uniform diagnostic approach.

Comparing studies reporting T. gondii seroprevalence in small ruminants from different countries is quite difficult due to methodological variations such as the type, sensitivity, specificity, and cut-off values of the serological tests used, as well as the characteristics of the target populations [3]. Nevertheless, current literature demonstrates that T. gondii infections are widespread among sheep and goats globally [6, 26, 33]. In the present study, the recombinant TgSAG2-based iELISA method, widely used in epidemiological research and known for its high diagnostic reliability, was employed. This technique is favored for its field applicability and high sensitivity in detecting specific antibodies associated with both acute and chronic infections [32], thus ensuring robust serological data. Accordingly, the national seroprevalence of T. gondii was determined to be 30.51% in this study, consistent with prevalence ranges reported in many other countries [6, 34, 35]. The highest seropositivity was detected in the Black Sea Region (38.54%) and the lowest in the Central Anatolia (19.27%) and Mediterranean Regions (21.35%), with the differences between regions being statistically significant (P< 0.001). This variation aligns with findings that moist and temperate climates support longer oocyst survival in the environment [36]. Age-based evaluations showed that seropositivity in sheep > 12 months of age (33.92%) was significantly higher than in the 0–12 months group (18.41%) (P< 0.001), indicating that cumulative exposure over time increases infection risk [35]. Although location-based analysis showed numerically higher seropositivity in rural areas (57.4%) compared to urban centers (42.6%), this difference was not statistically significant (P = 0.318). However, higher rates in rural areas may be attributed to risk factors such as open grazing, uncontrolled water sources, and proximity to stray or domestic cats. In conclusion, the findings validate the use of TgSAG2-based iELISA for reliable serological assessment and highlight the influence of age, geographical region, and environmental exposure on infection dynamics. These insights underscore the need for region-specific, risk-based strategies to effectively control T. gondii infections in sheep populations.

Although N. caninum infection can occasionally lead to abortion, birth of weak lambs, neonatal mortality, and even clinical symptoms in adult sheep, its economic, clinical, and epidemiological significance in ovine health remains uncertain [31]. Earlier studies failed to establish a clear association between neosporosis and abortion in sheep, as no evidence of infection was detected in various tissues or body fluids of aborted fetuses [37]. However, more recent reports of N. caninum in sheep flocks from various countries have brought attention to its potential impact on the sheep industry [10, 38–41]. González-Warleta et al. [41] reported that the lambing rate in N. caninum seropositive ewes was significantly lower than that of seronegative ones. Following this recognition, research interest in the global seroprevalence of N. caninum in sheep has grown [42]. Dubey et al. [31] emphasized that test type, cut-off value, breed, age, sex, and husbandry conditions can all influence N. caninum seroprevalence rates. In the present study, the nationwide seroprevalence in Türkiye was determined to be 5.21%, indicating a limited but noteworthy presence of infection in the sheep population. This finding aligns with earlier regional studies in Türkiye, which reported rates ranging from 2.1% to 12.4% [13, 16–19]. Notably, the highest regional prevalence in the current study was recorded in the Black Sea Region (11.46%), suggesting the influence of regional risk factors. Statistically significant differences among regions (P < 0.001) point to the roles of environmental factors, flock management practices, and the distribution of definitive hosts such as dogs. While no statistically significant age-related difference was observed (P= 0.485), this may suggest either a lack of age-dependent susceptibility or continued exposure across age groups. Nevertheless, higher seroprevalence in rural settings supports the hypothesis that farm management systems and dog contact are key contributors to transmission. Additionally, our group previously reported a 50.4% seroprevalence in ewes with a history of abortion [10], further supporting the hypothesis that N. caninum contributes to reproductive losses and economic burden. These results underscore the importance of improving biosecurity measures and minimizing contact with potential infection sources to effectively manage and control the spread of neosporosis in sheep populations.

Serological analyses conducted in this nationwide study revealed that both T. gondii and N. caninum infections in sheep exhibit patterns of endemic instability in Türkiye. Endemic instability refers to a state in which infection occurs sporadically and at relatively low prevalence but maintains the potential to trigger periodic outbreaks due to a high proportion of susceptible individuals in the population [20, 21, 43]. This scenario typically emerges when natural exposure fails to induce adequate herd immunity, thereby complicating epidemiological control efforts [21, 43]. The 30.51% national seroprevalence of T. gondii indicates widespread infection, and the particularly high prevalence in the Black Sea Region (38.54%) may be associated with environmental factors [3, 6]. Although N. caninum shows a lower general prevalence of 5.21%, the higher rate observed in the Black Sea Region (11.46%) also underscores the importance of regional risk factors [9]. These findings collectively indicate a lack of stable endemicity, which increases vulnerability to outbreaks and exacerbates economic losses by preventing the establishment of protective flock-level immunity [22]. The absence of licensed vaccines against T. gondii and N. caninum in Türkiye complicates control efforts. In this regard, the development and application of effective immunoprophylactic tools, such as Toxovax®, which has demonstrated protective efficacy against T. gondii in other countries, could play a critical role in mitigating reproductive losses and enhancing herd immunity [24, 44]. The lack of a commercial vaccine for N. caninum is a significant gap, and accelerating vaccine development is of critical importance. However, the existing vaccine candidates have only been tested in cattle models, and their efficacy in sheep remains unknown [45]. In summary, the endemic instability of T. gondii and N. caninum infections in Türkiye highlights the need for vaccine development and effective preventive strategies to mitigate outbreak risks and establish herd immunity. Supporting future research in this direction is crucial for implementing sustainable and economically viable sheep farming practices in Türkiye.

In this study, co-infections with T. gondii and N. caninum were detected, revealing a complex epidemiological interaction within sheep populations across Türkiye. The co-infection prevalence was determined as 3.50%, emphasizing that both pathogens simultaneously circulate and present concurrent exposure risks. Given that both parasites can be transmitted transplacentally, this concurrent circulation, particularly during gestation, suggests a plausible contribution of vertical transmission to the observed seropositivity patterns [46]. Interestingly, our data indicated that a significant proportion (64.14%, 94/140) of N. caninum infections occurred as co-infections with T. gondii, while a smaller but noteworthy proportion (11.46%, 94/820) of T. gondii infections were accompanied by N. caninum. Similar trends were reported by Ceylan et al. [10], who found an even higher prevalence of co-infections in sheep with a history of abortion: 82.76% (48/58) of N. caninum infections and 63.16% (48/76) of T. gondii infections occurred as dual infections. Taken together with reports detecting parasite DNA/antigen in fetal/placental tissues, these patterns reinforce the need to explicitly consider congenital transmission in abortion investigations and flock-level risk models. These findings suggest that N. caninum tends to co-infect rather than appear as a mono-infection, potentially due to shared environmental exposure routes or common risk factors facilitating simultaneous infection by both parasites. From a control standpoint, peri-gestational biosecurity measures, restricting dog access to placental/fetal materials, minimizing feline contamination of feed and water, and monitoring serostatus in pregnant ewes, should be prioritized to interrupt vertical and horizontal transmission pathways [46, 47]. Previous researches have indicated possible immunological interactions and cross-reactivity between these pathogens, complicating accurate diagnosis and potentially exacerbating the severity of reproductive losses and associated economic impacts [11, 12]. Therefore, it is crucial to employ diagnostic methods with high specificity, such as recombinant antigen-based iELISA used in this study, to accurately differentiate between these pathogens. Further research into the immunopathological implications and economic consequences of co-infections is essential to inform comprehensive control strategies, mitigate reproductive losses, and improve overall flock health management.

Conclusion

This nationwide seroepidemiological study demonstrated that T. gondii infection is widespread among sheep across Türkiye, while N. caninum shows a more limited but regionally significant distribution. Statistically significant regional differences and age-associated increases in seroprevalence were observed for T. gondii, whereas N. caninum infections appeared more sporadic and less age-dependent. The observed endemic instability of both pathogens suggests that natural exposure is insufficient to confer effective herd immunity, leaving flocks vulnerable to periodic outbreaks. Notably, the high proportion of N. caninum cases occurring as co-infections with T. gondii indicates shared transmission routes, further complicating diagnosis and control. These findings highlight the importance of routine surveillance and the implementation of region-specific, risk-based control strategies. In the absence of licensed vaccines, improving biosecurity measures and limiting exposure to definitive hosts are currently the most viable approaches. Future research should focus on vaccine development and the immunopathological consequences of co-infections to reduce economic losses and improve reproductive performance in sheep flocks.
