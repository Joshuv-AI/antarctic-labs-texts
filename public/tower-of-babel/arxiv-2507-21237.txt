(2,2)-GB Codes: Classification and Comparison with weight-4 Surface Codes
François Arnault 1 , Philippe Gaborit 1 , Nicolas Saussay 1

Abstract
Generalized Bicycle (GB) codes offer a compelling alternative to surface codes for quantum error correction. This paper focuses on (2,2)-Generalized Bicycle codes, constructed from pairs of binary circulant matrices with two non-zero elements per row. Leveraging a lower bound on their minimum distance, we construct three novel infinite families of optimal (2,2)-GB codes with parameters [ [ 2 ​ n 2 , 2 , n ] ] [[2n^{2},2,n]] , [ [ 4 ​ r 2 , 2 , 2 ​ r ] ] [[4r^{2},2,2r]] , and [ [ ( 2 ​ t + 1 ) 2 + 1 , 2 , 2 ​ t + 1 ] ] [[(2t+1)^{2}+1,2,\ 2t+1]] . These families match the performance of Kitaev’s toric code and the best 2D weight-4 surface codes, reaching known theoretical limits. In particular, the second family breaks a long-held belief by providing optimal even-distance GB codes, previously deemed impossible. All are CSS codes derived from Cayley graphs. Recognizing that standard equivalence relations do not preserve their CSS structure, we introduce a CSS-preserving equivalence relation for rigorous comparison of Cayley graph-based CSS codes. Under this framework, the first two families are inequivalent to all previously known optimal weight-4 2D surface codes, while the third family is equivalent to the best-known odd-distance 2D surface code. Finally, we classify all extremal, non-equivalent ( 2 , 2 ) (2,2) -GB codes with length below 200 and present a comparison table with existing notable 2D weight-4 surface codes.



I
Introduction

I-A

Motivation

Quantum computers hold the promise of revolutionizing computation by efficiently solving problems that are intractable for standard computers, as demonstrated Shor’s algorithm
[
1
]
. However, the inherent fragility of quantum information (decoherence) and the presence of environmental noise make quantum computers susceptible to errors during computation. These errors accumulate over time, potentially leading to incorrect syndrome measurements and erroneous results.

To enable long-duration quantum computation, quantum error correction is essential.
This process involves employing quantum codes, syndrome measurement circuits, and decoding algorithms to detect and correct errors as they occur. As errors can accumulate even during syndrome measurement and decoding, the error correction process must be both fast and reliable. To ensure reliable quantum computation, the physical error rate must be kept below a critical threshold, allowing for effective error suppression
[
2
]
.

I-B

Previous results:

Kitaev codes, introduced in 2003, were the first quantum low-density parity-check (qLDPC) codes with minimum distance scaling linearly with the square root of the code length
[
3
]
. Since then, various generalizations of Kitaev codes have been developed in an effort to improve their parameters. Notable examples include Kitaev’s optimized 45-degree rotated codes
[
4
]
, which offer enhanced minimum distance, and surface codes, which provide higher rates.
However, neither of these generalizations has managed to surpass the square root scaling of the minimum distance.

Until recently, all known qLDPC codes were limited by a square root scaling of the minimum distance. However, in recent years, significant progress has been made, with the theoretical existence of asymptotically good qLDPC codes, possessing positive rate and linear minimum distance scaling, being established
[
5
,
6
]
.
While these codes offer superior theoretical performance, their practical implementation remains challenging compared to surface codes, which exhibit high error thresholds and efficient decoding algorithms.

I-C

Contributions

Currently, Generalized Bicycle (GB) codes, denoted as
(a,b)
-GB when constructed from circulant matrix pairs with
a
and
b
non-zero elements per row, stand out as one of the most promising alternatives to surface codes for practical implementation. A recent breakthrough by the IBM research team
[
7
]
highlighted their potential by introducing a family of (3,3)-GB codes that match the error thresholds of surface codes while significantly reducing encoding overhead.

Within this framework,
(2,2)
-GB codes form a particularly appealing subclass. As generalizations of Kitaev codes and their optimized variants
[
11
]
, they combine structural simplicity, planar layouts, and local weight-four stabilizers, features highly attractive for physical implementation. However, despite the identification of optimal odd-distance (2,2)-GB codes by Kovalev and Pryadko
[
11
]
, the literature has largely lacked (2,2)-GB codes with a minimum distance scaling linearly with the square root of the code length, especially for even distances.

Drawing inspiration from Haah, Hastings, and O’Donnell’s suggestion
[
8
]
that toric code geometry alterations could yield codes with improved minimum distance scaling, this paper introduces a novel approach for constructing high-performing (2,2)-GB codes.

Our approach blends graph theory and arithmetic, viewing these codes as CSS codes whose stabilizer generator matrices are incidence matrices of undirected Cayley graphs over abelian groups with two generators. In this interpretation,
(2,2)
-GB codes correspond to Cayley graphs of the form
(\mathbb{Z}/n\mathbb{Z},\overline{a},\overline{b})
.

Within this perspective, we derive a general lower bound on the minimum distance of these codes, based on the shortest Manhattan norm of non-zero vectors in associated
\mathbb{Z}^{2}
lattices. This bound proved to be an indispensable tool, enabling the explicit construction of three infinite families of optimal weight-4 2D surface codes:

•

GB
(1+X,1+X^{n},n^{2})
of parameters
[[2n^{2},2,n]]

•

GB
(1+X,1+X^{2r-1},2r^{2})
of parameters
[[4r^{2},2,2r]]

•

GB
(1+X,1+X^{2t+1},t^{2}+(t+1)^{2}\,)
of parameters
[[(2t+1)^{2}+1,2,2t+1]\!]

It is worth noting that family with parameters
[[(2t+1)^{2}+1,2,2t+1]\!]
was previously identified by Kovalev and Pryadko as (2,2)-GB codes, rivalling the best 2D surface codes for odd distances
[
11
]
. Their construction,
GB(1+X^{2t^{2}+1},X+X^{2t^{2}-1},t^{2}+(t+1)^{2})
, was also inspired by periodic lattices. Crucially, our construction of the
[[4r^{2},2,2r]]
family directly addresses a long-standing question: that no GB construction could yield optimal even-distance 2D codes with such parameters
[
13
]
. This is particularly significant as the best previously known GB family for even distances,
[[4r^{2}+4,2,2r]]
existed only for even
r
.

Beyond merely achieving good parameters, the practical utility of quantum codes hinges on their decoding properties and feasibility of implementation. To effectively categorize and study these codes, appropriate notions of equivalence are essential, much like identifying equivalent problems in complexity theory. Here, all codes under study are CSS codes derived from Cayley graphs. However, the standard equivalence relation on quantum error-correcting codes, where
\mathcal{Q}
and
\mathcal{Q}^{\prime}
are equivalent if
\mathcal{Q}^{\prime}=\mathbf{U}\mathcal{Q}
for
\mathbf{U}
being a unitary operator is ill-suited for these codes rigorous comparison.

This is because it fails to preserve either the CSS structure or the underlying graph structure, both of which are crucial given that established decoding algorithms for (2,2)-GB codes, such as the renormalization algorithm
[
14
]
, rely on these very properties.

To rigorously assess their novelty, we defined a tailored CSS code equivalence relation that preserves both the CSS and the underlying graph structures. Under this relation, we show that the first two families are inequivalent to any known Kitaev or even-distance surface codes (with initial simulations indicating distinct weight generator polynomials for their associated codes), while our third family offers an alternative realization of the optimal odd-distance 2D surface code.

While Pryadko and Wang had initiated a study on the performance of GB codes based on their weights, including
GB(1+X,1+X^{a},n)
with length
2n
where
n
is a integer for which
2
is a primitive root
[
15
]
, our work significantly advances prior research. This paper presents a complete study of all (2,2)-GB codes, providing a comprehensive comparison between our newly constructed (2,2)-GB codes and known local 2D surface codes built using periodic lattices.

Our study concludes with a complete classification of top-performing, non-equivalent (2,2)-GB codes with lengths under 200 and a comparison with notable 2D weight-4 surface codes. This culminating effort provides a rich dataset for future practical quantum code implementations, offering a valuable resource for identifying highly structured codes with optimal decoding properties.

I-D

High-Level Overview

This section details our approach to developing new, optimal (2,2)-Generalized Bicycle (GB) codes and definitively proving their structural distinctness from existing 2D weight-4 high-performing surface codes.

I-D
1
Construction

Our method for constructing (2,2)-GB codes harnesses the unique interplay between topological features of Cayley graphs with two generators and their geometric structures:

•

Graph Representation:
We interpret (2,2)-GB codes as quantum codes derived from unoriented Cayley graphs with two generators.

•

Lattice Association:
We define a corresponding 2D lattice, allowing us to map graph cycles to lattice elements.

•

Minimum Distance Guarantee:
We demonstrate that the code’s minimum distance is lower bounded by
\lambda(\mathbb{L})
, representing the shortest Manhattan norm of a non-zero vector in the associated lattice.

•

Targeted Design:
We identify specific GB codes whose associated lattices
\mathbb{L}
feature a large
\lambda(\mathbb{L})
that is also straightforward to compute. This strategy enables us to achieve desired minimum distances.

I-D
2
Comparison

To confirm the novelty of our newly constructed codes, we developed a robust comparison framework:

•

Unified Perspective:
We provide a unified view of our GB codes and 2D surface codes with periodicity vectors by interpreting both as CSS codes derived from unoriented Cayley graphs on abelian groups.

•

Specialized Equivalence Relation:
We establish a new, specific equivalence relation for CSS codes that preserves the structure of their underlying graphs.

•

Graph Isomorphism Link:
For the codes under investigation, we show that code equivalence directly implies isomorphism of their underlying graphs.

•

Group Isomorphism Implication:
We further prove that this graph isomorphism necessitates the isomorphism of their associated abelian groups.

•

Distinctness Proof:
By conclusively demonstrating that the underlying abelian groups of our novel codes are not isomorphic to those of established optimal 2D weight-4 surface codes (like Kitaev codes), we rigorously prove their distinctness.

II
Notations and relevant facts

Quantum error-correcting codes (QECCs) are defined as sub-vector spaces within the
n
-qubit complex vector space
(\mathbb{C}^{2})^{\otimes n}
.

II-A

Representing Qubits and Operators

We typically use the standard basis for a single qubit:

|0\rangle=\begin{pmatrix}1\\
0\end{pmatrix}
and
|1\rangle=\begin{pmatrix}0\\
1\end{pmatrix}
.
For an
n
-qubit system, where each
t_{i}\in\{0,1\}
, the computational basis vectors of the complex vector space
(\mathbb{C}^{2})^{\otimes n}
are commonly written as
|t_{1}\rangle\otimes\cdots\otimes|t_{n}\rangle
, or more simply as
|t_{1}\dots t_{n}\rangle
for conciseness.

Any unitary operator
\mathbf{U}
acting on an
n
-qubit state is expressed as a combination of Pauli operators:

i^{m}\mathbf{O}_{1}\otimes\mathbf{O}_{2}\otimes\dots\otimes\mathbf{O}_{n}

where
\mathbf{O}_{j}\in\{\mathbf{I},\mathbf{X},\mathbf{Y},\mathbf{Z}\}
are the identity and standard Pauli matrices, and
m\in\{0,1,2,3\}
accounts for a phase multiplier.

It is common practice to represent these Pauli operators using two binary strings,
\mathbf{u},\mathbf{v}\in\{0,1\}^{n}
, by mapping
\mathbf{U}=i^{m}\mathbf{X}^{\mathbf{u}}\mathbf{Z}^{\mathbf{v}}
to the pair
(\mathbf{u},\mathbf{v})
. Here, the operator
\mathbf{X}^{\mathbf{u}}
denotes
\mathbf{X}_{1}^{u_{1}}\otimes\dots\otimes\mathbf{X}_{n}^{u_{n}}
and
\mathbf{Z}^{\mathbf{v}}
denotes
\mathbf{Z}_{1}^{v_{1}}\otimes\dots\otimes\mathbf{Z}_{n}^{v_{n}}
. When two quantum operators are multiplied, their corresponding binary pairs
(\mathbf{u},\mathbf{v})
are simply added modulo 2.

II-B

Stabilizer Codes

An
[[n,k,d]]
stabilizer code
\mathcal{Q}
is a
2^{k}
-dimensional subspace of
(\mathbb{C}^{2})^{\otimes n}
defined by an Abelian stabilizer group
\mathbb{S}=\langle\mathbf{G}_{1},\dots,\mathbf{G}_{n-k}\rangle
, generated by
n-k
commuting Pauli operators, and that does not contain the negative identity operator (
-\mathbf{I}
). The associated code
\mathcal{Q}
consists of all states
|\psi\rangle
that remain unchanged when acted upon by any operator
S\in\mathbb{S}
:

\mathcal{Q}=\{|\psi\rangle\in(\mathbb{C}^{2})^{\otimes n}:\mathbf{S}|\psi\rangle=|\psi\rangle,\forall\mathbf{S}\in\mathbb{S}\}

Each stabilizer code can also be represented by a binary generator matrix
\mathbf{H}=(\mathbf{A}_{X}|\mathbf{A}_{Z})
. In this matrix, each row corresponds to a generator
\mathbf{G}_{i}
, where the
\mathbf{u}
vector forms a row in
\mathbf{A}_{X}
and the
\mathbf{v}
vector forms a row in
\mathbf{A}_{Z}
. For completeness,
\mathbf{H}
may include linearly dependent rows. The crucial condition for the stabilizer generators to commute is expressed in terms of these binary matrices as:

\mathbf{A}_{X}\mathbf{A}_{Z}^{\intercal}+\mathbf{A}_{Z}\mathbf{A}_{X}^{\intercal}=0\pmod{2}

II-C

CSS codes

Calderbank-Shor-Steane (CSS) codes
[
9
,
10
]
are a crucial subclass of stabilizer codes.
They are characterized by stabilizer generators that are products of only
\mathbf{X}
or only
\mathbf{Z}
operators.
This structure allows them to be represented by a generator matrix:

\mathbf{H}=\begin{pmatrix}\mathbf{H}_{X}&0\\
0&\mathbf{H}_{Z}\end{pmatrix}

where
\mathbf{H}_{X}
and
\mathbf{H}_{Z}
are two binary matrices whose row spaces are orthogonal, i.e.,
\mathbf{H}_{X}\mathbf{H}_{Z}^{\intercal}=\mathbf{0}
.

The code length
n
, representing the number of physical qubits, is the number of columns of
\mathbf{H}_{X}
(or of
\mathbf{H}_{Z}
). The code dimension
k
, corresponding to the number of logical qubits, is given by
k=n-\mathrm{rank}\,\mathbf{H}_{X}-\mathrm{rank}\,\mathbf{H}_{Z}
. The minimum distance
d
of the code is defined as the minimum of
d_{X}
and
d_{Z}
, where
d_{X}
(resp.
d_{Z}
) denotes the smallest Hamming weight of a codeword of
\ker\mathbf{H}_{X}
(resp.
\ker\mathbf{H}_{Z}
) that is not in
rs(\mathbf{H}_{Z})
, the row space of
\mathbf{H}_{Z}
(resp. not in
rs(\mathbf{H}_{X}
) the row space of
\mathbf{H}_{X}
).

A key property of the graph

F

Remark II.5
.

Different orderings of the vertices, edges and faces of the graph

Introduction to GB codes

II-F
1
Circulant matrices

For an integer
n\geq 1
and a polynomial
A(X)=\sum_{i=0}^{n-1}a_{i}X^{i}\in\mathbb{F}_{2}[X]_{\leq n-1}
, the circulant matrix
Circ(A(X),\,n)
is given by:

Circ(A(X),\,n)=\begin{pmatrix}a_{0}&a_{n-1}&\dots&a_{1}\\
a_{1}&a_{0}&\ddots&\vdots\\
\vdots&\ddots&\ddots&a_{n-1}\\
a_{n-1}&\dots&a_{1}&a_{0}\end{pmatrix}.

The algebra of
n\times n
circulant matrices
over
\mathbb{F}_{2}
is isomorphic to that of polynomials
in
\mathbb{F}_{2}[X]/(X^{n}-1)
. This isomorphism arises because any circulant matrix can be expressed as a polynomial in the companion matrix of
X
. A direct consequence is that the product of any two circulant matrices is commutative.

Throughout this article, we consider vectors in
\mathbb{F}_{2}^{n}

as columns. Under this convention, the
product
Circ(A(X),n)\mathbf{b}
, where
\mathbf{b}=\begin{pmatrix}b_{0}\\
\vdots\\
b_{n-1}\end{pmatrix}
, directly
corresponds to the polynomial product:

(\sum_{i=0}^{n-1}a_{i}X^{i})\cdot(\sum_{i=0}^{n-1}b_{i}X^{i})\mod X^{n}-1

II-F
2
Generalized bicycle (GB) codes

The concept of generalized bicycle (GB) codes, introduced by Pryadko and Kovalev in 2013
[
11
]
, extends the Bicycle Codes previously studied by MacKay et al.
[
12
]
. While traditional Bicycle codes are defined by a single circulant matrix
\mathbf{A}
in their stabilizer generator matrices, specifically
\mathbf{H}_{X}=\mathbf{H}_{Z}=[\mathbf{A},\mathbf{A}^{\intercal}]
, GB codes utilize two distinct circulant matrices.

For binary polynomials
A(X),B(X)\in\mathbb{F}_{2}[X]_{\leq n-1}
, the GB code
GB(A(X),B(X),n)
is constructed with stabilizer generator matrices
\mathbf{H}_{X}=[\mathbf{A},\mathbf{B}]
and
\mathbf{H}_{Z}=[\mathbf{B}^{\intercal},\mathbf{A}^{\intercal}]
, where
\mathbf{A}=Circ(A(X),n)
and
\mathbf{B}=Circ(B(X),n)
. The code has a length of
2n
and a dimension given by
2\deg(\gcd(A(X),B(X),X^{n}-1))
. Since
\mathbf{H}_{Z}
can be obtained from
\mathbf{H}_{X}
through row and column permutations, the minimum distance of the code satisfies
d=d_{X}=d_{Z}

[
13
]
.

The isomorphic relationship between circulant matrices and polynomials over
\mathbb{F}_{2}[X]/(X^{n}-1)
allows us to express the code conditions of
GB(A(X),B(X),n)
in the polynomial domain. For a column vector
\mathbf{c}=\begin{pmatrix}u\\
v\end{pmatrix}\in\mathbb{F}_{2}^{2n}
with
U(X)
and
V(X)\in\mathbb{F}_{2}[X]_{\leq n-1}
the polynomials associated with
\mathbf{u}
and
\mathbf{v}
, respectively, the following holds true:

•

\mathbf{c}
belongs to the kernel of
\mathbf{H}_{X}
if and only if its associated polynomials satisfy the equality

A(X)U(X)+B(X)V(X)\equiv 0\mod X^{n}-1

•

\mathbf{c}
belongs to the row space of
\mathbf{H}_{Z}
if and only if there exists a polynomial
H(X)\in\mathbb{F}_{2}[X]
such that:

\left\{\begin{aligned} U(X)&\equiv B(X)H(X)&\mod X^{n}-1\\
V(X)&\equiv A(X)H(X)&\mod X^{n}-1\end{aligned}\right.

In this work, we aim to maximize the minimum distance of (2,2)-GB codes generated by two binary polynomials
A(X)
and
B(X)
with exactly two non-zero terms.

As the next proposition summarizes, in our study of large-distance (2,2)-GB codes, we only need to investigate cases where
A(X),B(X)\in\mathbb{F}_{2}[X]_{\leq n-1}
have non-zero constant terms.

Proposition II.6
.

Let
n>0
and
A(X),B(X)\in\mathbb{F}_{2}\left[X\right]_{\leq n-1}
be two polynomials.

•

If
k
is an integer coprime with
n
, then
GB(A(X),B(X),n)
and

GB(A(X^{k})\mod X^{n}-1,B(X^{k})\mod X^{n}-1,n)
have the same parameters
[
13
]
. In particular, for distinct integers
r,s\geq 1
, if
r
is coprime with
n
, then

d(GB(1+X^{r},1+X^{s},\,n))=d(GB(1+X,1+X^{\alpha},\,n))

where
\alpha=st\mod n
with
t=(r\mod\,n)^{-1}
.

•

For any integers
i,j\in\mathbb{N}
the codes
GB(A(X),B(X),n)
and

GB(A(X)X^{i}\mod X^{n}-1,B(X)X^{j}\mod X^{n}-1,n)
have the same parameters.

Let

and

\mathbf{A}=Circ(A(X),n),\,\mathbf{B}=Circ(B(X),n)
and
\mathbf{C}=Circ(X,n)
. The map

\displaystyle h:\mathbb{F}_{2}^{2n}

\displaystyle\to\mathbb{F}_{2}^{2n}

\displaystyle\begin{pmatrix}\mathbf{u}\\

II-G

Study of (2,2)-GB code

Let
A(X)=1+X^{a}\in\mathbb{F}_{2}[X]_{\leq n-1}
and
B(X)=1+X^{b}\in\mathbb{F}_{2}[X]_{\leq n-1}
be two polynomials over
\mathbb{F}_{2}
. We define their associated circulant matrices as
\mathbf{A}=Circ(A(X),n)
and
\mathbf{B}=Circ(B(X),n)
.

II-G
1
Abstract graph associated with
GB(A(X),B(X),n)

The stabilizer generator matrices of
GB(A(X),B(X),n)
can be directly interpreted in the context of the graph

(\mathbb{Z}/n\mathbb{Z},\overline{a},\,\overline{b})
(as defined in Definition
II.1
).

Here, the matrix
\mathbf{H}_{X}=\left[\mathbf{A},\,\mathbf{B}\right]
corresponds to the vertex-edge incidence matrix of the graph, while the matrix
\mathbf{H}_{Z}=\left[\mathbf{B}^{\intercal},\,\mathbf{A}^{\intercal}\right]
corresponds to the face-edge incidence matrix of the graph. From this perspective,
GB(A(X),B(X),n)
is precisely, the CSS code
CSS(\mathbb{Z}/n\mathbb{Z},\overline{a},\,\overline{b})
.

While this abstract perspective will be crucial for distinguishing equivalent codes, representing
GB(A(X),B(X),n)
on concrete 2D surface lattice proved vital in the research of (2,2)-GB codes that outperform Kitaev codes. In the following sections, we explain how to build such representation.

II-G
2
Lattice associated with
GB(A(X),B(X),n)

The 2D lattice associated with
GB(1+X^{a},1+X^{b},n)
is given by the kernel of the
\mathbb{Z}
-linear application

\begin{array}[]{ccccl}\Phi&:&\mathbb{Z}^{2}&\to&\mathbb{Z}/n\mathbb{Z}\\
&&(u,v)&\mapsto&au+bv\mod n\end{array}

In the special case where
A(X)=1+X
and
B(X)=1+X^{\alpha}
, the corresponding lattice is
\mathbb{Z}\begin{pmatrix}n\\
0\end{pmatrix}\bigoplus\mathbb{Z}\begin{pmatrix}\alpha\\
-1\end{pmatrix}
. With this mapping, the code
GB(A(X),B(X),n)
can thus be interpreted as a 2D surface code defined on a torus with periodicity vectors
\begin{pmatrix}n\\
0\end{pmatrix}
and
\begin{pmatrix}\alpha\\
-1\end{pmatrix}
.

This next proposition shows that such code’s minimum distance is at least the smallest Manhattan norm of any non-zero vector in its corresponding lattice.

II-G
3

and

d_{min}\geq\lambda(\mathbb{L})=\min\{\,||\mathbf{u}||_{1}\,\,|\,\,\mathbf{u}\in\mathbb{L}\setminus\{0\}\}

where

0\end{pmatrix}\bigoplus\mathbb{Z}\begin{pmatrix}\alpha\\
-1\end{pmatrix}
is a
\mathbb{Z}^{2}

This connection can be generalized to a broader class of GB codes, as shown in the following corollary.

Let

•

Define

Let

Proposition III.1
.

Let
\mathcal{C}\,:\,C_{0}\xrightarrow{}\dots\xrightarrow{}C_{r}
be a walk in
(\mathbb{Z}/n\mathbb{Z},\,\,\overline{1},\,\,\overline{\alpha})

\gamma_{\mathcal{C}}

\mathcal{C}

The coordinates of
\mathbf{P}_{r}
are given by:

\mathbf{P}_{r}=\begin{pmatrix}n_{\overline{1}}(\mathcal{C})-n_{-\overline{1}}(\mathcal{C})\\
n_{\overline{\alpha}}(\mathcal{C})-n_{-\overline{\alpha}}(\mathcal{C})\end{pmatrix}
where for

\epsilon

\mathbb{L}=\mathbb{Z}\begin{pmatrix}n\\
0\end{pmatrix}\bigoplus\mathbb{Z}\begin{pmatrix}\alpha\\
-1\end{pmatrix}

Proof:
Recall that
\mathbb{L}=\mathbb{Z}\begin{pmatrix}n\\
0\end{pmatrix}\bigoplus\mathbb{Z}\begin{pmatrix}\alpha\\
-1\end{pmatrix}
consists of all vectors
\begin{pmatrix}x\\
y\end{pmatrix}\in\mathbb{Z}^{2}
satisfying
x+\alpha y\equiv 0[n]
.

The endpoint
\mathbf{P}_{r}=\begin{pmatrix}n_{\overline{1}}(\mathcal{C})-n_{-\overline{1}}(\mathcal{C})\\
n_{\overline{\alpha}}(\mathcal{C})-n_{-\overline{\alpha}}(\mathcal{C})\end{pmatrix}
of the associated
\mathbb{Z}^{2}

has a Manhattan norm

If
\mathcal{C}:\,C_{0}\xrightarrow{}\dots\xrightarrow{}C_{r}=C_{0}

IV
Application of Theorem
II.7

In quantum coding theory, a core objective is to design quantum codes with the largest possible minimum distance, as this directly enhances fault tolerance. For (2,2)-GB codes, their minimum distance is fundamentally constrained to scale with the square root of the code length.

While lattice-based toric codes have historically represented the best known 2D surface codes with weight-four generators in terms of minimum distance (see Section
II-E2
), our work makes a significant leap. We leverage Theorem
II.7
, demonstrating that constructing GB codes with large minimum distances effectively reduces to identifying associated lattices whose non-zero vectors possess large Manhattan norms.This insight allows us to construct explicit families of GB codes that attain optimal parameters, matching the performance of the best-known 2D weight-4 surface codes
[
4
]
. Our method hinges on carefully choosing lattices where we can control the invariant
\lambda(\mathbb{L})
, which directly bounds the minimum distance.

While Kovalev and Pryadko
[
11
]
demonstrated how to construct optimal odd-distance surface codes using the GB formalism (e.g.,
GB(1+X^{2t^{2}+1},X+X^{2t^{2}},t^{2}+(t+1)^{2})
with parameters
[(2t+1)^{2}+1,2,2t+1]
), we introduce an alternative family that achieves the same optimal odd-distance scaling through a distinct construction.

More notably, Pryadko and Wang
[
13
]
claimed that optimal even-distance 2D surface codes—characterized by periodicity vectors
\begin{pmatrix}r\\
r\end{pmatrix},\begin{pmatrix}r\\
-r\end{pmatrix}
and parameters
[\![4r^{2},2,2r]]
—could not be realized using GB codes. According to their results, the next closest GB-compatible constructions only reached parameters
[\![4r^{2}+4,2,2r]]
for even values of

. Contrary to that belief, our approach yields a new family of (2,2)-GB codes that meets these optimal even-distance parameters, providing the first known GB realization of such codes.

These constructions are summarized in the following proposition:

V
Equivalence Relations for Quantum Codes

In the previous section, we introduced three families of Generalized Bicycle (GB) codes that achieve optimal performance, matching the best known 2D toric codes with weight-four generators. Now, to rigorously establish the originality of our contribution, it is essential to determine whether these newly constructed codes represent genuinely new structures or are simply equivalent to known optimal codes.

To address this, we first delve into the concept of equivalent quantum codes. In quantum coding theory, codes are generally deemed "the same" if they are interconvertible via transformations that preserve their essential error correction capabilities (such as error detection, correction, and decoding properties). However, as will be demonstrated, various types of equivalence relations exist, each possessing distinct behaviours and implications.

V-A

General Quantum Equivalence relation

V-A
1
Definition

Two quantum codes,
\mathcal{Q}_{1}
and
\mathcal{Q}_{2}
, are equivalent if one can be transformed into the other by a unitary operation
\mathbf{U}
, such that
\mathcal{Q}_{2}=\mathbf{U}\mathcal{Q}_{1}
. For stabilizer codes, this
\mathbf{U}
must be a sequence of qubit permutations and local Clifford transformations which are defined as tensor products of single-qubit Clifford gates, meaning
\mathbf{U}=\mathbf{U}_{1}\otimes\dots\otimes\mathbf{U}_{n}
, where each
\mathbf{U}_{i}
is composed of Hadamard, phase, and CNOT gates.

V-A
2
Limitations

When a code
\mathcal{Q}_{1}
is stabilized by a set of operators
\mathcal{S}
, the equivalent code
\mathcal{Q}_{2}
is stabilized by the transformed set
\mathbf{U}\mathcal{S}\mathbf{U}^{\dagger}
. However, this general equivalence relation does not always preserve the specific structure of CSS codes. This is because local Clifford gates like the Hadamard (
\mathbf{H}
) transform Pauli
\mathbf{X}
operators into
\mathbf{Z}
operators (
\mathbf{H}\mathbf{X}\mathbf{H}^{\dagger}=\mathbf{Z}
) and
\mathbf{Z}
operators into
\mathbf{X}
operators (
\mathbf{H}\mathbf{Z}\mathbf{H}^{\dagger}=\mathbf{X}
). Similarly, the Phase (
\mathbf{S}
) gate transforms
\mathbf{X}
into
\mathbf{Y}
(
\mathbf{S}\mathbf{X}\mathbf{S}^{\dagger}=\mathbf{Y}=i\mathbf{X}\mathbf{Z}
). Since CSS codes require their stabilizer generators to be composed
only
of
\mathbf{X}
or
\mathbf{Z}
operators (and identity), these transformations can change the fundamental form of the generators, thus breaking the CSS structure.

To compare the GB optimal codes (identified in Section
IV
) with the optimal weight-4 2D surface codes, which are both derived from Cayley graphs on abelian groups, our focus will be on equivalence relations that preserve both their CSS structure and their underlying graph structure.

V-B

CSS Graph-preserving (CGP) Equivalence

V-B
1
Definition:

Let
\mathcal{Q}
and
\mathcal{Q}^{\prime}
be two quantum codes of length
n
. We say that
\mathcal{Q}
is CGP-equivalent to
\mathcal{Q}^{\prime}
, denoted
\mathcal{Q}\sim\mathcal{Q}^{\prime}
, if and only if there exists a permutation
\phi
of the qubit indices
\{1,\dots,n\}
such that
\mathcal{Q}^{\prime}=\mathbf{P}_{\phi}(\mathcal{Q})
where
\mathbf{P}_{\phi}\in GL_{2^{n}}(\mathbb{C})
is a permutation matrix acting on basis states as follows:

\mathbf{P}_{\phi}|t_{1}\dots t_{n}\rangle=|t_{\phi(1)}\dots t_{\phi(n)}\rangle
for any computational basis state
|t_{1}\dots t_{n}\rangle
.
This implies that
\mathcal{Q}^{\prime}
consists of all states formed by permuting the qubits of states in
\mathcal{Q}
:

\mathcal{Q}^{\prime}=\left\{\sum_{t\in\mathbb{F}_{2}^{n}}\alpha_{t}|t_{\phi(1)}\dots t_{\phi(n)}\rangle\quad\middle|\quad\sum_{t\in\mathbb{F}_{2}^{n}}\alpha_{t}|t_{1}\dots t_{n}\rangle\in\mathcal{Q}\right\}

V-B
2
CSS Structure Compatibility:

Two CGP-equivalent stabilizer codes,
\mathcal{Q}
and
\mathcal{Q}^{\prime}
(i.e.,
\mathcal{Q}^{\prime}=\mathbf{P}_{\phi}(\mathcal{Q})
), have the same parameters and their stabilizer groups are directly related. A Pauli operator
\mathbf{O}_{1}\otimes\dots\otimes\mathbf{O}_{n}
stabilizes
\mathcal{Q}
if and only if the permuted operator
\mathbf{O}_{\phi(1)}\otimes\dots\otimes\mathbf{O}_{\phi(n)}
stabilizes
\mathcal{Q}^{\prime}
. Crucially, this implies that the CSS code structure is preserved under such equivalence:
\mathcal{Q}
is a CSS code if and only if
\mathcal{Q}^{\prime}
is also a CSS code.

V-B
3
Relation between Stabilizer Generator Matrices

For two stabilizer codes
\mathcal{Q}
and
\mathcal{Q}^{\prime}
that are CGP-equivalent (i.e.,
\mathcal{Q}^{\prime}=\mathbf{P}_{\phi}(\mathcal{Q})
), their respective generator matrices,
\mathbf{H}=\begin{pmatrix}\mathbf{H}_{X}&0\\
0&\mathbf{H}_{Z}\end{pmatrix}
and
\mathbf{H}^{\prime}=\begin{pmatrix}\mathbf{H}_{X}^{\prime}&0\\
0&\mathbf{H}_{Z}^{\prime}\end{pmatrix}
, are directly related. The row spaces of their submatrices are connected by the following equalities:

\left\{\begin{aligned} rs(\mathbf{H}_{X}^{\prime})=rs(\mathbf{H}_{X}\mathbf{Q})\\
rs(\mathbf{H}_{Z}^{\prime})=rs(\mathbf{H}_{Z}\mathbf{Q})\end{aligned}\right.

where
\mathbf{Q}
denote the
n\times n
permutation matrix corresponding to
\phi
.

V-B
4
Graph Structure Compatibility

For the class of codes under consideration—where the generator submatrices
\mathbf{H}_{X},\mathbf{H}_{X}^{\prime}
and
\mathbf{H}_{Z},\mathbf{H}_{Z}^{\prime}
correspond respectively to the vertex-edge incidence and face-edge incidence (i.e., dual vertex-edge incidence) matrices of Cayley graphs—the conditions
rs(\mathbf{H}_{X}^{\prime})=rs(\mathbf{H}_{X}\mathbf{Q})
and
rs(\mathbf{H}_{Z}^{\prime})=rs(\mathbf{H}_{Z}\mathbf{Q})
play a central role.

These constraints induced by the CGP-equivalence between codes, guarantee a bijective correspondence between the edges of the underlying graphs, as well as their duals. Consequently, cycles in one graph (or its dual) are mapped directly to cycles in the other, a property known in graph theory as 2-isomorphism.

While more restrictive than general stabilizer code equivalence, the CGP-equivalence enables a comparison between codes that faithfully preserves the graph-theoretic structure at the heart of their construction.

As we will show in the next section, in the specific case of the GB and optimal weight-4 surface codes examined here, having 2-isomorphism of the associated graphs is, in fact, equivalent to full graph isomorphism. This means the incidence relations between edges are fully preserved, providing an even stronger form of structural equivalence.

Remark V.1
.

Consider two CGP-equivalent CSS codes,
\mathcal{Q}
and
\mathcal{Q}^{\prime}
, based on Cayley graphs (
\mathcal{G}
and
\mathcal{G}^{\prime}
), whose stabilizer generator matrices satisfy
rs(\mathbf{H}_{X}^{\prime})=rs(\mathbf{H}_{X}\mathbf{Q})
and
rs(\mathbf{H}_{Z}^{\prime})=rs(\mathbf{H}_{Z}\mathbf{Q})
where
\mathbf{Q}
is the permutation matrix.

This directly implies a one-to-one correspondence, induced by
\mathbf{Q}
, between the edges of
\mathcal{G}
and
\mathcal{G}^{\prime}
. As a result, every simple cycle in
\mathcal{G}
maps directly to an equivalent
simple cycle
in
\mathcal{G}^{\prime}
through this correspondence.

Proof of Remarks
V.1
:
We can represent the power set of edges for both graph
\mathcal{G}
and
\mathcal{G}^{\prime}
as
\mathbb{F}_{2}^{N}
. The mapping from
\mathbf{v}\in\mathbb{F}_{2}^{N}
to
\mathbf{Q}^{-1}\mathbf{v}\in\mathbb{F}_{2}^{N}
is a bijective transformation. This transformation establishes a one-to-one correspondence between edge subsets of
\mathcal{G}
and
\mathcal{G}^{\prime}
, ensuring that any simple cycle in
\mathcal{G}
maps to a simple cycle in
\mathcal{G}^{\prime}
.

Consider a simple cycle
\mathcal{C}_{\mathbf{v}}
in the graph
\mathcal{G}
. Since,
\mathbf{H}_{X}
is a vertex-edge incidence matrix of
\mathcal{G}
, this means that, the cycle
\mathcal{C}_{\mathbf{v}}
corresponds to a vector
\mathbf{v}\in\ker(\mathbf{H}_{X})
. Applying the transformation, we find that
\mathbf{Q}^{-1}\mathbf{v}\in\ker(\mathbf{H}_{X}\mathbf{Q})
. Because
rs(\mathbf{H}_{X}\mathbf{Q})^{\perp}=rs(\mathbf{H}_{X}^{\prime})^{\perp}=\ker(\mathbf{H}_{X}^{\prime})
, it follows that
\mathbf{Q}^{-1}\mathbf{v}\in\ker(\mathbf{H}_{X}^{\prime})
. This directly means that
\mathcal{C}^{\prime}_{\mathbf{Q}^{-1}\mathbf{v}}
, the corresponding edge subset in
\mathcal{G}^{\prime}
, is indeed a cycle within
\mathcal{G}^{\prime}
.

Now, let’s assume for contradiction that
\mathcal{C}^{\prime}_{\mathbf{Q}^{-1}\mathbf{v}}
is not a simple cycle in
\mathcal{G^{\prime}}
. This implies there must exist a proper sub-cycle
\emptyset\subsetneq\mathcal{C}^{\prime}_{\mathbf{h}}\subsetneq\mathcal{C}_{\mathbf{Q}^{-1}\mathbf{v}}
in
\mathcal{G}^{\prime}
. This sub-cycle is represented by a non-zero vector
\mathbf{h}=\mathbf{Q}^{-1}\mathbf{w}\in\ker(\mathbf{H}_{X}^{\prime})=\ker(\mathbf{H}_{X}\mathbf{Q})
, where the support of
\mathbf{h}
is a proper subset of the support of
\mathbf{Q}^{-1}\mathbf{v}
. Since
\mathbf{Q}
is a permutation matrix, this implies that
\mathbf{w}\in\ker(\mathbf{H}_{X})
and its support is a proper subset of the support of
\mathbf{v}
. Consequently, the cycle
\mathcal{C}_{\mathbf{w}}
in graph
\mathcal{G}
corresponding to
\mathbf{w}
, is a proper subset of
\mathcal{C}_{\mathbf{v}}
, contradicting the simplicity of
\mathcal{C}_{\mathbf{v}}
. ∎.

VI
Comparing GB codes to notable 2D Surface Codes

Both our novel Generalized Bicycle (GB) codes introduced in Section
IV
and the 2D surface codes detailed in Section
II-E2
are founded upon degree-4 regular Cayley graphs that exhibit strong connectivity. This shared structural basis allows for a rigorous comparison between them.

A core contribution of our work is proving the distinctness of our two new GB code families from Kitaev toric codes and optimal 2D weight-4 surface codes. This proof hinges on a critical graph-theoretic insight: for graphs possessing the specific structure of those underlying our codes, a 2-isomorphism (which preserves cycles) implies a full graph isomorphism (preserving incidence relations).

Definition VI.1

(Graphs isomorphism)
.

Two graphs
\mathcal{G}
and
\mathcal{H}
are isomorphic if and only if there is a bijective map
\tau:\mathcal{G}\to\mathcal{H}
such that:
u
and
v
are neighbours in
\mathcal{G}
if and only if
\tau(u)
and
\tau(v)
are neighbours in
\mathcal{H}
.

Precisely, our argument leverages the following established graph-theoretic results, particularly Whitney’s Theorem.
[
16
]

VI-A

Whitney’s Theorem

Definition VI.2

(3-connectedness)
.

A graph
\mathcal{G}
is triply-connected if it is connected and removing any two distinct
(u,v)
vertices, leaves the remaining graph
\mathcal{G}-\{u,v\}
still connected.

Theorem VI.3

(Whitney’s Theorem
[
16
]
)
.

Let
\mathcal{G}_{1},\,\mathcal{G}_{2}
be two triply connected graphs. If there is a one-to-one correspondence between their edges such that simple cycles in
\mathcal{G}_{1}
corresponds to simple cycles in
\mathcal{G}_{2}
then
\mathcal{G}_{1}
and
\mathcal{G}_{2}
are isomorphic.

VI-B

Overview of the Distinctness Proof

Our proof strategy is to reduce the question of code CGP-equivalence to the existence of group isomorphisms, for which we already know the answer.

Both proofs demonstrating the distinctness between optimal 2D surface codes and our Generalized Bicycle codes will follow a consistent pattern. Since these are CSS codes derived from unoriented Cayley graphs (
\mathcal{G}
and
\mathcal{G}^{\prime}
) based on abelian groups (
\mathbb{G}
and
\mathbb{G}^{\prime}
), we will proceed as follows:

1.

First, we will establish that the underlying graphs,
\mathcal{G}
and
\mathcal{G}^{\prime}
, are triply-connected.

2.

Next, we will prove by contradiction that the codes are not CGP-equivalent. If we assume they are CGP-equivalent, their associated graphs,
\mathcal{G}
and
\mathcal{G}^{\prime}
, must be 2-isomorphic. By Whitney’s Theorem, this 2-isomorphism would then imply that the graphs themselves are isomorphic in the traditional sense.

3.

Finally, we will show that this graph isomorphism must induce a group isomorphism between their associated groups
\mathbb{G}
and
\mathbb{G}^{\prime}
. However, these groups will not be isomorphic, thus leading to a contradiction and proving the distinctness of the codes.

(\mathbb{Z}^{2}/\mathbb{L},\begin{pmatrix}1\\

\psi
is a group morphism

Finally, we will show that for specific orderings on the (edges, vertices and faces) the vertex-edge incidence matrices
\mathbf{H}_{X},\mathbf{H}_{X}^{\prime}
and face-edge incidence matrices
\mathbf{H}_{Z},\mathbf{H}_{Z}^{\prime}
of the two graphs satisfy the relations:

\left\{\begin{aligned} \mathbf{H}_{X}^{\prime}&=\mathbf{H}_{X}\\
rs(\mathbf{H}_{Z}^{\prime})&=rs(\mathbf{H}_{Z})\end{aligned}\right.

from which the code CGP-equivalence, and thus the equivalence will follow.

Step 1: Constructing the group isomorphism

Let
\eta
be the map defined as:

\begin{array}[]{ccccl}\eta&:&\mathbb{Z}/\left(t^{2}+(t+1)^{2}\right)\mathbb{Z}&\xrightarrow{}&\mathbb{Z}^{2}/\mathbb{L}\\
&&T\mod(t^{2}+(t+1)^{2})&\longmapsto&T\begin{pmatrix}1\\
0\end{pmatrix}\mod\mathbb{L}\end{array}

\eta
is a group isomorphism that sends
\overline{1}\mapsto\begin{pmatrix}1\\
0\end{pmatrix}
and
\overline{2t+1}\mapsto-\begin{pmatrix}0\\
1\end{pmatrix}\mod\mathbb{L}

\eta
is well-defined:
Let
k,q\in\mathbb{Z}
be two integers such that
\overline{k}=\overline{q}
in
\mathbb{Z}/\left(t^{2}+(t+1)^{2}\right)\mathbb{Z}
. By hypothesis,
\exists\lambda\in\mathbb{Z},\,\,k-q=\lambda(t^{2}+(t+1)^{2})
. Thus:

k\begin{pmatrix}1\\
0\end{pmatrix}-q\begin{pmatrix}1\\
0\end{pmatrix}=\lambda\left((t+1)\begin{pmatrix}t+1\\
-t\end{pmatrix}+t\begin{pmatrix}t\\
t+1\end{pmatrix}\right)

So
k\begin{pmatrix}1\\
0\end{pmatrix}-q\begin{pmatrix}1\\
0\end{pmatrix}\in\mathbb{L}=\mathbb{Z}\begin{pmatrix}t\\
t+1\end{pmatrix}\bigoplus\mathbb{Z}\begin{pmatrix}t+1\\
-t\end{pmatrix}
.
Thus,
\eta(\overline{k})=\eta(\overline{q})
.

Moreover,
\eta
is group homomorphism, sending
\overline{1}
to
\begin{pmatrix}1\\
0\end{pmatrix}\mod\mathbb{L}
and
\overline{2t+1}
to
-\begin{pmatrix}0\\
1\end{pmatrix}\mod\mathbb{L}
.

\eta
is bijective:
Since
\mathbb{L}
is a sub-lattice of
\mathbb{Z}^{2}
, the order of
\mathbb{Z}^{2}/\mathbb{L}
is given by:

|\mathbb{Z}^{2}/\mathbb{L}|=det\begin{pmatrix}t+1&t\\
-t&t+1\end{pmatrix}=(t+1)^{2}+t^{2}

which corresponds to the order of
\mathbb{Z}/\left(t^{2}+(t+1)^{2}\right)\mathbb{Z}
.

Moreover,
\eta
is an injective map. Indeed, if
k\in\mathbb{Z}
is such that
\eta(\overline{k})=0_{\mathbb{Z}^{2}/\mathbb{L}}
, then:

k\begin{pmatrix}1\\
0\end{pmatrix}\in\mathbb{L}=\mathbb{Z}\begin{pmatrix}t\\
t+1\end{pmatrix}\bigoplus\mathbb{Z}\begin{pmatrix}t+1\\
-t\end{pmatrix}.

Therefore, there exist
\lambda,\mu\in\mathbb{Z}
such that
\,k\begin{pmatrix}1\\
0\end{pmatrix}=\lambda\begin{pmatrix}t\\
t+1\end{pmatrix}+\mu\begin{pmatrix}t+1\\
-t\end{pmatrix}
. This yield two integer equations:

k=\lambda t+\mu(t+1)\quad\text{and}\quad\mu t=\lambda(t+1)

Since,
t
and
t+1
are coprime it must be that
t
divides
\lambda
, and
t+1
divides
\mu
. Thus, there exists integers
p,q
such that
\lambda=pt
and
\mu=q(t+1)
.
Substituting these into the two previous equations:

k=pt^{2}+q(t+1)^{2}\quad\text{and}\quad qt(t+1)=pt(t+1)

Since
t\geq 1
then,
p=q
and thus
k=p\left(t^{2}+(t+1)^{2}\right)
. Thus, we have proved that
\eta(\overline{k})=0_{\mathbb{Z}^{2}/\mathbb{L}}
implies
\overline{k}=\overline{0}
in
\mathbb{Z}/\left(t^{2}+(t+1)^{2}\right)\mathbb{Z}
. Hence,
\eta
is injective.

In conclusion, since
\eta
is an injective homomorphism between groups of the same finite size, it is an isomorphism.

Step 2: Proving
\eta
is graph isomorphism:

We have shown that the map
\eta
defined by

\begin{array}[]{ccccl}\eta&:&\mathbb{Z}/\left(t^{2}+(t+1)^{2}\right)\mathbb{Z}&\xrightarrow{}&\mathbb{Z}^{2}/\mathbb{L}\\
&&\overline{T}&\longmapsto&T\begin{pmatrix}1\\
0\end{pmatrix}\mod\mathbb{L}\end{array}

is a group isomorphism sending
\overline{1}
to
\begin{pmatrix}1\\
0\end{pmatrix}\mod\mathbb{L}
and
\overline{2t+1}
to
-\begin{pmatrix}0\\
1\end{pmatrix}\mod\mathbb{L}
.

Two vertices
\overline{T}
and
\overline{S}
are neighbours in the graph
(\mathbb{Z}/\left(t^{2}+(t+1)^{2}\right)\mathbb{Z},\,\,\overline{1},\,\,\overline{2t+1})
if and only if
\overline{T}-\overline{S}\in\{\pm\overline{1},\,\,\pm\,\overline{2t+1}\}
, which due to the isomorphism
\eta
, is equivalent to:

\eta(\overline{T}-\overline{S})=\eta(\overline{T})-\eta(\overline{S})\in\{\pm\begin{pmatrix}1\\
0\end{pmatrix}\mod\mathbb{L},\pm\,\begin{pmatrix}0\\
1\end{pmatrix}\mod\mathbb{L}\}

Hence,
\eta
induces a graph isomorphism between the two graphs
(\mathbb{Z}/\left(t^{2}+(t+1)^{2}\right)\mathbb{Z},\,\,\overline{1},\,\,\overline{2t+1})
and
(\mathbb{Z}^{2}/\mathbb{L},\,\,\begin{pmatrix}1\\
0\end{pmatrix}\mod\mathbb{L},\,\,\begin{pmatrix}0\\
1\end{pmatrix}\mod\mathbb{L})
.

Step 3: Proving the codes CGP-equivalence

Proving the CGP-equivalence of these two codes necessitates demonstrating that their respective incidence matrices,
\mathbf{H}_{X},\mathbf{H}_{X}^{\prime}
and
\mathbf{H}_{Z},\mathbf{H}_{Z}^{\prime}
are related by the following expressions
rs(\mathbf{H}_{X}^{\prime})=rs(\mathbf{H}_{X}\mathbf{Q})
and
rs(\mathbf{H}_{Z}^{\prime})=rs(\mathbf{H}_{Z}\mathbf{Q})
where
\mathbf{Q}
represents a permutation matrix.

Specifically, we will demonstrate that for specific, carefully chosen orderings, the vertex-edge and face-edge incidence matrices of
\mathbb{Z}/\left(t^{2}+(t+1)^{2}\right)\mathbb{Z}
(namely
\mathbf{H}_{X}
and
\mathbf{H}_{Z}
) and
\mathbb{Z}^{2}/\mathbb{L}
(namely
\mathbf{H}_{X}^{\prime}
and
\mathbf{H}_{Z}^{\prime}
) can be made to satisfy the relations:

\left\{\begin{aligned} \mathbf{H}_{X}^{\prime}&=\mathbf{H}_{X}\\
rs(\mathbf{H}_{Z}^{\prime})&=rs(\mathbf{H}_{Z})\end{aligned}\right.

Step 3.1 Choosing the orderings:

Since
\eta
induces a graph isomorphism between the two graphs
(\mathbb{Z}/\left(t^{2}+(t+1)^{2}\right)\mathbb{Z},\,\,\overline{1},\,\,\overline{2t+1})
and
(\mathbb{Z}^{2}/\mathbb{L},\,\,\begin{pmatrix}1\\
0\end{pmatrix}\mod\mathbb{L},\begin{pmatrix}0\\
1\end{pmatrix}\mod\mathbb{L})
then these two graphs have the same number of edges and vertices.

For the vertices, we establish a parallel ordering. For each
i\in\left[[0,t^{2}+(t+1)^{2}-1|\right],
we define: the
i
-th vertex in
\mathbb{Z}/\left(t^{2}+(t+1)^{2}\right)\mathbb{Z}
as
S_{i}=\overline{i}
and the
i
-th vertex in
\mathbb{Z}^{2}/\mathbb{L}
as
T_{i}=\eta(\overline{i})
.

On the edges, we define the ordering such that: in
(\mathbb{Z}/\left(t^{2}+(t+1)^{2}\right)\mathbb{Z},\,\,\overline{1},\,\,\overline{2t+1})
the
k
-th edge is
\{\overline{x},\overline{y}\}
if and only if in
(\mathbb{Z}^{2}/\mathbb{L},\,\,\begin{pmatrix}1\\
0\end{pmatrix}\mod\mathbb{L},\begin{pmatrix}0\\
1\end{pmatrix}\mod\mathbb{L})
the
k
-th edge is
\{\eta(\overline{x}),\,\eta(\overline{y})\}
.

With this specific ordering, the vertex-edge incidence matrices of these two graphs are identical, i.e
\mathbf{H}_{X}^{\prime}=\mathbf{H}_{X}
.

Step 3.2 Proving
rs(\mathbf{H}_{Z}^{\prime})=rs(\mathbf{H}_{Z})
:

The rows of
\mathbf{H}_{Z}
are the characteristic vectors of the faces of
(\mathbb{Z}/\left(t^{2}+(t+1)^{2}\right)\mathbb{Z},\,\,\overline{1},\,\,\overline{2t+1})
, which are of the form:

\overline{T}\to\overline{T}+\overline{1}\to\overline{T}+\overline{1}+\overline{2t+1}\to\overline{T}+\overline{2t+1}\to\overline{T}

Similarly, the rows of
\mathbf{H}_{Z}^{\prime}
are the characteristic vectors of the faces of
(\mathbb{Z}^{2}/\mathbb{L},\,\,\begin{pmatrix}1\\
0\end{pmatrix}\mod\mathbb{L},\begin{pmatrix}0\\
1\end{pmatrix}\mod\mathbb{L})
which corresponds to 4-cycle of vertices within
\mathbb{Z}^{2}/\mathbb{L}
, of the form:

S\to S+\begin{pmatrix}1\\
0\end{pmatrix}\to S+\begin{pmatrix}1\\
1\end{pmatrix}\to S+\begin{pmatrix}0\\
1\end{pmatrix}\to S

We will now demonstrate that the rows of both matrices are the same. First, let’s start by proving that
\{\text{rows of }\mathbf{H}_{Z}\}\subseteq\{\text{rows of }\mathbf{H}_{Z}^{\prime}\}
.

Let
\mathbf{v}
be a row of
\mathbf{H}_{Z}
. By definition of
\mathbf{v}\in\mathbb{F}_{2}^{2(t^{2}+(t+1)^{2})}
,
\mathbf{v}
is the characteristic vector of a face of
(\mathbb{Z}/\left(t^{2}+(t+1)^{2}\right)\mathbb{Z},\,\,\overline{1},\,\,\overline{2t+1})
. Such a face
\mathcal{F}
is defined by vertices:

\overline{T}\to\overline{T}+\overline{1}\to\overline{T}+\overline{1}+\overline{2t+1}\to\overline{T}+\overline{2t+1}\to\overline{T}

By our chosen ordering,
\mathbf{v}
is also the characteristic vector of
\eta(\mathcal{F})
, the image of this face under the graph isomorphism
\eta
:
\eta(\overline{T})\to\eta(\overline{T+1})\to\eta(\overline{T+1+2t+1})\to\eta(\overline{T+2t+1})\to\eta(\overline{T})
.

Since
\forall q\in\mathbb{Z},\,\,\eta(\overline{q})=\begin{pmatrix}q\\
0\end{pmatrix}\mod\mathbb{L}
and
\begin{pmatrix}2t+1\\
0\end{pmatrix}\mod\mathbb{L}=-\begin{pmatrix}0\\
1\end{pmatrix}\mod\mathbb{L}
, then
\eta(\mathcal{F})
corresponds to the following cycle within
\mathbb{Z}^{2}/\mathbb{L}
:

\begin{pmatrix}T\\
0\end{pmatrix}\to\begin{pmatrix}T+1\\
0\end{pmatrix}\to\begin{pmatrix}T+1\\
-1\end{pmatrix}\to\begin{pmatrix}T\\
-1\end{pmatrix}\to\begin{pmatrix}T\\
0\end{pmatrix}

Since the graphs are unoriented, this corresponds to a valid face in
\mathbb{Z}^{2}/\mathbb{L}
:

\begin{pmatrix}T\\
-1\end{pmatrix}\to\begin{pmatrix}T+1\\
-1\end{pmatrix}\to\begin{pmatrix}T+1\\
0\end{pmatrix}\to\begin{pmatrix}T\\
0\end{pmatrix}\to\begin{pmatrix}T\\
-1\end{pmatrix}

Therefore,
\mathbf{v}
is the characteristic vector of a face of
\mathbb{Z}^{2}/\mathbb{L}
, which means
\mathbf{v}
is a row of
\mathbf{H}_{Z}^{\prime}
.

Now, let’s demonstrate that
\{\text{rows of }\mathbf{H}_{Z}^{\prime}\}\subseteq\{\text{rows of }\mathbf{H}_{Z}\}
.
Let
\mathbf{w}
be a row of
\mathbf{H}_{Z}^{\prime}
. By definition of
\mathbf{w}\in\mathbb{F}_{2}^{2(t^{2}+(t+1)^{2})}
,
\mathbf{w}
is the characteristic vector of a face of
(\mathbb{Z}^{2}/\mathbb{L},\,\,\begin{pmatrix}1\\
0\end{pmatrix}\mod\mathbb{L},\begin{pmatrix}0\\
1\end{pmatrix}\mod\mathbb{L})
. Such a face
\mathcal{F_{\mathbb{L}}}
is defined a sequence of vertices of
\mathbb{Z}^{2}/\mathbb{L}
:

\begin{pmatrix}x\\
y\end{pmatrix}\to\begin{pmatrix}x+1\\
y\end{pmatrix}\to\begin{pmatrix}x+1\\
y+1\end{pmatrix}\to\begin{pmatrix}x\\
y+1\end{pmatrix}\to\begin{pmatrix}x\\
y\end{pmatrix}

Since the map
\eta:\mathbb{Z}/\left(t^{2}+(t+1)^{2}\right)\mathbb{Z}\to\mathbb{Z}^{2}/\mathbb{L}
,
u\mapsto\begin{pmatrix}u\\
0\end{pmatrix}\mod\mathbb{L}
is an isomorphism and
\eta(\overline{2t+1})=-\begin{pmatrix}0\\
1\end{pmatrix}\mod\mathbb{L}
, there exists a unique
\overline{k}\in\mathbb{Z}/\left(t^{2}+(t+1)^{2}\right)\mathbb{Z}
such that:
\eta(\overline{k})=\begin{pmatrix}x\\
y\end{pmatrix}\mod\mathbb{L}
. Therefore
\mathcal{F}_{\mathbb{L}}
corresponds to:
\eta(\overline{k})\to\eta(\overline{k+1})\to\eta(\overline{k+1-(2t+1)})\to\eta(\overline{k-(2t+1)})\to\eta(\overline{k})
.

By our chosen ordering,
\mathbf{w}
is also the characteristic vector of
\eta^{-1}(\mathcal{F}_{\mathbb{L}})
the face whose vertices are the preimages under
\eta^{-1}
of the vertices of
\mathcal{F}_{\mathbb{L}}
:

\overline{k}\to\overline{k+1}\to\overline{k+1-(2t+1)}\to\overline{k-(2t+1)}\to\overline{k}

This is a face of the graph
(\mathbb{Z}/\left(t^{2}+(t+1)^{2}\right)\mathbb{Z},\,\,\overline{1},\,\,\overline{2t+1})
. Consequently
\mathbf{w}
is a row of
\mathbf{H}_{Z}
.

Conclusion:
The set of rows of
\mathbf{H}_{Z}
is identical to the set of rows of
\mathbf{H}_{Z}^{\prime}
. Hence, in particular,
rs(\mathbf{H}_{Z})=rs(\mathbf{H}_{Z}^{\prime})
.

In conclusion, the code
GB(1+X,1+X^{2t+1},t^{2}+(t+1)^{2})
is CGP-equivalent, and thus equivalent under the standard notion of quantum code equivalence introduced in Definition
V-A1
, to the optimal odd-distance 2D surface code of parameters with periodicity vectors
\begin{pmatrix}t\\
t+1\end{pmatrix}
and
\begin{pmatrix}t+1\\
-t\end{pmatrix}
. ∎

VII
Classification of (2,2)-GB codes

This section details our classification of (2,2)-GB codes, a specialized subclass of CSS codes, intrinsically linked to Cayley graphs of the form
(\mathbb{Z}/n\mathbb{Z},a,b)
.

Our main goal is to systematically identify, for all lengths below 200, the best-performing (2,2)-GB codes up to CGP-equivalence. This classification is crucial, as the practical decoding performance of these codes directly depends on their underlying graph structures. In this context, the CSS Graph-preserving (CGP) equivalence relation, defined in Definition
V-B1
, corresponds precisely to 2-isomorphisms between these associated graphs, thus revealing a deep structural correspondence key to their performance.

Since any (2,2)-GB code can be decomposed into a direct sum of codes of the form
GB(1+X^{a},1+X^{b},n)
where
\gcd(a,b,n)=1
, our classification efforts focused on this canonical form, which serves as the foundational component in the broader construction of GB codes.

VII-A

Method:

To achieve this classification, we first identified all unique graph representatives under the isomorphism equivalence relation. Recognizing that some of these representatives might still be 2-isomorphic, we proceeded to compute the parameters of all associated GB codes. This step is critical because 2-isomorphic codes are guaranteed to share the same parameters, thus providing a strong initial filter.

Finally, for any set of codes that yielded identical parameters, we performed a manual verification to confirm their non-2-isomorphism. This verification was conducted using two primary methods:

•

3-Connectivity Verification:
We checked if the underlying graphs of all listed GB codes were 3-connected. According to Whitney’s Theorem, for 3-connected graphs, non-isomorphism directly implies non-2-isomorphism. Crucially, for our work, all codes of the form
GB(1+X^{a},1+X^{b},n)
with
1\leq a<b\leq n-2
and
gcd(a,b,n)=1
have underlying graphs that exhibit this property for
n
between 4 and 100.

•

Permutation Equivalence Analysis:
Alternatively, we ensured that the row spaces of the different vertex-edge incidence matrices were not permutation equivalent. This serves as another robust method to distinguish non-CGP-equivalent codes even if they share parameters.

The computational aspects of this classification were performed using SageMath and Magma. Specifically, SageMath was utilized for determining equivalence classes of isomorphic graphs and evaluating their k-connectivity, while Magma was employed for computing the minimum distance of the quantum codes.

IX
Conclusion

In this work, we significantly advance the understanding and practical construction of (2,2)-GB codes for quantum error correction. Our primary contribution is the establishment of a lower bound on the minimum distance, directly tied to the shortest Manhattan norm of non-zero vectors in associated
\mathbb{Z}^{2}
-sublattices. This bound proved to be an indispensable theoretical tool, systematically guiding the explicit construction of high-performing codes, a feat that would be challenging, if not impossible, without it.

Leveraging this bound, we successfully constructed three novel infinite families of optimal (2,2)-GB codes:
[[2n^{2},2,n]]
,
[[4r^{2},2,2r]]
, and
[[2(t^{2}+(t+1)^{2}),2,2t+1]]
. Notably, the family with parameters
[[4r^{2},2,2r]]
fills a previously perceived gap, as these optimal even-distance codes were widely thought to have no GB code equivalent. Through a specialized CSS code equivalence relation (CGP-equivalence) tailored to the inherent structure of Cayley-graph based CSS codes, we rigorously demonstrated that two of our families (
[[2n^{2},2,n]]
and
[[4r^{2},2,2r]]
) are indeed non-CGP-equivalent to established Kitaev and optimal even-distance 2D surface codes. Our third family,
[[2(t^{2}+(t+1)^{2}),2,2t+1]]
, was shown to be equivalent (in the standard quantum code sense) to the optimal odd-distance 2D surface code, offering an alternative construction.

Finally, this work delivers a comprehensive classification of all top-performing non-equivalent (2,2)-GB codes with lengths under 200 and a detailed comparison table with existing notable 2D weight-4 surface codes. For a deeper dive into the full classification of (2,2)-GB, our GitHub repository
[
17
]
offers full access to the comprehensive classification of (2,2)-GB codes with lengths under 200.

This culminating effort provides a rich dataset for future practical quantum code implementations, and its structured nature makes it a crucial resource for researchers seeking codes with excellent decoding characteristics.

This research opens several promising avenues for future investigation. A direct extension involves classifying GB and broader CSS codes under larger equivalence relations to further validate distinctness and explore links to decoding algorithms. We also envision adapting our graph-theoretic methodology to construct superior GB codes with higher-weight generators, potentially by extending our framework to hypergraphs or more complex unoriented Cayley graph.
