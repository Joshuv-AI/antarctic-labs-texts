Hunting by humans affects the navigation of two endangered mammals in Zimbabwe and Brazil
Authors: Andrea Presotto, Malvern Karidozo, Priscila Suscke, Christian Ciattei, William Langbauer, Marguerite Madden, Noah Krach, Richard Fayrer-Hosken, Richard Byrne, Ferrell Osborn, Patrícia Izar
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Human activities have significantly altered natural habitats, changing the behavior of many animals. As human encroachment on natural habitats increases, animals must adapt their behavior to couple with these rapid environmental changes. The landscape of fear (LOF) refers to prey perception of risk, which leads to behavioral adaptations throughout an animal’s lifetime. Animals might choose habitats with lower potential energy intake and lower perceived risk over those with higher potential energy intake and higher perceived risk, thus trading off food quality for safety. Here, we investigate whether two endangered mammal species increase the frequency with which they use their travel routes as a navigation strategy to avoid human-influenced areas and traumatic-risk events. Thus, creating a habitual route system to avoid these events and human activities. Using GIS, primary movement data, and remote sensing, we demonstrated that two endangered species increase their use of habitual routes in landscapes where hunting by humans is present. This means using their LOF potentially to focus on signs of risky locations, and human activities, including hunting. These analytical approaches raise questions about whether some species opt for less demanding navigation strategies to balance their focus between navigation and monitoring for signals of risk.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-026-56803-z.

Introduction

A growing body of research reveals that anthropogenic activities can have detrimental effects on wildlife1–5. These impacts are observed across various dimensions, including habitat change6, significant declines in species populations7, compromised conditions among individuals8, behavioral modification9,10, and the potential decline of animal cultural transmission11–13. Some species may exhibit immediate responses to human pressures6,10 such as hunting14, including increased cryptic behavior15, heightened vigilance16, and increased alarm calls9. Anthropogenic-induced behavioral changes such as these can influence the way in which species move through and interact with their environment17.

As human encroachment on natural landscapes increases, species capable of adapting their behavior to rapid environmental changes are better equipped to respond to these threats. Behavioral responses to human presence are diverse, with species differing in how they structure space use relative to human proximity18. Some species may be attracted to human-modified areas. For example, prey species may associate human presence with safety and use it as a refuge from predators, whereas predators may avoid humans because of the risk conflict19–21. In contrast, human activities can amplify the effects of the landscape of fear (LOF)22,23 of many species. The LOF refers to an animal’s perceived predation risk across its habitat24,25. This perception of predation risk alters space usage24,26 as species may avoid certain areas that they perceive as high-risk27, consequently utilizing habitats with lower food quality and lower predation risk over habitats with higher food quality and higher predation risk28–30. From large mammals9,31 to birds32, species can change their space use patterns based on the anticipation of threats associated with the physical landscape in specific locations within their range, avoiding high-risk areas and minimizing predation risk19–21,33.

Species have been reported to use either cognitive maps or habitual route strategies for navigation34, and evidence suggests that, under certain environmental conditions, some species may combine these strategies and switch between them. For example, some species may rely on cognitive maps in familiar areas and habitual routes at the periphery of their range35, whereas others may show opposite patterns, using habitual routes in their familiar area and cognitive maps in the periphery36. The cognitive map strategy is complex, as species that use it must understand spatial relationships37–41 that enable them to navigate without immediate cues37,42,43 while calculating shortcuts44,45. Habitual routes are less cognitively demanding46. The term “habitual routes” is often considered “common sense” since it refers to the process of using established routes to move between two locations. Animals traveling by habitual routes rely on familiar paths they have built over time47,48. These routes form networks, with nodes acting as decision-making locations37,49. By following and modifying the habitual route system, through the association and memorization of resource-related spatial cues37, animals can find efficient ways to move between important locations50. Increased human activity in large mammal habitats, and subsequent landscape changes provide a unique opportunity to understand the role of such disturbances in species navigation strategies.

The discussion about the use of habitual routes and what they represent in terms of a species’ navigation capacity has increased in the last decade40,51, contributing to our understanding of the complexity of these routes by different species46,52. In this study, a habitual route is considered habitual if it is reused within a potential visual range within which the species can see and recognize these routes36. One question that remains is whether the frequency of habitual route usage is forced by physical landscape configuration, meaning that an animal responds to the context of the landscape they travel over, or whether these frequently used routes could also be a response to the animal’s perception of risk53. The actual presence of risk or the perception of risk could act as a trade-off between the lower cognitive demands of following habitual routes and the allocation of more attention to detecting potential dangers within the species’ range, particularly in landscapes where human hunting triggers animals’ perception of predation risk. For example, for endangered species, intense human pressures such as hunting may be particularly detrimental when hunting exceeds the species’ ability to develop safer travel routes to avoid hunters54,55. Additionally, endangered species’ habitats are often fragmented and reduced, increasing the likelihood of direct and indirect anthropogenic influences on these populations56.

Here, we tested whether two endangered species change the habitual route number due to risk, habitat type and their interaction. To test this hypothesis, we selected two species in two distinct environments: the Savannas of Victoria Falls, Zimbabwe, and the Atlantic rainforest of Bahia, Brazil. We analyzed the individual navigation of ten male African elephants (Loxodonta africana) and a group of yellow-breasted capuchin monkeys (Sapajus xanthosternos). One potential outcome of our analysis is that habitual routes are susceptible to anthropogenic-induced change, as the low cognitive demands of following these routes can allow for increased attention to detecting potential risks within the animals’ range. Both of these species may significantly alter the frequency with which they use habitual routes in response to human activities or perceptions of risk. The elephants’ number of habitual routes was tested in landscapes with human presence, using habitat types and hunting status as proxies for human activities. The capuchins’ habitual route use was tested in proximity to behavioral and predation risk events, using these locations as a proxy for hunting by humans and natural predation.

African elephants

Elephants are a particularly interesting case for testing whether navigation strategies adjust according to human activities. Male African elephants (Loxodonta africana) can perceive different threat levels imposed by humans, recognize human hunters using olfactory, acoustic, and visual cues, and respond to both humans and nonhuman predators57–59. These responses can manifest as behavioral changes in male elephants, with individuals present during hunting episodes increasing their distance from the hunt site, and individuals not present during those events showing no behavioral changes60. In the Makgadikgadi Pans National Park in Botswana, young male elephants were found to be less likely to travel alone on previously-used elephant pathways than expected by chance, which suggests that these elephants adjusted their behavior based on the perceived risk of traveling alone on these paths61. In Udawalawe National Park, Sri Lanka, male and female elephants (Elephas maximus maximus) monitor vehicle noises and vibrations. The presence of vehicles increases elephants’ alertness, fear, and stress behaviors62.

Changes in habitat types can influence the speed and likelihood with which female and male African elephants move, with speeds increasing within habitat types related to croplands63. In the Samburu Game Reserve, Kenya, female elephants use protected areas during the day, spending more time near rivers within protected areas than outside of them, due to the presence of livestock and herders64. Both male and female elephants travel large distances65, selecting some route segments more frequently than others66. These characteristics of navigation routes provide a deeper understanding of elephant space use in response to fear; however, it is still unclear whether human activities influence the navigation strategies of African elephants, including changes in the frequency with which they utilize habitual navigation routes.

Yellow-breasted capuchin monkeys

Like elephants, the yellow-breasted capuchin monkey is a well-suited species to investigate their perception of predation risk. Capuchin monkeys have an extended lifespan and proportionally large brains67. Robust capuchin monkeys of the genus Sapajus demonstrate remarkable cognitive abilities in problem-solving, including tool use68,69. Capuchins’ learning flexibility allows them to take advantage of diverse ecological contexts and to adjust their behavior to environmental stimuli70,71. A previous study9 found that yellow-breasted capuchin monkeys traded off food quality for safety, reducing their intensity of space use in areas with a high perceived risk of human hunting9.

The genus Sapajus presents phenotypic diversity in its navigation strategies72. Black capuchins (Sapajus nigritus) in the Atlantic Forest of Brazil travel using cognitive maps to reach high-quality food sources and switch to habitual routes when foraging for leaves and invertebrates36. In contrast, bearded capuchins (Sapajus libidinosus) in the Cerrado-Caatinga ecotone of Brazil use a system of high-fidelity habitual routes during consecutive years, marked with constantly used intersection points for navigational decision-making40. Both black and bearded capuchins face low predation risk, but bearded capuchins emit alarm calls four times as often as black capuchins, indicating increased risk perception9,72. The bearded capuchins’ perception of predation risk is higher than that of the black capuchins at the Atlantic rainforest site. More than one-third of bearded capuchins’ alarm calls resulted from human presence or the presence of domestic animals, including dogs72. This indicates that human presence in the bearded capuchins’ habitat influences their perception of risk more strongly than the risks present in the protected areas where black capuchins live.

The endangered African Elephants73(Loxodonta africana) and the critically endangered73 yellow-breasted capuchin monkeys (Sapajus xanthosternos) have shown well-developed cognitive abilities9,74, spatial memory35,75, and sophisticated assessments of risk9,57. We tested whether the presence of human activities and the proximity to behavioral and risk events influence the number of habitual routes used by these endangered species (Fig. 1). Under this hypothesis, we might see an increase in the number of habitual routes in high-risk areas, as these routes could represent (a) safer paths76, (b) human avoidance77, and (c) limited movement across human landscapes17. We used telemetry data and field-collected GPS locations to test these predictions and quantified the number of habitual routes for the ten male elephants and one group of yellow-breasted capuchin monkeys. Results

We found that both elephants and capuchins changed the frequency of habitual route usage in response to human-influenced landscapes and risk perception respectively.

Within the range of the 10 elephants (Fig. 2a), we found an individual variation in the frequency of habitual route use among the elephants (random intercept (SD = 0.76, 95% CrI = [0.48–1.23]) (Fig. 2b). The Bayesian R² indicated that the model explained approximately 43% of the variance in the number of habitual routes (Bayesian R² of 0.432, 95% CrI = [0.428–0.436]), indicating a moderate explanatory fit for this model. However, while Bayesian R² can be useful it should be interpreted carefully (see discussion). Relative to water in non-hunting locations, the intercept was negative (β = −2.11, 95% CrI = [−2.58 to −1.61]). Among the main effects, habitual route use was lower in woodland (β = −0.26, 95% CrI = [−0.28 to −0.23]), bushland (β = −0.22, 95% [CrI = −0.24 to −0.20]), and grassland (β = −0.03, 95% CrI = [−0.05 to −0.02]) relative to water, whereas effects for wetland, agriculture, urban, and bare soil were uncertain (Table 1). Habitual route use was also lower in hunting areas than in non-hunting areas (β = −0.21, 95% CrI = [−0.27 to −0.16]), and it decreased with increasing route length (β = −0.12, 95% CrI = [−0.12 to −0.11]). Some of the interactions between habitat type and hunting indicated that the effect of hunting on the number of habitual routes differed across habitat types. In hunting areas, the elephants showed higher numbers of habitual routes in woodlands (β = 0.38, 95% CrI = [0.32 to 0.43]), agriculture (β = 0.20, 95% CrI = [0.13 to 0.28]), urban areas (β = 0.15, 95% CrI = [0.03 to 0.27]), bushlands (β = 0.35, 95% CrI = [0.30 to 0.41]), and grasslands (β = 0.13, 95% CrI = [0.07 to 0.18]). In contrast, the interaction between habitat type and hunting for wetland (β = 0.02, 95% CrI = [−0.07 to 0.12]) or bare soil (β = 0.19, 95% CrI = [−0.37 to 0.72]) showed no clear effect on the number of habitual routes (Table 1).

Discussion

Overall, our data sets indicated that hunting by humans was associated with variation in the number of habitual routes used by both studied species. The results suggested that elephants and yellow-breasted capuchin monkeys varied the number of habitual routes used in locations where human activities and risk perception occurred. In Victoria Falls, the changes in the frequency of the habitual routes by the elephants were habitat dependent. In hunting locations, the elephant navigation strategy differed across habitats. In the Una Biological Reserve, capuchins increased the frequency of habitual route use to its highest level when traveling near locations with evidence of human hunting.

The frequency with which elephants used habitual routes decreased when these routes were long. Woodlands and bushlands alone contributed to decreasing elephants’ frequency of habitual route use, along with hunting areas alone. However, when woodlands, agriculture, urban areas, bushlands, and grasslands were exposed to hunting, the elephants increased the number of habitual routes. Although agricultural fields within hunting areas affected the elephants’ use of habitual routes, the elephants did not avoid crossing these fields, where most of the conflicts occur; instead, they appeared to cross these locations using the remaining patches of woodland between them. In Laikipia, Kenya, elephants prefer areas with low human activity, but they do not seem to avoid agricultural fields despite the increased risk of conflict with humans and the risk of elephant mortality63. In Victoria Falls, it is common to find corridors of woodland surrounded by human activities. This landscape fragmentation might also explain the heavy use of habitual routes by elephants40 in the woodlands surrounded by agriculture (Fig. 4a and b) simply because these areas represent the potential woodland corridor the elephants need to travel when crossing agricultural fields. Thus, the more straightforward explanation for elephants’ heavy use of habitual routes in woodlands and agriculture could be limitations imposed by that landscape40. There is also a possibility that the benefits of accessing highly valuable food items may outweigh the elephants’ risk perception.

An alternative explanation for increasing the frequency of using habitual routes within certain habitats in hunting areas, such as woodlands, is that woodlands may provide an environment for elephants to establish a system of safe routes, allowing them to avoid humans, as observed in wild boars (Sus scrofa) crossing urban areas78. Within hunting areas, our results also showed an increased frequency of habitual route use when the elephants crossed the bushes, and grassland. Bushes and grassland within hunting areas represent the greatest exposure large mammals experience when traveling over open canopy habitats. In these areas, the use of remembered safe routes might contribute to elephants increasing their frequency of habitual route use. Bushes and grassland also cover large areas within these elephant ranges and encompass many hunting-permitted locations in Victoria Falls. In these habitats, choosing safe habitual routes might be more essential than in areas where it is possible for elephants to hide. This pattern of route usage is pronounced in habitats where hunting activities were allowed, making the fragmented landscape explanation alone weak when the habitats are within protected areas (Fig. 4a).

Elephants also increased the frequency of habitual route use in urban areas within hunting locations. Urban areas, however, have well-defined street networks that elephants may use to move through the town. Therefore, the urban landscape may also promote habitual route use, much as cities shape human navigation79. However, because certain habitat types were independently associated with fewer habitual routes, and hunting alone was also associated with a decrease in habitual route use, the interaction between habitat type and hunting status should be interpreted with caution. The interaction between habitat type and hunting status indicated that the effect of hunting differed on habitat context, such as that the main effect of habitat type and hunting considered separately could be weakened or strengthened in certain areas.

Given their behavioral plasticity and cognitive abilities80, it is possible that the pressures of adapting to rapid environmental changes affect elephants differently between locations. In Victoria Falls, elephants rely heavily on the habitual route strategy, which is likely to help them respond to unpredictable events when human hunting poses a threat. Humans are mobile predators who move through different habitats in search of prey. By creating a system of habitual routes in hunting areas, elephants may limit their movement to these routes, adding another function to their cognitive abilities. By limiting their movement to a habitual route network, elephants may benefit in two ways: increasing their alertness to signs of hunting22,62,81 and restricting their travel to areas remembered as safe – that is, using their LOF.

The yellow-breasted capuchins increased the number of habitual routes when traveling near locations associated with evidence of hunting, terrestrial predators, and silent movement (Fig. 4c). Human hunting is already known to influence the space use of this population, as the group avoided the high nutritional fruits of agroforestry (Cabruca) areas and instead fed in low-nutritional forests because of the presence of hunters in the agroforestry areas9. Our results further showed that hunting locations also affected habitual route use, particularly when the group traveled close to hunting areas. The capuchins also increased the habitual route use near where they exhibited cryptic behavior and near locations where terrestrial predators occurred. This indicates that the group relies on its LOF to potentially create a set of safer routes to travel near risky areas. Interestingly, in areas where the capuchin group exhibited cryptic behavior, they also increased the number of habitual routes. This finding suggests an efficient combination of habitual route navigation and quiet movement through the forest canopy.

Our findings show that elephants and capuchins changed their navigation strategy according to the level of risk posed by different sections of their habitats. Both species limited their navigation to a habitual route system, potentially supporting the allocation of attention to identifying signs of risk, including human hunting22,62,81. This behavior sheds light on how species traveling across diverse habitats adjust their navigation strategies due to their LOF. Although both studied species seem to use habitual routes due to human presence, they are both known for their behavioral flexibility72,82. Therefore, it is possible that our results are limited by studying a single capuchin group and a limited number of individual elephants. Thus, these findings should be interpreted with caution due to the small sample size they represent. Future studies including larger samples of individuals and groups, measures of individual variation, and higher resolution landscape data would help determine whether the trends we found also appear in other species, other locations, or even in the same species across different locations.

Another limitation that could be addressed in future studies is that we did not account for temporal scale linking behavioral response and risk events for the capuchin group. Instead, we used the total number of habitual routes to measure the distance between these routes and the location of all events recorded during the study period. Our analysis replicates previous studies of habitual routes35,36,38,40,47, but we recognize that the chosen distance threshold can influence how habitual routes were defined. This threshold was based on the estimated distance at which elephants and capuchins could visually detect one route from another, which may have affected the degree of route aggregation. In addition, some predictors showed collinearity due to an imbalance of hunting and non-hunting areas, which should be considered when interpreting the magnitude of individual model coefficients. Caution should also be used when interpreting the Bayesian R2. We reported the Bayesian R2 as a postfitting summary, not as a measure used to fit the model because it represents the proportion of variation in the number of habitual routes explained by the model83. Therefore, Bayesian R2 should not be treated as the primary criterion for evaluating model performance in this study.

The LOF has been shown to induce behavioral changes in several taxa24,84,85, and human activities are recognized as a significant factor contributing to such changes in wild animals78,85,86. Although habitual routes may be advantageous to elephants and yellow-breasted capuchins regarding their LOF, the constant use of such routes reduces their travel flexibility. Recognizing the behavioral adaptations developed by large mammals in response to hunting is critical for understanding how these adaptations impact their space use and navigation. Elephants and capuchins, like many other mammals, play a role in recovering forests63. Thus, limiting these species to a habitual route network may have long-term effects on seed dispersal and the ecological niches of forest connections, thereby affecting animal and plant species that have a mutual or symbiotic relationship with them.

Method

Study areas and Subjects

African elephants (Loxodonta africana)

We investigated habitual routes within the range of ten independent adult male elephants over 3,408 days (Figure S1A). The data for African elephants were collected within and around Victoria Falls, Zimbabwe (18° 06’ S, 25° 51’ E). The Victoria Falls elephant population’s range includes the town of Victoria Falls and 22 parks around it, some of which border Zambia and Botswana. We used the IUCN (IUCN, 2024) conservation classification for these parks (Table S1). In Zimbabwe, evolving human activity has significantly altered African elephant habitat. During the 2000 s, Zimbabwe’s landscape underwent structural changes when the land tenure system converted millions of hectares of large commercial farms to smaller, individually owned agricultural holdings. These changes created a mosaic of land uses and increased the prevalence of human activities, such as agriculture, tourism, and urbanization, with which the elephants living in Victoria Falls must interact while traveling. Therefore, elephant ranges in this study include areas that do not follow the IUCN designation. For example, agricultural fields are neither officially classified as hunting areas nor protected areas by the IUCN. However, most elephant-human conflicts occur around agricultural fields63,87. Therefore, this study classified these areas as hunting areas because of the high frequency of conflict with people due to crop raiding, often resulting in elephant mortality87.

Out of the 22 parks, 10 (or 45%) are classified as protected areas with no hunting activity, and the areas where hunting may occur do not differ in size from those of the protected areas (N = 22, mean 27,482 hectares, SD = 27,516 hectares), H (2) = 4.291, p = 0.117 (Figure S1B).

The subjects were adult males of different ages (Table S2 and Appendix S1- Aging the elephants) and traveled independently, sometimes in pairs or triplets. Each of the ten elephants was from a different herd. Not all bulls overlap with the same range, although they all live in areas surrounding Victoria Falls (Figure S1A). ZimParks identified the elephants in collaboration with the Victoria Falls Wildlife Trust and the Connected Conservation Trust.

Yellow-breasted capuchin monkeys (Sapajus xanthosternos): We followed one group of capuchins (Príncipe) that was habituated before data collection began. The group composition ranged from 32 to 37 individuals: six adult males, 10 adult females, four sub adult males, and 17 immature individuals88. This species occurs in the Brazilian Atlantic Rainforest and the Atlantic Rainforest-Caatinga ecotone. The study group lives in Una Biological Reserve (15° 10ʹS and 39° 03ʹW), an 18,500-hectare conservation area in Bahia, Brazil (Fig. 1). Una Biological Reserve is a lowland tropical rainforest with no marked dry season89. The reserve is part of the larger Atlantic Forest, a highly fragmented area of forest that encompasses much of eastern Brazil. The yellow-breasted capuchins at Una Biological Reserve have several natural predators: three species of carnivores (Puma concolor, Leopardus sp., and Eira barbara), snakes (Boa constrictor and Lachesis muta), and four species of raptors (Spizaetus tyrannus, S. melanoleucus, S. ornatos, and Harpia harpyja). However, many of these predator species, specifically big cats and raptors, are found at low density in the capuchins’ home range4,90. Predation risk is assumed to be high in Cabruca due to high human hunting pressures and the presence of domestic dogs88. Within the group home range, there are three predominant vegetation types: mature forest, secondary forest, and an agroforestry system named Cabruca91. The area of mature forest is the least disturbed by humans. The secondary forest hosts a few exotic species, introduced before the area’s designation as a Biological Reserve, such as the African oil palm (Elaeis guineensis) and the jackfruit (Artocarpus heterophyllus). Cabruca hosts agricultural activities, with the main impact on the forest structure being the replacement of the understory vegetation with introduced cacao trees91. Cacao trees and jackfruits are highly productive and produce larger fruits than native vegetation91. The different landscapes at Una support distinct food sources and intensity of predation pressure, with the most nutritious food sources and highest predation pressure from human hunting both found in the agroforest9.

Spatial data

Elephants

Each elephant was fitted with a GPS collar from Africa Wildlife Tracking (AWT), and the geographic locations were received using the OGI Inmarsat Satellite (OGI-SAT). The collars were deployed by ZimParks and the Victoria Falls Wildlife Trust (Appendix S2).

Capuchins

Three trained observers followed the group on foot from dawn to dusk. During the three-month training period, the observers collected data and compared their observations for validation, meaning they were describing the behavior consistently among themselves. The data collection began after the concordance of the observations reached 85% agreement9. The monthly observations ranged from 9 to 13 days over 2,126 h of observation (Table 3). We used a Garmin Map 76 CSx handheld GPS (precision ≤ 9 m), resulting in 6,322 discrete coordinate locations.

Habitual routes

We first used a GIS procedure to transform discrete coordinate point locations into lines. A point is a geographic coordinate measured at a different time. To represent movement, we created segments into consecutive points using ArcGIS Pro, which creates mathematical vectors from point to point into a complete line. The set of points collected for one day represented the daily route for that day. These lines represent the subjects’ daily travel routes92. The individual vector lines were compared using a script that counted the number of habitual routes at each location. A location was defined as 100-meter buffer (for elephants) due to the possibility that these routes could be visually accessed from each other35. A route not within 100 m of another route was discarded, as it was not classified as a habitual route. By analyzing only the routes that were not repeated within the same month, we adopted the method already applied with the same purpose35,36,47, which also allows for a more representative analysis by not repeatedly counting the same route due to resource distribution within the same month36. We applied the same definition and criteria for the capuchin group but used 50 m of a previously used route93. One route represented the distance traveled by an elephant for 24 h (Table S2, days per elephant). For capuchins, one daily route started when the group woke up and finished when the group stopped at the nightly sleeping tree. The result was the total habitual routes35 traveled by each individual or group during the study period (Figure S2).

Elephants

We calculated the frequency of habitual routes per elephant in both hunting and protected areas, and within each habitat type (Table 4). We measured the length of the habitual routes because longer routes might have resources distributed around them throughout the year46. Thus, their length might affect their frequency of use when animals revisit resource locations.

Capuchins

For the capuchin group, we used the forest type area (mature, secondary, and Cabruca) to represent the different habitats in their home range. We calculated the distance from habitual routes to each behavioral event or evidence of predation that could have influenced the capuchins’ perception of predation risk during the study period (Table 4). Because social and antipredator strategies are shaped by perceived rather than actual risk, we assessed predation pressure by recording all alarm calls produced by the study groups and, whenever possible, identifying the stimulus that triggered them. We did not account for the temporal effect between the traumatic event and habitual route usage. We only used the spatial distribution of both. We used GIS to calculate the distance of each habitual route to the geographic GPS location of the traumatic events collected while observing the monkeys9.

We used the all-occurrence method94 to collect locations of behaviors, evidence of predation risk, and human hunting as proxies for the capuchins’ perception of predation risk, as defined by the five observations below.

Statistical Analysis

To test the prediction that the number of habitual routes would vary according to the habitat and distance to events for two species (Table 4), we fitted separated Bayesian negative binomial mixed-effect model with a log link for each species. Models were fitted using “brms” package95 in R version 4.5.296. Both models used posterior estimation performed through Stan. For the elephant model, we ran four Markov chains for 4,000 iterations each, including 2,000 warm-up iterations, yielding a total of 8,000 post warmup draws for inference. For the monkey model, we ran four Markov chains for 6,000 iterations each, including 2,000 warm-up iterations, yielding a total of 16,000 post warmup draws for inference. We used weakly informative priors for our models, assuming normal distribution. We checked the convergence of the four chains using the Gelman-Rubin’s convergence “Rhat” value97. We used the Leave-One-Out Cross Validations method and used the top model for inference98. The effects of covariates were considered substantial if their 95% Bayesian credible interval did not overlap with 099.
