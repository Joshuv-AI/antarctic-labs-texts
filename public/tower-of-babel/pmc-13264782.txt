Robotic transaxillary thyroidectomy in children: a retrospective institutional study
Authors: Esmat Najjar, Mor Shaked Shukrun, Aiman Elmograbi, Patrick Aïdan
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundRobotic transaxillary thyroidectomy is an established procedure in adults, offering a scar-free-in-the-neck result. Application of this technique in the pediatric population, where thyroid disease can be more aggressive and cosmetic outcomes carry lifelong developmental and psychosocial significance, remains limited and underreported. This study reports the feasibility, safety, and perioperative outcomes of robotic transaxillary thyroidectomy in a pediatric cohort.MethodsSingle-institution retrospective cohort study of all pediatric patients (age ≤18 years) who underwent robotic transaxillary thyroidectomy at our institution between January 2012 and December 2022. Descriptive statistics were used; no formal comparative analysis was performed owing to the absence of a contemporaneous control group.ResultsFifteen patients were included (mean age 14.3±2.5 years; 13 adolescents, 2 children ≤12 years; 80% female). Indications: indeterminate cytology (Bethesda III/IV, n=6, 40%), biopsy-confirmed papillary thyroid carcinoma (PTC, n=7, 46.7%), and benign goiter (n=2, 13.3%). Eight patients (53.3%) underwent lobectomy; seven (46.7%) total thyroidectomy; central neck dissection (CND) in five (33.3%). Mean operative time was 210±45 minutes (range, 140–300 minutes), decreasing to 180±30 minutes in the last five cases—substantially longer than published open pediatric benchmarks (65–134 minutes). No conversion to open surgery occurred. All 15 patients underwent routine postoperative flexible nasolaryngoscopy (FNL); no vocal cord motion impairment was identified. Two patients (13.3%) experienced transient hypocalcaemia; one (6.7%) an axillary seroma; both resolved without sequelae. R0 resection was achieved in all malignant cases. No PTC recurrences were observed at median 2.8-year follow-up (range, 0.5–5.0 years). No validated QoL or cosmetic assessment was performed in this pediatric cohort.ConclusionsIn this small single-institution series, robotic transaxillary thyroidectomy was technically feasible and safe in carefully selected pediatric patients, with low morbidity and short hospital stays. These findings are hypothesis-generating only and do not establish superiority over conventional open thyroidectomy. The substantially longer operative time and absence of validated patient-reported outcome data are key limitations. Prospective multi-centre studies with validated QoL instruments and a contemporaneous control group are urgently needed.

Highlight box

Key findings

• Robotic transaxillary thyroidectomy was technically feasible and safe in 15 carefully selected pediatric patients (mean age 14.3±2.5 years), with no conversions to open surgery, no permanent recurrent laryngeal nerve injuries, and no permanent hypoparathyroidism.

• Transient hypocalcaemia occurred in two patients (13.3%) and one axillary seroma in one patient (6.7%); both resolved without sequelae.

• R0 resection was achieved in all malignant cases, with no papillary thyroid carcinoma recurrences at a median follow-up of 2.8 years.

What is known and what is new?

• Robotic transaxillary thyroidectomy is an established, safe technique in adult patients that avoids a visible neck scar and has been associated with quality of life (QoL) benefits in multiple psychosocial domains.

• This study presents one of the largest single-institution series of robotic transaxillary thyroidectomy in a pediatric and adolescent cohort, demonstrating that the technique is technically feasible in this population with acceptable perioperative morbidity.

What is the implication, and what should change?

• Robotic transaxillary thyroidectomy may offer psychosocial benefits related to avoidance of a visible neck scar in children and adolescents, given their longer life expectancy and developmental vulnerability to scar-related distress; however, the substantially longer operative time and absence of validated pediatric QoL data remain critical limitations.

• Larger prospective multi-centre studies with validated QoL instruments, a contemporaneous control group, and formal cost-effectiveness analysis are urgently needed before broader adoption in pediatric practice can be recommended.

Introduction

Scientific background and rationale

Conventional cervical thyroidectomy has been the standard surgical approach for thyroid disease since its introduction by Kocher in the late 19th century (1,2). While the procedure is safe, it leaves a permanent visible neck scar that disproportionately affects the psychosocial well-being of younger patients, particularly women. Robotic transaxillary thyroidectomy offers a scar-free-in-the-neck alternative and has been demonstrated safe and effective for both benign and malignant thyroid conditions in adults (3-5).

Beyond cosmetic considerations, the quality of life (QoL) implications of the surgical approach have become increasingly recognised. In our adult institutional series of 131 patients [Tzelnick et al. (6)]—the largest published QoL comparison of robotic versus conventional hemithyroidectomy using a validated thyroid-specific instrument—the robotic group demonstrated significantly better postoperative QoL on 7 of 13 ThyPRO scales: anxiety (P<0.0001), depressive symptoms [odds ratio (OR) =0.31, 95% confidence interval (CI): 0.11–0.88, P=0.032], cognitive impairment (P<0.0001), hypothyroid symptoms (P=0.0014), hyperthyroid symptoms (P=0.0018), eye symptoms (P<.0001), and impaired sex life (P=0.0063). These differences persisted after multivariate adjustment for age, sex, and pathology. Notably, no significant difference in cosmetic ThyPRO scores was found (P=0.1028), suggesting that the QoL benefits extend beyond scar avoidance alone and likely reflect scar-related emotional sequelae.

In the pediatric population, these considerations carry even greater weight. Children and adolescents have a longer life expectancy, heightened susceptibility to anxiety and depression related to visible scars, and ongoing psychosocial development during which body image plays a critical formative role (6,7). Pediatric thyroid disease also presents unique surgical challenges: children frequently present with more aggressive malignancies, higher rates of multifocal disease, and bilateral pathology requiring total thyroidectomy (8-10). To date, only a handful of small series and case reports have described robotic transaxillary thyroidectomy in children, and comprehensive outcome data remain scarce (7).

Objectives

This study reports our institution’s decade-long experience with robotic transaxillary thyroidectomy in a pediatric cohort, contextualised against our published adult QoL data (6). The primary objective was to evaluate feasibility and safety. Secondary objectives included assessing surgical efficacy and describing the operative time trend across the case series. We present this article in accordance with the STROBE reporting checklist (available at https://gs.amegroups.com/article/view/10.21037/gs-2026-1-0020/rc).

Methods

Study design

This was a single-institution retrospective cohort study. This is a descriptive single-arm case series without a contemporaneous control group. No randomisation or matching was performed. All comparisons with open thyroidectomy outcomes are derived exclusively from published literature benchmarks; they do not represent an internally controlled comparison (see Figures 1,2; sections Limitations and Interpretation and comparison with published literature).

Setting

The American Hospital of Paris, Paris, France—a tertiary care centre with an established robotic surgery programme. Both operating surgeons (E.N. and A.P.) had independently completed >100 adult transaxillary robotic thyroidectomies at this institution prior to the first pediatric case in 2012, as documented in Tzelnick et al. (6). Study period: January 1, 2012 to December 31, 2022.

Participants

All pediatric patients (age ≤18 years at surgery) who underwent robotic transaxillary thyroidectomy during the study period were eligible. Surgical indications: (I) thyroid nodules with indeterminate cytology (Bethesda III/IV); (II) biopsy-confirmed differentiated thyroid carcinoma without gross extrathyroidal extension or lateral nodal metastasis; and (III) benign thyroid disease with compressive or cosmetic indication. Selection for the robotic approach additionally required patient/guardian preference for a scar-free-in-the-neck procedure after comprehensive counselling on both approaches, including the QoL outcomes reported in our adult series (6).

Exclusion criteria: (I) tumours requiring lateral neck dissection; (II) locally advanced or invasive tumour (T4); (III) previous neck surgery or irradiation; (IV) anatomical factors precluding the transaxillary approach.

The study was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki and its subsequent amendments. This study was approved by the Institutional Review Board of the American Hospital of Paris. Informed consent was waived in this retrospective study.

Variables and data sources

Data were extracted by two authors (A.E., M.S.S.) using a standardised form: demographics [age, sex, body mass index (BMI)], surgical indication, extent of surgery, operative time (skin incision to closure), estimated blood loss (EBL), conversion to open surgery, robotic platform, hospital stay, and histopathology.

Primary outcomes: postoperative complications including hypocalcaemia, recurrent laryngeal nerve (RLN) injury confirmed by routine flexible nasolaryngoscopy (FNL) performed on all patients prior to discharge, haematoma, seroma, chyle leak, and surgical site infection. Secondary outcomes: hospital stay, operative time, EBL, conversion rate, and—for malignant cases—tumour size, lymph node yield and positivity, R0 resection, and disease recurrence. Validated QoL and cosmetic assessments were not performed in this pediatric cohort; for contextual benchmarking, QoL data are drawn from our adult series (6).

Surgical technique

All surgeries were performed by two senior surgeons (E.N. and A.P.), both of whom had independently performed >100 adult transaxillary robotic thyroidectomies at this institution prior to the first pediatric case in 2012, as documented in Tzelnick et al. (6). The da Vinci Si system (Intuitive Surgical, Inc.; cases 1–10, 2012–2017) or da Vinci Xi system (cases 11–15, 2018–2022) was used. The technique has been described in detail previously (4,11). Briefly, under general anaesthesia with the patient supine and the ipsilateral arm abducted, a 5–6 cm incision was made within the axillary skin crease. A subplatysmal working space was created by blunt and sharp dissection extending superiorly to the thyroid cartilage, medially to the sternocleidomastoid muscle, and inferiorly to the clavicle. A 30° robotic camera and three 5-mm instrument arms were inserted. Thyroid lobe(s) were mobilised; the recurrent laryngeal nerve and parathyroid glands were meticulously identified under magnified robotic vision. Devascularised parathyroid glands were autotransplanted into the sternocleidomastoid muscle. Prophylactic central neck dissection (CND) was performed selectively for confirmed or suspected papillary thyroid carcinoma (PTC).

Bias

Selection bias is inherent to this retrospective case series. Patients were not randomised; only those meeting defined clinical criteria and expressing preference for the remote-access approach were included. This preference-based selection is analogous to our adult series (6), in which patients who elected robotic surgery were younger and had fewer comorbidities. The study population represents a surgically favourable subgroup and results should not be extrapolated to all children requiring thyroidectomy. Information bias was minimised by standardised data extraction and objective outcome criteria.

Study size

The total number of eligible pediatric patients over the 11-year period (~1.4 cases/year) is 15. No a priori sample size calculation was performed; this is a descriptive case series. The small sample size severely limits statistical power and generalisability, as explicitly acknowledged throughout.

Statistical analysis

Normally distributed continuous variables are presented as mean ± standard deviation (SD); non-normally distributed variables as median and range. Categorical variables are presented as counts and percentages. Operative time trends were examined graphically (Figure 3). No formal inferential statistics were performed owing to the small sample size. SPSS Version 25 was used for data management.

Results

Participant flow and characteristics

During January 2012–December 2022, records of 25 pediatric patients who underwent thyroid surgery were screened. Ten were excluded: eight underwent conventional open thyroidectomy (patient/guardian preference or failure to meet eligibility criteria) and two had prior neck surgery (a pre-specified exclusion criterion). The remaining 15 constitute the study cohort (Figure 1). No loss to follow-up occurred.

Demographic and clinical characteristics are summarised in Table 1. Mean age was 14.3±2.5 years (range, 9–18 years). By age subgroup: 13 patients (86.7%) were adolescents (13–18 years) and two (13.3%) were younger children (≤12 years; ages 9 and 11 years). Both younger children underwent lobectomy for benign nodules and had uneventful perioperative courses; formal statistical subgroup comparison is not feasible given n=2. Twelve patients (80%) were female, consistent with the marked female predominance in thyroid disease and the cosmetic motivation driving selection of the robotic approach (6). Mean BMI was 21.5±3.1 kg/m2. Indications: indeterminate cytology (Bethesda III/IV, n=6, 40%), confirmed PTC (n=7, 46.7%), benign goiter (n=2, 13.3%).

Pathological findings

Histopathology confirmed malignancy in 7 cases (46.7%), all PTC. Mean tumour size was 1.6 cm (range, 0.8–2.5 cm). Five of seven PTC patients (71.4%) had multifocal disease. Of the five patients who underwent CND, three (60%) had lymph node metastases (1–3 positive nodes each). Mean lymph node yield on CND was 6.4 (range, 4–9), within the range of published benchmarks for open CND in pediatric PTC. R0 resection was achieved in all 15 cases (100%).

Postoperative outcomes and complications

Outcomes are summarised in Table 3. All 15 patients underwent routine postoperative FNL prior to discharge; no vocal cord motion impairment was detected and no patient reported voice changes at follow-up. Two patients (13.3%), both after total thyroidectomy, experienced transient biochemical hypocalcaemia managed with oral calcium supplementation (resolved within one week). No permanent hypocalcaemia occurred. One patient (6.7%) developed an axillary seroma, which resolved with single aspiration. No haematomas, chyle leaks, surgical site infections, or permanent RLN injuries occurred.

Operative time trend—descriptive analysis

A graphical description of operative time across the case sequence is presented in Figure 3. Mean operative time decreased from 240±40 minutes (Phase 1, cases 1–5) to 207±35 minutes (Phase 2, cases 6–10) to 180±30 minutes (Phase 3, cases 11–15)—a 25% reduction. This is a descriptive trend analysis only; formal CUSUM learning-curve analysis was not feasible with n=15. The observed temporal reduction in operative time likely reflects multiple confounders: increasing surgeon and team experience, the da Vinci Si → Xi platform transition (2018), and evolving patient selection—not a single isolatable learning-curve effect.

Discussion

Key results

In this retrospective single-institution series, all 15 robotic transaxillary thyroidectomies were completed without conversion, with minimal blood loss and no permanent complications. All patients had normal vocal cord function on routine postoperative laryngoscopy. These results are hypothesis-generating; they cannot establish superiority over conventional open thyroidectomy, which remains the standard of care for pediatric thyroid disease.

Limitations

This study has several important limitations:

Small sample size: n=15 over 11 years (~1.4 cases/year) severely limits statistical power, precludes subgroup analysis, and prevents meaningful conclusions about oncologic safety equivalence. Prior published pediatric robotic thyroidectomy series typically include 20–26 patients; our series is at the upper range of this sparse literature.

Interpretation and comparison with published literature

Our complication rates (13.3% transient hypocalcaemia, 0% permanent RLN injury) are within or below published ranges for open pediatric thyroidectomy [8–20% and 0–2%, respectively; Vouchara et al., 2024 (8)], though direct statistical comparison is not possible (Figure 2). In our adult series (6), complication profiles were broadly similar between robotic and conventional groups (P=0.604), with complications concentrated in the first 3 years of the programme (2012–2015)—consistent with the temporal pattern observed in the present series.

Quality of life and cosmetic outcomes: in the absence of validated pediatric QoL data, our adult institutional series (6) provides the closest contextual reference. Tzelnick et al. (6) demonstrated in 131 adults (63 robotic, 68 conventional) that robotic hemithyroidectomy was independently associated with better postoperative QoL in six ThyPRO domains after multivariate adjustment: depressive symptoms (OR =0.31; P=0.032), anxiety (P<0.0001), cognitive impairment (P<0.0001), hypothyroid symptoms (P=0.0014), hyperthyroid symptoms (P=0.0018), and eye symptoms (P<0.0001). No significant difference in cosmetic ThyPRO scores was found (P=0.1028), implying that the emotional and psychological benefits observed in the robotic group likely reflect scar-related emotional sequelae and broader well-being gains. These findings may be hypothesised to be particularly relevant to the pediatric population, in whom young women are especially susceptible to anxiety, depression, and scar-related distress.

Operative time: our mean of 210±45 minutes (Phase 3 mean: 180±30 minutes) is substantially longer than the published open pediatric benchmark of 65–134 minutes [Vouchara et al., 2024 (8)]—a time premium of approximately 46–115 minutes at the most experienced phase. In our adult series (6), longer operative time was also documented in the robotic group; despite this, patients reported significantly better postoperative QoL. Nonetheless, this additional anaesthesia and theatre exposure constitutes a clinically significant consideration in a pediatric population and must be prospectively evaluated alongside patient-reported outcomes.

Learning curve: adult literature indicates 35–50 cases are required to achieve performance plateau in robotic thyroidectomy (12,13). In our adult series (6), a steep learning curve was documented in the first 3 years (2012–2015). Both surgeons had established robotic proficiency from the adult programme before performing the first pediatric case, substantially mitigating de novo learning effects. The 25% reduction in operative time across the 15 pediatric cases reflects overall programme maturation—including the Si → Xi platform transition, team experience, and patient selection—rather than a single isolatable learning-curve effect. Our 15-case series cannot substantiate paediatric-specific procedural proficiency.

Oncologic outcomes: R0 resection was achieved in all cases. Mean lymph node yield on CND was 6.4 (range, 4–9), within the range of published benchmarks for open CND in pediatric PTC. Concerns about reduced nodal yield with robotic CND have been raised (3); recent large adult series demonstrate comparable yield in experienced hands (14). No recurrences were detected at median 2.8-year follow-up; longer follow-up remains essential.

Generalisability, applicability, and cost

The present study cannot establish whether robotic transaxillary thyroidectomy should be broadly adopted in pediatric practice. Feasibility at an experienced high-volume centre does not imply cost-effectiveness or generalisability. Whether the QoL and cosmetic benefits demonstrated in our adult series (6) are similarly realised in pediatric patients—and whether they justify the substantially longer operative time, higher cost, and steep institutional learning curve—cannot be answered by this study.

Robotic thyroidectomy carries higher equipment and operative costs than open surgery, amplified by approximately 1–2 additional hours of theatre and anaesthesia time. Formal cost-effectiveness analysis was not performed and represents an urgent research priority before broader adoption is recommended.

Future directions: multi-institutional prospective studies should incorporate the ThyPRO questionnaire or equivalent validated instruments, BODY-Q, or cosmetic-specific outcome measures; a contemporaneous open thyroidectomy control group; formal CUSUM learning-curve analysis; cost-effectiveness data; and stratified analysis of younger children versus adolescents.

Conclusions

In this small single-institution retrospective series—one of the largest published pediatric robotic transaxillary thyroidectomy cohorts—the procedure was technically feasible and safe in carefully selected patients, with no permanent complications, short hospital stays, and normal vocal cord function on routine postoperative laryngoscopy in all cases. These findings are hypothesis-generating only. Our adult institutional series (6) provides strong evidence that the robotic approach confers significant QoL benefits in multiple psychosocial domains; these benefits may be hypothesised to be particularly relevant to the pediatric and adolescent population given their heightened susceptibility to scar-related psychological distress and longer life expectancy. However, the absence of validated QoL data in the present pediatric cohort, the substantially longer operative time, inherent selection bias, and limited oncologic follow-up are critical constraints. Larger prospective multi-centre studies with validated QoL instruments, formal comparative design, and cost-effectiveness analysis are urgently needed before broader adoption can be recommended.
