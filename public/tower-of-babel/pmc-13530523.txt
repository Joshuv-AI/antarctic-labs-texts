Adverse Maternal Outcomes of Cesarean Section: Prevalence, Complications, and Predictors Among Mothers Who Underwent Cesarean Delivery at Yirgalem General Hospital, Sidama Region, Ethiopia – A Cross-Sectional Study
Authors: Aregahegn Dona, Kassech Assefa, Amelo Bolka
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundDespite its life-saving potential, cesarean delivery (CD) remains a major concern due to its increased risk of intra/post-operative complications. This study therefore evaluated adverse maternal outcomes following cesarean section (CS) and identified correlated factors among women who delivered at Yirgalem General Hospital.MethodsA retrospective cross-sectional study was conducted from March 4 to 25, 2024, involving 372 mothers. Data were collected using a structured checklist through a review of medical records of women who gave birth by CS between February 6, 2022 and January 5, 2024. The data were entered into EpiData 3.1, and then exported to SPSS version 21 for analysis. Association between outcome variable and its predictors was examined using bivariable and multivariable logistic regression analysis. Statistical significance was defined as a p-value ≤0.05, with adjusted odds ratios (AORs) and 95% confidence intervals reported.ResultsThe overall prevalence of maternal adverse outcome following CS was 27.7% (95% CI: 23.3%, 32.4%). Significant factors of adverse outcome encompassed rural residence (AOR = 2.05, 95% CI: 1.09 - 3.86), lack of antenatal care follow-up (AOR = 2.26, 95% CI: 1.22 - 4.21), and a history of pre-existing medical illness (AOR = 2.31, 95% CI: 1.26 - 4.23).ConclusionThe occurrence of maternal adverse outcome following CS in the study setting was notably high. Rural residence, lack of ANC, and pre-existing medical conditions were significant determinants. Strengthening healthcare access in rural areas, improving antenatal care coverage, and ensuring early diagnosis and management of medical conditions are essential to reducing complications associated with cesarean delivery.Keywords: cesarean delivery, maternal healthcare, poor outcome

Introduction

Cesarean delivery (CD) is the most commonly performed surgical procedure in obstetrics worldwide. It entails the surgical extraction of the fetus, placenta, and membranes through incisions made in the abdominal and uterine walls. 1 When appropriately indicated and skillfully performed, CD can significantly improve maternal and neonatal outcomes. However, suboptimal execution may lead to adverse consequences that outweigh its benefits.2,3

CD remains a vital intervention for preserving the lives of mothers and infants, particularly with the support of modern surgical techniques, antibiotics, and blood transfusion services. Despite its life-saving potential, CD is associated with higher rates of maternal morbidity and mortality compared to vaginal delivery, as reported across both developed and developing countries. 4

Global cesarean delivery rates reveal striking disparities, with Latin America and the Caribbean leading at 40.5%, followed by Northern America (32.3%), Oceania (31.1%), Europe (25%), Asia (19.2%), and Africa (7.3%). In Africa alone, rates vary dramatically from 3.5% in sub-Saharan regions to 27.8% in the North. 5 This wide variation underscores the complex interplay of healthcare access, clinical practices, and socioeconomic factors influencing CD utilization. Despite its life-saving potential, cesarean delivery remains a major concern due to its association with increased maternal health risks. Research indicates that women undergoing CD are more vulnerable to a range of complications, including surgical site infections, hemorrhage, thromboembolism, and visceral injuries. These adverse outcomes can significantly impact maternal health, often necessitating extended hospitalization and additional medical care.3,4,6

Despite the global rise in CS rates, maternal and neonatal morbidity and mortality following these procedures have also increased. 7 Cesarean delivery is associated with a substantially higher risk of maternal mortality; an approximately eightfold increase in the risk of maternal death and an 8 to 12 fold rise in maternal morbidity compared to vaginal delivery. 8 According to the American College of Obstetricians and Gynecologists, the maternal mortality rate following cesarean birth is significantly elevated, at 35.9 per 100,000 live births, compared to 9.2 per 100,000 for vaginal births. Postoperative complications frequently reported after CS include chronic pelvic pain, deep dyspareunia, severe hemorrhage requiring hysterectomy, inadvertent transverse uterine incisions necessitating bladder repair, postpartum dysfunction, and an increased risk of spontaneous miscarriage.9-14

Various studies carried out in Ethiopia have shed light on the significant public health issue posed by poor maternal outcome following CD in the country. A previous study revealed that 56% of mothers who underwent CS faced complications postoperatively, with common issues including anemia and surgical site infections.2,4 Factors such as maternal age, pre-existing medical conditions, not using prophylactic antibiotics prior to surgery, not receiving antenatal care, the timing and technique of CD, and multiple pregnancies have been recognized as predictors of maternal adverse outcomes of CS.4,6,8,15

Understanding the maternal poor outcome and its risk factors associated with CD is essential for providing a comprehensive and current perspective on the issue. Such insights enhance our knowledge of the potential adverse effects and determinants linked to poor health outcomes of cesarean sections.14,15 However, in Ethiopia, in spite of increasing complications associated with CS, evidence on adverse maternal outcome and its predictors remains limited in the Sidama region. Existing studies have largely focused on the prevalence of CS.

Hence, this study aims to assess the maternal adverse outcome, complications, and risk factors of CS among mothers delivered at Yirgalem general hospital in the Sidama region, Ethiopia. The findings from this investigation are expected to generate robust evidence to inform policymakers and healthcare professionals, thereby supporting evidence-based decision-making in the planning, implementation, and improvement of maternal healthcare services.

Methods

Study Design

A retrospective cross-sectional study design was employed to assess maternal adverse outcomes and associated factors among women who underwent cesarean delivery at Yirgalem General Hospital.

Study Setting

The study was conducted at Yirgalem General Hospital, which is located in Yirgalem town, Sidama Region, Ethiopia. It is located 318 kilometers south of Addis Ababa, the capital city of Ethiopia, and 45 kilometers from the regional capital, Hawassa. The Yirgalem General Hospital serves as a teaching hospital, with a catchment population of more than 4 million. The hospital has seven departments; obstetrics and gynecology is one of the departments where more than 820 operations are conducted annually. Within our study period, Yirgalem General Hospital handled 4,519 deliveries in total, of which 1,219 (26.9%) were cesarean deliveries.

Participants

Eligibility Criteria

All cesarean deliveries performed after the period of fetal viability (≥28 weeks of gestational age) were included for this study; however, those with missing charts and lacking complete documentation were excluded from the study.

Source Population

The source population included all mothers who gave birth via CS at Yirgalem General Hospital.

Study Population

The study population consisted of all records of mothers who gave birth via CS from February 6, 2022 to January 5, 2024 at Yirgalem General Hospital and met the eligibility criteria.

Variables

Outcome Variable

The outcome variable for this study was maternal adverse outcome of cesarean section, defined as the occurrence of maternal death or at least one of the following unfavorable conditions: infection, bleeding, fever, wound dehiscence, or fistula. 16

Independent Variables

The independent variables included: Socio-demographic characteristics (age, place of residence), Obstetric characteristics (parity, gestational age, frequency of CS, labor status, duration of labor), Health service-related factors (ANC follow-up, referral status, circumstance of surgery), Types of anesthesia used during the procedure, Sources of referral, Coexisting medical illnesses (diabetes mellitus, hypertension, heart disease, and anemia).

Data Sources/Measurement

Data were collected from client records using a designed, structured data extraction format adapted from previous studies.2,4,6,15 containing important preoperative, intraoperative and postoperative data. The data extraction format included variables on socio-demographic characteristics, obstetric history, and medical history, indications for CS, intraoperative complications, and postoperative complications.

Bias

To minimize information bias, data collectors were trained on the standardized data extraction procedure. The data extraction tool was pretested on 5% of the sample size at Leku General Hospital prior to the actual data collection period, and necessary modifications were made based on pretest results. Both supervisors and principal investigators provided day-to-day onsite supervision throughout the data extraction period to ensure data quality.

Study Size

The required sample size was calculated using a single population proportion formula, based on the following assumptions: a 30% prevalence of maternal adverse outcomes following CS from a previous study (in Southern Ethiopia), 4 a 95% confidence interval, a 5% margin of error, and 15% of non-response rate. With these parameters, the initial sample size was 323. After adjusting for the 15% of the sample as a precaution against potential loss owing to incomplete records, the final sample size was set at 372.

Quantitative Variables

The following quantitative variables were collected and analyzed: Maternal age (continuous variable, categorized as <20 years, 20-34 years, ≥35 years), Gestational age (categorized as preterm: <37 weeks, term: 37-42 weeks, post-term: >42 weeks), Duration of labor (categorized as <18 hours, ≥18 hours), and Parity (categorized as 1, 2-5, >5).

Statistical Methods

Data Processing

Data were coded and entered into Epi-Data version 3.1, then exported to SPSS version 21 for analysis. Descriptive statistics, such as frequency distributions and proportions, were computed for socio-demographic and other key variables.

Analytical Methods

Binary logistic regression was performed to identify factors associated with adverse maternal outcomes. Variables with a p-value <0.25 in the bivariable analysis were entered into the multivariable logistic regression model to control for confounders. Adjusted odds ratios (AOR) with 95% confidence intervals were reported. A p-value <0.05 was considered statistically significant. Model fit was assessed using the Hosmer-Lemeshow goodness-of-fit test (p-value = 0.785). Multicollinearity was evaluated using variance inflation factor (VIF), and all variables had VIF less than 10, indicating no significant multicollinearity.

Operational Definitions

Adverse outcome of CS: This encompasses occurrence of maternal death, or at least one of the following unfavorable conditions: infection, bleeding, fever, wound dehiscence, and fistula. 16

Non-reassuring fetal heart rate patterns (NRFHB): Represents change in baby’s heart rate that diverges from the normal range or shows signs of stress.

Results

Participants

A total of 372 maternal medical records were reviewed, yielding a 100% response rate. All 372 records met the eligibility criteria and were included in the final analysis.

Descriptive Data

Socio-Demographic Characteristics of the Study Participants

The mean age of the mothers was 25.8 ± 4.8 years, with 336 (90.3%) aged between 20 and 34 years. Most participants resided in rural areas (270, 72.6%), while 102 (27.4%) were from urban areas. Among the 212 referral cases admitted to the hospital, 57.0% were referred by other healthcare facilities and 43.0% presented by self-referral.

Maternal Obstetric and Medical History

Of the mothers in this study, 314 (84.4%) attended antenatal care for their most recent pregnancy. Regarding parity, 233 (62.7%) of mothers were multiparous. More than 89% underwent cesarean delivery at term. A total of 309 mothers (83.1%) had emergency cesarean sections, and 317 (85.2%) were experiencing their first cesarean. The majority, 321 (86.3%), went into spontaneous labor before cesarean delivery, while 51 (13.7%) had surgery before labor began. Regarding referrals, 160 participants (43.0%) were transferred from other healthcare facilities (Table 1). Non-reassuring fetal heart rate pattern (NRFHRP) was the most frequent indication for CS, accounting for 23.9% of cases (Figure 1). Other indications included cephalo-pelvic disproportion (18.8%), abnormal fetal presentation (14.0%), and previous cesarean section (8.1%).

Adverse Outcomes Following CS

Regarding poor maternal outcome following CS, the prevalence of maternal adverse outcomes was 27.7% (95% CI: 23.3%, 32.4%). Among intraoperative complications, the most common were intra-partum hemorrhage (68.9%), extension of the uterine incision (37.9%), and surgical site infection (35.9%). In the postoperative period, the most frequently reported complications were postpartum hemorrhage (51.5%) and the need for blood transfusion (39.8%) (Figure 2).

Outcome Data

Factors Associated With Adverse Outcome of CS

Bivariate analysis indicated that adverse outcomes following CS were associated with several factors: rural residence, referral status, absence of antenatal care (ANC) follow-up, pre-existing medical conditions, and undergoing emergency cesarean delivery. However, in the multivariable logistic regression analysis, only three variables—place of residence, ANC follow-up, and history of medical illness—were significantly associated with adverse CS outcomes.

Specifically, mothers residing in rural areas had 2.05 times higher odds of experiencing adverse cesarean outcomes compared to those living in urban areas (AOR = 2.05; 95% CI: 1.09, 3.86). Likewise, mothers who did not receive ANC follow-up were 2.26 times more likely to have unfavorable outcomes compared to those who had ANC (AOR = 2.26; 95% CI: 1.22, 4.21). Additionally, mothers with a history of medical illness were 2.31 times more likely to experience adverse outcomes than those without such a history (AOR = 2.31; 95% CI: 1.26, 4.23) (Table 2).

Discussion

Key Results

This study assessed the prevalence and predictors of maternal adverse outcome following cesarean section (CS). The overall prevalence of adverse outcome was 27.7% (95% CI: 23.3, 32.4%). Findings revealed several significant predictors of adverse outcome, including place of residence, ANC follow-up, and a history of pre-existing medical conditions. Intra-partum and postpartum hemorrhage were identified as the most common intraoperative and postoperative complications, respectively. The primary indications for CS were NRFHR, cephalo-pelvic disproportion, and abnormal fetal presentation.

Interpretation

The reported prevalence of poor-outcome following cesarean section (27.7%) in the current study aligns with the findings of similar studies previously conducted in Ethiopia.4,15,17 However, this figure is relatively higher compared to earlier reports from the Harari region (22%), 18 South-East Ethiopia (21%), 19 and other African countries, where the prevalence was 17.4%. 20 The observed discrepancy may be attributed to differences in the level and capacity of health facilities, variations in the study settings and time periods, as well as the nature of care provided to patients referred from other health institutions, which could contribute to an increased rate of maternal complications.

The study has found that the place of residence has a significant effect on the negative outcome of CS. Accordingly; mothers who live in rural areas are two times more likely to experience adverse outcomes of CS when compared to urban dwellers. This might be due to the fact that rural dwellers are less likely to use a health facility during pregnancy and typically arrive after prolonged labor.6,9 Due to this, mothers become vulnerable to difficulties and as a result more CS are performed on an emergency basis when issues or problematic labor develop. Again, living in urban areas is associated with higher rates of better maternal and neonatal outcomes following CS due to improved access to healthcare services, socioeconomic status, and educational attainment that contribute to the positive outcomes.3,15

In this study, ANC follow-up was positively associated with the outcomes of CS delivery. Mothers who did not attend ANC follow-ups for their most recent pregnancy experienced a double-fold increase of adverse CS outcome when compared to mothers who had ANC contacts. This finding is in agreement with previous studies conducted in Ethiopia.4,16 This could be the result of delayed access to medical facilities and low health-seeking behavior. Lack of ANC follow-up significantly increases the risk of maternal and neonatal complications in women undergoing cesarean sections. Ensuring adequate ANC, especially for high-risk pregnancies, is crucial to improve outcomes and reduce adverse events associated with CS deliveries. 19

This study revealed a significant association between women’s pre-existing medical conditions and maternal adverse outcome of cesarean delivery. Women with a history of pre-existing medical conditions during pregnancy were at a notably higher risk of experiencing adverse outcome following a CS. This finding is in line with other studies conducted in Ethiopia15,21-24 and in Ghana. 25 In this study, hypertension, anemia and diabetes mellitus were the most frequently recorded previous medical illnesses among study participants. Hypertension significantly impacts maternal outcomes during CS procedure, leading to various complications for both the mother and the fetus. Women with hypertension are more likely to experience postoperative complications such as infections, bleeding, and longer recovery times. The presence of high blood pressure can complicate anesthesia management and increase the risk of cardiovascular events during and after surgery.4,8 Diabetes mellitus significantly affects maternal outcomes during CS, leading to various complications like increased rates of wound infections, delayed healing, and postpartum hemorrhage. In addition, anemic women may face a higher risk of postoperative complications, such as infections, excessive bleeding, longer recovery times and increased risk of cardiovascular events during and after surgery.22,26,27

Generalizability

The findings of this study may be generalizable to other similar healthcare settings in the Sidama region and other parts of Ethiopia with comparable healthcare infrastructure and patient populations. However, caution should be exercised in generalizing to other settings with different healthcare systems and population characteristics.

Limitations

Although this study addresses important issues regarding maternal health, it has some limitations. First, due to its cross-sectional study design, the study cannot establish true cause-and-effect relationships between risk factors and adverse outcomes. Second, this study focuses on a single institution, making it difficult to generalize the findings to other hospitals in the region. Third, the retrospective nature of the study relied on the completeness and accuracy of medical records, which may have been subject to documentation errors or missing data. Fourth, some potentially important variables, such as socioeconomic status and educational level, were not consistently documented in the medical records and could not be included in the analysis.

Conclusion

This study revealed notably high prevalence of unfavorable outcome of CS in the study setting. Rural residence, lack of ANC, and pre-existing medical conditions were significant determinants. Strengthening healthcare access in rural areas, improving antenatal care service utilization, and ensuring early diagnosis and management of medical conditions are essential to reducing complications associated with cesarean delivery. To better understand the factors driving negative cesarean outcomes, future mixed-method studies should expand the sample to include other regional hospitals.
