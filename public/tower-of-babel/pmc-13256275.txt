Allergen immunotherapy (AIT) remains the only disease‐modifying treatment for IgE‐mediated allergic diseases, providing long‐term symptom relief and preventing disease progression. Despite a century of clinical experience, patient selection for AIT continues to challenge clinicians, particularly when balancing diagnostic precision, expected efficacy, safety, and patient preference. Building on recent advances in diagnostics and consensus recommendations, this article outlines a pragmatic algorithm for selecting patients suitable for AIT across respiratory, food, and venom allergies.

AIT decision‐making is multifactorial and relies on accurate diagnosis with regards to consistent clinical history corroborated by allergen sensitization and consideration of patient‐specific factors as well as the availability of high‐quality AIT products. Severity grading [1], frequency of exposure, and risk of future reactions guide the next step of therapeutic consideration. The formalization of diagnostic and therapeutic pathways has proven valuable across allergy disciplines. These approaches emphasize transparency, reproducibility, and individualized care. The integration of such algorithmic frameworks into clinical practice aims to optimize AIT indication, minimize inappropriate prescriptions, and ensure treatment success. Of note, patient adherence to AIT treatment is crucial, and therefore a shared decision taking into consideration patient's values and preferences is mandatory for improving clinical outcomes and enhancing patient engagement.

In patients suffering from quality of life (Qol) impairing moderate to severe IgE‐mediated reactions against airborne allergen sources, polysensitization is common. Efficacy of AIT is in general specifically linked to the allergen source treated with. Thus, evaluation and denomination of clinically most relevant triggers is key. Correlation of IgE sensitization profiles with clinical disease can be challenging. Molecular allergy diagnostics help to differentiate between genuine and cross‐reactive in vitro and in vivo response. Clinical relevance must be determined by means of provocation testing if not clarified by standard testing (Figure 1) [2].

On the basis of phase 3 clinical trials and real‐world experience, guidelines on food allergy management support implementing oral immunotherapy (OIT) into routine clinical practice [3, 4]. After a preliminary proper diagnosis and identification of clinically relevant food allergen sources (Figure 2), a shared decision‐making approach has to explore patient's goals and preferences, as well as a thorough review of potential alternatives in case of side effects of very low threshold reactions (e.g., omalizumab in combination with OIT or as monotherapy) – according to local available therapeutic options, outcomes, benefits, and risks. Counseling patients is imperative for the management of side effects [5]. In order to minimize the latter, mitigation strategies are under investigation including low dose protocols, commencement in below the age of 12 months [6, 7] and alternative routes of administration (e.g., epicutaneous or sublingual) [8].

In insect venom allergy, clinical identification of the culprit insect after a systemic sting reaction is often not possible. Double‐positivity against honeybee venom and wasp (yellow jacket) occurs in up to 50% of patients [9]. Molecular allergy diagnosis (MA) facilitates a precise identification of the culprit insect (Figure S1) [10, 11], in particular in patients that did not identify the culprit insect. In addition, the ratio between sIgE levels to different venoms can be used as a marker indicative of recent venom exposure, in particular when MA is not available [12]. In certain regions (e.g., Southern Europe), double sensitization to Vespula and Polistes venoms is very frequent and diagnostic tools including MA are not always sufficient to identify the culprit insect [10, 11]. Specific IgE inhibition testing offers a potential solution in cases of Polistes‐Vespula double‐positivity, as the method may allow the identification of the culprit insect by inhibiting cross‐ reactive IgE reactivity against the other insect and distinction between cross‐reactivity and true double sensitization [10, 11]. When sIgE values are below detection limit despite a history of severe anaphylaxis, the basophil activation test (BAT) provides an additional sensitive test option [11]. However, both methods are costly and require specialized infrastructure and are therefore not subject to clinical routine use.

Due to new evidence regarding co‐factors contributing to disease severity and the fact that successful application of AIT can be lifesaving in anaphylactic patients, some traditional contraindications were recently omitted or at least moved from “absolute” to “relative.” Nevertheless, management of restrictive conditions must be considered ahead of AIT prescription and during the course of treatment (Table S1) [5, 13]. Food allergy immunotherapy, specifically OIT, is generally contraindicated in patients with uncontrolled or severe asthma, active eosinophilic esophagitis (EoE), or other eosinophilic gastrointestinal diseases. Other significant contraindications include the use of beta‐blockers, severe cardiovascular disease, and poor compliance/adherence to recommendations due to potential side effects and risks in side effect management, malignancies, and severe autoimmune diseases due to a lack of evidence and due to theoretical considerations. Patients bearing a higher risk of anaphylactic reactions due to elevated basal mast cell tryptase (e.g., due to mastocytosis, alpha‐tryptasaemia, or cKIT mutations) require specific counseling before starting AIT [9]. In patients with insect sting anaphylaxis and confirmed mastocytosis, an increased VIT maintenance is recommended that should be applied prolonged or even lifelong. In cases that show systemic reactions during updosing or maintenance VIT, anti‐IgE treatment such as Omalizumab should be considered as off‐label add‐on treatment.

In conclusion, a structured, algorithm‐driven approach enhances precision in selecting candidates for allergen immunotherapy. By integrating diagnostic advances, guideline recommendations, and patient‐centered care principles, clinicians can optimize outcomes and resource use. The future of AIT lies not merely in improved formulations but in systematic, individualized decision‐making supported by pragmatic clinical algorithms.
