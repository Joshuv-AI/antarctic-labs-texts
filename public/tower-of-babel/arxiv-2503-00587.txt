Dual-Input Dynamic Convolution for Positron Range Correction in PET Image Reconstruction
Youness Mellak, Alexandre Bousse, Thibaut Merlin, Élise Émond, Dimitris Visvikis

Abstract
Positron range blurring degrades positron emission tomography image resolution, particularly for high-energy emitters like gallium-68. We introduce Dual-input Dynamic Convolution, a novel computationally efficient approach trained with voxel-specific positron range point spread function s from Monte Carlo simulations and designed to be utilized within an iterative reconstruction algorithm to perform PR correction. By dynamically inferring local blurring kernels through a trained convolutional neural network, Dual-input Dynamic Convolution captures complex tissue interfaces more accurately than prior methods. Crucially, it also computes the transpose of the positron range operator, ensuring consistency within iterative positron emission tomography reconstruction. Comparisons with a state-of-the-art, tissue-dependent correction confirm the advantages of Dual-input Dynamic Convolution in recovering higher-resolution details in heterogeneous regions, including bone-soft tissue and lung-soft tissue boundaries. Experiments across digital phantoms, Monte Carlo-simulated data, and patient scans verify that Dual-input Dynamic Convolution remains clinically practical through GPU-accelerated convolutions, offering near-Monte Carlo accuracy while significantly reducing computation times. These results underline Dual-input Dynamic Convolution’s potential as a routine tool in positron emission tomography imaging, improving both resolution and fidelity without placing excessive demands on reconstruction resources.



I
Introduction

Positron emission tomography (PET) is a nuclear imaging technique that visualizes molecular and metabolic processes by detecting pairs of gamma photons emitted during positron-electron annihilation. During a PET scan, a radiopharmaceutical—a biologically active molecule labeled with a positron-emitting radionuclide—is administered to the patient. As the radionuclide decays, it emits positrons, which travel a short
distance through tissue before annihilating with electrons. This distance, also referred to as positron range, displaces the annihilation site from the original tracer location, introducing an inherent blur into the reconstructed image. The positron range is governed by two factors: the radionuclide’s positron endpoint energy (the maximum kinetic energy of emitted positrons) and the electron density of the surrounding tissue (e.g., dense bone attenuates positrons more effectively than low-density lung tissue). For widely used radionuclides such as fluorine-18 which has a low endpoint energy (0.634 MeV), the positron range is minimal (0.6 mm in water). This blur is negligible compared to the 2–4 mm spatial resolution of modern positron emission tomography scanners, enabling precise imaging of glucose metabolism in oncology. However, clinical demands increasingly require isotopes with higher positron energies. Gallium-68, used for prostate cancer imaging, exhibits a 1.9 MeV endpoint energy and a positron range of 2.9 mm in water. Similarly, rubidium-82, employed in cardiac perfusion studies, has a 3.4 MeV endpoint energy and a positron range of 5.9 mm. These positron range values exceed the resolution of the scanner, leading to significant blurring that distorts quantitative metrics such as lesion size and standardized uptake valuess. This problem is amplified in heterogeneous tissues (e.g., tumor-lung interfaces), where abrupt changes in electron density further widen the positron range distribution.

Various PR correction methods have been developed to mitigate blurring effects caused by positron range in positron emission tomography imaging, particularly for radionuclides such as gallium-68
[
1

The first involves reducing the travel distance of the positron by applying strong magnetic fields to confine its trajectory
[
2

3

The second approach consists in applying PR correction before reconstruction (pre-reconstruction) using deconvolution techniques on measured projections
[
4

5
]
. Although effective in homogeneous regions, this method assumes a uniform blurring profile, limiting its accuracy in heterogeneous tissues where spatially varying positron range effects are significant.

The third approach applies corrections directly to reconstructed positron emission tomography images, offering a practical solution when incorporating corrections during acquisition or reconstruction is not feasible. For example, Deep-PRC
[
6

7

uses convolutional neural network to map gallium-68-blurred images to fluorine-18-like images which was trained on images reconstructed from Monte Carlo-simulated data, effectively reducing blurring. [

While promising, these models are limited to homogeneous kernels, restricting their applicability in heterogeneous tissues.

The fourth approach integrates PR correction directly into the iterative reconstruction process by modeling spatially-variant positron range effects in the forward model using voxel-specific convolution kernels. Materials and Methods

II-A

Problem Formulation

The objective of positron emission tomography reconstruction is to retrieve an activity image
\bm{x}=[x_{1},\dots,x_{J}]^{\top}\in\mathbb{R}^{J}
from a measurement
\bm{y}=[y_{1},\dots,y_{I}]^{\top}\in\mathbb{R}^{I}
,
I
being the number of detector pairs in the positron emission tomography system, by matching the expected measurement
\bar{\bm{y}}(\bm{x})=[\bar{y}_{1}(\bm{x}),\dots,\bar{y}_{I}(\bm{x})]^{\top}\in\mathbb{R}^{I}
, given by the linear relation

\bar{\bm{y}}(\bm{x})=\bm{H}\bm{x}+\bm{r}

(1)

where
\bm{H}\in\mathbb{R}^{I\times J}
represents the positron emission tomography system matrix, such that
[\bm{H}]_{i,j}
denotes the probability that an emission originating from voxel
j
leads to an annihilation event producing a pair of
\upgamma
-photons detected by detector pair
i
, and
\bm{r}\in\mathbb{R}^{I}
is a background vector representing expected scatter and randoms. The reconstruction is performed via an optimization problem of the form

\min_{\bm{x}}\,\ell(\bm{y},\bar{\bm{y}}(\bm{x}))

(2)

where
\ell
is a loss function that evaluates the goodness of the fit between
\bm{y}
and
\bar{\bm{y}}(\bm{x})
, generally defined as the negative Poisson log-likelihood, i.e.,
\ell(\bm{y},\bar{\bm{y}})=\sum_{i}-y_{i}\log\bar{y}_{i}-\bar{y}_{i}
, in which case solving (
2
) is achieved via an expectation maximization algorithm
[
18
]

which computes the estimate
\bm{x}^{(q+1)}
at iteration
q+1
from the estimate
\bm{x}^{(q)}
at iteration
q
with the updating rule

\bm{x}^{(q+1)}=\frac{\bm{x}^{(q)}}{\bm{H}^{\top}\bm{1}}\bm{H}^{\top}\left(\frac{\bm{y}}{\bm{H}\bm{x}^{(q)}+\bm{r}}\right)\,.

(3)

where all vector operations are to be understood element-wise.

The positron emission tomography system matrix
\bm{H}
depends on the system’s geometry, the linear attenuation three-dimensional image
\bm{\mu}\in\mathbb{R}^{J}
—usually derived from an anatomical image such as computed tomography (CT) or magnetic resonance (MR)—and positron range which depends on the three-dimensional electronic density image
\bm{\rho}\in\mathbb{R}^{J}
. In the context of positron emission tomography imaging,
\bm{\mu}
and
\bm{\rho}
are strongly correlated and therefore we assume that positron range is determined by
\bm{\mu}
. The matrix
\bm{H}
can be decomposed as
[
19
]

\bm{H}=\bm{A}(\bm{\mu})\bm{P}\bm{B}(\bm{\mu})

(4)

where
\bm{A}(\bm{\mu})\in\mathbb{R}^{I\times I}
is a diagonal matrix representing the attenuation factors along the lines of response for each detector pair,
\bm{P}\in\mathbb{R}^{I\times J}
is the positron emission tomography geometric projector defined such that
[\bm{P}]_{i,j}
is the probability that an an annihilation taking place at voxel
j
is detected on
i
in absence of attenuation (taking into account sensitivity and detector resolution), and
\bm{B}(\bm{\mu})
is the positron range blurring operator defined such that
[\bm{B}(\bm{\mu})]_{j^{\prime},j}
is the probability that a positron emitted in
j
interacts with an electron in
j^{\prime}
.

The geometric projector
\bm{P}
is known from the system’s manufacturer, while
\bm{A}(\bm{\mu})
can be computed by integrating
\bm{\mu}
along each line of response. The positron range blurring operator
\bm{B}(\bm{\mu})
is more challenging, as it performs position-dependent blurring. Consequently, it is often replaced by the identity matrix or a position-independent blurring operator
[
4
]
, which may underestimate positron range in regions with low electron density, such as the lungs.

A convolutional neural network can be trained to approximate
\bm{B}(\bm{\mu})\bm{x}
by taking
\bm{x}
and
\bm{\mu}
as inputs and directly producing an image with positron range blurring applied
[
15
]
. While computationally efficient, this approach projector
\bm{P}

\mathbf{B}(\bm{\mu})^{\top}
, leading to the use of an unmatched forward model in the iterative scheme (

Matrix Formulation

The blurring operator
\bm{B}(\bm{\mu})\in\mathbb{R}^{J\times J}
models the positron range-induced spatial blurring, transforming an activity distribution image
\bm{x}\in\mathbb{R}^{J}
into an annihilation distribution image
\bm{z}=[z_{1},\dots,z_{J}]^{\top}\in\mathbb{R}^{J}
defined as

\bm{z}=\bm{B}(\bm{\mu})\bm{x}\,,

(5)

which represents the spatial locations where positrons undergo annihilation. The attenuation map
\bm{\mu}
governs this process by defining the local electron density and tissue composition, which influence positron propagation before annihilation. In the following, we assume that positron range is bounded. More precisely, we assume that a positron emission at voxel

m\triangleq\mathrm{card}(\mathcal{N}_{j})=11^{3}

For all

is denoted

and is entirely determined by

for a given radiotracer, and we assume that annihilation is certain, i.e.,

\sum_{k\in\mathcal{N}_{j}}w_{j\to k}=1\,.

(6)

In other words, the vector
\bm{w}_{j}=\{w_{j\to k}\}_{k\in\mathcal{N}_{j}}\in\mathbb{R}^{m}

\bm{z}

\displaystyle z_{k}=\sum_{j\in\mathcal{N}_{k}}w_{j\to k}\cdot x_{j}

[\bm{B}(\bm{\mu})]_{k,j}=\begin{cases}w_{j\to k}&\text{if}\ j\in\mathcal{N}_{k}\,,\\

(8)

III-A

Experimental Setup and Dataset for Positron Range Correction Evaluation

The performance of the proposed method was benchmarked against the spatially-variant and tissue-dependent PR correction method by
[
14
]

[
14

Instead of modeling fully spatially-variant kernels, the method approximates positron range effects by selecting and combining precalculated homogeneous Monte Carlo-derived point spread function’s for different tissue types (e.g., lung, soft tissue, bone). Attenuation correction maps from attenuation images guide the spatial assignment, and voxel-specific kernels are estimated by weighting and normalizing contributions from adjacent tissue types to ensure smooth transitions and activity conservation across interfaces.
All computations were accelerated using GPU parallelization with PyTorch, achieving substantial improvements in computational efficiency without compromising accuracy.

We first evaluated the accuracy of the positron range blurring on digital phantoms (Experiment 1), then in image reconstruction on Monte Carlo-simulated data (Experiment 2) and patient data (Experiment 3).

IV
Discussion

A primary advantage of the proposed Dual-input Dynamic Convolution approach is its ability to generate positron range blurring kernels with an accuracy similar to that of Monte Carlo simulations. This strategy bridges a long-standing gap in PR correction: it achieves rigorous physics-based modeling of annihilation distributions and can be readily incorporated into an expectation maximization algorithm while requiring only a few seconds to process an entire emission volume. Another critical feature is the forward–backward operator consistency inherent to the Dual-input Dynamic Convolution design. Unlike schemes that only incorporate forward PR correction (i.e., in the forward projection), our approach guarantees the convergence of the expectation maximization algorithm. While the present PyTorch-based implementation is efficient, further accelerations could be achieved with a native CUDA implementation or using advanced GPU programming frameworks such as Triton
[
24
]
.

Compared to prior PR correction methods, Dual-input Dynamic Convolution offers substantial benefits in both precision and speed. Early approaches precomputed few generic kernels for different materials, or utilized simple deconvolutions; although computationally efficient, these approaches often fail at modeling positron range at lung–soft tissue or bone–soft tissue interfaces. Recent anisotripic spatially-variant kernels improve accuracy but still rely on combining multiple precomputed kernels, sometimes introducing trade-offs in accuracy or speed. In contrast, Dual-input Dynamic Convolution spatially-varient point spread function in real time for each voxel neighborhood, thus maintaining Monte Carlo-like fidelity even in complex, inhomogeneous regions. The method’s efficiency stems from its GPU-based convolutional design: the heavy computation of blurring is delegated to highly optimized parallel operations, enabling fast kernel estimation across large images without sacrificing the high fidelity needed for accurate quantification (cf. Appendix). Notably, the full computation of spatially-variant and tissue-dependent and Dual-input Dynamic Convolution for an entire Extended Cardiac-Torso phantom volume takes approximately 18 seconds, demonstrating that the proposed approach remains practical for clinical applications with graphics processing unit acceleration.

Our preliminary results on patient data indicate that Dual-input Dynamic Convolution performs on par with spatially-variant and tissue-dependent in homogeneous regions. Further experiments should be conducted on tumors located at the interface between different tissue types.

From a clinical perspective, achieving accurate PR correction can significantly improve image resolution and lesion detectability, particularly for higher-energy tracers such as gallium-68. The ability to correct for range-induced blurring in lung or bone interfaces offers more consistent quantitative accuracy across the field of view. By delivering sharper images and preserving quantitative consistency for a wide array of positron emitters, Dual-input Dynamic Convolution has the potential to improve positron emission tomography imaging standards and expand the use of isotopes previously considered too susceptible to range effects.

V
Conclusion

In conclusion, this study introduced Dual-input Dynamic Convolution as an efficient and accurate framework for positron range correction in PET imaging. By combining local attenuation maps with activity information, Dual-input Dynamic Convolution dynamically estimates high-resolution blurring kernels, matching Monte Carlo accuracy at a fraction of the computational cost. Unlike previous methods that rely on precomputed or approximate models, Dual-input Dynamic Convolution’s predictive approach integrates seamlessly into iterative reconstruction and preserves consistency between forward and backward operations. Demonstrations on digital phantoms and patient data confirm its ability to improve image resolution and quantitative accuracy, especially for high-energy positron emitters. These results underscore the clinical potential of Dual-input Dynamic Convolution for routine positron emission tomography, enabling near–MC-level corrections without prohibitive run times and thus contributing to more reliable disease detection and characterization.
