Microbiological aetiology of paediatric ventilator-associated pneumonia and risk factors for carbapenem-resistant acinetobacter and Pseudomonas in a tertiary-care hospital in Thailand
Authors: Nicharee Chruakanchana, Napaporn Chantasrisawad, Wirada Horapong, Suvaporn Anugulruengkitt
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
SummaryBackgroundPaediatric ventilator-associated pneumonia (VAP) worsens outcomes and mortality. Characterizing local microbiology and resistance patterns is crucial for empirical therapy and guiding targeted infection prevention and control.ObjectiveThis study aimed to determine the aetiology of VAP, identify risk factors associated with VAP caused by carbapenem-resistant (CR) Acinetobacter and Pseudomonas, and assess the clinical burden and outcomes of VAP in paediatric patients.MethodsWe conducted a retrospective study of patients under 18 years of age diagnosed with VAP between January 2022 and December 2024, using Centres for Disease Control and Prevention/National Healthcare Safety Network (CDC/NHSN) surveillance criteria. Data including demographics, microbiology, and outcomes were retrieved from electronic medical records. Risk factors for CR-VAP were assessed using logistic regression.ResultsA total of 71 VAP patients with 95 episodes, were analysed (median age: 7.1 months [interquartile range {IQR}, 2–63]; 49% male). The VAP incidence rate showed a decreasing trend (9.1 to 4.7 per 1000 ventilator-days, 2022–2024). The median time to VAP onset was 25 days (IQR, 13–51) postintubation. The predominant isolated pathogens were Gram-negative bacteria, specifically Acinetobacter baumannii (22%; 64% CR), Pseudomonas aeruginosa (19%; 56% CR), and Stenotrophomonas maltophilia (19%). Staphylococcus aureus (8%; 21% Methicillin-resistant Staphylococcus aureus [MRSA]) was the most common Gram-positive isolate. Thirteen patients (18%) experienced multiple VAP episodes. The overall 90-day mortality rate was 24%. Prolonged hospitalization was linked to carbapenem-resistant A. baumannii (CRAB) (adjusted odds ratio [aOR], 4.3; 95% confidence interval [CI], 1.36–13.61; P = 0.013), while prolonged intubation was associated with carbapenem-resistant P. aeruginosa (CRPA) (aOR, 6.61; 95% CI, 1.42–30.85; P = 0.016).ConclusionVAP remains a challenge in the paediatric setting, characterized by a high prevalence of multi-drug-resistant pathogens, particularly CR Acinetobacter and Pseudomonas. Continued surveillance, antimicrobial stewardship, and intensified prevention are essential to reduce VAP burden and improve outcomes.Keywords: Ventilator-associated pneumonia, Aetiology, Children, Carbapenem-resistant, Risk factor

Introduction

Hospital-acquired infections (HAIs) are a leading cause of morbidity and mortality in paediatric populations worldwide [1]. Among these, hospital-acquired pneumonia (HAP) is particularly common and severe, especially in critically ill children requiring advanced respiratory support [2]. A substantial proportion of paediatric HAP cases occur in patients receiving mechanical ventilation (MV), manifesting as ventilator-associated pneumonia (VAP) [3]. Both HAP and VAP are associated with adverse clinical outcomes, including prolonged MV, extended intensive care unit (ICU) and hospital stays, escalating healthcare costs, and increased mortality rates [[4], [5], [6]].

VAP typically manifests as a complication in patients undergoing endotracheal (ET) intubation and receiving MV for more than 48 h [7]. In paediatric settings, it remains a primary device-associated infections, affecting approximately 5–10% of mechanically ventilated patients. The reported incidence is commonly reported as cases per 1000 ventilator days varying widely across institutions and regions, reflecting differences in patient demographics and the diagnostic criteria employed [[4], [5], [6]].

The pathogenesis of VAP is multi-factorial, involving the microaspiration of contaminated oropharyngeal secretions, colonization of the aerodigestive tract, biofilm formation on ET tubes, compromised host immune defences, and extended duration of MV [8]. Microbiologically, VAP is frequently driven by Gram-negative organisms, especially Pseudomonas aeruginosa and Acinetobacter baumannii, alongside Gram-positive pathogens such as Staphylococcus aureus [9]. However, the distribution of causative organisms and the burden of antimicrobial resistance vary substantially across regions. Studies from high-income countries generally report lower rates of carbapenem resistance, whereas data from global and Asian settings show marked differences in incidence, aetiology, and antimicrobial resistance of VAP [[10], [11], [12]]. This geographic heterogeneity underscores the importance of local antimicrobial resistance surveillance to inform appropriate empiric therapy and guide effective clinical and policy decisions.

The emergence of carbapenem-resistant (CR) A. baumannii (CRAB) and P. aeruginosa (CRPA) represents a critical public health threat. According to reports from National Antimicrobial Resistant Surveillance in Thailand (NARST), the prevalence of CRAB and CRPA is as high as 76.3% and 18.6%, respectively [13]. These ‘difficult-to-treat’ pathogens often leave clinicians with limited therapeutic options, frequently necessitating the use of more toxic agents like colistin. Addressing this challenge requires a dual-pillar strategy: robust infection prevention and control (IPC) measures and active antibiotic stewardship programs (ASPs). However, the success of these interventions depends on a thorough understanding of local epidemiological data, particularly within paediatric populations, as ‘one-size-fits-all’ strategies are often inadequate.

Therefore, population-specific data are essential to tailor empiric antibiotic protocols and refining IPC strategies. We conducted this study to determine the microbial aetiology of VAP, identify risk factors for carbapenem-resistant Acinetobacter and Pseudomonas infections, and assess the clinical burden and outcomes among paediatric patients at a tertiary care hospital in Thailand.

Methods

Study designs

A retrospective descriptive study was conducted at King Chulalongkorn Memorial Hospital (KCMH), a tertiary-care hospital in Bangkok, Thailand. The study period included all paediatric patients diagnosed with VAP between January 2022 and December 2024. The study population consisted of patient aged <18 years who were admitted to the hospital and required invasive MV. Eligible cases were identified through the hospital infection control surveillance databases and electronic medical record systems.

The study protocol was approved by the Institutional Review Board of the Faculty of Medicine, Chulalongkorn University (IRB no. 0626/67).

Case definitions and study population

VAP was defined according to the Centres for Disease Control and Prevention/National Healthcare Safety Network (CDC/NHSN) surveillance criteria for paediatric locations. A diagnosis was confirmed if a patient had been on invasive MV for >2 consecutive calendar days (the day of ventilator placement being Day 1), and the MV was in place on the day of the event or the day before. Moreover, diagnosis required a combination of clinical evidence of infection and new or progressive radiographic findings consistent with pneumonia [14]. To address potential recurrences in the same patient, a new VAP episode was defined only after the completion of the 14-day Repeat Infection Timeframe (RIT) from the initial event, provided that the patient subsequently met a completely new set of clinical, radiographic, and microbiological diagnostic criteria in accordance with CDC/NHSN guidelines [14].

Patients were included if they were <18 years of age at the time of admission, had received invasive MV for >2 consecutive calendar days, and met the CDC/NHSN surveillance criteria for paediatric VAP [14]. To ensure a focused analysis based on the criteria, patients with HAP not associated with MV, those with community-acquired pneumonia (CAP) at admission and cases of respiratory tract colonization without clinical evidence of infection were excluded. Additionally, cases with incomplete medical records that precluded confirmation of VAP diagnosis were omitted [14].

Microbiological testing

Respiratory specimens, including ET aspirates and bronchoalveolar lavage (BAL) fluid, were collected at the clinician's discretion upon clinical suspicion of VAP. Upon receipt at the hospital's microbiology laboratory, specimens were directly inoculated on to standard agar plate media and incubated under appropriate conditions. Following visible bacterial growth, initial organism identification was primarily performed using matrix-assisted laser desorption/ionization time-of-flight mass spectrometry (MALDI-TOF MS; VITEK™ MS, bioMérieux, Marcy l’Étoile, France). Antimicrobial susceptibility testing (AST) was subsequently conducted using a combination of methods tailored to the specific organism, including automated systems e.g. VITEK™ 2 (bioMérieux, Marcy-l'Étoile, France) and manual techniques such as disk diffusion or the E-test (bioMérieux, Marcy-l'Étoile, France). Specifically, AST for A. baumannii and P. aeruginosa was performed using the disk diffusion method. All AST results were interpreted in accordance with the Clinical and Laboratory Standards Institute (CLSI) guidelines current at the time of isolation. Results were analysed alongside clinical and radiographic data to confirm the aetiology of each VAP episode.

Data collection

Data were extracted from the infection control surveillance database and electronic medical records. Collected variables included demographic characteristics (age and sex), primary underlying diagnoses, and the duration of MV prior to the onset of VAP. Clinical data encompassed signs and symptoms at the time of diagnosis, radiographic findings, microbiological culture results, including antimicrobial susceptibility profiles, and antimicrobial treatments. Outcome measures recorded for analysis included the length of stay in ICU and in-hospital mortality. All data abstraction was performed by trained research personnel using a standardized case report form to ensure consistency. All patient identifiers were removed prior to analysis to ensure confidentiality.

Statistical analysis

Categorical variables were summarized as frequencies and percentages, while continuous variables were summarized as mean with standard deviations (SDs) or medians with interquartile ranges (IQRs), as appropriate. Univariable logistic regression analysis was performed to identify potential risk factors associated with in-hospital mortality. Variables with a P-value <0.10 in univariable analysis were subsequently included in a multi-variable logistic regression model to determine independent risk factors for in-hospital mortality. Results were reported as odds ratios with 95% confidence intervals (CIs). A P-value <0.05 was considered statistically significant. Statistical analyses were performed using STATA version 19.5 (Stata Corp., College Station, TX, USA).

Results

Incidence density of ventilator-associated pneumonia

Between January 2022 and December 2024, 71 paediatric patients were diagnosed with VAP (Table I) with 95 episodes. The incidence density of VAP over the 3-year study period was 6.6 per 1000 ventilator-days. The rate decreased from 9.1 per 1000 ventilator-days in 2022 to 6.1 in 2023, reaching a low of 4.7 in 2024.

Baseline characteristics and clinical outcomes of patient with ventilator-associated pneumonia

A total of 71 paediatrics patients were included; the median age was 7.1 months (IQR, 2—63); range, <1—181 months, with a nearly equal sex distribution (49.2% male). One-quarter of the cohort (N = 18) was hospitalized for respiratory tract infections. Most patients (77.4%) had underlying comorbidities, including cardiac conditions (25.4%) and chronic respiratory diseases (9.9%). While most patients experienced a single episode of VAP, 13 patients (18.3%) developed multiple VAP episodes during their hospital stay. From a total of 95 VAP episodes, the majority occurred in intensive care settings, with the paediatric intensive care unit (PICU), neonatal intensive care unit (NICU), and intermediate paediatric cardiac care unit (IPCCU) accounting for 75.8% (N = 72/95) of VAP episodes. The median durations from intubation and admission to VAP onset were 25 days (IQR, 13–51) and 73 days (IQR, 16–88), respectively.

Regarding airway interface, ET intubation was the most prevalent method (87.3%), using cuffed tubes in 50.5% of those instances. Tracheostomies were present in 12.7% of the cohort. Notably, 30.5% of patients required re-intubation prior to the VAP event.

The overall 90-day mortality rate among patients following VAP diagnosis was 23.9% (N = 17/71). Among the survivors, the majority were successfully discharged home (63.4%, N = 45/71), while 11.3% (N = 8/71) required transfer to long-term care or secondary facilities for continued management. Evaluating clinical cure was challenging due to significant baseline comorbidities, including cardiac conditions and chronic respiratory diseases, which often confounded radiological and oxygenation assessments. Among patients successfully extubated (N = 55), the median duration of MV was 21 days (IQR, 15–32). Notably, patients extubated after CRAB or CRPA VAP (N = 20) required a longer duration of ventilation than those with non-CR strains (N = 16) (49 days (IQR, 24–138) vs 34 days (IQR, 26–51), P = 0.286). While the difference between CR and non-CR strains did not reach significance, the extension in ventilation duration became statistically significant when comparing CRAB/CRPA cases to those involving other organisms (N = 19) (49 days (IQR, 24–138) vs 28 days (IQR, 14–42), P = 0.02).

Pathogen distribution and antimicrobial resistance

A total of 166 organisms were isolated from 95 VAP episodes, with polymicrobial infections identified in 49 episodes (51.6%). Of these, only one polymicrobial episode (yielding A. baumannii and Elizabethkingia anophelis) was diagnosed via BAL, while the remaining 48 episodes were identified from ET aspirates. Endotracheal aspirate was the primary method used to diagnose 93 VAP episodes (97.9%). The other two episodes (2.1%) were diagnosed via BAL, yielding A. baumannii and E. anophelis (N = 1) and Aspergillus spp. (N = 1). Gram-negative bacteria predominated (76.7%, N = 127), with A. baumannii (21.7%, N = 36), P. aeruginosa (19.3%, N = 32), and Stenotrophomonas maltophilia (19.3%, N = 32) identified as the most frequently pathogens (Figure 1). Carbapenem resistance was highly prevalent; specifically, 63.8% of A. baumannii isolates were CR (N = 23/36, CRAB). Most CRAB were classified as pandrug-resistant (PDR) or difficult-to-treat, showing resistance to piperacillin/tazobactam, ceftriaxone, ceftazidime, imipenem, meropenem, amikacin, gentamicin, trimethoprim/sulfamethoxazole, ciprofloxacin, and levofloxacin (Table II). Consequently, patients with CRAB infection required salvage therapy with colistin, sulbactam and tigecycline. Similarly, 56.2% of P. aeruginosa isolates were CR (N = 18/32, CRPA), although 87.1% and 77.4% remained susceptible to ceftazidime and piperacillin/tazobactam, respectively (Table II). Carbapenem-resistant enterobacterales (CRE) were less common, accounting for 11.1% of Klebsiella spp. and 2.0% of Escherichia coli. Among Gram-positive isolates, Staphylococcus aureus was the most prevalent (8.1%), of which 21.4% were methicillin-resistant (N = 3/14, MRSA).

Risk factors for carbapenem-resistant Acinetobactor baumannii and Pseudomonas aeruginosa

Among 29 patients with 36 A. baumannii isolates, 62.0% (18/29) experienced CR. One patient from whom CRAB was isolated later experienced an infection with non-CR A. baumannii VAP. In the univariate analysis, only a prolonged duration from admission to VAP onset (≥45 days) was significantly associated with CRAB (odds ratio [OR], 4.3, 95% CI, 1.36–13.61; P = 0.013). Although factors such as re-intubation (OR, 0.37; P = 0.092) met the threshold for multi-variable inclusion (P < 0.1), the backward stepwise selection identified prolonged hospitalization as the sole independent predictor in the final multi-variable model (adjusted OR [aOR], 4.3, 95% CI 1.36–13.61; P = 0.013) (Table III). Other variables, including sex, tracheostomy status, and type of ET tube, showed no significant association with CRAB VAP.

Discussion

Our findings demonstrate a significant reduction in VAP incidence density over the three-year study period, coinciding with the implementation of a paediatric IPC programme targeting central line–associated bloodstream infections (CLABSI). Despite this favourable trend, the microbiological burden of CR pathogens remained substantial, with more than half of A. baumannii and P. aeruginosa isolates exhibiting carbapenem resistance. In our cohort, an extended duration of MV prior to VAP onset was associated with a higher likelihood of isolating carbapenem-resistant pathogens (specifically CRPA). These findings suggest that, in paediatric VAP, antimicrobial resistance may exert a greater impact on healthcare resource utilization, underscoring the importance of local surveillance data to guide empiric antimicrobial therapy and the need for integrated IPC and ASPs.

The incidence of VAP declined from 9.1 to 4.7 per 1000 ventilator-days over the study period during CRBSI bundle implementation. We hypothesize that the hand-hygiene and aseptic technique components of the CRBSI bundle provided a cross-protecting effect for VAP. However, it has been reported that reductions in VAP incidence did not uniformly translate into improved survival [12,15]. This observation suggests that reducing incidence alone may be insufficient to improve outcomes without parallel strategies focused on early recognition, timely initiation of appropriate empiric therapy, and prevention of antimicrobial resistance [12,15].

The microbiological profile was dominated by Gram-negative bacteria (76.7%), specifically A. baumannii, P. aeruginosa, and S. maltophilia. Of particular concern is the high rate of CR (63.7% in CRAB and 56.2% in CRPA). This aligns with reports from PICUs across multiple regions, where Gram-negative bacteria consistently predominate as causative pathogens of VAP and CRAB and CRPA are predominant [12,16]. The presence of PDR A. baumannii isolates necessitated the use of salvage therapies like colistin and tigecycline, which are associated with increased toxicity and complexity in paediatric dosing. Studies from several middle-income countries in Asia, including China and India, have reported a predominance of Gram-negative organisms with substantially higher rates of Multidrug resistance (MDR) and CR, particularly among A. baumannii, with 50.2–77.6% being CRAB [17,18], which was similar to our cohort. However, global data showed that the proportion of MDR and CR isolates varies between countries [12,[19], [20], [21]].

This gradient across countries highlights that the burden of CR-VAP is not uniform. Regional antimicrobial pressure, differences in empirical antibiotic practices, and variable implementation of infection prevention measures play a significant role in shaping local resistance epidemiology. This reinforces the importance of interpreting resistance patterns within local and regional contexts rather than extrapolating from data generated elsewhere [15,20]. Crucially, inappropriate initial antimicrobial therapy has been associated with delayed clinical response and increased mortality [17]. In addition, the high rate of polymicrobial infections in our study (51.6%) further complicates the clinical management, often requiring appropriate broad-spectrum initial therapy.

The 90-day mortality rate of 23.9% reflects the high acuity of our cohort, 77.4% of whom had co-morbidities. In our cohort, CRAB/CRPA VAP was associated with a median of 49 days of MV. While the 15-day disparity compared with non-CR strains (34 days) did not reach statistical significance, the magnitude of this difference remains clinically substantial in the context of hospital resource utilization. This non-significant result likely reflects an underpowering due to the limited sample size and the high intra-group variability inherent in resistant infections (IQR, 24–138).

In our study, prolonged hospitalization (≥45 days) was the independent predictor of CRAB, suggesting that A. baumannii may be increasingly endemic in the hospital environment, colonizing patients over time. Similarly, prolonged intubation (≥45 days) was the predictor for CRPA. These findings likely reflect cumulative antibiotic exposure, prolonged contact with hospital flora, and repeated invasive procedures, all of which increase the risk of colonization and subsequent infection with MDR organisms [16,18]. Prior exposure to carbapenems has been consistently identified as a key driver of carbapenem resistance, further supporting the importance of judicious antibiotic use [18].

Indeed, longer ICU admissions increase the cumulative risk of exposure to the hospital environment, where these opportunistic pathogens are endemic, while prolonged stays frequently necessitate multiple or extended courses of broad-spectrum antibiotics, creating a selective pressure that eradicates susceptible microflora and promotes colonization by resistant strains [7,17]. Although prolonged intubation and MV are frequent risk factors across various CR infections, making it challenging to isolate pathogen-specific risk factors, there are distinct biological mechanisms that facilitate P. aeruginosa. While most respiratory pathogens typically colonize the oropharynx first, P. aeruginosa frequently colonizes the trachea directly, suggesting that the intubated airway itself serves as the primary site for its acquisition and biofilm formation [7]. Interestingly, CRPA occurring in non-ICU wards carried a high adjusted OR of 7.62. This may reflect less stringent adherence to IPC bundles in general wards than the highly monitored ICU environment suggesting that IPC programmes and ASPs should extend to all units where ventilated children are managed.

The strengths of this study include comprehensive local microbiological data and detailed risk factor analysis over a three-year period. However, there are several limitations, including the retrospective nature of the study and the difficulty in distinguishing between airway colonization and true infection, particularly in cases where polymicrobial organisms were identified. Despite these constraints, the three-year study duration provides a clear longitudinal perspective on the temporal trends of resistant organisms within a tertiary-care hospital.

In conclusion, while IPC measures have successfully reduced VAP rates, the burden of CR remains high, lengthening the duration of MV. The predominance of CRAB and CRPA underscores the importance of local surveillance data to guide empiric antibiotic selection. Strengthening IPC and ASPs, both within and beyond the ICUs, is essential to improving outcomes in children with VAP, reinforcing how general hygiene bundles can offer broad, cross-protective clinical benefits.
