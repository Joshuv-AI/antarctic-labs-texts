Expert perspectives on the ecosystem of medical AI oversight in the GenAI era
Authors: Stephen Gilbert, Tinglong Dai
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractIn this third article of a three-article series on interviews with Dr Shantanu Nundy, an advisor in the office of the Commissioner of the US FDA on AI governance and digital health policy, we explore how the oversight of generative AI (GenAI) and large language model (LLM)-enabled medical devices extends beyond the remit of any single regulator, requiring coordination among government agencies, health systems, and practicing clinicians. We also discuss practical advice for innovators seeking to engage productively with the FDA.Subject terms: Business and industry, Health care, Scientific community, Social sciences

Is the regulation of AI-enabled tools in health a task that, although clearly complicated, is for one organisation to grapple with, or is it the type of challenge that requires the integration of thought, action and procedure across multiple bodies and agencies? This is the question we discuss in this third article in a series of articles addressing emergent challenges in the regulation of AI-enabled digital health.

Introduction to Dr Shantanu Nundy

In the first article in this three-part series, we spoke with Dr Shantanu Nundy about the TEMPO pilot1. In the second article, we discussed the regulation of LLM-enabled health tools2. Dr Nundy is a practicing primary care physician, serial innovator, and advisor in the FDA Commissioner’s Office working on AI issues across the agency. Because he is a contractor, not a federal employee, the views expressed here are his own and should not be interpreted as official FDA policy.

Responsibility overlaps in the oversight of medical AI

We had a wide-ranging discussion with Dr Shantanu Nundy in early February 2026, in which we discussed the latest developments and practical challenges to the regulation of wellness and LLM-enabled health technologies. The final section of our discussion deals with how the responsibilities for AI governance are split between government and its agencies, and the practice of medicine, spanning the health systems and medical practitioners who work within them, directly using AI tools in the treatment of their patients. The interview has been edited for clarity and references have been added to help the reader understand the context.

Stephen Gilbert

I’ve long been intrigued by the in-progress US Algorithm Accountability Act (AAA)3, which is in various different iterations through Congress, and has included proposals for algorithmic audit and oversight of AI in hospitals. If we consider the potential impact of LLM-enabled systems in hospitals, we could have many AIs that interact within the electronic health record. We may soon be far from the old paradigm, where there is an individual manufacturer making something and considering all of its interactions4. What do you think of the regulation of the system level as opposed to the individual device manufacturer?

Shantanu Nundy

In my view, assuring the safety and effectiveness of medical AI will require multiple actors. FDA has a crucial role, but I think of device authorization or approval as providing the floor, not the ceiling. FDA can say that a product has met a minimum standard of safety and effectiveness. But decisions about whether to deploy it, how to deploy it, how to monitor it in practice, and how to optimize its use sit with health systems, clinicians, payers, and potentially additional oversight bodies. That is not entirely new. Drug regulation works similarly: FDA reviews evidence from controlled studies, but clinicians decide whether a drug makes sense for a particular patient, Pharmacy and Therapeutics (P&T) committees decide whether to place it on formulary, and payers decide whether it creates enough clinical and economic value to provide coverage. AI will require the same kind of ecosystem approach, although the capabilities of those actors - and perhaps the set of actors itself - may need to evolve.

Tinglong Dai

As someone who has been part of this overall ecosystem for a long time, what are some of the common misunderstandings about the FDA’s AI regulatory process, and what are some of the best-kept secrets — things unknown to the general public, to industry, or to researchers?

Shantanu Nundy

One common misunderstanding is that FDA lacks AI expertise. In reality, the agency has authorized over 1000 AI-enabled devices and 100 s of drugs where AI was used in product development. It also has substantial in-house scientific and technical capacity. The Office of Science and Engineering Laboratories (OSEL)5 for example, includes researchers and data scientists whose job is to develop regulatory science tools that reviewers can use to evaluate products. They have done foundational work on image analysis, drift, and synthetic data.

A second point is that people often rely too heavily on second-hand interpretations of FDA policy. The guidances themselves are public, and FDA now routinely pairs major guidance updates with town halls, presentation materials, and other public-facing content. The recent general wellness6 and CDS7 updates are good examples. For many companies, going directly to the primary source is more useful than relying solely on third parties.

Lastly, there are many ways to engage FDA directly, and people should use them. Some companies worry that asking a basic question - ‘Is my product regulated?’ - will put them on the agency’s radar in a negative way. In my experience, the opposite is closer to the truth. There are informal channels, such as the digital health inbox, and formal ones, such as Q-Submissions8, which let companies engage early. Those conversations can clarify whether a product is likely to require review and what evidence to generate. They are also valuable to the agency, especially in fast-moving areas like AI, because edge cases help stress-test policy frameworks.

Summary

The oversight of health AI, particularly GenAI, cannot rest with the FDA alone. As Dr Nundy argues, the agency sets a regulatory floor for safety and efficacy, but implementation decisions, whether and how to deploy AI tools in clinical practice, necessarily involve health systems, clinicians, insurers, and potentially new oversight actors. For innovators, the practical takeaway is clear: the FDA’s scientists and reviewers welcome early engagement, and the agency offers multiple formal and informal channels for dialogue. An open exchange with the FDA can help set medical AI on the path to authorization, reimbursement, and meaningful patient impact. Where to next for FDA GenAI regulation? This third article of this three-part series hints at some directions. Remarks by Dr. Michelle Tarver, the FDA’s Director of the Center for Devices and Radiological Health (CDRH) at the AAMI neXus conference (Leesburg, VA) on April 17, 2026, indicated that there will be major policy announcements from the FDA on GenAI regulation during the course of this year. Just as it is challenging for the digital medicine community to keep up with developments of GenAI, we also have work to do in staying up to date with advances in US AI regulation.
