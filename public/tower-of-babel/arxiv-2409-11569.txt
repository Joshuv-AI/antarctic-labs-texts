Optimal Investment with Costly Expert Opinions
Christoph Knochenhauer, Alexander Merkel, Yufei Zhang

Abstract
We consider the Merton problem of optimizing expected power utility of terminal wealth in the case of an unobservable Markov-modulated drift. What makes the model special is that the agent is allowed to purchase costly expert opinions of varying quality on the current state of the drift, leading to a mixed stochastic control problem with regular and impulse controls involving random consequences. Using ideas from filtering theory, we first embed the original problem with unobservable drift into a full information problem on a larger state space. The value function of the full information problem is characterized as the unique viscosity solution of the dynamic programming PDE. This characterization is achieved by a new variant of the stochastic Perron’s method, which additionally allows us to show that, in between purchases of expert opinions, the problem reduces to an exit time control problem which is known to admit an optimal feedback control. Under the assumption of sufficient regularity of this feedback map, we are able to construct optimal trading and expert opinion strategies.



1
Introduction

Over the last three decades, there has been tremendous interest in
continuous-time portfolio optimization problems with partial observations. These
problems are typically set up such that the agent is assumed to observe the
prices of financial assets whose returns have an unobservable drift. In the
Markovian case, these problems can be formulated as
hidden Markov models which, by using ideas from filtering theory, can be
transformed into full information problems at the cost of a higher-dimensional
state space; c.f.
[
16
]
.
In fact, three special cases are known in which the transformed problem
remains finite-dimensional.
In the Bayesian case as e.g. considered in
[
25
]
,
the hidden component of the drift is a static random variable of arbitrary
distribution. In contrast, the Kalmann–Bucy case as e.g. studied in

[
6
]
arises if the unobservable drift is taken to
be an Ornstein–Uhlenbeck process. Finally, there is the Wonham case as e.g. considered
in
[
32
]
in which the drift is modulated by an unobservable
continuous-time finite-state Markov chain.
There are many extensions of these baseline models, as for example problems with
different objective functionals and more general financial market models including
jump diffusion models and non-Markovian dynamics; see for example
[
22
,
28
,
29
,
31
,
34
]
and
the references therein for a non-exhaustive list of the vast literature in this field.

In the present article, we consider an extension of the problem in which the
agent can purchase noisy expert opinions on the current state of the drift.
That is, an expert opinion at time
t
is assumed to take the form

Z=q\mu_{t}+(1-q)\mathcal{N},

(1)

where
\mu_{t}
denotes the unobservable drift at time
t
,
\mathcal{N}
is an
independent random variable causing the expert opinion to be noisy, and

q\in[0,1]
is the quality of the expert opinion.
In general, the inclusion of expert opinions as above is not a new idea and
has been studied in
[
17
,
18
,
19
,
35
]
. We furthermore mention
the papers
[
36
,
37
]
which investigate
diffusion approximations of expert opinions as the time period in between the
arrival of new expert opinions tends to zero. Finally, there is an
alternative approach to expert opinions which are revealed continuously in
time; see in particular
[
11
]
and the related articles

[
12
,
13
]
.

What these articles have in common is that they assume that expert opinions
arrive periodically in time, that is, either at discrete times, Poisson times,
or continuously in time. In particular, expert opinions are treated as exogenous,
and the additional information they contain on the true state of the drift is
given to the agent free of charge. In our model,
on the other hand, we assume that the agent has to purchase expert opinions
in exchange for a strictly positive fee. To be precise, we consider a financial
market
model similar to
[
32
]
in which there is a single risky
asset with drift modulated by a finite-state continuous-time Markov chain, i.e. we place ourselves in the Wonham setting. In addition to choosing a trading strategy,
the agent can decide on the timing of purchase and quality level of expert
opinions. Since each purchase is assumed
to incur a strictly positive cost and the agent’s wealth is finite, an expert
opinion strategy is naturally modeled as an impulse control. This leads to the
formulation of the optimization problem as a mixed stochastic control problem
with partial observations involving both regular controls (the trading strategy)
and impulse controls (the expert opinion strategy). Moreover, due to the
noise
\mathcal{N}
in the expert opinion (
1
), impulse
controls have random consequences in the sense of
[
21
,
26
]
.

The precise model specifications and the formulation of the investment objective
can be found in Section
2
. Let us highlight here that special
care needs to be taken in the setup of the information structure as it may
be optimal to purchase multiple expert opinions at the same time instant, hence
requiring multiple simultaneous updates of the agent’s information set.
In Section
3
, we apply standard
filtering results to transform the partial observation problem into a full
information problem at the cost of increasing the state space. This transformation
is a classical first step in the study of partial observation problems and
has the advantage of putting the dynamic programming machinery at our disposal.

The dynamic programming PDE of the full information problem, which takes the
form of Hamilton–Jacobi–Bellman quasi-variational inequalities (HJBQVI), is
studied in detail in Section
4
. To be precise,
we show that the HJBQVI admit a unique continuous viscosity solution denoted
by
V^{+}
. This is achieved by a variant of the stochastic Perron’s method
which combines ideas which have previously appeared in

[
2
,
5
]
. Typically, the reason for
choosing the stochastic Perron’s method is that it allows to characterize
the value function of a stochastic control problem as the unique viscosity
solution of the associated dynamic programming PDE without having to prove the
notoriously challenging dynamic programming principle first
[
8
]
.
We, on the other hand, content ourselves (at this point) with the insight that
the unique viscosity solution
V^{+}
dominates the value function of the full
information problem.

After having constructed the unique viscosity solution of the HJBQVI, we
proceed in Section
5
by showing that, on the set
\mathbb{C}

of states on which purchases of expert opinions are strictly suboptimal,
the full information problem reduces to an exit time control problem. The
intuition here is that on
\mathbb{C}
, the agent only chooses the trading strategy
and can ignore purchases of expert opinions up until the time of exit from
\mathbb{C}
.
This connection is formalized by another version of the stochastic Perron’s method.
More precisely, writing
V^{\mathbb{C}}
for the value function of the exit time
control problem, we first argue that the pointwise supremum
V^{-}
of a suitable
set of stochastic subsolutions of the exit time control problem is a
viscosity supersolution of the associated dynamic programming PDE such that

V^{-}\leq V^{\mathbb{C}}
. On the other hand, it is straightforward to show that
the unique viscosity solution
V^{+}
of the HJBQVI is a viscosity subsolution of the
dynamic programming PDE of the exit time control problem with
V^{+}\geq V^{\mathbb{C}}
.
A comparison principle for the PDE shows that
V^{-}=V^{\mathbb{C}}=V^{+}
, thus
establishing the connection between the full information problem and the
exit time control problem.

The advantage of establishing this connection is that the exit time control
problem is known to admit an optimal control, which we can use to construct
an optimal trading strategy and optimal expert opinion strategy for the full
information problem. This construction is also carried out in
Section
5
. A verification theorem furthermore shows
that the unique viscosity solution
V^{+}
of the HJBQVI coincides with the
value function of the full information problem, thus completing the main
results of this paper.

The construction of
the optimal strategies, however, involves a number of subtle technicalities and
requires several novel ideas, hence making up a significant part of the
main contributions of this article.
First, as usual for partial observation problems, the associated
full information problem is highly degenerate in the sense that the
(1+N)
-dimensional
state process is driven by a one-dimensional Brownian motion. In particular,
due to the lack of uniform ellipticity, there is no hope to find a classical
solution of the HJBQVI, and hence classical verification-type arguments to
construct optimizers is out of reach. To work around this problem, we
employ an extension of the verification procedure developed in

[
3
,
4
,
5
,
7
]

which allows to construct optimal expert opinion strategies (or, more generally,
impulse controls) from continuous viscosity solutions. One central assumption
in this type of construction is that the underlying stochastic process
satisfies the strong Markov property. Unfortunately, in our situation, this
is in general not guaranteed to hold as trading strategies (the regular
controls) can in principle be path-dependent and lead to non-Markovian state
processes. In the literature, there is very little work on impulse control
of non-Markovian processes. Let us mention
[
15
,
24
]
in this direction, but highlight that none of the approaches
developed in these articles seems feasible in our setting.

For the resolution of the lack of Markovianity, we make particular use of the connection
to the exit time control problem. More precisely, according to the results in

[
20
]
, our exit time control problem
admits an optimal control. Using this, we establish the martingale optimality principle
which shows that the composition of the value function of the exit time control
problem with the optimally controlled state process is a martingale. This martingale
property is sufficient to replace the strong Markov arguments in the impulse
verification machinery of
[
3
,
4
,
5
,
7
]
.

Another challenge in the construction of the optimal strategies is that the
optimal control of the exit time control problem provided by

[
20
]
is only known to exist in a weak formulation of
the control problem, that is, on a particular probability space with a
particular driving Brownian motion and particular filtration. We work around
this issue by assuming that the stochastic differential equation associated
with the optimal control admits a strong solution, hence allowing us to
construct the optimal state process on arbitrary probability spaces and
arbitrary driving Brownian motions. While we could drop this assumption by
posing our optimal investment problem in a weak formulation as well, it is
our impression that the additional technicalities involved in doing so would
rather distract from the main contributions of the paper. In that sense,
our assumption of existence of a strong solution is simply a means of
keeping the technical burden (and length) of this paper reasonable.

2
Formulation of the Optimal Investment Problem

For a given finite time horizon
T>0
, let
(\Omega,\mathcal{F},\mathbb{F},\mathbb{P})
be a complete
filtered probability space such that
\mathbb{F}=(\mathcal{F}_{s})_{s\in[0,T]}
satisfies the
usual assumptions of completeness and right-continuity and write

\mathcal{F}_{\infty}:=\mathcal{F}_{T}
. On this space, we
assume that we are given an adapted continuous-time Markov chain

Y=(Y_{s})_{s\in[0,T]}
with càdlàg paths. We assume that
Y
takes values in

\{e_{1},\dots,e_{N}\}
, the canonical unit vectors in
\mathbb{R}^{N}
, where
N\in\mathbb{N}

denotes the number of regimes of the economy. We take as given an
\mathcal{F}_{0}
-measurable
random variable
y_{0}
with values in
\{e_{1},\dots,e_{N}\}
and assume that
Y_{0}=y_{0}
.
The distribution of
y_{0}

is determined by the probability vector
p_{0}=(p_{0}^{1},\dots,p_{0}^{N})\in\Delta^{N}
,
where

p^{n}_{0}:=\mathbb{P}[y_{0}=e_{n}],\quad n=1,\dots,N,\qquad\text{and}\qquad\Delta^{N}:=\Bigl\{p\in[0,1]^{N}:\sum_{n=1}^{N}p^{n}=1\Bigr\}.

Moreover, the generator matrix
of
Y
is denoted by
Q\in\mathbb{R}^{N\times N}
, so that
Y
can be written as

\mathrm{d}Y_{s}=QY_{s}\mathrm{d}s+\mathrm{d}M_{s},\quad Y_{0}=y_{0}\sim p_{0},\quad s\in[0,T],

where
M=(M_{s})_{s\in[0,T]}
is a càdlàg martingale satisfying
M_{s}=\mathbb{E}[Y_{s}-Y_{0}|\mathcal{F}_{s}]
,

s\in[0,T]
. Finally, we take as given a one-dimensional standard
(\mathbb{F},\mathbb{P})
-Brownian motion

B
independent of
Y
.

The financial market is assumed to consist of a risk-free asset with zero returns
and a single risky asset
S=(S_{s})_{s\in[0,T]}
which satisfies

\displaystyle\mathrm{d}S_{s}

\displaystyle=S_{s}\bigl[\mu^{\intercal}Y_{s}\mathrm{d}s+\sigma\mathrm{d}B_{s}\bigr],\quad S_{0}=s_{0},\quad s\in[0,T],

where
s_{0}>0
is the deterministic initial price,
\mu\in\mathbb{R}^{N}
are
the returns in the respective states of the economy, and
\sigma>0
is the
volatility.

We assume below that the agent observes the risky asset
S
, but does not observe the
state of the economy
Y
directly. Instead, the current state of the economy is
filtered from the risky asset price. In addition, we assume that the agent has
access to costly expert opinions of varying quality. As we will assume the cost
for an expert opinion to be strictly positive, the agent’s expert opinion strategies
are naturally modeled as impulse controls.

Definition 2.1
.

An expert opinion strategy
\nu=(\tau,q)
consists of an increasing sequence
of
\mathbb{F}
-stopping times
\tau=(\tau_{k})_{k\in\mathbb{N}}
, the expert opinion times,
and an
(\mathcal{F}_{\tau_{k}})_{k\in\mathbb{N}}
-adapted,
[0,1)
-valued process

q=(q_{k})_{k\in\mathbb{N}}
, the quality levels.
The set of all expert opinion strategies is denoted by
\mathcal{A}^{exp}_{pre}
.

Expert opinion strategies
\nu=(\tau,q)\in\mathcal{A}^{exp}_{pre}
reveal
additional information on the state of the economy
Y
as follows. Given
k\in\mathbb{N}
,
the
k
-th expert opinion
Z_{k}^{\nu}=Z_{k}^{\tau,q}
obtained at time

\tau_{k}
with quality level
q_{k}
is defined on
\{\tau_{k}<\infty\}
by

Z_{k}^{\nu}:=q_{k}\mu^{\intercal}Y_{\tau_{k}}+(1-q_{k})\mathcal{N}_{k},

(2)

where
\mathcal{N}=(\mathcal{N}_{k})_{k\in\mathbb{N}}
, the noise in the expert opinions, is a sequence
of independent and identically distributed random variables, independent of
Y

and
B
, and assumed absolutely continuous with respect to the Lebesgue measure
with continuous density
\phi:\mathbb{R}\to[0,\infty)
.
From (
2
), we see that the choice of
q_{k}=0
results
in purely noisy observation
\mathcal{N}_{k}
whereas for
q_{k}\uparrow 1
, effectively,
the true state of the economy is revealed to the agent.
To motivate the particular form of (
2
), note that, in
terms of information, observing
Z^{\nu}_{k}
with
q_{k}>0
is equivalent to observing

\frac{1}{q_{k}}Z_{k}^{\nu}=\mu^{\intercal}Y_{\tau_{k}}+\frac{1-q_{k}}{q_{k}}\mathcal{N}_{k}

(3)

since
q_{k}
is chosen by the agent, hence observable. The quality level
q_{k}
therefore controls the amount
of independent noise in the expert opinion. While
(
3
) has a more
straightforward economic interpretation, the advantage of working with
(
2
) instead is that this representation is continuous
in
q_{k}=0
, leading overall to cleaner arguments below.

At any given point in time, the agent’s observations consist of the risky asset price
S

and the acquired expert opinions
Z^{\nu}
up to that point. To define the observation
filtration rigorously, let us fix an expert opinion strategy

\nu=(\tau,q)\in\mathcal{A}^{exp}_{pre}
.
With this, we first introduce the filtrations

\mathcal{Y}^{S}_{s}:=\sigma\bigl(S_{r}:r\in[0,s]\bigr)\quad\text{and}\quad\mathcal{Z}^{\nu,k}_{s}:=\sigma\bigl((\mathbb{I}_{\{\tau_{k}\leq r\}},Z_{k}^{\nu}\mathbb{I}_{\{\tau_{k}\leq r\}}):r\in[0,s]\bigr)\text{ for all }k\in\mathbb{N},\quad s\in[0,T],

where
\mathbb{I}_{A}
denotes the indicator function of an event
A
. The observation
filtration
\mathcal{Y}^{\nu}=(\mathcal{Y}^{\nu}_{s})_{s\in[0,T]}
associated with an expert opinion
strategy
\nu
is then defined as

\mathcal{Y}^{\nu}_{s}=\mathcal{Y}^{S}_{s}\bigvee_{k\in\mathbb{N}}\mathcal{Z}^{\nu,k}_{s}\bigvee\mathfrak{N},\quad s\in[0,T],

where
\mathfrak{N}
denotes the system of
\mathbb{P}
-nullsets. Similarly, the observation filtration
restricted to the first
k\in\mathbb{N}_{0}
expert opinions is denoted by
\mathcal{Y}^{\nu;k}=(\mathcal{Y}^{\nu,k}_{s})_{s\in[0,T]}
and given by

\mathcal{Y}^{\nu,k}_{s}=\mathcal{Y}^{S}_{s}\bigvee_{j=1,\dots,k}\mathcal{Z}^{\nu,j}_{s}\bigvee\mathfrak{N},\quad s\in[0,T].

Remark 2.2
.

Special care needs to be taken as multiple expert opinions may be bought at the
same time instant. Both our definition of expert opinion strategies and of the information
filtration account for this possibility.

Trading is modeled in terms of fractions of wealth invested in the risky asset.
To be precise, a trading strategy is an
\mathbb{F}
-progressively measurable process
\pi
taking values
in the interval
\Pi:=[\underline{\pi},\overline{\pi}]\subset\mathbb{R}
with boundaries

\underline{\pi}<\overline{\pi}
and such that
0\in\Pi
. Given

\nu=(\tau,q)\in\mathcal{A}^{exp}_{pre}
, we write

\tau_{\infty}:=\lim_{k\to\infty}\tau_{k}
for the accumulation point of

\tau
. The wealth process
W^{w;u}=W^{u}=(W^{u}_{s})_{s\in[0,\tau_{\infty})\cap[0,T]}
associated with
u:=(\pi,\nu)

is defined as the solution of

\mathrm{d}W^{u}_{s}=\pi_{s}W^{u}_{s}\bigl[\mu^{\intercal}Y_{s}\mathrm{d}s+\sigma\mathrm{d}B_{s}\bigr]-\sum_{k\in\mathbb{N}}K(\tau_{k},q_{k})\delta_{\tau_{k}}(\mathrm{d}s),\quad W^{u}_{0}=w,\quad s\in[0,\tau_{\infty})\cap[0,T],

(4)

with
w\geq 0
the initial wealth and the function
K:[0,T]\times[0,1)\to(0,\infty)

modeling the cost of expert opinion acquisition. The cost function
K
is assumed
to be jointly continuous and strictly increasing in the second argument. Moreover,
we assume that there exists
K_{\min}>0
such that
K(t,q)\geq K(t,0)\geq K_{\min}

for all
(t,q)\in[0,T]\times[0,1)
and that
K(t,\,\cdot\,)
is coercive in that
for each wealth level
w\geq 0
there exists a quality level
q_{w}\in[0,1)
such that

K(t,q)\geq w,\quad(t,q)\in[0,T]\times[q_{w},1).

(5)

Observe that this assumption implies that
\lim_{q\uparrow 1}K(t,q)=\infty
for
all
t\in[0,T]
, meaning that revealing the true state of the economy is assumed to
be infinitely costly.

Definition 2.3
.

Let
u=(\pi,\nu)
, where
\pi
is a trading strategy and
\nu=(\tau,q)\in\mathcal{A}^{exp}_{pre}
.
We say that
u
is an admissible strategy for an initial wealth of
w\geq 0

provided that, for all
k\in\mathbb{N}
,

\pi
is
\mathcal{Y}^{\nu}
-progressively measurable,

\tau_{k}
is a
\mathcal{Y}^{\nu,k-1}
-stopping time,

q_{k}
is
\mathcal{Y}^{\nu,k-1}_{\tau_{k}}
-measurable, and

W^{w;u}\geq 0
.
The set of all such strategies is denoted by
\mathcal{A}(w)
. Given
u=(\pi,\nu)\in\mathcal{A}(w)
,
we also speak of
\pi
as an admissible trading strategy and
\nu

as an admissible expert opinion strategy.

We shall shortly see that the admissibility constraint
W^{w;u}\geq 0

guarantees that
\tau
does not accumulate before time
T
. Intuitively, this is
because the agent starts with a finite amount of wealth and each expert opinion
costs at least
K_{\min}>0
. Since the financial market is free of arbitrage
opportunities, this implies that only finitely many expert opinions can be
financed.

Before making this argument rigorous, it is convenient to fix some notation first.
Given
u\in\mathcal{A}(w)
, we subsequently always assume that the trading strategy in
u

is denoted by
\pi
and the expert opinion strategy is denoted by
\nu=(\tau,q)
.
Moreover, with a slight abuse of notation, we write
(\pi,\tau,q)
in place of

(\pi,(\tau,q))
.
While our notation carefully disentangles expert opinions even if they are
purchased simultaneously, it is sometimes more convenient to combine such simultaneous
purchases. This can be achieved as follows. Given
\nu=(\tau,q)\in\mathcal{A}^{exp}_{pre}
,
we let
\tilde{\tau}=(\tilde{\tau}_{k})_{{k\in\mathbb{N}}}
denote the jump times of the càdlàg process

I=(I_{s})_{s\in[0,\infty)}
given by

I_{s}:=\sum_{k\in\mathbb{N}}\mathbb{I}_{\{\tau_{k}\leq s\}},\quad s\in[0,\infty).

Clearly, any jump in
I
is due to at least one expert opinion and the accumulation
time
\tau_{\infty}
of
\tau
coincides with the first time that
I
takes
the value
+\infty
. Moreover, while
\tau
is in general increasing, the sequence

\tilde{\tau}
is even strictly increasing on
[0,\infty)
. Given
k\in\mathbb{N}
, the set of indices of
the expert opinions purchased at time
\tilde{\tau}_{k}
is given by

J_{k}:=\bigl\{j\in\mathbb{N}:\tau_{j}=\tilde{\tau}_{k}\bigr\}.

Note that any such set of indices can be written in the form
J_{k}=\{j_{k,1},j_{k,2},\dots,j_{k,|J_{k}|}\}
if
|J_{k}|
is finite or
J_{k}=\{j_{k,1},j_{k,2},\dots\}
otherwise, where by convention we assume that

j_{k,1}<j_{k,2}<\dots
are ordered. In fact, it holds that

j_{k,i+1}=j_{k,1}+i
for all
i=0,1,\dots,|J_{k}|-1
and

(J_{k})_{k\in\mathbb{N}}
is a (random) partition of
\mathbb{N}
. Put differently,
J_{k}
contains
the indices of the
k
-th batch of expert opinions and the
i
-th expert opinion
of that batch is the
j_{k,i}
-th expert opinion in total. For ease of notation,
we subsequently write

\tau_{k,i}:=\tau_{j_{k,i}},\quad q_{k,i}:=q_{j_{k,i}},\quad Z^{\nu}_{k,i}:=Z^{\nu}_{j_{k,i}},\quad\mathcal{N}_{k,i}:=\mathcal{N}_{j_{k,i}},\quad\mathcal{Y}^{\nu,k,i}:=\mathcal{Y}^{\nu,j_{k,i}}.

Given a trading strategy
\pi
, the wealth process
W^{u}
associated with

u=(\pi,\tau,q)
can be written as

\mathrm{d}W^{u}_{s}=\pi_{s}W^{u}_{s}\bigl[\mu^{\intercal}Y_{s}\mathrm{d}s+\sigma\mathrm{d}B_{s}\bigr]-\sum_{k\in\mathbb{N}}\sum_{i=1}^{|J_{k}|}K(\tilde{\tau}_{k},q_{k,i})\delta_{\tilde{\tau}_{k}}(\mathrm{d}s),\quad W^{u}_{0}=w_{0},\quad s\in[0,\tau_{\infty})\cap[0,T].

(6)

Finally, the wealth after the
i
-th expert opinion of the
k
-th batch is denoted by

W^{u}_{k,i}:=W^{u}_{k,i-1}-K(\tilde{\tau}_{k},q_{k,i}),\quad W^{u}_{k,0}:=W^{u}_{\tilde{\tau}_{k}-}\quad\text{on }\{\tilde{\tau}_{k}\leq T\}

(7)

under the convention
W^{u}_{0-}:=w_{0}
.
With the notation settled, let us now proceed by showing that admissible strategies
purchase only finitely many expert opinions.

Lemma 2.4
.

For any
w\geq 0
and
u=(\pi,\tau,q)\in\mathcal{A}(w)
we have
\tau_{\infty}>T
.

Proof.

Let
u\in\mathcal{A}(w)
and define
A_{k,i}:=\{\tau_{k,i}\leq T\}
for
i=1,\dots,|J_{k}|
and
k\in\mathbb{N}
.
Moreover, let

A:=\Bigl\{\lim_{k\to\infty}\tau_{k}\leq T\Bigr\}=\bigcap_{k\in\mathbb{N}}\bigcap_{i=1,\dots,|J_{k}|}A_{k,i}

and assume by contradiction that
\mathbb{P}[A]>0
. Recall that for
k\in\mathbb{N}
and
i=1,\dots,|J_{k}|

the
i
-th expert opinion in the
k
-th batch corresponds to the
j_{k,i}
-th expert opinion
overall. Since the cost for expert opinions is lower bounded by
K_{\min}>0
, we see that

0\leq W_{k,i}^{u}\leq w+\int_{0}^{\tau_{k,i}}\pi_{s}W_{s}\bigl[\mu^{\intercal}Y_{s}\mathrm{d}s+\sigma\mathrm{d}B_{s}\bigr]-j_{k,i}K_{\min}\quad\text{ on }A_{k,i}.

In particular, this implies

j_{k,i}K_{\min}-w\leq\int_{0}^{\tau_{k,i}}\pi_{s}W_{s}\bigl[\mu^{\intercal}Y_{s}\mathrm{d}s+\sigma\mathrm{d}B_{s}\bigr]\quad\text{on }A.

Now the financial market satisfies “No Free Lunch with Vanishing Risk”, see

[
14
, Theorem 8.2.1]
, and hence the sequence of integrals

\int_{0}^{\tau_{k,i}\wedge T}\pi_{s}W_{s}\bigl[\mu^{\intercal}Y_{s}\mathrm{d}s+\sigma\mathrm{d}B_{s}\bigr],\quad k\in\mathbb{N},

is bounded in probability; see
[
14
, Lemma 8.2.4]
. On the
other hand,
j_{k,i}K_{\min}-w\to\infty
on
A
as
k\to\infty
, from which
we obtain a contradiction to
\mathbb{P}[A]>0
.
∎

The previous lemma justifies modeling expert opinion purchases as impulse controls.
Moreover, it follows that for any admissible strategy
u\in\mathcal{A}(w)
, the wealth process

W^{u}
is well-defined on all of
[0,T]
. As such, the optimization problem in which
the agent maximizes expected utility of terminal wealth over all admissible strategies
is well-posed. More precisely, given a constant relative risk aversion parameter

\alpha\in(0,1)
, we denote by

U:[0,\infty)\to[0,\infty),\qquad w\mapsto U(w):=\frac{1}{1-\alpha}w^{1-\alpha},

the associated power utility function,
so that the expected utility of terminal wealth
is given by

\mathcal{J}(w;u):=\mathbb{E}\bigl[U\bigl(W^{w;u}_{T}\bigr)\bigr]\quad\text{subject to \eqref{eq:WEE}},\qquad w\geq 0,\;u\in\mathcal{A}(w).

(8)

With this, the optimization problem considered in this article can be written as

\sup_{u\in\mathcal{A}(w)}\mathcal{J}(w;u),\qquad w\geq 0.

(PO)

Formally, this is a stochastic control problem with partial observations featuring
both a “classical” absolutely continuous control
\pi
and an “impulse” control

(\tau,q)
. Moreover, due to the noise
\mathcal{N}
in the expert opinions, the impulse
controls have random consequences as in
[
21
,
26
]
.

3
Transformation to Full Information

As usual for stochastic control problems with partial observations, the first step
is to use filtering techniques to embed the original optimization
problem (
PO
) into another stochastic control problem
with full information at the cost of an enlarged state space. The necessity for
such an embedding arises from the
fact that
Y
is not adapted to the observation filtration
\mathcal{Y}^{\nu}
,
hence the drift
\pi W^{u}\mu^{\intercal}Y
of the wealth process
W^{u}
given
in (
4
) is, in general, also not adapted to
\mathcal{Y}^{\nu}
. As such, classical
solution techniques based on dynamic programming are not directly applicable and
it is more convenient to transform the problem to one with full information.

The first step in the transformation is the characterization of the conditional distribution
of the hidden Markov chain
Y
given the observations
\mathcal{Y}^{\nu}
for any strategy

u=(\pi,\nu)=(\pi,\tau,q)\in\mathcal{A}(w)
. For this, we denote by
p^{\nu,n}
the
optional projection of
\mathbb{I}_{\{Y=e_{n}\}}
onto
\mathcal{Y}^{\nu}
for
n=1,\dots,N
.
In particular, it holds that

p^{\nu,n}_{s}=\mathbb{P}\bigl[Y_{s}=e_{n}\big|\mathcal{Y}^{\nu}_{s}\bigr],\quad n=1,\dots,N,\;s\in[0,T],

and the vector-valued process
p^{\nu}=(p^{\nu,1},\dots,p^{\nu,N})^{\intercal}

is
\mathcal{Y}^{\nu}
-optional and takes values in
\Delta^{N}
.
In line with (
7
), we furthermore define

p^{\nu,n}_{k,i}:=\mathbb{P}\bigl[Y_{\tau_{k,i}}=e_{n}\big|\mathcal{Y}^{\nu,k,i}_{\tilde{\tau}_{k}}\bigr],\quad p^{\nu,n}_{k,0}:=p^{\nu,n}_{\tilde{\tau}_{k}-},\quad k\in\mathbb{N},\;i=1,\dots,|J_{k}|,\;n=1,\dots,N,

(9)

again with the convention
p^{\nu}_{0-}:=p_{0}
, where we recall that
p_{0}
is
the initial (unconditional) distribution of
Y_{0}
. Next, we introduce the innovations
process
I^{\nu}=(I^{\nu}_{s})_{s\in[0,T]}
as

\mathrm{d}I^{\nu}_{s}:=\frac{1}{\sigma}\bigl[\mathrm{d}R_{s}-\mu^{\intercal}p^{\nu}_{s}\mathrm{d}s\bigr]=\mathrm{d}B_{s}+\frac{1}{\sigma}\mu^{\intercal}(Y_{s}-p^{\nu}_{s})\mathrm{d}s,\quad I_{0}^{\nu}=0,\quad s\in[0,T],

(10)

where
R:=\log(S)
denotes the return process, which, by Itô’s formula and
(
10
), satisfies

\mathrm{d}R_{s}=\Bigl[\mu^{\intercal}Y_{s}-\frac{1}{2}\sigma^{2}\Bigr]\mathrm{d}s+\sigma\mathrm{d}B_{s}=\Bigl[\mu^{\intercal}p^{\nu}_{s}-\frac{1}{2}\sigma^{2}\Bigr]\mathrm{d}s+\sigma\mathrm{d}I^{\nu}_{s},\quad R_{0}=\log(s_{0}),\quad s\in[0,T].

A classical result in filtering theory guarantees that for any
u\in\mathcal{A}(w)
, the innovations
process
I^{\nu}
is a standard
(\mathcal{Y}^{\nu},\mathbb{P})
-Brownian motion; see e.g.
[
9
, Lemma 22.1.7]
for the case in which the observation
filtration is generated by a diffusion. Observe that, here, we do make use of the fact
that the price process
S
and the return process
R
generate the same filtration;
see
[
17
, Section 2]
.

We can now rewrite the dynamics of the wealth process using the innovations process
as driving noise, which results in the drift of the wealth process to be adapted
to the observation filtration. More precisely, for any
u=(\pi,\nu)\in\mathcal{A}(w)
,
the wealth process
W^{u}
can be written as

\mathrm{d}W_{s}^{u}=\pi_{s}W^{u}_{s}\bigl[\mu^{\intercal}p_{s}^{\nu}\mathrm{d}s+\sigma\mathrm{d}I^{\nu}_{s}\bigr]-\sum_{k\in\mathbb{N}}\sum_{i=1}^{|J_{k}|}K(\tilde{\tau}_{k},q_{k,i})\delta_{\tilde{\tau}_{k}}(\mathrm{d}s),\quad W_{0}^{u}=w,\quad s\in[0,T].

(11)

From this representation of the wealth process, we immediately see that the drift

\pi W^{u}\mu^{\intercal}p^{\nu}
is
\mathcal{Y}^{\nu}
-adapted. However, we are still missing
the dynamics of the conditional probability vector
p^{\nu}
to get a full description
of the state. As in
[
30
, Theorem 9.1]
,
the dynamic evolution of
p^{\nu}
in between the arrival of new expert opinions is given by

\mathrm{d}p^{\nu}_{s}=Q^{\intercal}p^{\nu}_{s}\mathrm{d}s+\frac{1}{\sigma}\bigl(\diag[\mu]-\mu^{\intercal}p^{\nu}_{s}\bigr)p^{\nu}_{s}\mathrm{d}I^{\nu}_{s},\quad p^{\nu}_{\tilde{\tau}_{k}}=p^{\nu}_{k,|J_{k}|},\quad s\in[\tilde{\tau}_{k},\tilde{\tau}_{k+1})\cap[0,T],

for all
k\in\mathbb{N}
, where
\diag[\mu]\in\mathbb{R}^{N\times N}
is the diagonal matrix
with diagonal entries given by
\mu
.
Moreover, for
k\in\mathbb{N}
and
i=1,\dots,|J_{k}|
, the
arrival of the new expert opinion

Z_{k,i}^{\nu}=q_{k,i}\mu^{\intercal}Y_{\tilde{\tau}_{k}}+(1-q_{k,i})\mathcal{N}_{k,i}

leads to an update of the conditional probabilities, which can
be computed using Bayes’ rule, that is

\displaystyle p^{\nu,n}_{k,i}

\displaystyle=\mathbb{P}\bigl[Y_{\tilde{\tau}_{k}}=e_{n}\,\big|\,\mathcal{Y}^{\nu,k,i}_{\tilde{\tau}_{k}}\bigr]

\displaystyle=\mathbb{P}\bigl[Y_{\tilde{\tau}_{k}}=e_{n}\,\big|\,\mathcal{Y}^{\nu,k,i-1}_{\tilde{\tau}_{k}}\vee Z_{k,i}^{\nu}\bigr]

\displaystyle=\frac{\mathbb{P}\bigl[Z_{k,i}^{\nu}\in\mathrm{d}z\,\big|\,\mathcal{Y}^{\nu,k,i-1}_{\tilde{\tau}_{k}}\vee Y_{\tilde{\tau}_{k}}=e_{n}\bigr]\mathbb{P}\bigl[Y_{\tilde{\tau}_{k}}=e_{n}\,\big|\,\mathcal{Y}^{\nu,k,i-1}_{\tilde{\tau}_{k}}\bigr]}{\mathbb{P}\bigl[Z_{k,i}^{\nu}\in\mathrm{d}z\,\big|\,\mathcal{Y}^{\nu,k,i-1}_{\tilde{\tau}_{k}}\bigr]}

\displaystyle=\frac{\mathbb{P}\bigl[Z_{k,i}^{\nu}\in\mathrm{d}z\,\big|\,\mathcal{Y}^{\nu,k,i-1}_{\tilde{\tau}_{k}}\vee Y_{\tilde{\tau}_{k}}=e_{n}\bigr]\mathbb{P}\bigl[Y_{\tilde{\tau}_{k}}=e_{n}\,\big|\,\mathcal{Y}^{\nu,k,i-1}_{\tilde{\tau}_{k}}\bigr]}{\sum_{m=1}^{N}\mathbb{P}\bigl[Z^{\nu}_{k,i}\in\mathrm{d}z,Y_{\tilde{\tau}_{k}}=e_{m}\,\big|\,\mathcal{Y}^{\nu,k,i-1}_{\tilde{\tau}_{k}}\bigr]}

\displaystyle=\frac{\phi\bigl((Z_{k,i}^{\nu}-q_{k,i}\mu^{n})/(1-q_{k,i})\bigr)p_{k,i-1}^{\nu,n}}{\sum_{m=1}^{N}\phi\bigl((Z_{k,i}^{\nu}-q_{k,i}\mu^{m})/(1-q_{k,i})\bigr)p_{k,i-1}^{\nu,m}}.

In light of this representation, we define the update function for the conditional
probabilities as
\Xi:\mathbb{R}\times\Delta^{N}\times[0,1)\to\Delta^{N}

component-wise for each
n=1,\dots,N
by

\Xi^{n}(z,p,q):=\frac{\phi\bigl((z-q\mu^{n})/(1-q)\bigr)p^{n}}{\sum_{m=1}^{N}\phi\bigl((z-q\mu^{m})/(1-q)\bigr)p^{m}},\quad(z,p,q)\in\mathbb{R}\times\Delta^{N}\times[0,1).

Observe that
\Xi
is continuous by continuity of
\phi
.
Next, the update can equivalently be expressed as

\Xi^{n}(z,p,q)-p^{n}=\zeta^{n}(z,p,q)p^{n},

where the continuous function
\zeta:\mathbb{R}\times\Delta^{N}\times[0,1)\to\mathbb{R}^{N}
is defined component-wise by

\zeta^{n}(z,p,q):=\frac{\phi\bigl((z-q\mu^{n})/(1-q)\bigr)}{\sum_{m=1}^{N}\phi\bigl((z-q\mu^{m})/(1-q)\bigr)p^{m}}-1,\quad(z,p,q)\in\times\mathbb{R}\times\Delta^{N}\times[0,1),\;n=1,\dots,N.

(12)

Remark 3.1
.

The conditional distribution of
Y
given
\mathcal{Y}^{\nu}
can be described by a finite-dimensional
system of equations since
Y
takes only finitely many values. Other cases in which
the filter is finite-dimensional are the Bayesian case of
Y
being a static random variable
as for example in
[
25
]
or the Gaussian case of
Y
being an
Ornstein–Uhlenbeck process as for example in
[
6
]
.

Having derived the dynamics of
p^{\nu}
essentially completes the transformation
of the partial observation problem to an equivalent full information problem.
However, in order to apply the tools of dynamic programming, we still have to
embed the full information problem into an entire family of optimization problems
parametrized in terms of the initial time
t\in[0,T]
, initial wealth
w\in[0,\infty)

at time
t
, and initial conditional distribution
p\in\Delta^{N}
of
Y
at time
t
.
This embedding is standard and outlined here merely for the sake of introducing
the proper notation.

We begin by setting

\mathbb{S}:=[0,\infty)\times\Delta^{N},\quad\mathbb{S}_{T}:=[0,T)\times\mathbb{S},\quad\text{and}\quad\overline{\mathbb{S}}_{T}:=[0,T]\times\mathbb{S}

for the state space, the time-augmented state space, and the time-augmented state
space including terminal time, respectively. Elements of
\mathbb{S}
are denoted by
x=(w,p)=(w,p^{1},\dots,p^{N})
and we occasionally switch between these representations
without explicitly mentioning this.
The drift of the
state process is given by
f=(f^{w},f^{p}):\mathbb{S}\times\Pi\to\mathbb{R}\times\mathbb{R}^{N}
with

f^{w}(w,p,\pi):=\pi w\mu^{\intercal}p\quad\text{and}\quad f^{p}(p):=Q^{\intercal}p,\quad(w,p,\pi)\in\mathbb{S}\times\Pi.

(13)

Similarly, the diffusion coefficient

\Sigma=(\sigma^{w},\sigma^{p}):\mathbb{S}\times\Pi\to\mathbb{R}\times\mathbb{R}^{N}
is defined through

\sigma^{w}(w,\pi):=\pi w\sigma\quad\text{and}\quad\sigma^{p}(p):=\frac{1}{\sigma}(\diag(\mu)-\mu^{\intercal}p)p.\quad(w,p,\pi)\in\mathbb{S}\times\Pi.

(14)

Jumps in the state process due to the purchase of new expert opinions are given in
terms of the continuous function

\gamma=(\gamma^{w},\gamma^{p}):\mathbb{R}\times[0,T]\times\Delta^{N}\times[0,1)\to\mathbb{R}\times\mathbb{R}^{N}
given by

\gamma^{w}(z,t,p,q):=-K(t,q)\quad\text{and}\quad\gamma^{p}(z,t,p,q)=\zeta(z,p,q)\diag[p]

for
(z,t,p,q)\in\mathbb{R}\times[0,T]\times\Delta^{N}\times[0,1)
.
For any
(t,x)=(t,w,p)\in\overline{\mathbb{S}}_{T}
, we subsequently write
\mathcal{A}(x):=\mathcal{A}(w)

and given an
admissible strategy
u\in\mathcal{A}(x)
, the
\mathbb{S}
-valued state process

X^{u;t,x}=(X^{u;t,x}_{s})_{s\in[t,T]}
is given as the unique strong solution of

\mathrm{d}X^{u;t,x}_{s}=f\bigl(X^{u;t,x}_{s},\pi_{s}\bigr)\mathrm{d}s+\Sigma\bigl(X^{u;t,x}_{s},\pi_{s}\bigr)\mathrm{d}I^{\nu}_{s}+\sum_{k\in\mathbb{N}}\sum_{i=1}^{|J_{k}|}\gamma\bigl(Z^{\nu}_{k,i},\tilde{\tau}_{k},p^{\nu}_{k,i-1},q_{k,i}\bigr)\delta_{\tilde{\tau}_{k}}(\mathrm{d}s)

(15)

for
s\in[t,T]
with initial condition
X^{u;t,x}_{t}=x
, and
where we impose the convention
p^{\nu}_{k,0}:=p^{\nu}_{\tilde{\tau}_{k}-}
.
In line with the notation introduced above,
we often write
X^{u}
in place of
X^{u;t,x}
if the initial condition is clear from
the context, we generally denote the components of
X^{u}
by
X^{u}=(W^{u},p^{\nu})
, and
for
i=1,\dots,|J_{k}|
and
k\in\mathbb{N}
we write

X_{k,i}^{u}:=(W_{k,i}^{u},p_{k,i}^{\nu})
for the state process after the

i
-th expert opinion of the
k
-th batch.

Lemma 3.2
.

There exists
C>0
such that

\mathbb{E}\Bigl[\sup_{s\in[t,T]}\bigl|X^{u;t,x}_{s}\bigr|^{2}\Bigr]\leq C\bigl(1+|x|^{2}\bigr)

for any
(t,x)\in\overline{\mathbb{S}}_{T}
and any admissible strategy
u\in\mathcal{A}(x)
.

Proof.

Writing
X^{u}=X^{u;t,x}
and
X^{u}=(W^{u},p^{\nu})
, we first make use of the fact
that all entries of
p^{\nu}
are
[0,1]
-valued to estimate

\bigl|X^{u}_{s}\bigr|^{2}\leq(N+1)\Bigl[\bigl|W^{u}_{s}\bigr|^{2}+\sum_{n=1}^{N}\bigl|p^{\nu,n}\bigr|^{2}\Bigr]\leq(N+1)\bigl|W^{u}_{s}\bigr|^{2}+N(N+1).

Next, we note that
W^{u}\leq\hat{W}^{u}
, where
\hat{W}^{u}
corresponds to the wealth
process
W^{u}
in the absence of expert opinion cost, that is

\mathrm{d}\hat{W}_{s}^{u}=f^{w}\bigl(\hat{W}^{u}_{s},p^{\nu}_{s},\pi_{s}\bigr)\mathrm{d}s+\sigma^{w}\bigl(\hat{W}^{u}_{s},\pi_{s}\bigr)\mathrm{d}I^{\nu}_{s},\qquad\hat{W}^{u}_{t}=w,\qquad s\in[t,T].

Since
\Pi
and
\Delta^{N}
are bounded, we conclude that
\hat{W}
solves a
Lipschitz SDE with Lipschitz constant independent of

u
. But then a standard moment estimate such as

[
27
, Corollary 2.2.12]
shows that there exists a constant

K>0
which does not depend on
u
and
t
such that

\mathbb{E}\Bigl[\sup_{s\in[t,T]}\bigl|X^{u}_{s}\bigr|^{2}\Bigr]\leq N(N+1)+(N+1)\mathbb{E}\Bigl[\sup_{s\in[t,T]}\bigl|\hat{W}^{u}_{s}\bigr|^{2}\Bigr]\leq N(N+1)+(N+1)K\bigl(1+|w|^{2}\bigr).

We conclude since
|w|\leq|x|
.
∎

With this, we can now formulate the dynamic full information problem as follows.
The expected utility of terminal wealth for a given initial condition

(t,x)\in\overline{\mathbb{S}}_{T}
and admissible control
u\in\mathcal{A}(x)
is

\mathcal{J}(u;t,x):=\mathbb{E}\bigl[U\bigl(W^{u;tx}_{T}\bigr)\bigr]\quad\text{subject to \eqref{eq:state-aux},}

and the value function, in turn, is defined as

V:\overline{\mathbb{S}}_{T}\to[0,\infty),\quad(t,x)\mapsto V(t,x):=\sup_{u\in\mathcal{A}(x)}\mathcal{J}(u;t,x).

(FI)

4
Construction of the Viscosity Solution of the HJBQVI

In this section we begin with our characterization of the value function as the
unique viscosity solution of the Hamilton–Jacobi–Bellman quasi-variational
inequalities (HJBQVI) associated with the full information problem. For this,
we employ a version of the stochastic Perron’s method; see

[
1
,
2
]
for early developments
and
[
4
]
for impulse control problems.
Similarly to
[
3
,
4
,
5
]
, we choose a suitable class of superharmonic functions for the
set of stochastic supersolutions.
A classical argument is employed to show that the pointwise minimum
of the set of stochastic supersolution is a viscosity subsolution of the HJBQVI.
In contrast to the classical stochastic Perron’s method for impulse control problems

[
3
,
4
]
, however, we then follow a similar argument
as in
[
5
]
and show directly that the pointwise minimum is in
fact the unique continuous viscosity solution of the HJBQVI. As most of the
technical arguments in this section are well-known from the literature, we defer the
proofs to Appendix
A
.

Note that, in order to show that the HJBQVI characterizes the value function,
after this section we are still left with showing that the pointwise minimum of
stochastic supersolutions coincides with the value function. This is achieved by
means of a novel argument proving that the full information problem can be
reduced to an exit time control problem. Once this is achieved, we use a
verification theorem to establish the viscosity characterization of the value
function
V
and construct optimal strategies in the process.

4.1
The HJBQVI

To introduce the HJBQVI, we write
\Gamma:\mathbb{R}\times\overline{\mathbb{S}}_{T}\times[0,1)\to\mathbb{S}

for the continuous function

\Gamma(z,t,x,q):=x+\gamma(z,t,p,q),\quad(z,t,x,q)\in\mathbb{R}\times\overline{\mathbb{S}}_{T}\times[0,1),

(16)

which maps the current state
x
to the new state
\Gamma(z,t,x,q)
after
the purchase of an expert opinion
z
of quality
q
at time
t
. Observe
that since the expert opinion cost
K
is bounded away from zero, it is possible
that the agent does not have sufficient wealth to buy any expert opinion at all.
In light of this, we subsequently introduce the set-valued mapping

\mathcal{D}(t,x):=\bigl\{q\in[0,1):w\geq K(t,q)\bigr\},\quad(t,x)=(t,w,p)\in\overline{\mathbb{S}}_{T}.

With this, the subset of the state space
\overline{\mathbb{S}}_{T}
on which the agent cannot
purchase any expert opinions is given by

\overline{\mathbb{S}}_{T}^{\emptyset}:=\bigl\{(t,x)\in\overline{\mathbb{S}}_{T}:\mathcal{D}(t,x)=\emptyset\bigr\}=\bigl\{(t,w,p)\in\overline{\mathbb{S}}_{T}:w<K(t,0)\bigr\}.

Observe that the complement is the closed set given by

\overline{\mathbb{S}}_{T}\setminus\overline{\mathbb{S}}_{T}^{\emptyset}=\bigl\{(t,w,p)\in\overline{\mathbb{S}}_{T}:w\geq K(t,0)\bigr\}.

Next, note that monotonicity and continuity of
K

imply that there exists a continuous function
\chi:\overline{\mathbb{S}}_{T}\to\mathbb{R}

such that

\mathcal{D}(t,x)=[0,\chi(t,x)],\quad(t,x)\in\overline{\mathbb{S}}_{T}.

In particular, by monotonicity of
K
, it holds that
\chi<0
on

\overline{\mathbb{S}}_{T}^{\emptyset}
and
\chi
is determined implicitly by

K\bigl(t,\chi(t,x)\bigr)=w,\quad(t,x)=(t,w,p)\in\overline{\mathbb{S}}_{T}\setminus\overline{\mathbb{S}}_{T}^{\emptyset}.

Finally, note that
\chi<1
since
K
is assumed coercive.

Now fix
(t,x)=(t,w,p)\in\overline{\mathbb{S}}_{T}
. By definition, the optimal expected
utility the agent can obtain by starting from this state is
V(t,x)
. If the
agent decides to purchase an expert opinion of the form
Z=q\mu^{\intercal}Y_{t}+(1-q)\mathcal{N}
for a quality level
q\in[0,1)
and a realization of noise
\mathcal{N}\sim\phi
,
the expected optimal expected utility after this purchase is

\mathbb{E}\bigl[V\bigl(t,\Gamma(Z,t,x,q)\bigr)\bigr]=\sum_{n=1}^{N}p^{n}\mathbb{E}\Bigl[V\Bigl(t,\Gamma\bigl(q\mu^{n}+(1-q)\mathcal{N},t,x,q\bigr)\Bigr)\Bigr].

In general, such a purchase is suboptimal, suggesting that

V(t,x)\geq\sum_{n=1}^{N}p^{n}\mathbb{E}\Bigl[V\Bigl(t,\Gamma\bigl(q\mu^{n}+(1-q)\mathcal{N},t,x,q\bigr)\Bigr)\Bigr],

with equality only if the purchase of
Z
is optimal. This leads to the definition
of the intervention operator
1
1

1

We use the convention

\sup\{\emptyset\}=-1
to handle the case
\mathcal{D}(t,x)=\emptyset
.

\mathcal{M}[v](t,x):=\sup_{q\in\mathcal{D}(t,x)}\sum_{n=1}^{N}p^{n}\mathbb{E}\Bigl[v\Bigl(t,\Gamma\bigl(q\mu^{n}+(1-q)\mathcal{N},t,x,q\bigr)\Bigr)\Bigr],\quad(t,x)\in\overline{\mathbb{S}}_{T},

(17)

acting on measurable non-negative functions
v:\overline{\mathbb{S}}_{T}\to[0,\infty)
. In

Next, we denote by
\mathcal{H}:\mathbb{S}\times\Pi\times\mathbb{R}^{1+N}\times\mathcal{S}^{1+N}\to\mathbb{R}
the
Hamiltonian of the state process given by

\mathcal{H}(x,\pi,r,M):=f(x,\pi)^{\intercal}r+\frac{1}{2}\trace\bigl[\Sigma(x,\pi)\Sigma(x,\pi)^{\intercal}M\bigr],\quad(x,\pi,r,M)\in\mathbb{S}\times\Pi\times\mathbb{R}^{1+N}\times\mathcal{S}^{1+N},

theory of viscosity solutions, we denote by

\mathrm{LSC}(\overline{\mathbb{S}}_{T})

standard definition of viscosity solutions for discontinuous HJBQVI as in, e.g.,

Definition 4.1
.

A locally bounded function
v:\overline{\mathbb{S}}_{T}\to\mathbb{R}
is a viscosity subsolution
of (

(t,x)\in\mathbb{S}_{T}
and any
\varphi\in\mathcal{C}^{1,2}(\mathbb{S}_{T})

v^{*}(t,x)=\varphi(t,x)

\min\Bigl\{F\bigl(x,\varphi_{t}(t,x),\mathrm{D}_{x}\varphi(t,x),\mathrm{D}_{x}^{2}\varphi(t,x)\bigr),v^{*}(t,x)-\mathcal{M}[v^{*}]^{*}(t,x)\Bigr\}\leq 0.

Similarly,
v
is a viscosity supersolution of (
HJBQVI
) if, for any

(t,x)\in\mathbb{S}_{T}
and any
\varphi\in\mathcal{C}^{1,2}(\mathbb{S}_{T})
such that
v_{*}-\varphi
attains
a global minimum at
(t,x)
with
v_{*}(t,x)=\varphi(t,x)
, we have

\min\Bigl\{F\bigl(x,\varphi_{t}(t,x),\mathrm{D}_{x}\varphi(t,x),\mathrm{D}_{x}^{2}\varphi(t,x)\bigr),v_{*}(t,x)-\mathcal{M}[v_{*}]_{*}(t,x)\Bigr\}\geq 0.

Finally,

is a viscosity solution of (
HJBQVI
) if it is both a viscosity
subsolution and a viscosity supersolution of (
HJBQVI

The main reason why we repeat the definition here is to explicitly point out the,
at first sight, rather odd double use of the semi-continuous envelopes in

\mathcal{M}[v^{*}]^{*}
and
\mathcal{M}[v_{*}]_{*}

operator does, in general, not preserve semi-continuity. More precisely, as the
following result shows,
\mathcal{M}
preserves upper semi-continuity so that

\mathcal{M}[v^{*}]^{*}=\mathcal{M}[v^{*}]
, but the same is generally not true for the lower
semi-continuous envelope.

Lemma 4.2
.

Let
v:\overline{\mathbb{S}}_{T}\to[0,\infty)
. Then the following statements hold.

i)

If
v\in\mathrm{USC}(\overline{\mathbb{S}}_{T})
, then
\mathcal{M}[v]\in\mathrm{USC}(\overline{\mathbb{S}}_{T})
.

ii)

If
v\in\mathrm{LSC}(\overline{\mathbb{S}}_{T})
, then the restrictions of
\mathcal{M}[v]
to
\overline{\mathbb{S}}_{T}^{\emptyset}

and
\overline{\mathbb{S}}_{T}\setminus\overline{\mathbb{S}}_{T}^{\emptyset}
are lower semi-continuous
on their respective domains.

The proof of this result is standard and follows along the same lines as

[
5
, Lemma 5.1]
. There is a minor additional challenge since
the set of quality levels
[0,1)
is not compact, which can be overcome using
that the cost function
K
is coercive. The proof is reported in Appendix
A
.

4.2
The Comparison Principle

The first step in the construction of the unique viscosity solution of the HJBQVI
is a comparison principle which we shall eventually use to prove uniqueness and
continuity of the viscosity solution. The arguments follow the standard machinery
as in
[
3
,
5
]
by first constructing a strict
supersolution of the HJBQVI and then proving the comparison principle via
a perturbation method.

Lemma 4.3
.

For any choice of
\beta\in(0,\alpha]
, there exist constants
A,C>0

and a strictly positive continuous function
\kappa:\overline{\mathbb{S}}_{T}\to(0,\infty)
such that

\psi:\overline{\mathbb{S}}_{T}\to(0,\infty),\quad(t,x)\mapsto\psi(t,x):=\Bigl(A+\frac{1}{1-\beta}w^{1-\beta}\Bigr)e^{C(T-t)}

is a strict supersolution of the HJBQVI in the sense that
\psi(T,w,p)\geq U(w)

for all
(p,w)\in\mathbb{S}
and

\min\Bigl\{F\bigl(x,\psi_{t}(t,x),\mathrm{D}_{x}\psi(t,x),\mathrm{D}_{x}^{2}\psi(t,x)\bigr),\psi(t,x)-\mathcal{M}[\psi](t,x)\Bigr\}\geq\kappa(t,x)>0,\quad(t,x)\in\mathbb{S}_{T}.

Remark 4.4
.

The proof of Lemma
4.3
reveals that
the function
\psi
with
\beta=\alpha
and
A=0
is supersolution provided that

C>0
is chosen sufficiently large. It is, however, in general no longer a strict
supersolution.

With the strict supersolution at hand, we can derive the following perturbation
result which goes back to
[
23
, Lemma 3.2]
.

Lemma 4.5
.

Fix
\rho>1
, let
u\in\mathrm{USC}(\overline{\mathbb{S}}_{T}),v\in\mathrm{LSC}(\overline{\mathbb{S}}_{T})
, and
\psi
,
\kappa
as in
Lemma
4.3
. Define the perturbations

u^{\rho}:=\frac{\rho+1}{\rho}u-\frac{1}{\rho}\psi,\quad v^{\rho}:=\frac{\rho-1}{\rho}v+\frac{1}{\rho}\psi.

(18)

If
u
is a viscosity subsolution of (
HJBQVI
), the perturbation
u^{\rho}

is a viscosity subsolution of the perturbed equation

\min\Bigl\{F\bigl(x,u^{\rho}_{t}(t,x),\mathrm{D}_{x}u^{\rho}(t,x),\mathrm{D}_{x}^{2}u^{\rho}(t,x)\bigr),u^{\rho}(t,x)-\mathcal{M}[u^{\rho}]^{*}(t,x)\Bigr\}+\frac{1}{\rho}\kappa(t,x)=0,\;(t,x)\in\mathbb{S}_{T}.

Similarly, if
v
is a viscosity supersolution of (
HJBQVI
), the perturbation

v^{\rho}
is a viscosity supersolution of the perturbed equation

\min\Bigl\{F\bigl(x,v^{\rho}_{t}(t,x),\mathrm{D}_{x}v^{\rho}(t,x),\mathrm{D}_{x}^{2}v^{\rho}(t,x)\bigr),v^{\rho}(t,x)-\mathcal{M}[v^{\rho}]_{*}(t,x)\Bigr\}-\frac{1}{\rho}\kappa(t,x)=0,\;(t,x)\in\mathbb{S}_{T}.

The role of the perturbation
u^{\rho}
is to to control the growth of the subsolution

u
without sacrificing the subsolution property. Using this, we can establish
the following comparison principle.

Theorem 4.6
.

Let
u\in\mathrm{USC}(\overline{\mathbb{S}}_{T})
be a viscosity subsolution and
v\in\mathrm{LSC}(\overline{\mathbb{S}}_{T})
be a viscosity supersolution
of (
HJBQVI
) for which there exists
K>0
such that

0\leq u(t,x),v(t,x)\leq K\bigl(1+|x|^{1-\alpha}\bigr),\quad(t,x)\in\overline{\mathbb{S}}_{T}.

(19)

If, in addition,
u
and
v
satisfy the boundary conditions

u(T,x)\leq v(T,x),\quad x\in\mathbb{S},\quad\text{and}\quad u(t,0,p)=0,\quad(t,p)\in[0,T]\times\Delta^{N},

(20)

then
u\leq v
on all of
\overline{\mathbb{S}}_{T}
.

4.3
Construction of the Viscosity Solution

We are now ready for the construction of the unique viscosity solution
of the HJBQVI. The ideas follow
[
3
,
4
]

and especially
[
5
]
with some minor additional
considerations required due to the presence of the classical control, i.e. the
trading strategy
\pi
. We begin by introducing a suitable notion of stochastic
supersolutions.

Definition 4.7
.

The set of stochastic supersolutions of (
HJBQVI
), denoted by
\mathcal{V}^{+}
,
is the set of functions
h:\overline{\mathbb{S}}_{T}\to\mathbb{R}
satisfying

(
\mathcal{V}^{+}_{1}
)

h\in\mathrm{USC}(\overline{\mathbb{S}}_{T})
;

(
\mathcal{V}^{+}_{2}
)

h
is lower bounded and there exists
K>0
such that

h(t,x)\leq K\bigl(1+|x|^{1-\alpha}\bigr),\quad(t,x)\in\overline{\mathbb{S}}_{T};

(
\mathcal{V}^{+}_{3}
)

h
satisfies the terminal inequality

h(T,x)\geq U(w),\quad x=(p,w)\in\mathbb{S};

(
\mathcal{V}^{+}_{4}
)

for each
(t,x)\in\overline{\mathbb{S}}_{T}
, each admissible strategy

u=(\pi,\nu)\in\mathcal{A}(x)
,
each
k\in\mathbb{N}
, each pair of
\mathcal{Y}^{\nu}
-stopping times
\theta\leq\rho
taking values in

[\tilde{\tau}_{k},\tilde{\tau}_{k+1}]\cap[t,T]
, and each
\mathcal{Y}^{\nu}_{\theta}
-measurable

\mathbb{S}
-valued random variable
\xi
with
\mathbb{E}[|\xi|^{2}]<\infty
, it holds that

h(\theta,\xi)\geq\mathbb{E}\bigl[h(\rho,X^{u;\theta,\xi}_{\rho-})\big|\mathcal{Y}^{\nu}_{\theta}\bigr];

(
\mathcal{V}^{+}_{5}
)

h\geq\mathcal{M}[h]
on
\overline{\mathbb{S}}_{T}
.

It is easy to see that
\mathcal{V}^{+}
is non-empty. Indeed, as emphasized in
Remark
4.4
, the function
\psi
with
\beta=\alpha

and
A=0
is a supersolution of the HJBQVI if
C>0
is chosen sufficiently large.
This function obviously satisfies
(\mathcal{V}^{+}_{1})
, it satisfies the growth condition

(\mathcal{V}^{+}_{2})

\psi(t,x)\leq K\bigl(1+|x|^{1-\alpha}\bigr),\quad(t,x)\in\overline{\mathbb{S}}_{T},

for
K:=e^{CT}/(1-\alpha)
and the boundary conditions
(\mathcal{V}^{+}_{3})

\psi(T,x)=U(w),\quad x=(w,p)\in\mathbb{S},\quad\text{and}\quad\psi(t,0)=0,\quad t\in[0,T].

Finally,
(\mathcal{V}^{+}_{4})
is an immediate consequence of an application of Itô’s formula
and using that
\psi
is a supersolution of the HJBQVI, whereas
(\mathcal{V}^{+}_{5})
follows
directly from the supersolution property and the fact that
\psi(T,x)=U(w)
is
strictly increasing in
w
.

Moreover, let us briefly show that any
h\in\mathcal{V}^{+}
dominates the value function.
For this, fix
(t,x)\in\overline{\mathbb{S}}_{T}
and
u\in\mathcal{A}(x)
. By
(\mathcal{V}^{+}_{4})
and pathwise uniqueness,
we see that

\mathbb{E}\bigl[h\bigl(\tilde{\tau}_{k},X^{u;t,x}_{\tilde{\tau}_{k}}\bigr)\bigr]\geq\mathbb{E}\bigl[h\bigl(T\wedge\tilde{\tau}_{k+1},X^{u;t,x}_{T\wedge\tilde{\tau}_{k+1}-}\bigr)\bigr],\quad\text{on }\{t\leq\tilde{\tau}_{k}\leq T\},\quad k\in\mathbb{N}_{0},

where
\tilde{\tau}_{0}:=0
. Moreover, from multiple applications of
(\mathcal{V}^{+}_{5})
it follows that

\mathbb{E}\bigl[h\bigl(T\wedge\tilde{\tau}_{k+1},X^{u;t,x}_{T\wedge\tilde{\tau}_{k+1}-}\bigr)\bigr]\geq\mathbb{E}\bigl[h\bigl(T\wedge\tilde{\tau}_{k+1},X^{u;t,x}_{T\wedge\tilde{\tau}_{k+1}}\bigr)\bigr].

Combining these two inequalities and finally using the terminal inequality
(\mathcal{V}^{+}_{3})
,
we arrive at

h(t,x)\geq\mathbb{E}\bigl[h\bigl(T,X^{u;t,x}_{T}\bigr)\bigr]\geq\mathbb{E}\bigl[U\bigl(W^{u;t,x}_{T}\bigr)\bigr],

and maximizing the right-hand side over
u\in\mathcal{A}(x)
yields
h(t,x)\geq V(t,x)
as claimed.

The pointwise infimum of all stochastic supersolutions is the function

V^{+}:\overline{\mathbb{S}}_{T}\to[0,\infty),\quad(t,x)\mapsto V^{+}(t,x):=\inf_{h\in\mathcal{V}^{+}}h(t,x).

We show in this section that
V^{+}
is the unique continuous viscosity solution
of the HJBQVI. From the discussion above, we already know that
V^{+}\geq V\geq 0
,
where
V
is the value function of the full information problem. Moreover, by

(\mathcal{V}^{+}_{3})
and by the properties of the particular stochastic supersolution
\psi\in\mathcal{V}^{+}
,
it follows that

V^{+}(T,x)=U(w),\quad x=(w,p)\in\mathbb{S},\quad\text{and}\quad V^{+}(t,0,p)=0,\quad(t,p)\in[0,T]\times\Delta^{N}.

Finally, by
[
1
, Proposition 4.1]
, the infimum in the
definition of
V^{+}
can be restricted to a countable subset of
\mathcal{V}^{+}
, from
which it is straightforward to see that
V^{+}\in\mathcal{V}^{+}
, i.e.
V^{+}
is actually
the pointwise minimum of the members of
\mathcal{V}^{+}
. Using the classical stochastic
Perron machinery, one can show that
V^{+}
is a viscosity subsolution of the HJBQVI.

Theorem 4.8
.

V^{+}
is a viscosity subsolution of (
HJBQVI
) satisfying

0\leq V^{+}(t,x)\leq K\bigl(1+|x|^{1-\alpha}\bigr),\quad(t,x)\in\overline{\mathbb{S}}_{T},

for a constant
K>0
and such that

V^{+}(T,x)=U(w),\quad x=(w,p)\in\mathbb{S},\quad\text{and}\quad V^{+}(t,0,p)=0,\quad(t,p)\in[0,T]\times\Delta^{N}.

(21)

Conversely, it is not difficult to show that
V^{+}
is also a viscosity supersolution
of the HJBQVI. This is in fact an immediate consequence of the properties of members
of
\mathcal{V}^{+}
and hence true for a much larger class of functions.

Proposition 4.9
.

Every Borel-measurable function
h:\overline{\mathbb{S}}_{T}\to\mathbb{R}
satisfying
(\mathcal{V}^{+}_{2})
to
(\mathcal{V}^{+}_{5})

in the definition of
stochastic supersolutions is a viscosity supersolution of the HJBQVI. In particular,
this is true for the choice of
h=V^{+}
, in which case it furthermore holds that

0\leq(V^{+})_{*}(t,x)\leq K\bigl(1+|x|^{1-\alpha}\bigr),\quad(t,x)\in\overline{\mathbb{S}}_{T},

for some constant
K>0
and

(V^{+})_{*}(T,x)=U(w),\quad x=(w,p)\in\mathbb{S}.

Combining the subsolution property in Theorem
4.8

and the supersolution property in Proposition
4.9

with the comparison principle in Theorem
4.6
yields the
final conclusion of this section, the characterization of
V^{+}
as the unique
continuous viscosity solution of the HJBQVI.

Corollary 4.10
.

V^{+}
is the unique continuous viscosity solution of (
HJBQVI
) in the
class of non-negative functions satisfying the growth condition (
19
)
and the boundary condition (
21
).

5
An Exit Time Control Problem and Optimal Strategies

The HJBQVI suggests that the optimal expert opinion strategy is characterized
by the partition

\mathbb{C}:=\bigl\{(t,x)\in\overline{\mathbb{S}}_{T}:V^{+}(t,x)>\mathcal{M}[V^{+}](t,x)\bigr\},\quad\mathbb{D}:=\bigl\{(t,x)\in\overline{\mathbb{S}}_{T}:V^{+}(t,x)=\mathcal{M}[V^{+}](t,x)\bigr\}

of the state space
\overline{\mathbb{S}}_{T}
in the sense that in the “continuation”
region
\mathbb{C}
, it is strictly suboptimal to purchase an expert opinion whereas
it is optimal to purchase an expert opinion as soon as the time-augmented state
process
(t,X_{t})
enters the “intervention” region
\mathbb{D}
. As wealth is finite,
purchases of expert opinions will eventually take the state process back into the
continuation region
\mathbb{C}
. In particular, the optimally controlled state process
never spends a positive amount of time in
\mathbb{D}
. To construct an optimal trading
strategy
\pi
, it should therefore suffice to restrict to the continuation
region
\mathbb{C}
. This section paves the way for this argument by introducing a
suitable exit time control problem on
\mathbb{C}
and showing that its value function
coincides with the value function
V
of the full information problem and the
pointwise minimum
V^{+}
of the stochastic supersolutions.

5.1
The Exit Time Control Problem and its Viscosity Characterization

Throughout this section, we denote by

\mathcal{A}^{\circ}(x):=\bigl\{\pi:(\pi,\circ)\in\mathcal{A}(x)\bigr\}

the set of admissible trading strategies corresponding to a fixed expert opinion
strategy
\circ
which does not purchase any expert opinions on
[0,T]
. With
the expert opinion strategy fixed, we subsequently omit
\circ
from our notation
of the state processes. Another consequence of
\circ
being fixed is that the
innovations process
I=I^{\circ}
and the filtration
\mathcal{Y}=\mathcal{Y}^{\circ}
are fixed
in the present setting. Finally, note that
\mathcal{A}^{\circ}(x)
does not depend on
x
,
so we subsequently write
\mathcal{A}^{\circ}
for the set of admissible trading strategies of
the exit time control problem.

Next, we write
\partial\mathbb{C}
and
\overline{\mathbb{C}}

for the boundary and the closure of
\mathbb{C}
, respectively. With this, let

\mathcal{S}:=\bigl\{(t,x)\in\mathbb{C}:t<T\bigr\}\quad\text{and}\quad\partial^{*}\mathcal{S}:=\bigl\{(t,x)\in\partial\mathbb{C}:t<T\bigr\}\cup\bigl\{(t,x)\in\overline{\mathbb{C}}:t=T\bigr\}

be the state space and the parabolic boundary of the state space of the exit
time control problem, respectively. In line with this notation, we furthermore
write
\overline{\mathcal{S}}:=\mathcal{S}\cup\partial^{*}\mathcal{S}=\overline{\mathbb{C}}
for the closure
of the state space. For any initial datum
(t,x)\in\overline{\mathcal{S}}
and any
admissible trading strategy
\pi\in\mathcal{A}^{\circ}
, we write

\tau_{\mathcal{S}}^{\pi}:=\inf\bigl\{s\in[t,T]:X^{\pi;t,x}_{s}\not\in\mathcal{S}\bigr\}

for the first exit time of
X^{\pi;t,x}
from the state space. With this, the cost
functional and the value function of the exit time control problem are given by

\mathcal{J}^{\mathbb{C}}:\mathcal{A}^{\circ}\times\overline{\mathcal{S}}\to[0,\infty),\quad(\pi,t,x)\mapsto\mathcal{J}^{\mathbb{C}}(\pi;t,x):=\mathbb{E}\bigl[V^{+}\bigl(\tau_{\mathcal{S}}^{\pi},X^{\pi;t,x}_{\tau_{\mathcal{S}}^{\pi}}\bigr)\bigr]\quad\text{subject to \eqref{eq:state-aux} with $\nu=\circ$.}

and

V^{\mathbb{C}}:\overline{\mathcal{S}}\to[0,\infty),\quad(t,x)\mapsto V^{\mathbb{C}}(t,x):=\sup_{\pi\in\mathcal{A}^{\circ}}\mathcal{J}^{\mathbb{C}}(\pi;t,x).

(EP)

Finally, for the sake of completeness, let us mention that the growth estimate
in Lemma
3.2
is still valid for any state process
X^{\pi;t,x}

since
(\pi,\circ)\in\mathcal{A}(x)
and
\overline{\mathcal{S}}\subseteq\overline{\mathbb{S}}_{T}
.

The goal of this section is to show that
V^{\mathbb{C}}=V^{+}
on
\overline{\mathcal{S}}
.
For this,
we once again employ a version of the stochastic Perron’s method. More precisely,
we begin by introducing the set of stochastic subsolutions of the exit time control
problem and show that their pointwise maximum
V^{-}
is a viscosity supersolution
of the Hamilton–Jacobi–Bellman (HJB) equation

F\bigl(x,V_{t}^{-}(t,x),\mathrm{D}_{x}V^{-}(t,x),\mathrm{D}_{x}^{2}V^{-}(t,x)\bigr)=0,\quad(t,x)\in\mathcal{S},

(HJB)

satisfying the boundary inequality

V^{-}(t,x)\geq V^{+}(t,x),\quad(t,x)\in\partial^{*}\mathcal{S}.

Since, conversely, the pointwise minimum of the set of stochastic subsolutions of
the full information problem
V^{+}
is easily seen to be a viscosity subsolution
of this HJB, another comparison principle shows that
V^{-}=V^{+}
on
\overline{\mathcal{S}}
.
Finally, we argue that
V^{-}\leq V^{\mathbb{C}}\leq V^{+}
on
\overline{\mathcal{S}}
,
from which the desired characterization of
V^{\mathbb{C}}
follows.

Definition 5.1
.

The set of stochastic subsolutions of (
HJB
), denoted by
\mathcal{V}^{-}
,
is the set of functions
h:\overline{\mathcal{S}}\to\mathbb{R}
satisfying

(
\mathcal{V}^{-}_{1}
)

h\in\mathrm{LSC}(\overline{\mathcal{S}})
;

(
\mathcal{V}^{-}_{2}
)

h
is lower bounded and there exists
K>0
such that

h(t,x)\leq K\bigl(1+|x|^{1-\alpha}\bigr),\quad(t,x)\in\overline{\mathcal{S}};

(
\mathcal{V}^{-}_{3}
)

h
satisfies the boundary inequality

h(t,x)\leq V^{+}(t,x),\quad(t,x)\in\partial^{*}\mathcal{S};

(
\mathcal{V}^{-}_{4}
)

for each stopping
time
\theta
taking values in
[0,T]
and each
\mathcal{Y}_{\theta}
-measurable
random variable
\xi
with
\mathbb{E}[|\xi|^{2}]<\infty
, there exists
\pi\in\mathcal{A}^{\circ}

such that

h(\theta,\xi)\leq\mathbb{E}\bigl[h(\rho,X^{\pi;\theta,\xi}_{\rho})\big|\mathcal{Y}_{\theta}\bigr]

for any stopping time

with values in

.

Once again, it is straightforward to see that the set of stochastic subsolutions
is non-empty since, e.g.,
0\in\mathcal{V}^{-}
. It is furthermore straightforward to see
that each

is dominated by

. Indeed, for each

we can apply

with

to find

such that,
with

,

h(t,x)\leq\mathbb{E}\bigl[h(\tau^{\pi}_{\mathcal{S}},X^{\pi;t,x}_{\tau^{\pi}_{\mathcal{S}}})\bigr]\leq\sup_{\pi\in\mathcal{A}^{\circ}}\mathbb{E}\bigl[h(\tau^{\pi}_{\mathcal{S}},X^{\pi;t,x}_{\tau^{\pi}_{\mathcal{S}}})\bigr]\leq\sup_{\pi\in\mathcal{A}^{\circ}}\mathbb{E}\bigl[V^{+}(\tau^{\pi}_{\mathcal{S}},X^{\pi;t,x}_{\tau^{\pi}_{\mathcal{S}}})\bigr]=V^{\mathbb{C}}(t,x),

where the last inequality is due to the boundary inequality
(\mathcal{V}^{-}_{3})
.
Next, we introduce the pointwise supremum

V^{-}:\overline{\mathcal{S}}\to[0,\infty),\quad(t,x)\mapsto V^{-}(t,x):=\sup_{h\in\mathcal{V}^{-}}h(t,x),

and we proceed to show that
V^{-}
is a viscosity subsolution of (
HJB
).
As this is once again a classical stochastic Perron argument very similar to

[
2
,
33
]
, the proof is deferred to the
appendix.

Theorem 5.2
.

V^{-}
is a viscosity supersolution of (
HJB
) satisfying

0\leq V^{-}(t,x)\leq V^{\mathbb{C}}(t,x)\leq V^{+}(t,x)\leq K\bigl(1+|x|^{1-\alpha}\bigr),\quad(t,x)\in\overline{\mathcal{S}},

for a constant
K>0
and such that

V^{-}(t,x)=V^{+}(t,x),\quad(t,x)\in\partial^{*}\mathcal{S}.

Using the viscosity supersolution property and given our results in
Section
4
, it is now straightforward to show that

V^{-}=V^{+}=V^{\mathbb{C}}
on
\overline{\mathcal{S}}
. Indeed, we argue below that
V^{+}
is a
viscosity subsolution of (
HJB
) and another comparison principle then
implies the result.

Corollary 5.3
.

The restriction of
V^{+}
to
\overline{\mathcal{S}}
is a viscosity subsolution of
(
HJB
) on
\mathcal{S}
.

Proof.

This is an immediate consequence of the fact that
V^{+}
is a viscosity subsolution
of (
HJBQVI
) together with
V^{+}>\mathcal{M}[V^{+}]
on
\mathcal{S}
.
∎

Next, we observe that the comparison principle for
(
HJB
)
follows from
a straightforward adaptation of the proof the comparison principle for
(
HJBQVI
)
.

Theorem 5.4
.

Let
u\in\mathrm{USC}(\overline{\mathcal{S}})
be a viscosity subsolution and
v\in\mathrm{LSC}(\overline{\mathcal{S}})
be a viscosity
supersolution of (
HJB
) for which there exists a constant
K>0
such that

0\leq u(t,x),v(t,x)\leq K\bigl(1+|x|^{1-\alpha}\bigr),\quad(t,x)\in\overline{\mathcal{S}}.

If, in addition,
u
and
v
satisfy the boundary condition

u\leq v\quad\text{on }\partial^{*}\mathcal{S},

then
u\leq v
on all of
\overline{\mathcal{S}}
.

Proof.

The proof works analogously to the proof of Theorem
4.6
.
The only differences are that any occurrence of
\mathbb{S}_{T}
has to be replaced by
\mathcal{S}
,
step 2, handling of the non-locality of (
HJBQVI
), can be skipped, and
instead of arguing that
(t^{*},x^{*})
cannot satisfy

, in the present situation
we have to show that
(t^{*},x^{*})\not\in\partial^{*}\mathcal{S}
. This, however, is an
immediate consequence of the boundary condition
u\leq v
on
\partial^{*}\mathcal{S}
.
∎

Combining the supersolution property of
V^{-}
in
Theorem
5.2
with the subsolution property of
V^{+}

in Corollary
5.3
together with the boundary identity

on

and the general set of inequalitites

on

, the comparison principle in

implies that these three functions
actually coincide and constitute the unique continuous viscosity solution of

).

Corollary 5.5
.

It holds that
V^{-}=V^{\mathbb{C}}=V^{+}
on
\overline{\mathcal{S}}
and
V^{\mathbb{C}}
is the unique
continuous viscosity solution of (
HJB
) in the class of non-negative
functions satisfying the growth condition (

) and which are
equal to

on
\partial^{*}\mathcal{S}
.

5.2
On the Construction of Optimal Strategies

We conclude this article by showing how to construct optimal strategies. The
central observation is that the connection of the full information optimal
investment problem to the exit time control problem considered in the
previous subsection allows us to separate the construction of the optimal
trading strategy as the solution of the exit time control problem from
the construction of the optimal expert opinion strategy using a variant of
the impulse control verification arguments developed in the last decade
in
[
3
,
4
,
5
,
7
]
.

There are, however, a few subtle technicalities to take into account in the
construction of an optimal strategy. For the impulse control verification
procedure, a central assumption is the strong Markov property of the candidate
optimal state process.
Since trading strategies are only assumed to be progressively measurable,
there is no guarantee that the state processes in our problem
are Markov processes.
In
[
20
]
, existence of optimal feedback controls
for a class of stochastic control problems including our exit time control
problem (up to a minor upper boundedness assumption, which is not impedient
for the validity of their results)
has been studied. However,
unfortunately for our purposes, the result is in general only valid for
a weak formulation of the control problem, that is, on a particular
probability space with a particular filtration and Brownian motion.
Since we are using the strong formulation here, we cannot apply their
results directly.

There are several ways to work around these issues. One possibility is to
formulated the original problem in a weak formulation to begin with,
another is to allow for additional independent randomness to lift the weak
optimal control to a strong one. Both of these techniques are well-understood,
but come with a technical burden which in our opinion would distract from the main
contributions of this article. We therefore
assume
below
that the optimal feedback control constructed in
[
20
]

is sufficiently regular so that the stochastic differential equation
for the optimally controlled state process admits a strong solution.

Towards a precise formulation of our assumption, let us begin by
recalling one of the central results in
[
20
]
,

Assumption 1

The stochastic differential equation (

Next, let us turn to the candidate for the optimal expert opinion strategy.
For this, recall the sets

The intuition of these sets is that in
\mathbb{C}

strictly suboptimal whereas purchases of expert opinions are optimal in
\mathbb{D}

Since

that there exists a measurable

\hat{q}:\overline{\mathbb{S}}_{T}\setminus\overline{\mathbb{S}}_{T}^{\emptyset}\to[0,1],\quad(t,x)\mapsto\hat{q}(t,x)

such that, for all
(t,x)\in\overline{\mathbb{S}}_{T}\setminus\overline{\mathbb{S}}_{T}^{\emptyset}

\hat{q}(t,x)\in\mathcal{D}(t,x)\;\text{ and }\;\mathcal{M}[V^{+}](t,x)=\sum_{n=1}^{N}p^{n}\mathbb{E}\Bigl[V^{+}\Bigl(t,\Gamma\bigl(\hat{q}(t,x)\mu^{n}+(1-\hat{q}(t,x))\mathcal{N},t,x,\hat{q}(t,x)\bigr)\Bigr)\Bigr].

With the feedback maps
\hat{\pi}
and
\hat{q}
at hand, we can now
introduce the candidate optimal strategy
u^{*}=(\pi^{*},\nu^{*})
with

\nu^{*}=(\tau^{*},q^{*})
as follows.
For an initial state
(t,x)\in\overline{\mathbb{S}}_{T}
, we set

(\tau_{0}^{*},\xi_{0}^{*},I_{0}^{*}):=(t,x,I^{\circ})
and iteratively for all
k\in\mathbb{N}

\displaystyle\hat{X}^{k}

\displaystyle:=\hat{X}^{\tau_{k-1}^{*},\xi_{k-1}^{*},I_{k-1}^{*}},

\displaystyle\tau_{k}^{*}

\displaystyle:=\inf\bigl\{s\in[\tau_{k-1}^{*},T]:\bigl(s,\hat{X}^{k}_{s}\bigr)\in\mathbb{D}\bigr\},

\displaystyle q_{k}^{*}

\displaystyle:=\hat{q}\bigl(\tau_{k}^{*},\hat{X}^{k}_{\tau_{k}^{*}}\bigr)\mathbb{I}_{\{\tau_{k}^{*}\leq T\}},

\displaystyle\xi_{k}^{*}

\displaystyle:=\Gamma\bigl(q_{k}^{*}\mu^{\intercal}Y_{\tau_{k}^{*}}+(1-q_{k}^{*})\mathcal{N}_{k},\tau_{k}^{*},\hat{X}^{k}_{\tau_{k}^{*}},q_{k}^{*}\bigr)\mathbb{I}_{\{\tau_{k}^{*}\leq T\}},

\displaystyle\nu_{k}^{*}

\displaystyle:=(\tau_{j}^{*},q_{j}^{*})_{0\leq j\leq k},

\displaystyle I^{*}_{k}

\displaystyle:=I^{\nu_{k}^{*}},

and, with this,

\pi^{*}:=\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}\hat{\pi}(\,\cdot\,,\hat{X}^{k})\mathbb{I}_{[\tau_{k-1}^{*},\tau_{k}^{*})\cap[0,T]}.

To understand what is going on in the construction of
u^{*}
, let us first
write
X^{*}=(W^{*},p^{*})
as short-hand notation for
X^{u^{*};t,x}
. As long
as
X^{*}\in\mathbb{C}
, no expert opinions are being purchased and the agent
follows the optimal exit time control
\hat{\pi}
. In particular, we have

X^{*}=\hat{X}^{k}
on
[\tau_{k},\tau_{k+1})\cap[0,T]
. Whenever
X^{*}

hits
\mathbb{D}
, an expert opinion of quality
\hat{q}
is purchased. Observe
that such a purchase causes a jump in
X^{*}
, but it is in general not
guaranteed that this jump takes
X^{*}
back to
\mathbb{C}
. However, since
each purchase results in a loss of wealth of at least
K_{\min}
and
since
\overline{\mathbb{S}}_{T}^{\emptyset}\subset\mathbb{C}
, it follows that there
can be at most finitely many purchases at any point in time before the
state process jumps back to
\mathbb{C}
.

Next, it is clear that
\pi^{*}
is a
\Pi
-valued and
\mathbb{F}
-progressively
measurable process and that
\nu^{*}\in\mathcal{A}_{pre}^{exp}
. In fact, writing

\mathcal{Y}^{*}:=\mathcal{Y}^{\nu^{*}}
and
\mathcal{Y}^{*,k}:=\mathcal{Y}^{\nu^{*},k}
, it is clear that

\pi^{*}
is even
\mathcal{Y}^{*}
-progressive,
\tau_{k}^{*}
is a
\mathcal{Y}^{*,k-1}
-stopping

is
\mathcal{Y}^{*,k-1}_{\tau_{k}^{*}}
-measurable for all

.
Finally, since

on

and
by the properties of
\Gamma
, we see that
W^{*}\geq 0
. We have therefore
argued that
u^{*}\in\mathcal{A}(w)
, that is,
\pi^{*}
is an admissible trading
strategy and
\nu^{*}
is an admissible expert opinion strategy.
With this, we can prove the main result of this article showing that
u^{*}

is indeed optimal and
V=V^{+}
, hence characterizing

as the unique
continuous viscosity solution of (

Theorem 5.6
.

For any
(t,x)\in\overline{\mathbb{S}}_{T}
, the associated strategy
u^{*}
is
optimal for the full information optimal investment problem and

V(t,x)=V^{+}(t,x)
.

Proof.

Step 1. We show that
V^{+}(\,\cdot\,,\hat{X}^{k})
is a martingale on

[\tau^{*}_{k-1},\tau^{*}_{k}]\cap[t,T]
for each
k\in\mathbb{N}
. For this, we first
observe that

V^{+}(\,\cdot\,,\hat{X}^{k})=V^{\mathbb{C}}(\,\cdot\,,\hat{X}^{k})\quad\text{on }[\tau^{*}_{k-1},\tau^{*}_{k}]\cap[t,T]

since
\hat{X}^{k}
is
\overline{\mathcal{S}}
-valued on that time interval.
Moreover,
\hat{X}^{k}
is an optimally controlled state process
for the exit time control problem with initial state

(\tau_{k-1}^{*},\hat{X}^{k}_{\tau_{k-1}^{*}})
. The martingale property
of
V^{*}
therefore follows from the martingale optimality principle.
Indeed, let
\rho
be an arbitrary stopping time taking values
in
[\tau^{*}_{k-1},\tau^{*}_{k}]\cap[t,T]
whenever this interval
is non-empty and let
\rho
be equal to
\tau^{*}_{k-1}
otherwise.
By optimality of
\hat{\pi}
and pathwise uniqueness of
\hat{\pi}
, we see that

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\bigl[V^{\mathbb{C}}(\rho,\hat{X}^{k}_{\rho})\bigr]

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}\Bigl[\mathbb{E}\bigl[V^{+}\bigl(\tau^{\hat{\pi}}_{\mathcal{S}},\hat{X}^{t,x,I^{\circ}}_{\tau^{\hat{\pi}}_{\mathcal{S}}}\bigr)\bigr]_{(t,x)=(\rho,\hat{X}^{k}_{\rho})}\Bigr]

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}\Bigl[\mathbb{E}\bigl[V^{+}\bigl(\tau^{\hat{\pi}}_{\mathcal{S}},\hat{X}^{t,x,I^{*}_{k-1}}_{\tau^{\hat{\pi}}_{\mathcal{S}}}\bigr)\bigr]_{(t,x)=(\rho,\hat{X}^{k}_{\rho})}\Bigr]

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}\Bigl[V^{+}\bigl(\tau^{\hat{\pi}}_{\mathcal{S}},\hat{X}^{\rho,\hat{X}^{k}_{\rho},I^{*}_{k-1}}_{\tau^{\hat{\pi}}_{\mathcal{S}}}\bigr)\Bigr]

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}\Bigl[V^{+}\bigl(\tau^{\hat{\pi}}_{\mathcal{S}},\hat{X}^{\tau_{k-1}^{*},\hat{X}^{k}_{\tau^{*}_{k-1}},I^{*}_{k-1}}_{\tau^{\hat{\pi}}_{\mathcal{S}}}\bigr)\Bigr]=\mathbb{E}\Bigl[V^{+}\bigl(\tau^{\hat{\pi}}_{\mathcal{S}},\hat{X}^{k}_{\tau^{\hat{\pi}}_{\mathcal{S}}}\bigr)\Bigr].

In particular, the same identity is true for
\rho:=\tau_{k-1}^{*}
, hence

\mathbb{E}\bigl[V^{\mathbb{C}}(\rho,\hat{X}^{k}_{\rho})\bigr]=\mathbb{E}\bigl[V^{\mathbb{C}}(\tau_{k-1}^{*},\hat{X}^{k}_{\tau_{k-1}^{*}})\bigr],

and we conclude by optional sampling.

Step 2. For each
k\in\mathbb{N}
, we show that

\mathbb{E}\bigl[V^{+}(\tau_{k}^{*},\hat{X}^{k}_{\tau_{k}^{*}})\mathbb{I}_{\{\tau_{k}^{*}<T\}}\bigr]=\mathbb{E}\bigl[V^{+}(\tau_{k}^{*},\hat{X}^{k+1}_{\tau_{k}^{*}})\mathbb{I}_{\{\tau_{k}^{*}<T\}}\bigr].

For this, we first observe that
\hat{X}^{k}_{\tau_{k}^{*}}\in\mathbb{D}
on

\{\tau_{k}^{*}<T\}
. Thus, by choice of
q_{k}^{*}
,

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\bigl[V^{+}(\tau_{k}^{*},\hat{X}^{k}_{\tau_{k}^{*}})\mathbb{I}_{\{\tau_{k}^{*}<T\}}\bigr]

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}\Bigl[\mathcal{M}[V^{+}](\tau_{k}^{*},\hat{X}^{k}_{\tau_{k}^{*}})\mathbb{I}_{\{\tau_{k}^{*}<T\}}\Bigr]

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}\Bigl[V^{+}\Bigl(\tau_{k}^{*},\Gamma\bigl(Z^{\nu^{*}}_{k},\tau_{k}^{*},\hat{X}^{k}_{\tau_{k}^{*}},q_{k}^{*}\bigr)\Bigr)\mathbb{I}_{\{\tau_{k}^{*}<T\}}\Bigr]

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}\bigl[V^{+}(\tau_{k}^{*},\xi_{k}^{*})\mathbb{I}_{\{\tau_{k}^{*}<T\}}\bigr]=\mathbb{E}\bigl[V^{+}(\tau_{k}^{*},\hat{X}^{k+1}_{\tau_{k}^{*}})\mathbb{I}_{\{\tau_{k}^{*}<T\}}\bigr].

Step 3. Conclusion. Using the previous two steps, we see that

\displaystyle V^{+}(t,x)

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}\Bigl[V^{+}(\tau_{1}^{*},\hat{X}^{1}_{\tau_{1}^{*}})\mathbb{I}_{\{\tau^{*}_{1}<T\}}+V^{+}(T,\hat{X}^{1}_{T})\mathbb{I}_{\{\tau^{*}_{1}\geq T\}}\Bigr]

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}\Bigl[V^{+}(\tau_{1}^{*},\hat{X}^{2}_{\tau_{1}^{*}})\mathbb{I}_{\{\tau^{*}_{1}<T\}}+V^{+}(T,\hat{X}^{1}_{T})\mathbb{I}_{\{\tau^{*}_{1}\geq T\}}\Bigr]

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}\Bigl[V^{+}(\tau_{2}^{*},\hat{X}^{2}_{\tau_{2}^{*}})\mathbb{I}_{\{\tau^{*}_{1}<T\}}\mathbb{I}_{\{\tau^{*}_{2}<T\}}+V^{+}(T,\hat{X}^{2}_{T})\mathbb{I}_{\{\tau^{*}_{1}<T\}}\mathbb{I}_{\{\tau_{2}^{*}\geq T\}}+V^{+}(T,\hat{X}^{1}_{T})\mathbb{I}_{\{\tau^{*}_{1}\geq T\}}\Bigr]

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}\Bigl[V^{+}(\tau_{2}^{*},\hat{X}^{2}_{\tau_{2}^{*}})\mathbb{I}_{\{\tau^{*}_{2}<T\}}+V^{+}(T,\hat{X}^{2}_{T})\mathbb{I}_{\{\tau^{*}_{1}<T\}}\mathbb{I}_{\{\tau_{2}^{*}\geq T\}}+V^{+}(T,\hat{X}^{1}_{T})\mathbb{I}_{\{\tau^{*}_{1}\geq T\}}\Bigr].

Now since
V^{+}(T,w,p)=U(w)
and
U>\mathcal{M}[U]
, we conclude that

\{T\}\times\mathbb{R}_{+}\times\mathbb{R}^{N}\subset\mathbb{C}
and hence
\hat{X}^{1}_{T}=X^{*}_{T}
on

\{\tau^{*}_{1}\geq T\}
and
\hat{X}^{2}_{T}=X^{*}_{T}
on
\{\tau^{*}_{1}<T\}\cap\{\tau_{2}^{*}\geq T\}
.
Thus, in particular,

\displaystyle V^{+}(t,x)

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}\Bigl[V^{+}(\tau_{2}^{*},\hat{X}^{2}_{\tau_{2}^{*}})\mathbb{I}_{\{\tau^{*}_{2}<T\}}+U(W^{*}_{T})\mathbb{I}_{\{\tau^{*}_{1}<T\}}\mathbb{I}_{\{\tau_{2}^{*}\geq T\}}+U(W^{*}_{T})\mathbb{I}_{\{\tau^{*}_{1}\geq T\}}\Bigr]

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}\Bigl[V^{+}(\tau_{2}^{*},\hat{X}^{2}_{\tau_{2}^{*}})\mathbb{I}_{\{\tau^{*}_{2}<T\}}+U(W^{*}_{T})\mathbb{I}_{\{\tau_{2}^{*}\geq T\}}\Bigr].

Iterating this argument yields

V^{+}(t,x)=\mathbb{E}\Bigl[V^{+}(\tau_{k}^{*},\hat{X}^{k}_{\tau_{k}^{*}})\mathbb{I}_{\{\tau^{*}_{k}<T\}}+U(W^{*}_{T})\mathbb{I}_{\{\tau_{k}^{*}\geq T\}}\Bigr],\quad k\in\mathbb{N}.

Sending
k\to\infty
, using dominated convergence and the fact that
\lim_{k\to\infty}\tau_{k}^{*}>T
,
it follows that

V^{+}(t,x)=\lim_{k\to\infty}\mathbb{E}\Bigl[V^{+}(\tau_{k}^{*},\hat{X}^{k}_{\tau_{k}^{*}})\mathbb{I}_{\{\tau^{*}_{k}<T\}}+U(W^{*}_{T})\mathbb{I}_{\{\tau_{k}^{*}\geq T\}}\Bigr]\\
=\mathbb{E}\bigl[U(W^{*}_{T})\bigr]=\mathcal{J}(u^{*};t,x)\leq V(t,x)\leq V^{+}(t,x),

which concludes the proof.
∎

-\varphi^{\eta}_{t}(s,X^{u;\theta,\xi}_{s})-\mathcal{H}\bigl(X^{u;\theta,\xi}_{s},\pi_{s},\mathrm{D}_{x}\varphi^{\eta}_{t}(s,X^{u;\theta,\xi}_{s})\mathrm{D}_{x}^{2}\varphi^{\eta}_{t}(s,X^{u;\theta,\xi}_{s})\bigr)\\
\geq F\bigl(X^{u;\theta,\xi}_{s},\varphi^{\eta}_{t}(s,X^{u;\theta,\xi}_{s}),\mathrm{D}_{x}\varphi^{\eta}_{t}(s,X^{u;\theta,\xi}_{s}),\mathrm{D}_{x}^{2}\varphi^{\eta}_{t}(s,X^{u;\theta,\xi}_{s})\bigr)>0,\qquad s\in[\theta,\vartheta\wedge\rho).

Finally, we have
\varphi^{\eta}\geq h^{\eta}
on
\mathcal{B}_{\delta/2}(\bar{t},\bar{x})
and
hence, upon combining these arguments, we conclude that

\mathbb{I}_{A}h^{\eta}(\theta,\xi)\geq\mathbb{E}\Bigl[\mathbb{I}_{A}\varphi^{\eta}\bigl(\vartheta\wedge\rho,X^{u;\theta,\xi}_{(\vartheta\wedge\rho)-}\bigr)\Big|\mathcal{Y}^{\nu}_{\theta}\Bigr]\geq\mathbb{E}\Bigl[\mathbb{I}_{A}h^{\eta}\bigl(\vartheta\wedge\rho,X^{u;\theta,\xi}_{(\vartheta\wedge\rho)-}\bigr)\Big|\mathcal{Y}^{\nu}_{\theta}\Bigr].

(37)

Next, let
B:=\{\vartheta<\rho\}
. Since
\rho\leq\tilde{\tau}_{k+1}
, it follows
that

X^{u;\theta,\xi}_{(\vartheta\wedge\rho)-}=X^{u;\theta,\xi}_{\vartheta}\in\partial\mathcal{B}_{\delta/2}(\bar{t},\bar{x}):=\bigl\{(t,x)\in\overline{\mathbb{S}}_{T}:|(t,x)-(\bar{t},\bar{x})|=\delta/2\bigr\}

since
X
is continuous on
[\tilde{\tau}_{k},\tilde{\tau}_{k+1})\cap[0,T]
. But this
implies

\displaystyle\mathbb{I}_{A}\mathbb{I}_{B}h^{\eta}\bigl(s,X^{u;\theta,\xi}_{(\vartheta\wedge\rho)-}\bigr)=\mathbb{I}_{A}\mathbb{I}_{B}V^{+}\bigl(s,X^{u;\theta,\xi}_{\vartheta}\bigr)

\displaystyle\geq\mathbb{I}_{A}\mathbb{I}_{B}\mathbb{E}\Bigl[V^{+}\bigl(s,X^{u;\theta,\xi}_{\rho-}\bigr)\Big|\mathcal{Y}^{\nu}_{\vartheta}\Bigr]

\displaystyle\geq\mathbb{I}_{A}\mathbb{I}_{B}\mathbb{E}\Bigl[h^{\eta}\bigl(s,X^{u;\theta,\xi}_{\rho-}\bigr)\Big|\mathcal{Y}^{\nu}_{\vartheta}\Bigr]

(38)

by property
(\mathcal{V}^{+}_{4})
of
V^{+}
, pathwise uniqueness of
X
, and the general inequality

h^{\eta}\leq V^{+}
. Finally, on the complement
A^{c}
of
A
, we have

h^{\eta}(\theta,\xi)=V^{+}(\theta,\xi)
, and hence the same argument yields

\mathbb{I}_{A^{c}}h^{\eta}(\theta,\xi)\geq\mathbb{I}_{A^{c}}\mathbb{E}\Bigl[h^{\eta}\bigl(s,X^{u;\theta,\xi}_{\rho-}\bigr)\Big|\mathcal{Y}^{\nu}_{\theta}\Bigr].

(39)

Combining (
37
), (
38
), and
(
39
) therefore shows that

h^{\eta}(\theta,\xi)\geq\mathbb{E}\Bigl[h^{\eta}\bigl(s,X^{u;\theta,\xi}_{\rho-}\bigr)\Big|\mathcal{Y}^{\nu}_{\theta}\Bigr],

that is,
h^{\eta}
satisfies
(\mathcal{V}^{+}_{4})
and hence the proof is complete.
\boldsymbol{\square}

Proof of Proposition
4.9
.

The growth condition on
(V^{+})_{*}
is obvious since it is already satisfied for
V^{+}
.
Regarding the terminal condition, note that
V^{+}(T,x)=U(w)
implies that

V^{+}(T,x)_{*}\leq U(w)
, so we only have to prove the reverse inequality. For this
let
\{(t_{k},x_{k})\}_{k\in\mathbb{N}}\subset\overline{S}_{T}
be a sequence converging to
(T,x)

with
x\in\mathbb{S}
such that
V^{+}(t_{k},x_{k})\to V^{+}(T,x)_{*}
. Consider the trading strategy

\pi=0\in\Pi
and an expert opinion strategy
\nu=\circ
which does not purchase any
expert opinions at all. With this, it follows that
W^{0,\circ}
is constant.
Using the property
(\mathcal{V}^{+}_{4})
of
V^{+}
and
V^{+}(T,x)=U(w)
, it follows that

(V^{+})_{*}(T,x)=\lim_{k\to\infty}V^{+}(t_{k},x_{k})\geq\limsup_{k\to\infty}\mathbb{E}\Bigl[V^{+}(T,X^{\pi,\circ;t_{k},x_{k}})\Big|\mathcal{Y}^{\circ}_{t_{k}}\Bigr]=\limsup_{k\to\infty}U(w_{k})=U(w),

where we use the notation
x_{k}=(w_{k},p_{k})
for
k\in\mathbb{N}
. In total, we have therefore
argued that
V^{+}(T,x)_{*}=U(w)
as claimed and we can move on to the viscosity
supersolution property.

For this, let
h
be a measurable function satisfying
(\mathcal{V}^{+}_{2})
to
(\mathcal{V}^{+}_{5})
. We
first observe that

h\geq\mathcal{M}[h]\geq\mathcal{M}[h_{*}]\geq\mathcal{M}[h_{*}]_{*}

since
h
satisfies
(\mathcal{V}^{+}_{5})
. Now
\mathcal{M}[h_{*}]_{*}
is a lower semi-continuous function,
hence
h_{*}\geq\mathcal{M}[h_{*}]_{*}
since
h_{*}
is by definition the largest lower semi-continuous
function dominated by
h
. Now fix
(\bar{t},\bar{x})\in\mathbb{S}_{T}
and let

\varphi\in\mathcal{C}^{1,2}(\mathbb{S}_{T})
be a test function for the viscosity supersolution property
of
h
at
(\bar{t},\bar{x})
, which is to say that
h_{*}(\bar{t},\bar{x})=\varphi(\bar{t},\bar{x})

and
\varphi-h_{*}
attains a global maximum at
(\bar{t},\bar{x})
. To conclude,
it suffices to show that

F\bigl(\bar{x},\varphi_{t}(\bar{t},\bar{x}),\mathrm{D}_{x}\varphi(\bar{t},\bar{x}),\mathrm{D}^{2}_{x}\varphi(\bar{t},\bar{x})\bigr)\geq 0.

(40)

For this, let us fix a sequence
\{(t_{k},x_{k})\}_{k\in\mathbb{N}}\subset\mathbb{S}_{T}
converging to

(\bar{t},\bar{x})
such that
h(t_{k},x_{k})\to h_{*}(\bar{t},\bar{x})
. By continuity of

\varphi
, it follows that

0\leq\gamma_{k}:=h(t_{k},x_{k})-\varphi(t_{k},x_{k})\to 0\quad\text{as }k\to\infty.

Now choose a sequence
\{\delta_{k}\}_{k\in\mathbb{N}}
of strictly positive real numbers
such that

\lim_{k\to\infty}\delta_{k}=0=\lim_{k\to\infty}\frac{\gamma_{k}}{\delta_{k}}.

Let moreover
\eta>0
, consider a constant trading strategy
\pi\in\Pi
, and
denote again by
\circ
the expert opinion strategy which does not purchase any
expert opinions at all. Writing
X^{k}:=X^{\pi,\circ;t_{k},x_{k}}
, we
introduce the stopping times

\rho_{k}:=\inf\bigl\{s\in[t_{k},T]:|X^{k}_{s}-x_{k}|>\eta\bigr\}\wedge(t_{k}+\delta_{k})\wedge T,\quad k\in\mathbb{N}.

Using property
(\mathcal{V}^{+}_{4})
,
h\geq\varphi
, and finally Itô’s formula, it
follows that

\displaystyle h(t_{k},x_{k})

\displaystyle\geq\mathbb{E}\bigl[h(\rho_{k},X^{k}_{\rho_{k}})\bigr]

\displaystyle\geq\mathbb{E}\bigl[\varphi(\rho_{k},X^{k}_{\rho_{k}})\bigr]

\displaystyle=\varphi(t_{k},x_{k})+\mathbb{E}\Bigl[\int_{t_{k}}^{\rho_{k}}\varphi_{t}(s,X^{k}_{s})+\mathcal{H}\bigl(X^{k}_{s},\pi,\mathrm{D}_{x}\varphi(s,X^{k}_{s}),\mathrm{D}^{2}_{x}\varphi(s,X^{k}_{s})\bigr)\mathrm{d}s\Bigr],

where the stochastic integral vanishes since its integrand is bounded on
[t_{k},\rho_{k}]
.
Rearranging terms and dividing by
\delta_{k}
shows that

\frac{\gamma_{k}}{\delta_{k}}-\mathbb{E}\Bigl[\frac{1}{\delta_{k}}\int_{t_{k}}^{\rho_{k}}\varphi_{t}(s,X^{k}_{s})+\mathcal{H}\bigl(X^{k}_{s},\pi,\mathrm{D}_{x}\varphi(s,X^{k}_{s}),\mathrm{D}^{2}_{x}\varphi(s,X^{k}_{s})\bigr)\mathrm{d}s\Bigr]\geq 0.

Now
\rho_{k}(\omega)=t_{k}+\delta_{k}
for eventually all
k\in\mathbb{N}
and
\mathbb{P}
-almost
every
\omega\in\Omega
. Hence, as
k\to\infty
, the mean-value theorem implies

-\varphi_{t}(\bar{t},\bar{x})-\mathcal{H}\bigl(\bar{x},\pi,\mathrm{D}_{x}\varphi(\bar{t},\bar{x}),\mathrm{D}^{2}_{x}\varphi(\bar{t},\bar{x})\bigr)\geq 0.

Since this holds for any
\pi\in\Pi
, we conclude that (
40
)
holds and the proof is complete.
\boldsymbol{\square}

Proof of Theorem
5.2
.

We begin with the growth condition on
V^{-}
. Since we already know that

V^{-}\leq V^{\mathbb{C}}
and that
V^{+}
satisfies the desired growth condition,
we only have to show that
V^{\mathbb{C}}\leq V^{+}
. For this, we fix

(t,x)=(t,w,p)\in\overline{\mathcal{S}}\subset\overline{\mathbb{S}}_{T}
and consider an
arbitrary strategy
\pi\in\mathcal{A}^{\circ}
. Recall that, in particular,

\nu:=(\pi,\circ)\in\mathcal{A}(w)
. Hence, we may apply the supermartingale
property
(\mathcal{V}^{+}_{4})
of
V^{+}
with
(\theta,\rho,\xi):=(t,\tau^{\pi}_{\mathcal{S}},x)
to obtain

V^{+}(t,x)\geq\mathbb{E}\bigl[V^{+}\bigl(X^{\pi;t,x}_{\tau_{\mathcal{S}}^{\pi}}\bigr)\bigr].

As this holds for any
\pi\in\mathcal{A}^{\circ}
, we conclude that

V^{+}(t,x)\geq\sup_{\pi\in\mathcal{A}^{\circ}}\mathbb{E}\bigl[V^{+}\bigl(X^{\pi;t,x}_{\tau_{\mathcal{S}}^{\pi}}\bigr)\bigr]=V^{\mathbb{C}}(t,x)

as claimed. The remainder of the proof is very similar in spirit to that of
Theorem
4.8
. One of the main differences is that,
in addition to the viscosity supersolution property on
\mathbb{S}_{T}
, we also have to
establish the boundary inequality
V^{-}(t,x)\geq V^{+}(t,x)
for all

(t,x)\in\overline{\mathcal{S}}
. We therefore proceed in two steps.

Step 1. The supersolution property on
\mathcal{S}
. We first take note
that
V^{-}
is lower semi-continuous as the supremum of lower semi-continuous
functions and proceed to argue towards a contradiction by assuming the existence
of
(\bar{t},\bar{x})\in\mathcal{S}
, a test function
\varphi\in\mathcal{C}^{1,2}(\mathcal{S})
such that

V^{-}-\varphi
has a strict global minimum equal to zero at
(\bar{t},\bar{x})
,
and such that the supersolution property fails, that is

F\bigl(\bar{x},\varphi_{t}(\bar{t},\bar{x}),\mathrm{D}_{x}\varphi(\bar{t},\bar{x}),\mathrm{D}_{x}^{2}\varphi(\bar{t},\bar{x})\bigr)=-3\kappa<0

for some
\kappa>0
. In particular, there exist
\bar{\pi}\in\Pi
and such that

-\varphi(\bar{t},\bar{x})-\mathcal{H}\bigl(\bar{x},\bar{\pi},\mathrm{D}_{x}\varphi(\bar{t},\bar{x}),\mathrm{D}^{2}_{x}\varphi(\bar{t},\bar{x})\bigr)\leq-2\kappa.

Using the continuity of

\mathcal{H}
, it follows that there exists
\delta>0
sufficiently small to guarantee
that
\overline{\mathcal{B}}_{\delta}(\bar{t},\bar{x})\subset\mathcal{S}
and such that

-\varphi_{t}(t,x)-\mathcal{H}\bigl(x,\bar{\pi},\mathrm{D}_{x}\varphi(t,x),\mathrm{D}^{2}_{x}\varphi(t,x)\bigr)\leq-\kappa,\quad(t,x)\in\overline{\mathcal{B}}_{\delta}(\bar{t},\bar{x}).

Next, since the minimum of
V^{-}-\varphi
at
(\bar{t},\bar{x})
is strict and

\overline{\mathcal{B}}_{\delta}(\bar{t},\bar{x})\setminus\mathcal{B}_{\delta/2}(\bar{t},\bar{x})

is compact, there exists
\eta_{0}\in(0,\kappa)
such that

V^{-}(t,x)+\eta_{0}\geq\varphi(t,x),\quad(t,x)\in\overline{\mathcal{B}}_{\delta}(\bar{t},\bar{x})\setminus\mathcal{B}_{\delta/2}(\bar{t},\bar{x}).

(41)

For
\eta\in(0,\eta_{0})
, we finally define
\varphi^{\eta}=\varphi+\eta
and

h^{\eta}:=\begin{cases}V^{-}\vee\varphi^{\eta}&\text{on }\overline{\mathcal{B}}_{\delta}(\bar{t},\bar{x}),\\
V^{-}&\text{else.}\end{cases}

Observing that

\varphi^{\eta}(\bar{t},\bar{x})=\varphi(\bar{t},\bar{x})+\eta=V^{-}(\bar{t},\bar{x})+\eta>V^{-}(\bar{t},\bar{x}),

we conclude that

h^{\eta}(\bar{t},\bar{x})=\varphi^{\eta}(\bar{t},\bar{x})>V^{-}(\bar{t},\bar{x}),

so that we arrive at a contradiction to the maximality property of
V^{-}
if
we can show that
h^{\eta}\in\mathcal{V}^{-}
. Since
h^{\eta}=V^{-}
outside of

\mathcal{B}_{\delta/2}(\bar{t},\bar{x})
by (
41
)
and since both
V^{-}
and
V^{-}\vee\varphi^{\eta}
are lower semi-continuous, it
follows that
h^{\eta}
is lower semi-continuous, i.e. it satisfies
(\mathcal{V}^{-}_{1})
.
Lower boundedness and the growth condition in
(\mathcal{V}^{-}_{2})
for
h^{\eta}
are also
immediate since
0\leq V^{-}\leq V^{+}
,
V^{+}
satisfies the growth condition,
and
V^{-}
and
h^{\eta}
differ at most on a compact set. The boundary inequality

(\mathcal{V}^{-}_{3})
holds as
\overline{\mathcal{B}}_{\delta}(\bar{t},\bar{x})\subset\mathcal{S}
,
hence
h^{\eta}=V^{-}
on
\partial^{*}\mathcal{S}
, and
V^{-}
satisfies the boundary
inequality since
V^{-}\in\mathcal{V}^{-}
.

It remains to show that
h^{\eta}
satisfies
(\mathcal{V}^{-}_{4})
. For this, a stopping
time
\theta
with values in
[0,T]
and a square-integrable,

\mathcal{Y}_{\theta}
-measurable random variable
\xi
. We denote by

\pi^{\theta}\in\mathcal{A}^{\circ}
the strategy obtained from applying
(\mathcal{V}^{-}_{4})
to

V^{-}
with initial datum
(\theta,\xi)
. Moreover, we introduce the event

A:=\bigl\{(\theta,\xi)\in\mathcal{B}_{\delta/2}(\bar{t},\bar{x})\text{ and }V^{-}(\theta,\xi)<\varphi^{\eta}(\theta,\xi)\bigr\}

and, with this, define

\hat{\pi}:=\bar{\pi}\mathbb{I}_{A}+\pi^{\vartheta}\mathbb{I}_{A^{c}}\quad\text{and}\quad\hat{\xi}:=X^{\hat{\pi};\theta,\xi}_{\hat{\theta}},\quad\text{where}\quad\hat{\theta}:=\bigl\{s\in[\theta,T]:\bigl(s,X_{s}^{\hat{\pi};\theta,\xi}\bigr)\in\partial\mathcal{B}_{\delta/2}(\bar{t},\bar{x})\bigr\}.

Finally, we let
\pi^{\hat{\theta}}\in\mathcal{A}^{\circ}
denote the strategy obtained
from applying
(\mathcal{V}^{-}_{4})
to
V^{-}
with initial datum
(\hat{\theta},\hat{\xi})

and define yet another strategy

\pi:=\hat{\pi}\mathbb{I}_{[0,\hat{\theta}]}+\pi^{\hat{\theta}}\mathbb{I}_{(\hat{\theta},T]}.

Clearly, we have
\pi\in\mathcal{A}^{\circ}
. At this point, we can argue as in
the proof of Theorem
4.8
to show that
for any stopping time
\rho
with values in
[\theta,\tau^{\pi}_{\mathcal{S}}]

it holds that

h^{\eta}(\theta,\xi)\leq\mathbb{E}\bigl[h\bigl(\rho,X^{\pi;\theta,\xi}_{\rho}\bigr)\big|\mathcal{Y}_{\theta}\bigr],

hence resulting in the desired contradiction. This establishes the viscosity
property on
\mathcal{S}
.

Step 2. The boundary condition. Since
V^{-}\in\mathcal{V}^{-}
, we already know that

V^{-}\leq V^{+}
on
\partial^{*}\mathcal{S}
by property
(\mathcal{V}^{-}_{3})
. Hence it only
remains to show the reverse inequality. Our argument follows the ideas of

[
3
, Proposition 5.5]
. By contradiction, suppose that
we can find
(\bar{t},\bar{x})\in\partial^{*}\mathcal{S}
such that

V^{-}(\bar{t},\bar{x})-V^{+}(\bar{t},\bar{x})=-\kappa<0

for some
\kappa>0
. Given
\delta,\epsilon>0
, we subsequently write

\displaystyle\mathcal{B}_{\delta,\epsilon}

\displaystyle:=\bigl\{(t,x)\in\overline{\mathcal{S}}:|\bar{t}-t|<\delta,|\bar{x}-x|<\epsilon\bigr\},

\displaystyle\overline{\mathcal{B}}_{\delta,\epsilon}

\displaystyle:=\bigl\{(t,x)\in\overline{\mathcal{S}}:|\bar{t}-t|\leq\delta,|\bar{x}-x|\leq\epsilon\bigr\},

\displaystyle\mathcal{D}_{\delta,\epsilon}

\displaystyle:=\overline{\mathcal{B}}_{\delta,\epsilon}\setminus\mathcal{B}_{\delta/2,\epsilon/2}.

Using continuity of
V^{+}
, there exists
0<\epsilon<\kappa
such that

V^{-}(\bar{t},\bar{x})-V^{+}(t,x)\leq-\epsilon<0,\quad(t,x)\in\overline{\mathcal{B}}_{\epsilon,\epsilon}.

Moreover, by lower semi-continuity,
V^{-}
is lower bounded on

\overline{\mathcal{B}}_{\epsilon,\epsilon}
and hence there exists
\beta>0
sufficiently
small such that, for all
0<\delta\leq\epsilon
simultaneously,

V^{-}(\bar{t},\bar{x})<\frac{\epsilon^{2}}{4\beta}-\epsilon+\inf_{(t,x)\in\overline{\mathcal{B}}_{\delta,\epsilon}}V^{-}(t,x).

(42)

Now for a constant
K>\delta/(4\beta)
, observe that the function

\psi(t,x):=V^{-}(\bar{t},\bar{x})-\frac{1}{\beta}|x-\bar{x}|^{2}-K(\bar{t}-t)

satisfies
\psi_{t}(t,x)=K
and
\mathcal{H}(x,\pi,\mathrm{D}_{x}\psi(t,x),\mathrm{D}^{2}_{x}\psi(t,x))
is
bounded on
\Pi\times\overline{\mathcal{B}}_{\epsilon,\epsilon}
. Hence, if
K
is
sufficiently large, it holds that

F\bigl(x,\psi_{t}(t,x),\mathrm{D}_{x}\psi(t,x),\mathrm{D}^{2}_{x}\psi(t,x)\bigr)<0,\quad(t,x)\in\overline{\mathcal{B}}_{\epsilon,\epsilon}.

Now with
K
being fixed, we choose
\delta\leq\min\{\epsilon/(2K),\epsilon\}
.
Since
|\bar{x}-x|\geq\epsilon/2
and
\bar{t}-t\geq-\delta
on
\mathcal{D}_{\delta,\epsilon}

and by using (
42
), we conclude that

\psi(t,x)=V^{-}(\bar{t},\bar{x})-\frac{1}{\beta}|x-\bar{x}|^{2}-K(\bar{t}-t)\\
<V^{-}(t,x)-\epsilon+K\delta\leq V^{-}(t,x)-\frac{\epsilon}{2},\quad(t,x)\in\mathcal{D}_{\delta,\epsilon},

where the last inequality follows from
\delta\leq\epsilon/(2K)
. Similarly,
using again that
\bar{t}-t\geq-\delta\geq-\epsilon/(2K)
on

\overline{\mathcal{B}}_{\delta,\epsilon}
, we have

\psi(t,x)\leq V^{-}(\bar{t},\bar{x})-K(\bar{t}-t)\leq V^{-}(\bar{t},\bar{x})+\frac{\epsilon}{2}\leq V^{+}(t,x)-\frac{\epsilon}{2},\quad(t,x)\in\overline{\mathcal{B}}_{\delta,\epsilon}.

Now for
0<\eta<\epsilon/2
, we first define
\psi^{\eta}:=\psi+\eta
and,
with this,

h^{\eta}:=\begin{cases}V^{-}\vee\psi^{\eta}&\text{on }\overline{\mathcal{B}}_{\delta,\epsilon},\\
V^{-}&\text{else}.\end{cases}

An argument as in the proof of Theorem
4.8

shows that
h^{\eta}\in\mathcal{V}^{-}
. On the other hand, by definition of
\psi^{\eta}

and
\psi
, it holds that

\psi^{\eta}(\bar{t},\bar{x})=\psi(\bar{t},\bar{x})+\eta>V^{-}(\bar{t},\bar{x}),

contradicting the maximality of
V^{-}
in
\mathcal{V}^{-}
, hence concluding the
proof.
\boldsymbol{\square}
