Primary ectopic meningioma of the brachial plexus: A case report and literature review
Authors: Segev Gabay, Marga Serafimova, Rostislav Novak, David Nikomarov, Euvgeni Vlodavsky, Yuval Shapira
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Background: Meningioma is the most common primary intracranial tumor. Less commonly, it may also arise extracranially in ectopic locations. Primary ectopic meningioma arising from the brachial plexus is extremely rare, with only two published cases. We present a case of primary brachial plexus meningioma, proven by histological analysis, and a review of the literature.Case Description: A 25-year-old man presented with swelling in the left supraclavicular region. Magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) demonstrated the presence of a mass arising from the brachial plexus. Initial pathological diagnosis and repeat biopsies were inconclusive and interpreted as a low-grade mesenchymal tumor with uncertain biologic potential. The proposed differential diagnosis included epithelioid schwannoma, atypical epithelioid neoplasm, or a low-grade malignancy. The fact that the differential diagnosis included both benign possibilities and low-grade malignancies raised complex questions about the preferred treatment plan. The surgical techniques necessary for each of these diagnoses vary. Typically, soft-tissue malignancies necessitate extensive resection, while in benign tumors, limited resection may suffice. Extensive resection within the brachial plexus carries substantial neurological and, therefore, functional morbidity. Finally, the patient underwent marginal resection of the tumor. The final pathological diagnosis was concluded as meningioma. The patient recovered completely, without neurological deficits, and repeated MRI demonstrated no residual or recurrence of the lesion.Conclusion: Although uncommon, we recommend considering the rare possibility of meningioma as a differential diagnosis for brachial plexus tumors, particularly in cases where the initial pathological examination is inconclusive for more prevalent tumors such as schwannoma and neurofibroma. Due to the rarity of this pathology, conclusive treatment recommendations cannot be made, especially regarding the decision to follow-up versus surgical resection. This debate is particularly pertinent in instances where the malignant potential of the pathology is uncertain and where surgical intervention carries a significant risk of functional impairment. We believe that maximal and safe surgical excision is the primary method of treatment, which potentially confers an excellent prognosis.

INTRODUCTION

Meningiomas are tumors that arise from the meningothelial cells of the arachnoid, known as arachnoid cap cells, and are usually attached to the dura.[13] They are the most common primary intracranial tumors, representing about 35% of all central nervous system (CNS) tumors, approximately 20% of all intracranial tumors[13,19] and about 12% of all spinal meningiomas.[12]

According to the latest World Health Organization (WHO) classification of CNS tumors (WHO-5), published in 2021, meningiomas have a broad morphological spectrum reflected histologically in 15 subtypes.[13]

Intracranial meningiomas are most commonly located in the supratentorial compartment, parasagittal, and at the convexities.[19] Spinal meningiomas are most commonly located in the intradural extramedullary compartment.[12]

In rare cases, it may arise extracranially, in diverse locations, which can result in inappropriate clinical management.[20]

Primary extracranial meningioma arising from the brachial plexus is extremely rare. To the best of our knowledge, there are only two published case reports in the literature.[3,27]

We present a case of primary brachial plexus meningioma, proven by biopsy and histological analysis.

CASE REPORT

A 25-year-old male, generally healthy, was admitted to the hospital due to swelling in the left supraclavicular region for approximately 3 months. He had a traumatic left clavicular fracture 7 years prior, which was treated conservatively. He denied any shortness of breath, dysphagia, or hoarseness. The patient did not have any neurological symptoms, and his examination was unremarkable. Electrophysiological studies, including electromyography and nerve conduction studies, were interpreted as normal.

On clinical examination, the mass was found to be firm, approximately 5 cm in diameter, situated between the pectoralis major muscle and clavicular bone, non-tender, mobile, and non-pulsatile. The patient did not have any skin changes. Magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) demonstrated a large 5 × 3 cm mass situated near the subclavian vessels, with a high-intensity T2 signal and low-intensity T1 signal [Figure 1], homogeneously enhancing after contrast administration [Figure 2].

Magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) showing the same mass (red arrows), high intensity homogeneous enhancement on T1 after contrast gadolinium administration (T1 +Gd).

Fine-needle biopsy (FNB) of the mass showed a cluster of cells positive for vimentin and negative for keratin, with only sparse cells positive for Ki67. Due to the small volume of the specimen, a repeat biopsy was performed, which revealed cores of mesenchymal tumor composed of whorls of plump spindle and epithelioid mesenchymal cells without nuclear atypia. It was interpreted as a low-grade mesenchymal tumor with some features of epithelioid schwannoma. Due to the uncertainty of the diagnosis, a repeat FNB was done, which revealed similar pathological findings.[6]

The case was sent for consultation at an international referral center for soft-tissue oncopathology. The negative SOX10 stain argued strongly against epithelioid schwannoma, with uncertainty about the biologic potential of the tumor. A diagnosis of atypical epithelioid neoplasm was rendered, and the recommendation was to repeat the biopsy.

The clinical picture at this stage consisted of several significant points for discussion regarding the treatment plan. First, there was an uncertain pathological diagnosis, with a differential diagnosis that included the possibility of a benign neoplastic process or a low-grade malignant process. Second, the process was located in a “strategic” location, in proximity to the brachial plexus, where surgery for wide excision and obtaining an R0 resection (the surgical technique required for malignant soft-tissue tumors) could lead to functional impairment. In contrast, surgical resection in an attempt to avoid damage to the neural structures would be a marginal resection (at least R1) – a technique that may be appropriate for benign soft-tissue tumors, but certainly not sufficient for a malignancy.

After a multi-disciplinary discussion, the consensus was that follow-up alone would not suffice. The decision was to proceed with surgery for marginal resection (R1), intending to avoid nerve damage and the opportunity of a larger volume sample for pathological examination. In case the final pathology was concluded as malignant, a repeat surgery would be needed, aimed to extend the resection margins, including the nerve components, with significant morbidity.

During the surgery, the nerve trunks and cords of the brachial plexus were entrapped by the neoplasm without a clear separation plane. Maximal safe resection was performed, leaving areas of the tumor capsule around involved neurovascular structures, and with the preservation of the function of the brachial plexus.

The pathological findings were similar to the previous biopsy. The case was sent for consultation again, in the same international referral center for soft-tissue oncopathology, and this time, a diagnosis of a meningothelial meningioma, WHO grade 1, was made.

Consequently, an MRI of the entire CNS, encompassing the brain and spinal cord, was conducted in search of a primary meningioma that could potentially be the source. However, no other findings were found. Following surgery, the patient experienced an excellent recovery with no neurological deficits. As the tumor was benign, no further treatment was required. Repeated MRI demonstrated no residual tumor or recurrence. Follow-up over 5 years since the surgery has been uneventful.

Pathology

The microscopic structure of the tumor was characterized by solid and alveolar proliferation of monomorphic epithelioid cells with vesicular nuclei and eosinophilic cytoplasm. Focal whorls of tumor cells around the small blood vessels strongly resembled the pattern seen in meningiomas of the CNS [Figure 3]. Positive epithelial membrane antigen immunostain supports this impression. Pathological material was sent to an international musculoskeletal pathology center for a consultation with a preliminary diagnosis of epithelioid perineurioma versus meningioma of peripheral nerves. The diagnosis of meningioma was confirmed in light of further support by positive immunohistochemical stain for somatostatin receptor, performed in this laboratory.

DISCUSSION

Theoretically, meningiomas may arise anywhere that arachnoid cap cells are found, and indeed, uncommonly, meningiomas occur in intraventricular, intraparenchymal, or calvarial intraosseous locations.[20] Meningioma originating from ectopic arachnoid cap cells located outside the skull or spine, in an extradural location, is called ectopic meningioma, constituting <2% of all meningiomas.[9,11,18] While the intracranial location is more common among women, in ectopic meningiomas, both genders are equally affected.[20] To give the diagnosis of an ectopic meningioma, the extracranial location should be the primary one, originating independently, and should not be confused with a secondary extracranial extension of intracranial meningioma, which is estimated to occur in up to 20% of cases, nor with meningioma metastases occurring in <1% of patients diagnosed with meningioma.[4,23] The origin of ectopic meningiomas is unclear, with several different mechanisms speculated, including displacement of arachnoid cap cells through the skull foramina, origination from multipotent mesenchymal stem cells, entrapment during embryologic development, and a traumatic event that displaces arachnoid cap cells.[3,20] Locations of extracranial meningiomas that have been reported in the literature are diverse and include, among others, the scalp, parapharyngeal space, neck, parotid gland, and foot.[1,16,17,24,26]

Primary nerve tumors arising in the brachial plexus are uncommon, with the most common pathologies reported being benign nerve sheath tumors, including schwannomas and neurofibromas.[8] Secondary involvement of the brachial plexus by meningioma through the extension of an intraaxial lesion is unusual but has been described, as well as rare cases of secondary involvement by metastases from primary intracranial malignant meningioma.[7,14,15,21,22] In our case, the tumor is not related to or associated with any brain or spine pathology.

Although the entity of post-traumatic meningiomas in the cranium and spine has been described, none have been described in the brachial plexus following a traumatic clavicle fracture.[10] In the case described here, there was indeed a history of a clavicle fracture, but in our opinion, the two are unrelated. This is due to the relatively long timespan between the two events and the fact that the fracture did not cause any neurovascular compromise, leading us to assume that there was no substantial contact between the fracture fragment and the nerves or vessels. Nonetheless, we do acknowledge the possibility that tumor cells residing within the clavicle could have migrated and proliferated following the fracture.

Cases of extracranial meningiomas originating primarily from the peripheral nerves of the brachial plexus are sparse. This pathology has not been demonstrated or mentioned in multiple large case series regarding brachial plexus tumors.[2,5,8] To the best of our knowledge, only two cases have been reported.[3,27] Interestingly, the pathology results of the initial biopsies, both in the case report by Coons and Johnson, and in our case, were interpreted as schwannoma, and later, following resection, were diagnosed as a meningioma.

The long-term prognosis of brachial plexus meningiomas is unclear, mainly due to the rarity of this pathology. It can be argued that because the histology is the same as in ordinary meningiomas, brachial plexus meningiomas should be treated similarly. Surgical excision of the mass should be performed if possible. In cases where tumor resection is achieved with preservation of the function of the brachial plexus, and the histological grade is WHO I, the prognosis is expected to be excellent. In cases where the histological grade is higher (WHO 2 or 3), it might be associated with worse prognosis, due to parenchymal invasion of tumor cells, expected to lead to less chance of achieving complete resection and potentially higher recurrence rates.[25]

CONCLUSION

Primary extracranial meningiomas of the brachial plexus are extremely rare. To the best of our knowledge, we are presenting the third known case of primary brachial plexus meningioma with long-term follow-up. Although rare, meningioma should be included in the differential diagnosis of brachial plexus tumors, especially when the pathology is inconclusive. In cases where the histological evaluation is not clear, with only a small amount of tissue available for evaluation, a larger sample size is recommended. As these tumors are histologically similar to their intracranial counterparts, we believe that surgical excision with preservation of nerve function is the primary goal of treatment.
