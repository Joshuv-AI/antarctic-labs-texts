Avoiding the Price of Adaptivity: Inference in Linear Contextual Bandits via Stability
Samya Praharaj, Koulik Khamaru, [0.3cm] Department of Statistics, Rutgers University

Abstract
Statistical inference in contextual bandits is complicated by the adaptive, non-i.i.d. nature of the data. A growing body of work has shown that classical least-squares inference may fail under adaptive sampling, and that constructing valid confidence intervals for linear functionals of the model parameter typically requires paying an unavoidable inflation of order d ​ log ⁡ T \sqrt{d\log T} . This phenomenon—often referred to as the price of adaptivity —highlights the inherent difficulty of reliable inference under general contextual bandit policies. A key structural property that circumvents this limitation is the stability condition of Lai and Wei ( 28 ) , which requires the empirical feature covariance to concentrate around a deterministic limit. When stability holds, the ordinary least-squares estimator satisfies a central limit theorem, and classical Wald-type confidence intervals—designed for i.i.d. data— become asymptotically valid even under adaptation, without incurring the d ​ log ⁡ T \sqrt{d\log T} price of adaptivity. In this paper, we propose and analyze a penalized EXP4 algorithm for linear contextual bandits. Our first main result shows that this procedure satisfies the Lai–Wei stability condition and therefore admits valid Wald-type confidence intervals for linear functionals. Our second result establishes that the same algorithm achieves regret guarantees that are minimax optimal up to logarithmic factors, demonstrating that stability and statistical efficiency can coexist within a single contextual bandit method. Finally, we complement our theory with simulations illustrating the empirical normality of the resulting estimators and the sharpness of the corresponding confidence intervals.

Introduction

Statistical inference under adaptive data collection has become a central challenge in modern learning systems. Unlike classical settings where data are sampled independently of past observations, adaptive algorithms—such as contextual bandits and reinforcement learning policies—select actions based on previously observed losses. This feedback loop creates intricate dependencies that distort the distribution of both covariates and errors, often rendering standard inferential tools invalid. Even when estimators remain consistent, their asymptotic distributions can deviate substantially from classical theory, complicating uncertainty quantification and hypothesis testing

stability

At a high level, a bandit algorithm is

(

(

1.4
Related Work

The challenges of performing statistical inference under adaptive data collection have been
well documented in recent years. Because adaptive policies bias the distribution of covariates and
errors, classical inferential procedures may become invalid. This breakdown has been observed
empirically (
44
;
41
) and supported by theoretical analyses
(
36
;
38
;
39
). To counteract these issues, several
methodological approaches have been proposed, including online debiasing techniques
(
25
;
8
;
27
) and procedures based on inverse
propensity scores (
19
;
12
;
46
;
34
;
31
).
These methods address inference in adaptive environments more broadly, but do not directly
resolve the structural constraints that give rise to the
\sqrt{d\log T}
“price of adaptivity” in
contextual bandits.

A distinct line of work focuses on identifying conditions under which classical, Wald-type
asymptotic inference is restored despite adaptivity. The seminal paper of

28
introduced the notion of
stability
, showing that if the empirical design
covariance converges to a deterministic limit, then least-squares estimators satisfy a central limit
theorem and Wald confidence intervals regain asymptotic validity.
This perspective has motivated a growing literature on stability-based inference for bandit
algorithms (
24
;
26
;
16
;
18
;
21
;
20
;
17
). However, subsequent work has shown that many commonly used
bandit algorithms fail to satisfy the Lai–Wei stability condition, leading to substantial
under-coverage when Wald intervals are applied naively
(
18
;
37
).
This underscores the central question motivating our work:
can one design adaptive
algorithms that are simultaneously stable and statistically efficient?

Contextual and adversarial bandit algorithms are frequently derived from mirror descent
(
29
), a framework that has been applied extensively in online
optimization (
2
;
3
;
7
;
43
)
and that underlies widely used adversarial bandit methods such as EXP3
(
4
), EXP4 (
5
), Tsallis-INF
(
47
;
33
), and OFTRL (
23
).
Despite their strong regret guarantees, the stability properties of these algorithms are not well
understood, and existing analyses suggest that many of them may not support valid Wald-type
inference.

Finally, an alternative approach to inference in adaptive settings relies on non-asymptotic,
anytime-valid confidence intervals constructed via concentration inequalities for self-normalized
martingales. This line of work builds on the foundational results of de la Peña et al. (
9
;
10
) and includes several refined analyses
(
1
;
22
;
42
).
These intervals hold uniformly over time and do not rely on asymptotic arguments, but the price
paid is typically much wider confidence intervals compared to those achievable under stability,
reflecting the worst-case nature of anytime-valid guarantees.

In this section we state our main results.

4.1

A key component of our analysis is that the empirical average of the weight vectors

\displaystyle\frac{1}{T}\sum_{t=1}^{T}w_{t,k}-w^{\star}_{T,k}\;\xrightarrow{P}\;0,\qquad k\in[K].

\displaystyle\Sigma^{\star}_{T}=T\sum^{K}_{k=1}w^{\star}_{T,k}\Sigma_{k}\qquad\text{with}\qquad\Sigma_{k}:=\mathbb{E}_{x\sim\mathcal{P_{X}}}\left(\sum_{a\in\mathcal{A}}\pi_{k}(a\mid x)\cdot c(x,a)c(x,a)^{\top}\right)

1
) with step size
\eta=\sqrt{\frac{\log K}{|\mathcal{A}|T}}
, and tuning parameters
\epsilon=\frac{1}{KT}
,
\lambda=\frac{\gamma_{T}}{\sqrt{T}}
with
\gamma_{T}\rightarrow\infty
and
T/\log^{2}T\gg\gamma_{T}
, is stable; we have

\displaystyle\Sigma_{T}^{\star-1}\cdot\mathrm{S}_{T}\xrightarrow{p}\mathbf{I}_{d},

with
\Sigma_{T}^{\star}
from (
25
). Consequently, for any fixed
a\in\real^{d}
we have

\displaystyle\frac{a^{\top}(\widehat{\beta}_{\mathrm{OLS}}-\beta^{\star})}{\widehat{\sigma}\sqrt{a^{\top}S_{T}^{-1}a}}\xrightarrow{d}\mathcal{N}\left(0,1\right)

(26a)

where
\widehat{\beta}_{\mathrm{OLS}}
is the least square estimate, and
\widehat{\sigma}
is any consistent estimator of noise standard deviation
\sigma
.

Throughout we assume that the sample covariance matrix
S_{T}
is invertible. If
S_{T}
is not invertible, one can construct a ridge estimator with the same guarantees. We highlight the technical details in Section
6
.
A direct consequence of Theorem
1
is that the Wald confidence interval
(
4
) is asymptotically exact: for any fixed
a\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
,

\displaystyle\lim_{T\to\infty}\mathbb{P}\!\left(a^{\top}\beta^{\star}\in\mathcal{I}^{\mathrm{Wald}}_{T}(a)\right)\;=\;1-\alpha.

Thus, stability ensures that the classical (nonadaptive) form of the Wald interval remains
valid despite the adaptively collected data. See Section
6.1
for a proof of Theorem
1
.

4.2
Regret guarantees

We next turn to regret guarantees. The following theorem quantifies how the choice of
\gamma_{T}
influences the regret, formalizing the tradeoff introduced by the stabilizing penalty.

Theorem 2
.

Suppose Assumptions
(A1)
-
(A4)
are in force. Then,
for
T\geq 4
, under the stepsize

\eta=\sqrt{\frac{\log K}{TK}}

and tuning parameters

\epsilon=\frac{1}{KT}
and
\lambda=\frac{\gamma_{T}}{\sqrt{T}}
,
Algorithm
1
satisfies

\displaystyle\mathrm{Regret}(T)\;\leq\;8\sqrt{TK\log K}\;+\;\gamma_{T}\log(KT)\sqrt{T}\;+\;\frac{4\gamma_{T}^{2}\log^{3}(KT)}{K^{2}\sqrt{T}}.

(27)

We prove Theorem
2
in Section
6.2
.
Setting
\gamma_{T}=\sqrt{\log T}
yields regret of order

O\!\left(\sqrt{TK\log(KT)}\right)
, matching minimax lower bounds up to
logarithmic factors. Thus, despite being explicitly regularized for
inferential stability, the procedure retains the characteristic efficiency of
the EXP4 family.

5
Experiments

We evaluate the empirical behavior of the proposed
penalized–EXP4
algorithm in a stochastic contextual bandit environment with linear loss structure. In this section we provide the simulation details for the standard OLS estimator. Experimental details for the ridge estimator are presented in Appendix
B

5.1
Simulation Environment

The loss model follows a block–sparse linear structure

\displaystyle\ell_{t}=\langle x_{t},\theta_{a_{t}}\rangle+\varepsilon_{t},

where
\varepsilon_{t}\sim\text{Unif}(-0.1,0.1)
and
x_{t}\in\mathbb{R}^{d_{x}}
is a normalized Gaussian context vector with
\|x_{t}\|_{2}\leq 1
. Each arm
a\in\{1,\dots,A\}
possesses an unknown parameter
\theta_{a}\in\mathbb{R}^{d_{x}}
, and the global coefficient vector

\displaystyle\beta^{\star}=(\theta_{1},\dots,\theta_{A})\in\mathbb{R}^{Ad_{x}}

is normalized to satisfy
\|\beta^{\star}\|_{2}\leq 1
. The learner never observes
\theta_{a}
; instead, only bandit feedback
\ell_{t}
is revealed.

We encode actions through a sparse block feature map

\displaystyle c(x,a)=\big(0,\dots,x,\dots,0\big)\in\mathbb{R}^{Ad_{x}},

Our experiments are conducted in two settings. The first assumes experts based on softmax policies, and the second replaces them with six layer neural network policies. We outline the specifics of each configuration in the following sections.

Simulation setting with softmax experts

Each expert network produces action probabilities through a softmax map:

\displaystyle\pi_{k}(a\mid x)=\frac{e^{\left\langle u_{a,k},x\right\rangle}}{\sum_{a\in\mathcal{A}}e^{\left\langle u_{a,k},x\right\rangle}}

where,
k\in[K]
and the entries of the weight vector
u_{a,k}
are i.i.d. draws from
\mathcal{N}(0,12)
distribution. In our experiments we set
A=K=5
and
d_{x}=10
.

Simulation setting with neural experts

Unlike the previous setting, the experts now form neural policies with a six-layer ReLU architecture. The expert policy is a six-layer neural network given by

\displaystyle x\;\longrightarrow\;h_{1}\;\longrightarrow\;h_{2}\;\longrightarrow\;h_{3}\;\longrightarrow\;h_{4}\;\longrightarrow\;h_{5}\;\longrightarrow\;h_{6}\;\longrightarrow\;\operatorname{softmax}(\text{logits}),

where the hidden layers satisfy

\displaystyle h_{i}=\operatorname{ReLU}\!\left(W_{i}h_{i-1}+b_{i}\right),\qquad i=1,\ldots,6,

with
h_{0}=x
. The entries of the weight matrices
W_{i}
are i.i.d. draws from a standard Gaussian random variable.
The resulting expert policy is

\displaystyle\pi(a\mid x)=\frac{\exp(\text{logits}_{a})}{\sum_{a^{\prime}=1}^{A}\exp(\text{logits}_{a^{\prime}})}.

In our experiments we set
A=3
and
K=5
and
d_{x}=50
.

Proof of Theorems

In this section we prove Theorems
1
and
2
.
Recall that
S_{T}=\sum^{T}_{t=1}c(x_{t},a_{t})c(x_{t},a_{t})^{\top}
where, the random vector
c(x,a)\in\real^{d}
satisfies

\displaystyle\sup_{x,a}\|c(x,a)\|_{2}<\infty.

(28)

By stability (definition (
1
)) we mean that the sample covariance matrix satisfies the following property.

\displaystyle{\Sigma^{\star}_{T}}^{-1}S_{T}\;\stackrel{{\scriptstyle p}}{{\rightarrow}}\;\mathbf{I}\qquad\text{where}\;\;\{{\Sigma^{\star}_{T}}\}\;\;\text{is a sequence of non-random positive definite matrices.}

(29)

Let us define the matrix

\displaystyle Y_{t}:=c(x_{t},a_{t})c(x_{t},a_{t})^{\top}.

We denote by
\mathcal{F}_{t}:=\sigma(x_{1},a_{1},r_{1},\ldots,x_{t},a_{t},\ell_{t})
, the
\sigma
-field generated by all observations up to time
t
,
including
x_{t}
,
a_{t}
and
\ell_{t}
.
Since
x_{t}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle iid}}{{\sim}}\mathcal{P_{X}}
, the conditional expectation

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[Y_{t}\mid\mathcal{F}_{t-1}]=\mathbb{E}_{x_{t}}\!\left[\sum_{a\in\mathcal{A}}Q_{t}(a\mid x_{t})\,c(x_{t},a)c(x_{t},a)^{\top}\right]=\sum^{K}_{k=1}w_{t,k}\Sigma_{k}

where the matrices

\displaystyle\Sigma_{k}:=\mathbb{E}_{x}\!\left[\sum_{a\in\mathcal{A}}\pi_{k}(a\mid x)\,c(x,a)c(x,a)^{\top}\right],\qquad k=1,\ldots,K

(30)

are population-level second moments under each expert.
In order to simplify notations, throughout we assume
\sigma=1
.

6.1
Proof of Theorem
1

We first prove stability holds for the standard OLS estimator, assuming that
S_{T}
is invertible, followed by the proof for the modified ridge estimator. Once the stability condition (
1
) is verified, the asymptotic normality claim (
26a
) follows directly by invoking Theorem 3 from
28
along with an application of Slutsky’s theorem.

Part (a): proof of stability condition for
S_{T}
(
29
)

In order to prove the stability of the sample covariance matrix
S_{T}
, we need to first prove that the average of the
random
weight vectors
\{w_{t}\}_{t\geq 0}
converge to a non-random vector, which we call
w_{T}^{\star}
. Concretely,

\displaystyle\frac{1}{T}\sum_{t=1}^{T}w_{t,k}-w^{\star}_{T,k}\;\stackrel{{\scriptstyle p}}{{\rightarrow}}\;0

(31)

for all experts
k\in[K]
.

Proof of weight stability (
31
):

We begin by recalling that
\widetilde{g}_{t}=\widehat{g}_{t}+\lambda\nabla R(w_{t})
. Let
\tilde{R}_{\lambda}(w):=\lambda R(w)+\langle\bar{g}^{\star},w\rangle
. If
f
is any
M
-strongly convex function, then
f+g
is also
M
-strongly convex if
g
is a linear function (
35
). This result implies that
\tilde{R}_{\lambda}
is
\lambda
strongly convex. Now, for any arbitrary
y\in\Delta_{\epsilon}
we have,

\displaystyle\dfrac{1}{T}\sum_{t=1}^{T}\mathbb{E}\langle\tilde{g}_{t},w_{t}-y\rangle

\displaystyle=\dfrac{1}{T}\sum_{t=1}^{T}\mathbb{E}\langle\bar{g}^{\star}+\lambda\nabla R(w_{t}),w_{t}-y\rangle

\displaystyle=\dfrac{1}{T}\sum_{t=1}^{T}\bigg(\mathbb{E}\langle\bar{g}^{\star},w_{t}-y\rangle+\lambda\mathbb{E}\langle\nabla R(w_{t}),w_{t}-y\rangle\bigg)

\displaystyle\overset{(i)}{\geq}\dfrac{1}{T}\sum_{t=1}^{T}\bigg(\mathbb{E}\langle\bar{g}^{\star},w_{t}-y\rangle+\lambda\mathbb{E}(R(w_{t})-R(y))\bigg)

\displaystyle\geq\dfrac{1}{T}\sum_{t=1}^{T}\bigg(\mathbb{E}[\langle\bar{g}^{\star},w_{t}\rangle+\lambda R(w_{t})]-\mathbb{E}[\langle\mu,y\rangle+\lambda R(y)]\bigg)

\displaystyle\overset{(ii)}{=}\dfrac{1}{T}\sum_{t=1}^{T}\mathbb{E}\bigg[\tilde{R}(w_{t})-\tilde{R}(y)\bigg]

\displaystyle\overset{(iii)}{\geq}\mathbb{E}\bigg[\tilde{R}(\bar{w}_{T})-\tilde{R}(y)\bigg]

\displaystyle\overset{(iv)}{=}\mathbb{E}\bigg[D_{\tilde{R}_{\lambda}}(\bar{w}_{T},y)+\langle\nabla\tilde{R}_{\lambda}(y),\bar{w}_{T}-y\rangle)\bigg]

(32)

Where
D_{\tilde{R}_{\lambda}}
is the Bregman divergence defined in terms of
\tilde{R}_{\lambda}
. The first equality above follows by the unbiasedness of the gradient estimate
\widehat{g}_{t}
(Section
2.2
). Equation
(i)
above follows from convexity of
R
, equation
(ii)
follows from definition of
\tilde{R}
and
(iii)
holds due to convexity of
\tilde{R}_{\lambda}
. Equality
(iv)
follows from the definition of Bregman divergence. Note that the above discussion holds for any
y\in\Delta_{\epsilon}
. Let us define
w^{\star}_{T}
as follows

\displaystyle w^{\star}_{T}=\arg\min_{w\in\Delta_{\epsilon}}\tilde{R}_{\lambda}(w)

(33)

If we choose
y=w^{\star}_{T}
then by applying the first order optimality criteria of equation (
33
) and Pinsker’s inequality we have

\displaystyle\langle\nabla\tilde{R}_{\lambda}(w^{\star}_{T}),\bar{w}_{T}-w^{\star}_{T}\rangle\geq 0\quad\text{and,}\ \ D_{\tilde{R}_{\lambda}}(\bar{w}_{T},w^{\star}_{T})\geq\dfrac{\lambda}{2}\|\bar{w}_{T}-w^{\star}_{T}\|_{1}^{2}

(34)

From equations (
6
) and (
34
), we arrive at the following lower bound:

\displaystyle\dfrac{1}{T}\sum_{t=1}^{T}\mathbb{E}\langle\tilde{g}_{t},w_{t}-w^{\star}_{T}\rangle\geq\dfrac{\lambda}{2}\mathbb{E}\|\bar{w}_{T}-w^{\star}_{T}\|_{1}^{2}

(35)

Now, the following lemma provides an upper bound on
\frac{1}{T}\sum_{t=1}^{T}\mathbb{E}\langle\tilde{g}_{t},w_{t}-w^{\star}_{T}\rangle
.

Lemma 1
.

Under the setup of Theorem
1
, we have

\displaystyle\dfrac{1}{T}\sum_{t=1}^{T}\mathbb{E}\langle\tilde{g}_{t},w_{t}-w^{\star}_{T}\rangle\leq 5{\frac{\sqrt{|\mathcal{A}|\log K}}{\sqrt{T}}}+\frac{4\gamma_{T}^{2}\log(K)\log^{2}\left(KT\right)}{|\mathcal{A}|^{2}\sqrt{T}}

We prove this lemma in Section
6.2

. From equation (
35
) and Lemma
1
it follows from Jensen’s inequality that,

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[\|\bar{w}_{T}-w^{\star}_{T}\|_{1}]

\displaystyle\leq\sqrt{\frac{2}{\lambda_{T}}}\sqrt{\dfrac{1}{T}\sum_{t=1}^{T}\mathbb{E}\langle\tilde{g}_{t},w_{t}-w^{\star}_{T}\rangle}

\displaystyle\leq\sqrt{10{\frac{\sqrt{|\mathcal{A}|\log K}}{\sqrt{T}\lambda_{T}}}+\frac{8\gamma_{T}^{2}\log(K)}{\lambda_{T}|\mathcal{A}|^{2}T\sqrt{T}}\log^{2}\left(KT\right)}

\displaystyle=\sqrt{10{\frac{\sqrt{|\mathcal{A}|\log K}}{\gamma_{T}}}+\frac{8\gamma_{T}\log(K)\log^{2}\left(KT\right)}{|\mathcal{A}|^{2}T}}

Hence, if
|\mathcal{A}|
and
K
do not vary with
T
then for any
\gamma_{T}\rightarrow\infty
such that
T/\log^{2}T\gg\gamma_{T}
it follows that
\frac{1}{T}\sum_{t=1}^{T}w_{t,k}-w^{\star}_{T,k}\;\stackrel{{\scriptstyle p}}{{\rightarrow}}\;0
for all experts
k\in[K]
.

Now we are ready to prove stability of
S_{T}
. We first decompose
S_{T}
into two parts:

\displaystyle S_{T}=M_{T}+B_{T}\qquad\text{where}

(36a)

\displaystyle D_{t}:=Y_{t}-\mathbb{E}[Y_{t}\mid\mathcal{F}_{t-1}],\;\;M_{T}:=\sum_{t=1}^{T}D_{t},\;\;\text{and}\;\;B_{T}:=\sum_{t=1}^{T}\mathbb{E}[Y_{t}\mid\mathcal{F}_{t-1}].

(36b)

Now, we define

\displaystyle\frac{1}{T}{\Sigma^{\star}_{T}}:=\sum^{K}_{k=1}w^{\star}_{T,k}\Sigma_{k}

(37)

where

\displaystyle\Sigma_{k}:=\mathbb{E}_{x\sim\mathcal{P_{X}}}\left(\sum_{a\in\mathcal{A}}\pi_{k}(a\mid x)\cdot c(x,a)c(x,a)^{\top}\right)

We show, using Lemma
2
stated below, that

\displaystyle\dfrac{S_{T}}{T}=\dfrac{M_{T}}{T}+\dfrac{B_{T}}{T}=\underbrace{\dfrac{M_{T}}{T}}_{o_{\mathbb{P}}(1)}+\underbrace{\left[\dfrac{B_{T}}{T}-\frac{1}{T}{\Sigma^{\star}_{T}}\right]}_{o_{\mathbb{P}}(1)}+\frac{1}{T}{\Sigma^{\star}_{T}}

(38)

Lemma 2
.

Let
\Sigma_{k},M_{T}
and
B_{T}
be as defined in equations (
36a
). Suppose that there exists non-random weight vector
w^{\star}_{T}=(w^{\star}_{T,1},\ldots,w^{\star}_{T,K})^{\top}
such that
\frac{1}{T}\sum_{t=1}^{T}w_{t,k}-w^{\star}_{T,k}\;\stackrel{{\scriptstyle p}}{{\rightarrow}}\;0
for all experts
k\in[K]
. Then we have,

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[\bigg|\bigg|\dfrac{M_{T}}{T}\bigg|\bigg|_{op}\right]\lesssim\dfrac{1}{\sqrt{T}}\quad\text{and},\quad\mathbb{E}\left[\bigg|\bigg|\dfrac{B_{T}}{T}-\sum^{K}_{k=1}w^{\star}_{T,k}\Sigma_{k}\bigg|\bigg|_{op}\right]\leq\mathbb{E}[\|\bar{w}_{T}-w^{\star}_{T}\|].

(39)

We prove this Lemma in Appendix
C
. Let
\lambda^{\star}
be the minimum eigenvalue among
\lambda_{\min}(\Sigma_{k})
for all
k\in[K]
. Note that
\lambda_{\min}\left(\frac{1}{T}{\Sigma^{\star}_{T}}\right)\geq\lambda^{\star}>0
by Assumption
(A3)
. Hence, the minimum eigenvalue of
\Sigma_{T}^{\star}/T
— which is a convex combination of
\Sigma_{k}^{\prime}s
— is also lower bounded by
\lambda^{\star}
. This fact combined with the decomposition (
38
) yields

\displaystyle{\Sigma^{\star}_{T}}^{-1}S_{T}\xrightarrow{\mathbb{P}}\mathbf{I}.

.

Part (b) : Proof of stability condition for ridge estimator

In this section we show that if we consider a ridge estimator, with penalty term
\lambda_{rid}\ll\sqrt{T}
, then Theorem
1
holds. Let
R_{T}:=S_{T}+\lambda_{rid}\ \mathbf{I}
and consider the ridge estimator

\hat{\beta}_{rid}:=R_{T}^{-1}S_{T}\ \beta^{\star}+\ R_{T}^{-1}\sum^{T}_{t=1}z_{t}\varepsilon_{t}
, where
z_{t}=c(x_{t},a_{t})
. Our statistic of interest is the following

\displaystyle\mathcal{R}_{1}(T):=\dfrac{a^{\top}(\hat{\beta}_{rid}-\beta^{\star})}{\sqrt{a^{\top}R_{T}^{-1}a}}

(40)

We are interested to show that for the design matrix
R_{T}=S_{T}+\lambda_{rid}\mathbf{I}
, there exists a sequence of deterministic, positive definite matrices
\{\tilde{\Sigma}_{T}\}
such that

\displaystyle\tilde{\Sigma}^{-1}_{T}R_{T}\xrightarrow{\mathbb{P}}\mathbf{I}

(41)

Now, if we choose our penalty term
\lambda_{rid}\ll T
then for the ridge estimator we have the decomposition of
R_{T}
:

\displaystyle\dfrac{R_{T}}{T}=\underbrace{\dfrac{M_{T}}{T}}_{o_{\mathbb{P}}(1)}+\underbrace{\left[\dfrac{B_{T}}{T}-\frac{1}{T}{\Sigma^{\star}_{T}}\right]}_{o_{\mathbb{P}}(1)}+\underbrace{\frac{\lambda_{rid}}{T}}_{o_{\mathbb{P}}(1)}\mathbf{I}+\frac{1}{T}{\Sigma^{\star}_{T}}

(42)

Therefore, applying Lemma
2
along with the fact that
\lambda_{\min}\left(\frac{1}{T}{\Sigma^{\star}_{T}}\right)>0
shows that property (
41
) is satisfied by choosing
\tilde{\Sigma}_{T}={\Sigma^{\star}_{T}}
, where
{\Sigma^{\star}_{T}}
is as defined in equation (
37
).

By substituting the value of
\hat{\beta}_{rid}
in equation (
40
), we obtain the following decomposition

\displaystyle\mathcal{R}_{1}(T)=\underbrace{\dfrac{a^{\top}(R_{T}^{-1}S_{T}-\mathbf{I})}{\sqrt{a^{\top}R_{T}^{-1}a}}\beta^{\star}}_{\mathcal{T}_{1}(T)}\ \ +\ \ \underbrace{\dfrac{a^{\top}R_{T}^{-1}}{\sqrt{a^{\top}R_{T}^{-1}a}}\sum^{T}_{t=1}z_{t}\varepsilon_{t}}_{\mathcal{T}_{2}(T)}

(43)

The term
\mathcal{T}_{1}(T)
in equation (
43
) is the bias induced by the regularization. As
R_{T}:=S_{T}+\lambda_{rid}\ \mathbf{I}
we note that
R_{T}^{-1}S_{T}-\mathbf{I}=R_{T}^{-1}(S_{T}-R_{T})
, which is equal to
-\lambda_{rid}\ R_{T}^{-1}
. Therefore,
\mathcal{T}_{1}(T)
can be rewritten as

\displaystyle\mathcal{T}_{1}(T)=-\lambda_{rid}\dfrac{a^{\top}R_{T}^{-1}}{\sqrt{a^{\top}R_{T}^{-1}a}}\beta^{\star}

Let
k_{T}:=\sqrt{a^{\top}\left(\frac{1}{T}{\Sigma^{\star}_{T}}\right)^{-1}a}/\sqrt{a^{\top}R_{T}^{-1}a}
and
\mathcal{M}_{T}=k_{T}\times R_{T}^{-1}{\Sigma^{\star}_{T}}
. Then,

\displaystyle\mathcal{T}_{1}(T)=\frac{-\lambda_{rid}}{\sqrt{T}}\dfrac{a^{\top}\left(\frac{1}{T}{\Sigma^{\star}_{T}}\right)^{-1}}{\sqrt{a^{\top}\left(\frac{1}{T}{\Sigma^{\star}_{T}}\right)^{-1}a}}\beta^{\star}+\frac{-\lambda_{rid}}{\sqrt{T}}\dfrac{a^{\top}[\mathcal{M}_{T}-\mathbf{I}]\left(\frac{1}{T}{\Sigma^{\star}_{T}}\right)^{-1}}{\sqrt{a^{\top}\left(\frac{1}{T}{\Sigma^{\star}_{T}}\right)^{-1}a}}\beta^{\star}

Therefore, as
\lambda_{\min}(\frac{1}{T}{\Sigma^{\star}_{T}})
is uniformly bounded away from zero and
\lambda_{\max}(\frac{1}{T}{\Sigma^{\star}_{T}})
is uniformly bounded above, it follows from stability (equation (
41
)) that by choosing
\lambda_{rid}\ll\sqrt{T}
,
\mathcal{M}_{T}\xrightarrow{\mathbb{P}}\mathbf{I}
and consequently, the bias
\mathcal{T}_{1}(T)
converges to
0
in probability, as
T\rightarrow\infty
.

Now, for
\mathcal{T}_{2}(T)
we have a similar decomposition:

\displaystyle\mathcal{T}_{2}(T)=\underbrace{\dfrac{a^{\top}\left(\frac{1}{T}{\Sigma^{\star}_{T}}\right)^{-1}}{\sqrt{a^{\top}\left(\frac{1}{T}{\Sigma^{\star}_{T}}\right)^{-1}a}}\frac{1}{\sqrt{T}}\sum^{T}_{t=1}z_{t}\varepsilon_{t}}_{I_{1}(T)}\ \ +\ \ \underbrace{\dfrac{a^{\top}[\mathcal{M}_{T}-\mathbf{I}]\left(\frac{1}{T}{\Sigma^{\star}_{T}}\right)^{-1}}{\sqrt{a^{\top}\left(\frac{1}{T}{\Sigma^{\star}_{T}}\right)^{-1}a}}\frac{1}{\sqrt{T}}\sum^{T}_{t=1}z_{t}\varepsilon_{t}}_{I_{2}(T)}

Now, from central limit theorem for martingale difference sequence (
15
),
I_{1}(T)\xrightarrow{d}\mathcal{N}(0,1)
. This result, combined with the fact that
\mathcal{M}_{T}\xrightarrow{\mathbb{P}}\mathbf{I}
implies
I_{2}(T)
converges to
0
in probability. This completes the proof.

6.2
Proof of Theorem
2

We first restrict the comparator
w
to the truncated simplex. Without loss of generality we assume arm
1
is
among
the optimal arms. Now define

\displaystyle w^{\star}=(1,0,\ldots,0)\qquad\text{and}\qquad w_{\epsilon}=\big(1-(K-1)\varepsilon,\;\varepsilon,\ldots,\varepsilon\big)\in\Delta_{\varepsilon}.

To prove this theorem, we shall apply the master equation stated below.

Lemma 3
.

For any sequence of contexts
\{x_{t}\}_{t=1}^{T}
and stochastic losses
\{\ell_{t}\}_{t=1}^{T}
,
the outputs

Conclusion

We studied statistical inference in linear contextual bandits under adaptive data collection, with a
focus on the validity of classical Wald-type confidence intervals. By introducing a penalized variant
of the EXP4 algorithm, we demonstrated that it is possible to simultaneously enforce the Lai–Wei
stability condition and achieve regret guarantees that are minimax optimal up to logarithmic
factors. As a consequence, ordinary least-squares estimators satisfy a central limit theorem, and
Wald confidence intervals for linear functionals are asymptotically valid without incurring the

\sqrt{d\log T}
price of adaptivity. Together, these results establish that stability and statistical efficiency are not
fundamentally incompatible in contextual bandit problems, and that careful algorithmic design can
recover classical inferential guarantees even under adaptive sampling.

Several directions remain open for future work. It would be of interest to extend the present
analysis to settings with growing feature dimension or fully adaptive
contexts. Understanding whether analogous stability–regret tradeoffs can be achieved remains an important and challenging question.

Auxiliary Lemmas

In this section we present some well-known results on Bregman divergence for the sake of completeness, followed by the proofs of Lemma

Some Results on Bregman Divergence

We begin with two standard identities for Bregman divergences that will be used repeatedly.
Let
\phi:\mathcal{D}\to\mathbb{R}
be a differentiable, strictly convex function on an open convex set
\mathcal{D}\subset\mathbb{R}^{K}
.
The Bregman divergence generated by
\phi
is

D_{\phi}(x,y)\;=\;\phi(x)-\phi(y)-\langle\nabla\phi(y),\,x-y\rangle,\qquad x,y\in\mathcal{D}.

Lemma 6

(Bregman three-point identity)
.

For any differentiable and strictly convex function
\phi:\mathcal{D}\to\mathbb{R}

and any
x,x^{+},y\in\mathcal{D}
,

\big\langle\nabla\phi(x)-\nabla\phi(x^{+}),\,x-y\big\rangle\;=\;D_{\phi}(y,x)\;-\;D_{\phi}(y,x^{+})\;+\;D_{\phi}(x,x^{+}),

where
D_{\phi}(u,v)=\phi(u)-\phi(v)-\langle\nabla\phi(v),\,u-v\rangle

is the Bregman divergence generated by
\phi
.

Proof.

By the definition of the Bregman divergence,

\displaystyle D_{\phi}(y,x)-D_{\phi}(y,x^{+})+D_{\phi}(x,x^{+})

\displaystyle=\big(\phi(y)-\phi(x)-\langle\nabla\phi(x),y-x\rangle\big)-\big(\phi(y)-\phi(x^{+})-\langle\nabla\phi(x^{+}),y-x^{+}\rangle\big)

\displaystyle\quad\;\;+\big(\phi(x)-\phi(x^{+})-\langle\nabla\phi(x^{+}),x-x^{+}\rangle\big).

The
\phi(\cdot)
terms cancel, giving

-\langle\nabla\phi(x),y-x\rangle+\langle\nabla\phi(x^{+}),y-x^{+}\rangle-\langle\nabla\phi(x^{+}),x-x^{+}\rangle.

Since
y-x^{+}=(y-x)+(x-x^{+})
, the last two inner products combine to

\langle\nabla\phi(x^{+}),y-x\rangle
.
Thus the entire expression simplifies to

\langle\nabla\phi(x^{+})-\nabla\phi(x),\,y-x\rangle\;=\;\langle\nabla\phi(x)-\nabla\phi(x^{+}),\,x-y\rangle,

which proves the desired identity.
∎

Definition 2

(Bregman projection)
.

Let
X\subset\mathcal{D}
be closed and convex.
For
y\in\mathcal{D}
, the (right) Bregman projection of
y
onto
X
is

\Pi_{X}^{\phi}(y)\;\in\;\arg\min_{x\in X}D_{\phi}(x,y).

Lemma 7

(Bregman Pythagorean theorem)
.

Let
X\subset\mathcal{D}
be nonempty, closed, and convex, and let
x^{+}=\Pi_{X}^{\phi}(y)
.
Then for all
x\in X
,

D_{\phi}(x,y)\;\geq\;D_{\phi}(x,x^{+})\;+\;D_{\phi}(x^{+},y).

Proof.

By first-order optimality for the convex problem
\min_{x\in X}D_{\phi}(x,y)
,

\big\langle\nabla_{x}D_{\phi}(x,y)\big|_{x=x^{+}},\,x-x^{+}\big\rangle\;\geq\;0\quad\text{for all }x\in X.

Since
\nabla_{x}D_{\phi}(x,y)=\nabla\phi(x)-\nabla\phi(y)
, we have

\big\langle\nabla\phi(x^{+})-\nabla\phi(y),\,x-x^{+}\big\rangle\;\geq\;0.

Apply Lemma
6
with
(x,y,z)=(x^{+},y,x)
to obtain

\big\langle\nabla\phi(x^{+})-\nabla\phi(y),\,x-x^{+}\big\rangle=D_{\phi}(x,x^{+})-D_{\phi}(x,y)+D_{\phi}(x^{+},y).

Rearranging yields the stated inequality.
∎

Proof of Lemma

|\ell_{t}|\leq 1
almost surely.
By definition of the local dual norm,

\displaystyle\|\widehat{g}_{t}\|_{w_{t},*}^{2}=\sum_{k=1}^{K}w_{t,k}\widehat{g}_{t,k}^{2}=\ell_{t}^{2}\sum_{k=1}^{K}w_{t,k}\,\frac{\pi_{k}^{2}(a_{t}\mid x_{t})}{Q_{t}^{2}(a_{t}\mid x_{t})}.

Conditioning on
x_{t}
and summing over
a_{t}\sim Q_{t}(\cdot\mid x_{t})
gives

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\big[\|\widehat{g}_{t}\|_{w_{t},*}^{2}\mid x_{t}\big]

\displaystyle=\sum_{a\in\mathcal{A}}Q_{t}(a\mid x_{t})\,\Big\{\ell_{t}^{2}\sum_{k=1}^{K}w_{t,k}\,\frac{\pi_{k}^{2}(a\mid x_{t})}{Q_{t}^{2}(a\mid x_{t})}\Big\}

\displaystyle=\sum_{a\in\mathcal{A}}\ell_{t}^{2}\sum_{k=1}^{K}w_{t,k}\,\frac{\pi_{k}^{2}(a\mid x_{t})}{Q_{t}(a\mid x_{t})}

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{a\in\mathcal{A}}\ell_{t}^{2}\sum_{k=1}^{K}w_{t,k}\,\frac{\pi_{k}(a\mid x_{t})\,\max_{j}\pi_{j}(a\mid x_{t})}{Q_{t}(a\mid x_{t})}

\displaystyle=\sum_{a\in\mathcal{A}}\ell_{t}^{2}\,\max_{j}\pi_{j}(a\mid x_{t})\frac{\sum_{k=1}^{K}w_{t,k}\pi_{k}(a\mid x_{t})}{Q_{t}(a\mid x_{t})}

\displaystyle=\sum_{a\in\mathcal{A}}\ell_{t}^{2}\,\max_{j}\pi_{j}(a\mid x_{t})\;\leq\;|\mathcal{A}|\,\max_{x,a}\ell_{t}^{2}\;\leq\;|\mathcal{A}|,

where we used
Q_{t}(a\mid x_{t})=\sum_{k}w_{t,k}\pi_{k}(a\mid x_{t})
in the third line,
and
\max_{x,a}\ell_{t}^{2}\leq 1
in the last inequality. This completes the proof.
