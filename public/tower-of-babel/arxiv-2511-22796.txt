Correlations between superconducting and resistive anisotropies
Sayan Banerjee, Harley D. Scammell, Mathias S. Scheurer

Abstract
There are multiple possible origins of transport anisotropies in metals and superconductors. For instance, rotational symmetry can be spontaneously broken in the normal state as a result of electronic nematic order inducing anisotropies in an otherwise s s -wave superconducting phase. Another possibility is that the dominant source of rotational symmetry breaking is the superconductor itself and its vestiges that may survive in the normal state. We here theoretically analyze the correlations of transport anisotropies in the normal and the corresponding superconducting phase for different scenarios of broken symmetry, either coming solely from the normal state, solely from the superconductor and its vestiges in the metallic regimes, or from both simultaneously. We further include both zero-momentum and finite-momentum pairing; we develop a theory of vestigial order for the latter, characterized by broken rotational and translational symmetry. Our findings reveal that the relative transport anisotropies in the normal and superconducting phases sensitively depend on the scenario, including the form of vestigial order and, in some cases, the parity of the superconducting order parameter. As such, measuring the directional dependence of the critical current and resistivity can provide strong constraints on the origin of rotational symmetry breaking. We demonstrate our findings in minimal models relevant to twisted multilayer graphene, rhombohedral graphene, and twisted transition metal dichalcogenides.

Symmetries are a key guiding principle for our understanding of both metallic and superconducting states

In contrast, for strongly interacting systems, electronic correlations can drive additional symmetry breaking transitions that do not stem from the lattice itself. In the case of electronic nematic order

When a superconductor (SC) emerges from such a symmetry-reduced electron liquid, the superconducting condensate will inherit the same (broken) symmetries, resulting in anisotropic superconducting properties. Intervalley pairing

II.1
General Formalism

Before presenting explicit calculations, we start by discussing the general methodology to extract normal state and resistive properties for intervalley-paired SCs. We shall first focus on the case where the normal state has TRS and the band dispersions in the two valleys,
\nu=\pm
, are just mirror images of one another, related by
\boldsymbol{k}\rightarrow-\boldsymbol{k}
.
Although the generalization to include spin is straightforward, we will, for the simplicity of the presentation, assume that either the Fermi surfaces are spin polarized or spin and valley are locked. This applies to twisted TMDs, such as twisted WSe
2

36
;
102
, as a result of the strong spin-orbit coupling and is further natural for the spin polarized half-metal phases in rhombohedral graphene
38

2

104
, or for pairing around filling fraction
2
in twisted multi-layer graphene
10
;
65

As a result of TRS, pairing between opposite valleys and momenta
\boldsymbol{k}
and
-\boldsymbol{k}
is favored, and the superconducting order parameter has to transform under an irreducible representation (IR) of the normal-state point group. While interband pairing can lead to interesting superconducting properties
21
;
79
, we will here focus on IRs with band-diagonal order parameters. Then the relevant active low-energy electrons can be described by the creation operators
c^{\dagger}_{\boldsymbol{k},\nu}
and the pairing Hamiltonian is given as

\displaystyle\begin{split}\mathcal{H}_{c}=g_{c}\sum_{\boldsymbol{k}}\sum_{j=1}^{d_{\Gamma}}\left[\eta_{j}(\boldsymbol{q})f_{j}(\boldsymbol{k})c^{\dagger}_{\boldsymbol{k}+\boldsymbol{q},+}c^{\dagger}_{-\boldsymbol{k}+\boldsymbol{q},-}+\text{H.c.}\right].\end{split}

(1)

Here,
j
runs over
d_{\Gamma}
components of the IR
\Gamma
with form factors
f_{j}(\boldsymbol{k})
. For the point groups relevant to us here, only
d_{\Gamma}=1,2
are possible and we will discuss examples with both
d_{\Gamma}=1
and
d_{\Gamma}=2
in this work.

As explained above, our goal is to compare transport anisotropies in the superconducting and normal state. As such, we will consider normal-state Hamiltonians,
\mathcal{H}_{0}=\sum_{\boldsymbol{k},\nu=\pm}c^{\dagger}_{\boldsymbol{k},\nu}E_{\boldsymbol{k},\nu}^{{\phantom{\dagger}}}c^{\phantom{\dagger}}_{\boldsymbol{k},\nu}
, where
C_{3z}
rotational symmetry is broken. The associated dispersion can take either of the following forms:

\displaystyle E_{\boldsymbol{k},\nu}

\displaystyle=\xi_{\boldsymbol{k},\nu}+\boldsymbol{\Phi}\cdot\boldsymbol{g}(\boldsymbol{k})\quad\text{or}

(2a)

\displaystyle E_{\boldsymbol{k},\nu}

\displaystyle=\xi_{\boldsymbol{k},\nu}+\delta\epsilon_{\boldsymbol{k},\nu}.

(2b)

Here
\xi_{\boldsymbol{k},\nu}=\epsilon_{\nu\cdot\boldsymbol{k}}
, such that
\xi_{\boldsymbol{k},\nu}=\xi_{-\boldsymbol{k},-\nu}
, is the single-particle dispersion for valley
\nu
, which obeys
\epsilon_{C_{3z}\boldsymbol{k}}=\epsilon_{\boldsymbol{k}}
. This first form (
2a
) describes symmetry breaking as a consequence of moiré nematic order or effectively the presence of strain
52
;
17
;
90
;
50
;
11
;
61
, which we will parameterized by
\boldsymbol{\Phi}=\Phi(\cos{\theta},\sin{\theta})
; meanwhile, Eq. (
2b
) models the consequence of a vestigial order
\delta\epsilon_{\nu,\boldsymbol{k}}
that modifies the bare dispersion. These are the two principal sources of breaking the symmetry that we will discuss in this work. To preserve TRS (and, if present,
C_{2z}
symmetry), we choose
\boldsymbol{g}(\boldsymbol{k})
to be even in momentum
\boldsymbol{k}
and to transform as a vector under
C_{3z}
(specifically IR
E
of the point group
D_{3}
or
E_{2}
of
D_{6}
). In the limit of small
\boldsymbol{k}
, its leading behavior is given by
\boldsymbol{g}(\boldsymbol{k})\sim(k_{y}^{2}-k_{x}^{2},2k_{x}k_{y})
and we take the Brillouin-zone-periodic extension with the fewest number of nodal lines.

In order to probe such
C_{3z}
symmetry breaking in experiments, one typically measures the resistivity/conductivity tensor, which is sensitive to such anisotropies. Within a Boltzmann transport approach, the conductivity tensor in the relaxation-time approximation is given as (see e.g.
81
where a similar notation is used)

\sigma^{\phantom{\dagger}}_{\alpha\beta}=\frac{2e^{2}\tau}{\mathcal{V}}\sum_{\nu=\pm}\left\langle(\boldsymbol{v}^{\phantom{\dagger}}_{\boldsymbol{k},\nu})^{\phantom{\dagger}}_{\alpha}(\boldsymbol{v}^{\phantom{\dagger}}_{\boldsymbol{k},\nu})^{\phantom{\dagger}}_{\beta}\right\rangle_{\nu},

(3a)

where
(\alpha,\beta)\in\{x,y\}
and all the nonuniversal properties are encoded in the generalized average

\braket{\dots}_{\nu}\equiv\frac{4}{TN}\sum_{\boldsymbol{k}}\frac{\tau^{\phantom{\dagger}}_{\boldsymbol{k},\nu}/\tau}{\cosh^{2}\hskip-1.00006pt\left(\frac{E_{\boldsymbol{k},\nu}-\mu}{2T}\right)}\dots

(3b)

and in the velocities
\boldsymbol{v}_{\boldsymbol{k},\nu}

=

\boldsymbol{\nabla}_{\boldsymbol{k}}E_{\boldsymbol{k},\nu}
. Here,
N
is the number of moiré unit cells,
\mathcal{V}
is the area of the moiré unit cell, and
\tau_{\boldsymbol{k},\nu}^{-1}
is the relaxation-time scattering rate for momentum
\boldsymbol{k}
in valley
\nu
. The average scattering time is defined as
\tau

=

N^{-1}\sum_{\boldsymbol{k}}\tau_{\boldsymbol{k},\nu}
, which is the same in both valleys, due to TRS. Considering a current applied along
a direction that makes an angle
\Omega
with the
x
axis, and assuming a momentum-independent scattering time
\tau_{\boldsymbol{k},\nu}=\text{const.}
, one can now extract the angular dependence of longitudinal resistivity as

\rho^{\phantom{\dagger}}_{L}(\Omega)=\frac{1}{\sigma_{0}}-\frac{1}{\sigma_{0}^{2}}\left(\lambda\cos 2\Omega+\delta\sin 2\Omega\right),

(4)

where
\lambda=(\sigma_{xx}-\sigma_{yy})/2
,
\sigma_{0}=(\sigma_{xx}+\sigma_{yy})/2
and
\delta=\sigma_{xy}
.

Equipped with normal state transport, we now focus on the superconducting phase. Assuming that the SC arises from such a normal state, we take an attractive interaction (
g_{c}>0
) and perform a mean field decoupling to obtain
H_{s}=\mathcal{H}_{0}+\mathcal{H}_{c}+\sum_{\boldsymbol{q},j}|\eta_{j}(\boldsymbol{q})|^{2}/g_{c}
. Integrating out the electrons, and expanding up to quadratic order in the free energy, we get

\mathcal{F}\sim\sum_{\boldsymbol{q},j=1}^{d_{\Gamma}}a_{j}(\boldsymbol{q})|\eta_{j}(\boldsymbol{q})|^{2}+\mathcal{O}(\eta^{4}).

(5)

It holds
a_{j}(\boldsymbol{q})=\frac{1}{g_{c}}-\Gamma^{j}(\boldsymbol{q})
with

\Gamma^{j}(\boldsymbol{q})=\frac{1}{2N}\sum_{\boldsymbol{k}}\frac{\tanh{\frac{\mathcal{E}^{\Phi}_{\boldsymbol{k},\boldsymbol{q},+}}{2T}}+\tanh{\frac{\mathcal{E}^{\Phi}_{\boldsymbol{k},\boldsymbol{q},-}}{2T}}}{\mathcal{E}^{\Phi}_{\boldsymbol{k},\boldsymbol{q},+}+\mathcal{E}^{\Phi}_{\boldsymbol{k},\boldsymbol{q},-}}|f_{j}(\boldsymbol{k})|^{2},

(6)

where
N
is the number of unit cells and
\mathcal{E}_{\boldsymbol{k},\boldsymbol{q},\nu}^{\Phi}=E^{\Phi}_{\boldsymbol{k}+\nu\boldsymbol{q},+}

Restricting the analysis to single-

states,
\eta(\boldsymbol{q})\propto\delta_{\boldsymbol{q},\boldsymbol{q}_{0}}
, we can then obtain the current
\boldsymbol{J}(\boldsymbol{q})=2e\partial_{\boldsymbol{q}}\mathcal{F}
. The critical current

along a certain direction

oriented along

To compare the relative anisotropies between the superconducting critical current and normal state resistivity, we define the quantity

\zeta_{xy}:=\frac{\text{sgn}[\bar{J}_{c}(\Omega=0)-\bar{J}_{c}(\Omega=\pi/2)]}{\text{sgn}[\bar{\rho}_{L}(\Omega=0)-\bar{\rho}_{L}(\Omega=\pi/2)]}.

We here defined

By construction,

instead of stating whether the maxima of

and

\Omega=0
and
\pi/2
[where there are technically only local minima, pinned by reflection symmetries, see, e.g., Fig.
3
(a)]. In those cases,

is a more useful quantity to distinguish the behavior.

Nematic SC

Arising from

Next, we shall explore the scenario where the source of

\displaystyle\begin{split}S_{\text{int}}=&\int_{k,q}[\boldsymbol{f}(\boldsymbol{k})\cdot\boldsymbol{\eta}(q)c_{k+q,-}^{\dagger}c_{-k+q,+}^{\dagger}+\text{H.c.}]\\

III.1
Vestigial phases of superconducting order

To develop a theory of vestigial phases of this form of finite-momentum superconductivity, we start from the associated action description,

\displaystyle\begin{split}S_{3q}&=S_{0}+\sum_{j=1}^{3}\int_{k,\delta q}\eta_{j}(\delta q)f(\boldsymbol{k})c_{k+q_{j}+\delta q}^{\dagger}c_{-k+q_{j}+\delta q}^{\dagger}\\
&\quad+\sum_{j=1}^{3}\int_{\delta q}\left|\eta_{j}(\delta\boldsymbol{q})\right|^{2}\left(m^{2}+\delta\boldsymbol{q}^{2}+\Omega_{n}^{2}\right)+S_{V},\end{split}

(23)

where
S_{0}=\int_{k}c_{k}^{\dagger}(-i\omega_{n}+\epsilon_{\boldsymbol{k}})c^{{\phantom{\dagger}}}_{k}
and

S_{V}=\int_{x}\left[u(\text{tr}[\rho])^{2}+v\sum_{j\neq j^{\prime}=1}^{3}\rho_{j,j^{\prime}}\rho_{j^{\prime},j}\right].

(24)

Here, we introduced
\rho_{j,j^{\prime}}=\eta_{j}^{*}\eta_{j^{\prime}}
as possible composite order parameters. As before, we decouple the quartic terms using Hubbard-Stratonovich fields

\displaystyle\begin{split}S_{V}\rightarrow&\int_{x}\left[\sum_{j\neq j^{\prime}}[\rho_{j,j^{\prime}}\psi_{j^{\prime},j}+\text{H.c}]+i\sum_{j}\phi_{j}\rho_{j,j}\right]\\
&+\int_{x}\sum_{j}\frac{1}{4u}\phi^{2}_{j}-\int_{x}\sum_{j\neq j^{\prime}}\frac{1}{2\tilde{v}}\psi_{j,j^{\prime}}\psi_{j^{\prime},j}.\end{split}

(25)

\tilde{v}=u+v

on the saddle-point level, which becomes exact in the limit where the number of components of
\boldsymbol{\eta}
is infinitely large (large
N

1\boldsymbol{q}
state, one would only have one of the components of

2\boldsymbol{q}

1\boldsymbol{q}
state to Appendix

3

\displaystyle=-\sum_{j}\int_{\delta q}\frac{1}{[\tilde{\chi}_{j}(\delta q)]^{-1}}D_{j}^{\dagger}(\delta\boldsymbol{q})D_{j}^{{\phantom{\dagger}}}(\delta\boldsymbol{q}),

\displaystyle S_{2}

IV
Conclusion

In this work, we have studied the relation between the angular dependence of the normal-state resistivity
\rho(\Omega)
and of the critical current
J_{c}(\Omega)
for different microscopic origins of rotational symmetry breaking, which we summarize in Table
1
. Our study is primarily motivated by two-dimensional systems, such as rhombohedral graphene or twisted van der Waals materials, where these angular dependencies can be accessed via sunbeam Hall bar geometries
16
;
67
and rotational symmetry breaking and superconductivity are ubiquitous in the phase diagram. This is why we use models with two valley degrees of freedom and a hexagonal lattice symmetry, although the formalism can be straightforwardly applied to other scenarios as well.

We started with the scenario in which the only source of rotational symmetry breaking comes from the normal state, while the superconducting order parameter is isotropic. In that case, the maxima of the critical current and of the normal-state resistivity occur along perpendicular directions. Since these maxima need not be perfectly aligned with high-symmetry directions of the lattice, we here use
\zeta_{xy}=-1
[see Eq. (
7
) for formal definition] to compactly describe the situation of misaligned anisotropies along the two perpendicular high-symmetry directions
\Omega=0
and
\pi/2
.
We then contrasted this behavior with the scenario where also the superconducting order itself is nematic, i.e., transforms non-trivially under rotations, taking into account that the normal-state anisotropies determine the specific superconducting order parameter configuration. Here, the result depends on the parity of the superconducting order parameter
\Delta(\boldsymbol{k})
. If
\Delta(\boldsymbol{k})=\Delta(-\boldsymbol{k})
(“nematic, even” in Table
1
), we obtain the same behavior as in the first scenario with an isotropic pairing state. However, an odd-parity order parameter,
\Delta(\boldsymbol{k})=-\Delta(-\boldsymbol{k})
, yields the opposite behavior,
\zeta_{xy}=1
, if the strain/nematic order in the normal state is sufficiently weak. If the latter is large enough to dominate the anisotropy inside the superconductor, we are then effectively again back to the first scenario and find
\zeta_{xy}=-1
.

The third distinct type of scenario arises when the superconductor is the
only
source of broken rotational symmetry, including in the resistive state via the emergence of a vestigial nematic state. We show that this leads to the opposite behavior,
\zeta_{xy}=1
, i.e., the maximal critical current and normal-state resistivity are along the same direction, irrespective of the parity of the order parameter.

Finally, we also considered pairing within a single valley, i.e., when time-reversal symmetry is already broken in the normal state, which naturally leads to pairing at finite center-of-mass momentum
\boldsymbol{q}\neq 0
. This, in turn, allows for interesting forms of non-superconducting vestigial orders. Motivated by the experimental results in Ref.
67
, we focus on a translational-symmetry-breaking unidirectional pair-density wave (
2\boldsymbol{q}
PDW in Table
1
) which also leads to a charge-density modulated vestigial phase in the normal state. We show that both
\zeta_{xy}=-1
and
\zeta_{xy}=1
are possible, depending on whether the charge-density modulations or additional translational-symmetry-preserving terms associated with the superconducting vestiges dominate.

Taken together, our findings show that these relative transport anisotropies in the superconductor and related normal state can provide crucial insights. For instance, the maximum critical current and normal-state resistance being aligned, as seen in
113
, indicates that the superconducting order parameter is either odd parity with its direction determined by a normal-state anisotropy that is unrelated to the superconductor or the dominant source of rotational symmetry breaking comes from the superconductor alone, including in the normal state due to nematic paraconductivity or vestigial nematic order. If the relative anisotropy
\zeta_{xy}
is found to change sign, e.g., as a function of displacement field or filling, only the former of the two scenarios is naturally consistent. Furthermore, our findings for the valley polarized case show that the observations of Ref.
67
, where
\zeta_{xy}=-1
, imply that the charge-density modulated part of the vestigial order dominates.

Appendix A
Intervalley pairing: Mean field theory of nematic vestigial order

In this section, we derive the mean-field theory for the vestigial order arising from the nematic superconductor with pairing in between valleys. Reiterating the effective action from the main text,

\displaystyle\begin{split}S_{\text{int}}&=\int_{k,q}[\boldsymbol{f}(\boldsymbol{k})\cdot\boldsymbol{\eta}(q)c_{k+q,-}^{\dagger}c_{-k+q,+}^{\dagger}+\text{H.c.}]+\int_{x}\frac{1}{4v}\boldsymbol{\tilde{\Phi}}^{2}+\int_{q}\boldsymbol{\eta}^{\dagger}(q)\begin{pmatrix}m_{1}^{2}+\boldsymbol{q}^{2}+\Omega_{n}^{2}&0\\
0&m_{2}^{2}+\boldsymbol{q}^{2}+\Omega_{n}^{2}\\
\end{pmatrix}\boldsymbol{\eta}(q).\end{split}

(30)

We now integrate out the fields
\boldsymbol{\eta},\boldsymbol{\eta}^{\dagger}
to obtain a four-fermion effective interaction of the form

S_{\text{int}}^{\text{eff}}=-\sum_{k,k^{\prime},q,j}\frac{f_{j}(\boldsymbol{k})f_{j}(\boldsymbol{k}^{\prime})}{m_{i}^{2}+\boldsymbol{q}^{2}+\Omega^{2}_{n}}c_{k+q,-}^{\dagger}c_{-k+q,+}^{\dagger}c_{-k^{\prime}+q,+}^{{\phantom{\dagger}}}c_{k^{\prime}+q,-}^{{\phantom{\dagger}}}.

(31)

From this, we then obtain an effective Hamiltonian of the form

H^{\text{eff}}=\sum_{\boldsymbol{k},\nu}\xi_{\boldsymbol{k},\nu}c_{\boldsymbol{k},\nu}^{\dagger}c_{\boldsymbol{k},\nu}^{{\phantom{\dagger}}}-\sum_{\boldsymbol{k},\boldsymbol{k^{\prime}},\boldsymbol{q},j=1,2}\frac{f_{j}(\boldsymbol{k})f_{j}(\boldsymbol{k}^{\prime})}{m_{j}^{2}+\boldsymbol{q}^{2}}c_{\boldsymbol{k}+\boldsymbol{q},-}^{\dagger}c_{-\boldsymbol{k}+\boldsymbol{q},+}^{\dagger}c_{-\boldsymbol{k}^{\prime}+\boldsymbol{q},+}^{{\phantom{\dagger}}}c_{\boldsymbol{k}^{\prime}+\boldsymbol{q},-}^{{\phantom{\dagger}}}

(32)

Performing a mean-field decoupling in the direct channel, we get

H^{\text{eff}}_{\text{MF}}=\sum_{\boldsymbol{k},\nu}\epsilon_{\boldsymbol{k},\nu}c_{\boldsymbol{k},\nu}^{\dagger}c_{\boldsymbol{k},\nu}^{{\phantom{\dagger}}}-\sum_{\boldsymbol{k},\boldsymbol{q},j=1,2}\frac{f_{j}^{2}(\boldsymbol{k})}{m_{j}^{2}+\boldsymbol{q}^{2}}[\langle c_{\boldsymbol{k}+\boldsymbol{q},-}^{\dagger}c_{\boldsymbol{k}+\boldsymbol{q},-}^{{\phantom{\dagger}}}\rangle c_{-\boldsymbol{k}+\boldsymbol{q},+}^{\dagger}c_{-\boldsymbol{k}+\boldsymbol{q},+}^{{\phantom{\dagger}}}+c_{\boldsymbol{k}+\boldsymbol{q},-}^{\dagger}c_{\boldsymbol{k}+\boldsymbol{q},-}^{{\phantom{\dagger}}}\langle c_{-\boldsymbol{k}+\boldsymbol{q},+}^{\dagger}c_{-\boldsymbol{k}+\boldsymbol{q},+}^{{\phantom{\dagger}}}\rangle]

(33)

H^{\text{eff}}_{\text{MF}}=\sum_{\boldsymbol{k},\nu}\left(\epsilon_{\boldsymbol{k},\nu}-\sum_{j,\boldsymbol{q}}f_{j}^{2}(\boldsymbol{k})\frac{N_{\boldsymbol{k}-2\boldsymbol{q}}}{m_{j}^{2}+\boldsymbol{q}^{2}}\right)c_{\boldsymbol{k},\nu}^{\dagger}c_{\boldsymbol{k},\nu}^{{\phantom{\dagger}}}.

(34)

We take
N_{\boldsymbol{k}}\sim n_{\text{F}}(\epsilon_{\boldsymbol{k}})
where

is the Fermi distribution function as a first order approximation (“one shot Hartree Fock") to self-consistent mean-field solution
N_{\boldsymbol{k}}
.
