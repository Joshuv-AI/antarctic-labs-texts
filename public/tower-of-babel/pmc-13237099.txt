FNDC1 is closely related to poor prognosis and immune cell infiltration in gastric cancer
Authors: Meixia Zhang, Qingzi Fu, Bo Jiang, Zhenzhen Yang, Qiaofeng Chen, Peng Chen, Shiyu Zhang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Fibronectin type III domain containing 1 (FNDC1) in gastric cancer (GC) progression and immune cell infiltration remains unclear. This study aimed to investigate the impact of FNDC1 on GC progression and to elucidate its association with immune cell infiltration in GC. FNDC1 expression profiles in GC and paired normal tissues were analyzed using datasets from the TCGA, GTEx, and GEO databases, with validation conducted through quantitative real-time PCR (qPCR) and immunohistochemistry (IHC). Differentially expressed genes (DEGs) associated with FNDC1 were identified through gene set enrichment analysis (GSEA) and functional enrichment analysis. We evaluated the role of FNDC1 in GC immune cell infiltration. In addition, Cox regression and Kaplan-Meier analyses were conducted on FNDC1 for its prognostic significance and correlation with clinical variables. We evaluated the role of FNDC1 in gastric cancer cell invasion and migration via Transwell and wound-healing assays following FNDC1 knockdown and overexpression. Finally, survival probabilities in GC patients were predicted by nomogram construction. FNDC1 expression in GC tissues significantly increased relative to that in matched normal samples (P < 0.001), as confirmed by qPCR and IHC (both P < 0.05). There were 359 DEGs associated with ECM Receptor Interaction, Focal Adhesion, and the Degradation of the Extracellular Matrix signaling pathway. FNDC1 expression was positively related to macrophage and NK cells infiltration (macrophage, r = 0.564, P < 0.001; NK cells, r = 0.566, P < 0.001; as validated by IHC and further confirmed by in vitro co-culture assays). Additionally, FNDC1 expression was strongly related to T stage (P < 0.001), pathological stage (P < 0.001), histological grade (P < 0.01), histological type (P < 0.001), anatomic neoplasm subdivision (P < 0.001), and unfavorable overall survival (OS, P < 0.05). In vitro cell experiments indicate that FNDC1 expression is closely associated with the invasion and migration of gastric cancer cells. Our established nomogram effectively predicted the 1-, 3-, and 5-year OS probabilities in patients with GC (C-index [95% CI] = 0.714 [0.689–0.738]). FNDC1 is strongly associated with a dismal prognostic outcome and immune cell infiltration in patients with GC.

Introduction

Gastric cancer (GC), a highly aggressive malignancy of the gastrointestinal tract, ranks fifth in cancer-related mortality and is the fourth leading cause of cancer-associated deaths globally1. Although the early diagnosis rate of GC exceeds 50% in certain countries, such as South Korea and Japan2, it remains markedly low in most parts of the world, contributing to poor prognostic outcomes3. Therefore, the identification of biomarkers closely associated with early GC detection and prognosis is critically needed.

The extracellular matrix protein Fibronectin type III domain containing 1 (FNDC1) contributes to the biological mechanisms underlying blood vessel formation4,5. Accumulating evidence has demonstrated that elevated FNDC1 expression is significantly correlated with tumor progression and unfavorable clinical outcomes in colorectal cancer6 and lung adenocarcinoma7. Elevated FNDC1 expression has been observed across multiple cancer types, where it potentially facilitates lung adenocarcinoma (LUAD) metastasis through JAK2-STAT3 signaling pathway activation8. However, the role of FNDC1 in GC has not yet been elucidated.

The emergence of immunotherapy has markedly improved clinical outcomes for many patients with advanced stage malignancies9. Compared with conventional chemotherapy, anti-her2 therapy and anti-programmed cell death 1 (PD-1) antibodies have shown remarkable therapeutic effects in untreated GC patients10. New anti-HER2 therapeutics, such as disitamab vedotin (RC48) and T-DXd, have made great achievements in the treatment of GC11. Antitumor immunotherapy efficacy should be predicated by taking effector cell infiltration in the tumor microenvironment (TME) as a prerequisite12. Anti-ctla-4 (T lymphocyte-associated antigen 4) monoclonal antibodies exert their effects on tumors through restoring T-cell growth and extending T-cell stimulation13. T cells are among the tumor-infiltrating lymphocytes (TILs), while additional prevalent TILs include NK cells and macrophages. Consequently, comprehending both the TME and TILs in GC might establish a basis for enhancing the anti-immunotherapy efficacy for GC. Nevertheless, it remains unreported whether FNDC1 can impact TIL levels in GC.

The present work examined the effect of FNDC1 on GC via the Cancer Genome Atlas (TCGA), Gene Expression Omnibus (GEO) and Genotype-Tissue Expression (GTEx) databases through bioinformatics analyses, which cover differential gene (DEG) identification, functional enrichment, immune cell infiltration, clinical correlation, survival analyses and gene set enrichment analysis (GSEA). Additionally, overall survival (OS) in patients with GC was predicted through the construction of a nomogram. Finally, the aforementioned bioinformatics analyses were validated using clinical data and tissue samples from patients with histologically confirmed GC.

Materials and methods

Data acquisition and processing

RNA-seq data were collected from 414 TCGA-derived GC and 36 adjacent non-carcinoma tissues (https://portal.gdc.cancer.gov/, November 24, 2024). Additionally, RNA-seq data from 174 non-carcinoma gastric tissues were acquired from the GTEx project through the UCSC Xena platform (http://xena.ucsc.edu/, November 24, 2024). Duplicate samples or those with insufficient information were excluded from subsequent analyses. R software (version 4.2.1) was used to perform log2-transformation of the RNA-seq data. FNDC1 differential expression in pan-cancer and GC tissues

Statistical analyses were conducted using appropriate methods tailored to the distributional characteristics of the data, employing the stats and car packages in R. Data visualization was performed using the ggplot2 package. ROC curve analyses were carried out with the pROC package, and the resulting outputs were visualized using ggplot2.To further validate the differential expression of FNDC1 in GC compared with adjacent non-carcinoma tissues, two gene expression profile datasets from the GEO—GSE13911 and GSE54129—were analyzed using the GEO2R online tool (https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/geo/, accessed November 27, 2024). Subsequently, ROC curve analyses were performed to evaluate the prognostic significance of FNDC1 in GC.

Quantitative real-time PCR (q-PCR) of FNDC1 expression in GC and non-carcinoma samples

The Ethics Committee of the First Affiliated Hospital of Nanchang University (CDYFY-IACUC-202312QR022) approved our study. All human participants provided informed consent, and the study conformed to the Declaration of Helsinki. Between March and June 2024, GC and non-carcinoma biopsy samples were continuously harvested from 20 GC patients at the Endoscopy Center of the First Affiliated Hospital of Nanchang University (Nanchang, Jiangxi). These obtained tissues were soaked in RNA protective solution before immediate storage in a − 80 °C freezer. No patients received previous antitumor treatments, such as chemotherapy or radiotherapy. Moreover, patients who developed concomitant cancers were excluded from the analysis.

RNA extraction and q-PCR

First, TRIzol reagent (R0016, Beyotime) was used to extract total tissue RNA, which was subsequently used to prepare first-strand cDNA using the PrimeScript™ RT Reagent Kit with gDNA Eraser (RR047A, Takara). Thereafter, we adopted the 2–∆∆Ct method to compute FNDC1 gene expression, with GAPDH being used as an endogenous reference.

IHC

This study retrospectively enrolled 80 patients who underwent surgery for GC at the First Affiliated Hospital of Nanchang University between June 2022 and December 2022. All cases were pathologically confirmed as primary gastric adenocarcinoma, with no prior history of other malignancies or previous anticancer treatments. For the IHC analysis, paired GC and matched non-carcinoma samples (≥ 5 cm away from the tumor edge) were processed. Formalin-fixed, paraffin-embedded blocks were prepared to construct tissue microarrays. Section (4 μm) were subjected to xylene deparaffinization and gradient alcohol rehydration. Following antigen retrieval and blocking, a primary antibody against FNDC1 (PA5-56602; Thermo Fisher Scientific; 1:500) was added and incubated with the slides at 4 °C for 12 h. Subsequently, an HRP-conjugated secondary polymer (KIT-5009, MXB Biotechnologies, 1:10000) was applied and incubated for 40 min at 26 °C. Chromogenic detection was performed with diaminobenzidine (DAB) substrate (P0202, Beyotime).

Three randomly selected fields per sample were imaged using a light microscope (Nikon Eclipse Ci-L). Staining intensity was quantitatively analyzed with ImageJ software (version 1.53t). Graphical presentation and statistical analyses were conducted using GraphPad Prism version 8.0.

Identification of DEGs in the high- and low-FNDC1 expression groups

In line with FNDC1 expression, the top 50% of samples were assigned to the high-expression group, whereas the remaining samples were assigned to the low-expression group. The DESeq2 package in R was used to identify DEGs for which P.adj < 0.05 and the logFC absolute value was greater than 1.5. The results of the identified differential genes are presented in volcano maps.

Functional enrichment of DEGs

When the IDs of the DEGs were converted via the “Hs.eg.db” package of R software, the clusterProfiler package was utilized for subsequent functional enrichment with thresholds of a q value < 0.2 and p.adj < 0.05. GSEA visualization and statistical analysis were completed with the clusterProfiler package, utilizing the C2 collection in MSigDB. Gene set permutations were conducted 10,000 times, with the significance thresholds adjusted to P < 0.05 and a false discovery rate (FDR) < 0.25.

Immune cell infiltration

Immune cell infiltration within tumor tissues was assessed through single-sample GSEA (ssGSEA) with the GSVA package14, which utilizes published gene signatures for quantification15. Statistical analysis was carried out to investigate the relationship between FNDC1 expression and immune infiltration levels via Spearman’s correlation, and the Wilcoxon signed-rank test was used to analyze differential immune infiltration patterns in the FNDC1 expression groups.

For immunohistochemical evaluation, FNDC1 expression levels were quantified by calculating the average optical density (AOD) from tissue samples of 80 GC patients using ImageJ software. Patients were stratified into high and low FNDC1 expression cohorts based on the median AOD value. From each cohort, 10 cases were randomly selected for additional immunohistochemical staining to assess macrophage populations, following the protocol outlined in “IHC” section. NK cells and macrophages were respectively identified with anti-CD56 (MAB-0743) and anti-CD68 (MAB-0041) antibodies (both from MXB biotechnologies). Quantitative analysis of marker-positive areas was conducted via ImageJ, and GraphPad Prism 8.0 was used to visualize and analyze the data.

THP-1 cells (1 × 10⁶) were stimulated with 320 nM PMA (MedChemExpress, 16561-29-8) for 24 h to induce differentiation into macrophage-like cells. The differentiated cells were then co-cultured with gastric cancer cell line MKN7 in a Transwell system (0.4 μm pore size, Corning, 3413). Specifically, 5 × 10⁵ MKN7 cells were seeded into the upper chamber, while the PMA-treated THP-1-derived macrophages were placed in the lower chamber of a 6-well plate. Following 48 h of co-culture, macrophages were harvested for subsequent qRT-PCR analyses.

Clinical correlation analysis of FNDC1 in GC

The relationships between FNDC1 expression levels and clinicopathological features were assessed using logistic regression analysis and Wilcoxon signed-rank tests. To assess how the FNDC1 level and additional clinicopathological features affect survival outcomes in GC patients, univariate and multivariate Cox regression analyses were conducted. Multivariate Cox regression was further utilized to identify factors independently predicting GC prognosis.

Nomogram establishment and validation

We developed a nomogram integrating all independent prognostic factors to estimate 1-, 3-, and 5-year survival probabilities for GC patients. The prognostic variables included were based on previously published studies16. Nomogram construction was performed using R software, specifically employing the rms and survival packages. Model parameters were determined through bootstrap resampling, with 200 iterations conducted for each sample group to enhance stability. The predictive performance of the nomogram was assessed using the concordance index (C-index), with values approaching 1 indicating higher predictive accuracy.

Cell culture

Human gastric cancer cell lines MKN7 (ZM0715) were obtained from Zhongqiao Xinzhou Cell Research. Cells were cultured at 37 °C under 5% CO₂ in MEM medium (8120294, Gibco, USA) supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (A511-001, Lonsera, Uruguay) and 1% penicillin/streptomycin (P1400, Solarbio, China).

Lentiviral transduction

For FNDC1 knockdown, lentiviral vectors were constructed using the pLKO.1-Puro backbone, containing either FNDC1-targeting shRNAs or a non-targeting control. Lentiviral particles were generated by co-transfecting HEK-293T cells with the lentiviral vector plasmid, psPAX2 (BR036, Fenghui), and PMD2.G (BR037, Fenghui) using Lipofectamine™ 3000 (L3000150, Thermo Fisher). Viral supernatants were collected at 48 and 72 h, filtered (0.22 μm), and used to infect MKN7 cells. Stable cell lines were selected with 2 µg/mL puromycin.

Protein extraction and western blotting

Cells were lysed on ice using RIPA buffer (P0013K, Beyotime, China) containing PMSF (P0100, Solarbio, China). Lysates were centrifuged (12,000 × g, 15 min, 4 °C), and supernatants were mixed with loading buffer (P1015, Solarbio, China), denatured (100 °C, 10 min), and stored at − 20 °C.

Proteins were separated by SDS-PAGE, transferred to PVDF membranes (0.22 μm), and blocked with 5% non-fat milk. Membranes were incubated with primary antibody (57946, CST, UK; 1:1000) overnight at 4 °C, followed by incubation with fluorescent secondary antibody (5151P, CST, UK; 1:10000) for 1 h at room temperature. Signals were detected using a Li-COR Odyssey system.

Transwell invasion assay

Cell invasion was analyzed using Matrigel-coated (60 µL; #256234, BD Biosciences) Transwell inserts (8 μm; Corning). Gastric cancer cells (5 × 10⁴) in serum-free medium were seeded in the upper chamber, with complete medium containing 10% FBS in the lower chamber. After 48 h, non-invading cells were removed, and invaded cells were fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde, stained with 0.1% crystal violet, and counted microscopically.

Wound healing assay

Cells were seeded in 6-well plates and cultured to approximately 95% confluency. A straight wound was generated in the monolayer using a sterile pipette tip. Following two gentle washes with PBS to remove detached cells, fresh serum-free medium was added to suppress cell proliferation. Images of the wound area were captured at 0 and 24 h under a phase-contrast microscope. The relative migration distance was quantified by measuring the wound width at each time point using ImageJ software.

Results

FNDC1 expression significantly increases inside pan-cancer and GC

FNDC1 is significantly upregulated in multiple malignancies, such as GC (Fig. 1A, P < 0.001). TCGA- and GTEx-derived data, comprising 414 GC tissues and 210 normal or adjacent non-tumor tissues, revealed that FNDC1 expression markedly increased in GC samples compared with that in their non-carcinoma counterparts (Fig. 1B, P < 0.001). Furthermore, in 27 paired GC and adjacent tissue samples from the TCGA, FNDC1 expression apparently increased inside tumors compared with that in their matched non-tumor counterparts (Fig. 1C, P < 0.001). In addition, ROC curve analysis (Fig. 1D) demonstrated that FNDC1 expression has a high predictive value for GC diagnosis (AUC = 0.945, 95% CI 0.928–0.962).

FNDC1 expression is tightly linked to immune cell infiltration

FNDC1 expression was positively correlated with NK cells and macrophages infiltration (Fig. 5A–C, NK cells, R = 0.566, P < 0.001; macrophages, R = 0.564, P < 0.001). Compared to low-FNDC1 samples, high-FNDC1 samples exhibited significantly elevated enrichment scores for NK cells (Fig. 5E, P < 0.001), while macrophages enrichment indices showed a non-significant increasing trend (Fig. 5D, P > 0.05). To further investigate these associations, we performed IHC staining of NK cells (CD56) and macrophages (CD68). Compared to adjacent non-tumor tissues, GC specimens demonstrated significantly increased proportions of both CD56-positive areas (Fig. 6A, C, and E, P < 0.0001) and CD68-positive regions (Fig. 6B, D and F, P < 0.0001) as quantified by digital image analysis. We employed a co-culture model to investigate the impact of FNDC1 expression in GC cells on tumor-associated macrophages (TAMs). qPCR analysis demonstrated that macrophage markers were significantly upregulated in the FNDC1 overexpression group, whereas they were markedly downregulated in the FNDC1 knockout group ((Fig. 6G, P < 0.001).

FNDC1 promotes the migration and invasion of gastric cancer cells

To explore the functional role of FNDC1 in gastric cancer progression, we generated FNDC1-knockout (KO) and FNDC1-overexpression (OE) models in MKN7 gastric cancer cell lines. Western blot analysis validated the successful manipulation of FNDC1 expression, showing a significant reduction in FNDC1 protein levels in KO cells and a substantial increase in OE cells relative to control cells (Fig. 7A,B).

Functional assays demonstrated that FNDC1 knockdown markedly impaired the migratory and invasive capacities of both cell lines. In contrast, FNDC1 overexpression robustly enhanced these aggressive phenotypes (Fig. 7C–E).

Nomogram establishment and validation

According to multivariate Cox regression, risk factors independently affecting GC OS (histological grade, age, FNDC1 expression level, and primary therapy outcome) were incorporated to construct a nomogram (Fig. 9D) for predicting 1-, 3-, and 5-year survival rates in patients with GC. Calibration plots (Fig. 9E) demonstrated that our nomogram performed satisfactorily in predicting the 1-, 3-, and 5-year survival probabilities (C-index [95% CI] = 0.714 [0.689–0.738]).

Discussion

According to bioinformatics analysis, FNDC1 expression markedly increased in GC samples. These findings were further validated by qPCR and IHC analyses. Moreover, as revealed by the analysis of ROC curves, FNDC1 is a potential diagnostic biomarker related to GC.

A total of 174 downregulated and 185 upregulated DEGs associated with FNDC1 were identified. GO annotation revealed that these DEGs were primarily enriched in terms related to collagen-containing extracellular matrix, extracellular matrix structural constituent and extracellular matrix structural constituent conferring compression resistance. The KEGG enrichment analysis and GSEA results demonstrated that FNDC1-associated DEGs were predominantly enriched in the following pathways: ECM-receptor interaction, focal adhesion, degradation of the extracellular matrix, extracellular matrix organization, NABA core matrisome, and the αvβ3 integrin pathway. The tumor cells and tumor microenvironment (TME), which constitute the major components of solid tumors, comprise diverse cellular elements, including immune cells and extracellular matrix17. Extensive studies have demonstrated that ECM remodeling in tumor microenvironment is critically involved in gastric cancer initiation and progression18,19. Compelling evidence has established MMP-220, MMP-721, and COL12A121 as pivotal ECM regulators in gastric cancer pathogenesis, with demonstrated roles in driving tumor invasion and predicting adverse survival outcomes. Transmembrane integrins play critical roles in promoting tumorigenesis and drug resistance22,23. Matrix stiffening not only facilitates cellular differentiation, autophagy, and invasive behavior24,25, but also accelerates hepatocellular carcinoma cell migration by enhancing aerobic glycolysis26. Furthermore, immune infiltration analysis demonstrated a significant association between FNDC1 expression levels and tumor immune cell infiltration, particularly involving macrophages and NK cells. The TME comprises various components, including stromal cells and immune cells, which interact dynamically to influence cancer progression and metastasis27,28. The complex crosstalk between stromal and immune elements plays a pivotal role in shaping the TME and driving the development of GC28. Moreover, interactions between the extracellular matrix and its receptors significantly impact the structure and function of the TME29, influencing carcinogenesis30, and tumor development30. M2-like macrophages promote GC metastasis by secreting multiple potent proangiogenic cytokines31. Consequently, FNDC1 may have a significant effect on modulating the TME and extracellular matrix in GC, warranting further investigation.

Previous bioinformatic analyses have demonstrated that upregulated FNDC1 expression is significantly associated with poor prognosis in GC32. Overexpression of FNDC1 may promote metastasis and invasion in lung adenocarcinoma through activation of the JAK2-STAT3 signaling pathway8. Furthermore, emerging evidence suggests that FNDC1 could enhance proliferation, invasion, and migration of breast cancer cells by modulating the Wnt/β-catenin pathway32. The study revealed significant associations between FNDC1 expression and GC features such as pathological T stage, pathological stage, histological grade, race, histological type, and anatomic neoplasm subdivision. High FNDC1 expression was strongly correlated with poor OS. Overexpression of FNDC1 promoted the invasive and migratory capabilities of gastric cancer cells, whereas knockdown of FNDC1 inhibited these malignant behaviors in vitro. These findings collectively suggest that FNDC1 may serve as a pivotal regulator in gastric tumorigenesis, progression, and differentiation. Studies have demonstrated that inhibition of FNDC1 suppresses gastric cancer progression by interfering with the formation of the Gβγ‑VEGFR2 complex33. However, the precise underlying mechanisms involved, particularly how FNDC1 influences the immune microenvironment of gastric cancer, still require further investigation.

Multivariate Cox regression analysis revealed that histological grade, age, FNDC1 expression, and primary therapy outcome were prognostic factors that independently predict the OS of GC patients. From these factors, a prognostic nomogram was established to predict survival probability in patients with GC. The calibration curves demonstrated good predictive accuracy of the established nomogram, suggesting its potential clinical utility for guiding individualized treatment strategies in GC management.

Conclusions

The main findings of this study can be summarized as follows:

First, FNDC1 expression is significantly elevated in GC tissues compared to non-carcinoma samples, suggesting its potential as a diagnostic biomarker for GC. Second, FNDC1 may contribute to tumor progression and prognosis by remodeling the TME and extracellular matrix, highlighting its promise as a therapeutic target. Third, histological grade, age, FNDC1 expression level, and primary therapy outcome appear to function as independent risk factors influencing overall survival in GC patients. Finally, the nomogram developed in this study demonstrated predictive accuracy for estimating patient survival probability. Nonetheless, despite the integration of data from multiple online databases and clinical specimens, the findings require further validation in larger, independent cohorts to confirm their reliability.
