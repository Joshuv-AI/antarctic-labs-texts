Is Cardiovascular Mortality Among People with Epilepsy increasing? A retrospective US population study
Authors: Ashley Curtis, Daniela Markovic, Andrew Liao, Somnath Ganapa, Mark Gaertner, Christopher M DeGiorgio
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Abstract AbstractIntroductionPeople with epilepsy are at higher risk for hypertension, diabetes, hyperlipidaemia and obesity than the US general population. It is unknown whether these risk factors translate to increased cardiovascular mortality compared with the US general population.ObjectiveTo examine changes in the proportions of deaths due to cardiovascular causes among people with epilepsy in the USA.HypothesisCardiovascular mortality among people with epilepsy is rising in the USA compared with the general population over the period from 2000 to 2019.Design/methodsRetrospective, longitudinal US population study of all deaths among people with epilepsy due to cardiovascular causes compared with the US general population for the years 2000 through 2019. Introduction

People with epilepsy are at elevated risk for cardiovascular disease and stroke compared with the general population.1,6 Based on data from the National Health and Nutrition Examination Survey (NHANES), hypertension affects 53% of adults with epilepsy (vs 39% of adults without epilepsy), while diabetes affects 25% of adults with epilepsy (vs 12% of adults without epilepsy). Contributing factors to the elevated cardiovascular risk among people with epilepsy include higher rates of poverty, poor diet, sedentary lifestyle and developmental disability.1,4 Antiseizure medications also contribute to elevated cardiovascular risk.7 8 It is unknown if higher cardiovascular risk among people with epilepsy translates to higher cardiovascular mortality.

We examined the Centers for Disease Control and Prevention (CDC) Multiple Cause of Death WONDER Database (CDC WONDER) to determine trends in cardiovascular mortality in the general population and among people with epilepsy, using the ICD-10 code G40.0–G40.91. We defined cardiovascular mortality as deaths in which the underlying cause was ischaemic heart disease, cerebrovascular disease, diabetes or hypertension. The purpose of this study is to test the null hypothesis: changes in the proportions of cardiovascular deaths among people with epilepsy for the years 2000–2019 are no different from those in the US general population. We also explored whether trends in mortality between populations varied by race and gender.

Materials and methods

This US population-based retrospective study examined all epilepsy-related deaths and all deaths in the US general population from 2000 to 2019. All causes of death were reported using the International Statistical Classification of Diseases and Related Health Problems, 10th Revision (ICD-10). Underlying causes of death are defined as ‘the disease or injury which initiated the train of events leading directly to death, or the circumstances of the accident or violence which produced the fatal injury’ on CDC WONDER. Multiple causes of death are defined as causes that contributed to death but were not determined to be the initiating factor in death. Epilepsy-related deaths were defined as all deaths among people with epilepsy (defined as ICD-10 codes G40.0–G40.91), which includes both underlying and multiple causes of death. Cardiovascular death was defined as deaths with the underlying cause attributed to one of the four diseases: ischaemic heart disease (ICD-10 I20–I25), cerebrovascular diseases (ICD-10 I60–I69), diabetes mellitus (ICD-10 E10–E14) and hypertensive disease (ICD-10 I10–I15). Source data were obtained from the CDC WONDER using a Python program designed to access the CDC WONDER API for the Data Query Web Service and return counts of mortality for the user-specified CDC WONDER parameters. The source data are open access and can be accessed at https://wonder.cdc.gov.9 10 Data were stratified by predefined age categories (35–64 years, 65–74 years and ≥75 years) as provided as an output on the CDC WONDER and divided into 5-year intervals between 2000 and 2019. The data were then evaluated by race (black and non-black) and gender (male and female) for stroke and ischaemic heart disease.11 For diabetes and hypertension, numbers were insufficient to make reliable race and gender comparisons in those with epilepsy, as the CDC considers categories with any number of deaths under 20 to be unreliable.

Statistical analysis

We examined temporal trends in the proportions of total deaths due to cardiovascular causes among individuals with epilepsy and the general US population from 2000 to 2019. Relative proportions of each cause were calculated for each population as a percentage of total deaths and stratified by age categories (35–64 years, 65–74 years and ≥75 years). We divided the period 2000–2019 into four 5-year intervals and evaluated temporal changes in log-odds of cause-specific death proportions using logistic regression. We report ORs and 95% CIs comparing the last versus the first interval for each population, with separate models for the four prespecified causes. We also computed age-standardised proportions and examined trends in standardised proportions using logistic regression with contrasts after age stratification to obtain age-standardised ORs. The logistic models included fixed effects of time interval, population type (epilepsy versus general) and their interaction to assess differential temporal effects on cause-specific mortality. P values for time and interaction effects were adjusted by the false discovery rate (16 comparisons: 4 age groups×4 diseases). An exploratory analysis included three-way interactions (time×population×gender or race) to evaluate subgroup differences. We employed segmented regression to identify breakpoints in ischaemic heart disease death trends and applied piecewise linear spline models with knots at estimated breakpoints to compare postbreak slopes, verifying model fit with the likelihood ratio tests. All analyses were performed using SAS V.9.4 (Copyright 2016 by SAS Institute, Cary, North Carolina, USA). Segmented regression models were performed using R V.4.1.3.

Patient and public involvement

Given the nature of this project, active patient and public involvement was not possible. The research was based on aggregated, de-identified mortality data that does not contain individual patient information or personal identifiers. Our research question focused on population-level analysis rather than individual experiences, which would require direct input from patients or the public.

Results

Deaths due to cerebrovascular disease

The crude proportions of cerebrovascular-related deaths among the epilepsy population increased 16.6% over the 20-year period, from 7.0% in 2000–2004 to 8.2% in 2015–2019 (online supplemental table). Table 1 shows the age-standardised ORs for the proportions of deaths among people with epilepsy versus the US general population for 2000–2014, compared with 2015–2019. The standardised OR of epilepsy versus the general population for this period was 1.55 (95% CI 1.34 to 1.78, SE 0.11, p<0.0001). The change in age-adjusted proportions of cerebrovascular deaths among people with epilepsy versus the US general population is graphically shown in figure 1. Age-adjusted proportions of cerebrovascular deaths among people with epilepsy increased from 5.52% in 2000–2004 to 6.98% in 2015–2019 (OR 1.28, 95% CI 1.12 to 1.48, p<0.001). For specific age groups, the increase in the proportions of cerebrovascular deaths among people with epilepsy was significant for the 35–64-year age group (percentage increase=76%, from 2.4% in 2000–2004 to 4.3% in 2015–2019; χ2 0.001, q<0.001) (figure 2). In contrast, the crude proportion of cerebrovascular deaths in the general population declined by 21.8% from 6.9% of all deaths in 2000–2004 to 5.4% in 2015–2019 (χ2 0.001). The decline in cerebrovascular deaths in the general population was consistent across all age groups (q<0.001). Between-groups comparisons were significant for epilepsy versus the general population for the 35–64-year age group (OR 2.03 between groups, 95% CI 1.60 to 2.58, p<0.0001, q=0.0002) (see figure 2).

Deaths due to diabetes

Diabetes-related deaths among people with epilepsy were rare in 2000–2004 (107 cases) but increased to 428 in 2019. The crude proportions of deaths due to diabetes among people with epilepsy increased 53.9% from 1.7% during 2000–2004 to 2.6% in 2015–2019 (χ2, p=0.001). Table 1 shows the age-standardised ORs for the proportions of deaths due to diabetes among people with epilepsy versus the US general population for 2000–2014, compared with 2015–2019. The standardised OR of epilepsy versus the generalised population for this period was 1.46 (95% CI 1.17 to 1.83, p<0.0008).

The change in age-adjusted proportions of cerebrovascular deaths among people with epilepsy versus the US general population over time is shown in figure 1. Age-adjusted proportions of diabetes deaths among people with epilepsy increased from 1.67% in 2000–2004 to 2.48% in 2015–2019, an increase of 32.6% (OR 1.50, 95% CI 1.20 to 1.87, p=0.001). For specific age groups, the increase in the proportion of diabetes deaths was significant in the 35–64-year age group (percentage change: +79% from 1.3% to 2.4%; p<0.001) (figure 2). This increase is in contrast with the general population, which experienced a significantly smaller increase of +9.6% in the proportion of diabetes-related deaths from 3.40% to 3.76% (figure 2). The between-group comparison for epilepsy compared with the general population was significant for the 35–64 age group (OR: 1.63, 95% CI 1.18 to 2.25, p=0.0028, q=0.0224). No significant between-group differences were noted for any other age groups (online supplemental table).

Deaths due to ischaemic heart disease

The crude proportion of total deaths among people with epilepsy due to ischaemic heart disease declined from 10.1% during 2000–2004 to 7.0% in 2015–2019, a 30.7% reduction over time (χ2 0.001). The change in age-adjusted proportions of cerebrovascular deaths among people with epilepsy versus the US general population over time is shown in figure 1.

Age-adjusted proportions of ischaemic heart disease deaths decreased significantly in both the epilepsy population and general population from 18.55% to 12.85% (OR 0.65, 95% CI 0.65 to 0.65, p<0.0001) in the general population and from 9.69% to 6.77% in the epilepsy population (OR 0.68, 95% CI 0.61 to 0.75, p<0.0001). When analysing by specific age groups, these decreases were significant for all age groups (q<0.001), except for the 35–64-year age group. In the general population, a within-groups decline of 35.6% occurred, with ischaemic heart disease-related deaths dropping from 21.1% of all deaths in 2000–2004 to 13.6% in 2015–2019 (p=0.001). In the epilepsy population, the proportion of deaths due to ischaemic heart disease for the 35–64-year age bracket declined at a slower rate than the general population (OR 1.22, 95% CI 1.04 to 1.44, p=0.0161) (figure 3). However, when corrected for multiple comparisons using the FDR adjustment, the q value trends were not significant (q=0.0859) (online supplemental table).

Trend changes in the proportions of ischaemic heart disease for epilepsy (top figure) versus the US general population.

We then analysed year-to-year changes in the proportions of deaths due to ischaemic heart disease in the epilepsy population versus the general population using a segmented regression model. The downward slope in the proportions of ischaemic heart disease for the epilepsy population from 2000 to 2011 was −0.265 per year (p<0.001). However, after 2011, the model detected a significant reversal in slope from −0.265 to −0.012, nullifying the earlier decline (breakpoint year=2011.57, SE 2.204, p=0.07). Between-group differences in the slopes for the epilepsy population versus the general population were significant after the breakpoint (slope difference p<0.001). The overall trend for the general population remained negative (−0.292) in contrast to patients with epilepsy, for whom the slope became effectively zero (figure 3).

Deaths due to hypertension

Between 2000–2004 and 2015–2019, the crude percentage of hypertensive disease-related deaths among people with epilepsy increased 66.7% from 2.01% to 3.35% for all deaths (χ2, p=0.001). Age-adjusted proportions of deaths due to hypertension increased significantly for the epilepsy population and the US general population. No significant between-group differences were identified overall for the age-standardised data (online supplemental table) or for any specific or any age group (figure 2).

Gender differences

We examined the trends in cardiovascular deaths over time for both the epilepsy and general populations, comparing trends by gender. Regarding cerebrovascular deaths for ages 35–64 years, our analysis indicated that while the general population experienced a decrease in the proportions of deaths from cerebrovascular diseases over the last two decades, individuals with epilepsy had experienced an increase. This trend was consistent for both men (between groups: OR 1.693, p=0.001) and women (between groups: OR 2.730, p<0.001). In terms of ischaemic heart disease mortality for ages 35–64 years, men with epilepsy did not experience the same decrease in the proportions of deaths as men in the general population (between groups: OR 1.387, p=0.001). For women, the change in the proportions of ischaemic heart disease deaths was not significantly different between the epilepsy population and the general population (between groups: OR 0.980, p=0.888). Although the trends of mortality were different for men and women, the extent to which these trends differed between the epilepsy and general populations was not statistically significant. Additionally, trends over time comparing the epilepsy and general populations did not vary significantly by gender for any other age groups.

Racial differences

We explored the trends in cardiovascular deaths over time for both the epilepsy and general populations, comparing the trends by race. For cerebrovascular mortality in individuals aged 35–64 years, the general population experienced a decrease in the proportions of deaths over the last two decades, while individuals with epilepsy experienced an increase. This trend was consistent across both non-black (between groups: OR 2.064, p<0.0001) and black individuals (between groups: OR 2.008, p=0.0016). Regarding ischaemic heart disease mortality for ages 35–64 years, non-black individuals with epilepsy did not show the same decrease in the proportions of deaths as non-black individuals in the general population (between groups: OR 1.2508, p=0.0163). For black individuals, the trends were similar but less pronounced and not significantly different when comparing the epilepsy population to the general population (between groups: OR 1.0955, p=0.6214). While the trends in mortality differed between non-black and black individuals, the extent to which these trends varied between the epilepsy and general populations was not statistically significant. Additionally, trends over time comparing the epilepsy and general populations did not significantly differ by race for any other age groups.

Discussion

We report the results of a US population study of cardiovascular causes of death among people with epilepsy over the 20-year period from 2000 to 2019. The primary findings of this study indicate age-standardised mortality for cerebrovascular disease and diabetes increased significantly among people with epilepsy compared with the US general population over the 20-year period (p<0.001) (figure 1). In addition, age-stratified comparisons indicate that the proportions of deaths due to cerebrovascular disease and diabetes increased significantly among people with epilepsy compared with the general population for ages 35–64 years (figure 2). A novel, unexpected finding is a trend reversal in the proportions of deaths from ischaemic heart disease among people with epilepsy. Prior to 2011, the proportion of deaths due to ischaemic heart disease among people with epilepsy declined steadily at a slope of −0.265 annually. After 2011, the slope changed from −0.265 to virtually zero (figure 3) in contrast to the continued decline in the US general population (people with epilepsy versus general population slope difference: p<0.001 after the breakpoint year).

Since 1990, cardiovascular deaths in the USA have followed a secular downward trend.12 Annual age-adjusted cardiovascular mortality declined 22% from 1990 to 2013.13 The reduction in cardiovascular deaths can be attributed to a combination of risk factor reduction as well as medical and surgical interventions.14 Preventive measures have been furthered by public health efforts promoting smoking cessation, increased physical activity and combating obesity.15 Prevention also includes reducing risk factors such as diabetes, hypertension and high cholesterol.15 Medical and surgical interventions, such as revascularisation, initial treatment of unstable angina and heart failure management, have also significantly contributed to the decline in cardiovascular mortality.14 15 Using a previously validated statistical model, Ford et al have estimated that primary prevention resulted in a 44% decline in cardiovascular mortality, and medical and surgical interventions have resulted in a decline of approximately 47%.15

Increasing cardiovascular risk factors among people with epilepsy

Despite significant advances in reducing cardiovascular risk in the general population, our findings raise two critical questions: first, why is mortality due to ischaemic heart disease among people with epilepsy no longer declining as compared with the general population? (figure 3). Second, why is cardiovascular mortality increasing in the 35–64-year age group, especially since this age group is the focus of primary prevention strategies?14,17 The answers to these questions may be found in recent studies of cardiovascular disease among people with epilepsy by Terman et al in a US study using the NHANES data set and Mayer et al in a large French cohort with epilepsy.2 3 In the US study, 154 individuals with epilepsy were compared with 17 961 without epilepsy. In the US NHANES epilepsy cohort, cardiovascular risk was significantly higher for those with epilepsy versus those without epilepsy.2 Importantly, the mean age was 48.6 years (SD 17.9 years), which is closely aligned with the 35–65-year age range found to be at risk in our study. The mean 10-year ASCVD score in the epilepsy cohort was 6.1% (SD 8.2%) versus 5.7% (SD 0.9%) in those without epilepsy.2 In the French epilepsy cohort, Mayer et al reported that cardiovascular deaths were significantly higher among people with epilepsy than in the general population (RR 2.12, 85% CI 2.04 to 2.20).3 The mean age was 61.4 years (SD 20.5), which is somewhat older but includes the median age and age range identified in the present study.

People with epilepsy have a higher risk for cardiovascular risk factors than the general population.1,6 Similar to Terman et al,2 Bensken et al also found elevated cardiovascular risk among people with epilepsy.4 They examined Medicaid claims data and found that the rate of hypertension was significantly higher in adults with epilepsy than in the general population.4 Diabetes affected 25% of people with epilepsy compared with 12% of those without epilepsy.4

Hyperlipidaemia is also a major comorbidity among people with epilepsy. In a population-based study, Harnod et al found a significant increase in the risk of hyperlipidaemia among people with epilepsy versus the general population.18 Harnod et al found that of 900 patients with epilepsy in Taiwan, the incidence of hyperlipidaemia was 34.14 per 1000 person-years versus 26.96 in the general population (incidence ratio of 1.28).18 Treatment with antiseizure medications contributes to the increased risk of hyperlipidaemia.8 19 Mintzer et al, using the MarketScan Commercial and Medicare databases, evaluated prescription data from 11 374 newly treated patients with epilepsy.19 Treatment with enzyme-inducing agents (phenytoin, carbamazepine and phenobarbital) was strongly associated with a higher risk of developing hyperlipidaemia (OR 1.22, 95% CI 1.066 to 1.408, p<0.001).19 Switching from enzyme-inducing Anti-Seizure Medications (ASMs) to newer ASMs that are not enzyme-inducing resulted in significant reductions in plasma lipids, C-reactive protein and homocysteine.8 Furthermore, Josephson et al found an increase in the incidence of cardiovascular disease in patients receiving enzyme-inducing ASMs.20 However, Lee-Lane et al reported that although enzyme-inducing ASMs were associated with higher rates of hyperlipidaemia, this did not translate to higher rates of major cardiovascular events.21 Non-enzyme-inducing agents, like Depakote, also contribute to weight gain and metabolic syndrome.22

Obesity is also a major problem among people with epilepsy. In a single-centre study of 554 newly diagnosed individuals with epilepsy aged 16–87 years, 55.2% were overweight, 37.7% were obese and 4.3% were morbidly obese.23 The authors found that the factors contributing to the development of obesity were refractory epilepsy and polytherapy.23 No association was found with epilepsy type and duration. Nazish conducted a meta-analysis in 2023 and found that the rates of obesity were higher among people with epilepsy across all age groups than those without epilepsy.24 Contributing factors include antiseizure medications, lack of exercise, valproic acid-related insulin resistance and other metabolic disturbances such as mitochondrial dysfunction and endocrine dysfunction.24 Ladino and Téllez-Zenteno suggest that the higher proportion of psychiatric disease found among people with epilepsy may lead to eating disorders contributing to obesity.25

Diabetes is also more prevalent among people with epilepsy than in the general population. Of the 154 epilepsy cases in the NHANES cohort, 25% with epilepsy had comorbid diabetes versus only 12% of the cohort without epilepsy (difference p<0.01).2 In a large in-hospital cohort of 92 438 people with diabetes, Li et al reported that people with epilepsy and comorbid diabetes suffered higher hospital mortality rates and worse outcomes.26 Hospital mortality over 30 days was significantly higher among people with diabetes and epilepsy (1.8% vs 1.1% (95% CI 0.98 to 2.60)) compared with people with diabetes without epilepsy.26 The rates of serious complications like sepsis and pneumonia were higher in those with diabetes and epilepsy than in people with diabetes and without epilepsy (OR 1.68 for pneumonia and 1.34 for sepsis).26

Terman et al investigated the hypothesis of whether people with epilepsy experienced higher rates of cardiovascular risk factors because of lower awareness and poorer control of risk factors and worse health behaviours.2 Interestingly, people with epilepsy had higher rates of awareness of their diagnoses of diabetes and hypertension and were highly compliant with their intake of statins.2 The authors speculated that the increased rates of cardiovascular risk factors were due to the higher prevalence of smoking and poorer diets among people with epilepsy.2 They concluded that the increased risk of cardiovascular disease among people with epilepsy could be attenuated by focusing more on lifestyle changes, such as smoking cessation and improving diet, rather than more pharmacologic interventions.2

Finally, there is a strong relationship between cardiovascular disease and the development of a later onset of epilepsy. Stroke is a well-known cause of epilepsy.27 Poststroke epilepsy occurs in about 10% of all individuals who sustain a stroke.27 Hypertension is now an increasingly recognised independent cause of epilepsy.28 29 Data from the Framingham study of adults aged >45 years found that the presence of midlife hypertension increases the risk of late-onset epilepsy by 93%.28 Interestingly, in a large German cohort of 168 612 subjects, aged >18 years, with hypertension, who were treated with an angiotensin receptor blocker, there was a 23% reduction in the development of new-onset seizures within 5 years.29

Overall, the literature provides strong evidence of increased cardiovascular risk among people with epilepsy. The findings of this study should serve to alert physicians to increase the level of surveillance and treatment of cardiovascular risk factors, especially in the critical age group from 35 to 64.

Strengths and limitations

Strengths

The primary strength of this study is the use of the CDC WONDER, which collects mortality data from all death certificates in the USA. Mortality data are validated at local and state levels to ensure accuracy. ICD-10 codes at the time of death are listed and can be sorted by age, gender, race and underlying causes, as well as contributing causes of death. This allows for an accurate search of mortality data by cause of death. The use of administrative datasets like the CDC WONDER is a validated method that effectively captures the US epilepsy mortality when using the ICD-10 codes G40.0–G40.9.30 Another advantage of the CDC WONDER is the large amount of data available, except for some cardiovascular causes in the years 2000–2001 for diabetes. Also, for the entire study period, the ICD-10 codes for epilepsy and cardiovascular disease remained the same, ensuring continuity of data coding for within-group and between-group comparisons.

Limitations

One possible limitation of this study is the definition of epilepsy used for this study. The ICD-10 codes G40–G40.91 were used as the primary search. However, other possible codes that include seizures are R56 and R56.8 (unspecified convulsions), which were not included in this study. Though adding ICD-10 codes R56 and R56.8 may allow for more numbers, these codes are often associated with seizures due to toxic, metabolic or other provoked causes rather than epilepsy. Mbizvo et al found that ICD-10 G40 (G40–G40.9) has a higher positive predictive value compared with ICD-10 code R56 (per Mbizvo et al, ICD-10 codes G40–G1 have a positive predictive value of 86% versus 69% for ICD-10 code R56.8).30 Another limitation relating to the use of ICD-10 codes is that they may not accurately represent the prevalence of common chronic diseases such as hypertension (I10). Mbizvo et al30 found that G40–G40.91 was an accurate estimate of epilepsy; however, the sensitivity of using I10 to estimate hypertension was found to range between 40.9% and 84.1%.29 Therefore, the use of ICD-10 codes to represent mortality may vary in accuracy depending on the diagnosis.

Some of the analyses, particularly when further divided by age, gender and race, were limited due to small sample sizes, especially for diabetes for the years 1999 and 2000 when the number of epilepsy deaths was lower. The CDC WONDER lists data as unreliable if there were fewer than 20 deaths in a single year under any category. This led to a lack of significance in some categories, especially diabetes in the years 1999 and 2000.

Finally, in addition to ischaemic heart disease, there are other cardiac causes of death, such as heart failure and cardiac arrhythmias, which can lead to death among people with epilepsy. We did not include these causes, which are coded under I30–I31 as ‘other forms of heart disease’. A large population study in Europe on cardiovascular mortality and epilepsy did capture heart failure and cardiac arrhythmias as significant causes of death among people with epilepsy.3 However, in the CDC dataset, the number of those with ‘other forms of heart disease’ (including cardiac arrhythmias and heart failure) was very rare, representing 2.3% of all epilepsy deaths in 2000 (37/1568 epilepsy deaths) and an identical 2.3% of all epilepsy deaths for the year 2019 (13/4903 epilepsy deaths). The low numbers of ‘other forms of heart disease’ in this dataset would have made accurate statistical analysis difficult.

Conclusions

This US population study of cardiovascular causes of death among people with epilepsy for the years 2000–2019 demonstrates that age-adjusted proportions of deaths due to cardiovascular causes, specifically cerebrovascular disease and diabetes, increased among people with epilepsy compared with the US general population. This increase in cardiovascular causes of death occurred primarily in the critical 35–64-year age range, where risk factor modification would have the greatest long-term benefits. Highly concerning is the trend reversal in the longstanding decline in the proportions of deaths due to ischaemic heart disease among people with epilepsy.

Increased surveillance and attention to cardiovascular risk factors among people with epilepsy are indicated, especially in the critical 35–64-year age group, where primary prevention may improve cardiovascular risk and reduce cardiovascular death.
