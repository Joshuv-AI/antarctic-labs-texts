Real-World Experience of Erenumab in Patients with Migraine in Germany: The SPECTRE Study
Authors: Charly Gaul, Astrid Gendolla, Dagny Holle, Hartmut Göbel, Mirja Koch, Caroline Baufeld, Cordula Weiss
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractIntroductionTo provide real-world insights into migraine patient population in Germany treated with erenumab, focusing on the prescription patterns and reasons for the initial dosage choice.MethodsSPECTRE was an observational, non-interventional, multicenter, open-label, single-arm study in patients treated with erenumab according to approved local dose and guidelines. The study enrolled adult patients (n = 571; Germany: 105 sites) with migraine who had received erenumab for not more than 3 months before the start of the study.ResultsThe mean (standard deviation) patient age was 45.0 (12.3) years, and most patients were female (89.0%), Caucasian (97.6%), and non-smokers (85.1%). The starting dose of erenumab was 70 mg in 68.5% of patients and 140 mg in 31.5%. The proportion of patients with 140 mg as the starting dose was the highest (43.5%) in those aged 30–40 years. The most common reason for starting a higher dose of erenumab 140 mg was severity of migraine (47.4%). During the observational period, the proportion of patients taking erenumab 140 mg increased to 64.6% (visit 5; V5) after 12 months. Due to attrition of patients towards the end of the study (V9: 90 participants), data at V9 must be interpreted with caution. At least one dose change was performed in 45.3% of patients (i.e., erenumab 70 to 140 mg or 140 to 70 mg), 21.2% of patients attempted at least once to discontinue treatment (i.e., period with erenumab discontinuation and no other antibody treatment for migraine prevention), and 15.3% discontinued erenumab treatment, mainly because of no or insufficient treatment response (13.5%). The mean time until the first omission attempt was 332.3 (range 37–633) days. Constipation (12.1%) was the most frequently reported adverse event, in line with the summary of product characteristics (SmPC) of erenumab.ConclusionMost patients with migraine were prescribed erenumab 70 mg as the starting dose. No new safety signals were observed for erenumab versus the previous trials.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1007/s40122-024-00658-7.Keywords: Erenumab, Migraine, Monoclonal antibodies, Preventive medication

Key Summary Points

Why carry out this study? The SPECTRE study was initiated shortly after the introduction of erenumab into the market. At that time, only data from controlled clinical studies were available.There was a lack of real-world experience with erenumab, and no data on the efficacy of the two different doses in the standard clinical practice were available.This study will help physicians gain confidence in selecting the appropriate starting dose and in the efficacy of erenumab in real-world practice, particularly for patients with comorbidities. What was learned from the study? The SPECTRE study showed the real-world treatment patterns and characteristics of patients with migraine in Germany who were treated with erenumab.Data from this observational real-world study reflect the data in the summary of product characteristics.The presence of comorbidities did not influence the effect of erenumab in patients with migraine. Erenumab is therefore promising for the treatment of patients with migraine who present psychiatric, cardiac, autoimmune, and/or gastrointestinal disorders.

Introduction

Migraine is the second leading cause of years lived with disability worldwide [1, 2]. According to the Global Burden of Disease study [3], the estimated global prevalence of migraine increased to 1.1 billion in 2019 from 721.9 million in 1990. In Germany, the prevalence of migraine was 16.9 million in 2019 [3], and women were 4.7 times more likely to experience migraine than men [4]. Migraine has an impact on the quality of life of people who are affected as well as their families [5–7], and is associated with a high socioeconomic burden in the adult population [5, 8].

Based on the headache frequency, migraine is classified as episodic migraine (EM: < 15 headache days per month, which on some days are migraine) and chronic migraine (CM: ≥ 15 headache days per month for 3 months, including ≥ 8 migraine days per month) [9]. The burden of migraine increases substantially with headache frequency. For instance, patients with CM have lower health-related quality of life and more severe disability than those with EM [10].

The traditional oral preventive treatments were not specifically designed for migraine and have often shown limited efficacy and/or tolerability, thereby negatively influencing patients’ adherence to the prescribed medication and resulting in suboptimal outcomes [11–14]. Antibodies targeting the calcitonin gene-related peptide (CGRP) pathway are a promising therapeutic option that might alter the current treatment paradigm of migraine prevention, leading to improved treatment outcomes for patients. Erenumab is the first and only fully human monoclonal antibody directed against the canonical CGRP receptor [15]. Erenumab received approval from the European Commission in July 2018 for the prevention of migraine in adults who have at least four migraine days a month [16]. According to the summary of product characteristics (SmPC), the recommended dose of erenumab is 70 mg every 4 weeks; however, some patients may benefit from a dose of 140 mg every 4 weeks [16]. The efficacy and safety of erenumab (70 mg and 140 mg) were demonstrated in the STRIVE study in patients with EM [17] and a pivotal phase 2 CM study [18]. Furthermore, findings from a phase 3b study in patients with EM (who had failed 2–4 previous migraine preventive treatments) demonstrated clinical benefits for the higher dose of erenumab (140 mg) [19].

As erenumab was the first drug of this new class on the market, it was crucial to gather information about local routine clinical practice. Over time, available data support a therapeutic gain with an increase in erenumab dose from 70 to 140 mg, and a rationale for initiating 140 mg in specific patients [20]. In the absence of biomarkers or predictors of treatment response, it is unclear which patient characteristics might influence the physician’s choice regarding dose selection.

This non-interventional study was performed to collect real-world data on the migraine patient population in Germany treated with erenumab. The aim was to understand and identify real-world prescription patterns of migraine prevention with erenumab by characterizing patients based on their starting dose and analyzing possible treatment changes during the observational period. Identified prescription patterns will shed more light on physicians’ treatment algorithms in standard clinical practice. The study assessed the influence of comorbidities and other medical conditions, as well as migraine characteristics, on the treatment decision.

Methods

Study Design

SPECTRE was an observational, non-interventional, multicenter, open-label, single-arm study conducted across 105 sites in Germany. This study captured real-world data on erenumab treatment in patients with migraine in accordance with the approved local dose and guidelines. The study was conducted between June 2019 and January 2021. The study consisted of an optional baseline evaluation period (particularly in patients who had not initiated erenumab treatment at screening) and a 2-year observational period. Due to the decision to discontinue this study 1 year earlier than planned, the minimum observational period per patient was reduced to 12 months (according to the study protocol amendment) (Fig. 1).

Study design

The baseline evaluation period was used to assess the eligibility of patients for enrollment in this study and collect their baseline characteristics. Patients who were on erenumab treatment (for not more than 3 months before screening) started the observational period right away if the baseline characteristics were sufficiently documented during screening (visit 1; V1). Patients treated with erenumab were observed at least once per quarter to collect available data in a real-world setting. Because of the non-interventional design, no study-specific visit schedule was imposed on patients, to avoid interference with their routine clinical care. Overall, one baseline evaluation and up to eight visits were planned to be documented within the observational period (unscheduled visits were documented in the next planned follow-up visit). Supplementary Table 1 gives an overview of the study schedule.

The study was approved by the ethics committee of the Medical Association of Hessen, Germany, and by participating in the study, all sites accepted this approval. Written informed consent was obtained from all participants.

Patients

Adults aged 18–99 years who were diagnosed with migraine, those either starting erenumab or already on preventive treatment with erenumab (for not more than 3 months), and participants who agreed to keep a migraine diary (electronic per mobile application or paper-based) as well as to complete patient-reported outcomes (PROs) questionnaires on-site throughout the study duration were included. Other inclusion criteria were migraine frequency of ≥ 4 monthly migraine days (MMD; based on International Classification of Headache Disorders (ICHD-3) [9]) on average across the last 3 months before the first injection of erenumab based on retrospective reporting, and sufficient baseline documentation (monthly headache days [MHD], MMD, pain intensity, monthly days without pain, monthly days with acute medication, current and past preventive treatments, comorbidities). Patients were excluded if they were simultaneously participating in another Novartis study or any study that included treatment with a study medication or procedure with an indication for migraine.

Patients were allowed to withdraw from the study prematurely due to withdrawal of informed consent or if they were not available to present at the study site for at least 6 months during the observational period. Patients who prematurely withdrew from the study were not replaced.

Study Endpoints

The primary endpoint was the proportion of patients starting with erenumab 70 mg or 140 mg, both in the overall patient population and stratified by main reason for prescription and by category of prior treatment failure. The variables of the primary endpoint were as follows: (1) proportion of patients initiating treatment with erenumab 70 mg or 140 mg in the subgroups: (i) patients with ≥ 4 treatment failures, (ii) patients with specific psychiatric or cardiac or autoimmune or gastrointestinal (GI) comorbidities; (2) assessment of preferred erenumab starting dose in (i) different age groups (< 30, 30–40, 50–60, > 60 years), (ii) patients with 4–7, 8–14, or ≥ 15 headache days per month; (3) relation to years since diagnosis; and (4) relation to baseline Headache Impact Test-6 (HIT-6™).

The other endpoints were to assess the (1) time point of change in treatment (change of dose or treatment discontinuation), (2) proportion of patients undergoing an omission attempt, (3) reduction in MHD and MMD, (4) proportion of responders characterized based on comorbidities, (5) quality of life evaluated by HIT-6, (6) treatment satisfaction (Treatment Satisfaction Questionnaire for Medication [TSQM]), (7) impact of erenumab treatment on patient comorbidities, (8) course of medication overuse in those who fulfilled the criteria at baseline, and (9) work productivity of patients. The study also assessed the incidence of adverse events (AEs).

Statistical Analysis

The full analysis set (FAS) included all patients who met the selection criteria and with documentation of the starting dose of erenumab. The safety analysis set (SAF) included all patients who met the selection criteria and had at least one documented erenumab dose during the study.

It was planned to enroll patients from approximately 150 sites in Germany. This study aimed to include 1960 patients diagnosed with migraine and treated with erenumab as per local label and clinical practice. However, fewer patients were able to be enrolled than anticipated. It was estimated that a sample size of 572 patients in this observational study would be sufficient to estimate the proportion of patients treated with either dose with precision (i.e., radius of the 95% confidence interval) of at least 4.1%, and the sample size also led to reasonable precision (7%) for the estimates within subgroups consisting of 10%. Another specific factor that was assumed to be prognostic for a dose choice and was evaluated in this trial was the presence of specific comorbidities. Within these relevant subgroups of comorbidities comprising 5% to 15% of the total study population of previous studies STRIVE [17] and LIBERTY [19], precision of 18.2% and 10.6%, respectively, was given.

Quantitative and qualitative data were analyzed descriptively. Analyses of conditional endpoints were based on the number of subjects fulfilling the respective condition. For variables with multiple answers, each possible answer was described by the number and proportion of patients with the respective answer as well as an “any” category (at least one answer given). The percentages were based on the total number of patients in the analysis set. Non-coded open-text entries were listed.

Statistical analysis was conducted using SAS® software (version 9.4. or higher; SAS Institute, Inc., Cary, NC, USA).

Results

Patient Disposition

A total of 571 patients were enrolled at 105 sites; 15 patients did not meet at least one selection criterion and/or did not document a starting dose for erenumab and were excluded. Therefore, 556 patients were included in the FAS and SAF. Of the 556 patients, 99 (17.9%) completed the study over the course of 2 years. The majority of patients (64.4%; n = 293) did not complete the study because the study was terminated early by the sponsor.

Demographic and Baseline Characteristics

Demographics and baseline characteristics are summarized in Table 1. The mean (standard deviation; SD) age of the patients was 45.0 (12.3) years. The majority of the patients were female (89.0%; n = 495), Caucasians (97.6%; n = 521), and non-smokers (85.1%; n = 458). Migraine developed at a mean age of 25.3 (14.1) years before baseline; and time since migraine diagnosis was 17.0 (13.2) years before baseline. There were 63.3% (n = 352) of patients who had CM according to ICHD-3 criteria [9]. Almost all patients (99.3%; n = 552) were treated with at least one previous preventive therapy at baseline. The most common previous treatments were topiramate (78.2%), beta-blocker (75.5%; metoprolol [50.7%], propranolol [13.8%], bisoprolol [11.0%]) and amitriptyline (69.2%). A total of 97.5% of patients took acute medication in the 3 months preceding baseline. The most common acute medications were triptans (100%) and non-steroidal anti-inflammatory drugs (NSAIDs) (45.3%). Among triptans, the most common were sumatriptan (37.8%) and rizatriptan (27.5%). The most common NSAID used was ibuprofen (28.8%). Patients reported on average 3.7 (1.5) treatment failures (i.e., lack of efficacy of a prior preventive treatment). The disease burden of migraine was high in the study participants at baseline: patients experienced on average 14.6 (6.7) MHD and 10.8 (5.4) MMD, and on average patients took acute medication on 10.3 (5.5) days per month.

Primary Outcomes

Starting Dose of Erenumab in the Overall Population

The starting dose of erenumab was 70 mg in 68.5% (n = 381) of patients and 140 mg in 31.5% (n = 175) of patients (Fig. 2).

Starting dose of erenumab in overall population (FAS)

Starting Dose of Erenumab in the Subgroups of Patients

The proportion of patients with a starting dose of 70 mg or 140 mg erenumab did not differ by < 4 or ≥ 4 treatment failures (Table 2). The proportion of patients with 140 mg as starting dose was highest (43.5%; n = 40) in the patients aged 30 to ≤ 40 years versus other age groups. Erenumab 140 mg was less frequently used as the starting dose in patients with 4 to ≤ 7 MHD (22.6%; n = 14) or with ≥ 15 MHD and < 8 MMD (26.3%; n = 10) versus other subgroups. The proportion of patients with 140 mg as starting dose decreased with increasing time since migraine diagnosis (< 1 year: 44.0% [n = 11], 1 to ≤ 5 years: 37.5% [n = 39], ≥ 5 years: 29.3% [n = 125]). The proportion of patients with erenumab 70 mg or 140 mg as starting dose stratified by HIT-6 showed no meaningful difference between the subgroups. More than 69.5% (n = 214) of patients were prescribed a starting dose of erenumab 70 mg regardless of psychiatric, cardiac, autoimmune, and/or GI disorders. There was no difference observed in choice of starting dose between patients with or without psychiatric, cardiac, autoimmune and/or GI disorders. In all the subgroups, erenumab 70 mg was the commonly prescribed starting dose (Table 2).

Time Until First Dose Change

The median time between treatments was 29.7 days (range 25.0–96.0), and the median number of injections administered during the study was 14.0 (range 1.0–27.0), corresponding to 12.0 (range 0.5–17.7) injections per year per patient. The most common reason for the first dose increase (39.2%; n = 94) or decrease (58.3%; n = 7) was “according to prescribing information.” For 252 (45.3%) patients, at least one dose change (i.e., 70 mg to 140 mg or 140 mg to 70 mg) was performed during the study. The erenumab dose was increased (i.e., from 70 to 140 mg) in 250 patients (45.0%) and decreased (i.e., from 140 to 70 mg) in 43 patients (7.7%) anytime during the study. For 240 patients, the first change was a dose increase and occurred on average after 5 months. The dose was increased in 250 (45.0%) cases (i.e., 70–140 mg; occurred on average after 5.0 months) and decreased in 43 (7.7%) cases (i.e., 140–70 mg; occurred on average after 6.2 months).

Treatment Interruption

During the study, 118 (21.2%) patients attempted at least once to discontinue treatment. On average, the median time until the first omission attempt was 336.0 (range 37–633) days. The mean duration of omission attempt (i.e., period with discontinuation of erenumab and no other antibody treatment) was 75.2 (range 3–447) days. A total of 92 (78.0%) patients restarted erenumab after their omission attempt. There were 90 patients (76.3%) who received erenumab 140 mg versus 29 patients (24.6%) who received erenumab 70 mg as the last dose before the omission attempt. A total of 83 patients (70.3%) were treated with the same dose before and after the omission attempt. In this study, 29 patients (24.6%) discontinued erenumab treatment after the omission attempt.

Drug Discontinuation

During the study, 85 (15.3%) patients discontinued treatment with erenumab after 7.1 months on average. Twenty-two patients who started treatment with another monoclonal antibody during the study received a mean of 8.2 (SD 3.9) injections of erenumab during 10.6 (SD 3.9) months before switching. The therapy was discontinued most frequently because of “no treatment response” (8.3%; n = 46) or “insufficient improvement of migraine” (5.2%; n = 29). The most common reason for omission attempt was “good effectiveness” (9.0%; n = 50) (Table 3).

Change in MHD and MMD

In more than half of the patients (59.2%), 4–5 injections were administered before the first visit with a reduction in MHD by ≥ 50%. The diary data were available for 507 patients (91.2%). Overall, the mean number of MHD and MMD decreased during the study with erenumab treatment. After 6 months, the mean number of MHD and MMD decreased by −7.0 (6.6) and −4.9 days (5.9), respectively (Fig. 3). The mean (SD) number of MHD and MMD decreased by −8.0 (7.2) and −5.7 (6.2) between the first and last visit, respectively.

MMD and MHD and at selected visits (FAS)

After 6 months (visit 3; V3), more than half of the patients (55.4%; n = 237) showed a reduction in MHD by ≥ 50%. This proportion increased after 1 year (visit 5; V5: 59.7%; n = 197) and 2 years (visit 9; V9: 70.0%; n = 42). In 22.9% (n = 98) of patients, MHD were reduced by ≥ 75% after 6 months (Fig. 3). After 1 and 2 years, 30.9% (n = 102) and 46.7% (n = 28) showed a reduction of MHD by ≥ 75%, respectively.

Change in Non-migraine Symptoms

At baseline, 37.0% (n = 202) and 35.8% (n = 196) of patients suffered from insomnia and fatigue, respectively. In addition, 28.6% (n = 156) of patients had pain in different parts of the body at baseline. During the study, the proportion of patients with non-migraine symptoms decreased for all symptoms (Table 4).

Treatment Response by Comorbidity (Change in MHD)

About three-fourth of the patients (75.9%; n = 422) had at least one comorbidity (Table 1). At month 6, more than 50% of patients with comorbidities (psychiatric disorder [54.3%; n = 88], depression [51.9%; n = 56], cardiac disorder [58.8%; n = 10], autoimmune disorder [51.8%; n = 57]) showed a decrease in MHD by ≥ 50%, except for patients with GI disorders (39.0%; n = 16) (Table 5). Patient-Reported Outcomes

Headache Impact Test-6: At baseline, the mean HIT-6 score was 64.5 (range 0–100, with 100 being worst), with lower values for patients previously treated with erenumab versus naïve patients (62.0 [6.9] versus 66.5 [4.7]). The mean HIT-6 scores improved by −6.8 (8.2) after 6 months (visit 3; V3) and by −9.3 (9.2) after 2 years (visit 9; V9) (Fig. 4). At baseline, headaches had a severe impact (HIT-6 score 60) on patients’ lives in 83.1% (previously treated: 71.3%, naïve: 92.5%) and no or little impact (HIT-6 score < 50) in 2.3% of patients. At visit 3 (V3) and visit 9 (V9), a severe impact was reported in 43.5% and 35.6% of patients and no or little impact in 18.7% and 27.1% of patients, respectively.

Mean headache impact test (HIT-6) scores at selected visits (FAS)

Treatment Satisfaction Questionnaire for Medication: At baseline, mean TSQM scores were 72.4 for “global satisfaction,” 64.9 for “effectiveness,” 74.3 for “side effects,” and 80.4 for “convenience” (Fig. 5). At month 6, the mean TSQM score was 83.5 for “global satisfaction,” 73.8 for “effectiveness,” 89.1 for “side effects,” and 88.4 for “convenience.”

Mean Treatment Satisfaction Questionnaire for Medication (TSQM) scores at selected visits (FAS)

At baseline, the mean number of days per month incapacitated for work or with sick leave due to migraine was 3.9 (6.8) days. The mean number of days of patients not at work/school (month 6: 1.1 [2.3] days, month 12: 0.9 [2.1] days, and month 24: 0.8 [2.0] days) and with reduced performance at work/school (month 6: 1.6 [2.6] days, month 12: 1.4 [2.4] days, and month 24: 1.3 [2.5] days) decreased from month 6 to month 24.

Overview of Adverse Events

In total, 776 AEs were reported in 52.9% (n = 294) patients. A total of 6.5% (n = 36) of patients experienced at least one serious adverse event (SAE), and in 6.3% (n = 35) of patients, the AE led to the withdrawal of erenumab (Table 6). One fatal SAE was reported in a 63-year-old female patient who suffered from cerebral infarction. This fatal SAE was assessed by the physician as unrelated to erenumab. In 2.5% (n = 14/556) of all patients, an adverse drug reaction (ADR) was observed after a dose increase. The most common AEs were (by System Organ Class [SOC] terms; > 15%) GI disorders (19.8%), nervous system disorders (16.5%), and general disorders and administration site conditions (13.1%) (Table 6). In this study, constipation (12.1%) was the most frequently reported AE, in line with the SmPC of Aimovig® (erenumab) [16]. No new safety signals were identified.

Of the 776 AEs, 8.8% (n = 68) were serious and 12.3% (n = 95) were severe. More than half of the patients recovered from AEs (59.4%; n = 447). There were 32.7% (n = 252) patients with AEs who showed a causal relationship with erenumab. Discussion

Data from the SPECTRE study showed the real-world treatment patterns and characteristics of patients with migraine in Germany who were treated with erenumab. The age of enrolled patients was in line with known epidemiological data for migraine, which show that migraine is the most common neurological disorder in young to middle-aged patients. In this study, most patients (89.0%) enrolled were female, which also reflects the higher prevalence of migraine in women. Notably, about 75.9% suffered from comorbidities, mainly depression (27.2%). The burden of migraine was high at baseline: on average, patients had 14.6 (6.7) MHD and 10.8 (5.4) MMD and took acute medication 10.3 (5.5) days per month. The high disease burden at baseline is also reflected in its impact on the patients’ work life: 62.6% of patients felt partly or strongly limited in their employability/productivity because of their migraine; the average incapacitation for work or sick leave amounted to 3.9 days per month. The patients’ treatment history was in many instances quite long and unsatisfactory: although almost all patients had received prior or current preventive therapy (99.3%), more than half of the patients (52.4%) had experienced at least four treatment failures. This is in line with the German reimbursement requirements for statutory health insurance at the time of the study and compliance by the prescribing physicians.

The SPECTRE study was initiated shortly after the introduction of erenumab to the market, and at that time, only data from controlled clinical studies were available. Consequently, there was a lack of real-world experience with erenumab, and no data were available on the efficacy of the two different doses in standard clinical practice. Additionally, patients with comorbidities and other medical conditions, who had been excluded from the controlled clinical studies, had not been previously studied. The objective was to gain a comprehensive understanding of erenumab usage and evaluate its efficacy in a real-world setting. Specifically, the study aimed to gain insights into the appropriate starting dose (70/140 mg) for individual patients. This objective does not necessitate a comparator; thus, a single-arm design without comparator was chosen in this study.

According to the SmPC [16], the recommended dose of erenumab is 70 mg every 4 weeks. However, some patients may benefit from a dose of 140 mg every 4 weeks. In line with the SmPC of erenumab [16], we found that most patients (68.5%) were prescribed 70 mg erenumab as the initial dose at the study start. It must be considered that at the time when the study recruitment started, only erenumab 70 mg dose was available on the market, and increasing the dose resulted in doubling the treatment cost. The higher starting dose of 140 mg was used most often in patients aged 30–40 years. The most frequently cited reason (47.4%; n = 83) for initiating the higher starting dosage was the severity of migraine as assessed by the physician. Further, the study results did not allow for the determination of other factors that may have governed the decision for the choice of the initial dose strength. It is noteworthy that the presence of comorbidities had not influenced the physicians’ decision of choosing one erenumab dose over the other (70 mg or 140 mg).

In this study, at least one dose change was performed (i.e., erenumab 70 mg to 140 mg or 140 mg to 70 mg) in nearly half of the patients (45.3%). Those dose changes mainly occurred to increase the dose from 70 to 140 mg (45.0% of patients) anytime during the study. The dose increase (n = 240) and decrease (n = 12) occurred on average after 5.0 months and 6.2 months, respectively. Effectiveness was the reason for omission attempt or discontinuation in most of the patients, i.e., physicians paused treatment to evaluate the disease-modifying effect of the treatment and restarted if needed after withdrawal. Upon initiation of erenumab, the disease burden decreased considerably, as evidenced by both clinical improvement (reduction in MMD and MHD) and improvement in PROs (reduction in HIT-6 scores). The disease burden in patients with comorbidities also decreased over the course of the study, as evidenced by ≥ 50% and ≥ 75% reduction in MHD. In addition, treatment with erenumab reduced the proportion of patients with non-migraine symptoms like dizziness, allodynia, pain, hormonal problems, premenstrual syndrome, and insomnia over 2 years.

The efficacy of erenumab was determined at months 4 to 6, and the improvement in MMD and MHD in SPECTRE occurred within the first 6 months of erenumab treatment. In this large clinical trial, MMD had decreased from 8.3 at baseline by 3.2/3.6 (70 mg/140 mg erenumab, respectively) at months 4 to 6. In the SPECTRE study, starting from a higher number of MMD (10.8), the improvement at month 6 was 4.9 MMD (SPECTRE study has patients with CM, hence the higher MMD). The results were therefore largely in line with those observed in the STRIVE study [17]. Furthermore, the HIT-6 scores decreased up to month 6 by more than 6 points, which qualifies as a clinically relevant improvement (minimally important clinical difference [MCID] of ≥ 6, according to Houts et al. [21]). Similar to the improvement in clinical symptoms, patients’ treatment satisfaction was also improved during the observational period. In this study, the safety data were in accordance with the known safety profile of erenumab. In this study, constipation (12.1%) was the most frequently reported AE, in line with the SmPC of erenumab [16].

This study has several limitations, the first of which is the observational nature of the study. The strength of this observational study, i.e., its reflection of real-world practice, is also the source of its inherent limitations. Despite their limitations due to the observational nature, the results from this study will help physicians gain confidence in selecting the appropriate starting dose and in the efficacy of erenumab in real-world practice, particularly for patients with comorbidities. The data quality of this observational study is naturally lower than that of a controlled clinical trial, as data may be incomplete and prone to several types of bias. Due to the non-interventional nature of the study, selection bias by the physicians, i.e., selective recruitment of participants based on convenience, and on the patients’ side, self-presentation and recall biases upon completing questionnaires must be considered when interpreting results. Second, an analysis of the non-migraine symptoms under a higher dose of erenumab was not conducted. The data obtained within this study were only assessed using descriptive statistics and were not used for comparative analysis. During the study, patients withdrew for various reasons, which reduced the sample size, particularly for later study visits. Due to early termination and slow recruitment rates, the anticipated number of participants and sites was not reached, thereby limiting the representational quality of the patient cohort.

Conclusion

Data from this observational real-world study from Germany are in line with the data in the SmPC. Patients with migraine were mostly prescribed a starting dose of 70 mg during the time of the study initiation, and there was no impact of comorbidities or patient characteristics (prior treatment failures, like MHD, HIT-6 scores, time since diagnosis) on the prescription pattern. Improvements in the clinical parameters (MMD and MHD), PROs, and non-migraine symptoms were observed within 6 months of erenumab initiation, in line with the global clinical trials. The presence of comorbidities did not influence the effect of erenumab in patients with migraine; hence, erenumab is promising in patients with migraine who present psychiatric, cardiac, autoimmune, and/or GI disorders.
