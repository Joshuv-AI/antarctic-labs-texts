Neuronal Regulation and Locomotion in Rats Following Spinal Cord Injury: Reconciling Behavioral Scales With Neuroelectrophysiology
Authors: Angelo H ALL
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractProgress in translating spinal cord injury (SCI) therapies from bench to bedside remains bottlenecked by a critical paradox in preclinical phenotyping: the frequent divergence between behavioral recovery metrics and objective neurophysiological outcomes. For decades, the Basso, Beattie, and Bresnahan (BBB) open-field locomotor scale has served as the standard subjective metric for assessing hindlimb function in rodent models. However, while behavioral scales often capture substantial endogenous recovery and central pattern generator (CPG) autonomy, they frequently fail to correlate with objective neuro-electrophysiological metrics, such as motor and somatosensory evoked potentials (MEPs/SSEPs), particularly during the acute and sub-acute phases of injury. This perspective evaluates the neuroanatomical and pathophysiological mechanisms underlying this discrepancy. We dissect how the spatial distribution of contusive forces, the demyelination of anatomically spared fibers, and transient spinal shock alter early electrophysiological signaling without permanently arresting long-term behavioral plasticity. Furthermore, we highlight key neuroanatomical differences—specifically the dorsal preservation of the corticospinal tract in rodents versus the lateral architecture in humans—that complicate cross-species translation. Ultimately, we argue that relying solely on locomotor scales overestimates functional tract integrity, and we advocate for a paradigm shift toward integrating synchronized behavioral and electrophysiological profiling to improve the predictive validity of preclinical SCI trials.Keywords: behavioral scales, neuro-electrophysiology, rats, spinal cord injury

Introduction

Several seminal investigations highlight a critical divergence between gross behavioral assessments—such as the Basso, Beattie, and Bresnahan (BBB) scoring system commonly used in rodents—and underlying neuro-electrophysiological outcomes in spinal cord injury (SCI) models. Specifically, historical literature underscores that considerable overground locomotor recovery can occur despite severe disruptions to descending spinal pathways, though the extent of this recovery exhibits distinct species-specific profiles. In pioneering rodent and feline models, Eidelberg et al demonstrated that near-intact overground locomotion could be maintained following substantial spinal cord hemisections. Conversely, evaluating these phenomena in non-human primates, Loy et al established that macaque monkeys (Macaca fascicularis) similarly retain or rapidly recover robust overground motor performance following lateral hemi-transections. These parallel historical findings underscore that cross-species behavioral adaptation and residual structural plasticity often mask profound neuro-electrophysiological deficits, confounding direct translational comparisons between rodent scales and primate motor outcomes. While behavioral scoring systems showed significant functional compensation, electrophysiological assessments of the cut ascending sensory and descending motor pathways confirmed a total localized block in signal transduction across the lesion. This contrast highlights the role of spinal circuit reorganization or localized plasticity that masks long-tract structural damage.1,2

Similarly, All et al. and García-Alías et al. explicitly compared the longitudinal progression of moderate mid-thoracic contusive SCI over an 8-week period using MEPs and BBB testing. The authors documented distinct, non-linear temporal patterns between motor behavior and electrophysiology. While BBB scores showed progressive and robust behavioral recovery over time, the relative MEP power spectral amplitudes remained significantly depressed. This underscores that gross overground movement metrics often drastically overestimate the recovery or conduction integrity of descending supraspinal tracts.3,4

Furthermore, Bareyre et al. and Courtine et al. performed extensive work mapping the corticospinal and rubrospinal tracts post-injury, consistently highlighting the formation of intraspinal bypass circuits. Their work shows that while a rodent can achieve an excellent BBB score with weight-supported plantar stepping and coordination, classical cortical MEPs remain completely absent or severely delayed upon electrical or magnetic stimulation. The electrophysiology registers a highly damaged main pathway, whereas the behavioral score reflects a functional success built entirely on compensatory routing.5,6

Historical Context and Operational Principles of the BBB Scale

In 1995 and 1996, Basso, Beattie, and Bresnahan published two seminal papers introducing a standardized motor behavioral rating scale to assess locomotor outcomes following rodent (rat and mouse) spinal cord injury (SCI).7,8 Widely recognized as the BBB scoring system, this multi-stage metric categorizes injury severity and characterizes endogenous sensorimotor recovery, focusing on rat hindlimb kinematics. Today, the BBB scale remains the standard metric for longitudinal evaluation of SCI progression and therapeutic efficacy in rat models.

The BBB system is a reproducible, sensitive, and cost-effective observational tool requiring no specialized instrumentation. It quantifies hindlimb locomotion on a scale from 0 (complete paralysis) to 21 (normal locomotion), where each score defines a distinct kinematic phenotype. The assessment spans three distinct recovery phases: (i) Early phase (Scores 0–7): isolated or joint-restricted movements, (ii) Intermediate phase (Scores 8–14): transition to consistent weight-bearing and forelimb-hindlimb coordination, and (iii) Late phase (Scores 15–21): consistent plantar stepping, precise paw placement, and trunk/tail stability.

Critical functional milestones are marked by the transitions from score 7 to 8 and 14 to 15. Methodologically, the protocol demands a four-minute observation of an individual rodent by two independent, blinded examiners positioned orthogonally around a standardized open field (a smooth plastic arena 90 cm in diameter and 30 cm in depth). Examiners meticulously evaluate bilateral hindlimb joint articulation and displacement, recording independent scores using a standardized matrix. Inter-rater discrepancies are resolved by adopting the lower score to prevent overestimation of recovery. Crucially, examiners must not touch or externally stimulate the animal, save for occasional acoustic cues (e.g., scratching the arena wall) to prompt movement. Assessments commence one-week post-injury and proceed at weekly intervals. Testing must be strictly standardized by time of day and laboratory environment to control for circadian variations in rodent activity and confounding hypersensitivities to ambient noise or olfactory cues. The testing arena undergoes rigorous disinfection between subjects to eliminate pathogenic cross-contamination.

Clinical and Experimental Neuroelectrophysiology

Complementing behavioral metrics, neuroelectrophysiology provides a sophisticated, high-resolution platform for the direct functional assessment of axonal tracts, though it requires specialized hardware, software, and technical expertise.3,9-17 Clinically, intraoperative neurophysiological monitoring is vital for safeguarding central nervous system (CNS) integrity against ischemic insults during complex neurosurgeries and serves as a diagnostic standard for neurological profiling.18-20 To evaluate ascending somatosensory pathways, peripheral nerves are electrically stimulated to propagate micro-volt signals through the neuraxis to the primary somatosensory cortex. Quantitative analysis of these somatosensory evoked potentials (SSEPs) relies on measuring signal amplitude, latency, frequency, and spectral power across various relay points, including proximal sensory nerves and spinal tracts. Conversely, motor evoked potentials (MEPs) assess the integrity of descending motor pathways by stimulating the motor cortex or cranial spinal segments and recording the resulting electromyographic (EMG) activity in target muscle groups.3,9-11,20

Discrepancies Between Behavioral and Electrophysiological Metrics

Despite the utility of the BBB scale, it remains a subjective semi-quantitative metric. Its output is inherently confounded by the animal’s systemic health, post-operative pain thresholds, and volitional motivation to explore. Furthermore, accurate scoring depends entirely on investigator vigilance in detecting micro-movements of lower limb joints.7,8 Consequently, behavioral profiling should complement, rather than replace, objective functional assessments of CNS sensory and motor pathways via SSEPs and MEPs.

Intriguingly, behavioral and neurophysiological trajectories often diverge, yielding poorly correlated trends during the acute and sub-acute phases of SCI.21,22 This discrepancy is primarily explained by the anatomical architecture of the rodent spinal cord and the specific pathophysiology of contusion injuries (Figures 1 and 2).

A contusive insult delivers a rapid mechanical force to a localized segment of the spinal cord parenchyma. This kinetic energy is primarily dissipated within the central Gray matter, destroying mostly large-diameter myelinated fibers and culminating in a central necrotic cavity. The surrounding White matter undergoes variable secondary degeneration, where the final lesion volume correlates directly with the initial impact force.3,12-14 However, sub-populations of anatomically intact “spared fibers” frequently persist within the subpial rim. 23 Following injury, the acute loss of endogenous, myelin-producing oligodendrocytes leaves these spared axons demyelinated, rendering them functionally silent and vulnerable.24-26 Furthermore, during the acute phase of SCI, the phenomenon of “spinal shock” temporarily suppresses even anatomically healthy pathways, arresting action potential propagation (depolarization/hyperpolarization) and rendering tracts electro-physiologically unreactive. 3

Neuroanatomical Divergence and Plasticity Mechanisms

Crucially, rodent hindlimb locomotion does not strictly depend on intact supraspinal inputs. Central pattern generators (CPGs) and local neural circuitry within the lumbar ventral horn, distal to the lesion site, can autonomously coordinate rhythmic motor outputs.27-31 This physiological autonomy highlights the critical need to utilize both subjective locomotion scores and objective neuro-electrophysiological metrics as complementary tools:

This divergence is further compounded by neuroanatomical differences between species. 3 The corticospinal tract (CST) is the primary descending pathway modulating volitional motor control. Following a thoracic contusion injury in rats, the ventral and dorsal CST components at the S1 spinal level frequently remain partially preserved, sustaining lower limb muscle motility. 29 Conversely, the human motor system relies on large lateral and ventral CSTs and lacks a dorsal component, meaning this specific anatomical preservation is absent in humans. Finally, the post-injury chronological window introduces further complexity: subsequent structural reorganization of neural networks and the neuroplasticity of spared perilesional neurons drive long-term behavioral recovery. Meanwhile, during the acute phase, transient spinal shock suppresses MEP amplitudes or abolishes signals entirely, temporarily masking the true anatomical preservation of the motor pathways.31-36

Discussion and Conclusion

The phenomenon of elevated BBB scores despite severe supraspinal disruption underscores the autonomous capability of intraspinal neural circuitry. In rodent models, striking functional compensation occurs even with minimal anatomical preservation; structural retention of as little as 1% of functional White matter axons can yield observable hindlimb articulation, while 10% tissue sparing frequently preserves substantial weight-bearing locomotion. 3 Conversely, this robust functional compensation is absent in humans—a divergence directly driven by species-specific differences in the neuroanatomy and functional topology of the corticospinal tract (CST).

While the CST serves as the primary conduit for volitional motor control in both species, its anatomical architecture diverges significantly:

• Human CST Architecture: Dominated by a massive lateral tract, which is structurally essential for fine motor precision and highly volitional, fractionated movement.

• Human CST Architecture: Dominated by a massive lateral tract, which is structurally essential for fine motor precision and highly volitional, fractionated movement.

• Rodent CST Architecture: Characterized by a dominant dorsal component that is largely absent in humans. This dorsal tract primarily orchestrates phylogenetically primitive, rhythmic, and reflexive locomotor patterns.

• Rodent CST Architecture: Characterized by a dominant dorsal component that is largely absent in humans. This dorsal tract primarily orchestrates phylogenetically primitive, rhythmic, and reflexive locomotor patterns.

• Shared Traits: Both species share a minor ventral CST component that contributes to axial muscle control and gross voluntary movement.

Moreover, the termination patterns of CST axons within the spinal cord Gray matter laminas differ markedly between rodents and primates. Due to this distinct anatomical distribution, rats subjected to mid-thoracic contusive SCI frequently exhibit preserved ventral and dorsal CST responses at the lumbar (S1) level. This localized preservation preserves downstream motor neuron drive, fuelling autonomous hindlimb motility and driving elevated BBB scores.37,38

Although establishing a comprehensive framework for integrating behavioral and electrophysiological assessments is highly desirable, creating a universally applicable, quantitative integration model remains exceptionally challenging. To address this, BBB scoring can be utilized primarily to capture real-time, functional compensatory mechanics, while neuro-electrophysiological tools can be deployed longitudinally to track structural pathway integrity. Synthesizing these complementary datasets allows researchers to achieve a more comprehensive characterization of experimental SCI, significantly enhancing the practical utility of both methods.

In conclusion, while the BBB locomotor rating scale remains an invaluable, cost-effective tool for profiling the temporal progression of SCI, it is inherently incapable of resolving underlying axonal pathophysiology or distinguishing true tract regeneration from local intraspinal compensatory mechanisms. Relying solely on behavioral metrics masks critical underlying neuropathology. Consequently, incorporating comprehensive, objective in vivo assessments—specifically multimodal neuroelectrophysiology via SSEPs and MEPs—is clinically and experimentally imperative to decouple automated spinal circuitry from supraspinal integrity across the distinct chronological phases of spinal cord injury.
