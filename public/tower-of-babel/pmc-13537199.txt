Educational Outcomes of Virtual and in-Person Wilderness Medicine Boot Camp Training Among Primary Care Sports Medicine Fellows
Authors: Kylie Manuppelli, Nathaniel Reed Geyer, Jessica Parascando, Shawn F Phillips, Cayce Onks, Jayson Loeffert
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundMedical boot camps provide simulation and skills training in structured environments. Wilderness Medicine (WM) is a core part of Primary Care Sports Medicine fellowships, teaching participants medical care in rigorous environments. A WM curriculum was delivered using a boot camp–style instructional format.PurposeThis study aimed to compare the effects of virtual versus in-person WM boot camp participation on fellows’ knowledge and comfort with WM topics.MethodsA WM boot camp was performed virtually and in-person in two consecutive years (2022 and 2023). Knowledge of individual topics was assessed using a pre and posttest and comfort in these topics immediately following the boot camp was assessed using a post-boot camp self-reported 5-point Likert scale survey. Results of these were analyzed in SAS v9.4 using exact tests for ordinal and nominal outcomes; p<0.05 indicated significance.ResultsIn-person (n=27) and virtual (n=24) participants showed significant improvement in comfort and knowledge following the boot camp. Both groups demonstrated immediate knowledge improvement, with a median post‑test score increase from six to nine out of ten (p<0.0001). Groups were not seen as statistically different. Regarding confidence, post‑boot camp surveys demonstrated improvement in most topics compared to pre-event surveys, with a significant difference seen between the groups for the topic of improvisational splinting (p<0.0001). In-person participants reported greater improvement in comfort with splinting. In-person participants reported no desire to change their learning format, whereas virtual participants demonstrated a neutral preference for format change (median: 3; p<0.0001).ConclusionBoth virtual and in-person WM boot camp formats produced improvements in knowledge and comfort. These findings act as a preliminary educational evaluation for the support of hybrid education models. Given the limitations of this study, it is necessary for continued evaluation of virtual and in-person learning modalities across medical specialties to guide future medical education.

Introduction

A medical “boot camp” is an intensive, focused educational intervention in which training on a defined topic is delivered in a condensed, structured format. These programs typically integrate simulation-based learning, hands-on skills practice, and targeted review of core knowledge. The implementation of boot camps during transitional periods into new clinical roles is designed to facilitate the acquisition of essential competencies within a limited timeframe.1,2 This educational strategy has been widely adopted across undergraduate medical education, residency, and fellowship training in multiple specialties as an effective modality for improving clinical skills, procedural proficiency, and practical competence.3,4 In addition, this allows the implementation of multiple learning theories into medical education (ie Kolb’s Experiential Learning Theory, Social Learning Theory, Constructivism).

The COVID-19 pandemic precipitated widespread social distancing measures and public health restrictions that necessitated a rapid transition from in-person instruction to virtual learning platforms.5,6 The pandemic required medical schools and hospital systems nationwide to adapt both didactic and experiential learning to remote formats.7–10 Because medical boot camps traditionally rely on in-person interaction, hands-on practice, and simulation, these restrictions posed significant challenges to their implementation.2,11 In response, a previously established and successful in-person Wilderness Medicine (WM) boot camp for Primary Care Sports Medicine (PCSM) fellows was adapted into a fully virtual educational experience.7

The implementation of virtual education spans the continuum of medical training, from undergraduate medical education to fellowship programs. Studies have demonstrated successful learning outcomes through this virtual format.12,13 Studies have also shown that in-person events can be successfully transitioned to virtual experiences.14,15 Few investigations have directly compared virtual and in-person educational formats.

To the authors’ knowledge, no studies have directly compared virtual and in-person instruction within the context of medical boot camp training, nor have studies been published evaluating the same educational event being performed simultaneously virtual and in-person and comparing educational outcomes. Addressing this gap is critical to informing the continued development and optimization of virtual boot camps within graduate medical education. It is currently uncertain how a virtual educational program might compare to its in-person counterpart.

The primary aim of this study was to determine whether a virtual boot camp format is comparable to an in-person format for teaching WM concepts to PCSM fellows. We compared these modalities in improving learners’ comfort with and knowledge of core WM topics. To address this objective, we analyzed pre- and post-boot camp survey data from two consecutive years of the same WM boot camp delivered in a hybrid format. By comparing responses from participants attending virtually and in-person, we hypothesized that there would be no statistically significant differences in knowledge acquisition or self-reported comfort with WM topics between the two groups, suggesting similar delivery of traditionally hands-on WM education via either virtual or in-person platforms.

Materials and Methods

We submitted this study to the institutional review board (Penn State University). The survey tool was determined to be exempt from formal review given the nature of the research. We have completed a prospective survey-based cohort study to evaluate pre- and post-evaluations of WM knowledge and comfort.

Curriculum Design

During the COVID-19 pandemic, we developed a virtual WM boot camp in response to social distancing recommendations implemented in 2020. In 2022, the program transitioned to a hybrid format, allowing participants to choose between virtual and in-person attendance. Loeffert et al described the structure and outcomes of the prior virtual-only WM boot camps.7,14

We developed the boot camp content using Accreditation Council for Graduate Medical Education (ACGME) PCSM fellowship curricular requirements and core concepts emphasized in WM–Emergency Medicine fellowship training programs.16 Core topics for the boot camp were identified as altitude sickness, cold injury, heat injury, splinting, extraction decision making, and mass casualty. Appendices 1 and 2 illustrate the daily schedules for the 2022 and 2023 boot camps.

Throughout the boot camp, participants engaged with presentation style lectures. These were given simultaneously to both in-person and virtual participants. Lectures presented case scenarios and encouraged questions and discussion from all participants. Effort was put forth to engage virtual and in-person participants equally. The only variation to this involved improvisational splinting. During this topic, both groups received a lecture with instruction regarding improvisational splinting. Following this, the in-person group was able to practice splinting techniques with provided splinting materials for about 15 minutes, under the supervision and instruction of course presenters. The virtual group watched this portion of the session through teleconference and was encouraged to practice with whatever resources they had available to them. Additionally, at the conclusion of the in-person boot camp, a series of standardized patient scenarios were held, allowing for the practice of concepts learned earlier in the day. Virtual participants were dismissed at the conclusion of the lecture style format, and thus were not able to participate in this portion of the boot camp.

Presenters

All presenters were faculty members affiliated with ACGME-accredited PCSM fellowship programs and held a Certificate of Added Qualification (CAQ) in sports medicine. Across both the 2022 and 2023 boot camps, presenters had between 2 and 18 years of teaching experience (mean: 8.5 years). All faculty had prior experience delivering both in-person and virtual instruction and were familiar with the hybrid educational platform before the boot camp.

Participants

Participants included PCSM fellows from fellowship programs across the United States as well as interested fellowship faculty members. Recruitment occurred through an Email distributed via the national PCSM fellowship listserv, accessed through the presenting institution’s PCSM fellowship program. We contacted and invited all PCSM fellowship program directors nationwide to share the opportunity with their fellows and faculty, regardless of participants’ primary residency training backgrounds. Fellows self-chose to attend virtually or in-person.

Inclusion criteria consisted of current PCSM fellows. We invited affiliated fellowship faculty members to join but excluded in the study results. No additional applicable exclusion criteria beyond limiting participation to invited PCSM fellows. Due to the hybrid format and variability in attendance throughout the day, precise participant counts were difficult to determine. The maximum number of registered participants logged in during the event was 67 in 2022 (17 in-person, 50 virtual) and 54 in 2023 (13 in-person, 41 virtual). Program Evaluation

Participants completed pre- and post-boot camp surveys (Appendices 3 and 4) designed to assess self-reported comfort with key WM topics addressed during the curriculum. These topics included altitude sickness, cold injury, heat injury, improvisational splinting, extraction decision-making, and mass casualty preparedness. We recorded responses using a 5-point Likert scale, with scores ranging from 1 (“not very comfortable”) to 5 (“very comfortable”). Similar survey methodologies have been used in the evaluation of prior medical education boot camps.2,4,7,12,14 These metrics have been used and described in previously published studies.

We assessed knowledge acquisition by using a pre- and post-boot camp test administered concurrently with the surveys (Appendix 5). We evaluated each WM topic using two multiple-choice questions.

While there were 30 in-person and 91 virtual participants over the 2 years, this does not reflect the number analyzed. In the in-person group, 1 person did not complete the pre and 2 did not complete the post, resulting in a final number of presurvey 29, post surveys 28, and 27 who completed both the pre and post. Similarly in the virtual group, 32 did not complete the presurvey, 65 did not complete the post, resulting in a final number of presurvey 57, post survey 26, and 24 individuals who completed both the pre and post surveys. One person from the virtual group completed the post survey but did not complete the post test. Pre and post values were calculated using all available data for those specific variables, however, to calculate p-values, only participants who completed both the pre and post questionnaire could be included in order to calculate the change that occurred from pre to post boot camp analysis.

Data Analysis

We conducted univariate analyses for participant primary specialties (eg, Family Medicine, Internal Medicine, Pediatrics, Internal Medicine-Pediatrics, Physical Medicine and Rehabilitation, and other backgrounds), by in-person, virtual, and total participants.

Likert scales are ordinal data with a natural order, but unknown means, allowing for ranking and determining the central tendency by quartiles and median, and accounting for the unequal distributions of ordinal categories. Likert-based outcomes using ordinal methods yield statistical power and unbiased interpretation better than dichotomization or continuous values.17

Sample size calculation based on the effect size of logistic regression analysis uses odd ratios to estimate power. The challenge is that the analysis uses secondary data already collected, so the analysis may be underpowered because of missing data and a convenience sample based on people who enrolled in the program and may have dropped out or not completed the survey. The logistic regression power analysis, using two tailed, odds ratio: 5.76, Pr (Y=1|X=1) H0: 0.15, alpha: 0.05, power: 0.8, R2 other X: 0, X distribution: Binomial, and X parm π: 0.5, resulting in 57 people.

Ansari–Bradley test is used to test the null hypothesis that two population distribution functions correspond to the two sample are identical against the alternative hypothesis of difference by dispersion or scale, comparable to the folded F-test for continuous outcomes.17 The Kolmogorov–Smirnov test is a nonparametric statistical test used to compare two probability distributions to determine if they are from the same underlying distribution. The Wilcoxon rank sum test (also called the Mann–Whitney U-test) is a nonparametric test that compares two independent samples to assess whether their population distributions differ, often interpreted as a difference in medians when certain assumptions hold.17 To account for 57 people with small cell counts, this required exact tests.

For between-group and post-test–only comparisons involving ordinal data, the exact Ansari–Bradley test, the non-parametric equivalent of the folded F-test for ordinal and non-normal variables, assessed for equal medians. When dispersion was not significantly different (p≥0.05), the exact Wilcoxon signed-rank test was used to compare medians; when dispersion differed significantly (p<0.05), the exact Kolmogorov–Smirnov test was applied.18 Within-group ordinal comparisons were conducted using an SAS macro described by Wicklin and Hemedinger (2023), located on GitHub, which applies exact Pratt’s modified Wilcoxon signed-rank test.19 Pratt proposed a modified Wilcoxon signed-rank test to address zero differences by retaining them in the analysis while assigning value only to the non-zero differences, thereby avoiding the bias introduced by discarding zero values.20

With nominal categorical variables, we analyzed using Fisher’s exact test for between-group comparisons. For within-group comparisons, we utilized exact binary logistic regression models, adjusting for group and pre-test values.

Pre- and post-test comparisons between virtual and in-person participants were analyzed using two-sided statistical significance (p<0.05) SAS software (Version 9.4; SAS Institute, Cary, NC; RRID:SCR_008567). Ordinal Likert-scale data were summarized using medians and interquartile ranges (IQR). We assessed the statistical associations of the outcomes and predictors by SAS PROCS FREQ, LOGISTICS, MEANS, and UNIVARIATE.

Discussion

We met the aim of this study, which was to determine whether a virtual boot camp format is comparable to an in-person format for teaching WM concepts to PCSM fellows. Largely in accordance with our initial hypothesis, both in-person and virtual participants demonstrated significant improvements in knowledge and self-reported comfort across most wilderness medicine topics (p<0.0001), with no significant differences between formats except for improvisational splinting (p<0.001).

The widespread implementation of isolation precautions and social distancing during the COVID-19 pandemic necessitated a rapid reimagining of medical education, particularly the adaptation of traditionally hands-on learning experiences to virtual platforms. Across clinical education, virtual modalities have been successfully implemented for medical students and residents in fields including surgery, radiology, and dermatology.10,21–23 Our findings suggest that WM, traditionally taught through immersive, hands-on experiences in austere environments, can be effectively taught through a boot camp–style curriculum using both virtual and in-person instruction, with comparable educational outcomes.

Prior studies have shown that virtual boot camps represent a practical and effective approach to teaching clinical skills.9,12,14 To our knowledge, this study is among the first to directly compare outcomes from the same boot camp delivered simultaneously in virtual and in-person formats. Participants perceived comfort and knowledge acquisition evaluated across six core WM topics. Following both instructional modalities, participants reported significant improvements in comfort levels across most topics. Importantly, we observed no significant differences between virtual and in-person participants for any topic except improvisational splinting.

It was interesting to see the decline in comfort of those attending in-person regarding extraction decision making. The extraction decision making lecture was prerecorded each year and provided to participants before the boot camp. It is possible that the in-person group had exaggerated comfort when taking the presurvey and then lost comfort after having the opportunity to perform standardized patient scenarios replicating real wilderness scenarios and experiencing the challenges faced in these encounters. Virtual participants did not get this hands-on opportunity and perhaps their comfort increased after developing increased comfort on other topics. An alternate theory is that because the virtual group ended their day earlier than in-person participants, perhaps they were able to watch the prerecorded video and then take the post survey. The in-person group was asked to watch the video before the boot camp (but after completing the presurvey). The closer proximity to watching the video may have biased the survey response.

Participants attending in-person reported greater improvement in comfort with improvisational splinting than those attending virtually. This finding is unsurprising, as splinting is a tactile skill that requires hands-on manipulation, physical assessment, and familiarity with available materials. Future virtual iterations of the boot camp may benefit from enhanced video demonstrations, real-time guided practice, and the incorporation of remote hands-on components to better support learners’ skill development.

Knowledge assessment results further support the hybrid boot camp model. Both virtual and in-person participants demonstrated significant improvements in post-test scores, with no significant differences between groups. Together with subjective comfort assessments, these findings support the idea that virtual and in-person instruction within this WM boot camp are educationally similar in regards to immediate outcomes following the boot camp. The ability to deliver traditionally hands-on content through virtual platforms has meaningful implications for accessibility, scalability, and dissemination of medical education.

Post-event evaluations indicate that participants in both groups perceived the boot camp as impactful to their fellowship education, reporting improvements in knowledge and comfort. Ratings related to whether the boot camp adequately taught WM concepts were slightly lower than anticipated, with median scores of three for in-person participants and four for virtual participants. Virtual participants rated their overall experience more favorably than in-person participants, an unanticipated finding. Explanations include increased comfort, reduced fatigue, and enhanced convenience associated with remote participation. When asked whether they would change their learning modality for future boot camps, in-person participants unanimously preferred to maintain the same format, whereas virtual participants expressed more neutral preferences. This may reflect the tradeoff learners experience between the convenience of virtual learning and the tangible benefits of hands-on engagement.

The study population is broadly generalizable to PCSM fellowship training, as participants represented a diverse group of fellows from programs across the United States. The hybrid format facilitated participation across geographic regions, program sizes, and time zones, further expanding access and engagement. It should be noted that fellows self-selected their attendance mode rather than being randomized, potentially making both groups not fully comparable to start, or biasing their outcomes and thus limiting causal inference.

Limitations and Future Directions

One limitation of this observational study is that objective assessments relied on self-reported survey measures. As with all self-report instruments, these data are susceptible to response bias, including social desirability bias, which may lead participants to overestimate their comfort levels. Given the high attrition rate among virtual participants, self-selection bias must be considered. Perhaps only those most engaged during the day completed the survey, skewing the final results positively. Additionally, we implemented no safeguards to ensure that post-test knowledge assessments reflected independent recall without the use of external resources.

Future research may address these limitations by incorporating more standardized objective assessments, such as observed skill demonstrations, video-submitted procedures, or Objective Structured Clinical Examination (OSCE)–style scenarios. These approaches may better evaluate whether self-reported comfort translates into practical competence. Additionally, long‑term follow‑up assessment, 6–12 months post‑boot camp, to assess knowledge and comfort retention should be considered to evaluate if changes are limited to only the immediate period following the boot camp or if they persist.

The adaptation of hands-on skills for virtual learners also posed challenges. Although virtual and in-person participants received identical instructional content and visual aids, in-person learners benefited from real-time physical manipulation of tools and peer collaboration. While we encouraged virtual participants to practice using available household materials, further optimization may include providing standardized skill kits or advance preparation checklists to improve parity between formats. It is reasonable to recognize that providing and distributing these types of prepared kits can be costly and may not be possible for all educational activities.12

Additionally, participation in virtual education requires access to appropriate technology and reliable internet connectivity. While this was not a limitation for our participants, it may affect implementation in resource-limited settings. Future programs could explore asynchronous modules or low-bandwidth alternatives to enhance accessibility.

Given the specialty-specific nature of this boot camp, the sample size is relatively small and further narrowed by the high virtual attrition rate. This is not dissimilar from similar studies, which often involve small sample sizes due to niche learner populations.2,4 These studies included 16 and 30 participants respectively, far less than the current sample size. While this may limit statistical power, it reflects an inherent challenge in evaluating focused, specialty-based educational interventions, yet demonstrates our boot camp’s ability to include and evaluate more participants than prior studied educational boot camps. As this was an observational study and recruitment was limited to those who completed the pre/post study/test, it is interesting to note that our final inclusion for study change (n=51) is only slightly below the 57 recommended by the post‑hoc power analysis. Only PCSM fellows were included in our study population, so future work should evaluate hybrid boot camp models in other learner groups, including medical students and residents.

Conclusion

This study provides preliminary evidence supporting the feasibility of virtual and in-person instruction within a WM boot camp curriculum for PCSM fellows to similarly provide educational benefit immediately post boot camp. Both formats resulted in significant improvements in learner comfort and knowledge. Given the limitations of the study, further research is needed to better understand the comparison between in-person and virtual learning. Ongoing evaluation of these models will help inform evidence-based educational practices and guide the development of accessible, effective, and scalable training programs in medicinal education.
