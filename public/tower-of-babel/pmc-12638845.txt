Proline-rich 15 depletion enhances Nrf2 cascade to promote osteoblast survival from oxidative injury
Authors: Zhiqiang Zhou, Zhi-gao Jin, Xiao-feng Qi, Ze-yu Qian, Tian-yu Shi, Peng Zhang, Chang-gong Lan, Yi Wang, Dong Liu
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractReducing oxidative stress via enhancing Nrf2 cascade activation can efficiently protect osteoblasts. Here siRNA-based screening of nuclear proteins identified PRR15 (proline-rich 15) as a potential regulator of the Nrf2-ARE pathway in osteoblasts. In primary murine osteoblasts, silencing PRR15 using targeted shRNA or CRISPR/Cas9-mediated knockout (KO) enhanced hydrogen peroxide (H₂O₂)-induced activation of the Nrf2 cascade, reducing oxidative stress and cell death. PRR15 silencing or KO also counteracted H₂O₂-induced suppression of proliferation and downregulation of differentiation markers (Runx2 and Col1a1) in murine osteoblasts. Similarly, PRR15 silencing in primary human osteoblasts amplified Nrf2-ARE activation and mitigated H₂O₂-induced oxidative stress. In contrast, PRR15 overexpression inhibited Nrf2 activation, leading to increased apoptosis under oxidative stress in murine osteoblasts. Importantly, Nrf2 silencing by targeted shRNA reversed the protective effects of PRR15 silencing/KO against H₂O₂-induced cytotoxicity in murine osteoblasts. PRR15 interacts with BACH1 (BTB and CNC Homology 1) to negatively regulate Nrf2 signaling, as PRR15 overexpression or silencing had no effect on Nrf2-ARE pathway activation in BACH1-silenced murine osteoblasts. In vivo, osteoblast-specific PRR15 knockdown via adeno-associated virus (AAV) injection reduced oxidative stress and ameliorated trabecular bone loss in ovariectomized mice. Thus PRR15 silencing enhances Nrf2 cascade to promote the survival of osteoblasts exposed to oxidative stress.Subject terms: Stress signalling, Apoptosis

Introduction

Osteoporosis and osteonecrosis are two prevalent systemic bone disorders characterized by substantial bone mass reduction and progressive impairment of bone microarchitecture1–3. Research indicates that the excessive generation of reactive oxygen species (ROS) and the ensuing oxidative damage to bone cells are key factors driving the onset and advancement of both osteoporosis and osteonecrosis4–6. In particular, ROS induces severe oxidative damage to osteoblasts, causing significant bone deterioration7–9. Osteoblasts are specialized mesenchymal cells primarily responsible for the synthesis, deposition, and mineralization of the bone matrix, playing a critical role in bone formation and homeostatic regulation of skeletal remodeling10,11. In vitro models of osteoblast oxidative injury commonly use oxidative agents, such as hydrogen peroxide (H2O2), dexamethasone, and nicotine12–18. These agents can lead to marked oxidative stress, causing mitochondrial dysfunction, lipid peroxidation, protein degradation, and DNA damage, collectively resulting in pronounced osteoblasts’ death and apoptosis13–16,18,19.

The nuclear factor erythroid 2-related factor 2 (Nrf2) cascade plays a crucial role in safeguarding osteoblasts from oxidative stress12,19–23. Under basal conditions, Nrf2 is negatively regulated by kelch-like ECH-associated protein 1 (Keap1), which promotes Nrf2 protein ubiquitination and proteasomal degradation24–28. Upon exposure to oxidative stimuli, electrophilic stressors or various chemicals, Keap1 undergoes conformational changes, leading to the release of Nrf2 protein24–28. Nrf2 protein then stabilizes in the cytosol and translocates to the nucleus and binds to antioxidant response elements (ARE) within the promoter regions of many target genes24–28. This interaction initiates the transcription and expression of a diverse array of cytoprotective genes involved in antioxidant defense, detoxification, and the maintenance of cellular redox balance, including heme oxygenase-1 (HO1) and NAD(P)H:quinone oxidoreductase 1 (NQO1)24–28.

Numerous studies have demonstrated that pharmacological agents can effectively mitigate oxidative injury in osteoblasts by stimulating Nrf2 signaling pathway. For instance, pharmacological activation of Nrf2 using compounds like 4-octyl itaconate (4-OI)12 and iKeap120 has been shown to protect osteoblasts from H2O2-induced oxidative damage. These agents act by disrupting the Keap1-Nrf2 complex, thereby allowing for Nrf2’s nuclear translocation and activation of ARE genes. Another promising compound, MIND4-17, activated Nrf2 signaling, thereby protecting osteoblasts from oxidative stress and cell death induced by H2O218. Similarly, Han et al. reported that chlorogenic acid mitigated oxidative stress and apoptosis in MC3T3-E1 osteoblastic cells exposed to H2O2 by activating the Nrf2 pathway through PI3K/Akt signaling17. In addition to pharmacological interventions, genetic strategies have also been employed to enhance Nrf2 signaling in osteoblasts. MicroRNAs, such as miR-45515 and miR-452321, have been found to target specific genes involved in Nrf2 regulation, leading to its activation and subsequent protection against oxidative stress. These findings collectively highlight the critical role of Nrf2 in safeguarding osteoblasts from oxidative damage and suggest that targeting this pathway may offer therapeutic potential for bone diseases associated with oxidative stress.

Proline-Rich 15 (PRR15) is a poorly characterized nuclear protein encoded by the PRR15 gene, distinguished by a high proline content, which is typically associated with facilitating protein-protein interactions and signaling complex assembly29. Initially identified in studies of embryogenesis, PRR15 has since been implicated in critical cellular processes, such as proliferation, differentiation, and survival, particularly in the context of tissue development and cancer biology29–31. Its expression is tissue-specific, with notable upregulation in gastrointestinal and reproductive tissues, as well as in various malignancies, including colorectal and endometrial cancers29–32. The expression and potential role of PRR15 in osteoblasts remain unclear. Our study demonstrates that PRR15 functions as an important negative regulator of the Nrf2 signaling in osteoblasts. Silencing PRR15 activated Nrf2 signaling and promoted osteoblast survival under oxidative stress.

Results

Screening of nuclear proteins reveals PRR15 as a potential regulator of Nrf2-ARE pathway in cultured osteoblasts

The nucleus plays a critical role in the activation of the Nrf2 signaling cascade25,33,34, which is essential for cellular defense mechanisms against oxidative stress. In this study, we aimed to screen a set of nuclear proteins with largely unknown functions for their potential role in modulating the Nrf2-ARE pathway. Specifically, we investigated eight nuclear proteins: C1orf109, Ccdc124, C12orf43, MKI67IP, PRR15, PRR16, RRP8, and ZC3H12C. Verified siRNAs targeting these proteins were introduced to knock down their expression. To induce basal Nrf2-ARE activity, MC3T3-E1 murine osteoblastic cells were treated with H₂O₂. Among the eight nuclear proteins tested, only PRR15 knockdown significantly elevated HO1 mRNA expression (Fig. 1A), NQO1 mRNA expression (Fig. 1B), and ARE activity (Fig. 1C) in H₂O₂-treated MC3T3-E1 osteoblastic cells. Knockdown of the remaining seven nuclear proteins did not result in any significant changes in these parameters (Fig. 1A–C). The applied siRNAs selectively reduced their target mRNA levels without influencing other mRNAs in MC3T3-E1 osteoblastic cells (Fig. S2A). Furthermore, in primary murine osteoblasts, H₂O₂-induced HO1 mRNA expression was also significantly augmented following the PRR15 siRNA treatment (Fig. 1D), while siRNAs targeting the other nuclear proteins remained ineffective (Fig. 1D). As shown, each applied siRNA selectively reduced its respective target mRNA levels, with no observed impact on non-target mRNAs in primary murine osteoblasts (Fig. S2B). These findings suggest that PRR15 might play a unique role in regulating the Nrf2-ARE pathway in osteoblasts.

PRR15 silencing augments H2O2-induced Nrf2 cascade activation in primary murine osteoblasts

To investigate the role of PRR15 in Nrf2 cascade activation, a shRNA-mediated approach was employed to suppress PRR15 expression in primary murine osteoblasts. Lentiviral transduction with PRR15 shRNAs, sh-mPRR15-sq1 and sh-mPRR15-sq2, followed by puromycin selection, successfully established stable murine osteoblasts. Compared to control murine osteoblasts expressing a scrambled control shRNA (“shC”), PRR15 mRNA and protein levels were significantly reduced in sh-mPRR15-sq1/2-expressing murine osteoblasts, irrespective of H2O2 treatment (Fig. 2A, B). As anticipated, PRR15 silencing did not alter the mRNA expression of Nrf2 or Keap1 in the primary murine osteoblasts, with or without H2O2 treatment (Fig. 2C, D). Furthermore, H2O2-induced Nrf2 protein stabilization was unaffected by PRR15 silencing (Fig. 2B, E). Keap1 protein expression was also unchanged in PRR15-silenced murine osteoblasts, regardless of H2O2 treatment (Fig. 2B, E). However, a notable finding was the significant enhancement of ARE activity in PRR15-silenced murine osteoblasts (Fig. 2F), despite unchanged H2O2-induced Nrf2 protein nuclear translocation (Fig. 2G). Moreover, H2O2-induced expression of Nrf2-dependent genes, including HO1 and NQO1, was markedly elevated in PRR15-silenced murine osteoblasts at both the mRNA and protein levels (Fig. 2H–J). It’s important to note that a solitary PRR15 shRNA treatment did not activate the Nrf2 signaling cascade in primary murine osteoblasts (Fig. 2A–J). These results suggest that PRR15 plays a pivotal role in modulating Nrf2 signaling in response to H2O2 in murine osteoblasts, potentially by influencing nuclear signaling related to ARE activation.

Silencing PRR15 in primary murine osteoblasts mitigates H₂O₂-induced oxidative stress

Enhancement of the Nrf2 signaling cascade is well-documented for its protective role against H₂O₂-induced oxidative injury in cultured osteoblasts12,15,18–20. Accordingly, we investigated whether silencing PRR15 could mitigate H₂O₂-induced oxidative damage. As demonstrated, in primary murine osteoblasts, PRR15 knockdown using targeted shRNAs, sh-mPRR15-sq1 and sh-mPRR15-sq2 (see Fig. 2), significantly inhibited the H₂O₂-induced increase in both CellROX red fluorescence intensity (Fig. 3A) and DCF-DA green fluorescence intensity (Fig. 3B), both of which are markers of ROS production and oxidative stress. Furthermore, PRR15 silencing prevented H₂O₂-induced mitochondrial depolarization, as evidenced by the reduction in JC-1 red aggregate-to-green monomer transition (Fig. 3C). To further substantiate the oxidative stress in H₂O₂-treated murine osteoblasts, the GSH/GSSG ratio was markedly decreased (Fig. 3D); This reduction was attenuated following PRR15 knockdown (Fig. 3D). In addition, PRR15 silencing inhibited H₂O₂-induced lipid peroxidation, as indicated by a decrease in TBAR intensity (Fig. 3E). As a biomarker of oxidative DNA damage, γ-H2AX expression was assessed, and PRR15 knockdown significantly suppressed H₂O₂-induced γ-H2AX levels in murine osteoblasts (Fig. 3F). Importantly, treatment with PRR15 shRNAs alone did not alter ROS production or oxidative injury in primary murine osteoblasts (Fig. 3A–F). Therefore, silencing PRR15 in primary murine osteoblasts mitigated H₂O₂-induced oxidative stress, without affecting baseline oxidative status.

Silencing PRR15 mitigates H₂O₂-induced apoptotic, anti-proliferative and anti-differentiation effects in primary murine osteoblasts

Previous studies have demonstrated that enhancing the Nrf2 signaling cascade plays a protective role in inhibiting H₂O₂-induced cytotoxicity in cultured osteoblasts or osteoblastic cells12,15,18–20. In our current investigation, we found that H₂O₂-induced elevations in caspase-3 and caspase-7 activities were significantly suppressed in primary murine osteoblasts expressing sh-mPRR15-sq1 or sh-mPRR15-sq2 (Fig. 4A, B). Furthermore, PRR15 knockdown inhibited H₂O₂-induced release of cytochrome C into the cytosol (Fig. 4C) and significantly reduced caspase-3 cleavage (Fig. 4D), in the treated murine osteoblasts. This suggests that PRR15 silencing effectively inhibited the activation of key apoptotic pathways. The shC-control murine osteoblasts showed significant activation of apoptosis upon H₂O₂ stimulation, as indicated by increased numbers of TUNEL-positive nuclei (Fig. 4E, F). Following PRR15 silencing, H₂O₂-induced apoptosis was substantially inhibited, confirming the role of PRR15 in modulating cell apoptosis under oxidative stress (Fig. 4E, F). Additionally, further analyses demonstrated that knockdown of PRR15 via sh-mPRR15-sq1 or sh-mPRR15-sq2 alleviated the H₂O₂-induced reduction in cell viability, as measured by CCK-8 optical density (OD) values (Fig. 4G), and significantly decreased H₂O₂-induced cell death (Fig. 4H) in primary murine osteoblasts. Nuclear EdU staining assays revealed that H₂O₂ treatment significantly reduced the proliferation of primary murine osteoblasts, as evidenced by a decrease in EdU-positive nuclei (Fig. 4I). Strikingly, PRR15 silencing attenuated this anti-proliferative effect, restoring EdU incorporation levels (Fig. 4I). To support impaired differentiation, H₂O₂ exposure downregulated the expression of differentiation markers Runx2 (Runt-related transcription factor 2) and Col1a1 (collagen type I alpha 1) in primary murine osteoblasts (Fig. 4J, K). This suppression was partially reversed upon PRR15 knockdown (Fig. 4J, K). These findings highlight the potential of silencing PRR15 to mitigate oxidative stress-induced damage and apoptosis in cultured murine osteoblasts.

PRR15 silencing enhances Nrf2 pathway activation, mitigates H₂O₂-induced oxidative stress in primary human osteoblasts

To investigate the role of PRR15 in human osteoblasts, primary human osteoblasts (from Dr. Liang’s group21,22) were transduced with a lentivirus expressing a shRNA targeting human PRR15 (“sh-hPRR15”). This resulted in a significant reduction in PRR15 protein levels, regardless of H₂O₂ treatment (Fig. 5A). PRR15 silencing by sh-hPRR15 did not affect H₂O₂-induced Nrf2 protein stabilization or Keap1 protein expression (Fig. 5A). However, it increased the expression of HO1 and NQO1, downstream targets of Nrf2, in H₂O₂-stimulated human osteoblasts (Fig. 5B). Consistent with the activation of the Nrf2 pathway, sh-hPRR15 attenuated H₂O₂-induced ROS production, as measured by CellROX/DCF-DA fluorescence intensity (Fig. 5C, D). Additionally, sh-hPRR15 inhibited H₂O₂-induced mitochondrial depolarization, shown by an increase in JC-1 green monomers (Fig. 5E). Moreover, sh-hPRR15 mitigated H₂O₂-induced apoptosis, or TUNEL-positive nuclei ratio increasing (Fig. 5F), cell viability reduction (Fig. 5G), and cell death (Fig. 5H) in the primary human osteoblasts. Furthermore, PRR15 silencing counteracted the H₂O₂-induced suppression of osteoblast proliferation, as demonstrated by the restoration of nuclear EdU incorporation (Fig. 5I). In addition, PRR15 knockdown alleviated H₂O₂-driven downregulation of differentiation markers Runx2 and Col1a1 in human osteoblasts (Fig. 5J, K). Therefore, PRR15 silencing enhanced Nrf2 pathway activation, mitigated H₂O₂-induced oxidative stress and apoptosis in primary human osteoblasts.

PRR15 knockout in murine osteoblasts enhances Nrf2 pathway activation and mitigates H₂O₂-induced oxidative stress

To eliminate the potential off-target effect of the applied shRNAs and to achieve complete depletion of PRR15, the CRISPR/Cas9 gene-editing approach was employed to knockout (KO) PRR15. Primary murine osteoblasts expressing Cas9 were transduced with one of the two lentiviral CRISPR/Cas9-PRR15-KO constructs containing different sgRNAs targeting PRR15. This procedure generated single stable murine osteoblasts, designated as ko-mPRR15-sg1 and ko-mPRR15-sg2 (with different sgRNAs). In comparison with control murine osteoblasts, which were transduced with the lentiviral CRISPR/Cas9-control construct (referred to as “koC”), PRR15 protein expression was ablated in the ko-mPRR15 murine osteoblasts (Fig. 6A). Consistent with the results obtained using shRNA, CRISPR/Cas9-mediated PRR15 KO did not influence H₂O₂-induced stabilization of Nrf2 protein (Fig. 6A). Additionally, Nrf2 mRNA and Keap1 levels, both at the mRNA and protein levels, remained unaltered in H₂O₂-treated ko-mPRR15 murine osteoblasts (Fig. 6A, B). However, H₂O₂-induced ARE activity (Fig. 6C), as well as the mRNA and protein expression of HO1 and NQO1 (Fig. 6D–F), were significantly increased in ko-mPRR15-sg1/2 osteoblasts. These findings further demonstrate that PRR15 KO enhanced H₂O₂-induced activation of the Nrf2 signaling in murine osteoblasts.

Additional functional analyses demonstrated that PRR15 KO attenuated H₂O₂-induced ROS production, as evidenced by the decreased fluorescence intensity of CellROX and DCF-DA (Fig. 6G, H). Furthermore, PRR15 KO mitigated H₂O₂-induced mitochondrial depolarization, indicated by reduced accumulation of JC-1 green monomers in ko-mPRR15 murine osteoblasts (Fig. 6I). Supporting the antioxidant role of PRR15 KO, we observed that the H₂O₂-induced reduction in the GSH/GSSG ratio (Fig. 6J) was alleviated in PRR15 KO osteoblasts. PRR15 KO alone did not significantly induce ROS production or oxidative damage in murine osteoblasts (Fig. 6G–J). In ko-mPRR15-sg1/2 murine osteoblasts, H₂O₂-induced apoptosis, as indicated by the increased ratio of TUNEL-positive nuclei (Fig. 6K), was substantially inhibited. The EdU assay further revealed that PRR15 KO inhibited the anti-proliferative effects of H₂O₂ in primary murine osteoblasts (Fig. 6L). Moreover, PRR15 KO significantly attenuated H₂O₂-induced reductions in cell viability (Fig. 6M) and prevented cell death (Fig. 6N) in murine osteoblasts. Additionally, PRR15 KO mitigated the H₂O₂-mediated downregulation of Runx2 and Col1a1 in murine osteoblasts (Fig. 6O, P).

PRR15 overexpression inhibits Nrf2 activation and exacerbates H₂O₂-induced apoptosis in primary murine osteoblasts

Given the protective role of PRR15 depletion on oxidative stress in osteoblasts, we hypothesized that PRR15 overexpression might have the opposite effect. To test this, primary murine osteoblasts were transduced with a lentiviral construct expressing murine PRR15, and stable murine osteoblasts (“oe-mPRR15”) formed after puromycin selection. PRR15 protein levels were significantly increased in oe-mPRR15 cells, both in the presence and absence of H₂O₂ treatment (Fig. 7A). However, PRR15 overexpression did not affect H₂O₂-induced Nrf2 protein stabilization or Keap1 protein expression (Fig. 7A). Importantly, H₂O₂-induced expression of HO1 and NQO1 was inhibited in oe-mPRR15 cells (Fig. 7B), suggesting Nrf2 inhibition. H₂O₂-induced ROS production, as measured by the increased CellROX fluorescence intensity, was augmented in PRR15-overexpressing murine osteoblasts (Fig. 7C). Additionally, PRR15 overexpression exacerbated H₂O₂-induced mitochondrial depolarization (measured by JC-1 green monomers accumulation, Fig. 7D), GSH depletion (Fig. 7E), and lipid peroxidation (measured by TBAR intensity, Fig. 7F). These effects ultimately led to increased apoptosis (increased TUNEL-positive nuclei ratio, Fig. 7G) and reduced cell viability (Fig. 7H) in PRR15-overexpressing murine osteoblasts. Together, PRR15 overexpression inhibited Nrf2 activation and exacerbated H₂O₂-induced apoptosis in primary murine osteoblasts.

PRR15 associates with BACH1 to negatively regulate Nrf2 signaling activation

To further substantiate that enhancing Nrf2 signaling is necessary for the antioxidant response induced by PRR15 depletion, two distinct lentiviral shRNA constructs targeting Nrf2, designated as sh-mNrf2-a and sh-mNrf2-b, were utilized in ko-mPRR15 murine osteoblasts. Both constructs effectively silenced Nrf2 protein levels in H₂O₂-treated ko-mPRR15 osteoblasts (Fig. 8A). Moreover, protein expression of HO1 and NQO1 was largely attenuated (Fig. 8A). Importantly, the cytoprotective effects conferred by PRR15 KO against H₂O₂ exposure, as assessed through viability (Fig. 8B), cell death (Fig. 8C), and apoptosis (Fig. 8D) assays, were completely abrogated following Nrf2 silencing. In line with other studies12,20, Nrf2 silencing exacerbated H2O2-induced cytotoxicity in cultured murine osteoblasts (Fig. 8B–D). These findings demonstrate that enhancing Nrf2 cascade activation is a critical mediator of the antioxidant defense mechanisms triggered by PRR15 depletion in osteoblasts.

Notably, co-immunoprecipitation (Co-IP) assays performed on nuclear lysates revealed that PRR15 associated with BACH1, but not directly with Nrf2 following H₂O₂ treatment (Fig. 8E). As a known negative regulator of Nrf2 signaling, BACH1 inhibits Nrf2 activity by competing for binding to ARE, thereby repressing the transcription of Nrf2 target genes35,36. In our study, silencing BACH1 using a lentiviral sh-mBACH1 construct significantly enhanced H₂O₂-induced mRNA expression of Nrf2 target genes HO1 and NQO1 in murine osteoblasts (Fig. 8F, G). Interestingly, when BACH1 was silenced, modulating PRR15 expression through sh-mPRR15-sq1 or oe-mPRR15 had no effect on HO1 and NQO1 mRNA levels in H₂O₂-treated osteoblasts (Fig. 8F, G). These results suggest that PRR15 possibly associates with BACH1 to negatively regulate Nrf2 signaling activation in osteoblasts. qPCR results further confirmed that the expression of target genes was altered following treatment with the corresponding viral constructs, with no cross-interference observed (Fig. 8H, I). Additionally, Nrf2 mRNA levels remained unaffected (Fig. 8J).

Osteoblast-specific PRR15 knockdown ameliorates oxidative stress and trabecular bone loss in ovariectomy mice

To elucidate the functional role of PRR15 in osteoblast function, we employed an osteoblast-specific knockdown strategy using AAV-mediated delivery of shRNA targeting PRR15. The shRNA sequence was driven by the Col1a1 promoter in the AAV vector to ensure osteoblast-specific knockdown (PRR15-obKD). AAV particles (at 1013 vg/mL, 5 μL per tibia) were injected directly into the tibial bone marrow to achieve localized shRNA delivery to osteoblasts. To assess the functional impact of PRR15-obKD, we utilized the ovariectomy (OVX) model of postmenopausal osteoporosis. Micro-computed tomography (micro-CT) analysis revealed that PRR15-obKD substantially mitigated trabecular bone loss in OVX mice (Fig. 9A). Quantitative assessment showed significant amelioration in the reduction of bone volume/tissue volume (BV/TV, %, Fig. 9B) and bone mineral density (BMD, Fig. 9C) in trabecular bone following PRR15 knockdown in osteoblasts. Furthermore, PRR15-obKD significantly mitigated the decrease in trabecular thickness (Tb.Th; Fig. 9D) and trabecular number (Tb.N; Fig. 9E), as well as the elevated trabecular separation (Tb.Sp; Fig. 9F) observed in OVX mice. The cortical bone BMD, which exhibited a slight decrease eight weeks post-OVX, was also preserved with PRR15-obKD (Fig. 9G). The cortical thickness (Ct.Th; Fig. 9H) showed no significant difference between PRR15-obKD-treated OVX mice and AAV-shC-treated OVX mice.

Female C57BL/6 mice underwent bilateral ovariectomy (OVX) to induce bone loss. Two weeks post-surgery, mice were injected with either Col1a1-PRR15-shRNA-AAV (“PRR15-obKD”) or control Col1a1-shRNA-AAV (“AAV-shC”) into the tibial bone marrow. Mice were sacrificed eight weeks following OVX surgery. Representative micro-CT images of trabecular and cortical bone were obtained (A). Oxidative stress in osteoblasts is an important mediator of OVX-induced bone density reduction, supported by ROS-driven functional impairment, apoptotic pathways, and rescue via antioxidant strategies37–39. To determine whether PRR15-obKD activated antioxidant mechanisms, SOD activity was measured in tibial tissue. SOD activity was markedly reduced in the OVX group eight weeks post-surgery (Fig. 9I), but PRR15-obKD treatment significantly restored it (Fig. 9I). In OVX mice, RANKL (receptor activator of nuclear factor-κB ligand) is upregulated due to estrogen deficiency, driving osteoclast-mediated bone resorption and osteoporosis40,41. Here we found that PRR15-obKD largely inhibited RANKL upregulation in OVX mice (Fig. 9J). Gene expression analysis confirmed effective PRR15 knockdown in PRR15-obKD mice (Fig. 9K), while PRR15 expression remained unchanged in the OVX-only group (Fig. 9K). TARP staining assay showed that the number of osteoclasts significantly increased in AAV-shC-treated OVX mice (Fig. 9L), and this increase was not significantly affected by PRR15-obKD (Fig. 9L). Collectively, these findings demonstrate that osteoblast-specific PRR15 knockdown significantly ameliorated oxidative stress and trabecular bone loss in OVX mice.

Discussion

Osteoblasts, the cells responsible for bone formation, are highly susceptible to oxidative stress, which can lead to their dysfunction and apoptosis42,43. In conditions like osteoporosis or osteonecrosis, increased oxidative stress has been observed, resulting in the accumulation of ROS and impairment of osteoblast function, eventually leading to reduced bone density and bone necrosis42,43. The transcription factor Nrf2 plays a pivotal role in protecting osteoblasts from oxidative injury by regulating the expression of antioxidant and cytoprotective genes12,15,17,18,44–46. Upon activation, Nrf2 translocates to the nucleus and binds to ARE in the promoters of target genes, thereby inducing the expression of a wide array of antioxidant enzymes and proteins such as SOD, catalase, glutathione peroxidase, and HO126,34,47. These antioxidant defenses help neutralize ROS and mitigate the oxidative injury in osteoblasts. Forced activation of Nrf2 signaling in osteoblasts or osteoblastic cells, through the use of various pharmacological agents (i.e., 4-OI, iKeap1, compound 16, MIND4-17) or genetic strategies, has been shown to significantly inhibit oxidative damage induced by H2O2 and other oxidative stimuli12,15,17,18,44–46. This activation not only enhances the survival and function of osteoblasts under oxidative stress but also promotes their differentiation and mineralization, which are essential for maintaining bone health42,43.

It is important to explore the detailed mechanisms of Nrf2 signaling due to its central role in regulating cellular defense against oxidative stress in osteoblasts12,15,17,18,44–46. Our findings unequivocally demonstrate that PRR15 acts as a potent negative modulator of Nrf2 pathway activation within osteoblastic cells. Targeted KO of PRR15 in primary murine or human osteoblasts led to a marked upregulation of the Nrf2 pathway in response to oxidative stress, resulting in a concomitant reduction of intracellular ROS levels, and attenuating cell death. PRR15 silencing or KO also counteracted H₂O₂-induced suppression of proliferation and downregulation of differentiation markers (Runx2 and Col1a1) in osteoblasts. Conversely, ectopic overexpression of PRR15 in murine osteoblasts suppressed H₂O₂-induced Nrf2 pathway activation, leading to a marked increase in apoptotic cell death. These results collectively suggest a conserved role for PRR15 in regulating Nrf2 signaling in osteoblasts and highlight its importance in maintaining cellular redox balance and survival under oxidative stress conditions.

BACH1 is a key negative regulator of Nrf2 signaling, primarily through its ability to compete with Nrf2 for binding to ARE in the promoters of target genes35,36. Under normal conditions, BACH1, a transcriptional repressor, binds to ARE and suppresses the expression of antioxidant and cytoprotective genes35,36. However, when cells experience oxidative stress, such as exposure to H2O2, Nrf2 is stabilized and translocates to the nucleus, where it displaces BACH1 from the ARE, allowing for the transcription of antioxidant genes. Our studies showed that PRR15 interacts with BACH1 to inhibit Nrf2 signaling in cells exposed to oxidative stress, reinforcing the repressive effect of BACH1 on Nrf2’s transcriptional activity. In the absence of BACH1 (as in BACH1-silenced murine osteoblasts), PRR15 did not influence Nrf2-ARE activation, suggesting that its inhibitory effect on Nrf2 is mediated specifically through its interaction with BACH1. Understanding this dynamic provides insights into how Nrf2 activity is tightly regulated and could reveal potential targets for enhancing the Nrf2 pathway to protect osteoblasts from oxidative injury.

In the present study, we showed that osteoblast-specific PRR15 knockdown via AAV injection effectively reduced oxidative stress and ameliorated trabecular bone loss in OVX mice, underscoring its therapeutic potential in bone-related pathologies such as osteoporosis8,11,48,49. In the context of postmenopausal osteoporosis, characterized by increased oxidative stress and enhanced osteoblast damage8,11,48,49, targeting PRR15 may restore bone homeostasis by mitigating oxidative damage and reducing bone resorption. AAV-mediated gene therapy, with its ability to achieve osteoblast-specific targeting, offers a promising strategy for addressing oxidative stress-related bone loss, potentially providing sustained therapeutic effects and improving clinical outcomes in these debilitating conditions.

Methods

Reagents

Serum, medium, antibiotics, CCK-8, puromycin, polybrene, and hydrogen peroxide (H2O2) were purchased Sigma (St Louis, Mo). Antibodies targeting PRR15 and PRR16 as well as fluorescence dyes were purchased from Thermo Fisher Scientific (Waltham, MA) unless otherwise mentioned. The PRR15 antibody was from Dr. Zheng50. All other antibodies and validated mRNA primers used in this study were kindly provided by Dr. Jiang51,52.

Cells

With the help from Dr. Liang’s group21,22, trabecular bone fragments were obtained from healthy donors following written informed consent. The fragments were finely minced, washed with phosphate-buffered saline (PBS), and subjected to enzymatic digestion using a combination of collagenase and trypsin to facilitate the release of osteoblasts from the bone matrix. Following digestion and thorough washing, the isolated primary human osteoblasts were cultured in DMEM supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS), penicillin, streptomycin, and ascorbic acid to promote cellular growth and differentiation. The primary murine osteoblasts were provided by Dr. Xu’s group12,20. Briefly, cells were harvested from neonatal mouse calvaria using sequential collagenase digestion. These cells were cultured under conditions mimicking the native bone microenvironment, promoting their growth and differentiation into mature osteoblasts. The MC3T3-E1 murine osteoblastic cell line, kindly provided by Dr. Zhou20, was cultured under standard conditions in α-MEM supplemented with 10% FBS. Cells were regularly monitored to maintain their growth and viability. The protocols of using primary human cells were approved by the Ethics Committee of the Second affiliated Hospital of Soochow University.

Small interfering RNA (siRNA)

Osteoblasts or osteoblastic cells were seeded in 6-well plates to achieve 50% confluence before transfection. A 200 nM verified siRNA solution (all provided by Genechem) was prepared by diluting 5 µL siRNA in 95 µL Opti-MEM, while 6 µL Lipofectamine RNAiMAX was diluted in 100 µL Opti-MEM. These solutions were combined and incubated for 10 min to allow complex formation. The siRNA-Lipofectamine complex was added dropwise to the wells, and the cells were incubated at 37 °C for 48 h. Gene knockdown was always verified by qPCR assays.

ARE reporter activity assay

As previously described52, the primary murine osteoblasts or MC3T3-E1 murine osteoblastic cell were genetically modified by transduction with a firefly luciferase reporter vector driven by an ARE promoter52. Following the transduction, cells were exposed to various experimental treatments and ARE reporter activity was then assessed by quantifying firefly luciferase expression. This was achieved by measuring the luminescence intensity produced by the luciferase enzyme, with higher luminescence reflecting increased ARE activation.

qPCR

TRIzol reagents (Takara, Otsu, Japan) were employed to extract total RNA, with its concentration measured spectrophotometrically at 260 nm (A260). Next cDNA was synthesized using a Reverse Transcription Kit (Applied Biosystems, Foster City, CA). qPCR reactions were performed with SYBR Green real-time PCR Kit (Takara, Otsu, Japan) on a CFX Opus 96 Real-Time PCR System (BioRAD, Hercules, CA). mRNA levels were quantified using the 2-ΔΔCt method, normalized to glyceraldehyde-3-phosphate dehydrogenase (GAPDH). PRR15 mRNA primers were from Dr. Zheng50.

Western blotting

Cells were lysed with RIPA buffer (Beyotime, Wuxi, China) containing protease inhibitors (Beyotime), with protein concentration determined using a BCA kit (Thermo Fisher Invitrogen). Proteins were separated by SDS-PAGE and transferred to PVDF membranes (Millipore, Burlington, MA). The practice involved cutting the PVDF membrane after transfer, based on the molecular weight of the target band (approximately 15–20 kD above and below), as the colored markers were not visible during X-ray exposure. After blocking (using 10% non-fat milk in PBST), membranes were incubated with primary and secondary antibodies. During X-ray exposure, we aligned the film with the marks to ensure the observed band corresponds to the target protein’s expected size. The target band and its surrounding area were then cut from the image, using a rectangular outline. Finally, the rectangular image was imported into Photoshop, where the specific band of interest was further cropped to the desired width for the final figure. The uncropped rectangular blotting images with the molecular markers are listed in the Fig. S1. To exact cell nuclear fraction, the cell homogenate was centrifuged at 1000 × g for 10 min at 4 °C to pellet the nuclei. The nuclear pellet was washed and resuspended in nuclear extraction buffer for further analysis. The same quantified lysate batch was tested in sister gels, and all experiments were carried out five biological repeats.

Co-immunoprecipitation

As describedin refs. 12,20, the nuclear fraction lysates were pre-cleared and incubated with the anti-PRR15 antibody overnight. After the overnight incubation, the antibody-protein complexes were captured by protein A/G agarose beads (Sigma) to facilitate immunoprecipitation. The beads were then washed to remove non-specifically bound proteins. Subsequently, the bound proteins were eluted from the beads, separated by SDS-PAGE, and analyzed by Western blotting to detect PRR15 and its associated proteins.

shRNA-mediated gene silencing

To effectively reduce PRR15 expression, osteoblasts were subjected to shRNA-mediated knockdown experiments. Lentiviral particles carrying PRR15 shRNA sequences within a GV369 construct, procured from Genechem (Shanghai, China), were introduced into the cultured osteoblasts at a multiplicity of infection of 10–11. After a 36 h infection period, osteoblasts were selected for stable PRR15 knockdown using puromycin (2–3 μg/mL) over three consecutive passages. Two distinct shRNA sequences targeting murine PRR15, sh-mPRR15-sq1 (target sequence: ATGCTTCACCCAAGTTCAGAGTATG) and sh-mPRR15-2# (target sequence: GTCCCTCAGGTCAGATAAGTTGTGT), were employed. Additionally, one shRNA sequence targeting human PRR15, sh-hPRR15 (target sequence: TCAATTGGGTTCTCAATATTT), was employed. Control osteoblasts were infected with lentiviral particles containing a scramble shRNA sequence (“shC”, Genechem). The levels of PRR15 mRNA and protein in stable osteoblasts were closely monitored over time. To silence Nrf2 expression in primary murine osteoblasts, lentiviral particles carrying the shRNA construct targeting mouse Nrf2 (sh-mNrf2-a, targeting: CCAAAGCTAGTATAGCAATAA or sh-mNrf2-b, targeting CCCGAATTACAGTGTCTTAAT) were introduced. After selecting for cells resistant to puromycin, the stable cell population of Nrf2-silenced osteoblasts was established. To downregulate BACH1 (BTB and CNC Homology 1) expression in cultured murine osteoblasts, lentiviral particles harboring BACH1-specific shRNA (Genechem) were transduced into the osteoblasts. Subsequently, stable cells were established through puromycin-based selection. The expression targeted gene in the stable cell populations was always checked.

PRR15 overexpression

To enhance PRR15 expression, murine osteoblasts were subjected to lentiviral transduction with a murine PRR15-expressing vector (GV369 construct, Genechem) at a multiplicity of infection (MOI) of 12. This vector contained the murine PRR15 open reading frame sequence ([NM_030024.3]) and was introduced into the cells for a period of 36 h. Subsequently, osteoblasts were cultured in complete medium supplemented with puromycin to establish stable PRR15-overexpressing murine osteoblasts after four passages. The levels of PRR15 mRNA and protein in these stable osteoblasts were regularly monitored. An empty vector was utilized to transduce the control constructs.

CRISPR/Cas9-mediated KO of the PRR15

The murine osteoblasts were initially infected with a lentivirus expressing Cas9, followed by selection of stable clones. Afterwards, these clones were transduced with one of two CRISPR/Cas9-PRR15-KO lentiviral constructs, each containing a PRR15-targeting sgRNA (ko-mPRR15-sg1: target sequence: CTGCTAGTCCGATCTGAGTG, PAM: AGG; or ko-mPRR15-sg2: target sequence: TCCGGGGTCCCCAAGGATAT, PAM: TGG), both provided by Genechem (Shanghai, China). Stable cell clones were selected using puromycin and seeded into 96-well plates at single-cell densities. Sequencing confirmed successful gene editing at the PRR15 locus, and Western Blotting was used to verify KO status. Single-cell-derived PRR15 KO murine osteoblasts were generated. As a control, the murine osteoblasts were treated with a lentivirus carrying an empty CRISPR/Cas9 vector (“koC”), also sourced from Genechem.

Cell viability and death assays

Murine or human osteoblasts (seeded at 4000 cells/well) were placed in 96-well plates and subjected to designated treatments. After predetermined incubation periods (48 h), 15 µL/well of CCK-8 reagent (Sigma) was added, and absorbance was measured at 450 nm using a microplate reader to assess cell viability. Cell death was quantified by trypan blue exclusion using a commercial staining kit (Biyuntian, Wuxi, China).

Lipid peroxidation assay

The lipid peroxidation was measured using the thiobarbituric acid reactive substances (TBAR) assay kit (Cayman Chemical, Ann Arbor, MI) according to the manufacturer’s protocol. Cell lysates (40 µg protein per treatment) were incubated with thiobarbituric acid (TBA) at 94 °C for 55 min, allowing malondialdehyde (MDA) to form adducts with TBA. The MDA-TBA complexes were quantified spectrophotometrically at 535 nm, and MDA concentrations were calculated from a standard curve.

GSH/GSSG ratio determination

The GSH (reduced glutathione) to GSSG (oxidized glutathione) ratio was evaluated using a kit from Thermo-Fisher Scientific Invitrogen (Waltham, MA). Lysates were incubated with DTNB [5,5’-dithiobis-(2-nitrobenzoic acid)], glutathione reductase, and NADPH (nicotinamide adenine dinucleotide phosphate) to drive the reaction. Absorbance was measured at 450 nm to quantify the GSH/GSSG ratio, which was normalized to the protein concentration.

TUNEL/EdU staining

The murine or human osteoblasts cultured on cover-slips were subjected to applied treatment for specified durations. Cells were then washed, fixed, permeabilized (with 0.15% Triton X-100 in citrate buffer), and incubated with TUNEL/EdU dye (Biyuntian, Wuxi, China). The nuclei were counterstained with 4′,6-diamidino-2-phenylindole (DAPI, Biyuntian, Wuxi, China) and visualized under a fluorescence microscope.

Cytosolic cytochrome c ELISA

After designated treatments, murine or human osteoblasts were harvested, washed with PBS, and lysed in cold buffer without detergents. After homogenization, the lysate was centrifuged at 1000 × g to remove nuclei and at 10,000 × g to pellet mitochondria. The cytosolic fraction was collected for the cytochrome c ELISA (Cell Signaling Tech) based on the attached protocol. Absorbance was measured at 450 nm, and cytosolic cytochrome c levels were quantified against a standard curve and normalized to total protein.

Caspase-3/7 activity measurement

Caspase-3 and Caspase-7 activities were quantified using commercially available colorimetric assay kits (Thermo-Fisher Invitrogen, Waltham, MA) according to the manufacturer’s protocols. Protein lysates were prepared at a concentration of 0.8 μg/μL (25 μg protein lysates per sample) and incubated with specific substrates for caspases. Enzymatic cleavage of the substrates resulted in the production of a colorimetric product, which was measured spectrophotometrically at 425 nm. A standard curve was generated to ensure accurate quantification of caspase activity.

JC-1 staining

To assess mitochondrial membrane potential (MMP), osteoblasts undergoing the designated treatments were incubated with JC-1 dye (2.5 μg/mL) for 20 min, followed by a washing step to remove excess dye. The reduction in MMP was visualized by fluorescence microscopy, where a increase in green fluorescence intensity indicated a loss of MMP.

Other fluorescence dye assays

Osteoblasts (murine or human) were cultured on coverslips and exposed to specific experimental conditions. After the designated incubation period, cells were treated with fluorescent probes, including CellROX or DCF-DA. Unbound dye was removed by thorough washing with ice-cold PBS. Fluorescence microscopy was performed to capture fluorescence images. Quantitative analysis of relative fluorescence intensity (RFI) was subsequently conducted using ImageJ software.

Animal studies

Six-week-old female C57BL/6 mice, weighing 20.1–20.4 g, were obtained from SLAC Laboratory Animal Co. (Shanghai, China). Animals were allocated to treatment groups using randomization. The treatments, measurements, and animal/cage locations were randomized, and all operators remained blinded to group allocation throughout the experiment and outcome assessment. All animals were maintained in a controlled environment with regulated temperature and humidity, on a strict 12 h light/12 h dark cycle. Anesthesia was induced via intraperitoneal administration of a ketamine (80 mg/kg)/xylazine (4 mg/kg) cocktail. To perform ovariectomy (OVX)53, the mice underwent bilateral OVX, a surgical procedure that involves making small incisions to remove both ovaries53. After surgery, mice were monitored carefully for recovery and housed under standard conditions with food and water available ad libitum. Eight weeks post-OVX, high-resolution micro-CT scans were acquired for both OVX-treated and control groups using a 1176 in-vivo CT scanner (Bruker Corporation, Billerica, CA). Data reconstruction and bone erosion assessment were conducted using CT analyzer software and a custom Fiji script, respectively. Key bone parameters, including trabecular bone volume fraction (BV/TV) and bone mineral density (BMD) for both trabecular and cortical bone, were quantified. Following micro-CT scans, right tibias were collected for further analysis. To assess oxidative stress in the bone, superoxide dismutase (SOD) activity was measured in fresh bone tissue using a commercially available ELISA kit (RayBiotech, Suzhou, China). In addition, an osteoblast-specific knockdown strategy was employed using adeno-associated virus (AAV)-mediated delivery of shRNA of PRR15 (from Dr. Zheng50). The AAV vector was designed to express the shRNA sequence under the control of the Col1a1 (collagen type I alpha 1 chain) promoter, ensuring osteoblast-specific knockdown of PRR15 (PRR15-obKD). AAV particles were generated and purified, and the viral titers were determined prior to in vivo injection. Mice were anesthetized, and the tibial region was sterilized. A small incision was made to expose the tibial bone, and a fine needle was used to inject the AAV particles (at 1013 vg/mL, 5 μL per tibia) directly into the tibial bone marrow cavity. The injection site was then sutured, and the animals were allowed to recover under standard post-operative care. We have complied with all relevant ethical regulations for animal use. Carbon dioxide (CO2) inhalation was utilized for the euthanasia of the mice per AVMA guidelines. All experimental procedures were approved by the Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee (IACUC) of the Second affiliated Hospital of Soochow University and followed the ARRIVE guidelines and all other relevant ethical regulations for animal use.

TRAP (tartrate-resistant acid phosphatase) staining

Following decalcification in 10% EDTA for four weeks, bone specimens were dehydrated through a graded ethanol series, cleared in xylene, and embedded in paraffin blocks. Sections of 5 μm thickness were cut using a microtome. Osteoclasts were visualized via TRAP staining, and images of stained sections were acquired using light microscopy.

Statistics and Reproducibility

Researchers conducting the experiments were blinded to the group assignments for in vitro studies. Normally distributed data were expressed as mean ± standard deviation (SD). ANOVA followed by Scheffe’s post-hoc test (SPSS 23.0, SPSS Co., Chicago, IL) was employed when comparing differences among multiple groups. Two groups’ comparison was made using a two-tailed unpaired T-test (Excel 2007). Statistical significance was defined as P < 0.05.
