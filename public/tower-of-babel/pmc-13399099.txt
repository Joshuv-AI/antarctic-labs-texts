Assessment of triglyceride‐glucose composite indices associated mortality risk in diabetes‐related heart failure
Authors: Qijie Zhao, Xiao Cai, Xing Wang, Jing Jin
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTBackgroundPatients with diabetes are at an increased risk of heart failure. The triglyceride‐glucose index increases the risk of diabetes‐related heart failure (dHF). We aimed to validate a practical clinical risk score for dHF in a large diabetic cohort and assess whether this score can identify dHF patients with an elevated triglyceride‐glucose (TyG) index who have poor outcomes.MethodsParticipants were drawn from multiple NHANES cycles (1999–2018). A simplified TRS‐HFDM score (0–6 points) and TyG‐related parameters were calculated in 3,455 participants with dHF. Candidate variables were evaluated using multivariable Cox proportional hazards models, Kaplan–Meier curves, and restricted cubic splines (RCS); independent predictors with statistical significance were included. Sensitivity analyses tested robustness.ResultsKaplan–Meier analysis revealed that higher levels of TRS‐HFDM were significantly associated with poor dHF participants survival. Multivariate‐adjusted Cox regression analysis revealed that high quartile levels of TyG‐related indices were significantly associated with mortality in participants with dHF [TyG‐Age: HR = 6.94, 95% CI: 4.34–11.08, P < 0.001; TyG‐eGFR: HR = 3.78, 95% CI: 2.66–5.37, P < 0.001]. The participants in the highest quartile of TyG‐Age had a six‐fold increased risk of mortality compared to those in the lowest quartile (95% CI: 3.93–10.24). Nonlinear trends were observed between TyG‐Age indices and mortality of dHF participants (P for overall < 0.0001; P for nonlinear < 0.0001). The sensitivity analyses supported the positive correlations between TyG‐related indices and mortality of the TRS‐HFDM‐determined dHF population.ConclusionTyG‐related indices, especially TyG‐Age, have strong prognostic value for mortality in adults with dHF. TyG‐Age and TRS‐HFDM may serve as surrogate biomarkers for clinical management of dHF.

INTRODUCTION

Diabetes and heart failure (HF) are highly prevalent diseases associated with substantial morbidity and mortality. HF is one of the most common comorbidities among diabetes patients. 1 Most recently, the epidemiological study based on the global population revealed that the overall trend of diabetes will have a 46% increase in the next three decades, and North America and the Caribbean approximately from 51 to 63 million. 2 Among the U.S. population, it was estimated that over 26.4% of the diabetes population had HF risk, especially in terms of older adults. 3 Accumulating evidence suggested that the series of cardiovascular symptoms coexisting in diabetic individuals such as heart failure, coronary artery disease, and angina. 2 , 4 The clinical trials demonstrated that successful control of multiple risk factors [hyperglycemia, obesity, hypertension, dyslipidemia, insulin resistance (IR), etc.] can reduce the risk of diabetes‐related cardiovascular events by ≥40%. 5 , 6 , 7 The reality is that the quality‐of‐life survival for those suffering from HF remains poor; symptoms and health management are crucial through each stage of diabetic HF complications. 8 , 9 Nevertheless, identifying the prognostic biomarkers and designing tailored follow‐up advice for the dHF risk population remains challenging. 10

The triglyceride‐glucose (TyG) index is important for evaluating cardiovascular disease (CVD) risks among the general population. 11 TyG‐related indices show promising value in predicting the onset and progress of multiple metabolic diseases, and in reflecting the interplay between lipid and glucose metabolisms. 12 Emerging evidence highlighted that the TyG index also maintained the high capability of identifying individuals at risk of diabetes‐related CVD. 12 , 13 Meanwhile, the Thrombolysis in Myocardial Infarction risk score for heart failure in diabetes (TRS‐HFDM), a simple validated clinical risk score, can be used to identify patients with a greater increase in diabetes‐related heart failure (dHF) risks. 14 The integer‐based risk score TRS‐HFDM using readily available clinical characteristics has been successfully proved to provide remission for administration in diabetes cardiovascular outcomes. 15 However, TRS‐HFDM inherently has no discrimination for non‐heart failure‐related outcomes (including cardiovascular death and all‐cause mortality), and thereby cannot distinguish the dHF‐specific mortality risk. 16 To date, few studies with limited sample sizes have evaluated the association between TyG‐related indices or TRS‐HFDM and the risk of cardiovascular events in individuals with diabetes. Whether these parameters could serve as surrogate biomarkers for health care in the dHF population remains unknown, especially in countries with high diabetes burdens.

To fill the research gaps, we therefore sought to explore the associations between TRS‐HFDM and TyG‐related indices with mortality of adults with dHF, based on a large‐scale population‐based cohort. This is a preliminary study to explore the links between TRS‐HFDM and TyG‐related indices in the clinical outcomes of adults with dHF, which may bring additional benefits to the clinical management of adults with dHF.

MATERIALS AND METHODS

Study population

This prospective study was conducted over 10 cycles of interviews from (1999–2018 years) in the National Health and Nutrition Examination Survey (NHANES) database. A total of 3,455 adults with diabetes were screened consecutively for eligibility. The inclusion criteria were: (1) diagnosed with diabetes; (2) measurement of standard biochemistry profiles from blood and/or urine samples. The exclusion criteria were as follows: (1) aged >80 years or <18 years; (2) missing follow‐up time or missing variables for calculating TRS‐HFDM; (3) tumor caused death. The detailed flow was presented in Figure 1. The NHANES database systematically gathered nationally representative health‐related data on noninstitutionalized individuals, and the executed protocols were approved by the National Center for Health Statistics (NCHS) Research Ethics Review Board. The specific descriptions of the NHANES database can be found on the website (https://www.cdc.gov/nchs/nhanes/).

The flow of the enrolled patients throughout the trial.

Clinical characteristics and covariates

The demographic information of participants was from the NHANES database. We selected more than 35 candidate risk indicators for the current analysis, based on their etiology relevance, prevalence and the ability to readily collect the variables from medical records of diabetes patients. Selected risk indicators included age, race (Hispanic, non‐Hispanic White, non‐Hispanic Black, or Others), marital status (not married, married or living with partner), educational level (≤high school, college, or university), gender (male or female), established CVD, estimated glomerular filtration rate (eGFR), total cholesterol (TC), height, BMI, history of heart failure, hypertension, established peripheral artery disease, as well as lifestyle habits (smoking, exercise and drinking) and medication history, which were retrieved from the electronic recording report.

Definition of dHF

The definition of diabetes‐related heart failure (dHF) risks was based on TRS‐HFDM scores from the previous guideline. 14 Participants who met one or more of the following criteria: [1] Prior heart failure history (pHF: Yes = 2, No = 0); [2] Coronary artery disease (CAD: Yes = 1, No = 0); [3] eGFR [<60 mL/(min × 1.73m2) =1, >60 mL/(min × 1.73m2) =0]; [4] Urine albumin/creatinine ratio (UAR: >300 mg/g = 2, 30–300 mg/g = 1, <30 mg/g = 0). Consequently, dHFs were defined as TRS‐HFDM score larger than 1. TRS−HFDM=pHF+CAD+eGFR+UARscore eGFR(MDRD)=186×Scr−1.154×Age−0.203Female×0.742

TyG‐related indices measurements

There were four TyG‐related indices of interest measured in the present study. The TyG index was measured by the peripheral blood test [TG, and fasting blood glucose (FBG)] of the participants according to the previous literature. Specifically, the indices of TyG, TyG‐BMI, TyG‐Age, and TyG‐eGFR were calculated based on the following equations: (1) TyG=lnTGmg/dL×FBGmg/dL/2 (2) TyG−BMI=lnTGmg/dL×FBGmg/dL/2×eGFRkg/m2 (3) TyG−Age=lnTGmg/dL×FBGmg/dL/2×Age(Year) (4) TyG−eGFR=lnTGmg/dL×FBGmg/dL/2×eGFRmin×1.73m2 The dHF population was classified into four groups by the quartiles of the TyG index, TyG‐BMI index, TyG‐Age index, and TyG‐eGFR index, respectively, and the group at the 1st quartile was set as the reference. Moreover, in the HF risk‐specific derivation cohort, demographic characteristic‐based adjustment was established and the TyG‐related indices 1st quartile was set as the reference.

Outcome measurements

The primary outcome of this study was the dHF‐relevant mortality of the diabetes population. The secondary outcome included diabetes‐caused death and hypertension‐specific mortality in the diabetes population. The mortality data for the follow‐up population were obtained from the NHANES telephone calls or interview public‐use linked mortality files, which were correlated with NCHS and the National Death Index (NDI) through a probability matching algorithm. The ICD‐10 (International Statistical Classification of Diseases, 10th revision) was used to check the causes of mortality. The period of follow‐up was calculated from the date when the interview or telephone calls were initially taken to either the date of the patient's death or December 31, 2019.

Statistical analysis

According to the analytic guideline of the NHANES database (https://wwwn.cdc.gov/nchs/nhanes/tutorials/weighting.aspx), complex sampling designs and sampling weights from U.S. civilian noninstitutionalized population were taken into consideration in our investigation. The non‐normally distributed variables were presented as median and interquartile range (IQR). The Kolmogorov–Smirnov test was used to check the normality assumption distribution of each variable. Categorical variables were expressed as frequencies and percentages (percentage, %). Differences in continuous variables were assessed using analysis of variance (anova) or the Kruskal–Wallis H test, whereas differences in categorical variables were evaluated by using the chi‐square test. We compared the baseline characteristics between three TRS‐HFDM definite dHF groups (High risk, Moderate risk, and Low risk) of patients with diabetes. In addition, to eliminate bias caused by sample size, flexible Standardized Mean Difference (SMD) was utilized to determine the stratified cohort's key features with a set cut‐off of >0.3. We also compared the TyG‐related indices score level in different dHF groups.

To explore the differences in event‐caused survival among the different TyG‐related indices groups in diabetes participants with HF risk, Kaplan–Meier (KM) survival analyses were performed with different quartiles of the TyG‐related indices. In this study, the time‐to‐event was defined as the follow‐up duration in person‐months, based on the survival status (deceased or alive) recorded in the NHANES repository. Univariate and multivariable Cox proportional hazard models were used to estimate the association of the TyG‐related indices with mortality rates of the three outcomes, including all‐cause, HF risk as well as hypertension in the diabetes population. Variables that displayed notable differences among the TRS‐HFDM stratification, along with those of significant clinical relevance, were chosen as potential covariates for inclusion in present multivariable Cox regression analysis. Specifically, model 1 served as the unadjusted analysis. The model 2 adjusted for model 1 variables plus baseline demographic characteristics, including gender, hypercholesterolemia, and race, and model 3 adjusted for model 2 variables plus part of life condition, including PIR, education, and marital status. In the fully adjusted model 4, we accounted for model 3 plus personal lifestyle characteristics, namely smoking, liquor, glycemic control, sitting time, and sleeping time. Missing data were imputed using multiple imputation methods. Furthermore, restricted cubic spline (RCS) transformations with five knots were conducted to flexibly model and visualize the relationship of TyG‐related indices and event‐caused mortality in participants with diabetes. To evaluate predictive performance, we employed the receiver operating characteristic curve (ROC) and calculated the area under the curve (AUC) to evaluate the TyG‐related indices in combination with TRS‐HFDM score in dHF. To enhance the robustness of the current study, we conducted several sensitivity analyses between TyG‐related indices and cardiovascular disorder subgroups mortality in the diabetes population. Subgroup analyses were performed based on various factors, including HF history, CVD, hypercholesterolemia, and individuals with high TC (>200 mg/dL). To assess potential interactions, the regression models included cross‐product terms between the TyG‐related indices and each potential effect modifier, and likelihood ratio tests were used to determine the significance of these interactions.

All statistical analyses of the present study were conducted by using the R software (version 4.3.1, https://www.r‐project.org/). A two‐tailed p‐value <0.05 was considered statistically significant.

The demographic characteristics of diabetes HF risk participants according to TRS‐HFDM stratification

In the groups stratified by TRS‐HFDM score, both HbA1c (7.6) and fasting blood glucose (FBG) (3.47 mmol/L) mean level in high‐risk cohort were significantly higher than the other two groups. Meanwhile, the high‐risk cohort diabetes patients were presented with a longer history of diabetes. In total, the median age of the study population was 62.00 years, median high‐density lipoprotein cholesterol (HDL‐C) was 46.00 mg/dL, 47% were female, and 85.6% of participants had a BMI index higher than 25 kg/m2. The participants' ratio of income to poverty (PIR) in high‐risk (k = 1.59) and moderate‐risk (k = 1.81) are relatively lower than low‐risk (k = 2.20) population. Over 50% of the participants in high‐risk (n = 445) and moderate‐risk (n = 645) population had an educational level below high school. The majority of the adults with dHF were characterized by comorbidities like hypertension [high‐risk, n = 610 (78.7%); moderate‐risk, n = 817 (64.4%)] and hypercholesterolemia [high‐risk, n = 519 (67%); moderate‐risk, n = 765 (60.3%)]. More than 60% of the dHF participants have a liquor history. The median level of the high‐risk cohort TyG index was 9.26 and TyG‐BMI index was 299.4, as well as TyG‐Age and TyG‐eGFR were 610.9 and 815.27, respectively. The non‐low‐risk population presented higher TyG‐related indices when compared with the low‐risk group (Table 1). The TyG‐related indies variables were highly correlated with the increased gradient of TRS‐HFDM score‐based dHF participants (Figure S1).

At the time of the investigation, the correlation between the lifestyle habits change and dHF was significant (Table S1). In horizontal comparison, participants with glycemic control (P < 0.001), exercise (P = 0.574), weight control (P = 0.512), taking diabetes medication (P = 0.574), and retinopathy (P < 0.001) showed a decreased risk gradient in dHF, respectively. While, excessive sitting (P = 0.002), sleeping (P < 0.001), and breathing difficulty (P < 0.001) experienced a higher risk for dHF. Moreover, participants without chest pain, standing difficulty, and with hypertension drugs administration demonstrated a likelihood of a lower risk of HF (P < 0.001, respectively).

Association between TyG‐related indices and TRS‐HFDM in adults with diabetes

Given the positive correlation between TyG‐related indices and TRS‐HFDM scores, these indices may be associated with disease progression and clinical outcomes in patients with dHF. Therefore, we further investigate the incremental prognostic value beyond current usage of TRS‐HFDM, and evaluated its predictive role for mortality risk in patients with dHF. During the follow‐up median period of up to 63 months, 482 deaths occurred, including 153 participants who died from diabetes, 118 died from CVD, and 211 died from other causes. Of note, 48.3% of diabetes‐caused death (n = 74) was accompanied by hypertension‐inducing factors, wherein nearly 42% of deaths were associated with CVD (31/74). Kaplan–Meier (KM) analysis revealed that TyG with high quartile levels showed poor overall survival (OS) probability in diabetes mellitus (DM) participants (P < 0.0001) (Figure 2a). Additionally, high levels of TyG‐Age, TyG‐BMI, or TyG‐eGFR index (all P < 0.001 by log‐rank test) presented with better stratification effects for cause mortality for participants when compared with TyG alone (Figure 2b–d). The higher TyG‐related indices (including TyG‐Age, TyG‐BMI, or TyG‐eGFR) were significantly associated with poor OS among the diabetes population (all P < 0.0001). Consistently, higher TRS‐HFDM score dHF also significantly associated with poor OS in diabetes participants (P < 0.0001; Figure S2).

In the joint analyses, combinations of TyG‐related indices and TRS‐HFDM evaluation have an obviously poor survival stratification for dHF population. The lowest survival probability of TyG in dHF participants reached 0.45 (P < 0.0001; Figure 2e), and low TyG quartile subgroup (25%)‐related survival risk ratio has been better adjusted. Calibration was assessed graphically in the dHF‐specific validation cohort by integrating TyG‐related indices (including Age, BMI and eGFR) with survival outcomes, as well as by calculating the log‐rank statistics (Figure 2f–h). The better discrimination in poor survival outcomes of dHF population was shown by TyG‐Age (Figure 2f) and TyG‐eGFR (Figure 2h; P < 0.0001). In dHF population, we observed that solo glycemic control did not have an obvious impact on TyG‐related indices defined survival outcomes (Figure S3a–d). Regarding increasing exercise in dHF participants, we extracted a small sample size cohort, and results showed a positive effect of exercise on TyG‐Age (P = 0.014) and TyG‐eGFR (P = 0.046)‐related survival outcomes (Figure S3e–h).

Association between TyG‐related indices and cause‐specific mortality of dHF population

The Cox regression analyses showed the association between TyG‐related indices like TyG‐BMI, TyG‐eGFR, and TyG‐Age in diabetes‐related mortality. For the diabetes cohort, a strong gradient of hazard ratio (HR) risk was consistent in both the TyG (index at 4th quartile: HR = 4.21, CI95%: 3.03–5.85) and TyG‐Age (index at 4th quartile: HR = 7.81, CI95%: 5.63–10.82) (Figure 3a). In addition, compared with the diabetes cohort, the dHF population showed a consistent and upward risk ratio in TyG (index at 4th quartile: HR = 7.50, CI95%: 4.55–12.38) and TyG‐Age (index at 4th quartile: HR = 8.13, CI95%: 5.19–12.75) (Figure 3b). The TyG‐eGFR also presented with a significant risk tendency [Q2 (HR = 2.37, 95%CI95%: 1.71–3.29) vs Q3 (HR = 2.70, CI95%: 1.93–3.77) vs Q4 (HR = 4.56, CI95%: 3.43–6.08), P < 0.001] in the dHF cohort. Not surprisingly, a borderline positive correlation was observed between the TyG index and diabetes hypertension mortality with quartile classification, TyG (HR = 4.26, CI95%: 2.91–6.22) and TyG‐Age (HR = 7.58, CI95%: 4.92–11.66) (Figure 3c).

Validation of TyG, TyG‐age, and TyG‐eGFR‐related dHF‐specific mortality risk population

The multivariate‐adjusted Cox regression models were used to assess the correlations between the TyG, TyG‐Age and TyG‐eGFR and outcomes in dHF population. In all four models, the TyG index 4th quartile was associated with a higher incidence of dHF risk‐specific death [Unadjusted (Model 1) HR 7.50, CI95%: 4.55–12.38, P < 0.001; baseline adjusted (Model 2) HR 7.44, CI95%: 4.48–12.36, P < 0.001; inherent quality adjusted (Model 3) HR 7.39, CI95%: 4.45–12.30, P < 0.001; full lifestyle adjusted (Model 4) HR 7.47, CI95%: 4.48–12.46, P < 0.001] (Figure 4a). Meanwhile, the TyG‐Age and TyG‐eGFR as continuous variables were also significantly associated with dHF‐specific mortality, TyG‐Age (index at 4th quartile: Model 1: HR 8.13, CI95%: 5.19–12.75, P < 0.001; Model 2: HR 7.14, CI95%: 4.52–11.28, P < 0.001; Model 3: HR 6.94, CI95%: 4.34–11.08, P < 0.001; Model 4: HR 6.35, CI95%: 3.93–10.24, P < 0.001) (Figure 4b), TyG‐eGFR (index at 4th quartile: Model 1: HR 4.56, CI95%: 3.43–6.08, P < 0.001; Model 2: HR 3.98, CI95%: 2.79–5.66, P < 0.001; Model 3: HR 3.78, CI95%: 2.66–5.37, P < 0.001; Model 4: HR 3.33, CI95%: 2.34–4.73, P < 0.001) (Figure 4c).

The ROC curve analysis demonstrated that the composite score of TyG‐Age (AUC = 0.583, CI95%: 0.559–0.609) displayed superior predictive accuracy in both diabetes and dHF‐specific mortality compared to TyG (AUC = 0.534, CI95%: 0.509–0.558) alone (Figure S4a–f).

The nonlinear trend of TyG‐related indices with mortality outcomes of adults with dHF

The multivariate‐adjusted restricted cubic splines (RCS) plots revealed a linear trend between TyG index and hypertension‐caused mortality of the diabetes population (all P for overall = 0.018) (Figure 5a–c). Furthermore, a nonlinear trend was observed in the relationships between TyG‐Age as well as TyG‐eGFR indices and the diabetes‐caused mortality (P for nonlinearity = 0.001 and 0.003, respectively) (Figure 5d,g). The RCS analysis revealed a J‐shaped nonlinear association between the TyG‐Age index and dHF‐specific mortality in the diabetes population (P for nonlinearity <0.001) (Figure 5e). Additionally, the nonlinear RCS curve between TyG‐Age index and hypertension‐caused mortality initially remained constant and then increased rapidly (all P for overall <0.001) (Figure 5f). The curves trend showed a broad line and significant linear associations between the TyG‐eGFR index and dHF and hypertension‐caused mortality (all P for overall <0.001; Figure 5h,i).

Subgroup and sensitivity analyses

The subgroup analyses revealed a heightened predictive significance of TyG‐related indices in specific demographic groups: individuals with chest pain, Hispanic individuals, TC 200–239, etc (especially notable in the TyG‐Age and TyG‐eGFR indices) (Figures S5–S7). Meanwhile, we conducted a series of sensitivity analyses to check the robustness of the primary findings. First, there was an increasing gradient of TyG‐related indices associated with mortality risk in diabetes participants with HF history (Tables S2–S5). Second, consistent associations between TyG‐related indices and CVD‐caused mortality in the diabetes population were observed (Tables S6–S9). Additionally, the TyG‐related indices showed a similar association with the mortality outcomes of adults with dHF and hypercholesterolemia in diabetes populations (Tables S10–S13). Last, the results were still robust in individuals with the high TC level (>200 mg/dL) in the dHF‐specific mortality risk outcomes (Tables S14–S17).

DISCUSSION

In the current study, we observed and validated an integrated clinical stratification scheme for evaluating dHF as well as cause‐specific mortality in the diabetes population. This simple risk score includes six routinely assessed clinical variables: history of diabetes, history of hypertension, TRS‐HFDM, and TyG‐related indices (Age, eGFR, and BMI). The TyG‐related indices (Age and eGFR) had discrimination in the dHF population, with a ROC index of 0.583–0.656 and >4‐fold gradient of mortality risk in the TRS‐HFDM evaluated dHF participants with DM. Notably, the TyG‐Age index stratification appeared to perform well in TRS‐HFDM determined dHF mortality risk compared with all‐cause or hypertension diabetes populations. Besides, we preliminarily observed the nonlinear association between the TyG‐Age index with all‐cause and dHF‐specific mortality risk in the diabetes population. The results of this analysis indicated that the integration of TyG‐related indices and TRS‐HFDM score scheme may be surrogate prognostic biomarkers for the dHF management, as well as offering a practical strategy to assist clinicians with risk stratification and counseling for dHF patients.

In the CVD, HF represents the end stage of most symptoms. It is well established that HF is a frequent and prognostically important CVD complication of diabetes. 17 The data from statistical studies observed that the development of HF reached 9.03% in the whole subpopulation of diabetes from more than 0.6 million HF patients. 18 Noteworthy, similar with our findings, the dHF in Poland is significantly high after the age of 60 years as well as higher in females. 18 Despite the enormous progress in predicting the incidence and adverse outcomes as well as therapy in the last decades, HF and diabetes prevalence continues to rise dramatically over time. 2 , 19 Thus, identifying more specific and clinically accessible predictors for future events in diabetes patients would be one of the most important achievements in this research field and bring cost‐saving effects on the management of this particular population. In terms of this, several biomarkers have been proposed, validate, and applied in clinical practice for HF in diabetes that rely primarily on single traditional risk factors but lack the ability to overcome complex pathophysiological conditions, 20 such as natriuretic peptides (NPs), C‐reactive protein and electrocardiography. 21 , 22

TyG index, as a simple, readily available, and reliable alternative biomarker for IR, which is also associated with the onset and prognosis of numerous cardiometabolic‐related diseases. 23 Notably, the TyG as well as modified TyG indices have been proved to be accurate in predicting the CVD development and cardiovascular mortality. 24 Meanwhile, among adults with diabetes, Berg et al. 14 proposed TRS‐HFDM as a novel indicator for HF risk prediction in diabetes, with an integer‐based clinical risk score including HF history, coronary artery disease, estimated glomerular filtration rate, and urine albumin‐to‐creatinine ratio. However, the TRS‐HFDM risk score has been only validated and presented with excellent discrimination in two clinical trial cohorts of type 2 diabetes (T2D). 16 , 25 Current evidence suggests that diabetes plays a key role in cardiovascular and renal outcomes. 26 Further complicating the relationship between diabetes and HF is the fact that several energy metabolism alterations and metabolite accumulation, most notably TyG metabolism, have been shown to increase the risk of cardiac dysfunction. 26 Several mechanisms have been proposed to investigate the association between diabetes and heart failure, while the pathophysiology and underlying mechanisms remain incompletely illustrated. 27 , 28 Due to the concerns of cardiovascular safety of diabetes management, 29 evidence‐based combination treatment like lipid‐lowering agents, antihypertensive therapies, and antihyperglycemic treatment strategies will protect against and prevent cardiovascular complications of diabetes according to precise clinical indications. 30 Although the positive correlation between TyG index and CVD have been proposed, 31 , 32 the predictive value and prognostic effect of integrated dHF indicators in un‐symptomatic diabetes population remained unclear. In the current study, we filled part of the research gap and took it further based on one representative nationwide cohort.

Our findings revealed that higher integrated TyG index and TRS‐HFDM evaluation were significantly associated with dHF‐specific mortality risk in diabetes populations, with adjustment for a series of demographic characteristics. The glucose and lipid metabolism disorders reflected in the elevated TyG index may be an early sign of adverse events in cardiovascular events of diabetes. 31 , 33 The mechanisms by which each of these clinical indicator classes achieve their diabetes HF detriment remain unclear; however, multiple mechanistic studies have been devoted to exploring the onset of cardiovascular complications of diabetes. 34 , 35 Regardless, the distinct outcomes for each of the diabetes‐specific classifications highlight the need for an integrated TyG index and TRS‐HFDM stratification scheme to optimally evaluate dHF. Therefore, incorporating the TyG index into TRS‐HFDM score protocols may facilitate the identification of individuals with dHF. However, the moderate predictive accuracy of the TyG index prompted us to investigate additional adjusted TyG‐related indices for better predicting the HF risk outcomes of adults with diabetes. In terms of this, strong evidence has demonstrated that Age and eGFR were simple and useful indicators to reflect the cardiovascular disorder and were significantly associated with CVD‐related mortality. 18 , 36 Noteworthy, TyG‐related indices (like TyG‐WC and TyG‐BMI) have exhibited preferable predictive accuracy than the original TyG index in identifying the occurrence and mortality risks in cardiometabolic‐related diseases. 37 , 38 In our study, the TyG‐Age index was observed to be more closely associated with poor outcomes of the dHF population when compared with the original TyG index, which indicated the optimal role of integrating TyG‐related indices with TRS‐HFDM score in assessing HF risk in diabetes. Our analysis confirmed that the increased dHF mortality risk rate gradient in TyG‐Age is consistent regardless of baseline risk. Consistently, higher TyG‐Age is associated with metabolic disorder symptoms, such as hypertension, hypercholesterolemia, and CAD, which can directly or indirectly contribute to the progression of dHF. Moreover, certain therapeutic interventions have demonstrated effectiveness in ameliorating cardiac remodeling in patients with HF. 39 By using a simple, validated TRS‐HFDM clinical risk stratification scheme and TyG‐related indices for diabetes patients, clinicians can better characterize the dHF profile of their patients and identify those patients who have the greatest absolute reduction in dHF from prophylactic drug therapy.

There are still some restrictions in our investigation. First, the derivation cohort database did not include details of diabetes history or complication data; therefore, future longitudinal investigations are required to obtain causality inferences about the current findings. Second, although we have attempted to search the literature and adjust for potential confounders as much as possible, considering that HF is a composite of a series of complex and multifaceted diseases, there may still exist some unidentified or unmeasured confounders that may also play a role in increasing HF risk in diabetes. Examining the influences of TyG‐related indices on young, otherwise suboptimal healthy individuals who are at risk for HF due to family diabetic history would be beneficial to determine if combined TRS‐HFDM strategy could decrease their predictive bias and thus their poor outcomes. Third, the study population in our study was confined to the US population; whether the findings could be applied to other regions with different races is worth exploring. It will be necessary to conduct future studies incorporating other populations to validate the predictive abilities of TRS‐HFDM in combination with TyG‐related indices in dHF population.

CONCLUSIONS

In conclusion, our findings suggest that elevated levels of TyG‐related indices are correlated with increased risks of all‐cause and dHF‐specific mortality risk in adults with diabetes. Furthermore, compared to the original TyG index, the TyG‐Age index in combination with TRS‐HFDM demonstrates an enhanced prognostic effect on mortality risk outcomes in adults with dHF, making it a simple and easily calculable clinical biomarker for managing diabetes‐related cardiovascular complications. These findings underscore the importance of integrating TyG‐related indices with the TRS‐HFDM stratification scheme in predicting and managing dHF from diabetes. Future well‐designed, longitudinal, prospective studies are warranted to validate our findings.
