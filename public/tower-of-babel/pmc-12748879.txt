Effect of early goal directed mobilization on clinical outcomes in mechanically ventilated ICU patients
Authors: Wanzhu Zhang, Peilin Yu, Mengting Yu, Jie Mi
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractPatients with mechanical ventilation (MV) in the ICU are prone to various complications. It was proved that early mobility was effective, it improve functional status in patients with chronic diseases, and reduce disability. This study aimed to investigate the effects of early goal-directed mobilization (EGDM) on clinical outcomes of MV patients in ICU. Patients with mechanical ventilation in the ICU are generally confined to bed leading to limited mobility that may have detrimental effects on different body systems. EGDM prevents or reduces these effects and improves outcomes in patients following critical illness. The aim of this research is to investigate the effects of EGDM on clinical outcomes of MV patients in ICU. 64 adult patients in the ICU who were undergoing mechanical ventilation assigned into two groups. With one group received conventional early mobilization (admitted to the intensive care unit [ICU] between August 2023 and January 2024), while the other group received EGDM (admitted to the ICU between February 2024 and June 2024). The capacity of mobility (Medical Research Council (MRC)), length of ICU stay, duration of mechanical ventilation, complication, and mortality at ICU discharge. The capacity of mobility was increased (MRC 37.67 ± 10.00 in EGDM vs. 29.67 ± 8.84 in EM, p < 0.001), the duration of mechanical ventilation and hospital stay is shorter Mechanical ventilation time 6.19 ± 3.03 in EGDM vs. 9.64 ± 3.05 in EM, p < 0.001; ICU stay 310.44 ± 3.92 in EGDM vs. 15.47 ± 4.38 in EM, p < 0.001). The implementation of EGDM in the ICU improved mobilization, increased muscle strength, and shortened the duration of hospital stay and mechanical ventilation. Introduction

The number of intensive care unit (ICU) survivors has been increasing annually1. However, long-term bedridden ICU patients, especially those who have been mechanically ventilated (MV), are at an increased risk of developing various complications2, including ICU-acquired weakness (ICU-AW), muscle atrophy, and aspiration pneumonia, which significantly diminishes both treatment adherence and patient comfort3. These complications can diminish treatment adherence, patient comfort, and lead to prolonged hospitalization, increased mortality, and significant impairments in both short-term and long-term functional ability, thereby elevating healthcare costs and social burdens4.

Recently, early goal-directed mobilization (EGDM) in the intensive care unit (ICU) has been recommended to prevent or reduce physical dysfunction. Several guidelines and expert concencus support the clinical benefits of EGDM in ICU settings5–8. Randomized controlled trials have demonstrated that EGDM significantly enhance functional recovery and reduce ICU-acquired weakness in critically ill patients9,10. Since the 2000s, EGDM has been increasingly applied in clinical practice. Previous studies have reported that EGDM exerts various beneficial effects, including improving the long-term prognosis of patients11, decreasing the occurrence of complications, shortening the duration of mechanical ventilation and hospitalization in ICU12, and mitigating delirium level and frequency. Lord et al. suggested that initiating EGDM after the stabilization of the vital signs of ICU patients may enhance the tolerance of patients and promote the safety of their activities13. Additionally, Meyer et al. highlighted the potential of EGDM in enhancing the mobility of ICU patients14. Yu Ping et al. implemented the EGDM program and successfully improved the awareness of self-care among patients, boosted their confidence in activities, and significantly enhanced their self-care abilities, facilitating their rehabilitation. This finding is consistent with the conclusions of other domestic studies15.

Despite this established evidence, a critical gap remains in the standardized application and protocolization of EGDM, particularly for mechanically ventilated patients. While tools like the ICU Mobility Scale (IMS) have been developed to objectively assess and guide mobility levels16–18. The practical integration of such scales into a structured EGDM protocol, and its specific impact on key clinical outcomes like muscle strength, duration of mechanical ventilation, and ICU length of stay in this specific population, requires further empirical investigation. Many studies affirm the benefits of EGDM, but there is a need for more research quantifying its effects on these precise metrics within the context of daily clinical practice. This study aimed to investigate the effect of a structured EGDM protocol, on specific clinical outcomes including muscle strength, duration of mechanical ventilation, and ICU length of stay in mechanically ventilated ICU patients. We hypothesized that the EGDM protocol would lead to significant improvements in these outcomes compared to conventional early mobilization.

Methods

Study design

This quasi-experimental study was conducted in a mixed medical-surgical intensive care unit (ICU) at the First Affiliated Hospital of Chongqing Medical University. Informed written consent was obtained from all participants or their responsible family member. Ethical approval was obtained from the Ethics Committee of the First Afliated Hospital of Chongqing Medical University (Document No.2023 − 221).

Allocation and blinding

The assessors were blinded, In the data provided to the assessors, variables such as date information and group labels that might expose patient information were removed or masked. A standardized data collection form was used, which only displayed unique identifiers for each patient, ensuring that the outcome assessors responsible for measuring the primary and secondary outcomes remained blinded to the group allocation throughout the data analysis phase. Clinical staff delivering the interventions were not blinded due to the nature of the therapy.

Setting and sample

This prospective study enrolled patients admitted to the Critical Care ICU between August 1, 2023, and June 30, 2024. Patients admitted from August 1, 2023, to January 31, 2024, received conventional early mobilization (EM), while those admitted from February 1, 2024, to June 30, 2024, were treated using the early goal-directed mobilization (EGDM) approach. Data were collected prospectively from electronic medical records to compare outcomes between the two groups. We have carefully considered these background information, the study systematically collected extensive baseline patient characteristic data, including demographic information, clinical features, and other variables that might be related to both time and outcome.

Between August 1, 2023, and January 31, 2024, 54 patients aged ≥ 18 years were admitted to the ICU and treated based on the conventional EM approach. Meanwhile, 48 patients aged ≥ 18 years were admitted to the ICU between February 1, 2024, and June 30, 2024, and treated based on the EGDM approach. The inclusion criteria were as follows: (a) patients receiving intensive care for at least 48 h after ICU admission; (b) patients aged ≥ 18 years; (c) patients receiving invasive or non-invasive mechanical ventilation; (d) patients with stable hemodynamics; (e) patients without severe visual or hearing impairment; (f) patients who are conscious; (g) among patients who were admitted to the ICU multiple times during the study, only the first admission was considered.

Study procedure

In this study, patients who met the inclusion criteria were assigned to either the EGDM group or the EM group based on the timing of their ICU admission. Patients admitted during the first phase were assigned to the EM group, while those admitted during the second phase were assigned to the EGDM group. There be no difference between the patient admitted in the morning and the patient admitted in the evening. A standardized data collection form was used, which only displayed unique identifiers for each patient, ensuring that the assessors remained blinded to the group allocation. The research team, responsible for intervention coordination and protocol adherence, included ICU-trained nurses, doctors, and physiotherapists. The multidisciplinary team underwent comprehensive training on the respective protocols (EM or EGDM) prior to the start of each study phase. Early mobility interventions involve the following five essential stages: sitting up in bed, sitting at the bedside, standing, transitioning from the bed to a chair, and ambulating19. The EM group received conventional treatment performed by physiotherapists, movement was initiated upon waking, including routine interventions such as positional changes, passive range of motion exercises, cycling, and physiotherapy (e.g., bed mobility, transfer training, limb flexion and balance training). The start time and level of mobility for each patient were decided by physiotherapists, patients were shifted from passive mobilization to active mobilization. The EGDM group received the same level of clinical care as EM except for the goal-oriented mobility program, the patient was guided by the IMS scale. The IMS assesses the highest level of mobility attainable by the patient, serving as a benchmark to direct and encourage the patient to accomplish early daily mobilities and establish the maximum threshold to enhance the prognosis of patients. The study used the Sinicized IMS scale to evaluate the highest level of mobility achievable by patients. The IMS achieving weighted Kappa and criterion validity values of 0.84 and 0.872, and these data have been published. Which indicated good reliability and internal consistency. Table 1 shows the detailed protocols of the IMS applied in this study. The highest achievable activity level of each patient was assessed, and individualized activity goals were established based on the patient’s tolerance. Each morning, the multidisciplinary team, led by the charge nurse, assessed the patients and set a target activity level for the day. The daily activity goals are formulated based on the patients’ consciousness state and muscle strength level, Te intensive activity level included exercise and posture changes: level 1 (passive activity), level 2 (sitting in bed, responsive to instruction and muscle strength ≤ 2), level 3 (sitting in chair, responsive to instruction and muscle strength ≥ 3), level 4 (standing), and level 5 (ambulation). The treatment plan was dynamically modified based on the patient’s response and the preset activity goal, moving beyond just scoring to active, targeted efforts to achieve the goal. The activities were structured according to predefined levels (Table 2), which detailed the personnel, content, and duration for each mobility level, as established by an expert panel based on existing guidelines and consensus7,8. To ensure the achievement of the goal, the clinical team determines the progress and obstacles of implementation through multi-disciplinary closed-loop communication. The clinical team modifies the treatment plan based on the proposed activity goals or the patients’ responses to the activities, in accordance with the opinions received during the professional discussion process, until the goals are achieved. During the EGDM process, the patient’s condition should be closely observed. The patient’s heart rate, respiratory rate, blood pressure, SpO2, sweating, and other discomforts should be closely monitored. If the patient is intolerant, the training should be stopped promptly and the patient adjusted to a comfortable and tolerant position to allow sufficient rest. After the next assessment of safety, the training can be resumed. The criteria for initiation and continuation of the EGDM program follow the safety indexdescribed in the expert consensus, and some points wereas follows: heart rate (HR) between 60 and 120 beats per minute, no use or increased dose ofvascular drugs, mean arterial pressure (MAP) between 65and 120 mmHg, respiratory rate (RR) ≤ 30 breaths per minute, noarrhythmias or acute ischemia, no active bleeding, and no prescription of bed rest. The respiratory oxygen inspiratory fraction (FiO2) ≤ 60% and positive endexpiratory pressure (PEEP) ≤ 10 cmH2O with mechanical ventilation. Adverse eventswere recorded during early mobility including unplanned extubation, falls, unbearable pain postural hypotension, arrhythmia or any other factors that cause changes in the condition.

Outcomes

The primary outcome was muscle strength, assessed using the Medical Research Council (MRC) sum score20,21,22. Twelve muscle groups (bilaterally: shoulder abduction, elbow flexion, wrist extension, hip flexion, knee extension, ankle dorsiflexion) were evaluated on a 0–5 scale, resulting in a total sum score ranging from 0 (quadriplegic) to 60 (normal strength). The MRC sum score was assessed by a trained, blinded assessor within 24 h of ICU discharge or successful extubation. Following the intervention, all patients were assessed after maintaining a resting state for at least 30 min to minimize potential confounding factors. The MRC score categorizes muscle strength as follows: level 0, no muscle strength; level 1, weak muscle strength; level 2, poor muscle strength; level 3, moderate muscle strength; level 4, good muscle strength; level 5, physiological muscle strength21,22. The secondary outcome including the patient’s ICU length of stay(the number of days from the first admission to the ICU until the day of discharge from the ICU, the first day of admission to the ICU was considered day 1), Mechanical ventilation duration(the duration of invasive or non-invasive mechanical ventilation).

Sample size

The sample size calculation was based on detecting a clinically significant difference in the primary outcome, the MRC sum score, between groups23. σ is the standard deviation of the sample, and δ is the difference between the means of the two samples. If the test level α = 0.05 and the error probability of type II β = 0.10, then µα/2 = 1.96 and µβ = 1.28. In the cited literature, the mean difference δ of the samples with impaired muscle strength is 3.2, and σ is the calculated standard deviation S = 4.0, the sample size was calculated as follows: N1 = N2 = 2 × (1.96 + 1.28) × (4.0/3.2) × 2 ≈ 33. Considering the possibility of sample shedding, the sample size is increased by 10%. ((33 + 33) × (1 + 10%) = 72). Among them, n = 72 cases reached the target and were included in the primary analysis.

Data analysis

In this study, Excel and SPSS 27.0 were used for data entry, sorting, and statistical analysis. Continuous data are represented as mean ± standard deviation (x̄ ± s). Means between groups were compared using the t-test, confirmed by the Shapiro-Wilk test; otherwise, the Mann-Whitney U test was used. Categorical data are represented as frequency (%) and compared using the χ2 test. Differences were considered significant at P < 0.05. All statistical tests were performed using two-sided tests with an α level of 0.05. The normality assumption was tested. In addition to the independent sample t-test and chi-square test, we applied a pre-defined significance threshold (α = 0.05) for the primary outcome, while Bonferroni correction was used for the secondary outcome.

Primary outcome analysis

All evaluations were completed within the specified time. The MRC scores in the EGDM group were markedly higher than those in the EM group (P < 0.05) (Table 4). Indicating a notable improvement in muscle strength among patients receiving early goal-directed mobilization. Specifically, the mean MRC score in the EGDM group was 37.67 ± 10.00 compared to 29.67 ± 8.84 in the EM group, including shoulder abduction, elbow flexion, and knee extension, as assessed by the MRC scale. A slight increase in muscle strength may translate into an expansion of limb range of motion or an improvement in movement coordination. The findings suggest that EGDM effectively enhances muscle strength in ICU patients, potentially mitigating the risk of ICU-acquired weakness and supporting faster functional recovery.

Discussion

Patients in the ICU often present with severe conditions that necessitate careful management of mobility interventions. Early mobilization (EM) has been widely reported as a safe and practical approach for critically ill patients, and our findings further support this notion. In this study, the patient’s muscle strength has improved, which facilitates the patient’s further mobility. The length of hospital stay and the duration of mechanical ventilation have been shortened, thereby improving the patient’s prognosis. Additionally, no adverse events, such as accidental falls, postural hypotension, or unplanned extubation, were observed in either the EM or EGDM groups. This aligns with previous studies, which have demonstrated the safety of early mobilization in ICU settings when implemented with appropriate monitoring and protocols24,25. While some patients exhibited transient changes in respiration, blood pressure, or heart rate during initial out-of-bed activities or the use of assistive devices like in-bed cycling, these changes were clinically manageable and did not result in detrimental effects. Similar findings have been reported in other studies, where early mobilization was shown to be well-tolerated by ICU patients, even those with severe conditions26. The absence of serious adverse events in our study reinforces the safety profile of EGDM and supports its feasibility in clinical practice. However, due to the small sample size of this study and the low incidence of adverse events, a larger sample is needed in the future to test its safety.

The implementation of EGDM not only reduced the risk of mobility-related complications but also contributed to improved patient outcomes, including enhanced muscle strength, reduced mechanical ventilation duration, and shorter ICU length of stay. These benefits are consistent with the growing body of evidence highlighting the positive impact of goal-directed mobilization on patient recovery and resource utilization27,28. For instance, a study by Schaller found that structured early mobilization programs significantly reduced ICU stay and improved functional outcomes in critically ill patients. However, during the implementation of EGDM, we also encountered some challenges. The implementation of the EGDM was contingent upon daily multidisciplinary coordination for assessment and goal-setting, a process that demanded considerable time and consistent communication, variations in staff availability and expertise may affected the patient’s early mobility quality. And the requirement for coordinated scheduling, communication, and goal-setting, substantially increased the workload and pressure on nurses. Furthermore, patient-related factors, such as fatigue and limited cooperation, posed additional challenges to adherence to prescribed mobility levels.

In conclusion, our findings suggest that within a relatively small and specific group of people, EGDM is a safe and effective intervention for ICU patients, mitigating complications and optimizing recovery, compared with those in the EM group, muscle strength was significantly higher, and mechanical ventilation and hospital stay durations were significantly lower in the EGDM group (P < 0.05). These findings indicate that EGDM promotes the overall recovery of patients. However, several limitations must be considered. The quasi-experimental design, with allocation based on admission time period, is a primary limitation. While we collected extensive baseline data and found no significant differences, this design choice introduces a potential risk of temporal bias and unmeasured confounding compared to a randomized controlled trial. Furthermore, the single-center nature of the study may limit the generalizability of the findings. Future research should involve multi-center randomized controlled trials to confirm these results and include long-term follow-up to assess the impact of EGDM on functional status and quality of life post-ICU discharge.

Conclusions

In summary, some positive trends in patient outcomes were observed, it suggest that EGDM may be a feasible approach for mechanically ventilated patients in the ICU, though further research is needed to confirm its safety and efficacy, the results contribute to the evidence supporting the potential benefits of EGDM in critically ill, mechanically ventilated patients. Future research should involve multi-center randomized controlled trials to confirm efficacy and more rigorously evaluate safety. Early implementation of EGDM may warrant consideration, but its benefits and risks should be evaluated through larger, rigorous studies before definitive recommendations can be made.

Limitations

This study was conducted at a single center, uses a non-randomized, time-based allocation, which introduces a potential for temporal bias. And his study did not address the quality of life post-discharge after the initiation of EGDM. Thus, multi-center studies should be carried out and long-term follow-ups conducted in the future.
