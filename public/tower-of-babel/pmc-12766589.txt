The Lancet Regional Health—Southeast Asia: 2025 year in review
Authors: Shalini Garg, Bhavani Nivetha, Apoorva Dharmendra
Published: 2025

The close of the year brings about a need for pause and reflection. 2025 marks the fourth year since the inception of The Lancet Regional Health Southeast Asia, and we have strived to uphold our original aim of providing a voice to all the diverse stakeholders of the WHO Southeast Asia region—including researchers, clinicians, and policy makers. We remain committed to strengthening the health research ecosystem and to publishing robust, impactful evidence that represents the people and health concerns of the region.

Our editorials have been notably varied this year, with topics including oral health, road traffic accidents, drowning prevention, and measles elimination. Perhaps of most importance are the editorials highlighting injustice in the areas of disability inclusion, road traffic accidents, and HIV response, topics that have not been given sufficient priority. The platform has also highlighted important social concerns, particularly for groups whose voices would otherwise go unheard. The February editorial addressed the endemic violence against children in the region, in all its forms. The journal has been a strong platform for under-represented geographies such as Timor-Leste and Bhutan, and for marginalised population groups including LGBTQ+ communities, people living with disabilities, and individuals with mental health conditions—highlighting legal and social barriers to health access and the need for systemic change. Our January issue emphasised the urgent need to embed disability and queer health in medical education systems, aligning with our ongoing commitment to health equity for all people in the region.

This commitment to representation is seen in the opinion pieces authored by experts, which have delivered timely insights into social dimensions of health issues. This December issue drives advocacy on adolescent pregnancy in the region, a previously under-recognised public health priority with policy relevance. These pieces provide an opportunity to discuss overlooked yet important topics, such as caste-based social discrimination against mortuary workers in Bangladesh and linguistic inclusivity in health care.

This year, the journal highlighted the need for policy change on key regional issues such as neonatal care and preconception nutrition. Our series, in collaboration with The Lancet Global Health, drew attention to the urgent need for primary care reform in the region—a low hanging fruit with the potential to positively impact wellbeing across the region. Our August editorial, on the gaps in universal health coverage in the region, was topical and offered an important alternate perspective for media reporting. Further, we examined systemic issues in mental health services and primary health-care financing and provided a platform for neglected tropical diseases such as leprosy, leishmaniasis, and melioidosis.

Recognising the significant burden of non-communicable diseases (NCDs) in the region, the journal focused on research that influences NCD prevention and explored its complexity, as discussed in the November editorial highlighting the unique nature of diabetes in the region. The journal covered topics such as food retail policy, food labelling, and ultra-processed food consumption. Additionally, we have published novel, regionally relevant evidence, like identification of protein targets for dyslipidaemia and cardiovascular diseases among south Asian populations and a family-led care model for diabetes management.

Mental health remains an important topic for the journal. A major theme this year has been child and adolescent mental health. We explored topics such as prevalence and determinants of adolescent mental health, exam-related suicides, and search engine ads for suicide prevention. The need for community engagement in mental health was highlighted in research detailing determinants of psychiatric institutionalisation and via a trial involving community health workers in the management of psychosis.

Although we acknowledge the intrinsic value of regionally sourced evidence, a few papers have, by virtue of their subject matter, gone further, highlighting broader issues affecting the region. For example, this issue's editorial addresses the recent deaths of children in India from contaminated medicines and the regulatory weaknesses in pharmaceutical industry. A paper published in June this year discussed a preventable neuromelioidosis outbreak in Tamil Nadu, India, with a high case-fatality rate, drawing media attention to this important concern. Our feature discussing the ubiquity and risks of look-alike, sound-alike (LASA) drugs in the Indian health-care landscape led to a government crackdown on drug nomenclature and prompted policy debates on naming standards.

As we enter 2026, we reaffirm our dedication to regionally relevant, inclusive reporting. Artificial intelligence (AI) has made 2025 pivotal, with all spheres of work being disrupted by this new technology. We envision that health in the region will move forward with AI or risk falling behind. We will seek research in this space, including on AI's implications for regional health systems. We will also place continued emphasis on oncology, surgery, neurology, and paediatrics, while mental health and maternal and child health will remain our priorities in 2026. Given the region's density and diversity, representing its population is essential. In the coming years, we will aim to showcase publications from under-represented countries and under-researched topics in the region to broaden regional perspectives. We head into the new year confident that The Lancet Regional Health—Southeast Asia will continue to publish rigorous, unbiased research from the region and further our mission of being a regional voice that brings out the best science for better lives.
