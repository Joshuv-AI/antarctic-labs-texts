Discovery of basaltic and magnesian andesite magmas within the Hunga volcanic system, Tonga arc
Authors: Yoshihiko Tamura, Tomoki Sato, Richard Wysoczanski, Kevin Mackay, Iona McIntosh, Qing Chang, Takashi Miyazaki, Bogdan Vaglarov, Yasuhiro Hirai
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
The climactic explosive eruption of Hunga volcano in January 2022 destroyed a significant portion of the Hunga Tonga-Hunga Ha’apai islands, deepening the caldera floor by over 600 m and depositing 3.5 km3 of material onto its submarine flanks. In April–May 2022, just three months after the event, we conducted a rapid-response marine survey. Through hard-rock dredging across the submerged edifice, we recovered a comprehensive sample suite to investigate the geochemical evolution of the Hunga magma system. The dredged rocks span a compositional range from basalt to rhyolite, categorized into four groups: basalt–basaltic andesite (50.9–53.2 wt%), magnesian andesite (53.6–55.7 wt%), andesite–dacite (54.3–64.8 wt%), and rhyolite (70.8–73.2 wt%). A distinct 5 wt% gap exists between the dacites and rhyolites. While previously reported subaerial andesites largely overlap with the andesite–dacite group, our findings reveal a broader compositional diversity. These new data suggest the co-existence of both primary basaltic and primary andesitic magmas, offering new insights into the plumbing system of the 2022 eruption and similar arc volcanoes.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-026-59118-1.

Introduction

The violent 2022 eruption of the mostly submarine Hunga volcano in the Kingdom of Tonga had impacts around the globe. Following minor precursory eruptions that began on 20 December 2021, on 15 January 2022 a large-scale eruption occurred that sent an eruption plume into the stratosphere, triggering atmospheric shockwaves and tsunami activity in all ocean basins, and delivered volcanoclastic density currents down the volcano’s submarine flanks that severed critical submarine telecommunications cables1.

At sea level, the presence of the Hunga volcano is indicated only by two minor islands, but these islands mark the rim of a central caldera atop a > 1500 m conical volcanic edifice. With geological sampling prior to the 2022 eruption restricted to only the subaerial island deposits by the expense and logistical difficulties of conducting marine surveys, the vast majority of the Hunga volcano remained unexplored and unsampled, severely limiting the understanding of its genesis and eruption record. In many ways, Hunga volcano is thus typical of the numerous marine volcanoes located in oceanic arcs like the Tonga-Kermadec and Izu-Ogasawara-Mariana. The violence and destructive impacts of the 2022 Hunga eruption have catalysed research efforts to understand Hunga volcano’s eruption processes, the generation and evolution of its magmatic system, and the degree to which these factors – and their associated hazards – may be replicated at other ocean arc volcanoes.

In this context, the National Institute of Water and Atmospheric Research (NIWA) in New Zealand and the Japan Agency for Marine-Earth Science and Technology (JAMSTEC) collaborated to conduct a rapid response marine survey of Hunga volcano in April–May 2022, just 3 months after the climactic eruption. By conducting hard-rock dredges across the submerged edifice, the survey aimed to obtain a comprehensive sample suite to investigate the geochemical evolution of the Hunga magma system.

Geological setting and sample collection

The Tonga–Kermadec subduction system in the southwestern Pacific extends for 2,550 km between Tonga and New Zealand, and is one of the clearest examples of an intraoceanic convergent plate margin on the modern Earth2. The northern system, where Hunga volcano is located (Fig. 1a), includes the very rapid back-arc extension of Lau Basin, which is opening at a rate that increases northwards to a maximum of ~ 160 mm yr-1. The convergence rates of the Pacific Plate across the Tonga trench increase northwards to a maximum of ~ 240 mm yr-1, as previously reported by Bevis et al.3. The GPS station at Tongatapu, the closest station to Hunga volcano, indicates that the local opening rate of the Lau Basin and the convergence rate of the Pacific Plate are approximately 91 and 164 mm yr-1, respectively3. Hunga volcano (20.5532°S, 175.3841°W) is a mostly submarine volcano situated 68 km NNW of Nuku`alofa, the capital city of the Kingdom of Tonga located on Tongatapu Island. The cone-shaped edifice is topped by a central caldera ~ 6 km in diameter. Prior to the climactic 2022 eruption, portions of this caldera rim formed shallow reefs and the emergent uninhabited islands of Hunga Tonga and Hunga Ha’apai and were the locations for historically observed eruptions in 1912, 1937, 1988, 2009, and 2014/154–6. Following a field survey conducted in 2015, the detailed stratigraphic and geochemical investigations of the deposits exposed on these ~ 2 km long, ~ 100 m high islands6,7 provide the majority of what is known about Hunga volcano’s eruptive record prior to the eruptive activity of 2021/22. The Hunga Tonga and Hunga Ha’apai islands are the remnants of a volcanic cone destroyed by at least two caldera-forming eruptions, the latest of which occurred in 1040–1180 CE and left an ignimbrite deposit on the islands and ashfall on Tongatapu5. Geochemical analysis of the subaerially exposed lava and pyroclastic deposits revealed a narrow compositional range of basaltic andesite to andesite7. The climactic explosive eruption in January 2022 destroyed much of these islands and deepened the caldera floor by over 600 m, depositing 3.5 km3 of material onto the submarine flanks8. However, the overall shape of the Hunga volcano edifice, which rises from a water depth of > 1,500 m below sea level (mbsl), remains almost unchanged.

Research cruise TAN2206 of R/V Tangaroa was conducted in April–May 2022, to conduct hard-rock sampling across the previously unexplored submarine edifice that comprises the vast majority of the Hunga volcano. Volcanic rocks were collected by dredge hauls at nine sites around the main Hunga edifice in water depths of 320 to 1,375 mbsl, and also at two sites located on an unnamed submarine cone ~ 39 km SSW of the Hunga Tonga and Hunga Ha’apai islands in water depths of 120 to 460 mbsl (dredge locations shown in Table 1 and Fig. 1b). While it remains uncertain whether the dredged material includes products from the 2022 eruption, the potential inclusion of such recent material would be advantageous for our study. It would allow us to integrate the most recent magmatic products into our comprehensive geochemical investigation of the pre-existing edifice, providing a more complete evolutionary history of the Hunga volcano.

Results

Dredged samples were fresh and covered by little or no manganese oxides. Most of them are dense lava blocks to support that most of them were in situ lavas, rather than pumice that could come from anywhere. Representative samples were selected from each dredge and analysed for their geochemistry. In total, 87 samples were processed for whole-rock geochemistry, of which 20 were selected for trace element analysis; 10 of these were further analysed for isotopic composition (see Methods section for analytical details). These new data for the submarine Hunga volcanic edifice are presented together with previously reported whole-rock geochemical data of the subaerial volcanic products (lavas and pyroclasts) of the Hunga Tonga-Hunga Ha’apai islands7 and K2O contents, trace elements and isotope compositions of the volcanic islands of Tofua9 and Fonualei10 located to the north of Hunga in the Tonga arc Fig. 1a.

New whole-rock major and trace element geochemistry and isotopic composition data for Hunga volcano expand the known range of its magma compositions and suggest the presence of mantle-derived bimodal magmatism involving distinct basalt and andesite primary melts.

The dredged rocks span a compositional range from basalt to rhyolite. Most andesite lavas have Mg-number values (atomic ratio of 100 Mg/(Mg + ∑Fe)) > 30 and less than 10 wt% MgO, which are high Mg# andesites similar to the bulk continental crust defined by Kelemen12. Here, andesites having Mg-number values higher than 50 are further classified as magnesian andesites. Accordingly, we identify four compositional groups for the dredged Hunga samples: basalt–basaltic andesite (50.9–53.2 wt % SiO2), magnesian andesite (53.6–55.7 wt % SiO2), andesite–dacite (54.3–64.8 wt% SiO2), and rhyolite (70.8–73.2 wt % SiO2), with a distinct SiO2 gap of 5 wt % between the dacites and rhyolites (Fig. 2). These compositional groups are used in subsequent figures and discussion. Table 1 shows the compositions recovered in each dredge.

Basalts and basaltic andesites were only recovered in dredge H08, located on the SE flank of the main Hunga edifice (Fig. 1b). The rhyolite samples are all pumice, which can be easily transported after eruption due to its low density, raising the possibility that they originated from other volcanoes in the region. Given this uncertainty, they are not discussed further in this study.

In the Harker diagrams (Fig. 2), the magnesian andesites and andesite–dacites define continuous geochemical trends; specifically, CaO, MgO, and Mg-number values decrease, while TiO2, Na2O, and K2O increase with increasing SiO2 from magnesian andesite to andesite–dacite. These variations are consistent with internal fractional crystallization within this suite, as Zr/Y ratios remain nearly constant. In contrast, the basalt to basaltic andesite samples are characterized by low MgO (3–4 wt%) and Mg-number values (32–41), forming a distinct group that is clearly offset from the magnesian andesite–dacite trends. This lack of continuity indicates that the two suites are not related by a single liquid line of descent.

Hunga basalts have light REE (LREE; La, Ce, Pr, and Nd) depleted patterns without negative Eu anomalies and have lower La/Sm than Hunga magnesian andesites and andesite-dacites. In contrast, the magnesian andesite and andesite–dacite patterns are less LREE-depleted and mutually parallel with values increasing from magnesian andesite to andesite-dacite (Fig. 3a). The MORB-normalized incompatible element patterns (Fig. 3b) show that all Hunga compositions exhibit the typical signature of subduction zones, being enriched in elements mobile in aqueous fluids and sediment melts (e.g. Ba, U, K, and Pb), and all have significant negative Nb and Ta anomalies. Once again, patterns for magnesian andesites and andesite-dacites are mutually parallel with values increasing from magnesian andesite to andesite-dacite. Interestingly, despite the more differentiated nature of the basalts (evidenced by low Mg-number values and low wt% MgO; Fig. 2) compared to the magnesian andesites, basalts have similar heavy REE (HREE; Ho, Er, Tm, Yb, and Lu) patterns to magnesian andesites, and have lower incompatible elements such as Ba, Th, Nb, Ta, and light REEs than magnesian andesites (Fig. 3b).

Comparing Hunga to the neighboring Tofua and Fonualei volcanoes reveals distinct isotopic characteristics. The 87Sr/86Sr ratios of Tofua are comparable to those of the Hunga basalts, whereas Fonualei exhibits higher 87Sr/86Sr than any of the Hunga compositions. Both Tofua and Fonualei display lower 143Nd/144Nd ratios relative to the entire Hunga suite. Regarding lead isotopes, Fonualei is characterized by more depleted 206Pb/204Pb, 207Pb/204Pb, and 208Pb/204Pb ratios than Hunga. While the 206Pb/204Pb values of Tofua overlap with the Hunga basalts and magnesian andesites, its 207Pb/204Pb and 208Pb/204Pb ratios are lower than those of Hunga at a given 206Pb/204Pb. As shown in Fig. 5, the Pb isotopic compositions of the Hunga lavas define an array sub-parallel to those of Tofua9 and Fonualei10. It is important to note a slight systematic offset arising from differences in analytical protocols: our Pb isotope data are externally normalized to the NIST SRM-981 values of Baker et al.17, whereas the Tofua and Fonualei data were reported as measured values without such external normalization. If our data were adjusted to align with their unnormalized scale—applying shifts of -0.0056, -0.0080, and -0.0372 for 206Pb/204Pb, 207Pb/204Pb, and 208Pb/204Pb, respectively—the Hunga data points would plot directly within the Tofua and Fonualei isotopic fields. This suggests that the observed offset is a primary function of inter-laboratory calibration rather than a distinct mantle source.

Finally, the 176Hf/177Hf ratios of all Hunga samples fall within the range defined for the Tonga group by Turner et al.16.

Discussion

Expanded compositional range of the Hunga edifice

Prior to our marine survey, compositional data for Hunga volcano were restricted to subaerial samples from the small Hunga Tonga-Hunga Ha’apai islands that existed at the summit until their destruction in the 2022 climactic eruption. These earlier samples spanned a narrow range from basaltic andesite to andesite7. Specifically, older pyroclastic units (including the 1040–1180 CE ignimbrite) and products from the 2009 and 2014–15 eruptions were predominantly andesitic, while lavas from the pre-caldera edifice ranged from basaltic andesite (all ≥ 55 wt% SiO2) to andesite.

The samples recovered in this study span a wide compositional range from basalt to rhyolite. However, our primary objective is to characterize the newly discovered mantle-derived magmatic suite (basalt and magnesian andesite) and its implications for arc magmatism. Consequently, we focused our comprehensive trace element and isotopic analyses on these in-situ magmatic products. While the rhyolitic pumices provide interesting regional context, they were identified as allochthonous (rafted) components early in our investigation. Detailed geochemical fingerprinting to determine their specific provenance is beyond the scope of this study and remains a subject for future research.

Our new dataset reveals that the greater Hunga edifice encompasses a far broader compositional spectrum than previously recognized, including basalt, basaltic andesite, magnesian andesite (MA), andesite, and dacite. Notably, while basaltic compositions were recovered only from the deep SE flank (Site H08), MAs are widely distributed across the edifice, including the unnamed SSW cone (Table 1, Fig. 1b). We propose that the discovery of these mafic end-members acts as a geochemical "Rosetta Stone," providing the necessary keys to decipher the sub-volcanic architecture and primary magmatic processes that were previously obscured by more evolved erupted products.

Although Site H08 is a satellite vent, we argue that it constitutes an integral part of the active Hunga magmatic system. This interpretation is supported by observations at Nishinoshima volcano (Ogasawara arc), which is similar in scale to Hunga. For decades, Nishinoshima primarily erupted andesites, but the 2020 activity saw a sudden shift to basaltic magmas that were geochemically comparable to those from surrounding knolls 15 km away (Tamura et al.18). This parallel demonstrates that in large submarine arc volcanoes, basaltic magmas can be generated within the broad magmatic system and may erupt from different centers (summit vs. satellite vents) over time. Furthermore, the basalt recovered from Site H08 lacks any manganese oxide crust, which strongly suggests its geological youth and its relevance to recent magmatic activity rather than being an ancient remnant.

The primitive nature of these samples is further highlighted by comparison with other arc systems. The Hunga MAs (6–8 wt% MgO, Mg# > 50) are significantly more magnesian than the andesites of Nishinoshima (2–3 wt% MgO, Mg# ~ 30) and Kibblewhite (4–6 wt% MgO, Mg# ~ 50), both of which have been established as mantle-derived through extensive modeling and mineralogical studies (Tamura et al.19; Hirai et al.20). By logical extension, if those systems represent mantle-derived lineages, the more primitive geochemistry of the Hunga samples provides compelling evidence that they represent primary or near-primary melts.

Genetic relationships of Hunga magmas

Previously reported subaerial andesites overlap with, and are largely encompassed by, the andesite-dacite group identified in our dredged samples. While cognate enclaves (50.6–54.3 wt% SiO2) were reported in the 2014/15 subaerial deposits7, we observed only small enclaves in three of our submarine samples. These enclaves were too small to be physically separated; therefore, these three samples were processed as bulk rocks for XRF analysis. The resulting major element compositions showed no significant deviation from the other enclave-free samples, suggesting that the presence of these minor enclaves did not significantly bias the bulk rock data.

Trace element and isotopic analyses were not performed on these specific samples due to the small size of the enclaves and their negligible impact on bulk compositions. According to Brenna et al.7, these enclaves exhibit higher Mg#, Al2O3, and CaO, and lower TiO2 and FeO* compared to our dredged basalt-basaltic andesite samples (Fig. 2). This chemical signature is consistent with the interpretation that the subaerial enclaves represent cumulate rocks rather than true liquid magma compositions. Consequently, these enclaves are excluded from the following discussion on magmatic evolution.

The Hunga basalt-basaltic andesites are geochemically distinct from the magnesian andesite and andesite-dacite suites. Their lower Zr/Y (1.2–1.3 vs. 1.6–1.9) and MgO (3.2–4.1 wt% vs. 5.6–8.3 wt%) values, combined with the trends observed in Harker diagrams (Fig. 2), indicate that crystal fractionation of the identified basalts cannot produce the magnesian andesite or andesite-dacite compositions. Instead, the andesites and dacites form a continuous evolutionary trend originating from the magnesian andesites. This relationship is further supported by parallel trace element and REE patterns (Fig. 3), as well as consistent incompatible element (Fig. 4) and Sr–Nd-Pb isotope ratios (Fig. 5).

These multiple lines of evidence suggest that the andesites and dacites of Hunga volcano are derived from a magnesian andesite parental magma via crystal fractionation. The high Mg# (> 50) of these magnesian andesites strongly suggests that mantle-derived andesite represents a primary magma in this system19,20. Meanwhile, the distinct Zr/Y ratios, trace element/REE patterns, and isotopic signatures of the SE flank basalts indicate that a depleted, mantle-derived basaltic melt constitutes a second, independent primary magma within the Hunga volcanic system.

Subduction components and mantle melting processes

Incompatible element ratios (Fig. 4) provide critical insights into the contribution of subduction-derived components and the degree of mantle melting. Following the established criteria of Pearce et al.21 and Tamura et al.22, we utilize Ba/Nb as a proxy for total subduction addition, Ba/Th for shallow fluid-derived components, La/Sm for deep sediment-melt components, and Nb/Yb as an indicator of the degree of mantle melting (where lower values represent higher degrees of melting).

Although these trace element ratios can be influenced by multiple factors—including mantle heterogeneity and sediment subduction—the framework proposed by Pearce et al.21 is specifically designed to isolate the subduction-derived flux from the background mantle wedge signature in arc settings. This approach is particularly well-suited for the Hunga system, as its intra-oceanic arc setting minimizes the complications of continental crustal contamination, allowing for a more robust characterization of the primary magmatic processes.

Comparing Hunga with Tofua and Fonualei (Fig. 4), the elevated Ba/Th and Ba/Nb ratios in the Hunga suite indicate a significantly greater subduction contribution, primarily via hydrous fluids, relative to the Tofua and Fonualei magmas. This enhanced fluid flux likely triggered a higher degree of partial melting in the mantle wedge beneath Hunga, as evidenced by its lower Nb/Yb values. Notably, the SE flank basalts exhibit the highest Ba/Th and Ba/Nb and the lowest Nb/Yb values among all Hunga samples. This suggests that these basalts originated from the highest degree of fluid-fluxed melting, which is consistent with their overall lower concentrations of incompatible elements compared to the magnesian andesites (Fig. 3).

Furthermore, the 208Pb/204Pb vs. 206Pb/204Pb systematics (Fig. 5) show that the Hunga magmas plot along a trend extending from Lau Basin lavas (e.g., ODP Site 836; Fig. 1a). This trend is interpreted as a mixing line between an Indian MORB-type mantle source15 and a subduction-derived flux. In contrast, the isotopic trends for Tofua9 and Fonualei10 diagonally intersect this mixing line. These relationships suggest a latitudinal shift in the Pb isotopic composition of the slab-derived fluid along the arc. Such a variation is consistent with the findings of Castillo et al.23, who identified latitudinal compositional heterogeneity in the subducting oceanic crust that directly influences the geochemical signatures of Tonga-Kermadec arc lavas.

Significantly, these low-pressure primary magnesian andesites tightly cluster with other reported primary andesitic and boninitic compositions from thin-crust arc systems across the western and southwestern Pacific. These include primary melts from Nishinoshima19 and Kibblewhite20, as well as boninitic melt inclusions from Volcano A in the Tonga Arc28 and White Island in the Kermadec Arc29. The consistency between the Hunga magnesian andesites and these regional primitive arc melts supports the hypothesis that primary high-SiO2 melts can be generated via fluid-fluxed melting at the upper part of the mantle wedge (around 0.5 GPa), a process considered characteristic of intra-oceanic arcs with thin overriding crust. These thermodynamic and petrological constraints collectively provide strong indicators that the Hunga plumbing system may harbor two independent primary magmatic lineages generated at different mantle depths, providing a more robust and balanced framework for interpreting the Hunga magmatic system.

Generation and evolution of primary basaltic and andesitic magmas

Our new geochemical data suggest the co-existence of both primary basaltic and primary andesitic magmas within the Hunga volcanic system. The occurrence of two distinct primary magmas has been previously proposed for Kibblewhite volcano in the Kermadec arc20 and Nishinoshima volcano in the Ogasawara arc19. Both locations are characterized by thin oceanic crust (< 20 km).

In such thin-crust settings, mantle diapirs can ascend to shallower depths prior to melt segregation. Under hydrous conditions at low pressures (< ~ 1 GPa), melts in equilibrium with mantle peridotite are inherently andesitic27,30. Given that the crustal thickness of the submarine Tonga volcanic front is approximately 20 km31,32, we propose that the models developed for Kibblewhite and Nishinoshima are also applicable to Hunga volcano. We propose a three-stage evolution for the Hunga magmatic system to account for these observations: First, mantle melting beneath the thin crust (at low pressure) is inferred to produce primary andesitic magmas. Second, efficient crystal fractionation from these primary andesites generates the magnesian andesites, which further differentiate into the andesite–dacite suite that dominates Hunga volcano. Third, mantle melting at higher pressures produces primary basaltic magmas, which evolve into the recovered basaltic compositions (Mg# 30–40) during their ascent to the surface.

Implications for the 2022 eruption and similar arc volcanoes

While direct stratigraphic control and geochronological constraints linking the dredged samples definitively to the 15 January 2022 eruption are currently absent, the discovery of these lithologies offers a critical advancement in understanding the volcano’s potential for large-scale events. In the context of deep-sea exploration, the recovery of the H08 basalt represents a “Rosetta Stone” event. Even if these samples reflect different stages of the volcano’s long-term history, the petrological evidence for the existence of a bimodal primary magmatic system (basalt and magnesian andesite) provides a necessary framework that was previously missing.

We propose that the presence of basaltic magmas on the flanks of Hunga volcano suggests their potential involvement in triggering the 2022 caldera-forming eruption. A compelling parallel exists with the 2020 eruption of Nishinoshima volcano (Ogasawara arc, Japan). Long considered a predominantly andesitic system like Hunga, Nishinoshima’s violent 2020 activity was triggered by the ascent of basaltic magmas18 geochemically comparable to those from surrounding knolls 15 km away. In that instance, the ascending basaltic magma displaced the existing andesitic reservoir, leading to a transition from lava flows in 2019 to explosive eruptions in 202018. This “concurrent evidence” supports a working hypothesis where the injection of primitive, gas-rich basaltic magmas acts as a highly plausible mechanism for triggering the unprecedented 2022 Hunga event.

In the Hunga system, the basaltic samples exhibit exceptionally high Ba/Nb and Ba/Th ratios (Fig. 4), indicating a strong enrichment in subduction-derived volatile components. Such volatiles may have fueled the gas-rich, climactic explosion33. While Ukstins et al.34 focused on the mingling of different andesitic magmas as the immediate trigger for extreme gas pressure, our discovery of basaltic products provides a broader context for the system’s rejuvenation. Furthermore, our findings provide a petrological “missing link” for the geophysical models of Le Mével et al.35,36, who suggested that the tapping of deeper, gas-rich magmatic reservoirs could explain the intensity of the 2022 event.

Future research, including electron probe microanalysis (EPMA) of phenocrysts to constrain P–T conditions and melt inclusion analyses to determine volatile fractions37, will be essential to further elucidate the sub-volcanic plumbing system. Such studies will clarify how volatile behavior governed the unprecedented violence of the Hunga eruption.

Methods

Sampling and sample preparation

Marine scientific research and volcanic rock sampling within the Exclusive Economic Zone of the Kingdom of Tonga were conducted under the official research permits issued by the Tonga Geological Services (TGS), Ministry of Lands and Natural Resources, Government of the Kingdom of Tonga. Rock samples were recovered using two types of gear during the TAN2206 cruise. The rock dredge (ROCK), equipped with a metal rim and teeth, was targeted at volcanic substrates identified via Deep-Towed Imaging System (DTIS) video transects. Dredging was conducted in uphill or across-ridge directions for durations up to 15 min. The seamount sled (SLED), measuring 1.5 m × 1.0 m with a 0.5 m vertical opening, was towed downslope at approximately 1 knot. Although primarily designed for biological sampling, it also provided geological materials. Following recovery, representative sub-samples were selected, thoroughly cleaned with fresh water to remove sediments and biological remnants, and air-dried.

For geochemical analysis, rock chips (5–10 mm) were desalinated by soaking in purified water at 40 °C for 1–3 weeks. The completion of desalination was confirmed using silver nitrate (AgNO3) solution test. All samples were sonicated in acetone, followed by ultrapure water. The samples were then coarsely crushed in an iron mortar, and any altered fragments were removed by handpicking. Bulk rock powders were prepared using a vibration mill (Multi-Beads Shocker, Yasui Kikai Co., Osaka, Japan) with polycarbonate capsules and high-purity alumina rods.

Major and trace element analyses

Major and selected trace elements (V, Cr, Ni, Rb, Sr, Y, and Zr) were determined by X-ray fluorescence (XRF) using RIGAKU ZSX Primus II at JAMSTEC. Major elements were analyzed on glass beads (0.4 g powder mixed with 4 g anhydrous Li2B4O7 flux), while trace elements were measured on pressed powder discs following the method of Tani et al.38. Major element data are reported as normalized values to 100% on a volatile-free basis, with total iron expressed as FeO*.

Comprehensive trace element abundances (including REE, Sc, Ni, Rb, Sr, Y, Zr, Nb, Cs, Ba, Hf, Ta, Pb, Th, and U) were determined by inductively coupled plasma mass spectrometry (ICP-MS) using Thermo Fisher Scientific iCAP Q systems at JAMSTEC. Sample digestion followed the methods of Chang et al.39. Approximately 50 mg of powder was dissolved in an HF-HClO4 mixture, followed by sequential dissolution in HClO4 and HNO3. Final solutions were diluted by a factor of 10,000 in 2% HNO3 with trace amounts of HF. The GSJ reference material JB-2 was analyzed alongside the samples to validate the analytical method.

Sr, Nd, Pb, and Hf isotope analyses

For isotopic analysis, powders were strongly leached with 6N HCl and 1N HF, rinsed with Milli-Q water, and dried. Sample digestion and chemical separation followed Miyazaki et al.40, utilizing the fully automated COLUMNSPIDER system41. Total procedural blanks for Sr, Nd, Pb, and Hf were < 244, < 4, < 31, and < 16 pg, respectively.

Sr and Nd isotope ratios were measured by thermal ionization mass spectrometry (TIMS) using a Thermo Fisher Scientific Triton TI. Ratios were normalized to 86Sr/88Sr = 0.1194 and 146Nd/144Nd = 0.7219. During the study, NIST SRM-987 yielded 87Sr/86Sr = 0.710285 ± 23 (2 S.D., n = 4) and JNdi-1 yielded 143Nd/144Nd = 0.512100 ± 26 (2 S.D., n = 3).

Pb and Hf isotope ratios were determined by multi-collector (MC)-ICP-MS using a Thermo Fisher Scientific Neptune. Pb mass fractionation was corrected using Tl as an external standard and standard bracketing with NIST SRM-98142. Measured SRM-981 values were 206Pb/204Pb = 16.930 ± 6, 207Pb/204Pb = 15.483 ± 5, and 208Pb/204Pb = 36.669 ± 17 (2 S.D., n = 17). Data were normalized to the SRM-981 values of Baker et al.43 (206Pb/204Pb = 16.9416, 207Pb/204Pb = 15.500, and 208Pb/204Pb = 36.7262). Hf ratios were normalized to 179Hf/177Hf = 0.732544. JMC475 yielded 176Hf/177Hf = 0.282194 ± 08 (2 S.D., n = 16) and was adjusted to a value of 0.2821645.
