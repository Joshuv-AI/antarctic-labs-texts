Identification of pivotal genes associated with sugar and organic acid metabolism during Prunus Mume fruit ripening via metabolomic and transcriptomic analyses
Authors: Chenlu Luo, Xinyue Zhu, Jieru Chen, Hongtin Liu, Zichun Ma, Wei Zhang, Li Deng, Meifang Liang, Hai Liao, Jiayu Zhou
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractThe fruit of Prunus mume, a plant with medicinal and edible properties, contains sugars and organic acids as its vital ingredients, yet, their metabolic characteristics and regulatory mechanisms remain unclear. This study firstly investigated the metabolic dynamics of sugars and organic acids during four stages of fruit ripening, and hence sucrose and citrate were identified as the predominant sugar and organic acid, respectively. ABA correlated positively with citrate and sucrose accumulation. Then, 79.60 Gb transcriptome sequencing of four different stages facilitated the identification of candidate genes playing role in accumulation of sugars and organic acids, respectively. As a result, sucrose accumulation was associated with upregulation of SPS (LOC103341128), SUS (LOC103323057) and glgc (LOC103320871), alongside the downregulation of INVs (LOC103337231, LOC103333352, and LOC103341755). Citrate accumulation was largely driven by the upregulation of PEPC (LOC103318857) and CS (LOC103328745), and downregulation of NADP-IDH (LOC103340345) and GS (LOC103321068, LOC103338669). Enzymatic assay confirmed that CS (LOC103328745) encoded a functional enzyme in TCA cycle. Furthermore, correlation along with clustering analyses reinforced the close linkage among ABA, citrate and sucrose during fruit ripening. Beyond the metabolic enzymes, 328 transcription factors and 97 ABC transporters showing positive correlation with a least one type of sugar or organic acid might be responsible for the regulation and transportation of sugars and organic acids metabolism. Among them, AP2 (LOC103337713) was proposed as the core transcription factor by protein-protein interactome analysis and ABA was highlighted as a central regulatory switch governing sugar and organic acid metabolism in P. mume. This work provides not only valuable insights into the regulative mechanism of fruit sweetness and acidity in P. mume, but also potential strategy for further agricultural application in quality improvement of P. mume.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12870-025-07946-0.Keywords: Prunus mume, Metabolome, Transcriptome, Sugar, Organic acid

Introduction

Sugars and organic acids present in fruits are pivotal determinants of fruit quality, profoundly influencing nutritional values, flavor-related characteristics, and ultimately, consumer acceptance [1]. Their accumulation in fruits is affected by an interplay of fruit varieties, environmental factors (e.g., water availability, light exposure, temperature, and soil properties), as well as cultivation practices [2, 3]. Given the complexity of the processes involved in the accumulation of these compounds, a comprehensive understanding of their metabolic characteristics and regulatory mechanisms is essential for enhancing fruit quality. The composition and concentration of sugars and organic acids in fruit is primarily governed by their synthetic, degradative, and storage processes [2]. Sucrose, the principal product of photosynthesis, is transported from leaves to fruits using sugars will eventually be exported transporter (SWEET) and sucrose transporters (SUT) [4, 5]. Within the fruit, invertases (INV) and sucrose synthase (SUS) are mainly responsible for the utilization of sucrose [6, 7]. The expression of specific SUS gene can exhibit a negative correlation with soluble sugar and sucrose content, as demonstrated in carrot, underscoring its role in sucrose utilization [8]. Additionally, the co-expression of SUS and SWEET genes was found to enhance hexose content in grape berries by regulating sugar hydrolysis and transport [9]. Conversely, the transcription level of the PsSUS4 gene, along with sucrose content, increased concurrently through the manipulation of the TF PsbHLH58, highlighting the complex regulatory mechanisms governing sugar metabolism [10]. Collectively, the biosynthesis, degradation, and transport of sugars are interconnected within a sophisticated regulatory network encompassing a diverse array of genes.

Organic acids, which function as respiratory substrates derived from sugars, constitute a class of key intermediates in the tricarboxylic acid (TCA) cycle. Among these, citrate and malate represent the most predominant types, although a variety of organic acids, such as quinic acid and succinic acid, are also identified across different plant species [2, 11, 12]. A set of enzymes including citrate synthase (CS), aconitase (ACO), adenosine triphosphate-citrate lyase (ATP-ACL) and NADP-dependent isocitrate dehydrogenase (NADP-IDH) in either the mitochondria or the cytosol are involved in the citrate metabolism [2, 13, 14]. Malate is another significant organic acid that plays a crucial role in maintaining pH balance in the cytoplasm and is also involved in stress responses, whose metabolism is dominantly associated with TCA cycle and glyoxylate shunt. The majority of citrate and malate in fruit is stored in vacuoles, a process mediated by proton pumps or channel proteins [15]. Previous studies on kumquat demonstrated that the phosphoenolpyruvate carboxylase (PEPC) and malate dehydrogenase (NAD-MDH) genes are positively correlated with the accelerated accumulation of malate, whereas high expression levels of NAD-IDH and glutamine synthetase (GS) genes are associated with citrate degradation during fruit development [2]. Jia et al. [11] suggested that malate accumulation primarily depended on increased synthesis catalyzed by NAD-MDH and PEPC. On the contrary, in fruit of Prunus salicina, the content of malate increased with the increasing PEPC activity and decreasing NAD-MDH activity [16]. In addition, in blueberries, citrate accumulation was linked to ACL, glutamate decarboxylase, and glutamate transporter, while malate accumulation was associated with NAD-MDH [12]. Although the high accumulation of citrate in Actinidia eriantha was found to be caused by the increased CS activity [11], accumulation of citrate in P. salicina was depended mainly on the increased PEPC and ACO activities [16]. However, an study on Citrus sinensis by Chen et al. [17] found no association between CS and citrate accumulation. Overall, the divergent roles of various genes underscore the complex and species-specific nature of the genetic regulation governing sugar and organic acid metabolism, highlighting the necessity for fundamental research on various plants that are rich in sugars and organic acids.

Prunus mume Sieb. et Zucc, commonly referred to as Chinese Mei or greengage plum, is an acidic fruit indigenous to China, with its earliest documentation found in “Shennong’s Classic of Materia Medica”, over 2,000 years [18]. Currently, this species is widely cultivated in various Asian countries, including Japan, Korea, and Vietnam, where it is valued both as an ornamental flowering tree and a fruit-bearing tree, yielding substantial harvests. Due to its extensive nutritional and functional properties, P. mume has gained acceptance in multiple countries, and its unique characteristics contribute to its commercialization. The P. mume fruit, also recognized as Japanese apricot, has been utilized as a functional food in Japan since ancient times. Furthermore, evidence suggests that extracts of P. mume exhibit various medicinal properties, due to its richness in anthocyanins, organic acids, phenolic acids, and sugars [19], with the balance of sugars and organic acids contributing to the fruit’s palatability. Additionally, the Chinese Pharmacopeia (2020) designates organic acids as quality markers for assessing the medicinal value of P. mume. Despite several studies identifying key genes and pathways involved in sugar and organic acid metabolism, a comprehensive transcriptome-scale identification of the pivotal genes related to these metabolic processes in P. mume remains insufficient. Therefore, the objective of this study is to characterize the metabolic profiles, examine the dynamic changes in the composition of sugars and organic acids, analyze the expression of genes encoding essential enzymes, and explore the correlations among various indicators throughout the fruit ripening process of P. mume. Accordingly, the results provide valuable insights into the regulative mechanism of fruit sweetness and acidity in P. mume, and this study would be helpful for further agricultural application in quality improvement of P. mume.

Materials and methods

Material preparation

The P. mume cultivar, Japanese apricot, was cultivated within the P. mume planting base located in Dayi County, Chengdu City, Sichuan Province, China. From March to June 2024, the fresh P. mume fruits of four-year-old P. mume trees were randomly selected from the middle and upper parts of the crown of ten trees at 11 am. For each tree, 20 representative fruits were harvested at 62, 90, 110, and 138 days after flower bloom (DAFB, designated as S1, S2, S3 and S4 stages, respectively), among which the fruit had reached physiological maturity at the S4 stage. These trees were about 2.5 m tall and spaced at 4 × 5 m. Fertilizers were supplied to meet standard requirements, and insects and diseases were controlled according to local agriculture department recommendations. All the collected P. mume fruit samples were rapidly frozen in liquid nitrogen and stored at -80 ℃ until further analysis. Three biological replicates were prepared for each developmental stage.

Determination of physicochemical properties

The vertical and horizontal diameters of whole fruits of each stage were measured using vernier caliper. The fruit shape index was calculated as the ratio of vertical diameter to horizontal diameter. As no hardened kernel was visible at the S1 stage, the whole fruits were used for the following pH values, titratable acidity, transcriptomic and metabolomic (including the sweetness index) assays. In the S2, S3 and S4 stages, the material for the above assays do not include the kernel. 0.5 g of fruit was ground in liquid nitrogen, and subsequently extracted with 10 mL distilled water for 2 h at room temperature. The mixture was filtered followed with dilution to constant volume, and the pH value was monitored using a precision pH meter (PHS-2 F, Shanghai Leici, China). For the titratable acidity, the mixture was extracted at 80 ℃ for 30 min, filtered and then diluted to constant volume. Titratable acidity was measured using 0.01 N NaOH as a titrant and phenolphthalein as an endpoint indicator. All experiment were performed with no less than three biological replicates. As described previously [20], the sweetness index was calculated based on the relative concentration of different sugar fractions, which were replaced by the relative abundance sourced from metabolomic data in this study. Sweetness index equals 2.3 × (fructose relative abundance) + 1.35 × (sucrose relative abundance) + 1 × (glucose relative abundance) + 0.81 × (sorbitol relative abundance).

Metabolome in fruit development of P. mume

The twelve P. mume fruit samples (four different ripening stages, three replicates) were ground into powder in liquid N2, respectively. One hundred milligrams of freeze-dried sample was dissolved in 500 µL of 80% methanol solution. After the solution was vortexed, it was placed in an ice bath for 5 min. Then, the solution underwent centrifugation at 15,000 ×g for 20 min at 4 ℃ and the supernatant was diluted with LC-MS grade water to a final concentration of 53% methanol. Prior to LC-MS/MS analysis, the supernatant underwent centrifugation again at 15,000 × g at 4 ℃ for 20 min. The blank samples (Blank) were used to confirm no residual substances remained in LC-MS/MS system. To ensure the reliability of the LC-MS/MS system, quality control (QC) samples were created by mixing equal volumes of samples from each experimental treatment.

Following an established protocol [21], quasi-targeted metabolomics was employed on a SCIEX QTRAP® 6500 + mass spectrometer (AB Sciex Pte. Ltd., Framingham, MA, USA) in Novogene Co., Ltd (https://www.novogene.com/us-en/). Analysis was conducted on a Xselect HSS T3 C18 column (2.50 μm, 150 mm × 2.1 mm, Waters, USA) using a gradient elution, consisting of eluent A (0.1% formic acid-water) and eluent B (0.1% formic acid-acetonitrile), at a flow of 0.4 mL/min. Gradient elution was conducted as follows: 2% B for 2.0 min; 2–100% B for 15.0 min; 100% B for 17.0 min; 100–2% B for 17.1 min; and 2% B for 20.0 min. For the positive/negative polarity mode, QTRAP® 6500 + mass spectrometer adopted Curtain Gas of 35 psi, Collision Gas of Medium, IonSpray Voltage of 5500 V (-4500 V for negative polarity mode), Temperature of 550 ℃, Ion Source Gas of 1:60, Ion Source Gas of 2:60.

Principal components analysis (PCA) and Partial least squares discriminant analysis (PLS-DA) were performed using metaX (a flexible and comprehensive software for processing metabolomics data). Metabolites with VIP > 1, fold change ≥ 2 or ≤ 0.5, and p-value < 0.05 were considered to be differential metabolites (DEMs).

Transcriptome in fruit development of P. mume

Total RNA of the 12 collected samples at four different ripening stages was isolated, whose quality was further evaluated by Agilent 2100 Bioanalyzer. The qualified RNA was chosen for RNA-seq library construction and subsequently sequenced on an Illumina HiSeq 6000 platform by Novogene Corporation (http://cn.novogene.com). Those low-quality reads that contained Ns (where N indicates ambiguous bases in the reads) or contained low-quality bases (Q20 values ≤ 95%) of more than 50% were filtered, and hence the clean reads were accordingly retained [21]. The clean reads were aligned to the reference genome (GCF_000346735.1) using HISAT2 software. Functional annotation was achieved by aligning sequences against seven database queries (NR, GO, COG, KOG, SwissProt, KEGG and PFAM), using BLASTX with an E-value of 1 × 10− 5. The transcript abundance of genes was calculated as FPKM (fragments per kilobase of exon model per million mapped fragments). The log2 (fold change) from the RNA-seq data were subjected to hierarchical clustering using DESeq2, and the differentially expressed genes (DEGs) were defined as genes with a log2 (fold change) ≥ 1 along with false discovery rate (FDR) ≤ 0.05 between two groups.

Real-time PCR

To verify the transcriptional levels obtained from transcriptome, the real-time PCR was conducted following the method of Liao et al. [21]. In brief, gene primers were designed using Primer Premier software (Supplementary Table 1). The UBQ2 was used as the reference gene [22]. The reaction mixture (20µL) contained 10 µL SYBR Green Master Mix, 0.3 µL of each primer (10 µM) and 5 µL DNA template. The reaction procedure was performed as an initial denaturation at 95 °C for 30 s, followed by 40 cycles at 95 °C for 3 s and 60 °C for 30 s. Relative gene expression was calculated using the 2−ΔΔCT method [23].

Protein-protein interactome prediction

In the STRING database (https://cn.string-db.org), the DEGs were matched to the Arabidopsis thaliana genome to obtain orthologous sequences and then the interactome was constructed with a confidence score cutoff of ≥ 0.40 (medium confidence). The predicted interaction network was subsequently visualized and analyzed in Cytoscape software (v3.8.2, https://cytoscape.org/download.html) [24]. Within the predicted network, nodes were defined as proteins showing a significant correlation edge (FDR ≤ 0.05) with respective orthologs.

Protein sequence and preliminary enzymatic validation of CS (LOC103328745) protein

Prior to the recombinant expression, the presence of a signal peptide in the CS (LOC103328745) protein was predicted using SignalP v4.1 (https://services.healthtech.dtu.dk/services/SignalP-4.1/) [25] and hence a signal peptide of 43 amino acids at the N-terminus was found. To avoid the negative effect of signal peptide on the prokaryotic expression, the product did not include the N-terminal signal peptide of 43 amino acids. Following the method of Liao et al. [21], the codon-optimized CS gene was ligated into pGEX-4P vector using BamHI and XhoI sites and recombinant vector was subsequently transferred to E. coli BL21 (DE3). When the OD600 of bacterial solution reached approximately 0.6 ~ 0.8, 0.1 mM IPTG (final concentration) was added to drive the production of CS protein. After 16 h of cultivation, the cell was harvested by centrifugation at 5,000 rpm for 15 min. Then, the cell was lysed by lysozyme followed by sonication and the supernatant was collected by centrifugation at 12,000 rpm for 10 min. Following the addition of supernatant to a glutathione-agarose affinity column, column bound CS protein was eluted with eluting-buffer (50 mM Tris-HCl, pH 8.0, 10 mM reduced glutathione) which was later removed by dialysis. 12% SDS-PAGE was conducted to check purification profile. The homogeneity of purified CS was detected by Nano LC ultimate 3000 system coupled with Q-Exactive plus mass spectrometer (ThermoFisher Scientific). The CS activity was determined using Citrate Synthase (CS) Activity Assay Kit BC1060 (Solarbio Cor, China) [26].

Statistical analysis

All results were expressed as mean ± standard deviation (SD) and were subjected to analysis of variance (ANOVA) using SPSS software (version 29; IBM, USA). Statistical significance was set at p < 0.05 [24].

Results

Dynamic changes in indicter of P. mume fruit quality

The peel of P. mume fruit demonstrated a color transition from green to reddish-yellow throughout the ripening process (Fig. 1A). Both titratable acidity and pH values were assessed throughout the entire ripening process. During the maturation of P. mume, titratable acidity exhibited a progressive increase from S1 stage to S3 stage, with a total increase of 197.52% compared to the S1 stage. This was followed by a marked increase in titratable acidity was observed at S4 stage, with a 51.01% rise relative to the S3 stage. Furthermore, the pH value consistently decreased throughout the maturation process, reaching its lowest point at S4 stage, where it measured 2.01 (Fig. 1B and C). The sweetness index also demonstrated significant variations during the ripening of P. mume. From S1 stage to S3 stage, the sweetness index increased by 145.88%, indicating a significant accumulation of sugars or other sweet-tasting compounds as the fruit matured. This upward trend persisted into S4 stage, where the sweetness index further increased by 136.44% compared to S3 stage, ultimately reaching its peak value (Fig. 1D). These results indicate that the sweetness of P. mume intensifies considerably during the later stages of ripening, particularly from S3 stage to S4 stage.

Additionally, both the horizontal and vertical diameters exhibited a marked increase during the initial stages, followed by a stabilization phase (Supplementary Fig. 1A). Concurrently, the fruit shape index significantly decreased during the early stages (from S1 stage to S2 stage) before experiencing a slight increase (Supplementary Fig. 1B). At the S1 stage, the fruit shape was characterized as a narrow and elongated oval, whereas in the later stages (i.e., S2, S3, and S4 stages), the shape gradually evolved to a more rounded form. In alignment with the observed trend in fruit volume, the fruit weight exhibited a substantial increase from S1 stage to S3 stage, followed by a modest rise from S3 stage to S4 stage (Supplementary Fig. 1C). The kernel (endocarp) was first observed at the S2 stage and underwent a hardening process during ripening. Notably, both the horizontal and vertical diameters of the kernel peaked at the S3 stage (Supplementary Fig. 1D and 1E), while the weight of the kernel also reached its maximum at the S3 stage, exhibiting a 21.22% increase compared to the S2 stage. Additionally, the kernel-to-pulp ratio consistently decreased from S2 stage to S4 stage, indicating a gradual reduction in the proportion of the kernel within the fruit (Supplementary Fig. 1F).

Composition and content of sugars and organic acids

Sugars and organic acids serve not only as essential nutrients and flavor components in the fruit of P. mume but also play a pivotal role in determining its medicinal properties. The levels of different sugars and organic acids in P. mume fruit at various ripening stages were assessed using quasi-targeted metabolomics. As shown in Fig. 2A, a total of 1,102 metabolites were identified across four ripening stages of P. mume fruit, with 8.89% and 7.99% classified as their derivatives, respectively. The high reproducibility of the metabolomic data was confirmed by Pearson correlation coefficients, which were no less than 0.985 among biological replicates within the same group (Fig. 2B), ensuring reliability for subsequent analyses.

Furthermore, the analysis identified 12 soluble sugars and 12 organic acids associated with fruit flavor and quality, as documented in the existing literature on P. mume fruit. Among these, sucrose, sorbitol, and maltose were identified as the 3 predominant sugars during the ripening process of P. mume, collectively accounting for over 83.98% of the total identified sugars, with statistical significance. As the fruit ripened, the concentrations of sucrose, sorbitol, and maltose exhibited a gradual accumulation, with a pronounced increase observed at the S4 stage, showing increases of 10.28-fold, 1.45-fold, and 11.16-fold, respectively, compared to the S1 stage (Fig. 3A, Supplementary Fig. 2). Furthermore, citrate, L-malate, and quinic acid were identified as the 3 primary organic acids throughout the ripening of P. mume fruit, together constituting more than 94.89% of the identified organic acids, with their concentrations also demonstrating statistical significance (Fig. 3B, Supplementary Fig. 3). The concentration of citrate peaked at the S3 stage, exhibiting a 4.80-fold increase relative to the S1 stage, and remained stable at the S4 stage. In contrast, L-malate reached its peak concentration at the S2 stage before declining to 57.85% of its S2 level by the S4 stage. Conversely, quinic acid exhibited its highest concentration at the S1 stage, subsequently decreasing to 63.31% of its initial level by the S4 stage during the ripening process. The significant increase in citrate concentration may contribute to the elevated acidity of P. mume fruit, as evidenced by the strong positive correlation observed between citrate and titratable acidity (R = 0.756, p < 0.01).

Dynamic changes in phytohormones during fruit maturation

This study analyzed the changes in the concentrations of ABA, GA7, indole-3-acetic acid (IAA), jasmonic acid (JA), GABA, and trans-zeatin riboside (tZR) across four ripening periods. Among these, ABA, IAA, JA, and tZR were classified as DEMs, while GA7 and GABA exhibited no significant differences (FC < 2) during the observed periods. The concentration of ABA initially decreased before sharply increasing, ultimately reaching a maximum at the S4 stage, with a 35.77-fold increase compared to the S1 stage. The highest concentrations of IAA and tZR were recorded at the S1 stage, exhibiting increases of 6.64-fold and 114.16-fold, respectively, compared to the S4 stage. In contrast, the peak concentration of JA was observed at the S2 stage, which was 2.86-fold higher than that at the S4 stage (Fig. 3C, Supplementary Fig. 4).

The correlation between various plant hormones and a range of phenotypes were further assessed in Supplementary Table 3. ABA exhibited a strong positive correlation with sorbitol (r = 0.842, p < 0.01), sucrose (r = 0.824, p < 0.01), maltose (r = 0.751, p < 0.01), and citrate (r = 0.583, p < 0.05), whereas tZR demonstrated a significant positive correlation with quinic acid (r = 0.761, p < 0.01). These results provide robust evidence that the accumulation of sugars and organic acids in P. mume fruit is linked to a complex network of plant hormones. Notably, fruit size and weight showed a strong negative correlation with IAA, JA and tZR. This observation aligns with the highest concentrations of IAA, JA, and tZR recorded in the early developmental stages (S1 and S2 stages) of P. mume fruit.

The clustering analysis of DEMs

To enhance the understanding of the metabolic changes occurring during the ripening of P. mume fruit, 600 DEMs were categorized into four clusters using K-means clustering algorithm (Fig. 3D). Metabolites in subcluster 1 exhibited a gradual accumulation throughout the entire ripening process. Conversely, the metabolites in subcluster 2 demonstrated a decrease during the middle stages of ripening, followed by a significant enrichment in the later stages. Additionally, the metabolites in subclusters 3 and 4 were predominantly accumulated during the early stages of fruit ripening. Among the key sugars and organic acids identified in P. mume fruit, citrate, oxalic acid, D-galacturonic acid, sucrose, maltose, and raffinose were classified within subcluster (1) In contrast, sorbitol, galactose, xylose, L-rhamnose, arabinose, and additional galactose were grouped into subcluster (2) Shikimic acid, succinic acid, and quinic acid were categorized in subcluster (3) Regarding plant hormones, ABA was included in subcluster 1, while tZR and JA were assigned to subcluster 4.

Transcriptome sequencing of P. mume fruit and analysis of differentially expressed genes

To identify genes potentially associated with variations in sugars and organic acids, fruit samples of P. mume at four developmental stages were subjected to RNA sequencing (RNA-seq). This process yielded over 79.60 Gb of clean data, with a Q30 score exceeding 94.51%. On average, 86.60% clean reads were uniquely mapped to the P. mume genome. Pearson correlations were calculated to evaluate the consistency between biological replicates, resulting in an R2 value greater than 0.864.

The distribution of genes across five expression levels exhibited relative consistency across all developmental stages, with the following proportions: 38.14%-45.69% of genes expressed at levels of 0 < FPKM ≤ 1, 8.12%-9.32% at 1 < FPKM ≤ 3, 19.5%-25.05% at 3 < FPKM ≤ 15, and 18.43%-21.56% at 15 < FPKM ≤ 60. Additionally, approximately 6.39%-8.57% of genes demonstrated very high expression levels (60 < FPKM) across different samples (Supplementary Fig. 5A). Furthermore, a total of 658 genes were uniquely expressed at a single stage, with 853 genes identified in the S1 stage, 251 in the S2 stage, 248 in the S3 stage, and 302 in the S4 stage (Supplementary Fig. 5B).

When comparing the S1 stage to subsequent stages, a total of 2,088 (S2), 2,393 (S3), and 3,044 (S4) genes were found to be upregulated, while 1,784 (S1), 2,886 (S2), and 4,449 (S4) genes were downregulated over time (Table 2). Consistent with the metabolomic results, the highest number of DEGs was observed in the comparison between S1 stage and S4 stage.

According to the GO and KEGG analyses, the sugar- and organic acid-related genes were enriched in S1 vs. S3 and S1 vs. S4 comparisons. Within the GO analysis in the comparison of S1 vs. S3 stages, the top two subcategories in BP category were carbohydrate metabolic process (149 DEGs) and cellular carbohydrate metabolic process (44 DEGs), while carbohydrate metabolic process (186 DEGs) was the leading subcategory in BP category in S1 vs. S4 comparisons (Supplementary Fig. 6). Besides, the co-expression analysis of DEGs was also conducted utilizing the K-means clustering method. Before the co-expression analysis, the DEGs were filtered to ensure that each gene underwent a stringent multiple testing correction (R > 0.9) and was associated with at least 1 DEM. Consequently, a total of 10,214 DEGs were categorized into 4 co-expression clusters (Supplementary Fig. 8), correlating with metabolic pathways during the fruit ripening process of P. mume.

The DEGs involved in the metabolism of sugars

In Fig. 4A, the expression of 71 genes associated with sugar metabolism showed a complex pattern during the ripening of fruit, comprising 42 upregulated genes and 29 downregulated genes (Supplementary Table 3). The metabolic pathway involved in sugars is drawn in Supplementary Fig. 9A. Specifically, the upregulated genes included three sucrose-phosphate synthase (SPS) genes, one SUS gene, two sorbitol dehydrogenase (SDH) genes, one ectonucleotide pyrophosphatase/phosphodiesterase family member 1/3 (ENPP1_3) gene, one UTP–glucose-1-phosphate uridylyltransferase (UGP2) gene, two glucose-1-phosphate adenylyltransferase (glgC) genes, two starch synthase (glgA) genes, one granule-bound starch synthase (WAXY) gene, four alpha-amylase (AMY) genes, two beta-amylase (BMY) genes, one phosphoglucomutase (PGM) gene, one 6-phosphofructokinase (PFK) gene, five fructose-bisphosphate aldolase (ALDO) genes, one glyceraldehyde 3-phosphate dehydrogenase (GAPDH) gene, one phosphoglycerate mutase (PGAM) gene, one Phosphoglycerate mutase-like enzyme (GPMI) gene, one pyruvate kinase (PK) genes, one phosphoenolpyruvate carboxykinase (PCKA) genes, one hexokinase (HK) gene, one glucose-6-phosphate 1-dehydrogenase (GD6P) genes, two 6-phosphogluconate dehydrogenase (PGD) genes, two ribulose-phosphate 3-epimerase (RPE) genes, one alpha-glucan water dikinase (GWD) gene, and one phosphoglucan, water dikinase (PWD) gene, all of which were found to be upregulated in P. mume fruit.

The DEGs involved in the metabolism of organic acids

The metabolism of citrate, malate, and quinic acid is intricately interconnected with pyruvate metabolism, the TCA cycle, the acetyl-CoA pathway, the GABA shunt, and the shikimate pathway (Supplementary Fig. 9B). A total of 36 DEGs were identified during the ripening of fruit based on comparative transcriptomic analysis, comprising 24 upregulated and 12 downregulated genes (Fig. 4B and Supplementary Table 5). Within pyruvate metabolism, 15 DEGs were enriched across four enzymatic pathways, with four genes associated with the PDH being upregulated. Additionally, genes encoding PEPC and PK exhibited significant differential expression in P. mume fruit, with one PEPC and two PK genes being upregulated. Furthermore, two out of four genes encoding NADP-dependent malate dehydrogenase (NADP-ME) were also found to be upregulated. In the TCA cycle, nine DEGs corresponding to six enzymatic complexes were identified, including two CSs and ACO gene that were upregulated, while one IDH gene was downregulated. Additionally, the 2-oxoglutarate dehydrogenase complex (OGDC) and SDH were implicated in succinate metabolism, with one member from each of these complexes being upregulated in this study. During fruit ripening, 1/3 NAD-MDHs (LOC103338505) was upregulated. Regarding the acetyl-CoA pathway, two PEPCK genes were specifically upregulated in P. mume fruit. In the GABA shunt associated with citrate degradation, two glutamate decarboxylase (GAD) genes, three GSs, and one glutamate dehydrogenase (GDH) were upregulated, while two additional GSs were downregulated. In the shikimate metabolic pathway, three genes encoding 3-deoxy-d-arabino-heptulosonate-7-phosphate synthase (DAHPS) were identified, consisting of two downregulated members and one upregulated member.

Subsequently, the correlation coefficients (R-values) between the previously mentioned DEGs and three predominant organic acids (e.g., citric, malic, and quinic acid) were calculated (Supplementary Table 6). For citric acid, the genes encoding DAHPS (LOC103335912), SDH (novel.942), and CS (LOC103328745) demonstrated strong positive correlations, with R-values of 0.911 (p < 0.01), 0.884 (p < 0.01), and 0.847 (p < 0.01), respectively. In contrast, the ACO (LOC103340381), PEPCK (LOC103319416), GS (LOC103344275), PK (LOC103323960), PDH (LOC103332130, LOC103325136, and LOC103341467), and OGDC (LOC103329297) exhibited moderate positive correlations (0.6 ≤ R < 0.8, p < 0.05). Regarding L-malic acid, the genes encoding CS (LOC103323169), NAD-MDH (LOC103334965), and PK (LOC103327196) also showed moderate positive correlations (0.6 ≤ R < 0.8, p < 0.05). For quinic acid, the genes encoding MDH (LOC103325583), GS (LOC103338669), PEPC (LOC103326102), and DAHPS (LOC103319557) exhibited moderate positive correlations (0.6 ≤ R < 0.8, p < 0.05), with GS (LOC103338669) showing the highest correlation (R = 0.796, p < 0.01).

Protein-protein interaction prediction

Subsequently, a protein-protein interaction (PPI) network was constructed to identify central genes. Given the complex regulation of organic acid and sugar metabolism, proteins associated with biosynthetic enzymes, TFs, and transporter carriers, whose transcriptional levels exhibited significant correlations with at least 1 of the 3 primary organic acids and sugars, were incorporated into the STRING database. Therefore, a total of 90 enzymes related to organic acid and sugar metabolism, 322 TFs, and 80 transporter carriers were included in the analysis. Figure 6 illustrates the analysis of the predicted PPI network, which facilitates the visualization and evaluation of the functional associations among the aforementioned proteins. The results indicated that 388 nodes interacted with an average of 2820.76 neighbors, resulting in 1410 edges (average number of neighbors = 7.27; average local clustering coefficient = 0.436; PPI enrichment p-value < 1.0e-16) (Fig. 6A and Supplementary Table 7). Among these nodes, AP2 (LOC103337713) emerged as the principal node, interacting with up to 61 candidate proteins, including 53 TFs from various families and seven enzymes associated with sugar and organic acid metabolism. Furthermore, the enzymes related to organic acid and sugar metabolism may form a multienzyme complex due to the close interaction network among these enzymes (Fig. 6B and C), which aids in elucidating the potential mechanisms underlying the crosstalk between organic acids and sugars in fruit. For instance, the proteins pgm (LOC103321420), HK (LOC103327827), and TKL-1 (Transketolase, LOC103331769) may function as central catalysts within the sugar-metabolic enzymes, interacting with 47, 44, and 44 candidate proteins, respectively. Additionally, the proteins CS (LOC103328745), PEPC (LOC103326102), and PK (LOC103323960) were identified as central catalysts in the multienzyme complex associated with organic acid metabolism, with 39, 35, and 31 interacting members, respectively. In Supplementary Tables 4 and 6, AP2 (LOC103337713) exhibited a negative correlation with three types of sugars as well as citrate, highlighting its potential contribution to the accumulation of sugars and organic acids in P. mume fruits.

Cis-acting elements analysis of significant genes

The identification of cis-acting elements is instrumental in elucidating the regulatory mechanisms of key genes. In this study, a total of 27 genes were subjected to cis-acting element analysis, which included 21 candidate pivotal genes associated with sugar and organic acid metabolism, five central catalysts (except the overlapped CS), and one central TF, AP2, as determined through proposed PPI prediction (Supplementary Table 8). Notably, the classification of central catalysts is attributed to both candidate pivotal genes and central catalysts. The cis-acting elements were identified using PlantCare (http://bioinformatics.psb.ugent.be/webtools/plantcare/html/) and subsequently visualized using TBtools. The results indicated that the promoters of these genes contain multiple cis-acting elements that are involved in responses to environmental stress and plant hormones (Fig. 7). With the exception of the promoters of INV (LOC103341755), GS (LOC103338669), TKL-1 (LOC103331769), glgC (LOC103320871), and AMY (LOC103321611), the promoters of the remaining 22 candidate pivotal genes exhibited ABRE elements, which are responsive to ABA. These results suggest that the AP2 may play a crucial role in mediating responses to various plant hormones, including ABA, IAA, and gibberellins (GA), as well as in regulating plant growth and development and responding to multiple environmental stresses such as light, low temperature, and drought.

Protein sequence and preliminary enzymatic validation of CS (LOC103328745)

Despite the fact that several candidate pivotal genes were suggested, it is crucial to carry out functional validation on these candidate genes. Herein, the enzymatic activity of Cs (LOC103328745) was preliminarily validated. The recombinant CS protein was successfully expressed in transformed E. coli BL21 and purified, exhibiting an apparent molecular weight of 56 kDa (Supplementary Fig. 10A), which exceeded the predicted mass of 26 kDa due to the GST-tag in the N-terminus. The CS-containing gel bands were digested by trypsin followed with ESI-MS/MS, showing a 61% sequence converge with the theoretical sequence, thereby confirming the homogeneity of purified CS protein (Supplementary Fig. 10B). The enzymatic activity of purified CS protein was subsequently determined to be 105.06 U/mg, confirming that the CS gene encodes a functional enzyme in TCA cycle. Overall, this result provides a candidate target for metabolic regulation of organic acid metabolism, by which the quality of P. mume will be substantially improved in the future.

Discussion

Identification of predominant sugar and organic acid in P. mume fruit

The composition of sugars and organic acids varies significantly among different fruit crops. Fruits can be categorized into three types based on the predominant soluble sugar: the sucrose accumulation type, the fructose accumulation type, and the glucose accumulation type [27]. Regarding organic acids, fruits can be classified into two categories according to the dominant organic acid: the citrate-dominant type and the malate-dominant type [27]. Furthermore, organic acids are integral to various stages of plant development and exert a significant impact on the central metabolic processes within plants [28]. In accordance with the metabolomic data, the overall changes in metabolites, as well as those within the categories of “carbohydrates and their derivatives” and “organic acids and their derivatives”, exhibited similar trends during fruit ripening, suggesting that metabolic activity intensifies as the fruit matures. Additionally, the progressive upregulation of metabolites in the “carbohydrates and their derivatives” and “organic acids and derivatives” categories may be associated with the increase in sweetness and titratable acidity, respectively (Fig. 1B and D). Specifically, the contents of three primary sugars, along with nine other soluble sugars associated with flavor development, were evaluated, by which sucrose, sorbitol, and maltose constituted the majority of soluble sugars in P. mume fruit, with sucrose being the most abundant. All three sugars exhibited an increase during the ripening process, with sucrose and maltose showing similar trends (Fig. 3A, Supplementary Fig. 2). Comparable instances were observed in Prunus armeniaca, P. persica, and Ananas comosus, where sucrose was the predominant soluble sugar [29, 30]. In contrast, in Prunus avium, glucose and fructose were the two major soluble sugars present in similar concentrations, while sucrose and sorbitol were found at lower levels [27].

In numerous plant species, citrate and malate are recognized as two predominant organic acids found in fruits. Citrate is the primary organic acid in A. comosus and berries [12], whereas malate is the most abundant organic acid in Malus domestica and Prunus domestica [31]. This study identified citrate, malate, and quinic acid as the three most significant organic acids, with citrate being the most prominent. Notably, despite the close genetic relationship between P. domestica and P. mume, the concentration of citrate in P. domestica was found to be nearly 100 times lower than malate [31]. Furthermore, the concentration of citric acid increased during the ripening of P. mume fruit, while the levels of malate and quinic acid decreased (Fig. 3B, Supplementary Fig. 3). The dynamic change in organic acids in this study was consistent with previous literature [32], where citric acid increased and malate decreased during the ripening of Japanese apricot fruit, and finally the content of citric acid was higher than that of malate. Based on the composition and concentration of sugars and organic acids at final ripening stage in its fruits, P. mume was proposed to be classified as a sucrose-accumulating and citrate-dominant type.

Identification of candidate pivotal genes involved in sugar accumulation

Three families of enzymes (e.g., SPS, SUS, and INV) are primarily responsible for sucrose metabolism [6, 7, 29]. Recent studies have shown positive correlations between SPS transcript level and sucrose accumulation in yellow peach fruit [33], and SUS transcript level and sucrose in P. salicina [10]. Consequently, the upregulation of SPS and SUS genes may contribute to the elevated accumulation of sucrose. Among the identified SPS and SUSs in P. mume, SPS (LOC103341128) and SUS (LOC103323057) exhibited a significantly positive correlation with sucrose levels, suggesting that both genes may play a role in the high concentration of sucrose. As a crucial product of photosynthesis, sucrose is transported through the phloem into fruits and stored in vacuoles. Additionally, invertases, which exist as cell-wall, vacuolar, and cytosolic isoforms, are responsible for converting stored sucrose into fructose and glucose, which are subsequently utilized for metabolism and signaling [34]. The knockout of a vacuolar INV in Solanum lycopersicum resulted in increased fruit sucrose content, accompanied by enhanced fruit firmness and elevated total soluble solids [34]. Similarly, in P. mume fruit, three INVs (LOC103337231, LOC103333352, and LOC103341755) exhibited a negative correlation with sucrose levels, potentially leading to a reduced hydrolysis of sucrose. glgC catalyzes the initial step in glycogen synthesis by converting glucose-1-phosphate into adenosine diphosphate (ADP)-glucose, the precursor for glycogen and starch biosynthesis. Research on glgC from Astragalus membranaceus has demonstrated its effect on carbon distribution between starch and sucrose metabolism, as evidenced by a positive correlation between glgC levels and sucrose content [35]. Similarly, a positive correlation was observed between the glgC in P. mume glgC (LOC103320871) and sucrose content, indicating that glgC may regulate sucrose accumulation by balancing carbon flux in starch and sucrose metabolism. In summary, the enhanced synthesis coupled with decreased degradation of sucrose likely contributes significantly to the high accumulation of sucrose in P. mume, with the potential key regulatory nodes being SPS (LOC103341128), SUS (LOC103323057), glgC (LOC103320871), and three INVs (LOC103337231, LOC103333352, and LOC103341755).

Maltose, the primary product of starch degradation, plays a critical role in the rhythmic turnover of starch in plants. Two enzyme families, AMY and beta-amylase, are responsible for the hydrolysis of starch. AMY catalyzes the cleavage of alpha-1,4-glucosidic bonds in an endo-type manner. This enzyme is significant in starch degradation; for instance, the suppression of AMY expression in P. persica fruit resulted in a substantial reduction in starch and fruit-cracking [36]. Notably, a significant positive correlation was observed between the levels of two AMYs (LOC103321611 and LOC103321179) and maltose content, indicating that the upregulation of AMY gene expression contributes to increased maltose levels. In contrast to alpha-amylase, BMY functions as an exonuclease, with its enzymatic mechanism involving a glutamic acid-glutamate pair. The overexpression of the BMY gene resulted in reduced starch content in peach [37] and increased maltose content in Arabidopsis [38]. In this study, a significant positive relationship was identified between E3.2.1.2 (beta-amylase, LOC103322923) and maltose content, suggesting that BMY plays a crucial role in maltose accumulation in P. mume. In addition to the action of beta-amylase, the breakdown of granular starch also necessitates the activities of GWD and PWD [39]. During fruit development and ripening, starch is degraded while the sugars are accumulated [40]. Downregulation of GWD or PWD levels in plants results in a similar starch excess phenotype [41], while increasing transcript abundance of GWD occurs during grape berry development [42]. In this study, the increasing expression of GWD (LOC103319462) and PWD (LOC103320724) genes closely aligned with the dynamic improvement in maltose content, with significant positive contributions observed for both. Collectively, these results indicate that maltose regulation in P. mume fruit is predominantly governed by starch degradation, with its accumulation likely orchestrated by the genetic control of these four pivotal candidate genes.

Both sorbitol and sucrose function as translocating sugars in numerous species within the Rosaceae family [43]. It has been extensively documented that three functional genes (e.g., aldose-6-phosphate reductase (A6PR), sorbitol-6-phosphate phosphatase (SorPP), and SDH are primarily responsible for the synthesis and degradation of sorbitol [28, 44]. During the ripening of P. mume fruit, only one differentially expressed gene, SDH (LOC103333266), was identified, which exhibited a positive correlation with sorbitol accumulation. Given that SDH is a rate-limiting enzyme in sorbitol catabolism, its upregulation may lead to a reduction in sorbitol accumulation. The sorbitol biosynthetic pathway initiates from glucose-6-phosphate through glycolysis, as well as the glucose and starch pathways [44]. The potential conversion of sorbitol to fructose may be counterbalanced by the upregulation of several genes, including PFK9 (LOC103327814), GAPDH (LOC103325740), PK (LOC103333690), GD6P (LOC103322104), WAXY (LOC103344039), and AMY (LOC103321611), which could enhance the supply of glucose-6-phosphate and subsequently increase sorbitol accumulation. Similar positive correlation was also observed in pear, where the enhanced activities of GD6P, PFK and PK accompanied with the promoted accumulation of sorbitol [45]. Furthermore, sorbitol can be transported from photosynthetic tissues, and this uptake is reversibly inhibited by hexoses, such as fructose [46]. For P. mume fruit, the relatively low level of fructose suggests that the uptake of sorbitol should not be overlooked. Therefore, the observed increase in sorbitol levels may be attributed to the upregulation of glycolysis, the glucose and starch pathways, as well as the uptake mechanisms.

Identification of candidate pivotal genes involved in organic acid accumulation

During the ripening of P. mume fruit, concentrations of malate and quinic acid exhibited a decline, whereas the concentration of citrate increased. This pattern mirrors the observed changes in malate acid and citrate concentrations in Japanese apricot fruit. PEPC is directly responsible for the oxaloacetate synthesis followed by the condensation of oxaloacetic acid and acetyl-CoA by CS to produce citrate. ACO catalyzes the reversible conversion of citrate to isocitrate [47], which is further metabolized into 2-oxoglutarate by IDH. Previous studies have revealed the positive correlation between PEPC, CS and citric acid in peach, strawberry and Actinidia eriantha [11, 48, 49], as well as the negative relationship between IDH and citrate in peach and kumquat [2, 49]. During ripening of P. mume fruit, one PEPC (LOC103318857) and two CSs (LOC103323169 and LOC103328745) were found to be up-regulated, while one NADP-IDH (LOC103340345) was down-regulated. The up-regulation of PEPC and CS during fruit maturation may be linked to enhanced citrate synthesis, whereas the down-regulation of NADP-IDH could contribute to a reduction in citrate degradation. Furthermore, correlation analysis revealed that PEPC (LOC103318857) and CS (LOC103328745) exhibited a positive correlation with citrate accumulation, while NADP-IDH (LOC103340345) showed a negative correlation with citrate accumulation (Table 6), underscoring their potential roles as regulatory nodes in citrate accumulation.

In the GABA shunt, GDH facilitates the amination of 2-oxoglutarate (a synthetic reaction) or the deamination of glutamate (a catabolic reaction), thereby modulating the direction of the reaction in accordance with metabolic requirements. GAD catalyzes the reversible conversion of glutamate to GABA and carbon dioxide, which represents the primary pathway for GABA biosynthesis [50]. GS catalyzes the synthesis of glutamine from glutamate, ATP and ammonium [11]. According to metabolomic data from P. mume, the concentrations of both glutamate and glutamine decreased by 37.77% and 40.92%, respectively, in comparison to the S1 stage. This reduction suggests that the metabolic flux from 2-oxoglutarate to glutamate and from glutamate to glutamine in the GABA shunt was downregulated. Notably, one GDH (LOC103333008), two GADs (LOC103327380 and LOC103327381) and three GSs (LOC103328723, LOC103344301 and LOC103344275) genes involved in GABA shunt were upregulated, while two GSs (LOC103321068 and LOC103338669) were downregulated that could block the formation of GABA [11]. Consequently, the regulation of both the synthesis and degradation pathways of citrate contributed to its accumulation. Therefore, PEPC (LOC103318857), CS (LOC103328745), NADP-IDH (LOC103340345) and GSs (LOC103321068 and LOC103338669) emerged as potential critical control points for citrate accumulation [2, 11, 16].

Similar to citrate, both the synthesis and degradation processes affect the accumulation of malate. PEPC and NAD-MDH mainly contribute to malate synthesis. In P. salicina and Eriobotrya japonica, the observed differences in malate content may be partially attributed to variations in PEPC activity [51, 52]. In Cerasus species and Eriobotrya japonica, NAD-MDH positively correlates with malate accumulation [51, 53]. In this study, the expression levels of NAD-MDH (LOC103334965) and PEPC (LOC103324100) increased during the early developmental stages, followed by a decline to lower levels at maturity, which was positively correlated with the trend in malate accumulation. These results suggest that the downregulation of NAD-MDH and PEPC may reduce the rate of malate synthesis.

The degradation of malate in fruit is predominantly affected by the activity of two enzyme families: PEPCK and NADP-ME [23]. PEPCK catalyzes the decarboxylation of OAA to PEP, thereby activating the “Pyruvate Carboxylation Shunt” that links organic acid metabolism with gluconeogenesis. In grapes, an increase in PEPCK levels coincides with malate degradation [54]. Notably, one specific PEPCK (LOC103319416) was significantly upregulated during fruit ripening, demonstrating a strong negative correlation with malate accumulation. These results suggest that malate degradation may be a consequence of enhanced gluconeogenesis, which aligns with the gradual increase in the levels of three sugars: sucrose, maltose, and sorbitol.

NADP-ME is another enzyme responsible for the degradation of malate. In P. mume fruit, the expression of NADP-ME (LOC103340826) was found to be upregulated, which was associated with a decrease in malate accumulation. In P. salicina and apple, negative correlation between NADP-ME and malate levels has been reported [52, 55], suggesting that the upregulation of NADP-ME may be responsible for the observed reduction in malate. Consequently, the genes NAD-MDH (LOC103334965), PEPC (LOC103324100), PEPCK (LOC103319416) and NADP-ME (LOC103340826) have been identified as candidate pivotal genes that regulate malate accumulation.

Quinic acid constitutes the third most abundant organic acid in P. mume fruit, accounting for 2.50% of the identified organic acids. Its biosynthesis proceeds via the shikimate pathway, catalyzed sequentially by three enzymes: 3-deoxy-7-phosphoheptulonate synthase (DHS), dehydroquinate synthase (DHQS), and quinate dehydrogenase (QDH) [56](Supplementary Fig. 9). DHS utilizes PEP derived from glycolysis and erythrose 4-phosphate (E4P) sourced from the pentose phosphate pathway as substrates to produce DAHP. Subsequently, DHQS and QDH facilitate the conversion of DAHP to quinic acid [12]. In this study, one differentially expressed DHS (LOC103319557) exhibited a significant correlation with changes in quinic acid concentration, while no variations were observed in the expression of DHQS and QDH. The downregulation of DHS likely attenuates flux through the shikimate pathway [12], and hence LOC103319557 is proposed as a candidate pivotal gene in the metabolism of quinic acid.

Role of plant hormones in fruit ripening

The ripening of fruit, accompanied by the accumulation of organic acids and sugars, is coordinately regulated by a complex signaling network of a variety of plant hormones. The results of the correlation analysis indicated that ABA plays a crucial role in the metabolism of sugars and organic acids during the ripening of P. mume fruit (Supplementary Table 3). ABA was believed to plays an important role in regulating the fruit ripening and senescence in peach [57], and accelerate postharvest softening of prunes (P. domestica) via modulating the cell wall metabolism [58]. Besides, ABA is recognized as one of the most significant hormones associated with plant response to environmental stress [59]. In V. vinifera, the application of exogenous ABA enhanced SUS activity, which was subsequently associated with improved accumulation of sugars and anthocyanins [60]. Furthermore, the application of ABA has been shown to enhance the drought tolerance of wheat by promoting organic acid accumulation [61]. Notably, the simultaneous application of ABA and sucrose synergistically accelerated the ripening of strawberry fruit by enhancing ripening efficiency [4]. In this study, an increase in ABA concentration showed a positive correlation with sucrose, maltose, and sorbitol accumulation. This suggests that ABA not only promotes sugar accumulation but also plays a regulatory role in fruit ripening, either independently or in addition to sugars. Furthermore, ABA exhibited a strong association with citrate accumulation in P. mume fruit (p < 0.05). This result aligns with previous study indicating that exogenous ABA application increases CS activity and increases citrate accumulation [62]. Given that environmental stress poses significant challenges to fruit production and quality, the elevated levels of ABA and citrate in P. mume fruit may collectively enhance adaptability to environmental stressors.

GABA is a non-protein amino acid that plays a significant role in promoting health within the human nervous system, and several GABA-enriched foods have been commercialized [63]. Notably, GABA levels exhibit a rapid decline during the ripening stage of S. lycopersicum [50], which are among the major crops that are rich in GABA. This decrease may be attributed to the conversion of GABA back into the TCA cycle, where it is transformed into citrate using malate. On the contrary, the degradation citrate through GABA shunt in the cytosol was proposed to be an impact on fruit acidity of peach [15]. However, no significant correlation observed between GABA concentration and citrate in P. mume fruit. That strong negative correlation between IAA, JA and tZA, and fruit size and weight suggested that these three plant hormones may play a role during the early ripening period of P. mume fruit (Supplementary Table 3). Liu et al. [38] proposed that tZR is involved in the process of fruit cell division during fruit development. The research results indicate that the concentration of tZR reaches its peak at an early developmental stage (Fig. 3C), which aligns with the results reported in previous study [64]. The observed decline in tZR concentration during the later stages of development may facilitate the balance between fruit overripening and quality loss.

Regulatory network governing sugar and organic acid metabolism

Transcriptional regulatory networks play a crucial role in the spatial and temporal regulation of secondary metabolite biosynthesis. The correlation between TFs and transport carriers likely indicates their regulatory functions in the metabolism and transport of sugars and organic acids, respectively. Based on proposed PPI analysis, the AP2 TF has been identified as a central regulator that negatively affects the accumulation of sugars and organic acids. Plant-specific AP2/ERF TFs possess a conserved AP2/ERF domain, consisting of 60–70 amino acid residues associated with DNA binding [24]. Previous studies have demonstrated that the AP2/ERF TF negatively modulates sugar accumulation, citrate accumulation and fruit size [65–67]. However, AP2 has also been shown to interact with various TFs, such as bHLH, NAC, MYB and WRKY TFs, that are positively correlated with the accumulation of sugars and organic acids, suggesting that it may play a positive role in these processes. Among these, bHLH TFs, characterized by their basic helix-loop-helix structures, play a crucial role in determining sugar contents [10]. NAC TFs play important roles in citrate accumulation and fruit ripening by modulating transcript levels of downstream genes [68, 69]. MYB TFs, recognized as selectively expanded members within the plant kingdom, are integral to regulatory networks that govern tissue development, and organic acid metabolism owing to their capacity to bind to MYB cis-elements in target genes [70]. Notably, the expression of MYB TFs was significantly induced by sucrose in tea trees [71], suggesting a complex interplay between MYB TFs and sugar metabolism. In P. avium, MADS7 TF is an indispensable positive regulator of fruit ripening and softening, soluble sugar accumulation and anthocyanin accumulation [72]. Previous studies in various fruit species have unveiled the TF-mediated regulation is associated with interactions between different TFs, protein kinases and transporters, establishing complex regulatory TF network [68, 73–75]. Consequently, further research on the interactions within the TF network is required to clarify the interactive functions of TFs in the accumulation of sugars and organic acids in P. mume. Furthermore, we also observed interaction between SWEET and TFs and metabolic enzymes, suggesting that sugar transportation might play role in the sugar accumulation in the P. mume fruit. To date, three classes of sugar transporters have been identified in plants: glucose transporters, sodium-glucose symporters, and SWEET. Sugar transporters are responsible for the efflux of sugars across tissues, and their overexpression significantly increase the sucrose content in fruit of P. persica [76].

The analysis of proposed PPI identified six key catalysts (e.g., pgm, HK, CS, transketolase-1 (TKL-1), PEPC, and PK) that are integral to sugar and organic acid metabolism, facilitating the formation of significant enzyme complexes. Among these, CS is posited to play a crucial role in citrate biosynthesis. This CS gene was directedly chosen for sequence and enzymatic validation, offering a target for genetic manipulation aimed at sugar and organic metabolism. The remaining five catalysts exhibited substantial interactions with candidate pivotal enzymes implicated in sugar and organic acid metabolism. For example, pgm was found to interact with seven candidate pivotal enzymes, which are encoded by genes GS (LOC103338669 and LOC103321068), SUS (LOC103323057), CS (LOC103328745), NAD-MDH (LOC103334965), INVs (LOC103337231 and LOC103341755). This suggests that pgm, in addition to other enzymes, may potentially regulate the accumulation of sugar and organic acid through both direct and indirect mechanisms. This result aligns with correlation analyses indicating that pgm exhibits a high correlation (R > 0.8) with three kinds of sugars. The role of pgm has been previously highlighted by Nie et al. [77] who found that PsPGM1 was highly correlated with sucrose accumulation during fruit ripening of P. salicina. Therefore, their interactions may not only manipulate metabolic flux but also facilitate crosstalk among various metabolic pathways.

The transcriptional expression of numerous genes is modulated by the interaction between cis-regulatory elements in their promoters and TFs [78, 79]. To elucidate the regulatory network governing sugar and organic acid metabolism, this study predicted the cis-acting elements present in the promoters of 27 candidate pivotal genes. The results indicate that the majority of these genes are involved in the ABA-dependent signaling cascade (Fig. 7, Supplementary Table 8), although a variety of genes exhibited differential number of ABA-responsive elements. In addition to metabolomic data, transcriptomic data, proposed PPI analysis, and cis-element evaluations conducted in this study, it appears that the metabolism of sugars and organic acids in P. mume may be affected by the increased accumulation of ABA. ABA, a well-known hormone that is associated with fruit ripening, softening and anthocyanin in Prunus species, elicited significant responses in most of the candidate pivotal genes [57, 58, 80, 81]. Consequently, these pivotal proteins likely assembled into complexes that regulate the expression or activity of other proteins, ultimately altering the composition and concentration of sugars and organic acids in the fruit. Notably, it is essential to investigate whether the exogenous application of ABA can modulate the sugar and organic acid contents in P. mume fruit.

Overall, the findings of this study provide basis for potential application in cultivation, breeding and fruit processing of P. mume. First, 27 candidate key genes encoding metabolic enzymes and transcription factors were predicted through integrated metabolome and transcriptome. These genes represent promising targets for genetic modification to modulate the accumulation of sugars and organic acids in P. mume. For instance, CRISPR-Cas-mediated gene editing technology is hopeful to manage the expression of AP2 gene or the identified CS gene, thereby potentially manipulating the metabolism of sugars and organic acids in P. mume. Second, molecular marker-assisted breeding has been widely utilized to develop excellent farming germplasms. These candidate genes might expand the pool of molecular markers for breeding high-quality P. mume germplasms. Third, given the positive correlation observed between ABA and the accumulation of sugars and organic acids during fruit ripening, it is plausible that exogenous application of ABA or ABA analogs might enhance the production of sugars and organic acids during fruit ripening and (or) postharvest process. This is supported by Zaharah et al. [82], who found that exogenous application of ABA on mango fruit triggered accumulation of sugars during postharvest process. Alternatively, genetic modification on the ABA-related signaling pathway may offer a strategy to elevate the endogenous ABA content. For instance, Bastías et al. [83] demonstrated that overexpression of an ABA-regulated transcription factor conferred to higher content of sugars and organic acids in tomato fruit by upregulating the expression of INV and SUS genes.

Conclusion

This study conducted the first integrated metabolomic and transcriptomic analysis on fruits of P. mume across various ripening stages, providing insights into pivotal genes related to the sugar and organic acid metabolism. The results indicated that sucrose and citrate were the predominant sugar and organic acid, with their accumulation likely regulated by ABA. The accumulation of sugars and organic acids was linked to a variety of metabolic pathways, including TCA, GABA shunt, glycolysis, gluconeogenesis, starch degradation, sucrose hydrolysis, and as well as the shikimate pathway. Genes encoding pivotal metabolic enzymes for three major sugars and organic acids were identified, underscoring the need for further functional validation of these candidate genes. Besides, a regulatory network incorporating metabolic enzymes, TFs and transport carriers was constructed to elucidate the complex metabolic regulation, leading to the prediction of six additional candidate pivotal genes. These candidate pivotal genes were strongly proposed to function as key nodes within this network, offering potential targets for genetic manipulation aimed at modulating sugar and organic acid metabolism. Finally, ABA was proposed as a core switch point triggering the sugar and organic acid accumulation by eliciting the response of candidate pivotal genes, and hence it is hopeful to regulate sugar and organic acid contents by exogenous ABA application. In summary, these proposed pivotal genes may be developed into novel gene biomarkers for fruit quality.
