An exploratory study to determine the feasibility and processes needed to implement the Immunization Neighborhood to increase HPV vaccination among African American adolescents and adolescents in the general population: A multi-partner perspective
Authors: Jennifer Cunningham-Erves, Freneka Minter, Pamela C. Hull, Amanda F. Dempsey, Alexandria DeMons, Mariya Chalise, Consuelo H. Wilkins
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTLimited education and healthcare access are major contributors to Human Papillomavirus (HPV) vaccination disparities among African American adolescents. The Immunization Neighborhood (IN) involves enhanced communication and collaboration among providers to address people’s needs by expanding access points and education to increase vaccination. Because needs of immunization providers and parents to effectively collaborate are unknown, this study explored the feasibility of and processes needed for IN utilization across key partners: pediatricians, pharmacists, school nurses, health department staff, and African American parent–child dyads. We conducted a semi-structured interviews in a qualitative, descriptive study with a purposive sample of 30 African American parent–child dyads and 37 immunization providers in Middle Tennessee. The Consolidated Framework for Implementation Research (CFIR) guided inductive-deductive thematic analysis. We identified facilitators and barriers to IN utilization for four CFIR domains: (1) Intervention characteristics; (2) Outer setting; (3) Inner setting; and (4) Individual characteristics. Across CFIR domains and partners, key perceived facilitators include convenience, accessibility, extended hours, partner coordination, and existing subsidized programs. Perceived barriers included fragmented vaccination tracking, limited vaccine registry interoperability, existing policies, and lack of knowledge and trust in immunization providers and settings. Our study highlights the need for multi-component, collaborative, and patient-centered strategies for IN implementation to improve adolescent HPV vaccination rates, especially among African American adolescents. Policy changes to reduce out-of-pocket expenses, increase insurance coverage, require reporting to state immunization registries, encourage collaborative practices, and allow HPV vaccine delivery in schools could potentially enhance IN impact.

Introduction

Minoritized and underserved populations suffer disproportionately from HPV and associated cancer outcomes, resulting in a substantial health disparity. The highest prevalence of HPV infection, oral and genital, is among African Americans.1–4 For HPV cancers, African American women continue to bear the brunt of cervical cancer with a mortality rate of 10.1 per 100,000 compared to 4.7 per 100,000 for non-Hispanic White women5 after the removal of women who had a prior hysterectomy. African Americans also have a significantly shorter overall survival for oropharyngeal cancer at 39% compared to 65% for non-Hispanic Whites,6–9 based on 2010 to 2016 patient data in the National Cancer Database, regardless of healthcare access.10 These rates primarily reflect providers’ barriers (e.g., lack of patient-centered care), socio-demographic factors,11–17 and patients presenting for care at later, incurable stages.

The HPV vaccine, safe and effective, prevents the most common high-risk HPV types causing 93% of HPV-associated cancers. The two-dose HPV vaccination series is recommended for 9–14 y, and a three-dose series for 15–26 y.18 In 2024, U.S. initiation and completion rates for all adolescents aged 13–17 y are both below the national targets of 80%,19 78.2% for initiation, and 62.9% for completion.20 Despite African Americans’ initiation rates surpassing the national rate at 78.3%, completion rates were 59.0%.20 Major contributors to African American adolescents being significantly less likely to complete HPV vaccination include healthcare access barriers, provider recommendation and communication gaps, and lack of knowledge and misinformation on HPV and vaccination.21 Furthermore, the COVID-19 pandemic led to a 71%22 drop in healthcare visits among 7–17-y-olds, with HPV vaccination rates falling by 75% in early stages. The National HPV Roundtable and National Cancer Institute-designated Cancer Centers are urging HPV vaccinations to get back on track.23 Collaborative efforts among immunization providers, within the medical home and alternative, non-traditional settings, are necessary to improve HPV vaccine education and access.24

The term Immunization Neighborhood (IN), coined by the American Pharmacists Association (APhA) and the National Adult and Influenza Immunization Summit, describes the “collaboration, coordination, and communication among immunization partners dedicated to meeting the immunization needs of the patient and protecting the community from vaccine-preventable diseases.” The IN can enhance access and convenience, reduce missed opportunities, and address lack of knowledge, suboptimal or lack of recommendations, and low parental confidence.25 Existing literature has not adequately explored why current delivery models have failed to or identified needs of immunization providers to effectively collaborate to improve HPV vaccination among adolescents in general and reduce disparities among African American adolescents. Nor has it sufficiently incorporated multi-stakeholder perspectives using an implementation framework. Studies have primarily explored views toward,26–35 or tested, interventions within individual alternative settings, such as pharmacies, schools, and health departments, to increase HPV vaccination.29–41 For example, Vercruysse et al.42 found that a school-based HPV vaccination clinic was acceptable for most providers and parents. Among parents with children aged 11–17 y, only 29% were willing to vaccinate their child at a pharmacy, with predictors being the perception of pharmacists being skilled to provide the vaccine, pharmacies offering healthcare services for children, such as walk-in, having an older adolescent, or needing to complete the series. Emerging studies have characterized partnerships among immunization providers, with school and health departments primarily partnering with healthcare physicians.43 Though few in number, studies have explored views toward collaboration between two immunization providers44 and determined their actual impact.45,46

This qualitative study explores the feasibility and processes needed for the utilization of the Immunization Neighborhood across providers (pediatricians, pharmacists, school nurses, and health department staff) and African American parent–child dyads to maximize HPV vaccination education and access to increase rates among adolescents, especially those who are African American.

Materials and methods

Study design and setting

We used a qualitative, descriptive study design to conduct semi-structured interviews among 30 African American parent–child dyads and 37 immunization providers (i.e., pharmacists, pediatricians, school nurses, and health department staff) located in Middle Tennessee, where HPV vaccination completion received by adolescents is suboptimal at 54.3%20 compared to the National Healthy People 2030 target of 80%.19 This study was conducted between August 2016 and May 2019. Meharry Medical College Institutional Review Board (Protocol #:16-06-569) approved this study. COREQ guidelines were used for reporting this qualitative research.47

The original Consolidated Framework for Implementation Research (CFIR)48 guided the data analysis. Sample and recruitment

We used a purposive sampling method to recruit African American parent–child dyads and immunization providers. Parent inclusion criteria were as follows: (1) female or male, (2) English-speaking, (3) have, care for, or make medical decisions for a male or female adolescent aged 11 to 17, and (4) African American. Adolescent inclusion criteria were as follows: (1) female or male; (2) English-speaking; (3) aged 11 to 17; (4) African American; and (5) parental consent. Immunization provider inclusion criteria were as follows: (1) a pediatrician or nurse practitioner, Coordinated School Health Director or School Health Services Director, pharmacist, or health department personnel (e.g., administrator, nurse practitioner, or physician assistant); (2) able to or in the process of being able to administer the HPV vaccine to adolescents ages 11 to 17; (3) English-speaking; and (4) male or female. These provider types were chosen as they are the primary providers of children’s immunizations. The goal was to interview a minimum of 10 interviews per category as this study was exploratory. Guest et al. states that coding saturation is usually met within the first 6–12 interviews, although 12 interviews are needed to meet thematic saturation.49

Parent–child dyads were recruited from community organizations (e.g., schools, children’s sports events, churches), clinical settings (e.g., pediatric clinic waiting rooms), health fairs, online listservs at Meharry Medical College and Vanderbilt University (VU), using flyers and e-mails. ResearchMatch (RM),50 a national recruitment registry, was also used to contact parents. Immunization providers were recruited using professional organizations, e-mail, word-of-mouth, and phone.

Data collection

Study protocol

One researcher (Lead author J.C.E.) and two graduate students (K.K.T. and J.A.D.) conducted the interviews. The lead researcher, an expert in qualitative research, trained graduate-level students in qualitative research methods for interview consistency. Parent–child interviews were conducted in-person: (1) in separate rooms, or (2) the child did their interview first and then the parent. Parental influence was reduced by asking parents to sit behind the child and remain quiet during the interview. Provider interviews were conducted via phone. Before the audio-recorded interview, parents and immunization providers provided electronic consent and took a brief socio-demographic survey using REDCap.51,52 For example, participants were about asked their willingness to get (i.e., parents) or collaborate (i.e., providers) with each of the alternative settings for adolescent HPV vaccination (response options: “yes” or “no”). For adolescents, parents provided consent, and then adolescents provided assent. Interviews lasted 30–60 min for parents and immunization providers and 15–30 min for adolescents. Parents and immunization providers received a $50 gift card and adolescents a $20 gift card. Commercial transcriptions were assigned unique identification numbers and checked for accuracy by graduate students.

Interview protocol

Initial interview protocols for each partner were developed using existing literature and researcher experiences. They were iteratively adapted over 6 months using feedback from members of the HPV Cancer Free TN Coalition, formally Cervical Cancer Free TN.53 This coalition is comprised of local, community-based partners to address cervical cancer screening and HPV vaccination for all eligible Tennesseans. Topics of each partner protocol were loosely mapped to CFIR domains, allowing us to ask relevant questions for each domain while exploring emerging issues. Data analyses

Descriptive statistics (frequencies and percentages) were conducted to describe participants’ socio-demographics. Led by a PhD-level psychologist, the Vanderbilt University Medical Center Qualitative Research Core conducted qualitative data analyses. Two transcripts of each immunization partner were initially independently coded by two coders guided by CFIR and IN concept. To establish reliability in the codebook, transcripts were reviewed, and any discrepancies were reconciled by discussion until consensus was reached, as determined via the coders and supervising faculty.54 The remaining transcripts were divided between the two coders, and each set of transcripts coded separately to better understand the views and experiences of each partner.55

Line-by-line transcript coding was conducted, and codes were compared between the two coders until coding saturation was reached.49 Iterative inductive/deductive approach56,57 using comparative analysis was used to combine codes and form high-order themes. Rigor was established using thick, rich descriptions. Microsoft Excel 2016 and SPSS version 29 were used to manage and analyze data.

Rigor58

The trustworthiness of the findings was established using the criteria credibility, transferability, dependability, and conformability.59 Credibility was established with study oversight by a trained qualitative researcher who worked with other qualitative and research experts to develop the interview guide content to represent the study phenomenon. There was data and investigator triangulation. Data triangulation was done via data collection across four immunization providers and parent–child dyads, which were compared across partners and with the literature. Investigator triangulation occurred with three coders who conducted data analysis independently and then reached a consensus via discussion. Transferability was determined via thick, rich descriptions of the research process and findings for study replication. For thick rich descriptions, we used probes for depth. Themes were summarized separately and subsequently. The research protocol including activities (i.e., data collection and analysis) have been archived.

For dependability, preliminary findings were reviewed by the study research team, and interpretations that did not align with the data were corrected. Conformability was established via objectivity and reflexivity. For objectivity, the coders acknowledged and reserved their biases while following strict, standardized analysis procedures to detach the coders from the study phenomenon. For methodological reflexivity, the researchers chose the semi-structured interviews as they acknowledge the sensitivity around the topic and the need to offer a “safe” space for individuals to discuss their personal views. For ethical challenges, findings were reviewed to ensure participant views were represented anonymously while minimizing harm during data collection and presentation.

Results

Socio-demographics

Study participants were 30 African American parent–child dyads and 37 immunization providers (9 physicians, 10 school nurses, 10 health department staff, and 8 pharmacists). Among African American parents, the majority were female (93.3%) with most having a college degree or higher (58.7%) and a mean age of 46 y. There was almost an equal distribution by gender and HPV vaccination status among the African American children, with a mean age of 14 y. Among providers, the majority (73.0%) were female. Race distribution varied among physicians (44.4% African American, 44.4% Caucasian, and 11.1% Asian), yet the majority of school nurses (90%), health department staff (90%), and pharmacies (62.5%) were Caucasian.

Willingness to collaborate

Willingness to use alternative settings (i.e., parents) or partner with other providers was almost equally distributed across participants. However, health department staff stated low levels of willingness (30%) to partner with pharmacists, and only 30% of parents were willing to use schools for their child’s vaccination. Domain 1: intervention characteristics

Facilitators

In our study, relative advantage, trialability, perceived costs, evidence-based patient education, and intervention source were perceived as facilitators for IN utilization.

Relative advantage

The convenience of pharmacies and schools was emphasized across all partners as a relative advantage in IN utilization for HPV vaccination. Most pharmacies have multiple locations with extended hours and allow walk-ins, increasing parental vaccination access. “It is just easier to go to the pharmacy … they are more open and do not have to have an appointment. They can go at their convenience more so than the doctor’s office,” a pharmacist stated. Schools were also considered geographically convenient and successful in increasing vaccine access for many children, allowing parents or children to avoid missing work or school. A school nurse highlighted, “The convenience of it has been a huge factor because we try to make it as easy as possible for the parents. Additional benefits were increased “touchpoints” to address parental hesitations and misinformation on the vaccine.

A few pharmacists viewed the potential collaboration as a “game changer” to also increase access to all childhood immunizations. A few school nurses also perceived that IN utilization could “offer broader and better healthcare.” Increased convenience financially due to health departments assistance programs was an advantage stated by most health department staff.

Trialability

To support the trialability of IN utilization, immunization providers perceived appropriate training was necessary to build experience and ease concerns. Additionally, practice agreements and access to and use of immunization registries (e.g., Tennessee Immunization Information System (TennIIS)) across providers were stated to facilitate tracking immunization rates (e.g., HPV vaccination) while supporting adolescents’ continuity of care. A pediatrician stated, “Clinics with access to state immunization registries, like Tennessee’s TennIIS, facilitate tracking vaccination status.”

Perceived cost

Pharmacists and health department staff described how IN utilization could support some providers’ operating and infrastructure limitations, offering the vaccine and/or providing it at lower costs or for free. “We are a 340B clinic … we are able to offer our shots at lower prices as compared to some of your standalone pharmacies … some of this stuff is free, and that may be another reason why they [pediatricians] send people here,” a pharmacist stated. A health department staff member stated, “Parents get adolescent vaccines at the health department because it is less expensive. Pediatricians do not offer the vaccines because some do not qualify or participate in the VFC program.” Another health department staff member explained, “Some parents can’t afford to take time off work to bring their children to the clinic, and others may not have insurance coverage for certain vaccines.” Additionally, pharmacists described how they offer real-time costs and billing to help reduce parental financial barriers.

Evidence-based patient education

Many pharmacists, pediatricians, and health department staff emphasized the importance of and could provide evidence-based education on HPV vaccination (e.g., cancer prevention, safety) to counter HPV vaccine misinformation, reduce hesitancy, and potentially increase acceptance. A pediatrician described his experience stating, “There have been studies and information shared that this vaccine prevents cancer. Parents respond positively to hearing about its benefits from a trusted provider.” A health department staff member mentioned, “Proactive education during visits addresses patient and family concerns, helping them feel more comfortable with vaccination decisions.” The idea of multiple sources providing the same messaging would be beneficial was stated by a health department staff member.

Intervention source

Providers either highlighted how they could collaborate to build the IN and utilize it collectively or with specific providers, especially if providers could not stock vaccines in their own practice. Two pharmacists discussed needing to establish a “collaborative practice” across providers with access to patient records. One pharmacist mentioned, “Collaboration with pediatric providers and health departments is essential. A collaborative pharmacy practice agreement could help improve coordination and communication.” Another pharmacist highlighted, “Collaborative practice agreements between pharmacies and healthcare providers can strengthen the healthcare chain.”

Many providers further perceived establishing partnerships to mitigate logistical barriers. A pharmacist said, “I think the best thing that we could do would be to have a collaborative care practice that would allow us to do whatever needed to be done when the patient came in. And have access to all their information as well between the doctor’s office and here. So then when they’ve had a vaccine or not.” A pediatrician emphasized, “The Immunization Neighborhood concept, with collaboration and coordination among stakeholders, could help improve access and avoid duplication of vaccinations.”

Barriers

Potential barriers to IN utilization for HPV vaccination include complexity, actual cost of vaccination delivery, and the intervention source.

Complexity

EHR (electronic health record) interoperability challenges, especially the inability to access TennIIS (state immunization registry), lack of an EHR, or not being required to report to TennIIS, were concerns across providers. One pharmacist did not have an EHR. “Honestly, the vaccine program has not been very giving of pharmacists’ access to the TennIIS system. The access to it is very limited, and I think that’s a barrier,” a pharmacist stated. A physician further elaborated, “We lose tracking of patients when they get vaccines elsewhere, especially at places like MinuteClinics or pharmacies, making it hard to verify completion.” This was especially concerning since adolescents need to complete a 2-dose or 3-dose series.

Logistical barriers were identified across IN providers. A school nurse highlighted needing parental consent, scheduling around school activities, and vaccine storage issues. A pharmacist mentioned the need for parental consent and school board consent to provide the vaccine in schools. Health department staff discussed suboptimal parental follow-up to appointment reminders for multi-dose vaccines like HPV vaccination.

Actual cost

Some providers highlight that the costs of providing HPV vaccination could negatively impact IN utilization. Pharmacies often face low or inconsistent reimbursement, making it difficult to offer certain vaccines at a sustainable cost. “Sometimes insurance companies are not paying for them, and sometimes those patients that are really in need are underinsured,” a pharmacist stated. Pediatricians, especially those in private practice, report the financial burden and the risk of losing money on unused doses. A pediatrician said, “We do not stock vaccines like HPV due to the financial risk of wastage; losing tens of thousands on unused vaccines in the past has made it unsustainable.” The cost of storing and managing vaccine stock were barriers. A school nurse stated, “I do see that as a barrier, especially for schools to store that and be responsible for vaccines that have to be kept at a certain temperature, monitored, checked.” This led some pediatricians and pharmacists to refer patients elsewhere for vaccinations and could limit their participation in providing the vaccination.

Intervention source

Participants across groups, primarily pharmacists and physicians, highlighted a lack of coordination, indirectly influenced by competition, as an IN utilization barrier. A pharmacist stated, “If you are not doing it in conjunction with a practitioner’s office, I think that they could see that as a threat or competition.” Many physicians were also concerned that expanding vaccination services in pharmacies could weaken the medical home by changing primary care providers as the first point of contact and disrupting continuity of care.

Outer setting

Facilitators

Outer setting facilitators for IN utilization include focusing on the patient’s needs and resources, as well as external policies and incentives.

Patient needs & resources

All providers emphasized that IN utilization could increase vaccination access geographically and financially for some patients. A health department staff member described how they provide low-cost or free vaccines, “Parents get adolescent vaccines at the health department because it is less expensive.” Even a pediatrician mentioned, “For uninsured patients, the health department and VFC program offer vaccinations at discounted or no cost, which ensures financial barriers do not prevent immunization.” Providers also recognized how the convenience of pharmacies’ and school-based vaccination programs could help parents access childhood vaccinations. A school nurse stated, “Having a school-based clinic or special vaccination day would be helpful for that to get them all to come out at once and get their vaccinations.” They also perceived IN utilization as efficient, a way to build parental vaccine confidence and a solution to logistical access barriers.

External policies and incentives

Policies requiring vaccination reporting by IN providers to state immunization registries (e.g., TennIIS) were deemed critical for accurate tracking. A health department staff member mentioned, “The requirement to enter immunizations into TennIIS ensures a centralized record, aiding in tracking and preventing duplicate or missed vaccinations.”

Barriers

Outer setting barriers include external policies and incentives, along with patients’ needs and resources.

External policies and incentives

Providers across settings highlighted the possibility for HPV vaccination school mandates to increase for parental compliance with the IN-provider recommendations and potentially lower hesitancy. A pediatrician indicated, “School vaccination requirements help overcome barriers by mandating certain vaccines, which reduces resistance from parents for those specific vaccines.” A health department staff member further reflected on existing yet effective IN mandates that support an HPV vaccination mandate. She stated, “Tdap is required for seventh-grade entry, which helps drive compliance. The threat of exclusion from school motivates parents to ensure their children are vaccinated.”

Patient needs & resources

Poor vaccine confidence and logistical barriers can negatively impact IN utilization.

Parental Vaccine Confidence

Providers perceived that a lack of or limited knowledge and misinformation heavily influenced parental vaccine confidence, subsequently impacting uptake in the IN. A physician stated, “They read a lot of information, some misinformation talking about vaccines and themselves, and they are wondering what germs are we putting into their children and how is that going to affect them?” A health department staff highlighted, “Parents believe the flu vaccine causes illness or that the HPV vaccine is unnecessary or unsafe due to misinformation from social media and the internet.”

A few providers also realized a lack of parental confidence in or mistrust of IN providers in general and in alternative settings, especially among African American parents, which could complicate efforts. A school nurse stated, “Some parents do not trust school-based providers because they assume they are less qualified than community providers, even though they often have the same credentials.” A pharmacist affirmed, stating that “There’s always been this issue with trust, especially among African Americans, because of past medical injustices.” A health department staff member mentioned, “Deep-seated biases, particularly among African American communities, lead to skepticism of vaccines due to historical mistrust in healthcare.”

Logistical barriers

Health department staff and school nurses highlighted transportation and location as negatively impacting vaccination access or timely vaccinations among adolescents. A health department staff shared, “Many families in rural areas struggle to access healthcare services due to transportation issues and long distances to the nearest clinic.” Another health department staff member revealed, “Transportation issues are a significant barrier for patients coming back for follow-up doses. Parents struggle with work schedules or finding rides to the clinic.” A school nurse stated, “Transportation isn’t a problem for some families, but for others, It is a significant barrier, especially in rural or low-income communities.” Another school nurse mentioned, “There are a lot of homes that do not have transportation, period. They do not have a vehicle, and there are single-vehicle families, which makes it difficult for them to attend appointments.”

Domain 2: inner setting

Facilitators

Inner setting facilitators include readiness for implementation (e.g., infrastructure and collaborations across healthcare settings) and implementation climate (e.g., learning and supportive climate).

Readiness for implementation: available resources

Access to resources (i.e., knowledgeable staff, systems, and storage refrigerators) to educate about and provide vaccinations was stated to support IN implementation. Health department staff stated they had proactive knowledgeable staff educating parents on vaccines (e.g., HPV). A school nurse mentioned, “We have a school nurse in every school, so we could literally have clinics at every school that could function.” A pediatrician noted their “system allows us to check the state registry for vaccination history, ensuring we can follow up on patients’ vaccine completion even if they visit other clinics.” Another school nurse indicated, “every school nurse health office has a refrigerator, and the nurses check the temperature daily to make sure It is within the guidelines for medication storage.”

Implementation climate: compatibility

A few providers perceived a collaborative culture within and across alternative settings within the IN enables facile implementation of vaccination programs that increase access and reduce effort duplication. A health department staff member mentioned, “We collaborate with regional offices to receive lists of patients needing vaccines, which supports proactive outreach.” A school nurse indicated, “Partnerships with the health department and local hospitals have been key in implementing successful vaccination programs.” Pediatricians also mentioned that some clinics have established partnerships with pharmacies and health departments to refer, even if the clinic does not stock certain vaccines.

Many health departments, pediatricians, and pharmacies had EHRs integrated with state immunization systems (e.g., TennIIS), facilitating vaccine tracking and follow-up for patients across different providers. This readiness for implementation, supported by the necessary technology and infrastructure, enhances the efficiency and accuracy of vaccine delivery. Both schools and health department staff collaborated with external partners (e.g., pharmacies or community health clinics) to provide additional resources (e.g., vaccines, storage, and trained staff) when needed and expertise for vaccine administration.

Implementation climate: learning climate

Access to ongoing provider training and education is an inner setting facilitator perceived needed to stay up-to-date on vaccination guidelines, best practices, and strategies to engage patients effectively in the IN. Some providers described their existing training that could be used in the IN. A health department staff member stated, “We train our nurses to reframe HPV as a cancer prevention vaccine, which has been key in addressing parental concerns and improving uptake.” Another one indicated, “Our staff are encouraged to educate parents using the latest guidelines and evidence, fostering a supportive and informed work environment.”

Many pharmacists and health department staff indicated a supportive climate that encourages learning, adaptation, and shared goals among staff would be helpful in IN utilization. For example, they discussed their facilities have protocols for patient follow-up to ensure vaccine series completion (e.g., reminders for second and third HPV doses). This type of climate was perceived as fostering accountability through systematic tracking of vaccinations, thereby improving completion rates.

Barriers

An inner setting barrier includes a poor implementation climate due to lack of resources and organizational support.

Implementation climate: compatibility

Though viewed as beneficial, providers across settings perceived IN utilization as a threat to medical providers’ autonomy in providing HPV vaccination to adolescents. Concerns about competition and medical home preference could make IN utilization counterproductive efforts. Some pediatricians worried it might disrupt the child’s primary care medical home, leading to fragmented, duplicated, substandard, or uncoordinated care (e.g., failing to report back to the medical home). One pediatrician described, “That’s not going to be beneficial. Like I said, also, if the school system does all the vaccines, then they ought to be the kids’ medical home. If you want to do that, that is fine, but again, when you start fracturing the care, it does make it more difficult.” A few school nurses acknowledged some parents preferred vaccinations in a doctor’s office and were uncomfortable with school-based vaccinations, especially without their presence. One school nurse mentioned, “There’s resistance from parents who believe vaccines should be administered in a doctor’s office rather than at school,” and another indicated, “Parents are hesitant and uncertain about having vaccinations done at school without them being present.”

Implementation climate: relative priority

Many providers stated that limited coordination complicates vaccine tracking and negatively impacts their ability to deliver vaccines accurately, further fragmenting care. A health department staff member mentioned, “Coordination between different providers is often lacking, leading to gaps in vaccination records and missed opportunities for immunizations.”

Some pharmacists’ lack of access to TennIIS was cited as a critical barrier. One stated, “Pharmacies often do not have access to the same immunization records as healthcare providers, which can lead to duplication or missed doses.” A pediatrician expressed, “We occasionally encounter issues with getting accurate vaccination records from schools and pharmacies, which complicates our ability to provide comprehensive care.” Another pediatrician indicated, “The lack of a unified system for tracking immunizations across different providers leads to fragmented care and potential gaps in vaccination coverage.” Logistical barriers related to system integration compatibility were perceived to further complicate coordination.

Implementation climate: organizational barriers

Providers noted limited resources and fragmented organizational processes that could hinder IN utilization. Pharmacists reported difficulty accessing the state immunization tracking system. A pharmacist described, “The vaccine program has not been very giving of pharmacists’ access to the TennIIS system. The access to it is very limited, and I think that is a barrier.” A few pediatricians also faced challenges in tracking patients’ vaccinations after referring them to other providers. “Documentation isn’t always shared between outside clinics and the medical home, which creates confusion about whether patients have received their vaccines.,” a pediatrician stated. While IN utilization could help providers with financial or storage limitations, they acknowledged these issues could be barriers. A pediatrician highlighted, “In our organization, not all clinics are equipped to store or administer vaccines, which forces us to refer patients to other locations, creating logistical challenges.” Schools also face logistical hurdles, including limited refrigeration for storing vaccines and liability and safety concerns. A school nurse indicated, “I do see that as a barrier, especially for schools to store that and be responsible for vaccines that have to be kept at a certain temperature, monitored, checked.”

Domain 4: individual characteristics

Immunization providers and African American parents and their children identified facilitators and barriers to IN utilization based on individual characteristics.

Provider characteristics

Facilitators

Provider characteristics identified to facilitate IN use were their IN knowledge and beliefs.

Knowledge and beliefs

A few providers have demonstrated a clear understanding of IN utilization benefits. A health department staff stated, “Well, yes, I’m very familiar with that concept. And the Immunization Neighborhood to me is the network of various places where patients can access immunizations that are recommended for him or her.” Pediatricians saw IN utilization as having many pathways for care and should be communicated to others. One pediatrician indicated, “So, when you talk about an Immunization Neighborhood, where I guess you have lots of pathways maybe to get something done, and making sure that It is communicated back to the people that need to know.”

Barriers

Providers’ lack of knowledge and/or perceptions of the IN and the perceived stages of change for use may impede its utilization.

Knowledge and beliefs

Most providers were unfamiliar with the IN concept but viewed it as beneficial once explained. A health department staff member said, “I have not heard of the concept, but I think I can sort of understand what it means based on what we have done here at the health department.” A school nurse added, “I’ve never called it or heard it Immunization Neighborhood, but that’s basically what we’re doing. Collaborating and partnering together for the greater good of the community, to get everyone vaccinated.” Some noted it was necessary to educate providers about the IN to increase acceptance.

Stages of change (perceived)

Some providers were not ready for IN utilization, primarily based on past experiences, external policies, or overall perceptions about the provider(s). A health department staff member stated, “Some providers are hesitant to share immunization records with us, citing privacy concerns or lack of trust in our systems.” A pharmacist mentioned, “Not all healthcare providers are open to partnering with pharmacies for vaccination initiatives, which limits our ability to reach more patients.” A pediatrician stated, “Some healthcare providers are not willing to collaborate with pharmacies, viewing them as competitors rather than partners in patient care.” A school nurse commented, “Limited willingness to collaborate with health departments can stem from past negative experiences or differing organizational goals.”

Parent-child characteristics

Facilitators

Knowledge and beliefs about IN concept: parent perspective

While parents preferred their child’s provider, many acknowledged the benefits of using alternative settings in the IN to complete the HPV vaccine series. One parent stated, “Actually, we’ve been several times because we get in and out, and they [pharmacists] seem knowledgeable. No issues with them. That will be my second choice.” Parents appreciated pharmacies for shorter wait times, convenient hours, and perceived expertise. A parent conveyed, “I feel like pharmacists do have that knowledge about the vaccines because It is a pharmacy. They are supposed to be informed about the vaccines that they are handling and be able to inform on the side effects and things. If you have questions, they’ll be qualified to answer a question or should be, in my opinion.” Similarly, parents viewed the health department as offering quick and efficient visits.

When asked about receiving HPV and oral cancer information and education from their child’s dentist, most parents were open to receiving it, and many expressed strong interest. One parent indicated, “Yeah, I would because I didn’t realize it until we sat down here that the dentist could be involved with HPV, as it relates to maybe oral health. So yeah, because there again, that I chose this person, I’ve established this relationship, I’m willing to receive information.” Parents preferred multiple formats for receiving information (e.g., pamphlets or brochures, visual aids (videos and posters), and one-on-one conversations). A parent expressed, “I do want a brochure that I can look at. I do want to have a website … visuals in the office … if he [dentist] has a video that he wants to show about the vaccine or about oral cancer … Just sometimes it helps to see what happens … ”

Knowledge and beliefs about IN concept: child perspective

Children were mainly open to HPV vaccination completion at pharmacies, as it was “convenient,” “quick,” and “informative.” A child mentioned her experience with a pharmacist, “It was quick and easy, it was clean, they had plenty of brochures and plenty of posters that were informative, and she was able to answer all my questions because she did not have a whole thing of patients waiting for her.” Although unfamiliar with the health department, most children were willing to receive vaccinations there once it was explained, although unsure of the providers. Many children were open to receiving oral cancer and HPV information from their dentist, if clear, accessible, and in multiple formats to address different learning styles (e.g., videos in the waiting room, pamphlets to take home, or one-on-one discussions). One child mentioned, “I feel like It is a good thing because there’s never anything bad about spreading information about something life-threatening.”

Barriers

Knowledge and beliefs about IN concept: parent perspective

Overall, many parents preferred their child initiate and complete HPV vaccination with their regular provider, citing comfort, continuity of care, and established relationships. Use of alternative settings in the IN was concerning being unaware of providers’ credentials and legitimacy, especially among pharmacists, along with lack of continuity of care and difficulty in tracking immunizations. A parent stated, “My thought about that person [pharmacist] you know is make sure that they are qualified to do it. You know, you do not know who they are.” Others expressed privacy concerns and perceived pharmacies to be more business-oriented than patient-centered. A parent wanted to ensure “That who I’m going to is not going to shuffle me around like just another number.”

Many parents distrusted the health department due to its government affiliation and impersonal environment. They felt “just like a number” and found the environment unwelcoming. Most parents opposed school-based vaccinations, citing concerns about cleanliness, inconsistent staff, and inadequate care for adverse reactions. One parent expressed, “I would not want to be bothered with the school setting because It is not always clean, and there are many germs. I have even seen school officials neglect hygiene, so I would not trust it as a clean environment.” They also worried offering vaccines could disrupt school. Parents had mixed feelings or were uninterested in vaccine information from dentists, but preferred information from other healthcare providers as they were cautious and trustworthy.

Knowledge and beliefs about IN concept: child perspective

Most children were unfamiliar with the credentials and processes of alternative immunization providers in the IN. One stated, “Because I do not necessarily know what they do at a health department, and it kind of sounds terrifying, no, I do not like the idea of doing that. I think It is like weird. I do not know why DHS pops in my brain, like health department, like the place where you get food stamps at. That is what I think of when you say “health department” for some reason. I do not know. I’ve never been to one. I guess I cannot really answer it.” Children also highlighted lack of opportunities for queries to increase comfort. Many were unaware that dentists could provide HPV and oral cancer information. One indicated, “I didn’t know dentists had that kind of information. I thought they only knew about oral cancer, you know, of the mouth.” Another mentioned, “I did not know that. I just went to the dentist the other day, on Monday, so I feel like they did not tell me anything – except for I had two cavities – and I wish they would have told me that and I never knew that you could even get mouth cancer or something like that.”

Discussion

To our knowledge, this is the first study to explore the feasibility and potential processes for IN utilization to increase HPV vaccination among adolescents in general and those who are African American from a multi-partner, implementation-focused perspective. We chose CFIR, a commonly used implementation research framework60,61 in prior vaccination implementation studies to present the findings.

Alternative vaccination settings, such as pharmacies, schools, and health departments, emerged in our study as acceptable IN settings to increase HPV vaccine accessibility. Consistent with past studies,30,62–65 alternative settings were perceived to increase uptake due to walk-in availability, improving financial access, extended hours, location, and education opportunities. This reinforces that IN utilization supports HPV vaccination equity through geographic and systemic expansion, addressing persistent barriers within traditional medical homes and individual alternative settings. The observed finding may, in part, explain the findings of HPV vaccination rates being higher in alternative settings compared to traditional clinics.64,66,67 For example, past studies demonstrate use of mobile health clinics in vaccine delivery by health departments as an efficacious approach for hard-to-reach and medically underserved populations.66,68

Subsidized vaccine programs demonstrate substantial success in individual immunization programs via reducing out-of-pocket expenditures for uninsured and/or underinsured parents.64,69 Our research supports access to these programs (e.g., 340B Drug Pricing Program and Vaccine for Children [VFC]) could address structural barriers that could negatively impact IN implementation. Efforts are underway to support expansion of these programs into alternative settings (i.e., pharmacies) to further improve HPV vaccination coverage.65,70 Moreover, our study overwhelmingly supported the need for providers to have access to and use immunization registries (e.g., TennIIS in Tennessee). Mandating HPV vaccination documentation in immunization registries is critical to not only improve data quality but also to determine child eligibility for recommendations and reminders across IN providers.71–73 Therefore, policy development and advocacy mandating universal immunization registry reporting for all immunization providers is critical for IN success.

Studies demonstrate a strong physicians’ recommendation using the presumptive approach as one of the strongest predictors of HPV vaccine uptake and completion.63,74,75 Physicians who have received communication training are more prepared to engage with hesitant parents and significantly influence their decision-making.76 Our findings expand this concept for the need for communication training across immunization providers to support consistent messages and coordinated delivery to increase knowledge of, trust in, and uptake of HPV vaccination across settings. Therefore, the added value of the IN is the increased number of trained, trusted messengers to build parental HPV vaccine confidence.70 Further research is warranted on the impact of communication training among providers within alternative settings in general and how this training can support IN utilization.

Like past studies,26,65 the need for a collaborative approach was highlighted to effectively implement vaccination programs that increase access while reducing duplication of efforts.77 Findings further suggest that effective team engagement and coordination across providers could increase HPV vaccine education and recommendation rates. Engaging staff members (i.e., nurses, physician assistants, and medical assistants) is necessary to support a pro-vaccine culture in these settings, especially if physicians are under time constraints.77 However, our study demonstrates reservations in partnering across providers and parent–child dyads. Therefore, these partners should be educated on different roles and services across immunization providers in general to positively influence attitudes toward the providers, settings, and utilization of the IN.

Despite the benefits of the IN, multi-level strategies will be needed for effective IN utilization. Unsurprisingly, providers, in our study, advocated for a mandate as it was perceived it will take longer for HPV vaccination rates to catch-up with other required childhood vaccinations.78,79 Gaining acceptance of a HPV vaccine mandate is a long-term challenge but can positively impact IN utilization.76 Research literature shows that EHR interoperability issues continue to fuel fragmented documentation of immunization records, leading to missed vaccinations, delays, or dose duplication.61,73,80 Plus, not all partners use consistently, have equal access to, or integrate EHRs into the workflow, highlighting the collaborative challenges among providers. This suggests that expanding EHR access will not fully address gaps in HPV vaccination coverage even when vaccines are available across multiple settings, as lack of data integration can still weaken coordinated delivery across providers. Studies should further explore this idea post-pandemic since pharmacies were a major distributor of the COVID-19 vaccinations,81,82 though financial challenges persist with fewer than 100 pharmacies currently being enrolled in the VFC program.65 Emphasizing VFC participation does not interfere with standard workflow with overall management requiring only 30–60 min per month could increase participation.65

While schools are advantageous in offering in HPV vaccination due to direct access to large adolescent populations, challenges in vaccine storage including cold chain management and parental consent remain.42,83 Of interest, our study identified many parents and children being uncomfortable with schools being a place for health service delivery as they were viewed as a place of education. Last, while the health departments have been long-standing immunization providers, it was not surprising for a few parents to discuss distrust in their providers and services with their government association. This reflects the ongoing need to be proactive to implement strategies to increase trustworthiness in immunizations and providers, especially those located within distrusted entities.84,85

The awareness and willingness varied across providers on IN utilization demonstrates differences in motivations, perceived threat, and trust.86 This suggests collaboration is not seen as a universally positive or necessary action, but rather a strategic choice based on individual gain, perceived effort, and social impact.87 Therefore, formalized agreements, defined roles, standardized and shared documentation, and accountability mechanisms to translate the partnership into coordinated action are needed. Furthermore, culturally appropriate education and community-based outreach strategies are necessary to address social norms (e.g., stigma related to HPV vaccination) and structural factors (e.g., lack of information and education on IN and trust in IN providers)63,85,86,88 to improve the willingness of African American parents and their children to use the IN. This can address concerns based on findings related to provider qualifications, strategies to ensure privacy, and legitimacy.

Limitations

The interview protocol was not originally designed for the CFIR, which limited the probing in each domain. Because widespread IN utilization has not yet occurred, our study did not include the CFIR domain “Implementation Process.” Furthermore, we were unable to establish a direct analytical link between the IN model and vaccination success. This study had a limited sample from a single southern US state. So, findings may not be generalizable to other states if the goal is to expand across states. Also, interviewees’ opinions cannot be broadly generalized to a greater population within provider groups (e.g., providers in urgent care who provide children vaccinations) and across the adolescent population nor children in general. While the sample size per immunization stakeholder meets the guidelines of a qualitative study to determine coding saturation, overall, the sample size is smaller so thematic saturation was not met.49,89 Lastly, interviews were conducted prior to the COVID-19 pandemic, in which some of these barriers may no longer exist.

Conclusion

Our study highlights the need for multi-component, collaborative, and patient-centered strategies to support IN utilization and improve adolescent HPV vaccination rates, especially among African American adolescents, by addressing multi-level barriers. This research serves as a guide for further exploration, formation, and utilization of the IN. Practice implications include provider expansion, reduction in missed opportunities, and strong yet unified recommendations and communication. Implementation implications include the use of multi-modal, collaborative approaches while leveraging technology and mobile clinics to support IN utilization. Policy changes to reduce out-of-pocket expenses and insurance, require reporting to state immunization registries, encourage collaborative practices, and allow and facilitate school-located delivery of HPV vaccine mandates are potential strategies for optimizing IN impact. Future research should explore this multi-sector strategy across states and parents representing all eligible children (i.e., starting at age 9) including diverse backgrounds while developing and testing a rigorous intervention trial to determine the impact of IN utilization on HPV vaccination rates.
