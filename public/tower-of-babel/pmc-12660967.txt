Inhibiting TRPV4 improves α-synuclein degradation through autophagy-lysosomal pathway in the MPP+-induced cell model of parkinson’s disease
Authors: Yuncheng Bai, Hui Zang, Zhengbin Chen, Renwan Yin, Wenjing Yang, Jia Luo, Qingjie Ma, Na Liu
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractParkinson’s disease (PD) is a prevalent neurodegenerative disorder often accompanied by dementia in its advanced stages. The pathogenesis of PD dementia involves abnormal autophagy and the accumulation of phosphorylated at serine 129 α-synuclein (pS129 α-syn). Proper functioning of the autophagy-lysosomal pathway (ALP) is essential for the effective degradation of pS129 α-syn. Our previous studies implicated the calcium channel transient receptor potential vanilloid 4 (TRPV4) in dopaminergic neuron degeneration by demonstrating that its overexpression induces endoplasmic reticulum stress and inflammation, driving neuronal loss-a hallmark of PD. This study aimed to investigate the role of TRPV4 in ALP-mediated pS129 α-syn clearance in the 1-methyl-4-phenylpyridinium ion (MPP+)-induced PC12 cells. We observed that MPP+ upregulated TRPV4 expression, reduced cell viability, and increased pS129 α-syn levels. Critically, all these effects were reversed by TRPV4 siRNA. Furthermore, TRPV4 siRNA restored cellular autophagic flux, which was impaired by MPP+. Treatment with either TRPV4 siRNA or TRPV4 special inhibitor HC067047 attenuated the MPP+-induced elevation of microtubule associated protein 1 light chain 3B (LC3B) and p62, while restoring the expression of lysosome-associated membrane protein 1 (LAMP1) and mature cathepsin D - key indicators of ALP functionality. These results suggest that TRPV4 silencing enhances α-syn degradation via the ALP, highlighting its potential as a therapeutic target for PD.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-025-26513-z.Keywords: Parkinson’s disease, TRPV4, Autophagy-lysosomal pathway, Α-synucleinSubject terms: Biochemistry, Cell biology, Drug discovery, Neurology, Neuroscience

Introduction

In the advanced stages of Parkinson’s disease (PD), cognitive dysfunction is a common and debilitating symptom that significantly impairs quality of life. A key factor in the processive cognitive decline is the accumulation of alpha-synuclein (α-syn) in the brain. Phosphorylated at serine 129 α-syn (pS129 α-syn) is the predominant form of α-syn that is located in Lewy bodies. Excessive pS129 α-syn disrupts synaptic plasticity and promotes neuronal death, ultimately leading to cognitive impairment1.

PD is influenced by environmental factors, and 1-methyl-4-phenylpyridinium ion (MPP+) is widely used to establish in vitro PD cell models2. Calcium-mediated excitotoxicity is a well-recognized mechanism in PD pathogenesis. Transient receptor potential vanilloid 4 (TRPV4), a nonselective calcium cation channel widely expressed brain neurons, plays a critical role in this process. The channel is preferentially permeable to Ca2+ relative to other monovalent cations (e.g., Na+, K+, Cs+, Li+). TRPV4 functions as a polymodal sensor, integrating activation signals from diverse sources: lipid mediators (e.g., arachidonic acid), thermal energy, ionic shifts (H+, citrate), mechanical cues, and oxidative microenvironments3. Dysregulation of TRPV4 channels in neuronal systems is implicated in multiple brains disorders, including Alzheimer’s disease, cerebral ischemia, brain tumors, epilepsy, and migraine4.

Our previous study was the first to demonstrate that TRPV4 overexpression triggers dopaminergic neuron loss in the SN, leading to motor dysfunction5. Additionally, TRPV4 upregulation induces endoplasmic reticulum (ER) stress and neuroinflammation. However, the role of TRPV4 in α-syn clearance and cognitive impairment in PD remains unexplored. In this study, we investigated whether TRPV4 modulates α-syn degradation via the autophagy-lysosomal pathway (ALP) in an MPP+-induced PD cell model. Our findings reveal a novel mechanism by which TRPV4 influences cognitive dysfunction in PD, suggesting its potential as a new therapeutic target.

Materials and methods

Drugs, antibodies, and regents

MPP+ (Cat. No. D048) and HC067047 (Cat. No. SML0203) were obtained from Sigma-Aldrich-Corporation. Chloroquine phosphate (Cat. No. HY-17589) was purchase from MedChemExpress, China. Primary antibodies targeting TRPV4 (Cat. No. ab39260), pS129 α-syn (Cat. No. ab51253), microtubule associated protein 1 light chain 3B (LC3B, Cat. No. ab192890), p62 (Cat. No. ab109012), lysosome-associated membrane protein 1 (LAMP1, Cat. No. ab208943) and cathepsin D (Cat. No. ab75852) were purchased from Abcam. RFP-GFP-LC3 adenovirus was purchased from HANBIO, China.

Cell culture, SiRNA transfection, and drug treatment

PC12 cells (rat pheochromocytoma-derived cell line) were obtained from the Kunming Institute of Zoology and maintained in DMEM medium supplemented with 10% heat-inactivated fetal bovine serum, 5% heat-inactivated horse serum, and antibiotics (100 U/ml penicillin and 100 µg/ml streptomycin). Cells were cultured at 37 ℃ in a humidified atmosphere of 5% CO2.

For siRNA transfection, PC12 cells were seeded in 6-well plates (1 × 105 cells/well) for 12 h. TRPV4 siRNA was transfected using LipofectamineTM3000 (Invitrogen). The sequences (synthesized by Genepharma, China) were as follows: TRPV4 siRNA, sense: 5’- G C U UAACA AUGACGG UCUUTT-3’; anti-sense: 5’-AAGACCGUCAUUGUUAAGCTT-3’. Negative control siRNA, sense: 5’-UUCUCCGAACGUGUCACGUTT-3’; anti-sense: 5’-ACGUGACA CGUUCGGAGAATT-3’. The contents of 0.33 µg siRNA and 5 µl LipofectamineTM3000 were separately diluted in 125 µl serum-free Opti-MEM and incubated for 10 min at room temperature (RT). Then, the diluted siRNA and the LipofectamineTM3000 solutions were mixed, incubated for 20 min at RT, and subsequently added to the cells. After 24 h transfection, cells were stimulated with 0.3 mM MPP+ for 24 h before harvest.

For TRPV4 inhibitor assay, PC12 cells were seeded in 6-well plates (2 × 105 cells/well) for 12 h. Cells were pretreated with 10 µM HC-067047 for 30 min prior to 0.3 mM MPP+ exposure. For chloroquine experiments, cells (2 × 105 cells/well) were treated with 25 µM Chloroquine phosphate for 24 h. Cells were harvested post-treatment for analysis.

Cell viability

After transfection and 0.3 mM MPP+ treatment, PC12 cells were reseeded in 96-well plate at 1.0 × 104 cells/well (final volume 200 µl), and stabilized for 4 h. Cells were then incubated with 10 µl CCK-8 reagent (Dojindo) for 2 h at 37 ℃ in darkness. Absorbance at 450 nm was measured using a Bio-Rad microplate reader.

Confocal fluorescence microscopy

Cells were seeded in confocal microscopy-specific dishes and cultured for 12 h. After treatment with MPP+ and TRPV4 siRNA transfection, cells were transduced with RFP-GFP-LC3 adenovirus at a multiplicity of infection (MOI) of 50 for 24 h. Subsequently, cells were washed with PBS, fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde, and mounted with glycerol. Autolysosomes and autophagosomes were observed using a confocal fluorescence microscopy (Nikon, Japan).

The number of GFP and RFP dots was determined by manual counting of fluorescent puncta in three fields from three different cell preparations with a 60 objective. The number of dots per cell was obtained by dividing the total number of dots by the number of nuclei in each microscopic field.

Real-time quantitative polymerase chain reaction (RT-qPCR)

Total RNA was extracted using TRIZOL regent (Invitrogen, USA) according to the manufacturer’s protocol. RNA was reverse transcribed into complementary DNA using a reverse transcription kit. Quantitative PCR was performed using SYBR Green PCR Master Mix (Thermo fisher scientific, USA) on a Real-time PCR System. For this, primers for each gene were used as follows: TRPV4: 5’-AGAAAGCGCCCATGGATT-3‘(forward) and 5’-TCTGTGGCTGCTTCTCTACG-3‘(reverse), β-actin: 5’-CTGTGTGGATTGGTGGCTCT-3’ (forward), 5’-GCTCAGTAACAGTCCGCCTA-3‘(reverse). The reaction conditions consisted of an initial denaturation at 95 °C for 10 min, followed by 40 cycles of 95 °C for 15 s and 60 °C for 1 min. Gene expression levels were calculated using the comparative 2− DDCt method with β-actin serving as the endogenous reference gene.

Western blot analysis

Equal amounts of protein samples were separated by SDS-PAGE of 10% for TRPV4 and LAMP1, while SDS-PAGE of 12% for pS129 α-syn, LC3B, p62 and cathepsin D. Then proteins in the gels were transferred to PVDF membranes (Millipore, USA). Membranes were blocked with 10% skim milk overnight at 4℃. After blocking, the membranes were incubated with primary antibodies against TRPV4 (1:1000), pS129 α-syn (1:1000), LC3B (1:1000), p62 (1: 1000), LAMP1 (1: 1000), cathepsin D (1: 1000) overnight at 4 ℃. Following extensive washing, membranes were incubated with peroxidase-conjugated secondary antibodies (1:10000, anti-mouse or anti rabbit IgG, KPL, USA). The epitope was visualized using an ECL Western blot detection kit (Millipore, USA) and quantified by densitometric analysis using ImageJ software. To normalize for loading variations, the intensity of each target protein band was divided by the intensity of the corresponding loading control band (β-actin). The resulting ratios were then normalized to the mean value of the control group to express the data as fold change relative to control.

Statistical analysis

All data are expressed as mean ± SD values. Statistical analysis were performed using SPSS software. The assumption of normality was evaluated using the Shapiro-Wilk test, while the homogeneity of variance was assessed with the Brown-Forsythe test. Analysis of variance (ANOVA) followed by a post hoc Bonferroni multiple comparison test was used to compare control and treated groups. Unpaired Student’s t-test was performed for between-group comparisons. P value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

TRPV4 SiRNA treatment ameliorated MPP+-induced cytotoxicity and reduced α-syn accumulation in PC12 cells

Both mRNA and protein expression levels of TRPV4 were downregulated following TRPV4 siRNA transfection, as confirmed by RT-PCR and western blot (Fig. 1A and B). MPP+ exposure markedly decreased cell viability, while TRPV4 siRNA pretreatment effectively attenuated this cytotoxic effect (Fig. 1C). Notably, quantitative analysis revealed that MPP+ treatment significantly increased pS129 α-syn accumulation, whereas TRPV4 siRNA intervention substantially reduced pS129 α-syn aggregation (Fig. 1D). This attenuation of pathogenic α-syn aggregation rescued PC12 cells (Fig. 1E). These experimental data indicate that TRPV4 may play an essential role in mediating α-syn accumulation under neurotoxic conditions, though further investigation is required to elucidate the underlying molecular mechanisms.

(B) TRPV4 siRNA effectively knocked down the protein level of TRPV4. Unpaired Student’s t-test was used to analyze the data. (C) MPP+ (0.3 mM) decreased PC12 cell the viability, while TRPV4 siRNA pretreatment restored cell survival. The data were analyzed by ANOVA followed by Bonferroni’s post-hoc test. (D) TRPV4 siRNA treatment prevented MPP+-induced pS129 α-syn overexpression, ANOVA followed by Bonferroni’s post-hoc test was used to analysis. (E) Representative images showing TRPV4 siRNA protected against MPP+-induced PC12 cell loss. Negative: negative control, cells transfected with empty vector. Scale bar: 100 μm. n = 3 independent experiments, quantitative data = mean ± SD, statistical significance: *P < 0.05, **P < 0.01, ***P < 0.001.

TRPV4 SiRNA restored the autophagic flux depressed by MPP+

Given that α-syn oligomerization and autophagy dysfunction are key pathological features of PD, we investigated autophagic activity using RFP-GFP-LC3 adenovirus transfection. Observations via confocal laser scanning microscopy (CLSM) revealed that GFP fluorescence (indicating LC3 recruitment to autophagic vesicle membranes) is quenched in acidic environments (e.g., functional autolysosomes), while RFP fluorescence persists (Fig. 2A and B). Treatment with 0.3 mM MPP+ markedly reduced RFP signal and increased GFP signal in PC12 cells, leading to elevated yellow puncta (merged fluorescence) indicative of autophagosomes accumulation due to impaired ALP (Fig. 2C). Chloroquine was used to indicate the autophagy inhibition. It inhibits late-stage autophagy by raising lysosomal pH, thereby blocking autophagosome-lysosome fusion, inactivating cathepsins and disrupting autophagic flux6. Notably, TRPV4 siRNA pretreatment significantly reversed these alterations, demonstrating that TRPV4 knockdown rescued MPP+-induced autophagy impairment. These findings suggest that promotion of α-syn clearance by TRPV4 inhibition may be mediated by the restoration of autophagic flux.

(B) Quantitative analysis of GFP-LC3 and RFP-LC3 puncta per cell across treatment groups. (C) Statistical analysis of autolysosomes/autophagosomes per cell among experimental conditions. TRPV4 obstructs autophagic influx through damaging ALP

The ALP comprises three critical stages: autophagosome formation, autophagosome-lysosome fusion, and lysosomal degradation, with LC3B, p62, LAMP1 and cathepsin D serving as key molecular markers. Our data revealed that MPP+ treatment markedly elevated the protein levels of autophagosome-associated markers LC3B-II and p62, while TRPV4 siRNA pretreatment effectively normalized these changes (Fig. 3A and B). Furthermore, TRPV4 siRNA restored the MPP+-induced decrease of lysosomal markers LAMP1 and cathepsin D (Fig. 3C and D), suggesting that TRPV4 knockdown significantly rescued both the fusion and degradation phases of ALP. These findings collectively indicated that TRPV4 inhibition relieved MPP+-induced ALP dysfunction, thereby reducing pathological α-syn accumulation.

(C) (D) TRPV4 siRNA treatment rescued MPP+-mediated suppression of LAMP1 and cathepsin D. n = 3 independent experiments. Negative: negative control, cells transfected with empty vector. Quantitative data = mean ± SD, ANOVA followed by Bonferroni’s post-hoc test was used to analysis, statistical significance: *P < 0.05, **P < 0.01, ***P < 0.001.

HC067047 reduced MPP+-induced pS129 α-syn accumulation by restoring ALP function

Building upon our previous findings, we employed HC067047, a specific TRPV4 antagonist, to further investigate TRPV4’s role in α-syn clearance in our MPP+-induced PD cell model. Notably, HC067047 treatment significantly attenuated the MPP+-induced elevation of pS129 α-syn, LC3B-II and p62 protein levels (Fig. 4A, B and C), and countered the suppression of LAMP1 and cathepsin D (Fig. 4D and E). These results not only confirm TRPV4’s critical involvement in ALP dysfunction but also highlight the therapeutic potential of TRPV4 inhibition for PD and PD-related dementia.

Discussion

MPP+, the neurotoxic metabolite of the 1-Methyl-4-phenyl-1,2,3,6-tetrahydropyridine (MPTP), is a well-characterized environmental neurotoxin that selectively damages dopaminergic neurons in substantia nigra (SN)7. Our prior studies established that TRPV4 inhibition mitigates ER stress and apoptosis in PD cell models8. Subsequent studies further identified TRPV4 as a critical regulator in PD pathogenesis5. The present work provides the first demonstration that TRPV4 blockade directly enhances clearance of pathological pS129-α-synuclein through ALP.

Generally, α-syn maintains synaptic structure and neural plasticity, which are essential for learning, memory and occurrence9. However, under pathological conditions such as mitochondrial dysfunction, ER stress, oxidative stress, or neuroinflammation, α-syn becomes prone to misfolding and aggregation10,11. Accumulating evidence has established pathogenic α-syn aggregation as a hallmark of neurodegeneration disease including PD, PD dementia and Alzheimer’s disease12,13. Normally, only a small fraction of α-syn is pS129 α-syn. In Lewy bodies, over 90% of the α-syn is pS129-modified, and this enrichment strongly correlates with dementia. In present study, we demonstrate for the first time that TRPV4 silencing obviously decreased the high level of pS129 α-syn in MPP+-induced PC12 cells. While previous studies have linked ER disfunction and neuroinflammation to α-syn accumulation14,15, the specific role of TRPV4 in modulating autophagic α-syn clearance requires further investigation.

To elucidate the role of TRPV4 in autophagy-mediated α-syn clearance, we employed RFP-GFP-LC3 adenovirus transfection the PC12 cells prior to MPP+ treatment. Previous research has demonstrated that the neurotoxin MPP⁺ induces ALP dysfunction16. Our findings demonstrated that TRPV4 siRNA effectively restored MPP+-impaired autophagic flux. Importantly, we identified TRPV4 silencing rescued ALP function by (1) normalizing autophagosome-lysosome fusion and (2) promoting pS129 α-syn degradation. However, the precise molecular mechanisms underlying TRPV4-mediated regulation of ALP required further investigation.

Autophagy is a highly conserved lysosomal degradation process essential for maintaining neuronal homeostasis by clearing damaged organelles and protein aggregates, including pathogenic α-syn-containing Lewy bodies. The ALP is regulated by key molecular markers: LC3B and p62 serve as indicators of autophagic flux, with their accumulation reflecting impaired lysosomal function, while LAMP1 mediates autophagosome-lysosome fusion17 and cathepsin D (a major lysosomal protease) degrades misfolded proteins18. In general, cathepsin D depredates the unfolded or oxidized protein such as α-syn via autophagy. In turn, deposition of α-syn generates a disruption of lysosomes, along with impairing LAMP1 and cathepsin D19,20. Of cause, the environmental neurotoxin MPP+ not only causes α-syn aggregation, but also impairs the autophagy flux and ALP, and induces redistribution and reduction of LAMP1 and cathepsin D16,20,21. In the present study, we found TRPV4 siRNA could significantly restore ALP function by normalizing LC3B/p62 levels, rescuing LAMP1/cathepsin D expression, and enhancing autophagic flux, ultimately reducing pS129 α-syn accumulation. These results indicate that TRPV4 silencing could significantly alleviate ALP dysfunction induced by MPP+ and subsequently reduce the accumulation of pS129 α-syn.

While our data focused on the stage following autophagosome formation, the precise mechanism by which TRPV4 and Ca2+ influx inhibit autophagosome-lysosome fusion remains to be fully elucidated. Based on existing literature and our results, we propose a plausible hypothesis. TRPV4-mediated Ca2+ entry, may critically influence the fusion machinery. Firstly, Ca2+ is a well-known regulator of membrane fusion events. The fusion of autophagosomes with lysosomes requires accessory proteins, such as LAMP1. Enhancing Ca2+-dependent calpain protease activity through Ca2+ influx leads to cleavage of LAMP122. Secondly, TRPV4-dependent Ca2+ signals might directly impact lysosomal function and acidity. Optimal lysosomal protease activity depends on an acidic pH, and lysosomal membrane integrity and fusion capacity are regulated by Ca2+ release through channels like lysosomal Ca2+ channel mucolipin TRP channel 1 (TRPML1)23. It is conceivable that excessive cytosolic Ca2+ influx disrupts Ca2+ homeostasis, thereby impairing lysosomal function and its ability to fuse with autophagosomes.

In this in vitro study, we demonstrate that TRPV4 impairs pS129 α-syn degradation through disruption of the ALP in the MPP+-induced PD cell models (Fig. 5). These findings not only elucidate a novel TRPV4-dependent autophagic mechanism of α-syn clearance in PD pathogenesis but also highlight TRPV4 inhibitors as potential oral neuroprotective agents for PD treatment. As the leading investigational TRPV4 antagonist, orally administered GSK2798745 has completed multinational Phase I/II trail, demonstrating therapeutic potential for disorders involving TRPV4 dysregulation.

The molecular mechanism by which TRPV4 regulates α-syn expression through modulation of the ALP in MPP+-treated PC12 cells.

However, our study has limitations. Firstly, regarding the effects of the neurotoxin MPP+ on lysosomes, research indicates a dual effect: it both weakens lysosomal function and may also lead to a reduction in lysosome number. However, functional impairment is usually the more central and earlier occurring event, and this impairment itself may indirectly cause changes in lysosome number23. Therefore, our study focused solely on the effects of TRPV4 silencing on MPP+-induced lysosomal dysfunction and the trend of autolysosomes. Secondly, while these findings provide important mechanistic insights, we acknowledge that the current research is limited to in vitro models. Future in vivo investigations will be essential to validate these observations and explore the therapeutic potential of TRPV4 modulation in PD.
