Epigenetic analysis of sheep mycoplasma pneumonia and the effect of FOXF1 on Mycoplasma ovipneumoniae infection
Authors: Kai Huang, Jiangbo Cheng, Qiangsheng Lu, Jie Peng, Kunchao Han, Xiaoxue Zhang, Lvfeng Yuan, Huibin Tian, Fadi Li, Weimin Wang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTMycoplasma pneumonia (MP), as a global infectious disease in sheep, seriously affects the production performance of sheep and economic benefits of sheep industry. However, current research on sheep resistance to MP remains limited. To address this gap and explore the potential epigenetic regulation of sheep MP resistant, this study employed high-throughput sequencing techniques (ATAC-Seq and CUT&Tag) to analyze epigenetic modifications in lung tissue from healthy and MP-affected sheep and reveal differential epigenetic landscapes associated with disease resistance. Integrating transcriptome analysis related to MP and genome-wide association studies (GWAS), FOXF1 was identified as a candidate gene for MP-resistance in sheep. We established a Mycoplasma ovipneumoniae (MO)-infected sheep alveolar epithelial cell model and regulated FOXF1 expression in cells through interference and overexpression techniques to study MO’s adhesion and damage. The results showed that activation promoters or enhancer elements in FOXF1 introns of healthy lungs may enhance its transcription. FOXF1 overexpression reduced MO-mediated adhesion damage to cells, while knock-down increased it. Our work has enriched the gene pool for anti-pneumonia and studied the role of the FOXF1 gene in MO-infected cells, accumulating reliable genetic resources for sheep MP disease resistant breeding.KEYWORDS: Sheep, mycoplasma pneumonia, Mycoplasma ovipneumoniae, epigenetics, FOXF1, anti-pneumonia

Introduction

The global livestock industry is flourishing, directly providing consumers with meat, milk, eggs, etc [1–3], becoming an important pillar of modern agricultural economy. The high-density livestock farming model has improved production efficiency, but it also increases the risk of outbreaks of highly infectious diseases among populations [4]. The traditional prevention and control model that relies on drug treatment and vaccination has many drawbacks, such as drug residues and increased pathogen resistance, making animal infectious diseases a major threat to global food safety, public health, and economic development [5,6]. In this context, the importance of animal disease resistance breeding has become increasingly prominent. Disease resistance traits belong to low to medium heritability traits, which are jointly influenced by the environment and host genes, among which disease resistance genes are the genetic basis of animal disease resistance [7]. The key strategy to address the challenge of animal infectious diseases is to screen and identify excellent disease resistant genes to cultivate animal breeds with stronger pathogen resistance.

As an important component of animal husbandry, sheep breeding industry faces many disease challenges, among which Mycoplasma pneumonia (MP) caused by Mycoplasma ovipneumoniae (M. ovipneumoniae, MO) in sheep, also known as chronic non-progressive pneumonia, is particularly common and highly contagious. The patient has cough, high fever, difficulty breathing, growth arrest, and presents with fibrous pneumonia, pulmonary parenchymal lesions, lung abscess, pleurisy, and even death [8]. Since its first isolation in 1963, MO has gradually been discovered worldwide, seriously endangering the health of wild animals such as Tibetan antelopes [9,10] and bighorn sheep [11,12], as well as sheep and goat populations [13,14], resulting in high mortality rates and decreased population growth rates, hindering the sustainable development of sheep farming. Therefore, screening for disease-resistant genes at the genetic level and conducting disease-resistant breeding constitute a key approach to the prevention and control of MP.

Recently, the genetic mechanism and candidate gene research of sheep MP resistance traits have been highly studied by scholars around the world. Wang et al. [15] first discovered that MHC-DRB1 gene polymorphism affects susceptibility to MP of sheep, with MvaI bb and HaeII ee being susceptible genotypes, while Mval cc and HaeII dd are resistant genotypes. Li et al. [16] compared the transcriptomes of lung of Bashbai sheep (Bbs) and Alashan hybrid sheep (Ahs) infected with MO using RNA sequencing technology and identified 20 differentially expressed genes (DEGs) closely related to MO infection, including SPLUC1, P2×7 R, DQA, SOCS3, HO-1, and SP-A. Furthermore, studies have identified genes associated with the virulence factors and pathogenicity of M. ovipneumoniae, such as glpF, glpK, glpD, hlyA, and hlyC [8], which are closely related to the adhesion of Mycoplasma to host epithelial cells. These indicate that the study of MP-resistance genes in sheep is mainly conducted through three approaches: firstly, screening genomic regions related to disease resistance at the genomic level; The second is to identify resistance genes through transcriptome analysis of the lungs of resistant and susceptible individuals, but this approach is difficult to identify key genetic variations at the whole genome level; The third is to conduct association studies with resistance indicators based on virulence factors or immune related genes, however, the candidate genes identified are mostly broad-spectrum resistance genes, lacking recognition of specific MP-resistance genes in sheep. Therefore, it is urgent to integrate phenotype, genome, transcriptome, and epigenome data of disease resistance traits to comprehensively reveal the genetic regulation of sheep MP-resistance traits to overcome the limitations of single omics methods in analyzing its complexity.

Early research was mostly conducted on individual animals, lacking studies on the pathogenicity of MO at the cellular level. As the first line of defense in animal defense mechanisms, respiratory epithelial cells play a crucial role in the pathogenesis of lung diseases. Adhesion of MO to sheep respiratory epithelial cells promotes the development of pneumonia [17,18]. Immunohistochemical analysis showed that there is a high load of MO antigen in lung lesions [19,20], which can colonize epithelial cells and cytoplasm in the trachea and lungs [21]. Cultivating sheep respiratory epithelial cells is crucial for studying the pathogenic and anti-disease mechanisms of MO. However, there are currently no reports on the integration of multi omics and molecular cell biology for key gene function validation.

This study, based on a Hu sheep population with accurate MP phenotyping, used ATAC-Seq and CUT&Tag to identify epigenetic modifications in lungs from healthy and diseased groups, including chromatin openness and histone modifications. By integrating pneumonia-associated DEGs from transcriptome analysis with genome-wide association studies (GWAS) of pneumonia pathological scores, we identified FOXF1 as a sheep MP-resistance gene. We next constructed a MO-infected sheep alveolar epithelial cells model and using interference and overexpression techniques to regulate FOXF1 expression, and found that FOXF1 reduces MO-induced cell adhesion and damage, while knock-down of FOXF1 increased the damage. This study provides data support for understanding the genetic basis and molecular mechanisms of sheep MP-resistance.

Materials and methods

Sample collection and data sources

To screen candidate genes associated with disease resistance against sheep MP across the entire genome, we selected two healthy (healthy group) and two Hu sheep lung tissue samples with typical pneumonia lesions (diseased group) based on lung phenotype data (n = 912) collected from a sheep farm (Wuwei City, Gansu Province, China) where MP was naturally prevalent in previous studies [22], for subsequent DNA extraction and epigenetic sequencing (ATAC-Seq, H3K4me3 CUT&Tag, H3K27ac CUT&Tag). The above experimental samples were all kept from Lanzhou University. In addition, a 7-day-old healthy male Hu sheep was purchased from Lanzhou Xinyuan Modern Agricultural Technology Development Co., Ltd. (Lanzhou, Gansu, China) and brought back to the laboratory for disinfection treatment. According to the recommendation of the Chinese Association for Laboratory Animal Sciences, lambs were euthanized by intravenous injection of excessive pentobarbital sodium (120 mg/kg), followed by immediate collection of sterile lung tissue for primary alveolar epithelial cell culture experiments. All animal experiments and procedures described above were conducted in accordance with Chinese laws and institutional guidelines, ensuring compliance with animal welfare standards. What’s more, we cited the MP-related RNA-Seq data (GSA accession: CRA022717) stored in the National Genomics Data Center, Chinese Academy of Sciences (https://ngdc.cncb.ac.cn/gsa) for correlation analysis of multiple omics, and the gene set related to GWAS and transcriptome DEGs for multi-omics integration analysis came from publicly available previous work [22].

Clinical and histopathological examinations

Four lung tissue samples used for sequencing were clinically examined for inflammatory lesions of the lung surface and lung elasticity status, and lung gross photographs were saved. At the same time, 1 cm3 tissue samples were extracted from typical lesions or normal areas of the lungs and soak them in a 4% formaldehyde solution. According to the hematoxylin and eosin (H&E) staining procedure, the fixed lung tissue is embedded in paraffin, sliced, stained with hematoxylin and eosin, and finally subjected to histopathological examination using an optical microscope.

ATAC-Seq

The ATAC-Seq experiment was conducted using previously described methods [23–25]. Firstly, approximately 4 g of frozen lung tissue samples (n = 4) were crushed in liquid nitrogen and immediately added with 2 ml pre-cooled lysis buffer. The fully lysed mixture was then passed through a 40 µm cell filter to remove tissue clumps and the filtrate centrifuged at 2200 × g for 20 min at 4°C to remove the supernatant. Cold phosphate-buffered saline (PBS) was used to wash the precipitated coarse nuclei twice. The nuclei were then counted using a blood cell counter. Incubate approximately 5000 cell nuclei from each reaction with Tn5 transposase reaction mixture at 37°C for 1 h. Subsequently, we purified and recovered the DNA using a PCR Purification Kit (QIAGEN, 28,106). For library generation, we used the NEBNext® Ultra™ II DNA Library Prep Kit (NEB). Following successful quality inspection, the library will undergo 150-bp Paired-End (PE) sequencing on the Illumina platform.

CUT&Tag experiment

CUT&Tag profiling of the lung (n = 4) was performed as previously described [26]. The steps for collecting cell nuclei are consistent with those used in the ATAC-Seq experiment mentioned above. For CUT&Tag, the extracted nuclei were mixed with concanavalin A-coated magnetic beads (BP531; BioMag Plus) and incubated for 20 min at 37°C. Approximately 10,000 nuclei per reaction were re-suspended in 50 µl buffer containing the corresponding primary antibody (H3K4me3, Abcam, ab8580, 1/50 dilution; H3K27ac, Abcam, ab4729, 1/50 dilution) and then incubated overnight at 4°C. The primary antibody was removed by centrifugation, and the nuclei were incubated with 50 µl buffer containing secondary antibody (goat anti-rabbit IgG, Abcam, ab6702) for 2 h at 4°C, followed by washing twice with washing buffer. After centrifugation, the following steps for tag-mentation, library amplification and purification were conducted with NovoNGS CUT&Tag2.0 High-Sensitivity Kit (N259-YH01, Novoprotein Scientific Inc.). The CUT&Tag libraries were subjected to next-generation sequencing on an Illumina NovaSeq platform, and 150-bp PE reads were generated.

ATAC-Seq and CUT&Tag data analysis

The raw data of all samples were processed with Fastp (v0.19.11) to remove adapter sequences and low-quality reads. Subsequently, the clean reads were aligned with the sheep reference genome (Oar_v1.0) using Bowtie2 (v2.2.5) [27]. Samtools (v1.5) was used for data filtering and BAM format conversion. Picard (v2.23.3) was employed to remove duplicate reads. Bedtools (v2.27.1) [28] was used to remove mitochondrial reads. All multiple mapped reads, mitochondrial reads, and low-quality reads were removed. The ATACseqQC package [29] of R (v1.16.3) was used to analyze the size distribution of insertion fragments for each sample. The sequence quality of each lung tissue sample in the ATAC-Seq, H3K4me3 CUT&Tag, and H3K27ac CUT&Tag experiments is summarized in Supplementary Tables S1-S3. MACS2 (v2.2.7.1) [30] was used for peak calling with the parameter “−q 0.05‘. This means that a region is defined as a peak when the Q value < 0.05, indicating open chromatin regions or histone modifications. Samtools merged two biological replicates’ de-duplicated BAM files and then converted them into bigwig files using Deeptools” bamCoverage (v3.5.0) with the parameter set to “−normalizeUsing RPKM.” Peak visualization was performed on bigwig files using the Integrative Genomics Viewer (IGV v2.8.0.01) [31]. The computeMatrix and plotHeatmap tools in Deeptools helped create heatmaps centered on peaks. The average distribution curves of signal strength near transcription start sites (TSSs) and transcription end sites (TESs) were generated using computeMatrix and plotProfile from Deeptools. The R package ChIPseeker (v1.38.0) assigned peaks of each sample to the Oar_v1.0 genome [32]. The DiffBind package [33] in R was used to analyze differentially accessible chromatin regions (DARs, p < 0.05) between the healthy and the diseased groups as well as differentially marked histone regions (DMHs, p < 0.05). Volcano plots and heatmaps of these differential peaks/epigenetic regions (DARs/DMHs) were generated using the ggplot package and the dba.plotHeatmap function.

Multi-omics comprehensive analysis

To analyze the correlation of read distributions among RNA-Seq, ATAC-Seq, and CUT&Tag data in lung tissue, the de-duplicated BAM files were first converted to BED format using Bedtools [34]. The entire sequence was then divided into 500 bp windows. Finally, Pearson correlation coefficients were calculated, and the results were visualized using the ComplexHeatmap package. In order to identify the associated genes through DARs (p < 0.05) and DMHs (p < 0.05), we assigned differential epigenetic regions (DARs/DMHs) to the nearest genes based on genomic distance using ChIPseeker. Peaks located within ±3 kb of TSS were considered promoter-associated, while peaks located within ±500 kb of TSS were treated as distal regulatory elements [35]. In order to associate gene expression with its upstream cis-regulatory elements and locate genes related to the phenotype of pneumonia lesions on the genome, based on the up- or down-regulated DARs/DMHs associated genes in epigenome (ATAC-Seq, H3K4me3 CUT&Tag, and H3K27ac CUT&Tag) and up- or down-regulated DEGs in transcriptome, we intersected the associated genes in the epigenome, DEGs in the transcriptome, and GWAS-associated genes [22] to screen for the final candidate gene to explain the differences in pneumonia phenotype. The intersection research results were visualized by venn.diagram function in R. Gene Ontology (GO) functional enrichment and Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) pathway enrichment analysis were performed for genes that intersect in three or more omics using DAVID (https://david.ncifcrf.gov/) [36]. The DARs/DMHs annotated by these genes were performed Motif enrichment analysis by using HOMER (v4.8) and findMotifsGenome.pl scripts [37]. Additionally, the epigenetic peak trajectories of candidate gene were examined in IGV.

MO culture and passage

The MO strain (strain number GSWWX349) was preserved and donated by Dr Jie Peng from Gansu Agricultural University. The culture medium for MO was perpared according to the following proportions: PPLO 21 g/L, 25% yeast extract 100 ml/L, horse serum 200 ml/L, ampicillin (50 mg/mL) 1 ml/L, 1% phenol red solution 3 ml/L, and then the pH was adjusted to 7.8–8.0. We resuscitated the MO strain into a sterile tube according to the ratio of frozen bacterial solution: culture medium = 1:10, and then placed it in a 37°C constant temperature incubator. After about a week of cultivation, the yellow color displayed by the phenol red indicator indicates that MO has proliferated significantly in the culture medium. At this point, we continued to culture the yellowed MO bacterial solution in fresh medium at a ratio of 1:10. We cultured the MO bacterial solution using this passage method until it steadily turned yellow at a fixed number of days and then measured the concentration of the bacterial solution.

siRNA synthesis and vector construction

Based on the predicted sheep FOXF1 mRNA sequence in NCBI, different sites were selected to design siRNA. Two pairs of siRNA (siRNA1-FOXF1 and siRNA2-FOXF1) targeting the sheep FOXF1 gene, as well as a negative control siRNA, were synthesized by GenePharma Co., Ltd. (Suzhou, Jiangsu, China). The sequence information is shown in Supplementary Table S4.

By constructing the pAdTrack-CMV-FOXF1 shuttle plasmid and transferring it into competent cells of Escherichia coli BJ5183 containing the adenovirus backbone vector pAdEEasy-1, homologous recombination was completed to obtain the adenovirus overexpression vector pAdEasy-FOXF1 (Ad-FOXF1). After PacI linearization identification, the vector was transfected into 293A cells for adenovirus packaging and propagation. The packaged adenovirus Ad-FOXF1 was subjected to virus titer detection.

Cell culture and treatment

To obtain primary sheep alveolar epithelial cells, lung tissue was first minced and digested sequentially with 0.25% trypsin and 0.1% type I collagenase at 37°C for approximately 30 min. Subsequently, the cells were stored in a basal medium (DMEM/F-12, ThermoFisher Scientific, Shanghai, China) supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS). They were then seeded into 25 cm2 culture flasks at an appropriate density and cultured in a 37°C, 5% CO2 incubator for primary culture. The medium was replaced with fresh medium after 12 h and then refreshed every 2 d thereafter. When the primary alveolar epithelial cells reached approximately 80% confluence, they were digested with 0.25% trypsin and passaged at a ratio of 1:3. Cells of passaged 3 (F3) were used for subsequent experiments.

The siRNA primers were diluted with Diethyl Pyrocarbonate (DEPC) water to 20 μmol/L before use. Alveolar epithelial cells were transfected with siRNA targeting FOXF1 using Lipofectamine™ 3000, while those transfected with negative control siRNA were used as negative controls (NC). The final concentration of transfected siRNA-FOXF1 was 0.08 μmol/L. Adenovirus Ad-FOXF1 and control empty virus Ad-GFP were added to F3 generation alveolar epithelial cells (12 well plate) at different doses (0.6 μL, 1 μL, 2 μL) to determine the optimal experimental dose. Cells treated with siRNAs or adenovirus were cultured in a 37°C, 5% CO2 incubator for 6 h, then replaced with fresh medium and continued to be cultured for 24 h. MO was co-cultured with sheep alveolar epithelial cells at the optimal multiplicity of infection (MOI) for 2 h, then the medium was discarded, and the cells were washed repeatedly with PBS to remove MO not adhered to the cells. By observing the growth status and GFP green fluorescent protein expression of alveolar epithelial cells under an inverted fluorescence microscope, the optimal dose of Ad-FOXF1 was selected for overexpression experiments. Total RNA was extracted from cells using the Trizol method, and the interference efficiency or overexpression efficiency of FOXF1 was detected using real-time reverse transcription polymerase chain reaction (qRT-PCR) technology. Alveolar epithelial cells treated with siRNA or Ad-FOXF1 were subjected to MO challenge and rinsing, followed by detection of MO load, MO bacterial solution concentration, caspase 3/8 enzyme activity, and cell proliferation/toxicity in each treatment group.

Total RNA extraction, purification, and qRT-PCR

Total RNA was extracted from alveolar epithelial cells using the Trizol method. The RevertAid First Strand cDNA Synthesis Kit (Thermo Scientific Co., Ltd., China) was used for reverse transcription to generate cDNA. SYBR Green Assay Kit (Takara) was used for qRT-PCR detection to obtain FOXF1 gene expression. Primer sequences were listed in Supplementary Table S4. FOXF1 expression levels were normalized by β-actin to assess overexpression and knockdown efficiencies. The Bio-Rad CFX384 instrument was used for qRT-PCR detection.

Determination of bacterial solution concentration using color change unit (CCU) method

Prepare 12 sterile tubes each containing 1 ml of MO medium. A 0.1 ml volume of the bacterial solution to be tested was added to the first tube and mixed thoroughly. Then, 0.1 ml of the solution from the first tube was transferred to the second tube and mixed well. Repeated this process, transferring 0.1 ml from each subsequent tube to the next, until the last tube was evenly mixed. All test tubes were then placed in a 37°C incubator for cultivation. The CCU value is determined based on the last tube that shows a color change, with the number of that tube representing the bacterial count in that volume of bacterial solution. For example, if the fifth tube is the last one to show a color change, the concentration of the bacterial solution is 105 CCU/ml. If the sixth tube is the last one to show a color change, the concentration is 106 CCU/ml.

Detection of M. ovipneumoniae load

According to the M. ovipneumoniae load detection method [22], DNA was extracted from sheep alveolar epithelial cells, and LDH expression was detected by fluorescence quantitative PCR (qPCR). The expression results were standardized by β-tubulin to quantify the load of M. ovipneumoniae in alveolar epithelial cells. qPCR reaction (10 μL): 1 μL DNA, 0.5 μL each primer (10 μM), 5 μL TB Green Premix Ex Tag II (Takara), 3 μL ddH2O. Quantitative measurements were performed in triplicate using the Bio-Rad CFX384 sequence detector, and the quantitative program was set as follows: pre-denaturation at 95°C for 3 min, denaturation at 95°C for 10 s, annealing at 60°C for 10 s, extension at 72°C for 15 s, 40 cycles.

Detection of caspase 3/8 enzyme activity

The caspase 3/8 Activity Assay Kits (Beyotime Biotechnology, Shanghai, China) were used to measure the activity of caspase 3/8 enzymes in cell lysates. The kit provides the p-nitroaniline (pNA) standard, which is generated by caspase 3/8 catalysis, and the absorbance (OD) values of different concentrations (0, 10, 20, 50, 100, and 200 µM) of standard at 405 nm were measured to plot the pNA standard curve. Cells treated differently were added to 100 µL of lysis buffer in quantities of 2 million and then centrifuged to obtain the supernatant samples to be tested. The caspase 3 and caspase 8 in the samples can catalyze the substrates acetyl-Asp-Glu-Val-Asp p-nitroanilide (Ac-DEVD-pNA) and acetyl-Ile-Glu-Thr-Asp p-nitroanilide (Ac-IETD-pNA) to produce yellow pNA, respectively. Therefore, the absorbance of the supernatant samples at 405 nm can be determined, and the activity of caspase 3 and caspase 8 in the cell lysates can be calculated based on the pNA standard curve.

CCK-8 assay

Cell proliferation or toxicity was assessed using the Cell Counting Kit-8 (CCK-8) assay kit (APExBIO Technology LLC, Houston, USA). Cells from different treatment groups were seeded into a 96 well plate and cultured for 48 h. Subsequently, 10 µL of CCK-8 solution was added to each well, and the plate was incubated in a 37°C, 5% CO2 incubator for 2 h. The OD value was measured at 450 nm using a microplate reader.

Statistical analysis

qRT-PCR and qPCR data were processed using the 2−ΔΔCt method. The average value within each experiment was expressed relative to the expression of the internal control gene. One-way ANOVA and independent t-test were done to estimate the difference of different groups followed by Dunnett’s test for multiple comparison, all data were expressed as “means ± standard error (SE).” A p < 0.05 and p < 0.01 were considered to indicate significance and extreme significance, respectively. The GraphPad Prism (v6.0) was used to draw the bar graphs.

Results

Phenotypic examination and experimental design of Mycoplasma pneumonia resistance in sheep

Through clinical diagnosis and histopathological examination of Hu sheep lungs, we observed that the lungs of the healthy group had a uniform pink appearance, with a soft and elastic texture, and a foamy sensation upon firm pressure. In contrast, the diseased group’s lung lesions began at the apex, then spread to the cardiac lobe and the anterior edge of the diaphragmatic lobe, with congestion in other parts of the lung. There were extensive hepatoid lesion areas in the lung parenchyma, with clear boundaries between the consolidated areas and the healthy parts. Histologically, lung tissue showed no significant pathological changes in the healthy group, with appropriate size and quantity of alveoli, normal morphology of type I and II alveolar cells, moderate width of alveolar septa, and clear visible capillaries. In contrast, the diseased lung tissue underwent collapse and consolidation, with some alveoli in the consolidation area filled with monocytes, and capillaries congested. The bronchi contained mucus mixed with neutrophils and monocytes, and lymphocytes infiltrated around the bronchi (Figure 1).

In this study, four lung samples were subjected to open chromatin analysis using ATAC-Seq, H3K4me3/H3K27ac histone modification CUT&Tag to elucidate the epigenetic characteristics of sheep resistance to MP. Then, we combined the RNA-Seq analysis results of 35 lungs and the GWAS results of 266 lung pathological scores obtained previously to screen for candidate genes against MP. Finally, we conducted functional validation experiments on the candidate genes at the cellular level using MO challenge tests (Figure 1).

Overview of epigenetic data

ATAC-Seq, H3K4me3 CUT&Tag, and H3K27ac CUT&Tag were used to study genome-wide accessible chromatin regions and histone modification regions associated with sheep MP resistance. We evaluated the quality of the libraries by analyzing the lengths of inserted fragments. All libraries from ATAC-Seq, H3K4me3 CUT&Tag, and H3K27ac CUT&Tag showed the expected results, with most fragment lengths <250 bp, and the inserted fragments of all samples exhibited consistent distribution characteristics, indicating good data quality (Supplementary Fig. S1). For the distribution of fragment length, many previous epigenetic data have also shown similar phenomena [38,39]. Finally, three sequencing methods obtained a total of 817,796,264, 176,521,016, and 211,992,080 raw reads, respectively. After filtering, 790,312,576, 171,273,364, and 206,613,796 clean reads were uniquely mapped to the sheep genome (Supplementary Tables S1-S3). The mapped rate of all samples exceeded 94.88%, with the highest reaching 98.91%, indicating that the overall alignment effect of clean reads was good.

The differences between various pulmonary detection methods were evaluated through Pearson correlation coefficients (PCC). Hierarchical clustering results determined the correlation between each sequencing data, followed by biological replicates (Figure 2(A)). Among them, there was a strong positive correlation between the three epigenetic datasets, while their correlation with the transcriptome data was weaker. The distribution of peaks across the gene bodies showed that these three active regulatory mark peak signals were strong within the TSS ±3kb region (Figure 2(B)). The heatmap of peak signals revealed markedly higher H3K4me3 and H3K27ac enrichment around TSSs in the diseased group relative to the healthy group, indicating that these prominent peaks may constitute key regulatory sites in resistance to MP (Figure 2(C)). In addition, there were differences in peak annotation regions in the genome between the healthy group and the diseased group. Although most peaks were located in promoter regions, the number of H3K27ac marks peaks annotated to the promoter region in the diseased lungs was significantly lower than that in the healthy lungs, followed by a decrease in the number of H3K4me3 marks peaks (Figure 2(D)).

For ATAC-Seq, 358 DARs were up-regulated and 609 down-regulated in the diseased vs. healthy group (Figure 3(A,B)). For H3K4me3, 894 DMHs were up-regulated and 1249 down-regulated (Figure 3(C,D)). For H3K27ac, 2049 DMHs were up-regulated and 2782 down-regulated (Figure 3(E,F)).

Multi-omics comprehensive analysis identified the FOXF1 as a candidate gene against sheep MP

Compared to the healthy group, the diseased group showed 348 up- and 557 down-regulated genes annotated in the DARs from ATAC-Seq; 829 up- and 1,090 down-regulated genes annotated in the DMHs from H3K4me3 CUT&Tag; and 1,952 up- and 2,223 down-regulated genes annotated in the DMHs from H3K27ac CUT&Tag. For RNA-Seq, the diseased group exhibited 1606 up- and 1121 down-regulated DEGs compared to the healthy group [22]. Meanwhile, the GWAS of pulmonary pathology scores identified 30 genes significantly associated with Mycoplasma pneumonia in sheep [22].

In order to integrate multi-omics results and screen genes related to MP, we intersected up-regulated genes in the epigenome, up-regulated DEGs from the transcriptome, and GWAS-associated genes. We obtained genes that exhibited lower chromatin accessibility, or weaker H3K4me3/H3K27ac signals, or lower expression in the healthy group and then assessed their association with MP in sheep. The Venn diagram revealed 50 overlapping genes among the three to five gene sets; only one, LOC101120093, was derived from the GWAS-associated gene list (Supplementary Fig. S2).

Correspondingly, we intersected down-regulated genes in the epigenome, down-regulated DEGs from the transcriptome, and GWAS-associated genes, and obtained genes that showed higher chromatin accessibility, or stronger H3K4me3/H3K27ac signals, or higher expression in the healthy group, and determined whether these genes were associated with MP in sheep. Intersection analysis can help identify genes with high transcriptional activity in healthy sheep lungs, which may serve as candidate genes for disease resistance. The Venn diagram identified 120 overlapping genes across three to five gene sets (Figure 4(A)). These genes were associated with molecular functions (MF) such as DNA-binding transcription factor activity, RNA polymerase II-specific, and RNA polymerase II cis-regulatory region sequence-specific DNA binding. They were also involved in biological processes (BP) including negative regulation of transcription by RNA polymerase II, immune response, and angiogenesis (Figure 4(B)). In terms of cellular components (CC), these genes were highly enriched in the plasma membrane. Additionally, KEGG pathway analysis revealed that these genes were primarily enriched in pathways such as the Ras signaling pathway, cell adhesion molecules, and cytokine-cytokine receptor interaction (Figure 4(C)). Among these 120 genes, FOXF1 and CCL1 were derived from the GWAS-associated genes, implying significant association with MP in sheep. However, only the FOXF1 gene displayed high chromatin openness, and stronger H3K4me3 and H3K27ac signals (both p < 0.05) in the healthy group (Figure 4(D)), as well as higher expression (p < 0.05) (Figure 4(A)). Motif prediction was conducted on the down-regulated differential epigenetic regions related to the gene set, and it was found that significant enrichment for the transcription factor FOXF1 motif (p < 0.01), which occupied 7.20% of the examined sequences (Supplementary Table S5). Collectively, we identified FOXF1 as a candidate gene for sheep against MP.

Knockdown of FOXF1 expression makes sheep alveolar epithelial cells more susceptible to MO infection

F3 generation sheep alveolar epithelial cells adhered to the wall and grew to about 80% confluence. MO of different MOI (10, 30, 50, and 100) was co-cultured with cells for different times (2 and 4 h) to allow MO to adhere to the epithelial cells. When MO infects alveolar epithelial cells for 2 h, the cell survival rate decreases more significantly with the increase of MOI compared to 4 h of infection. After 2 h of infection with 30 MOI and 50 MOI MO, the cell survival rate was significantly lower (p < 0.05) compared to infection with 10 MOI MO, while after 2 h of infection with 100 MOI MO, the cell survival rate was the lowest, decreasing by about 35% (Figure 5(A)). Finally, an infection condition of 30 MOI for 2 h was chosen for subsequent MO challenge experiments on alveolar epithelial cells.

Two pairs of siRNA targeting the FOXF1 gene (siRNA1-FOXF1 and siRNA2-FOXF1) were able to successfully knock down the expression of FOXF1 in sheep alveolar epithelial cells, with interference efficiencies exceeding 90% (both p < 0.01) (Figure 5(B)). Among them, siRNA2-FOXF1 had a better interference effect, and siRNA2-FOXF1 was used for subsequent interference experiments. After interfering with FOXF1 expression, sheep alveolar epithelial cells were infected with 30 MOI MO for 2 h. Non adherent MO was washed away with PBS, and DNA was extracted from harvested cells to detect MO loading. The results showed that reducing the expression of FOXF1 in alveolar epithelial cells significantly increased the MO loading compared to the NC group (p < 0.05) (Figure 5(C)). At the same time, the alveolar epithelial cells transfected with siRNA negative control and siRNA2-FOXF1, respectively, and then infected with MO were washed and prepared into cell suspensions. The relative content of MO in the cell suspensions of each treatment group was determined by CCU method. The results showed that knocking down FOXF1 expression in sheep alveolar epithelial cells resulted in a higher relative content of MO compared to the NC group (1010 CCU/ml vs. 107 CCU/ml) (Figure 5(D)). By examining the impact of MO infection on the activity of and caspase 8 in cells transfected with siRNA2-FOXF1, we discovered that, compared with the NC group, knocking down FOXF1 expression significantly elevated the activity of caspase 3 (p < 0.01) and caspase 8 (p < 0.05) in alveolar epithelial cells (Figure 5(E)), indicating an enhanced promotion effect of MO on caspase 3 and caspase 8 activity. CCK-8 detection further confirmed that knocking down FOXF1 expression significantly reduced the survival rate of alveolar epithelial cells under MO infection (p < 0.01) (Figure 5(F)).

Overexpression of FOXF1 reduces susceptibility of sheep alveolar epithelial cells to MO

The fluorescence staining of sheep alveolar epithelial cells infected with different doses of Ad-GFP (control) and Ad-FOXF1 showed that only the cells infected with 1 μL adenovirus Ad-FOXF1 had normal morphology, with most showing green fluorescence, indicating successful adenovirus transfection and similar infection rates to the control group (Figure 6(A)). Therefore, subsequent overexpression experiments were performed at the optimal dose of 1 μL Ad-FOXF1 for transfection. qRT-PCR detection revealed that FOXF1 was successfully overexpressed in sheep alveolar epithelial cells, with an overexpression efficiency eight times that of the control group (p < 0.01) (Figure 6(B)). After being infected with 30 MOI MO for 2 h, sheep alveolar epithelial cells overexpressing FOXF1 showed a 77% decrease in MO load compared to control (p < 0.01) (Figure 6(C)). At the same time, CCU method also showed lower relative MO content (106 CCU/ml vs. 109 CCU/ml) (Figure 6(D)). Compared with the control group, overexpression of FOXF1 reduced the activity of caspase 3 (p < 0.05) and caspase 8 in alveolar epithelial cells (Figure 6(E)), indicating that FOXF1 weakened the promoting effect of MO on caspase 3 and caspase 8 activity. In addition, CCK-8 detected that the survival rate of alveolar epithelial cells overexpressing FOXF1 was slightly higher than that of the control group (p < 0.05) (Figure 6(F)).

Discussion

Animals exhibit resistance or susceptibility to various pathogens during their growth and development. Disease resistance serves as a key reference trait for target diseases in farm animal breeding programs [40,41]. This capacity to withstand disease represents a unique host mechanism that varies among individuals, making it amenable to genetic improvement [42].

In recent years, studies have explored sheep’s resistance to MP through genetic and transcriptional analyses, yet epigenetic regulatory mechanisms remain understudied. Epigenetics involves heritable changes in gene expression without DNA sequence alterations, including DNA methylation, histone modifications, chromatin remodeling, and non-coding RNA regulation [43–45]. These mechanisms govern gene transcription and ultimately influence cellular function. Dysregulation of epigenetic processes has been implicated in diverse diseases, including cancer [46,47], neurodevelopmental and neurodegenerative diseases [48,49], inflammatory bowel disease [50,51], and hypoxia related diseases [52]. Thus, profiling the epigenetic landscape is essential for understanding gene regulatory mechanisms during disease pathogenesis. MP is characterized by severe pulmonary inflammation and immune dysfunction [53,54]. Based on the previous identification of phenotype data for large-scale MP in sheep, multiple healthy and severely pneumonia sheep were screened out, and ATAC-Seq and CUT&Tag experiments were conducted on the lungs to decipher the genetic basis and biological mechanisms of sheep resistance to MP. This provides valuable insights into the epigenetic regulation mechanism of anti-pneumonia formation and the treatment of pneumonia related diseases.

This study used ATAC-Seq technology to detect chromatin accessibility in the lungs of MP sheep compared to healthy lungs, aiming to identify chromatin accessibility differences regulating the host response to pathogen invasion. CUT&Tag was utilized to detect DNA-protein interactions. Quality control (QC) is key to ATAC-Seq and CUT&Tag data analysis, ensuring downstream analysis accuracy and reliability by checking sequencing read quality, contamination, and insertion length distribution. Our sequencing results met QC metrics established in extensively studied ATAC-Seq and CUT&Tag data from previous reports [29,55,56]. Currently, our study presents the MP-based epigenetic map of sheep lung tissue. The vicinity of TSS is a popular area for transcription factor binding, with high chromatin openness [34,57]. Our map reveals significantly enriched ATAC signals and histone H3K4me3/H3K27ac signals within the TSS ±3kb range across all lung samples. Eukaryotes employ multiple mechanisms to regulate chromatin accessibility, including dynamic interactions among histones, transcription factors, and chromatin remodelers. During gene transcription, histones within the nucleosome core undergo post-translational modifications via at least 80 known covalent modification pathways [43,58], such as methylation, acetylation, phosphorylation, and ubiquitination. Methylation is a common modification of numerous lysine residues in histones, with forms including H3K4me1, H3K4me3, H3K9me3, H3K27me3, and H3K36me3. Among these, H3K4me3 is associated with active promoters and serves as a marker for open chromatin regions [59]. Histone acetylation represents another extensively studied chromatin modification, closely associated with the unwinding of local chromatin and the initiation of transcription. For example, acetylation at the H3K27 site indicates the presence of active promoters and enhancers in this region [60]. Our map reveals significantly higher histone H3K4me3 and H3K27ac signals within TSS regions in diseased lungs compared to healthy controls, suggesting that the phenotype of MP in sheep may be related to the regulation of promoters and enhancers. Examining genome-wide chromatin accessibility revealed that ATAC signal peaks and histone H3K4me3/H3K27ac peaks were most abundant in promoter regions relative to other areas. Diseased lungs exhibited proportionally fewer H3K4me3 and H3K27ac peaks on promoters than healthy lungs, particularly H3K27ac peaks. This aligns with H3K4me3 being associated with active promoters, while the H3K27ac signal peak may form open chromatin in other locations of the diseased lung genome, such as intron regions, and play a regulatory role as an enhancer.

We integrated pneumonia phenotype differences with epigenome, transcriptome, and genomic data to identify key genes for sheep MP resistance. Multi-omics differential analysis revealed that compared to the healthy group, ATAC-Seq identified 609 down-regulated DARs in the diseased group, characterizing 557 down-regulated genes; H3K4me3 CUT&Tag identified 1,249 down-regulated DMHs representing 1,090 down-regulated genes in the diseased group; H3K27ac CUT&Tag identified 2,782 down-regulated DMHs representing 2,223 down-regulated genes; RNA-Seq identified 1,121 down-regulated DEGs in the diseased group. In summary, the intersection analysis of multiple omics revealed a candidate gene FOXF1 that requires special attention, and it is also an associated gene for the lung pathology score GWAS based on large-scale genome sequencing, which reasonably demonstrates the regulatory potential of this gene for the onset of MP in sheep. For healthy lungs, the intronic regions of the FOXF1 gene exhibit a more open chromatin state, and these open regions are associated with histone H3K4me3 and H3K27ac binding. It is speculated that this region is activated as a promoter or enhancer related regulatory element and participates in transcriptional regulation of FOXF1 gene expression, ultimately leading to high expression of FOXF1 at the RNA level. In addition, the genes with high activity in healthy sheep identified by multi-omics cross analysis are enriched in functional terms such as RNA polymerase II specificity, DNA-binding transcription factor activity, and RNA polymerase II cis-regulatory region sequence-specific DNA binding. The motif of transcription factor FOXF1 was predicted to be significant enriched in the highly open chromatin region of healthy lungs, further supporting the above analysis. Researchers have previously contributed to exploring candidate genes and genetic variations for sheep MP disease resistance [15,61]. For example, researchers from China Agricultural University conducted analysis of the genomes of 3938 samples from 129 sheep populations and all 7 wild relatives, and found that alleles infiltrated into specific regions of the PADI2 gene on chromosome 2 were associated with anti-pneumonia [62]. Our research has enriched the reserve library of resistance genes against MP in sheep.

MO first invades the host by adhering to respiratory epithelial cells, and then colonizes on the surface of respiratory mucosa and alveolar cells, causing apoptosis of alveolar epithelial cells and tracheal epithelial cells, while stimulating macrophages to undergo severe inflammatory reactions, causing inflammatory damage to nearby tissues [63,64]. Therefore, alveolar epithelial cells and alveolar macrophages are the primary targets of MO infection. Existing reports on MO primarily focus on its pathogenic mechanisms, with limited studies investigating the resistance of host alveolar epithelial cells to MO infection based on disease-resistant genes. To understand the functional role of the FOXF1 gene in cellular responses to MO infection, we established an optimal MO challenge model using sheep alveolar epithelial cells. In vitro MO challenge experiments demonstrated that higher MOI levels induced more severe apoptosis in sheep alveolar epithelial cells. Xue et al. [65] similarly confirmed that increased MOI and prolonged challenge duration resulted in lower cell survival rates using a sheep bronchial epithelial cell model cultured from the air–liquid interface (ALI). The 2- or 4-h infection period determined in this study is sufficient for MO adhesion. Because purified sheep alveolar epithelial cells in vitro differ from the in vivo environment and cannot achieve immune-related functions based on macrophages and other cells, prolonged MO co-culture time without the relevant immune clearance defense system directly leads to excessive toxicity to sheep alveolar epithelial cells and collective apoptosis. This contradicts the research objectives of MO adhesion and damage mechanisms.

FOXF1, a transcription factor, is expressed in pulmonary endothelial cells (ECs), fibroblasts, and mesenchymal precursor cells [66,67]. It is recognized as a key regulator of lung development [68,69], lung regeneration [70], and microvascular repair following acute lung injury [71,72]. Research indicated that FOXF1 directly binds to the R-Ras promoter region, thereby activating its transcriptional activity and regulating its expression [73]. R-Ras is involved in multiple cellular processes, including mediating cell adhesion to the extracellular matrix via integrin activation [74], stabilizing endothelial cell adhesion through vascular endothelial cell adhesion molecules [75], and regulating cell survival via the PI3K/AKT pathway [76]. Heterozygous deficiency of FOXF1 (FOXF1±) resulted in pulmonary hypoplasia, lung lobe fusion, and reduced pulmonary angiogenesis [77,78]. FOXF1 maintained endothelial barrier function by stimulating the expression of vascular endothelial adhesion proteins and S1PR1 [71]. Increased endothelial permeability in FOXF1-deficient mice exacerbated pulmonary inflammation and fibrosis. Enhancing FOXF1 expression in endothelial cells was expected to alleviate pulmonary fibrosis [73]. Numerous studies have highlighted its critical role in lung development, post-injury repair, homeostasis, and injury responses. However, most research has targeted humans or mice, with no reports on sheep-based studies or gene functional validation at the sheep cellular level. This study is the first to use MO to attack sheep alveolar epithelial cell models and to regulate FOXF1 expression in cells through interference and overexpression methods. By comparing the MO load, MO relative content, and survival or apoptosis of cells after MO adhesion between different treatment groups, the possible role of FOXF1 in MO infected cells can be understood. It is well known that caspases are key regulators in the apoptotic pathway. The cleavage of PARP by caspase 3 is considered one of the hallmarks of apoptosis [79], and caspase 8 serves as an upstream initiator of caspase 3 in the extrinsic apoptotic pathway [80,81]. Monitoring caspase 3 and caspase 8 activity in cells reflects the highly regulated apoptotic response to various pro-apoptotic stimuli.

Our results indicate that promoters or enhancer elements in FOXF1 introns are more active in healthy sheep lungs, thereby boosting its transcription. Overexpression of FOXF1 significantly mitigated MO-induced injury in sheep alveolar epithelial cells, whereas reduced expression heightened their susceptibility to MO damage. This supports the resistance of FOXF1 to MO infection at the cellular level. Previous studies have associated FOXF1 expression with MO load in sheep lungs and pneumonia pathology scores, confirming its potential role in sheep resistance to MP at the individual level [22], further supporting our identification of FOXF1 as a resistance gene against sheep MP. Future research should further validate the regulatory mechanism of the FOXF1 against MP and its effects on growth and development in sheep infected with MO. This will accumulate reliable genetic resources for disease-resistant breeding against sheep MP and provide new insights for developing new sheep breeds (lines) resistant to MP.
