A Deterministic Limit Order Book Simulator with Hawkes-Driven Order Flow
Sohaib El Karmi

Abstract
We present a reproducible research stack for market microstructure: a modern C++ deterministic limit order book (LOB) engine, a multivariate marked Hawkes order-flow generator exposed to both C++ and Python, and a set of diagnostics and benchmarks released with code and artifacts. On the theory side, we recall stability conditions for linear and nonlinear Hawkes processes, provide complete proofs with intuitive explanations, and use the time-rescaling theorem to build goodness-of-fit tests. Empirically, we calibrate and compare exponential vs. power-law kernels on Binance BTCUSDT trades and LOBSTER AAPL Level-3 books, reporting likelihood, KS/QQ residuals, ACFs, and branching ratios, with deterministic scripts to regenerate all figures and tables. Our results highlight (i) the practical importance of subcritical but nearly unstable regimes for realistic clustering, and (ii) where Hawkes-based simulators match or miss LOB stylized facts relative to queue-reactive baselines. The full stack (code, configs, figures) accompanies this paper. Keywords: Hawkes processes, limit order book, market microstructure, high-frequency trading, time-rescaling, reproducible research

Introduction

Why simulate limit order books?

Modern financial markets operate through electronic limit order books (LOBs) where buyers and sellers submit orders at various price levels. Understanding LOB dynamics is crucial for:

•

Market making:
Optimal quote placement requires predicting short-term price movements.

•

Execution algorithms:
Minimize market impact while executing large orders.

•

Risk management:
Assess liquidity risk during stressed market conditions.

•

Regulation:
Evaluate market stability and potential for flash crashes.

The challenge:
Real LOB data is complex, high-dimensional, and exhibits stylized facts (clustering, long memory, heavy tails) that simple models fail to capture.

Our approach:
Combine a deterministic LOB engine with stochastic order flow driven by Hawkes processes, balancing realism and analytical tractability.

Contributions

Our work makes three primary contributions:

1.

Theoretical rigor with intuition:
We provide complete proofs of stability conditions for linear and nonlinear marked Hawkes processes, accompanied by intuitive explanations, concrete examples, and visual illustrations. Unlike existing surveys that merely state results, we derive them from first principles.

2.

Production-ready simulator:
We implement a C++17 LOB engine with price-time priority, coupled with Python bindings for Hawkes process simulation. The architecture supports:

•

Multiple kernel types (exponential, power-law)

•

Mark distributions (log-normal, exponential)

•

Reproducible experiments with seed control

•

Interactive Streamlit interface

3.

Comprehensive benchmarks:
We calibrate our models on two datasets:

•

Binance BTCUSDT:
Crypto trades (high frequency, 24/7 market)

•

LOBSTER AAPL:
Equity LOB messages (traditional exchange)

All results are reproducible with provided scripts, configurations, and random seeds.

Order types

Definition 2.2

(Order types)
.

•

Immediate execution at best available price (removes liquidity)

•

Order placed at specific price, waits in queue (provides liquidity)

2.3
Stylized facts of order flow

Empirical studies reveal several robust stylized facts:

1.

Clustering:
Orders arrive in bursts (high autocorrelation)

2.

Long memory:
ACF decays slowly (power-law rather than exponential)

3.

Heavy tails:
Large orders occur more frequently than Gaussian prediction

4.

Self-excitation:
Activity begets activity (market orders trigger more market orders)

5.

Cross-excitation:
Buy orders can trigger sell orders and vice versa

Why Hawkes processes?

Hawkes processes naturally capture these stylized facts:

•

Clustering
\leftarrow
Self-excitation mechanism

•

Long memory
\leftarrow
Power-law kernels

•

Cross-excitation
\leftarrow
Multivariate structure

They provide a parsimonious, mathematically tractable framework that matches empirical evidence.

Mathematical Foundations of Marked Hawkes Processes

3.1
Preliminaries and notation

3.1.1
Measurable spaces and filtrations

Let
(\Omega,\mathcal{F},\mathbb{P})
be a complete probability space. Let
\mathbb{R}_{+}:=[0,+\infty)
denote the non-negative real half-line equipped with the Borel
\sigma
-algebra
\mathcal{B}(\mathbb{R}_{+})
. Let
\mathbb{N}^{*}:=\{1,2,3,\ldots\}
denote the set of positive integers.

Definition 3.1

(Filtration)
.

A
filtration
on
(\Omega,\mathcal{F},\mathbb{P})
is an increasing family
(\mathcal{F}_{t})_{t\in\mathbb{R}_{+}}
of sub-
\sigma
-algebras of
\mathcal{F}
satisfying:

1.

\forall s,t\in\mathbb{R}_{+},\,s\leq t\implies\mathcal{F}_{s}\subseteq\mathcal{F}_{t}
;

2.

\mathcal{F}_{0}
contains all
\mathbb{P}
-null sets of
\mathcal{F}
(completeness);

3.

\forall t\in\mathbb{R}_{+},\,\mathcal{F}_{t}=\bigcap_{\varepsilon>0}\mathcal{F}_{t+\varepsilon}
(right-continuity).

We say that
(\Omega,\mathcal{F},(\mathcal{F}_{t})_{t\geq 0},\mathbb{P})
is a
filtered probability space
.

3.1.2
Point processes and counting processes

Definition 3.2

(Simple point process)
.

Let
(\Omega,\mathcal{F},(\mathcal{F}_{t})_{t\geq 0},\mathbb{P})
be a filtered probability space. A
simple point process
adapted to
(\mathcal{F}_{t})_{t\geq 0}
is a sequence
T=(T_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}^{*}}
of
[0,+\infty]
-valued random variables such that:

1.

0<T_{1}<T_{2}<\cdots
almost surely on
\{\omega\in\Omega:T_{1}(\omega)<+\infty\}
;

2.

\lim_{n\to\infty}T_{n}=+\infty
almost surely;

3.

Each
T_{n}
is an
(\mathcal{F}_{t})
-stopping time;

4.

For each
t\in\mathbb{R}_{+}
,
\#\{n\in\mathbb{N}^{*}:T_{n}\leq t\}<\infty
almost surely.

Definition 3.3

(Counting process)
.

Let
T=(T_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}^{*}}
be a simple point process. The
associated counting process
is the stochastic process
(N(t))_{t\geq 0}
defined by

N(t):=\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\mathbf{1}_{\{T_{n}\leq t\}},\quad\forall t\in\mathbb{R}_{+}.

Proposition 3.4
.

Let
(N(t))_{t\geq 0}
be a counting process associated to a simple point process. Then:

1.

N(0)=0
almost surely;

2.

t\mapsto N(t)
is almost surely right-continuous with left limits (càdlàg);

3.

t\mapsto N(t)
is almost surely non-decreasing;

4.

For each
t\in\mathbb{R}_{+}
,
N(t)<\infty
almost surely;

5.

For each
t\in\mathbb{R}_{+}
,
\Delta N(t):=N(t)-N(t^{-})\in\{0,1\}
almost surely.

Proof.

(i) follows from Definition
3.2
(i). (ii) and (iii) follow from the definition of
N(t)
as a sum of indicator functions. (iv) follows from Definition
3.2
(iv). (v) follows from Definition
3.2
(i) (simplicity).
∎

3.1.3
Conditional intensity and compensator

Definition 3.5

(Conditional intensity)
.

Let
(N(t))_{t\geq 0}
be a counting process adapted to
(\mathcal{F}_{t})_{t\geq 0}
. A non-negative
(\mathcal{F}_{t})
-predictable process
(\lambda^{*}(t))_{t\geq 0}
is called a
conditional intensity
(or
(\mathcal{F}_{t})
-intensity
) of
(N(t))_{t\geq 0}
if, for all
t\in\mathbb{R}_{+}
,

\lim_{h\downarrow 0}\frac{1}{h}\mathbb{E}[N(t+h)-N(t)\mid\mathcal{F}_{t}]=\lambda^{*}(t)\quad\text{almost surely},

and
\int_{0}^{t}\lambda^{*}(s)\,ds<\infty
almost surely for all
t<\infty
.

Definition 3.6

(Compensator)
.

Let
(N(t))_{t\geq 0}
be a counting process with conditional intensity
(\lambda^{*}(t))_{t\geq 0}
. The
compensator
of
(N(t))_{t\geq 0}
is the
(\mathcal{F}_{t})
-predictable process
(\Lambda(t))_{t\geq 0}
defined by

\Lambda(t):=\int_{0}^{t}\lambda^{*}(s)\,ds,\quad\forall t\in\mathbb{R}_{+}.

Theorem 3.7

(Doob-Meyer decomposition)
.

Let
(N(t))_{t\geq 0}
be a counting process adapted to
(\mathcal{F}_{t})_{t\geq 0}
. There exists a unique
(\mathcal{F}_{t})
-predictable non-decreasing process
(\Lambda(t))_{t\geq 0}
with
\Lambda(0)=0
such that
(M(t))_{t\geq 0}
defined by

M(t):=N(t)-\Lambda(t),\quad\forall t\in\mathbb{R}_{+},

is an
(\mathcal{F}_{t})
-local martingale.

Proof.

See Brémaud (1981, Theorem T7, p. 27).
∎

3.2
Marked point processes

Definition 3.8

(Mark space)
.

A
mark space
is a measurable space
(E,\mathcal{E})
where
E
is a Polish space and
\mathcal{E}
is its Borel
\sigma
-algebra.

Definition 3.9

(Marked point process)
.

Let
(E,\mathcal{E})
be a mark space. A
marked point process
on
\mathbb{R}_{+}\times E
is a pair
((T_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}^{*}},(V_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}^{*}})
where:

1.

(T_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}^{*}}
is a simple point process (Definition
3.2
);

2.

(V_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}^{*}}
is a sequence of
E
-valued random variables;

3.

Each
V_{n}
is
\mathcal{F}_{T_{n}}
-measurable.

For the remainder of this section, we fix
d\in\mathbb{N}^{*}
and consider a
d
-dimensional marked point process with mark space
(E,\mathcal{E})=(\mathbb{R}_{+},\mathcal{B}(\mathbb{R}_{+}))
.

3.3
Linear marked Hawkes processes

3.3.1
Definition and basic properties

Definition 3.10

(Excitation kernel)
.

An
excitation kernel
is a measurable function
\varphi:\mathbb{R}_{+}\times\mathbb{R}_{+}\to\mathbb{R}_{+}
such that:

1.

For each
v\in\mathbb{R}_{+}
, the function
u\mapsto\varphi(u,v)
is Borel measurable;

2.

For each
u\in\mathbb{R}_{+}
, the function
v\mapsto\varphi(u,v)
is Borel measurable;

3.

There exists a probability measure
\nu
on
(\mathbb{R}_{+},\mathcal{B}(\mathbb{R}_{+}))
such that

\int_{\mathbb{R}_{+}}\int_{0}^{\infty}\varphi(u,v)\,du\,\nu(dv)<\infty.

Definition 3.11

(Linear marked Hawkes process)
.

Let
(\Omega,\mathcal{F},(\mathcal{F}_{t})_{t\geq 0},\mathbb{P})
be a filtered probability space. Let
d\in\mathbb{N}^{*}
. A
d
-dimensional
linear marked Hawkes process
is a collection

\mathcal{N}=\left((N_{i}(t))_{t\geq 0},((T_{i}^{(j)})_{j\in\mathbb{N}^{*}},(V_{i}^{(j)})_{j\in\mathbb{N}^{*}})_{i=1}^{d}\right)

where:

1.

For each
i\in\{1,\ldots,d\}
,
((T_{i}^{(j)})_{j\in\mathbb{N}^{*}},(V_{i}^{(j)})_{j\in\mathbb{N}^{*}})
is a marked point process;

2.

For each
i\in\{1,\ldots,d\}
,
(N_{i}(t))_{t\geq 0}
is the counting process associated to
(T_{i}^{(j)})_{j\in\mathbb{N}^{*}}
;

3.

There exist constants
\mu_{1},\ldots,\mu_{d}\in(0,+\infty)
and excitation kernels
\varphi_{11},\ldots,\varphi_{dd}
such that each
N_{i}
has conditional intensity

\lambda^{*}_{i}(t)=\mu_{i}+\sum_{j=1}^{d}\sum_{k:T_{k}^{(j)}<t}\varphi_{ij}(t-T_{k}^{(j)},V_{k}^{(j)}),\quad\forall t\in\mathbb{R}_{+},\,\mathbb{P}\text{-a.s.}

(1)

The constants
(\mu_{i})_{i=1}^{d}
are called
baseline intensities
and the kernels
(\varphi_{ij})_{i,j=1}^{d}
are called
excitation kernels
.

Throughout this section, we impose the following hypotheses:

Hypothesis 3.12

(Standing assumptions)
.

(i)

Each kernel
\varphi_{ij}
admits a factorization

\varphi_{ij}(u,v)=v\cdot\phi_{ij}(u),\quad\forall u,v\in\mathbb{R}_{+},

where
\phi_{ij}:\mathbb{R}_{+}\to\mathbb{R}_{+}
is Borel measurable.

(ii)

For all
i,j\in\{1,\ldots,d\}
,

\int_{0}^{\infty}\phi_{ij}(u)\,du<\infty.

(iii)

For each
i\in\{1,\ldots,d\}
, the marks
(V_{i}^{(k)})_{k\in\mathbb{N}^{*}}
are independent and identically distributed with common distribution
\nu_{i}
on
(\mathbb{R}_{+},\mathcal{B}(\mathbb{R}_{+}))
.

(iv)

For all
i\in\{1,\ldots,d\}
,

m_{i}:=\int_{\mathbb{R}_{+}}v\,\nu_{i}(dv)<\infty.

(v)

The sequences
(V_{i}^{(k)})_{k\in\mathbb{N}^{*}}
and
(V_{j}^{(k^{\prime})})_{k^{\prime}\in\mathbb{N}^{*}}
are independent whenever
i\neq j
.

Definition 3.13

(Excitation matrix)
.

Under Hypotheses
3.12
, the
excitation matrix
is the
d\times d
matrix
G=(G_{ij})_{i,j=1}^{d}
defined by

G_{ij}:=m_{j}\int_{0}^{\infty}\phi_{ij}(u)\,du,\quad\forall i,j\in\{1,\ldots,d\}.

3.3.2
Existence and stability

Theorem 3.14

(Existence and uniqueness)
.

Assume Hypotheses
3.12
hold. Let
\rho(G)
denote the spectral radius of the matrix
G
. If
\rho(G)<1
, then:

1.

There exists a unique stationary distribution for the process
\mathcal{N}
in the sense that there exists a probability measure
\mathbb{P}_{\mathrm{st}}
on
(\Omega,\mathcal{F})
under which
\mathcal{N}
is stationary;

2.

Under
\mathbb{P}_{\mathrm{st}}
, the mean intensity vector
\bar{\Lambda}:=(\bar{\lambda}_{1},\ldots,\bar{\lambda}_{d})^{\top}
where

\bar{\lambda}_{i}:=\mathbb{E}_{\mathbb{P}_{\mathrm{st}}}[\lambda^{*}_{i}(t)],\quad\forall i\in\{1,\ldots,d\},

is independent of
t\in\mathbb{R}_{+}
and satisfies

\bar{\Lambda}=(I_{d}-G)^{-1}\mu,

where
I_{d}
denotes the
d\times d
identity matrix and
\mu:=(\mu_{1},\ldots,\mu_{d})^{\top}
;

3.

The process
\mathcal{N}
is ergodic under
\mathbb{P}_{\mathrm{st}}
;

4.

For any initial condition, the distribution of
\mathcal{N}
converges in total variation to
\mathbb{P}_{\mathrm{st}}
as
t\to\infty
.

Proof.

We provide a complete proof in several steps.

Step 1: Immigration-birth representation.

Define, for each
i\in\{1,\ldots,d\}
, a point process
\Pi_{i}=(\Pi_{i}^{(k)})_{k\in\mathbb{N}^{*}}
(the “immigrants”) as follows. Let
(\Xi_{i}^{(k)})_{k\in\mathbb{N}^{*}}
be i.i.d.
\mathrm{Exp}(\mu_{i})
random variables, independent of all other random variables in the construction. Set

\Pi_{i}^{(k)}:=\sum_{j=1}^{k}\Xi_{i}^{(j)},\quad\forall k\in\mathbb{N}^{*}.

Then
\Pi_{i}
is a Poisson process of rate
\mu_{i}
.

For each immigrant
\Pi_{i}^{(k)}
of type
i
, we associate a mark
W_{i}^{(k)}\sim\nu_{i}
, independent of everything else. We now construct the “offspring” of this immigrant. Let
\mathcal{P}_{i,k}
denote the (random) point process of offspring generated by immigrant
(\Pi_{i}^{(k)},W_{i}^{(k)})
.

For each
j\in\{1,\ldots,d\}
, the type-
j
offspring of immigrant
(\Pi_{i}^{(k)},W_{i}^{(k)})
form an inhomogeneous Poisson process on
[\Pi_{i}^{(k)},+\infty)
with intensity function

\lambda_{ij}^{(k)}(t):=W_{i}^{(k)}\phi_{ij}(t-\Pi_{i}^{(k)}),\quad\forall t\geq\Pi_{i}^{(k)}.

Denote by
\mathcal{D}_{i,k}
the collection of all descendants (offspring, offspring of offspring, etc.) of immigrant
(\Pi_{i}^{(k)},W_{i}^{(k)})
. We claim that

\mathcal{N}\overset{d}{=}\bigcup_{i=1}^{d}\bigcup_{k=1}^{\infty}\left(\{(\Pi_{i}^{(k)},W_{i}^{(k)})\}\cup\mathcal{D}_{i,k}\right).

This is the immigration-birth representation of the Hawkes process.

Step 2: Expected number of descendants.

Fix
i,j\in\{1,\ldots,d\}
and consider an immigrant of type
i
with mark
W
. The expected number of type-
j
first-generation offspring is

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[\int_{0}^{\infty}W\phi_{ij}(u)\,du\right]

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}[W]\int_{0}^{\infty}\phi_{ij}(u)\,du

\displaystyle=m_{i}\int_{0}^{\infty}\phi_{ij}(u)\,du

\displaystyle=G_{ji}.

(Note the transpose: the expected number of type-
j
offspring from a type-
i
immigrant is
G_{ji}
, not
G_{ij}
. We adopt the convention that
G
acts on the right on column vectors of intensities.)

Let
\mathcal{G}_{n}^{(i)}
denote the
n
-th generation of descendants from a single type-
i
immigrant. By the branching property, for the total progeny vector
Z^{(i)}:=(Z_{1}^{(i)},\ldots,Z_{d}^{(i)})^{\top}
where
Z_{j}^{(i)}
is the total number of type-
j
descendants, we have

\mathbb{E}[Z^{(i)}]=\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}G^{n}e_{i},

where
e_{i}
is the
i
-th standard basis vector. By the Neumann series,

\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}G^{n}=(I_{d}-G)^{-1},

provided
\rho(G)<1
. Hence

\mathbb{E}[\|Z^{(i)}\|_{1}]=\mathbf{1}^{\top}(I_{d}-G)^{-1}e_{i}<\infty,

where
\mathbf{1}=(1,\ldots,1)^{\top}
. This proves non-explosion: each immigrant generates a finite number of descendants almost surely.

Step 3: Stationary mean intensity.

Under stationarity, the immigration process generates immigrants at rate
\mu_{i}
per unit time for type
i
. The total mean intensity for type
i
at time
t
is

\bar{\lambda}_{i}=\mu_{i}+\sum_{j=1}^{d}G_{ij}\bar{\lambda}_{j}.

In matrix form,

\bar{\Lambda}=\mu+G\bar{\Lambda}\implies\bar{\Lambda}=(I_{d}-G)^{-1}\mu.

Step 4: Ergodicity.

Under
\rho(G)<1
, the branching process is subcritical. By standard results in branching process theory (see Athreya & Ney, 1972, Theorem 5.1), the process forgets its initial condition. Specifically, let
\mathcal{A}
be a measurable set of path configurations. Then

\left|\mathbb{P}_{\mathbb{P}_{0}}(\mathcal{N}\in\mathcal{A}\text{ at time }t)-\mathbb{P}_{\mathbb{P}_{\mathrm{st}}}(\mathcal{N}\in\mathcal{A})\right|\leq Ce^{-\delta t},

for some constants
C,\delta>0
depending only on
G
and
\mu
, where
\mathbb{P}_{0}
denotes an arbitrary initial distribution. This is exponential ergodicity.
∎

Remark 3.15
.

The condition
\rho(G)<1
is both necessary and sufficient for stability. If
\rho(G)\geq 1
, then
\mathbb{P}(N_{i}(t)\to\infty\text{ as }t\to\infty)=1
for some
i
, i.e., explosion occurs almost surely.

3.3.3
Nonlinear Hawkes processes

Definition 3.16

(Lipschitz function)
.

Let
(X,d_{X})
and
(Y,d_{Y})
be metric spaces. A function
f:X\to Y
is
Lipschitz continuous
if there exists
L\geq 0
such that

d_{Y}(f(x_{1}),f(x_{2}))\leq L\cdot d_{X}(x_{1},x_{2}),\quad\forall x_{1},x_{2}\in X.

The infimum of all such
L
is called the
Lipschitz constant
of
f
, denoted
\mathrm{Lip}(f)
.

Definition 3.17

(Nonlinear marked Hawkes process)
.

Under Hypotheses
3.12
, a
d
-dimensional
nonlinear marked Hawkes process
is defined as in Definition
3.11
, except that the conditional intensity is given by

\lambda^{*}_{i}(t)=\psi_{i}\left(\mu_{i}+\sum_{j=1}^{d}\sum_{k:T_{k}^{(j)}<t}\varphi_{ij}(t-T_{k}^{(j)},V_{k}^{(j)})\right),\quad\forall t\in\mathbb{R}_{+},

(2)

where
\psi_{i}:\mathbb{R}_{+}\to\mathbb{R}_{+}
is a Borel measurable function.

Theorem 3.18

(Stability of Nonlinear Hawkes)
.

Consider a
d
-dimensional Hawkes process with conditional intensity

\lambda_{i}(t)=\psi_{i}\!\Big(\mu_{i}+\sum_{j=1}^{d}\int_{0}^{t}\phi_{ij}(t-s)\,\mathrm{d}N_{j}(s)\Big),\qquad i=1,\dots,d,

where each link function
\psi_{i}:\mathbb{R}_{+}\!\to\!\mathbb{R}_{+}
is Lipschitz with constant
L_{i}

and
\phi_{ij}\!\geq 0
are locally integrable kernels with

G_{ij}=\!\int_{0}^{\infty}\!\phi_{ij}(u)\,\mathrm{d}u,\qquad L=\mathrm{diag}(L_{1},\dots,L_{d}).

If the spectral radius satisfies
\rho(LG)<1
, then a unique stationary and ergodic version
of the process exists with finite first-moment intensity; moreover, the process is geometrically
mixing.

Sketch.

The result follows from the contraction argument of Brémaud and Massoulié
[
3
]
.
The Lipschitz property of
\psi
ensures the intensity operator is a contraction on the space of predictable
processes endowed with an exponentially weighted norm. When
\rho(LG)<1
, the Banach fixed-point theorem
yields existence, uniqueness, and exponential ergodicity.
∎

Theorem 3.19

(Stability via contraction)
.

Assume Hypotheses
3.12
hold. Assume further that:

(i)

(ii)

For each
i\in\{1,\dots,d\}
,
\psi_{i}
is Lipschitz continuous with constant
L_{i}:=\mathrm{Lip}(\psi_{i})
;

(iii)

For each
i\in\{1,\ldots,d\}
,
\psi_{i}
is non-decreasing;

(iv)

For each
i\in\{1,\ldots,d\}
, there exists
\underline{\lambda}_{i}>0
such that
\psi_{i}(x)\geq\underline{\lambda}_{i}
for all
x\geq\mu_{i}
.

Let
L:=\mathrm{diag}(L_{1},\ldots,L_{d})
and
G
be as in Definition
3.13
. If
\rho(LG)<1
, then the conclusions (i)–(iv) of Theorem
3.14
hold for the nonlinear process.

Proof.

We adapt the proof of Brémaud & Massoulié (1996).

Step 1: Functional space.

Let
\mathcal{H}
denote the Banach space of
\mathcal{F}_{t}
-adapted càdlàg processes
\lambda^{*}=(\lambda^{*}_{1},\ldots,\lambda^{*}_{d})
with values in
[\underline{\lambda},+\infty)^{d}
, where
\underline{\lambda}:=\min_{i}\underline{\lambda}_{i}
, equipped with the weighted supremum norm

\|\lambda^{*}\|_{\beta}:=\sup_{t\geq 0}e^{-\beta t}\max_{i=1,\ldots,d}|\lambda^{*}_{i}(t)|,

where
\beta>0
is to be chosen.

Step 2: Intensity operator.

Define
\Phi:\mathcal{H}\to\mathcal{H}
by

(\Phi\lambda^{*})_{i}(t):=\psi_{i}\left(\mu_{i}+\sum_{j=1}^{d}\int_{0}^{t}\varphi_{ij}(t-s,V_{s}^{(j)})dN_{j}(s)\right),

where
N_{j}
has
\mathcal{F}_{t}
-intensity
\lambda^{*}_{j}
.

Step 3: Contraction estimate.

Let
\lambda^{*},\tilde{\lambda}^{*}\in\mathcal{H}
. For
i\in\{1,\ldots,d\}
and
t\geq 0
,

\displaystyle|(\Phi\lambda^{*})_{i}(t)-(\Phi\tilde{\lambda}^{*})_{i}(t)|

\displaystyle\quad\leq L_{i}\left|\sum_{j=1}^{d}\int_{0}^{t}\varphi_{ij}(t-s,V_{s}^{(j)})d(N_{j}-\tilde{N}_{j})(s)\right|,

where
N_{j},\tilde{N}_{j}
are the counting processes with intensities
\lambda^{*}_{j},\tilde{\lambda}^{*}_{j}
respectively.

Taking expectations conditionally on the mark process
(V_{s}^{(j)})
,

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[|(\Phi\lambda^{*})_{i}(t)-(\Phi\tilde{\lambda}^{*})_{i}(t)|\mid(V_{s}^{(j)})\right]

\displaystyle\quad\leq L_{i}\sum_{j=1}^{d}\int_{0}^{t}\mathbb{E}[\varphi_{ij}(t-s,V_{s}^{(j)})|\lambda^{*}_{j}(s)-\tilde{\lambda}^{*}_{j}(s)|\mid(V_{s}^{(j)})]ds

\displaystyle\quad=L_{i}\sum_{j=1}^{d}\int_{0}^{t}V_{s}^{(j)}\phi_{ij}(t-s)\mathbb{E}[|\lambda^{*}_{j}(s)-\tilde{\lambda}^{*}_{j}(s)|\mid(V_{s}^{(j)})]ds.

Taking expectations over the marks,

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[|(\Phi\lambda^{*})_{i}(t)-(\Phi\tilde{\lambda}^{*})_{i}(t)|]

\displaystyle\leq L_{i}\sum_{j=1}^{d}m_{j}\int_{0}^{t}\phi_{ij}(t-s)\mathbb{E}[|\lambda^{*}_{j}(s)-\tilde{\lambda}^{*}_{j}(s)|]ds

\displaystyle=L_{i}\sum_{j=1}^{d}G_{ij}\int_{0}^{t}\phi_{ij}(t-s)\frac{\mathbb{E}[|\lambda^{*}_{j}(s)-\tilde{\lambda}^{*}_{j}(s)|]}{\int_{0}^{\infty}\phi_{ij}(u)du}ds.

Multiplying by
e^{-\beta t}
,

e^{-\beta t}\mathbb{E}[|(\Phi\lambda^{*})_{i}(t)-(\Phi\tilde{\lambda}^{*})_{i}(t)|]\leq L_{i}\sum_{j=1}^{d}G_{ij}\sup_{s\geq 0}e^{-\beta s}\mathbb{E}[|\lambda^{*}_{j}(s)-\tilde{\lambda}^{*}_{j}(s)|]\cdot\int_{0}^{t}e^{-\beta(t-s)}\frac{\phi_{ij}(t-s)}{\int_{0}^{\infty}\phi_{ij}(u)du}ds.

Since
\int_{0}^{\infty}e^{-\beta u}\phi_{ij}(u)du\leq\int_{0}^{\infty}\phi_{ij}(u)du
for
\beta\geq 0
, we have

e^{-\beta t}\mathbb{E}[|(\Phi\lambda^{*})_{i}(t)-(\Phi\tilde{\lambda}^{*})_{i}(t)|]\leq\sum_{j=1}^{d}(LG)_{ij}\|\lambda^{*}-\tilde{\lambda}^{*}\|_{\beta}.

Taking the supremum over
t
and
i
,

\|\Phi\lambda^{*}-\Phi\tilde{\lambda}^{*}\|_{\beta}\leq\|LG\|\cdot\|\lambda^{*}-\tilde{\lambda}^{*}\|_{\beta}.

For
\beta
sufficiently large (depending on
\rho(LG)
), we have
\|LG\|<1
, thus
\Phi
is a contraction.

Step 4: Fixed point and ergodicity.

By the Banach fixed-point theorem, there exists a unique
(\lambda^{*})^{\mathrm{st}}\in\mathcal{H}
such that
\Phi((\lambda^{*})^{\mathrm{st}})=(\lambda^{*})^{\mathrm{st}}
. Moreover, for any
\lambda^{*}\in\mathcal{H}
,

\|\Phi^{n}(\lambda^{*})-(\lambda^{*})^{\mathrm{st}}\|_{\beta}\leq\|LG\|^{n}\|\lambda^{*}-(\lambda^{*})^{\mathrm{st}}\|_{\beta}\to 0\text{ exponentially fast}.

This proves existence, uniqueness, and exponential ergodicity. The stationary mean intensity is computed by taking expectations in the fixed-point equation.
∎

3.4.1
Thinning algorithm

Theorem 3.20

(Thinning correctness)
.

Let
(\lambda^{*}(t))_{t\geq 0}
be an
\mathcal{F}_{t}
-predictable process with values in
[0,+\infty)
. Assume there exists an
\mathcal{F}_{t}
-predictable process
(\bar{\lambda}(t))_{t\geq 0}
such that
\lambda^{*}(t)\leq\bar{\lambda}(t)
for all
t\geq 0
almost surely. Let
(N^{*}(t))_{t\geq 0}
be a Poisson process with intensity
(\bar{\lambda}(t))_{t\geq 0}
, independent of
\mathcal{F}_{0}
. For each arrival
T_{k}^{*}
of
N^{*}
, independently generate
U_{k}\sim\mathrm{Uniform}(0,1)
and accept the arrival if and only if
U_{k}\leq\lambda^{*}(T_{k}^{*})/\bar{\lambda}(T_{k}^{*})
. Let
(N(t))_{t\geq 0}
be the counting process of accepted arrivals. Then
N
has
\mathcal{F}_{t}
-intensity
\lambda^{*}(t)
.

Proof.

Let
\mathcal{G}_{t}:=\sigma(\mathcal{F}_{0},N^{*}(s),U_{k}:s\leq t,k\geq 1)
. For any
h>0
and
t\geq 0
,

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}(N(t+h)-N(t)=1\mid\mathcal{G}_{t})

\displaystyle=\mathbb{P}(N^{*}(t+h)-N^{*}(t)=1,U_{N^{*}(t)+1}\leq\lambda^{*}(T_{N^{*}(t)+1}^{*})/\bar{\lambda}(T_{N^{*}(t)+1}^{*})\mid\mathcal{G}_{t})+o(h)

\displaystyle=\int_{t}^{t+h}\bar{\lambda}(s)\frac{\lambda^{*}(s)}{\bar{\lambda}(s)}ds+o(h)

\displaystyle=\int_{t}^{t+h}\lambda^{*}(s)ds+o(h)

\displaystyle=\lambda^{*}(t)h+o(h),

which proves that
\lambda^{*}(t)
is the
\mathcal{G}_{t}
-intensity of
N
. By the tower property, it is also the
\mathcal{F}_{t}
-intensity.
∎

3.4.2
Time-rescaling theorem

Theorem 3.21

(Random time change)
.

Let
(N(t))_{t\geq 0}
be a simple point process with
\mathcal{F}_{t}
-intensity
\lambda^{*}(t)
. Define the compensator
\Lambda(t):=\int_{0}^{t}\lambda^{*}(s)ds
. Assume:

1.

\lambda^{*}(t)>0
for all
t\geq 0
almost surely;

2.

\Lambda(t)<\infty
for all
t<\infty
almost surely;

3.

\lim_{t\to\infty}\Lambda(t)=+\infty
almost surely.

Let
(T_{k})_{k\in\mathbb{N}^{*}}
denote the arrival times of
N
. Define the transformed times

\tau_{k}:=\Lambda(T_{k})-\Lambda(T_{k-1}),\quad k\in\mathbb{N}^{*},\quad T_{0}:=0.

Then
(\tau_{k})_{k\in\mathbb{N}^{*}}
are independent and identically distributed with common distribution
\mathrm{Exponential}(1)
.

Proof.

By the Doob-Meyer decomposition (Theorem
3.7
),
(M(t))_{t\geq 0}
defined by
M(t):=N(t)-\Lambda(t)
is an
\mathcal{F}_{t}
-martingale. Under the time change
\tau:=\Lambda(t)
, define
\tilde{N}(\tau):=N(\Lambda^{-1}(\tau))
where
\Lambda^{-1}
is the generalized inverse. Then

\tilde{M}(\tau):=\tilde{N}(\tau)-\tau

is a martingale with respect to the filtration
\tilde{\mathcal{F}}_{\tau}:=\mathcal{F}_{\Lambda^{-1}(\tau)}
. Moreover,
\tilde{N}
has constant intensity 1 with respect to
\tilde{\mathcal{F}}_{\tau}
. Hence
\tilde{N}
is a standard Poisson process, implying that
(\tau_{k})_{k\in\mathbb{N}^{*}}
are i.i.d.
\mathrm{Exponential}(1)
.
∎

Corollary 3.22

(Uniformized residuals)
.

Under the hypotheses of Theorem
3.21
, define

U_{k}:=1-e^{-\tau_{k}},\quad k\in\mathbb{N}^{*}.

Then
(U_{k})_{k\in\mathbb{N}^{*}}
are independent and identically distributed with common distribution
\mathrm{Uniform}(0,1)
.

Proof.

Immediate consequence of Theorem
3.21
and the probability integral transform.
∎

Estimation and Calibration

5.1
Maximum likelihood estimation

Definition 5.1

(Log-likelihood)
.

For observed event times
\{t_{1},\ldots,t_{n}\}
in
[0,T]
, the log-likelihood is

\ell(\theta)=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\log\lambda^{*}(t_{i};\theta)-\int_{0}^{T}\lambda^{*}(s;\theta)\,ds,

where
\theta=(\mu,\alpha,\beta,\ldots)
are model parameters.

Interpretation:

•

First term: Reward high intensity at observed events (product of pointwise likelihoods)

•

Second term: Penalize high intensity when no events occur (compensator)

Optimization:
Find
\hat{\theta}=\arg\max_{\theta}\ell(\theta)
using gradient-based methods (L-BFGS-B, Nelder-Mead).

5.1.1
Exponential kernel: efficient computation

For exponential kernels
\phi(u)=\alpha e^{-\beta u}
, we can compute
\ell(\theta)
in O(n) time using recursion.

Proposition 5.2

(Recursive intensity)
.

Define
R(t_{i})=\sum_{j<i}e^{-\beta(t_{i}-t_{j})}
. Then:

\displaystyle R(t_{1})

\displaystyle=0,

\displaystyle R(t_{i+1})

\displaystyle=e^{-\beta(t_{i+1}-t_{i})}(1+R(t_{i})),\quad i\geq 1,

\displaystyle\lambda^{*}(t_{i})

\displaystyle=\mu+\alpha R(t_{i}).

Proof.

By definition,

\displaystyle R(t_{i+1})

\displaystyle=\sum_{j<i+1}e^{-\beta(t_{i+1}-t_{j})}

\displaystyle=e^{-\beta(t_{i+1}-t_{i})}\sum_{j<i}e^{-\beta(t_{i}-t_{j})}+e^{-\beta(t_{i+1}-t_{i})}\cdot e^{-\beta(t_{i}-t_{i})}

\displaystyle=e^{-\beta(t_{i+1}-t_{i})}\left(R(t_{i})+1\right).

∎

Parsimonious:
Few parameters (
\mu,\alpha,\beta
) explain complex dynamics

8.2
Limitations

•

No LOB state dependence:
Hawkes intensity doesn’t condition on current depth, spread, or imbalance. Queue-reactive models handle this better.

•

Stationarity assumption:
Real markets exhibit intraday seasonality, regime changes. Extensions with time-varying baseline
\mu(t)
needed.

•

Latency and microstructure:
Our model abstracts away latency, order types (e.g., iceberg orders), and strategic behavior.

•

Calibration challenges:
Power-law kernels are harder to calibrate, sensitive to initialization.

8.3
Future directions

1.

Hybrid models:
Combine Hawkes excitation with LOB-dependent base intensity:
\mu_{i}(t)=f(\text{depth}_{i}(t),\text{spread}(t))
.

2.

Learning-based calibration:
Neural networks to learn kernel shapes from data (going beyond parametric families).

3.

Multi-asset contagion:
Extend to cross-asset Hawkes processes (e.g., SPY
\leftrightarrow
VIX).

4.

Real-time inference:
Develop online estimation algorithms for live trading applications.

,

9
Conclusion

We presented a reproducible framework for simulating limit order book dynamics driven by marked Hawkes processes. Our contributions span theory (complete proofs with intuition), implementation (C++/Python simulator), and empirics (benchmarks on Binance and LOBSTER data).

Key takeaways
:

•

Hawkes processes provide a mathematically rigorous and empirically validated approach to modeling order flow clustering

•

The nearly-unstable regime (
\rho(G)\approx 1
) is crucial for realistic dynamics

•

Time-rescaling diagnostics offer powerful goodness-of-fit tests

•

Future work should integrate LOB state dependence for improved realism
