Effectiveness and safety of mirikizumab in ulcerative colitis: real-world data from the Latium Net
Authors: Marco Murgiano, Paola Balestrieri, Emma Calabrese, Gionata Fiorino, Stefano Festa, Daniela Pugliese, Federica Di Vincenzo, Pierluigi Puca, Simone Parello, Valeria Vespasiano, Alice Colella, Irene Marafini, Francesca Profeta, Elisa Foscarini, Alfredo Papa, Loris Riccardo Lopetuso, Giovanni Monteleone, Michele Cicala, Antonio Gasbarrini, Lucrezia Laterza, Franco Scaldaferri
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectiveTo evaluate the effectiveness and safety of mirikizumab in patients with ulcerative colitis in clinical practice.MethodsWe conducted a retrospective, multicenter cohort study across five Italian inflammatory bowel disease centers. Patients initiating mirikizumab between November 2024 and May 2025 were included. The primary endpoint was steroid-free clinical remission (SFCR) at 12 weeks, defined as the absence of rectal bleeding and near-normal stool frequency without corticosteroids. Secondary outcomes included clinical response (≥30% improvement in stool frequency and rectal bleeding), biochemical response (normalisation of fecal calprotectin (FCP) and C-reactive protein (CRP)), and safety (any adverse events). Follow-up at 24 weeks was assessed when available. Subgroup analyses were performed according to prior exposure to biologics (including ustekinumab) and induction regimen.ResultsA total of 95 patients were included (mean age 49.9±16.9 years; 50.5% male); 12.6% were biologic naïve and 17 (17.9%) received extended induction. At week 12, 43.2% of patients (41/95) achieved SFCR, 61.1% (58/95) clinical response, and 32/95 (33.7%) biochemical remission. SFCR rates were similar between biologic-naïve and biologic-experienced patients (42.6% vs 43.8%, p=0.906). SFCR rates were 36.2% and 88.2% in patients receiving extended and standard induction, respectively (p=0.002). At week 24, prior ustekinumab exposure was associated with numerically lower SFCR, although this difference was not statistically significant (38.5% vs 73.7%; OR=0.22, 95% CI 0.05 to 1.01, p=0.052). Changes in biomarkers were not statistically significant at week 12 (CRP Δ−0.08 mg/dL, p=0.464; FCP Δ−249 µg/g, p=0.127). Three patients discontinued treatment due to lack of efficacy. No adverse events were observed.ConclusionMirikizumab demonstrated meaningful clinical effectiveness and a favourable safety profile in a real-world setting, including in biologic-experienced patients.Keywords: INTERLEUKINS, STEROID-SPARING EFFICACY, ULCERATIVE COLITIS, Treatment Outcome

Introduction

Ulcerative colitis (UC) is a chronic inflammatory disease of the colon, resulting in symptoms such as rectal bleeding, diarrhea, abdominal pain, and urgent bowel movements.1 In UC, despite significant therapeutic advances, a substantial proportion of patients do not achieve or maintain sustained remission, highlighting the ongoing unmet need for more effective therapies.2 This unmet need has driven the development of novel therapeutic agents with improved efficacy and safety profiles.3

Mirikizumab is a humanised immunoglobulin G4λ (IgG4λ) monoclonal antibody (~143.7 kDa) that targets the p19 subunit of interleukin-23 (IL-23), a key cytokine involved in the pathogenesis of UC. It is produced in Chinese Hamster Ovary (CHO) cells and glycosylated with complex biantennary glycans. As an IgG4λ antibody, it has limited effector functions, with low complement activation and Fcγ receptor engagement, which contributes to a favourable safety profile by minimising off-target immune responses.4 Mirikizumab was granted marketing authorisation in the European Union in May 2023 and received Food and Drug Administration approval in the USA in October 2023 for the treatment of UC.5 By selectively inhibiting IL-23 signaling—while sparing the IL-12 pathway—mirikizumab disrupts a critical driver of the Th17 inflammatory cascade.4 IL-23 plays a central role in sustaining mucosal inflammation through the activation and maintenance of Th17 cells, which in turn produce downstream proinflammatory cytokines such as IL-17 and IL-22.6 Through inhibition of IL-23p19, mirikizumab helps modulate this pathogenic immune response, promoting mucosal healing and reducing intestinal inflammation in patients with UC.

In contrast, ustekinumab is a monoclonal antibody that targets the shared p40 subunit of IL-12 and IL-23, thereby inhibiting both pathways. While p40 blockade decreases IL-23-mediated inflammation, it also suppresses IL-12 signaling, which is important for host immune regulation.7 In contrast, selective IL-23 inhibition by mirikizumab specifically regulates the Th17 inflammatory cascade while preserving IL-12 function. This mechanistic difference may provide a rationale for the use of mirikizumab in patients previously exposed to ustekinumab, as selective IL-23p19 inhibition may offer a more targeted blockade of IL-23 signalling compared with dual IL-12/23 inhibition, potentially addressing residual IL-23-driven inflammatory activity in a subset of patients with heterogeneous disease pathways. The efficacy and safety of mirikizumab in moderate-to-severe UC have been demonstrated in large phase III randomised controlled trials (RCTs). In the LUCENT-1 induction trial, 1281 patients were randomised to mirikizumab or placebo, with significantly higher rates of clinical remission at 12 weeks in the mirikizumab arm compared with placebo (24.2% vs 13.3%; 99.875% CI 3.2 to 19.1; p=0.00006).8 In the subsequent LUCENT-2 maintenance study, 431 patients who responded to induction therapy were rerandomised to receive mirikizumab or placebo, with a significantly higher proportion of patients maintaining clinical remission at 40 weeks in the mirikizumab arm compared with placebo (49.9% vs 26.1%; 95% CI 15.3 to 31.7, p<0.001).9

Since the stringent eligibility and exclusion criteria of RCTs often exclude patients with comorbidities, prior exposure to other biologic agents, and other sources of heterogeneity, trial populations may not mirror routine practice.10 This gap underscores the need for additional real-world studies to verify whether the benefits observed under controlled trial conditions are reproducible in everyday clinical care. Real-world observational studies, however, have reported outcomes in smaller cohorts: a multicenter retrospective study of 74 patients showed clinical response and remission rates of approximately 70% and 18%, respectively, after 12 weeks of induction11; another real-world cohort of 52 patients reported a 44.2% clinical remission rate at 12 weeks12; and a prospective observational study of 22 patients reported improvements in both clinical and biochemical outcomes at 12 weeks, with clinical remission increasing from 30% at baseline to 83% and corticosteroid-free remission rising from 15% to 78%.13 While previous real-world studies were limited by small sample sizes and short follow-up, the present multicenter Italian cohort includes 95 patients—both biologic naïve and multifailure, including those with prior ustekinumab exposure—providing more robust evidence on the short-term effectiveness and safety of mirikizumab, with follow-up of up to 24 weeks in a subset of patients.

Methods

Study design and population

We conducted a retrospective, multicenter study across five Italian centers from Latium Net, which is a collaborative network of inflammatory bowel disease referral centers in the Lazio region (Italy). Reporting of this study follows the Strengthening the Reporting of Observational Studies in Epidemiology (STROBE) guidelines (online supplemental file 1). Consecutive adult patients with a confirmed diagnosis of UC who received mirikizumab between November 2024 and May 2025 were enrolled. A minimum follow-up of 12 weeks after the first mirikizumab dose was required. Patients were enrolled irrespective of prior biologic exposure (biologic naïve and biologic-experienced). Exclusion criteria included diagnosis of indeterminate colitis, age <18 years, inability to provide informed consent, and concomitant treatment with other advanced therapies.

Treatment exposure

Mirikizumab was administered according to the European Medicines Agency (EMA)-approved regimen: intravenous induction with 300 mg at weeks 0, 4, and 8, followed by subcutaneous maintenance with 200 mg every 4 weeks starting at week 12. According to the local prescribing information, patients with inadequate response or high risk of relapsing after the standard three intravenous induction doses could receive an extended induction consisting of three additional intravenous administrations at weeks 12, 16, and 20.

Data collection

Data were retrieved from electronic medical records using a standardised case report form. Baseline variables included demographics (age, sex, smoking status), age at diagnosis, disease extent (Montreal classification), concomitant medications (oral/topical mesalamine; oral/topical corticosteroids; thiopurines or methotrexate), and prior use of biologic agents. Collected data also included the Mayo Endoscopic Score (MES); stool frequency and rectal bleeding subscores, as defined by the modified Mayo Score; adverse events (AEs); and biomarkers such as C-reactive protein (CRP) and fecal calprotectin (FCP). The baseline MES was defined as the most recent endoscopy performed within 6 months before the first dose of mirikizumab.

Outcomes

The primary endpoint was steroid-free clinical remission (SFCR) at 12 weeks, defined as clinical remission without any systemic corticosteroids during the evaluation period.

Secondary endpoints included: clinical response at 12 and 24 weeks, defined as a ≥30% decrease in the total patient-reported outcome (PRO-2) (rectal bleeding+stool frequency) and a ≥1-point decrease in rectal bleeding or a rectal bleeding subscore of 0–1; clinical remission at 12 and 24 weeks (PRO-2: rectal bleeding=0 and stool frequency 0–1)14; SFCR at 24 weeks; and clinical and biochemical remission at 12 and 24 weeks (PRO-2: rectal bleeding=0, stool frequency 0–1 and FCP level <250 µg/g).15 Safety was assessed as a secondary outcome, with any AEs during treatment recorded.

Clinical and biochemical outcomes were compared in subgroup analysis across prespecified subgroups: (1) prior biologic exposure (≥2 biologics vs ≤1 biologic), (2) prior ustekinumab exposure versus ustekinumab naïve and (3) extended versus standard induction of mirikizumab.

Given the retrospective design of the study, outcomes were analysed using a per-protocol approach. Only patients who received at least one dose of mirikizumab and had available follow-up data at the specified time points (12 and 24 weeks) were included in the analysis of each endpoint. Patients with missing data for a given time point were excluded from that analysis.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analyses were performed using Stata V.18 (StataCorp, College Station, Texas, USA). Categorical variables were expressed as absolute frequencies and percentages and compared using the χ2 test or Fisher’s exact test, as appropriate. Continuous variables were reported as mean±SD or median with IQR, depending on data distribution, and compared using the Student’s t-test or Mann-Whitney U test. Longitudinal changes in continuous variables (eg, CRP) were evaluated using paired t-tests or the Wilcoxon signed-rank test, depending on normality. A two-sided p value <0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Ethical considerations

The Ethics Review Committee waived the requirement for written informed consent and approved the use of an opt‐out consent procedure. All eligible patients were informed about the study via notices displayed at the participating centers and through information in their medical records and could decline participation at any time. Patients unable to provide informed consent were excluded from the study. No patients opted out of the study.

Results

Study population

A total of 104 patients were initially screened for inclusion. Nine patients were excluded: two due to indeterminate colitis (IBD-unclassified), three because they were receiving concomitant dual biologic therapy, and four for having a follow-up shorter than 12 weeks. This resulted in 95 patients included in the final analysis. The included patients had a balanced gender distribution (50.5% male), and the mean age was 49.9±16.9 years. Most patients (87.4%) had failed at least one biologic therapy, with 29.5% having failed three or more classes. The mean duration from diagnosis to first biologic therapy was 11 years (SD±9.12). At baseline, one patient (1.1%) was already in clinical remission according to the modified Mayo Score. Most patients presented with pancolitis (51.6%). In 26 patients, mirikizumab was selected as a first-line therapy (onlinesupplemental figures 1 2). Detailed baseline characteristics are reported in table 1.

24-week outcomes

32 patients completed the 24-week follow-up. Of these, 20 patients (62.5%) achieved clinical response, and 19 (59.4%) achieved both clinical remission and SFCR. Among the 20 patients with complete clinical and biochemical data, three (15.0%) simultaneously achieved clinical remission and normalisation of FCP (<250 µg/g) (figure 1).

Mean CRP showed a reduction from 2.00±2.89 mg/dL at baseline to 0.88±1.94 mg/dL (Δ−1.12 mg/dL), although this change was not statistically significant (p=0.059). Mean FCP significantly decreased from 1750.2±1943.20 µg/g at baseline to 1043.2±1122.70 µg/g (p=0.038).

No significant differences were observed between subgroups according to prior biologic exposure or need for extended induction. However, patients previously exposed to ustekinumab demonstrated numerically lower SFCR rates at 24 weeks compared with those without prior exposure, although the difference was not statistically significant (38.5% vs 73.68%; OR=0.22, 95% CI 0.05 to 1.01, p=0.052) (table 4).

Safety

Three patients discontinued treatment due to lack of efficacy. No significant AEs were reported during both the 12-week and 24-week follow-up periods, confirming the favourable safety profile of mirikizumab.

Discussion

Consistent with the LUCENT trials, which showed higher remission and response rates with mirikizumab versus placebo, our multicenter real-world cohort demonstrated meaningful short-term effectiveness, with outcomes maintained or further improved at 24 weeks, supporting effectiveness across both biologic-naïve and biologic-experienced patients.

At the 12-week follow-up, 41 out of 95 patients (43.16%) achieved SFCR, increasing to 19 out of 32 patients (59.38%) at 24 weeks. Moreover, 58 out of 95 patients (61.05%) achieved a clinical response at 12 weeks, which was maintained in 20 out of 32 patients (62.50%) at the 24-week follow-up.

These findings indicate reasonable rates of SFCR achieved at 12 weeks, with further improvement observed at 24 weeks. In contrast, clinical response rates were favourable at 12 weeks and remained stable at the 24-week follow-up.

Compared with the LUCENT trial population, our real-world cohort included a higher proportion of biologic-experienced patients and a greater prevalence of treatment-refractory disease. Despite these differences, short-term SFCR and response rates were broadly comparable.

Similarly, a real-world, single-center prospective observational study published in 2024, involving a cohort of 22 patients—most with long-standing disease and 70% previously exposed to three or more biologic or advanced therapies—demonstrated a significant improvement in clinical outcomes following initiation of mirikizumab; the rate of clinical remission increased from 30% at baseline to 83% at 12 weeks, and SFCR rose from 15% to 78% over the same period.13 Another retrospective cohort study involving 52 patients treated with mirikizumab across three centers reported a reduction in the partial Mayo Score from a median of 5 to 2 over 12 weeks. At 12 weeks, 44.2% of patients achieved clinical remission, while CRP normalisation was observed in 67.3% of cases.12

Although these studies differ in terms of patient populations, baseline characteristics, and definitions of remission, the overall magnitude of both SFCR and clinical response in our study is comparable, supporting the real-world effectiveness of mirikizumab.

We further analysed the outcomes by stratifying patients based on their prior exposure to biologic therapies, comparing those who had received two or more biologics with those who had received fewer than two. Analysis of the data indicates that neither clinical response nor SFCR outcomes demonstrated statistically significant differences between the two comparison groups at the 12-week and 24-week follow-up assessments.

These findings suggest comparable outcomes of mirikizumab in patients treated as a first-line biologic therapy or after prior biologic exposure, as no statistically significant differences were observed between groups. Therefore, mirikizumab may be a viable therapeutic option across different lines of treatment, supporting its use in different patient subgroups, including multifailure populations.

To date, our study is the first real-world analysis explicitly distinguishing between first-line patients and multibiologic failures, providing new insights into treatment outcomes across these distinct populations.

According to current knowledge, similar results have been reported in a study involving 17 patients, of whom 12 had received more than two biologics, and four had received more than four, with a reduction in both clinical and biochemical disease activity, suggesting clinical and biochemical effectiveness of mirikizumab in a cohort of predominantly treatment-experienced patients with UC.16

We further analysed outcomes by stratifying patients according to whether they received standard or extended induction. At 12 weeks, both groups achieved clinically relevant rates of SFCR. However, remission rates were numerically higher among patients receiving standard induction, likely reflecting confounding by indication.

These results should be interpreted in light of the clinical context and the approved label, in which extended induction is specifically intended for patients with a suboptimal response to the standard regimen. Accordingly, comparisons between groups reflect differences in baseline treatment indication rather than head-to-head efficacy. Therefore, our findings align with real-world treatment algorithms and support the use of extended induction as an option to optimise outcomes in this treatment-refractory population.

Lastly, we performed a similar analysis based on previous treatment with ustekinumab.

This subanalysis showed that SFCR at 24 weeks was numerically lower in patients previously exposed to ustekinumab compared with ustekinumab-naïve patients (p=0.052). However, subgroup analyses at 12 weeks suggested higher response rates in patients with prior ustekinumab exposure, a finding that was not confirmed at 24 weeks. This apparent discrepancy may be explained by differences in sample size, follow-up availability, and the exploratory nature of subgroup analyses, and should therefore be interpreted with caution. Although this difference was not statistically significant, prior ustekinumab showed the most consistent association with reduced response in both univariate and multivariate analyses.

This observation may instead reflect confounding by indication, as patients previously exposed to ustekinumab are more likely to represent a treatment-refractory population. In this context, the reduced effectiveness observed may be related to greater disease severity rather than differences in the mechanism of action.

Conversely, a multicenter retrospective observational study comparing induction therapy with mirikizumab in patients with UC previously exposed to ustekinumab versus those naïve to ustekinumab demonstrated comparable rates of clinical remission at 12 weeks in both groups (44%, 11 of 25 in the ustekinumab-exposed group and 12 of 27 in the ustekinumab-naïve group), suggesting that, at least in the short term, mirikizumab effectiveness is comparable regardless of prior ustekinumab exposure.12

One of the intermediate therapeutic targets defined by Selecting Therapeutic Targets in Inflammatory Bowel Disease – Update II (STRIDE-II) is the reduction of inflammatory biomarkers, such as CRP and FCP, which are expected to normalise within a few months.2 In our study, changes in CRP at both 12 and 24 weeks were not statistically significant (p=0.464 and p=0.059, respectively), while FCP showed a statistically significant reduction at 24 weeks (p=0.038). These findings are consistent with other real-world evidence reporting a biochemical response under similar therapeutic conditions.12 13 17 Based on the available evidence, no real-world retrospective studies have assessed the 24-week course of FCP and CRP values in the overall population exposed to mirikizumab.

Despite the encouraging results of our study, several limitations should be acknowledged. The retrospective design inherently limits the ability to infer causal relationships and may introduce selection bias. Although 95 patients were included in the mirikizumab treatment group, a larger sample size would improve the statistical power and enhance the generalisability of our findings. The multicenter design reduces treatment-related bias; however, the analysis was limited to four centers within the Lazio regional network, which may restrict the external validity of the results. Additionally, approximately 46% of patients exhibited normal or only mild disease activity at baseline, potentially influencing the evaluation of clinical response. In line with real-world clinical practice, some endoscopic assessments were performed 6–12 months before initiating mirikizumab, a time interval that may not accurately capture the true baseline status. Finally, follow-up data at 24 weeks were available for only 33% of the cohort, and missing data may have introduced bias in the evaluation of outcomes at this time point. Inconsistencies between CRP and FCP measurements further underscore the need for longer and more complete follow-up to better assess the long-term effectiveness of mirikizumab. A further limitation is the absence of a comparator group not treated with mirikizumab, which prevents direct evaluation of treatment effectiveness relative to other therapeutic options; this should be considered when interpreting the results. Comparative analyses using propensity-matched or controlled designs are needed to clarify the relative benefit of mirikizumab in real-world settings. Lastly, the relatively short follow-up does not allow firm conclusions regarding the long-term safety of mirikizumab.

In conclusion, this real-world study supports the effectiveness and safety of mirikizumab. In our population, mirikizumab was primarily used in the context of moderate-to-severe UC. Our findings are overall consistent with those from RCTs, with mirikizumab proving effective in both biologic-naïve and biologic-experienced patients with UC. Although not statistically significant, a potential reduction in effectiveness in ustekinumab-experienced patients cannot be excluded and warrants further investigation in larger, adequately powered studies.
