A Slicing Model for Transport Networks with Traffic Burst Control and QoS Compliance for Traffic Flows
Aitor Encinas-Alonso 1, Carlos M. Lentisco 1, Ignacio Soto 1, Luis Bellido 1, David Fernandez 1

Abstract
Network slicing has emerged as a key network technology, providing network operators with the means to offer virtual networks to vertical users over a single physical network infrastructure. Recent research has resulted mainly in techniques for managing and deploying network slices, but the implementation of network slices on a real physical transport network infrastructure has received much less attention. Standardization bodies, such as the Internet Engineering Task Force (IETF) , have provided some implementation recommendations. Still, there is a lack of mechanisms to implement network slices capable of handling traffic bursts while simultaneously meeting the Quality of Service (QoS) requirements of the traffic flows associated with the slices. In this paper, we propose a novel fine-grained resource control mechanism to implement transport network slices that meet traffic QoS requirements while both accepting limited traffic bursts, and enabling efficient bandwidth sharing within and across slices. The mechanism is executed at the edge of the transport network. The proposed model aligns with current standards on network slicing and has been tested on an experimental platform. Using this platform, we have conducted an extensive experimental campaign that demonstrates that our proposal can effectively control traffic bursts generated within the network slices while maximizing bandwidth utilization across the network.

I
Introduction

Although the deployment of commercial 5G networks is a reality in many countries worldwide, the most advanced features proposed in the
3rd Generation Partnership Project (3GPP)
5G standards are still under investigation. Among them, network slicing promises to revolutionize the way in which network infrastructures are exploited. With network slicing, a network infrastructure can be shared to provide different and isolated network services
[
1
]
. Network slicing allows network operators to provide different communication services with tailored quality levels. Examples are
Ultra Reliable Low Latency Communications (URLLC)
,
enhanced Mobile Broadband (eMBB)
, or
massive Internet of Things (mIOT)
. Network slicing techniques can be applied to the different segments that compose a mobile network: the
Radio Access Network (RAN)
, the
Transport Network (TN)
, and
Core Network (CN)
. While the
RAN
and
CN
fall under the domain of
3GPP
, the
TN
belongs to the
IETF
’s domain. A mobile network operator may predefine a set of network slices, allowing external customers to subscribe to the communication services that best suit their applications. Additionally, operators can enable customers to define their own network slices based on specific needs. Vertical industries are expected to be one of the main beneficiaries of network slicing, obtaining customized connectivity services through a shared network infrastructure.

Up to this date, the state of the art has focused on the problem of allocating network resources to different network slices efficiently, especially in the
RAN
segment of the network. Regarding the transport network, the
IETF
has defined models for controlling the allocation of resources to different network slices, but these models do not provide the ability to limit the impact on the
QoS
of the traffic associated with services delivered over such slices.

Our proposal is applied to a scenario where standard network slices such as
URLLC
and
eMBB
are sharing the physical network infrastructure with a network slice for
Teleoperated Driving (ToD)
.
ToD
is an advanced automotive industry use case that enables drivers to operate vehicles remotely
[
2
]
.

In our target scenario, a
ToD
service provider signs a contract with an operator of a public mobile network to hire a network slice for the
ToD
service. Within the
ToD
service, various traffic flows with different
QoS
requirements (video, telemetry, and commands) are generated. Consequently, the network slice for
ToD
must be capable of providing differentiated traffic treatment for each type of traffic flow within the slice.

The main contributions of this work are:

•

The design of a flexible slicing model for transport networks, named
Hierarchically Controlled Transport Network Slicing (HCTNS)
, aligned with current
IETF
standards, enhancing bandwidth sharing between traffic classes and slices while providing explicit control over how bandwidth is allocated. The model also ensures consistent
QoS
behavior for all traffic accepted in the
TN
within the same traffic class, or, in its absence, within the same slice.

•

HCTNS
incorporates a novel traffic policer that optimizes the utilization of available bandwidth both across slices and within individual slices.

•

A new traffic burst control mechanism that enables the network to accommodate traffic bursts while managing their impact on the
QoS
experienced by the different traffic flows in the
TN
through configurable parameters.

•

A performance evaluation, based on an implemented prototype, showing that by applying our proposal it is possible to meet the strict
QoS
requirements of services such as
ToD
. The results confirm the advantages of
HCTNS
in ensuring that all traffic flows within any network slice adhere to the defined
QoS
requirements.
Moreover, the experiments show that our proposal outperforms the 5G transport network slicing model that is being standardized by the IETF and a reference proposal in the literature.

reviews the related work and highlights the contributions of this paper to the state of the art.
Section
III
describes the IETF 5G transport network slicing model, which serves as the reference model for our proposed work.
Section
IV
describes the
HCTNS
network slicing and QoS model.
Section
V
presents our testbed with a simplified version of our proposed network slicing model for 5G transport networks, configured to support teleoperated driving services sharing the same physical network with other typical and constrained slices. This section is also dedicated to the evaluation of the proposal. Finally, Section

summarizes the main conclusions of our work.

]

[

Network slice management, coordination and signaling between

II-B

Network Slicing Literature

There is extensive literature covering the application of network slicing techniques across the different segments of the network. Wang et al.
[
12
]
followed an approach based on queuing disciplines implemented over a software-defined network based on P4. They proposed a hierarchical queuing structure that has two levels. The first one is composed of eight round robin queues that ensure a proper load balance between the processed traffic. In the second level, each round robin queue is connected to four priority queues. In this way, it is possible to limit the delay of different traffic classes in each round robin queue. The problem with this approach is that it is not aligned with the model proposed by
IETF
for
TNs
, for instance, because it does not consider a policer controlling the traffic entering the network.

Other works
[
13
]
,
[
14
]
proposed using traffic shaping mechanisms based on
Hierarchical Token Bucket (HTB)
for providing each network slice with a bandwidth guarantee and allowing a network slice to consume unused bandwidth by other slices. The proposed traffic shaping mechanism provides traffic isolation between network slices that are sharing the network infrastructure. Raussi
et al.

[
15
]
also proposed the use of traffic shaping mechanisms based on
HTBs
to improve the reliability of communications in smart grids scenarios with wired connections. Lin et al.
[
16
]
implemented
QoS
framework over a P4-based network composed of four functional blocks: a classifier, a marker, a policer and a packet scheduler based on priority queues. In this work, all traffic marked as
“green”
from all of the slices is enqueued into the same priority queue, so, it is not possible to control the delay of the
“green”
traffic of each slice. Additionally, traffic marked as
“yellow”
is enqueued in a lower priority queue, so it does not meet the latency requirements.
Chen
et al.
proposal
[
17
]
uses two priority queues, one for the
“green”
traffic coming from all the slices, and a lower priority one for
“yellow”
traffic from all the slices and for non-sliced or best-effort traffic (this is a similar arrangement to
[
16
]
). The lower priority queue is further divided into four queues using a
Deficit Round Robin (DRR)
to separate different types of flows, which allows to control the sharing of the available bandwidth beyond the one guaranteed to the network slices. This proposal meets the bandwidth slice requirements and has a mechanism to control the sharing of the additional bandwidth available, but because all the slices share the same queue, bandwidth sharing among them is not controlled and delay constraints are not addressed. In general, proposals that use
“yellow”
traffic to enable bandwidth sharing have the drawback that losses and delay for such traffic cannot be controlled.

Huin
et al.

[
18
]
and Martin
et al.

[
19
]
have followed a different approach, where network resources are exclusively dedicated to each slice. While this approach provides a high level of isolation between slices, it reduces network utilization and efficiency by preventing the sharing of unused bandwidth from one slice with others, potentially leading to under-utilization of resources.

None of the above works addressed the problem of control the delay of different types of traffic flows. On the other hand,
Chang
et al.

[
20
,
21
]
proposed a network slicing technique at the data link layer (L2) that is able to meet the latency and bandwidth requirements of different network slices by using a queuing system based on priority queues and Active Queue Management (AQM). The proposed model is implemented over a network of P4-programmable data plane switches, an approach that is still not aligned with
IETF
priorities. Besides, a worst-case scenario with bursty traffic, as proposed in this paper, has also not been analyzed either.

Baba et al.
[
22
]
analyzed the impact of micro-bursts on the
QoS
of the 5G network, comparing the case of using a priority queuing scheduler versus a
Weighted Fair Queuing (WFQ)
scheduler. However, they did not propose a solution to limit this impact.

Regarding vehicular networks, several works
[
23
,
24
,
25
,
26
,
27
]
focused on the management of resources on the radio interface to support network slicing. The work in
[
28
]
, in addition to studying slicing techniques in the radio interface, also explored how to apply network slicing in the core of the network, and proposed the use of priority queues to achieve a latency in mission critical traffic lower than in best effort traffic. For the specific case of
ToD
, the work in
[
29
,
30
]
identified the need to define a network slice capable of meeting the strict
QoS
requirements of the service.

None of the existing works in the state of the art align with the slicing model defined by the
IETF
for 5G
TNs
. Additionally, they fail to account for multiple flows with different
QoS
requirements per slice or to address how to limit delays while guaranteeing bandwidth requirements in worst-case scenarios under bursty traffic conditions. The solution proposed in this paper makes it possible to satisfy both the bandwidth and the latency requirements of
TNs
slices and incorporate traffic burst control for worst-case scenarios.

III
Background: The IETF Network Slice Model

This section provides an overview of the key features of the slicing model being defined by the
IETF
for transport networks.

The slicing model defined by the
IETF

[
11
,
9
]
is composed of three parts. First, the model defines how the
3GPP
5G network slice identifier, namely the
Single - Network Slice Selection Assistance Information (S-NSSAI)
, which cannot be used in the
IETF
transport network domain, can be identified in this domain by using L2/L3 header fields such as the VLAN Identifier or the MPLS label. The second part of the model focuses on how the
QoS
indicators used in the 5G network are mapped to values of the DSCP field of the IP header to mark the traffic in the transport network, with concrete recommendations for this mapping process given in
[
10
]
. Finally, the model defines how the data plane traffic is treated in the transport network so that the
QoS
requirements of the flows are correspondingly satisfied.
Our proposal, described in Section
IV
, focuses on network realization, but we also describe the other features considered to be part of our model
.

An
IETF
network slice is defined between a set of
Service Demarcation Points (SDPs)
, that is, points of attachment for customers connecting their corresponding network slices. An
SDP
has a unique identifier in the provider network scope, for instance, an IP address, a VLAN tag, an MPLS label, or an interface/port number. The network slice provides connectivity between the
SDPs
and satisfies the latency and bandwidth requirements agreed between the network slice provider and the network slice consumer in the
Service Level Agreement (SLA)
.

Below, we explain the components of the
IETF
network slice model in more detail.

III-A

Network Slicing Identification

End-to-end network slices in the
3GPP
domain are identified by
S-NSSAIs
that are not visible in the
IETF
domain.
S-NSSAIs

are managed in the
RAN
and core segments of the 5G network, for example, to apply differentiated treatment in terms of
QoS
to the slices defined in the network. But, since the
S-NSSAI
is not visible in the
IETF
domain, different mechanisms have been proposed for the
TN
to be able to identify the network slice that is associated to the incoming data traffic. These mechanisms, known as hand-off methods
[
9
,
8
]
, are based on the use of L2, L3, or L4 identifiers. The main hand-off methods are detailed below:

•

Virtual Local Area Network (VLAN)
hand-off. A
VLAN
tag is added to all the traffic exchanged between the transport network and the
RAN
or core networks. The VLAN ID is used by the
Provider Edge (PE)
transport network nodes to identify the 5G network slice. Each
SDP
is represented by a
VLAN
ID (or double
VLAN
with QinQ) and each
VLAN
represents a separated logical interface on the
PEs
. The VLAN ID is only used to identify the 5G network slice, so the VLAN tag is removed by the
PE
routers when forwarding the data traffic within the transport network.

•

IP hand-off (IPv4 or IPv6). It consists on establishing associations between
S-NSSAIs
and source/destination IP addresses. The association can be done in three different ways: (1) the
S-NSSAI
is associated to the IP address allocated to the gNB or the
User Plane Function (UPF)
; (2) the
S-NSSAI
is associated to a range of IP addresses allocated to a set of gNBs and
UPFs
; (3) the
S-NSSAI
is associated to a subset of bits of an IP address, or (4) the
S-NSSAI
is associated to a
Differentiated Services Code Point (DSCP)
value.

•

Multiprotocol Label Switching (MPLS)
Label hand-off.
MPLS
labels are used by the
PE
nodes to infer the identification of the 5G network slice.

•

User Datagram Protocol (UDP)
source port hand-off.
UDP
source ports carried over
GPRS Tunnelling Protocol (GTP)
-U tunnels are used to identify the 5G network slice.

Regardless of the identification method used, it is also necessary to map 5G network slices to
TN
slices. In
[
9
]
, the
IETF
proposes three alternatives for making this mapping

III-B

QoS Flow Identification

As in the previous case, the
3GPP

QoS
identifiers are only visible in the
3GPP
domain, which makes it necessary to translate them in the
IETF
domain for providing different
QoS
treatments in the
TNs
for each
3GPP
5G slice or for each 5G
QoS
flow within each
3GPP
5G slice.

Each
QoS
flow in the
3GPP
domain, either sent from the
UPF
to the
RAN
or vice versa, traverses the
TN
through a
GTP
tunnel, and it is identified by a
QoS flow Identifier (QFI)
that is carried in the
GTP
header. Using these
QFIs
, gNBs and
UPFs
apply the
QoS
policies defined in the
3GPP
domain. A
QFI
is associated with a
5QI
that indicates the
QoS
characteristics of the traffic flow, such as the
Packet Delay Budget (PDB)
or the priority level. Packets from
QoS
flows are marked by using the
DSCP
field of the IP header. The
IETF
describes an example of how the mapping between a
5QI
and a
DSCP
can be done
[
10
]
. A
5QI
can take multiple values, as has been defined in TS.23.501
[
3
]
, and, in practice,
5QIs
with similar characteristics are mapped to the same
DSCP
value. Packets received on the ingress ports of the
PE
routers of the transport network are marked with these
DSCP
values. These marks enable the
PE
routers to classify the incoming traffic and associate it with a
TN

QoS
class, so traffic can be treated according to the characteristics defined by the corresponding
5QIs
. This association is defined by a label in the headers added to the packets when transported through a tunnel in the transport network, commonly referred to as the
TN

QoS
mark. These tunnels can be implemented over MPLS networks (and in this case, the Traffic Class field is used) or over IPv6 (
DSCP
values are used in this case). In the
IETF
model, eight transport network classes have been considered, as typical router hardware supports up to eight traffic queues per port. These
TN

QoS
classes determine a
Per-Hop-Behaviour (PHB)
enforced to packets in each
TN
node. From the eight available transport network classes, according to
[
10
]
, four are reserved for the data plane traffic and the remaining four for control plane traffic. Consequently, data traffic is grouped into four
5QI
categories:

•

Group 1: delay-critical traffic with
Guaranteed Bit Rate (GBR)
, allowing packet losses between
10^{-6}
and
10^{-4}
).

•

Group 2: traffic with moderated delay and varying packet loss levels.

•

Group 3: other
GBR
traffic not included in Groups 1 or 2.

•

Group 4:
5QI
values assigned for non-
GBR
traffic.

For describing our proposal, the above groups will be referred as
TN

QoS
class A,
TN

QoS
class B,
TN

QoS
class C, and
TN

QoS
class D, respectively.

III-C

IETF Network Slice Realization

An important part of the
IETF
model described in
[
9
]
is the realization of the transport network slices. Implementing a network slice in the
IETF
domain requires combining different network mechanisms. The mechanisms identified in the
IETF
draft are the following: L2VPN and/or L3VPN service instances for logical separation of the slices; fine-grained resource control for enforcing the bandwidth contract at the edges of the provider network for each
QoS
flow and slice; coarse-grained resource control for applying
QoS
mechanisms to flows aggregated in traffic classes within the transport network; and capacity planning/management mechanisms ensuring that enough capacity is available across the transport network for the network slices. Below, a more
detailed
description of these mechanisms is provided.

III-C
1
L2VPN and/or L3VPN for slices isolation

L2VPN and/or L3VPN service instances might be deployed for achieving logical separation of slices. This results in an additional outer header due to the packet encapsulation carried out in the border nodes hosting the services instances, i.e., in the
PEs
. This also provides a clear separation between the
DSCP
value that is used to identify the
QoS
in traffic received from the
3GPP
domain and the
TN

QoS
mark used to determine the
QoS
in the
TN
. These mechanisms might also be used to deploy different underlay transport paths optimized according to the
latency and bandwidth requirements specified in the

SLAs
of different 5G
QoS
flows. For example, for traffic with low latency requirements, a path with fewer hops would be preferable.

It is important to note that the
TN

QoS
classes and the underlay transport path have different targets. The
TN

QoS
class defines the
PHB
enforced to packets that transit the
TN
, whereas the underlay transport determines the overall path taken by packets based on the operator’s requirements. These underlay transports can be realized through various mechanisms, such as
Resource Reservation Protocol - Traffic Engineering (RSVP-TE)
or
Segment Routing - Traffic Engineering (SR-TE)
tunnels.

III-C
2
Fine-grained resource control at the edge of the TN

This mechanism works as an admission control that enforces the bandwidth contract at the edge of the provider network by using traffic policers. A
PE
receiving traffic from the 5G network, i.e., an ingress
PE
, enforces a rate limitation policy to guarantee bandwidth per slice and per traffic class within each slice. Since resources are controlled for each traffic class and/or slice, this approach is referred to as fine-grained resource control. The
IETF
describes a model (known as
5QI-aware model
) in which the traffic policer is implemented by means of a hierarchical model. On the one hand, the
PE
includes a traffic policer per slice (
slice policer
). This traffic policer follows a two-rate three-color rate limiter approach. Traffic exceeding the
Peak Information Rate (PIR)
(maximum bitrate) is dropped. Traffic under the
Committed Information Rate (CIR)
is marked as “green”, and traffic between the
CIR
and
PIR
is marked as “yellow”. In this way “yellow” traffic will be dropped in case of congestion. On the other hand, the
PE
includes a traffic policer per
QoS
class. This traffic policer follows a single-rate two-color rate limiter approach, where only a
CIR
is defined and all traffic exceeding this
CIR
is dropped. The class policer does not apply any rate limitation to the best effort traffic, allowing this type of traffic to use the bandwidth in the slice that is not used by the
QoS
classes.

A
PE
forwarding traffic to the 5G network, i.e., an egress
PE
, may optionally incorporate a hierarchical scheduler or shaper that ensures the guaranteed bandwidth for each
CIR
slice and for each
CIR

QoS
class defined.

In this paper, we propose a novel fine-grained resource control based on a
HTB
scheduler composed of three levels (Section
IV
.
IV-B
).

III-C
3
Coarse-grained resource control in the transit links

For the transit nodes of the transport network, the
IETF
proposes a simplified traffic treatment mechanism based on the combination of priority queues and
WFQ
or
DRR
queues. As a result, there is no fine-grained resource control at the transit nodes as they do not perform traffic treatments per traffic class. Instead, they handle data traffic following a DiffServ model that supports up to eight transport network
QoS
classes (as explained in Section
III
.
III-B
). All the slices defined in the
3GPP
domain are mapped to a
TN

QoS
class in the
IETF
domain. In scenarios where the number of classes or slices is greater than the number of
TN

QoS
classes, it is possible to aggregate multiple slices or classes into a single
TN

QoS
class.

Typically, traffic classes with strict latency requirements are associated with priority queues, while others are linked to
WFQ
or
DRR
non-priority queues. Non-priority queues are configured so that their weights are adjusted according to the
QoS
requirements of the classes sharing a
TN

QoS
class. With this coarse-grained resource control, traffic from best effort classes or traffic marked as “yellow” can be discarded during network congestion events while maintaining the
QoS
of the traffic flows in other classes.

This flat
QoS
model, based on traffic scheduling/prioritization, entails a different
QoS
treatment which is enforced in all
TN
nodes at transit links to apply the same
PHB
for all packets that belong to each
TN

QoS
class.

III-C
4
Capacity planning/management

Traffic Engineering (TE)
mechanisms, based on technologies such as
RSVP-TE
or
SR-TE
tunnels, are employed to ensure that sufficient capacity is available along the transport network for all the accepted slices. This is a topic that is not covered in this paper.

IV
HCTNS Model

IV-A

General View

Fig. 1:
General view of the
HCTNS
model.

A general view of the network slicing model proposed in this paper is illustrated in Fig.
1
, which also shows where our proposal for the ingress
fine-grained resource control
is placed. The top of the figure shows a simplified view of a 5G transport network, illustrating how data traffic is double-encapsulated into two tunnels: GTP and IPv6. The bottom of the figure shows the protocol stack for packets transmitted in the uplink direction.

Our design for the fine-grained ingress resource control addresses some limitations we have identified in the
IETF
model (described in Section
III
.
III-C
): (1) Inflexible and inefficient bandwidth sharing between different 5G
QoS
classes and slices. The
IETF
model allows unused bandwidth in a transport network class to be used only by the best-effort traffic. In contrast,
HCTNS
improves resource utilization between 5G
QoS
classes and between slices because the bandwidth not consumed by a traffic class is made available for use by other traffic classes in the same slice or by other network slices. To address this, we propose a three-level hierarchical ingress policing mechanism, including a new top-level policer, referred to as
global policer
(1st level), followed by
slice policers
(2nd level), and
class policers
(3rd level). Furthermore, we propose two mechanisms to determine how the bandwidth is shared between the 5G
QoS
classes of a slice and between the slices. (2) Lack of a traffic burst control mechanism. Some services may need a traffic burst control for meeting their applications demands. However, traffic bursts, as it will be demonstrated in Section
V
, can increase packet delays and compromise the
QoS
levels agreed in the
SLAs
associated with the classes and slices. For instance, packet delay in classes associated to
TN

QoS
class B may be increased when there are traffic bursts in the traffic classes that are assigned to
TN

QoS
class A. To address this,
HCTNS
incorporates a mechanism to control traffic bursts. This mechanism is based on the definition of two new parameters at the class policers. The three-level structure of the
HTB
that we propose controls the maximum accepted rate, i.e., global
CIR
, while also guaranteeing the
CIRs
per class and slice even in worst-case networks scenarios with bursty traffic. (3) In the
IETF
model, “yellow” traffic (the traffic of a class that arrives at the transport network’s ingress at a rate between the
CIR
and the
PIR
) can cause network congestion, and may be discarded at transit nodes of the transport network. In contrast, our approach ensures that all traffic admitted by the policers and forwarded through the transport network is treated with the same
QoS
guarantees, without causing congestion, and with no packet losses.

HCTNS
, in addition to our proposed
fine-grained ingress resource control
component, incorporates some of the features of the slicing model defined by the
IETF
(Section
III
). These features are the following:

•

Network Slice Identification (Section

.

•

QoS

HCTNS

values used in the

DSCP

•

Regarding coarse-grained resource control, our model is closely aligned with the model proposed by the

Although the paper focuses on 5G transport networks, HCTNS has been designed based on the IETF framework, allowing the proposed network slicing model to be applied to any IP or MPLS network. A detailed description of the fine-grained resource control mechanism proposed is explained below.

IV-B

A New Model for Fine-Grained Resource Control

Fine-grained resource control
is implemented in the ingress and egress
PE
routers of the transport network, which handle, respectively, various data plane functions in their input and output ports. As Fig.
2
illustrates, the input port of a
PE
is responsible for classifying the incoming packets according to the values of the VLAN tag and the
DSCP
header fields. This classification identifies the network slice and its associated
QoS
requirements. Based on these identifiers, packets are enqueued into a
HTB
queuing system that implements our proposed three-level hierarchical ingress policing mechanism. Additionally, the identifiers are then used to map the packet to the corresponding
TN

QoS
class. As Fig.
2
shows, the output port is responsible for sending the traffic over a IPv6 tunnel that carries its own
DSCP
mark. This marking is used by the coarse-grained resource control mechanism to identify the
TN

QoS
class and apply the appropiate
PHB
treatment. Packets are then enqueued into a queuing system based on priority and
DRR
queues, ensuring they are processed according to the
QoS
level defined for their
TN

QoS
class.

Fig. 2:
Input and output port processing at the
PE
ingress node.

The three-level hierarchical ingress policing mechanism proposed in this paper, implemented using the
HTB
rate-limiting algorithm, is illustrated in Fig.
3
. The
HTB
allows organizing traffic into a hierarchy of classes (or nodes), with each class ensuring its bandwidth guarantees. As shown in Fig.
3
, only the leaf nodes of the
HTB
tree have an associated queue, where packets can wait to be transmitted to an output port of the
PE
router. The tree-based hierarchical structure of the
HTB
allows classes under the same parent node to share bandwidth not consumed by the other classes. This is a key advantage of our proposal. The
IETF
in
[
9
]
employs a two-level hierarchy (slice and class) for its policing mechanism, which makes bandwidth sharing between slices and 5G
QoS
classes inefficient and without
QoS
guarantees. In the
IETF
model, unused bandwidth can only be used by the best-effort class within the same slice.

Fig. 3:
Three-level hierarchical ingress policing mechanism.

Fig.
3
shows the global policer at the root of the
HTB
tree. The global policer limits the total amount of traffic allowed to pass through the ingress
PE
router, defined by the
CIR
parameter. In our proposal, packets exceeding the
CIR
limit are discarded, making
CIR
and
PIR
equivalent in this case. All traffic admitted to traverse the transport network will be treated as “green” by the transit nodes, and will not be dropped, as we will explain later. The global policer is also important to handle the bursty traffic, since it controls that, on average, the total maximum capacity is not exceeded.

Fig. 4:
HTB
operation diagram.

The second level of the
HTB
hierarchy is occupied by slice policers, which enforce rate limiting at the slice level. These policers are defined by two parameters:
CIR
and
PIR
, which, in this case, may not be equivalent. The
CIR
represents the guaranteed bitrate of the slice, while the
PIR
defines its maximum. The sum of the
CIR
for all the slices must not exceed the
CIR
of the global policer. Regarding bandwidth sharing, any slice can make use of the bandwidth not consumed by other slices. The maximum bandwidth a slice can borrow from the root node is equal to its
PIR
-
CIR
. Slices where the
CIR
is equal to the
PIR
are not allowed to borrow bandwidth, making this an interesting method for controlling bandwidth consumption.

The third level of the
HTB
hierarchy is occupied by class policers, which are used by slices transporting various
QoS
flows with different
QoS
requirements (e.g., slice 1 and 2 in Fig.
3
). In network slices where all traffic flows receive the same
QoS
treatment, only the global and slice policers are applied. Class policers are defined by four parameters:
CIR
,
PIR
,
Committed Burst Size (CBS)
, and
Peak Burst Size (PBS)
. As explained later, the
CBS
and the
PBS
parameters play a key role in controlling the bursty traffic of the slices. On the other hand, the sum of the
CIR
values for all classes must not exceed the
CIR
of the corresponding slice policer. Besides, any class can make use of the bandwidth not consumed by other classes within the same slice (i.e., under the same parent node).

Our bandwidth sharing mechanism allows all slices and classes to borrow excess available bandwidth equally. However,
HCTNS
also introduces two more advanced methods to specify how excess or unused bandwidth can be shared between slices and, within a slice, among different
QoS
classes. These methods define how bandwidth is distributed when several nodes under the same parent node in the
HTB
try to obtain bandwidth from that parent node at the same time. The methods are implemented by controlling the distribution of tokens that is used to assign bandwidth in the
HTB
as we will explain later.

•

Weight/Quantum-based bandwidth sharing. The available bandwidth is shared among slices and
QoS
classes based on weights (relative) or quantums (fix amount of bytes) assigned to the slice and/or class policers. When defined at the slice level, the average bandwidth a slice
\theta
can borrow from the global available bandwidth (assuming that all the slices have packets to transmit), is calculated as:
B_{\theta}=B_{r}\cdot\frac{w_{\theta}}{\sum_{i=1}^{N}w_{i}}
, where
N
is the total number of slices,
B_{r}
is the global available bandwidth,
w_{\theta}
is the weight/quantum assigned to slice
\theta
and
w_{i}
the weight/quantum assigned to the
i
-th slice. When defined at both the slice and the class levels, the average bandwidth a class
\phi
within a slice
\theta
can borrow from the global available bandwidth (assuming that all classes within the same slice have packets to transmit) is calculated as:
B_{\theta,\phi}=B_{r}\cdot\frac{w_{\theta}}{\sum_{i=1}^{N}w_{i}}\cdot\frac{w_{\phi}}{\sum_{j=1}^{C}w_{j}}
, where C is the total number of classes within the slice
\theta
,
w_{\phi}
the weigh/quantum assigned to slice
\phi
and
w_{j}
the weight/quantum assigned to the
j
-th class within the slice
\theta
.

•

Priority-based bandwidth sharing. In this method, each slice and/or
QoS
class is assigned a priority value, with lower values indicating higher priority. The available global bandwidth is shared based on these priority levels. When defined at the slice level, slices with higher priority are granted bandwidth first, either until the bandwidth is exhausted or the slices have reached their
PIR
limit. When defined at both the slice and class levels, the bandwidth each class within a slice can borrow from the global available bandwidth depends on both the priority of the slice and the priority of the class within that slice.

These methods can be combined in various ways. For instance, the weight-based method may be applied at the slice level while the priority-based method may be applied at the class level. Another option is to use both methods at both levels, as they are not mutually exclusive. Nevertheless, regardless of the bandwidth sharing method employed, it is important to note that the
CIR
of each slice and
QoS
class is always guaranteed because we are sharing only the available excess bandwidth.

The use of a
HTB
on the ingress port of the
PE
router does not impact packet delay, despite the typical behavior of
HTBs
. The packet scheduler of the
HTB
first dequeues all traffic under the
CIR
limit for each leaf node, and after that, traffic between
CIR
and
PIR
. This causes traffic between
CIR
and
PIR
to wait for transmission, potentially violating the service delay requirements. However, in our proposal, the
HTB
operates on the
PE
ingress interface, where packets traverse the switching fabric at a very high speed. As a result, packet delay is negligible when processed by our
HTB
-based fine-grained resource control mechanism.

The control of traffic bursts, i.e., traffic received at an unlimited rate for a bounded time, is a key feature of our proposal. Each class policer is defined by two parameters that regulate traffic bursts for the
QoS
flows: the
CBS
and the
PBS
. The
CBS
indicates the maximum number of bits that can be transmitted above the
CIR
during a burst period, while the
PBS
indicates the maximum number of bits allowed to be transmitted above the
PIR
during the same period. To better illustrate how bandwidth sharing and traffic burst control works in our proposal, Fig.
4
presents the internal structure of the proposed three-level
HTB
.

Fig.
4
shows that each policer is associated with two token buckets. The bucket on the right, hereinafter referred to as
CIR
-bucket
is filled with tokens at the
CIR
rate, ensuring a guaranteed bandwidth. The bucket on the left, hereinafter referred to as
PIR
-bucket
, is filled with tokens at the
PIR
rate, ensuring that traffic does not exceeds a maximum bandwidth. The size of each bucket is determined by the
CBS
and
PBS
parameters. Buckets cannot store more tokens than their capacity (
CBS
or
PBS
) allows, so the tokens received when the buckets are full are discarded. Having this in mind, an
HTB
works as follows. If a packet arrives in the queue but there are no tokens available in the
PIR
-bucket
, the maximum bandwidth has been reached and the packet must wait in the queue until new tokens become available. If the
PIR
-bucket
is not empty, the
CIR
-bucket
is checked. If there are tokens available in the
CIR
-bucket
, the packet is within the
CIR
limit, and can be transmitted to the switching fabric, consuming a token from both buckets. Tokens consumed in the buckets of a child node must also be deducted from the parent nodes. For example, when a packet consumes tokens from the buckets associated with a class policer, tokens are also consumed from the buckets associated with the slice and global policers. In case there are no tokens available in the class policer
CIR
-bucket
, the child node requests tokens to the parent (slice) node, which first checks its
PIR
-bucket
. If no tokens are available in the
PIR
-bucket
the slice’s maximum bandwidth has been reached and the packet must wait in the queue. If the
PIR
-bucket
is not empty, and there are tokens available in the slice’s
CIR
-bucket
, the child node can borrow bandwidth from the parent (slice) node and the packet can be transmitted to the switching fabric (tokens are consumed in the slice and in the global level). If no tokens are found at the slice level (the slice
CIR
-bucket
is empty), the slice policer requests tokens to the global policer. In summary, if the child node and its ancestors have no tokens for the packet transmission, the packet waits in the queue. However, if tokens are available in the
HTB
tree, tokens are consumed at different levels to maintain bandwidth control at both the slice and the global levels. The
HTB
queues have a limited size, and when they are full, packets begin to be dropped. This behavior enables the enforcement of the policying function.

In the following, we discuss how the
HTB
can be configured to control traffic bursts. Traffic burst control is effectively disabled in a bucket when it is sized to store just the tokens needed to transmit a single packet. If the traffic burst control is set in the
CIR
-bucket
, but not in the
PIR
-bucket
, packets can be dequeued at most at the
PIR
rate. This is because packet transmissions require the simultaneous consumption of tokens from both the
CIR
and the
PIR
buckets, and the
PIR
-bucket
is only storing tokens for transmitting one packet. In contrast, if the traffic burst control is set in the
PIR
-bucket
but not in the
CIR
-bucket
, the child node may request additional tokens to its parent node, as Fig.
4
shows. However, this does not guarantee the transmission of the traffic burst either. Finally, if the traffic burst control is set in both the
CIR
and
PIR
buckets, packets can be dequeued immediately as long as there are tokens available in both buckets, regardless of the availability of tokens in the buckets of the parent nodes. This is the configuration we have selected to control traffic bursts in our proposal, with the
PBS
and
CBS
set to the same value. Using this configuration, the
PE
router can accept traffic bursts during a period that depends on the size of the buckets. After that burst period, a new burst will only be accepted if packets arrive at the ingress
PE
port at a lower rate than the
CIR
rate, so that the
CIR
-bucket
can be refilled, and at a lower rate than the
PIR
rate, so that the
PIR
-bucket
can also be refilled.

Our approach consists on enabling traffic burst control exclusively in leaf nodes, which implies specifying values for
CBS
and
PBS
only in the buckets associated with classes. To be able to accept traffic bursts, defining traffic burst control in the class policers is mandatory. Otherwise, even if the buckets associated with the slice and the global policer have tokens, a packet from a
QoS
class received at a rate higher than the class policer
PIR
would not be dequeued until a new token is received in the buckets associated with the class policer. The values of
CBS
and
PBS
will control the burst size allowed per
QoS
class. When the burst size is smaller than the
PBS
/
CBS
, there are sufficient tokens to forward all packets in the burst to the switching fabric at the rate they are received. However, if the burst size exceeds the
PBS
/
CBS
, only part of the burst is forwarded at the rate packets are received. This forwarding ends when both buckets are depleted. The remainder of the burst will be enqueued in the
HTB
queue until it reaches its capacity and will be transmitted at the
CIR
rate defined by the class policer. Any additional packets beyond the queue’s limit will be discarded.

As shown in Fig.
4
, packet transmission is allowed if there are tokens in the class policer, regardless the availability of tokens in the parent nodes. However, when packet transmission is allowed, tokens are consumed from the policers at all levels. This may result, when sending traffic bursts, in the parent nodes not having enough tokens in their buckets. In such cases, the token counters in the
HTB
parent nodes can take negative values, which can be considered as ”tokens to be paid”. Nodes with ”negative” tokens cannot share tokens with their child classes, until the counters return to positive values. During this period, traffic will only flow at the
CIR
rate of the leaf nodes. This ensures that while the global maximum rate of the parent node may be temporally exceeded, it is eventually compensated to maintain the average maximum rate of the parent node within the defined limit.

Fig. 5:
Input and output port processing at the
PE
egress node.

As Fig.
5
shows, a fine-grained resource control is also implemented in the
PE
egress routers. The input port of the
PE
router terminates the IPv6 tunnel, forwarding packets to the output ports of the
PE
based on their IP destination address. On the physical output ports, various logical interfaces are defined with the VLAN-tagged mode enabled to carry tags identifying the network slices in the
3GPP
domain. The output port applies a fine-grained resource control using a hierarchical two-level
DRR
queuing system. We have adopted the same model proposed by the
IETF
in
[
9
]
, and shown in Fig.
6
. The first
DRR
level is used to differentiate the 5G
QoS
classes within the same network slice, while the second
DRR
level differentiates the slices.

Fig. 6:
Hierarchical

DRR
queuing system, as proposed by the
IETF
.

The
IETF
resource control mechanism can maintain the
CIR
of 5G slices and their associated traffic classes even in scenarios with network congestion. However, thanks to the global policer proposed in
HCTNS
, if a slice sends traffic above its
CIR
it is mainly because there is unused bandwidth available from other slices. So, by applying
HCTNS
, the 5G slices are allowed to consume more bandwidth without causing network congestion.

Although the model has been described considering uplink communications, the concepts described here are also valid for downlink traffic.

This approach ensures that the buckets have time to refill, allowing multiple bursts per class to be observed rather than just one. During

During

In the
[
16
]
model, the bursts accepted from all traffics are classified as “green”, being forwarded to the highest priority queue. As the priority queue is not saturated, these packets are transmitted. When bursts are accepted, the highest priority queue uses a higher transmission rate, which reduces the transmission rate of the lower priority queue, causing the downward spikes in bandwidth in Fig.
b.
In
HCTNS
, a very close bandwidth behavior (Fig.
c) is observed, accepting a similar amount of burst packers. In this case, the configured

and

TN

HTB
ingress queue. If this queue becomes full, any additional packets are discarded. As explained in Section
IV
.
IV-B
, when traffic burst control is enabled and bursts are accepted by the class policers (or the slice policer for
URLLC
traffic), both the slice and global policers may temporally have “negative” tokens. Consequently, BE traffic is not admitted by the ingress policer until the slice policer’s bucket recovers the tokens it owed restoring a positive balance. Until the global policer’s bucket is not fully recovered, BE traffic cannot borrow bandwidth beyond the
eMBB
slice’s
CIR
. As a result, during burst periods, the bandwidth available to the BE traffic shows also temporary downward spikes. This mechanism ensures that, while packets arriving at rates exceeding the
CIR
defined in the global policer may be temporally accepted, the average bandwidth will remain within the defined limits.

As shown in Fig.
d, traffic bursts impact the latency of all flows when using the
[
16
]
model. This is because the accepted burst packets (“green”) are forwarded to the highest priority queue, regardless of the traffic flow to which they belong. As a result, all burst traffic from the flows undergoes similar treatment. For example, all traffic flows experience a similar maximum latency, around 15 ms. Packets marked as “yellow” are forwarded to the lowest priority queue, which is congested. Since BE traffic continuously enters the lowest priority queue at 100 Mbps and the lowest priority queue cannot transmit a packet if there are packets in the highest priority queue, most “yellow” packets from the burst will be discarded due to queue overflow. For the same reason, “yellow” packets, such as those from
URLLC
traffic, experience latencies exceeding 130 ms, which is unacceptable.

In

PE

PE

CIR
, which is adjusted to the available network capacity, packets do not accumulate over time in these queues. Therefore, it is crucial to properly dimension the queue sizes to handle bursty traffic effectively, preventing packet loss.

Since

QoS

VI
Conclusions

In this paper, we have proposed
HCTNS
, a slicing model that employs a three-level hierarchical
HTB
to implement a fine-grained resource control mechanism at the ingress node of the transport network. This mechanism is combined with coarse-grained resource control in the transport network nodes to allow meeting the bandwidth and latency requirements of services defined within the slices, even in worst-case scenarios and with bursty traffic, while achieving a high level of network bandwidth sharing both within and across slices. One of the strengths of
HCTNS
is that all the traffic admitted to be processed in the transport network is treated with
QoS
guarantees, not only the traffic under the guaranteed bitrate but also traffic exceeding it (up to certain limit), and even for limited traffic bursts.

In real deployments, each network segment operates independently as an administrative domain, meaning that end-to-end performance depends on the specific traffic management solutions implemented in each segment. HCTNS ensures that the latency, bandwidth, and packet loss requirements defined in the SLAs are met within the transport network, contributing to their fulfillment end-to-end. While HCTNS does not impact the performance of the RAN or core network, it is essential for maintaining service guarantees in the transport network and, by extension, across the entire system.

An experimental platform combining virtual and physical network elements has allowed us to verify that
HCTNS
outperforms not only the model being standardized by the
IETF
, but also a state-of-the-art reference approach, in terms of bandwidth utilization, latency and traffic burst control.

As future steps for this research, we believe that centralized policies are needed to guarantee end-to-end delay bounds in the transport network. In addition, models are required to translate these policies into configurations for data plane functions in the transport network equipment. This includes the establishment and configuration of IPv6 tunnels based on
SR
with routing algorithms driven by
QoS
metrics.
