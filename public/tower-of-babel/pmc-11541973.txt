The Association Between Constipation and Positron Emission Tomography and Blood-Based Biomarkers in Older Cognitively Unimpaired Adults with Higher Amyloid-β Burden
Authors: Heling Chu, Chuyi Huang, Fang Xie, Qihao Guo
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
IntroductionConstipation may be linked to cognitive decline and a higher risk of Alzheimer’s disease (AD). We aimed to investigate the association between constipation and positron emission tomography (PET) and blood-based AD biomarkers in older cognitively unimpaired (CU) adults with higher Aβ burden.MethodsConstipation was diagnosed according to Rome IV criteria and the severity of constipation was evaluated by using a validated self-reported questionnaire. The participants underwent the examination of plasma AD biomarkers and 18F-florbetapir PET and 18F-MK6240 PET scans; the latter was only performed in the validation cohort. Correlation and multiple linear regression analyses were used to investigate the association between constipation and AD biomarkers.ResultsTwo cohorts were included in our study. A total of 404 older participants with 126 of whom Aβ-PET positive were enrolled in the development cohort. Multiple linear regression analysis showed constipation was associated with plasma t-Tau, p-Tau-181, and neurofilament light chain (NfL) in participants with Aβ-PET (+). Meanwhile, no/mild constipation was associated with lower Aβ-PET standard uptake value ratio. The association between constipation and plasma biomarkers was different in the subgroups stratified by age, sex and APOE ε4 genotype. The above associations were further validated in the validation cohort containing 36 Aβ-PET (+) participants. Importantly, no/mild constipation was associated with less Tau burden evaluated by 18F-MK6240 PET Braak stages.ConclusionOur data indicate that no/mild constipation may be associated with lower plasma t-Tau, p-Tau-181, and NfL as well as less Aβ and Tau burden in older CU adults with Aβ deposition. Improving constipation and being away from defecation disorders may help reduce the risk of AD development.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1007/s40120-024-00666-x.

Introduction

Alzheimer’s disease (AD), as the most common cause of dementia, accounts for 60–80% of cases. AD and related dementias contribute to a progressive decline in daily activities in older adults [1]. AD is a continuum starting with a prolonged phase of β-amyloid (Aβ) accumulation without symptoms. Brain accumulation of Aβ precedes cognitive decline in people who were cognitively unimpaired (CU) [2]. Thus, timely intervention to the risk factors may contribute beneficial effects to prevent cognitive declines in this population [3]. Lifestyle changes show evidence for reducing the risk of dementia due to AD. As a result, identification of the modifiable lifestyle-related risk factors in older adults of preclinical AD and the subsequent interventions may bring about promising early prevention.

Functional constipation is a functional bowel disorder with the predominant symptoms of difficult, infrequent, or incomplete defecation, which is quite common in the older population [4]. Higher prevalence of constipation is associated with all-cause dementia in older individuals [5]. It has also been revealed that patients with AD frequently have constipation [6]. Moreover, constipation is associated with an increased risk of AD in large cohorts [7]. However, few studies have concerned the relationship between constipation and AD biomarkers in older CU participants with positive Aβ-positron emission tomography (PET).

Besides the well-known concept that PET biomarkers are crucial for presenting AD pathology, blood-based biomarkers show their superiority, such as minimally invasive, safe, and convenient beyond cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) biomarkers. Despite of the possible validation, blood-based biomarkers include the plasma Aβ42/40 ratio, phosphorylated Tau (p-Tau), and neurofilament light chain (NfL) are expected to represent each AT(N) category [8]. Importantly, plasma p-Tau-181 is associated with CSF p-Tau-181 and positive Tau-PET, which also predicts subsequent development of dementia due to AD [9]. What is more, blood-based biomarkers may be useful for selecting individuals eligible for treatments and monitoring the corresponding effects in AD management [10].

In the current study, we investigated the association between constipation and PET and blood-based AD biomarkers in older CU adults with higher Aβ burden verified by positive of Aβ-PET. Also, similar investigation was simultaneously performed in participants with negative of Aβ-PET and total participants. The results were further validated by additionally using Tau-PET. The aim was to determine whether constipation could be a potential target for reducing AD pathology.

Methods

Study Participants

Two cohorts were included. The participants aged 50–85 years from Chinese Preclinical Alzheimer's Disease Study (C-PAS) cohort in Shanghai Sixth People’s Hospital Affiliated to Shanghai Jiao Tong University School of Medicine were recruited as a retrospective study (development cohort: Jan 2019–September 2021; validation cohort: October 2021–October 2022). The criteria for inclusion and exclusion were on the basis of our previous study [11]. CU was determined when (1) the score of education-adjusted Chinese version of Mini-Mental State Examination (MMSE) was more than 24 for > 6 years of education, or more than 20 for 1–6 years of education [12]; and (2) the participants did not meet the diagnostic criteria of MCI [13] or dementia [14] with no impairment of any cognitive domain. Standardized neuropsychological assessment was also performed and single molecule array (Simoa) was used for testing plasma AD biomarkers. The participants underwent 18F-florbetapir and 18F-MK6240 PET scans within 3 months after the collection of blood samples to evaluate Aβ and Tau deposition. Only the participants in the validation cohort underwent a 18F-MK6240 PET scan. All participants or their next-of-kin signed the informed consent before being enrolled. This study was performed in accordance with the principles of the Declaration of Helsinki. Shanghai Sixth People’s Hospital Affiliated to Shanghai Jiao Tong University School of Medicine Ethics Committee granted the ethical approval (No. 2019-032).

Clinical Data and Cognitive Assessment

The basic clinical data containing age, sex, years of education, body mass index (BMI), apolipoprotein E (APOE) ε4 genotype, smoking, alcohol consumption, the history of hypertension, coronary heart disease, hyperlipidemia, and diabetes mellitus were recorded. MMSE and the Chinese version of Montreal Cognitive Assessment-Basic (MoCA-BC) [15] were used to evaluate the general cognitive performance. In order to assess whether there was impairment of any cognitive domain, the standardized neuropsychological assessment, containing Auditory Verbal Learning Test (AVLT) 30-min delayed free recall and AVLT recognition [16], Boston Naming Test (BNT) [17], Animal Verbal Fluency Test (AFT) [18], and Shape Trail Test Part A and B (STT-A and STT-B) [19], was also performed.

Assessment of Constipation

The constipation of our study represented functional constipation and was diagnosed according to Rome IV criteria fulfilled for the last 3 months with symptom onset at least 6 months prior to diagnosis [4].

The severity of constipation was evaluated by using a validated self-reported questionnaire including the choice of never (score 0), mild, no medication required (score 1) and severe, medication required (score 2).

Amyloid and Tau PET Imaging

The 18F-florbetapir and 18F-MK6240 PET scans were performed to assess the burden of brain Aβ and Tau at the Huashan hospital PET center applying a PET/CT system (Biograph mCT Flow PET/CT; Siemens, Erlangen, Germany). The protocol was according to our previous report [20]. Visual rating was used for defining 18F-florbetapir PET positive images [21]. We calculated the global standard uptake value ratios (SUVRs) by weighted averaging of the cortical regions of interest (ROIs) [22]. The inferior cerebellum was used as the reference region for calculating 18FMK-6240 SUVRs. Tau PET was defined as positive when the SUVRs for each ROI were 2.5 or higher standard deviations (SDs) than young CU individuals’ mean SUVRs [23]. PET Braak stages were defined as previously reported [24]. Braak stages were determined only when the previous and current stages were positive but higher stages failed to achieve [23]. Three nuclear medicine specialists blinded to the clinical data performed the PET image interpretation and the results were decided by the agreement of at least two specialists.

Measurements of Plasma AD Biomarkers

Plasma AD biomarkers including Aβ42, Aβ40, t-Tau, p-Tau-181, and NfL were detected via Simoa in accordance with previous studies [20, 25]. We performed the reagent pretreatment and sample loading by the instructions of the manufacture. The four-parameter curve fit algorithm using Simoa HD-1 Analyzer software was used for determining the concentrations of each biomarker (pg/mL).

Statistical Analysis

In the development cohort, we performed statistical analyses to the Aβ-PET positive and negative groups by using SPSS 22.0. Categorical variables data were presented as percentages and compared using Chi-square tests or Fisher exact tests (2-tailed). Depending on the data distribution and the number of groups, data for continuous variables were expressed as means with SDs or medians with interquartile ranges (IQRs) and analyzed using two-tailed Student's t test, one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA), Mann–Whitney U test, or Kruskal–Wallis H test. P < 0.05 was used for judging the statistical significance. Spearman’s correlation analysis was applied to correlate constipation with each biomarker. The association between constipation and the biomarkers was determined depending on a multiple linear regression model adjusted for sex, age, BMI, education, APOE ε4 genotype, MoCA-BC, smoking, alcohol consumption, and previous history, such as hypertension, hyperlipidemia, coronary heart disease, and diabetes mellitus. Besides P values, we also reported unstandardized coefficient estimates (β), standardized estimates (SE), and 95% confidence intervals (CI) of SE. We also conducted subgroup analyses stratified by age (> 65 years, ≤ 65 years), sex (male, female) and APOE ε4 genotype (ε4 carriers, non-carriers) to explore the potential association in all subgroups. The same multiple linear regression model was used for investigating the association between constipation and plasma and PET biomarkers including Tau-PET Braak stages in Aβ-PET positive individuals in the validation cohort. The figures were prepared by GraphPad Prism 9.00 software. Statisticians blinded to the groups conducted all the analyses.

Results

Characteristics of the Participants

A total of 404 CU participants (126 males and 209 females) with the average age of 64.4 ± 7.8 years were included in the development cohort. Aβ-PET positive was found in 126 participants. The constipation scores were 0 (77.8%), 1 (16.7%), and 2 (5.5%) in the Aβ-PET-positive participants.

Validation cohort contained 36 Aβ-PET-positive participants. The numbers of participants with different Braak stages were as follows: Braak 0 (16), Braak I (6), Braak II (6), Braak III (5), Braak IV (1), Braak V (2), and Braak VI (0). Discussion

Our current study mainly investigated the association between constipation and PET and plasma AD biomarkers, especially in older CU adults with higher Aβ burden. The results revealed that no/mild constipation was associated with lower level of plasma t-Tau, p-Tau-181, and NfL. Subsequently, subgroup analyses stratified by age, sex, and APOE ε4 genotype brought out more useful information. The above associations were further validated and constipation also had association with Tau burden evaluated by PET Braak stages. These findings have not been reported previously, which suggests that constipation could be considered somewhat an independent predictor more or less relevant for AD pathology.

Constipation is a common symptom in elderly people, which may affect their quality of life. It was demonstrated that high prevalence of constipation was observed in patients with all-cause dementia [5]. Meanwhile, it has been demonstrated that the severity of dementia is related to constipation possibly due to the decrease in water intake and mobility in patients with AD [26]. Moreover, a significant correlation has been found between constipation and faster progression of AD symptoms [27]. To our knowledge, constipation is a modifiable risk factor always resulting from loss of dietary fiber consumption and reduction of physical exercise in older people. Also, laxatives are commonly used for alleviating constipation. Nevertheless, the use of laxatives to improve constipation was associated with higher risk of dementia [28], possibly owing to the disturbance of gut microbiota by the laxatives. Therefore, it suggests that developing an optimal lifestyle consisting of beneficial dietary and exercise habits may be the better choice to ameliorate constipation followed by the probable cognitive improvement. Furthermore, the subgroup analyses stratified by age, sex, and APOE ε4 genotype were conducted, which may indicate the associations of constipation with different biomarkers in the special groups. Age and sex are common risk factors of AD [29]. Moreover, APOE ε4 carriers have a higher lifetime incidence of AD and an earlier onset [30]. Unfortunately, constipation did not correlate with any of the plasma biomarkers in APOE ε4 carriers. However, no/mild constipation was associated with lower Aβ-PET SVUR in CU APOE ε4 carriers with Aβ-PET (+), indicating that improving constipation may be more likely to reduce Aβ pathology.

Compared with the CSF biomarkers, blood-based biomarkers are non-invasive and more accessible in routine clinical cares, which may enable earlier and faster diagnoses and assist risk evaluation, early identification, and management of AD [31]. Plasma Aβ42/40 may be a predictor for the conversion from CU to clinical MCI and dementia with AD pathophysiology [32]. Meanwhile, plasma NfL could be regarded as a biomarker to predict the development of neurodegeneration in AD subjects in longitudinal studies [33]. Importantly, the various plasma p-Tau isoforms, e.g., 181, 217, and 231, have shown to be in agreement with Tau biomarkers in CSF, amyloid and Tau PET, neuropathological changes, and diagnosis across the AD continuum moderately to excellently [31]. Moreover, it has been revealed that, compared with the Aβ-negative population, high-plasma p-Tau181 carries incremental risks of dementia due to AD in CU participants with positive Aβ [34]. Based on the 2018 National Institute on Aging-Alzheimer’s Association (NIA-AA) Research Framework, the AT(N) biomarker profiles presenting A+T+ can be classified into the biomarker category of AD [35]. In addition, in the 2021 International Working Group recommendations of clinical diagnosis of AD, CU individuals are considered to have high risk of AD if CSF or PET indicates Aβ positive and Tau positive [36]. Therefore, we focused on the Tau biomarkers in CU individuals with Aβ-PET (+) and further validated the results by using Tau-PET. Tau-PET Braak stages have the potential to stratify patients according to their brain patterns of Tau propagation and to provide information on the patients’ Tau pathology and disease stages [24]. We found that constipation was associated with Tau burden evaluated by Tau-PET Braak stages, suggesting no/mild constipation may have more evidence to reduce the risk of AD dementia. As constipation is a kind of modifiable lifestyle which could be a tractable target for early intervention, we speculate that early improvement of constipation may be helpful for delaying the pathological process of AD.

The possible mechanisms of the association between constipation and AD are complicated and unclear. In AD mice, the AD pathological condition induced constipation through alteration of the excretion parameters, changes in the histological structure, mucin secretion, muscarinic acetylcholine receptors downstream signaling pathway, and endoplasmic reticulum stress response of the colon [37]. The gut–brain axis theory may partially explain the association between constipation and AD. In this theory, the central nervous system communicates with the enteric nervous system, intestinal mucosa, and muscle layer through afferent and efferent pathways. The pathological injury of the axis may influence intestinal activity and result in constipation [38]. Constipation can cause disturbance of gut microbiome and gut dysbiosis, which affects the gut–brain axis and contributes to neurological disorders including AD [39, 40]. Previous study indicates that, in AD mice, constipation can increase Aβ accumulation and microglia levels in the brain, accompanied by the decrease of the transcription of defense against bacteria in the colonic tissue [7]. It has been demonstrated that constipation decreases fecal short-chain fatty acids (SCFAs), which may be due to the disturbance of the gut microbiome [41]. In addition, researchers have found that specific differences are in the gut microbiome at the preclinical stage of AD and that the species most associated with preclinical AD status included Oscillibacter and Faecalibacterium prausnitzii [42], which are related to the metabolic abnormality of SCFAs [43, 44]. Importantly, the gut microbiome correlated with preclinical AD neuropathology containing Aβ and Tau pathological biomarkers [42]. Therefore, we speculate that improving constipation in the early stage of AD may help to ameliorate the disturbance of the gut microbiome, and then increase the beneficial metabolites like SCFAs to reduce AD pathology such as Aβ and Tau burden.

Limitations

Several limitations of this study should be considered when interpreting the results. It was a single-center retrospective study with a relatively small sample size. Meanwhile, the agreement of CSF and plasma biomarkers has not been demonstrated as the CSF biomarkers were not detected. Moreover, the detailed evaluation of constipation such as stool consistency, bowel movements, quality of life as well as dietary habits was not investigated. We can further use the validated questionnaires such as Constipation Scoring System, Patient Assessment of Constipation-Symptom and Patient Assessment of Constipation-Quality of Life to make a detailed evaluation. In addition, the current work had not studied the gut microbiota which may be involved in the mechanisms of the association between constipation and AD biomarkers. Besides, other biomarkers like neuroinflammatory markers especially glial fibrillary acidic protein were not been included. The study had not included easily accessible tools, possibly feasible in clinical practice. For instance, we can further investigate the association between consumption and magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) biomarkers and whether non-specific inflammatory parameters such as neutrophil-to-lymphocyte ratio, C-reactive protein and interleukins are involved in the mechanism of constipation to influence AD pathology. Further prospective study with long-term follow-up containing the improvement of the above limitations should be conducted to validate our findings.

Conclusions

Our data indicate that no/mild constipation is associated with lower plasma t-Tau, p-Tau-181, and NfL in older CU adults with Aβ deposition determined by a 18F-florbetapir PET scan, which is further validated in the validation cohort. Similar findings were not observed in participants with Aβ-PET (-). The associations between constipation and plasma biomarkers may be different in the subgroups stratified by age, sex and APOE ε4 genotype. Moreover, no/mild constipation also has association with Tau burden evaluated by 18F-MK6240 PET Braak stages. To validate the findings and enable them to clinical practice, further study in the participants with preclinical AD should observe whether improving constipation and being away from defecation disorders could prevent the cognitive decline and conversion to cognitive impairment in a long-term follow-up. Also, the changes of multiple AD biomarkers should be investigated. Moreover, whether constipation improvement could ameliorate cognitive function in patients with MCI or mild dementia due to AD should also be studied. Furthermore, different biomarkers may be used for evaluating the effects of constipation improvement on AD pathology in the individuals with different ages, sexes and APOE ε4 genotypes according to our findings.
