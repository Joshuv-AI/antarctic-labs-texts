A Tight Lower Bound on Trees in Graphs
Chase Wilson

Abstract
Mubayi and Verstraete conjectured that if T T is a tree on t + 1 t+1 vertices, then any n n -vertex graph G G with average degree d d contains at least n d ( d − 1 ) ⋯ ( d − t + 1 ) nd(d-1)\cdots(d-t+1) labeled copies of T T as long as d d is sufficiently large compared to t t . We prove this is true and show that when the diameter of T T is at least 3 3 , equality holds iff G G is the disjoint union of cliques of size d + 1 d+1 . When the diameter is 2 2 , equality holds iff G G is d d -regular.

Let

n

[
11
]
. The

that for any bipartite graph

\liminf_{n\rightarrow\infty}\frac{|\mathrm{Hom}(H,G)|}{n^{|V(H)|}}\geq p^{|E(H)|}.

(1)

For progress on this conjecture, see Conlon, Lee and Sidorenko
[
3
]
and the references therein.

A special case is the problem of counting walks of a specific length: if

[

[
15
]

[
5

. Mulholland and Smith
[

and, independently, Blakley and Roy

[

]

Erdős and Simonovits

conjectured further that

is non-decreasing over odd values of

[

A

Let

Organization.
We prove Lemma

by bounding the entropy of a random “greedy” embedding of

into

[

]
. Analogous entropy arguments have been used to get tight lower bounds for

Entropy

Our proof of Theorem
1
uses entropy methods. We briefly describe entropy and state its properties that we will use.
If
S
is a finite set and
X,Y
are
S
-valued random variable, then the
entropy of
X
is given by

H[X]=\sum_{y\in\mathrm{Im}(X)}P(X=y)\log\left(\frac{1}{P(X=y)}\right)

and the
conditional entropy

H[X|Y]
is given by

H[X|Y]=E_{y\sim Y}[H[(X|Y=y)]]

Two of the basic properties of entropy are as follows:

Proposition 1
.

Let
X,X_{1},X_{2},\dots,X_{k}
be random variables where
X
has values in
S
and
X_{i}
has values in
S_{i}
for
1\leq i\leq k
.

1
.

[Uniform bound]

H[X]\leq\log(|\mathrm{Im}(X)|)
with equality iff
X
has uniform distribution.

2
.

[The Chain Rule]

(X_{1},X_{2},\cdots,X_{k})
is an
S_{1}\times\cdots\times S_{k}
random variable and
H[X_{1},\cdots,X_{k}]=H[X_{1}]+H[X_{2}|X_{1}]+\cdots H[X_{k}|X_{1},\cdots,X_{k-1}]
.

The Model Proof

Let us begin by showing how to prove Theorem
1
under the assumption that
P(\phi_{i}=v)=\deg(v)/2|E(G)|
for every
v\in V(G)
, which is true in graphs which have e.g. girth at least
t
. In general, however, this is false, but as we will see the quantities
P(\phi_{i}=v)
and
\deg(v)/2|E(G)|
are very close together and the real proof of Theorem
1
is an adaptation of the following proof to deal with this small error.

We compute the entropy of
\phi
. By the chain rule,

H[\phi]=H[\phi^{1}]+\sum_{i=1}^{t-1}H[\phi^{i+1}|\phi^{i}]

Since
\phi^{1}
is uniform over all edges,
H[\phi^{1}]=\log(2|E(G)|)=\log(nd)
and

H[\phi^{i+1}|\phi^{i}]=\sum_{v\in V}\frac{d(v)}{2|E(G)|}\log(n_{+}(v))\geq\sum_{v\in V}\frac{d(v)}{2|E(G)|}\log(d(v)-i)\geq\log(d-i)

where the second inequality is Jensen’s Inequality aplied to the function
x\log(x-i)
. This gives
\log(|\mathrm{Inj}(T,G)|)\geq H[\phi]\geq\log(nd(d-1)\cdots(d-t+1))
, and exponentiating proves Theorem
1
.

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{v\in V}L(v)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{u,v\in V(G)}\frac{2|E|}{d(v)}P(\phi^{i}\in\Gamma_{i}(v,u))L(v)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\sum_{u,v\in V(G)}P(\phi^{i}\in\Gamma_{i}(v,u))\frac{L(v)}{c_{v}}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{4}\sum_{u,v\in V(G)}P(\phi^{i}\in\Gamma_{i}(v,u))\frac{L(v)}{c_{v}}+P(\phi^{i}\in\Gamma_{i}(u,v))\frac{L(u)}{c_{u}}

\displaystyle\geq\frac{1}{8}\sum_{u,v\in V(G)}P(\phi^{i}\in\Gamma_{i}(v,u))\left(\frac{L(v)}{c_{v}}+\frac{L(u)}{c_{u}}\right)

\displaystyle\geq\frac{1}{8}\sum_{u,v\in V(G)}P(\phi^{i}\in\Gamma_{i}^{nc}(v,u))\left(\frac{L(v)}{c_{v}}+\frac{L(u)}{c_{u}}\right)

Each term is non-negative because the function
x\log(x-k)
is convex when
x\geq 2k
. Similary, by convexity,
L(v)/c_{v}
is increasing when
c_{v}\geq 1
and decreasing when
i/(d-i)\leq c_{v}\leq 1
.
∎

Here is a short argument that gets a bound

|\mathrm{Inj}(T,G)|\geq n(d-t(t-1))^{t}=\left(1-O\left(\frac{t^{3}}{d}\right)\right)n(d)_{t}

Proof.

For simplicity assume that

. Instead of requiring our walk to be a path i.e. not intersecting itself, an adversary will prevent us from taking certain steps; they will try to minimize the number of walks we can take by - at step

On the one hand, our adversary can restrict us to only taking paths by forbidding us from traveling to vertices we have already visited. On the other hand, the adversary’s optimal strategy does not need to depend on the history of our walk - indeed at step

they are simply trying to minimize the number of walks of length

we can take from that point onward. So for every vertex

. Let

. Then the number of walks we can make, is at least the total number of walks in

which has average degree at least

. So, by a well known lower bound on the number of walks in a graph
[

Lemma 7.1

(Error Bound on Jensen’s Inequality)
.

Let

and

. Then

\left(\frac{1}{d}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\frac{d_{i}}{n}\log(d_{i}-k)\right)-\log(d-k)=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\left[c_{i}\log\left(\frac{c_{i}d-k}{d-k}\right)-(c_{i}-1)\left(\frac{d}{d-k}\right)\right]\\

In general for a differentiable function

Applying this to

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\left[c_{i}\log\left(\frac{c_{i}d-k}{d-k}\right)-(c_{i}-1)\left(\frac{d}{d-k}\right)\right]
