Correlation of Sclerostin Serum Levels with the Pathogenesis of Coronary Artery Disease
Authors: Shahlaa Shafiq Rozoqi, Thikra Ali Allwsh
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Background:Sclerostin is primarily known for its role in inhibiting bone formation; however, emerging evidence suggests that it may also play a role in vascular health. Coronary artery disease (CAD) is a heart condition characterized by the narrowing or blockage of the coronary arteries. The relationship between sclerostin and CAD is complex and not yet fully understood. The presented study was to investigate a novel perspective on sclerostin by evaluating its levels as a potential marker for diagnosing stable CAD and examining its relationship with cardiac biochemical variables.Materials and Methods:A total of 160 individuals participated in this study, divided into two groups: 80 patients with stable coronary heart disease (CHD) and 80 individuals in the control group.Results:The result showed a highly significant decrease in sclerostin levels in the serum of the patient group (71.256 pg/mL) compared to the control group (98.426 pg/mL). In addition, there was a significant reduction in the level of sclerostin in all patient subgroups compared to the control subgroups, according to age. Additionally, there were significant differences (P < 0.001) in sclerostin levels between the male and female groups in both patient and control groups. The results showed a negative correlation between sclerostin and creatine kinase-MB (CK-MB), troponin, lactate dehydrogenase (LDH), glutamate oxaloacetate transaminase (GOT), glutamate pyruvate transaminase (GPT), C-reactive protein (CRP), and myoglobin, while the relation with albumin and total protein is a positive correlation (P < 0.01).Conclusion:Sclerostin may play a crucial role in the development of CAD and could serve as a marker for diagnosing and monitoring its progression.

INTRODUCTION

Coronary heart disease (CHD) is one of the fatal chronic diseases that leads to the death of millions of people annually in different parts of the world.[1] This illness encompasses a broad range of clinical disorders, including myocardial infarction (MI), unstable angina, stable angina, and sudden death.[2] Important complications are heart failure and arrhythmia.[3] A coronary artery blockage results in an insufficient blood supply to the heart, which causes the symptoms of coronary artery disease (CAD).[4] The most frequent sign of CAD is MI, which is brought on by the degradation of the coronary artery endothelium or the disruption of a susceptible atherosclerotic plaque.[5] A coagulation cascade is started when the atherosclerotic plaque ruptures and releases thrombogenic materials.[6] An increase in cardiac biomarkers indicates the permanent necrosis of myocardial cells that ultimately results from MI.[7]

The pathogenesis of CHD is complex and still poorly understood. Low bone mass and high vascular calcification burden are frequently found in elderly patients, but the underlying mechanism is not clear yet.[8910] As a novel regulator of bone resorption, sclerostin is also expressed in atherosclerosis, but its role is controversial.[11] Sclerostin is an important bone remodeling protein, but may also play a role in cardiovascular disease. In preclinical models, sclerostin has been reported to protect against abdominal aortic aneurysm and atherosclerosis formation and prevent cardiac rupture.[121314] Sclerostin is recognized to be an endogenous inhibitor of the Wnt pathway; therefore, it is foreseeable that the Wnt inhibitory function of sclerostin is important in a tissue such as the heart.[1516] Moreover, Wnt is involved in various aspects of heart diseases;[17] thus, the function of sclerostin in the heart in Wnt-independent ways would also be very interesting and beneficial to investigate.

There are several clinical tests to diagnose CHD or predict its occurrence. The earlier the diagnosis, the greater the chance of controlling it.[18] Cardiac biochemical tests are essential diagnostic tools used to evaluate the health and function of the heart, particularly in detecting myocardial injury or disease.[19] These tests measure specific biomarkers released into the bloodstream when cardiac cells are damaged.[2021] The most commonly assessed biomarkers include cardiac troponins (cTnI and cTnT), which are highly sensitive and specific indicators of myocardial injury, making them the gold standard for diagnosing acute myocardial infarction (AMI).[22] Other markers, such as creatine kinase-MB (CK-MB) and myoglobin, are also used but are less specific than troponins.[23] Additionally, natriuretic peptides, such as B-type natriuretic peptide (BNP), are evaluated to assess heart failure and ventricular stress. However, the weaknesses of cardiac biochemical tests include potential false positives and negatives, influenced by factors such as renal failure or other systemic illnesses that may alter biomarker levels.[24] Moreover, while these tests are informative in diagnosis, they must be interpreted in conjunction with clinical findings and other diagnostic procedures to avoid misdiagnosis or over-reliance on biochemical markers alone.[2526] These tests, in conjunction with clinical symptoms, electrocardiograms, and imaging studies, play a critical role in diagnosing, monitoring, and managing cardiovascular diseases effectively. Presently, there are no simple, non-invasive tests available to specifically detect unstable atherosclerotic plaque.[27]

This study aimed to explore a new perspective of sclerostin by using its level as a marker for diagnosing CHD in stable coronary disease and to find the correlation of sclerostin concentration and other biochemical cardiac variables.

MATERIALS AND METHODS

The study design is a case-control study, which included 160 individuals distributed into two groups: 80 patients with stable CHD, ages ranging between 46 and 74 years, and 80 samples, which are the control group, with 46–70 years. The patients’ samples were collected from Shar hospital in Erbil city and the heart center in Mosul city from March to August 2024, after diagnoses of the cases were confirmed by the cardiologist who depended on electrocardiogram and coronary angiogram, which were carried out in the above-mentioned hospitals. The sample collection was limited to patients with stable CHD only and not suffering from diabetes or any other chronic disease. The above patient and control groups were divided into subgroups according to demographic data, comprising age and sex. Serum was collected from patients and control individuals by collecting blood samples from them and centrifuging for 10 min at 2500 rpm within 30 min.

The study adhered to all legal and ethical standards and requirements. Approval was obtained from the Ministry of Health/Nineveh Health Directorate, Mosul, Iraq (Protocol Number: 18187). Written informed consent was obtained from all participants, and the consent forms were signed on May 13, 2024.

Sclerostin concentration, highly sensitive C-reactive protein (hs-CRP), and myoglobin were measured in serum by using an enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay (ELISA) kit from Sunlong company (China); creatine kinase-MB (CK-MB), glutamate oxaloacetate transaminase (GOT), glutamate pyruvate transaminase (GPT), lactate dehydrogenase (LDH), total protein, and albumin were measured by using Bio Labs kit from Bio Labs company from France; and troponin by Combas.

By using the SPSS program (version 28) (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA), the mean and standard deviation were found and compared between the two main groups, control and patient, and a t-test was used to find the significant change in mean value; in addition, the analysis of variance (ANOVA) test was used for comparing between subgroups; and the Pearson test was used to find the correlation between sclerostin and the biochemical cardiac parameters.

RESULTS

The current study included 80 patients with stable CHD and 80 healthy individuals as a control group. The average age of the control group was 52.55 years, which closely matched the average age of the patient group at 55.75 years. The results demonstrated a highly significant decrease in sclerostin concentration in the serum of the patient group (71.256 pg/mL) compared to the control group (98.426 pg/mL), with a significance level of P < 0.001, as shown in Figure 1.

DISCUSSION

CHD is the leading cause of death in contemporary society, causing more than one-third of deaths attributed to cardiovascular disease.[28] CHD has been considered a pathological process of the dysregulated remodeling of the arterial wall, leading to the formation of plaques,[29] then leading to changes in various biomarkers and increased osteogenesis occurring in the plaques.[30313233]

The results of this study indicate a highly significant decline in serum sclerostin levels in patients with CHD compared to normal individuals. This finding aligns with Milovanova et al., who emphasized that decreasing sclerostin levels are a risk factor for cardiovascular complications in end-stage renal patients.[34] Additionally, the recent results agree with He et al., who documented decreased levels in elderly patients with CHD. Conversely, our results contradict Li et al., who reported an increase in sclerostin associated with vascular calcification.[35] The difference between our findings and previous studies may be due to the inclusion of patients with other chronic diseases alongside CHD. The significant reduction in sclerostin levels observed in the patient group suggests that sclerostin might be a contributing factor to heart disease, possibly due to its low expression in atherosclerotic plaques.[3637] This study also compared patients and healthy controls after dividing them into subgroups based on age. The results in Table 1 showed a significant decrease in sclerostin concentration with age, which contrasts with the findings from healthy subgroup analyses that showed a significant increase in sclerostin levels.

It is worth noting that this is the first study to compare protein concentration at different ages for heart patients. However, for healthy people, it has been previously documented in two studies that sclerostin increases with age, and the reason may be due to decreased osteocalcin, which is a protein that is involved in bone metabolism and has a reverse action with sclerostin, or due to a decrease in bone formation with increased age.[38] Unlike control subgroups, the decrease of sclerostin in patient subgroups may be due to the formation of plaques, which increase the osteogenesis in plaques,[39] and serum levels of bone metabolic factors, especially the osteocytic factor sclerostin, are sensitive inflammatory markers. This glycoprotein is a factor secreted largely by osteocytes in the bone and serves a negative role in osteogenesis.[40] Because cardiac development parallels the ossification process of cartilage development into bone,[41] sclerostin may play a role in the heart, either Wnt-dependent or independent. Although relatively unknown, it is nonetheless very important for both the Wnt-dependent and independent studies to investigate the underlying mechanism of sclerostin and to evaluate its potential as a therapeutic strategy in treating different heart diseases. Fluctuating sclerostin levels indicate the presence of a strong association with the status of CHD. This knowledge prompted much work on the potential role of sclerostin in the diagnosis, treatment, and prognosis evaluation of CHD.

Table 2 shows a relationship of sclerostin levels with sex; serum sclerostin levels were higher in males than in females. The reasons may be because of total-body skeletal mass; the larger skeleton in men simply may produce and release more sclerostin into the circulation.[42] Or may be due to bone mineral density or sex hormone variation.[43]

This concept is reinforced by Table 4, which supports the existence of a strong relationship between sclerostin deficiency and the cardiac biochemical tests. The raised inflammation markers (troponin and CRP) are documented as markers of MI,[44] and the diagnostic efficacy of biochemical markers CK-MB and LDH is increased in CHD.[454647] These biomarkers increase in the serum due to myocardial cell injury and necrosis.

Another indicator also supports this concept, as romosozumab, which is a sclerostin inhibitor, elevates the risk of myocardial infection in osteoporosis patients. Meanwhile, animal studies confirm that sclerostin deficiency is associated with heart disease. Additionally, injection of sclerostin reduced the development of atherosclerosis.[48] All these data may be because a decrease in sclerostin level means decreased inhibition of the Wnt pathway, which may lead to calcium accumulation, leading to the form of hardened plaques and arterial stiffness. This study highlights that sclerostin acts as an inhibitor of the Wnt/β-catenin signaling pathway, which plays an important role in promoting osteogenesis. Reduced sclerostin expression may increase Wnt/β-catenin activity in endothelial cells, potentially contributing to increased plaque instability and atherosclerosis progression. However, the interaction between sclerostin, Wnt/β-catenin signaling, and atherosclerosis development remains a matter of debate, highlighting the need for further research to elucidate these underlying mechanisms.

CONCLUSION

Sclerostin may play a crucial role in CAD and could potentially be utilized as a marker for monitoring the progression of this condition. Vascular calcification, a hallmark of advanced CAD, involves the osteogenic differentiation of vascular smooth muscle cells (VSMCs). Sclerostin inhibits the Wnt/β-catenin pathway, which promotes osteogenesis. A reduction in sclerostin levels may enhance Wnt/β-catenin signaling in endothelial cells, thereby promoting plaque instability and the progression of atherosclerosis. However, this remains a controversial topic and necessitates further research.
