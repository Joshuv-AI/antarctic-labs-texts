MSI2 in the dentate gyrus and ovary regulates the reproductive endocrine homeostasis of female mice
Authors: Haodong Liu, Penghui Li, Yang He, Mingyang Yu, Zelin Zhang, Xiaolong Li, Hefeng Zhu, Bingkun Teng, Jiaxin Fan, Junzhe Yin, Wenchao Yang, Qinqin Hao, Yujie Chen, Yongqiang Li, Junguang Ren, Guifang Cao, Shuying Liu, Haijun Li, Chenguang Du
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Musashi2 (MSI2) plays a crucial role in neural stem cells and reproductive regulation, yet its mechanism in integrating the neuro-endocrine system remains poorly understood. This study investigates how MSI2 influences ovarian function by using conditional knockout mice for Msi2 in the brain and ovary. Our results show that KRT14/MSI2 neurons in the dentate gyrus (DG) of the hippocampus directly project to the ovary. Deletion of Msi2 in the DG and ovary increases follicle number and litter size, but causes a decline in fertility and follicle reserve after 6 months of age. AAV-mediated specific knockout of Msi2 in the DG raises follicle-stimulating hormone (FSH), and luteinizing hormone (LH) levels, upregulates Hsd3b1 and Star expression in the ovary, and decreases Cyp17a1 expression, with a synergistic effect observed in DG and ovary double-knockout mice. Chemogenetic activation of the locus coeruleus (LC) → DG circuit inhibits FSH/LH secretion and reduces reproductive capacity. These findings highlight the critical role of MSI2 in regulating steroid synthesis and gonadotropin secretion through the DG→ovary axis, with its deletion leading to reproductive aging in an early reproductive enhancement followed by accelerated depletion pattern. This provides a potential strategy for targeting neuro-endocrine interactions to intervene in reproductive diseases.

Introduction

The neuroendocrine system regulates reproductive functions through the hypothalamic-pituitary-gonadal axis (HPG)1. The RNA-binding protein Musashi2 (MSI2) affects reproduction by binding and regulating gonadotropin genes in the pituitary2. Msi2 deficiency reduces the number of follicles, ovulation rate, and embryo survival rate in the ovary3, while Msi2 overexpression in the testis disrupts spermatogenesis4, highlighting MSI2 as a key regulator of mammalian fertility. However, the circuit connections and regulatory mechanisms of MSI2 in neuroendocrine-ovarian interactions remain unclear.

Pseudorabies Virus (PRV) based circuit tracing has provided a crucial methodology for delineating neural connections governing reproductive regulation. This technique enabled the inaugural mapping of neural circuitry between the ovaries and the central nervous system5. Furthermore, it revealed the hippocampal dentate gyrus (DG) as a pivotal central hub that, via its indirect neural projections to the hypothalamus6,7, participates in regulating reproductive-associated neuroendocrine pathways8. Functional studies have shown that damage to the hippocampus, including the DG, leads to delayed vaginal opening, a reduced number of healthy follicles, and decreased secretion of estradiol and progesterone9, indicating that the DG may also promote the secretion of reproductive hormones. The DG forms a functional connection with the locus coeruleus (LC) via the corticotropin-releasing factor (CRF)-norepinephrine (NE) signaling pathway10. Both CRF and NE can indirectly inhibit the HPG axis, disrupt GnRH pulses, and regulate gonadal hormone secretion11,12. Neurons expressing neuronal nitric oxide synthase (nNOS) are present in the LC13. Moreover, GnRH and nNOS neurons show anatomical colocalization14. Nitric oxide (NO) generated by nNOS regulates GnRH pulses through the cGMP signaling pathway15, and estrogen enhances nNOS activity and NO synthesis efficiency by activating NMDA receptors, forming a positive feedback loop for GnRH/LH secretion16. These findings construct a cascade process of DG-LC→gonadal hormones, laying the foundation for exploring the MSI2-mediated neuro-ovarian pathway.

MSI2 plays a role in maintaining neuronal lineages and neural progenitor cells. Recent studies have demonstrated that MSI2 maintains functional expression in mature neurons of the adult central nervous system, such as parvalbumin interneurons and basal ganglia projection neurons17; however, its physiological functions in mature neurons, particularly the mechanisms underlying its role in neuroendocrine regulation, remain to be elucidated. Notably, its homolog Msi1 affects the synthesis and release of neuroendocrine signals18. In the ovary, Msi2 is involved in follicular development and regulates the homeostasis of reproductive functions3. This indicates that the functions of MSI2 and its regulatory networks in different tissues show functional similarities, suggesting that MSI2 may form a bidirectional regulatory pathway connecting the central nervous system and the ovary. To analyze this pathway, in this study, based on the specific expression of keratin 14 (KRT14) in the DG, we used the Cre-loxP system to construct a conditional knockout mouse model of MSI2 (Krt14Cre/+::Msi2Flox/Flox), combined with (adeno-associated virus, AAV)-specific knockout, viral tracing, and chemogenetic regulation techniques, to analyze the dual regulatory roles of MSI2 in the central neural pathway and ovarian follicular development.

Results

DGKRT14/MSI2 neurons innervate the ovary through multiple synaptic connections

To analyze the central nuclei that send neural innervation to the ovary, retrograde tracing pseudorabies virus (PRV-CAG-EGFP) carrying green fluorescent protein was injected into the bilateral ovaries of Krt14Cre/+::RosaTom/+ mice (Rosa indirectly indicates KRT14 expression). The results showed significant labeling signals in brain regions such as the paraventricular nucleus (PVN), dorsomedial hypothalamus (DMH), lateral hypothalamic area (LHA), piriform cortex (Pir), DG, zona incerta (ZI), LC, and basolateral amygdala nucleus (BLA). Among them, there were KRT14/PRV/MSI2 triple-positive signals in the DG (Fig. 1A, B). To further validate the degree of colocalization between KRT14 and MSI2, we injected Cre-dependent AAV-DIO-EGFP into the DG of Krt14Cre/+ mice for in situ genetic labeling and performed MSI2 immunostaining, revealing high colocalization between the two markers (Fig. S1A). To ensure that genetic reporters accurately reflect endogenous KRT14 expression, we compared the correspondence between Krt14Cre/+::RosaTom/Tom and anti-KRT14 antibody signals in the DG, which showed high consistency (Fig. S1C, D). Furthermore, the two genetic reporter systems, AAV-DIO-EGFP and RosaTom/Tom, demonstrated highly overlapping labeling of KRT14-positive cells within the DG (Fig. S1E), and similar results were validated in ovarian granulosa cells (Fig. S1F). MSI2 is widely expressed across multiple brain regions (Fig. S2A). Importantly, MSI2 in the DG showed colocalization with the mature neuronal marker NeuN (Fig. S2B), suggesting that MSI2 is also expressed in mature neurons. These results demonstrate the presence of DG neurons that specifically express KRT14/MSI2, and these neurons may participate in the neural innervation regulation of the ovary through polysynaptic pathways.

KRT14-mediated MSI2 deletion leads to enhanced early female mouse fertility

To verify the role of MSI2 in the hypothalamus and ovary in reproductive regulation, conditional knockout mice for MSI2 in KRT14-expressing cells (Krt14Cre/+::Msi2Flox/Flox, Msi2 cKO) were constructed. Immunofluorescence, Western blot, and RT-qPCR confirmed that the expression of MSI2 protein and mRNA was significantly decreased in the ovaries and DG neurons of Msi2 cKO mice (Fig. 2A–D). Phenotypic analysis showed that the litter size of Msi2 cKO mice increased (Fig. 2E). However, it did not affect the age at first estrus, vaginal opening, or body composition (Fig. 2F, G). Further detection revealed that the diestrus of Msi2 cKO mice was shortened, while the proestrus and estrus phases were prolonged (Fig. S3 A); meanwhile, serum hormone levels changed, with decreased levels of estradiol (E2) and testosterone (T), and increased levels of progesterone (P4), follicle-stimulating hormone (FSH), and luteinizing hormone (LH) (Fig. S3B). In addition, the expression of steroidogenic factors related to hormones and reproductive ability was detected. Subsequently, transcriptome sequencing analysis was performed on the ovarian of Msi2 cKO mice. Differential analysis showed that there were 1975 differentially expressed genes in Msi2 cKO mice, among which 911 were upregulated and 787 were downregulated (Fig. S4A). Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) revealed pathways related to reproduction, development, and metabolism, including “progesterone-mediated oocyte maturation”, “oocyte meiosis”, and “PPAR signaling pathway”. Downregulated genes were enriched in the “progesterone-mediated oocyte maturation” and “oocyte meiosis” pathways (Fig. S4B). Gene Ontology (GO) enrichment analysis showed that these differentially expressed genes were involved in cell proliferation, differentiation, apoptosis, protein binding, and transcriptional regulation (Fig. S4C). Among the key steroid hormone synthesis-related genes, it was observed that the expression of Hsd3b1 and Star was significantly upregulated (Table S1). These transcriptional changes were confirmed by Western blot and immunohistochemistry results. Msi2 deletion led to upregulation of Hsd3b1 and Star expression and inhibition of Cyp17a1 in the ovary (Fig. S4D, S5A, B). In conclusion, MSI2 dynamically regulates the activity of steroidogenic enzymes and the secretion of gonadotropins.

MSI2 knockout leads to the exhaustion of late-stage follicles

To elucidate the mechanism underlying the dynamic changes in fertility caused by Msi2 knockout, the follicle response capacity was evaluated by intraperitoneal injection of pregnant mare serum gonadotropin (PMSG). The results showed that the number of follicles in Msi2 cKO mice significantly increased after PMSG stimulation (Fig. S6A, B). Further analysis of follicle composition revealed that the number of primordial follicles in the Msi2 cKO mice was lower than that in the control mice, while the numbers of primary, secondary, and antral follicles increased, and the proportion of atretic follicles decreased (Fig. S6C, D), suggesting that Msi2 deletion accelerates their recruitment into the developing follicle pool. Notably, although the number of follicles increased dramatically, there was no difference in the diameter of follicles at all levels between the two groups (Fig. S6E), indicating that Msi2 knockout mainly regulates follicle activation efficiency rather than growth rate.

To detect the long-term fertility of Msi2 cKO mice, a mating test at 12 months of age was conducted. The results showed that before 6 months of age, the number of follicles and the number of offspring in Msi2 knockout mice increased. However, the number of offspring in the Msi2 cKO mice decreased after 7 months of age, and the number of follicles decreased synchronously (Fig. S6F, G). The results indicate that although Msi2 knockout enhances follicle recruitment and improves fertility in the short term, the premature depletion of the primordial follicle pool leads to a decline in late fertility.

MSI2 Supplementation Partially Restores Estrous Cycle and Hormones in DG

To clarify the regulatory specificity of MSI2 in the DG on neuroendocrine regulation, AAV overexpression virus (AAV-MSI2-mCherry) or control virus (AAV-mCherry) was injected into the DG of Msi2 cKO and control mice respectively to perform in situ complementation of Msi2. The estrous cycle, hormone and steroid factor levels were evaluated 3 weeks after virus expression (Fig. S7A). Western blot results showed that the MSI2 protein level in the DG region of the complementation mice was restored to the level of the control mice, while the MSI2 expression level in the overexpression mice was higher than that of the control mice (Fig. S7B). Phenotypic analysis indicated that Msi2 complementation could restore the estrous interval of Msi2 cKO mice to the same level as that of control mice (Fig. S7C, D). The follicle-stimulating hormone (FSH) level in the proestrus was lower than that of the control mice, while the luteinizing hormone (LH) level in the estrus was higher than that of the control mice (Fig. S7E). The expression levels of Hsd3b1, Star and Cyp17a1 in the ovary were the same as those of control mice (Fig. S7F). Conversely, overexpression of Msi2 in the DG of control mice prolonged the estrous interval, shortened the proportion of the estrus phase, decreased the LH level in the estrus, downregulated Star expression, and upregulated Cyp17a1 expression (Fig. S7C–F). The above results confirm that Msi2 knockout enhances FSH/LH signaling and steroidogenic enzyme expression, while Msi2 overexpression disrupts the dynamic balance of the hypothalamic-pituitary-ovarian (HPO) axis and inhibits key steroidogenic genes.

DG and MSI2 synergistically regulate the reproductive ability of mice in the ovary

To clarify the tissue specificity of MSI2 in reproductive regulation, Msi2 was targeted knocked out in the DG (DG-KO), ovary (Ovary-KO), and combined sites (DG+Ovary=Co-KO) of Msi2 Flox/Flox mice through AAV-Cre (Fig. 3A). Western blot results confirmed that the MSI2 protein expression in the DG and ovary decreased respectively 3 weeks after Cre virus expression. To verify the specificity of DG knockout, the transcriptional levels of Msi2 in the neighboring brain regions Cornu ammonis area 1 (CA1) and Cornu ammonis area 3 (CA3) were analyzed. The results showed that only the mRNA in DG significantly decreased, while there were no significant changes in the CA1 and CA3 regions (Fig. 3B), indicating that AAV-Cre-mediated gene knockout is region-specific. Phenotypic analysis showed that compared with the control mice, the estrous cycle interval was shortened in the DG-KO mice, the levels of LH during proestrus and estrus increased, the expressions of Hsd3b1 and Star in the ovary were upregulated, Cyp17a1 was downregulated, and the number of offspring increased; in the Ovary-KO mice, the proestrus period was further prolonged, the levels of FSH during proestrus and LH during estrus increased significantly, the expressions of Hsd3b1/Star in the ovary increased, Cyp17a1 decreased, and the number of offspring increased. Notably, the Co-KO mice exhibited a synergistic enhancement effect: the proportion of proestrus and the increase amplitude of FSH/LH levels were both higher than those in the DG-KO mice, Hsd3b1/Star expression increased, Cyp17a1 decreased, and the number of offspring increased compared with the control mice (Fig. 3C–H). Monosynaptic inputs of DGMSI2 neurons

The upstream input network of DG MSI2 neurons was analyzed using rabies virus (RV) (Fig. 4A). AAV-DIO-TVA-mCherry and AAV-DIO-RVG helper viruses were injected into the DG of Msi2CreERT2/+ mice to specifically label MSI2-positive neurons, enabling them to express the TVA receptor and RVG glycoprotein. Three weeks later, RV-EnvA-ΔG-EGFP virus was injected. Utilizing the targeted infection mechanism mediated by TVA, retrograde monosynaptic labeling was achieved. The results showed that mCherry/EGFP double-labeled neurons were visible at the injection site DG (Fig. 4B), and the EGFP signal was specifically distributed in multiple upstream brain regions, including A29, A30, PVN, ZI, (pedunculopontine tegmental nucleus) PPTg, (medial vestibular nucleus) MVN, (periaqueductal gray) PAG, (preoptic nucleus) PnO, (substantia nigra pars reticulata) SNR, LPAG, (dorsomedial hypothalamus) DMH, (interpeduncular nucleus) IPN, and LC (Fig. 4C). Quantitative analysis revealed that LC provided the densest innervation to DG MSI2 neurons (Fig. 4D). To further verify the downstream pathways of DGMSI2 neurons, AAV-DIO-ChR2-EGFP was used for anterograde tracing (Fig. S9A). The results showed that its axonal fibers were densely projected to brain regions such as (gigantocellular reticular nucleus) GI, LHA, (edinger westphal nucleus) EW, 3 N, 12 N, and LC, which regulate cognition, feeding, and reproduction (Fig. S9B). It is worth noting that both retrograde and anterograde tracing showed a bidirectional and dense connection between LC and DG, suggesting that they may form a closed-loop regulatory network.

Activation of LCMSI2-DG neural projections disrupts ovarian development and estrous cycle

To verify the role of the LCMSI2-DG neural pathway in reproductive regulation, chemogenetic technology was used to specifically activate the projections of MSI2 neurons in the LC-DG. Meanwhile, multi-channel electrodes were implanted above the LC to detect the chemogenetic activation effect (Fig. 5A, B). Three weeks after virus injection, CNO was intraperitoneally injected into mice in the mCherry mice and the hM3Dq mice, and in vivo electrophysiological recordings were performed. After 30 days of chronic activation, it was found that the number of offspring in the hM3Dq mice was lower than that in the mCherry mice (Fig. 5E), accompanied by an extended inter-estrus interval and shortened proestrus and estrus phases (Fig. 5F). In addition, activation led to a decrease in the mRNA levels of Hsd3b1, Star, Cyp19a1 and Esr2, and an upregulation of Cyp17a1 expression in the ovaries of hM3Dq mice (Fig. 5G). Hormone detection revealed a decrease in FSH levels during proestrus and decreases in LH levels during proestrus, estrus, and metestrus (Fig. 5H, I). Histological analysis further showed a reduction in the number of follicles and corpora lutea in hM3Dq mice (Fig. 5J, K).

Based on the widespread expression of MSI2 in neuroendocrine regulation, its colocalization characteristics with nNOS were further explored. Immunofluorescence double labeling showed that MSI2 and nNOS were highly co-expressed in the LC and the laterodorsal tegmental nucleus (LDT) (Fig. S10A, B), suggesting that they may participate in reproductive regulation through functional coupling. To analyze the effect of LCMSI2-DG circuit activation on nNOS activity, c-Fos staining revealed an increase in the proportion of c-Fos/nNOS neurons in the LC area of hM3Dq mice (Fig. S10C, D), and Western blot detected an increase in the phosphorylation level of nNOS (p-nNOS) (Fig. S10E), indicating that circuit activation can specifically enhance the NO synthesis activity of LC-nNOS neurons. These results suggest that activation of the LC-DG MSI2 neuronal circuit disrupts the reproductive homeostasis of female mice, and LCnNOS neurons may play a synergistic role therein.

MSI2 knockout does not affect the phosphorylation nNOS and GnRH neurons

Since the activation of MSI2 in the LC can regulate the p-nNOS, we further examined the effect of Msi2-specific knockout on the p-nNOS. The expression of p-nNOS protein was compared between Msi2 cKO and Control mice by immunofluorescence and Western blot. The results showed that Msi2 cKO did not affect the expression level of p-nNOS protein (Fig. S11A, B). Given that previous studies have reported the colocalization of nNOS with gonadotropin-releasing hormone (GnRH) neurons, we examined the distribution of GnRH neurons. Notably, GnRH neurons are not expressed in the LC region (Fig. S12C). On this basis, we examined the ARC, a classic brain region expressing GnRH neurons. The results revealed that Msi2 knockout did not lead to changes in the number of MSI2 and GnRH neurons in this region (Fig. S12D, E). Collectively, these data indicate that the specific deletion of Msi2 does not affect the level of p-nNOS protein in the LC or the number of GnRH neurons in the ARC.

The regulatory effect of conditional knockout of DG combined with LC chemogenetic activation on reproductive ability

Since activating the LC-DG MSI2 circuit disrupts the reproductive homeostasis of mice, while knocking out Msi2 in the DG short-term enhances reproductive ability, the DG and LC neural circuits were targeted and intervened through the dual-virus system of AAV-Cre-EGFP (or AAV-EGFP) and AAV/Retro-hSyn-hM3Dq-mCherry (or AAV/Retro-hSyn-mCherry) (Fig. S12A). Fluorescence imaging results showed that EGFP in the DG colocalized with the somata of mCherry neurons from the LC, further demonstrating the existence of the LC-DG circuit. The mCherry signal was successfully targeted to the LC, confirming the specificity of virus delivery. Subsequently, the effects of activating this loop on reproductive endocrinology and fertility were examined. The results showed that activating the LC-MSI2-DG circuit (DGEGFP:LChM3D) led to estrous cycle disorders, while knocking out DG-Msi2 (DGCre:LChM3D) blocked this effect (Fig. S12B). This result indicates that MSI2 is a key mediator for LC signals to be transmitted to the DG. Activating LC input (DGEGFP:LChM3D) significantly reduced FSH/LH levels during the proestrus period and inhibited the expression of Hsd3b1/Star in the ovaries (Fig. S12C–E). However, knocking out Msi2 (DGCre:LChM3D) could block the regulation of these steroid molecules by LC activation (Fig. S12E), proving that MSI2 in the DG is a downstream factor for LC signal regulation of hormone synthesis. The number of offspring born to mice in the LC activation mice (DGEGFP:LChM3D) was significantly reduced, consistent with the decrease in hormone levels. When DG-Msi2 was knocked out and LC was activated (DGCre:LChM3D), the number of offspring returned to the control mice level (Fig. S12F), proving that Msi2 deletion can counteract the reproductive inhibitory effects of LC activation, and its function is a key hub for the LC-DG pathway to regulate reproduction.

Discussion

This study confirmed the neural connection between the DG and the ovary. Conditional knockout of DG and Msi2 in the ovary altered the estrous cycle, levels of steroidal factors, and circulating hormones in female mice, suggesting that MSI2 has regulatory functions both in the central nervous system and the ovary, maintaining reproductive homeostasis by regulating the expression of gonadotropins and steroidal factors.

PRV, with its ability to retrogradely cross multiple synapses, provides a crucial tool for parsing the complex neural innervation of the ovary5. Gerendai team injected PRV into the ovaries of rats and revealed the anatomical basis for the multilevel central regulation of the ovary19. We detected PRV labeling in the PVN, LHA, ZI, and LC, which is consistent with the previously observed multisy naptic pathways from the ovary to the hypothalamus20. As a key integrative center in the hypothalamus, reproductive regulatory signals are transmitted from the PVN to preganglionic sympathetic neurons and then sent to ovarian nerves by postganglionic neurons, forming a central-to-peripheral regulatory circuit21. In this study, PRV labeling was detected in the hippocampal DG, a non-classical brain region involved in reproductive regulation, which differs from the results observed in hypothalamic nuclei in previous studies. We speculate that this difference may be due to the virus titer. The titer of the modified PRV used in this study reached 9 × 10⁹ PFU/mL (higher than the commonly used 1×10⁹ PFU/mL), and its high infectivity accelerated the retrograde trans-synaptic process, covering more upstream synaptic connections.

Previous studies have demonstrated that systemic knockout of Msi2 leads to a decline in fertility3. However, conditional knockout of Msi2 mediated by Krt14 initially exhibited a fertility enhancement phenotype opposite to that of systemic knockout. We speculate that the expression of KRT14 is specific. Immunofluorescence results showed that it is mainly located in granulosa cells and theca cells in the ovary. This expression limitation causes Msi2 to be knocked out only in the secondary follicle stage, while the function of MSI2 in primordial follicles is preserved. This may be the key reason why the fertility of conditional knockout mice is not impaired or even enhanced. In contrast, systemic knockout of Msi2 disrupts the basic function of this gene, thereby causing problems such as abnormal activation of primordial follicles and ovulation defects. For example, systemic knockout leads to hormonal imbalance, primordial follicle pool defects, ovulation disorders, or infertility. Specific knockout of the Wntless in granulosa cells leads to reduced fertility, manifested as corpus luteum shrinkage and miscarriage. Specific knockout of Paxillin enhances fertility22. These phenotypic differences highlight the complexity of the molecular regulatory network that is specific to the stage of follicular development and cell type.

The expression of KRT14 in the brain has rarely been reported in the literature. Through lineage tracing combined with immunofluorescence, we detected colocalization of KRT14 and MSI2 in the DG. Considering that antibody labeling may underestimate actual expression levels due to insufficient epitope exposure, lineage tracing and viral labeling provided more reliable evidence. Although KRT14 is traditionally regarded as an epithelial cell marker, an increasing number of tissue-specific genes have been found to possess cross-tissue functions. Given that the specificity of the KRT14-Cre system may be controversial, we adopted complementary validation strategies: First, we utilized the neuron-specific AAV-hSyn-Cre system to achieve precise knockout in the DG region, excluding non-neuronal signal interference; Second, through chemogenetic activation of the LC → DG circuit, we functionally confirmed the necessity of DG-MSI2 neurons in reproductive regulation. Consistent results obtained from multiple independent approaches enhanced the credibility of our findings. Future studies will employ DG-specific tools such as Prox1-CreERT2 for systematic validation, combined with single-cell transcriptomic analysis to elucidate the expression characteristics and biological significance of KRT14 in the DG.

Knockout of Msi2 affects the number of follicles and fertility. When Msi2 is absent, the activation rate of primordial follicles increases. The rapid activation of the primordial follicle pool is a prerequisite for an increase in the subsequent number of follicles, but its own quantity will decrease due to overactivation23. We speculate that Msi2 knockout temporarily inhibits follicular atresia by increasing hormone and steroid levels in the early stage, or promotes the activation of primordial follicles by enhancing the PI3K/AKT signaling pathway, thereby leading to an increase in ovulation rate in the short term24. However, long-term overactivation of follicles accelerates the exhaustion of follicular reserves, affects follicular development, and ultimately leads to a decrease in the number of offspring. This phenotype has been verified in models such as Lin28a and Aspm25,26.

The absence of Msi2 relieves the inhibition of the HPG axis and increases the secretion of FSH/LH. LH upregulates the expression of Star through the cAMP/PKA pathway27, and its elevated level accelerates the synthesis of pregnenolone28; while FSH synergizes with LH through the PI3K/Akt pathway to jointly induce the expression of Hsd3b129, driving the conversion of pregnenolone to progesterone. MSI2 may bind and stabilize Esr1 mRNA to enhance estrogen signaling. The absence of Msi2 weakens the function of Esr1, thereby affecting the expression of its downstream target Cyp17a1. In addition, the reduced expression of Cyp17a1 hinders the conversion of progesterone to androstenedione, resulting in the accumulation of progesterone. At the same time, the synthesis of androstenedione and testosterone is interrupted. The reduction in androgen receptors further limits testosterone-dependent E2 generation30. Under physiological conditions, E2 and testosterone inhibit the negative regulation of LH/FSH secretion by GnRH pulses from the hypothalamus31,32. In the knockout mice, the decrease in the levels of both weakens this inhibitory effect. Although the elevated progesterone may partially inhibit GnRH pulses, the dominant effect is the weakened negative feedback of E2/testosterone, ultimately driving an increase in GnRH release and an elevation of LH/FSH, forming a positive feedback loop of gonadotropins33.

Specific knockout results have demonstrated that MSI2 in the DG regulates the expression of steroidogenic genes, and this effect is consistent with the trend observed in ovarian knockouts. Considering the functional characteristics of MSI2 as an RNA-binding protein34,35, we hypothesize that MSI2 binds to specific regions of Hsd3b1/Star mRNA, recruiting the mRNA degradation complex or inhibiting its translation. After MSI2 knockout, the inhibitory effect is relieved, leading to upregulation of their expression. More importantly, the combined knockout of Msi2 in the DG and ovary produces a synergistic effect, enhancing the ability to regulate steroids. This indicates that DG-MSI2 neurons indirectly regulate ovarian function through the HPO axis, and the effect is amplified when combined with the absence of ovarian.

Overexpression experiments have demonstrated the specificity of DG MSI2 in neuroendocrine regulation. However, the AAV used for overexpression has broad-spectrum expression characteristics, which may lead to the expression of MSI2 in non-neuronal cells such as glial cells in the DG region. This non-specific expression may indirectly regulate neuroendocrine function by affecting the neuronal microenvironment or neuron-glia interactions. Although we have taken measures such as stereotaxic injection and setting up an AAV-mCherry control group to minimize non-specific effects, their influence cannot be completely excluded. It is worth noting that MSI2 overexpression successfully restored the abnormal estrous cycle and hormone levels in cKO mice, which corresponds to the phenotypes produced by DG-specific knockout, supporting the key role of MSI2 in DG neurons. Future research will employ neuron-specific promoters (such as CaMKIIα) or Cre-dependent expression systems to achieve more precise cell-type-specific targeting, further clarifying the precise mechanism of MSI2 in DG-HPO axis regulation.

Anterograde and retrograde tracing experiments have confirmed that the LC is an important projection nucleus of the DG, suggesting that the LC plays a role in the reproductive neural circuit. It should be noted that no projections from the classic reproductive regulatory nuclei, such as the POA or ARC, to the DG were detected, indicating that the POA/ARC may not be the direct upstream nuclei of the DG. The POA/ARC may indirectly regulate the DG through endocrine/humoral pathways. Additionally, RV can only label direct projections and cannot mark potential indirect pathways36. In the future, the use of specific trans-synaptic viral tools may help clarify whether the POA/ARC indirectly regulates the DG through secondary neurons.

Chemogenetic activation of MSI2 in the LC-DG pathway reduces the levels of Hsd3b1/Star. The decrease in HSD3B1 activity inhibits the conversion of progesterone, while the reduction in Star limits the transport of cholesterol into mitochondria37, weakening the efficiency of steroid synthesis. The upregulation of Cyp17a1 may enhance the conversion of progesterone to androstenedione, compensating for androgen synthesis38. Meanwhile, estrous cycle disorders disrupt the rhythm of gonadotropins, inhibiting Cyp19a1, whose activity depends on the synergistic regulation of FSH and ESR239. The reduction in estrogen synthesis further inhibits Esr2 expression. nNOS regulates GnRH release through the soluble guanylate cyclase (sGC)-cyclic guanosine monophosphate (cGMP) pathway, and its colocalization and synaptic connections with GnRH neurons provide an anatomical basis for reproductive regulation40,41. Notably, chemogenetic activation of MSI2 neurons in the LC-DG pathway significantly increases the colocalization ratio of c-Fos/nNOS and nNOS activity, indicating that MSI2 activation can specifically drive the nNOS pathway. We speculate that the NO produced after activation diffuses to the ovary via the neuro-humoral pathway, inhibiting Cyp19a1 expression; or interfering with the sGC-cGMP pathway to inhibit GnRH pulses42,43. It is worth noting that Msi2 knockout does not affect the p-nNOS protein level in the LC, suggesting that Msi2-specific knockout may not directly regulate the phosphorylation level of nNOS in the LC. Activation of p-nNOS may mainly depend on the calmodulin/CaMKII pathway44. Moreover, since Msi2 was knocked out in the DG, it indicates that the regulation of nNOS by MSI2 in the LC has brain region and cell type specificity. The LC is enriched with noradrenergic neurons, and the activity of nNOS in these neurons may be mainly regulated by the β-adrenergic receptor or the cAMP-protein kinase A (PKA) cascade, rather than the MSI2-RNA interaction. Previous studies have shown colocalization of nNOS and GnRH neurons14, but the current results found no GnRH expression in the LC, which is consistent with the reported distribution pattern of GnRH neurons. Furthermore, Msi2 knockout did not change the number of MSI2 and GnRH neurons in the ARC, and combined with the unchanged p-nNOS in the LC, this suggests that the regulatory effect of Msi2-specific knockout on GnRH may be independent of the LC-nNOS signaling pathway.

Specific knockout and chemogenetic activation experiments have revealed the bidirectional regulatory role of MSI2 in the LC-DG pathway. MSI2 affects the translation of target genes through post-transcriptional regulation45. Activation of the LC may affect the ability of the DG to integrate estrogen46, leading to estrous cycle disorders; when LC activation and DG knockout are performed simultaneously, hormonal and genetic indicators return to baseline levels, confirming that MSI2 is a key switching node for LC signaling. This bidirectional regulation extends to reproductive phenotypes. Both LC activation and DG knockout lead to changes in the number of offspring born, and this phenotypic reversal may be related to the pulsatility of GnRH and the release of gonadotropins47.

Methods

Animals

All experiments were conducted in accordance with institutional and national guidelines for the care and use of animals. The following strains were used: Krt14Cre/+ (B6N.Cg-Tg(KRT14-cre)1Amc/J, RRID:018964, C57BL/6 N background)48 and RosaTom/+ (B6.Cg-Gt(ROSA)26Sortm14(CAG-tdTomato)Hze/J, RRID:007914, C57BL/6 J background)49, both from The Jackson Laboratory (Bar Harbor, ME, USA); Msi2CreERT2/+ (Msi2tm1.1(cre/ERT2)Reya, Cat. No. 7548734)50 from Cyagen Biosciences (Santa Clara, CA, USA), Msi2CreERT2/+ line was originally obtained on a C57BL/6 J background and was backcrossed to C57BL/6 J. This line was maintained as heterozygotes and bred to preserve the desired genetic background; Msi2Flox/Flox (C57BL/6Smoc-Msi2tm1(flox)Smoc, Cat. No. NM-CKO-241595, C57BL/6S background)51 from the Shanghai Model Organisms Center (Shanghai, China); and wild-type C57BL/6 J mice from Beijing Vital River Laboratory Animal Technology Co., Ltd. (Beijing, China). All experiments used female mice (6–8 weeks old; 17–18 g).

Breeding schemes included crossing Krt14Cre/+ with RosaTom/+ or Msi2Flox/Flox mice to generate double transgenic lines. Msi2CreERT2/+ and floxed alleles were maintained on a C57BL/6 background by crossing heterozygous carriers with wild-type mice. Cre or CreERT2 was transmitted from both maternal and paternal lineages. Genotyping was performed by PCR. Genomic DNA was extracted from tail biopsies using the TIANamp Genomic DNA Kit (Tiangen Biotech, Cat#DP304). PCR reactions were set up in a 25 μL volume containing 1× PCR buffer, 2.0 mM MgCl₂, 200 μM dNTPs (Takara, Cat#4030), 0.4 μM allele-specific primers, and 1 U Taq DNA polymerase (Takara, Cat#R001A). The amplification conditions included an initial denaturation at 95 °C for 5 min, followed by 35 cycles of 95 °C for 30 s, 58–62 °C for 30 s, and 72 °C for 45 s, with a final extension at 72 °C for 5 min. PCR products were separated on 2% agarose gels, stained with GelRed Nucleic Acid Gel Stain (Biotium, Cat#41003), and visualized under UV illumination. Primer sequences are provided in Table S2.

For CreERT2 induction, tamoxifen (Sigma-Aldrich, Cat. No. T5648) was dissolved in corn oil (10 mg/mL), and Msi2CreERT2/+ mice received intraperitoneal injections (50 μg/g) daily for 7 consecutive days, starting one week before viral injection.

Mice were housed under standard conditions (12-h light/dark cycle, lights on 06:00–22:00) with free access to food and water. All procedures were approved by the Animal Welfare and Experimental Animal Ethics Committee of Inner Mongolia Agricultural University (Approval No. NND2021535). We have complied with all relevant ethical regulations for animal use.

Stereotactic injection

Mice were anesthetized with 2% isoflurane and fixed on a stereotaxic apparatus (Neurostar). Body temperature was maintained at 37 °C using a heating pad. A 5 μL Hamilton microsyringe was used to inject the virus (0.3 μL/site) into the DG (AP: -1.5 mm; ML: ±1.5 mm; DV: -1.8 mm) or LC (AP -5.04 mm, ML ± 0.98 mm, DV -3.8 mm) at a rate of 0.1 μL/min. The injection needle was left in place for 10 min after injection to prevent backflow. After surgery, the mice were placed in a 37 °C incubator to recover and then returned to their original cages. All AAV vectors were used at working titers of approximately 1 × 10¹² vg/mL, A full list of viral vectors, their sources, and titers is provided in Supplementary Table 3.

To label KRT14 in the DG, Cre-dependent AAV-DIO-EGFP was stereotaxically injected into the DG of Krt14Cre/+ or Krt14Cre/+::RosaTom/Tom mice (300 nL per site); tissues were collected 3 weeks post-injection for MSI2 immunostaining or direct imaging.

Anterograde tracing: AAV9-DIO-ChR2-EGFP was unilaterally injected into the DG of Msi2CreERT2/+ mice. Three weeks after virus expression, neural fiber projections were detected.

Retrograde monosynaptic neuronal tracing: AAV9-EF1α-DIO-mCherry-TVA and AAV9-EF1α-DIO-RVG (1:1; total volume 200 nL) were unilaterally injected into the DG of Msi2CreERT2/+ mice. Three to four weeks later, 350 nL of RV-ENVA-EGFP (titer 1 × 1013 vg/mL) was injected at the same coordinates. Viral expression was detected 7 days after RV injection.

Chemogenetic activation of LCMSI2-DG: AAV/R-DIO-Flpo was injected into the DG of Msi2CreERT2/+ mice, and AAV-hSyn-fDIO-h3MDq-mCherry (300 nL) or AAV-hSyn-fDIO-mCherry (300 nL) was bilaterally injected into the LC. Three weeks after virus expression, CNO (Sigma-Aldrich, Cat#34233-69-7) was dissolved in sterile saline at 1 mg/mL and administered intraperitoneally at 0.5 mg/kg body weight. Fresh solution was prepared before each injection. Subsequently, mice were anesthetized with 2% isoflurane, and retro-orbital blood was collected using a heparinized glass capillary tube. Approximately 200–300 μL of blood was obtained per mouse and transferred into non-anticoagulant tubes. Serum was separated by centrifugation at 12,000 × g for 25 min and stored at –80 °C.

Overexpression of Msi2: AAV9-MSI2-mCherry or the control virus AAV9-mCherry without MSI2 was injected into the DG of Krt14Cre/+::Msi2Flox/Flox or Msi2Flox/Flox mice. Three weeks after virus expression, the estrous cycle was detected. Four weeks later, hormone level detection and analysis of steroid synthesis pathway factors were performed.

AAV-hsyn-Cre-specific knockout: AAV-hSyn-Cre-EGFP was injected into the DG (DG knockout), ovary (ovary knockout), or both DG and ovary (co-knockout) of Msi2Flox/Flox mice. EGFP-dependent on Cre was injected into the DG and ovary as a control.

AAV knockout combined with chemogenetic activation: AAV-hSyn-Cre-EGFP was injected into the DG of Msi2Flox/Flox mice, and AAV-hSyn-hM3D-mCherry (or AAV-hSyn-mCherry) was targeted to the LC. Three weeks after virus expression, LCMSI2-DG neuronal activity was activated via CNO. Details of the virus are provided in Table S3.

PRV retrograde trans-synaptic tracing

Mice were anesthetized with 2% isoflurane and placed on a heated surgical stage, small dorsolateral flank incisions were made bilaterally to expose the ovaries. Using a Hamilton syringe, 1.5 μL of PRV531-EGFP (9 × 10⁹ pfu/mL, BrainVTA) was precisely injected into the bilateral ovaries. Following the injection, the incisions were closed with sutures. Post-operative analgesia was administered with meloxicam (5 mg/kg, s.c.), and prophylactic antibiotics (penicillin, 50,000 U/kg, i.p.) were given to prevent infection. The animals were then returned to their cages for recovery. During sample collection, deeply anesthetize mice with pentobarbital sodium (100 mg/kg, i.p.). Then perfuse the mice through the heart with physiological saline, followed by 4% paraformaldehyde. After sacrificing the mice, collect tissues (brain and ovary) for fixation. Preserve them in 30% sucrose solution for cryoprotection, and then prepare frozen sections.

Body Weight and Body Composition

Randomly divide Krt14Cre/+::Msi2Flox/Flox and Msi2Flox/Flox mice into two groups. Provide them with standard feed and monitor body weight changes weekly under single-cage breeding conditions. After 16 weeks, mice were anesthetized with sodium pentobarbital (100 mg/kg, i.p.), followed by cervical dislocation for euthanasia. Without perfusion, major fat depots (gonadal, inguinal subcutaneous, perirenal, mesenteric) and large skeletal muscles (quadriceps, gastrocnemius, tibialis anterior) were rapidly dissected and weighed immediately on an analytical balance. Fat mass was calculated as the sum of the adipose depots, lean mass as the sum of the skeletal muscles, and the fat-to-lean ratio was derived accordingly.

Immunofluorescence

After the mice were anesthetized with sodium pentobarbital (100 mg/kg, i.p.), a thoracotomy was performed. A cannula was inserted into the left ventricle, and the right atrium was incised. Saline and pre-cooled 4% paraformaldehyde (pH 7.4, Sigma-Aldrich) were perfused sequentially. After fixation, brain and ovary tissues were taken and dehydrated in 30% sucrose solution until the tissues sank. Continuous coronal sections of 30 μm were prepared using a cryostat microtome (Slee, Germany).

The sections were rinsed with PBS (3 × 5 min), treated with 1% Triton X-100 (T8787, Sigma-Aldrich, pH 8.68) for membrane permeabilization at 4 °C for 60 min, and then blocked with 3% normal donkey serum (017-000-121, Jackson Laboratory) at room temperature for 30 min. Subsequently, they were incubated with the primary antibody at 4 °C for 24 h and the corresponding secondary antibody of the same species at 25 °C for 120 min in sequence. Negative controls were processed simultaneously with sections to which no primary antibody was added.

Signals were acquired using a laser confocal microscope. The number of neurons and colocalization coefficients were quantitatively analyzed by Image J. The colocalization index was calculated using the Manders colocalization coefficient reported in previous studies. Details of the antibodies are provided in Table S4.

Paraffin sections and H&E staining

After fixation of ovarian tissue in 4% paraformaldehyde for 24 h, samples were rinsed in running water for 8 h, dehydrated through a graded ethanol series (70–100%, 45 min each), and cleared in xylene for 1 h. The tissue was immersed in paraffin for 1 h and then embedded in hard paraffin to allow natural solidification. Sections of 5 μm were prepared continuously using a microtome (with an interval of 25 μm to avoid double counting of structures), and the slides were dried at 37 °C overnight. The sections were dewaxed with xylene I/II (20 minutes each), rehydrated through a gradient of ethanol to distilled water. For nuclear staining, Mayer’s hematoxylin (StatLab) was incubated at room temperature for 2 min, differentiated with 10% acetic acid for 1 min, and then treated with a bluing agent (Fisher Scientific) for 2 min. For cytoplasmic staining, Eosin Y (StatLab) was used for 1 min, followed by washing with increasing concentrations of ethanol solutions (90, 95, 100%) for 2 min each, and two rounds of transparency with xylene for 5 min each. After being sealed with neutral balsam, the slides were cured at room temperature for 12 h. The cover slips were mounted on the slides and left overnight.

Immunohistochemistry

Prepare 5 μm consecutive sections from paraffin-embedded mouse ovarian tissues. After gradient dewaxing with xylene (10 min × 3 times) and gradient rehydration with ethanol (100–70% ethanol, 3 min × 2 times each), immerse the sections in preheated pH 6.0 citrate buffer solution and heat in a 95 °C water bath for 30 min. Samples were allowed to cool naturally to room temperature and then washed with 0.01 M PBS (3 × 5 min). Endogenous peroxidase activity was subsequently blocked with 3% hydrogen peroxide in methanol for 10 minutes. Subsequently, use a blocking/antibody diluent containing 0.4% Triton X-100 and 2% normal donkey serum for primary antibody treatment (incubate at 4 °C overnight; refer to Table S4 for specific antibody information) and secondary antibody treatment: A proprietary polymer-based reaction enhancer solution (ZSGB-BIO PV-9001 kit) was applied at 37 °C for 20 min before HRP-conjugated antibody incubation. After thorough washing with PBS between each step (3 × 5 min), the sections were incubated with DAB solution at room temperature for 5 minutes, rinsed under running water, counterstained with hematoxylin for 20 s, differentiated with lithium carbonate, and blued in running water (mark the cell nuclei). Finally, the sections were dehydrated through a graded ethanol series, cleared in xylene, and mounted with neutral balsam. For the negative control, use a diluent containing 0.4% Triton X-100 instead of the primary antibody for synchronous treatment. Acquire images with a stereomicroscope.

RT-PCR

Mice were anesthetized with sodium pentobarbital (100 mg/kg, i.p.) and euthanized by cervical dislocation. The brains and ovaries were then rapidly harvested. The RNeasy Mini kit (Qiagen, Cat#74104) was used to extract total RNA from DG, CA1, CA3, and ovarian samples and determine the purity. Subsequently, the High Capacity cDNA Reverse Transcription Kit (Applied Biosystems, Cat#4368814) was used to reverse transcribe total RNA into cDNA. Real-time quantitative PCR was performed using SYBR Green Master Mix (Applied Biosystems) on a Bio-Rad CFX96 system. Specific primers were designed to prevent amplification of genomic DNA. The PCR protocol included a denaturation step (95 °C, 30 s), followed by 40 cycles of amplification and quantification procedures (95 °C, 15 s; 60 °C, 15 s; 72 °C, 15 s). The expression levels were calculated based on the 2−ΔCt method. The primer sequences are shown in Table S5.

Western blotting

Mice were anesthetized with sodium pentobarbital (100 mg/kg, i.p.), followed by cervical dislocation for euthanasia. The brains and ovaries were then rapidly collected. The ovarian tissue was rapidly frozen in liquid nitrogen and ground into powder, and then processed synchronously with the DG of brain tissue: The brain tissue was placed in pre-cooled artificial cerebrospinal fluid, and 200 μm thick sections were prepared using a vibratome (Leica VT1000). A 1.5 mm diameter brain tissue puncher (Brain Punch) was used to precisely locate the DG area around the third ventricle. The target tissue was obtained through an annular incision and a plunger ejection technique. Proteins were extracted with RIPA buffer containing 1% SDS and protease inhibitors (CoWin Biosciences, Cat#2333S), the samples were subjected to transient ultrasonic treatment (90 V, 15 s × 3 times) to increase protein extraction efficiency. The protein concentration was determined by the BCA method (Biosharp BL521). A total of 20 μg of total protein was separated by SDS-PAGE and transferred onto a PVDF membrane. The membrane was blocked with 5% non-fat milk at room temperature for 1 h, and then incubated successively with the primary antibody (incubated at 4 °C for 12—16 h) and the IRDye® 800 labeled secondary antibody (incubated at 25 °C in the dark for 1 h). The membrane was washed with TBST. An Odyssey infrared imaging system (LI-COR) was used for scanning and quantification, with β-Tubulin or GAPDH as an internal reference for normalization.

Estrous cycle monitoring

The onset of puberty was evaluated by daily assessment of vaginal opening and subsequent first estrus in experimental and control female mice. At 10 a.m., 20 μL of saline was drawn with a micropipette and dropped onto the vaginal orifice. After three repeated rinses, the mixed solution was collected and dropped in the center of a glass slide. It was air-dried away from light at 37 °C for 10 min. An optical microscope was used to observe the cell types and their densities: proestrus is dominated by nucleated epithelial cells, estrus shows a large number of non-nucleated keratinized squamous cells, metestrus presents a mixture of keratinized cells and white blood cells, and diestrus is predominantly characterized by white blood cells.

Fertility Testing

(1) Pair 8-week-old Krt14Cre/+::Msi2Flox/Flox female mice with wild-type male mice for one week and record the number of offspring. To evaluate long-term fertility, set up parallel groups for continuous pairing over 12 months and count the number of offspring monthly.

(2) Chemical genetic activation of the LCMSI2-DG neural circuit: For Msi2CreERT/+ female mice injected with the AAV-fDIO-hM3Dq-mCherry virus, intraperitoneally inject CNO (1 mg/kg) daily at 9:00 am for 30 consecutive days to specifically activate the projections of MSI2-positive neurons in the LC-DG and simultaneously record the number of offspring litters.

(3) AAV-Cre-specific knockout experiment: Stereotactically inject AAV-Cre-EGFP into the DG or ovaries of Msi2Flox/Flox female mice. After the virus is expressed for 3 weeks, pair them with male mice and compare the effects of ovarian knockout and DG knockout on the number of offspring. Set up a group injected with an empty vector virus as a control. Four groups are set for comparison: ① Control; ② DG Cre knockout (DG cKO); ③ Ovary Cre knockout (Ovary cKO); ④ Combined DG + Ovary knockout (Co cKO).

(4) AAV-Cre knockout combined with chemical genetic experiment: inject AAV-Cre-EGFP into the DG of Msi2Flox/Flox female mice and AAV1-hSyn-hM3Dq-mCherry virus into the LC. After 3 weeks of expression, synchronously inject CNO during the pairing period to explore the synergistic effect of neural circuit activation and gene knockout. Four groups are set for comparison: ① DGEGFP:LCmCherry; ② DGCre:LCmCherry; ④ DGEGFP:LChM3D; ③ DGCre:LChM3D.

Follicle Counting

After determination of the estrous cycle, Msi2 cKO and control mice were intraperitoneally injected with PMSG (7.5 IU per mouse) and euthanized 48 hours later by CO₂ inhalation followed by cervical dislocation, after which ovarian tissues were collected for serial sectioning or RNA extraction. According to the international follicle classification standards, follicles were classified into primordial follicles, primary follicles, secondary follicles, antral follicles, and atretic follicles. The number of each type of follicle was counted, and the proportion of normal follicles and atretic follicles was calculated. To avoid double counting, only secondary antral follicles with a clearly visible oocyte nucleus were selected. The maximum cross-sectional diameter of these follicles was measured using ImageJ software, and the same follicle that appeared repeatedly in serial sections was excluded. To evaluate the long-term effects of conditional knockout of Msi2 on ovarian reserve, ovaries of cKO and Control mice at 3, 6, 9, and 12 months of age were subjected to H&E staining. The bright-field images of ovarian sections were scanned using a confocal microscope, and the number of primordial follicles was manually counted.

RNA sequencing

Female Msi2 cKO and control mice were anesthetized with sodium pentobarbital (100 mg/kg, i.p.) and euthanized by cervical dislocation. The ovaries were then rapidly removed and stored at –80 °C. RNA-seq libraries were prepared using the Illumina TruSeq RNA Sample Preparation Kit (Illumina, Cat# RS-122) according to the manufacturer’s protocol. The libraries were sequenced by Biomarker Technologies Co., Ltd. (Qingdao, China) using the NovaSeq 6000 sequencing system (Illumina, Inc.). The Illumina high-throughput sequencing platform was used to sequence the cDNA libraries, generating a large amount of high-quality data. Most of the base sequence quality scores exceeded Q30. Stringent quality control measures were implemented, including removal of reads containing adapters, low-quality reads (including reads with an N ratio greater than 10%) and reads with more than 50% of bases having a quality score Q ≤ 10. After quality control, the highest-quality clean data were obtained in FASTQ format. The mapped reads and total gene length were normalized. For samples with biological replicates, DESeq2 was used to analyze the gene expression differences between sample combinations, thus identifying differentially expressed genes under two biological conditions. The threshold for differential gene detection was a fold change ≥ 2 and a false discovery rate of 0.05. Sequencing statistics are shown in Table S6.

Hormone detection

Mice were anesthetized with 2% isoflurane in oxygen, and retro-orbital blood collection was performed using a heparinized glass capillary tube inserted into the medial canthus of the eye. Approximately 200 μL of blood was obtained per mouse and immediately transferred into plain collection tubes for subsequent serum separation. After standing at room temperature for 20 min, separate the serum by centrifugation at 12,000 × g for 25 min. Aliquot the serum and store it at -80 °C. Detection of reproductive hormone levels (E2, T, P4, FSH, LH) was performed by enzyme-linked immunosorbent assays (ELISA) at Wuhan Sevier Biotechnology Co., Ltd. All procedures were performed in accordance with the manufacturer’s instructions.

Electrophysiological Recording

For electrophysiological recordings, mice were anesthetized with 2% isoflurane and placed in a stereotaxic frame. Multichannel electrodes (Neuronexus, 8 channels, 1–2.5 mm spacing) were implanted above the LC. Electrodes were fixed with dental cement and mice were allowed to recover for 7 days. In mice injected with AAV-fDIO-hM3Dq-mCherry virus and control virus, neural activity was continuously recorded for 30 min using the Cereplex Direct amplifier system (bandpass filter: 0.1 Hz-5 kHz). This included a baseline period (10 min before CNO injection) and an intervention period (20 min after injection). The sampling rate was set to 40 kHz to acquire electrophysiological data. For data analysis, the original local field potential power spectral density plots were generated using the Kaiser window Fourier transform; subsequently, a two-dimensional median filter (with a time-domain resolution of 30 s and a frequency-domain resolution of 1 Hz) was applied to smooth the spectrum to eliminate noise. A reference baseline was established based on the channel-averaged spectrum during the baseline period before injection, and a normalized spectral curve was extracted through time-frequency averaging.

Statistics and Reproducibility

All statistical analyses were performed using GraphPad Prism 8.0 software. A total of 238 female mice were used, with individual animals considered as experimental units for analysis. The sample size was not predetermined by statistical methods but was similar to the sample sizes reported in previous literature. The researchers responsible for data collection and analysis remained blinded to the experimental conditions. Animal grouping was conducted using a completely randomized design. Any experimental mice with virus injection sites or electrode tip locations outside the target area were excluded from the data analysis. All experiments were repeated at least once or on multiple groups of mice, and the observed results were consistent. Statistical analysis involved assessing data normality using the Kolmogorov-Smirnov test. Parametric tests (two-sample t-test, one-way/repeated measures ANOVA) were used for normally distributed data, while non-parametric tests were used for non-normal data. Multiple comparisons were performed using Tukey’s post hoc test after one-way ANOVA, and Šídák correction was applied for two-way ANOVA. Data are presented as mean ± standard error of the mean (Mean ± SEM). A p-value less than 0.05 was considered statistically significant. Complete sample numbers and statistical parameters are detailed in the Figureure legends corresponding to each graph.
