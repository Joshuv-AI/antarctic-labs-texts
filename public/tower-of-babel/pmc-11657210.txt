Comprehensive analysis of the prognostic and immunological role of cavins in non-small cell lung cancer
Authors: Kelin Mou, Huan Wang, Siqi Zhu, Jing Luo, Jianmei Wang, Lin Peng, Yulin Lei, Yunke Zhang, Shike Huang, Huarong Zhao, Gang Li, Li Xiang, Yuhao Luo
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
Caveolae, specialized and dynamic subdomains of the plasma membrane, have a crucial role in diverse cellular functions encompassing endocytosis, signal transduction, mechanosensation, lipid storage, and metabolism. Cavin family proteins are indispensable for caveolar formation and function. An increasing number of studies have found that cavins are involved in tumor growth, invasion, metastasis, and angiogenesis and may have dual roles in the regulation of cancer. However, the expression and prognostic value of cavins in non-small cell lung cancer (NSCLC) remain unexplored. In this study, the expression, survival data, immune infiltration, and functional enrichment of cavins in patients with NSCLC were investigated using multiple databases. Furthermore, different subtypes of cavin-binding proteins were identified through protein-protein interaction networks and k-means clustering. The results showed that the expression of Cavin-1–3 in NSCLC tissues was significantly lower than that in normal tissues, and that Cavin-2 is the major subtype of cavin that inhibits NSCLC progression. It regulates downstream signaling pathways, modulates the infiltration of immune cells and influences the prognosis of NSCLC. Related experiments also confirmed that Cavin-2 promotes the proliferation and metastasis of NSCLC cells. These findings suggest that cavins and their binding proteins may be novel biomarkers for NSCLC prognosis and immunotherapy, providing new treatment options for NSCLC.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12885-024-13280-9.

Introduction

Lung cancer is on the rise worldwide and remains the leading cause of cancer-related deaths worldwide. Non-small cell lung cancer (NSCLC) makes up 80–85% of all lung cancer cases [1], with lung adenocarcinoma (LUAD) and lung squamous cell carcinoma (LUSC) being the predominant types. Surgical resection is commonly performed for early-stage NSCLC, which has a good prognosis. Late-stage NSCLC usually requires multimodal therapy and in recent years targeted molecular therapy has become an effective treatment method for gene targeting [2]. Therefore, understanding the underlying molecular mechanisms of NSCLC may guide its treatment.

Caveolae, cavernous invaginated structures of plasma membrane subdomains with diameters of 60–80 nm, were first observed in the 1950s [3]. In addition to neurons and lymphoid cells, numerous different cell types have been reported to possess caveolae, which are abundant in adipocytes, endothelial cells, and muscle cells. In mammals, the Cavin family consists of four proteins: Cavin-1 (polymerase 1 and transcript release factor), Cavin-2 (serum deprivation response protein), Cavin-3 (SDR-related gene product that binds to c-kinase), and Cavin-4 (muscle-related skeletal and cardiac). Caveolar formation necessitates the presence of the Cavin family of proteins, as revealed by immunofluorescence experiments [4]. According to previous studies, Cavin-1 is a potential caveolar envelope protein that regulates the function and structure of caveolae. Cavin-2 can induce morphological changes in caveolae and interact with Cavin-1, leading to the formation of caveolin complexes and maintenance of the structural stability of caveolae [5]. Cavin-3 participates in the formation of caveolar vesicles, and its loss impairs intracellular vesicle transport [6]. Cavin-4 is specifically found in muscles as a part of the cavin complex [7], related to the sarcolemmal caveolae complexes.

An increasing number of studies are finding that the Cavin family of proteins is involved in tumor growth, invasion, metastasis, and angiogenesis, and may have dual roles in the regulation of cancer. Cavin-1 has tumor-specific antitumor and pro-tumor roles. In pancreatic cancer and glioblastoma, Cavin-1 can promote tumor invasiveness and drug resistance [8]. Cavin-1 can also promote drug resistance in colorectal cancer cells [9] but reduces chemotherapy resistance in prostate cancer cells [10]. Cavin-2 has tumor-suppressive properties in various types of cancer. Cavin-2 expression is suppressed in tumors of the prostate, breast, and kidney [11]. In addition, our previous studies have confirmed that Cavin-2 is a potential downstream target of TGF-β, inhibiting the proliferation and metastasis of gastric cancer cells by suppressing fatty acid metabolism and epithelial-mesenchymal transition (EMT) [12, 13]. Compared with Cavin-1 and − 2, there have been few studies on Cavin-3 and − 4 in cancer. However, their roles in tumors require further investigation.

Abnormal expression, mutation, and prognosis of cavins have been partially reported for lung cancers. However, the molecular functions, interaction networks, and potentially regulated pathways remain unknown. Through comprehensive analysis, this study investigated the expression and mutation of cavins in NSCLC (including LUAD and LUSC) and confirmed that they may be a new prognostic factor and immune infiltration predictor of NSCLC.

Materials and methods

Datasets

RNA-Seq transcriptome information and clinicopathological data of 453 patients with LUAD and 489 patients with LUSC were acquired from TCGA (https://portal.gdc.cancer.gov/).

GEPIA2 dataset

GEPIA2 (gepia2.cancer-pku.cn) was used to assess the expression of cavin mRNA in NSCLC and normal samples as well as the expression of cavin mRNA at different cancer stages. “TCGA normal + GTEx normal” module was selected to search for matched normal data. The method for differential analysis was one-way ANOVA, using the disease state (Tumor or Normal) as a variable to calculate differential expression.

Human Pathology Atlas

The Human Pathology Atlas (www.proteinatlas.org), which contains images of histological sections from normal and cancerous tissues obtained by immunohistochemistry (IHC), was utilized to assess the protein expression of cavins in NSCLC. Antibodies were labeled with 3,3’-diaminobenzidine, and the resulting brown staining indicated where the antibody bound to its corresponding antigen.

Kaplan–Meier Plotter

Cavin mRNA expression was evaluated using the online database Kaplan–Meier (KM) Plotter (www.kmplot.com), which contains gene expression data and survival information of patients with lung cancer. To analyze the overall survival (OS) of patients with lung cancer, patient samples were split into two groups by median expression (high versus low expression) and assessed using a KM survival plot with hazard ratios with 95% confidence intervals (CIs) and log-rank P values.

PPI network construction

We used the STRING online database (version 11.5; http://STRING-db.org) for retrieving interaction genes. For visualizing potential gene interactions, a protein-protein interaction (PPI) network map was constructed using Cytoscape software (version 3.9.1, https://cytoscape.org/).

STRING consensus clustering

The ConsensusClusterPlus tool in R was used to conduct consensus clustering to identify the molecular subtypes linked to cavins. Subsequently, the ideal cluster numbers between k = 2–10 were assessed, and this process was replicated 1,000 times to guarantee that the results would be stable. The pheatmap tool in R was used to create the cluster map.

The identification of genes with differential expression

Differential mRNA expression was evaluated by employing the t-test function in R software. To address any potential false-positive TCGA data, we examined adjusted P-values. The screening criteria for identifying differential mRNA expression were set as adjusted P < 0.05 and |log2 fold change| > 0.67.

Functional enrichment analysis

To compare the differential signaling pathways and biological effects between the Cavin-2_High and Cavin-2_Low subtypes, we carried out Gene Ontology (GO) and Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) analyses. The Sanger Box online tool (http://sangerbox.com/home.html) with the “clusterProfiler” package was used for evaluating GO and KEGG pathways.

Gene set enrichment analysis

During the enrichment process in the MSigDB Collection(c2.cp.kegg.v7.4. symbols.gmt), Gene Set Enrichment Analysis (GSEA) is used to identify the genes involved in evaluating pathways and molecular mechanisms associated with differential expression between the Cavin-2_High and Cavin-2_Low subtypes. The minimum gene set was fixed as five and the maximum gene sets were fixed as 5,000 with 1,000 rounds of resampling. A P value of < 0.05 and an FDR of < 0.25 were considered statistically significant.

Somatic mutation analysis

The NSCLC samples were collected from TCGA GDC Data Portal in the “maf” format, providing somatic mutation data. Subsequently, the R software’s “Maftools” package was utilized to generate waterfall plots.

Survival analysis

KM analysis was conducted to evaluate the expression of cavins and compare the OS rates of different cavin subtypes. The patients’ OS in lung cancer was analyzed by dividing the patient samples into two groups (high and low expression) according to the median expression. KM survival curves were used for evaluation, including a 95% CI and log-rank P-value.

Cell lines and culture

The human NSCLC cell lines A549 used in this study were purchased from Guangzhou Xiaofan Technology and authenticated using the short tandem repeat method. The RPMI-1640 medium (Hyclone, Logan, UT, USA) with 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS) (GIBCO BRL, Invitrogen, Grand Island, NY, USA) was used to culture all cell lines, which were kept in a humidified chamber at 37 °C with 5% CO2.

Construction of plasmids and cell transfection

In order to achieve stable overexpression, the Cavin-2 sequences were inserted into the pcDNA3.1 plasmid vector, while an empty plasmid was used as a control. The cells were then transfected with the plasmids using the Lipofectamine 2000 kit (Invitrogen, Carlsbad, CA, USA) following the instructions provided by the manufacturer. Additional File Table S1 includes a list of all the sequences.

Quantitative realtime PCR

TRIzol reagent was utilized to extract total RNA from NSCLC cells. To perform reverse transcription, 1 µg of total RNA and a High Capacity cDNA Reverse Transcription Kit (Toyobo, FSQ-101, Shanghai, China) were used, following the protocol provided by the manufacturer. For quantitative real-time PCR (qRT-PCR), an Mx-3000P Quantitative PCR System (Applied Biosystems, Foster City, CA, USA) was employed. GAPDH expression served as a control to standardize the original mRNA concentration in the cells. The 2–ΔΔCT method was applied to determine the expression of the target gene. The primer sequences can be found in Additional File Table S2.

CCK-8 assay

Cells were added to 96-well plates at a density of 2 × 103 cells per well in 100 µL of complete medium, and 10 µL of CCK-8 reagent (RiboBio, Guangzhou, China) was added for 1 h each day following 3 days of culture. The absorbance of each well at 450 nm was measured using a microplate reader.

Transwell assay

A total of 3 × 104 cells were placed in the upper layer of the Transwell membrane, while the lower chamber contained 10% FBS to stimulate cell migration. Following incubation at 37 °C with 5% CO2 for 24 h, the cells that migrated through the membrane were stained with crystal violet for 20 min and examined under a microscope.

Wound healing assay

A total of 5 × 105 cells were initially placed in six-well plates and allowed to reach confluency. To create wounds of the same length, the cells were then scratched using a 100 µL pipette tip before being cultured in basal medium. Following the removal of detached cellular fragments through a PBS wash, each wound was observed and captured under an inversion microscope at 0 and 24 h. ImageJ software was utilized to calculate the migration of the cells.

Western blot analysis

Separate proteins by 12% SDS-PAGE, transfer to nitrocellulose membrane (Bio-Rad), block in 5% BSA, incubate with special primary antibodies included anti-Cavin-2, PI3K, AKT, WNT1, WNT5A, WNT11, α-Tubulin and GAPDH, incubate with secondary anti- Mouse/Rabbit IgG. The details of these antibodies were listed in Additional File Table S3. Finally, the images with associated molecular weights indicated for each antibody were collected with Tanon-5200 Chemiluminescent Imaging System (Tanon, China).

Results

Relationship between cavin expression levels and clinical pathological parameters in NSCLC

The GEPIA2 dataset was used to compare cavin mRNA expression between lung cancer and normal lung tissues. The results showed that the average mRNA expression levels of Cavin-1, -2, and − 3 were significantly lower in LUAD and LUSC tissues compared to those in normal tissues, while the mRNA expression level of Cavin-4 was low in both NSCLC and normal tissues, with no significant difference between the two groups (Fig. 1A and B). Next, the expression of cavin proteins in lung cancer tissues was analyzed using the Human Pathology Atlas database. IHC analysis of the database showed that Cavin-1, -2, -3, and − 4 exhibited significantly low or no expression in NSCLC tissues (Fig. 1C).

Relationship between cavin expression and NSCLC prognosis

KM curves and log-rank test analysis showed that in patients with LUAD and LUSC, Cavin-2 mRNA expression was significantly positively correlated with OS (P < 0.05), while the expression levels of Cavin-1 and − 3 mRNA were significantly negatively correlated with OS in patients with LUAD (P < 0.05) but showed no significant difference in patients with LUSC (P > 0.05) (Fig. 2A). Based on the above bioinformatics analyses, it was speculated that Cavin-2 can inhibit the occurrence and development of NSCLC, ultimately leading to a good prognosis for patients. In addition, the relationship between cavin mRNA expression and the staging of patients with lung cancer was analyzed using the GEPIA2 database. There was no significant correlation (P > 0.05) between the mRNA expression of Cavin-1, -2, and − 3 and patient staging in LUAD and LUSC (Fig. 2B).

Prediction of cavin binding proteins and different cavin binding protein subtypes

Based on the results of our previous studies, it was confirmed that Cavin-2 inhibits the progression of gastric cancer through protein binding. To further understand the role of cavins in lung cancer, PPI network analysis was conducted using the STRING database to reveal the relationship between cavins and related genes. The STRING database showed that 10 proteins closely interacted with Cavin-1, -2, and − 3, including epidermal growth factor receptor protein (EGFR), E3 ubiquitin ligase TRIM72 protein, a typical marker of LUAD TTF1 protein, methyltransferase NNMT, and protein kinase A anchoring proteins AKAP12, LIPE, FHL2, S100A10, CAV1, and CAV3 (Fig. 3A). Subsequently, we examined the expression patterns of cavin-associated genes in samples derived from both normal tissues and NSCLC, obtained from the TCGA database. Cavin-1, -2, and − 3; CAV1; CAV3; and AKAP12 were overexpressed in normal lung tissues, whereas FHL2 was overexpressed in LUAD and LUSC (Fig. 3B).

Next, consensus clustering was used to identify the clusters of proteins that bind to cavins. After k-means clustering, four clusters (subtypes AD1–4) in TCGA-LUAD cohort were identified with different cavin protein expression patterns (Fig. 4A, C), whereas three clusters (subtypes SC1–3) in TCGA-LUSC cohort were identified with different protein expression patterns (Fig. 4B, D). Further survival analysis indicated that these subtypes, based on the combination of cavin binding proteins, exhibited different clinical outcomes. Overall, in LUAD, the AD1 subtype showed a significantly better prognosis, whereas the AD4 subtype showed a poorer prognosis (Fig. 4G). In LUSC, the SC1 subtype showed a significantly worse prognosis, whereas the SC2 subtype showed a better prognosis (Fig. 4H). Through heatmaps of the two lung cancer clusters, Cavin-2 was found to be highly expressed in the AD1, SC2 and SC3 subtypes, indicating a Cavin-2_High subtype, and low expression in the AD4 and SC1 subtypes, indicating a Cavin-2_Low subtype (Fig. 4E, F). This is consistent with the single-gene survival analysis shown in Fig. 2, suggesting that high Cavin-2 expression is associated with a better clinical prognosis in NSCLC. Therefore, it was speculated that Cavin-2 could serve as a prognostic biomarker for NSCLC, and different subtypes were classified based on high or low protein expression for further analysis.

Analysis of differentially expressed genes and signaling pathways of different cavin subtypes

The above bioinformatics analysis suggested that the Cavin-2_High subtype has better clinical outcomes, whereas the Cavin-2_Low subtype has a poorer prognosis. To understand the molecular mechanisms of the different subtypes, we identified differentially expressed genes (DEGs) and key signaling pathways associated with the Cavin-2_High and Cavin-2_Low subtypes. In LUAD, 857 DEGs were identified with 698 genes up-regulated and 159 genes down-regulated (Fig. 5A, C). In LUSC, 1,481 DEGs were identified with 629 genes up-regulated and 852 genes down-regulated (Fig. 5B, D). These genes were enriched in signaling pathways and intercellular interactions involved in tumor development, including the Wnt, PI3K-Akt, IL-17, and TGF-β signaling pathways; ion transport regulation; and transmembrane transport protein activity regulation (Fig. 5E, F).

Somatic mutations and tumor microenvironment landscapes cavin subtypes

It was noticed that there was a clear somatic mutation spectrum between the Cavin-2_High and Cavin-2_Low subtypes (Fig. 6A–D), with TP53 and TTN being the most common mutations. Their abnormal expression and mutations in tumors may be the exact cause of tumor occurrence and development. In LUAD, the mutation frequency of TP53 and TTN in the Cavin-2_High subtype was low, only 34.1% and 33.3%, respectively, while the mutation frequency of TP53 and TTN in the Cavin-2_Low subtype was as high as 60.5% and 55.8%, respectively. In LUSC, the mutation frequency of TP53 and TTN in the Cavin-2_High subtype was also lower than that in the Cavin-2_Low subtype.

The tumor microenvironment in both the Cavin-2_High and Cavin-2_Low subtypes was also assessed. Figure 7A and C summarize the results of the immune cell death-related classification of 452 cases of LUAD and 489 cases of LUSC from TCGA database. Overall, there were significant differences (P < 0.05) in the matrix, immune, and estimated scores between the different cavin subtypes (Fig. 7B, D). In LUAD, subtypes AD2 and AD3 exhibited a lower immune score and higher tumor purity, while subtypes AD1 and AD4 showed a higher immune score and lower tumor purity. In LUSC, the SC2 subtype demonstrated lower immune score and higher tumor purity, whereas the SC3 subtype showed a higher immune score and lower tumor purity. It is worth noting that there was no significant difference in the matrix, immune, and estimated scores between the Cavin-2_High and Cavin-2_Low subtypes of LUAD (P < 0.05). It was speculated that the Cavin-2_High and Cavin-2_Low subtypes may have different immune cells, ultimately leading to different clinical prognoses. Therefore, the immune infiltration differences in 22 immune cells between the two subtypes was further evaluated using the CIBERSORT method combined with the LM22 feature matrix. Specifically, in LUAD, the proportions of CD8 T cells, naive B cells, and resting CD4 memory T cells were significantly increased in Cavin-2_High subtype patients (Fig. 7E), whereas in LUSC, the proportions of naïve B cells, CD8 T cells, and activated CD4 memory T cells were significantly increased in Cavin-2_High subtype patients (Fig. 7F).

Given the potential biological role of Cavin-2 in the tumor immune response, the relationship between the Cavin-2 risk scoring and the tumor microenvironment was extensively studied. The results showed that in LUAD, patients with higher risk scores were significantly negatively correlated with naive B cells and resting memory CD4 T cells (P < 0.05) and significantly positively correlated with activated memory CD4 T cells (P < 0.05) (Fig. 7G). In contrast, LUSC patients with higher risk scores were significantly negatively correlated with activated memory CD4 T cells and CD8 T cells (P < 0.05), and positively correlated with resting memory CD4 T cells (P < 0.05) (Fig. 7H). This suggests that Cavin-2 activates different immune cells in NSCLC, thereby mediating immunotherapy.

Cavin-2 inhibits tumor proliferation and metastasis in vitro

Given that Cavin-2 is the main cavin subtype that inhibits tumor progression, the role of Cavin-2 in NSCLC cells was further investigated. In vitro experiments were conducted in A549 cells using a plasmid-mediated overexpression system, followed by rescue experiments with the addition of the Wnt pathway activator Wnt3a and the PI3K/AKT pathway activator SC79. The expression levels of Cavin-2 in NSCLC cells were detected by qRTPCR, and the results showed that the elevated Cavin-2 expression induced by overexpression could be significantly reversed by the addition of Wnt3a or SC79 (Fig. 8A). The CCK-8 assay showed that Cavin-2 inhibited the proliferation of NSCLC cells, while the addition of Wnt3a or SC79 increased the proliferative capacity of the cells (Fig. 8B). Transwell and wound healing assays demonstrated that Cavin-2 significantly contributes to the reduction of NSCLC cell metastasis, whereas the addition of Wnt3a or SC79 enhanced the migratory ability of the cells (Fig. 8C, D). Additionally, western blot analysis showed that overexpression of Cavin-2 suppressed the protein expression of PI3K, AKT, WNT1, WNT5A, and WNT11 (Fig. 8E).

Based on these results, we conclude that Cavin-2 is a tumor suppressor gene involved in multiple tumor development pathways, including PI3K/AKT and WNT, playing a critical role in mediating the proliferation and migration of NSCLC cells.

Discussion

Previous studies have investigated the expression of several Cavin factors and their relationship with tumor occurrence and progression in individual cancers, and some results have demonstrated the function of Cavin-1 and − 2 as tumor suppressors. For example, overexpression of Cavin-1 and − 2 reduces the invasiveness and metastasis of pancreatic and breast cancer cells [8, 48–51], but in contrast, Cavin-1 can enhance aerobic glycolysis in colorectal cancers to promote tumor cell growth [52, 53]. This suggests that cavins have a dual effect of promoting and inhibiting tumors, depending on the type and stage of the tumor. However, the mechanisms and functions of cavins in cell lung cancer remain unexplored. In this study, bioinformatics analysis was used to investigate the expression, prognostic value, and function of four cavin factors in NSCLC and the research results were validated in vitro. It is hoped that these findings will contribute to improving treatment plans for patients with NSCLC and predicting the prognosis.

In this study, the expression of Cavin-1, -2, and − 3 in NSCLC tissues was significantly lower than that in normal tissues. Survival analysis suggested that low Cavin-2 expression was associated with a poorer OS in patients with NSCLC, whereas high Cavin-1 and − 3 expression was associated with poorer OS in patients with LUAD. Our previous study confirmed that Cavin-2 interacts with ERK at the protein level. It inhibits the ERK/PPAR pathway and suppresses the key fatty acid metabolism gene, carnitine palmitoyltransferase 1 A. On the other hand, it deactivates the ERK-nf-kb pathway, inhibiting EMT in tumor cells and ultimately suppressing the proliferation and metastasis of gastric cancer [12, 13]. Therefore, it was speculated that cavins may also participate in tumor progression through PPIs in NSCLC, and Cavin-2 may be the main subtype that inhibits tumor progression. Using protein interaction networks and k-means clustering, four and three cavin-binding protein subtypes were identified in LUAD and LUSC, respectively. Based on the protein expression of Cavin-2, Cavin-2_High and Cavin-2_Low subtypes were classified. In NSCLC, the Cavin-2_High subtype was significantly associated with a better prognosis and was accompanied by decreased protein expression of EGFR, S100A10, FHL2, and NNMT, which was consistent with the single-gene survival analysis results for Cavin-2. However, the protein interaction results mentioned are based on public database analyses, and we still lack some experimental validations, such as co-immunoprecipitation or pull-down assays. Additionally, our clustering analysis focused on the biological significance of the high and low Cavin-2 expression groups, while the biological relevance of other clustering subtypes in non-small cell lung cancer still requires further investigation.

It has been reported that various cancers, such as hepatocellular carcinoma [54], pancreatic ductal adenocarcinoma [55], lung cancer [56], and breast cancer [57], display a poor prognosis when there is a significant expression of S100A10, FHL2, and NNMT. Moreover, EGFR is considered the most common driver in NSCLC [58–60]. Our analysis also suggests that Cavin-2 and its binding proteins can lower the mutation frequencies of TP53 and TTN in cell lung cancer, thereby inhibiting tumor initiation and progression. TP53 (tumor protein p53) is a key tumor suppressor gene that regulates gene expression, cell cycle, DNA repair, and other pathways to maintain normal cell growth and function. However, mutations in TP53 lead to the loss of its tumor-suppressive function, which promotes the development and growth of cancer cells [61–64].

Titin (TTN) is one of the largest proteins in the human body and plays important structural and functional roles in the cardiac and skeletal muscles. However, recent studies have suggested that abnormal TTN expression and mutations in tumors may be novel tumor driver genes. Xingyu et al. suggested that the co-occurrence of TTN and TP53 mutations may participate in the development of LUSC by regulating downstream pathways and signaling networks of other co-expressed genes [65]. In addition, GO and KEGG analyses showed that Cavin-2 and its binding proteins may regulate tumor occurrence and development signaling pathways in NSCLC, including the Wnt, PI3K-Akt, IL-17, and TGF-β signaling pathways, affecting patient prognosis.

GSEA suggested that Cavin-2 and its binding proteins may affect tumor metabolism and cell-cell interactions, such as fatty acid metabolism, amino acid metabolism, tight junctions, adherens junctions, cytokine receptor interactions, and the regulation of the actin cytoskeleton. Therefore, it was concluded that Cavin-2 can regulate the prognosis of patients with NSCLC by affecting the expression and mutation frequency of tumor-related proteins and modulating downstream signaling pathways.

Recently, it has been reported that NEAT1, induced by Cavin-1, plays a role in regulating PD-L1 and enhancing immune evasion in glioblastoma [66]. Our results also suggest that in NSCLC, Cavin-2 and its binding proteins significantly increase the proportions of resting CD4 memory T cells, activated CD4 memory T cells, and primitive B cells, leading to a lower risk of occurrence. This suggests that Cavin-2 and its binding proteins mediate immune therapy in NSCLC by activating immune cell secretion.

It is worth mentioning that in vitro experiments were also conducted to validate the analysis results. Consistent with the bioinformatics analysis, relevant experiments confirmed that Cavin-2 promotes the proliferation and metastasis of NSCLC cells. Combining the results of the above bioinformatics analysis and those of previous studies, it was concluded that Cavin-2 is the main subtype of cavins that inhibit NSCLC and can serve as a potential target for immunotherapy and as a biomarker for predicting the prognosis of NSCLC. Further validation using in vitro and in vivo experiments is required to explore the relationship between immune cell infiltration and prognosis. Additionally, we lack experimental validation of how Cavins may alter caveolae function and structure in NSCLC. Monteiro et al. demonstrated that the interaction between caveolae and invadopodia coordinates the adhesion and proteolytic remodeling of the extracellular matrix to support tumor cell dissemination. Whether changes in Cavin-2 expression in NSCLC affect caveolae function and structure still requires further investigation.
