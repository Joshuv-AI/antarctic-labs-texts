Mentors' Experiences of Nursing Students' Peer Learning in Clinical Practice: A Systematic Review of Qualitative Studies
Authors: Sanna Kulojärvi, Sara Palander, Toni Haapa, Erika Jarva, Agostinho A C Araújo, Kristina Mikkonen, Jonna Juntunen
Published: 2026

Introduction

Clinical practice is a crucial part of nursing education. The purpose of clinical practice is to develop nursing students' skills in critical thinking, decision‐making and interprofessional collaboration, ensuring they acquire the knowledge and skills required for care (Immonen et al. 2019; Arkan et al. 2018). According to Directive 2013/55/EU, clinical practice must be conducted under the guidance of qualified healthcare professionals (EU, European Union 2013), which, in practice, primarily means learning under the guidance of a mentor (Comparcini et al. 2020). Primarily, mentoring in clinical practice occurs through a mentor–student relationship (Pitkänen et al. 2018). In literature, mentors are referred to by different terms, such as clinical facilitator, clinical supervisor and clinical preceptor (Griffiths et al. 2022). In this review, these terms will be used to refer to a mentor.

Healthcare organisations worldwide are currently facing a shortage of nurses, and estimates suggest this shortage is increasing. By 2030, it is projected that the shortage of nurses will persist. At the same time, there are challenges in the availability of clinical placements (WHO, World Health Organization 2020, 2021). Considering this collaborative, peer learning‐based clinical practice model, in which more than one nursing student practices under the mentorship of a registered nurse, can be pivotal for the future workforce (Markowski et al. 2021). Additionally, clinical practice placements must offer the best possible learning opportunities for nursing students, thereby ensuring their success later in their professional careers (Jayasekara et al. 2018). Peer learning is an innovative model that offers possibilities to strengthen and enhance the professional competence development of nursing students (Nelwati et al. 2020). The core of peer learning lies in the participation of two or more students during the clinical placement (Markowski et al. 2021).

From a mentor's perspective, there has been little research conducted on peer learning in clinical practice. Additionally, previous research on mentors' experiences of peer learning in clinical practice has been conducted in individual healthcare settings, resulting in scattered evidence. Therefore, this systematic review aims to generate and synthesise knowledge about peer learning in clinical practice from the perspective of clinical practice mentors of nursing students.

Background

Nursing education is regulated in Directive 2013/55/EU of the European Parliament and of the Council. According to Directive 2013/55/EU, nursing education must last at least 3 years (180 credits), with at least half of the credits allocated to clinical practice (EU, European Union 2013). For example, in Finland, a nursing degree is obtained at a university of applied sciences. The nursing qualification is also included in the education of midwives, paramedics and public health nurses (Ministry of Education and Culture and Ministry of Social Affairs and Health 2023, 76). In this review, the term nursing student refers to nursing, midwifery, paramedic and public health nursing students.

Clinical practice occurs in various healthcare settings, which are shaped by physical spaces and multiple factors, including psychosocial and interactional factors, organisational culture and teaching and learning factors (Flott and Linden 2015). Several factors influence nursing students' clinical learning environment, including individuals in different roles (e.g., mentors, nurses, doctors, clinical personnel, patients, patient relatives and peers), the overall educational atmosphere, the impact of the educational institution, as well as students' own perceptions of clinical learning and their personality traits (Arkan et al. 2018). The engagement and collaboration between mentors and academic nursing teachers are essential in creating a positive learning environment for nursing students (Ekstedt et al. 2019).

Clinical practice in changing and complex environments is central to the development of core nursing competences (Mikkonen et al. 2019). Clinical practice aims to orient students by guiding them in applying knowledge and skills in the workplace and in key tasks (Mikkonen et al. 2019). During clinical practice, students integrate theoretical knowledge with practical work in a supportive environment (ANMAC, Australian Nursing, and Midwifery Accreditation Council 2017; Mikkonen et al. 2019). In addition, clinical practice contributes to the cultural adaptation of nursing realities, such as shift work and the demands of the work environment (ANMAC, Australian Nursing, and Midwifery Accreditation Council 2017).

Mentors are healthcare professionals who work simultaneously as registered nurses with the responsibility of patient care and mentoring of nursing students (Tuomikoski, Ruotsalainen, Mikkonen, Miettunen, et al. 2020). Mentoring nursing students' clinical practice involves teaching and mentoring, including instruction in practical skills, conducting student assessments, and providing support for learning during clinical placements (Tuomikoski, Ruotsalainen, Mikkonen, and Kääriäinen 2020). Mentoring can be conducted in either a direct or indirect manner. In direct mentoring, the mentor personally guides and observes, or works with, the person being supervised, providing guidance and advice. In indirect mentoring, the mentor works with the student in the same unit without constant observation but remains available when needed (ANMAC, Australian Nursing, and Midwifery Accreditation Council 2017). The role of mentors in clinical practice is to support the development of nursing students' competence and professional growth (Mikkonen et al. 2019; Tuomikoski, Ruotsalainen, Mikkonen, and Kääriäinen 2020). In addition to clinical competence, the assessment of competence in clinical practice focuses on nursing students' communication, ethical decision‐making, cooperation and critical thinking (Immonen et al. 2019).

Mentoring of nursing students requires versatile mentoring skills. Results of Tuomikoski, Ruotsalainen, Mikkonen, and Kääriäinen (2020) indicate that mentoring competence encompasses creating an interactive relationship with the student, developing the mentor's characteristics and cooperation with stakeholders, providing goal‐oriented mentoring, supporting students' development in the nursing profession and facilitating students' learning process. Mentoring competence can be promoted through mentoring education; however, the mentor's age also influences competencies, work experience and the frequency of mentoring (Mikkonen et al. 2021).

Professional clinical practice has traditionally been organised through a mentorship model, where each student is paired with a designated mentor to guide their learning (Pitkänen et al. 2018). In recent years, however, nursing education has increasingly incorporated collaborative learning approaches that expand beyond the traditional one‐to‐one relationship. These include models such as Developing and Learning Care Units, Collaborative Learning in Practice and Dedicated Education Units (Holst 2023; Markowski et al. 2021), as well as interprofessional clinical practice opportunities (Juntunen et al. 2025). A defining feature of these collaborative models is the emphasis on peer learning, which enables students to learn not only from mentors and clinical staff but also from one another, thereby enriching the overall learning experience (Markowski et al. 2021).

Peer learning refers to the acquisition of knowledge and skills between peers of equal or similar status through active collaboration, assistance and mutual support (Topping 2005). It is grounded in constructivist and sociocultural learning theories, which conceptualise students as active agents who co‐construct shared understanding through interaction and participation in authentic learning situations (Carlson and Stenberg 2020). From a social constructivist perspective, learning emerges through dialogue and the negotiation of differing viewpoints, leading to deeper individual comprehension (Männistö et al. 2020). Vygotsky's sociocultural theory further emphasises that learning occurs within the zone of proximal development, where students advance with the support of more capable peers. In clinical environments, peer learning operationalises this principle by enabling students to question, explain, scaffold and jointly interpret patient care situations, thereby creating socially mediated meaning and enhancing clinical competence (Vygotsky 1978; Männistö et al. 2020).

Peer learning is also theoretically supported by Bandura's social cognitive theory, particularly the concept of self‐efficacy. Learning arises through the reciprocal interaction of personal factors, behaviours, and environmental influences, all of which are activated in peer learning settings. Observational learning, modelling, verbal encouragement and shared performance experiences contribute to students' perceptions of mastery, thereby strengthening self‐efficacy (Bandura 1997; Wille et al. 2025). Higher self‐efficacy subsequently enhances motivation, clinical reasoning and resilience in demanding clinical situations.

Taken together, these theoretical foundations demonstrate that peer learning in clinical education is pedagogically and theoretically robust: It promotes collaborative knowledge construction, supports progression through scaffolded interaction, and simultaneously reinforces students' confidence in their own clinical abilities. Peer learning can be operationalised in various ways, such as pairing junior nursing students with senior students in mentoring relationships, or arranging collaboration between students at the same educational level, where peers guide and support each other (Markowski et al. 2021).

A variety of peer learning models have been used in nursing students' clinical practice, such as the 2:1 model, where two students of different educational levels participate in learning with the support of one clinical placement student mentor (Markowski et al. 2021; Stenberg and Carlson 2015). In addition, collaborative learning models exhibit characteristics of peer learning. For example, in Dedicated Education Unit learning environments, students work either alone or in pairs with the support of a single clinical placement mentor in a healthcare setting, where a unit and staff are engaged in teaching. Collaboration between academic institutions and clinical placements is strong, and academic institutions support clinical placements and provide training for unit staff to mentor students (Moscato et al. 2013). In a Collaborative Learning in Practice learning environment, 3–6 students work together in a specific clinical learning environment with the support of one clinical placement mentor, who has received additional training. Students work together, and the mentor's role is to support, encourage and engage students in using their own knowledge in patient‐centred care (Hill et al. 2020).

Previous studies have explored the benefits and challenges of peer learning in clinical practice and its impact on students' self‐reported performance (Markowski et al. 2021; Pålsson et al. 2017; Ravanipour et al. 2015). Nursing students have found that peer learning has a positive impact on their professional and personal development (Nelwati et al. 2018). Peer learning is beneficial for students, as it allows them to communicate and practice with another person in a similar learning position (Markowski et al. 2021). Peer learning has been found to have positive effects on students' self‐efficacy, accuracy, clinical skills and confidence in clinical practice (Pålsson et al. 2017; Markowski et al. 2021; Ravanipour et al. 2015). In clinical practice, peer learning has been found to contribute to the development of students' collaboration skills while reducing students' stress and anxiety and practice errors (Markowski et al. 2021; Ravanipour et al. 2015).

Related to mentoring in peer learning settings, previous studies indicate that mentors perceive mentoring as an essential part of their professional role (Spets et al. 2024) and they view themselves as engaged and responsible for providing high‐quality learning experiences for nursing students (Raustøl et al. 2024). However, mentoring two or more nursing students in clinical practice using a peer learning model requires a new mindset and approach to mentoring (Raustøl et al. 2024). From the mentors' point of view, the advantages of peer learning for nursing students include learning from one another, reflecting together, increased independence and confidence and developing communication skills and working collaboratively (Spets et al. 2024).

Previous literature reviews on peer learning have, for example, explored students' experiences of peer learning in clinical practice (Carey et al. 2018) and the advantages and disadvantages of different collaborative mentoring models (Markowski et al. 2021). However, previous literature reviews have not, from the perspective of clinical practice mentors, explored the experiences of peer learning in clinical practice, including associated learning outcomes.

The Review

Aim

The aim of this systematic review was to synthesise evidence on mentors' experiences in peer‐learning clinical practice. The research question was:

What are the experiences of the mentors in mentoring nursing students during their peer learning in clinical practice?

Methods

This systematic review was conducted according to the Joanna Briggs Institute's (JBI) guidelines for qualitative evidence synthesis (Lockwood et al. 2024) and reported according to the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta‐Analysis (PRISMA) statement (Page et al. 2021).

Design

This qualitative meta‐aggregation was conducted to investigate mentors' experiences in mentoring nursing students during peer‐learning clinical practice. The philosophical ground of meta‐aggregation is based on pragmatism and transcendental phenomenology. The meta‐aggregation approach was chosen for this systematic review because it is well‐suited for synthesising qualitative data. The objective of the meta‐aggregation approach was to synthesise all relevant qualitative evidence to achieve a comprehensive and in‐depth description of a phenomenon. (Lockwood et al. 2024).

Inclusion and Exclusion Criteria

Inclusion and exclusion criteria were established by following the PiCo criteria (Population, Phenomenon of interest, Context). In this review, the population (P) consisted of mentors of nursing, midwifery, paramedic and public health nursing students. Mentors to other healthcare students, such as physiotherapist students, were excluded. The phenomenon of interest (I) was peer learning in clinical practice education. For this systematic review, peer learning in clinical practice was defined as a model in which two or more students practice simultaneously, emphasising a student–activating approach. The peer learning model involves two or more students preparing, solving, discussing, and reflecting together before seeking help from a mentor or further discussing the topic. To identify eligible studies, the core elements of peer learning (collaboration, reflection, communication, self‐assessment and peer assessment) were considered. Context (Co) presents clinical practice as a learning environment involving peer learning. Peer learning in other settings (classroom or simulation–based teaching) was excluded. Studies with a qualitative design or mixed‐methods studies with qualitative data that could be extracted separately were included in this review. Search Outcome

In the first stage, the search terms were formulated in alignment with the research question, with support from an information specialist. In the second stage, in January 2024, the search was conducted in all included databases: Scopus, CINAHL, Ovid MEDLINE, Web of Science, ProQuest (Education Collection) and MEDIC. A supplementary search using the same search terms and databases was conducted in September 2024. In both searches, the results were limited to studies published in Finnish, Swedish and English, with no restrictions on publication date. The search terms for all databases are shown in Appendix S1. In the third stage, all identified references from each database were imported into the reference management system Covidence. After removing 190 duplicates, 542 studies were identified. Of these studies, 459 were deemed irrelevant. The full‐text screening included 83 studies, of which 66 did not meet the inclusion criteria. Finally, the remaining 17 studies were included in this review. The PRISMA flow diagram demonstrating the information flow through the screening process is described in Figure 1. In the screening process, two independent reviewers (KS or PS and HT, AA, MK or JJ) conducted title and abstract screening, followed by full‐text screening (KS and PS, HT or JJ), considering the inclusion and exclusion criteria. Any disagreements were solved by the third reviewer.

Quality Appraisal

The methodological quality assessment of the included studies was conducted using JBI's checklist for qualitative research (Lockwood et al. 2024). Two independent reviewers critically appraised all studies included in this systematic review. A third reviewer solved conflicts of appraisal between the two reviewers. The appraisal list for qualitative studies was also used to assess the quality of the qualitative component in the included mixed‐method studies. Of the studies included in this review, five studies scored 7/10, one study scored 8/10, seven studies scored 9/10, and four studies scored 10/10 (Appendix S2).

Data Extraction

Data from each included study were extracted and organised into a separate document. This extracted information comprised the authors, year, country, study objective, methodology, methods, data analysis, participants, setting and main qualitative findings (Table 2). Synthesis

Synthesis was conducted inductively by using JBI's method of meta‐aggregation. As a method, meta‐aggregation aims to synthesise findings from any qualitative research related to the phenomenon of interest. This is followed by an interpretation of the data, which remains faithful to the original meanings provided by the researchers of the included studies. In data synthesis, the findings are categorised and further developed by integrating categories to create a synthesis (Lockwood et al. 2024). Prior to synthesis, all findings from each included study were extracted, and a credibility rating was assigned to each finding. There are three levels of credibility: unequivocal (U): the findings are accompanied by a contextually rooted, detailed, rich and associated illustration, credible (C): The findings are accompanied by an illustration lacking detail, richness or clear association with it, and not supported (NS): the findings are not supported by the data or there are no data to support the finding (Lockwood et al. 2024). Findings which were supported with explicit illustrations were assigned as U, and findings which had credible illustrations, but not as explicit, were assigned as C. In turn, findings with irrelevant illustrations or illustrations that were not clearly expressed were assigned an NS rating. Only the U and C findings were included in meta‐aggregation. One crucial part of the meta‐aggregation is to assess the confidence in the synthesised findings using the ConQual approach (Lockwood et al. 2024). In ConQual, each paper is initially ranked from high to very low, and subsequently, its dependability and credibility are assessed (Lockwood et al. 2024). Results

This systematic review includes 17 studies from five countries; Australia (Gray et al. 2023; Henderson et al. 2020), Canada (Chojecki et al. 2010), Indonesia (Wuryanto et al. 2017), Sweden (Barimani et al. 2022; Dyar et al. 2020; Holst et al. 2017; Jassim et al. 2022; Josse‐Eklund et al. 2023; Kjällquist‐Petrisi and Hommel 2021; Mamhidir et al. 2014; Nilsson and Lindberg 2024; Nygren and Carlson 2017; Vuckovic et al. 2019; Vuckovic and Landgren 2021) and United Kingdom (Markowski et al. 2022; Williamson et al. 2020). A total of 186 mentors were involved in the included studies. The studies were conducted in university hospitals, tertiary care hospitals, primary healthcare settings, outpatient clinics and elderly care home settings. The peer learning models used in the studies were mostly two students/one mentor model (Barimani et al. 2022; Chojecki et al. 2010; Henderson et al. 2020; Jassim et al. 2022; Josse‐Eklund et al. 2023; Mamhidir et al. 2014; Kjällquist‐Petrisi and Hommel 2021; Nilsson and Lindberg 2024; Nygren and Carlson 2017; Vuckovic et al. 2019; Vuckovic and Landgren 2021; Wuryanto et al. 2017) but there were also three students/one mentor model (Markowski et al. 2022), 2–4 students/one mentor model (Dyar et al. 2020), Developing and Learning Care Unit (Holst et al. 2017), Collaborative Learning in Practice model (Williamson et al. 2020) and Collaborative Clusters Education Model (Gray et al. 2023). Of the 17 studies, 16 employed a qualitative design and one was a mixed‐methods study.

The data were extracted into the three synthesised findings: (1) Importance of pedagogical support for peer learning; (2) Mentors' support needs for mentoring in a peer learning model and (3) Characteristics of a favourable peer learning environment in clinical practice. These synthesised findings were generated from 11 categories (Figure 2). A total of 81 findings were categorised, of which 53 were unequivocal (U), 23 were credible (C), and 5 were not supported (NS) (Appendix S3).

Results.

Importance of Pedagogical Support for Peer Learning

The importance of pedagogical support for peer learning encompasses six categories, comprising 46 findings. Mentors noted that peer learning in clinical practice requires a shift in pedagogical thinking among both mentors and nursing students. Mentors described that, in the peer learning model, they supported interactive collaboration among nursing students and students' self‐efficacy and personal growth. Pedagogical support also includes recognising students' discomfort with the peer learning model. Student assessment in peer‐learning models is also an element of pedagogical support.

Change in Pedagogical Thinking

Peer learning in clinical practice necessitates a new mindset and a shift in pedagogical thinking (Barimani et al. 2022; Chojecki et al. 2010; Dyar et al. 2020; Holst et al. 2017; Markowski et al. 2022; Williamson et al. 2020; Wuryanto et al. 2017). A change in pedagogical thinking encompasses adapting to the peer learning model (Barimani et al. 2022), shifting the mentors' roles, and reevaluating students' roles (Dyar et al. 2020). Mentors noted that nursing students grow enormously through peer learning, once they realise the resources within themselves and their peers (Barimani et al. 2022). Mentors noted that peer learning reduces students' anxiety, as working with peers provides physical and emotional support (Chojecki et al. 2010). A change in pedagogical thinking also involves creating a balance among nursing students, allowing them to learn together. To achieve this balance, mentors used reflection both individually and in collaboration with students (Holst et al. 2017). Mentors reported that mentoring in the peer learning model can drift between the old and new mentoring models for them (Markowski et al. 2022). Mentors noted that the new learning responsibilities associated with peer learning can be frightening for nursing students at first (Williamson et al. 2020). At the same time, mentors felt that the peer learning model is easier to apply than the traditional model (Wuryanto et al. 2017). Additionally, peer learning facilitated nursing students' learning more effectively and in a shorter time compared with the traditional model (Chojecki et al. 2010).

… I keep hands on my back and a zip on my mouth as much as possible … because I want to see that they (the students) develop and do those things that I think that they should do … (Holst et al. 2017).

Supporting Interactive Collaboration

Mentors described that in their role as a mentor, they supported nursing students to communicate and collaborate in a peer learning model (Barimani et al. 2022; Chojecki et al. 2010; Dyar et al. 2020; Jassim et al. 2022; Kjällquist‐Petrisi and Hommel 2021; Mamhidir et al. 2014; Markowski et al. 2022; Vuckovic et al. 2019; Williamson et al. 2020). Mentors described how, in the peer learning model, they strengthened nursing students' communication and collaboration by bouncing questions back to the students, allowing them to discuss the issues further (Barimani et al. 2022; Dyar et al. 2020; Mamhidir et al. 2014). Mentors heard nursing students discussing issues and bouncing ideas, and through that, mentors heard students vocalise things which they would not necessarily hear (Chojecki et al. 2010). For successful peer‐to‐peer communication among nursing students, mentors have expressed that the relationship between students should be equal rather than competitive (Jassim et al. 2022). Mentors emphasised that, in the peer learning model, nursing students work as a team and that part of that involves learning from one another (Chojecki et al. 2010).

Mentors found it rewarding to see nursing students working together and solving tasks, and they noticed that some students required minimal support for collaboration, despite having never met before (Vuckovic et al. 2019). Mentors observed that when collaboration was effective between nursing students, they could pass on and share the knowledge they had received from the mentor with other students. Conversely, if there was insecurity, the students worked together to find more information (Kjällquist‐Petrisi and Hommel 2021). Mentors believed that when peers collaborated, they learned from one another and from the collective experience (Vuckovic and Landgren 2021). Mentors perceived that through common reflections, nursing students could collaboratively search for information to fill knowledge gaps (Markowski et al. 2022). Mentors also observed that, in the peer learning model, nursing students supported and cared for one another (Williamson et al. 2020).

I try to teach them that you're working as a team. And you have to learn with everyone. (Chojecki et al. 2010, 9).

Supporting Self‐Efficacy

Mentors described how, in the peer learning model, they supported and strengthened nursing students' self‐efficacy by assigning them responsibility and integrating them into the team (Dyar et al. 2020; Kjällquist‐Petrisi and Hommel 2021; Mamhidir et al. 2014; Williamson et al. 2020). In the peer learning model, mentors gave responsibility and expected students to take the lead, plan and command the whole shift. Instead of students following mentors, mentors followed students (Dyar et al. 2020). When collaboration was effective and trust between students and mentors was established, mentors felt they could step back and provide support when needed (Kjällquist‐Petrisi and Hommel 2021; Mamhidir et al. 2014). Mentors felt that it is important to give students in the team space and make them feel they are worthwhile members of the team (Dyar et al. 2020). Mentors received feedback from students that, because of collaborative learning, they felt more ready to work as a registered nurse (Williamson et al. 2020).

… they were working very well together… it felt natural to them…I took the bird's perspective… in a way … it felt easier in this precepting model… (Preceptor 6). (Kjällquist‐Petrisi and Hommel 2021).

Supporting Students' Personal Growth

Mentors noticed that in the peer learning model, nursing students became more confident and independent during their clinical practice (Barimani et al. 2022; Jassim et al. 2022; Nilsson and Lindberg 2024; Nygren and Carlson 2017; Vuckovic and Landgren 2021). Mentors supported nursing students in growing their confidence by providing them with opportunities to be close to patients while mentors were nearby (Barimani et al. 2022). Also, mentors perceived that nursing students' common reflections encourage their thinking, problem‐solving and ability to make assessments (Jassim et al. 2022). Mentors have found that peer learning helps students better manage stress than the traditional model (Jassim et al. 2022). In addition to confidence, mentors noted that nursing students developed their leadership skills and took more responsibility for younger students (Markowski et al. 2022). While students were more confident and responsible for their own learning, mentors felt that they didn't need to be present all the time (Nilsson and Lindberg 2024). Mentors observed that when students viewed each other as a support for learning and consulted with one another, they became more independent together, which in turn allowed mentors to grant more freedom to students (Nygren and Carlson 2017; Vuckovic and Landgren 2021).

I see that the students' common reflections have made them more courageous in thinking and in solving problems. They have also become faster and more independent with patient contacts, allowing them to make their own assessments and then come back to me as a preceptor and discuss. (P 6) (Jassim et al. 2022).

Recognition of Students' Discomforts Related to the Peer Learning Model

Mentors described that differences between nursing students can be challenging for peer learning (Henderson et al. 2020; Mamhidir et al. 2014; Nilsson and Lindberg 2024; Vuckovic and Landgren 2021). The differences between students may be due to personality, poor adaptation to mentoring by other students or the level of knowledge (Henderson et al. 2020; Mamhidir et al. 2014). The imbalance between students can make them feel insecure, while the student who takes on more responsibility can put pressure on the other student who is withdrawing (Nilsson and Lindberg 2024). Mentors believed they needed to step in and lead discussions with students to ensure equal learning opportunities (Vuckovic and Landgren 2021).

Using [peer learning] can be difficult, for example, when they [the students] work together. and when they have to plan and provide for the patients' care. then if one of the students is very competent and the other has not developed so much and they don't have the same level of knowledge, then it can be difficult (2). (Mamhidir et al. 2014).

Assessing Students in a Peer Learning Model

Student assessment in the peer learning model was described as an ongoing process which occurred from new perspectives (Gray et al. 2023; Nygren and Carlson 2017; Williamson et al. 2020). Mentors described that through discussions with other mentors, they were very aware of students' development (Gray et al. 2023). Mentors reported that some students felt anxious about assessments due to the collaborative learning approach, but discussions between mentors helped them assess students appropriately (Williamson et al. 2020). Additionally, mentors noted that during the assessment, they considered the student's level of education (Gray et al. 2023). Also, peer discussions provided mentors with valuable insights into students' levels of knowledge (Nygren and Carlson 2017).

…we've got to look at the level of education they're at and what they've covered. Otherwise, second years would never get fives (CF4). (Gray et al. 2023).

Characteristics of a Favourable Peer Learning Environment in Clinical Practice

Characteristics of a favourable peer‐learning environment in clinical practice are grouped into three categories, with 21 findings. Mentors noted that peer learning requires careful preparation. They also emphasised the importance of creating a physical environment that facilitates peer learning. Mentors stated that peer learning in clinical practice also requires consideration of its feasibility in various healthcare environments. Mentors need to maintain the principles of peer learning so that nursing students have their own space for learning, rather than being used for other activities, such as filling staff shortages in the unit.

Preparation for the Peer Learning Model

Preparation for peer learning requires educating staff before implementing and planning workload, as well as considering physical spaces (Barimani et al. 2022; Markowski et al. 2022; Nilsson and Lindberg 2024; Williamson et al. 2020). Mentors expressed that it is vital to inform and train staff and mentors before implementing peer learning, as this helps to split the workload (Markowski et al. 2022). It was noted that a heavy workload risks undermining the pedagogical strategy of peer learning, which is why the workload should be appropriate (Barimani et al. 2022). Some units had a separate education coordinator who supported staff once a week during peer learning, which was found to be engaging (Williamson et al. 2020). Also, it was noted that physical spaces in clinical placements are important to consider in preparation for peer learning (Nilsson and Lindberg 2024)

It must not be the case that you have responsibility for even more women in labour when you have peer learning, because the students take care of everything. You can't because there will be no guidance in that way, there will be no pedagogical strategy. (Preceptor seminar #4) (Barimani et al. 2022).

Maintaining the Principles of Peer Learning by a Mentor

Mentors described one of their key duties as maintaining the principles of peer learning (Henderson et al. 2020; Markowski et al. 2022; Williamson et al. 2020). This refers to situations in clinical practice where mentors defended nursing students' right to learn through peer learning. These situations could occur between students if one student refused to be mentored by another (Henderson et al. 2020) or when colleagues sought to assign students to other tasks despite already having allocated patients (Markowski et al. 2022). Mentors believed it was essential to maintain nursing students' supernumerary status and provide them with peace and space to practice and learn (Williamson et al. 2020). To maintain the principles of peer learning, mentors stepped in to address issues by discussing them (Henderson et al. 2020; Markowski et al. 2022; Williamson et al. 2020).

The students will help, but [colleagues] be aware that they have got their allocated patients, they can help you, but not, “let me use your student”, no… Collaborative Learning in Practice Midwife 2. (Markowski et al. 2022).

Creating a Learning Environment That Enables Peer Learning

Mentors described that it is vital to create a physical learning environment that enables peer learning (Henderson et al. 2020; Holst et al. 2017; Jassim et al. 2022; Josse‐Eklund et al. 2023; Kjällquist‐Petrisi and Hommel 2021; Mamhidir et al. 2014; Nilsson and Lindberg 2024; Wuryanto et al. 2017). Mentors noted that it is essential to consider the feasibility of peer learning across diverse and evolving healthcare settings and to find innovative ways to implement it (Jassim et al. 2022). It is also worth considering the suitability of peer learning in light of the workplace's characteristics. Busy hospital wards may not allow slower‐paced learning for younger nursing students, which raised concerns among mentors, who described it as vital to consider the health care environment as a learning environment for peer learning (Henderson et al. 2020; Nilsson and Lindberg 2024). Mentors stated that for nursing students to learn and understand the patient's overall situation, they must have only a few patients at the beginning (Holst et al. 2017). Additionally, it is essential to have a physical environment that facilitates peer learning, ensuring sufficient space for both mentors and students (Jassim et al. 2022). Mentors believe that in clinical placements, it is essential to create a warm, welcoming atmosphere where nursing students feel safe (Josse‐Eklund et al. 2023). Additionally, it was crucial to maintain the structure of peer learning and mentoring acceptance, allowing nursing students to continue learning with the same patients (Mamhidir et al. 2014; Nilsson and Lindberg 2024). Mentors described that when conditions in clinical placements were good, they had more time to mentor and reflect with nursing students (Kjällquist‐Petrisi and Hommel 2021). Mentors expressed that the peer learning model made documentation easier than the traditional model (Wuryanto et al. 2017).

The students need to feel safe. Not only as peers but also on the ward. This can be achieved through a warm reception, showing that they are welcome, involving them in the team and being open to questions. I have found that when students enter the work group and the work is completed quickly, they can focus more on what they are actually here to learn. (Main preceptor 2) (Josse‐Eklund et al. 2023).

Mentors' Support Needs for Mentoring in a Peer Learning Model

Mentors' support needs for mentoring in a peer‐learning model are categorised into two areas, yielding 13 findings. Mentors believed that mentoring within the peer learning model requires support from the work community, such as the work unit and management, as well as support from other mentors. Mentors also felt that they needed educational support for peer learning.

Support From the Work Community

Combining mentoring and patient care duties was perceived as either challenging or simple, depending on the mentor's own role definition (Dyar et al. 2020). Feelings of inadequacy as a mentor were linked to mentors' perceptions of themselves as teachers, whereas mentors who perceived themselves as team players saw mentoring and patient care as natural (Dyar et al. 2020). A heavy workload and patients who required urgent care made it difficult for mentors to step back, as peer learning takes time (Holst et al. 2017). Mentors wished for more peace in the mentoring process, so they could have more time and space to mentor, support and reflect with nursing students (Jassim et al. 2022). Mentors needed more support and understanding of mentoring in the peer learning model from management and colleagues (Jassim et al. 2022). Additionally, the work community challenges, such as short staffing and sickness absence, were perceived as reflecting mentoring, making it challenging (Markowski et al. 2022). Due to mentoring several nursing students simultaneously, mentors often felt inadequate (Nygren and Carlson 2017). Mentors emphasised the importance of maintaining a continuous dialogue among mentors, both in formal and informal meetings and through reflections (Dyar et al. 2020). Mentors felt that the mentoring they received from another mentor in challenging situations during peer learning was necessary and supportive (Nygren and Carlson 2017).

… I wish more time and space could be given to the preceptor to support the students and reflect with them. For example, I was interrupted while reflecting with the students at the end of the day. I was interrupted and told to do things, even though others were available and could perform the task. (P 4). (Jassim et al. 2022).

Educational Support of Peer Learning for Mentors

Implementing a new peer learning model made mentors feel insecure, and they required educational support for their mentoring roles (Henderson et al. 2020). The education they received before mentoring in the peer learning model was perceived as beneficial (Jassim et al. 2022).

I think it was good that I had the opportunity for education before, so that I can use the activities and understand their purpose. It would probably have been more difficult if I had not received some education and background about it before. I honestly do not know if I could have done it. (P 1). (Jassim et al. 2022).

Discussion

This meta‐aggregation provides a comprehensive overview of mentors' experiences in mentoring nursing students during peer learning clinical practice. Three synthesised findings were identified: (1) Importance of pedagogical support for peer learning, (2) Mentors' support needs for mentoring in a peer learning model and (3) Characteristics of a favourable peer learning environment in clinical practice. It is known that mentoring in clinical practice requires pedagogical competences from mentors to support nursing students' learning (Tuomikoski, Ruotsalainen, Mikkonen, and Kääriäinen 2020). Our results show that mentoring in the peer learning model requires a shift in pedagogical thinking, and the mentoring approach may differ between the old and new models. Raustøl et al. (2024) confirm that a new pedagogical approach and the mentoring of more than one student simultaneously can initially create feelings of overwhelm for mentors. At the same time, the new pedagogical approach prompts mentors to reconsider their role in peer learning, in which nursing students actively learn from one another (Raustøl et al. 2024). Although pedagogical competence can be supported through mentoring training, it is also influenced by the mentor's individual characteristics, such as motivation, patience and flexibility (Mikkonen et al. 2020; Tuomikoski, Ruotsalainen, Mikkonen, and Kääriäinen 2020).

The collaboration is key to nursing students' learning in a peer learning model (Pålsson et al. 2017), and therefore, mentors' roles as supporters can be considered essential for strengthening peer collaboration. In collaboration to develop nursing skills, nursing students practice teamwork (Pålsson et al. 2021). It is highly important for nursing students to develop their teamwork skills in clinical practice, as they will work with other healthcare professionals after graduation (Nelwati et al. 2018). Therefore, mentors need to support the development of teamwork among nursing students. However, difficulties in collaboration, varying levels of knowledge among nursing students, or competition among students can hinder peer learning (Markowski et al. 2021; Spets et al. 2024). On the other hand, competition among nursing students can be beneficial by making students aware of their learning needs through comparison with peers (Stenberg and Carlson 2015). For students with different levels of knowledge to achieve their learning goals, it is important that mentors have a variety of support strategies at their disposal (Källberg et al. 2025). Feelings of inadequacy and the tendency to compare oneself with peers may emerge during clinical practice, potentially affecting students' ability to collaborate effectively. Supporting students in navigating these challenges can strengthen peer learning and promote a more constructive learning environment. (Araújo et al. 2022).

Previous studies have demonstrated that peer learning in clinical practice enhances nursing students' perceived self‐efficacy more effectively than the traditional model (Markowski et al. 2021; Pålsson et al. 2017). According to Wang et al. (2025), self‐efficacy fuels students' motivation and self‐confidence; the higher a student's perceived self‐efficacy, the stronger their motivation to learn. In addition, self‐efficacy supports students' self‐confidence and ability to solve challenges (Wang et al. 2025). To support nursing students' self‐efficacy, it is vital that students feel part of a team. Building mutual trust between mentors and students through personalised approaches, such as getting to know students and calling them by name, helps nursing students become legitimate team members (Dyar et al. 2019). According to Markowski et al. (2021), peer learning can enhance nursing students' confidence and their ability to perform certain skills. Also, peer learning positively impacts nursing students' independence (Dyar et al. 2019). To strengthen nursing students' independence, mentors should provide space for active learning and give nursing students the lead (Raustøl et al. 2024). Previous studies indicate that peer learning in clinical practice raises nursing students' readiness to work as registered nurses (Christiansen and Bell 2010; Nelwati et al. 2018).

Peer learning can involve some disadvantages, such as competition between nursing students (Stenberg and Carlson 2015). Additionally, differences in personality and knowledge levels can lead to conflicts among nursing students (Carey et al. 2018; Spets et al. 2024). Findings from this review indicate that recognising students' discomfort with peer learning is part of mentoring students in clinical practice. It is essential to address negative competition between students, even though resolving conflicts between students may be stressful for mentors (Stenberg and Carlson 2015). In challenging situations, Raustøl et al. (2024) found that mentors valued support from the university.

Student assessment is a crucial part of mentoring (Tuomikoski, Ruotsalainen, Mikkonen, and Kääriäinen 2020). Mentors in this review described discussions with other mentors to monitor the development of nursing students in clinical practice. Findings by Tuomikoski, Ruotsalainen, Mikkonen, and Kääriäinen (2020) indicate that, from the mentors' perspective, a part of mentoring competence involves conducting student assessments in collaboration with other stakeholders, such as associate mentors and colleagues. Review findings indicate that the collaborative nature of peer learning can create a sense of insecurity regarding assessment among nursing students. It is known that nursing students can perceive clinical assessment as stressful (Immonen et al. 2019). Therefore, nursing students need to understand how their learning is assessed in clinical practice (Helminen et al. 2017). The findings of this review indicate that the interactive nature of peer learning offers an alternative approach to assessing nursing students' knowledge and competence, as students reflected and vocalised their learning with their peers. Sharing experiences reinforces nursing students' learning and helps them to develop their knowledge and skills (Carey et al. 2018). Additionally, student‐to‐student reflection supports students' higher‐level cognitive knowledge in reflection with mentors (Stenberg et al. 2020).

The successful implementation of peer learning in clinical practice requires a comprehensive range of preparation. The implementation of peer learning can sometimes be inadequate, which can be supported by standardised preparation (Hill et al. 2020). Before introducing peer learning models in clinical placements, it is essential to consider the specific characteristics of the healthcare setting and the number of students involved (Markowski et al. 2021; Spets et al. 2024). It is also vital to ensure that mentors, staff and students have a common understanding of roles in peer learning (Hill et al. 2020; Wareing et al. 2018), as well as that the staff and students are equally engaged in peer learning (Källberg et al. 2025). Thus, in a peer‐learning model, mentors are responsible for mentoring and monitoring nursing students' learning, ensuring it occurs in accordance with the principles of peer learning. Flott and Linden (2015) highlight the importance of formalised education, practical learning strategies and appropriate assessment techniques for mentors to facilitate the learning of nursing students. Additionally, physical spaces and equipment are closely linked to nursing students' acquisition of skills and knowledge in clinical practice (Flott and Linden 2015), which is why attention must be paid to the physical learning environment. Despite preparation for peer learning, the realities of working life, such as the shortage of nurses and mentors, can pose challenges to its implementation (Källberg et al. 2025).

The results of this review indicate that mentors require support from the work community to facilitate peer learning. According to the results, some mentors also reported feeling inadequate in their mentoring role, which may be partly related to their level of experience. According to Spets et al. (2024), mentors with less professional experience tended to feel less confident than mentors with working experience. In addition, the country in which a mentor works may affect their perceived mentoring competence, as differences in the resources allocated to education can affect perceived competence (Mikkonen et al. 2021). Tuomikoski, Ruotsalainen, Mikkonen, and Kääriäinen (2020) emphasise the importance of mentoring education for developing mentoring competence, as mentoring competence can significantly impact nursing students' experiences of clinical practice and their attitudes towards the profession. Contradictorily, at the same time, in most countries, mentors are not required to undergo mentoring education but rather learn their duties through on‐the‐job mentoring experience (Dobrowolska et al. 2016). In addition to mentoring education, support from unit managers enhances mentors' performance (Mårtensson et al. 2013). Additionally, mentors must be familiar with the pedagogical approach to peer learning and their role in it (Raustøl et al. 2024); therefore, mentor education is essential. In addition to support from the work community, this review shows that mentors need support from other mentors and management. Mentors value being part of a community of practice for peer learning, which enables them to share experiences and discuss mentoring‐related issues with one another and with seniors (Raustøl et al. 2024).

Strengths and Limitations of the Work

This systematic review was conducted following the JBI guidelines for qualitative synthesis, which add transparency to this review. However, some limitations must be acknowledged when interpreting the findings.

The majority of the included studies were conducted in Western, high‐income contexts, which restricts the global transferability of the results. Cultural and institutional differences, such as varied pedagogical traditions, concepts of learning, educational frameworks for mentor training, hierarchical norms in clinical practice, mentors' workload and organisational resources dedicated to mentoring, may influence how mentors experience and implement peer learning in other regions. In countries with more collectivist cultures, more hierarchical clinical environments, or less formal mentor training, mentors' expectations, responsibilities and views of peer learning may vary significantly from those described in the included studies. Therefore, the findings should be interpreted cautiously, recognising that mentors' experiences might differ across diverse global healthcare and educational systems.

To provide better scientific evidence, grey literature and data from development projects were excluded from the review, which is important to consider in assessing credibility. Additionally, the included studies featured other participants, such as nursing students and educators. Perhaps because of this, there were no illustrations from mentors for every finding, so these findings had to be excluded from the data. In addition, only studies in Finnish, Swedish and English were included in this review. To strengthen the results of this systematic review, unsupported findings were excluded from the synthesis, but they are presented in the appendix.

Recommendations for Further Research

More research on peer learning in clinical practice is needed to achieve a more comprehensive understanding of its benefits across settings, including among multilingual and culturally diverse nursing students. Additionally, nursing students' competence in peer mentoring in clinical practice is an interesting topic for future research, as well as learning outcomes among nursing students at different levels of knowledge, such as in the beginning and final years of nursing studies. Additionally, the results of interprofessional peer learning in clinical practice are noteworthy and warrant further examination.

Implications for Policy and Practice

To promote and maintain mentoring skills within the peer learning model, it is essential to provide mentoring education for mentors and resources to support mentoring nursing students in clinical practice. Mentoring and supporting nursing students' development in their clinical practice is not only a mentor's responsibility but also the whole work unit's shared task. That is why it is necessary to create favourable conditions for peer learning in clinical practice. Also, considering the need for the future nursing workforce, it is essential to increase nursing students' clinical placement capacity. This can be achieved by utilising well‐prepared, effectively implemented collaboration‐based clinical practice models that enable more nursing students to practice simultaneously.

In addition, mentor training should include structured, evidence‐based content on facilitating peer learning, supporting student autonomy, providing constructive feedback and balancing pedagogical responsibilities with clinical workload. Institutions can further strengthen mentoring capacity by allocating protected time for mentoring, offering formal preparation programmes and establishing support mechanisms such as mentor networks or reflective supervision. These organisational strategies can help ensure that mentors are well‐equipped to implement peer learning and that clinical environments provide the necessary conditions for high‐quality student learning.

Conclusion

The role of the mentor is a significant factor for nursing students, enabling them to learn according to the principles of peer learning and, in doing so, reap the full benefits and positive learning experiences. Although mentors perceive peer learning as beneficial for nursing students' learning in clinical practice, transitioning mentoring from a traditional to a peer‐learning model requires a different pedagogical approach and competence. The change from the traditional mentoring model to the peer learning model can be supported with mentoring education. Additionally, it is crucial to carefully prepare before implementing peer learning in various learning environments, including the number of students, physical spaces and the suitability of peer learning. In addition, clinical practice conducted through peer learning requires a commitment to peer‐learning pedagogy, not only from mentors but from the entire work unit.
