Analytical Performance of a Novel Nanopore Sequencing for SARS‐CoV‐2 Genomic Surveillance
Authors: Mulatijiang Maimaiti, Lingjun Kong, Qi Yu, Ziyi Wang, Yiwei Liu, Chenglin Yang, Wenhu Guo, Lijun Jin, Jie Yi
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTThe genomic analysis of SARS‐CoV‐2 has served as a crucial tool for generating invaluable data that fulfils both epidemiological and clinical necessities. Long‐read sequencing technology (e.g., ONT) has been widely used, providing a real‐time and faster response when necessitated. A novel nanopore‐based long‐read sequencing platform named QNome nanopore has been successfully used for bacterial genome sequencing and assembly; however, its performance in the SARS‐CoV‐2 genomic surveillance is still lacking. Synthetic SARS‐CoV‐2 controls and 120 nasopharyngeal swab (NPS) samples that tested positive by real‐time polymerase chain reaction were sequenced on both QNome and MGI platforms in parallel. The analytical performance of QNome was compared to the short‐read sequencing on MGI. For the synthetic SARS‐CoV‐2 controls, despite the increased error rates observed in QNome nanopore sequencing reads, accurate consensus‐level sequence determination was achieved with an average mapping quality score of approximately 60 (i.e., a mapping accuracy of 99.9999%). For the NPS samples, the average genomic coverage was 89.35% on the QNome nanopore platform compared with 90.39% for MGI. In addition, fewer consensus genomes from QNome were determined to be good by Nextclade compare with MGI (p < 0.05). A total of 9004 mutations were identified using QNome sequencing, with substitutions being the most prevalent, in contrast, 10 997 mutations were detected on MGI (p < 0.05). Furthermore, 23 large deletions (i.e., deletions≥ 10 bp) were identified by QNome while 19/23 were supported by evidence from short‐read sequencing. Phylogenetic analysis revealed that the Pango lineage of consensus genomes for SARS‐CoV‐2 sequenced by QNome concorded 83.04% with MGI. QNome nanopore sequencing, though challenged by read quality and accuracy compared to MGI, is overcoming these issues through bioinformatics and computational advances. The advantage of structure variation (SV) detection capabilities and real‐time data analysis renders it a promising alternative nanopore platform for the surveillance of the SARS‐CoV‐2.Keywords: accuracy, genome surveillance, nanopore sequencing, QNome, SARS‐CoV‐2

Introduction

COVID‐19 is an acute respiratory infectious disease caused by SARS‐CoV‐2. It was first reported in Wuhan, China, in December 2019 and then rapidly spread to worldwide, causing socio‐economical losses at an unanticipated scale [1, 2]. Since its initial emergence into human populations in late 2019, SARS‐CoV‐2 has been rapidly evolving into overlapping waves of several major variants including Alpha, Beta, Gamma, Delta, and Omicron, extending the pandemic over multiple seasons [3]. The swift progression of the epidemic has garnered global attention, prompting concerns about whether this outbreak is propelled by the emergence of novel SARS‐CoV‐2 variants [4, 5]. This underscores the significance of sustained, long‐term monitoring of SARS‐CoV‐2 at the genomic level.

Genomic surveillance of SARS‐CoV‐2 is currently performed using a combination of next‐generation sequencing (NGS) technologies and bioinformatics analysis [6]. Several NGS strategies for SARS‐CoV‐2 whole genome sequencing (WGS) have been developed [7]. To increase the sensitivity and work efficiently when the viral load is relatively low, some targeted enrichment methods, including hybrid capture‐based, amplicon‐based, and ampliseq approaches have been developed [8, 9, 10]. As for the sequencing technology, the short‐read sequencing technologies like Illumina MiSeq and others, which allows accurate detection of single nucleotide variations (SNVs) and small indels, were widely used [11, 12, 13]. However, it has been reported that these sequencing technologies are unable to provide complete viral genome analysis in a single read [14], and they are expensive as they require special infrastructure [15]. In contrast, long‐read sequencing technologies, such as nanopore sequencing, generate long reads that allow for the detection of both single nucleotide and structural variations (SVs) within a shorter time and cost effectively [15, 16, 17].

Nanopore sequencing, developed by Oxford Nanopore Technologies (ONT) has emerged as a valuable tool for SARS‐CoV‐2 genomic surveillance due to its speed, portability, and cost‐effectiveness [14, 18]. It offers a reliable alternative for rapid and efficient monitoring of COVID‐19 outbreaks, with sensitivity comparable to other methods like Ion Torrent sequencing [16]. The main advantages of nanopore sequencing include ease of library preparation, real‐time data analysis, affordable costs, and high accuracy of results [16]. Nanopore sequencing has been successfully used for WGS of SARS‐CoV‐2 in wastewater samples, allowing for variant monitoring at a population level [19].

QNome is a nanopore sequencing platform developed by Qitan Technology (QitanTech, Chengdu, China), a company that embodies its own unique technology, separate from the well‐known ONT platform. It has been used for rapid bacterial genome assembly, such as Klebsiella pneumoniae [20, 21], Citrobacter portucalensis and Citrobacter freundii [22], whereas SARS‐CoV‐2 genome sequencing has not been documented. The MGI sequencing platform, developed by MGI Tech (a subsidiary of BGI Group) and based on DNBSEQ (DNA Nanoball Sequencing) technology, is also used in worldwide and has quickly become a significant player in the genomics industry due to its innovative products and cost‐effective solutions [23, 24]. BGI Group has launched a Global Initiative on Open‐source Genomics for SARS‐CoV‐2 (GIOG‐S, https://giogs.genomics.cn/) and makes its platforms, MGI, for multiplex PCR sequencing and ultra‐deep metatranscriptomic sequencing available to global research teams within GIOG‐S. In this study, the analytical characteristics of QNome sequencing were compared with the short‐read sequencing platform MGI to assess the performance of long‐read sequencing technology and explore its use for SARS‐CoV‐2 genome surveillance.

Methods

Study Design

In this study, we conducted a comparative evaluation of the analytical performance of the QNome platform against the MGI platform using synthetic SARS‐CoV‐2 controls and clinical nasopharyngeal swabs (NPS). Both controls and samples underwent extraction, enrichment, and sequencing on the QNome and MGI platforms, with the resulting sequence data processed through workflows specifically optimized for each platform. By sequencing SARS‐CoV‐2 controls, we assessed read length, mapping quality, accuracy, and reproducibility. Accuracy was evaluated by analyzing read‐level error rates and erroneous variant consensus, while reproducibility was determined by sequencing three independent replicates on both platforms. By sequencing clinical samples, the genome coverage and data size of samples were compared to determine which platform performs better in producing high‐coverage consensus genomes. The coverage depth of 11 key genes related to SARS‐CoV‐2 transmission and immune response was analyzed to assess each platform's effectiveness in detecting critical viral genes. Mutations and SVs detection (substitutions, large insertions, and deletions) were compared for sensitivity and accuracy. Finally, phylogenetic trees were used to evaluate the accuracy of lineage assignment and variant identification.

SARS‐CoV‐2 Controls

Synthetic controls of SARS‐CoV‐2 used in this study were manufactured by GeneWell and commercially available (Catalog item CA2206). The genome of SARS‐CoV‐2 (Wuhan‐Hu‐1, GeneBank: MN908947.3) was divided into ten fragments, and each fragment was synthesized separately and inserted into a lentiviral expression plasmid. The 293 T cells transfected with the lentiviral expression plasmid were cultured to collect the pseudovirus containing the sequence of SARS‐CoV‐2, which was noninfectious and without any genomic changes. It was sequenced and confirmed to be identical to the Wuhan‐Hu‐1 reference strain.

The inactivated SARS‐CoV‐2 variant (hCoV‐19/Hangzhou/ZJU‐12/2021, GenBank: EPI_ISL_3127444) was provided by Division II of In Vitro Diagnostics for Infectious Diseases, Institute for In Vitro Diagnostics Control, National Institutes for Food and Drug Control.

Sample Collection and Processing

A total of 120 NPS samples were collected from patients admitted to Peking Union Medical College Hospital (PUMCH) from November 2022 to October 2023. The samples were detected for SARS‐CoV‐2 RNA using real‐time polymerase chain reaction (RT‐PCR), and then the remaining samples that had Ct values below 30 were stored in −80°C until further use. All samples underwent 1 freeze‐thaw cycle. The PUMCH Ethics Committee has granted approval for this study (I‐PJ22768).

RNA Extraction and PCR Enrichment

RNA from the NPS and SARS‐CoV‐2 controls was extracted using the Nucleic Acid Extraction‐Purification Kit (Sansure Biotech, Changsha, China) on the Natch 96 Nucleic Acid Extraction System (Sansure Biotech, Changsha, China), according to the manufacturer's protocol. The extracted RNA samples were reverse‐transcribed into cDNA and further amplified using the Agenmic SARS‐CoV‐2 target enrichment kit (ATR05‐05) with the ARTIC v4.1 primer set (https://github.com/artic-network/artic-ncov2019/tree/master/primer_schemes/nCoV-2019/V4.1). The pool 1 and pool 2 PCR amplification products were combined and purified.

Library Construction and WGS

For QNome sequencing, longer amplicons (~400/1000 bp) were prepared for nanopore sequencing using the PCR Barcoding Kit (QPB‐V1.1.1), according to the operating manual. A total of six samples were multiplexed on a Qcell‐384 flow cell and sequenced using a QNome‐3841 device (Qitan Tech, Chengdu, China).

For MGI sequencing, DNA libraries were prepared from the purified PCR product by using the Agenmic DNA Library Prep Kit for MGI AMD02‐05 (Agenmic Biotech, Fuzhou, China). The libraries were pooled and sequenced on a MGISEQ‐200 platform with the single‐end 75 bp sequencing option.

Bioinformatics Analysis

Analysis of Sequencing Data

For QNome sequencing, the raw data underwent rigorous quality control utilizing the QPreasy software (v1.15.3, Qitan Tech, Chengdu, China), adhering strictly to the default parameters. This process ensured that only reads with a Q‐score exceeding 7 were retained. Subsequently, the Porechop software (v0.2.4) was employed to meticulously clean the raw reads by eliminating adapter sequences, resulting in high‐quality reads, all based on the default parameters. These high‐quality reads were then precisely aligned to the Wuhan‐Hu‐1 reference genome (MN908947.3) using minimap2 (v2.28) with the specific parameter ‐ax map‐ont. The iVar software (v1.4.2) was utilized to methodically trim primer sequences from the termini of the read alignments. The consensus sequences were subsequently constructed using the Medaka medaka_consensus tool, with the parameter ‐mr941_min_hac_variant_g507, ensuring accuracy and reliability. The variant candidates were identified with the aid of the medaka variant tool.

For MGI sequencing, the resulting reads was filtering using fastp (v0.23.4), with the default settings. The bwa software (v0.7.18, bwa‐mem) was employed to accurately align the filtered reads to the Wuhan‐Hu‐1 reference genome (MN908947.3), adhering to the default parameters [25]. Primer sequences were trimmed from the termini of the read alignments using iVar (v1.4.2, with specific parameter ‐e) [26] ensuring precision. The consensus genome was constructed using the iVar software (v1.4.2), with a threshold setting of ‐t 0.5, to maintain the highest standards of quality. Mutations, including SNVs and indels, were detected using bcftools (v1.20) [27] mpileup tool with the parameters ‐‐no‐BAQ ‐‐min‐BQ 5 ‐‐annotate FORMAT/AD followed by call tool with the ‐c ‐‐variants‐only ‐‐ploidy 1 options.

Only mutations that met the criteria of a quality score (QUAL) of at least 20 and a depth of at least 30 were retained for both platforms, ensuring the integrity and reliability of the results.

The consensus genome sequences produced by both platforms were clade assigned and quality assessed using Nextclade (v3.3.1).

SV Analysis

QNome nanopore sequencing reads were aligned to the Wuhan‐Hu‐1 reference genome (MN908947.3) using the long‐read mapper NGMLR (v0.2.7) with default parameters (‐t 8 ‐x ont). SVs were detected using the third‐generation sequencing SV caller Sniffles (v1.0.11), with a minimum variant length of 10 bp and at least 20 supporting reads (‐l 10 ‐s 20). For MGI short reads, we employed the integrated prediction method Delly (v1.2.6) with default parameters to detect SVs. These SV candidates were then manually inspected to verify the evidence and breakpoint position resolution using accustom script that integrates both long and short reads result.

Phylogenetic Analysis

The consensus genomes produced by both platforms were aligned using MAFFT (v7.525) with default parameters. A Maximum‐likelihood (ML) tree was constructed using IQTREE (v2.3.3) with 1000 replicates for ultrafast bootstrap and the best‐fitting nucleotide substitution model TIM + F + G4. The tree was visualized using FigTree (v1.4.4).

Statistical Analysis

Due to the non‐parametric nature of the data, a two‐sided Mann–Whitney U test was used to compare the number of consensus genomes produced by QNome and MGI, classified within each category of the online Nextclade sequence analysis tool. The two‐sided Mann–Whitney U test was also used to compare the difference in genomic coverage, number, and type of mutations detected between QNome and MGI.

Results

Analytical Sequencing Performance for SARS‐CoV‐2 Controls

To assess the accuracy of QNome sequencing, we firstly used two types of SARS‐CoV‐2 controls: synthetic controls of Wuhan‐Hu‐1 strain and inactivated virus of the Delta variant. These controls were sequenced on QNome and MGI sequencing platforms in three independent replicates. Despite differences in read lengths, two sequencing platforms exhibited high consensus‐level accuracy in sequencing the SARS‐CoV‐2 variant genomes, with no erroneous variant calls observed (Table 1), and had an average mapping quality score of approximately 60, which equals to mapping accuracy of 99.9999% (Supporting Information S1: Figure S1A,B), indicates there is a very high confidence that the reads were accurately mapped to the correct locations in the reference genome. However, the read‐level error rates for substitution and insertion‐deletion (indel) mutations in both global genome and low complex sites (LCS) were significantly higher in QNome compared to MGI (Supporting Information S1: Figure S1C,D, Table 1).

Analytical Coverage Quality of Consensus Genomes in Clinical Samples

120 NPS samples were collected at PUMCH to evaluate the sequencing performance of the QNome platform in clinical samples. These samples were analyzed using both the QNome and MGI sequencing platforms, and the clinical characteristics of the patients were documented in Supporting Information S2: Table S1. The consensus genome assembly, generated using Medaka for QNome and iVar for MGI, had a minimum sequencing depth of 30X. The results, as presented in Table 2, indicated that the QNome platform produced 115 consensus genomes in 16 h, with a data size of 6543.44 MB and coverage of 89.35%, while the MGI platform generated 120 consensus genomes in 10 h, with a data size of 43625.95MB and coverage of 90.39%. However, the consensus genomes that passed the QC used for GISAID submissions (> 80% genome coverage) from the QNome were similar with MGI (87.83% vs. 87.50%, Table 2). It is worth noting that five consensus genomes were unable to be produced due to sample quality issues on QNome platform.

To conduct a more thorough examination of the quality of consensus genomes obtained from the QNome platform, an analysis was performed on the quality assessment and coverage distribution of these genomes. A total of 115 consensus genomes generated by QNome and 120 by MGI were assessed using Nextclade to evaluate genome quality. Nextclade categorizes genomes as either good, mediocre, or bad based on criteria such as missing data, mixed sites, private mutations, clustered mutations, frameshifts, and misplaced stop codons. The study revealed a significant difference in the total number of consensus genomes and those classified as good generated by the QNome and MGI platforms (p < 0.05, Figure 1A). However, there was no significant difference in the number of genomes classified as mediocre and bad (Figure 1A, p > 0.05). The genome coverage of MGI ranged from 86.33% to 99.95%, while the genome coverage of QNome ranged from 30.24% to 99.99%. A significant difference in genome coverage between the two platforms was observed (Figure 1B, p < 0.05).

The impact of various genes of the SARS‐CoV‐2 virus on infective transmission, antibody resistance, drug resistance, and T cell epitope prompted an examination of the coverage of eleven genes (ORF1ab, S, ORF3a, E, M, ORF6, ORF7a, ORF7b, ORF8, N, and ORF10) on two sequencing platforms. Analysis revealed that the sequencing depths of all genes of 115 consensus genomes on the QNome platform were significantly lower (p < 0.05), and the average sequencing coverages of four genes (ORF1ab, S, N, and ORF10) were less than those of the other genes compared to the MGI platform (p < 0.05), as illustrated in Figure 1C,D.

Analytical Mutation and SV of Genome

Mutations and SVs can significantly influence the virus function and evolution. In our study, we assessed the frequency of mutations and SVs in 120 NPS. The results revealed a total of 9004 mutations identified using QNome sequencing, with substitutions being the most prevalent, in contrast, 10997 mutations were detected on MGI. (Figure 2A, Supporting Information S2: Table S2, p < 0.05). However, there was no significant difference in the number of insertions and deletions (Figure 2B). As for the SVs, in the analysis of 115 consensus genomes on the QNome platform, we identified 23 candidate deletions ranging from 15 to 1036 base pairs in size, with no other types of SVs detected (Table 3). Of these deletions, 19 out of 23 were supported by split short‐read alignments in matched MGI libraries, and one variant, a 26‐bp deletion in the genome, was detected in 18 specimens (Supporting Information S1: Figure S2A). One out of the 23 candidates had the deletion confirmed by short‐read evidence, but the precise breakpoint position was not accurately determined by QNome reads (Supporting Information S1: Figure S2B). However, the identification of four deletions in genes ORF1ab, ORF7a, and N by the QNome platform was not supported by the MGI libraries. (Supporting Information S1: Figure S2C).

Phylogenetic Analysis

The consensus genomes produced by the QNome and MGI platforms were subjected to analysis using Nextclade, resulting in a comparison of 112 samples. Within this sample set, 19 were categorized into distinct lineages, with five of them falling within the same clade as identified by both platforms. Additionally, the genome coverage of 5 out of the 19 samples was below 70%, while 11 samples exhibited coverage exceeding 95% on both platforms. Moreover, three samples showed discrepancies between the two platforms (Table 4). Overall, the Pango lineage of consensus genomes of SARS‐CoV‐2 on QNome concorded 83.04% with MGI. A phylogenetic tree was constructed using IQTREE to visually represent the relationships between samples belonging to different clades, as illustrated in Figure 3. Notably, two samples highlighted in blue were found to be grouped together on the same branch.

Discussion

WGS and phylogenetic analyses of viral genomes are essential for comprehending the evolution and outbreak dynamics of the virus, ultimately aiming to mitigate the spread of the disease [28]. As the pandemic emerged, there was a necessity to expedite the provision of genomic data for epidemiological surveillance, leading to the introduction of new sequencing platforms alongside Illumina, recognized for its high accuracy [29]. Except for Illumina, MGI has also gained widespread global adoption due to its comparable performance and more competitive pricing [23], and has also played a crucial role in SARS‐CoV‐2 genomic surveillance [30, 31]. In a clinical environment, the necessity for expeditious SARS‐CoV‐2 genome sequencing protocols that are readily implementable and automated, as well as adaptable and expandable, is of paramount importance. The ONT nanopore sequencing platform is widely utilized globally; however, we present data on a novel nanopore sequencing platform developed in China for WGS of SARS‐CoV‐2 in healthcare settings.

To ensure the reliability and accuracy of QNome sequencing, SARS‐CoV‐2 controls were used in this study. Previous studies assessing the accuracy of NGS have utilized synthetic RNA controls for SARS‐CoV‐2, which enabled the evaluation of approximately 95% of the SARS‐CoV‐2 genome sequence due to the inability to amplify the entire genome [14, 32]. In our research, we extended this approach by incorporating not only synthetic RNA for SARS‐CoV‐2 but also an inactivated delta variant of the virus. This addition was made to enhance the thoroughness of our accuracy assessment, ensuring that our methodology could capture and analyze a broader representation of the virus's genetic material. Similar to ONT [14], the QNome platform exhibited higher read‐level error profiles compared to MGI in both the global genome and LCS. This discrepancy in error profiles could be attributed to several factors, including homopolymer regions, systematic and random errors, GC content bias, basecalling challenges, and technological limitations in the nanopore instruments and chemistry [33, 34]. However, after aligning and combining multiple reads, the QNome platform was more proficient at producing a final, corrected sequence that closely resembled the true genetic makeup of the virus stain.

Nanopore sequencing of SARS‐CoV‐2 has the potential to produce high‐quality genome sequences, with sample quality playing a critical role in achieving optimal results. Several studies have performed comparative analyses of SARS‐CoV‐2 RNA sequencing between fresh and frozen samples, demonstrating a reduction in genome coverage in the latter. Specifically, the median genome coverage in frozen samples decreased from 94% to 88% when compared to fresh samples [19, 35]. However, some studies have reported no negative effects on sequencing quality associated with frozen samples [36]. In our retrospective study, the average coverage and passing GISAID QC of QNome were equivalent to MGI with the frozen samples. Nevertheless, the average genome coverage and the number of consensus genomes classified as good were inferior to those produced by the MGI platform. This discrepancy is particularly notable given that five samples could not be sequenced due to poor quality, indicating that the NPS samples are more vulnerable to degradation when processed on the QNome platform compared to MGI. Furthermore, the QNome platform exhibited significantly reduced sequencing depth for both the S gene and the ORF1ab gene in the LCS, resulting in a statistically significant decrease in sequencing coverage relative to the MGI platform. This difference could have a significant impact on the virus's transmissibility, infectivity, and ability to evade the immune system, highlighting the importance of further surveillance and research in this area [37]. As enveloped viruses like SARS‐CoV‐2 are known to be sensitive to freeze‐thaw cycles [38, 39], further emphasizing the need for careful handling and storage of samples.

The precise identification of indels holds significant importance in the comprehension of the virus’ evolutionary processes [40]. In this study, the MGI platform detected a significantly higher number of mutations compared to the QNome platform for the same samples. The insertions and deletions observed on the QNome platform were no more than those on the MGI platform, a trend similar to ONT sequencing [41, 42], attributable to the intrinsic technological features and the inherent nature of the sequencing procedures [33]. However, in an examination of SV mutations, while the QNome platform identified 23 deletions, only five deletions were discerned in specific regions, including ORF1ab, ORF7a, ORF8, and N genes, falling short of the numbers reported by Rowena A [14]. The reason for this can be attributed to the fact that the accessory genes of the Omicron variants analyzed in this research might have fewer SVs compared to other variants [43]. Nevertheless, 19 out of the 23 deletions detected in SV mutations were corroborated by split short‐read alignments in matched MGI libraries, indicating the reliability of QNome sequencing in detecting large deletions.

Accurate identification of lineages is crucial for tracing transmission chains and developing diagnostic methods and treatments [44]. A minimum threshold of 70% genome coverage is advised for confidently assigning a lineage [45]. In our study, the genome coverages of 5 out of 19 samples with different lineages were below 70%, rendering the assignment of reliable lineages unfeasible. The variations in Pango lineage and clade classifications were influenced by the number and nature of mutations [46], with QNome exhibiting lower number of mutations but higher number of read‐level error rates compared to the MGI platform. Therefore, further analysis is required for lineage samples with genome coverages exceeding 90% identified by QNome to ascertain the validity of mutations versus potential sequencing errors. We also conducted a comparative analysis of the time and cost associated with the two platforms, as detailed in Table 2. Regarding cost, QNome is priced at $60 per sample, making it significantly more economical than the MGI platform. However, the MGI platform demonstrates superior speed, particularly for high‐throughput sequencing tasks. Despite this, QNome sequencing has the runtime of sequencing with 16 h, which was not longer than the ONT [42]. Notably, QNome provides real‐time signal acquisition, enabling results to be obtained within a few hours for high‐quality libraries. In contrast, the MGI platform requires a signal acquisition time of 10 h, with further analysis only feasible post‐run.

In conclusion, while QNome nanopore sequencing still faces challenges of read quality and accuracy in comparison to MGI, the continuous advancements in bioinformatics and computational algorithms are gradually addressing these limitations. The ability to detect SVs along with the shortened runtime makes QNome sequencing a promising and an alternative nanopore platform for SARS‐CoV‐2 genome surveillance.
