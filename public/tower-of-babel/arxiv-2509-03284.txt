Two-loop vacuum polarization in a Coulomb field
S. A. Volkov, V. A. Yerokhin, Z. Harman, C. H. Keitel

Abstract
The leading-order two-loop vacuum-polarization potential, linear in the Coulomb field of a nucleus, was first derived in the seminal 1955 work of Källén-Sabry. The higher-order two-loop vacuum-polarization corrections, however, have remained unknown until now. In this work, we compute Coulomb corrections to the Källén-Sabry potential, specifically those involving three, five, and seven Coulomb interactions inside the vacuum-polarization loop. The potentials are evaluated in momentum space and subsequently used to calculate one-electron energy shifts. Our results drastically reduce the theoretical uncertainty of the two-loop vacuum-polarization contribution to transition energies, which is required for next-generation tests of bound-state QED in heavy one and few-electron ions as well as for the determination of nuclear charge radii.



I
Introduction

Theoretical and experimental investigations of the Lamb
shift in atomic systems provide stringent tests of bound-state quantum electrodynamics (QED)
theory, including the regime where the electron-nucleus coupling
parameter
Z\alpha
is not small
(where
Z
is the nuclear charge number and
\alpha
is the fine-structure constant)

Indelicato 2019
.
QED effects of first order in
\alpha
have been extensively calculated and verified
by numerous experiments
Mohr et al. 1998
. In contrast,
calculations of two-loop QED effects, of order
\alpha^{2}
, are not
fully completed yet.
In hydrogen-like atoms, the two-loop effects typically determine the theoretical
uncertainty of energy levels and thus represent a central challenge in advancing our
understanding of these systems
Yerokhin et al. 2019
.
Similarly, in few-electron highly charged ions, two-loop QED corrections contribute significantly
to the theoretical uncertainties, thereby limiting comparisons with available experimental data

Beiersdorfer et al. 1993
;
Beiersdorfer et al. 1995
and possible determinations of nuclear charge radii

Yerokhin
et al. 2025a
.

The two-loop QED effects have been extensively investigated during the last decades, both within
methods based on the
Z\alpha
expansion

Czarnecki et al. 2005
;
Jentschura et al. 2005
;
Dowling et al. 2010
;
Czarnecki and Szafron 2016
and also within the
all-order (in
Z\alpha
) approach
Yerokhin et al. 2003
;
Yerokhin et al. 2006
.
In particular, the two-loop self-energy correction has been evaluated nonperturbatively
in
Z\alpha

Yerokhin et al. 2024
;
Yerokhin
et al. 2025b
as well as most of two-loop diagrams with
vacuum-polarization loops
Yerokhin et al. 2008
.
The largest uncertainty of the two-loop QED contribution for hydrogen-like atoms
comes
Yerokhin et al. 2019
from two uncalculated effects:
(i) Coulomb corrections to the two-loop vacuum polarization
and (ii) the electron self-energy with a light-by-light-scattering
insertion into the photon line.
The goal of the present investigation is to compute the first of the two missing
effects, which is an important step towards completing
the long-standing project of calculation of the full set
of one-electron two-loop QED diagrams.

The vacuum-polarization (VP) potential can be conveniently represented

Blomqvist 1972
in the form of
a double expansion in
\alpha
and
Z\alpha
,

\displaystyle V_{\rm VP}(\mathbf{r})=\sum_{i,j}V_{ij}(\mathbf{r})\equiv\sum_{{i=1,2,\ldots}\atop{j=1,3,\ldots}}\alpha^{i}\,(Z\alpha)^{j}\,\widetilde{V}_{ij}(\mathbf{r})\,.

(1)

According to the Furry theorem, the expansion terms with even powers of
Z\alpha
vanish, so the
summation over
j
includes only odd
j
’s.

At the one-loop (
i=1
) level, the VP potential has been extensively studied in the literature.
The first term of the expansion,
V_{11}(\mathbf{r})
, is the well-known Uehling potential
Uehling 1935
.
The next-order term,
V_{13}(\mathbf{r})
,
was derived by Wichmann and Kroll
Wichmann and Kroll 1956
,
with its explicit coordinate-space form obtained in Ref.
Blomqvist 1972
.
The Coulomb corrections of order
(Z\alpha)^{3}
and higher to the one-loop VP are commonly referred to as
the Wichmann-Kroll potential,
which has been calculated to all orders in
Z\alpha
numerically

Soff and Mohr 1988
;
Manakov et al. 1989
;
Persson et al. 1993
. For the point nuclear model,
accurate approximate formulas for the Wichmann-Kroll potential are available in the
literature

Fainshtein et al. 1991
;
Manakov and Nekipelov 2012
.

The two-loop (
i=2
) VP potential has been studied to a significantly lesser extent.
Only the leading
Z\alpha
-expansion term,
V_{21}
–
commonly referred to as the Källén-Sabry potential –
is presently known
Källen and Sabry 1955
.
Explicit formulas for this potential can be found in
Refs.
Blomqvist 1972
;
Fullerton and G.A. Rinker 1976
.
Some partial results for higher-order VP energy shifts were reported in Ref.
Plunien et al. 1998
.
The main achievement of the present investigation is the computed Coulomb corrections to the Källén-Sabry potential
of order
(Z\alpha)^{3}
,
(Z\alpha)^{5}
, and
(Z\alpha)^{7}
,
namely, potentials
{V}_{23}
,
{V}_{25}
, and
{V}_{27}
.

The potentials
V_{ij}
induce corrections to energy levels given by the
their expectation values with the Dirac wave function of the reference state. Starting with the
second order in
\alpha
, there are also additional (so-called, reducible) VP contributions
arising from iterations of VP potentials of lower orders in
\alpha
.

It is important that the
Z\alpha
-expansion of the VP potential in Eq. (
1
)
remains useful not only when the parameter
Z\alpha
is small, but even as it approaches unity, as is the case for heavy ions.
For example, in the case of the
1s
state of uranium (
Z=92
),
V_{11}
contributes
-94
eV,
V_{13}
contributes
4.6
eV,
and
V_{15}
contributes
0.57
eV, with the net effect of the remaining one-loop VP tail amounting to merely
0.17
eV.
This is in contrast to the
Z\alpha
expansion of
corrections to energies
, which converge poorly in the high-
Z
regime.
For example, for the
1s
state of uranium, the leading
Z\alpha
-expansion term gives
-64
eV, significantly deviating from the full result
of
-89
eV.
With this in mind, we will adopt the
Z\alpha
expansion of the potential in Eq. (
1
) in our present work
for calculations of the two-loop VP potential.
This approach should remain applicable for most atoms of practical interest, with the exception of superheavy elements.

An additional advantage of using the expansion of Eq. (
1
) is that the potentials
\widetilde{V}_{ij}

do not depend on
Z
and, indeed, on any other parameters.
As a result, the potentials
\widetilde{V}_{23}
,
\widetilde{V}_{25}
, and
\widetilde{V}_{27}
, once computed and stored on a grid, can be employed for
evaluating energy shifts in arbitrary atoms, including muonic, antiprotonic, and other exotic atoms.

The paper is organized as follows. In Sec.
II
we describe the method of calculation
of the VP potentials in the momentum space.
Sec.
III
describes calculations of energy shifts.
Sec.
IV
presents the numerical results obtained for the VP potential and the corresponding

The relativistic units (
\hbar=c=m=1

We use bold face (
\mathbf{p}
) for three-vectors and italic style (

Four-vectors have the form

The Coulomb potential is

II
CALCULATION OF POTENTIALS

We calculate here the potentials
{V}_{23}
,
{V}_{25}
, and
{V}_{27}
.
The calculation is performed in momentum space, with the Fourier transform of the potential defined by

{V}(\mathbf{p})=\int e^{-i\mathbf{p}\cdot\mathbf{r}}\,{V}(\mathbf{r})\,d^{3}\mathbf{r}.

The Feynman diagrams contributing to
{V}_{23}
,
{V}_{25}
, and
{V}_{27}

are shown in Fig.
1
, Fig.
2
, and Fig.
3
, respectively.
The set of Feynman diagrams for
{V}_{ij}
is obtained by taking all possible
graphs that satisfy the following conditions:

•

they are connected;

•

they have one external photon line,
j
Coulomb-potential insertions, no other external lines;

•

they have
i
independent loops;

•

they do not have odd electron loops (Furry’s theorem).

In this work, we focus on two-loop diagrams with
i=2
.
In our approach, the graphs (2)-(9)
in Fig.

We employ methods of free QED in our calculation.
To this end, we first
unfold
each Feynman diagram.
Unfolding means replacing each Coulomb-potential insertion with a Coulomb-propagator
line extending to an artificially-added vertex,
see Fig.
4
for an example.
In the figure, the additional vertex is shown as a boxed dot,
and the dashed lines represent the Coulomb propagators.
The new vertex has a fictitious external line that carries away the excess momentum, so that momentum conservation holds.
The vertex contributes no multiplier and carries no tensor index.
In the Feynman gauge, the photon, electron, and Coulomb propagators are (up to prefactors)

\frac{i(\not{\mathit{q}}+m)}{\mathit{q}^{2}-m^{2}+i0},\quad\frac{-ig_{\mu\nu}}{\mathit{q}^{2}+i0},\quad\frac{-\delta(\mathit{q}_{0})\delta_{\mu 0}}{\mathbf{q}^{2}},

(2)

respectively.
The unfolded diagrams for
{V}_{23}
,
{V}_{25}
, and
{V}_{27}
have 4, 6, and 8 loops, respectively.
The large number of loops makes the computation of these Feynman diagrams particularly demanding and time consuming.

Figure 4:
The unfolded Feynman diagrams corresponding to (6) in Fig.
2
.

In this work, we remove all divergences by performing point-by-point subtractions in the Feynman parametric space before integration. We do not employ dimensional regularization or any other schemes based on expansions around singular points, which are commonly used in the literature.
Point-by-point subtraction methods
are relatively rare in quantum field theory calculations, because of the complicated structure of divergences in Feynman diagrams.
However, they become advantageous in numerical computations of diagrams with many loops.
These methods were developed for calculations of
the free electron anomalous magnetic moment
Levine and Wright 1973
;
Carroll and Yao 1974
;
Cvitanović and Kinoshita 1974
;
Aoyama et al. 2012
;
S. Volkov 2024
;
Aoyama et al. 2025
; developments for other problems also exist
Adzhemyan and Kompaniets 2014
.

Our method is based on a subtraction procedure similar to the Bogoliubov-Parasiuk-Hepp-Zimmermann (BPHZ) renormalization scheme
Bogoliubov and Parasiuk 1957
;
Hepp 1966
;
Zimmermann 1969
. In our approach,
Zimmermann’s forest formula
Zavialov and Stepanov 1965
;
Zimmermann 1969
is applied directly. This formula implements renormalization “in place” by adding counterterms to each Feynman amplitude. According to Zimmermann’s forest formula, the renormalized Feynman amplitude is defined as follows:

•

A summation over forests
F
of ultraviolet (UV) divergent subdiagrams is performed; a forest is a set of subdiagrams, where each pair of subdiagrams is either nested or non-intersecting.

•

In each term, we replace the Feynman amplitude of each subdiagram
G^{\prime}\in F
with a momentum-space polynomial by applying suitable projections. The transformation is organized such that it is first applied to smaller subgraphs (with respect to inclusion), and then carried over to larger subgraphs, ultimatively yielding the final amplitude for this term.

•

The coefficient of the term is
(-1)^{|F|}
.

With
N_{l}
,
N_{\gamma}
denoting the numbers of external electron and photon (or Coulomb) lines of a subdiagram, the UV-divergent subdiagrams in QED are:
electron self-energy
(
N_{l}=2
,
N_{\gamma}=0
),
vertex-like
(
N_{l}=2
,
N_{\gamma}=1
),
photon self-energy
(
N_{l}=0
,
N_{\gamma}=2
), and
photon-photon scattering
(
N_{l}=0
,
N_{\gamma}=4
). All such subdiagrams of the original Feynman diagram must be subtracted. However, additional subdiagrams must also be included in the forest formula for the unfolded diagram, namely those with exactly two photon external lines (one fictitious) and no other external lines (except those coming to a fictitious vertex). These correspond to lower-order potential insertions and are also UV divergent. For example, in Fig.
4
the following subdiagrams must be subtracted:
\{1,2,3,4\}
,
\{6,7,8,9\}
,
\{6,7,8,9,11,12,13\}
,
\{1,2,\ldots,14\}
, where subgraphs are encoded by sets of graph lines. The full diagram is always included. In Zimmermann’s forest formula, we take the value at zero momenta for these special subdiagrams, as well as for photon-photon scattering subgraphs. Both subtraction contributions vanish identically due to gauge invariance once all Feynman diagrams are summed.

The subtraction projectors for electron self-energy and vertex-like subdiagrams in Zimmermann’s forest formula are usually
chosen to correspond to QED on-shell renormalization conditions. The drawback of this choice is that
these conditions generate infrared (IR) divergences
1
1

We avoid IR divergences by using the projector
U
defined as

(U\Gamma)_{\mu}(\mathit{p},\mathit{q})=a((m_{0})^{2})\gamma_{\mu},

\quad(U\Sigma)(\mathit{p})=u(m^{2})+v(m^{2})m+v((m_{0})^{2})(\not{\mathit{p}}-m),

where

\mathit{p}-\frac{\mathit{q}}{2}
and
\mathit{p}+\frac{\mathit{q}}{2}
are the incoming and outgoing electron momenta, respectively,

\mathit{q}
is the photon momentum,

\Gamma_{\mu}(\mathit{p},\mathit{q})
is a vertex-like Feynman amplitude,

\Gamma_{\mu}(\mathit{p},0)=a(\mathit{p}^{2})\gamma_{\mu}+b(\mathit{p}^{2})\mathit{p}_{\mu}+c(\mathit{p}^{2})\not{\mathit{p}}\mathit{p}_{\mu}+d(\mathit{p}^{2})(\not{\mathit{p}}\gamma_{\mu}-\gamma_{\mu}\not{\mathit{p}}),

\Sigma(\mathit{p})
is an electron self-energy Feynman amplitude,

\Sigma(\mathit{p})=u(\mathit{p}^{2})+v(\mathit{p}^{2})\not{\mathit{p}},

and
(m_{0})^{2}
is a free parameter. In our calculations, we set

(m_{0})^{2}=-5m^{2}

to minimize numerical cancellations inside integrals (this is motivated by our numerical experience).
Similar projectors were introduced previously for the free electron
g
-factor calculations: first in Ref.
Volkov 2016
with
(m_{0})^{2}=m^{2}
, and later in the current form in Ref.
Volkov 2023
.
The projector
U
preserves the Ward identity, extracts the mass part completely, and does not generate IR divergences. It introduces an electron wave-function renormalization factor relative to standard QED renormalization, but this factor does not appear in our case, as our Feynman diagrams have no external electron lines.

\int_{z\geq 0}F(\mathbf{p},z_{1},\ldots,z_{n})\delta(z_{1}+\ldots+z_{n}-1)d^{n}z

(3)

for each Feynman diagram.
Here,
z_{j}
corresponds to the

unfolded
diagram,
and
\mathbf{p}
is the external momentum. These integrals are finite and can be computed separately for each diagram and then summed.
Our procedure for obtaining the functions

involves the Schwinger parametrization as an intermediate step, as follows:

•

Introduce the Schwinger parametrization for the propagators in Eq. (
2

\displaystyle(\not{\mathit{q}}+m)e^{iz_{j}(\mathit{q}^{2}-m^{2}+i\varepsilon)},\quad ig_{\mu\nu}e^{iz_{j}(\mathit{q}^{2}+i\varepsilon)},

\displaystyle i\delta(\mathit{q}_{0})\delta_{\mu 0}e^{iz_{j}(\mathbf{q}^{2}+i\varepsilon)},

where
z_{1},\ldots,z_{n}\geq 0
are the Schwinger parameters,
\varepsilon>0
is an infrared regulator, and

labels the diagram line.

•

Perform the loop-momentum integrations. We assume the forest formula described above is applied, but
the whole diagram is excluded from it
.
The momentum integral diverges, but at this step we ignore the divergence and use the standard formulas for multidimensional Gaussian integrals with polynomial factors. We obtain

\displaystyle F^{\text{Schw}}(\mathbf{p},z_{1},\ldots,z_{n},\varepsilon)=\frac{1}{D_{0}(z)^{1/2}D_{1}(z)^{3/2}}

\displaystyle\times\left[\sum_{l=0}^{s/2}R_{l}(\mathbf{p}^{2},z)\right]e^{iA(z)\mathbf{p}^{2}+iB(z)-\varepsilon\sum_{j=1}^{n}z_{j}}

\displaystyle+\text{counterterms},

where

and

are the first Symanzik polynomials

Weinzierl 2010

•

Switch to Feynman parameters by

\displaystyle F(\mathbf{p},z)=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{\mathbf{p}^{2}}\lim_{\varepsilon\rightarrow+0}\int_{0}^{+\infty}\lambda^{n-1}

\displaystyle\times

\displaystyle\left[F^{\text{Schw}}(\mathbf{p},\lambda z,\varepsilon)-F^{\text{Schw}}(\mathbf{0},\lambda z,\varepsilon)\right]d\lambda\,.

(4)

where the denominator

The integration over

\displaystyle F(\mathbf{p},z)=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{D_{0}(z)^{1/2}D_{1}(z)^{3/2}}

\displaystyle\times\left\{\frac{W_{0}(z)}{\mathbf{p}^{2}}\log\left(1+\frac{A(z)}{B(z)}\mathbf{p}^{2}\right)\right.

\displaystyle+\sum_{l=1}^{s/2}\left[\frac{W_{l}(z)}{\mathbf{p}^{2}}\left(\frac{1}{(A(z)\mathbf{p}^{2}+B(z))^{l}}-\frac{1}{B(z)^{l}}\right)\right]

\displaystyle\left.+\sum_{l=1}^{s/2}\frac{Y_{l}(\mathbf{p}^{2},z)}{(A(z)\mathbf{p}^{2}+B(z))^{l}}\right\}+\text{counterterms}\,,

The value
F(\mathbf{0},z)

\mathbf{p}=\mathbf{0}

\mathbf{p}=\mathbf{0}

\mathbf{p}=\mathbf{0}

We evaluate the integrals (
3
) numerically using the Monte Carlo method. It is well known that the convergence rate of Monte Carlo integration depends critically on the choice of the probability density function (PDF). Adaptive algorithms that fit the PDF to the integrand exist in the literature, but they can only adjust a relatively small number of parameters. For the complicated structure of typical integrands in Feynman-parameter integrations, such adaptive algorithms become inefficient at high dimensionality. In the present work, we employ a nonadaptive algorithm based on a theoretical analysis of the integrand’s behavior in Feynman-parametric space. This algorithm provides a unique PDF for each Feynman diagram. The problem of constructing this PDF is closely related to constructing
a good upper bound on the integrand absolute value and to proving the finiteness of renormalized Feynman amplitudes.
We borrow several ideas from these analyses.

To construct the PDF, we partition the integration domain into so-called Hepp sectors
Hepp 1966
:

z_{j_{1}}\geq z_{j_{2}}\geq\ldots\geq z_{j_{n}}\,.

Each ordering of the Feynman parameters
defines one Hepp sector.
For example, for
{V}_{27}
we have
n=18

18!=6402373705728000
sectors per diagram.

Our PDFs take the form

C\times\frac{\prod_{l=2}^{n}\left(z_{j_{l}}/z_{j_{l-1}}\right)^{\mathrm{Deg}(\{j_{l},j_{l+1},\ldots,j_{M}\})}}{z_{1}\times z_{2}\times\ldots\times z_{n}}+\text{stability terms},

where
\mathrm{Deg}(s)
are positive numbers defined on
all nonempty proper subsets of the set of all internal lines of the diagram.
For example, for
{V}_{27}
we have
2^{18}-2=262142
such numbers per diagram.
Functions of similar form were first used in Ref.
Speer 1968
. The necessity of stability terms and their general construction were discussed in Ref.
Volkov 2018

Efficient random-sample generation with such PDFs was described in Ref.
Volkov 2017
.
The procedure for obtaining
\mathrm{Deg}(s)
and stabilization terms, as well as further details, will be presented elsewhere.

The most resource-intensive part of the computation is the Monte Carlo integration itself. We employed NVidia P100 GPUs (accompanied by one Intel Xeon E5-2698 CPU core per GPU). Since all divergences are cancelled numerically under the integral sign, the subtractions may lead to large round-off errors. To control these, we used interval arithmetic: at each arithmetic operation we generate an interval
[x_{1};x_{2}]

Once numerical values of the VP potentials are available, the corresponding

reference state. Since the potentials are obtained
in the momentum representation in this work, it is

\displaystyle\langle V_{ij}\rangle=\int\frac{d^{3}\mathbf{p}_{1}}{(2\pi)^{3}}\,\int\frac{d^{3}\mathbf{p}_{2}}{(2\pi)^{3}}\,\psi_{a}^{{\dagger}}(\mathbf{p}_{1})\,V_{ij}(\mathbf{p}_{1}-\mathbf{p}_{2})\,\psi_{a}(\mathbf{p}_{2})\,.

(5)

Yerokhin
et al. 2025c

leaving a

\displaystyle\langle V_{ij}\rangle=

\displaystyle\ \frac{1}{32\pi^{5}}\int_{0}^{\infty}dp_{1}\,dp_{2}\int_{-1}^{1}d\xi\,(p_{1}p_{2})^{2}\,V_{ij}(q)\,

\displaystyle\times\Big[g_{a}(p_{1})\,g_{a}(p_{2})\,P_{l_{a}}(\xi)+f_{a}(p_{1})\,f_{a}(p_{2})\,P_{\overline{l}_{a}}(\xi)\Big]\,,

p_{1}=|\mathbf{p}_{1}|

p_{2}=|\mathbf{p}_{2}|

,

and

Yerokhin

V
Summary

We performed calculations of Coulomb corrections to the leading-order
two-loop vacuum-polarization potential derived 70 years ago
by G. Källén and A. Sabry
Källen and Sabry 1955
. Specifically,
we derived the two-loop vacuum-polarization potentials

V_{23}
,
V_{25}
, and
V_{27}
which
have three, five, and seven Coulomb interactions inside the vacuum-polarization loop,
respectively.
The potentials were computed in momentum representation and stored
on a grid. Furthermore, we
evaluate expectation values of these potentials with the reference-state wave function
and obtain the corresponding energy shifts.
In the limit of small nuclear charges, our results agree with the
analytical result of Krachkov and Lee
Krachkov and Lee 2023
.
Our calculation drastically reduces the uncertainty of the two-loop vacuum-polarization
correction to transition energies, even for high-
Z
ions such as uranium.
