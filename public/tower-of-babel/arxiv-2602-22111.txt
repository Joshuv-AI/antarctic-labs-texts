Seedless Reduction of Feynman Integrals
Leonardo de la Cruz, David A. Kosower

Abstract
We show how to construct a complete set of lowering operators, whose successive application reduces an arbitrary Fenyman integral to a combination of master integrals. The construction builds systems of equations for generic integral indices using IBP-generating vectors. The solution to each system is a lowering operator.



I
Introduction

Frontier calculations in quantum field theory typically involve
hundreds, thousands, or even tens of thousands of Feynman integrals.
These integrals are not independent. There are many linear
relations between them, which can be obtained by writing
down total derivatives. These relations, known as
integration-by-parts (IBP) identities, reduce the number of
independent integrals to a much smaller number.
The idea of using IBP identities was introduced by
Chetyrkin and Tkachov
49
;
18
,
and systematized by Laporta
32
.

Solving
the system of IBP relations gives the relations reducing dependent
integrals to basis integrals. Conceptually, solving the
IBP system is just a matter of performing Gaussian elimination,
but the sizes of systems of interest means that great care
and ingenuity has to be invested in building general-purpose
systems to perform these reductions. Over the years,
researchers have written a number of codes to carry out this
task:
AIR

8
,

FIRE

42
;
44
,

Reduze

48
;
52
,

LiteRed

34
;
35
,

Kira

29
;
31
,
NeatIBP+Kira

55
,
and
Blade

27
.
These implement Laporta’s approach with sophisticated improvements. Researchers have also investigated how to choose a basis of master integrals
41
;
50
;
14
;
13
. Nonetheless, IBP reductions remain
an important bottleneck to performing amplitude calculations at higher loop order.

Feynman integrals depend on invariants of the external
momenta, and also on the exponents
of the propagators and of numerator factors. The latter are
called
indices
.
In standard Laporta approaches, one solves systems of
equations for specific values of indices.
Moreover, in following Laporta’s approach, one of the choices
to be made
is the initial choice of ‘seed’ integrals, which should make it
efficient to solve for desired target integrals. This
particular choice has been the subject of increased scrutiny
recently
51
;
47
;
31
;
44
.

Some authors have explored recurrence relations for generic exponents;
see for instance ref.
(
45
, Chapter 5)
and references therein. Smirnov
made an attempt to use
them in earlier versions of
FIRE
(versions 1–3),
but abandoned them in later versions as explained in ref.
43
.
Later, Lee made use of these ideas in

LiteRed
, obtaining some general rules heuristically and mixing them with a
Laporta approach for fixed values of some exponents.

Codes using the Laporta approach inevitably introduce higher powers of denominators

aka
‘dotted’ integrals. In Yang–Mills and gravity theories such integrals are not needed,
and they are typically removed as part of solving the IBP system.
They can be avoided entirely by making use of special vectors, known as

IBP-generating vectors
, in generating the total
derivatives
26
;
40
;
10
. The
NeatIBP

code
54
implements these ideas (using the Baikov
representation
9
) following earlier ideas
33
;
25
;
12
;
11
. Ita used these vectors in developing a formalism for separating surface terms
28
, and then Abreu
et al.
used them for numerical unitarity at higher loops
5
;
4
;
7
;
6
;
2
;
1
.

More recently,
Smith and Zeng
46
have made use of IBP-generating vectors
to develop an improved set of
rules for generic exponents alongside use of a standard
Laporta approach for specific values of some exponents. Liu
and Mitov
36
have considered a different approach to developing rules for
generic exponents. Feng, Li, Liu, Ma, and Zhang
21
and
Chen, Feng, and Zhang
17
have
developed reduction rules for generic exponents based on
generating functions and differentiation.
Parallel
developments
37
;
22
;
24
;
53
;
23
;
15
;
16
use
intersection theory to simplify Feynman integrals.
Page and Song
38
studied the connection
between intersection theory and IBP-generating vectors;
Coro, Novichkov, Page, and Song
19

then built on this to explore integral reduction based on
Landau analysis.

In this article, we investigate an approach different from the Laporta one,
in which seeds are avoided entirely.
We develop a complete set of
operators that reduce integrals with generic
indices to simpler integrals. Repeated application of the rules will reduce an
arbitrary integral to a combination of basis integrals. We rely on IBP-generating vectors as a key part of our construction.
This follows part of the spirit of work by
one of the authors
30
, which sought to find closed-form
solutions for one class of numerators.

This paper is organized as follows. We present the general setup and approach in Sect.
II
.
In Sect.
III
, we illustrate our approach with the massless double box.
We consider the pentabox in Sect.
IV
. We discuss some implementation considerations
in Sect.
V
, and give our conclusions in Sect.
VI
.

II
Setup and Strategy

We consider dimensionally regulated Feynman integrals in
D
space-time dimensions,

I[\mathcal{N}({\boldsymbol{\ell}},\boldsymbol{k})]=\int\prod_{j=1}^{L}\frac{\mathrm{d}^{D}\ell_{j}}{(2\pi)^{D}}\;\frac{\mathcal{N}({\boldsymbol{\ell}},\boldsymbol{k})}{\mathcal{D}_{1}^{a_{1}}\cdots\mathcal{D}_{c}^{a_{c}}}\,,

(1)

where
{\boldsymbol{\ell}}
denotes the
L
loop momenta, and
\boldsymbol{k}

the set
of
n_{e}
external momenta, which are strictly
four-dimensional.
The denominators have the form,

\displaystyle\mathcal{D}_{e}=(M_{e})^{jr}\ell_{j}\cdot\ell_{r}+2(Q_{e})^{jr}\ell_{j}\cdot k_{r}+Y_{e}+i\varepsilon\,,

(2)

where the matrices
M_{e}
and
Q_{e}
have dimensions

L\times L
and
L\times(n_{e}-1)
respectively. We use an
additional set of expressions of the same general form which
are independent of the denominators, called irreducible
scalar products (ISPs) and denoted here by
J_{i}
.
Along with the denominators and one chosen scalar product
s_{0}

of external momenta, these give us a basis for
expressing any scalar product of external and loop momenta.
Accordingly, we can express a general
numerator
\mathcal{N}({\boldsymbol{\ell}},\boldsymbol{k})

in terms of
\mathcal{D}
s,
J
s, and
s_{0}
. We rewrite other
scalar products
s_{ij}=(k_{i}+k_{j})^{2}
as

\chi_{ij}s_{0}
in terms of dimensionless parameters
\chi_{ij}
.
Numerator terms with factors
of any
\mathcal{D}
simply give us similar integrals with fewer
denominators, so without loss of generality we can consider
numerators built out of a product of
J
s,

I[\boldsymbol{a};-\boldsymbol{{\bar{a}}}]=\int\prod_{j=1}^{L}\frac{\mathrm{d}^{D}\ell_{j}}{(2\pi)^{D}}\;\frac{J_{1}^{{\bar{a}}_{1}}\cdots J_{{\bar{c}}}^{{\bar{a}}_{{\bar{c}}}}}{\mathcal{D}_{1}^{a_{1}}\cdots\mathcal{D}_{c}^{a_{c}}}\,.

(3)

We follow the usual convention that indices
{\bar{a}}

associated to numerator factors take negative values, along
with the usual labeling convention for the integral. We expect
the approach
described here to be applicable with small modifications
to linearized or eikonal propagators, but we will not consider them in the
present article.

We will make use of IBP-generating vectors
26
.
Each ‘vector’ is an
L
-tuple of Lorentz vectors, designed
to avoid introducing
doubled propagators into IBP relations. Denoting the
loop index
by
A
, each generator vector satisfies,

v_{A}^{\mu}\,\frac{\partial\mathcal{D}_{i}}{\partial\ell_{A}^{\mu}}=g_{i}\mathcal{D}_{i}\,,\quad\textrm{no\ sum\ on\ }i\,,

(4)

where the
A
index is implicitly summed over. In this constraint,
each
g_{i}
is a polynomial in the denominators, ISPs,
and
s_{0}
,
or equivalently in the set of ‘variables’ consisting of
all dot products of loop momenta with themselves, or with a
basis of
n_{b}=\min(n_{e}-1,4)
external momenta,

\{\ell_{i}^{2}\,,\ell_{i}\cdot\ell_{j}\,,\ell_{i}\cdot k_{j}\,,s_{0}\}\,.

(5)

Each element of
v_{A}^{\mu}
is a sum over coefficients times basis
vectors,

v_{A}^{\mu}=\sum_{j=1}^{L}f_{Aj}^{\vphantom{\mu}}\ell_{j}^{\mu}+\sum_{j=1}^{n_{b}}h_{Aj}^{\vphantom{\mu}}b_{j}^{\mu}\,.

(6)

The coefficients
f_{Aj}
and
h_{Aj}
are polynomials in
the variables (
5
). We define
the operator
\mathop{\rm cdeg}\nolimits
to
extract the degrees of these coefficients in the variables (
5
),

\mathop{\rm cdeg}\nolimits v_{A}^{\mu}=\mathop{\rm deg}\nolimits(f_{Aj})=\mathop{\rm deg}\nolimits(h_{Aj})\,.

(7)

In this equation,
\mathop{\rm deg}\nolimits
is the degree of its argument in
terms of the variables (
5
). The polynomials are necessarily
homogeneous. (Four basis vectors suffice because as usual
we take all external vectors to be strictly four-dimensional.)

Thanks to eq. (
4
), a typical total derivative
takes the form,

\frac{\partial}{\partial\ell_{A}^{\mu}}\biggl[\frac{v_{A}^{\mu}\,J_{1}^{{\bar{a}}_{1}}\cdots J_{{\bar{c}}}^{{\bar{a}}_{{\bar{c}}}}}{\mathcal{D}_{1}^{a_{1}}\cdots\mathcal{D}_{c}^{a_{c}}}\biggr]=\frac{\frac{\partial}{\partial\ell_{A}^{\mu}}\bigl[v_{A}^{\mu}\,J_{1}^{{\bar{a}}_{1}}\cdots J_{{\bar{c}}}^{{\bar{a}}_{{\bar{c}}}}\bigr]}{\mathcal{D}_{1}^{a_{1}}\cdots\mathcal{D}_{c}^{a_{c}}}-\frac{J_{1}^{{\bar{a}}_{1}}\cdots J_{{\bar{c}}}^{{\bar{a}}_{{\bar{c}}}}\sum_{j=1}^{c}g_{j}}{\mathcal{D}_{1}^{a_{1}}\cdots\mathcal{D}_{c}^{a_{c}}}\,.

(8)

An IBP relation generated with
v
does not have doubled
propagators if none were present originally; and more
generally, does not increase the powers of denominator
factors. Using all generating vectors,
we obtain a complete set of IBP relations, so that effectively
their use block-diagonalizes the system of equations. The

NeatIBP
code uses such generating vectors (in Baikov
variables) to obtain IBP relations.

The basic building block in our approach is an IBP equation
generated with a single generating vector out of the set of

n_{v}
,

0=R_{i}(\boldsymbol{a};-\boldsymbol{{\bar{a}}})=\int\prod_{j=1}^{L}\frac{\mathrm{d}^{D}\ell_{j}}{(2\pi)^{D}}\;\frac{\partial}{\partial\ell_{A}^{\mu}}\biggl[\frac{v_{iA}^{\mu}\,J_{1}^{{\bar{a}}_{1}}\cdots J_{{\bar{c}}}^{{\bar{a}}_{{\bar{c}}}}}{\mathcal{D}_{1}^{a_{1}}\cdots\mathcal{D}_{c}^{a_{c}}}\biggr]\,.

(9)

We allow only equations with negative or zero values of the

{\bar{a}}
indices.
We define
d_{i}
to be the degree of the
i^{\rm th}
vector,
and
d_{0}
the minimum degree of all vectors,

\displaystyle d_{i}

\displaystyle=\mathop{\rm cdeg}\nolimits v_{i}^{\mu}\,,

(10)

\displaystyle d_{0}

\displaystyle=\min_{i}d_{i}\,.

We will make use of a triangular sublattice of the nonnegative integer lattice
\mathbb{Z}_{\geq 0}^{n}
,

\mathbb{T}^{n}[m]=\bigl\{\boldsymbol{s}\in\mathbb{Z}^{n}_{\geq 0}\big|\,|\boldsymbol{s}|_{1}=m\bigr\}\,.

(11)

Here,
|\boldsymbol{s}|_{1}
is the sum of the components of
\boldsymbol{s}
.
We will call
m
the
level
, and the individual
elements
shifts
. For example,

\mathbb{T}^{3}[1]=\{(1,0,0)\,,(0,1,0)\,,(0,0,1)\}\,.

(12)

Using such a sublattice, we can build sets of IBP relations
for a given vector at a given level,

S_{i}^{[m]}(\boldsymbol{a};-\boldsymbol{{\bar{a}}})=\bigcup_{\boldsymbol{s}\in\mathbb{T}^{{\bar{c}}}[m]}R_{i}(\boldsymbol{a};\boldsymbol{s}-\boldsymbol{{\bar{a}}})\,.

(13)

We shift only the indices associated to the ISPs, and we exclude
equations where any such index is positive (that is,
where the ISP would appear in the denominator). We then
build a set of relations from all
n_{v}
vectors
for the given integral topology. We take all
numerators to be of the same degree,

S^{[m]}(\boldsymbol{a};-\boldsymbol{{\bar{a}}})=\bigcup_{i=1}^{n_{v}}s_{0}^{m}S_{i}^{[m+d_{i}-d_{0}]}(\boldsymbol{a};\boldsymbol{s}-\boldsymbol{{\bar{a}}})\,.

(14)

We multiply by the power of
s_{0}
to keep the dimensions
of equations of different levels the same.
For a given target integral,
with generic or fixed
exponents
\boldsymbol{a}
and
\boldsymbol{{\bar{a}}}
,
we assemble as many of these as required to find a linear
combination of the IBP relations so that the target integral
has a nonzero coefficient and that the linear combination
includes no top-level
integral with any exponent
{\bar{a}}_{i}
larger
than that of the target integral. (That is,
all top-level integrals with any ISP index more negative than
in the target integral are unwanted, and we eliminate them.)
Solving the resulting
relation for the target integral gives us a
lowering

operator. Such lowering operators can be thought of as
carefully chosen linear combinations of textbook lowering and
raising operators
45
. We can write a
complete set as follows,

C^{{[N_{\textrm{max}}]}}(\boldsymbol{a};-\boldsymbol{{\bar{a}}})=\bigcup_{m=0}^{N_{\textrm{max}}}S^{[m]}(\boldsymbol{a};-\boldsymbol{{\bar{a}}})\,.

(15)

Here,
N_{\textrm{max}}
is determined iteratively,
by increasing
m
and adding sets of IBP relations until
the defining conditions can be met.
The lowering operator we obtain lowers one or more indices,
and avoids raising any index in
top-level integrals. In general, the resulting
lowering operator will not be unique, and we will be able to
impose further constraints to obtain different choices of
operator. This is a novel aspect of our approach.
Only the final result of repeatedly applying
all relevant lowering operators to a given integral, yielding
an expression in terms of a chosen basis of integrals, will
be unique. Similar but less systematically organized sets were used in refs.
30
;
3
.

\displaystyle\mathcal{D}_{1}

\displaystyle=\ell_{1}^{2}\,,\quad\mathcal{D}_{2}=(\ell_{1}-k_{1})^{2}\,,\quad\mathcal{D}_{3}=(\ell_{1}-K_{12})^{2}\,,\quad

(16)

\displaystyle=\ell_{2}^{2}\,,\quad\mathcal{D}_{5}=(\ell_{2}-k_{4})^{2}\,,\quad\mathcal{D}_{6}=(\ell_{2}-K_{34})^{2}\,,\quad\mathcal{D}_{7}=(\ell_{1}+\ell_{2})^{2}\,.

(17)

\displaystyle F_{1}:\penalty

\displaystyle\ell_{1}\leftrightarrow\ell_{2}\,,k_{1}\leftrightarrow k_{4}\,,k_{2}\leftrightarrow k_{3}\,;

\displaystyle F_{2}:\penalty

\displaystyle\ell_{1}\rightarrow K_{12}-\ell_{1}\,,\ell_{2}\rightarrow K_{34}-\ell_{2}\,,k_{1}\leftrightarrow k_{2}\,,k_{3}\leftrightarrow k_{4}\,.

\displaystyle F_{1}:\penalty

\displaystyle F_{1}:\penalty

\displaystyle I[a_{1},a_{2},a_{3},a_{4},a_{5},a_{6},a_{7},a_{8},a_{9}]\longrightarrow I[a_{4},a_{5},a_{6},a_{1},a_{2},a_{3},a_{7},a_{9},a_{8}]\,;

(21)

\displaystyle F_{2}:\penalty

There are three independent generating vectors, one of degree
(
\mathop{\rm cdeg}\nolimits
) one, and two of degree two. The first vector is,

\displaystyle v_{1;1}^{\mu}

\displaystyle=2k_{1}\cdot\ell_{1}\,k_{2}^{\mu}\!+\!(2\,k_{1}\!-\!\ell_{1})\cdot\ell_{1}\,k_{3}^{\mu}\!-\!(2\,(k_{2}\!+\!k_{3})\cdot\ell_{1}\!-\!\ell_{1}^{2})\,k_{1}^{\mu}\!+\!(s_{12}\!-\!2(k_{1}\!-\!k_{3})\cdot\ell_{1})\,\ell_{1}^{\mu}\,,

(22)

\displaystyle v_{1;2}^{\mu}

\displaystyle=2k_{1}\cdot\ell_{2}\,k_{2}^{\mu}\!-\!(2\,(k_{2}\!+\!k_{3})\cdot\ell_{2}\!+\!\ell_{2}^{2})\,k_{1}^{\mu}\!+\!(s_{12}\!+\!2(k_{1}\!-\!k_{3})\cdot\ell_{2})\,\ell_{2}^{\mu}\!+\!(2\,k_{1}\!+\!\ell_{2})\cdot\ell_{2}\,k_{3}^{\mu}\,.

The first subscript is the vector number, and the second
the loop index. The expressions for the other two vectors are
lengthier. All vectors are given in the auxiliary file

db-vectors.m
. We computed them using

Singular

20
.

III.2
Boundary Lowering Operators

As explained in the previous subsection,
the lowering operator (
29

the coefficients
\gamma_{1,2,3,4}
given
in eq. (

applied to all integrals with

{\bar{a}}_{1}\geq{\bar{a}}_{2}\geq 1
, but not with

{\bar{a}}_{2}=0

We need a specialized ‘boundary’ operator to handle
integrals with ISP exponents
({\bar{a}}_{1},0)
.
Here, level shifts
0
and
1
do not suffice to find
a lowering operator. When we add level shift
2
,
we obtain nine equations from

C^{{[2]}}(\boldsymbol{1};-{\bar{a}}_{1},0)
and eight constraints, and thus
a unique lowering operator. We obtain
an operator of the form,

\displaystyle\hskip-8.53581pt\hat{L}^{[\partial]}({\bar{a}}_{1},0)=I[\boldsymbol{1};-{\bar{a}}_{1},0]

\displaystyle\longrightarrow\gamma_{5}\,s_{12}\,I[\boldsymbol{1};1-{\bar{a}}_{1},0]+\gamma_{6}\,s_{12}^{2}\,I[\boldsymbol{1};2-{\bar{a}}_{1},0]+\textrm{daughters}\,,

with,

\displaystyle\gamma_{5}

\displaystyle=-\frac{2-{\bar{a}}_{1}\,(1-\chi)-\chi+3\,\epsilon}{1-{\bar{a}}_{1}+2\,\epsilon}\,,

(35)

\displaystyle\gamma_{6}

\displaystyle=\frac{\chi\,(2-{\bar{a}}_{1}+\epsilon)}{1-{\bar{a}}_{1}+2\,\epsilon}\,.

The daughter terms contain indices as large
as
3-{\bar{a}}_{1}
, and so this operator
can be applied freely to all integrals with ISP exponents

({\bar{a}}_{1}\geq 3,0)
. We do not
expect to find an operator that can reduce the integral with
ISP exponents
(1,0)
, as
it is a master in the usual Laporta ordering.
It turns out that the coefficients of integrals containing
an index
3-{\bar{a}}_{1}
are all proportional to

2-{\bar{a}}_{1}
, so that we can apply the operator
(with appropriate care) to the case
(2,0)
as well.
In general, we might have to obtain special ‘edge’ operators,
where all
{\bar{a}}_{i}
have fixed values,
for such cases. The operator
in eq. (
34

46
where instead they mix in the use of a standard Laporta approach.

III.3
Propagator Lowering Operators

The use of generating vectors ensures that IBP equations
don’t introduce propagators with powers above
1

if they weren’t present initially. In general, because of
gauge invariance, expressions for amplitudes in Yang–Mills
theory and gravity do not require such integrals. Nonetheless,
they may arise through the process of expansions (such as the
post-Newtonian expansion in gravity), through differentiation
with respect to external momenta in deriving
differential equations, or after integrating out inner two-
or three-point loops.
While generating vectors do not introduce such dotted
integrals, they can of course be used to remove them. In
this subsection, we discuss the construction of lowering
operators that lower propagator indices greater than
1

V
Implementation

Within
Mathematica
or other symbolic math systems,
one can implement the lowering operators as rules relying on
pattern matching. For numerical implementation in other
frameworks, such as
Python
or
C++
, we
need a different paradigm. There are two that appear
suitable. These are a recursive implementation, with
caching of intermediate results (for example, via a
hash table); and use of sparse matrices.

Each lowering operator for specific values of the
indices can be represented as a sparse matrix.
Nonzero entries are indexed by a pair of vectors
giving the indices of the integral being reduced,
and the integral appearing in its reduction.
The entry values can be fully numerical, if using rational
reconstruction methods to obtain the dependence on the
dimensional regulator
\epsilon
, or a series of coefficients
up to the required order in it. The starting values
are likewise given by a sparse vector. One then
carries out the reduction by multiplying a sequence
of sparse matrices. With maximum ISP exponents

(m_{1},m_{2})
, one expects to store
\mathcal{O}(m_{1}m_{2})

sparse matrices (or equivalently, hash-table entries
for the recursive approach).

While the optimal choice
of lowering operators is not yet clear, we can estimate
an upper bound on the number of operations required
for the reduction of top-level double-box integrals
by using the wall-hugging operator described in section
III.1
.
Taking
m_{1}\sim m_{2}\sim m
,
and ignoring constant factors, we see that
\mathcal{O}(m)

applications of the wall-hugging operator would be
required to get to a configuration with

{\bar{a}}_{2}=0
, at which point another
\mathcal{O}(m)

applications of a boundary operator would be required to
get to master integrals. Each sparse matrix involved
may have up to
\mathcal{O}(m)
terms, so that overall we will need

\mathcal{O}(m^{2})
operations for the reduction of the top-level
integrals. For comparison, a naive Laporta approach
will start with
\mathcal{O}(m^{2})
different integrals, requiring
Gaussian elimination on an
\mathcal{O}(m^{2})\times\mathcal{O}(m^{2})
matrix.
Without any tricks, this would require

\mathcal{O}(m^{6})
operations.

VI
Conclusions

In this article, we have shown how to construct a complete
set of lowering operators for Feynman integrals. Each
application of such an operator reduces an integral
with arbitrary indices to simpler top-level and
daughter integrals. A successive application of lowering
operators will reduce all top-level integrals to
master integrals. In general these operators could also
be applied to integrals with non-integer indices. Similar operators can of course be
constructed for daughter integrals. We studied two
examples, the planar double box in Sect.
III
,
and the planar pentabox in Sect.
IV
.
The required lowering operators for the
double box are given in
eqs. (
29
,
32
,
34
,
35
)
for undotted integrals,
and in the auxiliary
file
db-operators.m
as well alongside
lowering operators for dotted integrals.

Our construction generally yields operators with free
parameters. These parameters can be chosen to enforce
special properties of the lowering operators. We have given an example of such a refinement in eq. (
33
). We leave
an investigation of the optimal way to choose these parameters,
along with the optimal way to build lowering operators for
daughter integrals, to future investigation.
