Neural operators for solving nonlinear inverse problems
Otmar Scherzer otmar.scherzer@univie.ac.at, Thi Lan Nhi Vu thi.lan.nhi.vu@univie.ac.at, Jikai Yan jikai.yan@univie.ac.at

Abstract
We consider solving a probably infinite dimensional operator equation, where the operator is not modeled by physical laws but is specified indirectly via training pairs of the input-output relation of the operator. Neural operators have proven to be efficient to approximate operators with such information. In this paper, we analyze Tikhonov regularization with neural operators as surrogates for solving ill-posed operator equations. The analysis is based on balancing approximation errors of neural operators, regularization parameters, and noise. Moreover, we extend the approximation properties of neural operators from sets of continuous functions to Sobolev and Lebesgue spaces, which is crucial for solving inverse problems. Finally, we address the problem of finding an appropriate network structure of neural operators.

1. Introduction

In this paper, we consider
nonlinear inverse problems
, which are formulated as finding the solution of a
nonlinear
operator equation

F[{\tt x}]={\tt y}\;,

(1.1)

where the operator
F:\mathcal{D}(F)\subseteq{\tt X}\to{\tt Y}
is defined on the domain
\mathcal{D}(F)
, which is a subset of an infinite-dimensional Hilbert space
{\tt X}
, and maps into a Hilbert space
{\tt Y}
.

Inverse problems are often ill-posed, meaning that their solutions do not depend continuously on the data
{\tt y}
. Specifically, if
{\tt y}^{\delta}\to{\tt y}
, there is no guarantee that a solution
{\tt x}^{\delta}
of
Equation 1.1
with data
{\tt y}^{\delta}
(presumably it exists) will converge to a solution of
Equation 1.1
.

Therefore, a regularization technique needs to be applied to solve
Equation 1.1
in a stable manner. One of the most widely studied regularization method is Tikhonov regularization, which approximates a solution of
Equation 1.1
by a minimizer
{\tt x}^{\alpha\delta}
of the quadratic Tikhonov functional

\mathbf{T}^{\alpha\delta}[{\tt x}]:=\|F[{\tt x}]-{\tt y}^{\delta}\|_{\tt Y}^{2}+\alpha\|{\tt x}-{\tt x}^{(0)}\|_{\tt X}^{2}\quad\text{over}\quad{\tt x}\in\mathcal{D}(F),

(1.2)

with
\alpha>0
for some fixed point
{\tt x}^{(0)}\in\mathcal{D}(F)
. It is common to put
{\tt x}^{(0)}=0
when dealing with linear operator, and we do the same here. Tikhonov regularization has been generalized to convex regularization, which consists in minimization of

\mathbf{T}_{\mathcal{R}}^{\alpha\delta}[{\tt x}]:=\|F[{\tt x}]-{\tt y}^{\delta}\|_{\tt Y}^{2}+\alpha\mathbf{R}[{\tt x}]\quad\text{over}\quad{\tt x}\in\mathcal{D}(F),

(1.3)

where
\mathbf{R}:{\tt X}\to[0,\infty]
is a functional, typically convex and proper. The most important generalized variational regularization methods are
total variation minimization
(see
[
26
]
) and
sparsity regularization
(see
[
13
]
). A general convex regularization functional is often associated with a formulation in a Banach space (see, for instance,
[
27
,
29
]
). We do not consider these generalizations, because the considered simple examples have been evaluated more thoroughly in the quadratic setting. Generalizations can, of course, be done, but they lead to technical complications (see
[
24
]
).

For computational purposes, the Tikhonov functional is discretized in the following way:

(i)

The space
{\tt X}
is approximated by a sequence of nested subspaces
\left\{{\tt X}_{\tt m}:{\tt m}\in\mathds{N}\right\}
such that

\overline{\bigcup_{{\tt m}\in\mathds{N}}{\tt X}_{\tt m}}=\overline{\lim_{{\tt m}\to\infty}{\tt X}_{\tt m}}={\tt X}\;.

(ii)

A family of operators
\left\{F_{\tt n}:{\tt n}\in\mathds{N}\right\}
is constructed to approximate
F
uniformly in a bounded neighborhood of a solution
{\tt x}^{\dagger}
of
Equation 1.1
. Specifically, there exists a ball
\mathcal{B}_{\tt r}({\tt x}^{\dagger})
with radius
{\tt r}>0
such that

\|F[{\tt x}]-F_{\tt n}[{\tt x}]\|_{\tt Y}\leq\rho_{\tt n}\quad\text{for all}\quad{\tt x}\in\mathcal{D}(F)\cap\mathcal{B}_{\tt r}({\tt x}^{\dagger}),

(1.4)

where
\rho_{\tt n}\to 0
as
{\tt n}\to\infty
.

Discretized Tikhonov regularization consists in
approximate minimization
of the functional

\mathbf{T}_{{\tt m}{\tt n}}^{\alpha\delta}[{\tt x}]:=\|F_{\tt n}[{\tt x}]-{\tt y}^{\delta}\|_{\tt Y}^{2}+\alpha\|{\tt x}-{\tt x}^{(0)}\|_{\tt X}^{2}

(1.5)

over
{\tt X}_{\tt m}
. We denote an approximate minimizer of
\mathbf{T}_{{\tt m}{\tt n}}^{\alpha\delta}
by

{\tt x}^{\alpha\delta}_{{\tt m}{\tt n}}\in{\tt X}_{\tt m}\cap\mathcal{D}(F)\;.

(1.6)

We work with
approximate minimizer
because, in general, the minimizer of the discrete functional
\mathbf{T}_{{\tt m}{\tt n}}^{\alpha\delta}
(see
Equation 1.5
) might not exist. In practice, one computes some
{\tt x}\in{\tt X}
such that

\mathbf{T}_{{\tt m}{\tt n}}^{\alpha\delta}[{\tt x}]-\inf_{{\tt X}_{\tt m}}\mathbf{T}_{{\tt m}{\tt n}}^{\alpha\delta}\leq\eta\,,

(1.7)

which means that we look for an element
{\tt x}\in{\tt X}
such that
\mathbf{T}_{{\tt m}{\tt n}}^{\alpha\delta}[{\tt x}]
meets the infimum with a tolerance
\eta
, for which the magnitude can be analytically specified. Such an element
{\tt x}_{{\tt m}{\tt n}}^{\alpha\delta\eta}
is referred to as an approximate minimizer. More details can be found in
[
22
,
24
]
.

Traditionally, spline spaces have been used for
{\tt X}_{\tt m}
, and finite element operators have been used for
F_{\tt n}
(see
[
21
,
22
,
24
]
). In this setting, discretizations
{\tt m}
and
{\tt n}
can be determined such that
{\tt x}_{{\tt m}{\tt n}}^{\alpha\delta\eta}
is an optimal approximation in
{\tt X}_{\tt m}
of the minimum-norm solution
{\tt x}^{\dagger}
of
Equation 1.1
. The conceptual difference of neural operators is that they are learned from training data (see
Equation 3.4
below).

To simplify the analysis, we constrain ourselves to operator approximations rather than to finite-dimensional approximations of
{\tt X}
. That is, we consider the operator approximation
F_{\tt n}
defined on
{\tt X}
rather than on
{\tt X}_{\tt m}
. This assumption allows us to reduce the technicality of this paper.

The central question of this paper is whether the approximate minimizers of the Tikhonov functionals defined on
{\tt X}
with
neural operators

F_{\tt n}
approximating
F
, namely,

\mathbf{T}_{{\tt n}}^{\alpha\delta}[{\tt x}]:=\|F_{\tt n}[{\tt x}]-{\tt y}^{\delta}\|_{\tt Y}^{2}+\alpha\|{\tt x}-{\tt x}^{(0)}\|_{\tt X}^{2}\,,

(1.8)

are optimal approximations of the regularized solutions of the functional
\mathbf{T}^{\alpha\delta}
, defined in
Equation 1.2
.

Neural operators, as used in this paper, are defined as DeepONet in
[
20
]
. The idea of neural operator traces back to
[
9
,
10
]
, where the concepts of learning functionals and operators were first developed. Building on the DeepONet framework and inspired by the physics-informed neural networks

[
25
]
, other types of neural operators have been developed in
[
30
]
. In parallel with DeepONet-based implementations, the Fourier-neural operator has been introduced in
[
19
]
.

One of the difficulties associated with neural operators is that
quantitative
error estimates are not as advanced as those for finite difference and finite element approximation operators. Therefore, in turn,
quantitative
regularization theory, as established for classical approximation methods as in
[
21
,
22
,
24
]
, needs to be developed, which is a concern of this paper. In particular, we concentrate on simple examples to explain the technical challenges:

(i)

Examples of inverse problems:
First, we recall two examples of inverse problems, originally proposed in
[
14
]
, which demonstrate the optimal interactions between discretizations
{\tt m}
,
{\tt n}
, noise
\delta
and the regularization parameter
\alpha
. We consider these examples first in the classic setting with finite element approximations
F_{\tt n}
(see
Section 2
), and subsequently with neural operators learned from training samples (see
Section 5
and
Section 6
).

(ii)

Challenges of neural operators:
We recall the definition of
neural operators
from
[
20
]
and highlight some challenges when applying them as part of a regularization method (see
Section 3
). We propose modifications to neural operators that enable their use as surrogate operators for approximately solving
Equation 1.1
(see
Section 6
). A particular challenge is that regularization analysis is significantly simpler in Hilbert spaces. Therefore, we require neural operators to be defined on Sobolev and Lebesgue spaces.

(iii)

Priors for neural operators:
Training a neural operator involves solving for a high-dimensional set of parameters. Since the training process is highly nonlinear, strategies for determining appropriate priors are essential to identify locally optimal training parameters (see for instance
[
28
]
). One such strategy is discussed in
Section 4
.

Throughout this paper, we make the following assumptions:

•

Equation 1.1
has a solution and there exists a minimum-norm solution
{\tt x}^{\dagger}
. That is,

{\tt x}^{\dagger}:=\text{argmin}\left\{{\tt x}\in\mathcal{D}(F):{\tt x}\text{ solves }\autoref{eq:op}\right\}\;.

•

The operator
F
is Fréchet-differentiable. Furthermore, the Fréchet-derivative is locally uniformly bounded in a neighborhood of
{\tt x}^{\dagger}
, that is, there exist
{\tt r}>0
and
L>0
such that

\|F^{\prime}[{\tt x}]-F^{\prime}[{\tt x}^{\dagger}]\|\leq L\|{\tt x}-{\tt x}^{\dagger}\|_{\tt X}\text{ for all }\|{\tt x}-{\tt x}^{\dagger}\|_{\tt X}\leq{\tt r}\;.

(1.9)

2. Examples (classics with finite elements)

In this section, we review two simple inverse problems and how they are solved with Tikhonov regularization with classical finite element operator approximations.

Example 2.1
(The
a
-example).

For a given
{\tt f}\in L^{2}(0,1)
, we consider the differential equation for
{\tt y}
:

\begin{cases}\quad-\left({\tt x}{\tt y}^{\prime}\right)^{\prime}(s)&={\tt f}(s)\text{ for all }s\in(0,1),\\
\quad{\tt y}(0)={\tt y}(1)&=0,\end{cases}

(2.1)

where
{\tt x}\in H^{1}(0,1)
is a parameter. The parameter
{\tt x}
is often denoted by
a
in the literature (see
[
14
,
22
]
), which motivates our name of the example.
For this problem, the forward operator is defined as

\displaystyle F:\mathcal{D}(F):=\left\{{\tt x}\in H^{1}(0,1):{\tt x}\geq\nu>0\right\}

\displaystyle\to L^{2}(0,1)\;

(2.2)

\displaystyle{\tt x}

\displaystyle\mapsto{\tt y}=:F[{\tt x}].

We first consider the classical
finite element approach
:
Let
{\tt Y}_{\tt n}
denote the space of linear splines on a uniform mesh with mesh size
{\tt n}^{-1}
, vanishing at the boundary. The finite element approximation of
Equation 2.1
is
{\tt y}_{\tt n}\in{\tt Y}_{\tt n}
, which satisfies:

\left<{\tt x}{\tt y}_{\tt n}^{\prime},\tt y^{\prime}\right>_{L^{2}}=\left<\tt f,\tt y\right>_{L^{2}}\quad\text{for all}\quad\tt y\in{\tt Y}_{\tt n}.

(2.3)

The finite element approximation
F_{\tt n}
of the operator
F
is given by:

\displaystyle F_{\tt n}:\mathcal{D}(F):=\left\{{\tt x}\in H^{1}(0,1):{\tt x}\geq\nu>0\right\}

\displaystyle\to L^{2}(0,1)\;

(2.4)

\displaystyle{\tt x}

\displaystyle\mapsto{\tt y}_{\tt n}=:F_{\tt n}[{\tt x}].

The approximation error of
F_{\tt n}
has been intensively studied and is given by (see, for instance,
[
7
,
23
,
11
]
):

\|F[{\tt x}]-F_{\tt n}[{\tt x}]\|_{L^{2}}=\|{\tt y}-{\tt y}_{\tt n}\|_{L^{2}}=\mathcal{O}((1+\|{\tt x}\|_{H^{1}}){\tt n}^{-2}),

(2.5)

that is,
\rho_{\tt n}=\mathcal{O}({\tt n}^{-2})
.

With such estimates, it is possible to derive optimal convergence rates for
{\tt x}_{{\tt m}{\tt n}}^{\alpha\delta}
, which we recall here for the convenience of readers. We summarize the essential assumptions for
[
22
, Corollary 3.5]
:

•

Let
{\tt x}^{\dagger}\in H^{2}(0,1)
and assume that
\tt f
is sufficiently smooth such that
{\tt y}={\tt y}^{0}=F[{\tt x}^{\dagger}]\in H^{2}(0,1)\cap H^{1}_{0}(0,1)
(this is the noise-free data). Between the noise-free data
{\tt y}
and noisy
{\tt y}^{\delta}
, we assume that

\|{\tt y}-{\tt y}^{\delta}\|_{\tt Y}\leq\delta\;.

(2.6)

•

We further assume that the
source condition
(as postulated in
[
14
,
22
]
) holds:

\begin{array}[]{c}\varphi:={\tt x}^{\dagger}-{\tt x}^{(0)}\in H^{3}(0,1)\text{ satisfies }\varphi^{\prime}(0)=\varphi^{\prime}(1)=0,\\
\text{ The }L^{2}\text{-norm of }r\in(0,1)\mapsto\int_{0}^{r}\frac{(I-\Delta)({\tt x}^{(0)}-{\tt x}^{\dagger})}{{\tt y}^{\prime}[{\tt x}^{(0)}]}(s)ds\in H^{2}(0,1)\cap H_{0}^{1}(0,1)\text{ is sufficiently small.}\end{array}

(2.7)

With
\eta
as in
Equation 1.7
, we make the choice of parameters

\alpha\sim\max\left\{\delta,{\tt n}^{-2}\right\},\;\eta\sim\alpha^{2},

(2.8)

which gives the optimal convergence rate for the regularized solution (as defined in
Equation 1.6
):

\|{\tt x}_{\tt n}^{\alpha\delta\eta}-{\tt x}^{\dagger}\|_{H^{1}}=\mathcal{O}(\sqrt{\delta}+{\tt n}^{-1})\;.

(2.9)

Example 2.2
(The
c
-example).

Let
{\tt f}\in L^{2}(0,1)
be given. Now, we consider the differential equation

\begin{cases}\quad-{\tt y}^{\prime\prime}(s)+{\tt x}(s){\tt y}(s)&={\tt f}(s)\text{ for all }s\in(0,1),\\
\quad\quad\quad\quad{\tt y}(0)={\tt y}(1)&=0,\end{cases}

(2.10)

with parameter
{\tt x}\in L^{2}(0,1)
.
{\tt x}
is often denoted by
c
in the literature, which gives this example its name. Here, the forward operator is defined as

\displaystyle F:\mathcal{D}(F):=\left\{{\tt x}\in L^{2}(0,1):{\tt x}\geq\nu>0\right\}

\displaystyle\to L^{2}(0,1)\;.

\displaystyle{\tt x}

\displaystyle\mapsto{\tt y}=:F[{\tt x}],

The finite element approximation of the operator
F
on the finite-dimensional subspace of linear spline
{\tt Y}_{\tt n}
with
{\tt n}-1
internal nodes of
{\tt Y}=L^{2}(0,1)
is given by

\displaystyle F_{\tt n}:\mathcal{D}(F):=\left\{{\tt x}\in L^{2}(0,1):{\tt x}\geq 0\right\}

\displaystyle\to L^{2}(0,1)\;

(2.11)

\displaystyle{\tt x}

\displaystyle\mapsto{\tt y}_{\tt n}=:F_{\tt n}[{\tt x}].

Here,
{\tt y}_{\tt n}\in{\tt Y}_{\tt n}
is the unique solution of

\left<{\tt y}_{\tt n}^{\prime},\tt y^{\prime}\right>_{L^{2}}+\left<{\tt x}{\tt y}_{\tt n},\tt y\right>_{L^{2}}=\left<\tt f,\tt y\right>_{L^{2}}\quad\text{ for all }\quad\tt y\in{\tt Y}_{\tt n}.

Let
0<\nu<{\tt x}^{(0)}\in H^{2}(0,1)
be such that

\frac{{\tt x}^{\dagger}-{\tt x}^{(0)}}{{\tt y}[{\tt x}^{(0)}]}\in H^{2}(0,1)\cap H_{0}^{1}(0,1)\text{ with }\text{a sufficiently small $L^{2}$-norm}.

(2.12)

Analogous to
Section 2
, we have
\rho_{\tt n}=\mathcal{O}({\tt n}^{-2})
and we derive an optimal choice of parameters,

\alpha\sim\max\left\{\delta,{\tt n}^{-2}\right\},\;\eta\sim\alpha^{2},

(2.13)

and obtain the following convergence rate for the regularized solution:

\|{\tt x}_{\tt n}^{\alpha\delta\eta}-{\tt x}^{\dagger}\|_{L^{2}}=\mathcal{O}(\sqrt{\delta}+{\tt n}^{-1})\;.

(2.14)

We emphasize that in
[
22
]
, additional results with various kinds of smoothness assumptions have been shown.

The goal of this paper is to derive error estimates of the form
Equation 2.9
based on neural operators.

3. Neural operators

Neural operators
are novel tools for approximating
nonlinear
operators (see
[
20
]
). The approach extends neural network approximations of functions and functionals to operators.

We use the following notation:

Notation 3.1
.

{\tt X}=\left\{{\tt x}:\Omega_{\tt X}\subseteq\mathds{R}^{m}\to\mathds{R}\right\}
and
{\tt Y}=\left\{{\tt y}:\Omega_{\tt Y}\subseteq\mathds{R}^{n}\to\mathds{R}\right\}
are normed spaces of functions with norms
\|\cdot\|_{\tt X}
and
\|\cdot\|_{\tt Y}
, respectively. We assume that
\Omega_{\tt X}
and
\Omega_{\tt Y}
are bounded domain with piecewise smooth boundaries and that they satisfy the cone property (see
[
1
]
). Unless explicitly stated otherwise, this assumption will always be assumed to hold.

We refer to
{\tt Y}
as the
data space
and
{\tt X}
as the
image space
in accordance with the terminology of
[
6
,
4
]
.

A variety of activation functions are used in neural network theory (see, for instance,
[
12
,
18
]
). Neural operators are defined based on sigmoidal activation functions, as described below:

Definition 3.2
(Sigmoidal function).

A strictly monotonically increasing and differentiable function
\sigma:\mathds{R}\to\mathds{R}
is called
sigmoidal
if it satisfies

\sigma(t)\to\begin{cases}1&\text{as }t\to+\infty\\
0&\text{as }t\to-\infty\end{cases}.

(3.1)

For examples, the logistic sigmoid function, the hyperbolic tangens and the arctangens are sigmoidial.

To define neural operators, we have to assume that the elements of
{\tt X}
allow for point evaluations. For instance, we can assume that every function
{\tt x}\in{\tt X}
is continuous. Then, the operator
F
can be approximated by a
neural operator
, which is defined as follows (see
[
12
,
9
,
10
]
):

Definition 3.3
(Neural operator).

The operator
F_{\tt n}
defined by

{\tt x}\mapsto F_{\tt n}[{\tt x}](\vec{t}):=\sum_{j=1}^{N_{j}}\sum_{k=1}^{N_{k}}\alpha_{j,k}\sigma\left(\sum_{l=1}^{N_{l}}w_{j,k,l}{\tt x}(\vec{s}_{j})+\theta_{j,k}\right)\sigma({\vec{w}}_{j}^{T}\vec{t}+\zeta_{j})\text{ for all }\vec{t}\in\Omega_{\tt Y}\subseteq\mathds{R}^{n}\,,

(3.2)

where the coefficients are given by

\mathcal{T}_{\tt n}=\begin{pmatrix}\underbrace{\alpha_{j,k}}_{\in\mathds{R}}&\underbrace{w_{j,k,l}}_{\in\mathds{R}}&\underbrace{{\vec{w}}_{j}}_{\in\mathds{R}^{n}}&\underbrace{\theta_{j,k}}_{\in\mathds{R}}&\underbrace{\vec{s}_{j}}_{\in\Omega_{\tt X}\subseteq\mathds{R}^{m}}&\underbrace{\zeta_{j}}_{\in\mathds{R}}&\end{pmatrix}_{\tiny\begin{array}[]{c}j=1,\cdots,N_{j}\\
k=1,\cdots,N_{k}\\
l=1,\cdots,N_{l}\end{array}},

(3.3)

is called neural operator.
Here,
{\tt n}=\left|\mathcal{T}_{\tt n}\right|
and
\sigma
is a
sigmoidal function
as defined in
Section 3
. Note that the numbers of coefficients
N_{j},N_{k},N_{l}
are fixed and do not vary with respect to
j,k,l
. This means that we do not require counts that depends on two indices, such as
N_{kj}
, and so on.

The
universal approximation theorem for operators
from
[
10
,
20
]
guarantees the existence of coefficients

\mathcal{T}_{\tt n}
such that the corresponding neural operator
F_{\tt n}
uniformly approximates the operator
F
on a compact subset of continuous images in
{\tt X}
. The convergence analysis of algorithms for training is complicated: In the numerical example in
[
28
,
18
]
, the authors analyzed iterative regularization methods for training and observed the importance of priors. The analysis will get even more complicated when operators are approximated.

Definition 3.4
(Training of neural operators).

The coefficients
\mathcal{T}_{\tt n}
of a neural operator are determined through supervised training samples

\mathcal{S}_{\tt N}:=\left\{(\hat{{\tt x}}^{(\ell)},\hat{{\tt y}}^{(\ell)}):\hat{{\tt y}}^{(\ell)}={F[\hat{{\tt x}}^{(\ell)}]},\ell=0,1,\cdots,{\tt N}\right\}\,,

(3.4)

such that

\displaystyle\hat{{\tt y}}^{(\ell)}(\vec{t}_{\rho})

\displaystyle=\sum_{j=1}^{N_{j}}\sum_{k=1}^{N_{k}}\alpha_{j,k}\sigma\left(\sum_{l=1}^{N_{l}}w_{j,k,l}\hat{{\tt x}}^{(\ell)}(\vec{s}_{j})+\theta_{j,k}\right)\sigma({\vec{w}}_{j}^{T}\vec{t}_{\rho}+\zeta_{j})=F_{\tt n}[\hat{{\tt x}}^{(\ell)}](\vec{t}_{\rho})\;.

(3.5)

Here
\left\{\vec{t}_{\rho}:\rho=1,\cdots,Q\right\}
are sampling points in
\Omega_{\tt Y}
, which we assume to be given.

We also use the training data centered at
(\hat{{\tt x}}^{(0)},\hat{{\tt y}}^{(0)})
, which is given by

\mathcal{S}_{\tt N}^{0}:=\left\{({\tt x}^{(\ell)},{\tt y}^{(\ell)}):=(\hat{{\tt x}}^{(\ell)},\hat{{\tt y}}^{(\ell)})-(\hat{{\tt x}}^{(0)},\hat{{\tt y}}^{(0)}):\ell=1,\cdots,{\tt N}\right\}\;.

(3.6)

In the following, we concentrate on specific aspects of neural operators, which hinder direct application as surrogate operators for solving inverse problems:

(i)

The operator must be analyzed in Lebesgue and Sobolev spaces (see
Section 2
). Sobolev spaces are somewhat easier to handle because they can often be compactly embedded into spaces of continuous functions (see
Section 5.1
). This fact is used for analyzing the
a
-example (see
Section 2
) with a neural operator, which surrogates the nonlinear operator
F
. For Lebesgue spaces, such as required in the
c
-example (see
Section 2
), additional smoothing of the neural operator is required. To establish this approximation, we require balancing the smoothing parameter to determine the optimal regularization parameter (see
Section 6
).

(ii)

We prove some universal approximation theorems for neural operators in Sobolev and Lebesgue spaces. However, unlike for finite element approximations of
F
, we do not obtain uniform estimates with respect to
{\tt n}
; see
Equation 2.5
and
Equation 4.7
. In other words, neural operators can be applied for solving inverse problems, but they lack an a-priori choice for the optimal discretization.

We begin with the second item to explain the different types of approximations.

4. Priors for training of neural operators

Training of neural operators is highly nonlinear and therefore is a complex computational problem. Efficient implementation requires priors to determine coefficients
\mathcal{T}_{\tt n}
and their counts
N_{j},N_{k},N_{l}
(see
Equation 3.3
) before initiating the actual training process with a gradient descent algorithm. In what follows, we describe the proposed strategy. In particular, our calculations demonstrate the dependence on the expert data.

We start by approximating a linear operator with neural operators.

4.1. Neural operators for linear operator regression

In this subsection, we assume that the operator
F
in
Equation 1.1
is linear. In order to learn the linear operator, we use a strategy developed in
[
5
]
, which consists in orthonormalizing the centered training images
{\tt x}^{(\ell)}
,
\ell=1,\dots,{\tt N}
(see
Equation 3.6
), with Gram-Schmidt and computing the according data. Gram-Schmidt requires that the training images
{\tt x}^{(\ell)}
,
\ell=1,\dots,{\tt N}
, are linearly independent. Additionally, we require that the linear operator
F
has a trivial null space. Consequently, the training data
{\tt y}^{(\ell)}
are also linearly independent. More sophisticated algorithms have been investigated in
[
17
]
. In particular, the techniques developed there do not require the assumptions that the training images are linearly independent and that
F
has a trivial null space.

In the following, we denote by

\displaystyle\left\{\underline{{\tt x}}^{(\ell)}:\ell=1,\dots,{\tt N}\right\}\text{ an orthonormalized family obtained from }\left\{{\tt x}^{(\ell)}:\ell=1,\dots,{\tt N}\right\},\text{ and }\underline{{\tt y}}^{(\ell)}=F\underline{{\tt x}}^{(\ell)}.

We emphasize that under the above assumptions, there exist explicit formulas for
\underline{{\tt y}}^{(\ell)}
(see
[
6
,
3
]
).
Let

\displaystyle{\tt X}_{\tt N}

\displaystyle:=\text{span}\left\{\underline{{\tt x}}^{(\ell)}:\ell=1,\dots,{{\tt N}}\right\}=\text{span}\left\{{\tt x}^{(\ell)}:\ell=1,\dots,{{\tt N}}\right\}

(4.1)

\displaystyle\text{and}\quad{\tt Y}_{\tt N}

\displaystyle:=\text{span}\left\{\underline{{\tt y}}^{(\ell)}:\ell=1,\dots,{{\tt N}}\right\}=\text{span}\left\{{\tt y}^{(\ell)}:\ell=1,\dots,{{\tt N}}\right\},

which are the compact subsets of
{\tt X}
and
{\tt Y}
, respectively. For every image
{\tt x}\in{\tt X}_{\tt N}
, we have the basis expansion

{\tt x}=\sum_{\ell=1}^{\tt N}\left<{\tt x},\underline{{\tt x}}^{(\ell)}\right>_{\tt X}\underline{{\tt x}}^{(\ell)},

and from the linearity of
F
, we obtain

{\tt y}=F{\tt x}=\sum_{\ell=1}^{\tt N}\left<{\tt x},\underline{{\tt x}}^{(\ell)}\right>_{\tt X}F\underline{{\tt x}}^{(\ell)}=\sum_{\ell=1}^{\tt N}\left<{\tt x},\underline{{\tt x}}^{(\ell)}\right>_{\tt X}\underline{{\tt y}}^{(\ell)}\;\in{\tt Y}_{\tt N}\;,

where
\vec{t}\in\Omega_{\tt Y}\mapsto\underline{{\tt y}}^{(\ell)}(\vec{t})
are functions and

{\tt x}\mapsto\left<{\tt x},\underline{{\tt x}}^{(\ell)}\right>_{\tt X}
are functionals. Now, we define an operator

\displaystyle F_{\tt N}^{\#}:{\tt X}

\displaystyle\to{\tt Y}

(4.2)

\displaystyle{\tt x}

\displaystyle\mapsto\sum_{\ell=1}^{\tt N}\left<{\tt x},\underline{{\tt x}}^{(\ell)}\right>_{\tt X}\underline{{\tt y}}^{(\ell)}\;.

Note that
F_{\tt N}^{\#}
and
F
coincide on
{\tt X}_{\tt N}
, that is,
F_{\tt N}^{\#}|_{{\tt X}_{\tt N}}=F|_{{\tt X}_{\tt N}}
. Moreover,
F_{\tt N}^{\#}=0
on
{\tt X}_{\tt N}^{\perp}
.
The operator
F_{\tt N}^{\#}
is expressed as a sum of products of functionals
{\tt x}\mapsto\left<{\tt x},\underline{{\tt x}}^{(\ell)}\right>_{\tt X}
, which can be approximated by networks as in
[
9
,
10
]
, and functions

\vec{t}\mapsto\underline{{\tt y}}^{(\ell)}(\vec{t})
, which can be approximated using standard neural network approximations of functions
(see, for instance,
[
12
]
).

By substituting the neural network approximations of functionals and functions into
Equation 4.2
, and
under the assumption that every function
\underline{{\tt y}}^{(\ell)},\ell=1,\dots,{\tt N}
, is continuous, we obtatin that

\displaystyle F_{\tt N}^{\#}{\tt x}(\vec{t})

\displaystyle=\sum_{\ell=1}^{\tt N}\left<{\tt x},\underline{{\tt x}}^{(\ell)}\right>_{\tt X}\underline{{\tt y}}^{(\ell)}(\vec{t})

(4.3)

\displaystyle\approx\sum_{\ell=1}^{\tt N}\left(\sum_{k=1}^{N_{k}(\ell)}C_{k}^{(\ell)}\sigma\left(\sum_{l=1}^{N_{l}(\ell)}w_{k,l}^{(\ell)}{\tt x}(\vec{s}_{l})+\theta_{k}^{(\ell)}\right)\right)\left(\sum_{j=1}^{N_{j}(\ell)}c_{j}^{(\ell)}\sigma\left({\vec{w}}_{j}^{(\ell)T}\vec{t}+\zeta_{j}^{(\ell)}\right)\right)

\displaystyle=\sum_{\ell=1}^{\tt N}\sum_{j=1}^{N_{j}(\ell)}\sum_{k=1}^{N_{k}(\ell)}\alpha_{j,k}^{(\ell)}\sigma\left(\sum_{l=1}^{N_{l}(\ell)}w_{k,l}^{(\ell)}{\tt x}(\vec{s}_{l})+\theta_{k}^{(\ell)}\right)\sigma\left({\vec{w}}_{j}^{(\ell)T}\vec{t}+\zeta_{j}^{(\ell)}\right):=\tilde{F}_{\tt n}[{\tt x}](\vec{t})

for all
{\tt x}\in{\tt X}
and
\vec{t}\in\Omega_{\tt Y}
.
\tilde{F}_{\tt n}
is a nonlinear operator used to approximate the linear operator
F_{\tt N}^{\#}
. Note that, for nonlinear operators we use brackets,
[\cdot]
, for operator evaluations, which are left out for linear operators. In comparison to the structure of the neural operator in
Equation 3.2
, there is an additional summation over
\ell=1,\ldots,{\tt N}
.

From this, and with
N_{\alpha}:=\max\left\{N_{\alpha}(\ell):\ell=1,\ldots,{\tt N}\right\}
for
\alpha=j,k,l
, we obtain

\displaystyle\tilde{F}_{\tt n}[{\tt x}](\vec{t})

\displaystyle=\sum_{\ell=1}^{\tt N}\sum_{j=1}^{N_{j}}\sum_{k=1}^{N_{k}}\alpha_{j,k}^{(\ell)}\sigma\left(\sum_{l=1}^{N_{l}}w_{k,l}^{(\ell)}{\tt x}(\vec{s}_{l})+\theta_{k}^{(\ell)}\right)\sigma\left({\vec{w}}_{j}^{(\ell)T}\vec{t}+\zeta_{j}^{(\ell)}\right)

(4.4)

\displaystyle=\sum_{j=1}^{N_{j}{\tt N}}\sum_{k=1}^{N_{k}{\tt N}}\tilde{\alpha}_{j,k}\sigma\left(\sum_{l=1}^{N_{l}{\tt N}}\tilde{w}_{k,l}{\tt x}(\vec{s}_{l})+\tilde{\theta}_{k}\right)\sigma\left(\tilde{{\vec{w}}}_{j}^{T}\vec{t}+\tilde{\zeta}_{j}\right).

As can be seen from this equation, the operator
\tilde{F}_{\tt n}
in
Equation 4.4
explains the structure of the neural operators in
Equation 3.2
. The so-called
branch net
describes the neural functional approximation and the
trunk net
describes the neural function approximation. Furthermore, in
Equation 4.3
, there is a connection between the network structure and the number of training data, which seems to be unexplored so far. The cited terminology is from
[
20
]
.

Now, we give an approximation result for linear operators with neural operators:

Corollary 4.1
.

Let
F:{\tt X}\to{\tt Y}
be a linear operator, and let
\tilde{F}_{\tt n}
,
F_{\tt N}^{\#}
be as defined in
Equation 4.3
. Assume that there exist rate functions
q,r:\mathds{N}\to(0,\infty)
such that for all
{\tt x}\in\mathcal{B}_{\rho}({\tt x}^{\dagger})
and
\ell=1,\ldots,{\tt N}
, the following estimates hold:

\left|\left<{\tt x},\underline{{\tt x}}^{(\ell)}\right>_{\tt X}-\sum_{k=1}^{N_{k}}C_{k}^{(\ell)}\sigma\left(\sum_{l=1}^{N_{l}}w_{k,l}^{(\ell)}{\tt x}(\vec{s}_{l})+\theta_{k}^{(\ell)}\right)\right|\leq q({\tt N})

(4.5)

and

\left\|\vec{t}\mapsto\underline{{\tt y}}^{(\ell)}(\vec{t})-\sum_{j=1}^{N_{j}}c_{j}^{(\ell)}\sigma\left({\vec{w}}_{j}^{(\ell)T}\vec{t}+\zeta_{j}^{(\ell)}\right)\right\|_{\tt Y}\leq r({\tt N})\;.

(4.6)

Then, it holds that

\displaystyle\|F{\tt x}-\tilde{F}_{\tt n}[{\tt x}]\|_{\tt Y}

\displaystyle\leq\|F{\tt x}-F_{\tt N}^{\#}{\tt x}\|_{\tt Y}+\|F_{\tt N}^{\#}{\tt x}-\tilde{F}_{\tt n}[{\tt x}]\|_{\tt Y}

\displaystyle\leq\underbrace{\|(I-P_{\tt N})F\|}_{=:\nu_{\tt N}}+{\tt N}q({\tt N})r({\tt N})\;,

(4.7)

where
P_{{\tt N}}
is the orthonormal projector onto
{\tt Y}_{\tt N}
.

\nu_{\tt N}
denotes the operator norm of
(I-P_{\tt N})F
, which converges to
0
when
F
is compact.

Equation 4.7
means that

\rho_{\tt n}\leq\nu_{\tt N}+{\tt N}q({\tt N})r({\tt N})\;.

This shows the importance of
\nu_{\tt N}
, since a crude estimate with
I-P_{\tt N}
does not converge to
0
.

Example 4.2
.

•

Let us assume that
{\tt X}=L^{2}(0,1)
. Then, by approximation with trapezoidal rule, if
{\tt x}
and
\underline{{\tt x}}^{(\ell)}
are twice differentiable, with the notation
t_{k}=\frac{k}{N_{k}}
,
k=0,1,\ldots,N_{k}
, we have:

\displaystyle\left<{\tt x},\underline{{\tt x}}^{(\ell)}\right>_{L^{2}}

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{1}{\tt x}(t)\underline{{\tt x}}^{(\ell)}(t)dt

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2N_{k}}{\tt x}(0)\underline{{\tt x}}^{(\ell)}(0)+\frac{1}{N_{k}}\sum_{k=1}^{N_{k}-1}{\tt x}(t_{k})\underline{{\tt x}}^{(\ell)}(t_{k})+\frac{1}{2N_{k}}{\tt x}(1)\underline{{\tt x}}^{(\ell)}(1)+\mathcal{O}(N_{k}^{-2})\;.

To determine the parameters
w_{k}
and
\theta_{k}
, we solve the implicit equation

{\tt x}(t_{k})\underline{{\tt x}}^{(\ell)}(t_{k})=\sigma(w_{k}{\tt x}(t_{k})+\theta_{k})=\sigma\left(\sum_{l=1}^{N_{l}}w_{k,l}^{(\ell)}{\tt x}(\vec{s}_{l})+\theta_{k}^{(\ell)}\right).

(4.8)

Note that in this case, for the sake of simplicity, we ignore the dependence on
N_{l}
.
Let us assume that
{\tt x}
and
\underline{{\tt x}}^{(\ell)}
are bounded between
(0,1)
. Then, from
Equation 4.8
, we can get the explicit relation

\sigma^{-1}\left({\tt x}(t_{k})\underline{{\tt x}}^{(\ell)}(t_{k})\right)=w_{k}^{(\ell)}{\tt x}(t_{k})+\theta_{k}^{(\ell)}\;.

(4.9)

We choose the coefficients
({\overline{w}}_{k}^{(\ell)},{\overline{\theta}}_{k}^{(\ell)})
such that
Equation 4.9
holds, and

\|(w_{k}^{(\ell)},\theta_{k}^{(\ell)})\|_{2}\to\min.

In other words, we get an explicit formula for the coefficients, which can be chosen even independent of
N_{l}
.

Moreover, comparing with
Equation 4.3
, the coefficients
C_{k}^{(\ell)}
are determined by

C_{k}^{(\ell)}=\begin{cases}\frac{1}{2N_{k}}&\text{ for }k=0\text{ and }N_{k},\\
\frac{1}{N_{k}}&\text{ otherwise. }\end{cases}

If we choose the number of discretization points in the trapezoidal rule to match the number of training data points (i.e., if
N_{k}={\tt N}
), then we obtain the rate

q({\tt N})={\tt N}^{-2}\;.

•

The derivation of an estimate for
r({\tt N})
is more difficult. Typically, quantitative estimates for the
L^{2}
-error of the function approximation indicate that the error decreases at a rate of
\mathcal{O}(N_{j}^{-1/2})
and is bounded by a term proportional to the
\mathcal{L}^{1}
-norm of the network’s coefficients (see
[
8
]
). However, this norm is not beneficial in regularization theory. Nevertheless, if we assume that it carries over to the desired norms in regularization theory, we would get the following estimate for the last term on the right-hand side of
Equation 4.7
:

{\tt N}q({\tt N})r({\tt N})=\frac{1}{{\tt N}\sqrt{N_{j}}}\,.

(4.10)

For
N_{j}\in\mathds{N}
, there exists a number
p>0
such that
N_{j}\sim{\tt N}^{p}
, and the chosen rate becomes

{\tt N}q({\tt N})r({\tt N})=\mathcal{O}({\tt N}^{-1-\frac{p}{2}})\,.

Now, we come to the main theorem of this section:

Theorem 4.3
(
F
linear).

Let
{\tt x}_{\tt n}^{\alpha\delta\eta}
be an approximate minimizer of the approximate Tikhonov functional

\tilde{\mathbf{T}}_{{\tt n}}^{\alpha\delta}[{\tt x}]:=\|\tilde{F}_{\tt n}[{\tt x}]-{\tt y}^{\delta}\|_{\tt Y}^{2}+\alpha\|{\tt x}\|^{2}_{{\tt X}}\,,

(4.11)

where
\tilde{F}_{\tt n}
is defined in
Section 4.2
.
We assume that the following source condition holds

{\tt x}^{\dagger}=F^{*}\omega,

(4.12)

for
\omega\in N(F^{*})^{\perp}
, and that

\|F{\tt x}-\tilde{F}_{\tt n}[{\tt x}]\|_{\tt Y}\leq\rho_{\tt n}

holds locally uniformly in a neighborhood of
{\tt x}^{\dagger}
. If we choose

\alpha\sim\max\{\delta,\rho_{\tt n}\}\;\text{ and }\;\eta\sim\alpha^{2}\,,

then

\|{\tt x}_{\tt n}^{\alpha\delta\eta}-{\tt x}^{\dagger}\|_{L^{2}}=\mathcal{O}(\sqrt{\delta}+\sqrt{\rho_{\tt n}})=\mathcal{O}(\sqrt{\delta}+\sqrt{\nu_{\tt N}+{\tt N}q({\tt N})r({\tt N})})\;.

Remark 4.4
.

The term
\rho_{\tt n}
frequently appears in regularization theory when operator perturbations are considered (see
[
16
,
14
,
21
]
). In the context of finite element approximation, it has been shown (see
[
22
]
) that when
{\tt Y}_{\tt N}
is the space of linear splines, one has
\rho_{\tt n}=\mathcal{O}({\tt n}^{-2})
(note that for finite element approximations we have
{\tt n}\sim{\tt N}
)
in
Section 2
and
Section 2
.
q({\tt N})
(see
Equation 4.5
) is the approximation error of the discretization of an inner product (typically using a quadrature formula) and
r({\tt N})
(see
Equation 4.6
) represents the approximation error of the expert data. Because both of them can be selected independently of the number of training samples
{\tt N}
, the second term in the error estimate
Equation 4.7
seems to be of less importance.

We have shown how to explicitly calculate a neural operator that approximates a linear operator
F
. If
F
is nonlinear, we can use the aforementioned strategy as motivation.

5. Neural operators in Sobolev spaces

As we have seen in
Section 2
and
Section 2
, in inverse problems, the forward operators
F
is often defined on subsets of Sobolev or Lebesgue spaces.

The ultimate goal of this section is to show that the operator
F
defined on subsets of Sobolev spaces can be locally approximated by a neural operator
F_{\tt n}
in
Equation 3.2
. The nonlinear operator
\tilde{F}_{\tt n}
, as defined in
Equation 4.3
on the right-hand side, is a specific form of
F_{\tt n}
, which could be used to explain the structure of
F_{\tt n}
. Consequently, results of this and the next section can also be transfer to
\tilde{F}_{\tt n}
.

5.1. Approximation theorems in Sobolev spaces

We begin with some definitions and results from the literature, which are necessary to prove an extension of the universal approximation results for
operators
on Sobolev spaces.

Definition 5.1
(Continuous operators and functionals).

Let
({\tt X},\|\cdot\|_{\tt X}),({\tt Y},\|\cdot\|_{\tt Y})
be Banach spaces and let
\tt M
be a subset of
{\tt X}
. An operator
F:\tt M\to{\tt Y}
is continuous on
\tt M
if for every open set
\tt V\subseteq{\tt Y}
, there exists an open set
\tt U\subseteq{\tt X}
such that the inverse image
F^{-1}(\tt V)=\tt M\cap\tt U
.

In particular, a
functional

F:\tt M\to\mathds{R}
is continuous at
{\tt x}_{0}\in\tt M
if for every
\varepsilon>0
, there exists a neighborhood
\tt U\subseteq{\tt X}
of
{\tt x}_{0}
such that
\left|{F[{\tt x}]}-{F[{\tt x}_{0}]}\right|<\varepsilon
for all
{\tt x}\in\tt U
.
F
is continuous on
\tt M
if it is continuous at every point of
\tt M
.

We consider compactness of sets in the following sense:

Definition 5.2
(Compactness).

Let
({\tt V},\|\cdot\|_{\tt V})
be a normed spaces and
{\tt U}\subseteq{\tt V}
. We say that
{\tt U}
is compact in
{\tt V}
if every sequence in
{\tt U}
has a subsequence that converges in the
\|\cdot\|_{V}
-norm to an element in
{\tt V}
.

In the following, we review universal theorems concerning functions, functionals, and operators as studied in
[

]
.

(5.1)

where

is defined in

We assume that the nonlinear source condition holds,

{\tt x}^{(0)}-{\tt x}^{\dagger}=F^{\prime}[{\tt x}^{\dagger}]^{*}\omega\text{ with }L\|\omega\|_{\tt Y}<1,

(5.2)

of the regularized solution.

6. Neural operators in
L^{2}

Instead of approximate minimization of the Tikhonov functional (defined in
Equation 1.8
), we consider finding an approximate minimizer the minimizer of

\mathbf{T}_{\xi{\tt n}}^{\alpha\delta}[{\tt x}]:=\|F_{\tt n}[{\tt x}]-{\tt y}^{\delta}\|_{\tt Y}^{2}+\alpha\|{\tt x}-{\tt x}^{(0)}\|^{2}_{{\tt X}}\,

(6.1)

over the set of
mollified functions

{\tt X}_{\xi}:=\left\{{\tt x}_{\xi}:{\tt x}\in L^{2}(\Omega_{\tt X})\right\}\;.

Due to the continuity of mollified functions, neural operators can be directly applied to them, as point evaluations are well-defined. This means that every term in
Equation 6.1
is also well-defined.

To analyze
Equation 6.1
, we review the method of
mollification
:

Definition 6.1
.

For a function
{\tt x}\in L^{2}(\Omega_{\tt X})
, which is extended by zero outside of
\Omega_{\tt X}\subseteq\mathds{R}^{m}
, the set of mollifications

\left\{{\tt x}_{\xi}:\xi>0\right\}
is defined by

\vec{s}\in\mathds{R}^{m}\mapsto{\tt x}_{\xi}(\vec{s}):=(\phi_{\xi}*{\tt x})(\vec{s})=\int_{\mathds{R}^{m}}\phi_{\xi}(\vec{s}-\vec{t}){\tt x}(\vec{t})\ d\vec{t},

where

\phi_{\xi}(\vec{s}):=\frac{1}{\xi^{m}}\phi\left(\frac{\vec{s}}{\xi}\right)\;

are called
mollifiers
.
Here,

\vec{s}\mapsto\phi(\vec{s}):=\begin{cases}C\exp\left(\displaystyle\frac{1}{|\vec{s}|^{2}-1}\right)&\text{if }|\vec{s}|<1\\
0&\text{if }|\vec{s}|\geq 1\end{cases}\in C^{\infty}(\mathds{R}^{m})

denotes the standard mollifier with a constant
C
selected such that
\int_{\mathds{R}^{m}}\phi(\vec{s})\,\mathrm{d}\vec{s}=1.

The functions in
\left\{{\tt x}_{\xi}:\xi>0\right\}\subseteq C_{0}^{\infty}(\mathds{R}^{m})
satisfies the following properties (see for instance
[
2
,
15
]
):

Lemma 6.2
.

(i)

{\tt x}_{\xi}\in C^{\infty}(\Omega_{\xi})
, where
\Omega_{\xi}:=\{\vec{s}\in\Omega_{\tt x}:\text{dist}(\vec{s},\partial\Omega_{\tt X})>\xi\}.

(ii)

{\tt x}_{\xi}\xrightarrow{\xi\to 0}{\tt x}\text{ almost everywhere}
.

(iii)

\|{\tt x}_{\xi}\|_{L^{2}}\leq\|{\tt x}\|_{L^{2}}
.

(iv)

\|{\tt x}_{\xi}-{\tt x}\|_{L^{2}}\xrightarrow{\xi\to 0}0
.

(v)

For all
s\leq k
and
{\tt x}\in H^{k}(\Omega_{\tt X})
,

\|{\tt x}_{\xi}-{\tt x}\|_{H^{s}}\leq C\xi^{k-s}\|{\tt x}\|_{H^{k}}=\mathcal{O}(\xi^{k-s})\;.

We show the well-definedness of the mollified Tikhonov-regularized solutions, which are the minimizers of
Equation 6.1
:

Theorem 6.3
(
F_{\tt n}
from
Equation 3.2
in
L^{2}
).

For every
\epsilon>0
, there exist
\xi>0
and a vector
\mathcal{T}_{\tt n}
, with associated operator
F_{\tt n}
from
Equation 3.2
, such that locally uniformly in
L^{2}(\Omega_{\tt X})
, it holds:

\|F[{\tt x}]-F_{\tt n}[{\tt x}_{\xi}]\|_{L^{2}}<\epsilon\;.

Specifically, for every
{\tt x}^{*}\in\mathcal{D}(F)
, there exists a ball
\mathcal{B}_{\tt r}({\tt x}^{*})
with radius
{\tt r}>0
such that

\rho_{{\tt n}\xi}:=\sup_{{\tt x}\in\mathcal{B}_{\tt r}({\tt x}^{*})}\|F[{\tt x}]-F_{\tt n}[{\tt x}_{\xi}]\|_{L^{2}}\leq\epsilon.

(6.2)

Proof
:

Let
\epsilon>0
and
{\tt x}^{*}\in L^{2}(\Omega_{\tt X})
be fixed. From the Frèchet-differentiability of
F
,
it follows that there exists a
\xi>0
such that the following holds locally uniformly within an
L^{2}(\Omega_{\tt X})
-neighborhood
\mathcal{B}_{\tt r}({\tt x}^{*})
with radius
{\tt r}>0
of
{\tt x}^{*}
:

\|F[{\tt x}]-F[{\tt x}_{\xi}]\|_{L^{2}}<\frac{\epsilon}{2}.

Moreover, from the property
\|{\tt x}_{\xi}\|_{L^{2}}\leq\|{\tt x}\|_{L^{2}}
(see
Lemma 6.2
,(iii)), the subset
\{{\tt x}_{\xi}:{\tt x}\in\mathcal{B}_{\tt r}({\tt x}^{*})\}\subseteq\mathcal{B}_{\tt r}({\tt x}^{*})\cap{\tt X}_{\xi}
is uniformly bounded and equicontinuous. Therefore, by the Arzelà-Ascoli theorem, this subset is compact in
C({\overline{\Omega_{\tt X}}})
. This compactness satisfies the condition of
Section 5.1
.

As a result, it holds locally uniformly within
\mathcal{B}_{\tt r}({\tt x}^{*})
that

\|F[{\tt x}_{\xi}]-F_{\tt n}[{\tt x}_{\xi}]\|_{L^{2}}<\frac{\epsilon}{2}.

Combining this estimates with the earlier bound yields the assertion.

\square

Remark 6.5
.

Equation 6.3
looks curious in the sense that on the right-hand side one would expect a term
\xi^{2}
instead of
\xi
. However, we cannot directly get the estimate for
\|{\tt x}_{{\tt n}\xi}^{\alpha\delta\eta}-{\tt x}^{\dagger}\|_{L^{2}}
from the estimate of

by triangle inequality. Indeed,

\|{\tt x}_{{\tt n}\xi}^{\alpha\delta\eta}-{\tt x}^{\dagger}\|_{L^{2}}\leq\|{\tt x}_{{\tt n}\xi}^{\alpha\delta\eta}-{\tt x}^{\dagger}_{\xi}\|_{L^{2}}+\|{\tt x}^{\dagger}_{\xi}-{\tt x}^{\dagger}\|_{L^{2}},

but the estimate for

does not satisfy the source condition

Instead, we can use the triangle inequality in the range of

for some constant

\|F_{\tt n}[{\tt x}_{\xi}]-F[{\tt x}]\|_{L^{2}}\leq\|F_{\tt n}[{\tt x}_{\xi}]-F[{\tt x}_{\xi}]\|_{L^{2}}+\|F[{\tt x}_{\xi}]-F[{\tt x}]\|_{L^{2}}\leq\mathcal{O}(\rho_{{\tt n}\xi}+\xi^{2})

From this, we can apply the technique in

\|{\tt x}_{{\tt n}\xi}^{\alpha\delta\eta}-{\tt x}^{\dagger}\|_{L^{2}}

Conclusion

In this paper, we studied Tikhonov regularization for solving inverse problem, where neural operators are used as surrogates for the forward operator. The developed theory is based on the theory of finite dimensional approximations of Tikhonov regularization. These results show how to choose the regularization parameter in dependence of the discretization and the approximation error of the surrogate operator. Traditionally, for operator approximation, finite element and finite difference have been used, while here we study neural operators, which are computed from supervised training data.
We have explored the application of neural operators in regularization at the hand of two basic examples of inverse problems. For applications, we realized that the existing approximation results of neural operators is not sufficient for our purposes because the standard regularization theory requires the approximation rates and formulations in Sobolev and Lebesgue spaces, rather than spaces of continuous functions. Our motivation for neural operators provides valuable insights into the structure of these operators and confirms results from the literature, showing that the number of representing parameters
\mathcal{T}
can be significantly reduced in practice, thereby simplifying the highly complex training process.
The analysis of this paper is supported by a series of examples, where analytical computations offer further insights into the structure of neural operators.
