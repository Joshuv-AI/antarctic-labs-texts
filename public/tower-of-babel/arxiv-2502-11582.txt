Curves on Compact Arithmetic Quotients of Hyperbolic 2-ball
Zhehao Li

Abstract




1.
Introduction

Complex hyperbolic
n
-ball
\mathbb{B}^{n}
is a higher dimensional analogue of the unit 1-ball
\mathbb{B}^{1}
in hyperbolic geometry. It is the domain
\mathbb{B}^{n}=\{z\in\mathbb{C}^{n}:|z|<1\}
, or equivalently
\mathbb{P}V_{-}
the projectivization of the negative cone
V_{-}=\{u\in V:h(u,u)<0\}
for
V\cong\mathbb{C}^{n+1}
equipped with a hermitian form
h
with signature
(n,1)
. In this paper we study the following compact arithmetic quotient of
\mathbb{B}^{2}
. We start from
K=\mathbb{Q}(\sqrt{D})
a quadratic field with discriminant
D>0
and a CM extension
F=K(\sqrt{\alpha})
for some totally negative
\alpha\in K
. We have 2 pairs of embeddings of
F\to\mathbb{C}
:
\sigma_{1},\bar{\sigma}_{1},\sigma_{2},\bar{\sigma}_{2}
. Consider
(V,h)
where
V\cong F^{\oplus 3}
is an
F
-vector space equipped with a hermitian form
h
with signature
(2,1)
under the first pair of embeddings and definite under the other pair of embeddings. Then the special unitary group
\Gamma:=SU(h^{\sigma_{1}},\mathcal{O}_{F})
that preserves
h
and has entries in
\mathcal{O}_{F}
gives an arithmetic lattice, and its action on
(F\otimes\mathbb{C},h^{\sigma_{1}})
induces a compact arithmetic quotient of
\mathbb{B}^{2}
. Denote
D^{\prime}
to be the norm of the relative discriminant of the CM extension
F/K
. We proved the following.

Theorem 1.1
.

For any genus
g
, there exists
D_{0}(g)>0
and
D^{\prime}_{0}>0
such that for
D>D_{0}(g)
and
D^{\prime}>D^{\prime}_{0}
, the corresponding
\Gamma\backslash\mathbb{B}^{2}
defined as above has no complex curves of genus
g
.

This also has a moduli interpretation in terms of abelian sixfolds with
\mathcal{O}_{F}
-endomorphism. See Section
5.2
for detail. We say that a skew-Hermitian form
T
over
\mathbb{C}
has signature
(m,n)
if the Hermitian form
i^{-1}T
has signature
(m,n)
.

Theorem 1.2
.

Fix a skew-Hermitian form
T
over
F
such that
T
is of signature
(2,1)
under a pair of complex embeddings of
F
and is definite under the other pair. For any complex curve
C
of genus
g
, consider abelian schemes
f:\mathcal{A}\to C
of dimension 6 with
\mathcal{O}_{F}
-endomorphism of type (
\mathcal{O}_{F}^{3}
,
T
). Then there exists
D_{0}(g)>0
and
D^{\prime}_{0}>0
such that for
D>D_{0}(g)
and
D^{\prime}>D^{\prime}_{0}
, such type of abelian schemes must be isotrivial.

The proof uses the method of volume estimates, which has been used in, for example,
[
HT02
,
HT12
,
BT13
,
BT18
,
BT19
]
to prove interesting results in algebraic geometry. In contrast to existing literature, we use it to understand the geometry of quotient singularities. Compared to similar objects such as Hilbert modular surfaces, notable new phenomena for arithmetic quotient of 2-balls include the existence of 1-dimensional quotient singularities given by some special curves, and non-cyclic quotient singularities at the intersection of these curves. This requires a volume estimate to give a bound on the intersection number between these stacky special curves and any other complex curves. Compared to the volume estimates in
[
Mem23
]
, our work focuses on the geometry near the quotient singularities instead of the boundaries.

2.
Background

2.1.
Complex Hyperbolic 2-ball

In this section we define complex hyperbolic
n
-balls and summarize useful facts about the
n=2
case. See
[
Gol99
]
or
[
Par03
]
for detail.

2.1.1.
The projective model

The unit ball in
\mathbb{C}^{n}
and the Bergman metric, which is a natural metric with constant negative holomorphic sectional curvature, give a model for complex hyperbolic
n
-space
\mathbb{B}^{n}
. When
n=1
, this is the usual Poincaré unit disc model of the hyperbolic plane. We now give a brief review of
\mathbb{B}^{n}
, especially the case where
n=2
.

A Hermitian form on
\mathbb{C}^{n}
is a
\mathbb{C}
-valued
\mathbb{R}
-bilinear form
h:\mathbb{C}^{n}\times\mathbb{C}^{n}\to\mathbb{C}
such that for any fixed
w\in\mathbb{C}^{n}
,
v\mapsto h(v,w)
is
\mathbb{C}
-linear, and for any
v,w\in\mathbb{C}^{n}
,
h(v,w)=\overline{h(w,v)}
. Alternatively, if we fix a basis of
\mathbb{C}^{n}
, so
v,w
can be considered as column vectors, then a Hermitian form
h
is given by
(v,w)\mapsto v^{H}J_{h}w
for some Hermitian matrix
J_{h}
. Here the superscript
H
means the Hermitian conjugate, i.e. the transpose of the element-wise complex conjugation of the matrix, and thus a matrix
J
is Hermitian if
J=J^{H}
.

Let
V
be
\mathbb{C}^{n+1}
equipped with a Hermitian form
\langle\cdot,\cdot\rangle
of signature
(n,1)
. One example is the standard Hermitian form
h_{std}(u,v)=u_{1}\bar{v}_{1}+\cdots+u_{n}\bar{v}_{n}-u_{n+1}\bar{v}_{n+1}
. Since
\langle u,u\rangle
is real for any
u\in V
, we can define subsets:

negative

\displaystyle V_{-}

\displaystyle=\{u\in V:\langle u,u\rangle<0\}

null

\displaystyle V_{0}

\displaystyle=\{u\in V\backslash\{0\}:\langle u,u\rangle=0\}

positive

\displaystyle V_{+}

\displaystyle=\{u\in V:\langle u,u\rangle>0\}

and for
u\in V
we denote
u<0
(resp.
u>0
) if
u\in V_{-}
(resp.
V_{+}
). Since
\langle\lambda u,\lambda u\rangle=|\lambda|^{2}\langle u,u\rangle
for any scalar
\lambda\in\mathbb{C}^{*}
and any
u\in V
, the projectivization map
\mathbb{P}:\mathbb{C}^{n+1}\to\mathbb{P}_{\mathbb{C}}^{n}
preserves the sign of
\langle u,u\rangle
and thus
\mathbb{P}V_{-}
,
\mathbb{P}V_{0}
, and
\mathbb{P}V_{+}
are well-defined. The projective model of complex hyperbolic
n
-space
\mathbb{B}^{n}
is defined to be the negative lines in
V
, i.e.
\mathbb{B}^{n}:=\mathbb{P}V_{-}
. Thus, its boundary
\partial\mathbb{B}^{n}=\mathbb{P}V_{0}
.

We can define a natural metric on
\mathbb{B}^{n}
called the Bergman metric. First we denote the “tance” function for any
u,v\in V_{-}\cup V_{+}
:

\mathrm{ta}(u,v):=\frac{\langle u,v\rangle\langle v,u\rangle}{\langle u,u\rangle\langle v,v\rangle}\in\mathbb{R}

It has the following useful properties:

(i)

\mathrm{ta}(u,v)=\mathrm{ta}(v,u)
for any
u,v\in V_{-}\cup V_{+}
;

(ii)

\mathrm{ta}(\lambda u,v)=\mathrm{ta}(u,v)
for any
\lambda\in\mathbb{C}^{*}
and
u,v\in V_{-}\cup V_{+}
, so
\mathrm{ta}(\cdot,\cdot)
is well-defined on
\mathbb{P}V_{-}\cup\mathbb{P}V_{+}
;

(iii)

\mathrm{ta}(u,v)\geq 1
if
u,v\in V_{-}
;
\mathrm{ta}(u,v)\geq 0
if
u,v\in V_{+}
;
\mathrm{ta}(u,v)\leq 0
if
u\in V_{+}
and
v\in V_{-}
.

For the proof, only
\mathrm{ta}(u,v)\geq 1
for
u,v\in V_{-}
is not trivial. It can be explicitly proved in the case of the standard Hermitian form and then extended to the general case by Cayley transforms which will be defined below. Now the distance function
d(\cdot,\cdot)
on
\mathbb{B}^{n}
given by the Bergman metric is defined to be

\cosh^{2}\left(\frac{d(u,v)}{2}\right):=\mathrm{ta}(u,v):=\frac{\langle u,v\rangle\langle v,u\rangle}{\langle u,u\rangle\langle v,v\rangle}

for
u,v\in\mathbb{B}^{n}
, or alternatively,

ds^{2}=\frac{-4}{\langle u,u\rangle^{2}}\det\begin{pmatrix}\langle u,u\rangle&\langle u,du\rangle\\
\langle du,u\rangle&\langle du,du\rangle\end{pmatrix}.

The
-4
normalizes its holomorphic sectional curvature to be
-1
.

The Bergman metric is invariant to the choice of the Hermitian form in the sense that for different choices of Hermitian forms, the complex hyperbolic
n
-spaces are isometric. More specifically, we have the standard Hermitian form
h_{std}(u,v)=u_{1}\bar{v}_{1}+\cdots+u_{n}\bar{v}_{n}-u_{n+1}\bar{v}_{n+1}
corresponding to the diagonal matrix
J_{std}
with diagonal entries
1,\cdots,1,-1
. Then consider
J
any Hermitian matrix of signature
(n,1)
, so
J
can be unitarily diagonalized with real eigenvalues, i.e. we can find a matrix
T\in\mathrm{GL}_{n+1}(\mathbb{C})
such that
J=T^{H}J_{std}T
. Then we have an isometry called Cayley transform
(\mathbb{C}^{n+1},J)\to(\mathbb{C}^{n+1},J_{std}),\ \ u\mapsto Tu
. When we consider
h_{std}(u,v)=u_{1}\bar{v}_{1}+\cdots+u_{n}\bar{v}_{n}-u_{n+1}\bar{v}_{n+1}
, any point
[u_{1},\cdots,u_{n+1}]\in\mathbb{P}V_{-}
corresponds to a point
(u_{1}/u_{n+1},\cdots,u_{n}/u_{n+1})
inside the open unit ball in
\mathbb{C}^{n}
, so we also call
\mathbb{B}^{n}
the complex hyperbolic
n
-ball.

2.1.2.
Isometries

Consider
V\cong\mathbb{C}^{n+1}
equipped with a Hermitian form
h(\cdot,\cdot)=\langle\cdot,\cdot\rangle
of signature
(n,1)
. If we fix a basis of
V
, the Hermitian form is represented by a Hermitian matrix
J
, and linear transformations
M
on
V
preserving the Hermitian form satisfy:

\langle Mu,Mv\rangle=v^{H}M^{H}JMu=\langle u,v\rangle=v^{H}Ju.

for any
u,v\in V
, so it is equivalent to requiring
M^{H}JM=J
. Thus they define the unitary group:

U(J):=\{M\in M_{n+1}(\mathbb{C}):\langle Mu,Mv\rangle=\langle u,v\rangle\ \ \forall u,v\in V\}\cong U(n,1).

The last isomorphism is induced by Cayley transform
T
:
U(J)\to U(J_{std})=:U(n,1),\ \ M\mapsto TMT^{-1}
. Since
\det(M^{H}JM)=\det(J)
, we also have
|\det(M)|=1
. Since scalar multiplication
\lambda I_{n+1}
for any
\lambda\in\mathbb{C}^{*}
commutes with any
M\in U(J)
,
U(J)
is well-defined on
\mathbb{P}V
, and the kernel of this action on
\mathbb{P}V
is
\{\lambda I:|\lambda|=1\}\cong U(1)\subset U(J)
. Then, we define the projective unitary group

\mathbb{P}U(J):=U(J)/\{\lambda\cdot id:|\lambda|=1\}=SU(J)/\{\lambda\cdot id\in SU(J):|\lambda|=1\}

whose element always has a representative with determinant 1 in
SU(J)
. Indeed,
\mathbb{B}^{n}
is homogeneous:

Lemma 2.1

(Lemma 3.1.3 of
[
Gol99
]
)
.

\mathbb{P}U(J)
acts transitively on
\mathbb{B}^{n}
.

Then we focus on the case
n=2
and list some results about the classification of isometries on
\mathbb{B}^{2}
. In the literature they are mostly proved for the case where
J=J_{std}
, but the statements also apply to the general case since they do not depend on the choice of basis.

To state the classification, we first have the following trichotomy:

Definition 2.2
.

A holomorphic complex hyperbolic isometry is

(i)

loxodromic
if it fixes exactly two points of
\partial\mathbb{B}^{2}
;

(ii)

parabolic
if it fixes exactly one point of
\partial\mathbb{B}^{2}
;

(iii)

elliptic
if it fixes at least one point of
\mathbb{B}^{2}
.

For
M\in SU(J)
, we have the following restriction on its characteristic polynomial, which is a consequence of
M^{H}JM=J
.

Lemma 2.3

(Lemma 6.2.5 of
[
Gol99
]
)
.

If
M\in SU(J)
and
\lambda_{0}
is an eigenvalue of
M
, then
\bar{\lambda}_{0}^{-1}
is also an eigenvalue of
M
. Therefore, if we denote
t:=\mathrm{tr}(M)
, then the characteristic polynomial of
M
is
\lambda^{3}-t\lambda^{2}+\bar{t}\lambda-1
. ∎

Now we can state the classification of isometries.

Theorem 2.4

(Theorem 6.2.4 of
[
Gol99
]
)
.

Consider
M\in SU(J)
.

(i)

M
is loxodromic if and only if one of the eigenvalues
\lambda
has
|\lambda|\neq 1
. In this case, if we denote
\lambda=re^{i\theta}
for
r,\theta\in\mathbb{R}
, then the three eigenvalues are
re^{i\theta}
,
r^{-1}e^{i\theta}
,
e^{-2i\theta}
. If they have eigenvectors
u,v,w
respectively, then

\langle u,u\rangle=\langle v,v\rangle=\langle u,w\rangle=\langle v,w\rangle=0,\ \ \langle w,w\rangle>0,\ \ \langle u,v\rangle\neq 0.

(ii)

M
is parabolic if and only if it has a repeated eigenvalue whose eigenspace is spanned by one null vector. It is called pure parabolic (resp. screw parabolic) if the dimension of this eigenspace is 3 (resp. 2).

(iii)

M
is elliptic if and only if it can diagonalized and all the eigenvalues have norm 1. We have the following cases:

(a)

M
only has one fixed point in
\mathbb{B}^{2}
. In this case, let the fixed point be given by a negative eigenvector
u
with eigenvalue
\lambda_{1}
. Then either we have a repeated eigenvalue
\lambda_{2}=\lambda_{3}\neq\lambda_{1}
corresponding to an eigenspace spanned by two positive vectors, or all eigenvalues
\lambda_{1},\lambda_{2},\lambda_{3}
are distinct and each of
\lambda_{2},\lambda_{3}
corresponds to a positive eigenspace.
M
is called a complex reflection about a point in the first case, and is regular elliptic in the second case.

(b)

M
is called a complex reflection about a line or boundary elliptic when the fixed points in
\mathbb{B}^{2}
form a complex line. In this case, this line corresponds to a 2-dimensional eigenspace spanned by both positive and negative vectors. In other words, we can find eigenvectors
u,v,w
corresponding to eigenvalues
\lambda_{1}=\lambda_{2}\neq\lambda_{3}
with
u<0
and
v,w>0
.

(c)

The last case is where
M
corresponds to scalar multiplication by a third root of unity.

By Lemma
2.3
, the trace of an isometry determines the characteristic polynomial, so we have the following by computation of the resultant.

Theorem 2.5

(Theorem 6.2.4 of
[
Gol99
]
)
.

Let
f(\tau)=|\tau|^{4}-8\mathrm{Re\,}(\tau^{3})+18|\tau|^{2}-27
. Let
M\in SU(J)
. Then

(i)

M
is loxodromic if and only if
f(\mathrm{tr}(M))>0
;

(ii)

M
has distinct eigenvalues if and only if
f(\mathrm{tr}(M))<0
;

(iii)

M
has repeated eigenvalues if and only if
f(\mathrm{tr}(M))=0
.

When
M\in SU(J)
is diagonalizable, we can pick some eigenvectors as a basis of
\mathbb{C}^{3}
. Using the orthogonal relations above we have the following useful identities.

Corollary 2.6
.

Consider
M\in SU(J)
.

(i)

When
M
is loxodromic, denote the three eigenvalues to be
re^{i\theta}
,
r^{-1}e^{i\theta}
,
e^{-2i\theta}
and their respective eigenvectors
v_{1},v_{2},v_{3}
. Then for any
u\in(\mathbb{C}^{3},J)
,

(2.7)

u=\frac{\langle u,v_{2}\rangle}{\langle v_{1},v_{2}\rangle}v_{1}+\frac{\langle u,v_{1}\rangle}{\langle v_{2},v_{1}\rangle}v_{2}+\frac{\langle u,v_{3}\rangle}{\langle v_{3},v_{3}\rangle}v_{3}.

and thus

(2.8)

1=\frac{\langle u,v_{2}\rangle\langle v_{1},u\rangle}{\langle v_{1},v_{2}\rangle\langle u,u\rangle}+\frac{\langle u,v_{1}\rangle\langle v_{2},u\rangle}{\langle v_{2},v_{1}\rangle\langle u,u\rangle}+\frac{\langle u,v_{3}\rangle\langle v_{3},u\rangle}{\langle v_{3},v_{3}\rangle\langle u,u\rangle}=:w+\bar{w}+\mathrm{ta}(u,v_{3})

if we denote
w:=\frac{\langle u,v_{2}\rangle\langle v_{1},u\rangle}{\langle v_{1},v_{2}\rangle\langle u,u\rangle}
.

(ii)

When
M
is regular elliptic or a complex reflection about a point (resp. boundary elliptic), we can find eigenvectors
v_{1},v_{2},v_{3}
corresponding to eigenvalues
\lambda_{1},\lambda_{2},\lambda_{3}
respectively such that
\lambda_{1}\neq\lambda_{2}
and
\lambda_{1}\neq\lambda_{3}
(resp.
\lambda_{1}=\lambda_{2}\neq\lambda_{3}
), and

\langle v_{1},v_{1}\rangle<0,\ \ \langle v_{2},v_{2}\rangle,\langle v_{3},v_{3}\rangle>0,\ \ \langle v_{1},v_{2}\rangle=\langle v_{1},v_{3}\rangle=\langle v_{2},v_{3}\rangle=0

and consequently, for any
u\in(\mathbb{C}^{3},J)
,

(2.9)

u=\frac{\langle u,v_{1}\rangle}{\langle v_{1},v_{1}\rangle}v_{1}+\frac{\langle u,v_{2}\rangle}{\langle v_{2},v_{2}\rangle}v_{2}+\frac{\langle u,v_{3}\rangle}{\langle v_{3},v_{3}\rangle}v_{3}.

and thus

(2.10)

1=\frac{\langle u,v_{1}\rangle\langle v_{1},u\rangle}{\langle v_{1},v_{1}\rangle\langle u,u\rangle}+\frac{\langle u,v_{2}\rangle\langle v_{2},u\rangle}{\langle v_{2},v_{2}\rangle\langle u,u\rangle}+\frac{\langle u,v_{3}\rangle\langle v_{3},u\rangle}{\langle v_{3},v_{3}\rangle\langle u,u\rangle}=:\sum^{3}_{j=1}\mathrm{ta}(u,v_{j})

2.1.3.
Complex Lines and Distance Formulas

One can classify the totally geodesic subspaces of
\mathbb{B}^{n}
using the general theory of symmetric spaces:

Theorem 2.11

(Section 3.1.11 of
[
Gol99
]
)
.

The only totally geodesic subspaces of
\mathbb{B}^{n}
are either complex linear subspaces or totally real totally geodesic submanifolds.

When
n=2
, they are complex lines and totally real Lagrangian planes. Complex lines are the usual complex lines in
\mathbb{C}^{2}
that intersect the open unit 2-ball when we consider
\mathbb{B}^{2}
as the open unit 2-ball. Equivalently, a complex line
L\subset\mathbb{B}^{2}
is the projectivization of the intersection between a 2-dimensional linear subspace of
V=(\mathbb{C}^{3},J)
and the negative cone
V_{-}
. Therefore, it can be determined by its polar vector:
L=\{z\in\mathbb{B}^{2}:\langle z,n\rangle=0\}
for some
n\in V_{+}
. Totally real Lagrangian planes are embedded copies of the real hyperbolic space
\mathbb{H}^{2}_{\mathbb{R}}
. When we consider the standard Hermitian form, they are the images under a
\mathbb{P}U(J_{std})
-action of the space given by those points in
\mathbb{B}^{2}
with real coordinates.

Then we list some distance formulas related to complex lines.

Proposition 2.12

(Corollary 6.7 of
[
Par03
]
)
.

Let
L
be a complex line with polar vector
n
. For any
z\in\mathbb{B}^{2}
, the distance
d(z,L)
from
z
to
L
is given by

\cosh^{2}\left(\frac{d(z,L)}{2}\right)=1-\mathrm{ta}(z,n)\geq 1.

Proposition 2.13

(Section 3.3.2 of
[
Gol99
]
or Proposition 6.8 of
[
Par03
]
)
.

Let
L_{1},L_{2}
be complex lines with polar vector
n_{1},n_{2}
. Then

(i)

If
\mathrm{ta}(n_{1},n_{2})>1
, then
L_{1}
and
L_{2}
do not intersect in
\mathbb{B}^{2}\cup\partial\mathbb{B}^{2}
(which is called ultraparallel) and

\cosh^{2}\left(\frac{d(L_{1},L_{2})}{2}\right)=\mathrm{ta}(n_{1},n_{2})>1.

(ii)

If
\mathrm{ta}(n_{1},n_{2})=1
, then
L_{1}
and
L_{2}
intersect on
\partial\mathbb{B}^{2}
(which is called asymptotic) or coincide.

(iii)

If
\mathrm{ta}(n_{1},n_{2})<1
, then
L_{1}
and
L_{2}
intersect in
\mathbb{B}^{2}
.

2.2.
Arithmetic Quotient of Type I

Let
K
be a totally real number field of degree
m
. Consider a CM extension
F:=K(\sqrt{\alpha})
for some totally negative
\alpha\in K
. The embeddings come in complex conjugate pairs:
\sigma_{1},\bar{\sigma}_{1},\cdots,\sigma_{m},\bar{\sigma}_{m}
. Then, take a Hermitian form
h(\cdot,\cdot)=\langle\cdot,\cdot\rangle
in
n+1
variables with coefficients in
F
, i.e.

\langle z,w\rangle:=w^{H}Jz,\ \ z,w\in F^{n+1}

for some Hermitian matrix
J=J^{H}\in M_{n+1}(F)
, such that for the first pair of embeddings
(\sigma_{1},\bar{\sigma}_{1})
,
h^{\sigma_{1}}
and
h^{\bar{\sigma}_{1}}
are of signature
(n,1)
, and for all other pairs of embeddings
(\sigma,\bar{\sigma})=(\sigma_{2},\bar{\sigma}_{2}),\cdots,(\sigma_{m},\bar{\sigma}_{m})
,
h^{\sigma}
and
h^{\bar{\sigma}}
are definite. Then under the first pair of embeddings, these define an arithmetic lattice
\Gamma:=SU(h^{\sigma_{1}},\mathcal{O}_{F}):=SU(h^{\sigma_{1}})\cap SL_{3}(\mathcal{O}_{F})
and the Shimura variety
X:=\mathbb{B}^{n}/\Gamma
. We will focus on the case where
K=\mathbb{Q}(\sqrt{D})
is a real quadratic field with discriminant
D>0
. We identify
F
with
\sigma_{1}(F)
.

The lattices constructed in this way are cocompact since
SU(n+1)
is compact and then we can apply
[
McR11
, Theorem 5.2]
or
[
MR13
, Lemma 8.1.3]
. Therefore, by Godement’s compactness criterion,
\Gamma
has no nontrivial unipotent elements. Furthermore, this implies that there is no parabolic element in
\Gamma
by Theorem
2.5
.

3.
Geometry of Elliptic Points

Now we will focus on the situation in the main theorem. We fix the setting and notation as follows:

K:=\mathbb{Q}(\sqrt{D})
with discriminant
D>0
.
F:=K(\sqrt{\alpha})
a CM extension with the norm of the relative discriminant
D_{2}>D^{\prime}_{0}
for some fixed
D^{\prime}_{0}
such that there is no non-totally real integer
z\in\mathcal{O}_{F}
with
|z|\leq 3
in all embeddings. Denote its complex embeddings
\sigma_{1},\bar{\sigma}_{1},\sigma_{2},\bar{\sigma}_{2}
. Take a Hermitian form
h(\cdot,\cdot)=\langle\cdot,\cdot\rangle
represented by a matrix
J
on
F^{3}
with coefficients in
F
such that
h^{\sigma_{1}}
and
h^{\bar{\sigma}_{1}}
are of signature
(2,1)
, and
h^{\sigma_{2}}
and
h^{\bar{\sigma}_{2}}
are definite. Consider the arithmetic lattice
\Gamma:=SU(h^{\sigma_{1}},\mathcal{O}_{F}):=SU(h^{\sigma_{1}})\cap SL_{3}(\mathcal{O}_{F})
and the arithmetic quotient
X:=\Gamma\backslash\mathbb{B}^{2}
.

(3.1)

3.1.
Repulsion of Elliptic Points

This section is dedicated to show repulsion of elliptic points, for example, isolated elliptic points tend to be far apart when the discriminant
D
of the real quadratic field defining the space is big. Such repulsion phenomena for Shimura varieties have been shown in certain cases in terms of level structures. For instance,
[
BT16
]
showed the repulsion of cusps and special subvarieties for products of modular curves in terms of the level structure, and
[
BT18
]
proved similar results for cusps and points of Hilbert modular varieties.

Note that in this section we are showing the repulsion of the preimages of elliptic points of
X
in
\mathbb{B}^{2}
, i.e. the points in
\mathbb{B}^{2}
stabilized by some elements in
\Gamma
. The repulsion can descend to
X
since the action of
\Gamma
is properly discontinuous, and it is useful when we derive the multiplicity bounds in Section
4
.

Now, we call points (resp. complex lines) in
\mathbb{B}^{2}
(or their images in
X
) stabilized by an elliptic element inside the arithmetic lattice
elliptic points
(resp.
elliptic complex lines
), and an elliptic point
u
is an
isolated elliptic point
if for some elliptic element
M
inside its stabilizer group,
u
is an isolated point in the locus that
M
stabilizes. The precise statement of repulsion of isolated elliptic points is the following.

Proposition 3.2
.

Follow the notation in (
3
). For any given
R>0
, there exists
D_{0}(R)>0
such that when
D>D_{0}(R)
, if
u
and
v
are distinct isolated elliptic points on
\mathbb{B}^{2}
, then their distance
d_{\mathbb{B}^{2}}(u,v)>R
.

We first assume that
u
and
v
are stabilized by
M_{1}
and
M_{2}
in
\Gamma
respectively, where
M_{1}
and
M_{2}
have the same set of eigenvalues. First we identify possible orders of elliptic elements in this context.

Lemma 3.3
.

For
K:=\mathbb{Q}(\sqrt{D})
with discriminant
D>0
and
F:=K(\sqrt{\alpha})
a CM extension, the maximal possible order of elliptic elements is
18
if
D>21
. If we further assume that
F\neq K(\sqrt{\alpha})
for
\alpha=-1,-2,-3,-7
, then the only possible triples of eigenvalues are
(1,-1,-1),(1,\omega,\omega^{2}),(1,i,-i),(1,-\omega,-\omega^{2})
.

Proof.

Since
\Gamma
acts properly discontinuously, the order
n
of an elliptic element
M\in\Gamma
is finite, and the eigenvalues
\{\lambda_{1},\lambda_{2},\lambda_{3}\}
of
M
are n-th roots of unity. Therefore, the splitting field
F(\{\lambda_{j}\}_{j})
of the characteristic polynomial of
M
over
F
should include the n-th cyclotomic field, but the possible values of
[F(\{\lambda_{j}\}_{j}):\mathbb{Q}]
are 4,8,12, so
n
can only be a divisor of them and now there are only finitely many cases. When
[F(\{\lambda_{j}\}_{j}):\mathbb{Q}]
is 8 or 12,
F(\{\lambda_{j}\}_{j})=\mathbb{Q}(\zeta_{n})
must contain
K:=\mathbb{Q}(\sqrt{D})
, which is impossible when
D>21
(
n=21
is the maximal odd integer with
[\mathbb{Q}(\zeta_{n}):\mathbb{Q}]\leq 12
). Assuming
[\mathbb{Q}(\zeta_{n}):\mathbb{Q}]
is 4 or 6 and
D>9
,
\mathbb{Q}(\zeta_{n})
adjoining
\sqrt{D}
will be a nontrivial field extension and it must give
F(\{\lambda_{j}\}_{j})
, so
\mathbb{Q}(\zeta_{n})
must contain
\sqrt{\alpha}
. Now we can check each case:
n
cannot be 5 or 10 as
\mathbb{Q}(\zeta_{5})
contains no imaginary quadratic field;
n
can be 8 only when
F=K(\sqrt{-2})
or
K(i)
;
n
can be 12 only when
F=K(\sqrt{-3})orK(i)
;
n
can be 7 or 14 only when
F=K(\sqrt{-7})
;
n
can be 9 or 18 only when
F=K(\sqrt{-3})
.

Thus, when
F\neq K(\sqrt{\alpha})
for
\alpha=-1,-2,-3,-7
, the only possible values of
n
are 2,3,4,6. Applying
\lambda_{1}\lambda_{2}\lambda_{3}=1
and
\lambda_{1}+\lambda_{2}+\lambda_{3}\in\mathcal{O}_{F}
, we finish the classification of possible triples of eigenvalues.
∎

From
2.1.2
, we know that when the triple of eigenvalues is
(1,-1,-1)
and there is a negative eigenvector corresponds to eigenvalue
-1
, the elliptic element will stabilize a complex line given by the
(-1)
-eigenspace. Except this case, the other types of elliptic elements correspond to isolated elliptic points.

Come back to the setting of Proposition
3.2
. By Theorem
2.4
, we can let
\{u_{j}\}_{j=1,2,3}\in F(\{\lambda_{j}\}_{j=1,2,3})
(resp.
\{v_{j}\}_{j=1,2,3}
) be the pairwise orthogonal eigenvectors of the elliptic element
M_{1}
(resp.
M_{2}
), and
\{\lambda_{j}\}_{j=1,2,3}
(resp.
\{\tilde{\lambda}_{j}\}_{j=1,2,3}
) are the corresponding eigenvalues. The arithmetic of the Hermitian form gives the following.

Lemma 3.4
.

Follow the notation in (
3
). For any given
R>0
, there exists
D_{0}(R)>0
such that when
D>D_{0}(R)
,
|\mathrm{tr}(M_{1}M_{2})|^{2}
and
2\mathrm{Re\,}(\mathrm{tr}(M_{1}M_{2}))
are rational integers, and
\mathrm{Re\,}(\mathrm{tr}(M_{1}M_{2}))\leq|\mathrm{tr}(M_{1}M_{2})|\leq 3
.

Proof.

Since
\{u_{j}\}_{j=1,2,3}
(resp.
\{v_{j}\}_{j=1,2,3}
) give a pairwise orthogonal basis, we can apply (
2.7
) to
v_{k}
(resp.
u_{k}
) to get the following useful equation for any fixed
k\in\{1,2,3\}
:

(3.5)

\sum_{j=1}^{3}\mathrm{ta}(v_{k},u_{j})=\sum_{j=1}^{3}\frac{\langle v_{k},u_{j}\rangle\langle u_{j},v_{k}\rangle}{\langle u_{j},u_{j}\rangle\langle v_{k},v_{k}\rangle}=1\ \ \ \left(resp.\sum_{j=1}^{3}\mathrm{ta}(u_{k},v_{j})=\sum_{j=1}^{3}\frac{\langle u_{k},v_{j}\rangle\langle v_{j},u_{k}\rangle}{\langle v_{j},v_{j}\rangle\langle u_{k},u_{k}\rangle}=1\right).

To compute
\mathrm{tr}(M_{1}M_{2})
, first note that the orthogonal relations give the following:

\displaystyle\left(u_{1}\ u_{2}\ u_{3}\right)^{H}J\left(u_{1}\ u_{2}\ u_{3}\right)

\displaystyle=\begin{pmatrix}\langle u_{1},u_{1}\rangle&&\\
&\langle u_{2},u_{2}\rangle&\\
&&\langle u_{3},u_{3}\rangle\\
\end{pmatrix}

\displaystyle\implies\left(u_{1}\ u_{2}\ u_{3}\right)^{-1}

\displaystyle=\begin{pmatrix}1/\langle u_{1},u_{1}\rangle&&\\
&1/\langle u_{2},u_{2}\rangle&\\
&&1/\langle u_{3},u_{3}\rangle\\
\end{pmatrix}\left(u_{1}\ u_{2}\ u_{3}\right)^{H}J

Using this we can rewrite the diagonalized
M_{1}
and
M_{2}
and get:

\displaystyle\mathrm{tr}(M_{1}M_{2})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\mathrm{tr}\left(\left(u_{1}\ u_{2}\ u_{3}\right)\begin{pmatrix}\lambda_{1}&&\\
&\lambda_{2}&\\
&&\lambda_{3}\\
\end{pmatrix}\left(u_{1}\ u_{2}\ u_{3}\right)^{-1}\left(v_{1}\ v_{2}\ v_{3}\right)\begin{pmatrix}\tilde{\lambda}_{1}&&\\
&\tilde{\lambda}_{2}&\\
&&\tilde{\lambda}_{3}\\
\end{pmatrix}\left(u_{1}\ u_{2}\ u_{3}\right)^{-1}\right)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\mathrm{tr}\Bigg(\begin{pmatrix}\frac{\lambda_{1}}{\langle u_{1},u_{1}\rangle}&&\\
&\frac{\lambda_{2}}{\langle u_{2},u_{2}\rangle}&\\
&&\frac{\lambda_{3}}{\langle u_{3},u_{3}\rangle}\\
\end{pmatrix}\left(u_{1}\ u_{2}\ u_{3}\right)^{H}J\left(v_{1}\ v_{2}\ v_{3}\right)

\displaystyle\qquad\begin{pmatrix}\frac{\tilde{\lambda}_{1}}{\langle v_{1},v_{1}\rangle}&&\\
&\frac{\tilde{\lambda}_{2}}{\langle v_{2},v_{2}\rangle}&\\
&&\frac{\tilde{\lambda}_{3}}{\langle v_{3},v_{3}\rangle}\\
\end{pmatrix}\left(v_{1}\ v_{2}\ v_{3}\right)^{H}J\left(u_{1}\ u_{2}\ u_{3}\right)\Bigg)

(3.6)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{j,k=1}^{3}\lambda_{j}\tilde{\lambda}_{k}\mathrm{ta}(u_{j},v_{k})

Until the end of this lemma, let
u_{3}
(resp.
v_{3}
) denote the negative eigenvector, so the other two eigenvectors are positive, and
\mathrm{ta}(u_{3},v_{3})
gives the distance between the elliptic points. This gives:

\displaystyle|\mathrm{tr}(M_{1}M_{2})|

\displaystyle=\left|\sum_{j,k=1}^{3}\lambda_{j}\tilde{\lambda}_{k}\mathrm{ta}(u_{j},v_{k})\right|\leq\sum_{j,k=1}^{3}\left|\mathrm{ta}(u_{j},v_{k})\right|

\displaystyle=\mathrm{ta}(u_{3},v_{3})+\sum_{j,k=1}^{2}\mathrm{ta}(u_{j},v_{k})-\sum_{j=1}^{2}(\mathrm{ta}(u_{j},v_{3})+\mathrm{ta}(u_{3},v_{j}))

\displaystyle=\mathrm{ta}(u_{3},v_{3})+(\mathrm{ta}(u_{3},v_{3})+1)-2(1-\mathrm{ta}(u_{3},v_{3}))

\displaystyle\mathrm{by\ }(\ref{eq:expand})

\displaystyle=4\mathrm{ta}(u_{3},v_{3})-1

In our setting, we can extend the embeddings
F\xhookrightarrow{}\mathbb{C}
to
F(\lambda_{j})\xhookrightarrow{}\mathbb{C}
while fixing the embeddings of the eigenvalues, and we use the same notation for these extended embeddings. Then the argument above fits into the embedding
\sigma_{2}
that gives the definite hermitian form:

\displaystyle|\mathrm{tr}(M_{1}M_{2})^{\sigma_{2}}|

\displaystyle=\left|\sum_{j,k=1}^{3}\lambda_{j}\tilde{\lambda}_{k}\mathrm{ta}^{\sigma_{2}}(u_{j}^{\sigma_{2}},v_{k}^{\sigma_{2}})\right|\leq\sum_{j,k=1}^{3}\left|\mathrm{ta}^{\sigma_{2}}(u_{j}^{\sigma_{2}},v_{k}^{\sigma_{2}})\right|=3

where the last equality is due to (
3.5
).

Since
\mathrm{ta}(u_{3},v_{3})
is bounded by
\cosh^{-1}(R/2)
,
|\mathrm{tr}(M_{1}M_{2})|^{2}
is an integral element in
\mathcal{O}_{\mathbb{Q}(\sqrt{D})}
with both embeddings in
\mathbb{R}
nonnegative and bounded by fixed numbers. When
D
is big, this is possible only when
|\mathrm{tr}(M_{1}M_{2})|^{2}\in\mathbb{Z}
, and thus
|\mathrm{tr}(M_{1}M_{2})|^{2}=|\mathrm{tr}(M_{1}M_{2})^{\sigma_{2}}|^{2}\leq 9
. The same argument applies to
2\mathrm{Re\,}(\mathrm{tr}(M_{1}M_{2}))
.

∎

Further investigation of
\mathrm{tr}(M_{1}M_{2})
will finish the proof.

Proof of Proposition
3.2
.

Since
|\mathrm{tr}(M_{1}M_{2})|^{2}
is a rational integer bounded by 9, when the norm of the relative discriminant of
F/K
is large enough to make sure there is no non-totally real integer
z\in\mathcal{O}_{F}
with
|z|\leq 3
in all embeddings,
\mathrm{tr}(M_{1}M_{2})
will be real. Note that this can be independent from
D
. We first look at the case where
(\lambda_{1},\lambda_{2},\lambda_{3})=(\tilde{\lambda}_{1},\tilde{\lambda}_{2},\tilde{\lambda}_{3})=(\lambda,\bar{\lambda},1)
for
e^{i\theta}=\lambda\not\in\mathbb{R}
. Denote
t=\lambda+\bar{\lambda}
. Since
\mathrm{ta}(u_{j},v_{k})\in\mathbb{R}
for
j,k=1,2,3
,
\mathrm{tr}(M_{1}M_{2})\in\mathbb{R}
means:

\displaystyle\mathrm{Im\,}(\mathrm{tr}(M_{1}M_{2}))

\displaystyle=\sum_{j,k=1}^{3}\mathrm{Im\,}(\lambda_{j}\tilde{\lambda}_{k})\mathrm{ta}(u_{j},v_{k})

\displaystyle=\sin(\theta)(\mathrm{ta}(u_{1},v_{3})+\mathrm{ta}(u_{3},v_{1}))-\sin(\theta)(\mathrm{ta}(u_{2},v_{3})+\mathrm{ta}(u_{3},v_{2}))

\displaystyle\qquad+\sin(2\theta)\mathrm{ta}(u_{1},v_{1})-\sin(2\theta)\mathrm{ta}(u_{2},v_{2})

\displaystyle=\sin(\theta)(\cos(\theta)-1)(\mathrm{ta}(u_{1},v_{1})-\mathrm{ta}(u_{2},v_{2}))=0

Since
\lambda\not\in\mathbb{R}
, this implies that
a:=\mathrm{ta}(u_{1},v_{1})=\mathrm{ta}(u_{2},v_{2})
. Apply the same argument to
\mathrm{Im\,}(\mathrm{tr}(M_{1}^{-1}M_{2}))=0
, we obtain
b:=\mathrm{ta}(u_{1},v_{2})=\mathrm{ta}(u_{2},v_{1})
. Therefore,
\mathrm{ta}(u_{j},v_{3})
and
\mathrm{ta}(u_{3},v_{j})
are all equal to
1-a-b
.

Now we check each case of possible pairs of negative eigenvectors. First,
(u_{1},v_{3})
cannot be both negative, since otherwise
\mathrm{ta}(u_{1},v_{3})=\mathrm{ta}(u_{2},v_{3})=1-a-b
should have opposite signs by definition, but they cannot be 0 as
\mathrm{ta}(u_{1},v_{3})\geq 1
. This also applies to
(u_{2},v_{3})
,
(u_{3},v_{1})
, and
(u_{3},v_{2})
. Then, suppose that
(u_{1},v_{1})
are negative eigenvectors. It means that
a=\mathrm{ta}(u_{1},v_{1})\geq 1
,
b<0
,
\mathrm{ta}(u_{3},v_{3})=2a+2b-1\geq 0
, and
\mathrm{ta}(u_{1},v_{3})=\mathrm{ta}(u_{2},v_{3})=1-a-b
should have opposite signs. Thus,
1-a-b=0
, and we have

\displaystyle\mathrm{tr}(M_{1}^{-1}M_{2})

\displaystyle=\mathrm{ta}(u_{3},v_{3})+\mathrm{ta}(u_{1},v_{1})+\mathrm{ta}(u_{2},v_{2})+\bar{\lambda}^{2}\mathrm{ta}(u_{1},v_{2})+\lambda^{2}\mathrm{ta}(u_{2},v_{1})

\displaystyle=(2a+2b-1)+2a+(\bar{\lambda}^{2}+\lambda^{2})(1-a)

\displaystyle=(t^{2}-1)+(4-t^{2})a

\displaystyle\geq(t^{2}-1)+(4-t^{2})=3

Since
|\mathrm{tr}(M_{1}^{-1}M_{2})|\leq 3
, we have
\mathrm{tr}(M_{1}^{-1}M_{2})=3
and thus
M_{1}^{-1}M_{2}=I
. This argument also applies to
(u_{1},v_{2})
,
(u_{2},v_{1})
, and
(u_{2},v_{2})
. The last case left is when
(u_{3},v_{3})
are negative eigenvectors. Then

\displaystyle\ \ \mathrm{tr}(M_{1}M_{2})+\mathrm{tr}(M_{1}^{-1}M_{2})+\mathrm{tr}(M_{1}M_{2}^{-1})+\mathrm{tr}(M_{1}^{-1}M_{2}^{-1})

\displaystyle=4\mathrm{ta}(u_{3},v_{3})+2t(\mathrm{ta}(u_{1},v_{3}+\mathrm{ta}(u_{2},v_{3})+\mathrm{ta}(u_{3},v_{1})+\mathrm{ta}(u_{3},v_{2}))

\displaystyle\qquad+(\bar{\lambda}^{2}+\lambda^{2}+2)(\mathrm{ta}(u_{1},v_{1})+\mathrm{ta}(u_{2},v_{2})+\mathrm{ta}(u_{1},v_{2})+\mathrm{ta}(u_{2},v_{1}))

\displaystyle\mathrm{by}\ (\ref{eq:trace_sum_of_ta})

\displaystyle=4\mathrm{ta}(u_{3},v_{3})+4t(1-\mathrm{ta}(u_{3},v_{3}))+t^{2}(\mathrm{ta}(u_{3},v_{3})+1)

\displaystyle\mathrm{by}\ (\ref{eq:expand})

\displaystyle=(t-2)^{2}\mathrm{ta}(u_{3},v_{3})+t^{2}+4t

Since
u_{3}
and
v_{3}
correspond to eigenvalue 1,
\mathrm{ta}(u_{3},v_{3})\in F
. Moreover, the computation above shows that
(t-2)^{2}\mathrm{ta}(u_{3},v_{3})\in\mathcal{O}_{F}\cap\mathbb{R}=\mathcal{O}_{K}
and it is bounded in both real embeddings of
K=\mathbb{Q}(\sqrt{D})
, so when
D
is big,
(t-2)^{2}\mathrm{ta}(u_{3},v_{3})\in\mathbb{Z}
. However, this means that
1\leq\mathrm{ta}(u_{3},v_{3})=\mathrm{ta}^{\sigma_{2}}(u_{3}^{\sigma_{2}},v_{3}^{\sigma_{2}})\leq 1
, so
\mathrm{ta}(u_{3},v_{3})=1
.

In conclusion, in all the possible cases, both negative eigenvectors in fact represent the same point.

Now the only case left is the case where
(\lambda_{1},\lambda_{2},\lambda_{3})=(\tilde{\lambda}_{1},\tilde{\lambda}_{2},\tilde{\lambda}_{3})=(-1,-1,1)
and
u_{3},v_{3}<0
, but then

\displaystyle\mathrm{tr}(M_{1}M_{2})

\displaystyle=\mathrm{ta}(u_{3},v_{3})+\sum_{j,k=1}^{2}\mathrm{ta}(u_{j},v_{k})-\sum_{j=1}^{2}(\mathrm{ta}(u_{j},v_{3})+\mathrm{ta}(u_{3},v_{j}))

\displaystyle=\mathrm{ta}(u_{3},v_{3})+(\mathrm{ta}(u_{3},v_{3})+1)-2(1-\mathrm{ta}(u_{3},v_{3}))

(3.7)

\displaystyle=4\mathrm{ta}(u_{3},v_{3})-1

\displaystyle\geq 4-1=3

so
|\mathrm{tr}(M_{1}M_{2})|\leq 3
gives that
M_{1}M_{2}=I
.

Now we have proved the case where the respective stabilizers
M_{1}
and
M_{2}
for
u
and
v
have the same set of eigenvalues. For the general case, we first obtain the bound
D_{0}(R)
of
D
such that the distance between isolated elliptic points with conjugate stabilizers has a lower bound
2R
. Then for isolated elliptic points
u
and
v
with stabilizers
M_{1}
and
M_{2}
, since
2R<d(v,M_{1}v)\leq d(v,u)+d(u,M_{1}v)=2d(u,v)
, we have
d(u,v)>R
.

∎

The proposition above only applies to isolated elliptic points. For distinct elliptic elements which stabilize complex lines, it is possible that these two stacky lines intersect. This happens in one of the simplest examples: consider
J=diag(1,1,-\sqrt{D})
and elliptic elements
M_{1}=diag(1,-1,-1)
and
M_{2}=diag(-1,1,-1)
representing flipping over the two coordinate axes, and then the loci stabilized by them intersect at the origin
[0,0,1]
. This example also shows that the isotropy groups are not necessarily cyclic in this context. However, we still have the following repulsion result in this case.

Proposition 3.8
.

Follow the notation in (
3
). For any given
R>0
, there exists
D_{0}(R)>0
such that when
D>D_{0}(R)
, if
M_{1}
and
M_{2}
are elliptic elements with eigenvalues
(-1,-1,1)
stabilizing distinct complex lines, then either their distance
d_{\mathbb{B}^{2}}(L_{1},L_{2})>R
, or they intersect at an isolated elliptic point.

Proof.

Denote
L_{1},L_{2}
the complex lines stabilized by
M_{1},M_{2}
respectively. We can assume that
(\lambda_{1},\lambda_{2},\lambda_{3})=(\tilde{\lambda}_{1},\tilde{\lambda}_{2},\tilde{\lambda}_{3})=(-1,-1,1)
and
u_{1},v_{1}<0
.

If
L_{1}\cap L_{2}\neq\emptyset
, then we can pick
u_{1}=v_{1}
to be the intersection point. Note that
M_{1}M_{2}
should stabilize
u_{1}
, and the corresponding eigenvalue is
(-1)^{2}=1
. Thus,
M_{1}M_{2}
is either the identity which means
M_{1}=M_{2}^{-1}=M_{2}
, or an elliptic element stabilizing an isolated elliptic point.

If
L_{1}\cap L_{2}=\emptyset
, then since the space is compact, they cannot be asymptotic and thus
\mathrm{ta}(u_{3},v_{3})>1
. However,
\mathrm{ta}(u_{3},v_{3})^{\sigma_{2}}\leq 1
, so
\mathrm{ta}(u_{3},v_{3})\not\in\mathbb{Q}
. From (
3.7
) and Lemma
3.4
we see that
8\mathrm{ta}(u_{3},v_{3})=2\mathrm{tr}(M_{1}M_{2})+2\in\mathcal{O}_{F}\cap\mathbb{R}=\mathcal{O}_{K}
. Thus,
8\mathrm{ta}(u_{3},v_{3})\in\mathcal{O}_{K}\backslash\mathbb{Z}
. Then if we pick
D_{0}
with
\sqrt{D_{0}}\geq 8\cosh^{2}(R/2)
, then

8\cosh^{2}\left(\frac{d(L_{1},L_{2})}{2}\right)=8\mathrm{ta}(u_{3},v_{3})>8\mathrm{ta}(u_{3},v_{3})-8\mathrm{ta}(u_{3},v_{3})^{\sigma_{2}}\geq\sqrt{D}>8\cosh^{2}\left(\frac{R}{2}\right).

∎

As a corollary, the proofs of Proposition
3.2
and
3.8
above give strong restrictions on possible stabilizer groups:

Corollary 3.9
.

Follow the notation in (
3
). Then for any given
R>0
, there exists
D_{0}(R)>0
such that when
D>D_{0}(R)
, the order of possible stabilizer groups of an isolated elliptic point is no more than
48
.

Proof.

We first summarize what we got in the proof of Proposition
3.2
in this situation: for
M_{1},M_{2}\in\Gamma
stabilizing
u\in\mathbb{B}^{2}
with the same triple of eigenvalues
(\lambda,\bar{\lambda},1)
where
\lambda\in\{-1,\omega,i,-\omega\}
:

(1)

When
\lambda\neq-1
, the eigenvalues of
M_{1},M_{2}
corresponding to
u
cannot be respectively 1 and
\lambda
(or
\bar{\lambda}
).
(\ast)

(2)

If eigenvalues of
M_{1},M_{2}
corresponding to
u
are the same non-real number (resp. complex conjugate non-real numbers), then
M_{1}=M_{2}
(resp.
M_{1}=M_{2}^{-1}
).

(3)

When both eigenvalues of
M_{1},M_{2}
corresponding to
u
are 1, nothing was proved.

Now we generalize these to the case where
M_{1},M_{2}\in\Gamma
stabilizing
u\in\mathbb{B}^{2}
with probably different triples of eigenvalues. Let the eigenvalues of
M_{1},M_{2}
corresponding to
u
be
\lambda_{1},\lambda_{2}
respectively.

(1)

\lambda_{1},\lambda_{2}
cannot be respectively 1 and
\lambda
for
\lambda\neq\pm 1
: otherwise, note that
M_{1}M_{2}
and
M_{2}
are elliptic elements whose eigenvalue corresponding to
u
is
\lambda
, so by
(\ast)
,
M_{1}M_{2}=M_{2}
and thus
M_{1}=I
.

(2)

When one of
\lambda_{1},\lambda_{2}
is not real,
M_{1},M_{2}
should be in the same cyclic group. Let
\lambda_{j}
be a primitive
n_{j}
-th root of unity for
j=1,2
. We check each case as follows. If
(n_{1},n_{2})=(3,4)
or
(4,6)
, then
M_{1}M_{2}
is an elliptic element of order at least 12, which contradicts Lemma
3.3
. If
(n_{1},n_{2})=(2,4)
, then
M_{1}M_{2}
and
M_{2}^{-1}
are elliptic elements with the same eigenvalue corresponding to
u
, so by
(\ast)
,
M_{1}M_{2}=M_{2}^{-1}
, and
M_{1}
is in the cyclic group generated by
M_{2}
. Similar argument also works when
(n_{1},n_{2})=(2,6),(3,6),(2,3)
.

(3)

When
\lambda_{1},\lambda_{2}\in\{\pm 1\}
,
M_{1},M_{2}
may not be in the same cyclic group. However, consider
u^{\perp}:=\{v\in V:\langle u,v\rangle=0\}
. The restriction of the hermitian form on
u^{\perp}
is positive definite, so the subgroup
G_{1}
consisting of elliptic elements whose eigenvalues corresponding to
u
are 1 now descends to lie in a copy of
SU(2)
given by
(u^{\perp},\langle\cdot,\cdot\rangle)
. By the classification of finite subgroup of
SU(2)
and our constraints on orders of elements,
G_{1}
must be among cyclic groups
\mathbb{Z}/n\mathbb{Z}
for
n=2,3,4,6
, dihedral groups
D_{n}
of order
2n
for
n=2,3,4,6
, tetrahedral group
A_{4}
, and octahedral group
S_{4}
. For the full stabilizer group
G
of
u
, if
G\backslash G_{1}
is nonempty, it should consist of elliptic elements whose eigenvalues corresponding to
u
are -1, i.e. they stabilize complex lines passing through
u
. In this case, for any
M\in G\backslash G_{1}
,
M^{2}=I
and
M^{-1}G_{1}M
consists of elliptic elements whose eigenvalues corresponding to
u
are 1, so
M^{-1}G_{1}M=G_{1}
. Therefore, the index of
G_{1}
in
G
is at most 2.

In conclusion, if
u\in\mathbb{B}^{2}
has a nontrivial stabilizer group, then it is either a cyclic group of order
n
whose eigenvalues corresponding to
u
are those
n
-th roots of unity for
n\in\{2,3,4,6\}
, or it is isomorphic to a finite subgroup of
SU(2)
of order at most 24 (or its extension of index 2).
∎

For a specific choice of the CM extension
F=K(\alpha)
, this bound may be improved by further analysis on possible finite subgroups of
SU(2)
. See
[

]
for detail.

For stabilizer groups of an elliptic complex line, this classification is trivial in our case: if two nontrivial elliptic elements stabilize the same complex line, then they have the same 2-dimensional
(-1)
-eigenspace and thus the same orthogonal
1
-eigenspace, so they have to be the same.

3.2.
Injectivity Radius

Recall that the injectivity radius
\rho_{X}(x)
of a point
x\in X
is defined as half of the length of the shortest closed geodesic through
x
. In our setting, we do not have a uniform lower bound of injectivity radii since the closed geodesics of points close to an elliptic point can be arbitrarily small. However, if we only consider loxodromic elements, we do have the following lower bound of injectivity radii.

Proposition 3.10
.

Follow the notation in (
3
). Let the injectivity radius of a point
x\in X
given by loxodromic elements be

\rho_{X}^{Lox}(x):=\min_{\mathrm{M\ loxodromic}}d(x,Mx)/2.

Then there exists
D_{0}>0
such that when
D>D_{0}
,
\rho_{X}^{Lox}(x)\geq\displaystyle\cosh^{-1}\left(\frac{D^{1/4}-1}{2}\right)
.

Proof.

For a loxodromic element
M
, by Theorem
2.4
we write its eigenvalues as
\lambda
,
\bar{\lambda}^{-1}
and
\bar{\lambda}/\lambda
with
|\lambda|>1
, and if their respective eigenvectors are
v_{1},v_{2},v_{3}
, then
1=w+\bar{w}+\mathrm{ta}(u,v_{3})
for any
u\in V\backslash V_{0}
and
w:=\frac{\langle u,v_{2}\rangle\langle v_{1},u\rangle}{\langle v_{1},v_{2}\rangle\langle u,u\rangle}
. Suppose that
x\in X
is represented by
u\in V_{-}
. Since
u\in V_{-}
and
v_{3}\in V_{+}
,
\mathrm{ta}(u,v_{3})\leq 0
, and thus
\mathrm{Re\,}(w)\geq 1/2
.

Before we compute the injectivity radius, first note that when the discriminant
D
grows,
|\lambda|
will become large as follows:
\mathrm{tr}(M)=\lambda+\bar{\lambda}^{-1}+\bar{\lambda}/\lambda
satisfies
\mathrm{tr}(M)\in\mathcal{O}_{F}
and
|\mathrm{tr}(M)^{\sigma_{2}}|\leq 3
since
M^{\sigma_{2}}\in U(3)
only has eigenvalues of norm 1. Thus,
|\mathrm{tr}(M)|^{2}
is either a rational integer bounded by 9, or an integer in
\mathcal{O}_{K}\backslash\mathbb{Z}
, but by the proof of Proposition
3.2
, the first case implies that
M
is elliptic, so
|\mathrm{tr}(M)|^{2}\in\mathcal{O}_{K}\backslash\mathbb{Z}
. This gives that
|\mathrm{tr}(M)|^{2}=(|\mathrm{tr}(M)|^{2}-|\mathrm{tr}(M)^{\sigma_{2}}|^{2})+|\mathrm{tr}(M)^{\sigma_{2}}|^{2}\geq\sqrt{D}
.

Now we compute the injectivity radius. Note that
\mathrm{ta}(u,Mu)=\frac{\langle Mu,u\rangle\langle u,Mu\rangle}{\langle Mu,Mu\rangle\langle u,u\rangle}=\left|\frac{\langle Mu,u\rangle}{\langle u,u\rangle}\right|^{2}
. Then since
Mu=\lambda\frac{\langle u,v_{2}\rangle}{\langle v_{1},v_{2}\rangle}v_{1}+\bar{\lambda}^{-1}\frac{\langle u,v_{1}\rangle}{\langle v_{2},v_{1}\rangle}v_{2}+\frac{\bar{\lambda}}{\lambda}\frac{\langle u,v_{3}\rangle}{\langle v_{3},v_{3}\rangle}v_{3}
,

\displaystyle\left|\frac{\langle Mu,u\rangle}{\langle u,u\rangle}\right|=

\displaystyle\left|\lambda w+\bar{\lambda}^{-1}\bar{w}+\bar{\lambda}/\lambda(1-w-\bar{w})\right|

\displaystyle\geq

\displaystyle\left||\lambda w+\bar{\lambda}^{-1}\bar{w}|-|\bar{\lambda}/\lambda(1-w-\bar{w})|\right|=|\lambda w+\bar{\lambda}^{-1}\bar{w}|-(w+\bar{w}-1)

\displaystyle\geq

\displaystyle\mathrm{Re\,}(|\lambda|w+|\bar{\lambda}^{-1}|\bar{w})-(w+\bar{w}-1)=\mathrm{Re\,}(w)(|\lambda|+|\lambda|^{-1}-2)+1

\displaystyle\geq

\displaystyle(|\mathrm{tr}(M)|-3)/2+1

\displaystyle\geq

\displaystyle\frac{D^{1/4}-1}{2}.

∎

If the closed geodesics of a point given by an elliptic element fixing one single point is small, then this point should be close to this isolated elliptic point. This geometric intuition can be made precise as follows.

Proposition 3.11
.

For any given
R>0
, if
d_{\mathbb{B}^{2}}(u,Mu)<R
for some
u\in\mathbb{B}^{2}
and some elliptic element
M
of order 2, 3, 4, or 6, with isolated fixed point
v_{1}\in\mathbb{B}^{2}
, then
d_{\mathbb{B}^{2}}(u,v_{1})<R
.

Proof.

Let
v_{1},v_{2},v_{3}
be pairwise orthogonal eigenvectors of
M
and
\lambda_{1},\lambda_{2},\lambda_{3}
be their corresponding eigenvalues. Apply (
2.7
) to
u
and we have

Mu=\lambda_{1}\frac{\langle u,v_{1}\rangle}{\langle v_{1},v_{1}\rangle}v_{1}+\lambda_{2}\frac{\langle u,v_{2}\rangle}{\langle v_{2},v_{2}\rangle}v_{2}+\lambda_{3}\frac{\langle u,v_{3}\rangle}{\langle v_{3},v_{3}\rangle}v_{3}

and then

\displaystyle\left|\frac{\langle Mu,u\rangle}{\langle u,u\rangle}\right|

\displaystyle=\left|\lambda_{1}\mathrm{ta}(u,v_{1})+\lambda_{2}\mathrm{ta}(u,v_{2})+\lambda_{3}\mathrm{ta}(u,v_{3})\right|

\displaystyle\geq|\mathrm{Re\,}(\mathrm{ta}(u,v_{1})+\lambda_{2}/\lambda_{1}\mathrm{ta}(u,v_{2})+\lambda_{3}/\lambda_{1}\mathrm{ta}(u,v_{3}))|

Since
\sum_{j=1}^{3}\mathrm{ta}(u,v_{j})=1
,
\mathrm{ta}(u,v_{2}),\mathrm{ta}(u,v_{3})\leq 0
and
|\lambda_{2}/\lambda_{1}|=|\lambda_{3}/\lambda_{1}|=1
, we have
\mathrm{Re\,}(\mathrm{ta}(u,v_{1})+\lambda_{2}/\lambda_{1}\mathrm{ta}(u,v_{2})+\lambda_{3}/\lambda_{1}\mathrm{ta}(u,v_{3}))\geq\mathrm{ta}(u,v_{1})+\mathrm{ta}(u,v_{2})+\mathrm{ta}(u,v_{3})=1
. Thus,

\displaystyle\left|\frac{\langle Mu,u\rangle}{\langle u,u\rangle}\right|

\displaystyle\geq\mathrm{Re\,}(\mathrm{ta}(u,v_{1})+\lambda_{2}/\lambda_{1}\mathrm{ta}(u,v_{2})+\lambda_{3}/\lambda_{1}\mathrm{ta}(u,v_{3}))

\displaystyle=\mathrm{ta}(u,v_{1})+\mathrm{Re\,}(\lambda_{2}/\lambda_{1})\mathrm{ta}(u,v_{2})+\mathrm{Re\,}(\lambda_{3}/\lambda_{1})\mathrm{ta}(u,v_{3})

\displaystyle\geq(1-k)\mathrm{ta}(u,v_{1})+k

where
k
is the largest real part of nontrivial
n
-th roots of unity, i.e.
k=-1,-1/2,0,1/2
for
n=2,3,4,6
respectively. Therefore,

\cosh(d_{\mathbb{B}^{2}}(u,v_{1}))=2\mathrm{ta}(u,v_{1})-1\leq 2\frac{\left|\frac{\langle Mu,u\rangle}{\langle u,u\rangle}\right|-k}{1-k}-1<4\cosh(R/2)-3\leq\cosh(R).

∎

Similarly, we have the following for elliptic complex lines.

Proposition 3.12
.

For any given
R>0
, if
d_{\mathbb{B}^{2}}(u,Mu)<R
for some
u\in\mathbb{B}^{2}
and some elliptic element
M
stabilizing a complex line
L
with normal vector
n
, then
d_{\mathbb{B}^{2}}(u,L)<R/2
.

Proof.

Let
v_{1},v_{2},n
be pairwise orthogonal eigenvectors of
M
and
-1,-1,1
be their corresponding eigenvalues. Then

\left|\frac{\langle Mu,u\rangle}{\langle u,u\rangle}\right|=\left|-\mathrm{ta}(u,v_{1})-\mathrm{ta}(u,v_{2})+\mathrm{ta}(u,n)\right|=1-2\mathrm{ta}(u,n)<\cosh(R/2).

Thus,

\cosh^{2}\left(\frac{d_{\mathbb{B}^{2}}(u,L)}{2}\right)=1-\mathrm{ta}(u,n)<\frac{\cosh(R/2)+1}{2}=\cosh^{2}\left(\frac{R}{4}\right).

∎

4.
Multiplicity Bounds

In this section, we will conclude two kinds of multiplicity bounds for any curve
C\subset X=\Gamma\backslash\mathbb{B}^{2}
inside our compact quotient of the hyperbolic 2-ball. The first one is the multiplicity bound of
C
at a point
x\in C\subset X
in terms of the volume of
C
in a geodesic ball centered at
x
. This is a corollary of a theorem by Hwang and To in
[
HT02
]
:

Theorem 4.1
.

Follow the notation in (
3
). Then for any complex analytic curve
C\subset X
and an isolated elliptic point
x\in C
with stabilizer group
G_{x}
, let
B_{x,r}
be the geodesic ball centered at
x
with radius
r
. Then there exists
D_{0}(r)>0
such that when
D>D_{0}(r)
,

\mathrm{vol}(C\cap B_{x,r})\geq\frac{4\pi}{|G_{x}|}\sinh^{2}\left(\frac{r}{2}\right)\mathrm{mult}_{x}(C).

Proof.

[
HT02
, Theorem 1]
says that, for any complex analytic curve
\tilde{C}\subset\mathbb{B}^{2}
and any point
\tilde{x}\in\tilde{C}
, the geodesic ball
\tilde{B}_{\tilde{x},r}
centered at
\tilde{x}
with radius
r
satisfies

\mathrm{vol}(\tilde{C}\cap\tilde{B}_{\tilde{x},r})\geq 4\pi\sinh^{2}\left(\frac{r}{2}\right)\mathrm{mult}_{\tilde{x}}(\tilde{C}).

Now we use Proposition
3.2
for
R=3r
to pick the
D_{0}
, i.e. for
D>D_{0}
, distances between distinct isolated elliptic points are greater than
3r
. Then for
C\subset X
and
x\in C
, consider
\tilde{x}\in\mathbb{B}^{2}
a preimage of
x
under the quotient map
\tilde{\phi}:\mathbb{B}^{2}\to X
. Now the restriction
\phi=\tilde{\phi}|_{\tilde{B}_{\tilde{x},r}}:\tilde{B}_{\tilde{x},r}\to B_{x,r}
is a surjection. For any
p\in B_{x,r}\backslash\{x\}
, consider
\phi^{-1}(p)
. When
D
is large,
\tilde{p},M\tilde{p}\in\tilde{B}_{\tilde{x},r}
implies that
M
is not loxodromic by Proposition
3.10
. If
M
is elliptic,
\tilde{p},M\tilde{p}\in\tilde{B}_{\tilde{x},r}
implies that
d(\tilde{p},M\tilde{p})<2r
, and then by Proposition
3.11
and
D>D_{0}
,
M
is in the stabilizer group of
x
. Therefore,
|\phi^{-1}(p)|
is at most the order of the isotropic group of
x
, which is bounded by 48 by Corollary
3.9
.
∎

The other one is a multiplicity bound for the intersection of any curve
C
with an elliptic complex line
L\subset X
in terms of a geodesic tubular neighborhood of
L
. Most of the rest of this section is dedicated to prove the following theorem, while we will derive a relative version at the end of this section.

Theorem 4.2
.

Follow the notation in (
3
). For any complex analytic curve
C\subset X
and a set of elliptic complex line
\{L_{j}\subset X\}_{j}
, let
W_{L,r}=\{p\in X:d(p,L)<r\}
be the geodesic tubular neighborhood of
L
with radius
r
. Then for any
r
, there exists
D_{0}(r)>0
such that when
D>D_{0}(r)
,

\mathrm{vol}(C\cap\cup_{j}W_{L_{j},r})\geq\frac{\pi}{6}\sinh^{2}\left(\frac{r}{2}\right)\sum_{j}(C\cdot L_{j})

The proof will use the idea in
[
HT12
, Theorem 1]
, where a similar bound was proved for the intersection between any curve and the diagonal inside a product of hyperbolic curves. The idea here is to find a semi-positive (1,1)-form defined in the neighborhood of the complex line
L
, such that its integration against
L
over
W_{L,r}
is bounded by the volume of
L
and its potential function is plurisubharmonic with desired pole orders along
L
. Then this gives a multiplicity bound through computation of the Lelong numbers.

Before we derive a result about the quotient space, we will first do the computation on
\tilde{W}_{\tilde{L},r}
for one elliptic complex line
\tilde{L}\subset\mathbb{B}^{2}
. The statement on
\mathbb{B}^{2}
we will prove is the following:

Proposition 4.3
.

Follow the notation in (
3
). For any complex analytic curve
\tilde{C}\subset\mathbb{B}^{2}
and an elliptic complex line
\tilde{L}\subset\mathbb{B}^{2}
, let
\tilde{W}_{\tilde{L},r}=\{p\in\mathbb{B}^{2}:d_{\mathbb{B}^{2}}(p,\tilde{L})<r\}
be the geodesic tubular neighborhood of
\tilde{L}
with radius
r
. Then for any
r
, there exists
D_{0}(r)>0
such that when
D>D_{0}(r)
,

\mathrm{vol}(\tilde{C}\cap\tilde{W}_{\tilde{L},r})\geq 4\pi\sinh^{2}\left(\frac{r}{2}\right)(\tilde{C}\cdot\tilde{L}).

By Proposition
2.12
, let

\tilde{\mu}(p)=\tanh^{2}\left(\frac{d(p,\tilde{L})}{2}\right)=\frac{-\mathrm{ta}(p,n)}{1-\mathrm{ta}(p,n)}

where
n
is a normal vector of
L
, and the tubular neighborhood in
\mathbb{B}^{2}
is

\tilde{W}_{L,r}=\{p\in\mathbb{B}^{2}:d(p,L)<r\}=\{p\in\mathbb{B}^{2}:\tilde{\mu}(p)<2\tanh^{2}(r/2)\}.

Since the metric is invariant to different choices of hermitian forms, it suffices to prove the case for
J=diag(1,1,-1)
. We can also assume the complex line
\tilde{L}
has normal vector
n=[1,0,0]\in\mathbb{P}V^{+}
as the
\mathbb{P}U(2,1)
-action is transitive on
\mathbb{B}^{2}
. Consider
p=(z,w)=[z,w,1]\in\mathbb{P}V^{-}
. Then we have

\tilde{\mu}=\frac{|z|^{2}}{1-|w|^{2}}.

We first conclude a criterion for plurisubharmonic function when
\log\tilde{\mu}
is the input.

Lemma 4.4
.

For any given
r_{*}>0
, let
s_{*}:=\log(\tanh^{2}(r_{*}/2))
. Then for any
C^{2}
function
f:[-\infty,s_{*}]\to\mathbb{R}
, the function
f\circ(\log\tilde{\mu})
is plurisubharmonic on
\tilde{W}_{\tilde{L},r}\backslash\tilde{L}
if
f^{\prime\prime}(s),f^{\prime}(s)\geq 0
for
s<s_{*}
.

Proof.

It suffices to check that
i\partial\bar{\partial}(f(\log\tilde{\mu}))
is semipositive. We can check this by writing down the
(1,1)
-form in coordinates
(z,w)
: we say that a
(1,1)
-form
\omega
on
\mathbb{B}^{2}
is represented by a matrix
M
if

M=\left.\begin{pmatrix}\omega\left(\frac{\partial}{\partial z},\frac{\partial}{\partial\bar{z}}\right)&\omega\left(\frac{\partial}{\partial z},\frac{\partial}{\partial\bar{w}}\right)\\
\omega\left(\frac{\partial}{\partial w},\frac{\partial}{\partial\bar{z}}\right)&\omega\left(\frac{\partial}{\partial w},\frac{\partial}{\partial\bar{w}}\right)\end{pmatrix}\right|_{(z,w)}.

Then
i\partial\bar{\partial}\tilde{\mu}
and
i\partial\tilde{\mu}\wedge\bar{\partial}\tilde{\mu}
are respectively represented by:

(4.5)

\begin{pmatrix}\frac{1}{1-|w|^{2}}&\frac{\bar{z}w}{(1-|w|^{2})^{2}}\\
\frac{z\bar{w}}{(1-|w|^{2})^{2}}&\frac{|z|^{2}(1+|w|^{2})}{(1-|w|^{2})^{3}}\end{pmatrix}\text{\ \ and\ \ }\tilde{\mu}\cdot\begin{pmatrix}\frac{1}{1-|w|^{2}}&\frac{\bar{z}w}{(1-|w|^{2})^{2}}\\
\frac{z\bar{w}}{(1-|w|^{2})^{2}}&\frac{|z|^{2}|w|^{2}}{(1-|w|^{2})^{3}}\end{pmatrix}.

Since

i\partial\bar{\partial}(f\circ\log\tilde{\mu})=\frac{f^{\prime}(\log\tilde{\mu})}{\tilde{\mu}}\cdot i\partial\bar{\partial}\tilde{\mu}+\frac{f^{\prime\prime}(\log\tilde{\mu})-f^{\prime}(\log\tilde{\mu})}{\tilde{\mu}^{2}}\cdot i\partial\tilde{\mu}\wedge\bar{\partial}\tilde{\mu},

it is represented by

\displaystyle\frac{f^{\prime}(\log\tilde{\mu})}{\tilde{\mu}}\cdot\begin{pmatrix}\frac{1}{1-|w|^{2}}&\frac{\bar{z}w}{(1-|w|^{2})^{2}}\\
\frac{z\bar{w}}{(1-|w|^{2})^{2}}&\frac{|z|^{2}(1+|w|^{2})}{(1-|w|^{2})^{3}}\end{pmatrix}+\frac{f^{\prime\prime}(\log\tilde{\mu})-f^{\prime}(\log\tilde{\mu})}{\tilde{\mu}^{2}}\cdot\tilde{\mu}\cdot\begin{pmatrix}\frac{1}{1-|w|^{2}}&\frac{\bar{z}w}{(1-|w|^{2})^{2}}\\
\frac{z\bar{w}}{(1-|w|^{2})^{2}}&\frac{|z|^{2}|w|^{2}}{(1-|w|^{2})^{3}}\end{pmatrix}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle f^{\prime\prime}(\log\tilde{\mu})\cdot\begin{pmatrix}\frac{1}{|z|^{2}}&\frac{\bar{z}w}{|z|^{2}(1-|w|^{2})}\\
\frac{z\bar{w}}{|z|^{2}(1-|w|^{2})}&\frac{|w|^{2}}{(1-|w|^{2})^{2}}\end{pmatrix}+f^{\prime}(\log\tilde{\mu})\cdot\begin{pmatrix}0&0\\
0&\frac{1}{(1-|w|^{2})^{2}}\end{pmatrix},

and it is positive semidefinite when
f^{\prime}(s),f^{\prime\prime}(s)\geq 0
since the two matrices in the last row have zero determinants and positive traces.
∎

In the following we will consider the function
F(s)=-2\log(1-e^{s})
. It can be constructed following the idea by Hwang and To in
[
HT12
]
. We first verify that
\tilde{\omega}_{F}:=i\partial\bar{\partial}(F\circ\log\tilde{\mu})
is semipositive and bounded by the volume form in our setting.

Lemma 4.6
.

\tilde{\omega}_{F}:=i\partial\bar{\partial}(F\circ\log\tilde{\mu})
satisfies
0\leq\tilde{\omega}_{F}\leq\omega_{\mathbb{B}^{2}}
, where
\omega_{\mathbb{B}^{2}}
is the Kähler form given by the Bergman metric.

Proof.

Since
s\in[-\infty,0)
,

F^{\prime}(s)=\frac{2e^{s}}{1-e^{s}},\ \ \ F^{\prime\prime}(s)=\frac{2e^{s}}{(1-e^{s})^{2}},

are nonnegative and by Lemma
4.4
,
\tilde{\omega}_{F}
is semipositive. The Kähler form
\omega_{\mathbb{B}^{2}}
is represented by

\frac{2}{(1-|z|^{2}-|w|^{2})^{2}}\begin{pmatrix}1-|w|^{2}&\bar{z}w\\
z\bar{w}&1-|z|^{2}\end{pmatrix}.

We plug
F
into the computation in the proof of Lemma
4.4
and we get that
\tilde{\omega}_{F}
is represented by

\displaystyle\frac{2|z|^{2}(1-|w|^{2})}{(1-|z|^{2}-|w|^{2})^{2}}\cdot\begin{pmatrix}\frac{1}{|z|^{2}}&\frac{\bar{z}w}{|z|^{2}(1-|w|^{2})}\\
\frac{z\bar{w}}{|z|^{2}(1-|w|^{2})}&\frac{|w|^{2}}{(1-|w|^{2})^{2}}\end{pmatrix}+\frac{2|z|^{2}}{1-|z|^{2}-|w|^{2}}\cdot\begin{pmatrix}0&0\\
0&\frac{1}{(1-|w|^{2})^{2}}\end{pmatrix}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{2}{(1-|z|^{2}-|w|^{2})^{2}}\cdot\begin{pmatrix}1-|w|^{2}&\bar{z}w\\
z\bar{w}&\frac{|z|^{2}(1-|z|^{2}-|w|^{2})}{(1-|w|^{2})^{2}}\end{pmatrix}.

Since
1-|z|^{2}-|w|^{2}>0
,
\frac{|z|^{2}(1-|z|^{2}-|w|^{2})}{(1-|w|^{2})^{2}}<\frac{1-|z|^{2}-|w|^{2}}{1-|w|^{2}}\leq\frac{1-|z|^{2}-|w|^{2}+|z|^{2}|w|^{2}}{1-|w|^{2}}=1-|z|^{2}
, and thus
\tilde{\omega}_{F}\leq\omega_{\mathbb{B}^{2}}
.
∎

Similar to Lemma 21 in
[
BT16
]
, we have the following.

Lemma 4.7
.

\frac{1}{2\sinh^{2}(r/2)}\int_{\tilde{C}\cap\tilde{W}_{\tilde{L},r}}\tilde{\omega}_{F}
is an increasing function of
r
.

Proof.

By Stokes’ theorem,

\displaystyle\int_{\tilde{C}\cap\tilde{W}_{\tilde{L},r}}\tilde{\omega}_{F}

\displaystyle=F^{\prime}(\log\tanh^{2}(r/2))\int_{\tilde{C}\cap\tilde{W}_{\tilde{L},r}}i\partial\bar{\partial}\log\tilde{\mu}

\displaystyle=2\sinh^{2}(r/2)\int_{\tilde{C}\cap\tilde{W}_{\tilde{L},r}}i\partial\bar{\partial}\log\tilde{\mu}

because we can approximate
F
by a linear function of slope
F^{\prime}(\log\tanh^{2}(r/2))
at the boundary of
\tilde{W}_{\tilde{L},r}
without changing the integral of the interior. Moreover, applying Lemma
4.4
to
f(s)=s
shows that
\log\tilde{\mu}
is plurisubharmonic, and then the claim follows.
∎

Now we can conclude the proof as in the proof of Theorem 19(b) in
[
BT16
]
.

Proof of Proposition
4.3
.

By Lemma
4.6
and Lemma
4.7
, we have

\int_{\tilde{C}\cap\tilde{W}_{\tilde{L},r}}\omega_{\mathbb{B}^{2}}\geq\int_{\tilde{C}\cap\tilde{W}_{\tilde{L},r}}\tilde{\omega}_{F}\geq 2\sinh^{2}(r/2)\cdot\lim_{r\to 0}\int_{\tilde{C}\cap\tilde{W}_{\tilde{L},r}}i\partial\bar{\partial}\log\tilde{\mu}.

We can bound the limit by a local calculation of Lelong numbers the same as in
[
HT12
]
with minor modifications: for points in a small polydisc neighborhood
U_{j}
of each intersection point
(0,w_{j})\in\tilde{C}\cap\tilde{L}
,
\tilde{\mu}=\frac{|z|^{2}}{1-|w|^{2}}\leq\lambda|z|^{2}
for some fixed
\lambda>1
as there are only finitely many
U_{j}
, and this constant term
\lambda
will have no impact when we take the limit to compute the Lelong number. The same as in
[
HT12
]
, we can conclude that

\lim_{r\to 0}\int_{\tilde{C}\cap\tilde{W}_{\tilde{L},r}}i\partial\bar{\partial}\log\tilde{\mu}\geq 2\pi\cdot(\tilde{C}\cdot\tilde{L}).

∎

Now we continue to prove Theorem
4.2
, i.e. descend Proposition
4.3
to the quotient space
X=\Gamma\backslash\mathbb{B}^{2}
and consider a finite set of elliptic complex lines
\{L_{j}\subset X\}_{j}
. For each
L_{j}
, pick a preimage
\tilde{L}_{j}\subset\mathbb{B}^{2}
, so all the preimages of
L_{j}
are given by
\Gamma\cdot\tilde{L}_{j}
.

For an elliptic complex line
\tilde{L}\subset\mathbb{B}^{2}
, let
\Gamma_{\tilde{L}}\subset\Gamma
be the stabilizer group of
\tilde{L}
. Since
\tilde{L}
is determined by its normal vector
n
, the stabilizer is in fact
\Gamma_{\tilde{L}}=\{M\in\Gamma:M\cdot n=\lambda n\text{ for some }\lambda\in\mathbb{C}^{*}\}
if we consider
n\in V_{+}
. Note that
\tilde{W}_{\tilde{L},r}
is invariant under the action of
M\in\Gamma_{\tilde{L}}
since
\mathrm{ta}(M\cdot p,n)=\mathrm{ta}(p,M^{-1}\cdot n)=\mathrm{ta}(p,n)
.

To derive a result on
\cup_{j}W_{L_{j},r}
, we consider the covering given by the disjoint union of tubular neighborhoods of
\{\tilde{L}_{j}\}_{j}
, and we will show that the sizes of the fibers are bounded.

Lemma 4.8
.

Follow the notation in (
3
). For any given
r>0
, there exists
D_{0}>0
such that the number of elements in the fibers of the quotient map

\tilde{\pi}:\sqcup_{j}\tilde{W}_{\tilde{L}_{j},r}\to\cup_{j}W_{L_{j},r}

is at most 24 for any
D>D_{0}
.

Proof.

Assume that it is not injective at
p\in\cup_{j}W_{L_{j},r}
. It means that by rewriting the indices we can assume that
p
has a preimage
\tilde{p}\in\tilde{W}_{\tilde{L}_{1},r}
such that there is either a distinct
\tilde{p}_{0}\in\tilde{W}_{\tilde{L}_{1},r}
with
M\cdot\tilde{p}=\tilde{p}_{0}
for
M\in\Gamma
, or a not necessarily distinct
\tilde{p}_{0}\in\tilde{W}_{\tilde{L}_{2},r}
with
M\cdot\tilde{p}=\tilde{p}_{1}
for
M\in\Gamma
. In the first case,
\tilde{p}_{0}\neq\tilde{p}
means that
M\not\in\Gamma_{\tilde{L}_{1}}
, so
M^{-1}\cdot\tilde{L}_{1}
is a distinct elliptic complex line in
\mathbb{B}^{2}
, and
\tilde{p}=M^{-1}\cdot\tilde{p}_{0}\in\tilde{W}_{\tilde{L}_{1},r}\cap\tilde{W}_{M^{-1}\cdot\tilde{L}_{1},r}
. In the second case, since
\tilde{L}_{1}
and
\tilde{L}_{2}
have distinct images in
X
,
M\cdot\tilde{L}_{1}
and
\tilde{L}_{2}
are distinct in
\mathbb{B}^{2}
, and we have
\tilde{p}_{0}=M\cdot\tilde{p}\in\tilde{W}_{\tilde{L}_{2},r}\cap\tilde{W}_{M\cdot\tilde{L}_{1},r}
.

In both cases,
\tilde{p}
is now in the intersection of tubular neighborhoods of two distinct elliptic complex lines. Let
\tilde{L}_{a},\tilde{L}_{b}
denote these two elliptic complex lines and let their nontrivial stabilizers in

and

, when

is large, disjoint elliptic complex lines should be far apart and thus

is empty. If these two elliptic complex lines intersect, then by Proposition
3.8
, the intersection point is an isolated elliptic point stabilized by

. Note that
\tilde{p}_{0}\in\tilde{W}_{\tilde{L}_{b},r}
implies

, and then by

,

\displaystyle d(\tilde{p},M_{a}M_{b}\cdot\tilde{p})

\displaystyle\leq d(\tilde{p},M_{b}\cdot\tilde{p})+d(M_{b}\cdot\tilde{p},M_{a}M_{b}\cdot\tilde{p})

\displaystyle<2r+6r

\displaystyle=8r.

Then by Proposition
3.11

\tilde{p}
is inside the ball centered at the intersection point of radius
8r

3.2

In conclusion, we have shown that when

\sqcup_{j}\tilde{W}_{\tilde{L}_{j},r}

∎

5.
Proof of Main Theorems

5.1.
Proof of Theorem
1.1

Consider any irreducible curve
C\subset X
of genus
g
. The idea of the proof is to derive two contradictory inequalities in terms of the volume and the genus of the curve when the discriminant
D
is large. We will first show the case where
C
does not have generic stacky-ness, i.e. it is not a component of an elliptic complex line. The other case will follow using the same strategy.

Step 1
.

An upper bound of
\mathrm{vol}_{X}(C)
when
C
is not a component of an elliptic complex line

Let
U\subset C
be the open subset of
C
with all elliptic points removed. Then
U
can be uniformized by
\mathbb{H}
. Apply Schwarz’s lemma to the hyperbolic metric on
X
and the uniformized metric on
\mathbb{H}
of constant curvature
-1
, and then by Gauss-Bonnet we have

(5.1)

\frac{1}{4\pi}\mathrm{vol}_{X}(C)\leq-\chi(C)\leq 2g-2+\#\{\text{elliptic points}\}

where
\chi(C)
is the orbifold Euler characteristic. This inequality will become an upper bound of
\mathrm{vol}_{X}(C)
if we can show that
\#\{\text{elliptic points}\}<c\cdot\mathrm{vol}_{X}(C)
for some small constant
c<\frac{1}{4\pi}
. Now we will first show a bound of
\#\{\text{elliptic points}\}
for a fixed number
R>0
, and later we can see how to pick
R
in terms of the genus
g
.

Step 1.1
.

an upper bound of numbers of elliptic points on
C
given by elliptic complex lines

Consider all elliptic complex lines
\{L_{j}\}_{j}
in
X
that intersect
C
. Then by Theorem
4.2
when
D
is large we have:

\#\{\text{elliptic points given by elliptic complex lines}\}\leq\sum_{j}(C\cdot L_{j})\leq\frac{\mathrm{vol}_{X}(C)}{\frac{\pi}{6}\sinh^{2}\left(\frac{r}{2}\right)}.

Step 1.2
.

an upper bound of numbers of elliptic points on
C
given by isolated elliptic points

By Theorem
4.1
and Proposition
3.2
, when
D
is large, the balls of radius
r
centered at isolated elliptic points are disjoint, and thus

\#\{\text{isolated elliptic points counted by multiplicity}\}\leq\frac{\mathrm{vol}_{X}(C)}{\frac{\pi}{12}\sinh^{2}\left(\frac{r}{2}\right)}.

In conclusion, we have

\frac{1}{4\pi}\mathrm{vol}_{X}(C)\leq 2g-2+\frac{\mathrm{vol}_{X}(C)}{\frac{\pi}{18}\sinh^{2}\left(\frac{r}{2}\right)}

and when
\sinh^{2}\left(\frac{r}{2}\right)>72
, we obtain an upper bound of
\mathrm{vol}_{X}(C)
.

Step 2
.

In the meantime, consider any point
p
on
C
. By Theorem
4.1
, we have a lower bound of
\mathrm{vol}_{X}(C)
in terms of the injectivity radius of this point. We want this lower bound to be larger than the upper bound we just obtained when
D
is large, which will imply that such
C
cannot exist. If a point on
C
with injectivity radius
r=R_{1}
can lead to such a contradiction, it suffices to find a point on
C
whose distance to any isolated elliptic points is greater than
2R_{1}
and to any elliptic lines is greater than
R_{1}
when
D
is big by Proposition
3.10
,
3.11
and
3.12
.

Suppose we cannot, i.e. the whole curve
C
is contained in a union of hyperbolic balls of radius
2R_{1}
and tubes of radius
R_{1}
.
C
is compact so this cover can be finite. Denote the centers of these balls
x_{1},\cdots,x_{m_{1}}
and complex lines for these tubes
L_{1},\cdots,L_{m_{2}}
. The relative versions of the volume inequalities (Proposition
4.10
and
4.9
) give that for some
R_{2}>R_{1}
we have

\displaystyle\mathrm{vol}(C\cap B_{x,2R_{2}})

\displaystyle\geq 26\mathrm{vol}(C\cap B_{x,2R_{1}})

\displaystyle\mathrm{vol}(C\cap W_{L,R_{2}})

\displaystyle\geq 26\mathrm{vol}(C\cap W_{L,R_{1}})

By Proposition
3.2
and
3.8
, when
D
grows we can make sure each point in the union for
R_{2}
is covered by at most 1 ball and 24 tubes. Then, we have

\displaystyle 26\mathrm{vol}_{X}(C)

\displaystyle\leq 26(\sum_{j=1}^{m_{1}}\mathrm{vol}(C\cap B_{x_{j},2R_{1}})+\sum_{j=1}^{m_{2}}\mathrm{vol}(C\cap W_{L_{j},R_{1}}))

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{j=1}^{m_{1}}\mathrm{vol}(C\cap B_{x_{j},2R_{2}})+\sum_{j=1}^{m_{2}}\mathrm{vol}(C\cap W_{L_{j},R_{2}})

\displaystyle\leq 25\mathrm{vol}_{X}(C)

which is a contradiction. Therefore, there is no curves of genus
g
when
D
is large if this curve is not an elliptic complex line.

Step 3
.

When
C
is a component of an elliptic complex line, this is similar: we still have (
5.1
) except there is a factor of
1/2
on the right hand side because of the generic quotient singularity of the line, and the order of the elliptic points might be different, but it is bounded by the original order. Since intersection points of elliptic complex lines are isolated elliptic points, we only have to consider isolated elliptic points on the curve. Consequently, (
5.1
) becomes:

\frac{1}{4\pi}\mathrm{vol}_{X}(C)\leq\frac{1}{2}\Bigg[2g-2+\frac{\mathrm{vol}_{X}(C)}{\frac{\pi}{12}\sinh^{2}\left(\frac{r}{2}\right)}\Bigg].

and gives an upper bound of
\mathrm{vol}_{X}(C)
for large

. For the lower bound of
\mathrm{vol}_{X}(C)
, the same idea applies except that we do not need to consider tubular neighborhood by Proposition
3.8
. ∎

5.2.
Moduli interpretation

In this section we will prove Theorem
1.2
. We will first describe the Hodge structures the compact ball quotient parametrizes and their correspondence with the abelian varieties in the theorem.

To describe the Hodge structures we want, first note that
n
-balls parametrize
\mathbb{C}
-Hodge structures: an element
v\in\mathbb{P}V_{-}
defines a 1-dimensional subspace of
V
, and the Hermitian form
h
polarizes the decomposition
V=\mathbb{C}v\oplus v^{\perp}
as it is negative definite on
\mathbb{C}v
and positive definite on
v^{\perp}
. This can be extended to an integral Hodge structure using the arithmetic of the lattices as follows.

Consider a
\mathbb{Z}
-lattice
H_{\mathbb{Z}}\cong\mathcal{O}_{F}^{3}
of rank 12 equipped with an
\mathcal{O}_{F}
-action, so
H_{\mathbb{C}}=\mathbb{C}\otimes_{\mathbb{Z}}H_{\mathbb{Z}}
can be decomposed into a direct sum of eigenspaces of the
\mathcal{O}_{F}
-action
H_{\mathbb{C}}\cong H^{\sigma_{1}}\oplus H^{\bar{\sigma}_{1}}\oplus H^{\sigma_{2}}\oplus H^{\bar{\sigma}_{2}}
where the
\mathcal{O}_{F}
-action on each component is given by the corresponding complex embedding of
F\subset\mathbb{C}
. For each embedding
\sigma:F\to\mathbb{C}
, consider
\pi_{\sigma}:H_{\mathbb{C}}=\mathbb{C}\otimes_{\mathbb{Z}}H_{\mathbb{Z}}\to\mathbb{C}_{\sigma}\otimes_{\mathcal{O}_{F}}H_{\mathbb{Z}}\cong\mathbb{C}^{3}
given by identity map, where
\mathbb{C}_{\sigma}
emphisizes that the
\mathcal{O}_{F}
action on
\mathbb{C}
is given by
\sigma
. For any embedding
\sigma^{\prime}:F\to\mathbb{C}
, any
t\in\mathcal{O}_{F}
, and
v\in H^{\sigma^{\prime}}
,
\sigma^{\prime}(t)\pi_{\sigma}(v)=\pi_{\sigma}(\sigma^{\prime}(t)v)=\pi_{\sigma}(t\cdot v)=\sigma(t)\pi_{\sigma}(v)
, which implies that
\pi_{\sigma}(v)=0
when
\sigma\neq\sigma^{\prime}
. Therefore,
\pi_{\sigma}
is indeed a projection onto the
\sigma
-eigenspace and it gives a canonical way to identify the
\sigma
-eigenspace. For example,
1\otimes v\in H_{\mathbb{C}}=(\sigma_{1}(v),\bar{\sigma}_{1}(v),\sigma_{2}(v),\bar{\sigma}_{2}(v))
for
v\in H_{\mathbb{Z}}
.

Note that under this identification, the complex conjugation in
H_{\mathbb{C}}
is not given by complex conjugation in each eigenspace. In fact, consider
\sum_{j}b_{j}\otimes v_{j}\in H^{\sigma}\subset\mathbb{C}\otimes H_{\mathbb{Z}}
. Then the action of
t\in\mathcal{O}_{F}
gives
\sum_{j}\sigma(t)b_{j}\otimes v_{j}=\sum_{j}b_{j}\otimes tv_{j}
. Taking complex conjugation of the coefficients we can see that
\sum_{j}\bar{b}_{j}\otimes v_{j}
is in the eigenspace corresponding to
\bar{\sigma}
. Consequently, for
x=(x_{1},x_{2},x_{3},x_{4})\in H_{\mathbb{C}}\cong H^{\sigma_{1}}\oplus H^{\bar{\sigma}_{1}}\oplus H^{\sigma_{2}}\oplus H^{\bar{\sigma}_{2}}
,
\bar{x}=(\bar{x}_{2},\bar{x}_{1},\bar{x}_{4},\bar{x}_{3})
.

Now for each
v\in\mathbb{P}V^{\sigma_{1}}_{-}
, we can define a polarized integral Hodge structure on
H_{\mathbb{C}}
of weight
w=-1
by picking the negative and positive definite parts respectively for each pair of complex embeddings of
F
. Concretely, fix a nonzero element
\alpha\in\mathcal{O}_{F}\cap i\mathbb{R}
with
\mathrm{Im\,}(\sigma_{1}(\alpha))<0
. Without loss of generality we assume the hermitian form
-i\alpha h
is positive definite under
\sigma_{2}
, and then we define

H^{-1,0}=\mathbb{C}v\oplus\bar{v}^{\perp_{\bar{\sigma}_{1}}}\oplus 0\oplus H^{\bar{\sigma}_{2}},

and
H^{0,-1}=\overline{H^{-1,0}}
gives

H^{0,-1}=v^{\perp_{\sigma_{1}}}\oplus\mathbb{C}\bar{v}\oplus H^{\sigma_{2}}\oplus 0,

so we do have the Hodge decomposition
H_{\mathbb{C}}=H^{0,-1}\oplus H^{-1,0}
. Now we can polarize this Hodge structure using
\alpha h
: for
x,y\in H_{\mathbb{C}}\cong V^{\sigma_{1}}\oplus V^{\bar{\sigma}_{1}}\oplus V^{\sigma_{2}}\oplus V^{\bar{\sigma}_{2}}
,

Q:H_{\mathbb{C}}\otimes\bar{H}_{\mathbb{C}}\to\mathbb{C}(1)\ \ \ (x,\bar{y})\mapsto\sum_{\sigma}\sigma(\alpha)h^{\sigma}(\pi_{\sigma}(x),\pi_{\sigma}(y)),

and by construction it does define a polarization on the real structure. To see that it takes integer values on
H_{\mathbb{Z}}
, consider
u,v\in H_{\mathbb{Z}}
, and then
Q(u,\bar{v})=Q(u,v)=\mathrm{tr}_{F/\mathbb{Q}}(\alpha h(u,v))\in\mathbb{Z}
.

Now we summarize the moduli interpretation of our compact quotient of hyperbolic 2-balls as follows. Fix any Hermitian form
h:F^{3}\times F^{3}\to F
with coefficients in
\mathcal{O}_{F}
such that it is of signature
(2,1)
under the first pair of complex embeddings and definite under the other pair of embeddings, and also fix a nonzero element
\alpha\in\mathcal{O}_{F}\cap i\mathbb{R}
with
\mathrm{Im\,}(\sigma_{1}(\alpha))<0
. Then for any
v\in\mathbb{B}^{2}
we have constructed an integral Hodge structure for a
\mathbb{Z}
-lattice
H_{\mathbb{Z}}\cong\mathcal{O}_{F}^{3}
of rank 12, polarized by
Q
defined above. Since polarized integral Hodge structures of weight
-1
correspond to abelian varieties by taking
H_{\mathbb{C}}/(H^{0,-1}+H_{\mathbb{Z}})
, we can state the moduli interpretation in terms of abelian varieties. For an abelian variety
A
with
\mathcal{O}_{F}
-multiplication
\mathcal{O}_{F}\xhookrightarrow{}\mathrm{End}(A)
,
H_{1}(A,\mathbb{Z})
has an induced
\mathcal{O}_{F}
-module structure, and we say that
A
has a polarization given by a skew-Hermitian form

defined over

is a Riemann form that polarizes

Lemma 5.2
.

When

To continue the construction above, note that the associated positive definite Hermitian form on

is

\mathrm{tr}_{F/\mathbb{Q}}(-\alpha h)

For any abelian variety

-endomorphism whose

\mathcal{O}_{F}
