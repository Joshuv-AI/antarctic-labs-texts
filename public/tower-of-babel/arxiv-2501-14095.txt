Improved subsample-and-aggregate via the private modified winsorized mean
Kelly Ramsay, Dylan Spicker

Abstract
We develop a univariate, differentially private mean estimator, called the private modified winsorized mean , designed to be used as the aggregator in subsample-and-aggregate. We demonstrate, via real data analysis, that common differentially private multivariate mean estimators may not perform well as the aggregator, even in large datasets, motivating our developments. We show that the modified winsorized mean is minimax optimal for several, large classes of distributions, even under adversarial contamination. We also demonstrate that, empirically, the private modified winsorized mean performs well compared to other private mean estimates. We consider the modified winsorized mean as the aggregator in subsample-and-aggregate, deriving a finite sample deviations bound for a subsample-and-aggregate estimate generated with the new aggregator. This result yields two important insights: (i) the optimal choice of subsamples depends on the bias of the estimator computed on the subsamples, and (ii) the rate of convergence of the subsample-and-aggregate estimator depends on the robustness of the estimator computed on the subsamples. Key words and phrases: Differential privacy, robust statistics, mean estimation, subsample-and-aggregate, adversarial contamination.



1
Introduction

Subsample-and-aggregate is a powerful algorithm that can produce a differentially privately version of any statistic
(
Nissim et al.,, 2007
)
.
For instance, through subsample-and-aggregate, complex machine learning models can be made private easily, with minimal additional programming.
The algorithm is a two-step procedure, relying on a “subsample” step and an “aggregate” step.
The idea, in brief, is to first split the data into disjoint subsets.
Then, on each of these subsets, a nonprivate version of the desired statistic is computed.
Next, the set of nonprivate statistics are combined, using a differentially private aggregator, producing a private estimate of the parameter of interest.
Generally, if the mean of the subsamples is desired, the private aggregator is a basic differentially private clipped mean
(
Nissim et al.,, 2007
)
or a widened winsorized mean
(
Smith,, 2011
)
.
However, to achieve pure differential privacy, both of these aggregators require tight input bounds on the nonprivate statistics.

It is natural to consider more recent, differentially private mean estimators as the private aggregator. These have a weaker dependence, if any, on input bounds.
However, currently, replacing the aggregator with any of the existing differentially private mean estimators comes with at least one of the following drawbacks: (i) only a weaker notion of differential privacy is satisfied, (ii) many subsamples are required for a stable mean estimate, resulting in large sample size requirements, (iii) the new algorithm has strong dependence on tuning parameters, (iv) strong assumptions on, or knowledge of, the population distribution is required, or (v) the estimator is not practically computable.

Here, we develop a univariate, differentially private mean estimator, called the
private modified winsorized mean
, or, PMW mean.
Our estimator is designed to be used as the aggregator in subsample-and-aggregate and avoids these drawbacks.
The PMW mean can satisfy either pure or zero-concentrated differential privacy, performs well in small samples or under adversarial contamination, has weak dependence on input bounds and other tuning parameters, and can be used to estimate the mean of a general distribution, with only two moments.
The proposed estimator combines the winsorized mean of
Lugosi and Mendelson, (2021)
and the private quantile estimation procedure of
Durfee, (2024)
.

1.1
Related work

Differentially private mean estimation has been well-studied.
In the univariate setting, clipped and trimmed means have been the main approach
(
Foygel Barber and Duchi,, 2014
;
Karwa and Vadhan,, 2017
;
Bun and Steinke,, 2019
;
Kamath et al.,, 2020
;
Tsfadia et al.,, 2022
;
Durfee,, 2024
)
.
The clipped mean procedure operates by selecting an interval
[a,b]
, either empirically or
a priori
, and projecting the dataset onto the interval.
An additive noise mechanism is then used to ensure privacy.
The trimmed mean functions similarly, removing extreme observations instead of projecting them.
Existing estimators using these approaches have at least one of the previously mentioned drawbacks.
In particular,
Tsfadia et al., (2022)
consider approximately differentially private mean estimation.
For stronger forms of privacy, many mean estimation algorithms require certain components of the population distribution as algorithm inputs, such as the number of moments
(
Foygel Barber and Duchi,, 2014
;
Kamath et al.,, 2020
)
or a lower bound on the variance
(
Bun and Steinke,, 2019
)
.
Conversely, algorithms aimed that they can be implemented without knowledge of the population distribution lack minimax optimality or similar statistical guarantees
(
Tsfadia et al.,, 2022
;
Durfee,, 2024
)
.
The most similar work is that of
Bun and Steinke, (2019)
, who develop a zero-concentrated differentially private trimmed mean estimate, combined with smooth sensitivity.
Their estimator avoids many of the drawbacks listed, however, a lower bound on the variance is required, and it is unclear how to choose the smoothing parameter.
In addition, their approach removes data from the estimator, which can be problematic at low sample sizes (see Section
5
).

Multivariate estimators can be applied to univariate estimation problems.
In the multivariate setting, approximate differentially private mean estimation has been well-studied
(
Liu et al., 2021a,
;
Brown et al.,, 2021
;
Esfandiari et al.,, 2022
;
Duchi et al.,, 2023
;
Brown et al.,, 2023
;
Dagan et al.,, 2024
)
.
Under stronger privacy guarantees, many authors have considered mean estimators for a sample of subgaussian data
(
Kamath et al.,, 2018
;
Bun et al.,, 2019
;
Cai et al.,, 2021
;
Biswas et al.,, 2020
;
Liu et al., 2021b,
)
.
Several authors have relaxed this assumption using moment conditions on the population distribution, through contamination models, or both
(
Hopkins et al.,, 2022
;
Yu et al.,, 2024
)
.
Unfortunately, some of these algorithms are not currently practically computable
(
Hopkins et al.,, 2022
;
Brown et al.,, 2021
;
Liu et al., 2021b,
)
.
For the computable estimators, at small sample sizes (
n<1000
) we have observed empirically that they do not perform as well as univariate estimators applied coordinate-wise, and may be unstable or highly sensitive to tuning parameters (see Section
6
).
To use these estimators in subsample-and-aggregate, we would then require thousands of subsamples, and, thus, a very large sample size.

Beyond mean estimation, several authors have considered variants of subsample-and-aggregate
(
Smith,, 2011
;
Bassily et al.,, 2018
;
Jordon et al.,, 2019
;
Amin et al.,, 2022
)
.
Most of these focus on the “subsample” step of the algorithm
(
Papernot et al.,, 2016
;
Bassily et al.,, 2018
;
Jordon et al.,, 2019
)
.

Amin et al., (2022)
and
Smith, (2011)
consider alternatives for the private aggregator.

Smith, (2011)
show that using a private widened winsorized mean as the aggregator results in the same asymptotic distribution as the nonprivate estimator used in the subsample step.
However, this aggregator is sensitive to input bounds, unless we relax the setting to approximate differential privacy.

Amin et al., (2022)
consider an approximately differentially private, multivariate median aggregator in the setting of linear regression.

1.2
Contributions

Our main contributions are: (i) We develop a new, univariate, differentially private mean estimator, designed to be used as the aggregator in subsample-and-aggregate. (ii) We show that this estimator is minimax optimal for several large classes of distributions, even under adversarial contamination (Theorem
1
). As a corollary, we present a finite sample deviation bound for subsample-and-aggregate with the new aggregator (Corollary
1
). (iii) We demonstrate that the estimator performs well relative to competitors in simulation (Section
5
). (iv) We demonstrate via, data analysis, that common differentially private multivariate mean estimators do not perform well as the aggregator, even with data with 2300 subjects (Section
6
). (v) As a by-product of our analysis, we introduce a zero-concentrated differentially private version of the unbounded private quantile algorithm
(
Durfee,, 2024
)
, as well as concentration inequalities for these quantile estimates (Lemma
1
and Lemma
2
–
3
). In addition, we correct an error in the proof of Theorem 1 of
Lugosi and Mendelson, (2021)
(Appendices
G
and
C
).

Preliminary material

2.1
Problem statement

First, we formalize the problem.
Suppose that we have a random sample of size
2n
, with at most
2\eta n
points, (
0\leq\eta\leq 1/2
),
corrupted
in that they have been changed to arbitrary values, where the corrupted values may depend on the uncorrupted values.
Denote the corrupted sample
X_{1}^{\prime},\ldots,X_{n}^{\prime},Y_{1}^{\prime},\ldots,Y_{n}^{\prime}
.
Assume that the uncorrupted points, denoted
X_{1},\ldots,X_{n},Y_{1},\ldots,Y_{n}
, are independent and identically distributed according to a univariate distribution
F
with mean
\mu\in\mathbb{R}
and variance
\sigma^{2}<\infty
.
The aim is to estimate
\mu
while maintaining differential privacy (defined in Section
2.2
).
This setup is summarized as Condition
1
.

Condition 1
.

Take an independent, identically distributed sample
X_{1},\ldots,X_{n},Y_{1},\ldots,Y_{n}
, drawn from a univariate distribution
F
with mean
\mu\in\mathbb{R}
and variance
\sigma^{2}<\infty
, to be the uncorrupted points.
The points
X_{1}^{\prime},\ldots,X_{n}^{\prime},Y_{1}^{\prime},\ldots,Y_{n}^{\prime}
then differ arbitrarily from the uncorrupted points at at most
2\eta n
points,
0\leq\eta\leq 1/2
, where the corrupted values may depend on the uncorrupted values.

2.2
Differential privacy

Next, we introduce the concepts from differential privacy used in this work
(
Dwork et al.,, 2006
;
Dwork and Roth,, 2014
)
.
Define a dataset of size
n\in\mathbb{N}
to be a set of
n
real numbers, and let
\mathcal{D}_{n}
be the set of datasets of size
n
.
We say that a dataset
\mathbf{x}_{n}\in\mathcal{D}_{n}
, is adjacent to another dataset
\mathbf{y}_{n}\in\mathcal{D}_{n}
if
\mathbf{x}_{n}
and
\mathbf{y}_{n}
differ by exactly one point.
Let
\mathcal{A}_{n}
be the set of pairs of adjacent datasets of size
n
.
Let
G_{\mathbf{x}_{n}}
be a probability distribution over
\mathbb{R}^{d}
with
d\in\mathbb{N}
that depends on
\mathbf{x}_{n}
.
That is,
G_{.}\colon\mathcal{D}_{n}\to\mathcal{M}_{1}(\mathbb{R}^{d}),
where
\mathcal{M}_{1}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
is the set of (Borel) probability distributions on
\mathbb{R}^{d}
.
If
G_{\mathbf{x}_{n}}
is absolutely continuous, let
g_{\mathbf{x}_{n}}
be the associated density.
For
\alpha>1
, denote the
\alpha
-Rényi Divergence between distributions,
F
and
H
,
{\rm D}_{\alpha}(F|H)
.
We now define
(pure) differential privacy
(PDP)
(
Dwork et al.,, 2006
)
and
zero-concentrated differential privacy
(zCDP)
Bun and Steinke, (2016)
.

Definition 1
.

The quantity
\theta\sim G_{\mathbf{x}_{n}}
is
\varepsilon
-differentially private (
\varepsilon
-PDP),
\varepsilon>0
, if

\sup_{(\mathbf{y}_{n},\mathbf{z}_{n})\in\mathcal{A}_{n}}\sup_{x\in\mathbb{R}}\frac{g_{\mathbf{y}_{n}}(x)}{g_{\mathbf{z}_{n}}(x)}\leq e^{\varepsilon},

and is
\rho
-zero-concentrated differentially private (
\rho
-zCDP),
\rho>0
, if, for all
\alpha\in(1,\infty)
,

\sup_{(\mathbf{y}_{n},\mathbf{z}_{n})\in\mathcal{A}_{n}}{\rm D}_{\alpha}(G_{\mathbf{y}_{n}}|G_{\mathbf{z}_{n}})\leq\alpha\rho.

Here,
\theta
is a
d
-dimensional differentially private quantity and
\varepsilon,\rho
are the
privacy budgets
, with smaller values of
\varepsilon,\rho
producing higher levels of privacy.
Quantities that are
\varepsilon
-PDP are also
\varepsilon^{2}/2
-zCDP.
The following two properties are useful for developing differentially private estimates.

Proposition 1
.

The following hold:

•

Post-processing:
For any function
f
that does not depend on
\mathbf{x}_{n}
, defined on a subset of
\mathbb{R}^{d}
, if
\theta
satisfies
\varepsilon
-PDP (
\rho
-zCDP), then
f(\theta)
satisfies
\varepsilon
-PDP (
\rho
-zCDP).

•

Composition:
If
\theta_{1},\theta_{2}
satisfy
\varepsilon_{1}
-PDP and
\varepsilon_{2}
-PDP (
\rho_{1}
-zCDP and
\rho_{2}
-zCDP), respectively, then
(\theta_{1},\theta_{2})
satisfies
(\varepsilon_{1}+\varepsilon_{2})
-PDP (
(\rho_{1}+\rho_{2})
-zCDP).

Proposition
1
first states that functions of differentially private quantities are still private, and second that releasing two differentially private quantities results in a jointly differentially private estimate, with a larger privacy parameter.

Next, we introduce a simple way to generate a differentially private statistic: additive noise mechanisms.
For a univariate statistic
T\colon\mathcal{D}_{n}\to\mathbb{R}
, define the global sensitivity of
T
as

\mathrm{GS}(T)=\sup_{(\mathbf{y}_{n},\mathbf{z}_{n})\in\mathcal{A}_{n}}|T(\mathbf{y}_{n})-T(\mathbf{z}_{n})|.

We have the following proposition, introducing the Laplace and Gaussian mechanisms.

Proposition 2
.

Let
T\colon\mathcal{D}_{n}\to\mathbb{R}
be any statistic with
\mathrm{GS}(T)<\infty
.
If
Z
is a standard Laplace random variable, and
Z^{\prime}
is a standard Gaussian random variable, then

T(\mathbf{x}_{n})+\mathrm{GS}(T)Z/\varepsilon,\ \text{ and }\ T(\mathbf{x}_{n})+\mathrm{GS}(T)Z^{\prime}/\sqrt{2\rho},

satisfy
\varepsilon
-PDP and
\rho
-zCDP, respectively.

Proposition
2
illustrates how correctly calibrated noise can be added to a statistic to ensure differential privacy.

2.3
Lugosi and Mendelson’s modified winsorized mean

We now review the non-private inspiration for the proposed estimator.

Lugosi and Mendelson, (2021)
recently showed that, out of the class of non-private estimators, a modified winsorized mean estimator is minimax optimal, for the model given in Condition
1
.
Given a clipping proportion
0<p<1/2
, the idea is to first use half of the sample,
\mathbf{Y}_{n}^{\prime}=\{Y_{i}^{\prime}\}_{i=1}^{n}
, to estimate the
p
th quantile and the
(1-p)
th quantile of
F
.
We denote these quantile estimates by
\hat{\xi}_{p}
and
\hat{\xi}_{1-p}
, respectively.
Then, we project the other half of the observations
\mathbf{X}_{n}^{\prime}=\{X_{i}^{\prime}\}_{i=1}^{n}
onto the interval
[\hat{\xi}_{p},\hat{\xi}_{1-p}]
.
The sample mean of the projected observations is taken as the final estimate of
\mu
.
Formally, for
0<q\leq 1
, let
\hat{\xi}_{q}
be the (left-continuous)
q
th quantile of the empirical distribution defined by
\mathbf{Y}_{n}^{\prime}
.
For
a\leq b
, define

\phi_{a,b}(x)=\begin{cases}a&x<a\\
x&a\leq x\leq b\\
b&x>b\end{cases}.

The non-private clipped mean is

\hat{\mu}_{p}=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\frac{1}{n}\phi_{\hat{\xi}_{p},\hat{\xi}_{1-p}}(X_{i}^{\prime}).

Lugosi and Mendelson, (2021)
proved an upper bound on
|\hat{\mu}_{p}-\mu|
that holds with probability
1-\delta
, with
\delta
at most exponentially small in
n
.
Their result shows that taking
p=8\eta+12\log(4/\delta)/n
yields an estimator that is minimax optimal.
Here, we extend
\hat{\mu}_{p}
to the differentially private setting.
The motivation is that, for a carefully chosen
p
, the optimality in the nonprivate setting should translate to the private setting, provided we are able to estimate extreme quantiles well under the restriction of differential privacy.

2.4
Private quantile estimation

A private version of the clipped mean,
\hat{\mu}_{p}
, requires differentially private estimation of extreme quantiles.
We make use of the
unbounded
private quantile estimator, developed by
Durfee, (2024)
.
We use the unbounded quantile estimator as it has been shown to estimate extreme quantiles consistently, whereas other private quantile estimators do not
(
Ramsay and Diaz-Rodriguez,, 2024
)
.
Specifically, when there is no contamination, our mean estimator requires a private analogue of an
O(1/n)
quantile.
It is expected that a private
O(1/n)
th quantile would be consistent for the infimum of the support of
F
, as
n\to\infty
.

Ramsay and Diaz-Rodriguez, (2024)
show that other private quantile estimators do not have this property, however, the unbounded estimator does.

We present the modified estimator given by
Ramsay and Diaz-Rodriguez, (2024)
.
Let
V,V_{1},V_{2},\ldots
be a sequence of independent, standard exponential random variables.
Suppose that we wish to estimate the
q
th quantile of the population distribution privately, where the desired quantile is an upper quantile, (i.e.,
1/2\leq q\leq 1
).
First, we supply the algorithm with: a grid size parameter
\beta>1
, a lower bound on the desired quantile
\ell
, a dataset with empirical distribution
F_{n}
, and two privacy budgets
\varepsilon_{1},\varepsilon_{2}>0
.
Given these parameters, the algorithm proceeds as follows: (i) Compute
\hat{q}=q+V/n\varepsilon_{1}
and set
i=1
. (ii) Increase
i
by one, stopping at the first
i
such that
F_{n}(\beta^{i}+\ell-1)+V_{i}/n\varepsilon_{2}>\hat{q}
.
(iii) Output
\beta^{i}+\ell-1
.
If
0<q<1/2
, we supply an upper bound on the desired quantile,
u
. Then, we negate the dataset, and run the upper-quantile algorithm, with input quantile
1-q
and
\ell=-u
.
This produces a negated estimate of the lower quantile that we negate to get the final quantile estimate.
We refer to these quantile estimates by
\tilde{\xi}_{q}\coloneqq\tilde{\xi}_{q,\mathbf{X}_{n}}(\varepsilon_{1},\varepsilon_{2},\ell,u,\beta)
.
The algorithm is
(\varepsilon_{1}+\varepsilon_{2})
-differentially private
(
Durfee,, 2024
)
.

We show (Lemma
1
) that replacing
\varepsilon_{i}
with
\sqrt{\rho_{i}}
and taking
V,V_{1},V_{2},\ldots
to be standard Gaussian random variables produces
(\rho_{1}+\rho_{2})
-zero-concentrated differentially private quantiles.
We denote the estimates generated by the zero-concentrated differentially private version of the algorithm by
\tilde{\xi}_{q}^{\prime}\coloneqq\tilde{\xi}_{q,\mathbf{X}_{n}}^{\prime}(\rho_{1},\rho_{2},\ell,u,\beta)
.
As a by-product of our analysis, we prove a simple concentration result concerning both versions of these private quantiles (Lemmas
2
-
3
).

3
A new differentially private mean estimator

We now introduce our private mean estimator, the PMW mean, drawing inspiration from
Lugosi and Mendelson, (2021)

Given a clipping proportion
0<p<1/2
and private quantile parameters
\ell,u\in\mathbb{R}
,
\beta>1
, and
\varepsilon_{1},\varepsilon_{2}>0
(PDP) or
\rho_{1},\rho_{2}>0

\tilde{\xi}_{p}

\tilde{\xi}_{1-p}
(PDP) or
\tilde{\xi}_{p}^{\prime}

\tilde{\xi}_{1-p}^{\prime}
(zCDP).
Recall that these are the private estimates of the extreme quantiles, based on
\mathbf{Y}_{n}^{\prime}

The data

[\tilde{\xi}_{p},\tilde{\xi}_{1-p}]

[\tilde{\xi}_{p}^{\prime},\tilde{\xi}_{1-p}^{\prime}]

Lastly, we take the sample mean of the projected observations, and add calibrated noise via the Laplace mechanism (PDP) or the Gaussian mechanism (zCDP).
Given
4e^{-n}<\delta<1

\mu

Here, for two real numbers

For notational simplicity, we hide the dependence of

Definition 2

Remark 1

(On choosing

.

For minimax optimality, we require
\log(\beta/(\beta-1))\lesssim\log(4/\delta)

Combining this with Condition

This ensures that the bound (

t
-distribution with three degrees of freedom,
b_{n}\gtrsim 1,
when
u=2\xi_{1-5\zeta/4}
and
\ell=-u
.
Thus, we must take a finer grid when applying the procedure to distributions with bounded support, but a coarse grid is acceptable for distributions with unbounded support.

Remark 2

(On choosing
u
and
\ell
)
.

Condition
2
requires that
\ell<\xi_{5\zeta/4}<\xi_{1-5\zeta/4}<u
.
Then, (
1
) implies that taking
\log(u-\ell)\lesssim\log(1/\delta)
produces a PMW mean that is minimax optimal.
Choosing
\ell,u
thus amounts to ensuring these two bounds are satisfied.
First, observe that, in general, we take
\delta
to be small, say,
\delta=n^{-k}
for some
k>0
.
The bound
\log(u-\ell)\lesssim\log(1/\delta)
then requires that
u-\ell
is at most polynomial in
n
.
Now, we need
u,\ell
such that
\ell<\xi_{5\zeta/4}<\xi_{1-5\zeta/4}<u
.
For
F
with bounded support, this is straightforward.
If the support is unbounded, we can specify
u,\ell
according to the following argument.
We have that, by Chebyshev’s inequality,
5\zeta/4\leq\sigma^{2}/(\xi_{1-5\zeta/4}-\mu)^{2}
.
This implies that
\xi_{1-5\zeta/4}\lesssim\sigma/\zeta^{1/2}+\mu
.
Therefore, Condition
2
is implied by
u\gtrsim\sigma/\zeta^{1/2}\vee\mu
.
Then, using
\delta=n^{-k}
,
\sigma/\zeta^{1/2}\lesssim\sigma\sqrt{n/k\log n}.

Assuming that
\mu,\sigma
are constant in
n
, we take
u\geq n^{1/2+r},

\ell\leq-n^{1/2+r},
for some
r>0
.
For large
n
, this choice will imply Condition
2
.
If one has instead (potentially very loose) bounds such that
-\mu_{0}<\mu<\mu_{0}
and
\sigma<\sigma_{0}
, then taking
u\gtrsim\sqrt{n\sigma_{0}}\vee\mu_{0}
and
\ell\lesssim-(\sqrt{n\sigma_{0}}\vee\mu_{0})
implies Condition
2
, and ensures that
u-\ell
is at most polynomial in
n
.

Remark 3

(Weakening Condition
2
)
.

Condition
2
requires that the interval
(\ell,u)
contains most of the uncontaminated data.
This condition can be relaxed to only require that
u>\xi_{5\zeta/4}
and
\ell<\xi_{1-5\zeta/4}
, (i.e.,
u
is an upper bound on the lower quantile and
\ell
is a lower bound on the upper quantile).
This will come at the expense of replacing
u-\ell
in (
1
) with
\log n
.
This modification still constitutes a minimax optimal rate, up to logarithmic terms.
However, given that the bounds on the data can be very loose, as per (
1
), and the results in Section
5
, we leave Condition
2
unchanged.

Remark 4

(Subgaussian distributions and more)
.

As usual, (e.g., as in the work of
Lugosi and Mendelson, (2021)
), we can derive stronger bounds under stronger assumptions.
We use Chebyshev’s inequality to generate the first and last terms in (
1
).
This can be replaced with the tightest tail bound given the assumptions.
For instance, if we assume the uncontaminated data are subgaussian, we use a subgaussian tail bound to get tighter deviation bounds.
In that case, the PMW mean also achieves optimal rates.

Remark 5

(Multivariate case)
.

The PMW mean can be extended in a coordinate-wise fashion to give a simple multivariate estimator.
Given the coordinate-wise nature, this estimator will not achieve minimax optimality.
For instance, even under no contamination, the coordinate-wise estimator has error at least
d^{3/2}/\sqrt{n}\epsilon
, which is sub-optimal
(
Kamath et al.,, 2020
)
.
Another way of looking at this is to see that the coordinate-wise estimator has error at least
\sqrt{d\eta}
.
There are (non-private) estimators (e.g., in
Lugosi and Mendelson, (2021)
) which depend on the contamination only through
\sqrt{\eta\lambda_{1}}
, where
\lambda_{1}
is the largest eigenvalue of the covariance matrix.
It stands to reason that we should be able to do better than
\sqrt{d\eta}
in the private setting, since we are often adding noise in a clever way to non-private estimators.
It may be possible to extend our estimator to a more efficient multivariate estimator, say a private version of the multivariate estimator of
Lugosi and Mendelson, (2019)
.
However, even non-privately, this is a difficult computational problem
(
Lugosi and Mendelson,, 2019
)
.
The goal of this work was to develop a practical and fast algorithm to use in subsample-and-aggregate, not to develop a minimax optimal multivariate estimator.
As such, we leave extensions of the proposed estimator to the multivariate setting for future work.

4
A more practical private clipped mean

Next, we present adjustments to the proposed estimator
\tilde{\mu}^{\prime}
that we recommend in practice, as well as a proposed practical version of
\tilde{\mu}^{\prime}
, denoted by
\tilde{\mu}^{\prime}_{p}
.
First, note that splitting the sample in half to compute each of the two steps of the estimator does not have any algorithmic or intuitive justification.
It is an arbitrary choice that simplifies the proof of the deviation bound.
Therefore, when computing
\tilde{\mu}^{\prime}_{p}
, we modify the algorithm and use all data in each estimation step.
Note that this does not change the privacy guarantees of the estimator.

Next, observe that no effort was made to optimize the constants in the clipping parameter
\zeta
in the theoretical exposition of Section
3
.
Therefore, using
\zeta
as a guide, we present the following practical version of the clipping parameter, which will be used in simulation for both
\tilde{\mu}^{\prime}_{p}
and
\tilde{\mu}^{\prime}
.
The user is asked to select two parameters,
0<C<0.025n
and
0\leq\eta<1/2
.
As before,
\eta
is the maximum expected level of contamination.
The new parameter,
C
, represents the number of observations the user would like to clip from either end, if the contamination level was 0.
We cap
C
at
0.025n
as otherwise, at very small sample sizes,
C
may be larger than
n/2
.
Finally, the clipping parameter is computed to be
C/n\vee\eta
, and is used in place of
\zeta
.
We use a simulation study to investigate different values of
C
and
\eta
, and found that the value of
C
was not particularly important (see Appendix
I.4
).
In addition, as expected, we found that smaller
\eta
was better for uncontaminated, light-tailed, uncontaminated data (Gaussian, Exponential and Gaussian mixtures) but larger
\eta
was preferable for heavy tailed or contaminated data (Contaminated Gaussian and student
t
) (see Appendix
I.4
).

Next, not covered by the present theory, is how to best split the total budget
\rho
amongst
\rho_{1},\rho_{2},
and
\rho_{3}
.
For this, we simulated the performance of the estimator under three scenarios: more weight to the quantile estimation, equal weight to the mean estimation and quantile estimation, and more weight to the mean estimation (see Appendix
I.5
).
We found that this was not particularly important, except at very small samples, and so we simply split the budget evenly between the mean estimation and quantile estimation, specifically setting
\rho_{1}=\rho_{2}=\rho/4
and,
\rho_{3}=\rho/2
in the following simulation results.

6
Improved subsample-and-aggregate

We now demonstrate that the proposed mean estimator can be used to improve subsample-and-aggregate.
Subsample-and-aggregate, developed by
Nissim et al., (2007)
, and extended by
Smith, (2011)
, is a method for estimating virtually any quantity, privately.
Define a multivariate dataset to be a set of
N=2n
,
p
-dimensional vectors, and let
\mathcal{D}_{N,p}
be the set of all such datasets.
Consider a multivariate dataset, say
\mathbf{X}_{N}
, a set of
N
realizations of
p
-dimensional random vectors, that are independent and identically distributed.
To compute a statistic
T\colon\mathcal{D}_{N,p}\to\mathbb{R}^{d}
for
d\geq 1
, from
\mathbf{X}_{N}
, privately, we:
(i) Split the dataset
\mathbf{X}_{N}
into
m
disjoint groups of size
k=\lfloor N/m\rfloor
, say
\mathbf{X}_{N}^{(1)},\ldots,\mathbf{X}_{N}^{(m)}
.
(ii) Compute
T_{1},\ldots,T_{m}
, where
T_{i}=T(\mathbf{X}_{N}^{(i)})
.
(iii) Apply a private mean estimator to the set of subsampled estimates
T_{1},\ldots,T_{m}
to generate a private estimate of
T(\mathbf{X}_{N})
.
As mentioned previously, the private aggregator in subsample-and-aggregate algorithm is typically either the traditional private clipped mean, or the private widened winsorized mean applied coordinate-wise
(
Nissim et al.,, 2007
;
Smith,, 2011
)
.
If poor input bounds are provided for the coordinates of
T(\mathbf{X}_{N})
, this approach introduces a high level of noise to the private estimate.
Moreover, we will demonstrate that other popular private mean algorithms can be unstable, or have large errors, when applied to small sample sizes and the dimension is not very large.
Critically, in the context of subsample-and-aggregate, we are often computing the mean of a small sample, given that we must first split the data into groups large enough for
T_{1},\ldots,T_{m}
to be stable.
This motivates our proposal of
\tilde{\mu}_{p}
or
\tilde{\mu}^{\prime}_{p}
applied coordinatewise as the aggregator.
We demonstrate this observation below, via a real-world application to linear mixed modelling.

First, we state a corollary of Theorem
1
, which yields the finite sample accuracy of the private estimate generated from subsample-and-aggregate.
Assume that
\mathbf{X}_{N}^{\prime}
is an adversarially corrupted version of
\mathbf{X}_{N}
, where at most
\lfloor n\eta\rfloor
observations are corrupted.
Let
\mathrm{Bias}_{N,\eta}(T)=\mathbb{E}(T(\mathbf{X}_{N}^{\prime}))-\theta
be the bias of the estimator
T
.
Next, given
T\colon\mathcal{D}_{N}\to\mathbb{R}
, let
\tilde{T}
and
\tilde{T}^{\prime}
be the subsample-and-aggregate estimates generated when
\tilde{\mu}
and
\tilde{\mu}^{\prime}
are used for the aggregation step, respectively.
Let
\mathbf{X}_{N}^{\prime(1)},\ldots,\mathbf{X}_{N}^{\prime(m)}
be a uniformly random set of disjoint subsets of size
k=\lfloor N/m\rfloor
chosen from
\mathbf{X}_{N}^{\prime}
, and let
G_{k}
be the distribution of
T(\mathbf{X}_{N}^{\prime(1)})
.
Lastly, define

h(m,\eta,\delta,\beta,u,\ell,\varepsilon_{3})=\sqrt{\eta}\vee\sqrt{\frac{(\log\left(\left[\frac{\beta(u-\ell)}{\beta-1}\right]\vee 4\right)/\delta)}{m}}\vee\frac{1}{\sqrt{m}\varepsilon_{3}}.

For a positive integer
m
, let
[m]=\{1,\ldots,m\}
.

Corollary 1
.

Given
T\colon\mathcal{D}_{N}\to\mathbb{R}
and
m\in[N]
, suppose that
T(\mathbf{X}_{N}^{\prime(1)})
has variance
\sigma_{T,k}^{2}<\infty
, and
\ell<\inf_{i\in[m]}T(\mathbf{X}_{N}^{\prime(i)})<\sup_{i\in[m]}T(\mathbf{X}_{N}^{\prime(i)})<u
, and that for all
\eta\geq 0
,
4e^{-N}<\delta<1
,
\varepsilon_{1},\varepsilon_{2},\rho_{1},\rho_{2}>3/56
, and
\varepsilon_{3},\rho_{3}>0
, if
\beta,u,\ell\in\mathbb{R}
are such that Condition
3
holds for
F=G_{k}
, then, with probability at least
1-\delta,

|\tilde{T}(\mathbf{X}_{N}^{\prime})-T(F)|\lesssim|\mathrm{Bias}_{N,\eta}(T)|+\sigma_{T,k}h(m,\eta,\delta,\beta,u,\ell,\varepsilon_{3}),

and
|\tilde{T}(\mathbf{X}_{N}^{\prime})^{\prime}-T(F)|
is upper bounded (in the sense of
\lesssim
) by the sample quantity, but with
\varepsilon_{3}
replaced with
\sqrt{\rho_{3}}
.

Note that we do not necessarily need the restriction

\ell<\inf_{i\in[m]}T(\mathbf{X}_{N}^{\prime(i)})<\sup_{i\in[m]}T(\mathbf{X}_{N}^{\prime(i)})<u,

as this is stronger than what is required by Condition
2
, especially in light of Remark
3
.
However, this condition makes the theorem easier to read and, since the bounds can be loose in practice, this restriction is not a practical concern.

Corollary
1
yields two important insights.
First, the optimal choice of
k
depends on the bias of
T
.
Second, under contamination, the rate of convergence of
\tilde{T}
depends on the robustness of
T
.
Suppose that
\eta=0
.
Taking the privacy parameters,
\ell
,
u
, and
\beta
to be fixed, the rate of convergence is determined by two competing parameters,
|\mathrm{Bias}_{N,\eta}(T)|
and
\sigma_{T,k}/m^{1/2}
.
Provided that
\sigma_{T,k}=O(k^{-1/2})
, then
\sigma_{T,k}/m^{1/2}=O(N^{-1/2})
, which is the minimax optimal rate of convergence for many univariate problems.

By choosing a statistic
T
that has a variance
O(k^{-1/2})
, the error of subsample-and-aggregate is bounded by
|\mathrm{Bias}_{N,\eta}(T)|+O(n^{-1/2})
.
It remains then to select
k
large enough so that
|\mathrm{Bias}_{N,\eta}(T)|=O(N^{-1/2})
.
If
T
is unbiased, then any choice of
k
is sufficient for a convergence rate of
O(N^{-1/2})
.
If
T
is biased, then
k
has to be large enough so that the bias is negligible.
For instance, take
F=\mathcal{N}(0,\sigma^{2})
and
T
to be the maximum likelihood estimator of the variance of the normal distribution,
|\mathrm{Bias}_{N,\eta}(T)|=\sigma^{2}/k
and
\sigma_{T,k}\lesssim 1/\sqrt{k}
.
In this case, taking
k\gtrsim\sqrt{N}
results in a convergence rate of
O(N^{-1/2})
, which is the optimal convergence rate in the nonprivate setting.

For the second point, aside from the
\sqrt{\eta}
term resulting from the mean estimation, note that the level of contamination is reflected in a finite-sample verion of the
maximum bias
of the estimator
T
.
The finite-sample maximum bias at level
\eta
for
N
samples is the maximum distance
T_{i}(\mathbf{x}_{N}^{\prime})
can be moved by corrupting
\eta
-fraction of the points in the dataset
\mathbf{x}_{N}^{\prime}
,
(
Huber,, 1981
)
.
Namely, if the sample is contaminated, in general,
|\mathrm{Bias}_{N,\eta}(T)|\leq|\mathbb{E}(T_{i}(\mathbf{x}_{k}^{\prime}))-\theta|+\sup_{\mathbf{x}_{k}}|\mathbb{E}(T_{i}(\mathbf{x}_{k}^{\prime}))-\mathbb{E}(T_{i}(\mathbf{x}_{k}))|
.
This is the sum of the bias of
T
in the uncontaminated setting and the contamination bias of
T
for the dataset
\mathbf{x}_{k}^{\prime}
.
If contamination is suspected, it is necessary to select a statistic
T
that is “robust with high probability,” in the sense that for most datasets,
T
is not easily contaminated.
This is different from the stricter requirement of small global sensitivity, where it is required that
\sup_{\mathbf{x}_{k},\mathbf{x}_{k}^{\prime}}|T_{i}(\mathbf{x}_{k}^{\prime})-T_{i}(\mathbf{x}_{k})|
is bounded.
For example, the univariate median has infinite global sensitivity, but will generally be robust with high probability
(
Dwork and Lei,, 2009
;
Avella-Medina and Brunel,, 2019
;
Ramsay and Spicker,, 2023
)
.

We now turn to an application of the improved subsample-and-aggregate.
We consider the data of 2327 healthcare workers, whose K10 combined depression and anxiety scores were tracked throughout the course of the COVID-19 pandemic
(
Gutmanis et al.,, 2024
)
.
One of the aims of the study was to estimate the effects of time, season, and periods of high mitigation on K10 scores.
Given that this is longitudinal data, the authors applied a linear mixed effects model, specifically a random intercept model, to the log-transformed K10 scores.
We estimate the same model privately, using the proposed improved subsample-and-aggregate.
Specifically, we estimate the coefficients of time, winter, and mitigation level, as well as the intercept, the between subject variance, and the within subject variance.
We further compare the output to the original subsample-and-aggregate, and subsample-and-aggregate algorithms where the aggregate step uses one of several state-of-the-art private multivariate mean estimation methods: PRIME
Liu et al., 2021a ()
, the private Huber estimator
(
Yu et al.,, 2024
)
, or the Coinpress estimator
(
Biswas et al.,, 2020
)
.

c,c^{\prime}>0
such that

\displaystyle\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\phi_{\xi_{\zeta/4,X},\xi_{1-\zeta/4,X}}(X_{i})

\displaystyle\leq\mu+\mathcal{E}(4\zeta,X)

\displaystyle\hskip 20.00003pt+c\Bigg(\sigma\sqrt{\frac{\log(4/\delta)}{n}}+\frac{2\xi_{1-\zeta/4,\tilde{X}}\log(4/\delta)}{3n}

\displaystyle\hskip 80.00012pt+\frac{2\mathcal{E}(\zeta/4,X)\log(4/\delta)}{3n}\Bigg)

\displaystyle\leq\mu+c^{\prime}\mathcal{E}(\zeta/4,X)

\displaystyle\hskip 40.00006pt+c\left(\sigma\sqrt{\frac{\log(4/\delta)}{n}}+\frac{2\xi_{1-\zeta/4,\tilde{X}}\log(4/\delta)}{3n}\right).

Applying Chebyshev’s inequality yields that

\frac{\zeta}{4}=\Pr\left(\tilde{X}\geq\xi_{1-\zeta/4,\tilde{X}}\right)\leq\frac{\sigma^{2}}{\xi_{1-\zeta/4,\tilde{X}}^{2}}\implies\xi_{1-\zeta/4,\tilde{X}}\leq\frac{\sqrt{4}\sigma}{\sqrt{\zeta}}.

Since
\delta/4\geq e^{-n}
then
\log(4/\delta)\leq n
, and so
\sqrt{\log(4/\delta)/n}\geq\log(4/\delta)/n
.
Then, using the result from Chebyshev’s inequality, with probability at least
1-\delta/2
,

\displaystyle\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\phi_{\xi_{4\zeta,X},\xi_{1-\zeta/4,X}}(X_{i})-\mu

\displaystyle\lesssim\mathcal{E}(\zeta/4,X)+\sigma\sqrt{\frac{\log(4/\delta)}{n}}.

Lastly, we bound
\mathcal{E}(\zeta/4,X)
above.
Using the triangle inequality, Holder’s inequality and Chebyshev’s inequality,

\displaystyle|E((X-\xi_{1-a})\mathbbm{1}\left\{X>\xi_{1-a})\right\}|

\displaystyle\leq\mathbb{E}(|\tilde{X}\mathbbm{1}\left\{X>\xi_{1-a}\right\}|)+\mathbb{E}(|\xi_{1-a}|\mathbbm{1}\left\{X>\xi_{1-a}\right\})

\displaystyle\leq\sigma\Pr\left(X>\xi_{1-a}\right)^{1/2}+\sigma\sqrt{a}

\displaystyle\lesssim\sigma\sqrt{a}.

Similarly,
|E((X-\xi_{a})\mathbbm{1}\left\{X<\xi_{a})\right\}|\lesssim\sigma\sqrt{a}
.
Taken together, these two inequalities give
\mathcal{E}(\zeta/4,X)\lesssim\sigma\sqrt{\zeta}
.
Thus, with probability at least
1-\delta/2
,

\displaystyle\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\phi_{\xi_{4\zeta,X},\xi_{1-\zeta/4,X}}(X_{i})-\mu

\displaystyle\lesssim\sigma\sqrt{\zeta}+\sigma\sqrt{\frac{\log(4/\delta)}{n}}.

An analogous argument holds to give a similar lower bound on the same quantity.

Next, we consider

\left|\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\phi_{\xi_{4\zeta,X},\xi_{1-\zeta/4,X}}(X_{i})-\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\phi_{\xi_{4\zeta,X},\xi_{1-\zeta/4,X}}(X_{i}^{\prime})\right|,

following the argument in
Lugosi and Mendelson, (2021)
.
Using Chebyshev’s inequality, note that the maximal gap for a single term in the summation,

\left|\phi_{\xi_{4\zeta,X},\xi_{1-\zeta/4,X}}(X_{i})-\phi_{\xi_{4\zeta,X},\xi_{1-\zeta/4,X}}(X_{i}^{\prime})\right|\leq|\xi_{1-\zeta/4,X}|+|\xi_{4\zeta,X}|\lesssim\sigma/\sqrt{\zeta}.

Since
\phi_{\xi_{4\zeta,X},\xi_{1-\zeta/4,X}}(X_{i})=\phi_{\xi_{4\zeta,X},\xi_{1-\zeta/4,X}}(X_{i}^{\prime})
for all but
2\eta n
(or fewer) points, we get that

\left|\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\phi_{\xi_{4\zeta,X},\xi_{1-\zeta/4,X}}(X_{i})-\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\phi_{\xi_{4\zeta,X},\xi_{1-\zeta/4,X}}(X_{i}^{\prime})\right|\lesssim\eta\sigma/\sqrt{\zeta}\lesssim\sigma\sqrt{\zeta}.

The last inequality follows since, by assumption,
\eta\leq\zeta/16
.

Recall that

\tilde{\mu}=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\phi_{\tilde{\xi}_{\zeta},\tilde{\xi}_{1-\zeta}}(X_{i}^{\prime})+Z_{1}\frac{(\tilde{\xi}_{1-\zeta}-\tilde{\xi}_{\zeta})}{n\varepsilon_{3}}\leq\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\phi_{\xi_{4\zeta},\xi_{1-\zeta/4}}(X_{i}^{\prime})+Z_{1}\frac{(\tilde{\xi}_{1-\zeta}-\tilde{\xi}_{\zeta})}{n\varepsilon_{3}}.

Combining this with (
9
), the preceding tail argument, and the fact that
\Pr\left(Z_{1}\geq 1\right)=e^{-n}\leq\delta/4
, we have that with probability at least
1-\delta,

|\tilde{\mu}-\mu|\lesssim\sigma\sqrt{\zeta}+\sigma\sqrt{\frac{\log(4/\delta)}{n}}+\frac{Z_{1}(\tilde{\xi}_{1-\zeta}-\tilde{\xi}_{\zeta})}{n\varepsilon_{3}}\\
\lesssim\sigma\sqrt{\zeta}+\sigma\sqrt{\frac{\log(4/\delta)}{n}}+\frac{Z_{1}(\xi_{1-\zeta/4}-\xi_{\zeta/4})}{n\varepsilon_{3}}\\
\lesssim\sigma\sqrt{\zeta}+\sigma\sqrt{\frac{\log(4/\delta)}{n}}+\frac{\xi_{1-\zeta/4}-\xi_{\zeta/4}}{n\varepsilon_{3}}.

It suffices to bound the term
\xi_{1-\zeta/4}-\xi_{\zeta/4}
.
To this end, note that
\xi_{1-\zeta/4}-\xi_{\zeta/4}=\xi_{1-\zeta/4,X}-\xi_{\zeta/4,X}
.
In addition, we have that
\zeta\gtrsim 1/n
and so

\xi_{1-\zeta/4}-\xi_{\zeta/4}\leq 2(\xi_{1-\zeta/4,X}\vee(-\xi_{\zeta/4}))\leq 2(\xi_{1-1/4n,X}\vee(-\xi_{1/4n,X})).

Suppose that
\xi_{1-1/4n,X}>0
, then Chebyshev’s inequality implies that

\displaystyle 1/4n

\displaystyle=\Pr\left(X\geq\xi_{1-1/4n,X}\right)\leq\Pr\left(|X|\geq\xi_{1-1/4n,X}\right)\leq\frac{\sigma^{2}}{\xi_{1-1/4n,X}^{2}}

\displaystyle\hskip 60.00009pt\implies\xi_{1-1/4n,X}\lesssim\sigma\sqrt{n}.

In addition, if
\xi_{1-1/4n,X}<0
, then
\xi_{1-1/4n,X}\lesssim\sigma\sqrt{n}
.
Similarly, suppose that
\xi_{1/4n,X}<0
. Again, Chebyshev’s inequality implies that

\displaystyle 1/4n

\displaystyle=\Pr\left(X\leq\xi_{1/4n,X}\right)\leq\Pr\left(|X|\geq-\xi_{1/4n,X}\right)\leq\frac{\sigma^{2}}{\xi_{1/4n,X}^{2}}

\displaystyle\hskip 60.00009pt\implies-\xi_{1/4n,X}\lesssim\sigma\sqrt{n}.

Thus,
\xi_{1-\zeta/4}-\xi_{\zeta/4}\lesssim\sigma\sqrt{n}.
∎

Appendix D
Zero-concentrated DP quantiles (zCDP)

We now define a version of the estimator that satisfies a weaker notion of privacy called zero-concentrated DP.
The algorithm proceeds as follows:
Let
V,V_{1},V_{2},\ldots\sim\mathcal{N}(0,1)
.
Given
\beta>1
, a lower bound
\ell
, data set
\{Y_{i}\}_{i=1}^{n}
with empirical distribution
F_{n}
, and quantile
q>1/2
, we do the following:

1.

First, compute
\hat{q}=q+V/n\sqrt{\rho}
.

2.

While
F_{n}(\beta^{i}+\ell-1)+V_{i}/n\sqrt{\rho}<\hat{q}
, increase
i
by one at a time. Otherwise, stop.

3.

If we stop at
k
, then output
\beta^{k}+\ell-1
.

If instead
1/2>q\to 0
, we negate everything and take an upper bound as input instead of lower bound. The idea is that the upper bound is far from the minimum, etc.
Call these estimates
\tilde{\xi}_{q,\mathbb{Y}_{n}}
. We will omit
\mathbb{Y}_{n}
when it is obvious.

We first show that adding Gaussian noise with variance
1/n^{2}\rho
to the queries in the private quantile estimation results in
\rho
-zCDP.

Lemma 1
.

For all
0\leq q\leq 1
and
\rho_{1},\rho_{2}>0
,
\tilde{\xi}_{q}^{\prime}
satisfies
(\rho_{1}+\rho_{2})
-zCDP.

Proof.

Consider neighboring datasets
\mathbf{x}_{n},\mathbf{x}_{n}^{\prime}
with empirical distribution functions,
F_{n}
and
F_{n}^{\prime}
, respectively.
Let
{\rm D}_{\alpha}(P||Q)
denotes the
\alpha
-Rényi Divergence between two measures
P
and
Q
.
That is,

{\rm D}_{\alpha}(P||Q)=\frac{1}{\alpha-1}\log\int\left(\frac{dP}{dQ}\right)^{\alpha}dQ.

For two random variables on the same probability space,
X\sim P
and
Y\sim Q
, let
{\rm D}_{\alpha}(X||Y)={\rm D}_{\alpha}(P||Q)
.
The aim is to show that for any
\alpha>1
,
1/2<q<1
and any pair of adjacent databases
\mathbf{x}_{n},\mathbf{x}_{n}^{\prime}
, we have that

{\rm D}_{\alpha}(\tilde{\xi}_{q,\mathbf{x}_{n}}||\tilde{\xi}_{q,\mathbf{x}_{n}^{\prime}})\leq\alpha\rho.

To this end, let
K
be the random integer at which the process stops on the original dataset
\mathbf{x}_{n}
, and let
K^{\prime}
be the random integer at which the process stops on the neighboring dataset
\mathbf{x}_{n}^{\prime}
.
It follows by post-processing, (see Lemma 15 of
Bun and Steinke, (2016)
) that
{\rm D}_{\alpha}(\tilde{\xi}_{q,\mathbf{x}_{n}}||\tilde{\xi}_{q,\mathbf{x}_{n}^{\prime}})\leq{\rm D}_{\alpha}(K||K^{\prime})
, since
\tilde{\xi}_{q,\mathbf{x}_{n}^{\prime}}=\beta^{K}+\ell-1
.
It suffices to show that

{\rm D}_{\alpha}(K||K^{\prime})\leq\rho\alpha
for all
\alpha>1
.

To this end, let
M\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\text{d}}}{{=}}K
, let
\nu_{j}
be the joint distribution of the sequence of random variables
Z=(K,V_{1}/n\sqrt{\rho},\ldots,V_{j-1}/n\sqrt{\rho},V_{j+1}/n\sqrt{\rho},\ldots)
and let
\nu_{j}^{\prime}
be the distribution of
Z^{\prime}=(K^{\prime},V_{1}/n\sqrt{\rho},\ldots,V_{j-1}/n\sqrt{\rho},V_{j+1}/n\sqrt{\rho},\ldots)
.
Take
I
to be the function returning the first element, so that
I(Z)=K
and
I(Z^{\prime})=K^{\prime}
.
It follows by Lemma 15 of
Bun and Steinke, (2016)
(post-processing) that

{\rm D}_{\alpha}(K||K^{\prime})={\rm D}_{\alpha}(I(\nu_{j})||I(\nu_{j}^{\prime}))\leq{\rm D}_{\alpha}(\nu_{j}||\nu_{j}^{\prime}).

We now consider
{\rm D}_{\alpha}(\nu_{j}||\nu_{j}^{\prime})
for arbitrary
j
.
Let
Q_{j}
and
Q_{j}^{\prime}
denote the distribution of
K
and
K^{\prime}
conditional on
(V_{1}/n\sqrt{\rho},\ldots,V_{j-1}/n\sqrt{\rho},V_{j+1}/n\sqrt{\rho},\ldots)
.
Now, let
\mu_{j}
be the joint distribution of the sequence of random variables
(V_{1}/n\sqrt{\rho},\ldots,V_{j-1},V_{j+1}/n\sqrt{\rho},\ldots)
.
Next, we have that by Lemma 15 of
Bun and Steinke, (2016)
(composition),

{\rm D}_{\alpha}(\nu_{j}||\nu_{j}^{\prime})\leq\sup_{j\geq 0}[{\rm D}_{\alpha}(Q_{j}||Q_{j}^{\prime})+{\rm D}_{\alpha}(\mu_{j}||\mu_{j})]=\sup_{j\geq 0}{\rm D}_{\alpha}(Q_{j}||Q_{j}^{\prime}).

It then suffices to show that, for any
j\geq 0
, we have that
{\rm D}_{\alpha}(Q_{j}||Q_{j}^{\prime})\leq\alpha\rho
.
To this end, let
(v_{1}/n\sqrt{\rho},\ldots,v_{j-1}/n\sqrt{\rho},v_{j+1}/n\sqrt{\rho},\ldots)
be the realizations of the sequence of random variables
(V_{1}/n\sqrt{\rho},\ldots,V_{j-1}/n\sqrt{\rho},V_{j+1}/n\sqrt{\rho},\ldots)
.
Next, for any positive integer
k
, let
z_{k}=F_{n}(\beta^{k}+\ell-1)
,
\tau_{k}=\max_{i<k}[F_{n}(\beta^{i}+\ell-1)+v_{i}]
,
z_{k}^{\prime}=F^{\prime}_{n}(\beta^{k}+\ell-1)
and
\tau_{k}^{\prime}=\max_{i<k}[F_{n}^{\prime}(\beta^{i}+\ell-1)+v_{i}]
.
To prove the result, we first have that

\Pr\left(K=k\Big|\{V_{i}=v_{i}\}_{i=1}^{k-1},\{V_{i}=v_{i}\}_{i=k+1}^{\infty}\right)\\
=\Pr\left(\tau_{k}\leq\hat{q}<z_{k}+V_{k}/n\sqrt{\rho}\Big|\{V_{i}=v_{i}\}_{i=1}^{k-1},\{V_{i}=v_{i}\}_{i=k+1}^{\infty}\right)\\
=\Pr\left(\tau_{k}\leq\hat{q}<z_{k}+V_{k}/n\sqrt{\rho}\Big|\{V_{i}=v_{i}\}_{i=1}^{k-1}\right)\coloneqq b_{k}.

Next, letting
\varphi(x)
be the standard normal density,
q^{\prime}(x)=q+x/n\sqrt{\rho}+\tau_{k}^{\prime}-\tau_{k}
and

\kappa(x)=x+n\sqrt{\rho}(\tau_{k}^{\prime}-\tau_{k}+z_{k}-z_{k}^{\prime}),

by definition, it holds that

\displaystyle b_{k}

\displaystyle=\int_{\mathbb{R}}\int_{\mathbb{R}}\mathbbm{1}\left\{\tau_{k}<q+x/n\sqrt{\rho}<z_{k}+y/n\sqrt{\rho}\right\}\varphi(x)\varphi(y)dxdy.

In the above integral, it is helpful to note that
x
corresponds to the random variable
V
, and
y
corresponds to the random variable
V_{k}
. Moreover, the indicator depends on
(V_{1},\dots,V_{k-1})
only through
\tau_{k}
. Since
V
and
V_{k}
are both independent of
(V_{1},\dots,V_{k-1})
, the conditional densities are equal to the marginal densities.
Continuing,

\displaystyle b_{k}

\displaystyle=\int_{\mathbb{R}}\int_{\mathbb{R}}\mathbbm{1}\left\{\tau_{k}<q+x/n\sqrt{\rho}<z_{k}+y/n\sqrt{\rho}\right\}\varphi(x)\varphi(y)dxdy

\displaystyle=\int_{\mathbb{R}}\int_{\mathbb{R}}\mathbbm{1}\left\{\tau_{k}<q^{\prime}(x)-\tau_{k}^{\prime}+\tau_{k}<\kappa(y)/n\sqrt{\rho}-\tau_{k}^{\prime}+\tau_{k}+z_{k}^{\prime}\right\}\varphi(x)\varphi(y)dxdy

\displaystyle=\int_{\mathbb{R}}\int_{\mathbb{R}}\mathbbm{1}\left\{\tau_{k}^{\prime}<q^{\prime}(x)<\kappa(y)/n\sqrt{\rho}+z_{k}^{\prime}\right\}\varphi(x)\varphi(y)dxdy.

Next, we make the change of variable
u_{1}=\kappa(y)/n\sqrt{\rho}
and
u_{2}\coloneqq u_{2}(x)=q^{\prime}(x)-q
.

\displaystyle b_{k}

\displaystyle=(n^{2}\rho)^{-1}\int_{\mathbb{R}}\int_{\mathbb{R}}\mathbbm{1}\left\{\tau_{k}^{\prime}<u_{2}+q<u_{1}+z_{k}^{\prime}-\tau_{k}^{\prime}\right\}\varphi(n\sqrt{\rho}(u_{2}+\tau_{k}-\tau_{k}^{\prime}))

\displaystyle\hskip 100.00015pt\times\varphi(n\sqrt{\rho}(u_{1}+\tau_{k}-z_{k}+z_{k}^{\prime}-\tau_{k}^{\prime}))du_{1}du_{2}

\displaystyle=\Pr\left(\tau_{k}^{\prime}<U_{2}+q<U_{1}+z_{k}^{\prime}\right)

\displaystyle=\Pr\left(\tau_{k}^{\prime}<U_{3}+q<z_{k}^{\prime}\right)\coloneqq h_{k}(U_{3}),

where
U_{1}\sim\mathcal{N}(\tau_{k}^{\prime}-\tau_{k}-z_{k}^{\prime}+z_{k},(n^{2}\rho)^{-1}),U_{2}\sim\mathcal{N}(\tau_{k}^{\prime}-\tau_{k},(n^{2}\rho)^{-1})
and
U_{3}=U_{2}-U_{1}
, with
U_{1}\perp U_{2}
.
By definition, i.e., the properties of the normal distribution, we have that
U_{3}\sim\mathcal{N}(\tau_{k}^{\prime}-\tau_{k}-\tau_{k}^{\prime}+\tau_{k}+z_{k}^{\prime}-z_{k},2(n^{2}\rho)^{-1})=\mathcal{N}(z_{k}^{\prime}-z_{k},2(n^{2}\rho)^{-1})
.
In addition, observe that by definition,

\displaystyle\Pr\left(K^{\prime}=k\Big|\{V_{i}=v_{i}\}_{i=1}^{k-1},\{V_{i}=v_{i}\}_{i=k+1}^{\infty}\right)

\displaystyle\hskip 60.00009pt=\Pr\left(\tau_{k}^{\prime}\leq\hat{q}<z_{k}^{\prime}+V_{k}/n\sqrt{\rho}\Big|\{V_{i}=v_{i}\}_{i=1}^{k-1},\{V_{i}=v_{i}\}_{i=k+1}^{\infty}\right)

\displaystyle\hskip 60.00009pt=h_{k}((n\sqrt{\rho})^{-1}(V-V_{j})).

Using these facts yields that

\displaystyle{\rm D}_{\alpha}(Q_{j}||Q_{j}^{\prime})

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\alpha-1}\sup_{j>0}\mathbb{E}_{M}\left[\left(\frac{\Pr\left(K=M\Big|\{V_{i}=v_{i}\}_{i=1}^{j-1},\{V_{i}=v_{i}\}_{i=j+1}^{\infty}\right)}{\Pr\left(K^{\prime}=M\Big|\{V_{i}=v_{i}\}_{i=1}^{j-1},\{V_{i}=v_{i}\}_{i=j+1}^{\infty}\right)}\right)^{\alpha-1}\right]

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\alpha-1}\sup_{j>0}\mathbb{E}_{M}\left[\left(\frac{h_{j}(U_{3})}{h_{j}((n\sqrt{\rho})^{-1}(V-V_{j}))}\right)^{\alpha-1}\right]

\displaystyle\leq\frac{1}{\alpha-1}\sup_{j>0}\mathbb{E}_{M}\left[\left(\frac{U_{3}}{(n\sqrt{\rho})^{-1}(V-V_{j})}\right)^{\alpha-1}\right]

\displaystyle=\alpha 2\rho n^{2}\mathbb{E}(U_{3})^{2}/2

\displaystyle=\alpha\rho n^{2}\mathbb{E}(U_{3})^{2},

where the second last line uses the fact that the Rényi divergence between two normal distributions with variance
\sigma^{2}
and means
\mu_{1},\mu_{2}
is given by
\frac{1}{2}\alpha(\mu_{1}-\mu_{2})^{2}/2\sigma^{2}.

Now, we just need to bound the expectation
\mathbb{E}(U_{3})
, from which it follows by definition that
-1/n<\mathbb{E}(U_{3})<1/n
.
As a result, we have that
{\rm D}_{\alpha}(Q_{j}||Q_{j}^{\prime})\leq\alpha\rho,
which completes the proof.
∎

Appendix E
Proving finite sample bounds for private quantiles

The following lemma gives a concentration result related to the purely differentially private quantile computed from any sample.

Lemma 2
.

Given the corrupted sample
Y_{1}^{\prime},\ldots,Y_{n}^{\prime}
, and
t>0
, we have that for all
1/2<q\leq 1
,
\ell\leq\hat{\xi}_{q}
, and
1<\beta\leq\left.(\hat{\xi}_{q+t}-\ell+1)\right/(\hat{\xi}_{q-t}-\ell+1)
it holds that

\displaystyle\Pr\left(|q-F_{n}(\tilde{\xi}_{q})|>t\right)

\displaystyle\leq\left(\frac{\log(\hat{\xi}_{q-t}+1-\ell)}{\log\beta}+1\right)\exp\left(-nt(\varepsilon_{1}\wedge\varepsilon_{2})\right),

and for all
0<q\leq 1/2
,
u\geq\hat{\xi}_{q}
, and
1<\beta\leq\left.(u-\hat{\xi}_{q-t}+1)\right/(u-\hat{\xi}_{q+t}+1)
it holds that

\displaystyle\Pr\left(|q-F_{n}(\tilde{\xi}_{q})|>t\right)

\displaystyle\leq\left(\frac{\log(u-\hat{\xi}_{q+t}+1)}{\log\beta}+1\right)\exp\left(-nt(\varepsilon_{1}+\varepsilon_{2})\right).

Proof of Lemma
2
.

We start by demonstrating the first inequality.
Let
\hat{q}=q+V/n\varepsilon_{1}\geq q
.
Observe that

\Pr\left(|q-F_{n}(\tilde{\xi}_{q})|>t\right)=\Pr\left(q-F_{n}(\tilde{\xi}_{q})>t\right)+\Pr\left(F_{n}(\tilde{\xi}_{q})-q>t\right)\coloneqq I+II.

First, we must ensure that there is some
k\in\mathbb{N}
such that
F_{n}(\beta^{k}-1+\ell)
that is within
t
of
q
.
Let
k
be the integer at which the process terminates.
We have that

\displaystyle q-F_{n}(\tilde{\xi}_{q})\geq t

\displaystyle\iff\beta^{k}-1+\ell\leq\hat{\xi}_{q-t}\iff k\leq\frac{\log(\hat{\xi}_{q-t}-\ell+1)}{\log\beta}\coloneqq k_{1}

\displaystyle F_{n}(\tilde{\xi}_{q})-q\geq t

\displaystyle\iff\beta^{k}-1+\ell\geq\hat{\xi}_{q+t}\iff k\geq\frac{\log(\hat{\xi}_{q+t}-\ell+1)}{\log\beta}\coloneqq k_{2}.

Thus, as long as there is an integer between
k_{1}
and
k_{2}
, there is a valid
k\in\mathbb{N}
. This is equivalent to saying
1\leq k_{2}-k_{1}
, giving

\displaystyle 1

\displaystyle\leq\frac{\log(\hat{\xi}_{q+t}-\ell+1)}{\log\beta}-\frac{\log(\hat{\xi}_{q-t}-\ell+1)}{\log\beta}\implies\beta\leq\frac{\hat{\xi}_{q+t}-\ell+1}{\hat{\xi}_{q-t}-\ell+1}.

This holds by assumption, and so a valid
k
must exist.
We now consider
I
.

Using the definition of
k
, the fact that
V
and
\{V_{i}\}_{i=1}^{\infty}
are standard exponential random variables and a union bound, it holds that

\displaystyle\Pr\left(k<k_{1}\right)

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{i=1}^{k_{1}}\Pr\left(V_{i}/n\varepsilon_{2}\geq\hat{q}-F_{n}(\beta^{i}+\ell-1)\right)

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{i=1}^{k_{1}}\Pr\left(V_{i}/n\varepsilon_{2}\geq\hat{q}-F_{n}(\beta^{k_{1}}+\ell-1)\right)

\displaystyle=\sum_{i=1}^{k_{1}}\Pr\left(V_{i}/n\varepsilon_{2}\geq q+V/n\epsilon_{1}-F_{n}((\hat{\xi}_{q-t}-\ell+1)+\ell-1)\right)

\displaystyle\leq k_{1}\Pr\left(V_{k_{1}}-V\geq tn(\varepsilon_{1}\wedge\varepsilon_{2})\right)

\displaystyle=\frac{\log(\hat{\xi}_{q-t}+1-\ell)}{2\log\beta}\exp\left(-nt(\varepsilon_{1}\wedge\varepsilon_{2})\right).

Now, we consider
II
.
Observe that

\displaystyle II=\Pr\left(k>k_{2}\right)

\displaystyle=\prod_{i=1}^{k_{2}}\Pr\left(V_{i}/n\varepsilon_{2}+F_{n}(\beta^{i}+\ell-1)\leq\hat{q}\right)

\displaystyle\leq\Pr\left(V_{i}/n\varepsilon_{2}+F_{n}(\beta^{k_{2}}+\ell-1)\leq\hat{q}\right)

\displaystyle=\Pr\left(V_{i}/n\varepsilon_{2}+F_{n}((\hat{\xi}_{q+t}-\ell+1)+\ell-1)\leq q+V/n\epsilon_{1}\right)

\displaystyle\leq\Pr\left(tn(\varepsilon_{2}\wedge\varepsilon_{2})\leq V-V_{i}\right)

\displaystyle\leq\frac{1}{2}\exp\left(-nt(\varepsilon_{1}\wedge\varepsilon_{2})\right).

Then, for the second inequality, since
0<q\leq\frac{1}{2}
, the algorithm is run with
\ell=-u
, quantile
1-q
, and each data point negated, defining
Y=-X
. Then
\tilde{\xi}_{q,X}=-\tilde{\xi}_{q^{\prime},Y}
,
\hat{\xi}_{q,X}=-\hat{\xi}_{q^{\prime},Y}
, and
F_{n,X}(k)=1-F_{n,Y}(-k)
.

Note that
0<q\leq\frac{1}{2}\iff\frac{1}{2}<q^{\prime}\leq 1
,
u\geq\hat{\xi}_{q,X}\iff\ell\leq\hat{\xi}_{q^{\prime},Y}
, and

1\leq\beta\leq\frac{u-\hat{\xi}_{q-t,X}+1}{u-\hat{\xi}_{q+t,X}+1}\iff 1\leq\beta\leq\frac{-\ell+\hat{\xi}_{1-(q-t),Y}+1}{-\ell+\hat{\xi}_{1-(q+t),Y}+1}\\
\iff 1\leq\beta\leq\frac{\hat{\xi}_{q^{\prime}+t,Y}-\ell+1}{\hat{\xi}_{q^{\prime}-t,Y}-\ell+1}.

Thus, the first inequality can be applied with
\ell=-u
,
q^{\prime}
, and
Y
. This gives

P(|q^{\prime}-F_{n,Y}(\tilde{\xi}_{q^{\prime}-t,Y})|>t)\leq\left(\frac{\log(\hat{\xi}_{q^{\prime},Y}+1-\ell)}{\log(\beta)}+1\right)\exp\left(-nt(\epsilon_{1}\wedge\epsilon_{2})\right).

Note that

|q^{\prime}-F_{n,Y}(\tilde{\xi}_{q^{\prime},Y})|=|1-q-(1-F_{n,X}(-\tilde{\xi}_{q^{\prime},Y}))|=|F_{n,X}(\tilde{\xi}_{q,X})-q|.

For the upper bound, consider that

\frac{\log(\hat{\xi}_{q^{\prime},Y}+1-\ell)}{\log(\beta)}=\frac{\log(-\hat{\xi}_{1-(1-q-t),X}+1+u)}{\log(\beta)}=\frac{\log(u-\hat{\xi}_{q+t,X}+1)}{\log(\beta)}.

This completes the proof.
∎

The following lemma gives a concentration result related to the zero-concentrated differentially private quantile computed from any sample.

Lemma 3
.

Given the corrupted sample
Y_{1}^{\prime},\ldots,Y_{n}^{\prime}
, and
t>0
, we have that for all
1/2<q\leq 1
,
\ell\leq\hat{\xi}_{q}
, and
1<\beta\leq(\hat{\xi}_{q+t}-\ell+1)/(\hat{\xi}_{q-t}-\ell+1)

Appendix G
Error in Lugosi and Mendelson (2021)

Here, we demonstrate that there is an error in the proof of Theorem 1 of
Lugosi and Mendelson, (2021)
.
Namely, one step uses the inequalities

\mathbb{E}\phi_{\xi_{2\zeta,\tilde{X}},\xi_{1-\zeta/2\tilde{X}}}(X)\leq\mathbb{E}(\tilde{X}\mathbbm{1}\left\{X\geq\xi_{1-\zeta/2,X}\right\}),

and

\mathbb{E}\phi_{\xi_{2\zeta,\tilde{X}},\xi_{1-\zeta/2,X}}(X)\geq-\mathbb{E}(\tilde{X}\mathbbm{1}\left\{X\leq\xi_{2\zeta,X}\right\}),

which do not hold, see page 400 and 401 of
Lugosi and Mendelson, (2021)
.
We show they do not hold by counterexample.
For the remainder of this section, we use the notation of
Lugosi and Mendelson, (2021)
.

G.1
The lower bound

Using the notation in the paper, on page 401 at the very top, it states

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\phi_{\mu+Q_{2\epsilon}(\overline{X}),\mu+Q_{1-\epsilon/2}(\overline{X})}(X)\geq\mu-\mathbb{E}[\overline{X}\mathbbm{1}_{\overline{X}\leq Q_{2\epsilon}(\overline{X})}].

This inequality does not hold.
Observe that, using the fact that
\mathbb{E}(\overline{X})=0
,

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\phi_{\mu+Q_{2\epsilon}(\overline{X}),\mu+Q_{1-\epsilon/2}(\overline{X})}(X)-\mu

\displaystyle=Q_{2\epsilon}(\overline{X})\mathbb{E}(\mathbbm{1}_{\overline{X}\leq Q_{2\epsilon}(\overline{X})})+Q_{1-\epsilon/2}(\overline{X})\mathbb{E}(\mathbbm{1}_{\overline{X}\geq Q_{1-\epsilon/2}(\overline{X})})

\displaystyle\hskip 27.74982pt+\mathbb{E}(\overline{X}(1-\mathbbm{1}_{\overline{X}\geq Q_{1-\epsilon/2}(\overline{X})}-\mathbbm{1}_{\overline{X}\leq Q_{2\epsilon}(\overline{X})}))

\displaystyle=Q_{2\epsilon}(\overline{X})\mathbb{E}(\mathbbm{1}_{\overline{X}\leq Q_{2\epsilon}(\overline{X})})+Q_{1-\epsilon/2}(\overline{X})\mathbb{E}(\mathbbm{1}_{\overline{X}\geq Q_{1-\epsilon/2}(\overline{X})})

\displaystyle\hskip 27.74982pt-\mathbb{E}(\overline{X}\mathbbm{1}_{\overline{X}\geq Q_{1-\epsilon/2}(\overline{X})})-\mathbb{E}(\overline{X}\mathbbm{1}_{\overline{X}\leq Q_{2\epsilon}(\overline{X})})

\displaystyle=-\mathbb{E}(\overline{X}\mathbbm{1}_{\overline{X}\leq Q_{2\epsilon}(\overline{X})})+Q_{2\epsilon}(\overline{X})\mathbb{E}(\mathbbm{1}_{\overline{X}\leq Q_{2\epsilon}(\overline{X})})

\displaystyle\hskip 27.74982pt+\mathbb{E}((Q_{1-\epsilon/2}(\overline{X})-\overline{X})\mathbbm{1}_{\overline{X}\geq Q_{1-\epsilon/2}(\overline{X})}).

It then suffices to find some
F
such that

\displaystyle Q_{2\epsilon}(\overline{X})\mathbb{E}(\mathbbm{1}_{\overline{X}\leq Q_{2\epsilon}(\overline{X})})+\mathbb{E}((Q_{1-\epsilon/2}(\overline{X})-\overline{X})\mathbbm{1}_{\overline{X}\geq Q_{1-\epsilon/2}(\overline{X})})<0.

The term
\mathbb{E}((Q_{1-\epsilon/2}(\overline{X})-\overline{X})\mathbbm{1}_{\overline{X}\geq Q_{1-\epsilon/2}(\overline{X})})\leq 0
for all
F
.
Then, for example, standard normal distribution has
Q_{2\epsilon}(\overline{X})\mathbb{E}(\mathbbm{1}_{\overline{X}\leq Q_{2\epsilon}(\overline{X})})<0
, so the bound cannot hold in general.

G.2
The upper bound

We now give a counterexample to

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\phi_{\mu+Q_{2\epsilon}(\overline{X}),\mu+Q_{1-\epsilon/2}(\overline{X})}(X)\leq\mu+\mathbb{E}[\overline{X}\mathbbm{1}_{\overline{X}>Q_{1-\epsilon/2}(\overline{X})}],

(10)

for all
\epsilon<1/2
,
E(\overline{X})=0
and
F
such that
Var(\overline{X})<\infty
.
Consider a random variable
\overline{X}\sim F
, where
F
is a discrete distribution with four points,
x_{1}<x_{2}<0<x_{3}<x_{4}
.
Suppose that
\Pr\left(X=x_{i}\right)=p_{i}
for

and suppose that
\overline{X}
has mean 0:
\mathbb{E}(\overline{X})=\sum_{i=1}^{4}x_{i}p_{i}=0
.
If
x_{i}
are finite, then the variance of
\overline{X}
is finite.
Therefore,
F
is in the class of considered distributions. (Continuous analogues of this example can be derived, by placing concentrated density in a neighbourhood of
x_{i}
for each
x_{i}

First, for
p_{1}<2\epsilon<p_{2}+p_{1}
and
p_{1}+p_{2}<1-\epsilon/2\iff\epsilon<2(p_{4}+p_{3})
, it holds that

\mathbb{E}\phi_{\mu+Q_{2\epsilon}(\overline{X}),\mu+Q_{1-\epsilon/2}(\overline{X})}(\overline{X})=(p_{1}+p_{2})x_{2}+(p_{3}+p_{4})x_{3},

To prove that (

, we must show that
((p_{1}+p_{2})x_{2}+(p_{3}+p_{4})x_{3})>x_{4}p_{4}
for appropriate
p_{i}
and

.
So we just need a solution to the set of constraints given by:

\displaystyle 0<\epsilon<1/2,\ \epsilon<2(p_{4}+p_{3}),\ p_{1}<2\epsilon<p_{2}+p_{1},\ x_{1}<x_{2}<0<x_{3}<x_{4},

\displaystyle p^{\prime}1_{4}=1,\ x^{\prime}p=0,\ 0<p_{i}<1,\ ((p_{1}+p_{2})x_{2}+(p_{3}+p_{4})x_{3})>x_{4}p_{4}.

Taking
p_{1}=0.01
,
p_{2}=0.6
,
p_{3}=0.38
,

,

,
x_{1}=-57.1
,
x_{2}=-1
,
x_{3}=3
,

gives a solution.
You can get a similar counterexample taking

to be a continuous distribution which is a mixture of uniform densities on the neighborhoods
x_{i}\pm\delta

, with weights
p_{1},\ldots,p_{4}
. (You must also adjust
\epsilon
by
\delta
).

Appendix H
Skewed distributions and trimmed means

Here, we show that the non-private trimmed mean is inconsistent when
m/n\to p>0
as
n\to\infty
and the distribution is exponential. It follows by Slutsky’s theorem that the private trimmed mean is also inconsistent. (The proof can easily be extended to any skewed distribution, but it is simpler to present a concrete case.)

Lemma 4
.

Suppose that
\eta=0
and
F
is the standard exponential distribution. Then, when
m/n\to p>0
as
n\to\infty
, the non-private trimmed mean is inconsistent.

We have that, by the law of large numbers, the (non-private) trimmed mean converges in probability to
\mathbb{E}(X|X\in[-\log(1-p),-\log(p)])
as
n\to\infty
, where
X\sim Exp(1)
.
Observe that for
0<a<b<\infty
, it holds that

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}(X|X\in[a,b])

\displaystyle=\int_{a}^{b}xe^{-x}dx

\displaystyle=-xe^{-x}|_{a}^{b}+\int_{a}^{b}e^{-x}dx

\displaystyle=-be^{-b}+ae^{-a}-e^{-b}+e^{-a}

\displaystyle=(a+1)e^{-a}-(b+1)e^{-b}.

It follows that

\mathbb{E}(X|X\in[-\log(1-p),-\log(p)])=(1-\log(1-p))(1-p)-(1\log p)p\neq 1.

Therefore, for any non-vanishing clipping parameter
m=\lfloor pn\rfloor
, the trimmed mean is inconsistent. It follows by Slutsky’s theorem that the private trimmed mean is also inconsistent.
