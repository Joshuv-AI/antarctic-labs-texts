Deep Learning for Markov Chains: Lyapunov Functions, Poisson’s Equation, and Stationary Distributions
Yanlin Qu, Jose Blanchet, Peter Glynn

Abstract
Lyapunov functions are fundamental to establishing the stability of Markovian models, yet their construction typically demands substantial creativity and analytical effort. In this paper, we show that deep learning can automate this process by training neural networks to satisfy integral equations derived from first-transition analysis. Beyond stability analysis, our approach can be adapted to solve Poisson’s equation and estimate stationary distributions. While neural networks are inherently function approximators on compact domains, it turns out that our approach remains effective when applied to Markov chains on non‑compact state spaces. We demonstrate the effectiveness of this methodology through several examples from queueing theory and beyond.

Introduction

The use of Lyapunov functions to settle stability questions in the setting of Markovian queues has become a standard tool within the research community. However, the construction of appropriate Lyapunov functions to determine the stability of many models remains challenging, and settling stability questions via the use of this method requires great creativity and insight in order to build suitable Lyapunov functions; see, for example, Chapter 16.4 of
Meyn and Tweedie 2012
and Chapter 3.2 of
Dai and Harrison 2020
.

In this paper, we show how deep learning can be used to numerically compute Lyapunov functions. In particular, given a Markov chain
X=(X_{n}:n\geq 0)
living on state space
\mathcal{S}\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{d}
with one-step transition kernel
P=(P(x,dy):x,y\in\mathcal{S})
, the typical Lyapunov function used to settle stability involves a non-negative function
v=(v(x):x\in\mathcal{S})
satisfying the inequality

(Pv)(x)\leq v(x)-1,

(1.1)

for
x\in A^{c}
and some suitable set
A
, where
(Ph)(x)=\int_{\mathcal{S}}h(y)P(x,dy)
for a generic function
h
; see Chapter 11.3 of
Meyn and Tweedie 2012
. The function
v
is then known to provide an upper bound on
\mathbb{E}_{x}T_{A}
, where
\mathbb{E}_{x}
is the expectation on the path-space of
X
conditioned on
X_{0}=x
, and
T_{A}=\inf\{n\geq 0:X_{n}\in A\}
. In fact, our deep learning algorithm computes the minimal non-negative function
v
satisfying (
1.1
), namely the function
v^{*}(x)=\mathbb{E}_{x}T_{A}
that satisfies (
1.1
) with equality.

More generally, we leverage deep learning to globally compute expectation functions of the form

u^{*}(x)=\mathbb{E}_{x}\left[\sum_{k=0}^{T}\exp\left(-\sum_{j=0}^{k-1}\beta(X_{j})\right)r(X_{k})\right],

(1.2)

for
r\geq 0
and
x\in C
, where
T=\inf\{n\geq 0:X_{n}\in C^{c}\}
. Note that expected hitting times, exit probabilities, and infinite horizon expected discounted rewards (obtained by making
C^{c}
the empty set) are all special cases of (
1.2
).
By conditioning on the first transition of the Markov chain (i.e. using
first-transition analysis
(FTA)), these functions can be characterized by integral equations.

We train neural networks to satisfy these equations by minimizing the integrated squared residual error with respect to the network parameters using stochastic gradient descent (SGD). To obtain an unbiased gradient estimator, we use the double-sampling trick that utilizes two independent and identically distributed (i.i.d.) copies of the first transition; see
Goda and Kitade 2023
. We have used this idea in related work of ours on computing rates of convergence to equilibrium for Markov chains; see
Qu et al. 2024
. This idea has also been used to compute splitting probabilities and to predict rare events without simulating long sample paths, thereby achieving great efficiency; see
Strahan et al. 2023
;
Cheng and Weare 2024
and the references therein. In the context of reinforcement learning, the same idea can be used to perform policy evaluation, leading to the
residual gradient algorithm
; see
Baird 1995
;
Baird and Moore 1998
;
Sutton and Barto 2018
(Chapter 11.5).

Our main contribution in this paper is to expose the use of deep learning to the queueing and applied probability communities, as a means of solving the linear integral equations that arise from Markov chain models. In the course of explaining these ideas, we describe how deep learning can be applied to compute global approximations to the solution of FTA equations arising within the general setting of (
1.2
). As far as we are aware, this generalization is new. In particular, as explained above, our proposed approach therefore provides a vehicle for deep-learning-based numerical construction of Lyapunov functions. Hence, deep learning can potentially play a role in settling the types of stability questions that often arise in Markov chain modeling applications.

In addition to the integral equations that arise within the setting of FTA expectations and probabilities, this paper also contributes suitable deep-learning-based algorithms for solving the integral equations that arise within the context of stationary distribution computation and also that of Poisson’s equation. To be specific, when
X
is positive recurrent with a unique stationary distribution
\pi
,
Poisson’s equation
involves computing the solution
u^{*}
to the linear integral equation given by

u(x)-(Pu)(x)=r(x)-\pi r,

(1.3)

for
x\in\mathcal{S}
, where
\pi r=\int_{\mathcal{S}}r(y)\pi(dy)
is the centering constant. Since this constant is typically unknown, the approach used to solve (
1.2
) must be adapted to address this issue; this contribution is also new. We further note that the unknown probability measure
\pi
arising in the setting of stationary distribution computation is a measure rather than a function (as in (
1.2
)) and hence the deep learning algorithm for computing
\pi
also requires a slight modification to the FTA deep learning approach we propose. Poisson’s equation plays a key role in the central limit theorem for Markov chains, and in other calculations related to additive functionals of Markov chains; see, for example,
Maigret 1978
and
Kurtz 1981
.

The theory and practice of deep-learning-based function approximation requires that the domain of the functions being approximated be compact. We show how the deep learning method can be applied to Markov chains on non-compact state spaces, despite the fact that neural networks are inherently function approximators on compact domains. An additional contribution of this paper is our careful analysis of the sample complexity for the deep learning approximations to the solutions to the equations arising in the computation of FTA expectations and probabilities. In particular, Theorem
3
shows that, if the target function
u^{*}
on
K\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{d}
is
s
-Hölder continuous, then the mean squared error of the empirical risk minimizer (ERM) over clipped ReLU networks scales as
n^{-\frac{2s}{2s+d}}
up to logarithmic factors, with an explicit conditioning factor reflecting the implicit fixed-point contraction associated with the linear integral equation arising in FTA settings. This connects the smoothness
s
and dimension
d
of the problem to the number of samples required to achieve a prescribed accuracy.

This paper is organized as follows: In Section
2
, we apply deep learning to compute FTA-type quantities (
1.2
), including Lyapunov functions. In Section
3
, we develop the corresponding finite-sample guarantee. In Section
4
, we apply deep learning to solve Poisson’s equation (
1.3
), without computing the centering constant.
In Section
5
, we apply deep learning to estimate stationary distributions.
In Section
6
, we extend our approach to non-compact state spaces. In Section
7
, we demonstrate the effectiveness of our approach through several examples from queueing theory and beyond.

Neural networks are flexible function approximators that can be trained to satisfy functional equations such as (

If

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[u_{\theta}(X_{0})-\Gamma(X_{0},X_{1})-e^{-\beta(X_{0})}u_{\theta}(X_{1})I(X_{1}\in C)\right]

Differentiating the expression above yields a simple unbiased gradient estimator

\displaystyle\hat{l}^{\prime}(\theta)=

\displaystyle\left[u_{\theta}(X_{0})-\Gamma(X_{0},X_{1})-e^{-\beta(X_{0})}u_{\theta}(X_{1})I(X_{1}\in C)\right]

Suppose that we use SGD to minimize

We then present the theorem and discuss the roles of network architecture and smoothness. The proof is in Section

Assumption 4

(Geometry, sampling, discount, and domination)
.

(i)

(ii)

(iii)

(Conditional moment of one–step contribution) There exists a finite constant

(iv)

(Killed–kernel domination) There exists

(v)

(Second–to–first moment control for the weight) There exists a finite constant

Item (iv) is a key quantitative ingredient: it says that the mass of

\ell_{u}(r,v,w,y):=u(r)-v-wu(y)

and the product loss

\begin{gathered}h_{u}(r,v,w,y,\tilde{v},\tilde{w},\tilde{y}):=\ell_{u}(r,v,w,y)\,\ell_{u}(r,\tilde{v},\tilde{w},\tilde{y}),\\
\widehat{\mathcal{L}}_{n}(u):=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}h_{u}(\chi_{i}),\;\;\mathcal{L}(u):=\mathbb{E}[h_{u}(\chi_{i})].\end{gathered}

(3.3)

Let
\hat{u}_{n}\in\arg\min_{u\in\mathcal{F}_{W,L,B}}\widehat{\mathcal{L}}_{n}(u)
be an empirical risk minimizer (ERM). The minimizer exists provided the parameters are restricted within a compact set, which we assume in our development.

Why the product loss?

Conditioning on
R_{i}
,
(V_{i},W_{i},Y_{i})
and
(\tilde{V}_{i},\tilde{W}_{i},\tilde{Y}_{i})
are independent, so
\mathcal{L}(u)=\mathbb{E}[\mathbb{E}[\ell_{u}(R_{i},V_{i},W_{i},Y_{i})|R_{i})]^{2}]
. Since
u^{*}
solves (
3.1
),
\mathbb{E}[\ell_{u^{*}}(R_{i},V_{i},W_{i},Y_{i})|R_{i}]\equiv 0
and hence

\mathcal{L}(u)=\|(I-H)(u-u^{*})\|_{2}^{2}.

(3.4)

Thus, minimizing
\mathcal{L}
amounts to minimizing the fixed–point residual in
L^{2}(\nu)
; the product form in (
3.3
) yields an unbiased gradient estimator (Algorithm
1
).

We now state the finite–sample guarantee. It displays the usual bias–variance tradeoff: a smoothness–driven approximation term and a localized–complexity estimation term. The fixed–point conditioning enters only through
\kappa
.

Theorem 3

(Finite–sample guarantee; minimax rate)
.

Suppose Assumptions
4
–
6
hold. There exists a constant
c_{1}<\infty
depending only on
B
,
\|\tilde{r}\|_{\infty}
,
\|u^{*}\|_{\infty}
,
\sigma_{W}
,
C_{W}
,
\Lambda
such that, for all
\delta\in(0,1)
, with probability at least
1-\delta
,

\|\hat{u}_{n}-u^{*}\|_{L^{2}(\nu)}^{2}\leq\frac{c_{1}}{\kappa^{2}}\,\Bigg(\underbrace{W^{-2s/d}}_{\text{approximation}}+\underbrace{\frac{W\,L\,\log W\cdot\log n+\log(1/\delta)}{n}}_{\text{estimation}}\Bigg).

(3.5)

In particular, choosing
L=[\log n]
and
W=[n^{\frac{d}{2s+d}}]
gives (up to logarithmic factors)

\|\hat{u}_{n}-u^{*}\|_{L^{2}(\nu)}^{2}=O(\kappa^{-2}n^{-2s/(2s+d)}
), which is the minimax rate over Hölder classes.

Beyond ReLU (architectures) and the role of smoothness.

The approximation term in (
3.5
) is controlled by how well the hypothesis class approximates
C^{s}
. For ReLU networks, the bound
W^{-2s/d}
with depth
L\gtrsim\log W
is standard; see, e.g.,
Yarotsky 2017
;
Yarotsky 2018
;
Suzuki 2018
;
Siegel 2023
;
Mhaskar 1996
. The analysis extends with minor changes to other piecewise–polynomial activations (same pseudo–dimension scaling) and to Barron–type two–layer networks, for which the integrated error can scale as
O(1/n)
when
u^{*}
belongs to a Barron class
Barron 1993
. Smoothness
s
dictates the approximation rate and hence the bias term; the variance term is driven by capacity via the pseudo–dimension bound recalled below.

4
Solving Poisson’s equation

Let
X
be a Markov chain with stationary distribution
\pi
, and suppose
r
is
\pi
-integrable. Solving Poisson’s equation

u-Pu=r-\pi r

(4.1)

requires additional care, because the centering constant
\pi r=\int_{\mathcal{S}}r(y)\pi(dy)
is often not available. Fortunately, since
\pi r
is constant, it can be eliminated by applying
I-P
to both sides, yielding

u-2Pu+P^{2}u=r-Pr

(4.2)

where
(P^{2}u)(x)=\mathbb{E}_{x}u(X_{2})
. The following result shows that (
4.2
) does not admit any additional solutions beyond those of (
4.1
).
Let
L^{1}(\pi)=\{u:\mathcal{S}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}\;\text{such that}\;\int_{\mathcal{S}}|u(x)|\pi(dx)<\infty\}
.

Proposition 4
.

If (
4.1
) has a unique solution in
L^{1}(\pi)
that is unique within
L^{1}(\pi)
up to an additive constant, the same is true for (
4.2
).

Proof.

Let
w
be a solution of (
4.2
), i.e.,
(I-P)^{2}w=(I-P)r
. Define
v=(I-P)w
. Then

(I-P)v=(I-P)r\;\;\Rightarrow\;\;(I-P)(v-r)=0.

Since
(I-P)u=r-\pi r
has a unique solution up to a constant,
v-r
must be a constant function.
Then

v(x)-r(x)=c\;\;x\in\mathcal{S}\Rightarrow\;\;((I-P)w)(x)=r(x)+c,\;\;x\in\mathcal{S}.

By applying
\pi
to both sides,
c
must be
-\pi r
, so
w
is a solution of (
4.1
).
∎

When
X
is a positive recurrent Harris chain, the assumption of Proposition
4
holds; see Proposition 1.1 of
Glynn and Meyn 1996
.

Estimating stationary distributions

Let
X
be a positive recurrent Markov chain with stationary distribution
\pi
. Suppose the one-step transition kernel admits a bounded and continuous density
p
with respect to a reference probability measure
\eta
, i.e.,
P(x,dy)=p(x,y)\eta(dy)
for
x,y\in\mathcal{S}
. The following result shows that
\pi
must also admit a bounded and continuous density with respect to
\eta
, i.e.,
\pi(dy)=\pi(y)\eta(dy)
.

Proposition 5
.

If
p(x,y)
is bounded and continuous in
y
, then
\pi
has a density
\pi(\cdot)
and
\pi(\cdot)
is bounded and continuous.

Proof.

For any measurable
A\subseteq\mathcal{S}
, the global balance equation gives

\pi(A)=\int_{\mathcal{S}}\pi(dx)P(x,A)=\int_{\mathcal{S}}\pi(dx)\int_{A}p(x,y)\eta(dy)=\int_{A}\left(\int_{\mathcal{S}}\pi(dx)p(x,y)\right)\eta(dy).

Hence
\pi\ll\eta
with density

\pi(y)=\int_{\mathcal{S}}\pi(dx)p(x,y)\leq\sup_{x,y\in\mathcal{S}}p(x,y)<\infty.

If
y_{n}\rightarrow y
, then by the continuity of
p(x,\cdot)
and the bounded convergence theorem,

\pi(y_{n})=\int_{\mathcal{S}}\pi(dx)p(x,y_{n})\rightarrow\int_{\mathcal{S}}\pi(dx)p(x,y)=\pi(y),

as
n\rightarrow\infty.

∎

When
\pi
is bounded and continuous and
\mathcal{S}
is compact, we may use a neural network
\{\pi_{\theta}:\theta\in\Theta\}
to approximate
\pi
, by minimizing the integrated squared residual of the global balance equation

l(\theta)=\int_{\mathcal{S}}\left(\pi_{\theta}(y)-\int_{\mathcal{S}}\pi_{\theta}(x)\eta(dx)p(x,y)\right)^{2}\nu(dy).

where
\nu
is a probability measure.
Note that the minimizer of

is
\pi
up to a multiplicative constant. To make the learning goal unique, we can enforce that
\pi_{\theta}
takes a prescribed value (e.g., 1) at a specific location (e.g., 0), which can be done by training

\tilde{\pi}_{\theta}(x)=\pi_{\theta}(x)-\pi_{\theta}(0)+1

instead of
\pi_{\theta}(x)
.
Let
Y_{0}\sim\nu
and
Z_{1},Z_{-1}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\text{iid}}}{{\sim}}\eta
. Then

l(\theta)=\mathbb{E}\left[\left[\pi_{\theta}(Y_{0})-\pi_{\theta}(Z_{1})p(Z_{1},Y_{0})\right]\left[\pi_{\theta}(Y_{0})-\pi_{\theta}(Z_{-1})p(Z_{-1},Y_{0})\right]\right],

which can be minimized via SGD (Algorithm
3
) as before. Note that this approach is useful when the transition density is known. We note that
Z_{1},Z_{-1}
are sampled from the reference measure

Dealing with non-compact state spaces

As discussed earlier, an important application of our deep learning methodology is to the computation of Lyapunov functions. Such functions arise naturally to the analysis of stability questions for Markov chains taking values in a non-compact state space. Clearly, we can not expect an algorithm that terminates in finite time to globally approximate a function with an unbounded domain (because to encode the approximation then consumes infinite memory). over

(Algorithm
4
).

8
Conclusions

This paper demonstrates the potential of combining deep learning and simulation to study Markov chains. Under the unified FTA-RGA (first-transition analysis & residual gradient algorithm) framework, we can construct Lyapunov functions, solve Poisson’s equation, and estimate stationary distributions, for Markov chains on compact or non-compact state spaces. As we discussed earlier in our sample complexity analysis, we have left some considerations for future research, in particular, involving the role of the parameter
\Lambda
which is connected with the choice of the sampling density and the number of steps required to implement the residual network. We expect that these issues will manifest themselves in the high dimensional setting prominently.

9
Proof of Theorem
3

The proof follows the localization program of Bartlett–Bousquet–Mendelson (BBM)
Bartlett et al. 2005
;
Koltchinskii 2006
. We first record definitions and basic tools, then prove each proposition in turn, and finally assemble the oracle inequality.

9.1
Preliminaries: definitions and tools

Pseudo–dimension.

For a class
\mathcal{G}
of real–valued functions on a set
\mathcal{Z}
, the
pseudo–dimension

\operatorname{Pdim}(\mathcal{G})
is the largest integer
m
such that there exist points
z_{1},\ldots,z_{m}\in\mathcal{Z}
and thresholds
s_{1},\ldots,s_{m}\in\mathbb{R}
with the following property: for every labeling
\sigma\in\{-1,+1\}^{m}
there exists
g\in\mathcal{G}
with
\operatorname{sign}(g(z_{i})-s_{i})=\sigma_{i}
for all
i
. Pseudo–dimension reduces to VC–dimension for
\{0,1\}
–valued classes and controls combinatorial complexity for real–valued classes; see, e.g.,
(
Bartlett et al. 2019
, Section 3)
and
Langer 2021
.

Empirical and Rademacher complexity.

Given i.i.d. data
Z_{1},\ldots,Z_{n}
with law
P
and a class
\mathcal{G}\subset L^{2}(P)
, the
empirical Rademacher complexity
is

\mathfrak{R}_{n}(\mathcal{G}):=\mathbb{E}\Bigg[\sup_{g\in\mathcal{G}}\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\varepsilon_{i}\,g(Z_{i})\,\Big|\,Z_{1},...,Z_{n}\Bigg],

where
\varepsilon_{1},\ldots,\varepsilon_{n}
are i.i.d. Rademacher signs (i.e., uniform on
\{1,-1\}
) independent of the sample. Symmetrization and contraction principles (see
(
Bartlett et al. 2005
, Sec. 2–3)
) convert population–empirical deviations into Rademacher averages and allow us to bound the latter via combinatorial parameters such as
\operatorname{Pdim}
.

Local Rademacher complexity and the BBM fixed point.

Let
g_{u}:=h_{u}-h_{u^{*}}
denote the
excess loss
. For
r>0
, define the localized class

\mathcal{G}(r):=\big\{g_{u}:u\in\mathcal{F}_{W,L,B},\;\mathbb{E}[g_{u}]\leq r\big\}.

Set
\psi(r):=\mathbb{E}\,\mathfrak{R}_{n}(\mathcal{G}(r))
. The BBM theory shows that, under a Bernstein–type variance control (next paragraph), the estimation error is governed by the smallest
\gamma^{\star}>0
(the
local fixed point
) solving
\psi(\gamma^{\star})\leq c\,\gamma^{\star}
for an absolute constant
c>0
; see
(
Bartlett et al. 2005
, Theorem 3.3 & 3.5)
and
(
Koltchinskii 2006
, Section 3)
. This yields a high–probability “oracle inequality” with
\gamma^{\star}
as the dominant complexity term.

Bernstein (variance–excess–risk) condition.

A family
\{g_{u}\}
satisfies a (linear) Bernstein condition with constant
C_{\rm B}
if

\operatorname{Var}(g_{u})\leq C_{\rm B}\,\mathbb{E}[g_{u}]

for all
u
under consideration. For square–loss–type problems, this is standard (the curvature of the loss forces variance to be controlled by excess risk). In our product–loss setting, we verify such a condition directly below; see
Bartlett et al. 2005
;
Koltchinskii 2006
.

9.2
Detailed proofs of the propositions

Proposition 6

(Coercivity)
.

Under Assumption
4
,

\|\tilde{H}\|_{L^{2}(\nu)}\leq\sup_{x\in K}\tilde{H}(x,K)^{1/2}\cdot\sqrt{\Lambda}.

Consequently, for all
w\in L^{2}(\nu)
,

\kappa\|w\|_{2}\leq\|(I-\tilde{H})w\|_{2}\leq(1+\|\tilde{H}\|_{L^{2}(\nu)})\|w\|_{2}\leq 2\|w\|_{2},

(9.1)

with
\kappa
as in (
3.2
).

Proof.

Note that
\tilde{H}
is a substochastic kernel, so the Cauchy-Schwarz inequality implies that for any
f\in L^{2}(\nu)
,

(\tilde{H}f)^{2}(x)\leq(\tilde{H}f^{2})(x)\tilde{H}(x,K)

for
x\in K
. So, part (iii) of Assumption
4
implies that

\left\lVert\tilde{H}f\right\rVert_{2}^{2}\leq\int_{K}\nu(dx)(\tilde{H}f^{2})(x)\tilde{H}(x,K)\leq\Lambda\int_{K}\nu(dy)f^{2}(y)\cdot\sup_{x\in K}\tilde{H}(x,K)

and hence

\|\tilde{H}\|_{L^{2}(\nu)}\leq\sup_{x\in K}\tilde{H}(x,K)^{1/2}\cdot\sqrt{\Lambda}.

The two–sided bound (
9.1
) follows from
\|(I-\tilde{H})w\|_{2}\geq(1-\|\tilde{H}\|_{L^{2}(\nu)})\|w\|_{2}=\kappa\|w\|_{2}
and
\|(I-\tilde{H})w\|_{2}\leq\|w\|_{2}+\|H\|_{L^{2}(\nu)}\|w\|_{2}\leq 2\|w\|_{2}
.
∎

Proposition 7

(Bernstein / variance–excess–risk control)
.

There exists a constant
C_{\rm B}<\infty
(depending only on
B
,
\sigma_{V}^{2}
,
C_{W}
,
\Lambda
, and
\kappa
) such that, for all
u\in\mathcal{F}_{W,L,B}
,

\operatorname{Var}\!\left(h_{u}(R,V,W,Y,\tilde{V},\tilde{W},\tilde{Y})-h_{u^{*}}(R,V,W,\tilde{V},\tilde{W},\tilde{Y})\right)\leq C_{\rm B}\,\mathcal{L}(u).

Moreover,
\mathcal{L}(u)=\|(I-\tilde{H})(u-u^{*})\|_{2}^{2}
and
|h_{u}|
has finite second moment under Assumption
4
.

Proof (step–by–step).

Write
\varrho:=u-u^{*}
and define

m_{\varrho}(r):=\mathbb{E}[\ell_{u}(r,V,W,Y)-\ell_{u^{*}}(r,V,W,Y)\mid R=r],\qquad s_{\varrho}(r):=\mathbb{E}[(\ell_{u}-\ell_{u^{*}})^{2}\mid R=r].

Since
u^{*}
solves (
3.1
), we have
m_{\varrho}(r)=(I-\tilde{H})\varrho(r)
, hence
\mathcal{L}(u)=\mathbb{E}[m_{\varrho}(R)^{2}]
. Also,

\ell_{u}-\ell_{u^{*}}=\varrho(R)-W\,\varrho(Y).

Therefore,

\displaystyle s_{\varrho}(R)

\displaystyle=m_{\varrho}(R)^{2}+\operatorname{Var}(\varrho(R)-W\,\varrho(Y)\mid R)

(9.2)

\displaystyle\leq m_{\varrho}(R)^{2}+\mathbb{E}[W^{2}\,\varrho(Y)^{2}\mid R].

(9.3)

By Assumption
4
(v),
\mathbb{E}[W^{2}\,\varrho(Y)^{2}\mid R]\leq C_{W}\,(\tilde{H}\,\varrho^{2})(R)
. Integrating and using Assumption
4
(iv), we obtain

\mathbb{E}[s_{\varrho}(R)]\leq\mathbb{E}[m_{\varrho}(R)^{2}]+C_{W}\,\Lambda\,\|\varrho\|_{2}^{2}.

By Proposition
6
,
\|\varrho\|_{2}\leq\kappa^{-1}\,\|(I-\tilde{H})\varrho\|_{2}=\kappa^{-1}\,\|m_{\varrho}\|_{2}
. Hence

\mathbb{E}[s_{\varrho}(R)]\leq\Big(1+C_{W}\,\Lambda\,\kappa^{-2}\Big)\,\mathcal{L}(u).

(9.4)

Next, decompose the excess loss
g_{u}:=h_{u}-h_{u^{*}}
as

g_{u}=(\ell_{u}-\ell_{u^{*}})\,\ell_{u}+\ell_{u^{*}}\,(\ell_{u}-\ell_{u^{*}}).

Conditioning on
R
and using independence of
(V,W,Y)
and
(\tilde{V},\tilde{W},\tilde{Y})
given
R
,

\mathbb{E}[g_{u}^{2}\mid R]\leq 2\,s_{\varrho}(R)\,\Big(\mathbb{E}[\ell_{u}^{2}\mid R]+\mathbb{E}[\ell_{u^{*}}^{2}\mid R]\Big).

Since outputs are clipped to
[-B,B]
, write
(a+b+c)^{2}\leq 3(a^{2}+b^{2}+c^{2})
and note that, for
\nu
-a.e.
R
,

\ell_{u}^{2}\leq 3\,\big(u(R)^{2}+V^{2}+W^{2}u(Y)^{2}\big),\qquad\ell_{u^{*}}^{2}\leq 3\,\big(u^{*}(R)^{2}+V^{2}+W^{2}u^{*}(Y)^{2}\big).

Let

H_{K}:=\sup_{x\in K}\tilde{H}(x,K)=\sup_{x\in K}\mathbb{E}[W\mid R=x],

so the supremum is taken over
x\in K
. By Assumption
4
(iii) and (v), and clipping
|u|\leq B
,

\mathbb{E}[\ell_{u}^{2}\mid R]\leq 3\,\big(B^{2}+\sigma_{V}^{2}+B^{2}\,\mathbb{E}[W^{2}\mid R]\big)\leq 3\,\big(B^{2}+\sigma_{V}^{2}+B^{2}\,C_{W}\,H_{K}\big).

Moreover, since
u^{*}
is continuous and
K
is compact we use its sup-norm on
K
:

\|u^{*}\|_{\infty}:=\sup_{x\in K}|u^{*}(x)|<\infty,

so similarly

\mathbb{E}[\ell_{u^{*}}^{2}\mid R]\leq 3\,\big(\|u^{*}\|_{\infty}^{2}+\sigma_{V}^{2}+C_{W}\,H_{K}\,\|u^{*}\|_{\infty}^{2}\big).

Thus we may take

C^{\prime}:=3\,\Big[(B^{2}+\|u^{*}\|_{\infty}^{2})+\sigma_{V}^{2}+C_{W}\,H_{K}\,(B^{2}+\|u^{*}\|_{\infty}^{2})\Big],

which yields the uniform bound

\mathbb{E}[\ell_{u}^{2}\mid R]+\mathbb{E}[\ell_{u^{*}}^{2}\mid R]\leq C^{\prime}.

Taking expectations and applying (
9.4
) yields

\mathbb{E}[g_{u}^{2}]\leq C_{\rm B}\,\mathcal{L}(u),

with
C_{\rm B}:=2\,C^{\prime}\,(1+C_{W}\,\Lambda\,\kappa^{-2})
. Finally,
\operatorname{Var}(g_{u})\leq\mathbb{E}[g_{u}^{2}]
and
\mathcal{L}(u^{*})=0
.
∎

Proposition 8

(Localized complexity and oracle inequality)
.

Let
\mathcal{H}_{W,L,B}:=\{h_{u}:u\in\mathcal{F}_{W,L,B}\}
and
g_{u}=h_{u}-h_{u^{*}}
. Denote by
\gamma^{\star}
the BBM fixed point for the localized class
\mathcal{G}(r)=\{g_{u}:\mathbb{E}[g_{u}]\leq r\}
. Then there exist universal constants
c_{1},c_{2}>0
such that, for all
\delta\in(0,1)
, with probability at least
1-\delta
,

\mathcal{L}(\hat{u}_{n})-\mathcal{L}(u^{*})\leq c_{1}\Big(\inf_{u\in\mathcal{F}_{W,L,B}}\big[\mathcal{L}(u)-\mathcal{L}(u^{*})\big]+\gamma^{\star}\Big)+c_{2}\,\frac{\log(1/\delta)}{n}.

(9.5)

Proof (BBM road map with details).

Set
g_{u}=h_{u}-h_{u^{*}}
and note that
\mathbb{E}[g_{u}]=\mathcal{L}(u)
by (
3.4
). By the symmetrization inequality (see
(
Bartlett et al. 2005
, Sec. 2)
), for any
r>0
,

\mathbb{E}\Big[\sup_{g\in\mathcal{G}(r)}\big(\mathbb{E}[g]-\widehat{\mathbb{E}}_{n}[g]\big)\Big]\leq 2\,\mathbb{E}\,\mathfrak{R}_{n}(\mathcal{G}(r)),

(9.6)

where
\widehat{\mathbb{E}}_{n}
is the empirical mean. Under the Bernstein condition (Proposition
7
),
\operatorname{Var}(g)\leq C_{\rm B}\,\mathbb{E}[g]
for all
g\in\mathcal{G}(r)
, so the localized class has
L^{2}(P)
radius
O(\sqrt{r})
. Applying the localized deviation bound of
(
Bartlett et al. 2005
, Theorem. 3.3 & 3.5)
(see also
(
Koltchinskii 2006
, Sec. 3)
) yields: there exist constants
c_{1},c_{2}>0
such that, with probability at least
1-\delta
, simultaneously for all
u
,

\mathbb{E}[g_{u}]\leq\widehat{\mathbb{E}}_{n}[g_{u}]+c_{1}\,\psi(\mathbb{E}[g_{u}])+c_{2}\,\frac{\log(1/\delta)}{n},

(9.7)

where
\psi(r)=\mathbb{E}\mathfrak{R}_{n}(\mathcal{G}(r))
. Let
\gamma^{\star}
solve
\psi(\gamma^{\star})\leq\gamma^{\star}/(2c_{1})
. Then (
9.7
) implies, for all
u
with
\mathbb{E}[g_{u}]\geq\gamma^{\star}
,

\mathbb{E}[g_{u}]\leq 2\,\widehat{\mathbb{E}}_{n}[g_{u}]+2c_{2}\,\log(1/\delta)/n.

Taking
u=\hat{u}_{n}
(an ERM of
\widehat{\mathcal{L}}_{n}
) and comparing to the best
u\in\mathcal{F}_{W,L,B}
(peeling on the event
\mathbb{E}[g_{\hat{u}_{n}}]\geq\gamma^{\star}
and its complement) yields (
9.5
). This is the standard BBM oracle inequality; see
(
Bartlett et al. 2005
, Theorem. 3.5)
.
∎

Proposition 9

(Bounding the local fixed point)
.

Let
d_{W,L}:=\operatorname{Pdim}(\mathcal{F}_{W,L,B})
. There exists
c>0
such that

\gamma^{\star}\leq c\,\frac{d_{W,L}\,\log n}{n}.

(9.8)

Proof (from products to linear classes).

Recall
g_{u}=(\ell_{u}-\ell_{u^{*}})\ell_{u}+\ell_{u^{*}}(\ell_{u}-\ell_{u^{*}})
. For any fixed sample
\{(R_{i},V_{i},W_{i},Y_{i},\tilde{V}_{i},\tilde{W}_{i},\tilde{Y}_{i})\}_{i=1}^{n}
, the multiplier inequality (a consequence of contraction; see
(
Bartlett et al. 2005
, Sec. 2–3)
) gives

\mathfrak{R}_{n}(\mathcal{G}(r))\leq\big(B_{\ell}+B_{\ell}^{\!*}\big)\,\mathfrak{R}_{n}\!\left(\mathcal{L}_{0}\right),

(9.9)

where

\mathcal{L}_{0}:=\big\{(r,v,w,y)\mapsto\ell_{u}(r,v,w,y)-\ell_{u^{*}}(r,v,w,y):u\in\mathcal{F}_{W,L,B},\ \mathbb{E}[g_{u}]\leq r\big\}.

Note that
\ell_{u}-\ell_{u^{*}}
is linear in
\varrho=u-u^{*}
:

\ell_{u}(r,v,w,y)-\ell_{u^{*}}(r,v,w,y)=\varrho(r)-w\,\varrho(y).

Let

H_{K}:=\sup_{x\in K}\tilde{H}(x,K)=\sup_{x\in K}\mathbb{E}[W\mid R=x].

Conditioning on the sample and using Cauchy–Schwarz for the weighted term,

\displaystyle\mathfrak{R}_{n}(\mathcal{L}_{0})

\displaystyle\leq\frac{1}{n}\,\mathbb{E}_{\varepsilon}\sup_{\varrho}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\varepsilon_{i}\,\varrho(R_{i})\;+\frac{1}{n}\,\mathbb{E}_{\varepsilon}\sup_{\varrho}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\varepsilon_{i}\,W_{i}\,\varrho(Y_{i})

(9.10)

\displaystyle\leq\mathfrak{R}_{n}\!\left(\mathcal{F}_{W,L,2B}^{\mathrm{eval}}\right)\;+\Big(\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}W_{i}^{2}\Big)^{\!1/2}\,\mathfrak{R}_{n}\!\left(\mathcal{F}_{W,L,2B}^{\mathrm{eval}}\right),

(9.11)

where
\mathcal{F}_{W,L,2B}^{\mathrm{eval}}:=\{x\mapsto v(x):v\in\mathcal{F}_{W,L,2B}-\mathcal{F}_{W,L,2B}\}
is an evaluation class (differences absorbed into the enlarged clipping range
2B
). Taking expectations over the sample and using Assumption
4
(v) together with
\sup_{x}\mathbb{E}[W\mid R=x]=H_{K}
, we obtain

\mathbb{E}\,\mathfrak{R}_{n}(\mathcal{L}_{0})\;\leq\;\big(1+\sqrt{C_{W}\,H_{K}}\big)\,\mathbb{E}\,\mathfrak{R}_{n}\!\left(\mathcal{F}_{W,L,2B}^{\mathrm{eval}}\right).

(9.12)

Standard pseudo–dimension bounds (e.g.,
(
Bartlett et al. 2019
, Sec. 3)
) yield

\mathbb{E}\,\mathfrak{R}_{m}(\mathcal{F}_{W,L,2B}^{\mathrm{eval}})\;\leq\;2Bc_{0}\,\sqrt{\frac{d_{W,L}\,\log(m/d_{W,L})}{m}},

(9.13)

for some constant
c_{0}
. Combining (
9.9
), (
9.12
), and (
9.13
), and applying the BBM localization calculus (variance
\leq r
up to constants, by Proposition
7
) gives

\psi(r)\;\leq c(B_{\ell}+B_{\ell}^{\!*})\,\big(1+\sqrt{C_{W}\,H_{K}}\big)\,\sqrt{\frac{d_{W,L}\,\log n}{n}}\,\sqrt{r},

for some constant
c
. Thus the fixed point
\gamma^{\star}
solving
\psi(\gamma^{\star})\leq\gamma^{\star}/(2c_{1})
satisfies

\gamma^{\star}\ =\;O(\frac{d_{W,L}\,\log n}{n}),

as claimed.
∎

Proposition 10

(Pseudo–dimension of ReLU networks)
.

For ReLU networks with at most
W
nonzero weights and depth
L
, the pseudo–dimension satisfies

d_{W,L}=O(W\,L\,\log W),

(9.14)

see
Bartlett et al. 2019
;
Langer 2021
. Differences of networks and output clipping change the bound only by absolute constant factors.

Proposition 11

(Approximation by ReLU and related networks)
.

Under Assumption
5
, for any
\varepsilon\in(0,1)
there exists
u_{\varepsilon}\in\mathcal{F}_{W,L,B}
with
L\geq c_{0}\log(1/\varepsilon)
for some
c_{0}>0
and with a number of nonzero weights
W=O(\varepsilon^{-d/s})
such that

\|u_{\varepsilon}-u^{*}\|_{\infty}\leq\varepsilon.

(9.15)

Consequently,
\inf_{u\in\mathcal{F}_{W,L,B}}\|u-u^{*}\|_{L^{2}(\nu)}^{2}=O(W^{-2s/d})
when
L\geq c_{0}\log W
. See
Yarotsky 2017
;
Yarotsky 2018
;
Suzuki 2018
;
Siegel 2023
;
Mhaskar 1996
.

Proof (sketch for completeness).

The cited works construct deep ReLU networks that implement localized approximants with exponentially efficient reuse of parameters across dyadic partitions; this yields the
W^{-s/d}
uniform approximation rate with depth
O(\log W)
. Squaring and integrating over
\nu
yields the
L^{2}(\nu)
rate
W^{-2s/d}
.
∎

Remark 1
.

The Barron–type alternative in
Barron 1993
provides
O(1/n)
rates under spectral smoothness (Barron norm) rather than Hölder smoothness. We mention this alternative as this type of regularity assumption is also common in the literature, but we do not pursue that here.

Proof of Theorem
3
.

By (
3.4
) and Proposition
11
, there exists
u_{W,L}\in\mathcal{F}_{W,L,B}
with
\mathcal{L}(u_{W,L})=O(W^{-2s/d})
. Apply the oracle inequality (
9.5
) with
u=u_{W,L}
and use Proposition
9
together with (
9.14
) to obtain

\mathcal{L}(\hat{u}_{n})=O(W^{-2s/d})+O((W\,L\,\log W\cdot\log n+\log(1/\delta))/n).

Finally, use (
3.4
) and the lower bound in (

) with

to translate

∎

9.3

A Lyapunov condition to establish contraction

Suppose that
\nu
has a probability density
\eta=(\eta(y):y\in K)
on
K
(wrt Lebesgue measure) that is strictly positive and continuous (and hence bounded above and below). We further assume that
X
has a transition density. In particular,

P(x,B)=\int_{B}p(x,y)dy

for
x\in\mathcal{S}
and (measurable)
B\subseteq\mathcal{S}
. If there exists a non-negative function
(q(x,y):x\in C-K,y\in K)
such that

\int_{C-K}e^{-\beta(x)}p(x,z)q(z,y)\leq q(x,y)-e^{-\beta(x)}p(x,y)

for
x\in C-K
,
y\in K
, then the Lyapunov bound

\mathbb{E}_{x}\left[\exp\left(-\sum_{j=0}^{\tau_{K}-1}\beta(X_{j})\right)I(X_{\tau_{K}}\in dy)\right]\leq q(x,y)dy

for
x\in C-K
,
y\in K
holds (where
\tau_{K}=\inf\{n\geq 1:X_{n}\in K\}
). Hence, if there exists
c<1
such that

e^{-\beta(x)}p(x,y)+\int_{C-K}e^{-\beta(x)}p(x,z)q(z,y)\leq c\eta(y)

for
x,y\in K
, then

\int_{K}\eta(x)dx\tilde{H}(x,dy)\leq c\eta(y)dy

i.e.,
\nu\tilde{H}\leq c\nu
, which yields the fact that
\tilde{H}
is a contraction on
L^{2}(\nu)
, since

\nu(\tilde{H}w)^{2}\leq\nu\tilde{H}w^{2}\leq c\nu w^{2}.
