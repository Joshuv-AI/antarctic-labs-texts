Atopic dermatitis and attention‐deficit/hyperactivity disorder comorbidity in children and adolescents: Epidemiology, mechanisms, and clinical management
Authors: Qingyang Zhao, Yuan Liang, Ying Liu, Chunping Shen, Mutong Zhao, Jing Tian, Lei Jiao, Jing Sun, Ying Gu, Qiong Wu, Shan Wang, Lin Ma
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTAtopic dermatitis (AD) and attention‐deficit/hyperactivity disorder (ADHD) frequently co‐occur in children and adolescents, increasing the disease burden and clinical complexity. Accumulating evidence suggests that children with AD have a higher risk of incident ADHD, a risk that is particularly pronounced in those with early‐onset, severe AD, or significant sleep disturbances. The underlying mechanisms are hypothesized to involve the dysregulation of the “inflammation–itching–neurobehavioral” axis. This narrative review synthesizes current evidence regarding the epidemiology, potential mechanisms, and clinical management of this comorbidity. We propose that proactive screening and aggressive control of skin inflammation and pruritus are key to disrupting this vicious cycle.

INTRODUCTION

Atopic dermatitis (AD) is a common chronic, inflammatory, relapsing, pruritic skin disease, primarily characterized by recurrent itching and eczematous lesions. Its global prevalence in children is approximately 20%. 1 Attention‐deficit/hyperactivity disorder (ADHD) is a common neurodevelopmental disorder in childhood, characterized by core symptoms of inattention, hyperactivity, and impulsivity, which often persistently impact a child's academic performance, social interactions, and family functioning. In recent years, numerous epidemiological studies have indicated a comorbidity between AD and ADHD in children and adolescents, with the risk of ADHD being approximately 30%–50% higher in the AD population than in the general population. 2 This cross‐system clinical association may increase the disease burden on patients and families, complicate diagnosis and management, and is receiving increasing clinical attention. 3

Current evidence suggests potentially shared or interactive pathophysiological pathways between AD and ADHD. 4 , 5 The underlying mechanisms involve multiple interrelated pathways: type 2 inflammatory mediators associated with AD may influence neurodevelopment and function through systemic inflammatory pathways 5 , 6 ; chronic sleep deprivation due to pruritus may directly impair daytime cognition and behavioral regulation 7 , 8 ; and the chronic “itch–scratch” cycle can act as a persistent stressor, disrupting hypothalamic‐pituitary‐adrenal (HPA) axis function and related neurotransmitter balance. 9 , 10 Furthermore, genetic susceptibility and early life environmental factors (e.g., maternal immune status and infant medication use) may also contribute to the shared pathogenic background. 11 , 12 Observational studies generally support an association between AD and ADHD. However, recent analyses using genetic instrumental variables (e.g., Mendelian randomization) suggest a different interpretation. 13 This link may not be driven by a direct genetic causal pathway; rather, it may be attributable to secondary consequences of AD activity or environmental confounding factors.

Three related but non‐equivalent concepts were distinguished in this review. Formally diagnosed ADHD refers to a clinical diagnosis based on the criteria in DSM‐5‐TR or ICD‐11, defined by developmentally inappropriate and impairing levels of inattention, restlessness, and impulsivity, with a threshold number of symptoms of inattention or hyperactivity‐impulsivity (or both). 14 , 15 ADHD symptoms denote the core behavioral features measured by validated rating scales (e.g., Vanderbilt, Conners, and Child Behavior Checklist [CBCL]). 16 , 17 These symptoms may be present irrespective of whether the full diagnostic threshold is reached. ADHD‐like behaviors refer to transient attentional or behavioral difficulties that arise secondary to the symptomatic burden of AD, particularly chronic pruritus, sleep fragmentation, and psychosocial stress, and typically improve with effective AD treatment. 4 , 18 In clinical practice, oppositional defiant disorder frequently co‐occurs with ADHD and should be considered in the differential diagnosis, 19 given that the symptoms may either overlap with or be exacerbated by the behavioral consequences of AD. Indeed, pruritus and sleep loss in AD have been shown to correlate positively with oppositional defiance scores. 20

This review aims to synthesize current epidemiological findings regarding AD–ADHD comorbidity in children and adolescents, to evaluate the strength and sources of heterogeneity in this association, to elucidate the potential biological and behavioral mechanisms connecting these two conditions, and to propose the “inflammation‐itching‐neurobehavioral axis” as an integrative explanatory framework. Additionally, it seeks to serve as a practical reference for clinicians in pediatrics, dermatology, and psychiatry, facilitating early identification, comprehensive management, and ultimately improving overall prognosis.

SEARCH STRATEGY

For this narrative review, we conducted a literature search of PubMed from inception to March 2026 using the following search terms applied to titles and abstracts: (“atopic dermatitis” OR “eczema”) AND (“attention deficit hyperactivity disorder” OR “ADHD”) AND (“child” OR “children” OR “childhood” OR “adolescent” OR “pediatric”). The search was confined to English‐language publications. Eligible publications encompassed systematic reviews and meta‐analyses, Mendelian randomization studies, observational studies (including cohort, case‐control, and cross‐sectional designs), and preclinical mechanistic studies that examined the association between AD and ADHD or related neurobehavioral outcomes in children and adolescents. Reference lists of retrieved articles were also manually screened for additional relevant studies. Given the narrative nature of this review, we did not conduct a formal quality assessment or meta‐analysis.

EPIDEMIOLOGICAL EVIDENCE OF ASSOCIATION

Numerous studies have confirmed the comorbidity between childhood AD and ADHD; however, the strength, nature, and clinical significance of this association remain unclear.

Bidirectional association supported by observational evidence

Substantial observational evidence indicates a significant bidirectional association between AD and ADHD. Children with AD are at a significantly higher risk of ADHD. Regarding the risk of ADHD in patients with AD, two recent meta‐analyses have yielded comparable pooled estimates: Wang et al. 7 reported an odds ratio (OR) of 1.34 (95% confidence interval [CI]: 1.25–1.44) in all ages, and Cheng et al. 2 reported an OR of 1.28 (95% CI: 1.18–1.40). Notably, Cheng et al. 2 conducted age‐stratified analyses and found that the association with ADHD diagnosis was stronger in school‐age children (6–18 years: OR = 1.38, 95% CI: 1.28–1.48) than in preschoolers (<6 years: OR = 1.12, 95% CI: 1.07–1.17). A cross‐sectional study using a Korean national dataset reported that patients with AD had a significantly higher incidence of ADHD (OR = 1.48; 95% CI: 1.27–1.72). 21 For pediatric populations, a global systematic review found the pooled prevalence of ADHD in children with AD was 9% (95% CI: 4.78%–13.37%). 22 Longitudinal studies further elucidate this temporal association: a Danish national registry study showed that children diagnosed with AD before age 5 had a 30% increased risk of subsequently being diagnosed with ADHD compared with the general population (hazard ratio [HR] = 1.3, 95% CI: 1.2–1.5) 23 ; a large cohort study from China found that children diagnosed with AD before age 2 had a 16% increased risk of subsequent ADHD diagnosis, with the risk being particularly pronounced in those with earlier onset or more severe AD 24 ; a prospective cohort study from Singapore similarly showed that eczema diagnosed by 18 months of age was associated with an approximately 1.3‐fold increased risk of ADHD symptoms at 54 months of age (adjusted RR = 2.3). 25 This association may persist into adulthood: a case‐control study based on the US found that adults with AD were 2.66 times more likely to have an ADHD diagnosis compared to matched controls. 26

On the other hand, the reciprocal association is also evident, with children having ADHD showing a significantly higher risk of developing AD. Multiple large studies indicate that children with ADHD have a significantly higher risk of being diagnosed with AD than the general pediatric population (OR = 1.53, 95% CI: 1.42–1.64). 27 A retrospective study using the TriNetX database in individuals under 26 years further confirmed that, compared to matched neurotypical controls, both males and females with ADHD alone had a significantly increased risk of AD (OR = 1.277 and 1.289, respectively). 28

Factors influencing the association

The strength of the association between AD and ADHD is influenced by multiple factors. Understanding these factors helps identify high‐risk populations for early clinical identification and management.

AD severity and clinical course

AD severity is a core influencing factor. A global systematic review showed that the prevalence of ADHD was significantly higher in children with moderate (15%) or severe (16%) AD than in those with mild AD (7%). 22 However, a recent cross‐sectional study from Saudi Arabia reported no significant association between AD severity and ADHD symptoms in either children or adults. 29 Furthermore, according to a 2025 trajectory modeling study, the age of onset and the disease course pattern of AD are important predictors of subsequent ADHD risk. The study identified four AD course trajectories, among which the early‐onset persistent trajectory was associated with the highest risk of ADHD (OR = 3.7, compared to the late‐onset persistent trajectory), whereas the mid‐onset transient and late‐onset persistent trajectories carried relatively lower risks. 30

The mediating and reinforcing role of sleep disturbance

Sleep disturbance is highly prevalent in children with AD and acts as a mediator and reinforcing factor in the AD–ADHD association, linking skin symptoms to daytime neurobehavioral problems. Children with both severe AD and significant sleep insufficiency (0–3 nights of sufficient sleep per week) had an OR of 16.83 for ADHD, substantially higher than children with only sleep disturbance (OR = 1.83) or only mild‐to‐moderate AD (OR = 1.56). 31 A study of Brazilian school children found that children with AD of varying severity all experienced sleep problems, and those with severe AD had significantly higher scores for “difficulties initiating and maintaining sleep”. 8 Chronic nocturnal itching and sleep fragmentation likely impair daytime cognitive function, leading to daytime inattention, hyperactivity, and impulsivity, which can exacerbate or manifest as ADHD symptoms. While biologically plausible, the quality of evidence for this clinical association is currently rated as low. 7 , 32 Future prospective studies are needed to clarify the causal relationship and explore the potential for reducing ADHD risk by optimizing AD treatment and improving sleep quality.

Comorbidities and clinical manifestations

AD often co‐occurs with other atopic diseases such as asthma and allergic rhinitis. A prospective US urban birth cohort study found that children with asthma, AD, or allergic rhinitis had a significantly higher risk of ADHD than those without these conditions, and children with multiple atopic diseases exhibited an even greater risk. 33 Additionally, the prevalence of ADHD among offspring is higher when both the mother and child have atopic diseases, suggesting that shared familial factors, such as genetic background or family environment, may contribute to the co‐occurrence of atopic constitution and ADHD. 33

Beyond comorbidity patterns, the clinical presentation of AD may be related to specific behavioral problems in ADHD. For example, a cross‐sectional study in children with AD found that those with prurigo nodularis (PN), resulting from intense itching and repetitive scratching, had higher ADHD impulsivity/hyperactivity scores than those without PN, independent of AD severity. 34 This finding suggests that chronic itch–scratch cycles and difficulties in impulse control may share similar physiological‐psychological mechanisms, potentially forming a vicious cycle. 34

Divergent conclusions on causal association hypotheses

Despite substantial observational evidence of an association, some recent studies have challenged the hypothesis of a direct biological causal link between the two conditions.

Regarding cohort studies, a large population‐based cohort study using UK health records found no significant association between childhood AD and ADHD risk after extensive adjustment for confounders (HR: 1.02, 95% CI: 0.97–1.06), and severe AD was even associated with a reduced risk of ADHD (HR: 0.76, 95% CI: 0.58–0.98). 35 Possible explanations include that patients with severe AD may receive more aggressive systemic anti‐inflammatory treatment that could potentially ameliorate neuroinflammation, or that prominent AD symptoms might lead to under‐ascertainment of co‐occurring ADHD symptoms (detection bias). 35 Additionally, a bidirectional Mendelian randomization analysis found that genetically determined increased risk for AD did not lead to a higher risk of ADHD (OR = 1.02, 95% CI: 0.93–1.11), and vice versa (OR = 0.90, 95% CI: 0.76–1.07). 13 These negative findings challenge a direct causal interpretation of the AD–ADHD association. Although the “inflammation–itch–neurobehavioral axis” remains a plausible framework, the Mendelian randomization results suggest that the observed comorbidity may be largely explained by a phenocopy phenomenon: the burden of AD (chronic pruritus, sleep disruption, and psychosocial stress) produces behavioral changes that mimic ADHD, rather than AD directly causing ADHD through shared genetic or inflammatory pathways. This interpretation does not invalidate the axis but reorients it toward a non‐causal, symptom‐driven mechanism, which has important clinical implications (see the following part). This suggests that the comorbidity may not be directly driven by shared genetic underpinnings.

In treatment‐focused case‐control studies, Kruse et al. 4 found that although the proportion of children with moderate‐to‐severe AD who screened positive for ADHD on the parent‐completed Vanderbilt scale did not differ significantly from that of healthy controls, the AD group showed significantly higher frequencies of several inattention symptom items. Using objective computerized continuous performance tests in adolescents with moderate‐to‐severe AD who had not been diagnosed with ADHD, Paller et al. 18 found that attentional function in these adolescents did not differ from that expected in the general age‐ and sex‐matched population. These findings suggest that core clinical manifestations of AD itself, such as intense pruritus and sleep disruption, may lead to ADHD‐like behavioral features, which might represent a temporary functional mimicry due to symptom burden rather than an independent neurodevelopmental disorder. Furthermore, a systematic review and meta‐analysis by So et al. 36 indicated that pooling data from four studies did not reveal a significant association between eczema and ADHD, and the analysis showed no significant heterogeneity.

Therefore, in clinical practice, besides recognizing the co‐occurrence of AD and ADHD, practitioners need to distinguish between two scenarios: one is a temporary attentional and behavioral problem triggered by AD symptoms (e.g., itching and poor sleep) that may improve with better skin disease control; the other is an independent and persistent ADHD meeting full diagnostic criteria.

Broader comorbidity network

The AD–ADHD association should be understood within a broader disease context. Children with ADHD not only have a higher risk of AD but also have a significantly increased risk of asthma and allergic rhinitis. 27 , 37 This pattern suggests that the AD–ADHD link may be partly driven by a shared “atopic” or “type 2 inflammation” background. Studies confirm that children with ADHD have higher markers of allergic inflammation (e.g., total IgE and eosinophil percentage) and lower serotonin (5‐HT) levels. 38

Moreover, the neuropsychiatric comorbidity spectrum in children with AD extends beyond ADHD, including significantly elevated risks of anxiety and depression 22 , 39 , 40 and a clear association with autism spectrum disorder (pooled OR 1.87). 2 This constitutes a substantial overall neuropsychiatric comorbidity burden in children with AD that requires consideration during comprehensive assessment and management. 41 , 42

POTENTIAL MECHANISMS UNDERLYING THE ASSOCIATION

The comorbidity of childhood AD and ADHD suggests potentially shared pathophysiological foundations. Chronic type 2 inflammation accompanying skin barrier dysfunction can influence central nervous system function through multiple pathways. 5 , 6 On one hand, sleep disruption caused by intense pruritus can directly impair attention and behavioral control mediated by the prefrontal cortex; 9 , 41 on the other hand, chronic itching, as a persistent stressor, can disrupt the homeostasis of the HPA axis and neurotransmitter systems such as dopamine and norepinephrine. 10 These two pathways are not independent; sleep deprivation can exacerbate stress responses, while stress can intensify itch perception and sleep disturbances, forming a vicious cycle. Within this framework, shared genetic backgrounds between AD and ADHD (e.g., genes related to immune‐inflammatory pathways) constitute a common susceptibility basis. 11 In addition, factors such as maternal immune status during pregnancy and early environmental exposures shape the developmental trajectories of both the immune and nervous systems from early life. 43 Understanding these interactions has clinical implications for managing AD–ADHD comorbidity through multi‐dimensional strategies targeting inflammation control, sleep improvement, and stress modulation.

Immune–Inflammatory pathways

The core of AD involves type 2 inflammation mediated by interleukin (IL)‐4 and IL‐13. This chronic inflammation is not confined to the skin; it has been hypothesized to influence neurodevelopment through systemic inflammatory pathways and is linked to multiple comorbidities, including autoimmune diseases, mood disorders, and metabolic conditions. 5 , 6 Preclinical and early‐life observational evidence supports the neurodevelopmental impact of Th2 inflammation. Effects can begin early in life; a maternal Th2 inflammatory environment (e.g., elevated IL‐13) in the third trimester has been linked to childhood behavioral problems, suggesting a potential role for early immune programming. 43 Animal studies show that excessive early‐life exposure to IL‐4 can lead to reduced myelination, cognitive impairment, and developmental delay. 44 , 45 , 46 Such early immune activation can persistently alter neurodevelopmental pathways and influence immune function in adulthood. 5 , 47 , 48 Circulating inflammatory mediators can cross the blood–brain barrier or alter its permeability, subsequently activating microglia and astrocytes within the central nervous system, impairing synaptic plasticity and myelination processes. 49 These changes are particularly significant in key brain regions involved in learning, memory, and executive function (e.g., hippocampus and prefrontal cortex), potentially forming the neural basis for cognitive and behavioral disorders. In adults, functional MRI studies show that during allergic reactions, patients with AD exhibit specific activation in brain regions such as the anterior cingulate cortex and insula, which are also implicated in ADHD. 47 , 50 Direct evidence in children is currently lacking. Genome‐wide pleiotropy analysis provides genetic support for this link, indicating that AD and ADHD share multiple genetic loci enriched in inflammatory signaling pathways like TNF and JAK‐STAT. 11 In pediatric cohorts, real‐world observational data show that controlling AD inflammation with IL‐4/IL‐13 inhibitors (e.g., dupilumab) in children is associated with a significantly reduced risk of subsequent neuropsychiatric diagnoses. 51 However, this association could be explained by indirect pathways, such as improved sleep and reduced stress following better skin control, rather than direct neuroprotection. While promising, these findings do not establish causality.

Sleep disturbance

Chronic sleep disruption and fragmentation caused by intense pruritus represent the most direct and critical pathway through which cutaneous inflammation translates into daytime neurobehavioral symptoms. A meta‐analysis of observational studies indicates that AD patients with sleep disturbances have a significantly increased risk of ADHD symptoms or diagnosis (OR = 2.43). 7 Clinical studies have also observed that children with AD commonly experience difficulty initiating and maintaining sleep, the severity of which correlates with skin lesion severity and directly contributes to daytime inattention, irritability, and hyperactivity. 4 , 8 This creates a vicious cycle: itching disrupts sleep, and insufficient sleep subsequently impairs prefrontal cortex function. As one of the last brain regions to mature, the prefrontal cortex is a critical hub in limbic‐cortical circuits supporting higher‐order cognitive functions such as decision‐making, attentional control, impulse inhibition, and emotional regulation—its impairment can directly exacerbate attention and behavioral control problems and may worsen emotional stress and itch perception. 52 Additionally, AD‐related inflammatory factors may directly affect sleep–wake regulatory centers. Sleep and circadian rhythm disruption are key links connecting AD to ADHD. In mouse models, AD has been suggested to reduce melatonin and disrupt the HPA axis, while melatonin supplementation can ameliorate this imbalance and ADHD‐like behaviors. 9 It should be noted that infant use of first‐generation sedating antihistamines may be associated with an increased risk of ADHD, 53 possibly by interfering with rapid eye movement sleep and affecting brain development, warranting cautious use.

Itch‐induced neuroendocrine dysregulation

Chronic itching, as a persistent psychosomatic stressor, can lead to dysfunction in neuroendocrine and neurotransmitter systems. In children, blunted HPA axis reactivity to acute psychosocial stress has been observed in those with ADHD, including those with comorbid AD and ADHD, with reduced cortisol responses. 10 In children with AD, this HPA axis hyporeactivity is significantly associated with ADHD‐like behaviors such as inattention and impulsivity, 10 suggesting that HPA axis stress response may be an important biological pathway linking AD and ADHD. Chronic stress states can impair prefrontal cortex development and disrupt the balance of neurotransmitters like dopamine and norepinephrine, which are crucial for attention and executive function. 54 In mouse models, AD‐induced psychological stress overactivates the HPA axis and disrupts catecholamine metabolism in the locus coeruleus‐prefrontal cortex‐striatum circuit, producing neurochemical profiles resembling ADHD. This process can be reversed by melatonin intervention. 9 Furthermore, reduced 5‐HT levels are observed in individuals with ADHD, and 5‐HT may participate in inflammatory or atopic processes by influencing immune cells, potentially constituting a biochemical link between inflammation, itching, and impulsive behavior. 38 It is important to note that some AD treatments (e.g., corticosteroids) can also affect HPA axis function, complicating clinical assessment. 55

In mouse models, two recent studies have uncovered a direct neuro‐immune circuit linking psychological stress to AD exacerbation. 56 , 57 Both demonstrate that anxiety‐driven sympathetic activation releases norepinephrine, which acts via β2‐adrenergic receptors to recruit and activate basophils (via CCL6‐CCR1) and eosinophils (via CCL11‐CCR3), amplifying skin inflammation. 56 , 57 These findings provide a mechanistic anchor for the “inflammation–itch–neurobehavioral axis” proposed here. However, direct evidence in children with AD is not yet available, and this pathway remains to be validated in pediatric cohorts.

Shared genetic and environmental risk background

The initiation and perpetuation of the aforementioned pathways have a common foundation: shared genetic susceptibility and early environmental exposures. Genetic studies have not only revealed overlaps in immune–inflammatory pathway genes between AD and ADHD but also suggested other shared loci potentially related to neurodevelopment. 11 Concurrently, environmental factors begin shaping the developmental trajectories of both the immune and nervous systems from early life: maternal immune activation during pregnancy (e.g., due to allergy or infection) is a risk factor for behavioral and emotional problems in offspring, 43 maternal exposure to certain environmental pollutants or medications during pregnancy, 58 infant use of sedating medications, 53 and low birth weight 37 have also been identified as shared risk factors.

However, current models do not fully explain the association. For instance, one study did not find significant mediation of specific inflammatory factors during pregnancy or in cord blood, nor of childhood gut microbiota, in the link between childhood eczema and subsequent ADHD symptoms. 25 Another study found that children with ADHD often have reduced hemoglobin levels and elevated allergy markers, 38 which might participate in ADHD pathogenesis by affecting brain oxygen supply or neurotransmitter synthesis. These findings highlight the complexity of mechanisms in this field.

Integrated framework: the inflammation–itch–neurobehavioral axis

The evidence discussed above can be integrated into a conceptual framework, the “inflammation–itch–neurobehavioral axis,” as illustrated in Figure 1. AD‐associated type 2 inflammation drives chronic pruritus, which then triggers two interconnected pathways. The first is behavioral: itching disrupts sleep and impairs prefrontal cortex‐dependent attention and impulse control. The second is neuro‐immune. Chronic pruritus and associated anxiety activate cutaneous sympathetic nerves. Emerging evidence from animal models suggests that this sympathetic activation amplifies skin inflammation through neuro‐immune interactions involving basophils and eosinophils. These two pathways are not independent. Sleep loss worsens stress, and stress intensifies itch, forming a self‐perpetuating cycle. Clinically, interventions that target inflammation, pruritus, sleep, or stress may interrupt the cycle and improve both skin and neurobehavioral outcomes.

Schematic of the “inflammation–itch–neurobehavioral axis” linking AD to ADHD‐like behaviors in children and adolescents. AD is characterized by type 2 inflammation (IL‐4, IL‐13, TSLP) and skin barrier dysfunction, which drive chronic pruritus. Chronic itch leads to neurobehavioral dysfunction through two interconnected pathways. Pathway A (classic mechanism): Chronic pruritus disrupts sleep, leading to prefrontal cortex dysfunction, which manifests as ADHD‐like behaviors including inattention, hyperactivity, and impulsivity. Pathway B (emerging neuro‐immune circuit): Chronic pruritus and associated anxiety activate cutaneous sympathetic nerves, increasing TH+ fiber density and norepinephrine release. Norepinephrine engages β2‐adrenergic receptors on basophils and eosinophils. In basophils, this promotes CCL6 production, which recruits additional basophils via CCR1 in a positive feedback loop. In eosinophils, sympathetic activation promotes CCL11 production, recruiting eosinophils via CCR3. Recruitment and activation of these immune cells amplify skin inflammation, which in turn exacerbates pruritus, closing a self‐reinforcing vicious cycle. This pathway is currently supported by preclinical evidence and requires further validation in children. The two pathways converge to amplify both inflammation and neurobehavioral symptoms. Clinical interventions targeting skin inflammation, pruritus, sleep, or specific neuro‐immune components (e.g., β2‐adrenergic receptors, CCR1, CCR3) may disrupt this cycle and improve both dermatologic and neurobehavioral outcomes. AD, atopic dermatitis; ADHD, attention‐deficit/hyperactivity disorder; CCL, C‐C motif chemokine ligand; CCR, C‐C motif chemokine receptor; IL, interleukin; TH, tyrosine hydroxylase; TSLP, thymic stromal lymphopoietin.

CLINICAL IMPLICATIONS AND COMPREHENSIVE MANAGEMENT STRATEGIES

Based on the understanding of the AD–ADHD comorbidity, clinical practice should adopt an integrated management perspective. The key lies in proactively identifying high‐risk children. For those with moderate‐to‐severe, early‐onset (onset before age 5), or significant sleep disturbance associated with AD, screening for ADHD and related behavioral functions is recommended. This can be achieved using internationally standardized assessment tools, such as the Vanderbilt rating scales, to systematically evaluate ADHD symptoms and comorbid behavioral problems, while also paying attention to sleep quality, emotional state, and nutritional indicators. 8 , 21 , 22 , 29 , 38 , 39 , 44 , 54 , 59

Conversely, when assessing for ADHD, attention should also be paid to skin symptoms and allergic history. 27 , 37 Management strategies should revolve around the core disease mechanisms: the primary task is active control of skin inflammation and pruritus to mitigate the subsequent vicious cycle from its source, and the potential neurobehavioral benefits of emerging targeted therapies are noteworthy in this regard 51 ; secondly, direct intervention for resulting sleep disturbances and chronic stress is necessary, for example, through sleep hygiene guidance and non‐pharmacological itch management to improve daytime function 4 , 9 ; if comorbid ADHD is confirmed and impairs daily life, standardized treatment should be initiated according to ADHD guidelines.

In children with comorbid AD and ADHD, attention should be paid to the possible bidirectional effects of ADHD medications on skin symptoms and sleep. When ADHD is independently diagnosed, first‐line medications include stimulants (e.g., methylphenidate) and non‐stimulants (e.g., atomoxetine). A meta‐analysis of placebo‐controlled studies in youth with ADHD found that methylphenidate significantly increases the risk of insomnia (relative risk 2.78 for initial insomnia). 60 In children with AD, where sleep disruption already exacerbates pruritus and disease activity, stimulant‐induced insomnia may theoretically worsen the clinical course. Atomoxetine has been associated with cutaneous adverse reactions, including rash and pruritus; the same source also reports excoriation (skin picking) in 2%–4% of patients, a behavior that could potentially aggravate AD symptoms. 61 Conversely, successful treatment of ADHD might reduce impulsive scratching, although direct evidence is lacking. A retrospective cohort study found that dupilumab treatment for AD was associated with decreased subsequent use of psychostimulants for ADHD, 62 suggesting a bidirectional relationship. Currently, high‐quality evidence to guide medication choice in this specific comorbid population is insufficient. Clinicians should monitor sleep quality and skin symptoms when initiating or adjusting ADHD medications.

Comprehensive patient education and family support are essential throughout, effectively enhancing disease management capabilities and treatment adherence. 6 , 28 , 40 , 63 , 64 Effective management relies on close multidisciplinary collaboration and referral mechanisms involving dermatology, psychiatry/psychology, and developmental‐behavioral pediatrics, ensuring that the affected children receive integrated medical, psychological, and educational support, ultimately reducing their overall disease burden.

LIMITATIONS

This review has several limitations that should be considered when interpreting its findings. First, it is a narrative review rather than a systematic review. Therefore, we did not perform a formal quality assessment of included studies, and the selection of articles may be subject to selection bias. Second, the studies summarized in this review are heterogeneous in design, including cross‐sectional, cohort, case‐control, meta‐analyses, Mendelian randomization, and preclinical animal studies. This heterogeneity limits direct comparisons and the ability to draw unified conclusions. Third, diagnostic criteria and screening tools for ADHD varied considerably across studies: some used structured clinical interviews according to DSM or ICD criteria, whereas others relied on parent‐reported screening scales (e.g., Vanderbilt and CBCL). Such variability may affect the reported prevalence and strength of associations. Fourth, most of the epidemiological evidence is observational, which precludes causal inference. Although Mendelian randomization studies did not support a genetic causal link between AD and ADHD, residual confounding or unmeasured environmental factors cannot be fully excluded. Fifth, the majority of mechanistic evidence comes from preclinical animal models or adult human studies; direct evidence from pediatric cohorts is limited, particularly for the newly described neuro‐immune circuits. Therefore, the proposed “inflammation–itch–neurobehavioral axis” remains a hypothesis that requires prospective testing. Despite these limitations, this review synthesizes the best available evidence and provides a clinically useful framework for understanding the AD–ADHD comorbidity.

OUTLOOK AND SUMMARY

Current research confirms a significant comorbidity between AD and ADHD, with a more pronounced association particularly in children with early‐onset, severe AD, or those accompanied by sleep disturbances. However, genetic studies have not supported a direct causal relationship, suggesting the observed association may primarily stem from secondary consequences arising from the AD disease state. The currently more comprehensive explanatory framework is the “inflammation‐itching‐neurobehavioral axis”: AD‐related chronic inflammation and pruritus, by inducing sleep disturbances, interfering with neuroendocrine homeostasis, and other pathways, impact nervous system function and development, manifesting as behavioral features like inattention, hyperactivity, and impulsivity.

In clinical management, proactive assessment for ADHD‐related symptoms is recommended in high‐risk children with AD. Treatment should focus on effectively controlling inflammation and alleviating pruritus to ameliorate secondary sleep and behavioral issues. Future prospective studies are needed to further elucidate the non‐genetic mechanisms and to evaluate whether targeting neuro‐immune pathways can simultaneously improve both skin inflammation and neurobehavioral outcomes in affected children.
