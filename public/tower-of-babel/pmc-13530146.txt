Quantum state-to-state reaction dynamics of F + HD → HF + D via a single partial wave shape resonance state
Authors: Daofu Yuan, Jiayu Huang, Shihao Li, Wentao Chen, Chang Luo, Yuxin Tan, Zhibing Lu, Yiyang Shu, Yu Zhang, Tao Wang, Bina Fu, Xingan Wang, Dong H. Zhang, Xueming Yang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Understanding how reactions proceed through the transition state is central to chemistry, but isolating quantum resonance effects is challenging, especially above the reaction barrier where direct pathways usually dominate. Here we show that a single partial wave shape resonance, temporary trapping of the reacting partners behind an angular‑momentum barrier, can control the reaction F(2P3/2) + HD(v = 0, j = 0) → HF + D at a collision energy of 2.90 kcal mol−1. High-resolution crossed molecular beams scattering imaging reveals persistent oscillations across the full scattering‑angle range for the HF(v′=3, j′=1) product channel. Quantum dynamics calculations trace these oscillations to a quasibound D-HF(v′=3, L = 21) complex in the post-transition‑state region with a lifetime of about 50 fs. These results provide a clear, fully state‑resolved example of resonance‑governed reactive scattering above the barrier, linking selected reactant and product quantum states through an identifiable intermediate resonance state.

Introduction

Chemical reactions are often shaped by the transition-state (TS) region, which forms a dynamical bottleneck along the reaction coordinate. A quantum resonance state transiently trapped in the TS region plays a particularly interesting and important role in chemical reactions, demonstrating the quantum nature of chemical reactivity1–5. Quantum state-resolved crossed molecular beams scattering method4 as well as negative ion photodetachment spectroscopy technique6 have provided powerful tools to probe the structure and dynamics of reaction resonances in elementary chemical reactions. In a crossed molecular beam scattering experiment, one can track a chemical reaction in a quantum state-to-state chemistry manner7,8. For chemical reactions with quantum resonances, we can further identify the specific quantum resonance state of the reaction intermediate that predominantly controls the reaction dynamics, so that chemical reactions can now be investigated at the level of quantum state-to-state-to-state chemistry. In the present study, we provide such a case where the reaction resonance state in the F + HD reaction is a single, well-defined partial wave shape resonance.

The F + H2 reaction has been a benchmark system for reaction resonance studies, and it has also provided a great testing ground to develop an accurate physical picture of reaction resonances. The experimental evidence of resonance was observed in this reaction in the 1980s, when Neumark et al. detected clearly forward scattering for the HF(v′=3) product relative to the backward scattering HF products in the v′=1 and 2 states9,10. Since then, the issue of resonances in the F + H2 reaction and its isotopologues has been extensively investigated using the reactive scattering methods and theoretical dynamical calculations5,11–27, as well as the negative ion photodetachment spectroscopy technique6,28,29. Most of these studies were carried out at collisional energies below the barrier, where the reaction takes place via resonance-enhanced tunneling by mostly Feshbach resonances in which the resonance state is on the v′=3 vibrationally adiabatic potential (VAP) while the reaction occurs via coupling to the lower (v′=2,1) VAP channels. Much less attention has been given to the issue of how resonance affects the dynamics of this reaction at collision energies higher than the barrier, where the direct reaction pathway likely obscures the resonance pathway. In our previous study21, we investigated the issue of the forward scattering HF(v′=3) channel in the F + H2 reaction at a collision energy near its threshold and concluded that the forward scattering of HF(v′=3) was mainly caused by a slow-down mechanism over the exit barrier. Thus far, it has not been clearly addressed whether and how the forward scattering HF(v′=3) signal observed in the original study9,10 is related to reaction resonance in this benchmark system. This question is particularly challenging at collision energies considerably higher than the reaction barrier, where the direct pathway typically dominates, and multiple partial waves contribute, unlike in low-energy inelastic collisions where only very few partial waves contribute30–32.

Crossed-molecular-beam arrangement, coupled with time-sliced velocity map ion imaging (VMI) using the near-threshold resonance-enhanced multiphoton ionization (REMPI) scheme33, enables measurements of rotational-state-resolved differential cross sections (DCSs) with both very high translational energy resolution and high angular resolution. Using this approach, the geometric-phase effect in H + HD → H2 + D has been observed34 and a striking horseshoe-shaped feature in the HF(v′=2, j′) DCS pattern from the F + HD reaction was detected and attributed to peculiar quantum interferences between spin-orbit split partial waves27. The unique high angular resolution of this experimental setup allows us to detect subtle quantum effects in chemical reactions.

In this work, we present a high-resolution crossed-beam imaging study of the F + HD→HF + D reaction at a collision energy of 2.90 kcal/mol, which is noticeably higher than the barrier ( ~ 1.8 kcal/mol) of this reaction. Rotationally state-resolved DCSs are measured and reveal pronounced angular oscillations for the HF(v′=3, j′=1) product. Combined with quantum dynamics calculations, we demonstrate an explicit example of shape resonance in the F + HD reaction at energies above the reaction barrier.

Results

Experimental observation of pronounced oscillations for the HF(v′=3, j′=1) product in the entire scattering angular range

In this high-resolution crossed molecular beam (CMB) experiment, a pulsed beam of F atoms was generated using a pulsed discharge of a gas mixture of 5% F2 seeded in helium, and the beam velocity was 1.86 km/s with a speed ratio of 15. The HD molecular beam was produced by the supersonic expansion of HD gas through a liquid nitrogen-cooled pulsed valve at a stagnation pressure of 13 bar, resulting in a beam velocity of 1.24 km/s with a speed ratio of 30. This ensures that the HD molecules in the beam are predominantly in the lowest ro-vibrational state. Both the F atom and HD beams were collimated using double skimmers to minimize beam divergence. The two beams crossed at an angle of 160° in the scattering chamber with a center-of-mass collision energy of 2.90 kcal/mol. By employing a two-color vacuum ultraviolet and ultraviolet (VUV + UV) near-threshold laser ionization scheme for the D atom in conjunction with the time-sliced VMI technique, rotationally state-resolved DCSs with detailed angular structures of the HF (v′, j′) products were clearly acquired, as illustrated in Fig. 1a. The experimental image (Fig. 1a) reveals that the HF products are primarily populated in vibrational states v′=2 and 3. The well-resolved rings correspond to specific ro-vibrational states. The main scattering peak of the HF(v′=2) products is in the sideways-to-backward direction, but with an interesting horseshoe-shaped feature observed in the forward angular region, which is similar to the result at 2.10 kcal/mol for the same reaction27. The most interesting observation of this study is the scattering image of HF(v′=3) products shown in Fig. 1b. The angular distribution of HF(v′=3) products exhibits fine and persistent oscillations across the full scattering angular range, with a scattering maximum in the forward direction. The observed oscillations extending across the full scattering angular range are quite unusual in a reactive scattering experiment. In most reactions, even when oscillatory structures are resolved in rotational-state-specific DCSs, they are typically confined to a limited angular sector rather than persisting over the entire angular range. This is because broad-angle oscillations are readily washed out when many partial waves and direct-reaction pathways contribute. For the same reaction investigated at the collision energy of 2.10 kcal/mol, there are no clear oscillations for the HF(v′=3) product, while striking angular oscillations for this product appear at 2.90 kcal/mol.

Based on the high-resolution image shown in Fig. 1a, product translational energy distributions, P(ET), were derived as a function of scattering angle. As shown in Fig. 2, the translational energy distribution of D atoms was analyzed at a scattering angle of 54°. The rotational states of HF products were clearly resolved. This allowed us to extract the state-specific DCS for this reaction. This analysis was performed by fitting the P(ET) distributions using Gaussian functions for each rotational state of the HF products, based on the accurate energy term values of the HF ro-vibrational levels. The angular oscillations are the most pronounced for HF (v′=3, j′=1). We therefore obtained the angular distributions for the HF product in this specific quantum state by fitting the data across the whole scattering angular range. The angular distribution for this state is then plotted in Fig. 3a, with an estimated uncertainty of approximately 10%. Quantum dynamics calculations illustrating quantum state-to-state-to-state reaction dynamics

In an effort to understand these oscillation patterns observed in the experiment, we performed fully quantum mechanical state-to-state scattering calculations for F(2P3/2) + HD(v = 0, j = 0) → HF + D on the CSZ potential energy surface (PES)26,35 using the time-independent ABC program36. The calculations were carried out to full convergence, yielding product quantum state-resolved integral cross section (ICS) and DCS. The theoretical results performed at 2.90 kcal/mol are then compared to the experimental results at the same collision energy.

The calculated image is shown in Fig. 1c and is overall in excellent agreement with the experimental image (Fig. 1b) for HF(v′=3). The detailed oscillations in the theoretical calculations (Fig. 3) are also in remarkable agreement with the experimental results in the full scattering angular range. This provides a solid ground to interpret and understand theoretically the underlying dynamics of the observed oscillations for the HF(v′=3) product. From the calculated contributions of different partial waves to the ICS for the HF(v′ = 3, j′ = 1) product, shown in Supplementary Fig. 1, it is obvious that the maximum contribution comes from the J = 22 partial wave. The contribution of other individual partial waves first increases, due largely to the (2 J + 1) degeneracy factor in the ICS, then decreases as the rising centrifugal barrier suppresses reactivity. The two helicity (K) components show slightly different behaviors because different helicity paths sample different effective centrifugal potentials. For the K = 1 component, the partial-wave ICS contribution peaks at a significantly higher J value than that for K = 0, reflecting a larger centrifugal barrier in the K = 0 channel.

The most interesting result from the above analysis is the dominance of a single partial wave J = 22 in the ICS, which is highly unusual. We then calculated the DCS contribution from the J = 22 and that from all remaining partial waves, as shown in Fig. 3b. The squared scattering amplitude associated with J = 22 is defined as |f(22) | 2, and that from the coherent sum of the lower partial waves as |f(0–21) | 2. It is evident that the |f(22) | 2 term is substantially larger than that of |f(0–21) | 2 at small scattering angles and comparable to |f(0–21) | 2 at angles larger than 30°. Furthermore, the |f(22) | 2 curve itself exhibits strong angular oscillations, closely resembling the oscillation pattern observed in the experiment. This means that the single partial wave (J = 22) plays the dominant role not only in the overall forward scattering but also in the peculiar oscillation patterns. Therefore, it is particularly important to understand the dynamical origin of the single J partial wave contribution.

The dominant single partial wave contribution is seen in both ICS and DCS for the HF(v′=3, j′=1) product, implying the presence of a possible resonance state at this collision energy. We then computed the J = 22 reaction probability for HF(v′=3, j′=1) in the collision energy range from 2.4 to 3.4 kcal/mol, as shown in Fig. 4a. There is a prominent peak right at the collision energy of 2.90 kcal/mol, a clear signature of reaction resonance for this partial wave. The one-dimensional vibrationally adiabatic potentials (VAPs) for different orbital angular momentum L were calculated and shown in Fig. 4b. It is quite clear that as L increases, the centrifugal term raises the effective potential, producing a centrifugal barrier and an adiabatic well capable of supporting quasi-bound vibrational states. For the HF product with v′ = 3, j′ = 1 and total J = 22, the allowed orbital angular momentum in the product channel is L = J − j′, …, J + j′ = 21–23. In this case, the lowest value L = 21 is energetically favored, meaning that the J = 22 partial wave for the j′ = 1 product proceeds along the VAP associated with L = 21. As shown in Fig. 4b, the centrifugal barrier for L = 21 can clearly support a shape resonance state at the collision energy of 2.90 kcal/mol, leading to the HF(v′=3) product via resonance-enhanced quantum tunneling (REQT). In principle, for L values ranging from 10 to 22, each partial wave can support a shape resonance state at a specific collision energy. However, at lower collision energies, the available exit-channel energy is not sufficient to produce HF(v′ =3); thus, the reactive flux mostly couples downward to lower vibrational levels, such as v′ = 2, 1, 0, showing as Feshbach resonance features. At higher collision energies, the formation of v′ = 3 products becomes energetically accessible. However, as L increases, the increasing centrifugal contribution makes the corresponding VAP progressively shallower. Thus, it will be more difficult to support a quasi-bound state on those VAPs for larger L values. In a small energy range, it is possible that the VAP well is sufficiently deep to support a resonance state, and the energy is also above the v′ = 3 product channel. The shape resonance state HF(v′=3)-D (L = 21) is clearly such a case, which gives rise to the striking dynamical signatures of the strong forward scattering and lasting oscillations in the entire scattering angular range. Time-dependent quantum wave-packet calculations have also been performed to obtain time-independent scattering wavefunctions at the collision energy of 2.90 kcal/mol, by performing a Fourier transform of the time-dependent wavefunctions. The 2D contour plot of the wave function at 2.90 kcal/mol (Fig. 4c) clearly shows three nodes along the H–F coordinate in the D-HF complex. This three-node pattern persists into the asymptotic product region, suggesting that the resonance state is the confined (003) state in the D-HF(v′=3) VAP well with the orbital angular momentum L = 21 (rotational quantum number of the transition state complex).

Discussion

Combining state-resolved differential cross-section measurements with quantum scattering calculations, we provide direct evidence that a shape resonance imprints persistent oscillatory structures over the entire scattering angular range of the HF(v′=3, j′=1) product from the F + HD(v = 0, j = 0) reaction. The HF(v′=3, j′=1) product channel occurs mainly via the shape resonance state trapped in the D-HF(v′=3) reaction intermediate in the transition state region. This is a remarkable manifestation of quantum state-to-state-to-state chemistry (Fig. 5), in which not only the reactant and the product are in well-characterized quantum states, but the transition state of the main reaction pathway is also a quasi-trapped quantum state with all quantum numbers assignable. This study presents a compelling case for a shape resonance in a chemical reaction. It is noteworthy that the dynamics of the reaction for the HF(v′=3, j′=1) channel at this collision energy, even above the barrier where direct dynamics are expected to dominate, are largely controlled by the resonance state in the post-barrier region, rather than the traditional transition state at the barrier. The concept of transition state, which traditionally only considers the barrier top region, should thus be expanded to the entire transition state region stretching from the van der Waals complex area in the entrance channel to that in the exit channel, because quantum resonances in the van der Waals regions can also greatly affect chemical reactivity.Fig. 5Schematic diagram illustrating the state-to-state-to-state mechanism.Reactant quantum states: F(2P), HD(v = 0, j = 0); Quantized transition state: HF(v′=3)···D (L = 21); Product quantum states: HF(v′=3, j′=1), D(2S). Methods

Experimental methods

The F (2P3/2) + HD (v = 0, j = 0) → HF + D reaction was studied at a collision energy of 2.90 kcal/mol using a crossed molecular beam (CMB) apparatus equipped with time-sliced velocity map imaging (VMI). A pulsed beam of F atoms was generated by discharging a mixture of 5% F2 in He (1 μs duration, 20 Hz, stagnation pressure: 6 bar) through a General Valve, producing a beam velocity of 1.86 km/s. The contribution from spin–orbit-excited F (2P1/2) was negligible. The HD molecular beam was produced by supersonic expansion through a liquid-nitrogen-cooled Even–Lavie valve (20 Hz, HD purity: 97%, backing pressure: 13 bar). (3 + 1) resonance-enhanced multiphoton ionization (REMPI) spectroscopy near 285.6 nm confirmed that approximately 97% of HD was in the ground rovibrational state (v = 0, j = 0). The HD beam had a velocity of 1.24 km/s and a speed ratio of approximately 30. Both beams were double-skimmed and intersected at an angle of 160° in the scattering chamber.

D-atom products were detected using two-color (1 + 1′) threshold ionization. Vacuum-ultraviolet (VUV) light at 121.6 nm was generated by two-photon resonant four-wave mixing (2ω1 – ω2) in approximately 10 Torr of Kr. ω1 (212.5 nm, ~2 mJ/pulse) was provided by a dye laser pumped by the 355 nm output of an Nd:YAG laser. ω2 (846.5 nm, ~4 mJ/pulse) came from a second dye laser pumped by the 532 nm output of the same Nd:YAG laser. Fine-tuning of ω1 produced 121.6 nm light suitable for the n = 1 → 2 excitation of D atoms. A third dye laser (pumped by a separate Nd:YAG laser) delivered the UV ionization light at 364.5 nm ( ~ 8 mJ/pulse, ω3). BBO crystals were used for frequency doubling. All lasers operated at 20 Hz and were synchronized with the molecular beams and the imaging system.

The recoil velocity of the D⁺ ions was below 5 m/s. The ions were extracted by ion optics and detected using a 70-mm dual microchannel plate (MCP) coupled with a P43 phosphor screen. Time-sliced imaging was achieved by applying a 20-ns high-voltage pulse to the MCP. Signals were recorded by a CCD camera (LaVision Pro Plus 2 M) with real-time event counting. To ensure uniform detection, ω₂ was scanned across the Doppler profile of the D atom products. The speed of the product was calibrated using the O+ signal from the photodissociation of O2 at ∼225 nm, and the speed resolution is about 1% for the current imaging system.

The kinetic-energy spectrum shown in Fig. 2 is extracted at θ = 54°. The choice of 54° is not physically special. It is a representative angle where both v′ = 2 and v′ = 3 signals are sufficiently strong to demonstrate the rotational-state-resolved features on the same scale. The same fitting protocol is applied at all scattering angles to obtain the angular distribution curve of HF(v′ = 3, j′ = 1), as shown in Fig. 3a.

Time-independent calculations

The quantum mechanical scattering calculations reported in this work were performed on the improved CSZ potential energy surface (PES)26,35 using the ABC program36. The numerical parameters used in the calculations are listed in Supplementary Table 1.

At a collision energy of 2.90 kcal/mol, the calculations converge at total angular momentum J = 23, with the forward-scattering peak dominated by a single partial wave, J = 22, which contributes nearly half of the total intensity at small angles. Vibrationally resolved reaction probabilities for J = 22 (Fig. 4a) show a sharp peak at 2.90 kcal/mol in v′=3, consistent with resonant behavior. The peak in v′=3 arises from a shape resonance, where the D-HF product is transiently trapped behind the centrifugal barrier of the L = 21 vibrational adiabatic potential well. This explains the anomalous dominance of J = 22 in the forward scattering.
