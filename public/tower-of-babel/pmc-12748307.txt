Association between childhood obesity and polycystic ovary syndrome: a two-sample Mendelian randomization study
Authors: Shiyang Wei, Yafeng Wang, Niping Liu, Renfeng Zhao
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractPurposeThe causal relationship between childhood obesity and polycystic ovary syndrome (PCOS) risk remains unclear, despite established links between adult obesity and PCOS. This study employed a two-sample Mendelian randomization approach to examine whether childhood obesity might have a causal effect on PCOS development.MethodsA genome-wide association study (GWAS) identifies SNPs associated with childhood obesity based on 13848 individuals of European ancestry. Data at the summary level for PCOS were obtained from a GWAS conducted by the FinnGen consortium, which included a large sample size of 118870 individuals, consisting of 642 cases and 118228 controls. MR methods including inverse variance weighted (IVW), MR-Egger, weighted median, simple mode, and weighted mode analysis were performed in this study. Sensitivity analyses were conducted to evaluate the precision and robustness of the findings.ResultsThe impact of genetically determined childhood obesity on PCOS is substantial, as evidenced by the IVW analysis (OR = 1.507, 95% CI 1.193–1.903; P = 5.77 × 10−4) and the weighted median analysis (OR = 1.578, 95% CI 1.183–2.104; P = 1.91 × 10−3). The main findings were supported by sensitivity analyses, which yielded consistent estimates using the IVW and MR-Egger methods.ConclusionsIt is suggested that an elevated prevalence of childhood obesity could be associated with an increased risk of PCOS. The prevention of PCOS should be promoted in childhood populations with high obesity. Additional investigation is necessary to confirm these discoveries and explore the underlying biological processes.Keywords: Polycystic ovary syndrome, Childhood obesity, Mendelian randomization, Causal effect

Background

Polycystic ovary syndrome (PCOS) is a prevalent hormonal disorder that affects approximately 5 to 20% of females in their fertile period [1]. This condition is characterized by irregular menstrual cycles without ovulation, elevated levels of androgen, the formation of ovarian cysts, and potential infertility [2]. Additionally, PCOS is associated with various metabolic abnormalities, including insulin resistance, obesity, and dyslipidemia [3]. Despite ongoing research, the precise etiology of PCOS remains elusive; however, it is believed to be a multifaceted disorder influenced by genetic, hormonal, and metabolic factors [4]. Metabolic markers associated with PCOS, such as blood glucose levels and sex hormones, may not exhibit discernible changes during adolescence. The diagnostic criteria employed for identifying PCOS in adults may not adequately capture this condition in adolescents, thereby presenting a challenge in managing early-onset PCOS. The increasing prevalence of obesity worldwide has become a significant concern. Additionally, the observation of obesity has been suggested as a potential predictor for the susceptibility to various systemic ailments, encompassing metabolic disorders, diabetes, cardiovascular diseases, and cancer [5, 6]. Research has confirmed that PCOS is linked to an increased risk of adult obesity [7]. However, an increasing body of evidence suggests that the origins of PCOS may be associated with early developmental stages, potentially even during prenatal development [8, 9]. Numerous observational studies have established correlations between adverse childhood experiences and the likelihood of developing PCOS in the future [9, 10]. Additionally, childhood obesity has been identified as a potential risk factor for menstrual irregularities and PCOS in adulthood. Nevertheless, the precise causal relationship between childhood obesity and PCOS remains uncertain.

In Mendelian randomization (MR) analysis, genetic variants associated with exposure are used as instrumental variables, and the causal connections between exposures and outcomes were analyzed [11]. By virtue of the random assignment of genetic variations during conception, MR studies are less susceptible to reverse causation or confounding biases commonly encountered in conventional observational studies [12]. Specifically, the objective of this research is to employ the aforementioned technique to examine the causal relationship between childhood obesity and PCOS. The findings of this analysis are expected to provide valuable insights into the etiology of PCOS and potentially inform the development of preventive and early intervention strategies.

Materials and methods

Study design

To investigate the potential causal effects of childhood obesity on PCOS, we employed a two-sample MR analysis approach using genome-wide association studies (GWAS) data. The purpose of this study was to investigate the potential causal relationship between childhood obesity and PCOS, taking into consideration the following assumptions: the genetic instrumental variables used in the analysis should exhibit a strong and reliable association with childhood obesity; there should be no correlation between the genetic instrumental variables and the confounding factors that may influence both childhood obesity and PCOS; the genetic instrumental variables should exclusively influence the risk of PCOS through childhood obesity, as depicted in Fig. 1. The study progression is visually represented in Fig. 2, which presents a flowchart.

Data sources

We obtained GWAS information on childhood obesity and PCOS from the IEU OpenGWAS project database (https://gwas.mrcieu.ac.uk/). We selected a study that examined childhood obesity, specifically the GWAS-ID 'ieu-a-1096', which presented summary statistics for females of European descent (5530 cases and 8318 controls) and 2442739 single nucleotide polymorphisms (SNPs). Additionally, we opted for a study pertaining to PCOS, specifically identified by the GWAS-ID 'finn-b-E4_POCS', which encompassed a total of 118870 samples, consisting of 642 cases and 118228 controls, and 16379676 genotyped SNPs. All datasets were publicly accessible as summarized findings from GWAS.

Instrumental variables selection

The instrumental variables (IVs) were selected based on the following criteria. Potential IVs were identified by selecting SNPs that were associated with exposure at a locus-wide significance threshold of P < 5 × 10–6. The linkage disequilibrium (LD) between the SNPs was calculated, and only the SNP with the smallest p-value was kept if its R2 was less than 0.0001. Additionally, a clumping window size of 10000 kb was used. Excluded were the SNPs with a minor allele frequency of 0.01 or lower. When there were palindromic SNPs, the allele frequency information was used to infer the forward strand alleles. It is possible that weak instrument bias exists as indicated by conditional F-statistics of some variable MRs being less than 10 [13].

Statistical analysis

In order to ascertain the existence of a causal relationship between childhood obesity and PCOS, several analytical approaches were employed, including weighted median, MR-Egger, IVW, weighted mode, and simple mode analyses. The IVW approach was utilized to investigate the association between each SNP and PCOS by employing fixed-effects meta-analysis in conjunction with Wald estimates [14]. In cases where multiple genetic variants were present in the MR instruments, the MR-Egger regression test was employed [15]. The weighted median method was used to calculate the causal estimate for each SNP as the median of a set of ratio estimates [16].The Cochran Q-test was utilized to assess the heterogeneity among the instrumental variables. Significance testing was performed using a q-value of 0.01, following adjustment for false discovery rate (FDR). A suggestive association was observed between childhood obesity and PCOS, as indicated by a P value less than 0.05 but a q-value greater than or equal to 0.1. All statistical analyses were conducted using R version 4.3.0. For the MR analysis, the TwoSampleMR package version 0.5.7 (available at https //github.com/MRCIEU/TwoSampleMR) [17] was employed.

The current study employed an MR analysis to investigate the relationship between childhood obesity and PCOS. Specifically, the study examined the reverse association, treating childhood obesity as the risk factor and PCOS as the outcome. Based on the selection criteria, a total of 15 SNPs were identified as being associated with childhood obesity. These SNPs were used as genetic instruments to assess the exposure. The causal effect of childhood obesity on PCOS

The estimates of the causal association between childhood obesity and PCOS were consistent with two sensitivity analysis methods: inverse variance weighted odds ratio (OR) of 1.507 [95% confidence interval (CI) 1.193–1.903; P = 5.77 × 10−4) and weighted median OR of 1.578 (95% CI 1.183–2.104; P = 1.91 × 10–3). However, the MR-Egger analysis (OR = 3.249, 95% CI 1.027–10.279; P = 0.068), simple mode (OR = 1.738, 95% CI 1.035–2.919; P = 0.057), and Weighted mode (OR = 1.623, 95% CI 0.985–2.674; P = 0.080) did not yield statistically significant evidence of a causal relationship. The forest plot (Fig. 3) and scatter plot (Fig. 4) display the estimated impacts of each genetic variant on the results. In Fig. 4, the scatter plot showcases the estimated causal effects of each individual SNP on the overall risk of PCOS.

Discussion

This study aimed to explore the potential causal relationship between childhood obesity and PCOS development using a two-sample MR approach. Both the weighted median and inverse variance weighted methods consistently demonstrated that childhood obesity positively influences PCOS risk, supporting a significant causal connection where childhood obesity elevates the likelihood of developing PCOS in later life.

These findings are consistent with previous observational studies. For instance, Aarestrup et al. [8] reported that elevated childhood BMI was associated with a significantly higher risk of PCOS at ages 7 and 13. Similarly, a Northern Finnish birth cohort study revealed that women with PCOS had lower birth weight, earlier adiposity rebound, and a higher prevalence of subsequent obesity [18]. Furthermore, overweight adolescents have been shown to have an elevated risk of PCOS in adulthood compared to their normal-weight peers [19].

The present study demonstrates a significant association between elevated childhood obesity and an increased susceptibility to PCOS, consistent with previous observational investigations that have similarly reported a positive correlation between childhood obesity and PCOS [9, 20, 21]. However, our research provides more robust evidence of a causal relationship by utilizing genetic variations as instrumental variables, thereby mitigating the potential influence of confounding factors or reverse causality. Given the rising global prevalence of both conditions, these findings highlight an urgent need for early public health interventions. Addressing childhood obesity could mitigate not only PCOS risk but also its associated metabolic and reproductive sequelae, such as insulin resistance, type 2 diabetes, and infertility [22, 23]. Despite the potential to avert PCOS and mitigate obesity-related complications in individuals with PCOS, investigations into the prospective effects of childhood obesity on PCOS remain limited. Such research could greatly impact women’s health, particularly those with high-risk factors for overweight/obesity.

Despite the lack of clarity regarding the underlying mechanisms, numerous hypotheses have been put forth to elucidate the link between childhood obesity and PCOS. Primarily, a correlation has been observed between adult obesity, metabolic disorders such as type 2 diabetes, and the elevated prevalence of childhood obesity [24, 25]. The mechanisms underlying this relationship remain incompletely understood but may involve endocrine and metabolic pathways. Childhood obesity is often accompanied by excess adiposity, which elevates circulating levels of estrogen, leptin, and pro-inflammatory cytokines, potentially disrupting ovarian function and folliculogenesis [26–28]. Furthermore, a higher body mass index during childhood is indicative of a higher degree of insulin resistance [29]. Additionally, higher childhood BMI is correlated with insulin resistance [30], which exacerbates hyperandrogenism and anovulation in PCOS [31–33]. Ethnic variations in cardiometabolic risk trajectories further suggest that the strength of these associations may differ across populations [34, 35], highlighting the need for diversified cohort studies.

Limitations

Several limitations of this study should be acknowledged. First, some SNPs used as instrumental variables had F-statistics below 10, indicating potential weak instrument bias that might affect MR estimates. Second, differences in the diagnostic criteria for PCOS across the original GWAS could introduce heterogeneity. Third, all summary-level data were drawn from European-ancestry populations, limiting the generalizability of our findings to other ethnic groups. Fourth, the number of genetic instruments was relatively small (n = 14), which may restrict statistical power and precision. Finally, while the MR design reduces confounding, unmeasured pleiotropy may still influence the results. Although sensitivity analyses did not detect horizontal pleiotropy, residual bias cannot be entirely ruled out.

Conclusion

In summary, this MR study provides genetic evidence supporting a causal effect of childhood obesity on PCOS risk. These results reinforce the importance of early-life weight management as a potential strategy for PCOS prevention. Future studies should prioritize larger, multi-ethnic GWAS cohorts with uniform PCOS diagnostic criteria, incorporate stronger genetic instruments, and explore mediating physiological pathways linking early adiposity to PCOS pathogenesis.
