Stable inversion of potential in nonlinear wave equations with cubic nonlinearity
Xi Chen, Shuai Lu, Ruochong Zhang

Abstract
. This paper investigates inverse potential problems of wave equations with cubic nonlinearity. We develop a methodology for establishing stability estimates for inversion of lower order coefficients. The new ingredients of our approach include trilinear approximations of nonlinear response operators, symbol estimates of distorted plane waves, and lower order symbol calculus.

Unique inversion of coefficient for hyperbolic equations has been studied extensively. For time-independent cases, Belishev–Kurylev’s boundary control method

Stability of inversion is more involved and entails subtle quantitative estimates for local measurements. The framework of boundary control is essentially qualitative and thus unlikely to deliver desired quantitative results. On simple Riemannian manifolds, Stefanov–Uhlmann
[
34
]
reduced the stable determination of coefficients for linear wave equations to that of metric rigidity
[

]
and X-ray transforms
[
32
]
. In the non-simple case, Bao–Zhang
[
2
]
established sensitivity results on manifolds with fold-regular conditions by utilizing Gaussian beams.

The research of stable inversion for nonlinear hyperbolic equations is rather recent. Lassas–Liimatainen–Potenciano-Machado–Tyni
[
23
,
24
,
22
]
established stability results for semilinear wave equations in bounded domains. They proved that the coefficient of nonlinear terms can be stably recovered from full boundary measurements as long as the observation errors of Dirichlet-to-Neumann maps are small.

We aim to develop a general framework of stable inversion of lower order coefficients for nonlinear hyperbolic equations with a small amount of datum. Compared with stability for linear equations, uniqueness of recovery, and inversion in full data DtN models, we encounter the following novel hurdles :

Issue 1
.

How to measure nonlinear response operators?

Unlike linear equations, there is no natural operator space to accommodate nonlinear response operators. However, it is important in stability problems to convert the error estimates for nonlinear response operators to certain operator norms. For this purpose, we introduce some trilinear response operators to approximate nonlinear source-to-solution maps for (
1.1
). The difference of two such trilinear operators turns out to be the leading part of the difference of two source-to-solution maps. Therefore, it is legitimate to employ the norms of trilinear operators to measure nonlinear response operators.

Issue 2
.

How to establish quantitative estimates for underlying coefficients?

For the purpose of uniqueness, it suffices to prove injectivity results for some transformations. For hyperbolic PDEs, this is usually achieved by doing singularity analysis and removing non-singular and less singular terms. With regard to stabilities, the key matter is that those neglected terms in uniqueness problems, such as smooth terms, will no longer be negligible but become unavoidable errors. In order to quantify to what extent such errors affect inversion results, it has to entail rather precise quantitative estimates for relevant transformations. The trilinear response operators are essentially trilinear Fourier integral operators and their symbols actually include some integral transformations of underlying coefficients. Hence some refined symbol calculi and estimates are pivotal to derive quantitative estimates for those transformations.

Issue 3
.

How to capture quantitative data of potentials in nonlinear equations?

Zero-order coefficients are less visible than others, as potentials never appear in the principal terms of our local measurements. Consequently, routine principal symbol calculus provides no information for them. To remedy this, we have to go further and analyse transport equations for lower order symbols to detect integral transformations of potentials. In additional, the product in nonlinearity severely complicates the symbolic structure of nonlinear conormal waves. Therefore, we perform
subtle order analysis to find out the exact leading terms and subleading terms in the expansion of multilinear conormal waves.

Issue 4
.

How to achieve stable inversion with a small amount of datum?

Compared with classical DtN models, the source-to-solution type measurements made in Theorem
1.1
carry rather limited data. DtN models utilize the boundary information to recover coefficients in the interior, while Theorem
1.1
requires only the knowledge in a small spatial neighbourhood to reconstruct the coefficients in the exterior. Such amount of information is proven to be insufficient for inverse coefficient problems of time-dependent linear equations, see e.g.
[
35
]
. For nonlinear equations, one may however exploit the nonlinear scattering data to prove the uniqueness pointwisely. On the other hand, the measurement for stability problems, which is in the form of Sobolev norms of solutions, is far less informative than the pointwise knowledge of solutions in uniqueness problems. Therefore, we employ more robust tools, such as energy estimates and stationary phase estimates, to deal with such measurement of neighbourhoodwise type.

In summary, we propose the following roadmap to solve stable inversion problems of lower order coefficients in nonlinear wave equations.

Estimates for symbolic errors

To focus on how to remedy the issues above, we look only at the cubic wave equation in Minkowski spacetime in the present paper. In ongoing and future projects, we shall develop this framework for more general semilinear and quasilinear models in globally hyperbolic Lorentzian manifolds.

2.2.
Principal symbol calculus

Next, we shall go a bit further to compute the principal symbol and lower order symbols of a paired conormal distribution
u
along a bicharacteristic curve
\beta(s):=(\gamma(s),\dot{\gamma}^{\flat}(s))
emanating from
(\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{\xi})\in\partial\Lambda_{1}
, where the superscript
\cdot^{\flat}:T_{p}X\rightarrow T_{p}^{\ast}X
denotes the musical isomorphism. Without loss of generality, we translate

to

By
[

\sigma[u]:=\hat{u}\omega
with non-vanishing smooth half-density
\omega

\mathscr{L}_{H_{\Box}}(\hat{u}\omega)=\left(H_{\Box}(\hat{u})+\frac{1}{2}\left(\mathrm{div}\,H_{\Box}\right)\hat{u}\right)\omega.

After plugging this into the transport equation (
2.3
), we restrict to the bicharacteristic curve
\beta(s)
and divide the equation by

. It then gives

H_{\Box}\hat{u}(\beta(s))+\frac{1}{2}\left(\hat{u}\cdot\mathrm{div}\,H_{\Box}\right)(\beta(s))=0.

By denoting the integral factor

(2.4)

\rho(s):=\frac{1}{2}\int_{0}^{s}\mathrm{div}\,H_{\Box}(\beta(t))dt

and using the fact

H_{\Box}\hat{u}(\beta(s))=\partial_{s}(\hat{u}\circ\beta)(s),

we reduce the transport equation to

\partial_{s}(\hat{u}\circ\beta)(s)+\hat{u}(\beta(s))\partial_{s}\rho(s)=0.

Multiplying the equation by

leads to

(2.5)

\left\{\begin{aligned} \partial_{s}\left(e^{\rho(s)}\hat{u}\circ\beta(s)\right)&=0\\
\hat{u}(\beta(0))&=\hat{u}(0,\boldsymbol{\xi}),\end{aligned}\right.

which amounts to

(2.6)

e^{\rho(s)}\hat{u}(\beta(s))\equiv\hat{u}(0;\boldsymbol{\xi}).

Write

(2.7)

\alpha(\beta(s)):=e^{-\rho(s)}\omega(\beta(s))\omega^{-1}(\beta(0)).

By the proof of
[

\sigma[u](\beta(s))=\alpha(\beta(s))\sigma[u](\beta(0)).

If

(2.9)

e^{\rho(s)}\hat{u}(\gamma(s),\lambda\dot{\gamma}^{\flat}(s))=\lambda^{\mu}e^{\rho(s)}\hat{u}(\beta(s)),\quad\lambda>0.

2.3.
Lower order symbol calculus

For a solution
u
to (
2.1

where

solves the free wave equation

\Box u^{\mathrm{fre}}=f

and

is the remainder. Then it is clear from Proposition

\left\{\begin{aligned} u^{\mathrm{fre}}&\in I^{\mu}(\Lambda_{0},\Lambda_{1})\quad\mbox{with $\sigma\left[u^{\mathrm{fre}}\right]=\sigma[u]$};\\

In virtue of the identities

(\Box+V)u^{\mathrm{rem}}=(\Box+V)u-\Box u^{\mathrm{fre}}-Vu^{\mathrm{fre}}=-Vu^{\mathrm{fre}},

the principal symbol of

satisfies the following transport equation

\displaystyle\begin{cases}\mathscr{L}_{H_{\Box}}\sigma\left[u^{\mathrm{rem}}\right]=-\imath V\sigma[u]\quad&\text{on }\Lambda_{1}\backslash\partial\Lambda_{1},\\
\sigma\left[u^{\mathrm{rem}}\right]=-V\sigma[\Box]^{-1}\sigma[u]\quad&\text{on }\Lambda_{0}\backslash\partial\Lambda_{1},\\
\sigma\left[u^{\mathrm{rem}}\right]=-V\mathscr{R}\left(\sigma[\Box]^{-1}\sigma[u]\right)\quad&\text{on }\partial\Lambda_{1}.\end{cases}

Denoting
\sigma[u]:=\hat{u}\omega
and
\sigma\left[u^{\mathrm{rem}}\right]:=\hat{u}^{\mathrm{rem}}\omega

After restricting to the bicharacteristic curve
\beta(s):=(\gamma(s),\dot{\gamma}^{\flat}(s))
and removing the half-density
\omega
, we obtain

H_{\Box}\hat{u}^{\mathrm{rem}}(\beta(s))+\frac{1}{2}\left(\hat{u}^{\mathrm{rem}}\mathrm{div}\,H_{\Box}\right)(\beta(s))=-\imath V(\gamma(s))\hat{u}(\beta(s)).

It follows that

\partial_{s}\left(e^{\rho(s)}\hat{u}^{\mathrm{rem}}(\beta(s))\right)=-\imath V(\gamma(s))e^{\rho(s)}\hat{u}(\beta(s)),

which is solved by

(2.10)

\displaystyle e^{\rho(s)}\hat{u}^{\mathrm{rem}}(\beta(s))

\displaystyle=\hat{u}^{\mathrm{rem}}(\beta(0))-\imath\int_{0}^{s}V(\gamma(\tilde{s}))e^{\rho(\tilde{s})}\hat{u}(\beta(\tilde{s}))d\tilde{s}

\displaystyle=\hat{u}^{\mathrm{rem}}(0,\boldsymbol{\xi})-\imath\hat{u}(0,\boldsymbol{\xi})\int_{0}^{s}V(\gamma(\tilde{s}))d\tilde{s},

with initial value given by

\sigma\left[u^{\mathrm{rem}}\right](0,\boldsymbol{\xi})=-V(0)\mathscr{R}\left(\sigma[\Box]^{-1}\sigma[u]\right)(0,\boldsymbol{\xi}).

Plugging (
2.7
) and (
2.8

\sigma[u^{\mathrm{rem}}](\beta(s))=\alpha(\beta(s))\sigma[u^{\mathrm{rem}}](0,\boldsymbol{\xi})-\imath\sigma[u](\beta(s))\int_{0}^{s}V(\gamma(\tilde{s}))d\tilde{s}.

The first term on the RHS of (

Proposition 2.2
.

Away from

Proof.

Proposition

3.
Nonlinear conormal waves

In this section, we shall elucidate the special solutions, which are of great use for inversion of potential for (
1.1
).

In contrast with linear waves, nonlinear waves can propagate anomalously, i.e. the singularities of nonlinear waves propagate not only along the bicharacteristic curves. However, Bony
[
4
,
5
]
, Rauch-Reed
[
29
]
, and Melrose-Ritter
[
26
]
proved for conormal waves that the spreading of such singularities is limited to the production of conormal singularities. Such phenomena can be exploited to construct special solutions for inversion.

Conceptually, we would like to make special nonlinear waves such that

•

the sources are small and made within
\mho
;

•

the nonlinear waves are approximated by linear waves;

•

the waves travel out of
\mho
and return back;

•

the nonlinear scattering in
\mathbb{D}\setminus\mho
can be observed in
\mho
.

Then we can show that some scattering transforms encoded in such special solutions recover the desired coefficients in certain stable ways. This technique was pioneered by
[
21
]
, where
4
linear waves were utilized to approximate quadratic nonlinearity. The strategy in the present paper however invokes
3
linear waves for (
1.1
). This was formulated in
[
6
]
for cubic equations and applied to more general cases in
[
12
,
13
]
. In contrast with the principal symbol calculus and qualitative methods therein, we conduct symbol calculus, not only for principal terms but also for remainder terms, to extract the quantitative information of the potentials.

3.4.
Trilinear response operators

For any
f\in\mathscr{B}^{s}_{\varrho}(\mho),
the solution to (
1.1
) admits the Taylor’s expansion (
3.7
). Hence, the source-to-solution map
\mathcal{P}_{V,h}
, restricted to
\mathscr{B}^{s}_{\varrho}(\mho)
, takes the explicit form

\displaystyle\mathcal{P}_{V,h}:\mathscr{B}^{s}_{\varrho}(\mho)

\displaystyle\longrightarrow\mathscr{H}^{s}(\mho)

\displaystyle f

\displaystyle\longmapsto\sum_{j=1}^{3}u_{(j)}\varepsilon_{(j)}+\frac{1}{6}u_{(123)}\varepsilon_{(1)}\varepsilon_{(2)}\varepsilon_{(3)}+O(|\varepsilon|^{4})+C^{\infty}(\mho).

We want to measure the discrepancy of two source-to-solution maps
\mathcal{P}_{V,h}
and
\mathcal{P}_{\tilde{V},\tilde{h}}
provided that
(V,h)
and
(\tilde{V},\tilde{h})
agree within
\mho
. We denote by
\tilde{u}
the solution with respect to
(\tilde{V},\tilde{h})
.

Since
\mho
is geodesically convex, the first order terms
u_{(j)}
are linear conormal waves propagating within
\mho
and thus carry no information from
\mathbb{D}\setminus\mho
. Since the underlying coefficients are known in
\mho
, the first order terms with respect to
(V,h)
and
(\tilde{V},\tilde{h})
must agree therein, modulo a smooth function, regardless of inputs. Namely,

u_{(j)}|_{\mho}=\tilde{u}_{(j)}|_{\mho}\mod C^{\infty}(\mho),\quad\forall f\in\mathscr{H}^{s}(\mho).

Therefore, it is the third order term
u_{(123)}
that serves as the leading term with information from
\mathbb{D}\setminus\mho
. It can be viewed the restriction to
(\mathscr{H}^{s}(\mho))^{3}
of a trilinear operator, which is defined by

\displaystyle T_{V,h}\left(f_{(1)},f_{(2)},f_{(3)}\right):=u_{(123)}|_{\mho},\quad\mbox{for $f_{(j)}\in\mathscr{H}^{s}(\mho)$.}

Lemma 3.3
.

The map
T_{V,h}
is a bounded trilinear operator,

\displaystyle\mathscr{H}^{s}(\mho)\times\mathscr{H}^{s}(\mho)\times\mathscr{H}^{s}(\mho)

\displaystyle\longrightarrow\mathscr{H}^{s}(\mho)

\displaystyle\left(f_{(1)},f_{(2)},f_{(3)}\right)

\displaystyle\longmapsto u_{(123)}|_{\mho},

with operator norm

\left\|T_{V,h}\right\|:=\sup\left\{\|u_{(123)}\|_{\mathscr{H}^{s}(\mho)}:\left\|f_{(j)}\right\|_{\mathscr{H}^{s}(\mho)}=1\right\}.

Proof.

The trilinearity is immediate from (
3.4
) and (
3.6
).
Since
u_{(j)}
solves (
3.4
), the existence theorem for linear wave equations and finite speed propagation yields

\|u_{(j)}\|_{\mathscr{H}^{s}(\mathbb{D})}\lesssim\|f_{(j)}\|_{\mathscr{H}^{s}(\mho)}.

Moreover, applying the existence theorem to (
3.6
) and Schauder’s lemma
[
28
, Theorem 6.2.2]
to product
hu_{(1)}u_{(2)}u_{(3)}
shows that

\|u_{(123)}\|_{\mathscr{H}^{s}(\mho)}\lesssim\|hu_{(1)}u_{(2)}u_{(3)}\|_{\mathscr{H}^{s}(\mathbb{D})}\lesssim\prod_{j=1}^{3}\|f_{(j)}\|_{\mathscr{H}^{s}(\mho)}.

∎

Lemma 3.4
.

Let the observation error
\delta>0
be small. If
\mathcal{P}_{V,h}-\mathcal{P}_{\tilde{V},\tilde{h}}
satisfies (

) in the cubic semilinear model,

Let

be the solution to (
1.1
) with source

of the form (
3.3
), and

the solution of (

. It follows that

Applying

\displaystyle u

\displaystyle=\sum_{j=1}^{3}\varepsilon_{(j)}u_{(j)}-Q\left(h\left(\sum_{j=1}^{3}\varepsilon_{(j)}u_{(j)}-Q(hu^{3})\right)^{3}\right).

Noting

\displaystyle u

\displaystyle=\sum_{j=1}^{3}\varepsilon_{(j)}u_{(j)}-Q\left(h\left(\sum_{j=1}^{3}\varepsilon_{(j)}u_{(j)}\right)^{3}\right)\mod|\varepsilon|^{5}\mathscr{B}_{1}^{s}(\mho)

\displaystyle=\sum_{j=1}^{3}\varepsilon_{(j)}u_{(j)}-\sum_{\alpha_{1}+\alpha_{2}+\alpha_{3}=3}\frac{3!}{\alpha_{1}!\alpha_{2}!\alpha_{3}!}Q\left(h\prod_{j=1}^{3}\varepsilon_{(j)}^{\alpha_{j}}u_{(j)}^{\alpha_{j}}\right)\mod|\varepsilon|^{5}\mathscr{B}_{1}^{s}(\mho),

within
\mho

For any

and

\lx@add@cssclass{ltx_eqn_lefteqn}$\displaystyle\mathcal{P}_{V,h}\left(\sum_{j=1}^{l}\varepsilon_{(k_{j})}f_{(k_{j})}\right)$\mbox{}\hfil\\
=\sum_{j=1}^{l}\varepsilon_{(k_{j})}u_{(k_{j})}-\sum_{\alpha_{k_{1}}+\cdots+\alpha_{k_{l}}=3}\frac{3!}{\alpha_{k_{1}}!\cdots\alpha_{k_{l}}!}Q\left(h\prod_{j=1}^{l}\varepsilon_{(k_{j})}^{\alpha_{k_{j}}}u_{(k_{j})}^{\alpha_{k_{j}}}\right)\mod|\varepsilon|^{5}\mathscr{B}_{1}^{s}(\mho)

within
\mho

\displaystyle:=\mathcal{P}_{V,h}\left(\varepsilon_{(k_{1})}f_{(k_{1})}+\varepsilon_{(k_{2})}f_{(k_{2})}\right)-W_{V,h}(k_{1})-W_{V,h}(k_{2})

such that

|\nabla V|\leq M,\quad|\nabla\tilde{V}|\leq M\quad\text{on }\mathbb{D}.

We consider a function

(4.12)

l(s):=V(y)-\tilde{V}(y)-2\sqrt{2}Ms,\quad\forall s\in[0,s_{\mathrm{out}}].

and claim

(4.13)

\displaystyle V(\gamma_{\mathrm{out}}(s))-\tilde{V}(\gamma_{\mathrm{out}}(s))\geq l(s),\quad\forall s\in[0,s_{\mathrm{out}}];

(4.14)

\exists s^{\ast}\in(0,s_{\mathrm{out}}]
such that
l(s^{\ast})=0
.

If (
4.13
) is not true, then there exists
s_{0}\in[0,s_{\mathrm{out}}]
such that

(4.15)

V(\gamma_{\mathrm{out}}(s_{0}))-\tilde{V}(\gamma_{\mathrm{out}}(s_{0}))<l(s_{0}).

Since
V(y)-\tilde{V}(y)>0
, we have
s_{0}\neq 0
. Then there exists
s_{1}\in(0,s_{0}]
such that

\displaystyle\left(V(\gamma_{\mathrm{out}}(s_{0}))-\tilde{V}(\gamma_{\mathrm{out}}(s_{0}))\right)-\left(V(\gamma_{\mathrm{out}}(0))-\tilde{V}(\gamma_{\mathrm{out}}(0))\right)

\displaystyle=s_{0}\times\left.\frac{d}{ds}\left(V(\gamma_{\mathrm{out}}(s))-\tilde{V}(\gamma_{\mathrm{out}}(s))\right)\right|_{s=s_{1}}\geq-2\sqrt{2}Ms_{0}.

However, (
4.15
) yields that

\displaystyle\left(V(\gamma_{\mathrm{out}}(s_{0}))-\tilde{V}(\gamma_{\mathrm{out}}(s_{0}))\right)-\left(V(\gamma_{\mathrm{out}}(0))-\tilde{V}(\gamma_{\mathrm{out}}(0))\right)

\displaystyle<l(s_{0})-V(\gamma_{\mathrm{out}}(0))+\tilde{V}(\gamma_{\mathrm{out}}(0))

\displaystyle=V(\gamma_{\mathrm{out}}(0))-\tilde{V}(\gamma_{\mathrm{out}}(0))-2\sqrt{2}Ms_{0}-V(\gamma_{\mathrm{out}}(0))+\tilde{V}(\gamma_{\mathrm{out}}(0))

\displaystyle=-2\sqrt{2}Ms_{0}.

Moreover, (
4.14
) readily follows from
(V-\tilde{V})|_{\mho}\equiv 0
.

Combining (
4.13
) and (
4.14
) together shows that

\int_{0}^{s_{\mathrm{out}}}V(\gamma_{\mathrm{out}}(s))-\tilde{V}(\gamma_{\mathrm{out}}(s))ds-\int_{s^{\ast}}^{s_{\mathrm{out}}}V(\gamma_{\mathrm{out}}(s))-\tilde{V}(\gamma_{\mathrm{out}}(s))ds\\
=\int_{0}^{s^{\ast}}V(\gamma_{\mathrm{out}}(s))-\tilde{V}(\gamma_{\mathrm{out}}(s))ds\geq\int_{0}^{s^{\ast}}l(s)ds>0

Write
\gamma_{\mathrm{out}}(s^{\ast}):=y^{\ast}
. If
y^{\ast}\in\mho
, the second term on the LHS vanishes. If
y^{\ast}\notin\mho
, The LHS is equal to

I_{V-\tilde{V}}(y,z)-I_{V-\tilde{V}}(y^{\ast},z),

and bounded from above by
C\delta^{\mu_{0}}
. Thus,

0<\int_{0}^{s^{\ast}}l(s)ds\leq C\delta^{\mu_{0}}.

Moreover, (
4.14
) implies

s^{\ast}=\frac{V-\tilde{V}}{2\sqrt{2}M}.

Integrating
l(s)
from
0
to
s^{\ast}
yields that

\frac{\left|V(y)-\tilde{V}(y)\right|^{2}}{4\sqrt{2}M}\leq C\delta^{\mu_{0}},

which proves the proposition.
∎
