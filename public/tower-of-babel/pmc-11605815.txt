Development, validation and economic evaluation of a machine learning algorithm for predicting the probability of kidney damage in patients with hyperuricaemia: protocol for a retrospective study
Authors: Zhengyao Hou, Yong Yang, Bo Deng, Guangjie Gao, Mengting Li, Xinyu Liu, Huan Chang, Hao Shen, Linke Zou, Jinqi Li, Xingwei Wu
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractIntroductionAccurate identification of the risk factors is essential for the effective prevention of hyperuricaemia (HUA)-related kidney damage. Previous studies have established the efficacy of machine learning (ML) methodologies in predicting kidney damage due to other chronic diseases. Nevertheless, a scarcity of precise and clinically applicable prediction models exists for assessing the risk of HUA-related kidney damage. This study aims to accurately predict the risk of developing HUA-related kidney damage using a ML algorithm, which is based on a retrospective database.Methods and analysisThis retrospective study aims to collect clinical data on outpatients and inpatients from the Sichuan Provincial People’s Hospital, China, covering the period from 1 January 2018 to 31 December 2021 with a focus on patients diagnosed with ‘hyperuricaemia’ or ‘gout’. Predictive models will be constructed using techniques such as data imputation, sampling, feature selection and ML algorithms. This research will evaluate the predictive accuracy, interpretability and fairness of the developed models to determine their clinical applicability. The net benefit and net saving will be calculated to gauge the economic value of the model. The most effective model will then undergo external validation and be made available as an online predictive tool to facilitate user access.Ethics and disseminationThe Ethics Review Committee at Sichuan Provincial People’s Hospital granted approval for the ethical review of this study without requiring informed consent. The findings of the study will be disseminated in a peer-reviewed journal.

Introduction

Hyperuricaemia (HUA) is a medical condition characterised by elevated levels of uric acid (UA) in the bloodstream resulting from metabolic abnormalities.13 Research involving cell culture, animal models and epidemiological studies has shown that HUA can induce renal damage through urate deposition, leading to the onset of acute kidney injury, chronic kidney disease (CKD), urate nephropathy and related complications.48 The treatment of HUA-related kidney damage is both expensive and of limited efficacy, which significantly affects the quality of life and prognosis for patients.9 10 For instance, CKD is significantly exacerbated by the economic burden of renal replacement therapy in patients with end-stage renal disease, along with the increased risks of morbidity and mortality associated with related complications.11

Prophylactic interventions are essential in the management of HUA-related kidney damage. Previous studies have identified numerous risk factors for HUA-related kidney damage, including UA levels, disease duration, gender, age and dietary patterns.101214 Effective management and intervention for these risk factors can prevent renal damage. Nevertheless, the risk-based preventive strategy is inherently passive and may result in the excessive use of preventive interventions, thereby heightening the burden on patients. For proactive prevention, the accurate prediction of HUA-related kidney damage is crucial. Traditional regression prediction methods, although interpretable and theoretically sound, are constrained in their ability to manage complex data structures, thus failing to satisfy clinical prediction requirements. Recently, machine learning (ML) techniques have gained prominence in clinical prediction models, offering the capability to manage complex nonlinear relationships and to adapt automatically to diverse data patterns through feature learning.15

Previous research has confirmed the effectiveness of ML techniques in predicting kidney damage due to chronic conditions such as diabetes and hypertension.1619 To the best of our knowledge, sophisticated ML methodologies have not been used to predict HUA-related kidney damage. The accuracy and clinical applicability of current studies are constrained by the lack of a robust framework for model development and evaluation, in addition to the challenges posed by sample heterogeneity. This study seeks to use real-world data from a Chinese population to create and validate ML prediction models for the clinical assessment of kidney damage risk in patients with HUA. These models are intended to improve patient risk stratification and will be rigorously evaluated across various dimensions, including data preprocessing, feature selection, model performance, interpretability, fairness, economic value and external validation. The predictive algorithm will be made available for clinical application through a publicly accessible online platform.

Methods and analysis

Data collection

This retrospective study aims to collect data on outpatient and inpatient encounters for patients diagnosed with ‘hyperuricaemia’ or ‘gout’ at Sichuan Provincial People’s Hospital, China, between 1 January 2018 and 31 December 2021. The inclusion criteria for individual patients will be age 18 years or older, a requirement of at least two clinic visits and a minimum interval of 1 year between these visits. Qualified professionals will conduct data extraction in accordance with established protocols to ensure the acquisition of comprehensive diagnostic and therapeutical data for the study cohort. The data collected will include basic demographic information for both outpatient and inpatient populations, along with comprehensive details on diagnoses, medication prescriptions, laboratory test results, physical examinations and related costs. In the data extraction process, each patient’s unique identifier will be explicitly defined to maintain data confidentiality and enable data linkage. Furthermore, the timing of reporting for each indicator will be established during extraction to support longitudinal analysis of medication usage patterns.

The primary outcome measure of this study will be HUA-related kidney damage, which involves the activation of the renin-angiotensin-aldosterone system, the deposition of UA crystals and inflammatory processes.20 21 Kidney damage will be defined as a urinary albumin excretion rate (UAER) of ≥30 mg/day or an estimated glomerular filtration rate (eGFR) of <60 mL/(min·1.73 m2).22 Patients with pre-existing kidney damage at the time of the initial visit or who do not undergo UA, UAER or eGFR tests at any follow-up visits will be excluded from the study. This study aims to forecast the short-term and long-term impacts of HUA on kidney damage across observation intervals of 6 months, 1 year and 2 years.

Data cleaning

Clinical data within Hospital Information Systems and Laboratory Information Systems may be prone to errors or inconsistencies during data entry and processing. Hence, we will standardise the nomenclature for diagnoses, medications and laboratory tests by referencing the ‘Tenth Revision of the International Statistical Classification of Diseases and Related Health Problems’ (ICD-10), the ‘Drug Registration Information’ provided by the National Medical Products Administration and the ‘Directory of Clinical Laboratory Tests for Medical Institutions’ issued by the National Center for Clinical Laboratories.

We will conduct descriptive statistical analyses of the data to assess logical relationships, consistency, completeness and the presence of outliers. Data identified as problematic will be categorised, summarised and managed through deletion, replacement or retention based on the error type and its impact level. A subset of data samples will be randomly selected for detailed examination and comparison to verify the accuracy and completeness of the dataset. A comprehensive log of modifications and traces will be kept, and data will be indexed by unique patient identifiers. This method enables the merging of tables with distinct information while safeguarding patient privacy and avoiding the disclosure of personal data. The raw data will be converted from a long to a wide format, facilitating statistical analysis with each row corresponding to a sample and each column to a variable. The corresponding information will be recorded at the intersections of the rows and columns. All steps, decisions and findings during the data cleansing process will be meticulously documented for audit and to facilitate subsequent data processing.

Data preprocessing

Data prescreening entails the exclusion of variables with inadequate information from the dataset, determined by a specified threshold of missing data. In this study, we will evaluate the completeness of each variable and use those with adequate completeness (greater than 20%) as inputs for the ML algorithm. Variables with more than 80% missing data, over 80% single-value occurrences and coefficients of variation less than 0.1 will be excluded.23 Missing data for the remaining variables will be imputed using four methods: no imputation, simple imputation, random forest imputation and a modified random imputation approach.23 To mitigate the adverse effects of data imbalance on predictive accuracy, we will employ diverse data sampling methods including no sampling, random upsampling, random undersampling, the Synthetic Minority Oversampling Technique (SMOTE) and, its variant, Borderline SMOTE.23 The selection of input variables will be contingent on the completeness of the data collected. These variables will encompass demographic attributes (eg, age and gender), disease-related factors (eg, diagnosis, comorbidities and pertinent test outcomes) and medication attributes (eg, medication for the disease and associated comorbidities). Prior to training the ML model, three feature selection techniques will be employed to determine the variables for inclusion: all-variable inclusion, Lasso regression and the Boruta algorithm.24

Model development

Sixty datasets will be generated using four data-filling techniques, five data sampling techniques and three feature screening techniques. The model’s output variable will be the occurrence of kidney damage, a binary categorical variable. Input variable types may include binary, continuous, multinomial unordered and multinomial ordered variables. Considering that model performance can be affected by various input variable types, we will use multiple ML algorithms to model the data, aiming to identify the algorithm that achieves optimal performance on the development dataset. The following ML algorithms will be employed for modelling: Logistic Regression, Decision Tree, AdaBoost, Gradient Boosting, XGBoost, CatBoost, lightGBM, K-Nearest Neighbors, Random Forest, Stochastic Gradient Descent, Support Vector Machines and an Ensemble method.2326 The ensemble algorithm will aggregate the predictions of the top five models, assess their performance with the area under the receiver operating characteristic curve (AUROC) and derive the final results via a voting mechanism.24

The dataset will be divided into a training set and a test set in an 8:2 ratio.23 The model will then be trained on the training set to minimise the loss function. Furthermore, a 10-fold cross-validation will be conducted on the training dataset. This involves dividing the dataset into ten equally sized folds. The algorithm will be trained on nine folds (constituting 90% of the training data) and validated on the remaining fold (constituting 10% of the training data). The performance metrics will be averaged across all iterations to obtain a final result.

Model evaluation

After training all the models, we will use quantitative metrics such as AUROC, accuracy, precision, recall, F1 score and area under the precision-recall curve to assess model performance and determine the best predictive model. The AUROC is deemed the primary evaluation metric, where higher values signify a superior model performance.25 To assess model interpretability, we will use Shapley Additive Explanation (SHAP).24 SHAP values assess the individual contribution of each feature to the predictive model’s output, representing an advanced technique for interpreting ML models. SHAP summary plots, integrating feature importance and effects, display the SHAP value for each feature across all samples, quantifying the feature’s influence (positive or negative) on the prediction. Stratified analysis will be employed to evaluate the fairness of the ML model, which is gauged by the level of association between the model’s predictions and sensitive attributes. To explore potential gender-specific risk factors for kidney damage and to determine if gender-stratified models enhance performance (evaluated by AUROC), we will construct gender-stratified models.27 This is important because blood UA thresholds may differ between genders. This study will evaluate the model’s fairness by considering additional factors, such as age.

Sample size validation will be conducted to evaluate the effect of sample size on enhancing model prediction accuracy.23 The training set will be randomly resampled at intervals of 10%, 20%, 30%…up to 100% to assess the model’s predictive performance using the test set. This procedure will be iterated 100 times, and the outcomes will be depicted in a line graph to ascertain the optimal sample size for the model that exhibits the highest performance.

Economic evaluation

To thoroughly evaluate the predictive efficacy and economic implications of the model, we will employ the metrics of net benefit and net saving to assess its economic value. By analysing these metrics, our objective is to determine the model that offers the greatest economic utility. In the evaluation process, we will use the confusion matrix to quantify the predictions in terms of true positives (TP), false positives (FP), true negatives (TN) and false negatives (FN).

We will calculate the net benefit across different threshold probabilities and then conduct a decision curve analysis. The threshold probability is the predictive probability that triggers a therapeutical intervention. Sensitivity is defined as the proportion of positive cases correctly identified by the model, and it is calculated as Sensitivity = TP / (TP + FN). Specificity is defined as the proportion of negative cases correctly identified by the model, and it is calculated as Specificity = TN / (FP + TN). Prevalence denotes the proportion of the disease within the development dataset. The net benefit is computed using the formula Net Benefit = Sensitivity × Prevalence − (1 − Specificity) × (1 − Prevalence).

In calculating net saving, we will refer to the relevant literature to obtain detailed cost and health outcome data, including the per capita prevention costs (PPC), per capita treatment costs (PTC) and the success rate for kidney damage prevention (PSR). Avoided costs represent the treatment costs for patients successfully prevented and are calculated as Avoided costs = PSR × TP × PTC. Total costs consist of the prevention cost (PC), the direct treatment cost for unprevented cases (DCUC) and the indirect treatment cost due to prevention failure (ICPF). PC for predicted positive cases is calculated as PC = PPC × (FP + TP). DCUC is calculated as DCUC = PTC × FN. ICPF is calculated as ICPF = (1 − PSR) × TP × PTC. The formula for calculating net saving is: Net Saving = Avoided costs − Total costs.

External validation

The training dataset for the model will be sourced exclusively from the Sichuan Provincial People’s Hospital in China. Consequently, the model’s generalisability to populations with characteristics substantially different from those in the training dataset may be limited. To evaluate the model’s ability to generalise across diverse populations, data from the Second People’s Hospital of Shandong Province will be used for external validation. The Sichuan and Shandong provinces are geographically separated by approximately 1500 kilometres, and there are significant differences between their populations in terms of physical constitution, dietary habits and lifestyle. Should the external validation reveal a decrease in model performance, we plan to incorporate these external validation datasets into the training set to improve the model’s generalisation capacity. To enhance the model’s generalisability, it is essential to consider integrating data from a wider range of regions and more diverse populations into the training set. In subsequent research, we intend to collaborate with other research institutions to continuously perform external validation studies.

Open clinical prediction tool

This study will employ the top-performing predictive algorithms to create an open-access clinical prediction tool, which will be available as a website accessible on desktops, tablets and smartphones. Throughout the study, Python (The Python Software Foundation, Fredericksburg, USA) will be used. Hyperparameter tuning will be conducted in accordance with the guidelines provided by the Python libraries.

Statistical analysis

Categorical variables will be presented as absolute numbers and frequencies, whereas continuous variables will be characterised by medians and interquartile ranges. Model performance metrics will be computed with a 95% CI. Given that this is a retrospective study, a post hoc analysis will be performed to evaluate the sample size at an alpha level of 0.05.

Guidelines

The study will be reported following the Transparent Reporting of a multivariable prediction model for Individual Prognosis or Diagnosis (TRIPOD): the TRIPOD statement.28

Ethics and dissemination

This endeavour will necessitate a dataset originally pertaining to clinical diagnosis and treatment to be cleaned and organised. It is important to obtain ethical approval, including informed consent from patients, before using any clinical diagnostic and treatment data. Since the study will use retrospective data, the Ethics Review Committee of the Sichuan Provincial People’s Hospital has approved the ethical review for this study, waiving the requirement for informed consent. The findings of this study will be submitted for publication in a peer-reviewed journal. To protect patient confidentiality, the data will not be disseminated. Data collection and preprocessing for this project are ongoing, with analyses scheduled to commence in April 2024. The project is anticipated to be finalised by December 2024.

Discussion

This study aims to develop a ML-based clinical prediction model for identifying the risk of HUA-related kidney damage.29 Initially, data collection and preprocessing will be conducted to establish a high-quality dataset for model development. This will include preliminary data screening to exclude irrelevant variables, missing value imputation to ensure data completeness, sampling to balance data distribution and feature selection to identify key factors associated with the risk of kidney damage. Subsequently, a variety of ML algorithms will be employed for model development, with algorithmic comparison and optimisation aimed at constructing an efficient and robust prediction model. The developed model’s performance will be comprehensively evaluated to ensure its stability and reliability for practical applications. Finally, the study will assess the model’s generalisability through external validation. This model is anticipated to provide a scientific foundation and technical support for the early detection and prevention of HUA-related kidney damage.

To facilitate informed decision-making regarding HUA treatment, the accurate prediction of HUA-related kidney damage is critical. Psychological studies have shown that humans have limitations in considering multiple variables for judgement, whereas predictive models can process a large number of variables and are typically more accurate and less susceptible to bias. ML algorithms have been demonstrated to be an effective approach for developing patient-specific predictive tools that support decision-making in clinical practice. An online predictive tool for HUA-related kidney damage could assist internists in identifying the most beneficial personalised treatment regimen for each patient.30 31 In instances where the estimated risk is high, pharmacological interventions should be contemplated.3235

In this study, we will use real-world data from local healthcare institutions to develop ML algorithms and predictive tools. This approach differs from the conventional strategy of employing large healthcare databases for model development.16 36 By employing this methodology, we seek to enhance the external validity and applicability of the model to the local population. In subsequent stages of the research, we will incrementally integrate data from other regions and diverse populations to further refine the model and enhance its performance across diverse geographic areas and demographic segments.

Compared with studies relying on traditional regression models for prediction, ML techniques are better suited for handling large-scale sample sizes and complex data structures.15 16 This study offers significant advantages over similar research employing ML, particularly in the following aspects.3638 We will quantify the economic value of the computational model by computing its net benefit and net saving. The assessment of the model’s economic value aids in the reduction of unnecessary medical costs. Furthermore, to comprehensively evaluate the model’s generalisability, we will validate its predictive performance in new regions and diverse populations, ensuring its adaptability across different settings and demographics. This study will also delve into the interpretability of ML models, aiming to elucidate the mechanisms behind the predictions, thereby enhancing model transparency and clinical credibility.39 To facilitate the model’s application in clinical practice, we plan to deploy it on an accessible online platform, allowing clinicians to use this tool conveniently in their medical decision-making.

It is critical to regularly check data integrity and labelling throughout the model development process. Any errors in data cleaning and preprocessing can significantly impact the predictive performance of the model by artificially inflating or deflating its accuracy. Transparency and comprehensive documentation of the data cleaning and preprocessing process are essential to facilitate review and validation. Defining outcome labels has a significant impact on model performance and ultimate utility. Many clinical concepts lack computable definitions, and the available definitions are often created for large-scale epidemiological studies rather than patient-level predictions. This study will gather input from clinicians and informatics specialists to ensure the accurate delineation of target endpoints, recognising that differing definitions of outcomes may impact the inclusion criteria for various patient cohorts. It is crucial to acknowledge that ML prediction models for binary outcomes are primarily suited for risk stratification, and the interpretation of individual risk should be contextualised within the clinical scenario when supporting medical decision-making processes.
