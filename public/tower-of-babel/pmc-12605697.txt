Lablab purpureus bioactive fractions as potent anticancer agents: an in vitro and in Silico study
Authors: Smitha S. Bhat, Sushma Jahagirdar, Sarana Rose Sommano, D. Rashmi, Pradeep Matam, Sheikh F. Ahmad, Haneen A. Al-Mazroua, Gamaleldin I. Harisa, Shiva Prasad Kollur, Chandan Shivamallu, Shashanka K. Prasad
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundPlant-based bioactive compounds are essential for the discovery of drugs, especially in cancer research, because they include a wide range of chemicals and are very specific to certain types of cells. In earlier research, Lablab purpureus (LP), a legume rich in nutrients and with ethnomedicinal significance, showed encouraging antioxidant and anticancer potential. However, the specific bioactive components that cause these effects are still unknown. The objective of this study was to use a bioactivity-guided approach to isolate and characterise cytotoxic fractions from LP seed methanol extract, followed by in vitro and in silico evaluation.MethodsColumn chromatography and liquid-liquid partitioning were used to separate the fractions from the LP seed methanol extract. DPPH, FRAP, and ABTS assays were performed to determine the individual fractions with high antioxidant activity. Lablab Purpureus Methanol seed extract Methanol fraction (LPMMF 1.5), was hence subjected to MTT assay against MCF-7 and A549 cells for 24, 48, and 72 h. Further, dual AO/EtBr staining was performed to evaluate apoptosis induction. FTIR and HR-LCMS analyses were performed to identify the phytocompounds in LPMMF 1.5. The identified compounds were subjected to ADME screening, molecular docking, MM-GBSA, 500 ns molecular dynamics simulations, per-residue energy decomposition, and metadynamics to evaluate their stability in binding with cancer-specific targets (PARP1, CDK2, and CCNB1).ResultsLPMMF 1.5 had the most antioxidant activity and the strongest cytotoxicity of the tested fractions, with IC50 values of 36.96 µg/ml (MCF-7) and 76.14 µg/ml (A549) at 72 h. AO/EtBr staining revealed the induction of apoptosis in treated MCF-7 and A549 cells. Picroside-1 and other bioactives were identified by HR-LCMS. In silico studies revealed that Picroside-1 formed very stable complexes with PARP1, CDK2, and CCNB1, and the docking scores of Picroside-1 were found to be better than those of standard inhibitors. MD simulations and MM-GBSA confirmed that the binding was stable and that important residues were involved. Metadynamics provided additional validation of the binding energetics.ConclusionsThis study identified LPMMF 1.5 as a bioactive fraction with significant antioxidant and anticancer activity. Picroside-1 emerged as a key compound, exhibiting strong affinity and stable interactions with cancer-relevant targets. These findings support its potential development as a plant-derived anticancer lead candidate.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1007/s12672-025-03904-2.

Background

Natural compounds derived from plants are crucial resources for pharmacological discovery, making significant contributions to primary health and medical research [1]. Remarkably, over half of the prescription cancer pharmacotherapies originate from plant-based natural substances, which are essential in the battle against cancer [2]. Specifically, in oncology, owing to their varied chemical compositions and biological functions, bioactive molecules are useful as they aid in the advancement of novel and comprehensive cancer-treating approaches, providing abundant benefits, including structural complexity and evolutionary refinement for biological purposes [3, 4]. Furthermore, natural products often possess a multitude of bioactive compounds that exhibit greater chemical diversity than traditional synthetic small molecule libraries, thereby increasing the probability of uncovering cancer-fighting agents [5].

Lablab purpureus (LP) is a nutritionally significant and most diverse domesticated legume species, renowned for its versatile and multifunctional utilities. It has been utilized in traditional medicinal practices to cure gastrointestinal disorders and other ailments. Its flowers were used in Ayurveda to treat uterine inflammation and promote menstruation, while its seeds were used as remedies for cholera, diarrhoea, and food poisoning. Packed with fiber, proteins, carbs, and minerals, it promotes digestive health and is a cheap staple food in southern India [6]. It can also be used as cattle feed. Despite its advantages, the crop is still underutilized because of its limited palatability and anti-nutritional qualities, even though its bioactive compounds have encouraging biomedical potential. Flavonoids (luteolin, apigenin, genistein), isoflavones, lectins (FRIL, dolichin), alkaloids, phenolics, saponins (lablabosides A–F), terpenoids, are all found in this plant. Numerous pharmacological effects are attributed to these bioactives. According to pharmacological studies, L. purpureus has potent anti-inflammatory, anti-bacterial, antifungal, antiviral, anti-diabetic, hepatoprotective, hypolipidemic, anti-anemic, anticoagulant, and insecticidal qualities. It is noteworthy for its anticancer potential, as compounds like genistein, lectins, brassinolides, and spermidine/spermine induce apoptosis, inhibit angiogenesis, modulate metastasis, and regulate multiple signaling pathways (e.g., NF-κB, STAT3, TGF-β, VEGF). Pharmacological research, phytochemical characterization, and ethnobotanical data point to L. purpureus as a valuable but underutilized crop with substantial biomedical potential. The development of affordable, plant-based treatments for cancer, infections, and chronic illnesses may result from additional mechanistic research and clinical validation [6].

Oxidative stress, often associated with the onset, progression, and resistance to treatment of cancer, is characterized by an imbalance between the generation of reactive oxygen species (ROS) and the body’s antioxidant defenses. Excess ROS not only cause lipid peroxidation, protein oxidation, and DNA damage (mutations, strand breaks), but they also change redox-sensitive signaling pathways that control cell survival, proliferation, and apoptosis, such as NF-κB, MAPK, and p53. Antioxidants reduce oxidative DNA damage and pro-tumorigenic signaling, which mitigates these effects by scavenging ROS and restoring redox homeostasis. Importantly, it has been shown that a range of antioxidants found in natural products can either directly block ROS-induced genomic instability that encourages tumor growth or (ii) change the redox balance of cancer cells to specifically induce apoptosis in malignantly transformed cells. ROS refers to a group of unstable, reactive, partially reduced oxygen derivatives, including singlet oxygen, hypochlorous acid, hydrogen peroxide, the superoxide anion, and the hydroxyl radical [56–58]. In both healthy and malignant cells, these serve as second messengers in cell signaling and are vital for a number of biological functions. ROS function as signaling transducers at low to moderate concentrations, promoting angiogenesis, drug resistance, invasion, migration, and proliferation of cancer cells (58–60). In other words, the homeostasis of cancer cells, which is involved in the development of cellular processes like proliferation, differentiation, migration, and cell death, depends on sufficient levels of ROS. High ROS levels, on the other hand, damage cancer cells and ultimately lead to cell death (61). Numerous studies have shown that immunotherapies and other anticancer treatments that alter ROS levels exhibit encouraging in vitro and in vivo outcomes. ROS are produced by the majority of chemotherapeutic agents, and many of them can change the redox homeostasis of cancer cells [56]. Anthracyclines (doxorubicin, epirubicin, and daunorubicin), alkylating agents, platinum coordination complexes (cisplatin, carboplatin, and oxaliplatin), and camptothecins (topotecan and irinotecan) are the major drugs that increase ROS in cancer cells (62). Two main causes of the increase in ROS in response to chemotherapeutic agents are the production of ROS in the mitochondria and the suppression of the cellular antioxidant system [56]. For instance, one of the most popular and successful chemotherapeutic agents for a variety of cancers, cisplatin, causes mitochondria-dependent ROS, which leads to nuclear DNA damage and ultimately cell death. The production of ROS linked to cisplatin is caused by a direct impact on mitochondrial DNA, which hinders the production of electron transport chain proteins (63).

Previous studies on LP phytochemicals extracted from different parts of the plant have demonstrated the antioxidant and cytotoxic potential of crude extracts [6]. Previously, we have ascertained the potent antioxidant, anti-inflammatory, and anticancer efficacy of the methanolic crude extract of LP seeds (LPM) against MCF-7 (breast) and A549 (lung) cancer cells. LPM was shown to cause apoptosis, and S phase, and G2/M phase cell cycle arrest in MCF-7 and A549 cells, respectively [7]. In addition, LP seeds and their copper oxide nanoparticles have been identified to have antiviral activity against bovine alpha herpes virus [8]. A specific bioactive substance called genistein, a soy isoflavonoid, has also been found in LP. It has been shown to have anticancer properties and is presently undergoing clinical trials to determine its anti-breast cancer effectiveness [9]. However, the exact bioactive compound(s) responsible for the anticancer activities of the L. purpureus is/are yet to be identified.

Bioactivity-guided fractionation techniques play an essential role in natural product discovery because they allow the separation of the bioactive fraction from complex mixtures, including plant, fungal, or marine extracts. Specifically, in the framework of antitumour drug design research, these approaches aim at uncovering candidate therapeutic agents by establishing a relationship between the structure of a chemical compound and biological action [10, 11]. As previously determined, LPM was found to demonstrate potent anticancer activity against MCF-7 and A549 cells; hence, in this study, the bioactivity-directed fractionation of the LPM crude extract was performed to identify the most potent bioactive fraction, and its anticancer activity was validated on the same MCF-7 and A549 cell lines.

Methods

Extraction

Lablab purpureus (LP) pods were purchased as a single batch from a single local vendor in the Mysore district of Karnataka and authenticated at the Indian Council of Agricultural Research – Indian Institute of Horticulture Research, Bangalore, India (Supplementary Fig. 1). The seeds were separated from the pods by hand and washed with tap water before drying in the shade for ten days, and pulverised with a mixer grinder. For the extraction, 60 g of seed powder was processed with 300 ml of methanol for 4 h at 50 °C using the Soxhlet method [7, 12]. The extracted phytochemicals were filtered (with Whatman filter paper No. 1), and the filtrates were concentrated using rotary evaporation, followed by air-drying. The respective extract was labelled LP Methanol extract (LPM).

Liquid-Liquid (L-L) partition chromatography

500 mg of Lablab purpureus methanol seed extract (LPM) was dissolved in 10 ml of methanol and subjected to separation with the liquid-liquid partition chromatography using methanol, chloroform, and hexane solvents [13]. The solvent-soluble fractions were collected, air-dried, and labelled as LPMMF (LPM Methanol Fraction), LPMCF (LPM Chloroform Fraction), and LPMHF (LPM Hexane Fraction), respectively.

TLC

Silica gel 60 Å F254 TLC plates with a thickness of 250 μm were used for the TLC analysis. 1.5 µl of LPMMF, LPMCF, and LPMHF samples dissolved in methanol (10 mg/ml) were spotted onto the TLC plate and allowed to dry. A mixture of chloroform and methanol in a 9.5:0.5 ratio was used as the mobile solvent system. The spot end of the TLC plate was later dipped into the mobile phase. The plate was removed after the run and allowed to dry. The spots were visualized using visible light, short-wave ultraviolet (254 nm), and long-wave ultraviolet (366 nm) light. Column chromatography

500 µg of LPMMF, the methanol fraction obtained after L-L extraction, was dissolved in 3 mL of chloroform. The sample was subjected to column chromatography using a glass column (length 60 cm x 1.5 cm) comprising 25 g of silica gel (60–120 mesh). The sample was eluted with 50 ml of a mixture of eluents (methanol and chloroform) in a gradient of concentrations, with a gradual decrease in chloroform and an increase in methanol concentration (Fig. 1). 5 ml fractions were collected continuously. The flow rate was set to 1 ml/minute. The fractions were labelled LPMMF 1.1 to LPMMF 1.10 and so on till LPMMF 7.1 to LPMMF 7.10, as per the order of the elute obtained.

2.Ferric reducing antioxidant power (FRAP) assay: The FRAP assay was conducted in accordance with Benzie and Strain’s (1999) protocol [15]. A freshly prepared FRAP reagent comprising 2.5 ml of 10 mM 2,4,6-trypyridyl-s-triazine heated to 50 °C for 5 min, 2.5 ml of 20 mM FeCl3 solution, and 25 ml of 300 mM acetate buffer at pH 3.6 was used in the assay. The samples (LPMMF pools at a 1 mg/ml concentration) or ferrous sulfate standards at different concentrations (200, 400, 600, 800, and 1000 µg/ml) were incubated with 190 µl of the reagent for 30 min in a darkened room. A ferrous sulphate standard curve was used to convert the absorbance, which was measured at 593 nm, to FRAP units.3.Assessment of total phenol content (TPC): TPC was measured using the Folin-Ciocalteu (FC) method, which was standardized to microtiter plate volumes [16]. 150 µl of 10% FC reagent was combined with 30 µl of the fraction that had been dissolved in 95% methanol. This mixture was incubated in the dark for one hour before 120 µl of a 6% sodium bicarbonate solution was added. The absorbance was measured at 760 nm. The standard used was gallic acid, and the standard curve that was produced was given as milligrams of gallic acid equivalent (GAE) per gram of dry extract.The pool demonstrating the highest free radical scavenging activity, ferric oxide reducing potential, and TPC was selected, which was found to be LPMMF 1 in this case, for individual testing of the fractions. Antioxidant activity evaluation assays (DPPH, FRAP, and ABTS assays) were performed on the individual fractions present within the LPMMF 1 pool to identify the most antioxidant fraction.

8.Molecular docking and dynamics simulations of phytochemical-protein interactions, energy decomposition, trajectory, and metadynamics analysis: The identified phytochemicals were further subjected to ADME property prediction using the Qikprop application in Schrodinger [30]. Phytochemicals identified using HR-LCMS, with adequate ADME properties were subjected to molecular docking against the proteins, cell division protein kinase 2 (CDK2: PDB 3IG7, 1.80 Å, chain A, sequence length 299 residues), poly [ADP-ribose] polymerase 1 (PARP1: PDB 7KK2, 1.70 Å, chain A, sequence length 352 residues), and cyclin B1 (CCNB1: PDB: 4Y72, chain B, sequence length of 273, resolution of 2.30 Å). In addition to the selected phytochemical ligands, the standard pre-bound ligands in the protein 3IG7 and 4Y72 (EFP and LZ9, respectively) were used for comparison, as well as the standard drug Talazoparib for 7KK2. The selected proteins and ligands were prepared, and receptor grids were generated around the active sites. Further, the ligands were docked against the proteins with the Schrodinger glide suite (v 14.2) [31–40]. Protein-ligand complexes with the highest number of interactions and lowest glide gscore energies were further validated with molecular dynamics (MD) simulations. The protein-ligand complexes were submerged in orthorhombic simulation boxes filled with a TIP3P solvent system, and the OPLS5 force field was applied. The temperature and pressure were set to 300 K and 1.01325 bar, respectively, and the total simulation duration was 500 ns. Additionally, thermal MM/GBSA was employed to investigate the energy stability of all the complexes. The MMGBSA was executed utilizing a script to compute the binding free energy of the complexes. 9.Metadynamics analysis: The equilibration stage for the metadynamics simulations followed the same protocol as described for the MD simulations. Metadynamics production runs were performed for 50 ns each to calculate the binding free energies of the ligands bound to their respective proteins. The collective variables included the distance and the centre of mass between the ligand and the amino acid residues involved in key interactions. A Gaussian hill width of 0.05 Å was maintained consistently across all runs. The simulations employed the NPT ensemble, with key parameters including a Gaussian hill height of 0.03 Kcal/mol deposited at intervals of 0.09 ps. The temperature was maintained at 300 K using the Nose-Hoover chain thermostat with a relaxation time of 1 ps, and the pressure was stabilized at 1.01325 bar using the Martyna-Tobias-Klein barostat with a relaxation time of 2 ps in isotropic coupling mode. Statistical analysis

All experiments were conducted in triplicate, and the results were expressed as the mean of 3 independent experiments ± SD, which were calculated using GraphPad Prism version 10. The results were subjected to two-way ANOVA to analyse the differences in treatment (of sample, and positive controls) and no treatment, wherever applicable. Tukey’s post hoc test was used after ANOVA and the “p” value of < 0.05 was considered significant.

LPMMF 1.5 fraction exhibited the highest antioxidant activity

Preliminary evaluation of the antioxidant potency of the pooled fractions was evaluated using the DPPH assay. Among these pools, LPMMF 1 and LPMMF 2 pools exhibited the highest DPPH free radical scavenging activity with 62.7 ± 0.89% and 42.37 ± 1.02% DPPH radical scavenging activity, respectively (Fig. 3a). Based on these results, both pools were further analysed using the FRAP assay, where LPMMF 1 exhibited the highest FRAP value (400.95 ± 1.05 µmol Fe²⁺ equivalents per gram of fraction) compared to LPMMF 2 (246.24 ± 0.68 µmol Fe²⁺ equivalents per gram of fraction) (Fig. 3b and c). Further, the TPC of the fraction pools was measured using a gallic acid standard curve. LPMMF 1 and LPMMF 2 were found to have 0.0256 mg and 0.0172 mg Gallic acid Equivalent per gram of dry fraction, respectively. Based on its higher phenolic content and antioxidant activity, the LPMMF 1 pool was selected for further evaluation of its constituent fractions with antioxidant assays.

Fractions LPMMF 1.1-LPMMF 1.10 (from LPMMF 1 pool) were assessed for their free radical scavenging activity and ferric ion-reducing ability. Among these, LPMMF 1.5 showed the highest free radical scavenging, ferric ion reducing ability (77 ± 1.41% DPPH radical inhibition, 580 ± 2 µmol Fe²⁺ equivalents per gram of LPMMF 1.5, respectively), followed by LPMMF 1.8 (68.5 ± 0.707% inhibition and 470 ± 2 µmol Fe²⁺ equivalents per gram of LPMMF 1.8) (Fig. 4a, b). Hence, ABTS scavenging activity was determined only for the LPMMF 1.5 and LPMMF 1.8 fractions. Among the tested samples, Ascorbic acid standard exhibited 88.15% scavenging activity at 1000 µg/ml. In comparison, LPMMF 1.5 and LPMMF 1.8 displayed moderate scavenging activities, with 72 ± 0.76% and 63.83 ± 0.35% inhibition at 1000 µg/ml, respectively. A concentration-dependent increase in radical scavenging activity was observed for all tested samples. LPMMF 1.5 consistently showed higher activity than LPMMF 1.8 across all concentrations. However, both extracts had lower activity than the ascorbic acid positive control. Notably, the radical scavenging activity of LPMMF 1.5 increased significantly from 24.86% (62.5 µg/ml) to 72% (1000 µg/ml), while LPMMF 1.8 exhibited a similar trend, reaching 63.83% at 1000 µg/ml (Fig. 4c). Further qualitative phytochemical analysis of LPMMF 1.5 fraction revealed the presence of major phytochemical groups, including glycosides, cardiac glycosides, carbohydrates, phenols, steroids, and terpenoids, highlighting potential bioactive properties (Table 4).

Chemical characterization of LPMMF 1.5 fraction led to the detection of many phytochemicals

FTIR: LPMMF 1.5 fraction was characterized using FTIR, ¹H NMR, and ¹³C NMR spectroscopy. The FTIR spectrum displayed multiple characteristic absorption bands, indicating a wide range of functional groups across the mixture (Fig. 15). A broad band at 3369 cm⁻¹ suggests the presence of O–H or N–H groups, while strong absorptions at 2919 and 1650 cm⁻¹ correspond to aliphatic C–H and carbonyl (C = O) stretching, respectively. The band at 2130 cm⁻¹ may reflect the presence of nitriles or alkynes, and several peaks between 1400 and 1000 cm⁻¹ indicate C–H bending, C–O, and C–N stretching. The complex fingerprint region (below 1000 cm⁻¹) supports the presence of multiple structurally diverse compounds.

Molecular dynamics simulations (MDS) lasting 500 ns each were used to evaluate the structural stability, binding interactions, and dynamic behaviour of the 7KK2-Picroside-1, 7KK2-Talazoparib, and 7KK2-Lactitol complexes. Each system was built using the OPLS5 force field and solvated with approximately 13,000 water molecules to ensure physiological relevance. Upon analysis, the backbone RMSD of 7KK2 in all three complexes remained within 1.0 Å to 3.0 Å, suggesting structural stability throughout the simulations. However, subtle variations were observed, with the 7KK2-Picroside-1 complex showing RMSD fluctuations from 1.2 Å to 2.8 Å, stabilizing after 100 ns, the 7KK2-Lactitol complex showing similar RMSD behaviour, maintaining stability early in the simulation with fluctuations between 1.0 Å and 3.0 Å, and the 7KK2-Talazoparib complex exhibiting a slightly lower fluctuation range of 1.2 Å to 2.7 Å, indicating a highly stable protein-ligand system (Fig. 19a-c). RMSF evaluation showed that flexible loop regions exhibited the highest deviations, while α-helices and β-strands remained structurally stable (Fig. 19d-f).

In the 7KK2-Picroside-1 complex, ARG878 exhibited a combination of backbone donor, backbone acceptor, and side-chain donor interactions between the ligand and protein, maintained for 95% and 78% of the trajectory, with an energy contribution of -7.34 Kcal/mol. TYR896 functioned predominantly as a backbone donor, contributing to 98% of the simulation with an interaction energy of -5.77 Kcal/mol. Water-mediated interactions were significant, with ASP770 forming water bridges for 95% of the trajectory and ASP766 contributing for 47% of the simulation (Fig. 20a, b). The 7KK2-Lactitol complex demonstrated strong binding stability, with HIS862 (ΔGbind of -5.83 Kcal/mol), TYR907 (ΔGbind of -8.17 Kcal/mol), and TYR896 (ΔGbind of -7.12 Kcal/mol) contributing significantly to the ligand’s binding free energy. Hydrophobic contacts and water-mediated bridges further strengthened ligand accommodation within the active site (Fig. 20c, d). In the 7KK2-Talazoparib complex, ARG878 maintained backbone donor interactions for 81% of the trajectory, contributing ΔGbind of -2.69 Kcal/mol, while TYR896 played a key role as a backbone donor for 99% of the simulation with an energy contribution of -5.63 Kcal/mol. Water bridges were prominently formed by ASP770 (85%, ΔGbind of 1.12 Kcal/mol) and GLY863 (84%, ΔGbind of 0.74 Kcal/mol). Additionally, HIS862 exhibited hydrophobic π-π stacking interactions with the ligand for 80% of the trajectory, contributing − 4.65 Kcal/mol (Fig. 20e, f) .

The protein structure in the 7KK2-Picroside-1 complex contributed to an overall percentage of 51.18% secondary structural elements (SSEs) with 30.85% helices and 20.33% strands, confirming that 7KK2 maintained a well-preserved fold. In comparison, while the protein in the 7KK2-Lactitol complex maintained 30.75% helices and 18.92% strands, resulting in 49.67% SSEs, the 7KK2 protein in the 7KK2-Talazoparib complex had a similar composition with 30.85% helices and 19.13% strands, totalling 49.98% SSEs. The ligand RMSD in 7KK2-Picroside-1 fluctuated between 0.8 Å and 3.2 Å, stabilizing between 1.6 Å and 2.8 Å during the final 200 ns. The 7KK2-Lactitol complex exhibited stable ligand RMSD behaviour, indicating that Lactitol remained within the binding site without significant displacement. 7KK2-Talazoparib showed a much more constrained fluctuation range between 0.5 Å and 0.75 Å, suggesting a highly stable binding conformation. The radius of gyration (rGyr) values further supported these observations, with 7KK2-Picroside-1 fluctuating between 4.2 Å and 5.1 Å. 7KK2-Lactitol also displayed a stable rGyr, confirming a well-folded structure, and 7KK2-Talazoparib maintained a more compact range of 3.8 Å to 3.9 Å. Talazoparib exhibited the highest stability with the lowest ligand RMSD (0.5 Å–0.75 Å), a compact rGyr (3.8 Å–3.9 Å), and minimal solvent exposure (25 Å²–75 Å²), meanwhile, Picroside-1, demonstrated strong binding interactions, showed slightly higher fluctuations in ligand RMSD (0.8 Å–3.2 Å) and SASA (40 Å²–120 Å²), indicating moderate solvent exposure. Lactitol, on the other hand, showed strong binding contributions from HIS862, TYR907, and TYR896, but exhibited moderate stability compared to Talazoparib (Fig. 21a-c).

Metadynamics analysis identified Picroside-1 to be a stable ligand that bound to the chosen proteins

The Free Energy Surface (FES) is a crucial output of metadynamics simulations, representing the free energy landscape of a system as a function of selected collective variables. In ligand binding and unbinding studies, the FES provides insights into the most stable binding states, the energy barriers between different conformations, and the pathway for ligand dissociation. Energy minima, or wells, correspond to the most stable ligand binding positions—the deeper the well, the more stable the interaction. The energy needed for a ligand to change binding modes or completely dissociate is represented by energy barriers, which show up as peaks between minima. Therefore, the FES produced by these simulations provides crucial information about the dissociation pathways, binding strength, and ligand stability.

The binding stability and unbinding mechanisms of three ligands—Picroside-1, EFP, and lactitol—with the 3IG7 protein were examined using metadynamics simulations over a 50 ns time span (Fig. 29). Two major energy minima at roughly 5.5 Å and 8 Å were seen in the FES of Picroside-1, indicating two stable binding conformations. While the free energy increased dramatically beyond 9 Å, indicating a significant energetic barrier to unbinding, the global minimum, approximately − 14 Kcal/mol, indicated moderate stability. This implied that Picroside-1 might undergo conformational transitions prior to total dissociation, even though it could bind steadily. Lactitol, on the other hand, showed a global minimum of about − 100 Kcal/mol, indicating stronger binding. From 5 Å to 15 Å, the stable binding region spanned, indicating favourable interactions over a sizable range. Lactitol could switch between multiple binding modes or interact flexibly within the binding pocket before fully dissociating, as indicated by the steep energy rise that occurred beyond 20 Å, which signalled the transition to an unbound state. With a deep free energy minimum of roughly − 160 Kcal/mol, EFP showed the best binding stability. Throughout a wide range (10–12 Å), the energy stayed comparatively constant, indicating robust and long-lasting interactions with the protein. A significant unbinding barrier was indicated by a sharp increase in energy above 14 Å, indicating that EFP is extremely resistant to dissociation once bound. EFP showed the highest binding stability among the three ligands in 3IG7, as indicated by its high energy barrier for unbinding and deep free energy minimum. Picroside-1 demonstrated a moderate binding strength with multiple stable states and a lower unbinding barrier, while lactitol came next, displaying strong but flexible interactions. These variations in the free energy landscapes indicated that Picroside-1 could move between several binding states prior to unbinding, while EFP was the least likely to dissociate.

Similarly, over a 50 ns simulation period, metadynamics simulations were conducted to examine the binding stability and unbinding mechanisms of Talazoparib, lactitol, and picroside-1 with the 7KK2 protein (Fig. 29). A stable binding conformation was indicated by the deep free energy minimum of about − 120 Kcal/mol in the FES of Picroside-1. Picroside-1 maintained stable interactions while permitting a certain amount of flexibility within the binding pocket, according to the collective variable’s smooth energy transition. A moderate barrier to unbinding was suggested by the slow energy increase above a threshold, suggesting that even though the ligand and 7KK2 form a stable complex, the ligand may change conformations before completely dissociating. A comparable binding free energy minimum of roughly − 120 Kcal/mol was shown by lactitol. This ligand may have more flexibility within the binding pocket or adopt several stable conformations prior to unbinding, as indicated by the free energy landscape’s more gradual decline. Lactitol is more likely to dissociate than the other two ligands, according to the comparatively lower energy barrier. With a global free energy minimum of roughly − 250 Kcal/mol, Talazoparib showed the best binding stability. At about 22 Å, the FES showed a significant energy increase, indicating a significant unbinding barrier. This implied that, in comparison to lactitol and picroside-1, Talazoparib had a substantially lower propensity to dissociate after binding to 7KK2. Talazoparib established strong and long-lasting interactions within the binding site, as demonstrated by the steep free energy profile. Comparing the three ligands bound to 7KK2, Talazoparib exhibited the highest binding stability, followed by Picroside-1 and Lactitol.

Metadynamics simulations were also conducted to examine the binding stability and unbinding mechanisms of Picroside-1, Lactitol, and LZ9 with the 4Y72 protein over a 50 ns simulation period (Fig. 29). The FES of Picroside-1 exhibited a deep free energy minimum of approximately − 80 Kcal/mol, indicating a stable binding conformation. The relatively smooth energy landscape along the collective variable suggested that Picroside-1 maintained stable interactions within the binding pocket while allowing for some conformational flexibility. Although the ligand was stable, it might change conformations before completely dissociating, as indicated by the slow rise in energy above a particular threshold, which suggested a moderate barrier to unbinding. With a binding free energy minimum of roughly − 120 Kcal/mol, lactitol demonstrated a higher binding affinity than Picroside-1. The free energy landscape exhibited a more gradual descent, indicating that Lactitol might experience greater flexibility within the binding pocket or multiple stable conformations before unbinding. Out of the three ligands, LZ9 showed the highest binding stability, with a global free energy minimum of about − 120 Kcal/mol. The FES profile showed a sharp increase in energy beyond 17.5 Å along CV1, indicating a significant unbinding barrier, suggesting that LZ9 was much less likely to dissociate once it bound to 4Y72 than Picroside-1 and Lactitol. The steep free energy profile confirmed that LZ9 formed deep and persistent interactions within the binding site. Lactitol exhibited moderate stability, characterized by a well-defined binding pocket with a discernible unbinding barrier. Picroside-1 showed the best binding affinity, characterized by an efficient free energy minimum and a less flexible binding interaction, indicating a lower possibility of detachment.

Discussion

Historically, medicinal plants and their phytoconstituents have been the source of numerous drugs for treating various diseases, including cancer. Currently, the chemotherapeutic options for breast and lung cancers are of minimal success with the existing drugs, mainly owing to the evolution of resistant cancers and the toxicity of the existing anticancer drugs [42]. Lablab purpureus (LP), an edible bean, has traditionally been of gastronomic and medicinal value in South and Southeast Asian regions. L. purpureus has been utilised in traditional medicinal practices to treat a variety of gastrointestinal as well as uterine disorders. Globally, research conducted on the evaluation of anticancer activities of L. purpureus has focused only on assessing the in vitro cytotoxicity of crude extract on various cancer cell lines, while the exact phytocompounds and the molecular mechanisms responsible for this have not been looked into. In addition, there are little to no evidence about the antiproliferative activity of the edible L. purpureus seeds. Addressing these lacunae, the bioactivity-mediated fractionation of L. purpureus seeds was performed to identify the most potent anti-cancer fraction and validate its potential anticancer activity.

Previously, the bioactivity-mediated fractionation of many plants has been reported, performed using a number of techniques, including column chromatography to identify fractions with anticancer effects [43–46]. In the current study, liquid-liquid partition chromatography was employed to separate LPM into three solvent fractions using methanol, chloroform, and hexane, based on polarity. The methanol fraction yielded the highest amount, indicating that most polar phytochemicals were extracted into this fraction, while LPMCF and LPMHF contained partially polar and non-polar compounds, respectively. TLC analysis revealed better compound separation in LPMMF, with two distinct spots under UV light (Rf values 0.48 and 1.0). In contrast, LPMCF and LPMHF displayed only one major spot, suggesting a lower complexity of phytochemical constituents. These results indicated that LPMMF retained the most diverse range of bioactive compounds, making it a promising candidate for further purification and bioactivity evaluation. Hence, to further refine LPMMF, column chromatography was performed, leading to the isolation of 70 individual fractions. The fractionation process aimed to segregate phytochemicals based on their differential solubility and affinity to the stationary phase. Among the obtained fractions, LPMMF 1.5 emerged as a promising bioactive fraction, with a high yield (583.6 mg) and potent antioxidant properties. LPMMF 1.5 displayed the strongest antioxidant activity (77% DPPH inhibition, 580 FRAP value). The ABTS assay further confirmed that LPMMF 1.5 exhibited antioxidant activity, reinforcing its potential as a key bioactive fraction. Phytochemical analysis of LPMMF 1.5 established the presence of phenols, glycosides, cardiac glycosides, steroids, and terpenoids. TLC analysis of LPMMF 1.5 established the presence of multiple bioactive compounds, as evidenced by distinct bands under visible and UV light. The observed Rf values (0.48 and 1.0) were consistent with those detected in the parent LPMMF fraction, indicating the retention of key bioactive constituents during the fractionation process.

Sipahli et al. (2023) also evaluated the in vitro anticancer and apoptotic effect of low-molecular-weight peptides derived from Lablab purpureus (L.) Sweet seeds using a comprehensive workflow involving enzymatic hydrolysis with pepsin, ultrafiltration, and subsequent purification. The 3 kDa fraction showed the strongest cytotoxic potential against cancer cell lines, with IC50 values of 13.6 µg/ml for A549 and 20.48 µg/ml for MCF-7. The peptide’s selective toxicity was highlighted by the HEK-293 cells, which showed a significantly higher IC50 value of 145.1 µg/ml. This fraction was chosen for additional fractionation due to its outstanding efficiency. Upon RP-HPLC purification, Fraction 2 was identified as the most bioactive sub-fraction. This purified peptide displayed IC50 values of 141.8 µg/ml (A549), 250 µg/ml (MCF-7), and 179 µg/ml (HEK-293), which, although higher than the original 3 kDa fraction, still indicated targeted activity towards cancer cells over healthy ones. The fraction’s apoptotic effect was extensively characterized using Annexin V-PI staining, which revealed that 85.4% of A549 and 89.6% of MCF-7 cells underwent early apoptosis, while 93.9% of HEK-293 cells showed early apoptosis with 0.3% remaining viable. These results suggested that while the peptide fraction does affect non-cancerous cells, its primary apoptotic activity was directed at the cancerous cells [47].

Unlike previous investigations focused on proteins, the present study considered evaluating the secondary metabolites of LP. The cytotoxic potential of LPMMF 1.5 on MCF-7 and A549 cancer cell lines was determined using the MTT assay over different time points, of 24, 48, and 72 h, at varying concentrations ranging from 12.5 to 250 µg/ml. The results indicated concentration- and time-dependent inhibition of cancer cell viability in both the breast and lung cancer cell lines. Notably, at 250 µg/ml, MCF-7 cells exhibited 50 ± 1.41% inhibition at 24 h, increasing to 56.67 ± 0.66% at 48 h and peaking at 70.48 ± 0.22% inhibition after 72 h of incubation. Likewise, A549 cells showed 45 ± 2.16% inhibition at 24 h, 57.37 ± 0.74% at 48 h, and 63.67 ± 1.03% at 72 h. Significant morphological changes, including cell shrinkage, membrane blebbing, and the presence of necrotic debris, were evident in the treated cells, along with the observed cytotoxicity, even at the lowest treatment concentration (12.5 µg/ml). These alterations implied that LPMMF 1.5 triggered apoptotic pathways in MCF-7 and A549 cells, which intensified with time. Standard chemotherapeutic agents, Doxorubicin for A549 cells and Cisplatin for MCF-7 cells, were used to compare the efficacy of LPMMF 1.5. With IC50 values of 26.87 µg/ml at 24 h, 14.10 µg/ml at 48 h, and 4.15 µg/ml at 72 h, the cytotoxic effects of Cisplatin on MCF-7 cells increased over time. With an IC50 of 10.48 µg/ml at 24 h, 8.68 µg/ml at 48 h, and 6.02 µg/ml at 72 h, Doxorubicin also demonstrated strong cytotoxic effects on A549 cells. Comparable cytotoxicity was only shown by LPMMF 1.5 at concentrations greater than 250 µg/ml. However, LPMMF 1.5’s time-dependent rise in cytotoxicity was comparable to that of Doxorubicin and Cisplatin in each cell line, confirming its effectiveness against cancer cells.

To determine the apoptotic effects of various plant extracts, AO and EtBr staining is widely used [48, 49]. Through the use of AO and EtBr dual staining, the proapoptotic properties of LPMMF 1.5 in both cells have been confirmed in this investigation. It was highly probable that LPMMF 1.5 caused apoptosis-mediated cytotoxicity in both tested cell lines because a considerable percentage of orange and yellow-stained cells were seen in treated samples when compared to controls. This effect was comparable to Cisplatin (for MCF-7) and Doxorubicin (for A549), indicating its potential as an alternative therapeutic agent. A proposed mechanism of action of the antioxidant and anticancer action of LPMMF 1.5 on cancer cells has been summarized in Fig. 30.

Purwanti et al. (2022) described the occurrence of over 60 bioactives in the ethanol extract of LP, which was assessed using HR-LCMS. Among them, phenolic acids and flavonoids were predominant, with notable compounds including ferulic acid, coumaric acid, daidzein, rutin, and isoquercetin [50]. To investigate anti-diabetic potential, molecular docking was employed, targeting key diabetes-related enzymes: α-amylase, DPP4, and PTP1B. Fourteen compounds demonstrated strong binding affinities with these enzymes. Ursolic acid showed the highest inhibition of α-amylase with a binding energy of − 10.1 Kcal/mol. 19-nortestosterone exhibited strong affinity for PTP1B (–9.3 Kcal/mol), while rutin bound effectively to both α-amylase (–8.8 Kcal/mol) and DPP4 (–9.1 Kcal/mol). Compounds such as 19-Norandrostenedione, 19-Nortestosterone, Icariside B1, Ilicic Acid, and Psilostachyin B exhibited favourable physicochemical properties [50]. We have found similar compounds reported in the LPMMF 1.5 fraction of the seed methanolic extracts. Nonetheless, there exists no in silico analysis of LP phytochemicals to evaluate their anticancer properties. Filling the gaps in the availability of computational evaluation of the LP anticancer activity, we have identified LP phytochemicals using HR-LCMS analysis and subjected them to in silico analysis to validate our experimental observations and to elucidate the possible mechanisms of anticancer action. The ADME analysis ensured the selection of drug-like compounds based on Lipinski’s Rule of Five, filtering out molecules with unfavourable pharmacokinetic properties [51]. The molecular docking study provided an initial understanding of ligand-protein interactions by evaluating binding affinities and interaction mechanisms. The 93 molecules that adhered to Lipinski’s rule of 5 were subjected to molecular docking against three different cancer-specific protein targets. Cyclin B1, which plays a major role in mitotic progression and is often overexpressed in cancers such as breast, lung and colorectal, making it a promising cancer therapeutic target. The cell cycle’s transition from the G2 phase to mitosis is regulated by cyclin B1 (G2/mitotic-specific cyclin B1, PDB: 4Y72). Its role in apoptosis and its interactions with apoptotic signalling pathways have long been associated with its function in cell cycle regulation [52]. An established target in anticancer treatment, poly (ADP-ribose) polymerase 1 (PARP1, PDB: 7KK2) is an essential enzyme in DNA repair, as PARP1 inhibition damages DNA, it can cause cancer cells to die [53]. Since cell division protein kinase 2 (CDK2, PDB: 3IG7) plays a critical role in controlling cell division, especially at the G1/S phase transition, and its dysregulation has been connected to the emergence of several cancers, blocking CDK2 with small molecule inhibitors may prevent tumour growth by impeding the progression of the cell cycle [54]. Picroside-1 showed the highest binding affinity among the tested ligands for a variety of proteins, especially for 7KK2 (-8.3 Kcal/mol), 3IG7 (-9.366 Kcal/mol), and 4Y72 (-8.027 Kcal/mol). Multiple hydrogen bonds, π-π stacking interactions, and salt bridges stabilised these strong binding interactions. This implied that ligands derived from nature might provide better pharmacokinetic characteristics as well as competitive binding qualities. The proteins’ binding pockets contained numerous hydrogen bonds and hydrophobic interactions, suggesting a high probability of stable complex formation and calling for additional investigation through molecular dynamics simulations.

The structural stability and dynamic behaviour of the aforementioned docked ligand-protein complexes were evaluated using molecular dynamics simulations (MDS) over a 500-nanosecond (ns) trajectory. All complexes retained structural stability, as indicated by the RMSD analysis, and protein RMSD values, which are a measure of binding stability, consistently fell between 1.0 Å and 3.6 Å. While the 7KK2-Talazoparib complex displayed the lowest fluctuation range (1.2–2.7 Å), suggesting a highly stable binding mode, the 7KK2-Picroside-1 complex displayed early fluctuations (1.2–2.8 Å) prior to stabilisation. Talazoparib had the most restricted movement (0.5–0.75 Å), according to the ligand RMSD analysis, indicating strong binding stability. On the other hand, lactitol and picroside-1 exhibited mild oscillations but remained within the active site, indicating stable yet adaptable interactions. According to RMSD analysis, the Picroside-1 complex for the 3IG7 protein exhibited stable interactions over time, fluctuating between 2.0 Å and 2.8 Å. Although lactitol fluctuated a little more (from 2.4 Å to 3.2 Å), it was still tightly bound in the active site. A more flexible binding conformation was indicated by EFP’s slightly greater variation (1.5 Å to 4.0 Å). According to the ligand RMSD trends, EFP showed moderate flexibility, while Picroside-1 showed the most stable binding, followed by Lactitol. Picroside-1 had the strongest binding interactions, according to binding free energy calculations, followed by lactitol and the prebound ligand EFP. With an RMSD range of 2.4 Å to 3.2 Å, the MDS results for 4Y72 showed that Picroside-1 stayed consistently stable, stabilising even more after 400 ns. While lactitol fluctuated between 2.6 and 3.5 Å, the prebound reference ligand LZ9 showed the greatest degree of fluctuation (1.2 to 3.6 Å), indicating a more dynamic binding mode. With only slight variations throughout the trajectory, ligand RMSD values showed that Picroside-1 and lactitol maintained dependable binding interactions. A well-preserved conformation was confirmed by the secondary structure analysis, which revealed that 4Y72-Picroside-1 retained 56.95% of its helices without any β-strands. Understanding the strength and stability of interactions between proteins and ligands is essential for comprehending their potential as inhibitors or modulators in biological systems. This information was obtained through the use of Molecular Mechanics/Generalized Born Surface Area (MM-GBSA) binding free energy calculations. Stronger and more stable interactions were indicated by lower binding free energy values. The most favourable binding energy was displayed by 4Y72-Picroside-1, whereas the interactions between 7KK2-Picroside-1 and 3IG7-Picroside-1 were weaker and less stable. While 4Y72-Lactitol had binding energies ranging from − 71.79 Kcal/mol to -90.59 Kcal/mol, 7KK2-Lactitol and 3IG7-Lactitol had moderate binding energies.

To assess the ligands’ binding stability and unbinding mechanisms, metadynamics simulations were run for 50 ns. Important information about the most stable binding conformations and possible ligand dissociation pathways was obtained from the free energy surface analysis. Because of their deep free energy minima and high barriers to unbinding, Talazoparib (for 7KK2), LZ9 (for 4Y72), and EFP (for 3IG7) showed the most stable binding among the control/reference ligands. Picroside-1 and lactitol were the non-control ligands that continuously demonstrated robust and stable binding to a variety of targets. Two major energy minima were observed by picroside-1 with the 3IG7 protein, indicating two stable binding conformations. With a global minimum of about − 100 Kcal/mol, EFP showed stronger binding. With a deep free energy minimum of roughly − 160 Kcal/mol, EFP showed the best binding stability. Likewise, a stable binding conformation with a deep free energy minimum of roughly − 120 Kcal/mol was demonstrated by picroside-1, lactitol, and Talazoparib with the 7KK2 protein. With a global free energy minimum of roughly − 250 Kcal/mol, Talazoparib showed the best binding stability. Likewise, with the 4Y72 protein, Picroside-1 exhibited moderate stability with a well-defined binding pocket and an unbinding barrier, while Lactitol demonstrated the best binding affinity with an efficient free energy minimum and a less flexible binding interaction, suggesting a lower chance of detachment [55, 56].

In addition, Picroside-1 was predicted to have a variety of other pharmacological activities, using PASS prediction. Picroside-1’s anticancer activity against triple-negative breast cancer (MDA-MB-231) cells has previously been determined through in vitro investigations. The IC50 value for cell viability assays was 95.3 µM, indicating a concentration-dependent decline. According to DNA fragmentation and Annexin V-PI flow cytometric analyses, apoptosis analysis showed a 20% increase in the early apoptotic phase at concentrations of 0, 50, 75, and 100 µM. Picroside-1 treatment was found to decrease the mitochondrial membrane potential and cause cell cycle arrest in the G0/G1 phase [57]. Similarly, it was reported with significant cytotoxicity and anti-invasion activity towards MCF-7 cells with a decrease in the activity of Matrix Metalloproteins, collagenases, and gelatinases, inhibiting breast cancer progression [58]. However, no anticancer activity evaluations of lactitol have been reported thus far. The findings of this study suggest that Picroside-1 could serve as a capable prime compound for further anticancer drug advancement. The observed high binding affinities, stable interactions, and favourable binding free energies indicated their potential to act as inhibitors or modulators for their respective protein targets.

Nonetheless, the evaluation of the mechanistic pathways of anticancer effect of LPMMF 1.5, along with further purification of the LPMMF 1.5 fraction, followed by in vitro and in vivo anticancer evaluation of Picroside-1, is essential to validate the observations made during the in silico studies.

Conclusion

This study successfully used a bioactivity-guided technique to separate and isolate the cytotoxic fractions from the methanol extract of Lablab purpureus seeds. The methanol fraction LPMMF 1.5 had a strong ability to fight free radicals and potent dose- and time-dependent cytotoxicity against MCF-7 and A549 cancer cell lines, and caused apoptosis. Chemical profiling showed the presence of multiple bioactive phytochemicals, including Picroside-1, which had strong binding affinity and stable interactions with cancer-specific targets PARP1, CDK2, and CCNB1 in molecular docking and simulation studies. Further mechanistic evaluation of the anticancer effect of LPMMF 1.5 as well as in vitro evaluation of the anticancer effect of Picroside-1 could lead to the development of plant-based cancer-fighting drugs.
