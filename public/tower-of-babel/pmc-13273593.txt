Electrical stimulation and stem cell subdural implantation decrease microglia reactivity after spinal cord injury
Authors: Loris Mannino, Paolo Marracino, Fernando Gisbert Roca, Eric Lopez-Mocholi, Maria Pedraza-Boti, Dario Bernardi, Marco Balucani, Franck M Andre, Lluis M Mir, Sergiy Ivashchenko, Francisco Navarro-Páez, Cristina Martinez-Ramos, Manuel Monleón-Pradas, Iago Lopez-Grobas, Javier Buceta-Fernandez, Micol Colella, Sara Fontana, Micaela Liberti, Francesca Apollonio, Caterina Merla, Claudia Consales, Victoria Moreno-Manzano
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundContusive spinal cord injury (SCI) leads to severe and permanent motor, sensory, and autonomic deficits, resulting from both the initial mechanical damage and subsequent secondary pathological cascades. Besides, electrical stimulation (ES) and stem cell therapies have emerged as promising strategies to promote axonal regeneration and neuronal plasticity.MethodsWe designed a new implantable device, an Electro Pulsed Biohybrid (EPB) device, to provide local ES and carry stem cells (hMSC and iNSC) for subdural implantation, wired and wireless controlled. We assessed locomotion and sensory outputs, cell migration, neuroinflammation, gliosis, fibrosis, and neuronal survival.ResultsThe consecutive application of microsecond pulsed electric fields (μsPEFs) into two different configurations during ten days and further continuous current during five days significantly enhanced the migration and engraftment of the implanted hMSC and a significant reduction in the number of microglia injury-dependent reactive cells. The ES did not exacerbate gliosis, fibrosis, neuropathic pain, or neuronal loss after primary trauma, instead, the electrically stimulated animals in comparison with the non-stimulated controls were able to perform better reducing the time during running. Consistent results were obtained with a wireless and wired configuration for the ES supply.ConclusionsThe applied sequence of μsPEFs and direct current local stimulation contributed to the early immunomodulation, reducing the acute immunoreactivity involved in further secondary damage, and enhanced implanted hMSC migration, providing a versatile platform for cell therapy and ES combinatorial approach in the SCI treatment.Keywords: Spinal cord injury, Electrical stimulation, Bio-hybrid approach, Stem cells, Immunomodulation, Micro pulses, Direct current

Introduction

Spinal cord injury (SCI) is a dramatic event that results in a disabling condition, characterized by the loss of motor and sensory functions [1] often accompanied by temporary or permanent deficits in sensation, muscle strength, or the ability to control bowel and bladder functions [2], among others. The pathophysiology of SCI encompasses both primary and secondary injury mechanisms. The primary injury triggers immediate neuronal cell death, vascular disruption, and interrupts the neural fiber tracts in the spinal cord due to mechanical trauma [1]. This is followed by a secondary cascade involving reactive gliosis, microglial activation, and extracellular matrix remodeling and cavity formation, all of which exacerbate tissue damage and impair regeneration [3]. Activated astrocytes, microglia, and macrophages, migrating fibroblasts, pericytes, and Schwann cells contribute to the formation of the cavity at the lesion core [4]. The inhibitory extrinsic microenvironment generated by the infiltrated and reactive cells, combined with the limited intrinsic regenerative capacity of adult neurons, together with the anatomical inherent limitations for local interventions, makes SCI one of the most challenging disorders to treat [2].

Besides the well-accepted limited capacity of the adult central nervous system (CNS) for the primary neuronal damage regeneration [5], strategies focusing on reducing the secondary damage and neuroprotection provide reliable opportunities for better prognosis and further neuroplasticity [6]. Among them, the therapeutic effect of the stem cell transplantation has been largely reported [7]. We previously described that the intramedullary transplantation of a neural stem cells (NSC) and mesenchymal stem stems (MSC) confers benefits compared with the single cell treatment, such as preserving neuronal fibers and reducing scar tissue [8]. Others, also reported that the combination of the stem cells significantly reduced demyelination [9], provides neurotrophic factors [10] and cell survival [11], or diminishing the inflammatory responses [12]. Interestingly, the mix of MSC and NSC, combined with epidural electrical stimulation (ES), recently showed to significantly inhibited microglial polarization [11]. ES has also emerged as a pioneering approach [13]. Living cells essentially generate electric fields and currents due to the movement of various ions such as Ca2+, K+, and Na+, and the gradients of these ions across the cell membrane [14]. Electrically excitable cells, including neurons, rely on electrical fields for intercellular communication, which is crucial for physiological processes, such as synaptic transmission [15], and ES is one of the most used methods to manipulate stem cell fate [16], [17]. In particular, ultrashort-pulsed electric fields (PEFs), with durations ranging from nanoseconds to milliseconds, are delivered at high intensity and can charge the cell membrane, creating large localized electric fields across it in a closer physiological manner [18]. The PEFs prompt the Ca2+ to diffuse from the extracellular environment into the cytosol [19], promoting the activation of calcium-dependent signaling pathways. These cascades regulate the proliferation and survival of MSCs and iNSCs [20] but the amplitude and duration of the pulses can be sufficient to promote their differentiation. Recent studies showed that ES promoted neurogenic-like phenotype of MSCs, increasing expression of early neuronal markers [21]. Similarly, Patil et al. (2023) reported that ES accelerated neuronal maturation and functional integration in neural stem cells derived from induced pluripotent stem cells (iNSCs) [22], conferring enhanced properties for regenerative therapies in neuronal applications. On the other hand, ES by direct current (DC) stands out for its ability to induce axonal elongation, modulating cytoskeletal dynamics and intracellular signaling pathways such as PI3K/Akt and MAPK/ERK, facilitating axonal growth and directional guidance [[22], [23], [24]]. Taken together, these findings support the potential benefit of ES by PEFs and DC, offering a multifaceted approach for potential amelioration of inflammatory responses from the secondary damage after spinal cord injury. Here, we study the impact of cumulative ES by locally applying PEFs and DC through a new implantable device also functionalized with hMSCs and iNSCs covering the injured spinal cord in rats, wired and wireless controlled.

Materials and methods

Wired system for ES

The EPB active electrode was externally connected as described in Fig. 1A and B to receive the desired ES. The μsPEF protocols were delivered using the dedicated generator manufactured and provided by the University of Zaragoza, setting an input voltage of 10 V [25], whereas for DC stimulation, 45 mV were applied by the HP 6114A Precision Power Supply (Hewlett-Packard). These conditions were numerically defined through microdosimetric evaluations, as reported in dedicated works [[25], [26], [27]], to guarantee that the electric field induced on the cells mixture reached the intensities known from in vitro experiments. In addition, a dedicated in vivo numerical study was performed using a high-resolution rat anatomical model (ViZOO Neurorat v4.0) with SCI under μsPEFs [27] and DC protocols [28], to verify whether the electric field levels previously identified in vitro could be effectively reproduced within the complex and heterogeneous in vivo scenario.

To control the stability of the connection throughout the experiments, the system impedance was measured before and after each stimulation session, while electric signals were continuously monitored. The impedance was measured in vivo before and after the stimulation with the BK PRECISION 2831E Digital Multimeter. The acquired values are in the range of tens of kOhms, in line with literature values found under similar conditions [29]. Current during μsPEF stimulation was monitored using the Rohde & Schwarz RT-ZC20B current probe, while DC signals were tracked with a standard voltage probe. All electrical signals were acquired and visually inspected using the Rohde & Schwarz RTO-2014 Oscilloscope. The intensity of current pulses reached up to 90 mA. Additionally, a FLIR 325a thermal camera was used to monitor temperature, ruled out overheating risk. Further details are provided in the Supplementary Material section Fig. S1.

Wireless system for ES

For the wireless control of the ES (Fig. 1A–C,G) a Windows 10-based software application was developed to control the MCU and associated electronic components (illustrated in Supplementary Fig. S2).

Any laptop running the software and equipped with a Bluetooth interface can connect to an EPB device via its MAC address, enabling remote control of the stimulation procedure. Following completion of the stimulation, the MCU periodically searches for the control laptop, requiring a stable power source (hence a rechargeable battery). The software also allows individual control of a large number of devices within a networked pool.

hMSCs and iNSCs culture

Human adipose-derived mesenchymal stem cells (hMSCs) were isolated from surgical waste from individuals undergoing elective lipoaspiration after obtaining a written informed consent from all of the donors, in accordance with French and European legislations (Art.L. 1245–2 du Code de la Santé Publique), which establishes that authorization from an ethics committee is not required. The isolation procedure of the cells as well as their characterization by surface antigen analysis and assessment of multipotency were previously described [30]. Briefly, the isolated and expanded hMSCs population were adherent to plastic under standard culture conditions, expressed CD105, CD73, and CD90, and lack the expression of CD45, CD34, CD14/CD11b, CD79a/CD19, and HLA-DR. Cells were grown in DMEM high glucose with GlutaMAX (Gibco, 31966-120 047) supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (Sigma, F7524), 100 U/mL penicillin and 100 mg/mL streptomycin (Gibco, 15140122) in adherent conditions in treated petri dishes, at 37 °C in a humidified incubator with 5% CO2. Confluent cultures were detached with trypsin/EDTA (0.05%, Gibco) and split 1:3 once a week until implantation at passages 4-5.

The iNSCs were induced from the C2mRNA iPSC line as previously described [31]. Briefly, iPSC colonies were grown over Matrigel Growth factor Reduced (Corning) and maintained with mTeSR1 (cGMP; StemCell Technologies) until 90% confluency. From day 1 to 4, we supplemented the mTeSR1 media with 500 ng/ml of Noggin (cAT. 120-10C; Peprotech) + 10 μM of SB431542 (TGF-β RI Kinase Inhibitor VI, Invitrogen) to inhibit the activity of ALK4 and ALK5. From day 7 we used the glial restrictive media (GRM) supplemented with 0.1 μM of LDN193189 (DM-3189; a selective BMP type I receptor inhibitor, which efficiently inhibits ALK2 and ALK3 (Cat. 1435934-00-1; Stemcell technologies). From day 8 neural rosettes were evidenced and were mechanically cut and transfer into a new plate coated with poly-Ornitine/Laminin in GRM+ 0.1 μM + LDN193189 + 10 ng/ml bFGF (Gibco Recombinant Human FGF-Basic; Thermofisher, Cat. PHG0368). From day 8 to day 17 cells were maintained in GRM+ 0.1 μM of LDN193189 + 10 ng/ml bFGF until confluency. After day 18 iNSCs were maintained in GRM+10 ng/ml bFGF, detached upon confluency with TrypLE Express (12604-013, Gibco). Cell cultures were always maintained at 37 °C, 70-90% relative humidity, 5% CO2 and atmospheric oxygen until implantation at day 25-28. The generation of iPSC and their implementation in this study were approved by the Dirección General de Investigación y Alta Inspección Sanitaria (Generalitat Valenciana, Valencia, Spain) upon approval from the clinical ethical committee at La Fe Hospital with reference number 2023-734-1.

For neuronal induction, the MSCs or iNSC, in neuronal media (Neurobasal with B27 supplement and 100 ng/ml BDNF) were subjected to in vitro micropulsed stimulation (300V/cm, bipolar pulse of 750μ+750μs) one pulse a day for 5 days. β3Tubulin immune-staining was used to determine the neuronal induced phenotype.

One week before cell implantation, hMSCs and iNSCs were transduced to express the reporter proteins using the pLL3.7 lentiviral constructs carrying GFP or mScarlet, respectively, under the CMV obliquus promoter at MOI 10 to generate the hMSCs_GFP and iNSCs_mScarlet lines.

Traumatic spinal cord injury by moderate contusion rat model

Female athymic nude rats Hsd:RH-Foxn1 rnu, ∼250 g (Janvier Labs, Spain) Rat colonies were bred and maintained at the Animal Experimentation Unit of the Research Institute Príncipe Felipe (CIPF, Valencia, Spain) following guidelines established by the European Communities Council Directive (86/609/ECC) and the Spanish Royal Decree 53/2013. Rats were housed under standard temperature conditions with controlled 12 h light/dark cycles with ad libitum access to food and water, managed by professionally trained staff. All experimental procedures were previously supervised and approved by the Animal Care and Use Committee of the Research Institute Prince Felipe with the identification codes 2021/VSC/PEA/0009 and 2023-VSC-PEA-0174. The experimental protocol included humane endpoint criteria when severe signs of distress were observed. Rats were subcutaneously pre-medicated with morphine (2.5 mg/kg) and anesthetized with 2% isoflurane in a continuous oxygen flow of 1 L/min. Laminectomy was performed at the thoracic vertebrae 8 to expose the spinal cord and induce a traumatic contusion applying 125 kdyn force using the Infinite Horizon Impactor (Precision System and Instrumentation Corp., USA), as we also previously described [32].One week after SCI, all animals were subjected to an additional intervention for the EPB implantation. Rats were pre-medicated with morphine (2.5 mg/kg) and anesthetized with 2% isoflurane in a continuous oxygen flow of 1 L/min. The original incision was reopened to get access to the laminectomized and exposed injured spinal cord area, ensuring a window of about 4,5 mm x 2,5 mm. Under the surgical microscope, and immediately before the EPB implantation, the dura mater was opened using a 26G needle for subdural space implantation. Then, the EPB electrode was covered with a mix of iNSCs and hMSCs (0,5 million of iNSCs_mScarlet + 0,8 million of hMSCs_GFP) suspended in a total of 3 μl of a growth factor cocktail (GFc) in order to improve the implanted cell survival as previously described [33]. Then, the EBP was subdurally implanted with the active area positioned, covering the spinal cord injured area. For wired and external stimulator connection, wires connected to the EPB were exteriorized and sutured to the skin; for the wireless system, the internal stimulator connected by a cable to the EPB active area was positioned and immobilized with lateral sutures in the subcutaneous space in the dorsal side, immediately below the skin original incision. The wound was sutured and the animals were individually housed. Animal care and bladder expression was performed daily until vesical reflex recovered (at the second or third week after injury). All animals were subcutaneously administered buprenorphine twice daily (0.1 mg/kg) for 4 days and enrofloxacin once a day for a week after surgeries (5 mg/kg). Locomotor evaluation test (see below) were performed once a week until the end of the experiment. Animals were sacrificed with pentobarbital intraperitoneal administration (100 mg/kg) 16 days post implantation (DPI) for the short-term experimental end point group and at 46 DPI for the long-term experimental end point group. Then, they underwent a first intra-cardiac perfusion of cold 0.9% NaCl solution, aimed to wash the circulatory system from the blood, followed by cold freshly prepared 4% PFA, aimed to fix the tissue. Spinal cords were dissected, kept in 4% paraformaldehyde solution overnight, washed in 0.1M phosphate buffer and cryopreserved with 30% of sucrose solution for at least 2 days.

Locomotor and motor function assessment

Locomotor performance was evaluated weekly using the 21-point Basso, Beattie, and Bresnahan (BBB) locomotion scale. Evaluations were conducted in a blinded manner by analyzing digitally recorded videos of individual animals [34]. A score from 0 to 21 was assigned to the locomotor function of the hind limbs, where 0 indicates complete absence of movement and 21 corresponds to normal locomotion comparable to that of an uninjured animal. The BBB scale assesses parameters including joint movements, paw placement, weight support, forelimb-hindlimb coordination, and trunk stability.

The inclined plane test was used to assess postural and body balance strength by measuring the maximum angle of inclination that rats could maintain without slipping or falling [35]. Rats were placed facing uphill on the inclined plane, and the angle was gradually increased until the animal was unable to maintain its position, with the highest tolerated angle recorded as the score.

Grip strength was quantified using the grip test, where animals were allowed to grasp a grid connected to a force transducer. The maximum force exerted by the animal to hold onto the handle, measured in grams, was recorded as the grip strength score [36].

Post spinal cord injury, balance and fine motor coordination were assessed with the narrow beam test. Rats were trained to traverse a narrow elevated beam, and the maximum distance (cm) walked along the beam without falling was recorded. This test provides a sensitive measure of motor deficits affecting balance and coordination [37].

Quantitative gait analysis was performed using the CatWalk system. This automated setup measured several parameters including the run duration (s) and the base of support (cm), which reflect overall locomotor speed and postural stability respectively. The CatWalk test allows precise assessment of footfall patterns and interlimb coordination during voluntary locomotion [38].

Sensory sensitivity assessment – von Frey and Hot plate tests

The Von Frey allodynia test was performed using the ascending-stimulus method as described [39]. Von Frey filaments in ascending order were applied an equal number of times (three) for each paw until a withdrawal was recorded and assigned as the “mechanical withdrawal threshold” (g). Animals were individually placed in transparent acrylic boxes on an elevated wire mesh platform, allowing access to the plantar surface of the hind paws. Calibrated von Frey filaments (15–100 g) were applied perpendicularly to the central plantar surface of each hind paw until slight bending of the filament was observed. The von Frey test consists of thin, calibrated plastic filaments of varying gauges or stiffness, each designed to apply a specific force. The mechanical withdrawal threshold is defined as the minimum filament force that reliably elicits a withdrawal reflex, such as a sharp paw withdrawal. This threshold serves as an indicator of mechanical nociception and is used to evaluate the ability of the animals to detect noxious mechanical stimuli [40]. The mean per paw was plotted.

The thermal test was performed to evaluate thermal hyperalgesia in animals after SCI. Thermal test studies the animal behavior upon a thermal stimulus by observation of the hind paw withdrawal from a heat-radiant surface 102 (Ugo Basile Hot/Cold Plate, 35150). The thermal ramp started at 25 °C and increases at a rate of 0.07 °C/s to a maximum of 47 °C over a total duration of 5 min. Animals maintained a quadrupedal position, being their behavior monitored throughout the test. The temperature at which the first nociceptive response, such as paw withdrawal and licking, was observed was recorded as the thermal threshold. The mean of the maximum temperature reached was then plotted. A cut-off temperature was set to prevent tissue damage in case of absent response [41].

Both tests were performed at 1, 2, and 3 weeks post-implantation to monitor the progression of sensory function. All procedures were carried out in a quiet, temperature-controlled environment to minimize external influences and animal stress.

Tissue processing, histology, and quantitative analysis

A 1.5 cm thoracic segment was obtained from the collected spinal cords, cryopreserved in 30% sucrose for at least 48 h and then embedded in Tissue-Tek OCT (Sakura Finetek Europe BV) for solid blocks generation. Cryopreserved tissues were sectioned in sagittal 35 μm sections and collected in six series in wells containing cryoprotectant solution (25% v/v glycerol, 30% v/v ethylene glycol in 0.1Mphosphate buffer). For immunofluorescence staining floating sections were blocked and permeabilized with a solution of 10% FBS, 5% donkey serum, and 0.1% Triton X-100 in TBS for 1h at room temperature and incubated with primary antibody solution overnight in a humid chamber. Conjugated secondary antibodies Alexa Fluor 488, 555 and 647, against the respective IgG, were used. Slides were finally stained with DAPI (1:1000, no. Images for the histological analysis were acquired from a Leica Thunder inverted microscope DMi8, equipped with a PL-APO 10×/1.40 air objective and a CMOS camera (Leica DFC9000 GTC). Köhler illumination was applied to enhance image contrast and to find the right plane with the bright field channel. Excitation was achieved using the Spectra-X Light Engine LED lamp, which includes eight solid-state LEDs configured with Quad filter settings. This configuration enables imaging of four different fluorophores without the need to change filters, significantly reducing acquisition time. For microscope control and image acquisition, the Leica Application Suite X (LAS X) software was used, and image analysis and quantifications were performed using ImageJ.

To assess cellular and molecular changes in the injured spinal cord, a comprehensive set of quantifications was carried out, integrating immunohistochemical analyses with morphometric normalization strategies. Cell quantification involved the use of specific markers to identify implanted cells: RFP+, GFP+, STEM121+, CD105+, and SOX2+ signals were employed to distinguish human mesenchymal stem cells (hMSCs) and induced neural stem cells (iNSCs), as detailed in the results section. Co-staining with β3tubulin was used to evaluate neuronal differentiation. All cellular counts such as β3Tubulin+/hMSCs (or iNSCs), were normalized to the area of the defined region of interest (ROI). All cellular count such as STEM121+, CD105+/STEM121+, SOX2+/STEM121+ were normalized to the number of total implanted cells, total implanted hMSCs and total implanted iNSCs, respectively. Neuronal tissue preservation and inflammatory responses were assessed by quantifying the area positive for β3tubulin and CCL2, respectively. These stained areas were normalized against the total sagittal section area. Furthermore, pNF-H staining was also analyzed to assess axonal preservation, and its positive area was likewise normalized. For a more refined measure of inflammation within neuronal regions, the ratio of CCL2-positive area to β3Tubulin-positive area was calculated. Astrocyte reactivity was evaluated by analysing GFAP intensity around the lesion. The GFAP-negative area (representing the lesion site [42],)was expanded by 350 μm to define the ROI, and GFAP expression in this region was measured. The intensity of GFAP in this perilesional region was then normalized to the GFAP expression in the rest of the sagittal section, and values were averaged per slice per animal. Total cavity area was measured and normalized to the total sagittal section area. Lesion extent was further characterized by calculating the percentage of dorsal and ventral cavity area in relation to the total cavity area. Microglial activation states were assessed by analysing Iba1+ cells normalized to the nuclei present in the region of interest considered. Ramified (resting) microglia were identified using ImageJ's “Analyse Particles” tool, applying a circularity range of 0–0.25 and a minimum size threshold of 25 μm. The percentage of resting microglia was calculated relative to the total number of Iba1+ particles exceeding this size threshold, with all values normalized to the number of nuclei when appropriate. Finally, endogenous CD105+ cells (non-implanted) were quantified by subtracting the CD105+/STEM121+ (implanted) population from the total CD105+ cell count, with normalization based on the number of nuclei in the region of interest.

Statistical analysis

Data were represented as mean ± standard error mean (SEM) and analyzed using GraphPad Prism Software. Statistical analysis was performed using two-way ANOVA or mixed model for multiple comparisons and Student's t-test when two conditions were compared, as indicated in each figure with Tukey post hoc correction when applicable. All bar plots are presented as the mean ± SEM. Significance levels: ∗p < 0.05, ∗∗p < 0.01, ∗∗∗p < 0.001, and ∗∗∗∗p < 0.0001. The sample size for each analysis represented in the corresponding figure is shown in Supplementary Table S2.

Generation of a new implantable electrode suitable for in vivo ES in a SCI rat model

We generated an implantable electrode, the Electro-Pulsed Biohybrid (EPB) device, designed to deliver μsPEFs and DC stimulation for spinal cord injury, enabling remote in vivo activation and data collection. It comprised two macro-components: the active electrode and the signal generator (Fig. 1A). The system was designed for external supply connection (wired system; Fig. 1B) or for fully internal implantation and autonomous control (wireless system; Fig. 1C). Functional locomotion and histological analysis were in vivo tested both after the implantation of the wired (Fig. 2, Fig. 3, Fig. 4, Fig. 5, Fig. 6) or the wireless system (Fig. 7).

The active electrode was designed to match the geometry of the spinal cord injured area (2 mm wide by 4 mm long) and accommodate its mechanical properties. To this end, we developed a flexible electrode by integrating porous gold conductive tracks into an ultrathin film of amorphous poly-lactic acid (PLA) (Fig. 1D). The mechanical properties of the PLA components used in the fabrication of the EPB device, were studied and reported in earlier work [[43], [44], [45]] yielding a Young modulus of 1370 ± 87 MPa, a breaking load of 170 ± 4 MPa, and an elongation at break of 4 ± 0.5%. According to these results, PLA components exhibit a relatively high elastic modulus and tensile strength comparable to conventional semi crystalline polymers, but their toughness and elongation at break are limited. A thin film of about 15 μm of hyaluronic acid (HA), placed over the PLA membrane (Fig. 1D), at the contrary side of the active area, was prepared and characterized as we previously described [46], aiming to minimize adhesiveness [[47], [48], [49]], 50] preventing the formation of fibrotic adherences of neighboring tissues at the implantation site. The integration of porous gold within the polymer matrix resulted in a flexible and mechanically robust electrode and high resistance to bending (Supplementary video). This configuration also ensures low resistance variations (∼0.1 Ω per cm), ensuring a uniform distribution of the electric field, preserving electrical continuity within the external environment, allowing the metal tracks to stimulate only the implanted injured area effectively, overcoming commonly associated limitations with conventional bulk metal electrodes [51]. The inner section of the porous gold sample, as revealed by scanning electron microscopy (SEM, Fig. 1E–E) well illustrates the homogenous internal structure of the porous gold, with highly interconnected nanostructured gold domains. The element mapping detail for a representative case is show in Supplementary Fig. S3.

A continuous lane of PLA-microfibers (10 μm in diameter) was deposited, forming a monolayer placed on the active side of the electrodes by heat-sealing (Fig. 1F). The microfibrils provided a highly aligned surface with an enhanced cell adhesion and axonal elongation properties as we previously described [[52], [53], [54]], [55], in direct contact with the spinal cord.

For wireless operation, the signal generator was encapsulated in a biocompatible resin (Liqcreate Bio-Med Clear® resin) capsule with 26 × 23 × 12.5 mm external dimensions, fabricated via additive manufacturing. The package included the electrical and electronic components (Fig. 1G), as well as the energy supply provided by a single rechargeable 3V battery, ensuring stable operation for up to 40 days (Fig. 1G'). The selection of electronic components determined both the circuitry for signal generation and the software developed for the internal operation of the EPB, as well as for wireless communication with external Bluetooth-enabled interfaces. The electrical components needed to generate the desired electric stimuli are illustrated in Supplementary Fig. S2, where a detailed scheme with the key electronic components is provided. Both the circuit and the associated components were assembled on a custom-designed printed circuit board (PCB). The active electrode area of the EPB, prior to implantation, was covered with a mix of human stem cells (0.5 × 106 iNSCs + 0.8 × 106 hMSCs) embedded in 3 μl of a growth factor cocktail mix [33] (Fig. 1G, inset). Both, hMSC and iNSC were on monolayer cultures (Fig. 1H, phase contrast images, upper panels) in growing media before implantation. Before implantation, both cell types were subjected to in vitro stimulation (300V/cm, bipolar pulse of 750μ+750μs, for 5 days), evidencing their neuronal induced phenotype by immune staining with β3Tubulin (red, Fig. 1H, lower panels) without showing significant cell toxicity (data not shown).

Microsecond PEFs and direct current ES were applied through a wire-controlled EPB, including a mix of human stem cells, covering the injured spinal cord

A moderate contusion at the eighth thoracic segment was induced in adult female nude rats SCI, mimicking a clinically relevant incomplete injury and causing partial paralysis as we previously described [32]. One week later, at the subacute stage, the active electrode area of the EPB covered with a mix of human stem cells (0.5 × 106 iNSCs + 0.8 × 106 hMSCs) embedded in 3 μl of a growth factor cocktail mix [33] (as illustrated in Fig. 1G, inset) was subdurally implanted covering the spinal cord injured area. The stimulated group was externally connected to the pulses external generator (manufactured by UniZaragoza, Spain) to deliver bipolar microsecond PEFs (μsPEFs). First, intending to promote cell proliferation, bipolar 25 μs + 25 μs μs PEFs (μsPEFs), for the positive and negative pulse phases, were repeated every minute for 30 min per day for five consecutive days (Fig. 2A, purple). During the following five days, intending to promote cell differentiation, a single bipolar 750 μs + 750 μs μsPEFs was apply per day (Fig. 2A, orange). The amplitude and duration of the applied pulses were select based on previous data. In Vallet et al., we recently showed that the increase in the frequency of Ca2+ oscillations would induce cell proliferation, while decreasing it would promote differentiation in the hMSC, in response to cell-specific-induced media [20]. In Innamorati G et al., we also demonstrated that the short μsPEFs stimulation significantly increased the proliferation of the iNSC after three days of stimulation [17]. An extended duration of the applied pulses to 750 μs blocks the natural calcium oscillations, allowed both, the hMSC and iNSC to induce the expression of the early neuronal marker β3Tubulin, five days after stimulation, suggesting an induced neuronal-like phenotype (Fig. 1H, lower panels). In Camera F et al., we previously showed the effect of the μsPEFs, inducing the expression of neuronal-like transcripts, for hMSC and a human neuroblastoma cell line (SH-SY5Y), evidencing the induced expression of β3Tubulin and the neurotrophin BDNF after pulsed electrical stimulation in both cell types [21]. In both μsPEFs protocols the pulse amplitude was selected to guarantee an electric field distribution of tens of kV/m [[25], [26], [27]], [56]. The bipolar rectangular μsPEFs selected programs were chosen to reduce electrolytic detrimental effects either through the co-implanted mix of iNSCs and hMSCs or the closer spinal cord tissue. During the last five days, direct current (DC) was applied for 30 min a day using an external DC generator (HP 6114A Precision Power Supply, Hewlett-Packard) (Fig. 2A, green) ensuring electric field intensities of 150 V/m beneath the EPB, to support axonal elongation as previously described [22]. Therefore, the ES full program started one day after EPB implantation and one week after SCI, ending sixteen days post-implantation (16 DPI; Fig. 2A). The not-treated group (Sham), SCI and EPB implanted animals, did not receive the electrical stimulus.

All animals were weekly assessed for motor function using several behavioral tests. The Basso, Beattie and Bresnahan (BBB) locomotor rating scale for open field for free voluntary movement and hind limb function analysis [34] (Fig. 2B and C), the inclined plane test for hind-limb strength and postural stability [35] (Fig. 2D and E), the Grip strength test for motor neuron function, spinal circuitry integrity, and muscle atrophy [36] (Fig. 2F and G), the Narrow beam test for balance, coordination, and fine motor control of the hindlimbs [37] (Fig. 2H and I) and the gait analysis test using the CatWalk XL device for coordination, paw placement accuracy, weight-bearing, and interlimb symmetry [57] (Fig. 2J–L). No significant differences were observed in the BBB neither in the inclined plane score (Fig. 2C and E). During the gait analysis, the stimulated animals demonstrated to run faster, with lower run durations at the fourth week (in comparison with the sham group (Fig. 2K, red line), indicating an improvement in their capacity for a coordinated and efficient running, whereas the sham group significantly lose it over time, that would be associated to the injury secondary damage detrimental and progressive effect [58]. Additional parameters acquired during gait analysis (Fig. 2L), such as the base of support and the stride length, although they failed to evidence significant differences among groups, a positive trend with a gradual improvement up to the forth week after implantation was also evidenced.

Overall, these findings support the hypothesis that the applied ES program through subdural EPB implantation, although not significantly improving voluntary and demanding locomotor outcomes, is associated with improved running performance.

Implanted wired EPB and subdural ES promote hMSCs and iNSCs migration and integration into the injured spinal cord

We investigated whether the cumulative application of μsPEFs followed by DC stimulation through the functionalized EPB device implanted in the subdural space influences the behavior of implanted stem cells in the injured spinal cord tissue both immediately after ES (at 16DPI) and in the long-term (46 DPI). Total number of hMSCs and iNSCs was quantified by co-staining of STEM121 for human-specific antigen recognition [59], with CD105 for MSC [30], or SOX2, for iNSCs [60], in sagittal sections (Fig. 3A). Representative images of double staining are shown for each case in Fig. 3B and C. We found that the total number of human engrafted cells, positive for STEM121 was significantly higher after ES at 16 DPI (Fig. 3D), and most of the human cells corresponded to the hMSCs, double-stained for CD105+/STEM121+ (Fig. 3E). In agreement with previous reports on the role of ES in stem cell recruitment and tissue regeneration [61]. Nevertheless, no statistically significant difference was found for the iNSC detection by double-staining for SOX2+/STEM121+ in the stimulated group (Fig. 3F, p = 0.09), in which the total numbers of migrating cells were always very low, suggesting a limited contribution of this cell type in a long-term after implantation. Additionally, a potential tendency for cell-type-specific responsiveness to ES can justify the preferential migration of the MSC [22]. Nevertheless, further analysis of the total migrating human cells, into the spinal cord at 46 DPI the did not show significant differences among groups (Fig. 3G–I), indicating that ES primarily exerts an early and transient effect on cell migration, as well as a long-term poor survival of migrating cells into the parenchyma in long-term evaluation. The impact of the applied ES protocols during early stages, at 16 DPI, in the neuronal differentiation of the migrating cells, by double staining with β3Tubulin (neuronal marker) and GFP (for hMSCs) or RFP (for iNSCs), did not reveal any significant difference for any case compared with the sham condition (Fig. 3J and K), indicating a preferential response to the applied ES for the undifferentiated cells.

These findings suggest that, while ES has been reported to influence stem cell fate and promote neurogenesis in vitro [17], [62], in vivo responses are limited and need further evaluation, taking into account the temporal, intensity, or concomitant signaling cues added by the injury during long-term evaluation.

The wired EPB implantation and subdural ES did not affect the cavity formation and astroglial reactivity

The SCI epicenter consists of a non-neural scar-forming lesion core surrounded by an astrocytic border that acts as a physical barrier for the axonal regrowth, interrupting the nerve signal transmission [63]. Nevertheless, the efficient astroglial border formation is essential in the early stages to contain and limit the spread of the injury [64]. Here, we investigate the effect of the EPB implantation and ES on the cavity formation and astrocytic reactivity at the injury epicenter (Fig. 4A and B). The cavity area showed a significant reduction over time (16 DPI: 21.41% ± 4.96%, n = 15; 46 DPI: 8.66% ± 2.37%, n = 13; p < 0.05) from early formation (at 16 DPI) to its consolidation (at 46 DPI) and ES did not significantly affect cavity size at either time point (Fig. 4C and D). As expected, the injury cavity was always significantly higher in the dorsal area due to the dorsal contusion (Fig. 4E and F). Analysis of GFAP border reactivity, assessed by GFAP expression and staining intensity within 350 μm around the cavity, also revealed no detectable effect of ES at all evaluated time points (Fig. 4G and H). For the comparative analysis of neuronal tissue preservation surrounding the injury core (indicated with an asterisk in Fig. 4I and J), the remaining neuronal fibers and somas were evaluated by B3Tub staining at 16 and 46 DPI (Fig. 4I and J), and no significant differences between groups at any of the evaluated time points (Fig. 4K and L).

These results indicate that, although EPB implantation and ES did not significantly reduce cavity size or astrocytic reactivity, they also did not exacerbate lesion progression or glial reactivity, also, without harmful effects on the neuronal tissue. This is a crucial finding, as certain interventions aimed at modulating the post-injury environment have been shown to aggravate tissue damage or increase scar formation [[65], [66], [67]].

Wired EPB implantation and subdural ES reduce early microglial reactivity

Neuroinflammation plays a key role in the development and progression of numerous diseases, such as neurodegenerative diseases or trauma-induced ones, such as SCI [67]. Notably, recent advancements have highlighted the importance of ES as a promising non-invasive method to regulate immune cell phenotype and activity for neuropreservation [[68], [69], [70]]. To evaluate the impact of the EPB implantation and ES on the modulation of the inflammatory cells we quantified the resident microglia, and the infiltrated macrophages, positive for the Iba1 staining [70], at the lesion core, rostral and caudally as indicated in Fig. 5A and B. Interestingly, ES significantly reduced the percentage of Iba1+ cells compared to the Sham condition at 16 DPI in all analyzed regions (Fig. 5C). Nevertheless, no differences were observed at the chronic stage, 46 DPI (Fig. 5D).

Notably, microglial activation is linked to an increase in cell body size and an increase in its roundness, indicating a morphological shift from a ramified, 'resting' state to an 'activated', amoeboid-like phenotype. This phenotypical shift is characterized by a reorganization of the cell's structure, reflecting its enhanced response to injury or disease [71]. Then, we evaluated the Iba + cells morphology and quantified the percentage of ramified-shape resting Iba1+ cells normalized for the total Iba1+ cells. Both groups at both times of analysis, at 16 DPI (Figs. 5E) and 46 DPI (Fig. 5F), showed a similar percentage of the resting Iba1+ cells compared to Sham SCI rats in all the considered regions except for the region 0.75 cm caudally, in long-term evaluation. But, at 16 DPI Stimulated SCI rats showed a decreased percentage of the resting Iba1+ cells at the injury core (Fig. 5G). No significant differences were observed at 46 DPI (Fig. 5H). Immediately close to the EPB, the percentage of total and ramified ‘resting’ Iba1+ cells did not show significant differences among groups at any time (Fig. 5I and J).

Our findings reveal that ES administered by the EPB system significantly reduced the presence of Iba1+ myeloid cells in the spinal cord at 16 DPI, suggesting a direct anti-inflammatory effect during the acute phase post-SCI. This aligns with previous reports that ES can modulate immune cell activity and attenuate neuroinflammation [72], [73]. However, this reduction was not sustained at 46 DPI, indicating that the immunomodulatory effect of EPB may require prolonged or repeated stimulation to achieve long-term immune regulation. Despite the reduced number of Iba 1+ cells, no significant differences were observed in microglial morphology at both 16 and 46 DPI, in most regions. This suggests that EPB primarily limits immune cell recruitment rather than altering the activation state or phenotype of microglial cells. Furthermore, the analysis of ramified resting Iba1+ cells normalized to total nuclei showed only a modest, localized reduction at the lesion core region, indicating a spatially restricted shift in microglial activation. Importantly, EPB stimulation did not exacerbate immune activation at any time point or anatomical level, maintaining an immune profile comparable to that of control animals, particularly in the region directly adjacent to the active area of the EPB system. This supports the safety of the EPB approach in terms of neuroimmune compatibility. Together, these data suggest that EPB stimulation exerts a measurable early anti-inflammatory effect post-SCI without inducing deleterious immune activation.

We also explored the potential impact of the ES in CCL2 (Fig. 6A and B) because it is upregulated early after SCI [73], contributing to the recruitment and activation of the immune cells, particularly the infiltrating macrophages [74]. No differences were observed at any time when normalized to total cell numbers (Fig. 6C and D). While CCL2 can be produced by neurons, influencing their excitability and synaptic transmission, it can also contribute to neuroinflammation and pain [75]. Then, we additionally quantified the CCL2 expression in the β3Tubulin + area, but no differences were found (Fig. 6E and F). Concurrently, endogenous fibrotic cells, expressing among others, the CD105 mesenchymal marker (Fig. 6G and H), migrate to the injury site, and contribute to the scarring process [76]. Interestingly, we found that at 16 DPI Stimulated SCI rats showed a significant decrease of endogenous CD105+ cells compared to Sham SCI rats (Fig. 6I), but not at 46 DPI (Fig. 6J), pointing to a possible early-stage interference with fibrotic mechanisms of scar formation. Since fibrotic cells contribute to scar formation and cytokine regulation, this reduction may also reflect an indirect anti-inflammatory effect of the stimulation, potentially reducing inhibitory barriers to regeneration [77]. Taken together, these findings suggest that ES through the biohybrid EPB device may influence specific cellular contributors to the inflammatory and fibrotic microenvironment, particularly during the acute post-injury phase.

Subdural ES by a wireless-controlled EPB implant after SCI

The wireless system with a fully implantable electrical source generator (as illustrated in Fig. 1C) reproduced the key findings found by using a wired external ES controller (data shown in Fig. 2, Fig. 3, Fig. 4, Fig. 5, Fig. 6). The clinical assessment of the motor functions using the open field and BBB locomotor rating scale [34] confirmed the previously reported effect. Sham and Stimulated animals showed comparable spontaneous locomotor recovery of the hind-limb functionality (Fig. 7A). Accordingly, ES did not induce additional sensory responses (Fig. 7B and C). A higher number of implanted cells migrated and engrafted into the spinal cord tissue was also found early after ES (Fig. 7D and E)higher numbers for hMSCs (Fig. 7F), and no differences for iNSCs (Fig. 7G).

Phosphorylated neurofilament heavy subunit (pNF-H) expression levels increased after SCI, with higher levels correlating with more severe injuries [78], showing no differences by the ES application (Fig. 7I). The cavity formation and astrocytic reactivity were also comparable among groups (Fig. 7J–L,K,L).

Iba1 staining confirmed previous results, showing that stimulated SCI rats had a significantly decreased percentage of the Iba1+ cells compared to Sham SCI rats in all the considered regions (Fig. 7M), with no changes in the total infiltrated macrophages (Fig. 7N), positive for CD68 [79]. The resting Iba1+ cells significantly decreased by the ES (Fig. 7O and P). But, the region above the lesion showed a comparable percentage of Iba1+ cells, even ramified 'resting' one, compared to Sham animals (Fig. 7Q).

In conclusion, applying ES through the biohybrid EPB device via a wireless connector for the internal stimulator system reproduces the previously described results obtained with a wired external stimulator. The new ES system did not induce additional sensory or painful responses, enhanced migration of hMSCs, and a significant reduction in Iba1+ microglia across the lesion site. No impact was observed on axonal preservation, glial scarring, or cavity size. Taken together, these findings confirmed that wireless EPB can reproduce the immunomodulatory and cell migration benefits of a wired system, while offering a more versatile and translatable technology.

Discussion and conclusion

The present study provides compelling evidence that subdural pulsed ES through a new EPB device, functionalized with co-implanted hMSCs and iNSCs, exerts a selective and phase-specific immune modulatory effect on the injured spinal cord microenvironment following incomplete SCI in a rat model.

While functional motor recovery, assayed by the open field test and BBB score for hind-limb locomotor performance analysis, Inclined plane (for postural and body balance strength) the Grip (for hind limb strength), and Narrow beam (for skilled coordinated walking), did not reveal a significant improvement following ES application, nor did it show any detrimental effects. Nevertheless, gait analysis using the CatWalk system demonstrated that the stimulated animals were able to run faster, indicating a progressive enhanced motor coordination one month post stimulation. These results are in agreement with recent report, applying subdural electrical stimulation, in which the sustained improvements appears since one month after stimulation [80]

The use of μsPEFs is supported by earlier studies demonstrating that such stimuli promote neurite outgrowth and differentiation of hMSCs and iNSCs [20]. Here, we demonstrated that the in vivo ES application increases the migration of hMSC, but not for iNSC, with a limited cell survival upon injury. Contrary to ours and others in vitro demonstration [17,20,21], the applied pulsed ES in vivo did not revealed an induced neuronal-like phenotypic differentiation of either hMSCs or iNSCs. This may be due to an anticipated cell death of the differentiated cells, or by a negative influence of the hostile extrinsic injury microenvironment on the molecular signaling cascades.

Analysis of lesion cavity size, astrocytic border formation, and astroglial reactivity revealed no detectable effects of ES, indicating that the stimulation does not exacerbate tissue damage or interfere with the healing of the injury. No major changes were observed in the recruitment of endogenous fibrotic CD105+ cells (negative for STEM121 staining). While these cells may contribute to tissue repair, their activation is also linked to chronic scarring and fibrosis in SCI [76].

Notably, a key finding of this study is the early, significant reduction in the number of Iba1+ myeloid cells in stimulated animals at 16 DPI, observed across all spinal regions surrounding the lesion. The applied ES provided by the wired or wireless systems exerts an early neuromodulatory effect during the acute post-injury phase, in agreement with additional reports [80]. The MSC, well known as immune modulators [12] could also contribute to that effect, since conditioned media of MSC in cell culture, subjected to the full ES produce, mimicking the in vivo stimulation, significantly reduced the secretion of IL1 β of M1 macrophages, however, due to the lack of an experimental group with no cells, we cannot assume a potential synergistic effect. Despite the reduction in total Iba1+ cells, including endogenous microglia and macrophages, the proportion of ramified or resting microglial cells remained unchanged as well as the total number of CD68-expressing macrophages, suggesting a selective effect on the microglia activation. Importantly, no signs of exacerbated Iba1+ signals were detected at later stages, supporting the safety of the applied ES for neuromodulatory interventions.

Sensory and pain processing were carefully assessed here using Von Frey and thermal nociceptive testing. These revealed no adverse sensory or nociceptive alterations induced by wireless stimulation, suggesting that the ES does not exacerbate neuropathic pain or trigger maladaptive plasticity. This is crucial, given the high prevalence and disabling nature of post-SCI neuropathic pain in patients.

Collectively, our findings demonstrate that the applied pulsed ES reduces acute neuroinflammation and promotes hMSC migration and then engraftment from the subdural implantation, proving a new system for cell transplantation and local ES.
