People who use wheelchairs
Authors: Lisa Freeman
Published: 2024

Some physicians are not comfortable caring for patients with disabilities, despite the prevalence of this population1

Self-reflection on attitudes and beliefs may mitigate bias and discrimination. Structural changes — for which funding is sometimes available — can ensure universal accessibility. Equitable access to health care will improve as physicians gain comfort and confidence caring for patients with disabilities.2

Patients should be asked about why they use a wheelchair, their capabilities and desires, and their visit goals

The patient’s quality of life should not be assumed. Some physicians may presume a lower quality of life for people with physical disabilities,1,2 including those who use wheelchairs, which can contribute to bias, discrimination, and differential care.3

Clinical settings should be accessible to people who use wheelchairs

Entrances should be free of barriers and steps, and should have automatic doors or door-opening buttons. Waiting areas should have space for mobility devices and clear passageways. Washrooms must be accessible, with sufficient space for a wheelchair. Clinical equipment should work for all people (e.g., height-adjustable examination tables).4

An effective referral is one that is accessible to the patient

Medical equipment and spaces are often not accessible for patients who use wheelchairs. This, along with bias, leads to patients with disabilities receiving differential medical care compared with patients without disabilities.5

Staff should interact appropriately and respectfully with people who use wheelchairs

Staff should speak directly to the patient — rather than a family member, attendant, or interpreter — to aid communication and fully include the patient.6 Patients (or their mobility devices) should not be touched or moved without permission and direction to do so.
