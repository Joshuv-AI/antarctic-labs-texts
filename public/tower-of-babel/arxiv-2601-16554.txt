Multidimensional compound Poisson approximations for symmetric distributions
V. Čekanavičius, S. Jokubauskienė

Abstract
Distribution of the sum of independent identically distributed symmetric lattice vectors is approximated by the accompanying compound Poisson law and the second-order Hipp-type signed compound Poisson measure. Bergström -type asymptotic expansion is constructed. The accuracy of approximation is estimated in the total variation metric and, in many cases, is of the order O ⁡ ( n − 1 ) O(n^{-1}) . Keywords: compound Poisson approximation, convolution of distributions, sums of random vectors, total variation metric, the first uniform Kolmogorov theorem MSC 2000 Subject Classification : Primary 60F99; Secondary 60G50;

Introduction

Compound Poisson (CP) approximations have many applications and are one of the popular fields of research in modern Probability Theory, see, for example,
[
2
,
5
,
6
,
11
,
18
]
and the references therein. Research results in this field are numerous. However, the absolute majority of results are proved for one-dimensional random variables. The situation is very different for random vectors (r.v.s), where results are few and usually obtained only for Kolmogorov or Prokhorov metrics, see
[
7
,
21
]
and the references therein. In this paper, for sums of lattice identically distributed symmetric r.v.s, we adapt Le Cam’s convolution approach and properties of metrics to prove results in total variation. Apart from the general case, when no moment assumptions are imposed, we consider mixtures of distributions concentrated on separate lines with finite analogues of variances, demonstrating how known one-dimensional results can be applied to obtain the multidimensional estimates.

We introduce necessary notation. The sets of all integers and natural numbers are denoted resp. by
\mathbb{Z}
and
I
N
.
By
{\cal M}_{d}
and
{\mathcal{F}}_{d}\subset{\cal M}_{d}
we denote the set of all finite (signed)
d
-dimensional measures and the set of all
d
-dimensional distributions, respectively. Let
\,I_{\vec{a}}\in{\mathcal{F}}_{d}\,
be the distribution concentrated at
\vec{a}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
,
I=I_{\vec{0}}
, where
\vec{0}=(0,0,\dots,0)\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
. Let
M,V\in{\cal M}_{d}
, then their convolution, for any
d
-dimensional Borel set
A
is defined as

(M*V)\{A\}=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}M\{A-\vec{x}\}V\{{\mathrm{d}}\vec{x}\}.

By
{\cal L}(\vec{X})
we denote the distribution of a r.v.
\vec{X}
. Note that if
S_{n}=\vec{X}_{1}+\vec{X}_{2}+\dots+\vec{X}_{n}
, where all r.v.s. are independent identically distributed (i.i.d.) and
{\cal L}(\vec{X}_{1})=F
, then
{\cal L}(S_{n})=F^{*n}
, where power is understood in the convolution sense. In this paper we prefer measure notation over r.v.s, since the main tools used for the proofs are properties of norms and convolutions.

Exponential measure for
M\in{\cal M}_{d}
is defined as

\exp\{M\}=\sum_{j\geqslant 0}{M^{*j}}/{j!}
,
where
M^{*0}=I
.

Compound Poisson distribution with parameter
\lambda>0
and compounding distribution
F\in{\mathcal{F}}_{d}
is defined as
\exp\{\lambda(F-I)\}
. Note that CP distribution can be expressed also as
a distribution of random sum
\vec{Y}=\sum_{j=0}^{\pi_{\lambda}}\vec{X}_{j}
, where
\vec{X},\vec{X}_{1},\vec{X}_{2},\dots
are i.i.d. r.v.s independent from Poisson random variable
\pi_{\lambda}
and
{\cal L}(\,\vec{X})=F
. CP distribution
\exp\{F\!-\!I\}
is called
accompanying distribution
to
F\in{\mathcal{F}}_{d}
. If
F\in{\mathcal{F}}_{d}
, but
\lambda<0
, then
\exp\{\lambda(F\!-\!I)\}
is called signed compound Poisson measure (SCP). All CP distributions are infinitely divisible.

For
F,G\in{\mathcal{F}}_{d}
total variation distance and total variation norm are defined as

d_{{}_{TV}}\!(F,G):=\sup_{B}|F\{B\}-G\{B\}|=:\frac{1}{2}\|F-G\|_{{}_{TV}},

where supremum is taken over all
d
-dimensional Borel sets. Total variation norm can also be defined via Hahn decomposition, see
[
5
]
, p. 228. For
F,G\in{\mathcal{F}}_{d}
with
supp\,F,\,supp\,G\subset\mathbb{Z}^{d}

\|F-G\|_{{}_{TV}}=\sum_{\vec{m}\in\mathbb{Z}^{d}}|F\{\vec{m}\}-G\{\vec{m}\}|.

To make proofs more concise the same symbol
\,C\,
is used to denotes all absolute constants. If ambiguities can arise we supply
C
with indices.
Symbol
\,C(N)
denotes constants that depend on
N
.
Multiplication is superior to division.
For any
x\!\in\!\mathbb{R}
let
\lfloor x\rfloor
denote the integer part of
x
.

\bar{e}_{m}=(0,\dots,0,1,0,\dots,0)

\bar{e}_{d}=(0,\dots,0,1)

\vec{X}_{1},\dots,\vec{X}_{n}
be independent r.v.s taking values in
\mathbb{Z}^{d}
and let

\displaystyle{\cal L}(\vec{X}_{i})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle q_{i}I+\sum_{m=1}^{d}p_{i,m}F_{m},\quad q_{i}=\hbox{\rm\hbox{I}\kern-1.62498ptP}(\vec{X}_{i}=\vec{0}),\quad q_{i}+\sum_{m=1}^{d}p_{i,m}=1,

\displaystyle supp\,F_{m}

\displaystyle\subset

\displaystyle\{k\bar{e}_{m},\ k=\pm 1,\pm 2,\dots\},\quad F_{m}\{k\bar{e}_{m}\}=F_{m}\{-k\bar{e}_{m}\},

\displaystyle\lambda_{m}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{i=1}^{n}p_{i,m},\quad\sigma^{2}_{m}=2\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}k^{2}F_{m}\{k\bar{e}_{m}\},\quad 0\leqslant q_{i},p_{i,m}\leqslant 1,

\displaystyle\alpha

\displaystyle\sum_{i=1}^{n}g(2(1-q_{i}))\min\bigg\{2^{-3/2}\sum_{m=1}^{d}\frac{p_{i,m}^{2}}{\lambda_{m}},\Big(\sum_{m=1}^{d}p_{i,m}\Big)^{2}\bigg\},

\displaystyle g(x)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle 2\sum_{s=2}^{\infty}\frac{x^{s-2}}{s!}(s-1)=\frac{2{\mathrm{e}}^{x}({\mathrm{e}}^{-x}-1+x)}{x^{2}},\quad x\neq 0.

Observe that in above setting coordinates of

are uncorrelated and, for example,

If

O(n^{-1})

In the case of i.i.d r.v.s.

d_{{}_{TV}}\!\left(\left(qI+\sum_{m=1}^{d}p_{m}F_{m}\right)^{*n},\quad\exp\left\{n\sum_{m=1}^{d}p_{m}(F_{m}-I)\right\}\right)\leqslant\frac{17.34}{n}\left(\sum_{m=1}^{d}\sqrt{1+\sigma_{m}}\right)^{2},

Results

3.1
The general case of vectors with possibly correlated coordinates

We will demonstrate that for identically distributed symmetric r.v.s, the assumptions used for (
2
) can be significantly weakened.
Let

\vec{X}\sim F\in{\mathcal{F}}_{d}^{s},\qquad supp\,F\subset\mathbb{Z}^{d},\qquad q:=F\{\vec{0}\}=\hbox{\rm\hbox{I}\kern-1.62498ptP}(\vec{X}=\vec{0})\in(0,1).

(3)

Assumptions (
3
) are more general than used in (
1
), (
2
). Indeed, in (
2
) it was required that
q\geqslant 4/5
, meanwhile in (
3
)
q
can be reasonably small. Moreover, non-zero probabilities are not restricted to coordinate axes. Further let
p_{\vec{x}}:=\hbox{\rm\hbox{I}\kern-1.62498ptP}(\vec{X}=\vec{x})=F\{\vec{x}\}
and
\sum_{\vec{x}}
denotes summation over all
\vec{x}\in\mathbb{Z}^{d}\setminus\{\vec{0}\}
.
Let

\delta(y)=\sum_{\vec{x}}\min\left(1,y{\mathrm{e}}p_{\vec{x}}\right).

(4)

Note that
\delta(y)\leqslant y{\mathrm{e}}(1-q)
,
\delta(y/k)\leqslant\delta(y)
if
k\geqslant 1
and
\delta(y)\leqslant 2N
if
supp\,F=\{\vec{0},\pm\,\vec{x}_{1},\dots,\pm\,\vec{x}_{N}\}
.

The following theorem gives a general idea about what constant can be expected for large
n
.

Theorem 3.1

Let assumption (
3
) hold. Then, for any
n\in\hbox{\rm\hbox{I}\kern-1.98611ptN}

d_{{}_{TV}}\!(F^{*n},\exp\{n(F\!-\!I)\})\leqslant\frac{4\delta^{2}(n/2)}{{\mathrm{e}}^{2}n}\left(1+\frac{C(\delta(nq)+1)}{q^{4}\sqrt{n}}\right)+\frac{C(1-q)^{9/2}}{q^{5}n\sqrt{n}}.\\

(5)

The following shorter version of Theorem
3.1
with the smaller power of
q

Theorem
3.1
*

Let assumption (
3
) hold. Then, for any
n\in\hbox{\rm\hbox{I}\kern-1.98611ptN}

d_{{}_{TV}}\!(F^{*n},\exp\{n(F\!-\!I)\})\leqslant\frac{C(1+\delta^{2}(n))}{nq^{7/2}}.

(6)

Theorem
3.1
can be used even if (

Example 3.1

Let

and from (

d_{{}_{TV}}\!(F^{*n},\exp\{n(F\!-\!I)\})\leqslant Cn^{-1/3}

Let assumption (

First we formulate general facts about exponential measures. Let
M,V\in{\cal M}_{d}
,
k\in\hbox{\rm\hbox{I}\kern-1.98611ptN}

\exp\{M\}*\exp\{V\}=\exp\{M\!+\!V\}

{\mathrm{e}}^{M}=I+M+\frac{M^{*2}}{2!}+\cdots+\frac{M^{*k}}{k!}+\frac{M^{*(k+1)}}{k!}*\int_{0}^{1}{\mathrm{e}}^{\tau M}(1-\tau)^{k}{\mathrm{d}}\tau.
