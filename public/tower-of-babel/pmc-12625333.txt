Prognostic significance of cytogenetic and molecular features in pediatric acute myeloid leukemia: a meta-analysis
Authors: Qing Huang, Zhijie Ling, Wei Zhang, Minghai Zhang
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundPediatric acute myeloid leukemia (AML) is a clinically and genetically heterogeneous malignancy with variable outcomes. Accurate risk stratification based on cytogenetic and molecular markers is essential for guiding therapy. However, the prognostic impact of several key genomic alterations remains inconsistent across studies. This meta-analysis aims to evaluate the prognostic significance of cytogenetic and molecular abnormalities in pediatric AML and clarify their association with survival outcomes.MethodsA systematic search was conducted across PubMed, EMBASE, Scopus, Web of Science, and CENTRAL up to May 5, 2025. Studies were included if they reported survival outcomes in pediatric patients (≤ 18 years) with de novo AML and evaluated cytogenetic or molecular markers. Data were extracted and synthesized using a random-effects model. Hazard ratios (HRs) or risk ratios (RRs) were pooled for overall survival (OS), event-free survival (EFS), disease-free survival (DFS), complete remission (CR), and relapse risk (RR). Heterogeneity was assessed using I² statistics, and risk of bias was evaluated using the Newcastle-Ottawa Scale.ResultsThirty-nine studies encompassing over 1,645 pediatric patients were included in the meta-analysis. WT1 overexpression was significantly associated with OS (RR = 1.38, 95% CI: 1.17–1.63). KIT mutations were linked to inferior OS (RR = 0.69, 95% CI: 0.57–0.84), but not to CR, DFS, or relapse risk. FLT3-ITD mutations showed no consistent prognostic effect (RR = 0.97, 95% CI: 0.65–1.46), with substantial heterogeneity (I² = 83%). CEBPA mutations did not significantly impact EFS (RR = 1.00, 95% CI: 0.93–1.07), and neither RAS mutations nor EVI1 overexpression demonstrated prognostic relevance. Publication bias was minimal, and sensitivity analyses confirmed the robustness of pooled estimates.ConclusionWT1 overexpression and KIT mutations (in selected cytogenetic contexts) are validated as adverse prognostic indicators in pediatric AML. Conversely, FLT3-ITD and CEBPA mutations require nuanced interpretation due to variable effects and methodological heterogeneity. These findings support the integration of molecular profiling into pediatric AML risk stratification and underscore the need for harmonized, prospective studies to refine prognostic models in this population.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12885-025-14761-1.Keywords: Pediatric AML, Cytogenetics, Molecular markers, Prognosis, WT1, FLT3, KIT, Meta-analysis

Background

Acute myeloid leukemia (AML) is a biologically and clinically heterogeneous hematologic malignancy characterized by the clonal proliferation of myeloid precursors in the bone marrow, leading to ineffective hematopoiesis and bone marrow failure. Although AML predominantly affects adults, it accounts for approximately 15–20% of all childhood leukemias and is the second most common form of pediatric leukemia after acute lymphoblastic leukemia (ALL) [1, 2]. In adult AML, molecular and cytogenetic risk stratification is well-established and has directly influenced the incorporation of targeted therapies and transplant strategies into clinical guidelines [1]. However, similar stratification frameworks in pediatric AML remain less developed due to limited pediatric-specific data, smaller cohorts, and age-related biological differences [3]. Prognostic evaluation in pediatric AML is multifactorial, relying on a combination of clinical, cytogenetic, and molecular parameters to inform treatment intensity and risk-adapted therapeutic strategies. Traditionally, clinical parameters such as age at diagnosis, initial white blood cell count, and response to induction therapy have been employed to assess prognosis. However, these factors alone have proven insufficient to fully capture the biological diversity of pediatric AML or to predict outcomes with high fidelity [4]. As a result, increasing attention has been directed toward cytogenetic abnormalities and molecular mutations that underpin disease pathogenesis and progression. Advances in high-throughput sequencing technologies and molecular diagnostics have facilitated the identification of numerous genetic alterations with prognostic significance, enabling refined risk classification and the development of targeted therapies [5].

Cytogenetic abnormalities, including structural chromosomal rearrangements and numerical aberrations, play a central role in the pathophysiology and risk stratification of AML. Recurrent chromosomal translocations such as t(8;21)(q22;q22), inv(16)(p13.1q22), and t(15;17)(q24;q21) define distinct biologic subtypes with characteristic morphologic, immunophenotypic, and clinical features. In pediatric AML, the presence of t(8;21) or inv(16), collectively referred to as core-binding factor (CBF) leukemias, is generally associated with a favorable prognosis, provided no co-occurring adverse mutations such as KIT are present [6]. Conversely, cytogenetic abnormalities such as monosomy 7, monosomy 5, or complex karyotypes are associated with high-risk disease and poor treatment outcomes. The integration of these cytogenetic findings into pediatric AML classification schemas has markedly improved prognostication and therapeutic decision-making [7]. Beyond karyotypic changes, a wide array of molecular alterations has emerged as critical determinants of prognosis in pediatric AML. Among these, mutations in genes involved in transcription regulation (e.g., CEBPA, RUNX1), signal transduction (e.g., FLT3, RAS), epigenetic modification (e.g., DNMT3A, TET2), and tumor suppression (e.g., TP53, WT1) have been extensively studied. FLT3 internal tandem duplications (FLT3-ITD), for instance, confer a high risk of relapse and poor survival, particularly when present at high allelic ratios or in conjunction with other mutations such as NPM1 [8]. Mutations in the WT1 gene, which encodes a zinc-finger transcription factor implicated in normal hematopoiesis, have also been associated with adverse prognosis, especially when co-occurring with FLT3-ITD. Conversely, biallelic CEBPA mutations often portend a more favorable outcome and are currently considered a marker of good risk [9].

Incorporating molecular data into risk stratification models has transformed the management landscape of pediatric AML. The identification of prognostically significant mutations has not only enhanced the accuracy of risk classification but also paved the way for the development of targeted therapeutic agents. For example, the use of FLT3 inhibitors such as midostaurin and gilteritinib in FLT3-mutated AML has shown promise in improving outcomes and is being actively investigated in pediatric trials [10]. Similarly, therapies targeting epigenetic regulators and aberrant signaling pathways are under development and hold potential for integration into standard care in the near future [11]. Despite these advances, challenges remain in the application of molecular profiling to clinical practice. The rarity of some mutations, the co-occurrence of multiple genetic lesions, and the dynamic nature of clonal evolution complicate the interpretation of molecular data and their prognostic relevance. The present meta-analysis aims to comprehensively evaluate the prognostic significance of cytogenetic and molecular features in pediatric AML.

Methods

Search strategy

This meta-analysis was conducted in accordance with the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses (PRISMA) 2020 guidelines. A comprehensive literature search was systematically performed across five major electronic databases: PubMed, EMBASE, Scopus, Web of Science, and the Cochrane Central Register of Controlled Trials (CENTRAL). The search covered the period from database inception through May 5, 2025. A combination of Medical Subject Headings (MeSH) and keyword terms were employed to capture a broad range of relevant studies. The core search terms included “acute myeloid leukemia” OR “AML” AND “pediatric” OR “childhood” AND “prognosis” OR “outcome” OR “survival” AND “cytogenetic” OR “molecular marker” OR specific genes such as “WT1”, “FLT3”, “NPM1”, “CEBPA”, “MLL”, and chromosomal abnormalities like “monosomy 7”, “t(8::21)”, and “inv(16)”. The search strategy was adapted for each database to ensure optimal retrieval of relevant studies. Filters were not initially applied for language, publication type, or study design to maximize sensitivity Table 1S.

However, only articles published in English were included in the final analysis. Duplicate records were removed using EndNote X9 reference management software. Reference lists of included studies and related review articles were manually searched to identify any additional eligible publications.

Eligibility criteria

Eligibility criteria were determined a priori and applied consistently throughout the selection process. Studies were included if they met the following criteria: (1) involved pediatric patients defined as age ≤ 18 years at diagnosis; (2) reported on individuals with de novo, non-therapy-related acute myeloid leukemia; (3) investigated the prognostic impact of at least one cytogenetic or molecular alteration such as FLT3-ITD, WT1 mutation or overexpression, NPM1 mutation, CEBPA mutation, t(8::21), or inv(16); (4) reported survival-related outcomes including overall survival (OS), event-free survival (EFS), disease-free survival (DFS), or relapse rate; and (5) presented or allowed estimation of effect sizes, preferably hazard ratios (HRs) with 95% confidence intervals (CIs), or other effect metrics like risk ratios (RRs) or odds ratios (ORs).

Exclusion criteria were applied to eliminate studies that would confound the results or lack methodological rigor. These included studies involving mixed adult-pediatric populations without separate pediatric subgroup analysis, case reports, conference abstracts, narrative or systematic reviews, and editorials. Studies focusing on special subtypes such as therapy-related AML, AML in Down syndrome, or AML secondary to bone marrow failure syndromes were excluded to maintain a homogeneous population representative of de novo pediatric AML. Studies with insufficient data for extraction or survival analysis were also excluded.

Study selection and data extraction

Two reviewers independently screened all titles and abstracts to identify potentially eligible studies. Full-text versions were then assessed for inclusion based on the pre-specified criteria. Any disagreements were resolved by consensus or through arbitration by a third reviewer. For each included study, relevant data was extracted independently by the same two reviewers using a standardized and pilot-tested data extraction form. Extracted variables included: first author, year of publication, country of origin, study design (prospective, retrospective), sample size, age range and median, sex distribution, types of molecular or cytogenetic features analyzed, treatments administered (if available), duration of follow-up, and all reported survival outcomes. Where specified, the type of CEBPA mutation (monoallelic, biallelic, bZIP) was recorded. However, due to inconsistent reporting, the meta-analysis included studies with mixed CEBPA mutation types, and stratification was not feasible. For studies that reported Kaplan–Meier curves without explicit hazard ratios, data points were extracted using Engauge Digitizer (version 12.1), and time-to-event outcomes were reconstructed using the method described by Tierney et al., which has been validated for meta-analysis of survival data [12].

Outcomes

Survival endpoints were defined based on the original study definitions. In general, OS was defined as the time from diagnosis to death from any cause. EFS was defined as the time from diagnosis to the first event, including relapse, treatment failure, or death. DFS was typically defined as the time from achievement of CR to relapse or death in remission.

Quality assessment

Assessment of methodological quality and risk of bias was performed independently by two reviewers using validated tools. For observational cohort studies, the Newcastle-Ottawa Scale (NOS) was used. This scale assesses studies across three domains: selection (up to 4 stars), comparability (up to 2 stars), and outcome (up to 3 stars), with a maximum score of 9. Studies scoring 6 or more were considered to be of high quality. Discrepancies in quality ratings were resolved through discussion or consensus with a third reviewer.

Publication bias was assessed using funnel plots and tested statistically with Egger’s regression test. Where asymmetry was detected, the trim-and-fill method was applied to assess its potential impact on the pooled estimates.

Statistical analysis

Meta-analyses were conducted using Review Manager (RevMan version 5.4) and Stata (version 17.0, StataCorp). Pooled hazard ratios and their 95% confidence intervals were calculated for dichotomous outcomes using a random-effects model, specifically the DerSimonian and Laird method, which accounts for between-study heterogeneity. Where studies reported other effect sizes (e.g., odds ratios or risk ratios). Heterogeneity across studies was evaluated using the I² statistic and the Chi-square-based Cochran’s Q test. An I² value greater than 50% was considered to indicate substantial heterogeneity. All p-values were two-sided, and statistical significance was defined as p < 0.05.

Results

Search results

The initial search yielded a total of 6,171 records across all databases. After removing 3,557 duplicates, 2,614 unique titles and abstracts were screened. Of these, 121 full-text articles were assessed for eligibility. A total of 39 studies met all inclusion criteria and were included in the final meta-analysis. PRISMA flow diagram depicting the study selection process

Study characteristics

The 39 included studies were published between 2003 and 2024 and enrolled pediatric patients (age ≤ 18 years) with newly diagnosed de novo AML. Study populations spanned various geographic regions, including North America, Europe, China, Japan, and the Middle East. Patient characteristics were generally comparable across studies, with median ages ranging from 4 to 13 years and balanced gender distributions. The molecular and cytogenetic features evaluated varied widely and included commonly studied markers such as FLT3-ITD, WT1, CEBPA, KIT, NPM1, EVI1, and RAS mutations. Treatment regimens were generally consistent with institutional or cooperative group protocols, including Children’s Oncology Group (COG), AML-BFM, and JCCG trials. Outcomes were reported at standardized time points (typically 3- or 5-year OS or EFS) using Kaplan–Meier analysis, with most studies providing HR, RR, or sufficient data to compute these values. Several studies provided data disaggregated by mutational subgroups, enabling subgroup meta-analyses. Outcomes

KIT mutations

A total of 18 studies evaluated the prognostic impact of KIT mutations on clinical outcomes in pediatric acute myeloid leukemia (AML), focusing on complete remission (CR), disease-free survival (DFS), overall survival (OS), and relapse risk.

Complete remission (CR)

Eight studies reported CR rates by KIT mutation status, including 153 events among 591 controls and 181 among KIT-mutant patients. The pooled risk ratio was 1.00 [95% CI: 0.93–1.07], indicating no statistically significant difference in remission rates between KIT-mutant and wild-type groups. The analysis used a fixed-effects model, justified by no observed heterogeneity (I² = 0%, p = 0.61). The overall effect test was non-significant (Z = 0.08, p = 0.94), suggesting KIT mutations do not influence initial treatment response Fig. 2. The funnel plot was symmetric, suggesting a low risk of publication bias Fig. 3.

Forest plot summarizing the impact of KIT mutations on complete remission (CR) in pediatric AML

Funnel plot assessing publication bias among studies evaluating CR by KIT mutation status

Overall survival (OS)

Eleven studies provided data on OS, encompassing 448 events in the mutant group and 2406 in controls. The meta-analysis produced a pooled RR of 0.70 [95% CI: 0.56–0.87], indicating a statistically significant adverse impact of KIT mutations on overall survival (Z = 3.25, p = 0.001). A random-effects model was applied due to moderate heterogeneity (I² = 58%, p = 0.008). These results confirm KIT mutations as an adverse prognostic factor for long-term survival in pediatric AML Fig. 4. Funnel plot inspection revealed no evidence of small-study effects Fig. 5.

Disease-free survival (DFS)

Eight studies explored DFS, with 143 events among KIT-mutated cases and 572 events in the control group. The pooled risk ratio was 0.94 [95% CI: 0.81–1.08]. Although this estimate suggested a slight trend toward inferior DFS in mutated cases, the difference was not statistically significant (Z = 0.90, p = 0.37). Moderate heterogeneity was observed (I² = 42%, p = 0.10), and a fixed-effects model was applied Fig. 1S.

Relapse risk

Relapse outcomes were reported in five studies. The pooled risk ratio was 1.24 [95% CI: 0.86–1.79], suggesting a trend toward increased relapse among KIT-mutated patients, although this was not statistically significant (Z = 1.14, p = 0.25). Heterogeneity was negligible (I² = 0%, p = 0.73), and the fixed-effects model was appropriate Fig. 2S.

WT1 mutation

A total of five studies assessed the prognostic significance of WT1 positivity in pediatric AML, with a combined sample of 228 patients in the WT1-positive group and 3,855 in the WT1-negative group. The pooled RR for OS was 1.38 [95% CI: 1.17–1.63], indicating a significantly higher risk in WT1-positive patients. The heterogeneity among studies was moderate (I² = 50%), and the test for overall effect was statistically significant (Z = 3.76, p = 0.0002). These findings suggest that WT1 overexpression is associated with inferior outcomes in pediatric AML Fig. 3S.

CEBPA mutation

Eight studies investigated the impact of CEBPA wild-type mutations on survival outcomes in children with AML. The pooled analysis included 181 patients with CEBPA mutations and 591 controls without the mutation. The meta-analysis found no significant association between CEBPA status and outcome, with a pooled RR of 1.00 [95% CI: 0.93–1.07]. The included studies largely did not distinguish between monoallelic and biallelic CEBPA mutations. Thus, the pooled analysis reflects an aggregated CEBPA-mutant population. Heterogeneity was negligible (I² = 0%), and the test for overall effect was non-significant (Z = 0.08, p = 0.94), indicating that CEBPA wild-type mutation does not appear to independently influence survival Fig. 4S.

FLT3-ITD mutation

Six studies assessed the prognostic impact of FLT3-ITD mutations. These included 148 patients with FLT3-ITD and 779 controls. The pooled RR was 0.97 [95% CI: 0.65–1.46], showing no statistically significant association. However, there was considerable heterogeneity among studies (I² = 83%, p < 0.0001), and the overall test of effect was non-significant (Z = 0.13, p = 0.90). These results suggest that the prognostic impact of FLT3-ITD in pediatric AML may vary across populations or depend on other co-occurring risk factors Fig. 5S.

RAS mutation

Eight studies evaluated RAS mutations with respect to event-based outcomes in pediatric AML, including a total of 143 RAS-mutant and 572 control patients. The combined risk ratio was 0.94 [95% CI: 0.81–1.08], indicating no significant difference between the groups. Moderate heterogeneity was present (I² = 42%), and the effect estimate was non-significant (Z = 0.90, p = 0.37) Fig. 6S.

EVI1 overexpression

Three studies evaluated the prognostic effect of EVI1 overexpression, involving 77 patients in the experimental group and 356 controls. The pooled risk ratio was 0.98 [95% CI: 0.71–1.35], with moderate heterogeneity (I² = 57%). The test for overall effect showed no significant difference (Z = 0.15, p = 0.88), indicating that EVI1 may not serve as a consistent standalone prognostic marker in pediatric AML Fig. 7S.

Discussion

This meta-analysis of 39 studies provides a comprehensive evaluation of the prognostic significance of major cytogenetic and molecular markers in pediatric AML. Our findings reaffirm that certain markers such as WT1 overexpression are associated with inferior outcomes, while others, including FLT3-ITD and CEBPA mutations, demonstrate variable or context-dependent prognostic value. The results also underscore the nuanced role of KIT mutations and highlight areas of ongoing uncertainty regarding markers such as RAS and EVI1.

The association between WT1 positivity and poor prognosis in pediatric AML was among the most consistent findings in our analysis, supported by a pooled RR of 1.38 [95% CI: 1.17–1.63]. This is in line with multiple high-quality studies, including Tarlock et al., who demonstrated that WT1 mutations or overexpression conferred a significant risk of relapse and death, particularly when co-existing with FLT3-ITD [25]. Similarly, Ho et al. reported WT1 expression as an independent predictor of adverse event-free survival in children with AML, highlighting its potential use as a molecular risk classifier [46]. In contrast, a subset of studies such as Guolo et al. did not find WT1 alone to be prognostically significant after multivariable adjustment, particularly in patients who underwent stem cell transplantation. These discrepancies may reflect differences in WT1 detection methods (mutation vs. mRNA overexpression), patient cohorts, or treatment strategies [50].

CEBPA mutations, especially biallelic variants, have been widely regarded as favorable prognostic markers in both adult and pediatric AML. Our meta-analysis, however, did not detect a significant association between CEBPA mutations and survival (RR = 1.00 [95% CI: 0.93–1.07]). This finding appears to contrast with prior studies, such as Hollink et al. (2009), which showed significantly better outcomes in pediatric AML patients with biallelic CEBPA mutations, even after adjustment for other covariates. One possible explanation is that some of the studies included in our analysis did not distinguish between monoallelic and biallelic mutations—known to have divergent clinical implications. Moreover, the relatively low frequency of CEBPA mutations in pediatric AML (approximately 5–10%) limits statistical power in many single studies, reinforcing the importance of well-annotated pooled data [20].

The FLT3-ITD mutation is well established as a poor prognostic marker in adult AML, particularly when the allelic ratio is high. In pediatric AML, however, our results revealed no statistically significant association with survival (RR = 0.97 [95% CI: 0.65–1.46]), and heterogeneity was substantial (I² = 83%). This variability is echoed in literature. For example, Meshinchi et al. demonstrated worse outcomes in FLT3-ITD-positive children, particularly in the absence of NPM1 mutations [10]. In contrast, Bazarbachi et al. reported no adverse impact of FLT3-ITD in patients receiving allogeneic stem cell transplantation, suggesting that treatment context may modify its effect. Moreover, the lack of standardized reporting of allelic burden in many studies reduces comparability. These inconsistencies suggest that FLT3-ITD may not be a standalone high-risk marker in pediatric AML but rather a component of a more complex mutational landscape [51].

Our analysis of KIT mutations revealed a dichotomy: while no significant associations were found for complete remission, disease-free survival, or relapse risk, KIT mutations were linked to poorer overall survival in a separate analysis (RR = 0.69 [95% CI: 0.57–0.84]). These findings are consistent with previous studies that reported adverse survival in pediatric patients with core-binding factor (CBF) AML harboring KIT mutations. For instance, Pollard et al. (2010) and Shimada et al. (2012) found that the presence of KIT mutations in t(8::21)-positive AML increased relapse rates and reduced OS [24, 35]. On the contrary, research by the Japanese Children’s Cancer Group (JCCG) suggested that risk-adapted therapy may offset the negative impact of KIT mutations. These contrasting results reflect the broader debate about whether KIT mutations confer universally poor prognosis or only within certain cytogenetic subgroups [46]. Importantly, our findings suggest that KIT mutations alone may not influence early treatment response but can have long-term survival implications, possibly via increased relapse susceptibility.

In contrast to the relatively well-characterized prognostic role of FLT3, CEBPA, and WT1, the implications of RAS mutations remain unclear. Our meta-analysis showed no significant association between RAS mutations and adverse events (RR = 0.94 [95% CI: 0.81–1.08]). This result is in line with Balgobind et al., who found that RAS mutations, despite being relatively common, did not significantly impact EFS or OS in pediatric AML [33]. However, some studies such as Stirewalt et al. reported modestly worse outcomes in RAS-mutant cases, especially in the context of high blast counts or chemoresistance [52]. It is likely that RAS mutations play a cooperative rather than dominant role in leukemogenesis and their effect may depend heavily on co-mutational profiles and treatment exposures.

The prognostic value of EVI1 overexpression, a known high-risk marker in adult AML, remains controversial in pediatric cases. Our pooled analysis yielded a non-significant RR of 0.98 [95% CI: 0.71–1.35], aligning with Balgobind et al., who found no consistent effect in younger patients. One explanation is that EVI1 overexpression often coexists with other unfavorable cytogenetic features, such as monosomy 7 or MLL rearrangements, which themselves drive poor prognosis. Furthermore, EVI1 testing is not routinely incorporated into most pediatric AML protocols, limiting data availability and cross-study consistency [49].

Strengths and limitations

This meta-analysis is one of the most comprehensive to date on pediatric AML, synthesizing data from 39 studies across diverse populations and applying rigorous quality assessment. It identifies both consistent and variable prognostic markers, offering guidance for future research. However, several limitations should be noted. This study has several important limitations. First, substantial statistical heterogeneity was observed in some analyses, particularly for FLT3-ITD (I² = 83%), likely due to differences in study populations, mutation detection methods, and therapeutic approaches. Second, while HRs are the ideal measure for time-to-event outcomes such as OS and EFS, several included studies did not report HRs, requiring the use of RRs instead, which may reduce the precision of pooled survival estimates. Third, most studies did not stratify FLT3-ITD cases by allelic ratio (high vs. low) or co-mutation with NPM1, despite these factors having established prognostic importance. Consequently, our analysis reflects a mixed FLT3-ITD population, which may mask known high-risk subgroups. Fourth, data on HSCT were not consistently available within subgroups such as FLT3-ITD, limiting our ability to assess its modifying effect on prognosis. Fifth, for CEBPA, most studies did not distinguish between monoallelic, biallelic, or bZIP mutations, leading to pooled results from heterogeneous mutation profiles. Sixth, there was significant heterogeneity in treatment protocols across studies, including differences in chemotherapy backbones, access to HSCT, and use of targeted therapies such as gemtuzumab ozogamicin, which may have influenced survival outcomes and limited the interpretability of pooled effect estimates.

Research implications

Moving forward, future research should prioritize prospective multicenter studies with harmonized testing and reporting standards. Pediatric-specific risk stratification models must evolve to include molecular interactions, not just single-mutation assessments. The incorporation of measurable residual disease (MRD) and clonal evolution analysis will further refine prognostication and help identify candidates for targeted therapies, such as FLT3, MEK, or menin inhibitors. Additionally, expanded access to sequencing platforms will enable broader integration of genomic data into clinical care, allowing for dynamic risk assessment and adaptive treatment planning.

Conclusion

In conclusion, this meta-analysis confirms the prognostic relevance of certain cytogenetic and molecular alterations in pediatric AML, particularly WT1 overexpression and KIT mutations in specific contexts, while challenging the uniform applicability of markers such as FLT3-ITD and CEBPA. These findings support the continued evolution of molecularly informed, individualized treatment strategies in childhood AML. As molecular profiling becomes routine, the pediatric AML community must collaborate to translate genomic insights into clinically actionable prognostic frameworks, ultimately improving survival and quality of life for affected children.
