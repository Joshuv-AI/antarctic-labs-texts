Death anxiety and its associated factors among palliative care nurses in China: a multicenter cross-sectional study
Authors: Jinyun Zhang, Qian Wu, Sen Wang, Liangkun Xu, Heng Xiao, Bingxin Liu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundTo investigate the current status of death anxiety among palliative care nurses, explore its associated factors based on the Conservation of Resources theory, and provide an empirical reference to inform future targeted clinical interventions.MethodsA multicenter cross-sectional study was conducted among certified palliative care nurses affiliated with 44 hospitals across Shanxi Province, China. The survey link was broadly distributed to all certified palliative care specialist nurses who had completed the standardized training program organized by the Shanxi Nursing Association. Ultimately, 318 valid questionnaires were obtained from nurses affiliated with 44 hospitals (valid questionnaire rate = 95.21%). Data were collected using a general information questionnaire, the Chinese version of Templer-Death Anxiety Scale (CT-DAS), the Positive Psychological Capital Questionnaire (PPQ), and the Perceived Organizational Support Scale (POS). Theory-driven hierarchical linear regression analysis was performed to examine factors associated with death anxiety.ResultsThe median total score for death anxiety among the palliative care nurses was 45.00 (IQR: 44.00–47.00), with 95.0% (n = 302) scoring above the screening cutoff (≥ 35) for high death anxiety. Hierarchical linear regression analysis (R2 = 0.660, P < 0.001) revealed that age, female gender, having no children, higher monthly income (≥ 8,000 RMB), and a nursing tenure of ≥ 5 years were positively associated with death anxiety, whereas positive psychological capital and perceived organizational support were negatively associated with death anxiety (all P < 0.05).ConclusionA substantial proportion of palliative care nurses experienced high death anxiety, which was statistically associated with a combination of sociodemographic, occupational exposure, and psychological and organizational resource factors. Given the cross-sectional design, these findings are hypothesis-generating. Background

Death anxiety refers to an overt or covert negative emotional response that arises when an individual anticipates the inevitability of death or confronts a mortal threat [1, 2]. In recent years, China has witnessed a steady rise in the burden of chronic diseases and cancer, driving a subsequent surge in the demand for palliative care services [3, 4]. Palliative care nurses, as the core providers of end-of-life care, are routinely confronted with the complex needs of patients and their families during the terminal phase [5]; however, their persistent exposure to death and intense emotional drain place them at substantial risk for developing high levels of death anxiety. This urgent issue not only threatens their psychological well-being but also erodes their clinical proficiency, obstructs meaningful end-of-life communication, and delays the effective roll-out of advance care planning [6, 7]. According to Conservation of Resources (COR) theory, individuals have an innate tendency to acquire and protect resources, whereas resource loss or the inability to gain new resources can trigger negative emotions such as anxiety and burnout [8]. Within this framework, palliative care nurses facing frequent mortality cues are highly susceptible to severe resource depletion; if not replenished in a timely manner, their personal resource reserves will diminish at an accelerated rate, eventually plunging them into a resource loss spiral [9, 10]. While internal psychological resources and external organizational support may offset this depletion, it remains to be explored which specific resource dimensions and individual sociodemographic heterogeneities are associated with death anxiety levels in this population [11]. To date, domestic research on nursing-related death anxiety has largely focused on traditional high-risk settings such as emergency departments or oncology units, leaving palliative care nurses—a specialized cohort facing continuous exposure to mortality—insufficiently examined [12–14]. Therefore, the present study focuses on palliative care nurses, aiming to evaluate their current level of death anxiety, explore its associated factors within the Conservation of Resources framework. Specifically, we hypothesized that higher positive psychological capital (as an internal resource) and higher perceived organizational support (as an external resource) would be associated with lower death anxiety.

Methods

Participants and eligibility criteria

Palliative care nurses were recruited from secondary and tertiary hospitals across Shanxi Province, China. All enrolled participants were certified palliative care specialist nurses who had successfully completed the standardized Palliative Care Specialized Nurse Training Program organized by the Shanxi Nursing Association and obtained the official Palliative Care Specialist Nurse Qualification Certificate. At the time of data collection, rather than being concentrated exclusively in dedicated hospice wards, these certified nurses were actively working across various clinical departments (such as oncology, geriatrics, and intensive care units). Within their respective units, their professional roles involved serving as the primary care providers and clinical resources for palliative and end-of-life care. To be eligible for inclusion, participants were required to meet the following criteria: (1) have at least three years of experience in frontline clinical nursing within emergency and critical care, oncology, geriatrics, or other relevant units; (2) have directly provided end-of-life care and experienced patient mortality; and (3) provide voluntary informed consent. Conversely, nurses were excluded if they: (1) were currently undergoing standardized nursing training, advanced clinical training, or short-term departmental rotations; (2) had been absent from work for more than three months during the survey period; or (3) had participated in any research projects related to death anxiety or psychological interventions within the past month.

Sample size estimation

Based on Kendall’s criterion for sample size estimation, a sample size of 5 to 10 times the number of independent variables is recommended for regression analysis. Given that 22 independent variables were included in the hierarchical linear regression analysis in this study, and accounting for a 20% potential invalid response rate, the required sample size was calculated to be between 138 and 275. Based on this calculation, the minimum required sample size was 138. To ensure adequate statistical power for the hierarchical regression analysis and to account for potential invalid responses, we aimed to recruit at least 275 participants. Ultimately, 318 valid questionnaires were collected, exceeding the minimum requirement.

Data collection and procedure

Data collection was conducted between May and June 2026 using the Wenjuanxing online platform. To reach all potential target participants, the survey link and QR code were widely distributed by the training program coordinators through the official online communication network of the Shanxi Nursing Association to all certified palliative care nurses across the participating hospitals in the program. Ultimately, completed responses were successfully obtained from nurses affiliated with 44 hospitals (comprising 25 tertiary hospitals and 19 secondary or lower-level hospitals). The landing page explicitly detailed the research objectives, voluntary participation, confidentiality policies, and completion guidelines, allowing all participants to complete the survey anonymously and independently after providing informed consent. To ensure data integrity, all items were set as mandatory to prevent missing data. Additionally, IP address restrictions and a ‘one WeChat account, one submission’ limit were applied to eliminate duplicate entries, and responses with a completion time of less than three minutes, logically contradictory answers, or uniform response patterns were strictly excluded during data cleaning. A total of 334 questionnaires were submitted by eligible nurses, and after excluding 16 invalid responses, 318 valid questionnaires were retained, yielding a valid questionnaire rate of 95.21%.

Instruments

Data collection was operationalized using a self-administered electronic questionnaire comprising four sections:

General information questionnaire

A sociodemographic form, self-designed by the research team, was utilized to collect participants’ baseline characteristics. These included gender, age, marital status, educational level, years of service in palliative care, and prior exposure to death education.

The Chinese version of Templer-Death Anxiety Scale (CT-DAS)

This instrument was employed to evaluate participants’ levels of death anxiety. Originally developed by Templer [15] and subsequently translated and culturally adapted into Chinese by Yang et al. [16], the scale comprises 15 items mapped across four dimensions: the emotional dimension (6 items), distress and pain (4 items), time consciousness (2 items), and the cognitive dimension (3 items). While the original scale utilized a dichotomous format, the adapted Chinese version employs a 5-point Likert response format ranging from 1 (strongly disagree) to 5 (strongly agree). Prior to statistical analysis, reverse-scored items (Items 2, 3, 5, 6, 7, and 15) were recoded. The total score spans from 15 to 75, with higher cumulative scores reflecting a greater severity of death anxiety. Based on the validation of this 5-point Likert adaptation in Chinese populations [16], a cutoff score of ≥ 35 was applied to identify “high” levels of death anxiety. Importantly, this threshold serves as a descriptive indicator of occupational psychological distress rather than a clinical diagnostic criterion. In the present study, the scale demonstrated excellent internal consistency, with a Cronbach’s α coefficient of 0.903.

Positive Psychological Capital Questionnaire (PPQ)

Developed by Zhang et al. [17], this tool was utilized to assess participants’ psychological resources. It consists of 26 items divided into four distinct dimensions: self-efficacy (7 items), hope (6 items), resilience (7 items), and optimism (6 items). Responses are rated on a 7-point Likert scale ranging from 1 (completely disagree) to 7 (completely agree). Cumulative total scores span from 26 to 182, with higher scores reflecting a greater reservoir of positive psychological capital. In the present study, the instrument exhibited excellent internal consistency, with a Cronbach’s α coefficient of 0.900.

Perceived Organizational Support (POS)

Originally developed by Professor Chen [18], this instrument was utilized to evaluate the level of external environmental support perceived by the participants. It consists of 14 items mapped across two dimensions: emotional support (items 1–10) and instrumental support (items 11–14). Responses are rated on a 5-point Likert scale ranging from 1 (strongly disagree) to 5 (strongly agree). Cumulative total scores span from 14 to 70, with higher scores reflecting a greater degree of perceived organizational support. In the present study, the scale exhibited excellent internal consistency, with a Cronbach’s α coefficient of 0.902.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analysis was performed using SPSS version 27.0. Prior to analysis, the raw data exported from the Wenjuanxing platform were independently double-checked and cleaned by two researchers to ensure data integrity. As all continuous variables and scale scores showed a non-normal distribution (evaluated by Kolmogorov-Smirnov and Shapiro-Wilk tests, P < 0.001), continuous data were presented as Medians and Interquartile Ranges (M [Q1, Q3]), while categorical variables were summarized as frequencies and percentages (n, %). Non-parametric Mann-Whitney U tests or Kruskal-Wallis H tests were conducted to evaluate univariate differences in death anxiety scores across sociodemographic and occupational subgroups, with Bonferroni-corrected post hoc pairwise comparisons applied for multi-category variables. Spearman rank correlation analysis was utilized to assess the relationships among positive psychological capital, perceived organizational support, and death anxiety.

To address potential hospital-level clustering across the 44 hospitals, an unconditional two-level linear null model with hospital specified as a random intercept was fitted. The estimated variance component for the hospital random intercept was 0.000 (residual variance = 42.432), yielding an Intraclass Correlation Coefficient (ICC) of < 0.001. This confirmed that hospital-level clustering was negligible and supported the validity of standard multivariable linear regression. Subsequently, a theory-driven, three-block hierarchical linear regression analysis was performed to identify factors statistically associated with death anxiety. Variable selection for the model was strictly guided by the study’s conceptual framework based on the Conservation of Resources (COR) theory (Fig. 1). To comprehensively adjust for potential confounders, the examined sociodemographic and occupational characteristics (with the exception of religious affiliation, which was excluded due to extreme demographic homogeneity) were forced into the model as covariates in Block 1 and Block 2, regardless of their univariate significance, followed by the entry of the core theoretical resources in Block 3.

Conceptual framework of variables associated with death anxiety among palliative care nurses. Notes: This framework was developed by the authors based on the Conservation of Resources (COR) theory and the study's conceptual model

Multi-categorical independent variables were re-encoded into dummy variables with prespecified baseline reference categories. Assumptions for multivariable linear regression were systematically verified: residual normality was evaluated using normal P-P plots and residual histograms, homoscedasticity was verified via scatter plots, observation independence was supported by the Durbin-Watson statistic (DW = 2.395), and influential cases were ruled out using Cook’s distance (all values < 0.10). Multicollinearity was evaluated using Variance Inflation Factors (VIF); while structural multicollinearity was observed among specific dummy categories of high clinical exposure, all primary theoretical variables (Positive Psychological Capital and Perceived Organizational Support) demonstrated VIF < 2.0, indicating that multicollinearity did not compromise the precision of the parameter estimates for these core variables. Model outputs report unstandardized coefficients (B) with 95% Confidence Intervals (95% CI), standardized coefficients (β), t-statistics, exact P-values, and model fit indices (R², Adjusted R², F, and ΔR²). All statistical tests were two-tailed, and statistical significance was established at P < 0.05.

Results

Sociodemographic characteristics and univariate analysis

A total of 318 palliative care nurses participated in this study (median age: 36.00 years, IQR: 30.00–42.00), predominantly female (95.3%), having children (72.6%), and Bachelor’s degree holders (81.8%).The median total score for death anxiety among the cohort was 45.00 (IQR: 44.00–47.00). Based on the validated cutoff score of ≥ 35 for the CT-DAS [16], 302 out of 318 participants (95.0%) scored above the screening threshold for high death anxiety. Detailed baseline characteristics and univariate analysis results are presented in Table 1. Although some subgroups displayed identical median values, the non-parametric Kruskal-Wallis test examines rank distributions, which may differ significantly even when medians are similar. Univariate analysis revealed that death anxiety scores differed significantly by gender, marital status, having children, years of nursing experience, monthly income, frequency of night shifts, frequency of providing end-of-life care, frequency of attending funerals, and frequency of death education or training (all P < 0.05). Overall, higher death anxiety was observed among female, childless, longer-tenured (≥ 5 years), and higher-income nurses, as well as those under greater occupational strain (≥ 4 night shifts/month and ≥ 6 end-of-life care exposures/month). Conversely, frequent funeral attendance and death education (≥ 5 times) were associated with lower anxiety levels. Other baseline characteristics showed no significant differences (P > 0.05).

Correlations among positive psychological capital, perceived organizational support, and death anxiety

Descriptive statistics and Spearman rank correlation coefficients for the key continuous variables are detailed in Table 2. Spearman correlation analysis revealed that death anxiety was significantly and negatively correlated with the total score of positive psychological capital (rs = -0.431, P < 0.01) and perceived organizational support (rs = -0.321, P < 0.01). Furthermore, all subdimensions of positive psychological capital (self-efficacy, resilience, hope, and optimism) and perceived organizational support (emotional support and instrumental support) demonstrated statistically significant negative correlations with death anxiety (all P < 0.01).

In the final model (Model 3), female gender, older age, childlessness (coded as 1 = No, with 0 = Yes as reference), longer nursing experience (≥ 5 years compared to the reference group of < 5 years), and higher monthly income (≥ 8,000 RMB compared to the reference group of < 5,000 RMB) were significantly associated with higher death anxiety scores (all P < 0.05). Crucially, after adjusting for all sociodemographic and occupational covariates, positive psychological capital (B = -0.073, β = -0.200, P < 0.001) and perceived organizational support (B = -0.064, β = -0.104, P = 0.020) demonstrated significant negative statistical associations with death anxiety scores.

Discussion

Current status of death anxiety among palliative care nurses

In the present study, the median death anxiety score among palliative care nurses was 45.00 (IQR: 44.00–47.00). As reported in the results, a substantial majority of the participants (95.0%) scored above the cutoff (≥ 35), highlighting a widespread and intense occupational psychological burden within this group. This heightened state of death anxiety is closely tied to the unique occupational exposures palliative care nurses encounter [19]. In their daily practice, they are continuously in contact with end-of-life patients and frequently confront both patient mortality and familial grief, making them more vulnerable to feelings of powerlessness and negative emotions [20]. Additionally, influenced by the traditional Chinese cultural taboo surrounding death, the death anxiety and emotional stress endured by palliative care nurses in their work lack a venue for open expression and resolution [21].

Guided by the COR theory, this high level of death anxiety may reflect the overextension of both internal and external resources among palliative care nurses. Conversely, the resource replenishment available to them is markedly limited [22]. As shown in Table 2, the positive psychological capital score of this group (median: 123.00, IQR: 115.00–134.00) was at a moderate level, which is lower than the findings reported by Teng et al. [23]. Furthermore, their score for perceived external organizational support [median: 48.50, IQR: 41.00–55.00] was at a lower-middle level, consistently trailing behind the levels observed in similar high-acuity nursing cohorts such as those evaluated by Muhaidat et al. [24].

The underlying reason may be related to the fact that palliative care in China is still in its developmental stage, and medical institutions remain underdeveloped in resource allocation and the construction of deep emotional support systems. From a COR perspective, this persistent state of high consumption and low replenishment aligns with a conceptual resource loss spiral, which corresponds to the observed pattern of lower supportive resources alongside higher death anxiety scores.

Psychological and organizational resources associated with death anxiety: a COR perspective

As shown in Table 4, both positive psychological capital (B = -0.073, P < 0.001) and perceived organizational support (B = -0.064, P = 0.020) were significantly and negatively associated with death anxiety scores in the final hierarchical model. In line with previous literature highlighting the primary role of internal and external resources in mitigating occupational strain [25, 26], our findings confirm that personal psychological strengths and institutional support independently serve as critical supportive resources associated with lower death anxiety when nurses confront mortality exposure.

As a core internal personal resource, higher positive psychological capital conceptually corresponds to the inner resilience necessary to confront mortality. Grounded in Conservation of Resources theory, nurses with higher psychological capital often possess greater confidence in end-of-life care and communication skills, enabling them to better navigate mortality events and mitigate acute resource depletion. Paradoxically, contrary to traditional curative paradigms focused on recovery, palliative care inevitably confronts the objective reality of the final cessation of life, which places continuous strain on the psychological capacity of nurses. This clinical reality highlights a potential tension between technical controllability and existential value within palliative care environments. Consequently, technical confidence alone may be insufficient to address the spiritual and existential challenges inherent in end-of-life care, further underscoring why overall positive psychological capital, encompassing practical confidence and inner resilience, remains essential in relation to lower death anxiety levels.

Perceived organizational support represents a fundamental external resource within the Conservation of Resources framework. In clinical environments characterized by continuous mortality exposure, adequate organizational backing can provide nurses with necessary structural and psychological safety. When nurses perceive strong support from their institutions, it may facilitate resource replenishment and mitigate the psychological strain associated with end-of-life care. Conversely, a perceived shortfall in organizational support, particularly regarding structured psychological debriefing, may leave nurses with delayed psychological replenishment when directly encountering the cessation of life. From a COR perspective, when external support systems fail to timely offset continuous emotional depletion, nurses are at a greater risk of entering a resource loss spiral. This theoretical framework provides a potential interpretation for the significant negative association observed between organizational support and death anxiety, highlighting the possible role of external structures in sustaining nurse well-being.

Occupational characteristics in relation to death anxiety: tenure and income

Results from the hierarchical linear regression analysis indicated a statistically significant association between monthly income and death anxiety after controlling for demographic confounders. Specifically, using nurses earning less than 5,000 RMB per month as the reference group, nurses earning 8000 RMB per month or higher presented significantly higher death anxiety scores (B = 2.749, P = 0.003). This phenomenon may reflect an imbalance between external monetary compensation and internal psychological pressure among higher earning cohorts. Within current healthcare compensation structures, higher salary tiers frequently coincide with senior professional titles or advanced career standing. From a Conservation of Resources perspective, senior nurses managing continuous clinical mortality exposure often bear elevated professional expectations and psychological responsibility, which may correspond to greater psychological resource depletion. This finding corresponds to research by Gao et al. [27] and Kan et al. [28] regarding occupational stress among experienced healthcare personnel. However, as role overload and job demands were not directly measured in this study, this interpretation remains speculative and requires empirical verification in future research.

Similar to the income variable, the association between occupational tenure and death anxiety exhibited stage-specific variation. Using nurses with less than 5 years of experience as the reference group, all other tenure categories demonstrated significantly higher death anxiety scores. Nurses with less than 5 years of tenure presented the lowest relative anxiety levels, mirroring findings by Li et al. [29]. This may occur because in early career phases, novice nurses primarily focus on mastering clinical operational norms and standardized workflows, remaining in an initial adaptation stage regarding psychological resource expenditure from frequent patient deaths. In the 11 to 15 years tenure category, death anxiety scores reached a relative peak (B = 2.571, P < 0.001). From a Conservation of Resources framework, mid-career nurses accumulate longer exposure to clinical mortality, which may correspond to more pronounced psychological resource depletion. For nurses with 15 years of tenure or more, death anxiety scores decreased slightly compared with the 11 to 15 years group (B = 2.006, P = 0.004), yet remained significantly higher than those of the reference group (< 5 years). This trend suggests stage-specific differences in the accumulation and depletion of psychological resources when nurses encounter clinical mortality exposure across different tenure phases.

Sociodemographic factors associated with death anxiety

Results from the hierarchical linear regression analysis in Table 4 indicated that gender, age, and parental status demonstrated statistically significant associations with death anxiety scores among palliative care nurses after controlling for demographic confounders. From the perspective of COR theory, these sociodemographic variations reflect heterogeneities in nurses’ personal psychological resource reservoirs, depletion patterns, and replenishment channels when confronting clinical mortality exposure.

Specifically, female palliative care nurses presented significantly higher death anxiety scores compared with male nurses [30, 31]. According to COR theory, individuals exhibit differences in expression and coping defense strategies when responding to occupational stress. Female nurses generally display a higher willingness to express their emotions when facing psychological pressure and are more inclined to openly acknowledge internal psychological resource depletion; conversely, male nurses tend to employ emotional suppression strategies to maintain a superficial resource balance [32]. However, given the unmeasured psychological mechanisms and the small male sample (n = 15), this explanation remains speculative.

Regarding age, the present study observed a positive association between age and death anxiety scores. Although previous literature indicates that older nurses report lower death anxiety due to accumulated professional experience [33], COR theory suggests that advancing age involves a natural expenditure of personal baseline resources, such as physical health and energy, alongside heightened concerns regarding health and aging [1]. When this natural expenditure of personal physical resources coincides with high intensity mortality exposure in palliative care wards, it may correspond to greater psychological resource depletion and consequently higher death anxiety scores.

Concerning parental status, nurses without children presented relatively higher death anxiety scores, consistent with the findings of Jin et al. [34]. From the resource replenishment and buffering perspective of COR theory, intimate family relationships provide crucial external social support resources, while raising children fosters a sense of generative continuity, which may theoretically offer a potential psychological resource buffer when individuals cope with clinical mortality stress [19]; in contrast, childless nurses may possess relatively fewer such specific resource replenishment channels when repeatedly confronting clinical mortality stimuli, thereby corresponding to higher death anxiety scores. However, this association remains exploratory as actual social support variables were not directly measured.

According to the COR theory, religious beliefs and spiritual faith are often conceptualized as part of internal personal resources, and their significance in contexts involving mortality warrants attention. However, restricted by high sample homogeneity, the present study was unable to incorporate this variable into the multivariable model, thereby precluding an empirical evaluation of the association between coping mechanisms based on faith and death anxiety. This limitation indicates that the relationship between religious and spiritual resources and death anxiety remains to be observed in broader and more heterogeneous populations.

Implications for practice and nursing management

As an observational study, our findings offer exploratory practical insights for nursing leadership to optimize palliative care support systems. First, nursing management may reevaluate the conventional assumption that equates higher salary tiers or advanced professional tenure with greater psychological resilience. Given the higher death anxiety levels observed among senior and mid tenure nurses, healthcare institutions may clarify job role boundaries and optimize task allocation to avoid placing excessive professional expectation burdens on core clinical staff.

Second, nursing managers may explore stage specific, full career cycle psychological resource replenishment strategies. Leadership may direct attention toward nurses in the 11 to 15 years tenure category who presented relatively higher death anxiety scores, offering targeted occupational stress alleviation strategies and regular psychological counseling. Meanwhile, healthcare institutions could establish voluntary peer exchange platforms to encourage senior nurses with 15 or more years of tenure to share mature coping experiences with early career nurses, offering experiential resource buffering before severe resource depletion occurs in novice cohorts.

Finally, adhering to principles of workplace equity and health protection, organizational support mechanisms should remain universally accessible to all palliative care nurses. Nursing leadership should avoid implementing stereotypical or non voluntary scheduling adjustments based solely on demographic labels. Simultaneously, statutory rights and reasonable health accommodations for pregnant and lactating nurses must be safeguarded according to law, alongside establishing flexible support systems based on individual voluntary requests. Healthcare institutions may consider enhancing universal employee welfare security systems and establishing routine, confidential psychological counseling channels. Furthermore, conducting voluntary death education workshops and existential reflection seminars across the workforce may assist nurses in constructing mature perspectives on life and death, potentially supporting their psychological adjustment and coping capacity when confronting clinical mortality exposure.

Limitations

Several methodological limitations should be considered when interpreting the findings of this study. First, the cross-sectional design inherently restricts the ability to establish temporal sequences among variables. Therefore, the observed associations should be viewed as hypothesis-generating exploratory results rather than definitive evidence of causal relationships or protective mechanisms. Second, although multiple demographic confounders were controlled, unmeasured residual confounding cannot be completely ruled out. Unmeasured factors such as specific living arrangements, objective social support networks, and clinical night shift workloads may exert potential influences on the observed associations. Moreover, while childlessness demonstrated an independent statistical association with higher death anxiety, the proposed underlying mechanisms remain exploratory. As such, this hypothesis requires future studies to directly measure mediating constructs such as generative continuity in order to be empirically tested. Third, while hospital-level clustering was evaluated and found to be negligible, we acknowledge that a full multilevel analytical framework was not ultimately adopted. Additionally, sampling was geographically restricted to 44 hospitals within Shanxi Province using convenience sampling. This introduces potential uncertainty regarding hospital-level representativeness across regions with differing economic development or healthcare resource allocations, while also risking potential selection bias and non-response bias among invited staff. Fourth, the reliance on self-report questionnaires may introduce information bias, social desirability bias, and potential common method bias when participants reflect on sensitive mortality topics. Fifth, extreme demographic homogeneities—such as the substantial gender imbalance (95.3% female, n = 303) and a lack of religious diversity (96.9% reporting no religious affiliation)—along with smaller sample sizes in certain subgroups (such as male nurses or specific income tiers) constrain the statistical power and generalizability of subgroup analyses. Sixth, although all participants were certified palliative care specialist nurses, their current clinical assignments may vary across departments (e.g., oncology, geriatrics, or emergency care). Future studies focusing exclusively on nurses in dedicated hospice or palliative care units may further clarify the specific influence of workplace context on death anxiety. Finally, the categorization and scoring classification methods applied to continuous or ordinal variables may carry measurement classification limitations. To address these methodological limitations, future longitudinal or multicenter prospective studies incorporating broader geographic regions, demographically and religiously diverse cohorts are recommended to further examine these complex psychological associations.

Conclusions

In summary, the findings of this observational study indicate that death anxiety scores were present at a relatively high level among palliative care nurses. Multiple demographic, occupational, and psychological factors including age, gender, parental status, years of nursing experience, monthly income, positive psychological capital, and perceived organizational support demonstrated statistically significant associations with death anxiety scores. Notably, higher salary levels were not associated with lower death anxiety scores, which may suggest that financial compensation alone does not necessarily correspond to reduced psychological distress in palliative care environments.

Moving forward, longitudinal and interventional studies are warranted to confirm these observed associations and evaluate targeted strategies for mitigating death anxiety. Practically, healthcare institutions may consider developing exploratory, demand-based support systems across the full career cycle. Measures such as facilitating voluntary death education workshops, establishing confidential psychological counseling channels, and strengthening organizational support networks may offer valuable psychological resources for nurses when coping with clinical mortality exposure.
