Reduced soil microbial diversity and altered co-occurrence networks associated with Phytophthora nicotianae infection in flue-cured tobacco fields
Authors: Ayodeji Bello, Jing Yuan, Kamal Chhetri, Ran Yang, Jiayu Dong, Priyanka Gangwar, Ruizhu Shi, Zhenshan Chen, T. David Reed, Ryan Stewart, Yiming Feng, Yuan Zeng
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTPhytophthora nicotianae is a persistent and highly destructive soilborne oomycete that causes black shank of tobacco and continues to threaten its production despite integrated management. Although soil microbiomes are increasingly recognized as important components of disease outcomes, little is known about how soil microbial communities vary with P. nicotianae DNA concentrations and black shank pressure under field conditions. We examined relationships among 17 soil physicochemical properties, microbial community features, P. nicotianae DNA levels, and black shank outcomes in three flue-cured tobacco fields sampled repeatedly throughout one growing season. Phytophthora nicotianae DNA concentrations were associated with several soil physicochemical properties. The field with the lowest disease pressure had higher microbial alpha diversity, the simplest bacterial network, and a moderately complex fungal network. In contrast, the field with the highest black shank mortality showed the lowest alpha diversity, the most complex bacterial network, and the least complex fungal network. Over the growing season, microbial alpha diversity declined, and disease outcomes were associated with field-specific shifts in microbial community composition. Trichoderma, Fusarium, and Globodera were most abundant in the high-disease field, whereas multiple bacterial groups within Firmicutes, Proteobacteria, and Actinobacteriota were negatively associated with P. nicotianae in the lower-disease fields. These results indicate that P. nicotianae DNA abundance and black shank pressure are associated with soil physicochemical properties and field-dependent patterns in microbial diversity, community composition, network topology, and several microbial taxa. Our study provides a field-based foundation for future research to experimentally test candidate microbial indicators and microbe–pathogen relationships relevant to black shank risk assessment.IMPORTANCEBlack shank is a persistent soilborne disease that threatens tobacco production and is difficult to manage because the pathogen survives in soil and disease outcomes vary from field to field. This study shows that measuring the pathogen alone may not fully explain disease risk. Across three Virginia tobacco fields, black shank pressure was linked to soil properties and differences in the soil microbial community. Fields with lower disease pressure had higher microbial diversity and different groups of bacteria associated with lower levels of Phytophthora nicotianae, the causal agent of black shank. These findings suggest that future disease-risk assessment may be improved by considering soil conditions and microbial indicators together with pathogen detection. The work provides a foundation for identifying soil and microbial features that could support more targeted and sustainable black shank management strategies.

INTRODUCTION

Phytophthora nicotianae, a soilborne oomycete pathogen and the causal agent of black shank disease in tobacco, poses a significant threat to tobacco production in the United States and other producing countries (1, 2). The pathogen infects tobacco at all growth stages (3), thrives in warm temperatures (23–28°C) and high soil moisture, and spreads rapidly within fields primarily through its motile zoospores. With its thick-walled chlamydospores, the pathogen can persist in soil without the presence of a host for a long period of time, making it difficult to eradicate (1, 3).

Black shank disease management mainly relies on an integrated approach that includes the use of resistant tobacco varieties carrying Ph genes, the Wz gene, or polygenic resistance loci (1, 4), along with chemical control using oomyceticides, such as metalaxyl, mefenoxam, fluopicolide, and oxathiapiprolin (5). However, repeated planting of single-gene (Ph or Wz gene) resistant tobacco varieties has resulted in P. nicotianae populations that overcome plant resistance (6). The use of chemical treatments also raises environmental concerns and may increase the risk of developing fungicide-resistant pathogen strains (7, 8). Soil organic amendments and cover crops, such as mustard, have been tested to manage P. nicotianae populations in the soil and reduce black shank severity; however, inconsistent results were found (9). Thus, there is a need to better understand the field-level biological and environmental factors associated with black shank development, including how soil microbial communities vary with P. nicotianae abundance and black shank disease pressure.

Over the past decade, interest has increased in developing strategies that leverage soil microbiomes to enhance crop productivity and reduce soilborne pathogen pressure (10, 11) since soil microbiota influence plant health through nutrient cycling, plant-growth promotion, competition with pathogens, and mediation of disease resistance (12). Understanding differences between microbial communities in fields with low and high disease pressure is important. In some disease-suppressive soils, microbial communities have been associated with distinct microbial taxa, higher microbial diversity, or more connected microbial networks (13, 14). However, pathogen invasion and disease development can also disrupt native soil microbial communities, leading to shifts in community structure and function that may favor disease development (15). For example, infections by Ralstonia solanacearum and Verticillium dahliae have been reported to reduce microbial diversity and enrich non-pathogenic or opportunistic organisms (16–18).

Soil structure, through its aggregation of mineral and organic matter, governs microbial community assembly by regulating oxygen, water, and nutrient availability (19, 20). In parallel, soil physicochemical properties, such as pH, organic carbon, nitrogen, moisture content, as well as agronomic factors, such as crop management history, further influence microbial metabolism, growth, and spatial distribution across successional and seasonal gradients (21–23). Variation in soil structure, physicochemical properties, and soil microbiome composition may subsequently influence soilborne pathogen activity and disease outcomes (24). Nevertheless, there remains limited understanding of how soil physical, chemical, and biological factors covary during plant disease development, hindering the identification of environmental factors associated with plant disease risk.

Despite the importance of P. nicotianae to tobacco production, no studies have considered how soil microbiota vary with P. nicotianae DNA dynamics and black shank pressure under field conditions. Clarifying these relationships may help identify microbial indicators associated with pathogen pressure and disease outcome. Here, we examined associations among soil physicochemical properties, P. nicotianae DNA concentrations, black shank pressure, and soil microbial diversity, composition, and co-occurrence network structure across three flue-cured tobacco fields naturally infested with P. nicotianae. We hypothesized that (i) microbial community diversity and composition would differ among fields and across sampling time points due to environmental heterogeneity and variations in disease pressure, and (ii) microbial co-occurrence network structure would differ among fields with different levels of black shank pressure.

MATERIALS AND METHODS

Experimental design

Three tobacco fields with a history of black shank disease were selected for study during the 2023 growing season. These farms were in Dinwiddie County (Barnes Farms), Brunswick County (C&C Farms), and Charlotte County (Ferrel Farms) in Virginia. Weather data from May to September 2023 were obtained from NOAA (25), with average temperatures ranging from 21.2 to 22.4°C and total precipitation of 60.9 to 64.3 cm across sites. The fields differed in farming practice, soil texture, soil map unit (https://websoilsurvey.nrcs.usda.gov), and selected pre-planting and pre-harvest soil physicochemical properties (Table 1). Agronomic practices in Barnes and C&C were conducted according to conventional practices recommended by the Virginia Cooperative Extension (26), whereas Ferrel was managed following the United States Department of Agriculture National Organic Program standards (27). At each field, three plots of 10 m × 10 m were established as replicated observational plots, with a minimum distance of 100 m between plots. No experimental treatments were applied. The flue-cured tobacco cultivar ‘NC196,’ which possesses the Php gene providing immunity to P. nicotianae race 0 and partial polygenic resistance to race 1, was used in all plots across three study fields.

Soil collection, sample processing, and measurements of soil physicochemical properties

Soil samples were collected monthly at each plot from pre-planting to pre-harvest. For each plot, 10 soil cores from the root-zone area (i.e., within 10 cm of each tobacco plant) were collected from a depth increment of 0–20 cm on the bed following a zig-zag pattern with a standard 2.2-cm diameter × 53 cm long soil probe (28) (AMS, American Falls, ID, USA). Soil cores from the same plot were combined, thoroughly mixed, and transported in an icebox to the Plant Pathology Laboratory at the Virginia Tech Southern Piedmont Agricultural Research and Extension Center (SPAREC) in Blackstone, VA, USA. Plant roots and debris were manually removed from soil samples before DNA extraction. A portion of each pre-planting and pre-harvest soil sample was sent to Ward Laboratories, Inc. (Kearney, NE, USA) for routine analysis to determine soil physicochemical properties, including soil pH, organic matter by loss on ignition, phosphorus by Mehlich-3 extraction, magnesium, calcium, sodium, and potassium by ammonium acetate extraction, nitrate-N by KCl extraction, sulfate by Mehlich-3 extraction, zinc, iron, manganese, and copper by DTPA extraction, sum of cations, and soil texture fractions. Principal component analysis was conducted in R (Version 4.3.2) using the prcomp function from the stats package (29) to investigate variation in pre-planting and pre-harvest soil properties among fields.

DNA extraction, PCR amplification, and microbial sequencing

For each collected soil sample, DNA was extracted in duplicate, with each extraction performed on 0.25 g of soil using the DNeasy PowerSoil Pro Kit (Qiagen, Germany) according to the manufacturer’s protocol. The resulting DNA samples were quantified using a NanoDrop spectrophotometer (Thermo Scientific, USA). A portion of each DNA sample was sent to Novogene for bacterial 16S ribosomal RNA (16S rRNA) and fungal Internal Transcribed Spacer (ITS) library preparation and amplicon sequencing according to Novogene’s protocol. The V3–V4 regions of 16S rRNA were amplified with primers 341F (5′-CCTAYGGGRBGCASCAG-3′) and 806R (5′-GCACTACNNGGGTATCTAAT-3′) (30, 31), and ITS2 regions were amplified with ITS3-2024F (5′-GCATCGATGAAGAACGCAGC-3′) and ITS4-2409R (5′-TCCTCCGCTTATTGATATGC-3′) (32). Extraction blanks and non-template controls were included in the aforementioned polymerase chain reactions and showed no visible bands by gel electrophoresis. Amplicons were then purified with magnetic beads and quantified with Agilent 5400 Fragment Analyzer (Agilent Technologies, USA). The same amount of PCR products from each sample was end-repaired, A-tailed, and further ligated with barcodes and Illumina adapters. The quality of the resulting libraries was assessed with Qubit and the Agilent Bioanalyzer 2100 system. The quantified libraries were pooled and sequenced on Illumina NovaSeq using paired-end chemistry.

Quantification of P. nicotianae DNA concentration and assessment of black shank disease severity

Soil DNA was used to estimate P. nicotianae DNA concentration at each sampling time point using a TaqMan-based quantitative real-time PCR (qPCR) assay following the procedure described by Li et al. (33), with modifications by comparing to an in-house prepared standard curve. Briefly, a 20 μL reaction mixture consisted of 2 μL DNA, 10 μL of 2× PrimeTime Gene Expression Master Mix (Integrated DNA Technologies) containing 0.1 μM 50 × ROX reference dye, 0.5 μL of each 10 μM forward (5′-CCTATCAAAAAAAAGGCGAACG-3′) and reverse primers (5′-TACACGGAAGGAAGAAAGTCAAG-3′), along with 0.25 μL of 10 μM probe (5′-CGGACACTGATACAGGCATACTTCCAGG-3′) labeled with FAM reporter dye and TAMRA quencher. The qPCR program included 1 cycle of initial denaturation at 95°C for 3 min, followed by 45 cycles of 95°C for 15 s and 63°C for 30 s on an Applied Biosystems QuantStudio3 Real-Time PCR System (Life Technologies, USA) to obtain cycle threshold (Ct) values. To develop a standard curve, we extracted DNA from a pure culture of P. nicotianae grown in 10% clarified V8 liquid media for 7 days at 28°C with constant agitation at 150 rpm. One microliter of a 10-fold serial dilution of extracted P. nicotianae DNA was added to 0.25 g of sterile soil, which was prepared from soil samples collected at the SPAREC tobacco black shank nursery, followed by DNA extraction. The DNA concentrations of P. nicotianae standard curve were 1.8 × 108, 1.8 × 107, 1.8 × 106, 1.8 × 105, 1.8 × 104, and 1.8 × 103 in fg/g of soil. A linear regression equation was generated by plotting Ct values against the log₁₀-transformed DNA concentrations of the standard curve. Standard curves were accepted when slopes ranged from −3.3 to −3.6, corresponding to amplification efficiencies of approximately 90–100%, and when the coefficient of determination (R2) was ≥0.98. The Ct values of tested soil samples were then applied to the linear equation to calculate P. nicotianae DNA concentration.

Black shank disease was assessed monthly in each plot by recording plant mortality. Plant mortality was attributed to black shank when plants showed symptoms typical of the disease, including rapid wilting, dark necrosis at the lower stem, and pith discing. When symptoms resembled bacterial wilt or Fusarium wilt, necrotic tissues were collected and brought to the lab for immunostrip testing to confirm P. nicotianae infection. The monthly tobacco mortality rate (%) caused by black shank disease was calculated as the number of dead plants divided by the total number of plants in each plot.

The area under the disease progress curve (AUDPC) was calculated using the mortality rate recorded at each time point (34) to compare the disease severity among fields.

Bioinformatics and statistical analyses

Operational taxonomic units processing and quality control

The bioinformatics procedure used in this study followed the Mothur pipeline described by Zeng et al. (35), with some modifications. Briefly, contigs were created from the overlapping read pairs of each 3′ and 5′ end reads, and sequences and PCR errors were reduced, in which contigs containing ambiguous bases and longer than 450 bp for 16S rRNA or 380 bp for ITS were removed. The unique sequences were merged and aligned to the SILVA (silva.nr_v138.fasta) or UNITE ITS (UNITEv9_sh_99.fasta) databases and further de-noised to allow up to two nucleotide differences between sequences. Subsequently, chimeric sequences were detected and removed using the VSEARCH algorithm (36). The processed sequences were classified using the Bayesian classifier and clustered into operational taxonomic units (OTU) at 97% identity. Although OTUs generally provide lower sequence-level resolution than amplicon sequence variants (ASVs), OTU clustering was used to provide community-level resolution for comparing broad microbial patterns among fields and sampling times, their tolerance to sequencing errors, and downstream computational efficiency (37). OTUs with abundance sums equal to 0 were excluded after sparse abundances (counts ≤ 1) were set to 0, and the resulting matrices were used for statistical analyses.

Microbial diversity calculation

For alpha-diversity analysis, bacterial and fungal OTU tables were rarefied to the minimum sequencing depth within each data set using the rarefy function in the vegan package. Rarefied richness and Shannon diversity indices at the OTU level were then calculated from the rarefied OTU tables using the estimate_richness function in the phyloseq package (38). The Shannon index and rarefied richness for each soil sample were visualized using GraphPad Prism software (version 10.2.0), and generalized linear mixed models (GLMMs) in SAS OnDemand (PROC GLMM; https://welcome.oda.sas.com) were used to evaluate how they vary as functions of field, sampling time, and their interactions. When a fixed effect was significant, least squares means (LS-means) were compared using the Tukey’s adjustment (a = 0.05). The dissimilarities in bacterial or fungal community compositions among fields and over time were assessed using non-metric multidimensional scaling (NMDS) based on Bray–Curtis dissimilarity matrices with the vegan package (39). The effects of location, sample time, and their interaction on microbial composition were tested using permutational multivariate analysis of variance (PERMANOVA) with the adonis function in the vegan package using 9,999 permutations. The community compositions were visualized using tidyverse (40), ggplot2 (41), and ggprism (42) packages. Differences in the relative abundances of the top 10 bacterial and fungal phyla and genera among fields and sampling times were assessed using the Kruskal–Wallis test in the dplyr package (43). In addition, the top 10 bacterial and fungal genera were compared within each field and across sampling times within a field using pairwise two-sided multiple-comparison analyses with the Dwass, Steel, Critchlow-Fligner method in SAS. Prior to network construction, OTUs with a relative abundance below 0.01% of the total bacterial or fungal taxa were removed (44).

Co-occurrence network construction

Microbial association networks for bacterial or fungal OTUs and P. nicotianae were constructed using the Sparse Correlations for Compositional data (SparCC) algorithm (45) implemented from the official GitHub repository. To reduce noise, OTUs with fewer than 10 reads in each sample were removed prior to analysis. Pairwise correlations were estimated using SparCC with 200 iterations (-ni 200). To assess significance, 200 permutation data sets were generated, and empirical P-values were calculated. Associations among OTUs were retained if they had |r| ≥ 0.7 and P < 0.01. The resulting correlation matrix was processed in R using tidyverse, reshape2 (46), and dplyr packages to generate edge and node tables for network visualization. The network properties, such as nodes, number of edges, modularity, and average degree, were determined and visualized using the Gephi (V0.10.0) program. A lower threshold |r| ≥ 0.5 and P < 0.01 was used to include OTUs that had significant associations with P. nicotianae in the overall network, as previous research has reported that correlations with threshold |r| ≥ 0.46 and P < 0.01 are statistically significant when dealing with a large number of DNA samples per site (47). In the final networks, node size was scaled by the relative abundance of each OTU, node color was assigned according to phylum, and edge color differentiated positive (co-occurrence) versus negative (co-exclusion) correlations between significantly associated nodes.

Correlation and predictive modeling

Phytophthora nicotianae DNA concentrations were compared among fields and among sampling times within each field using GLMMs, with pairwise comparisons adjusted using Tukey’s method as described above. Spearman correlation analysis was conducted to determine the relationships between pre-planting soil physicochemical variables and concentration of P. nicotianae DNA using the rcorr function in the Hmisc package (48). To assess associations between in-season alpha diversity and P. nicotianae, we fit linear regressions in SAS (PROC REG) after log-transforming the pathogen variable as log(y + 1) to address distributional skewness. Similarly, a machine learning approach was employed to examine relationships between fungal and bacterial OTUs and P. nicotianae DNA concentrations under both untransformed and log-transformed scales. The OTU data were standardized using StandardScaler to ensure comparable feature scales across all measurements.

RESULTS

Temporal P. nicotianae dynamics

Pre-planting P. nicotianae DNA concentrations in soil differed among the three fields, with Barnes showing the lowest amount (346.75 ± 569 fg/g of soil), followed by C&C (1.7 × 104 ± 2.0 × 104) and then Ferrel (5.3 × 104 ± 5.8 × 104) (PField × Sampling Time < 0.0001; Data S1A and B). The in-season P. nicotianae DNA concentrations remained generally modest but displayed field-specific peaks at different sampling times (Fig. 1A through C; P-valuesSampling Time < 0.0001; Data S1A). For example, P. nicotianae concentration was the highest at the third sampling time (T3; 2 months after planting) in Ferrel (1.9 × 105 ± 4.0 × 105 fg/g of soil; P < 0.0001; Data S1C) when visible black shank symptoms were observed (Fig. 1C and D). At C&C, pathogen concentrations remained at similar levels until 3 months after planting (T4; 2.7 × 105 ± 1.7 × 105 fg/g of soil; Fig. 1B; P < 0.0001; Data S1C), although plant mortality was found as early as T2 (1 month after planting; Fig. 1D). At Barnes, P. nicotianae DNA concentrations in soil began increasing from T2 and peaked at T4, reaching 7 × 103 ± 4.5 × 103 fg/g of soil (Fig. 1A; P < 0.0001; Data S1C). However, no visible symptoms were recorded at this location until T5 (Fig. 1D). Despite dynamic variations in P. nicotianae concentration across fields, tobacco mortality caused by black shank disease continued to increase, with a more rapid rise observed at C&C compared to Ferrel and Barnes (Fig. 1D). However, the AUDPC scores did not differ significantly (P = 0.8975) among C&C (6.5 ± 1.198), Ferrel (1.5 ± 0.768), and Barnes (0.33 ± 0.289).

Soil physicochemical factors

Of the 17 soil physicochemical parameters, 10 properties significantly correlated with pre-planting P. nicotianae DNA concentration (Table 2; Source Data S7). Organic matter, magnesium, calcium, the amount of nitrogen within the nitrate ion (NO3−–N), iron, manganese, and the percentage of clay and silt had a significant positive correlation with pre-planting P. nicotianae DNA concentrations, while sodium and the percentage of sand were negatively correlated with P. nicotianae concentrations. Several of the soil physicochemical variables were also strongly correlated with one another. For example, organic matter, magnesium, calcium, nitrate-N, manganese, silt, and clay were positively correlated with one another, whereas sand was negatively correlated with many of the measured variables. However, these soil physicochemical associations did not fully explain field-level disease outcomes because Ferrel had the highest pre-planting P. nicotianae DNA concentration but lower black shank pressure than C&C.

Microbial diversity and community composition associated with P. nicotianae and black shank pressure

To evaluate microbial features associated with field-level disease mortality and pathogen DNA concentration, we compared bacterial and fungal alpha diversity, community composition, and their correlations with P. nicotianae DNA concentrations. Rarefied richness and Shannon index of bacterial OTUs during the crop season were significantly higher in Barnes, followed by Ferrel, and then C&C (Fig. 2A and B; rarefied richness: PBarnes vs. C&C < 0.0001; PBarnes vs. Ferrel < 0.0001; PC&C vs. Ferrel = 0.3362; Shannon: PBarnes vs. C&C < 0.0001; PBarnes vs. Ferrel < 0.0001; PC&C vs. Ferrel = 0.0011). In contrast, fungal diversity showed a different pattern, with higher rarefied richness and Shannon diversity observed in Ferrel and Barnes compared with C&C at most sampling times (Fig. 2D and E; rarefied richness: PBarnes vs. C&C < 0.0001; PBarnes vs. Ferrel = 0.0217; PC&C vs. Ferrel < 0.0001; Shannon: PBarnes vs. C&C < 0.0001; PBarnes vs. Ferrel = 0.9962; PC&C vs. Ferrel < 0.0001). C&C, where the highest black shank disease mortality was recorded, had the lowest bacterial and fungal richness and Shannon diversity (Fig. 2A, B, D, and E). Across all fields, bacterial and fungal richness and Shannon diversity generally declined over the sampling period (P-valuesSampling Time < 0.01), although a transient increase in Shannon diversity was observed at the pre-harvest sampling point in Barnes. After log-transformation of P. nicotianae DNA concentrations, bacterial rarefied richness and Shannon diversity showed significant linear relationships with P. nicotianae concentrations (P-values < 0.05; Fig. 3A and B). Fungal Shannon diversity was also negatively associated with pathogen concentration (P = 0.0497; Fig. 3D), whereas fungal rarefied richness was not significantly correlated with pathogen abundance (P = 0.2939; Fig. 3C).

Microbial community composition differed significantly among fields based on Bray–Curtis dissimilarity (PERMANOVA: Pbacteria = 0.001; Pfungi = 0.001; Fig. 2C and F). In contrast, samples from different sampling times within the same field showed limited separation in NMDS ordination, and sampling time alone did not significantly explain community composition (Fig. 2; P > 0.05). However, significant field-by-sampling-time interactions were detected for both bacterial and fungal communities (P = 0.001), indicating that temporal community dynamics differed among fields.

The XGBoost regression models based on OTU profiles captured variation associated with P. nicotianae DNA concentrations, although model performance varied among train–test splits (Fig. S1). The fungal OTU-based model (Fig. S1A1 and A3) showed stronger predictive potential than the bacterial model, suggesting that fungal community composition may provide greater information for estimating P. nicotianae abundance in tobacco fields. However, model performance was less reliable for samples with low pathogen DNA concentrations (Fig. S1A2 and A4, Fig. S1B2 and B4).

Field- and season-specific shifts in soil bacterial and fungal community composition

The composition and relative abundance of dominant bacterial and fungal phyla were not significantly different among fields (Fig. S2; P-values > 0.05). Greater variation was observed at the genus level. Among the dominant bacterial genera, several groups commonly linked to plant–microbe interactions or biocontrol potential, including Sphingomonas, Nocardioides, Pseudarthrobacter, and Bacillus (Fig. 4A; Data S2 and S3), differed significantly among fields (P < 0.05; Table 3). Similarly, fungal community composition at the genus level varied (Table 3; P < 0.05), with Trichoderma, Fusarium, and Globodera showing field-specific patterns relevant to black shank disease mortality (Fig. 4C; Data S2 and S3).

DISCUSSION

Black shank development reflects interactions among pathogen abundance, soil physicochemical properties, and microbial communities

In-season P. nicotianae DNA concentrations varied strongly over time and peaked at different sampling points. However, these dynamics did not correspond consistently with tobacco mortality and AUDPC scores, indicating that black shank development in field settings is not determined by pathogen DNA abundance alone. Rather, it is likely shaped by interactions among pathogen presence, host condition, soil physicochemical properties, weather, and microbial communities (49–53). The positive associations between P. nicotianae DNA concentrations and organic matter, calcium, iron, magnesium, manganese, nitrate-N, and percentages of silt and clay suggest that pathogen DNA was greater in soils with higher nutrient availability and finer-textured fractions. Finer-textured soils generally have greater water-holding capacity and cation retention than sandier soils, which may create conditions that support pathogen persistence or detectable pathogen DNA under favorable moisture conditions (54). Although the sand percentage was negatively associated with P. nicotianae DNA concentration in this study, soil texture effects on pathogen movement are likely context-dependent (55).

Soil microbial alpha diversities differed significantly among fields and declined throughout the cropping season, with a non-significant late-season rebound in Barnes, indicating that higher pathogen loads or associated disease pressure coincide with reduced microbial alpha diversity and that these relationships may reflect enrichments in specific microbial groups that respond to disease-affected roots, including taxa within Proteobacteria and Actinobacteria (56–58). In contrast, microbial community composition was primarily structured by field, as supported by both NMDS ordinations and PERMANOVA tests. Although within-field differences among sampling times were not visually distinct, PERMANOVA detected a significant location and sampling time interaction for both bacterial and fungal communities, indicating that seasonal changes in community composition were field dependent. The association between microbial community composition and P. nicotianae DNA concentration further suggests that bacterial and fungal OTU profiles may contain pathogen-associated signals in field soils. Overall, these results suggest that each field maintained a distinct microbial community structure shaped largely by local soil characteristics, while still undergoing field-specific seasonal shifts that may have been influenced in part by pathogen activity and disease occurrence.

Field-specific microbial taxa show associations with black shank pressure and P. nicotianae abundance

Several bacterial genera commonly linked to plant–microbe interactions and biocontrol potential, including Sphingomonas, Nocardioides, Pseudarthrobacter, Bacillus, and Streptomyces, showed significant differences among fields and, for a subset of taxa, at specific sampling timepoints. The abundance of Trichoderma also varied dramatically among fields, with the highest levels observed at C&C, the field with the highest black shank mortality. This difference may be attributed, in part, to soil physicochemical properties, such as the more neutral pH at C&C, that favor Trichoderma establishment (59). Within C&C, Fusarium increased from T3 to T4 alongside P. nicotianae concentrations and showed a significant positive association over that interval, whereas the corresponding association for Globodera was not significant. Because Fusarium includes important tobacco root pathogens (e.g., Fusarium oxysporum, F. solani, and F. annulatum) (60), and Globodera comprises plant-parasitic nematodes (61), the co-variation observed for Fusarium during peak P. nicotianae abundance is notable. However, metagenomic sequencing and targeted isolation will be needed to classify the taxa involved and test mechanisms underlying their co-occurrence with P. nicotianae under high disease pressure.

Microbial network complexity does not necessarily indicate disease suppression

Network analysis further highlighted field-specific differences in microbial co-occurrence structure associated with P. nicotianae. Previous studies suggested that complex and highly connected soil microbial networks are often associated with disease-suppressive soils (13, 62). However, using a stringent SparCC threshold (|r| ≥ 0.7, P < 0.01), bacterial networks were most complex at C&C and least complex at Barnes, contrary to our second hypothesis. This result indicates that greater microbial network complexity does not necessarily correspond to lower disease pressure and, in this system, may instead reflect shifts in bacterial co-occurrence associated with disease-affected roots. In Barnes, where disease pressure was low, bacterial networks showed lower overall complexity but a higher number of major modules, consistent with a more compartmentalized community structure that may be more resilient to disturbance (16, 62).

Microbial taxa negatively associated with P. nicotianae represent candidates for functional validation

Many Firmicutes, especially Bacillus and Paenibacillus, are known to produce antimicrobial metabolites, lytic enzymes, and compounds that can induce host defenses (63–65). Their exclusive occurrence among OTUs negatively associated with P. nicotianae in the lower-disease fields (Barnes and Ferrel) is consistent with a potential role for Firmicutes in pathogen suppression or competitive exclusion. Similarly, the greater number of Proteobacteria negatively associated with P. nicotianae in Barnes and Ferrel suggests that competitive interactions and biofilm formation may be relevant to pathogen-associated community structure (66, 67). Ferrel also harbored unique Actinobacteriota OTUs that were negatively associated with P. nicotianae, which may be relevant because many Actinobacteria include taxa capable of producing secondary metabolites frequently implicated in disease-suppressive soils (68). Although SparCC-based networks can identify field-specific associations in compositional microbiome data, they do not demonstrate direct microbial interactions, antagonism, or causal effects on disease development. Therefore, taxa negatively associated with P. nicotianae should be considered microbial candidates for future isolation, genomic, metabolomic, and functional assays rather than confirmed suppressive organisms.

In this study, we included replicated plots within each field and repeated sampling in the growing season, which allowed us to detect field-specific microbial and pathogen-associated patterns. However, repeating the same field experiment across consecutive years was not feasible because cooperating growers rotate tobacco with other crops, limiting access to the same tobacco fields and disease conditions in subsequent years. Therefore, future studies across additional tobacco production seasons and sites could further evaluate the broader applicability of the microbial patterns identified here.

Conclusions

This study improves our understanding of how soil physicochemical properties and microbial community structure are associated with P. nicotianae abundance and black shank disease mortality in flue-cured tobacco fields. Notably, the field with the lowest disease pressure had higher microbial alpha diversity, the simplest bacterial network, and a moderately complex fungal network, whereas the field with the highest black shank mortality had the lowest alpha-diversity, the most complex bacterial network, and the least complex fungal network. These field-level patterns suggest that microbial community features co-vary with disease pressure in ways that are not explained by pathogen inoculum levels alone. More broadly, our study suggests that black shank risk assessment may benefit from integrating pathogen detection with soil physicochemical and microbiome information, including candidate microbial indicators negatively associated with P. nicotianae. Future studies across additional fields, seasons, and production systems will be needed to test the broader applicability of these soil and biological indicators and their relevance to microbiome-informed disease management.
