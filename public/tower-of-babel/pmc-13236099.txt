Health care through an Indigenous lens: insights from a university hospital photovoice study
Authors: Sahar Fazeli, Ka Man Ho, Claudia Mitchell, Romina Pace
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackground:Indigenous Peoples in Canada continue to experience profound health inequities rooted in colonialism, systemic racism, and structural barriers in health care systems. We sought to examine the experiences of Eeyou/Eenou patients receiving care and people supporting a patient at a university-affiliated hospital centre in Quebec, to inform culturally safe and equitable care delivery.Methods:We employed a participatory qualitative design, using photovoice and semistructured interviews co-developed with the Cree Board of Health and Social Services of James Bay. Patients and support people contributed photographs, captions, and interviews between September 2023 and March 2024. We analyzed data using thematic and content analysis, following principles of relational accountability and reflexivity. We shared results with participants to confirm accuracy and meaning.Results:Twenty patients and 11 support people participated. Participants generated 102 images and narratives reflecting 3 interrelated themes: barriers to care — racialized neglect, poor communication, isolation, financial strain, and delays in care; healing experiences — respectful, empathetic care, and connection to culture, family, and community; and pathways for improvement — increased access to interpreters, Elders, spiritual services, and culturally inclusive hospital spaces.Interpretation:We found that anti-Indigenous racism and systemic inequities persist in an academic hospital. Yet, moments of relational and culturally grounded care foster trust and healing, demonstrating that transformation is possible. Embedding Indigenous knowledge systems, family involvement, and community partnership within hospital structures can advance reconciliation in practice and fundamentally transform patient care experiences across health care systems.

In Canada, Indigenous Peoples experience substantial health disparities compared with the non-Indigenous population.1 Colonialism continues to shape barriers to care, including fragmented services, poor coordination between provincially funded hospitals and federal nursing care, and high turnover of health care staff.1,2 Institutions create additional barriers through complex policies, language limitations, funding restrictions, and requirements to travel outside local communities.2

Beyond structural barriers, Indigenous Peoples in Canada face pervasive discrimination in health care settings, exacerbating challenges in accessing quality care. A 2024 Statistics Canada report found that 24% of off-reserve First Nations people, 23% of Inuit, and 18% of Métis people faced racism or discrimination from health care professionals in the past year, with off-reserve First Nations and Métis women reporting rates nearly double those of men.3 Indigenous patients often avoid health care facilities because of past negative experiences of dismissive and substandard care.4,5 Studies indicate that some health care professionals stereotype Indigenous patients as lazy, homeless, or drug-seeking, often attributing their illnesses to alcohol or drug use without proper assessment.6

University-affiliated teaching hospitals serve as tertiary care centres for remote Indigenous communities, positioning them as sites where medical training, institutional authority, and care standards are established and disseminated. Understanding the experiences of Indigenous patients in these settings can illuminate how systemic racism operates within high-resource institutional contexts and what improvement is needed. Inequities in access and treatment for Indigenous Peoples are well documented, 1–3,7 yet most studies have relied on conventional interviews or surveys that often fail to capture the emotional, relational, and cultural dimensions central to Indigenous patients’ experiences of care.8,9 Photovoice offers a culturally appropriate alternative that amplifies participant voices and enables authentic expression of experiences that traditional methods cannot adequately capture.10 In this study, we sought to create a space where Eeyou patients and their support people could express their hospital experiences in ways that align with Indigenous traditions of storytelling and visual expression.11–14 By centring participants’ own images and narratives, we sought to document both barriers and moments of healing, offering concrete, community-informed pathways for enhancing cultural safety within hospital care.

Methods

Participants and study setting

Participants in this study were from Eeyou Istchee, a vast subarctic territory in northern Quebec comprising 11 Eeyou/Eenou (Cree) communities and about 21 000 residents. The Eeyou Istchee spans more than 450 000 km2 along the eastern coast of James Bay and inland regions. People living in Eeyou Istchee have deep relationships with the land, language, and traditional practices, such as hunting, fishing, and trapping. These ties shape a worldview in which health is relational — grounded in balance among people, community, and the natural environment.

Although local health services exist in each community, residents requiring specialized or tertiary care are referred to southern hospitals, most often the McGill University Health Centre (MUHC) in Montréal. The MUHC is one of Quebec’s largest academic health networks and a major referral centre for northern and Indigenous patients. As a tertiary teaching hospital affiliated with McGill University, it provides advanced diagnostics, surgical interventions, and specialty services not available in northern communities. The MUHC has established commitments to equity, diversity, and Indigenous health.

For Eeyou patients and support people, receiving care at the MUHC involves navigating an unfamiliar, urban, and predominantly non-Indigenous health care environment. The long distances — ranging from 795 km for Mistissini to more than 1300 km for Whapmagoostui15,16 — mean that journeys often involve dislocation from family, community, and land, which can amplify feelings of vulnerability and cultural isolation.

Study design

We employed a participatory qualitative design, using photovoice17 and semistructured interviews developed in collaboration with the Cree Board of Health and Social Services of James Bay (CBHSSJB) Research Advisory Committee (RAC), which includes Elders and community members. The RAC specifically requested photovoice to help Eeyou patients document their experiences at the MUHC. Photovoice is a participatory visual methodology that uses photography to help participants document, reflect on, and communicate critical issues in their lives. This method empowers marginalized populations to convey experiences and challenges that may be difficult to express verbally, offering new perspectives often overlooked.11 Particularly relevant for Indigenous Peoples, photovoice builds on storytelling traditions, strengthens community connections, and integrates Indigenous Ways of Knowing into research.12,18,19

This study was grounded in Indigenous research methodologies that prioritize relational knowledge embedded within interconnected worldviews in which humans are inseparable from land, community, and ancestors.13,14 For Eeyou peoples, this relational interconnection — characterized by balance and harmony within all relations rather than individual biological function — shapes how health and illness are understood and experienced. Photovoice privileges participant voice and symbolic expression, aligning with Eeyou storytelling traditions and enabling collective meaning-making rather than data extraction. These epistemological foundations — emphasizing reciprocity and accountability to community — guided all methods and analyses.

Recruitment

We used purposive sampling to recruit adult patients from Eeyou Istchee who received hospital care within the year before enrolment (September 2023 to March 2024) and were either currently hospitalized or attending outpatient follow-up in Montréal. Some photographs were taken during hospital admissions, and others were captured in outpatient and community settings. We recruited participants either during specialized inpatient or outpatient care at the MUHC or during the postdischarge recovery period in Montréal. The postdischarge recovery period referred to the time when patients remained in Montréal to attend follow-up outpatient medical appointments before returning to their home communities, while residing in dedicated accommodations provided by the CBHSSJB or in private residences of their choosing (e.g., with friends or family). We also invited support people (family, friends, or community members) who had accompanied patients during stays in Montréal. A CBHSSJB nurse (K.M.H.) facilitated recruitment through bilingual posters at CBHSSJB accommodations in Montréal and at hospital sites. Participants received honoraria for each research activity to recognize and respect their time and expertise.

Photovoice workshops and interviews

We obtained informed consent for photovoice workshops and interviews, including permission to share and publish photographs and accompanying narratives for purposes of research dissemination. The photovoice methodology guided participants through 6 sequential steps: learning the method, capturing photos, reflecting in groups, crafting narratives, selecting images, and sharing findings with the community (Appendix 1, Figure A1, available at www.cmaj.ca/lookup/doi/10.1503/cmaj.250777/tab-related-content). Author K.M.H. and a Cree-speaking facilitator coordinated in-person workshops at CBHSSJB Montréal accommodations, with instruction on visual ethics,20 photovoice principles, and photography basics. Participants used either their personal cellphones or tablets supplied by the research team to capture images. Participants were asked to “Take pictures that show your struggles and challenges as an Indigenous patient at the MUHC” and “Take pictures that show the solutions for these issues.”

We conducted semistructured interviews in person or by telephone, at participants’ preference, to explore hospital experiences in greater depth. We developed 6 guiding questions with the CBHSSJB RAC. The guiding questions constituted the core interview framework, and the interviewers used probing prompts flexibly to encourage elaboration, clarify responses, and deepen discussion as appropriate (Appendix 2, Supplementary Table 1, available at www.cmaj.ca/lookup/doi/10.1503/cmaj.250777/tab-related-content). All sessions were audio-recorded and transcribed with participants’ consent.

Data analysis

We combined thematic analysis with visual–verbal content analysis. 21 We used thematic analysis to identify recurring experiences and meanings in participants’ narratives through systematic coding and categorization. Concurrently, we used visual–verbal content analysis to examine visual elements (e.g., composition, symbolism, subjects, foreground and background) alongside participant captions and interview text, enabling integrated interpretation of how participants visually and verbally communicated meaning about their experiences during hospital admission. Two non-Indigenous researchers (S.F. and R.P.) with training in participatory visual methods and Indigenous methodologies independently coded all data through iterative rounds of open coding and thematic analysis. We treated participant captions as primary data, honouring participants’ own interpretations of experiences during hospital admission. We resolved discrepancies through collaborative discussion until team consensus was reached. Interpretive choices were guided by reflexive dialogue between researchers and in ongoing consultation with the CBHSSJB RAC, which helped identify potential misinterpretations and ensured culturally grounded findings. Final validation workshops — conducted in person or via video conference — allowed participants to review and refine emerging interpretations, directly informing the final analysis.

Team composition and reflexivity

The research team consisted of non-Indigenous clinicians and researchers working in long-term partnership with Eeyou/Eenou patients, support people, and the CBHSSJB. Author K.M.H., a nurse who has lived and worked in Eeyou Istchee for more than 15 years, and author R.P., a physician who has consulted in Eeyou Istchee for more than 10 years, contributed clinical expertise and long-standing relationships with the communities, while author S.F. led the photovoice design and visual–verbal analysis in collaboration with author C.M., an expert in participatory visual and arts-based methodologies with extensive experience working alongside Indigenous communities. Aware of their positions within colonial health and academic institutions, the authors engaged in ongoing reflexive practice, considering how their social locations, training, and institutional roles influenced study design and interpretation. Dialogue with Eeyou/Eenou participants and the CBHSSJB RAC, including participant review of emerging themes, helped identify potential misinterpretations and supported an analysis that remained accountable to Eeyou/Eenou experiences, priorities, and understandings of health.

Ethics approval and Indigenous research ethics

Ethics approval was received from the MUHC Research Ethics Board. The CBHSSJB RAC maintained governance over research design, data interpretation, and dissemination. Participants retained ownership of their photographs and transcripts, and the community controlled access to all data through the RAC. Findings were returned through community exhibitions and workshops co-designed with participants. This approach ensured adherence to OCAP principles (ownership, control, access, possession) and positioned the Eeyou community as both researcher and primary beneficiary.

Results

Participants

Twenty patients (10 females, 10 males; aged 40 to 77 yr) and 11 support people (8 females, 3 males; aged 18 to 68 yr) participated. Patients from Waskaganish (12), Chisasibi (5), Mistissini (2), and Whapmagoostui (1) were admitted to hospital or were recovering from various chronic and acute conditions, with stays in Montréal ranging from days to years (Table 1). Longer stays, extending beyond 1 year, were primarily related to ongoing treatment needs, such as hemodialysis and other long-term specialist care. Participants submitted 3 to 5 photos each, totaling 102 images with captions. All participant captions are reproduced as originally written, preserving participants’ voices and their interpretation of their photos.

Themes

In our analysis of photos, captions, and interviews, we identified 3 primary themes — barriers to care, healing experiences, and pathways for improvements — with several subthemes.

Barriers to care

Racialized neglect

Participants reported being dismissed or stereotyped on the basis of their Indigenous identity (Table 2). Photographs documented physical isolation — patients positioned in corners or away from nursing stations — and unequal treatment, where Indigenous patients from remote communities were overlooked while those with hospital connections received attentive care (Figure 1 and Appendix 1, Figure A2). One patient reported feeling that they had been given a label automatically before they had spoken a word because of their Cree identity (Table 2, participant X). Pain expressions were dismissed — participants told they were “pretending” or a “crybaby” — framed by patients as colonial stereotypes (Table 2, participant H). Neglect also extended to medical preferences, as a participant recounted stating an intention to get some natural medicines, to which a health care worker responded with a raised voice to dismiss them (Table 2, participant Q).

Poor communication

Participants described rushed consultations with complex medical terminology, sometimes leaving them unclear about their diagnoses or lacking proper explanation (Table 2). One stated, “They talk so fast — I don’t even know what they said I have.” Another highlighted the anxiety created by insufficient information: “Doctors should be more informative … give the patient as much information as possible so they are not worried about what’s going to happen.” The MUHC operates bilingually in French and English but lacks dedicated Cree interpretation services, requiring patients from Eeyou Istchee — where Cree is the preferred language — to rely on family or support people for translation.22 This language barrier prevented participants from asking clarifying questions; many felt intimidated requesting explanation when unable to fully understand (Table 2).

Isolation

Many patients from Eeyou Istchee experienced simultaneous abandonment: they were separated from family and disconnected from cultural land and practices (Table 2). Photographs documented the emotional toll of this separation. A long hospital hallway showed a granddaughter supported by a community member during a critical moment — a stand-in for absent family back home (Appendix 1, Figure A3). Another patient expressed existential longing captured in an image of a tea kettle on a burning fire, symbolizing community and camp life left behind (Figure 2). A third image contrasted the cold urban setting with distant home, representing the cultural disconnection experienced during extended separation (Appendix 1, Figure A4).

“Homesick. Stranded in the city for 7 years, I often think about when my turn will come for me to return home to be with my family. I don’t want to return home in a ‘box.’ I want to be able to spend time with my family when I am still alive.”

Financial burdens

Participants reported financial hardship from prolonged medical stays in Montréal. One participant reported having to sell belongings to pay their bills (Table 2, participant AA). Participants reported how a prolonged medical stay threatens their housing and financial stability, creating stress that undermines healing (Table 2). A photograph showed a security uniform hanging in a closet with the caption, “Extra Income; My uniform, I start tomorrow at 7 am. It’s hard being a patient in Montréal with no income” (Appendix 1, Appendix Figure A5).

Delays in care

Prolonged waits compounded physical and psychological burden while extending separation from community supports, intensifying the cumulative strain of hospital admission far from home (Table 2). A photograph documented how excessive wait times generated anticipatory anxiety tied to disconnection (Appendix 1, Figure A6). Participants documented that, upon transfer, fragmented care systems left patients’ medical records inaccessible, requiring them to retell their medical histories and delaying treatment on arrival in Montréal (Appendix 1, Figure A7).

Healing experiences

Participants described moments of healing grounded in relational care, cultural continuity, and mutual respect (Table 2). When health care professionals engaged with patients about their cultural background and identity, it created profound healing. One participant reflected on how a nurse getting to know a patient helps ease a patient’s experience and make them feel more welcome (Table 2, participant L). A photograph (Appendix 1, Figure A8) of a surgical wound healing after brain surgery further illustrated how, when patients felt safe and cared for, physical and emotional recovery proceeded in tandem. Beyond these relationships, access to cultural and traditional practices provided additional support. Participants brought personal items from home — musical instruments, traditional foods, photographs of family — and described these as sources of “comfort,” “life,” and “healing” (Appendix 1, Figure A9 and Figure A10).

Pathways for improvements

Participants identified tangible strategies for improving hospital care centred on 3 key areas: enhanced communication, facilitated family and community presence, and integration of cultural and spiritual supports (Table 2). Access to interpreters would bridge language barriers, ensuring Eeyou patients fully understand their care. One participant emphasized the importance of having an interpreter to help patients understand what was being communicated (Table 2, participant I). Improved wayfinding and orientation support would help patients navigate large, unfamiliar hospital environments (Table 2). Greater flexibility in family visits could reduce loneliness and support healing, as exemplified by a photograph showing family members holding hands, highlighting the comfort these connections bring (Figure 3). Incorporating Eeyou cultural activities, access to traditional healers, and spiritual services like smokeless smudging and traditional foods would create a more welcoming and healing atmosphere (Table 2, participants R and Z).

“Accompanying my family after a big surgery. Family presence speeds up the recovery.”

Interpretation

We found that Eeyou patients’ descriptions of health care experiences within an academic health centre were of barriers to care, healing experiences, and pathways for improvement. Such a site is where medical training, institutional authority, and care standards are established and disseminated. Academic hospitals shape clinical practices that extend far beyond their walls, making their commitment to equitable, culturally safe care essential to systemic change. Through this study, participants documented barriers alongside protective relationships and concrete solutions for more equitable care by visualizing and interpreting institutional dynamics themselves, offering evidence that extends beyond conventional interviews to reveal patient expertise and actionable directions for change.

Our study highlights interconnected barriers. Participants documented racialized neglect through photographs of physical isolation and unequal treatment, rooted in colonial stereotypes that dismissed their pain and medical preferences. These interactions are not isolated incidents but reflect structural anti-Indigenous racism wherein institutional norms, implicit biases, and policies systematically disadvantage Indigenous patients.1,5,6,23,24 Notable cases, including the death of Joyce Echaquan, an Atikamekw woman who live-streamed racist abuse by hospital staff in Quebec, and the preventable death of Brian Sinclair in Winnipeg, underscore how racial bias can have fatal consequences.25,26 Moreover, communication barriers intensify these inequities. Institutional failures — including lack of dedicated Indigenous interpreters, use of complex medical terminology, and absence of cultural mediation — leave patients uncertain about diagnoses and treatment plans, compounding distrust and disengagement from care, particularly for chronic disease management.27–29 Disconnection deepens through isolation; hospital admission is particularly difficult for Indigenous patients, as relocation disrupts connection to family and home while requiring adaptation to unfamiliar, predominantly non-Indigenous environments with health care providers who often lack cultural awareness.30 Participants in this study documented profound loneliness through photographs depicting prolonged family separation — including a deathbed vigil and a cold cityscape — intensified by absence of culturally relevant supports, such as familiar foods, spiritual practices, and community connection. For Eeyou peoples, whose worldview grounds health in relational balance, this separation disconnected patients from sources of healing. Financial burdens and delays in care compounded these barriers for participants. Although many patients experience these challenges, Indigenous patients from remote communities in this study faced disproportionate burdens, such as extended stays away from home without income, forcing some to work while receiving care or to sell belongings. These financial burdens forced participants to navigate the competing priorities of healing from illness while managing household obligations from afar. Cumulative delays during transfer from local health centres to academic health care centre, and extended emergency department waits meant patients faced compounded delays and disruptions in care continuity that heightened distress and potentially worsened health outcomes.31,32 These cascading barriers — rooted in geographic remoteness, financial strain, and structural inequities — systematically disadvantage Indigenous patients. Addressing such harms requires systemic accountability, including mandatory antiracism education, Indigenous-led policy oversight, and mechanisms for patients to report discrimination.24

Despite these challenges, patients in our study found that healing occurred when they felt genuinely heard and respected by health care workers, documenting evidence of good care through photographs depicting physical recovery and well-being, illustrating how respectful care enables tangible healing. These findings echo those of Pilarinos and colleagues, 5 who found that respectful, patient-centred communication fosters trust among Indigenous patients, and of Hole and colleagues33 and Clifford and colleagues,34 who identified cultural safety as essential to positive health experiences. Our results extend this literature by showing how visual and participatory methods can illuminate the emotional texture of healing — revealing that recovery involves not only physical treatment but also affirmation of belonging, dignity, and cultural identity. Healing was not merely the absence of illness but a re-establishment of balance between body, mind, and community. These moments demonstrate that cultural safety, when genuinely practised, can serve as a protective determinant of health, countering alienation and fostering resilience. As participants conveyed through both their words and images, being treated with respect and understanding allowed them to “feel safe.”

Participants identified interconnected strategies for culturally safe care grounded in Indigenous worldviews and relational accountability. Trained interpreters and patient navigators were identified as essential to bridging communication gaps and fostering mutual respect — they improve comprehension while acting as cultural brokers to enhance trust and empathy between Indigenous patients and health care providers.35 Participants also emphasized that flexibility in visiting policies and accommodating family and community supports were vital to well-being, particularly given that extended hospital stays often meant months of separation from loved ones, underscoring how relational dimensions of care affirm connection, belonging, and continuity between home and hospital.33,34 The integration of Elders, traditional healers, and cultural activities within hospital settings are shown to improve mental health outcomes, foster holistic healing, and rebuild trust between Indigenous communities and health care institutions.36–38 Access to traditional foods and dedicated community gathering spaces for prayer and mutual support became sources of healing for participants in our study, exemplifying the transformative power of cultural continuity and spiritual community within institutional settings. These findings echo broader calls in Indigenous health research to embed cultural and spiritual supports within mainstream care, not as add-ons but as expressions of equity and respect.39,40 Together, our findings reflect participants’ collective vision: culturally safe care requires institutions to move beyond individual cultural “competence” toward structural change and shared accountability.

Limitations

This study’s findings reflect situated knowledge of one Indigenous community and a single hospital centre, limiting generalizability; however, they offer deep contextual understanding and emphasize relational trust, which is a generalizable concept.41 Photovoice’s design involves self-selection bias — participants willing to engage in creative visual documentation and group discussion may differ systematically from those who declined, potentially skewing findings toward more vocal or visually expressive community members. As non-Indigenous researchers, the lead analysts’ interpretations were inevitably shaped by their standpoint outside Indigenous communities, although partnership with the CBHSSJB RAC and a CBHSSJB nurse facilitator strengthened cultural safety and community trust.

Conclusion

Through photovoice, Eeyou patients documented systemic harms alongside resilience and cultural strength. Their reflections — that access to interpreters, Elders, traditional healers, and family promote healing — suggest Indigenous sovereignty and self-determination are required rather than mere service improvements. Decolonizing health care demands relational accountability and Indigenous decision-making authority, moving beyond cultural competence frameworks to fundamental institutional transformation aligned with Indigenous worldviews and rights.
