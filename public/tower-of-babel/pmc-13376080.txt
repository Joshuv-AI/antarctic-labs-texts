Obesity-induced oleic acid metabolic dysregulation may exacerbate osteoarthritis through the degradation of SOX9
Authors: Haoke Li, Jiawei Fu, Juan Liu, Bokai Zhang, Chenhui Cai, Zaoqing Zhang, Tianying Ma, Xu Hu, Yang Zhang, Peifang Yan, Chengcheng Wang, Rui Zuo, Chao Zhang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractObesity-related osteoarthritis (OA) is a degenerative joint disease accompanied by metabolic disorders. However, the specific metabolites and their molecular mechanisms remain unclear. Here, we establish a mouse model of obesity by feeding a high-fat diet (HFD) and find that HFD feeding promotes articular cartilage degeneration and accelerates OA progression. By using the untargeted metabolomics analysis, we identify oleic acid (OLA), a common monounsaturated fatty acid, as a significantly upregulated metabolite accumulating in articular cartilage. We further show that OLA disrupts extracellular matrix (ECM) homeostasis and promotes ubiquitin-mediated degradation of the key upstream transcription factor sex-determining region Y-type high-mobility group box protein 9 (SOX9) in murine chondrocytes and human cartilage explants. RNA-sequencing and further immunofluorescence staining reveal aberrant activation of Ca2+/calmodulin-dependent protein kinase 2 (CaMK2) in the articular cartilage of obese mice. Furthermore, we find that the activation of CaMK2 is induced by OLA, leading to ubiquitin-mediated degradation of SOX9 and subsequent ECM disruption in chondrocytes. Notably, OLA-induced ECM disruption and SOX9 degradation are effectively reversed by a CaMK2 inhibitor. These findings suggest that an OLA-CaMK2-SOX9 molecular axis may be involved in the pathogenesis of obesity-related OA and that CaMK2 could be a potential therapeutic target.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1007/s00018-026-06261-7.Keywords: Osteoarthritis, SOX9, Oleic acid, Obesity, CaMK2

Introduction

Osteoarthritis (OA) is the most common degenerative joint disease, characterized by articular cartilage degradation, subchondral bone remodeling and synovial inflammation [1–3], which primarily affects weight-bearing joints such as the knees and hips [4]. The primary pathological mechanisms of OA include inflammation [5], chondrocyte dysfunction, extracellular matrix (ECM) degradation, and an imbalance in catabolism and anabolism within the joint [6]. At present, there is no effective targeted drug for OA [7], and the current treatment strategy still focuses on pain relief and symptom control [8]. The structural integrity of articular cartilage depends on ECM homeostasis [9]. The disruption of ECM homeostasis leads to the development of OA, characterized by the upregulation of catabolic enzymes such as matrix metalloproteinase 13 (MMP13) and a disintegrin and metalloproteinase with thrombospondin motifs (ADAMTS5), and the downregulation of anabolic matrix components, including aggrecan (ACAN) and type II collagen (COL2A1) [10]. As a critical transcription factor, SOX9 (sex-determining region Y-type high-mobility group box protein 9) plays a key role in maintaining cartilage ECM homeostasis by directly binding to the promoters or enhancers of its target genes, such as ACAN and COL2A1 [11–13]. Therefore, elucidating the regulatory mechanisms of SOX9 is essential for understanding the pathogenesis of OA.

Obesity is one of the major risk factors for the pathogenesis and progression of OA [14, 15]. In recent years, the global prevalence of obesity-related OA has been rising steadily [16]. Although this risk is partly attributable to the elevated mechanical load on weight-bearing joints, growing evidence indicates that mechanical stress alone cannot fully explain the link, as non-weight-bearing joints are also frequently affected in obese individuals [17]. Current perspectives suggest that obesity represents a cluster of systemic metabolic disorders, characterized by significantly altered levels of lipid metabolites in obese individuals. These metabolites may directly contribute to the pathogenesis of OA [18, 19]. For instance, it has been demonstrated that a high-cholesterol diet promotes cartilage degeneration and OA in mice through the downregulation of LRP3 [20]. Moreover, high-fat-diet (HFD) -induced obesity accelerates OA progression via the p53-FOXO3 pathway [21]. Studies on serum and cartilage samples from OA patients have revealed significant associations between the disease and various lipid metabolites, such as fatty acids (FAs) [22, 23]. Furthermore, research has demonstrated that FAs accumulate in OA cartilage preceding the appearance of histological changes, suggesting that FAs may play a driving role in OA pathogenesis [24, 25]. However, the specific metabolites and their pathogenic mechanisms in obesity-induced OA are poorly understood.

Fatty acids (FAs) are classified into saturated fatty acids (SFAs), monounsaturated fatty acids (MUFAs), and polyunsaturated fatty acids (PUFAs) based on the length of their carbon chains and the number of double bonds [23, 26]. Oleic acid (OLA) is a common monounsaturated fatty acid that can be synthesized endogenously [27, 28]. A study utilizing time of flight secondary ion mass spectrometry (TOF-SIMS) revealed a higher concentration of OLA in human OA cartilage compared to normal cartilage, suggesting a potential association between OLA and the pathogenesis of OA [29]. However, the role of OLA in OA remains unclear [23, 30]. In this study, we established a high-fat diet (HFD)-induced obese mouse model and found that HFD accelerates OA progression. To investigate the specific metabolites involved, we performed metabolomics and identified a significant accumulation of OLA in the articular cartilage of HFD-fed mice. We further showed that OLA disrupted extracellular matrix (ECM) homeostasis and promoted ubiquitin-mediated degradation of the key upstream transcription factor SOX9 in murine chondrocytes and human cartilage explants. Subsequent RNA-sequencing analysis revealed that OLA activates calcium/calmodulin-dependent protein kinase 2 (CaMK2), triggering the ubiquitin-mediated degradation of the key transcription factor SOX9. This cascade ultimately disrupts ECM homeostasis and accelerates OA progression. Our study confirms the existence of an OLA-CaMK2-SOX9 regulatory axis, which may be involved in the pathogenesis of obesity-related OA and provides a potential molecular target for its treatment.

Materials and methods

Animal model

In this study, all animal experimental procedures were approved by the Experimental Animal Ethics Committee of the Army Medical University (Approval Number: AMUWEC20255396). C57BL/6 mice were acquired from Hunan SJA Laboratory Animal Co., Ltd. and maintained in a specific pathogen-free (SPF) environment. All animal procedures were conducted under a 12-h light/dark cycle at approximately 21 °C, with food and water provided ad libitum. To investigate the impact of high-fat diet (HFD)-induced obesity on osteoarthritis, 4-week male mice were fed either a HFD containing 60 kcal% fat (XTHF60, Xietong Pharmaceutical Bio-Engineering Co., Ltd., China) or a standard diet (SD) for 12 weeks. Subsequently, osteoarthritis was induced by anterior cruciate ligament transection (ACLT), while the control group received sham surgery. Briefly, under general anesthesia, a medial arthrotomy was performed followed by transection of the anterior cruciate ligament to create joint instability. Sham-operated animals underwent the identical surgical procedure but without ACLT. The knee joints were collected for analysis six weeks after surgery.

In this study, A total of 40 mice were included in this study, divided equally between the SD and HFD groups (n = 20 per group). For each group, 12 mice were allocated for histological analysis (6 subjected to sham surgery and 6 to ACLT surgery), 4 mice were used for metabolomics, and 4 mice were used for transcriptome sequencing.

Metabolomics analysis

The mice were euthanized for cartilage collection after 12 weeks of HFD or SD feeding. Articular cartilage was harvested from the knee joints according to an established protocol [31]. In brief, the hindlimbs were dislocated at the hip, rinsed with ice-cold PBS, and the articular cartilage of the tibia and femur was carefully dissected. Using a microscope, approximately 1 mm of cartilage tissue was trimmed from the joint surfaces, with all surrounding connective and bone tissues removed. The collected cartilage samples were immediately flash-frozen in liquid nitrogen and stored at − 80 °C for subsequent analysis.

We performed untargeted metabolomic analysis of mouse knee cartilage using the Metware Cloud Analysis Platform. Tissue samples from the HFD group (n = 4) and the SD group (n = 4) were homogenized after thawing. Subsequently, 400 µL of ice-cold methanol: water (7:3, v/v) containing an internal standard was added to 20 mg of the resulting powder. After vortexing and ice incubation, the extract was centrifuged (12,000 rpm, 10 min, 4 °C). The supernatant (300 µL) was held at -20 °C for 30 min, recentrifuged, and the final extract (200 µL) was analyzed by LC-MS. Chromatography used a Waters ACQUITY Premier HSS T3 column with a water/acetonitrile (both with 0.1% formic acid) gradient over 10 min at 0.4 mL/min and 40 °C. Mass spectrometry was performed in information-dependent acquisition (IDA) mode (Sciex Analyst TF 1.7.1) with an ESI source (TEM 550 °C; DP ± 60 V). Full mass scans (m/z 50–1000) were used to trigger data-dependent MS/MS scans (m/z 25–1000) with a collision energy of ± 30 V. Differential metabolites were defined by VIP > 1 (OPLS-DA model, MetaboAnalystR) and p < 0.05 (t-test), with model validation via 200 permutations.

RNA sequencing (RNA-seq) for the cartilage transcriptome

RNA sequencing was performed on cartilage specimens (HFD, n = 4; SD, n = 4) harvested as described above, using the Metware Cloud Analysis Platform. RNA from cartilage was extracted using CTAB (Sangon Biotech, Cat. No. A600108-0500) combined with PBIOZOL Reagent (Hangzhou Bioer, Cat. No. BSC55M1). The extracted RNA was dissolved in DEPC-treated water (Thermo Fisher, Cat. No. AM9920) and treated with DNase I (RNase-free, NEB, Cat. No. M0303S) to remove genomic DNA. RNA quantity was measured using a Qubit 4.0 Fluorometer (Thermo Fisher) with the Equalbit RNA BR Kit (Vazyme, Cat. No. Equations 212-02), and integrity (RQN) was assessed on a Qsep400 system (Guangding Biotech). PolyA-tailed mRNAs were enriched with VAHTS mRNA Capture Beads 2.0 (Vazyme, Cat. No. N403-C4), fragmented, and reverse-transcribed into first-strand cDNA. Strand-specific libraries were constructed through dUTP-based second-strand synthesis, followed by end repair, dA-tailing, and adapter ligation. The libraries (with insert sizes of 250–350 bp) were purified using VAHTS DNA Clean Beads (Vazyme, Cat. No. N411-03), PCR-amplified on a Bio-Rad T100 Thermal Cycler, and finally resuspended in nuclease-free water (Thermo Fisher, Cat. No. AM9930). Library quality control included concentration measurement (using Qubit with the Equalbit 1× dsDNA HS Kit, Vazyme, Cat. No. Equations 121-02) and fragment size analysis (on Qsep400). Qualified libraries were pooled and sequenced on an Illumina NovaSeq × Plus platform to generate 150 bp paired-end reads using sequencing-by-synthesis chemistry.

Primary cell isolation and culture

Primary murine chondrocytes were isolated from 5- to 6-day-old mice as previously described [32]. While this preparation contains a mixture of growth plate and articular chondrocytes, we refer to these cells as “epiphyseal chondrocytes” throughout the manuscript to accurately reflect their origin. Briefly, the cartilage was digested with 0.2% type II collagenase (C6885-5G, Sigma-Aldrich). After digestion, the cells were resuspended in high-glucose DMEM (GIBCO) medium containing 10% fetal bovine serum (HyClone) and 1% penicillin/streptomycin (SV30010, HyClone), and maintained at 37 °C in a humidified environment with 5% CO₂.

Clinical sample collection and preparation

All protocols were approved by the Ethics Committee of the Army Medical University (Approval Number: 2025-yd390-01). Human OA cartilage samples were obtained from a total of 3 patients diagnosed with OA and undergoing total knee arthroplasty (TKA): Patient 1 (male, 62 years, BMI 28.2), Patient 2 (male, 71 years, BMI 26.8), Patient 3 (male, 58 years, BMI 27.2). The excised lateral femoral condylar cartilage was collected. To minimize the impact of inter-individual heterogeneity, cartilage from each patient was evenly divided into explants of approximately 5 × 5 × 1 mm. Explants were cultured in DMEM medium containing either OLA (150 µM) or vehicle control, with 3 explants per condition. The culture medium was refreshed every 2–3 days. After 14 days, the specimens were collected, fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde, and processed for subsequent histological and immunohistochemical analyses.

Oleic acid (OLA) solutions preparation

Based on established protocols [33], free fatty acid (FFA) stock solutions were prepared by complexing free fatty acids with fatty acid-free bovine serum albumin (BSA). Specifically, oleic acid (ST2053,Beyotime, China) was first dissolved in 100 mM NaOH with heating to 70 °C to ensure complete solubilization. Then, this stock solution was diluted into a pre-warmed 20% fatty acid free BSA (ST025,Beyotime, China) solution to achieve the final OLA working concentration, while maintaining a final BSA concentration of 10% (w/v). Finally, the stock OLA solutions were filtered through a 0.22 μm filter and stored at 4 °C. The control solution containing identical concentrations of NaOH and BSA but without any lipids, was prepared using the procedure.

Small interfering RNA transfection

Cells were seeded in 6-well plates and cultured for 24 h to reach 80% confluency. Transfection was performed with CaMK2α siRNA (sense: CAGCGUUCAGUUAAUGGAA; antisense: UUCCAUUAACUGAACGCUG) or Luc siRNA (sense: CUUACGCUGAGUACUUCGA; antisense: UCGAAGUACUCAGCGUAAG) as the negative control using RNATransMate (E607402-0500, Sangon Biotech, China) according to the manufacturer’s protocol. Knockdown efficiency was confirmed by western blot analysis.

Histology, immunohistochemistry (IHC), and immunofluorescence (IF) assessment

Human cartilage specimens or whole murine knee joints were fixed in 4% neutral-buffered paraformaldehyde (Servicebio, China). The samples were then decalcified for 7 days in decalcification solution (Servicebio). Following dehydration through a graded ethanol series, the tissues were embedded in paraffin and sectioned into 5-μm-thick consecutive sections. For histological evaluation, sections were stained with Safranin O/Fast Green (G1371, Solarbio, China) and Toluidine Blue (G3668, Solarbio, China) to assess proteoglycan content and cartilage structure according to the manufacturers' instructions. All sections were evaluated using the Osteoarthritis Research Society International (OARSI) grading system by three independent observers to assess histopathological changes in osteoarthritic cartilage. The OARSI histopathology grading was performed in a blinded and randomized manner by three independent evaluators.

For immunostaining, the sections were incubated with 3% H₂O₂ for 15 min to inactivate endogenous peroxidase. Antigen retrieval was performed using sodium citrate buffer (P0083, Beyotime, China) at 95 °C for 15 min. After blocking with 5% BSA for 1 h at room-temperature, the sections were incubated with primary antibodies against collagen type II (ab34712, Abcam, 1:200), MMP13 (18165-1-AP, Proteintech, China, 1:200), SOX9 (AB5535, Merck, 1:200), and p-CaMK2 (T59748S, Abmart, China, 1:200) overnight at 4 °C. For IHC staining, after washing with PBS, the sections were incubated at room temperature for 30 min with HRP-conjugated secondary antibodies (G1301 and G1302, Servicebio). Then, color reaction was performed by the DAB kit (G1212, Servicebio) for 20 min. The integrated optical density (IOD) values of the stained proteins were quantified using ImageJ software, and the relative values were normalized to those of the control group. For IF staining, following primary antibody incubation and washing, sections were incubated with fluorescent secondary antibodies (AS053, AS060, Abclonal, China, 1:200) for 1 hour at room temperature. After three PBS washes, nuclei were stained with DAPI, and images were acquired using a fluorescence microscope. Fluorescence intensity was quantified with ImageJ and normalized to the control group.

Immunoblot analysis

Primary murine epiphyseal chondrocytes were cultured and treated with the CaMK2 inhibitor KN93 (10 μM; HY-15465, MedChemExpress) or activator DCP-LA (200 nM; HY-108599, MedChemExpress). After treatment, cells were lysed on ice for 20 min in RIPA buffer (P0013B, Beyotime, China) supplemented with phosphatase and protease inhibitors. The protein supernatants were separated by SDS-PAGE and transferred to PVDF membranes. Subsequently, the membranes were blocked with 5% non-fat milk in TBST and incubated overnight at 4 °C with primary antibodies against Aggrecan (A11691, Abclonal, China, 1:1000), collagen type II (ab34712, Abcam, 1:1000), β-Actin (AC026, Abclonal, 1:5000), SOX9 (82630, Cell Signaling Technology, 1:1000), p-CaMKII (Thr286) (12716, Cell Signaling Technology, 1:1000), and CaMKII (4436, Cell Signaling Technology, 1:1000). After washing three times with TBST, the membranes were incubated with horseradish peroxidase (HRP)-conjugated secondary antibodies at room temperature for 1 h. Immunoreactive protein bands were detected using an ECL substrate with a BIO-RAD ChemiDoc XRS+ system. Semi-quantitative analyses of the images were conducted using ImageJ.

RNA extraction, and real-time quantitative PCR

Total RNA was isolated using TRIzol reagent (Invitrogen, USA) according to the manufacturer’s protocol. A total of 1000 ng of RNA was reverse-transcribed into cDNA using the 1st Strand cDNA Synthesis SuperMix (11141ES60, Yeasen, Shanghai, China). Quantitative real-time PCR was performed using SYBR Green Master Mix (11184ES08, Yeasen) on a Realplex real-time PCR detection system (Eppendorf). GAPDH was used as an endogenous control for normalization. The relative gene expression levels were analyzed via the comparative Ct (2^–ΔΔCt) method. All reactions were performed in technical triplicates with at least three independent biological replicates. The primer sequences are listed in Supplementary Table 1.

Nile red staining

Primary mouse chondrocytes were serum starved for 12 h, followed by OLA treatment for 8 h. Then, the cells were fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde for 15 min, and stained with 1 µM Nile Red (HY-D0718, MedChemExpress) for 10 min at room temperature. Nuclei were stained with DAPI, and images were captured using a fluorescence microscope. Fluorescence intensity per cell was quantified using ImageJ, and the relative values were normalized to the control group.

Ca2+ staining

Primary mouse chondrocytes were serum starved for 12 h, followed by OLA treatment for 24 h. Then, Ca2+ was detected using the Fluo 4 AM (S1060, Beyotime, China) according to the manufacturer’s protocol. The cells were washed three times with PBS after medium removal. Subsequently, they were incubated with 2 µM Fluo 4 AM (diluted in PBS) at 37 °C in the dark for 30 min, followed by three additional PBS washes. Fluorescence images were acquired using a fluorescence microscope and quantified with ImageJ software.

Pellet culture

For chondrogenic differentiation, 5 × 10⁶ chondrocytes were collected by centrifugation at 400 × g for 5 min in a 15 mL centrifuge tube from each group. Then, the cell pellets were resuspended in chondrogenic differentiation medium (MUXMX-90041, OriCell, China). The medium was changed every two days. After 2 weeks of culture, the pellets were fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde for subsequent histological analysis.

Immunoprecipitation

After the indicated treatments, cells were treated with 10 µM MG132 (HY-13259, MedChemExpress) for 8 h before harvesting. Then the cells were lysed using lysis buffer (P0013, Beyotime) [pH 7.5, 20 mM Tris-HCl, 150 mM NaCl, 1% (v/v) Triton X-100, 1 mM PMSF, and 1× protease inhibitor]. After centrifugation (12,000 × g, 4 °C, 10 min), the supernatants were collected and incubated with protein A/G magnetic beads conjugated with 2 µg of anti-SOX9 antibody (67439-1-IG, Proteintech) or 2 µg of normal IgG (as a negative control) at 4 °C for overnight. Then, the magnetic beads which had already combined with the target protein were subsequently washed three times with lysis buffer, and were prepared for western blot analysis. After immunoprecipitation, the immunoprecipitated SOX9 was detected by immunoblotting with an anti-SOX9 antibody (82630, Cell Signaling Technology), and its ubiquitination levels were detected with an anti-ubiquitin antibody (10201-2-AP, Proteintech). The ratio of ubiquitin signal to immunoprecipitated SOX9 was quantified using ImageJ.

Statistical analysis

All statistical analyses were performed using GraphPad Prism version 8.0. Data are presented as mean ± standard deviation. Comparisons between two independent groups were made using the two-tailed Student’s t-test or the Mann–Whitney rank sum test for non-parametric data. For comparisons across multiple groups, one- or two-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) was employed. The normality of data distribution within each group was assessed using the Shapiro-Wilk test prior to the selection of parametric tests. The sample size (n) refers to the number of biologically independent samples. Statistical significance was defined as *P < 0.05, **P < 0.01, and ***P < 0.001.

Results

High-fat diet (HFD) promotes articular cartilage degeneration and accelerates the progression of osteoarthritis (OA) in mice

To clarify the effects of obesity on knee articular cartilage, the mice were fed with a HFD for 12 weeks to induce the obesity models, while the control group received a standard diet (SD) (Fig. 1A). Subsequently, mice were subjected to anterior cruciate ligament transection (ACLT) surgery, which is commonly used to establish OA models, or a sham surgery for 6 weeks. After this, HFD-fed mice showed higher body weight and serum triglyceride (TG) levels compared to the SD-fed group (Fig. 1B, C, D). Safranin O-fast green staining revealed that HFD feeding promoted articular cartilage degeneration compared to the SD-fed group. We further aimed to elucidate whether the HFD exacerbated pathological changes in OA. The combination of HFD and ACLT resulted in more severe cartilage degeneration than ACLT alone (Fig. 1E). OARSI scores for the Medial Femoral Condyle (MFC) and Medial Tibial Plateau (MTP) were consistent with this trend (Fig. 1F). Meanwhile, immunohistochemistry (IHC) staining revealed that type II collagen (COL2A1) was significantly reduced in the articular cartilage of HFD-fed mice. Furthermore, the HFD enhanced the degeneration of the articular cartilage and exacerbated a reduction in type II collagen (COL2A1) induced by ACLT surgery (Fig. 1G, H). Collectively, these results demonstrate that, whether administered alone or in conjunction with ACLT surgery, the HFD not only spontaneously induces the progression of OA but also exacerbates the ongoing pathological processes.

HFD causes metabolic dysregulation of oleic acid (OLA), leading to its accumulation in knee articular cartilage

To screen the specific metabolites in HFD-induced OA, untargeted metabolomics was employed to analyze metabolites in the articular cartilage of mice. Quality Control (QC) results indicate that experimental technical errors have a negligible impact (Fig. S1A-D). Principal component analysis was performed to assess group separation, which revealed significant differences between groups and limited variation within groups (Fig. 2A). The volcano plot revealed metabolites with statistically significant differences, showing that 139 metabolites were up-regulated and 148 were down-regulated in the HFD group compared to the SD group (Fig. 2B). The corresponding heatmap showed the classification of these metabolites and their distribution across the different groups (Fig. 2C). To identify the most potentially influential metabolites, we selected the top 50 metabolites based on Variable Importance in Projection (VIP) scores. Analysis revealed that fatty acyl metabolites accounted for the highest proportion of these 50 metabolites (Fig. 2D). The heatmap further revealed an increase in fatty acyl metabolites in the HFD group (Fig. 2E). Following this, a combination of VIP scores and Spectral Match Scores (which indicate confidence in metabolite identification) identified FFA (18:1), also known as oleic acid (OLA), as the most promising candidate within this class (Fig. 2F). Among all ten upregulated differential fatty acyl metabolites, OLA ranks 5th in fold-change and 2nd in significance. From the perspective of significance, OLA also holds a high priority (Supplementary Table 2). The violin plot illustrated the distribution of OLA across the experimental groups (Fig. 2G). As OLA, a common monounsaturated fatty acid (MUFA), can be synthesized endogenously [27, 28], we subsequently examined the expression of genes involved in its biosynthesis by RT-PCR. The mRNA levels of Acaca, Elovl6, FASN, SCD1, and SCD2 were significantly increased in the articular cartilage of HFD-fed mice compared to the SD-fed controls (Fig. 2H). Together, these findings demonstrate that HFD feeding disturbs OLA metabolism and promotes its accumulation in the articular cartilage of the knee.

OLA promotes cartilage degeneration and disrupts extracellular matrix (ECM) homeostasis in chondrocytes

To investigate the role of OLA in articular cartilage, cartilage explants obtained from OA patients during total knee arthroplasty (TKA) were treated with exogenous OLA. Compared to the control group, the OLA-treated group exhibited a significant reduction in proteoglycan content, as demonstrated by Safranin O and Toluidine Blue staining (Fig. 3A). Furthermore, immunohistochemistry (IHC) revealed a marked decrease in type II collagen (COL2A1) expression and a significant increase in MMP13 expression in cartilage explants following OLA treatment (Fig. 3B). To further investigate the impact of OLA on chondrocytes, mouse primary chondrocytes were treated with OLA. Nile Red staining revealed increased lipid droplet formation (Fig. 3C). We further found that OLA treatment reduced the protein levels of type II collagen (COL2A1) and aggrecan (ACAN) (Fig. 3D), while having no significant effect on chondrocyte viability (Fig. S2). Consistently, RT-PCR analysis showed that OLA downregulated the mRNA expression of COL2A1 and ACAN but upregulated the expression of the catabolic genes ADAMTS5 and MMP13 (Fig. 3E). Furthermore, results from Alcian blue, Safranin O, and Toluidine Blue staining indicated that the proteoglycan content was markedly reduced in the three-dimensional pellet-cultured mouse chondrocytes (Fig. 3F). These results demonstrate that OLA promotes cartilage degeneration and disrupts ECM homeostasis in chondrocytes.

OLA destabilizes SOX9 and promotes its ubiquitin-mediated degradation

Given that SOX9 is a key upstream transcription factor for COL2A1 and ACAN, we further explored whether OLA regulates SOX9. The immunofluorescence results showed a marked reduction of SOX9 protein levels in HFD-fed mice compared to SD-fed controls, observed following both sham and ACLT procedures (Fig. 4A). Furthermore, the HFD exacerbated a reduction in SOX9 induced by ACLT surgery (Fig. 4A). OLA treatment also reduced SOX9 protein levels in cartilage explants (Fig. 4B). Furthermore, western blot analysis showed that SOX9 was downregulated by OLA treatment in a concentration-dependent manner (Fig. 4C) as well as in a time-dependent (Fig. 4D) manner in murine primary chondrocytes. Interestingly, the RT-PCR result revealed a discrepancy between SOX9 mRNA and protein levels: SOX9 protein was significantly downregulated upon OLA treatment, whereas its mRNA levels remained largely unchanged (Fig. 4E). These findings suggest that OLA may directly target the SOX9 protein without affecting its RNA transcription. We therefore hypothesize that OLA induces a post-translational modification of SOX9. To test this hypothesis, we employed the proteasome inhibitor MG132. Western blot analysis revealed that MG132 treatment largely reversed the OLA-induced downregulation of SOX9 protein (Fig. 4F). Additionally, in the presence of the protein synthesis inhibitor cycloheximide (CHX), OLA decreased the half-life of SOX9 protein (Fig. 4G). Further immunoprecipitation revealed that OLA treatment enhanced the ubiquitination of SOX9 (Fig. 4H). Collectively, these results demonstrate that OLA promotes the ubiquitination and subsequent degradation of SOX9.

CaMK2 is activated by HFD feeding in cartilage

To elucidate the mechanism by which HFD-induced OLA accumulation regulates SOX9 and its downstream ECM homeostasis, we performed RNA-seq on articular cartilage from HFD-fed and SD-fed mice. QC results indicate that experimental technical errors have a negligible impact (Fig. S3A, B), at the same time, the sequencing data is of high quality (Supplementary Table 3). Principal component analysis revealed distinct transcriptomes between the two groups (Fig. 5A). A volcano plot showed the statistically significant differentially expressed genes (Fig. 5B), and hierarchical clustering clearly segregated the groups based on their expression profiles (Fig. 5C). Gene Ontology (GO) analysis revealed significant enrichment in the Molecular Function category for the term “ion channel activity” (Fig. 5D). KEGG pathway analysis indicated that differentially expressed genes were enriched in the calcium signaling pathway (Fig. 5E). Furthermore, Gene Set Enrichment Analysis (GSEA) confirmed the activation of the calcium signaling pathway in the articular cartilage of HFD-fed mice (Fig. 5F). The calcium signaling pathway influences intracellular calcium concentration, thereby regulating the activity of calcium-dependent proteins. As a key downstream regulator, CaMK2 is directly activated in a calcium concentration-dependent manner [34–36]. Based on our transcriptomic results, we hypothesize that HFD feeding may activate CaMK2. To test this, we examined CaMK2 activation in the murine knee joint via immunofluorescence staining. The results revealed higher levels of p-CaMK2 in the articular cartilage of HFD-fed mice compared to SD-fed controls (Fig. 5G). However, the results also showed that ACLT surgery did not lead to the activation of CaMK2 both in HFD and SD groups (Fig. 5G). Based on the above findings, we speculate that the activation of CaMK2 may be associated with HFD feeding. As previously demonstrated, HFD leads to OLA accumulation in cartilage. Therefore, we propose that OLA is responsible for the CaMK2 activation. Since the activation of CaMK2 is associated with intracellular calcium concentration, Fluo 4 AM staining was performed to detect the intracellular concentration of Ca2+. Quantification of fluorescence intensity indicated that the Ca2+ concentration in OLA treatment was significantly higher than that in control group (Fig. S4). Western blot analysis further demonstrated that OLA treatment activated CaMK2 (as indicated by p-CaMK2) in primary murine chondrocytes in vitro (Fig. 5H). OLA also increased the levels of p-CaMK2 in cultured human cartilage explants (Fig. 5I). Together, these results indicate that CaMK2 is activated by HFD feeding in cartilage.

Activation of CaMK2 negatively regulates ECM homeostasis

To investigate the role of CaMK2 activation in HFD-induced OA, a CaMK2 agonist DCP-LA was used in primary mouse chondrocytes in vitro. The results showed that DCP-LA treatment decreased the protein levels of collagen type II (COL2A1) protein and aggrecan (ACAN) proteoglycan (Fig. 6A) and markedly reduced proteoglycan content (Fig. 6B). At the mRNA level, CaMK2 activation downregulated the expression of COL2A1 and ACAN and upregulated the expression of MMP13 and ADAMTS5 (Fig. 6C). As mentioned above, OLA disrupts ECM homeostasis and activates CaMK2. We therefore hypothesize that OLA regulates ECM homeostasis through CaMK2 activation. To test this, we co-treated chondrocytes with OLA and the CaMK2 inhibitor KN93. Results demonstrated that KN93 treatment significantly rescued the OLA-induced reduction in COL2A1 and ACAN protein levels (Fig. 6D). Consistent with this, proteoglycan loss, as indicated by Alcian blue and Toluidine Blue staining, was also markedly reversed by KN93 (Fig. 6E). Furthermore, RT-PCR analysis confirmed that KN93 treatment restored the mRNA expression of COL2A1 and ACAN that was reduced by OLA and suppressed the OLA-induced upregulation of MMP13 and ADAMTS5 (Fig. 6F). Furthermore, knockdown of CaMK2 via siRNA attenuated the OLA-induced reduction in collagen type II (COL2A1) and aggrecan (ACAN) protein levels (Fig. 6G).

CaMK2 regulates SOX9 stability via the ubiquitin-proteasome system

As we have previously demonstrated that OLA-activated CaMK2 negatively regulates ECM and that OLA reduces SOX9 stability via ubiquitination, we hypothesize that CaMK2 is a key regulator of the OLA-induced, ubiquitination-mediated degradation of SOX9. To test this hypothesis, we treated mouse primary chondrocytes with the CaMK2 agonist DCP-LA. Immunoblot analysis showed that SOX9 protein levels decreased dose-dependently with DCP-LA treatment (Fig. 7A). Furthermore, the OLA-induced reduction of SOX9 was largely reversed by the CaMK2 inhibitor KN93 (Fig. 7B). Knockdown of CaMK2 also attenuated the OLA-induced reduction in SOX9 (Fig. 7C). These findings confirm that CaMK2 activation negatively regulates SOX9 expression. Moreover, MG132 treatment suppressed the DCP-LA-induced decrease in SOX9 (Fig. 7D). Immunoblot analysis also revealed a significantly shortened SOX9 protein half-life following DCP-LA treatment (Fig. 7E). Importantly, OLA-induced shortening of SOX9 half-life was reversed by KN93 (Fig. 7F). Immunoprecipitation assays revealed that ubiquitination levels of SOX9 increased following DCP-LA treatment (Fig. 7G). Additionally, the OLA-induced increase in SOX9 ubiquitination was suppressed by KN93 co-treatment (Fig. 7H). Collectively, these results demonstrate that CaMK2 promotes SOX9 degradation via the ubiquitin-proteasome pathway.

Discussion

In this study, we propose a novel molecular regulatory axis that may contribute to OA pathogenesis in obesity-induced metabolic dysregulation. Our findings reveal that OLA, a common monounsaturated fatty acid that accumulates in the articular cartilage of HFD-fed mice, may act as a critical pathogenic metabolite in OA. We further demonstrate that OLA disrupts ECM homeostasis by activating CaMK2, which promotes ubiquitin-proteasome-mediated degradation of the key transcription factor SOX9. However, the specific E3 ubiquitin ligase and the direct mechanism of phosphorylation remain unknown. Collectively, our data support a proposed model—the OLA-CaMK2-SOX9 axis—which offers a new explanation for OA pathogenesis (Fig. 8). Further studies are warranted to validate this model and to elucidate the specific E3 ligase involved and the directness of the phosphorylation events.

Feeding mice a high-fat diet (HFD) induces aberrant metabolism and oleic acid (OLA) accumulation in the knee articular cartilage. Accumulated OLA is proposed to promote CaMK2 phosphorylation and activation. Activated CaMK2 may then facilitate the ubiquitin-mediated degradation of SOX9 (via an unidentified E3 ligase, denoted by “?”), leading to suppressed transcription of extracellular matrix (ECM) anabolic genes, including COL2A1 and ACAN. Collectively, this axis may contribute to accelerated cartilage degradation and osteoarthritis progression. OA is the most prevalent chronic joint disease, primarily affecting individuals over 65 years of age [37]. Although the risk factors of OA such as aging, gender, muscle strength, obesity, and mechanical alignment have been recognized, the pathogenesis of OA remains poorly understood [38, 39]. Obesity is one of the key risk factors contributing to the progression of OA [40]. Moreover, current perspectives recognize that obesity is a cluster of systemic metabolic disorders, characterized by altered levels of lipid metabolites [41, 42]. However, the specific metabolites responsible for OA remain largely unknown [43, 44]. Our initial experiments confirmed that HFD-induced obesity markedly promoted articular cartilage degeneration and accelerated OA progression. In order to find the key metabolites, we performed metabolomic analysis on knee articular cartilage from HFD-fed mice. By screening the top 50 metabolites based on VIP scores, we found that fatty acyl metabolites accounted for the highest proportion. Fatty acyl metabolites mainly include fatty acids (FA), oxidized lipids, and carnitines [45]. These metabolites play roles in cellular energy metabolism, specifically in FA metabolism and oxidation [46, 47]. In recent years, accumulating evidence has indicated that FA metabolism and oxidation play important roles in chondrocytes and may be implicated in the pathogenesis of OA [48]. Previous studies by Nick van Gastel et al. demonstrated that lipid deficiency in skeletal stem cells promotes chondrogenic gene expression and maintains cartilage function, as chondrocytes primarily rely on glycolysis rather than FA oxidation for energy production [49]. A recent study further revealed that FA oxidation in articular cartilage promotes degenerative changes and contributes to OA pathogenesis [18]. These findings collectively indicate that FA metabolites may be a critical factor in the etiology of OA, yet the specific FA and their mechanisms in obesity-related OA remain to be defined [50]. Our research is a deeper exploration of this area. Using metabolomics, we identified oleic acid (OLA), a monounsaturated fatty acid, as an important metabolite accumulating in the articular cartilage of HFD-fed mice. This finding is particularly confirmed by previous human studies using time-of-flight secondary ion mass spectrometry (TOF-SIMS) that also reported elevated OLA levels in OA cartilage [29]. However, the precise role of OLA in OA pathogenesis remains to be fully elucidated.

Our study proposes a novel perspective on the pathogenesis of OA associated with obesity. We find that OLA accumulating in the articular cartilage of HFD-fed mice may contribute to the acceleration of OA progression. In human OA cartilage explants and mouse primary chondrocytes, OLA treatment promoted proteoglycan loss, downregulated key ECM components (collagen type II protein and aggrecan proteoglycan), and upregulated catabolic enzymes (MMP13 and ADAMTS5). This disruption in ECM homeostasis represents an important phenotype in OA pathogenesis. To identify the upstream regulator coordinating this anabolic-catabolic imbalance, we focused on SOX9, the master transcription factor for COL2A1 and ACAN. We found that OLA reduced SOX9 protein levels without altering its mRNA levels. Furthermore, we confirmed that OLA promotes SOX9 ubiquitination and shortens its protein half-life. Collectively, these experiments demonstrate that OLA promotes ubiquitination-mediated SOX9 degradation in chondrocyte, leading to ECM degeneration and may exacerbate OA.

To explore the mechanism of OLA in obesity-related OA, we performed transcriptome sequencing on articular cartilage of HFD-fed mice to screen the downstream targets of OLA. KEGG enrichment analysis revealed significant alterations in the calcium signaling pathway in HFD-fed mice. This pathway governs a series of cellular processes, including proliferation, gene transcription, and apoptosis [51, 52]. It has been shown previously that FA (including OLA) can increase cytoplasmic Ca2+ concentration by acting as endogenous ionophores to induce Ca2+ influx or by stimulating endoplasmic reticulum Ca2+ release [53, 54]. The activation of calcium signaling pathway is initiated by the release of calcium ions (Ca2+) [55, 56]. Thus, the accumulation of OLA in articular cartilage may lead to the activation of calcium signaling pathways, which is confirmed in our transcriptome sequencing by GSEA. Meanwhile, this elevation of Ca2+ is bound by the high-affinity sensor calmodulin (CaM), which activates downstream regulators such as the Ca2+/calmodulin-dependent protein kinases (CaMKs) [57]. After the formation of the Ca2+/calmodulin complex, members like CaMK2 and CaMK4 undergo phosphorylation and activation [58]. Like other fatty acids, OLA is a natural CaMK2 agonist [59, 60]. It was also confirmed in our experiments that OLA activated CaMK2 in both murine chondrocytes and human cartilage explants in vitro.

The role of CaMK2 in OA has been explored in some studies. In human OA cartilage, phosphorylated CaMK2 (p-CaMK2) was significantly upregulated and drove chondrocyte hypertrophy, matrix degradation, and pro-inflammatory responses by modulating key pathways like BMP/SMAD [61]. Shurei Sugita et al. found that CaMK2δ was activated during OA development, formed a protein complex with Hes1, and thereby promoted the expression of hypertrophic and catabolic markers, including ADAMTS5 and MMP13, as well as inflammatory mediators like IL-6 [62]. A similar mechanism is demonstrated by the action of basic calcium phosphate (BCP) crystals, which are commonly found in OA joints. These crystals up-regulate intracellular calcium levels, leading to increased CaMK2 phosphorylation and activation, and thereby promote OA progression [63]. These studies demonstrate that CaMK2 plays an important role in OA pathogenesis. Our findings provide further evidence and confirm that CaMK2 activation is also observed in the model of obesity-related OA. Interestingly, we observed aberrant CaMK2 activation in the HFD model, but not in the ACLT model. This finding further indicates that the mechanisms driving cartilage degeneration may be different between the HFD and ACLT models. In our study, the effects of an HFD and ACLT surgery on OARSI scores, COL2A1 and SOX9 protein appear to be largely independent and additive, with no strong synergistic interaction observed. This suggests that HFD and ACLT may operate through relatively distinct pathological pathways. For instance, HFD likely exerts its influence primarily via metabolic dysregulation, whereas ACLT mainly induces mechanical stress alteration, subchondral bone remodeling, and post-traumatic inflammation. These distinct pathways likely contribute additively to cartilage damage, explaining why the HFD combined with ACLT group exhibited the most severe OA phenotype. These results reveal the clinical necessity of concurrently addressing both metabolic risk and mechanical factors in the comprehensive management of OA.

In this study, multi-omics analysis of an HFD-induced obesity mouse model revealed OLA accumulation and CaMK2 activation in the articular cartilage. In vitro studies showed that OLA promotes SOX9 ubiquitination and ECM degradation via CaMK2. A previous study demonstrated that conditional knockout of Sox9 using Sox9fl/fl, AcanCreERT2 mice results in spontaneous OA and exacerbates post-traumatic osteoarthritis [64], in which genetic ablation of SOX9 serves as the primary driver of OA pathogenesis. In our HFD-induced obesity model, we found that HFD feeding directly leads to articular cartilage degeneration and exacerbates surgically induced osteoarthritis. Notably, a reduction in SOX9 protein levels was also observed in this model, suggesting a potential correlation with previous studies involving conditional Sox9 knockout. Both models exhibited reduced SOX9 expression, which was accompanied by varying degrees of cartilage degeneration. Moreover, the OA was exacerbated following surgical intervention in both models. These convergent findings suggest that the pathological outcomes observed in these two distinct experimental systems may be attributable to decreased SOX9 protein levels. These findings establish SOX9 as a critical transcription factor essential for maintaining chondrocyte identity, and its reduction may contribute to the pathogenesis of osteoarthritis. Our in vitro studies identified an OLA-CaMK2-SOX9 regulatory axis that promotes SOX9 degradation by modulating its protein stability. Therefore, we speculate that the aberrant accumulation of OLA observed in obesity-related OA may also contribute to disease pathogenesis through this same regulatory axis. This integrated strategy, combining a stable animal model, multi-omics analyses, and experimental validation, uncovers a novel metabolite-mediated mechanism axis that may be involved in obesity-related OA.

Several limitations of this study should be acknowledged. First, a key limitation of the present study is the modest sample size of n = 4 per experimental group, which constrains the statistical power of our metabolomics analyses, the limited number of biological replicates reduces the generalizability of our metabolomic findings, as the observed patterns may not fully represent the variability of the target organization. Second, although our mouse model and in vitro data strongly support that CaMK2 is the key regulator in OLA-induced cartilage degradation, the role of CaMK2 in obesity-induced OA requires further validation, such as using conditional knockout mice or specific inhibitors. Third, the molecular mechanism by which activated CaMK2 promotes SOX9 ubiquitination remains to be fully elucidated. One hypothesis is that CaMK2 directly phosphorylates SOX9, which is recognized by a specific E3 ubiquitin ligase. Another is that CaMK2 may phosphorylate and activate the E3 ligase of SOX9. We will investigate these areas in the future.

In conclusion, our findings propose a novel mechanistic pathway underlying obesity-associated OA. We have identified that OLA, a potential pathogenic metabolite, activates CaMK2, leading to ubiquitin-mediated degradation of SOX9. This cascade ultimately disrupts ECM homeostasis and promotes cartilage destruction. In brief, the OLA-CaMK2-SOX9 axis may play a critical role in obesity-related OA pathogenesis and provide a potential strategy for OA treatment.
