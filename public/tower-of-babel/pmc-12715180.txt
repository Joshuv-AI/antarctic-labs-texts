Medicaid's role in critical care after Medicaid expansion: evidence from Virginia
Authors: Sarah J Marks, Kristina E Rudd, Chethan Bachireddy, Julie M Donohue, Derek C Angus, Theodore J Iwashyna, Andrew J Barnes
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractIntroductionMedicaid provides access to care for low-income patients facing life-threatening illnesses who are cared for in intensive care units (ICUs). Despite the growth of Medicaid coverage with the Affordable Care Act, little is known about Medicaid's role in critical care for the Medicaid Expansion population, adults ages 19-64.MethodsUsing hospital discharge data from Virginia, we examined payer composition between 2016 and 2023 and analyzed 2023 demographic and clinical data for adults ages 19-64.ResultsMedicaid's share of ICU stays more than doubled from 2016 (14.1%) to 2023 (31.8%). While only 25.6% of Medicaid hospitalizations involved ICU care, these stays account disproportionately for charges (51.7%), hospital days (36.9%), and readmissions (32.3%). Common reasons for admission include potentially preventable conditions: sepsis, diabetes, heart failure, and alcohol use. Medicaid patients, despite being younger than their commercially insured counterparts, have more comorbidities (4+ comorbidities: 49.9% vs 38.9%) and are more likely to be readmitted in adjusted models (29.7% [95% Confidence Interval: 29.1-30.4] vs 24.3% [95% Confidence Interval: 23.6%-25.1%]).ConclusionsThis work demonstrates the crucial role of Medicaid as a payer for seriously ill adults and the need for increased attention by Medicaid programs to ICU patients before, during, and after hospitalization.

Medicaid provided healthcare coverage to nearly one in four Americans in 2023. Its importance has grown with the passage of the Affordable Care Act, through which over 23 million low-income adults 19-64 years old gained access to healthcare during times of serious illness and afterwards through Medicaid Expansion.1-4 Seriously ill patients requiring a high level of care including those who have or are at risk for organ failure, are often cared for in an intensive care unit (ICU).5 In addition to the high costs of ICU care, patients admitted to the ICU often have long lengths of stay, frequent rehospitalization, and increased morbidity and mortality post-ICU stay.6-11 Yet there is little information about Medicaid's role in financing critical care use, especially among non-elderly adults, the primary target of Medicaid expansion.

While studies have examined how the use and characteristics of intensive care vary by insurance status and type of insurance,3,12-16 no studies have looked at the demographic and clinical characteristics of the 19-64-year-old working-age Medicaid population post-the Affordable Care Act, the primary target of Medicaid expansion. Given the high costs and poor health outcomes associated with intensive care use, more information is needed about these patients, including from the payer's perspective. Our objective was to characterize the population of 19-64-year-olds with critical illnesses served by Medicaid and provide insights into the role of Medicaid in financing their care. We used data from hospital discharges in Virginia, a state that expanded Medicaid in January 2019, to examine these objectives.

Study data and methods

We analyzed hospital discharge data from the Virginia Health Information (VHI) database, which includes stays for all patients admitted to Virginia hospitals. We included all patients admitted from 2016 to 2023 to understand changes over time and analyze the characteristics of admissions using data from 2023. To better understand the population most impacted by the Affordable Care Act's expansion of Medicaid and consistent with previous studies,17,18 we limited our analysis to acute care hospitals for adults ages 19-64 at the time of admission. The VHI data provided charge data for 14 types of care based on revenue codes. We defined a hospital stay as including an ICU stay if the intensive care charge was greater than zero. Our main variable of interest was payer (Medicaid, Medicare [for non-elderly people with disabilities disabled], Commercial Insurance, Other Government, etc.). We characterized the principal diagnosis using the Healthcare Cost and Utilization Project's (HCUP) Clinical Classification Software which aggregates ICD-10-CM codes into clinically meaningful categories.19 We identified comorbidities using HCUP's Elixhauser Comorbidity Software.20

As a potential indicator of quality of care and health outcomes for these patients,21-23 we analyzed hospital readmissions, using a linear probability model of any hospital readmission to the same or any other Virginia hospital within 90 days for all discharges. We adjusted for age group, sex, race/ethnicity, diagnosis related group, hospital length of stay, and the Elixhauser Index comorbidities, quartile of Social Deprivation Index and rurality based on a patient's ZIP Code. We used quarter and hospital fixed effects; we also clustered standard errors for discharge hospitals. All analysis was performed in SAS 9.4, Stata 18, and R 4.3.3.

Our study has several limitations. First, our data are at the level of the hospital discharge; we are unable to identify unique individuals across hospital stays. Second, there are general limitations to administrative data which is generated for billing purposes rather than clinical care. We only have information about what was coded at the time of hospitalization; in particular, the number and type of comorbidities may be underestimated. Third, we have limited information about the ICU stay itself including both the specific number of ICU days and the full costs associated with those days. Fourth, our data are from only one state (Virginia) and the findings may not be generalizable to other states.

Study results

We identified 821 756 ICU stays among adults ages 19-64 from 2016 to 2023, including 96 067 ICU stays in 2023, representing 42% of all ICU stays for adults 19 and older in that year. The share of Medicaid-covered ICU stays for adults ages 19-64 grew dramatically over this 6-year period, from 14.1% in 2016 to 31.8% in 2023, with the greatest increase coming during the first year Virginia's Medicaid expansion in 2019 (13.4% of stays in 2018 and 26.5% of stays in 2019) (Figure 1). During the same period, the share of uninsured and “other” payer ICU stays dropped substantially. Among Medicaid-paid hospitalizations, 25.6% included an ICU stay. However, these hospitalizations accounted for 51.6% of total hospital charges, 36.9% of total hospital days, and 32.4% of all hospital readmissions (Appendix Figure S1). Medicare, the uninsured, and the “other” category generally had higher percentages of ICU-related hospitalizations, hospital days, charges, and readmissions, while commercial insurance and other government paid stays had lower percentages.

The demographics of Medicaid paid ICU stays varied substantially from those of other payers (Table 1; Appendix Table S1). Over 41.7% of Medicaid paid ICU stays were for adults ages 19-44, compared to only 16.2%-28.6% of other payers. While the share of 19-44-year-olds was highest among the uninsured (46.5%), among those were insured, Medicaid was the single largest payer for this age group. Medicaid was also the largest payer for racial/ethnic minorities and Medicaid paid stays were significantly more likely to be for members of racial/ethnic minority groups compared to other payers (Medicaid: 50.2% vs 37.6%-45.5%).

The type of admission, length of stay, and discharge destination all varied by payer (Table 1; Appendix Table S1). Compared to commercial insurance paid stays Medicaid paid ICU stays were also more likely to be emergent/urgent vs elective (92.7% vs 86.7%), more closely matching Medicare (91.9%) and uninsured stays (95.8%). Medicaid stays were also longer than commercially paid ones or stays for the uninsured (Medicaid: 33.1% were ≥7 days vs 27.2% for commercially insured and 20.4% for the uninsured), although they were not as lengthy as Medicare paid stays (37.0% were ≥7 days). Medicaid stays were less likely to result in a discharge to home than commercially insured or uninsured stays (Medicaid: 58.5%; uninsured: 74.1%; commercially insured: 67.6%) and were more likely to have been discharged “against medical advice” (5.2%) than any other payer (range: 1.6%-2.7%; uninsured higher at 5.6%).

Except for Medicare paid ICU stays, all of which were for beneficiaries with eligible due to a disability given our inclusion criteria, Medicaid paid ICU stays involved patients with a greater number of comorbidities than other payers. Almost half (49.9%) of Medicaid paid ICU stays were for patients with four or more comorbidities compared to 38.9% of commercially insured stays and 68.9% of Medicare paid stays (Appendix Figure S2). The types of comorbidities also varied substantially. Medicaid paid stays were almost four times more likely to be for patients with a drug use disorder diagnosis, almost three times more likely to be for patients with a psychosis-related diagnosis, and one and a half times more likely to be for patients with a liver disease compared to commercially paid stays (Appendix Table S2).

Principal diagnosis differed across payers (Appendix Table S3). While sepsis was the principal diagnosis for admission across all payers, the percentage of sepsis ICU stays varied by payer, with Medicaid having the third highest percentage of sepsis related admissions (14.8%) after Medicare (17.8%) and “Other payers” (14.9%), Medicaid had the highest percentage of diabetes principal diagnoses (6.0%) and second highest percentage of heart failure and alcohol-related admissions, with acute myocardial infarction being relatively less common among Medicaid paid ICU stay compared to other payers.

In models adjusting for a range of demographic and clinical factors, we found that Medicaid paid ICU stays were more likely to result in a readmission within 90 days of discharge (29.7%; 95% Confidence Interval: 29.1%-30.4%) than all other payers except for Medicare (Appendix Figure S3). Specifically, Medicaid paid ICU stays had more than 5% point higher chance of readmission than commercially insured stays.

Discussion

To better understand the role of Medicaid in caring for critically ill patients, we examined the frequency and type of Medicaid paid ICU stays in Virginia. We found that Medicaid played an increasingly large role as a payer of ICU stays from 2016-2023 including during the COVID-19 pandemic, with the greatest increase following Virginia's Medicaid expansion in 2019 and that those stays were responsible for disproportionate shares of charges, hospital days and readmissions within Medicaid. Medicaid members, despite their younger age, had far more comorbidities compared to other payers except for non-older adult Medicare enrollees for whom eligibility is due to disability, ALS or end stage renal disease.24 While sepsis was the most common diagnosis across all payers, it was more frequent diagnosis among Medicaid paid stays than commercially insured or uninsured. We also found that Medicaid ICU stays were more likely to be for other conditions that might be preventable such as diabetes, heart failure, and alcohol use.25 In models adjusting for these comorbidities and diagnosis-related groups, Medicaid members were more likely to be readmitted within 90 days of hospital discharge than all other payers (except Medicare) as well as the uninsured.

Our findings on the growing role of Medicaid are consistent with work showing an increase in Medicaid critical bed occupancy from 2000 to 2010 and as a coverage source for adults hospitalized with asthma, chronic obstructive pulmonary disease, and heart failure in four expansion states in 2015.26,27 These findings have several policy implications for state Medicaid agencies and the Centers for Medicare and Medicaid Services, particularly with the passage of the One Big Beautiful Bill Act, that imposes work requirements and more frequent eligibility redeterminations will make continuous enrollment for many Medicaid expansion members more difficult.28 Medicaid members, who are by definition low-income and already more economically and socially vulnerable, are at higher risk of poor health outcomes both during and after their ICU stay. Patients who experience financial stress are more likely to experience depression and anxiety after discharge.29 State contractual requirements with Medicaid managed care organizations and 1115 waivers that provide more intensive care coordination for both medical and social needs may offer potential policy levers to reduce both initial hospitalizations with ICU stays as well as readmissions after critical care.30

In summary, Medicaid plays an increasingly large role in serving critically ill patients, whose care is highly resource intensive. Medicaid ICU patients tend to be younger, have higher levels of comorbidities and are more likely to be members of minoritized groups than non-Medicaid patients. Strategies to prevent initial hospitalizations that require critical care and decrease readmissions may improve the long-term health outcomes of this vulnerable group.
