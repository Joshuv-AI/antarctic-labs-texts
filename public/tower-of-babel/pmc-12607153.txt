Adolescents as co-designers: adapting human-centered design to develop school-based mental health strategies in northern Ghana
Authors: Claudia L Leung, Priscilla Kukua Goka, Barnabas Atangongo, Mansur Mohammed Musah Bingle, Ishmael Adu, Abdul Rashid Atchulo, Laud Boateng, Keng-Yen Huang, Neda Laiteerapong, Gbana Limann, Anna Volerman, Peter Mintir Amadu, William Frank Hill Koomson
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundAdolescents in low- and middle-income countries (LMICs) face significant mental health challenges, yet their perspectives are often underrepresented in the design of preventive strategies. Co-design approaches, such as human-centered design (HCD), offer a promising way to tailor interventions and implementation strategies to adolescents’ needs and local context. In LMICs, these methods require careful adaptation to address resource constraints, limited design literacy, and cultural norms. This study documents how HCD was adapted to engage adolescents in northern Ghana as co-designers of school-based mental health preventive strategies.MethodsGuided by the first two phases of HCD, we conducted two workshops with 24 students from 12 public senior high schools in Tamale, Ghana. Workshop 1 (Inspiration) used structured, case-based discussions informed by the Consolidated Framework for Implementation Research (CFIR) to explore adolescents’ perspectives on mental health. Workshop 2 (Ideation) used interactive choice-based activities to elicit youth-generated strategies. To align with cognitive and sociocultural factors, we incorporated scaffolded facilitation, hands-on activities, and peer-led engagement. Qualitative data from facilitator notes, artifacts, and audio-confirmed summaries were synthesized using structured rapid qualitative analysis.ResultsAdolescents identified key mental health concerns, including stigma, peer and family influences, and fears about confidentiality. Gender-specific discussions revealed culturally rooted concerns, such as peer pressure and substance use among boys and limited support-seeking among girls. Adolescents prioritized five school strategies: teacher training, curricular integration, mentorship programs, activities that promote positive thinking and mindfulness, and entertainment-based mental health education. Youth demonstrated a conceptual shift from viewing mental health as an individual problem to a shared responsibility across schools and communities. Formation of an Adolescent Advisory Board reflected youth interest in sustained leadership and co-design.ConclusionsContextualized co-design methods can meaningfully engage adolescents in LMIC settings and support the development of culturally grounded, feasible, and youth-prioritized mental health strategies. Structured facilitation enhances both the inclusivity and authenticity of adolescent engagement. This study contributes to implementation science by presenting a replicable co-design framework with policy relevance and providing a foundation for multilevel intervention development in resource-constrained educational systems.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12889-025-25012-0.Keywords: Adolescent mental health, Co-design, Human-centered design, School-based interventions, Low- and middle-income countries

Introduction

Adolescents in low- and middle-income countries (LMICs) face a disproportionate burden of mental health challenges [1, 2]. Globally, 10–20% of adolescents experience mental health conditions, with anxiety, depression, and emotional and behavioral disorders being the most prevalent [3]. Despite this high prevalence, mental health systems in LMICs are often under-resourced, constrained by shortages of trained professionals, pervasive stigma, and limited integration of mental health within health and education systems [4]. In Ghana, for example, national policies support adolescent health, including mental health, but translation into practice is hampered by limited awareness, weak delivery integration, and insufficient bottom-up engagement with adolescents themselves [5]. Engaging youth perspectives directly in developing preventive strategies is critical to creating sustainable solutions that reflect their needs, lived experiences, and sociocultural contexts.

Co-creation approaches have gained traction as promising strategies for intervention and implementation strategy development across diverse settings [6]. They share similarities with other community-engaged strategies such as community-based participatory research, which engages communities throughout the research process [7, 8], and participatory action research, which emphasizes collaborative research and action [9]. However, co-creation is distinct in its focus on involving end-users (those who use or deliver an intervention) as active partners in developing and refining solutions [10, 11]. Co-creation encompasses a spectrum of engagement, from consultative methods (where end users provide feedback) to fully collaborative processes (where end users and researchers iteratively co-develop strategies) [10, 12, 13]. Within this broader umbrella, we use the term “co-design” to describe the structured engagement of end users as co-developers of solutions to a defined problem [11]. Co-design is increasingly applied to engage adult stakeholders in developing interventions or implementation strategies that address delivery constraints and foster ownership and sustainability [14].

By contrast, co-design with adolescents remains less common, particularly in LMICs. Most documented examples are from high-income countries, where participatory methods are supported by robust health and education systems [15]. In LMICs, youth co-design has been used to improve cultural relevance and intervention acceptability. For example, the FOOTPATHS FOR MAMA intervention in Malawi engaged pregnant adolescents to co-design a mental health educational booklet [16, 17], while the SAMA project in India engaged students, teachers, and parents in the development of a school-based mental health program [18, 19]. However, many LMIC studies describe what adolescents contributed to co-design, but not how their participation was structured to overcome participation barriers and ensure authentic, meaningful engagement [20–23].

Engaging adolescents in co-design presents both opportunities and challenges. To be meaningful, engagement must be developmentally appropriate and contextually grounded. Co-design offers the advantage of integrating adolescents’ lived experiences and priorities directly into intervention design, increasing the likelihood that resulting strategies will be acceptable within their contexts. Evidence suggests that youth desire genuine involvement [24], and their participation enhances both relevance and uptake of mental health interventions [25, 26]. However, adolescence is a time of significant growth and variability, and youth within the same age group may have developmental differences in cognitive processing, communication skills, and health literacy [27]. In low-resource, cross-cultural settings, these challenges are compounded by limited exposure to creative or abstract thinking exercises, such as brainstorming, which are more commonly used in high-income education contexts [28–30]. For example, in Ghana, classroom environments often emphasize rote memorization, which may limit comfort with open-ended, participatory methods [31–33]. Hierarchical power dynamics, resource limitations, and a scarcity of trained co-design facilitators can further hinder authentic engagement [34, 35]. Conceptual frameworks such as Hart’s Ladder of Participation offer guidance for assessing depth of youth involvement to ensure that engagement does not become superficial or tokenistic [36]. However, practical guidance on tailoring co-design strategies to adolescents’ developmental stages and sociocultural contexts remains scarce [10].

To incorporate meaningful youth input into the development of preventive mental health strategies, we adapted a human-centered design (HCD) approach to engage adolescents in the co-design of implementation strategies for a school-based mental health program. HCD is a form of co-design that emphasizes empathy, creativity, and iterative testing to generate contextually grounded solutions [37, 38]. Our broader study aims to improve the delivery of evidence-based preventive interventions (e.g., resilience training, mental health education) through a multicomponent implementation model that leverages school and community collaboration. In this paper, we aim to (1) document a structured approach for effectively engaging adolescents in the development of intervention and implementation strategies; (2) illustrate context-specific adaptations of co-design in the Ghanaian sociocultural setting; and (3) identify adolescent-generated mental health priorities to inform implementation. By sharing processes, lessons learned, and outcomes, this study contributes practical, replicable insights for tailoring co-design to support authentic youth participation in LMIC settings.

Methods

Study setting and population

The study was conducted in Tamale, Ghana, the largest urban center in northern Ghana characterized by rapid urbanization and social changes that may influence adolescent mental health [39–44]. Public secondary education in Ghana is free and under the purview of Ghana Education Services, which facilitated access to schools and students. The study population included student representatives from all twelve public senior high schools across the two districts of Tamale: Tamale Metropolis and Sagnarigu. Each school nominated two students (one male and one female, unless single-gender) who met the following criteria: (1) aged ≥ 15 years, (2) currently enrolled in their final two years of senior high school, (3) able and willing to discuss perspectives on mental health and wellness, and (4) had a parent or guardian reachable for consent. Ethical approval was obtained from the Tamale Teaching Hospital Ethical Review Committee (Study ID: TTHERC/20/11/23/02) and the New York University Langone Health Institutional Review Board (Study ID: i23-00955). Participants provided personal or parental written informed consent for participation in this study.

Study design and data collection

This HCD study was embedded within a broader implementation research study to develop and evaluate a multicomponent, school-based preventive mental health implementation model for adolescents in northern Ghana (Fig. 1). The overall study aims to strengthen collaboration between schools and community-based mental health organizations to improve delivery of evidence-based preventive mental health strategies (e.g., mental health education, resilience training, mindfulness-based practices). The co-design activities reported here were conducted to inform the selection and tailoring of strategies to be integrated into the model.

Overview of study phases and co-design activities to develop a school-based preventive mental health model using human-centered design methods in northern Ghana. The process began with a formative research phase that identified key barriers and facilitators to school-based mental health support through key informant interviews and surveys guided by the Consolidated Framework for Implementation Research (CFIR). These findings informed Workshop 1 (Inspiration Phase), which engaged adolescents to explore mental health perceptions, barriers, and facilitators using case-based discussions and contextualized graphics. Workshop 2 (Ideation Phase) guided adolescents in co-designing preferred school-based mental health strategies through interactive choice-based activities. The process culminated in the establishment of an Adolescent Advisory Board to sustain youth engagement during the implementation phase.

We adapted the IDEO HCD process for its structured yet flexible approach, which emphasizes empathy, creativity, and iterative prototyping in solution development [45]. IDEO’s HCD approach has been shown to effectively operationalize implementation strategies in healthcare settings, supporting stakeholder engagement and enhancing the fit of interventions to real-world contexts [46]. The first author’s prior experience adapting IDEO’s HCD approach in cross-cultural and healthcare contexts [47, 48], along with evidence of its successful use in other environments [46, 49, 50], provided both continuity and a basis to tailor co-design for adolescent mental health in Ghana. In this study, we applied the first two stages of HCD to elicit perspectives on mental health challenges (Inspiration) and collaboratively generate potential solutions (Ideation).

Workshop planning and facilitation were conducted by a multidisciplinary research team with expertise in mental health, education, human-centered design, and adolescent engagement. The team included a psychiatrist (WFK), a psychologist with expertise in youth mobilization (PMA), a former schoolteacher (ARA), graduate students (IA, MMMB, PKG, BA), and a pediatrician-researcher with prior experience applying HCD in resource-limited settings (CLL). All team members, except for one, were local Ghanaians, ensuring that workshop activities were contextually grounded in cultural and linguistic norms. Drawing from this diverse expertise, the team proactively identified potential engagement challenges and crafted structured activities to address them (Fig. 2).

Workshop 1: understanding adolescent perspectives on mental health (inspiration phase)

Workshop 1 explored adolescent perceptions of mental health, including perceived barriers, facilitators, and support systems. Formative interviews with guidance and counseling coordinators and community-based mental health leaders highlighted potential challenges to adolescent participation: (1) limited familiarity with mental health concepts, (2) discomfort discussing sensitive topics like stigma and substance use, (3) developmental variability in cognitive and communication skills, and (4) the need to build trust with facilitators.

To address these challenges, we scaffolded adolescent participation using interactive case scenarios, embedded discussion prompts, and trust-building activities (Table 1). Case scenarios were used to depersonalize sensitive topics such as peer pressure, substance use, and academic stress, and were visually depicted using Adobe Stock character sets adapted to reflect local slang, character types, and social context (Fig. 3A). Discussion questions were developed using the Consolidated Framework for Implementation Research (CFIR), a structured, theory-driven approach for evaluating multilevel determinants of mental health program implementation [51, 52]. CFIR is particularly well suited to this context, as youth mental health is shaped by individual, organizational, and system-level factors such as health literacy, institutional resources, and stigma [53–56]. Its application in other school- and community-based mental health studies in LMICs further underscores its utility for capturing multilevel barriers and facilitators in Ghana [57–59]. Using CFIR alongside case scenarios enabled adolescents to engage with potential challenges while supporting a comprehensive evaluation of implementation barriers and opportunities. Constructs included individual knowledge and beliefs, perceived barriers and facilitators, and environmental and interpersonal influences. The interview guide and related materials used in this study are available as Supplementary Files 1 and 2.

Facilitators began the workshop with a clear explanation of workshop goals (“our goal is to learn from you and your experiences”), expectations (“please speak honestly and freely”), and confidentiality policies, including how responses would be compiled and de-identified. Additional engagement mechanisms included movement-based icebreaker activities (e.g., charades), and a group exercise to collaboratively define “mental health” and “mental wellness.” This activity established a common conceptual foundation while allowing facilitators to gauge knowledge levels and adjust the discussion pace accordingly. Discussions alternated between large- and small-group formats, with gender-segregated sessions for culturally sensitive topics.

Workshop 2: co-designing school-based mental health strategies (ideation phase)

Workshop 2 aimed to elicit adolescent preferences for preventive mental health implementation strategies. Modifications to the workshop structure were informed by facilitator debriefs and insights from Workshop 1 and addressed three key challenges: (1) communicating the abstract concept of “prevention” in mental health, (2) shifting focus from individual willpower to school-level strategies, and (3) sustaining attention throughout the session.

To support engagement and reduce cognitive load, we designed choice-based activities to scaffold decision-making (Table 1). Recognizing adolescents’ limited prior exposure to preventive mental health strategies, we provided a menu of evidence-based options rather than asking adolescents to generate ideas de novo. This ensured the proposed strategies were realistic, actionable, and within the scope of implementation.

For individual-level strategies, adolescents built a personalized “Mental Health Toolkit,” by selecting from printed tools labeled with preventive interventions and implementation strategies such as “find a mentor,” or “talk to a counselor” (Fig. 3B). To introduce the concept of prevention, facilitators used a time travel analogy in which adolescents designed a toolkit for a peer experiencing mental distress – first in the present and then retrospectively before symptoms emerged (Fig. 3C). This helped clarify the difference between reactive and proactive mental health strategies.

For school-level strategies, adolescents engaged in a robotics-style activity to build a School Mental Health Mascot. Working in pairs, adolescents used plastic building blocks to design mascots representing their vision for a supportive school environment. Each pair assigned their mascot a mental health “superpower” and selected up to four preventive strategies from a menu of evidence-based interventions to embed in their mascot’s design. This activity fostered creativity while guiding the participants toward actionable and feasible solutions. To further sustain engagement, the activity included a school competition component, as school pride was identified as a key motivator in Workshop 1.

Data collection and analysis

Data were collected from multiple sources, including structured discussion notes, facilitator debriefs, workshop artifacts (e.g., worksheets, written ideas), and audio recordings. Four notetakers (MMMB, CLL, PKG, IA) used structured templates aligned with the CFIR-informed discussion guide to capture small- and large-group discussions. Audio recordings were uploaded to an auto-transcription software (Otter.ai), which generated searchable transcripts used to identify relevant segments for direct audio review.

Data were analyzed using rapid qualitative analysis, a structured method of data reduction that facilitates timely, actionable insights while maintaining analytic rigor [60, 61]. Instead of line-by-line coding, we used a deductive approach that organized data according to CFIR constructs, while retaining flexibility to capture inductive insights when novel concepts emerged [62]. Rapid qualitative analysis is increasingly used in pragmatic implementation research and was well-suited to our iterative design process, given the short intervals between workshops and the overarching goal of informing intervention development. Our approach followed recent consensus guidance on best practices in rapid qualitative analysis, which emphasize the rigor, credibility, and utility of matrix-based, framework-informed methods for implementation research in real-world contexts [63].

Discussion notes were compiled into a structured summary matrix, organized by notetaker (rows) and discussion questions (columns). Triangulating across multiple data sources (notes, facilitator debriefs, artifacts, and direct audio) enhanced credibility, while having multiple notetakers enabled cross-validation. When discrepancies or insufficient detail arose, the transcripts were used to locate the relevant segment for direct audio review. Themes were derived through an iterative process: two team members (MMMB, BA) independently reviewed the summary matrix and developed preliminary thematic summaries for each discussion question. These were compared and refined through team discussion, with unresolved areas clarified through targeted audio review. Final themes were confirmed during whole-team consensus meetings.

To strengthen rigor, we drew on established qualitative quality criteria: multiple analysts independently reviewed the data to reduce individual bias (investigator triangulation); structured templates provided an audit trail to enhance dependability; and team discussions considered how researcher perspectives could influence interpretation, supporting reflexivity. While the study was not designed to achieve thematic saturation, consistent themes were observed across both workshops, and no substantially new perspectives emerged by the conclusion of data collection, reflecting thematic convergence appropriate to a rapid qualitative approach.

Results

A total of 24 adolescents (ages 16–23 years; 45% male, 55% female) from all twelve public senior high schools participated in the workshops. Results are presented by workshop phase, highlighting planning considerations, implementation adaptations, and key insights.

Workshop 1: adolescent perspectives on mental health

Workshop 1 explored how adolescents conceptualized mental health, including barriers and facilitators and perceived roles of schools in supporting wellbeing. Initial engagement was cautious, reflecting unfamiliarity with facilitators and uncertainty about expectations. Establishing a group definition of mental health fostered a shared conceptual understanding which helped facilitators pace the discussion and informed refinements for Workshop 2. However, some students still provided vague or tangential responses, suggesting variability in mental health literacy. Notably, many adolescents framed solutions at an individual level, focusing on personal resilience to overcome challenges rather than the role of systemic school or community supports.

Interactive case scenarios and cartoon visuals effectively depersonalized sensitive topics such as stigma, peer pressure, and cultural expectations, which created space for open dialogue. Gender-segregated small groups created safer spaces for sharing sensitive topics like stigma and gender-based cultural expectations. Engagement was strongest in small group settings and declined toward the session’s end, likely due to attention fatigue. Graduate student co-facilitators, who were closer in age to participants, played a key role in building rapport and sustaining participation.

Workshop 1 surfaced several contextual factors influencing adolescent mental health in schools and communities (Table 2). Barriers included limited mental health literacy, fear of judgement, stigma, and concerns about confidentiality from peers and staff. Adolescents described teachers as overly focused on academic achievement and often unaware of students’ mental health needs. Cultural influences also emerged: male students described community norms that normalized substance use, while female students cited gender roles that hindered open discussions about distress or coping strategies. These insights directly shaped Workshop 2, particularly by informing strategies to sustain engagement and to shift conversations from individual coping towards school- and system-level interventions. A detailed analysis of barriers and facilitators will be presented in a separate publication.

Workshop 2: co-designing school-based mental health strategies

Compared to Workshop 1, adolescents showed markedly higher engagement. The Mental Health Toolkit activity helped participants identify and evaluate individual strategies for managing stress and emotional challenges. The Time Travel exercise helped participants conceptualize preventive mental health by contrasting proactive and reactive strategies and reinforcing the value of addressing stress and well-being before escalation. The most frequently selected individual strategies were mentorship, fostering positive peer relationships, and increasing access to mental wellness education.

The School Mascot Activity generated the highest levels of creativity and enthusiasm. From this exercise, five priority school strategies emerged: (1) training and supporting teachers, (2) integrating mental health education into the curriculum, (3) implementing mentorship programs, (4) teaching positive thinking and mindfulness, and (5) using entertainment-based methods for mental health promotion. Combining engaging activities with guided choices allowed participants to share ideas freely while generating strategies that were both feasible and contextually relevant. Adolescents responded enthusiastically to this approach, but most ideas remained at a conceptual level, highlighting the need for further multilevel stakeholder co-design to refine, operationalize, and implement these strategies.

Post-workshop: formation of the adolescent advisory board

Following Workshop 2, participants, parents, and teachers independently reached out to the research team, expressing a strong interest in continued engagement. Recognizing the value of sustaining adolescent input, the research team formed an Adolescent Advisory Board to ensure sustained youth involvement in intervention co-design and refinement. Although not originally part of the study protocol, the advisory board emerged as an organic and valued extension of the participatory process.

The board meets quarterly through a combination of WhatsApp-based discussion threads and in-person meetings. At least two representatives participate in stakeholder meetings with schools and community partners, ensuring adolescents contribute directly to design decisions and provide structured feedback on feasibility and acceptability. In collaboration with Ghana Education Services and local community organizations, a rotation system is being developed to add new members and graduate departing students so that the board continues to reflect current perspectives.

The structure of the Adolescent Advisory Board is expected to evolve in response to members’ needs and priorities, but the iterative approach ensures that youth voices continue to inform the development, adaptation, and delivery of preventive strategies. For long-term sustainability, responsibility for convening and coordinating the board is being transitioned from the research team to a local community-based mental health organization. This transition aims to embed youth engagement within local structures and promote continued ownership beyond the research period.

Discussion

This study adapted co-design methods to engage adolescents in developing school-based mental health strategies in northern Ghana. While co-design is increasingly recognized as a valuable strategy for intervention development, its application with adolescents in LMICs requires adaptations to overcome sociocultural and structural participation barriers. Our findings contribute to the growing literature on adolescent co-design in resource-limited settings in three ways. First, we demonstrate that structured facilitation and choice-based engagement activities supported adolescents to articulate concrete intervention priorities. Second, contextually adapted co-design methods helped overcome sociocultural and cognitive barriers, fostering meaningful engagement in a setting where participatory methods are less familiar. Third, structured activities supported adolescents in reframing mental health from an individual responsibility toward a broader school- and community-level concern.

A structured model for adolescent co-design

Co-design methods with adolescents in LMICs must account for developmental, cultural, and structural factors that influence participation [64]. Building on our experience in northern Ghana, we developed a structured model (Fig. 4) that outlines key inputs, workshop components, and outputs that supported meaningful adolescent engagement. This model synthesizes practical adaptations from our study and offers a replicable structure for similar contexts.

Structured model for adolescent co-design in cross-cultural, low-resource contexts. This figure was developed through co-design workshops in northern Ghana and illustrates how key inputs and co-design workshop components supported meaningful adolescent engagement in school-based mental health strategy development. By addressing cultural and developmental barriers, the model provides a practical and replicable structure to guide youth co-design in LMIC contexts

Inputs included a guiding framework (CFIR), a deliberate focus on areas where youth input could meaningfully inform adaptation, and developmentally aligned workshop goals. The choice of facilitation team was equally important: young Ghanaian co-facilitators and a multidisciplinary team helped mitigate power hierarchies, promote trust, and foster open communication. Careful attention to pacing, sequencing, and continuity further sustained engagement while minimizing cognitive and emotional fatigue.

Within workshops, structured design features such as case-based prompts, guided choice activities, and visual analogies scaffolded participation. These tools helped adolescents navigate unfamiliar concepts, identify contextually relevant priorities, and reframe mental health not only as an individual responsibility but also as a collective challenge requiring structural and community supports. For example, the Mental Health Toolkit and Time Travel Analogy enabled adolescents to conceptualize preventive strategies in developmentally appropriate ways. Establishing a shared conceptual foundation at the outset of Workshop 1 was especially important in a setting with limited formal mental health education.

The model illustrates not only that structured co-design can generate concrete outputs, such as prototype strategies and early implementation insights, but also that it can cultivate youth leadership as a core outcome rather than an ancillary benefit. In our study, the Adolescent Advisory Board emerged organically from participants’ sustained interest, demonstrating how co-design can extend beyond discrete workshops into long-term leadership roles. By drawing attention to the process mechanisms (e.g., scaffolding, guided choice, facilitator composition) that made engagement both feasible and meaningful, our model advance co-design literature beyond documenting outputs. It provides a replicable structure to guide future youth co-design in LMIC settings.

Unique processes & adaptations for the Ghanaian sociocultural context

Engaging adolescents in co-design within LMICs presents distinct sociocultural challenges, including hierarchical power structures, gender norms, and stigma surrounding mental health [64, 65]. Our study identified several key adaptations that enhanced participation in northern Ghana. First, gender-segregated discussions created safe spaces for sensitive topics, allowing adolescents to speak more openly. Second, peer-driven engagement strategies such as group work and involvement of graduate student facilitators helped foster trust and relatability. Third, activities that leveraged school identity and pride, such as the Mascot exercise, tapped into familiar social structures to sustain motivation and creativity.

These adaptations align with broader evidence on youth engagement in LMICs, which emphasizes tailoring facilitation strategies to local norms to enhance feasibility and effectiveness [35]. Yet few co-design studies in these settings systematically document cultural and structural adaptations, making replication challenging [10, 12]. Our findings reinforce the importance of local expertise and multidisciplinary teams to navigate sociocultural barriers and co-create context-specific engagement strategies. In particular, young Ghanaian facilitators played a critical role in mitigating adult-youth hierarchies and fostering an environment where adolescent voices were viewed as legitimate and central to the design process. This intentional structuring of roles reduced status differences and promoted a more equitable and collaborative co-design experience.

Despite these efforts, power dynamics may have still influenced participation and interpretation. Adolescents may have moderated their responses to perceived expectations from facilitators or peer dynamics, particularly in larger or mixed-gender groups. Likewise, interpretation of workshop data was conducted by a research team that included both Ghanaian and international collaborators. While this diversity enhanced reflexivity during analysis, we also employed structured consensus-building techniques to minimize interpretive bias. Future co-design efforts could be further strengthened by involving adolescents directly in the analysis phase, which would promote greater authenticity, shared ownership, and equity in interpreting findings.

Adolescent-identified mental health priorities & conceptual shifts

Through our structured co-design process, adolescents identified five priority strategies for school-based mental health: teacher training, integration of mental health into the curriculum, implementation of mentorship programs, teaching of positive thinking and mindfulness, and use of entertainment-based methods for mental health education. These strategies emerged from choice-based activities that helped ensure selected strategies were both evidence-informed and contextually feasible. This prioritization process also encouraged reflection on the roles and responsibilities of schools and communities in supporting adolescent well-being.

Importantly, the workshops fostered a shift in how adolescents conceptualized mental health challenges and solutions. Initially, many described mental health as a matter of personal responsibility, emphasizing resilience and willpower as primary coping mechanisms. However, through scaffolded activities and contextual examples, adolescents reframed their understanding to include the roles that schools and communities can play in supporting their well-being. This shift is reflected in their selected interventions, which balance individual-level strategies such as mentorship and mindfulness with school-level approaches such as curriculum integration and teacher training. Prior research suggests that choice-based engagement models enhance adolescent decision-making by reducing cognitive load and making abstract concepts more accessible [66, 67]. Our findings extend this evidence by showing that structured facilitation can also shift perceptions of mental health from an individual burden to a shared responsibility. This highlights the potential of co-design to transform mental health narratives in LMIC settings.

Translating youth priorities into policy and practice

Findings from this study offer actionable insights for education policy and implementation strategies in Ghana. The five adolescent-prioritized strategies are both contextually grounded and aligned with existing mandates of Ghana Education Services’ Guidance and Counselling Units and Ghana Health Services’ School Health Education Program. Yet, despite supportive policies, implementation remains uneven and under-resourced across schools, reflecting the broader gaps documented in recent evaluations of adolescent health policy implementation in Ghana [5]. Youth-informed co-design may help close this gap by identifying feasible, acceptable, and developmentally appropriate strategies that fit within existing school infrastructure.

To ensure feasibility, the menu of strategies presented during workshops drew on evidence-based interventions shown to be effective in low-resource settings. In addition to youth input, we engaged school personnel, Ghana Education Services representatives, and community-based mental health leaders from the outset to ensure contextual fit and future implementation potential. Findings from the youth workshops have been shared with these stakeholders and will inform the next phase of co-design, which will convene multilevel partners to refine strategies and address systemic barriers to integration.

The organic emergence of the Adolescent Advisory Board signals strong youth interest in sustained involvement beyond ideation. While most co-design studies have emphasized product-oriented outputs, our findings demonstrate that intentional facilitation strategies can also generate sustained interest and capacity for adolescent co-leadership. We have since institutionalized the board’s role through continued meetings and their inclusion in broader stakeholder convenings. Although Ghana Education Services policy frameworks do not yet formalize adolescent leadership in program design or evaluation, mechanisms such as school-based youth mental health committees or regional youth councils could build on this model to promote youth co-leadership and accountability. Future research should examine how adolescent participation can be embedded within governance structures to support long-term implementation, scale-up, and system responsiveness.

Study limitations

This study has several limitations. First, our recruitment approach may have introduced selection bias, as adolescents were nominated by school personnel based on communication ability and willingness to discuss mental health. These criteria helped to avoid including students who might feel uncomfortable with sensitive discussions but may have favored those who were more verbally fluent, engaged, or trusted by staff. As a result, perspectives of youth who are more socially withdrawn, academically struggling, or at greater risk of mental health challenges may be underrepresented. Second, the focus on in-school youth overlooks out-of-school adolescents, whose views on preventive interventions and responses to co-design may differ. Third, our study population was slightly older than expected. While the expected enrollment age for senior high school in Ghana is 15 to 17 years, delayed school entry is common, especially in rural areas, and often results in students over 18 years. Because this enrollment pattern is common across Ghana, it likely does not limit the applicability of our findings to other senior high schools in similar settings.

Conclusions

This study demonstrates how HCD can be strategically adapted to enhance adolescent participation in the development of school-based mental health interventions in LMICs. By integrating structured facilitation, developmentally aligned tools, peer-driven strategies, and culturally responsive adaptations, we developed a replicable model of adolescent co-design that addresses both cognitive and sociocultural barriers to engagement. A key contribution is the documentation of a structured, co-design model that can guide future implementation across other resource-limited settings.

Future research should explore the long-term impact of adolescent engagement on implementation outcomes, assessing whether structured youth participation influences fidelity, scalability, and real-world adoption. Expanding these methods to reach out-of-school youth and marginalized populations will be essential to ensure broader inclusivity. To support integration into national mental health and education systems, future work must also examine how structured co-design can be feasibly embedded into routine policy and practice in low-resource schools to promote sustainability at scale. Advancing youth-inclusive implementation strategies has the potential to strengthen mental health systems and improve outcomes for adolescents by ensuring interventions are both contextually relevant and youth-centered.
