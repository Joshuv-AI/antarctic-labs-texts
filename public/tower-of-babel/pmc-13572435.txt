TogoPhosTAC as a delivery-ready platform for targeted protein dephosphorylation
Authors: Yu-Yu Chen, Dong-Ting Ke, Yen-Chun Lee, Hong-Ru Chen, Hsiang-Ying Lin, Chun-Hsin Yeh, Yu-Ching Ko, Meng-Han Tu, Chien-Hung Yu, Zhenyi Hu, Po-Han Chen
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractPhosphorylation-targeting chimeras (PhosTACs) enable targeted protein dephosphorylation by recruiting phosphatases through induced proximity. However, the direct recruitment of phosphatase subunits or holoenzymes with small molecules remains challenging, as suitable ligands are scarce and often compromise enzymatic activity or cellular function. Here, we present togoPhosTAC, a hybrid modality that integrates a small-molecule PhosTAC, an engineered FKBP12F36V-phosphatase, and a lipid nanoparticle delivery system. This strategy allows delivery of preassembled PhosTAC–FKBP12F36V-phosphatase complexes or PhosTAC–phosphatase mRNA, enabling rapid and efficient intracellular dephosphorylation. We demonstrate that togoPhosTAC can selectively dephosphorylate EGFR, α-synuclein, and tau in biological contexts, providing a versatile strategy that circumvents the need for genetically engineered phosphatases. We also find togoPhosTAC further enhances tau dephosphorylation as well as its disaggregation in cellulo. Importantly, intrahippocampal or intranasal delivery of togoPhosTAC in PS19 tau transgenic male mice leads to a marked reduction in pathological tau phosphorylation across multiple sites (Ser202, Thr205, Thr231, Ser396, and Ser404), decreases pathological tau burden in related brain regions, and improves Alzheimer’s disease–related behavioral deficits. Together, these findings establish a versatile and generalizable approach for precise protein dephosphorylation in disease-relevant systems, overcoming key limitations in phosphatase-recruiting drug discovery.Subject terms: Phosphorylation, Animal disease models, Phosphoproteins, Drug delivery, Protein design

Introduction

Protein phosphorylation on Ser, Thr, or Tyr residues is a dynamic and reversible process regulated by kinases and phosphatases. Contrary to the actions of the kinases, the removal of phosphoryl groups is governed by protein phosphatases that are classified into several groups, including protein Ser/Thr phosphatases and protein Tyr phosphatases. As phosphorylation dynamically regulates protein–protein interactions and can influence protein aggregation, dysregulated protein phosphorylation is implicated in numerous diseases, For instance, mutations in receptor tyrosine kinases (RTKs), such as the epidermal growth factor receptor (EGFR), often disrupt phosphorylation patterns in lung tissue, promoting tumor progression and conferring resistance to RTK-targeted therapies1,2. On the other hand, Alzheimer’s (AD) and Parkinson’s (PD) diseases are correlated with tau and α-synuclein hyperphosphorylation, respectively, eventually leading to neurodegeneration3–9.

Given that dysregulated phosphorylation is involved in many pathophysiological conditions, manipulating kinase or phosphatase activity has been an attractive direction for the pharmaceutical industry. As of 2026, over 90 kinase inhibitors have been approved by the U.S. Food and Drug Administration (FDA), with more than half targeting RTKs or other TKs10,11. Many RTK inhibitors (RTKi) are potent and effective in inhibiting tyrosine kinases and their downstream pathways, however, achieving specificity remains a major challenge since most are ATP-competitive molecules with inherent limitations in selectively targeting individual kinases. On the contrary, ligand development for phosphatases has only limited breakthroughs for the past few decades. This is partly due to phosphatases were generally considered promiscuous toward protein substrates, the homogeneity and positively charged nature among phosphatases’ active sites12,13, which inevitably limits the bioavailability of potential phosphatase drugs. Nonetheless, accumulating evidence has demonstrated clinical benefits of inhibiting or activating phosphatase activities in a variety of disease settings. For example, inhibiting the tyrosine phosphatase SHP2 activity reduces RTK-mediated oncogenesis14, while several allosteric inhibitors for SHP2 have already advanced into clinical trials15. Thus, developing compounds or tools that selectively modulate phosphatase activity represents a promising strategy for clinical applications.

Heterobifunctional molecules are emerging drug modalities that have been applied to recruit multiple proteins simultaneously via induced proximity to achieve biological effects. In one prominent example, proteolysis targeting chimera (PROTAC) recruits an E3 ubiquitin ligase to achieve polyubiquitination of the targeted protein and cause irreversible protein degradation16–20. Analogous to the PROTAC design, phosphorylation targeting chimera (PhosTAC) engages the phosphatase for proximity-induced protein dephosphorylation13,21–26. This approach employs bifunctional molecules to recruit a phosphatase to a protein of interest (POI), thereby reducing the chance of off-target effects. For example, PP2A-Bα or PP1C recruiting peptides have been used to generate DEPTACs for targeted tau dephosphorylation27,28, while PP5 PHORC combines PP5 activators with an ASK1 inhibitor or tau binders to selectively dephosphorylate p-ASK1T838 and p-Tau, respectively29,30.

In our early PhosTACs studies, a chemical-induced proximity approach was applied to prove the concept of this modality. In particular, we used the dTAG FKBP12F36V ligands- AP1867, which has the drug-like properties, to recruit the FKBP12F36V tagged-PP2AA or PTPN222–24. By genetically engineering FKBP12F36V–phosphatase fusion phosphatases, we bypassed the need to identify phosphatase-binding ligands on one sides of the PhosTAC molecules, while ligands targeting EGFR, HaloTag7-tagged fusion proteins, or tau were incorporated on the opposite sides to enable induced proximity between the engineered phosphatases and their substrates. Here, PTPN2 functions as an endogenous regulator of EGFR31, whereas PP2AA represents the scaffold (A) subunit of PP2A. By recruiting endogenous regulatory (B) and catalytic (C) subunits, the intracellular assembly of the complete PP2A holoenzyme enables dephosphorylation of α-synuclein and tau32,33.

Advances in mRNA vaccines and de novo protein design have expanded the scope of biologic drug discovery. Indeed, similar approaches have been applied for targeted protein degradation, such as bioPROTACs and mRNA PROTACs34–36. We reasoned that the FKBP12F36V fusion protein and its ligand AP1867 could be used as a modality adapter in a generalizable system connecting the POI and phosphatases. In addition, the FKBP12F36V-AP1867 connection system not only can be applied in cell lines with encoded DNA sequences but also as an engineered and deliverable protein or mRNA design. In other words, we have transformed the DNA-encoded FKBP12F36V fusion system into adaptable mRNA and protein-based modalities named− togoPhosTAC.

In this study, we introduce togoPhosTAC, a pre-assembled complex of PhosTAC small molecules with FKBP12F36V-tagged phosphatase proteins or their mRNAs, encapsulated in nanoparticles for intracellular delivery. This strategy enables efficient and selective dephosphorylation of disease-relevant proteins, including EGFR, α-synuclein, and tau, without the need for genetically engineered phosphatases. We demonstrate that the protein-based modality achieves rapid dephosphorylation at nanomolar concentrations in cellular systems, while the mRNA-based approach provides sustained activity and translational potential in an Alzheimer’s disease mouse model. Collectively, our findings establish togoPhosTAC as a versatile and adaptable platform for targeted protein dephosphorylation, offering an alternative to conventional phosphatase ligand discovery and a foundation for next-generation strategies in precision protein modulation.

Results

Conceptual design of togoPhosTACs

The FKBP12F36V fusion protein/dTAG system has been widely applied in targeted protein degradation and dephosphorylation22–24,37–39. We adapted the dTAG system into our togoPhosTAC concept. In the togoPhosTAC system, instead of delivering the DNA construct encoding FKBP12F36V fusion phosphatase system, we tested the delivery of a pre-fused small molecule and protein complex encapsulated in the nanoparticle for TPDephos (Fig. 1a) and the delivery of small molecules combined with mRNA encoding FKBP12F36V-PP2AA (Fig. 1b). Herein, PTPN2 and PP2AA were fused to FKBP12F36V to generate fusion phosphatase, and three previously reported PhosTAC small molecules, GePhos1, PhosTAC7, and Tau 2–8 were utilized to target EGFR, Halotag7-tagged tau/α-synuclein, and tau, respectively (Fig. 1c)22–24. The GePhos1 was designed to target EGFR via a gefitinib warhead, whereas PhosTAC7 binds to Halotag7 protein through a chloroalkane terminal, which was subsequently replaced with the tau tracer PI-2620 that possesses high affinity to both mixed 3 R/4 R or 4 R tau isoforms40–42, yielding Tau 2–8 that directly targets tau proteins. The pre-fused PhosTAC small molecules and phosphatase complexes were prepared and delivered by lipid-based nanoparticles such as PULSin® and 80-O14B, while the delivery of small molecules with mRNA was achieved using jetMESSENGER® for in-cellulo applications and in vivo-jetRNA® for in vivo administration. The potency and feasibility of the togoPhosTAC concept were demonstrated via the dephosphorylation efficiency of our proteins of interest.

Pre-fused togoPhosTAC with GePhos1 dephosphorylates EGFR and downstream signal in cellulo

The recombinant 6xHis-FKBP12F36V-PTPN2 was purified using the nickel-nitrilotriacetic acid (Ni-NTA) resin and size-exclusion chromatography (SEC) (Fig. 2b). After validating the expression with Coomassie blue staining and Western blot (WB) (Supplementary Fig. 1a, b), the phosphatase activity was confirmed with the p-nitrophenyl phosphate (pNPP) assay. The FKBP12F36V-PTPN2 showed robust activity, while the tyrosine phosphatase inhibitor vanadate fully suppresses its activity (Fig. 2c).

Next, we tested the delivery efficiency with different dosages of PULSin®, a commercially available cationic amphiphile molecule that can deliver proteins into cells. We found a ratio of 1 μg (16 nM) of protein to 3 μL of PULSin® showed the most robust FKBP12F36V-PTPN2 delivery into cells (Supplementary Fig. 1c). We then pre-fused the GePhos1 and FKBP12F36V-PTPN2 and encapsulated the pre-fused complex with PULSin®.

For targeted dephosphorylation of wild-type EGFR in HeLa cells, 2 μg (33 nM) of FKBP12F36V-PTPN2 was first mixed with DMSO or 100 nM GePhos1 and then encapsulated with 6 μL PULSin® reagent for delivery. Two hours after delivering the pre-fused cargos, EGF (100 ng mL−1) was added to initiate tyrosine phosphorylation of EGFR. The results showed significantly reduced Y1068 EGFR phosphorylation level when the pre-fused GePhos1 and FKBP12F36V-PTPN2 were co-delivered (Fig. 2d). The comparatively lower total EGFR levels in groups 1 and 2 may result from a high amount of EGF (100 ng mL−1) stimulation, a condition known to promote EGFR phosphorylation followed by protein endocytosis, ubiquitination, and degradation43,44, while the co-delivery of FKBP12F36V-PTPN2 and GePhos1 in group 3 restored total EGFR levels, further demonstrating successful dephosphorylation under togoPhosTAC treatment. Also, the ectopically delivered FKBP12F36V-PTPN2 was observed within the cell lysates using anti-FKBP12 antibody, which detects the endogenous FKBP12 (at 12 kDa) and the ectopically delivered fusion-protein at the proper molecular weight (61 kDa). Next, we compared the dephosphorylation efficiency when delivering a recombinant PTPN2 protein with (61 kDa) or without (49 kDa) the FKBP12F36V tag (Fig. 2e). The recombinant PTPN2 without FKBP12F36V tag was also purified and validated with a pNPP assay to justify its activity (Supplementary Figs. 1d, f). We found delivering the recruitable FKBP12F36V-PTPN2 demonstrated significantly higher ability in dephosphorylating EGFR and reduced its downstream AKT phosphorylation (Fig. 2e, f), suggesting that the FKBP12F36V tag and the AP1867 system can locate the engineered FKBP12F36V-PTPN2 more efficiently to EGFR for dephosphorylation. Applying the same strategy to the HCC827 EGFR mutant cell line also showed consistent results on the mutant EGFR. We observed robust FKBP12F36V-PTPN2 delivery and EGFR dephosphorylation on Y1068 in the presence of EGF stimulation (Fig. 2g). We further examined the downstream signaling of the EGFR pathway in the presence of togoPhosTAC. Consistent with EGFR dephosphorylation, phosphorylation on SHC and GAB1 were significantly reduced (Fig. 2h), suggesting the potential of EGFR togoPhosTAC in manipulating signaling events.

Pre-fused togoPhosTAC dephosphorylates Halotag7-tagged tau and α-synuclein in cellulo

To expand our application for targeted protein dephosphorylation, we also tested the feasibility of togoPhosTAC in the FKBP12F36V-PP2AA/PhosTAC7/tau system (Fig. 3a)23. Similarly, the recombinant phosphatase scaffold subunit 6xHis-FKBP12F36V-PP2AA protein was generated from E. coli and purified via Ni-NTA resin and SEC, pure and soluble recombinant 6xHis-FKBP12F36V-PP2AA proteins were collected and validated by Coomassie blue stain and WB (Fig. 3b–d, red box). To test other lipid-based molecules for effective protein delivery, we used 80-O14B (Supplementary Fig. 2a), which was reported to deliver CRISPR-Cas9 proteins and pre-fused PROTACs45–47. 80-O14B is a cationic molecule and bio-reducible lipid that can encapsulate high molecular weight and negative charge proteins45. We proposed that 80-O14B can encapsulate PhosTAC molecules and FKBP12F36V-PP2AA and deliver the pre-fused complex into cells for intracellular targeted protein dephosphorylation.

We tested the feasibility of tau togoPhosTAC by adopting the Halotag7-2N4R/2N3R tau expression system described in the previous study23. Briefly, Halotag7-2N4R/2N3R tau was cloned and inserted into the 293FlpIn-Trex cells, in which the expression of Halotag7-2N4R-mClover/2N3R-mRuby2 tau can then be induced by tetracycline. Note that we are using the stable cell line that only expresses the protein substrate tau (Halotag7-2N4R-mClover/2N3R-mRuby2 tau, referred to as Halotag-2N4R/2N3R tau or tau) without cellular FKBP12F36V-PP2AA. Ideally, FKBP12F36V-PP2AA with the adaptable PP2AA recruiting handle (FKBP12F36V) will be pre-fused with the PhosTAC7 small molecules in the nanoparticle system and delivered into cells expressing Halotag-2N4R/2N3R tau. As expected, when 5 μg (61 nM) FKBP12F36V-PP2AA and 500 nM PhosTAC7 were delivered into the target cells, we observed tau dephosphorylation at Thr231 in a dose-dependent manner using 25 μg (35 μM) of 80-O14B and further augmented at 50 μg (70 μM) (Fig. 3e). Importantly, we found PhosTAC7 and the delivered FKBP12F36V-PP2AA proteins formed a ternary complex with tau protein within cells, indicating successful delivery of both small molecules and FKBP12F36V-PP2AA into the target cells. This ternary complex also enhanced the assembly of the catalytic subunit PP2AC to tau, which provides the catalytic activity of the PP2A holoenzyme (Fig. 3f). With the optimal dose of 80-O14B for tau dephosphorylation, we next tested different PhosTAC7 concentrations. We found tau phosphorylation at Thr231 was completely abolished at 100 nM of PhosTAC7, while the declining efficacy was observed when PhosTAC7 concentration exceeded 500 nM (Fig. 3g). This observation reflects the “hook effect” associated with ternary complex formation48, which typically yields a bell-shaped response curve—achieving maximal efficacy at an optimal drug concentration but declining toward baseline at higher concentrations49. This finding demonstrated that togoPhosTAC operates through a catalytic, ternary complex–driven mechanism, and then we would like to identify the lowest effective concentration of PhosTAC7 in the togoPhosTAC system. To our surprise, PhosTAC7 significantly dephosphorylates tau at 5 nM and reaches maximum activity (Dephosmax = 99%) at 100 nM for pT231 residues (Fig. 3h). While the other phosphorylation site Thr181 is also significantly dephosphorylated of more than 50% (Fig. 3h). Meanwhile, the expression of the delivered recombinant FKBP12F36V-PP2AA can be observed in the same cell lysates as probed by the anti-FKBP12 antibody at the predicted molecular weight (81 kDa). It is worth noting that in our previous research, in which PhosTAC7 was evaluated in cell lines expressing both Halotag-2N4R/2N3R tau and FKBP12F36V-PP2AA, maximum PhosTAC7 activity was observed between 250 to 1000 nM23. The togoPhosTAC concept revealed that the pre-fused small molecule and protein complex may increase targeted protein dephosphorylation efficiency and lower the required PhosTAC7 dosage, potentially due to the pre-formation of the PhosTAC7 and FKBP12F36V-phosphatase increases the chance to assembly a ternary complex with tau.

In another example, we adopted togoPhosTAC to test targeted α-synuclein dephosphorylation, given phosphorylation at Ser129 is correlated with α-synuclein aggregation (Supplementary Fig. 2b)50. As side-by-side control, we expressed α-synuclein A53T-mClover, α-synuclein A53T-mRuby2-HaloTag7 and FKBP12F36V-PP2AA into the HEK 293 Flp-In Trex cells. The engineered cells were treated with PhosTAC7 to evaluate dephosphorylation potency. We found 100 and 500 nM PhosTAC7 can reproducibly dephosphorylate Ser129 of α-synuclein for approximately 40% (Supplementary Fig. 2c). On the other hand, we encapsulated 5 μg (61 nM) FKBP12F36V-PP2AA and various amounts of PhosTAC7 with 50 μg (70 μM) of 80-O14B. After confirming the delivery of recombinant FKBP12F36V-PP2AA, we found the pre-fused togoPhosTAC reduces 45% of pSer129 with only 10 nM of PhosTAC7 (Supplementary Fig. 2d). Consistently with the tau togoPhosTAC results, the PhosTAC7 dosage in the pre-fused system was 10-fold lower than that of the treatment in the engineered cell line (Supplementary Fig. 2c).

In sum, we have demonstrated that tau and α-synuclein can be significantly dephosphorylated with the togoPhosTAC system using only 5 and 10 nM of PhosTAC7 molecules, respectively, suggesting that we may raise the potency of PhosTAC small molecules by incorporating the pre-formed binary complex in the togoPhosTAC treatment.

Pre-fused Tau 2-8 in togoPhosTAC system dephosphorylates and reduces aggregation of tau

To expand the application potential of the togoPhosTAC system, we next applied Tau 2–8 small molecules to directly target tau protein(Fig. 4a)23. Initially, 500 nM Tau 2–8 were pre-fused with 5 μg (61 nM) of FKBP12F36V-PP2AA and encapsulated by 50 μg (70 μM) of 80-O14B to evaluate tau dephosphorylation. Compared with DMSO or Tau 2–8 alone, we found Tau 2-8 togoPhosTACs significantly reduced tau phosphorylation at pT231 (Fig. 4b). Similarly, tau dephosphorylation can be observed using as low as 100 nM of Tau 2–8 molecules (Fig. 4c). Note that Tau 2–8 was only effective above 1 μM when recruiting FKBP12F36V-PP2AA in the engineered cell lines, the pre-fused togoPhosTAC system demonstrated effective tau dephosphorylation at sub-micromolar range23. By the Phos-tagTM SDS-PAGE, which traps the phosphorylated proteins, we observed increased tau species mobility in the Tau 2-8 togoPhosTAC groups compared with Tau 2–8 treatment alone (Fig. 4d), suggesting phosphorylated tau species were reduced in the togoPhosTAC-treated group irrespective of the phosphorylation sites. In addition, the delivery of Tau 2–8 with FKBP12F36V-PP2AA did not affect the phosphorylation of AKT, which is a canonical substrate of PP2A, suggesting Tau 2-8 directs FKBP12F36V-PP2AA to tau for targeted dephosphorylation (Supplementary Fig. 3a)51,52.

To understand whether togoPhosTAC-mediated tau dephosphorylation affects tau aggregation, we next purified 0N4R tau proteins as seeds to initiate tau aggregation in our system (Supplementary Figs. 3b, c)53. The purified 0N4R tau protein was incubated with heparin for 24 to 72 h, and the formation of tau aggregated seeds was monitored via fluorescence signal generated after binding of Thioflavin T (ThT) in vitro (Fig. 4e). Compared with the no-seeding group, the addition of tau seeds into the reporter cells that express 2N4R tau-mClover and 2N3R tau-mRuby increases fluorescent puncta formation. The visualization of green and red signals was derived from mClover (2N4R tau) and mRuby2 (2N3R tau), respectively, whereas the yellow-like puncta reflected the co-localization of proteins labeled with different fluorescent reporters, suggesting tau seeds successfully initiated tau aggregation (Fig. 4f). After initiating tau aggregation, Tau 2-8 and FKBP12F36V-PP2AA togoPhosTAC were treated and compared with the seeds-only control group. Indeed, togoPhosTAC significantly reduces tau puncta formation (Fig. 4g, h). In sum, our data suggests targeted tau dephosphorylation by togoPhosTACs is correlated with reduced tau aggregation.

mRNA togoPhosTAC system dephosphorylates tau in cellulo

With the success of co-delivering the engineered phosphatase proteins and PhosTACs for targeted protein dephosphorylation, we next test if delivery of an engineered phosphatase mRNA dephosphorylates tau as well (Fig. 5a). The FKBP12F36V-PP2AA construct was sub-cloned into an engineered vector capable of in vitro transcription (IVT). To maximize the mRNA stability, a CleanCap® is added to the 5’end of the transcribed mRNA, and 50 adenosine nucleotides were included in the 3’tail of the mRNA. In addition, pseudouridine was used instead of uridine during mRNA synthesis. The mRNA integrity was confirmed via gel electrophoresis (Fig. 5a). The purified mRNA and Tau 2-8 were then co-delivered via a commercially available lipid-based nanoparticle. After the mRNA and Tau 2-8 delivery, the FKBP12F36V-PP2AA protein expression can be detected as early as 1 h and sustained as long as 72 h (Fig. 5b). Next, we tested if mRNA and Tau 2-8 delivery dephosphorylates tau as soon as the FKBP12F36V-PP2AA protein is expressed. We found that significant reduced tau phosphorylation at both T181 and T231 residues as early as 6 h of delivery, and 50% tau dephosphorylation with only 100 nM of Tau 2-8 molecules (Fig. 5c and Supplementary Fig. 4a). Furthermore, significant tau dephosphorylation can still be observed as long as 48 and 54 hours after mRNA delivery with low Tau 2-8 dosages (Fig. 5d, e). We also observed that only 10 nM of Tau 2-8 was sufficient to achieve approximately 50% reduction of the pT231 signal compared with the DMSO control after 48 hours of delivery (Fig. 5d), as this effect was not achieved by expression of FKBP12 F36V–PP2AA alone (Supplementary Fig. 4b). This demonstrates that Tau 2-8 can function as a guide at nanomolar concentrations, enabling targeted tau dephosphorylation by directing FKBP12 F36V–PP2AA to the target. In sum, our mRNA PhosTAC modality provides another useful tool for targeted intracellular protein editing.

We next investigated the responsiveness of mRNA togoPhosTAC in the AD-relevant PS19 transgenic mice. The PS19 mouse model is widely used in tauopathy research, as it expresses human tau (1N4R isoform) carrying the P301S mutation and develops neurofibrillary tangles and exhibits tauopathy-related neurodegeneration beginning as early as 6 months of age54,55. We used stereotaxic coordinates to target the right dorsal hippocampus of 8-month-old PS19 mice, which had developed tau pathology9. To minimize sex-related variability, only male mice were used in these experiments. Based on our prior results showing that expression of FKBP12F36V–PP2AA alone or treatment with Tau 2-8 alone is insufficient to achieve efficient tau dephosphorylation, we focused the in vivo comparison on the most informative conditions for evaluating the induced-proximity mechanism. Accordingly, we compared DMSO vehicle control, Tau 2-8 alone, and Tau 2-8 combined with mRNA encoding FKBP12F36V–PP2AA in the following animal study.

Three 8-month-old PS19 mice per group were treated with a single dosage of DMSO vehicle control, Tau 2-8 alone, or Tau 2-8 combined with mRNA encoding FKBP12F36V-PP2AA (Tau 2-8 + mRNA). Three days after the treatment course, the hippocampus was collected to first examine the neuronal integrity and potential cell death56. Of note, mice treated with DMSO vehicle, Tau 2–8 alone, or mRNA togoPhosTAC (Tau 2-8 + mRNA) all exhibited normal activity and grooming behavior, with no signs of distress or toxicity. No evidence of neuronal loss was observed, as indicated by preserved microtubule-associated protein 2 (MAP2) expression. Furthermore, apoptosis was not detected, as total caspase-3 levels remained unchanged and cleaved caspase-3 was absent on WB. (Fig. 6c).

Following this, we examined the efficacy of togoPhosTAC in targeted tau dephosphorylation in the PS19 mice hippocampus. First, we found a significant reduction at pT231 and significant dephosphorylation at the AT8 epitope (pSer202/pThr205), the canonical AD pathological marker, while we did not observe a significant reduction of the total tau protein (Fig. 6d)57. Moreover, we found the overall level of tau phosphorylation was reduced by single dosage mRNA togoPhosTAC (Tau 2-8 + mRNA) compared with the vehicle or Tau 2-8 only group, as visualized by the faster migration pattern via the Phos-tag™ SDS-PAGE, which trapped the hyperphosphorylated protein (Fig. 6e). Aside from tau dephosphorylation, co-delivery of Tau 2-8 with FKBP12F36V-PP2AA mRNA did not alter the mobility of canonical PP2A substrates including neurofilament-L and AKT in the Phos-tag™ SDS-PAGE51,52,58, indicating that Tau 2-8 selectively directs FKBP12F36V-PP2AA to tau for targeted dephosphorylation, thereby alleviating concerns regarding potential off-target effects (Fig. 6e). Taken together, these results demonstrate that togoPhosTAC can be delivered into the hippocampus and is effective in reducing pathological tau phosphorylation.

Intranasal delivery of mRNA togoPhosTAC reduces pathological tau phosphorylation and alleviates behavioral deficits

Having established that direct hippocampal delivery effectively induces tau dephosphorylation without detectable toxicity, we next investigated whether the mRNA togoPhosTAC system could be delivered using a non-invasive strategy. To this end, we examined intranasal administration in 8-month-old PS19 mice (Fig. 7a). Intranasal delivery did not alter neuronal integrity markers, including MAP2, caspase-3, and PARP1, indicating that the treatment did not induce detectable neuronal damage (Fig. 7b). Importantly, tau phosphorylation was significantly reduced in treated mice, as evidenced by decreased levels of AT8 and pT231 in the olfactory bulb, while total tau levels remained unchanged (Fig. 7c). Consistent with these biochemical findings, immunofluorescence staining revealed reduced pathological tau burden across multiple brain regions. Representative images of AT8, NeuN (RNA-binding protein fox-1 homolog 3, RBFOX3), and DAPI staining showed decreased AT8 immunoreactivity in the olfactory bulb, cortex, and hippocampus of treated mice. Quantitative analysis confirmed a significant reduction in AT8 mean fluorescence intensity across these regions (Fig. 7d, e).

To further assess the effects of intranasal togoPhosTAC delivery on tau phosphorylation, we examined additional phosphorylation sites and conformational tau pathology. WB analysis showed that phosphorylation at S396 and S404 was significantly reduced following treatment compared with the vehicle or Tau 2-8 alone group (Supplementary Fig. 5a-c)59. We next assessed pathological tau conformation using the MC1 antibody60. Immunofluorescence analysis revealed comparable MC1 staining across the olfactory bulb, cortex, and hippocampus, with no significant differences in mean fluorescence intensity between groups (Supplementary Fig. 5d, e). Although multiple tau phosphorylation epitopes (AT8, pS396, and pS404) were reduced following treatment, MC1 immunoreactivity remained unchanged, suggesting that the intervention primarily attenuates tau phosphorylation before detectable changes in tau conformational pathology occur.

We next examined whether the biochemical improvement in tau pathology translated into functional benefits. Behavioral assessments using the elevated plus maze (EPM) to evaluate anxiety-related behavior, novel object recognition (NOR), and Morris water maze (MWM) paradigms to assess short-term and spatial memory demonstrated behavioral improvements in treated mice61–63. Behavioral experiments were performed with n = 5 (vehicle), n = 5 (Tau 2-8 alone), and n = 6 (Tau 2-8 + mRNA) mice per group. Intranasal mRNA togoPhosTAC (Tau 2-8 + mRNA) group increased the time spent in the open arms of the EPM and improved recognition memory in the NOR task compared with the vehicle groups. In the MWM task, treated mice exhibited increased time spent in the target quadrant during the probe test, suggesting improved spatial memory retention (Fig. 7f–k). Together, these findings demonstrate that the mRNA-based togoPhosTAC system can be delivered to the brain, selectively reduce pathological tau phosphorylation, and further rescue behavioral deficits in PS19 mice.

Discussion

Targeted protein modification via bifunctional molecules or induced proximity has become an emerging tool for academic research and is increasingly being explored as a therapeutic modality. However, discovering recruiting elements for a PTM modifier remains technically challenging. In fact, one of the major breakthroughs for PROTAC development is the discovery of small-molecule CRBN and VHL ligands. Similarly, the key barrier for targeted protein modifications such as phosphorylation, dephosphorylation, O-GlcNAcylation, and acetylation is to identify an allosteric activator, a recruitable handle, or a binder for the enzymes without compromising their activity64. To date, affinity-based small molecules have shown limited progress compared with activity-based inhibitors.

Alternatively, togoPhosTAC represents an adaptable biological modality that integrates bifunctional molecules with protein and mRNA delivery systems. By adapting the original FKBP12F36V-based PhosTAC system, we developed a hybrid and versatile small molecule–biologic platform for TPDephos, thereby addressing current challenges in ligand identification and overcoming limitations in phosphatase drug discovery, which is associated with its nature of targeting phosphatase active sites12. By using two protein delivery systems, PULSin® and 80 O-14B, we demonstrate that co-delivery of a directed FKBP12F36V-phosphatase with PhosTAC molecules induces rapid dephosphorylation of EGFR, α-synuclein, and tau (Figs. 2–4 and Supplementary Fig. 2).

In addition, we adapted the system into an mRNA-based platform to broaden its applicability. We first demonstrated a sustained effect of mRNA togoPhosTAC in cellulo (Fig. 5), establishing the foundation for co-delivery of mRNA and PhosTAC molecules. We then targeted AD–related tau hyperphosphorylation by applying the Tau 2-8 based system in PS19 mice to demonstrate in vivo feasibility (Fig. 6). We showed that mRNA togoPhosTAC can be delivered into the hippocampus via intrahippocampal injection, effectively reducing pathological tau phosphorylation (Fig. 6d) without evidence of neuronal loss or apoptosis (Fig. 6c). Notably, phosphorylation-specific migration pattern of canonical PP2A substrates, including neurofilament-L and AKT, remained largely unchanged (Fig. 6e), indicating that the system may regulate PP2A activity in a directed manner. This preferential reduction in tau phosphorylation is likely driven by Tau 2-8 mediated recruitment of FKBP12F36V-PP2AA to tau, thereby concentrating PP2A phosphatase activity at the target protein.

To enhance translational potential, we demonstrated that mRNA togoPhosTAC can be effectively delivered to the brain via a non-invasive intranasal route as a therapeutic module (Fig. 7). This delivery not only achieves targeted reduction of pathological tau phosphorylation, including T231 and the AT8 epitope, with additional decreases at S396 and S404 (Fig. 7c and Supplementary Fig. 5a–c), but also alleviates several behavioral deficits in PS19 mice, including anxiety-like behavior, short-term and spatial memory, as assessed by EPM, NOR, and MWM—standard behavioral paradigms in preclinical Alzheimer’s disease studies (Fig. 7f–k). Notably, the treatment also significantly reduces AT8 immunoreactivity across the olfactory bulb, cortex, and hippocampus (Fig. 7d, e), consistent with the established transport of intranasally delivered molecules into the central nervous system via the olfactory and trigeminal nerve pathways65,66. The prominent reduction of pathological tau in the olfactory bulb suggests that the therapeutic module likely enters the brain via the olfactory epithelium and subsequently spreads to connected cortical and hippocampal circuits65,67. Such propagation along olfactory-cortical pathways may facilitate broader modulation of tau pathology across limbic and cortical networks68,69.

However, while the present findings support the feasibility of intranasal delivery for CNS-targeted dephosphorylation, several translational limitations must be acknowledged. Critically, the olfactory epithelium in rats and mice constitutes a substantially larger proportion of the nasal cavity volume compared to that in humans70. This anatomical discrepancy may lead to an overestimation of the efficiency of olfactory-mediated brain delivery observed in preclinical models, highlighting that rodent systems should be considered as preliminary models for evaluating intranasal delivery feasibility in vivo65,71. Despite growing interest in intranasal nanoparticle delivery, achieving uniform distribution throughout the brain remains challenging. Many studies report preferential accumulation in the olfactory bulb and pons, consistent with transport via olfactory and trigeminal nerve pathways. In contrast, the extent to which cerebrospinal fluid or vascular pathways contribute to broader brain distribution has yet to be fully elucidated66,72. An in-depth understanding of delivery pathways and their contributions, alongside validation in large-animal models, organoids, or even AI-driven systems, will be needed to narrow the gap between preclinical research and clinical applications71–74.

As for the mechanistic focus, our findings establish togoPhosTAC as a plug-and-play FKBP12F36V/dTAG-based proximity platform for the modular manipulation of protein activity. Like a universal power adapter, togoPhosTAC allows different effectors to be “plugged in” without altering the basic delivery framework. This capability extends across multiple modalities, including OGTACs for targeted O-GlcNAcylation75, AceTAG for inducible acetylation76, NICE systems for transcriptional control77, FKBP12F36V-E3-based PROTAC analogs37, other emerging bump-and-hole technologies, and de novo designed protein binders. Critically, many proximity systems depend on genetic engineering to express effector proteins, a requirement that can limit translation into complex or in vivo models. Our togoPhosTAC system enables intracellular protein or mRNA delivery with tunable dosage and timing, thus bypassing DNA-based expression requirements.

Other than expanding chemical biology applications, we believe our current approach has additional merits, especially in the phosphatase-impaired environment. Abnormal phosphatase activity has been observed in many diseases, recruiting an impaired phosphatase inevitably limits the potential of PhosTAC application in the disease environment. For example, hyperphosphorylation of tau in the Alzheimer’s disease brain is in part due to the impaired activity of PP1, PP2A, and PP578. Our approach by delivering an engineered and directed phosphatase mRNA or protein not only facilitates TPDephos in pathological conditions but also avoids the off-target effects commonly observed with nonspecific phosphatase overexpression (Fig. 6e and and Supplementary Fig. 3a).

Also, as PP2A is the predominant phosphatase accounting for more than 70% of total tau phosphatase activity, recapitulating normal tau phosphorylation levels through PP2A activators has been explored as a potential therapeutic strategy toward Alzheimer’s disease79. Several studies have demonstrated the efficacy of PP2A activators, including the antineoplastic cancer drug sodium selenate80, the anti-diabetic drug metformin81, and fingolimod, a multiple sclerosis drug that functions as an indirect PP2A activator82,83. In addition, accumulating evidence indicates that small-molecule activators of PP2A (SMAPs) can selectively stabilize PP2A holoenzymes and are orally bioavailable, brain-penetrant, and efficacious in vivo84–86. Despite these advances, many PP2A-activating agents exhibit pleiotropic effects that complicate mechanistic interpretation, and the holoenzyme specificity, context-dependent signaling, and potential cytotoxicity of SMAPs remain incompletely characterized86–88. In this context, the highly specific mechanism of PhosTACs, recruiting phosphatases to substrates, may offer an alternative approach with fewer side effects compared with broad-spectrum PP2A activators26,89. As multiple strategies are being pursued79,89–92, we propose that togoPhosTAC represents a mechanistically distinct strategy in this field.

While phosphatase-targeting molecules generally exhibit poor permeability and suboptimal pharmacological properties13, their clinical translation, particularly for brain targets that require effective tissue penetration and specificity, may benefit from recent advances in nanoparticle technologies. The BBB-crossing lipid nanoparticles incorporating specialized amino lipids and targeting modules have enabled successful mRNA delivery to the central nervous system (CNS) in animal models93–95, and several clinical trials are now investigating their potential for neurodegenerative diseases93,94,96–99. Moreover, the combination of nanocarrier-based delivery systems with PROTACs has emerged as a promising strategy to address the inherent limitations of bifunctional small-molecule systems, enabling improved tissue specificity, enhanced cellular uptake, and protection from immune clearance through nanoparticle modification100,101. The incorporation of PhosTACs, which modulate protein phosphorylation rather than inducing degradation, may offer distinct advantages for therapeutic development.

To improve intranasal delivery efficiency and overcome limitations such as uneven brain distribution, mucociliary clearance, and restricted mucosal permeability, emerging surface-engineering nanotechnologies may be incorporated. For example, odorranalectin- or odor-receptor ligand-based modifications could enhance olfactory epithelial targeting and nasal uptake102,103. Separately, lactoferrin-functionalized lipid nanoparticles may improve brain delivery through receptor-mediated transcytosis (RMT), leveraging the upregulation of lactoferrin receptors in respiratory epithelial cells and neurons in AD104. In addition, incorporating tetrahydroisoquinoline-related structures may further enhance parenchymal penetration via dopamine receptor-mediated endocytosis105. Beyond nanoparticle design itself, administration approaches including nasal drops, nasal sprays, and modified systems under development may target different anatomical nasal regions and provide more comprehensive mucosal coverage106,107. Combining these formulations with in situ gelling systems may also enhance nasal retention and reduce mucociliary clearance108, while the supplementation of mucoadhesive agents or permeation enhancers serves as another potential optimization strategy to improve drug permeability106.

Among various BBB-crossing therapeutic approaches, the transferrin receptor (TfR) remains a premier target for brain-targeted delivery via RMT-mediated transport across brain endothelial cells91. A notable milestone is the recent FDA approval of tividenofusp alfa (Avlayah), a fusion protein comprising iduronate-2-sulfatase (IDS) fused to an engineered TfR-binding Fc domain, designed to treat both neurological and peripheral manifestations of mucopolysaccharidosis II by leveraging RMT to transport the fusion enzyme across the brain endothelium109. While many TfR-binding engineered systems, including antibodies, antisense oligonucleotides, and nanoparticles, are typically administered via intravenous systemic treatment110–114, recent studies have also demonstrated that transferrin-functionalized nanoparticles can be adapted for intranasal delivery to promote brain targeting while reducing systemic exposure115. In parallel, numerous alternative brain-targeting nanoparticle strategies are currently being explored116, leveraging other characterized BBB endothelial receptors117, specific transporters118,119, targeted transmembrane domain interactions120, or AI-driven design and validation121,122. Importantly, the modular nature of this induced-proximity dephosphorylation strategy may enable broad adaptation across emerging CNS delivery platforms. Moving forward, integrating this system with advanced nanoplatforms or genetically engineered fusion constructs represents an important direction for expanding its therapeutic potential.

In sum, by modifying PhosTAC molecules and the recruited phosphatases, we demonstrate that the togoPhosTAC strategy can be applied to multiple therapeutic targets, including EGFR, tau, and α-synuclein, and that downstream signaling and tau aggregation can be regulated through modulation of phosphorylation status. By transitioning from protein-based delivery to an mRNA-based platform for therapeutic application and using AD-related tauopathy as a model, we show that Tau 2-8 based mRNA togoPhosTAC enables TPDephos at multiple pathological tau sites and ultimately rescues behavioral deficits in PS19 mice. Although the pharmacokinetics and pharmacodynamics (PK/PD) in vivo, as well as the broader impact on cellular physiology, remain to be elucidated, and substantial work is still required for therapeutic evaluation. We believe this work represents an important first step.

To express the recombinant proteins, vectors expressing 6xHis-PTPN2, 6xHis-FKBP12F36V-PTPN2, 6xHis-FKBP12F36V-PP2AA were transformed into Escherichia coli BL21(DE3) strain and cultured at 37 °C in 2 L LB flasks, respectively. When optical density (OD595) reached 0.6, either 1 mM isopropyl β-d-thiogalactopyranoside (IPTG) or 0.5 mM IPTG (for PTPN2 without FKBP12F36V tag only) was added for another 18 hr shaking at 18 °C. The bacteria were harvested by centrifugation (2775 x g, 15 °C, 20 min) and resuspended in lysis buffers (For 6xHis-FKBP12F36V-PTPN2 and 6xHis-FKBP12F36V-PP2AA recombinant proteins: 50 mM Trizma HCl, 300 mM NaCl, 10 mM imidazole, 0.5 mM TCEP, pH7.5, protease inhibitor cocktail; For 6xHis-PTPN2: 50 mM Tris, 500 mM NaCl, 5 mM imidazole, 0.1% Triton X-100, pH 8.0, protease inhibitor cocktail), followed by sonication (MISONIX Sonicator 3000, 4 min, 50% on/off cycle, output level: 6.0) and centrifugation (20,450 x g, 4 °C, 30 min). The supernatants were then collected and purified with Ni-NTA resin. The 6xHis-FKBP12F36V-PTPN2 were subsequently dialyzed in PBS containing 4% glycerol overnight at 4 °C. Next, the dialyzed samples were briefly spun down to remove precipitated proteins, and the supernatant was then subjected to size-exclusion chromatography (SEC, ÄKTA) using an S75 column equilibrated with PBS. As for the 6xHis-FKBP12F36V-PP2AA, the eluent from the Ni-NTA column was directly subjected to SEC using the S200 column equilibrated with buffer containing 50 mM Tris-HCl, 50 mM NaCl, and 1 mM DTT (pH 8.0), while the eluent of 6xHis-PTPN2 was directly subjected to SEC using the S75 column equilibrated with buffer containing 50 mM Tris-HCl, 150 mM NaCl, 0.5 mM DTT, and 5% glycerol (pH 7.4). All the soluble protein fractions from SEC were then aliquoted, snap-freezed, and stored at − 80 °C.

p-Nitrophenyl phosphate (pNPP) assay

The tyrosine dephosphorylation activity of both FKBP12F36V-PTPN2 and PTPN2 were measured within a reaction mixture (100 μl) containing 20 nM phosphatases and the indicated concentration of pNPP molecules. The phosphatases for the in vitro assay were diluted using PBS, while the indicated concentration of pNPP was prepared in the substrate buffer (25 mM HEPES, 50 mM NaCl, 1 mM DTT). The reaction was initiated by mixing the phosphatases and pNPP in the substrate buffer and then incubated at 37 °C for 1 hr. Also, 20 μM vanadate (a potent inhibitor of protein tyrosine phosphatases) was added into the mixture as the negative control group of the experiment. The amount of product, p-nitrophenol, is determined by reading the absorbance at 405 nm.

Purification of Tau 0N4R P301S protein

We transformed the pRK172-6xHis-0N4R-P301S-tau-HiBiT construct (Addgene #187835) into the Escherichia coli BL21(DE3) strain and cultured at 37 °C in 2 L LB flasks53. When optical density (OD595) reached 0.6, 0.5 mM IPTG was added for another 16 hr shaking at 16 °C. The bacteria were harvested by centrifugation (2775 x g, 4 °C, 30 min) before the pellets were resuspended in lysis buffer (14 mM β-mercaptoethanol, 300 mM NaCl, 25 mM HEPES, 10 mM imidazole, 1% Triton X-100, phosphatase inhibitor cocktail and protease inhibitor cocktail). The bacteria were lysed via sonication (MISONIX Sonicator 3000, 4 min, 50% on/off cycle, output level: 6.0) followed by centrifugation (20450 x g, 4 °C, 30 min) to collect the supernatants, which were then preliminary purified with Ni-NTA resin and subjected to SEC using an S75 column equilibrated with PBS and 1 mM DTT. All the soluble protein fractions from SEC were then aliquoted, snap-freezed, and stored at − 80 °C.

Preparation of in vitro transcription (IVT) mRNA encoding FKBP12F36V-PP2AA

The FKBP12F36V-PP2AA was amplified via PCR from the pcDNA5/FRT/TO-FKBP12F36V-PP2AA template. Following this, it was subcloned into an engineered vector that incorporates the T7 promoter sequence (5’TAATACGACTCACTATAAGG3’, where the italicized part denotes the T7 promoter, while the underscored ‘A’ indicates the transcription start site, facilitating the incorporation of CleanCap® Reagent AG (TriLink). This vector is also equipped with a synthetic poly-adenine (A50) sequence. The plasmids were then linearized with AflII restriction enzyme (NEB) and purified using a DNA purification kit (Alphagen Bio). During IVT, HiScribe® T7 High Yield RNA Synthesis Kit (NEB) and Clean Cap® Reagent AG were utilized to synthesis and add a functional 5’cap on the mRNA products, while pseudo-uridine (MedChemExpress #HY-112582C) was used instead of uridine. After the overnight in vitro transcription process, the residual DNA was removed by DNase I digestion followed by LiCl precipitation to purify the IVT-mRNA products. The integrity and quantity of the IVT-mRNA were evaluated by denaturing agarose gel electrophoresis, and the final concentration of product was determined using a NanoDrop Lite spectrophotometer (Thermo Scientific).

GePhos1 pre-fused togoPhosTAC delivery

1.5 × 105 HeLa cells in 1 mL of medium per well were first cultured in 12-well plates for 24 hr, and then the medium was replaced by serum-free DMEM for 24 hr serum starvation. On the other hand, 1 × 105 HCC827 cells in 1 mL of medium per well were cultured in 12-well plates for 48 hr. By the time for GePhos1 pre-fused togoPhosTAC delivery, purified FKBP12F36V-PTPN2 (or PTPN2) were diluted to 1 mg mL-1 using PBS, while GePhos1 molecules were diluted to the indicated concentration using DMSO (dimethyl sulfoxide). 2 μg of diluted protein and 0.65 μM of GePhos1 (for a final concentration of 100 nM) were mixed in 150 μL 20 mM HEPES buffer provided by PULSin® manufacturer, and the mixture was then incubated for 10 min at room temperature, forming the pre-fused PhosTAC complex. Next, 6 μL of PULSin® reagent was mixed into the mixture and then incubated at room temperature for 15 min, forming the togoPhosTAC mixture. The medium inside the well was removed, and the cells were washed by 300 μL PBS once, followed by administration of 150 μL togoPhosTAC mixture incorporated with 850 μL Opti-MEM.

PhosTAC7 / Tau 2-8 pre-fused togoPhosTAC delivery

0.8 × 105 HEK 293 Flp-In T-Rex cells expressing Halotag7-tau variants in 1 mL of medium per well were cultured in 12-well plates for 24 hr. On the other hand, 1 × 105 HEK 293 Flp-In T-Rex cells expressing Halotag7-α-synuclein A53T in 1 mL of medium per well were cultured in 12-well plates for 24 hr. The medium was then replaced by DMEM containing tetracycline (2 μg mL−1) for 48-hr induction of either Halotag7-tau variants or Halotag7-α-synuclein A53T. By the time for PhosTAC7/Tau 2-8 pre-fused togoPhosTAC delivery, purified FKBP12F36V-PP2AA were first diluted to 1 mg mL−1 using phosphate buffer (16.7 mM Na2HPO4, 8.3 mM NaH2PO4, pH 7.2, 0.22 μm filtered), while PhosTAC molecules were diluted to the indicated concentration using DMSO. 50 μg of diluted protein and 1 μM of PhosTAC molecules (for a final concentration of 100 nM) were mixed in 1 mL phosphate buffer, and the mixture was then incubated for 30 min at room temperature, forming the pre-fused PhosTAC. Next, 5 μL of 80-O14B (100 mg mL−1, dissolved in 25 mM sodium acetate solution, pH 5.5) was mixed into the mixture and incubated at room temperature for 15 min. We then combine the mixture containing encapsulated pre-fused PhosTAC with 9 mL Opti-MEM by gently inverting within a centrifuge tube, forming the togoPhosTAC reagent. The medium inside the well was then removed, followed by administration of 1 mL togoPhosTAC reagent per well for pre-fused togoPhosTAC delivery.

Tau seeding assay

HEK 293 Flp-In T-Rex cells expressing tau variants were plated at 3 × 104 cells in 300 µl of medium per well within an 8-well chamber (Ibidi, #80826), and the cells were allowed to adhere for 24 hr. Then, we replaced the medium with DMEM containing tetracycline (2 μg mL-1) to induce Halo-tau variants expression for another 24 hr, followed by tau seeding using the aggregated tau 0N4R P301S protein. Prior to tau seeding, 10 μM of the prepared tau 0N4R P301S monomer was first incubated with 20 μM heparin, 2 mM DTT and 1X protease inhibitors in PBS for 72 hr at 37 °C shaking at 220 rpm, forming the aggregated tau seeds. Next, the 40 µL aggregated tau seeds were incubated with 45 µL lipofectamine 2000 (ThermoFisher) for 30 min at room temperature, and the mixture was then mixed with Opti-MEM containing tetracycline (2 μg mL−1) and 1% penicillin-streptomycin(P/S), forming the tau seeding reagent dispersed in a total 3 mL reagent. The DMEM containing tetracycline in the chamber was removed, and 200 µL of the tau seeding reagent per well was then added for 24 hr treatment. Next, the tau seeding reagent was removed and replaced with DMEM containing tetracycline (2 μg mL−1) for 48 hr. Prior to pre-fused togoPhosTAC administration, the medium was replaced by Opti-MEM containing tetracycline (2 μg mL−1) for another 24 hr, while the togoPhosTAC reagent was prepared with 500 nM Tau 2-8, 5 μg FKBP12F36V-PP2AA and 50 μg 80-O14B in 1 mL volume following the previously described protocol. After 6-hr treatment of togoPhosTAC, the reagent was removed, followed by cell fixation using 4% paraformaldehyde solution (BD Biosciences, #554714) at 4 °C for 20 min. After the fixing solution was removed, the samples were immersed in PBS and stored at 4 °C until confocal microscopy analysis.

Thioflavin T (ThT) assay

10 μM of the prepared tau 0N4R P301S monomer (Addgene #187835) was incubated with 20 μM heparin, 2 mM DTT and 1X protease inhibitors in PBS for 24–72 hr at 37 °C shaking at 220 rpm. Tau aggregation was detected and quantified by Thioflavin T (ThT, Sigma-Aldrich) fluorescence readout (excitation 485/20 nm; emission 528/20 nm) in a Synergy H1 plate reader (BioTek) with 5 μM tau aggregates and 15 μM ThT.

Confocal microscopy analysis for tau puncta

Confocal images were acquired on a laser scanning confocal microscope (FV3000, Olympus) using 488 nm and 594 nm lasers for the excitation of mClover and mRuby2 fused with tau variants, respectively. These images were captured by a 40X or 100X objective lens and then processed by FV31S-SW Viewer software. Puncta corresponding to tau seeded aggregation within each image were then counted by an experiment operator that is blind to the group design of the togoPhosTAC treatment. Puncta in the merge image generated by either mClover, mRuby2 or both two signals were counted, and the total puncta per field was normalized to cell count per field for the evaluation of tau aggregation.

mRNA togoPhosTAC in cellulo delivery

3 × 104 (for 6-hr treatment) or 1.5 × 104 (for 48 hr and 54 hr treatment) HEK 293 Flp-In Trex cells expressing Halotag7-tau variants in 500 µL of medium per well were seeded in 24-well plates for 24 hr before tetracycline (2 μg mL−1) induction of Halo-tau variants for 48 hr. By the time of mRNA togoPhosTAC delivery, 500 ng of IVT-mRNA encoding FKBP12F36V-PP2AA and the indicated concentration of Tau 2-8 molecules were mixed with 2 μL jetMESSENGER® transfection reagent in 50 μL transfection buffer provided by jetMESSENGER® manufacturer. Pipetting was delicately done to ensure that the nanoparticles contained could encapsulate the IVT-mRNA and the Tau 2-8 molecules thoroughly, and the mixture was incubated at room temperature for another 15 min. The medium inside the well was then removed, followed by administration of 50 μL of the mixture incorporated with 450 μL Opti-MEM.

mRNA togoPhosTAC intrahippocampal delivery

A total of 10 µg IVT-mRNA (1 µg/µL, encoding FKBP12F36V-PP2AA) was diluted in 170 µL of in vivo-jetRNA® transfection buffer and gently pipetted 10 times to ensure homogeneity. 20 µL of in vivo-jetRNA® reagent was then added, followed by 10 pipetting cycles, and the mixture was incubated at room temperature for 15 min. Subsequently, 5 µM of tau 2-8 solution (in 1 µL) was added and pipetted 10 times. An additional 10 µL of in vivo-jetRNA® reagent was then incorporated, followed by 10 pipetting cycles. The mixture was then incubated for another 15 min at room temperature to allow nanoparticle formation. For intrahippocampal administration, 3 µL of the final preparation—containing approximately 150 ng IVT-mRNA and 5 µM Tau 2-8—was injected per dose into the hippocampus of mice.

mRNA togoPhosTAC intranasal delivery

Mice were lightly anesthetized with isoflurane and placed in a supine position. The mRNA formulation (as for intrahippocampal delivery) containing 150 ng IVT-mRNA and 5 µM Tau 2-8 was administered dropwise into the nostrils using a pipette tip. For each administration, 2 µL of solution was delivered into each nostril, allowing the solution to be inhaled through the nasal cavity. This procedure was repeated five times with a 5 min interval between administrations, resulting in a total dose of 10 µL per nostril per day. The treatment was performed once daily for 10 consecutive days. After each administration, mice were maintained in the supine position for several minutes to facilitate absorption through the nasal epithelium. Animals were then returned to their home cages and monitored until they fully recovered from anesthesia.

RNA extraction and qPCR analysis

Hippocampal tissue (~ 30 mg) from wild-type C57BL/6 mice was dissected and immediately homogenized in 300 µl of RNAzol® (Molecular Research Center). Total RNA was then extracted following the manufacturer’s protocol, with an additional purification step using 4-bromoanisole (BAN) to further eliminate DNA contamination. The purified RNA was dissolved in nuclease-free water, and genomic DNA removal and cDNA synthesis were then performed using the TOOLSQuant II Fast RT Kit (Biotools, KRT-BA06). The final product was mixed with ORA™ SEE qPCR Probe Mix, 2X (HighQu, #QPP0401) for real-time PCR analysis using a BioRad T100 thermal cycler. GAPDH was used as the reference gene to calculate relative gene expression levels across different samples. The PCR primer sequences were as follows: GAPDH126 (forward, 5’-ACCCCTTCATTGACCTCAAC-3’; reverse, 5’-TCGCTCCTGGAAGATGGTGAT-3’) and FKBP12(F36V)-PP2AA (forward, 5’-CAGATGAGTGTGGGTCAGAG-3’; reverse, 5’-TGATGCTGTTGAGGCGAAG-3’). The PCR primers for GAPDH were provided by Dr. Wei-Yu Chen’s lab at NCKU.

Immunohistochemistry

Brain tissues from the indicated conditions were fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde (PFA) at 4 °C overnight with gentle agitation, followed by cryoprotection in 30% sucrose. Samples were then frozen and stored at − 20 °C until further processing. Cryosections (20 μm thickness) were prepared and incubated in a blocking and permeabilization buffer consisting of 3% normal goat serum (NGS) and 0.2% Triton X-100 in PBS. Sections were incubated with primary antibodies overnight at 4 °C in 1% NGS/0.2% Triton X-100/PBS and subsequently treated with appropriate secondary antibodies for 2 hr at room temperature. The primary antibodies used were as follows: mouse anti-phospho-tau (Ser202/Thr205; AT8, Invitrogen), mouse anti-MC1 (conformation-specific tau antibody; previously described60, rabbit anti-NeuN (Abcam).

Quantification of AT8/MC1 fluorescence

Images were acquired using a ZEISS LSM 700 confocal microscope with a 63× objective, using an image matrix of 1024 × 1024 pixels, a pixel size of 0.2 × 0.2 µm, and an 8-bit image depth. Confocal images were collected as z-stacks with a slice interval of 0.4 µm. Immunofluorescence signals were quantified using ImageJ software (National Institutes of Health). For AT8 and MC1 analyses, background and signal thresholds were determined using the ImageJ Threshold tool based on representative images with the highest signal intensity. The average threshold values were calculated and then applied uniformly to all images using the Set Threshold function. After thresholding, the mean fluorescence intensity (MFI) of the signal and background was measured using the Measure tool, and background intensity was subtracted from the signal intensity. At least three fields per sample were analyzed. The resulting values were averaged and used for statistical analysis.

Elevated plus maze (EPM) test

Mice were placed in the center of an elevated plus maze (arms 6.2 × 75 cm; two arms enclosed by 20 cm-high side walls; elevated 63 cm above the floor) and allowed to explore for 5 min to assess anxiety-like behavior. Automated tracking software was used to quantify the cumulative time each mouse spent in the open and closed arms.

Novel object recognition (NOR) test

Novel object recognition (NOR) was used to evaluate recognition memory. The test was conducted in a 25 × 25 × 25 cm acrylic box placed in a separate behavioral testing room. Animal behavior was recorded and analyzed using video tracking. The experiment consisted of three phases: habituation, training, and testing. During the training phase, mice were allowed to explore two identical objects for 10 min. After a 20 min retention interval, one of the familiar objects was replaced with a novel object. Mice were then allowed to explore the objects during the testing phase. Because mice naturally prefer novel objects, increased exploration of the novel object indicates intact recognition memory. The recognition index was calculated as the proportion of time spent exploring the novel object relative to the total exploration time.

Morris water maze analysis

The Morris water maze (MWM) test was performed to evaluate spatial learning and memory, following a protocol adapted from Vorhees63. Briefly, a circular pool containing opaque water was used, with a hidden platform submerged in the center of one quadrant. Four conspicuous spatial cues were placed around the pool to facilitate spatial navigation. During the training phase, mice underwent four trials per day with an inter-trial interval of 10–20 min. In each trial, mice were allowed to search for the hidden platform for up to 60 s. If a mouse failed to locate the platform within this time, it was gently guided to the platform and allowed to remain there for 10 s. Swimming trajectories were recorded using an overhead camera. On the day following training, a probe test was conducted in which mice were released from the quadrant opposite to the platform location, and their movements were recorded. Behavioral data, including swimming trajectories and time spent in each quadrant, were analyzed using Noldus EthoVision XT software.

PhosTAC7 treatment for FKBP12F36V-PP2AA / Halotag7-α-synuclein A53T cell line

1 × 105 HEK 293 Flp-In Trex cells expressing both FKBP12F36V-PP2AA and Halotag7-α-synuclein A53T in 1 mL of medium per well were cultured in 12 well plates for 24 hr, and then the medium was replaced by DMEM containing tetracycline (2 μg mL−1) for 48 hr induction of both FKBP12F36V-PP2AA and Halotag7-α-synuclein A53T-mRuby2 expression. After 48-hr induction, we replaced DMEM containing tetracycline with DMEM containing the indicated concentration of PhsoTAC7 for 24-hr treatment. The cells were then collected and analyzed via western blotting.

Ternary complex pull-down assay

1 × 106 HEK 293 Flp-In Trex cells expressing Halotag7-tau variants were seeded into 100 mm dishes for 24 hr incubation, followed by 48 hr tetracycline (2 μg mL-1) treatment for inducing expression of Halo-tau variants. PhosTAC7 pre-fused togoPhosTAC was then prepared and treated for 6 hr, and the cells were then washed by PBS twice before collecting. The cells were collected with lysis buffer (150 mM NaCl, 1 mM EDTA, 25 mM Tris-HCl, and 1% Triton X-100) and centrifuged at 27237 xg for 20 min at 4 °C to collect the supernatant. After bicinchoninic acid (BCA) assay analysis, protein samples were adjusted to the same quantity and co-incubated with Halo-Trap agarose for 16 hr at 4 °C. After the incubation, the agarose was then washed three times with diluted lysis buffer (75 mM NaCl, 0.5 mM EDTA, 12.5 mM tris- HCl, and 0.5% Triton X-100), eluted with sample buffer, and analyzed via western blotting with the indicated antibodies.

Sample process and western blotting

After the togoPhosTAC administration toward EGFR signaling, EGF (100 ng mL−1) was added for 5–15 min (as indicated) before sample collection by radioimmunoprecipitation assay buffer (RIPA, 0.05 M Tris-HCl, 0.15 M NaCl, 0.25% deoxycholic acid, 1% NP-40, 1 mM EDTA, pH 7.4, EMD Millipore) with 1% SDS. The collected samples were then passed through a 23 G syringe 10 times to help lysing cells. On the other hand, the sample collection for HEK 293 Flp-In Trex cells was done by RIPA and the following centrifugation (27237 xg, 4 °C, 20 min) to collect the supernatants. For the in vivo study, the hippocampus of PS19 mice were rinsed once with PBS and homogenized in RIPA lysis buffer (20 mM HEPES, pH 7.8, 150 mM NaCl, 1 mM EDTA, 0.1% Triton X-100, 50 mM NaF, 1 mM dithiothreitol, and protease inhibitor cocktail). The lysates were centrifuged at 13,000 xg for 10 min, and the resulting supernatants were collected. Protein concentrations were determined using a BCA kit (Pierce). All the protein samples were then diluted using sample buffer, denatured at 95 °C for 10 min and subjected to SDS-PAGE. After running with a voltage of 80–100 V for 2 hr, the gel was cut according to the pre-stained protein ladder and transferred to the PVDF membrane. The membrane was blocked by 5% milk in TBS (tris-buffered saline) at room temperature and then incubated with primary antibody overnight at 4 °C. After being washed 3 times by TBST (TBS, 0.1% Tween- 20), the membrane was incubated with secondary antibody at room temperature and then washed by TBST for at least 3 times. For protein delivery experiments, Phos-tag SDS-PAGE gels were prepared with final concentrations of 25 μM Phos-tag acrylamide and 50 μM ZnCl₂. For mRNA delivery experiments, the gels were prepared with final concentrations of 20 μM Phos-tag acrylamide and 40 μM ZnCl₂. Electrophoresis was performed at a constant current of 10 mA for 200 min. Following electrophoresis, the gels were incubated in transfer buffer containing 10 mM EDTA for 10 min with three buffer changes, followed by one 10 min incubation in transfer buffer without EDTA prior to protein transfer. For Phos-tagTM SDS-PAGE, a conventional protein ladder was used only as a reference to estimate the relative migration position of the target proteins, but not for accurate molecular-weight determination. The final membrane was imaged using an enhanced chemiluminescence (ECL) substrate with the iBright Imaging System. The antibodies used in this study are as follows: anti-EGFR (ThermoFisher, #MA5-13269 and Abclonal, #A23452), anti-EGFR pY1068 (Cell Signaling, #3777), anti-AKT (Cell Signaling, #9272), anti-AKT pS473 (Cell Signaling, #4060 T), anti-SHC pY239/240 (Cell Signaling, #2434 s), anti-GAB1 (Cell Signaling, #3232), anti-GAB1 pY627 (Cell Signaling, #3233 T), anti-tau (tau-13, Santa Cruz, #21796), anti-pT181 tau (ThermoFisher, MN1050), anti-pT231 tau (ThermoFisher, MN1040), anti-pS396 tau (Abclonal, #AP1028), anti-pS404 tau (Abclonal, #AP1378), anti-AT8 (ThermoFisher, MN1020), anti-MAP2 (Proteintech, #67015-1-Ig), anti-Caspase-3 (Cell Signaling, #9665), anti-PARP1 (Abclonal, # A27791), anti-Neurofilament L (Abclonal, #A0257), anti-α-synuclein (BD Biosciences, #610787), anti-pS129 α-synuclein (Abcam, #ab51253), anti-PTPN2 (Abclonal, #A22564), anti-PP2AC (Cell Signaling, #2259), anti-FKBP12 (Santa Cruz, #133067), anti-GAPDH (Santa Cruz, #32233), anti-vinculin (Abcam, #ab129002), and anti-β-Actin (Abclonal, #AC004).

Statistical analysis

The western blots were analyzed via iBright Analysis Software (Desktop Version 5.2.1). All the original quantified data were adjusted to the relative ratio of the average value of the control group, which was denoted as 1. The significance between groups was determined by the P-value and indicated in all figures (* P < 0.05, ** P < 0.01, *** P < 0.001, **** P < 0.0001). Comparisons between multiple groups were analyzed by one-way ANOVA using PRISM (version 10.0; GraphPad), while the comparisons between two groups were analyzed by an unpaired t test. The result of the pNPP assay was analyzed by fitting the data points with non-linear regression to the Michaelis-Menten kinetics using PRISM (version 10.0; GraphPad).
