Live birth after uterine-sparing treatment of pyometra following abdominal myomectomy: A case report
Authors: Kiley Hunkler, David Boedeker, Yovanni Casablanca, Micah Hill
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
Pyometra is rare and refers to purulent material in the uterine cavity, which can lead to uterine perforation. A hysterectomy is the historic treatment for perforated pyometra due to the condition's high morbidity and mortality. A 33-year-old woman with uterine factor infertility underwent an abdominal myomectomy that was complicated by post-operative, perforated pyometra. She received conservative treatment with surgical debridement and broad-spectrum antibiotics. Following her recovery and subsequent infertility treatments, she had a term live birth through in vitro fertilization. The case highlights the value of uterine-sparing treatment in carefully selected patients with post-operative, perforated pyometra, in order to preserve fertility.

Introduction

Pyometra, the collection of purulent material in the uterine cavity, affects 0.1–0.5 % of gynecologic patients [[1], [2], [3]]. Atrophic postmenopausal cervicitis [3,4], intrauterine devices [3,4], and malignancy [1,5,6] are classic associations. Uterine perforation is a rare sequala and can be mistaken as gastrointestinal tract perforation [6].

Most patients with nonperforated pyometra are asymptomatic [3], but perforated pyometra carries a mortality rate of 25–40 % [4] due to associated bacteremia, peritonitis, and septic shock [2]. Fewer than 100 cases of pyometra with spontaneous perforation have been reported [4,6], and even fewer post-operative, perforated pyometra. Historically, a hysterectomy is the preferred treatment [1,6], but its use in an emergent setting or without evidence of malignancy has been questioned [1,7].

The case reported here concerns a woman with post-operative, perforated pyometra 19 days after an abdominal myomectomy for uterine factor infertility who was treated with uterine-sparing surgery and antibiotics, allowing for subsequent pregnancy and live birth following in vitro fertilization (IVF). This report urges consideration of more conservative treatments for post-operative, perforated pyometra to enable fertility preservation.

Case Presentation

A 33-year-old woman underwent an abdominal myomectomy for uterine factor infertility. Her past obstetric history was notable for a miscarriage and no other pregnancies. Four fibroids were removed from two anterior incisions: a 9 cm International Federation of Gynecology and Obstetrics (FIGO) type six fundal fibroid, a 2 cm FIGO type two anterior fibroid, and 8 cm FIGO type five anterior fibroid, and a 2 cm FIGO type four fibroid. The uterine cavity was not visibly entered during surgery, and uterine defects were closed in multiple layers using 0-polyglactin suture. Within the standard of care, the patient did not receive pre-operative antibiotics.

On post-operative day 19, she presented to the emergency department reporting a fever at home to 102.5 F, associated chills, and malodorous vaginal discharge. She had no other significant past medical history or prior abdominal surgeries. On arrival she was afebrile, tachycardic to 114 beats per minute, normotensive, and tachypneic with a respiratory rate of 20 breaths per minute. A CT scan revealed multiple fluid collections with foci of air both within and adjacent to the uterus (Fig. 1). An abdominal hysterectomy was recommended due to suspected post-operative, perforated pyometra, but the patient strongly desired to preserve her uterus and retain her ability to carry a pregnancy. After extensive counseling, conservative uterine-sparing surgery was agreed, pending intraoperative findings. On hospital day 1, postoperative day 20 from the patient's index surgery, she underwent an exploratory laparotomy where purulent fluid filled the abdominal cavity due to a defect in the anterior uterine wall. The uterus was opened, and the infected and necrotic myometrium and endometrium were sharply debrided. Pulsed lavage was performed of the infected endometrial cavity. An intrauterine drain was placed, and the uterus was closed in four layers of purse-string suturing using size 0 poliglecaprone 25 suture. The myometrium and serosa were closed in baseball fashion with a size 2–0 poliglecaprone 25 suture. A wound vacuum was applied, and the patient was continued on broad-spectrum antibiotics. Two days later, a wound washout revealed healthy-appearing tissue above and below the fascia without a need for further debridement. The fascia was closed with 0-polydioxanone suture.

The patient's intrauterine drain was expelled while she was urinating and was not replaced, given the minimal drainage. Her antibiotics were narrowed and after two wound vacuum exchanges, she was discharged home on hospital day 9 on oral antibiotics, linezolid, ciprofloxacin, and metronidazole, for 20 days total. Following several outpatient wound vacuum exchanges, the patient's skin incision closed by secondary intention five months after her initial surgical debridement.

The patient was treated with estrogen for 30 days to develop her endometrial lining and 10 days of progesterone therapy to initiate a withdrawal bleed, followed by two months of combined oral contraceptive pills. Hysteroscopy revealed a normal endometrial cavity without adhesions, and she subsequently had a normal menses while on contraceptive pills. Six months after her myomectomy, she was cleared to attempt pregnancy.

After an additional six months of infertility, the patient followed up and her male partner was diagnosed with asthenospermia. With care taken to ensure monofollicular development, she underwent three natural cycles with intrauterine inseminations (IUIs), followed by three ovulation induction cycles with letrozole and IUIs, none of which resulted in a pregnancy.

Following a second hysteroscopy with a normal-appearing endometrial cavity, the patient underwent an IVF cycle with her partner's sperm and obtained one cryopreserved blastocyst. Subsequent embryo transfer resulted in a chemical pregnancy. A second IVF cycle yielded two cryopreserved blastocysts, one of which was thawed and transferred, resulting in an intrauterine pregnancy three and a half years after the abdominal myomectomy.

The patient's pregnancy was complicated by chronic hypertension without requiring medications and diet-controlled gestational diabetes. Notably, she did not experience a placental disorder nor uterine rupture, and she underwent a primary low transverse cesarean section at 38 weeks of gestation complicated by dense fascial and uterine adhesions. She delivered a live male infant with APGAR scores of 3 and 8, weighing 3431 g. Discussion

Perforated pyometra has been reported in the literature as a spontaneous occurrence, typically in post-menopausal women [3,6], and rarely associated with a prior myomectomy. In cases of post-operative, perforated pyometra, the infection likely spreads through uterine defects in the compromised myometrium and into the peritoneal cavity. In the case reported here, the patient was noted to have infection communicating with the peritoneum via an anterior uterine defect at the site of her anterior uterine incision performed during her myomectomy.

Early recognition of perforated pyometra is critical for clinical outcomes. Ultrasonography is generally considered the imaging modality of choice and can show intrauterine fluid [3], passage of gas from reproductive organs to the peritoneal cavity, and thin areas concerning for uterine perforation [4]. However, ultrasound may fail to differentiate between differential diagnoses in the consideration of pyometra, such as hematomas and leiomyomas [7], whereas computed tomography (CT) scans can characterize intrauterine and extrauterine fluid and masses [2]. In the present case, the patient's CT scan showed a clear uterine wall perforation, and therefore an ultrasound scan was not ordered, as the surgical plan for an exploratory laparotomy was unlikely to change.

Causative organisms that have been associated with pyometra include Staphylococci and Streptococci species, Escherichia coli, Peptostreptococcus, Klebsiella, Anaerobius bacteroides, and certain strains of Lactobacilli [2,6,7]. In line with the literature, the patient's wound cultures resulted with coagulase-negative Staphylococci, and her blood cultures remained negative. She was initially started on piperacillin/tazobactam in the emergency department, and then transitioned to intravenous meropenem, vancomycin, and clindamycin per infectious disease recommendations. As an outpatient, she received oral linezolid, ciprofloxacin, and metronidazole.

With respect to risk factors, the patient's development of gestational diabetes suggests that she likely had underlying insulin resistance that could have contributed to the progression of her pyometra. One retrospective review found that diabetes mellitus was the most common underlying disease in patients with perforated pyometra [3]. Although this diagnosis is rare, it is important to consider perforated pyometra in a patient with insulin resistance who presents with a fever, pain, and concerning findings such as pneumoperitoneum on imaging.

A hysterectomy is considered the historical, definitive treatment for perforated pyometra [1,6,7]; however conservative treatment approaches, such as CT-guided drainage [7], have been proposed in well selected patients with the primary goal of drainage of pus and purulent material [1,3]. The present case report adds to the literature supporting conservative management by describing successful treatment of post-operative, perforated pyometra with an intrauterine drain, surgical debridement, and broad-spectrum antibiotics.

In terms of the patient's abdominal myomectomy, the American College of Obstetrics and Gynecologists recommends to “consider” rather than endorse consistent use of cefazolin due to a myomectomy's classification as a “laparotomy without entry into the bowel or vagina” (8). Both a myomectomy and a hysterectomy are considered Class II or clean contaminated wounds as both operations involve the genital tract (8), yet only one uses standard antibiotic prophylaxis. One prophylactic dose of two grams of cefazolin at the time of the patient's abdominal myomectomy in the present case may have lessened her subsequent infection, though this is a very rare case and antibiotic prophylaxis may not have changed the clinical course.

To our knowledge, this is the first report of a successful IVF pregnancy and live birth following post-operative, perforated pyometra treated conservatively. However, caution should be used prior to generalizing the findings from a single patient. In this case, a gynecologic oncologist (author Y.C.) performed the surgical debridement and subsequent abdominal washouts. The procedure was technically challenging and may not have been completed at an institution with less resources or less experienced surgeons.

This case illustrates that uterine-sparing therapy can be considered in carefully selected and well-counseled patients who develop post-operative, perforated pyometra following an abdominal myomectomy. Furthermore, clinicians should keep a high index of suspicion for this rare complication in at-risk patients to diagnose, treat, and prevent further morbidity and mortality.
