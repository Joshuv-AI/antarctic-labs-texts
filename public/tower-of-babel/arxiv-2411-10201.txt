A sharp higher order Sobolev embedding
Raul Hindov, Shahaf Nitzan, Jan-Fredrik Olsen, Eskil Rydhe

Abstract
We obtain sharp embeddings from the Sobolev space W 0 k , 2 ​ ( − 1 , 1 ) W^{k,2}_{0}(-1,1) into the space L 1 ​ ( − 1 , 1 ) L^{1}(-1,1) and determine the extremal functions. This improves on a previous estimate of the sharp constants of these embeddings due to Kalyabin.



1
Introduction

In this paper we give a proof of a Sobolev-type inequality with a sharp constant and an explicit extremal function. This is motivated by the more general problem of calculating sharp constants and identifying extremal functions for Sobolev embeddings
W^{k,p}_{0}(-1,1)\subset L^{q}(-1,1)
. That is, inequalities of the form

\left(\int_{-1}^{1}|f|^{q}\mathop{\mathrm{\mathstrut d}}\nolimits\!x\right)^{\frac{1}{q}}\leq c_{k,p,q}\left(\int_{-1}^{1}\left|f^{(k)}\right|^{p}\mathop{\mathrm{\mathstrut d}}\nolimits\!x\right)^{\frac{1}{p}}

for functions
f:\mathbb{R}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
with support in
[-1,1]
and that satisfy
f^{(k)}\in L^{p}(\mathbb{R})
. In particular, for integers
k\geq 1
,
f
satisfies the boundary conditions
f^{(j)}(\pm 1)=0
for all
0\leq j<k
.

We shall consider the case
q=1
,
p=2
, and integers
k\geq 1
, for which the sharp constants and extremal functions do not seem to be known. See, e.g., the surveys by Mitrinović et al.
[
5
, Chapter II]
, Kuznetsov and Nazarov
[
3
]
, and Nazarov and Shcheglova
[
6
]
.

Our main result is as follows.

Theorem 1
.

For all integers
k\geq 1
and
f\in W^{k,2}_{0}(-1,1)
, we have the sharp inequality

\int_{-1}^{1}|f(x)|\mathop{\mathrm{\mathstrut d}}\nolimits\!x\leq\frac{1}{(2k-1)!!\sqrt{k+\frac{1}{2}}}\left(\int_{-1}^{1}|f^{(k)}(x)|^{2}\mathop{\mathrm{\mathstrut d}}\nolimits\!x\right)^{\frac{1}{2}}.

(1)

The extremal functions are given by the Landau kernels,
L_{k}(x)=(1-x^{2})^{k}
.

This theorem improves a bound due to Kalyabin
[
2
]
. Indeed, for
p=2
,
q\in(0,\infty)
and
k\in\mathbb{Z}_{+}
, Kalyabin obtained that

\frac{\sqrt{k+\frac{1}{2}}}{2^{k}k!}\left(\mathcal{L}(kq)\right)^{1/q}\leq c_{k,2,q}\leq\frac{\left(\mathcal{L}((k-\frac{1}{2})q)\right)^{1/q}}{2^{k}(k-1)!\sqrt{k-\frac{1}{2}}},

where
\mathcal{L}(s)=\int_{-1}^{1}(1-x^{2})^{s}\mathop{\mathrm{\mathstrut d}}\nolimits\!x
. A straight-forward calculation reveals that the constant from Theorem
1
is identical to Kalyabin’s lower estimate in the case
q=1
, implying that it was indeed sharp.

It is well-known that sharp constants of Sobolev embeddings can be connected to minimal eigenvalues of certain boundary value problems (see, e.g.,
[
1
]
). In our case, the sharp constant in Theorem
1
is connected to the minimal eigenvalue of the boundary value problem

\left\{\begin{aligned} (-1)^{k}u^{(2k)}(x)&=\lambda\frac{u(x)}{|u(x)|}\int_{-1}^{1}|u(t)|\mathop{\mathrm{\mathstrut d}}\nolimits\!t,&&x\in[-1,1],\\[5.69054pt]
u^{(j)}(\pm 1)&=0,&&j\in\{0,1,\ldots,2k-1\}.\end{aligned}\right.

Indeed, we have

\displaystyle\min_{u\neq 0}\frac{\left((-1)^{k}u^{(2k)},u\right)}{\left(\frac{u}{|u|}\left\|u\right\|_{L^{1}(-1,1)},u\right)}

\displaystyle=\min_{u\neq 0}\frac{\left(u^{(k)},u^{(k)}\right)}{\left\|u\right\|^{2}_{L^{1}(-1,1)}}

\displaystyle=\min_{u\neq 0}\frac{\left\|u^{(k)}\right\|_{L^{2}(-1,1)}^{2}}{\left\|u\right\|^{2}_{L^{1}(-1,1)}}=\left(\frac{1}{c_{k,2,1}}\right)^{2},

where
(\cdot,\cdot)
is the inner product in
L^{2}(-1,1)
and the first equality follows upon
k
times partially integrating and using the boundary conditions. In
[
1
]
, a similar example is provided for the case
p=q=2
.

2
Proof of Theorem
1

The main idea of the proof is to consider a class of explicit left inverses of the differential operator
f\mapsto f^{(k)}
of the form

\displaystyle f(x)=\int_{-1}^{1}B_{k}(x,y)f^{(k)}(y)\mathop{\mathrm{\mathstrut d}}\nolimits\!y,\quad x\in\mathbb{R}.

By the Cauchy-Schwarz inequality, we obtain from this integral representation that

\displaystyle\int_{-1}^{1}|f(x)|\mathop{\mathrm{\mathstrut d}}\nolimits\!x\leq\left\|f^{(k)}\right\|_{L^{2}(-1,1)}\left\|\int_{1}^{1}|B_{k}(x,y)|\mathop{\mathrm{\mathstrut d}}\nolimits\!x\right\|_{L^{2}_{(-1,1;\mathop{\mathrm{\mathstrut d}}\nolimits\!y)}}.

The inequality of Theorem
1
is then obtained by connecting the expression involving the norm of
B_{k}(x,y)
to a minimization problem having Legendre polynomials as minimizers. Finally, sharpness is obtained by noting that Landau kernels are extremal functions for the resulting inequality.

Before proceeding with a proof, we discuss some notation. For integers
k\geq 1
and
p\in[1,\infty)

we define the Sobolev space

W^{k,p}_{0}(-1,1)=\left\{f:\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R}\mid\operatorname{supp}f\subseteq[-1,1],\ f^{(k)}\in L^{p}(\mathbb{R})\right\}.

For each integer
0\leq j<k
, the derivative
f^{(j)}
is absolutely continuous since
f^{(j-1)}(x)=\int_{-1}^{x}f^{(j)}(t)\mathop{\mathrm{\mathstrut d}}\nolimits\!t
and
L^{p}(-1,1)\subset L^{1}(-1,1)
. Hence, as mentioned in the introduction, since
f
has support in
[-1,1]
, it follows that
f^{(j)}(\pm 1)=0
for each such
j
.
The norm of
f
in
W^{k,p}_{0}(-1,1)
is defined by
\|f\|_{W^{k,p}_{0}(-1,1)}=\|f^{(k)}\|_{L^{p}(-1,1)}
.
By
\mathbbm{1}_{X}
, we denote the function that equals 1 if condition
X
is satisfied, and 0 otherwise. By,
\delta_{nm}
we denote the standard Kronecker delta function.

Now we give the details of the proof.

Remark
.

By part (
iii
) of the proposition, the integral operator induced by
b_{k}
is a left-inverse to the differential operator
f\mapsto f^{(k)}
. Moreover, such a left-inverse is not unique; the polynomial
Q
, which may depend on

, provides a parametrization of a family of left-inverses. By part (
ii
),
b_{k}(x,y)
is discontinuous across the

y\neq 0

Proof.

Property (
i
) follows by direct computation, and property (
ii
) follows immediately from the definition of
b_{k}(x,y)
.

To prove the reproducing property (
iii
), we use integration by parts repeatedly.
Indeed, for
x<0
, we have

\int_{\mathbb{R}}b_{k}(x,y)f^{(k)}(y)\mathop{\mathrm{\mathstrut d}}\nolimits\!y=\sum_{j=1}^{k-1}\left[\frac{(x-y)^{k-j}}{(k-j)!}f^{(k-j)}(y)\right]_{-1}^{x}+\int_{-1}^{x}f^{\prime}(y)\mathop{\mathrm{\mathstrut d}}\nolimits\!y=f(x),

where all the terms in the sum vanish due to the boundary conditions on
f

The case
x\geq 0
is treated similarly.
Finally, since
Q
is a polynomial of
\deg Q<k
, it follows that

\int_{\mathbb{R}}Q(y)f^{(k)}(y)\mathop{\mathrm{\mathstrut d}}\nolimits\!y=0.

Lemma 1
.

.
Given any integer
k\geq 1
, let

\displaystyle f_{k}(y)=y^{k-1}\mathbbm{1}_{y>0},\quad y\in\mathbb{R}.

Moreover, let
Q
be a polynomial of degree at most
k-1
, such that
f_{k}(y_{n})=Q(y_{n})
for
k+1
distinct real numbers
y_{1}<\ldots<y_{k+1}
.
Then either
Q(y)\equiv y^{k-1}
or
Q(y)\equiv 0
. In particular,
this implies that either
y_{1}\geq 0
or
y_{k+1}\leq 0
.

Proof.

If
k=1
, then
Q
is a constant function. From this, it follows immediately that if
Q(y)-\mathbbm{1}_{y>0}
vanishes at two distinct points, then either
Q(y)\equiv 1
or
Q(y)\equiv 0
. Moreover, these points have to be both
non-positive
or both
non-negative
. This argument is easily extended to
k=2
using the linearity of
Q
and the hypothesis that
Q(y)-y\mathbbm{1}_{y>0}
is to vanish at three distinct points.

For general
k>2
, we proceed by induction. To this end, suppose that
Q
is a polynomial of degree at most
k-1
and that

g(y)=Q(y)-y^{k-1}\mathbbm{1}_{y>0}
vanishes at
k+1
distinct points. As
g
has a continuous
derivative, the mean value theorem implies that

g^{\prime}(y)=Q(y)-(k-1)y^{k-2}\mathbbm{1}_{y>0}
vanishes at at least
k
distinct points. It follows by the induction hypothesis, that these
k
points are either all
non-positive
or all
non-negative
. In particular, this means that
Q(y)-y^{k-1}\mathbbm{1}_{y>0}
vanishes at
k
points that are either all
non-positive
or all
non-negative
. Since
Q
is of degree
k-1
, the conclusion follows.
∎

Remark
.

For
k\geq 1
, let

be as above. Then the lemma implies that if a polynomial

\displaystyle B_{k}(x,y)=b_{k}(x,y)-\sum_{n=1}^{k}p_{n}(y)b_{k}(x,y_{n}).

2.2
Connection to a minimisation problem that leads to the Sobolev-type inequality (
1
)

Suppose that
f\in W_{0}^{k,2}(-1,1)
and
B_{k}(x,y)
as above. Then we have

\displaystyle f(x)=\int_{-1}^{1}B_{k}(x,y)f^{(k)}(y)\mathop{\mathrm{\mathstrut d}}\nolimits\!y,\quad x\in\mathbb{R}.

Applying the Cauchy–Schwarz inequality, we obtain that

\displaystyle\|f\|_{L^{1}(-1,1)}

\displaystyle\leq\left\|f^{(k)}\right\|_{L^{2}(-1,1)}\left\|\int_{-1}^{1}|B_{k}(x,y)|\mathop{\mathrm{\mathstrut d}}\nolimits\!x\right\|_{L^{2}(-1,1;\mathop{\mathrm{\mathstrut d}}\nolimits\!y)}

\displaystyle=\left\|f^{(k)}\right\|_{L^{2}(-1,1)}\left\|\int_{-1}^{1}B_{k}(x,y)\mathop{\mathrm{\mathstrut d}}\nolimits\!x\right\|_{L^{2}(-1,1;\mathop{\mathrm{\mathstrut d}}\nolimits\!y)},

where the final equality is immediate from Proposition
2
(
v
).

It follows from Proposition
1
(
i
) that

\displaystyle k!\int_{-1}^{1}B_{k}(x,y)\mathop{\mathrm{\mathstrut d}}\nolimits\!x=(-y)^{k}-\sum_{n=1}^{k}(-y_{n})^{k}p_{n}(y).

Since the polynomials
p_{n}
are of degree
k-1
and satisfy
p_{n}(y_{m})=\delta_{nm}
, for
n,m\in\{1,2,\ldots,k\}
, the right-hand side of the above expression is a monic polynomial of degree
k
with distinct zeros
y_{1},y_{2},\ldots,y_{k}
.
Since we can choose the zeros
y_{1},y_{2},\ldots,y_{k}
freely, any monic polynomial with distinct zeros can be obtained in this way. It follows that

\displaystyle\|f\|_{L^{1}(-1,1)}\leq\frac{\|f^{(k)}\|_{L^{2}(-1,1)}}{k!}\min_{\begin{subarray}{c}p\text{ monic}\\
\deg p=k\end{subarray}}\|p\|_{L^{2}(-1,1)}.

It is well-known that the unique minimizers are given by the monic Legendre polynomials

P_{k}(y)=\frac{k!}{(2k)!}\frac{\mathop{\mathrm{\mathstrut d}}\nolimits^{k}\!}{\mathop{\mathrm{\mathstrut d}}\nolimits\!y^{k}}\left[(y^{2}-1)^{k}\right].

Since we could not find a convenient reference explaining this fact, we point out that
the minimising property in the
L^{2}(-1,1)
norm follows from the construction of the monic Legendre polynomials as the orthogonalization of powers
1,x,x^{2},\ldots
on the interval
-1\leq x\leq 1
with respect to the Lebesgue measure. From this it follows that any monic polynomial
p
of order
k
can be written in the form
p(x)=P_{k}+c_{k-1}P_{k-1}(x)+\ldots+c_{0}
. Hence,

\left\|p\right\|^{2}_{L^{2}(-1,1)}=\left\|P_{k}\right\|_{L^{2}(-1,1)}^{2}+\sum_{n=1}^{k-1}c_{n}^{2}\left\|P_{n}\right\|_{L^{2}(-1,1)}^{2}.

Clearly, this expression is minimised by choosing
c_{0}=c_{1}=\ldots=c_{k-1}=0
.
Since the
L^{2}(-1,1)
norm of the monic Legendre polynomial (see, e.g.
[
4
]
) is

\displaystyle\left\|P_{k}\right\|_{L^{2}(-1,1)}^{2}

\displaystyle=\left(\frac{k!}{(2k-1)!!\sqrt{k+\frac{1}{2}}}\right)^{2}.

we conclude that,

\displaystyle\left\|f\right\|_{L^{1}(-1,1)}\leq\frac{1}{(2k-1)!!\sqrt{k+\frac{1}{2}}}\left\|f^{(k)}\right\|_{L^{2}(-1,1)}.

2.3
Sharpness of the inequality in Theorem
1

While the proof of the sharpness of the inequality in Theorem
1
is implicitly contained in
[
2
]
, we provide a proof for the sake of completeness.

Lemma 2
.

For all integers
k\geq 0
, we have equality in (
1
) if and only if
f
is equal to a constant multiple of the Landau kernel
L_{k}(x)
. In particular, the best constant of the equality is given by

\frac{\left\|L_{k}\right\|_{L^{1}(-1,1)}}{\left\|L_{k}^{(k)}\right\|_{L^{2}(-1,1)}}=\frac{1}{(2k-1)!!\sqrt{k+\frac{1}{2}}}.

Proof.

Recall that
L_{k}(x)=(1-x^{2})^{k}
. Writing
L_{k}(x)=(1-x)^{k}(1+x)^{k}
, we obtain
by repeated integration by parts that

\int_{-1}^{1}|L_{k}(x)|\mathop{\mathrm{\mathstrut d}}\nolimits\!x=\frac{(k!)^{2}}{(2k)!}\frac{2^{2k+1}}{2k+1}

and, moreover, that

\begin{split}\int_{-1}^{1}\left(L_{k}^{(k)}(x)\right)^{2}\mathop{\mathrm{\mathstrut d}}\nolimits\!x&=(k!)^{2}\frac{2^{2k+1}}{2k+1}.\end{split}

We conclude that

\frac{\left\|L_{k}\right\|_{L^{1}(-1,1)}}{\left\|L_{k}^{(k)}\right\|_{L^{2}(-1,1)}}=\frac{k!}{(2k)!}\sqrt{\frac{2^{2k+1}}{2k+1}}=\frac{1}{(2k-1)!!\sqrt{k+\frac{1}{2}}}.

∎
