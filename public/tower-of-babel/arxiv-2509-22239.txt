Permutation closure for multiple context-free languages
Andrew Duncan, Murray Elder, Lisa Frenkel, Mengfan Lyu

Abstract
. We prove that the permutation closure of a multiple context-free language is multiple context-free, which extends work of Okhotin and Sorokin [LATA 2020] who showed closure under cyclic shift , and complements work of Brandstädt [1981, RAIRO Inform. Théor.] (resp. Brough et al. [2016, Discrete Math. Theor. Comput. Sci.]) who showed the same result for regular, context-sensitive, recursively enumerable (resp. EDT0L and ET0L) languages. In contrast to Okhotin and Sorokin who work with grammars, our proof uses restricted tree stack automata due to Denkinger [DLT 2016].

20F10, 68Q45

1.
Introduction

Multiple context-free
languages are a generalisation of context-free languages, introduced by
Seki, Matsumura, Fujii and Kasami
[
5
,
11
]
. Each multiple context-free language can be parameterised by a positive integer
k
, where a language is
k
-multiple context-free if it is generated by a
k
-multiple context-free grammar, or equivalently it is accepted by a
k
-restricted tree-stack automaton
[
3
]
. Context-free languages are exactly the
1
-multiple context-free languages.

For a language
L
and positive natural number
N
,
Brandstädt
[
1
]
considered the languages

C^{N}(L)=\left\{w_{\sigma(1)}\cdots w_{\sigma(N)}\middle|w_{1}\cdots w_{N}\in L,\sigma\in S_{N}\right\}

where
S_{N}
is the symmetric group on
N
letters,
and showed that regular, context-sensitive and recursively enumerable languages are closed under the operator
C^{N}
.
Brough, Ciobanu, Elder and Zetzsche

[
2
]
showed that ET0L and EDT0L languages are also closed under the operator
C^{N}
.
It is clear that if the Parikh image of a language
L
is semi-linear then so is that of the permutation closure of
L
, so having semi-linear Parikh image is closed under
C^{N}
.
The operator
C^{2}
is called
cyclic shift
. Context-free languages are closed under cyclic shift

[
7
,
9
]
,
but not closed under
C^{3}

[
1
]
.

Recently Okhotin and Sorokin
[
8
]
showed that, for any positive integer
k
, the class of
k
-multiple context-free languages is preserved under cyclic shift.
Here we radically
extend this result to show that being multiple context-free is closed under the operator
C^{N}
for any
N
.

Theorem A
.

Let
k,N\in\mathbb{N}_{+}
. The permutation closure
C^{N}(L)
of a
k
-multiple context free language
L
is
(k+N+3)
-multiple context-free.

It follows, for example, that
C^{N}
of a context-free language is
(N+4)
-multiple context-free (and ET0L by
[
2
]
).

In contrast to Okhotin and Sorokin
[
8
]
who work with grammars, our proof relies on the
k
-restricted tree stack characterisation
[
3
]
due to Denkinger.
The proof of our main lemma (
Lemma

3.6
) is technical,
but the main idea is that one can modify a restricted tree stack automaton by enlarging the state set and vertex label alphabet,
to record a finite amount of additional data in each vertex label of the tree stack which can be used to simulate reading factors of a word
w_{1}\cdots w_{N}
in an alternative order (according to a fixed permutation
\sigma\in S_{N}
).

Okhotin and Sorokin also consider the class of
well-nested

k
-multiple context-free languages, and show that the cyclic shift of a well-nested
k
-multiple context-free language is a well-nested
(k+1)
-multiple context-free language.

Note that
Brandstädt’s definition of

C^{N}(L)

is for a fixed value
N
.
Suppose instead we define

D(L)=\{w_{\sigma(1)}\ldots w_{\sigma(N)}\mid w_{1}\ldots w_{N}\in L,\sigma\in\cup_{N=1}^{\infty}S_{N}\}.

Then even regular languages are not closed under the operator
D
, since for example

D((abc)^{*})=\text{MIX}_{3}
, which is
2
-multiple context-free
[
10
]
and not context-free.

The article is organised as follows. In
Section

2
we furnish the definition of
k
-restricted tree stack automaton, fix notation and state some basic results, then in
Section

3
we prove the main result.

2.
Preliminaries

2.1.
Notation

We use
\mathbb{N}_{0},\mathbb{N}_{+}
to denote the sets of natural numbers starting at
0,1
respectively, and write
[i,j]
for the set of integers

\{k\in\mathbb{Z}\,\mid\,i\leqslant k\leqslant j\}
when
i,j\in\mathbb{Z},i\leqslant j
.
If
\Sigma
is a set, we let denote
\Sigma^{*}
the set of all words (finite length strings) with letters from
\Sigma
, including the
empty word

\varepsilon
of length
0
,
\Sigma^{+}
the set of positive length words with letters from
\Sigma
, and
|\Sigma|
the cardinality of the set.
We use
\sqcup
to denote the disjoint union of two sets. An
alphabet
is a finite set.
For any set
\Sigma
and letter
x\not\in\Sigma
we let
\Sigma_{x}
denote
\Sigma\sqcup\{x\}
.
If
u,v\in\Sigma^{*}
we say
v
is a
factor
of
u
if there exist
\alpha,\beta\in\Sigma^{*}
so that
u=\alpha v\beta
.

For the grammar definition of a
k
-multiple context free language see
[
11
,
10
]
.
We require the following well-known facts.

Proposition 2.1

(See for example
[
11
]
)
.

If
\Sigma,\Gamma
are alphabets,
L,L_{1}\subseteq\Sigma^{*}
are
k
-multiple context-free and
R\subseteq\Sigma^{*}
is a regular language
then

(i)

L\cup L_{1}
is
k
-multiple context-free;

(ii)

L\cap R
is
k
-multiple context-free;

(iii)

\psi(L),\phi^{-1}(L)
are
k
-multiple context-free for any homomorphisms
\psi\colon\Sigma^{*}\to\Gamma^{*},\phi\colon\Gamma^{*}\to\Sigma^{*}
.

2.2.
Tree stack automata

We recall the definitions of trees and tree stacks, following
[
4
]
(see also
[
3
,
6
]
).

Definition 2.2

(Tree with labels in
C
)
.

Let
C
be an alphabet and
@\not\in C
.
Let
\xi
be a partial function from
\mathbb{N}_{+}^{*}
to
C_{@}
(
=C\sqcup\{@\}
) with a non-empty, finite and prefix-closed domain denoted
\operatorname{dom}(\xi)
such that
\xi(\nu)=@
if and only if
\nu=\varepsilon
.
Then
\xi
defines a labelled rooted tree
T
with vertices
V(T)=\operatorname{dom}(\xi)
, where
\nu\in V(T)
is labelled
\xi(\nu)
, root
\varepsilon
,
and edges
E(T)=\{\{\nu,\nu n\}\mid\nu n\in\operatorname{dom}(\xi)\}
.

We will occasionally abuse notation and write
\xi=T
.
Note that
\operatorname{dom}(\xi)
being non-empty and prefix-closed ensures that every tree with labels in
C
includes a root vertex
\varepsilon
, and every vertex is connected to the root vertex
\varepsilon
by an edge path.

Definition 2.3

(Below/above, child/parent)
.

In a rooted tree
T
with vertex
\nu
, we say a vertex is
below

\nu
if it lies in the connected component of
T\setminus\{\nu\}
containing the root, and
above

\nu
if it is not below (so
\nu
itself is considered to be
above

\nu
by this definition). Equivalently,
\mu
is below
\nu
if
\mu
is not a prefix of
\nu
, and above
\nu
if
\nu
is a prefix of
\mu
.
If
\nu n\in\operatorname{dom}(\xi)
for some
n\in N_{+}
then
\nu n
is above
\nu
by definition. As standard, we call
\nu n
a

child
of
\nu
, and say
\nu
is the
parent
of
\nu n
.

Note that no vertex is below the root by this definition, and
that there is no requirement in the definition of a tree that a vertex
\rho
with a child
\rho n
,
n>1
, also has a child
\rho m
for
m\in[1,n-1]
.

Definition 2.4

(Unlabelled tree)
.

Let
T
be the labelled rooted tree defined by
\xi\colon\mathbb{N}_{+}^{*}\to C_{@}
as in
Definition

2.2
. Define the
unlabelled tree
associated to
T
,
denoted by
\overline{T}
, as the tree with unlabelled vertices
V(\overline{T})=\operatorname{dom}(\xi)
and edges
E(\overline{T})=E(T)
.
In this case we say
T

has unlabelled tree

\overline{T}
.

(Tree stack over

Let
\xi\colon\mathbb{N}_{+}^{*}\to C_{@}

.

To define a tree stack automaton, we
define the following predicates and (partial) functions.
The predicates are used to check if the pointer of a tree stack has a certain label, namely:

(1)

\operatorname{eq}(c)=\{(\xi,\rho)\in\operatorname{TS}(C)\mid\xi(\rho)=c\}
where
c\in C_{@}
(so
(\xi,\rho)\in\operatorname{eq}(c)
, or
\operatorname{eq}(c)
is true for the tree stack
(\xi,\rho)
, if the label of
\rho
is
c
)

(2)

\operatorname{true}

(for any tree stack regardless of the current label pointed to);

and the
following (partial) functions enable
both construction of and movement around a tree:

(1)

\operatorname{id}\colon\operatorname{TS}(C)\rightarrow\operatorname{TS}(C)
where
\operatorname{id}(\xi,\rho)=(\xi,\rho)
for every
(\xi,\rho)\in\operatorname{TS}(C)
. This is a function that makes no change to the tree stack

(2)

For each
(n,c)\in\mathbb{N}_{+}\times C
let
\operatorname{push}_{n}(c)\colon\operatorname{TS}(C)\rightarrow\operatorname{TS}(C)
be the
partial function defined, whenever
\rho n
is not an address in
\operatorname{dom}(\xi)
,
by
\operatorname{push}_{n}(c)((\xi,\rho))=(\xi^{\prime},\rho n)
,
where
\xi^{\prime}
is the partial function defined as

\xi^{\prime}(\nu)=\begin{cases}\xi(\nu),&\nu\in\operatorname{dom}(\xi)\\
c,&\nu=\rho n\end{cases}.

Thus
\operatorname{push}_{n}(c)
adds a new vertex
\rho n
labelled by
c
, a new edge from
\rho
to
\rho n
and moves the pointer from
\rho
to
\rho n
, provided
\rho n
was not already a vertex of
\xi

(3)

For each
n\in\mathbb{N}_{+}
let
\operatorname{up}_{n}\colon\operatorname{TS}(C)\rightarrow\operatorname{TS}(C)
be
the partial function defined, whenever
\rho n\in\operatorname{dom}(\xi)
, by
\operatorname{up}_{n}((\xi,\rho))=(\xi,\rho n)
.
So
\operatorname{up}_{n}
moves the pointer of a tree stack to an existing child address

(4)

\operatorname{down}\colon\operatorname{TS}(C)\rightarrow\operatorname{TS}(C)
is a partial function defined as
\operatorname{down}(\xi,\rho n)=(\xi,\rho)
(so down moves the pointer from a vertex to its parent and may be applied to any tree stack unless its
pointer is already pointing to the root, which has no parent

(5)

For each
c\in C
let
\operatorname{set}(c)\colon\operatorname{TS}(C)\rightarrow\operatorname{TS}(C)
be the function defined,
whenever
\rho\neq\varepsilon
, by
\operatorname{set}(c)((\xi,\rho))=(\xi^{\prime},\rho)
, where
\xi^{\prime}
is the partial function defined as

\xi^{\prime}(\nu)=\begin{cases}\xi(\nu),&\nu\in\operatorname{dom}(\xi)\setminus\{\rho\}\\
c,&\nu=\rho\end{cases}.

Thus
\operatorname{set}(c)
relabels the vertex pointed to by the pointer with the letter
c\in C
(and may be applied to any tree stack except
(\xi,\varepsilon)
).

Note that none of the partial functions
\operatorname{id}
,
\operatorname{push}_{n}(c)
,
\operatorname{up}_{n}
,
\operatorname{down}
or
\operatorname{set}(c)
remove any vertices from a tree stack, and only
\operatorname{push}_{n}(c)
adds a vertex.

Definition 2.6

(Tree stack automata)
.

A
tree stack automaton (TSA)
is a tuple

\mathcal{A}=(Q,C,\Sigma,q_{0},\delta,Q_{f})

where
Q
is a finite
state set
,
C_{@}
is an alphabet of
tree-labels
(with
@\notin C
),
\Sigma
is the alphabet of
terminals
,
q_{0}\in Q
is the
initial state
,

Q_{f}\subseteq Q
is the subset of
final states
, and
\delta=\{\sigma_{1},\dots,\sigma_{|\delta|}\}

is a finite set of
transition rules
of the form
\sigma_{i}=(q,x,p,f,q^{\prime})
where

(1)

q,q^{\prime}\in Q
are the
source
and
target
states of
\sigma_{i}
,

(2)

x\in\Sigma_{\varepsilon}
is the
input letter
for
\sigma_{i}
,

(3)

p
is the predicate of
\sigma_{i}
(i.e. either
\operatorname{eq}(c)
, for some
c\in C_{@}
, or
\operatorname{true}
),

(4)

f
is the
instruction
of
\sigma_{i}
, so is one of the functions
\operatorname{id}
,
\operatorname{push}_{n}(c)
,
\operatorname{up}_{n}
,
\operatorname{down}
, or
\operatorname{set}(c)
for
c\in C,n\in\mathbb{N}_{+}
.

Note that each coordinate of
\mathcal{A}
has a finite description.
The tree stack automaton operates by starting in state
q_{0}
with a tree stack initialised at
(\{(\varepsilon,@)\},\varepsilon)
,
reading each letter of an input word
w\in\Sigma^{*}
with arbitrarily many
\varepsilon
letters interspersed, applying transition rules from
\delta
, if they are allowed, (eg.
\operatorname{push}_{n}
unless
\rho n
is already a vertex; the target state of the applied transition should also coincide with the source state of the following one). In other words, a composition of transitions is allowed if it determines
a well defined function which consumes input letters whilst moving between tree stacks.

Definition 2.7

(Run; accepted word; language of a TSA)
.

An allowed sequence of transitions
\tau_{1}\cdots\tau_{r}\in\delta^{*}
is called a
run
of the automaton.
A run is
valid

if it
finishes in a state in
Q_{f}
; if
w\in\Sigma^{*}
is the input word consumed during a valid run we say that
\mathcal{A}

accepts

w
(the definition does not
a priori

put a requirement on the final position of the pointer, but see
2.15
).
Define the language
L(\mathcal{A})
to be the set of all words
w\in\Sigma^{*}
accepted by
\mathcal{A}
.
