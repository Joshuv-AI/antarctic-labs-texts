A note on the Macías topology
Jhixon Macías and Reyes Ortiz

Abstract
. In this paper, we study some properties of the closure operator in the Macías topology on infinite integral domains. Moreover, under certain conditions, we present topological proofs of the infiniteness of maximal ideals and non-associated irreducible elements, taking advantage of the hyperconnectedness of the Macías topology. Additionally, some problems are proposed.



2020 Mathematics Subject Classification
54A05; 54G05; 54H11

1.
Introduction

In 1955, H. Furstenberg introduced a topology
\tau_{F}
on the integers
\mathbb{Z}
generated by the collection of arithmetic progressions of the form
a+b\mathbb{Z}
(
a,b\in\mathbb{Z},b\geq 1
) and with it presented the first topological proof of the infinitude of prime numbers, see
[
4
]
. A couple of years earlier (1953), M. Brown had introduced a topology
\tau_{G}
on the natural numbers
\mathbb{N}
that turns out to be coarser than Furstenberg’s topology induced on
\mathbb{N}
. This latter topology is generated by the collection of arithmetic progressions
a+b\mathbb{N}
with
a\in\mathbb{N}
and
b\in\mathbb{N}\cup\{0\}
such that
\gcd(a,b)=1
. It was not until 1959 that the topology introduced by Brown was popularized by S. Golomb (now known as Golomb’s topology) who in
[
5
]
proves that Dirichlet’s theorem on arithmetic progressions is equivalent to the set of prime numbers
\mathbb{P}
being dense in the topological space
(\mathbb{N},\tau_{G})
. Golomb also presents a topological proof of the infinitude of prime numbers, verifying that Furstenberg’s argument also works on
(\mathbb{N},\tau_{G})
. In 1997, a
Golomb topology
on any integral domain was defined by J. Knopemacher and S. Porubsky
[
6
]
and is also considered by P. L. Clark in
[
1
]
. In both cases, the proofs of Golomb and Furstenberg are generalized, respectively. On the one hand, J. Knopfmacher and S. Porubsky demonstrate that any integral domain (that is not a field) has an infinite number of maximal ideals if the set of its units is not open in its respective
Golomb space
, and on the other hand, P. L. Clark proves that a semiprimitive integral domain (that is not a field) in which every nonzero nonunit element has at least one irreducible divisor has an infinite number of non-associated irreducible elements.

In
[
3
]
the
Macías topology

\tau_{M}\subset\tau_{G}
is introduced, which is generated by the collection of sets
\sigma_{n}:=\{m\in\mathbb{N}:\gcd(n,m)=1\}
. Additionally, some of its properties are studied. For example, the topological space
(\mathbb{N},\tau_{M})
does not satisfy the
\mathrm{T}_{0}
separation axiom, satisfies (vacuously) the
\mathrm{T}_{4}
separation axiom, is ultraconnected, hyperconnected (therefore connected, locally connected, path-connected), not countably compact (therefore not compact), but it is limit point compact and pseudocompact. On the other hand, for
n,m\in\mathbb{N}
, it holds that
\textbf{cl}_{(\mathbb{N},\tau_{M})}(\{nm\})=\textbf{cl}_{(\mathbb{N},\tau_{M})}(\{n\})\cap\textbf{cl}_{(\mathbb{N},\tau_{M})}(\{m\})
where
\textbf{cl}_{(\mathbb{N},\tau_{M})}(\{n\})
denotes the closure of the singleton set
\{n\}
in the topological space
(\mathbb{N},\tau_{M})
. Furthermore, in
[
7
]
(usind the topology
\tau_{M}
), a topological proof of the infinitude of prime numbers is presented (different from the proofs of Furstenberg and Golomb). In the same work, the infinitude of any non-empty subset of prime numbers is characterized in the following sense: if
A\subset\mathbb{P}
(non-empty), then
A
is infinite if and only if
A
is dense in
(\mathbb{N},\tau_{M})
, see
[
7
, Theorem 4]
. The
Macías topology
on any integral domain was defined by J. Macías
[
8
]
, and some of its properties were studied (particularly on principal ideal domains). Moreover, a generalization of Macías’ proof was presented in the following sense: any principal ideal domain (that is not a field) has an infinite number of maximal ideals if the set of its units does not form an open set in the
Macías space
.

In this work, we study some properties of the closure operator in the Macías topology on infinite integral domains (Section
3
). In particular, we characterize when the closure of an element is the ideal generated by it (Theorem
3.2
), as well as topologically characterizing when a unique factorization domain is a principal ideal domain (Corollary
3.18
). We also proved that
\widetilde{M(R)}
and
M(R)
are ultraconnected. Additionally, at the end of this section, we propose some open problems. Moreover, in Section
4
, under certain conditions, we present two topological proofs of the infinitude of maximal ideals on infinite integral domains (Subsection
4.7
) and non-associated irreducible elements on infinite
Furstenberg domains
(Subsection
4.10
), taking advantage of the hyperconnectedness of the Macías topology.

2.
Preliminaries

Throughout this paper, we denote by
R
a commutative ring with identity
1=1_{R}\neq 0_{R}
, and without zero divisors (an integral domain) that is not a field (hence infinite). If an additional property on
R
is required, it will be mentioned as appropriate. In general, for
X\subset R
,
X^{0}=X\setminus\{0\}
. On the other hand, let us denote by
R^{\times}
and
R^{\#}
the set of units of
R
and the set
R^{0}\setminus R^{\times}
, respectively.

For each
r\in R
, define
\sigma_{r}:=\{s\in R:\langle r\rangle+\langle s\rangle=R\}
where for all
r\in R
,
\langle r\rangle
is the ideal generated by
r
. Let
\beta_{R}:=\{\sigma_{r}:r\in R\}
, that is,
\sigma_{r}
is the set of all elements that are
comaximal
to
r
. The set
\beta_{R}
is a basis for some topology on
R

[
8
, Theorem 2.1]
. We denote by
\widetilde{M(R)}
the topological space
(R,\tau_{R})
, where
\tau_{R}
is the topology generated by
\beta_{R}
.
The Macías topological space

M(R)
is the topological space
(R^{0},\tau_{R^{0}})
where
\tau_{R^{0}}
is the topology generated by the basis
\beta_{R^{0}}:=\{\sigma_{k}^{0}:k\in R^{0}\}
.

In
[
2
]
, the topological space
\widetilde{G(R)}
is defined, which is obtained by equipping
R
with the topology generated by the collection of
coprime cosets

{x+I}
where
x\in R
and
I
is a nonzero ideal of
R
.
The Golomb topological space

G(R)
is obtained by equipping
R^{0}
with the subspace topology of
\widetilde{G(R)}
.

The following theorem summarizes the properties of
\widetilde{M(R)}
and
M(R)
presented in
[
8
]
.

Theorem 2.1
.

The following propositions hold over
R
:

(1)

\sigma_{rs}=\sigma_{r}\cap\sigma_{s}
for all
r,s\in R
.

(2)

u\in R^{\times}
if and only if
\sigma_{u}=R
.

(3)

If
u\in R^{\times}
, then
u\in\sigma_{r}
for all
r\in R
.

(4)

0\in\sigma_{k}
if and only if
k\in R^{\times}
.

(5)

\textbf{cl}_{\widetilde{M(R)}}(\{0\})\subset\textbf{cl}_{\widetilde{M(R)}}(\{r\})
for all
r\in R
.

(6)

\textbf{cl}_{\widetilde{M(R)}}(\{ru\})=\textbf{cl}_{\widetilde{M(R)}}(\{ur\})=\textbf{cl}_{\widetilde{M(R)}}(\{r\})
for all
r\in R
and
u\in R^{\times}
.

(7)

If
u\in R^{\times}
, then
\textbf{cl}_{M(R)}(\{u\})=R^{0}
and
\textbf{cl}_{\widetilde{M(R)}}(\{u\})=R
.

(8)

\widetilde{M(R)}
and
M(R)
are topological semigroups.

(9)

\widetilde{M(R)}
and
M(R)
are hyperconnected.

(10)

Every open set in
\widetilde{M(R)}
(except for
\sigma_{0}=R^{\times}
) is open in
\widetilde{G(R)}
(The Macías topology is coarser than the Golomb topology).

(11)

M(R)
is indiscrete if and only if
R
is a field.

(12)

If R is a principal ideal domain then for any irreducible element
p
in
R
,
\textbf{cl}_{\widetilde{M(R)}}(\{p\})=\langle p\rangle
. Moreover, for any
x,y\in R
,
\textbf{cl}_{\widetilde{M(R)}}(\{xy\})=\textbf{cl}_{\widetilde{M(R)}}(\{x\})\cap\textbf{cl}_{\widetilde{M(R)}}(\{y\}).

(13)

If
R
is a principal ideal domain, then
\widetilde{M(R)}
and
M(R)
are ultraconnected and do not satisfy the separation axiom
\mathrm{T}_{0}
.

3.
Other Closure Properties on
\widetilde{M(R)}
and
M(R)

The results presented in this section were obtained while attempting to answer the following question.

Question 3.1
.

Let
x\in R
. When does it hold that
\mathbf{cl}_{\widetilde{M(R)}}(\{x\})=\langle x\rangle
?

The following theorem shows that it is necessary and sufficient for
\langle x\rangle
to be closed in
\widetilde{M(R)}
.

Theorem 3.2
.

Let
x\in R
. Then
\mathbf{cl}_{\widetilde{M(R)}}(\{x\})=\langle x\rangle
if and only if
\langle x\rangle
is closed in
\widetilde{M(R)}
. Similarly, if
x\in R^{0}
, then
\mathbf{cl}_{M(R)}(\{x\})=\langle x\rangle^{0}
if and only if
\langle x\rangle^{0}
is closed in
M(R)
.

Proof.

It is clear that
\mathbf{cl}_{\widetilde{M(R)}}(\{x\})=\langle x\rangle
implies that
\langle x\rangle
is closed in
\widetilde{M(R)}
. On the other hand, suppose
\langle x\rangle
is closed in
\widetilde{M(R)}
. Let
x_{0}\in\langle x\rangle
and
\sigma_{r}\in\mathcal{B}_{R}
such that
x_{0}\in\sigma_{r}
. Then
R=\langle x_{0}\rangle+\langle r\rangle\subset\langle x\rangle+\langle r\rangle\subset R
. Thus
x\in\sigma_{r}
, so
x_{0}\in\mathbf{cl}_{\widetilde{M(R)}}(\{x\})
and
\langle x\rangle\subset\mathbf{cl}_{\widetilde{M(R)}}(\{x\})
. Finally, since
\langle x\rangle
is closed by hypothesis,
\mathbf{cl}_{\widetilde{M(R)}}(\{x\})\subset\langle x\rangle
.
∎

Lemma 3.3
.

Let
x\in R
. Then
x\in\sigma_{x}
if and only if
x\in R^{\times}
.

Proof.

If
x\in\sigma_{x}
, then
R=\langle x\rangle+\langle x\rangle=\langle x\rangle
. Therefore,
x\in R^{\times}
. On the other hand, if
x\in R^{\times}
, then
x\in\sigma_{x}
(see Theorem
2.1
item (3)).
∎

We know that when
x\in R^{\times}
, we have
\mathbf{cl}_{\widetilde{M(R)}}(\{x\})=R
; moreover,
\mathbf{cl}_{\widetilde{M(R)}}(\{0\})\subset\mathbf{cl}_{\widetilde{M(R)}}(\{x\})
for all
x\in R
(see Theorem
2.1
, item (5) and item (7)). The following theorem shows, in general, what happens when
x\in R^{\#}
.

Theorem 3.4
.

Let
x\in R^{\#}
. Then
\langle x\rangle\subset\mathbf{cl}_{\widetilde{M(R)}}(\{x\})\subset R\setminus\sigma_{x}
. Similarly,
\langle x\rangle^{0}\subset\mathbf{cl}_{M(R)}(\{x\})\subset R^{0}\setminus\sigma^{0}_{x}
.

Proof.

That
\langle x\rangle\subset\mathbf{cl}_{\widetilde{M(R)}}(\{x\})
is given by Theorem
3.2
. On the other hand, since
x
is not a unit,
x\notin\sigma_{x}
(by Lemma
3.3
). Therefore,
R\setminus\sigma_{x}
is a closed set containing
x
, so
\mathbf{cl}_{\widetilde{M(R)}}(\{x\})\subset R\setminus\sigma_{x}
.
∎

Must note, the previous results hold in general for commutative rings with identity. But for the following results, one required to avoid zero divisors.

Corollary 3.5
.

Let
x,y\in R
. Then
\mathbf{cl}_{\widetilde{M(R)}}(\{xy\})=\mathbf{cl}_{\widetilde{M(R)}}(\{x\})\cap\mathbf{cl}_{\widetilde{M(R)}}(\{y\})
. Similarly, for
x,y\in R^{0}
, we have that
\mathbf{cl}_{M(R)}(\{xy\})=\mathbf{cl}_{M(R)}(\{x\})\cap\mathbf{cl}_{M(R)}(\{y\})
.

Proof.

By Theorem
3.4
,
\langle x\rangle\subset\mathbf{cl}_{\widetilde{M(R)}}(\{x\})
and
\langle y\rangle\subset\mathbf{cl}_{\widetilde{M(R)}}(\{y\})
. Note that
xy\in\langle xy\rangle\subset\langle x\rangle\subset\mathbf{cl}_{\widetilde{M(R)}}(\{x\})
. Similarly,
xy\in\mathbf{cl}_{\widetilde{M(R)}}(\{y\})
. Thus,
xy\in\mathbf{cl}_{\widetilde{M(R)}}(\{x\})\cap\mathbf{cl}_{\widetilde{M(R)}}(\{y\})
, and therefore,
\mathbf{cl}_{\widetilde{M(R)}}(xy)\subset\mathbf{cl}_{\widetilde{M(R)}}(\{x\})\cap\mathbf{cl}_{\widetilde{M(R)}}(\{y\})
. On the other hand, let
r\in\mathbf{cl}_{\widetilde{M(R)}}(\{x\})\cap\mathbf{cl}_{\widetilde{M(R)}}(\{y\})
. Let
\sigma_{k}\in\mathcal{B}_{R}
such that
r\in\sigma_{k}
. Then
x,y\in\sigma_{k}
, and consequently
xy\in\sigma_{k}
. Thus,
r\in\mathbf{cl}_{\widetilde{M(R)}}(\{xy\})
, and therefore
\mathbf{cl}_{\widetilde{M(R)}}\{x\}\cap\mathbf{cl}_{\widetilde{M(R)}}\{y\}\subset\mathbf{cl}_{\widetilde{M(R)}}\{xy\}
.
∎

Example 3.6
.

Consider the commutative ring with identity
R=\mathbb{Z}\times\mathbb{Z}
, which has zero divisors. Consider
\widetilde{M(R)}\simeq\widetilde{M(\mathbb{Z})}\times\widetilde{M(\mathbb{Z})}
(with the product topology). Note that

\mathbf{cl}_{\widetilde{M(R)}}(\{(2,0)\})=2\mathbb{Z}\times\{0\}\ \ \text{and}\ \ \mathbf{cl}_{\widetilde{M(R)}}(\{(0,3)\})=\{0\}\times 3\mathbb{Z},

which implies that

\mathbf{cl}_{\widetilde{M(R)}}(\{(2,0)\})\cap\mathbf{cl}_{\widetilde{M(R)}}(\{(0,3)\})=\emptyset.

On the other hand,

\mathbf{cl}_{\widetilde{M(R)}}(\{(2,0)\cdot(0,3)\})=\mathbf{cl}_{\widetilde{M(R)}}(\{(0,0)\})=\{0\}\times\{0\},

so that

\mathbf{cl}_{\widetilde{M(R)}}(\{(2,0)\})\cap\mathbf{cl}_{\widetilde{M(R)}}(\{(0,3)\})\neq\mathbf{cl}_{\widetilde{M(R)}}(\{(2,0)\cdot(0,3)\}).

Corollary
3.5

Proposition 3.9
.

Let
x\in R^{\times}
. Then
\mathbf{cl}_{\widetilde{M(R)}}(\{x\})=\mathbb{j}(\langle x\rangle)
. Similarly, let
x\in R^{\#}
. Then
\mathbf{cl}_{M(R)}(\{x\})=\mathbb{j}(\langle x\rangle)^{0}
.

Proof.

Let
r\in\mathbb{j}(\langle x\rangle)
. Let
\sigma_{k}\in\mathcal{B}_{R}
such that
r\in\sigma_{k}
.
Claim:

\langle x\rangle+\langle k\rangle=R
. If this were not the case, then there would exist a maximal ideal
\mathfrak{m}
such that
\langle x\rangle+\langle k\rangle\subset\mathfrak{m}
, but then
\langle x\rangle\subset\mathfrak{m}
, so
r\in\mathfrak{m}
. Thus,
R=\langle r\rangle+\langle k\rangle\subset\mathfrak{m}
, which would be absurd. Therefore, the
Claim
is true, which implies that
x\in\sigma_{k}
and thus
r\in\mathbf{cl}_{\widetilde{M(R)}}(\{x\})
. Hence,
\mathbb{j}(\langle x\rangle)\subset\mathbf{cl}_{\widetilde{M(R)}}(\{x\})
. On the other hand, let
r\in R\setminus\mathbb{j}(\langle x\rangle)
(
r\notin\mathbb{j}(\langle x\rangle)
). Then, there exists a maximal ideal
\mathfrak{m}
such that
\langle x\rangle\subset\mathfrak{m}
and
r\notin\mathfrak{m}
. Since
\mathfrak{m}
is maximal, the coset
r+\mathfrak{m}
is invertible, meaning that
\langle r\rangle+\mathfrak{m}=R
, so there exists
\alpha\in R
and
m\in\mathfrak{m}
such that
\alpha r+m=1
. Thus,
r\in\sigma_{m}
. Now, note that
\sigma_{m}\cap\mathfrak{m}=\emptyset
, because if there existed
y\in\sigma_{m}\cap\mathfrak{m}
, then
R=\langle y\rangle+\langle m\rangle\subset\mathfrak{m}
, which is absurd. Thus,
\sigma_{m}\cap\langle x\rangle=\emptyset
, so
x\notin\sigma_{m}
. Therefore,
r\notin\mathbf{cl}_{\widetilde{M(R)}}(\{x\})
. Hence,
\mathbf{cl}_{\widetilde{M(R)}}(\{x\})\subset\mathbb{j}(\langle x\rangle)
.
∎

It is said that
x,y\in R
have a greatest common divisor, denoted by
\gcd(x,y)
, if there is a unique minimal principal ideal containing the ideal generated by two given elements. A
GCD domain
is an integral domain with the property that any two elements have a greatest common divisor. We say that
x
and
y
are
coprime
if
\gcd(x,y)=1
. Comaximality always implies coprimality, but it is well known that the converse is not always true, particularly when
R
is not a principal ideal domain.

Theorem 3.14
.

If
R
is a GCD domain where coprimality implies comaximality (
\gcd(x,y)=1\Rightarrow\langle x\rangle+\langle y\rangle=R
), then
R\setminus\sigma_{p}=\langle p\rangle
if
p\in R
is an irreducible element (and therefore prime).

Proof.

By Theorem
3.4
, we know that
\langle p\rangle\subset R\setminus\sigma_{p}
. Now, let
x_{0}\in R\setminus\sigma_{p}
. Then
x_{0}\notin\sigma_{p}
, equivalently,
x_{0}
is not comaximal with
p
. Then, by hypothesis,
x_{0}
is not coprime with
p
, and since
p
is prime, it necessarily follows that
x_{0}\in\langle p\rangle
. Therefore,
R\setminus\sigma_{p}\subset\langle p\rangle
.
∎

Example 3.15
.

Theorem
3.14
, along with Theorem
3.4
, gives us a more direct proof of
[
8
, Theorem 3.1]
. Of course, if
R
is a principal ideal domain, then
R
is a GCD domain where coprimality implies comaximality. Therefore, in this case, for every irreducible element
p\in R
, by virtue of the aforementioned theorems,
\mathbf{cl}_{\widetilde{M(R)}}(\{p\})=\langle p\rangle
.

Theorem 3.16
.

If
R
is a GCD domain such that for every element
x\in R^{\#}
, it holds that
R\setminus\sigma_{x}=\langle x\rangle
, then coprimality implies comaximality.

Proof.

Let
x,y\in R^{\#}
be such that
\gcd(x,y)=1
. If
x
and
y
are not comaximal, then
x\notin\sigma_{y}
and
y\notin\sigma_{x}
. Then,
x\in R\setminus\sigma_{y}=\langle y\rangle
and
y\in R\setminus\sigma_{x}=\langle x\rangle
, which cannot be possible because
x
and
y
are coprime. Therefore, coprimality implies comaximality.
∎

Theorem 3.17
.

If
R
is a unique factorization domain, then for every irreducible element
p\in R
, it holds that
R\setminus\sigma_{p}=\langle p\rangle
if and only if coprimality implies comaximality.

Proof.

Suppose that
R\setminus\sigma_{p}=\langle p\rangle
for every irreducible element
p\in R
. Let
x,y\in R^{\times}
be coprime (
\gcd(x,y)=1
). Since
R
is a unique factorization domain, we can write
x=p_{1}^{\alpha_{1}}\cdot p_{2}^{\alpha_{2}}\cdots p_{n}^{\alpha_{n}}
and
y=q_{1}^{\beta_{1}}\cdot q_{2}^{\beta_{2}}\cdots q_{m}^{\beta_{m}}
. Since
x
and
y
are coprime, each
p_{i}
is coprime to each
q_{j}
. Now, by Theorem
2.1
, item (1), we have that
R\setminus\sigma_{x}=\bigcup_{i=1}^{n}R\setminus\sigma_{p_{i}}
, and similarly,
R\setminus\sigma_{y}=\bigcup_{i=1}^{m}R\setminus\sigma_{q_{i}}
. Then, by hypothesis,
R\setminus\sigma_{x}=\bigcup_{i=1}^{n}\langle p_{i}\rangle
and
R\setminus\sigma_{y}=\bigcup_{i=1}^{m}\langle q_{i}\rangle
. If we assume that
x
and
y
are not comaximal, then
x\notin\sigma_{y}
and
y\notin\sigma_{x}
, which would imply that
x\in\langle q_{i}\rangle
for some
i
and
y\in\langle p_{j}\rangle
for some
j
, which is absurd. Therefore, coprimality implies comaximality. The converse follows from Theorem
3.14
.
∎

Moreover, it is well known that if
R
is a unique factorization domain, then
R
is a principal ideal domain if and only if coprimality implies comaximality.

Corollary 3.18
.

If
R
is a unique factorization domain, then
R
is a principal ideal domain if and only if for every irreducible element
p\in R
, it holds that
\langle p\rangle=\mathbf{cl}_{\widetilde{M(R)}}(\{p\})=R\setminus\sigma_{p}
.

To conclude this section, we would like to propose the following problems.

Problem 3.19
.

We know that in a principal ideal domain, the closure in
\widetilde{M(R)}
of an irreducible is the ideal generated by the irreducible. In what other structures does this hold?

Problem 3.20
.

Is there an
R
such that every principal ideal is closed in
\widetilde{M(R)}
? Note, for example, that if
R=\mathbb{Z}
, then
\langle 8\rangle
is not closed in
\widetilde{M(R)}
, since
\mathbf{cl}_{\widetilde{M(R)}}(\{8\})=\langle 2\rangle
.

4.
On the Infinitude of Non-associated Irreducible Elements and Maximal Ideals

In this section, we aim to present a topological proof (using the space
M(R)
) of the following propositions.

Proposition 4.1
.

[
6
, Theorem 14]

If
R
is such that
\#R^{\times}<\#R
, then the set of maximal ideals of
R
is infinite.

Proposition 4.2
.

[
1
, Theorem 3.1]

If
R
is a Furstenberg domain with
\#R^{\times}<\#R
, then
R
has an infinite number of non-associated irreducible elements.

It is worth mentioning that we will use a completely different argument than the one presented in
[
8
, Subsection 3.16]
, because, as follows from the results of Section
3
, at least in unique factorization domains, it only works in principal ideal domains. Furthermore, as suggested by those same results, there is not much control over the closure operator in arbitrary integral domains (that are not fields).

Before we proceed with the proofs of the propositions, consider the following results.

Theorem 4.3
.

If the set
R^{\times}
is not open in
\widetilde{M(R)}
, then the set
R^{\#}
is dense in
\widetilde{M(R)}
. Similarly,
R^{\times}
is not open in
M(R)
.

Proof.

If there exists
\sigma_{r}\in\mathcal{B}_{R}
such that
\sigma_{r}\cap R^{\#}=\emptyset
, then
\sigma_{r}\subset R^{\times}
and thus
R^{\times}=\sigma_{r}
(see Theorem
2.1
item (3)) would be open in
\widetilde{M(R)}
, which is a contradiction. Thus,
R^{\#}
is dense in
\widetilde{M(R)}
.
∎

Theorem 4.4
.

If
\#R^{\times}<\#R
, then the set
R^{\times}
is not open in
\widetilde{M(R)}
. Similarly,
R^{\times}
is not open in
M(R)
.

Proof.

Let
k\in R^{0}
. Since
R
has no zero divisors, the function
\iota:R\to\sigma_{k}
given by
r\to 1+rk
is an injection, and so
|R|\leq|\sigma_{k}|\leq|R|
. Consequently, every open set in
\widetilde{M(R)}
has the cardinality of
R
, so
R^{\times}
cannot be open in
\widetilde{M(R)}
.
∎

These results lead to the following lemma, which plays an important role in the proof of the propositions.

Lemma 4.5
.

If
\#R^{\times}<\#R
, then the set
R^{\#}
is dense in
\widetilde{M(R)}
and in
M(R)
.

Similarly, the following lemma will also be useful.

Lemma 4.6
.

[
9
, Problem 25A]

The union of finitely many nowhere dense subsets of a topological space is nowhere dense.

Proof.

Let
Y
be a topological space. Let
N_{1},N_{2},\dots,N_{k}
be nowhere dense sets in
Y
and consider the set
N:=\{N_{i}:i=1,2,\dots,k\}
.
Let
\mathcal{O}
be a non-empty open set in
Y
. Then, by definition of nowhere dense set, for each
N_{i}
there exists a non-empty open set
\mathcal{O}_{i}
in
Y
such that
\mathcal{O}_{i}\subset\mathcal{O}
and
N_{i}\cap\mathcal{O}_{i}=\emptyset
. Let
\mathcal{O}^{*}:=\{\mathcal{O}_{i}:i=1,2,\dots,k\}
. Then
\mathcal{O}^{*}
is an open set in
Y
such that
\mathcal{O}^{*}\subset\mathcal{O}
and
N\cap\mathcal{O}^{*}=\emptyset
. Therefore,
N
is nowhere dense in
Y
.
∎

4.7.
Proof of Proposition
4.1

We begin with the following lemma.

Lemma 4.8
.

Let
I
be a proper ideal of
R
. Then
I
is nowhere dense in
\widetilde{M(R)}
. Similarly,
I^{0}
is nowhere dense in
M(R)
.

Proof.

Suppose that
I
is dense in
\widetilde{M(R)}
. Let
x\in I
. Since
I
is dense in
\widetilde{M(R)}
,
\sigma_{x}\cap I\neq\emptyset
. Let
y\in\sigma_{x}\cap I
. Then,

R=\langle y\rangle+\langle x\rangle\subset\langle x\rangle+\langle x\rangle=\langle x\rangle\subset I\subset R,

which implies that
I=R
, a contradiction. Therefore,
I
cannot be dense in
\widetilde{M(R)}
. Since
\widetilde{M(R)}
is hyperconnected (see Theorem
2.1
item (9)),
I
must necessarily be nowhere dense in
\widetilde{M(R)}
.
∎

Now we are ready for the proof of Proposition
4.1
, for which we will work with
M(R)
.

Proof.

Let
\mathfrak{M}
be the set of maximal ideals of
R
. Every element of
R
that is not a unit belongs to some maximal ideal of
R
. Thus, we can write

R^{0}=R^{\times}\cup\bigcup_{\mathfrak{m}\in\mathfrak{M}}\mathfrak{m}^{0}.

Then,

R^{\#}=\bigcup_{\mathfrak{m}\in\mathfrak{M}}\mathfrak{m}^{0}.

If
\mathfrak{M}
were finite, by Lemma
4.6
and Lemma
4.8
,
R^{\#}
would be nowhere dense, which is absurd by Lemma
4.5
.
∎

Note that by using Theorem
4.3
and Lemma
4.8
, we obtain the following theorem.

Theorem 4.9
.

In general, if
R^{\times}
is not open in
M(R)
, then the set of maximal ideals of
R
is infinite.

4.10.
Proof of Proposition
4.2

We begin with a definition followed by a lemma.

Definition 4.11
.

[
1
]

A Furstenberg domain is a commutative ring with a multiplicative identity distinct from the associative identity, without zero divisors, in wich every nonzero nonunit is divisible by an irreducible element.

Lemma 4.12
.

The set
R^{\#}
is equal to the set
\{R^{0}\setminus\sigma_{p}^{0}:p\in\mathcal{I}\}
where
\mathcal{I}
is the set of non-associated irreducible elements of
R
.

Proof.

Let
x\in\langle p\rangle^{0}
with
p\in\mathcal{I}
. If
x\notin R^{0}\setminus\sigma_{p}^{0}
, then
R=\langle x\rangle+\langle p\rangle\subset\langle p\rangle\subset R
. This would imply that
p\in R^{\times}
, which is a contradiction. Therefore,
\langle p\rangle\setminus\{0\}\subset R^{0}\setminus\sigma_{p}^{0}
for all
p\in\mathcal{I}
. On the other hand, it is clear that
\{R^{0}\setminus\sigma_{p}^{0}:p\in\mathcal{I}\}\subset R^{\#}
since
R^{\times}\subset\sigma_{r}^{0}
for all
r\in R^{0}
(see Theorem
2.1
item (3)), in particular for all
p\in\mathcal{I}
. Now, since
R
is a Furstenberg domain, we have
R^{\#}=\{\langle p\rangle^{0}:p\in\mathcal{I}\}
(see
[
1
, Lemma 3.3]
) and so
R^{\#}\subset\{R^{0}\setminus\sigma_{p}^{0}:p\in\mathcal{I}\}
,
from which the equality follows.
∎

Now we are ready for the proof of Proposition
4.2
, for which we will work with
M(R)
.

Proof.

Note that for each
p\in\mathcal{I}
,
R^{0}\setminus\sigma_{p}^{0}
is a non-trivial closed set in
M(R)
and therefore it is nowhere dense in
M(R)
since
M(R)
is hyperconnected (véase Theorem
2.1
item (9)). Now, if
R
had a finite number of non-associated irreducible elements, then
R^{\#}
would be nowhere dense in
M(R)
, as it would be the finite union of nowhere dense sets in
M(R)
(see Lemma
4.12
and Lemma
4.6
), which is not possible by Lemma
4.8
. Hence,
R
has an infinite number of non-associated irreducible elements. ∎

Note that by using Theorem
4.3
and Lemma
4.8
, the following theorem is obtained.

Theorem 4.13
.

In general, if
R^{\times}
is not open in
M(R)
and
R
is a Furstenberg domain, then
R
has an infinite number of non-associated irreducible elements.
