Comparative fall risk of patients treated with novel androgen receptor antagonists in prostate cancer: a systematic review and meta-analysis
Authors: Chunhua Wei, Yuesu Cai, Zhuangcheng Huang, Jiacheng Dai, Shengyang Mo, Langping Ling
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ObjectiveAndrogen receptor pathway inhibitors (ARPIs) are cornerstone treatments for advanced prostate cancer; however, their potential to increase fall risk remains a significant clinical concern. This meta-analysis aims to provide a rigorous, drug-specific evaluation of the association between novel ARPIs and the risk of falls.MethodsWe systematically searched PubMed, Web of Science, Cochrane Library, and ClinicalTrials.gov for Phase 2 or 3 randomized controlled trials (RCTs) comparing ARPIs (enzalutamide, apalutamide, and darolutamide) with control groups (placebo or non-steroidal antiandrogens [NSAA]). The primary outcomes were risk ratios (RRs) for all-grade and grade ≥ 3 falls. To account for multiplicity across correlated outcomes and the limited number of studies, pooled RRs were estimated using random-effects models with the restricted maximum-likelihood (REML) method. All analyses were performed on a logarithmically transformed scale, with 97.5% confidence intervals (CIs). Prediction intervals (PIs) were calculated to assess the dispersion of effects. Subgroup analyses were stratified by specific ARPI agents, control types, and clinical stages.ResultsEleven RCTs involving 12,239 patients were included. Overall, ARPIs were significantly associated with the increased risk of all-grade falls (RR 2.00, 97.5% CI 1.46–2.73, P < 0.0001, I2 = 77.6%; PI 0.67–5.99) and grade ≥ 3 falls (RR 2.15, 97.5% CI 1.32–3.52, P = 0.0008, I2 = 0%; PI 1.15–4.02). However, risk profiles varied substantially across individual agents. Enzalutamide was associated with the highest risk increase (RR 2.55 vs. placebo, 97.5% CI 1.62–4.01, I2 = 79.2%; RR 2.47 vs. NSAA, 97.5% CI 1.14–5.37, I2 = 71.8%), followed by apalutamide (RR 1.65, 97.5% CI 0.77–3.52, I2 = 87.2%). In contrast, darolutamide demonstrated a favorable safety profile with no statistically significant increase in the risk of all-grade falls (RR 1.25, 97.5% CI 0.87–1.79, I2 = 0%) or severe falls (RR 1.31, 97.5% CI 0.34–5.00).ConclusionsCurrent evidence indicates that the increased risk of falls associated with ARPI therapy varies significantly among individual agents, rather than being a uniform class effect. While enzalutamide and apalutamide are statistically associated with elevated fall risk, darolutamide appears to maintain a more favorable safety profile. However, these drug-specific comparisons remain exploratory due to subgroup imbalances. Clinicians should consider proactive fall-risk assessments and individualized treatment selection, particularly for elderly or frail populations.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12885-026-16146-4.

Introduction

Androgen receptor pathway inhibitors (ARPIs), specifically enzalutamide, apalutamide, darolutamide, and abiraterone acetate, have become standard treatments for advanced prostate cancer [1, 2]. These agents have demonstrated significant efficacy across disease stages, from metastatic hormone-sensitive prostate cancer (mHSPC) to non-metastatic and metastatic castration-resistant prostate cancer (nmCRPC and mCRPC) [3–5]. However, as with all effective therapies, understanding their complete safety profile is essential for optimal clinical management.

Among the various adverse effects associated with ARPIs [6–8], fall risk represents a particularly concerning safety issue that has received relatively limited attention in systematic reviews [9, 10]. Falls in cancer patients can lead to serious consequences including fractures, head injuries, reduced mobility, loss of independence, and increased mortality [11, 12]. For prostate cancer patients, who are typically older adults often with multiple comorbidities, falls can significantly impact quality of life and treatment adherence.

The pathophysiological mechanisms underlying ARPI-associated fall risk are multifactorial and may include treatment-related fatigue, musculoskeletal weakness, cognitive effects, and potential interactions with age-related physiological changes [13, 14]. Different ARPIs may confer varying fall risks due to their distinct pharmacological properties, particularly their ability to cross the blood–brain barrier and cause central nervous system effects such as dizziness, cognitive impairment, and balance disturbances [15].

While individual clinical trials have reported fall incidence as adverse events [16], the magnitude of risk remains imprecise when viewed in isolation. A comprehensive synthesis of available evidence is necessary to quantify this risk and inform clinical decision-making. Furthermore, understanding the differential risk profiles among various ARPIs can guide drug selection, particularly for patients with pre-existing fall risk factors.

This systematic review and meta-analysis aims to quantitatively synthesize evidence from randomized controlled trials to determine the association between ARPI use and fall risk in prostate cancer patients, with particular emphasis on differential effects among specific agents and the clinical significance of these findings.

Methods

Search strategy and selection criteria

We conducted this systematic review and meta-analysis following the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses (PRISMA) guidelines [17] and the Assessing the methodological quality of systematic reviews (AMSTAR) guidelines [18]. A comprehensive literature search was performed across PubMed, Web of Science, Cochrane Library, and ClinicalTrials.gov from inception through Apri 2026 (final search time). The search strategy incorporated relevant keywords and medical subject headings related to "prostate cancer," "androgen receptor pathway inhibitors," "enzalutamide," "apalutamide," "darolutamide," "fall," and "randomized controlled trials." The full study protocol was registered on the Open Science Framework (OSF, 10.17605/OSF.IO/GS8UY, https://osf.io/gs8uy).

Study eligibility was strictly defined according to the PICOS framework: (1) Population (P): Patients diagnosed with advanced prostate cancer (including non-metastatic castration-resistant, metastatic castration-resistant, and metastatic hormone-sensitive prostate cancer). (2) Intervention (I): Novel androgen receptor pathway inhibitors (ARPIs), specifically enzalutamide, apalutamide, or darolutamide (administered alongside ongoing androgen deprivation therapy [ADT]). (3) Comparator (C): Placebo or non-steroidal antiandrogens (NSAA, e.g., bicalutamide) combined with ongoing ADT. (4) Outcomes (O): The primary outcomes are the incidences of all-grade falls and severe (grade ≥ 3) falls. (5) Study Design (S): Phase 2 or 3 RCTs. We excluded non-randomized studies, phase 1 trials, reviews, meta-analyses, and studies that did not actively monitor or report fall-related adverse events as part of their predefined safety protocols (e.g., small, short-duration studies not aiming to evaluate long-term safety). Importantly, if a trial formally evaluated safety profiles but observed no falls, it was considered to yield valid 'zero event' data and remained eligible for inclusion, rather than being excluded for 'lack of data'.

Data extraction and quality assessment

Two investigators independently screened all identified records according to predefined selection criteria. Discrepancies were resolved through consensus or third-party adjudication. Using a standardized data extraction form, we collected information on study characteristics, patient demographics, intervention details, and fall outcomes. Fall-related adverse events were extracted based on the Common Terminology Criteria for Adverse Events (CTCAE) criteria as reported in the individual study protocols. To maintain consistency, all-grade falls were defined as any fall event (Grades 1–5), while severe falls were strictly defined as Grade 3 or higher, signifying events that are medically significant or require hospitalization.

Methodological quality was assessed using the Cochrane Risk of Bias tool (RoB 2) [19], evaluating five domains: randomization process, deviations from intended interventions, missing outcome data, outcome measurement, and selection of reported results. Studies were categorized as having "low risk," "some concerns," or "high risk" of bias for each domain. Importantly, to ensure methodological precision, all risk of bias assessments were specifically tailored to the primary outcome of interest (incidence of falls).

Statistical analysis

We calculated risk ratios (RRs) with 97.5% confidence intervals (CIs) for fall incidence between ARPI and control groups. Primary outcomes were pooled RRs for all-grade falls and grade ≥ 3 falls. While we recognized that Exposure-Adjusted Incidence Rates (EAIR) account for differential treatment durations, crude proportions were utilized as the primary effect measure to ensure maximum data consistency across all severity grades and clinical subgroups, as EAIR data for grade ≥ 3 events were underreported in the majority of primary trials.

Statistical heterogeneity was assessed using the I2 statistic, with values of 25%, 50%, and 75% representing low, moderate, and high heterogeneity, respectively. Between-study variance (tau2) was estimated using the restricted maximum-likelihood (REML) estimator. The standard z-distribution was utilized to calculate confidence intervals to ensure stability across small subgroups. To explore potential sources of clinical heterogeneity, we performed subgroup analyses based on the type of control group, ARPI type and clinical disease stage (mHSPC, nmCRPC, mCRPC, etc.). Specifically, trials were categorized into those comparing ARPIs with placebo plus standard of care (ADT) and those comparing ARPIs with active controls, such as standard NSAA. Differences between subgroups were evaluated using the chi-square test. Publication bias was assessed via Egger’s test for funnel plot asymmetry, with a p value < 0.05 indicating significant bias. All analyses were conducted using R software (version 4.2.1).

Results

Study selection and characteristics

Our systematic literature search initially identified 4,728 records. After the removal of 2,915 duplicates, 1,813 records underwent title and abstract screening. Subsequently, 82 full-text articles were assessed for eligibility. Ultimately, 11 randomized controlled trials [20–30], encompassing a total of 12,239 patients, met our predefined inclusion criteria and were included in the final quantitative synthesis (Fig. 1). Published between 2016 and 2024, the included trials evaluated three novel ARPIs: enzalutamide (six trials: ENZAMET, ARCHES, PROSPER, PREVAIL, STRIVE, and EMBARK), apalutamide (two trials: SPARTAN and TITAN), and darolutamide (three trials: ARAMIS, ARASENS, and ARANOTE).Fig. 1PRISMA flow diagram detailing the literature search and study selection process. It is important to note that while abiraterone acetate was initially considered, it was excluded from the meta-analysis because none of its eligible trials reported extractable data on fall events. More importantly, strictly limiting our synthesis to pure androgen receptor antagonists (enzalutamide, apalutamide, and darolutamide) advantageously eliminated the severe confounding risk of falls associated with the obligatory glucocorticoid co-administration required for abiraterone. Similarly, although the ACIS trial investigated apalutamide, it was excluded because its control arm (abiraterone acetate plus prednisone) violated our strict inclusion criteria of standard ADT or placebo. Utilizing an active ARPI in the control group would fail to isolate the true baseline safety profile of novel AR antagonists, and the mandatory concurrent steroid use in both trial arms would introduce an unresolvable confounding variable regarding fall risk.

Meta-analysis of all-grade falls

The forest plot for all-grade falls is presented in Fig. 2. A pooled analysis of 11 RCTs demonstrated that ARPI treatment significantly increased the risk of all-grade falls compared with the control arm (RR 2.00, 97.5% CI 1.46–2.73; P < 0.0001), albeit with substantial inter-study heterogeneity (I2 = 77.6%). The overall prediction interval (PI) ranged from 0.67 to 5.99.Fig. 2Forest plot evaluating the risk of all-grade falls associated with ARPIs. Forest plot evaluating the risk of all-grade falls associated with ARPIs. ARPI, androgen receptor pathway inhibitor; Enza, enzalutamide; Apa, apalutamide; Dar, darolutamide; NSAA, non-steroidal antiandrogen; ADT, androgen deprivation therapy; RR, risk ratio; CI, confidence interval

Subgroup analyses by treatment comparison revealed distinct risk profiles. When compared with placebo plus ADT, enzalutamide was associated with a more than 2.5-fold increase in fall risk (RR 2.55, 97.5% CI 1.62–4.01; PI 0.47–13.93). Notably, this elevated risk remained significant even when enzalutamide was compared with active standard non-steroidal antiandrogens (NSAAs) in the ENZAMET and STRIVE trials (RR 2.47, 97.5% CI 1.14–5.37). Among other agents, apalutamide showed a trend toward increased risk (RR 1.65, 97.5% CI 0.77–3.52), whereas darolutamide exhibited a more modest, non-significant trend (RR 1.25, 97.5% CI 0.87–1.79; PI 0.46–3.39). The test for subgroup differences was statistically significant (chi2 = 8.83, P = 0.03), indicating that the magnitude of fall risk varies depending on the specific ARPI agent and the control therapy utilized.

Although subgroup analyses by individual ARPIs were conducted to explore drug-specific effects, these findings should be interpreted with caution. An inherent numerical imbalance exists among the subgroups (enzalutamide, n = 6; apalutamide, n = 2; darolutamide, n = 3). Given that enzalutamide trials disproportionately drive the overall pooled weight, these drug-specific estimates should be interpreted as exploratory, as the limited number of trials for apalutamide and darolutamide constrains the statistical power to detect true inter-drug differences.

Meta-analysis of grade ≥ 3 falls

The forest plot for severe (grade ≥ 3) falls is summarized in Fig. 3. Overall, ARPIs were associated with a more than two-fold increase in the risk of severe falls (RR 2.15, 97.5% CI 1.32–3.52). The overall PI was 1.15 to 4.02, suggesting this elevated severe risk is likely to be observed consistently in future clinical settings. In subgroup analyses, the elevated risk of severe falls remained relatively consistent across treatment comparisons. Enzalutamide demonstrated comparable point estimates for risk increase whether compared with placebo plus ADT (RR 2.28, 97.5% CI 1.07–4.87; PI 0.28–18.88) or an active NSAA (RR 2.15, 97.5% CI 0.41–11.43); however, the latter comparison did not reach statistical significance at the stricter 97.5% confidence level, likely due to a limited number of severe events. Apalutamide was associated with an increase in severe falls that bordered on significance (RR 2.48, 97.5% CI 0.99–6.23), whereas darolutamide showed a non-significant trend (RR 1.31, 97.5% CI 0.34–5.00, based on single trial). The test for subgroup differences was not statistically significant (chi2 = 0.84, P = 0.84), suggesting that the increased risk of severe falls represents a relatively uniform class effect of potent AR pathway inhibition.

Similarly, the interpretability of drug-specific risks for grade ≥ 3 falls is severely restricted by sample size imbalances. Notably, while five trials evaluated enzalutamide and two evaluated apalutamide, darolutamide was represented by only a single trial reporting grade ≥ 3 events. This significant disparity necessitates extreme caution when comparing the severe fall-risk profiles across different ARPIs.

Publication bias

Visual inspection of the funnel plots for both all-grade (Supplemental Fig. 1) and grade ≥ 3 (Supplemental Fig. 2) falls revealed generally symmetrical distributions, suggesting a low likelihood of substantial publication bias. Given that the number of included trials for all-grade falls met the methodological threshold (n = 11), Egger's test was formally applied and confirmed the absence of significant publication bias (P = 0.891). For grade ≥ 3 falls (n = 8), where quantitative testing has limited statistical power, the exploratory Egger's test also remained non-significant (P = 0.413), which was highly consistent with the visual symmetry.

Heterogeneity and sensitivity analysis

Given the substantial heterogeneity observed for all-grade falls (I2 = 77.6%), we conducted further explorations to identify potential clinical sources beyond ARPI drug types. Expanded subgroup analyses revealed that clinical disease stage significantly contributed to the heterogeneity (test for subgroup differences: P = 0.0309)(Fig. 4). This indicates that patients across different progression states exhibit varying magnitudes of all-grade fall risk. To ensure the robustness of our findings against this heterogeneity, a systematic leave-one-out sensitivity analysis was performed for both all-grade(Supplemental Fig. 3) and grade ≥ 3 (Supplemental Fig. 4) falls. For all-grade falls, iteratively removing one study at a time demonstrated that no single trial disproportionately drove the results; the pooled Risk Ratios remained highly stable, ranging from 1.89 to 2.13, with all lower bounds of the stringent 97.5% CIs remaining strictly above 1.37. A similar stability was observed for grade ≥ 3 falls (RRs ranging from 1.91 to 2.34). This persistent consistency confirms that the overall elevated risk of falls is a reliable finding, and the residual variance is appropriately managed by the REML random-effects framework.

Discussion

Our meta-analysis provides critical evidence regarding the fall risk associated with ARPI therapy in patients with advanced prostate cancer. Crucially, our subgroup analyses demonstrate that the increased risk of falls is not a uniform class effect. While certain ARPIs significantly elevate the risk of both all-grade and severe falls, notable drug-specific differences exist, which have profound implications for treatment selection and proactive patient management. However, it is important to emphasize that as a study-level meta-analysis, our results provide generalized risk estimates rather than precise individual predictions. Therefore, these findings should not be viewed as absolute contraindications for any specific ARPI, but rather as a clinical 'red flag' that warrants a more comprehensive, patient-centered evaluation. The decision to initiate ARPI therapy must balance the clear survival benefits against the potential for treatment-emergent toxicities like falls.

Contrary to previous assumptions of a class-wide adverse event, we observed considerable variability among individual agents. Enzalutamide was associated with the highest and most consistent risk, demonstrating a more than 2.5-fold increase in all-grade falls compared with both placebo plus ADT and active NSAA plus ADT controls. Apalutamide also showed an elevated risk profile, particularly for severe (grade ≥ 3) falls. In striking contrast, darolutamide demonstrated a highly favorable safety profile, with only a modest, statistically non-significant trend for all-grade falls (RR 1.25) and no significant increase in severe events. The elevated risk of grade ≥ 3 falls observed with enzalutamide and apalutamide is particularly concerning. Severe falls can lead to fractures, head injuries, hospitalization, and an irreversible decline in quality of life [31, 32]. This highlights the necessity for proactive fall prevention strategies, especially in vulnerable or frail populations initiating these specific agents.

While our meta-analysis establishes a clear clinical association between specific ARPIs and increased fall risk, the precise biological mechanisms underlying these events remain speculative. It is hypothesized that the etiology is multifactorial, potentially encompassing central nervous system (CNS) effects (e.g., dizziness, cognitive impairment), musculoskeletal decline (e.g., fatigue, muscle weakness), and cardiovascular events (e.g., orthostatic hypotension) [33]. To delineate this drug-specific toxicity, it is crucial to distinguish between evidence-based pharmacokinetic findings and hypothesized mechanistic pathways.

From an evidence-based standpoint, preclinical studies have definitively shown that darolutamide exhibits significantly lower blood–brain barrier (BBB) penetration and reduced binding affinity for CNS gamma-aminobutyric acid type A (GABAA) receptors compared to enzalutamide and apalutamide [34]. Based on these established molecular characteristics, the prevailing hypothesis postulates that high CNS penetration is the primary driver of ARPI-related falls. Theoretically, darolutamide’s unique pharmacokinetic profile spares patients from centrally mediated neurotoxicities, which aligns with the lower clinical fall risk observed in our statistical analysis. However, it must be emphasized that the direct causal link between these receptor interactions and the clinical incidence of falls remains unproven and warrants dedicated prospective mechanistic studies.

From a clinical perspective, regardless of the exact underlying mechanism, our evidence-based findings advocate for a highly individualized approach to ARPI selection. Pretreatment assessments must include a comprehensive fall-risk evaluation incorporating age, baseline mobility, polypharmacy, and comorbidities [35]. For elderly patients or those with a high baseline frailty index, prioritizing agents with lower CNS penetration, such as darolutamide, may represent a safer therapeutic strategy. Conversely, if enzalutamide or apalutamide is initiated, proactive management—including physical therapy for balance conditioning, home safety evaluations, and routine monitoring for emerging risk factors—is essential. In cases of recurrent or severe falls during therapy, dose modification or switching to an ARPI with a superior CNS safety profile may be warranted [36].

A pivotal consideration in interpreting our findings is the inherent exposure-time bias in oncological RCTs. ARPIs significantly extend the duration of treatment compared to control therapies, meaning patients in the ARPI arms are observed for a much longer period. Consequently, the higher crude incidence of falls in the ARPI group may partly reflect this prolonged "time-at-risk" rather than an acute increase in toxicity. When examining Exposure-Adjusted Incidence Rates (EAIR; e.g., falls per 100 patient-years) reported in trials like ARAMIS and ARASENS, the risk difference often appears more modest than crude proportions suggest. However, since EAIR data for severe (grade ≥ 3) falls and specific sub-comparisons (e.g., vs. NSAAs) remain largely unavailable in primary publications, a comprehensive meta-analysis of adjusted rates was not feasible. Therefore, our reported RRs should be viewed as the cumulative risk over the total treatment course. Future trials should prioritize the standardized reporting of EAIR to allow for more precise safety comparisons.

Other potential limitations of our study must be noted. First, the analysis relies on aggregate, study-level data rather than individual patient data (IPD). Consequently, we are unable to adjust for specific patient-level confounders that heavily influence fall risk, such as baseline frailty, pre-existing comorbidities, polypharmacy, or the exact duration and type of concomitant treatments (e.g., background androgen deprivation therapy or prior docetaxel chemotherapy). Although the randomized design of the included trials theoretically balances these baseline prognostic factors between the treatment and control arms, the lack of IPD severely limits our ability to perform multivariable adjustments or to identify highly vulnerable patient subgroups that might be at a disproportionately higher risk of falls. Second, there is a lack of standardized reporting for fall events across the included trials. Although adverse events were generally graded according to the standardized CTCAE, the specific definitions, ascertainment strategies (e.g., active surveillance protocols versus passive spontaneous reporting), and clinical thresholds for documenting mild falls varied considerably among the studies. This lack of standardization inevitably introduces measurement bias. While severe (grade ≥ 3) falls are more likely to be captured uniformly due to their clinical severity, the incidence of all-grade falls is highly susceptible to recall bias and differential detection rates, likely serving as an underlying source of the observed heterogeneity. Third, a major limitation of this meta-analysis is the marked numerical imbalance in the available studies for each ARPI subgroup. Enzalutamide studies heavily dominate the pooled estimates, whereas apalutamide and darolutamide are represented by fewer trials. This is particularly evident in the severe (grade ≥ 3) fall analysis, where darolutamide is represented by only one trial. Such a distribution results in wider confidence intervals for these agents and limits our ability to determine if specific agents pose a higher relative risk. Future head-to-head trials or larger real-world evidence are required to refine these drug-specific safety profiles. Fourth, "all-grade falls" and "grade ≥ 3 falls" are inherently correlated outcomes. Although a multivariate meta-analysis is the optimal statistical approach for such data, it requires within-study covariance matrices or IPD, which were not available from the primary trials. Therefore, we were limited to performing separate univariate analyses. Fifth, it is important to distinguish between the meta-analysis of primary efficacy endpoints (such as OS or PFS), which typically utilizes Hazard Ratios adjusted for stratification variables, and the meta-analysis of safety outcomes. In this study, we relied on raw proportions of fall events because multivariable-adjusted estimates for specific toxicities are rarely provided in primary RCT reports. While stratified randomization in the original trials helps balance baseline risks, the use of unadjusted raw data in a meta-analysis may not fully account for confounding factors. This discrepancy between adjusted efficacy analysis and unadjusted safety pooling represents an inherent limitation in contemporary systematic reviews of clinical trial safety data.

Conclusion

In conclusion, rather than representing a uniform class effect, the risk of falls associated with ARPI therapy exhibits significant intra-class variability. Our analysis reveals that while enzalutamide and apalutamide are statistically associated with an elevated risk, darolutamide confers a comparatively favorable safety profile within the current landscape of clinical trials. These findings underscore the potential importance of individualized treatment selection. For elderly or frail patients identified at high baseline risk of falls, darolutamide may represent a more appropriate therapeutic consideration, whereas proactive fall-risk mitigation remains advisable when initiating enzalutamide or apalutamide. Given the study-level nature of this analysis and the imbalance in study numbers across subgroups, these results should be interpreted as exploratory and warrant further confirmation through prospective head-to-head trials.
