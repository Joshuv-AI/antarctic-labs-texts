COVID-19 antibody testing study: a nested substudy within Alberta’s Tomorrow Project (ATP) in Alberta, Canada
Authors: Sara Nejatinamini, Carmen Charlton, Shandra Harman, Jamil N Kanji, James D Kellner, Kristyn Lines, Kathleen Murdoch, Wendy Powell, Joseph Roberts, Will Rosner, Grace Shen-Tu, Graham Tipples, Jian-Yi Xu, Jennifer E Vena
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractPurposeThe Alberta’s Tomorrow Project (ATP) prospective cohort study was established in 2000 to investigate the causes of cancer and chronic disease. The cohort consists of almost 55 000 participants aged 35–69 years at the time of recruitment. From 2020 to 2022, ATP conducted a longitudinal substudy, the COVID-19 Antibody Testing (CAT) study, nested in this existing cohort, to understand the spread and impact of the SARS-CoV-2. In this cohort profile, we describe the CAT study design, recruitment and initial findings.ParticipantsIn this prospective cohort substudy, ~4000 participants completed online surveys and provided blood samples at a study centre every 4 months for 1 year, across four cities in Alberta, Canada. The study was launched on a rolling basis beginning in September 2020 and data collection was completed in May 2022. The surveys collected information on health and lifestyle factors, COVID-19 (testing, symptoms, vaccination, public health recommendations) and impacts of the pandemic (including economic, health services, mental health). Blood samples were tested for antinucleocapsid and antispike protein SARS-CoV-2 antibodies.Findings to dateA total of 4102 participants consented and attended a study centre at baseline, and almost 90% of these completed the study. Overall, participants were aged 61±10 years, 60% female, 12% came from rural areas, 45% had at least a bachelor’s degree, 24% reported a household income <$C75 000 and 39% were retired. About 15% of participants tested positive for antibodies induced by a SARS-CoV-2 infection over the course of the study, and about 18% of those who were infected reported long COVID (persistent symptoms for >4 weeks). By the end of the study, 96% of participants had received at least one COVID-19 vaccine dose. Through investigating other outcomes, it was observed that participants under 50 years of age were more likely to be assessed to have mild or moderate-to-severe anxiety and depressive symptoms compared with older participants. In addition, approximately 15% of participants reported a moderate to major impact on their ability to meet financial obligations.Future plansSerology results, together with health, lifestyle and sociodemographic data, and the continued follow-up of these participants as part of the broader ATP cohort study (planned through 2065), will provide opportunities to investigate the long-term sequelae of COVID-19 infection as well as the broader impacts of the pandemic on physical, mental and emotional health. Data are available to researchers on request through the ATP access process.Keywords: COVID-19, chronic disease, mental health, longitudinal studies

Introduction

The first case of COVID-19 in Alberta, Canada was identified in early March 2020 and the provincial government declared a public health emergency on 17 March 2020.1 A variety of provincial and national initiatives to conduct serological testing of the population were implemented in order to estimate the prevalence of SARS-CoV-2 infection, provide evidence as to the duration and dynamics of antibody responses2 and understand risk factors for severe COVID-19 infections (resulting in hospitalisation) as well as the consequences of mild/moderate COVID-19 infections. A major challenge was determining how to mobilise significant numbers of the population to assess these aspects through conducting research studies, in the context of a global pandemic.

Alberta’s Tomorrow Project (ATP), an established large longitudinal population cohort (n=55 000), offered a unique opportunity to study the spread of SARS-CoV-2 in Alberta.3 4 ATP was initiated in 2000 and recruited about 55 000 Alberta residents aged 35–69 years with no history of cancer (except non-melanoma skin cancer) from both urban and rural areas between 2000 and 2015. At recruitment, participants provided informed consent for follow-up data collection, data linkage and research use, and over 30 000 visited a study centre to provide physical measurements and biospecimens. Since recruitment was completed in 2015, ATP has continued to conduct follow-up data collections with participants. ATP is also part of the Canadian Partnership for Tomorrow Project (now Canadian Partnership for Tomorrow’s Health (CanPath)), a research platform and coalition of seven cohorts across Canada focused on strengthening population health science in Canada.5 In June 2020, Alberta’s provincial government commissioned ATP to implement a COVID-19 Antibody Testing (CAT) study to track the trend of SARS-CoV-2 infection in Albertan adults via serology testing and collection of associated health information related to symptoms and impacts of COVID-19. This approach aimed to examine the full spectrum of the pandemic’s effects, including antibody responses as well as broader physical, mental and financial impacts. In Autumn 2020, ATP’s efforts were supported by CanPath with funding from the federal COVID-19 Immunity Task Force (CITF), which aimed to coordinate SARS-CoV-2 immunity research across Canada.6 ATP’s existing infrastructure and engaged cohort made it well-positioned to lead this study in Albertans over age 40 years.

The objective of this paper is to describe the CAT study design, participant recruitment and methods as well as the data and biosamples that are available for request through ATP’s standard access procedures; data are also available for request through CanPath and the CITF. Selected descriptive findings are presented to illustrate the research opportunities available through the broad range of information collected, including demographic factors and socioeconomic-related impacts.

Cohort description

Study design

The CAT study was conducted as a substudy of ATP, and the overall study design was informed by the surveillance needs of Government of Alberta and the CITF, as well as by a study advisory committee of provincial experts in infectious disease, population health and epidemiology. ATP’s recruitment methods and full cohort characteristics have been described previously.3 4

For the CAT study, participants were invited to attend a study centre to have blood drawn for antibody testing at four timepoints over 1 year (baseline, 4 months, 8 months and 12 months) (figure 1). Participants were also invited to complete comprehensive surveys to capture information on COVID-19 testing, symptoms, treatment, public health recommendations and health and lifestyle information (eg, chronic conditions and mental health changes). Figure 1 describes the data and biosample collection and the timeframe of collection in the context of the COVID-19 pandemic in Alberta from 2020 to 2022. Participants were asked to consent to the survey data collection, blood sample collection, linkage to administrative health databases, sharing of their de-identified data with CanPath and CITF and use of their de-identified data for approved research projects.

Data and biosample collection summary and timeline. Overview of the data and biosamples collection in the COVID-19 Antibody Testing study (A) and timeframe of collection in relation to overall Alberta COVID-19 case counts in adults aged >40 years and key public health measures (B). The study collected information from participants via surveys and blood samples in study centres in four locations (Calgary, Red Deer, Edmonton and Lethbridge) at four timepoints (0, 4, 8 and 12 months). The launch of the study centres was staggered in order to initiate data and biosample collection as quickly as possible while balancing the time needed to establish the infrastructure and staffing for each study centre. Over the course of the study, Alberta had five waves of COVID-19 infections and three declared states of public emergency in which public health restrictions were implemented (eg, mandatory masking in public places, limits on social gatherings).

Four study sites (Calgary, Edmonton, Red Deer and Lethbridge) were selected to represent four of the five current health zones in the province, in which ~90% of the province’s population resided (online supplemental figure 1); Government of Alberta, 2023; Statistics Canada, 2023).1 The North zone was not included due to lower ATP participant density and overall large geographic spread in that region. Study site locations were established in existing Alberta Health Services facilities but had to be stood up de novo, including securing the location, procuring supplies and equipment and hiring and training staff. Study site operations were adapted over time in response to evolving public health and safety guidelines, including enhanced cleaning, physical distancing, mask use, symptom screening and revised clinic flow. Staff completed infection control training and followed strict personal protective equipment and disinfection protocols. A designated coordinator monitored updates, and a formal standard operating procedure ensured consistent implementation across all sites. To expedite the initiation of data collection, study centres were launched on a rolling basis as they became ready to open. To inform operations and participant flow, the Calgary study centre was launched in mid-September as a 2-week pilot study to trial blood collection and survey administration (n=336 invitations sent; n=188 participants completed the pre-appointment survey and n=182 visited the study centre) followed by a 2-week period to implement learnings. The Calgary study formally launched in mid-October 2020, followed by the opening of the other sites; data collection was completed in May 2022 (figure 1).

Patient and public involvement

ATP has an established Participant Advisory Committee (PAC), where 30 participants work directly with ATP to provide participant experience and perspectives on ATP’s activities. The PAC was consulted throughout the planning of the CAT study primarily on study logistics and operations, including the overall approach, study design and types of data collections, and anticipated challenges. Despite the very condensed timeframe (study commissioned in June 2020 and first study centre was piloted in mid-September 2020), PAC members were consulted on the burden and feasibility of study participation, including time commitments, and provided input on study centre logistics (eg, geographic location, accessibility, availability of parking, adequate space in waiting rooms to accommodate physical distancing, ensuring clear signage to study areas). They also advised on recruitment strategies and participant-facing materials such as invitation letters and study updates to ensure clarity and inclusivity. The committee also reviewed the CAT study baseline timepoint survey and their feedback was incorporated into the last version. Study updates and findings were shared with the committee throughout the study duration and the PAC members advised on the format and content of future participant communications and dissemination plans.

In addition to the PAC, input from participants was obtained on an ongoing basis through evaluation questions included at the end of each survey administered throughout the study (eg, questions about study centre visit experience and length of the survey). Since the CAT study centres were rolled out in phases, participant feedback was considered when planning for subsequent study centre implementations, and all feedback was documented for future operational considerations. CAT study participants could also contact ATP’s dedicated call centre to voice any concerns or questions. Finally, updates about the study were shared with the entire ATP cohort through ATP’s annual participant newsletters; the content of the newsletters was informed by questions and feedback from the PAC as well as participant feedback collated through the CAT study evaluation surveys.

Participant recruitment

ATP aimed to recruit approximately 1000 participants within 50 km (Calgary and Edmonton) or 100–200 km (Red Deer and Lethbridge) of each of the four major urban centres; these catchment areas allowed for recruitment of urban, semi-rural and rural participants. Recruitment targets aligned with the population demographics (age and sex) of the general Alberta population aged 40–84 years, since this was the relevant age range of ATP participants from whom to draw the study sample (2016 census data were used as it was the most recent dataset available, as the next census dataset became available in 2021).7 Participants aged 40–84 years who had an email address on file and were living in the study centre catchment areas were randomly selected and stratified by age and sex to receive invitations to the CAT study (figure 2). Exclusion criteria included contraindications for blood draw (described in online supplemental methods). A total of 9092 participants were invited to participate in the CAT study and 4638 completed an eligibility screener used to identify participants contraindicated for blood collection. Of these, 4428 participants signed the consent form and 4104 participants were considered fully enrolled by providing a blood sample for serology testing at baseline (n=324 did not attend a study centre; 32 of these participants completed the baseline survey but their survey data were excluded from the analysis because they did not provide a blood sample). However, two participants attended a study centre at baseline but did not complete a survey at any timepoints and were excluded from this analysis (final sample n=4102).

Data collection

Overall data collection and participant flow throughout the study are outlined in figure 1 and described in detail in online supplemental figure 2 (including survey and study centre appointment reminder protocols). Participants were invited to participate in the study via email; if interested, they completed an eligibility screener for blood draws (as described above and in the online supplemental methods) in the online ATP participant portal. Eligible participants provided their consent to participate and booked an appointment at their local study centre. Once they booked an appointment, they were sent a survey notification; the survey was accessible for 2 weeks (1 week prior to the study centre appointment and 1 week after). Only participants who attended a study centre and provided a blood sample at baseline were invited to participate in subsequent timepoints; however, if participants missed a subsequent timepoint (eg, 4 months), they were still invited for the following one (eg, 8 months).

At each timepoint, participants were invited to complete a survey that captured information including demographics, COVID-19 testing, symptoms, vaccination, health behaviours during the pandemic (such as adherence to public health recommendations), impacts on employment and income, access to healthcare services, substance use (smoking, e-cigarettes, cannabis, alcohol), health conditions and mental health. Further information on the surveys is available in the online supplemental methods and online supplemental table 1, as well as on the ATP website. Survey content was informed by: (1) the broad needs of Government of Alberta to inform policy decisions; (2) harmonisation with CanPath (which was conducting a separate but related CAT study) and the CITF; (3) alignment with two paediatric COVID-19 studies also funded by Government of Alberta to support provincial COVID-19 surveillance activities8 9; (4) alignment with previous ATP surveys and (5) other sources that would enable broader data comparison (eg, Statistics Canada). The surveys were updated at key points during the study to capture the most up-to-date information (eg, in January 2021 when COVID-19 vaccines became available; further details provided in the online supplemental methods). The 12-month timepoint also included an optional nutrition and physical activity survey (details provided in the online supplemental methods).

Biosample collection

Blood samples were collected in the study centres by trained phlebotomists. Prior to the blood draw, the phlebotomists collected information from participants including chemotherapy, radiation and blood transfusions in the past 12 months, the time participants woke up the morning of collection and the last time they had something to eat or drink besides water. The participant’s safety during the blood draw was also assessed, which included checking for any conditions that might contradict the blood draw, such as rashes or open sores on both arms.

At each timepoint of study centre appointment, one 5 mL serum separator tube (SST; gold-top; BD Vacutainer catalogue no. 367954) and one 8.5 mL SST (mottled-top; BD Vacutainer catalogue no. 367988) vacutainer of blood was collected from each participant. Tubes were allowed to clot for 30 min and then centrifuged at 1500 g for 10 min. The 5 mL tubes were then shipped on cold packs to the Alberta Precision Laboratories Public Health Laboratory (ProvLAB) for SARS-CoV-2 antibody testing and serum from the 8.5 mL tubes was aliquoted to cryovials and stored in −80°C freezers as back-up for antibody testing and for biobanking for future research use. At the 8-month timepoint, additional biosamples were collected for biobanking, including collection of two 4 mL vacutainers (red-top; BD Vacutainer catalogue no. 368975) for serum and three 10 mL EDTA vacutainers (lavender-top; BD Vacutainer catalogue no. 367525) for plasma, buffy coat and red blood cells. All biosamples collected for biobanking purposes were aliquoted to cryovials and stored locally in −80°C freezers, until shipping on dry ice for long-term storage at −80°C in the freezers at the ATP biobank in Calgary. Saliva collection was implemented at the 12-month appointment to enable future study of the oral microbiome and other research outcomes. Participants were asked to refrain from smoking and eating 1 hour prior to their appointment. Participants rinsed their mouth with 10 mL of alcohol-free mouthwash for 30 s, then spat the rinse into a 50 mL DNase/RNase-free conical tube. The collected biosample was aliquoted into four Nunc DNase/RNase-free cryovials using Ranin sterile DNase/RNase-free pipette tips. Saliva samples were shipped to Calgary on dry ice for storage at −80°C freezers in the ATP biobank for future research purposes.

Serology testing

Blood samples underwent antibody testing by Alberta ProvLab in Calgary and Edmonton. ProvLab provides public health and microbiology laboratory testing as well as supports testing, research, education and surveillance with a focus on infectious disease, and was responsible for provincial COVID-19 testing (serology and PCR-based testing).10 Antibody testing (further described in the online supplemental methods) was conducted for the nucleocapsid protein (to reflect a previous SARS-CoV-2 infection) and the spike protein (to reflect either a previous SARS-CoV-2 infection or receipt of one of the COVID-19 vaccines available at the time). The Abbott Architect SARS-CoV-2 IgG assay (Abbott Laboratories, Illinois, USA) was used to detect antinucleocapsid antibodies (samples with a signal/cut-off ratio (S/C) > 1.4 were considered positive) and the Abbott SARS-CoV-2 IgG II Quant assay for detecting antibodies against the spike receptor-binding domain (RBD) (samples >50 AU/mL considered positive).11 Qualitative results of antibody testing (ie, positive or negative test result) were returned to participants within 4 weeks of the study centre appointment (described further in the online supplemental methods). The nucleocapsid positivity rate was calculated as the proportion (%) of participants who tested positive for nucleocapsid antibodies over the total number of people tested each month. To determine overall infection rates, we considered both serology results (positive nucleocapsid antibody test) and self-reported survey data (ie, self-report of a positive PCR or rapid antigen test—further described in the online supplemental results).

Data comparisons

We compared the characteristics of the CAT study participants with the broader cohort of ATP participants who had completed a previous follow-up survey (Survey 2017) to understand the similarities or differences between the CAT study subcohort and the broader ATP cohort of participants. Survey 2017 was completed by 33 742 participants between 2017 and 2019 and represented the most recent data available for the majority of the ATP cohort, collected before the onset of the COVID-19 pandemic. Vaccine uptake of participants in the CAT study was also compared with data available from the general Alberta population aged 40–59 years, accessed June 2022 from the Government of Alberta (https://www.alberta.ca/stats/covid-19-alberta-statistics.htm#vaccinations). A cross-validation substudy was also conducted on the serology results to assess comparison with results obtained in the CanPath cohorts (described in online supplemental methods and results).

Statistical analysis

Means and SDs were used to describe continuous variables, and frequencies and percentages were used for categorical variables. Comparisons of categorical variables between two groups were conducted using the χ2 test and comparisons of continuous variables between two groups were performed with Student’s t-test. Generalised Estimated Equation models were used to analyse the main effect of time on mental health measure scores. The criterion for statistical significance was set as α≤0.05 (two-tailed). All analyses were performed using SAS Enterprise Guide V.8.3. The Strengthening the Reporting of Observational Studies in Epidemiology reporting guidelines were followed in reporting the results.

Findings to date

Recruitment and retention

As indicated in figure 2, overall, 45% of participants invited to the study consented and attended a study centre appointment and are included here for analysis (n=4102). The intended proportions for age groups were exceeded for the 60–69 years (32% vs 23% target) and 70–79 years (17% vs 11% target) age groups and were lower than target for the 40–49 years (19% vs 31% target), 50–59 years (30% vs 32% target) and 80–84 years (2% vs 4% target). This is reflective of the overall age distribution of the ATP cohort, with proportionally more participants over age 60 years, because participants were aged 35–69 years at the time of recruitment (2000–2015).

Figure 3 shows the retention of participants across the follow-up timepoints. Overall, 88% of participants attended all four timepoints, while approximately 90% completed three of four timepoints. In addition, 62% (n=2543) of participants completed the optional nutrition and physical activity survey at the 12-month timepoint.

Retention of participants throughout the study. Alberta’s Tomorrow Project participants were randomly selected within age/sex band targets in each study centre location for initial invitation to the COVID-19 Antibody Testing study. The proportion who attended a study centre was calculated from the total number who were invited at that timepoint, and the proportion who completed the survey was calculated from the number who attended the study centre. Small differences in the number of participants invited from one timepoint to the next timepoint were due to participant withdrawal, death or changes in contact information (resulting in loss to follow-up) over the course of the study (n=61 for all reasons combined).

A lower proportion of CAT study participants were female compared with the broader ATP cohort (60% vs 65%), due to efforts made to achieve a balanced recruitment of males and females into the CAT study. CAT participants were more likely to have a bachelor’s degree or higher level of education (45%) than Survey 2017 respondents (39%) and to work full-time or part-time (54% compared with 47%), likely due to the targeted recruitment for the CAT study being proportionally higher in participants aged <65 years compared with >65 years. Proportionally fewer CAT study participants had income <$C75 000 compared with the broader ATP cohort (24% vs 33%, respectively). A lower proportion of CAT respondents reported current smoking (3.3%) compared with the Survey 2017 respondents (5.8%). The prevalence of participants with body mass index (BMI) 25.0–29.9 kg/m2 observed in the CAT study (39.2%) was higher than in Survey 2017 respondents (35.7%), while the proportion of participants with BMI >30 kg/m2 was similar. The proportions of participants with a history of heart/circulatory and respiratory conditions were higher among CAT participants compared with the broader ATP cohort, although the proportion of participants with a history of cancer, stroke and mental health conditions was similar.

Antibody results

A total of 616 participants (15%) tested positive for nucleocapsid antibodies at least once over the course of the study. Monthly nucleocapsid positivity rates (figure 4) were relatively low (<5%) until January 2022, coinciding with the arrival of the highly infectious Omicron variant. By the end of the study in May 2022, the nucleocapsid positivity rate had risen to 29%. On each survey, participants were asked to report any positive COVID-19 tests (ie, PCR/rapid antigen tests—data reported in online supplemental results. Throughout the study period, 18% of participants were assessed to have been infected with COVID-19, as determined by a self-reported positive swab test and/or positive test for nucleocapsid antibodies. Out of the 616 participants who tested positive by serology, 44% also reported testing positive via PCR/rapid antigen testing. By contrast, 38% had not reported positive PCR/rapid antigen testing (75% of these had only reported negative PCR/rapid antigen test results and 25% had not reported any PCR/rapid antigen testing), and 18% of PCR/rapid antigen test-positive participants were negative by serology.

Monthly nucleocapsid positivity rate across the COVID-19 Antibody Testing (CAT) study. The data reflect the proportion of participants who tested positive for COVID-19 antibodies at least once over the course of the CAT study. Vaccination and public health recommendations

By the end of the study, 96% of participants reported receiving at least one dose of a COVID-19 vaccine, and 94% reported receiving two or more doses (online supplemental results and online supplemental table 3. Participants in the study had slightly higher vaccination rates compared with the general population of Alberta (online supplemental table 4). Throughout the study, participants were asked to self-report their adherence to key public health recommendations; most participants reported adhering to recommendations, including wearing masks and physical distancing (described in online supplemental result and online supplemental figure 4).

COVID-19 impacts

The surveys administered at each timepoint collected many variables to capture impacts of the COVID-19 pandemic, including physical, mental and emotional health factors as well as impacts on socioeconomic, health services access and others. Select preliminary findings are presented here to illustrate the range of data available for request to explore in more detail.

Long COVID

On the 12-month timepoint survey, participants who self-reported a previous SARS-CoV-2 infection were asked to indicate the total duration of their symptoms, including both mild and fluctuating episodes. Response options included <2 weeks, 2–3 weeks, 4–12 weeks and >12 weeks. Those reporting symptoms lasting 4 weeks or longer were then asked to provide details on the presence and severity of specific symptoms from a predefined list.

Data collected from these questions were used to estimate the prevalence of ‘long COVID’—here, defined as symptoms lasting for >4 weeks. In total, 529 (13%) participants self-reported ever having a SARS-CoV-2 infection and, of those, 12% (approximately one in eight) experienced symptoms for 4–12 weeks and 6% experienced symptoms for >12 weeks. Participant characteristics were compared between those who reported symptoms lasting longer than 4 weeks and those who experienced a SARS-CoV-2 infection but had symptoms that lasted <4 weeks (online supplemental table 5). Compared with participants who experienced a SARS-CoV-2 infection but did not have lasting symptoms, a greater proportion of participants who experienced long COVID had BMI ≥25 kg/m2 (77% vs 64%), reported having a mental health condition (33% vs 23%) and had not been vaccinated (14% vs 5%).

Mental health

Surveys administered at each timepoint included measures for anxiety and depression to capture changes in mental health among participants during the pandemic. Anxiety was captured using the Generalised Anxiety Disorder-7 (GAD-7) screening tool12 and depression was captured using the Patient Health Questionnaire-8 (PHQ‐8) screening tool.13 Overall, 6% and 8% scored positive for moderate to severe anxiety and depression, respectively, at some point over the study (figure 5). Self-reported mental health improved during the summer of 2021 but worsened in December 2021 and early 2022 (p<0.001, figure 5). The proportion of participants assessed to have mild or moderate-to-severe anxiety and depressive symptoms tended to be higher for those under 50 years of age compared with participants aged >50 years throughout the study (p<0.001, figure 5).

GAD-7 contains seven questions and participants selected an option for how often they experienced those feelings (including ‘not at all’—0, ‘several days’—1, ‘more than half the days’—2, ‘Nearly every day’—3) over the last 2 weeks. The scores were summed to provide an overall anxiety assessment score out of 21 and categorised into the following categories of anxiety severity: no/minimal anxiety (score 0–4), mild anxiety (score 5–9) or moderate-to-severe anxiety (score 10–21). Socioeconomic impacts

On the 12-month timepoint survey, participants were asked to describe the impact of COVID-19 on their ability to meet financial obligations or essential needs, such as rent or mortgage payments, utilities and groceries; response options included no impact, minor impact, moderate impact and major impact. The majority of CAT study participants reported minor (23%) or no (62%) impact on the ability to meet financial obligations; however, about 15% of participants reported moderate (12%) or major (3%) impact on their ability to meet financial obligations (online supplemental table 2). Participant characteristics were compared between those who reported no or minor impact on the ability to meet financial obligations and those who experienced moderate to major impact (online supplemental table 6). Compared with participants who experienced no or minor impact on ability to meet financial obligations, a greater proportion of participants who experienced moderate to major impact on ability to meet financial obligations were aged 40–59 years (56% vs 47%), had household income <$C100 000 (52% vs 35%), had children in the household (23% vs 17%), were employed full-time (43% vs 40%) and/or part-time (18% vs 13%), were unable to work because of sickness or disability (3% vs 1%), were unemployed (6% vs 1%) and were essential workers (59% vs 55%). There was a trend towards a higher proportion of participants who experienced long COVID among those who experienced moderate to major impact on ability to meet financial obligations.

Strengths and limitations

As an existing longitudinal cohort, ATP was well positioned to rapidly leverage its infrastructure to collect information about COVID-19 in Alberta. Data were shared throughout the study with the Government of Alberta to support decision-making. Data were also pooled with other COVID-19 data collected across Canada in both CanPath and the CITF to support future research in multiple disciplines; pooled data are available through these custodians. This study collected comprehensive data to capture COVID-19-related testing, symptoms, treatment, vaccination and public health recommendations, as well as economic, physical and mental health impacts of the pandemic. The self-reported data were complemented by laboratory assessment of SARS-CoV-2 antibody testing including the nucleocapsid and spike proteins, enabling more robust assessments of infection and immune response.

Strengths of this study include the geographic diversity of participants across the province, high retention rates and comprehensive and longitudinal survey data collection. Casting a recruitment net both within and beyond four major urban centres in Alberta, Canada, allowed the inclusion of participants from urban, semi-rural and rural areas. This diverse sampling will allow researchers to examine differences in health outcomes by geographic location. Participants were recruited through this longitudinal study from the general population of adults over the age of 40 years, including half of participants who were aged 60 years and older and 19% aged 70 years and older. The data can be used to compare and contrast findings from other studies, which were focused on specific populations (eg, hospitalised patients, healthcare workers). Retention rates were >90% for all timepoints, which resulted in low frequencies of missing data and a comprehensive dataset for longitudinal analyses. The surveys captured a broad range of data including sociodemographic, lifestyle, economic and physical and mental health factors that will allow for the study of multifaceted impacts on health and well-being related to COVID-19 infection as well as other effects of the broader social and related changes that occurred in the pandemic. For example, several studies conducted by other research groups have examined the impacts of COVID-19 and long COVID on mental and physical health, as well as social and economic outcomes14,16—areas that can also be investigated using the CAT study data. In addition to the self-reported data, participants provided consent for linkage with administrative health databases, which will allow for cross-referencing with vaccination information as well as tracking long-term health outcomes and health services use. More broadly than this study, ATP participant survey data are linked to environmental data (eg, neighbourhood air and noise pollution, land use, green spaces, neighbourhood material and social deprivation indices) through the Canadian Urban Environmental Health Research Consortium,17 which enables future analyses of interactions between personal factors and area-level exposures. Finally, efforts were made to harmonise data elements with other large research initiatives. For example, survey questions were harmonised with the other CanPath cohorts who conducted a separate antibody testing study; all harmonised data are available for request through CanPath. Core survey data were also harmonised for the CITF consortium6 and survey and antibody testing data are available through that platform.

Consideration of the limitations of this study is warranted. Consistent with other health research cohorts, previous comparisons with the Canadian Community Health Survey3 4 have shown that the ATP cohort tends to be older, have more women, a greater proportion of individuals with higher education and income and greater health indicators (eg, lower prevalence of smoking). In addition, the willingness to participate in a COVID-19 study, along with the requirement to complete surveys online and attend a study centre in person during the pandemic for collection of blood samples, may further bias the study group compared with the general population. Compared with the broader ATP cohort, CAT participants had different sociodemographic profiles (higher household income, education). However, CAT study participants had similar or greater prevalence of chronic health conditions compared with the broader ATP cohort (23.5% with BMI >30 kg/m2, 40.4% with heart/circulatory conditions, 23.4% with hypertension and 23.8% with respiratory conditions). Individuals with pre-existing heart or respiratory conditions were frequently identified as being at higher risk for severe COVID-19 outcomes, which may have increased their motivation to participate in the CAT study. Still, the CAT study participants have a range of sociodemographic, geographic and lifestyle factors to support exploration of these factors, including a small proportion who remained unvaccinated throughout the study (and only slightly lower than the general Alberta population—6% without two doses of vaccine in the CAT study vs 9% in the general population), and representativeness is not necessarily a barrier to addressing aetiological questions.18 Another limitation, common to survey-based studies, is that data were self-reported and may be subject to recall bias (eg, adherence to public health recommendations, dates of vaccination and COVID-19 infections). Linking to provincial healthcare databases and other health records can help address recall bias for some outcomes, such as vaccinations, which are well captured; however, these databases are less reliable for infection data, as Alberta (like most provinces) reduced or discontinued routine testing and case tracking of COVID-19 infections. Finally, the study did not include participants from the North health zone in Alberta due to logistical challenges including the region’s large geographic area, dispersed population and limited feasibility of establishing study centres. As a result, the findings may not fully capture issues unique to this region, such as differences in healthcare access, remote living conditions and distinct COVID-19 exposure risks. Antibody waning over time may have led to underestimation of prior SARS-CoV-2 infections. Our testing measured antibodies to both the nucleocapsid (N) protein and the spike (S) protein, which are the two most commonly targeted and widely recommended SARS-CoV-2 antigens for serological assessments.19 We recognise that our study used antinucleocapsid and antispike binding antibody assays only, and did not include other antigens (eg, membrane or envelope proteins) and neutralising antibody tests, which could have provided a more complete immune profile; however, these tests are less commonly used in diagnostic assays due to lower immunogenicity or limited validation.20 The Abbott RBD assay used also may underestimate spike seropositivity in individuals with antibodies against other regions of the spike protein.21 While discrepancies between RBD and full spike antibody responses are uncommon,19 the narrower focus of the Abbott RBD test could have resulted in missed or indeterminate cases. Therefore, caution is warranted when interpreting results from the RBD assay alone, as it may not capture the complete immune response. Finally, we did not compare characteristics between excluded and included participants due to the small proportion excluded and lack of detailed data, which may affect the representativeness of our findings.

Future plans

The CAT study collected comprehensive data related to COVID-19 (testing, symptoms, long COVID, serology) and sociodemographic, environmental and lifestyle factors (including mental health and access to healthcare services) as well as biological samples (blood samples at four timepoints over a year and saliva samples at one timepoint), which are available for researchers to request. Linkage to administrative healthcare databases as well as the availability of ATP survey data collected in the years before the pandemic and since that time allows for expanded opportunities for longitudinal analyses. For example, the mental health measures (GAD-7 and PHQ-8) were previously collected in 2020, and diagnosis of anxiety and depression was collected in 2020 and 2017. For more details on the availability of data before and after the COVID-19 pandemic, please visit the ATP website (www.myATP.ca). Finally, harmonisation of study variables with the broader CanPath and CITF cohorts allows for comparisons and data pooling to facilitate comparisons across Canada. The select findings presented here were included to illustrate the potential areas of interest that are possible using the CAT study data, which can be explored more deeply (eg, to identify subgroups most affected or important associated characteristics).

CAT study participants will continue to be followed as part of the broader ATP cohort study, which is planned to continue until 2065. ATP aims to conduct follow-up data collection every few years, capturing health and lifestyle indicators (eg, changes in sociodemographic characteristics, physical and mental health, risk factors such as alcohol intake, smoking, diet and physical activity and preventative behaviours such as cancer screening). Postpandemic data collections (eg, ATP administered a follow-up survey in 2023, which included COVID-19-related questions such as long COVID) will allow for longitudinally tracking within-person changes in physical and mental health to better understand the long-term impacts of the COVID-19 pandemic on health, economic and other outcomes. For example, there are concerns that health practices and modifiable risk factors for chronic disease have changed during the pandemic in ways that will result in an unintended increase in the risk of chronic diseases such as cardiovascular disease, obesity, diabetes and cancer.22 Findings from the Alberta Community Health Survey (2018–2021) showed that these changes may disproportionately affect certain populations, with individuals experiencing high material deprivation being less likely to meet recommended physical activity guidelines.23 In addition, linkage to administrative health databases allows for investigations of how the pandemic-related interruptions in health service may have affected rates of cancer and chronic disease diagnosis and management, which may emerge in the coming years.3 The harmonisation of much of the survey data with CanPath and CITF, combined with administrative health information, enables data pooling opportunities to support future research into the long-term impacts of the pandemic. Through this study, ATP has demonstrated the ability to mobilise for province-wide blood collections in support of seroprevalence surveillance efforts, enabling readiness for future public health threats. Additional biospecimen collections will be pursued in the future, for the CAT study participants as well as for the broader ATP cohort, as funding opportunities permit.
