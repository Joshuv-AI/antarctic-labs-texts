On the Interplay Between Hodge Projections and Bounded Harmonic Functions on Manifolds with Ends
Dangyang He and Adam Sikora

Abstract
. We investigate the L p L^{p} -boundedness of the Hodge projection in the setting of manifolds with ends. We examine its relationship to the Riesz transform and the space of bounded harmonic functions. In particular, we explore how the L p L^{p} -boundedness of the Hodge projection is connected to the structure of L 2 L^{2} harmonic one-forms and, subsequently, to the space of bounded harmonic functions.



1.
Introduction

Our primary objective is to determine the range of exponents
p
for which the Hodge
projection is bounded on
L^{p}
spaces over manifolds with ends. The Hodge projection and
the associated Hodge decomposition form a classical and important topic in harmonic and
geometric analysis. Foundational contributions to this field were made by Hodge and de Rham
(see
[
13
,
24
]
). For historical context and subsequent developments, we refer the
reader to
[
6
,
31
]
. For significant aspects of Hodge theory leading in directions
not directly related to our study, see
[
2
,
33
]
. For additional background on
related issues of Hodge theory, we refer to
[
6
,
28
,
29
]
.

Our focus lies on the analytic aspects of the theory, particularly those connected to studies
of the Riesz transform. In this context, our interest is motivated by the relationship between
the
L^{p}
-boundedness of the Hodge projection and the equivalence (or lack thereof) between
the Riesz and reverse Riesz transforms, see (
2
) and (
3
) below, as investigated by Auscher and Coulhon in
[
3
]
.

Recall that the Laplace–Beltrami operator can be defined via the closure of the quadratic form

\langle\Delta f,g\rangle=\int_{M}\Delta f(x)\,g(x)\,dx=\int_{M}\langle df,dg\rangle_{g},

for all
f,g\in C_{c}^{\infty}(M)
, where
d
denotes the exterior derivative. From this
definition it follows that

(1)

\displaystyle\|df\|_{2}=\|\Delta^{1/2}f\|_{2}.

In
[
34
]
, Strichartz posed the problem of determining the range of
L^{p}(M)
spaces
for which the Riesz transform

R=d\,\Delta^{-1/2}

is bounded. That is, for which
p
the operator norm

\|d\Delta^{-1/2}\|_{p\to p}=C<\infty

is finite, or equivalently for which
p
the inequality

(2)

\displaystyle\|df\|_{p}\leq C\|\Delta^{1/2}f\|_{p}

holds. The identity (
1
) shows immediately that
R
is bounded on
L^{2}(M)
.

Finally, the gradient
\nabla f
of a function is defined by the relation

Xf=\langle\nabla f,X\rangle

for all smooth vector fields
X
.

On a Riemannian manifold
M
, the gradient
\nabla f
can be identified with
df\in T^{*}M
via the canonical identification of
TM
and
T^{*}M
. Then the Riesz transform can be also denoted by
R=\nabla\Delta^{-1/2}
. In the sequel we always identify the tangent space with the 1-forms family.

•

A significant answer to Strichartz’s question was obtained by Coulhon and Duong in
[
9
]
. However, the aspects of the Riesz transform closer to our present study were obtained in works by Coulhon, Carron, Hassell, Nix, and the second-named author (see
[
7
,
19
,
21
]
). The reverse Riesz transform can be defined by the inequality

(3)

\|\Delta^{1/2}f\|_{p}\leq C\|\nabla f\|_{p}.

This inequality and its relation to the Hodge projection constitute the main rationale for the results investigated in
[
3
]
, which also form an important starting point for our study. The aspects of the reverse Riesz transform most closely connected to our work are discussed by the first-named author in
[
22
]
, and we explain these connections in Remark
1.7
below.
To discuss the Hodge projection notion in a more systematic way we consider again the exterior derivative
d
and define
\delta
as its formal adjoint with respect to the
L^{2}
-inner product, so that

\int\langle d\alpha,\beta\rangle_{g}\,dx=\int\langle\alpha,\delta\beta\rangle_{g}\,dx,\quad\forall\alpha\in C_{c}^{\infty}(\Lambda^{l-1}(M)),\quad\beta\in C_{c}^{\infty}(\Lambda^{l}(M)),

where
\langle\cdot,\cdot\rangle_{g}
is the metric-induced inner product on forms.

Let
M
be a smooth, complete, oriented Riemannian manifold (possibly with boundary, under suitable boundary conditions).
On the Hilbert space of square-integrable differential
l
-forms
L^{2}(\Lambda^{l}(M))
, the Hodge decomposition theorem asserts that

(4)

\displaystyle L^{2}(\Lambda^{l}(M))\;=\;\overline{dC_{c}^{\infty}(\Lambda^{l-1}(M))}\;\oplus\;\overline{\delta C_{c}^{\infty}(\Lambda^{l+1}(M))}\;\oplus\;\mathcal{H}_{L^{2}}^{l}(M),

where the closure is taken in the
L^{2}
-topology, and

\mathcal{H}_{L^{2}}^{l}(M)\;=\;\ker d\cap\ker\delta

denotes the space of
L^{2}
-harmonic
l
-forms, see
[
5
,
25
]
. The Hodge–de Rham Laplacian acting on
l
-forms is defined by

\Delta_{l}=d\delta+\delta d,

while on
0
-forms we simply write
\Delta
for the usual Laplace–Beltrami operator.
In terms of a quadratic form

\langle\Delta_{l}\eta,\zeta\rangle=\int_{M}\langle\Delta_{l}\eta,\zeta\rangle_{g}dx=\int_{M}\langle d\eta,d\zeta\rangle_{g}+\int_{M}\langle\delta\eta,\delta\zeta\rangle_{g}

for all
\eta,\zeta\in C_{c}^{\infty}(\Lambda^{l}(M))
.

Equivalently, as shown for instance in
[
5
]
, one has

\displaystyle\mathcal{H}_{L^{2}}^{l}(M)

\displaystyle=\{\omega\in L^{2}(\Lambda^{l}(M)):\Delta_{l}\omega=0\}

\displaystyle=\{\omega\in L^{2}(\Lambda^{l}(M)):d\omega=\delta\omega=0\}.

The
Hodge projections
arise naturally from (
4
):

•

The projection
d\Delta_{l-1}^{-1}\delta
is the orthogonal projection onto the exact component,

d\Delta_{l-1}^{-1}\delta:L^{2}(\Lambda^{l}(M))\to\overline{dC_{c}^{\infty}(\Lambda^{l-1}(M))}.

•

The projection
\delta\Delta_{l+1}^{-1}d
is the orthogonal projection onto the coexact component,

\delta\Delta_{l+1}^{-1}d:L^{2}(\Lambda^{l}(M))\to\overline{\delta C_{c}^{\infty}(\Lambda^{l+1}(M))}.

•

The projection
\mathcal{H}
is the orthogonal projection onto the harmonic component,

\mathcal{H}:L^{2}(\Lambda^{l}(M))\to\mathcal{H}_{L^{2}}^{l}(M).

Thus, for every
\omega\in L^{2}(\Lambda^{l}(M))
,

\displaystyle\omega\;=\;d\Delta_{l-1}^{-1}\delta\omega\;+\;\delta\Delta_{l+1}^{-1}d\omega\;+\;\mathcal{H}\omega.

In other terms, for every
l
-form
\omega\in L^{2}(\Lambda^{l}(M))
, we have the decomposition

\omega=\alpha+\beta+\mathcal{H}\omega,

with
\alpha
exact,
\beta
coexact, and
\mathcal{H}\omega
the harmonic component.

Next, observe that
\Delta_{l}
is non-negative and self-adjoint. By the spectral theorem, there exists a projection-valued measure
E(\cdot)
on
[0,\infty)
such that

\Delta_{l}=\int_{[0,\infty)}\lambda\,dE(\lambda).

The
Hodge projection

\mathcal{H}
is then given by

\mathcal{H}\;=\;E(\{0\}),

that is, the spectral projection of
\Delta_{l}
corresponding to the eigenvalue
0
. Equivalently,

\mathcal{H}=\chi_{\{0\}}(\Delta_{l}),

where
\chi_{\{0\}}
denotes the indicator function of the singleton
\{0\}
.

One central topic related to our investigation is the characterization of the class of

L^{2}
-harmonic 1-forms (see, for example,
[
5
]
and
[
15
]
).
In this manuscript, however, our focus lies more on the consequences and extended applications
of understanding this class of
L^{2}
-harmonic 1-forms rather than on developing a precise
description of it.

The notation
L^{2}
-harmonic 1-forms seems to be very closely related to the space of bounded harmonic function. At least in the setting of manifolds with ends which we investigate here the connection can have format of equivalent description. Pivotal contribution to studies of bounded harmonic functions can be found in
[
26
,
27
]
. For some interesting application of this concept, see
[

]
and
[

For 1-form family we can write
L^{2}
-Hodge decomposition described above in the following form

(5)

\displaystyle L^{2}(\Lambda^{1}(M))=\overline{dC_{c}^{\infty}(M)}^{L^{2}}\oplus\overline{\delta C_{c}^{\infty}(\Lambda^{2}(M))}^{L^{2}}\oplus\mathcal{H}_{L^{2}}^{1}(M),

where

(6)

\displaystyle\mathcal{H}_{L^{2}}^{1}(M)=\left\{\alpha\in L^{2}(\Lambda^{1}(M));d\alpha=\delta\alpha=0\right\}

refers to the space of
L^{2}

M
.

In what follows, we denote the orthogonal projection onto exact part by
\mathcal{P}=d\Delta^{-1}\delta
, the projection onto co-exact part by
\delta\Delta_{2}^{-1}d
, and the projection onto harmonic part by

\displaystyle I=d\Delta^{-1}\delta+\mathcal{H}+\delta\Delta_{2}^{-1}d.

Definition 1.1
.

Let
\mathcal{V}_{1},\ldots,\mathcal{V}_{l}
be complete, connected, non-compact Riemannian manifolds of the same dimension.
A Riemannian manifold
\mathcal{V}
is called a

of
\mathcal{V}_{1},\ldots,\mathcal{V}_{l}
, written

\mathcal{V}\;=\;\mathcal{V}_{1}\#\mathcal{V}_{2}\#\cdots\#\mathcal{V}_{l},

if there exists a compact subset

such that the complement
\mathcal{V}\setminus K
is the disjoint union of connected open subsets
\mathcal{U}_{i}\subset\mathcal{V}
,

, where each
\mathcal{U}_{i}
is isometric to
\mathcal{V}_{i}\setminus K_{i}
for some compact subset

.

The subsets
\mathcal{U}_{i}
are called the

of

.

In our investigation we focus on the following three classes of manifolds with ends.
The first class is

(
\textrm{M}_{1}
)

\displaystyle M\;=\;(\mathbb{R}^{n_{1}}\times M_{1})\#\cdots\#(\mathbb{R}^{n_{l}}\times M_{l}),

where

and each

is a smooth compact Riemannian manifold. We assume

The boundedness of the Riesz transform on manifolds of this type was studied in
[
7
,
21
]
.

The second class is

(
\textrm{M}_{2}
)

\displaystyle M\;=\;(\mathbb{R}^{2}\times M_{1})\#(\mathbb{R}^{n_{2}}\times M_{2}),

where

, and

n_{2}+\dim(M_{2})\;=\;2+\dim(M_{1})\;=\;N.

Here

and

are smooth compact Riemannian manifolds. The boundedness of the Riesz transform in this case was investigated in
[

]

The third class is the connected sum

(

)

\displaystyle M\;=\;\mathbb{R}^{2}\#\mathbb{R}^{2}.

Proofs of the above result, together with those of the subsequent theorems, are presented in the following sections. In the Introduction we confine ourselves to stating the main claims.

Theorem 1.2
.

Let

be a manifold with ends defined by (

1

(7)

\displaystyle\|\mathcal{P}\omega\|_{p}\leq C\|(I-\mathcal{H})\omega\|_{p},\quad\forall\,1<p<n_{*},

In addition, the following endpoint estimate holds

\displaystyle\|\mathcal{P}\omega\|_{n_{*}}\leq C\|(I-\mathcal{H})\omega\|_{(n_{*},1)},

In the above result,

Remark 1.3
.

Note that, by duality and Theorem

However, since

Remark 1.6

Remark 1.8
.

A general criterion due to Shen
[
32
, Theorem 3.1]
(see also
[
3
, Theorem 2.3]
) may be used to establish the boundedness of
\mathcal{P}
on
L^{p}
. This theorem states that if a manifold
M
satisfies the doubling condition (
66
) together with an
L^{q}
–
L^{2}
reverse Hölder inequality, then
\mathcal{P}
is bounded on
L^{p}
for all
2<p<q
. In particular, by
[
11
, Example 4]
, the reverse Hölder inequality holds on
\mathbb{R}^{n}\#\mathbb{R}^{n}
(
n\geq 3
) for every
1<q<n
. Consequently, Shen’s result implies the boundedness of
\mathcal{P}
on
L^{p}
whenever
n^{\prime}<p<n
, where
n^{\prime}=n/(n-1)
. This range agrees precisely with that obtained via the Riesz transform method (see Remark
1.7
(2)). By contrast, for the non-doubling manifolds considered in this article, the applicability of Shen’s criterion remains unresolved.

In this article, we show that the only obstruction to the
L^{p}
-boundedness of
\mathcal{P}
for
1<p\leq n_{*}^{\prime}
arises from the harmonic component of the
1
-form.

The terms responsible for the failure of boundedness are closely related to those encountered in the case of the Riesz transform, namely the regime
\lvert y\rvert\gg\lvert x\rvert
in the kernel of
\Delta^{-1/2}(x,y)
. Recall the asymptotic expansion from
[
7
]
: let
\overline{M}
denote a compactification of
M=\mathbb{R}^{n}\#\mathbb{R}^{n}
(
n\geq 3
), obtained by attaching two spheres at the ends. Then the Riesz potential satisfies

(11)

\displaystyle\Delta^{-1/2}(x,y)\sim\sum_{j=n-1}^{\infty}a_{j}(x)\,|y|^{-j},

as
y\to\partial\overline{M}
, with
x\in\overline{M}
. The leading coefficient
a_{n-1}(x)
is a nonconstant bounded harmonic function on
\overline{M}
. By the maximum principle,
da_{n-1}
does not vanish identically, so the leading term in the
y
-variable decays only as
\lvert y\rvert^{1-n}
. Such decay pairs boundedly only with functions in
L^{\,n-\epsilon}
(
\epsilon>0
). This explains why the Riesz transform on
M
is bounded on
L^{p}
precisely for
1<p<n
.

Next, we observe that

\mathcal{P}\;=\;d\Delta^{-1/2}\,(d\Delta^{-1/2})^{*}\;=\;RR^{*},

where
R
denotes the Riesz transform on
\overline{M}
. By
[
7
]
, it follows that
\mathcal{P}
is bounded on
L^{p}
whenever
n^{\prime}<p<n
.

For
f,g\in C_{c}^{\infty}(\Lambda^{1}(\overline{M}))\subset L^{2}(\Lambda^{1}(\overline{M}))
, consider the bilinear form

\langle\mathcal{P}f,\,g\rangle=\langle(I-\mathcal{H})f,\,\mathcal{P}g\rangle=\iint d\Delta^{-1/2}(x,y)\,R^{*}g(y)\,dy\;\;(I-\mathcal{H})f(x)\,dx.

We focus on the contribution from the region
y\in\partial\overline{M}
and
x\in\overline{M}
. Using the asymptotic expansion (
11
), the leading term yields

\int\Bigg(\int da_{n-1}(x)\,(I-\mathcal{H})f(x)\,dx\Bigg)\,|y|^{1-n}\,R^{*}g(y)\,dy.

Suppose now that

(12)

\displaystyle(I-\mathcal{H})f\;=\;d\alpha\;+\;\delta\beta,

with
\alpha\in C_{c}^{\infty}(\overline{M})
and
\beta\in C_{c}^{\infty}(\Lambda^{2}(\overline{M}))
.
Disregarding boundary terms, an integration by parts shows that the inner contribution vanishes, since

\delta(da_{n-1})=\Delta a_{n-1}=0\quad\text{and}\quad d(da_{n-1})=0.

Thus, when dualised against a
1
-form without a harmonic component, the problematic leading term is annihilated. By a standard duality argument, this suggests that the lower threshold for the
L^{p}
-boundedness of
\mathcal{P}
(namely
p\leq n^{\prime}
) can in fact be relaxed.

Indeed, our main results establish that in the present setting

\displaystyle\big|\langle\mathcal{P}f,\,g\rangle\big|\;\leq\;C\,\|(I-\mathcal{H})f\|_{p}\,\|g\|_{p^{\prime}},\qquad\forall\,1<p<n.

The upper threshold (
p=n
) persists, reflecting the boundedness of
R^{*}
, i.e.

\|R^{*}\|_{p\to p}\leq C\quad\text{if and only if}\quad n^{\prime}<p<\infty.

Whether this upper bound is sharp, however, remains an open question.

This perspective may also be understood in terms of an interior harmonic annihilation mechanism.
Indeed, owing to the symmetry of the kernel of the Riesz potential, the obstruction to the boundedness of
R^{*}
arises in the regime
|x|\gg|y|
, where

(13)

\displaystyle\Delta^{-1/2}(x,y)\;\sim\;\sum_{j=n-1}^{\infty}|x|^{-j}\,a_{j}(y).

By (
13
) and the decomposition (
12
), the leading term of
R^{*}(I-\mathcal{H})f(x)
in the range
|x|\gg|y|
expands as

\displaystyle|x|^{1-n}\int a_{n-1}(y)\,\delta(I-\mathcal{H})f(y)\,dy=|x|^{1-n}\int a_{n-1}(y)\,\Delta\alpha(y)\,dy=0,

which eliminates the lower threshold for the
L^{p}
-boundedness of
R^{*}
, namely
p=n^{\prime}
.

However, since
\mathcal{P}=RR^{*}
and
R
appears on the left, the upper threshold in the estimate

\displaystyle\|\mathcal{P}f\|_{p}\;\leq\;C\,\|(I-\mathcal{H})f\|_{p}

remains at
p=n
.

We emphasise that we do not assert the optimality of this upper bound
p<n
.

d_{z^{\prime}}(\Delta_{i}+k^{2})^{-1}(0,z^{\prime})
still converges as
k\to 0
. Second,

is an approximate solution in the sense:

\displaystyle(\Delta+k^{2})u_{i}+\Delta\phi_{i}=O\left(\textrm{ilg}(k)^{q}|z|^{-\infty}\right),\quad q\geq 2.

Therefore,

\displaystyle\int\left[d\phi_{i}(z)+du_{i}(z,k)\right]\cdot(I-\mathcal{H})\nu(z)dz=\int\left[-k^{2}u_{i}(z,k)+O\left(\textrm{ilg}(k)^{q}|z|^{-\infty}\right)\right]\xi(z)dz.

Now, (
33

|d_{z^{\prime}}(\Delta_{1}+k^{2})^{-1}(0,z^{\prime})|
converges. Thus, the integrand of

is pointwise bounded by

\displaystyle C\|\omega\|_{1}\left(k^{2}|u_{i}(z,k)|+\textrm{ilg}(k)^{q}|z|^{-\infty}\right)|\xi(z)|\to 0

as

is uniformly bounded. Moreover, for

\displaystyle|\mathcal{A}|\leq C_{\omega}\int(1+|z|^{-\infty})|\xi(z)|dz<\infty,

completing the proof.

Remark 3.2
.

In this remark, we give a supplementary density argument for the proof of Lemma

\displaystyle L^{2}(\Lambda^{1}(M))=\overline{dC_{c}^{\infty}(M)}^{L^{2}}\oplus\overline{\delta C_{c}^{\infty}(\Lambda^{2}(M))}^{L^{2}}\oplus\mathcal{H}_{L^{2}}^{1}(M).

2

Remark 3.5
.

We mention that Lemma
3.1
in a sense explains why
\mathcal{P}
can be bounded on
L^{p}
for
1<p<\infty

\mathbb{R}^{2}\#\mathbb{R}^{2}
. Indeed, if we consider
\mathbb{R}^{n}\#\mathbb{R}^{n}
for

, the situation would be different. Note that for
n\geq 3
, the space of
L^{2}
-harmonic 1-form is no longer trivial, i.e. for

, we should assume (the density argument, Remark
3.2
, is still applicable)

\displaystyle\nu=d\xi+\delta\eta+\gamma,

where
\gamma\in L^{2}(\Lambda^{1}(M))

, and this
\gamma
term does not annihilate

when

. In fact, Theorem
1.4
guarantees that
\gamma=dh
, where

is the bounded harmonic function on
M
. Moreover, by Lemma
2.1
,
h(z)\to 1
as

tends to the infinity of one end, say
E_{1}
(and tend to 0 at the infinity of another end) and

\displaystyle\int d[\phi_{i}(z)+u_{i}(z,k)]\cdot\gamma(z)dz

\displaystyle=\int[\phi_{i}(z)+u_{i}(z,k)]\Delta h(z)dz-\lim_{R\to\infty}\int_{\partial B(o,R)}[\phi_{i}(z)+u_{i}(z,k)]\frac{\partial}{\partial\nu}h(z)d\sigma

\displaystyle=\int\Delta[\phi_{i}(z)+u_{i}(z,k)]h(z)dz-\lim_{R\to\infty}\int_{\partial B(o,R)}\frac{\partial}{\partial\nu}[\phi_{i}(z)+u_{i}(z,k)]h(z)d\sigma.

There is no doubt that the main terms above both tend to 0 as
k\to 0
. However, the boundary terms do not vanish (for

\displaystyle\int_{\partial B(o,R)\cap E_{i}}\left|\phi_{i}(z)+u_{i}(z,k)\right|\left|\frac{\partial}{\partial\nu}h(z)\right|d\sigma\sim\int_{\partial B(o,R)\cap E_{i}}|z|^{1-n}d\sigma\sim R^{1-n}R^{n-1}=1,

\displaystyle\int_{\partial B(o,R)\cap E_{1}}\left|\frac{\partial}{\partial\nu}[\phi_{i}(z)+u_{i}(z,k)]\right||h(z)|d\sigma\sim\int_{\partial B(o,R)\cap E_{1}}|z|^{1-n}d\sigma\sim 1.

Therefore, we need to directly analyze the

-boundedness of the following operator:

(37)

\displaystyle\omega\mapsto\left[d\phi_{i}(z)+du_{i}(z,k)\right]\int d_{z^{\prime}}(\Delta_{i}+k^{2})^{-1}(0,z^{\prime})\phi_{i}(z^{\prime})\cdot\omega(z^{\prime})dz^{\prime}.

Note that for all

\displaystyle\left|\int d_{z^{\prime}}(\Delta_{i}+k^{2})^{-1}(0,z^{\prime})\phi_{i}(z^{\prime})\cdot\omega(z^{\prime})dz^{\prime}\right|\leq C\|\omega\|_{p}

only if

\displaystyle\int_{1}^{\infty}\left|d_{z^{\prime}}(\Delta_{i}+k^{2})^{-1}(0,z^{\prime})\right|^{p^{\prime}}r^{n-1}dr\leq C\int_{1}^{\infty}r^{(1-n)p^{\prime}+n-1}dr<\infty,

i.e.

. Meanwhile,

\displaystyle\|d\phi_{i}(z)+du_{i}(z,k)\|_{p}^{p}\leq C\int_{1}^{\infty}\left(|z|^{-\infty}+|z|^{1-n}\right)^{p}r^{n-1}dr\leq C\int_{1}^{\infty}r^{(1-n)p+n-1}dr<\infty

. This implies that the rank one operator defined by (

for

uniformly in

, and this range is precisely the range of boundedness of
\mathcal{P}
acting on
\mathbb{R}^{n}\#\mathbb{R}^{n}
for
n\geq 3
; see
[
22
, Corollary 3.6]
.

5.
L^{2}
Harmonic 1-Forms

This section is devoted to the proof of Theorem
1.4
.

Proof.

Let
\eta
be a harmonic
1
-form on a manifold
M
of the form

M\;=\;(\mathbb{R}^{n_{1}}\times M_{1})\#\cdots\#(\mathbb{R}^{n_{l}}\times M_{l}).

Define
h\colon M\to\mathbb{R}
by

h(x)\;=\;\int_{\Gamma}\eta,

where
\Gamma
is any smooth path from a fixed base point
x_{0}\in M
to
x
. Since
\eta
is closed (
d\eta=0
), the value of the integral is independent of the path
\Gamma
provided
M
is simply connected (i.e.
\pi_{1}(M)=0
).

As
\eta
is harmonic, it is smooth and satisfies
\eta\in L^{2}(M)\cap L^{\infty}(M)
. Hence,

|h(x)|\;\leq\;C\,d(x,x_{0}),

for some positive constant
C
, where
d(x,x_{0})
denotes the geodesic distance in
M
.

By construction,
dh=\eta
. The space of all such functions
h
with
dh=\eta\in L^{2}(M)
is linear of dimension
l
, generated by functions
h_{i}
,
i=1,\dots,l
, where each
h_{i}
tends to
1
at the
i
-th end and to
0
at all others. The corresponding space of
1
-forms spanned by
dh_{i}
has dimension
l-1
, since
d1=0
and
\sum_{i=1}^{l}h_{i}=1
.

Moreover, on each end
E_{j}
one has the pointwise estimate (see
[
21
, Lemma 2.7]
)

|dh_{i}(x)|\;\sim\;C\,|x|^{1-n_{j}},

which implies that
\eta=dh\in L^{p}(M)
if and only if
p>n_{j}^{\prime}
, where
n_{j}^{\prime}
is the critical Sobolev exponent associated with the
j
-th end.

Consequently, the harmonic projection operator satisfies

\|\mathcal{H}\|_{L^{p}\to L^{p}}<\infty\quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad n_{*}^{\prime}<p<n_{*},

where

n_{*}:=\min_{j}n_{j}

and
n_{*}^{\prime}:=\max_{j}n_{j}^{\prime}
.

This completes the proof of Theorem
1.4
for manifolds of type (

M
1

).
The argument for manifolds of type (

M
2

) is analogous, while the case (

M
3

) is established in
[
8
, Proposition 3.3]
.
∎

6.
Proof of the main results

We now complete the proofs of Theorems
1.2
and
1.5
.

Proof of Theorem
1.2
.

Let
M
be defined by (

M
1

).
Take
\omega,\nu\in C_{c}^{\infty}(\Lambda^{1}(M))
with
\omega\in L^{2}\cap L^{p}(\Lambda^{1}(M))
and
\nu\in L^{2}\cap L^{p^{\prime}}(\Lambda^{1}(M))
, where
1<p<n_{*}
.
Note that

\mathcal{P}=\mathcal{P}(I-\mathcal{H})=(I-\mathcal{H})\mathcal{P}.

By duality, it suffices to analyze the bilinear form

\langle\mathcal{P}\omega,\nu\rangle\;=\;\langle(I-\mathcal{H})\omega,\mathcal{P}\nu\rangle.

Recall that
\mathcal{P}_{k}=d(\Delta+k^{2})^{-1}\delta=\sum_{j=1}^{4}dG_{j}(k)\delta
for all
0<k\leq k_{0}
. For such

we write

\displaystyle|\langle(I-\mathcal{H})\omega,\mathcal{P}\nu\rangle|

\displaystyle\leq|\langle(I-\mathcal{H})\omega,\mathcal{P}_{k}\nu\rangle|+|\langle(I-\mathcal{H})\omega,(\mathcal{P}-\mathcal{P}_{k})\nu\rangle|

\displaystyle\leq|\langle(I-\mathcal{H})\omega,\mathcal{P}_{k}\nu\rangle|+\|(I-\mathcal{H})\omega\|_{2}\,\|(\mathcal{P}-\mathcal{P}_{k})\nu\|_{2}.

We claim that
\mathcal{P}_{k}\to\mathcal{P}
strongly in
L^{2}
, i.e.

\|(\mathcal{P}-\mathcal{P}_{k})\nu\|_{2}\to 0\quad\text{as }k\to 0,\qquad\forall\nu\in L^{2}(\Lambda^{1}(M)).

Indeed,

\mathcal{P}-\mathcal{P}_{k}=k^{2}d\Delta^{-1}(\Delta+k^{2})^{-1}\delta=k^{2}(\Delta_{1}+k^{2})^{-1}\mathcal{P},

where
\Delta_{1}
denotes the Hodge Laplacian on
1
-forms. Let
E_{\Delta_{1}}(\lambda)
be the spectral resolution of
\Delta_{1}
. Then

(54)

\displaystyle\|(\mathcal{P}-\mathcal{P}_{k})\nu\|_{2}^{2}=\int_{0}^{\infty}\left(\frac{k^{2}}{\lambda+k^{2}}\right)^{2}d\langle E_{\Delta_{1}}(\lambda)\mathcal{P}\nu,\mathcal{P}\nu\rangle.

For each
\lambda>0
, the integrand converges to
0
as
k\to 0
and is uniformly bounded by
1
. Moreover, since
\mathcal{P}\nu
lies in the orthogonal complement of
\mathcal{H}^{1}_{L^{2}}(M)
, we have
E_{\Delta_{1}}(\{0\})\mathcal{P}\nu=0
. Thus the integrand of (
54
) converges to
0
almost everywhere with respect to the spectral measure. By the dominated convergence theorem,

\|(\mathcal{P}-\mathcal{P}_{k})\nu\|_{2}\;\to\;0\quad\text{as }k\to 0,

since

\int_{0}^{\infty}d\langle E_{\Delta_{1}}(\lambda)\mathcal{P}\nu,\mathcal{P}\nu\rangle=\|\mathcal{P}\nu\|_{2}^{2}\leq\|\nu\|_{2}^{2}<\infty.

It remains to examine the limits

(55)

\displaystyle\lim_{k\to 0}\langle(I-\mathcal{H})\omega,\mathcal{P}_{k}\nu\rangle

\displaystyle=\lim_{k\to 0}\langle(I-\mathcal{H})\omega,d(G_{1}(k)+G_{3}(k))\delta\nu\rangle

(56)

\displaystyle\quad+\lim_{k\to 0}\langle(I-\mathcal{H})\omega,dG_{2}(k)\delta\nu\rangle

(57)

\displaystyle\quad+\lim_{k\to 0}\langle(I-\mathcal{H})\omega,dG_{4}(k)\delta\nu\rangle,

provided each limit is well defined.

We begin with (
55
). By Section
2.2
, this decomposes as

\displaystyle\langle(I-\mathcal{H})\omega,\mathcal{T}\nu\rangle+\langle(I-\mathcal{H})\omega,\mathcal{S}\nu\rangle

\displaystyle=\sum_{j=2,4}\langle(I-\mathcal{H})\omega,\mathcal{T}_{j}\nu\rangle

\displaystyle\quad+\sum_{j=1}^{2}\langle(I-\mathcal{H})\omega,\mathcal{R}_{j}\nu\rangle+\sum_{j=1}^{2}\langle(I-\mathcal{H})\omega,\mathcal{Q}_{j}\nu\rangle.

By Lemma
3.1
, the contributions of
\mathcal{R}_{2}
and
\mathcal{Q}_{2}
vanish as
k\to 0
. By Lemmata
3.6
,
3.7
,
3.8
, and
3.9
, the remaining terms are bounded by

C\,\|(I-\mathcal{H})\omega\|_{p}\|\nu\|_{p^{\prime}},\qquad 1<p<n_{*},

uniformly in
0<k\leq k_{0}
.

For (
56
), Lemma
4.1
yields the same bound uniformly in
k
for all
1<p<\infty
. The argument for (
57
) is analogous, by Lemma
4.2
.

In summary,

\lim_{k\to 0}\big|\langle(I-\mathcal{H})\omega,\mathcal{P}_{k}\nu\rangle\big|\;\leq\;C\,\|(I-\mathcal{H})\omega\|_{p}\|\nu\|_{p^{\prime}},\qquad 1<p<n_{*}.

The endpoint estimate follows by interpolation and the weak-type bounds in Lemmata
3.7
and
4.2
. This completes the proof.
∎

7.1.
An alternative method: interior harmonic annihilation

In this subsection, we call back a claim we made at the end of the introduction. Namely, we claim that Theorem
1.2
can be alternatively proved by a so-called interior harmonic annihilation method. Given the symmetry of the resolvent kernel, i.e.,
(\Delta+k^{2})^{-1}(z,z^{\prime})=(\Delta+k^{2})^{-1}(z^{\prime},z)
, we obtain by parametrix construction that

\displaystyle(\Delta+k^{2})^{-1}(z,z^{\prime})=\sum_{j=1}^{4}\tilde{G}_{j}(k)(z,z^{\prime}),

where
\tilde{G}_{j}(k)=G_{j}(k)
for
j=1,2
and

\displaystyle\tilde{G}_{3}(k)(z,z^{\prime})=\sum_{i=1}^{l}(\Delta_{i}+k^{2})^{-1}(z,z_{i}^{0})u_{i}(z^{\prime},k)\phi_{i}(z).

Moreover, the error term
\mathcal{E}(k)
defined by
(\Delta+k^{2})\sum_{j=1}^{3}\tilde{G}_{j}(k)=I+\mathcal{E}(k)
equals

7.2.
Remark on a previous result

In this subsection, we explain in detail why

Lemma 7.3
.

[

Let

Consider operator

\displaystyle\|\Delta^{-1/2}\delta\omega\|_{p}=\|\Delta^{1/2}\Delta^{-1}\delta\omega\|_{p}\leq C\|d\Delta^{-1}\delta\omega\|_{p}\leq C\|\omega\|_{p}.

∎

The above argument is clear except the step where we apply reverse Riesz inequality. Indeed, let us consider

. Apparently,

[

]

To better explain why reverse Riesz inequality should not hold in the above example, we need to recall some arguments from
[
22
]
. Let us recall the asymptotic formula (

\displaystyle\Delta^{-1/2}(x,y)\sim\sum_{j=n-1}^{\infty}a_{j}(x)\,|y|^{-j},\quad x\in\overline{M},\quad y\to\partial\overline{M},

\displaystyle\left\langle\nabla f,\nabla a_{n-1}(\cdot)\int|y|^{1-n}g(y)dy\right\rangle=\left\langle f,\Delta a_{n-1}(\cdot)\int|y|^{1-n}g(y)dy\right\rangle=0.

This implies that in the bilinear form (

Therefore, by construction,

for all

\displaystyle u_{1}(z,k)

\displaystyle=-\textrm{ilg}(k)\sum_{i}\beta_{i}\chi_{i}(r)K_{0}(kr)+\sum_{i}\chi_{i}(r)\Phi_{i}(z,k)

\displaystyle+\left(1-\chi_{1}(r)-\chi_{2}(r)\right)\left(\theta\Phi_{1}(z,k)+(1-\theta)\Phi_{2}(z,k)\right),

where
\theta=\frac{\beta_{2}}{\beta_{2}-\beta_{1}}

By a straightforward computation, one yields

\displaystyle(\Delta+k^{2})u_{1}(z,k)=-\textrm{ilg}(k)\sum_{i}\left[\beta_{i}\Delta\chi_{i}K_{0}(kr)-2\beta_{i}\nabla\chi_{i}\cdot\nabla(K_{0}(kr))\right]

\displaystyle+\sum_{i}\Delta\chi_{i}\Phi_{i}(z,k)+\sum_{i}\chi_{i}(\Delta+k^{2})\Phi_{i}(z,k)-2\sum_{i}\nabla\chi_{i}\cdot\nabla\Phi_{i}(z,k)

\displaystyle+(1-\chi_{1}-\chi_{2})\left[\theta(\Delta+k^{2})\Phi_{1}(z,k)+(1-\theta)(\Delta+k^{2})\Phi_{2}(z,k)\right]

\displaystyle+\sum_{i}\Delta\chi_{i}\left[-\theta\Phi_{1}(z,k)-(1-\theta)\Phi_{2}(z,k)\right]+\sum_{i}\nabla\chi_{i}\cdot\left[2\theta\nabla\Phi_{1}(z,k)+2(1-\theta)\nabla\Phi_{2}(z,k)\right].

We first consider the
\Delta\chi_{i}

\displaystyle\left[-\textrm{ilg}(k)\beta_{1}K_{0}(kr)+(1-\theta)(\Phi_{1}-\Phi_{2})\right]\Delta\chi_{1}+\left[-\textrm{ilg}(k)\beta_{2}K_{0}(kr)+\theta(\Phi_{2}-\Phi_{1})\right]\Delta\chi_{2}.

Note that

\displaystyle\big[\textrm{ilg}(k)\beta_{1}

\displaystyle\left(\log(k)+\log{r}+O(1)\right)+(1-\theta)(\beta_{1}-\beta_{2})\big]\Delta\chi_{1}

\displaystyle+\left[\textrm{ilg}(k)\beta_{2}\left(\log(k)+\log{r}+O(1)\right)+\theta(\beta_{2}-\beta_{1})\right]\Delta\chi_{2}

\displaystyle=\left[-\beta_{1}+O(\textrm{ilg}(k))\log{r}+\beta_{1}\right]\Delta\chi_{1}+\left[-\beta_{2}+O(\textrm{ilg}(k))\log{r}+\beta_{2}\right]\Delta\chi_{2}

\displaystyle=O(\textrm{ilg}(k))\log{r}\sum_{i}\Delta\chi_{i}

\displaystyle=O(\textrm{ilg}(k)r^{-\infty})\chi_{K},

where we use the fact

Next, we treat the

\displaystyle 2\big[\beta_{1}\textrm{ilg}(k)\nabla(K_{0}(kr))+

\displaystyle+2\left[\beta_{2}\textrm{ilg}(k)\nabla(K_{0}(kr))+\theta\left(\nabla\Phi_{1}(z,k)-\nabla\Phi_{2}(z,k)\right)\right]\cdot\nabla\chi_{2}

\displaystyle+(1-\chi_{1}-\chi_{2})\left[\theta\left((\Delta+k^{2})\Phi_{1}-v\right)+(1-\theta)\left((\Delta+k^{2})\Phi_{2}-v\right)\right]

\displaystyle=\textrm{ilg}(k)v_{1}+O(kr^{-\infty})\chi_{E_{1}\cup E_{2}}+O(k^{2}r^{-\infty})\chi_{K}

\displaystyle=\textrm{ilg}(k)v_{1}+O(kr^{-\infty}),
