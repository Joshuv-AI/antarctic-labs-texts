Material-Selective Deposition of Reactive Group-Functionalized Poly‑l‑lysine for DNA Sensing at Optical Waveguides
Authors: Samer Aphrham, Mark Verheijden, Jurriaan Huskens
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Precise localization of biomarkers within the sensing area of biosensors is crucial for enhancing performance and minimizing analyte loss to nonsensing regions. Commercial material-selective coatings enable site-specific biofunctionalization but are typically constrained by limited reactive chemistries, reducing their versatility and controllability. We present a selective coating strategy based on a poly-l-lysine (PLL) scaffold that retains the material-selective properties of a commercial carboxylic acid (COOH)-based coating for a Si3N4 waveguide with a poly­(ethylene glycol) (PEG)-covered SiO2 surrounding while enabling modular surface functionalization. The PLL scaffold was functionalized with controlled grafting densities of oligo­(ethylene glycol) (OEG) and cycloalkynes, facilitating the immobilization of azide-labeled DNA probes (pDNA) and enabling quantification of their hybridization. Using the hybridization of a dye-functionalized target DNA, we showed the selective deposition of the PLL onto the COOH-covered sensing areas. To understand the deposition behavior of PLL coatings, we varied the molar ratios of dibenzocyclooctyne (DBCO) and bicyclononyne (BCN) grafts. A higher DBCO content produced thicker layers, attributed to its hydrophobic-induced intramolecular interactions. Substitution with the less hydrophobic BCN yielded uniform coatings across all grafting densities, underscoring the role of hydrophobicity in scaffold deposition. pDNA immobilization on PLL coatings using a localized dispensing technique allowed for optimization of pDNA coupling conditions. This versatile and tunable approach offers enhanced control over surface biofunctionalization, paving the way for biosensors with improved spatial precision and probe density control.

Introduction

Biosensing technologies play a pivotal role in advancing molecular detection, enabling the precise analysis of biological markers with ever-increasing sensitivity and specificity. The demand for ultrahigh sensitivity in biosensors arises from the critical need to detect analytes at extremely low concentrations. , The sensitivity is vital for applications, including early cancer diagnosis, environmental monitoring, and diverse biomolecular research endeavors. However, achieving this level of sensitivity is challenging. A key limitation is the loss of target molecules due to nonspecific adsorption onto areas outside the active sensing region. , Such unintended adsorption not only reduces the effective analyte available for detection but also increases the background noise, thereby compromising measurement accuracy.

To address this challenge, the design of sensor surfaces must incorporate strategies that ensure selective binding of the biorecognition elements to the active sensing regions while minimizing nonspecific interactions elsewhere. This goal is typically achieved by coating nonsensing areas with antifouling layers, a crucial step in maximizing the sensitivity and specificity of the sensor. Among the most promising strategies are material-selective deposition techniques. , These methods exploit the chemical preference of a coating for specific materials, enabling the precise placement of functional layers on sensor regions of interest. Other areas remain uncoated or are passivated to inhibit binding. This is particularly relevant for optical biosensors, where the heterogeneous substrates and surface layouts present unique challenges in achieving optimal functionalization.

Material-selective coatings have been extensively studied to enhance the biosensor performance. Techniques such as atomic layer deposition (ALD) and molecular layer deposition (MLD) provide exceptional precision in depositing thin films and are compatible with diverse substrates, including copper/silicon dioxide (Cu/SiO2), making them highly attractive for biosensor applications. , Similarly, self-assembled monolayers (SAMs) have demonstrated their utility in surface functionalization by offering selective immobilization of reactive groups alongside antifouling properties. , Another notable approach involves the use of dimethyl sulfoxide (DMSO) and phosphate-buffered saline (PBS) cosolvent to create a material-selective “smart” polydopamine coating. , This method inhibits hydrogen-bond formation between catechol and SiO2, providing flexibility in the structural design and patterned surfaces containing SiO2 substrates. In the case of photonic biosensors, such as the one used in this study, commercially available coatings have been developed to achieve contrasting selective reactivity on silicon nitride (Si3N4) and SiO2 substrates. In such systems, carboxylic acid (COOH) reactive groups are confined to waveguide surfaces, while the remaining nonsensing areas are blocked with a poly­(ethylene glycol) (PEG) antifouling layer. Despite these advancements, significant challenges persist in developing material-selective coatings that are quantifiable, easy to implement, and adjustable to user needs. In particular, combining material-selective coatings with a reproducible and probe density-controlled functionalization remains a challenge.

In this study, we demonstrate an effective method for obtaining a DNA biorecognition layer on a commercial asymmetric Mach–Zehnder interferometric (aMZI) sensor, precoated with COOH and PEG groups on the sensing and nonsensing areas, respectively, while maintaining its material-selective properties. We achieve this by the electrostatic adsorption of poly-l-lysine (PLL) grafted with strained alkyne derivatives, which enables the binding of complementary azide-functionalized probe DNA. This enables controllable probe coverage and quantifiable surface modifications tailored to specific experimental needs. The subsequent sections detail the experimental setup and functionalization through PLL, validation of the selectivity by fluorescence and optical waveguide (aMZI) signal response, quantification of the surface functionalization, and optimization of the probe coupling yield by the use of local dispensing.

Results and Discussion

Instrumentation and Experimental Setup

A photonic waveguide-based asymmetric Mach–Zehnder interferometric (aMZI) biosensor was developed here, of which instrumentation and experimental setup were described previously. Briefly, the aMZI waveguide-based biochips utilized in this study contain six individual aMZI sensing elements on each chip (Figure S1). The chip surface is protected with a layer of SiO2 cladding, with specific sections of the cladding etched away to expose the Si3N4 waveguides, facilitating interaction with the surrounding environment. Each sensor operates independently and is monitored separately, enabling simultaneous parallel measurements by all six sensors. In order to achieve selective receptor binding to the Si3N4 sensors but not to the surrounding SiO2, the sensor surface can be commercially treated with a material-selective coating (Figure A), resulting in a thin organic layer with terminal carboxylic acid groups on the Si3N4 waveguide and a PEG-based antifouling layer on the remaining SiO2 surface area (the nonsensing area). Under physiological conditions, the carboxylic acid is mostly deprotonated, making the waveguide surface negatively charged.

Illustrations of the chip surface, (A) showing the material-selective coating where the SiN waveguide surface is coated with COOH groups and the nonsensing SiO2 surface is passivated with a PEG layer; (B) surface functionalization steps using grafted PLL-OEG-DBCO/BCN click groups, followed by injection of azide-terminated ssDNA probe and the hybridization with its complementary strand.

In order to functionalize the waveguide surface, we utilized the PLL coating methodology described earlier as an intermediate layer for further surface functionalization. , PLL is known to adsorb strongly by electrostatic interactions to form a thin layer and allows careful control over the surface probe density by tuning and measuring the grafting density in solution. Normally, PLL adsorbs to a large range of materials, including SiO2 and Si3N4, all of which have a negative surface charge. Yet, here we aim to achieve material-selective deposition of PLL onto the negatively charged organic, carboxy-functionalized silicon nitride waveguide areas while preventing adsorption onto the PEG-modified silicon oxide areas, thus retaining the material-selective properties of the preceding commercial coating.

Accordingly, PLL derivatives grafted with oligo­(ethylene glycol) (OEG) and OEG conjugated with an alkyne were prepared and characterized. The PLL grafting fractions of OEG and OEG-cycloalkyne are given as PLL-OEGx%-cycloalkyney%, where the x% is the total functionalized (OEG) lysine side chains including the fraction of OEG-cycloalkyne (y %) determined by 1H NMR spectroscopy (Figure S3). While the reaction rate of cycloalkynes (0.07–0.96 M–1s–1) is moderate compared to other cycloaddition systems, such as the exceptionally fast trans-cyclooctene (TCO)-tetrazine reaction (≥2000 M–1s–1), reaction kinetics alone do not determine the effectiveness of a bioorthogonal reaction, as stability under physiological conditions is also a critical factor. For example, bispyridyltetrazine exhibits poor stability, with estimated half-lives of 24 h in PBS, 7 h in serum, and only 3 h in blood. This instability significantly reduces the applicability of the TCO-tetrazine chemistry in contexts where long-term stability and prolonged storage are required. Therefore, in this study, we focus on two readily available strained alkyne derivatives known for their stability to be grafted on PLL, namely, dibenzocyclooctyne (DBCO) and bicyclo[6.1.0]­non-4-yne (BCN) (Figure B).

After preparation of the cycloalkyne-grafted PLL, the functionalization of the biosensor surface was followed in real time. This was achieved by continuously injecting buffer solution (running buffer) over the chip to precondition the sensor surface followed by sample injection containing the synthesized PLL. Thereafter, the azide-modified, single-strand probe DNA (pDNA) was immobilized, and finally a fluorescently labeled (with AT488), complementary, single-strand target DNA (tDNA) was introduced and allowed to hybridize to the pDNA (Figure B). Notably, to mitigate electrostatic repulsion from the surface, a high-ionic-strength saline-sodium citrate buffer (4x SSC) was employed during tDNA hybridization. Each injection was terminated by reintroducing the running buffer. The real-time signal shift obtained during the binding process is used to estimate the surface coverage (Γ) using a previously reported method. Upon completion of all steps, fluorescence intensity measurements were taken from both the modified and the unmodified surfaces.

Spatio-Selective Adsorption and Surface Quantification of PLL-OEG-DBCO/BCN

Initially, both premodified (i.e., with carboxylic acid moieties on Si3N4 and PEG on SiO2) and unmodified (with both bare Si3N4 and SiO2) chip surfaces were functionalized with either PLL-OEG30%-DBCO5% or PLL-OEG28%-BCN5%. At this stage, the multivalent electrostatic interactions facilitate the binding, establishing stable attachments between the positively charged lysine groups of the PLL and the negatively charged surfaces (COO– or SiO–). Hereafter, to establish a biorecognition DNA layer, 1 μM azide-modified probe DNA (pDNA) was introduced to the PLL-coated substrates, by which the PLL-appended cycloalkyne moiety undergoes strain-promoted alkyne–azide cycloaddition (SPAAC) with the azide group of the pDNA, leading to the formation of a stable DNA-based biorecognition layer.

Subsequently, the spatial selectivity of the deposition and functionalization process for the waveguide (Si3N4) vs. the surrounding (SiO2) surface areas was assessed by fluorescence imaging. Hereto, we employed a fluorescently labeled target DNA, tDNA, which is hybridized with the pDNA. Figure A presents a bright-field image of the aMZI sensor, with the Si3N4 waveguide appearing as a dark spiral surrounded by the SiO2 nonsensing area. Corresponding fluorescence images were obtained for unmodified as well as premodified chips, both after PLL deposition, pDNA coupling, and tDNA hybridization. On the unmodified surface, the green fluorescence from the tDNA appears uniformly across the whole sensor area covering both waveguide and surroundings (Figure B), indicating the nonselective adsorption of PLL on both the Si3N4 and SiO2 surface areas. In contrast, the COOH/PEG-modified chip exhibits prominent localized fluorescence on the Si3N4 waveguide only, with minimal fluorescence observed on the surrounding SiO2 (nonsensing) area (Figure C). This indicates that in the latter case, the PLL adsorption occurs selectively onto the COOH coating, while its adsorption onto the SiO2 areas is effectively blocked by the PEG layer present there. This qualitative comparison successfully demonstrates the feasibility of achieving a highly spatially selective and straightforward intermediate surface functionalization with the versatile PLL modified with reactive groups for obtaining a biorecognition layer on a waveguide structure.

(B) Fluorescence microscopy image of an unmodified (i.e., w/o material-selective coating) and (C) premodified biosensor surface (with COOH on the sensing area and PEG on the surrounding SiO2 area).

Next, the aMZI signal responses generated during each modification step were captured in real time (Figure ). During the binding of PLL-OEG-DBCO, both the premodified and unmodified surfaces exhibited significant signal response increases, which leveled off after approximately 5–10 min (Figure A), indicating surface saturation. A notable difference in saturation levels was observed, where the PLL-coated, COOH-modified surface (COOH–Si3N4) reached a saturation level (approximately 13,000 pm) that is about 50% higher than that of the PLL-coated bare silicon nitride surface (bare Si3N4; around 9000 pm). This difference is likely due to a higher charge density on the COOH coating compared with the bare Si3N4, allowing a higher amount of PLL to be adsorbed on the former. Notably, the signal responses for both pDNA and tDNA were similar on both surfaces, suggesting that despite the increased adsorption of PLL-OEG-DBCO on the modified surface, the highly negatively charged DNA restricts the achievable DNA surface coverage; in other words, upon saturation, the repulsion does not allow for further click groups on the surface to react with pDNA, as has been reported previously.

Real-time aMZI monitoring of injected samples on the pretreated Si3N4 surface with material-selective coating (COOH–SiN) and untreated surface (bare SiN) of (A) deposition of PLL-OEG-DBCO or PLL-OEG-BCN and pDNA followed by hybridization of the tDNA; (B) quantification of surface density on both COOH–SiN and bare SiN chips.

Surface densities were calculated from the real-time signal response data, as depicted in Figure B, using a methodology described before. The estimated densities of DBCO moieties on the Si3N4 and COOH surfaces are approximately 3.3 × 1013 molecules cm–2 and 4.5 × 1013 molecules cm–2, respectively. In contrast, only about 26% of the DBCO is occupied by pDNA (8 × 1012 molecules cm–2), and less than half of that (about 40%) is fully hybridized with tDNA (3 × 1012 molecules cm–2). For the modified surface, the pDNA occupation is around 17% and again about 40% of this is fully hybridized with tDNA. Both the lower pDNA density and the incomplete hybridization can be explained by electrostatic repulsion at these high probe and target densities. In fact, a densely packed layer of linear DNA on an ideal flat surface theoretically has a surface density of approximately 1.8 × 1013 molecules cm–2, assuming a hexagonal close packing (HCP) arrangement of the DNA with an approximately ∼1.25 nm2 effective radius, including a dynamic hydration shell surrounding the DNA strand. Since our DBCO density is higher, DNA dense packing is reached at incomplete DBCO conversion, and hybridization at the resulting pDNA layer is expected to be incomplete due to electrostatic repulsion. ,,

Comparison Between DBCO and BCN Click Groups

Interestingly, during the injection of PLL-OEG-BCN onto an unmodified sensor (Figure A), we observed a significantly lower saturation plateau compared with the deposition of PLL-OEG-DBCO (Figure S2). Also, the initial signal response observed during the reaction of pDNA to the PLL-OEG-BCN surface tends to be less steep, compared to that on PLL-OEG-DBCO, suggesting a slower kinetics of the SPAAC reaction for BCN compared to DBCO, aligning with previously reported findings. ,, The discrepancy in adsorbed amounts of PLL, however, can be attributed to differences in the lipophilicity of DBCO (calculated log P: clog P = 3.5) and BCN (clog P = 1.2), − leading to differences in the conformation of the cycloalkyne-appended PLL. The difference is also reflected in the surface density of the immobilized probes, with the DBCO-based scaffold achieving approximately twice the binding of the BCN-based scaffold (Figure B).

To study the effect of side group lipophilicity on the surface functionalization, we varied the DBCO and BCN grafting densities on the PLL while maintaining the total OEG grafting density constant during synthesis. After synthesis and quantification by H NMR, we obtained PLL-OEG x%-Y y% (x + y = 27 ± 4%), with grafting densities of Y (=DBCO, BCN) of 1% < y > 7% and 2% < y > 9% for DBCO and BCN, respectively. Hereafter, real-time monitoring was employed to track the binding of PLL-OEG x -DBCO y and PLL-OEG x -BCN y . Strikingly, PLL-OEG-DBCO with a grafting density of ≥5% produced a significantly higher signal response (∼8500 pm) in comparison with lower DBCO grafting densities (≤5%), which yielded on average ∼4000 pm (Figure , red dots). We hypothesize that for PLL with a high density of DBCO, the polymer chain undergoes a conformational change due to intramolecular interactions, possibly driven by DBCO π–π stacking and/or hydrophobic interactions, causing the chain to partially collapse into globular structures (Figure B). In this scenario, these PLL chains are more likely to adhere to the surface without unfolding, forming a thicker layer (with a higher average mass) on the surface. This increase in layer thickness also contributes to the higher signal response upon probe binding.

The BCN group, on the other hand, is known to be more hydrophilic than DBCO and does not contain an aromatic system that can lead to π–π stacking. Therefore, upon introduction of BCN onto the sensor surface, the real-time response (Figure A, blue dots) demonstrated similar binding shifts (∼4000 pm) for all PLL derivatives, even at higher BCN grafting densities (PLL-OEG x -BCN9%), suggesting uniform PLL-OEG-BCN coverages. As a further control, PLL was grafted with either a hydrophobic and aromatic FITC dye (PLL-FITC1%) or a hydrophilic OEG moiety (PLL-OEG20%) without incorporating functional click groups. The real-time measurements revealed that the hydrophobic FITC dye (Figure A, green square) induced a significantly higher signal response compared to the hydrophilic OEG linker (Figure A, black square), again suggesting an intramolecular stacking role of the hydrophobic FITC moieties leading to enhanced adsorption due to a globular PLL conformation. To further confirm this hypothesis, PLL-OEG x was grafted to include both FITC and BCN groups. As anticipated, the addition of a hydrophobic fluorescent group to the grafted PLL (PLL-OEG x -FITC1%-BCN9%) resulted in a higher signal response once again (Figure A, blue triangle). This provides further evidence that hydrophobic intramolecular interactions play a key role in influencing packing configurations and PLL surface coverage. These findings highlight the importance of structural and conformational changes in governing the behavior of the grafted PLL chains. It can be concluded that high DBCO grafting levels result in aggregation of the click groups, which may affect their reactivity and accessibility on the surface. Accordingly, we decided to focus on improving the usability of BCN as a functional group to attach probes, since the PLL layers are more reproducible in surface density and with exposure of the reactive groups to the probe solution across all grafting densities explored here.

Optimization of Probe Coupling to PLL-OEG-BCN

Despite the potential of PLL-OEG-BCN as an intermediate scaffold for biorecognition layers, it exhibits a lower reactivity with pDNA compared to that of DBCO (Figure A). This reduced reactivity presents a challenge in achieving surface saturation with pDNA, which is a crucial step in ensuring homogeneous and reproducible biofunctionalization. In order to overcome this limitation, we employed a droplet dispenser. The use of such a spotting technique provides several advantages, including the ability to functionalize different sensors selectively and to adjust conditions (e.g., pDNA concentration and incubation time) to accelerate functionalization. Prior to surface spotting, aMZI chips with a material-selective coating were functionalized with PLL-OEG x -BCN9%, as followed in real time (Figure S2). The obtained aMZI signal gave a higher saturation response relative to the unmodified surface. The trend mirrored previous observations for PLL-OEG-DBCO, where premodified surfaces exhibited higher saturation responses with PLL compared to unmodified ones (see above), likely due to increased surface charge, which may contribute to improved attachment of the PLL. Following PLL functionalization, azide-terminated DNA was immobilized onto individual aMZI sensor surfaces.We employed the deposition of droplets of more concentrated (10 μM) pDNA solutions onto the designated regions of the aMZI sensor surface, allowing for faster, controlled, and spatially selective functionalization. Two types of DNA were employed to assess biofunctionalization efficiency: the normal probe DNA (pDNA) for specific recognition of the complementary target DNA (tDNA) and a negative control nonprobe DNA (npDNA) to serve as a reference. To optimize the reaction conditions and gain insight into the functionalization kinetics, we systematically varied the incubation time across different sensors. The reaction duration was varied from 0.5 to 4 h, allowing us to assess the time-dependent nature of the probe functionalization process of the BCN click group.

Following immobilization, a concentration series of tDNA (ranging from 100 pM to 100 nM) was flowed over the chip, and the hybridization process to the sensors was monitored in real time (Figure A). A detectable hybridization signal was observed at concentrations as low as 100 pM. At this concentration, the tDNA surface density remained constant (1.7 × 1010 #·cm–2) across all aMZI sensors, indicating that hybridization was independent of incubation time (Figure B). However, at tDNA concentrations of ≥1 nM, the incubation time significantly influenced the hybridization efficiency on individual aMZI sensors, as reflected in the linear increase in surface density (Figure B, black dots).

Notably, no binding was detected on the sensor functionalized with npDNA, even at the highest concentrations of tDNA. This finding was corroborated by fluorescence intensity measurements, which revealed localized fluorescence primarily at the Si3N4 waveguides immobilized with pDNA. In contrast, no fluorescence was detected at the SiO2 (nonsensing areas) or on the (negative control) Si3N4 waveguide immobilized with npDNA (Figure C). These results confirm that preincubation with npDNA effectively prevented nonspecific DNA binding to the PLL-modified aMZI surface.

The optimization of pDNA coupling conditions during spotting on aMZI functionalized with PLL-OEG-BCN improved selective functionalization and enhanced hybridization efficiency relative to continuous injection on unmodified chips (Figure D). To further assess the selectivity of PLL-OEG-BCN independently of the material-selective coating, we performed a similar experiment on an unmodified chip surface. Here, PLL-OEG-BCN was uniformly deposited across the entire chip, whereas pDNA was applied locally to the aMZI sensor area. The hybridization of tDNA exhibited a higher surface density compared to continuous injection (Figure D). Additionally, the hybridization of tDNA was restricted to the designated (aMZI) area with no observable adsorption on the surrounding pristine PLL-OEG-BCN scaffold (as shown in Figure E). These results further underscore the role of PLL-OEG-BCN as a nonfouling scaffold and the improved probe coupling achieved during spotting.

PLL-OEG-BCN showed strong stability and performance across different functionalization levels (Figure A), making it a promising candidate for biosensor coatings. However, the linear trend shown in Figure B indicates incomplete pDNA immobilization even after 4 h. To optimize the ex situ incubation conditions, we conducted a series of experiments by varying both the concentration and incubation time of pDNA on the PLL-OEG-BCN scaffold. The real-time responses during the injection of 100 nM tDNA were then recorded and quantified, assuming a 1:1 probe-to-target hybridization ratio. As shown in Figure A, duplex DNA formation initially increased with pDNA concentration but decreased again beyond 30 μM. This is most likely due to electrostatic repulsion between the negatively charged free and surface-bound pDNA strands. This repulsion effect was more pronounced on bare Si3N4 surfaces, where even at 100 μM pDNA no saturation was observed (Figure B). A similar trend was observed when the incubation time was varied to a fixed pDNA concentration of 10 μM (Figure D). In contrast, the COOH-modified Si3N4 surface exhibited a significantly higher duplex DNA density, reaching approximately 4 × 1012 #·cm–2 after 4 h of incubation (Figure C). This is in agreement with previously reported maxima for highly densely packed pDNA surfaces. ,, Under these conditions, the assumption of 1:1 hybridization no longer holds, as the pDNA density may be significantly higher, but just a fraction of it is hybridized. Based on these findings, we conclude that a pDNA concentration of 10–30 μM and an incubation period of approximately 4 h are optimal for ex situ probe immobilization on PLL-OEG-BCN-coated surfaces. Although further increases in probe density may be achievable, they do not necessarily improve tDNA hybridization, particularly at lower tDNA concentrations (Figure A,B). At higher tDNA levels, electrostatic repulsion between DNA strands continues to limit hybridization efficiency. Overall, this systematic approach helped evaluate the efficiency of the BCN-azide reaction and guided the identification of optimal surface functionalization conditions.

Conclusions

In the present work, we achieved the surface-selective modification of COOH-treated Si3N4 waveguides with reactive click groups, leaving the PEGylated (nonsensing) areas unaffected. This was accomplished using poly-l-lysine grafted with a controllable density of cyclooctyne reactive groups as an intermediate layer. This strategy facilitated the attachment of azide-terminated DNA probes and resulted in a highly selective biorecognition layer. The modification steps were monitored in real time, and the signal response was used to quantify the surface density. The fluorescence intensity from the hybridized fluorescently labeled DNA target was acquired to assess biorecognition selectivity and homogeneity. Furthermore, the influence of hydrophobic-grafted moieties on the PLL chains was investigated. The results showed that BCN allows a reproducible thin layer to be formed on the sensor surface over a wide range of grafting densities. As an alternative, we adopted and optimized a nanoliter dispensing approach to enhance the azide reaction on BCN-grafted PLL.

While the DBCO click group remains a popular scaffold for surface functionalization, PLL-OEG-BCN offers a promising alternative. It has the potential to broaden DNA biorecognition immobilization and, in future studies, to extend to other classes of biomolecules. Similar to DBCO, its compatibility with peptides, antibodies, and aptamers enables the detection of a wide array of analytes, particularly in complex biological samples. Nonetheless, BCN is inherently less reactive than DBCO, which poses challenges for applications that require rapid probe conjugation. This limitation is especially critical when only small quantities of valuable probe materials are available. Studies have shown that modifying the azide’s electronic or hydrophobic properties can enhance BCN reactivity, with phenyl-azides in some cases outperforming DBCO. , In addition to the strain-promoted alkyne–azide cycloaddition (SPAAC) reaction, BCN can undergo highly reactive inverse-electron-demand Diels–Alder (IEDDA) cycloadditions with tetrazines or cyclopentadienones, thereby significantly expanding the range of compatible substrates and enhancing its utility across various bioorthogonal applications. , Further improvements could be achieved by tuning the grafting density of antifouling groups and exploring alternative bioorthogonal chemistries such as TCO–tetrazine ligation, biotin–avidin binding, or thiol–maleimide coupling. These strategies may improve the reaction kinetics, stability, and versatility. Integrating these approaches with amplification or plasmonic enhancement techniques may significantly boost the sensitivity and dynamic range, advancing the platform toward more robust diagnostic and biosensing applications.

The morphological differences observed upon the adsorption of PLL-OEG-DBCO at varying grafting densities present an interesting observation, likely driven by hydrophobic interactions and π–π stacking. These molecular interactions may induce a (modest) increase in packing density, consequently increasing both the surface roughness and overall surface density. Gaining insight into these morphological transformations could play a crucial role in refining nanocoating methodologies for a wide range of surface applications.

Materials and Methods

Materials

A phosphate buffer (PB) was prepared by mixing 4.82 g of dibasic sodium phosphate and 1.52 g of monobasic sodium phosphate to achieve a final phosphate concentration of 1.5 M at pH 7.4. Unless otherwise specified, all buffers and chemicals were obtained from Sigma-Aldrich. Poly-l-lysine hydrobromide (MW 15–30 kDa, polydispersity index <1.1) and MS­(PEG)4 (methyl-PEG-NHS-ester) were dissolved in phosphate-buffered saline (PBS; 137 mM NaCl, 2.7 mM KCl, 10 mM Na2HPO4, and 1.8 mM KH2PO4, pH 7.4) to make a stock solution of 10 mg/mL and in dimethyl sulfoxide (DMSO) to make a stock solution of 0.25 mM, respectively. Similarly, the DBCO-PEG4-NHS ester and endo-BCN-PEG4-NHS ester were purchased from Clickchemistrytools and BroadPharm, respectively, and both were dissolved also in DMSO to make a stock solution of 0.25 M. The aMZI chips were purchased from Lionix B.V. and treated with a material-selective coating by Surfix Diagnostics B.V. This treatment results in a thin layer with exposed carboxylic acid groups (COOH) on the Si3N4 waveguides and a PEG antifouling layer on the surrounding SiO2 surface (the nonsensing area). Lyophilized pDNA {5′-CTTGTTCTTTCTCTTCCTCGCCTGCACACACACACAC/Azide/-3′}, its Atto488-labeled complementary strand {5′-GCAGGCGAGGAAGAGAAAGAACAAGAAAAAGAGAAGGATGCAGAAAACAA/3ATTO488N/-3′}, and npDNA (5′-ACTGTGAACCTGTGTCTGCCACTGTCTCTCTCTCTCTTT/Azide/-3′) were ordered from IDTdna and dissolved in TE buffer (TE = 10 mM TRIS +0.1 mM EDTA) to a concentration of 1 mM or 100 μM and then stored in −20 °C. Upon hybridization of pDNA to its complementary tDNA strand, 25 base pairs form a stable duplex. Dispensing of diluted pDNA or npDNA in 1.5 M PB on the aMZI surface was done using the Scienion S3 by dispensing in total 28 nL of sample solution in humidity-controlled chamber at 60%. After incubation, the chip was subsequentially washed with 1x PBS, MQ, and 10x SSC, each for 15 min on the shaker. Hereafter, the chip was stored in a vial filled with 10 mM EDTA in PBS until further use.

PLL-OEG­(x)-Cycloalkyne­(y) Synthesis

All PLL-OEG-cycloalkyne polymers with varying degrees of cycloalkyne functionalization were synthesized following a modified version of the protocol reported by Duan et al. Poly­(l-lysine) hydrobromide (PLL·HBr) was dissolved in 1x PBS buffer (pH 7.4) to a concentration of 10 mg/mL. The desired stoichiometric ratios (relative to lysine monomer units) of methyl-OEG4-NHS ester and either DBCO-OEG4-NHS or BCN-OEG4-NHS ester were simultaneously added to the solution under vigorous stirring and allowed to react for 8 h at room temperature.

For reactions requiring a higher grafting density of BCN or DBCO (>4%), the PLL solution was first diluted in DMSO to a concentration of 5 mg/mL. The DBCO-OEG4-NHS or BCN-OEG4-NHS esters were then added dropwise at 5 min intervals; similarly, the addition of methyl-OEG4-NHS was followed, with continuous mixing throughout the reaction at 37 °C. Following the reaction, the crude mixture was purified using Spectra/por dialysis membrane (6–8 kDa MWCO, Thermo Fisher Scientific) against decreasing diluted PBS in Milli-Q water, until a full 24 h cycle in Milli-Q water. The filtered product was immediately freeze-dried overnight. Final compounds were stored at −20 °C, either as lyophilized powder or as 1 mg/mL stock solutions in D2O.

Methods

aMZI Data Acquisition and Fluidic Setup

During the real-time measurements, the input light (λ = 850 nm) propagates through the waveguide and generates an evanescent field. The light is then divided into two equal propagating spiral-shaped waveguides (Figure S1A) denoted as the “reference arm” and the “sensing arm”. The propagating beam at the reference arm is protected by a layer of SiO2; in contrast, in the sensor arm, the SiO2 layer is etched away to expose the Si3N4 waveguide as depicted (Figure S1B), thus allowing the evanescent field to interact with the liquid sample environment. When a target molecule binds to the sensor surface, it induces a change in the refractive index (RI) at the sensor arm relative to the reference arm. Such changes are recorded as a shift in phase difference of the light (Δλ) between sensing and reference arms.

During sample injection, a fluidic chamber made of Teflon is clamped on top of the biosensor chip (Figure S1C), and buffer solution (running buffer) is continuously introduced onto the chip surface and allowed to stabilize. In the case of tDNA hybridization, a 4x SSC buffer was selected as a running buffer and preconditioned for 1 h at 37 °C before introducing the tDNA sample. The experimental setup incorporates an automated switchable valve system to streamline the loading of 100 μL plug samples of tDNA into the fluidic system. Following loading, the plug was introduced into the fluidic chamber via a syringe pump filled with the designated running buffer (Figure S1D). This method ensures consistent, seamless, and stable transitions from the sample to running buffer with each injection, thereby enhancing reproducibility. The flow rate for all running buffer and sample injections was set at 20 μL/min.

Deposition of PLL onto Modified and Unmodified Chips

For the immobilization of grafted PLL, both material-selectively modified and piranha-treated (unmodified) aMZI surfaces were employed. In the case of the modified chips, the sensing areas can be selectively modified with biorecognition components by targeting the carboxylic acid groups of the primer layer (Figure S1B). In contrast, the unmodified aMZI chips were first submerged in a 3:1 mixture of sulfuric acid (H2SO4) and hydrogen peroxide (H2O2) (piranha solution) for 2 min, and then thoroughly washed and stored in Milli-Q water for no longer than 8 h, as the surface quality degrades over time. The piranha solution decomposes most organic residues on the surface, leaving a hydrophilic layer of hydroxyl groups (−OH).

For the injections of PLL-OEG-DBCO, PLL-OEG-BCN, or pDNA on an aMZI chip, a significantly longer incubation period is required; therefore, the fluidic system was replaced by a peristaltic pump connected directly to the inlet of the fluidic chamber. The peristaltic pump flow rate (25 μL/min) was kept constant during the entire experiment period. Except during injections, in which the peristaltic pump was first terminated, the inlet was placed in an Eppendorf containing samples of interest.

Fluorescence Microscopy

Fluorescence microscopic analysis was performed using an IX71 Epi-fluorescence microscope (Carl Zeiss AG, Germany) equipped with a 10× objective. For the AT488-labeled tDNA excitation, a filter cube of 460–490 nm was used, and the emission was collected using a 520 nm emission filter. Images were captured with a digital camera, an Olympus DP70 CCD camera, at a resolution of 1092 × 1080 pixels. Microscope settings of 100% illumination intensity and 1 s exposure time were fixed for all acquired images.
