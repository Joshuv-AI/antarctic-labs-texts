Spinal pain and associated factors among young motorcyclists a cross-sectional study with mediating analysis
Authors: Jona Edward Rajan, Balamurugan Janakiraman, Veeragoudhaman T.S., Hariharasudhan Ravichandran, Mohammad Sidiq, Jyoti Sharma, C. Nageswari, Faizan Kashoo, Vinitha Arasu, Vaibhav Agarwal, Neha Vyas, Mohammed M. Alshehri, Monira I. Aldhahi
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Spinal pain is a common musculoskeletal complaint among young adults and is sometimes influenced by lifestyle and behavioral factors. Motorcycles are the most common and convenient means of transportation in India, especially among the younger population. This study aimed to estimate the prevalence of spinal pain among young motorcycle riders and examine the predictors associated with spinal pain, while exploring the statistical mediating role of body mass index (BMI). The study was conducted at a large multidisciplinary higher education institution in India with approximately 60,000 students across 300 programs. As motorcycles are one of the common modes of transport, 422 eligible motorcycle riders were recruited for this study. Data were collected using systematic random sampling, a structured expert-validated questionnaire, and physical measurements. Bivariate and multivariate binary logistic regression model analysis was used to identify factors associated with spinal pain. A bootstrapped mediation analysis was performed to determine the extent to which the mediator variable (i.e., BMI) explained the association between the riding frequency and spinal pain. The overall cumulative prevalence of self-reported spinal pain experienced in the past year was 37.7% (95% CI: 32.7, 42.5). Factors like obesity (AOR 6.24, 95% CI: 1.9, 20.49), being overweight (AOR 2.78, 95% CI: 1.21, 6.37), not engaging in regular physical activity (AOR 2.24, 95% CI: 1.44, 3.48), experiencing fatigue while driving (AOR 2.66, 95% CI: 1.62, 4.42), and being female (AOR 1.87, 95% CI: 1.47, 2.97) were significantly associated with the likelihood of self-reported spinal pain among young motorcyclists in a multivariate logistic model with the level of significance set at 0.05. Furthermore, BMI appeared to partially explain the relationship between bike riding and spinal pain. Spinal pain was common among young motorcycle riders, and it is associated with higher BMI, riding fatigue, and physical inactivity. BMI partially explained the association between riding frequency and spinal pain. The findings highlight the potential importance of healthy body weight, regular physical activity, and addressing riding-related fatigue as part of spinal health strategies among young motorcycle riders.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-026-48458-7.

Introduction

Spinal pain is one of the most common musculoskeletal disorders globally and a major cause of disability among young adults. According to the Global Burden of Disease (GBD) study, low back pain alone affects about 570 million people globally and remains the leading cause of years lived with disability1, 2. The prevalence of spinal pain among young adults and university students ranged between 30% and 61%. In several low- and middle-income countries, particularly in South and Southeast Asia, motorcycles are one of the most common modes of transportation among young adults and university students3. Previous studies have reported a considerable burden of musculoskeletal symptoms among motorcycle riders, particularly affecting the neck and lower back4, 5.

A significant proportion of the younger population in India prefers riding bikes as their primary mode of transport6. Motorbikes are known for their fuel efficiency, maneuverability, mileage, parking convenience, and affordability when compared to four-wheelers7. Motorbikes constitute more than 75% of the total vehicle fleet operating across India8. According to the data collected from all states and union territories in 2023, India holds the record for registering the highest number of two-wheelers globally9, 10. Policies and regulations have been enforced to reduce motorcycle-related traffic fatalities. Motorbike riders are not only prone to sustaining fatal crash injuries but also musculoskeletal pain, which can be debilitating11. Musculoskeletal pain and dysfunction are often reported by bike riders, and the most common sources of pain originate from the muscles, connective tissues, joints, and bones12.

Motorcycles demand unique biomechanical challenges due to the complex interaction between the human body, the machine, and the road13. Literature reports that poor posture while riding, body vibration, traffic congestion, poor road conditions, prolonged riding hours, and fatigue may place mechanical stress on the cervical, thoracic, and lumbar spine14, 15. Over time, frequent and prolonged motorcycle use may affect spinal muscle flexibility and contribute to repetitive strain, leading to chronic musculoskeletal pain and dysfunction16. Cervical and low back pain are the most commonly reported spinal complaints among motorcycle riders, and despite improvements in road infrastructure and safety measures, musculoskeletal problems remain prevalent and are often linked to motorcycle ergonomics and riding posture16. The factors associated with the prevalence of spinal pain among motorbike users remain unclear. In a previous study, Liu et al. (2016)17 reported differences in fatal injury patterns of the musculoskeletal system between obese and normal body mass index motorbike riders. Their study highlighted the concerning issue of rider attributes in the prevalence of spinal pain. Passive commuting on a motorbike is associated with a sedentary lifestyle and obesity18.

The factors contributing to spinal pain among young motorcycle riders beyond posture and body vibration, and repetitive loading remain incomplete, and evidence examining behavioral and physiological pathways, such as BMI, remains scarce. Obesity can alter the mechanical stress on the spine among bike riders, and hence we hypothesize that BMI intervenes in the relationship between motorcycle riding and spinal pain among young riders. Therefore, this study aimed to estimate the prevalence of self-reported spinal pain among young motorcycle riders and to examine associated factors, including the potential mediating role of BMI in the relationship between riding frequency and spinal pain.

Methods

Study design and setting

A setting-based cross-sectional study was conducted among young bike riders at a large multidisciplinary university in India. This institution hosts approximately 60,000 students across 300 programs in medical and non-medical categories. Motorcycles are one of the common modes of transportation among students commuting to campus. The study population included all motorbike riders with a minimum of 1 year of riding experience (to allow sufficient exposure) and a valid Indian driving license during the study period (September 2024 to December 2024).

Sample size and sampling procedure

The required sample size (n) for this study was estimated using the single population proportion formula \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:(n=\frac{{Z}^{2}P\left(1-P\right)}{{d}^{2}})$$\end{document} for cross-sectional studies using the following assumptions: with limited evidence from India, we considered a prevalence (P) estimate of 50% to maximize the sample and allow adequate power, a 95% confidence interval (Z), and a margin of error of 5% (d). Based on these assumptions, the computed sample size was n = 385. Allowing for a 20% contingency and potential non-responses, the final sample size was determined to be n = 461.

Participants were recruited from five designated motorcycle parking zones (sampling frames) in the study area. These parking zones serve students from different academic program groups. As the bike riders entered the parking zone gate, they were approached and screened for eligibility based on the inclusion criteria. A proportional sample was allocated to each parking zone based on the parking capacity of each zone. Within the parking zone, participants were selected using a systematic random sampling technique. The sampling interval (k) was calculated by dividing the average number of motorcycles that were parked during the 5-working-day period before data collection by the required sample size for the zone. The first participant for each parking zone is randomly selected between the 1 st rider and the Kth rider, using a random lottery.

Eligibility criteria

Both genders, aged 18 years and above, who pursue higher education in the study area, hold a valid Indian driving license, and have ridden a motorbike for one year or more (to allow sufficient exposure to motorcycle riding), were considered for inclusion in the study. Individuals with recent injuries, spinal surgeries, acquired or any known deformities, and those who ride motorcycles less than 4 days a typical week or use special vehicles for the handicapped were excluded from participation in this study. Systematic random sampling was used to recruit samples from the parking zones of SRMIST.

Data collection procedure

Participants who fulfilled the selection criteria were explained the objectives of the study, and their consent was obtained. An extensive literature review was conducted to identify potential variables and possible interactions between the participants socio-demographic and clinical characteristics and bike riding. A structured and expert-validated questionnaire was developed to collect data, which consisted of socioeconomic and demographic factors, height, weight, BMI, and riding characteristics on the dependent variable, spinal pain19–21. The questionnaire was validated using a one-round Delphi method by receiving inputs from five experts in treating spinal pain (two orthopedists and three physiotherapists). The final version of the questionnaire contains the following components: Socio-demographic factors, physical anthropometric characteristics of the individual, characteristics of the road, characteristics of the motorbike riding, and spinal pain characteristics. The structured questionnaire used for data collection is attached as Supplementary File 1. The internal consistency of the spinal pain items included in the questionnaire was assessed using the Cronbach’s alpha coefficient. The reliability analysis demonstrated acceptable internal consistency (Cronbach’s α = 0.77), indicating that the items used to assess spinal pain characteristics were observed to be sufficiently correlated. The primary outcome variable was self-reported spinal pain in the past year. Predictor variables included age, sex, BMI, frequency of motorcycle riding, engagement in physical activity, fatigue during riding, and other riding characteristics. (Fig. 1).

Spinal pain was assessed using the Standardized Nordic musculoskeletal questionnaire, focusing on the spinal region (supplementary file 1). Participants were asked to report the presence of pain or discomfort in the spine anywhere between the first cervical vertebra and coccyx region, including the paraspinal areas. To help participants accurately identify and locate the pain region, a body diagram illustrating the spinal region was used. The frequency of riding was measured using a 5-point ordinal Likert scale (very rare, occasional, sometimes, frequently, and almost all the time) representing increasing levels of daily riding exposure.

Ethical considerations

Institutional Ethical Committee approval (SRMIEC-ST0224-1082) was obtained for this study from the SRM Medical College Hospital and Research Centre. This observational cross-sectional study was voluntarily registered in the Clinical Trials Registry of India (CTRI) to enhance transparency in reporting and documentation of the study protocol. The study adhered to the Declaration of Helsinki. All the participants read the participant information sheet containing instructions about voluntary participation, benefits, confidentiality of information, purpose, and risks and signed the written informed consent. The anonymity of the responses was strictly maintained.

Statistical analysis

All statistical analyses were performed using Jamovi version 2.6.26 for Windows. Descriptive statistics were used to summarize participant characteristics and variables. Continuous variables were expressed as mean and standard deviations, while the categorical variables were reported as frequencies and percentages. The statistical difference between the bike riders with and without spinal pain was assessed using Student’s t-test for continuous variables and the Chi-square test for categorical variables.

Prior to the correlation analyses, the distribution of continuous variables was assessed for the assumption of normality. Pearson’s correlation coefficient was performed to examine the relationship among key variables based on the conceptual mediation framework. To determine the independent variables associated with spinal pain, bivariate logistic regression analyses were conducted for each independent variable. Variables identified as significant in the bivariate analysis (p < 0.20) were subsequently entered in the multivariable logistic regression model to identify predictors of spinal pain. Results were reported as adjusted odds ratios (AORs) with 95% confidence intervals. Multicollinearity among the independent variables were assessed using variance inflation factors (VIF), the VIF ranged from 1.05 to 1.83. Given the cross-sectional design, the mediation analysis was conducted to explore if BMI explains the relationship between the motorcycle riding frequency and spinal pain rather than to establish causal pathways (Fig. 1). We used a bootstrap (5000 resamples) approach with bias-corrected confidence intervals (CI-BC)22. A p-value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant. According to the Sex and Gender Equity Reporting (SAGER) guidelines, we have reported the descriptive findings between males and females in Supplementary File 2.

Results

Among the 461 eligible bike riders who were approached, only 422 bike riders participated in this study, with a response rate of 91.5%. The most common reason for non-response is lack of time.

Socio-demographic and bike-related characteristics of the participants

Among the 422 participants included in this study, over 1/3rd (n = 159) of the bike riders reported experiencing spinal pain during the past year, indicating a substantial burden of spinal pain among young bike riders. The mean age (SD) of all participants was 22.84 ± 2.15. Participants who reported experiencing spinal pain had higher BMI and most of those who had spinal pain also reported having fatigue while riding (129/159, 81.1%). Regarding participation in regular physical activities, a modest response was obtained; 55.2% (n = 233) were not involved in any physical activity, and 44.8% (n = 189) were engaged in regular physical activity programs. Preliminary data analysis

The correlation analysis demonstrated moderate and significant relationships between several variables related to spinal pain. BMI and the frequency of bike riding showed a significant positive association with spinal pain, suggesting that both physiological and behavioral factors contribute to it. Sex was not significantly correlated with BMI, frequency of riding, or spinal pain. All the included variables demonstrated low to moderate correlations, indicating the absence of multicollinearity and supporting their inclusion in regression and mediating analysis (Table 2).

Factors associated with spinal pain

The multivariate logistic regression model identified several predictor variables of spinal pain among the young motorbike riders. Female bike riders are almost twice as likely (AOR: 1.87, 95% CI 1.47, 2.97) to report spinal pain compared to male riders. BMI categories emerged as an important predictor, with overweight and obese riders more likely to report spinal pain than their normal-weight counterparts (Overweight, AOR: 2.78, 95% CI 1.21, 6.37 and Obese, AOR 6.24, 95% CI 1.9, 20.49). The riders’ behavioral factors, like not engaging in regular physical activity and reporting fatigue while riding, were more likely to experience spinal pain. Variables like age category and number of riding days per week were not associated with reporting of spinal pain after adjusting for other variables (Table 3). The findings of the regression model suggest lifestyle and physiological factors might predict spinal pain among young bike riders. Mediating analysis

The mediation analysis examined if BMI explains the relationship between motorcycle riding frequency and spinal pain. The findings demonstrated that bike riding frequency was associated with higher BMI (path ‘a’, b = 0.883, SE = 0.171, p < 0.001, 95% BC-CI: 0.549, 1.22, p value < 0.001), and higher BMI was, in turn, associated with more reporting of spinal pain (path ‘b’, b = 0.034, SE = 0.006, p < 0.001, 95% BC-CI: 0.021, 0.046, p value = < 0.001). The indirect pathway through BMI accounted for approximately one-third (36–37%) of the total effect of riding frequency on spinal pain, indicating a meaningful proportion of the association between riding frequency and spinal pain. This finding indicates that BMI partially explains the relationship between riding behavior and spinal pain. The total effect of riding frequency on reported spinal pain was 0.0806, this indicates a positive association between riding frequency and spinal pain. Despite this mediation effect, the direct effect of riding frequency on spinal pain remained significant (0.0051) after accounting for BMI. This finding indicates the possibility of partial mediation of BMI in explaining the relation between riding frequency and spinal pain (Table 4).

Discussion

The primary aim of this study was to determine the 1-year prevalence of spinal pain among young motorcyclists in Chennai, India. The prevalence of spinal pain was 37.7% (95% CI: 32.7, 42.5), with 41.8% of females and 35.2% of males self-reporting spinal pain in the past 1-year. The key predictors of spinal pain were BMI, driving frequency, sex, physical activity, and fatigue.

The reported prevalence of spinal pain was moderate (37.7%), which is consistent with the findings reported from Nigeria (40.9%), Pakistan (51.6%), and Nepal (43.8%)18, 23, 24. This consistency could be due to the similarity in the frequency of motorcycle usage, traffic conditions, and standard of living in those study settings. In contrast, the prevalence reported in the present study is lower than that documented in Poland (58.4%), Thailand (58.3%), and Malaysia (53.83%)25–27. This inconsistency is due to motorbike-centric infrastructure, expressway and Federal Way systems, traffic congestion, long commuting times, and adverse weather conditions in those study settings, and more importantly, the variation in pain behavior between the study populations28, 29. In the present study, the participants routinely commuted on National Highway 45 (NH 45), Tamilnadu, India, extending 472 km with moderate traffic and well-established and well-maintained road infrastructure conditions.

The present study included collegiate-level individuals as participants with a mean age of 22.84 years, and 37% of them reported spinal pain in the past 1 year. In this age group, the musculoskeletal system functions at its peak level. Despite improved road and traffic conditions, individual characteristics such as awkward posture30, overuse injuries31, and physical fitness level32 are attributed to spinal pain. The transition from adolescence to young adulthood is characterized by increased cumulative stress and lifestyle factors, such as prolonged sitting, lack of physical activity, and other occupation-related factors33.

Obesity and being overweight were the major risk factors identified as being highly associated with spinal pain in motorcyclists in this study. The frequency of passive commuting on motorbikes decreases physical activity and worsens the body mass index34, 35. In a recent study among Colombian University students, the authors36 suggested that active commuting to and from the university promotes better cardiometabolic health than passive commuting37. The association between obesity and musculoskeletal pain is well documented in the literature37, 38. Increased body fat percentage and lean body mass combined with physical inactivity pose wide-ranging adverse effects, such as an increased risk of metabolic disorders and musculoskeletal dysfunctions. In individuals with an overweight and obese body mass index, biomechanical changes in the lumbar spine contribute to the incidence of low back pain. The lumbar lordosis angle, lumbosacral angle, sacral inclination angle, and lumbosacral disc angle tend to increase in individuals with truncal obesity. Poor physical fitness and abnormal lumbosacral angles increase shear and compressive stresses on spinal joints, resulting in pain and other dysfunctions39–41. Poor core stability, awkward/sustained static postures, and a sedentary lifestyle in overweight/obese individuals for prolonged hours increase the mechanical stress on the entire spine and intervertebral disc and result in cervical pain42–44.

Our findings support the hypothesis of the mediating effect of BMI on the association between driving frequency and spinal pain. This finding expands on the previously reported45–47 role of BMI in the effect on spinal pain, particularly low back pain. A higher BMI, especially during driving, exerts significant mechanical stress on the spine of bike riders, and vibratory factors (vibro-creep), driving postures, and more importantly, the transmitted road shocks are possible factors that could contribute to more reporting of spinal pain in this population48.

In the mediating analysis path ‘a’, bike riding frequency (independent variable) was observed to affect the BMI (dependent variable) of the riders. The frequency of motorcycle riding has been linked to BMI outcomes, particularly in urban settings. Previous evidence49 indicates that higher motorcycle use correlates with an increased risk of being overweight or obese. For instance, a study in Taiwan found that individuals in the highest quartile of motorcycle use had 50% higher odds of being overweight than those in the lowest quartile. This trend suggests that sedentary transport modes, such as motorcycle riding, may contribute to weight gain. In path ‘b’ of the mediation analysis, our study results demonstrated a significant effect of BMI on spinal pain. Although the relationship between BMI and spinal pain is complex and multifactorial, various studies50–52 have indicated that a higher BMI is generally associated with spinal pain. Segar et al. in 201650reported a 5-point increase in BMI correlated with greater leg pain scores, although the effect on back pain was less pronounced. In another study, Elgaeva et al51.found that BMI increased the odds of experiencing back pain by 1.15 times. In addition, the frequency of motorcycle riding has significant implications for spinal pain, particularly lower back pain (LBP)21. Research indicates that both the type of motorcycle and riding posture can contribute to the prevalence and intensity of spinal pain among riders. In contrast, a few studies52, 53 found that motorbike riding, weather, and road surface conditions appear to have little effect on spinal pain outcomes in motorcyclists.

The present study also revealed that 36.8% of spinal pain among riders was due to the indirect effect of bike-riding frequency mediated through increased BMI. This is similar to the findings of Gautam and Shrestha (2023), who reported that motorcyclists, especially those engaged in commercial activities, experienced high rates of LBP, with BMI being a critical risk factor24. Although the prevalence of spinal pain among motorcyclists can be attributed to various factors, it is exacerbated by higher BMI levels. Although the majority of the motorbike-riding participants were men, women were 1.8 times more likely to develop spinal pain. This is in contrast to other findings reported in the literature25, 54, which indicates that a higher percentage of male motorcyclists experience more spinal pain than their female counterparts. BMI, poor posture, and the physical demands of operating a motorcycle may contribute to spinal pain in females. Driving motorbikes with exaggerated anterior pelvic tilt for prolonged periods could result in muscular imbalances (lower crossed syndrome), such as weak abdominal and gluteal muscles, tight iliopsoas and lumbar extensors, and undue stress on the pelvic floor muscles. Tightness in the lumbar extensors can exaggerate the lumbar spine lordotic posture while riding a motorbike and can result in pain55.

Individuals experiencing fatigue are 2.6 times at risk of developing56 spinal pain. Physical fitness plays a key role in preventing fatigue while driving57. Muscle strength and endurance are necessary for maintaining an upright balance while riding motorbikes. Lack of muscle activity, insufficient force production, and poor postural control increase exhaustion and muscular fatigue58. Road monotony and perceptual motor adjustments (using handlebars, repeated clutch, appropriate engagement of hand and foot brake) are additional factors that could contribute to perceived fatigue while riding motorbikes59.

Similar to the findings reported in this study, a recent study from California60 stated that dependence on motor vehicles is associated with physical inactivity. Due to Long hauls61, urban sprawl62, staggered timings63, and multiple transfers in public transit, people prioritize motorbikes for commuting. Motorbike usage reduces the opportunity for active commuting and indirectly promotes sedentary lifestyle behaviors64. Lack of physical fitness and physical inactivity were the main factors contributing to spinal pain in young motorcyclists in this study. Motorbike design, vehicle ergonomics, and road conditions are often attributed to musculoskeletal pain. In addition, the physical fitness of the rider plays a major role in the prevention of spinal and other musculoskeletal discomfort in young motorcycle riders.

The present study had some limitations. First, the participants were young adults; hence, the results of the present study may not be generalizable to other age groups. Second, due to the complex interplay of individual factors, driving conditions, and vehicle design, data regarding posture while driving are difficult to evaluate. Third, the present study requires other anthropometric measures, such as waist circumference, shoulder breadth, and segmental limb lengths, to be associated with spinal pain. Fourth, the current study or analyses cannot establish a causal relationship between the variables under investigation. Finally, the study population may not represent the broader population of young motorcyclists in the community. Nevertheless, the university hosts a large student population from diverse socio-economic backgrounds, which might contribute to the evidence related to spinal pain among young bike riders.

Clinical implications and prevention

The findings of this study prompt a few key clinical and public health implications. The relatively high prevalence of spinal pain among young motorcycle riders warrants the need for early musculoskeletal health awareness in this population. More importantly, higher BMI was strongly associated with spinal pain and also partially explains the relationship between riding frequency and spinal pain. This suggests that excess body weight may increase mechanical stress on the spine during prolonged riding. These findings underscore the complex interaction between riders and machines (bikes) and the importance of weight management as a preventive or management strategy. In addition, fatigue during riding was associated with spinal pain, indicating that regular breaks, proper riding posture, and ergonomic awareness may help reduce spinal strain among frequent riders. Institutions where motorcycles are a common mode of transportation could consider implementing awareness programs and integrating musculoskeletal health promotion or student wellness programs. Future longitudinal studies are needed to understand the temporal relationships between behavioral, lifestyle variables and spinal pain among young motorcyclists. Multi-center studies involving riders from diverse educational, occupational, and community backgrounds are warranted to improve the external validity of these findings.

Conclusion

Spinal pain was relatively common among young motorcycle riders and was associated with behavioral and lifestyle factors. BMI partially explained the association between riding frequency and spinal pain, suggesting that body weight may play a role in the observed relationship and warrant further exploration of the complex interaction between the morphology of the human body and motorbikes. These findings highlight the potential importance of regular physical activity, maintaining body weight, and addressing fatigue while riding to reduce spinal pain risk among young motorbike riders..
