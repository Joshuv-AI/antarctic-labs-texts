Platelet Gene Expression in Systemic Lupus Erythematosus and Cardiovascular Health
Authors: Matthew A Muller, Elliot Luttrell-Williams, Hannah Bash, Macintosh G Cornwell, H Michael Belmont, Peter Izmirly, Haley Rosmann, Michael S Garshick, Tessa J Barrett, Stuart Katz, Kelly V Ruggles, Jill P Buyon, Jeffrey S Berger
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Visual AbstractKey Words: platelets, SLE, transcriptome, vascular health

Systemic lupus erythematosus (SLE) is a complex autoimmune disorder characterized by an undulating course and diverse clinical manifestations of varying severity.1,2 The spectrum of immune dysfunction in SLE adds to its heterogeneity, with cardiovascular disease identified as a major contributor to morbidity and mortality. The increased cardiovascular risk in SLE remains inadequately explained by traditional and SLE-associated risk factors, necessitating a deeper understanding of the molecular drivers of this comorbidity.3,4

In addition to their well-established role in thrombosis and hemostasis, platelets are immune cells with inflammatory effector cell properties. Accruing data support the nonhemostatic role of platelets in SLE.5, 6, 7, 8, 9 We and others demonstrated that individuals with SLE exhibit a hyperreactive platelet phenotype with increased platelet aggregation and increased receptor expression of activation makers.5,9,10 In addition, platelets isolated from these patients induce a proinflammatory and proadhesive endothelium, suggesting that platelets are pathogenic mediators of impaired vascular health and risk of cardiovascular disease.11 Platelets have been implicated in mesangial remodeling and glomerulosclerosis in lupus nephritis, as well as mediators of myeloid inflammation and macrophage cytokine secretion.6,12 Increased platelet activation in SLE induced by immune complexes, complement response, and danger-associated molecular patterns has been shown to cause downstream immune cell activation and increased circulating inflammatory biomarkers reflective of impaired vascular health.7,13

Platelet transcriptomics have been used for the identification of disease-specific molecular signatures. Prior data from our group demonstrated that the platelet transcriptome was highly enriched in pathways related to interferon response, WNT signaling, and RNA splicing in participants with SLE compared with matched control subjects.5 Using 31 transcripts, an SLE prevalence signature which discriminated SLE patients vs healthy control subjects was developed with machine learning. In addition, we created an algorithm that distinguished SLE with increased disease activity that correlated with changes in disease activity. This led to the generation of the SLE Activity Platelet–Gene Expression Signature (SLAP-GES).

The objective of this study was to validate SLAP-GES in an independent diverse cohort of patients with SLE and control subjects. Furthermore, the association of SLAP-GES with measurements of platelet activity and vascular health were investigated.

Methods

Study population

The study was conducted in accordance with policies of the New York University Langone Medical Center Institutional Review Board. Participants were recruited from the NYU Lupus Cohort, a prospective registry open to enrollment of any English-, Mandarin-, or Spanish-speaking patients age 18 years or older seen at NYU Langone Health or Bellevue Hospital Center since 2014 and who fulfill criteria for at least 1 the following classification schemes: 1) the American College of Rheumatology (ACR) revised classification criteria;14,15 2) the Systemic Lupus International Collaborating Clinics classification criteria;16 or 3) the European League Against Rheumatism (EULAR)/ACR classification criteria.17 All patients and control subjects were consented in accordance with the NYU and Bellevue Institutional Review Boards. Exclusion criteria for the study included the use of any dose of aspirin and/or an anticoagulant, a platelet count <100 × 109 g/L, and/or a hemoglobin level <9 g/dL. The patient population enrolled in the validation cohort is distinct from that described previously in the derivation cohort.5

At each visit, SLE disease activity defined by SELENA Systemic Lupus Erythematosus Disease Activity Index (SLEDAI) HYBRID was assessed.18, 19, 20 To evaluate clinical phenotypes, patients were assigned more granularly to 1 of 8 clinical categories based on dominance of laboratory and/or specific organ involvement to more precisely describe a clinically “dominant” phenotype. These categories are described in the following text. In addition, remission status as defined by the Doris Remission criteria21 was assessed at each visit. Medication use and dosages were also collected at each visit: glucocorticoids, hydroxychloroquine, immunosuppressants including azathioprine, mycophenolate mofetil, methotrexate, belimumab, and tacrolimus.

Lupus classification categories

We established hierarchical disease categories to evaluate patients based on distinct clinical and laboratory features known to be associated with SLE. This was done to provide greater clinical transparency and granularity than the point scale of the traditional SLEDAI. The 8 disease categories are arranged in descending order of disease activity albeit acknowledged that this may be arbitrary and the extent of organ dominance variable.

Blood collection

Blood was collected into tubes containing 3.2% (0.105 mol/L) sodium citrate (Becton Dickinson, #369714) for platelet activity measures, and platelet RNA collection. Platelet samples were processed immediately after blood collection. Blood was also collected into EDTA tubes for hematology analysis using the Sysmex XN-1000 analyzer.

Platelet RNA purification

After a 15-min rest following blood collection, the blood was centrifuged at 200 g for 10 minutes to obtain platelet-rich plasma. Following addition of acid citrate dextrose to the platelet-rich plasma in a 1:10 ratio and a 10-minute rest, the platelets were subjected to centrifugation at 1,000 g for 10 minutes. The pellet was resuspended in EasySep Buffer (STEMCELL Technologies) and incubated with microbeads to deplete leukocytes and red blood cells using EasySep Human CD45 Depletion Kit II and EasySep RBC Depletion Reagent respectively (STEMCELL Technologies). Efficiency of CD45+ cell and red blood cell depletion was confirmed by flow cytometry at the end of the isolation protocol. Isolated platelets were lyzed in 500 μL QIAzol (Qiagen) and stored at −80 °C until use.

RNA was extracted using Direct-zol MicroPrep Kits (ZYMO RESEARCH) according to manufacturer’s instructions. RNA quality and quantity were determined using an Agilent 2100 Bioanalyzer (Agilent Technologies). Sequencing libraries were generated with Clontech SMART-Seq HT kit (Takara Bio) for low-input mRNA per manufacturer’s instructions. The obtained libraries were quantified, normalized, pooled, and sequenced using the S1 100 flow cell on NovaSeq 6000 instrument (Illumina).

RNA sequencing

The processing of RNA sequencing FASTQ files was carried out using the Seq-N-Slide pipeline.22 This pipeline involves a series of steps, including quality control using FASTQC,23 trimming using Trimmomatic,24 screening using fastqscreen,25 processing with Picard,26 alignment to hg38 using STAR,27 and counting features using featureCounts.28

Subsequently, samples were filtered based on library size and depth. Libraries with fewer than 1,000,000 genes were removed, and genes with fewer than 4 counts per million in 75% of samples were also removed. To identify and eliminate sample outliers, agglomerative clustering and principal component analysis was employed.

COVID and psoriasis cohort

To investigate the relationship between SLAP-GES and other disease cohorts enriched in immune activation, we analyzed the platelet transcriptome in the setting of COVID and psoriasis.29, 30, 31 A description of each cohort was previously published.32, 33, 34, 35 Briefly, the COVID cohort included 8 patients with COVID and 10 matched control subjects.32,34 As described previously, the psoriasis cohort included 51 participants with psoriasis and 39 control subjects.33 Studies were approved by the New York University Langone Health Institutional Review Board. The COVID cohort was performed with a waiver of informed consent while participants in the psoriasis cohort signed written and informed consent.

Differential expression analysis and pathway enrichment

To identify differentially expressed genes between individuals with SLE and those without, we employed the DESeq2 Wald test.36 Gene set enrichment analysis (GSEA) was conducted using clusterprofiler37 version 4.8.3 and msigdbr38 version 7.5.1. Genes were preranked based on their log2 fold change (log2FC), allowing us to calculate enrichment for Gene Ontology (GO) and Hallmark terms in the Molecular Signatures Database.39

Lupus prevalence signature

We evaluated the applicability of a lupus prevalence signature, previously constructed of 31 genes derived from the overlap of genes: 1) differentially expressed in lupus compared with control subjects; 2) linked to an interferon response labeled gene coexpression network; and 3) linked to an WNT Signaling and RNA Splicing labeled gene coexpression network. To assess model performance, we utilized the area under the receiver-operating characteristic (AUROC) curve and area under the precision-recall curve (AUPRC), also known as average precision (AP). AP summarizes all of the available precisions, or positive predictive values, for all available recalls or sensitivity.

Lupus activity signature

Next, we validated the transcriptomics lupus activity signature delineated, SLAP-GES.5 The SLAP-GES score included 678 genes (397 up-regulated, 279 down-regulated), reflecting differential expression in serologically and/or clinically active (SLEDAI >0) vs inactive (SLEDAI = 0) disease. Of these 678 genes, 548 (313 up-regulated, 235 down-regulated) were expressed in the validation cohort after quality control. Samples were scored using singscore, a normalized rank-based single sample scoring metric.40 A tutorial to calculate SLAP-GES is available on github.41 We calculated the SLAP-GES for each participant in the new validation cohort and assessed its association with SLE activity. Finally, we sought to assess the clinical relevance of SLAP-GES by investigating its unadjusted and adjusted association with cardiovascular risk factors and measures of vascular health.5

Light transmission aggregometry

Light transmission aggregometry is the most used and accepted method to assess platelet function.42 Platelet aggregometry was conducted on a Helena AggRAM light transmission aggregometer based on the Born method.43 Platelet aggregation was measured in response to submaximal stimulation with epinephrine. Platelet aggregation in response to submaximal epinephrine was chosen as we and others have shown a distinct bimodal platelet aggregation response which is reproducible over time and is generalizable to other forms of platelet activation.44, 45, 46

Vascular health assessment

The glycocalyx and brachial artery reactivity testing examination were conducted during a fasting state in the morning. Participants had sublingual sidestream dark field microscopy to assess microvascular health, including microvascular percent red blood cell (RBC) filling, perfused boundary region (PBR), and perfusion time.47 Increased PBR indicates thinner glycocalyx with greater vulnerability to endothelial damage and, thus, impaired vascular health.47 Lower % RBC filling indicates poor microvascular perfusion.

Brachial arterial reactivity in response to increased blood flow (flow-mediated dilation [FMD]) and sublingual nitroglycerin (nitroglycerin-mediated dilation [NMD]) was determined noninvasively with a duplex ultrasound vascular imaging system connected to an 11-MHz linear array probe (Sonosite Inc), with imaging protocol adapted with permission from guideline recommendations as previously described.48,49

Statistical analysis

Data are presented as the mean ± SD, median (25th, 75th percentile) for continuous variables and number and percentage of patients. Differential expression analysis included the DESeq2 Wald test, and groups were compared with the Wilcoxon rank-sum (2 groups) or Kruskal-Wallis test (>2 groups).36 Data were normalized as the log2 transformed median of the ratios of normalized counts, unless otherwise specified. Before machine learning or clustering analysis, data were centered and scaled. AUROC and AUPRC are presented with 95% CIs. Normality was assessed through histogram inspection or the Shapiro-Wilks test. Parametric or nonparametric testing was performed based on the normality of the data. P values were adjusted using the Benjamini-Hochberg method to control for false discovery rate.50 Pearson or Spearman correlation coefficient (r) was used to evaluate the association of 2 continuous variables for normally and non-normally distributed data, respectively. Univariable and multivariable regression models were used to evaluate the association between the SLAP-GES with measurements of platelet activity and vascular health. Plots were made using ggplot2 in R.51 Unless specified otherwise, an adjusted P value cutoff of 0.05 was employed. The analysis was carried out using R version 4.4.2, and machine learning was carried out in python version 3.12.2 with scikit-learn version 1.2.2.52

Results

Study population clinical characteristics

We analyzed platelet RNA sequencing data from 89 participants: 71 with a diagnosis of SLE and 18 control subjects. Baseline characteristics of the population are noted in Table 1. Among SLE participants, mean age was 38 ± 13 years, and all participants were women. All races and ethnicities were represented. While age was older in participants with SLE than control subjects, sex, race, ethnicity, body mass index (BMI), and smoking status were comparable between groups. In the SLE cohort, the mean SLEDAI was 3.1 ± 2.8 (range of 0-12), 29 (43.3%) were classified as clinically active in at least 1 clinical domain, and 38 (56.7%) were in Doris remission.21

Differential expression in Lupus

Participants with SLE in the validation cohort were comparable to the SLE population in the derivation cohort (Table 1).5 We compared the expression of platelet genes between the 2 cohorts and found excellent agreement emphasized by a high explained variance in normalized gene expression between the cohorts (R2 = 0.91; 95% CI: 0.90-0.92) (Supplemental Figure 1).

In our validation cohort, a total of 488 genes (346 up-regulated and 142 down-regulated) were differentially expressed (Padj < 0.05) between lupus and control participants (Figure 1A). When comparing the differential expression in lupus vs control subjects with our derivation cohort,5 we found a significant correlation between SLE vs control in the derivation and validation cohort (r = 0.46; P = 2.2 × 10−16), with 303 of 488 genes (62.1%) differentially expressed between SLE and control subjects in both cohorts (Figure 1B). As expected, platelet interferon-mediated genes (eg, LGALS3BP, IFI27, and IFI6) were up-regulated in both the derivation and validation cohort.6,53 When we focused on the most significant genes with a |log2FC| >1 in both cohorts, the r value increased to 0.69 (P = 2.2 × 10−16). When examining biological pathways dysregulated between SLE and control subjects in our validation cohort, we found up-regulation of viral defense processes (normalized enrichment score = 2.42; Padj = 1.2 × 10−8) and interferon-mediated signaling pathway (normalized enrichment score = 2.20; Padj = 1.2 × 10−8), consistent with our findings from the derivation cohort (Figure 1B).

Lupus prevalence

In our previously published derivation cohort,5 a platelet signature of 31 transcripts was developed using a machine-based learning algorithm and performed well in discriminating participants with SLE vs control subjects (AUROC of 0.97; 95% CI: 0.94-1.00). In our new validation cohort, the same model performed well, with an AUROC of 0.84 (95% CI: 0.81-0.87) (Figure 2A) and an AUPRC of 0.96 (95% CI: 0.958-0.962) (Figure 2B). As expected, the SLE prediction probability was significantly different between SLE and control subjects (Figure 2C). Importantly, the SLE prevalence prediction probability was robust across subgroups, including stratification by age, race, ethnicity and BMI (Figure 2D).

Next, we assessed the reproducibility of our findings by investigating the SLE prevalence model from 20 participants with SLE and available platelet RNASeq at 2 timepoints (median of 4 months after their baseline collection). There was excellent correlation in the model's probability score of SLE over time (r = 0.73; P < 0.001) (Supplemental Figure 2).

Lupus activity signature

Using our previously derived SLE activity score,5 SLAP-GES, we investigated whether this score would discriminate SLE activity and clinical phenotypes in our validation cohort. The SLAP-GES was significantly different between inactive (SLEDAI 0) vs active (SLEDAI >0) SLE (0.34 ± 0.24 vs 0.51 ± 0.22; P = 0.004) (Figure 3A). There was a stepwise increase in SLAP-GES from control, inactive disease, serologically active disease (anti-dsDNA positive and/or low complement levels with no other SLEDAI domain scored), and active disease (P = 2.3 × 10−7) (Figure 3B). Following adjustment for age, race, ethnicity, and medication use, SLAP-GES remained associated with disease activity (P = 6.8 × 10−6). Next, we stratified SLAP-GES into quartiles and found that the majority of healthy individuals were in the lowest quartile while the majority of clinically active patients were in the highest quartile (Figure 3C). Among the 71 SLE participants, SLAP-GES was significantly higher in those who were clinically active than those in Doris remission (0.58 ± 0.22 vs 0.38 ± 0.22; P = 0.0006).

We then tested the activity signature’s performance in a subset of 20 SLE participants with platelet RNASeq at baseline and follow-up. SLEDAI did not differ over time (baseline 4.2 ± 3.8 vs follow-up 4.0 ± 3.4; P = 0.61) in this cohort. Of note, the value of SLAP-GES was consistent over time (r = 0.76; P = 9.0 × 10−5) suggesting that SLAP-GES is reproducible over time (Supplemental Figure 3).

Finally, because SLAP-GES reflects, in part, enrichment in the immune response, we investigated the association of SLAP-GES with COVID infection and psoriasis, another autoimmune disease distinct from SLE. We calculated SLAP-GES in those hospitalized with COVID (n = 8) vs those hospitalized without COVID (n = 10). Overall, average age was 63 ± 8 years, 27.8% were women, and there were no significant differences in demographics according to COVID status. SLAP-GES was not significantly different between patients with vs without COVID (P = 0.24) (Supplemental Figure 4). Next, we compared SLAP-GES in 51 patients with psoriasis and 39 control subjects. Overall, average age was 48 ± 16 years, 44.0% were women, and no significant differences in demographics were observed between patients with vs without psoriasis. Patients with psoriasis did not have a higher SLAP-GES score than those without psoriasis (P = 0.88) (Supplemental Figure 4).

SLAP-GES associates with functional cardiovascular measurements

To assess the functional relevance of SLAP-GES, we investigated its relationship with clinical variables, platelet activity, and vascular health. Although no difference was observed in SLAP-GES by age or ethnicity, SLAP-GES differed by race (P = 0.007) (Figure 4A). Because leukopenia and lymphopenia are associated with SLE disease activity,54, 55, 56 it is not surprising that SLAP-GES was inversely correlated with total WBC (r = −0.59; P = 1.8 × 10−9) and lymphocyte counts (r = −0.47; P = 5.2 × 10−6) (Figure 4B).

There was no significant correlation between SLAP-GES and platelet count (r = −0.13; P = 0.23) or platelet size (r = 0.06; P = 0.57). However, there was a significant association between SLAP-GES and markers of platelet activity. SLAP-GES was positively associated with leukocyte platelet aggregates (r = 0.36; P = 0.014), neutrophil platelet aggregates (r = 0.30; P = 0.043), and monocyte platelet aggregates (r = 0.24; P = 0.086), although the association with monocyte platelet aggregates did not reach statistical significance (Figure 4C).

Patients with SLE have vascular dysfunction and accelerated risk of cardiovascular disease.3,4 Thus, we examined the relationship between SLAP-GES and markers of macrovascular health (brachial artery FMD and NMD) as well as microvascular health (endothelial glycocalyx). SLAP-GES was inversely correlated with both FMD (r = −0.32; P = 0.045) and NMD (r = −0.39; P = 0.02) (Figure 4D), positively associated with PBR (r = 0.35; P = 0.011) and inversely correlated with percent RBC (r = −0.36; P = 0.009) from the glycocalyx (Figure 4D). Thus, correlates of impaired macrovascular and microvascular health are associated with increasing SLAP-GES.

To understand the clinical relevance of SLAP-GES beyond SLEDAI, we tested the association of SLEDAI with the same variables listed previously: clinical variables, platelet activity, and vascular health. As expected, SLEDAI was inversely correlated with WBC count and lymphopenia. In contrast, SLEDAI was not associated with platelet aggregation, macrovascular assessment of FMD or NMD, or microvascular heath as assessed by PBR (Supplemental Figure 5). There was an inverse correlation between SLEDAI and percent RBC (r = −0.44; P = 0.006) (Supplemental Figure 5). Altogether, these data suggest that in contrast to SLEDAI, SLAP-GES is associated with platelet activity and impaired vascular health.

Discussion

Accumulating data support the nonhemostatic role of platelets in SLE.7 In addition to their well-described role in thrombosis and hemostasis, platelets are increasingly recognized as potent effector cells in inflammation and altered immunity. In the setting of SLE, platelet activity is increased and SLE platelets have been found to induce endothelial cell activation, myeloid cell inflammation, and contribute to kidney damage.6,11,12

Previous analyses of the platelet transcriptome in SLE identified genes and pathways implicated in SLE pathogenesis, including those involved in interferon-mediated signaling and viral defense mechanisms.5,57 Prior data from our group demonstrated potent platelet effector properties, including platelet-mediated vascular dysfunction and myeloid inflammation. Interleukin-1b was released from activated platelets isolated from patients with SLE and induced endothelial cell activation and increased platelet adhesion.11 In the context of SLE, increased interactions between platelets and endothelial cells, monocytes, leukocytes may be pivotal in the heightened risk of thrombo-inflammation in SLE. We previously demonstrated that platelet released LGALS3BP is associated with lupus clinical activity, is higher in lupus nephritis, and acts as a key mediator of myeloid derived immune cell inflammation.6

Understanding the importance of platelets within SLE activity offers a unique perspective to better understand the pathogenesis of lupus activity and vascular health. Here we show SLAP-GES, a platelet signature of SLE activity, also correlates with platelet activity and vascular health. This study validates SLAP-GES while also pointing to a clinical use of SLAP-GES as a tool to understand vascular health in the context of SLE.

Validation of platelet genetic expression score

The current study prospectively validates prior findings from our group demonstrating the utility of platelet transcriptomics in discriminating both SLE prevalence and activity. Our prevalence signature of 31 genes was successful at discriminating between SLE and control participants, with consistent performance across demographics of age, race/ethnicity, and BMI. Furthermore, the activity signature, SLAP-GES, effectively distinguished between inactive and clinically active SLE and demonstrated a stepwise increase in score with disease activity progression. Importantly, the stability of these signatures over time suggests their potential as reliable indicators of disease status.

Clinical relevance and cardiovascular implications

We outline a platelet transcriptomic signature for SLE activity that is significantly associated with measures of platelet activity and vascular health, implying it may serve as an indicator of cardiovascular risk. SLAP-GES correlated with platelet aggregation and with markers of macrovascular and microvascular function. These associations remained significant even after adjusting for confounding factors, emphasizing the independent predictive value of platelet transcriptomics in assessing cardiovascular risk in SLE. Our findings raise the hypothesis that platelets are key mediators of increased cardiovascular disease commonly observed in patients with SLE. Our study also raises the hypothesis that the platelet transcriptome can identify SLE patients at high cardiovascular risk, and those most likely to benefit from antiplatelet therapy.

Study limitations

Several important limitations need to be borne in mind when interpreting this study. First, our transcriptomic data comprised entirely of women, and our results may not be reflective of men with SLE. However, SLE is much more prevalent in women than men. Second, disease severity of SLE was relatively well controlled, and thus, future studies of sicker patients with SLE are needed. Third, SLE participants were not followed for cardiovascular events, and therefore we focused on the association of SLAP-GES with cardiovascular risk, including platelet activity and vascular function. Finally, the impact of cardiovascular prevention medications on SLAP-GES was not evaluated. Nonetheless, prior work from our group suggest that P2Y12 inhibitors may be effective at reducing interferon-related signatures like SLAP-GES.34

Conclusions

We validated that our circulating platelet transcriptomic signature SLAP-GES is associated with SLE disease activity in a new independent population. This signature identifies individuals with SLE who have increased platelet activity and impaired vascular function.

Future studies should focus on elucidating the mechanistic underpinnings of platelet dysregulation in SLE and explore the utility of platelet transcriptomics in guiding personalized therapeutic approaches. Prospective and longitudinal studies assessing the prognostic use of SLAP-GES are warranted to determine the association of SLAP-GES with cardiovascular events in SLE. Altogether, our findings underscore the potential of platelet transcriptomics as a novel avenue for advancing precision medicine in SLE and improving cardiovascular risk assessment and management in this high-risk population.

COMPETENCY IN MEDICAL KNOWLEDGE: Among SLE patients, SLAP-GES can differentiate those with increased platelet activity and impaired vascular health.

TRANSLATIONAL OUTLOOK: This study shows that a novel, noninvasive blood-based test based on the circulating platelet transcriptome may be useful for assessing SLE disease activity and associated cardiovascular risk.
