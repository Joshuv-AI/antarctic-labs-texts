Transcription Factor EB-Mediated Lysosomal Function Regulation for Determining Stem Cell Fate under Metabolic Stress
Authors: Chang Woo Chae, Young Hyun Jung, Ho Jae Han
Published: 2023

ABSTRACT
Stem cells require high amounts of energy to replicate their genome and organelles and differentiate into numerous cell types. Therefore, metabolic stress has a major impact on stem cell fate determination, including self-renewal, quiescence, and differentiation. Lysosomes are catabolic organelles that influence stem cell function and fate by regulating the degradation of intracellular components and maintaining cellular homeostasis in response to metabolic stress. Lysosomal functions altered by metabolic stress are tightly regulated by the transcription factor EB (TFEB) and TFE3, critical regulators of lysosomal gene expression. Therefore, understanding the regulatory mechanism of TFEB-mediated lysosomal function may provide some insight into stem cell fate determination under metabolic stress. In this review, we summarize the molecular mechanism of TFEB/TFE3 in modulating stem cell lysosomal function and then elucidate the role of TFEB/TFE3-mediated transcriptional activity in the determination of stem cell fate under metabolic stress.

INTRODUCTION

In stem cell research, understanding the relevance of lysosomal function is significant due to its wide range of roles. Lysosomes play a crucial role in maintaining the equilibrium between self-renewal and differentiation processes in stem cells (Garcia-Prat et al., 2021; Villegas et al., 2019). Moreover, they affect cell cycle progression and proliferation by integrating nutritional availability with cellular responses. Furthermore, dysfunction of lysosomes can result in the accumulation of cellular waste and damaged components. Waste accumulation creates large vacuoles that impair growth and may lead to metabolic disorders (Bradić et al., 2023). Determining the impacts of lysosomal dysfunction-mediated metabolic disorders on stem cell maintenance is essential for understanding metabolic diseases (Ballabio and Bonifacino, 2020; Borger et al., 2017). This, in turn, can negatively impact stem cell functionality, facilitate the onset of senescence, and contribute to the decline associated with aging (Lee, 2022). Therefore, preserving optimal lysosomal function is crucial for maintaining stem cell function and differentiation potential. Dysregulation of certain factors can lead to a decline in the stemness features of cells, including their ability to self-renew and remain in a quiescent state. Understanding the intricate relationship among lysosomal function, metabolic diseases, and stem cell properties provides valuable insights about the processes that regulate stem cell activity. This understanding is crucial for the development of therapeutic uses of stem cells and regenerative strategies. Furthermore, it offers opportunities to augment the therapeutic potential of stem cell therapy and solve various medical obstacles related to lysosomal dysfunction. Thus, in this review, we summarize the transcription factor EB (TFEB)-mediated regulation of lysosomal function and examine its role and importance in the regulation of stem cell fate under metabolic stress.

REGULATORY MECHANISM OF TRANSCRIPTION FACTOR EB ON LYSOSOMAL FUNCTION OF STEM CELLS UNDER METABOLIC STRESS

TFEB is a member of the microphthalmia/transcription factor E (MiT/TFE) family, which consists of basic helix-loop-helix (bHLH) transcription factors (Kim et al., 2021; Raben and Puertollano, 2016). Members of the MiT/TFE family share highly conserved functional domains across different species, including those required for DNA binding, homo/heterodimerization, regulation of protein localization, and stability (La Spina et al., 2021). They regulate lysosomal gene expression via recognition of the coordinated lysosomal expression and regulation motif, a 10-base E-box-like palindromic sequence (GTCACGTGAC) conserved in several lysosomal genes within 200 base pairs of the transcription initiation site (Zhang et al., 2020). Therefore, TFEB/TFE3 is crucial for development, longevity, immunomodulation, and survival of organisms in response to stress (La Spina et al., 2021; Samie and Cresswell, 2015). Post-translational modifications, including phosphorylation, ubiquitination, methylation, glycosylation, and acetylation, can regulate the activity of proteins (Ramazi and Zahiri, 2021). Further, the nuclear translocation and transcriptional activity of TFEB proteins can be regulated by their phosphorylation and acetylation (Fig. 1). Additionally, it can be translocated to the nucleus, kept in the nucleus due to impaired nuclear export, or retained in the nucleus by means of specific phosphorylation sites (Lu et al., 2021). Two serine residues, Ser142 and Ser211, are the main phosphorylation sites of TFEB, and TFE3 is phosphorylated at Ser321, which in turn controls its activity and cellular localization. TFEB and TFE3 are localized in the cytoplasm and become inactive when Ser142, Ser211, and Ser321 are phosphorylated. In addition to phosphorylation, acetylation of lysine residues (K91, K103, K116, and K430) has been found to enhance TFEB activity due to its higher nuclear translocation capacity (Zhang et al., 2018a). Moreover, direct interaction with 14-3-3 proteins results in TFEB/TFE3 sequestration in the cytosol. Collectively, the transcriptional activity of TFEB/TFE3 is regulated by their post-translational modifications, subcellular localization, and interactions with proteins.

Transcriptional control tightly regulates stem cell fate determinations, such as self-renewal, quiescence, and differentiation (Williams and Hare, 2011; Young, 2011). Similar to other dividing cells, stem cells have a high requirement for anabolic precursors to replicate the genome and organelles while simultaneously expending ATP. Therefore, metabolic stress can affect stem cell fate and function (Shyh-Chang and Ng, 2017). Our previous reports have revealed the association between metabolic stress and stem cell function and studied the self-renewal, differentiation potential, and therapeutic effects of stem cells (Jeon et al., 2014; Jung et al., 2022; Lee et al., 2019; Yun et al., 2012). Furthermore, we recently reported that maintaining lysosomal function is critical in the pathogenesis of metabolic diseases (Chae et al., 2020; 2022; 2023). In addition to the degradation of intracellular components, lysosomes sense the environment and nutrient status and modulate regulatory signaling pathways to maintain metabolic homeostasis and cellular function under metabolic stress (Gros and Muller, 2023). In particular, representative nutrient-sensing kinases, AMP-activated kinase (AMPK) and the mammalian target of rapamycin complex 1 (mTORC1), induce cellular catabolism and anabolism by activating the lysosomal membrane (Wolfson and Sabatini, 2017). During nutrient starvation, the lysosomal protein complex vacuolar H+-ATPase (v-ATPase)-Ragulator activates AMPK by recruiting AXIN/Liver kinase B1. Furthermore, AXIN inhibits the guanine nucleotide exchange factor activity of Rag GTPase that activates mTORC1, thereby dissociating mTORC1 from lysosomes and inactivating it. In contrast, under sufficient nutrients, the v-ATPase-Ragulator complex recruits Rag GTPase to translocate mTORC1 to the lysosomal membrane. Additionally, mTORC1 is phosphorylated by Rag GTPase and another GTPase, Rheb, to promote an anabolic signaling pathway by inducing macromolecule biogenesis and inhibiting autophagy and lysosomal biogenesis (Zhang et al., 2014; Zoncu et al., 2011). Thus, as regulation of AMPK and mTORC1 activity in the lysosomal membrane is essential to maintain cellular metabolism and function, maintaining lysosomal function is important for stem cell fate determination.

Along with TFE3, TFEB primarily regulates lysosomal gene expression in stem cells (Garcia-Prat et al., 2021; Villegas et al., 2019). Thus, by phosphorylating TFEB at Ser211 and TFE3 at Ser321 and sequestrating them in the cytosol through the interaction of TFEB/TFE3 with 14-3-3 proteins, mTORC1 prevents TFEB/TFE3 from translocating to the nucleus. In contrast, dephosphorylation of TFEB/TFE3 by inhibiting mTORC1 blocks its association with 14-3-3 proteins, leading to its translocation to the nucleus. Additionally, mTORC1 regulates TFEB activity by phosphorylating Ser122, which means that mTORC1 regulates TFEB activity through multiple phosphorylations (Vega-Rubin-de-Celis et al., 2017). Furthermore, Ser142, another site involved in the nuclear translocation and activity of TFEB, is phosphorylated by ERK2, a member of the MAPK family, and mTORC1 (Settembre et al., 2011). However, whether ERK2 phosphorylates TFEB in the lysosomal membrane remains unclear, but it can be recruited to lysosomes through interaction with the v-ATPase-Ragulator complex (Wunderlich et al., 2001). With sufficient nutrients and mTORC1, ERK2-induced phosphorylation of TFEB in Ser142 was seen to maintain its cytoplasmic retention. Conversely, ERK2 no longer sustains TFEB phosphorylation under cell starvation, allowing it to be translocated to the nucleus and activated (Settembre et al., 2011). Protein kinase C (PKC), a family of serine/threonine kinases, can be activated by increasing diacylglycerol or calcium ion concentrations. Hormones and neurotransmitters promote lysosomal gene expression by activating specific PKC isoforms, such as PKCα and PKCδ, to stimulate mTORC1-independent signaling pathways. Activated PKCα and PKCδ inhibit GSK3β, a serine/threonine kinase that can induce TFEB/TFE3 phosphorylation required for 14-3-3 protein-mediated cytoplasmic sequestration, thus dephosphorylating TFEB/TFE3 and inducing its nuclear translocation (Li et al., 2016). The other isoform, PKCβ, stabilizes and increases TFEB activity by directly phosphorylating it on three serine residues at the C-terminus (Ferron et al., 2013). In addition to kinases-mediated regulation of TFEB/TFE3 activities, calcineurin, a serine/threonine phosphatase, also regulates the activities of TFEB/TFE3. Under starvation conditions, lysosomal calcium release through mucolipin 1 (MCOLN1) activates calcineurin to promote dephosphorylation and translocation to the nucleus (Medina et al., 2015). Additionally, calcium efflux from other major calcium storage organelles, such as mitochondria and endoplasmic reticulum, is likely to regulate calcineurin activity and TFEB/TFE3 dephosphorylation. Overall, metabolic signaling modulates the phosphorylation state of TFEB/TFE3 through kinase activity regulation such as mTORC1, ERK2, and PKC, and phosphatases such as calcineurin, and their association with 14-3-3 proteins retain them in the cytoplasm. Since the significance of lysosomes as a signaling hub linking nutrient sensing to stemness, cell cycle progression, differentiation, quiescence, and senescence of various types of stem cells has been identified, it is crucial to understand the metabolic stress-associated signaling for licensing stem cell fate by regulating lysosomal function via TFEB/TFE3 transcriptional activity (Fig. 2).

ROLE OF TFEB/TFE3-MEDIATED TRANSCRIPTIONAL ACTIVITY IN STEM CELL FATE DETERMINATION

Pluripotent stem cells

In stem cell biology, the capacity of cells to differentiate into numerous cell types that make up the body is termed pluripotency. This state of pluripotency lasts for 4-5 days in mice or 2 weeks in human embryos and then diminishes following the implantation process, with the polarization of epiblasts and transitions from the naïve state to the primed state of embryonic stem cells (ESCs) (Rossant and Tam, 2022). During mammalian embryo development, pluripotent stem cells (PSCs) transition between pre- and postimplantation stages, with recent research revealing an intermediate “formative state” between naïve and primed pluripotency in mouse and human PSCs (Pera and Rossant, 2021). PSCs undergo changes in their gene expression and developmental potential patterns, which involve the rapid rewiring of signaling pathways, transcription factors, and epigenetic regulators. These changes coordinate ESC fate responses to external signals, potentially offering reversibility and flexibility to developmental transitions (Furlan et al., 2023; Koh et al., 2022). Additionally, blastocyst development and ESC differentiation are associated with transcriptional activity, metabolic pathways changes, and epigenetic alterations; however, the relevance of the degree of metabolic activity of lysosomes remains unclear (Zhang et al., 2018b).

Stem cell fate determination depends on maintaining a delicate balance between signals that promote self-renewal and signals that promote differentiation, so much research has been conducted at the level of transcriptional regulation. Large-scale siRNA screening has shown that knockdown of Folliculin (Flcn) and Tsc2 inhibits the commitment of ESC and identified them as factors involved in response to changes of metabolic status by regulating mTOR activity. Flcn interacts with Fnip1 and Fnip2, which are involved in the intranuclear translocation of TFE3 (Betschinger et al., 2013). These findings indicate that the depletion of TFE3 from the nuclear compartment could potentially serve as a regulatory mechanism that allows the disruption of pluripotency networks responsible during embryonic development and the maintenance of ESC pluripotency. Villegas et al. (2019) analyzed genes that control ESC pluripotency using a genome-wide functional CRISPR/Cas9 screen. They found that the inactivation of TFE3 in the cytoplasm is observed during ESC differentiation and confirmed the involvement of Folliculin (FLCN)-associated RagC/D activity (Villegas et al., 2019). During ESC differentiation, the Rag heterodimer induces TFE3 phosphorylation, leading to its association with 14-3-3 proteins and sequestration into the cytoplasm, where it inhibits TFE3-induced transcriptional activity and promotes lineage-specific transcription (Villegas et al., 2019). These findings demonstrate that metabolic signaling triggered in the lysosome acts as a direct regulator of cell fate. Studies of amino acid availability in lysosomes have been conducted, providing new and extensive information on the molecular mechanisms that translate into the regulation of gene transcription, which ultimately governs the differentiation process from ESCs. The AMPK pathway plays a crucial role in controlling ESC cell fate, and ESCs lacking AMPK exhibit defects in endodermal differentiation and germ layer specification with a decrease in lysosomal function and inactivation of TFEB (Young et al., 2016). Recently, the role of TFEB in sustaining ESC pluripotency has been elucidated. Furthermore, Tan et al. (2021) have shown that TFEB in undifferentiated ESCs binds to the Nanog promoter and stimulates a heterodimer with SRY-Box transcription factor 2 (Sox2) regardless of autophagy-lysosomal functional activation. In particular, although TFEB knockout maintained pluripotency through the compensatory action of TFE3, double knockout of TFEB and TFE3 caused impaired ESC pluripotency (Gollwitzer et al., 2022). Another typical member of the class of bHLH leucine zipper transcription factors, c-Myc, is an induced pluripotent stem cell (iPSC) reprogramming factor. Moreover, it binds to E-boxes near the core promoter elements of target genes (Halazonetis and Kandil, 1991). The antagonistic relationship between c-MYC and TFEB/TFE3 in the transcriptional regulation of lysosomal and autophagy genes has been investigated to maintain the pluripotency of iPSCs (Annunziata et al., 2019). The increase in c-Myc protein levels, deemed controversial, was accompanied by a distinct decrease in TFEB levels, which explains the decreased lysosomal expression of proteins in iPSCs compared to parental fibroblasts. However, these results suggest that the function of c-Myc in regulating the pluripotency of PSCs remains controversial.

Although Tfe3 depletion facilitated the commitment of ESCs in vitro, Tfe3 mutant mice showed no distinct developmental phenotype, likely because different members of the MiTF/TFE family can functionally complement each other (Steingrímsson et al., 2002). However, regulating TFE3 activity may modulate PSC fate determination in vivo. Infusion of ESCs expressing Tfe3 fused with an estrogen receptor for tamoxifen-induced nuclear translocation maintained embryonic identity and development potential in vivo (Betschinger et al., 2013). Furthermore, pharmacological induction of TFE3 nuclear translocation converted PSCs from primed to naïve pluripotency and allowed the robust formation of mouse-human chimeric embryos when implanted into mouse blastocysts (Hu et al., 2020b). Thus, TFEB and TFE3 transcriptional activity in the nucleus in maintaining ESC pluripotency, in addition to inducing effects on lysosomal autophagy and catabolism, inhibits the expression of genes involved in differentiation while inducing transcriptional activity involved in the maintenance of pluripotency. In situations where lineage-specific differentiation is induced after implantation in the endometrium, TFEB/TFE3 can be inactivated in the cytoplasm to induce the expression of differentiation-related genes.

Hematopoietic stem cell

PSCs, including ESCs and iPSCs, are undifferentiated cells that can self-renew and potentially differentiate into all hematopoietic lineages, including hematopoietic stem cells (HSCs), hematopoietic progenitor cells (HPCs), and mature hematopoietic cells, in response to microenvironment signals, such as metabolites, growth factors, hormones, and cytokines (Thomson et al., 1998). The precise characterization of the role of lysosomes in maintaining the stemness and regenerative potential of adult stem cells and progenitor cells is well-elucidated compared to ESCs. The interplay between lysosomal activity and HSC activation is intricate and may vary depending on the specific circumstances. Ongoing research is being conducted in this field, with researchers persistently exploring the processes that regulate the quiescence, activation, and differentiation of HSCs. Moreover, recent studies have demonstrated that HSCs undergo metabolic changes and nutrient uptake during the transition from quiescent to activated HSCs and subsequent differentiation into HPCs, which are accompanied by internalization of nutrients through endocytosis and degradation in lysosomes (Ito et al., 2019; Palm and Thompson, 2017). TFEB expression was upregulated, and nuclear localization of the TFEB protein was dominant in quiescent HSCs compared to activated HSCs (Garcia-Prat et al., 2021). Quiescent HSCs contain larger lysosomes than activated HSCs; TFEB activity in the nucleus is a key factor for maintaining HSC quiescence (Liang et al., 2020). In the transcription regulatory network, c-Myc, expressed at low levels in quiescent HSCs, regulates the interaction between HSCs and their niche to govern the equilibrium existing between self-renewal and differentiation of HSCs (Sheng et al., 2021; Wilson et al., 2004). As a result, c-Myc and TFEB were found to be oppositely regulated during HSC activation, and the mass of lysosomes in activated HSCs was found to increase by LAMP1 staining, but the activity and turnover of lysosomes were found to be reduced (Garcia-Prat et al., 2021; Mutvei et al., 2020). Metabolically, to maintain the quiescence and stemness, hypoxia-inducible factor-1 alpha (HIF1α)-dependent glycolysis (anaerobic) is activated to reduce oxidative stress, but as HSCs differentiate into mature hematopoietic cells, mitochondrial oxidative phosphorylation (OXPHOS) metabolism (aerobic) is activated (Mattes et al., 2019). Despite the low OXPHOS activity and ROS generation in quiescent HSCs, mitochondrial mass is elevated (de Almeida et al., 2017; Takihara et al., 2019). Therefore, quiescent HSCs are characterized by several mitochondria with relatively low mitochondrial membrane potential (ΔΨm), the maintenance of which involves mitochondrial biogenesis and control of mitochondrial quality control through the clearance of damaged mitochondria. However, failure to maintain lysosomal activity in HSCs inhibits normal clearance of damaged mitochondria and leads to loss of HSC quiescence by PINK/Parkin-mediated mitophagy dysfunction (Ito et al., 2016; Murakami et al., 2021). The clearance of damaged mitochondria due to elevated mitochondrial activity requires asymmetric division of the autophagosome and lysosomal degradation machinery. Thus, maintaining lysosome number and function in HSCs may contribute to normal HSC daughter cell differentiation and fate determination (Loeffler et al., 2019). Essentially, lysosomal biogenesis is enhanced to compensate for reduced energy production owing to low mitochondrial activity, and coordinated lysosomal degradative activity is associated with the maintenance of HSC quiescence and stemness (Ito et al., 2016). As the crosstalk of organelles such as mitochondria and lysosomes, as well as how they maintain cellular functions, has become important factors that modify the transcriptional and epigenetic processes of HSCs, further research is required to understand the hematopoietic process and identify novel lysosome function regulatory targets to develop therapeutic strategies for hematopoietic disorders.

Stem cell senescence

Although cell senescence drives the developmental process and repair of damaged tissues, in aged organisms, it is a permanent state of cell cycle arrest that can contribute to the triggering of inflammatory responses and tumor formation, along with the accumulation of senescent stem cells involved in the regeneration of tissues (Hernandez-Segura et al., 2018). This accumulation of senescent stem cells is recognized as a hallmark of the aging process of tissues and organisms (López-Otín et al., 2013). TFEB-mediated lysosomal biogenesis prevents DNA damage, regulates epigenetic modification, maintains proteostasis through autophagy, and is involved in mitochondrial quality control, making it a key regulator of stem cell senescence and for enhancing regenerative capacity (Hu et al., 2020a; Wang et al., 2021). As TFEB/TFE3 functions as a critical regulator of lysosomal biogenesis, several regulatory mechanisms are involved in preserving optimal stem cell function. The most well-established candidates for mitigating senescence are mTORC1 inhibitors that promote the nuclear translocation of TFEB (Medina et al., 2015). In addition to mTORC1-dependent TFEB nuclear translocation, curcumin analogs induce TFEB activity through mTOR-independent signaling pathways, which have been studied to protect against aging and used as targets for the development of novel antisenescence or senolytic therapeutics by modulating TFEB-dependent lysosome biogenesis and cargo loading into the lysosomes (Song et al., 2016; Zhang et al., 2021). Additionally, researchers have revealed that chemical candidates that induce acidification of the lysosomes can prevent stem cell senescence by maintaining lysosomal integrity through the upregulation of LAMP1/2 expression and facilitating v-ATPase activity (Wang et al., 2018). Notably, Curnock et al. (2023) have demonstrated that the increase in lysosome biogenesis during oncogene-, replicative-, and irradiation-induced senescence is mediated by nuclear TFEB/TFE3 and that the accumulation of abnormal lysosomes correlates with the induction of the senescence phenotype. Thus, these findings suggest that while TFEB-dependent lysosomal biogenesis is important for lysosomal replenishment while maintaining lysosomal acidification, minimizing permeabilization of the lysosomal membrane and maintaining proteolytic activity is important for preventing cellular senescence and age-related disease. TFEB can be utilized as a target for developing antisenescence and senolytic agents.

CONCLUSION

Under metabolic stress, the underlying mechanisms involved in controlling lysosomal gene expression via transcriptional activation of TFEB/TFE3 are regulated by several factors, including phosphorylation, subcellular location, and protein interactions. Metabolic stress is crucial in regulating TFEB/TFE3-mediated lysosomal biogenesis and inter organelle communication by facilitating the production of several kinases, including mTORC1, ERK2, PKC, and calcineurin. Transcription factors affect both lysosomal function and stem cell fate determination (Fig. 3). The nuclear translocation of TFEB/TFE3 into PSCs preserves pluripotency by repressing genes associated with differentiation into progenitor cells. Conversely, these transcription factors can be confined to the cytoplasm under specific differentiation signals to activate genes relevant to differentiation. The interaction between cellular components, specifically organelles such as lysosomes and mitochondria, significantly impacts the regulation of transcriptional and epigenetic mechanisms crucial for maintaining the quiescence of HSCs and facilitating the replenishment of multiple blood cell lineages. Consequently, further investigation is required to enhance our understanding of hematopoiesis and identify potential therapeutic targets for hematopoietic disorders. Investigation of the transcriptional activity of TFEB/TFE3 in lysosomal biogenesis highlights its importance in the prevention of cellular senescence, hence offering potential avenues for the development of therapies aimed at preventing senescence-associated age-related diseases. In summary, as TFEB enhancement ameliorated lysosomal dysfunction in the pathogenesis of metabolic disorders (Chae et al., 2023), the manipulation of lysosomal gene expression by TFEB/TFE3, which is affected by metabolic stress, has significant implications for the determination of stem cell fate, maintenance of cellular function, and development of potential therapeutic strategies.
