Unbounded-width CSPs are Untestable in a Sublinear Number of Queries
Yumou Fei

Abstract
The bounded-degree query model, introduced by Goldreich and Ron ( Algorithmica, 2002 ), is a standard framework in graph property testing and sublinear-time algorithms. Many properties studied in this model, such as bipartiteness and 3-colorability of graphs, can be expressed as satisfiability of constraint satisfaction problems (CSPs). We prove that for the entire class of unbounded-width CSPs, testing satisfiability requires Ω ⁡ ( n ) \Omega(n) queries in the bounded-degree model. This result unifies and generalizes several previous lower bounds. In particular, it applies to all CSPs that are known to be 𝐍𝐏 \mathbf{NP} -hard to solve, including k k -colorability of ℓ \ell -uniform hypergraphs for any k , ℓ ⩾ 2 k,\ell\geqslant 2 with ( k , ℓ ) ≠ ( 2 , 2 ) (k,\ell)\neq(2,2) . Our proof combines the techniques from Bogdanov, Obata, and Trevisan ( FOCS, 2002 ), who established the first Ω ⁡ ( n ) \Omega(n) query lower bound for CSP testing in the bounded-degree model, with known results from universal algebra.

Introduction

Property testing is a class of algorithmic problems in which the algorithm is allowed to inspect only a small portion of the input. In exchange for this limited access, the goal is relaxed: instead of deciding whether the input exactly satisfies a given property, the tester must merely distinguish inputs that satisfy the property from those that are
far
from satisfying it, under an appropriate notion of distance.

In this paper, we consider the problem of testing satisfiability of constraint satisfaction problems (CSPs). We formally define CSPs as follows. First of all, every CSP considered in this paper is specified by a finite

We study the property testing of CSP satisfiability in the

1.2
Discussion: Testing vs. Approximation

A property tester can be viewed as an algorithm that approximates the distance of an input to satisfying a property — albeit in a very coarse sense, as it only distinguishes zero distance from sufficiently large distance. Nevertheless, many property testers (for example, the constant-query tester of
[
Yos11
]
for certain CSPs) operate by explicitly estimating this distance and checking whether it is close to zero. On the negative side, a hardness result for property testing (such as our
Theorem

1.6
) can often be interpreted as a hardness of approximation result. This connection to approximation algorithms was, in fact, one of the original motivations in
[
GGR98
]
, where graph property testing was first introduced.

In this subsection, we examine and discuss about our main result
Theorem

1.6
from the perspective of sublinear-time approximation algorithms.

1.2.2
MaxCSP vs. Satisfiability Testing

Whether property testing of CSP satisfiability has implications for MaxCSP problems depends on the notion of “distance to satisfiability” used by the property tester. In the
d
-bounded-degree model, having distance
\varepsilon
to satisfiability means that at least
\varepsilon dn
constraints must be removed to make the instance satisfiable. In contrast, an instance with value
1-\varepsilon
in the MaxCSP sense requires removing at least an
\varepsilon

fraction
of its constraints. Thus, testing CSP satisfiability in the bounded-degree model is analogous to solving
\mathsf{MaxCSP}(\Gamma)[1,1-\varepsilon]
, provided the CSP instance has
\Theta(n)
constraints.

However, a CSP instance in the
d
-bounded-degree model
\mathrm{BD}(d,n)
may have far fewer than
\Theta(n)
constraints. In fact, if an instance contains only
O(1)
constraints, it would be unreasonable to expect any sublinear-time algorithm to approximate its value, since with high probability the queried variables would not appear in any constraint. To address this, we introduce a modified model
\mathrm{BD}^{*}(d,\alpha,n)
, where
\alpha>0
is a fixed constant ensuring that all CSP instances considered contain at least
\alpha n
constraints.

Under this refinement,
Theorem

1.6
can be equivalently stated as follows:

Theorem 1.8
.

For any CSP template
(D,\Gamma)
of unbounded width, there exist constants
\varepsilon,\alpha\in(0,1)
and
d\in\mathbb{N}
such that any algorithm for
\mathsf{MaxCSP}(\Gamma)[1,1-\varepsilon]
in the
\mathrm{BD}^{*}(d,\alpha,n)
model must make
\Omega(n)
queries.

It is easy to see that
Theorem

1.8
implies
Theorem

1.6
. Due to the convenience provided by this rephrased version, in the rest of the paper we will work with
Theorem

1.8
instead of
Theorem

1.6
.

1.2.3
Sublinear Time vs. Polynomial Time

As discussed in
Section

1.2.2
, because of the specific notion of “distance to satisfiability” used in the bounded-degree model, satisfiability testing in this setting most naturally corresponds to MaxCSP problems on instances with
\Theta(n)
constraints. In contrast, the
dense graph model
(the more extensively studied model in graph property testing) uses another notion of distance, making it better aligned with MaxCSP problems on “dense” instances.

We argue that exactly due to this difference in instance regimes, the bounded-degree model provides a stronger connection to the study of polynomial-time algorithms for MaxCSPs, at least in the respect of worst-case approximation ratios. First, MaxCSP problems on general instances can often be reduced to bounded-degree instances (see, e.g.,
[
Tre01
]
), so bounded-degree instances already capture much of the hardness in the problems. Second, MaxCSP problems tend to be easier on dense instances (see, e.g.,
[
AKK95
]
). Indeed,
[
AS03
]
shows that every CSP admits a constant-query satisfiability tester in the dense graph model. In contrast, in the bounded-degree model, satisfiability testing already exhibits a wide spectrum of complexities across CSP templates, with query complexity ranging from constant, to
\widetilde{\Theta}(\sqrt{n})
, up to
\Theta(n)
.

We next discuss some results about MaxCSP in the polynomial-time setting that are most relevant to this work.

In the context of polynomial-time algorithms, for certain CSP templates
(D,\Gamma)
, the
perfect-completeness
problem
\mathsf{MaxCSP}(\Gamma)[1,1-\varepsilon]
can exhibit a markedly different level of complexity from the (potentially harder)
imperfect-completeness
problem
\mathsf{MaxCSP}(\Gamma)[1-\varepsilon^{\prime},1-\varepsilon]
, even when
\varepsilon^{\prime}\to 0
and
\varepsilon
is fixed.
For example, in the case of
\mathsf{3LIN}
(an unbounded-width CSP), the former problem admits a polynomial-time algorithm via Gaussian elimination, whereas the latter is
\mathbf{NP}
-hard (due to
[
Hås01
]
).
More generally, Dalmau and Krokhin
[
DK13
]
proved that the imperfect-completeness problem is
\mathbf{NP}
-hard for all unbounded-width CSP templates.

Theorem 1.9

(
[
DK13
]
)
.

For any CSP template
(D,\Gamma)
of unbounded width, there exists a constant
\varepsilon\in(0,1)
such that for every constant
\varepsilon^{\prime}\in(0,\varepsilon)
, the problem
\mathsf{MaxCSP}(\Gamma)[1-\varepsilon^{\prime},1-\varepsilon]
is
\mathbf{NP}
-hard.

In terms of conclusions, our result is not directly comparable with
Theorem

1.9
, for multiple reasons. First,
Theorem

1.9
is a hardness result for the imperfect completeness problem, while our
Theorem

1.8
shows hardness for the (potentially easier) perfect completeness problem. Second,
Theorem

1.9
is proved against polynomial-time algorithms, while
Theorem

1.8
is against sublinear-query algorithms. Although
\mathbf{NP}
-hardness is likely much stronger than a linear query lower bound, the latter does have the benefit of being unconditional. It is also not clear a priori whether some useful algorithms could have sublinear query complexity but super-polynomial time complexity, so
\mathbf{NP}
-hardness usually does not immediately imply query lower bounds in sublinear-time models, even when assuming
\mathbf{P}\neq\mathbf{NP}

In terms of techniques, however, the proof of

runs in direct parallel to that of

in

Preliminaries

As mentioned in
Section

1.2.3
, the proof of
Theorem

1.8
is by constructing a reduction from “linear equation” instances. As is the case in
[
BOT02
]
, the reduction is centered around the fact that the linear equations can be “simulated” by some constant-size gadgets built from constraints in the target template
(D,\Gamma)
. The following general definition captures such gadget constructions.

Definition 2.1
.

Given a relational structure
(D,\Gamma)
and a relation
R:D^{k}\rightarrow\{0,1\}
, we say that
R
is
generated
by
\Gamma
if there exists an instance
I=(V,\mathcal{C})\in\mathrm{CSP}(\Gamma)
such that the following holds:

(1)

The variable set
V
is
\{v_{1},\dots,v_{k},u_{1},\dots,u_{\ell}\}
for some integer
\ell\geqslant 0
.

(2)

For any tuple
(x_{1},\dots,x_{k})\in D^{k}
, we have
R(x_{1},\dots,x_{k})=1
if and only if there exists a satisfying assignment
\tau:V\rightarrow D
such that
\tau(v_{i})=x_{i}
for each
i\in[k]
.

The (possibly infinite) collection of all relations generated by
\Gamma
is denoted by
\langle\Gamma\rangle^{*}
.

Remark 2.2
.

In the setting of
Definition

2.1
, we will say that the instance
I
“simulates” the relation
R
on the variables
v_{1},\dots,v_{k}
. These variables will sometimes be referred to as “primary variables” later in this paper, while the other variables
u_{1},\dots,u_{\ell}
in
I
will be called “secondary variables.”

Over the past decades, the development of the powerful framework of universal algebra has enabled a systematic study of the power and limitations of gadget constructions for CSPs. In contrast to the case-by-case constructions used in earlier works such as
[
BOT02
,
YI10
,
ACC25
]
, we draw on established results in universal algebra to fully exploit the strength of gadget constructions. The universal-algebraic result (
Theorem

3.2
) used in the proof of
Theorem

1.8
is highly nontrivial (though by now perhaps standard) and serves as the main workhorse that allows us to unify the existing
\Omega(n)
query lower bounds. The remainder of this section is devoted to introducing the necessary background in universal algebra before formally stating the lemma in the next section.

2.1
Relational Structures

We begin by introducing some basic terminology for studying relational structures, as defined in
Definition

1.1
, with an emphasis on maps between different relational structures.

Definition 2.3
.

Let
(D_{1},\Gamma_{1})
and
(D_{2},\Gamma_{2})
be relational structures.
Suppose there exists a bijection between
\Gamma_{1}
and
\Gamma_{2}
such that paired relations have the same arity.
A map
\varphi:D_{1}\to D_{2}
is called a
relational homomorphism
if for every
R_{1}\in\Gamma_{1}
and
R_{2}\in\Gamma_{2}
paired under this bijection with arity
k
, and for all
x_{1},\dots,x_{k}\in D_{1}
,

R_{1}(x_{1},\dots,x_{k})=1\quad\implies\quad R_{2}\big(\varphi(x_{1}),\dots,\varphi(x_{k})\big)=1.

Throughout the rest of the paper, we consider maps between relational structures only when the relations of the source and target structures are bijectively matched, and corresponding relations have the same arity. Such pairs of structures are said to share the same
signature
.

Definition 2.4
.

Let
(D,\Gamma)
be a relational structure. A map
\varphi:D\rightarrow D
is called a
relational endomorphism
if it is a relational homomorphism from
(D,\Gamma)
to itself (under the obvious identification of relations). A relational endomorphism is called a
relational automorphism
if it is bijective and its inverse is also a relational endomorphism.
8
8

8

The second condition is actually unnecessary because we only deal with finite structures. In the finite setting, the inverse of a bijective relational endomorphism is automatically an endomorphism.
A relational structure is called a
core
if all of its relational endomorphisms are relational automorphisms.

The next task is to show that every relational structure can be “reduced” to a core.

Definition 2.5
.

Two relational structures
(D_{1},\Gamma_{1})
and
(D_{2},\Gamma_{2})
are said to be
homomorphically equivalent
if there exist two relational homomorphisms
\varphi_{1}:D_{1}\rightarrow D_{2}
and
\varphi_{2}:D_{2}\rightarrow D_{1}
(under the same identification between
\Gamma_{1}
and
\Gamma_{2}
).

Proposition 2.6
.

Every relational structure
(D,\Gamma)
is homomorphically equivalent to a core.

Proof.

Let
D_{1}\subseteq D
be a smallest subset for which there exists a relational homomorphism
\varphi
from
(D,\Gamma)
to
(D_{1},\Gamma_{1})
, where
\Gamma_{1}
is obtained by restricting the relations in
\Gamma
to
D_{1}
. Since the natural embedding
\iota:D_{1}\hookrightarrow D
is itself a relational homomorphism, we conclude that
(D,\Gamma)
and
(D_{1},\Gamma_{1})
are homomorphically equivalent.

We claim that
(D_{1},\Gamma_{1})
is a core. Otherwise, there exists a relational endomorphism
\varphi_{1}
on
(D_{1},\Gamma_{1})
that is not bijective. In particular, its image is a proper subset
D_{2}\subsetneq D_{1}
. But then the composition
\varphi_{1}\circ\varphi
defines a homomorphism from
D
to
D_{2}
, contradicting the minimality of
D_{1}
. Hence
(D_{1},\Gamma_{1})
must be a core.
∎

2.2
Algebraic Structures

Universal algebra enters the picture through the notion of
algebraic structures
.

Definition 2.7
.

An
algebraic structure
is a pair
(D,\mathcal{F})
where
D
is a finite set and
\mathcal{F}
is a (possibly infinite) collection of operations on
D
. Each operation
f\in\mathcal{F}
is a function
f:D^{k}\to D
for some positive integer
k
(the
arity
of
f
).

Although at first glance algebraic structures may seem to run parallel to relational structures, the two are in fact deeply interconnected through the following key definitions.

Definition 2.8
.

Let
f:D^{k}\to D
be an operation, and let
R:D^{\ell}\to\{0,1\}
be a relation on
D
. We say that
f

preserves

R
if for every matrix
(x_{i,j})\in D^{\ell\times k}
,

R(x_{1,j},\dots,x_{\ell,j})=1\text{ for all }j\in[k]

implies

R\big(f(x_{1,1},\dots,x_{1,k}),\dots,f(x_{\ell,1},\dots,x_{\ell,k})\big)=1.

Definition 2.9
.

An operation
f
is a
polymorphism
of a relational structure
(D,\Gamma)
if it preserves every relation
R\in\Gamma
. The (infinite) set of all polymorphisms of
(D,\Gamma)
is denoted by
\mathrm{Pol}(\Gamma)
.

Note that a unary polymorphism is exactly a relational endomorphism, as defined in
Definition

2.3
.

The following definition gives algebraic structures their own homomorphism maps. It is important not to confuse algebraic homomorphisms with relational homomorphisms.

Definition 2.10
.

Let
(D_{1},\mathcal{F}_{1})
and
(D_{2},\mathcal{F}_{2})
be algebraic structures.
Suppose there exists a bijection between
\mathcal{F}_{1}
and
\mathcal{F}_{2}
such that paired functions have the same arity.
A map
\varphi:D_{1}\to D_{2}
is called an
algebraic homomorphism
if for every
f_{1}\in\mathcal{F}_{1}
and
f_{2}\in\mathcal{F}_{2}
paired under this bijection with arity
k
, and for all
x_{1},\dots,x_{k}\in D_{1}
,

\varphi\big(f_{1}(x_{1},\dots,x_{k})\big)=f_{2}\big(\varphi(x_{1}),\dots,\varphi(x_{k})\big).

Definition 2.11
.

Let
(D_{1},\mathcal{F}_{1})
and
(D_{2},\mathcal{F}_{2})
be algebraic structures.

(1)

We say that
(D_{1},\mathcal{F}_{1})
is a subalgebra of
(D_{2},\mathcal{F}_{2})
if there exists an injective algebraic homomorphism
\varphi:D_{1}\rightarrow D_{2}
.

(2)

We say that
(D_{2},\mathcal{F}_{2})
is a homomorphic image of
(D_{1},\mathcal{F}_{1})
if there exists a surjective algebraic homomorphism
\varphi:D_{1}\rightarrow D_{2}
.

It turns out that a special class of algebraic structures, namely the
idempotent
ones, provides significant convenience in various contexts. Since idempotent structures play an important role in formulating the main universal-algebraic lemma (
Lemma

3.4
), we give the definition below.

Definition 2.12
.

An operation
f:D^{k}\rightarrow D
is
idempotent
if
f(x,x,\dots,x)=x
holds for all
x\in D
. We call algebraic structure
(D,\mathcal{F})
idempotent if every operation
f\in\mathcal{F}
is idempotent.

2.3
Irredundant Relations and Galois Duality

Given a relational structure
(D,\Gamma)
, let
\langle\Gamma\rangle
denote the (possibly infinite) set of relations that are preserved by
\mathrm{Pol}(\Gamma)
. It is not hard to see that every relation generated by
\Gamma
in the sense of
Definition

2.1
is preserved by
\mathrm{Pol}(\Gamma)
.
9
9

9

This observation is not used elsewhere in this paper.
Using the notation of
Definition

2.1
, this implies
\langle\Gamma\rangle^{*}\subseteq\langle\Gamma\rangle
.

A fundamental result of Geiger
[
Gei68
]
shows that if the notion of “relations generated by
\Gamma
” in
Definition

2.1
is suitably relaxed, then the resulting set
\langle\Gamma\rangle^{*}
actually coincides with
\langle\Gamma\rangle
. This result captures a deep “duality” between algebraic and relational structures (an instance of the more general concept of a
Galois connection
), which we do not elaborate on here. Instead, following Dalmau and Krokhin
[
DK13
]
, we state a variant of Geiger’s theorem asserting that any
irredundant
relation in
\langle\Gamma\rangle
is contained in
\langle\Gamma\rangle^{*}
(without relaxing
Definition

2.1
).

Definition 2.13
.

Let
R:D^{k}\rightarrow\{0,1\}
be a relation on a finite set
D
. We say that
R
is
irredundant
if for any distinct indices
i,j\in[k]
, there exists a tuple
(x_{1},\dots,x_{k})\in D^{k}
such that
R(x_{1},\dots,x_{k})=1
and
x_{i}\neq x_{j}
.

Theorem 2.14

(
[
Gei68
]
)
.

Let
(D,\Gamma)
be a relational structure and let
R:D^{k}\rightarrow\{0,1\}
be an irredundant relation on
D
. If every polymorphism of
(D,\Gamma)
preserves
R
, then
R\in\langle\Gamma\rangle^{*}
.

For the sake of completeness, a proof of
Theorem

2.14
is given in
Appendix

B
.

As an application of
Theorem

2.14
, we next define an “Endomorphism” relation for any given relational structure
(D,\Gamma)
and prove that it belongs to
\langle\Gamma\rangle^{*}
.

Definition 2.15
.

For any relational structure
(D,\Gamma)
, we define a relation
End_{\Gamma}:D^{D}\rightarrow\{0,1\}
as follows. For any tuple
y=(y_{x})_{x\in D}\in D^{D}
, we let
End_{\Gamma}(y)=1
if and only if the map
x\mapsto y_{x}
is a relational endomorphism on
(D,\Gamma)
.

Proposition 2.16
.

For any relational structure
(D,\Gamma)
, we have
End_{\Gamma}\in\langle\Gamma\rangle^{*}
.

Proof.

It is easy to see that
End_{\Gamma}
is irredundant, since the tuple
y\in D^{D}
defined by
y_{x}=x
for all
x\in D
satisfies the relation
End_{\Gamma}
. It suffices to show that
End_{\Gamma}
is preserved by all polymorphisms of
\Gamma
. Once we have that,
Theorem

2.14
implies that
End_{\Gamma}\in\langle\Gamma\rangle^{*}
.

Suppose
f\in\mathrm{Pol}(\Gamma)
is a
k
-ary polymorphism. For any
k
tuples
y^{(1)},\dots,y^{(k)}\in D^{D}
satisfying
End_{\Gamma}
, there are endomorphisms
\varphi_{1},\dots,\varphi_{k}
of
(D,\Gamma)
such that
\varphi_{i}(x)=y^{(i)}_{x}
for all
i\in[k]
and
x\in D
. It now suffices to verify that the tuple

\Big(f\big(\varphi_{1}(x),\dots,\varphi_{k}(x)\big)\Big)_{x\in D}\in D^{D}

satisfies
End_{\Gamma}
, or in other words, the map
x\mapsto f\big(\varphi_{1}(x),\dots,\varphi_{k}(x)\big)
is an relational endomorphism of
(D,\Gamma)
. But this is a special case of the general observation that for any relational structure, the collection of its polymorphisms is closed under composition.
10
10

10

In general, a
k
-ary polymorphism
f
composed with an
m
-ary polymorphism
g
yields a
km
-ary polymorphism
f\circ g
defined by
f\circ g(x_{1,1},\dots,x_{1,m},\dots,x_{k,1},\dots,x_{k,m})=f(g(x_{1,1},\dots,x_{1,m}),\dots,g(x_{k,1},\dots,x_{k,m}))

∎

3
Main Lemma from Universal Algebra

In this section, we state the main universal-algebraic result used in the proof of
Theorem

1.8
. Informally, the result asserts that any CSP template of unbounded width can simulate relations defined by linear equations over abelian groups, in the sense of
Definition

2.1
. The formal statement, however, is much more nuanced.

We begin by defining
\textsc{3Sum}_{\mathsf{G}}
, the set of relations that specify the sum of exactly three variables. Throughout the rest of the paper, Abelian groups denoted by
\mathsf{G}
are assumed to be finite and have at least two elements.

Definition 3.1
.

Let
\mathsf{G}
be an Abelian group. For any element
b\in\mathsf{G}
, we let
S^{\mathsf{G}}_{b}:\mathsf{G}^{3}\rightarrow\{0,1\}
be the relation defined by

S^{\mathsf{G}}_{b}(x_{1},x_{2},x_{3})=1\quad\text{if and only if}\quad x_{1}+x_{2}+x_{3}=b.

We use
\textsc{3Sum}_{\mathsf{G}}
to denote the relation set
\left\{S_{b}^{\mathsf{G}}\,\middle|\,b\in\mathsf{G}\right\}
.

We are now ready to state the following main result from universal algebra.

Theorem 3.2

(
[
BK14
]
)
.

Let
(D,\Gamma)
be a relational structure of unbounded width.
Assume further that
(D,\Gamma)
is a core and that all operations in
\mathrm{Pol}(\Gamma)
are idempotent.
Then there exists an Abelian group
\mathsf{G}
and a family of operations
\mathcal{F}\subseteq\mathrm{Pol}(\textsc{3Sum}_{\mathsf{G}})
such that
(\mathsf{G},\mathcal{F})
is a homomorphic image of a subalgebra of the polymorphism algebra
(D,\mathrm{Pol}(\Gamma))
.

The statement of
Theorem

3.2
is primarily derived from
[
BK14
, Conjecture 4.3]
, with additional ingredients drawn from other works, such as
[
Val09
]
. Further details concerning
Theorem

3.2
will be provided in
Appendix

D
.

The remaining goal of this section is to translate
Theorem

3.2
into the more explicit and directly applicable statement of
Lemma

3.4
. One aspect of
Theorem

3.2
that requires “translation” is the idempotency condition on
\mathrm{Pol}(\Gamma)
. Given a relational structure, its polymorphism algebra can be made idempotent by augmenting the relation set with all constant relations, defined as follows.

Definition 3.3
.

Let
D
be a finite set.
We denote by
\textsc{Const}_{D}
the set of all unary constant relations on
D
; that is, relations
R
of the form

R(x)=1\quad\text{if and only if}\quad x=b,

for some
b\in D
.

We are now ready to state and prove the following translated version of
Theorem

3.2
.

Lemma 3.4
.

Let
(D,\Gamma)
be a relational structure of unbounded width.
Then there exist a subset
D^{\prime}\subseteq D
, an Abelian group
\mathsf{G}
, and a surjective map
\varphi\colon D^{\prime}\to\mathsf{G}
such that any ternary relation
R\colon D^{3}\to\{0,1\}
of the form

R(x_{1},x_{2},x_{3})=1\quad\text{if and only if}\quad x_{1},x_{2},x_{3}\in D^{\prime}\text{ and }R^{\prime}(\varphi(x_{1}),\varphi(x_{2}),\varphi(x_{3}))=1,

(3.1)

for some
R^{\prime}\in\textsc{3Sum}_{\mathsf{G}}
,
belongs to the collection
\langle\Gamma\cup\textsc{Const}_{D}\rangle^{*}
.

Proof.

We divide the proof into the following steps.

Step 1: construction of
\varphi
.

Because of the presence of constant relations, every relational endomorphism of the structure
(D,\Gamma\cup\textsc{Const}_{D})
is the identity map.
By
Definition

2.4
, this implies that
(D,\Gamma\cup\textsc{Const}_{D})
is a core.
Furthermore, by
Proposition

A.4
,
(D,\Gamma\cup\textsc{Const}_{D})
also has unbounded width.
Since every polymorphism of
(D,\Gamma\cup\textsc{Const}_{D})
preserves the constant relations and is therefore idempotent, we can apply
Theorem

3.2
to obtain a subalgebra
(D_{1},\mathcal{F}_{1})
of
\bigl(D,\mathrm{Pol}(\Gamma\cup\textsc{Const}_{D})\bigr)
and a homomorphic image
(\mathsf{G},\mathcal{F}_{2})
of
(D_{1},\mathcal{F}_{1})
such that
\mathcal{F}_{2}\subseteq\mathrm{Pol}(\textsc{3Sum}_{\mathsf{G}})
, as illustrated in the following diagram.

{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\bigl(D,\mathrm{Pol}(\Gamma\cup\textsc{Const}_{D})\bigr)}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces(D_{1},\mathcal{F}_{1})}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces(\mathsf{G},\mathcal{F}_{2})}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\varphi_{1}}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\varphi_{2}}

Let
\varphi_{1}:D_{1}\rightarrow D
be the injective algebraic homomorphism from
(D_{1},\mathcal{F}_{1})
to
\big(D,\mathrm{Pol}(\Gamma\cup\textsc{Const}_{D})\big)
, and let
\varphi_{2}:D_{1}\rightarrow\mathsf{G}
be the surjective algebraic homomorphism from
(D_{1},\mathcal{F}_{1})
to
(\mathsf{G},\mathcal{F}_{2})
. Let
D^{\prime}=\varphi_{1}(D_{1})\subseteq D
be the image of
\varphi_{1}
. Then we may define a surjective map
\varphi:D^{\prime}\rightarrow\mathsf{G}
by letting
\varphi(x)=\varphi_{2}(\varphi_{1}^{-1}(x))
for any
x\in D^{\prime}
.

Step 2: properties of
\varphi
.

We claim that any polymorphism
f:D^{k}\rightarrow D
of the relational structure
(D,\Gamma\cup\textsc{Const}_{D})
also preserves any relation
R
of the form (
3.1
). Since
\varphi_{1},\varphi_{2}
are algebraic homomorphisms, by
Definition

2.10
we know that some
f_{1}\in\mathcal{F}_{1}
is implicitly paired with
f
and some
f_{2}\in\mathcal{F}_{2}
is in turn paired with
f_{1}
.

Let
(x_{i,j})\in D^{3\times k}
be a matrix of elements such that
R(x_{1,j},x_{2,j},x_{3,j})=1
for all
j\in[k]
. Therefore, by (
3.1
) it must be the case that
x_{\ell,j}\in D^{\prime}
for all
\ell\in[3]
and
j\in[k]
. We let
y_{\ell,j}=\varphi_{1}^{-1}(x_{\ell,j})\in D_{1}
. Now we have

\displaystyle\quad R\bigl(f(x_{1,1},\dots,x_{1,k}),f(x_{2,1},\dots,x_{2,k}),f(x_{3,1},\dots,x_{3,k})\bigr)

\displaystyle=R\Bigl(f\big(\varphi_{1}(y_{1,1}),\dots,\varphi_{1}(y_{1,k})\big),f\big(\varphi_{1}(y_{2,1}),\dots,\varphi_{1}(y_{2,k})\big),f\big(\varphi_{1}(y_{3,1}),\dots,\varphi_{1}(y_{3,k})\big)\Bigr)

\displaystyle=R^{\prime}\Big(\varphi_{2}\circ\varphi_{1}^{-1}\Big(f\big(\varphi_{1}(y_{1,1}),\dots,\varphi_{1}(y_{1,k})\big)\Big),\varphi_{2}\circ\varphi_{1}^{-1}\Big(f\big(\varphi_{1}(y_{2,1}),\dots,\varphi_{1}(y_{2,k})\big)\Big),

\displaystyle\quad\quad\quad\varphi_{2}\circ\varphi_{1}^{-1}\Big(f\big(\varphi_{1}(y_{3,1}),\dots,\varphi_{1}(y_{3,k})\big)\Big)\Big)

\displaystyle=R^{\prime}\Big(\varphi_{2}\big(f_{1}(y_{1,1},\dots,y_{1,k})\big),\varphi_{2}\big(f_{1}(y_{2,1},\dots,y_{2,k})\big),\varphi_{2}\big(f_{1}(y_{3,1},\dots,y_{3,k})\big)\Big)

\displaystyle=R^{\prime}\Big(f_{2}\big(\varphi_{2}(y_{1,1}),\dots,\varphi_{2}(y_{1,k})\big),f_{2}\big(\varphi_{2}(y_{2,1}),\dots,\varphi_{2}(y_{2,k})\big),f_{2}\big(\varphi_{2}(y_{3,1}),\dots,\varphi_{2}(y_{3,k})\big)\Big)

\displaystyle=R^{\prime}\Big(f_{2}\big(\varphi(x_{1,1}),\dots,\varphi(x_{1,k})\big),f_{2}\big(\varphi(x_{2,1}),\dots,\varphi(x_{2,k})\big),f_{2}\big(\varphi(x_{3,1}),\dots,\varphi(x_{3,k})\big)\Big)

(3.2)

Since
\mathcal{F}_{2}\subseteq\mathrm{Pol}(\textsc{3Sum}_{\mathsf{G}})
and
f_{2}\in\mathcal{F}_{2}
, it follows from the assumption
R^{\prime}\in\textsc{3Sum}_{\mathsf{G}}
that
f_{2}
preserves
R^{\prime}
. Combining this with the observation that

R^{\prime}\big(\varphi(x_{1,j}),\varphi(x_{2,j}),\varphi(x_{3,j})\big)=R(x_{1,j},x_{2,j},x_{3,j})=1\quad\text{for all }j\in[k],

we deduce that (
3.2
) evaluates to 1. Therefore, we conclude that
f
preserves
R
.

Step 3: applying Galois duality.

Since every relation
R^{\prime}\in\textsc{3Sum}_{\mathsf{G}}
is irredundant in the sense of
Definition

2.13
, it is easy to see that any relation
R:D^{3}\rightarrow\{0,1\}
defined according to (
3.1
) is also irredundant. Due to
Theorem

2.14
and the claim proved in Step 2 of the proof, it follows that
R\in\langle\Gamma\cup\textsc{Const}_{D}\rangle^{*}
.
∎

Hardness of Linear Equations

As mentioned earlier, we prove
Theorem

1.8
by constructing a reduction from the following hardness result for the “linear equation” template
(\mathsf{G},\textsc{3Sum}_{\mathsf{G}})
.

Theorem 4.1

(
[
BOT02
]
)
.

For any Abelian group
\mathsf{G}
and any
\varepsilon>0
, there exists a constant
d>0
such that
\mathsf{MaxCSP}(\textsc{3Sum}_{\mathsf{G}})[1,\frac{1}{|\mathsf{G}|}+\varepsilon]
requires
\Omega(n)
queries in the
\mathrm{BD}^{*}(d,\frac{d}{3},3n)
model.
11
11

11

The paper
[
BOT02
]
only proved
Theorem

4.1
in the case
\mathsf{G}=\mathbb{Z}/2\mathbb{Z}
, but the proof can easily be adapted to work for general Abelian groups.

However, unlike
[
DK13
]
, which applies the result of
[
Hås01
]
as a black box to prove
Theorem

1.9
, we cannot directly use
Theorem

4.1
due to the subtleties of our bounded-degree query model. In this section, we unpack the proof of
Theorem

4.1
and extract the components that can be explicitly leveraged in proving
Theorem

1.8
. For the sake of convenience, several aspects of the proof in
[
BOT02
]
are slightly modified in the presentation below.

The proof of
Theorem

4.1
proceeds by defining two probability distributions over
\mathrm{CSP}(\textsc{3Sum}_{\mathsf{G}})
instances, referred to as the YES and NO distributions. Instances drawn from the YES distribution typically have value 1, while those from the NO distribution typically have value at most
\frac{1}{|\mathsf{G}|}+\varepsilon
. The goal is to show that any sublinear-query algorithm cannot reliably distinguish between instances sampled from these two distributions.

A combinatorial structure used in both the YES and NO distributions is hypergraph perfect matching, formally defined as follows.

Definition 4.2
.

Let
n
be a positive integer, and let
D
be a finite set of cardinality
k\geqslant 2
. Consider the complete
k
-partite
k
-uniform hypergraph with vertex set
[n]\times D
, partitioned according to the second coordinate.
We write
\mathcal{M}_{D,n}
for the collection of all perfect matchings of this hypergraph (a
perfect matching
is a set of
n
hyperedges, no two of which share a common vertex). In other words, a matching
M\in\mathcal{M}_{D,n}
can be specified by
k
permutations
\pi_{x}:[n]\rightarrow[n]
, for
x\in D
, where
M
is the edge set

M=\left\{\big((\pi_{x}(i),x)\big)_{x\in D}\in\prod_{x\in D}\big([n]\times\{x\}\big)\,\middle|\,i\in[n]\right\}

A perfect matching
M\in\mathcal{M}_{D,n}
serves as a guide for placing constraints on variables. Viewing the vertex set
[n]\times D
as a collection of variables, each hyperedge of
M
corresponds to a tuple of variables on which a constraint can be imposed. In the setting of
Theorem

4.1
, we take
D=[3]
, and on each hyperedge
(v_{1},v_{2},v_{3})
we place a constraint from
\textsc{3Sum}_{\mathsf{G}}
.

The following definition specify the YES and NO distributions used to prove
Theorem

4.1
.

Definition 4.3
.

For any positive integers
n,d
and any Abelian group
\mathsf{G}
, we define
\mathcal{D}_{\mathrm{pair}}^{\mathsf{G},n,d}
to be the distribution over pairs of
\mathrm{CSP}\big(\textsc{3Sum}_{\mathsf{G}},[n]\times[3]\big)
instances obtained by sampling from the following procedure:

1.

Sample an assignment
\tau:[n]\times[3]\rightarrow\mathsf{G}
uniformly at random.

2.

Initialize empty instances
I_{\mathrm{yes}},I_{\mathrm{no}}
. Repeat
d
times:

•

Sample uniformly at random a
3
-partite perfect matching
M\in\mathcal{M}_{[3],n}
.

•

For each hyperedge
(v_{1},v_{2},v_{3})\in M
:

–

Add to
I_{\mathrm{yes}}
the constraint
\big((v_{1},v_{2},v_{3}),S^{\mathsf{G}}_{b}\big)
, where
b=\tau(v_{1})+\tau(v_{2})+\tau(v_{3})
.

–

Sample
b^{\prime}
uniformly from
\mathsf{G}
, and add to
I_{\mathrm{no}}
the constraint
\big((v_{1},v_{2},v_{3}),S^{\mathsf{G}}_{b^{\prime}}\big)
.

3.

Output the pair
(I_{\mathrm{yes}},I_{\mathrm{no}})
.

The marginal distributions of
\mathcal{D}_{\mathrm{pair}}^{\mathsf{G},n,d}
on the first and second components are denoted by
\mathcal{D}_{\mathrm{yes}}^{\mathsf{G},n,d}
and
\mathcal{D}_{\mathrm{no}}^{\mathsf{G},n,d}
, respectively.

By construction, the distributions
\mathcal{D}_{\mathrm{yes}}^{\mathsf{G},n,d}
and
\mathcal{D}_{\mathrm{no}}^{\mathsf{G},n,d}
satisfy the properties stated in the following proposition.

Proposition 4.4
.

In every instance
I
in the support of
\mathcal{D}_{\mathrm{yes}}^{\mathsf{G},n,d}
or
\mathcal{D}_{\mathrm{no}}^{\mathsf{G},n,d}
, each variable
v\in[n]\times[3]
is involved in exactly
d
constraints. Furthermore, every instance
I
in the support of
\mathcal{D}_{\mathrm{yes}}^{\mathsf{G},n,d}
is satisfiable.

The next lemma shows that instances sampled from the NO distribution typically have value at most
\frac{1}{|\mathsf{G}|}+\varepsilon
, as expected.

Lemma 4.5

(
[
BOT02
]
)
.

For any Abelian group
\mathsf{G}
and any
\varepsilon>0
, there exist a constant integer
d\geqslant 1
such that

\underset{I\sim\mathcal{D}_{\mathrm{no}}^{\mathsf{G},n,d}}{\mathbb{P}}\left[\mathrm{val}_{I}\geqslant\frac{1}{|\mathsf{G}|}+\varepsilon\right]=o(1),

where
o(1)
denotes a term tending to
0
as
n\to\infty
.

It remains to show that
\mathcal{D}_{\mathrm{yes}}^{\mathsf{G},n,d}
and
\mathcal{D}_{\mathrm{no}}^{\mathsf{G},n,d}
are indistinguishable to sublinear-query algorithms. The idea is that, although a random instance from the YES distribution is globally very different from a random instance from the NO distribution, they are locally very similar. Specifically, when one inspects only a sublinear-sized portion of the instances, the two distributions are actually almost indistinguishable. The following definition helps formalize this intuition.

Definition 4.6
.

Let
(D,\Gamma)
be a CSP template and let
V
be a set of variables.
We write
\mathrm{CSP}(\Gamma,V)\subseteq\mathrm{CSP}(\Gamma)
for the collection of all
\mathrm{CSP}(\Gamma)
-instances whose variable set is exactly
V
.

\delta=1/(50d^{2})
. By taking a union bound over all
U\subseteq[n]\times[3]
with
|U|\leqslant\delta n
, we have

\mathbb{P}\left[\text{condition \eqref{eq:locally_sparse} fails}\big.\right]\leqslant\sum_{t=1}^{\lfloor\delta n\rfloor}\binom{3n}{t}\left(\frac{5dt^{2}}{n^{2}}\right)^{2t/3}\leqslant\sum_{t=1}^{\lfloor\delta n\rfloor}\left(\frac{t}{n}\right)^{t/3}\leqslant o(1).\qed

As stated in
[
BOT02
]
, the conclusion of
Lemma

C.1
still holds if the constant
2/3
in (
C.1
) is replaced by any constant greater than
1/2
. The reason for choosing
2/3
in (
C.1
) is that it is the largest constant for which the following observation remains true.

Proposition C.2
.

Suppose
M^{(1)},\dots,M^{(d)}
are perfect matchings in
\mathcal{M}_{[3],n}
that satisfy (
C.1
). Then, for any subset
U\subseteq[n]\times[3]
with
|U|\leqslant\delta n
, the following hold:

(1)

The edge sets
M^{(i)}
, for
i\in[d]
, are pairwise disjoint.

(2)

There exists a total order on the union
\bigcup_{i=1}^{d}M^{(i)}
such that every hyperedge in this union is incident to at least one vertex that does not appear in any hyperedge preceding it in the order.

Proof.

The first item holds because no hyperedge can appear in more than one of the
M^{(i)}
’s; otherwise, there would exist a set
U
of three vertices such that
\sum_{i=1}^{d}\left|M^{(i)}[U]\right|\geqslant 2
, violating condition (
C.1
).

We now prove item (2). Let
U_{0}\subseteq U
denote the set of vertices incident to at least one hyperedge in the union
E:=\bigcup_{i=1}^{d}M^{(i)}
. Applying condition (
C.1
) to
U_{0}
implies that a uniformly random vertex from
U_{0}
is, in expectation, incident to fewer than two hyperedges in
E
. Hence, there exists at least one vertex in
U_{0}
that is incident to exactly one hyperedge in
E
.

We remove this hyperedge from
E
and update
U_{0}
to be the set of vertices incident to at least one hyperedge in the remaining set
E
. Repeating this process iteratively, we eventually remove all hyperedges from
E
. This procedure defines a total order on the edge set
\bigcup_{i=1}^{d}M^{(i)}
, where edges removed later are considered smaller in the order. The desired conclusion then follows directly from the construction.
∎

We now use
Lemma

C.1
and
Proposition

C.2
to prove
Lemma

4.7
.

Proof Sketch of
Lemma

4.7
.

Recall from
Definition

4.3
that for any sample
(I_{\mathrm{yes}},I_{\mathrm{no}})\sim\mathcal{D}_{\mathrm{pair}}^{\mathsf{G},n,d}
, the same collection of
d
perfect matchings
M^{(1)},\dots,M^{(d)}\in\mathcal{M}_{[3],n}
underlies both
I_{\mathrm{yes}}
and
I_{\mathrm{no}}
. Applying
Lemma

C.1
, we obtain a constant
\delta\in(0,1)
, and let
\mathcal{E}
denote the event that the condition (
C.1
) holds for
M^{(1)},\dots,M^{(d)}
.

We now condition on a choice of
M^{(1)},\dots,M^{(d)}
satisfying (
C.1
). Recall that for each hyperedge
\mathbf{v}=(v_{1},v_{2},v_{3})\in\bigcup_{i=1}^{d}M^{(i)}
, the instance
I_{\mathrm{yes}}
contains a constraint
(\mathbf{v},S^{\mathsf{G}}_{b})
and
I_{\mathrm{no}}
contains a constraint
(\mathbf{v},S^{\mathsf{G}}_{b^{\prime}})
, for possibly distinct “right-hand side” coefficients
b,b^{\prime}\in\mathsf{G}
. The right-hand sides of the equations in
I_{\mathrm{no}}
are chosen independently, whereas those in
I_{\mathrm{yes}}
are correlated through an underlying random assignment
\tau:[n]\times[3]\to\mathsf{G}
.

However, it follows from
Proposition

C.2
that for any variable set
U\subseteq[n]\times[3]
of size at most
\delta n
, the equations placed by
I_{\mathrm{yes}}
within
U
have right-hand sides that remain jointly independent. Consequently, conditioned on
M^{(1)},\dots,M^{(d)}
, the restricted random instances
\mathrm{Sub}_{U}[I_{\mathrm{yes}}]
and
\mathrm{Sub}_{U}[I_{\mathrm{no}}]
are identically distributed, as desired.
∎

C.2
Concentration of Value for Random Instances

The proofs of
Lemmas

4.5
and
5.4
both hinge on the following concentration inequality, known as the Azuma-Hoeffding inequality.

Proposition C.3

(
[
vH14
, Corollary 3.9]
)
.

Let
\{\mathscr{F}_{t}\}_{0\leqslant t\leqslant n}
be a filtration of
\sigma
-algebras on a probability space, and let
X
be a real-valued random variable. If there exists a constant
c>0
such that
\big|\mathbb{E}\left[X|\mathscr{F}_{t}\right]-\mathbb{E}\left[X|\mathscr{F}_{t-1}\right]\big|\leqslant c
almost surely for all
t\in[n]
, then

\mathbb{P}\left[\mathbb{E}\left[X|\mathscr{F}_{n}\right]-\mathbb{E}\left[X|\mathscr{F}_{0}\right]\geqslant\Delta\Big.\right]\leqslant\exp\left(-\frac{\Delta^{2}}{2nc^{2}}\right).

Proof Sketch of
Lemma

4.5
.

Recall from
Definition

4.3
that, in process of generating the instance
I\sim\mathcal{D}_{\mathrm{no}}^{\mathsf{G},n,d}
, the total of
dn
constraints are placed in
d
rounds. In each round, for each
j\in[n]
, exactly one constraint involving
(j,1)
is added. This naturally divides the stochastic process of generating
I
into
dn
time steps, where exactly one constraint is added to
I
in each step. Let
\mathscr{F}_{t}
be the
\sigma
-algebra corresponding to the information after
t
time steps are completed, for each
t\in\{0,1,\dots,dn\}
. For each fixed assignemnt
\tau:[n]\times[3]\rightarrow\mathsf{G}
, we define the random variable
X_{\tau}:=\mathrm{val}_{I}(\tau)
and apply
Proposition

C.3
to
X_{\tau}
and the filtration
(\mathscr{F}_{t})_{0\leqslant t\leqslant dn}
. Using a “switching” argument (e.g., see
[
Wor99
, Lemma 2.19]
), it is not hard to see that

\big|\mathbb{E}\left[X_{\tau}|\mathscr{F}_{t}\right]-\mathbb{E}\left[X_{\tau}|\mathscr{F}_{t-1}\right]\big|\leqslant\frac{2}{dn},

for any
t\in[dn]
. Furthermore, we have
\mathbb{E}\left[X_{\tau}|\mathscr{F}_{dn}\right]=X_{\tau}=\mathrm{val}_{I}(\tau)
and
\mathbb{E}\left[X|\mathscr{F}_{0}\right]=\mathbb{E}\left[X_{\tau}\right]=|\mathsf{G}|^{-1}
. Therefore, the conclusion of
Proposition

C.3
implies

\underset{I}{\mathbb{P}}\left[\mathrm{val}_{I}(\tau)\geqslant\frac{1}{|\mathsf{G}|}+\varepsilon\right]\leqslant\exp\left(-\frac{\varepsilon^{2}dn}{8}\right).

A union bound over all
\tau
then implies

\underset{I}{\mathbb{P}}\left[\mathrm{val}_{I}\geqslant\frac{1}{|\mathsf{G}|}+\varepsilon\right]\leqslant|\mathsf{G}|^{3n}\exp\left(-\frac{\varepsilon^{2}dn}{8}\right)\leqslant o(1),

if we set
d\geqslant 100\varepsilon^{-2}\ln|\mathsf{G}|
.
∎

We now turn to prove
Lemma

5.4
. The idea of the proof is similar to the above proof of
Lemma

4.5
, but more preparation is needed before starting the proof.

Definition C.4
.

For any nonempty finite set
D
, we define a relation
\textit{Perm}_{D}:D^{D}\rightarrow\{0,1\}
as follows. For any tuple
y=(y_{x})_{x\in D}\in D^{D}
, we let
\textit{Perm}_{D}(y)=1
if and only if the map
x\mapsto y_{x}
is a permutation of
D
.

Note that
\textit{Perm}_{D}
is the “maximal” sub-unique relation on
D
, as
R(y)\leqslant\textit{Perm}_{D}(y)
for any sub-unique relation
y:D^{D}\rightarrow\{0,1\}
.

Definition C.5
.

For any assignment
\tau:[n]\times D\rightarrow D
, we define its
Perm-value
to be

\textup{Perm-val}(\tau):=\underset{\mathbf{v}=(v_{x})_{x\in D}}{\mathbb{E}}\left[\textit{Perm}_{D}\big((\tau(v_{x}))_{x\in D}\big)\right],

where
\mathbf{v}=(v_{x})_{x\in D}
is a random hyperedge chosen by sampling each
v_{x}
from
[n]\times\{x\}
independently and uniformly at random.

Lemma C.6
.

For any assignment
\tau:[n]\times D\rightarrow D
such that
\textup{Perm-val}(\tau)\geqslant 1-\frac{1}{5|D|}
, there exists a permutation
\pi:D\rightarrow D
such that

\underset{i\in[n]}{\mathbb{P}}\left[\tau\big((i,x)\big)=\pi(x)\right]\geqslant\textup{Perm-val}(\tau)

(C.2)

holds for all
x\in D
.

Proof.

We first show the existence of a map
\pi:D\to D
satisfying (
C.2
) for all
x\in D
, and subsequently prove that the map
\pi
thus obtained is a permutation.

For any
x,z\in D
, let
F(x,z)

denote the fraction of indices
i\in[n]
such that
\tau((i,x))=z
.
Fix an element
t\in D
and consider a hyperedge
\mathbf{v}=(v_{x})_{x\in D}
. We decompose it into
v_{t}
and
\mathbf{v}_{\setminus\{t\}}=(v_{x})_{x\in D\setminus\{t\}}
.
For the map
x\mapsto\tau(v_{x})
to be a permutation of
D
, two conditions must hold:

(1)

The values
\tau(v_{x})
for
x\in D\setminus\{t\}
are pairwise distinct.

(2)

The value
\tau(v_{t})
is the unique remaining element of
D
.

With
\tau
fixed and
\mathbf{v}
chosen uniformly at random, conditioning on the requirement (1), the probability that (2) also holds is at most
\max_{z\in D}F(t,z)
. Consequently, the overall probability that
(\tau(v_{x}))_{x\in D}
forms a permutation (i.e.
\textup{Perm-val}(\tau)
) is at most
\max_{z\in D}F(t,z)
. Define
\pi(t)
as the element of
D
attaining this maximum, i.e.

F(t,\pi(t))=\max_{z\in D}F(t,z).

By the discussion above, for any
t\in D
we have

\underset{i\in[n]}{\mathbb{P}}\left[\tau\big((i,t)\big)=\pi(t)\right]=\max_{z\in D}F(t,z)\geqslant\textup{Perm-val}(\tau).

It remains to show that
\pi
is a permutation. For a uniformly random hyperedge
\mathbf{v}
, we have

\underset{\mathbf{v}=(v_{x})_{x\in D}}{\mathbb{P}}\left[\tau(v_{x})=\pi(x),\quad\forall\,x\in D\big.\right]\geqslant\prod_{x\in D}F(x,\pi(x))\geqslant\textup{Perm-val}(\tau)^{|D|}\geqslant\left(1-\frac{1}{5|D|}\right)^{|D|}.

If
\pi
were not a permutation, then

\textup{Perm-val}(\tau)=\underset{\mathbf{v}=(v_{x})_{x\in D}}{\mathbb{E}}\left[\textit{Perm}_{D}\big((\tau(v_{x}))_{x\in D}\big)\right]\leqslant 1-\left(1-\frac{1}{5|D|}\right)^{|D|}<1-\frac{1}{10|D|},

contradicting the assumption. Hence,
\pi
must be a permutation.
∎

We are now ready to prove
Lemma

5.4
.

Proof of
Lemma

5.4
.

We call an assignment
\tau:[n]\times D\rightarrow D
“bad” (with respect to
H
) if
\mathrm{val}_{\mathcal{I}[R,H]}(\tau)\geqslant 1-\varepsilon
but

\underset{(i,x)\in[n]\times D}{\mathbb{P}}\left[\tau\big((i,x)\big)\neq y_{x}\right]>2\varepsilon

(C.3)

for any tuple
y\in D^{D}
with
R(y)=1
. For any fixed assignment
\tau:[n]\times D\rightarrow D
, we will analyze the probability that it is bad with respect to a random hypergraph
H\sim\mathcal{H}_{D,n,\ell}
.

Case 1:
\textnormal{Perm-val}(\tau)\geqslant 1-2\varepsilon
.
Since
2\varepsilon\leqslant\frac{1}{5|D|}
, we may apply
Lemma

C.6
and obtain a permutation
\pi:D\rightarrow D
such that

\underset{i\in[n]}{\mathbb{P}}\left[\tau\big((i,x)\big)=\pi(x)\right]\geqslant 1-2\varepsilon

holds for any
x\in D
. If
R\big((\pi(x))_{x\in D}\big)=1
, then this contradicts the assumption that (
C.3
) holds for all
y\in D^{D}
with
R(y)=1
. Therefore, we must have
R\big((\pi(x))_{x\in D}\big)=0
.

Now if
\mathbf{v}=(v_{x})_{x\in D}
is a random hyperedge chosen by sampling each
v_{x}
from
[n]\times\{x\}
independently and uniformly at random, then

\underset{\mathbf{v}=(v_{x})_{x\in D}}{\mathbb{E}}\left[R\big((\tau(v_{x}))_{x\in D}\big)\right]\leqslant 1-\underset{\mathbf{v}=(v_{x})_{x\in D}}{\mathbb{P}}\left[\tau(v_{x})=\pi(x),\quad\forall\,x\in D\big.\right]\leqslant 1-(1-2\varepsilon)^{|D|}\leqslant 1-2\varepsilon.

Using the martingale argument in the proof of
Lemma

4.5
, it follows that

\underset{H}{\mathbb{P}}\left[\mathrm{val}_{\mathcal{I}[R,H]}(\tau)\geqslant 1-\varepsilon\right]\leqslant\exp\left(-\frac{\varepsilon^{2}\ell n}{8}\right).

Therefore, the probability that
\tau
is bad with respect to a random
H
is at most
\exp(-\varepsilon^{2}\ell n/8)
.

Case 2:
\textnormal{Perm-val}(\tau)<1-2\varepsilon
.
Since
R(y)\leqslant\textit{Perm}_{D}(y)
for all
y\in D^{D}
, by
Definition

C.5
we have

\underset{\mathbf{v}=(v_{x})_{x\in D}}{\mathbb{P}}\left[R\big(\tau(v_{x})_{x\in D}\big)\right]\leqslant\underset{\mathbf{v}=(v_{x})_{x\in D}}{\mathbb{P}}\left[\textit{Perm}_{D}\big(\tau(v_{x})_{x\in D}\big)\right]=\textup{Perm-val}(\tau)<1-2\varepsilon.

As in case 1, it follows again that
\tau
is bad with respect to
H
with probability at most
\exp(-\varepsilon^{2}\ell n/8)
.

In conclusion, any fixed assignment
\tau:[n]\times D\rightarrow D
is bad with respect to
H
with probability at most
\exp(-\varepsilon^{2}\ell n/8)
. By a union bound over all assignments
\tau
, we have

\underset{H}{\mathbb{P}}\left[\text{Some assignment }\tau\text{ is bad with respect to }H\big.\right]\leqslant|D|^{n|D|}\exp\left(-\frac{\varepsilon^{2}\ell n}{8}\right)=\exp(-\Omega(n)),

as long as
\ell\geqslant 100\varepsilon^{-2}|D|\ln|D|
.
∎

Appendix D
Varieties Admitting Affine-type

In this section, we briefly discuss the background of
Theorem

3.2
.

D.1
Varieties

Recall from
Definition

2.10
that the notion of an algebraic homomorphism is well-defined only when the operations of the source and target structures are placed in a bijective correspondence, and each pair of corresponding operations has the same arity. Such pairs of algebraic structures are said to share the same
signature
.

In universal algebra, it is often convenient to study entire classes of algebraic structures that share a common signature. For such a class, one can represent all operation sets uniformly by a single (possibly infinite) collection of
operation symbols
. Each operation symbol has a fixed finite arity but may represent different concrete operations in different structures. Even so, it is meaningful to consider
identities
among these operation symbols on a purely syntactic level. For instance, if
\mathcal{S}
is a set of operation symbols and
s_{1},s_{2}\in\mathcal{S}
, then

s_{1}(x_{1},s_{2}(x_{2},x_{3}))=s_{2}(s_{1}(x_{1},x_{2}),x_{3})\quad\forall\,x_{1},x_{2},x_{3}

is an example of an identity, which may or may not hold in a particular algebraic structure interpreting the symbols in
\mathcal{S}
.

Definition D.1
.

Let
\mathcal{S}
be a (possibly infinite) set of operation symbols, each with a finite arity.
A
term
is any finite expression built from variables
x_{1},x_{2},\dots
and the operation symbols in
\mathcal{S}
.
If
t_{1}
and
t_{2}
are terms, a syntactic statement of the form “
t_{1}=t_{2}
for all
x_{1},x_{2},\dots
” is called an
identity
.
Given a (possibly infinite) set of identities
\mathcal{T}
, the
variety

\mathcal{V}(\mathcal{S};\mathcal{T})
is the class of all algebraic structures interpreting
\mathcal{S}
such that every identity in
\mathcal{T}
holds.

Definition D.2
.

For any algebraic structure
\mathbb{A}
, we define the variety generated by
\mathbb{A}
to be
\mathcal{V}(\mathcal{S};\mathcal{T})
, where
\mathcal{S}
is the set of operation symbols interpreted by
\mathbb{A}
and
\mathcal{T}
is the set of identities in
\mathcal{S}
that hold for
\mathbb{A}
. The variety generated by
\mathbb{A}
is also denoted by
\mathcal{V}(\mathbb{A})
.

For example, it is not hard to see that any subalgebra or homomorphic image (defined in
Definition

2.11
) of
\mathbb{A}
is contained in
\mathcal{V}(\mathbb{A})
.

D.2
Types of Strictly Simple Algebras

Following conventions in universal algebra, we refer to an algebraic structure simply as an “algebra.” An algebra of
k
elements is said to be
simple
if
k\geqslant 2
and every homomorphic image of it has either 1 element or
k
elements. It is further said to be
strictly simple
if every subalgebra of it has either 1 element or
k
elements. Tame Congruence Theory, developed by Hobby and Mckenzie
[
HM88
]
, classified strictly simple algebras into five type: (1) the unary type, (2) the affine type, (3) the Boolean type, (4) the lattice type and (5) the semilattice type. Later, Szendrei
[
Sze92
]
provided a characterization of strictly simple
idempotent
algebras of all five types. We record the following immediate corollary of
[
Sze92
, Theorem 6.1]
; readers interested in further details are referred to
[
HM88
,
Sze92
]
.

Proposition D.3

(
[
Sze92
]
)
.

If
\mathbb{A}
is a strictly simple idempotent algebra of unary type or affine type, then there exists an Abelian group structure on the underlying set of
\mathbb{A}
such that for any operation
f
in
\mathbb{A}
of arity
k
, the equation

f(x_{1},\dots,x_{k})-f(y_{1},\dots,y_{k})+f(z_{1},\dots,z_{k})=f(x_{1}-y_{1}+z_{1},\dots,x_{k}-y_{k}+z_{k})

holds for all
x_{1},\dots,x_{k},y_{1},\dots,y_{k},z_{1},\dots,z_{k}\in\mathbb{A}
.

Using the idempotency condition, it is easy to deduce the following corollary.

Corollary D.4
.

Any strictly simple idempotent algebra of unary type or affine type has an Abelian group
\mathsf{G}
as its underlying set, and all of its operations belong to
\mathrm{Pol}(\textsc{3Sum}_{\mathsf{G}})
.

In general, a finite algebra (not necessarily strictly simple) is associated with a subset of the five possible types {unary, affine, Boolean, lattice, semilattice}. A variety
\mathcal{V}
is said to
admit
a type if that type appears in the type set of at least one algebra belonging to
\mathcal{V}
. The following theorem by Valeriote
[
Val09
]
is a crucial component of
Theorem

3.2
.

Theorem D.5

(
[
Val09
, Proposition 3.1]
)
.

Let
\mathbb{A}
be a finite idempotent algebra. If the variety
\mathcal{V}(\mathbb{A})
admits either the unary type or the affine type, then some homomorphic image of a subalgebra of
\mathbb{A}
is a strictly simple algebra of unary or affine type.

Note that homomorphic images and subalgebras of an idempotent algebra are also automatically idempotent.

The final ingredient in
Theorem

3.2
is the following characterization of bounded-width CSP templates in terms of the variety generated by its polymorphism algebra. It was conjectured by
[
LZ07
]
and proved by
[
BK14
]
.

Theorem D.6

(
[
BK14
, Conjecture 4.3]
)
.

Let
(D,\Gamma)
be a relational structure that is a core. Then
(D,\Gamma)
has unbounded width if and only if the variety generated by the algebra
(D,\mathrm{Pol}(\Gamma))
admits the unary type or the affine type.

It is then clear that
Theorem

3.2
follows from the combination of
Corollary

D.4
and
Theorems

D.5
and
D.6
.
