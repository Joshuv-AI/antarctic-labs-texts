Impact of placenta accreta spectrum and cesarean hysterectomy on quality of life and postpartum depression: a case-control study in Shiraz, Iran
Authors: Shaghayegh Moradi Alamdarloo, Atefe Hashemi, Mahsa Razeghi, Nasrin Asadi, Khadije Bazrafshan, Arash Mani, Hamide Barzegar
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundPlacenta accreta spectrum (PAS) is an increasingly prevalent condition in women, often necessitating cesarean hysterectomy, which can significantly impact various aspects of life. This study aimed to evaluate the quality of life and postpartum depression in women with PAS and compare them with women who underwent repeated cesarean sections.MethodsIn this case-control study, we conducted interviews with women after delivery. The SF-36 questionnaire was used to assess quality of life, and the Edinburgh Postnatal Depression Scale (EPDS) was used to assess postpartum depression. Data on demographics, neonatal outcomes, complications, rehospitalization, blood loss, ICU admission, and neonatal NICU admission were collected from hospital records. All data were entered into SPSS and analyzed using descriptive statistics, the Kruskal-Wallis test, Spearman’s Rho correlation, the Mann-Whitney U test, and post hoc tests.ResultsAmong 62 cases with PAS and cesarean hysterectomy and 75 control subjects with repeated cesarean sections, there was a significant difference between the groups in the physical function and pain domains of the SF-36, with lower scores observed in the case group (p-value < 0.001). Significant relationships were found between blood loss and both physical function (p-value = 0.005) and pain (p-value = 0.003). NICU admission was also significantly associated with bodily pain (p-value = 0.01) and physical function (p-value = 0.005). Maternal ICU admission significantly affected physical function (p-value = 0.016), and surgical complications were related to role limitations due to emotional problems (p-value = 0.028). Probable depression was observed in 37 cases and 39 controls(p-value = 0.89).ConclusionWomen with PAS face meaningful postoperative limitations—particularly in physical function and pain—that extend beyond surgical morbidity and highlight the need for specialized perioperative counseling, proactive rehabilitation strategies, and multidisciplinary follow-up. Despite these physical burdens, postpartum depressive symptoms were comparable between groups, indicating that mental health outcomes may depend on factors other than PAS alone.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12884-026-09407-4.

Background

During the postpartum period, the likelihood of maternal depression and anxiety rises due to emerging psychosocial stressors and hormonal changes, such as the decline in estrogen levels [1]. Although no definitive connection has been established between cesarean section and postpartum depression [2], there may be an association with emergency cesarean Sects [3, 4]. Antenatal depression in high-risk pregnancies has been reported to range from 12.5% to 44.2% across different studies [5].

Placenta accreta spectrum (PAS) has been on the rise over the years, largely due to the increasing rate of cesarean deliveries, its primary risk factor [6]. Hysterectomy is often unavoidable [7], which can lead to psychological and emotional challenges, in addition to other postpartum issues that women may experience. A diagnosis of placenta accreta had a profound psychological effect on pregnant women. Over half of the women experienced high anxiety levels about morbidity, mortality, and future sexual activities, as indicated in a study by Crocetto et al. [8]. Additionally, a study involving 69 women, including 17 diagnosed with PAS, found that those with PAS were significantly more likely to meet the criteria for probable post-traumatic stress disorder (PTSD) compared to those who had an uncomplicated cesarean birth, with rates of 40% versus less than 10%, respectively [9].

In this study, our aim was to assess the quality of life and depression levels among patients with placenta accreta and those who had cesarean deliveries, and to compare these groups to determine the impact of PAS on their lives.

Methods

Study design and population

This case-control study was designed to evaluate the post-operative quality of life in patients with placenta accreta spectrum undergoing cesarean hysterectomy, compared to a control group of healthy women undergoing repeated cesarean delivery. The study was conducted in hospitals affiliated with Shiraz University of Medical Sciences (SUMS) from 2015 to 2020.

Patients with a pathologically confirmed diagnosis of placenta accreta spectrum (including placenta accreta, increta, and percreta) who underwent cesarean hysterectomy at SUMS educational hospitals were enrolled the study as case group.

Healthy pregnant women with at least two prior cesarean sections enrolled as control group.

Exclusion Criteria includes confirmed major psychiatric problems, use of psychiatric medication during or before pregnancy and refusal of the interview for filling the questionnaire.

A data gathering form was used to collect demographic information and potential risk factors, including age, gravidity, history of previous cesarean sections, curettage, preoperative diagnosis, intraoperative details (blood loss, blood product transfusion, surgery duration), postoperative details (duration of hospitalization, ICU admission, surgical complications including bladder or bowel rupture, need for abdominal packing, postoperation collection), and newborn outcomes (APGAR score, birth weight, gestational age). This form was completed by an obstetrics and gynecology resident.

We have used two questionnaires Short Form survey (SF-36) and The Edinburgh Postnatal Depression Scale (EPDS) (both are attached as supplementary files) .

The SF-36 is a 36-item questionnaire that measures quality of life across eight domains: vitality, physical functioning, bodily pain, general health perceptions, physical role functioning, emotional role functioning, social role functioning, and mental health. The Persian version of SF-36 has been validated and found reliable in Iran by Montazeri et al., with an overall internal consistency of more than 0.7 and validated Sect [10]. Each of the eight sections of the SF-36 is scored on a scale from 0 to 100, with higher scores indicating better health status. The scores from the individual sections are aggregated to form a profile of the participant’s quality of life.

EPDS is a tool designed to measure depression specifically in the postnatal period. The Persian version of the EPDS has a Cronbach’s alpha of 0.7 and a validity coefficient of 0.44 when compared to the Beck Depression Inventory [11].

The EPDS consists of 10 items, each scored from 0 to 3. The total score ranges from 0 to 30, with higher scores indicating more depressive symptoms. A score of 13 or higher indicates probable depression. The SF-36 and EPDS questionnaires were administered via telephone by an obstetrics and gynecology resident, with the timing of questionnaire administration recorded. All participants provided verbal informed consent.

Statistical Analysis

Descriptive statistics were used to analyze demographic data and compare groups based on pathology and outcomes. The findings from the SF-36 and EPDS questionnaires were also analyzed descriptively based on their scoring systems.

Kruskal-Wallis Test used to compare more than two groups when the dependent variable is ordinal or continuous but not normally distributed.

Spearman’s Rho Correlation used to measure the strength and direction of association between two ranked variables.

Mann-Whitney U Test used to compare differences between two independent groups when the dependent variable is either ordinal or continuous but not normally distributed. We also utilized a post hoc test to further analyze the data and identify any significant differences between the groups.

Ethics

The study was approved by the Ethics Committee of Shiraz University of Medical Sciences (IR.SUMS.REC.1400.023) and was conducted in accordance with the ethical principles of the Declaration of Helsinki. Informed consent was obtained from all participants prior to enrollment.

The neonatal outcomes were as follows: 47 (75.8%) cases had live births compared to 74 (98.6%) in the control group. One neonate in each group (1.6% vs. 1.3%) was deceased, and 5 (8%) of the cases resulted in abortion (p-value = 0.01), which were terminated before 24 weeks gestational age.

Rehospitalization occurred in one patient from each group. Additional surgery was required in 10 (16.12%) of the cases, whereas none of the control group needed additional surgery (p-value < 0.001). Painful intercourse was experienced by one (1.6%) of the cases and 6 (8%) of the control group (p-value = 0.12).

Among all patients, 3 (4.8%) of the cases and 1 (1.3%) of the control group used medication for psychiatric problems after delivery.

Medical history assessment showed 9 patients with diabetes (3 in the case group and 6 in the control group), 2 patients with hypertension (both in the control group), 1 patient with both hypertension and diabetes (in the control group), 6 patients with hypothyroidism (in the control group), and 115 participants reported no previous medical problems. Discussion

As the incidence of PAS continues to rise, it is crucial to address its adverse effects and work towards prevention. Many women experience postpartum depression and quality-of-life issues following hysterectomy and cesarean sections. Our study highlighted impacts on bodily pain and physical function. Additionally, factors such as surgical complications, blood loss, maternal ICU admission, and neonatal NICU admission can affect various domains of quality of life.

There was a significant difference between women with PAS and the control group in the pain and physical function domains of the SF-36. Keenan et al. also reported that women with PAS had higher levels of pain and anxiety six months after giving birth, but there were no significant differences in the other SF-36 domains. By two years postpartum, the differences between the groups had disappeared [12]. This pattern suggests that although PAS may cause early postoperative physical limitations, some measures of quality of life may recover over time, which aligns with the partial improvement observed in our cohort. However, in our study, there was no significant difference in the interview timing. In contrast to our findings, Bartels et al. reported the highest scores in the SF-36 domains for physical function [13]. This discrepancy may reflect differences in population characteristics, severity of PAS, postoperative management protocols, or timing of assessment across studies. In addition, differences in healthcare systems and access to postpartum rehabilitation or physical therapy services may contribute to variations in recovery and physical functioning outcomes between studies. Cultural and social factors may also influence perceptions of role functioning and the reporting of physical limitations in Iranian women compared with Western populations. Grover et al. found that women with PAS experienced significantly lower quality of life in physical functioning, role functioning, social functioning, and pain at six months postpartum, though other quality of life domains were not significantly impacted [14]. Our findings complement this evidence by reinforcing that physical limitations and pain are the most consistently affected domains across studies. However, the absence of differences in other SF-36 domains in our cohort suggests that the psychological and social impact of PAS may be more heterogeneous and influenced by contextual factors, such as social support, healthcare access, and cultural perceptions of childbirth.

Surgical complications were associated with a decrease in the maternal role limitation emotional score, which aligns with findings from a study conducted in Egypt [15].

As mentioned in the results, blood loss was associated with physical function and bodily pain. Massive blood loss is typically seen in cases of PAS and cesarean hysterectomy. In our study, all significant blood loss incidents occurred in the case group. It has been reported that the psychological impact of hemorrhagic complications was not related to the amount of bleeding or the need for a hysterectomy, but was associated with whether the procedure was emergency or elective [16]. Our findings complement these observations by demonstrating that, although psychological outcomes may not directly correlate with blood loss volume, substantial hemorrhage appears to contribute meaningfully to physical limitations and pain perception in the postoperative period. This suggests a differential pathway in which blood loss has a stronger somatic rather than psychological impact. Moreover, the exclusive occurrence of major bleeding events in the PAS group underscores the severity of the condition and may partly explain the consistently poorer physical outcomes reported across studies.

Mothers of NICU infants experience significant psychological distress [17] due to various factors, such as worries about their baby’s long-term outcomes, separation from the infant, changes in their parental role, and feelings of helplessness. This distress is further exacerbated by a sense of loss in maternal roles and diminished feelings of control, leading to increased anxiety and depression [18]. Our results also revealed significantly lower scores in physical function and bodily pain, along with higher levels of depression, though the latter was not statistically significant in mothers of NICU infants.

According to our study, the EPDS scores indicated depression in both groups, with no statistically significant differences between them. Although other studies have shown that depression tends to decline over time [19], this was not observed in our findings.

Another finding was that there was no association between the number of live children and the scores on the SF-36 or EPDS. The relationship between the number of pregnancies and depression is mixed, Dame et al. found a significant correlation between parity and antenatal depression [20], while Natasha et al. observed a negative relationship [21], and Ragland et al. reported a positive link [22]. These conflicting results highlight the need for further research on this issue.

The mean SF-36 scores were significantly higher in patients with underlying diseases compared to those without. However, since only 13.5% of the patients had underlying conditions, we cannot draw definitive conclusions about the relationship between having an underlying disease and quality of life. This topic warrants further investigation in future studies.

The strength of our study lies in the use of two important questionnaires to assess postpartum psychological issues and the case-control design, which included repeated cesarean sections as a control group. This comparative approach provided a clearer understanding of the specific impacts of PAS. Additionally, we carefully considered and controlled for potential confounding variables such as the timing of the interview, the presence of underlying diseases, and maternal and neonatal admissions to ICU/NICU. This approach helped ensure that the differences observed between the groups were truly attributable to PAS and not to other factors.

However, one limitation of our study was the reluctance of some women to complete the questionnaires, which may have impacted the comprehensiveness of our findings. A potential limitation of this study is that the control group was restricted to women with ≥ 2 prior cesarean sections to improve comparability with PAS cases regarding baseline obstetric risk factors. Consequently, the findings may not be fully generalizable to women with fewer or no prior cesarean deliveries.

In conclusion, this study demonstrates that PAS is associated with substantial postoperative physical challenges, driven in part by the high burden of intraoperative blood loss and complex surgical interventions. These findings underscore the importance of anticipatory counseling, enhanced recovery pathways, and coordinated multidisciplinary care tailored to the needs of women with PAS. Although broader quality-of-life domains and postpartum depressive symptoms did not differ between groups, the consistent impact on physical functioning and pain highlights areas where clinical practice, postoperative support, and resource allocation can be optimized. Collectively, our results provide evidence to guide patient expectations, inform health-system planning in PAS referral centers, and support the development of targeted strategies to improve recovery and long-term well-being.
