Internet usage time and trust in doctors: evidence from China
Authors: Qiuming Gao, Xuechun Wang, Zhen Lin, Zangyi Liao
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundThe growth of internet use has greatly impacted many societal sectors, including healthcare. However, current evidence about the relationship between internet usage time and public trust on doctors is insufficient.ObjectiveTo examine the relationship between the time spent on the internet and the level of trust in doctors, and explore its mechanism.MethodsMultiple regression models are employed, utilizing a sample of 19,193 observations from the China Family Panel Studies (CFPS) in 2020. First, we regress the relative trust in doctors compared to trust in strangers on internet usage time. Subsample results across distinct population groups are explored. Second, we examine the mechanism of using the internet as an information channel through the mediation method, and further investigate the role of the internet as a communication platform by analyzing the moderation effects of socializing activity as well as social media engagement.ResultsWe find a significant negative relationship between increased internet usage time and trust in doctors. This correlation remains robust across different model specifications and variable measurements. The negative correlation is more pronounced among population groups who are elderly, male, married, with low educational levels, insured and residing in urban areas. The role of the internet as a source of information is validated as a mechanism producing the negative influence. The role of the internet as a communication platform tends to mitigate the negative consequences of internet usage, as engaging in online socializing activity can have a positive moderation effect. However, for social media engagement, its overall effect on trust in doctors is negative, potentially due to social media’s dual function as a communication platform and an information source.ConclusionsAlthough individual-focused literature tends to demonstrate a positive impact of the internet on trust in doctors in China, our study, which focuses on the general public, reveals contrasting results. Our findings imply that the primary manner in which internet usage, encompassing social media usage, influences public trust in doctors is by molding a collective perception of them. Consequently, the medical community may need to take a more proactive approach in expanding online communication channels for interacting with patients and the wider public.Keywords: Internet usage time, Trust in doctors, Public trust, Social media

Introduction

Since its inception, the internet has exerted extensive influence on socio-economic aspects and has been recognized for its capability to alter public perceptions and attitudes across various fields, including healthcare [1]. With the recent widespread adoption of mobile internet, this influence has intensified. The implications of increased internet use on public trust in doctors have become an issue of growing interest.

Existing literature on trust in doctors can be categorized into two levels. One is at the individual level, studying a patient’s trust in their physician [2–4]. This strand emphasized the role of individual factors, focusing on characteristics of both the patients and the treating physicians. Factors studied include the patient’s honesty during medical consultations, the physician’s protection of patient confidentiality, the communication skills exhibited by both parties, etc. The other stand of literature is at the societal level, investigating public trust in doctors in general or in the healthcare system [5]. These studies incorporate societal factors. For instance, they explore the heterogeneous perceptions among population groups [6] and the impacts of legal and other policy regulations [7]. In both of these dimensions, media has been found to be a significant influencer. For instance, exposure to television can lead to negative attitudes of doctors [8]. This may be related to the increasingly negative portrayal of doctors in media coverage [9]. Negative television reports in the medical field can erode trust in doctors [10]. In these studies, the way in which media plays a role is primarily by affecting the public perceptions of doctors, and its impact tends to be negative. Moreover, the selective reporting or fictional depictions in the media can influence patients’ health information-seeking. For example, a study found that the success rate of cardiopulmonary resuscitation depicted in TV dramas is significantly higher than actual medical statistics [11]. When patients use such incorrect health information during their communication with doctors, it can lead to distrust of doctors.

The internet, as a new media, has garnered attention in the literature on trust in doctors. Compared to traditional media, it provides a broader range of impact channels, making the combined effect vague. Firstly, the internet expands information dissemination, increasing patients’ access to health information [12]. The volume of information available on the internet is vast. This leads to patients becoming more actively engaged in communication with doctors, thus attaining an empowered position [13–16]. This alters the patient-doctor relationship. These studies show that the direction of the internet’s impact on trust depends not only on the quality of information [17], but also on how physicians respond. Being respectful and cooperative with patients helps mitigate skepticism and bolster trust. Secondly, the internet expands the avenues of patient-doctor communication. On the one hand, online diagnosis emerges as a novel mode of medical consultation, complementing offline appointments [18]. On the other hand, the utilization of internet tools enhances the communication efficiency. For instance, online applications enable frequent and thorough communication with doctors via chat modules; they also allow for patients’ accessing more medical process records, which fosters trust [19–21]. Thirdly, the social media on the internet al.lows patients and doctors to develop interactions beyond the confines of medical consultations [22]. Doctors’ postings on social media, along with their personal profiles, allow patients to portray the doctor beyond professionalism, which affects their trust [23]. Furthermore, social media, serving as a communication platform, functions as a vital source for gathering doctors’ word-of-mouth reputations [24]. Given the above influence channels, the direction of the internet’s impact on trust in doctors is uncertain and requires empirical examination.

China has been experiencing rapid development in the internet in recent years, especially mobile internet. The number of internet users and the average usage time have shown an increasing trend (see Fig. 1). Concurrently, tensions in the doctor-patient relationship have emerged as a matter of public concern [25]. In the Chinese context, research has also investigated the relationship between the internet and trust in doctors. Online health information seeking has been found to have a positive impact in most literature [26]. Online health information support from doctors enhances patient–physician interactions and tends to increase trust in doctors [27]. Doctors showcasing their profiles and academic publications on social media contribute to gaining more followers [28]. The positive effects observed in these studies may be related to their observational perspective. They are mostly based on small surveys conducted among visiting patients, thus revealing the trust at the individual level. At the public level, however, research based on large-scale survey data are scarce. Using data from the China Family Panel Studies (CFPS) which is a nationwide survey, Meng et al. (2022) compared the trust in doctors between internet users and non-internet users, and found that internet users tend to give lower trust scores [29]. This suggests that the impact of internet on public trust in doctors may differ from that on individual patients. Although this study provides insightful findings, their model setting only allows them to analyze the impact of internet access, rather than the internet usage intensity, and also limits them from extending to mechanism analysis and discussions on internet usage details.

This paper aims to provide empirical evidence on the impact of internet usage on public trust in doctors, using CFPS data from China. The basic results are obtained through a refined model setting, quantifying internet usage by time length. We validate the role of the internet as an information source through mediation methods, and distinguish it from other media forms. We then seek to explore the role of the internet as a communication platform, examining the moderation effects of online socializing activity as well as social media. Our main contributions lie in the identification of the underlying mechanism of internet usage time on trust in doctors and the extended discussions on online socializing activity and social media.

Methods

Data

China Family Panel Studies is a nationwide multidisciplinary longitudinal survey program led by the Institute of Social Science Survey at Peking University, which has been conducted biennially since 2010 [30]. It covers 25 provinces in China, targeting a sample size of 16,000 households. The CFPS sample is a multi-stage probability sample extracted through a rigorously designed implicit stratification method to ensure adequate geographic representation [31]. The method was designed collaboratively by Peking University and the University of Michigan. As a household survey, CFPS covers all household members and is therefore demographically representative. Informed consent to participate was obtained from all of the participants.

The questionnaires cover social, economic, demographic, educational, and health aspects at the individual (child or adult), family and community levels [32]. In the Adult questionnaire, the Internet Module covers internet use behaviors, and the Subjective Attitude Module contains ratings of trust in doctors and other groups of people. Because there is a mismatch in data collection for the key variable internet usage across years (which was measured as “spare time on the internet/hours per week” before 2020 and as “time spent on the internet/minutes per day” after 2020.), we constructed the study sample using only the most recent cross-sectional data, the 2020 Adult dataset.

The evaluation of trust in CFPS comes from scale ratings. This is a subjective assessment based on individual perceptions, making it not very meaningful to directly compare results between individuals. Therefore, we follow Wang et al. (2022) and use relative trust in doctors as the dependent variable [33]. It is calculated as the score of trust in doctors minus the score of trust in strangers. These two scores are obtained from the CFPS2020 adult questionnaire items: “N10024: To what extent do you trust strangers?” and “N10026: To what extent do you trust physicians?“. Participants were asked to respond on a scale from 10 (complete trust) to 0 (no trust at all) for each item.

Variables

The independent variable is the total time spent online, which is aggregated from the duration of internet use on mobile devices and computers. These data are obtained from the CFPS2020 adult questionnaire items: “U201A: On a typical day, how many minutes do you use your mobile devices to access the Internet?” and “U202A: On a typical day, how many minutes do you use your computer to access the Internet?”. We do not make a further differentiation between internet usage on mobile devices and computers, because computer usage is quite limited and almost all computer users also access the internet on a mobile device.1

Experiences involving doctor-patient contact may influence trust in doctors [34, 35]. The medical experience controls include all variables in the CFPS that might be related to doctor-patient contact, namely the type of healthcare facility most frequently visited; satisfaction with the infrastructure conditions of that facility; satisfaction with the therapeutic proficiency of that facility; history of hospitalization in the past year, and current health status.

The selection of personal characteristic controls is based on the routine controls used in the existing literature on patient-doctor relationships, including age, gender, marital status, education level, income, the presence of health insurance and urban or rural residence [35–37]. Due to the absence of personal income in CFPS2, we use household per capita income instead. Medical expenditure as a share of household expenditure is also included to measure healthcare burden. Robustness

The results obtained from the benchmark regression may be sensitive to changes in the model specification, measurement methods or data sample. To verify the reliability and stability of our findings, we use three types of methods to confirm the robustness of our benchmark results.

The first is to change the setting of the regression model. Multiple regression models we use for benchmark regression treat the dependent variable as a continuous variable, but trust ratings are actually ordinal values. Therefore, we use the Ordered Logit model, which takes into consideration the ordinal characteristic of the dependent variable, and repeat the regression to confirm the consistency of our findings. As shown in column 1 of Table 4, the coefficient for internet usage time remains negative and significant, which echoes the benchmark results.

The second type involves using alternative indicators for dependent and independent variables. For the dependent variable, the relative trust in the benchmark model is constructed by subtracting the scores of trust in strangers from those of trust in doctors. To confirm the robustness of our findings, we adopt a ratio-based approach as an alternative measure. Specifically, relative trust is calculated as (trust in doctors + 1) / (trust in strangers + 1). We employ this new dependent variable and repeat regression. The results are presented in column 2 of Table 4. It can be seen that the coefficient for internet usage time remains robust.

For the independent variable, internet usage time, considering that self-reported time may be biased, we change the measurement approach and segment the internet usage time to create new variables representing usage levels. Firstly, we create a dummy variable based on whether internet usage time is zero. Observations with zero values account for 40.3% of the whole sample. Repeating regression using this alternative explanatory variable, results in column 3 of Table 4 show that the coefficient for the dummy variable remains negative and significant, which is consistent with Meng et al. (2022) [29]. Secondly, we further divide internet usage hours into three segments: 0, [0,3], and above 3, and assign values of 0, 1, and 2 respectively. This way, we generate a 3-value variable Int_level. 3 h is chosen as the cutoff in order to roughly evenly distribute the internet-using observations, with 20.4% of the sample reporting internet usage time exceeding 3 h. Replacing the exact time usage with this level variable as the independent variable, column 4 shows that the result remains robust.

The third method is to repeat regressions using data from other years. Our analysis is based on cross-sectional data, which, while helpful in reflecting the most recent situation, may also display characteristics specific to certain years. In particular, the COVID-19 pandemic outbreak in 2020 might have caused abnormal changes in internet usage time and trust in doctors. Although China was in the early stages of the pandemic when the 2020 survey was conducted and was relatively less disrupted, concerns may still arise. Therefore, we use data from the previous two waves, 2018 and 2016, to repeat the benchmark regression. Internet usage time in these two years was measured differently, specifically as “weekly hours of internet usage in spare time”. The results are shown in Table 5. Columns 1 and 2 respectively use samples from the 2018 and 2016 waves composed of individuals from the benchmark regression sample, while columns 3 and 4 use the entire 2018 and 2016 wave data, respectively. It can be seen that the coefficients for internet usage time are consistently negative and significant, indicating the robustness of our findings across years.

Endogeneity

A major concern with using cross-sectional data and OLS regression is the endogeneity problem. Since our dependent variables relies on individual subjective ratings, it is likely that there are some (unobserved) omitted individual characteristics that both affect relative trust in doctors and are correlated with internet usage time. Omitting these variables in the regression can lead to inconsistent estimates of the explanatory variable. We use the instrumental variable (IV) method to alleviate this concern3.

We search for instruments in two directions. Firstly, we use the lag of the explanatory variable, i.e., internet usage time in 2018, as an instrument. A key prerequisite for using a lagged explanatory variable is that unobserved variables are not serially correlated [39]. Since the measurement of internet usage time in 2018 is different from that in 2020, this helps alleviate serial correlation. Secondly, we use monthly per capita household expenses on communication, electricity, and transportation & communication equipment, as three instrumental variables. Communication expenses include broadband internet fees and mobile fees, and transportation & communication equipment expenses include spending on cell phones. More internet usage time is associated with higher communication expenses, but the communication costs themselves have no effect on trust in doctors (in China, there is no GP or family doctor system, and patients and doctors do not communicate via phone). Similarly, more time spent online is associated with higher electricity costs and implies relatively higher expenditures on cell phone devices, but they are not directly associated with preferences for trust in doctors.

Table 6 presents the results of the instrumental variables regressions. We use the 2SLS estimator with robust standard errors. Column 1 uses the 2018 internet usage time as the instrument. From the first-stage regression, the coefficient on the instrument is significantly positive, indicating a good correlation with the endogenous variable. In the second stage, the coefficient of the endogenous variable is significantly negative, which confirms the robustness of the benchmark results. Column 2 uses expenses on communication, electricity and transportation & communication equipment as instruments. The first stage shows positive and significant coefficients for each instrument, and in the second stage the coefficient for the endogenous variable remains significantly negative. Column 3 then includes all the above instruments in the regression, and the results again echo our benchmark regression. We also conduct the test of overidentifying restrictions and the weak IV test on the regression in column 3, and the results indicate that all the instruments are exogenous and there is no weak instrument problem.

Population profile

Differences in personal characteristics may lead to differences in the impact of internet usage time. In this subsection, we examine these characteristics by splitting the sample into two subsamples based on each characteristic factor. For dummy variables such as Gender, Married, Insurance, and Urban, the sample is divided according to their values. For Age, the sample is divided into two groups: adults and the elderly according to whether the users are over 60 years old or not. For education level, considering that compulsory education in China ends at middle school, the sample is divided into a low education group (middle school and below) and a high education group (high school and above), with middle school as the dividing line.

We repeat regression on each subsample respectively, and the results are presented in Table 7. It can be seen that while the effect of internet usage time tends to be negative for all adults, it is more significant and pronounced in the elderly population (column 1). Existing literature suggests that the elderly population tends to have higher trust in doctors than the young population [37]. When the internet is present, the opposite result we find suggests that the elderly may be less able to select and process online information. When increased internet usage leads to an increase in online information, it may instead deteriorate the uptake of accurate information, resulting in a biased impression of doctors. Existing literature also finds that female and married populations have higher trust [35]. While men are more likely to be adversely affected compared to women (column 2), we find those who are married are more affected than those who are unmarried or divorced (column 3). Higher levels of education do help to avoid the negative impact of internet exposure (column 4), which echoes the literature [40]. It suggests that education may contribute to better information selection and processing abilities. The coefficients for the insured and uninsured groups are similar in value, although only the coefficient for the insured group is significant (columns 5). Those residing in urban areas do not exhibit resistance to this impact; instead, they experience more pronounced negative effects (columns 6). This may be due to the unique healthcare system in China. China does not have a GP or family doctor system. For outpatient care, urban residents usually go directly to hospitals, while rural residents typically visit village health offices or township health centers. This means that for rural residents, patients and doctors generally live in the same or nearby community and are closer and more familiar with each other. Contacts beyond medical visits may help to eliminate the disturbance of online information.

Discussions

Internet as an information channel

Importance of the internet as an information channel

Existing literature suggests that one of the main ways internet usage affects trust in doctors is through influencing health information seeking. We use the score rating of “importance of the internet as an information channel” in CFPS survey as a mediating variable, and apply Baron & Kenny’s (1986) mediation method to examine the existence of this influence pathway [41]4.

We first look at the impact of internet usage time on the importance of the internet as an information channel, using the absolute score (Imp_abs) as the dependent variable and internet usage time as the explanatory variable. The results are shown in column 1 of Table 8. It can be seen that the coefficient of internet usage time is significantly positive, indicating that increased internet usage contributes to enhancing the importance of the internet as an information channel.

Considering that the absolute values of the scores are still interfered by individual subjective factors, we then replace them with relative values that have been standardized. When the CFPS survey queried about the importance of the internet as an information channel, for comparison purposes it also asked about the importance of other media channels, including television, newspapers and magazines, radio, broadcasting, and mobile text message. Based on this we construct the relative scores as follows: relative score = importance score of the internet as an information channel - (sum of the importance scores of all 6 media as an information channel) / 6. Repeating the regression with the relative score (Imp_rel) as the dependent variable, the first part of column 2 presents the results. It can be seen that the coefficient of internet usage time remains positive and significant, indicating its promoting effect on the importance of the internet as an information source. Based on this result, the second part of column 2 incorporates both the relative score and internet usage time into the benchmark regression. It can be seen that the coefficient of internet usage time becomes insignificant, and its value shrinks substantially relative to the benchmark results, while the coefficient of the relative score is significantly negative. This suggests that the relative score serves as a good mediating variable. This implies that increasing internet usage time affects trust in doctors by enhancing the importance of the internet as an information channel. The mechanism is validated.

Internet versus other media forms

Because only internet use is recorded in CFPS and there is no information on the use and usage time of other media, we fail to control for the effects of other media in the benchmark regression. This could lead to an omitted variable problem, where the coefficient for internet usage could capture the overall effects of all media, rather than the specific impact of the internet. To alleviate this concern, we take an alternative approach based on data availability.

We generate a variable, Int_ratio, to indicate the relative importance of the internet compared to other media, defined as: Int_ratio = importance score of the internet as an information channel/ sum of the importance scores of other media as an information channel. Using this variable instead of internet usage time as the explanatory variable into the regression, the results are shown in column 1 of Table 9. It can be seen that the coefficient is negative and significant, indicating that an increase in the relative importance of the internet tends to lead to a decrease in trust in doctors. This suggests that the internet has an influence on trust beyond other media channels.

Socializing activity and social media

As previously mentioned, a distinctive feature of the internet is its interactivity [42]. While TV or newspapers can only be consumed unilaterally, the internet facilitates interactions through chatting, posting, and commenting, among other means, thereby promoting the communication and exchange of information, rather than merely serving as a source of information. In this subsection, we further explore this social attribute of the internet.

Internet usage for socializing

Even with the same amount of time spent online, whether it is dedicated to socializing or other activities can result in different perceptual consequences. We compare the impact of internet usage for socializing with other purposes.

The CFPS dataset does not distinguish the allocation of online time for each activity. Instead, we use respondents’ perceived importance of the internet for specific activities. These activities include socializing, entertainment, daily life, work, and studying. Since the evaluation of the importance of activities is also based on subjective scoring, we continue using the previous method to construct the relative importance of each activity. Specifically, it is calculated as follows: relative importance of an activity = the importance score of the internet for that activity - the importance score of the internet as an information channel.

We use the moderation method to examine the moderating effects of different activities on the impact of internet usage time5. By adding the interaction term of the importance of each activity and the internet usage time in the benchmark regression, we obtain the results shown in Table 10. It can be seen that the coefficient of the interaction term for socializing is significantly positive, while that for entertainment is negative. The coefficients of the interaction terms for other activities are insignificant. As the coefficients of the activities themselves are all insignificant, the total effect of each activity mainly lies in its interaction term.

The finding that internet usage for socializing is more likely to have a positive impact on trust in doctors suggests that communication and information exchange at the individual level can assist in mitigating the negative public image arising from internet browsing, since socializing activity involves the most frequent interpersonal interactions among all the activities. Interpersonal communication involves direct patient-to-patient communication or informal exchanges with doctors that directly enhance the quality of information [43, 44]. Moreover, this activity may contribute to an enhanced ability to analyze online information through information exchanges with various individuals and opinions [45, 46]. In contrast, online entertainment activity (e.g., listening to music or watching videos) is typically done alone, which may be why it is the only activity with a significantly negative coefficient. Daily life activities (e.g., shopping), as well as work and study activities, involve some interpersonal interaction due to the necessity of dealing with other relevant parties, but not as frequently as socializing, which may explain their insignificant coefficients.

Social media engagement

The emergence of social media is a major change brought about by the internet to social networking. Social media not only provides traditional socializing function of chatting, but also allows for posting and sharing of personal life and other information, thus serving as both a communication platform and an information channel. Here we examine the role of social media.

Regarding social media engagement, in the CFPS questionnaire, there is a question about “the frequency of sharing on WeChat Moments”. WeChat is the most popular socializing application in China, and “Moments” function allows for posting in a Facebook-like interface which can be commented on by one’s WeChat friends. The options provided under this question are never, once every few months, once a month, 2–3 times a month, 1–2 times a week, 3–4 times a week, almost every day and not applicable. We assign values starting from 1 to these options in ascending order of frequency and define a variable Int_sharing. We then put it and its interaction with internet usage time into the basic regression. The results are shown in column 1 of Table 11. It can be seen that the moderation effect of social media sharing on internet usage time is positive. This suggests that more social media engagement is likely to improve attitudes towards doctors as a moderator, which is in line with our findings on socializing activity. However, social media sharing itself has a negative influence on trust in doctors, which resembles the role of general internet usage as an information channel. The results in column 1 echo our previous findings that internet usage as an information channel has a negative impact on trust in doctors, while online socializing activity has an additional positive moderation effect on it. Given that the median internet usage time in our sample is 1.859, the overall impact of internet sharing tends to be negative (0.01*1.859 − 0.065 = − 0.046). It seems that, for social media, its impact as an information source overwhelms its impact as a communication platform. However, since we measure social media engagement is through its sharing function rather than its chatting function, this result should be interpreted cautiously.

Conclusion

This study examines the impact of internet usage time on public trust in doctors. Based on the 2020 CFPS data from China, multiple regression results indicate that increased time spent online is negatively associated with trust in doctors. The negative relationship is more pronounced among population groups who are elderly, male, married, with low educational levels, insured and residing in urban areas. The role of the internet as a source of information is validated as a mechanism producing the negative influence. The role of the internet as a communication platform tends to mitigate the negative consequences of internet usage, as engaging in online socializing activity can have a positive moderation effect. However, for social media engagement, its overall effect on trust in doctors is negative, potentially due to social media’s dual function as a communication platform and an information source.

Our findings suggest that although the internet has great potential for enhancing patient-doctor communication, its primary effect on the public may be shaping their general perception of doctors. The vast volume of information and the arise of social media amplify this portrayal.

As internet usage continues to deepen, doctors and healthcare institutions need to implement targeted strategies to counteract this negative impact. Actionable recommendations include:

1) Act more proactively in expanding online communication channels for interacting with patients and the public. In addition to establishing direct communication with patients through trusted social media platforms and organizing online Q&A sessions to respond to patient queries in real-time, it is also possible to organize online activities for the public. These activities include giving live lectures, posting informative videos, and engaging in online events with public figures or local communities. Such activities help to improve the public’s perception of doctors. It also helps to provide accurate and reliable health information to the public.

2) Increase online exposure to the public. Healthcare providers need to actively increase their presence by regularly updating their social media profiles and posting informative content. They can encourage doctors to share positive patient-doctor interaction stories and testimonials on social media and institutional websites. Personal stories and testimonials can humanize doctors and make them appear more approachable and trustworthy.

Simultaneously, healthcare authorities also need to adapt to the digital era. Actionable recommendations include:

1) Timely clarify negative or false medical information. When negative or false information is detected, it needs to be investigated in a timely manner. Based on the investigation results, the authorities need to actively speak out through their websites as well as major media outlets to address and correct media biases. This approach is not only for information that has already been widely disseminated, but also for potential rumors that may arise, which need to be clarified in advance to avoid misleading the public.

2) Provide educational content to help the public critically evaluate the health information they find online. The ability to identify and process information may be a key factor affecting the internet’s impact. In light of our finding that online entertainment negatively moderates the effect of internet usage time on trust in doctors, health authorities could use more entertaining approaches for education. For example, use short videos or animations to educate patients and the public on how to discern credible sources from unreliable ones. This education can reduce the impact of misinformation and build trust in healthcare providers.

The mediation method is a classic technique used to understand the mechanism through which an explanatory variable influences a dependent variable via a third variable, known as the mediator. Baron and Kenny’s (1986) mediation model involves two steps. First, verify that the explanatory variable has a significant effect on the mediator; second, verify that the mediator has a significant effect on the dependent variable in the presence of the explanatory variable. If both steps are confirmed, it proves that the explanatory variable influences the dependent variable through the mediator, thereby confirming the mechanism.

The moderation method is a technique used to examine whether the impact of the explanatory variable changes depending on the level of another variable, known as the moderator [41]. The moderation effect is typically obtained through the interaction between the moderator and the explanatory variable. This method helps to understand the conditions under which an effect occurs or the strength of the effect.
