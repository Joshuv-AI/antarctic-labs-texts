Repeated levosimendan administration guided by invasive hemodynamic assessment in postcardiotomy cardiogenic shock after off-pump coronary artery bypass grafting: a case report
Authors: Oleg Kuntsevich, Nikita Chibisov, Dmitry Levikov, Evgeny Rodionov
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Postcardiotomy cardiogenic shock is a life-threatening complication after coronary artery bypass grafting (CABG), especially in patients with severely impaired left ventricular systolic function. Management remains challenging, and criteria for repeated levosimendan administration in this setting are not well defined. We report the case of a 74-year-old man with ischemic cardiomyopathy and severely reduced preoperative left ventricular ejection fraction who underwent preoperative levosimendan optimization followed by off-pump CABG. Despite initial improvement, the patient developed postcardiotomy cardiogenic shock in the early postoperative period with progressive hypoperfusion, lactate elevation, worsening ventricular dysfunction, and a low cardiac index confirmed by Swan–Ganz catheterization. Intra-aortic balloon pump support was initiated in the early postoperative period, and norepinephrine was the only vasoactive agent used before repeated levosimendan administration. Because of persistent low-output physiology, invasive hemodynamic findings, and a previously favorable response to levosimendan, repeated levosimendan infusion was administered. Subsequent reassessment showed improvement in cardiac index, filling pressures, vascular resistance, ventricular function, and biochemical markers of hypoperfusion and organ dysfunction. This case illustrates that repeated levosimendan administration, guided by invasive hemodynamic assessment, was associated with hemodynamic improvement in postcardiotomy cardiogenic shock after CABG. These findings are hypothesis-generating and should be interpreted with caution. Further prospective studies are required to define patient selection criteria, optimal timing, and the role of repeated levosimendan administration in this setting.

Background

Postcardiotomy cardiogenic shock remains one of the most severe complications after cardiac surgery and is associated with high morbidity and mortality [1, 2]. Patients with severely reduced left ventricular ejection fraction undergoing coronary artery bypass grafting (CABG) are at particularly high risk of developing low cardiac output syndrome and refractory circulatory failure [3].

Levosimendan, a calcium sensitizer with inotropic and vasodilatory properties, has been increasingly used in cardiac anesthesia and perioperative cardiac surgical care [4–7]. Its mechanism of action is based on calcium sensitization and ATP-dependent potassium channel modulation, allowing enhancement of myocardial contractility without a substantial increase in myocardial oxygen consumption [8, 9]. In addition to its mechanistic rationale, several clinical studies have suggested potential perioperative hemodynamic benefits of levosimendan in patients with impaired ventricular function undergoing cardiac surgery [5, 6, 10, 11]. Experience from severe low-output heart failure has also contributed to the broader clinical interest in levosimendan as an alternative to conventional catecholamine-based inotropic support [12].

At the same time, most of the perioperative literature concerns single-dose or single-course administration, and robust criteria for repeated levosimendan administration in postcardiotomy cardiogenic shock remain lacking [7, 13]. Although repeated or intermittent levosimendan therapy has been explored in advanced chronic heart failure populations, including randomized and observational studies, these data cannot be directly extrapolated to postcardiotomy patients [14–17]. Furthermore, biomarkers such as NT-proBNP may reflect myocardial stress during the perioperative period, but their interpretation in the immediate postoperative setting remains complex and should be approached cautiously [18].

We present a case of repeated levosimendan administration guided by invasive hemodynamic assessment in a patient who developed postcardiotomy cardiogenic shock after CABG.

Case presentation

A 74-year-old man was admitted with progressive dyspnea and angina at minimal exertion corresponding to Canadian Cardiovascular Society class IV. His medical history was significant for ischemic heart disease and previous percutaneous coronary intervention with stenting of the right coronary artery. Coronary angiography revealed a hemodynamically significant 90% stenosis of the mid-left anterior descending artery and proximal occlusion of the circumflex artery. Preoperative transthoracic echocardiography demonstrated severe left ventricular systolic dysfunction, with left ventricular ejection fraction approximately 20–24%, left ventricular dilatation, and extensive regional wall motion abnormalities involving the posterior, lateral, inferior, and posterior septal walls. The preoperative NT-proBNP level was 8200 pg/mL.

Given the severely reduced left ventricular systolic function, preoperative optimization with levosimendan was performed at 0.1 µg/kg/min over 24 h. Seventy-two hours later, repeat echocardiography showed improvement in left ventricular ejection fraction to 33%, accompanied by partial clinical stabilization. The patient subsequently underwent off-pump coronary artery bypass grafting with two grafts: left internal mammary artery to the left anterior descending artery and a saphenous vein graft to the obtuse marginal branch. Total operative time was 212 min, and the main stabilization phase required for construction of the coronary anastomoses lasted 41 min.

In the early postoperative period, clinical deterioration developed during transfer to the intensive care unit. For this reason, intra-aortic balloon pump support was initiated early after surgery rather than prophylactically during the procedure. During the subsequent postoperative course, arterial hypotension, metabolic acidosis, rising lactate levels, and increasing norepinephrine requirements were observed. Norepinephrine was the only vasoactive agent used; no additional inotropes or vasopressors, including dobutamine, milrinone, adrenaline, or vasopressin, were administered before repeated levosimendan infusion. Baseline postoperative hemodynamic deterioration was reflected by blood pressure 85/55 mmHg, lactate 3.2 mmol/L, norepinephrine requirement 0.10 µg/kg/min, cardiac index 2.1 L/min/m², pulmonary capillary wedge pressure 19 mmHg, and systemic vascular resistance 1600 dyn·s·cm⁻⁵.

By postoperative day 3, the patient developed postcardiotomy cardiogenic shock with progressive hypoperfusion. Lactate increased to 5.1 mmol/L, LVEF decreased to 25%, transaminases rose to 180/215 U/L, and NT-proBNP increased to 15,200 pg/mL. Invasive hemodynamic assessment with a Swan–Ganz catheter demonstrated a cardiac index of 1.8 L/min/m², pulmonary capillary wedge pressure of 22 mmHg, and systemic vascular resistance of 1850 dyn·s·cm⁻⁵. Additional documented hemodynamic parameters at shock peak were consistent with persistent low-output circulatory failure, with mean arterial pressure 55–65 mmHg, central venous/right atrial pressure 12–18 mmHg, mean pulmonary artery pressure 30–40 mmHg, SvO₂ 45–55%, and ScvO₂ 50–60%. Renal and hepatic dysfunction were also present, including creatinine 152 µmol/L, estimated glomerular filtration rate 34 mL/min/1.73 m², total bilirubin 38 µmol/L, and direct bilirubin 17 µmol/L. Troponin level was 18 ng/mL. At this stage, IABP support was maintained in a 1:1 mode without documented limb ischemia or hemolysis. No clinically significant postoperative arrhythmia or evidence of infectious complication was documented during the period of hemodynamic deterioration.

Echocardiographic reassessment provided additional structural and functional characterization. Right ventricular dysfunction was documented, with TAPSE 10 mm, RV S’ 8 cm/s, visually estimated right ventricular ejection fraction 32%, and systolic pulmonary artery pressure 41 mmHg. Left ventricular dimensions remained enlarged, with LVEDD 60 mm and LVESD 42 mm at shock peak, while LV end-diastolic and end-systolic volume indices were 121 and 86 mL/m², respectively. Extensive regional wall motion abnormalities persisted, with akinesis of the basal and mid posterior, lateral, and inferior segments, as well as the posterior septal segment. No severe postoperative valvular lesion was identified: secondary mitral regurgitation was no more than moderate, and tricuspid regurgitation was moderate and associated with right ventricular dysfunction.

Given the prior favorable response to preoperative levosimendan, the absence of an alternative dominant cause of refractory shock, and the invasive hemodynamic profile of persistent low cardiac output with elevated filling pressures, repeated levosimendan infusion was administered six days after the initial dose at the same infusion rate. Following the second infusion, progressive hemodynamic improvement was observed. Cardiac index increased to 2.5 L/min/m², pulmonary capillary wedge pressure decreased to 16 mmHg, and systemic vascular resistance declined to 1350 dyn·s·cm⁻⁵. Mean arterial pressure improved to 70–80 mmHg, central venous/right atrial pressure decreased to 8–12 mmHg, mean pulmonary artery pressure to 25–30 mmHg, SvO₂ increased to 60–70%, and ScvO₂ exceeded 65%. Lactate decreased to 2.0 mmol/L, norepinephrine requirement fell to 0.03 µg/kg/min, and IABP support was subsequently discontinued and removed. Laboratory recovery was also observed, with creatinine decreasing to 104 µmol/L, estimated glomerular filtration rate increasing to 51 mL/min/1.73 m², total bilirubin decreasing to 22 µmol/L, direct bilirubin to 5 µmol/L, and troponin to 2.2 ng/mL. NT-proBNP decreased to 6300 pg/mL.

Follow-up echocardiography after therapy showed improvement in left ventricular ejection fraction to 39%, partial reverse remodeling with LVEDD 58 mm and LVESD 37 mm, and reduction of LV end-diastolic and end-systolic volume indices to 107 and 62 mL/m², respectively. Right ventricular function also improved, with TAPSE 14 mm, RV S’ 11 cm/s, visually estimated right ventricular ejection fraction 42%, and systolic pulmonary artery pressure 34 mmHg. Previously akinetic inferolateral segments became predominantly hypokinetic. The patient was transferred to the cardiology ward on postoperative day 8 and was discharged home on postoperative day 21 in stable condition. Discussion

Postcardiotomy cardiogenic shock remains a major cause of early morbidity and mortality after cardiac surgery, particularly in patients with markedly impaired preoperative ventricular function [1, 2]. In this context, low cardiac output syndrome may be understood as the postoperative hemodynamic pattern of reduced cardiac output, whereas postcardiotomy cardiogenic shock represents a more severe syndrome characterized by persistent low output, hypoperfusion, escalating support requirements, and evolving end-organ dysfunction. In such patients, perioperative optimization strategies are clinically important, but the choice and timing of inotropic support remain challenging [3, 7]. In the present case, the patient had severe ischemic cardiomyopathy with very low preoperative LVEF and subsequently developed a postoperative low-output state with end-organ hypoperfusion, making the clinical context especially high risk.

Levosimendan has several features that make it attractive in this setting. By increasing calcium sensitivity of the contractile apparatus and exerting vasodilatory effects through potassium channel modulation, it may improve cardiac performance without the same degree of increase in myocardial oxygen demand seen with catecholamine inotropes [8, 9]. This pharmacologic profile has supported its use in cardiac anesthesia and perioperative cardiac surgery [4–7]. In addition, prior studies in patients undergoing CABG, including off-pump procedures, have reported favorable effects on perioperative hemodynamics and facilitation of postoperative management [6, 10, 11]. More generally, evidence from severe low-output heart failure and cardiogenic shock literature has contributed to the rationale for considering levosimendan in critically ill patients with depressed cardiac output [12, 13].

However, most available evidence in cardiac surgery relates to a single perioperative infusion rather than repeated administration [5–7, 10, 11]. By contrast, repeated levosimendan treatment has been studied primarily in advanced chronic heart failure, where intermittent administration has been associated with symptomatic and hemodynamic benefits in selected patients [14–17]. These studies are clinically informative but differ substantially from the postcardiotomy setting with regard to timing, mechanisms of circulatory failure, and competing postoperative factors. Therefore, repeated administration in postcardiotomy cardiogenic shock cannot be considered established therapy on the basis of the chronic heart failure literature alone [14–17].

In the present case, invasive hemodynamic monitoring was central not merely for confirming low cardiac output, but for shaping the therapeutic interpretation of the patient’s deterioration. The combination of reduced cardiac index, elevated filling pressures, and increased systemic vascular resistance supported the presence of persistent pump failure with systemic hypoperfusion, rather than isolated vasoplegia or nonspecific postoperative instability. In this regard, invasive monitoring functioned as a decision-support tool in a clinical scenario in which broader cardiogenic shock management principles also emphasize careful hemodynamic phenotyping [1, 2].

Another important aspect is that repeated levosimendan administration in this patient was not preceded by escalation to other conventional inotropes such as dobutamine or milrinone. This should not be interpreted as evidence that levosimendan ought to be used routinely earlier than alternative agents. Rather, the decision in this case was individualized and influenced by several factors: the documented favorable response to the initial preoperative levosimendan infusion, the persistent low-output hemodynamic profile, and the intention to avoid additional catecholamine burden in a patient with severe ventricular dysfunction [7, 12, 13]. We believe that the favorable response in this patient was related to the presence of persistent low-output physiology with elevated filling pressures, the absence of a dominant alternative mechanical or septic cause of shock, and a previously documented favorable response to levosimendan. Escalation to additional mechanical circulatory support was not pursued because the patient remained supported with IABP, had no evidence of a mechanical surgical complication, and demonstrated a hemodynamic profile considered potentially responsive to pharmacologic unloading and inodilator support. The observed improvement after repeated infusion therefore should be interpreted as an association in a selected clinical context rather than proof of therapeutic superiority.

The case also illustrates the importance of cautious interpretation of ancillary biomarkers. NT-proBNP increased substantially during shock progression and decreased after hemodynamic recovery, which is compatible with changing myocardial stress; nevertheless, postoperative natriuretic peptide levels are influenced by multiple perioperative variables and should be regarded as supportive rather than definitive markers of response [4, 18].

Most importantly, this report remains a single-case observation. Although the clinical course suggests that repeated levosimendan administration, guided by invasive hemodynamic assessment, was associated with improvement in circulatory status, causality cannot be established. The value of this case lies in its hypothesis-generating nature and in highlighting a possible framework for patient selection, rather than in establishing efficacy of repeated levosimendan for postcardiotomy cardiogenic shock [2, 7, 13].

Conclusions

This case illustrates that repeated levosimendan administration, guided by invasive hemodynamic assessment, was associated with hemodynamic improvement in postcardiotomy cardiogenic shock after off-pump CABG. These findings are hypothesis-generating and should be interpreted with caution. Further prospective studies are required to define patient selection criteria, optimal timing, and the role of repeated levosimendan administration in this setting.
