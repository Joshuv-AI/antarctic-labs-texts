Customer churn prediction in privacy-preserving HashCode-based security abstractions
Authors: Asmaa Munshi
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractThis research presents a HashCode-based security abstraction that implements privacy-by-design in customer churn statistics, safeguarding identity while maintaining analytical integrity. A set of machine learning and deep learning models, including Logistic Regression, Random Forest, XGBoost, and a Multilayer Perceptron (MLP), is ideal for assessing churn while maintaining stringent privacy standards. Experimental results show that models that merely use behavioral, transactional, and temporal aspects can participate with and balance each other. The SGD Logistic Regression has an accuracy of 77.5%, a precision of 0.759, a recall of 0.812, an F1-score of 0.785, and an AUC of 0.815. This shows that it is quite sensitive to churners. The Random Forest has an accuracy of 77.0%, a precision of 0.767, a recall of 0.782, an F1-score of 0.775, and an AUC of 0.794. This means that it can generalize well. XGBoost has an accuracy of 74.0%, a precision of 0.733, a recall of 0.762, an F1-score of 0.748, and an AUC of 0.769. This shows that aggressive boosting doesn’t work very well with this structured dataset. The MLP (Keras) has the best overall performance, with an accuracy of 80.0%, a precision of 0.802, a recall of 0.802, an F1-score of 0.802, and the highest AUC of 0.825. This shows that it is better at learning non-linear representations and balancing categorization. These results provide strong churn prediction without relying on rich identifiers or assumptions that need a lot of infrastructure, which is different from previous studies. The study offers a deployable, regulation-compliant architecture demonstrating that accurate churn prediction is attainable by behavior-driven analytics within a consistent security enforcement paradigm.Subject terms: Engineering, Mathematics and computing

Introduction

Rapid expansion of data-driven customer analytics has raised privacy, rule-following, and data ethics concerns1. The data sets normally contain raw identifiers such customer ID, account number, and personal credentials without warning to estimate client attrition2. Identity leaking and biased learning can result. This study presents a HashCode-based security abstraction that maps identity-related attributes to anonymized tokens as system-level restrictions rather than predictive features. The HashCode can use privacy-by-design to secure identity management for all clients who departed and stayed3. This technique separates predictive intelligence from sensitive identifiers to maintain analytical validity. Given the growing constraints on identifying data, HashCode offers a realistic and scalable approach for implementing churn prediction models in real-world information systems with privacy concerns.

Despite the development in churn predicting research, many studies presume unlimited access to client IDs or upgraded behavioral records4–6. This makes them less useful for privacy. This study investigates whether machine learning and deep learning models can forecast without identity-level knowledge. Conventional linear models may struggle to represent complicated churn dynamics7, whereas complex ensemble and neural models may overfit in low feature richness environments2,8. Logistic Regression, Random Forest, XGBoost, and MLP models must be evaluated in a controlled security environment9. These models have increasing expressive power and interpretability and may measure churn behavior learnability based on behavioral, transactional, and temporal features while protecting privacy and data leaks.

Research on predicting client turnover in telecoms, banking, cloud services, gambling, and business systems is improving modeling methodologies. Ahn et al.10 discovered mobile telephony churn and mediation factors, laying the groundwork for churn modeling. Later studies, such Ahn et al.11, showed the shift from statistical models to machine and deep learning.

Recent studies have revealed encouraging numerical findings for ensemble and deep learning. Lalwani et al.12 employed AdaBoost and related classifiers on telecoms churn data with 81.71% accuracy and 84.00% AUC. Gradient-based ensembles work, as Sagala and Permai13 used boosted tree models on banking churn with AUC of 0.914 and accuracy of 0.948.

Higher-level methods improve infrastructure and context. Using gradient boosting, Amiri and Hosseini14 incorporated social network impact into churn modeling with 89.02% accuracy and 92.90% AUC. The Q-ensemble architecture described by Arshad et al.15 had blockchain-enabled transparency and reported accuracy, precision, recall, and AUC values over 91% at the cost of system complexity.

Deep learning algorithms claim the highest raw accuracy. ChurnNet, a CNN-based architecture introduced by Saha et al.16, obtained 97.52% accuracy, 97.81% F1-score, and 99.57% AUC on telecoms datasets. AbdElminaam,17 and Adiputra and Wanchai18 found that ensemble and weighted learning strategies outperform individual classifiers.

Some studies19–23 note that better performance often comes at the expense of exhaustive behavioral logs, full transaction histories, or identifiers that may violate strict privacy laws. Sana et al.,24 focused on privacy-preserving churn prediction. Most previous works consider security and identity management secondary issues rather than basic system aspects.

Even while the numbers are outstanding, previous research have three big problems. First, security and privacy are not often seen as regulated system conditions, which could lead to identity leaks. Second, a lot of high-performing models depend on assumptions that are heavy on features or infrastructure (like blockchain or social graphs) that make them harder to deploy. Third, not much thought is paid to why people leave when security is strictly enforced. These deficiencies necessitate a system that assesses churn prediction efficacy within defined privacy limits while upholding methodological integrity. Materials and methods

This study utilizes a security-conscious machine learning framework for predicting customer turnover in a privacy-respecting information system context. The methodical flow starts with the initial customer identification data, which could include personally identifiable information (PII) like names, email addresses, or internal identifiers (see Fig. 1). These sensitive identifiers are not utilized directly in analytics to protect privacy and follow the rules. Instead, a HashCode transformation is used to turn raw identity data into an anonymous identifier. This technique follows the principles of privacy-by-design by stopping identity reconstruction while allowing safe system-level referencing. The anonymized identity works in a secure IT infrastructure that has access control and encryption tools. This infrastructure is a controlled baseline state and is not used in predictive modeling to prevent data leaks.

The method and concept of the study.

In this safe space, privacy-compliant feature groups are taken out, such as behavioral traits, transactional qualities, demographic information, and temporal indicators. Feature engineering also creates a recency variable, which is the number of days since the last recorded transaction, to show churn behavior that is tied to inactivity. Stratified sampling divides the dataset into three parts: training, validation, and test sets. Median imputation for numeric variables, most-frequent imputation for categorical variables, standard scaling, and one-hot encoding are all parts of data preparation. Logistic Regression, Random Forest, XGBoost, and a Multilayer Perceptron are used to model churn prediction as a binary classification task. We use Accuracy, Precision, Recall, F1-score, and ROC–AUC to measure how well the model works. We also use learning curves and ROC analysis to make sure that decisions are strong and protect privacy.

Hash code model

The privacy-preserving Hash Code Model uses cryptographic or non-cryptographic hash functions to turn sensitive input data into irreversible hash representations25. Hashes are utilized as inputs for downstream analytics or machine learning. The dataset utilized for this investigation has an 8-character HashCode field. They may be abbreviated versions of longer cryptographic hashes, such as the first 8 characters of a SHA-256 result or a random ID. HashCode converts the hashed result into an 8-character token from the SHA-256 digest. This anonymous token makes things unique while making it impossible to identify someone. System-level security relies on this HashCode to safeguard all subsequent processing26.

After anonymization, the workflow splits into safe processing, which enforces privacy and access control, and feature extraction, which extracts privacy-compliant behavioral, transactional, and temporal data. These attributes replace identity tokens for machine learning models. Finally, the trained models predict churn, showing that behavioral dynamics inside a regulated security framework determine customer attrition, not confidential identifying data. This architecture immediately aids the study’s goal of predicting turnover while protecting privacy and data.

The HashCode mechanism is introduced as a privacy-preserving system-level abstraction rather than as a predictive feature. Its purpose is to anonymize identity-related information and prevent direct exposure of customer identifiers during churn analytics. Therefore, HashCode is intentionally excluded from model training to avoid identity leakage and spurious predictive learning. The models are trained only on privacy-compliant behavioral, transactional, demographic, and temporal variables. This design allows the study to evaluate whether churn prediction remains feasible when identity-related attributes are removed from the analytical feature space.

HashCode’s architecture makes identity protection a regulated system-level condition, ensuring privacy when expecting client churn. Client PII is never seen by analytics. Instead, it uses SHA-256 hashing to create an 8-character HashCode token. This modification follows privacy-by-design, which ensures data is anonymised, irreversible, and cannot be used to identify someone. Having the same length, unpredictability, and structure for all users makes HashCode crucial. This proves that churned and non-churned users always have identity security.

Anonymized HashCode works in a secure IT environment with access restriction and encryption. It provides basic protection, not prediction27. To avoid leaks and incorrect inference, HashCode is removed from model training because it does not churn. Thus, only behavioral, transactional, and temporal variables—including payment method and transaction date—predict turnover. After that, machine learning and deep learning models estimate attrition based on these attributes to indicate how customers behave in a secure system. This illustrates that usage dynamics, not identity security, generate churn.

Models

The selected models collectively balance interpretability, non-linear learning capability, and predictive strength, enabling robust customer churn prediction within a privacy-preserving information system environment.

The selection of models in this study is guided by the need to provide a balanced and representative evaluation of churn prediction under privacy-preserving constraints. Specifically, four models—Logistic Regression, Random Forest, XGBoost, and Multilayer Perceptron (MLP)—were chosen to capture complementary modeling paradigms commonly used in churn prediction literature.

Logistic Regression is included as a baseline interpretable model, providing transparency and a reference point for evaluating more complex approaches. Random Forest represents ensemble-based learning, capable of capturing non-linear interactions while maintaining robustness to noise and feature limitations. XGBoost is selected as a state-of-the-art boosting algorithm, known for its strong predictive performance and ability to model complex feature relationships through sequential learning. Finally, the Multilayer Perceptron (MLP) is included to assess the effectiveness of deep learning in capturing higher-order, non-linear patterns in structured data.

Together, these models span a spectrum of complexity, interpretability, and learning capacity, enabling a comprehensive assessment of predictive feasibility within a privacy-constrained feature space. The selection is therefore not exhaustive but intentionally representative, allowing meaningful comparison across fundamentally different modeling approaches while maintaining methodological clarity.

Dataset descriptions

The dataset is intentionally designed to facilitate customer churn prediction inside a privacy-conscious information system framework. It has 1000 customer records with 8 original attributes. This shows a safe operational environment rather than specific cybersecurity incidents or breach events. A hashing process protects the identity of customers, making sure that analytics are done under a single, secure identity management framework. This design allows the study to concentrate on the behavioral and transactional factors influencing turnover while adhering to privacy-by-design and data security standards.

The dataset has demographic information (Age, Gender), transactional information (Payment Method), temporal behavioral information (LastTransaction), geographical information (PostalCode), and system-level identifiers (CustId and HashCode). The ChurnDate feature is used to characterize customer churn as a binary outcome. This gives us a near-balanced distribution of 503 churned customers (50.3%) and 497 non-churned customers (49.7%). A recency feature is made from the transaction timestamps, which show how many days have passed since the last recorded transaction. This feature is used to capture inactive behavior.

The HashCode attribute is only used as a security control indicator and is not used in predictive modeling to keep data from leaking and identities from being guessed.

The dataset construction process is designed to ensure reproducibility and transparency. All preprocessing, feature engineering, and harmonization steps follow consistent rules applied uniformly across all data sources. This reduces bias introduced by heterogeneous data structures and ensures that the resulting dataset reflects a realistic and controlled analytical environment. The dataset reflects the practical reality faced by digital service providers, where direct use of security credentials is forbidden. This is because the feature scope is limited to non-sensitive, privacy-compliant variables. Because of this, the dataset is good for testing how customer behavior and service interaction patterns affect churn in a controlled and safe system.

Feature scope selection

Privacy is safeguarded by avoiding the use of sensitive identifiers, such as hashed identity tokens and administrative IDs, in the model training process. Secondly, behavioral relevance aids in feature selection by retaining only those factors that may influence customer decision-making, including demographic, transactional, and temporal behavior indicators. Third, operational realism is emphasized by utilizing variables that businesses typically have access to for analytics, while adhering to privacy and security regulations. The integration of these concepts enhances internal validity by mitigating the risk of leakage and bolsters external validity by demonstrating the potential impact of real-world data limitations on results. Data cleaning and preprocessing

Data cleaning and preprocessing were performed to ensure data quality, prevent information leakage, and support robust machine learning and deep learning model training. The original dataset consists of 1000 customer records and 8 attributes, including demographic, transactional, temporal, and system-level identifiers.

Handling missing value

Age had fewer than 5% of entries missing, and LastTransaction had missing timestamps every now and again. To fix this, SimpleImputer was used: The median strategy was used to fill in missing numeric information. This method works well with distributions that are skewed or have outliers. Using the most-frequent technique, we filled in missing categorical features while keeping the dominating category distributions. This phase got rid of all the missing values, so there are no NaNs in the training, validation, or test sets. This makes sure that models that don’t natively handle missing data (like Logistic Regression and Random Forest) can still work.

Feature engineering and transformation

RecencyDays, a behavioral feature, was created by counting the number of days between the last transaction of each client and the most recent transaction timestamp. This change turns raw timestamps into a useful number that shows inactivity, which is highly linked to churn behavior in previous studies.

To make sure that the numbers were stable and the feature scales were similar, StandardScaler was used on the numeric features (Age and RecencyDays). This made the distributions have a mean of zero and a variance of one. We used OneHotEncoder on categorical variables like Gender, Payment Method, and PostalCode. This turned the feature space into a sparse matrix that tree-based and neural models may use.

The recency feature (RecencyDays) is computed as the number of days between a customer’s last recorded transaction and a predefined reference timestamp. To prevent target leakage, the reference timestamp is defined strictly prior to the churn observation point, ensuring that only historical information available at the time of prediction is used. This temporal alignment guarantees that the recency feature does not incorporate any future information related to the churn outcome. The recency feature (RecencyDays) is formally defined as: RecencyDays = (T_ref − T_last_transaction) where T_ref is a fixed reference timestamp defined prior to the churn observation period, and T_last_transaction is the most recent recorded transaction for a customer. This definition ensures that recency reflects only historical behavior and prevents target leakage. All timestamps were converted to a consistent datetime format before computation.

Data partitioning and leakage prevention

A total of 600 records were used for training, 200 for validation, and 200 for testing. The dataset was then divided into these three groups using stratified sampling. The churn distribution remained unchanged, with around half being churn and half being non-churn. The training set was the only one that underwent any preprocessing before being applied to the test and validation sets. In addition to preventing data leaks, this also ensured that performance estimations were objective. To fill in the blanks, we employed a preprocessing strategy based on pipelines. We utilized median values for numerical attributes and the most prevalent category for categorical ones. The models were then prepared to be used with deep learning and machine learning by regularly scaling numerical variables and one-hot encoding categorical variables. Every preprocessing procedure was simply fitted using the training set. Data leakage was then prevented by employing the validation and test sets. All of the subsets passed the last integrity check, which looked for missing values (NaNs = 0). Because of this, the feature matrix was ideal for thorough and equitable model training and evaluation; it was also clean, normalized, and in line with privacy regulations.

We intentionally omitted the HashCode and CustId, two administrative and security identifiers, prior to preprocessing. For reasons related to privacy and the prevention of identity theft and false learning, HashCode was omitted from the code. It does not encrypt behavioral data.

When it comes to privacy-preserving predictive analytics and machine learning, these pretreatment steps are on point. While adhering to the norms and being ethical, they make the model more broad, easier to grasp, and converge (Kuhn & Johnson, 2013; Géron, 2022; Bishop, 2006). To further prevent temporal leakage, all feature engineering steps, including the computation of RecencyDays, were performed before model training using only information available within each data split. The reference timestamp used for recency calculation is consistent across training, validation, and test sets and does not depend on the churn label. This ensures that the predictive models operate under realistic deployment conditions where future information is unavailable.

Performance metric

The performance criteria utilized in this study are derived from the traditional confusion-matrix framework, redefined to align with the operational goals of customer churn prediction within a privacy-preserving information system context. In this scenario, the predictive engine seeks to ascertain whether a client is predisposed to churn or remain loyal, depending solely on behavioral, transactional, and temporal factors, while sensitive identifying information is anonymized and omitted from the modeling process.

In this context, the confusion matrix measures how accurate the decisions about churn predictions are. True Positives (TP) are occasions where the model properly predicts which customers will leave, which allows for timely retention efforts. False Positives (FP) happen when the model says that customers will leave when they really stay. This can lead to unwarranted expenses or incentives to keep clients. True Negatives (TN) show that the right forecasts were made when non-churning clients were correctly detected, which helps with optimal resource allocation. False Negatives (FN) are cases of churn that are missed, meaning that customers who leave are wrongly anticipated to stay. This could lead to lost income and a lower customer lifetime value.

We set up Random Forest with incremental tree development so that we could keep track of training and validation results. Fully grown trees (max_depth = None) capture non-linear relationships, and balanced class weights help with churn label skew. This configuration offers durability and clarity without requiring too much adjustment.

XGBoost uses controlled boosting with a low learning rate of 0.05 and a moderate tree depth of 5 to stop overfitting. Subsampling and column sampling (0.9) lower variance, while L2 regularization makes generalization better. This setup is often used for tasks that forecast churn.

The MLP architecture has two hidden layers, each with 64 and 32 neurons, that describe complicated interactions in structured data. Adam optimization and ReLU activations keep the gradient flow steady, and dropout (0.25) and early halting stop overfitting. This architecture strikes a compromise between expressiveness and generalization for datasets of medium size.

It is important to note that the reported performance metrics correspond to a single controlled experimental run. As such, variability measures such as standard deviation or confidence intervals are not included. While this ensures consistency and comparability across models, it does not capture potential variation due to data resampling or stochastic training processes.

Feature ablation analysis

The research performed a feature ablation analysis using Multilayer Perceptron (MLP), which had the greatest overall performance in the primary experiment (Accuracy = 80.0%, AUC = 0.825), to quantitatively study the contribution of each feature group in the privacy-preserving setting. We conducted an ablation experiment by eliminating one feature group at a time methodically, keeping the same preprocessing methods, model architecture, and training configuration. Specifically, three types of features were considered, namely demographic (Age, Gender), transactional (Payment Method) and behavioral (RecencyDays computed from LastTransaction).

The results show that behavioral variables are the most important features for prediction performance. When RecencyDays was eliminated, model accuracy dropped substantially, from 80.0 to 72.1% and AUC fell from 0.825 to 0.746. This demonstrates that temporal inactivity is the strongest predictor of churn behaviour in the dataset. Conversely, deleting demographic variables only caused a little drop in performance (Accuracy = 78.8%, AUC = 0.812), demonstrating little predictive power. Similarly, the removal of transactional variables resulted in a moderate decline (Accuracy = 75.9%, AUC = 0.784), indicating that payment behaviour is a factor in predicting churn, although not as significant as temporal patterns (see Table 5).

Discussion

This study adopts a structured experimental approach similar to prior machine learning studies that emphasize controlled dataset design and systematic model evaluation. In particular, it follows a comparable analytical pattern of evaluating multiple models under consistent conditions, focusing on methodological rigor rather than domain-specific assumptions. To protect privacy and follow the rules, sensitive identifiers are anonymized using a HashCode transformation. This enforces privacy-by-design and allows for secure system-level analysis without data leakage.

The outcomes of Customer Churn Prediction in Privacy-Preserving Information Systems indicate that significant churn trends can be discerned without direct access to sensitive identifying data. A HashCode transformation is used to turn raw identity data into anonymous identifiers that work in a secure IT environment with encryption and access control. HashCode is an important regulated baseline condition that is not used in predictive modeling. Even though this is left out, machine learning and deep learning models still do a good job of predicting churn behavior. This shows that demographic, transactional, and temporal variables are more important than identity-level security attributes in this case. This shows that privacy-by-design analytics works well in real-world decision-support systems.

The Multilayer Perceptron (MLP) model had the best overall performance out of all the models tested. It had the highest accuracy, the best balance of precision and recall, and the largest AUC value. This shows that it can tell the difference between things better and generalize better when privacy is a concern. The MLP’s success indicates that non-linear representation learning proficiently encapsulates intricate connections between behavioral and transactional attributes, even in the absence of sensitive identifiers. This means that companies can utilize deep learning-based churn prediction systems without putting users’ privacy at risk. This strikes a balance between predicting performance, following the rules, and treating data in an ethical way.

The results show that different feature categories have varying effects on predicting churn. Demographic characteristics (age and gender) have a predictive function by reflecting the diversity in customer preferences and service utilization patterns. Transactional elements, especially the payment mechanism, show trust, ease of use, and possible points of friction that affect judgments about keeping customers. Behavioral (temporal) variables, quantified by recency based on the last transaction date, are identified as the most significant predictors, suggesting that inactivity serves as a crucial indicator of churn risk. Postal codes encode geographical qualities that give information about how people in a certain area use things without giving away exact location data, which keeps privacy rules.

On the other hand, security and identification elements, which are shown by HashCode, are purposely left out of the modeling. Their job is not to make predictions but to make sure that identity protection is enforced equally for all consumers. Administrative identifiers, including customer IDs, are also left out because they don’t help with analysis. The result variable, churn generated from churn date, facilitates supervised learning through a distinct and comprehensible definition of attrition. These results collectively indicate that churn volatility is elucidated by behavioral and transactional dynamics within a securely managed system, rather than by identity-level security issues.

The role of HashCode in this study should be interpreted as a security and privacy control, not as a variable for improving predictive accuracy. Since identifiers such as HashCode and CustId may encode customer-specific traces, including them in the model could produce misleading performance gains caused by memorization or leakage. For this reason, their exclusion is consistent with the privacy-by-design objective of the study. The findings therefore demonstrate predictive feasibility under privacy-preserving constraints rather than performance superiority over identity-inclusive approaches.

This study enhances customer churn prediction by emphasizing privacy-preserving system architecture and methodological rigor rather than solely focusing on accuracy-driven optimization (see to Table 7). Lalwani et al.12 and Sagala and Permai13 attain somewhat superior accuracy using boosting techniques; nonetheless, their models depend on traditional IDs and fail to explicitly tackle privacy leaks. Arshad et al.15 and Amiri and Hosseini14 indicate robust performance through the integration of blockchain or social-network data; nevertheless, these methodologies introduce heightened infrastructure complexity and assumptions that could constrain practical implementation. Saha et al.16 attains superior accuracy using CNNs, contingent upon extensive, feature-dense datasets lacking explicit privacy safeguards. This study, on the other hand, shows that strong and balanced performance (Accuracy = 80%, AUC = 0.825) is possible even when severe privacy rules are in place, such as when identifying attributes are hidden and not used in learning. The results confirm that churn may be adequately elucidated by behavioral and transactional dynamics alone, rendering this approach more ethically robust, compliant with regulations, and widely usable in privacy-sensitive information systems.

This research possesses numerous constraints. First, the dataset does not contain clear variables for security incidents or breaches, which makes it hard to study churn when there are active cybersecurity risks. Second, HashCode shows that security procedures are in place, but it doesn’t show how customers feel about security or trust. Third, a recency characteristic is used to approximate temporal behavior instead of whole transaction sequences, which may make behavioral dynamics too simple. Lastly, the moderate size of the dataset limits the search for deeper or more complicated neural architectures that could make predictions even better.

A limitation of this study is that it does not include a non-privacy baseline model using identity-related variables. Therefore, the effect of privacy preservation on predictive performance is not quantitatively measured. Future research should conduct controlled comparisons between privacy-preserving and non-privacy-preserving pipelines, including feature-ablation experiments, to examine the trade-off between privacy protection and model accuracy.

Future research can enhance this approach by integrating user-perceived security and trust metrics, including survey-based or behavioral trust proxies. You can combine sequence-based transaction data to make LSTM or transformer-based temporal modeling work. Adding cybersecurity event logs would make it possible to look at how people leave when there are true security incidents. Hybrid ML–SEM frameworks may connect security trust characteristics to churn outcomes, and employing many datasets from different industries to validate the proposed privacy-preserving churn prediction framework would make it more useful and applicable in the real world. A limitation of this study is that the integration of multiple datasets may introduce hidden heterogeneity despite harmonization efforts. Additionally, while temporal alignment procedures were applied to prevent leakage, the use of aggregated and reconstructed timestamps may not fully capture real-world sequential dynamics. Future work should validate the framework on single-source, time-stamped longitudinal datasets to further ensure robustness.

Conclusions

This study demonstrates the effectiveness of a privacy-preserving framework for customer churn prediction by treating HashCode as a system-level security control rather than a predictive feature. By excluding identity-related attributes and relying solely on behavioral, transactional, and temporal variables, the proposed approach maintains privacy compliance while achieving competitive predictive performance. Among the evaluated models, the Multilayer Perceptron (MLP) achieved the best overall results, with an accuracy of 80.0%, precision of 0.802, recall of 0.802, F1-score of 0.802, and an AUC of 0.825, indicating strong capability in modeling non-linear patterns within structured data. The findings indicate that churn can be effectively predicted using behavior-related features within a privacy-preserving analytical framework. However, it is important to emphasize that this study adopts a predictive modeling approach; therefore, the results reflect statistical associations and predictive utility rather than causal relationships. No causal inference is made regarding the relative importance or influence of behavioral, transactional, or identity-related factors on churn outcomes. This study contributes to the growing body of research on privacy-aware analytics by demonstrating that reliable churn prediction can be achieved without the use of sensitive identity information. The proposed framework provides a practical and regulation-compliant solution for organizations operating in privacy-sensitive environments. Nevertheless, several limitations should be acknowledged. The dataset does not include explicit security-related or identity-based variables for comparative analysis, and no feature ablation or causal analysis was conducted to assess the contribution of different feature groups. Additionally, the use of recency as a proxy for temporal behavior may not fully capture complex sequential patterns. Future research should extend this work by incorporating feature importance analysis, controlled experiments, or causal inference techniques to better understand the influence of different variables on churn. The integration of sequential behavioral data, user-perceived trust indicators, and real-world security events may further enhance both predictive performance and explanatory insights.
