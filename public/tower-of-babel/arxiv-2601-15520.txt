Colour ratio in Prim’s ranking of bipartite graphs Thanks: Supported by the EPSRC EP/W033585/1 grant
Félix Kahane and Minmin Wang

Abstract
We consider a complete bipartite graph of size n n endowed with i.i.d. uniform edge weights and run Prim’s Algorithm to obtain a ranking of its vertices. Let ρ k ( n ) \rho^{(n)}_{k} be the proportion of black vertices among the first k k vertices in this ranking. We characterise the limit behaviour of ρ k ( n ) \rho^{(n)}_{k} as both n n and k k tend to infinity. Our results show that in general the limit of ρ k ( n ) \rho^{(n)}_{k} , when existing, differs from the overall proportion of the black vertices in the graph. AMS 2010 subject classifications : Primary 60C05, 05C80. Secondary 60J80, 60F15, 60G99. Keywords : Prim’s Algorithm, invasion percolation, bipartite graph, colour ratio

Introduction and main results

This work concerns minimum spanning tree.
Let
G=(V,E)
be a connected graph and suppose that each edge
e\in E
is associated with an
edge weight

u_{e}\in(0,\infty)
. We will call
(G,(u_{e})_{e\in E})
a
weighted graph
. For a subgraph
G^{\prime}
of
G
, the
weight of
G^{\prime}
is the sum of the edge weights over all the edges contained in
G^{\prime}
.
A
minimum spanning tree
(MST) of the weighted graph
(G,(u_{e})_{e\in E})
is a subgraph
T
of
G
that has the minimal weight among all the connected subgraphs of
G
on the vertex set
V
. It is not difficult to see that
T
is necessarily a tree, i.e. cycle-free. If the edge weights are all distinct, then there is a unique minimum spanning tree.

When
G
is
K_{n}
, the complete graph of
n
vertices, and the edge weights are i.i.d. random variables, the corresponding minimum spanning tree has been extensively studied in the literature; see
[
10
,
12
,
2
]
to name only a few.
Related models are also abundant, including MST of regular graphs
[
6
]
, of the hypercubes
[
17
]
, lattices
[
11
]
, as well as the Euclidean MST
[
5
]
among others.

We are interested in the minimum spanning tree of a complete bipartite graph.
We write
|A|
for the cardinality of a finite set
A
. Let
n_{w},n_{b}
be two natural numbers and
n=n_{b}+n_{w}
. From now on, we take
G
to be the complete bipartite graph
K_{n_{b},n_{w}}
on the vertex set
V_{n}=V^{b}_{n}\cup V^{w}_{n}
with
|V^{b}_{n}|=n_{b}
and
|V^{w}_{n}|=n_{w}
.
In the sequel, we will refer to vertices from
V^{b}_{n}
(resp. vertices from
V^{w}_{n}
) as
black vertices
(resp.
white vertices
).
We will be interested in the case where the size of the graph tends to infinity with a fixed colour ratio. More specifically, we assume that

\exists\,\theta\in(0,1):\quad\frac{n_{b}}{n}\xrightarrow{n\to\infty}\theta.

(1)

Denote by
E_{n}=\{e=\{v,w\},v\in V^{b}_{n},w\in V^{w}_{n}\}
the edge set of
K_{n_{b},n_{w}}
and let
\{U_{e}:e\in E_{n}\}
be a collection of i.i.d random variables with a common uniform distribution on
(0,1)
. We observe that with probability 1,
U_{e}
’s are all distinct. The (a.s. unique) minimum spanning tree of
(K_{n_{b},n_{w}},\{U_{e}:e\in E_{n}\})
can be identified using Prim’s Algorithm as follows.

Algorithm 1

(Prim’s Algorithm)
.

Given
K_{n_{b},n_{w}}=(V_{n},E_{n})
and the edge weights
\{U_{e}:e\in E_{n}\}
, define a sequence of vertices
(\sigma(k))_{1\leq k\leq n}
and a sequence of edges
(e_{k})_{1\leq k\leq n-1}
as follows.

Step 1.
Let
\sigma(1)
be a uniform element of
V_{n}
.

Step
\boldsymbol{k\geq 2}
.
Given
\Sigma(k-1):=\{\sigma(i):1\leq i\leq k-1\}
and
\{e_{i}:1\leq i\leq k-2\}
, let
e_{k-1}
be the a.s. unique edge that satisfies

U_{e_{k-1}}=\min\big\{U_{e}:e=\{v,v^{\prime}\},v\in\Sigma(k-1),v^{\prime}\in V_{n}\setminus\Sigma(k-1)\big\}.

Set
\sigma(k)
to be the endpoint of
e_{k-1}
that does not belong to
\Sigma(k-1)
.

Stop at step
\boldsymbol{n}
and return the graph
T_{n}
on the edge set
\{e_{k}:1\leq k\leq n-1\}
.

For
1\leq k\leq n
, denote by
T_{k}
the graph with the vertex set
\Sigma(k)
and edge set
\{e_{i}:1\leq i\leq k-1\}
. It is not difficult to see that
T_{k}
is a connected graph with
k
vertices and
k-1
edges, i.e. a tree. Algorithm
1
can be described as creating a subgraph
T_{k+1}
from
T_{k}
by adding a new vertex with the minimum increase of weights, for
1\leq k<n
.

It is known that
T_{n}
is the minimum spanning tree of
(K_{n_{b},n_{w}},\{U_{e}:e\in E_{n}\})
(
[
18
]
). Furthermore, Algorithm
1
also produces an ordered sequence
(\sigma(k))_{k\in[n]}
from the
n
vertices of
K_{n_{b},n_{w}}
, which we will refer to as the
Prim sequence
of the vertices of
K_{n_{b},n_{w}}
, as well as a sequence of
n-1
edges
(e_{k})_{k\in[n-1]}
, called
Prim edges
in the sequel. In the same terminology,
k
will be referred to as
Prim’s rank
or simply the

of
\sigma(k)
.

For
k\in[n]
, we denote

\Sigma^{b}(k)=\Sigma(k)\cap V^{b}_{n}\quad\text{and}\quad\Sigma^{w}(k)=\Sigma(k)\cap V^{w}_{n},

(2)

the respective subsets of black and white vertices of
T_{k}
. Our main object of interest is the following random variable that represents the proportion of black vertices in
T_{k}
:

\rho^{(n)}_{k}:=\frac{|\Sigma^{b}(k)|}{k}.

(3)

Clearly,
\rho^{(n)}_{n}
is simply the overall proportion of black vertices.
The aim of this work is to investigate the asymptotic behaviors of
\rho^{(n)}_{k}
as both

and

grow to infinity, possibly at different speeds. Recall from (
1
) that the overall proportion of black vertices converges to
\theta
.
However, our main results below show that unless

, i.e. asymptotic parity for black vs white vertex numbers, we will observe a different proportion than
\theta
along the Prim sequence. More specifically, we distinguish two regimes: the
sublinear regime
where
k\to\infty
but
k=o(n)
as

, and the

where

grows linearly with

Theorem 1

(Sublinear regime)
.

Assume that (
1
) holds.
Denote
\gamma_{\theta}=\sqrt{\frac{1-\theta}{\theta}}
.
Let
(\kappa_{n})_{n\geq 1}
be a sequence of positive integers that tends to infinity and satisfies

as

\rho^{(n)}_{\kappa_{n}}\xrightarrow{n\to\infty}\frac{1}{1+\gamma_{\theta}}=\frac{\sqrt{\theta}}{\sqrt{\theta}+\sqrt{1-\theta}}\quad\text{in probability}.

(4)

Theorem 2

(Linear regime)
.

Assume that (
1
) holds.
Let
\alpha_{\theta}=(\theta(1-\theta))^{-1/2}
. For each

\left\{\begin{array}[]{cl}\rho\ell&=\theta(1-e^{-\alpha_{\theta}\lambda(1-\rho)\ell})\\

Then

is a bijection from
(1,\infty)
to
(0,1)
. Denote by

the inverse function. For each

\rho^{(n)}_{\lfloor sn\rfloor}\xrightarrow{n\to\infty}\rho\circ\ell^{-1}(s)\quad\text{in probability}.

(6)

\text{for }\theta\in\Big(0,\frac{1}{2}\Big):\theta<\frac{1}{1+\gamma_{\theta}}<\frac{1}{2},\quad\text{and for }\theta\in\Big(\frac{1}{2},1\Big):\frac{1}{2}<\frac{1}{1+\gamma_{\theta}}<\theta.

Remark 1

(Influence of the starting vertex)
.

In Algorithm
1
, we have taken
\sigma(1)
to be a uniform vertex. Since under Assumption (
1
), the probability of
\sigma(1)
being a black vertex converges to a non-zero quantity, both limits in (
4
) and (
6
) still hold if
\sigma(1)
is chosen to be a uniform black (resp. white) vertex. Finally, by exchangeability, Theorems

and

Remark 2

(Interpretation in invasion percolation)
.

Prim’s Algorithm is equivalent to the model of invasion percolation (
[
20
,
19
]
). The model is well-defined for a general weighted graph, but let us take the case of
(K_{n_{b},n_{w}},(U_{e})_{e\in E_{n}})
to illustrate. In the context of invasion percolation on
K_{n_{b},n_{w}}
, the focus is on the sequence of subgraphs
(T_{k})_{k\in[n]}
, produced by Algorithm
1
. The evolution of
(T_{k})_{k\in[n]}
is meant to represent a growing cluster inside a disordered medium, always choosing the easiest next location to invade. As such,
(\rho^{(n)}_{k})_{k\in[n]}
tracks the proportion of black vertices in the cluster during the percolation.

Our approach to the problem is based upon a connection between Prim’s Algorithm and the Bernoulli bond percolation of
K_{n_{b},n_{w}}
. In fact, as we shall see, the pair
(\ell(\lambda),\rho(\lambda))
in Theorem
2
is linked to the size of the giant component in the bond percolation. Let us briefly describe this connection here.

Let
p\in(0,1)
and let
G(n_{b},n_{w},p)
be the subgraph of
K_{n_{b},n_{w}}
with the vertex set
V_{n}
and the edge set

E_{n}(p)=\{e\in E_{n}:U_{e}\leq p\}.

(7)

Since the edge weights are i.i.d. uniformly distributed, each edge of
K_{n_{b},n_{w}}
is present in
G(n_{b},n_{w},p)
independently with probability
p
.
The graph
G(n_{b},n_{w},p)
can have several connected components, and the vertex sets of these components have a simple representation in the Prim sequence
(\sigma(i))_{i\in[n]}
. Indeed, recall the Prim edges
(e_{i})_{i\in[n-1]}
and let

J_{1}=\inf\big\{i\in[n-1]:U_{e_{i}}>p\}\wedge n;

then the vertex set of the component of
G(n_{b},n_{w},p)
containing
\sigma(1)
is given by
\{\sigma(i):1\leq i\leq J_{1}\}
. More generally, for
j\geq 2
, define

J_{j}=\inf\{i>J_{j-1}:U_{e_{i}}>p\}\wedge n\quad\text{and}\quad m=\inf\{j\geq 1:J_{j}=n\}.

Proposition
4
below says that there are
m
connected components in
G(n_{b},n_{w},p)
, and the vertex set of the
j
th component is given by

\mathcal{C}_{j}(p):=\{\sigma(i):J_{j-1}+1\leq i\leq J_{j}\},\quad 1\leq j\leq m,

(8)

with the convention that
J_{0}=0
. This shows that the connected components of
G(n_{b},n_{w},p)
always consist of vertices of
consecutive
Prim’s ranks.

The above representation of the connected components as intervals in Prim’s ranking is known for various graphs and has led to many interesting applications; see for instance
[
3
,
15
,
9
,
2
,
11
,
1
]
.
Its current form for the complete bipartite graph underpins our proof of
Theorem
2
, where we tune
p
to a value in the supercritical regime that will match the size of the giant component to
sn
. We then rely on results about the giant component in
G(n_{b},n_{w},p)
to prove the convergence in (
6
).

For the proof of Theorem
1
, we require a stronger coupling between the Prim sequence and the bond percolation. More specifically, we adapt a graph exploration process from Broutin and Marckert
[
7
]
to the bipartite case, and apply it to
G(n_{b},n_{w},p)
with
p
at criticality. The convergence in (
4
) is then proved after a careful analysis of the stochastic processes that encode the graph exploration.

Discussion.

We discuss here some follow-up questions to Theorems
1
and
2
.

•

Fluctuations and Central limit theorems.
Both our main theorems can be viewed as a law-of-large-numbers type result. It is then natural to query the convergence rates in (
4
) and (
6
), under potentially stronger assumptions. For the sublinear regime, this is currently under investigation by the first author in
[]
. For the linear regime, we believe that the fluctuations are of order
\sqrt{n}
, and may be deduced from the results in
[
8
]
and a refinement of our arguments in Section
4
.

•

General colour schemes and branching process connection.
As the graph
G(n_{b},n_{w},p)
can be approximated with a two-type branching process (see Section
3
for more detail), Theorem
2
is related to the well-known convergence of types theorem for
supercritical
multi-type branching processes (
[
4
]
, V.6, Theorem 1), while Theorem
1
is in a similar spirit to Theorem 1 of
[
16
]
for
critical
branching processes. This leads us to believe that our proof can be readily adapted to a
d
-partite graph for any
d\geq 2
, and more generally the stochastic block model as studied in
[
8
]
.

•

Uniform convergence and exceptional vertices.
We use the notation from Theorems
1
and
2
. As pointed out in Remark
1
, the convergences in (
4
) and (
6
) also hold if we let
\sigma(1)
be any fixed vertex. For
v\in V_{n}
, denote by
(\sigma_{v}(i))_{i\in[n]}
the Prim sequence starting from
\sigma(1)=v
, and by

\rho^{(n)}_{k}(v)
the proportion of black vertices among
\{\sigma_{v}(i):i\in[k]\}
. The following question has been put to us by Vlad Vysotskiy: is it true that

\sup_{v\in V_{n}}\big|\rho^{(n)}_{sn}(v)-\rho\circ\ell^{-1}(s)\big|\xrightarrow{n\to\infty}0\ \text{ and }\sup_{v\in V_{n}}\Big|\rho^{(n)}_{\kappa_{n}}(v)-\frac{1}{1+\gamma_{\theta}}\Big|\xrightarrow{n\to\infty}0\text{ in probability}?

We believe that the first convergence will hold but the second will fail for
(\kappa_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
slowly enough. However, proving the claim is far beyond the scope of the current work; we thus leave it as open questions.

2
Graph exploration in Prim’s ranking

The aim of this section is two-fold. Firstly, we introduce a generalised graph exploration and discuss the properties of the vertex ordering induced by the exploration. This is largely inspired by Broutin and Marckert
[
7
]
, although the presentation here is new. Secondly, we explain, in the specific case of
K_{n_{b},n_{w}}
, how this leads to the representation (
8
) of the connected components in
G(n_{b},n_{w},p)
, which is the starting point for our proof of Theorem
2
, as well as a 2-neighbourhood exploration of
G(n_{b},n_{w},p)
that is the main tool in our proof of Theorem
1
.

2.1
Vertex ordering and graph exploration order

Let
V
be a nonempty finite set. An
ordering
on
V
is a bijection
\pi:\{1,2,3,\dots,|V|\}\to V
. For each
v\in V
,
\pi^{-1}(v)\in\mathbb{N}
is called the
\pi
-rank
of
v
. If
1\leq i\leq j\leq|V|
, we define the following subset of
V
:

\llbracket i,j\rrbracket_{\pi}=\big\{\pi(k):i\leq k\leq j\big\},

which consists of elements ranked between
i
and
j
.

Here we are interested in a specific class of ordering.
To introduce the class, we need the following notion. For a graph
G=(V,E)
and a subset of vertices
W\subseteq V
, we say that
W
is
connected
if the induced subgraph of
G
on
W
is connected, and two subsets
W,W^{\prime}
are
disconnected
from each other if there is no edge of the form
\{w,w^{\prime}\}
with
w\in W
,
w^{\prime}\in W^{\prime}
.

Definition 1

(Graph exploration order (GEO))
.

Let
G=(V,E)
be a (finite) graph. An ordering
\pi
on
V
is said to be a
graph exploration order
, GEO in short, for
G
if it satisfies the following property: for all
1\leq i\leq j\leq|V|
, if
\pi(i)
and
\pi(j)
belong to the same connected component of
G
, then there exists some
i^{\prime}\leq i
so that
\llbracket i^{\prime},j\rrbracket_{\pi}
is connected.

If
\pi
is the ordering induced by the usual breadth-first or depth-first exploration of a graph, then it is a GEO. Indeed, in these explorations, we extract a spanning tree from each connected component and the ancestors of each vertex in this tree are always explored before the vertex. Therefore, if
\pi(i)
and
\pi(j)
belong to the same connected component, then by taking
i^{\prime}
to be the
\pi
-rank of the root of the spanning tree of the component, we see that
\llbracket i^{\prime},j\rrbracket_{\pi}
contains all the ancestors of
\pi(i)
and
\pi(j)
.

For a graph
G=(V,E)
and two vertices
u,v\in V
, a
path from
u
to
v
is a finite sequence of vertices
(w_{j})_{1\leq j\leq k}
satisfying
w_{1}=u,w_{k}=v
and
\{w_{j},w_{j+1}\}\in E
for all
1\leq j\leq k-1
.
Let
\pi
be an ordering of
V
. Suppose that
u,v
are two vertices of
G
satisfying
\pi^{-1}(u)<\pi^{-1}(v)
.
We say that a path
(w_{j})_{1\leq j\leq k}
from
u
to
v
is
\pi
-good
if

\pi^{-1}(w_{1})<\pi^{-1}(w_{2})<\cdots<\pi^{-1}(w_{k}).

The following lemma summarizes the features of a GEO that are used in the main proofs.

Lemma 3
.

Let
G=(V,E)
be a graph and
\pi
a GEO for
G
. Let
C=(V_{C},E_{C})
be a connected component of
G
. The following statements hold true.

(i)

Let
k_{-}^{C}=\min\{\pi^{-1}(v):v\in V_{C}\}
and
k_{+}^{C}=\max\{\pi^{-1}(v):v\in V_{C}\}
; then we have

V_{C}=\big\llbracket k_{-}^{C},k_{+}^{C}\big\rrbracket_{\pi}.

(ii)

For every
v\in V_{C}
, there exists a
\pi
-good path from
\pi(k_{-}^{C})
to
v
.

Moreover, if
\pi
is an ordering of
V
such that (i) and (ii) hold for each connected component of
G
, then
\pi
is a GEO.

Proof.

Let us first show that if
\pi
is a GEO, then (i) and (ii) hold. We denote
W=\llbracket k_{-}^{C},k_{+}^{C}\rrbracket_{\pi}
.
Applying the definition of GEO to
k_{-}^{C}
and
k_{+}^{C}
, we can find some
k\leq k_{-}^{C}
so that
\llbracket k,k_{+}^{C}\rrbracket_{\pi}
is connected. However,
k_{-}^{C}
being the lowest
\pi
-rank in
V_{C}
implies that
k=k_{-}^{C}
, and therefore
W\subseteq V_{C}
. On the other hand, if
v\in V_{C}\setminus W
, then either
\pi^{-1}(v)<k_{-}^{C}
or
\pi^{-1}(v)>k_{+}^{C}
, contradicting in either case the definition of
k_{-}^{C}
or
k_{+}^{C}
. Hence, we must have
W=V_{C}
, which proves
(i)
. For
(ii)
, we will argue by an induction on
k=\pi^{-1}(v)-k_{-}^{C}
, the relative rank of
v
in
V_{C}
. If
k=1
, i.e.
v=\pi(k_{-}^{C})
, this is clearly true.
Suppose that the statement holds for any vertex of rank up to
k_{-}^{C}+k
and
|V_{C}|\geq k+k_{-}^{C}+1
. Let us consider
v=\pi(k_{-}^{C}+k+1)
. Applying the definition of GEO to
k_{-}^{C}
and
k_{-}^{C}+k+1
, we can find some
i\leq k_{-}^{C}
so that
\llbracket i,k_{-}^{C}+k+1\rrbracket_{\pi}
is connected. However,
k_{-}^{C}
being the lowest rank among the vertices of
V_{C}
means that it must be
i=k_{-}^{C}
. Hence, we can find some
u\in\llbracket k_{-}^{C},k_{-}^{C}+k\rrbracket_{\pi}
that is adjacent to
v
. By the induction assumption, there is a
\pi
-good path from
\pi(k_{-}^{C})
to
u
. Appending
v
to that path yields a
\pi
-good path from
\pi(k_{-}^{C})
to
v
. This proves
(ii)
.

Now suppose that
(i)
and
(ii)
hold for an ordering
\pi
. Suppose that
1\leq i\leq j\leq|V|
and
\pi(i),\pi(j)
both belong to the connected component
C=(V_{C},E_{C})
. Then
(i)
implies
\llbracket k_{-}^{C},j\rrbracket_{\pi}\subseteq V_{C}
. Now applying
(ii)
to each
v\in\llbracket k_{-}^{C},j\rrbracket_{\pi}
shows that the set is connected. We conclude that
\pi
is a GEO.
∎

Proposition 4

For each

Proof.

We first prove the representation of the connected components. To ease the notation, we will simply write

\llbracket J_{j-1}+1,J_{j}\rrbracket_{\sigma}

e_{1}\in E_{n}(p)
and therefore
\sigma(2)
is connected to
\sigma(1)
via
e_{1}
. Proceeding in this way, we find that for
1\leq i\leq J_{1}
,
\sigma(i)
is connected to
\llbracket 1,i-1\rrbracket_{\sigma}
. It follows that
\mathcal{C}_{1}
is connected in
G(n_{b},n_{w},p)
. If
m=1
, then there is nothing to show. Assume that
m>1
. Since
e_{J_{1}}
has the smallest edge weight among all the edges connecting
\mathcal{C}_{1}
to
V_{n}\setminus\mathcal{C}_{1}
and
U_{e_{J_{1}}}>p
, we see that none of these edges is present in
E_{n}(p)
. Hence,
\mathcal{C}_{1}
is disconnected from
V_{n}\setminus\mathcal{C}_{1}
, which shows that
\mathcal{C}_{1}
is a connected component.

More generally for
1\leq j\leq m-1
, suppose that we have shown that
\mathcal{C}_{j^{\prime}},j^{\prime}\leq j
, are connected components. This implies in particular that
\cup_{j^{\prime}\leq j}\mathcal{C}_{j^{\prime}}
are disconnected from
\cup_{j^{\prime}>j}\mathcal{C}_{j^{\prime}}
. On the other hand, if
J_{j+1}-J_{j}>1
, then the edge
e_{J_{j}+1}
is present in
E_{n}(p)
, connecting
\sigma(J_{j}+2)
to
\Sigma_{J_{j}+1}=\cup_{j^{\prime}<j}\mathcal{C}_{j^{\prime}}\cup\{\sigma(J_{j}+1)\}
. Combined with the previous argument, it can only be that the other endpoint of
e_{J_{j}+1}
is
\sigma(J_{j}+1)
. Proceeding in this way, we find that for
J_{j}<i\leq J_{j+1}
,
\sigma(i)
is connected to
\llbracket J_{j}+1,i-1\rrbracket_{\sigma}
. Hence,
\mathcal{C}_{j+1}
is connected in
G(n_{b},n_{w},p)
. The definitions of
e_{J_{j+1}}
and
J_{j+1}
then imply that
\mathcal{C}_{j+1}
must be disconnected from
\cup_{j^{\prime}>j+1}\mathcal{C}_{j^{\prime}}
. This shows that
\mathcal{C}_{j},1\leq j\leq m
, are the vertex sets of the connected components of
G(n_{b},n_{w},p)
.

To see why
\sigma
is a GEO, let
1\leq i_{1}<i_{2}\leq n
and suppose that
\sigma(i_{1}),\sigma(i_{2})
belong to the same connected component of
G(n_{b},n_{w},p)
. Thanks to the representation (
8
), we can find some
j\in[m]
so that
J_{j}+1\leq i_{1}<i_{2}\leq J_{j+1}
. Take
i^{\prime}=J_{j}+1
; then the previous arguments have shown that
\llbracket J_{j}+1,i_{2}\rrbracket_{\sigma}
is connected. This concludes the proof.
∎

Let
G=(V,E)
be a graph and
\pi
be an ordering on
V
. The following algorithm is an extension of the habitual breadth-first and depth-first explorations.
For a subset of vertices
A\subseteq V
, we define its
1
-neighborhood
as follows:

\mathcal{N}(A)=\big\{v\in V\setminus A:\exists\,a\in A\text{ such that }\{a,v\}\in E\big\}.

(9)

If
A=\{v\}
, we often write
\mathcal{N}(v):=\mathcal{N}(\{v\})
.

Algorithm 2

(Graph exploration in
\pi
-ordering)
.

Let
\hat{\pi}:\{1,2,\dots,|V|\}\to V
be defined as follows.

Step 1.
Set
\hat{\pi}(1)=\pi(1)
and let
\hat{\mathcal{A}}_{1}=\mathcal{N}(\hat{\pi}(1))
.

Step
\boldsymbol{2\leq k\leq|V|}
.

If
\hat{\mathcal{A}}_{k-1}
is nonempty, set
\hat{\pi}(k)
to be the element of
\hat{\mathcal{A}}_{k-1}
with the lowest
\pi
-rank; otherwise, set
\hat{\pi}(k)
to be the element of
V\setminus\{\hat{\pi}(1),\hat{\pi}(2),\dots,\hat{\pi}(k-1)\}
with the lowest
\pi
-rank. Let
\hat{\mathcal{A}}_{k}=\mathcal{N}(\{\hat{\pi}(1),\hat{\pi}(2),\dots,\hat{\pi}(k)\})
.

Proposition 5
.

Let
\hat{\pi}
be as in Algorithm
2
. If
\pi
is a GEO for
G
, then
\hat{\pi}=\pi
.

Proof.

Let us denote
k_{\ast}=\max\{k\in[n]:\pi(k)=\hat{\pi}(k)\}
. We note that since
\pi(1)=\hat{\pi}(1)
,
k_{\ast}>1
. Suppose that
k_{\ast}<|V|
. If
\hat{\mathcal{A}}_{k_{\ast}}=\varnothing
, then by definition
\hat{\pi}(k_{\ast}+1)
is the lowest
\pi
-ranking element of
V\setminus\{\hat{\pi}(i):1\leq i\leq k_{\ast}\}=\{\pi(i):k_{\ast}<i\leq|V|\}
, which is
\pi(k_{\ast}+1)
. This contradicts with the choice of
k_{\ast}
; so we must have
\hat{\mathcal{A}}_{k_{\ast}}\neq\varnothing
.
Denoting
v_{\ast}=\pi(k_{\ast}+1)
, we distinguish two cases.

Case 1:

v_{\ast}
is the lowest
\pi
-ranking vertex in its connected component. Thanks to Lemma
3
, we see that
\llbracket 1,k_{\ast}\rrbracket_{\pi}
is disconnected from
\llbracket k_{\ast}+1,|V|\rrbracket_{\pi}
. On the other hand, from Algorithm
2
and the choice of
k_{\ast}
, we deduce that

\hat{\mathcal{A}}_{k_{\ast}}=\bigcup_{i\leq k_{\ast}}\mathcal{N}\big(\hat{\pi}(i))\setminus\{\hat{\pi}(1),\hat{\pi}(2),\dots,\hat{\pi}(k_{\ast})\}=\bigcup_{i\leq k_{\ast}}\mathcal{N}\big(\pi(i))\setminus\llbracket 1,k_{\ast}\rrbracket_{\pi}.

(10)

It then follows from the connectivity property of
\llbracket 1,k_{\ast}\rrbracket_{\pi}
that we would have
\hat{\mathcal{A}}_{k_{\ast}}=\varnothing
in this case, which is impossible.

Case 2:

Let
k_{-}
be the lowest
\pi
-rank of the connected component containing
v_{\ast}
and we have
k_{-}<k_{\ast}+1
. Lemma
3
says that
\llbracket k_{-},k_{\ast}+1\rrbracket_{\pi}
is connected. Hence,
we can find some
k_{-}\leq k\leq k_{\ast}
so that
v_{\ast}\in\mathcal{N}(\pi(k))
. We note that (
10
) still holds and as a result, we find
v_{\ast}\in\hat{\mathcal{A}}_{k_{\ast}}
. According to the algorithm, we would then set
\hat{\pi}(k_{\ast}+1)=\pi(k_{\ast}+1)
, contradicting the definition of
k_{\ast}
.

To conclude, it is impossible to have
k_{\ast}<|V|
, and therefore
\hat{\pi}=\pi
.
∎

2.2
A 2-neighbourhood exploration of
G(n_{b},n_{w},p)

Algorithm
2
allows us to explore
G(n_{b},n_{w},p)
in Prim’s order. Propositions
4
and
5
combined then tell us that this amounts to examining the 1-neighhbourhood of each
\sigma(i)
in turn. For our proof in the sublinear regime however, it is more convenient to explore the graph from vertices of the same colour. This leads to the following exploration algorithm. We will need the following notation. For a subset
A\subseteq V_{n}
, we define the following set to be its
2-neighbourhood
:

\mathcal{N}^{2}(A)=\mathcal{N}\big(\mathcal{N}(A)\big)\setminus A.

(11)

We note in particular that
A
,
\mathcal{N}(A)
and
\mathcal{N}^{2}(A)
are disjoint from each other according to the definition.
In the case that
A=\{v\}
, we denote
\mathcal{N}^{2}(v)=\mathcal{N}^{2}(\{v\})
.

Algorithm 3

(2-neighbourhood exploration in Prim’s order)
.

Given
G(n_{b},n_{w},p)
and the Prim sequence
(\sigma(i))_{i\in[n]}
, define an ordering on the set of black vertices
\sigma^{b}:[n_{b}]\to V^{b}_{n}
as follows.

Step 1.
Set
\sigma^{b}(1)
to be the lowest ranking black vertex in
(\sigma(i))_{i\in[n]}
, and let

\mathcal{A}^{b}_{1}=\mathcal{N}^{2}(\sigma^{b}(1))
.

Step
\boldsymbol{2\leq k\leq n_{b}}
.

If
\mathcal{A}^{b}_{k-1}
is nonempty, set
\sigma^{b}(k)
to be the lowest ranking element of
\mathcal{A}^{b}_{k-1}
; otherwise, set
\sigma^{b}(k)
to be the lowest ranking element of
V^{b}_{n}\setminus\{\sigma^{b}(1),\sigma^{b}(2),\dots,\sigma^{b}(k-1)\}
.

Set
\mathcal{A}^{b}_{k}=\mathcal{N}^{2}(\{\sigma^{b}(1),\sigma^{b}(2),\dots,\sigma^{b}(k)\})
.

The following proposition says that the ordering
\sigma^{b}
is still consistent with Prim’s order. Recall that
\Sigma(i)=\llbracket 1,i\rrbracket_{\sigma}
.

Proposition 6
.

For
k\in[n_{b}]
, let
\tau^{b}_{k}
be the rank of the
k
-th black vertex in the Prim sequence, namely,
\tau^{b}_{k}=\min\{i:|\Sigma(i)\cap V^{b}_{n}|=k\}
. Then
\sigma^{b}(k)=\sigma(\tau^{b}_{k})
for each
k\in[n_{b}]
.

Proof.

Most of the arguments are similar to the ones used in the proof of Proposition
5
; so we only highlight the differences. Suppose that
k_{\ast}=\max\{k:\sigma^{b}(k)=\sigma(\tau^{b}_{k})\}<n_{b}
. The choice of
\sigma^{b}(k_{\ast}+1)
means that we only need to consider the case where
\mathcal{A}^{b}_{k_{\ast}}\neq\varnothing
.
Denote
v_{\ast}=\sigma(\tau^{b}_{k_{\ast}+1})
.

Case 1:

v_{\ast}
is the lowest
\sigma
-ranking black vertex in its connected components. Since connected components are intervals in Prim’s order, we deduce that for each
k\leq k_{\ast}
,
\sigma^{b}(k)=\sigma(\tau^{b}_{k})
is in a different connected component from
\{\sigma^{b}(k):k>k_{\ast}\}
. It follows that

\mathcal{A}^{b}_{k_{\ast}}=\bigcup_{k\leq k_{\ast}}\mathcal{N}^{2}\big(\sigma^{b}(k)\big)\setminus\big\{\sigma^{b}(k):k\leq k_{\ast}\big\}=\varnothing,

which contradicts the previous assumption.

Case 2:

v_{\ast}
is not the lowest
\sigma
-ranking black vertex in its connected components. Let us argue that
v_{\ast}\in\mathcal{A}^{b}_{k_{\ast}}
.
To that end, we first show that
I:=\{k\leq k_{\ast}:v_{\ast}\in\mathcal{N}^{2}(\sigma^{b}(k))\}
is nonempty.
Let
k_{-}
be the lowest
\sigma
-rank of the connected component containing
v_{\ast}
.
Then by Lemma
3

(ii)
, there is a
\sigma
-good path
\rho
from
\sigma(k_{-})
to
v_{\ast}
. By assumption, we can find another black vertex in the same component as
v_{\ast}
that has a lower rank, say
j\leq\tau^{b}_{k_{\ast}}
. Again, there is a
\sigma
-good path
\rho^{\prime}
from
\sigma(k_{-})
to
\sigma(j)
. The concatenated path
\rho\cup\rho^{\prime}
consists of vertices with ranks
\leq\tau^{b}_{k_{\ast}+1}
and contains at least one black vertex distinct from
v_{\ast}
. So it must contain a black vertex at (graph) distance 2 from
v_{\ast}
whose rank
j^{\prime}\leq\tau^{b}_{k_{\ast}}
.
By the definition of
k_{\ast}
, we then have
\sigma(j^{\prime})=\sigma^{b}(k)
for some
k\leq k_{\ast}
, which shows
I\neq\varnothing
. Since
v_{\ast}\notin\{\sigma^{b}(k):k\leq k_{\ast}\}
, this implies that

v_{\ast}\in\mathcal{A}^{b}_{k_{\ast}}=\bigcup_{k\leq k_{\ast}}\mathcal{N}^{2}\big(\sigma^{b}(k)\big)\setminus\big\{\sigma^{b}(k):k\leq k_{\ast}\big\}.

Step
k_{\ast}+1
of the algorithm then asserts
\sigma^{b}(k_{\ast}+1)=v_{\ast}
, contradicting the definition of
k_{\ast}
.
∎

Remark 3

(Graph contraction and GEO)
.

We describe here an alternative proof of Proposition
6
, based on the concept of graph contraction. If
G=(V,E)
is a graph and
V^{\prime}\subseteq V
,
let
H=(V^{\prime},E^{\prime})
be the graph on the vertex set
V^{\prime}
and the edge set
E^{\prime}
defined as follows:

E^{\prime}=\big\{\{v,u\}:v\in V^{\prime},u\in V^{\prime}\setminus\{v\},\exists\,W\subseteq V\setminus V^{\prime}\text{ s.t. }\{v,u\}\cup W\text{ is connected in }G\big\}.

We then say
H
is the contraction of
G
to
V^{\prime}
.
Suppose that
\pi
is a GEO for
G
and let
(v_{k})_{1\leq k\leq|V^{\prime}|}
be the elements of
V^{\prime}
ranked in increasing
\pi
-order. Then
\pi^{\prime}:k\mapsto v_{k}
,
1\leq k\leq|V^{\prime}|
, is an ordering on
V^{\prime}
.
One can show, that if
\pi
is a GEO for
G
, then
\pi^{\prime}
is a GEO for
H
. Taking
G=G(n_{b},n_{w},p)
and
V^{\prime}=V^{b}_{n}
, we obtain a new graph
H^{b}
on the vertex set
V^{b}_{n}
, where two black vertices are adjacent to each other if and only if they are adjacent to the same white vertex in

. The graph

is also called the random intersection graph in the literature
[
14
]
.
Since
\sigma
is a GEO for
G(n_{b},n_{w},p)
,
k\mapsto\sigma(\tau^{b}_{k})
,

, is then a GEO for

. Finally, we point out that
Algorithm
3
is equivalent to Algorithm
2
for
H^{b}
in
\sigma\circ\tau^{b}
-order. Proposition
5
then yields Proposition
6
.

Recall that

\Sigma^{b}(k)=\Sigma(k)\cap V^{b}_{n}
and
\Sigma^{w}(k)=\Sigma(k)\cap V^{w}_{n}
.
Instead of
\mathcal{A}^{b}_{k}
, it is sometimes more convenient to study the following set

\mathcal{O}^{b}_{k}:=\mathcal{A}^{b}_{k}\setminus\mathcal{A}^{b}_{k-1}=\mathcal{N}^{2}\big(\sigma^{b}(k)\big)\setminus\big(\mathcal{A}^{b}_{k-1}\cup\Sigma^{b}(\tau^{b}_{k})\big),\quad 1\leq k\leq n_{b},

with the convention that
\mathcal{A}_{0}=\varnothing
. We will view

as the 2-neighbourhood discovered at step

in Algorithm

. Note that by definition,
\mathcal{O}^{b}_{k}
,

, are disjoint. Let us introduce another representation of
\mathcal{O}^{b}_{k}
. For

, we consider

\mathcal{K}^{b}_{k}:=V^{b}_{n}\setminus\Big(\bigcup_{j\leq k-1}\mathcal{O}^{b}_{j}\,\bigcup\Sigma^{b}(\tau^{b}_{k})\Big)

(12)

to be the pool of black vertices available for discovery at step

; then we have

\mathcal{O}^{b}_{k}=\mathcal{N}^{2}(\sigma^{b}(k))\cap\mathcal{K}^{b}_{k}.

(13)

To see why (

\mathcal{A}^{b}_{k-1}\cup\Sigma^{b}(\tau^{b}_{k})=\bigcup_{j\in[k-1]}\mathcal{N}^{2}(\sigma^{b}(j))\,\bigcup\,\Sigma^{b}(\tau^{b}_{k})=\bigcup_{j\in[k-1]}\mathcal{O}^{b}_{j}\,\bigcup\,\Sigma^{b}(\tau^{b}_{k})=V^{b}_{n}\setminus\mathcal{K}^{b}_{k},

Indeed, we have

The first identity in (

Taking the union over

12

Next, let us define

\mathcal{K}^{w}_{1}=V^{w}_{n}\setminus\Sigma^{w}(\tau^{b}_{1});

more generally for

\mathcal{O}^{w}_{k-1}=\mathcal{N}\big(\sigma^{b}(k-1)\big)\cap\mathcal{K}^{w}_{k-1}\quad\text{and}\quad\mathcal{K}^{w}_{k}=\mathcal{K}^{w}_{k-1}\setminus\big(\mathcal{O}^{w}_{k-1}\cup\Sigma^{w}(\tau^{b}_{k})\big).

(16)

In analogue to

and
\mathcal{O}^{w}_{k}

3
Bond percolation of the complete bipartite graph

As indicated in Proposition
4
, there is a close connection between the Prim sequence and the bond percolation of
K_{n_{b},n_{w}}
. We collect here various properties of the bond percolation that are useful for our proofs. Broadly speaking, the results below mirror those for the bond percolation of the complete graph
K_{n}
, or equivalently the graph process
(G(n,p))_{p\in[0,1]}
.

We will use the following notation for probability distributions.
For
n\in\mathbb{N}
and
p\in[0,1]
, we denote by
\operatorname{Bin}(n,p)
the Binomial distribution with parameters
n
and
p
. It will be convenient to write
\operatorname{Bin}(0,p)
for the law of the degenerate random variable
X\equiv 0
. We also denote by Poisson
(c)
the Poisson distribution with mean
c\in(0,\infty)
.

3.1
A two-type branching process

Let
p\in(0,1)
and recall the graph
G(n_{b},n_{w},p)=(V_{n},E_{n}(p))
with the edge set
E_{n}(p)
from (
7
). It is not difficult to see that each black vertex in
G(n_{b},n_{w},p)
is adjacent to a random number of white vertices with a
\operatorname{Bin}(n_{w},p)
distribution, and each white vertex is adjacent to a random number of black vertices with a
\operatorname{Bin}(n_{b},p)
distribution.
From now on, we focus on the sparse regime: fix
\lambda\in(0,\infty)
and let

p=\frac{\lambda}{\sqrt{n_{b}n_{w}}},\quad n\geq 1.

(17)

It turns out that the local limit of
G(n_{b},n_{w},p)
, as
n\to\infty
, is closely connected to the following two-type branching process. Let
\{\mathbf{Z}_{k}=(Z^{(1)}_{k},Z^{(2)}_{k}):k\geq 0\}
be a
\mathbb{Z}_{+}^{2}
-valued Markov chain that evolves as follows.
For all
k\geq 1
, given
\mathbf{Z}_{k}=(z_{1},z_{2})\in\mathbb{Z}_{+}^{2}
, we have

Z^{(1)}_{k+1}=\sum_{i=1}^{z_{2}}Y^{(k,1)}_{i},\quad Z^{(2)}_{k+1}=\sum_{i=1}^{z_{1}}Y^{(k,2)}_{i},

where
(Y^{(k,1)}_{i})_{i\geq 1},(Y^{(k,2)}_{i})_{i\geq 1}
are two independent families of i.i.d. random variables with

Y^{(k,1)}_{1}\sim\text{ Poisson}(\lambda\gamma_{\theta}^{-1}),\quad Y^{(k,2)}_{1}\sim\text{ Poisson}(\lambda\gamma_{\theta}),

where
\gamma_{\theta}=\sqrt{(1-\theta)/\theta}
.
For
i,j\in\{1,2\}
, denote
m_{i,j}=\mathbb{E}[Z^{(j)}_{1}\,|\,Z^{(i)}_{0}=1]
. Straightforward computations show that

M:=\begin{pmatrix}m_{1,1}&m_{1,2}\\
m_{2,1}&m_{2,2}\end{pmatrix}=\begin{pmatrix}0&\lambda\gamma_{\theta}\\
\lambda\gamma_{\theta}^{-1}&0\end{pmatrix}.

has eigenvalues
\lambda,-\lambda
and
(1,\gamma_{\theta})
is a left eigenvector for
\lambda
.
The extinction probabilities are defined as

q_{1}(\lambda)=\mathbb{P}\big(\exists\,k\geq 1:\mathbf{Z}_{k}=(0,0)\,\big|\,\mathbf{Z}_{0}=(1,0)\big),\quad q_{2}(\lambda)=\mathbb{P}\big(\exists\,k\geq 1:\mathbf{Z}_{k}=(0,0)\,\big|\,\mathbf{Z}_{0}=(0,1)\big).

(18)

Theorem 7
.

Let
\gamma_{\theta}=\sqrt{\frac{1-\theta}{\theta}}
.
The extinction probabilities are given as follows.

(i)

(Sub)critical regime: if
\lambda\leq 1
, then
q_{1}(\lambda)=q_{2}(\lambda)=1
.

(ii)

Supercritical regime: if
\lambda>1
, then
(q_{1}(\lambda),q_{2}(\lambda))
is the unique solution in
(0,1)^{2}
of the fixed-point equation:

\left\{\begin{array}[]{l}x=e^{\lambda\gamma_{\theta}(y-1)}\\
y=e^{\lambda\gamma_{\theta}^{-1}(x-1)}\end{array}\right..

(19)

Furthermore, the only other solution of (
19
) is
(1,1)
.

Proof.

As
(\mathbf{Z}_{k})_{k\geq 0}
is 2-periodic, classic results on the multi-type branching processes such as Theorem 2 in Chapter V.2
[
4
]
do not apply directly. However, we note that
(Z^{(1)}_{2k})_{k\geq 0}
is an ordinary branching process where the offspring distribution is given by Poisson
(Y\cdot\lambda\gamma_{\theta}^{-1})
with
Y\sim
Poisson
(\lambda\gamma_{\theta})
.
Moreover, this branching process is supercritical if and only if
\lambda>1
.
Denote its extinction probability as

\tilde{q}_{1}(\lambda):=\mathbb{P}\big(\exists\,k\geq 1:Z^{(1)}_{2k}=0\,\big|\,\mathbf{Z}_{0}=(1,0)\big).

Then for each
\lambda>0
,
\tilde{q}_{1}(\lambda)
is the smallest solution of the equation:

s=\exp\Big\{\lambda\gamma_{\theta}\Big(e^{\lambda\gamma_{\theta}^{-1}(s-1)}-1\Big)\Big\}.

(20)

Criticality of the branching process then implies that for
\lambda\in(0,1]
, the equation has a unique solution with
\tilde{q}_{1}(\lambda)=1
, while for
\lambda>1
, the equation has two solutions,
1
and

\tilde{q}_{1}(\lambda)\in(0,1)
. Set

\tilde{q}_{2}(\lambda)=\exp\big\{\lambda\gamma_{\theta}^{-1}(\tilde{q}_{1}(\lambda)-1)\big\};

then
(\tilde{q}_{1}(\lambda),\tilde{q}_{2}(\lambda))
solves (
19
). On the other hand, if
(\hat{q}_{1}(\lambda),\hat{q}_{2}(\lambda))
is a solution of (
19
), then necessarily
\hat{q}_{1}(\lambda)
solves (
20
). We further have
\hat{q}_{1}(\lambda)\in(0,1)
if and only if
\hat{q}_{2}(\lambda)\in(0,1)
. We deduce that for
\lambda\in(0,1]
, (
19
) has a unique solution
(1,1)
, while for
\lambda>1
, (
19
) has two sets of solutions, given by
(1,1)
and
(\tilde{q}_{1}(\lambda),\tilde{q}_{2}(\lambda))
.

It remains to show that
q_{i}(\lambda)=\tilde{q}_{i}(\lambda)
,
i\in\{1,2\}
. Indeed, we note that
(Z^{(2)}_{2k})_{k\geq 0}
is also a branching process. It follows that
Z^{(2)}_{0}=0
implies
Z^{(0)}_{2k}=0
for all
k\geq 0
. Comparing the definitions of
q_{1}(\lambda)
with
\tilde{q}_{1}(\lambda)
, we see that
\tilde{q}_{1}(\lambda)\leq q_{1}(\lambda)
. On the other hand,
\mathbf{Z}_{k}=(0,0)
implies
\mathbf{Z}_{k^{\prime}}=(0,0)
for all
k^{\prime}\geq k
; hence
Z_{2\ell}^{(1)}=0
for all
2\ell\geq k
. We then find
q_{1}(\lambda)=\tilde{q}_{1}(\lambda)
, which shows
\tilde{q}_{1}(\lambda)=q_{1}(\lambda)
.
The case of
q_{2}(\lambda)
can be similarly argued.
∎

3.2
Size of the giant component

The
size
of a connected component
\mathcal{C}
of
G(n_{b},n_{w},p)
refers to the number of vertices contained in
\mathcal{C}
. Let
\mathcal{C}_{n}(\lambda)
be the largest connected component of
G(n_{b},n_{w},p)
with
p=\lambda/\sqrt{n_{b}n_{w}}
, breaking ties arbitrarily.
In parallel to the classical results on the Erdős–Rényi graph, the random bipartite graph
G(n_{b},n_{w},p)
also exhibits a phase transition in the size of
\mathcal{C}_{n}(\lambda)
as

, with a giant component emerging right after

, matching the critical point of the previous branching process

(

Denote by

and

Theorem 8

(
[

]
, Theorem 12)
.

Assume (
1
) and (
17
) and let
\lambda>1
. Recall
q_{1}(\lambda),q_{2}(\lambda)
from (

\frac{C^{\mathrm{b}}_{n}(\lambda)}{n}\to\theta\big(1-q_{1}(\lambda)\big)\quad\text{and}\quad\frac{C^{\mathrm{w}}_{n}(\lambda)}{n}\to(1-\theta)\big(1-q_{2}(\lambda)\big).

We have seen in Theorem

that
q_{i}(\lambda),i=1,2
, are solutions of a fixed-point equation. This allows us to deduce the following properties.

Lemma 9
.

Let
\gamma_{\theta}=\sqrt{\frac{1-\theta}{\theta}}
. The following statements are true.

(i)

For
i\in\{1,2\}
,
q_{i}(\lambda)
is differentiable and strictly decreasing on
(1,\infty)
; moreover,
q_{i}(1+)=1
and
q_{i}(\infty)=0
.

(ii)

As
\lambda\to 1+
, we have
q_{1}(\lambda)-1=\gamma_{\theta}(q_{2}(\lambda)-1)+o\big(|q_{2}(\lambda)-1|\big)
,

Proof.

For (i), we will only prove the properties for
q_{1}(\lambda)
; the case of
q_{2}(\lambda)
can be similarly argued. Recall that for
\lambda>1
,
q_{1}(\lambda)
is the unique solution in

of the fixed-point equation (
20
). Let us introduce

F(x,\lambda)=\lambda\gamma_{\theta}\Big(e^{\lambda\gamma_{\theta}^{-1}(x-1)}-1\Big)-\log x,\quad x>0,\;\lambda>1.

(21)

Then
F(q_{1}(\lambda),\lambda)=0
.
We note that
x\mapsto F(x,\lambda)
is convex with
F(0+,\lambda)=\infty
and
F(1,\lambda)=0
. Since
\lambda>1
, we have
q_{1}(\lambda)\in(0,1)
. Therefore, for each
\lambda>1
,
F(x,\lambda)
has two roots with
q_{1}(\lambda)
being the smaller one. It follows
\partial_{x}F(q_{1}(\lambda),\lambda)<0
. By the implicit function theorem,
q_{1}(\lambda)
is differential in
\lambda\in(1,\infty)
and we further have

q^{\prime}_{1}(\lambda)=-\frac{\partial_{\lambda}F\big(q_{1}(\lambda),\lambda\big)}{\partial_{x}F\big(q_{1}(\lambda),\lambda\big)}=-\frac{\gamma_{\theta}(e^{\lambda\gamma_{\theta}^{-1}(q_{1}(\lambda)-1)}-1)+\lambda(q_{1}(\lambda)-1)e^{\lambda\gamma_{\theta}^{-1}(q_{1}(\lambda)-1)}}{\partial_{x}F\big(q_{1}(\lambda),\lambda\big)}<0,

for all
\lambda>1
. This proves the monotonicity. In particular,
q_{1}(\infty):=\lim_{\lambda\to\infty}q_{1}(\lambda)
exists and is a real number in
[0,1)
. Meanwhile, we have

\log\big(q_{1}(\lambda)\big)=\lambda\gamma_{\theta}\Big(e^{\lambda\gamma_{\theta}^{-1}(q_{1}(\lambda)-1)}-1\Big).

(22)

By the monotone convergence theorem, the right-hand side above tends to

as
\lambda\to\infty
, which implies
q_{1}(\infty)=0
.
Next, taking
\lambda\to 1+
on the both sides of (

\log\big(q_{1}(1+)\big)=\gamma_{\theta}\Big(e^{\gamma_{\theta}^{-1}(q_{1}(1+)-1)}-1\Big).

Elementary arguments show that the function
F(x,1)

has a unique root at 1. Hence,
q_{1}(1+)=1
, which concludes the proof of
(i)
.

We have seen in
(i)
that both
q_{i}
are right-continuous at
\lambda=1

as

implies
(ii)

∎

Let us introduce

\ell(\lambda)=\theta\big(1-q_{1}(\lambda)\big)+(1-\theta)\big(1-q_{2}(\lambda)\big),\quad\rho(\lambda)=\frac{\theta\big(1-q_{1}(\lambda)\big)}{\ell(\lambda)}.

(23)

(iii)

As

Proof.

For
(i)

4
Proof in the linear regime

We prove here Theorem
2
. Recall the random graph
G(n_{b},n_{w},p)
and assume that
p
is as in (
17
).
Recall from Theorem
8
that for
\lambda>1
,
G(n_{b},n_{w},p)
contains a giant component
\mathcal{C}_{n}(\lambda)
with high probability.
Let us denote by
k_{-}(\lambda)
(resp.
k_{+}(\lambda)
) the lowest (resp. highest) rank of the vertices in
\mathcal{C}_{n}(\lambda)
.
Thanks to Proposition
4
, the vertex set of
\mathcal{C}_{n}(\lambda)
is precisely
\{\sigma(i):k_{-}(\lambda)\leq i\leq k_{+}(\lambda)\}
.

Lemma 11
.

For each
\lambda\in(1,\infty)
, the following convergence in probability takes place:

\frac{k_{-}(\lambda)}{n}\xrightarrow{n\to\infty}0.

Proof.

Recall that
C^{\mathrm{b}}_{n}(\lambda)
and
C^{\mathrm{w}}_{n}(\lambda)
are the respective numbers of black and white vertices in
\mathcal{C}_{n}(\lambda)
.
Take
v\in V_{n}
. Let us first show the following:

\mathbb{P}(v\in\mathcal{C}_{n}(\lambda)\,|\,C^{\mathrm{b}}_{n}(\lambda),C^{\mathrm{w}}_{n}(\lambda))\geq\frac{C^{\mathrm{b}}_{n}(\lambda)}{n_{b}}\mathbf{1}_{\{v\in V^{b}_{n}\}}+\frac{C^{\mathrm{w}}_{n}(\lambda)}{n_{w}}\mathbf{1}_{\{v\in V^{w}_{n}\}}.

(24)

To see why this is true, we first note that the probability of each configuration of
G(n_{b},n_{w},p)
only depends on the number of edges present in the configuration.
Denote by
\Pi_{n}
the set of all permutations
\pi:V_{n}\to V_{n}
satisfying
\pi(V^{b}_{n})=V^{b}_{n}
.
Let
\varrho
be a uniform element of
\Pi_{n}
.
Let
c_{b},c_{w}\in\mathbb{N}
and let
\Gamma_{n}
be the set of all graphs of
n_{b}
black vertices,
n_{w}
white vertices, and containing a largest connected component with
c_{b}
black vertices and
c_{w}
white vertices.
For
g\in\Gamma_{n}
, denote by
A(g;v)
the event that
v
belongs to a largest connected component of
g
.
If
v
is black and
g
has a unique largest connected component, then by summing over all the possible permutations, we have

\mathbb{P}\big(A(\varrho(g);v)\big)=\sum_{\pi\in\Pi_{n}}\frac{1}{n_{b}!n_{w}!}\mathbf{1}_{A(\pi(g);v)}=\frac{c_{b}}{n_{b}}.

This probability is even larger if
g
has more than one largest connected components.
We have

\mathbb{P}\big(v\in\mathcal{C}_{n}(\lambda),C^{\mathrm{b}}_{n}(\lambda)=c_{b},C^{\mathrm{w}}_{n}(\lambda)=c_{w}\big)=\sum_{g\in\Gamma_{n}}\mathbb{P}(G(n_{b},n_{w},p)=g)\mathbf{1}_{A(g;v)}.

On the other hand, noting that the law of
G(n_{b},n_{w},p)
is invariant by
\varrho
, we deduce that the previous probability can also be written as follows:

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\big(v\in\mathcal{C}_{n}(\lambda);C^{\mathrm{b}}_{n}(\lambda)=C^{\mathrm{b}}_{n},C^{\mathrm{w}}_{n}(\lambda)=c_{w}\big)

\displaystyle=\sum_{g\in\Gamma_{n}}\mathbb{P}\big(G(n_{b},n_{w},p)=g\big)\cdot\mathbb{P}\big(A(\varrho(g);v)\big)

\displaystyle\geq\frac{c_{b}}{n_{b}}\sum_{g\in\Gamma_{n}}\mathbb{P}\big(G(n_{b},n_{w},p)=g\big)

\displaystyle=\frac{c_{b}}{n_{b}}\cdot\mathbb{P}\big(C^{\mathrm{b}}_{n}(\lambda)=c_{b},C^{\mathrm{w}}_{n}(\lambda)=c_{w}\big).

The case of a white vertex can be argued similarly. Combing the two cases yields (
24
).

We say that a vertex is a lead if it is the lowest ranking vertex in its connected component of
G(n_{b},n_{w},p)
.
For
\ell\geq 1
, let
\zeta_{\ell}
be the rank of the
\ell
-th lead vertex and let
\mathcal{H}_{\ell}
be the sigma-algebra as defined in Section
A
. Let
N^{b}_{\ell}
and
N^{w}_{\ell}
stand for the number of black and white vertices among
\{\sigma(i):i\geq\zeta_{\ell}\}
. We note that
\zeta_{\ell},\sigma(\zeta_{\ell}),N^{b}_{\ell}
and
N^{w}_{\ell}
are all
\mathcal{H}_{\ell}
-measurable. Denote by
\hat{K}
the induced subgraph of
K_{n_{b},n_{w}}
on
\{\sigma(i):i\geq\zeta_{\ell}\}
and by
\hat{G}
the induced subgraph of
G(n_{b},n_{w},p)
on the same vertex set.
Thanks to Lemma
31

(ii)
,
\hat{K}
is a complete bipartite graph with
N^{b}_{\ell}
black vertices,
N^{w}_{\ell}
white vertices and i.i.d. uniform edge weights.
Suppose that
\mathcal{C}_{n}(\lambda)
is the
L
-th connected component in Prim’s rank. We note that given
\mathcal{H}_{\ell}
and
\{L\geq\ell\}
, we have

\{L=\ell\}=\{k_{-}(\lambda)=\zeta_{\ell}\}=\{\sigma(\zeta_{\ell})\in\mathcal{C}_{n}(\lambda)\}.

On the event
L\geq\ell
, a largest connected component of
G(n_{b},n_{w},p)
is also a largest connected component of
\hat{G}
. Noting that
N^{b}_{\ell}\leq n_{b},N^{w}_{\ell}\leq n_{w}
, we apply (
24
) to
\hat{G}
and
\sigma(\zeta_{\ell})
to find that

\mathbb{P}\big(L=\ell\,\big|\,L\geq\ell,\mathcal{H}_{\ell},C^{\mathrm{b}}_{n}(\lambda),C^{\mathrm{w}}_{n}(\lambda)\big)\geq\min\Big(\frac{C^{\mathrm{b}}_{n}(\lambda)}{n_{b}},\frac{C^{\mathrm{w}}_{n}(\lambda)}{n_{w}}\Big)=:\xi_{n}.

It follows that

\mathbb{P}\big(L>\ell\,\big|\,\xi_{n}\big)=\prod_{1\leq\ell^{\prime}\leq\ell}\mathbb{P}\big(L>\ell^{\prime}\,\big|\,L\geq\ell^{\prime},\xi_{n}\big)\leq(1-\xi_{n})^{\ell}.

This shows that
L
is stochastically bounded by a geometric random variable of mean
\xi_{n}^{-1}
. Thanks to Theorem
8
,
\xi_{n}
converges in probability to a strictly positive real number. Consequently, the distribution of
L
is tight. Let
C^{(2)}(\lambda)
be the size of the second largest connected component in
G(n_{b},n_{w},p)
. Theorem 12 in
[
13
]
shows that
C^{(2)}(\lambda)=o_{p}(n)
as
n\to\infty
. The conclusion now follows from the bound that
k_{-}(\lambda)\leq L\cdot C^{(2)}(\lambda)
.
∎

We are now ready for the main proof in the linear regime.

Proof of Theorem
2
.

Thanks to Lemma
10
, it only remains to prove the convergence in (
6
).
We have seen in Lemma
10
that
\ell:(1,\infty)\to(0,1)
is invertible. Fix
s\in(0,1)
and take
\lambda_{\ast}=\ell^{-1}(s)
, so that
\ell(\lambda_{\ast})=s
.
We note that the size of the giant component is given by
k_{+}(\lambda)-k_{-}(\lambda)+1
. Theorem
8
then says that for each
\lambda>1
,

\frac{1}{n}\big(k_{+}(\lambda)-k_{-}(\lambda)\big)\xrightarrow{n\to\infty}\ell(\lambda)\quad\text{in probability}.

Combining this with Lemma
11
, we find
k_{+}\big(\lambda_{\ast}\big)=sn+o_{p}(n)
, which then implies

\big|\Sigma^{b}(\lfloor sn\rfloor)\big|=\Big|\Sigma^{b}\big(k_{+}(\lambda_{\ast})\big)\Big|+o_{p}(n),\quad n\to\infty.

Lemma

also yields that
|\Sigma^{b}(k_{-}(\lambda_{\ast}))|\leq k_{-}(\lambda_{\ast})=o_{p}(n)
. Note that
|\Sigma^{b}(k_{+}(\lambda_{\ast}))|-|\Sigma^{b}(k_{-}(\lambda_{\ast}))|+1
corresponds to the number of black vertices in the giant component.
We deduce that as
n\to\infty
,

\displaystyle\rho^{(n)}_{\lfloor sn\rfloor}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{sn}\Big(\Big|\Sigma^{b}(\lfloor sn\rfloor)\Big|+o_{p}(n)\Big)=\frac{1}{sn}\Big(\Big|\Sigma^{b}\big(k_{+}(\lambda_{\ast})\big)\Big|+o_{p}(n)\Big)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{sn}\Big(\Big|\Sigma^{b}\big(k_{+}(\lambda_{\ast})\big)\Big|-\Big|\Sigma^{b}\big(k_{-}(\lambda_{\ast})\big)\Big|+o_{p}(n)\Big)=\frac{1}{sn}\Big(C^{\mathrm{b}}_{n}(\lambda_{\ast})+o_{p}(n)\Big).

The conclusion now follows from Theorem
8
and (
23
).

Lemma 12
.

Let

. The following statements hold true for

.

(i)

Let

. If

\sigma(i^{\ast})
is adjacent to
\sigma(i^{\ast}+1)
; it follows that the two vertices must have opposite colours.

Let us set
A^{\prime}_{k}=\{\sigma(i):i_{\ast}<i<\tau^{b}_{k}\}

and show that

A_{k}\setminus\bigcup_{j\leq k-1}\mathcal{O}^{w}_{j}\subseteq A_{k}^{\prime}\subseteq\mathcal{J}^{w}_{k}.

(30)

Let
\tau^{b}_{k-1}<i\leq i_{\ast}
; then as in the previous case, we can show that
\sigma(i)\in\cup_{j\leq k-1}\mathcal{O}^{w}_{j}

by identifying the lowest ranking black vertex in its neighbourhood. This shows that
A_{k}\setminus A_{k}^{\prime}\subseteq\cup_{j\leq k-1}\mathcal{O}^{w}_{j}
, which then implies the first inclusion in (
30
).
For the second, we note that an element of
A^{\prime}_{k}
is either ranked between
i_{\ast}
and
i^{\ast}
, and therefore disconnected from the rest of the graph due to the absence of black vertices, or the white lead vertex
\sigma(i_{\ast})=\sigma(\tau^{b}_{k}-1)
. In both cases, they belong to
\mathcal{J}^{w}_{k}
.
This also completes the proof of (
29
) in
Case II
.

The two cases combined completes the proof for (
29
), which in turn proves (
28
) and the first identity in (
26
). For the second identity in (
26
) concerning the black vertices, we can similarly reduce its proof to showing that

\Sigma^{b}(\tau^{b}_{k})\setminus\Big(\bigcup_{j\leq k-1}\mathcal{O}^{b}_{j}\Big)\subseteq\mathcal{I}^{b}_{k}.

(31)

For
k=1
, this is true as both sides are equal to
\{\sigma^{b}(1)\}
. For the induction step, it suffices to show that

\{\sigma^{b}(k)\}\setminus\Big(\bigcup_{j\leq k-1}\mathcal{O}^{b}_{j}\Big)\subseteq\mathcal{I}^{b}_{k}.

(32)

If
\sigma^{b}(k)\in\mathcal{I}^{b}_{k}
, there is nothing to prove. Suppose that
\sigma^{b}(k)
is not a root vertex.
We follow the same arguments of Case 2 in the proof of Proposition
6
. By considering the path from
\sigma^{b}(k)
to the lowest ranking black vertex in the connected component, we can find some
j\leq k-1
so that
\sigma^{b}(k)\in\mathcal{N}^{2}(\sigma^{b}(j))
. Let
j_{\ast}
be the smallest such
j
. Then
\sigma^{b}(k)\notin\cup_{j^{\prime}<j_{\ast}}\mathcal{O}^{b}_{j^{\prime}}
and
\sigma^{b}_{k}\notin\Sigma^{b}(\tau^{b}_{j_{\ast}})
. It follows that
\sigma^{b}_{k}\in\mathcal{O}^{b}_{j_{\ast}}\subseteq\cup_{j\leq k-1}\mathcal{O}^{b}_{j}
, which implies (
32
) and completes the proof.

Finally, for
(iv)
, let us first show the first inclusion there. For
k=1
, we have

\mathcal{N}\big(\sigma^{b}(1)\big)\subseteq\big(\mathcal{N}\big(\sigma^{b}(1)\big)\cap\mathcal{K}^{w}_{1}\big)\cup(V^{w}_{n}\setminus\mathcal{K}^{w}_{1})=\mathcal{O}^{w}_{1}\cup\mathcal{J}^{w}_{1},

since
\mathcal{J}^{w}_{1}=\Sigma^{w}(\tau^{b}_{1})=V^{w}_{n}\setminus\mathcal{K}^{w}_{1}
.
This proves the case
k=1
. Assuming
\mathcal{N}(\Sigma^{b}(\tau^{b}_{k-1}))\subseteq\cup_{j\leq k-1}\mathcal{O}^{w}_{j}\cup\mathcal{J}^{w}_{k-1}
, let us show that

\mathcal{N}\big(\sigma^{b}(k)\big)\setminus\mathcal{O}^{w}_{k}\subseteq\bigcup_{j\leq k-1}\mathcal{O}^{w}_{j}\bigcup\mathcal{J}^{w}_{k},

(33)

which will imply the induction step.
However, by the definition of
\mathcal{O}^{w}_{k}
, we have
\mathcal{N}\big(\sigma^{b}(k)\big)\setminus\mathcal{O}^{w}_{k}\subseteq V^{w}_{n}\setminus\mathcal{K}^{w}_{k}
. The inclusion in (
33
) now follows from the first identity in (
26
). For the identity concerning
\mathcal{A}^{b}_{k}
, we deduce from (
15
) and (

) that

\mathcal{A}^{b}_{k}=\bigcup_{j\in[k]}\mathcal{N}^{2}(\sigma^{b}(j))\setminus\Sigma^{b}(\tau^{b}_{k})=\bigcup_{j\in[k]}\mathcal{O}^{b}_{j}\setminus\Sigma^{b}(\tau^{b}_{k}).

This completes the proof of
(iv)
.
∎

We next present a decomposition of
\mathcal{O}^{b}_{k}
. For
k\in[n_{b}]
,
if
\mathcal{O}^{w}_{k}
is nonempty, list its members in increasing order of their Prim’s ranks as
(v_{k,i})_{1\leq i\leq O^{w}_{k}}
. Let
\mathcal{K}_{k,1}=\mathcal{K}^{b}_{k}
; for
1\leq i\leq O^{w}_{k}
, set

\mathcal{N}_{i}=\mathcal{N}(v_{k,i})\cap\mathcal{K}_{k,i}\quad\text{and}\quad\mathcal{K}_{k,i+1}=\mathcal{K}_{k,i}\setminus\mathcal{N}_{i}.

(34)

We also set

\mathcal{N}_{0}=\mathcal{N}\big(\sigma(\tau^{b}_{k}-1)\big)\cap\mathcal{K}_{k,O^{w}_{k}+1}

if
\sigma(\tau^{b}_{k}-1)\in\mathcal{J}^{w}_{k}\cap\mathcal{N}(\sigma(\tau^{b}_{k}))
, and
\mathcal{N}_{0}=\varnothing
otherwise.

Lemma 13
.

For each
k\in[n_{b}]
, the sets
\mathcal{N}_{i},0\leq i\leq O^{w}_{k}
, are pairwise disjoint, and we have

\mathcal{O}^{b}_{k}=\bigcup_{0\leq i\leq O^{w}_{k}}\mathcal{N}_{i}.

(35)

Proof.

It is straightforward from the definition that
\mathcal{N}_{i}\cap\mathcal{N}_{i^{\prime}}=\varnothing
for any
0\leq i^{\prime}<i\leq|\mathcal{O}^{w}_{k}|
.
To see why (
35
) is true, we first deduce from (
34
) that

\bigcup_{1\leq i\leq O^{w}_{k}}\mathcal{N}_{i}=\mathcal{N}(\mathcal{O}^{w}_{k})\cap\mathcal{K}^{b}_{k}.

On the other hand, it is clear from its definition that
\mathcal{N}_{0}\subseteq\mathcal{N}^{2}(\sigma^{b}(k))\cap\mathcal{K}^{b}_{k}=\mathcal{O}^{b}_{k}
.
Hence, for (
35
) to hold, it suffices to show that

\mathcal{O}^{b}_{k}\setminus\mathcal{N}(\mathcal{O}^{w}_{k})\subseteq\mathcal{N}_{0}.

(36)

Let
u\in\mathcal{O}^{b}_{k}\setminus\mathcal{N}(\mathcal{O}^{w}_{k})
, which in particular implies
u\in\mathcal{K}^{b}_{k}\subseteq V^{b}_{n}\setminus\Sigma^{b}(\tau^{b}_{k})
.
Since
u\in\mathcal{N}^{2}(\sigma^{b}(k))
, we can find some
v\in\mathcal{N}(\sigma^{b}(k))
so that
u\in\mathcal{N}(v)
. According to (
27
), we find that either
v\in\cup_{j\leq k}\mathcal{O}^{w}_{j}
or
v\in\mathcal{J}^{w}_{k}
. From
u\notin\mathcal{N}(\mathcal{O}^{w}_{k})
, we deduce that
v\notin\mathcal{O}^{w}_{k}
. If
v\in\mathcal{O}^{w}_{j}
for some
j\leq k-1
, then applying (
27
) and (
26
), we find

u\in\mathcal{N}^{2}\big(\Sigma^{b}(\tau^{b}_{k-1})\big)\subseteq\bigcup_{j\leq k-1}\mathcal{O}^{b}_{j}\subseteq V^{b}_{n}\setminus\mathcal{K}^{b}_{k},

which contradicts with
u\in\mathcal{K}^{b}_{k}
. So the only possibility is
v\in\mathcal{J}^{w}_{k}
. As
u\in\mathcal{N}(v)
, Lemma
12

(i)
implies that
v=\sigma(\tau^{b}_{j}-1)\in\mathcal{J}^{w}_{k}
. However, if
j\leq k-1
, then we would find
u\in\mathcal{N}^{2}(\Sigma^{b}(\tau^{b}_{k-1}))
, which is not permitted by the previous arguments. We conclude that
v=\sigma(\tau^{b}_{k}-1)\in\mathcal{J}^{w}_{k}
and
u\in\mathcal{N}(v)
. Since
u\notin\mathcal{N}(\mathcal{O}^{w}_{k})
, this then implies (
36
).
∎

Let
n,k\in\mathbb{N}
and
p\in[0,1]
.
Recall the notation
\operatorname{Bin}(n,p)
for the binomial distribution with parameters
n
and
p
. We denote by
\mathcal{D}(n,k,p)
the distribution of the following sum of random variables:

X=\sum_{i=1}^{k}X_{i},

(37)

where
X_{1}\sim\operatorname{Bin}(n,p)
and for
1\leq i<k
, conditional on
(X_{1},X_{2},..,X_{i})
,
X_{i+1}
has the binomial distribution with parameters
n-\sum_{j=1}^{i}X_{j}
and
p
. We also let
\mathcal{D}(n,0,p)
denote the law of degenerate random variable
X\equiv 0
.

Recall
K^{w}_{k}
,
K^{w}_{k}
,
O^{b}_{k}
and
O^{w}_{k}
from (
25
).
The following result describes the distributions of
O^{w}_{k}
and
O^{b}_{k}
. Recall the filtrations
(\mathcal{G}^{w}_{k})_{k\geq 1}
and
(\mathcal{G}^{b}_{k})_{k\geq 1}
from Section
A
. Roughly speaking,
\mathcal{G}^{w}_{k}
represents the information available prior to the exploration of the
k
-th black vertex in Algorithm
2
with respect to Prim’s ranking, while
\mathcal{G}^{b}_{k}
is the analogue for the 2-neighbourhood exploration in Algorithm
3
.

Proposition 14
.

Let
p\in[0,1]
. The following statements hold for
G(n_{b},n_{w},p)
and
k\in[n_{b}]
.

(i)

Conditional on
\mathcal{G}^{w}_{k}
, we have

O^{w}_{k}\sim\operatorname{Bin}\big(K^{w}_{k},p\big).

(ii)

Let
\mathcal{N}_{i}
be as in (
34
) and set
\Delta_{k}=\sum_{1\leq i\leq O^{w}_{k}}|\mathcal{N}_{i}|
. Then
O^{b}_{k}\geq\Delta_{k}
; if
O^{b}_{k}>\Delta_{k}
, then
\sigma(\tau^{b}_{k}-1)
is a white lead vertex.
Conditional on
\mathcal{G}^{b}_{k}
, we have

\Delta_{k}\sim\mathcal{D}\big(K^{b}_{k},O^{w}_{k},p\big).

Proof.

Recall that
\sigma^{b}(k)=\sigma(\tau^{b}_{k})
.
For
(i)
, we have

\mathcal{O}^{w}_{k}=\mathcal{N}\big(\sigma^{b}(k)\big)\cap\mathcal{K}^{w}_{k}=\big\{v\in\mathcal{K}^{w}_{k}:U_{\{\sigma^{b}(k),v\}}\leq p\big\}.

Since
L_{k}:=\{e=\{\sigma^{b}(k),v\}:v\in\mathcal{K}^{w}_{k}\}
is contained in the set
G^{w}_{k}
introduced in (
78
) and is
\mathcal{G}^{w}_{k}
-measurable, we apply Lemma
29
to see that conditional on
\mathcal{G}^{w}_{k}
,
\{U_{e}:e\in L_{k}\}
are i.i.d. uniformly distributed. It follows that conditional on
\mathcal{G}^{w}_{k}
,
O^{w}_{k}
has the
\operatorname{Bin}(K^{w}_{k},p)
distribution.

Proceeding to
(ii)
, we note that
O^{b}_{k}\geq\Delta_{k}
is an immediate consequence of Lemma
13
. Moreover,
O^{b}_{k}>\Delta_{k}
implies
\mathcal{N}_{0}\neq\varnothing
, in which case
\sigma(\tau^{b}_{k}-1)\in\mathcal{J}^{w}_{k}
by the definition of
\mathcal{N}_{0}
.
For the distribution of
\Delta_{k}
, it suffices to consider the case
O^{w}_{k}=m\in\mathbb{N}
, since the claim is trivially true if
O^{w}_{k}=0
. We have

\mathcal{N}_{1}=\{u\in\mathcal{K}^{b}_{k}:U_{\{u,v_{k,1}\}}\leq p\}.

Since
v_{k,1}\in\mathcal{O}^{w}_{k}\subseteq\mathcal{K}^{w}_{k}
, the edge set
\{\{u,v_{k,1}\}:u\in\mathcal{K}^{b}_{k}\}
is a subset of the set
G^{b}_{k}
defined in (
79
). According to Lemma
30

(ii)
, we have
|\mathcal{N}_{1}|\sim\operatorname{Bin}(K^{b}_{k},p)
conditional on
\mathcal{G}^{b}_{k}
. More generally, let us denote

M_{i}=\big\{\{u,v_{k,i}\}:u\in\mathcal{K}^{b}_{k}\big\},\quad 1\leq i\leq m.

Then
M_{i},1\leq i\leq m
, are disjoint subsets of
G^{b}_{k}
. Lemma
30

(ii)
applies to yield that

\{U_{e}:e\in M_{i}\},\quad 1\leq i\leq m,

are independent, and each is distributed as a collection of i.i.d. uniform variables given
\mathcal{G}^{b}_{k}
.
We note that

\mathcal{N}_{i}=\{u\in\mathcal{K}_{k,i}:U_{\{u,v_{k,i}\}}\leq p\}

is a function of
\{U_{e}:e\in M_{i}\}
and
(\mathcal{N}_{j})_{j<i}
.
We conclude that conditional on
\mathcal{G}^{b}_{k}
and
(\mathcal{N}_{j})_{j<i}
,
|\mathcal{N}_{i}|\sim\operatorname{Bin}(|\mathcal{K}_{k,i}|,p)
.
This then implies
(iii)
as
|\mathcal{K}_{k,i}|=K^{b}_{k}-\sum_{j<i}|\mathcal{N}_{j}|
.
∎

Based on the distributions identified in Proposition
14
, we next derive upper and lower bounds for
O^{w}_{k}
and
O^{b}_{k}
. We will need the following elementary property of the binomial distribution, whose proof is omitted.

Lemma 15
.

Let
\mathcal{G}
be a sigma-algebra and
m\in\mathbb{N}
,
p\in[0,1]
. Suppose that
Y
is
\mathbb{Z}
-valued random variable satisfying
0\leq Y\leq m
and
Y\in\mathcal{G}
. Suppose further that conditional on
\mathcal{G}
,
X\sim\operatorname{Bin}(Y,p)
.
Let
\xi
be such a random variable that conditional on
\mathcal{G}
,
\xi\sim\operatorname{Bin}(m-Y,p)
and is independent of
X
. Then
X+\xi
has the
\operatorname{Bin}(m,p)
distribution and is independent of
\mathcal{G}
.

We recall that
\mathcal{J}^{w}_{k}
contains all the white lead vertices of ranks up to
\tau^{b}_{k}
. We denote by
J^{w}(k)
its cardinality.

Lemma 16

(Upper bounds for
O^{w}_{k}
and
O^{b}_{k}
)
.

Let
p\in[0,1]
and
\Delta_{k}
be as in Proposition
14
.
There exist sequences of random variables
(\bar{\xi}^{w}_{k})_{k\in[n_{b}]}
and
(\bar{\xi}^{b}_{k})_{k\in[n_{b}]}
such that

(i)

\{\bar{\xi}^{w}_{k}:k\in[n_{b}]\}
is a collection of i.i.d. with common distribution
\operatorname{Bin}(n_{w},p)
;

(ii)

conditional on
(O^{w}_{k})_{k\in[n_{b}]}
,
\{\bar{\xi}^{b}_{k}:k\in[n_{b}]\}
is a collection of independent random variables where
\bar{\xi}^{b}_{k}
is distributed according to
\operatorname{Bin}(n_{b}\cdot O^{w}_{k},p)
;

(iii)

O^{w}_{k}\leq\bar{\xi}^{w}_{k}
and
\Delta_{k}\leq\bar{\xi}^{b}_{k}
for all
k\in[n_{b}]
.

Moreover, we can find a sequence of i.i.d. random variables
(\chi_{i})_{i\geq 1}
with common distribution
\operatorname{Bin}(n_{b},p)
such that for all
k_{0}\in[n_{b}]
,

\sum_{1\leq k\leq k_{0}}(O^{b}_{k}-\Delta_{k})\leq\sum_{1\leq i\leq J^{w}(k_{0})}\chi_{i}\,;

(38)

Proof.

We begin with the construction of
(\bar{\xi}^{w}_{k})_{k\in[n_{b}]}
. For
k\in[n_{b}]
, let
\xi_{k}
be a random variable such that conditional on
\mathcal{G}^{w}_{k}
, it is distributed according to
\operatorname{Bin}(n_{w}-K^{w}_{k},p)
and independent of
\mathcal{U}
and
(\xi_{i})_{i\leq k-1}
; also let
\bar{\mathcal{G}}^{w}_{k}
be the sigma-algebra generated by
\mathcal{G}^{w}_{k}
and
(\xi_{i})_{i\leq k-1}
. We then set for each
k\in[n_{b}]
:

\bar{\xi}^{w}_{k}=O^{w}_{k}+\xi_{k}\geq O^{w}_{k}.

Let us argue that
\bar{\xi}^{w}_{k}\in\bar{\mathcal{G}}^{w}_{k+1}
,
\bar{\xi}^{w}_{k}\sim\operatorname{Bin}(n_{w},p)
and is independent of
\bar{\mathcal{G}}^{w}_{k}
, which will imply
(i)
.
The fact that
\bar{\xi}^{w}_{k}\in\bar{\mathcal{G}}^{w}_{k+1}
is a direct consequence of
O^{w}_{k}\in\mathcal{G}^{w}_{k+1}
(Lemma
29
) and the definition of
\bar{\mathcal{G}}^{w}_{k}
. We will prove the two remaining properties by induction.
For
k=1
, this is a direct consequence of Lemma
15
since
\bar{\mathcal{G}}^{w}_{1}=\mathcal{G}^{w}_{1}
. Assume that the properties hold for all
i\leq k-1
. Proposition
14
says that conditional on
\mathcal{G}^{w}_{k}
,
O^{w}_{k}\sim\operatorname{Bin}(K^{w}_{k},p)
. Since by construction
\xi_{i}
is conditionally independent of
\mathcal{U}
given
\mathcal{G}^{w}_{i}
, and since
\mathcal{G}^{w}_{k}
is obtained from
\mathcal{G}^{w}_{i}
and a subset of
\mathcal{U}
,
\xi_{i}
is also conditionally independent of
\mathcal{U}
given
\mathcal{G}^{w}_{k}
, and therefore conditionally independent of
O^{w}_{k}
given
\mathcal{G}^{w}_{k}
. As
\bar{\mathcal{G}}^{w}_{k}
is obtained from
\mathcal{G}^{w}_{k}
and
(\xi_{i})_{i\leq k-1}
, we deduce that given
\bar{\mathcal{G}}^{w}_{k}
,
O^{w}_{k}
is still distributed as
\operatorname{Bin}(K^{w}_{k},p)
. Applying Lemma
15
yields that
\bar{\xi}^{w}_{k}\sim\operatorname{Bin}(n_{w},p)
and is independent of
\bar{\mathcal{G}}^{w}_{k}
.

The construction for
(\bar{\xi}^{b}_{k})_{k\in[n_{b}]}
is similar albeit slightly more involved. Recall the sets
\mathcal{N}_{i}
from (
34
). Proposition
14
combined with (
37
) says that
\Delta_{k}=\sum_{1\leq i\leq O^{w}_{k}}|\mathcal{N}_{i}|
is a sum of binomial variables, with
|\mathcal{N}_{1}|\sim\operatorname{Bin}(K^{b}_{k},p)
.
Note that
O^{w}_{k}\in\mathcal{G}^{b}_{k}
.
Applying Lemma
15
to
|\mathcal{N}_{1}|
and
\mathcal{G}^{b}_{k}
, we can find a random variable
\xi_{k,1}
such that given
\mathcal{G}^{b}_{k}
,
\xi_{k,1}\sim\operatorname{Bin}(n_{b},p)
and
|\mathcal{N}_{1}|\leq\xi_{k,1}
. We then note that conditional on
\mathcal{G}^{b}_{k}
and
|\mathcal{N}_{1}|
,
|\mathcal{N}_{2}|\sim\operatorname{Bin}(K^{b}_{k}-|\mathcal{N}_{1}|,p)
. Applying Lemma
15
allows us to find some
\xi_{k,2}
such that given
\mathcal{G}^{b}_{k}
and
|\mathcal{N}_{1}|
,
\xi_{k,2}\sim\operatorname{Bin}(n_{b},p)
, is independent of
\xi_{k,1}
and satisfies
|\mathcal{N}_{2}|\leq\xi_{k,2}
. Iterating this procedure, we can then find a collection
\{\xi_{k,i}:1\leq i\leq O^{w}_{k},k\in[n_{b}]\}
such that for
k\in[n_{b}]
,
conditional on
\mathcal{G}^{b}_{k}
and
\{\xi_{j,i}:1\leq i\leq O^{w}_{i},j\leq k-1\}
,
\{\xi_{k,i}:1\leq i\leq O^{w}_{k}\}
is
i.i.d.
\operatorname{Bin}(n_{b},p)
-distributed, independent of
\mathcal{U}
and verifies

\Delta_{k}\leq\bar{\xi}^{b}_{k}:=\sum_{i=1}^{O^{w}_{k}}\xi_{k,i}.

It follows that given
(O^{w}_{i})_{i\leq k}
,
(\bar{\xi}^{b}_{i})_{i\leq k}
is an independent sequence and
\bar{\xi}^{b}_{i}\sim\operatorname{Bin}(O^{w}_{i}\cdot n_{b},p)
.

It remains to construct
(\chi_{\ell})_{\ell\geq 1}
.
Recall
\zeta_{\ell}
and
\mathcal{H}_{\ell}
from Section
A
.
Lemma
31
implies that
\{\sigma(\zeta_{\ell})\in V^{w}_{n}\}
is
\mathcal{H}_{\ell}
-measurable. Moreover, since
\sigma(\zeta_{\ell})
is a lead vertex, and therefore disconnected from any lower ranking vertices, we obtain from Lemma
31
that given
\mathcal{H}_{\ell}
and
\sigma(\zeta_{\ell})\in V^{w}_{n}
, we have

\big|\mathcal{N}\big(\sigma(\zeta_{\ell})\big)\big|=\big|\mathcal{N}\big(\sigma(\zeta_{\ell})\big)\setminus\Sigma^{b}(\zeta_{\ell})\big|\sim\operatorname{Bin}(n_{b}-|\Sigma^{b}(\zeta_{\ell})|,p).

Applying Lemma
15
then allows us to find a sequence
(\chi^{\prime}_{\ell})_{\ell\geq 1}
such that given
\mathcal{H}_{\ell}
,
\chi^{\prime}_{\ell}\sim\operatorname{Bin}(n_{b},p)
and is independent of
(\chi^{\prime}_{i})_{i<\ell}
, and for all
\ell\geq 1
, we also have

\big|\mathcal{N}\big(\sigma(\zeta_{\ell})\big)\big|\mathbf{1}_{\{\sigma(\zeta_{\ell})\in V^{w}_{n}\}}\leq\chi^{\prime}_{\ell}\mathbf{1}_{\{\sigma(\zeta_{\ell})\in V^{w}_{n}\}}.

(39)

It follows that
\{\chi_{i}:i\geq 1\}:=\{\chi^{\prime}_{\ell}:\sigma(\zeta_{\ell})\in V^{w}_{n},\ell\geq 1\}
is i.i.d. with common distribution
\operatorname{Bin}(n_{b},p)
.

It remains to show (
38
). To that end, we recall from Lemma
13
and Proposition
14
that
O^{b}_{k}-\Delta_{k}=|\mathcal{N}_{0}|
. We also recall from the definition of
\mathcal{N}_{0}
that
|\mathcal{N}_{0}|>0
implies that we can find some
\ell\geq 1
so that
\tau^{b}_{k}-1=\zeta_{\ell}
and
\sigma(\zeta_{\ell})\in V^{w}_{n}
. Hence,

\displaystyle O^{b}_{k}-\Delta_{k}

\displaystyle=\sum_{\ell\geq 1}|\mathcal{N}_{0}|\mathbf{1}_{\{\tau^{b}_{k}-1=\zeta_{\ell},\sigma(\zeta_{\ell})\in V^{w}_{n}\}}\leq\sum_{\ell\geq 1}\big|\mathcal{N}(\sigma(\zeta_{\ell}))\big|\mathbf{1}_{\{\tau^{b}_{k}-1=\zeta_{\ell},\sigma(\zeta_{\ell})\in V^{w}_{n}\}}

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{\ell\geq 1}\chi^{\prime}_{\ell}\mathbf{1}_{\{\tau^{b}_{k}-1=\zeta_{\ell},\sigma(\zeta_{\ell})\in V^{w}_{n}\}},

where in the second inequality above we have used the definition of
\mathcal{N}_{0}
and (
39
) in the last line.
Summing over
k\leq k_{0}
we find that

\sum_{k\leq k_{0}}(O^{b}_{k}-\Delta_{k})\leq\sum_{k\leq k_{0}}\sum_{\ell\geq 1}\chi^{\prime}_{\ell}\mathbf{1}_{\{\tau^{b}_{k}-1=\zeta_{\ell},\sigma(\zeta_{\ell})\in V^{w}_{n}\}}\leq\sum_{\ell\geq 1}\chi^{\prime}_{\ell}\mathbf{1}_{\{\zeta_{\ell}\leq\tau^{b}_{k_{0}},\,\sigma(\zeta_{\ell})\in V^{w}_{n}\}}=\sum_{\ell\geq 1}\chi^{\prime}_{\ell}\mathbf{1}_{\{\sigma(\zeta_{\ell})\in\mathcal{J}^{w}_{k_{0}}\}},

where the last term is equal to the sum of the first
J^{w}(k_{0})
entries of
(\chi_{i})_{i\geq 1}
. The proof is complete.
∎

Lemma 17

(Lower bounds for
O^{w}_{k}
and
O^{b}_{k}
)
.

Let
\underline{n}_{w},\underline{n}_{b}\in\mathbb{N}
. There exist sequences of random variables
(\underline{\xi}^{w}_{k})_{k\in[n_{b}]}
and
(\underline{\xi}^{b}_{k})_{k\in[n_{b}]}
that satisfy

(i)

\{\underline{\xi}^{w}_{k}:k\in[n_{b}]\}
is a collection of i.i.d. random variables with common distribution
\operatorname{Bin}(\underline{n}_{w},p)
;

(ii)

conditional on
(O^{w}_{k})_{k\in[n_{b}]}
,
\{\underline{\xi}^{b}_{k}:k\in[n_{b}]\}
is a collection of independent variables and
\underline{\xi}^{b}_{k}\sim\operatorname{Bin}(O^{w}_{k}\underline{n}_{b},p)
for each
k\in[n_{b}]
.

(iii)

O^{w}_{k}\geq\underline{\xi}^{w}_{k}\mathbf{1}_{\{K^{w}_{k}\geq\underline{n}_{w}\}}
and
O^{b}_{k}\geq\underline{\xi}^{b}_{k}\mathbf{1}_{\{K^{w}_{k+1}\geq\underline{n}_{b}\}}
for all

.

Proof.

Let us denote the event
A_{k}=\{K^{w}_{k}\geq\underline{n}_{w}\}
. We note that

thanks to Lemma

.
Moreover, we have seen in the proof of Proposition

,

\big\{\mathbf{1}_{U_{e}\leq p}:e=\{\sigma^{b}(1),v\},v\in\mathcal{K}^{w}_{1}\}

(p)

be the subset of

consisting of the

\mathcal{K}^{\prime}_{1}\in\mathcal{G}^{w}_{1}
. We then set

\underline{\xi}^{w}_{1}=\big|\big\{\mathbf{1}_{U_{e}\leq p}:e=\{\sigma^{b}(1),v\},v\in\mathcal{K}^{\prime}_{1}\big\}\big|

on
A_{1}
, while on

\underline{\xi}^{w}_{1}
be a variable of
\operatorname{Bin}(\underline{n}_{w},p)
-distribution, independent of
\mathcal{G}^{w}_{1}
.
It is then clear that
\underline{\xi}^{w}_{1}\leq O^{w}_{1}
on
A_{1}
and
\underline{\xi}^{w}_{1}\sim\operatorname{Bin}(\underline{n}_{w},p)
. Since the distribution of
\underline{\xi}^{w}_{1}
remains the same on

and

\underline{\xi}^{w}_{1}
is in fact
independent of
\mathcal{G}^{w}_{1}

\underline{\xi}^{w}_{1}

\mathcal{G}^{w}_{2}

Proof in the sublinear regime

We prove Theorem

in this section. In fact, we will prove the following slightly stronger result. Recall from Theorem

that
\gamma_{\theta}=\sqrt{\frac{1-\theta}{\theta}}

Theorem 18

Assume that (

) holds.
Let

is an immediate consequence of Theorem

The rest of this section is organised as follows. In Section

6.1
An overview of the proof in the sublinear regime

We recall the random graph

with the edge set
E_{n}(p)
introduced in (

). Throughout Section

, we take

to be critical, i.e.

p=\frac{1}{\sqrt{n_{b}n_{w}}}.

Recall that
\tau^{b}_{k}
is the Prim rank of the
k
th black vertex.
The proof of Theorem

consists in estimating

\lfloor\kappa_{n}t\rfloor
entries in the Prim sequence.
As a first step towards this end, we consider the set
\Sigma^{w}(\tau^{b}_{k})
and provide an approximation of its size using quantities that appear in the exploration of
G(n_{b},n_{w},p)
. More specifically,
recall that
\mathcal{J}^{w}_{k}
is the set of white lead vertices of ranks up to
\tau^{b}_{k}
and
J^{w}(k)=|\mathcal{J}^{w}_{k}|
;
for
k\in[n_{b}]
, let

R(k)=|\Sigma^{w}(\tau^{b}_{k})|-J^{w}(k).

(40)

Proposition 19
.

Let the assumptions of Theorem
18
hold and let

.
The following convergence holds in probability:

\frac{1}{\kappa_{n}}J^{w}\big(|\Sigma^{b}(\kappa_{n})|\big)\xrightarrow{n\to\infty}0.

Proposition 20
.

Let the assumptions of Theorem
18
hold and let
p=\frac{1}{\sqrt{n_{b}n_{w}}}
.
The following convergence holds in probability:

\frac{1}{\kappa_{n}}\sup_{j\leq|\Sigma^{b}(\kappa_{n})|}\big|R(j)-\gamma_{\theta}j\big|\xrightarrow{n\to\infty}0.

Proposition

is proved in Section

and Proposition

in Section

. Both proofs rely on the 2-neigbhourhood exploration of

in Algorithm
3
.

The dual problem.

At various points in our proof, we will employ the following coupling.
By flipping the vertex colours–black to white and white to black–we obtain a graph
\hat{K}_{n_{b},n_{w}}
on the same vertex set
V_{n}
and the edge set
E_{n}
but with reversed vertex colours.
In particular, the proportion of black vertices in
\hat{K}_{n_{b},n_{w}}
is given by

\frac{n_{w}}{n}\xrightarrow{n\to\infty}\hat{\theta}:=1-\theta.

Run Algorithm
1
on
\hat{K}_{n_{b},n_{w}}
with the same edge weights
\{U_{e}:e\in E_{n}\}
and in
Step 1
start with the same
\sigma(1)
, which is also uniformly distributed on the vertex set of
\hat{K}_{n_{b},n_{w}}
.
Then it is not difficult to see that the algorithm will produce the same Prim sequence
(\sigma(k))_{k\in[n]}
as well as the same Prim edges
(e_{k})_{k\in[n-1]}
. Let
\tau^{w}_{k}
be the rank of the
k
th white vertex in
(\sigma(i))_{i\in[n]}
for
K_{n_{b},n_{w}}
.
Define
\hat{\Sigma}^{b}(k),\hat{\Sigma}^{w}(k)
,
\hat{\tau}^{b}_{k}
,
\hat{\tau}^{w}_{k}
to be the analogues of
\Sigma^{b}(k)
,
\Sigma^{w}(k)
,
\tau^{b}_{k}
,
\tau^{w}_{k}
for
\hat{K}_{n_{b},n_{w}}
. Note that we have

\hat{\Sigma}^{w}(k)=\Sigma^{b}(k),\quad\hat{\Sigma}^{b}(k)=\Sigma^{w}(k),\quad\hat{\tau}^{w}_{k}=\tau^{b}_{k}.

(41)

Let
\hat{G}(n_{b},n_{w},p)
be the subgraph of
\hat{K}_{n_{b},n_{w}}
with the edge set
E_{n}(p)
. Then
\hat{G}(n_{b},n_{w},p)
has the same distribution as
G(n_{w},n_{b},p)
; in particular, all our previous estimates apply to
\hat{G}(n_{b},n_{w},p)
.

We are now ready to prove Theorem
18
.

Proof of Theorem
18
.

Let us first show that

\rho^{(n)}_{\kappa_{n}}=\frac{|\Sigma^{b}(\kappa_{n})|}{\kappa_{n}}\xrightarrow{n\to\infty}\frac{1}{1+\gamma_{\theta}}\quad\text{in probability.}

(42)

To ease the notation, let us denote
\eta_{n}=|\Sigma^{b}(\kappa_{n})|
, which is bounded by both
\kappa_{n}
and
n_{b}
.
Applying in tandem (
40
), Proposition
19
and Proposition
20
, we find that

\big|\Sigma^{w}\big(\tau_{\eta_{n}}^{b}\big)\big|=R(\eta_{n})+J^{w}(\eta_{n})=R(\eta_{n})+o_{p}(\kappa_{n})=\gamma_{\theta}\eta_{n}+o_{p}({\kappa_{n}}),\quad n\to\infty.

Since by definition,
|\Sigma^{b}(\tau_{j}^{b})|=j
for each
j\leq n_{b}
, we can re-write the previous identity as follows:

\big|\Sigma^{w}\big(\tau^{b}_{\eta_{n}}\big)\big|=\gamma_{\theta}\big|\Sigma^{b}\big(\tau_{\eta_{n}}^{b}\big)\big|+o_{p}({\kappa_{n}}),\quad n\to\infty.

(43)

Recall
\hat{G}(n_{b},n_{w},p)
from above, where the proportion of black vertices converges to
1-\theta
. As noted previously, all our previous estimates also apply to
\hat{G}(n_{b},n_{w},p)
, so that we have the following analogue of (
43
):

\big|\hat{\Sigma}^{w}\big(\hat{\tau}^{b}_{|\hat{\Sigma}^{b}(\kappa_{n})|}\big)\big|=\gamma_{1-\theta}\big|\hat{\Sigma}^{b}\big(\hat{\tau}_{|\hat{\Sigma}^{b}(\kappa_{n})|}^{b}\big)\big|+o_{p}({\kappa_{n}}),\quad n\to\infty.

By the identity (
41
) and the fact that
\gamma_{1-\theta}=\gamma_{\theta}^{-1}
, we find that

\big|\Sigma^{b}\big(\tau_{|\Sigma^{w}(\kappa_{n})|}^{w}\big)\big|=\gamma_{\theta}^{-1}\big|\Sigma^{w}\big(\tau_{|\Sigma^{w}(\kappa_{n})|}^{w})\big|+o_{p}({\kappa_{n}}),\quad n\to\infty.

(44)

We note that if
\sigma(\kappa_{n})
is black, then
\tau_{|\Sigma^{b}(\kappa_{n})|}^{b}=\kappa_{n}
; otherwise
\tau_{|\Sigma^{w}(\kappa_{n})|}^{w}=\kappa_{n}
.
Hence, (
43
) and (
44
) together show that

\big|\Sigma^{b}(\kappa_{n})\big|=\gamma_{\theta}^{-1}\big|\Sigma^{w}(\kappa_{n})\big|+o_{p}(\kappa_{n})=\gamma_{\theta}^{-1}\big(\kappa_{n}-\big|\Sigma^{b}(\kappa_{n})\big|\big)+o_{p}(\kappa_{n}),\quad n\to\infty,

since
\big|\Sigma^{b}(\kappa_{n})\big|+\big|\Sigma^{w}(\kappa_{n})\big|=\kappa_{n}
. Re-arranging the terms and dividing both sides by
\kappa_{n}
yields (
42
). Now let
t\in(0,1]
. Since the sequence
(\lfloor\kappa_{n}t\rfloor)_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
also verifies the assumption of Theorem
18
, (
42
) applies to yield that

\frac{1}{\kappa_{n}}\big|\Sigma^{b}(\lfloor\kappa_{n}t\rfloor)\big|\xrightarrow{n\to\infty}\frac{t}{1+\gamma_{\theta}}\quad\text{in probability.}

We also point out that the previous convergence also holds trivially for
t=0
. Finally, the monotonicity of
|\Sigma^{b}(\lfloor\kappa_{n}t\rfloor)|
in

implies that for any
t\in[t_{1},t_{2}]\subseteq[0,1]
,

\frac{1}{\kappa_{n}}\big|\Sigma^{b}(\lfloor\kappa_{n}t_{1}\rfloor)\big|-\frac{t_{2}}{1+\gamma_{\theta}}\leq\frac{1}{\kappa_{n}}\big|\Sigma^{b}(\lfloor\kappa_{n}t\rfloor)\big|-\frac{t}{1+\gamma_{\theta}}\leq\frac{1}{\kappa_{n}}\big|\Sigma^{b}(\lfloor\kappa_{n}t_{2}\rfloor)\big|-\frac{t_{1}}{1+\gamma_{\theta}}.

Hence, by dividing
[0,1]
into sufficiently small sub-intervals, we can deduce the claimed uniform convergence from the previous pointwise convergence.
∎

Proof of Proposition
19

Recall that
\mathcal{J}^{w}_{k}
and
\mathcal{I}^{b}_{k}
stand respectively for the set of the white lead vertices and root vertices from
\Sigma(\tau^{b}_{k})
, and
J^{w}(k)=|\mathcal{J}^{w}_{k}|
,
I^{b}(k)=|\mathcal{I}^{b}_{k}|
. We prove Proposition
19
here, by first deriving a bound for

and then deducing a similar bound for
J^{w}(k)
using the duality argument. Throughout this subsection, we work under the assumptions of Theorem
18
and

.

Recall
\mathcal{O}^{b}_{k}
from (
13
) and
\mathcal{O}^{w}_{k}
from (
16
), and note that
O^{w}_{k}=|\mathcal{O}^{w}_{k}|
,
O^{b}_{k}=|\mathcal{O}^{b}_{k}|
. We set
S^{w}_{0}=S^{b}_{0}=0
and for
k\in[n_{b}]
,

S^{w}_{k}=\sum_{j\in[k]}O^{w}_{j},\qquad S^{b}_{k}=\sum_{j\in[k]}O^{b}_{j},

(45)

which corresponds to the respective sizes of the disjoint unions
\cup_{j\leq k}\mathcal{O}^{w}_{j}
and
\cup_{j\leq k}\mathcal{O}^{b}_{j}
. On the other hand, recall from (
27
) that

\mathcal{A}^{b}_{k}=\bigcup_{j\in[k]}\mathcal{O}^{b}_{j}\setminus\Sigma^{b}(\tau^{b}_{k})=\Big\{\sigma^{b}(i):i>k,\sigma^{b}(i)\in\bigcup_{j\in[k]}\mathcal{O}^{b}_{j}\Big\}.

(46)

Let us also denote

\mathcal{A}^{w}_{k}=\Big\{\sigma(i):i>\tau^{b}_{k},\,\sigma(i)\in\bigcup_{j\in[k-1]}\mathcal{O}^{w}_{j}\Big\},

(47)

and
A^{w}(k)=|\mathcal{A}^{w}_{k}|
,
A^{b}(k)=|\mathcal{A}^{b}_{k}|
. Recall
R(k)
from (
40
). We first establish some combinatorial properties of the graph exploration process.

Lemma 21
.

For all
k\in[n_{b}]
, we have

A^{w}(k)=S^{w}_{k-1}-R(k)\quad\text{and}\quad A^{b}(k)=S^{b}_{k}-k+I^{b}(k);

(48)

moreover,

I^{b}(k)-1=-\inf_{j\in[k-1]}(S^{b}_{j}-j).

(49)

Proof.

For the first identity in (
48
), we note that by definition,
\Sigma^{w}(\tau^{b}_{k})\subseteq V^{w}_{n}\setminus\mathcal{K}^{w}_{k}
. Using the decomposition in (
26
), we find that

\Sigma^{w}(\tau^{b}_{k})\setminus\mathcal{J}^{w}_{k}\subseteq\bigcup_{j\in[k-1]}\mathcal{O}^{w}_{j}.

Since
\mathcal{J}^{w}_{k}
is disjoint from any
\mathcal{O}^{w}_{j}
,
the difference of the two sets is given by

\bigcup_{j\in[k-1]}\mathcal{O}^{w}_{j}\setminus\Big(\Sigma^{w}(\tau^{b}_{k})\setminus\mathcal{J}^{w}_{k}\Big)=\bigcup_{j\in[k-1]}\mathcal{O}^{w}_{j}\setminus\Sigma^{w}(\tau^{b}_{k})=\mathcal{A}^{w}_{k}.

On the one hand,
R(k)=|\Sigma^{w}(\tau^{b}_{k})|-J^{w}(k)=|\Sigma^{w}(\tau^{b}_{k})\setminus\mathcal{J}^{w}_{k}|
; on the other hand,
S^{w}_{k-1}
is the size of the disjoint union
\mathcal{O}^{w}_{j},j\leq k-1
. Combining this with the previous argument, we have shown
S^{w}_{k-1}-R(k)=A^{w}(k)
for each
k\in[n_{b}]
.

Using the second decomposition in (
26
) and the fact that
\Sigma^{b}(\tau^{b}_{k})\subseteq V^{b}_{n}\setminus\mathcal{K}^{b}_{k}
, we find that

\Sigma^{b}(\tau^{b}_{k})\setminus\mathcal{I}^{b}_{k}\subseteq\bigcup_{j\leq k-1}\mathcal{O}^{b}_{j}\subseteq\bigcup_{j\leq k}\mathcal{O}^{b}_{j}.

It follows that

A^{b}(k)=\Big|\cup_{j\leq k}\mathcal{O}^{b}_{j}\Big|-\Big|\Sigma^{b}(\tau^{b}_{k})\setminus\mathcal{I}^{b}_{k}\Big|=S^{b}_{k}-(k-I^{b}(k)),

since
|\Sigma^{b}(\tau^{b}_{k})|=k
. This completes the proof of (
48
).

To prove (
49
), let us first show that for
1\leq k\leq n_{b}
,

\sigma^{b}(k)\in\mathcal{I}^{b}_{k}\quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad\mathcal{A}^{b}_{k-1}=\varnothing\quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad A^{b}(k-1)=0,

(50)

with the convention
\mathcal{A}^{b}_{0}=\varnothing
.
Indeed, if
\mathcal{A}^{b}_{k-1}\neq\varnothing
, then
\sigma^{b}(k)\in\mathcal{A}^{b}_{k-1}=\mathcal{N}^{2}(\Sigma^{b}(\tau^{b}_{k-1}))
. It follows that we can find some

\sigma^{b}(k)
can not be the lowest ranking black vertex in its component.
If, on the other hand,
\mathcal{A}^{b}_{k-1}=\mathcal{N}^{2}(\Sigma^{b}(\tau^{b}_{k-1}))=\varnothing
, then
\sigma^{b}(k)
is disconnected from any member of
\Sigma^{b}(\tau^{b}_{k-1})
, and consequently
\sigma^{b}(k)
is a root vertex. This proves (
50
).

Denote
M(k)=-\inf_{j\leq k}(S^{b}_{j}-j)
, and note that
M(k)
is non-decreasing and has maximal jump size of 1. Write
B_{k}=\{j\in[k-1]:M(j)=M(j-1)+1\}
; we have
M(k-1)=|B_{k}|
. On the other hand, (
50
) implies that
I^{b}(k)=|B^{\prime}_{k}|
, where
B^{\prime}_{k}:=\{0\leq j\leq k-1:A^{b}(j)=0\}

B_{k}=B^{\prime}_{k}\setminus\{0\}
.

To that end, we assume that the elements of

are

. Then for all

, we have
I^{b}(j)=1
and
A^{b}(j)\geq 1
. We then deduce from the second identity in (
48
) that
S^{b}(j)-j=A^{b}(j)-I^{b}(j)\geq 0
, so that
M(j)=0
for all
j<j_{1}
. We also find
S(j_{1})-j_{1}=A^{b}(j_{1})-I^{b}(j_{1})=-1
. This shows
j_{1}
is the smallest element of
B_{k}
. Similarly, for
j_{1}<j<j_{2}
, we have
I^{b}(j)=2
and
S^{b}(j)-j\geq-1
, as well as
S^{b}(j_{2})=-2
. Iterating this procedure, we see that the elements of
B_{k}
are given by
j_{1},j_{2},\dots,j_{\ell}
, which completes the proof of (
49
) by the previous arguments.

Proposition 22

(Law of large numbers for
S^{w}
)
.

For all
\varepsilon>0
, the following convergence holds:

\mathbb{P}\Big(\sup_{k\leq|\Sigma^{b}(\kappa_{n})|}\Big|S^{w}_{k}-\gamma_{\theta}k\Big|\geq\varepsilon\kappa_{n}\Big)\xrightarrow{n\to\infty}0.

(51)

Proof.

Thanks to Lemma
16
, we have

\forall\,k\in[n_{b}]:\quad S^{w}_{k}\leq\bar{S}^{w}_{k}:=\sum_{i\leq k}\bar{\xi}^{w}_{i}.

Denote
\gamma^{(n)}_{\theta}=\sqrt{n_{w}/n_{b}}
and recall
p=1/\sqrt{n_{b}n_{w}}
. We note that Lemma
16
also says that
(\bar{\xi}^{w}_{i})_{i\in[n_{b}]}
is i.i.d. and satisfies

\mathbb{E}\big[\bar{\xi}^{w}_{1}\big]=n_{w}p=\gamma^{(n)}_{\theta},\quad\operatorname{Var}\big(\bar{\xi}^{w}_{1}\big)=n_{w}p(1-p)\leq\gamma^{(n)}_{\theta}.

By independence, we then have
\operatorname{Var}(\bar{S}^{w}_{k})\leq k\gamma^{(n)}_{\theta}
. Doob’s maximal inequality then asserts that for any
\varepsilon>0
,

\mathbb{P}\Big(\sup_{k\leq\kappa_{n}}|\bar{S}^{w}_{k}-\gamma^{(n)}_{\theta}k|\geq\varepsilon\kappa_{n}\Big)\leq\frac{4\operatorname{Var}(\bar{S}^{w}_{\kappa_{n}})}{\varepsilon^{2}\kappa_{n}^{2}}\leq\frac{4\gamma^{(n)}_{\theta}}{\varepsilon^{2}\kappa_{n}}\xrightarrow{n\to\infty}0,

since
\gamma^{(n)}_{\theta}\to\gamma_{\theta}
under the assumption (
1
).
It follows that for sufficiently large
n
,

\mathbb{P}\Big(\sup_{k\leq\kappa_{n}}\big|\bar{S}^{w}_{k}-\gamma_{\theta}k\big|\geq\varepsilon\kappa_{n}\Big)\leq\mathbb{P}\Big(\sup_{k\leq\kappa_{n}}|\bar{S}^{w}_{k}-\gamma^{(n)}_{\theta}k|\geq\tfrac{1}{2}\varepsilon\kappa_{n}\Big)\xrightarrow{n\to\infty}0.

Since
S^{w}_{k}\leq\bar{S}^{w}_{k}
, we deduce that

\mathbb{P}\Big(\sup_{k\leq\kappa_{n}}\big(S^{w}_{k}-\gamma_{\theta}k\big)\geq\varepsilon\kappa_{n}\Big)\xrightarrow{n\to\infty}0.

(52)

We will shorthand
\eta_{n}=|\Sigma^{b}(\kappa_{n})|
.
To derive a matching lower bound, we first note that thanks to Lemma
12

(ii)
and
(iii)
, we have

n_{w}-K^{w}_{\eta_{n}}\leq S^{w}_{\eta_{n}}+J^{w}(\eta_{n}).

(53)

Note further that
\tau^{b}_{\eta_{n}}=\tau^{b}_{|\Sigma^{b}(\kappa_{n})|}\leq\kappa_{n}
, so that
J^{w}(\eta_{n})\leq|\Sigma(\kappa_{n})|=\kappa_{n}
.
Together with (
52
) and (
53
), and the fact that
\eta_{n}\leq\kappa_{n}
this implies
n_{w}-K^{w}_{\eta_{n}}=O_{p}(\kappa_{n})=o_{p}(n_{w})
as
n\to\infty
. Let us take
\delta_{n}=\sqrt{\kappa_{n}/n}
, so that
\delta_{n}\to 0
and

\liminf_{n\to\infty}\mathbb{P}\big(K^{w}_{\eta_{n}}\geq(1-\delta_{n})n_{w}\big)=1.

(54)

Let
Q_{n}
be the event that
K^{w}_{\eta_{n}}\geq(1-\delta_{n})n_{w}
. Applying Lemma
17
with
\underline{n}_{w}=(1-\delta_{n})n_{w}
, we can find a sequence of i.i.d. random variables
(\underline{\xi}^{w}_{k})_{k\in[n_{b}]}
with common distribution
