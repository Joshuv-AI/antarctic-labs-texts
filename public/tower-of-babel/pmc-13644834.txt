LGR6 couples maresin 1 signaling to glycolytic flux and protein lactylation for the rescue of diabetic cardiomyopathy
Authors: Bin Feng, Chao Chen, Lin Chen, Yuqiong Chen, Wenjun Mao, Peiran Yu, Hao Yu, Bimin Shi, Nannan Zhang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTBackgroundDiabetic cardiomyopathy (DbCM) is driven by metabolic inflexibility and impaired mitochondrial clearance. While the pro‐resolving lipid mediator Maresin 1 (MaR1) shows therapeutic promise, its capacity to correct the underlying metabolic‐epigenetic disturbances in DbCM remains undefined.MaterialsDb/db mice and palmitic acid (PA)‐exposed H9c2 cardiomyocytes were treated with MaR1. Cardiac function and structure were assessed by echocardiography and histological analysis. Myocardial oxidative stress, inflammation, and key molecular pathways were evaluated via immunoblotting, qPCR, immunohistochemistry, and transmission electron microscopy. The role of the putative receptor LGR6 was investigated using siRNA‐mediated knockdown.ResultsMaR1 improved cardiac diastolic function, attenuated pathological hypertrophy and interstitial fibrosis, and reduced oxidative stress and inflammatory markers in db/db mice. Mechanistically, MaR1 activated AMPK signaling, restoring the expression of crucial enzymes for both fatty acid oxidation (CPT1B) and glycolysis (PFK‐1, HK2). This metabolic reprogramming elevated intracellular lactate, which in turn replenished the deficient global protein lysine lactylation (Kla). Concurrently, MaR1 reactivated the PINK1/Parkin pathway, restoring mitophagy‐associated signaling. In vitro, exogenous lactate supplementation recapitulated these effects. Importantly, LGR6 silencing abrogated all MaR1‐induced benefits, including glycolytic activation, lactylation rescue, and mitophagic recovery.ConclusionMaR1 protects against DbCM by signaling through LGR6 to restore metabolic flexibility, thereby fueling a lactate‐dependent lactylation program that is critically associated with the reactivation of mitochondrial quality control via mitophagy. This study unveils a critical metabolic‐epigenetic axis as a novel therapeutic target.

INTRODUCTION

Diabetic cardiomyopathy (DbCM) constitutes a distinct pathological entity defined by myocardial structural and functional abnormalities that arise independently of coronary artery disease and hypertension, contributing significantly to the excess mortality associated with diabetes mellitus. 1 , 2 , 3 Pathophysiologically, the diabetic heart is characterized by profound metabolic disturbances, primarily metabolic inflexibility marked by an overreliance on fatty acid oxidation and a maladaptive suppression of glucose utilization. 4 , 5 , 6 This metabolic mismatch precipitates mitochondrial dysfunction, evidenced by impaired bioenergetics, excessive reactive oxygen species (ROS) production, and the accumulation of damaged organelles. 7 Under physiological conditions, mitochondrial quality is rigorously maintained by mitophagy, a selective autophagy pathway that eliminates dysfunctional mitochondria. However, in the setting of chronic diabetes, mitophagic flux is frequently blunted, leading to the accretion of ROS‐generating mitochondria that exacerbate inflammation and apoptosis, ultimately driving cardiomyocyte hypertrophy and interstitial fibrosis. 8 , 9 , 10 , 11 Despite current therapeutic advances, strategies capable of simultaneously restoring metabolic homeostasis and rescuing mitochondrial integrity remain a critical unmet clinical need.

The resolution of inflammation is increasingly recognized as an active, biochemically regulated process governed by a superfamily of lipids termed specialized pro‐resolving mediators (SPMs), including resolvins, protectins, and maresins. 12 , 13 MaR1, a docosahexaenoic acid (DHA)‐derived mediator produced by macrophages, exhibits potent anti‐inflammatory, anti‐fibrotic, and tissue‐regenerative properties. 14 Recent investigations demonstrate that MaR1 exerts protective effects in various models of organ injury—including obesity‐induced insulin resistance, hepatic steatosis, and diabetic nephropathy—by attenuating cytokine production and promoting the clearance of apoptotic cells. 15 , 16 , 17 , 18 Moreover, MaR1 has demonstrated cardioprotective efficacy against doxorubicin‐induced cardiotoxicity and ischemia/reperfusion injury by suppressing inflammation and preserving mitochondrial function. 19 , 20 However, the molecular mechanisms linking MaR1‐mediated resolution to the restoration of cardiomyocyte metabolic fitness remain incompletely understood. Specifically, whether MaR1 can ameliorate the bioenergetic deficits of the diabetic heart to reactivate mitophagy warrants further investigation. Emerging evidence suggests that SPMs may influence cellular metabolism, yet the downstream signaling axes connecting metabolic recovery to cellular repair machinery in DbCM have yet to be elucidated. LGR6 is a leucine‐rich repeat‐containing G protein‐coupled receptor originally recognized as an adult stem/progenitor cell marker. 21 Importantly, LGR6 has recently been identified as a functional receptor for MaR1 in pro‐resolving phagocyte responses, 22 suggesting that MaR1 may signal through LGR6 to connect inflammatory resolution with cellular metabolic repair.

A pivotal breakthrough in understanding the crosstalk between metabolism and cellular function is the identification of histone and non‐histone protein lactylation, a post‐translational modification derived from lactate. 23 Once dismissed solely as a metabolic waste product, lactate is now appreciated as a critical signaling molecule that, via lactylation, modulates gene expression and protein function. 24 , 25 In the cardiac context, protein lactylation has emerged as a key modulator of diverse pathological processes, ranging from the regulation of sarcomeric architecture during heart failure progression 26 to the orchestration of endothelial‐to‐mesenchymal transition and reparative gene transcription following ischemia–reperfusion injury. 27 , 28 , 29 Furthermore, recent studies indicate a complex relationship between lactylation and mitochondrial dynamics, wherein specific lactylation events modulate mitophagy and apoptosis. 30 , 31 Given the suppression of glycolysis in the diabetic heart, we hypothesized that a deficiency in lactate‐derived signaling might contribute to the failure of mitochondrial quality control, and that MaR1 could bridge this gap by reprogramming myocardial metabolism.

In the present study, by integrating in vivo physiological assessments with in vitro molecular mechanistic studies, we sought to investigate whether MaR1 intervention could restore the interplay between fatty acid/glucose metabolism and protein lactylation, thereby reactivating mitochondrial autophagy and attenuating characteristic myocardial injury.

MATERIALS AND METHODS

Reagents

MaR1 (purity ≥ 99%; catalog no.: 14114; CAS 1268720–28‐0) was obtained from AbMole (Shanghai, China). Antibodies targeting the following proteins were procured for analyses: PFK‐1 (no.: ab154804), CBP (no.: ab2832), and PINK1 (no.: ab324681) from Abcam (Waltham, Massachusetts); Parkin (no.: 32282), p62 (no.: 48402), LGR6 (no.: 393010), and p300 (no.: 48343) from Santa Cruz Biotechnology (Dallas, Texas); CPT1B (no.: 41803), LC3B (no.: 83506), AMPKα (no.: 5832), phosphorylated AMPKα (Thr172; no.: 50081), HK2 (no.: 2867), ACC (no.: 3676), phosphorylated ACC (Ser79; no.: 11818), and LDHA (no.: 2012) from Cell Signaling Technology (Danvers, Massachusetts); and pan‐lactylation (pan‐Kla; no.: 1401RM) from PTM BIO (Hangzhou, China). β‐actin (no.: AF0003) and appropriate species‐specific secondary antibodies were acquired from Beyotime (Shanghai, China). Levels of the inflammatory cytokines IL‐1β, IL‐6, and TNF‐α were determined using ELISA kits from Boshen Biotechnology (nos.: abs520001, abs552805, and abs552204, respectively; Shanghai, China). Markers of oxidative stress, including 3‐nitrotyrosine (3‐NT) and 8‐hydroxy‐2′‐deoxyguanosine (8‐OHdG), were quantified using ELISA kits from Elabscience Biotechnology (nos.: E‐EL‐0040 and E‐EL‐0028; Wuhan, China). Bafilomycin A1 (BafA1; no.: Y262115; Beyotime) was used as the lysosomal inhibitor.

Animals and treatment

To investigate the effects of MaR1 on DbCM, male db/db mice (n = 18) and age‐matched nondiabetic db/m controls (n = 6) were obtained at 6 weeks of age from the Animal Center of Nanjing Medical University (Nanjing, Jiangsu, China). Animals were housed under specific pathogen–free conditions (20°C ± 2°C; 45%–65% humidity; 12‐h light/dark cycle) with access to sterile chow and water ad libitum. Following a 1‐week acclimatization period to establish stable baseline conditions, food intake, body weight, and fasting blood glucose (FBG) levels were recorded. The db/db mice were randomized into three cohorts (n = 6 each) and compared with vehicle‐treated db/m controls. The four study arms received daily intraperitoneal injections for a duration of 22 weeks: (1) db/m + vehicle; (2) db/db + vehicle; (3) db/db + MaR1 (3 ng/mouse); and (4) db/db + MaR1 (10 ng/mouse). Disease progression was tracked longitudinally, with metabolic parameters (weight and FBG) assessed at 4‐week intervals. Upon study completion, animals were euthanized via an intraperitoneal injection of sodium pentobarbital overdose (200 mg/kg) to ensure rapid and humane death. After verifying the absence of respiratory and cardiovascular function and loss of reflexes, hearts were excised and weighed immediately to quantify hypertrophy, then snap‐frozen in liquid nitrogen and stored at −80°C for downstream molecular analysis.

Cell experimental design

Rat embryonic cardiomyocytes (H9c2), a rat‐derived myoblast cell line (CVCL_0286), directly mimic the pathological behavior of cardiomyocytes in cardiac diseases. H9c2 cells were purchased from the American Type Culture Collection (ATCC; CRL‐1446). Cells were maintained in Dulbecco's Modified Eagle Medium supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS) and 1% penicillin–streptomycin at 37°C in a humidified atmosphere containing 5% CO2. Lipotoxicity was induced by exposing cells to 300 μM palmitic acid (PA) for 24 h. The PA–BSA conjugate was prepared as previously described, 32 with a BSA vehicle serving as the control. To evaluate dose‐dependency and protective efficacy, cells were treated with MaR1 (20 nM or 50 nM) or sodium lactate (10 mM) 4 h before PA exposure. For autophagic‐flux analysis, control, PA, and PA plus MaR1 (50 nM) cultures were prepared in parallel with or without BafA1. BafA1 was applied at 40 nM for the final 4 h before cell harvest. To determine whether LGR6 mediates the protective effects of MaR1, cells were transfected with small interfering RNA (siRNA) targeting LGR6; control cells received a non‐targeting scrambled siRNA. At 24 to 48 h after transfection, cells were treated with 50 nM MaR1 followed by PA exposure. The effect of LGR6 knockdown on MaR1‐mediated signaling was assessed by western blot analysis.

Morphologic, immunohistochemical, and fluorescence analyses

To assess structural cardiac alterations, formalin‐fixed, paraffin‐embedded heart tissue was sectioned at a thickness of 4 μm. Sections were deparaffinized, rehydrated, and stained with hematoxylin and eosin (H&E) to examine cardiomyocyte morphology. Myocardial fibrosis was evaluated using Masson trichrome staining, with the collagen area quantified via the ImageJ software (National Institutes of Health). Immunohistochemical analysis was conducted to assess metabolic enzyme expression and protein lactylation. Following heat‐induced antigen retrieval in Tris‐EDTA buffer (pH 9.0; 98°C for 10 min), sections were blocked for endogenous peroxidase activity (3% H2O2) and nonspecific binding (5% BSA). Tissues were incubated overnight at 4°C with primary antibodies targeting LDHA (1:300) and pan‐Kla (1:200). Signal detection was achieved using a horseradish peroxidase–conjugated secondary antibody (1:500) and DAB substrate. The integrated optical density (IOD) of positive signals was quantified using ImageJ. Cardiomyocyte hypertrophy was evaluated through fluorescence staining. Deparaffinized sections were incubated in the dark with fluorophore‐conjugated wheat germ agglutinin (WGA) (1:250) to visualize cell membranes. Images were acquired using a Leica microscope. Cardiomyocyte surface area was calculated by analyzing a minimum of 40 randomly selected cells per section using ImageJ.

Echocardiographic assessment

Transthoracic echocardiography was performed to evaluate cardiac structure and function. Mice were anesthetized using 1.5%–2.0% isoflurane in 100% oxygen via a nose cone. Following induction of anesthesia, mice were placed in a supine position on a heating pad to maintain core body temperature at 37°C, with continuous electrocardiographic and heart rate monitoring. Isoflurane levels were carefully titrated to maintain a heart rate greater than 400 beats per minute to minimize cardiodepressant effects. Two‐dimensional (2D)–guided M‐mode imaging was acquired in the parasternal short‐axis view at the papillary muscle level using a Vevo 2,100 system (VisualSonics, Toronto, Canada). Left ventricular (LV) internal dimensions at end‐diastole (LVEDD) and end‐systole (LVESD) were measured to calculate fractional shortening (FS) and ejection fraction (EF). Diastolic function was assessed by pulsed‐wave Doppler interrogation of mitral valve inflow from the apical 4‐chamber view. Peak early (E wave) and late (A wave) diastolic transmitral flow velocities were recorded, and the E/A ratio was derived. All reported values represent the mean of three consecutive cardiac cycles. Analyses were conducted in a blinded manner to minimize bias.

Biochemical assessment in myocardial tissue

Myocardial tissue was weighed and homogenized in ice‐cold phosphate‐buffered saline (PBS). Following centrifugation at 12,000 × g for 15 min at 4°C, supernatants were harvested for analysis. Redox homeostasis was evaluated by quantifying the specific activities of superoxide dismutase (SOD) and catalase (CAT), as well as the content of malondialdehyde (MDA), a marker of lipid peroxidation; these assays were conducted using commercial kits (Nanjing Jiancheng Bioengineering Institute, China). Additionally, ELISAs were performed to determine the concentrations of 3‐nitrotyrosine (3‐NT; a marker of nitrosative stress), 8‐hydroxy‐2′‐deoxyguanosine (8‐OHdG; indicating DNA damage), and the pro‐inflammatory cytokines IL‐1β, IL‐6, and TNF‐α. All assays were executed according to the manufacturers' instructions, and values were normalized to the total‐protein concentration of the supernatants.

Quantitative real‐time PCR

To assess gene expression, total RNA was first extracted from tissues using RNAiso Plus (no.: 9108; Takara) per the manufacturer's instructions. Subsequently, 1 μg of the isolated RNA underwent reverse transcription using the PrimeScript RT reagent Kit (no.: RR047A; Takara). Gene transcript levels were quantified by real‐time PCR (qPCR) using SYBR Green chemistry (Applied Biosystems). For each sample, reactions were run in triplicate under standard cycling parameters. Data were analyzed using the 2−ΔΔCt method to determine relative mRNA expression, normalized to β‐actin mRNA levels. Table 1 details the specific primer sequences used in this analysis.

Western blot

Lysates from heart tissue and cultured cells were prepared in radioimmunoprecipitation assay (RIPA) buffer, and protein concentrations were determined using a bicinchoninic acid (BCA) assay kit (Beyotime, China). Equal amounts of protein were resolved by 12% to 15% sodium dodecyl sulfate–polyacrylamide gel electrophoresis (SDS‐PAGE) and transferred to polyvinylidene difluoride (PVDF) membranes. Following blocking, membranes were incubated overnight at 4°C with primary antibodies against PFK‐1 (1:1500), CPT1B (1:1500), AMPK (1:2000), phosphorylated AMPKα (p‐AMPKα; 1:600), PINK1 (1:1500), Parkin (1:1000), p62 (1:1000), LC3B (1:1000), pan‐Kla (1:500), CBP (1:1000), p300 (1:1000), HK2 (1:1000), LDHA (1:1500), and β‐actin (1:2500). Membranes were subsequently incubated for 1 h at room temperature with horseradish peroxidase (HRP)–conjugated secondary antibodies (1:4000). Protein bands were visualized using an enhanced chemiluminescence (ECL) substrate. Images were acquired using the Bio‐Rad ChemiDoc MP Imaging System (Bio‐Rad Laboratories, Hercules, CA, USA). Band intensity was quantified via densitometry using the ImageJ software.

Targeted silencing of LGR6 via RNA interference

To determine whether the biological activity of MaR1 is dependent on LGR6 signaling, loss‐of‐function experiments were conducted in H9c2 cells. Gene silencing was achieved by transfecting cells with Lipofectamine 2000 (Invitrogen) carrying either LGR6‐targeting siRNA or a non‐targeting control (Sangon Biotech). The specific LGR6 siRNA sequences utilized were: sense, 5′‐GGGUCUCCUUGCUGGACAAGC‐3′; and antisense, 5′‐UUGUCCAGCAAGGAGACCCAG‐3′. Following 6 h of transfection at 37°C, the complex was removed and replaced with fresh medium containing 50 nM MaR1 to challenge the cells under conditions of LGR6 depletion. Cultures were maintained for an additional 36 h before harvesting.

Transmission electron microscopy analysis

Fresh myocardial tissue was excised and immediately sectioned into blocks of approximately 1 mm3. Specimens were fixed in 2.5% glutaraldehyde at 4°C overnight, followed by postfixation with 1% osmium tetroxide (OSO4) for 1 to 2 h. En bloc staining was performed with 2% uranyl acetate. Tissues were dehydrated through a graded ethanol and acetone series, infiltrated, and embedded in epoxy resin; polymerization was conducted at 60°C for 48 h. Ultrathin sections (70–90 nm) were prepared via ultramicrotomy, mounted on copper grids, and counterstained with 4% uranyl acetate and lead citrate. Ultrastructural features were visualized using a transmission electron microscope (FEI Tecnai 10; Philips).

Measurement of extracellular lactate levels

Lactate levels in the cell culture supernatant were determined via a colorimetric assay (ab65330; Abcam). Following collection and centrifugation of the culture medium, supernatants were passed through 10‐kDa spin columns (ab93349; Abcam). This deproteinization step was essential to eliminate potential assay interference caused by endogenous lactate dehydrogenase present in the BSA. The resulting filtrates (50 μL) were then reacted with an equivalent volume of a mixture containing assay buffer, enzyme, and OxiRed probe in a 96‐well format. Samples were incubated for 30 min at room temperature, protected from light to preserve fluorometric stability. Absorbance was recorded at 570 nm, and final lactate concentrations (mM) were calculated relative to a linear standard curve after background correction.

NAD + and NADH quantification

H9c2 cells were lysed using the extraction buffer from the NAD+/NADH Assay Kit (Beyotime, China). Supernatants were collected after centrifugation (12,000 × g, 4°C). To measure NADH specifically, an aliquot of the supernatant was heated at 60°C for 30 min to decompose NAD+, while the unheated aliquot represented total NAD+/NADH. The samples were reacted with WST‐8 chromogenic solution according to the manufacturer's instructions. Absorbance was measured at 450 nm using a microplate reader. the NAD+ level was determined by subtracting NADH from the total pool (NAD+ = Total − NADH), and the NAD+/NADH ratio was calculated accordingly. All values were normalized to the protein concentration of the respective samples.

Statistical analysis

Quantitative data are expressed as mean (SD). Differences between two groups were analyzed using the Student's t‐test. Comparisons among multiple groups were performed using one‐way (one variable) or two‐way anova (two variables), followed by Bonferroni post hoc tests. Statistical significance was defined as P < 0.05. All statistical analyses and data visualization were performed using GraphPad Prism 8 (GraphPad Software, San Diego, CA, USA).

RESULTS

MaR1 ameliorates metabolic dysregulation and reverses cardiac remodeling in db/db mice

To evaluate the therapeutic efficacy of MaR1 in the context of chronic metabolic stress, we utilized the db/db murine model of type 2 diabetes. Over the 22‐week observation period, vehicle‐treated db/db mice developed a fulminant metabolic syndrome phenotype, distinguished by significant weight gain and severe glucose intolerance compared with db/m controls. MaR1 administration potently mitigated this systemic dysregulation, resulting in a dose‐dependent reduction in body weight and a marked improvement in glucose disposal efficiency, as quantified by a ~25% reduction in the area under the curve (AUC) during intraperitoneal glucose tolerance testing (IPGTT) (Figure 1a–d). MaR1 did not significantly reduce daily food or caloric intake relative to vehicle‐treated db/db mice (Figure S1). Parallel to this systemic alleviation, MaR1 conferred robust protection against the structural hallmarks of DbCM. Histologic analysis of untreated db/db hearts confirmed maladaptive remodeling, evidenced by pathological cardiomyocyte hypertrophy and diffuse interstitial fibrosis. MaR1 treatment effectively promoted the regression of this pathology, normalizing the heart weight–to–tibia length (HW/TL) ratio and abrogating collagen deposition (Figure 1e–i). These findings indicate that MaR1‐mediated cardiac protection is associated with systemic metabolic improvement and attenuation of myocardial remodeling. Moreover, echocardiography detected distinct diastolic dysfunction in db/m and db/db mice, mainly indicated as decreased E/A and increased E/e' and IVRT. However, MaR1 administration significantly ameliorated diastolic dysfunction of the diabetic heart (Table 2). For systolic function, no remarkable differences were found among the four groups in FS, EF, and cardiac output (CO). Representative images of echocardiography are shown in Figure 1j.

Activation of AMPK signaling by MaR1 restores redox homeostasis and metabolic flexibility

We first characterized the impact of MaR1 on the pathological microenvironment of the diabetic heart. The db/db myocardium exhibited a severe redox imbalance and sterile inflammation, evidenced by the depletion of antioxidant reserves (SOD and CAT), accumulation of oxidative byproducts (MDA, 3‐NT, and 8‐OHdG) (Figure 2a–e), and a surge in pro‐inflammatory cytokines (IL‐1β, IL‐6, and TNF‐α) (Figure 2f–h). MaR1 treatment potently resolved this proteotoxic stress, dose‐dependently restoring the myocardial redox capacity to near‐normal levels and suppressing inflammatory mediators. To delineate the molecular driver of this homeostatic restoration, we interrogated the AMPK pathway, a master regulator of metabolic flexibility. Immunoblotting revealed that the diabetic myocardium exhibited blunted AMPK phosphorylation and the concurrent suppression of rate‐limiting enzymes for both fatty acid oxidation and glycolysis (Figure 2i). MaR1 administration effectively broke this metabolic stalemate. By robustly reactivating AMPK signaling (Figure S2), MaR1 restored the protein abundance of CPT1B and PFK‐1, thereby reinstating metabolic flux. This was corroborated by transcriptional reprogramming showing the upregulation of genes governing fatty acid oxidation (Pgc1α, Pparα) and glucose utilization (Glut4, Hk2) (Figure 2j–m). Collectively, these data suggest that MaR1 alleviates oxidative and inflammatory damage via activation of the AMPK metabolic axis, concomitantly restoring metabolic flexibility.

MaR1 rescues mitochondrial injury and restores PINK1/Parkin‐associated mitophagy markers

Given the pivotal role of mitochondria in cardiac energetics, we assessed mitochondrial quality control mechanisms. Transmission electron microscopy (TEM) revealed that cardiomyocytes from vehicle‐treated db/db mice were burdened with a population of severely compromised mitochondria, exhibiting matrix swelling, cristae lysis, and extensive vacuolization—morphological hallmarks of failed clearance. MaR1 treatment remarkably preserved mitochondrial ultrastructure, maintaining cristae density and preventing organelle swelling (Figure 3a). We hypothesized that this preservation stemmed from the reactivation of mitophagy to purge damaged organelles. Indeed, molecular analysis confirmed a state of ‘mitophagic arrest’ in the diabetic myocardium, characterized by the suppression of the priming kinase PINK1 and the E3 ubiquitin ligase Parkin. This failure in the clearance machinery resulted in a functional blockade of autophagic signaling, evidenced by a depressed LC3‐II/LC3‐I conversion ratio and the pathological accumulation of the autophagy substrate and cargo receptor p62 (Figure 3b–f). MaR1 treatment effectively released this blockade. By restoring PINK1 and Parkin expression, MaR1 reinvigorated mitophagy‐associated markers. These findings indicate that MaR1 attenuates DCM by restoring the mitophagy‐associated machinery to eliminate the toxic pool of dysfunctional mitochondria.

Restoration of global lactylation by MaR1 in diabetic hearts

To dissect the link between metabolic recovery and functional rescue, we investigated the status of LDHA and lysine lactylation (Kla), a post‐translational modification derived from glycolytic lactate. The diabetic myocardium exhibited a marked lactylation deficit, accompanied by reduced LDHA abundance, consistent with suppressed glycolytic flux in the diabetic heart. MaR1 treatment dose‐dependently restored myocardial LDHA staining and global pan‐Kla immunoreactivity, as shown by immunohistochemistry and IOD‐based quantification (Figure 4a–c). We further assessed myocardial protein lactylation by pan‐Kla immunoblotting, which revealed broad pan‐Kla signals across multiple molecular‐weight ranges. These signals were markedly reduced in db/db hearts and restored by MaR1 treatment (Figure 4d,e). These complementary tissue staining and immunoblotting results support the conclusion that MaR1 restores global myocardial protein lactylation.

MaR1 restores autophagic flux and LGR6‐dependent protein lactylation and mitophagy‐associated signaling

To elucidate the mechanism linking metabolism and epigenetics, H9c2 cardiomyocytes were exposed to PA to induce lipotoxicity. PA exposure reproduced defects observed in vivo, characterized by reduced PINK1 and Parkin expression, decreased LC3‐II/LC3‐I ratio, and increased p62 accumulation, indicating disruption of mitophagy‐associated signaling under lipotoxic stress (Figure 5a). BafA1‐dependent LC3‐II accumulation and ΔLC3‐II were reduced by PA and restored by MaR1; MaR1 lowered basal p62 and restored BafA1‐sensitive p62 accumulation (Figure 5b). PA exposure also caused suppression of the cellular lactylation machinery, including the lactyl‐transferases CBP and p300 (Figure 5c,d).

DISCUSSION

This study delineates a novel metabolic‐epigenetic signaling framework through which the SPM MaR1 confers cardioprotection in DbCM. Beyond its canonical role in dampening inflammation, 22 our findings suggest that MaR1 participates in cardiomyocyte metabolic reprogramming and mitochondrial quality control. Mechanistically, our loss‐of‐function experiments indicate that LGR6 is required for MaR1‐induced restoration of glycolytic enzymes, intracellular lactate availability, NAD+/NADH balance, global protein lysine lactylation, and PINK1/Parkin‐associated mitophagy markers in PA‐challenged cardiomyocytes. We therefore interpret the MaR1‐LGR6 pathway as an upstream permissive signal that coordinates metabolic recovery with mitochondrial quality control, rather than as evidence that every downstream component is directly activated by LGR6. In vivo, the protective phenotype is also likely influenced by systemic improvement in glucose tolerance and by the established pro‐resolving actions of MaR1. These mechanisms are biologically connected in DbCM, in which glucolipotoxicity, unresolved inflammation, and mitochondrial injury reinforce one another. 33 Thus, MaR1 may protect diabetic hearts by integrating systemic metabolic improvement, inflammatory resolution, and cardiomyocyte metabolic‐epigenetic regulation.

Current models of diabetic cardiac metabolism posit that elevated fatty acid flux suppresses glucose oxidation through the Randle cycle. 34 , 35 While traditionally regarded as a compensatory adaptation to sustain ATP production, this glycolytic suppression is increasingly recognized as maladaptive, creating a distinct metabolic phenotype characterized by chronic lactylation deficiency. Supporting this, reduced LDHA expression and global lysine lactylation in db/db mice indicate that constitutive glycolytic flux may be required to sustain a basal lactylation tone for homeostatic gene expression and protein function. 36 , 37 It is crucial to note that cardiac lactate signaling exerts context‐dependent effects governed by the magnitude and compartmentalization of metabolic flux: while excessive lactate production in pressure‐overload hypertrophy or acute ischemia may drive maladaptive histone lactylation programs, 38 , 39 physiological lactate availability can support reparative gene expression and cellular recovery. 40 , 41 In the specific context of the diabetic heart, MaR1 ameliorates the foundational metabolic deficit by restoring the expression of key glycolytic enzymes, including PFK‐1 and HK2. However, the present data do not establish whether the protective effect of MaR1‐induced lactylation is mediated predominantly through histone lactylation‐dependent transcriptional regulation or through non‐histone protein lactylation. The recovery of CBP/p300 expression and the upregulation of metabolic genes, including Glut4, Hk2, Pgc1α, and Pparα, are compatible with a transcriptional regulatory component, because p300 has been implicated as a lactylation writer. 42 At the same time, the broad molecular‐weight distribution of pan‐Kla signals and emerging evidence that non‐histone lactylation regulates metabolic enzymes, sarcomeric proteins, and mitochondrial quality control pathways suggest that non‐histone lactylation may also contribute. 24 , 28 , 43 Accordingly, MaR1‐induced lactylation is best regarded as a metabolic‐transcriptional and post‐translational regulatory program that is associated with restoration of mitophagy and cardioprotection, rather than as a purely epigenetic mechanism. In the in vitro lipotoxicity model, sodium lactate supplementation increased CBP and p300 expression, suggesting that lactate may help restore a lactylation‐permissive cellular state. One plausible explanation is that exogenous lactate bypasses PA‐induced glycolytic suppression, replenishes intracellular lactate availability, and improves the metabolic/redox environment required for maintaining CBP/p300 expression. Therefore, we interpret lactate‐induced CBP/p300 upregulation as part of a feed‐forward metabolic‐lactylation program, while the precise mechanism by which lactate regulates CBP/p300 transcription, translation, or protein stability remains to be determined.

This study identifies LGR6 as a necessary transducer of MaR1‐mediated metabolic effects in cardiomyocytes. While the LGR family has traditionally been associated with Wnt signaling and stem cell niches, 21 the role of LGR6 in the myocardium has only recently been elucidated, particularly regarding its ability to attenuate pressure‐overload‐induced cardiac hypertrophy through metabolic reprogramming. 44 These results align with findings by Zhao et al., who characterized LGR6 as a regulator of mitochondrial biogenesis and ferroptosis defense in the diabetic heart. 45 The present findings extend this concept by showing that LGR6 knockdown abolishes the ability of MaR1 to restore glycolytic enzymes, lactate/NAD+ homeostasis, protein lactylation, and mitophagy‐associated markers. Nevertheless, whether AMPK is directly activated downstream of LGR6 or secondarily enhanced by improvement in cellular nutrient and redox status remains unresolved. LGR6 is known to engage Gs‐protein‐coupled cAMP signaling pathways, which may facilitate LKB1‐mediated phosphorylation of AMPK and provide a plausible receptor‐proximal link. 22 However, additional experiments using receptor‐proximal signaling assays, AMPK inhibition, or rescue approaches would be required to establish the exact order of these events. Similarly, the increase in CBP/p300 should be interpreted as part of a restored lactylation‐permissive state rather than as proof that p300 is a direct transcriptional target of LGR6 signaling.

Mitochondrial quality control is essential for maintaining myocardial homeostasis in the heart's high‐energy‐demand environment. 9 In DbCM, however, this protective system becomes impaired, leading to a state of mitophagic arrest driven in part by suppression of the PINK1/Parkin signaling pathway. 46 , 47 This failure results in the accumulation of dysfunctional, ROS‐producing mitochondria, which not only compromise cardiac bioenergetics but also trigger sterile inflammation via cytosolic sensors, such as the NLRP3 inflammasome. 48 Emerging evidence, highlighted by the efficacy of SGLT2 inhibitors, 49 suggests that restoring upstream mitophagic clearance is critical to halting disease progression. Here, we demonstrate that restoration of mitochondrial quality control is closely associated with the cardioprotective effect of MaR1. However, our data do not prove a simple direct receptor‐to‐mitophagy pathway. Instead, the recovery of PINK1/Parkin‐associated markers is likely coupled to MaR1‐LGR6‐dependent improvement in glycolytic flux, lactate availability, NAD+/NADH balance, and lactylation signaling. The observation that sodium lactate partially recapitulates MaR1‐like effects further supports the concept that nutrient and lactate‐derived signaling participate in mitophagy restoration. Together, these findings provide a mechanistic foundation for therapeutic strategies aimed at reinstating glycolytic flux and lactylation‐dependent cellular repair programs to preserve mitochondrial integrity in the diabetic heart.

Certain limitations warrant consideration. First, while we demonstrate the necessity of LGR6 and lactate for the MaR1 response, the precise histone and non‐histone targets of lysine lactylation in diabetic cardiomyocytes remain to be fully mapped. Future lactyl‐proteomic profiling, chromatin‐based assays, and site‐specific validation will be required to determine whether MaR1‐dependent protection is driven primarily by transcriptional regulation, non‐histone protein lactylation, or both. Second, although LGR6 knockdown indicates that LGR6 is required for MaR1‐induced metabolic recovery, the current study does not prove that AMPK activation, p300 upregulation, or PINK1/Parkin‐associated mitophagy are direct downstream events of LGR6 signaling. These processes may also be secondarily regulated by changes in nutrient availability, redox balance, and lactate‐derived signaling. Third, MaR1 was administered systemically and significantly improved glucose tolerance; therefore, reduced systemic metabolic stress may contribute to the observed improvement in cardiac function. In addition, macrophages are a major endogenous source and target of MaR1, and macrophage‐mediated inflammatory resolution may participate in the in vivo protection. Although our PA‐challenged H9c2 cell experiments support a macrophage‐independent cardiomyocyte‐associated MaR1‐LGR6 mechanism, we did not directly assess macrophage infiltration, macrophage polarization, or macrophage‐specific LGR6 signaling.

CONCLUSION

This study defines an LGR6‐dependent signaling framework in which MaR1 acts as an upstream regulator of cardiac metabolic and epigenetic plasticity. Through a program associated with AMPK activation, restored glycolytic flux, lactate‐dependent lactylation, and recovery of PINK1/Parkin‐associated mitophagic machinery, MaR1 links pro‐resolving signaling to improved mitochondrial quality control in DbCM. This mechanism identifies the metabolic‐epigenetic interface as a potential therapeutic target for mitigating mitochondrial dysfunction and structural remodeling in the diabetic heart.
