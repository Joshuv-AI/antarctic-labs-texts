Risk factors for low back pain amongst adults in Nigeria and South Africa: a systematic review
Authors: Matthew Fay, Michelle Black
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
BackgroundThe burden on the individual, society and healthcare providers of low back pain in the western world is well documented. Globalisation and urbanisation, it has been reported, has led to an increase in low back pain in developing countries such as those situated in Sub-Saharan Africa. Low back pain determinants are framed, according to the individual, social and cultural context. The aim of this systematic review was to determine the risk factors of low back pain in Nigeria and South Africa, whose growing and modernising economies operate alongside more traditional labour-intensive practices provide a comparison for modern-day Sub-Saharan Africa.MethodsStudy eligibility included: etiological studies (whose primary objective is to explore/determine risk factors of lower back pain within the population) to include published literature AND non-published manuscripts and ‘grey literature’ (i.e. conference abstracts, thesis and preprints where applicable), studies exploring MSK pain in general if more than 80% of the sample complained of LBP, studies focussing on Nigerian or South African residents, studies including individuals aged 18 years or over and reports printed in the English language. Risk of bias was determined using the AXIS and CASP critical appraisal tools. Owing to study heterogeneity a stratified synthesis was performed to analyse study data.ResultsThirty-nine studies were included. Overall, a total of twenty-two sociodemographic, lifestyle, occupational and psychosocial risk factors were identified. There is good quality evidence of an association between the following risk factors and low back pain: advancing age, female gender, obesity, nature and duration of work, posture, manual handling, perceptions of work, job autonomy and disease conviction.ConclusionThis review suggests common risk factors for low back pain exists in Nigerian and South African populations as they do in other countries.FundingNo funding was received for this systematic review.Trail registrationThe protocol for this review was registered on PROSPERO prior to commencement (protocol registration number: CRD 42023378363).Clinical trial numberNot applicable.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12891-024-08017-5.

Background

Low back pain (LBP), defined in this study as pain that occurs between the lower edge of the ribs and the buttock [1], is a major public health concern. With global prevalence between 30 and 85% [2, 3] it is arguably the most widespread musculoskeletal condition among developed and developing nations [4]. It is estimated that globally 149 million workdays are lost, annually, due to LBP. This results in considerable production loss [2] and can have substantial impact on the overall and financial well-being of an individual and society [3]. A recent review of LBP found lifetime, annual and point prevalence among African populations to be higher than estimates for global prevalence [3]. Coupled with the knowledge that higher prevalence of LBP has been associated with lower socioeconomic status and education levels, has led to suggestions that the burden of LBP would be greater in lower and middle income countries (LMICs) like those situated in Africa [4].

Despite growing concern, LBP and other musculoskeletal conditions, remain less prioritised in LMICs due to more pressing issues, such as HIV/AIDS [3]. In addition, epidemiologic challenges means there is a lack of accurate data, suggesting the true burden of LBP in African countries is not fully understood [3]. Given that known knowledge suggests the majority of LBP presentations are non-specific [2] a study into the risk factors of LBP in Sub-Saharan Africa is warranted so to offer insight into this overlooked phenomenon. Emphasis was placed on Nigeria and South Africa from which the majority of studies have been reported [3]. Factors, such as their relative strong economies, urbanisation and affluence, suggest patterns noted in the developed world could have an effect on LBP occurrence [3]. However, it was hypothesised that both would provide a comparison of work-practices and lifestyles of traditional manual labour intensive practices and modern non-traditional 'western' practices. In short, they provide a setting from which a wide spectrum of societal and cultural risk factors can be determined. Accordingly, the objective of this systematic review was to determine risk factors associated with low back pain amongst adults, defined as individuals aged 18 and over, in Nigeria and South Africa.

Methods

Search strategy and selection criteria

A search was conducted of the following electronic databases, Embase (1974 to June 2024), Medline (1946 to June 2024), PubMed (up to June 2024), CINAHL (up to June 2024) and Global Index Medicus (up to May 2024). Titles and abstracts were exported into RefWorks for the removal of duplicates. Subsequently, titles and abstracts were exported to Covidence and evaluated against predefined inclusion criteria. MF and MB conducted both initial screening of titles then further evaluation against inclusion criteria. Disagreements were resolved through discussion.

The search strategy was run through several databases (see Appendix 1 – 5 for the search strategies used). A combination of free text keywords and phrases and MESH terms were utilised in the PubMed and Medline searches. To limit the search to the anatomical area of the lower back, the MESH term “low back pain” was employed, to help increase terminology precision. However, due to the findings of several preliminary scoping searches it became clear that a number of potentially relevant studies retrieved would focus not just on pain in the lower back but would combine consideration of it with that of other spinal areas. Therefore, the approach taken with the free-text keywords was broader with terms around “spine pain”, “spinal pain” and “back pain” included, besides terms around “cervical pain” and “neck pain”. As regards geographical limits for the search, free-text keywords for “sub-Saharan Africa” were used in the first instance, and combined with suitable MESH terms for “Africa south of the Sahara”. Here, the paucity of studies covering low back pain in the sub-Saharan African region should be noted, which meant that the inclusion of studies that looked at back pain more generally in this geographical area of the African continent became necessary. Due to the believed social and cultural dynamics of both Nigeria and South Africa a decision was made to limit the review to these two countries with the hypothesis being that results could be applicable to other sub-Saharan countries (see Supplementary File 1 for further explanation). Boolean logic involving the use of the AND and OR operators was also applied to combine the different elements within the search strategy. Individual search terms were input and then combined using the OR search operator to give the search strategy visual clarity on the search terms used. Phrase searching using quotation marks “” and truncation/wildcard searching with the * symbol were also included in the search strategy with the aim of improving relevance within the search and suppressing recall of items not pertaining to the central topics of the search. The usage of the words risk* and/or determinant* was also made in the strategy with similar intentions.

The inclusion criteria that articles were in English and be limited to studies on humans were applied limitations on PubMed, along with criteria to exclude research studies around paediatrics. This meant that filters related to different age groups of adults had to be added in over single lines of search. Due to concerns over coverage of material in core databases such as Medline and Embase, other sources were interrogated: in particular Global Index Medicus (GIM).

Data collection and analysis

Measures of effect and statistics reported in the studies varied and findings are presented as reported in each study. Where studies reported outcomes and/or risk factors other than, and contributing to, LBP only those risk factors which could be reasonably attributed to LBP were extracted. Data was extracted and methodological quality was appraised. Both activities in their entirety by MF then a sample of extracted data and appraised studies were verified by MB. This added a degree of rigour to the process. In both instances, disagreements were resolved through discussion. A bespoke data extraction table (available upon request) was designed to capture relevant data: design, case definition, participant detail, measurement tool used for the outcome of LBP and the study’s main findings/conclusions. The Appraisal tool for [observational] Cross-Sectional Studies (AXIS) [5] was used for 32 studies (see Table 1) and the Critical Appraisal Skills Programme (CASP) tool for Case Control Studies [6] was used for one study (see Table 2). CASP critical appraisal results

Due to study heterogeneity, in respect to population, context, and definition of LBP, a stratified synthesis, in accordance with Synthesis without meta-analysis (SWIM) guidelines [37], was performed. In order to grade the strength of evidence a bespoke classification system was developed. It was determined that key criteria (i.e. those deemed particularly necessary to answer the research question) were evidence of sound population sampling and selection, appropriate and validated outcome measures, clear indication of how associations were to be measured and given significance and reporting of any confounding factors in the analysis.

AXIS

Eight of the 20 criteria were deemed key. Studies were rated as ‘good’ if they scored a ‘yes’ response in six or more of the assessed key criteria (see Table 1), ‘moderate’ if four or five had yes responses and ‘poor’ if three or less had yes responses. A further rating based on the entire critical appraisal tool was conducted. Studies were rated as ‘good’ if they scored a ‘yes’ response in fourteen or more of the total critical appraisal criteria (see Table 1), ‘moderate’ if seven to thirteen had yes responses and ‘poor’ if six or less had yes responses.

CASP

Six of the 12 criteria were deemed key. Studies were rated as ‘good’ if they scored a ‘yes’ response in five or more of the assessed key criteria (see Table 2), ‘moderate’ if three or four had yes responses and ‘poor’ if two or less had yes responses. A further rating based on the entire critical appraisal tool was conducted. Studies were rated as ‘good’ if they scored a ‘yes’ response in nine or more of the total critical appraisal criteria (see Table 2), ‘moderate’ if five to eight had yes responses and ‘poor’ if four or less had yes responses. To give a combined rating the lower of the two ratings held majority weight (i.e. a study rated ‘good’ against key criteria but ‘moderate’ against the entire criteria would be rated as ‘moderate’ overall).

Results

Prisma Flow Chart

In total, 342 titles were retrieved through electronic database searches (Fig. 1). Once duplicates were removed, 193 articles remained for further screening of abstracts and titles against the inclusion and exclusion criteria. The full text of 76 articles was obtained for review against the inclusion and exclusion criteria. In total, 39 articles were included in the review, which reported the risk factors for low back pain (LBP) in adults in Nigeria and South Africa.

Design

Of the included studies, 34 [2, 7, 8, 11–13, 16–35, 38–47] were cross-sectional in design, one [15] was presumed to be cross-sectional based on the content of the text though was not clearly stipulated by the author(s). Three studies were retrospective [9, 10, 14] in design and one [36] a prospective case – control study design.

Case definition

Anatomic Area

Thirty-one studies did not define or did not give a clear anatomical area for LBP [2, 7, 9, 10, 12–16, 18–23, 25, 27–36, 40–44]. Two studies delineated between either lower, mid or upper back [8] or separated the lower back from the upper back and neck [45]. Three studies specified the region between T12 and the gluteal folds [17, 26, 36] One study showed participants an area on a picture, but did not specify the area [24], and one study specified any region of the back as constituting back pain [38]. One study delineated between low back, neck, shoulder and hand/wrist pain but gave no clear anatomical area [39].

Cases classified as being activity limiting

Twenty studies gave no clear indication as to whether LBP was classified as activity limiting [7, 9–13, 17–20, 22–26, 28, 41, 42, 44, 45]. Ten studies assessed LBP as either activity limiting [8, 29, 32, 38, 39, 43] or determined activity limitations through outcome measures [2, 14, 16, 21]. Two studies [15, 27] sought the effects of LBP on the participant’s ability to either farm [15], or carry out their work based ‘duty’ [27] and one [36] determined if the pain was strong enough to make the worker absent from work. Six studies [30–35] utilised the Functional Rating Index (FRI) in order to determine a more stringent definition of LBP based on disability.

Critical appraisal

Based on the a priori appraisal criteria, the overall methodological quality of the studies in terms of their capacity to demonstrate an association between risk factors and low back pain (LBP) amongst adults in Nigeria and South Africa was rated as poor (6 studies [15, 22, 24, 36, 41, 42]), moderate (17 studies [7–10, 13, 14, 16, 19, 20, 23, 25, 27–29, 32, 40, 44] and good (15 studies [2, 11, 12, 17, 18, 21, 26, 30, 31, 33–35, 38, 39, 43, 45]. Of the previously identified key criteria those most poorly reported on were the justification of sample size (n = 28), evidence to support the reliability and validity of exposure or outcome measures (n = 19), internal consistency of results (n = 12), indication that the selection process was likely to select a representative population (n = 13) and clear indication of the statistical analysis/significance used (n = 9).

Sociodemographic

In total six sociodemographic related risk factors were identified: age, gender, a past medical history of LBP, education, marital status and place of residence. Two studies [26, 39] (n = 442) reported good quality evidence of an association (OR Range 0.09 to 0.18 and p = 0.0001) between advancing age and low back pain. Two studies [2, 18] (n = 676) reported good quality evidence of an association between middle age and low back pain (p = 0.000 and an OR 23.4). Two studies [2, 21] (n = 654) reported good quality evidence of an association between female gender and low back pain (OR Range: 10.26 to 12.4). One study [11] (n = 362) reported good quality evidence of an association between a history of lower back pain and/or trauma and current low back pain (OR 2.96). Two studies [18, 43] reported an association between education and low back pain. One study [18] (n = 122) reported good quality evidence of an association between secondary school education (p = 0.000) and one study [43] (n = 274) reported good quality evidence of a significant association between educational status/level and low back pain (p < 0.05). One study [18] (n = 122) reported good quality evidence of an association between being married and low back pain (p = 0.000).

Lifestyle

In total six lifestyle, related risk factors were identified across the studies: smoking, pregnancy, alcohol, weight, physical activity and domestic activities. One study [45] (n = 227) of good quality evidence indicated utilisation of the rear Infant Carrying Method (the carrying of a child on the back) as being associated with low back pain (participant opinion). One study [2] (n = 554) reported good quality evidence of an association between obesity and low back pain (OR 8.7).

Occupational

In total eight occupational activity related risk factors were identified across the studies: occupational activity, posture, heavy lifting, occupational psychosocial, job duration/hours worked, occupational stress, fatigue/tiredness and reduced knowledge of back care hygiene/ergonomics. Four studies [18, 26, 38, 43] (n = 838) reported good quality evidence of an association between petrol tanker driving (p ≤ 0.05), car driving (OR 5.52), driving motorcycles for 1 to 5 years (p = 0.000) and mechanics (OR 6.3) and low back pain. One study [12] (n = 97) reported good quality evidence of an association between teaching (including carrying/lifting equipment/teaching resources and computer use) (participant opinion) and low back pain. Five studies2, 6, 10, 33 and 57 (n = 1,759) (reported in an occupational environment) reported good quality evidence of an association between posture and low back pain (OR Range 2.09 to 6.9, p = 0.016 and participant opinion). Three studies2, 33 and 57 (n = 2150) reported good quality evidence of an association between heavy lifting/manual handling/handling large and bulky objects at arm’s length and low back pain (OR Range 3.8 to 7.2) and one study [38] (n = 684) reported good quality evidence of an association between vibration and low back pain (OR 5.4). Four studies [18, 26, 39, 43] (n = 838) reported good quality evidence of an association between hours worked and low back pain (OR 0.04, p = 0.012, p < 0.05 and p = 0.000). Two studies [18, 21] (n = 222) reported good quality evidence of an association between fatigue/tiredness and low back pain (OR 0.56 and p = 0.000).

Occupational psychosocial and stress

One study [33] (n = 109) reported good quality evidence of an association between negative perceptions of support at the workplace, or of the family support system, and low back pain (OR 3.29 and OR2.56 respectively). One study [38] (n = 684) reported good quality evidence of an association between inadequate task clarity or auxiliary support and low back pain (OR 3.57 and OR 1.8 respectively). Three studies16, 18 and 57 (n = 1463) reported good quality evidence of an association between work related fear avoidance beliefs and the development of low back pain (RR 6.07 and OR Range 1.1 to 1.8 respectively) and one study 57 (n = 700) reported good quality evidence of an association between physical activity related fear avoidance beliefs and the development of low back pain (OR 1.3). One study [38] (n = 684) reported good quality evidence of an association between a lack of job autonomy and low back pain (OR 3.9). One study [35] (n = 366) reported good quality evidence of an association between working under time pressures and deadlines and low back pain (OR 2.83). One study [38] (n = 684) reported good quality evidence of an association between noise in the environment and low back pain (OR 2.7).

Psychosocial

In total six psychosocial risk factors were identified across the studies: disease conviction, affective disturbance, denial, catastrophizing and depression/anxiety. One study [19] (n = 678) reported good quality evidence of an association between disease conviction, affective disturbance and denial and low back pain (OR 19.1, OR 3.3 and OR 4.2 respectively) and one study [17] (n = 700) reported good quality evidence of an association between anxiety and low back pain (OR 1.1).

Discussion

Outcomes in our study revealed an association between advancing age/old age and low back pain. This correlates with Williams et al., who determined a higher prevalence associated with older age [4] and Docking et al., who reported an increase in the prevalence of disabling back pain with age, amongst urban dwelling UK citizens of 75 years or over [48]. Though tentative, it suggests that advancing/old age may be a universal risk factor irrespective of socio-cultural context. Outcomes of our study also revealed an association between female gender and low back pain. Both Maniam et al., and Hurwitz et al., report higher prevalence amongst females, the former in Asia [49], whilst the latter reports good quality evidence of a higher prevalence among rural Chinese and Thai villagers, though no significant gender difference was found amongst Brazilian villagers [50]. In contrast, a greater association with low back pain amongst males than in females is reported in three of six studies of North American and Western European populations [51]. This trend is also reported within a review of African populations [3]. However, in the latter at least half of the studies, which were of industrial populations, included mostly or entirely males [3]. Evidence of this phenomenon was identified within this review in which the majority of studies, set in either light or heavy industry, comprised of mostly or entirely male populations. Of the five studies reporting female gender as a risk factor only two [2, 40] are of ‘general’ populations, suggesting that more evidence from community settings may be necessary before determining if either sex are at greater risk. Smoking, weight, alcohol and physical activity were associated with low back pain in our study. This finding is supported by commonly cited evidence [52–54] and suggests similar lifestyle risk factors are prevalent within the study population as they are in ‘western’ societies.

Our study’s outcomes revealed occupational activity, particularly driving and farming, to be associated with low back pain. Comparing this with contemporary literature a causal relationship between driving duration and work-related musculoskeletal disorders (WRMSD’s) was found to be inconclusive [55], suggesting further enquiry is necessary. Risk factors pertaining to farming are reported as slouched sitting, forward bending and lifting [50] and Williams et al., reports those in the poorer socioeconomic quintile and rural dwellers as having greater odds and higher intensity of back pain [4]. It suggests the action and sociodemographic background of farming may be risk factors for low back pain. This assumption is speculative; therefore, it is worth referring to more conclusive evidence suggesting within occupations there appears to be common risk factors. This is exemplified by strong evidence [55] of a weak relationship between the postural and lifting demands and a moderate relationship between the manual handling demands of driving on work related musculoskeletal disorders. Despite not specifically reporting on low back pain it is consistent with the current evidence that high spinal load, lifting, bending, and twisting are risk factors for low back pain [51, 54] and suggests transferability of evidence is reasonable. Indeed, of the risk factors identified from this review the majority pertain to posture, lifting, manual handling and or movement related risk factors, from a broad range of occupational settings. A balanced response would be to suggest that the findings of this review are inconclusive in identifying specific occupations as being a risk factor for low back pain. However, there is greater certainty that manual handling, heavy lifting, twisting and bending, actions common to many occupations, are risk factors for low back pain within the study population.

Our study outcomes’ also revealed an association between occupational psychosocial risk factors and low back pain specifically affective disturbance, pain coping statements and catastrophizing. Contemporary evidence supports these findings with depression and pain coping, characterised by catastrophizing, being commonly reported risk factors for non-specific and chronic low back pain [54]. Within the occupational setting, it was found support from within the workplace or family, lacking or negatively perceived, was a risk for low back pain and is supported by Hoogendoorn et al. [56], who report strong evidence for low social support in the workplace as a risk factor. However, the role of family support was not analysed. Control, a lack of autonomy and working under pressure and deadlines were risk factors this review assessed to be supported by good quality evidence, exemplified with the finding of a protective element of the ability to control the pace of work on low back pain [33]. It correlates with Buruck et al., who reported a reduction in the risk of developing chronic low back pain was associated with greater job control and decision authority. (The degree of overlap between workplace factors associated with both acute and chronic low back pain is reported in current literature [57]). However, Hoogendoorn et al., deemed insufficient evidence existed of an effect of low job control, except in blue-collar women for which there was a positive effect on low back symptoms. In summary, the evidence supporting job control as a risk factor for low back pain appears inconclusive. It is worth noting that comparison has been made between studies49, 50 in which there is heterogeneity between the geographical location, gender and occupation. Yet common occupational psychosocial risk factors albeit with differing levels of evidence and certainty has been demonstrated. It suggests a proportionate level of burden could exist within Nigerian and South African workplaces as they do in countries considered more affluent and more widely reported on.

Psychosocial factors may have a greater influence on the persistence and impact of acute and chronic low back pain, both of which generate significant costs for society [58]. Evidence from this review suggests psychosocial risk factors are applicable to the reviews’ population and may be applicable to populations throughout Sub-Saharan Africa. This raises concern, as the potential exists for low back pain to become an immense burden for low and middle-income countries in Sub-Saharan Africa; particularly in instances where health budgets are already restricted and channelled to other higher priority conditions [3]. This eventuality is more feasible due to the growth in prevalence of lifestyle determinants of musculoskeletal health, of which back pain is a major problem, such as, obesity, lack of physical activity, smoking, alcohol and work-related activity in developing countries. It is driven by rapid urbanisation, an associated decrease in physical activity, increasingly sedentary nature of occupations and modes of transport, increased intake of energy dense foods and smoking. In short, it is due to a shift in what constitutes a normal lifestyle [53].

In conclusion, this review suggests common risk factors for low back pain exist between Nigeria and South Africa and more economically developed nations. Rapid urbanisation in developing countries [53] may have already led to a greater prevalence in urban areas, calling for further research to determine if a difference between rural and urban areas exist [52]. It may highlight that in developing countries where urbanisation is most pronounced and uncontrolled, and rural prevalence currently underreported, a greater burden of lower back pain exists.

Clinical implications

This study has revealed good quality evidence for specific sociodemographic, lifestyle, occupational and psychosocial risk factors (see Table 4 for details). This may be of benefit for health personnel in identifying, and/or in designing interventions, targeted at populations who are at greater risk of lower back pain.

Limitations

Although a strength of this study was the number of factors identified as risks the heterogeneity of studies, and in particular estimates of effect, meant the ability to demonstrate certainty of a risk factor, beyond a single study, as contributing to low back pain is limited. Furthermore, the reliance on cross sectional studies limits the ability to demonstrate a cause-and-effect relationship between risk factors and low back pain; as epidemiological studies, generally conducted in a natural environment among free-living populations, means there are several other independent, confounding, factors that could influence the exposure mechanism and disease outcome [55].

Future research recommendations

Due to the high level of occupational studies included in this review more assessment of rural and urban communities are required to give greater clarity on the prevalence and determinants of low back pain at a community and household level. Emphasis should be placed on study design(s), which can improve certainty in the risk factor outcome relationship and on identifying, and limiting, the influence of confounding factors.
