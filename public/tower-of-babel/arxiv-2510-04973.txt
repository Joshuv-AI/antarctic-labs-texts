Quantum walks through generalized graph composition
Arjan Cornelissen

Abstract
In this work, we generalize the recently-introduced graph composition framework to the non-boolean setting. A quantum algorithm in this framework is represented by a hypergraph, where each hyperedge is adjacent to multiple vertices. The input and output to the quantum algorithm is represented by a set of boundary vertices, and the hyperedges act like switches, connecting the input vertex to the output that the algorithm computes. Apart from generalizing the graph composition framework, our new proposed framework unifies the quantum divide and conquer framework, the decision-tree framework, and the unified quantum walk search framework. For the decision trees, we additionally construct a quantum algorithm from an improved weighting scheme in the non-boolean case. For quantum walk search, we show how our techniques naturally allow for amortization of the subroutines’ costs. Previous work showed how one can speed up “detection” of marked vertices by amortizing the costs of the quantum walk. In this work, we extend these results to the setting of “finding” such marked vertices, albeit in some restricted settings. Along the way, we provide a novel analysis of irreducible, reversible Markov processes, by linear-algebraically connecting its effective resistance to the random walk operator. This significantly simplifies the algorithmic implementation of the quantum walk search algorithm, achieves an amortization speed-up for quantum walks over Johnson graphs, avoids the need for quantum fast-forwarding, and removes the log-factors from the query complexity statements.

Introduction

Over the last three decades, the search for efficient quantum algorithms for solving computational problems has led to a wide variety of algorithmic techniques. Notable examples include quantum Fourier sampling
[
Sho99
,
BV97
,
Sim97
]
, amplitude amplification and estimation
[

,
BHMT02
]

,

]
, quantum walks
[

,

,

,

,
AGJ21
]
, etc.

In this search, the number of queries to the computational problem’s input has often been used as a measure of efficiency for the designed algorithm. The
query complexity
of a computational problem is the minimum number of times we need to access that problem’s input to solve it. This number depends on the computational model, e.g., if we are allowed to use randomness to decide which part of the input to access, we refer to this measure as the
randomized query complexity
. Similarly, if we are allowed to query our input coherently in superposition, then we refer to this quantity as the
quantum query complexity
.

In a landmark result, Reichardt showed that the quantum query complexity is captured by a semi-definite program, called the
quantum adversary bound

[
Rei09
,
Rei11
]
. This sets quantum query complexity apart from its randomized counterpart, where no such characterization is known. Moreover, there is a constructive way to turn any feasible solution of the minimization version of the adversary bound into a quantum query algorithm, which was recently simplified considerably using transducers
[
BY23
,
BJY24
]
. Many algorithmic techniques that followed essentially boil down to constructing feasible solutions to the adversary bound in a smart way. Examples include the learning graph framework
[
Bel12a
]
, the electric network framework
[
Bel13
]
, the
st
-connectivity framework
[
BR12
,
JK17
,
JJKP18
]
, and the quantum divide-and-conquer framework
[
CKK+25
]
.

Recently, the graph composition framework was introduced, which unifies all of these
[
Cor25
]
. An instance of this framework is an undirected graph
G=(V,E)
, with source and sink nodes
s,t\in V
. For any input
x\in\mathcal{D}
in some finite domain
\mathcal{D}
, we define a subgraph
G(x)=(V,E_{x})
, where the presence of the edge
e\in E_{x}
can be determined by a subroutine
\mathcal{P}_{e}
, encoded (without loss of generality) as a span program on
\mathcal{D}
. The framework then constructs a quantum query algorithm that computes whether
s
and
t
are connected in the resulting subgraph
G(x)
.

An inherent limitation of the graph composition framework is that it only naturally uses decision problems, i.e., it can only access subroutines that solve decision problems, and similarly it can merely produce quantum algorithms that solve decision problems. It is worth noting that other quantum algorithmic frameworks work well with non-boolean inputs and outputs too, like the unified quantum walk framework
[
AGJ21
]
, or the decision-tree framework
[
BT20
,
CMP25
]
. This begs the question whether the graph composition framework can be generalized to the non-boolean setting, which we answer affirmatively in this work.

1.1
The generalized graph composition framework

In this work, we introduce the

State-conversion.

The state-conversion problem is the problem of converting a state

\displaystyle\widetilde{O}\left(\mathsf{S}+\sqrt{R_{\mathrm{eff}}(P^{t};\sigma\leftrightarrow M_{x})}(\sqrt{t}\mathsf{U}+\mathsf{C})+\mathsf{C}\right),

\displaystyle\text{(finding)}.

Here,
t\in\mathbb{N}
is an integer parameter in the construction of the algorithm, and
R_{\mathrm{eff}}(P^{t};\sigma\leftrightarrow M_{x})
represents the minimum effective resistance in the graph that is walked on if we take
t
steps of the random walk
P
, between the initial distribution
\sigma
and any distribution that has support in
M_{x}
. This result unifies four previous approaches,
[
Sze04
,
MNRS11
,
Bel13
,
DH17
]
.

Amortization by
[
Jef22
]
.

A property of random walks in the classical setting, is that they are amenable to amortization. That is, if figuring out which edge to traverse is much more costly for some vertices
v\in V
in the graph, but these vertices are not likely to be visited often, then we can take the “expected cost per step” in the algorithm’s analysis. This is crucial for, for instance, the best-known algorithm to compute the volume of a convex body
[
CV18
]
. In the quantum setting, on the other hand, we don’t know in which vertex we are in any of the intermediate steps, and hence we cannot easily access such amortization techniques.

Towards achieving such an amortization result, Jeffery developed a variable-time quantum walk search algorithm
[
Jef22
]
. They assume that it is free to select the edge that is to be traversed, but the edge traversal itself is costly. Their analysis also works with respect to time complexity, but here we merely state the query complexity results:

1.

A
setup routine
making
\mathsf{S}
queries that computes a superposition over a distribution
\sigma
as before.

2.

An
update routine
that for any
v\in V
and index
j\in[\deg(v)]
computes the
j
th neighbor of
v
, say
w
, and the index of
v
in the list of neighbors of
w
. The number of queries this routine makes is described by the random variable
\mathsf{T}_{vw,x}
.

3.

A
checking routine
that for any
v\in V
checks whether
v\in M_{x}
with a number of queries described by the random variable
\mathsf{C}_{v,x}
.

Now, Jeffery showed
[
Jef22
, Corollary 4.2]
that we can solve the detection version of quantum walk search in a cost that satisfies

\displaystyle\widetilde{O}\left(\mathsf{S}+\sqrt{\max_{x\in\mathcal{D}_{+}}R_{\mathrm{eff}}(P;\sigma-\nu_{x})\cdot\max_{x\in\mathcal{D}_{-}}\underset{\begin{subarray}{c}v\sim\pi\\
w\sim P_{v,\cdot}\end{subarray}}{\mathbb{E}}\left[\mathsf{T}_{vw,x}^{2}\right]}+\sqrt{\max_{x\in\mathcal{D}_{+}}\underset{v\sim\tau}{\mathbb{E}}\left[\left(\frac{(\nu_{x})_{v}}{\tau_{v}}\right)^{2}\right]\cdot\max_{x\in\mathcal{D}_{-}}\underset{v\sim\tau}{\mathbb{E}}\left[\mathsf{C}^{2}_{v,x}\right]}\right),

where
\mathcal{D}_{+}=\{x\in\mathcal{D}:M_{x}\neq\varnothing\}
,
\mathcal{D}_{-}=\mathcal{D}\setminus\mathcal{D}_{+}
,
\tau
is a distribution on
V
and
\nu_{x}
a distribution on
M_{x}\cap\supp(\tau)
. Moreover, the above complexities can be slightly improved if we know the expected query complexities beforehand.

Jeffery also specializes these bounds to the specific setting of detection in the MNRS-framework
[
Jef22
, Corollary 4.3]
, with the resulting query complexity being

\widetilde{O}\left(\mathsf{S}+\sqrt{\max_{x\in\mathcal{D}_{+}}\frac{1}{\underset{v\sim\tau}{\mathbb{P}}[v\in M_{x}]}}\left(\sqrt{\frac{1}{\delta}\max_{x\in\mathcal{D}_{-}}\underset{\begin{subarray}{c}v\sim\pi\\
w\sim P_{v,\cdot}\end{subarray}}{\mathbb{E}}\left[\mathsf{T}_{vw,x}^{2}\right]}+\sqrt{\max_{x\in\mathcal{D}_{-}}\underset{v\sim\tau}{\mathbb{E}}\left[\mathsf{C}^{2}_{v,x}\right]}\right)\sqrt{\log\frac{1}{\pi_{\min}}}\right),

where
\delta
is the spectral gap of the graph
G
, and where, again the squares can be moved outside the expectation if the expected query complexity is known beforehand.
4
4

4

We are sweeping some details under the rug here – Jeffery shows that one can even move the squares outside the expectation if the costs can be computed on the fly, or with a single query. We refer to their paper for the precise statements.

Our improvement.

In this work, we consider again a slightly different setting, where we have a database with possible states
\mathcal{S}
, such that for every vertex
v\in V
and input
x\in\mathcal{D}
, there exists a unique entry in the database
D_{v,x}\in\mathcal{S}
. The task, now, is to keep track of the database entry during the walk, such that we can use it in the checking routine. This mimics the approach taken in the quantum walk algorithm for the element-distinctness problem
[
Amb07
]
.

We assume that we are given access to the following subroutines, as feasible solutions to their corresponding state-reflection problems:

1.

For all
v\in V
, a setup routine
S_{v}
with witness sizes
(\mathsf{S}_{v})_{x}^{\pm}
, that computes
D_{v,x}
.

2.

For all
vw=e\in E
, an update routine
U_{e}
with witness sizes
(\mathsf{U}_{e})_{x}^{\pm}
, that updates
D_{v,x}
to
D_{w,x}
.

3.

For all
v\in V
, and
D\in\mathcal{S}
, a checking routine
C_{v,D}
, such that for all
x\in\mathcal{D}
, if
D=D_{v,x}
,
C_{v,D}
computes whether
v\in M_{x}
, with witness sizes
(\mathsf{C}_{v})_{x}^{\pm}
.

We now employ the generalized graph composition construction to obtain the following quantum algorithm that solves the detection version of the quantum walk search problem.

Theorem 1.8

(Simplified version of
theorem

8.2
)
.

Let
\mathcal{D}_{+}=\{x\in\mathcal{D}:M_{x}\neq\varnothing\}
and
\mathcal{D}_{-}=\mathcal{D}\setminus\mathcal{D}_{+}
. For all
x\in\mathcal{D}^{+}
, let
\mu_{x}
be a probability distribution on
\supp(\sigma)
, and
\nu_{x}
be a probability distribution on
M_{x}\cap\supp(\tau)
. Then, we can solve the detection version of the quantum walk search problem with high probability with a number of queries that satisfies
O(\sqrt{\mathsf{S}^{+}\mathsf{S}^{-}}+\sqrt{\mathsf{U}^{+}\mathsf{U}^{-}}+\sqrt{\mathsf{C}^{+}\mathsf{C}^{-}})
, where

\displaystyle\mathsf{S}^{+}

\displaystyle=\max_{x\in\mathcal{D}_{+}}\underset{v\sim\sigma}{\mathbb{E}}\left[\left(\frac{(\mu_{x})_{v}}{\sigma_{v}}\right)^{2}(\mathsf{S}_{v})_{x}^{+}\right],

\displaystyle\mathsf{S}^{-}

\displaystyle=\max_{x\in\mathcal{D}_{-}}\underset{v\sim\sigma}{\mathbb{E}}\left[(\mathsf{S}_{v})_{x}^{-}\right],

\displaystyle\mathsf{U}^{+}

\displaystyle=\max_{x\in\mathcal{D}_{+}}R_{\mathrm{eff}}(P;\mu_{x}-\nu_{x})\cdot\max_{e\in E}(\mathsf{U}_{e})_{x}^{+},

\displaystyle\mathsf{U}^{-}

\displaystyle=\max_{x\in\mathcal{D}_{-}}\underset{\begin{subarray}{c}v\sim\pi\\
w\sim P_{v,\cdot}\end{subarray}}{\mathbb{E}}\left[(\mathsf{U}_{vw})_{x}^{-}\right],

\displaystyle\mathsf{C}^{+}

\displaystyle=\max_{x\in\mathcal{D}_{+}}\underset{v\sim\tau}{\mathbb{E}}\left[\left(\frac{(\nu_{x})_{v}}{\tau_{v}}\right)^{2}(\mathsf{C}_{v,D_{v,x}})_{x}^{+}\right],

\displaystyle\mathsf{C}^{-}

\displaystyle=\max_{x\in\mathcal{D}_{-}}\underset{v\sim\tau}{\mathbb{E}}\left[(\mathsf{C}_{v,D_{v,x}})_{x}^{-}\right].

We generalize this result to the finding version in two cases, with very similar complexities. First, in the case where we are promised that the marked vertex is unique (
theorem

8.3
), and second, in the case where we know that the fraction of marked vertices is
\varepsilon
relative to some fixed probability distribution
\tau\in\Delta_{V}
, that is,
\mathbb{P}_{v\sim\tau}[v\in M_{x}]=\varepsilon
(
theorem

8.4
).

Analysis of our improvement.

The positive and negative witness complexities from the above theorem statement look quite unwieldy, and it is consequently not easy to see how they relate to the aforementioned query complexities that were obtained by
[
AGJ21
]
and
[
Jef22
]
. Indeed, in showing that we can recover their results from our complexities, we need to prove some new relations between the effective resistance in a graph
G
with resistances
r
, and the probability transition matrix
P
of a random walk on it. We prove that

Lemma 1.9

(Informal version of
lemma

3.2
)
.

Let
M=(V,P)
be an irreducible, reversible Markov process, with stationary distribution
\pi
. Let
\xi\in\mathbb{R}_{V}
such that
\sum_{v\in V}\xi_{v}=0
. We write
D=\diag(\pi)
, and
\widetilde{\xi}=D^{-1/2}\xi
. Then, we have the following two consequences:

1.

For integer
t\geq 1
,
R_{\mathrm{eff}}(P;\xi)\leq t\cdot R_{\mathrm{eff}}(P^{t};\xi)
.

2.

If the spectral gap of
P
is
\delta
, we have
R_{\mathrm{eff}}(P;\xi)\leq\|\widetilde{\xi}\|_{2}^{2}/\delta
.

Both results were already known through combinatorial arguments in the case where
\xi
is the difference of two standard basis vectors, i.e.,
\mathbbm{1}_{s}-\mathbbm{1}_{t}
, see e.g.,
[
vM23
]
. However, for our purposes we need the more general case where
\xi
is the difference between two probability distributions, and so we give a direct linear algebraic proof here. We deem this to be an independently interesting result in its own right.

Contributions.

Using the aforementioned analysis of the effective resistance, we are now able to recover the complexities from
[
AGJ21
]
and
[
Jef22
]
. We compare our results to theirs.

1.

Encoding the subroutines as feasible solutions to the adversary bound for state-conversion is beneficial over direct algorithmic access models, as used by
[
AGJ21
]
and
[
Jef22
]
. With algorithmic input models, namely, one can on the one hand consider
zero-error
quantum algorithms, which is inherently limited since some problems can be much harder to solve with zero-error than with bounded error (e.g., Grover search). On the other hand, one can consider
bounded-error
quantum algorithms, but then one has to deal with the build-up of errors throughout the procedure, complicating the construction and likely leading to polylogarithmic overhead of error reduction along the way.

Feasible solutions to the adversary bound possess both the desirable properties: they are exact in the sense that they solve the adversary bound exactly, and they are as powerful as bounded-error quantum query algorithms, due to the characterization of bounded-error quantum query complexity by the adversary bound
[
LMR+11
]
. Developments after
[
Jef22
]
suggest how to use Jeffery’s techniques without build-up of errors using Las-Vegas algorithms
[
BY23
]
, which we recover with our approach, see
theorem

4.5
.

2.

We recover the same query complexity as in
[
AGJ21
]
for the detection version of quantum walk search, but without any log-factors. See
theorem

8.5
. We obtain the same improved result for the finding case, if we are promised that there is only one marked vertex. We conjecture this result can also be obtained in the general finding case, but we leave that to future research.

3.

An interesting consequence of our improved analysis is that we recover the query bounds from
[
AGJ21
]
, for all values of the parameter
t
. However, the construction of the quantum algorithm we propose does not depend on
t
, i.e., it is merely an artifact of the analysis. Since the parameter
t
is used in the quantum fast-forwarding step of
[
AGJ21
]
, we remove the necessity of doing quantum fast-forwarding for quantum walk search, conceptually simplifying its implementation.

4.

We recover all the complexities from
[
Jef22
]
. With our improved analysis of the effective resistance, we can additionally improve the query complexity of the variable-time MNRS framework, by removing the additional factor
\sqrt{\log(1/\pi_{\min})}
. This was already conjectured to be possible by Jeffery, so we confirm their conjecture.

We additionally remark that this extra factor, although being only logarithmic, can indeed be very large. One of the most common usecases for the MNRS-framework is walking over Johnson graphs, which typically have exponentially many vertices. In those cases,
\pi_{\min}
is inevitably exponentially small, which means that
\log(1/\pi_{\min})
results in polynomial overhead.

Moreover, avoiding the extra overhead brings the results in line with the results from
[
CLM20
]
, who show that one can amortize the setup, update, and checking query complexities if these routines are constructed using learning graphs. This work generalizes that result to the case where these routines can be any feasible solution to the adversary bound.

1.4
Future directions

In the variable-time quantum walk search results that we obtain in this work are similar in flavor to those from
[
Jef22
]
, in the sense that the cost of
traversing
the edges is amortized. However, in some cases, it is the cost of
selecting
the next vertex to traverse to that we would like to traverse. We leave whether such an amortization can be achieved using our techniques for future research.

Furthermore, one of the most interesting problems in the context of quantum walks is the glued-trees problem, which obtains a provable exponential speed-up over its classical counterpart
[
CCD+03
,
Bel24
]
. It would be interesting to figure out if we can recover this exponential speed-up from the generalized graph composition framework, but we leave this for future work as well.

2.1
Notation

We write the set of natural numbers
\mathbb{N}=\{1,2,\dots\}
. Whenever we have an expression involving the symbol
\pm
, we interpret this as a pair of expressions where we put replace it with
+
and
-
, respectively. If we use the symbol
\pm
multiple times in the same expressions, we replace each with the same sign, and if we use
\mp
instead, we use the opposite sign. For example,
5\pm 3\mp 3=5
represents the two statements
5+3-3=5
and
5-3+3=5
.

Let
\Omega
be a finite set. We let
\Delta_{\Omega}
be the set of probability distributions on
\Omega
. We write
\mathbf{e}_{\omega}\in\mathbb{R}^{\Omega}
for the standard basis vector with its
1
at the position with index
\omega
.

Let
G=(V,E)
be an undirected graph. We associate to every edge
e\in E
an implicit direction, i.e., a starting and ending vertex
e_{-}\in V
and
e_{+}\in V
, respectively. For all
v\in V
, we write
N_{-}(v)\subseteq E
for all outgoing edges and
N_{+}(v)\subseteq E
for all incoming edges. We also write
N(v)=N_{-}(v)\cup N_{+}(v)
. As such,
N(v)\cap N(w)
is the set of all edges connecting
v
and
w
.

Let
d\in\mathbb{N}
and
f,g:\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}^{d}\supseteq\mathcal{D}\to\mathbb{R}
. We say that
f\in O(g)
if there exists a
C,M>0
such that for all
x\in\mathcal{D}
with
\left\|x\right\|\geq M
, then
|f(x)|\leq C\cdot|g(x)|
. We write
f\in\Omega(g)
if and only if
g\in O(f)
, and we write
\Theta(f)=O(f)\cap\Omega(f)
. Furthermore, we write
f\in\widetilde{O}(g)
, if there exists a
k\in\mathbb{N}
such that
f\in O(g\cdot\log^{k}(g))
. Similarly, we write
f\in\widetilde{\Omega}(g)
if and only if
g\in\widetilde{O}(f)
, and
\widetilde{\Theta}(f)=\widetilde{O}(f)\cap\widetilde{\Omega}(f)
. Finally, we write
f\in O(g\cdot\polylog((x_{j})_{j\in S}))
, for
S\subseteq[d]
, if there exist
k_{j}\in\mathbb{N}
, for every
j\in S
, such that
f\in O(g(x)\cdot\prod_{j\in S}\log^{k_{j}}(x_{j}))
.

2.2
Markov chains

We revisit the theory of Markov chains here. We base our exposition on
[
LP17
]
, but there exist plenty of alternative resources, e.g.,
[
Häg02
,
Kle08
]
.

Definition 2.1

(
[
LP17
, Section 1.1]
)
.

A Markov process
M=(\Omega,P)
is defined by:

1.

A finite state space
\Omega
.
5
5

5

In this work, we will always assume that
\Omega
is finite. There is also a vast literature on Markov chains that considers infinite state spaces, or even continuous ones, but this is beyond the scope of our work.

2.

A probability transition matrix
P\in\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}^{\Omega\times\Omega}
, such that every row sums to
1
.

If we take a random variable
X_{0}
according to some probability distribution on
\Omega
, and then select a sequence of random variables
X_{1},X_{2},\dots
such that for all
t\in\mathbb{N}
and
\omega_{1},\omega_{2}\in\Omega
,

\mathbb{P}[X_{t}=\omega_{2}|X_{t-1}=\omega_{1}]=P_{\omega_{1},\omega_{2}},

then we say that
X_{0},X_{1},X_{2},\dots
is a Markov chain. We say that the Markov chain transition from state
\omega_{1}\in\Omega
into state
\omega_{2}\in\Omega
with probability
P_{\omega_{1},\omega_{2}}
.

We discuss some properties of Markov chains.

Definition 2.2

(
[
LP17
, Sections 1.3 and 1.5]
)
.

Let
M=(\Omega,P)
be a Markov process.

1.

A distribution
\pi\in\Delta_{\Omega}
is a stationary distribution for
M
if
\pi=\pi P
.

2.

A Markov chain is irreducible if it is possible to reach any state from any other state through a sequence of transitions with non-zero probability. In other words, for all
\omega_{1},\omega_{2}\in\Omega
, there exists a
t\in\mathbb{N}
such that
(P^{t})_{\omega_{1},\omega_{2}}>0
.

Theorem 2.3

(
[
LP17
, Corollary 1.17]
)
.

If a Markov chain is irreducible, it has a unique stationary distribution, and
\pi_{\omega}>0
for all
\omega\in\Omega
.

A well-studied type of Markov processes are reversible. We introduce them formally here.

Definition 2.4

(
[
LP17
, Section 1.6]
)
.

Let
M=(\Omega,P)
be a Markov process with stationary distribution
\pi
. We say that
M
is reversible with stationary distribution
\pi
if for all
\omega_{1},\omega_{2}\in\Omega
, we have

\pi_{\omega_{1}}P_{\omega_{1},\omega_{2}}=\pi_{\omega_{2}}P_{\omega_{2},\omega_{1}}.

This condition is referred to as the detailed balance condition. If
M
is irreducible, then
\pi
is unique by
theorem

2.3
and so we simply say that
M
is reversible.

Note that this condition is a severe restriction, as it reduces the total number of degrees of freedom in
P
significantly. Nevertheless, this type of Markov processes is most important to us, because it has a direct connection to random walks on graphs, which we discuss next.

2.3
Random walks

We now focus our attention to random walks on graphs.

Definition 2.5

(
[
LP17
, Section 9.1]
)
.

Let
G=(V,E)
be an undirected graph, with resistances
r:E\to\mathbb{R}_{>0}
. Now, let
P\in\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}^{\Omega\times\Omega}
such that

P_{vw}=\sum_{e\in N(v)\cap N(w)}\frac{r_{v}}{r_{e}},\qquad\text{where}\qquad r_{v}=\left[\sum_{e\in N(v)}\frac{1}{r_{e}}\right]^{-1}.

The Markov process
M=(V,P)
is the random walk on
G
with resistances
r
.

We readily verify that
M
is indeed a valid Markov process, because all the row-sums of
P
are indeed
1
. We now derive some interesting properties of these random walks.

Theorem 2.6

(
[
LP17
, Section 9.1]
)
.

Let
G=(V,E)
be an undirected graph with resistances
r:E\to\mathbb{R}_{>0}
, and let
M=(V,P)
be the random walk on
G
with resistances
r
.

1.

Let
\pi\in\Delta_{V}
be defined by

\pi_{v}=\frac{R}{r_{v}},\qquad\text{where}\qquad R=\left[\sum_{v\in V}\frac{1}{r_{v}}\right]^{-1}.

Then,
M
is reversible with stationary distribution
\pi
.

2.

G
is connected if and only if
M
is irreducible. In that case,
\pi
is unique.

We see from the above theorem that random walks on connected undirected graphs generate irreducible, reversible Markov processes. It turns out that the converse is also true.

Theorem 2.7

(
[
LP17
, Section 9.1]
)
.

Let
M=(V,P)
be an irreducible, reversible Markov process, with stationary distribution
\pi
. Let
G=(\Omega,E)
be defined as

E=\{\{v,w\}:v,w\in V,P_{vw}>0\},\qquad\text{and}\qquad r_{\{v,w\}}=\frac{1}{\pi_{v}P_{vw}}.

Let
M^{\prime}
be the random walk on
G
with resistances
r
. Then,
M=M^{\prime}
.

Note that the above is well-defined, because we know from the detailed balance condition that
P_{vw}>0
if and only if
P_{wv}>0
, for any two states
v,w\in V
. Similarly, the definition or
r_{\{v,w\}}
makes sense because the expression on the right-hand side is symmetric in
v
and
w
due to the detailed balance condition.

2.4
Electrical networks

We can additionally think about undirected graphs with edge-resistances as electrical networks. We follow the exposition in
[
Cor25
]
, but very similar expositions can be found in
[
LP17
]
.

Definition 2.8

(
[
Cor25
, Section 4.1]
)
.

Let
G=(V,E)
be an undirected graph, with resistances
r:E\to\mathbb{R}_{>0}
.

1.

A flow is a function
f:E\to\mathbb{R}
. We embed all flows in the flow space
\mathbb{C}^{E}=\Span\{\left|e\right\rangle:e\in E\}
by

\left|f\right\rangle=\sum_{e\in E}f_{e}\sqrt{r_{e}}\left|e\right\rangle.

We refer to
\left\|\left|f\right\rangle\right\|^{2}
as the energy dissipated by
f
, or simply the energy of
f
.

2.

For any flow
f
, we define the net-flow
\delta_{f}:V\to\mathbb{R}
as

\delta_{f}(v)=\sum_{e\in N_{-}(v)}f_{e}-\sum_{e\in N_{+}(v)}f_{e}.

If
\delta_{f}\equiv 0
, then
f
is a circulation, and we define the circulation space as
\mathcal{C}=\{\left|f\right\rangle:f\text{ is a circulation}\}
.

3.

For any net-flow
\delta:V\to\mathbb{R}
, the set of corresponding flows
f
such that
\delta_{f}=\delta
forms an affine subspace, i.e.,
\left|f_{\delta}^{\min}\right\rangle+\mathcal{C}
. We refer to
f_{\delta}^{\min}
as the minimum-energy flow with net-flow
\delta
, and we write

R(G,r;\delta)=\left\|\left|f_{\delta}^{\min}\right\rangle\right\|^{2}.

We refer to this as the effective resistance of net-flow
\delta
in
G
with resistances
r
.

We observe that our definition of effective resistance agrees with the usual definition between two nodes
v,w\in V
. Indeed, if we take
\delta(v)=1
,
\delta(w)=-1
, and we take
\delta\equiv 0
everywhere else, then
R(G,r;\delta)
is indeed the effective resistance between
v
and
w
in
G
, as witnessed by Thomson’s principle (see e.g.,
[
LP17
, Theorem 9.10]
). As such, one can view our definition of effective resistance as a generalization of the usual version to more general net-flows
\delta
.

There exists a very natural way to compute the effective resistance through the graph Laplacian. We loosely follow the exposition as introduced in
[
vM23
]
.

Definition 2.9

(
[
vM23
, Section 2.1]
)
.

Let
G=(V,E)
be an undirected graph with resistance
r:E\to\mathbb{R}_{>0}
.

1.

The weighted incidence matrix is the matrix
B\in\mathbb{R}^{V\times E}
is defined as

B_{ve}=\begin{cases}\frac{1}{\sqrt{r_{e}}},&\text{if }e_{-}\neq e_{+}\land v=e_{-},\\
-\frac{1}{\sqrt{r_{e}}},&\text{if }e_{-}\neq e_{+}\land v=e_{+},\\
0,&\text{otherwise}.\end{cases}

2.

The graph Laplacian
L\in\mathbb{R}^{V\times V}
is defined as
L=BB^{T}
.

We now observe that the effective resistance can be computed through the Laplacian.

Theorem 2.10
.

R(G,r;\delta)=\delta^{T}L^{+}\delta
, where
L^{+}
is the Moore-Penrose pseudo-inverse of
L
.

One can find the proof of this theorem in
[
vM23
, Section 5.1]
for the case where
\delta=e_{v}-e_{w}
, for
v,w\in V
, but we need this result for arbitrary net-flows
\delta
. This is why we provide a self-contained proof here.

Proof of
theorem

2.10
.

It is elementary to check that for any flow
f
, we have
B\left|f\right\rangle=\delta_{f}
. As such, the minimum-energy flow
f
that has net-flow
\delta
satisfies
\left|f_{\delta}^{\min}\right\rangle=B^{+}\delta
. Now, we obtain

R(G,r;\delta)=\left\langle f_{\delta}^{\min}\middle|f_{\delta}^{\min}\right\rangle=\delta^{T}(B^{+})^{T}B^{+}\delta=\delta^{T}(BB^{T})^{+}\delta=\delta^{T}L^{+}\delta.\qed

For future reference, we relate the Laplacian of the graph that is generated from an irreducible, reversible Markov process to its probability-transition matrix.

Theorem 2.11

(
[
vM23
, Section 3.11]
)
.

Let
M=(V,P)
be an irreducible, reversible Markov process with stationary distribution
\pi
. Let
G
be the corresponding graph with resistances
r
. Let
L
be its Laplacian. Then,

L=\diag(\pi)(I-P)\qquad\Leftrightarrow\qquad P=I-\diag(\pi)^{-1}L.

2.5
Quantum adversary bound for state-conversion and transducers

We use the formulation of the quantum adversary bound for state conversion from
[
BJY24
, Section 8.1]
. This semi-definite optimization program was first introduced in
[
LMR+11
, Definition 3.2]
, also appeared in
[
Bel15
, Definition 8]
, and subsequently in
[
BY23
, Definition 7.3]
.

Definition 2.12

(Quantum adversary bound for state conversion)
.

Let
\mathcal{D}
be some finite domain, and for all
x\in\mathcal{D}
, we define
\left|\sigma_{x}\right\rangle\in\mathcal{V}
,
\left|\tau_{x}\right\rangle\in\mathcal{V}
and
O_{x}\in\mathcal{L}(\mathcal{M})
. Then,
P=\{(\left|\sigma_{x}\right\rangle,\left|\tau_{x}\right\rangle,O_{x})\}_{x\in\mathcal{D}}
is a state-conversion problem, and its adversary bound is the following optimization program:

\displaystyle\min\quad

\displaystyle\max_{x\in\mathcal{D}}\left\|\left|w_{x}\right\rangle\right\|^{2},

s.t.

\displaystyle\left\langle\sigma_{x}\middle|\sigma_{y}\right\rangle-\left\langle\tau_{x}\middle|\tau_{y}\right\rangle=\left\langle w_{x}\right|\left(\left(I_{\mathcal{M}}-O_{x}^{\dagger}O_{y}\right)\otimes I_{\mathcal{W}}\right)\left|w_{y}\right\rangle,

\displaystyle\forall(x,y)\in\mathcal{D},

\displaystyle\left|w_{x}\right\rangle\in\mathcal{M}\otimes\mathcal{W},\text{ where }\mathcal{W}\text{ is a Hilbert space},

\displaystyle\forall x\in\mathcal{D}.

We denote the optimal value by
\ADV(P)
. If all the states satisfy
\left\|\left|\sigma_{x}\right\rangle\right\|=\left\|\left|\tau_{x}\right\rangle\right\|=1
, for all
x\in\mathcal{D}
, then we say that this is a unit-norm state-conversion problem, and if the operations
O_{x}
are unitaries, we say that
P
is a unitary-oracle state-conversion problem. For a function
f:\Sigma_{1}\to\Sigma_{2}
, we define
\left|\sigma_{x}\right\rangle=\left|\perp\right\rangle
and
\left|\tau_{x}\right\rangle=\left|f(x)\right\rangle
, for all
x\in\mathcal{D}
, and we abbreviate the optimal value to
\ADV(\{(\left|\bot\right\rangle,\left|f(x)\right\rangle,O_{x})\}_{x\in\mathcal{D}})=\ADV(f,\{O_{x}\}_{x\in\mathcal{D}})
.

Note that this definition is subtly different from other versions of the adversary bound that can be found in the existing literature. We refer to
[
BJY24
, Section 8.2]
for a discussion on why these differ by a constant factor of
2
.

The relevance of the quantum adversary bound is highlighted by the fact that one can convert a feasible solution to a transducer.

Definition 2.13

(
[
BJY24
]
)
.

A transducer
U
is a unitary on
\mathcal{V}\oplus\mathcal{H}
. Let
\left|\sigma\right\rangle,\left|\tau\right\rangle\in\mathcal{V}
and
\left|w\right\rangle\in\mathcal{H}
. If
U
performs the mapping
\left|\sigma\right\rangle\oplus\left|w\right\rangle\overset{U}{\mapsto}\left|\tau\right\rangle\oplus\left|w\right\rangle
, then we say that
U
transduces
\left|\sigma\right\rangle
to
\left|\tau\right\rangle
, and we write
\left|\sigma\right\rangle\overset{U}{\rightsquigarrow}\left|\tau\right\rangle
, and we refer to
\left|w\right\rangle
as the catalyst for this transduction.

We now borrow the following properties of transducers.

Theorem 2.14

(
[
BJY24
, Theorem 5.1]
)
.

Let
U
be a transducer on
\mathcal{V}\oplus\mathcal{H}
. Then, for every
\left|\sigma\right\rangle\in\mathcal{V}
, there exists a unique
\left|\tau\right\rangle\in\mathcal{V}
such that
\left|\sigma\right\rangle\overset{U}{\rightsquigarrow}\left|\tau\right\rangle
. Moreover, the minimal catalyst, i.e., the catalyst
\left|w\right\rangle\in\mathcal{H}
for this transduction of minimal norm is uniquely defined. Finally, the mappings
U_{\rotatebox[origin]{-90.0}{$\rightsquigarrow$}\mathcal{V}}:\left|\sigma\right\rangle\mapsto\left|\tau\right\rangle
and
U_{\rightsquigarrow\mathcal{V}}:\left|\sigma\right\rangle\mapsto\left|w\right\rangle
are linear.

Finally, we observe that transduction actions can be emulated in quantum algorithms, and that the efficiency of this operation depends on the norm of the catalyst state.

Theorem 2.15

(
[
BJY24
, Theorem 5.5]
)
.

Let
U
be a transducer on
\mathcal{V}\oplus\mathcal{H}
, and
K>0
. Then, there exists a quantum circuit
\mathcal{A}
that calls
U
a total of
K
times, and that implements the mapping

\left\|\mathcal{A}\left|\sigma\right\rangle-U_{\rotatebox[origin]{-90.0}{$\rightsquigarrow$}\mathcal{V}}\left|\sigma\right\rangle\right\|\leq\frac{2\left\|U_{\rightsquigarrow\mathcal{V}}\left|\sigma\right\rangle\right\|}{\sqrt{K}}.

Next, it is shown that one can turn an adversary bound solution into a transducer.

Theorem 2.16

(
[
BJY24
, Section 8.1]
)
.

Let
\{\sigma_{x}\}_{x\in\mathcal{D}}\subseteq\mathcal{V}_{1}
,
\{\tau_{x}\}_{x\in\mathcal{D}}\subseteq\mathcal{V}_{2}
and
\{O_{x}\}_{x\in\mathcal{D}}\subseteq\mathcal{L}(\mathcal{M})
define a state-conversion problem
P
. Let
\{\left|w_{x}\right\rangle\}_{x\in\mathcal{D}}\subseteq\mathcal{M}\otimes\mathcal{W}
be a feasible solution to the quantum adversary bound for state conversion. Then, there exists a unitary
U
acting on
(\mathcal{V}_{1}\oplus\mathcal{V}_{2})\oplus(\mathcal{M}\otimes\mathcal{W})
, such that
U_{x}:=U\cdot((I_{\mathcal{V}_{1}}\oplus I_{\mathcal{V}_{2}})\oplus(O_{x}\otimes I_{\mathcal{W}}))
acts as

(U_{x})_{\rotatebox[origin]{-90.0}{$\rightsquigarrow$}(\mathcal{V}_{1}\oplus\mathcal{V}_{2})}:\left|\sigma_{x}\right\rangle\mapsto\left|\tau_{x}\right\rangle,\qquad\text{and}\qquad(U_{x})_{\rightsquigarrow(\mathcal{V}_{1}\oplus\mathcal{V}_{2})}:\left|\sigma_{x}\right\rangle\mapsto\left|w_{x}\right\rangle.

Consequently,
\mathsf{Q}(P)\in O(\ADV(P))
.

3
Effective resistances of Markov processes

In this section, we explore the connection between Markov processes and the effective resistance. We start by defining some shorthand notation:

Definition 3.1
.

Let
M=(V,P)
be an irreducible, reversible Markov process, and let
G=(V,E)
be the corresponding undirected graph with resistance function
r
, as in
theorem

2.7
. For any net-flow
\delta\in\mathbb{R}^{V}
, we write
R_{\mathrm{eff}}(P;\delta)=R_{\mathrm{eff}}(G,r;\delta)
. We also write

1.

For all
v,w\in V
, we write
R_{\mathrm{eff}}(G,r;v\leftrightarrow w)=R_{\mathrm{eff}}(G,r;\mathbbm{1}_{v}-\mathbbm{1}_{w})
, i.e., the effective resistance between vertices
v
and
w
.

2.

For any two distributions
\mu,\nu\in\Delta_{V}
, we write
R_{\mathrm{eff}}(G,r;\mu\leftrightarrow\nu)=R_{\mathrm{eff}}(G,r;\mu-\nu)
.

3.

For a distribution
\mu\in\Delta_{V}
and a set
M\subseteq V
, we write

R_{\mathrm{eff}}(G,r;\mu\leftrightarrow M)=\min_{\nu\in\Delta_{V}:\nu|_{V\setminus M}\equiv 0}R_{\mathrm{eff}}(G,r;\mu\leftrightarrow\nu).

We extend all these notations
R(G,r;\cdot)
to
R(P;\cdot)
as well.

The effective resistance
R_{\mathrm{eff}}(P;v\leftrightarrow w)
between two distinct vertices
v,w\in V
can be interpreted combinatorially as the “commute time” between
v
and
w
, i.e., the expected time it takes to start a random walk in
v
, hit
w
, and then come back to
v

[
LP17
, Proposition 10.7]
. Similarly, the effective resistance
R_{\mathrm{eff}}(P;\pi\leftrightarrow M)
can be interpreted as the expected time to hit
M
starting from a vertex sampled from
\pi
, where
\pi
is the stationary distribution of the random walk.

However, Apers, Gilyén and Jeffery realized that
[
AGJ21
]
these combinatorial interpretations fail in more complicated settings. For instance, they showed that the effective resistance
R_{\mathrm{eff}}(P;\mu\leftrightarrow M)
, with
\mu\in\Delta_{S}
and
S,M\subseteq V
, cannot be interpreted as some commute time between
S
and
M
. This inherently limits our analysis of the effective resistance, especially in cases where the net-flow
\delta
does not bear any apparent structure.

How these combinatorial interpretations come into play can be very nicely illustrated when we consider fast-forwarding walks. Indeed, suppose that we have a Markov process
M=(V,P)
, and now we consider
M^{t}:=(V,P^{t})
, i.e., the process that takes
t>1
steps of the original walk. Using combinatorial arguments, we can now easily prove that

R_{\mathrm{eff}}(P;v\leftrightarrow w)\leq t\cdot R_{\mathrm{eff}}(P^{t};v\leftrightarrow w).

Indeed, if we interpret the left- and right-hand side as commute times, then the inequality is obvious, because we are essentially considering the same random process on both sides of the equation, but on the left-hand side we are checking at every step whether we hit the vertex
w
or returned back to
v
, whereas on the right-hand side we check this only once every
t
steps. However, we cannot use these combinatorial arguments to prove, for instance, that
R_{\mathrm{eff}}(P;\delta)\leq t\cdot R_{\mathrm{eff}}(P^{t};\delta)
, for any net-flow
\delta
.

To mitigate this, we drop the combinatorial interpretation of the effective resistance, and interpret it linear-algebraically, i.e., using its relation to the graph Laplacian and the probability transition matrix
theorems

2.10
and
2.11
. This allows us to obtain the following result, which we expect to be independently interesting:

Lemma 3.2
.

Let
M=(V,P)
be an irreducible, reversible Markov process, with stationary distribution
\pi
. Let
\xi\in\mathbb{R}_{V}
such that
\sum_{v\in V}\xi_{v}=0
. We write
D=\diag(\pi)
, and
\widetilde{\xi}=D^{-1/2}\xi
. Then, we have the following two consequences:

1.

For integer
t\geq 1
,
R_{\mathrm{eff}}(P;\xi)\leq t\cdot R_{\mathrm{eff}}(P^{t};\xi)
.

2.

If the spectral gap of
P
is
\delta
, we have
R_{\mathrm{eff}}(P;\xi)\leq\|\widetilde{\xi}\|_{2}^{2}/\delta
.

Proof.

Recall from
theorem

2.3
that the stationary distribution
\pi
is uniquely defined. Next, recall from
theorem

2.11
that
L=D(I-P)=D^{1/2}(I-\widetilde{P})D^{1/2}
is the Laplacian of the graph that
M
walks on. We observe that
\widetilde{P}=I-D^{-1/2}LD^{-1/2}
is symmetric, and so we can diagonalize
\widetilde{P}
and write it as

\widetilde{P}=\sum_{j=1}^{r}\lambda_{j}v_{j}v_{j}^{T},\qquad\text{which implies}\qquad(I-\widetilde{P})^{+}=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}j=1\\
\lambda_{j}<1\end{subarray}}^{r}\frac{v_{j}v_{j}^{T}}{1-\lambda_{j}}.

Thus, we obtain that

R_{\mathrm{eff}}(P;\xi)=\xi^{T}L^{+}\xi=\widetilde{\xi}^{T}(I-\widetilde{P})^{+}\widetilde{\xi}=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}j=1\\
\lambda_{j}<1\end{subarray}}^{r}\frac{|\widetilde{\xi}^{T}v_{j}|^{2}}{1-\lambda_{j}}.

Now, for the first claim, let
t\geq 1
be an integer, and observe that
\widetilde{P}^{t}=D^{-1/2}P^{t}D^{1/2}
. Using
1/(1-x)\leq t/(1-x^{t})
, for all
x<1
, we observe that

R_{\mathrm{eff}}(P;\xi)=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}j=1\\
\lambda_{j}<1\end{subarray}}^{r}\frac{|\widetilde{\xi}^{T}v_{j}|^{2}}{1-\lambda_{j}}\leq t\cdot\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}j=1\\
\lambda_{j}<1\end{subarray}}^{r}\frac{|\widetilde{\xi}^{T}v_{j}|^{2}}{1-\lambda_{j}^{t}}=t\cdot R_{\mathrm{eff}}(P^{t};\xi).

For the second claim, observe that for all
\lambda_{j}<1
, we have
\lambda_{j}\leq 1-\delta
. Thus, we obtain

R_{\mathrm{eff}}(P;\xi)=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}j=1\\
\lambda_{j}<1\end{subarray}}^{r}\frac{|\widetilde{\xi}^{T}v_{j}|^{2}}{1-\lambda_{j}}\leq\frac{1}{\delta}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}j=1\\
\lambda_{j}<1\end{subarray}}^{r}|\widetilde{\xi}^{T}v_{j}|^{2}\leq\frac{\|\widetilde{\xi}\|_{2}^{2}}{\delta}.\qed

Along similar lines, we prove a lower bound on the effective resistance using linear-algebraic methods.

Lemma 3.3
.

Let
t\geq 1
be an integer. Let
M=(V,P)
be an irreducible, reversible Markov process, with stationary distribution
\pi\in\Delta_{V}
. Let
\sigma,\nu\in\Delta_{V}
have disjoint support. Then,
\sum_{v\in V}\nu_{v}^{2}/\pi_{v}\leq 2R_{\mathrm{eff}}(P^{t};\sigma-\nu)
.

Proof.

Recall from
theorem

2.11
that
D(I-P)=L\succeq 0
. Thus, we have
D^{1/2}(I-D^{1/2}PD^{-1/2})D^{1/2}\succeq 0
, and by consequence also
I-D^{1/2}PD^{-1/2}\succeq 0
. Moreover, the eigenvalues of
D^{1/2}PD^{-1/2}
are the same as those of
P
, because they are related by a similarity transform. We also have
\left\|P\right\|\leq 1
, and so we find that all these eigenvalues are in the interval
[-1,1]
. Combining everything implies that the eigenvalues of
I-D^{1/2}PD^{-1/2}
are contained in the interval
[0,2]
. Thus, all the eigenvalues of its Moore-Penrose pseudo-inverse are at least
1/2
, and since the unique
0
-eigenvector is
\sqrt{\pi}
, we find that
(I-D^{-1/2}PD^{-1/2})^{+}\succeq(I-\sqrt{\pi}\sqrt{\pi}^{T})/2
. Thus, we find that

\displaystyle R_{\text{eff}}(P^{t},\sigma-\nu)

\displaystyle=(\sigma-\nu)^{T}L^{+}(\sigma-\nu)=(\sigma-\nu)^{T}D^{-1/2}(I-D^{1/2}P^{t}D^{-1/2})^{+}D^{-1/2}(\sigma-\nu)

\displaystyle\geq\frac{1}{2}(\sigma-\nu)^{T}D^{-1/2}(I-\sqrt{\pi}\sqrt{\pi}^{T})D^{-1/2}(\sigma-\nu)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}(\sigma-\nu)^{T}D^{-1}(\sigma-\nu)-\underbrace{\frac{1}{2}(\sigma-\nu)\mathbbm{1}\mathbbm{1}^{T}(\sigma-\nu)}_{=0}=\frac{1}{2}\sum_{v\in V}\frac{(\sigma_{v}-\nu_{v})^{2}}{\pi_{v}}\geq\frac{1}{2}\sum_{v\in\supp(\nu)}\frac{\nu_{v}^{2}}{\pi_{v}}.\qed

4
Explicit transducers from adversary-bound solutions

In
[
BJY24
, Section 8.1]
, Belovs, Jeffery and Yolcu present a way to convert feasible solutions to the dual adversary bound for state conversion into transducers. However, this result only asserts the existence of such a transducer, but the proof is not constructive. In this section, we provide a constructive proof for the same result.

We start with a structural observation, namely that we can equivalently look at a reformulated version of the state-conversion problem, in which we are reflecting through exactly half of the input states. As such, we conveniently refer to this specific form of a state-conversion problem as a
state-reflection problem
.

Definition 4.1

(State-reflection problem)
.

Let
\mathcal{D}
be a finite set. For all
x\in\mathcal{D}
, let
\left|\sigma_{x}^{+}\right\rangle,\left|\sigma_{x}^{-}\right\rangle
be two orthogonal vectors in
\mathcal{V}
, and let
O_{x}
be an operation on
\mathcal{H}
, such that
O_{x}^{2}=I
. We refer to the state-conversion problem
\{(\left|\sigma_{x}^{\pm}\right\rangle,\pm\left|\sigma_{x}^{\pm}\right\rangle,O_{x})\}_{x\in\mathcal{D}}
as a state-reflection problem, and we write it as
R=\{(\left|\sigma_{x}^{+}\right\rangle,\left|\sigma_{x}^{-}\right\rangle,O_{x})\}_{x\in\mathcal{D}}
for short. Let
w:=\{\left|w_{x}^{\pm}\right\rangle\}_{x\in\mathcal{D}}
be a feasible solution to
R
, satisfying
O_{x}\left|w_{x}^{\pm}\right\rangle=\pm\left|w_{x}^{\pm}\right\rangle
. We refer to
\left|w_{x}^{+}\right\rangle
and
\left|w_{x}^{-}\right\rangle
as the positive and negative witnesses for
x
, and similarly to
\left\|\left|w_{x}^{+}\right\rangle\right\|^{2}
and
\left\|\left|w_{x}^{-}\right\rangle\right\|^{2}
as the positive and negative witness sizes for
x\in\mathcal{D}
, which write as
\mathsf{R}^{+}_{x}(w)
and
\mathsf{R}^{-}_{x}(w)
.

We now show that every unit-norm unitary-oracle state-conversion problem can be equivalently reformulated as a state-reflection problem. This equivalence only holds if we have access to both forward and backwards versions of the oracle
O_{x}
and
O_{x}^{\dagger}
, though, in other cases the situation might be more subtle.

Lemma 4.2
.

Let
P=\{(\left|\sigma_{x}\right\rangle,\left|\tau_{x}\right\rangle,O_{x})\}_{x\in\mathcal{D}}
be a unit-norm unitary-oracle state-conversion problem, with input and output and oracle spaces
\mathcal{V}_{1}
,
\mathcal{V}_{2}
and
\mathcal{H}
, respectively. For all
x\in\mathcal{D}
, we write

\left|\sigma_{x}^{\pm}\right\rangle=\frac{\left|\sigma_{x}\right\rangle\oplus\pm\left|\tau_{x}\right\rangle}{\sqrt{2}}\in\mathcal{V}_{1}\oplus\mathcal{V}_{2},\qquad\text{and}\qquad\overline{O_{x}}=(X\otimes I)(O_{x}\oplus O_{x}^{\dagger})\in\mathcal{L}(\mathcal{H}\oplus\mathcal{H}),

where
X\otimes I
acting on
\mathcal{H}\oplus\mathcal{H}
swaps both copies of
\mathcal{H}
. Then,
R=\{(\left|\sigma_{x}^{+}\right\rangle,\left|\sigma_{x}^{-}\right\rangle,\overline{O_{x}})\}_{x\in\mathcal{D}}
is a state-reflection problem,
\ADV(P)=\ADV(R)
, and there exists an optimal solution
\{\left|w_{x}^{\pm}\right\rangle\}_{x\in\mathcal{D}}
to
R
such that
\overline{O_{x}}\left|w_{x}^{\pm}\right\rangle=\pm\left|w_{x}^{\pm}\right\rangle
.

Proof.

We first show that
R
satisfies the conditions of a state-reflection problem, as outlined in
definition

4.1
. To that end, we check that
\left|\sigma_{x}^{+}\right\rangle
and
\left|\sigma_{x}^{-}\right\rangle
are orthogonal for all
x\in D
, since

\left\langle\sigma_{x}^{+}\middle|\sigma_{x}^{-}\right\rangle=\left(\frac{\left\langle\sigma_{x}\right|\oplus\left\langle\tau_{x}\right|}{\sqrt{2}}\right)\left(\frac{\left|\sigma_{x}\right\rangle\oplus\left|\tau_{x}\right\rangle}{\sqrt{2}}\right)=\frac{1}{2}\left(\left\langle\sigma_{x}\middle|\sigma_{x}\right\rangle-\left\langle\tau_{x}\middle|\tau_{x}\right\rangle\right)=0.

Next, we observe that
\overline{O_{x}}^{2}=I
, for all
x\in\mathcal{D}
, as

\overline{O_{x}}^{2}=(X\otimes I)(O_{x}\oplus O_{x}^{\dagger})(X\otimes I)(O_{x}\oplus O_{x}^{\dagger})=(X\otimes I)(X\otimes I)\cdot(O_{x}^{\dagger}\oplus O_{x})(O_{x}\oplus O_{x}^{\dagger})=I.

Thus,
R
indeed is a state-reflection problem.

Next, we prove that the adversary values are the same for
P
and
R
. To that end, let
\{\left|w_{x}\right\rangle\}_{x\in\mathcal{D}}
be a feasible solution to
P
. Now, for all
x\in\mathcal{D}
, we define

\left|w_{x}^{\pm}\right\rangle:=\frac{\left|w_{x}\right\rangle\oplus\pm O_{x}\left|w_{x}\right\rangle}{\sqrt{2}},

and we verify that this is a feasible solution to
R
. First, let
x\in\mathcal{D}
, and observe that

\overline{O_{x}}\left|w_{x}^{\pm}\right\rangle=\frac{\mathrm{SWAP}(O_{x}\left|w_{x}\right\rangle\oplus\pm O_{x}^{\dagger}O_{x}\left|w_{x}\right\rangle)}{\sqrt{2}}=\frac{\pm\left|w_{x}\right\rangle\oplus O_{x}\left|w_{x}\right\rangle}{\sqrt{2}}=\pm\left|w_{x}^{\pm}\right\rangle.

Next, let
x,y\in\mathcal{D}
, and observe that

\left\langle\sigma_{x}^{\pm}\middle|\sigma_{y}^{\pm}\right\rangle-\left(\pm\left\langle\sigma_{x}^{\pm}\right|\right)(\pm\left|\sigma_{y}^{\pm}\right\rangle)=\left\langle\sigma_{x}^{\pm}\middle|\sigma_{y}^{\pm}\right\rangle-\left\langle\sigma_{x}^{\pm}\middle|\sigma_{y}^{\pm}\right\rangle=0,

and on the other hand

\left\langle w_{x}^{\pm}\right|(I-\overline{O_{x}}^{\dagger}\overline{O}_{y})\left|w_{y}^{\pm}\right\rangle=\left\langle w_{x}^{\pm}\middle|w_{y}^{\pm}\right\rangle-(\pm\left\langle w_{x}^{\pm}\right|)(\pm\left|w_{y}^{\pm}\right\rangle)=\left\langle w_{x}^{\pm}\middle|w_{y}^{\pm}\right\rangle-\left\langle w_{x}^{\pm}\middle|w_{y}^{\pm}\right\rangle=0.

Thus, it remains to verify what happens for the inner products between
+
and
-
. To that end, let
x,y\in\mathcal{D}
, and observe that

\displaystyle\left\langle\sigma_{x}^{+}\middle|\sigma_{y}^{-}\right\rangle-\left\langle\sigma_{x}^{+}\right|(-\left|\sigma_{y}^{-}\right\rangle)=2\left\langle\sigma_{x}^{+}\middle|\sigma_{y}^{-}\right\rangle=2\left(\frac{\left\langle\sigma_{x}\right|\oplus\left\langle\tau_{x}\right|}{\sqrt{2}}\right)\left(\frac{\left|\sigma_{y}\right\rangle\oplus-\left|\tau_{y}\right\rangle}{\sqrt{2}}\right)

\displaystyle\qquad=\left\langle\sigma_{x}\middle|\sigma_{y}\right\rangle-\left\langle\tau_{x}\middle|\tau_{y}\right\rangle=\left\langle w_{x}\right|(I-O_{x}^{\dagger}O_{y})\left|w_{y}\right\rangle=2\left\langle w_{x}^{+}\middle|w_{y}^{-}\right\rangle=\left\langle w_{x}^{+}\right|(I-\overline{O_{x}}^{\dagger}\overline{O_{y}})\left|w_{y}^{-}\right\rangle.

Thus,
\{\left|w_{x}^{\pm}\right\rangle\}_{x\in\mathcal{D}}
is indeed a feasible solution to
R
, with the same objective function as
P
, and satisfying
\overline{O_{x}}\left|w_{x}^{\pm}\right\rangle=\pm\left|w_{x}^{\pm}\right\rangle
. Thus,
\ADV(R)\leq\ADV(P)
.

It remains to verify the reverse inequality. To that end, let
\{\left|w_{x}^{\pm}\right\rangle\}_{x\in\mathcal{D}}
be a feasible solution to
R
. Now, for all
x\in\mathcal{D}
and
b\in\{\pm\}
, we write
\left|(w_{x}^{\pm})_{b}\right\rangle=(I+b\overline{O_{x}})\left|w_{x}^{\pm}\right\rangle/2
, i.e.,
\left|(w_{x}^{+})_{+}\right\rangle
is the projection of
\left|w_{x}^{+}\right\rangle
onto the
+1
-eigenspace of
\overline{O_{x}}
, and similarly for the other choices for
b
and
\pm
. In particular, for all
b\in\{\pm\}
,
\overline{O_{x}}\left|(w_{x}^{\pm})_{b}\right\rangle=b\left|(w_{x}^{\pm})_{b}\right\rangle
, and for all
s,t\in\{\pm\}
, we have
\left\langle(w_{x}^{s})_{b}\middle|(w_{x}^{t})_{1-b}\right\rangle=0
. We let

\left|w_{x}\right\rangle:=\frac{(I\oplus O_{x}^{\dagger}\oplus I\oplus O_{x}^{\dagger})(\left|(w_{x}^{+})_{0}\right\rangle\oplus\left|(w_{x}^{+})_{1}\right\rangle+\left|(w_{x}^{-})_{1}\right\rangle\oplus\left|(w_{x}^{-})_{0}\right\rangle)}{\sqrt{2}}.

Then, for all
x,y\in\mathcal{D}
, we have

\displaystyle\left\langle w_{x}\right|(I-O_{x}^{\dagger}O_{y}\otimes I_{4})\left|w_{y}\right\rangle=\left\langle w_{x}\right|(I-O_{x}^{\dagger}O_{y}\oplus O_{x}^{\dagger}O_{y}\oplus O_{x}^{\dagger}O_{y}\oplus O_{x}^{\dagger}O_{y})\left|w_{y}\right\rangle

\displaystyle\qquad=\frac{1}{2}\sum_{b,s,t\in\{\pm\}}\left\langle(w_{x}^{s})_{b}\right|(I\oplus O_{x})(I-O_{x}^{\dagger}O_{y}\oplus O_{x}^{\dagger}O_{y})(I\oplus O_{y}^{\dagger})\left|(w_{y}^{t})_{1-b}\right\rangle

\displaystyle\qquad=\frac{1}{2}\sum_{b,s,t\in\{\pm\}}\left\langle(w_{x}^{s})_{b}\right|(I\oplus O_{x}^{\dagger}O_{y})\left|(w_{y}^{t})_{1-b}\right\rangle-\left\langle(w_{x}^{s})_{b}\right|(O_{x}O_{y}^{\dagger}\oplus I)\left|(w_{y}^{t})_{1-b}\right\rangle

\displaystyle\qquad=\frac{1}{2}\sum_{b,s,t\in\{\pm\}}\left\langle(w_{x}^{s})_{b}\middle|(w_{y}^{t})_{1-b}\right\rangle-\left\langle(w_{x}^{s})_{b}\right|(O_{x}O_{y}^{\dagger}\oplus O_{x}O_{y}^{\dagger})\left|(w_{y}^{t})_{1-b}\right\rangle

\displaystyle\qquad=\frac{1}{2}\sum_{b,s,t\in\{\pm\}}\left\langle(w_{x}^{s})_{b}\right|(I-\overline{O_{x}}^{\dagger}\overline{O_{y}})\left|(w_{y}^{t})_{1-b}\right\rangle=\frac{1}{2}\sum_{b,c,s,t\in\{\pm\}}\left\langle(w_{x}^{s})_{b}\right|(I-\overline{O_{x}}^{\dagger}\overline{O_{y}})\left|(w_{y}^{t})_{c}\right\rangle

\displaystyle\qquad=\frac{1}{2}\sum_{s,t\in\{\pm\}}\left\langle w_{x}^{s}\right|(I-\overline{O_{x}}^{\dagger}\overline{O_{y}})\left|w_{y}^{t}\right\rangle=\frac{1}{2}\sum_{s,t\in\{\pm\}}\left\langle\sigma_{x}^{s}\middle|\sigma_{y}^{t}\right\rangle-st\left\langle\sigma_{x}^{s}\middle|\sigma_{y}^{t}\right\rangle=\left\langle\sigma_{x}^{+}\middle|\sigma_{x}^{-}\right\rangle+\left\langle\sigma_{x}^{-}\middle|\sigma_{x}^{+}\right\rangle=\left\langle\sigma_{x}\middle|\tau_{x}\right\rangle,

and finally we observe that for all
x\in\mathcal{D}
,

\displaystyle\left\|\left|w_{x}\right\rangle\right\|^{2}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\left[\left\|\left|(w_{x}^{+})_{0}\right\rangle\right\|^{2}+\left\|\left|(w_{x}^{+})_{1}\right\rangle\right\|^{2}+\left\|\left|(w_{x}^{-})_{1}\right\rangle\right\|^{2}+\left\|\left|(w_{x}^{-})_{0}\right\rangle\right\|^{2}\right]=\frac{1}{2}\left[\left\|\left|w_{x}^{+}\right\rangle\right\|^{2}+\left\|\left|w_{x}^{-}\right\rangle\right\|^{2}\right]

\displaystyle\leq\ADV(R),

and so
\ADV(P)\leq\ADV(R)
.
∎

The previous theorem tells us that we can without loss of generality assume that a feasible solution to a state-reflection problem with oracle
O_{x}
has the property that for all
x\in\mathcal{D}
,
O_{x}\left|w_{x}^{\pm}\right\rangle=\pm\left|w_{x}^{\pm}\right\rangle
. We will assume this without further mention in the remainder of this text, and we remark explicitly here that it follows that
\left\langle w_{x}^{+}\middle|w_{x}^{-}\right\rangle=0
, for all
x\in\mathcal{D}
, since they live in different eigenspaces of
O_{x}
.

Next, we observe that for a state-reflection problem, the unitary
U
that is required to implement the transduction action becomes a reflection through a particular subspace. This is the objective of the following theorem.

Theorem 4.3

(Explicit implementation of transducers)
.

Let
R=\{(\left|\sigma_{x}^{\pm}\right\rangle,\pm\left|\sigma_{x}^{\pm}\right\rangle,O_{x})\}_{x\in\mathcal{D}}
be a state-reflection problem, with feasible solution
\{\left|w_{x}^{\pm}\right\rangle\}_{x\in\mathcal{D}}
. Let, let
\mathcal{A}\subseteq\mathcal{V}\oplus\mathcal{H}
be a subspace such that

\Span\left\{\begin{bmatrix}\left|\sigma_{x}^{+}\right\rangle\\
\left|w_{x}^{+}\right\rangle\end{bmatrix}\right\}_{x\in\mathcal{D}}\subseteq\mathcal{A}\subseteq\Span\left\{\begin{bmatrix}\left|\sigma_{x}^{-}\right\rangle\\
\left|w_{x}^{-}\right\rangle\end{bmatrix}\right\}_{x\in\mathcal{D}}^{\perp}.

Then, the unitary
U=2\Pi_{A}-I
is such that
U(I\oplus O_{x})
implements
\left|\sigma_{x}^{\pm}\right\rangle\overset{U(I\oplus O_{x})}{\rightsquigarrow}\pm\left|\sigma_{x}^{\pm}\right\rangle
.

Proof.

For any
x,y\in\mathcal{D}
, we have

\displaystyle(\left\langle\sigma_{x}^{+}\right|\oplus\left\langle w_{x}^{+}\right|)(\left|\sigma_{y}^{-}\right\rangle\oplus\left|w_{y}^{-}\right\rangle)

\displaystyle=\left\langle\sigma_{x}^{+}\middle|\sigma_{y}^{-}\right\rangle+\left\langle w_{x}^{+}\middle|w_{y}^{-}\right\rangle

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\left(\left\langle\sigma_{x}^{+}\middle|\sigma_{y}^{-}\right\rangle-\left\langle\sigma_{x}^{+}\right|(-\left|\sigma_{y}^{-}\right\rangle)+\left\langle w_{x}^{+}\right|(I-O_{x}^{\dagger}O_{y})\left|w_{y}^{-}\right\rangle\right)=0,

and so there indeed exists a subspace
\mathcal{A}
that satisfies this definition. Moreover, we have

\begin{bmatrix}\left|\sigma_{x}^{\pm}\right\rangle\\
\left|w_{x}^{\pm}\right\rangle\end{bmatrix}\overset{I\oplus O_{x}}{\mapsto}\begin{bmatrix}\left|\sigma_{x}^{\pm}\right\rangle\\
\pm\left|w_{x}^{\pm}\right\rangle\end{bmatrix}\overset{U}{\mapsto}\begin{bmatrix}\pm\left|\sigma_{x}^{\pm}\right\rangle\\
\left|w_{x}^{\pm}\right\rangle\end{bmatrix}.\qed

This explicit construction for the unitary
U
generalizes the explicit implementation of a transducer from a span program
[
Cor25
, Theorem 3.3]
. We also remark specifically that in the regular graph composition framework, one can take the space
\mathcal{A}
to be the unit-
st
flow space, to match the algorithmic implementation in
[
Cor25
]
.

Next, we observe that state-reflection problems exhibit a remarkable amount of flexibility. This is shown in the following lemma.

Lemma 4.4
.

Let
R=\{(\left|\sigma_{x}^{\pm}\right\rangle,\pm\left|\sigma_{x}^{\pm}\right\rangle,O_{x})\}_{x\in\mathcal{D}}
be state-reflection problem. Let
D\in\mathcal{L}(\mathcal{V})
be an invertible operator,
\alpha_{\pm}\in\mathbb{C}
, and let
R^{\prime}:=\{(\alpha_{+}D\left|\sigma_{x}^{+}\right\rangle,\alpha_{-}(D^{-1})^{\dagger}\left|\sigma_{x}^{\pm}\right\rangle,O_{x})\}_{x\in\mathcal{D}}
. Then, any feasible solution
w:=\{\left|w_{x}^{\pm}\right\rangle\}_{x\in\mathcal{D}}
for a state-reflection problem
R
can be turned into a feasible solution
w^{\prime}:=\{\alpha_{+}\left|w_{x}^{+}\right\rangle,\alpha_{-}\left|w_{x}^{-}\right\rangle\}_{x\in\mathcal{D}}
for
R^{\prime}
, where
x^{-\dagger}:=(x^{-1})^{\dagger}
. Consequently,

\ADV(R)\leq\sqrt{\max_{x\in\mathcal{D}}\mathsf{R}_{x}^{+}(w)\cdot\max_{x\in\mathcal{D}}\mathsf{R}_{x}^{-}(w)}.

Proof.

For all
x,y\in\mathcal{D}
, and
s,t\in\{\pm\}
, we have

\left(\alpha_{s}\left|w_{x}^{s}\right\rangle\right)^{\dagger}(I-O_{x}^{\dagger}O_{y})\left(\alpha_{t}\left|w_{y}^{t}\right\rangle\right)=\alpha_{s}^{\dagger}\alpha_{t}\left(\left\langle\sigma_{x}^{s}\middle|\sigma_{y}^{t}\right\rangle-st\left\langle\sigma_{x}^{s}\middle|\sigma_{y}^{t}\right\rangle\right)=2\delta_{s\neq t}(\left\langle\sigma_{x}^{s}\right|\alpha_{s}^{\dagger})(\alpha_{t}\left|\sigma_{y}^{t}\right\rangle).

By symmetry, it suffices to consider
s=+
and
t=-
, and we verify that the right-hand side becomes

2(\left\langle\sigma_{x}^{+}\right|\alpha_{+}^{\dagger})D^{\dagger}(D^{-1})^{\dagger}(\alpha_{-}\left|\sigma_{y}^{-}\right\rangle)=2\left(\left\langle\sigma_{x}^{+}\right|D^{\dagger}\alpha_{+}^{\dagger}\right)\left(\alpha_{-}(D^{-1})^{\dagger}\left|\sigma_{y}^{-}\right\rangle\right).\qed

We remark here that it seems to be quite tricky to see what these rescalings do to the subspace
\mathcal{A}
from
theorem

4.3
. Understanding this seems to be fundamental for time-efficient implementations of the algorithms we consider in this work. We leave this for future work.

We end this section by observing that we can interpret the construction from Jeffery
[
Jef22
]
as a feasible solution to the state-reflection program.

Theorem 4.5

(
[
Jef22
, Corollary 3.7]
)
.

Let
\mathcal{A}
be a quantum Monte-Carlo algorithm that implements the state-conversion problem given by
P=\{(\left|\sigma_{x}\right\rangle,\left|\tau_{x}\right\rangle,O_{x})\}_{x\in\mathcal{D}}
, with run-time given by the random variable
T_{x}
, for every
x\in\mathcal{D}
. Then, for any choice of
(\alpha_{t})_{t\in\mathbb{N}_{0}}\subseteq\mathbb{R}_{>0}
, we can implement a feasible solution
W:=\{\left|w_{x}^{\pm}\right\rangle\}_{x\in\mathcal{D}}
to the corresponding state-reflection problem
R:=\{(\left|\sigma_{x}\right\rangle\pm\left|\tau_{x}\right\rangle,\pm(\left|\sigma_{x}\right\rangle\pm\left|\tau_{x}\right\rangle),\overline{O_{x}})\}_{x\in\mathcal{D}}
, with complexities

\mathsf{W}_{x}^{+}=\mathbb{E}\left[\sum_{t=0}^{T_{x}-1}\alpha_{t}\right],\qquad\text{and}\qquad\mathsf{W}_{x}^{-}=\mathbb{E}\left[\sum_{t=0}^{T_{x}-1}\frac{1}{\alpha_{t}}\right].

In particular, by choosing
\alpha_{t}=1
, for all
t\in\mathbb{N}_{0}
, we obtain
\mathsf{W}_{x}^{\pm}=\mathbb{E}[T_{x}]
, and by choosing
\alpha_{t}=t+1
, we obtain
\mathsf{W}_{x}^{+}=\mathbb{E}[T_{x}(T_{x}+1)/2]\in O(\mathbb{E}[T_{x}^{2}])
, and
\mathsf{W}_{x}^{-}\in O(1+\mathbb{E}[\log(T_{x})])
.

Proof.

Apply the principle of deferred measurement to
\mathcal{A}
. The resulting algorithm is now a sequence of
U_{0},O_{x},U_{1},\dots,U_{T}
, with
T\in\mathbb{N}
. For all
t\in[T]_{0}
, let
\left|\psi_{x,t}\right\rangle
be the state of the algorithm after
U_{t}
. At every time-step
t\in\mathbb{N}_{0}
, we let
\mathcal{H}_{t}\subseteq\mathcal{H}
be the subspace of the state space on which the control qubits for the application of
O_{x}
equal
1
. We define

\left|w_{x}^{\pm}\right\rangle:=\bigoplus_{t=0}^{T-1}\sqrt{\alpha_{t}^{\pm 1}}\frac{\Pi_{\mathcal{H}_{t}}\left|\psi_{x,t}\right\rangle\oplus\pm O_{x}\Pi_{\mathcal{H}_{t}}\left|\psi_{x,t}\right\rangle}{\sqrt{2}}.

Then, we obtain

\mathsf{W}_{x}^{\pm}=\left\|\left|w_{x}^{\pm}\right\rangle\right\|^{2}=\sum_{t=0}^{T-1}\alpha_{t}^{\pm 1}\left\|\Pi_{\mathcal{H}_{t}}\left|\psi_{x,t}\right\rangle\right\|^{2}\leq\sum_{t=0}^{T-1}\alpha_{t}^{\pm 1}\mathbb{P}\left[T_{x}>t\right]=\mathbb{E}\left[\sum_{t=0}^{T_{x}-1}\alpha_{t}^{\pm 1}\right],

and for all
x,y\in\mathcal{D}
,

\displaystyle 2\left\langle w_{x}^{+}\middle|w_{y}^{-}\right\rangle

\displaystyle=\sum_{t=0}^{T-1}\left[\left\langle\psi_{x,t}\right|\Pi_{\mathcal{H}_{t}}\left|\psi_{y,t}\right\rangle-\left\langle\psi_{x,t}\right|O_{x}^{\dagger}\Pi_{\mathcal{H}_{t}}O_{y}\left|\psi_{y,t}\right\rangle\right]=\sum_{t=0}^{T-1}\left[\left\langle\psi_{x,t}\middle|\psi_{y,t}\right\rangle-\left\langle\psi_{x,t}\right|O_{x}^{\dagger}O_{y}\left|\psi_{y,t}\right\rangle\right]

\displaystyle=\sum_{t=0}^{T-1}\left[\left\langle\psi_{x,t}\middle|\psi_{y,t}\right\rangle-\left\langle\psi_{x,t+1}\middle|\psi_{y,t+1}\right\rangle\right]=\left\langle\psi_{x,0}\middle|\psi_{y,0}\right\rangle-\left\langle\psi_{x,T}\middle|\psi_{y,T}\right\rangle=\left\langle\sigma_{x}\middle|\sigma_{y}\right\rangle-\left\langle\tau_{x}\middle|\tau_{y}\right\rangle.\qed

5
Generalized graph composition

In this section, we generalize the graph composition framework from
[
Cor25
]
to the state-conversion setting. We perform the composition on the level of state-reflection problems – these seem to be more amenable to being composed than their state-conversion problem counterparts.

5.1
Hyperedges

The core idea is to interpret certain state-reflection problems as hyperedges in a graph, i.e., edges connecting more than two vertices. We start by formally introducing which state-reflection problems can be interpreted in this way.

Definition 5.1

(Hyperedge problem)
.

Let
V
be a finite set, and
R=\{(\left|\sigma_{x}^{\pm}\right\rangle,\pm\left|\sigma_{x}^{\pm}\right\rangle,O_{x})\}_{x\in\mathcal{D}}
be a state-reflection problem over
\mathcal{V}=\mathbb{C}^{V}
. Suppose in addition that we can write for all
x\in\mathcal{D}
,

\left|\sigma_{x}^{+}\right\rangle:=\delta_{x}\in\mathbb{C}^{V},\qquad\text{and}\qquad\left|\sigma_{x}^{-}\right\rangle=U_{x}\in\mathbb{C}^{V},

such that

U_{x}\text{ is constant on }\supp(\delta_{x}),\qquad\text{and}\qquad\sum_{v\in V}(\delta_{x})_{v}=0.

Then, we say that
R
is a hyperedge problem denoted by
\{(\delta_{x},U_{x},O_{x})\}_{x\in\mathcal{D}}
, and we say that it implements the net-flow
\delta_{x}
and the potential function
U_{x}
through the hyperedge with vertices
V
.

It is important to note that not every state-reflection problem implements a hyperedge. Indeed, we will see that we need the additional constraints outlined in the above definition to carry out the compositions in the upcoming sections. Nevertheless, we will show that the above definition is still sufficiently generic to implement quantum walks. It would be interesting to figure out if there is a generic conversion from state-reflection problems into this form, but we leave that for future research.

One can interpret a state-reflection program
R
implementing a hyperedge on the vertices
V
pictorially as in
fig.

1.1
, by drawing parallels to the electrical network framework. The idea is that for every
x\in\mathcal{D}
there is a net-flow on the vertices in the support of
\delta_{x}
, and since we require the sum of the entries of
\delta_{x}
to vanish, the total flow is conserved. The vertices that have a non-zero net-flow
(\delta_{x})_{v}
, the potential function has to be the same, but there can be some potential function difference with the other vertices adjacent to the hyperedge.

We observe that one can freely add or subtract constants to the potential functions, analogously to the electric network framework. We formalize that in the following lemma statement.

Lemma 5.2
.

Let
R=\{(\delta_{x},U_{x},O_{x})\}_{x\in\mathcal{D}}
, and for all
x\in\mathcal{D}
, let
C_{x}\in\mathbb{C}
. Let
R^{\prime}=\{(\delta_{x},U_{x}+C_{x}\mathbbm{1}_{V},O_{x})\}_{x\in\mathcal{D}}
. Then, the feasible region of
R
equals the feasible region of
R^{\prime}
.

Proof.

We compare the difference of the Gram matrices of the input and output states to the state-reflection problem. For all
x,y\in\mathcal{D}
, we have

\left\langle\sigma_{x}^{\pm}\middle|\sigma_{y}^{\pm}\right\rangle-(\pm\left\langle\sigma_{x}^{\pm}\right|)(\pm\left|\sigma_{y}^{\pm}\right\rangle)=0,

for any state-reflection problem, so it remains to focus on the cross-talk between
+
and
-
states. To that end, let
\left|(\sigma_{x}^{\pm})_{C}\right\rangle
be the states in the state-reflection problem for
R^{\prime}
, and similarly
\left|\sigma_{x}^{\pm}\right\rangle
for
R
. We observe that

\left\langle(\sigma_{x}^{+})_{C}\middle|(\sigma_{y}^{-})_{C}\right\rangle=\delta_{x}^{T}(U_{y}+C_{y}\mathbbm{1}_{V})=\delta_{x}^{T}U_{x}+C_{y}\delta_{x}^{T}\mathbbm{1}_{V}=\delta_{x}^{T}U_{x}=\left\langle\sigma_{x}^{+}\middle|\sigma_{y}^{-}\right\rangle,

and so the feasible regions of
R^{\prime}
and
R
are identical.
∎

Next, we observe that span programs can be converted into state-reflection problems, that implement hyperedges with just two endpoints, which we will refer to as
s
and
t
. The span program implements a unit flow from
s
and
t
for positive instances, and a unit potential difference for negative instances. Importantly, this characterization is an equivalence, so interpreting span programs this way is not lossy, not even up to constant factors.

Theorem 5.3

(Hyperedge from a span program)
.

Let
f:\mathcal{D}\to\{0,1\}
. For all
x\in\mathcal{D}
, we define net-flow
\delta_{x}
and potential function
U_{x}
on
\{s,t\}
, as

\delta_{x}=\begin{cases}\mathbbm{1}_{s}-\mathbbm{1}_{t},&\text{if }f(x)=1,\\
0,&f(x)=0,\end{cases}\qquad\text{and}\qquad U_{x}=\begin{cases}0,&\text{if }f(x)=1,\\
-\mathbbm{1}_{t},&f(x)=0.\end{cases}

On the one hand, let
\mathcal{P}=(\mathcal{H},x\mapsto\mathcal{H}(x),\mathcal{K},\left|w_{0}\right\rangle)
be a span program on
\mathcal{D}
that computes
f
. Let
\{\left|w_{x}\right\rangle\}_{x\in\mathcal{D}}
be a set of witnesses for
\mathcal{P}
. We write

\left|w_{x}^{+}\right\rangle=\begin{cases}\left|w_{x}\right\rangle,&\text{if }f(x)=1,\\
0,&\text{if }f(x)=0,\end{cases}\qquad\text{and}\qquad\left|w_{x}^{-}\right\rangle=\begin{cases}0,&\text{if }f(x)=1,\\
\left|w_{x}\right\rangle,&\text{if }f(x)=0.\end{cases}

Then,
\{\left|w_{x}^{\pm}\right\rangle\}_{x\in\mathcal{D}}
is a feasible solution for the hyperedge problem
R=\{(\delta_{x},U_{x},2\Pi_{\mathcal{H}(x)}-I)\}_{x\in\mathcal{D}}
.

Conversely, let
\{\left|w_{x}^{\pm}\right\rangle\}_{x\in\mathcal{D}}
be a feasible solution to the hyperedge problem
R=\{(\delta_{x},U_{x},O_{x})\}_{x\in\mathcal{D}}
, where the oracles
O_{x}
act on
\mathcal{H}
. We let
\mathcal{H}(x)
be the
+1
-eigenspace of
O_{x}
. Then, there exists a span program
\mathcal{P}=(\mathcal{H},x\mapsto\mathcal{H}(x),\mathcal{K},\left|w_{0}\right\rangle)
that computes
f
, with

C(\mathcal{P})^{2}\leq\max\left\{\max_{x\in f^{-1}(1)}\left\|\left|w_{x}^{+}\right\rangle\right\|^{2},\max_{x\in f^{-1}(0)}\left\|\left|w_{x}^{-}\right\rangle\right\|^{2}\right\}.

Proof.

For the first claim, we write
O_{x}=2\Pi_{\mathcal{H}(x)}-I
. Since
\left|w_{x}\right\rangle\in\mathcal{H}(x)
if
f(x)=1
, and
\left|w_{x}\right\rangle\in\mathcal{H}(x)^{\perp}
otherwise, we immediately observe that
O_{x}\left|w_{x}^{\pm}\right\rangle=\pm\left|w_{x}^{\pm}\right\rangle
. As such, for all
x,y\in\mathcal{D}
, we have

\left\langle w_{x}^{\pm}\right|(I-O_{x}^{\dagger}O_{y})\left|w_{y}^{\pm}\right\rangle=\left\langle w_{x}^{\pm}\middle|w_{y}^{\pm}\right\rangle-\left\langle w_{x}^{\pm}\middle|w_{y}^{\pm}\right\rangle=0,

which equals
\left\langle\sigma_{x}^{\pm}\middle|\sigma_{y}^{\pm}\right\rangle-(\pm\left\langle\sigma_{x}^{\pm}\right|)(\pm\left|\sigma_{y}^{\pm}\right\rangle)=0
.

Thus, it remains to consider the cross-talk between
+
and
-
. To that end, observe for all
x,y\in\mathcal{D}
that

\left\langle\sigma_{x}^{+}\middle|\sigma_{y}^{-}\right\rangle-\left\langle\sigma_{x}^{+}\right|(-\left|\sigma_{y}^{-}\right\rangle)=2\left\langle\sigma_{x}^{+}\middle|\sigma_{y}^{-}\right\rangle=2U_{x}^{T}\delta_{y}=2\delta_{f(x)=0}\delta_{f(y)=1},

and on the other hand,

\left\langle w_{x}^{+}\right|(I-O_{x}O_{y})\left|w_{y}^{-}\right\rangle=2\left\langle w_{x}^{+}\middle|w_{y}^{-}\right\rangle=2\left\langle w_{x}\middle|w_{y}\right\rangle\delta_{f(x)=1}\delta_{f(y)=0},

It remains to prove that
\left\langle w_{x}\middle|w_{y}\right\rangle=1
whenever
f(x)=1
and
f(y)=0
. Indeed, in that case we can write
\left|w_{x}\right\rangle-\left|w_{0}\right\rangle\in\mathcal{K}
, and
\left|w_{y}\right\rangle\in\mathcal{K}^{\perp}
, and so

\left\langle w_{x}\middle|w_{y}\right\rangle=\left\langle w_{0}\middle|w_{y}\right\rangle+(\left\langle w_{x}\right|-\left\langle w_{0}\right|)\left|w_{y}\right\rangle=1-0=1.

This completes the proof for the first claim.

For the converse, let

\mathcal{A}=\Span\left\{\begin{bmatrix}\left|\sigma_{x}^{+}\right\rangle\\
\left|w_{x}^{+}\right\rangle\end{bmatrix}\right\}_{x\in\mathcal{D}}=\Span\left\{\begin{bmatrix}\left|\sigma_{x}^{+}\right\rangle\\
\left|w_{x}^{+}\right\rangle\end{bmatrix}\right\}_{x\in f^{-1}(1)}.

Now, since the first two entries of any vector in
\mathcal{A}
sum to
0
, we can decompose
\mathcal{A}
into two parts, i.e.,

\mathcal{A}=\Span\left\{\begin{bmatrix}\mathbbm{1}_{s}-\mathbbm{1}_{t}\\
\left|w_{0}\right\rangle\end{bmatrix}\right\}\oplus\mathcal{K},

where
\left|w_{0}\right\rangle\in\mathcal{K}^{\perp}
. We observe for every
x\in f^{-1}(1)
that
\left|w_{x}^{+}\right\rangle-\left|w_{0}\right\rangle\in\mathcal{K}
, and so
\left|w_{0}\right\rangle\in\mathcal{K}+\mathcal{H}(x)
. Similarly, for
x\in f^{-1}(0)
, we have
1-\left\langle w_{x}^{-}\middle|w_{0}\right\rangle=0
, and so the span program computes
f
, and the witnesses are the same as for the state-reflection problem.
∎

There are also other ways to generate hyperedges. We specifically remark here that function evaluations and database updates fit neatly in this context, as witnessed by the following theorem.

Theorem 5.4

(Hyperedge from database update and function evaluation)
.

Let
\mathcal{D}
be a finite domain, and consider a database update problem with input
\xi_{x}\in\Sigma_{I}
output
\eta_{x}\in\Sigma_{O}
and oracle
O_{x}
satisfying
O_{x}^{2}=I
, for all
x\in\mathcal{D}
. Then, the state-reflection problem
R=\{(\left|\xi_{x}\right\rangle-\left|\eta_{x}\right\rangle,\left|\xi_{x}\right\rangle+\left|\eta_{x}\right\rangle,O_{x})\}_{x\in\mathcal{D}}
implementing the database update problem is also a hyperedge problem.

Proof.

We verify that the properties of
definition

5.1
are satisfied. To that end, we write
\delta_{x}:=\mathbbm{1}_{\xi_{x}}-\mathbbm{1}_{\eta_{x}}
and
U_{x}:=\mathbbm{1}_{\xi_{x}}+\mathbbm{1}_{\eta_{x}}
, and we observe that the sum of
\delta_{x}
vanishes, and that
U_{x}
is constant on
\supp(\delta_{x})
.
∎

It seems in general harder to turn a general state-conversion problem into a hyperedge. However, there are some other special cases we can also handle, most notably when we know that the flow vectors are some restriction of a distribution to an arbitrary subset.

Theorem 5.5
.

Let
V
be a finite set, and let
\pi\in\Delta_{V}
. For all
x\in\mathcal{D}
in a finite domain
\mathcal{D}
, let
M_{x}\subseteq V
, such that
\mathbb{P}_{v\sim\pi}[v\in M_{x}]=\varepsilon
, for some fixed
\varepsilon\in(0,1)
. We write

\left|\psi_{x}\right\rangle=\frac{1}{\sqrt{\varepsilon}}\sum_{v\in M_{x}}\sqrt{\pi_{v}}\left|v\right\rangle,

and we define the state-reflection problems
R=\{(\left|\perp\right\rangle+\left|\psi_{x}\right\rangle,\left|\perp\right\rangle-\left|\psi_{x}\right\rangle,O_{x})\}_{x\in\mathcal{D}}
and
R^{\prime}=\{(\mathbbm{1}_{\perp}-\frac{\pi|_{M_{x}}}{\varepsilon},\mathbbm{1}_{\perp}+\mathbbm{1}_{M_{x}},O_{x})\}_{x\in\mathcal{D}}
. Then, the feasible regions for
R
and
R^{\prime}
are identical, and
R^{\prime}
is a hyperedge problem.

Proof.

We use
lemma

4.4
with a diagonal operator
D
that acts as
-\diag(\sqrt{\pi})/\sqrt{\varepsilon}
on
\mathbb{C}^{V}
, and as identity on
\left|\perp\right\rangle
, to argue that the feasible regions are identical. Next, checking that
R^{\prime}
is a hyperedge problem is done by directly checking the conditions from
definition

5.1
.
∎

5.2
Composition of hyperedges

The core idea of the generalized graph composition framework is to take a hypergraph
G=(V,E)
, where we divide the vertices into two groups, the boundary vertices
B\subseteq V
and the internal vertices
V\setminus B
. Now, we associate a hyperedge problem to each of the hyperedges, in such a way that the net flow vanishes at every internal node, and such that the potential functions are uniquely defined at all vertices. We formalize this notion in the following definition.

Definition 5.6

(Generalized graph composition)
.

Let
G=(V,E)
be a hypergraph, with boundary vertices
B\subseteq V
, and let
\mathcal{D}
be a finite domain. For every hyperedge
e\in E
, let
R^{e}=\{(\delta_{x}^{e},U_{x}^{e},O_{x}^{e})\}_{x\in\mathcal{D}}
be a hyperedge problem. For all
v\in V
and
x\in\mathcal{D}
, we write
\delta_{x}(v)=\sum_{e\in N(v)}\delta_{x}^{e}(v)
. Suppose that

1.

For all internal nodes
v\in V\setminus B
, the net-flow vanishes, i.e.,
\delta_{x}(v)=0
.

2.

The potential function is uniquely defined at every vertex
v\in V
, i.e.,
U_{x}^{e}(v)=U_{x}(v)
, for all
e\in N(v)
.

Then, we say that
(G,B,(R^{e})_{e\in E})
is an instance of the generalized graph composition framework, that implements the hyperedge problem
R=\{(\delta_{x},U_{x},\bigoplus_{e\in E}O_{x}^{e})\}_{x\in\mathcal{D}}
on vertex set
B
.

The framework now describes how we can combine feasible solutions for the individual hyperedge problems into a feasible solution for the hyperedge problem on the boundary vertices. This is the objective of the following theorem.

Theorem 5.7

(Composition of hyperedges)
.

Let
(G=(V,E),B,(R^{e})_{e\in E})
be an instance of the generalized graph composition, implementing the hyperedge problem
R
. For all
e\in E
, suppose that
w^{e}=\{\left|(w^{e})_{x}^{\pm}\right\rangle\}_{x\in\mathcal{D}}
is a feasible solution to
R^{e}
. For all
x\in\mathcal{D}
, let

\left|w_{x}^{\pm}\right\rangle:=\bigoplus_{e\in E}\left|(w^{e})_{x}^{\pm}\right\rangle.

Then,
w=\{\left|w_{x}^{\pm}\right\rangle\}_{x\in\mathcal{D}}
forms a feasible solution to
R
, with
\mathsf{R}_{x}^{\pm}(w)=\sum_{e\in E}\mathsf{R}_{x}^{\pm}(w^{e})
, for all
x\in\mathcal{D}
.

Proof.

We check that the witnesses indeed form a feasible solution. To that end, recall that we only have to check the cross-talk between
+
and
-
states for state-reflection problems. So, let
x,y\in\mathcal{D}
, and we observe that

\displaystyle(\delta_{x})|_{B}^{T}(U_{y})|_{B}

\displaystyle=\delta_{x}^{T}U_{y}=\sum_{v\in V}\delta_{x}(v)U_{y}(v)=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}e\in E\\
v\in N(e)\end{subarray}}\delta_{x}^{e}(v)U_{y}^{e}(v)=\sum_{e\in E}(\delta_{x}^{e})^{T}U_{y}^{e}

\displaystyle=\sum_{e\in E}\left\langle(w_{x}^{+})^{e}\right|(I-(O_{x}^{e})^{\dagger}O_{y}^{e})\left|(w_{y}^{-})^{e}\right\rangle=\left\langle w_{x}^{+}\right|(I-O_{x}^{\dagger}O_{y})\left|w_{y}^{-}\right\rangle,

where we wrote
O_{x}=\bigoplus_{e\in E}O_{x}^{e}
, for all
x\in\mathcal{D}
.
∎

We proceed by showing how this subsumes the “simple” graph composition framework, i.e., the construction from

Theorem 5.9

6
Graph composition for non-boolean decision trees

In this section, we discuss how decision-trees can be turned into quantum algorithms. To that end, suppose that we have a decision tree that computes a function
f:\mathcal{D}\to\Sigma_{O}
, where
\mathcal{D}\subseteq\Sigma_{I}^{n}
for some input and output alphabets
\Sigma_{I}
and
\Sigma_{O}
.

The first result in this direction was obtained by Lin and Lin
[
LL16
]
, who considered the setting where
\Sigma_{I}=\Sigma_{O}=\{0,1\}
. They showed that a decision tree with depth
T
can be turned into a quantum query algorithm, where the number of queries depends on the structure of the tree. Specifically, color the edges of the decision tree in red and black, such that every node has at most one outgoing black edge. Let
G
be the maximum number of red edges along a path from the root to a leaf. Then, the total number of queries is
O(\sqrt{GT})
. This result was later generalized by
[
BT20
]
to the setting where
\Sigma_{I}
and
\Sigma_{O}
are arbitrary.

Next, this question was revisited in
[
CMP25
]
. They showed that in the case where
\Sigma_{I}=\{0,1\}
, one can assign a weighting scheme on the edges, that generalizes the coloring scheme from the earlier results. After that, it was shown in
[
Cor25
]
how to recover this result, in the case where
\Sigma_{I}=\Sigma_{O}=\{0,1\}
in from the graph composition framework. In both of these works, the question is left open whether such a weighting scheme can be imposed on decision trees with
\Sigma_{I}\neq\{0,1\}
.

Here, we employ the generalized graph composition framework to convert decision trees with non-binary inputs and outputs into quantum algorithms. We observe that this naturally gives rise to a weighting scheme, where the optimal assignment can be obtained by recursively solving a simple semi-definite program at every vertex. We also show that this can be used to recover the results from
[
BT20
,
CMP25
]
.

We start by defining a weighting scheme on decision trees.

Definition 6.1
.

Let
T=(V,E)
be a decision tree with root node
r\in V
, and
w:V\to\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}
. We say that
w
is a weighting scheme for
T
, if:

1.

w_{v}=0
for all leafs
v
.

2.

For any internal node
v
with children
C_{v}\subseteq V
, we have matrices
0\preceq X,Y\in\mathbb{C}^{C_{v}\times C_{v}}
such that
X[c_{1},c_{2}]-Y[c_{1},c_{2}]=1
, for all
c_{1},c_{2}\in C_{V}
such that
c_{1}\neq c_{2}
, and
w_{v}-w_{c}\geq X[c,c]+Y[c,c]
for all
c\in C_{v}
.

We say that
w_{r}
is the weight of the tree, and we write

\mathsf{WDT}(T)=\min_{w\text{ weighting scheme for }T}w_{r}.

We start by showing that these weighting schemes provide upper bounds on the query complexity of the function being computed by the decision tree.

Theorem 6.2
.

Let
T=(V,E)
be a decision tree with root node
r\in V
and weighting scheme
w
, computing a function
f:\Sigma_{I}^{n}\supseteq\mathcal{D}\to\Sigma_{O}
. Then, we can compute
f
with bounded error using
O(w_{r})
queries. Consequently,
\mathsf{Q}(f)\in O(\mathsf{WDT}(f))
.

Proof.

We use the generalized graph composition framework. To that end, we let
G=(V,E^{\prime})
be a hypergraph, such that for every internal node
v\in V
of the decision tree, with children
C_{v}
, we let
\{v\}\cup C_{v}
be a hyperedge in
G
. Now, we associate the function-evaluation hyperedge problem to it, that on an input
x
whose path from root to leaf reaches
v
, sends flow
1
from the parent node to its child that matches the query outcome, and assigns potential function
1
to these two nodes, and
0
everywhere else.

We now present a feasible solution to the hyperedge problem associated to the hyperedge with parent node
v
and children
C_{v}
. As
X
and
Y
are PSD, we can factorize
X=U_{+}^{\dagger}U
and
Y=U_{-}^{\dagger}U
, where for every
c\in C_{v}
we write
\left|u_{c}^{+}\right\rangle
and
\left|u_{c}^{-}\right\rangle
for the
c
th column in
U_{+}
and
U_{-}
, respectively. Now, to every input
x\in\mathcal{D}
that passes through node
v
on the path from root to leaf in
T
, we associate the vectors
\left|w_{x}^{\pm}\right\rangle=(\left|u^{\pm}_{x_{v}}\right\rangle\oplus\pm\left|u^{\mp}_{x_{v}}\right\rangle)\otimes(\left|\perp\right\rangle\pm\left|x_{v}\right\rangle)
, and we set
\left|w_{x}^{\pm}\right\rangle=0
otherwise. For all
x,y\in\mathcal{D}
whose paths pass through
v
, we let
x_{v}
and
y_{v}
be the value queried at node
v
, and observe that

\left\langle w_{x}^{+}\middle|w_{y}^{-}\right\rangle=\left(\left\langle u_{x_{v}}^{+}\middle|u^{-}_{y_{v}}\right\rangle-\left\langle u_{x_{v}}^{-}\middle|u^{+}_{y_{v}}\right\rangle\right)\cdot(1-\left\langle x_{v}\middle|y_{v}\right\rangle)=(X[x_{v},y_{v}]-Y[x_{v},y_{v}])\cdot\mathbbm{1}_{x_{v}\neq y_{v}}=\mathbbm{1}_{x_{v}\neq y_{v}}=\delta_{x}^{T}U_{y},

and in all other cases, the inner product is
0
.‘

Now, we combine all the feasible solutions using
theorem

5.7
, with all weights equal to
1
. The combined flow becomes simply from the root node of the graph to the leaf computed by the decision tree, and the potential function is
1
on these two vertices and
0
everywhere else. This is the function-evaluation state-reflection problem, so it remains to compute the witness sizes. To that end, for any input
x\in\mathcal{D}
, let
P_{x}
be the hyperedges that are traversed upon evaluating
x
, and
p(e)
the corresponding parent nodes. We find that

\displaystyle\mathsf{R}_{x}^{\pm}(w)

\displaystyle=\sum_{e\in P_{x}}(\mathsf{R}_{e})_{x}^{\pm}=\sum_{e\in P_{x}}\left\|\left|(w_{e})_{x}^{\pm}\right\rangle\right\|^{2}=2\sum_{e\in P_{x}}\left(\left\|\left|(u^{e})^{+}_{x_{p(e)}}\right\rangle\right\|^{2}+\left\|\left|(u^{e})^{-}_{x_{p(e)}}\right\rangle\right\|^{2}\right)

\displaystyle=2\sum_{e\in P_{x}}(X^{e}[x_{p(e)},x_{p(e)}]+Y^{e}[x_{p(e)},x_{p(e)}])\leq 2\sum_{e\in P_{x}}(w_{p(e)}-w_{x_{p(e)}})=2w_{r}=2\mathsf{WDT}(T),

and so the total complexity is upper bounded by
2\mathsf{WDT}(T)
.
∎

Next, we explain how the optimal weighting scheme can be recursively computed.

Theorem 6.3
.

Let
T=(V,E)
be a decision tree with root node
r\in V
. Its optimal weighting scheme is computed by setting all the weight of all the leaf nodes to
0
, and then for every internal node
v\in V
with children
C_{v}\subseteq V
, recursively computing the semi-definite program

\displaystyle w_{v}:=\min\quad

\displaystyle\max_{c\in C_{v}}(w_{c}+X[c,c]+Y[c,c])

s.t.

\displaystyle X[c_{1},c_{2}]-Y[c_{1},c_{2}]=1,

\displaystyle\text{if }c_{1},c_{2}\in C_{v},c_{1}\neq c_{2},

\displaystyle 0\preceq X,Y\in\mathbb{R}^{C_{v}\times C_{v}}.

Proof.

We immediately observe that the weighting scheme generated in this way is feasible for
T
, since the constraints of the SDP are the same as those in
definition

6.1
. Similarly, every valid weighting scheme for
T
indeed generates feasible solutions to all the SDPs. Finally, we observe that the objective function is monotone in the values of
w_{c}
, and so the value of
w_{r}
is optimized if and only if all of the intermediate SDPs are optimized.
∎

For future reference we also provide the dual of the SDP from
theorem

6.3
. For all internal nodes
v\in V
with children
C_{v}\subseteq V
, we have

\displaystyle w_{v}:=\max\quad

\displaystyle\left\|\diag(\{w_{c}:c\in C_{v}\})+\Gamma\right\|,

s.t.

\displaystyle\left\|\Gamma\right\|\leq 1,

\displaystyle\Gamma[c,c]=0,

\displaystyle\forall c\in C_{v},

\displaystyle\Gamma\in\mathbb{R}^{C_{v}\times C_{v}}.

We can now obtain the result from
[
BT20
]
by exhibiting an explicit weighting scheme. This is the objective of the following theorem.

Theorem 6.4
.

For every decision tree
T=(V,E)
, we have
\mathsf{WDT}(T)\leq 3\sqrt{GT}
.

Proof.

We exhibit a weighting scheme. We set the weights such that for every black edge
vv^{\prime}
, we have
w_{v}-w_{v^{\prime}}\geq\sqrt{G/T}
, and similarly for every red edge
w_{v}-w_{v^{\prime}}\geq 2\sqrt{T/G}-1
. We can indeed do this in a way that ensures
w_{r}\leq 3\sqrt{GT}
. It remains to prove that such a weighting scheme is feasible. To that end, let
k\in\mathbb{N}
be the number of outcomes of the query. We let
\alpha=\sqrt{T/G}\geq 1
,
v=[1/\sqrt{\alpha}]+[\sqrt{\alpha}]^{k-1}
, and

X:=vv^{T}\succeq 0,\qquad\text{and}\qquad Y:=\begin{bmatrix}0&([0]^{k-1})^{T}\\
[0]^{k-1}&(\alpha-1)J_{k-1\times k-1}\end{bmatrix}\succeq 0.

We verify for all
c,c^{\prime}\in C
with
c\neq c^{\prime}
satisfies
X[c,c^{\prime}]-Y[c,c^{\prime}]=1
,
X[1,1]+Y[1,1]=1/\alpha
, and
X[c,c]+Y[c,c]=2\alpha-1
.
∎

Finally, we recover the results from
[
CMP25
]
, by observing that in the case where the query has just two outcomes, the corresponding SDP can be solved analytically.

Theorem 6.5
.

For a decision tree with binary queries, the measure
\mathsf{WDT}(T)
introduced in
definition

6.1
equals the optimum of
[
CMP25
, Definition 1.5]
.

Proof.

Let
v\in V
be an internal node, with weights
w_{v}
and
w_{v^{\prime}}
on its children. Then, the optimal choice for the
\Gamma
-matrix in the maximization version of the SDP is the Pauli-
X
matrix, from which we observe that the optimal choice for
w_{v}
is

w_{v}=\left\|\begin{bmatrix}w_{v}&1\\
1&w_{v^{\prime}}\end{bmatrix}\right\|=\frac{w_{v}+w_{v^{\prime}}+\sqrt{(w_{v}-w_{v^{\prime}})^{2}+4}}{2}.

This recovers
[
CMP25
, Theorem 5.4]
.
∎

For the non-Boolean case, finding an explicit analytical solution for this optimization program seems elusive. We do remark, however, that these SDPs are very small (only of the size of the input alphabet), and that the total number of SDPs to solve is the same as the size of the tree. Thus, for input alphabets of constant size, computing the optimal weighting schemes can be done in time essentially linear in the size of the decision tree.

Graph composition for quantum divide and conquer

In this section, we relate our construction to the results from
[
CKK+25
]
. In
[
Cor25
]
, it is already shown how one can recover Strategy 1 from this paper using the regular graph composition framework. here, we show how Strategy 2 can be obtained from the generalized graph composition framework.

Theorem 7.1
.

Let
f^{\mathrm{aux}}:\Sigma^{n}\to\Lambda
be an auxiliary function, and for all
s\in\Lambda
and
i\in A
with
A
a finite set, let
f_{i}^{(s)}:\Sigma^{n}\to\{0,1\}
and let
g^{(s)}:\{0,1\}^{\Lambda\times A}\to\{0,1\}
. Then, for any
x\in\Sigma^{n}
, we can compute
h^{f^{\mathrm{aux}}(x)}(x):=g^{(f^{\mathrm{aux}}(x))}((f_{i}^{(f^{\mathrm{aux}}(x))}(x))_{i\in A})
, with a feasible solution that satisfies

\mathsf{R}_{x}^{+}(w)\leq(\mathsf{R}^{\mathrm{aux}})_{x}^{+}+(\mathsf{R}^{f^{\mathrm{aux}}(x)})_{x}^{+},\qquad\text{and}\qquad\mathsf{R}_{x}^{-}(w)=(\mathsf{R}^{\mathrm{aux}})_{x}^{-}+(\mathsf{R}^{f^{\mathrm{aux}}(x)})_{x}^{-}.

Proof.

The idea is to simply first perform the auxiliary hyperedge, and then attach all the hyperedges computing
h^{(s)}
to the corresponding output nodes. The resulting witness analysis then follows from
theorem

5.7
, by sending unit flow through both the hyperedges, and the potential function is simply
0
for all the nodes outside of this flow.
∎

The construction sketched here allows for more freedom than the one outlined in
[
CKK+25
]
. We can even take a feasible solution to the SDP from the previous section, and multiply it with our feasible solution element-wise, to better balance the costs of the difference branches of computation, dependent on
s\in\Lambda
. We leave investigating the potential of this approach for future research.

8
Graph composition for quantum walk search

In this section, we show how we can use the generalized graph composition framework to implement quantum walks. The idea of quantum walks originated in
[
Sze04
]
, in what became later known as the “hitting time framework”. This idea was later built upon by several works, most notably
[
MNRS11
]
,
[
Bel13
]
, and
[
DH17
]
, and these results were subsequently unified by
[
AGJ21
]
. Subsequently, Jeffery showed how some of these results can be amortized
[
Jef22
]
, and we build on those ideas here.

8.1
Construction

The idea of quantum walk search is as follows. Suppose we have a connected undirected graph
G=(V,E)
, with a resistance function on the edges
r:E\to\mathbb{R}_{>0}
. By
theorem

2.7
, this defines an irreducible, reversible Markov process
M=(V,P)
.

Now, let
\mathcal{D}
be a finite domain, and for all
x\in\mathcal{D}
, let
M_{x}\subseteq V
be the set of
marked
vertices. The goal is to
decide
whether
M_{x}\neq\varnothing
, or to
find
an element
v\in M_{x}
, if
M_{x}
is non-empty. We refer to these two problems as the
detection
and
finding
version of quantum walk search.

Intuitively, we can think of an algorithm solving the detection and finding version of quantum walk search as follows. We select a vertex
v\in V
according to some input distribution
\sigma\in\Delta_{V}
. Next, we randomly walk on the graph
G
according to the dynamics prescribed by the random walk
M
, and once every so often, we check if the vertex
v\in V
that we visit is contained in the set
M_{x}
. If it is, we have successfully solved both the detection and finding version of the problem, and if we can prove an upper bound on the time it takes to find such a marked vertex, if it exists, then we can also use this idea to verify that the set of marked vertices is empty, with some reasonable probability.

In order to aid the step where we are checking whether
v\in M_{x}
, it can sometimes be beneficial to store some information about
x
as we are walking on the vertices in
G
. To that end, we define a database with possible states
\mathcal{S}
, and we associate to every vertex
v\in V
and input
x\in\mathcal{D}
, a database entry
D_{v,x}\in\mathcal{S}
. The idea is to compute the database entry exactly in the initial sampling step, and then to update the database entry at every step of the random walk. We can then use the database entry
D_{v,x}
to check whether
v\in M_{x}
, or not.

In order to implement this approach quantumly, we need to be able to perform three operations as subroutines. That is, we will require to have hyperedges that implement the following three operations:

1.

For all
v\in V
, we need a
setup routine

S_{v}
that computes the database entry
D_{v,x}
, for all
x\in\mathcal{D}
.

2.

For all
vw=e\in E
, we need an
update routine
that updates the database entry
D_{v,x}
to
D_{w,x}
, for all
x\in\mathcal{D}
.

3.

For all
v\in V
and
D\in\mathcal{S}
, we need a
checking routine
that checks whether
v\in M_{x}
, for all
\in\mathcal{D}
, if
D=D_{v,x}
.

We bundle all these objects into a single
instance of quantum walk search
, and we formally define it below.

Definition 8.1

(Quantum walk search)
.

Let
G=(V,E)
be a connected, undirected graph, with resistances
r:E\to\mathbb{R}_{>0}
, such that
\sum_{e\in E}r_{e}^{-1}=1/2
. Let
\mathcal{D}
be a finite domain, and for all
x\in\mathcal{D}
, let
M_{x}\subseteq V
. Let
\mathcal{S}
be a set of database entries, and for all
v\in V
and
x\in\mathcal{D}
, let
D_{v,x}\in\mathcal{S}
. Now, assume we have feasible solutions implementing the following operations:

1.

For all
v\in V
, let
S_{v}
be a hyperedge that computes
D_{v,x}
, for all
x\in\mathcal{D}
. We write the positive and negative witness sizes as
(\mathsf{S}_{v})_{x}^{\pm}
.

2.

For all
vw=e\in E
, let
\mathsf{U}_{e}
be a hyperedge that updates
D_{v,x}
to
D_{w,x}
, for all
\in\mathcal{D}
. We write the positive and negative witness sizes as
(\mathsf{U}_{e})_{x}^{\pm}
.

3.

For all
v\in V
and
D\in\mathcal{S}
, let
C_{v,D}
be a span program that checks whether
v\in M_{x}
, for all
x\in\mathcal{D}
, whenever
D=D_{v,x}
. We write the positive and negative witness sizes as
(\mathsf{C}_{v,D})_{x}^{\pm}
.

Then, we say that
(G,r,x\mapsto M_{x},(v,x)\mapsto D_{v,x},\{S_{v}\}_{v\in V},\{U_{e}\}_{e\in E},\{C_{v,D}\}_{v\in V,D\in\mathcal{S}})
is an instance of quantum walk search.

We note that it is okay if
C_{v,D}
errs if
D\neq D_{v,x}
, i.e., if the database entry
D
is not the entry associated with the input
x
at vertex
v
, then it is okay that the checking routine fails. The construction of quantum walk search will make sure that this doesn’t impact the algorithm’s correctness.

We now show how we can solve the detection and finding versions of quantum random walk search, using these subroutines as hyperedges. We start with the detection version of the problem.

Theorem 8.2

(Graph composition for quantum walk search – detection version)
.

Let
(G,r,x\mapsto M_{x},(v,x)\mapsto D_{v,x},\{S_{v}\}_{v\in V},\{U_{e}\}_{e\in E},\{C_{v,D}\}_{v\in V,D\in\mathcal{S}})
be an instance of quantum walk search. Let
\sigma,\tau\in\Delta_{V}
. We write
\mathcal{D}^{+}=\{x\in\mathcal{D}:M_{x}\neq\varnothing\}
, and
\mathcal{D}^{-}=\mathcal{D}\setminus\mathcal{D}^{+}
. For all
x\in\mathcal{D}^{+}
, let
\mu_{x}\in\Delta_{\supp(\sigma)}
and
\nu_{x}\in\Delta_{M_{x}\cap\supp(\tau)}
. Then, there is a span program
\mathcal{P}
, that solves the detection version of quantum walk search, with witness sizes

\displaystyle w_{+}(x,\mathcal{P})

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{v\in\supp(\sigma)}\frac{(\mu_{x})_{v}^{2}}{\sigma_{v}}(\mathsf{S}_{v})_{x}^{+}+R_{\mathrm{eff}}(G,r\circ(\mathsf{U}_{\cdot})_{x}^{+};\mu_{x}-\nu_{x})+\sum_{v\in\supp(\tau)\cap M_{x}}\frac{(\nu_{x})_{v}^{2}}{\tau_{v}}(\mathsf{C}_{v,D_{v,x}})_{x}^{+},

\displaystyle\forall x\in\mathcal{D}^{+},

\displaystyle w_{-}(x,\mathcal{P})

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{v\in\supp(\sigma)}\sigma_{v}(\mathsf{S}_{v})_{x}^{-}+\sum_{e\in E}\frac{(\mathsf{U}_{e})_{x}^{-}}{r_{e}}+\sum_{v\in\supp(\tau)}\tau_{v}(\mathsf{C}_{v,D_{v,x}})_{x}^{-},

\displaystyle\forall x\in\mathcal{D}^{-}.

Proof.

We perform a generalized graph composition, with the following graph structure. For all
v\in V
, we define
V_{v}=\{(v,D):D\in\mathcal{S}\}
, and we use the new vertex set

V^{\prime}=\{s,t\}\cup\bigcup_{v\in V}V_{v},

with boundary vertices
B=\{s,t\}
. For all
v\in\supp(\sigma)
, we embed the hyperedge
S_{v}
between vertices
\{s\}\cup V_{v}
with weight
1/\sigma_{v}
, and for all
vw=e\in E
, we embed the hyperedge
U_{e}
between vertices
V_{v}\cup V_{w}
with weight
r_{e}
. Finally, for all
v\in\supp(\tau)
and
D\in\mathcal{S}
, we convert
C_{v,D}
into a hyperedge using
theorem

5.3
, and we embed it between vertices
\{(v,D)\}\cup\{t\}
with weight
1/\tau_{v}
.

We now construct a flow from
s
to
t
for
x\in\mathcal{D}^{+}
. To that end, we send flow
(\mu_{x})_{v}
through
S_{v}
, and
(\nu_{x})_{v}
through
C_{v,D_{v,x}}
. Thus, in order to satisfy flow conservation, we have to generate a net-flow of
\mu_{x}-\nu_{v}
through the update edges. We choose the minimum-energy flow through the graph
G
, with the resistances computed by
e\mapsto r_{e}\cdot(\mathsf{U}_{e})_{x}^{+}
. The corresponding positive witness complexity, hence, becomes

\mathsf{R}_{x}^{+}:=\sum_{v\in\supp(\sigma)}\frac{(\mu_{x})_{v}^{2}}{\sigma_{v}}(\mathsf{S}_{v})_{x}^{+}+R_{\mathrm{eff}}(G,r\circ(\mathsf{U}_{\cdot})_{x}^{+};\mu_{x}-\nu_{x})+\sum_{v\in\supp(\tau)}\frac{(\nu_{v})_{x}^{2}}{\tau_{v}}(\mathsf{C}_{v,D_{v,x}})_{x}^{+}.

Next, we construct a unit potential function
U_{x}
for
x\in\mathcal{D}^{-}
, which is
1
at
s
, and
0
at
t
. We assign a potential of
1
at every vertex in
V^{\prime}
that can be reached from
s
, and a potential of
0
at all the others. Then, all the nodes of the form
(v,D_{v,x})
and
s
have potential function
1
, and all the others have
0
. Thus, all the setup and update hyperedges contribute to the negative witness complexity, and all the checking routines of the form
C_{v,D_{v,x}}
contribute as well. This yields the negative witness complexity

\mathsf{R}_{x}^{-}:=\sum_{v\in\supp(\sigma)}\sigma_{v}(\mathsf{S}_{v})_{x}^{-}+\sum_{e\in E}\frac{(\mathsf{U}_{e})_{x}^{-}}{r_{e}}+\sum_{v\in\supp(\tau)}\tau_{v}(\mathsf{C}_{v,D_{v,x}})_{x}^{-}.

Now, we know from
theorem

5.3
that we can turn the resulting hyperedge back into a span program, and since we have exhibited a feasible solution with witness complexities
\mathsf{R}_{x}^{\pm}
, we can upper bound its witness sizes accordingly, by taking the maximum of
x\in\mathcal{D}^{\pm}
, respectively.
∎

We can generalize this result to the finding case as well. The result carries over immediately when we have the promise that
|M_{x}|=1
.

Theorem 8.3

(Graph composition for quantum walk search – finding version for unique marked element)
.

Let
(G,r,x\mapsto M_{x},(v,x)\mapsto D_{v,x},\{S_{v}\}_{v\in V},\{U_{e}\}_{e\in E},\{C_{v,D}\}_{v\in V,D\in\mathcal{S}})
be an instance of quantum walk search, with the promise that for all
x\in\mathcal{D}
,
M_{x}=\{m_{x}\}
. Let
\sigma,\tau\in\Delta_{V}
. For all
x\in\mathcal{D}
, let
\mu_{x}\in\Delta_{\supp(\sigma)}
. Then, we can find the marked vertex with complexities

\displaystyle\mathsf{R}_{x}^{+}

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{v\in\supp(\sigma)}\frac{(\mu_{x})_{v}^{2}}{\sigma_{v}}(\mathsf{S}_{v})_{x}^{+}+R_{\mathrm{eff}}(G,r\circ(\mathsf{U}_{\cdot})_{x}^{+};\mu_{x}-\nu_{x})+\frac{1}{\tau_{m_{x}}}(\mathsf{C}_{m_{x},D_{m_{x},x}})_{x}^{+},

\displaystyle\forall x\in\mathcal{D},

\displaystyle\mathsf{R}_{x}^{-}

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{v\in\supp(\sigma)}\sigma_{v}(\mathsf{S}_{v})_{x}^{-}+\sum_{e\in E}\frac{(\mathsf{U}_{e})_{x}^{-}}{r_{e}}+\sum_{v\in\supp(\tau)\setminus\{m_{x}\}}\tau_{v}(\mathsf{C}_{v,D_{v,x}})_{x}^{-},

\displaystyle\forall x\in\mathcal{D}.

Proof.

This follows from
theorem

5.9
. We use the same flow as in the detection case to upper bound the effective resistance, and we use the cut that cuts everything that can be reached from the root node from all the others. Since we are promised that there is a unique marked element, the resulting flow ends up connecting two vertices, which we can turn back into a state-reflection problem with unit-norm states using
theorem

5.4
in reverse.
∎

We also show how we can solve the finding problem in we know the fraction of marked vertices.

Theorem 8.4

(Graph composition for quantum walk search – finding version for known fraction of marked vertices)
.

Let
(G,r,x\mapsto M_{x},(v,x)\mapsto D_{v,x},\{S_{v}\}_{v\in V},\{U_{e}\}_{e\in E},\{C_{v,D}\}_{v\in V,D\in\mathcal{S}})
be an instance of quantum walk search. Let
\sigma,\tau\in\Delta_{V}
, and suppose that for all
x\in\mathcal{D}
, we have
\mathbb{P}_{v\sim\tau}[v\in M_{x}]=\varepsilon
for some fixed
\varepsilon>0
. For all
x\in\mathcal{D}
, let
\mu_{x}\in\Delta_{\supp(\sigma)}
, and
\nu_{x}=(\tau|_{M_{x}})/\varepsilon
. Then, we can find the marked vertex with complexities

\displaystyle\mathrm{R}_{x}^{+}

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{v\in\supp(\sigma)}\frac{(\mu_{x})_{v}^{2}}{\sigma_{v}}(\mathsf{S}_{v})_{x}^{+}+R_{\mathrm{eff}}(G,r\circ(\mathsf{U}_{\cdot})_{x}^{+};\mu_{x}-\nu_{x})+\frac{1}{\varepsilon^{2}}\sum_{v\in\supp(\tau)\cap M_{x}}\tau_{v}(\mathsf{C}_{v,D_{v,x}})_{x}^{+},

\displaystyle\forall x\in\mathcal{D},

\displaystyle\mathrm{R}_{x}^{-}

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{v\in\supp(\sigma)}\sigma_{v}(\mathsf{S}_{v})_{x}^{-}+\sum_{e\in E}\frac{(\mathsf{U}_{e})_{x}^{-}}{r_{e}}+\sum_{v\in\supp(\tau)\setminus M_{x}}\tau_{v}(\mathsf{C}_{v,D_{v,x}})_{x}^{-},

\displaystyle\forall x\in\mathcal{D}.

Proof.

The idea is to use the same flow as in the detection version for the positive witness analysis. For the negative witness analysis, we use the same idea as in the previous theorem, i.e., we let the potential function be
1
for all the vertices that we can reach from
s
, and
0
everywhere else. Now, we end up with the same situation as in
theorem

5.5
, and so we can again multiply with a diagonal to obtain a state-reflection problem with unit-norm states.
∎

It is at the moment not clear how this can be generalized to the setting where the fraction of marked vertices is unknown. We leave resolving this case for future research.

8.2
Relation to unified quantum walk search
[
AGJ21
]

We now relate our results to those obtained by Apers, Gilyén and Jeffery
[
AGJ21
]
. We first of all show that w.r.t. the query complexity, one can remove all logarithmic factors from the complexity statement.

Theorem 8.5
.

Let
(G,r,x\mapsto M_{x},(v,x)\mapsto D_{v,x},\{S_{v}\}_{v\in V},\{U_{e}\}_{e\in E},\{C_{v,D}\}_{v\in V,D\in\mathcal{S}})
be an instance of quantum walk search on
\mathcal{D}
, and let
\sigma\in\Delta_{V}
. We write

\mathsf{S}^{\pm}:=\max_{v\in D}\max_{x\in\mathcal{D}}(\mathsf{S}_{v})_{x}^{\pm},\qquad\mathsf{U}^{\pm}:=\max_{e\in E}\max_{x\in\mathcal{D}}(\mathsf{U}_{e})_{x}^{\pm},\qquad\text{and}\qquad\mathsf{C}^{\pm}:=\max_{v\in V}\max_{x\in\mathcal{D}}(\mathsf{C}_{v,D_{v,x}})_{x}^{\pm},

and we let
\mathsf{M}:=\sqrt{\mathsf{M}^{+}\mathsf{M}^{-}}
, with
\mathsf{M}\in\{\mathsf{S},\mathsf{U},\mathsf{C}\}
. Let
t\geq 1
, and let
R\geq 0
such that for all
x\in\mathcal{D}_{+}
, we have
R_{\mathrm{eff}}(P^{t},\sigma\leftrightarrow M_{x})\leq R
. Then, we can solve the detection version of the quantum walk search problem with a span program
\mathcal{P}
with complexity

C(\mathcal{P})\in O\left(\mathsf{S}+\sqrt{tR}\mathsf{U}+\sqrt{1+R}\mathsf{C}\right).

Proof.

First, we rescale all the hyperedges using
lemma

4.4
, such that we can without loss of generality assume that
\mathsf{M}^{-}=1
and
\mathsf{M}^{+}=\mathsf{M}^{2}
, for all
\mathsf{M}\in\{\mathsf{S},\mathsf{U},\mathsf{C}\}
.

Now, we use
theorem

8.2
to construct a generalized graph composition for the quantum walk search problem. We choose
\sigma\in\Delta_{V}
, and we let
\tau=(\sigma+\pi)/2\in\Delta_{V}
7
7

7

The idea to take the average between the stationary distribution and the target distribution was already used before in
[
Ben20
]
. Here, we rehash that idea for a different purpose: we want the routine to detect a marked vertex if the initial sample already finds it with high probability. Adding
\sigma_{v}
in the denominator prevents the effective resistance from blowing up in that case.
. Observe from
theorem

2.3
that
\tau
has full support. For all
x\in\mathcal{D}
for which
M_{x}\neq\varnothing
, we define
\mu_{x}=\sigma_{x}
, and we let
\nu_{x}
be the net-flow with support in
M_{x}
that minimizes the effective resistance between
\mu_{x}
and
\nu_{x}
in the graph induced by
P^{t}
. With
theorem

8.2
, we obtain a span program
\mathcal{P}
that solves the detection version of the quantum walk search problem.

It remains to compute the witness sizes. For a negative instance
x\in\mathcal{D}^{-}
, we have

w_{-}(x,\mathcal{P})\leq\sum_{v\in\supp(\sigma)}\sigma_{v}(\mathsf{S}_{v})_{x}^{-}+\sum_{e\in E}\frac{(\mathsf{U}_{e})_{x}^{-}}{r_{e}}+\sum_{v\in V}\tau_{v}(\mathsf{C}_{v,D_{v,x}})_{x}^{-}\leq 1+1+1\in O(1),

where we use that all of the negative witness complexities are upper bounded by
1
, that
\sigma
and
\tau
are probability distributions, and that the sum of the resistances is at most a constant because it corresponds to a Markov process
M=(V,P)
, as in
theorem

2.7
.

For a positive instance,
x\in\mathcal{D}_{+}
, we upper bound the positive witness complexity as

\displaystyle w_{+}(x,\mathcal{P})

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{v\in supp(\sigma)}\frac{(\mu_{x})_{v}^{2}}{\sigma_{v}}(\mathsf{S}_{v})_{x}^{+}+R_{\mathrm{eff}}(G,r\circ(\mathsf{U}_{e})_{x}^{+};\mu_{x}-\nu_{x})+\sum_{v\in M_{x}}\frac{(\nu_{x})_{v}^{2}}{\tau_{v}}(\mathsf{C}_{v,D_{v,x}})_{x}^{+}

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{v\in\supp(\sigma)}\sigma_{v}\mathsf{S}^{2}+R_{\mathrm{eff}}(G,r;\mu_{x}-\nu_{x})\mathsf{U}^{2}+2\sum_{v\in M_{x}}\frac{(\nu_{x})_{v}^{2}}{\sigma_{v}+\pi_{v}}\mathsf{C}^{2}.

We upper bound the second and third term separately. For the second term, we use
lemma

3.2
to obtain that

R_{\mathrm{eff}}(G,r;\mu_{x}-\nu_{x})\mathsf{U}^{2}=R_{\mathrm{eff}}(P;\mu_{x}-\nu_{t})\mathsf{U}^{2}\leq t\cdot R_{\mathrm{eff}}(P^{t};\mu_{x}-\nu_{x})\mathsf{U}^{2}=t\cdot R_{\mathrm{eff}}(P^{t};\sigma\leftrightarrow M_{x})\mathsf{U}^{2}\leq tR\mathsf{U}^{2}.

For the third term, we define
\nu_{x}^{\prime}:=\nu_{x}-\sigma|_{M_{x}}
. Now, we find that
\sigma-\nu_{x}=\sigma|_{V\setminus M_{x}}-\nu_{x}^{\prime}
, and moreover

\sum_{v\in M_{x}}(\nu_{x}^{\prime})_{v}=\sum_{v\in M_{x}}(\nu_{x})_{v}-\sum_{v\in M_{x}}\sigma_{v}=1-\sum_{v\in M_{x}}\sigma_{v}=\sum_{v\in V\setminus M_{x}}\sigma_{v}.

Combining this with
lemma

3.3
, we obtain that

\displaystyle\sum_{v\in M_{x}}\frac{(\nu_{x})_{v}^{2}}{\sigma_{v}+\pi_{v}}

\displaystyle=\sum_{v\in M_{x}}\frac{(\sigma_{v}+(\nu_{x}^{\prime})_{v})^{2}}{\sigma_{v}+\pi_{v}}=\sum_{v\in M_{x}}\frac{\sigma_{v}(\sigma_{v}+2(\nu_{x}^{\prime})_{v})}{\sigma_{v}+\pi_{v}}+\sum_{v\in M_{x}}\frac{(\nu_{x}^{\prime})_{v}^{2}}{\sigma_{v}+\pi_{v}}

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{v\in M_{x}}(\sigma_{v}+2(\nu_{x}^{\prime})_{v})+\sum_{v\in M_{x}}\frac{(\nu_{x}^{\prime})_{v}^{2}}{\pi_{v}}=\sum_{v\in M_{x}}\sigma_{v}+2\sum_{v\in V\setminus M_{x}}\sigma_{v}+\sum_{v\in M_{x}}\frac{(\nu_{x}^{\prime})_{v}^{2}}{\pi_{v}}

\displaystyle\leq 2+2R_{\text{eff}}(P^{t};\sigma|_{V\setminus M_{x}}-\nu_{x}^{\prime})=2+2R_{\text{eff}}(P^{t};\sigma-\nu_{x})=2(1+R_{\mathrm{eff}}(P^{t};\sigma\leftrightarrow M_{x}))\leq 2(1+R).

Putting everything together, we obtain that

w_{+}(x,\mathcal{P})\in O\left(\mathsf{S}^{2}+tR\mathsf{U}^{2}+(1+R)\mathsf{C}^{2}\right),

which using that for all
a,b>0
,
\sqrt{a+b}\in O(\sqrt{a}+\sqrt{b})
, implies the result.
∎

We remark here that this result relates to
[
AGJ21
, Theorem 1/Theorem 13]
, as well as
[
AGJ21
, Theorem 2/Theorem 27]
. The result we present here is weaker, in the sense that it only solves the detection version of the quantum walk search problem, whereas the results from Apers, Gilyén and Jeffery solve both the detection and the finding version of the problem simultaneously. On the other hand, it is stronger in the sense that it removes all logarithmic factors from the query complexity bound. On top of that, as we will see in the next section, our result can be extended to allow for amortization of the costs too.

We observe that the complexity statement in our theorem differs slightly from the one in
[
AGJ21
]
, as the prefactor in front of the checking cost,
\mathsf{C}
, is
\sqrt{1+R}
for us, whereas it is
\sqrt{R}
in
[
AGJ21
]
. This is because Apers, Gilyén and Jeffery et al. assume that the effective resistance between
\mu_{x}
and
\nu_{x}
is always at least a constant, i.e.,
R\in\Omega(1)
. However, it is possible to construct situations where this effective resistance is much smaller than one, i.e.,
R\ll 1
, in which case we still need an additive overhead of
\mathsf{C}
in the complexity statement.

Curiously, the construction of
[
AGJ21
]
requires a choice of
t
in the procedural description of the algorithm. Here, though, the parameter
t
only shows up in the analysis of the algorithm, but not in its implementation. Thus, there is an interesting qualitative improvement we make here, i.e., we give a single algorithm with a query complexity that beats any choice for the parameter
t
in the construction from Apers, Gilyén and Jeffery.

Finally, we remark that this result can be ported to the finding case as well, in the same settings as considered in the previous section. This also shaves off some of the log-factors in the finding results in
[
AGJ21
]
. However, the construction in
[
AGJ21
]
also allows for solving the finding version of the problem without knowing the fraction of marked vertices beforehand, so we leave it for future research to recover this setting from the graph composition construction as well.

8.3
Relation to quantum subroutine composition
[
Jef22
]

In this subsection, we compare the results we obtain in this work to those obtained by Jeffery
[
Jef22
]
. We start by obtaining a version of the variable-time quantum walk search span program, where all the weight is put on the negative witness complexity.

Theorem 8.6

(Variable-query quantum walk search – detection version)
.

Suppose we have the instance of quantum walk search
(G,r,x\mapsto M_{x},(v,x)\mapsto D_{v,x},\{S_{v}\}_{v\in V},\{U_{e}\}_{e\in E},\{C_{v,D}\}_{v\in V,D\in\mathcal{S}})
. Let
M=(V,P)
be the corresponding random walk on
G
, with stationary distribution
\pi\in\Delta_{V}
. Let
\sigma,\tau\in\Delta_{V}
be distributions with full support. We write, for all
v\in V
,
e\in E
and
D\in\mathcal{S}
,

\mathsf{S}_{v}^{\pm}:=\max_{x\in\mathcal{D}_{\pm}}(\mathsf{S}_{v})_{x}^{\pm},\qquad\mathsf{U}_{e}^{\pm}:=\max_{x\in\mathcal{D}_{\pm}}(\mathsf{U}_{e})_{x}^{\pm},\qquad\text{and}\qquad\mathsf{C}_{v,D}^{\pm}:=\max_{x\in\mathcal{D}_{\pm}}(\mathsf{C}_{v,D})_{x}^{\pm}.

Next, we take two different definitions of several quantities:

1.

Let
R:=\max_{x\in\mathcal{D}_{+}}R_{\mathrm{eff}}(P;\sigma\leftrightarrow M_{x})
, and for all
x\in\mathcal{D}_{+}
, let
\nu_{x}\in\Delta_{M_{x}}
be the target distribution that minimizes the flow between
\sigma
and
M_{x}
, i.e.,
R_{\mathrm{eff}}(P;\sigma\leftrightarrow M_{x})=R_{\mathrm{eff}}(P;\sigma-\nu_{x})
. For all
x\in\mathcal{D}_{+}
, let
\varepsilon_{x}:=\mathbb{E}_{v\sim\tau}[(\nu_{x})_{v}^{2}/\tau_{v}]^{-1}
, and let
\varepsilon:=\min_{x\in\mathcal{D}_{+}}\varepsilon_{x}
.

2.

For all
x\in\mathcal{D}_{+}
, let
\varepsilon_{x}=\mathbb{P}_{v\sim\tau}[v\in M_{x}]
, and
\nu_{x}:=(\tau|_{M_{x}})/\varepsilon_{x}\in\Delta_{M_{x}}
. Next, let
\varepsilon=\min_{x\in\mathcal{D}_{+}}\varepsilon_{x}
, and
R:=\max_{x\in\mathcal{D}_{+}}R_{\mathrm{eff}}(P;\mu_{x}-\nu_{x})
.

In both cases, we can solve the detection version of the quantum walk search problem with a span program
\mathcal{P}
, with complexities

\displaystyle w_{+}(x,\mathcal{P})

\displaystyle\in O\left(\frac{\underset{v\sim\sigma}{\mathbb{E}}[(\mathsf{S}_{v})_{x}^{+}]}{\underset{v\sim\sigma}{\mathbb{E}}[\mathsf{S}_{v}^{+}]}+\frac{R_{\mathrm{eff}}\left(G,r\circ\frac{(\mathsf{U}_{\cdot})_{x}^{+}}{\mathsf{U}_{\cdot}^{+}};\sigma-\nu_{x}\right)}{R}+\underset{v\sim\tau}{\mathbb{E}}\left[\frac{(\nu_{x})_{v}^{2}}{\tau_{v}}\cdot\frac{\varepsilon(\mathsf{C}_{v,D_{v,x}})_{x}^{+}}{\mathsf{C}_{v,D_{v,x}}^{+}}\right]\right)\subseteq O(1),

\displaystyle w_{-}(x,\mathcal{P})

\displaystyle\in O\left(\underset{v\sim\sigma}{\mathbb{E}}\left[\mathsf{S}_{v}^{+}\right]\cdot\underset{v\sim\sigma}{\mathbb{E}}\left[(\mathsf{S}_{v})_{x}^{-}\right]+R\cdot\underset{\begin{subarray}{c}v\sim\pi\\
w\sim P_{v\cdot}\end{subarray}}{\mathbb{E}}\left[\mathsf{U}^{+}_{vw}(\mathsf{U}_{vw})_{x}^{-}\right]+\frac{1}{\varepsilon}\cdot\underset{v\sim\tau}{\mathbb{E}}\left[\mathsf{C}_{v,D_{v,x}}^{+}(\mathsf{C}_{v,D_{v,x}})_{x}^{-}\right]\right),

for
x\in\mathcal{D}_{+}
and
x\in\mathcal{D}_{-}
, respectively.

Proof.

From
lemma

4.4
, we can rescale the setup routines so that for every
v\in V
,
(\mathsf{S}^{\prime}_{v})_{x}^{\pm}=(\mathsf{S}_{v})_{x}^{\pm}\cdot\mathbb{E}_{v\sim\sigma}[\mathsf{S}_{v}^{+}]^{\mp 1}
. Similarly for all
e\in E
, we rescale
U_{e}
such that
(\mathsf{U}_{e}^{\prime})_{x}^{\pm}=(\mathsf{U}_{e})_{x}^{\pm}\cdot(R\mathsf{U}_{e}^{+})^{\mp 1}
, and for all
v\in V
and
D\in\mathcal{S}
, we rescale
C_{v,D}
such that
(\mathsf{C}_{v,D}^{\prime})_{x}^{\pm}=(\mathsf{C}_{v,D})_{x}^{\pm}\cdot(\mathsf{C}_{v,D_{v,x}}^{+}/\varepsilon)^{\mp 1}
. Then, the resulting complexities follow from
theorem

8.2
, with the rescaled setup, update and checking routines.
∎

Using the above theorem, we can recover the results from
[
Jef22
, Corollary 4.2]
. The idea is that if we have quantum Las Vegas algorithms for the setup, update, and checking routines, then we can convert them into hyperedges using
theorems

4.5
and
5.4
. If we know the expected number of queries beforehand, we simply use
\alpha_{t}=1
for all
t\in\mathbb{N}_{0}
in
theorem

4.5
, which yields
\mathsf{W}_{x}^{\pm}=\mathbb{E}[T_{x}]
for every Las Vegas routine with stopping time
T_{x}
on input
x\in\mathcal{D}
. Since we now know all these numbers
\mathsf{W}_{x}^{\pm}
, we can use them in the construction in
theorem

8.6
. This yields all the complexities from
[
Jef22
, Corollary 4.2]
for known query complexities.

On the other hand, if we only know a crude upper bound
T
on their query complexities, we use
\alpha_{t}=1/(t+1)
, for all
t\in\mathbb{N}_{0}
in
theorem

4.5
. We obtain
\mathsf{W}_{x}^{+}\in O(\log(T))
, and
\mathsf{W}_{x}^{-}\in O(\mathbb{E}[T_{x}^{2}])
, for every Las Vegas routine with stopping time
T_{x}
on input
x\in\mathcal{D}
. Since we only need to know upper bounds on
\mathsf{W}_{x}^{+}
in
theorem

8.6
, this suffices for our construction. This yields all the complexities from
[
Jef22
, Corollary 4.2]
for unknown query complexities.

Note that we improve by a factor of
\log(T)
over
[
Jef22
, Corollary 4.2]
with our approach. The reason is because Jeffery also analyzes the time complexity of the construction, which requires an extra
\log(T)
-overhead. We show here that this overhead is not required if one only cares about queries.

We can now combine the previous theorem with
lemmas

3.2
and
3.3
, to obtain a variable-query result for the MNRS-framework. This improves over
[
Jef22
, Theorem 4.3]
.

Corollary 8.7

(Variable-query MNRS)
.

Suppose we have the instance of quantum walk search
(G,r,x\mapsto M_{x},(v,x)\mapsto D_{v,x},\{S_{v}\}_{v\in V},\{U_{e}\}_{e\in E},\{C_{v,D}\}_{v\in V,D\in\mathcal{S}})
. Let
\pi
be the stationary distribution of the random walk
P
on
G
, and let
\delta
be its spectral gap. For all
x\in\mathcal{D}_{+}
, let
\varepsilon_{x}:=\mathbb{P}_{v\sim\pi}[v\in M_{x}]
, and we write
\varepsilon:=\min_{x\in\mathcal{D}_{+}}\varepsilon_{x}
. Then, we can solve the detection version of the quantum walk search problem with a span program
\mathcal{P}
, whose complexities satisfy

\displaystyle w_{+}(x,\mathcal{P})

\displaystyle\in O(1),

\displaystyle w_{-}(x,\mathcal{P})

\displaystyle\in O\left(\underset{v\sim\pi}{\mathbb{E}}\left[\mathsf{S}_{v}^{+}\right]\cdot\underset{v\sim\pi}{\mathbb{E}}\left[(\mathsf{S}_{v})_{x}^{-}\right]+\frac{1}{\varepsilon}\left(\frac{1}{\delta}\cdot\underset{\begin{subarray}{c}v\sim\pi\\
w\sim P_{v\cdot}\end{subarray}}{\mathbb{E}}\left[\mathsf{U}^{+}_{vw}(\mathsf{U}_{vw})_{x}^{-}\right]+\underset{v\sim\pi}{\mathbb{E}}\left[\mathsf{C}_{v,D_{v,x}}^{+}(\mathsf{C}_{v,D_{v,x}})_{x}^{-}\right]\right)\right),

for
x\in\mathcal{D}_{+}
and
x\in\mathcal{D}_{-}
, respectively.

Proof.

We use the second claim from
theorem

8.6
, with
\sigma=\tau=\pi
. It then remains to prove that

R_{\mathrm{eff}}\left(G,r;\pi-\frac{\pi|_{M_{x}}}{\varepsilon_{x}}\right)\leq\frac{1}{\delta\varepsilon_{x}}.

To that end, we write
\xi:=\pi-\pi|_{M_{x}}/\varepsilon_{x}
and
D=\diag(\pi)
. We use the second claim from
lemma

3.2
and observe that

\displaystyle R_{\mathrm{eff}}\left(G,r;\pi-\frac{\pi|_{M_{x}}}{\varepsilon_{x}}\right)

\displaystyle\leq\frac{\left\|D^{-1/2}\xi\right\|_{2}^{2}}{\delta}=\frac{1}{\delta}\sum_{v\in V}\frac{\xi_{v}^{2}}{\pi_{v}}=\frac{1}{\delta}\left[\sum_{v\in V\setminus M_{x}}\pi_{v}+\sum_{v\in M_{x}}\left(\frac{1}{\varepsilon_{x}}-1\right)^{2}\pi_{v}\right]

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\delta}\left[1-\frac{2}{\varepsilon_{x}}\sum_{v\in V\setminus M_{x}}\pi_{v}+\frac{1}{\varepsilon_{x}^{2}}\sum_{v\in M_{x}}\pi_{v}\right]=\frac{1}{\delta}\left[\frac{1}{\varepsilon_{x}}-1\right]\leq\frac{1}{\delta\varepsilon_{x}}.\qed

The above analysis improves over
[
Jef22
]
by getting rid of the
\sqrt{\log(1/\pi_{\min})}
factor. This is significant, because one of the most prominent usecases of the MNRS-framework is implementing quantum walk search over a Johnson graph
J(n,r)
. Since the number of vertices of such a Johnson graph is
\binom{n}{r}
, and the stationary distribution is uniform, we obtain in this setting that
\log(1/\pi_{\min})=\log\binom{n}{r}\sim r\log(n)
, which introduces polynomial overhead in the cases where we choose
r
polynomial in
n
. Jeffery already conjectured just before
[
Jef22
, Corollary 4.3]
that this overhead is unnecessary, and we confirm that conjecture here.

Furthermore, the previous theorem is a generalization of
[
CLM20
, Theorem 3]
. There, the authors provide a theorem that amortizes the costs of the setup, update, and checking routines, in the same way as in
corollary

8.7
. However, their result only works when the quantum walk is performed on a Johnson graph, and the objective routines are given by learning graphs. We generalize to quantum walks on arbitrary graphs, and with the input routines being represented as feasible solutions to the adversary bound, rather than learning graphs.

Finally, we remark that we can port these results in exactly the same way as in the general case to the finding case, i.e., if we are promised that the marked vertex is unique, or if we know the fraction of marked vertices a priori. To the best of our knowledge, this is the first result of its kind that is able to solve the finding version of the quantum random walk search question on a general graph, while simultaneously allowing for amortization of its subroutines. We leave it for future work to figure out if one can also solve the finding question in the more generic sense, i.e., without knowing the fraction of marked vertices beforehand.
