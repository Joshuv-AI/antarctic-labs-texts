Abstract
. Division polynomials associated to an elliptic curve E / K E/K are polynomials ϕ n , ψ n 2 \phi_{n},\psi_{n}^{2} that arise from the sequence of points { n ​ P } n ∈ ℕ \{nP\}_{n\in\mathbb{N}} on this curve. If one wishes to study ℤ \mathbb{Z} –linear combination of points on E ⁡ ( K ) E(K) , we can use net polynomials Φ 𝒗 , Ψ 𝒗 2 \Phi_{\bm{v}},\Psi_{\bm{v}}^{2} which are higher–dimensional analogue of division polynomials. It turns out they are also elliptic nets, an n n –dimensional array with values in K K satisfying the same nonlinear recurrence relation that division polynomials do as well. Now further assume the elliptic curve E / K E/K has complex multiplication by an order of a quadratic imaginary field F ⊆ K F\subseteq K , we will prove a formula for the common valuation of Φ 𝒗 \Phi_{\bm{v}} and Ψ 𝒗 2 \Psi_{\bm{v}}^{2} associated to multiples of points by elements of an order in F F . 1.
Introduction

1.1.
Motivation

Let
E/\mathbb{Q}
be an elliptic curve defined over the rationals with the following Weierstrass equation:

(1.1)

E\colon y^{2}+a_{1}xy+a_{3}y=x^{3}+a_{2}x^{2}+a_{4}x+a_{6};\,a_{i}\in\mathbb{Z}

Let
P=(x,y)
be a non-torsion point on
E(\mathbb{Q})
,
n
an integer. Then we can express the point
nP
with the
n
-th division polynomials or the numerical values directly:

(1.2)

x(nP)=\frac{\phi_{n}(x(P))}{\psi_{n}^{2}(x(P))}=\frac{A_{n}(E,P)}{B_{n}^{2}(E,P)}\,\,\text{with}\,\,(A_{n},B_{n})=1\,\,\text{and}\,\,B_{n}>0.

with
A_{n}(E,P)
and
B_{n}(E,P)
two coprime integers and
B_{n}(E,P)\geq 0
. In
[
13
, Exercise 3.7]
, Silverman mentioned that the division polynomials
\{\psi_{n}\}_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
are a sequence of rational functions on
E
that satisfy the recurrence relation

(1.3)

\psi_{n+m}\psi_{n-m}\psi_{r}^{2}=\psi_{m+r}\psi_{m-r}\psi_{n}^{2}-\psi_{n+r}\psi_{n-r}\psi_{m}^{2}\,\,\text{for any}\,\,n>m>r.

Division polynomials were introduced to compute scalar multiplications of points on elliptic curves. However, when evaluated at a point on an elliptic curve, these polynomials are not usually coprime. This has led to an interest in the quantity
\min(\nu(\phi_{n}(P)),\nu(\psi_{n}^{2}(P)))
for a fixed discrete valuation
\nu
, commonly referred to as the
cancellation exponent
. By understanding or estimating this quantity, one can address problems related to Siegel’s famous theorem, which states that any elliptic curve
E/\mathbb{Q}
has only finitely many integral points.

For instance, Ayad used this concept to compute
S
-integral points of an elliptic curve of rank 1 (see
[
2
, Sections 6–8]
). Subsequently, Ingram
[
7
]
estimated the height of such points by bounding
|\psi_{n}(P)|
in terms of the denominator of
[n]P
, a process that involves analysing the valuations of
\psi_{n}(P)
at each prime. Building on this work, Stange
[
15
]
refined Ingram’s results, obtaining a bound that depends on the height ratio
h(E)/\hat{h}(P)
.
Meanwhile, Amir
[
6
]
focused on Mordell curves
E_{B}\mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}y^{2}=x^{3}+B
, demonstrating that, with finitely many exceptions, there are at most two values
1<n<11
for which
[n]P
is integral. While this significantly improves the bounds, it still depends on the valuation of division polynomials.

There have already been several attempts to find a formula for the cancellation exponent between the division polynomials
\psi_{n}^{2}
and
\phi_{n}
. For example, Cheon
[
4
]
provides a recursive formula at singular primes, utilising the set of points with nonsingular reduction
E_{0}(K)\subseteq E(K)
, which is a subgroup of finite index. Stange
[
15
]
, on the other hand, presents a closed formula whose parameters depend on the reduction properties of
P
and
E
. Yabuta and Voutier gave a formula in terms of the Kodaira symbol in
[
20
]
, while Naskręcki and Verzobio extended their results to elliptic curves over function fields in
[
8
]
. Understanding the cancellation exponent is particularly useful for the study of elliptic divisibility sequences (EDSs). This is closely related to the application to Siegel’s Theorem mentioned above, as finding all
n
for which
[n]P
is integral is equivalent to finding all
n
such that the denominator of the coordinates of the
n
-th term,
B_{n}
, equals
1
.

If the elliptic curve
E
now has complex multiplication (CM), it is natural to ask for objects with properties like division polynomials and EDSs, indexed by elements of an order of a quadratic imaginary field
\End(E)
, generated by the basis set
\{1,\omega\}
, instead of just by integers. There have already been some attempts to extend definitions and results related to EDSs and division polynomials to the case where
E
has complex multiplication. For instance, Streng
[
18
]
extended the definition of EDSs for elliptic curve points and established results concerning primitive divisors. Meanwhile, Satoh
[
10
]
proved the existence of generalised division polynomials for elliptic curves with complex multiplication and demonstrated that these polynomials satisfy certain recurrence relations. Therefore, if one also derives a formula for the cancellation exponent of CM division polynomials, it becomes possible to extend all the aforementioned applications to CM elliptic curves, taking the additional CM points into account.

In this paper, we define CM division polynomials using elliptic nets, as defined in
2.3
, rather than Satoh’s approach for two reasons: first, the behaviour of the generalised division polynomials defined by Satoh in
[
10
]
depends on whether or not the points in the kernel
E[\alpha]
sum to zero and whether
2
ramifies, splits or remains inert (interested readers are encouraged to see
[
10
]
or
[
16
]
). Second, elliptic nets are a ‘higher-rank’ generalisation of EDSs. In
[
15
]
, it was noted that analogous results for bounding integral points among linear combinations
[n]P+[m]Q
currently seem out of reach. However, if an elliptic curve
E
has complex multiplication by
\mathbb{Z}[\omega]
, one can consider the
\mathbb{Z}
-linear combination
[n]P+[m]\omega P
, which is effectively a multiple of a point
[n+m\omega]P
. Specifically, the author is now attempting to apply the explicit common valuation formula to use in the same spirit by generalising an argument of Ingram in
[
7
]
, with the aim of producing a bound on
n+m\omega
with the second largest norm such that the point
[n+\omega]P
is integral. Thus, we believe that defining CM elliptic divisibility sequences using elliptic nets could lead to improved bounds on counting problems and serve as a prototype for a linear combination version of the integral point problem, which is the motivation behind our interest in this subject.

1.3.
Main methodology and outline of the paper

The original proof of
Theorem

1.1
by Cheon and Hahn
[
4
]
relies heavily on the properties of the division polynomials
\psi_{n}
, such as their degrees and recursive structure. Since elliptic nets are defined by rational functions rather than polynomials, their strategy does not carry over directly. Instead, our approach is inspired by Naskręcki and Verzobio
[
8
]
and Panda
[
9
]
. In particular, we use the Néron local height function

\lambda\colon E(K)\setminus\{O\}\to\mathbb{R}

to compute the local height of the point
[\alpha]P
for
\alpha\in\End(E)=\mathbb{Z}[\omega]
. Once we obtain an explicit formula for this (see
3.2
), we can reconstruct the intermediate steps of
[
4
]
in the CM setting and thereby establish the formula in
Theorem

1.5
. Compared with the original proof of Cheon and Hahn, our use of the Néron local height function not only removes the dependence on division polynomials, thus making the argument more flexible and applicable to other problems involving elliptic curves or elliptic nets, but also avoids heavy machinery, resulting in a cleaner and more accessible proof.

However, working with the Néron local height function also introduces certain technical difficulties. Although the expression

\lambda([a]P+[b]Q)

is expected to be a quadratic function in
a
and
b
, based on the known formula for
\lambda([m]P)
, one must still determine the coefficients of
a^{2}
,
b^{2}
, and
ab
explicitly. In particular, the coefficient of the
ab
term is not obvious. Furthermore,
Theorem

1.5
extends the result of Cheon and Hahn, so the main challenge lies in identifying the additional terms needed to accommodate the CM setting. As illustrated in
Section

4.3
, repeated application of the local height formula in
3.2
produces numerous local height values which are difficult to trace where they come from; these ultimately arise from the new terms associated with CM points.

With regards to this, we outline the paper as follows: in Section 2, we review necessary facts about elliptic nets and the Néron local height function. The following Section 3 then provides the formula for the local height value of a linear combination of points, showing how it is related to elliptic nets. In Section 4, we will prove
Theorem

1.5
by repeated use of the formula in Section 3. Finally, we will also follow Verzobio’s strategy to show
Theorem

1.8
as an application in Section 5.

d_{K}
,
the ring of integers
\mathcal{O}_{K}
can be written as

\mathcal{O}_{K}=\mathbb{Z}[w_{K}],\quad w_{K}=\frac{d_{K}+\sqrt{d_{K}}}{2}.

The following result is taken from
[
5
, §7A, page 120, Lemma 7.2]
.

Lemma 2.1
.

Let
\mathcal{O}
be an order in a quadratic field
K
of discriminant
d_{K}
.
Then
\mathcal{O}
has finite index in
\mathcal{O}_{K}
, and if we set the conductor of
\mathcal{O}
to be
f=\left[\mathcal{O}_{K}\mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}\mathcal{O}\right]
, then

\mathcal{O}=\mathbb{Z}+f\mathcal{O}_{K}=\mathbb{Z}[fw_{K}].

Corollary 2.2
.

Every order
\mathcal{O}
in a quadratic field
K
of conductor

is of the form
\mathbb{Z}[\omega]
,
where either

\omega^{2}=D,\quad\text{or}\quad\omega^{2}-f\omega=D

for some integer
D
.

Proof.

Let
K=\mathbb{Q}(\sqrt{N})
with
N
a square–free integer.
Recall that one can describe the ring of integers as

\mathcal{O}_{K}=\begin{cases}\mathbb{Z}[\sqrt{N}],&\text{if }N\not\equiv 1\bmod 4,\\[10.00002pt]
\mathbb{Z}\!\left[\dfrac{1+\sqrt{N}}{2}\right],&\text{if }N\equiv 1\bmod 4.\end{cases}

Let
\{1,\omega\}
be a
\mathbb{Z}
–basis of
\mathcal{O}
.
If

, then
Lemma

2.1
implies that

\omega=f\sqrt{N}
, which has minimal polynomial

.
If

implies that

\omega=\left(\dfrac{1+\sqrt{N}}{2}\right)f

m_{2}(x)=x^{2}-fx+\dfrac{f^{2}(1-N)}{4}.

∎

2.2.
Elliptic divisibility sequence and elliptic net

An
elliptic divisibility sequence
(EDS) is a sequence
(W_{n})
that takes values in an integral domain and satisfies the recursion

W_{n+m}W_{n-m}W_{r}^{2}=W_{n+r}W_{n-r}W_{m}^{2}-W_{m+r}W_{m-r}W_{n}^{2}

for any integers
n,m,r
. Therefore, one can see that
\{\psi_{n}(P)\}_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
is also an EDS. In
[
17
]
, Stange generalised the concept of an elliptic sequence to an
n
–dimension array, also known as an
elliptic net
, which extends the recurrence relation above to ‘a higher rank’.

Definition 2.3

(Elliptic net)
.

Let
A
be a free finitely-generated abelian group and
R
be an integral domain. An
elliptic net
is any map
W\colon A\rightarrow R
with
W(\bm{0})=0
and, for any
\bm{p},\bm{q},\bm{r},\bm{s}\in A
,

W(\bm{p}+\bm{q}+\bm{s})W(\bm{p}-\bm{q})W(\bm{r}+\bm{s})W(\bm{r})\\
+W(\bm{q}+\bm{r}+\bm{s})W(\bm{q}-\bm{r})W(\bm{p}+\bm{s})W(\bm{p})\\
+W(\bm{r}+\bm{p}+\bm{s})W(\bm{r}-\bm{p})W(\bm{q}+\bm{s})W(\bm{q})=0.

We call the rank of
W
the rank of the elliptic net.

This is indeed a generalisation of EDS: note that for an elliptic net
W\mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}A\rightarrow R
, if we take
A=\mathbb{Z}
,
\bm{p}=m,\bm{q}=n,\bm{r}=r,\bm{s}=0
, then
W
is an EDS by definition since
W
is an
odd
function.

Lemma 2.4
.

[
17
, Proposition 2.1, page 6]

Let
A
be a free abelian group of finite rank, and
R
be an integral domain. Let
W\colon A\rightarrow R
be an elliptic net. Then for any
\bm{v}\in A,W(-\bm{v})=-W(\bm{v})
. In other words, an elliptic net is an odd function.

Proof.

If
W(\bm{v})=0
for all
\bm{v}\in A
, then
W
is an odd function trivially, so assume this is not the case.
If
\bm{v}=\bm{0}
, then by the definition of an elliptic net we must have
W(\bm{v})=0
, so suppose not all
\bm{p},\bm{q},\bm{r},\bm{s}\in A
are
\bm{0}
in the definition of an elliptic net.

Now take
\bm{p}=\bm{q}=\bm{v}
and
\bm{r}=\bm{s}=\bm{0}
to get
W(\bm{v})^{3}(W(\bm{v})+W(-\bm{v}))=0
. In particular, if we instead choose
\bm{p}=\bm{q}=-\bm{v}
, we have
W(-\bm{v})^{3}(W(\bm{v})+W(-\bm{v}))=0
as well. If
W(\bm{v})+W(-\bm{v})\neq 0
, then, since
R
is an integral domain, we have
W(-\bm{v})^{3}=W(\bm{v})^{3}=0
, and hence
W(-\bm{v})=W(\bm{v})=0
. This implies that
W(\bm{v})+W(-\bm{v})=0
, a contradiction.
∎

Just like division polynomials, we can relate elliptic nets to elliptic curves. In
[
17
]
, Stange has shown that there is a collection of rational functions on elliptic curves over arbitrary fields that satisfy the definition of elliptic nets. In fact, they describe the collection of denominators of linear combinations of points,
\sum_{i=1}^{n}[m_{i}]P_{i}
, on an elliptic curve, which means we are taking
A=\mathbb{Z}^{n}
in the definition of an elliptic net.

Definition 2.5

(Net polynomial and elliptic denominator net)

For an arbitrary field

(2.1)

The final fact about elliptic nets we need for this paper is the equivalence relation for elliptic nets, namely
scale equivalence
. This is done by relating them to certain ‘quadratic forms’.

Definition 2.7
.

Let
B
and
C
be abelian groups written additively. A function
F\colon B\rightarrow C
is a quadratic form if for all
x,y,z\in B
,

(2.5)

F(x+y+z)+F(x)+F(y)+F(z)=F(x+y)+F(x+z)+F(y+z).

If
F\colon B\rightarrow C
is a quadratic form, then it satisfies the parallelogram law, that is, for all
x,y\in B
,

(2.6)

F(x+y)+F(x-y)=2F(x)+2F(y).

Furthermore, the converse holds if
C
is 2-torsion free.

Proposition 2.8
.

Let
K
be a field and let
W\colon A\rightarrow K
be an elliptic net. Let
F\colon A\rightarrow K^{\times}
be a quadratic form. Then the function
W^{F}\colon A\rightarrow K
defined by

W^{F}(\bm{v})=W(\bm{v})F(\bm{v})

is also an elliptic net.

Proof.

We just need to show that
F(\bm{v})
also satisfies the recurrence relation of elliptic nets. To do so, write (
2.5
) and (
2.6
) in multiplicative form and multiply them together, then utilise the symmetry in quadratic forms. We omit the computation here, interested readers can refer to
[
17
, Proposition 6.1]
.
∎

Definition 2.9
.

We say two elliptic nets
W_{1}
and
W_{2}
are scale equivalent if there exists a quadratic form
F\colon A\rightarrow K
such that
W_{1}=W_{2}^{F}
.

2.3.
Local Néron height function

Height functions help us to relate the algebraic group law to the arithmetic properties of the algebraic point on elliptic curves. It is a natural question to ask for a given height function, whether it is possible to decompose it into a sum of local terms, one for each absolute value. For the canonical height function
\hat{h}(P)\colon E\rightarrow[0,\infty)
, Néron and Tate showed that there is a decomposition into local functions, which are now known as the
local Néron height function
. The following definition is taken from
[
12
, Chapter VI, page 455, Theorem 1.1]

Definition 2.10

(Local Néron height function)
.

Let
K
be a complete field with respect to a non-Archimedean absolute value
|\cdot|_{u}
so
u(\cdot)=-\log|\cdot|_{u}
. Let
E/K
be an elliptic curve with discriminant
\Delta
given by the following Weierstrass equation:

E\colon y^{2}+a_{1}xy+a_{3}y=x^{3}+a_{2}x^{2}+a_{4}x+a_{6}.

(1)

There is a unique function

\lambda\mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}E(K)\backslash\{O\}\to\mathbb{R}

satisfying the following properties:

\mathrm{i)}

\lambda
is continuous on
E(K)\backslash\{O\}
and is bounded on the complement of any
u
-adic neighbourhood of
O
.

\mathrm{ii)}

The limit

\lim_{P\to O}\left\{\lambda(P)+\frac{1}{2}u(x(P))\right\}

exists.

\mathrm{iii)}

For all
P\in E(K)\setminus E[2]
we have

\lambda([2]P)=4\lambda(P)+\nu((2y+a_{1}x+a_{3})(P))-\frac{1}{4}u(\Delta).

(2)

\lambda
is independent of the choice of Weierstrass equation for
E/K
.

(3)

Let
L/K
be a finite extension and let
w
be the extension of
u
to
L
. Then

\lambda_{w}(P)=\lambda_{u}(P)\,\,\text{for all}\,\,P\in E(L)\backslash\{O\}.

Then the function defined above is called the local Néron height function on
E
associated to
u
.

Remark 2.11
.

When we extend the valuation in (
3
), it is not always possible for both
w
and
u
to be normalised. Therefore, we would like to point out that the non-Archimedean valuation
u
is not necessarily normalised in this definition.

The following results we took from
[
12
, Chapter VI, page 476-7, Exercise 6.3, 6.4(e)]
.

Proposition 2.12
.

Let
K
be a number field,
E/K
be an elliptic curve. Then for all
P,Q\in E(K)
with
P,Q,P\pm Q\neq O
, the Néron height function associated to the non-Archimedean valuation
u

\lambda\colon E(K)\setminus\{O\}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
satisfies the quasi-parallelogram law

\lambda(P+Q)+\lambda(P-Q)=2\lambda(P)+2\lambda(Q)+u(x(P)-x(Q))-\frac{1}{6}u(\Delta).

Corollary 2.13
.

For any non
m
-torsion point
P
, with
m\in\mathbb{Z}
,

\lambda([m]P)=m^{2}\lambda(P)+u(\psi_{m}(P))-\frac{1}{12}(m^{2}-1)u(\Delta).

The above results demonstrate how the valuations of
\psi_{m}
are connected to local Néron heights, allowing us to avoid addressing any properties of division polynomials. To make it look neater without the valuation of the discriminant, we can normalise the local height funciton as in
[
11
,
Second normalisation
, page 90]
.

Definition 2.14
.

The normalised local Néron height function is defined to be

\widetilde{\lambda}(P)\coloneqq\lambda(P)-\frac{1}{12}u(\Delta).

Therefore, the ‘normalised’ quasi-parallelogram law and corollary above are respectively,

(2.7)

\widetilde{\lambda}(P+Q)+\widetilde{\lambda}(P-Q)=2\widetilde{\lambda}(P)+2\widetilde{\lambda}(Q)+u(x(P)-x(Q))

and

\widetilde{\lambda}([m]P)=m^{2}\widetilde{\lambda}(P)+u(\psi_{m}(P)).

One of the reasons why we choose this normalisation is because when a point
P
is not singular
\bmod u
, then the Néron local height function has a nice formula, which we take from
[
12
, Chapter VI, page 470, Theorem 4.1]

Theorem 2.15

Let
K
be a field complete with respect to a non-Archimedean valuation

be an elliptic curve, and choose a Weierstrass equation for

E\colon y^{2}+a_{1}xy+a_{3}y=x^{3}+a_{2}x^{2}+a_{4}x+a_{6}.

3.1.

When we replace
