Place‐based risk: Examining relationships between adolescent perceptions of community risk factors and adolescent substance use across geographic contexts
Authors: Melissa Pearman Fenton, Sarah D Lynne, Josie L Bryan, Catherine Woodstock Striley, Larry F Forthun
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractMixed evidence for the influence of structural and social factors on adolescent substance use behaviors exists across the rural–urban continuum. Therefore, this study explores how adolescent perceptions of structural and social community risk factors are associated with lifetime and past 30‐day use of alcohol, marijuana, cigarettes, and illicit drugs among adolescents in different geographic locations. Data from 9819 adolescents from the 2019 Florida Youth Substance Abuse Survey were used to estimate associations between adolescent perceptions of community risk factors and lifetime and past 30‐day alcohol, marijuana, cigarette, and illicit drugs using logistic regression models stratified by geographic location. Adolescent perceptions of community disorganization were significantly associated with lifetime and past 30‐day alcohol use among town and rural adolescents and illicit drug use among adolescents from all geographic locations. Findings point to shared and unique risk and protective processes in geographic locations with implications for future research and community prevention efforts.Keywords: adolescent, community, geographic factor, risk factor, social factors, structural factors, substance use

INTRODUCTION

Substance use rates vary across the rural–urban continuum. Evidence suggests that adolescents growing up in rural locales report higher rates of alcohol and tobacco use compared to adolescents in urban locales, while adolescents in urban and suburban locales reported significantly higher rates of marijuana use (Chan et al., 2016; Clark et al., 2011; Lambert et al., 2008; Patrick et al., 2013; Thrash & Warner, 2016; Warner, 2016; Warren et al., 2017). However, there are exceptions to these trends. Rural adolescents reported higher rates of past 30‐day and lifetime marijuana use (Coomber et al., 2011) and prescription opioid misuse (Monnat & Rigg, 2016) compared to their urban peers. While substance use behaviors vary based on where adolescents reside, problems associated with the use of any substance may be especially pronounced among rural and small‐town youth due to reduced access to treatment and prevention (Andrilla et al., 2018; DeVoe et al., 2009). In addition, the number of adolescents accessing prevention programs has been declining, especially among rural adolescents (Salas‐Wright et al., 2019). Due to these discrepancies in substance use rates across the rural–urban continuum and the lack of access in rural communities to treatment and prevention, studies must continue to document lifetime and past‐30‐day use of various substances to identify potential geographic health disparities and to inform community‐based prevention strategies for different sizes of geographic locations (Cambron et al., 2020; Chilenski, 2011; Feinberg, 2012; Nawi et al., 2021).

Theoretical orientation

Differences in rates of substance use along the rural–urban continuum may be explained by community factors, which differ based on the organizational structure and cultural beliefs that differentiate urban and rural communities (Witherspoon et al., 2023). Social disorganization theory posits that communities with less cohesive social structures (i.e., are more socially disorganized) have diminished prosocial norms that contribute to the development of deviant norms and behaviors, such as substance use. Communities with more cohesive social structures (i.e., are less socially disorganized) have enhanced prosocial norms that create opportunities for positive social bonds between young people, families, and societal institutions (Tittle, 2000). Rural areas may exhibit less social disorganization than urban areas, which in turn may influence the relationships between risk and protective factors and substance use behaviors within those locales (Osgood & Chambers, 2000; Sampson & Groves, 1989; Wilson & Donnermeyer, 2006). For instance, some research has documented protective rural effects for marijuana use trajectories, with higher risk for marijuana use throughout adolescence and young adulthood in urban and suburban locales (Warner, 2016). However, this protective effect was not observed for prescription opioid misuse, which was higher among rural adolescents compared to urban adolescents (Monnat & Rigg, 2016). Rural protective effects may be substance‐specific and reflect the influences of unique structural and social influences, yet remain understudied in the adolescent substance use literature (Ryerson et al., 2024; Saalfield & Haag, 2026).

Structural factors (e.g., built/physical environment, socio‐demographic) and social processes (e.g., community disorganization, social norms) reflect the social disorganization and social control within a community (Aneshensel, 2009; Catalano & Hawkins, 1996). Structural factors include socio‐demographic and environmental characteristics of communities such as economic disadvantage, enforcement of substance use laws, alcohol and tobacco outlet density, and empty or abandoned buildings, while social factors reflect the social processes within a community that influence the rejection or adoption of health risk behaviors such as social capital, the perceived acceptability of substance use, safety, and collective efficacy (Cambron et al., 2020; Jackson et al., 2014). In the current study, structural risk factors are represented by adolescent perceptions of the availability of substances and perceptions of enforcement of substance use laws. Social risk factors are represented by adolescent perceptions of community members' acceptance of adolescent substance use (i.e., norms favorable to substance use) and community disorganization, as this reflects adolescent perceptions of how structural features of their communities influence public social processes (e.g., fighting, safety, crime, drug selling, graffiti).

Community factors and adolescent substance use across the rural/urban continuum

Systematic reviews have documented mixed evidence for the influence of structural and social factors on adolescent substance use. Associations between area‐level disadvantage and increased alcohol use were more likely to be found in samples of adults than samples of adolescents (Bryden et al., 2013; Karriker‐Jaffe, 2011). A systematic review of multi‐level studies measuring neighborhood socio‐economic disadvantage using a wide range of archival and administrative data also found that neighborhood disadvantage was not a consistent, significant predictor of adolescent alcohol use (Jackson et al., 2014). However, there may be links between structural risk factors and the use of substances other than alcohol. For instance, research within a rural community found a relationship between economic risk, measured as percent of families within the school district below the poverty line and percent of students receiving free or reduced‐cost lunches, and higher community rates of adolescent lifetime cigarette use (Chilenski & Greenberg, 2009). There is also mixed evidence of the impacts of social processes on adolescent substance use. In one systematic review, evidence supported that high social capital and less tolerant attitudes toward drinking were associated with less alcohol use among adolescents, while another review found that social processes, including neighborhood attitudes towards drinking, social capital, and collective efficacy, were not associated with adolescent alcohol use (Bryden et al., 2013; Jackson et al., 2014). Findings from another systematic review suggest social processes, including the widespread, longstanding acceptance of opioid use and social capital gained through prescription opioid use, as driving and drawing factors in the nonmedical use of prescription opioids in rural Appalachia (Bolinski et al., 2019).

These mixed findings reflect differences in the level of measurement (e.g., individual‐, community‐level), who perceives the social and structural processes (e.g., adolescents, adults), how structural and social processes are measured (e.g., census data, respondent data, area social indicators), how community is defined (e.g., census‐tract, zip code, school district, rural/urban), and type of substance (e.g., alcohol, marijuana, cigarettes, nonmedical opioid use) (Bolinski et al., 2019; Bryden et al., 2013; Chilenski & Greenberg, 2009; Jackson et al., 2014; Karriker‐Jaffe, 2011; Saalfield & Haag, 2026). For example, one study of rural communities documented that adolescent perceived community supportiveness was associated with reduced likelihood of initiation and frequency of adolescent alcohol use, while adult perceptions of community supportiveness and collective efficacy were not significantly associated with adolescent alcohol use (De Haan et al., 2010). In the study that found associations between economic risk and cigarette smoking, collective efficacy, measured as adult perceptions of the level of active engagement, closeness, and investment of community members, was not significantly associated with adolescent alcohol or cigarette use (Chilenski & Greenberg, 2009). Youth perceive their communities differently than adults and prior research supports that their perceptions tend to be more closely related to their individual outcomes than more distal measures of community social and structural factors (Pratt et al., 2020). Studies using a variety of measurement strategies and statistical approaches to evaluate the contributions of community structural and social factors to adolescent use of a variety of substances are needed to further our understanding of these processes in geographic locations across the rural–urban continuum (Bolinski et al., 2019; Jackson et al., 2014; Saalfield & Haag, 2026).

In contrast to this mixed evidence, strong evidence supports the clustering of substance use outcomes by geographic area (Karriker‐Jaffe, 2011) and that community risk and protective processes operate differently in rural and urban places (Clark et al., 2011; Monnat & Rigg, 2016; Warren et al., 2015). Rurality shapes neighborhood and community factors that influence developmental processes from childhood through adulthood, including perceptions of community risk factors (Fenton et al., 2022; White et al., 2016). For example, adolescent perceptions that substances are easily available are associated with adolescent substance use and vary among rural and urban youth, with urban adolescents perceiving marijuana as more readily available compared to rural adolescents (Hawkins et al., 1992; Martino et al., 2008; Warren et al., 2015). Likewise, perceived community disorganization has been examined in both rural and urban areas. Community disorganization, frequently described as neighborhood problems such as crime, drug selling, and graffiti, and lower perceived neighborhood safety (Guttmannova et al., 2017), was associated with increased substance use for both rural and urban adolescents (Cambron et al., 2020; D. Lambert et al., 2008; S. F. Lambert et al., 2004; Winstanley et al., 2008). While youth in urban neighborhoods reported higher levels of perceived community disorganization compared to youth in rural neighborhoods (Brevard et al., 2013), adolescent perceptions of community risk (e.g., community disorganization, social norms favorable to substance use, availability of substances) and protective factors (e.g., collective efficacy, neighborhood safety and stability) were more influential for substance use outcomes for rural youth compared to their urban peers (Clark et al., 2011). Community risk and protective factors are uniquely associated with adolescent substance use behaviors in geographic locales across the rural–urban continuum; however, there is limited research that describes and compares these associations across urban, suburban, town, and rural locations.

Evidence of the influence of contextual factors on substance use behaviors along the rural–urban continuum is mixed, supporting a need to continue to examine these effects among adolescents from varying geographic locations (Cambron et al., 2020; Fagan et al., 2015; Ryerson et al., 2024; Saalfield & Haag, 2026). Adolescent perceptions of their communities are associated with their substance use behaviors and may be more influential than adult perceptions or community‐level estimates (De Haan et al., 2010; Pratt et al., 2020); however, there have been few examinations of associations between adolescent perceptions of community risk factors and the use of different substances by geographic location. Conducting stratified analyses of relationships between risk factors and outcomes can help identify geographic health disparities (Redmond et al., 2022) and account for the social determinants of health that contribute to these disparities among underserved populations (Doherty & Wells, 2025). Therefore, this study aims to describe associations between adolescent perceptions of community structural and social risk factors and lifetime and past 30‐day use of alcohol, marijuana, cigarettes, and illicit drugs among a large, representative sample of Florida adolescents stratified by geographic location (e.g., urban, suburban, town, and rural).

METHOD

Data for this research are from the 2019 Florida Youth Substance Abuse Survey (FYSAS). The FYSAS, an anonymous, in‐school survey, is administered annually using measures from Communities that Care Youth Survey (Arthur et al., 2002; Glaser et al., 2005) by the Florida Department of Children and Families (DCF). The survey aims to establish the prevalence of substance use and health risk behaviors and levels of risk and protective factors at the community, family, school, peer, and individual levels. A two‐stage, stratified cluster sampling strategy was used to select a random sample of students from classrooms in 93 middle and 82 high schools resulting in a representative sample of adolescents attending Florida public schools (Fenton et al., 2023). Seventy‐five percent of middle school students and 70% of high school students from schools that were selected to participate responded to the survey (Florida DCF, 2019). A passive informed consent procedure was used by most school districts with parents/guardians responsible for opting students out of the survey (Florida DCF, 2019). A University Institutional Review Board determined this secondary analysis was exempt from review.

Participants

A total of 9819 adolescents in grades 6 through 12, aged 10 to 19 (M = 14 years, SD = 2.07), participated in the 2019 FYSAS survey. Over half were female (52%). Adolescents reported their race/ethnicity as American Indian/Native American (5%); Asian (4%); Black/African American (22%); Native Hawaiian/Pacific Islander (1%); other (7%); Spanish/Hispanic/Latine (38%); and White/Caucasian (43%). Adolescents reported their parents' highest level of education (used as a proxy for socioeconomic status) with 9% less than high school, 18% completed high school, 17% completed some college, 33% completed college, and 23% completed graduate school or a professional degree. Over half of adolescents went to suburban schools (54%), with 22% attending urban schools, 17% attending rural schools, and 8% attending schools in towns.

MEASURES

Geographic location

The school location where the survey was administered, in 2019, served as the measure of rurality. National Center of Education Statistics (NCES) Locale Codes were assigned to each school. The NCES Locale codes use population size and distance to metro areas to differentiate locales using the U.S. Census Bureau's standard urban and rural definitions, core‐based statistical areas, and places (Geverdt, 2015). Three city categories, defined as a territory inside an urbanized area and principal city, were combined into urban; three categories for suburban, defined as a territory outside of a principal city and inside an urbanized area, were combined; two categories for town, defined as a territory inside an urban cluster (UC) that is between 0 and 35 miles from an urbanized area (UA), were combined; and two categories for rural, defined as a census‐defined rural territory that is between 0 and 25 miles from a UA, and a rural territory that is between 0 and 10 miles from a UC, were combined.1 While school location is not an exact approximation of home location, most students appear to live near their schools. The most common commute duration for adolescents traveling to school was between 5 and 10 min (Voulgaris et al., 2019). Therefore, school locale codes indicate the general geographic location of adolescents and provide a more accurate measure of rurality compared to a rural–urban dichotomy based on school county (see Table 1). Geographic location was measured by the NCES school location of the participating adolescent as urban (0), suburban (1), town (2), and rural (3).

Community disorganization

Community disorganization was measured as the mean of five items that asked how adolescents perceived their neighborhood with regards to crime, fights, abandoned buildings, graffiti, and safety on a 4‐point Likert‐type scale ranging from NO! (0) to YES! (3) (e.g., How much do each of the following statements describe your neighborhood: lots of empty or abandoned buildings; Glaser et al., 2005). The question about neighborhood safety, I feel safe in my neighborhood, was reverse coded YES! (0) and NO! (3) prior to calculating the mean scale score. Higher scores indicate more perceived community disorganization by adolescents and subsequently, more risk.

The community disorganization subscale is part of the Communities that Care Youth Survey, a survey developed to assess risk and protective factors across domains reliably among diverse adolescents in the U.S. (Glaser et al., 2005). Prior reliability of the scale ranged from α = .77 to α = .83 (Arthur et al., 2002; Guttmannova et al., 2017). In the current study, Cronbach's alpha was good (α = .82). Convergent validity was supported by significant positive and medium correlations with two scales measuring adolescent perceptions of two community risk factors: perceived availability of substances, r(8924) = 0.23, p < .001, and community norms favorable to substance use, r(8799) = 0.30, p < .001. The 2019 FYSAS did not measure the community protective factors typically included in the Communities that Care Survey (Glaser et al., 2005); however, discriminant validity was supported by low and negative correlations with adolescent reports of participation in organized sports outside of school, r(8971) = −0.02, p = .02, and participation in community clubs, r(8971) = −0.01, p = .42.

Availability of substances

Availability of each of the four substances (i.e., alcohol, marijuana, cigarettes, and illicit drugs) was measured by four items stating: “If you wanted to get [substance], how easy would it be for you to get some?” Responses were measured on a 4‐point Likert‐type scale ranging from very hard (0) to very easy (3). Higher responses indicate adolescent perceptions of greater access to a particular substance and subsequently, more risk.

Norms favorable to substance use

Norms were assessed by three items, for three substances, and asked; “How wrong would most adults in your neighborhood think it was for kids your age to: [alcohol, marijuana, cigarettes].” Responses were measured on a 4‐point Likert‐type scale ranging from very wrong (0) and not wrong at all (3). Higher responses indicate adolescent perceptions of adults viewing substance use in adolescence as normative and subsequently, more risk.

Lack of enforcement of substance use laws

Lack of enforcement of substance use laws were measured by two items for two of the substances: “If a kid [smoked marijuana or drank alcohol] in your neighborhood, would he or she be caught by the police?” Responses were measured on a 4‐point Likert‐type scale and reverse coded to YES! (0) and NO! (3). Higher responses indicate adolescent perceptions of a lack of enforcement of laws related to underage alcohol consumption and marijuana use and subsequently, more risk.

Lifetime substance use

Lifetime use of alcohol, marijuana, cigarette, and illicit drugs was measured by questions that asked adolescents to report the number of occasions that they had used alcohol, marijuana/hashish, and illicit drugs (methamphetamine, Ecstasy, GHB, Rohypnol, ketamine, LSD, PCP, hallucinogenic mushrooms, cocaine, crack, and heroin) in their lifetimes. Lifetime alcohol, marijuana, and illicit drug use were dichotomously coded, no use (0 occasions) and use (1 to 40 occasions). Adolescents were asked to report if they had ever smoked cigarettes. Lifetime cigarette use was dichotomously coded, no use (0, never) and use (1; once or twice, once in a while but not regularly, regularly in the past, or regularly now).

Past 30‐day substance use

Past 30‐day alcohol, marijuana, cigarette and illicit drug use were measured as past 30‐day use of that substance. Adolescents were asked to report the number of occasions they had beer, wine or hard liquor; used marijuana or hashish; or illicit drugs in the past 30 days. Adolescents were also asked to report how frequently they smoked cigarettes during the past 30 days. Past 30‐day alcohol, marijuana, and illicit drug use were dichotomously coded, no use (0 occasions) and use (1 to 40 occasions). Past 30‐day cigarette use was dichotomously coded, no use (not at all) and use (less than one cigarette per day, one to five cigarettes per day, about one‐half pack per day, about one pack per day, about one and one‐half packs per day, two packs or more per day).

Covariates

Age, a categorical control variable, had 10 response options ranging from “10” to “19 or older.” Sex, a dichotomous control variable, was coded female (0) and male (1). Participants reported their racial/ethnic identity using the following response options: “American Indian/Native American or Alaska Native,” “Asian,” “Black/African American,” “Spanish/Hispanic/Latino,” “Native Hawaiian or other Pacific Islander,” “White/Caucasian,” or “Other.” Race/ethnicity was dummy coded so that “1” indicated the selection of a racial and/or ethnic group and “0” corresponded to the reference group composed of all other ethnic/racial groups. Socioeconomic status (SES) was calculated as adolescent report of parents' highest level of education and coded as less than high school (0), completed high school (1), some college (2), completed college (3), and graduate school or professional degree (4).

Data analysis

Descriptive statistics were calculated for all variables. Missingness ranged from 7.9%–8.6% among community risk factor variables and 1% (sex) to 20% (SES) among covariates. Missingness in dependent variables was low for lifetime use of the four substances, ranging from 2.9% for lifetime cigarette use to 10.1% for lifetime illicit drug use. Missingness for past 30‐day use variables ranged from 3.2% for past 30‐day cigarette use to 13.4% for past 30‐day illicit drug use. The researchers determined that imputing missing data for all variables was necessary to reduce potential bias associated with listwise deletion and to increase the usable sample size for the stratified analyses. Imputation is a well‐accepted method for managing missing data that accounts for sampling variability across imputed datasets and results in valid estimates and standard errors in a pooled estimate (Allison, 2002; Little & Rubin, 2002). Two hundred complete datasets were generated to impute missing data on the covariates, predictors, and dependent variables in this study. Graham et al. (2007) recommend at least 100 imputed datasets be generated for regression analyses, especially as computational efficiency improves. Using the IMPUTE command, the 200 datasets were generated in a few minutes to reduce power falloff for detecting small effect sizes (Graham et al., 2007). All variables included in the study (e.g., covariates, community disorganization, availability of substances, norms favorable to substance use, lack of enforcement of substance use laws, lifetime and past 30‐day substance use outcomes) were included in the imputation procedure. The first half of each set of chain iterations in the Bayesian Markov Chain Monte Carlo (MCMC) estimation was discarded as the burn‐in, and the potential scale reduction criteria was met at 1100 iterations.

The substance‐specific logistic regression models were estimated to examine the associations between: (1) community disorganization, (2) availability of the substance, (3) norms favorable to use of that substance, and (4) lack of enforcement of laws related to that substance and lifetime and past 30‐day use of alcohol, marijuana, cigarette, and illicit drugs stratified by geographic location (i.e., urban, suburban, town, rural) on each of the 200 imputed datasets. A total of eight models were estimated. Substance‐specific community risk factors were modeled together to identify patterns of significant associations between risk factors and use of that substance among adolescents living in similarly sized geographic locations, while controlling demographic characteristics known to contribute to substance use behaviors during adolescence. This method was selected to identify the largest effect sizes and significant associations among all community structural and social factors for each substance and use behavior (e.g., lifetime, past 30‐day), similar to other research examining potential differences in associations between risk factors and health outcomes among subgroups of interest (Doherty & Wells, 2025; Redmond et al., 2022). Parameter estimates from each analysis were pooled across all datasets (Rubin, 1987) and pooled estimates are reported in the results. Some racial/ethnic groups represented a small proportion of the sample (e.g., Asian = 4%; Native Hawaiian/Pacific Islander = 1%); thus, when the sample was stratified by geographic location, it resulted in very small cell sizes (<20) within the different substance use models. In order to improve reliability and for confidentiality reasons, cell sizes less than 20 were suppressed in the substance‐specific models (Centers for Disease Control & Prevention, 2026). All regression models adjusted for covariates (i.e., age, sex, race/ethnicity, and SES) and utilized sampling weights. An a priori alpha level of p < .05 was set due to the exploratory nature of the analyses. Multiple imputation and analyses were conducted using Mplus Version 8.10 (Muthén & Muthén, 1998–2023).

Among adolescents residing in urban locations, 39% reported alcohol use, 23% reported marijuana use, 10% reported cigarette use, and 8.3% reported illicit drug use in their lifetimes. Fifteen percent of urban adolescents reported past 30‐day alcohol use, 12% reported past 30‐day marijuana use, 2% reported past 30‐day cigarette use, and 3% reported past 30‐day illicit drug use. Thirty‐six percent of suburban adolescents reported alcohol use, 18% reported marijuana use, 8% reported cigarette use, and 6% reported illicit drug use in their lifetimes. Among suburban adolescents, 14% reported past 30‐day alcohol use, 9% reported past 30‐day marijuana use, 1% reported past 30‐day cigarette use, and 3% reported past 30‐day illicit drug use. Among adolescents residing in towns, 31% reported alcohol use, 13% reported marijuana use, 11% reported cigarette use, and 11% reported illicit drug use in their lifetimes. Fourteen percent of adolescents living in towns reported past 30‐day alcohol use, 8% reported past 30‐day marijuana use, 2% reported past 30‐day cigarette use, and 6% reported past 30‐day illicit drug use. Thirty‐eight percent of rural adolescents reported alcohol use, 22% reported marijuana use, 11% reported cigarette use, and 10% reported illicit drug use in their lifetimes. Among adolescents living in rural areas, 16% reported past 30‐day alcohol use, 10% reported past 30‐day marijuana use, 3% reported past 30‐day cigarette use, and 3% reported past 30‐day illicit drug use.

The high mean scores for laws favorable to alcohol use and laws favorable to marijuana use and the large and significant correlation coefficient indicate that overall, youth perceived that laws related to underage drinking and marijuana use would not be enforced in their communities. All variables representing adolescent perceptions of community risk factors were significantly correlated with each other, with effect sizes ranging from small, in the case of community disorganization and lack of enforcement of marijuana use laws (r(8794) = 0.09, p < .001), to large in the case of norms favorable to alcohol use and norms favorable to marijuana use (r(9011) = 0.70, p < .001).

Community disorganization

Increased adolescent perceptions of community disorganization were significantly associated with increased odds of reporting lifetime and past 30‐day alcohol use among adolescents from towns (lifetime: b = 0.44, S.E. = 0.20, p = .03; past 30‐day: b = 0.62, S.E. = 0.29, p = .03) and rural areas (lifetime: b = 0.42, S.E. = 0.15, p = .005; past 30‐day: b = 0.55, S.E. = 0.19, p = .003), but not adolescents in urban (lifetime: b = 0.22, S.E. = 0.13, p = .08; past 30‐day: b = 0.15, S.E. = 0.16, p = .36) and suburban locales (lifetime: b = 0.12, S.E. = 0.08, p = .15; past 30‐day: b = 0.05, S.E. = 0.11, p = .68). Community disorganization was also significantly associated with lifetime cigarette use among adolescents from suburban areas (b = 0.26, S.E. = 0.12, p = .04) and rural areas (b = 0.70, S.E. = 0.19, p < .001) only. Increased adolescent perceptions of community disorganization were significantly associated with increased odds of reporting lifetime illicit drug use among adolescents from urban (b = 0.39, S.E. = 0.18, p = .03) and suburban (b = 0.48, S.E. = 0.11, p < .001) locales, towns (b = 1.15, S.E. = 0.23, p < .001), and rural areas (b = 0.78, S.E. = 0.20, p < .001). Among adolescents living in suburban areas (b = 0.43, S.E. = 0.17, p = .01), towns (b = 1.58, S.E. = 0.29, p < .001), and rural areas (b = 0.91, S.E. = 0.34, p = .007), increased perceptions of community disorganization were significantly associated with increased odds of reporting past 30‐day illicit drug use.

Availability of substances

Adolescents residing in all geographic locales who reported increased perceptions of ease of access to alcohol, marijuana, and cigarettes had significantly higher odds of reporting lifetime and past 30‐day use of alcohol, marijuana, and cigarettes. Among urban adolescents, increased perceptions of the availability of alcohol were significantly associated with lifetime alcohol use (b = 0.50, S.E. = 0.06, p < .001) and past 30‐day alcohol use (b = 0.51, S.E. = 0.07, p < .001). Urban adolescents with increased perceptions of the availability of marijuana also had higher odds of reporting lifetime (b = 0.86, S.E. = 0.07, p < .001) and past 30‐day (b = 0.83, S.E. = 0.10, p < .001) marijuana use. Significant associations were also found between increased perceptions of the availability of cigarettes and lifetime (b = 0.53, S.E. = 0.07, p < .001) and past 30‐day (b = 0.42, S.E. = 0.18, p = .02) cigarette use among urban adolescents.

Among suburban adolescents, increased perceptions of the availability of alcohol were associated with lifetime alcohol use (b = 0.58, S.E. = 0.04, p < .001) and past 30‐day alcohol use (b = 0.60, S.E. = 0.04, p < .001). Suburban adolescents with increased perceptions of the availability of marijuana also had higher odds of reporting lifetime (b = 1.01, S.E. = 0.05, p < .001) and past 30‐day (b = 1.12, S.E. = 0.08, p < .001) marijuana use. Significant associations were also found between increased perceptions of the availability of cigarettes and lifetime (b = 0.63, S.E. = 0.05, p < .001) and past 30‐day (b = 0.73, S.E. = 0.14, p < .001) cigarette use among suburban adolescents.

Among adolescents living in towns, increased perceptions of the availability of alcohol were associated with lifetime alcohol use (b = 0.45, S.E. = 0.11, p < .001) and past 30‐day alcohol use (b = 0.60, S.E. = 0.04, p < .001). Adolescents from towns with increased perceptions of the availability of marijuana also had higher odds of reporting lifetime (b = 0.78, S.E. = 0.13, p < .001) and past 30‐day (b = 0.77, S.E. = 0.17, p < .001) marijuana use. A significant association was also found between increased perceptions of the availability of cigarettes and lifetime cigarette use (b = 0.65, S.E. = 0.13, p < .001) among adolescents from small towns.

Among rural adolescents, increased perceptions of the availability of alcohol were associated with lifetime alcohol use (b = 0.64, S.E. = 0.06, p < .001) and past 30‐day alcohol use (b = 0.73, S.E. = 0.08, p < .001). Rural adolescents with increased perceptions of the availability of marijuana also had higher odds of reporting lifetime (b = 0.95, S.E. = 0.08, p < .001) and past 30‐day (b = 1.04, S.E. = 0.12, p < .001) marijuana use. Significant associations were also found between increased perceptions of the availability of cigarettes and lifetime (b = 0.43, S.E. = 0.08, p < .001) and past 30‐day (b = 0.84, S.E. = 0.18, p < .001) cigarette use among rural adolescents.

The perceived availability of illicit drugs was associated with increased odds of reporting past 30‐day and lifetime illicit drug use among adolescents from suburban locales (lifetime: b = 0.64, S.E. = 0.06, p < .001; past 30‐day: b = 0.64, S.E. = 0.08, p < .001), towns, (lifetime: b = 0.54, S.E. = 0.15, p < .001; past 30‐day: b = 0.42, S.E. = 0.20, p = .03), and rural areas (lifetime: b = 0.56, S.E. = 0.10, p < .001; past 30‐day: b = 0.63, S.E. = 0.15, p < .001), while the increased perception of illicit drug availability was significantly associated with lifetime illicit drug use only for adolescents in urban locations (b = 0.62, S.E. = 0.08, p < .001).

Norms favorable to substance use

Increased adolescent perceptions that adults in the community find adolescent substance use acceptable were significantly associated with increased odds of reporting past 30‐day use of alcohol, marijuana, and cigarettes among adolescents from all geographic locales. Specifically, increased adolescent perceptions of norms favorable to alcohol use were significantly associated with lifetime and past 30‐day alcohol use among adolescents from urban (lifetime: b = 0.47, S.E. = 0.08, p < .001; past 30‐day: b = 0.58, S.E. = 0.09, p < .001), suburban (lifetime: b = 0.46, S.E. = 0.05, p < .001; past 30‐day: b = 0.43, S.E. = 0.06, p < .001), town (lifetime: b = 0.51, S.E. = 0.13, p < .001; past 30‐day: b = 0.62, S.E. = 0.15, p < .001), and rural (lifetime: b = 0.44, S.E. = 0.10, p < .001; past 30‐day: b = 0.46 S.E. = 0.12, p < .001) locales. Adolescent perceptions that adolescent marijuana use is acceptable among adults in their community were also associated with increased lifetime and past 30‐day use of marijuana among adolescents living in urban (lifetime: b = 0.38, S.E. = 0.08, p < .001; past 30‐day: b = 0.30, S.E. = 0.10, p = .002), suburban (lifetime: b = 0.35, S.E. = 0.06, p < .001; past 30‐day: b = 0.41, S.E. = 0.07, p < .001), town (lifetime: b = 0.85, S.E. = 0.17, p < .001; past 30‐day: b = 0.86, S.E. = 0.20, p < .001), and rural (lifetime: b = 0.45, S.E. = 0.10, p < .001; past 30‐day: b = 0.60 S.E. = 0.11, p < .001) locales. Increased adolescent perceptions of norms favorable to cigarette use was significantly associated with lifetime cigarette use among adolescents living in urban (b = 0.42, S.E. = 0.11, p < .001), suburban (b = 0.30, S.E. = 0.08, p < .001), and town locales (b = 0.37, S.E. = 0.18, p = .04), but not rural areas (b = 0.11, S.E. = 0.12, p = .37). Past 30‐day cigarette use was also significantly associated with norms favorable to cigarette use among urban (b = 0.65, S.E. = 0.22, p = .004), suburban (b = 0.59, S.E. = 0.14, p < .001), town (b = 0.67, S.E. = 0.30, p = .02), and rural (b = 0.44, S.E. = 0.19, p = .02) adolescents.

Lack of enforcement of substance use laws

Increased adolescent perceptions of a lack of enforcement of underage drinking laws was significantly associated with lifetime and past 30‐day alcohol use among adolescents from urban (lifetime: b = 0.29, S.E. = 0.07, p < .001; past 30‐day: b = 0.32, S.E. = 0.10, p = .002), suburban (lifetime: b = 0.25, S.E. = 0.04, p < .001; past 30‐day: b = 0.30, S.E. = 0.06, p < .001), town (lifetime: b = 0.56, S.E. = 0.11, p < .001; past 30‐day: b = 0.28, S.E. = 0.18, p = .03), and rural (lifetime: b = 0.29, S.E. = 0.08, p < .001; past 30‐day: b = 0.31 S.E. = 0.12, p = .009) locales. Associations between lack of enforcement of marijuana use laws and marijuana use were non‐significant for adolescents living in towns (lifetime: b = 0.25, S.E. = 0.20, p = .21; past 30‐day: b = 0.13 S.E. = 0.27, p = .62) and rural (lifetime: b = −0.05, S.E. = 0.10, p = .60; past 30‐day: b = 0.03 S.E. = 0.14, p = .80) areas. Among urban adolescents, perceptions that marijuana use laws would not be enforced was significantly associated with increased odds of reporting past 30‐day marijuana use (b = 0.31 S.E. = 0.11, p = .004). For suburban adolescents, increased perceptions that marijuana use laws would not be enforced was significantly associated with increased odds of reporting lifetime marijuana use (b = 0.15 S.E. = 0.07, p = .03).

DISCUSSION

This study evaluated patterns of associations between adolescent perceptions of community risk factors and lifetime and past 30‐day substance use among adolescents in urban, suburban, town, and rural communities while controlling for age, sex, race/ethnicity and SES. In this study, rural adolescents reported the highest rates of past 30‐day alcohol use and lifetime and past 30‐day cigarette use, while youth from towns reported the highest rates of lifetime and past 30‐day illicit drug use. Urban adolescents reported the highest rates of lifetime alcohol use and lifetime and past 30‐day marijuana use. These geographic differences in rates support the continued use of discrete measures of geographic location to identify substances of concern in more distinct geographical contexts. Results also demonstrate that there are some distinct patterns of associations between perceived community risk factors and lifetime and past 30‐day use of different substances among adolescents residing along the rural–urban continuum. These differential associations point to the importance of examining how adolescent perceptions of community risk factors are related to substance use behaviors in different geographic locations and will be summarized and examined in this discussion.

Community disorganization represented adolescent perceptions of how structural features of their communities influence public social processes (e.g., fighting, safety, crime, drug selling, graffiti). Results showed that adolescent perceptions of community disorganization were significantly associated with increased odds of reporting lifetime and past 30‐day alcohol use, but only among adolescents from towns and rural areas. In contrast, adolescents perceiving high levels of community disorganization also had significantly higher odds of reporting lifetime cigarette use in suburban and rural locales. Prior research has documented lower perceptions of community disorganization among rural compared to urban adolescents (Brevard et al., 2013). In addition, community disorganization appears to be strongly associated with illicit drug use among adolescents. There were increased odds of reporting lifetime and past 30‐day illicit drug use among adolescents living in suburban, town, and rural areas and lifetime illicit drug use among adolescents living in urban areas when perceived community disorganization was high. Perhaps adolescents in towns and rural areas are seeing the impacts of rural out migration in the closing of businesses, reduction of services, and loss of social capital and perceiving more physical signs of community disorganization (Li et al., 2019), placing them at greater risk for alcohol and cigarette use than their more urban counterparts. This also suggests that addressing social problems (e.g., crime, fighting) and structural appearances (e.g., abandoned buildings, graffiti) in communities of all sizes may be important for preventing illicit drug use among adolescents.

The significant associations between community disorganization and adolescent alcohol, cigarette, and illicit drug use found in this study are contrary to prior research that found community disorganization and neighborhood factors were not associated with adolescent substance use (Fagan et al., 2015; Van Horn et al., 2007). This discrepancy may be explained by the measurement of community disorganization. Adolescent perceptions of their communities may be better indicators of exposure to community risk (Byrnes et al., 2006) and are more closely associated with their individual outcomes (Pratt et al., 2020). Future examinations of community influences on adolescent substance use behaviors should employ the conceptual environment‐place framework (Nicotera, 2007) to measure the community as both an environment and place. This more holistic measurement will be critical for further explorations of the significance of community disorganization in adolescent substance use in communities more distant from urban centers, where community disorganization may be more visible due to persistently higher rural poverty rates (Farrigan et al., 2023).

The perceived availability of substances was associated with increased odds both lifetime and past 30‐day use of all substances (i.e., alcohol, marijuana, cigarettes, and illicit drugs) among adolescents from all geographic locales. For rural and town adolescents, the significant associations between perceived availability of cigarettes and both lifetime and past 30‐day cigarette use are unsurprising based on the well‐documented disparities in cigarette smoking in rural compared to urban areas and the likelihood for increased access when adults in the community also smoke (Doogan et al., 2017; Erath et al., 2025; Kim & Selya, 2022; Pesko & Robarts, 2017). With the legalization of recreational marijuana use across the U.S. and growing numbers of dispensaries in communities of all sizes, perceptions of accessibility may be rapidly changing across geographic locations. This could contribute to increased adolescent perceptions of the availability of marijuana and significant associations with both lifetime and past 30‐day use among adolescents from all geographic locales. Research is needed to build our understanding of how legalization and geographic distribution of dispensaries impacts perceptions of accessibility and subsequent use behaviors among adolescents in communities of all sizes (Hinckley & Hopfer, 2021; Paschall & Grube, 2020).

Among adolescents across the rural–urban continuum, perceived availability of alcohol was significantly associated with both lifetime and past 30‐day alcohol use. Research demonstrates consistent links between the density of alcohol outlets and adolescent alcohol use (Shih et al., 2015), while total resident population is positively associated with the number of alcohol outlets in rural areas (Morrison, 2015). In this sample, density may be more consistent, and therefore perceptions similar across these geographic locales than seen in previous research (Warren et al., 2015). The findings in this study in regard to perceived availability and illicit drug use are also consistent with previous research that found that reports of neighborhood illicit drug selling were associated with more illicit drug use in a nationally representative sample of adolescents (Duncan et al., 2014). Together, these findings suggest that regulatory interventions that reduce access and availability of substances, such as controls on price through taxation, enforcement of minimum age laws, and enforcing retail outlet compliance with minimum age laws, are effective strategies to reduce adolescent substance use (Toumbourou et al., 2007) and should be explored by local governments in all sizes of communities.

Perceived norms favorable to the use of alcohol, marijuana, and cigarettes were significantly associated with increased odds of reporting lifetime and past 30‐day alcohol, marijuana, and cigarette use among adolescents from all geographic locales, with the exception of lifetime cigarette use for adolescents from rural areas. While strong associations between norms and substance use is unsurprising, it is interesting that norms favorable to cigarette use were also strong predictors of those behaviors among urban and suburban adolescents, where adolescent and adult rates of cigarette smoking are lower (Doogan et al., 2017). These findings support the need to work with adults in communities across the rural–urban continuum to address the perception that adolescent substance use is acceptable. Past research found that in rural communities with small, close social networks, parents, relatives, and other adults communicated mixed messages around substance use (e.g., I can use alcohol, but you cannot) and, at times, actively provided or encouraged substance use (Pettigrew et al., 2012). These messages and behaviors may contribute to cultures in rural communities that may normalize adolescent substance use; however, there remains limited research about how to change these norms through community‐level interventions (Das et al., 2016). Future research should explore how norms are shaped in different geographic contexts to inform mass media campaigns and tailored community‐based interventions that work with adults to address substance use norms.

Findings demonstrate that the perceptions that adolescents would be caught by police drinking or using marijuana were low. These perceptions that adolescents would not be caught by police drinking underage were significantly associated with lifetime and past 30‐day alcohol use among adolescents in all geographic locales. However, the associations between lack of enforcement of marijuana use laws were less strong and inconsistent. Inconsistent enforcement and sanctions for underage drinking laws across communities supports this finding (Calvert et al., 2020; Hafemeister & Jackson, 2004). However, calling for an increase in enforcement of these laws may increase the criminalization of youth, which could make them more likely to engage in hazardous drinking in the future (Friesen et al., 2022). Therefore, policies focused on adults in the community, such as social host liability laws and administrative penalties for outlets found selling substances to adolescents, are documented approaches for reducing adolescent alcohol use and should be investigated for their potential effects on other substances (Lynne‐Landsman & Wagenaar, 2015).

Strengths and limitations

Strengths of this study include the analysis of a large, diverse sample using a novel approach to investigate differences in associations between adolescent perceptions of community risk factors and adolescent use of four substances among geographic subgroups of adolescents. However, findings should be interpreted considering several limitations. First, this study analyzed cross‐sectional data, preventing any causal interpretations of community risk factor contributions to adolescent substance use behaviors. Second, the measure of rurality used school location and not home location, which could misrepresent the rurality of participants' homes compared to their schools. Future research should examine rurality based on both adolescent home location and their perceptions of the rurality in which they live to more precisely measure the influence of community risk factors in different geographic locales. Third, this study examined cigarette smoking in order to match risk factors with substance use behaviors and did not include nicotine vaping, which has increased among adolescents in recent years (Pierce et al., 2023). Although the 2019 FYSAS Survey did ask about nicotine vaping, the questions about adolescent perceptions of norms and availability were specific to cigarettes and conceptually did not fit with this outcome. Fourth, the measure of illicit drug use had to be dichotomized to cover any illicit drugs due to the low prevalence of reported use. Others have carefully characterized non‐medical prescription drug use and opioid use along the rural–urban continuum (Havens et al., 2011). This study is unable to provide such specificity. Fifth, there may be same source bias present as adolescents were reporting their perceptions of community risk factors and their substance use behaviors at the same time (Podsakoff et al., 2024). This could potentially bias the results, as an adolescent's substance use behaviors may shape their perceptions of community substance availability and norms. Sixth, due to small cell sizes in stratified models racial/ethnic groups were suppressed in models, limiting understanding of substance use among these racial/ethnic groups in different geographic locales. Seventh, this study was unable to control for adolescent gender identity and sexual orientation due to survey constraints (i.e., they did not ask these questions). There is evidence of health disparities in substance use with more negative outcomes among sexual and gender minority adolescents (Gerke et al., 2024). Finally, these data were collected in 2019, prior to the COVID‐19 pandemic and subsequent declines in adolescent substance use rates nationally (Miech et al., 2025). Exploratory results should be interpreted with these limitations in mind.

CONCLUSIONS

Among adolescents from different geographic locations, patterns of associations between adolescent perceptions of social and structural community risk factors and substance use behaviors had similarities and unique differences. Longitudinal research is necessary to understand the underlying processes that may be contributing to the patterns observed in associations between adolescent perceptions of community risk and their substance use behaviors, such as those observed for community disorganization among adolescents from towns and rural areas. This research should include multiple measures of community risk and protective factors that consider both perceptions of social processes and structural conditions (e.g., adolescent and adult perceptions of structural characteristics of their communities, adolescent and adult perceptions of social processes in their communities), in addition to objective measures of structural conditions (e.g., poverty, employment, crime, spatial data of alcohol outlet and marijuana dispensary density) for a holistic assessment of community. Community risk processes are unique in geographic locations and further longitudinal research is needed to inform community‐level interventions that can be tailored for implementation across the rural–urban continuum.
