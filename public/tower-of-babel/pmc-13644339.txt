Ontogeny and Identity of Tissue Macrophages in Human Aging
Authors: Matthias Nahrendorf
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Corresponding Author

Two studies published this year report that a substantial minority of microglia in the aged human brain are derived from bone marrow. The result deserves attention in this journal, and for a reason worth stating plainly. The framework we use to think about cardiac macrophages was largely built in the brain. Embryonic origin, maintenance by local self-renewal, and limited input from adult hematopoiesis were established for microglia first, and cardiac immunology adopted these concepts afterwards. Our field has borrowed from the microglial literature more often than it has contributed to it. When that literature revises itself, we should take notice.

Belk et al1 made use of a feature of aged hematopoiesis for tracking leukocytes to the brain. Hematopoietic stem cells accumulate somatic mutations throughout life, most of them neutral. When a clone expands, the mutations its founding cell carry rise in frequency and become detectable in blood cell progeny. Those same mutations can then be sought elsewhere in the body. Applied to 24 aged donors, the approach identified marrow-derived macrophage clones in every brain examined, with a median of approximately one-quarter of microglia carrying them.

Huang et al2 reached a similar conclusion by an unrelated route. They sequenced 149 cancer driver genes to high depth in 311 prefrontal cortex samples from the ROSMAP cohort, sorted nuclei using an antibody against CSF1R, and measured variant allele fractions in the sorted populations. Driver variants were enriched in brain macrophages relative to neurons by factors ranging from 2-fold to several-100-fold. For 3 variants, the allele fractions indicated that mutant cells constituted more than one-fifth of all CSF1R-positive nuclei. The same variants were present in matched blood and were distributed across 4 cortical regions and the cerebellum. Single-nucleus genotyping of more than 8,000 macrophage nuclei confirmed the enrichment. The 2 studies share no methodology and were designed to answer somewhat different questions. That they converge on similar magnitudes of monocyte contribution to microglia is a strong reason to take the finding seriously.

The result appeared surprising because of 2 influential experiments that, back in the day, challenged the notion that all tissue macrophages derive from monocytes. Ginhoux et al3 used genetic fate mapping in mice to establish that adult microglia descend from progenitors arising in the yolk sac before embryonic day 8. Ajami et al,4 using parabiosis, found essentially no contribution of circulating monocytes to the microglial pool. Based on these mouse data, microglia consequently became the canonical example of a tissue macrophage population that adult hematopoiesis does not reach.

It is worth discussing what "adult" meant in these mouse experiments. In the fate-mapping study, labeling was induced at approximately embryonic day 7, and the adult readout was taken 8 weeks after birth.3 The parabiosis experiments ran over comparably short intervals.4 The sealed compartment was established in young adult animals.

Our own measurements in myocardium illustrate why this matters. Using parabiosis, we found chimerism below 3% in cardiac macrophages at 6 weeks, against more than 30% in blood monocytes.5 On that measurement, the heart macrophage population also appears relatively closed to peripheral monocyte input.

The comparison requires 2 corrections, however. Both parabiosis and mutation-based lineage tracing report the current composition of the macrophage pool. What differs between them is the interval over which labelled cells could have entered: several weeks in the parabiosis experiments, against the whole period during which a clone was large enough to contribute detectably. The parabiosis figure also requires normalization, because partner-derived cells constituted only about one-third of the blood monocyte pool. A chimerism below 3% in cardiac macrophages therefore corresponds to roughly 8% of the pool having been replaced from the circulation within 6 weeks. Read in that way, the cardiac data were never consistent with a completely closed compartment. Cumulative composition is not, however, a simple sum of influx, because resident and incoming cells both divide.

Molawi et al6 provided the direct test in the heart. Following mice across a substantial fraction of their lifespan rather than a few weeks of it, they found progressive replacement of embryo-derived cardiac macrophages with age. The shift may have occurred as the self-renewal capacity of the embryonically derived population declined, rather than through any increase in monocyte recruitment. Composition may have changed because the resident cells stopped keeping pace. The heart macrophage compartment is not sealed but slowly permeable, and short experiments cannot detect this. Duration of observation, rather than a difference between species, may therefore account for much of what has appeared to distinguish the brain from other organs.

A second question is more interesting and remains genuinely open. It is well established that macrophage phenotype is shaped strongly by the tissue microenvironment, and that lineage origin and functional state are distinct properties that need not correspond. Neither point is in dispute. It follows that the direction of the microglial result is unsurprising: cells entering the brain parenchyma would be expected to acquire much of the local program. The unresolved question is quantitative. How much origin dependence persists after the niche has acted, and does the answer differ between tissues?

Cardiac data speak to this directly, and they run against the simplest expectation. In sex-mismatched transplant recipients biopsied more than 1 year after surgery—in a work from Kory Lavine's laboratory, on which I was a coauthor—CCR2-negative macrophages remained donor-derived and were replenished by local proliferation, while CCR2-positive macrophages were recipient-derived.7 A full complement of recipient monocytes circulated throughout that period. The resident compartment nonetheless retained its ancestry, its marker phenotype and its function. Niche instruction was evidently incomplete.

The brain literature is not unanimous on the phenotype either. Belk et al1 report that incoming cells become difficult to distinguish from resident microglia. Huang et al2 find classical microglial markers expressed, but observe that mutant cells adopt a disease-associated state in Alzheimer's brains and not in control brains. Silvin et al8 describe distinct ontogenies for disease-associated microglia and for inflammatory macrophages.

Whether the apparent difference between organs reflects assay sensitivity, the particular conditions of transplantation, or a real difference in how instructive the 2 niches are cannot be settled with the present data. Two cautions are worth recording: chromatin accessibility establishes cell class more reliably than it demonstrates equivalence between populations, and CCR2 expression in human myocardium may reflect activation state as well as lineage.

The disease association requires separate comment, because the 2 brain studies appear to disagree. Bouzid et al9 reported that clonal hematopoiesis is associated with protection from Alzheimer's disease, and Belk et al1 report the same direction. Huang et al2 report the opposite, namely an increased burden of driver variants in Alzheimer's brains, with higher allele fractions and stronger evidence of positive selection.

The discrepancy appears to be a function of variant allele fraction rather than a genuine contradiction. Jakubek et al10 found a negative association when analysis was restricted to variants above 8%, and a nonsignificant positive association when the threshold was lowered to 2%. Huang et al2 report the same reversal within their own data: among deleterious variants in clonal hematopoiesis genes, the OR falls below 1 at above approximately 4%.

The methodological point is more important than the epidemiological one. Assays differ in their detection thresholds, and here the threshold appears to determine the sign of the association. Methods that require clonal expansion to detect a lineage sample only the upper part of the distribution. This is reason for caution before drawing causal conclusions from either result, and it applies equally to the protective and the harmful direction.

An obvious next cardiac study could be performed with existing material. Passenger-based lineage tracing requires only blood and archival tissue.1 Applied to aged native human myocardium, with CCR2-negative and -positive macrophages sorted separately, it would establish whether marrow-derived cells accumulate in the resident cardiac compartment across a human lifespan. The heart transplant data establish what 1 year under allograft conditions shows.7 Several decades of time in a native heart has not been examined, and can now be examined without transplantation, irradiation, or immunosuppression as confounders. Either result would be informative. If the CCR2-negative compartment holds, the myocardium maintains a resident population that decades of exposure to marrow-derived cells does not replace. If it does not hold, the difference between organs is one of degree rather than of kind.

Macrophage origin, marker identity, and functional state are 3 separate questions, and the field has often treated them as related. The useful work now lies in measuring how tightly they are coupled in each tissue, rather than in establishing that they can come apart.
