Frequency-angle decoupling design for grid-etched piezoelectric MEMS cantilevers and its application to quasi-static micromirrors
Authors: Wenli Xue, Yongquan Su, Wanzhu Qiao, Yichen Liu, Yi Yang, Hongfeng Zhao, Pengcheng Zhang, Gencai Shen, Hao Chen, Yang Wang, Zhenyu Wu, Lihao Wang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Micro-electro-mechanical systems (MEMS) cantilever actuators are pivotal in applications ranging from micro-optics to precision manipulation. However, their performance is fundamentally constrained by a trade-off between dynamic response and static deformation. To address this limitation, this paper presents a segmented grid-etched technique for MEMS cantilever actuators that decouples the resonant frequency from the tip inclination angle and displacement. The proposed approach strategically modulates the stiffness and mass distribution along the cantilever by etching grid patterns on the segment near the free end, thereby creating a compliant zone for large deformation while preserving the stiffness of the segment near the fixed end. The proposed cantilevers were fabricated using a Cavity Silicon-On-Insulator (SOI)-based Single Piezoelectric Layer Double Release (C-SSD) process. Experimental results show that the fabricated cantilevers achieve a 103% increase in tip inclination angle and a 54% increase in tip displacement, while maintaining a resonant frequency above that of a conventional uniform cantilever. Additionally, the practical application of this approach is demonstrated in a biaxial piezoelectric micromirror that integrates resonant motion for the fast horizontal axis and quasi-static motion for the slow vertical axis. Notably, the grid-etched structure on the slow axis enhances the optical scan angle by 257% and the frequency–angle product by 64%, achieving a field of view (FOV) of 25°. Furthermore, the successful implementation of this grid-etched cantilever structure in a micromirror validates its potential as a general-purpose design strategy for a wide range of MEMS cantilever actuators.

Introduction

Micro-Electro-Mechanical Systems (MEMS) actuators are core components that convert diverse energy forms, such as electrical and thermal, into mechanical motion. They are widely employed in advanced industries, including consumer electronics, automotive systems, healthcare and biotechnology, optical communication, and aerospace. Among these, cantilever-based actuators offer a simple structure and ease of fabrication, making them an ideal platform for implementing diverse physical actuation mechanisms, such as piezoelectric, electrothermal, electrostatic, and electromagnetic actuation. For instance, in micro-optical systems, cantilever actuators are widely used in scanning micromirrors1,2, and microlens3, enabling precise beam control. In precision micro-nano manipulation, cantilever-based devices, such as microgrippers and microcages4,5, exhibit exceptional manipulation capabilities. Furthermore, in radio frequency (RF) communications, cantilevers are the core components of RF-MEMS switches6,7, and phase shifters8. Additionally, in nanofabrication, cantilever probe arrays play a critical role9,10. This widespread applicability underscores the significance of the cantilever structure in MEMS actuators.

The performance of cantilever actuators is constrained by a fundamental physical trade-off derived from classical beam theory: the balance between resonant frequency and static deformation. To enhance deformation capability, current research primarily follows two technical pathways.

The first approach focuses on increasing the driving force F. For different actuation mechanisms, researchers have developed specific enhancement strategies and encountered corresponding physical constraints, as summarized in Table 1. For instance, in electrothermal cantilever actuators, materials with a high coefficient of thermal expansion (CTE) mismatch—such as Al/SiO₂4, Cu/W12, SU8/graphene13, and PDMS/graphene14, are selected to amplify thermal bending. Additionally, others have improved temperature distribution and actuation efficiency by embedding heaters (e.g., Pt4), and optimizing thermal paths (e.g., using fishbone-shaped resistor structures10). For piezoelectric cantilever actuators, high piezoelectric coefficient materials like PZT15, or doping strategies (e.g., Nb-doped PZT16, Sc-doped AlN17) are employed to enhance the piezoelectric response. Additionally, bimorph structures are also utilized to generate larger bending moments18,19. For electromagnetic actuators, driving torque is enhanced through optimized magnetic circuit design, the use of larger or stronger magnets, and increasing the number of coil turns20,21. Regarding electrostatic cantilever actuators, the electrostatic force is typically enhanced by reducing the gap between the cantilever electrode and the underlying fixed electrode or increasing the actuation electrode area6–8. However, as summarized in Table 1, merely increasing the driving force encounters theoretical constraints. Piezoelectric actuation is limited by intrinsic material properties, such as the piezoelectric coefficient and breakdown field strength17. For electrothermal actuation, the constraints are dictated by the material’s CTE and thermal conductivity22. In the case of electrostatic actuation, challenges stem from the dielectric breakdown strength and pull-in phenomenon under high voltages23. For electromagnetic actuation, performance is constrained by the maximum driving current and the fundamental properties of the magnetic materials21. The second widely adopted strategy involves reducing the structural stiffness k to achieve larger displacement under the same driving force. Researchers have developed a series of universal stiffness optimization methods. These strategies include employing multi-segment serial beam structures to increase the effective length within a limited space, often combined with electrode optimization for large deformation12,16,24–38. Additional approaches involve localized reduction of cantilever thickness25,27,39–41, optimization of thickness ratios of film layers1,26,42, or the use of low Young’s modulus polymer materials to minimize bending stiffness while maximizing displacement20,21. Moreover, hollowed structures are designing to reduce the stiffness along the bending direction and residual stress is utilized to induce an initial large deformation4,5. The use of non-uniform beams, such as tapered-width beams6,43 and triangular beams8, creates stiffness gradients that promote increased deflection. Additionally, incorporating flexible spring architectures can further reduce local stiffness and enhance actuation displacement1,3,7,23,44–54. Nevertheless, all these methods confront a fundamental limitation by globally reducing stiffness, which inevitably compromises resonant frequency. Although some aforementioned studies have attempted to optimize performance by tailoring local mechanical properties, they have not systematically applied this concept to address the core trade-off between frequency and deformation in cantilevers. To overcome this limitation, we propose a structural decoupling strategy that operates not by globally altering stiffness or force, but by spatially redistributing stiffness and mass along the cantilever.

This study focuses on the development of a frequency–angle/displacement decoupled MEMS cantilever structure using a segmented grid-etched technique. The decoupling principle exploits an asymmetric dynamic response in cantilevers, where resonant frequency depends strongly on root stiffness and free-end mass. Selective grid etching confined to the free end reduces local mass to benefit frequency and increases local compliance to enhance static deformation, thereby breaking the classical trade-off. This systematic decoupling is achieved through a co-design of theory, structure, and fabrication: by establishing the spatial stiffness-mass decoupling as the guiding principle, implementing a grid-etched structure within a standard-thickness (40 µm) device layer, and innovatively applying the Cavity Silicon-On-Insulator (C-SOI) process to actively apply the compliance in the device layer. The paper is organized as follows: Section “Design concept” introduces the design concept, beginning with a theoretical analysis of the frequency-deformation trade-off and the decoupling principle, followed by finite-element simulation and optimization of uniform, fully etched, and segmented grid-etched cantilevers, including the influence of etch duty ratio. Section “Fabrication” details the fabrication process based on CSOI technology. Section “Test results” presents the experimental characterization results of the fabricated cantilevers, validating the proposed design. Furthermore, section “Application to quasi-static micromirror” demonstrates the application of the grid-etched cantilevers in a biaxial micromirror, with performance comparisons and a 2D scan demonstration. Finally, section “Conclusion” summarizes the findings and suggests potential directions for future work.

Design concept

This study aims to design a highly efficient cantilever actuation structure that decouples resonant frequency from static deformation through localized structural modulation. A piezoelectric-driven MEMS cantilever beam was employed due to its high linearity, low power consumption, rapid response, straightforward operational modes, and compact design55,56. To provide a theoretical foundation for the subsequent simulation analysis, and to elucidate the observed decoupling mechanism, a simplified analytical model is developed in the following subsection.

Finite-element simulation and design evolution

Guided by the theoretical analysis, a series of finite-element analysis (FEA) were performed to obtain precise numerical performance data, validate the decoupling principle, and optimize the design parameters for fabrication. As depicted in Fig. 1b, one end of the cantilever is fixed, and the surface is covered with a 1.5 µm Al0.8Sc0.2N piezoelectric film60. This material was selected because it offers a higher piezoelectric coefficient than AlN while maintaining better linearity than PZT61. The tip displacement Dtip is defined as the vertical displacement of the piezoelectric film’s free-end point. The tip inclination angle θ is extracted from the local slope of the simulated deformation profile at this point. Additionally, the detailed simulation and material parameters are provided in Supplementary Note 8.

To systematically evaluate the design strategy, seven cantilever variants were created and categorized into three groups as shown in Fig. 1c. Group 1 comprises uniform and fully grid-etched designs to establish the fundamental performance trade-off. Group 2 investigates segmented etching at different locations to validate decoupling. Group 3 examines the influence of etch duty ratio on performance.

The uniform cantilever design G1-U1 serves as the baseline. All designs adhere to the following base dimensions: total length L = 3 mm, width W = 0.3 mm, silicon device layer thickness T = 40 μm. For grid-etched designs, the etched segment is defined by two geometric parameters: A, the distance from the fixed end to the start of the grid structures (mm), and B, the length of the grid-etched region (mm). The etch geometry uses a fixed depth t = 20 μm, fixed gap b = 20 μm, and varied trench width a to achieve the desired duty ratio η. Simulation analysis of uniform and fully grid-etched cantilevers (Group 1)

FEA results for Group 1 confirm the classical performance trade-off. Figure 1d1,d2,d3 depict the dependence of the tip inclination angle on the geometric parameters L, W, and T for uniform beams, consistent with the theoretical trends. Entire thinning of the beam, design G1-U2, triples the tip inclination angle relative to baseline G1-U1 but halves the resonant frequency.

Figure 1d4 shows that grid etching along the entire beam produces the same trade-off where deeper etching increases the angle while reducing frequency. With 20 µm etch depth, design G1-FE performs nearly identically to G1-U2. Figure 1d5 investigates tip inclination angle and frequency versus etch duty ratio, showing variations within 20% for both parameters.

Segmented grid-etched cantilever design for decoupling (Group 2)

Segmented grid etching reveals a fundamental difference. While both designs significantly increase tip inclination angle, their frequency responses diverge. The root grid-etched design G2-S1 suffers severe frequency reduction, following the classical trade-off. Conversely, the free-end grid-etched design G2-SO achieves decoupling by providing substantial static gain while maintaining resonant frequency above the baseline level. This directly validates the decoupling principle from section “Two-segment cantilever and the decoupling mechanism”. Furthermore, a parametric sweep of A, where B = 3 − A, identifies the optimal etch length. Figure 1e1 shows that when A ≥ 1.5 mm, the frequency exceeds that of the baseline, while the angle is always higher due to the introduced compliance at the free end. The comprehensive performance f·θ peaks at A = 1.5 mm as shown in Fig. 1e2, confirming this as the optimal choice. Therefore, G2-SO is selected as the optimal decoupling design.

Grid parameter analysis (Group 3)

To finalize the fabrication process parameters for segmented grid etching, a comprehensive investigation was conducted on the influence of both etch depth and duty ratio for the optimal free-end location. Figure 1f1 shows that at 66% duty ratio, tip inclination angle increases with etch depth while frequency remains above baseline for depths up to 22 μm. Furthermore, f·θ exhibits monotonic improvement with etch depth as shown in Fig. 1f2. Considering process reliability, an etch depth of 20 µm was selected to avoid membrane fracture risk while maintaining effective decoupling.

At this fixed etched depth, grid etch duty ratios of 50% and higher consistently enable decoupling, with both tip inclination angle and frequency exceeding baseline values as shown in Fig. 1f3. Additionally, Fig. 1f4 shows that higher duty ratios improve the overall performance f·θ. The 66% duty ratio, corresponding to a trench width of 40 µm, was chosen as it balances performance with manufacturability, avoiding the uniform etching challenges of narrower trenches and the risk of device-layer membrane fracture of wider ones. Designs G3-D1 and G3-D2 were included for comparison.

Fabrication

The fabrication of the proposed cantilevers is based on a C-SOI process, with a key innovation in its application. Traditionally, C-SOI technology is utilized to pre-form cavities in the handle layer62,63, or in the buried oxide64. In this work, the pre-formed cavity is directly applied to the device layer. This approach enables localized mechanical compliance by etching a grid pattern of partial-depth cavities into the device layer wafer prior to bonding. Consequently, the decoupling structure is realized using a 40 µm standard-thickness device layer.

The grid-etched piezoelectric MEMS cantilevers are fabricated using a C-SOI-based Single piezoelectric layer Double release (C-SSD) process65, which consists of two primary stages: the preparation of the cavity SOI wafer and the fabrication of the piezoelectric cantilever.

C-SOI wafer preparation

The schematic process flow for preparing the cavity SOI wafer is illustrated in Fig. 2a. (1) The process begins with two 500 µm thick bare silicon wafers, serving as the device layer and handle layer, respectively. (2) A 20 µm deep reactive ion etch (DRIE) is conducted on the device layer wafer, using a conventional Bosch process, to create the grid patterns. (3) A 1 µm thick silicon dioxide (SiO2) layer is thermally grown on both sides of the handle layer wafer. (4) The device layer and handle layer wafers are then processed by silicon direct bonding. The bonding is performed at 50 °C under a vacuum of 10⁻⁵ mbar with an applied force of 10 kN for 10 min, followed by an annealing step at 1100 °C to enhance bond strength. (5) After bonding, the device layer of the bonded wafer pair is thinned to 40 µm via mechanical grinding using a grinding system (Tokyo Seimitsu HRG300), followed by chemical mechanical polishing (CMP) to achieve a smooth, uniformly thick device layer. The resulting cavity SOI wafer consists of a 500 µm thick handle layer, a 1 µm thick buried oxide (BOX) layer, and a 40 µm thick device layer containing 20 µm deep patterned cavities. The bonding quality is assessed using C-mode scanning acoustic microscopy (C-SAM; SAM 400, PVA TePla), as shown in Fig. 2b. The bonded regions exhibit high acoustic wave transmission, appearing as dark areas, while the cavity regions reflect the waves, resulting in bright areas. Minor bubbling is observed near the wafer edge, but this does not impact subsequent device fabrication as the device areas are fully bonded with high quality. Additionally, no undesired bonding is detected within the cavity regions. Piezoelectric cantilevers fabrication

After the preparation of the cavity SOI wafer, the cantilevers are fabricated by the process as illustrated in Fig. 2d. (1) The process begins with the thermal growth of a 1 µm thick SiO₂ on the wafer surface. (2) Subsequently, a molybdenum (Mo) bottom electrode (200 nm) and a Mo top electrode (200 nm) are deposited by magnetron sputtering, while the intermediate piezoelectric layer of AlScN (1.5 µm) is deposited by reactive magnetron sputtering. (3) These functional layers were then patterned using reactive ion etching (RIE) to define the top and bottom electrodes and the piezoelectric actuator regions. (4) Following this, a 600 nm thick passivation layer consisting of 300 nm SiO₂ and 300 nm Si₃N₄, was subsequently deposited via plasma-enhanced chemical vapor deposition (PECVD). (5) The passivation layer undergoes dry etching using RIE to create vias for electrical contacts. A 1 µm thick aluminum (Al) layer was subsequently deposited by magnetron sputtering and patterned via RIE to form electrical interconnections and bonding pads. It is crucial to maximize the thickness of the Al layer since the pads are patterned simultaneously during this step. (6) The cantilever beams are defined by DRIE patterning the device layer. (7) Finally, the handle layer is processed and etched from the backside using DRIE to release all movable structures. Test results

After fabrication, the resonant frequency and static deformation of the fabricated cantilevers were measured using Laser Doppler Vibrometry (LDV; Polytec MSA-500), and Digital Holographic Microscopy (DHM; Lyncee Tec DHM R-2100). All measurements were conducted at room temperature and atmospheric pressure.

To further evaluate the dynamic response, the quality factor (Q) and step-response settling time of the key designs were characterized. The Q values were extracted from resonant response measured by LDV and are obtained as averages across six devices. The settling time was measured following a 90 VDC step excitation, defined as the time required for the response to enter and remain within ±5% of the steady-state value. The grid-etched G2-SO exhibits an average Q of 577, representing a reduction of approximately 12% compared to the uniform baseline G1-U1, which has an average Q of 655, while the settling time improves from 104 ms to 92 ms as shown in Fig. 3f. These results indicate that the grid-etched structure introduces a moderate and acceptable level of additional damping, attributed in part to increased viscous damping from the larger surface area. Importantly, the grid-etched design also exhibits a faster settling time compared to the uniform baseline. These findings confirm that the proposed decoupling strategy not only enhances quasi-static performance but also maintains favorable dynamic characteristics. Application to quasi-static micromirror

Micromirrors are extensively employed in optical beam steering applications, such as LiDAR, AR/VR glasses, miniature projectors, and optical communication systems. Depending on their operational mode, micromirrors can be categorized as quasi-static or resonant. Quasi-static mirrors are designed to provide precise pointing and stable position holding for light transmission. However, unlike resonant micromirrors, they cannot leverage resonance for amplitude amplification, leading to significant challenges in achieving large-angle scanning.

Among the various actuation mechanisms available, piezoelectric micromirrors are particularly suitable for quasi-static operation due to their high integration, rapid response times, and excellent linearity. A key design challenge of quasi-static mirror involves managing the trade-off between scan angle and resonant frequency. Given the limitations inherent in the drive capabilities of piezoelectric materials, achieving substantial deflection angles while maintaining an adequate resonant frequency is heavily dependent on structural design. Current quasi-static micromirror designs primarily implement two structural strategies previously outlined in the Introduction. The first leverages flexible springs to reduce stiffness and generate large angle, often requiring thin device layers that impose stringent process requirements and lead to poor yield17,44,45,51–54. The second utilizes multi-segment serial beams to increase effective length, thereby directly employing the actuator’s inclination angle16,29,31–38. While these designs avoid complex spring structures, they are significantly reliant on the characteristics of the piezoelectric material, commonly using PZT due to its superior piezoelectric coefficient. This work follows the second strategy but employs AlScN, which offers superior linearity and reliability over PZT. To achieve the target deformation, the grid-etched technique is incorporated, which modulates stiffness to enhance deformation. This approach enables the use of a standard 40 μm device layer without resorting to flexible springs, thereby improving overall manufacturability and yield.

This section introduces a biaxial piezoelectric micromirror that operates with distinct mechanisms for horizontal and vertical scanning. The fast (horizontal) axis operates in resonant mode for high-speed scanning, while the slow (vertical) axis operates in quasi-static mode, enabling the mirror to perform raster scanning. For the mirror, its mechanical tilt half-angle is defined as θmech, representing the maximum static deflection under the applied peak drive voltage. The corresponding total optical field of view (FOV) is 4θmech, corresponding to the peak-to-peak optical scan angle. By utilizing AlScN as the actuation material and a 40 µm SOI device layer, the implementation of a grid-etched structure on the slow axis enhances its θmech by 257% and increases the Fslow-axis·θmech by 64% as measured, demonstrating significant improvements in performance metrics.

Figure 4a presents a schematic of the designed biaxial piezoelectric micromirror. The device size is 12 × 7 mm². The central circular mirror, with a diameter of 1.2 mm, is supported by a pair of torsion bars that facilitate rapid horizontal scanning and is actuated by a set of piezoelectric actuators. The inner one-dimensional (1D) scanning mechanism is supported by a pair of folded cantilevers for slow vertical scanning. The actuators for the slow axis are also organized into two groups based on the voltage polarity required for deflection. During operation, applying opposite voltages to adjacent actuator segments results in an additive effect on their angles, thereby amplifying the overall mirror deflection. Figure 4b illustrates the FEA simulation results depicting the two operational mode shapes of the device. Figure 4c illustrates the working principle of the S-shaped folded actuator, which comprises four folded cantilever segments of equal length. When actuated, each segment generates an equal inclination angle, denoted as α. By arranging electrodes appropriately, opposite voltages can be applied to adjacent segments, causing one segment to deflect upward while the other deflects downward. This configuration enables the accumulation of inclination angles, resulting in a total mirror tilting angle of 4α. Conclusion

This paper introduces a segmented grid-etched cantilever structure that decouples the resonant frequency from the tip inclination angle and displacement by modulating the distribution of stiffness and mass along the cantilever. Guided by theoretical analysis revealing the asymmetric weighting of stiffness and mass contributions, in which the free-end segment exerts a dominant influence on mass while contributing less to stiffness, the proposed design strategically reduces local stiffness and mass at the free end. Experimental results demonstrate that this method results in a 103% increase in the tip inclination angle and a 54% increase in tip displacement with the same resonant frequency. Furthermore, dynamic characterization shows that the grid-etched design introduces only a moderate damping penalty while maintaining favorable dynamic characteristics.

As a practical application, a biaxial piezoelectric micromirror is demonstrated, in which the fast axis operates in resonant mode with a frequency of 12.1 kHz and an FOV of 40°, while the slow axis operates in quasi-static mode. By applying the grid-etched structure to the slow-axis folded beams, the optical scan angle is enhanced by 257%, achieving an FOV of 25°. In loaded system of micromirror, the resonant frequency of the slow axis decreases from 489 Hz to 225 Hz due to the added mass and stiffness of the mirror plate and fast-axis structure. Nevertheless, the frequency–angle product f·θ is improved by 64%, demonstrating that the proposed strategy achieves a net gain in overall performance in the loaded system of micromirror despite the loss in dynamic performance.

Future work will extend the decoupling principle demonstrated here. This includes exploring continuously graded stiffness distributions for finer performance tailoring, optimizing the design for complex loaded system of micromirror, and integrating advanced actuation schemes such as bimorph structures to further enhance performance and functionality.
