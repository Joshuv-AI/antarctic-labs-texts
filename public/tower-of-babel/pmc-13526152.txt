Pleomorphic Adenoma of a Minor Salivary Gland of the Hard Palate Complicated by Surgical Site Infection: A Case Report
Authors: Alpha Muhindo Kavuyiro, Bienfait Mumbere Vahwere, Mihret Adane Woldemicheal, Charles Malisaba Posite, Eluzai Ndiwelubula, Jonas Makatsi, Franck Katembo Sikakulya, Yanurkis Duranones Rosales, Brian Agaba, Elizabeth Vargas Escalante
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundPleomorphic adenoma is the most common benign salivary gland tumor of the head and neck, predominantly affecting the parotid gland. Although minor salivary gland tumors are less common, the palate is a notable site, while involvement of the hard palate remains relatively rare. Complete surgical excision is the mainstay of treatment, with postoperative wound care or reconstruction tailored to the resulting defect. Although postoperative infection following oral surgery is uncommon, the risk increases with extensive tissue manipulation, flap elevation, bone exposure, or use of foreign materials and surgical packing. Infection associated with intraoral packing after excision of a palatal pleomorphic adenoma is exceptionally rare and may present challenges in postoperative management, highlighting the need for early recognition and a structured, evidence-informed approach to treatment.Case PresentationWe describe a case of a 29-year-old Ankole male who presented with a painless swelling in the oral cavity that had slowly increased in size over three years. Clinical and radiological evaluations suggested a benign tumor arising from the palatal salivary glands. Surgical excision was performed, and absorbable intraoral packing was placed in the surgical wound. The histopathological examination provided the diagnosis of pleomorphic adenoma. On the fifth day after surgery, the patient developed clinical signs of surgical site infection. The packing was removed, wound care was continued, and antimicrobial treatment was administered. His condition improved progressively, and complete wound healing was achieved after three weeks of daily dressing.ConclusionThis report presents an uncommon postoperative complication following excision of pleomorphic adenoma of a minor salivary gland of the hard palate. Infection of intraoral packing complicated the early postoperative course, but removal of the infected material, antibiotics, and regular local wound care led to satisfactory recovery. The case underlines the importance of close postoperative follow-up.

Introduction

Pleomorphic adenoma is widely recognized as the most common benign tumor affecting the salivary glands of the head and neck. It contributes to a substantial proportion of salivary gland neoplasms, representing about 40–70% of tumors arising from both major and minor salivary glands.1,2 When pleomorphic adenoma develops from accessory or minor salivary glands, the palate is the site most often involved, comprising nearly 60% of cases, followed by the upper lip, which represents around 20%.3 This predilection for the palate is attributed to the dense concentration of accessory salivary gland tissue within the palatal submucosa, which makes this anatomical region prone to salivary gland tumors of both benign and malignant nature.4

The standard treatment for pleomorphic adenoma of the palate is complete surgical removal of the lesion, followed by closure or reconstruction of the defect when indicated. This method usually provides good clinical outcomes and is associated with relatively few complications, including low rates of hematoma formation and local tumor recurrence.5 Although every surgical procedure aims to avoid adverse events, complications may still occur. Some can be prevented through proper planning and technique, while others may arise despite adequate surgical management.6 These complications may appear during the operation, shortly after surgery, or later in the postoperative course, including months or years after the initial procedure.7

Infection after oral surgical procedures is relatively uncommon, but certain operative conditions increase the risk. These include elevation of soft tissue flaps, exposure of bone, bone removal, inadequate irrigation of the surgical field, and persistence of foreign bodies or debris beneath the flap.8 Patient-related factors may further predispose to surgical site infection (SSI), particularly advanced age, smoking, multiple comorbidities, systemic illness such as type II diabetes mellitus, and congenital or acquired immune suppression.9

Postoperative septic inflammation in the head and neck region should be managed promptly because infection can extend to surrounding tissues or spread systemically. Management generally involves two complementary approaches: early antimicrobial treatment, often with broad-spectrum coverage, and surgical control of the infectious source when required. Surgical measures may include opening the previous operative site, removing infected material, draining any collection, and continuing regular wound cleaning and dressing until healing progresses.4,8,9 This case highlights the clinical presentation, diagnosis, surgical management, and postoperative complications of pleomorphic adenoma arising from a minor salivary gland in the hard palate, emphasizing on the occurrence and management of SSI.

Case Presentation

A 29-year-old Ankole male attended the Ear-Nose-and Throat clinic at Kampala International University Teaching Hospital with a painless intraoral mass that had been present for approximately three years. According to the patient, the swelling had enlarged gradually over time. He reported no previous medical condition or treatment related to the lesion, and this consultation represented his first presentation for the palatal swelling. Oral examination revealed a dome-like swelling on the right side of the hard palate. The surface mucosa was intact, smooth, and non-ulcerated. The lesion crossed the midline and extended from the region of tooth 13 to tooth 18. On palpation, the mass was well-circumscribed, firm, multinodular, fixed, non-tender, and non-pulsatile (Figure 1). Computed tomography of the head and neck (Figure 2) showed a right palatal lesion located at the roof of the oral cavity, measuring about 4.0×3.2 cm. No bony destruction or erosion of the hard palate was identified on imaging. From the clinical and radiological findings, pleomorphic adenoma of the hard palate was considered the working diagnosis, and the patient was scheduled for operative removal. Surgical Procedure and Postoperative Management

The operation was performed under general anesthesia. The airway was secured by orotracheal intubation with an endotracheal tube. A Boyle-Davis mouth gag was used to improve visualization and access to the operative field. Local anesthetic solution was then infiltrated around the lesion. The mucosa surrounding the tumor was incised, followed by careful wide dissection. The encapsulated mass was excised completely together with the involved mucoperiosteum and the overlying mucosal tissue (Figure 3). A surgical wick, referred to as mesh packing, was inserted into the operative cavity. Primary closure was achieved by advancing the adjacent mucosa and suturing the wound in layers to obtain a watertight closure. A palatal splint was placed postoperatively to reduce the likelihood of hematoma formation. The surgical specimen was sent for histopathological analysis. Microscopic examination (Figure 4) demonstrated a well-circumscribed lesion with fibrous capsule showing ductal proliferation composed of bland epithelial and myoepithelial cell within a fibro-myxoid stromal background. No evidence of malignant transformation was seen. These histological features were consistent with pleomorphic adenoma. On postoperative day five, the wound opened and produced whitish discharge, suggesting SSI. The sutures were removed, and the surgical wick was extracted from the wound (Figure 5). The patient was managed with amoxicillin-clavulanic acid, metronidazole, ibuprofen, and regular mouth gargling (3x daily with normal saline). On fifteenth postoperative day, the wound had become clean, although it remained deep with visible bone exposure; the surrounding edema had decreased (Figure 6). On day twenty-three after surgery, the wound was cleaner and shallower, with further reduction of swelling (Figure 7). Complete wound healing was achieved by the forty-fifth postoperative day (Figure 8). At the six-month follow-up visit, there was no clinical sign of recurrence. Discussion

Pleomorphic adenoma is the most common benign salivary gland tumor and involves most the parotid gland, with fewer cases involving the submandibular gland and minor salivary glands. Its occurrence in minor salivary glands of the hard palate is an unusual clinical finding.10 The lesion may occur over a broad age range and is often reported more frequently in adult women between the second and seventh decades of life. This present case differs from this usual pattern because the patient was a 29-year-old male.

Diagnosis of palatal pleomorphic adenoma begins with a careful review of the patient’s history and a complete intraoral and extraoral examination. The typical clinical finding is a painless, slowly progressive swelling of the hard palate.1 Imaging plays an important role in evaluating the size and local extension of the lesion. Computed tomography is useful for assessing tumor dimensions, bone remodeling, cortical erosion, and possible invasion, while magnetic resonance imaging provides better detail regarding soft tissue spread. Final diagnosis depends on histopathological confirmation. Microscopically, pleomorphic adenoma usually contains epithelial and myoepithelial elements arranged in different patterns within a fibro-myxoid or chondro-myxoid stroma.11 Although the majority of cases show typical benign morphology, rare variants with necrosis, cystic change, squamous metaplasia, hemorrhage, or tumor-related infarction have been reported, particularly in soft palate lesions.12

Salivary gland tumors are often identified in the parotid gland, less often in the submandibular (10–25%), minor salivary glands (9–20%), and sublingual (1–5%). About 80% of these are benign, with an increased frequency in the major salivary glands (~70%). Malignant tumors are very rare, with an incidence of <1.3 per 100,000 in habitants. In half of the cases, they involve minor salivary glands. When they are found in the major salivary glands, the submandibular and sublingual glands are most frequently affected. Secondary neoplastic lesions are almost exclusively at the level of the parotid and submandibular gland. Most commonly, they originate from a squamous cell carcinoma or a melanoma.13

For pleomorphic adenoma of the palate, wide local excision remains the recommended treatment. This approach aims to remove the tumor, its capsule, and an adequate margin of surrounding tissue, including mucosa and periosteum when these structures are involved.14 The choice of surgical technique is important because incomplete excision increases the possibility of recurrence. Simple enucleation is generally not advised, as it may leave microscopic tumor deposits or capsular remnants. Instead, a conservative wide excision with removal of the surrounding mucoperiosteal tissue is preferred. Bone curettage or bone removal may be required when there is evidence of bony invasion, but it is usually unnecessary when the underlying bone is intact.1

In this patient, the tumor was removed surgically, and mesh-like surgical packing was placed in the palatal defect to reduce dead space, prevent hematoma accumulation, and support local wound healing. However, on the fifth day after surgery, signs of wound infection developed. The SSI in this case would be explained by the collection of hematomas in the wound, the infection of the packing material or a poor blood flow to the tissue flap. The infected packing was removed, the wound was cleaned, and antibiotic therapy was maintained. With continued local wound care and medical treatment, the patient improved progressively and achieved complete healing by day forty-five. In oral surgery, postoperative complications have been reported in about 3.57% of cases, particularly after exodontia,15 with infection being among the major contributors to delayed wound healing.16 Therefore, SSI in the head and neck region should be managed promptly by draining any collection, regular wound cleaning, and dressing until healing progresses. Strict adherence to aseptic principles and maintenance of sterility are required.3,8,9

Limitations

This study focuses on a single-patient and features short-term follow-up and no control group; therefore, the findings may not be generalized.

Conclusion

This report highlights the importance of considering pleomorphic adenoma in patients with a gradually enlarging, painless palatal mass, even in resource-limited settings. Accurate diagnosis requires early clinical suspicion, appropriate imaging, and histopathological confirmation. Close postoperative follow-up is essential, particularly when packing or mesh-like materials are used, as infection may delay healing and functional recovery. Multidisciplinary collaboration, careful surgical technique, strict aseptic principles, and regular follow-up are important for timely management of complications and achieving good healing, preserved oral function, and acceptable cosmetic outcomes.

Take-Away Lessons

Pleomorphic adenoma of a minor salivary gland of the hard palate is a benign tumor with a favorable outcome when completely excised; however, postoperative problems may still develop. Infection involving surgical packing or mesh should be considered when there is wound separation, discharge, pain, swelling, or unexpectedly slow healing after surgery. Prompt removal of infected material, wound toilet, appropriate antibiotic therapy, and continued clinical review can enhance recovery and reduce prolonged postoperative morbidity.
