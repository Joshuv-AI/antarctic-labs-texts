Impact of drought on global food security by 2050
Authors: Vachel A Kraklow, Kirsten Paff, Darin Comeau, Kurt Solander, Travis R Pitts, Stephen F Price, Chonggang Xu
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractDrought is a major driver of crop production loss, threatening global food security as the population rises toward 9 billion by 2050. Using a process-based crop model within an Earth system model, we assess drought-related impacts on maize, soybean, rice, and wheat production at global and country levels. We then develop a food insecurity index combining drought impacts with socio-economic factors. Our results indicate that by 2050, globally averaged drought losses for combined maize, soybean, rice, and wheat production are <2%, though soybean losses reach 3.6%. Despite these small average changes at the global scale, 62 countries experience maximum production losses over 10%, and 24 countries over 20%. Our index identifies regions at greatest risk, including large parts of South America, Africa, Eastern Europe, and Southeast Asia. These results illustrate the need for drought adaptation to mitigate future drought impacts on crop production.Subject terms: Environmental health, Environmental economics

Introduction

Global food security faces unprecedented challenges as the population surges and drought becomes more frequent and severe1. By 2050, global food demand is projected to grow by 30–62%2 as the population is projected to increase to 9.8 billion3. Drought is the leading cause of agricultural production loss4, with three-fourths of the global harvested area experiencing drought-induced production losses between 1983 and 2009, corresponding to 166 billion U.S. dollars in production losses5. Between 1961 and 2014, droughts resulted in 3.0–10.4% and 3.1–12.2% reductions in detrended maize and soybean yields, respectively, in top-producing countries6. Despite these losses, per capita food supply has increased >30% since the 1960s, spurred by cropland expansion and increased inorganic fertilizer use7, as well as improved cultivars8. However, more frequent or severe droughts in the future could harm global crop production and potentially jeopardize the monumental progress made in reducing world hunger since the 1960s.

Previous studies have taken a range of approaches to quantify crop production losses associated with drought. Current approaches for assessing drought impacts primarily rely on statistical relationships between drought indices and historical observations5, or simulated yields in the future9. While existing methods are useful for quantifying and attributing drought impacts, they are often biased due to confounding factors such as planting dates, nitrogen fertilization, and irrigation practices. Statistical models can be useful for looking at historical trends, but they have limited applicability to future scenarios as they do not capture the inherent physiological processes underlying changes in crop production, and historical trends will likely no longer hold true under unprecedented future conditions10. Even studies that extend their statistical models to predict future drought impacts by incorporating projected changes in precipitation11 often do not account for other important future changes—such as elevated atmospheric CO₂ concentrations—which are expected to significantly influence global food production12. Since statistical models do not represent physiological processes, they cannot be used to fully isolate the impacts of specific influences, such as drought. Meanwhile, previous studies have coupled simulated drought impacts and economic factors. For example, Fraser et al.11 used hydrologic modeling and climate, crop distribution, and economic data to examine agricultural drought severity and its impact on food security. This is useful for identifying vulnerable food security “hot spots,” but the study did not explicitly simulate crop production, limiting its ability to accurately quantify the magnitude of drought impacts on food availability. To the best of our knowledge, no prior study has employed process-based models that incorporate CO₂ effects and other confounding factors (e.g., fertilization and irrigation practices) to specifically quantify the impact of drought in isolation on global-scale food production and the resulting food insecurity across major crop types under future climate conditions.

In this study, we use a process-based crop model, which accounts for CO2 fertilization effects, within a global land surface model (CLM512), to directly quantify the impact of drought on future global-scale food production and resulting food insecurity. We focus on the big four staple crops (e.g., maize, rice, soybean, and wheat) as they are four of the most widely produced crops globally13, accounting for two-thirds of food calories worldwide14. Our study simulates the crop production difference between “drought” and “no-drought” scenarios for the big four staple crops through 2050 by running the CLM5 crop model for both rainfed and irrigated crops. The crop model is driven by eight sets of climate forcings (i.e., a combination of temperature, precipitation, radiation, and humidity variables) projected by four Earth Systems Models (ESMs), each providing a drought and a no-drought scenario (see “Methods,” Supplementary Fig. 1, and Xu et al.15 for more details). The four ESMs span a range of equilibrium climate sensitivities from 2 to 5 °C (Supplementary Table 1). Each simulation is conducted under the intermediate greenhouse gas emission scenario, SSP2-4.5, which aligns most closely with projected carbon emissions under current global policies16,17.

Changes in crop production alone do not determine how vulnerable an area is to food insecurity under drought, as many socio-economic factors influence how easily one region can obtain food from another. We therefore investigate country-specific, drought-related food insecurity by developing an index that takes into account the percent reduction in crop production caused by drought, and socio-economic factors, such as country-level gross domestic product (GDP) per capita18, projected population growth19, country-level big four staple crop trade data20, food price inflation data21, and political instability data22. As high-income countries can more easily import food to compensate for domestic production losses than low-income countries23, this index captures both a country’s crop production loss due to future drought and its resilience to those losses. At the same time, food price inflation and political instability can negatively impact a country’s resiliency to future drought-caused crop production losses, regardless of GDP status. Based on this index, which accounts for multiple, sometimes competing factors, we assess the vulnerability of specific regions to food insecurity caused by future drought.

Results

Drought impacts at the global scale

In this study, drought-induced production losses refer to the difference in production between a drought and a no-drought scenario with all other factors held equal (see “Methods,” Supplementary Fig. 1, and Xu et al.15 for more details). Losses do not refer to changes between current and future production. Between 2020-2050, our results suggest that drought could reduce the globally averaged total big four staple crop production by ~0.2 to nearly 2% (Fig. 1), depending on the year and the ESM used. For individual crop types (i.e., maize, rice, soybean, or wheat), globally averaged production losses due to drought ranged from 0 to 3.6% between 2020 and 2050, with soybeans showing the highest losses and rice showing the lowest losses (Fig. 2). These losses represent globally averaged values over 5-year periods; however, the potential maximum impact of drought on crop production could be significantly more severe for any given year at the country level.

Drought impacts at the country level

Maximum drought-induced production losses for the big four staple crops averaged across the period 2040–2050 (herein after referred to as “mid-century”) at the individual country level showed far larger differences between the drought and no-drought scenarios than the globally averaged losses. Sixty-two countries are projected to experience >10% maximum big four crop production losses, and 24 countries are projected to experience maximum losses of >20% (Fig. 3). These regions coincide with major big four staple crop production areas (Supplementary Fig. 2), with certain regions potentially experiencing upwards of 40–50% drought-induced production losses in the worst-case scenarios simulated (Supplementary Fig. 3). The impact of drought varies depending on crop type, with soybean being the most vulnerable, experiencing up to 92.7% production losses (average country level maximum loss = 16.4% ± 2.97) in certain countries by mid-century (Fig. 4). Country-specific maximum production losses for maize, rice, and wheat could be as high as 76.0% (average country level maximum losses = 11.2% ± 1.78), 67.3% (average country level maximum loss = 12.5% ± 2.37), and 64.4% (average country level maximum loss = 11.6% ± 2.18), respectively, by mid-century.

Economic vulnerability and food insecurity

Using the maximum drought-induced production losses for the big four staple crops averaged over four ESMs by mid-century, we calculated three indices: a drought impact index (Fig. 5a), an economic vulnerability index (Fig. 5b), and a food insecurity index (Fig. 5c). For the drought impact index, the maximum drought-induced production losses (Fig. 3) were classified into three equal-weight quantiles: low drought impact (0–5.1%), intermediate drought impact (5.1–11.3%), and high drought impact (11.3–46.9%) (see “Methods”). The regions most impacted by future drought-induced production losses include large parts of Africa, South America, Eastern Europe, Southeast Asia, and Australia (Fig. 5a and Supplementary Fig. 4a, d).

The socio-economic vulnerability index, which is based on a country’s GDP, projected population growth19, fraction of consumed big four staple crops that are imported20, food price inflation21, and political instability22, was classified into three equal-weight quantiles: low vulnerability (−6.96 to 0.16), intermediate vulnerability (−0.16 to 2.83), and high vulnerability (2.83 to 8.8). When accounting for GDP, global trade factors, and population growth only, the regions most vulnerable to production losses include most of sub-Saharan Africa and Southeast Asia (Supplemental Fig. 4b). When we also account for inflation (Supplementary Fig. 4e) and political instability (Fig. 5b), we see similar patterns.

The food insecurity index, which is the combined effects of both the drought impact index (Fig. 5a) and economic vulnerability index (Fig. 5b), per country (Eq. 12 in “Methods”), was created by assigning the lowest values (1 and 2) to class 1 with lower risk of food insecurity, the middle values (3 and 4) to class 2 with intermediate risk, and the highest values (6 and 9) to class 3 with higher risk. The calculated food insecurity index showed that the majority of countries in Africa, Eastern Europe, Southeast Asia, Central and South America, as well as Russia and Australia, are identified as moderately to highly vulnerable to future drought impacts (Fig. 5c). Therefore, countries most vulnerable to global food insecurity are typically those with a high drought index, coupled with low to moderate GDP, large projected population growth, high inflation rates, and high political instability (Fig. 5c). For countries with severe drought impacts, but low socio-economic vulnerability, such as Australia, the impact of drought alone could be enough to put them in the moderate to high food insecurity brackets.

Discussion

Using a process-based crop model, our results illustrate that the food insecurity impacts of drought could be much more severe at the country and regional level than at the global scale, due to the combined impacts of drought severity and socio-economic factors, such as population growth, rising inflation rates, and political instability. In order to illustrate the complex interactions of drought-induced production losses, global trade, and socio-economic factors, we chose to highlight four case studies that focus on different crops and regions of concern.

Drought impacts on South American maize and soybean production

Drought-induced decreases in food production in major food-exporting countries could have devastating effects on the global food supply. For example, the recent 2017–2018 drought centered on the Pampas region of Argentina—considered one of the world’s breadbaskets for its maize and soybean cultivation24,25, and accounting for 28% of the country’s exports26—lowered Argentina’s maize and soybean exports by 15% and 33%, respectively, compared to the previous growing season27. This caused a 0.8% reduction in Argentina’s GDP28 and had cascading effects on the regional food supply. Bolivia and Uruguay are two of the countries in the region dependent upon Argentine exports; however, between 2017 and 2018, Bolivia and Uruguay’s big four crop imports from Argentina were reduced by 58% and 70%, respectively29. Our results suggest that Argentina may experience maximum production losses up to 23.3% for maize and 40.0% for soybeans by mid-century (Fig. 4). These results are supported by previous statistical modeling studies, which projected drought-induced maize and soybean production losses in Argentina up to 20% and 50% by 2100, respectively30. Our results also suggest maximum drought-induced maize production losses up to 9.3% and 38.7% for Bolivia and Uruguay, respectively, while maximum soybean production losses are expected to be up to 9.5% and 34.5%, respectively (Fig. 4). This will likely result in both increased demand for imports and decreased supply to the regional market in the future, which would substantially drive up regional food prices and potentially lead to increased inflation rates. Higher food prices and increased inflation rates could potentially lead to increased political instability, which was witnessed in Argentina between 1989 and 1991 under hyperinflation31. Additionally, high prices could accelerate the already rapid deforestation of the Chaco region of northern Argentina, southern Bolivia, and western Paraguay, as this region is suitable for the planting of drought-resistant soybean varieties32. Our food insecurity index suggests that Argentina, Bolivia, and Uruguay are the most vulnerable countries to future food insecurity within this region, as a result of moderate to extreme drought risks coupled with lower-middle incomes33, and high rates of inflation and/or political instability (Fig. 5c).

Drought impacts on South African maize production

Similar to the Argentine scenario, many southern African countries, including Botswana, Eswatini, Lesotho, Mozambique, Namibia, Zambia, and Zimbabwe, are reliant upon maize exports from South Africa20, which is the largest maize producer and net exporter in the region34. Roughly 50% of the cultivated area in South Africa is covered by maize35. The 2015–2017 drought, centered in the southwestern provinces of the country, was characterized as a one-in-a-hundred-year extreme event36. Caused by a strong El Niño, this drought resulted in a 50% reduction in maize production compared to the 5-year average, affecting over 29 million people37. This drought had cascading effects regionally, requiring humanitarian assistance in Zimbabwe, Malawi, Mozambique, and Madagascar38. Our analysis indicates that South Africa may experience maximum maize crop production losses up to 8.9% by mid-century (Fig. 4). Using an existing and a modified version of the crop model, CropSyst, Mangani et al.39 project even greater maize yield reductions of 18.7–30.0% by 2080 in the three main maize growing areas of South Africa. However, their modified CropSyst model was looking at extreme heat and drought, not drought alone. South Africa is a drought-prone country, having experienced frequent droughts over the past three decades40. Our results also suggest that maximum maize production losses may be up to 16% (Botswana), 24% (Eswatini), 41% (Lesotho), 10% (Mozambique), 7% (Namibia), 20% (Zambia), and 28% (Zimbabwe), respectively, by mid-century. These losses could lead to a substantially higher demand for food imported from South Africa, which could lead to higher inflation rates. Our food insecurity index for this region is moderate to high due to a combination of future drought impacts, population growth, high inflation, and political instability (Fig. 5c).

Drought impacts on Russian wheat production

Another key region of vulnerability is Russia, one of the world’s top grain exporters41. Western Russian wheat accounts for >60% of total grain production in the country42. During the severe flash drought of 2010, wheat production was reduced by >70% in the top wheat-producing areas, forcing the Russian Federation to ban the export of wheat in August 2010 to preserve wheat for its own consumption43. This had a cascading effect on countries heavily dependent upon Russian wheat exports44, threatening food security in import-dependent regions, such as the Middle East and North Africa45. Our results suggest that Russia, as a whole, may experience up to 6.1% maximum wheat production losses by mid-century (Fig. 4). These results are comparable to previous crop modeling projections forecasting a 10.3 ± 3.2% decrease in spring wheat yields in western Russia between 2050 and 205946. However, our results suggest that western Russia, specifically, could experience wheat production losses as high as 25% by mid-century (Supplementary Fig. 3), which could once again have cascading impacts on countries dependent upon Russian wheat exports. For example, Armenia and Azerbaijan are heavily reliant upon Russian wheat, with an average of 71.4% and 72.1% of their wheat product imports coming from Russia between 2015 and 201929. Our results suggest that both Armenia and Azerbaijan may experience maximum wheat production reductions up to 5.9% and 6.2%, respectively (Fig. 4), making them vulnerable to both internal effects of drought, as well as to drought-induced wheat production losses in Russia. However, as they are both upper-middle-income countries33, with moderate levels of inflation and political instability, they are less vulnerable from a socio-economic perspective and likely to have the resources to import wheat from other trade partners in the event of a drought impacting the wheat-producing areas of Russia.

Drought impacts on Thai rice production

Lastly, Thailand is one of the world’s top exporters of rice, contributing over 22% of global rice exports between 2017 and 201947. Our results suggest Thailand may experience up to 11.5% maximum reduction in rice production by mid-century (Fig. 4). Simulations of rainfed rice using the DSSAT crop model in the Songkhram River Basin of Thailand found similar reductions in mid-century rice yields, predicting 10.2% losses under the RCP4.5 scenario48. The 2015–2016 drought, caused by one of the most powerful El Niño events in recent history, resulted in exceptionally low water levels in the Mekong River, which is considered the backbone of Southeast Asia’s agricultural system and is heavily reliant upon monsoon rainfall49. This drought significantly impacted Thai rice production, decreasing it by 15% in 2015 and causing a 10.7% reduction in Thai rice exports20. While this drought did not result in serious impacts to countries dependent upon Thai rice, our results and the fact that most rice production in Thailand is rainfed47, suggest that Thailand could be highly vulnerable to future drought impacts with potentially negative implications for the global rice supply. For example, rice is considered a staple food among many African countries, with more than half of all African countries being net rice importers50, particularly from Thailand, Vietnam, Pakistan, and India51. Although Benin does produce rice, Benin purchased on average 70.8% of its rice imports from Thailand between 2015 and 201829. In an interview with Beninese rice farmers, 2015 was reported as the most severe drought year between 2013 and 201752. The drought led to a 598% and 1366% increase in rice imports from Thailand from 2014 to 2015 and 2016 values, respectively29. Our results suggest that Benin will see up to 16.2% maximum rice production reduction due to drought by mid-century (Fig. 4). When coupled with projected declines in Thai rice production, this domestic shortfall could pose a substantial risk to Benin’s future food security. Benin has multiple food insecurity risk factors, as it is considered a lower-middle income country33, with a population growth projection of 88%19, and a moderate drought risk (Supplementary Fig. 4c). However, our food insecurity index only has Benin at a moderate risk, due to both low inflation and low political instability counteracting the effects of drought, GDP, and population growth (Fig. 5c).

We found that rice had the lowest sensitivity to future drought of the four crops examined (Fig. 2). This is in line with the findings of Liu et al.30, which also suggested minimal rice yield sensitivity to future drought. As rice is typically grown in flooded fields, rice growing areas are usually characterized by abundant precipitation and irrigation infrastructure53,54, resulting in a lower drought impact than other staple crops. It is worth noting, however, that the spreading adoption of alternative, less water-intensive, rice growing practices, such as alternate wetting drying, could result in increased drought impacts, especially in more temperate rice growing regions such as Italy55.

Caveats and future research

While our study examined the maximum potential, drought-induced production reduction by mid-century for individual countries, we did not examine whether these drought losses occurred simultaneously across time and space, nor did we examine how likely concurrent droughts are in the future. Future studies focusing on the risks and impacts of multiple major crop-producing regions being hit by drought at the same time would help improve our understanding of drought impacts on food security. Additionally, although our crop projections are evaluated under multiple climate projections under SSP2-4.5, they rely on a single global crop model (CLM5). As a result, the findings may reflect structural and parameterization biases specific to CLM5. Future work that incorporates an ensemble of crop models would help strengthen the robustness and generality of our results.

Summary

Global food demand is predicted to grow by 30–62% by 20502. In order to meet this demand, it is estimated that we need to double crop production by 2050, which would require an annual rate of yield increase of 2.4%56. In the face of rising demand, any production losses could have significant negative food security implications. Concerningly, our results suggest that drought specifically could result in up to 2% mean big four production losses on a global scale by mid-century. Even more concerning is the fact that our results suggest losses could be substantially larger at the country scale, depending on the type of crop (Fig. 4). Our food insecurity index, which uses multiple socio-economic factors, highlights just how globalized the food market has become. Major staple crop exporters, such as Argentina, South Africa, Russia, and Thailand, could see significant drought-induced production reductions, leading to decreased food security for their trading partners, as well as at home. We found that food insecurity vulnerability is highest in countries with lower GDP, higher projected population growth, and higher rates of inflation and/or political instability. These results illustrate the need to substantially invest in irrigation infrastructure, adopt more drought-tolerant crops, and increase trade partnerships to mitigate future drought impacts on crop production.

Methods

Climate forcing

To build climate forcings (i.e., a combination of temperature, precipitation, radiation, and humidity variables) that feed into crop models, we used publicly available model outputs from the CMIP6 archive57 under SSP2-4.5(https://esgf-node.llnl.gov/search/cmip6/, Supplementary Table 2). We ran land model simulations forced by outputs from four different climate models, GFDL-ESM4, MPI-ESM1-2-HR, IPSL-CM6A-LR, and UKESM-1-0-LL (hereafter referred to as “GFDL,” “MPI,” “IPSL,” and “UKESM”), which approximately span the very likely range (2–5 °C) of equilibrium climate sensitivity58 (ECS; see Supplementary Table 1). Given the substantial overlap in the predicted climate ranges between the intermediate SSP2-4.5 scenario and the extreme SSP5-8.5 scenario by 2050, we believe it is adequate to use four different climate projections under the SSP2-4.5 scenario for the scope of this study.

Crop model

For our crop simulations, we used the Community Land Model version 5.0 (CLM512), which is the land model component of the Community Earth System Model Version 2 (CESM2)59. The crop model within CLM5 is based on the AgroIBIS model60–62. It simulates 61 major crop types and accounts for planting, phenology, nutrient fertilization, water limitation, land use change, and CO2 fertilization effects. Specifically, the photosynthesis of different leaf layers for C3 crops is based on the process-based Farquhar photosynthetic model63, which addresses three key questions related to rubisco-limited carboxylate rate, light-limited electron transport rate, and export-limited carbon utilization rate. Photosynthesis for C4 crops is based on the model of Collatz et al.64. When coupled with the Ball-Berry conductance model as described by Collatz et al.65, the photosynthetic model considers the environmental drivers of temperature, relative humidity, radiation, and CO2 concentration.

For this study, we selected the model outputs for the big four staple crops: maize, rice, soybean, and wheat. The model does not account for local varietal adaptation. The model was run at a spatial resolution of 0.94° by 1.25° and a time step of 30 min. Crop area was determined using the Coupled Model Intercomparison Project, Version 6 (CMIP657) Land Use Model Intercomparison Project (LUMIP66) dataset. CLM5 divides grid cells into rainfed or irrigated production using the dataset created by Portman et al.67. In the model, water stress is simulated based on a water limitation factor calculated from the soil moisture at different depths and root distribution for both drought and non-drought conditions. Irrigation is triggered if the rhizospheric soil moisture is below a critical threshold for the irrigated land fraction. The model assumes that sufficient water is always available for irrigation. Fertilizer application is determined by crop functional type and spatial location based on the LUMIP land use and land cover change time series66. The model simulates three phases of phenology. Phase 1 lasts from planting to leaf emergence. Planting occurs during a prescribed window and is triggered by temperature and growing degree days. Phase 2 lasts from leaf emergence to the start of grain fill. Finally, Phase 3 extends from the onset of grain fill to physiological maturity, concluding with harvest. The model simulates grain carbon, which is converted to harvested grain dry weight by assuming carbon is 45% of the total dry weight and harvesting efficiency is 85%63. The assumption of uniform and constant grain carbon is a limitation of the CLM5 model, and we acknowledge that grain carbon content can vary based on crop, growing conditions, and variety68. Although we acknowledge this uncertainty could impact the absolute crop production value, we do not expect this will impact our main results focusing on the percentage reduction in production by drought.

Modeling spin-up and historical validation

Per CMIP6 protocols, “future” simulations under SSP2-4.5 forcing start in 2015, immediately following the end of the historical period. The historical period starts in 1850, which is taken as the end of the pre-industrial era. An equilibrated pre-industrial initial condition for CLM5 was available as part of the standard support provided by CESM. From that initial condition, we ran a historical simulation from 1850 through 2014 forced by version 3 of the Global Surface Wetness Project dataset (GSWP3)69,70. GSWP3 uses observationally-constrained, reanalysis methods to provide a global time series of all atmosphere and land inputs necessary for running CLM5 and evolving its vegetation model through the historical era.

CLM5 has previously been validated against observational data63. However, here we further validate CLM5 crop model outputs by comparing modeled spatial production between 2000 and 2010 during the historical period against the Global Agro-Ecological Zone project71, which is a standard global dataset used for evaluating crop models (Supplementary Fig. 5). The comparison between modeled and observed crop production between 2000 and 2010 showed that our CLM5 model simulations are in good agreement with observations (Supplementary Fig. 5). Specifically, the root mean square errors (RMSE) for maize, rice, soybean, and wheat are 0.41, 0.12, 0.04, and 0.079 million tonnes/year, respectively. The corresponding correlation coefficients for maize, rice, soybean, and wheat are 0.67, 0.67, 0.41, and 0.49, respectively.

Climate forcing for SSP2-4.5 simulations

The historical and SSP-2.4.5-forced simulations using CLM5 are on a global 0.9375° by 1.25° latitude by longitude grid, while SSP2-4.5 outputs for the GFDL, MPI, IPSL, and UKESM models are on a range of different resolutions. This required a bilinear interpolation of these models’ climate forcings onto the CLM5 grid using the “akima” R package72. We took additional steps to ensure a smooth and continuous coupling between model transients in the historical simulation and those at the start of the future-forced simulations. Because outputs from the four climate models each originate from their own historical simulation, an abrupt step-change in climate forcing would occur if we coupled those outputs directly to the end point of our CLM5-based historical simulation. This would induce strong transients into the evolution of our CLM5 simulations at the historical-to-future boundary, which do not track the applied SSP2-4.5 atmospheric greenhouse gas and aerosol concentration forcing. To avoid this problem, we adopted an anomaly-based forcing approach whereby the time-varying outputs from each of the four climate models from 2015–2050 were recalculated as a ratio (precipitation (PR), specific humidity (HUSS), and solar radiation (RSDS)) or an absolute difference (temperature (TAS)) relative to a historical baseline reference period (2004–2013) common to all of the sets of model outputs. These differences were then added to the GSWP3-based climate forcing for the baseline time period to reconstruct a future forcing time series for each of the 4 models, which varied seamlessly relative to the baseline and were devoid of the coupling “shocks” noted above.

In addition to the considerations discussed above, this approach allowed us to develop and apply a second set of climate forcings for which we effectively “subtracted” the effects of drought from the future SSP2-4.5 climates. This second set of climate forcings was then used to run a set of counterfactual future simulations, i.e., to simulate future crop production under SSP2-4.5 forcing in the absence of drought. These simulations were compared against the “with drought” simulations to quantify the anticipated future effects of drought on crop production. We discuss the generation of these “no-drought” climate forcings next.

Analysis of drought impacts under SSP2-4.5

We assessed the impact of drought on crop production by constructing two contrasting climate forcings: (1) climate as built directly from CMIP6 model outputs with drought included, and (2) as built from the same outputs but with drought events (and hence drought impacts) “removed” (i.e., no drought). Drought events were defined based on the crop accessible soil water (CASW), which was calculated as the sum of water over the depth of the soil column weighted by the fraction of plant roots at different depths. Because the drought tolerance of plants depends on their local climate conditions, we defined drought based on shortages in the grid-cell-specific CASW. Specifically, droughts were characterized for any month in a given year for which the grid-cell-specific CASW was less than or equal to the tenth percentile for the same month during the historical reference period of 1900–2014. For every month of the year, this tenth percentile value provides a month and grid-cell-specific threshold to define drought. When a grid cell CASW was less than or equal to the threshold for a specific month during the 2015–2050 “future” simulation period, we identified this month as a drought month. For sites with high soil moisture content, even if the CASW for a certain month was less than the tenth percentile threshold, impacts on plant production could be small. Thus, we only used these percentile thresholds to define future drought periods when a specific month of the year with CASW less than the threshold coincided with the plant gross primary productivity (GPP) being significantly lower than for other years within the modeled 1900–2050 time series. We used a Welch two-sample t-test (α = 0.01) to test the significance between GPP and specific months of the year when CASW <10th percentile. To limit the impact of bias from temporal autocorrelation on the standard-error estimates in the t-test, we subsampled the time series by retaining one monthly output every 5 years for CASW values above the tenth percentile.

When a future “drought” month was identified, we recalculated the relevant climate variables (i.e., temperature, precipitation, radiation, and specific humidity) to construct a “no-drought” climate forcing. Specifically, we first fitted a smooth spline to the simulation output data for a specific climate variable (e.g., precipitation), per grid cell and month (e.g., April) in the 1900–2050 time series of model outputs. This spline was then taken to represent the mean climate conditions for the month of interest in the period of 2015–2050. To generate a “no-drought” forcing time series for the same climate variable, that variable’s value was removed and replaced in the climate forcing time series by its value given by the spline fit (Supplementary Fig. 1). This “no-drought” forcing is paired with the original forcing (i.e., the “drought” forcing without drought removed) to assess drought impacts. For more information about creating drought and no-drought climate forcings, see Xu et al.15.

By running the CLM5 crop model driven by a no-drought and a drought climate forcing, we then created a drought impact index by assessing the percentage reduction in crop production induced by drought. Specifically, this was calculated based on the annual production difference between the drought and no-drought climate forcings. As our study is focused on country-level food security, we summed the grid-cell-specific production reduction due to drought for all the grid cells within an individual country. It is possible that the drought impact is mitigated by areas not hit by drought within the country; however, we consider this as a fair evaluation of country-specific drought vulnerability due to internal trade distributing production across the country. We acknowledge that our results are affected by multiple sources of uncertainty, including those associated with the applied climate forcing, model initial conditions, model parameters, and model “structure” (i.e., the choice of crop model used here). A rigorous exploration of uncertainty in crop model projection is beyond the scope of the current study. In this paper, we do consider one of the largest potential uncertainties—that is associated with climate forcing—by considering four climate projections that span a wide range of ECS (see Fig. 1). We note that, by limiting our simulations and analysis to 2050, we avoid the majority of the uncertainty associated with an unknown future emissions trajectory (i.e., “scenario uncertainty”)73. We minimized the impact of uncertainty in initial conditions by starting the model from an initial condition based on a climate that is in agreement with observations. We did not account for the uncertainty in model parameters due to the substantial computational cost associated with a large ensemble of simulations. Accounting for model structural uncertainty requires the use of multiple different process-based crop models, something that is not easily allowed for in CLM5 and would require substantial coordination among different research groups. Hence, exploring this source of projection uncertainty was outside the scope of our study, but it would be a promising avenue for future research.

We set countries with production levels <10 Mg when averaged across all GCMs and drought and no-drought scenarios to NA. It was assumed that these countries did not produce significant amounts of the crop in question, or that the resolution of our model was too coarse to capture production for very small nations.

GDP per capita, global population change, food price inflation, and political instability

As food security is not only tied to production, but also to a country’s ability to pay for imported food, population size, inflation rate, and political stability, we also examined GDP, population trends, food price inflation rates, and political instability. To determine which countries would be most vulnerable to future food insecurity, we calculated the mean GDP per capita ($US) for every country with publicly available data18 between 2015 and 2019 (i.e., pre-COVID supply chain and economic shocks). For population data, we used publicly available estimated total population from the United Nations19. Using the annual total population, by 5-year age group, major area, region, and country, 1950–2100 (thousands) medium fertility variant data file, we summed up each 5-year age group to get an average annual total population per country for the years 2015–2019 and 2045–2049. We then calculated the percent change in population between the two time periods. For food price inflation data, we calculated the mean food price inflation rate per country between 2015 and 2019 from the Food and Agriculture Organization (FAO)21. For political stability data, we calculated the mean percentile rank per country between 2015 and 2019 using the “Political Stability and Absence of Violence/Terrorism: Percentile Rank” data from the World Bank22. For the sake of this study, we assumed that all these indicators remained stable between 2015–2019 and 2045–2049.
