Coping with copper: a bibliometric analysis of copper’s role in cancer pathogenesis and treatment
Authors: Chaofan Chen, Qi Su, Min Zi, Yunchun Duan, Zhiyun Zhang
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractThis bibliometric analysis on copper’s role in cancer pathogenesis and treatment, spanning from 2014 to 2024, provides a comprehensive overview of current trends and emerging research areas. It highlights China’s dominance in research output and identifies a seminal 2022 study by Tsvetkov Peter as the most cited, cementing its importance in the field. Central keywords such as “DNA damage,” “trace elements,” and “oxidative stress” reflect the critical roles copper plays in cancer biology. As the field continues to evolve, key areas of focus for future studies are expected to include copper homeostasis, the roles of cuproptosis and ferroptosis, and the development of cancer prognosis models based on cuproptosis. This analysis offers valuable insights for advancing both scientific inquiry and clinical applications in this area.Keywords: CiteSpace, VOSviewer, Copper homeostasis, Cuproptosis, Visualization analysis

Introduction

In 2022, global cancer statistics reported nearly 20 million new cases and close to 10 million cancer-related deaths. By 2050, projections suggest that the number of new cancer cases could increase by 77%, reaching 35 million annually [1]. Cancer remains a leading cause of death worldwide and poses significant public health, societal, and economic challenges. In the USA alone, approximately 611,720 cancer-related deaths are expected in 2024, marking cancer as the primary disease affecting life expectancy [2].

Cancer is a complex disease driven by uncontrolled cell proliferation and influenced by various tumorigenic factors [3]. The tumor microenvironment (TME), composed of immune cells, fibroblasts, and endothelial cells, plays a critical role in supporting tumor growth, angiogenesis, metastasis, and inflammation [4]. Cell death in cancer can occur through mechanisms such as apoptosis, pyroptosis, and ferroptosis [5]. Recently, cuproptosis—copper-induced cell death—has been identified as a novel form of regulated cell death linked to mitochondrial function and the lipoic acid pathway [6]. Disruptions in copper homeostasis, leading to elevated intracellular copper levels, can activate signaling pathways that affect tumor progression [7]. Elevated copper concentrations are often found in various cancers, including breast, lung, colorectal, and prostate cancers [8].

Copper’s role in cancer is complex, with both tumor-promoting and tumor-suppressive effects [6, 9]. Studies suggest that cuproptosis can modulate the TME, particularly by influencing immune cell populations like CD8 + T cells, and impact tumor response to therapies [10]. While copper promotes tumorigenesis in certain contexts, excessive copper accumulation can disrupt cellular balance and trigger cuproptosis, presenting a potential therapeutic target for cancer treatment [11].

Cu ionophores, such as elesclomol and disulfiram, have shown promise in enhancing cuproptosis by increasing intracellular copper levels [12]. Elesclomol, for example, forms a complex with extracellular copper, facilitating its entry into cells and increasing reactive oxygen species (ROS) levels, which can lead to tumor cell death [13]. Other Cu ionophores, like ATSM and curcumin, also promote cuproptosis in cancer cells [14, 15]. Nanomedicine-based delivery systems, which exploit tumor-specific characteristics like acidic environments and elevated ROS levels, can improve the precision of Cu and Cu ionophore delivery, enhancing therapeutic efficacy while minimizing side effects. For instance, nanoparticle systems such as DSF@PEG/copper-HMSNs and Au NCs-Cu2 + @SA-HA NHGs have been developed to target tumor cells and induce cuproptosis [16, 17].

Recent studies have highlighted copper dysregulation in cancer patients, emphasizing its potential role in cancer initiation, progression, and response to treatment. While this field of research is still evolving, further understanding of copper’s contributions to cancer biology could lead to novel therapeutic strategies. Given the growing complexity of copper-related research, bibliometric analysis offers a valuable tool to assess trends and identify key areas of focus in the study of copper and cancer treatment. This study aims to use bibliometric techniques to explore the research landscape from 2014 to 2024, providing an in-depth analysis of the interplay between copper and cancer and guiding future directions for research in this promising area.

Material and methods

Data retrieval

Bibliometric data were retrieved from Web of Science Core Collection (WoSCC) database on March 31, 2024, using the following keyword query: (TI = (cancer* OR anticancer* OR tumor* OR tumour* OR oncology OR neoplasm* OR carcinoma*) AND TI = (copper* OR “copper-induced” OR “copper-mediated” OR cuproptosis OR “copper-triggered”)). The search was restricted to articles and reviews written in English between January 1, 2014, and October 31, 2024. Our search resulted in 2060 relevant papers (Fig. 1).

Flowchart of the literature screening process

Data processing

For the search outcomes described previously, the full records and cited references were exported in both plain text and tab-delimited formats. These plain text files underwent analysis using CiteSpace (version 6.1.R6), the tab-delimited files were subjected to analytical processing in VOSviewer (version 1.6.20.0), and the Online Analysis Platform for Bibliometrics (OALM) accessible at http://bibliometric.com/.

Data analysis

This study employed three bibliometric tools to comprehensively analyze and validate the findings. CiteSpace was applied for co-occurrence, clustering, and trend analyses; VOSviewer was used for co-occurrence and clustering; and the OALM platform supported relational network analysis. Additionally, the research documented each journal’s name, impact factor (IF), and ranking (Q1 to Q4, indicating its quartile within the category) based on the 2023 Journal Citation Reports (JCR). Microsoft Excel was utilized to illustrate global publication trends, track the evolution of relevant research, and generate charts displaying rankings of various metrics.

Research ethics

The data sources utilized in our study were derived from publicly accessible databases. As such, obtaining approval from an ethics committee was deemed unnecessary.

Discussion

General information

Over the past decade, interest in copper’s role in cancer has surged, reflected in increasing publication numbers and citation counts. This growth accelerated after 2021, spurred by significant clinical advancements and a pivotal interdisciplinary conference. In 2020, the Copper Cancer Consortium—organized by Linda Vahdat, Mick Petris, and Nick Tonks at the Banbury Center, Cold Spring Harbor Laboratory—gathered leading researchers from chemical, biological, and clinical fields to discuss the copper-cancer connection and address existing challenges [18]. This consortium introduced the concept of “cuproplasia,” defined as regulated, copper-dependent cell proliferation, which is part of a broader spectrum termed “metalloplasias.” Cuproplasia links copper to multiple cellular processes, including mitochondrial function, antioxidant defenses, redox and kinase signaling, autophagy, and protein quality control. Exploring new copper-dependent signaling pathways presents translational opportunities to target disease vulnerabilities related to this essential metal.

The results indicate that China leads with 1153 research publications from 2014 to 2024, followed by India and the USA. While the USA has fewer publications, it exhibits a high centrality score (0.18), highlighting its influential role in the field. In China, research often combines copper with nanotechnology for cancer treatment. Notable examples include combining disulfiram and copper-cysteamine nanoparticles to induce mitochondrial damage and apoptosis in endometrial cancer cells [19], developing a glucose oxidase (GOx)-engineered nonporous copper(I) 1,2,4-triazolate coordination polymer for enhanced cuproptosis and photodynamic therapy [20], and creating nanomedicines that induce cuproptosis with αPD-L1 for improved cancer therapy [21]. Additionally, researchers developed mitochondria-targeted, copper-depleting nanoparticles (CDNs) to treat triple-negative breast cancer, showing that CDNs disrupt cellular energy processes, resulting in apoptosis [22]. This leadership reflects China’s substantial investments in science and technology, notably prioritizing nanotechnology in its 2006–2020 Medium and Long Term Development Plan, with a target of 2.5% of GDP for scientific investment by 2020 [23]. While China focuses heavily on technological applications, the USA has advanced the theoretical understanding of copper’s biological role, with significant research into fundamental mechanisms such as cuproplasia—the regulated, copper-dependent cell proliferation—and copper homeostasis, demonstrating its key position in conceptual advancements within the field [6, 18].

Leading in publication volume is Frontiers in Oncology, an open-access, international journal dedicated to the latest advancements in oncology research. It covers a broad spectrum of topics, including tumor biology, cancer immunology, tumor genetics, radiation oncology, and innovative cancer treatment strategies. Co-citation analysis predominantly highlights high-impact factor (IF) and Q1 journals, with nature reaching an impressive IF of 50.3. The strong representation of these high-quality, impactful journals reflects the considerable interest in research on copper’s role in cancer, offering valuable direction for future scholars in selecting appropriate journals for manuscript submissions in this field.

Liang Hong from Guangxi Normal University leads in publications (25), with a research focus on designing novel anti-tumor metal drugs targeting DNA and proteins. Notably, Liang and their team synthesized twelve new copper complexes (Cu(L1)2 to Cu(L12)2) to enhance chemotherapeutic efficacy. These complexes demonstrated significant antiproliferative effects against T24 cancer cells while maintaining lower toxicity to normal human cells (HL-7702 and WI-38) [24]. Another achievement is the discovery of a copper-based Mcl-1 inhibitor, which exhibited potent anti-tumor effects in vivo, inducing tumor necrosis and extending survival in an NCI-H460 xenograft model [25]. Additionally, Liang’s work includes the development of a Copper(II) agent designed to target His-146 and His-242 residues of human serum albumin nanoparticles, specifically aimed at overcoming cisplatin resistance and inhibiting metastasis in non-small cell lung cancer, illustrating the potential of copper-based agents in cancer treatment [26].

Research hotspots

Centrality is a key metric for assessing the significance and influence of nodes within a research domain. In this study, the top three keywords with the highest centrality are “DNA damage,” “trace elements,” and “oxidative stress.” These keywords highlight critical areas of focus in the study of copper’s role in cancer, reflecting their relevance to the underlying mechanisms and therapeutic targets in the field.

Essential trace elements, required by the human body in small quantities, play critical roles in a wide array of biological processes. Copper, for instance, alternates between Cu(II) and Cu(I) oxidation states, with Cu(I) predominating in the reducing cytoplasmic environment. This redox interconversion endows copper with unique properties essential for various physiological functions [27]. While copper deficiency impairs growth, its excess induces oxidative stress, cell death, and tissue damage [28]. The role of copper in cancer is multifaceted, exhibiting both tumor-promoting and tumor-suppressive effects [9]. Although the concept of cuproptosis was formally identified in 2022, foundational research on the toxicity of metal complexes dates back to 1996 [29]. Since then, the field has witnessed a steady increase in annual publications. With accumulating evidence highlighting the connection between cuproptosis and cancer progression, this unique cell death mechanism has emerged as a prominent focus in cancer therapy research [30, 31].

After import into cells, copper ions bind to chaperone proteins for transport to various cellular compartments, where they regulate critical processes. In the cytoplasm, the copper chaperone for superoxide dismutase (CCS) delivers copper to superoxide dismutase 1 (SOD1), which incorporates copper into its prosthetic group [32]. SOD1 mitigates oxidative stress by converting superoxide radicals (O₂•⁻) into oxygen (O₂) and hydrogen peroxide (H₂O₂). Elevated SOD1 levels in specific contexts promote oncogene-driven tumorigenesis by reducing copper-induced oxidative stress and enhancing ribosome biogenesis, as shown in HER2-positive breast cancer and KRAS^G12D-mutant non-small-cell lung cancer (NSCLC) models [33–35]. SOD1 may also act as a suppressor of cuproptosis due to its pro-survival functions. Additionally, CCS transfers copper to MEK1 and MEK2, cuproenzymes essential for their kinase activity [36]. These kinases, integral to the RAS-RAF signaling pathway, are key drivers of malignant transformation and drug resistance [37].

Copper ions are transported to the trans-Golgi network by antioxidant 1 copper chaperone (ATOX1), which binds Cu(I) in the cytoplasm and delivers it to copper-transporting ATPases, such as ATP7A and ATP7B [38]. Mediator of cell motility 1 (MEMO1) forms a Cu(I) complex and transfers copper to ATOX1, reducing redox activity and ROS production in breast cancer cells [39]. Elevated intracellular copper triggers ATP7A and ATP7B relocation to post-Golgi sites to facilitate copper efflux via exocytosis [40, 41]. ATOX1 upregulation confers genotoxic drug resistance and enhances breast cancer cell migration through copper-dependent transcriptional activation of mediator of DNA damage checkpoint 1 (MDC1) and protein-lysine 6-oxidase (LOX) [42, 43]. Inhibiting the ATOX1 pathway with agents such as DC_AC50, a dual inhibitor of ATOX1 and CCS, has shown promise in preclinical models of breast cancer and BRAFV600E-mutant melanoma by suppressing copper-dependent tumor growth and metastasis (cuproplasia) [44, 45].

Copper contributes to tumor development through multiple interconnected mechanisms, highlighting its critical role in cancer biology. First, copper supports increased energy supply by serving as a cofactor for mitochondrial enzymes, such as MT-CO1 and MT-CO2, which are essential for oxidative phosphorylation and ATP production—processes particularly vital for rapidly dividing cancer cells [46]. Second, copper disrupts tumor-suppressor protein functions, such as p53, by displacing zinc from its structure. This displacement leads to abnormal folding and loss of tumor-suppressive activities, while potentially increasing mutations in TP53, further promoting tumorigenesis [47]. Copper also activates oncogenic signaling pathways, including the RAS–RAF–MEK–ERK and PI3K–AKT–mTOR cascades, by acting as a cofactor for kinases like MEK1 and MEK2, thereby enhancing phosphorylation events crucial for tumor growth. This copper-dependent signaling vulnerability has been demonstrated in models of KRAS-mutant cancers [46]. Additionally, copper stimulates autophagy by acting as a cofactor for ULK1 and ULK2, which initiate the formation of autophagosomal structures, supporting cancer cell survival and proliferation [48]. Furthermore, copper enhances the activity of transcription factors such as HIF1α and NF-κB. Copper indirectly promotes HIF1α binding to its co-activator p300 and its target gene motifs [49], while copper-mediated oxidative stress triggers NF-κB activation by degrading IκB, its primary repressor [50, 51]. These mechanisms collectively highlight copper’s pivotal role in facilitating tumor progression and underscore its potential as a therapeutic target in oncology.

Copper plays a dual and paradoxical role in regulating oxidative stress. As a cofactor for SOD1, copper contributes to the detoxification of superoxide radicals, thereby protecting cells from oxidative damage. Conversely, excessive copper accumulation can exacerbate oxidative stress by participating in the Fenton reaction, which generates highly reactive hydroxyl radicals. These radicals can inflict severe cellular damage, disrupt cellular homeostasis, and even induce cell death, underscoring the fine balance required to maintain copper levels within physiological ranges.

The primary effectors of cuproptosis remain elusive, though metabolic pathways significantly modulate its tumor-suppressive potential. Hypoxia (1% O2) reduces lung cancer cell sensitivity to cuproptosis by promoting glycolysis over oxidative phosphorylation. Conversely, the TCA cycle-derived metabolite 4-octyl itaconate, generated by aconitate decarboxylase 1 (ACOD1), enhances colorectal cancer cell susceptibility to cuproptosis by inhibiting aerobic glycolysis, particularly at glyceraldehyde-3-phosphate dehydrogenase (GAPDH) [52]. Maternal embryonic leucine zipper kinase (MELK) suppresses cuproptosis in hepatocellular carcinoma by upregulating DLAT expression through the PI3K–mTOR pathway, suggesting a glycolytic role for DLAT in impeding cuproptosis [53]. These findings indicate that glycolytic activity is a key determinant of cancer cell sensitivity to cuproptosis, with glycolysis inhibitors like shikonin and lonidamine potentially enhancing cuproptosis-mediated tumor suppression.

Additionally, copper imbalance—either deficiency or overload—can deplete ATP levels. Excessive AMP-activated protein kinase (AMPK) activation sensitizes pancreatic cancer cells to cuproptosis [54], likely via suppression of glycolysis through CARM1-mediated GAPDH methylation [55]. Conversely, early AMPK activation may mitigate mitochondrial proteotoxic stress by triggering the mitochondrial unfolded protein response, potentially counteracting cuproptosis. These observations position AMPK as a crucial metabolic checkpoint, influencing cell fate under metabolic stress [56].

Future trends

Burst detection analysis has identified emerging research trends by highlighting keywords that have seen significant citation surges, indicating periods of heightened academic interest. Recent studies on copper’s diverse roles in cancer have pinpointed several trending topics, including “tumor microenvironment,” “prognostic models,” “hepatocellular carcinoma,” “cell death,” “copper homeostasis,” “microenvironment,” and “ferroptosis.” These bursts underscore a growing focus on the complex involvement of copper in cancer progression, metastasis, and therapeutic resistance. This paper will provide an in-depth exploration of three key areas: (1) copper homeostasis, (2) the roles of cuproptosis and ferroptosis, and (3) the impact of cuproptosis on cancer prognosis models.

Copper homeostasis

The maintenance of intracellular copper homeostasis relies on the interplay of four types of proteins, and its dysregulation can disrupt cellular metabolism and lead to cell death. Copper’s functional roles in cancer cells are tightly regulated through copper homeostasis mechanisms and interactions among these protein groups.

The first group comprises proteins involved in copper transport across membranes. Similar to small intestinal epithelial cells, tumor cells rely on CTR1 (encoded by SLC31A1) for copper uptake, with its expression levels directly influencing intracellular copper ion concentrations [57]. In cases of genetic SLC31A1 deficiency, SLC11A2 (also known as divalent metal transporter 1, DMT1) has been identified as a compensatory mechanism for copper uptake [58]. CTR1 primarily transports monovalent copper ions. To facilitate cellular uptake, divalent copper ions in the bloodstream are reduced to monovalent ions by STEAP proteins, allowing them to bind to and be transported via the His-Met-Asp clusters at CTR1’s N-terminal domain [59, 60]. Within mitochondria, copper is transferred from the intermembrane space to the matrix through the transmembrane protein SLC25A3 [61]. Systemically, copper distribution occurs in two stages. Dietary copper is initially transported from the gut to the liver and kidneys, bound to albumin and transcuprein [62]. Subsequently, copper is distributed from the liver to other tissues via ceruloplasmin, a circulating carrier protein that binds approximately 95% of copper in the bloodstream [63]. Ceruloplasmin is a critical regulator of cuproplasia sensitivity, as demonstrated by the suppression of tumor growth and angiogenesis in breast cancer mouse xenograft models using the copper chelator tetrathiomolybdate, which inhibits cuproenzymes, including ceruloplasmin [64].

The second group comprises proteins that bind and store copper ions, protecting cells from the cytotoxic effects of unbound labile copper, which can generate ROS. Key copper-binding molecules include metallothioneins (MTs) and glutathione (GSH), which act as natural chelators to sequester excess copper ions [65]. MT1 and MT2 not only bind copper but also inhibit ATP7A trafficking in the trans-Golgi network, as shown in murine models [66]. Copper-induced oxidative stress activates the antioxidant transcription factor NFE2L2 (NRF2), which regulates MT and GSH synthesis by upregulating genes such as GCLM and GCLC [67, 68]. Mutations in KEAP1, commonly seen in cancers like NSCLC, lead to sustained NFE2L2 activation, enhancing cancer cell survival under stress. This pathway helps mitigate cell death from oxidative stress, chemotherapy, and potentially cuproptosis [69, 70].

The third group includes copper ion chaperones, which ensure proper copper cellular function [71]. Cytoplasmic chaperone ATOX1 binds Cu(I) via two cysteine residues, delivering it to ATP7B for export. The copper chaperone for superoxide dismutase (CCS) interacts with superoxide dismutase 1 (SOD1), transferring copper ions and facilitating disulfide formation essential for SOD1 activity [72, 73].

The fourth group includes intra-mitochondrial copper ion chaperones, essential for the function of cytochrome c oxidase (COX), a key enzyme in oxidative phosphorylation. These chaperones support COX assembly and activity by transporting and managing copper ions [74]. Cytochrome c oxidase copper chaperone (COX17) transfers copper ions from the cytoplasm to the mitochondrial intermembrane space [75], delivering them to the cysteine residues of SCO1 via disulfide bonds [76]. Cytochrome c oxidase assembly factor 6 (COA6) functions as a thiol-disulfide oxidoreductase, reducing disulfide bonds in SCO1 and SCO2 to enable copper binding [77, 78]. A deficiency in COA6 disrupts the biogenesis of respiratory complex IV [79]. SCO1 and SCO2 not only mediate copper transfer from COX17 to COX during assembly but also play crucial roles in maintaining cellular copper homeostasis, with their loss resulting in decreased intracellular copper levels [79, 80].

The roles of cuproptosis and ferroptosis

Ferroptosis is a regulated form of cell death triggered by excessive iron ions that drive the Fenton reaction, disrupting redox balance, mitochondrial function, and lipid metabolism [81, 82]. It is initiated through pathways such as PUFA-PL synthesis, iron metabolism, and mitochondrial activity, which induce lipid peroxidation. Protective systems like GPX4-GSH, FSP1-CoQH, and DHODH-CoQH2 counteract this process by inhibiting lipid peroxidation [20]. Recent research links ferroptosis to various cancers, including gastric, glioma, breast, and CRC [7].

A notable interplay exists between ferroptosis and cuproptosis, with mitochondria serving as a central hub [6, 83]. Both processes are deeply rooted in mitochondrial metabolism, particularly the TCA cycle [84]. For instance, intermediates like α-ketoglutarate and succinate influence cysteine-deprivation-induced ferroptosis [83], while lipoylated DLAT aggregation triggers elesclomol-Cu-induced cuproptosis [6]. Additionally, disruptions in the electron transport chain (ETC) impair both processes, underscoring their shared reliance on mitochondrial energy dynamics [6, 84].

Glutathione (GSH) emerges as a critical mediator linking ferroptosis and cuproptosis. By neutralizing oxidative stress and chelating Fe and Cu ions, GSH inhibits both processes [85]. However, excessive GSH consumption by metal ions or its depletion via inhibitors like BSO can initiate ferroptosis and cuproptosis [6, 86]. Targeting GSH synthesis or promoting its depletion is a potential strategy to induce both forms of cell death simultaneously, offering therapeutic potential in cancer.

Reactive oxygen species (ROS) further connect ferroptosis and cuproptosis. Fe and Cu generate ROS through the Fenton reaction, and GSH depletion exacerbates oxidative damage, fostering lipid peroxidation and ferroptosis [87]. While Cu-induced cuproptosis primarily results from mitochondrial protein aggregation, ROS accumulation and GSH depletion create conditions conducive to ferroptosis. Despite differences in their initiation and execution mechanisms, the interconnected pathways of ferroptosis and cuproptosis provide valuable insights and open avenues for innovative cancer therapies.

The impact of cuproptosis on cancer prognosis models

Researchers are increasingly investigating the role of cuproptosis-related genes (CRGs) in cancer, utilizing online databases to assess their potential in predicting cancer characteristics and patient outcomes [30]. Despite the challenges posed by the diverse and sometimes contradictory roles of CRGs across various tumor types, significant insights have been gained. For instance, DLD is identified as a risk factor in uveal melanoma (UVM) [88], glioma [89], and lung adenocarcinoma (LUAD) [90], whereas LIAS serves as a protective factor in UVM [88], glioma [91], and pancreatic adenocarcinoma (PAAD) [92, 93]. Similarly, genes such as DLAT [91, 92], FDX1 [94], and PDHA1 [90, 95] demonstrate tumor-dependent dual roles in prognosis, highlighting the complexity of CRG functions in cancer biology.

Comprehensive pan-cancer analyses reveal that CRGs exhibit diverse profiles, including somatic nucleotide variants (SNVs), copy number variations (CNVs), methylation patterns, and involvement in essential signaling pathways [96]. These variations underscore their multifaceted influence on tumor progression and patient prognosis. For example, dihydrolipoamide branched chain transacylase E2 (DBT) acts as a protective factor in LUAD and cervical cancer [97, 98], while cytochrome c oxidase assembly factor 19 (COX19), associated with copper homeostasis, is a risk factor in gliomas [91, 99], emphasizing the broad biological significance of CRGs. In addition to the survival analysis-based models mentioned above, cuproptosis-related signatures developed using alternative methodologies have also demonstrated the ability to predict clinical prognosis. For instance, these signatures have been shown to effectively forecast outcomes for patients with bladder cancer and kidney renal clear cell carcinoma (KIRC) [100, 101].

In conclusion, while cuproptosis-related gene (CRG) signatures show promise in predicting tumor prognosis and chemotherapy efficacy, their clinical application is hindered by limited biological validation and discrepancies influenced by the tumor microenvironment. The observed relationship between CRGs and therapeutic agents like bortezomib highlights their potential in guiding cancer treatment [102, 103], but further experimental studies are essential to confirm these findings and deepen our understanding of the mechanisms linking cuproptosis to cancer progression and therapy.

Limitations

This study has several limitations, primarily its reliance on English-language articles from the Web of Science Core Collection (WoSCC) database. While WoSCC is extensive, it may not capture high-quality research published in other languages or indexed in alternative databases. Moreover, recent high-quality publications may be underrepresented due to citation delays, potentially affecting the accuracy of trend analyses. Despite these limitations, the study provides valuable insights into the evolution, hotspots, and emerging areas related to copper’s role in cancer pathogenesis and treatment. The decision to focus on WoSCC, despite the possibility of incomplete data, is justified, as alternative databases like PubMed or Embase do not provide the necessary full-text access and citation analysis required for a comprehensive bibliometric review.

Conclusion

The bibliometric analysis of copper’s role in cancer pathogenesis and treatment, based on publications from 2014 to 2024, reveals significant contributions and emerging trends. A 2022 study by Tsvetkov Peter, published in Science, stands out with the highest citation, reflecting his influential work in the field. Among the major research areas, three keywords with the highest centrality are “DNA damage,” “trace elements,” and “oxidative stress,” underscoring their critical roles in understanding copper’s involvement in cancer. Looking ahead, three key areas are likely to shape future research: (1) copper homeostasis, (2) the roles of cuproptosis and ferroptosis, and (3) the impact of cuproptosis on cancer prognosis models. This study provides a comprehensive roadmap for future investigations into these critical areas, offering insights into how copper may influence cancer progression and therapeutic strategies.
