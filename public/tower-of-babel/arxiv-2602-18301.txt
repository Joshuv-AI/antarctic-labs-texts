On the Semantic and Syntactic Information Encoded in Proto-Tokens for One-Step Text Reconstruction
Ivan Bondarenko, Egor Palkin, Fedor Tikunov

Abstract
Autoregressive large language models (LLMs) generate text token-by-token, requiring n n forward passes to produce a sequence of length n n . Recent work, Exploring the Latent Capacity of LLMs for One-Step Text Reconstruction (Mezentsev and Oseledets), shows that frozen LLMs can reconstruct hundreds of tokens from only two learned proto-tokens in a single forward pass, suggesting a path beyond the autoregressive paradigm. In this paper, we study what information these proto-tokens encode and how they behave under reconstruction and controlled constraints. We perform a series of experiments aimed at disentangling semantic and syntactic content in the two proto-tokens, analyzing stability properties of the e e -token, and visualizing attention patterns to the e e -token during reconstruction. Finally, we test two regularization schemes for “imposing” semantic structure on the e e -token using teacher embeddings, including an anchor-based loss and a relational distillation objective. Our results indicate that the m m -token tends to capture semantic information more strongly than the e e -token under standard optimization; anchor-based constraints trade off sharply with reconstruction accuracy; and relational distillation can transfer batch-level semantic relations into the proto-token space without sacrificing reconstruction quality, supporting the feasibility of future non-autoregressive seq2seq systems that predict proto-tokens as an intermediate representation.



1
Introduction

Autoregressive LLMs generate text in a left-to-right fashion by predicting the next token conditioned on the previously generated context.
This approach has a fundamental computational limitation: generating a sequence of length
n
typically requires
n
forward passes of the model.

Non-autoregressive (NAR) text generation aims to reduce this sequential bottleneck by generating multiple tokens (or the whole sequence) in parallel, typically at the cost of additional modeling assumptions, iterative refinement, or distillation from autoregressive teachers.
Recent NAR approaches and analyses provide a broad view of the design space and its trade-offs
[
2
]
.

Recent work,
Exploring the Latent Capacity of LLMs for One-Step Text Reconstruction
(Mezentsev and Oseledets), demonstrates that frozen LLMs can reconstruct hundreds of tokens from only two learned proto-tokens in a single forward pass.
This observation motivates moving beyond the purely autoregressive paradigm.

We view the frozen-LLM plus learned proto-token interface as a complementary perspective on NAR generation: instead of training a fully new NAR model end-to-end, one can (i) keep a strong pretrained decoder frozen, (ii) predict a small set of continuous proto-tokens, and (iii) decode an entire sequence in one pass.
Moreover, our experiments explore explicitly distillation-style constraints (relational distillation) that transfer semantic structure into the proto-token space, connecting this approach to common NAR training recipes
[
2
]
.

Given such a reconstruction mechanism, a natural question arises: can proto-tokens be used not only for reconstruction, but also for
controlled
sequence continuation?
To answer this question, we must understand what information the proto-tokens (
e
and
m
) contain and how they behave during generation.

In this work, we perform the following experimental study of proto-tokens:

1.

Semantic content encoded in proto-tokens.

2.

Syntactic content encoded in proto-tokens.

3.

Stability of the
e
-token under optimization and constraints.

4.

Attention visualization to the
e
-token during reconstruction.

5.

Experiments on imposing semantic structure on the
e
-token using a teacher vector.

2
Related Work

Our work is most directly influenced by
Mezentsev and Oseledets 2025
(“Exploring the Latent Capacity of LLMs for One-Step Text Reconstruction”).
That paper shows that, by feeding two trainable proto-tokens into a frozen LLM and optimizing only their input embeddings, one can reconstruct sequences hundreds of tokens in a single forward pass.
Proto-token embeddings are learned by minimizing a cross-entropy reconstruction objective.

For broader context, we refer to recent work on non-autoregressive generation methods and their training strategies, including distillation-based objectives
[
2
]
.

In particular, the input to the frozen LLM can be viewed as a sequence of embeddings where two proto-token embeddings are placed first, followed by a fixed number of repeated
m
embeddings:

X=\left[e,\;\underbrace{m,\;m,\;\ldots,\;m}_{T-1}\right].

(1)

Training optimizes only the proto-token embeddings while the model weights remain frozen. Reconstruction is learned with the standard cross-entropy objective:

\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{CE}}=-\frac{1}{T}\sum_{i=1}^{T}\log p_{\theta}(t_{i}\mid X_{<i})=-\frac{1}{T}\sum_{i=1}^{T}\log\left(\frac{\exp(z_{i-1,t_{i}})}{\sum_{c\in V}\exp(z_{i-1,c})}\right).

(2)

Here
z_{i-1,\cdot}
are the model logits at position
(i-1)
,
V
is the vocabulary, and
t_{i}
is the
i
-th target token.

We extend this direction by analyzing what the two proto-tokens encode.
In particular, we provide evidence that
e
and
m
contain both semantic and syntactic information, though to different degrees.
These observations suggest the feasibility of a stable non-autoregressive seq2seq architecture in which a separate model predicts proto-tokens from past context, and a frozen LLM decodes them into continuations (e.g., for question answering).

3
Method

3.1
Proto-token optimization for reconstruction

We first describe how we obtain proto-token embeddings for one-step reconstruction.
Let the target token sequence be
T=[t_{1},\ldots,t_{T}]
and let
d
be the hidden size of the frozen causal LLM.
For each example, we optimize two learnable vectors
e,m\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
and build a proto-token-only input embedding sequence

X=[e,m,m,\ldots,m]\in\mathbb{R}^{T\times d},

(3)

where
m
is repeated
(T-1)
times.
We feed
X
to the frozen causal LLM with the standard causal attention mask and obtain logits
Z\in\mathbb{R}^{T\times|V|}
.
Following the next-token prediction convention, the logits at position
(i-1)
are used to predict token
t_{i}
.
We optimize only
e
and
m
using AdamW
[
4
]
until the reconstruction token accuracy reaches a threshold (e.g.,
0.9
) or a maximum number of iterations is reached.

3.2
Semantic augmentation experiment

To probe semantic content in proto-token representations, for each text in a subset of the dataset we generate
6
lexical and
9
semantic augmentations.
For each variant, we optimize proto-token embeddings using the procedure above and then visualize the resulting representations using t-SNE
[
3
]
.
The core hypothesis is: if proto-tokens encode semantic information, then t-SNE projections of proto-tokens for paraphrases of the same example should cluster together, even when surface form differs
[
3
]
.

Lexical augmentations.

Lexical augmentations are based on introducing typos, including:
(i) random case flips; (ii) frequent orthographic errors; (iii) common keyboard typing errors; and (iv) random deletion/insertion/repetition/swaps of characters.

Semantic augmentations.

Semantic augmentations are paraphrases generated by an LLM (Qwen3-4B).

3.3
Imposing semantic structure on the
e
-token

Because the semantic clustering for the
e
-token was weak in the experiment above, we hypothesize that non-autoregressive optimization admits many good solutions for
(e,m)
, and random initialization can lead to
e
vectors that do not preserve semantic relations across examples.
This motivates adding additional constraints to encourage semantic structure.

Terminology.

Throughout the paper we use the terms
proto-tokens
,
e
-token
, and
m
-token
consistently.
We refer to the decoding setting studied here as
one-step reconstruction
(not “one-step generation”).

We evaluate two regularization schemes:

1.

Anchor loss.
We add a term that encourages
e_{i}
to stay close to a teacher embedding
t_{i}
(obtained from Qwen3-Embedding-8B). We also initialize
e_{i}
as
t_{i}
:

\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{anchor}}=1-\cos(e_{i},t_{i}).

(4)

We combine the reconstruction loss with optional regularizers:

\mathcal{L}=\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{CE}}+\lambda_{\mathrm{anchor}}\,\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{anchor}}+\lambda_{\mathrm{rel}}\,\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{rel}}.

(5)

The anchor term is weighted by a separate hyperparameter in the global loss.

2.

Relational distillation.
We penalize mismatch between pairwise similarity matrices (using MSE/Huber) computed from teacher embeddings
t_{i}
and student embeddings
e_{i}
within a batch:

\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{rel}}=\frac{1}{B(B-1)}\sum_{i\neq j}\ell\left(S^{(E)}_{ij},\,S^{(T)}_{ij}\right),

(6)

where
B
is the batch size,
S^{(T)}_{ij}=\cos(t_{i},t_{j})
(teacher) and
S^{(E)}_{ij}=\cos(e_{i},e_{j})
(student), and
\ell(a,b)=(a-b)^{2}
(MSE) or
\ell(a,b)=\mathrm{Huber}(a-b)
.

3.4
Syntactic experiment

To probe syntactic content, we generate seven classes of sentences using an LLM and context-free grammars:
(1) simple, (2) complex, (3) simple interrogative, (4) complex interrogative, (5) simple imperative, (6) complex imperative, and (7) one-clause (single-member) sentences.
We then test whether t-SNE projections of proto-token representations cluster by syntactic class.

4.2
Robustness to additive noise in proto-tokens

We study how additive noise affects reconstruction quality when perturbing the optimized proto-token embeddings, focusing on the stability of the reconstruction process under embedding-level disturbances.
Given optimized proto-tokens
(e,m)
for a text example, we add noise to the
e
-token and measure reconstruction token accuracy as a function of noise intensity and distribution.

Noise distributions.

We consider four types of noise:

1.

Gaussian:
\varepsilon\sim\mathcal{N}(0,\sigma^{2})
.

2.

Uniform:
\varepsilon\sim\mathcal{U}(a,b)
.

3.

Exponential:
\varepsilon\sim\mathrm{Exp}(\lambda)
.

4.

Sinusoidal:
\varepsilon=\sin(\omega i+\phi)
(structured perturbation).

Normalization.

We scale the noise to control its magnitude relative to the original embedding norm:

\tilde{\varepsilon}=\frac{\varepsilon}{\lVert\varepsilon\rVert_{2}}\cdot\alpha\cdot\lVert e\rVert_{2},

(7)

where
\alpha\in\{0.0,0.05,0.1,0.2,0.5,1.0\}
controls intensity.

Figure 4
:
Reconstruction accuracy as a function of noise intensity
\alpha
for different noise types.

As expected, reconstruction quality degrades as the noise level increases; different noise distributions exhibit similar trends in our setup.

Anchor loss.

With a small anchor weight (e.g.,
0.02

However, the final cosine similarity between

Increasing the anchor weight (e.g., to

Overall, these results do not support the hypothesis that

4.6
Syntactic content and attention patterns

Our syntactic clustering and attention analyses suggest that proto-tokens also encode information correlated with syntactic structure.
These observations support the working hypothesis that, if
m
is more semantically aligned, then
e
may carry more syntactic information; we leave a detailed quantitative study as future work.

5
Discussion and Conclusion

We studied proto-tokens used for one-step reconstruction with frozen LLMs and investigated what information they encode.
Our experiments indicate that:

•

Under unconstrained optimization, the
m
-token tends to capture semantic information more strongly than the
e
-token.

•

Anchor-based regularization that forces
e
toward a teacher sentence embedding introduces a sharp trade-off: stronger anchoring improves similarity but quickly harms reconstruction accuracy, and weak anchoring does not reliably align
e
with the teacher space.

•

Relational distillation is a more promising alternative: it transfers batch-level semantic relations into the proto-token space while preserving high reconstruction performance.

•

Proto-tokens appear to encode syntactic information as well, and attention patterns suggest that the model actively uses the
e
-token during decoding.

These findings support the feasibility of a non-autoregressive seq2seq pipeline in which a lightweight predictor maps context to proto-tokens, and a frozen LLM decodes them into text.
Future work includes (i) building an explicit proto-token predictor for controlled generation, (ii) quantifying syntactic vs. semantic disentanglement, and (iii) improving identifiability and stability of proto-token representations across optimization runs.
