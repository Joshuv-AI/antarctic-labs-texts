SLURG: Investigating the Feasibility of Generating Synthetic Online Fallacious Discourse
Cal Blanco Gavin D’Souza Hugo Lin Chelsey Rush

Abstract
In our paper we explore the definition, and extrapolation of fallacies as they pertain to the automatic detection of manipulation on social media. In particular we explore how these logical fallacies might appear in the real world i.e internet forums. We discovered a prevalence of misinformation / misguided intention in discussion boards specifically centered around the Ukrainian Russian Conflict which serves to narrow the domain of our task. Although automatic fallacy detection has gained attention recently, most datasets use unregulated fallacy taxonomies or are limited to formal linguistic domains like political debates or news reports. Online discourse, however, often features non-standardized and diverse language not captured in these domains. We present Shady Linguistic Utterance Replication-Generation (SLURG) to address these limitations, exploring the feasibility of generating synthetic fallacious forum-style comments using large language models (LLMs), specifically DeepHermes-3-Mistral-24B. Our findings indicate that LLMs can replicate the syntactic patterns of real data and that high-quality few-shot prompts enhance LLMs’ ability to mimic the vocabulary diversity of online forums .

1
Introduction

As the amount of misinformation continues to rise both online and off, misinformation detection has become a key task for the Natural Language Processing community. Being able to distinguish fact from fiction in online and real-word environments is a critical component of developing and maintaining public trust and social stability
Su et al. (2020)
.

1.1
Beyond Manipulation Detection: Fallacious Reasoning

Perhaps a step beyond misinformation detection is fallacy detection. Fallacy detection aims to identify faulty reasoning that occurs in media as a result of erroneous or invalid argument form or content
Dowden (2020)
. Fallacious arguments can occur across
any type of media
, including social media, speeches, advertisements, and even political debates
Danciu et al. (2014)
. Furthermore, research has demonstrated that fallacies are becoming more and more evident in propaganda and fake news initiatives
Musi et al. (2022)
, and can be used to promote specific agendas
Elsayed et al. (2020)
. For example, the statement ‘If we allow gay marriage, then in the future people will want to marry their pets,’ this is a
slippery slope fallacy
: the claim illogically argues that action A will inevitably lead to the negative outcome B, so action A should be avoided entirely
Corner et al. (2011)
.

Fallacious reasoning is a common hallmark of propaganda techniques which can may be used to sway public opinion or promote specific agendas
Tîrnăuceanu (2015)
. However, correctly identifying fallacious reasoning is an inherently difficult task because errors in reasoning can be context-dependent or rely on interpretation as opposed to abject truth. In addition, it is possible to arrive at a correct conclusion through fallacious reasoning; so goes the phrase ‘a stopped clock is right twice a day’.

The use of deep learning techniques for the automatic detection of fallacious reasoning has shown promising results for detecting propaganda and misinformation spread (
Abdullah et al. (2022)
;
Al-Omari et al. (2019)
;
Sourati et al. (2023)
).

1.2
Deep Learning for Automatic Detection of Fallacies

However, as the use of deep learning techniques for fallacy identification has gained more traction, it has become increasingly important to adopt a consensus when defining and classifying fallacies. Recent work on identifying fallacies in text has focused heavily on developing a united framework for defining, organizing, and labeling textual fallacies. Work from
Helwe et al. (2023)
seems to be the most promising in terms of its hierarchical organization, sociology-based definitions, and empirical approach for NLP-based labeling of fallacies. They suggest a unified, hierarchical taxonomy of fallacies that consolidates all of the fallacies currently being used for fallacy detection; the taxonomy and hierarchal structure of fallacies they suggest is presented in Figure
2
.

Although automatic fallacy detection has garnered more attention recent years, the majority of datasets are domain-specific and curated from news outlets, reasoning datasets, and political debates
Da San Martino et al. (2019)
;
Jin et al. (2022)
;
Goffredo et al. (2023)
. As a product of these domains, these datasets are often composed of formal language and domain-specific vocabulary. However, the vocabulary, conversational style, and structure of the language used by social media users on the internet varies drastically from the highly formal language used by politicians and news reporters.

1.3
The Rise of Synthetic Data

Although it used to take much longer for machine learning models to reach or surpass human-level performance on benchmark tasks, modern machine learning models are saturating benchmarks at increasingly faster rates
Kiela et al. (2021)
. As models improve faster and faster, the demand for high-quality, challenging datasets has also risen, ushering in the era of synthetic data generation. Generating synthetic data, specifically from large language models (LLMs) offer a possible solution to both increasing demand for large volumes of data, as well as reducing the cost incurred when using human annotators.

Synthetic data generation has shown great promise for improving model performance across many tasks in NLP.
Niu et al. (2024)
show that generating artificial user-agent interactions can improve the performance of dialogue state tracking models, even with lower amounts of real data. Of particular interest for this task, research such as that from
Balog et al. (2024)
investigate the feasibility of producing realistic synthetic user social media discussions.

1.4
Our Contribution

In this paper, we investigate the feasibility of generating synthetic social media posts using LLMs. We narrow the scope of our task by restricting the domain to discussion of Ukrainian-Russian conflict, following the 2025 Ukrainian Natural Language Processing (UNLP) conference’s
Shared Task on Detecting Social Media Manipulation
. Using the proposed taxonomy of fallacies, we aim to address a lack of informal, text-based data for improving the automatic detection of fallacious reasoning in social media posts.

We choose this task because we believe that the ability to correctly identify and flag fallacious reasoning on social media would protect individuals and society from manipulation, misinformation, and cognitive biases by preventing the spread of misinformation, making online discourse more constructive, and promoting media literacy. We believe that investigating the feasibility of generating synthetic data for this task is an important contribution because it addresses the domain-specific limitations of datasets currently available for this topic in the field of NLP.

1.5
Why This Task Is Important

As stated previously in the introduction the ability to discern manipulation and propaganda from social media posts is increasingly important. A large population of the world is actively using social media and browsing the platforms that we sourced this data from. Not only that but state actors have also been increasingly interested in the dissemination of propaganda on social media, as well as the detection of said propaganda
of Public Affairs (2024)
.

With our world increasingly becoming more digital we believe that being able to determine, and understand propaganda will be the main venue to combat misinformation.

Data

In order to obtain the domain-specific data necessary to approach the task, we scraped social media posts from two forum-based social media sites: Reddit and 4chan. We scraped 2,163 comments from 3 subreddits r/UkraineWarReport, r/UkraineConflict, and r/UkraineWar. Furthermore, we also obtained 1,627 comments from 4chan, sourced from the /pol/ (meaning ‘politically correct’) discussion board using regular expressions matching ‘ukraine’, ‘russia’, ‘putin’, or ‘zelensky’. We chose these specific social media platforms because they host diverse arguments and debates, user-generated content, and threaded discussions; this is ideal because the domain of discussion is fairly narrow while guaranteeing that discussion and discourse will be available. In addition, both of these sites allow for user anonymity and low levels of moderation, meaning that extreme rhetoric is more likely to appear. For this reason, a large portion of our scraped data contains profanity, harmful language, and extremist rhetoric. Although keeping this offensive language in our dataset made our task more difficult, we felt that it was important to capture this language use to create a dataset which accurately depicts the sentiment and expressivity of real users currently discussing these topics.

3
Methodology

3.1
Annotation

We compiled an annotation dataset by randomly sampling from both the Reddit and 4chan data given that the sample was longer than 32 characters. Each of the four members of our group annotated 150 randomized samples from across both datasets.

Then, we annotated the samples using tier one of the fallacy taxonomy and definitions established by
Helwe et al. (2023)
. We annotated the posts for three different kinds of fallacious reasoning
and
their spans:
fallacy of credibility
,
fallacy of logic
, and
appeal to emotion
. We chose to annotate samples based on tier one of the unified framework of fallacies because we felt that doing fine-grained annotation of with all 23 of the fallacy labels would be too difficult and time-consuming for the time allotted for this assignment. However, annotating for the three overarching labels defined in tier one was much more feasible. A full list of our guidelines and the fallacies being annotated is available in Appendix
A
.

Annotators agreed to annotate the smallest meaningful phrases containing fallacies, which could include subclauses or entire sentences. In addition, one span may be labeled with multiple fallacies, just as one sentence may also contain two different spans with two different fallacy labels.

The sample below shows how a sample was labeled by one of the annotators. This sample is labeled as an emotional fallacy because it is using an appeal to fear, an appeal to emotion fallacy, in order to discourage other users from engaging with the content they have posted. This annotation also contains a nested credibility fallacy that labels certain users as ‘Russian troll(s)’ to discredit their statements.

Sample Annotation

<emotional_fallacy> Every time <credibility_fallacy> a Russian troll </credibility_fallacy> argues with me on Reddit, more of his friends get visited by drones. </emotional_fallacy>

3.2
Inter-annotator Agreement

We evaluated the quality of our annotations by calculating the inter-annotator agreement (IAA) scores across all of our annotations using the Jaccard Index
Costa (2021)
. We elected to use this metric for calculating IAA because it quantifies the similarity and relative interiority across labeled spans.
The Jaccard Index is calculated as:

\text{IoU}(A,B)=\frac{|A\cap B|}{|A\cup B|}

Where A and B represent binary vectors of spans labeled by two different annotators. When neither annotator applied a particular label to a sample, we assigned perfect agreement (1.0). The overall agreement between annotator pairs was calculated as the average IoU across all three fallacy labels.
A confusion matrix of our IAA scores using the Jaccard Index is shown in Figure
5
. Annotators 1, 2, and 3 had average IAA scores exceeding 0.694, while annotator 4 ’s average IAA score ranged from 0.51 to 0.53.

Figure 5:

Evaluation Criteria

In order to evaluate the resulting synthetic data from our models we created three main criteria to judge with. The three criteria we used are as follows:

1.

Realism

•

How similar is the generated data to real comments

•

Does the models coherence match real user data

2.

Fallacy Accuracy

•

Is the logical fallacy in the generated data sensible

3.

Span Accuracy

•

Is the span for the logical fallacy accurately represented

Furthermore we evaluated the criteria on a Likert scale to avoid overuse of a middle / median value in judging. Each split has it’s own scoring in each of the criteria, as well as a final averaged score between the criteria to judge the split’s generation overall. However there is still a lot of informational value to each experiments individual score per criteria.

3.6
Implementation Details

Due to time and resource constraints we limit our experimentation to open source models. In particular, we use DeepHermes-3-Mistral-24B-Preview by Nous Research. We chose this model because it is relatively uncensored compared to closed models available via commercial API. Moreover, this model is trained to religiously follow user instructions, specifically generating structured outputs. This is essential for the purpose of our task since we direct to model to annotate fallacy spans with XML tags. For annotation, we used a temperature of
0.7
and a top_p (nucleus sampling) of
0.9
. Through manual trial and error, we found that a temperature of
1.2
and top_p of
0.9
gave us a good balance between novelty/creativity and coherence for generating synthetic text. We used Anthropic’s prompt generation tool to produce the Prompt Templates and ran the model using vLLM on 4 Nvidia RTX 3090 GPUs.

5.3
Quantitative Analysis of Generated Synthetic Data

Because they are auto-regressive, one of the main concerns regarding to the feasibility of using LLMs to generate synthetic user data is the fact that LLMs may be more likely to use repetitive vocabulary or syntactic structures. In addition to, diversity in content and structure is a key component of a useful benchmark, we also analyzed the generated synthetic data for both vocabulary and syntactic diversity.

We evaluated the vocabulary diversity and authorial style of DeepHermes-3-Mistral-24B when zero-prompted and few-shot prompted using the hapax-legomena ratio. This measure quantifies the ratio of
hapaxes
, token only occurring once per sentence, and is often used to compute authorial style as well as providing a statistical measure of text.

As shown in Figure
9
, the hapax-legomena ratio for the real data collected from Reddit and 4chan shows a very high distribution centering about 100%, with means of 0.887 and 0.872, respectively (note the almost solid lines of points clustered about 100%). This means that after removing English stopwords, at the sentence-level real user’s comments tended to consist of entirely unique tokens. When few-shot prompted with more gold-labeled annotations, the mean hapax-legomena ratio of the generated synthetic data increased from 0.762 to 0.852. Noteably, when few-shot prompted with 20% and 30% of the gold-labeled annotations, the hapax-legomena ratio of the generated data also became more heavily clustered about 100%. This suggests that when provided with few-shot prompts based on gold-label annotations, the model is better able to generate phrases with vocabulary diversity and authorial style comparable to the comments obtained from Reddit and 4chan.

Figure 9:
Hapax legomena ratio distribution for the synthetic data generated by DeepHermes-3-Mistral-24B when zero-shot prompted and few-shot prompted with gold labels and real data scraped from Reddit and 4chan.

In addition, we also analyzed the syntactic diversity of the sentences produced by DeepHermes. Interestingly, each of the datasets exhibited very similar distributions of subclauses, noun phrases (NP), prepositional phrases (PP), and verb phrases (VP). This suggests that
comments posted to online forums such as Reddit and 4chan may have a distinct syntactical style
, and may also indicate that DeepHermes-3-Mistral-24B has trained on this content and is thus able to
closely replicate the syntactic distributions found in the real data
.

Limitations

Our approach centered around being able to generate true to life synthetic data mimicking user comments on political discourse forums. While we did see a diverse set of opinions within the particular topic of the Ukraine Russia Conflict, we have also limited the scope of our ideation to that topic. Going forward we believe it would be beneficial to source data from more politically charged boards / forums to provide a even more generalizable dataset for detecting this type of speech.

Second, the identification of fallacies and manipulative discourse relies on predefined annotation guidelines, which may not capture the full range of nuanced argumentation strategies present in online discourse. Subtle rhetorical devices and emerging misinformation tactics may evade detection due to the static nature of our labeled dataset, and the dynamic nature of online discourse.

Third, the deep learning model used in our study required significant computational resources, making it challenging to deploy in real-time applications. The cost of existing SOTA models also deeply limited our ability to evaluate on more models. Additionally, models trained on our dataset may suffer from domain transfer issues when applied to different social media platforms or languages.

Finally, human annotators’ subjectivity in labeling fallacious and manipulative content poses another challenge. Despite quality control measures, inherent disagreements in classification may affect the reliability of the dataset and, consequently, the synthetic data generated by the models.

5.4
Addressing the Limitations

To address these limitations, we would make the following improvements to the annotation process:

1. Diversify Data Sources: To reduce bias introduced by focusing on war-based and politically charged discussions, we would expand the dataset to include a broader range of topics and social media platforms. This would enhance the model’s generizability across different contexts.

2. Refine Annotation Guidelines: To better capture nuanced argumentation strategies, we would iteratively refine annotation guidelines by incorporating insights from discourse analysis and misinformation studies. Engaging domain experts in linguistics, psychology, and political science could help improve the identification of manipulative rhetoric. Also importantly ensuring are all on the same page about the historical context, vocabulary, and ideology present in each topic would greatly increase our annotator agreement.

3. Utilize more models to perform evaluation on the gold label data to determine a good starting model for fallacy detection. Furthermore experiment with fine tuning models on this particular task to potentially reduce the computational cost of the task.

4. Reduce Subjectivity in Labeling: To minimize annotator subjectivity, we would introduce a consensus-based annotation approach, where multiple annotators label the same data and disagreements are resolved through discussion or statistical reconciliation methods. Additionally, training annotators with detailed case studies and real-world examples would improve consistency.

5. Enhance Domain Adaptability: Given domain transfer challenges, we would create a multi-domain annotation corpus by incorporating content from different languages and social media platforms. This would help train models that perform more robustly across varied contexts.

By implementing these changes, the annotation process would yield a more comprehensive, reliable, and adaptable dataset, ultimately improving the effectiveness of manipulation detection models.

6
Conclusion

Our exploration of synthetic data generation for logical fallacy detection in social media comments reveals both strengths and challenges. While large language models can generate content similar to real-world data, important differences remain.

Real web users employ diverse vocabularies with idiosyncratic expressions that models struggle to fully replicate.
Paradoxically, LLM-generated content often appears more coherent than authentic social media posts,
making it distinguishable from human writing. However, our analysis shows that
few-shot prompting with gold-labeled examples significantly improves vocabulary diversity metrics
as per figure
9
, bringing synthetic data closer to genuine Reddit and 4chan comments.

Annotating logical fallacies presents inherent challenges
due to subjectivity and the necessity of understanding broader context. The determination of user intention—whether someone is deliberately spreading misinformation or simply lacks understanding—further complicates both annotation and detection system development.

We observed that LLMs attempt to simulate "authentic" social media content by incorporating more profanity and inflammatory language. Interestingly, some posts collected from real platforms appeared suspiciously like LLM-generated content themselves, raising questions about the prevalence of synthetic content already existing in online forums.

Despite these challenges,
our research demonstrates the feasibility of using synthetic data for fallacious online discourse.
As synthetic content becomes increasingly common online, understanding how this data can be generated and classified remains crucial for maintaining the integrity of online discourse.

Ethical Considerations

The development and deployment of manipulation detection datasets and models raise important ethical considerations. One primary concern is the offensive and harmful language and rhetoric present in our dataset. Since our dataset is derived from social media discussions, it may reflect societal biases present in online discourse, potentially leading to disproportionate misclassifications of certain viewpoints. It also contains offensive language, harmful rhetoric, and extremist views. Although offensive, we believe that including uncensored data from online forums is a crucial aspect of building a dataset which can be used to accurately identify fallacious reasoning in online discourse.

Another critical issue is the risk of censorship and misuse. Automatic fallacy detection systems, if deployed without proper oversight, could be used to suppress legitimate discourse rather than mitigating misinformation or propaganda spread. Ensuring transparency in regards to how these models make decisions is crucial to ensuring that valid discourse is not censored or removed inadvertently. This risk of censorship is also problematically impacted by the subjective nature of misinformation, it would be extremely difficult to ensure that only "correct" information is posted to the internet without some how impacting or silencing the voices of people raising real concerns.

Privacy concerns also arise when analyzing social media data. Although our study focuses on publicly available content, the aggregation and analysis of conversations could raise ethical questions about user consent and data protection. It is essential to follow ethical guidelines for data handling and anonymization to safeguard individual privacy.

Lastly, there is a broader societal impact to consider. Misinformation detection, including fallacy identification, is an ongoing challenge, and the effectiveness of our approach must be balanced with considerations of free speech and open discourse. Researchers and practitioners should work closely with policymakers and the public to ensure that these technologies serve the collective good without infringing on rights and freedoms.

Appendix A
Taxonomy of Fallacies for Annotation

A.1
Fallacy of Credibility

•

ad hominem:
This fallacy involves attacking a person’s character or motives instead of addressing the substance of their argument.

•

ad populum:
This fallacy involves claiming that an idea or action is valid because it is popular or widely accepted.

•

appeal to authority:
This fallacy occurs when an argument relies on the opinion or endorsement of an authority figure who may not have relevant expertise or whose expertise is questionable. When applicable, a scientific consensus is not an appeal to authority.

•

appeal to nature:
This fallacy occurs when something is assumed to be good or desirable simply because it is natural, while its unnatural counterpart is assumed to be bad or undesirable.

•

appeal to tradition:
This fallacy involves arguing that something should continue to be done a certain way because it has always been done that way, rather than evaluating its merits.

•

tu quoque:
This fallacy occurs when someone’s argument is dismissed because they are accused of acting inconsistently with their claim, rather than addressing the argument itself.

A.2
Fallacy of Logic

•

causal oversimplification:
This fallacy occurs when a complex issue is reduced to a single cause and effect, oversimplifying the actual relationships between events or factors.

•

circular reasoning:
This fallacy occurs when an argument assumes the very thing it is trying to prove, resulting in a circular and logically invalid argument.

•

equivocation:
This fallacy involves using ambiguous language or changing the meaning of a term within an argument, leading to confusion and false conclusions.

•

false analogy:
This fallacy involves making an analogy between two elements based on superficial resemblance.

•

false causality:
This fallacy involves incorrectly assuming that one event causes another, usually based on temporal order or correlation rather than a proven causal relationship.

•

false dilemma:
This fallacy occurs when only two options are presented in an argument, even though more options may exist.

•

hasty generalization:
This fallacy occurs when a conclusion is drawn based on insufficient or unrepresentative evidence.

•

slippery slope:
This fallacy occurs when it is claimed that a small step will inevitably lead to a chain of events, resulting in a significant negative outcome.

•

straw man:
This fallacy involves misrepresenting an opponent’s argument, making it easier to attack and discredit.

•

fallacy of division:
This fallacy involves assuming that if something is true for a whole, it must also be true of all or some of its parts.

A.3
Appeal to Emotion

•

appeal to positive emotion:
Reasoning which includes positively emotionally charged language like pride, vanity, flattery, reverence, hope, optimism, pleasure, to invoke a positive emotional reaction from the audience.

•

appeal to fear:
Fear or threats are used as the main justification of the argument.

•

appeal to pity:
Uses sympathy or compassion as the main justification of an argument rather than reasoning or evidence.

•

appeal to anger:
Uses anger (toward a person, thing, or idea) or indignation as the justification for an argument.

•

appeal to ridicule:
An opponent’s argument is portrayed as absurd or ridiculous with the intention of discrediting it.

•

appeal to worse problem:
Dismisses an argument or problem by claiming there are more important issues to deal with.
