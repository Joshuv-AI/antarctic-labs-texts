Uniform boundedness of conformal energy for the 3D nonlinear wave equation
Jingya Zhao

Abstract
. In this paper, we study three-dimensional nonlinear wave equations under the null condition, a fundamental model in the theory of nonlinear wave-type equations, initially investigated by Christodoulou [ 6 ] and Klainerman [ 22 ] . For a class of large initial data, we establish global existence and linear scattering of solutions by combining refined energy estimates with a bootstrap argument. Moreover, we prove that the lower-order conformal energy remains uniformly bounded for all time. Keywords: Nonlinear wave equation; Null condition; Global existence; Conformal energy



1.
Introduction

1.1.
Model problem

We consider the following nonlinear wave equation in
\mathbb{R}^{1+3}
, which reads

-\Box w=P^{\gamma\alpha\beta}\partial_{\gamma}w\partial_{\alpha}\partial_{\beta}w+\partial^{\alpha}w\partial_{\alpha}w,

(1.1)

with the initial data prescribed at
t=0

(w,\partial_{t}w)|_{t=0}=(w_{0},w_{1}).

(1.2)

In the above,
\Box=m^{\alpha\beta}\partial_{\alpha}\partial_{\beta}=-\partial_{t}^{2}+\partial_{1}^{2}+\partial_{2}^{2}+\partial_{3}^{2}
denotes the wave operator associated with the Minkowski metric
m=\mathrm{diag}(-1,1,1,1)
. Indices are raised and lowered with respect to
m
. The coefficients
P^{\gamma\alpha\beta}=P^{\gamma\beta\alpha}
are constants satisfying the null condition

P^{\gamma\alpha\beta}\xi_{\gamma}\xi_{\alpha}\xi_{\beta}=0,\qquad\forall\,\xi\in\mathbb{R}^{4}\text{ with }\xi_{0}^{2}=\sum_{a=1}^{3}\xi_{a}^{2}.

The null condition, first introduced by Klainerman
[
20
,
22
]
, arises naturally in various nonlinear wave equations. Throughout this paper, we adopt the Einstein summation convention for repeated upper and lower indices. Greek indices
\alpha,\beta,\ldots
range over
\{0,1,2,3\}
, while Roman indices
a,b,\ldots
range over
\{1,2,3\}
.

1.2.
Main results

In this work, we investigate the nonlinear wave equation (
1.1
) with a class of large initial data. We establish the global existence of solutions together with linear scattering, prove the uniform boundedness of both the natural (ghost weight) energy and the lower-order conformal energy, and derive pointwise decay estimates. The main results are summarized below.

Theorem 1.1
.

Let
N\in\mathbb{N}
with
N\geq 5
. For any
K>1
, there exists an
\varepsilon_{0}>0
, depending polynomially on
K
, such that for all initial data
(w_{0},w_{1})
satisfying

\displaystyle\sum_{0\leq|I|\leq N}\|\langle x\rangle^{|I|}\nabla\nabla^{I}w_{0}\|_{L^{2}}+\sum_{0\leq|I|\leq N}\|\langle x\rangle^{|I|}\nabla^{I}w_{1}\|_{L^{2}}\leq K,

(1.3)

\displaystyle\sum_{0\leq|I|\leq N-1}\|\langle x\rangle^{|I|}\nabla\nabla\nabla^{I}w_{0}\|_{L^{2}}+\sum_{0\leq|I|\leq N-1}\|\langle x\rangle^{|I|}\nabla\nabla^{I}w_{1}\|_{L^{2}}\leq\varepsilon<\varepsilon_{0},

(1.4)

the Cauchy problem (
1.1
)–(
1.2
) admits a global-in-time solution
w
satisfying

|\partial w(t,x)|\leq C\langle t+|x|\rangle^{-1}\langle t-|x|\rangle^{-\frac{1}{2}},\quad|\partial\partial w(t,x)|\leq C\varepsilon\langle t+|x|\rangle^{-1}\langle t-|x|\rangle^{-\frac{1}{2}},

(1.5)

and the ghost weight energy is uniformly bounded, i.e.,

\sum_{|I|\leq N}\mathcal{E}_{gst}^{\frac{1}{2}}(t,\Gamma^{I}w)\leq C,

(1.6)

for some constant
C
independent of
t
. In addition, the solution
w
scatters linearly.

Remark 1.2
.

Nontrivial initial data
(w_{0},w_{1})
satisfying the conditions (
1.3
) and (
1.4
) do exist.
For instance, one may take the initial data of the form
[
13
]

\nabla w_{0}(x)=\frac{f_{\varepsilon}(x)+\varepsilon f(x)}{\|f_{\varepsilon}(x)+\varepsilon f(x)\|_{L^{2}}},\qquad w_{1}(x)=\frac{g_{\varepsilon}(x)+\varepsilon g(x)}{\|g_{\varepsilon}(x)+\varepsilon g(x)\|_{L^{2}}},

where
f,g
are arbitrary smooth functions with sufficiently fast decay at spatial infinity, and

f_{\varepsilon}(x)=\varepsilon^{\frac{3}{2}}f(\varepsilon x),\qquad g_{\varepsilon}(x)=\varepsilon^{\frac{3}{2}}g(\varepsilon x).

Such a choice ensures that the weighted norms in (
1.3
) remain bounded by
K
, while those in (
1.4
) can be made arbitrarily small by choosing
\varepsilon>0
sufficiently small.

Theorem 1.3
.

In addition to the assumptions of Theorem
1.1
, suppose further that the initial data

(w_{0},w_{1})
satisfy

\displaystyle\sum_{0\leq|I|\leq N}

\displaystyle\|\langle x\rangle^{|I|}\nabla^{I}w_{0}\|_{L^{2}}+\sum_{0\leq|I|\leq N-1}\|\langle x\rangle^{|I|+1}\nabla^{I}w_{1}\|_{L^{2}}

(1.7)

\displaystyle+\sum_{1\leq|I|\leq N-1}\|\langle x\rangle^{|I|}\nabla\nabla^{I}w_{0}\|_{L^{1}}+\sum_{0\leq|I|\leq N-1}\|\langle x\rangle^{|I|}\nabla^{I}w_{1}\|_{L^{1}}<+\infty.

Then the corresponding global solution
w
given by Theorem
1.1
enjoys the improved pointwise decay estimates

\left|w(t,x)\right|\leq C\langle t+|x|\rangle^{-1}\langle t-|x|\rangle^{-\frac{1}{2}},\quad\left|\partial w(t,x)\right|\leq C\langle t+|x|\rangle^{-1}\langle t-|x|\rangle^{-\frac{3}{2}},

(1.8)

and the conformal energy remains uniformly bounded for all
t\geq 0
, i.e.,

\sum_{|I|\leq N-3}\mathcal{E}_{con}^{\frac{1}{2}}(t,\Gamma^{I}w)\leq C,

(1.9)

for some constant
C
independent of
t
.

Remark 1.4
.

The main contribution of this paper is establishing the uniform boundedness of the lower-order conformal energy in (
1.9
). We recall that the natural (ghost weight) energy is defined as

\mathcal{E}_{gst}(t,w)=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{3}}((\partial_{t}w)^{2}+\sum_{a=1}^{3}(\partial_{a}w)^{2})(t,x){\rm{d}}x+\sum_{a=1}^{3}\int_{0}^{t}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{3}}\frac{|G_{a}w|^{2}}{\langle r-s\rangle^{1+2\delta}}{\rm{d}}x{\rm{d}}s,

and the conformal energy is defined as

\mathcal{E}_{con}(t,w)=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{3}}((L_{0}w)^{2}+w^{2}+\sum_{1\leq a<b\leq 3}|\Omega_{ab}w|^{2}+\sum_{a=1}^{3}|L_{a}w|^{2})(t,x){\rm{d}}x,

and one finds more details about the vector fields
L_{0},\Omega_{ab},L_{a}\ \mbox{and}\ G_{a}
in Section
2
.
We note, in general, it is more challenging to bound the conformal energy than the natural energy for wave equations (see (
2.8
) and (
2.9
)), and thus it is a nontrivial task to establish the uniform boundedness of the conformal energy in (
1.9
).

1.3.
Relevant results

Many physical models can be described by nonlinear wave equations, such as Dirac equations, Einstein equations, and Maxwell equations. Whether a wave equation admits a global solution is a central question in this field, and it is therefore of vital importance to explore the long-time behavior of such solutions.
In 1986, the global existence of solutions to 3D quasilinear wave equations with the null condition was first proved independently by Christodoulou
[
6
]
and Klainerman
[
22
]
. Their pioneering results were obtained through different approaches: Christodoulou used the conformal mapping method, while Klainerman employed the vector field method
[
21
]
. In 1993, Christodoulou and Klainerman
[
7
]
first established a landmark theory on the global nonlinear stability of Minkowski space. Later, Lindblad and Rodnianski
[
28
]
provided a new proof of the global stability of Minkowski spacetime in harmonic gauge.

Due to the slow decay of the waves, the two-dimensional case is substantially more delicate. The problem of global existence for quasilinear wave equations with the null condition in two space dimensions remained open until 2001, except for the cases of cubic terms or rotational invariance. Alinhac
[
2
]
obtained the global existence for 2D quasilinear wave equations with the null condition by constructing an approximate solution and the ghost weight method. However, the result in
[
2
]
only provided an upper bound with polynomial growth in time for the top-order energy of solutions. Motivated by this, Cai, Lei, and Masmoudi
[
5
]
in 2018 proved the uniform boundedness result for a class of quasilinear nonlinearities satisfying the strong null condition in the sense of Lei
[
23
]
. Subsequently, Dong, LeFloch, and Lei
[
11
]
extended this result and established the uniform boundedness of the top-order energy in
[
2
]
for general null nonlinearities by employing the hyperboloidal vector field method. More recently, Li
[
24
]
developed a new strategy that avoids the use of Lorentz boost vector fields and proved the uniform boundedness of the highest order norm of the solution to the two-dimensional quasilinear wave equation with standard quadratic null-type nonlinearities.

On the other hand, the proof in
[
2
]
required that the initial data have compact support. This restriction was later removed: in 2018, Cai, Lei, and Masmoudi
[
5
]
proved global well-posedness for a 2D fully nonlinear wave equation with small but non-compactly supported initial data, and extended their result to a class of 2D quasilinear equations. In 2020, Hou and Yin
[
16
]
first obtained global existence for general null quasilinear wave equations with non-compactly supported initial data in two dimensions by establishing a new class of weighted
L^{\infty}
-
L^{\infty}
estimates.

Relevant results for the 1-dimensional case can be referred to
[
25
,
29
]
. When the initial data are large, one can refer to
[
10
,
36
]
.

There are also various results concerning generalizations of the classical null condition. In 2003, Alinhac
[
3
]
studied global existence for an example of 3D quasilinear wave equations violating the null condition (see also
[
27
]
). Later, Lindblad and Rodnianski
[
28
]
came up with the weak null condition, which generalizes the null condition; see also the KMS condition in
[
17
]
by Katayama, Matoba and Sunagawa. Another related generalization is the non-resonance condition introduced by Pusateri and Shatah
[
31
]
.
Keir
[
19
]
established global existence for a large class of quasilinear wave equations under the weak null condition, by applying the
p
-weighted energy method developed in
[
8
]
. His approach also applies to the coupled Einstein-Maxwell system in harmonic coordinates and Lorenz gauge, as well as to various other scalar wave equations. Very recently, Dong, Li, and Yuan
[
12
]
established the uniform boundedness of the total energy for a massless Dirac-Klein-Gordon system in
(1+3)
-dimensional Minkowski spacetime, which can be regarded as a weak null wave system. Their analysis relies on a new weighted conformal energy estimate that compensates for the slow decay of solutions. Some further results about the weak null condition can be referred to
[
17
,
15
,
9
]
.

The wave system with multispeeds is also a topic of concern to many researchers. The global existence of solutions in nonlinear elastodynamics was established by Agemi
[
1
]
and Sideris
[
32
]
. Yokoyama
[
37
]
studied the global existence for three-dimensional systems of quasilinear wave equations with multispeeds. The global existence for 3D nonlinear wave equations with multispeeds under the null condition can be referred to
[
33
]
.

Last but not least, the precise pointwise behaviors for the wave equations have also drawn much attention. In 2020, Deng and Pusateri
[
9
]
considered a wave equation satisfying the weak null condition and showed the precise pointwise behavior of the differentiated solution near the light cone. Recently, Dong-Ma-Ma-Yuan
[
14
]
demonstrated the precise behavior of the solution to (
1.1
) with initial data prescribed on a hyperboloid.

1.4.
Outline of the proof

In this subsection, we present the key points of the proof in the paper. It is well known that the global existence and uniform boundedness of the natural energy for small-data solutions to the Cauchy problem (
1.1
)–(
1.2
) have already been established in the literature
[
35
,
30
]
. In this paper, we obtain the global existence and the uniform boundedness of natural energy by using the vector field theory and bootstrap argument. The ghost weight method of Alinhac plays an important role in the proof of the uniform boundedness estimate on the higher-order natural energy (see also
[
35
]
). A key challenge in establishing Theorem
1.1
is handling the nonlinearities induced by the large initial data.

The most difficult part in Theorem
1.3
lies in showing the uniform boundedness of the lower-order conformal energy. More specifically, we know that there is a
\langle t\rangle^{-1}
decay which is critical in the estimate on the conformal energy (see (
2.9
)). Therefore, we need to find a new way to prove the lower-order conformal energy is uniformly bounded. To achieve this, we apply the Fourier method to obtain a new
L^{2}
estimate of the undifferentiated solutions to the wave equations (see (
2.14
)). Then we can obtain the higher-order weighted estimates on the solution by commuting the wave equation with the vector fields. Based on these estimates, we in turn complete the proof of uniform boundedness of conformal energy up to order
N-3
. See Section
4
for more details.

1.5.
Organization of the paper

The paper is organized as follows. In Section
2
, we introduce the notation and basic estimates that will be used in Sections
3
and
4
. In Section
3
, we establish the global existence and linear scattering of the solution to the Cauchy problem (
1.1
)–(
1.2
), and obtain the uniform boundedness of the ghost weight energy. Section
4
is devoted to proving that the lower-order conformal energy is also uniformly bounded.

Notation and basic estimates

2.1.
Notation

Our problem is set in the
(1+3)
-dimensional spacetime
\mathbb{R}^{1+3}
. A spacetime point is denoted by
(t,x)=(x_{0},x_{1},x_{2},x_{3})
, with spatial radius
r=\sqrt{x_{1}^{2}+x_{2}^{2}+x_{3}^{2}}
. We set
\omega_{a}=\frac{x_{a}}{r}
for
a=1,2,3
, and write
x=(x_{1},x_{2},x_{3})\in\mathbb{R}^{3}
. Spacetime indices are represented by Greek letters
\{\alpha,\beta,\dots\}
, while spatial indices are denoted by Roman letters
\left\{a,b,\dots\right\}
.

The Japanese bracket is defined by
\langle\rho\rangle=(1+|\rho|^{2})^{\frac{1}{2}}
for
\rho\in\mathbb{R}
(and more generally
\rho\in\mathbb{R}^{3}
). We write
A\lesssim B
to mean that
A\leq C_{0}B
for some universal constant
C_{0}
. Similarly,
A\sim B
denotes
A\lesssim B
and
B\lesssim A
. Unless otherwise specified, we adopt the Einstein summation convention for repeated lower and upper indices. In what follows, we omit the subscript in
\|f\|_{L^{2}_{x}}
whenever no confusion arises, for
f\in L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{3})
.

Following Klainerman
[
22
]
, we introduce the standard set of vector fields in
\mathbb{R}^{1+3}

(1)

Translations:
\partial_{\alpha}:=\partial_{x_{\alpha}}
, for
\alpha=0,1,2,3
.

(2)

Rotations:
\Omega_{ab}:=x_{a}\partial_{b}-x_{b}\partial_{a}
, for
1\leq a<b\leq 3
.

(3)

Scaling vector field:
L_{0}:=t\partial_{t}+x^{a}\partial_{a}
.

(4)

Lorentz boosts:
L_{a}:=t\partial_{a}+x_{a}\partial_{t}
, for
a=1,2,3
.

We define the ordered set of vector fields

\Gamma=\left(\Gamma_{1},\dots,\Gamma_{11}\right)=\left(\partial,\Omega,L,L_{0}\right),

where

\displaystyle\partial=(\partial_{0},\partial_{1},\partial_{2},\partial_{3})=(\partial_{t},\nabla),\ \ \Omega=(\Omega_{12},\Omega_{13},\Omega_{23}),\ \ L=(L_{1},L_{2},L_{3}).

Moreover, for any multi-index
I=(I_{1},\dots,I_{11})\in\mathbb{N}^{11}
, we denote

\Gamma^{I}=\prod_{k=1}^{11}\Gamma_{k}^{I_{k}},\quad\mbox{where}\ \Gamma=(\Gamma_{1},\dots,\Gamma_{11}).

We denote the nonlinear terms by

Q_{P}(u,v)=P^{\gamma\alpha\beta}\partial_{\gamma}u\partial_{\alpha}\partial_{\beta}v,\quad Q_{0}(u,v)=\partial^{\alpha}u\partial_{\alpha}v.

We also introduce the good derivatives

G_{a}=\frac{1}{r}(x_{a}\partial_{t}+r\partial_{a})=\omega_{a}\partial_{t}+\partial_{a},\quad\mbox{for}\ a=1,2,3.

For future notational convenience, we denote

G=(G_{1},G_{2},G_{3}).

The Fourier transform of
f\in L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{3})
is defined by

\mathcal{F}(f)(\xi)=\hat{f}(\xi)=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{3}}f(x)e^{-ix\cdot\xi}{\rm{d}}x.

Basic estimates

In this subsection, we recall several preliminary estimates related to the vector fields and the null forms. We also collect some standard analytic tools, including the Klainerman–Sobolev inequality, Hölder inequality, and Hardy inequality, which will be frequently used in the subsequent analysis.

Lemma 2.1

(
[
34

)

For any smooth function

\sum_{\alpha=0}^{3}\left|[\partial_{\alpha},\Gamma^{I}]u\right|\lesssim\sum_{|J|<|I|}\sum_{\beta=0}^{3}|\partial_{\beta}\Gamma^{J}u|.

Lemma 2.2

(
[

34

For any smooth function

\langle t-r\rangle|\partial u|+\langle t+r\rangle|G_{a}u|\lesssim\sum_{|J|=1}|\Gamma^{J}u|,\quad\forall\ a=1,2,3.

(2.2)

Proof.

By an elementary computation, for
a=1,2,3

\displaystyle(t^{2}-r^{2})\partial_{t}u

\displaystyle=tL_{0}u-x^{a}L_{a}u,

\displaystyle(t^{2}-r^{2})\partial_{1}u

\displaystyle=tL_{1}u-x_{1}L_{0}u+x_{2}\Omega_{12}u+x_{3}\Omega_{13}u,

\displaystyle(t^{2}-r^{2})\partial_{2}u

\displaystyle=tL_{2}u-x_{2}L_{0}u-x_{1}\Omega_{12}u+x_{3}\Omega_{23}u,

\displaystyle(t^{2}-r^{2})\partial_{3}u

\displaystyle=tL_{3}u-x_{3}L_{0}u-x_{1}\Omega_{13}u-x_{2}\Omega_{23}u,

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{r}(L_{a}u+(r-t)\partial_{a}u)=\frac{1}{t}(L_{a}u-\frac{x_{a}}{r}(r-t)\partial_{t}u).

Combining the above identities, we arrive at (
2.2

Lemma 2.8

be the solution to the Cauchy problem

\left\{\begin{aligned} -\Box u(t,x)&=f(t,x),\\
(u,\partial_{t}u)|_{t=0}&=(u_{0},u_{1}),\end{aligned}\right.

with
f(t,x)
a sufficiently regular function. Then the following estimates hold.

(i)

[
2
]

Ghost weight energy estimate:

\displaystyle\mathcal{E}_{gst}^{\frac{1}{2}}(t,u)\lesssim\mathcal{E}_{gst}^{\frac{1}{2}}(0,u)+\int_{0}^{t}\|f(s,x)\|{\rm{d}}s,

(2.8)

where

\mathcal{E}_{gst}(t,u)=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{3}}|\partial u|^{2}{\rm{d}}x+\sum_{a=1}^{3}\int_{0}^{t}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{3}}\frac{|G_{a}u|^{2}}{\langle r-s\rangle^{1+2\delta}}{\rm{d}}x{\rm{d}}s,\quad 0<\delta\ll 1.

(ii)

[
4
]

Conformal energy estimate:

\displaystyle\mathcal{E}_{con}^{\frac{1}{2}}(t,u)\lesssim\mathcal{E}_{con}^{\frac{1}{2}}(0,u)+\int_{0}^{t}\|\langle s+r\rangle f(s,x)\|{\rm{d}}s,

(2.9)

where

\mathcal{E}_{con}(t,u)=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{3}}((L_{0}u)^{2}+u^{2}+\sum_{1\leq a<b\leq 3}|\Omega_{ab}u|^{2}+\sum_{a=1}^{3}|L_{a}u|^{2})(t,x){\rm{d}}x.

Proof.

Proof of (i).
Let
q(r,t)=\int_{-\infty}^{r-t}\langle s\rangle^{-1-2\delta}{\rm{d}}s
and set
e^{q}=e^{q(r,t)}
. Multiplying both sides of the equation by
e^{q}\partial_{t}u
, we have

\frac{1}{2}\partial_{t}(e^{q}|\partial u|^{2})+\frac{1}{2}e^{q}\sum_{a}\frac{|G_{a}u|^{2}}{\langle r-t\rangle^{1+2\delta}}-\partial^{a}(e^{q}\partial_{a}u\partial_{t}u)=f(t,x)(e^{q}\partial_{t}u).

Note that
e^{q}\sim 1
. Integrating over
\mathbb{R}^{3}
in
x
and from
0
to
t
in time, we get

\displaystyle\mathcal{E}_{gst}(t,u)

\displaystyle\lesssim\mathcal{E}_{gst}(0,u)+\int_{0}^{t}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{3}}|f(s,x)(\partial_{t}u)|{\rm{d}}x{\rm{d}}s

\displaystyle\lesssim\mathcal{E}_{gst}(0,u)+\int_{0}^{t}\|f(s,x)\|\mathcal{E}_{gst}^{\frac{1}{2}}(s,u){\rm{d}}s.

Applying Gronwall inequality to the above, we obtain (
2.8
).

Proof of (ii).
Consider the nonspacelike multiplier

\mathcal{M}_{0}=(r^{2}+t^{2})\partial_{t}+2rt\partial_{r},\quad\mbox{where}\ \partial_{r}=\omega^{a}\partial_{a}.

Multiplying both sides of the equation by
\mathcal{M}_{0}u+2tu
, we have

\displaystyle(-\Box u)(\mathcal{M}_{0}u+2tu)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}\partial_{t}[(r^{2}+t^{2})((\partial_{t}u)^{2}+\partial^{b}u\partial_{b}u)+4tr\partial_{r}u\partial_{t}u+4tu\partial_{t}u-2u^{2}+2\partial_{a}(x^{a}u^{2})]

\displaystyle-

\displaystyle\partial^{a}[(r^{2}+t^{2})\partial_{a}u\partial_{t}u+tx_{a}((\partial_{t}u)^{2}-\partial^{b}u\partial_{b}u)+2tr\partial_{r}u\partial_{a}u+2tu\partial_{a}u+\partial_{t}(x_{a}u^{2})].

Hence integrating over
\mathbb{R}^{3}
in
x
, we get

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}\partial_{t}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{3}}[(r^{2}+t^{2})(\partial u)^{2}+4tr\partial_{r}u\partial_{t}u+4tu\partial_{t}u-2u^{2}+2\partial_{a}(x^{a}u^{2})]{\rm{d}}x

(2.10)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\int_{\mathbb{R}^{3}}f(t,x)(\mathcal{M}_{0}u+2tu){\rm{d}}x.

Let

\displaystyle\tilde{E}(t,u)=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{3}}[(r^{2}+t^{2})(\partial u)^{2}+4tr\partial_{r}u\partial_{t}u+4tu\partial_{t}u-2u^{2}+2\partial_{a}(x^{a}u^{2})](t,x){\rm{d}}x.

A straightforward calculation shows that

\displaystyle\tilde{E}(t,u)=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{3}}[(L_{0}u+2u)^{2}+\sum_{1\leq a<b\leq 3}|\Omega_{ab}u|^{2}+\sum_{a=1}^{3}|L_{a}u|^{2}](t,x){\rm{d}}x.

By (
2.10
), we have

\tilde{E}(t,u)\lesssim\tilde{E}(0,u)+\int_{0}^{t}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{3}}|f(s,x)(\mathcal{M}_{0}u+2su)|{\rm{d}}x{\rm{d}}s.

Recall the definition of
\mathcal{M}_{0}
, it holds

\displaystyle\mathcal{M}_{0}u+2tu=

\displaystyle(r^{2}+t^{2})\partial_{t}u+2rt\partial_{r}u+2tu

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle t(L_{0}u+2u)+x^{a}L_{a}u.

Then

\tilde{E}(t,u)\lesssim\tilde{E}(0,u)+\int_{0}^{t}\|\langle s+r\rangle f(s,x)\|\tilde{E}^{\frac{1}{2}}(s,u){\rm{d}}s.

(2.11)

Applying Gronwall inequality to (
2.11
), we deduce

\tilde{E}^{\frac{1}{2}}(t,u)\lesssim\tilde{E}^{\frac{1}{2}}(0,u)+\int_{0}^{t}\|\langle s+r\rangle f(s,x)\|{\rm{d}}s.

(2.12)

It can be verified by careful calculations that

\displaystyle\mathcal{E}_{con}(t,u)\sim

\displaystyle\tilde{E}(t,u).

(2.13)

Together with (
2.13
), (
2.12
) yields the conformal energy estimate (
2.9
).

To describe the asymptotic behavior of solutions to the inhomogeneous wave equation, we recall the scattering result from

Lemma 2.9
.

Suppose that
u(t,x)
is the solution to the Cauchy problem

\left\{\begin{aligned} -\Box u(t,x)&=f(t,x),\\
(u,\partial_{t}u)|_{t=0}&=(u_{0},u_{1}).\end{aligned}\right.

Suppose that

\int_{0}^{+\infty}\|f(\tau,x)\|_{\dot{H}^{s-1}}{\rm{d}}\tau<+\infty,

then there exist a pair
(\tilde{u}_{0},\tilde{u}_{1})\in\dot{H}^{s}(\mathbb{R}^{3})\times\dot{H}^{s-1}(\mathbb{R}^{3})
and a constant
C
such that

\|(u,\partial_{t}u)^{T}-S_{W}(t)(\tilde{u}_{0},\tilde{u}_{1})^{T}\|_{\dot{H}^{s}\times\dot{H}^{s-1}}\leq C\int_{t}^{+\infty}\|f(\tau,x)\|_{\dot{H}^{s-1}}{\rm{d}}\tau.

In particular,

\lim_{t\rightarrow+\infty}\|(u,\partial_{t}u)^{T}-S_{W}(t)(\tilde{u}_{0},\tilde{u}_{1})^{T}\|_{\dot{H}^{s}\times\dot{H}^{s-1}}=0.

In the above,
S_{W}(t)
is denoted by

\begin{pmatrix}\cos(t|\nabla|)&|\nabla|^{-1}\sin(t|\nabla|)\\
-|\nabla|\sin(t|\nabla|)&\cos(t|\nabla|)\end{pmatrix}.

Here the operators are defined via the Fourier transform as

\displaystyle\left(|\nabla|^{-1}f\right)(x)

\displaystyle=\mathcal{F}^{-1}\left(|\xi|^{-1}\hat{f}(\xi)\right)(x),

\displaystyle\left(\cos(t|\nabla|)f\right)(x)

\displaystyle=\mathcal{F}^{-1}\left(\cos(t|\xi|)\hat{f}(\xi)\right)(x),

and other operators are defined similarly.

In addition,
S_{W}(t)(\tilde{u}_{0},\tilde{u}_{1})^{T}
solves

-\Box u=0,\quad(u,\partial_{t}u)|_{t=0}=(\tilde{u}_{0},\tilde{u}_{1}).

As the final result of this subsection, we establish the following lemma, which provides an
L^{2}
estimate for the solution to the 3D wave equation.

Lemma 2.10
.

Let
u=u(t,x)
be the solution to the Cauchy problem

\left\{\begin{aligned} -\Box u(t,x)&=f(t,x),\\
(u,\partial_{t}u)|_{t=0}&=(u_{0},u_{1}),\end{aligned}\right.

with
f(t,x)
a sufficiently regular function. Then for any
\eta>0
, we have

\displaystyle\|u(t,x)\|\lesssim\|u_{0}\|+\|u_{1}\|_{L^{1}\cap L^{2}}+\int_{0}^{t}\left(\langle s\rangle^{-\frac{\eta}{2}}\|f(s,x)\|_{L^{1}}+\langle s\rangle^{\eta}\|f(s,x)\|\right){\rm{d}}s.

(2.14)

Proof.

Taking the Fourier transform of the Cauchy problem with respect to
x
and applying Duhamel’s principle, we get

\left\{\begin{aligned} \partial_{tt}\hat{u}(t,\xi)+|\xi|^{2}\hat{u}(t,\xi)=\hat{f}(t,\xi),\\
\hat{u}(0,\xi)=\hat{u}_{0}(\xi),\quad\partial_{t}\hat{u}(0,\xi)=\hat{u}_{1}(\xi).\end{aligned}\right.

Solving this second-order ODE in
t
, we get the solution in Fourier space:

\hat{u}(t,\xi)=\cos(t|\xi|)\hat{u}_{0}(\xi)+\frac{\sin(t|\xi|)}{|\xi|}\hat{u}_{1}(\xi)+\int_{0}^{t}\frac{\sin((t-s)|\xi|)}{|\xi|}\hat{f}(s,\xi){\rm{d}}s,

for
(t,\xi)\in[0,\infty)\times\mathbb{R}^{3}
.

Using the Plancherel theorem and a polar coordinate transformation, we deduce

\displaystyle\|\cos(t|\xi|)\hat{u}_{0}(\xi)\|_{L^{2}_{\xi}}

\displaystyle\leq\|\hat{u}_{0}(\xi)\|_{L^{2}_{\xi}}\lesssim\|u_{0}\|,

\displaystyle\left\|\frac{\sin(t|\xi|)}{|\xi|}\hat{u}_{1}(\xi)\right\|^{2}_{L^{2}_{\xi}}

\displaystyle=\int_{|\xi|\leq 1}\frac{\sin^{2}(t|\xi|)}{|\xi|^{2}}|\hat{u}_{1}(\xi)|^{2}{\rm{d}}\xi+\int_{|\xi|\geq 1}\frac{\sin^{2}(t|\xi|)}{|\xi|^{2}}|\hat{u}_{1}(\xi)|^{2}{\rm{d}}\xi

\displaystyle\leq\|\hat{u}_{1}(\xi)\|^{2}_{L^{2}_{\xi}}+\int_{|\omega|=1}\int_{0}^{1}\sin^{2}(tr)|\hat{u}_{1}(r)|^{2}{\rm{d}}r{\rm{d}}\omega

\displaystyle\lesssim\|\hat{u}_{1}(\xi)\|^{2}_{L^{2}_{\xi}}+\|\hat{u}_{1}(\xi)\|^{2}_{L^{\infty}_{\xi}}

\displaystyle\lesssim\|u_{1}\|^{2}+\|u_{1}\|^{2}_{L^{1}},

\displaystyle\left\|\frac{\sin((t-s)|\xi|)}{|\xi|}\hat{f}(s,\xi)\right\|^{2}_{L^{2}_{\xi}}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\int_{|\xi|\leq\langle s\rangle^{-\eta}}\frac{\sin^{2}((t-s)|\xi|)}{|\xi|^{2}}|\hat{f}(s,\xi)|^{2}{\rm{d}}\xi+\int_{|\xi|\geq\langle s\rangle^{-\eta}}\frac{\sin^{2}((t-s)|\xi|)}{|\xi|^{2}}|\hat{f}(s,\xi)|^{2}{\rm{d}}\xi

\displaystyle\lesssim

\displaystyle\langle s\rangle^{-\eta}\|\hat{f}(s,\xi)\|^{2}_{L^{\infty}_{\xi}}+\langle s\rangle^{2\eta}\|\hat{f}(s,\xi)\|^{2}_{L^{2}_{\xi}}

\displaystyle\lesssim

\displaystyle\langle s\rangle^{-\eta}\|f(s,x)\|^{2}_{L^{1}}+\langle s\rangle^{2\eta}\|f(s,x)\|^{2},

for any
\eta>0.

Therefore, using again the Plancherel theorem, we obtain

\displaystyle\|u(t,x)\|

\displaystyle\lesssim\|\hat{u}(t,\xi)\|_{L^{2}_{\xi}}

\displaystyle\lesssim\|u_{0}\|+\|u_{1}\|_{L^{1}}+\|u_{1}\|+\int_{0}^{t}\left(\langle s\rangle^{-\frac{\eta}{2}}\|f(s,x)\|_{L^{1}}+\langle s\rangle^{\eta}\|f(s,x)\|\right){\rm{d}}s,

which implies (
2.14
).
∎

,

\displaystyle\lesssim

\displaystyle\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}|I_{1}|+|I_{2}|\leq|I|,|J|=1\end{subarray}}\|\langle s+r\rangle^{-1}\Gamma^{J}\Gamma^{I_{1}}w\partial\Gamma^{I_{2}}w\|

\displaystyle\lesssim
