Case Report: Massive polypoid early Epstein-Barr virus-associated gastric carcinoma in a young immunocompromised host
Authors: Ke Pu, Xinglong Wu, Tao Shu, Biguang Tuo, Guangyan Luo, Lianjun Di
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Epstein–Barr virus-associated gastric carcinoma (EBVaGC) typically presents as a solitary lesion in older men. We describe a 24-year-old woman with chronic diarrhea and immunosuppression who was found to have multiple gastric lesions. Laboratory evaluation revealed active EBV replication (2.55 × 105 copies/mL) and severe CD4 lymphopenia (180/μL). Esophagogastroduodenoscopy showed extensive gastric squamous metaplasia and multiple elevated lesions. The large antral mass was clinically suspected to represent advanced malignancy, and therefore, prompted distal gastrectomy, whereas the smaller fundic lesion was treated with endoscopic submucosal dissection. Histopathology unexpectedly revealed moderately-to-poorly differentiated adenocarcinomas with prominent lymphoid stroma at both sites. In situ hybridization confirmed EBV-encoded RNA positivity. Notably, both tumors were confined to the mucosa (pT1a), consistent with early gastric cancer. This case illustrates that EBVaGC may present as massive polypoid lesions mimicking advanced neoplasia in young, immunocompromised hosts. Careful integration of endoscopic and histopathological findings is essential for appropriate management.

Introduction

Epstein–Barr virus-associated gastric carcinoma (EBVaGC) represents a distinct subset of gastric cancers, characterized by specific clinicopathological and molecular features. Endoscopically, these lesions are often reddish, depressed, or submucosal tumor-like, and they typically arise in the upper stomach. However, unusual presentations do occur and may lead to misinterpretation as benign or advanced lesions. EBV-associated carcinoma of this type is rare, and the coexistence of gastric squamous metaplasia is even rarer. Herein, we report a case of multiple EBV-positive gastric carcinomas with concurrent squamous metaplasia, highlighting the endoscopic and pathological features that may facilitate their recognition.

Case presentation

A 24-year-old woman was referred for evaluation of chronic diarrhea. She reported a 4-year history of intermittent diarrhea, which had exacerbated over the preceding 2 months following an induced abortion, progressing to 7–8 episodes per day accompanied by abdominal pain. The patient denied hematochezia, nausea, vomiting, and a family history of malignancy. There was no long-term corticosteroid, chemotherapy, or other immunosuppressive medication exposure prior to admission. Nutritional status was intact, with normal serum albumin, pre-albumin, and hemoglobin. Negative urea breath test results and negative serum IgG serology (anti-CagA, anti-VacA, anti-urease) ruled out H. pylori infection. Autoimmune gastritis was ruled out by absent anti-intrinsic factor and anti-parietal cell antibodies, with concurrent serum ferritin at 272 μg/L, folate at 18.93 ng/mL, and vitamin B12 at 144 pg/mL. Serological screening for human immunodeficiency virus (HIV), hepatitis B virus (HBV), and hepatitis C virus (HCV) was also negative. Laboratory investigations revealed active Epstein–Barr virus (EBV) replication, with positive EBV-CA-IgA and EBV-EA-IgA, elevated EBV-CA-IgG, and a high viral load of 2.55 × 105 copies/mL. Immunophenotyping demonstrated severe T-cell lymphopenia, with a CD3 count of 296/μL, CD4 count of 180/μL, and CD8 count of 100/μL. Furthermore, she exhibited a pronounced systemic inflammatory response, characterized by markedly elevated interleukin-6 (1,118.03 pg/mL), interleukin-10 (17.28 pg/mL), and C-reactive protein (188.76 mg/L). The patient underwent chest and whole-abdominal computed tomography (CT). Plain and contrast-enhanced CT of the upper abdomen demonstrated that the antral lesion was confined to the gastric mucosa, with no regional lymphadenopathy (Supplementary Table 1).

Endoscopic and histopathological findings

The patient underwent upper gastrointestinal endoscopy after standard pre-procedure mucolytic lavage. White-light endoscopy (WLE) identified suspicious, well-demarcated squamous metaplasia involving the anterior wall of the gastric body and extending to the antrum (Figures 1a–c). Narrow-band imaging (NBI) combined with magnifying endoscopy revealed a lack of typical regular collecting venules (RCV), and instead showed a homogeneous brownish surface pattern with blurred or absent gastric pits, consistent with squamous metaplasia (Figures 1d, e). Furthermore, colonoscopy revealed diffuse nodular proliferative lesions in the terminal ileum (Figure 1f). Subsequent detailed endoscopic evaluation revealed multiple flat-elevated (Paris classification 0-IIa) lesions with irregular contours in the gastric fundus (Figures 2a–c), alongside an antral lesion mimicking a hyperplastic polyp (0-Is+IIa) with mucus adhesion and surrounding squamous metaplasia (Figure 2d). Magnifying endoscopy with narrow-band imaging (M-NBI) demonstrated a clear demarcation line (DL). The flat components exhibited an irregular microsurface (MS) pattern with a villous appearance and dilated, tortuous vessels strictly confined to the interpapillary sulci (IP) (Figures 2e, g). In contrast, the elevated areas displayed irregular reticular vessels and loss of the normal marginal crypt epithelial architecture (Figure 2f). Adjacent mucosal areas showed light-blue crest (LBC) positivity, while the lesion itself was characterized by an irregular microsurface pattern and the presence of a white opaque substance (WOS) (Figure 2h). Histological analysis of biopsy specimens using hematoxylin and eosin (H&E) staining correlated with the WLE findings (Supplementary Figure 1a). Microscopic examination revealed squamous metaplasia with complete obliteration of the native gastric glands. The mucosa was replaced by mature stratified squamous epithelium, characterized by polygonal cell arrangement and distinct cell boundaries (Supplementary Figure 1b). A clear basement membrane was visible beneath the epithelial layer, and the underlying lamina propria showed infiltration with chronic inflammatory cells (Supplementary Figure 1c). Crucially, no significant cellular atypia was observed, confirming the benign nature of the metaplastic changes. Subsequently, we utilized the squamous epithelium-specific antibody P40 to label the lesions, with low- and high-magnification micrographs shown in Supplementary Figures 1d, e, respectively. Diffuse nuclear P40 immunoreactivity was observed within the metaplastic epithelial layer of the lesion. These findings demonstrated that the epithelium at the lesion site underwent squamous metaplastic replacement.

Surgical and endoscopic resection with final pathological findings

Given the lesion’s bulky volume, polypoid morphology, pyloric deformation, ulceration, and stenosis, coupled with clinical suspicion of invasive malignancy, endoscopic resection was deemed inappropriate. In addition, the extensive lesion size and anatomical distortion of the pylorus rendered endoscopic submucosal dissection (ESD) technically challenging; therefore, the patient underwent gastrectomy. Gross inspection of the resected antral specimen identified three distinct polypoid elevated lesions within the gastric antrum, measuring 7.5 cm × 2.5 cm × 1.8 cm, 2.0 cm × 1.5 cm × 1.5 cm, and 1.5 cm × 1.0 cm × 0.5 cm, respectively (Figures 3a, b). Stepwise serial sectioning at 2.5-mm intervals clearly demarcated the border of each lesion. Histologically, all three lesions were classified as Lauren mixed-type adenocarcinomas, dominated by moderately differentiated tubular components (tub2) with focal poorly differentiated carcinoma (por1). Tumor invasion extended into the muscularis mucosae (M3). Neither lymphovascular nor perineural invasion was identified. Tumor infiltration was absent at the proximal and distal surgical resection margins, as well as within the greater omentum. Fifteen regional lymph nodes retrieved from the greater omentum were all free of metastatic carcinoma (0/15). Histopathological evaluation of specimen #14 confirmed moderately-to-poorly differentiated adenocarcinoma accompanied by reactive lymphoid follicular hyperplasia (Figure 3c). Epstein–Barr virus-encoded small RNA (EBER) in situ hybridization showed diffuse nuclear staining exclusively within neoplastic epithelial cells, encompassing both superficial and basal tumor layers; no EBER signal was detected in the background infiltrating lymphocytes (Figures 3d–f). The final diagnosis was revised to tubular-type EBV-positive gastric adenocarcinoma with lymphoid stroma (EBV-GCLS).

For the separate lesion located in the gastric fundus, ESD was performed for curative resection. This flat elevated lesion (Paris classification 0-IIa) occupied a total mucosal area of 3.3 cm × 1.3 cm, with the core malignant component measuring 1.3 cm × 1.0 cm. Serial sectioning at 2.5-mm intervals clearly defined the lesion’s circumferential boundary (Figures 4a, b). Histomorphologically, it was also a Lauren mixed-type adenocarcinoma, with moderately differentiated tubular adenocarcinoma (tub2) predominating over focal poorly differentiated carcinoma (por1). Immunohistochemical mucin phenotyping demonstrated a mixed mucin profile: partial cytoplasmic positivity for MUC5AC and MUC6, with complete loss of MUC2 expression. The tumor was limited entirely to the mucosal layer (pT1a, intramucosal), without surface ulceration (UL−). No vascular or lymphatic invasion was evident, and both horizontal and vertical ESD resection margins were negative for tumor involvement. Histopathological assessment of specimen #7 demonstrated moderately-to-poorly differentiated adenocarcinoma with prominent intercellular vacuolization and dense intratumoral lymphocytic infiltration (Figures 4c, d). In situ hybridization molecular testing revealed diffuse EBER nuclear expression restricted to the malignant epithelial cell population (Figure 4e, f). The lesion was finally confirmed as a tubular-type EBV-GCLS.

Discussion

Epidemiological studies have demonstrated that EBV-associated gastric carcinoma (EBVaGC) is significantly enriched in young populations. The overall global prevalence of EBVaGC among all gastric cancers is only 8.77%, whereas the positivity rate rises to 33% in patients aged ≤45 years, which is markedly higher than that in elderly individuals (1, 2). Unlike late-onset gastric cancer, which is primarily driven by chronic H. pylori infection, mucosal atrophy, and intestinal metaplasia, young patients lack long-term cumulative carcinogenic exposure. EBV-induced epigenetic dysregulation serves as the dominant oncogenic driver in early-onset gastric tumorigenesis (3). EBVaGC is recognized as a distinct molecular subtype, classically manifesting as a solitary, superficially depressed (type IIc) or submucosal tumor-like lesion in the proximal stomach (4, 5). However, accumulating evidence suggests considerable clinicopathological heterogeneity, and the conventional phenotypic criteria may not encompass all presentations (6). Our case represents a significant deviation from this established paradigm. We describe a young woman with multiple EBVaGC lesions, where the dominant antral tumor exhibited a massive, polypoid (type 0-I) morphology highly suggestive of advanced-stage disease. Despite its suspicious appearance, final pathology revealed the lesion to be confined to the mucosal layer, classifying it as early gastric cancer (EGC). This discrepancy is likely attributable to a predominantly exophytic growth pattern. Unlike typical infiltrative carcinomas that invade deeply into the gastric wall, this tumor expanded primarily into the lumen, explaining the discordance between its macroscopic size and its shallow depth of invasion. The unusual clinical presentation is accompanied by profound systemic immune collapse coupled with a hyper-inflammatory state. Although HIV, HBV, and HCV were ruled out, the patient exhibited severe T-cell lymphopenia (CD3: 296/μL, CD4: 180/μL, and CD8: 100/μL) alongside a massive EBV viral load (2.55 × 105 copies/mL). This collapse in cellular immunity likely crippled the host’s ability to surveil EBV, permitting unchecked viral oncogenesis. Concomitantly, the patient presented with a high level of cytokines, characterized by markedly elevated IL-6 (1,118.03 pg/mL) and C-reactive protein (CRP) (188.76 mg/L). In addition, diffuse nodular-like elevations were detected in the esophagus, duodenum, and terminal ileum.

Accumulating evidence has confirmed that EBVaGC preferentially develops in immunocompromised populations, including organ transplant recipients, patients with congenital immune defects, and individuals undergoing long-term immunosuppressive therapy (7–9). Impaired immune surveillance enables persistent EBV reactivation and sustained expression of viral oncoproteins LMP1 and EBNA1, which trigger excessive production of IL-6 and TNF-α and establish a self-perpetuating inflammatory loop. This chronic inflammatory state aggravates gastric epithelial DNA damage and impairs local antitumor immunity, thereby creating a pro-oncogenic microenvironment. Notably, chronic EBV infection and host immune dysfunction form a bidirectional vicious cycle. Persistent EBV infection depletes peripheral CD4+ and CD8+ T cells via multiple pathogenic pathways: viral gp350 mediates T-cell apoptosis through the Fas/FasL axis (10); EBV lytic proteins block major histocompatibility complex (MHC)-dependent antigen presentation and restrict T-cell clonal expansion (11); latent LMP1 upregulates PD-L1 and recruits immunosuppressive Tregs to induce profound T-cell exhaustion (12); and elevated viral loads trigger robust inflammatory responses that promote macrophage-mediated clearance of activated T cells (13). Nevertheless, due to the absence of complete immunological evaluation, including autoimmune screening, primary immunodeficiency workup, and serum immunoglobulin quantification, the precise causal relationship in this case remains unclear. We cannot definitively distinguish whether EBV-acquired secondary immunodeficiency drove gastric carcinogenesis, or whether an underlying primary immune defect facilitated persistent EBV infection and subsequent tumor progression.

Classical EBVaGC typically presents as solitary flat-depressed lesions confined to the proximal stomach. Multifocal polypoid lesions extending to the distal stomach represent an uncommon clinical phenotype, which is seldom reported in routine cohorts (14–16). Specifically, multifocal polypoid EBVaGC is exceedingly rare among patients with impaired immunity, with limited case reports documented in published literature. The present young patient with impaired immunity manifested this uncommon multifocal polypoid subtype, reflecting a unique clinicopathological phenotype. Mechanistically, persistent EBV infection drives uncontrolled proliferation of gastric columnar epithelium, resulting in localized polypoid mucosal bulging (15). Concurrent systemic immune deficiency further impairs tumor immune surveillance, allowing transformed epithelial cells to evade immune clearance and enable synchronous malignant transformation of multiple independent mucosal segments, which ultimately gives rise to multifocal tumorigenesis. Furthermore, extensive gastric squamous metaplasia was observed in this patient. Classically, gastric squamous metaplasia is regarded as a benign adaptive reparative response to long-standing gastric mucosal injury (17–19). Under the dual pressure of persistent EBV infection and systemic immune impairment, squamous metaplastic epithelium may acquire transformation potential (20). In immunocompromised hosts, the metaplastic foci are reset by EBV-specific CD8+ T cells. Persistent latent EBV infection induces gastric squamous metaplasia through epigenetic reprogramming: LMP1 and LMP2A trigger CpG hypermethylation to silence gastric lineage genes and activate squamous master regulator ΔNp63, driving transdifferentiation of multipotent gastric stem cells (21–23). Collectively, aberrant proliferative activity of gastric epithelial stem cells, defective host immune surveillance, and a unique squamous metaplastic background synergistically constitute a multi-hit oncogenic cascade. Notably, diffuse nodular elevations were endoscopically identified throughout the esophagus, duodenum, and terminal ileum. These widespread extra-gastric lesions initially implied systemic EBV-associated mucosal disease rather than an isolated gastric lesion, suggestive of underlying systemic immune dysregulation (24, 25). However, targeted histological biopsy revealed only moderate-to-severe acute and chronic inflammatory changes without atypical lymphoid proliferation, lymphoproliferative lesions, or malignant transformation. No EBV-specific morphological alterations were observed on routine HE staining, and thus, EBER in situ hybridization was not performed for these intestinal and esophageal specimens. Collectively, these histological findings confirm that the extra-gastric nodules represent benign reactive inflammatory hyperplasia rather than EBV-related neoplastic lesions. Nevertheless, considering the patient’s underlying immune imbalance and persistent EBV infection, long-term clinical and endoscopic surveillance is still warranted to monitor potential gastrointestinal neoplasia and rule out subsequent EBV-associated lymphoproliferative disorders (26, 27). Several limitations of the present case report need to be addressed. First, comprehensive differential workup targeting the etiology of severe T-cell lymphopenia was incomplete. We did not perform systematic screening covering primary immunodeficiency, hematologic disorders, pregnancy- or post-abortion-related immune shifts, nor did we detect serum immunoglobulin levels to further characterize the patient’s immune status. Second, this study lacks comprehensive molecular subtyping of this rare young-onset EBVaGC. No multi-omic or genomic profiling was performed to identify specific oncogenic driver genes and their dysregulated expression patterns during tumor development. Meanwhile, the potential genetic correlation between host immune deficiency and increased susceptibility to EBVaGC remains unclear, restricting further mechanistic interpretation of this distinctive clinical phenotype. Third, longitudinal laboratory follow-up data were insufficient. Although endoscopic surveillance was conducted after discharge, serial measurements of EBV serological profiles, viral load, and peripheral immune parameters were missing. Without these dynamic biomarkers, we cannot fully delineate the complete long-term disease course of this patient.

Conclusion

In conclusion, we report a rare case of Epstein–Barr virus (EBV)-associated early gastric carcinoma presenting with concurrent gastric squamous metaplasia. The lesion exhibited atypical, massive polypoid morphology in both the antrum and fundus, posing a significant diagnostic challenge due to its deceptive resemblance to advanced neoplasia. Mechanistically, this unusual presentation appears to be associated with profound immunosuppression, highlighting the impact of systemic immune dysregulation on tumor biology. Ultimately, this case underscores the critical role of integrating white-light endoscopy, magnifying endoscopy, and histopathological correlation for the accurate diagnosis and management of such unusual gastric lesions.
