Bayesian distributionally robust variational inequalities: regularization and quantification
Wentao Ma, Zhiping Chen, Xiaojun Chen

Abstract
We propose a Bayesian distributionally robust variational inequality (DRVI) framework that models the data-generating distribution through a finite mixture family, which allows us to study the DRVI on a tractable finite-dimensional parametric ambiguity set. To address distributional uncertainty, we construct a data-driven ambiguity set with posterior coverage guarantees via Bayesian inference. We also employ a regularization approach to ensure numerical stability. We prove the existence of solutions to the Bayesian DRVI and the asymptotic convergence to a solution as sample size grows to infinity and the regularization parameter goes to zero. Moreover, we derive quantitative stability bounds and finite-sample guarantees under data scarcity and contamination. Numerical experiments on a distributionally robust multi-portfolio Nash equilibrium problem validate our theoretical results and demonstrate the robustness and reliability of Bayesian DRVI solutions in practice. Keywords: distributional robustness; Bayesian asymptotics; data-driven; regularization method; quantitative stability

However, real-world data often exhibit multimodality, skewness, and heavy tails

To enhance modeling flexibility while retaining a parametric structure, finite mixture distributions (e.g., Gaussian mixtures) provide a powerful extension. ,

[

]
, and customer preference heterogeneity in marketing analytics

\theta^{c}=(\theta_{1}^{c},\ldots,\theta_{n}^{c})

Motivated by parametric DRO models

\displaystyle 0

\text{B}^{c}

\displaystyle\theta

This finite-dimensional parametric ambiguity set reduces computational complexity and facilitates practical implementation compared to infinite-dimensional ambiguity sets, while retaining its flexibility to capture essential distributional characteristics.
The predetermined radius

In particular, when

\Theta^{c}

B
c

The true ambiguity set

in (

Conventional approaches construct an approximation by replacing

with the maximum likelihood estimate (MLE)

and then taking the set
\mathbb{B}(\hat{\theta}_{N},r^{c})\cap\Theta

Here,

. Formally, applying Bayes’ rule to
{\mathcal{S}^{N}}
yields the pdf of the posterior distribution

p({\theta}|{\mathcal{S}^{N}})=\frac{f(\mathcal{S}^{N}|{\theta})p({\theta})}{\int_{\Theta}f(\mathcal{S}^{N}|{\theta})p({\theta})d{\theta}},

(1.3)

where
f(\mathcal{S}^{N}|{\theta}):=\prod_{i=1}^{N}f({\xi}^{i}|{\theta})

This posterior distribution coherently integrates prior knowledge—whether from historical data, expert judgment, or physical models—and new observations, providing an evolving quantification of parametric uncertainty. In the absence of informative priors, noninformative choices such as Jeffreys’ or uniform priors are commonly used.
Recent studies
[
22
,
45
,
50
]
use a Bayesian averaging approach in place of an ambiguity set and
[
44
]
considers a worst-case perspective on the Bayesian average to mitigate model uncertainty.
In this paper, we adopt the framework of
[
20

Building upon the above Bayesian framework, our goal is to construct a data-driven ambiguity set
\hat{\Theta}_{N}\subseteq\Theta

\mathcal{S}^{N}
(equivalently, by the posterior
\mathbb{P}_{\mathcal{S}^{N}}

with high probability. Specifically, for a predetermined posterior confidence level

and radius
\hat{r}_{N}

\mathbb{P}_{\mathcal{S}^{N}}\left\{\Theta^{c}\subseteq\hat{\Theta}_{N}\right\}\geq 1-\alpha

\hat{r}_{N}=\min\left\{r\;\big|\;r\geq 0,\ \mathbb{P}_{\mathcal{S}^{N}}\left\{\Theta^{c}\subseteq\mathbb{B}(\hat{\theta}_{N},r)\cap\Theta\right\}\geq 1-\alpha\right\}.

Based on this construction, replacing
\Theta^{c}

B

\displaystyle 0

\displaystyle\in\sum_{j=1}^{n}\theta_{j}\mathbb{E}_{Q_{j}}[\Phi(x,\xi)]+\mathcal{N}_{\mathcal{X}}(x),

(
\text{B}_{N}
)

\displaystyle\theta

\displaystyle\in\underset{{\theta}^{\prime}\in\hat{\Theta}_{N}}{\argmax}\sum_{j=1}^{n}{\theta}^{\prime}_{j}\mathbb{E}_{Q_{j}}[\varphi(x,\xi)].

To address the potential numerical instability arising from the non-uniqueness of solutions to the lower-level problem in (

B
N

B
N

):

\displaystyle 0

\displaystyle\in\sum_{j=1}^{n}\theta_{j}\mathbb{E}_{Q_{j}}[\Phi(x,\xi)]+\mathcal{N}_{\mathcal{X}}(x),

(
\text{B}_{N}^{\lambda}

\displaystyle\theta

\displaystyle\in\underset{{\theta}^{\prime}\in\hat{\Theta}_{N}}{\argmax}\sum_{j=1}^{n}{\theta}^{\prime}_{j}\mathbb{E}_{Q_{j}}[\varphi(x,\xi)]-\lambda\|{\theta}^{\prime}\|^{2},

Building on these formulations and the Bayesian construction, the main contributions of this paper are summarized as follows.

•

Modeling
.
We propose a Bayesian DRVI framework that integrates Bayesian inference with DRVI to unify robustness and flexibility in a data-driven setting.
By modeling the unknown true distribution within a finite mixture family, we transform the infinite-dimensional distributional ambiguity set into a finite-dimensional one.
The framework captures parametric uncertainty through Bayesian updating, which integrates prior knowledge with observed data. Moreover, we introduce a regularization term in the lower-level maximization problem to ensure numerical stability.

•

Analysis
.
We show that the lower-level maximization problem with a Bayesian parametric ambiguity set is equivalent to a risk-averse formulation with Bayesian risk measures
[

,
50
]
.
We prove that the solutions of (

) and (

B

) as

and

.
Moreover, we show that the unique maximizer of the regularized lower-level problem in (

B
N

) converges to the least-norm maximizer of the unregularized problem in (

B
N

) as
\lambda\downarrow 0
.
We further derive finite-sample guarantees and quantitative stability bounds that account for both data perturbation and regularization effects, addressing practical challenges in data-driven Bayesian DRVI estimation.

•

Applications
.
We apply the proposed Bayesian DRVI framework to a distributionally robust multi-portfolio Nash equilibrium problem, building on Bayesian parametric ambiguity sets as in
[
52
,
53
]
.
Our numerical results show that (i) our approach yields more accurate equilibrium solutions and lower utility error in finite samples than classical DRVI and SVI approaches, (ii) the data-driven equilibrium solutions converge to a solution of the Bayesian DRVI problem, and (iii) our method achieves superior out-of-sample performance, balancing payoff and tail-risk mitigation. These findings underscore the practical relevance and applicability of our method in real-world decision-making scenarios.

The paper is structured as follows. Section 2 introduces a motivating Bayesian DRVI example and explores the construction of data-driven ambiguity sets from the frequentist and Bayesian perspectives. Section 3 proves the existence of solutions to the data-driven Bayesian DRVI problem and analyzes the convergence of the solution of (

Model setup and background.

We begin by demonstrating how Bayesian parametric ambiguity sets can be effectively incorporated into the DRVI framework. The motivations and theoretical foundations of DRVI have been discussed in
[
23
,
46
]
. For completeness, we provide an illustrative example below, highlighting how Bayesian perspectives arise naturally in optimization and equilibrium problems.

Example 1
.

Finite mixture models provide a powerful framework for modeling complex distributions such as asset returns
[
36
]
. Consider two common interpretations of mixture models in finance: (i) if
Q_{1},\ldots,Q_{n}
represent asset returns under different market regimes from scenario analysis, then
\theta_{1},\ldots,\theta_{n}
are the occurrence probabilities of these regimes; (ii) if
Q_{1},\ldots,Q_{n}
reflect asset returns generated by different predictive models, then
\theta_{1},\ldots,\theta_{n}
quantify the credibility weights of the models.
Let
\xi\sim\sum_{j=1}^{n}\theta^{c}_{j}Q_{j}
denote the random asset return vector, and let
x\in\mathcal{X}
denote the corresponding portfolio weights. Following
[
53
]
, the distributionally robust portfolio selection problem can be formulated as:

\min_{x\in\mathcal{X}}\max_{\theta\in\hat{\Theta}_{N}}\sum_{j=1}^{n}\theta_{j}\mathbb{E}_{Q_{j}}[\varphi(x,\xi)],

(2.1)

where the loss (e.g., disutility) function
\varphi:\mathcal{X}\times\Xi\to\mathbb{R}
is convex and continuous in
x
. A pair
(x^{*},\theta^{*})\in\mathcal{X}\times\hat{\Theta}_{N}
is a saddle point of (
2.1
) if and only if the following conditions hold:

x^{*}\in\argmin_{x\in\mathcal{X}}\sum_{j=1}^{n}\theta^{*}_{j}\mathbb{E}_{Q_{j}}[\varphi(x,\xi)]\text{ and }\theta^{*}\in\argmax_{\theta\in\hat{\Theta}_{N}}\sum_{j=1}^{n}\theta_{j}\mathbb{E}_{Q_{j}}[\varphi(x^{*},\xi)].

(2.2)

Assume that
\varphi
is continuously differentiable in
x
and the expectation
\mathbb{E}_{Q_{j}}[\nabla_{x}\varphi(x,\xi)]
is well-defined for all
j=1,\ldots,n
, then (
2.2
) yields the following Bayesian DRVI formulation:

0\in\sum_{j=1}^{n}\theta_{j}\mathbb{E}_{Q_{j}}\left[\Phi\left(x,\xi\right)\right]+\mathcal{N}_{\mathcal{X}}\left(x\right)\text{ and }\theta\in\underset{{\theta}^{\prime}\in\hat{\Theta}_{N}}{\argmax}\sum_{j=1}^{n}{\theta}^{\prime}_{j}\mathbb{E}_{Q_{j}}\left[\varphi\left(x,\xi\right)\right],

with
\Phi(x,\xi):=\nabla_{x}\varphi(x,\xi)
. Now consider the multi-portfolio case with
I
accounts in
[
28
]
. Let
x^{i}\in\mathcal{X}_{i}\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{d_{i}}
denote the portfolio for account
i
, and let
x^{-i}
represent the other
I-1
accounts’ allocations. Let
\varphi_{i}:\mathcal{X}_{1}\times\ldots\times\mathcal{X}_{I}\times\Xi\to\mathbb{R}
be the loss function for account
i
. Inspired by
[
31
]
, the goal of distributionally robust multi-portfolio Nash
equilibrium is to find
\left(x^{1*},\ldots,x^{I*}\right)
and worst-case parameters
\theta^{i*}\in\hat{\Theta}_{N}
for each account
i
such that

\displaystyle x^{i*}\in\argmin_{x^{i}\in\mathcal{X}_{i}}\sum_{j=1}^{n}\theta^{i*}_{j}\mathbb{E}_{Q_{j}}\left[\varphi_{i}\left(x^{i},x^{-i*},\xi\right)\right],\quad i=1,\ldots,I,

(2.3)

\displaystyle\theta^{i*}\in\argmax_{\theta^{i}\in\hat{\Theta}_{N}}\sum_{j=1}^{n}\theta^{i}_{j}\mathbb{E}_{Q_{j}}\left[\varphi_{i}\left(x^{i*},x^{-i*},\xi\right)\right],\quad i=1,\ldots,I.

By the same argument as for (
2.2
), problem (
2.3
) yields the following Bayesian DRVI formulation:

\displaystyle 0\in\sum_{j=1}^{n}\theta^{i}_{j}\mathbb{E}_{Q_{j}}\left[\Phi_{i}\left(x^{1},\ldots,x^{I},\xi\right)\right]+\mathcal{N}_{\mathcal{X}_{i}}\left(x^{i}\right),i=1,\ldots,I,

\displaystyle\theta^{i}\in\underset{{\theta}^{\prime}\in\hat{\Theta}_{N}}{\argmax}\sum_{j=1}^{n}{\theta}^{\prime}_{j}\mathbb{E}_{Q_{j}}\left[\varphi_{i}\left(x^{1},\ldots,x^{I},\xi\right)\right],i=1,\ldots,I,

with
\Phi_{i}\left(x^{1},\ldots,x^{I},\xi\right):=\nabla_{x^{i}}\varphi_{i}\left(x^{1},\ldots,x^{I},\xi\right)
for all
i=1,\ldots,I
.

This example illustrates the versatility of the Bayesian DRVI framework for complex real-world decision problems, particularly in finance. When direct solutions to (
2.1
) or (
2.3
) are intractable, reformulating the problem as a Bayesian DRVI offers a computationally convenient alternative while preserving robustness. Below, we detail the construction of
\hat{\Theta}_{N}
.

2.1
Bayesian parametric ambiguity sets for distributions.

To construct practical ambiguity sets that contain the true parametric set
\Theta^{c}
with high probability, we first introduce two fundamental concepts of confidence regions with coverage guarantees in the DRO context.

Definition 1

(Frequentist guarantee)
.

A set
\mathcal{P}_{N}\subseteq\mathcal{P}
is called a level-
\alpha
frequentist confidence region if
\mathbb{P}_{\mathcal{S}^{N}|P^{c}}\left\{P^{c}\in\mathcal{P}_{N}\right\}\geq 1-\alpha
for a given
\alpha\in(0,1)
, where
\mathbb{P}_{\mathcal{S}^{N}|P^{c}}:=\Pi_{i=1}^{N}P^{c}
denotes the probability measure induced by
N
i.i.d. observations from
P^{c}
.

Definition
1
states that a frequentist confidence region
\mathcal{P}_{N}
contains the true distribution
P^{c}
with probability at least
1-\alpha
under repeated sampling. Common constructions that satisfy this criterion include ambiguity sets defined via moments
[
11
]
,
\phi
-divergences
[
1
]
, hypothesis tests
[
3
]
, and Wasserstein/
\ell_{1}
/
\ell_{\infty}
metrics
[
24
,
25
]
. Next, we introduce a Bayesian analogue of Definition
1
in the parametric setting.

Definition 2

(Posterior guarantee)
.

A set
\mathcal{P}_{\Theta_{N}}:=\{P_{\theta}\mid\theta\in\Theta_{N}\}\subseteq\mathcal{P}_{\Theta}
is called a level-
\alpha
Bayesian confidence region for a given
\alpha\in(0,1)
, if

\mathbb{P}_{\mathcal{S}^{N}}\left\{P_{\theta^{c}}\in\mathcal{P}_{\Theta_{N}}\right\}\geq 1-\alpha
.

In contrast to Definition
1
, the probability here is taken with respect to the posterior distribution
\mathbb{P}_{\mathcal{S}^{N}}
induced by the observed data.
The confidence region
\mathcal{P}_{\Theta_{N}}
in Definition
2
corresponds one-to-one to a parameter set
\Theta_{N}\subseteq\Theta
, which is commonly referred to as a level-
\alpha
Bayesian confidence set in
[
30
]
, satisfying

\mathbb{P}_{\mathcal{S}^{N}}\left\{{\theta^{c}}\in{\Theta_{N}}\right\}\geq 1-\alpha
.
For notational simplicity, we henceforth work directly with the parameter set
\Theta_{N}
.
Moreover, any
\Theta_{N}
that satisfies the posterior guarantee at level-
\alpha
automatically satisfies the Bayesian feasible guarantee in
[
20
, Definition 2]
at the same confidence level. For a detailed discussion on the trade-offs between frequentist and Bayesian methods, we refer the reader to
[
12
,
20
]
.

As multiple sets may satisfy Definition
2
, we aim to construct a computationally tractable minimal Bayesian confidence region
\Theta_{N}
, thereby avoiding excessive conservatism
[
7
]
.
Under mild regularity assumptions
[
20
]
, the posterior distribution
\mathbb{P}_{\mathcal{S}^{N}}
can be approximated by a normal distribution
\mathcal{N}(\hat{{\theta}}_{N},\Sigma_{N})
by the Bernstein-von Mises theorem
[
48
]
, with

\Sigma_{N}=\frac{1}{N}{\cal I}(\hat{\theta}_{N})^{-1}
and
{\cal I}(\hat{\theta}_{N})=-\nabla^{2}_{\theta}\log p(\theta|\mathcal{S}^{N})\Big|_{\theta=\hat{\theta}_{N}}
. This normal approximation is widely employed in the literature; see, e.g.,
[
9
,
20
,
52
]
.
Motivated by this, and applying a Bonferroni correction for simultaneous marginal coverage, we define the confidence radius

\hat{\delta}_{N}=\max_{1\leq j\leq n}\sqrt{\Sigma_{N,jj}}z_{1-\frac{\alpha}{2n}},

where
\Sigma_{N,jj}
is the
j
-th diagonal element of
\Sigma_{N}
and

z_{1-\frac{\alpha}{2n}}
is the
(1-\frac{\alpha}{2n})
-quantile of the standard normal distribution.
This choice leads to the explicit construction

\Theta_{N}:=\mathbb{B}(\hat{\theta}_{N},\hat{\delta}_{N})\cap\Theta=\bigl\{\theta\mid\|\theta-\hat{\theta}_{N}\|_{\infty}\leq\hat{\delta}_{N},\ \theta\in\Theta\bigr\}

(2.4)

as a computationally convenient approximation of the minimal level-
\alpha
Bayesian confidence region.

To quantify the distance between
\Theta_{N}
and
\{\theta^{c}\}
, we employ the metric
\mathbb{D}
induced by the
\ell_{\infty}
norm. Specifically, for a metric space
(\mathbb{X},\|\cdot\|_{\infty})
and any two subsets
S_{1},S_{2}\subseteq\mathbb{X}
, the discrepancy measure is defined as:

\mathbb{D}\left(S_{1},S_{2}\right)=\sup_{s_{1}\in S_{1}}\mathbf{d}\left(s_{1},S_{2}\right)=\sup_{s_{1}\in S_{1}}\inf_{s_{2}\in S_{2}}\|s_{1}-s_{2}\|_{\infty}.

Further, the Hausdorff distance is defined as
\mathbb{H}\left(S_{1},S_{2}\right)=\max\left\{\mathbb{D}\left(S_{1},S_{2}\right),\mathbb{D}\left(S_{2},S_{1}\right)\right\}
.

Assumption 1
.

For all
\theta\in\Theta
, (i) there exists an integrable function
g
such that
|\log f(\xi|\theta)|\leq g(\xi)
with
\mathbb{E}_{P_{\theta^{c}}}[g(\xi)]<\infty
, and (ii) there exist constants
0<c_{1}<c_{2}
such that the prior pdf
p(\theta)
satisfies
c_{1}\leq p({\theta})\leq c_{2}
.

Under Assumption
1
, we can establish the convergence of the Bayesian confidence region
\Theta_{N}
defined in (
2.4
) as
N\to\infty
.

Lemma 1
.

Under Assumption
1
,
\mathbb{H}({\Theta}_{N},\{\theta^{c}\})
converges to 0 almost surely as
N\to\infty
.

This follows from standard results (e.g.,
[
44
, Lemma 3.1]
) and is referred to as Bayesian consistency in
[
45
, Assumption 3.2]
.
Based on the Bayesian confidence region
\Theta_{N}
in (
2.4
) and Lemma
1
, we now construct the Bayesian parametric ambiguity set
\hat{\Theta}_{N}
with the minimal (i.e., smallest) radius that still contains
\Theta^{c}
with posterior probability at least
1-\alpha
.

Theorem 1
.

Let
\hat{r}_{N}:=r^{c}+\hat{\delta}_{N}
and
\hat{\Theta}_{N}:=\mathbb{B}(\hat{\theta}_{N},\hat{r}_{N})\cap\Theta
.
Then

\mathbb{P}_{\mathcal{S}^{N}}\left\{\Theta^{c}\subseteq\hat{\Theta}_{N}\right\}\geq 1-\alpha\text{ and }\hat{r}_{N}=\min\left\{r\;\big|\;r\geq 0,\ \mathbb{P}_{\mathcal{S}^{N}}\left\{\Theta^{c}\subseteq\mathbb{B}(\hat{\theta}_{N},r)\cap\Theta\right\}\geq 1-\alpha\right\}.

Furthermore,
\mathbb{H}(\hat{\Theta}_{N},\Theta^{c})
converges to 0 almost surely as
N\to\infty
.

Proof
.
Recall that the ambiguity set is defined using the
\ell_{\infty}
norm. Suppose that
\theta^{c}\in\Theta_{N}
, then for any
\theta\in\Theta^{c}
, we have

\|\theta-\hat{\theta}_{N}\|_{\infty}\leq\|\theta-\theta^{c}\|_{\infty}+\|\theta^{c}-\hat{\theta}_{N}\|_{\infty}\leq r^{c}+\hat{\delta}_{N}.

Thus, whenever
\theta^{c}\in\Theta_{N}
, the true ambiguity set
\Theta^{c}
is contained in
\hat{\Theta}_{N}
.
In terms of probability, this implies

\mathbb{P}_{\mathcal{S}^{N}}\left\{\Theta^{c}\subseteq\hat{\Theta}_{N}\right\}\geq\mathbb{P}_{\mathcal{S}^{N}}\left\{\theta^{c}\in\Theta_{N}\right\}\geq 1-\alpha.

To verify the minimality of
\hat{r}_{N}
, assume that there exists some
\delta^{\prime}_{N}<\hat{\delta}_{N}
such that

\mathbb{P}_{\mathcal{S}^{N}}\left\{\Theta^{c}\subseteq\mathbb{B}(\hat{\theta}_{N},\delta^{\prime}_{N}+r^{c})\cap\Theta\right\}\geq 1-\alpha.

For
\Theta^{c}\subseteq\mathbb{B}(\hat{\theta}_{N},\delta^{\prime}_{N}+r^{c})\cap\Theta
to hold, we must have
\theta^{c}\in\mathbb{B}(\hat{\theta}_{N},\delta^{\prime}_{N})\cap\Theta
.
This implies

\mathbb{P}_{\mathcal{S}^{N}}\left\{\theta^{c}\in\mathbb{B}(\hat{\theta}_{N},\delta^{\prime}_{N})\cap\Theta\right\}\geq\mathbb{P}_{\mathcal{S}^{N}}\left\{\Theta^{c}\subseteq\mathbb{B}(\hat{\theta}_{N},\delta^{\prime}_{N}+r^{c})\cap\Theta\right\}\geq 1-\alpha,

which contradicts the minimality of
\hat{\delta}_{N}
. Finally, the convergence of
\hat{\Theta}_{N}
to
\Theta^{c}
follows directly from Lemma
1
.

\hfill\blacksquare

Theorem
1
shows that, via Bernstein-von Mises theorem
[
48
]
with Bonferroni simultaneous marginal correction, the infinite-dimensional distributional ambiguity set over distributions can be reduced to a finite-dimensional ambiguity set
\hat{\Theta}_{N}=\mathbb{B}(\hat{\theta}_{N},\hat{r}_{N})\cap\Theta
with a posterior coverage guarantee.

2.2
Comparison with classical ambiguity sets.

In the following, we provide an example to illustrate the effectiveness of our approach compared to other ambiguity sets. Assume that the true data-generating distribution
P^{c}
follows a Student-
t
distribution with 3 degrees of freedom.

Given samples
\mathcal{S}^{N}:=\{\xi^{1},\ldots,\xi^{N}\}
from
P^{c}
, the
\phi
-divergence ambiguity set (e.g., modified
\chi^{2}
divergence) in
[
1
,
27
]
and the
\ell_{1}
norm ambiguity set in
[
16
,
24
,
49
]
that satisfy the frequentist guarantee at level-
\alpha
can be constructed as follows:

\text{$\ell_{1}$ norm: }\mathcal{P}^{\ell_{1}}_{N}=\Biggl\{\,P\;\bigg|\;p=(p_{1},\ldots,p_{N})\in\Delta^{N},\,\sum_{i=1}^{N}|p_{i}-\hat{p}_{N,i}|\leq\sqrt{\frac{2}{N}\log\!\left(\frac{2}{\alpha}\right)}\Biggr\},

\text{Modified }\chi^{2}\text{ divergence: }\mathcal{P}^{\chi^{2}}_{N}=\Biggl\{\,P\;\bigg|\;p=(p_{1},\ldots,p_{N})\in\Delta^{N},\,\sum_{i=1}^{N}\frac{(p_{i}-\hat{p}_{N,i})^{2}}{\hat{p}_{N,i}}\leq\frac{\chi^{2}_{N-1,1-\alpha}}{N}\Biggr\}.

Here,
\Delta^{N}:=\{p\mid\sum_{i=1}^{N}p_{i}=1,\ p\in\mathbb{R}^{N}_{+}\}
is the
N
-dimensional probability simplex and
\hat{p}_{N}:=(1/N,\ldots,1/N)
denotes the probability vector of the empirical distribution based on
\mathcal{S}^{N}
.
Each distribution
P
supported on
\mathcal{S}^{N}
is uniquely identified with its probability vector

p=(p_{1},\ldots,p_{N})\in\Delta^{N}
, where
p_{i}:=P(\xi=\xi^{i})
represents the probability mass at sample
\xi^{i}
.
The term
\chi^{2}_{N-1,1-\alpha}
denotes the
(1-\alpha)
-quantile of the
\chi^{2}_{N-1}
distribution with
N-1
degrees of freedom.

In our Bayesian scheme, we model
P^{c}
as a finite mixture of
n=5
Gaussian components

Q_{j}=\mathcal{N}(\mu_{j},\sigma_{j}^{2})
,
j=1,\ldots,5
.
Specifically, we set the component means and variances as

\mu_{1}=0,\ \mu_{2}=0,\ \mu_{3}=0,\ \mu_{4}=-2.8,\ \mu_{5}=2.8

and

\sigma_{1}^{2}=0.45,\ \sigma_{2}^{2}=1.1,\ \sigma_{3}^{2}=4,\ \sigma_{4}^{2}=0.8,\ \sigma_{5}^{2}=0.8
.
This construction yields a low-dimensional yet expressive representation: (i)
Q_{1}
models sharp peaks,
Q_{2}
captures moderate dispersion,
Q_{3}
reflects heavy tails, and (ii)
Q_{4}
and
Q_{5}
enable flexible adjustment to left- or right-skewed distributions. Applying the construction in (
2.4
), we can generate the corresponding Bayesian confidence region at the same level-
\alpha
within a 5-dimensional parameter set.

To visualize the resulting ambiguity sets, we plot the pointwise lower and upper envelopes of the cumulative distribution function (CDF) induced by each ambiguity set at level
\alpha=0.05
for sample sizes
N\in\{20,50,200\}
; see Figure
1
.

Figure 1:

\alpha=0.05
.

The CDF envelopes plotted in Figure
1
highlight three principal insights:

•

Superior nominal fit.
The Bayesian nominal CDF induced by
\hat{\theta}_{N}
tracks the true CDF closely even for small
N
and remains accurate even when the empirical CDF deviates (e.g.,
N=50
).

•

Conservatism.
For the same confidence level, the Bayesian confidence region typically yields narrower CDF envelopes than

and

•

Geometric simplicity.
The classical sets

is induced by a 5-dimensional

In short, by controlling distributional ambiguity via a low-dimensional geometry, Bayesian inference yields a DRVI problem that is both statistically accurate and computationally tractable.

Proposition 1
.

Suppose that
\mathbb{E}_{Q_{j}}[\Phi(\cdot,\xi)]

3.2

In this subsection, we prove the

as

. Due to the continuity of the functions and the compactness of
\mathcal{X}
, there exists

such that
\sup_{x\in\mathcal{X},j\in\{1,\ldots,n\}}\{|\mathbb{E}_{Q_{j}}\varphi(x,\xi)|,|\mathbb{E}_{Q_{j}}\Phi_{1}(x,\xi)|,\ldots,|\mathbb{E}_{Q_{j}}\Phi_{d}(x,\xi)|\}\leq M
. For each
j=1,\dots,n
, we define the component-wise lower and upper bounds for the mixture weights as
{\hat{\theta}}^{l}_{N,j}:=\hat{\theta}_{N,j}-\hat{r}_{N}
and
{\hat{\theta}}^{u}_{N,j}:=\hat{\theta}_{N,j}+\hat{r}_{N}.

The following result provides an alternative representation of the lower-level objective function, highlighting the risk-averse nature of our Bayesian DRVI framework.

Theorem 2
.

Let
J
be a categorical random variable taking values over
\{1,\dots,n\}
. Let

P^{l}
and
P^{u-l}
be the probability distributions of
J
with

P^{l}(J=j)=\frac{{\hat{\theta}}^{l}_{N,j}}{\sum_{i=1}^{n}{\hat{\theta}}^{l}_{N,i}}\text{ and }\ P^{u-l}(J={j})=\frac{{\hat{\theta}}^{u}_{N,j}-{\hat{\theta}}^{l}_{N,j}}{\sum_{i=1}^{n}(\hat{\theta}^{u}_{N,i}-{\hat{\theta}}^{l}_{N,i})}.

Then, for any fixed
x\in\mathcal{X}
, we have

\begin{split}\nu(x,\hat{\theta}_{N},\hat{r}_{N})=\sum_{j=1}^{n}{\hat{\theta}}^{l}_{N,j}\mathbb{E}_{P^{l}}\mathbb{E}_{Q_{J}}[\varphi(x,\xi)]+(1-\sum_{j=1}^{n}{\hat{\theta}}^{l}_{N,j})\mathrm{CVaR}^{0.5}_{P^{u-l}}\mathbb{E}_{Q_{J}}[\varphi(x,\xi)],\end{split}

where
\mathrm{CVaR}^{0.5}_{P^{u-l}}\mathbb{E}_{Q_{J}}[\varphi(x,\xi)]
represents the conditional value-at-risk (CVaR) of
\mathbb{E}_{Q_{J}}[\varphi(x,\xi)]
with respect to
P^{u-l}
at confidence level 0.5.

Proof
.
Without loss of generality, for a given
x
, we assume that the values
\varphi_{j}:=\mathbb{E}_{Q_{j}}[\varphi(x,\xi)]
are sorted in a non-decreasing order:

\varphi_{1}\leq\ldots\leq\varphi_{n}
.
Recall that
\nu(x,\hat{\theta}_{N},\hat{r}_{N})
equals the optimal value of the following optimization problem:

\begin{split}\sup_{\theta\geq 0}&\sum_{j=1}^{n}\theta_{j}\varphi_{j}\\
\text{s.t.}\ &\hat{\theta}_{N,j}^{l}\leq\theta_{j}\leq\hat{\theta}_{N,j}^{u},\ j=1,\ldots,n,\ e^{\top}\theta=1.\end{split}

(3.1)

Introducing Lagrange multipliers

d_{u}^{j}\geq 0
for the upper bounds,

d_{l}^{j}\geq 0
for the lower bounds,
and
d_{0}\in\mathbb{R}
for the equality constraint, the Lagrange dual problem of (
3.1
) can be reformulated as

\displaystyle\inf_{d_{u},d_{l}\geq 0,d_{0}}\sup_{\theta\geq 0}\sum_{j=1}^{n}\theta_{j}\varphi_{j}+\sum_{j=1}^{n}d_{u}^{j}(\hat{\theta}_{N,j}^{u}-\theta_{j})+\sum_{j=1}^{n}d_{l}^{j}(\theta_{j}-\hat{\theta}_{N,j}^{l})+d_{0}\left(1-\sum_{j=1}^{n}\theta_{j}\right)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\inf_{d_{u},d_{l}\geq 0,d_{0}}d_{0}+\sum_{j=1}^{n}(d_{u}^{j}\hat{\theta}_{N,j}^{u}-d_{l}^{j}\hat{\theta}_{N,j}^{l})+\sum_{j=1}^{n}\sup_{{\theta}_{j}\geq 0}(\varphi_{j}+d_{l}^{j}-d_{u}^{j}-d_{0})\theta_{j}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\inf_{d_{0}}d_{0}+\sum_{j=1}^{n}\inf_{\begin{subarray}{c}d_{u}^{j},d_{l}^{j}\geq 0\\
\varphi_{j}+d_{l}^{j}-d_{u}^{j}-d_{0}\leq 0\end{subarray}}\left\{d_{u}^{j}\hat{\theta}_{N,j}^{u}-d_{l}^{j}\hat{\theta}_{N,j}^{l}\right\}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\inf_{d_{0}}d_{0}+\sum_{j=1}^{j^{*}}(\varphi_{j}-d_{0})\hat{\theta}_{N,{j}}^{l}+\sum_{j=j^{*}+1}^{n}(\varphi_{j}-d_{0})\hat{\theta}_{N,{j}}^{u}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\inf_{d_{0}}d_{0}+\sum_{j=1}^{n}\hat{\theta}_{N,j}^{l}(\varphi_{j}-d_{0})+\sum_{j=j^{*}+1}^{n}(\hat{\theta}_{N,{j}}^{u}-\hat{\theta}_{N,{j}}^{l})(\varphi_{j}-d_{0})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{j=1}^{n}\hat{\theta}_{N,j}^{l}\varphi_{j}+\inf_{d_{0}}\left\{(1-\sum_{j=1}^{n}{\hat{\theta}}^{l}_{N,j})d_{0}+\sum_{j=1}^{n}(\hat{\theta}_{N,{j}}^{u}-\hat{\theta}_{N,{j}}^{l})\left[\varphi_{j}-d_{0}\right]^{+}\right\}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{j=1}^{n}{\hat{\theta}}^{l}_{N,j}\mathbb{E}_{P^{l}}\mathbb{E}_{Q_{J}}[\varphi(x,\xi)]+(1-\sum_{j=1}^{n}{\hat{\theta}}^{l}_{N,j})\inf_{d_{0}}\left\{d_{0}+2\mathbb{E}_{P^{u-l}}\left[\mathbb{E}_{Q_{J}}[\varphi(x,\xi)]-d_{0}\right]^{+}\right\}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{j=1}^{n}{\hat{\theta}}^{l}_{N,j}\mathbb{E}_{P^{l}}\mathbb{E}_{Q_{J}}[\varphi(x,\xi)]+(1-\sum_{j=1}^{n}{\hat{\theta}}^{l}_{N,j})\mathrm{CVaR}^{0.5}_{P^{u-l}}\mathbb{E}_{Q_{J}}[\varphi(x,\xi)],

where
[a]^{+}:=\max\{a,0\}
,
j^{*}:=\max\{j\in\{1,\ldots,n\}:\varphi_{j}<d_{0}\}
if
\varphi_{1}<d_{0}
, and
j^{*}:=0
otherwise. The last two equations follow from the definitions of
P^{l}
and
P^{u-l}
and the standard Rockafellar-Uryasev CVaR formula in
[
40
, Theorem 10]
, respectively.

\hfill\blacksquare

Theorem
2
provides an equivalent reformulation of the lower-level objective in terms of the Bayesian risk measure, which has been widely used in stochastic optimization and control
[
45
,
50
]
.
Leveraging this reformulation, we next show that

is Lipschitz continuous.

Lemma 2
.

Suppose that
\mathbb{E}_{Q_{j}}[\varphi(\cdot,\xi)]
is Lipschitz continuous with modulus
L_{\varphi,j}>0
for
j\in\{1,2,\ldots,n\}
. Then
\nu
is Lipschitz continuous.

Proof
.
Let
(x,\hat{\theta}_{N},\hat{r}_{N})
and
(y,\tilde{\theta}_{N},\tilde{r}_{N})
be two points in
\mathcal{X}\times\Theta\times[0,1]
.
Denote

\displaystyle\Delta:=\nu(x,\hat{\theta}_{N},\hat{r}_{N})-\nu(y,\tilde{\theta}_{N},\tilde{r}_{N})=\Delta_{1}+\Delta_{2},

where

\displaystyle\Delta_{1}

\displaystyle:=\sum_{j=1}^{n}(\hat{\theta}_{N,j}-\hat{r}_{N})\mathbb{E}_{Q_{j}}[\varphi(x,\xi)]-\sum_{j=1}^{n}(\tilde{\theta}_{N,j}-\tilde{r}_{N})\mathbb{E}_{Q_{j}}[\varphi(y,\xi)],

\displaystyle\Delta_{2}

\displaystyle:=\left(1-\sum_{j=1}^{n}(\hat{\theta}_{N,j}-\hat{r}_{N})\right)\mathrm{CVaR}^{0.5}_{P^{u-l}}\mathbb{E}_{Q_{J}}[\varphi(x,\xi)]-\left(1-\sum_{j=1}^{n}(\tilde{\theta}_{N,j}-\tilde{r}_{N})\right)\mathrm{CVaR}^{0.5}_{\tilde{P}^{u-l}}\mathbb{E}_{Q_{J}}[\varphi(y,\xi)].

For
\Delta_{1}
, by the triangle inequality and Lipschitz continuity, we obtain

\displaystyle|\Delta_{1}|

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{j=1}^{n}|\hat{\theta}_{N,j}-\hat{r}_{N}|\cdot|\mathbb{E}_{Q_{j}}[\varphi(x,\xi)]-\mathbb{E}_{Q_{j}}[\varphi(y,\xi)]|+\sum_{j=1}^{n}|\hat{\theta}_{N,j}-\tilde{\theta}_{N,j}+\tilde{r}_{N}-\hat{r}_{N}|\cdot|\mathbb{E}_{Q_{j}}[\varphi(y,\xi)]|

\displaystyle\leq n\max_{j}L_{\varphi,j}\|x-y\|+nM\left(\|\hat{\theta}_{N}-\tilde{\theta}_{N}\|_{\infty}+|\hat{r}_{N}-\tilde{r}_{N}|\right).

Moreover, for
\Delta_{2}
,
we have

\displaystyle|\Delta_{2}|

\displaystyle=n\hat{r}_{N}\mathrm{CVaR}^{0.5}_{{P}^{u-l}}\mathbb{E}_{Q_{J}}[\varphi(x,\xi)]-n\tilde{r}_{N}\mathrm{CVaR}^{0.5}_{\tilde{P}^{u-l}}\mathbb{E}_{Q_{J}}[\varphi(y,\xi)]

\displaystyle\leq n|\hat{r}_{N}-\tilde{r}_{N}|\cdot\left|\mathrm{CVaR}^{0.5}_{{P}^{u-l}}\mathbb{E}_{Q_{J}}[\varphi(x,\xi)]\right|+n\tilde{r}_{N}\left|\mathrm{CVaR}^{0.5}_{P^{u-l}}\mathbb{E}_{Q_{J}}[\varphi(x,\xi)]-\mathrm{CVaR}^{0.5}_{\tilde{P}^{u-l}}\mathbb{E}_{Q_{J}}[\varphi(y,\xi)]\right|

\displaystyle\leq nM|\hat{r}_{N}-\tilde{r}_{N}|+2n\max_{j}L_{\varphi,j}\|x-y\|,

where the last inequality follows from the representation of CVaR in
[
40
]
and the definition of
P^{u-l}
in Theorem
2
.
Combining the bounds on
\Delta_{1}
and
\Delta_{2}
yields the desired Lipschitz continuity.

\hfill\blacksquare

Theorem 3
.

Suppose that Assumption
1
holds and
\mathbb{E}_{Q_{j}}[\varphi(\cdot,\xi)]
is Lipschitz continuous with modulus
L_{\varphi,j}>0
for
j\in\{1,2,\ldots,n\}
. Then
\mathbb{D}\left(\mathfrak{X}(\hat{\theta}_{N},\hat{r}_{N}),\mathfrak{X}(\theta^{c},r^{c})\right)\to 0
almost surely as
N\to\infty.

Proof
.
Let
\{x_{N}\}
be a sequence with
x_{N}\in\mathfrak{X}(\hat{\theta}_{N},\hat{r}_{N})
. From the compactness of
\mathcal{X}
,
there exists a subsequence
\{x_{N_{k}}\}
of
\{x_{N}\}
such that
x_{N_{k}}\to x^{*}
as
k\to\infty
. It suffices to demonstrate that
x^{*}\in\mathfrak{X}(\theta^{c},r^{c})
.
For each
x_{N_{k}}\in\mathfrak{X}(\hat{\theta}_{N_{k}},\hat{r}_{N_{k}})
, there exists
{\theta}_{N_{k}}\in\hat{\Theta}_{N_{k}}
satisfying

\displaystyle 0

\displaystyle\in\sum_{j=1}^{n}{\theta}_{{N_{k}},j}\mathbb{E}_{Q_{j}}[\Phi(x_{N_{k}},\xi)]+\mathcal{N}_{\mathcal{X}}(x_{N_{k}}),

\displaystyle{\theta}_{N_{k}}

\displaystyle\in\underset{\theta\in\hat{\Theta}_{N_{k}}}{\argmax}\sum_{j=1}^{n}\theta_{j}\mathbb{E}_{Q_{j}}[\varphi\left(x_{N_{k}},\xi\right)].

Since
\Theta
is compact, there exists a subsequence (not relabeled)
\{{\theta}_{N_{k}}\}
such that
{\theta}_{N_{k}}\to{\theta}^{*}
.
Thus

\displaystyle\left|\sum_{j=1}^{n}{\theta}_{N_{k},j}\mathbb{E}_{Q_{j}}[\varphi\left(x_{N_{k}},\xi\right)]-\sum_{j=1}^{n}\theta_{j}^{*}\mathbb{E}_{Q_{j}}[\varphi\left(x^{*},\xi\right)]\right|

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\left|\sum_{j=1}^{n}{\theta}_{N_{k},j}\mathbb{E}_{Q_{j}}[\varphi\left(x_{N_{k}},\xi\right)]-\sum_{j=1}^{n}{\theta}_{N_{k},j}\mathbb{E}_{Q_{j}}[\varphi\left(x^{*},\xi\right)]\right|+\left|\sum_{j=1}^{n}{\theta}_{N_{k},j}\mathbb{E}_{Q_{j}}[\varphi\left(x^{*},\xi\right)]-\sum_{j=1}^{n}{\theta}_{j}^{*}\mathbb{E}_{Q_{j}}[\varphi\left(x^{*},\xi\right)]\right|

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\sum_{j=1}^{n}{\theta}_{N_{k},j}L_{\varphi,j}\left\|x_{N_{k}}-x^{*}\right\|+\sum_{j=1}^{n}|{\theta}^{*}_{j}-{\theta}_{N_{k},j}|\mathbb{E}_{Q_{j}}[\varphi\left(x^{*},\xi\right)]\to 0,

as
k\to\infty
. Based on the convergence in Theorem
1
and the Lipschitz property of

in Lemma

, it follows that
\nu(x_{N_{k}},\hat{\theta}_{N_{k}},\hat{r}_{N_{k}})\to\nu(x^{*},\theta^{c},r^{c})
almost surely as

. Since
\sum_{j=1}^{n}{\theta}_{N_{k},j}\mathbb{E}_{Q_{j}}[\varphi\left(x_{N_{k}},\xi\right)]=\nu(x_{N_{k}},\hat{\theta}_{N_{k}},\hat{r}_{N_{k}})
, we have
\sum_{j=1}^{n}{\theta}_{j}^{*}\mathbb{E}_{Q_{j}}[\varphi\left(x^{*},\xi\right)]=\nu(x^{*},\theta^{c},r^{c})
, which leads to

{\theta}^{*}\in\vartheta(x^{*},\theta^{c},r^{c}).

As
k\to\infty
, we have
\sum_{j=1}^{n}{\theta}_{N_{k},j}\mathbb{E}_{Q_{j}}[\Phi\left(x_{N_{k}},\xi\right)]\to\sum_{j=1}^{n}{\theta}_{j}^{*}\mathbb{E}_{Q_{j}}[\Phi\left(x^{*},\xi\right)]
. Since
\mathcal{N}_{\mathcal{X}}(\cdot)
is upper semicontinuous for the closed and convex
\mathcal{X}
and

0\in\sum_{j=1}^{n}{\theta}_{N_{k},j}\mathbb{E}_{Q_{j}}[\Phi\left(x_{N_{k}},\xi\right)]+\mathcal{N}_{\mathcal{X}}\left(x_{N_{k}}\right),

we obtain

\displaystyle 0

4
Stability of optimal solutions.

While Section 3 proves asymptotic convergence of solutions to (

B
N

λ

) as
N\to\infty
and
\lambda\downarrow 0
, practical applications rely on finite, often noisy datasets and a fixed positive
\lambda
.
This setting introduces two distinct sources of deviation from the idealized solution:
(i) sampling noise, which perturbs the estimated ambiguity parameters
(\hat{\theta}_{N},\hat{r}_{N})
, and (ii) the regularization bias due to
\lambda>0
.
These facts highlight the importance of stability analysis in ensuring robustness.
In particular, small perturbations in
\mathcal{S}^{N}
may generate a contaminated dataset
\tilde{\mathcal{S}}^{N}=\{\tilde{\xi}^{1},\dots,\tilde{\xi}^{N}\}
with shifted ambiguity parameters
(\tilde{\theta}_{N},\tilde{r}_{N})
, leading to the perturbed Bayesian DRVI problem:

\displaystyle 0

\displaystyle\in\sum_{j=1}^{n}\theta_{j}\mathbb{E}_{Q_{j}}[\Phi(x,\xi)]+\mathcal{N}_{\mathcal{X}}(x),

(
\tilde{\text{B}}_{N}^{\lambda}
)

\displaystyle\theta

\displaystyle\in\underset{{\theta}^{\prime}\in\tilde{\Theta}_{N}}{\argmax}\sum_{j=1}^{n}{\theta}^{\prime}_{j}\mathbb{E}_{Q_{j}}[\varphi(x,\xi)]-\lambda\|{\theta}^{\prime}\|^{2},

where the perturbed ambiguity set
\tilde{\Theta}_{N}:=\mathbb{B}(\tilde{\theta}_{N},\tilde{r}_{N})\cap\Theta
is constructed from the contaminated dataset
{\tilde{\mathcal{S}}^{N}}
.

This section investigates how the
x
-solution set responds to perturbations of ambiguity sets. In addition, we provide finite-sample guarantees that ensure statistical performance with high probability. Further, we analyze whether the perturbed solution set
\mathfrak{X}^{\lambda}(\tilde{\theta}_{N},\tilde{r}_{N})
closely approximates the solution set
\mathfrak{X}(\theta^{c},r^{c})
for sufficiently large
N
. If this holds, we can safely employ the perturbed solutions as reliable approximations to the Bayesian DRVI problem (

B
c

), even under data contamination.
We begin by establishing the following lemma, which provides a bound on the Hausdorff distance between ambiguity sets corresponding to different parameters.

Lemma 5

(Quantitative stability of Bayesian parametric ambiguity sets)
.

For any
\hat{\theta}_{N}
,
\tilde{\theta}_{N}\in\Theta
and
\hat{r}_{N},\tilde{r}_{N}\in[0,1]
, we have

\mathbb{H}\left(\hat{\Theta}_{N},\tilde{\Theta}_{N}\right)\leq\|\hat{\theta}_{N}-\tilde{\theta}_{N}\|_{\infty}+\left|\tilde{r}_{N}-\hat{r}_{N}\right|.

Proof
.
By the definition of the Hausdorff distance, we first show that

\mathbb{D}\left(\hat{\Theta}_{N},\tilde{\Theta}_{N}\right)\leq\|\hat{\theta}_{N}-\tilde{\theta}_{N}\|_{\infty}+\left|\tilde{r}_{N}-\hat{r}_{N}\right|.

(4.1)

If
\hat{\Theta}_{N}\subseteq\tilde{\Theta}_{N}
, (
4.1
) must hold since
\mathbb{D}\left(\hat{\Theta}_{N},\tilde{\Theta}_{N}\right)=0
. Therefore, we focus on the case
\hat{\Theta}_{N}\not\subseteq\tilde{\Theta}_{N}
. Consider
\theta^{\prime}\in\hat{\Theta}_{N}\backslash\tilde{\Theta}_{N}
and define
\zeta:=\frac{\tilde{r}_{N}}{\|\theta^{\prime}-\tilde{\theta}_{N}\|_{\infty}}
. Since
\theta^{\prime}\notin\tilde{\Theta}_{N}
, we have
{\tilde{r}_{N}}<{\|\theta^{\prime}-\tilde{\theta}_{N}\|_{\infty}}
, implying that
{\zeta}\in(0,1)
. Define
\bar{\theta}:={\zeta}\theta^{\prime}+(1-{\zeta})\tilde{\theta}_{N}
. Then,

\mathbf{d}(\bar{\theta},\tilde{\theta}_{N})=\|{\zeta}\theta^{\prime}+(1-{\zeta})\tilde{\theta}_{N}-\tilde{\theta}_{N}\|_{\infty}\leq{\zeta}\|\theta^{\prime}-\tilde{\theta}_{N}\|_{\infty}=\tilde{r}_{N},

which means that
\bar{\theta}\in\tilde{\Theta}_{N}
. Hence

\displaystyle\mathbf{d}\left(\theta^{\prime},\tilde{\Theta}_{N}\right)

\displaystyle\leq\|\theta^{\prime}-\bar{\theta}\|_{\infty}=\|\theta^{\prime}-{\zeta}\theta^{\prime}-(1-{\zeta})\tilde{\theta}_{N}\|_{\infty}

\displaystyle\leq(1-{\zeta})\|\theta^{\prime}-\tilde{\theta}_{N}\|_{\infty}=\|\theta^{\prime}-\tilde{\theta}_{N}\|_{\infty}-\tilde{r}_{N}

\displaystyle\leq\|\theta^{\prime}-\hat{\theta}_{N}\|_{\infty}+\|\hat{\theta}_{N}-\tilde{\theta}_{N}\|_{\infty}-\tilde{r}_{N}\leq\hat{r}_{N}+\|\hat{\theta}_{N}-\tilde{\theta}_{N}\|_{\infty}-\tilde{r}_{N}

\displaystyle\leq\|\hat{\theta}_{N}-\tilde{\theta}_{N}\|_{\infty}+|\hat{r}_{N}-\tilde{r}_{N}|.

By symmetry, the same bound holds for
\mathbb{D}\left(\tilde{\Theta}_{N},\hat{\Theta}_{N}\right)
, which completes the proof.

\hfill\blacksquare

Lemma 6
.

Suppose that the conditions in Theorem
5
hold. Then, for any
x\in\mathcal{X}
and
\lambda\geq 0
,

|\nu^{\lambda}(x,\hat{\theta}_{N},\hat{r}_{N})-\nu^{\lambda}(x,\tilde{\theta}_{N},\tilde{r}_{N})|\leq(nM+2\lambda)(\|\hat{\theta}_{N}-\tilde{\theta}_{N}\|_{\infty}+\left|\tilde{r}_{N}-\hat{r}_{N}\right|).

Proof
.
Note that

\displaystyle\nu^{\lambda}(x,\hat{\theta}_{N},\hat{r}_{N})-\nu^{\lambda}(x,\tilde{\theta}_{N},\tilde{r}_{N})

\displaystyle=\max_{\theta\in\hat{\Theta}_{N}}\left[\sum_{j=1}^{n}\theta_{j}\mathbb{E}_{Q_{j}}[\varphi(x,\xi)]-\lambda\|\theta\|^{2}\right]-\max_{\theta^{\prime}\in\tilde{\Theta}_{N}}\left[\sum_{j=1}^{n}\theta_{j}^{\prime}\mathbb{E}_{Q_{j}}[\varphi(x,\xi)]-\lambda\|\theta^{\prime}\|^{2}\right]

\displaystyle=\max_{\theta\in\hat{\Theta}_{N}}\min_{\theta^{\prime}\in\tilde{\Theta}_{N}}\left(\sum_{j=1}^{n}\theta_{j}\mathbb{E}_{Q_{j}}[\varphi(x,\xi)]-\lambda\|\theta\|^{2}-\sum_{j=1}^{n}\theta_{j}^{\prime}\mathbb{E}_{Q_{j}}[\varphi(x,\xi)]+\lambda\|\theta^{\prime}\|^{2}\right)

\displaystyle\leq nM\max_{\theta\in\hat{\Theta}_{N}}\min_{\theta^{\prime}\in\tilde{\Theta}_{N}}\|\theta-\theta^{\prime}\|_{\infty}+\lambda\max_{\theta\in\hat{\Theta}_{N}}\min_{\theta^{\prime}\in\tilde{\Theta}_{N}}\left|\|\theta\|^{2}-\|\theta^{\prime}\|^{2}\right|

\displaystyle\leq nM\max_{\theta\in\hat{\Theta}_{N}}\min_{\theta^{\prime}\in\tilde{\Theta}_{N}}\|\theta-\theta^{\prime}\|_{\infty}+2\lambda\max_{\theta\in\hat{\Theta}_{N}}\min_{\theta^{\prime}\in\tilde{\Theta}_{N}}\|\theta-\theta^{\prime}\|_{\infty}=(nM+2\lambda)\mathbb{D}\left(\hat{\Theta}_{N},\tilde{\Theta}_{N}\right).

Similarly, we can show that
\nu^{\lambda}(x,\tilde{\theta}_{N},\tilde{r}_{N})-\nu^{\lambda}(x,\hat{\theta}_{N},\hat{r}_{N})\leq(nM+2\lambda)\mathbb{D}\left(\tilde{\Theta}_{N},\hat{\Theta}_{N}\right)
. Thus, combining with Lemma
5
, we can draw the conclusion.

\hfill\blacksquare

The following result asserts that the optimal
\theta
-solution set of the lower-level maximization problem
\vartheta^{\lambda}(x,\hat{\theta}_{N},\hat{r}_{N})
is nonempty and upper semicontinuous near
(\hat{\theta}_{N},\hat{r}_{N})
. Although the maximization problem has a unique solution for
\lambda>0
, we use the
\theta
-solution set to include the case with
\lambda=0
.
To quantify upper semicontinuity, we need a growth condition on the objective function near the
\theta
-solution set. Inspired by the literature in stochastic optimization, we consider the growth function
\psi_{x}^{\lambda}
defined on
\mathbb{R}_{+}
by

\psi_{x}^{\lambda}(\tau):=\min_{\theta\in\hat{\Theta}_{N}}\left\{\nu^{\lambda}(x,\hat{\theta}_{N},\hat{r}_{N})-\left(\sum_{j=1}^{n}\theta_{j}\mathbb{E}_{Q_{j}}[\varphi(x,\xi)]-\lambda\|\theta\|^{2}\right)\,\big|\,\mathbf{d}\left(\theta,\vartheta^{\lambda}(x,\hat{\theta}_{N},\hat{r}_{N})\right)\geq\tau\right\},

i.e., near its
\theta
-solution set
\vartheta^{\lambda}(x,\hat{\theta}_{N},\hat{r}_{N})
, and the associated function

\Psi_{x}^{\lambda}(\eta):=\eta+(\psi_{x}^{\lambda})^{-1}(2\eta),

where
(\psi_{x}^{\lambda})^{-1}(t):=\sup\left\{\tau\,|\,\tau\geq 0,\psi_{x}^{\lambda}(\tau)\leq t\right\}
and
\eta\in\mathbb{R}_{+}
.

Theorem 6
.

Suppose that the conditions in Theorem
5
hold. Then, for any fixed
x\in\mathcal{X}
,
there exists a constant
\hat{L}\geq 1
such that

\displaystyle\vartheta^{\lambda}(x,\tilde{\theta}_{N},\tilde{r}_{N})

\displaystyle\subseteq\vartheta^{\lambda}(x,\hat{\theta}_{N},\hat{r}_{N})+\Psi_{x}^{\lambda}\left(\hat{L}\mathbb{D}(\tilde{\Theta}_{N},\hat{\Theta}_{N})\right)\mathbb{B}_{\infty}

\displaystyle\subseteq\vartheta^{\lambda}(x,\hat{\theta}_{N},\hat{r}_{N})+\Psi_{x}^{\lambda}\left(\hat{L}(\|\tilde{\theta}_{N}-\hat{\theta}_{N}\|_{\infty}+\left|\tilde{r}_{N}-\hat{r}_{N}\right|)\right)\mathbb{B}_{\infty}

holds for any
\tilde{\theta}_{N}\in\Theta
and
\tilde{r}_{N}\in[0,1]
.
Here
\mathbb{B}_{\infty}
denotes the unit ball under the
\ell_{\infty}
norm.

Proof
. The argument closely follows the structure of
[
41
, Theorem 9]
.
Let
{\theta}^{*}\in\vartheta^{\lambda}(x,\tilde{\theta}_{N},\tilde{r}_{N})
and
\bar{\delta}:=\mathbb{D}(\tilde{\Theta}_{N},\hat{\Theta}_{N})
,
there exists
\bar{\theta}\in\hat{\Theta}_{N}
such that
\|{\theta}^{*}-\bar{\theta}\|_{\infty}\leq\bar{\delta}
.
From Lemma
6
and the definition of
\psi_{x}^{\lambda}
, we have

\displaystyle 2(nM+2\lambda)\bar{\delta}

\displaystyle\geq(nM+2\lambda)\bar{\delta}+\nu^{\lambda}(x,\hat{\theta}_{N},\hat{r}_{N})-\nu^{\lambda}(x,\tilde{\theta}_{N},\tilde{r}_{N})

\displaystyle=(nM+2\lambda)\bar{\delta}+\nu^{\lambda}(x,\hat{\theta}_{N},\hat{r}_{N})-\left(\sum_{j=1}^{n}{\theta}^{*}_{j}\mathbb{E}_{Q_{j}}[\varphi(x,\xi)]-\lambda\|{\theta}^{*}\|^{2}\right)

\displaystyle\geq\nu^{\lambda}(x,\hat{\theta}_{N},\hat{r}_{N})-\left(\sum_{j=1}^{n}\bar{\theta}_{j}\mathbb{E}_{Q_{j}}[\varphi(x,\xi)]-\lambda\|\bar{\theta}\|^{2}\right)\geq\psi_{x}^{\lambda}\left(\mathbf{d}\left(\bar{\theta},\vartheta^{\lambda}(x,\hat{\theta}_{N},\hat{r}_{N})\right)\right)

\displaystyle\geq\inf_{\theta\in{\theta}^{*}+\bar{\delta}\mathbb{B}_{\infty}}\psi_{x}^{\lambda}\left(\mathbf{d}\left(\theta,\vartheta^{\lambda}(x,\hat{\theta}_{N},\hat{r}_{N})\right)\right)=\psi_{x}^{\lambda}\left(\mathbf{d}\left({\theta}^{*},\vartheta^{\lambda}(x,\hat{\theta}_{N},\hat{r}_{N})+\bar{\delta}\mathbb{B}_{\infty}\right)\right),

where the last inequality follows from the monotonicity of
\psi_{x}^{\lambda}
. This implies

\displaystyle\mathbf{d}\left({\theta}^{*},\vartheta^{\lambda}(x,\hat{\theta}_{N},\hat{r}_{N})\right)

\displaystyle\leq\bar{\delta}+\mathbf{d}\left({\theta}^{*},\vartheta^{\lambda}(x,\hat{\theta}_{N},\hat{r}_{N})+\bar{\delta}\mathbb{B}_{\infty}\right)

\displaystyle\leq\bar{\delta}+(\psi_{x}^{\lambda})^{-1}\left(2(nM+2\lambda)\bar{\delta}\right)\leq\hat{L}\bar{\delta}+(\psi_{x}^{\lambda})^{-1}(2\hat{L}\bar{\delta})=\Psi_{x}^{\lambda}(\hat{L}\bar{\delta})

where
\hat{L}:=\max\left\{1,nM+2\lambda\right\}
.
Thus we have
\vartheta^{\lambda}(x,\tilde{\theta}_{N},\tilde{r}_{N})\subseteq\vartheta^{\lambda}(x,\hat{\theta}_{N},\hat{r}_{N})+\Psi_{x}^{\lambda}\left(\hat{L}\mathbb{D}(\tilde{\Theta}_{N},\hat{\Theta}_{N})\right)\mathbb{B}_{\infty}
.
Since
\Psi_{x}^{\lambda}
is monotonically increasing, we further conclude that

\displaystyle\vartheta^{\lambda}(x,\hat{\theta}_{N},\hat{r}_{N})+\Psi_{x}^{\lambda}\left(\hat{L}\mathbb{D}(\tilde{\Theta}_{N},\hat{\Theta}_{N})\right)\mathbb{B}_{\infty}\subseteq\vartheta^{\lambda}(x,\hat{\theta}_{N},\hat{r}_{N})+\Psi_{x}^{\lambda}\left(\hat{L}(\|\tilde{\theta}_{N}-\hat{\theta}_{N}\|_{\infty}+\left|\tilde{r}_{N}-\hat{r}_{N}\right|)\right)\mathbb{B}_{\infty}.

Thus, the theorem follows.

\hfill\blacksquare

Remark 3
.

Suppose that there exist
p\geq 1
and

Then for each

Under these conditions, the function

Remark 4

When
\lambda>0
, the regularized objective function

is strictly concave. Near the unique maximizer

, a second-order expansion gives:

\nu^{\lambda}(x,\hat{\theta}_{N},\hat{r}_{N})-\left(\sum_{j=1}^{n}\theta_{j}\mathbb{E}_{Q_{j}}[\varphi(x,\xi)]-\lambda\|\theta\|^{2}\right)\geq\lambda\|\theta-\theta^{*}\|^{2}\geq c\lambda\|\theta-\theta^{*}\|_{\infty}^{2}

Thus, the growth function satisfies

For
\lambda=0

becomes linear in

\psi_{\theta^{c},r^{c}}^{\lambda}(\tau)\leq\mathbf{d}\left(0,\sum_{j=1}^{n}\theta^{*}_{j}\mathbb{E}_{Q_{j}}[\Phi\left(x^{*},\xi\right)]+\mathcal{N}_{\mathcal{X}}\left(x^{*}\right)\right)\leq\psi_{\theta^{c},r^{c}}^{\lambda}(\tau)-\frac{\delta}{2},

To establish part (ii), note that for any
\epsilon>0

N_{0}
such that for all

By the definition of
(\psi^{\lambda}_{\hat{\theta}_{N},\hat{r}_{N}})^{-1}
and
(\psi_{\theta^{c},r^{c}}^{\lambda})^{-1}
, we have that

(\psi_{\theta^{c},r^{c}}^{\lambda})^{-1}(t+\epsilon)\geq(\psi^{\lambda}_{\hat{\theta}_{N},\hat{r}_{N}})^{-1}(t).

Since
\epsilon
is arbitrary, we conclude that

\limsup_{N\to\infty}(\psi_{\hat{\theta}_{N},\hat{r}_{N}}^{\lambda})^{-1}(t)\leq(\psi_{\theta^{c},r^{c}}^{\lambda})^{-1}(t),

which completes the proof.
\hfill\blacksquare

Theorem 8

.

Lemma 9

Proof
.
To prove (i), let

\epsilon>0

\psi_{\theta^{c},r^{c}}^{\lambda}
, there exist

\theta^{*}\in\vartheta^{\lambda}\left(x^{*},\theta^{c},r^{c}\right)

Proposition 3
.

Assume that for each
j=1,\ldots,n
, (i) there exists
\beta\in[0,1)
such that for each
i=1,2
and all

F

is monotone and Lipschitz continuous on

Proof
.
For each
x\in\mathcal{X}_{1}\times\mathcal{X}_{2}
and direction

\displaystyle\limsup_{t\downarrow 0}q(t)

f_{\lambda}

\|g_{j}(x^{i})\|\leq\frac{\bar{\sigma}_{j}}{(1-\beta)}=:L_{\varphi,j},\ \|\nabla g_{j}(x^{i})\|\leq\mathbb{E}_{Q_{j}}\left[\frac{\|\xi\|^{2}}{(1-\beta)^{2}}\right]\leq\frac{\bar{\sigma}_{j}^{2}}{(1-\beta)^{2}}=:L_{g,j}.

\sigma_{e,1}^{2}=2\times 10^{-5}
,

\sigma_{e,2}^{2}=1\times 10^{-5}
, and

\sigma_{e,3}^{2}=0.5\times 10^{-5}
, reflecting higher asset-specific risk in downturns and lower risk in upturns. The mixture weight is set to
\theta^{c}=(0.5,0.25,0.25)
. Here, we set
\kappa=0.1
,
\lambda=0.01
and
r^{c}=0
to model a risk-neutral decision-maker.

We generate a large dataset
\{\xi^{s}\}_{s=1}^{N_{\rm sim}}
with
N_{\rm sim}=10^{6}
to simulate the true equilibrium solution
x^{c}
as a benchmark. For comparison, we compute the approximated solution
x^{*}
by four data-driven methods, SVI with sample average approximation (SAA) in
[
51
]
, DRVI problem with
\ell_{1}
norm and modified
\chi^{2}
ambiguity sets and our Bayesian method respectively, on training samples with size
N\in\{20,50,500,1000,3000\}
. All ambiguity sets are constructed under the same confidence level
\alpha=0.05
.
Each method is run over 100 independent trials. For each trial, to quantify the accuracy of the solution
x^{*}
, we compute the residual distance:
d(x^{*})=\|x^{*}-x^{c}\|
and the squared relative utility error
[
50
]
:

e(x^{*})=\left(\frac{U(x^{*})-U(x^{c})}{U(x^{c})}\right)^{2},\text{ where }U(x)=\frac{1}{N_{\rm sim}}\sum_{s=1}^{N_{\rm sim}}-\log\bigl(1+{\xi^{s}}^{\top}x^{1}\bigr)+\frac{\kappa}{2}\|x^{1}-x^{2}\|^{2}.

Here, since the game is symmetric across the two accounts, we focus on account 1’s utility without loss of generality.
Furthermore, to quantify out-of-sample performance, we generate a fixed test set
\{\xi^{t}\}_{t=1}^{N_{\rm test}}
with
N_{\rm test}=5000
. Based on the single-period returns
\{R_{t}={\xi^{t}}^{\top}x^{1*}\}_{t=1}^{N_{\rm test}}
, we further assess the out-of-sample performance of the optimal portfolio with additional metrics in
[
6
]
—Sharpe ratio (measuring return per unit of total risk) and risk-adjusted return on capital (RAROC; measuring return per unit of tail risk) at
10\%
tail level.
Specifically, let the out-of-sample mean and standard deviation of the returns be

\bar{R}=\frac{1}{N_{\rm test}}\sum_{t=1}^{N_{\rm test}}R_{t},\qquad\hat{\sigma}_{R}=\sqrt{\frac{1}{N_{\rm test}-1}\sum_{t=1}^{N_{\rm test}}\bigl(R_{t}-\bar{R}\bigr)^{2}}.

The Sharpe ratio is then

\widehat{\mathrm{SR}}=\frac{\bar{R}}{\hat{\sigma}_{R}}.

For the RAROC at
10\%
tail level, define the losses
l_{t}:=-R_{t}
.
Let the empirical value-at-risk be
\widehat{\mathrm{VaR}}_{10\%}
as the
10\%
-quantile of
\{l_{t}\}_{t=1}^{N_{\rm test}}
, and let the empirical expected shortfall of losses be

\widehat{\mathrm{ES}}_{10\%}=\frac{1}{\#\{t:l_{t}\geq\widehat{\mathrm{VaR}}_{10\%}\}}\sum_{t:l_{t}\geq\widehat{\mathrm{VaR}}_{10\%}}l_{t},

where
\#\{\cdot\}
denotes the cardinality (number of elements) of a set.
Then the RAROC at
10\%
tail level is calculated as

\widehat{\mathrm{R}}_{10\%}=\frac{\bar{R}}{\widehat{\mathrm{ES}}_{10\%}}.

The observations derived from Tables
1
-
4
lead to the following conclusions.

•

Consistency and estimation accuracy.

Across all sample sizes, the Bayesian DRVI attains the smallest mean residual distances to the benchmark
x^{c}
and the lowest squared relative utility errors (Tables
1
-
2
), demonstrating its superior estimation accuracy. Moreover, Table
1
confirms that the Bayesian solutions improve monotonically with
N
, consistent with the theoretical convergence results. In contrast, the
\ell_{1}
norm method is outperformed by both Bayesian and SAA methods, while the modified
\chi^{2}
scheme exhibits a persistent bias, indicative of over-conservatism that does not vanish with additional data.

•

Stability and variance reduction.

Tables
1
-
2
show that the Bayesian method maintains low variance across sample sizes, particularly for large
N
, and achieves the lowest variance among all methods for
N=3000
.
While the modified
\chi^{2}
approach also displays small variances, this is accompanied by pronounced bias, reflecting variance reduction driven by excessive pessimism rather than genuine robustness. In contrast, among the essentially unbiased approaches (
\ell_{1}
norm, Bayesian and SAA), the Bayesian method consistently attains both the lowest mean errors and the smallest variances, highlighting its superior stability and reliability in finite samples.

•

Risk-return performance.

The SAA and Bayesian methods form a top tier of performance for
N\geq 500
, achieving nearly identical and superior results in both the Sharpe ratio and RAROC at
10\%
tail level (Tables
3
-
4
). In smaller samples (
N=20,50
), SAA may achieve a slightly higher mean performance, but the Bayesian method typically exhibits smaller variance, confirming that it provides more stable risk-return trade-offs across different data realizations.
The inherent conservatism of the
\ell_{1}
and modified
\chi^{2}
methods leads to suboptimal decisions, resulting in consistently lower performance.
This demonstrates the substantial opportunity cost of using overly pessimistic ambiguity sets, while the Bayesian approach delivers excellent risk-adjusted and tail-controlled returns with improved reliability.

In summary, the proposed Bayesian DRVI method effectively balances the trade-off between robustness and optimality. It mitigates the over-conservatism inherent in traditional DRVI models, while simultaneously overcoming the instability of the SAA approach, especially in limited data scenarios. Consequently, it provides robust and accurate decisions for the multi-portfolio Nash equilibrium problem across all sample sizes.

6
Concluding remarks.

Building on the DRVI framework in
[
46
]
, we consider a Bayesian formulation (

B
c

) which captures distributional uncertainty through the parametric ambiguity set
\Theta^{c}
.
To ensure tractability, we construct a data-driven ambiguity set with posterior coverage guarantees and introduce the regularized Bayesian DRVI model (

B
N

λ

). Under mild assumptions, we prove the existence of solutions for both Bayesian DRVI (

B
N

) and its regularized counterpart (

B
N

λ

).
We further establish the convergence of the solutions of (

B
N

λ

) to a solution of the problem (

B
c

) as the sample size grows and the regularization vanishes.
Moreover, as
\lambda\downarrow 0
, the regularized maximizer of the lower-level problem in (

B
N

λ

) converges to the least-norm maximizer of the lower-level problem in (

B
N

).
To address practical finite-sample and contamination effects, we derive finite-sample guarantees for the optimal solutions of (

B
N

λ

) and quantify the solution stability between this formulation and its perturbed variant (

~
B

N

λ

).
The effectiveness of the proposed framework is demonstrated through its application to a distributionally robust multi-portfolio Nash equilibrium problem. By integrating Bayesian learning with DRVI, our framework provides a robust and practical foundation for decision-making under uncertainty.

It is natural to extend the Bayesian DRVI framework to stochastic generalized equations. While statistical robustness results for stochastic generalized equations have been established in
[
19
]
, analogous theoretical guarantees for the Bayesian DRVI framework remain an open question. We leave these extensions to future work.
