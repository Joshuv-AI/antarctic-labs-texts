Efficacy and safety of levosimendan in patients with ST‐segment elevation myocardial infarction undergoing primary percutaneous coronary intervention: The LEVOCEST trial
Authors: Francisco Bosa Ojeda, Corabel Méndez Vargas, Juan Lacalzada Almeida, María M Izquierdo Gómez, Alejandro Jiménez Sosa, Consuelo Rodríguez Jiménez, Alejandro Sánchez‐Grande Flecha, Marta Bosa Santana, Geoffrey Yanes Bowden
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundPrimary angioplasty is the standard procedure for patients with ST‐segment elevation myocardial infarction (STEMI). However, myocardial reperfusion results in additional cell damage. Levosimendan, due to its pleiotropic effects, may be a therapeutic alternative to prevent this damage. The objective of this study was to evaluate whether this drug can reduce infarct size in patients with STEMI.MethodsPatients were randomized to receive a 24‐h infusion of either levosimendan (0.1 μg/kg/min) or placebo after the primary angioplasty. The main objective was to assess the size of the infarct by cardiac resonance at 30 days and 6 months after the event. Other variables such as left ventricular ejection fraction (LVEF) and adverse ventricular remodeling (AVR) were assessed by speckle‐tracking echocardiography and magnetic resonance. Major adverse cardiovascular events (MACE) were also collected.Results157 patients were analysed (levosimendan, n = 79; placebo, n = 78). We found that after 6 months, patients treated with levosimendan had a greater reduction in infarct size (13.19% ± 9.5% vs.11.79% ± 9%, p = 0.001), compared with those in the placebo group (13.35% ± 7.1% vs. 13.43% ± 7.8%, p = 0.38). There were no significant differences in MACE between both groups.ConclusionsLevosimendan is a safe and effective therapeutic option for reducing infarct size in patients with STEMI.Keywords: levosimendan, myocardial infarction, primary angioplasty, reperfusion injury, ST‐segment elevation

INTRODUCTION

Acute coronary syndrome is a high‐prevalence disease in our environment and is associated with high mortality and morbidity. 1 In patients with STEMI, coronary occlusion causes mitochondrial abnormalities and alteration of the sarcolemma, leading to myocardiocyte necrosis. 2 In this case, treatment of the culprit lesion by primary percutaneous coronary intervention (PCI) is the therapeutic strategy of choice. Paradoxically, myocardial reperfusion causes additional damage. 3 Restoration of blood flow generates reactive oxygen and nitrogen species, causing mitochondrial dysfunction and reduced adenosine triphosphate (ATP) formation, resulting in lactate accumulation and a consequent decrease in intracellular pH, altering ionic kinetics. 3 , 4 In addition, increased proteolytic activity during reperfusion distorts myocardial cell architecture and physiology. 5 Therefore, it is necessary to limit infarct size and thus recover contractile function.

Different pharmacological strategies have been shown to have cardioprotective effects. 6 , 7 , 8 , 9 The results of their use in myocardial infarction in animal models have been promising; however, those obtained in humans have been disappointing. 6 , 10

One of the therapeutic alternatives showing promising clinical results is levosimendan, a calcium channel sensitizer and potassium channel opener; it increases calcium sensitivity of contractile proteins by binding to and stabilizing troponin C in myocardiocytes, leading to cyclic adenosine monophosphate (cAMP)‐independent inotropic effects, opens potassium channels (K‐ATP channels), causing vasodilation in smooth muscle cells, and activates K‐ATP channels in cardiac mitochondria, preventing cell damage due to ischemia‐reperfusion. 11 , 12 In addition, levosimendan exerts a series of pleiotropic properties, including anti‐inflammatory, antioxidant, and antiapoptotic effects, 13 , 14 and is also a safe therapeutic option as demonstrated by the different studies carried out in the context of heart failure with repeated infusions of the drug. 15 , 16 Levosimendan has been used in the context of ischemic heart disease. For instance, a preclinical study has shown that the use of levosimendan in animal models with myocardial infarction decreased infarct size. 17 Studies in patients with STEMI have shown that levosimendan improves stunned myocardial function, 18 hemodynamics and coronary flow reserve, 19 myocardial contractility, 20 and global cardiac function. 21

Considering that cardioprotection, by means of adjuvant treatments during PCI, seems an appropriate approach to reduce infarct size in STEMI patients, and that randomized clinical trials with levosimendan in this setting are scarce, the main objective of our study was to assess whether the myocardium can recover totally or partially after levosimendan infusion in the first 24 h after primary PCI in STEMI patients. In addition, as a secondary objective we analyzed LVEF, AVR, and MACE.

METHODS

Study design

The LEVOCEST study is a randomized, prospective, single‐center, double‐blind, placebo‐controlled clinical trial conducted at our center from November 2018 to October 2022. It was designed to evaluate the efficacy and safety of intravenous administration of levosimendan in the first 24 h after primary PCI in patients with STEMI.

The study was approved by the Ethics Committee of the Hospital Universitario de Canarias and the corresponding health authorities (Agencia Española del Medicamento y Productos Sanitarios, Ministerio de Salud) and developed according to the guidelines of the Declaration of Helsinki.

Patient characteristics

Patients of both sexes aged 18 to 85, with symptoms of STEMI of more than 30 min and less than 12 h of evolution, with ST‐segment elevation ascertained on electrocardiogram, were included. All patients who participated in the study signed the informed consent form.

The main exclusion criteria were cardiogenic shock, previous infarction and the absence of segmental contractility disorders on left ventriculography (Table S1).

Patients were randomized 1:1 to the treatment (levosimendan) or control (placebo) group by means of a 3‐digit code, after inclusion in the study and after primary PCI. This code identified the levosimendan or placebo ampoules, whose contents were indistinguishable, as they had the same color and size.

On arrival at the coronary care unit, the patient received a 24‐h infusion of levosimendan (0.1 μg/kg/min) or placebo. The infusion dose could be increased to 0.2 μg/kg/min if well tolerated or reduced to 0.05 μg/kg/min in case of clinical intolerance. To define clinical intolerance, we used data obtained from large clinical trials that identified side effects such as hypotension (BP < 80 mmHg), ventricular tachycardia, and headache. 22 , 23 , 24

Primary and secondary study variables

The primary variable of the study was the assessment of total or partial myocardial recovery following STEMI and after receiving levosimendan infusion after primary PCI. Infarct size (percentage of infarcted myocardium/percentage of left ventricle), was estimated by cardiac magnetic resonance (CMR) and quantified by late gadolinium enhancement at 30 days and at 6 months in both groups.

Secondary variables were cardiac troponin levels and the N‐terminal pro‐B‐type natriuretic peptide (NT‐proBNP) after PCI.

Left ventricular end‐diastolic volume and end‐systolic volume (LVEDV and LVESV), left ventricular (LV) mass and LVEF were also assessed by CMR at 30 days and 6 months after infarction. Additionally, transthoracic echocardiography was used to evaluate AVR parameters and speckle‐tracking echocardiography (STE) for LV segmental contractility disorders at 72 h and 6 months.

MACE (cardiovascular death, reinfarction or stroke, heart failure, and need for new revascularization of the culprit vessel) were collected at 6 months.

Transthoracic echocardiography—Speckle tracking

Four‐, two‐, and three‐chamber STE images were obtained. Longitudinal strain (LS) of LV segments was measured using QLAB Advanced Tissue Motion Quantification equipment (Phillips) equipped with STE analysis software. The software generated strain curves for each LV segment from which we estimated the maximum longitudinal systolic strain (MLSS) in the 16 segments of the three apical views. The global longitudinal strain (GLS) was calculated as the average of the observed segmental values of the MLSS.

Cardiac magnetic resonance

All images were obtained on 1.5 T equipment. LV short‐axis views were used to quantify ventricular volumes and LVEF. Quantitative analysis of ventricular mass, LVEDV and LVESV, and LVEF were determined using manual planimetry of the endocardial and epicardial edges. Quantification of infarcted myocardium was performed by acquisition of late gadolinium enhancement sequences. The infarct mass was measured in grams according to the extent of late enhancement using specific software (CardiacVX from GE).

Sample size determination

The sample size calculation was based on known data from previous studies that evaluated the cardioprotective effect of different molecules in STEMI. 7 , 9 Additionally, according to a previous study conducted at our center, 25 85% of patients in a cohort of 250 who were admitted with STEMI exhibited segmental abnormalities in left ventricular contractility 4 months after the acute episode. Therefore, to demonstrate that levosimendan could potentially reduce this percentage to at least 65%, considering a statistical power of 80%, a confidence level of 95%, and assuming a 10% loss of subjects, it was estimated that a total of 184 patients needed to be included.

Statistical analysis

Categorical variables were expressed as frequencies and percentages. Quantitative variables were expressed as mean, standard deviation (SD), and median (interquartile ranges). Percentages were compared with the Chi‐square or Fisher's exact test as appropriate. Quantitative and ordinal variables were analyzed by analysis of variance of repeated measures, Student's t‐test, Mann–Whitney U test or Wilcoxon test, as appropriate. Values of p < 0.05 were considered significant. All analyses were performed using SPSS 25.0 software (IBM; NY Corp.).

RESULTS

Patient characteristics

A total of 187 patients were included in the study and were randomized to receive levosimendan (N = 94) or placebo (N = 93). Fifteen patients were excluded from each group, finally analyzing 79 and 78 patients in the treatment and control groups, respectively (Figure 1).

Diagram showing the flowchart of patient recruitment, participants excluded in each group, the reasons, and patients included in each treatment group.

The analysis showed no statistically significant differences between the two treatment groups in terms of mean age of the patients, sex, risk factors, and time until opening of the artery, nor were there differences in the levels of cardiac troponin (peak value and 24 h after drug infusion) and NT‐proBNP. Significant differences were found regarding the culprit artery, with the right coronary artery being more frequent in the control group (56.4% vs. 38%; p = 0.01) and the left anterior descending artery in the levosimendan group (49.4% vs. 26.9%; p = 0.01). No significant differences were observed in TIMI flow or presence of collateral circulation (Table 1).

Levosimendan efficacy

Cardiac magnetic resonance results

Infarct size

An interaction effect was found between the drug and the measurement period (p = 0.04). Between 30 days and 6 months, infarct size (percentage of infarcted myocardium/percentage of left ventricle) was reduced in the levosimendan group (13.19% ± 9.5% vs.11.79% ± 9%, p = 0.001). No significant change was observed in the placebo group (13.35% ± 7.1% vs. 13.43% ± 7.8%, p = 0.38), (Figure 2). Infarct size (in grams [g]) was reduced in the levosimendan group (16.3 ± 12.5 g vs. 14.8 ± 12.1 g, p = 0.016). Also, no significant change was observed in the placebo group (17.5 ± 10.4 g vs. 17.05 ± 11 g, p = 0.28).

Evaluation of the infarct size (percentage of infarcted myocardium/percentage of left ventricle) by cardiac magnetic resonance parameters in both treatment groups at 30 days and 6 months.

Echocardiographic results

The analysis 72 h after the event revealed that patients treated with levosimendan had a better LVEF than those in the placebo group (60% ± 11.9% vs. 56% ± 9.3%; p = 0.06), although it did not reach statistical significance. In addition, these patients showed a significantly lower LVEDV (79.4 ± 21.9 vs. 90.0 ± 26.4 mL; p = 0.01). Concerning the wall motion score index (WMSI) and GLS, no differences were observed between the two groups at admission and at 6 months (Table 3).

Levosimendan safety

No significant differences were observed between groups in mortality (2.2% vs. 0%, p = 0.15) or in episodes of acute heart failure (3.2% vs. 1.1%, p = 0.31). DISCUSSION

Our study shows that the administration of levosimendan in the first 24 h after primary PCI reduces the size of the contused myocardium in patients with STEMI at 6 months. Moreover, this treatment strategy is safe as no increase in the number of MACE was observed.

Opening the culprit artery during STEMI limits infarct size and improves patient prognosis. 26 However, the reduction of reperfusion damage is still a challenge. 6 In the search for a molecule with cardioprotective effects during STEMI, levosimendan appears as a promising candidate. Previous studies in animal models showed that the administration of this substance increased the sensitivity of myofilaments to calcium, improving contractile function in the myocardium and ultimately reducing infarct size and AVR. 27 , 28 , 29 More recent studies, also in animal models, confirmed these findings, giving a cardioprotective role to levosimendan, 30 , 31 which could inhibit myocardial apoptosis. 32

Our results show that levosimendan administered after primary PCI in patients with STEMI significantly reduced infarct size at 6 months compared to the control group. Previous studies with different cardioprotective molecules showed a reduction in infarct size during the first weeks after angioplasty. 7 , 8 , 9 In our study, we observed this reduction 6 months after the acute episode, which would demonstrate that the development of cardiac fibrosis, and specifically interstitial fibrosis, is a complex, heterogeneous, and potentially reversible process that can last for months. Therefore, levosimendan, through its known anti‐inflammatory, antiapoptotic, and vasodilator effects, together with its long half‐life, would act in the different phases of fibrosis development and thus contribute to the reduction of the final size of the infarct. In this sense, other studies have also shown that interstitial fibrosis can be reversible when the disease that causes it is specifically treated. 33 , 34

Although the use of levosimendan in our patients produced a modest reduction in infarct size, we did not find statistically significant differences in troponin levels at 24 h, nor in the estimation of LVEF or in AVR parameters, presumably because of this modest reduction and the small sample size. However, a previous study with another cardioprotective molecule 9 did show significant differences in these parameters. We believe that this is explained by the fact that this study was performed exclusively in patients with anterior infarctions, with a larger myocardial territory at risk, and with ischemia times of less than 6 h. Our sample included infarcts of any location and up to 12 h from symptom onset, making the cardioprotective effect of our treatment strategy less evident. Furthermore, it should be noted that the control group experienced more frequent inferior infarctions, while the levosimendan group had a higher incidence of anterior infarctions, indicating that the myocardium at risk differs between the two populations. This discrepancy suggests that the groups may not be entirely comparable when evaluating the potential cardioprotective benefit of the drug. Nonetheless, a greater reduction in infarct size was observed in the levosimendan group. In contrast, other studies with a similar design and comparable inclusion criteria using different drugs, such as metoprolol or cyclosporine, have shown no significant changes between the treated and control groups, nor any differences in infarct size or AVR parameters. 35 , 36

On admission, patients treated with levosimendan had a better LVEF and significantly lower LVEDV measured by STE compared to those in the control group. These findings should be put in the context of LV loading conditions in the acute phase of infarction derived from the inodilator properties of levosimendan, not maintained at 6 months. 12 , 13 When assessing the effects of the drug on AVR by CMR, we found no significant differences between the two groups at 30 days and at 6 months.

Studies in humans regarding treatment with levosimendan in the setting of ischemic heart disease are scarce. Nevertheless, they have shown beneficial results. 20 , 21 , 25 , 26 Sonntag et al. studied the role of levosimendan in 24 patients with acute coronary syndrome and found that, compared to controls, patients treated with levosimendan after angioplasty showed an improvement in LVEF and a decrease in LVEDV and LVESV. 18 In our study we did not observe an increase in LVEF, when assessed 30 days and 6 months after levosimendan administration. However, these authors recorded LVEF values 10 min after angioplasty and 20 min after drug administration. Along the same lines, a study by De Luca et al. in 26 patients with acute myocardial infarction and left ventricular dysfunction showed that levosimendan administration improved both hemodynamics and coronary flow reserve compared to placebo. 19 In 2013, Husebye et al. published the LEAF trial, with a design similar to ours, in which they demonstrated that administration of levosimendan after PCI in patients with STEMI improved LV contractility. The authors found a significantly greater improvement in WMSI after 5 days in patients treated with levosimendan compared to the placebo group, but these differences were not maintained when analyzed on Day 42. 20 A more recent study evaluated the effects of preconditioning with levosimendan compared to dobutamine on AVR in 258 patients with acute myocardial infarction and found that preconditioning with levosimendan could protect cardiac function. The authors demonstrated that patients pretreated with levosimendan had lower levels of parameters such as troponin T and NT pro‐BNP after treatment, due to the regulatory properties of levosimendan on cytokines and neuroendocrine response, antagonizing the oxidative stress response, decreasing myocardial apoptosis, and repairing myocardial cell damage. 21 Our study cannot be directly compared with this one, since the design of both is different, as they only included patients with left ventricular dysfunction. However, we can speculate that pretreatment with levosimendan before primary PCI would result in less reperfusion injury and a greater reduction in infarct size as quantified by CMR, which would ultimately positively impact AVR and LVEF. This could support the use of this drug as early as possible in the setting of STEMI undergoing PCI.

It is important to note that in none of the studies performed with levosimendan 18 , 19 , 20 , 21 the infarct size was objectively assessed by CMR. Therefore, we believe that the LEVOCEST trial is the first study to demonstrate that the administration of levosimendan after PCI has a cardioprotective effect and reduces infarct size.

On the other hand, the aforementioned studies were performed in a small number of patients and did not provide data on the safety of levosimendan in acute myocardial infarction, except for Husebye et al. Our results are in line with the latter, in which the authors demonstrated that treatment with levosimendan was a safe therapeutic option, since they reported higher mortality in the control group. 20 We noticed that randomized patients in the placebo group had more MACE in the form of death and heart failure than the levosimendan group, although not significantly. Therefore, in our patients, administering levosimendan after primary PCI was a safe therapeutic option, regardless of LVEF, duration of ischemia, culprit artery, size, or location of the infarct. Considering the above, we believe that levosimendan could be used in STEMI to improve the patient's cardiac function and contribute to the reduction of infarct size.

Our study has the limitation that only one center participated in the clinical trial. However, 187 patients were included, reaching the sample size requirements despite the fact that it was conducted, in part, during the COVID‐19 pandemic.

Another possible limitation is that infarct size was measured in grams and as a percentage, and the percentage of salvaged myocardium and myocardium at risk was not measured. Due to the lack of consensus on which parameter and imaging technique is the most appropriate to use, we assessed infarct size by CMR, the reference method for this purpose, 37 giving robustness to the results obtained.

Furthermore, it cannot be ruled out that the different comorbidities of the patients or concomitant treatments (ACEI, ARB, statins…) may have influenced the response to treatment, although we did not observe significant differences between groups, except for the aforementioned culprit artery of the infarction. 6 It remains uncertain whether comorbidities may impact the efficacy of cardioprotective interventions.

Finally, although patients were followed for more than 6 months, we believe it would be interesting to record adverse events, especially MACE, at 2 and 5 years after drug administration.

CONCLUSIONS

Our results show that intravenous administration of levosimendan can be a safe therapeutic option to reduce infarct size for patients with STEMI regardless of their LVEF and hemodynamic status. Although multicenter, randomized studies are needed to confirm our results, the LEVOCEST clinical trial could lay the groundwork for a new indication for the drug, establishing a new cardioprotective strategy for disease management in routine clinical practice and, ultimately, changing the paradigm of acute myocardial infarction treatment.
