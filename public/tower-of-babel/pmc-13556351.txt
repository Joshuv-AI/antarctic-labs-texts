Wear-resistant, moldable mineral hydroplastics via nonsolvent-induced phase separation for adaptive architectural applications
Authors: Junqing Chen, Shanshan Wang, Erlantz Lizundia, Le Yu, Yuying Wu, Jie Zhou, Sen Wang, Jing Huang, Fengze Ding, Ziyang Lu, Lu Chen, Wengen Ouyang, Dingfeng Xu, Jun You, Chaoji Chen
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
The development of sustainable plastic alternatives derived from natural components, such as biopolymers and minerals, represents a promising strategy to mitigate the escalating problem of plastic pollution. Here, by employing a nonsolvent-induced phase separation (NIPS) strategy, a hydro-processable mineral-dominated structural material, called “mineral hydroplastic” (M-Hydroplastic), is developed. High-mineral-content (up to 75 wt %) hydrogels are fabricated through in situ polymerization of specific monomers and shaped under mild conditions through polymer chain rearrangement triggered by nonsolvent exposure. Further pressing-assisted desolvation optimizes the orientation of mineral sheets, yielding hydro-processable high-mineral-content plastics with combined features of mineral (flexural strength, 90.6 MPa; hardness, 0.23 GPa; and flame retardancy) and plastic (low density of ∼1.5 g cm−3 and facile moldability). Combined experimental and computational analyses reveal that strong intercomponent hydrogen bonding and a nacre-like micro-structure underpin the material’s exceptional mechanical performance. This versatile strategy is applicable to various minerals, producing a family of robust hydroplastics with tunable optical, thermal, and radiative properties. Such adaptability enables the design of multifunctional, flame-retardant materials for multi-scenario energy-efficient building applications. This work reconciles ceramic-like mechanical properties with polymer-like processability, providing crucial insights into designing next-generation plastic alternatives for engineering applications.

Introduction

Plastics are omnipresent in modern life due to their exceptional combination of light weight, cost-effectiveness, and versatile processability. Nevertheless, inherent limitations, including mechanical fragility, flammability hazards, and environmental persistence, continue to restrict their advanced application.1,2 Developing high-performance alternatives to conventional plastics from natural resources is an attractive and sustainable approach. Biomass-derived materials, such as cellulose, starch, chitin, and their derivatives, have attracted increasing attention for fabricating high-value-added bioplastics.3,4,5,6,7,8 However, current efforts largely center on films or aerogels, while the fabrication of high-performance bulk materials typically requires complex processing. Moreover, the inherent flammability of biomass remains a key challenge, demanding advanced flame-retardant materials to mitigate fire hazards.

Natural minerals, characterized by inherent mechanical robustness and flame retardancy, are abundant and serve as ideal matrices for constructing bulk materials as alternatives to plastics.9,10,11 However, their practical application as sustainable plastic substitutes is significantly hindered by critical processing challenges, notably energy-intensive fabrication procedures, such as thermal sintering at temperatures exceeding 1,000°C.12 A notable paradigm shift is exemplified by biomimetic nacre-like “brick-and-mortar” architectures, where organic mortars (nanocellulose) synergize with inorganic bricks (mica) to produce fully bio-derived structural materials with strength and toughness rivaling those of engineering plastics.13 Despite significant advancements in biomimetic designs for fabricating plastic substitutes from natural resources, achieving scalable production of structurally homogeneous bulk composites remains technically challenging, particularly in terms of effectively loading and bonding high-content mineral sheets.14 Moreover, conventional biomimetic materials often trade processability for mechanical strength, limiting their ability to be reshaped into complex forms. The combined constraints of fabrication complexity and poor formability continue to hinder their versatility and application in customized geometries.

Phase separation, a ubiquitous phenomenon in nature, triggered by variations in chemical composition, solvent properties, and external stimuli (e.g., temperature), has been extensively leveraged to design high-performance and multifunctional materials.15,16 Among various strategies, nonsolvent-induced phase separation (NIPS) stands out for its simplicity, mild conditions, and operational ease, offering exceptional flexibility in material shaping. For example, Liu et al. reported a wood-based composite with outstanding mechanical strength and tunable formability via NIPS, highlighting its considerable promise for advanced material fabrication.17 Additionally, solvent-induced shaping during phase separation facilitates stronger intercomponent interactions through conformational rearrangement, resulting in macroscopically enhanced mechanical properties.18,19 Notably, this phase separation is reversible, allowing the composite to be molded into different shapes and even recycled by changing the solvent environment, bypassing the constraints of traditional thermal molding methods.

Here, coupling phase-separated polymers with minerals provides a route distinct from conventional nacre-mimetic silicate-polymer composites based on static stacking and from water-regulated hydroplastic processing, which mainly tunes softness through water content. NIPS serves as a structural regulator, reorganizing the network and strengthening polymer mineral interfaces, enabling a tunable processing window for molding high mineral content composites and forming mechanically robust bulk materials. On this basis, we demonstrate an attractive strategy called “NIPS induced multiscale enhancement” to achieve high-mineral-content mineral hydroplastic (M-Hydroplastic) with combined features of mineral (mechanically strong and flame retardant) and plastic (light weight and facile moldability) (Figure 1A). Specifically, by employing natural mica as a structural “brick,” polyacrylamide (PAM) as the binding “mortar,” and cellulose nanofibers (CNFs) as reinforcing “glue,” a hydrogel was synthesized through in situ polymerization and then transformed into a rigid structural material upon ethanol (EtOH) stimulation. This strategy simultaneously achieved synergistic enhancement at the macroscale (brick-and-mortar structure), microscale and nanoscale (mica skeleton and CNF bridging), and molecular scale (hydrogen-bonding interaction and PAM conformational rearrangement). This multiscale design paradigm endows the material with mechanical properties comparable to engineering plastics while significantly outperforming conventional engineering plastics in wear resistance and flame retardancy. More intriguingly, by modulating the internal water content and hydration state, M-Hydroplastic undergoes rapid dehydration and hardening stages, transitioning from a soft gel to a rubber-like state (the critical point for deformation molding) and ultimately to a rigid solid exhibiting tunable stiffness and deformability index (DI; reflecting the material’s deformation capacity) (Figure 1B; Table 1). This reversible, phase separation-driven plasticity enables M-Hydroplastic to be gently molded into diverse geometries (Figure 1C). Moreover, the end-of-life M-Hydroplastic with unique hydro-processability could be easily recycled by a green and energy-efficient solution reshaping process (Figure S1). Furthermore, this gelation-solvent hardening methodology facilitates scalable production of M-Hydroplastics with adjustable mineral content (30%–75%) (Figure 1D). These M-Hydroplastics also demonstrate excellent macroscopic moldability and reprocessability (Figure 1E), enhancing their applicability across various scenarios. Importantly, the proposed strategy demonstrates broad material compatibility, successfully extending to various layered silicate minerals for constructing high-mineral-content M-Hydroplastics (Figure 1F), providing a versatile platform for developing high-performance and recyclable plastic alternatives. Moreover, the resulting M-Hydroplastics exhibit tunable optical, thermal, and radiative properties through compositional and structural modulation, enabling diverse functionalities such as flame retardancy, load-bearing capability, and energy-saving performance. Importantly, they also show a low climate change potential of 4.82 kg CO2 eq. kg−1, highlighting its decarbonization potential. Materials and methods

Materials

Natural mica was obtained from Anhui Gerui (Chuzhou, Anhui, China). Acrylamide (AM; 99%), N,N′-methylenebisacrylamide (MBAA; 99%), and ammonium persulfate (APS; 98%) were purchased from Aladdin Industrial. 2,2,6,6-tetramethylpiperidine-1-oxyl-oxidized cellulose nanofibrils (TEMPO-CNFs) was provided by Dingfeng Xu. Anhydrous EtOH was purchased from Chengdu Colon Chemical. Natural illite was obtained from Aokai Mineral Products (Shijiazhuang, Hebei, China). Natural kaolin was purchased from Hengyuan New Material (Zhengzhou, Henan, China). Natural chlorite was purchased from the Xinlei mineral powder processing plant (Shijiazhuang, Hebei, China). Natural montmorillonite was obtained from Junkai New Material (Shijiazhuang, Hebei, China). Natural vermiculite was obtained from the Liming mineral powder processing plant (Shijiazhuang, Hebei, China). Natural calcite was purchased from Hebei Hongyao Mineral Products Processing (Shijiazhuang, Hebei, China). All chemicals were not further purified. The experimental water was deionized water.

Preparation of CNF/mica dispersion

In a typical fabrication, mica, CNF (2.5% of mica mass), and deionized water were stirred at room temperature for 10 h as pretreatment to obtain CNF/mica dispersion with a solid content of 15%. Then, the dispersion was sonicated for 5 min.

Preparation of M-Hydroplastic

The CNF/mica dispersion was mixed with AM at different CNF/mica to AM mass ratios (30:70, 40:60, 50:50, 60:40, and 75:25). Afterward, APS (2% molar ratio to AM), MBAA (4% molar ratio to AM), and a certain amount of water were added, mixed evenly, and stored at 60°C for 1 h to obtain M-Hydrogel. The hydrogel was immersed in an EtOH solution until completely hardened. Finally, the M-Hydroplastic was obtained by pressing at 100 MPa and 50°C for 10 h. All data analyses were based on the M-Hydroplastic sample with 50% mica content (CNF/mica-to-AM mass ratio 50:50) unless specified otherwise.

Results and discussion

Structural evolution and NIPS mechanism of the M-Hydroplastic

Natural mineral sheets are difficult to disperse homogeneously in aqueous solution due to their high density and tend to settle and stratify. To address this issue, CNF (obtained by TEMPO oxidation) was employed. The abundant negatively charged carboxyl groups on CNF generate considerable steric hindrance and electrostatic repulsion, effectively stabilizing two-dimensional (2D) mineral sheets and ensuring their uniform dispersion in aqueous media.20 As shown in Figure S2, the CNF/mica suspension appears uniform without obvious sedimentation, indicating a stable dispersion of mica sheets in the CNF solution. Concurrently, mechanical stirring and sonication partially exfoliated the mica into thinner sheets, exposing additional active sites (Figures S3 and S4). These sites facilitated the attachment of CNF to the mica surfaces through hydrophobic interactions and hydrogen bonding between the CNF hydroxyl groups and the hydrophobic basal planes and defect edges of the mineral sheets. After uniform mixing of the CNF/mica suspension with AM followed by gelation, the PAM/CNF/mica hydrogel (M-Hydrogel) was obtained via in situ polymerization and exhibited a uniform distribution of mica sheets within the three-dimensional (3D) porous network of PAM, where mica sheets are trapped and engage in interfacial interactions with PAM and CNF (Figure 2A). Subsequently, upon exposure to EtOH, the PAM phase underwent phase separation, during which its conformational rearrangement pulled the embedded mica sheets closer together, leading to densification of the initially loose network (Figure 2B). Additionally, the mica sheets act as a rigid skeleton, providing structural support for the contraction of PAM chains during phase separation. Scanning electron microscopy (SEM) and energy dispersive X-ray spectroscopy (EDS) analyses reveal a more compact arrangement of mica sheets following phase separation, further confirming that the process induces structural densification (Figures 2C and 2D). In parallel, a large number of PAM/CNF intertwined to form a denser support network and bridges between mica sheets after phase separation, enhancing the structure at the nano/microscale. Interestingly, this densification induced by phase separation is tunable. Time-dependent Fourier transform infrared (FTIR) spectroscopy was employed to probe the interaction mechanism during phase separation (Figure S7). Prolonged phase separation led to increased exposure of N–H and C=O groups, accompanied by redshifts of their characteristic peaks (N–H, 1,617 cm−1; C=O, 1,660 cm−1), indicating enhanced hydrogen-bonding interactions.21 Spectral analysis in the 3,000–4,000 cm−1 region showed a decline in the double donor–double acceptor (DDAA) peak ratio and a rise in the single donor–single acceptor (DA) peak ratio after phase separation, both showing redshifts, suggesting reorganization and strengthening of the hydrogen-bonding network (Figure 2E). To elucidate the sequential response of functional groups during phase separation, 2D correlation spectra (COSs) were employed (Figure S8). Based on Noda’s judging rule and the correlation determination table (Table S1), the corresponding order is 1,617 cm−1 → 1,660 cm−1 (where → means prior to or earlier than), implying that the hydrogen bonds between N–H groups and water molecules are disrupted at an earlier stage of the phase separation process.22 Low-field nuclear magnetic resonance (LF-NMR) was employed to analyze changes in the hydrogen bond network during this process. As shown in Figures 2F and 2G, T21, T22, and T23 are present throughout the entire phase separation process, whereas T2b appears only after phase separation is complete, indicating the formation of a strong hydrogen bond network.23 The emergence of T2b likely arises from the formation of enclosed nanochannels between mica sheets, induced by PAM conformational rearrangement, which restricts water mobility.24 Overall, EtOH disrupts water-water hydrogen bonding, converting structural water into interfacial adsorbed water. Simultaneously, framework contraction (mica) and polymer chain rearrangement restrict water dynamics, reinforcing interfacial interactions. Meanwhile, hydrogen bonds form between the solvent and the amide groups in PAM, the hydroxylated CNF surface, and the exposed –OH or oxygen atoms on mica, collectively driving hydrogen bond reorganization within the system (Figure 2H).

Molecular dynamics (MD) simulation provides direct molecular-level support for the proposed solvent triggered reorganization.25 Relative to water, the total radius of gyration (Rg) of PAM is markedly reduced in EtOH, whereas the Rg of CNF remains essentially unchanged, indicating that the structural evolution mainly arises from rearrangement of the PAM network (Figure S9). Consistently, in EtOH the mass density profile of PAM becomes strongly enriched near the interface (close to the mica surface) and exhibits a sharper high density peak, reflecting PAM chain collapse and localized compaction under a poor solvent environment. The high degree of overlap between the density distribution areas of PAM and CNF suggests closer contact between the two components (Figure S10). Figure S11 shows that the hydrogen bond network of the system was significantly restructured after the introduction of mica. The number of hydrogen bonds between PAM and CNF increased significantly, indicating that the two formed closer molecular contact and stronger interfacial coupling under the constraint and adsorption of mica.19,26 Together, these MD results support solvent-driven polymer demixing and the resulting spatial reorganization of the network, reinforcing the mechanism proposed in this work and providing support for molecular-scale enhancement.

To further eliminate residual voids that could undermine the mechanical properties of the composite, a uniaxial pressing process was applied perpendicular to the material plane. This treatment improved alignment of the mica sheets and enhanced overall material densification. As a result, the M-Hydroplastic exhibited a more compact brick-and-mortar structure and a smoother surface morphology (Figures 2I, 2J, and S12). Notably, the phase separation and densification processes simultaneously improved the orientation of mica sheets in the material. As shown in Figures 2J and S13, the 2D small-angle X-ray scattering (SAXS) data show that the degree of orientation of mica in the M-Hydroplastic is close to 0.85, which is higher than that of the M-Hydrogel after NIPS (0.72) and the M-Hydrogel (0.27), indicating better alignment of the mica sheets in the M-Hydroplastic.

Mechanical properties and interaction mechanism of the M-Hydroplastic

To evaluate the reinforcing effect of CNF/mica, the mechanical properties of M-Hydroplastic were systematically compared with those of pure PAM. As shown in Figures 3A, 3B, and S14, the M-Hydroplastic exhibits 2.44 times higher tensile strength (18.5 compared with 45.2 MPa), 2.61 times higher flexural strength (34.7 compared with 90.6 MPa), and a 3.38 times higher flexural modulus (19.3 compared with 65.3 GPa). These enhancements are attributed to improved stress transfer efficiency enabled by CNF/PAM bridging and the dense brick-and-mortar structure.27 The hierarchical brick-and-mortar structure promotes delamination, crack branching, and multiple cracking near the crack tip. These mechanisms help dissipate localized stress and facilitate load redistribution, enhancing the M-Hydroplastic’s resistance to external forces.13,28 However, further increasing the CNF/mica content led to a decline in mechanical performance, likely due to excessive mica aggregation and non-uniform distribution (Figures S15 and S16). It is worth noting that, in the flexural strength-fracture toughness Ashby map, the M-Hydroplastic lies in the overlap between ceramics and metals/alloys and close to the upper range of plastics and elastomers, indicating a balanced combination of strength and toughness (Figure S17). Consistently, its flexural strength exceeds that of common building benchmarks such as basswood, balsa wood, cement board, and concrete slab (Figure S18). To assess resistance to dynamic loading, drop impact tests were conducted under identical conditions. As shown in Figures 3C and 3D, the M-Hydroplastic exhibits more than twice the peak force and total absorbed energy compared to PAM, attributed to the multiscale reinforcement effect. To further elucidate the interfacial interactions in M-Hydroplastic, density functional theory (DFT) was used to calculate the binding energies between PAM, CNF, and mica (Figure 3E). The binding energy between the components confirmed strong interfacial bonding among the components: mica-CNF (−3.02 eV) > mica-PAM (−1.33 eV) > CNF-PAM (−1.01 eV) > PAM-PAM (−0.88 eV) > CNF-CNF (−0.43 eV). The highest binding energy for mica-CNF suggests that CNF acts as a “glue” between the PAM matrix and mica skeleton, enhancing interfacial cohesion. The X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy (XPS) spectra clearly show that Al 2p, Si 2s, and Si 2p peaks shift toward lower binding energies, which can be attributed to interactions between the Al–O and Si–O layers of mica and the CNF and PAM components (Figures 3F and S19). These shifts provide further evidence of the formation of strong interactions among CNF, PAM, and mica.

Wear resistance, which directly influences surface durability and service life, is critical for practical applications. As hardness is a key factor governing wear resistance, it was evaluated using nanoindentation testing. As shown in the 2D hardness mapping distribution obtained by nanoindentation (Figures 3G and 3H), the M-Hydroplastic exhibits significantly higher hardness across all regions compared to PAM, confirming the reinforcing effect of mica on hardness. The corresponding statistics show that the hardness of the M-Hydroplastic (0.23 GPa) is 2.4 times higher than that of PAM (0.097 GPa) and even comparable to metallic Al (0.29 GPa) and polyoxymethylene (POM, 0.31 GPa), an engineering plastic known as “super steel” (Figure S20). More impressively, 3D and 2D scratch profiles from nanoscratch testing reveal that the M-Hydroplastic exhibits the least residual accumulation in track width, depth, and edge height, surpassing PAM, POM, and Al (Figures 3I and S21). This result further demonstrates the strong interfacial bonding between mica sheets, facilitated by strong interactions among the components within the dense M-Hydroplastic. Figure 3J shows a comparison of the relationship between hardness (H) and effective modulus (E∗), E∗ = E/(1 − ν2), where E is Young’s modulus, and ν is Poisson’s ratio.29,30 The dashed line shows the value of H3/E∗2, which indicates the contact load required to cause plastic deformation (i.e., wear resistance), with higher values indicating better wear resistance. Our M-Hydroplastic exhibits the highest H3/E∗2 value (∼48) of all compared materials, confirming its superior wear resistance over conventional engineering plastics.

Flame retardancy of the M-Hydroplastic

Flame retardancy is a key metric for plastics and their alternatives used in modern construction, such as in buildings, but most commercial plastics are flammable, with significant heat, smoke, and toxic gases releasing during combustion, posing severe safety, health, and environmental concerns. In this regard, we comprehensively evaluated the combustion behavior of the M-Hydroplastic. As shown in Figure 4A, the M-Hydroplastic rapidly forms a protective char layer under a 1,300°C butane flame and self-extinguishes immediately after flame removal, indicating excellent flame retardancy. In contrast, PAM, though self-extinguishing, burns intensely and deforms severely, while commercial POM is more prone to complete combustion (Figure S22). More intuitively, a house model constructed from the M-Hydroplastic maintains its integrity and flame resistance under direct butane torch exposure at 1,300oC (Figure S23). Standard industrial tests were conducted to further evaluate the flame retardancy of the M-Hydroplastic, including limiting oxygen index (LOI) and cone calorimetry. As shown in Figure 4B, the LOI value of the M-Hydroplastic is as high as 37.6% (48.6% higher than that of PAM), reaching the grade of non-flammable plastics, indicating that mica greatly improves the flame retardancy of the material. Consistently, in the UL-94 vertical burning test, the M-Hydroplastic achieves a V-0 classification, whereas PAM fails to obtain a rating, confirming the rapid self-extinguishing behavior and greatly reduced propensity for secondary ignition of the M-Hydroplastic. Cone calorimetry results further showed that the ignition time (TTI) of the M-Hydroplastic is significantly delayed compared to PAM, indicating its better flame retardancy (Figure S24). Figure 4C shows that all heat release rate (HRR) curves display two main peaks, with the first peak linked to surface layer combustion. PAM exhibits a sharp rise to the maximum HRR, suggesting poor char formation. In contrast, the mica in the M-Hydroplastic acts as both an insulating layer and a catalytic site to promote carbonization, resulting in a 58% reduction in peak HRR (pHRR). Additionally, the effective heat of combustion (EHC), total heat release (THR), total smoke release (TSR), and total smoke production (TSP) of the M-Hydroplastic were reduced by 42.6%, 18%, 53.8%, and 53%, respectively, compared with PAM, indicating lower heat release and substantially reduced smoke emission during combustion, minimizing potential harm to human health in fire scenarios (Figures 4D and S25). Figure 4E shows that the char generated from PAM pyrolysis encapsulates the mica, forming a protective layer that effectively inhibits flame spread. Therefore, the reduced thermal radiation and smoke release of the M-Hydroplastic are attributed to the char layer and mica barrier, which inhibit the transport, release, and combustion of volatile combustible intermediates. To further elucidate the flame retardancy mechanism of the M-Hydroplastic, both condensed and gaseous pyrolysis products were analyzed. As shown in Figure S26, the thermal degradation of the M-Hydroplastic and PAM in air involves three main stages: a dehydration stage below 220°C, an oxidative pyrolysis stage from 220°C to 450°C, and a carbon oxidation stage above 450°C. The element distribution results of the condensed phase show that, after combustion, it is mainly composed of Si and Al elements, demonstrating that mica is the main component of the condensed phase (Figure S27). The chemical composition of the condensed phase was further characterized by XPS and FTIR. As shown in Figure S28A, the post-combustion XPS spectrum of the M-Hydroplastic exhibits characteristic peaks for C 1s, O 1s, N 1s, Si 2s, Si 2p, and Al 2p, further confirming the presence of mica in the condensed phase. Notably, the ratio of C–O in the characteristic C 1s peaks of the condensed phase increased significantly, indicating the formation of substantial carbon (Figures S28B and S28C). The FTIR spectrum of the M-Hydroplastic residue shows characteristic peaks consistent with those of both mica and the PAM-derived char (Figure S29), confirming that the condensed phase primarily consists of a hybrid mica-char layer. Raman spectroscopy was used to analyze the degree of graphitization of the material during the pyrolysis process (a lower ID/IG value indicates a higher degree of graphitization). Figure 4F shows that the ID/IG value of the M-Hydroplastic is lower, suggesting that mica promotes carbon layer formation and enhances graphitization. This graphitized char acts as an effective barrier, impeding heat and oxygen transfer during combustion.42 Furthermore, FTIR was used to analyze the gaseous products released during the pyrolysis of PAM and the M-Hydroplastic. The absorbance of CO2 (2,400–2,200 cm−1), CO (2,180–2,100 cm−1), C–O/C–H (1,200–1,000 cm−1), H2O (4,000–3,500 cm−1), and NH3 (960–925 cm−1) can be recognized both in PAM and the M-Hydroplastic (Figures 4G and 4H).31 Compared to PAM, the M-Hydroplastic exhibits lower absorption intensities at the same functional group peaks, indicating reduced release of gaseous products. This suggests that the hybrid mica-char layer effectively suppresses volatile emission, reducing combustible gases within the solid phase and enhancing flame retardancy.43 As shown in Figure 4H, the appearance of the C=O peak at 1,740 cm−1 in the PAM spectrum indicates more complete pyrolysis.31 In contrast, its absence in the M-Hydroplastic confirms the barrier effect of the hybrid mica-char layer, which suppresses flame propagation and reduces the release of heat, smoke, and toxic gases. Compared with conventional plastics and other reported flame-retardant materials, the M-Hydroplastic exhibits clear advantages, highlighting its superior flame retardancy (Figure 4I).

Universality of the NIPS-induced multiscale reinforcement strategy

To further verify the universality of the NIPS-induced multiscale reinforcement strategy, five additional M-Hydroplastics were prepared using layered silicate minerals (montmorillonite [MMT], illite, kaolin, chlorite, and vermiculite [VMT]), CNF, and PAM. As shown in Figures 5A and S30, all synthesized composites exhibit an ordered brick-and-mortar structure resulting from phase separation and densification. DFT calculation was conducted to evaluate the interfacial binding energies in different M-Hydroplastics. As shown in Figures 5B and S31, the binding energy between CNF, PAM, and the layered silicate minerals is significantly higher than that between other component pairs in the corresponding M-Hydroplastic. These results confirm strong interactions among PAM, CNF, and the mineral components, which contribute to the enhanced mechanical performance of the M-Hydroplastics. The CNF- and PAM-mediated bridging, combined with strong interfacial bonding, leads to synergistic improvements in tensile strength, flexural strength, and modulus. As depicted in Figures 5C, 5D, and S32, compared with PAM, the M-Hydroplastics show a 1.4–2.3 times higher tensile strength, 2−3 times higher flexural strength, and 2–3.5 times higher flexural modulus. Simultaneously, the Shore D hardness of the M-Hydroplastics can reach 88 ± 3.16 HD (mica), 86.75 ± 0.96 HD (illite), 86.13 ± 1.11 HD (kaolin), 87.63 ± 0.85 HD (MMT), 86 ± 1.41 HD (chlorite), and 87.25 ± 2.18 HD (VMT), comparable to most engineering plastics and far exceeding that of common building materials such as wood and concrete slab. In addition, all M-Hydroplastics rapidly form a protective char layer under a 1,300°C butane flame and self-extinguish upon flame removal, demonstrating excellent flame retardancy (Figure S33). Application demonstration of the M-Hydroplastics in energy-saving buildings

The solvent-programmable M-Hydroplastics exhibit remarkable structural and functional adaptability, enabling their potential integration into adaptive architectures and buildings (Figure 6A). We first demonstrated that, by tuning the mineral and solvent contents, M-Hydroplastics exhibit tunable optical transparency ranging from 17% to 90%, enabling solvent-controlled adaptive light transmission for smart window applications (Figure 6B). Next, we demonstrate that the M-Hydroplastic can be readily molded into 3D honeycomb structure via solvent-induced deformation, which exhibits outstanding compressive resistance exceeding 3 MPa (Figures 6C and S35). This lightweight yet robust architecture originates from the highly ordered cellular framework, which efficiently distributes external stress and prevents structural collapse.44 Such excellent moldability and mechanical strength, combined with its flame retardancy, position our developed M-Hydroplastic as a promising candidate for the reinforced core layer of high-performance, fire-resistant wall panels.

More intriguingly, by combining compositional and structural tunability with solvent-induced processability, M-Hydroplastic provides a unique pathway toward energy-efficient radiative cooling systems. When mica is replaced with calcite, the resulting M-Hydroplastic displays enhanced optical regulation, exhibiting over 90% reflectivity across the solar spectrum (0.3–2.5 μm) and excellent emissivity within the mid-infrared atmospheric window (8–13 μm), both critical for passive daytime radiative cooling (Figure 6D).45,46 In addition, owing to solvent-induced surface plasticity, well-defined patterns can be conveniently embossed onto the M-Hydroplastic surface (Figures S36A and S36B), providing further design flexibility for functional or esthetic integration in building envelopes. An outdoor test of temperature drop was conducted at the School of Information Science, Wuhan University, China, (30° 31′ 48″ N, 114° 21′ 20″ E) from 14:00 to 16:40 on October 12, 2025 (Figures 6E, S36C, and S36D). During this period, the temperature measured under the M-Hydroplastic was, on average, 2.6°C lower than the ambient temperature, demonstrating the potential of M-Hydroplastic to effectively reduce substrate temperature through passive radiative cooling.

Global energy-saving simulations were further performed to evaluate the energy-saving potential of the M-Hydroplastic (Figure 6F). The highest energy savings, up to 35 MJ/m2, occur in equatorial and subtropical regions with intense solar radiation and strong cooling demand. This superior performance arises from the high solar reflectivity and radiative heat dissipation capacity of the M-Hydroplastic, which effectively suppresses heat gain and cooling energy consumption. The corresponding carbon footprint mitigation potential follows a similar pattern, reaching up to 25 t CO2 per year in densely populated or energy-intensive regions such as South Asia, Central Africa, and South America (Figure 6G). A comparison of representative cities (Figures 6H, S37, and S38) reveals that high-temperature and high-humidity regions, such as Hanoi, Phoenix, and Singapore, exhibit greater potential for energy savings and carbon reduction. In contrast, temperate cities like London and Berlin show moderate energy-saving effects but still benefit from notable cooling load reductions during summer. In high-insolation areas, annual cooling cost savings can reach several thousand US dollars.

To evaluate its service reliability in architectural settings, we conducted multiple cycles of reprocessing, UV aging, and high-humidity environment stability tests on the M-Hydroplastic. The results showed that, after multiple cycles of reprocessing and 14 days of UV exposure, its hardness and flexural strength remained generally stable (Figures S39 and S40). Considering its hydroplastic characteristics, we applied only a simple commercial waterproofing spray to the surface to improve moisture resistance. After being placed under 75%/97% relative humidity (RH) conditions for 7 days, changes in appearance and dimensions/weight were controllable, and it maintained structural integrity even after steel ball impact (Figure S41). Together, these results indicate that, although the performance of the M-Hydroplastic may experience slight degradation under simulated service conditions (high relative humidity and intensive UV light), it remains at a high level, further supporting its potential for lightweight building components.

To assess the potential environmental impacts of the M-Hydroplastic, we performed a cradle-to-gate LCA for 1 kg of M-Hydroplastic using primary lab-scale inventory data (Tables S2, S3, and S4). The climate change impact is 4.82 kg CO2 eq. kg−1 (Figure S42) and decreases to 4.56 kg CO2 eq. kg−1 with renewable electricity (−9.5%). Sensitivity and 5,000 run Monte Carlo analyses show low uncertainty (4.51–5.19 kg CO2 eq. kg−1, SD < 5%). This result is superior to that of most commonly used structural materials (Polyethylene terephthalate [PET], 3.81 kg CO2 eq. kg−1; Polypropylene [PP], 4.62 kg CO2 eq. kg−1; Poly(methyl methacrylate) [PMMA], 7.47 kg CO2 eq. kg−1; Polycarbonate [PC], 6.37 kg CO2 eq. kg−1; glass fiber-reinforced plastic [GFRP], 25.67 kg CO2 eq. kg−1; and Al, 19.05 kg CO2 eq. kg−1), highlighting its potential to reduce the inherent carbon content of structural components (Figure S43).

Overall, the M-Hydroplastic delivers remarkable energy, carbon, and economic benefits across diverse climates, offering a universal and scalable solution for passive cooling and sustainable building development.

Conclusion

In summary, we developed a high-mineral-content M-Hydroplastic with combined features of mineral (mechanically strong and flame retardant) and plastic (lightweight and flexible processability) through a universal NIPS-induced multiscale reinforcement strategy. The nonsolvent-induced conformational rearrangement of PAM chains, combined with auxiliary pressing, enhances interfacial bonding between mineral sheets. This leads to strong interactions among CNF, PAM, and mineral, ensuring excellent mechanical performance of the M-Hydroplastic, particularly its superior wear resistance, which outperforms many conventional engineering plastics. This strategy is applicable to various layered silicate minerals, demonstrating attractive universality and material adaptivity. Beyond its mechanical and processing merits, this approach enables multifunctional composites with tunable optical and thermal characteristics for flame-retardant and energy-efficient building systems while also delivering a climate change potential of 4.82 kg CO2 eq. kg−1, supporting decarbonization relative to adaptive and smart materials. Overall, this feasible and scalable strategy provides a promising pathway for developing high-performance mineral-dominated structural materials as plastic alternatives and promotes the sustainable utilization of natural biomass and mineral resources.
