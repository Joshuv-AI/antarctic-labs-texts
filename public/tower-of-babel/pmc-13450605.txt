Awareness and Dietary Practices Related to Fruit and Vegetable Consumption for Cancer Prevention in Mogadishu, Somalia: A Community-Based Cross-Sectional Study
Authors: Ibrahim Abubakar Abdi, Mohamed Ismael Iman, Abdiweli Mohamed Abdi
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundFruit and vegetable consumption is an important component of healthy dietary practices and noncommunicable disease prevention. However, evidence on awareness, dietary practices, and access barriers related to fruit and vegetable consumption remains limited in Somalia. This study assessed awareness and self-reported dietary practices related to fruit and vegetable consumption for cancer prevention among adults in Mogadishu.MethodsA community-based cross-sectional study was conducted among 420 adults in Mogadishu, Somalia, from November 2025 to February 2026. Participants were selected using a multistage sampling approach across the 20 districts of Mogadishu. Data were collected through a structured interviewer-administered questionnaire covering sociodemographic characteristics, knowledge and attitudes, dietary practices, lifestyle factors, and accessibility barriers. Descriptive statistics and Pearson’s chi-square tests were used to analyze the data, with statistical significance set at p<0.05.ResultsThe response rate was 99.5%. Overall, 71.4% of participants had heard that fruits and vegetables may help reduce cancer risk, and 85.0% agreed or strongly agreed that they are important for health. However, only 30.0% reported daily fruit intake, 40.0% reported daily vegetable intake, and 40.0% consumed only 0–1 serving per day. Limited access and food insecurity were reported by 40.0% and 45.0% of participants, respectively. Education level was significantly associated with cancer-prevention awareness (p<0.001), belief that low intake increases cancer risk (p=0.003), and attitudes toward fruit and vegetable importance (p<0.001). Access to fruits and vegetables and food insecurity were significantly associated with daily servings (both p<0.001).ConclusionAwareness and positive attitudes were common, but self-reported intake remained below recommended levels. Findings suggest a knowledge-practice gap associated with socioeconomic and accessibility barriers.

Introduction

Cancer remains a major public health challenge globally. GLOBOCAN 2022 estimated almost 20 million new cancer cases and close to 10 million cancer deaths worldwide, with a growing share of the burden occurring in low- and middle-income countries.1 Cancer prevention therefore requires attention not only to clinical services, but also to modifiable lifestyle and dietary factors that shape long-term noncommunicable disease risk.

Adequate consumption of fruits and vegetables is widely recognized as a key component of a healthy diet and an important strategy for reducing the risk of noncommunicable diseases, including several types of cancer. Fruits and vegetables provide dietary fiber, vitamins, minerals, and bioactive compounds with antioxidant and anti-inflammatory properties that may contribute to mechanisms relevant to cancer prevention. The World Health Organization recommends that people older than 10 years consume at least 400 g of fruits and vegetables per day as part of a healthy diet.2 Similarly, the World Cancer Research Fund/American Institute for Cancer Research recommends maintaining a healthy weight, being physically active, eating a diet rich in wholegrains, vegetables, fruit, and beans, and limiting fast foods, processed meat, sugar-sweetened drinks, and alcohol.3 These recommendations support the public health importance of fruit and vegetable consumption, but they do not imply that a cross-sectional survey can measure direct cancer-preventive effects.

In Somalia, the prevention of diet-related noncommunicable diseases occurs in a difficult context shaped by urbanization, food insecurity, changing dietary patterns, and limited access to diverse foods. Evidence from Hargeisa, Somaliland, has shown that low fruit and vegetable consumption, hypertension, overweight/obesity, and insufficient physical activity are important noncommunicable disease risk factors requiring population-level preventive attention.4 In addition, recent food-security reporting indicates that drought, displacement, high food prices, and disrupted livelihoods continue to affect household access to nutritious foods in Somalia.5 These structural barriers may limit regular consumption of fruits and vegetables even when awareness is present.

Despite the relevance of diet for noncommunicable disease prevention, population-specific evidence on awareness, attitudes, dietary practices, and access barriers related to fruit and vegetable consumption remains limited in Mogadishu. Most available evidence from Somalia focuses on infectious diseases, humanitarian nutrition, or general noncommunicable disease risk factors, leaving a gap in understanding how adults perceive fruit and vegetable consumption in relation to cancer prevention and what barriers affect actual intake.

Therefore, this study aimed to assess awareness and dietary practices related to fruit and vegetable consumption for cancer prevention among adults in Mogadishu, Somalia.

Methodology

Study Design, Setting and Period

This was a community-based cross-sectional study conducted among adults in Mogadishu, Somalia, to assess awareness and self-reported dietary practices related to fruit and vegetable consumption for cancer prevention.

Data were collected from 01 November 2025 to 25 February 2026 in selected districts of Mogadishu, Somalia. The study period was documented because seasonal variation, food prices, and availability may influence fruit and vegetable consumption patterns.

Study Population and Eligibility

The study population consisted of adults aged 18 years and above residing in Mogadishu during the study period. Individuals were eligible if they were permanent or usual residents of the selected areas and provided informed consent. Adults who were seriously ill, unable to communicate, or unwilling to participate were excluded.

Sample-Size Determination

The sample size was calculated using Cochran’s formula for single-population proportion studies: n = Z2p(1-p)/d2. Because no prior community-based estimate of adequate fruit and vegetable consumption for cancer prevention awareness in Mogadishu was available, a conservative prevalence of 50% was used to maximize the required sample size. Using a 95% confidence level (Z = 1.96), 5% margin of error (d = 0.05), and p = 0.50, the minimum sample size was 384. After allowing for approximately 10% non-response or incomplete data, the target sample size was 422.

Sampling Techniques and Procedure

A multistage sampling approach was used to select study participants from the 20 districts of Mogadishu, Somalia. In the first stage, all 20 districts of Mogadishu were included to ensure broad geographic representation of the city. In the second stage, communities or enumeration areas within each district were selected based on administrative boundaries and population distribution. In the third stage, households were selected from the selected communities using a systematic sampling approach where feasible. The total sample size was proportionally allocated across the 20 districts according to estimated population size or household distribution. Within each selected household, adults aged 18 years and above were screened for eligibility. When more than one eligible adult was available in the same household, one participant was selected using a simple random method to reduce selection bias. If an eligible participant was unavailable or declined to participate, the next eligible household was approached.

Data Collection Instrument and Procedure

The questionnaire was adapted after reviewing relevant literature and established dietary and cancer-prevention recommendations.4,6–9

The tool was organized into three main sections. Section A collected sociodemographic and health-related information, including gender, marital status, education level, place of residence, monthly income level, previous cancer diagnosis, family history of cancer, and presence of chronic disease. Section B assessed knowledge and attitudes toward fruits, vegetables, and cancer prevention, including awareness of the potential role of fruit and vegetable consumption in cancer prevention, knowledge of nutrients found in fruits and vegetables, perceived cancer risks associated with low intake, cancers linked to diet, perceived importance of fruits and vegetables, and perceived cancer-prevention methods. Section C assessed dietary practices, lifestyle factors, and accessibility, including fruit and vegetable consumption frequency, daily servings, processed food consumption, physical activity, smoking, alcohol consumption, access to fruits and vegetables, food insecurity, and willingness to increase intake. The questionnaire was reviewed by public health and nutrition experts to assess content relevance, clarity, and cultural appropriateness. It was then pre-tested among 42 adults outside the final study sample. Feedback from the pre-test was used to improve wording, flow, and comprehension of the questions. Internal consistency was assessed using Cronbach’s alpha, with values of 0.788 for the awareness and attitude items and 0.865 for the dietary practice and accessibility items. The final questionnaire is provided as Supplementary Material 1.

Trained data collectors conducted face-to-face interviews using the structured questionnaire. Before data collection, interviewers received training on the study objectives, informed consent procedures, questionnaire administration, neutrality during interviews, confidentiality, and respectful communication with participants. Written informed consent was obtained before each interview. Completed questionnaires were checked daily by supervisors for completeness, consistency, and missing responses before data entry.

Operational Definitions

Adequate fruit and vegetable intake was defined according to WHO recommendations as consuming at least 400 g of fruits and vegetables per day, equivalent to approximately five servings per day. Participants consuming below this threshold were classified as having inadequate intake. Awareness was assessed using questions related to the perceived health benefits of fruit and vegetable consumption, including its role in noncommunicable disease and cancer prevention. Access barriers included self-reported financial constraints, limited availability, high food prices, and household food insecurity.

Statistical Analysis

Data were entered, cleaned, and analyzed using SPSS version 27. Descriptive statistics were used to summarize participant characteristics, awareness, attitudes, dietary practices, and perceived barriers. Frequencies and percentages were reported for categorical variables. Pearson’s chi-square tests were used to assess associations between selected sociodemographic, lifestyle, and access-related variables and key outcomes. Effect sizes were estimated using Cramér’s V. Statistical significance was set at p<0.05.

Discussion

This study provides community-level evidence on awareness, attitudes, dietary practices, and perceived barriers related to fruit and vegetable consumption among adults in Mogadishu, Somalia. The main finding was a clear gap between awareness and reported practice: although 71.4% of participants had heard that fruits and vegetables may help reduce cancer risk and 85.0% expressed favorable attitudes toward their importance for health, only 30.0% reported daily fruit intake and 40.0% reported daily vegetable intake. In addition, most participants reported fewer than five daily servings, which is below the World Health Organization recommendation of at least 400 g/day of fruits and vegetables for people older than 10 years.2

The gap between awareness and reported behavior suggests that knowledge alone may be insufficient to improve dietary practices. This finding is consistent with public health evidence indicating that healthy dietary behavior is shaped not only by individual knowledge, but also by affordability, availability, household food security, cultural habits, and the broader food environment. In the present study, limited access to fruits and vegetables and food insecurity were frequently reported barriers, indicating that nutrition-promotion messages should be combined with practical measures that improve access to affordable and locally available fruits and vegetables.

The findings are relevant to the Somali noncommunicable disease context. A previous cross-sectional study in Somalia, identified low fruit and vegetable consumption, hypertension, overweight/obesity, and insufficient physical activity as important NCD risk factors.4 The present study extends this concern to Mogadishu by showing that inadequate fruit and vegetable intake coexists with frequent processed food consumption and limited physical activity. Although this study did not measure cancer incidence, clinical outcomes, or longitudinal dietary exposure, the reported behavioral patterns are consistent with modifiable lifestyle risk factors emphasized in international cancer-prevention recommendations.3

Food insecurity and access barriers should be interpreted within Somalia’s wider humanitarian and economic context. Drought, displacement, high food prices, and livelihood disruption continue to affect household access to diverse and nutritious foods.5 Therefore, interventions that focus only on individual knowledge may have limited impact unless accompanied by policies and programs that address affordability, market access, and household food security.

This study has several limitations. First, the cross-sectional design prevents assessment of temporality or causality; therefore, the findings should be interpreted as descriptive patterns and associations only. Second, dietary practices were self-reported and may have been affected by recall bias and social desirability bias. Third, although chi-square tests with Cramér’s V effect sizes were used to examine associations, multivariable regression was not performed because the study was primarily exploratory and descriptive, and some categorical self-reported outcomes had limited response distributions that could have produced unstable adjusted estimates.

Despite these limitations, the study contributes preliminary evidence from an underrepresented population and highlights the need for nutrition interventions that address both awareness and structural barriers to healthy dietary practices in Mogadishu. The findings suggest that improving fruit and vegetable consumption in this setting will require combined action on nutrition education, affordability, food availability, and household food security.

Conclusion

This cross-sectional study found that awareness and positive attitudes toward fruit and vegetable consumption were common among adults in Mogadishu, but reported intake remained below recommended levels for most participants. The findings suggest a knowledge-practice gap associated with socioeconomic constraints, food insecurity, limited access to fruits and vegetables, processed food consumption, and physical inactivity. Because the data were self-reported and collected at one point in time, the findings should be interpreted as descriptive patterns and associations, not as evidence of direct cancer-preventive effects. Within the Somali context, feasible public health responses should combine culturally appropriate nutrition education with practical actions to improve affordability and access, including primary health-care counseling, community-based nutrition programs, promotion of locally available foods, and linkage with food-security support for vulnerable households.
