Clinical characteristics and prognosis of acute myeloid leukemia and plasma cell neoplasms with bidirectional occurrence: a retrospective population-based study
Authors: Zhihong Tong, Min Chen, Ziyan Lv, Xiaoqing Yi
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
The bidirectional occurrence of acute myeloid leukemia (AML) and plasma cell neoplasms (PCN) is rare, and clinical characteristics and survival outcomes remain poorly defined. We conducted a retrospective population-based study using the Surveillance, Epidemiology, and End Results (SEER) database (2000–2022). Patients diagnosed with both AML and PCN were identified and categorized based on diagnostic sequence. Overall survival (OS) and cancer-specific survival (CSS) were analyzed using Kaplan–Meier methods and Cox proportional hazards models adjusting for demographic and clinical variables. The research cohort consisted mainly of elderly individuals (≥65 years old, 76.2%) and males (66.3%). The median survival time was 4.0 months (IQR: 1.0-13.0). Multivariable Cox regression analysis demonstrated that chemotherapy was associated with a significant survival benefit across all models of OS (Model III: HR = 0.54, 95% CI: 0.37-0.78, p = 0.001) and CSS (Model III: HR = 0.55, 95% CI: 0.35-0.85, p = 0.008). In contrast, radiotherapy showed no significant association with survival (all p>0.5). Kaplan-Meier analysis confirmed a significant survival advantage for patients receiving chemotherapy (log-rank p < 0.0001) but not for radiotherapy (log-rank p = 0.35). Subgroup analyses revealed that the protective effect of chemotherapy was consistent (all p for interaction > 0.5).In this population-based cohort, chemotherapy receipt was associated with improved survival among patients diagnosed with both AML and PCN. Given the observational nature of the study and limitations inherent to registry-based treatment ascertainment, these findings should be interpreted cautiously and warrant confirmation in studies designed to account for treatment timing and confounding.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-026-51561-4.

Introduction

Acute myeloid leukemia (AML), the most common acute leukemia in adults, is a highly heterogeneous hematologic malignancy originating from myeloid hematopoietic stem/progenitor cells. The incidence of AML was 3.7 per 100,000 people in 20061, and recent data from the United States reports a comparable annual incidence of 4.1 per 100,0002. Plasma cell neoplasms (PCN) constitute a group of malignant proliferative disorders derived from the plasma cell lineage. Accounting for nearly 20% of all hematologic malignancies, PCN primarily includes multiple myeloma, plasmacytoma, and plasma cell leukemia and is associated with a 5-year relative survival rate of 39%3. Although AML and PCN have sharply contrasting pathogenesis, clinical presentation, and treatment paradigms, sporadic case reports indicate that they can occur sequentially in the same patient4–8. Despite its rarity, this phenomenon is of considerable clinical and research interest. However, its population-based incidence, distinct clinical course, prognostic determinants, and underlying biologic mechanisms remain poorly characterized.

The Surveillance, Epidemiology, and End Results (SEER) database represents a large, population-based cohort of US cancer patients, offering a unique opportunity to study rare clinical phenomena such as the sequential occurrence of AML and PCN. Herein, we analyze a cohort of these patients to delineate their clinical characteristics and determine key prognostic factors influencing survival.

Methods

We identified all patients diagnosed with both AML and PCN between 2000 and 2022 from the SEER 21 registries database. Diagnoses were classified according to the International Classification of Diseases for Oncology, Third Edition (ICD-O-3). The AML cohort included the following ICD-O-3 histology codes: 9861/3, 9865/3, 9869/3, 9871/3, 9872/3, 9873/3, 9874/3, 9895/3, 9896/3, 9897/3, and 9911/3. The PCN cohort included codes 9731/3, 9732/3, 9733/3, and 9734/3 (Supplementary Table 1). We excluded patients identified solely through a death certificate or autopsy report.

Patients were categorized into three groups based on the sequence and temporal interval of their diagnoses: Group A (AML diagnosed first), Group B (PCN diagnosed first), and Group C (both malignancies diagnosed within the same calendar year). Extracted variables encompassed age at diagnosis, sex, race, marital status, year of diagnosis, treatment-related information (chemotherapy, radiotherapy, and time from diagnosis to treatment), and survival data (vital status and survival months). Overall survival (OS) was defined as time from diagnosis of the second malignancy to death from any cause or last follow-up. Cancer-specific survival (CSS) was defined as the time from the second malignancy diagnosis to death attributed to AML or PCN or last follow-up.

Statistical analysis

In our analysis, receipt of radiotherapy or chemotherapy was defined as having a recorded “yes” for the respective treatment variable; all other codes (including “no”, “unknown”, and “refused”) were classified as no treatment. Missing data for the time from diagnosis to treatment was handled using multiple imputation, assuming data was missing at random. Continuous variables were summarized using median and interquartile range (IQR) and compared using the Mann-Whitney U test or Kruskal-Wallis test as appropriate. Categorical variables were summarized as counts and percentages and compared using chi-square or Fisher’s exact test9. Survival distributions were estimated using the Kaplan-Meier method and compared using log-rank tests. Multivariable Cox proportional hazards regression models were constructed to evaluate factors associated with overall survival, adjusting for age, sex, race, diagnostic sequence group, diagnostic interval time (years), time from diagnosis to treatment(day), chemotherapy, and radiotherapy. Hazard ratios (HR) and 95% confidence intervals (CI) were reported. To assess consistency in the associations of chemotherapy and radiotherapy, we performed subgroup analyses using Cox regression models and tested interactions between treatment variables and key demographic factors (age, sex, race, and diagnostic sequence group).

Statistical analyses were carried out using R software (http://www.Rproject.org, The R Foundation) and Free Statistics software version 1.9.2.

Results

Based on the inclusion and exclusion criteria outlined in Figs. 1 and 261 patients were included in the final analysis. Table 1 summarizes baseline characteristics. The cohort comprised 62 patients aged < 65 years (23.8%), 99 aged 65–74 years (37.9%), and 100 aged ≥ 75 years (38.3%). Group C had the highest proportion of patients aged ≥ 75 years (24, 43.6%). Table 2 presents multivariable Cox regression analyses for OS and CSS. Chemotherapy was consistently associated with a significant survival benefit across all models. In the unadjusted model (Model I), patients receiving chemotherapy had a lower risk of death compared to those who did not (OS: HR = 0.40, 95% CI: 0.28–0.55, p < 0.001; CSS: HR = 0.42, 95% CI: 0.29–0.62, p < 0.001). After adjusting for age, gender, race, and marital status (Model II), the association remained significant (OS: HR = 0.52, 95% CI: 0.36–0.76, p = 0.001; CSS: HR = 0.53, 95% CI: 0.34–0.81, p = 0.004). In the fully adjusted model (Model III), which additionally controlled for age, gender, race, marital status, diagnostic interval time, time from diagnosis to treatment, and diagnostic sequence group, chemotherapy continued to demonstrate a significant association with improved survival (OS: HR = 0.54, 95% CI: 0.37–0.78, p = 0.001; CSS: HR = 0.55, 95% CI: 0.35–0.85, p = 0.008). By contrast, radiotherapy was not associated with OS and CSS in any models. In the unadjusted model the hazard ratios were non-significant (OS: HR = 0.83, 95% CI: 0.56–1.22, p = 0.341; CSS: HR = 0.84, 95% CI: 0.53–1.31, p = 0.432). After adjusting for age, gender, race, and marital status (Model II), radiotherapy remained non-significant (OS: HR = 1.01, 95% CI: 0.67–1.53, p = 0.968; CSS: HR = 1.04, 95% CI: 0.64–1.69, p = 0.865). In the fully adjusted model, radiotherapy was not associated with survival (OS: HR = 0.92, 95% CI: 0.6–1.42, p = 0.707; CSS: HR = 0.98, 95% CI: 0.6–1.61, p = 0.948).

Kaplan-Meier survival analysis was used to evaluate the impact of radiotherapy and chemotherapy on overall survival. As shown in Fig. 2, there was no statistically significant difference in overall survival between patients who received radiotherapy and those who did not (log-rank test, p = 0.35). In contrast, as shown in Fig. 3, patients receiving chemotherapy demonstrated a statistically significant survival advantage (log-rank test, p < 0.0001). Kaplan-Meier analysis for CSS yielded consistent results (Supplementary Figs. 1 and 2).

We conducted subgroup analyses to assess the consistency of chemotherapy and radiotherapy associations with survival across demographic and clinical subgroups (Figs. 4 and 5 for OS; Supplementary Fig. 3–4 for CSS). Chemotherapy was consistently associated with improved OS and CSS across most subgroups. For OS, significant associations were observed in patients aged 65–74 years (HR = 0.45, 95% CI: 0.24–0.85, p = 0.015), aged ≥ 75 years (HR = 0.56, 95% CI: 0.36–0.86, p = 0.008), females (HR = 0.43, 95% CI: 0.25–0.76, p = 0.004), males (HR = 0.37, 95% CI: 0.25–0.56, p < 0.001), and white patients (HR = 0.36, 95% CI: 0.25–0.53, p < 0.001); CSS analysis yielded similar results, with significant associations in patients aged ≥ 75 years (HR = 0.56, 95% CI: 0.34–0.92, p = 0.023), females (HR = 0.51, 95% CI: 0.26–0.99, p = 0.048), males (HR = 0.38, 95% CI: 0.24–0.61, p < 0.001), and white patients (HR = 0.39, 95% CI: 0.25–0.60, p < 0.001). By diagnostic sequence group, chemotherapy showed significant benefits in group B (OS: HR = 0.36, 95% CI: 0.25–0.53, p < 0.001; CSS: HR = 0.38, 95% CI: 0.25–0.59, p < 0.001). Tests for interaction between chemotherapy and diagnostic sequence were not significant for OS (p for interaction = 0.255) or CSS (p for interaction = 0.23), indicating consistent associations across groups (Fig. 4 and Supplementary Fig. 3). By contrast, radiotherapy was not associated with OS or CSS in any subgroup, with all hazard ratios non-significant across age, sex, race, and diagnostic sequence subgroups (all p > 0.05) and interaction p-values all > 0.05, confirming the consistent absence of radiotherapy association across all patient cohorts (Fig. 5 and Supplementary Fig. 4).

Discussion

This population-based retrospective study conducted a comprehensive analysis of the clinical characteristics and factors associated with survival in patients concurrently diagnosed with AML and PCN. This condition is exceptionally rare and clinically challenging. Our findings not only delineate the demographic features and treatment patterns of this distinct patient cohort but also provide key insights with direct clinical relevance while raising intriguing questions regarding its underlying biological mechanisms.

The baseline characteristics reveal that the majority of patients were elderly, with 38.3% being ≥ 75 years of age, and predominantly male (66.3%) and White (77.0%). These findings are consistent with the known epidemiology of both AML and PCN, which show increased incidence with advanced age and male predominance10–13. However, the relatively short median overall survival of 4.0 months starkly underscores the uniquely aggressive clinical course and profound therapeutic challenges posed by the sequential emergence of these two distinct hematologic malignancies. The increasing incidence of sequential diagnoses over time may reflect improved diagnostic sensitivity and patient survival, allowing for the manifestation of a second malignancy, but it may also hint at shared, underlying biological vulnerabilities.

The most striking finding of our study is the profound and consistent survival benefit associated with chemotherapy, which remained robust even after extensive multivariable adjustment. This reinforces the established role of systemic therapy as the cornerstone of AML management14–16 and provides the first high-level evidence that this principle holds true even in the context of a prior or subsequent PCN. The uniformity of this protective effect across all demographic subgroups, as confirmed by our interaction analyses, is particularly noteworthy. It suggests that the decision to administer chemotherapy should not be withheld based on age, gender, or race alone in this population, a crucial consideration for a cohort with such a dismal prognosis. The association between chemotherapy and improved survival was consistent across diagnostic sequence groups, suggesting that systemic therapy may play a role regardless of whether AML or PCN occurs first. The lack of significant association in Group C (same-year diagnosis) may reflect smaller sample size, greater clinical heterogeneity, or differences in disease biology that warrant further investigation. Radiotherapy plays an established role in managing localized complications of plasma cell neoplasms, including solitary plasmacytoma and palliative treatment of symptomatic bone lesions17,18. This study has not yet found any survival benefits of radiotherapy, which may reflect its local, non-systemic mechanism of action and specific indications, rather than treatment ineffectiveness.

While our study identifies key clinical factors, the biological mechanisms enabling sequential AML and PCN development remain speculative. Several non-mutually exclusive hypotheses may explain this phenomenon: clonal evolution from a shared pre-malignant clone with treatment-related selective pressure19–21; germline predisposition (e.g., DDX41) creating permissive genetic backgrounds20,22; and a dysregulated tumor microenvironment promoting survival of both malignant clones23,24. These hypotheses require validation in future studies incorporating genomic profiling.

For this rare high-risk cohort, our findings help establish a benchmark for clinical decision-making, underscoring the profound benefits of chemotherapy. Notably, our fully adjusted model demonstrated a significant reduction in risk ratios, highlighting the necessity for further investigation into specific treatment regimens. In this context, we should prioritize novel therapies such as Venetoclax-based combination regimens25,26, which have improved outcomes for elderly AML patients. Future studies should integrate comprehensive molecular analyses to decipher clonal architecture and identify targetable vulnerabilities. Understanding whether these malignancies harbor actionable mutations or dependencies will facilitate the development of personalized therapies beyond conventional cytotoxic treatments.

However, our retrospective, population-based study design carries inherent limitations. First, the potential for immortal time bias cannot be fully excluded, as patients must survive long enough to receive treatment, and treatment was modeled as a time-fixed rather than time-varying covariate. Second, treatment receipt was defined based on SEER variables, with all non-“yes” codes (including “no”, “unknown”, and “refused”) classified as no treatment; this approach may introduce misclassification bias. Third, although multiple imputation was performed for missing data on time from diagnosis to treatment assuming data were missing at random, the possibility of non-random missingness cannot be excluded. Fourth, confounding by indication represents a major concern, as patients receiving chemotherapy likely differ systematically from those who do not in ways not captured by SEER, including performance status, comorbidity burden, AML cytogenetic risk, PCN stage and subtype, frailty, and treatment goals; these unmeasured confounders may partially or fully explain the observed associations. Fifth, the binary classification of “chemotherapy” in SEER does not distinguish between AML-directed therapy, PCN-directed therapy, treatment intensity, or intent (curative vs. palliative); thus, the observed association should be interpreted as representing “receipt of any systemic therapy” rather than a specific regimen. Furthermore, the absence of molecular profiles and detailed causes of death constrains exploration of deeper biological mechanisms and therapeutic insights.

In this population-based cohort, receipt of chemotherapy was associated with improved overall survival. However, interpretation of this finding must be cautious given the observational design and inherent limitations of registry-based treatment data. Patients receiving chemotherapy likely represent a selected population with better functional status, lower comorbidity burden, and more favorable disease characteristics. Additionally, treatment timing relative to diagnosis introduces potential immortal time bias, which may exaggerate observed treatment effects.

Therefore, while chemotherapy receipt was associated with improved survival, these findings should be interpreted as hypothesis-generating rather than establishing causality. Future studies incorporating detailed clinical, genomic, and treatment timing data are needed to better define optimal management strategies in this rare population.
