Development and preliminary psychometric evaluation of an oral health questionnaire for chronic kidney disease (CKD-OHQ)
Authors: Yang Liu, Liyi An, Lin Xie, Zining Zhang, Jing Sun
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundOral health problems are common among patients with chronic kidney disease. Chronic kidney disease is associated with a broad range of oral manifestations, while poor oral health may accelerate chronic kidney disease progression, increase the risk of complications, and contribute to the overall disease burden. Despite their clinical importance, oral health problems often receive limited attention in routine nephrology care. This study aimed to develop and conduct a preliminary psychometric evaluation of the Chronic Kidney Disease Oral Health Questionnaire to assess nephrology healthcare professionals’ knowledge, attitudes, and self-reported practices regarding oral health management in patients with chronic kidney disease.MethodsThe initial questionnaire was developed using a knowledge-attitude-practice framework, a literature review, and two rounds of expert consultation. In October 2025, the preliminary questionnaire was administered online to nephrology healthcare professionals in Hubei, Anhui, and Jiangsu provinces, China. Content and face validity, internal consistency, and test-retest reliability were evaluated. Exploratory factor analysis was conducted to examine the construct validity of the attitude and practice dimensions.ResultsThe questionnaire comprised 25 items across three dimensions. A total of 169 valid responses were included. The scale-level content validity index was 0.95. Cronbach’s α coefficients were 0.97 for the attitude dimension and 0.98 for the practice dimension, whereas the Kuder–Richardson Formula 20 coefficient for the knowledge dimension was 0.46. The intraclass correlation coefficient for the total questionnaire was 0.95. Exploratory factor analysis of the attitude and practice items identified two factors that explained 83.34% of the total variance.ConclusionsThe questionnaire offers a preliminary structured approach to assessing nephrology healthcare professionals’ knowledge, attitudes, and self-reported practices regarding oral health management in patients with chronic kidney disease. Introduction

Chronic kidney disease (CKD) is an increasingly important contributor to mortality from non-communicable diseases [1–4]. It also imposes a substantial public health burden by reducing quality of life and increasing healthcare use [5]. Oral complications are common in people with CKD and may affect both soft and hard tissues. Reported manifestations include dry mouth, halitosis, altered taste, dysphagia, dental pain, periodontitis, dental caries, tooth mobility or loss, perioral dermatitis, mucosal lesions, and oral candidiasis [6–11]. Their reported prevalence ranges widely, from approximately 10% to 95% [12–16], and tends to increase as kidney function declines [17].

CKD and oral diseases may influence each other. CKD-related changes, including mineral and bone disorders, impaired immune function, reduced salivary flow, and elevated urea levels, may weaken oral defenses and damage periodontal tissues, increasing susceptibility to dental caries, periodontitis, and oral infections [18, 19]. In turn, oral infections may trigger systemic inflammation and transient bacteremia. These processes have been proposed as potential contributors to CKD progression and may partly explain the higher risks of cardiovascular complications and mortality observed in patients with poor oral health [18]. Oral health also affects the daily well-being of people with CKD [15–18]. Oral discomfort and impaired oral function may reduce dietary intake and are associated with poorer nutritional status [20], while changes in oral function or appearance may affect social interaction and self-esteem [21]. Integrating oral health management into routine CKD care may therefore support more comprehensive patient management and better long-term outcomes.

Despite its clinical importance, oral health often receives limited attention in CKD care. In economically developed regions of China, nearly 73% of patients with CKD reported oral problems during the previous year, but only 21.7% sought dental care [22]. Dental attendance was even lower in Southwest China, where only 14.05% of patients accessed such care [23]. Similarly, only 4.3% of dialysis patients in Japan received a dental referral from their physicians [24], while fewer than 30% of patients in Brazil were referred for dental care [25]. These consistently low rates of dental attendance and referral highlight the important role of nephrology healthcare professionals in recognizing oral health problems and connecting patients with appropriate dental services.

In this context, nephrology healthcare professionals’ knowledge, attitudes, and practices are critical to improving oral health management. However, available evidence indicates substantial gaps in knowledge [26–29]. In a US survey, only 61% of nephrology nurses correctly recognized the association between oral health and CKD [26]. In Brazil, more than 80% of nephrology healthcare professionals did not identify periodontal disease as a potential risk factor for CKD, while 42.2% of physicians and 38% of nurses did not routinely examine their patients’ oral cavities [25]. Clinical practices also appear inconsistent, particularly regarding the use of prophylactic antibiotics before dental procedures in patients with end-stage kidney disease [27]. Qualitative evidence further suggests that oral health management practices may be constrained by practical barriers, such as difficulty observing the oral cavity during dialysis and insufficient staffing [28]. Professional attitudes are even less well understood, with little known about nephrology healthcare professionals’ willingness to participate in or assume responsibility for oral health management in patients with CKD [28, 29].

To address these gaps, this study aimed to develop and conduct a preliminary psychometric evaluation of a questionnaire for assessing nephrology healthcare professionals’ knowledge, attitudes, and practices regarding oral health management in patients with CKD, with the goal of informing targeted professional education and clinical support.

Methods

Study design

This cross-sectional study was conducted in two phases. Phase I involved developing the Chronic Kidney Disease Oral Health Questionnaire (CKD-OHQ) and evaluating its content validity. Phase Ⅱ examined the questionnaire’s internal structure and other preliminary measurement properties. The development process was guided by the principles described by DeVellis RF [30].

Phase Ⅰ Questionnaire development

Conceptual framework

The questionnaire was developed using the knowledge-attitude-practice (KAP) framework, which is widely applied in health education and behavioral research [31]. The framework guided the organization of the questionnaire into three domains: knowledge of CKD-related oral health, attitudes toward oral health management, and oral health management practices among nephrology healthcare professionals.

Item generation

The initial item pool was generated through a targeted literature review and multidisciplinary research team discussions. PubMed, Web of Science, and the China National Knowledge Infrastructure were searched from database inception to July 15, 2025. The search combined terms related to kidney disease, including “chronic kidney disease,” “end-stage kidney disease,” “dialysis,” and “kidney transplantation,” with terms related to oral health and professional practice, including “oral health,” “oral manifestations,” “oral care,” “dental care,” “periodontal disease,” “xerostomia,” “knowledge,” “attitude,” and “practice.”

Studies were considered relevant if they addressed oral manifestations, risks, assessment, prevention, or management in patients with CKD, or healthcare professionals’ knowledge, attitudes, and practices regarding oral health. Relevant concepts were extracted and organized into the knowledge, attitude, and practice domains. Duplicate statements were removed, and items addressing the same concept or clinical behavior were combined. The preliminary item pool was then reviewed and refined by a multidisciplinary research team comprising a nephrology department director, a nurse manager, two nephrology physicians, two nephrology nurses, and a dentist. The team assessed the items for content coverage, clinical relevance, and clarity, and revised the wording where necessary. The resulting item pool was subsequently submitted to formal expert consultation for item selection.

Item selection

Experts evaluated the importance of each item using a 5-point Likert scale and commented on its clarity and feasibility. They were also invited to recommend changes to item wording, content, or structure. Expert agreement was assessed using the coefficient of variation (CV) and the item selection rate. The item selection rate was calculated as the proportion of experts who assigned an importance score of 4 or 5. In the first Delphi round, items with a CV > 0.25 or a selection rate < 70% were removed. The remaining items were revised according to expert feedback. In the second round, a stricter selection criterion was applied: items with a selection rate < 80% were removed, and the retained items were further refined based on expert feedback.

Content and face validity

Following item selection, content validity was independently evaluated to further assess the relevance and representativeness of the retained items. Ten experts—five nephrology specialists, two nephrology nursing experts, two dental experts, and one expert in psychometrics—rated the relevance of each item using a 4-point Likert scale. The item-level content validity index (I-CVI) and scale-level content validity index (S-CVI) were then calculated. Items with an I-CVI < 0.70 were removed, those with values between 0.70 and 0.79 were revised, and those with an I-CVI > 0.79 were retained [32]. The S-CVI was calculated as the mean of the I-CVI values across all items, with a value > 0.80 considered acceptable [31].

Face validity was assessed by pretesting the questionnaire with 15 nephrology healthcare professionals. Participants provided feedback on the clarity and relevance of the items and on the overall questionnaire layout.

Phase Ⅱ Psychometric properties

Questionnaire

The CKD-OHQ was originally developed and psychometrically evaluated in Chinese. It was designed to assess nephrology healthcare professionals’ knowledge, attitudes, and practices regarding oral health management across the CKD care spectrum, including non-dialysis CKD, dialysis, and kidney transplantation. The English version was directly translated from the original Chinese questionnaire for review and presentation purposes. The questionnaire consisted of two sections. The first collected demographic information, including sex, age, professional role, educational background, professional title, years of work experience, hospital level, and previous oral health-related training. The second contained 25 items across three dimensions: knowledge (10 items), attitude (5 items), and practice (10 items). The knowledge dimension included six single-choice items (items 1, 2, 6, 7, 8 and 10) and four multiple-response items (items 3, 4, 5 and 9). The response format was determined by the item content: single-choice items were used when one best answer could be identified, whereas multiple-response items were used when several correct options together comprised the complete knowledge being assessed. Single-choice items were scored as 1 for a correct response and 0 for an incorrect response. Multiple-response items used an all-or-none scoring method: 1 point was awarded only when all correct options and no incorrect options, were selected; otherwise, the item was scored 0. The attitude and practice items were rated on 5-point Likert scales. Response options ranged from 1 (strongly disagree) to 5 (strongly agree) for attitude items and from 1 (never) to 5 (always) for practice items. Higher dimension scores indicated greater knowledge, more positive attitudes, and more proactive self-reported practices regarding oral health management in CKD. A higher total score indicated a higher overall level across the three dimensions.

Participants

Healthcare professionals working in nephrology departments, hemodialysis centers, or peritoneal dialysis centers were recruited. Participants were eligible if they (1) held a valid medical practitioner or registered nurse license and (2) were involved in the diagnosis, treatment, or nursing care of patients with CKD. Interns and healthcare professionals undertaking short-term clinical training were excluded because they were not independently engaged in routine CKD care. Based on the recommended minimum item-to-participant ratio of 1:5 [30], at least 125 participants were required for the 25-item questionnaire. After allowing for an additional 10% to account for incomplete or invalid responses, the target sample size was set at a minimum of 138 participants.

Data collection

Participants were recruited by convenience sampling from tertiary and secondary hospitals in Hubei, Anhui, and Jiangsu provinces, China, to include healthcare professionals from different institutional and professional backgrounds. Data were collected online through the Wenjuanwang platform in October 2025. The questionnaire link was distributed to eligible participants with the assistance of designated liaison staff at the participating hospitals.

To evaluate the test-retest reliability of the total CKD-OHQ, a subsample of 30 participants from one participating hospital who had completed the initial survey (T1) completed the questionnaire again exactly two weeks later (T2). At T1, each participant was assigned an automatically generated serial number containing no personal identifiers. Participants entered the same number at T2 to enable anonymous matching of their responses. Both assessments were administered online through the same platform using consistent procedures. The research team provided no feedback on participants' responses and delivered no study-related training or oral health educational materials between T1 and T2.

To support data quality, the platform restricted duplicate submissions from the same IP address and required responses to all items. Questionnaires were excluded if they were completed in less than 2 min or contained the same response for 10 or more consecutive items. The same exclusion criteria were applied to both the T1 and T2.

Reliability

Internal consistency was assessed using the Kuder-Richardson Formula 20 (KR-20) for the dichotomously scored knowledge dimension and Cronbach’s α for the attitude and practice dimensions [33]. A KR-20 coefficient ≥ 0.5 and Cronbach’s α coefficient ≥ 0.70 were considered acceptable [34]. Test-retest reliability for the total CKD-OHQ score was assessed using the two-way mixed-effects intraclass correlation coefficient (ICC) model. An ICC > 0.75 indicated good test-retest reliability [34].

Construct validity

Exploratory factor analysis (EFA) was conducted on the attitude and practice items using principal axis factoring with direct oblimin rotation. The suitability of the data for factor analysis was assessed using the Kaiser–Meyer–Olkin measure and Bartlett’s test of sphericity. Factors were retained based on eigenvalues > 1.0 and visual inspection of the scree plot. Factor loadings were examined using the pattern matrix, and items with a loading ≥ 0.40 were retained. The final factor was interpreted based on both the statistical results and the conceptual content of the items [34, 35].

Statistical analysis

Data were analyzed using IBM SPSS 26.0. Continuous variables were summarized as mean ± standard deviation when normally distributed and as median and interquartile range when non-normally distributed. Categorical variables were summarized as frequencies and percentages.

Ethical considerations

The Ethical Committee of Union Hospital, Tongji Medical College, Huazhong University of Science and Technology approved this research (UHCT230185). Electronic informed consent was obtained from all participants before they began the survey. Participation was voluntary, and responses were collected anonymously and treated confidentially. Participants could discontinue the survey at any time before submission.

Results

Demographic characteristics

A total of 189 questionnaires were collected. Of these, 20 were excluded because they were completed in less than 2 min, leaving 169 valid responses for analysis. More than half were aged 31–40 years (53.85%), and 72.19% held a bachelor’s degree. Approximately one-third had 5–10 years of professional experience. In addition, 77.51% reported that they had not received any previous training in oral health management for patients with CKD.

Item selection

All 18 experts invited to participate in the first Delphi round responded. Based on the predefined criteria, one item was removed. Following the experts’ qualitative feedback, four additional items were removed, overlapping items were combined, and four items were revised. These changes reduced the item pool from 45 to 26 items.

In the second round, 15 of the 18 invited experts responded, giving a response rate of 83.3%. One additional item was removed according to the predefined criteria, while the remaining items were retained without further revision. The resulting preliminary CKD-OHQ comprised 25 items: 10 knowledge items, 5 attitude items, and 10 practice items.

Content and face validity

All 10 experts completed the content validity evaluation. The I-CVI values ranged from 0.90 to 1.00, and the S-CVI was 0.95. During the pretest, minor revisions were made to improve item wording, clarity, and structure. The questionnaire was considered clear, easy to understand, and feasible to complete within the expected time frame, with all respondents completing it in less than 10 min.

Reliability

Internal consistency

The KR-20 coefficient for the knowledge dimension was 0.46. Further item analysis showed that removing two items together would increase the KR-20 coefficient to 0.60. For the attitude dimension, Cronbach’s α was 0.97, corrected item-total correlations ranged from 0.897 to 0.943, and Cronbach’s α after deletion of individual items ranged from 0.962 to 0.970. Inter-item correlations ranged from 0.829 to 0.954. For the practice dimension, Cronbach’s α was 0.98, corrected item-total correlations ranged from 0.681 to 0.948, and Cronbach’s α after deletion of individual items ranged from 0.970 to 0.980. Inter-item correlations ranged from 0.497 to 0.964. Detailed item-level results and the full inter-item correlation matrices are provided in Supplementary Tables S1-S4.

Test-retest reliability

The ICC for the total CKD-OHQ score was 0.95 (95% CI: 0.89–0.97).

Construct validity

EFA was conducted on the 15 attitude and practice items. The Kaiser-Meyer-Olkin value was 0.92, and Bartlett’s test of sphericity was statistically significant (χ² = 4079.03, p < 0.001). Based on the Kaiser criterion and the scree plot (Fig. 1), two factors were extracted. Together, they explained 83.34% of the total variance after rotation, with Factor 1 accounting for 55.41% and Factor 2 for 27.93%.

Scree plot

The pattern matrix showed that Factor 1 comprised the 10 practice items (B1-B10), with loadings ranging from 0.70 to 0.96. Factor 2 comprised the five attitude items (A1-A5), with loadings ranging from 0.90 to 0.97. All items loaded strongly on their intended factors, and no meaningful cross-loadings were observed (Table 3).

Discussion

This study developed the CKD-OHQ and conducted a preliminary psychometric evaluation of its measurement properties. The findings showed that the attitude and practice dimensions demonstrated high internal consistency and EFA identified a two-factor structure corresponding to the proposed domains. In contrast, the knowledge dimension showed low internal consistency. Taken together, these findings provide preliminary evidence regarding the measurement properties of the CKD-OHQ but also indicate that the questionnaire as a whole requires further refinement and validation.

Nephrology healthcare professionals can support CKD-related oral health management by identifying potential oral problems, providing patient education, performing basic oral assessments, and facilitating dental referral and collaboration. Their involvement in these activities may be shaped by their knowledge and the importance they attach to oral health. Assessing these factors is therefore important, yet previous surveys conducted in different countries have relied largely on study-specific assessment instruments [25–27, 29]. Differences in instrument content and design limit direct comparison of findings across populations and clinical settings. Moreover, limited information is available on how these instruments were developed and psychometrically evaluated, making it difficult to assess the reliability and validity of the resulting data. The CKD-OHQ was therefore developed to address these limitations by providing a more consistent and systematically evaluated instrument for assessing nephrology healthcare professionals’ knowledge, attitudes, and practices.

Beyond its potential practical value, the psychometric performance of the CKD-OHQ also warrants careful consideration. The attitude and practice dimensions demonstrated high internal consistency and EFA supported a two-factor structure corresponding to the intended domains. High corrected item-total correlations and factor loadings, together with the absence of meaningful cross-loadings, provide preliminary support for the internal consistency and structural validity of these dimensions. However, the combination of very high Cronbach’s α coefficients, corrected item-total correlations, and inter-item correlations—and, for some items, very high factor loadings—may indicate conceptual overlap and limited incremental information. This raises the possibility of item redundancy and suggests that both dimensions may benefit from further item review and refinement. In addition, attitude scores were concentrated near the upper end of the scale, which may limit the ability of some items to distinguish between participants with different attitude levels and may partly reflect socially desirable responding.

Compared with the attitude and practice dimensions, the knowledge dimension showed substantially lower internal consistency. The total knowledge score should therefore be interpreted with caution. One possible explanation is the broad scope of the dimension, which covers disease mechanisms, oral manifestations, preventive strategies, and clinical care procedures. Although these topics are all relevant to CKD-related oral health, they represent distinct areas of knowledge and may not be expected to correlate strongly with one another. The all-or-none scoring method used for the multiple-response items may also have contributed to the low KR-20 coefficient. Under this approach, participants received 1 point only when they selected all correct options and no incorrect options. The rule was intended to ensure that a correct response reflected complete understanding and to limit credit from guessing or indiscriminate option selection. However, it did not distinguish partial knowledge from complete misunderstanding: participants who missed only one correct option received the same score as those who answered the item entirely incorrectly. This may have reduced the variation in item scores and limited the ability of some items to distinguish between different levels of knowledge.

Further item analysis showed that removing two knowledge items would increase the KR-20 coefficient to 0.60. Although their removal would improve internal consistency, both items addressed clinically important content and contributed to the breadth of the knowledge domain. They were therefore retained at this preliminary stage, pending further evaluation in larger and more diverse samples.

The knowledge dimension was not included in the EFA because the conventional approach used in this study relied on a Pearson correlation matrix. This approach is better suited to continuous or approximately continuous variables and may not adequately represent the relationships among dichotomously scored knowledge items. The knowledge dimension was therefore evaluated preliminarily through several complementary methods. Item-level correct-response rates were reported to describe item difficulty, and changes in the KR-20 coefficient after deletion of each item were examined to identify potentially problematic items. The overall KR-20 coefficient was also calculated to assess the internal consistency of the knowledge dimension. Future studies should further examine item difficulty, item discrimination, and option-level response patterns, and compare alternative scoring methods. These analyses could clarify how scoring methods affect score distributions, item discrimination, and internal consistency. With an adequate sample, methods suitable for dichotomous data, such as categorical factor analysis based on a tetrachoric correlation matrix, item response theory, or Rasch analysis, could also be used to evaluate the latent structure and measurement performance of the knowledge items [36, 37].

The total CKD-OHQ score showed high test-retest reliability over an exact two-week interval. However, the test-retest analysis was based on only 30 participants from a single participating hospital. Their demographic and professional characteristics were not formally compared with those of the overall sample; therefore, the representativeness of the retest subsample could not be established. In addition, participants from the same hospital may share similar clinical environments, organizational practices, and professional characteristics, which may limit the representativeness of the retest subsample and the generalizability of the findings. Although the research team provided no feedback on participants' answers, training, or educational materials between the initial test (T1) and the retest (T2), participants’ exposure to external information or independent learning during this interval was not assessed. Any resulting changes in knowledge, attitudes, or reported practices may have affected the test-retest results. Test-retest reliability should therefore be examined further in larger, more diverse, and independently recruited samples.

Most CKD-OHQ items were designed to assess aspects of CKD-related oral health management in which both physicians and nurses may participate, including recognizing oral manifestations, understanding the relationship between CKD and oral disease, conducting basic oral assessment, providing patient education, identifying potential oral problems, and coordinating with dental services. However, some practice items, particularly those concerning formal referral or treatment decisions, may be influenced by differences in professional roles, authority and scope of practice. Responses to these items may therefore reflect professional responsibilities as well as actual engagement in oral health management. Because the sample consisted predominantly of nurses, and neither measurement invariance nor differential item functioning was examined, it remains unclear whether CKD-OHQ scores have the same meaning for physicians and nurses. Future studies should recruit more balanced professional samples, assess whether the items function similarly across the two groups, and determine whether some practice items require more role-neutral wording or an option such as “not within my professional responsibilities.”

Limitations

Several limitations should be considered. First, the sample size limited the scope and robustness of the psychometric evaluation. Although EFA was conducted, a larger sample is needed to confirm the proposed factor structure and allow independent validation. Second, the restricted geographical coverage and predominance of nurses in the sample may limit the generalizability of the findings across regions and professional groups. Third, the CKD-OHQ is limited by the scope of its current measurement approach. The practice dimension assesses self-reported frequency and therefore cannot confirm whether reported practices reflect actual clinical behavior. In addition, the KAP framework focuses mainly on individual-level knowledge, attitudes, and practices, whereas oral health management may also be shaped by organizational resources and access to dental services. These contextual factors were not directly assessed in the present study.

Implication for practice

The CKD-OHQ offers a preliminary structured approach to assessing nephrology healthcare professionals’ knowledge, attitudes, and self-reported practices regarding oral health management in patients with CKD. With further refinement and validation, it may help departments identify areas requiring additional professional education or clinical support, guide the development of targeted training, and inform reviews of relevant clinical processes.

The questionnaire could also support the development of a standardized educational feedback module. After completing the questionnaire, participants could receive correct answers, concise evidence-based explanations, and clinically relevant guidance, thereby linking assessment with professional learning.

At the departmental or organizational level, future use of a refined and validated CKD-OHQ may support continuous quality improvement, encourage the integration of oral health into routine CKD care, and strengthen collaboration between nephrology and dental services. The instrument itself does not prescribe a fixed schedule for oral health assessment or intervention in patients with CKD; appropriate timing should instead be determined by patient needs and local clinical pathways.

Conclusions

The CKD-OHQ is a preliminary structured instrument for assessing nephrology healthcare professionals’ knowledge, attitudes, and self-reported practices regarding oral health management in patients with CKD. This study provides initial evidence of its measurement properties, although further refinement and validation are needed. Following evaluation in larger, more diverse, and independently recruited samples, the instrument may help identify professional education and clinical support needs and strengthen collaboration between nephrology and dental services.
