USP18 Serves as a Key Mediator of cGAS‐STING for Cardiac Aging in Diabetes
Authors: Xunxi Deng, Zhiyi Yin, Shi Tai, Hejun Jiang, Yuying Zhou, Yongjun Wang, Liyao Fu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTDiabetes accelerates cardiac aging, but the molecular mechanisms that link metabolic stress to myocardial senescence remain insufficiently defined. This study investigated factors driving diabetes‐associated cardiac aging with a focus on the role of USP18. Type 2 diabetes mellitus (T2DM) was induced in mice using a high‐fat diet combined with streptozotocin. Cardiac structure, function, and molecular alterations were evaluated by echocardiography, histology, RNA sequencing, western blotting, qPCR, and immunofluorescence. H9C2 cardiomyocytes cultured under high‐glucose conditions were used for in vitro validation. Diabetic mice exhibited diastolic dysfunction, myocardial hypertrophy, and fibrosis, accompanied by increased p53 and p21 expression. RNA‐seq and protein analyses consistently identified USP18 as significantly upregulated in diabetic hearts. Elevated USP18 was associated with activation of the cGAS–STING pathway, increased TBK1 phosphorylation, and enhanced IL‐6 and IL‐1β production. High‐glucose–treated cardiomyocytes similarly showed USP18 induction, stabilization of cGAS–STING signaling, and enhanced senescence‐related responses. USP18 contributes to cardiac aging in diabetes by promoting cGAS–STING activation and inflammatory senescence. Targeting USP18 may represent a potential strategy to alleviate myocardial aging under diabetic conditions.Keywords: cGAS–STING, deubiquitination, diabetic cardiac aging, USP18

Introduction

Cardiac aging is a physiological process marked by a gradual decline in cardiac function, alongside multiple cellular and molecular changes [1]. This process leads to structural and functional deterioration, increasing the susceptibility of the heart to stress and the risk of cardiovascular morbidity and mortality in older adults [2, 3]. Key hallmarks of cardiac aging include left ventricular (LV) hypertrophy, compromised diastolic performance, limited systolic reserve capacity, progressive arterial stiffening, and declining endothelial function [4]. These changes contribute to a higher incidence of heart failure and cardiovascular mortality in older adults. Despite its clinical significance, the molecular mechanisms that drive cardiac aging remain incompletely understood.

Type 2 diabetes mellitus (T2DM) is a major global metabolic disorder marked by chronic hyperglycemia and insulin resistance [5]. Beyond its well‐known vascular and renal complications, T2DM is increasingly recognized as an accelerator of organ aging, including in the heart [6]. Accumulating evidence demonstrates that diabetic conditions induce oxidative stress, mitochondrial dysfunction, advanced glycation end‐products (AGEs) accumulation, and low‐grade chronic inflammation—all of which promote cardiomyocyte senescence and fibrosis [7, 8]. T2DM is a major driver of cardiometabolic disorders and represents a leading risk factor for cardiovascular morbidity and mortality worldwide. Chronic hyperglycemia, insulin resistance, and dyslipidemia collectively contribute to endothelial dysfunction, myocardial remodeling, and systemic inflammation. These alterations promote the development of diabetic cardiomyopathy, characterized by diastolic dysfunction, myocardial fibrosis, and impaired cardiac metabolism, even in the absence of overt coronary artery disease or hypertension. Importantly, accumulating evidence suggests that these cardiometabolic disturbances converge on pathways of cellular senescence and inflammaging, thereby accelerating cardiac aging under diabetic conditions [9, 10, 11, 12]. Transcriptomic and histological analyses have confirmed that T2DM hearts exhibit enhanced expression of senescence markers such as p16INK4a and p21, alongside impaired diastolic function and reduced regenerative potential [13, 14]. However, the precise molecular links between diabetic metabolic stress and accelerated cardiac aging are not fully elucidated, leaving a critical gap for potential therapeutic intervention.

Among the signaling pathways implicated in aging‐related tissue dysfunction, the cyclic GMP–AMP synthase–stimulator of interferon genes (cGAS–STING) axis has emerged as a key regulator. Activation of cGAS–STING links cytosolic DNA sensing to TBK1/IRF3‐ and NF‐κB–dependent inflammatory responses [15, 16]. Recent evidence shows that activation of this pathway in cardiac tissue induces inflammation, mitochondrial dysfunction, cardiomyocyte senescence, ventricular stiffness, and myocardial fibrosis—hallmark features of cardiac aging [17, 18, 19]. Conversely, genetic deletion or pharmacologic inhibition of STING attenuates myocardial inflammation, reduces fibrotic remodeling, and improves cardiac function, indicating that excessive cGAS–STING signaling accelerates cardiac aging–related phenotypes [20, 21]. In metabolic diseases such as T2DM, hyperglycemia, lipotoxicity, and mitochondrial damage promote cytosolic DNA leakage that activates cGAS–STING signaling, thereby amplifying inflammation and tissue injury [22, 23]. These findings suggest that cGAS–STING may represent a critical molecular link between diabetic metabolic stress and cardiac aging.

Ubiquitin‐specific protease 18 (USP18), originally identified as an ISG15 isopeptidase, is a well‐established regulator of type I and III interferon signaling [24]. Emerging evidence indicates that USP18 modulates innate immune activation by stabilizing STING through recruitment of the deubiquitinase USP20, thereby enhancing downstream inflammatory signaling [25]. Given that chronic inflammation and senescence are central to diabetic cardiac aging, USP18 represents a plausible upstream regulator linking T2DM‐associated metabolic stress to aberrant cGAS–STING activation.

Therefore, this study aimed to determine whether T2DM accelerates cardiac aging and to investigate the role of USP18 in modulating the cGAS–STING pathway during this process. In this study, we hypothesized that diabetes accelerates cardiac aging and aimed to elucidate the underlying molecular mechanisms. Using a high‐fat diet plus low‐dose streptozotocin mouse model and high‐glucose–treated cardiomyocytes, we assessed structural, functional, and molecular indicators of cardiac aging and examined whether USP18 contributes to diabetes‐induced acceleration of cardiac aging by regulating cGAS–STING–mediated inflammatory signaling. A comprehensive analysis of cardiac structure, function, and molecular alterations, including mRNA and protein expression, revealed distinct features of cardiac aging in diabetes‐affected hearts, such as myocardial fibrosis, myocardial hypertrophy, and diastolic dysfunction. These findings suggest an association between T2DM and cardiac aging. Therefore, this study aimed to investigate whether USP18 regulates the cGAS–STING pathway and contributes to cardiac aging in diabetes.

Methods

Animals

Approximately 40 male C57BL/6 J mice (6–8 weeks old) were obtained from Hunan SJA Laboratory Animal Co. Ltd. (Changsha, China) and housed in a specific pathogen‐free environment (humidity, 50 ± 5%; temperature, 20°C–22°C). The mice were divided into normal and high‐fat feeding groups. The normal group mice (n = 20) were fed a standard diet, and the high‐fat group mice (n = 20) were fed a high‐fat diet (60 kcal% fat; D12492, Research Diets) for 8 weeks. During the eighth week, they received intraperitoneal injections of low‐dose STZ (40 mg/kg) for five consecutive days to induce pancreatic β‐cell dysfunction. Mice were monitored weekly for fasting blood glucose levels with an Accu‐Chek Performa glucometer and were classified as diabetic if their fasting blood glucose levels were 11.1 mmol/L or above on three consecutive checks.

Ethics Statement

The Second Xiangya Hospital of Central South University Experimental Animal Welfare Ethics Committee approved all animal experiments in this study. (Approval no. 2025.0567).

Western Blotting

Total protein was extracted from cells and tissues using RIPA lysis buffer (P0013B, Beyotime Biotechnology, China), and protein concentrations were quantified using a bicinchoninic acid assay kit (CW00143, Jiangsu Cowin Biotech Co. Ltd., China). Protein samples were separated by SDS‐PAGE and transferred onto Immuno‐Blot PVDF membranes (IPVH00010, Millipore, USA). The membranes were blocked with 10% skim milk (AWB0004b, Abiowell, China) in Tris‐buffered saline with Tween‐20 (TBST) for 2 h at room temperature and then incubated overnight at 4°C with primary antibodies against USP18 (A16739, Abclonal, China), STING (AB288157, Abcam), cGAS (38418A17, ThermoFisher), IL‐1β (AB234437, Abcam), IL‐6 (AB259341, Abcam), p53 (80077‐1‐RR, Proteintech), p21 (28248‐1‐AP, Proteintech), TBK1 (AB40676, Abcam), pTBK1 (AWA56830, Abiowell), and GAPDH (60004‐1‐Ig, Proteintech). Following a TBST wash, the membranes were incubated at room temperature for 2 h with horseradish peroxidase‐conjugated secondary antibodies (anti‐mouse or anti‐rabbit). Protein bands were detected using an enhanced chemiluminescence detection reagent (PK10003, Proteintech, China). GAPDH served as a loading control to confirm equal protein loading across samples.

STING and USP18 were sequentially detected on the same membrane using a stripping and reprobing protocol, with GAPDH serving as the common internal control.

Immunofluorescence

Immunofluorescence staining was performed to detect USP18 and STING in mouse cardiac tissue sections. Overnight incubation of the sections at 4°C was performed using primary antibodies against USP18 (A16739, Abclonal; 1:200) and STING (AB288157, Abcam; 1:50). To control for nonspecific binding, isotype control antibodies were applied under identical conditions. Following washing, the sections were incubated for 45 min at 37°C with secondary antibodies diluted in TBST. Next, a tyramine salt solution was applied dropwise and incubated for 10 min at room temperature in the dark, followed by elution in a water bath for 20 min. To further reduce background interference, sections were blocked with 10% serum for 30 min at 37°C before undergoing a second round of primary and secondary antibody incubation under the same conditions. A second tyramine salt treatment was then performed for 10 min in the dark. After DAPI staining, the sections were mounted with fluorescent mounting medium and stored at 4°C in a light‐protected environment. Negative controls were prepared using nonspecific IgG antibodies. Imaging was conducted using an Olympus fluorescence microscope.

Cell Culture and Treatment

H9C2 cells (Procell Life Science & Technology Co. Ltd., China) were cultivated in Gibco's DMEM/F‐12 (1:1) medium, which was enriched with 10% fetal bovine serum, 1% penicillin‐streptomycin, and normal glucose (5.5 mmol/L, NG). Cells underwent treatment with normal glucose (5 mM) or high glucose (37.5 mM) for a duration of 96 h. All cultures were maintained at 37°C in a humidified atmosphere with 5% CO2.

RNA Extraction

Using TRIzol reagent (Invitrogen, CA, USA) and following the manufacturer's guidelines, total RNA was isolated from mouse heart tissues. The purity and concentration of the RNA were measured with a NanoDrop 2000 spectrophotometer (Thermo Scientific, USA), and RNA integrity was evaluated with an Agilent 2100 Bioanalyzer (Agilent Technologies, Santa Clara, CA, USA). Sequencing libraries were prepared using the VAHTS Universal V6 RNA‐seq Library Prep Kit following the manufacturer's protocol. Transcriptome sequencing and bioinformatic analyses were performed by OE Biotech Co. Ltd. (Shanghai, China).

RNA Sequencing and Analysis of Differentially Expressed Genes

The Illumina NovaSeq 6000 platform was used to conduct RNA sequencing. Significantly differentially expressed genes (DEGs) were identified through differential expression analysis using DESeq2, with criteria of a Q‐value less than 0.05 and a fold change either above 2 or below 0.5. To illustrate gene expression patterns across groups and samples, hierarchical clustering of DEGs was conducted using R (v3.2.0). A volcano plot displaying the top 10 upregulated and 10 downregulated DEGs was created using the ggradar package in R. Functional enrichment analysis was performed using Gene Ontology (GO) [26], Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) [27], Reactome, and WikiPathways. Significantly enriched terms were identified using a hypergeometric distribution. Bubble plots illustrating enrichment analysis results were generated using R (v3.2.0).

Quantitative Real‐Time

PCRGene expression was analyzed using a Bio‐Rad optical detection system (Bio‐Rad, Hercules, CA, USA) in conjunction with a SYBR Green Real‐Time PCR Kit (AG11701, Accurate Biology). β‐Actin served as the reference gene for normalization. The primers used were USP18 Forward, 5′‐GAGCTTGGATTTCAGTCAGGTT‐3′ and Reverse, 5′‐CATTGAAGCAGAACCACTTTCC‐3′; and β‐Actin Forward, 5′‐TACTGAGCTGCGTTTTACACC‐3′, and Reverse, 5′‐TCCTGAGTCAAAAGCGCCAA‐3′. The relative expression of target genes was calculated using the 2 − ΔΔCt method.

Statistical Analysis

All data are presented as mean ± standard error of the mean (SEM). GraphPad Prism 9.0 (GraphPad Inc., San Diego, CA, USA) was used for data visualization. Normality was assessed using the Shapiro–Wilk test. As no significant deviation from normality was detected in the datasets analyzed, parametric tests were applied. p < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

Diabetic State Accelerates Cardiac Aging‐Related Changes

Transthoracic echocardiography revealed substantial impairment in systolic performance in diabetic mice compared with healthy counterparts, as indicated by a marked reduction in left ventricular ejection fraction and fractional shortening (Figure 1A–C). Additionally, diastolic function was impaired in diabetic mice, characterized by an increased left ventricular internal diameter at end‐diastole, interventricular septal thickness at end‐diastole and Tei index. These findings, along with increased diastolic interventricular septal thickness and reduced left ventricular internal diameter at end‐diastole, suggest myocardial hypertrophy in diabetic mice (Figure 1D–F).

At the molecular level, the expression of senescence‐associated markers p21 and p53 was significantly upregulated in the cardiac tissues of diabetic mice (Figure 1G–I). Morphometric analysis further confirmed myocardial hypertrophy, as displayed by an increased cross‐sectional area of cardiomyocytes in diabetic mice compared with controls (Figure 1J). Gross morphological evaluation revealed an enlarged heart in diabetic mice, consistent with hypertrophic remodeling (Figure 1K). Furthermore, left ventricular collagen volume was increased in diabetic mice, indicating myocardial fibrosis (Figure 1L).

Collectively, these findings demonstrate that diabetic mice exhibit significant structural and functional alterations, including myocardial hypertrophy, diastolic dysfunction, and fibrosis, which are cardiac aging–like changes.

RNA Sequencing Reveals Key Genes and Pathways in Cardiac Aging in Diabetes

To identify pathogenic genes associated with diabetes‐induced acceleration of cardiac aging, bulk RNA‐seq was performed on cardiac tissues from diabetic and normal mice. Volcano plots and heat maps were generated to visualize DEGs (Figure 2A,B). The volcano plot highlighted significant DEGs between diabetic and normal mice (Figure 2A). The ten most upregulated genes were LOC115486416, Cyp4f15, Apol9a, Hmgcs2, Chrna2, Olfm2, Sfrp5, Ifi206, Card11, and Apol9b, whereas the ten most downregulated genes were Pah, Cyp1a1, Abat, Sv2c, Lrrc4b, Adcy1, Scgb1c1, Gm40652, Gnb3, and Hsbp1l1. GO analysis revealed that DEGs were significantly enriched in biological processes (BP), cellular components (CC), and molecular functions (MF). Specifically, BP terms included defense responses to viruses, cellular responses to interferon‐beta, and regulation of the circadian rhythm. CC analysis indicated enrichment in the collagen‐containing major histocompatibility complex class I peptide‐loading complex, cytosol, and Schaffer collateral–CA1 synapses. MF analysis highlighted 2′–5′ oligoadenylate synthetase activity, double‐stranded RNA binding, and catalytic activity (Figure 2C). KEGG pathway analysis further demonstrated enrichment in viral infectious diseases, immune system regulation, transport and catabolism, hepatitis C, and HIV‐1 infection (Figure 2D). These findings suggest that viral infections, immune responses, and inflammation play pivotal roles in diabetes‐induced cardiac aging.

The 30 most upregulated and downregulated genes were further investigated, revealing several genes linked to inflammation and aging, including Card11, Ifi206, Hmgcs2, Chrna2, Olfm2, and USP18. Among these candidates, USP18 was prioritized based on the following criteria: (1) its consistent upregulation at both transcriptomic and protein levels; (2) its established role in regulating innate immune signaling; and (3) prior evidence linking USP18 to STING‐dependent inflammatory pathways. These features suggested that USP18 may serve as a key upstream regulator connecting metabolic stress to inflammatory signaling.

USP18 Expression is Markedly Upregulated in the Diabetic Heart

Western blotting and immunofluorescence staining confirmed a significant upregulation of USP18 at the protein level in the hearts of diabetic mice compared with controls (Figure 3A–E), consistent with the findings of our bioinformatics analysis. Furthermore, qPCR demonstrated a marked upregulation of Usp18 mRNA in diabetic hearts (Figure 3F), further supporting its potential role in diabetes‐induced cardiac pathology.

USP18 is Associated With Enhanced Stability of the cGAS–STING Pathway and Activates Downstream Inflammatory and Aging‐Related Molecules in Diabetic Mice

The observed upregulation of USP18 in diabetic hearts prompted further investigation into its role in diabetes‐induced cardiac aging. Given the well‐established link between cardiac aging and inflammation [28], the initial focus was on inflammatory pathways. The cGAS‐STING pathway, which is associated with aging and inflammation [29], emerged as a potential mediator. Notably, as previously reported, USP18 promotes an innate antiviral response by deubiquitinating STING [25], suggesting that USP18 may contribute to diabetes‐induced acceleration of cardiac aging via the cGAS‐STING pathway. To explore this mechanism, we analyzed the expression of key cGAS‐STING pathway proteins in diabetic hearts. Western blotting revealed significant upregulation of cGAS and STING at the protein level in diabetic mice compared with controls (Figure 4A–D). Immunofluorescence staining further confirmed increased STING expression in diabetic hearts (Figure 4E,F). Additionally, phosphorylated TBK1 (Ser172; pTBK1), a key kinase downstream of STING, was upregulated in diabetic hearts, accompanied by increased IL‐6 and IL‐1β expression (Figure 4G–L).

To further validate the role of USP18 in diabetes‐induced acceleration of cardiac aging, we established an in vitro model using rat cardiomyocytes (H9C2) exposed to high glucose conditions to mimic diabetes. Western blotting revealed a significant upregulation of p53 at the protein level, a key senescence‐associated marker, in cells treated with high glucose (Figure 5A,B). In line with the in vivo findings, USP18 expression was markedly upregulated under high glucose conditions (Figure 5C,D). Furthermore, the cGAS‐STING pathway was triggered. Discussion

Compared with other DEGs, USP18 uniquely integrates interferon signaling, ubiquitin regulation, and innate immune activation, making it a compelling candidate for mechanistic investigation. Our data demonstrate that USP18 upregulation is associated with activation of the cGAS–STING pathway. Although the precise molecular mechanism remains to be fully elucidated, previous studies suggest that USP18 may regulate STING stability through deubiquitination‐dependent mechanisms [30]. Therefore, it is plausible that USP18 enhances cGAS–STING signaling by stabilizing STING in diabetic cardiomyocytes. However, direct evidence for this interaction was not provided in the present study and warrants further investigation. These findings reveal a novel mechanistic link between metabolic stress and innate immune activation in the aging diabetic heart.

Growing evidence indicates that cardiac aging is accelerated in diabetes [31]. However, the precise mechanisms underlying diabetes‐induced acceleration of cardiac aging remain incompletely understood. Prior studies have suggested that oxidative stress, mitochondrial dysfunction, AGEs, and chronic low‐grade inflammation create a pro‐senescent microenvironment in the diabetic myocardium [7, 32, 33]. Our data confirm these pathological features and extend current understanding by identifying USP18 as a critical upstream regulator connecting hyperglycemia to innate immune activation. The robust elevation of USP18 at both transcriptional and protein levels in diabetic myocardium suggests that metabolic stress may induce USP18 expression as part of a maladaptive antiviral‐like response, a concept supported by recent literature describing metabolic–immune crosstalk in aging tissues.

A central finding of this work is that USP18 activation coincides with pronounced stimulation of the cGAS–STING pathway in diabetic hearts. Although cGAS–STING signaling is established as a driver of inflammaging in neurodegeneration, vascular disease, and diabetic retinopathy, direct evidence linking this pathway to cardiac aging has been limited. Our study provides in vivo and in vitro evidence that hyperglycemia activates cGAS–STING signaling in cardiomyocytes, accompanied by increased TBK1 phosphorylation and the secretion of SASP cytokines such as IL‐6 and IL‐1β, which are key components of the senescence‐associated secretory phenotype (SASP), which may reinforce senescence through paracrine signaling. Given that mitochondrial damage and nuclear DNA leakage are hallmarks of diabetes‐induced cardiac stress, cytosolic DNA accumulation has been proposed as a potential trigger for cGAS activation. However, cytosolic DNA accumulation was not directly assessed in the present study, and its contribution to cGAS–STING activation in diabetic cardiomyocytes requires further investigation. In this context, USP18‐mediated stabilization of the cGAS–STING axis may represent a potential mechanism by which metabolic stress is linked to sustained inflammatory signaling and cardiomyocyte senescence.

Although prior studies indicate that USP18 can modulate STING activity by recruiting USP20 and regulating ISG15 conjugation [25], our findings raise the possibility that USP18 directly enhances cGAS–STING stability in diabetic cardiomyocytes. Despite previous studies have shown that USP18 can regulate STING activity via recruitment of USP20, our data do not distinguish whether USP18 directly deubiquitinates cGAS or STING, or functions indirectly through ISGylation or other regulatory pathways. Therefore, the precise molecular substrate of USP18 in this context remains to be defined. Future studies involving ubiquitination assays, co‐immunoprecipitation, and loss‐of‐function models will be necessary to determine whether USP18 directly targets cGAS, STING, or upstream regulators. Notably, although USP18 represents a potential therapeutic target, its systemic inhibition may carry risks. USP18 is a critical regulator of interferon signaling and antiviral immunity, and its dysregulation may lead to impaired host defense or excessive immune activation. Furthermore, a multidimensional regulatory relationship exists between USP18 and STING, wherein USP18 modulates STING stability and function via distinct mechanisms across various pathological contexts. For example, in the tumor immune microenvironment (TIME): STING is frequently stabilized by ISGylation. As a major deISGylating enzyme, USP18 removes the ISG15 moiety from STING, thereby facilitating its degradation and subsequently impairing anti‐tumor immunity [34]. In acute lung injury (ALI), IFIT3 functions as a scaffold protein that bridges the interaction between USP18 and STING. This recruited USP18‐STING complex enhances STING stability, which amplifies pro‐inflammatory signaling and exacerbates M1 macrophage polarization and pulmonary damage [35]. In sepsis‐induced acute kidney injury (SI‐AKI): USP18 directly interacts with STING to mediate its deubiquitination. This stabilization of STING promotes ferroptosis and triggers a robust inflammatory response [30].

Therefore, future therapeutic strategies targeting USP18 should consider tissue‐specific modulation or pathway‐selective interventions to minimize potential adverse effects.

This study has several additional limitations. H9C2 cells, although widely used, are derived from embryonic rat myocardium and lack key structural and metabolic characteristics of adult cardiomyocytes, including mature contractile organization, electrophysiological properties, and fatty acid–dependent metabolism. Therefore, caution should be exercised when extrapolating these findings to adult or human cardiac physiology. Future validation using primary cardiomyocytes or human iPSC‐derived cardiomyocytes will be essential to strengthen translational relevance. Moreover, while cGAS–STING activation correlates strongly with senescence markers in our models, causality should be further validated using pharmacological or genetic inhibition of this pathway. Nonetheless, our findings provide a compelling rationale for targeting USP18–cGAS–STING signaling as a therapeutic strategy for diabetes‐associated cardiac aging.

In summary, this study uncovers a novel role of USP18 as an upstream regulator of cGAS–STING–mediated inflammatory senescence in the diabetic heart. By linking metabolic stress to innate immune activation, USP18 may represent a promising target for preventing or alleviating cardiovascular aging in patients with diabetes.

Conclusion

In conclusion, this study identifies USP18 as a novel regulator of diabetes‐induced acceleration of cardiac aging, linking metabolic stress to activation of the cGAS–STING pathway and inflammatory senescence. Our findings provide evidence that hyperglycemia‐induced upregulation of USP18 is associated with enhanced innate immune signaling and cardiac remodeling. Although the precise molecular mechanisms require further investigation, these results highlight USP18 as a potential therapeutic target for mitigating cardiac aging in diabetes. Future studies focusing on mechanistic validation and translational models will be essential to advance these findings toward clinical application.
