Prompting Strategies for Enabling Large Language Models to Infer Causation from Correlation
Eleni Sgouritsa, Virginia Aglietti, Yee Whye Teh, Arnaud Doucet, Arthur Gretton, Silvia Chiappa

Abstract
The reasoning abilities of Large Language Models ( LLM s) are attracting increasing attention. In this work, we focus on causal reasoning and address the task of establishing causal relationships based on correlation information, a highly challenging problem on which several LLM s have shown poor performance. We introduce a prompting strategy for this problem that breaks the original task into fixed subquestions, with each subquestion corresponding to one step of a formal causal discovery algorithm, the PC algorithm. The proposed prompting strategy, PC-SubQ , guides the LLM to follow these algorithmic steps, by sequentially prompting it with one subquestion at a time, augmenting the next subquestion’s prompt with the answer to the previous one(s). We evaluate our approach on an existing causal benchmark, Corr2Cause : our experiments indicate a performance improvement across five LLM s when comparing PC-SubQ to baseline prompting strategies. Results are robust to causal query perturbations, when modifying the variable names or paraphrasing the expressions.

Introduction

Advances in scaling Large Language Models (
LLM
s) and training data sizes have led to unprecedented capabilities across a wide range of tasks.
Several works
(
Huang & Chang, 2023
;
Sun et al., 2023
)
focus on enhancing
LLM
s on various reasoning tasks, such as arithmetic, commonsense, logical or causal reasoning.
It is often argued that the ability to demonstrate reasoning cannot be solely overcome by increasing model scale
(
Rae et al., 2021
,
inter alia
)
.
An active area of research is therefore investigating prompting approaches in order to elicit and enhance reasoning in
LLM
s
(
Qiao et al., 2023
)
.
Wei et al. (2022)
proposed Chain-of-Thought (
CoT
) prompting, where a language model is prompted to generate a series of thoughts that mimic the reasoning process a person might employ when solving a task. For settings in which the problem to solve is harder than the demonstration examples, various works suggest to decompose the original complex task into simpler subtasks that are easier to solve
(
Zhou et al., 2023a
;
Khot et al., 2023
;
Dua et al., 2022
)
.

Among the different reasoning capabilities, the ability to reason about cause and effect is fundamental to human intelligence, playing a pivotal role in scientific understanding and accurate decision-making.
Most existing research on causal reasoning and
LLM
s investigates whether they can serve as a knowledge base for causal relationships
(
Kıcıman et al., 2023
;
Willig et al., 2022
;
Zečević et al., 2023
;
Long et al., 2022
)
, thus assessing the
commonsense reasoning
capabilities of
LLM
s.
More recent work
(
Jin et al., 2023
;
Jin et al., 2024
)
focuses, instead, on their
formal (pure) causal reasoning
skills, i.e., their ability to reason using known procedures and formal rules from the causal inference literature
(
Pearl, 2000
;
Pearl & Mackenzie, 2018
;
Spirtes et al., 2000
)
. One such ability, studied in
Jin et al. (2024)
, is that of
inferring causal relationships from correlation statements
. In this case,
correlations or conditional independencies about a set of variables are given in natural language, for instance, “Ice cream sales correlate with shark attacks”, and the validity of a causal relationship between a pair of variables is inferred, for instance, whether “Ice cream sales cause shark attacks”. This is an important skill for
LLM
s as the training corpus may not directly contain certain causal relationships between variables, but instead events’ co-occurrence or correlation statements. To this end,
Jin et al. (2024)
formulated
Corr2Cause
, a causal benchmark that assesses this ability, on which they showed
LLM
s to perform poorly.

Expanding the task of inferring causal relationships from correlation statements, one could more generally
infer the causal structure from correlation statements
, that is, identify the entire
structure instead of a specific causal relationship between a pair of variables.
We refer to this task as
Natural Language Causal Discovery
(
NL-CD
)
to contrast it with standard/statistical
Causal Discovery
(
S-CD
) considered in the statistical causality literature
(
Pearl, 2000
;
Pearl & Mackenzie, 2018
;
Spirtes et al., 2000
)
, that identifies the causal graph based on observed data.
NL-CD
, instead, is an
LLM
task that is based on natural language correlation statements.
Even though there exist known statistical algorithms for certain causal tasks, our goal is to instead investigate the ability of
LLM
s to solve such tasks. This motivation is also shared in
Jin et al. (2024)
and
Jin et al. (2023)
for causal tasks, in
Markeeva et al. (2024)
and
Veličković et al. (2022)
for algorithmic tasks and in other works for arithmetic tasks.
In this work, we address
NL-CD
by introducing a prompting strategy,
PC-SubQ
.
Inspired by prompting approaches that decompose a complex task into simpler ones,
PC-SubQ
leverages one of the most widely used algorithms for
S-CD
, the
PC
algorithm
(
Spirtes et al., 2000
)
, in order to decompose the
NL-CD
task.
In particular,
PC-SubQ
decomposes the original task into several
fixed
subtasks, each corresponding to one step of the
PC
algorithm. We guide the
LLM
to follow these
PC
steps by having few-shot examples for each subtask and sequentially prompting the
LLM
, augmenting the next subtask with the answer(s) to the previous one(s). Given the final inferred graph, one can subsequently pose various causal queries, such as the ones included in
Corr2Cause
.
We assess
PC-SubQ
on
Corr2Cause
and consistently observe improved performance compared to existing prompting strategies across five
LLM
s.
Moreover, unlike models fine-tuned on
Corr2Cause

(
Jin et al., 2024
)
,
LLM
s’ performance using
PC-SubQ
is robust to query perturbations: even though the few-shot examples are based on
Corr2Cause
textual expressions, performance does not drop when variable names are modified or expressions are paraphrased.
In summary, our main contributions are:

•

We propose a prompting strategy that addresses
NL-CD
by guiding the
LLM
to reason through applying the
PC
algorithm steps, thus also offering transparent and interpretable reasoning steps.

•

We evaluate
PC-SubQ
on
Corr2Cause
and show that it outperforms a range of widely used prompting strategies across five
LLM
s.

•

We show that
PC-SubQ
is robust to causal query perturbations in terms of variable renaming and paraphrasing, and can be applied unchanged to examples with natural stories.

Related Work

Causal reasoning and
LLM
s

There is an increasing attention on the strengths and limitations of
LLM
s on causal tasks
(
Kıcıman et al., 2023
;
Willig et al., 2022
;
Zečević et al., 2023
;
Zhang et al., 2023
)
.
A large body of existing literature assesses how well
LLM
s capture commonsense and domain knowledge about causal relations
(
Kıcıman et al., 2023
;
Long et al., 2022
;
Zhang et al., 2023
;
Tu et al., 2023
;
Vashishtha et al., 2023
;
Jiralerspong et al., 2024
)
.
Consider, for example, the BIG-bench Cause and Effect task of deciding which of the two events, "The driver turned the wipers on" and "It started raining", caused the other
(
Authors, 2023
)
. This question could be answered by using commonsense knowledge about the purpose of wipers.
Commonsense knowledge captured by
LLM
s can be used alongside existing causal methods as a proxy for human domain knowledge
(
Kıcıman et al., 2023
;
Long et al., 2022
;
Long et al., 2023
)
. Indeed, recent studies suggest to improve
S-CD
methods either by incorporating
LLM
s’ imperfect knowledge about causal relationships
(
Long et al., 2023
;
Abdulaal et al., 2024
)
or through providing an
LLM
-based oracle of conditional independence relations as input to the
PC
algorithm
(
Cohrs et al., 2024
)
.
On a more critical perspective,
Willig et al. (2022)
and
Zečević et al. (2023)
argue that
LLM
s are just “causal parrots” which recite the causal knowledge embedded in the training data, and so we cannot expect any sort of generalisation.

Another way of inferring causal relationships is through formal causal reasoning, that is, by employing known rules and algorithms from the causal inference literature
(
Pearl, 2000
;
Spirtes et al., 2000
)
to reach a causal conclusion. In this case, the inference rules hold independently of the specific instantiation of the variables, as opposed to commonsense reasoning which highly relies on the semantics of the variable names.

Jin et al. (2024)
and
Jin et al. (2023)
analyse the difference between these two reasoning abilities, i.e., commonsense and formal causal reasoning, and introduce benchmarks,
Corr2Cause
and
CLadder
, respectively, to test the formal causal reasoning abilities of
LLM
s. Specifically,
Corr2Cause
consists of queries about causal relationships given correlation statements, while
CLadder
asks causal inference queries across all rungs of the Ladder of Causation
(
Pearl & Mackenzie, 2018
)
for a given underlying causal graph. Both works observe that it is highly challenging for a range of
LLM
s to answer these causal queries.
In particular,
Jin et al. (2024)
mainly evaluate off-the-shelf or fine-tuned
LLM
s on
Corr2Cause
and find them to perform poorly or not to be robust to causal query perturbations.
We instead propose a multi-step prompting strategy (
PC-SubQ
) that is robust to causal query perturbations, produces interpretable reasoning steps and does not require
LLM
retraining.

Prompting strategies

Since the popularization of few-shot prompting
(
Brown et al., 2020
)
, there has been a lot of follow-up work on prompting strategies. In Chain-of-Thought (
CoT
) prompting
(
Wei et al., 2022
)
, a few chain of thought demonstrations are provided as examples in the prompt. This significantly improves the ability of
LLM
s to perform complex reasoning on a range of tasks, with examples of surpassing even fine-tuned models.
To address more complicated tasks, several follow-up works suggest to decompose the original complex task into simpler sub-tasks that are easier to solve
(
Zhou et al., 2023a
;
Khot et al., 2023
;
Dua et al., 2022
)
.
Least-to-most prompting
(
Zhou et al., 2023a
)
solves these simpler problems in sequence, with each subproblem being facilitated by previously solved ones.
Unlike least-to-most prompting that infers the subquestions using an appropriate prompt, we instead design a
fixed
set of subquestions that is used unchanged across the whole dataset.
For that, we take advantage of the fact that there is a common underlying (causal) algorithm that can be followed to solve all causal queries, so we design the subproblems accordingly to correspond to the
PC
steps.

Jin et al. (2023)
use a prompting strategy comprising of a chain of subquestions without demonstrations, in order to answer various causal query types based on a given causal graph.
Unlike the above works, we do not carry over to the next subquestion all history of previous ones, but propagate much less information (usually just the final answer to the previous subquestion) in order to save context length and guide the
LLM
to focus on the most relevant parts of information. Finally, our work connects to literature on algorithmic reasoning
(
Zhou et al., 2023b
;
Sel et al., 2023
;
Nye et al., 2021
)
.
Zhou et al. (2023b)
use an algorithmic prompt to unlock algorithmic reasoning abilities in
LLM
s.

Preliminaries

Graph definitions

A graph
{\cal G}\coloneqq\langle\bm{X},\bm{E}\rangle
consists of a set of nodes
\bm{X}
and a set of edges
\bm{E}
connecting the nodes. An
undirected graph
consists of undirected edges denoted as
X-Y
, while a
directed graph
has directed edges denoted as
X\rightarrow Y
. A
path
from
X
to
Y
is a sequence of distinct nodes
(X,\ldots,Y)
in which there is an edge between every pair of successive nodes. A
directed path
is a path whose edges are directed and pointing from preceding towards following nodes. A
Directed Acyclic Graph
(
DAG
) is a directed graph with no cycles, i.e. no directed paths starting and ending at the same node. The
skeleton
of a
DAG
is the undirected graph resulting from ignoring all arrowheads.
When
X\rightarrow Y
,
X
is called the
parent
of
Y
and, consequently,
Y
the
child
of
X
. A
collider
is a node where at least two arrowheads meet, for example, in the path
(X,Z,Y)
,
Z
is a collider if
X\rightarrow Z
and
Y\rightarrow Z
. A
v-structure
consists of two edges whose arrows point to the same node and whose tails are not connected by an edge: for example, the path
(X,Z,Y)
forms a v-structure if
X\rightarrow Z
,
Y\rightarrow Z
and
X
and
Y
are not connected.
A path between two nodes is said to be
unblocked
conditioned on a set of nodes
\bm{Z}
, if for every collider
W
in the path, either
W
or a descendant of
W
is in
\bm{Z}
and no non-collider in the path is in
\bm{Z}
. A
blocked
path is a path that is not unblocked. Two disjoint sets of nodes
\bm{A}
and
\bm{B}
are said to be
d-separated
given a set of node
\bm{Z}
if every path between any node in
\bm{A}
and any node in
\bm{B}
is blocked conditioned on
\bm{Z}
.

Markov condition

Consider the nodes in a
DAG

\cal{G}
representing random variables with
P(\bm{X})
denoting their joint distribution.

1

For simplicity, we henceforth refer to a
DAG
with its nodes representing random variables jointly distributed according to
P(\bm{X})
simply as a
DAG
.
The joint distribution is
Markov
to
\cal{G}
if the following condition, called the
Markov condition
, holds for all
\bm{A},\bm{B},\bm{Z}
:

\bm{A}
,
\bm{B}
d-separated given
\bm{Z}
in
\cal{G}

\Rightarrow

\bm{A}\perp\!\!\!\!\perp\bm{B}\,|\,\bm{Z}
.
In other words, the Markov condition entails a set of conditional independence relations which can be found by the d-separations encoded in the graph. Two
DAG
s are called Markov equivalent or alternatively said to belong to the same Markov Equivalence Class (
MEC
) if they entail the same set of conditional independence relations and can be compactly represented as a partially directed graph, so-called Completed Partially Directed Acyclic Graph (
CPDAG
).

Causal discovery

A causal
DAG
is a
DAG
in which edges represent causal influence, with
X\rightarrow Y
denoting that
X
is a direct cause of
Y
.
Causal Discovery (
CD
) is the task of (possibly partially) learning the causal
DAG
between a set of variables based on a data sample from their joint distribution.
CD
methods can be categorised into constraint-based
(
Spirtes et al., 2000
)
, score-based-methods
(
Chickering, 2002
)
and methods restricting the function class
(
Shimizu et al., 2006
;
Hoyer et al., 2008
;
Peters et al., 2014
)
. Constraint-based methods rely on conditional independence tests between the variables
\bm{X}
in order to rule-out edges in the constructed
DAG
, assuming faithfulness, that is that the only conditional independencies are those implied by d-separation
(
Spirtes et al., 2000
)
. The Peter-Clark (
PC
) algorithm
(
Spirtes et al., 2000
)
is one of the most widely used constraint-based methods, that assumes no unobserved confounders (direct common causes of two or more measured variables).
Its final output is a
CPDAG
or equivalently a
MEC
, including
DAG
s, entailing the same set of conditional independence relations, which cannot be further distinguished without additional assumptions.

.

correlates with

. However,
A
is independent of
B
.
Hypothesis
:
A

.

Output Label
:

The benchmark is constructed as follows. First, all unique
DAG
s with
N=2,\ldots,6
nodes are generated. For every

DAG
s are clustered into

Given a set of conditional independencies and a causal hypothesis as input, the output label represents the validity of the hypothesis and is generated as follows: if all

Everything is, then, verbalised into natural language, with the verbalised sets of conditional independencies comprising the

LLM

Proposed Prompting Strategy:
PC-SubQ

We address the
NL-CD
task of inferring causal structure from correlation statements.
Prompting an
LLM
to decompose a complex problem into simpler ones was recently shown to improve performance
(
Zhou et al., 2023a
;
Khot et al., 2023
;
Dua et al., 2022
)
. Inspired by this, we propose a prompting strategy,
PC-SubQ
, which exploits the steps of a popular
CD
algorithm, the
PC
algorithm
(
Spirtes et al., 2000
)
, in order to decompose the
NL-CD
task.
In particular, we break the original task into several
fixed
subquestions, each corresponding to one step of the
PC
algorithm (Fig.
1
).
We explicitly guide the
LLM
to follow these steps, by sequentially prompting the
LLM
with one
PC
subquestion at a time, augmenting the next subquestion’s prompt with the answer(s) to the previous one(s).
Few-shot
CoT
examples
(
Wei et al., 2022
)
are provided for every subquestion with demonstrations of how to solve this specific subquestion (Fig.
2
). Subquestions and corresponding few-shots examples are used unchanged across all
NL-CD
queries, since
PC
is a common algorithm that can be followed to solve all such causal queries.

In the following, we first provide the steps of the
PC
algorithm
(
Spirtes et al., 2000
)
and then their correspondence to our subquestions:

0.

Initialise fully connected graph:

Construct a complete undirected graph of all variables.

1.

Construct skeleton:
Eliminate as many edges as possible using conditional independence relations: remove edge
X-Y
if
X
is independent of
Y
given some conditioning set
Z
.

2.

Orient edges to form v-structures:
For all paths
(X,Z,Y)
, such that edge
X-Y
is absent and
Z
is not in any conditioning set that makes
X
and
Y
independent: orient arrows such that a v-structure is created, i.e.,
X\rightarrow Z
and
Y\rightarrow Z
.

3.

Orient edges to not form extra v-structures:
Possibly orient some more edges such that no extra v-structures are introduced: For paths
(X,Z,Y)
with only one of the two edges oriented towards
Z
, orient the other edge away from
Z
.

The correspondence between subquestions (
SubQ
) and
PC
algorithm steps is as follows:
SubQ
1 refers to step 0 of the
PC
algorithm,
SubQ
2 to step 1,
SubQ
3-6 to step 2 and
SubQ
7 to step 3. The third step of the
PC
algorithm, orienting v-structures, is the most complicated one, thus it was split into more fine-grained steps corresponding to four subquestions.
Overall,
SubQ
1-7 correspond to the four steps of the
PC
algorithm, and aim to guide the
LLM
to infer the (possibly partially directed) causal graph.
The last
SubQ
8 questions, instead, the validity of the causal hypothesis between a pair of variables given the causal graph inferred from the previous seven subquestions.
We prepend few-shot examples, each with a
CoT
reasoning, to each subquestion. We use 1-4 shots per subquestion except for
SubQ
8 that uses 11 shots with diverse examples covering a range of possible causal hypotheses.

Notice that the few-shot

subquestion and not any of the previous ones. This allows the demonstrations for two subquestions to not necessarily refer to the same example query: for instance, in Fig.

Robustness analysis

In the following, we analyse the robustness of
PC-SubQ
to causal query perturbations.

Jin et al. (2024)
designed two robusteness tests, variable refactorization and paraphrasing, with the purpose of determining whether the high performance of their fine-tuned models was only due to overfitting on the
Corr2Cause
training data. For variable refactorization, they modified the variable names from
A,B,C,\ldots
to
Z,Y,X,\dots
, while for paraphrasing, they changed the expression describing the causal relation to a semantically equivalent alternative (e.g., "causes" to "affects"). Running the fine-tuned models on refactored and paraphrased
Corr2Cause
test sets, they observed a drastic performance drop, showing that these models had not robustly learned the causal skill as they failed to generalize out-of-distribution. Overfitting is not an issue in our case, since we do not retrain the
LLM
s. Nevertheless, inspired by this robustness analysis, we check robustness by perturbing
PC-SubQ
prompts such that they differ from the causal queries in the test dataset (see Appendix
C
for an example). In particular, we modify
PC-SubQ
few-shots and subquestions in terms of variable refactoring and paraphrasing, while keeping the test set (
[Premise]
and
[Hypothesis]
) fixed.
Figure
5
(left) depicts the corresponding results using PaLM 2. Remarkably, the performance does not drop at all in case of refactoring and only slightly drops in case of paraphrasing.

Finally, we performed one more robustness test. One could imagine further scenarios of variable refactoring or paraphrasing by using natural instead of symbolic variables names, for instance, "Paracetamol is correlated with the capacity to empathize" instead of "
A
correlates with
B
".
For tasks involving natural variable names, notice that it may be non trivial to determine whether the
LLM
used formal causal reasoning or commonsense knowledge to reach its answer. Nevertheless, these natural examples could be considered more appealing and of wider interest.
Indeed,
Jin et al. (2024)
envision future extensions of the
Corr2Cause
dataset to various settings
and provide a case study for how to generate more natural stories, with two such stories provided in their Appendix. Running PaLM 2 with
PC-SubQ
(keeping the subquestions and few-shots fixed, containing only symbolic variable names) on these two natural stories produces correct reasoning traces and answers, as shown in Figure
5
(right). Only the output of the final
SubQ
8 is depicted, while the output of all subquestions can be found in Appendix
D
. Note that in both examples the reasoning and final answers are correct even though we did not provide any natural story examples as few-shots.

6
Discussion and Conclusion

We introduced
PC-SubQ
, a prompting strategy that enables
LLM
s to improve their performance on the task of inferring causal relationships from correlation statements.

PC-SubQ
decomposes the original
NL-CD
task into several
fixed
subquestions, corresponding to the
PC
algorithm steps, which compose the prompt chain given to an
LLM
.
Evaluated on
Corr2Cause
,
PC-SubQ
outperforms a range of widely used prompting strategies across five
LLM
s. Furthermore, performance is robust to different variable instantiations or paraphrased causal queries. Remarkably, applied to examples with natural stories,
PC-SubQ
produces correct reasoning traces and answers, even though few-shot demonstrations only contain examples with symbolic variable names.
Additionally, by guiding the
LLM
to reason through applying algorithmic steps, we gain more transparency and interpretability of the results: for instance, it is possible to trace which steps are responsible for an erroneous final answer by inspecting the output.
We provide such an example in Appendix
E
in which the erroneous final answer is only due to an error in the reasoning of the final
SubQ
8 (confusing a directed edge with an undirected one), while the inferred graph of
SubQ
7 and all previous reasoning traces and answers up to
SubQ
7 are correct.
The proposed approach could be more generally applicable to address tasks that have a common underlying algorithm which can be applied to all examples to solve the task: each subquestion would then correspond to a step of this shared algorithm.
Finally, as with all prompting frameworks, there is no need to fine-tune a model using several thousands of annotated examples; instead, only a few (often less than 10) demonstrations are enough.

Our work has the following limitations. Current evaluations refer only to the output of the final
SubQ
8, for which we have ground truth available.
Intermediate evaluations would be desirable in order to quantitatively assess intermediate answers, instead of only qualitatively inspecting the reasoning traces (as in Appendix
E
).
It would be particularly interesting to have evaluations for the output of
SubQ
7, i.e., the inferred causal graph. Nevertheless, since the causal hypotheses of
Corr2Cause
cover causal relationships between
every
pair of variables, current evaluations should be representative of the quality of the inferred graph.
Additionally, compared to few-shot prompting that requires only one
LLM
call, the proposed chain of prompts induces increased inference time due to the 16
LLM
calls, two per subquestion for the reasoning and answer parts, needed. Future work should thus focus on making the strategy more efficient.
Furthermore, when the task complexity increases, as measured by the number of variables, performance is affected. This limitation might be mitigated for natural stories, since they often do not refer to more than 3 or 4 variables.
Indeed, our results with such natural scenarios are very promising. Areas of future research could include constructing more
NL-CD
benchmarks with natural stories, beyond the two available ones, on which to employ
PC-SubQ
.
The formal causal reasoning of
PC-SubQ
could be further combined with the commonsense knowledge captured by
LLM
s for these scenarios.
Finally, an alternative path to tackle
NL-CD
could be via Tool Use, by interrupting the
LLM
reasoning process and calling a registered Python tool
(
Yao et al., 2023
;
Parisi et al., 2022
)
, with the
PC
algorithm implemented. Notice that the
LLM
would still be required to understand the task in order to appropriately convert the input for the Python function and decide when to call it. We think of these two paths as complementary: as with other tasks, such as the ability to multiply two numbers or solve a math or physics problem, it is desirable for the
LLM
to have certain skills and not solely rely on external tools, which also may not be always available.
