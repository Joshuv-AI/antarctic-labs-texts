An explicit exotic representation of a rank-one simple Lie group via convex bodies
François Fillastre, Yusen Long, David Xu

Abstract
In [ DP12 ] , Delzant and Py showed that there exist continuous irreducible isometric actions of PSL 2 ⁡ ( ℝ ) \operatorname{PSL}_{2}(\mathbb{R}) on the infinite-dimensional hyperbolic space ℍ ∞ \mathbb{H}^{\infty} . Such continuous irreducible actions do not exist on the hyperbolic spaces ℍ n \mathbb{H}^{n} when n > 2 n>2 and their associated embeddings ℍ 2 → ℍ ∞ \mathbb{H}^{2}\to\mathbb{H}^{\infty} given by the orbit maps were later called exotic by Monod and Py in [ MP14 ] . In this article, we produce a continuous and irreducible representation of PSL 2 ⁡ ( ℝ ) → Isom ⁡ ( ℍ ∞ ) \operatorname{PSL}_{2}(\mathbb{R})\to\Isom(\mathbb{H}^{\infty}) using the hyperbolic model for convex bodies introduced in [ DF22 ] . This yields a convex cocompact PSL 2 ⁡ ( ℝ ) \operatorname{PSL}_{2}(\mathbb{R}) -action on the infinite-dimensional hyperbolic space ℍ ∞ \mathbb{H}^{\infty} , of which the compact quotient over the minimal PSL 2 ⁡ ( ℝ ) \operatorname{PSL}_{2}(\mathbb{R}) -invariant convex set is homeomorphic to the 2-dimensional oriented Banach–Mazur compactum. Moreover, we study the geometry of one of its orbit maps and compute the Hausdorff dimension of the limit set of this representation.

Introduction

Let
\mathbb{H}^{n}
be the
n
-dimensional real hyperbolic space for
n\geq 1
. With a theorem of Karpelevich and Mostow
[
Kar53
,
Mos55
]
(or see
[
BZ04
]
for a geometric proof in the hyperbolic cases), one can conclude the following fact:
any continuous isometric action of the group
\Isom(\mathbb{H}^{n})
on
\mathbb{H}^{m}
, for
0<n\leq m<\infty
, preserves the image of a totally geodesic embedding
\mathbb{H}^{n}\hookrightarrow\mathbb{H}^{m}\,.
However, this conclusion no longer holds if
m=\infty
.

In
[
BIM05
]
, Burger, Iozzi and Monod first constructed a family, parametrised by
0<t<1
, of continuous representations
\rho_{t}^{T}\colon\mathrm{Aut}(T)\to\Isom(\mathbb{H}^{\infty})
, where
T
is a separable simplicial tree. This can be seen as realised by an
\mathrm{Aut}(T)
-equivariant quasi-isometric embedding
T\hookrightarrow\mathbb{H}^{\infty}
. More than a decade later, this construction was generalised to any real tree by
[
DSU17
, §13.1]
and
[
Mon20
]
, thus also providing a family of continuous representations
\Isom(\mathbb{H}^{1})\simeq\Isom(\mathbb{R})\to\Isom(\mathbb{H}^{\infty})
for
0<t<1
, which do not preserve a bi-infinite geodesic line in
\mathbb{H}^{\infty}
. Similar phenomena for the hyperbolic plane
\mathbb{H}^{2}
have also been observed in
[
DP12
]
, where Delzant and Py built a family of continuous representations
\rho_{t}\colon\Isom(\mathbb{H}^{2})\to\Isom(\mathbb{H}^{\infty})
indexed by
0<t<1
that are irreducible,
i.e.
the
\rho_{t}
do not preserve any proper hyperbolic subspace of
\mathbb{H}^{\infty}
. Their construction was then generalised by Monod and Py in
[
MP14
]
to obtain continuous irreducible representations
\Isom(\mathbb{H}^{n})\to\Isom(\mathbb{H}^{\infty})
. In the sequel, we will call a continuous irreducible representation of this kind an
exotic representation
. Later, by introducing the notion of
kernel of hyperbolic type
, Monod and Py were able to produce exotic representations
\Isom(\mathbb{H}^{\infty})\to\Isom(\mathbb{H}^{\infty})

[
MP19
]
. Moreover, in
[
MP14
,
MP19
]
, it is shown that the examples above actually give the complete list of exotic representations
\Isom(\mathbb{H}^{n})\to\Isom(\mathbb{H}^{\infty})
for
2\leq n\leq\infty
up to conjugacy in
\Isom(\mathbb{H}^{\infty})
.

The main topic of this article will be an explicit exotic representation

\Isom(\mathbb{H}^{2})\simeq\operatorname{PSL}_{2}(\mathbb{R})\to\Isom(\mathbb{H}^{\infty})~.

In
[
DP12
]
, Delzant and Py studied the
\operatorname{PSL}_{2}(\mathbb{R})
-action on
L^{2}(\mathcal{S}^{1})
, where here
\mathcal{S}^{1}
is identified with the boundary at infinity of the hyperbolic plane (compare with our action in Section

\operatorname{PSL}_{2}(\mathbb{R})
-action, that allow them to build a hyperbolic space of infinite dimension and induce a family of irreducible representations
\operatorname{PSL}_{2}(\mathbb{R})\to\Isom(\mathbb{H}^{\infty})
. See §
4.3

In the present article, we will present another construction of an exotic representation

\rho:\operatorname{PSL}_{2}(\mathbb{R})\to\Isom(\mathbb{H}^{\infty})

that is different from the Delzant–Py construction or the Monod–Py construction (see §
4.3
). It is based on a hyperbolic model for convex bodies that was first introduced by Debin and the first author
[
DF22
]
and later studied by the second author
[
Lon25
]
for infinite-dimensional cases. It will appear that our
\rho
is conjugate to one of the representations they introduced.

Let us briefly explain our construction of
\rho
. The area of symmetric convex bodies in the Euclidean plane can be polarised to obtain the so-called
mixed area
, that satisfies the
Minkowski inequality
, which resembles the reversed Cauchy–Schwarz inequality, see Remark
2.11
. Identifying convex bodies with their support functions, the mixed area can be extended to a bilinear form over a Sobolev space, and the Poincaré–Wirtinger inequality says that it has a Lorentzian signature (Lemma
2.1
). In this model, the plane symmetric convex bodies with given constant area are naturally identified to a subset
C_{\mathcal{K}}
of an infinite-dimensional hyperbolic space
\mathbb{H}^{\infty}
. In particular, this gives
a map from the space
\mathcal{E}_{c}^{\pi}
of symmetric ellipses with fixed area
\pi
to
\mathbb{H}^{\infty}
. The space
\mathcal{E}_{c}^{\pi}
is the
\operatorname{PSL}_{2}(\mathbb{R})
orbit of the centred unit disc, hence we may identify
\mathcal{E}_{c}^{\pi}
and

to obtain a map

\iota\colon\mathbb{H}^{2}\simeq\mathcal{E}^{\pi}_{c}\hookrightarrow\mathbb{H}^{\infty}

that enjoys some striking geometric properties:

.

The map

Point
(T5)
seems to be a folklore result, but we are not aware of any proof. Here it is an immediate consequence of our interpretation of
\iota(\mathbb{H}^{2})
as a space of ellipses.
Indeed, it is well known that a finite sum of cardinality at least two of non-homothetic ellipses is never an ellipse, see Proposition

Point

We will provide two different proofs of
(T6)

BJ97

[

, Definition 7.1.2]
for definition). In particular, the critical exponent of

.

In

the

-dimensional oriented Banach–Mazur compactum

From different aspects, Section
4
is dedicated to distinguish our construction.

In §
4.1
, we first check
\iota
is the only
\rho
-equivariant map from
\mathbb{H}^{2}
to
\mathbb{H}^{\infty}
. We then prove that any continuous
\rho

In §

, introduced in
[
MP19
]
, and explain how it is related to our construction. More precisely, this notion is an analogue to the notion of positive definite kernels for constructing embeddings into Hilbert spaces via the renowned Gelfand–Naimark–Segal construction (

), see
[
BdlHV08
, Appendix C]
. Moreover, this notion should not be confused with the kernel of
complex
hyperbolic type introduced in
[

]
, which was used to produce exotic complex hyperbolic representations
\operatorname{PSL}_{2}(\mathbb{R})\to\Isom(\mathbb{H}^{\infty}_{\mathbb{C}})
by Ruiz Stolowicz in
[
RS24
]
. As a byproduct of interpreting the mixed area as a kernel of hyperbolic type, we obtain an extension of the Minkowski inequality for plane convex bodies, see Proposition
4.5

There are different ways to embed
\mathbb{H}^{2}
in a
\operatorname{PSL}_{2}(\mathbb{R})
-equivariant way into
\mathbb{H}^{\infty}
, and these embeddings all have quasi-isometric images. In §
4.3
, we first note that they do not necessarily reduce to an isometry. However, if the associated representations are irreducible, then
[
MP14
, Theorem B]
states that the orbits of the unique
\operatorname{SO}_{2}(\mathbb{R})
-fixed point are isometric. In particular,
\iota(\mathbb{H}^{2})
and

are isometric. We verify this fact by a direct calculation at the end of §
4.3
.

Two further questions emerged as a natural continuation of what has been studied in the present article. The first one is:
can we construct an exotic representation
\rho\colon\Isom(\mathbb{H}^{n})\to\Isom(\mathbb{H}^{\infty})
using convex bodies?
The answer is no, and the present construction is a dimensional accident, as we now explain.

The hyperbolic embeddings of convex bodies studied by the first and second authors in
[
DF22
]
and
[
Lon25
]
respectively are not limited to
\mathbb{R}^{2}
: the support function map provides a convex embedding of the space of convex bodies of
\mathbb{R}^{n}
with Steiner point at the origin (that generalises symmetry of convex bodies) into the infinite-dimensional hyperbolic space.
One may wonder whether this construction can be applied to the volume-preserving action of
\operatorname{SL}_{n}(\mathbb{R})
over the ellipsoids in
\mathbb{R}^{n}
in order to derive a representation of
\operatorname{SL}_{n}(\mathbb{R})
into
\Isom(\mathbb{H}^{\infty})
. Actually, for
n\geq 3
, the Lie group
\operatorname{SL}_{n}(\mathbb{R})
has Kazhdan’s Property (T), which prevents it from acting non-elementarily by isometries on any real hyperbolic space, see for example
[
BdlHV08
, §1.4 and §2.6]
. Also, the hyperbolic embedding of convex bodies in higher dimensions is based on the intrinsic area, which coincides with the volume only for the plane. For example, for convex polyhedra, it is, up to a dimensional constant, the sum of the 2-dimensional volume of the faces of dimension 2, and this is not preserved by
\operatorname{SL}_{n}(\mathbb{R})
for
n>2
. So, in higher dimension, our construction provides an embedding of the symmetric space of
\operatorname{SL}_{n}(\mathbb{R})
into
\mathbb{H}^{\infty}
, but the action is not isometric. We also remark that in dimension
2
, the hyperbolic plane is homothetic to the symmetric space
\operatorname{SL}_{2}(\mathbb{R})/\operatorname{SO}_{2}(\mathbb{R})
endowed with the Killing metric.

Also, in higher dimensions, the boundary at infinity is always given by the symmetric segments. But the convex hull of this boundary at infinity gives all the symmetric convex bodies only for the plane (Lemma
2.18
), but not for higher dimensions.

The second question is:
can we describe
\rho_{t}
for
t\not=\frac{1}{2}
using convex bodies?
. As the form
\mathbf{A}
is a kernel of hyperbolic type on the support functions of plane symmetric convex bodies with positive area, one can immediately deduce from
[
MP19
, Theorem 3.10]
that for
0<s\leq 1
, the power
(\mathbf{A}(\cdot,\cdot))^{s}
remains a kernel of hyperbolic type on the same set, which then yields again an exotic representation
\operatorname{PSL}_{2}(\mathbb{R})\to\Isom(\mathbb{H}^{\infty})
conjugate to
\rho_{\frac{s}{2}}
. Hence, the representation
\rho_{\frac{s}{2}}
for
0<s\leq 1
also enjoys properties similar to Theorem
1.1
and Theorem
1.2
. Actually, Theorem
1.1
is already known for any
\rho_{t}

(0<t<1)
, while
(T6)
is shown to be true for any
\rho_{t}
in the present article. However,
[
MP19
, Theorem 3.10]
does not apply for
s>1
, this means that our construction cannot go higher than the parameter
\frac{1}{2}
for the time being.

2
Hyperbolic model for plane convex bodies

In this section, we will first review the hyperbolic model for convex bodies in
[
DF22
,
Lon25
]
by going through the tools that were used in the construction. In reviewing this construction, we will also mention some conclusions that can be drawn directly from it.

2.1
Hyperbolic space

First, let us recall the notion of an infinite-dimensional real hyperbolic space using the hyperboloid model. Let
\mathcal{H}
be a Hilbert space over
\mathbb{R}
carrying an inner product
\langle\cdot,\cdot\rangle_{\mathcal{H}}
.
For
x\in\mathbb{R}\oplus\mathcal{H}
, denote
x=x_{0}+x_{\mathcal{H}}
.
Define the quadratic form
B\colon(\mathbb{R}\oplus\mathcal{H})\times(\mathbb{R}\oplus\mathcal{H})\to\mathbb{R}
as

\displaystyle B\big(x,y\big)\coloneqq x_{0}y_{0}-\langle x_{\mathcal{H}},y_{\mathcal{H}}\rangle_{\mathcal{H}}~.

(2.1)

The
hyperboloid model
for the real hyperbolic space is given by

\mathbb{H}^{\infty}\coloneqq\left\{x\in\mathbb{R}\oplus\mathcal{H}\mid x_{0}>0\text{ and }B(x,x)=1\right\}

and is equipped with a distance
d_{\mathbb{H}^{\infty}}\colon\mathbb{H}^{\infty}\times\mathbb{H}^{\infty}\to\mathbb{R}_{+}
defined as

d_{\mathbb{H}^{\infty}}(x,y)=\cosh^{-1}\left(B(x,y)\right)~.

Indeed, such a quadratic form
B
satisfies the
reversed Cauchy–Schwarz inequality
: for
(x_{0},x_{\mathcal{H}}),(y_{0},y_{\mathcal{H}})
in
\mathbb{R}^{+}\oplus\mathcal{H}
such that
B\big(x,x\big)>0,B\big(y,y\big)>0
, then

~B\big(x,y)^{2}\geq B\big(x,x\big)B\big(y,y\big)

(2.2)

with equality if and only if the two vectors are proportional. In particular,
if
x,y\in\mathbb{H}^{\infty}
, then
B(x,y)\geq 1
, with equality if and only if
x=y
.

This space is a complete Riemannian manifold of constant sectional curvature equal to
-1
. It is therefore a
\mathrm{CAT}(-1)
space and admits a boundary at infinity
\partial_{\infty}\mathbb{H}^{\infty}
defined as the set of equivalence classes of geodesic rays, two geodesic rays
c_{1},c_{2}:[0,\infty)\to\mathbb{H}^{\infty}
being equivalent if and only if they are asymptotic,
i.e.
there exists a constant
K\geq 0
such that
d_{\mathbb{H}^{\infty}}(c_{1}(t),c_{2}(t))\leq K
for all
t\geq 0
. We refer to
[
BH99
, Chapter II.8]
for the definition and properties of the boundary at infinity for general complete
\mathrm{CAT(0)}
spaces.

Alternatively, we may also define
\mathbb{H}^{\infty}
as the set of positive lines in
\mathbb{R}\oplus\mathcal{H}
,

\mathbb{H}^{\infty}\coloneqq\left\{[x]\in\mathrm{P}(\mathbb{R}\oplus\mathcal{H})\mid B(x,x)>0\right\}

where
\mathrm{P}(\mathbb{R}\oplus\mathcal{H})
is the projectivization of
\mathbb{R}\oplus\mathcal{H}
. With this definition, the boundary of
\mathbb{H}^{\infty}
as a subset of the topological space
\mathrm{P}(\mathbb{R}\oplus\mathcal{H})
is the set of isotropic lines in
\mathbb{R}\oplus\mathcal{H}
,
\left\{[x]\in\mathrm{P}(\mathbb{R}\oplus\mathcal{H})\mid B(x,x)=0\right\}
. It is known that this (topological) boundary coincides with the boundary at infinity
\partial_{\infty}\mathbb{H}^{\infty}
. Indeed, being
\mathrm{CAT(-1)}
implies that
\partial_{\infty}\mathbb{H}^{\infty}
can be identified with the Gromov boundary of
\mathbb{H}^{\infty}
by
[
DSU17
, Proposition 4.4.4]
. Besides, the identification between the Gromov boundary and the topological boundary was proved for example in
[
DSU17
, Proposition 3.5.3]
. The descriptions of
\partial_{\infty}\mathbb{H}^{\infty}
as the isotropic cone in
\mathbb{R}\oplus\mathcal{H}
and as the set of classes of geodesic rays in
\mathbb{H}^{\infty}
will both be useful in the remaining of this article.

Moreover, we will define
\dim\mathbb{H}^{\infty}\coloneqq\dim\mathcal{H}
, because it is clear from the hyperboloid model that
\mathbb{H}^{\infty}
is a
\mathcal{H}
-manifold,
i.e.
it is locally homeomorphic to
\mathcal{H}
. Given two Hilbert spaces of the same dimension, one can construct an isomorphism between them via a bijection between their respective orthonormal basis. Consequently, we can conclude that two hyperbolic spaces are isometric if and only if they have the same dimension,
i.e.
the hyperbolic space of a given dimension is unique up to isometry (see also for example
[
BIM05
, Propositions 2.7 and 3.7]
). Hence, we are able to identify all infinite-dimensional separable real hyperbolic spaces, and in the present article we will use the notation
\mathbb{H}^{\infty}
for such a space up to isometry.

We end this subsection by remarking that by considering its intersections with finite-dimensional vector spaces, the distance
d_{\mathbb{H}^{\infty}}
is the one given by the induced Riemannian metric
g_{\mathbb{H}^{\infty}}
over
\mathbb{H}^{\infty}
, that
\mathbb{H}^{\infty}
has isometries acting transitively
[
BIM05
, Lemma 3.6]
, and that it is the same as in the finite-dimensional case where distance isometries are Riemannian isometries
[
GJR09
]
. We refer to
[
DSU17
]
for more details.

2.2
Space of periodic even functions

Let
H^{1}_{\operatorname{even}}
be the Hilbert subspace of even functions of the Sobolev space on the unit circle
\mathcal{S}^{1}
, endowed with the Sobolev norm
\|\cdot\|_{H^{1}}
given by:

\|h\|^{2}_{H^{1}}=\|h\|^{2}_{L^{2}}+\|h^{\prime}\|^{2}_{L^{2}}~.

By Stone–Weierstraß Theorem, we can approximate these functions by polynomials on the unit circle, so
H^{1}_{\operatorname{even}}
is separable and thus of countably infinite dimension. Parametrizing the circle by
(\cos(\theta),\sin(\theta))
for
\theta\in[0,2\pi)
, the space
H^{1}_{\operatorname{even}}
will then consist of
\pi
-periodic functions on
[0,2\pi)
. We then endow
H^{1}_{\operatorname{even}}
with the bilinear form

\mathbf{A}(h_{1},h_{2})=\frac{1}{2\pi}\int_{0}^{2\pi}(h_{1}h_{2}-h^{\prime}_{1}h^{\prime}_{2})~,

(2.3)

and denote

\mathbf{A}(h)\coloneqq\mathbf{A}(h,h)=\frac{1}{2\pi}\|h\|^{2}_{L^{2}}-\frac{1}{2\pi}\|h^{\prime}\|^{2}_{L^{2}}~,

where
h^{\prime}
is the derivative of the function
h
with respect to
\theta
.
We will denote by
\mathbf{1}
the constant function equal to one. Hence

\mathbf{A}(\mathbf{1})=1~.

Let
L_{0}
be the line of constant functions in
H^{1}_{\operatorname{even}}
, and
L_{0}^{\bot}
be its
L^{2}
-orthogonal.

Lemma 2.1
.

\forall h\in L_{0}^{\bot},\frac{3}{16\pi}\|h\|_{H^{1}}^{2}\leq-\mathbf{A}(h)\leq\frac{1}{2\pi}\|h\|^{2}_{H^{1}}

(2.4)

Proof.

The right-hand side inequality in (
2.4
) is immediate as
-2\pi\mathbf{A}(h)\leq\|h^{\prime}\|^{2}_{L^{2}}\leq\|h\|_{H^{1}}^{2}
.

As the unique even function on the circle that is the restriction of a linear form of the plane is the zero function, by Poincaré–Wirtinger Inequality for integral zero cases,

\forall h\in L_{0}^{\bot},\|h\|_{L^{2}}^{2}\leq\frac{1}{4}\|h^{\prime}\|_{L^{2}}^{2}

(2.5)

with equality if and only if
h=0
.
From (
2.5
), we obtain
-2\pi\mathbf{A}(h)\geq 3\|h\|^{2}_{L^{2}}\geq\frac{3}{4}\|h\|^{2}_{L^{2}}
but also
-2\pi\mathbf{A}(h)\geq\frac{3}{4}\|h^{\prime}\|^{2}_{L^{2}}
, and left-hand side inequality in (
2.4
) is obtained by summing those two inequalities.
∎

For any
h\in H^{1}_{\operatorname{even}}
, let us denote

\pi_{0}(h)=\frac{1}{2\pi}\int_{0}^{2\pi}h=\mathbf{A}(h,\mathbf{1})~.

(2.6)

Clearly, the two functions
h-\pi_{0}(h)\mathbf{1}
and
\mathbf{1}
are
\mathbf{A}
-orthogonal, hence
\pi_{0}(h)\mathbf{1}
is the orthogonal projection of
h
onto
L_{0}
. For future reference, it is worth noting that

\mathbf{A}(h)=\mathbf{A}\left(h-\pi_{0}(h)\mathbf{1}\right)+\pi_{0}(h)^{2}~.

(2.7)

Besides, we can provide
H^{1}_{\operatorname{even}}
with coordinates such that (
2.1
) holds, with the Hilbert space
\mathcal{H}
beeing
L_{0}^{\bot}
endowed with
-\mathbf{A}
.
Since
H^{1}_{\operatorname{even}}
is of countable infinite dimension, we have

\mathbb{H}^{\infty}\coloneqq\Big\{h\in H^{1}_{\operatorname{even}}\mid\mathbf{A}(h)=1,\ \pi_{0}(h)>0\Big\}

endowed with the distance

d_{\mathbb{H}^{\infty}}(h_{1},h_{2})\coloneqq\cosh^{-1}(\mathbf{A}(h_{1},h_{2}))~.

(2.8)

Let us present some basic facts about the topology of
\mathbb{H}^{\infty}
. Let us first make a convenient remark.

Remark 2.2
.

By the definition (
2.8
) of
d_{\mathbb{H}^{\infty}}
,
d_{\mathbb{H}^{\infty}}(h_{i},h)\to 0
is equivalent to
\mathbf{A}(h_{i},h)\to 1
. But
h_{i},h\in\mathbb{H}^{\infty}
, hence

\mathbf{A}(h_{i}-h)=2-2\mathbf{A}(h_{i},h)
, so
d_{\mathbb{H}^{\infty}}(h_{i},h)\to 0
is equivalent to
\mathbf{A}(h_{i}-h)\to 0
.

Lemma 2.3
.

The map
\pi_{0}\big|_{\mathbb{H}^{\infty}}:(\mathbb{H}^{\infty},d_{\mathbb{H}^{\infty}})\to\mathbb{R}
is continuous.

Proof.

Let
h_{i}\to h
in
\mathbb{H}^{\infty}
. For any
i
, consider the vector space spanned by
L_{0},h,h_{i}
(that may be of dimension 1, 2 or 3). The intersection with
\mathbb{H}^{\infty}
gives a finite-dimensional hyperbolic space and the distance between
h_{i}
and
h
is preserved, as well as their image for
\pi_{0}
. The distance between those images can then be explicitly computed.
∎

Lemma 2.4
.

d_{\mathbb{H}^{\infty}}
and
\|\cdot\|_{H^{1}}
define the same topology on
\mathbb{H}^{\infty}
.

Proof.

Let
(h_{i})_{i}
be a sequence of
\mathbb{H}^{\infty}
and
h\in\mathbb{H}^{\infty}
. We will use Remark
2.2
.

Let us suppose that
h_{i}\to h
for
\|\cdot\|_{H^{1}}
.
Hence
(h_{i})_{i}

converges to
h
in
L^{2}
and
(h^{\prime}_{i})_{i}

converges to
h^{\prime}
in
L^{2}
, so it follows immediately from the definition of
\mathbf{A}
that
\mathbf{A}(h_{i}-h)\to 0
.

Conversely, let us suppose that
h_{i}\to h
for
d_{\mathbb{H}^{\infty}}
,
i.e.

\mathbf{A}(v_{i})\to 0
with
v_{i}=h_{i}-h
.
By Lemma
2.3
,
\pi_{0}(v_{i})\to 0
,
hence
\mathbf{A}\left(v_{i}-\pi_{0}(v_{i})\mathbf{1}\right)\to 0
by (
2.7
), and by (
2.4
),

\left\|v_{i}-\pi_{0}(v_{i})\mathbf{1}\right\|_{H^{1}}\to 0
. Using again
\pi_{0}(v_{i})\to 0
, that gives
\|v_{i}\|_{H^{1}}\to 0
.
∎

Lemma 2.5
.

A subset of
\mathbb{H}^{\infty}
that is bounded for
d_{\mathbb{H}^{\infty}}
is also bounded for
\|\cdot\|_{H^{1}}
.

Proof.

A subset of
\mathbb{H}^{\infty}
that is bounded for
d_{\mathbb{H}^{\infty}}
is contained in an open ball centred at
\mathbf{1}
, so it suffices to prove that such a ball of radius
c
is bounded for
\|\cdot\|_{H^{1}}
. So, let

h\in\mathbb{H}^{\infty}
such that
d_{\mathbb{H}^{\infty}}(h,\mathbf{1})\leq c
.
By the definition of the distance, for such a
h
, we have

\pi_{0}(h)=\frac{1}{2\pi}\int_{0}^{2\pi}h=\mathbf{A}(h,\mathbf{1})=\cosh(d_{\mathbb{H}^{\infty}}(h,\mathbf{1}))\leq\cosh(c)\eqqcolon k>1,

so by (
2.7
) and (
2.4
),

0<\mathbf{A}(h)<-\frac{3}{16\pi}\|h-\pi_{0}(h)\mathbf{1}\|^{2}_{H^{1}}+k^{2}

so

\|h\|_{H^{1}}^{2}=\|h-\pi_{0}(h)\mathbf{1}\|_{H^{1}}^{2}+\|\pi_{0}(h)\mathbf{1}\|^{2}_{H^{1}}<Ck^{2}

for some constant
C>0
.
∎

Remark 2.6
.

It is worth remarking that the arguments above are not limited to functions on the unit circle
\mathcal{S}^{1}
but also available in higher dimensions. It follows that we can proceed a similar construction of
\mathbb{H}^{\infty}
starting from even functions on
\mathcal{S}^{n}
for
n\geq 1
. More generally, for
L^{2}
-functions on a probability space, Malliavin calculus will also allow us to construct
\mathbb{H}^{\infty}
with a similar strategy, see
[
Lon25
, Section 4]
.

2.3
Space of symmetric convex bodies

This construction of the infinite-dimensional hyperbolic space has a clear geometric interpretation,
i.e.
the symmetric plane convex bodies can be associated to even functions on
\mathcal{S}^{1}
.
Recall that a convex body is called
symmetric
if it is centrally symmetric with respect to the origin,
i.e.

-K=K
.
The philosophy comes from the integral geometry and geometric probability theory, where geometric objects are identified with functions or random variables that describe their shapes.

In this section, we refer to
[
Gro96
,
Sch14
]
for all the facts we mention about convex bodies.

Let
\mathcal{K}_{c}
be the collection of convex bodies in the plane with non-empty interior. Let
\overline{\mathcal{K}}_{c}
be the set of symmetric convex bodies possibly with empty interior.
Let
\langle\cdot,\cdot\rangle
be the usual scalar product on
\mathbb{R}^{2}
. The
support function

\textsc{supp}(K)\colon\mathcal{S}^{1}\to\mathbb{R}
of
K

is defined by

\textsc{supp}(K)(x)=\sup_{k\in K}\langle x,k\rangle~.

(2.9)

Besides, the support functions of convex bodies in
\mathbb{R}^{2}
are exactly the functions over
\mathcal{S}^{1}
whose one-homogeneous extensions over
\mathbb{R}^{2}
are convex. This provides strong regularity properties. See
[
MM06
]
for other characterizations. If
K
is contained in the centred ball of radius
R
, then
\textsc{supp}(K)
is
R
-Lipschitz. Also,
K
being symmetric is equivalent to
\textsc{supp}(K)(x)=\textsc{supp}(-K)(x)=\textsc{supp}(K)(-x)
over
\mathcal{S}^{1}
. Hence for a convex body
K\in\overline{\mathcal{K}}_{c}
, its support function
\textsc{supp}(K)
belongs to
H^{1}_{\operatorname{even}}
.

So we have a well-defined map

\displaystyle\textsc{supp}\colon\overline{\mathcal{K}}_{c}

\displaystyle\to H^{1}_{\operatorname{even}}

\displaystyle K

\displaystyle\mapsto\textsc{supp}(K)

and the main interest of this map is that it preserves basic operations on convex bodies, namely it is additive,
i.e.

\textsc{supp}(K)+\textsc{supp}(K^{\prime})=\textsc{supp}(K+K^{\prime})~,

(2.10)

where

K+K^{\prime}\coloneqq\{v+w\in\mathbb{R}^{2}\mid v\in K,w\in K^{\prime}\}

is the
Minkowski sum
of the two convex bodies. Also,

\forall\lambda>0,~\textsc{supp}(\lambda K)=\lambda\textsc{supp}(K)~.

(2.11)

We endow
\overline{\mathcal{K}}_{c}
with the subspace topology, which is the topology induced by the Hausdorff distance
d_{\operatorname{Haus}}
.
This classical results allows to express the Hausdorff distance in a simple way.

Lemma 2.7
.

The map

\textsc{supp}\colon(\overline{\mathcal{K}}_{c},d_{\operatorname{Haus}})\to(H^{1}_{\operatorname{even}},d_{L^{\infty}})

is an isometry, where
d_{L^{\infty}}
is the distance defined by
\|\cdot\|_{L^{\infty}}
.

From this and Arzelà–Ascoli’s theorem follows the famous Blaschke selection theorem:

Proposition 2.8
.

(\overline{\mathcal{K}}_{c},d_{\operatorname{Haus}})
is a proper metric space, i.e. every bounded closed subset is compact. In turn it is locally compact and complete.

The regularity properties of the support functions implies the following.

Lemma 2.9
.

The map

\textsc{supp}\colon(\overline{\mathcal{K}}_{c},d_{\operatorname{Haus}})\to(H^{1}_{\operatorname{even}},\|\cdot\|_{H^{1}})

is continuous.

Proof.

For a convergent sequence of convex bodies, their support functions converge for the uniform distance, and hence for the
L^{2}
-distance. Moreover, if
K_{i}\to K
, then
h_{i}\coloneqq\textsc{supp}(K_{i})
are uniformly bounded, so by
[
Gro96
, Lemma 2.2.1]
, their gradients are uniformly bounded and converge almost everywhere. This implies that they converge in
L^{2}
, and as a result,
h_{i}
converge to
h=\textsc{supp}(K)
in
H^{1}
-norm.
∎

Lemma 2.10
.

\textsc{supp}(\overline{\mathcal{K}}_{c})
is closed for
\|\cdot\|_{H^{1}}
.

Proof.

Let
h_{n}\in\textsc{supp}(\overline{\mathcal{K}}_{c})
such that
h_{n}\to h
for
\|\cdot\|_{H^{1}}
. Hence, it converges for the
L^{2}
-norm.
By
[
Vit85
, Theorem 3]
, the space of support functions is closed for the
L^{2}
-norm, so
h
is a support function.
∎

Let
\mathrm{S}
be the set of centrally symmetric segments of the plane with positive length. Then

\overline{\mathcal{K}}_{c}=\mathcal{K}_{c}\sqcup\mathrm{S}\sqcup\{0\}~.

(2.12)

Note that for
K\in\mathcal{K}_{c}
,
\textsc{supp}(K)
is positive, as the origin is contained in the interior of
K
.

Figure 2
:
To Remark
2.11
. Parametrisation of the boundary of a convex body by the support function.

Remark 2.11
.

Let
K
be a strictly convex body on the Euclidean plane whose boundary
\partial K
is
C^{2}
. By strict convexity, there is a tangent line
\ell
orthogonal to
(\cos(\theta),\sin(\theta))
which meets
\partial K
at a unique point
c_{K}(\theta)
. This gives a parametrisation
c_{K}:\mathcal{S}^{1}\to\partial K
of the boundary. As
h_{K}(\theta)\coloneqq\textsc{supp}(K)(\theta)
is exactly the distance from
\ell
to the origin of
\mathbb{R}^{2}
, there exists a function
g
such that

c_{K}(\theta)=h_{K}(\theta)\binom{\cos\theta}{\sin\theta}+g(\theta)\binom{-\sin\theta}{\cos\theta}
. As
c_{K}^{\prime}(\theta)
is tangent to
\partial K
, we have
\langle c_{K}^{\prime}(\theta),\binom{\cos\theta}{\sin\theta}\rangle=0
, that gives the following (see Figure
2
):

c_{K}(\theta)=h_{K}(\theta)\binom{\cos\theta}{\sin\theta}+h_{K}^{\prime}(\theta)\binom{-\sin\theta}{\cos\theta}~.

(2.13)

If
\textsc{area}(K)
is the area of the convex body
K
, the Green–Riemann formula and (
2.13
) give

\textsc{area}(K)=\frac{1}{2}\int_{\partial K}x\mbox{d}y-y\mbox{d}x=\frac{1}{2}\int_{0}^{2\pi}h_{K}(h_{K}+h_{K}^{\prime\prime})~.

As
h_{K}(0)=h_{K}(2\pi)
and
h_{K}^{\prime}(0)=h_{K}^{\prime}(2\pi)
,

0=\int_{0}^{2\pi}(h_{K}h_{K}^{\prime})^{\prime}=\int_{0}^{2\pi}h_{K}^{\prime 2}+\int_{0}^{2\pi}h_{K}h_{K}^{\prime\prime}~,

we obtain

\textsc{area}(K)=\pi\cdot\mathbf{A}(\textsc{supp}(K))~.

(2.14)

This formula holds for any convex body by approximation, and was first proved by Blaschke (
[
Bla14
]
, see also
[
Sch14
, p.298]
and the references therein).

The constant
\pi
is easy to check: if
D
is the centred unit disc of the Euclidean plane, then
\mathbf{1}=\textsc{supp}(D)
, and

\mathbf{A}(\mathbf{1})=1
but
\textsc{area}(D)=\pi
.

Up to this multiplicative constant,
\mathbf{A}(\textsc{supp}(K_{1}),\textsc{supp}(K_{2}))
corresponds to the
mixed area
of the convex bodies
K_{1}
and
K_{2}
. This has many geometrical implications, for example the reversed Cauchy–Schwarz inequality (
2.2
) for the Lorentzian bilinear form
\mathbf{A}
gives on
\mathcal{K}_{c}
the famous
Minkowski inequality
(or
Alexandrov–Fenchel inequality
in higher dimensions):

\textsc{area}(K_{1},K_{2})^{2}\geq\textsc{area}(K_{1})\textsc{area}(K_{2})~,

(2.15)

with equality if and only if
K_{1}=\lambda K_{2}
for
\lambda>0
.

Also,

\pi_{0}(\textsc{supp}(K))=\frac{1}{2\pi}\mathrm{per}(K)~,

(2.16)

where
\mathrm{per}(K)
is the perimeter of
K
. Moreover,
(
2.15
) with
K_{2}=D
is the isoperimetric inequality.

If
\mathcal{K}_{c}^{\pi}
is the subset of elements of
\mathcal{K}_{c}
of area
\pi
, then its image by
supp
is a subset of
\mathbb{H}^{\infty}
. Let
\textsc{supp}^{\pi}
be the restriction of
supp
to
\mathcal{K}_{c}^{\pi}
.

Lemma 2.12
.

The preimage by

\textsc{supp}^{\pi}\colon(\mathcal{K}_{c}^{\pi},d_{\operatorname{Haus}})\to(\mathbb{H}^{\infty},d_{\mathbb{H}^{\infty}})

of a bounded set is bounded.

Proof.

Let
X
be a set in
\mathbb{H}^{\infty}
bounded for
d_{\mathbb{H}^{\infty}}
. By Lemma
2.5
, it is bounded for the
H^{1}
-norm. Then it is bounded for the
L^{2}
-norm. So there exists a
C
such that for any
h\in X\cap\textsc{supp}^{\pi}(\mathcal{K}_{c}^{\pi})
, the
L^{2}
-distance between
\mathbf{1}
and
h
is less than
C
. By a theorem of Vitale
[
Vit85
, Corollary 2]
, there exists a positive dimensional constant
\alpha
such that

\displaystyle\alpha\frac{\|h-\mathbf{1}\|_{L^{\infty}}^{3}}{1+\|h-\mathbf{1}\|_{L^{\infty}}}\leq\|h-\mathbf{1}\|_{L^{2}}^{2}~.

(2.17)

Hence, there is a constant
C^{\prime}
such that

\frac{\|h-\mathbf{1}\|_{L^{\infty}}^{3}}{1+\|h-\mathbf{1}\|_{L^{\infty}}}\leq C^{\prime}

that implies that
\|h-\mathbf{1}\|_{L^{\infty}}
is uniformly bounded over
X\cap\textsc{supp}^{\pi}(\mathcal{K}_{c}^{\pi})
.
∎

Lemma 2.13
.

The map
\textsc{supp}^{\pi}:(\mathcal{K}_{c}^{\pi},d_{\operatorname{Haus}})\to(\mathbb{H}^{\infty},d_{\mathbb{H}^{\infty}})
is a continuous injective map.

Proof.

Injectivity comes from the fact that convex bodies are uniquely determined by their support function.
Let us check the continuity. From Lemma
2.9
,
(h_{i})_{i}

converge to
h
in
L^{2}
and
(h^{\prime}_{i})_{i}

converge to
h^{\prime}
in
L^{2}
, so it is immediate from the definition of
\mathbf{A}
that
\mathbf{A}(h_{i}-h)\to 0
,
that gives
d_{\mathbb{H}^{\infty}}(h_{i},h)\to 0
by Remark
2.2
.
∎

Lemma 2.14
.

The continuous map
\textsc{supp}^{\pi}:(\mathcal{K}_{c}^{\pi},d_{\operatorname{Haus}})\to(\mathbb{H}^{\infty},d_{\mathbb{H}^{\infty}})
is proper,
i.e.
the preimage of a compact set is compact.

Proof.

Let
X\subset(\mathbb{H}^{\infty},d_{\mathbb{H}^{\infty}})
be a compact subset. In particular, it is closed and bounded as we are in a metric space.
Its preimage by
\textsc{supp}^{\pi}
is closed (Lemma
2.13
) and bounded (Lemma
2.12
), hence compact (Proposition
2.8
).
∎

Proposition 2.15
.

The map
\textsc{supp}^{\pi}:(\mathcal{K}_{c}^{\pi},d_{\operatorname{Haus}})\to(\mathbb{H}^{\infty},d_{\mathbb{H}^{\infty}})
is a homeomorphism onto its image.

Proof.

This simply follows from the preceding results, as a continuous proper injective mapping from a locally compact space into a metric space is a homeomorphism onto its image.
∎

Remark 2.16
.

From the finite-dimensional case,
the image of a geodesic between two points in
\mathbb{H}^{\infty}
is the intersection between
\mathbb{H}^{\infty}
and the plane spanned by the lines in
H^{1}_{\operatorname{even}}
defined by the two points. From (
2.10
), it follows that the image of the hyperbolic geodesic between
\textsc{supp}(K_{1})
and
\textsc{supp}(K_{2})
is the image of

t\mapsto p(t)\coloneqq\frac{\textsc{supp}(K_{t})}{\mathbf{A}(K_{t})^{1/2}}

where
K_{t}=(1-t)K_{1}+tK_{2}
for
0\leq t\leq 1
.

It is a general fact that the midpoint
h
in
\mathbb{H}^{\infty}
between two points
h_{1},h_{2}\in\mathbb{H}^{\infty}
is the projection onto
\mathbb{H}^{\infty}
(along the vector line of the midpoint of the segment) of the midpoint of the segment in
H^{1}_{\operatorname{even}}
,
i.e.

h=(h_{1}+h_{2})/2\mathbf{A}((h_{1}+h_{2})/2))^{1/2}
. Indeed,

d_{\mathbb{H}^{\infty}}(h_{1},h)=\cosh^{-1}\frac{\mathbf{A}(h_{1},h_{2})+1}{\mathbf{A}(h_{1}+h_{2})^{1/2}}=d_{\mathbb{H}^{\infty}}(h_{2},h)~.

In turn, if
K_{1},K_{2}\in\mathcal{K}_{c}^{\pi}
, the midpoint of their images by
supp
in
\mathbb{H}^{\infty}
differ from
K_{1}+K_{2}
by a dilation, or equivalently, they are homothetic,
i.e.

C\cdot\textsc{supp}(K_{1}+K_{2})
for some
C>0
.

Remark 2.17
.

By convexity,
\textsc{supp}^{\pi}(\mathcal{K}_{c}^{\pi})
endowed with the distance induced by the hyperbolic distance, is a space with curvature bounded from below and above by
-1
.
It has not the geodesic extension property, otherwise, by a classical result of V. Beretovskij, it would be a finite-dimensional Riemannian manifold. See
[
DF22
]
for the details.

2.4
Segments and ellipses

In the following, we denote by
\operatorname{CH}
the closed convex hull, for the convexity which is defined according to the context. If we want to insist on which topology is being used, we use a subscript:
\operatorname{CH}_{\operatorname{Haus}}
(resp.
\operatorname{CH}_{H^{1}}
) if one considers
d_{\operatorname{Haus}}
(resp.
\|\cdot\|_{H^{1}}
), and so on. Let us note that the Hausdorff distance is naturally used over non-empty compact sets of the plane, and in this space, the subspace of convex bodies is closed
[
Sch14
, Theorem 1.8.6]
.

The set of
symmetric zonoids

is the closure (for the Hausdorff topology) of the convex hull of the set
\mathrm{S}
of symmetric segments of positive length introduced in (
2.12
).
By construction, a symmetric zonoid is centrally symmetric with respect to the origin. The following famous result says that the converse holds.
This is false in higher dimensions, see for example
[
Sch14
, Corollary 3.5.7]
. We provide a simple proof for convenience.

Lemma 2.18
.

Each symmetric plane convex body is a symmetric zonoid. In other words,
\overline{\mathcal{K}}_{c}=\operatorname{CH}_{\operatorname{Haus}}(\mathrm{S})
. Moreover,
\overline{\mathcal{K}}_{c}=\operatorname{CH}_{\operatorname{Haus}}(\mathcal{E}_{c})
where
\mathcal{E}_{c}
is the space of symmetric ellipses.

Proof.

That is obvious for segments and the origin.
Any convex body of positive area can be approximated by
convex polygons in
\mathcal{K}_{c}
for the Hausdorff distance, so it suffices to prove that any symmetric convex polygon is a symmetric zonoid. Indeed, we have that any such polygon
P
is a sum of symmetric segments, see Figure
3
.

Any symmetric segment can be obtained as the limit of symmetric ellipses, hence
\mathrm{S}\subset\operatorname{CH}_{\operatorname{Haus}}(\mathcal{E}_{c})
, and then

\overline{\mathcal{K}}_{c}\subset\operatorname{CH}_{\operatorname{Haus}}(\mathrm{S})\subset\operatorname{CH}_{\operatorname{Haus}}(\mathcal{E}_{c})\subset\overline{\mathcal{K}}_{c}

where the last inclusion is the closedeness of
\overline{\mathcal{K}}_{c}
.
∎

Figure 3
:
To the proof of Lemma
2.18
. Sides of a symmetric polygon
P
can be grouped into parallel pairs with equal lengths. For such a pair,
P
is the sum of a symmetric convex polygon
P^{\prime}
and a symmetric segment
S
, parallel to the elements of a pair of parallel sides.

By the properties of additivity (
2.10
) and continuity (Lemma
2.9
), and the fact that the image of
\overline{\mathcal{K}}_{c}
is closed (Lemma
2.10
) we obtain the following:

Proposition 2.19
.

The sets
\textsc{supp}(\overline{\mathcal{K}}_{c})
,
\operatorname{CH}_{H^{1}}(\textsc{supp}(\mathrm{S}))
, and
\operatorname{CH}_{H^{1}}(\textsc{supp}(\mathcal{E}_{c}))
coincide.
\Box

The following corollary of Proposition
2.19
allows us to approximate elements in
H^{1}_{\operatorname{even}}
using support functions.

Corollary 2.20
.

The differences of support functions form a dense vector subspace in
H^{1}_{\operatorname{even}}
(with respect to
H^{1}
-topology). In particular, the linear combinations of support functions of ellipses span a dense vector subspace of
H^{1}_{\operatorname{even}}
.

Proof.

We will first show that differences of support functions generate a dense vector subspace. Note that any
\pi
-periodic
C^{\infty}
function over the circle can be written as the difference of two support functions. Indeed, a
\pi
-periodic function
h
on the circle is a support function if and only if its degree one homogeneous extension is convex,
i.e.
its Hessian is positive semi-definite, that is equivalent to
h^{\prime\prime}+h\geq 0
. In turn, for any smooth function
h
, there is a constant
c
such that
h+c\mathbf{1}
will achieve this condition. As smooth functions are dense in Sobolev space, the first assertion follows. The second assertion follows from this one together with Proposition
2.19
.
∎

3
An exotic representation via convex bodies

In this section, we will see that the hyperbolic embedding of convex bodies induces an irreducible representation
\operatorname{PSL}_{2}(\mathbb{R})\to\Isom(\mathbb{H}^{\infty})
and deduce from the geometric properties of the embedding some more descriptions about the representation with elementary arguments.

3.1
The
\operatorname{PSL}_{2}(\mathbb{R})
action

The special linear group
\operatorname{SL}_{2}(\mathbb{R})
acts continuously onto
\mathbb{R}^{2}
, in turn it acts continuously onto
\overline{\mathcal{K}}_{c}
by the action

\operatorname{SL}_{2}(\mathbb{R})\times\overline{\mathcal{K}}_{c}\to\overline{\mathcal{K}}_{c}~,~(A,K)\mapsto AK\coloneqq\{Ak\mid k\in K\}.

As the convex bodies are symmetric, we actually have a
\operatorname{PSL}_{2}(\mathbb{R})
action onto
\overline{\mathcal{K}}_{c}
, namely
[A]K\coloneqq AK
for any representative
A\in\operatorname{SL}_{2}(\mathbb{R}))
. Abusing notation, we will often denote
[A]
by
A
.

There is a representation
\rho
of

\operatorname{SL}_{2}(\mathbb{R})
onto the linear mappings of
H^{1}_{\operatorname{even}}
defined by, for
A\in\operatorname{SL}_{2}(\mathbb{R})

and
h\in H^{1}_{\operatorname{even}}
seen as a function on the circle
\mathcal{S}^{1}\subset\mathbb{R}^{2}
,

\rho(A)(h)(x)=|A^{\intercal}x|h(A^{\intercal}\cdot x)

(3.1)

where
\cdot
is the following action of
\operatorname{SL}_{2}(\mathbb{R})
on the unit circle:

\displaystyle\forall x\in\mathcal{S}^{1}\subset\mathbb{R}^{2},M\cdot x\coloneqq\frac{Mx}{|Mx|}~.

(3.2)

Note that the map
\rho(A)(h)
is even for
h\in H^{1}_{\operatorname{even}}
, thus is actually well-defined for
A\in\operatorname{PSL}_{2}(\mathbb{R})
. As the map is essentially the composition of a multiplication by a bounded map and a right composition by a diffeomorphism, its image stays in
H^{1}_{\operatorname{even}}
.

For
K\in\overline{\mathcal{K}}_{c}
, from the definition of
\textsc{supp}(K)
(
2.9
) one has,

\textsc{supp}(AK)=\rho(A)(\textsc{supp}(K))~.

(3.3)

Remark 3.1
.

In the following, we will often use the fact that

\mathcal{E}_{c}^{\pi}\subset\mathcal{K}_{c}^{\pi}
, the collection of symmetric ellipses in
\mathbb{R}^{2}
of area
\pi
, is the orbit of
D
for the action of
\operatorname{PSL}_{2}(\mathbb{R})
. In turn, support functions of ellipses in
\mathbb{H}^{\infty}
are the
\rho(\operatorname{PSL}_{2}(\mathbb{R}))
-orbit of
\mathbf{1}
.

Lemma 3.2
.

For any
A\in\operatorname{PSL}_{2}(\mathbb{R})
,

\mathbf{A}
is
\rho(A)
-invariant on
H^{1}_{\operatorname{even}}
.

Proof.

A
preserves the area of symmetric plane convex bodies, hence by (
2.14
) and (
3.3
),
\rho(A)
preserves

\mathbf{A}
for the support functions of convex bodies. The sum of two support functions is a support function, so with

2\mathbf{A}(\textsc{supp}(K_{1}),\textsc{supp}(K_{2}))=\mathbf{A}(\textsc{supp}(K_{1})+\textsc{supp}(K_{2}))-\mathbf{A}(\textsc{supp}(K_{1}))-\mathbf{A}(\textsc{supp}(K_{2}))

the bilinear form
\mathbf{A}(\cdot,\cdot)
is preserved by
\rho(A)
over the support functions. Hence as

\mathbf{A}(\textsc{supp}(K_{1})-\textsc{supp}(K_{2}))=\mathbf{A}(\textsc{supp}(K_{1}))+\mathbf{A}(\textsc{supp}(K_{2}))-2\mathbf{A}(\textsc{supp}(K_{1}),\textsc{supp}(K_{2})),

\rho(A)
preserves
\mathbf{A}

for the difference of support functions. Then Corollary
2.20
implies that
\mathbf{A}
is
\rho(A)
-invariant on the entire
H^{1}_{\operatorname{even}}
.
∎

Lemma 3.3
.

The representation
\rho
is continuous with respect to the strong operator topology (SOT): for every
h\in H^{1}_{\operatorname{even}}
, we have
\rho(A_{n})h\to h
whenever
A_{n}\to\mathrm{Id}\in\operatorname{PSL}_{2}(\mathbb{R})
.

Proof.

The action is clearly continuous for
C^{\infty}
functions, that are dense in
H^{1}_{\operatorname{even}}
.
∎

Restricting to
\mathbb{H}^{\infty}\subset H^{1}_{\operatorname{even}}
, we obtain a representation that we still denote by
\rho
,

\rho:\operatorname{PSL}_{2}(\mathbb{R})\to\operatorname{Isom}(\mathbb{H}^{\infty})~.

Remark 3.4
.

The representation
\rho
is faithful, since for each non-trivial
A\in\operatorname{PSL}_{2}(\mathbb{R})
, one can find a convex body
K\in\mathcal{K}_{c}^{\pi}
such that
AK\neq K
.

For the following lemma, compare with
[
MP14
, Lemma 3.9]
.

Lemma 3.5
.

The only fixed point in
\mathbb{H}^{\infty}
for the action of
\rho(\operatorname{SO}_{2}(\mathbb{R}))
is
\mathbf{1}
.

Proof.

From the action (
3.1
), one sees that
\rho(\operatorname{SO}_{2}(\mathbb{R}))
acts on
H^{1}_{\operatorname{even}}
by precomposing the functions by a rotation of the circle
\mathcal{S}^{1}
. Since
\rho(\operatorname{SO}_{2}(\mathbb{R}))
acts transitively on
\mathcal{S}^{1}
, the only fixed points of this action are the constant functions in the line
L_{0}\subset H^{1}_{\operatorname{even}}
. As
L_{0}\cap\mathbb{H}^{\infty}
is reduced to
\{\mathbf{1}\}
,
\mathbf{1}\in\mathbb{H}^{\infty}
is the unique fixed point for the
\rho(\operatorname{SO}_{2}(\mathbb{R}))
action on
\mathbb{H}^{\infty}
.
∎

Recall that for a Hilbert space
\mathcal{H}
and a collection of self-adjoint bounded linear operators
M\subset\mathcal{B}(\mathcal{H})
, the
(bounded) commutants
of
M
, denoted by
M^{\prime}
, is the collection of the bounded linear operators on
\mathcal{H}
that commute with all operators
A\in M
,
i.e.

M^{\prime}\coloneqq\{B\in\mathcal{B}(\mathcal{H})\mid AB=BA,~\forall A\in M\}~.

Similarly, we say that
M\subset\mathcal{B}(\mathcal{H})
is
irreducible
if there is no proper non-trivial
M
-invariant subspace in
\mathcal{H}
. The following lemma is a sufficient condition for irreducibility of representations and is classical in representation theory.

Lemma 3.6
.

Let
\mathcal{H}
be a Hilbert space and
M\subset\mathcal{B}(\mathcal{H})
be a collection of self-adjoint bounded linear operators. If
M^{\prime}=\{v\mapsto rv\mid r\in\mathbb{R}\}
, then
M
is irreducible.

Proof.

Suppose that
M
has a proper invariant subspace
V\subset\mathcal{H}
. Then consider the orthogonal projection
P\colon\mathcal{H}\to V
. It is not difficult to verify that
P\in M^{\prime}
by checking directly the commutativity. But the only possibility that such an orthogonal projection
P
is of the form
v\mapsto rv
for some
r\in\mathbb{R}
is when
r=0
,
i.e.
the
M
-invariant proper subspace
V=\{0\}
.
∎

With Lemma
3.6
, we are able to deduce the irreducibility of the representation
\rho
.

Proposition 3.7
.

The representation
\rho\colon\operatorname{PSL}_{2}(\mathbb{R})\to\Isom(\mathbb{H}^{\infty})
is irreducible.

Proof.

We first remark that due to our construction of
\mathbb{H}^{\infty}
, showing the irreducibility of
\rho
is equivalent to proving that the
\operatorname{PSL}_{2}(\mathbb{R})
-action on
H^{1}_{\operatorname{even}}
defined by (
3.1
) is irreducible. By Lemma
3.6
, it suffices to show that its commutants are multiplications by constant. Indeed, let
P
be a commutant of
\rho(\operatorname{PSL}_{2}(\mathbb{R}))
. Then we have

\rho(A)P\mathbf{1}=P\rho(A)\mathbf{1}=P\mathbf{1}

for any
A\in\mathrm{SO}_{2}(\mathbb{R})<\operatorname{PSL}_{2}(\mathbb{R})
. This implies that
P\mathbf{1}
is also a constant function
c\mathbf{1}
. More generally, for any
A\in\operatorname{PSL}_{2}(\mathbb{R})
, we have after (
3.3
)

P\textsc{supp}(AD)=\rho(A)P\mathbf{1}=c\rho(A)\mathbf{1}=c\textsc{supp}(AD)~.

Since
P
is a linear map, we have
Ph=ch
for every
h
that is a linear combination of support functions of ellipses, see Remark
3.1
. Since
P
is bounded and is thus continuous, now applying Corollary
2.20
, we can conclude that
P\colon h\mapsto ch
for every
h\in H^{1}_{\operatorname{even}}
.
∎

Remark 3.8
.

In
[
MP14
]
, continuous representations of
\operatorname{PSL}_{2}(\mathbb{R})\to\Isom(\mathbb{H}^{\infty})
are also constructed by building non-totally geodesic
\operatorname{PSL}_{2}(\mathbb{R})
-equivariant embeddings of
\mathbb{H}^{2}
into
\mathbb{H}^{\infty}
. But the ambient space
\mathbb{H}^{\infty}
constructed in
[
MP14
]
can admit a
\operatorname{PSL}_{2}(\mathbb{R})
-invariant proper subspace. In order to obtain irreducible representations, it is necessary to pass to a proper subspace of
\mathbb{H}^{\infty}
, which remains still infinite-dimensional. In contrast, for the construction via convex bodies, Proposition
3.7
shows that there is no need to pass to a subspace.

3.2
Exotic embedding of the hyperbolic plane

Recall that
\mathcal{E}_{c}^{\pi}\subset\mathcal{K}_{c}^{\pi}
is the collection of symmetric ellipses in
\mathbb{R}^{2}
of area
\pi
. This space is homogeneous for
\operatorname{SL}_{2}(\mathbb{R})
since every ellipse of
\mathcal{E}_{c}^{\pi}
can be realised as an image of the unit disk
D\in\mathcal{E}_{c}^{\pi}
under a linear transformation of
\operatorname{SL}_{2}(\mathbb{R})
. First note that from (
3.1
)
and (
3.3
), the support function of an ellipse is given by

\textsc{supp}(AD)(x)=|A^{\intercal}x|~.

(3.4)

Note also that for each ellipse
E\in\mathcal{E}_{c}^{\pi}
, its stabiliser
\Stab(E)<\operatorname{SL}_{2}(\mathbb{R})
is conjugate to
\operatorname{SO}_{2}(\mathbb{R})
in
\operatorname{SL}_{2}(\mathbb{R})
. It turns out that this space can be identified with the real hyperbolic plane
\mathbb{H}^{2}
. More precisely, if we consider the Poincaré upper half-plane model for
\mathbb{H}^{2}
, then we have a well-defined map

\displaystyle\begin{split}\operatorname{E}\colon\mathbb{H}^{2}&\longrightarrow\mathcal{E}_{c}^{\pi}\subset\mathcal{K}_{c}^{\pi}\\
A(i)&\longmapsto AD\end{split}

(3.5)

where
A(i)
is the action by homography on
i\in\mathbb{H}^{2}
,
i.e.
if
A=\begin{pmatrix}a&b\\
c&d\end{pmatrix}
,
A(i)=\frac{ai+b}{ci+d}
.

Remark 3.9
.

Let us denote
T_{s}\coloneqq\operatorname{diag}(e^{2s},e^{-2s})
. It is easy to see that

d_{\mathbb{H}^{2}}(i,T_{s}(i))=s

and also that for any
A\in\operatorname{PSL}_{2}(\mathbb{R})
, there is
s\in\mathbb{R}
and
R,R^{\prime}\in\operatorname{SO}_{2}(\mathbb{R})
such that
A=R^{\prime}T_{s}R
.

Lemma 3.10
.

The map
\operatorname{E}
defined by (
3.5
) is a homeomorphism onto its image
\mathcal{E}_{c}^{\pi}\subset(\overline{\mathcal{K}}_{c},d_{\operatorname{Haus}})
.

Proof.

The map

\operatorname{E}
is clearly a bijection between
\mathbb{H}^{2}
and
\mathcal{E}_{c}^{\pi}
, and is clearly equivariant for the action of
\operatorname{PSL}_{2}(\mathbb{R})
, which acts continuously on both
\mathcal{E}_{c}^{\pi}
and
\mathbb{H}^{2}
. Hence
it is a continuous injective map. We will check that it is a proper map, that reduces to show that the preimage of a bounded set is bounded.

Let
A\in\operatorname{SL}_{2}(\mathbb{R})
such that
d_{\operatorname{Haus}}(AD,D)<C
.
By Lemma
2.7
and (
3.1
), it is easy to see that rotations are isometries for the Hausdorff distance, so we may only consider the case when
A=T_{s}=\operatorname{diag}(e^{s/2},e^{-s/2})
.
By our assumption, using Lemma
2.7
again and
(
3.4
), we have

C>d_{\operatorname{Haus}}(T_{s}D,D)=\sup_{\theta}\left|\left|T_{s}^{\intercal}\binom{\cos\theta}{\sin\theta}\right|-1\right|=|e^{s/2}-1|.

Hence, in our case,
1\leq e^{s/2}<1+C
, and

d_{\mathbb{H}^{2}}(T_{s}(i),i)=s
is clearly bounded.
∎

Proposition 3.11
.

The map

\iota\coloneqq\textsc{supp}^{\pi}\big|_{\mathcal{E}_{c}^{\pi}}\circ\operatorname{E}:\mathbb{H}^{2}\to\mathbb{H}^{\infty}

is a
C^{\infty}
-embedding.

Proof.

By Proposition
2.15
and Lemma
3.10
,
\iota
is a topological embedding. As
\rho(\operatorname{PSL}_{2}(\mathbb{R}))
acts by isometries onto
\iota(\mathbb{H}^{2})
, it suffices to prove that it is
C^{\infty}
in a neighbourhood of
i\in\mathbb{H}^{2}
and of maximal rank at
i
,
i.e.
the image of the derivative
\mathrm{d}_{i}\iota\colon T_{i}\mathbb{H}^{2}\to T_{\iota(i)}\mathbb{H}^{\infty}
is of maximal dimension
2
.

Let
s>0
and
A_{t,s}=\begin{pmatrix}s&st\\
0&1/s\end{pmatrix}
.
We will parametrise a neighbourhood of
i
in the Poincaré upper half-plane by a neighbourhood
U
of
(0,1)\in\mathbb{R}^{2}
using

(t,s)\mapsto(A_{t,s}(i))=s^{2}i+s^{2}t~.

Abusing notation, we denote
\iota:U\to\mathbb{H}^{\infty}
the composition of
\iota
with the above map.

As

\iota(t,s)=\iota(A_{t,s}(i))=\rho(A_{t,s})\iota(i)=\rho(A_{t,s})\mathbf{1}

by (
3.1
),

\iota(t,s)(\theta)=\left|A_{t,s}^{\intercal}\binom{\cos\theta}{\sin\theta}\right|~.

Note that if
(t,s)
is sufficiently close to
(0,1)
, then
(t,s,\theta)\mapsto\iota(t,s)(\theta)
is uniformly bounded from below by a positive constant
c
. Indeed,
let
0<a<1
and
0<c<a
. Then for any
s,1/s\in(a,\frac{1}{a})
,
|t|<a^{2}-c^{2}
, and for all
\theta\in[0,2\pi]
,

\displaystyle\iota^{2}(t,s)(\theta)=

\displaystyle\left|A_{t,s}^{\intercal}\binom{\cos\theta}{\sin\theta}\right|^{2}=(s^{2}+s^{2}t^{2})\cos^{2}\theta+\frac{1}{s^{2}}\sin^{2}\theta+2t\cos\theta\sin\theta

\displaystyle\geq

\displaystyle s^{2}\cos^{2}\theta+\frac{1}{s^{2}}\sin^{2}\theta+2t\cos\theta\sin\theta\geq a^{2}-|t|>c^{2}~.

Similarly, we can show that it is also uniformly bounded from above by a constant
C>0
.

We will consider
\iota
as a map from
U
to

(H^{1}_{\operatorname{even}},\|\cdot\|_{H^{1}})
, that will give the wanted result by Lemma
2.4
. Let
X
be the matrix
\begin{pmatrix}0&0\\
1&0\end{pmatrix}
, so that
A_{t+h,s}^{\intercal}=A_{t,s}^{\intercal}+shX
.
The functions

\displaystyle f_{h}(\theta)\coloneqq

\displaystyle\frac{\iota(t+h,s)(\theta)-\iota(t,s)(\theta)}{h}=\frac{\iota^{2}(t+h,s)(\theta)-\iota^{2}(t,s)(\theta)}{h(\iota(t+h,s)(\theta)+\iota(t,s)(\theta))}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{\langle sX\binom{\cos\theta}{\sin\theta},(2A_{t,s}^{\intercal}+shX)\binom{\cos\theta}{\sin\theta}\rangle}{\iota(t+h,s)(\theta)+\iota(t,s)(\theta)}

pointwise converge when
h\to 0
to

\frac{\partial\iota}{\partial t}(t,s)(\theta)=\frac{\langle\binom{0}{s\cos},A_{t,s}^{\intercal}\binom{\cos\theta}{\sin\theta}\rangle}{\iota(t,s)(\theta)}~.

For
|h|
sufficiently small, the functions
f_{h}
are bounded by a constant function that does not depend on
h
. Hence by the Lebesgue dominated convergence theorem,
f_{h}
converges to
\frac{\partial\iota}{\partial t}(t,s)
in
L^{2}(\mathcal{S}^{1})
as
h\to 0
.
It is also clear that as a function in
\theta
, the function
f_{h}^{\prime}
pointwise converges to
\frac{\partial\iota}{\partial t}(t,s)^{\prime}
, and is uniformly bounded by a constant function that does not depend on
h
. Then by the Lebesgue dominated convergence theorem,
f_{h}^{\prime}
converges in
L^{2}(\mathcal{S}^{1})
to
\frac{\partial\iota}{\partial t}(t,s)^{\prime}
. As consequence,
f_{h}
converges to
\frac{\partial\iota}{\partial t}(t,s)
in
H^{1}_{\operatorname{even}}
, that is then the first partial derivative of
\iota
at
(t,s)
.

In the same way, a direct computation shows that
the limit when
h\to 0
of

g_{h}(\theta)=\frac{\iota(t,s+h)(\theta)-\iota(t,s)(\theta)}{h}

and is equal to

\frac{\partial\iota}{\partial s}(t,s)(\theta)=\frac{\left\langle\begin{pmatrix}1&0\\
t&-\frac{1}{s^{2}}\end{pmatrix}\binom{\cos\theta}{\sin\theta},A_{t,s}^{\intercal}\binom{\cos\theta}{\sin\theta}\right\rangle}{\iota(t,s)(\theta)}~.

The same argument gives that those two partial derivatives are continuous in
H^{1}_{\operatorname{even}}
around
(0,1)\in\mathbb{H}^{2}
. Actually, it is not difficult to see that all derivatives (with respect to
s,t
or
\theta
) of those functions are uniformly bounded by constants, hence applying recursively the arguments above, it follows that
\iota
is a
C^{\infty}
-map.

At
(0,1)\in\mathbb{H}^{2}
,

\frac{\partial\iota}{\partial t}(0,1)(\theta)=\cos(\theta)\sin(\theta),\quad\frac{\partial\iota}{\partial s}(0,1)(\theta)=2\cos^{2}(\theta)-1

and it is easy to verify that these two vectors are independent in
H^{1}_{\operatorname{even}}
, hence
\iota
has maximal rank at
(0,1)
.
∎

We now compute the extrinsic distance between two points of
\iota(\mathbb{H}^{2})
.

Lemma 3.12
.

For any
A\in\operatorname{SL}_{2}(\mathbb{R})
, we have

d_{\mathbb{H}^{\infty}}(\iota(A(i)),\iota(i))=\cosh^{-1}\Big(\frac{1}{2\pi}\int_{0}^{2\pi}|A^{\intercal}(\cos\theta,\sin\theta)^{\intercal}\big|~\mathrm{d}\theta\Big).

(3.6)

Proof.

Let
A\in\operatorname{SL}_{2}(\mathbb{R})
. The distance induced by
\mathbf{A}
onto
\mathbb{H}^{\infty}
is given by

d_{\mathbb{H}^{\infty}}(h,k)=\cosh^{-1}\mathbf{A}(h,k)

hence we have

\displaystyle d_{\mathbb{H}^{\infty}}(\iota(A(i)),\iota(i))

\displaystyle=\cosh^{-1}\Big(\mathbf{A}(\textsc{supp}(AD),\textsc{supp}(D))\Big)

\displaystyle=\cosh^{-1}\Big(\mathbf{A}(\textsc{supp}(AD),\mathbf{1})\Big)

\displaystyle=\cosh^{-1}\Big(\frac{1}{2\pi}\int_{0}^{2\pi}\textsc{supp}(AD)\Big),

and the result follows by (
3.4
).
∎

Let
g_{\iota}
be the pull-back over the upper half-plane by
\iota
of the metric induced by
g_{\mathbb{H}^{\infty}}
over
\iota(\mathcal{E}_{c}^{\pi})
.

Proposition 3.13
.

We have
g_{\iota}=\frac{3}{8}g_{\mathbb{H}^{2}}
, in particular the metric
g_{\iota}
has constant negative curvature
-\frac{8}{3}
.

Proof.

The group

\operatorname{PSL}_{2}(\mathbb{R})
acts isometrically and transitively on the tangent vectors of both metrics, so it suffices to prove the assertion at one point and at one tangent vector at that point. For
s>0
, on the one hand, for
d_{\mathbb{H}^{2}}
the path
c(s)=\begin{pmatrix}e^{s/2}&0\\
0&e^{-s/2}\end{pmatrix}(i)
is a geodesic, hence

g_{\mathbb{H}^{2}}(c^{\prime}(0),c^{\prime}(0))=1~,

and on the other hand

\displaystyle d_{\mathbb{H}^{\infty}}\big(\iota(c(s)),\mathbf{1}\big)

\displaystyle\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\eqref{eq:extrinsic distance}}}{{=}}\cosh^{-1}\left(\frac{1}{2\pi}\int_{0}^{2\pi}\sqrt{e^{s}\cos(\theta)^{2}+e^{-s}\sin(\theta)^{2}}~\mathrm{d}\theta\right)

\displaystyle=\cosh^{-1}\left(1+\frac{3s^{2}}{16}+o(s^{2})\right)

\displaystyle=\ln\left(1+o(s)+\sqrt{\left(1+\frac{3s^{2}}{16}+o(s^{2})\right)^{2}-1}\right)

\displaystyle=\ln\left(1+o(s)+\sqrt{\frac{3}{8}}s\sqrt{1+\epsilon(s)}\right)

\displaystyle=\ln\left(1+\sqrt{\frac{3}{8}}s+o(s)\right)=\sqrt{\frac{3}{8}}s+o(s)~.

Therefore,

\sqrt{g_{\iota}(c^{\prime}(0),c^{\prime}(0))}=\sqrt{g_{\mathbb{H}^{\infty}}(\iota_{*}(c^{\prime}(0)),\iota_{*}(c^{\prime}(0)))}=\lim_{s\searrow 0}\frac{d_{\mathbb{H}^{\infty}}\big(\iota(c(t)),\mathbf{1}\big)}{s}=\sqrt{\frac{3}{8}}

which proves the desired result.
∎

Since the curvature of
\iota(\mathbb{H}^{2})
is lower than the ambient curvature
-1
,
\iota(\mathbb{H}^{2})
is not totally geodesic.

The above descriptions lead to the study of the shape of the surface
\iota(\mathbb{H}^{2})\subset\mathbb{H}^{\infty}
.

Proposition 3.14
.

The surface
\iota(\mathbb{H}^{2})
is a minimal surface.

Proof.

The argument comes from
[
DP12
]
, we give it in full details for convenience.
Let
(e_{1},e_{2})
be an orthonormal basis of
T_{\mathbf{1}}\iota(\mathbb{H}^{2})
. The surface
\iota(\mathbb{H}^{2})
is minimal if

\eta\coloneqq-\tilde{D}^{N}_{\tilde{e}_{1}}\tilde{e}_{1}-\tilde{D}^{N}_{\tilde{e}_{2}}\tilde{e}_{2}=0

for any extension
\tilde{e_{i}}
of
e_{i}
(
i=1,2
) to
\mathbb{H}^{\infty}
, where
\tilde{D}^{N}_{\tilde{e}_{i}}\tilde{e}_{i}
is the normal part of
the
\mathbb{H}^{\infty}
Levi-Civita connection

\tilde{D}_{\tilde{e}_{i}}\tilde{e}_{i}
, see
[
GHL04
, Remark 5.21]
. Note that as a trace,
\eta
does not depend on the choice of the orthonormal basis.

Suppose that
\eta
is non zero. Then it is transverse to
\iota(\mathbb{H}^{2})
. Let
f\in\rho(\operatorname{SO}_{2}(\mathbb{R}))
. The point
\mathbf{1}
is fixed by
f
, and
f
acts isometrically on
T_{\mathbf{1}}\iota(\mathbb{H}^{2})
. It is also
an isometry of
T_{\mathbf{1}}\mathbb{H}^{\infty}
, and preserves the orthogonal splitting given by
T_{\mathbf{1}}\iota(\mathbb{H}^{2})
and its orthogonal part, as well as the connection. In turn, the vector
\eta
is invariant for
f
.
Hence, the geodesic passing through
\mathbf{1}
and directed by
\eta
is invariant for
f
, and as
f
is an isometry, it acts by translation on the geodesic. But
f
fixes a point on this geodesic, this implies that it fixes the geodesic pointwise. This contradicts the fact that
\mathbf{1}
is the only fixed point of
f
(
cf.
Lemma
3.5
). So
\eta
must be zero.
∎

Proposition 3.15
.

There exists a constant
C
such that for all
x,y\in\mathbb{H}^{2}
,

\left|d_{\mathbb{H}^{\infty}}(\iota(x),\iota(y))-\frac{1}{2}d_{\mathbb{H}^{2}}(x,y)\right|\leq C~.

Proof.

By transitivity of the action of
\operatorname{PSL}_{2}(\mathbb{R})
over
\mathbb{H}^{2}
, it suffices to prove the result for
x=i
and
y=T_{s}(i)
, with
T_{s}=\operatorname{diag}(e^{s/2},e^{-s/2})
.

Note that
\left|T_{s}^{\intercal}(\cos\theta,\sin\theta)^{\intercal}\right|=\sqrt{e^{s}\cos^{2}\theta+e^{-s}\sin^{2}\theta}
. We can estimate that

\left|T_{s}^{\intercal}(\cos\theta,\sin\theta)^{\intercal}\right|\leq\sqrt{e^{s}(\cos^{2}\theta+\sin^{2}\theta)}=e^{s/2}

and that

\left|T_{s}^{\intercal}(\cos\theta,\sin\theta)^{\intercal}\right|\geq\sqrt{e^{s}\cos^{2}\theta}=e^{s/2}|\cos\theta|~.

Hence
\cosh^{-1}(2e^{s/2}/\pi)\leq d_{\mathbb{H}^{\infty}}(\iota(T_{s}(i)),\iota(i))\leq\cosh^{-1}(e^{s/2})
after Lemma
3.12
.
On the one hand
d_{\mathbb{H}^{2}}(T_{s}(i),i)=s
, and on the other hand

\cosh^{-1}(x)=\ln(x)+\ln(1+\sqrt{1-1/x^{2}})
,
hence

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}=\lim_{s\to+\infty}\frac{\cosh^{-1}(2e^{s/2}/\pi)}{s}\leq\lim_{s\to+\infty}\frac{d_{\mathbb{H}^{\infty}}(\iota(T_{s}(i)),\iota(i))}{d_{\mathbb{H}^{2}}(T_{s}(i),i)}

\displaystyle\quad\leq\lim_{s\to+\infty}\frac{\cosh^{-1}(e^{s/2})}{s}=\frac{1}{2}~.

∎

The following fact seems to be well-known, but we are not able to find a reference, so we will provide here a simple proof, provided to us by Will Sawin
[
Saw
]
.
Note that the same arguments remain valid for ellipsoids in higher dimensions as well.

Proposition 3.16
.

Let
(E_{k})_{k}\subset\mathcal{E}_{c}^{\pi}
be pairwise non-homothetic ellipses. Then for any
n\geq 2
and any positive constants
(c_{k})_{k}
, the Minkowski sum
\sum_{k=1}^{n}c_{k}E_{k}
is not again an ellipse.

Proof.

From (
3.4
), we can see that the support function of an ellipse is the square root of a quadratic form. In particular, considering the one-homogeneous extension of the support function to
\mathbb{R}^{2}
, for each
k
, we can write for any
(x,y)\in\mathbb{R}^{2}

\textsc{supp}(E_{k})(x,y)=\sqrt{Q_{k}(x,y)}~,

where
Q_{k}(x,y)
is a quadratic form on
\mathbb{R}^{2}
. Each of these support functions
\sqrt{Q_{k}(x,y)}
can then extend to multibranched holomorphic functions on
\mathbb{C}^{2}-\Lambda_{k}
, where
\Lambda_{k}
consists of the singularities of
\sqrt{Q_{k}(x,y)}
and is a union of two complex lines. Note that
E_{n}
and
E_{m}
are homothetic if and only if
\Lambda_{n}=\Lambda_{m}
. However, the support function

\textsc{supp}\left(\sum_{k=1}^{n}c_{k}E_{k}\right)=\sum_{k=1}^{n}c_{k}\sqrt{Q_{k}}

has singularities
\bigcup_{k=1}^{n}\Lambda_{k}
, which cannot be again a union of two complex lines. Hence, the Minkowski sum
\sum_{k=1}^{n}c_{k}E_{k}
is not an ellipse.
∎

A consequence of the above proposition is the following result on the dimension of hyperbolic space spanned by points on the minimal surface
\iota(\mathbb{H}^{2})\subset\mathbb{H}^{\infty}
:

Corollary 3.17
.

Any
n+1
distinct points in
\iota(\mathbb{H}^{2})\subset\mathbb{H}^{\infty}
generate a hyperbolic subspace of dimension
n
in
\mathbb{H}^{\infty}
.

Proof.

Suppose for contradiction that
n+1
distinct points
\big(\textsc{supp}(E_{k})\big)_{k}
in
\iota(\mathbb{H}^{2})\subset\mathbb{H}^{\infty}
generate a hyperbolic subspace of dimension
d<n
. Then it implies that there exists at least a point, say
\textsc{supp}(E_{n+1})
, contained in the convex hull of the others. By virtue of Remark
2.16
, this would imply that

E_{n+1}=\sum_{k=1}^{n}c_{k}E_{k}

for some constant
c_{k}\geq 0
, contradicting Proposition
3.16
.
∎

3.3
The closed convex hull

In this subsection, we derive several properties of
C_{\mathcal{K}}\coloneqq\textsc{supp}^{\pi}(\mathcal{K}_{c}^{\pi})
.

Here, we recall that a subset
C\subset(X,d)
in a geodesic metric space is
convex
if for any
x,y\in C
, all minimizing geodesics between
x
and
y
are contained in
C
.

Proposition 3.18
.

C_{\mathcal{K}}

is a closed convex set in
(\mathbb{H}^{\infty},d_{\mathbb{H}^{\infty}})
, and a proper metric space for the induced distance.

Proof.

It follows from (
2.10
) and (
2.11
) that
\textsc{supp}(\overline{\mathcal{K}}_{c})
is a convex cone in
H^{1}_{\operatorname{even}}
, and hence
\textsc{supp}^{\pi}(\mathcal{K}_{c}^{\pi})
is a convex subset of
\mathbb{H}^{\infty}
.
The closeness is given by Lemma
2.10
and Lemma
2.4
.

Let
X
be a bounded closed set of
\textsc{supp}^{\pi}(\overline{\mathcal{K}}_{c})
. By Lemma
2.13
its preimage is closed, and bounded by Lemma
2.12
, hence compact by
Proposition
2.8
. So
X
is compact, again by the continuity of
\textsc{supp}^{\pi}
.
∎

As segments have zero area,
\textsc{supp}(\mathrm{S})
is a subset of the isotropic cone of
\mathbf{A}
. In the following we will identify it as a subset of the boundary at infinity of
\mathbb{H}^{\infty}
. In the rest of this subsection, we will write
\operatorname{CH}_{\mathbb{H}^{\infty}}
the closed convex hull in
\mathbb{H}^{\infty}
.

Let
\Gamma\subset\partial_{\infty}\mathbb{H}^{\infty}
be the subset defined by the image of the segments
\textsc{supp}(\mathrm{S})
. Let
\operatorname{CH}_{\mathbb{H}^{\infty}}(\Gamma)
be the intersection of all the closed convex subsets of
\mathbb{H}^{\infty}
whose boundary at infinity contains
\operatorname{CH}_{\mathbb{H}^{\infty}}(\Gamma)
. With these notations, Proposition
2.19
gives the following:

Corollary 3.19
.

We have
C_{\mathcal{K}}=\operatorname{CH}_{\mathbb{H}^{\infty}}(\Gamma)
,
\partial_{\infty}C_{K}=\Gamma
and
C_{\mathcal{K}}=\operatorname{CH}_{\mathbb{H}^{\infty}}(\iota(\mathbb{H}^{2}))
.
\Box

Now we can deduce the following result. Its proof is taken from Lemma 4.1 in
[
MP14
]
.

Lemma 3.20
.

The subset
C_{\mathcal{K}}
is the minimal (with respect to inclusion) closed convex invariant subspace of
\mathbb{H}^{\infty}
for
\rho(\operatorname{PSL}_{2}(\mathbb{R}))

Proof.

If
C^{\prime}
is a
\rho(\operatorname{PSL}_{2}(\mathbb{R}))
-invariant closed convex set, it must contain
\mathbf{1}
by Lemma
3.5
. Hence it must contain the orbit of
\mathbf{1}
that is
\iota(\mathbb{H}^{2})
, and by Corollary
3.19
, it contains
C_{\mathcal{K}}
.
∎

Note that
\mathbb{H}^{\infty}
is a
\operatorname{CAT}(-1)
space. For any closed convex subset in
\mathbb{H}^{\infty}
, there exists a unique point with the shortest
d_{\mathbb{H}^{\infty}}
distance to
\mathbf{1}\in\mathbb{H}^{\infty}
. By (
2.16
), the distance

d_{\mathbb{H}^{\infty}}(\mathbf{1},h)=\cosh^{-1}\pi_{0}(h)~,

and in particular, if
h=\textsc{supp}(K)\in\textsc{supp}(\mathcal{K}_{c}^{\pi})
, then

d_{\mathbb{H}^{\infty}}(\mathbf{1},h)=\cosh^{-1}\left(\frac{1}{2\pi}\mathrm{per}(K)\right).

As
\cosh^{-1}
increases monotonically, having the shortest
d_{\mathbb{H}^{\infty}}
distance for
h
in a closed convex subset of
\textsc{supp}(\mathcal{K}_{c}^{\pi})
is equivalent to being the optimal solution to the isoperimetric problem in this convex subset.

From the above discussions as well as Remark
2.16
, we can see that the bi-infinite geodesic line in
C_{\mathcal{K}}\subset\mathbb{H}^{\infty}
connecting
\nu,\omega\in\Gamma
consists of the support functions of parallelograms of area
\pi
homothetic to
av+bw
for some
a,b>0
, where
\textsc{supp}(v)=\nu
and
\textsc{supp}(w)=\omega
. If, in particular, we wish to find the nearest point projection of
\mathbf{1}
to this bi-infinite geodesic line, it is equivalent to find the optimal solution to the isoperimetric problem among these parallelograms.

Indeed, we can deduce the following result that will be used:

Lemma 3.21
.

Let
\nu,\omega\in\Gamma
be distinct. Let
h
be the nearest point projection of
\mathbf{1}
to the bi-infinite geodesic line connecting
\nu,\omega\in\Gamma
, with
v,w\in\mathcal{S}
unit symmetric segments satisfying
\textsc{supp}(v)=\nu
and
\textsc{supp}(w)=\omega
. Then
h=\textsc{supp}(P)
for
P\in\mathcal{K}_{c}^{\pi}
homothetic to
v+w
,
i.e.
a rhombus.

Proof.

Note that
\mathrm{per}(av+bw)=2(a+b)
and
\textsc{area}(av+bw)=ab\det(v,w)=\pi
. By the AM-GM inequality, as
ab=\pi/\det(v,w)
is a constant, the quantity
a+b
is minimised if and only if
a=b
, and it is when
d_{\mathbb{H}^{\infty}}(h,\mathbf{1})
is minimised.
∎

It is proved in Proposition 4.4 in
[
MP14
]
that

\rho(\operatorname{PSL}_{2}(\mathbb{R}))
is the isometry group of
C_{\mathcal{K}}
using representation theory. Here below, we will provide an alternative proof with more geometric flavour.

Proposition 3.22
.

The isometry group
\Isom(C_{\mathcal{K}})
is isomorphic to
\operatorname{PSL}_{2}(\mathbb{R})
and is exactly the subgroup in
\Isom(\mathbb{H}^{\infty})
that preserves
C_{\mathcal{K}}
.

Proof.

For the metric induced over
C_{\mathcal{K}}
by the hyperbolic metric, any element in
\rho(\operatorname{PSL}_{2}(\mathbb{R}))
induces an isometry on
C_{\mathcal{K}}
. Moreover,
\rho
is faithful (Remark
3.4
), hence
we can view
\operatorname{PSL}_{2}(\mathbb{R})
as a subgroup of
\Isom(C_{\mathcal{K}})
. Now it suffices to show it is the entire group.

Given any
g\in\Isom(C_{\mathcal{K}})
, it sends bi-infinite geodesics to bi-infinite geodesics,
i.e.
it may be identified with a bijection among symmetric parallelograms (a sum of two segments) of area
\pi
. More specifically, let
P=\sqrt{\pi}[0,1]^{2}
be the centred square in
\mathcal{K}_{c}^{\pi}
. By Lemma
3.21
, it is the nearest point projection of
\mathbf{1}
to a bi-infinite geodesic. Since
g
is an isometry, then
gP
will be nearest point projection of
g\mathbf{1}
to the
g
-image of that bi-infinite geodesic, which is again a parallelogram. Let
v_{1},v_{2}
be the two independent vectors such that
\sqrt{\pi}v_{1}
and
\sqrt{\pi}v_{2}
are the vectors of two sides of the parallelogram
gP
and such that the symmetric unit segment parallel to
v_{1}
(resp.
v_{2}
) is the
g
-image of the vertical (resp. horizontal) symmetric unit vector, viewed as elements in
\Gamma
. In particular, we have
|\det(v_{1},v_{2})|=\textsc{area}(gP)/\pi=1
. Now the isometry
g
can be associated to the unique element in
\operatorname{PSL}_{2}(\mathbb{R})
sending
\big((0,1),(1,0)\big)
to
(v{{}_{1},v_{2}})
. Moreover, this gives a well-defined group homomorphism
\Isom(C_{\mathcal{K}})\to\operatorname{PSL}_{2}(\mathbb{R})
, of which the inverse is the inclusion
\operatorname{PSL}_{2}(\mathbb{R})\hookrightarrow\Isom(C_{\mathcal{K}})
.
∎

Classical properties of convex bodies immediately translate into the following result. The first part was proved in Proposition 4.3 in
[
MP14
]
. Due to our characterisation of the convex hull, we can obtain a more precise description.

Theorem 3.23
.

The action of
\operatorname{PSL}_{2}(\mathbb{R})
on
C_{\mathcal{K}}
is proper and cocompact. Moreover, the quotient of
C_{\mathcal{K}}
is homeomorphic to the 2-dimensional oriented Banach–Mazur compactum.

Indeed, it is classical that the action of the affine group on convex bodies (with non-empty interior) gives a Hausdorff compact quotient (a
compactum
). It is for example a step in the proof of Benzécri’s compactness theorem about convex bodies in the projective plane, see
[
Gol22
]
. It also appears in the study of the (2-dimensional) Banach–Mazur compactum. Recall that it is the set of isometry classes of 2-dimensional Banach spaces, endowed with a natural distance. It is a compact metric space. It is naturally homeomorphic to the quotient of
C_{\mathcal{K}}
(endowed with the topology given by the Hausdorff distance) by
\operatorname{GL}_{2}(\mathbb{R})
. Its topological properties are well studied, see e.g.
[
AB98
,
Rep99
,
AR00
,
Ant00
]
. See
[
Mac51
]
for a different approach.

As we are considering equivalence classes of convex bodies with fixed area for the action of
\operatorname{SL}_{2}(\mathbb{R})
, our quotient is homeomorphic to the quotient of the space of convex bodies up to orientation preserving linear maps, that is a double cover of the Banach–Mazur compactum. It is called the
oriented Banach–Mazur compactum
in
[
AB98
]
. Those facts immediately yield Theorem
3.23
.

In the references mentioned above, all the proofs are based on a continuous retract by deformation from the space of convex bodies to the space of ellipses (different ellipses can be successfully associated to a convex body, and their denominations are not uniform in the literature, see
[
Sch14
, Section 10.12]
).

3.4
Hausdorff dimension of the limit set

Since
\Gamma
is the boundary at infinity of
\iota(\mathbb{H}^{2})\subset\mathbb{H}^{\infty}
and
\rho(\operatorname{PSL}_{2}(\mathbb{R}))
acts transitively on
\iota(\mathbb{H}^{2})
, it is clear that
\Gamma
is the limit set of
\rho(\operatorname{PSL}_{2}(\mathbb{R}))
. In this subsection, we will compute explicitly its Hausdorff dimension with respect to the visual distance on
\partial_{\infty}\mathbb{H}^{\infty}
.

Let
D_{\mathbf{1}}
be the visual distance on
\partial_{\infty}\mathbb{H}^{\infty}
based at
\mathbf{1}
. For our hyperbolic space, it has a very simple expression: if
\zeta,\eta\in\partial_{\infty}\mathbb{H}^{\infty}
,

D_{\mathbf{1}}(\zeta,\eta)=\sin\left(\frac{1}{2}\angle_{\mathbf{1}}(\zeta,\eta)\right)~

(3.7)

where
\angle_{\mathbf{1}}(\zeta,\eta)\in[0,\pi]
is the angle for the hyperbolic metric
between the unique geodesic rays starting at
\mathbf{1}
toward
\zeta
and
\eta
respectively, see
[
DSU17
, Section 3.5]
.

Recall that
\Gamma\subset\partial_{\infty}\mathbb{H}^{\infty}
is made of isotropic vectors for
\mathbf{A}
that correspond to directions in the plane (
i.e.
, each direction is a homothety class of symmetric segments).
We will denote by
\angle(\zeta,\eta)\in[0,\pi]
their angle in the plane.

Proposition 3.24
.

For two elements
\zeta,\eta
in
\Gamma
,
then

D_{\mathbf{1}}(\zeta,\eta)=\frac{\sqrt{\pi}}{2}\sqrt{\sin(\angle(\zeta,\eta))}~.

Proof.

We write (
3.7
)
under the form

D_{\mathbf{1}}(\zeta,\eta)=\sqrt{\frac{1-\cos(\angle_{\mathbf{1}}(\zeta,\eta))}{2}}

and we will compute the cosine of the angle.

The points
\zeta,\eta\in\partial_{\infty}\mathbb{H}^{\infty}
correspond to two isotropic lines for
\mathbf{A}
. Let
v_{1},v_{2}
be two vectors colinear to those lines, such that
\pi_{0}(v_{i})=\mathbf{A}(\mathbf{1},v_{i})=1
. The vectors
v_{i}-\mathbf{1}
are orthogonal to
L_{0}
and may be identified with vectors of
T_{\mathbf{1}}\mathbb{H}^{\infty}
, tangent to the geodesic rays of
\mathbb{H}^{\infty}
from
\mathbf{1}
to
\zeta
and
\eta
.
We have

\mathbf{A}(v_{1}-\mathbf{1},v_{2}-\mathbf{1})=\mathbf{A}(v_{1},v_{2})-1=\frac{1}{2}\mathbf{A}(v_{1}+v_{2})-1

where the last equality holds because the vectors are isotropic. Similarly,
\mathbf{A}(v_{1}-\mathbf{1})=-1
, hence

\cos(\angle_{\mathbf{1}}(\zeta,\eta))=\frac{-\mathbf{A}(v_{1}-\mathbf{1},v_{2}-\mathbf{1})}{\sqrt{-\mathbf{A}(v_{1}-\mathbf{1})}\sqrt{-\mathbf{A}(v_{2}-\mathbf{1})}}=1-\frac{1}{2}\mathbf{A}(v_{1}+v_{2})~,

and

D_{\mathbf{1}}(\zeta,\eta)=\frac{1}{2}\sqrt{\mathbf{A}(v_{1}+v_{2})}~.

By Remark
2.14
,
\mathbf{A}(v_{1}+v_{2})
is
\frac{1}{\pi}
times the area of the plane parallelogram obtained by adding the segments directed by the direction determined by
\zeta
and
\eta
. By (
2.16
), those segments have perimeter
2\pi\pi_{0}(v_{i})=2\pi
, hence length
\pi
. In turn, the area of the parallelogram is

\pi^{2}\sin(\angle(\zeta,\eta))
.
∎

We recall that for a compact metric space
(K,d)
, its
Hausdorff dimension
with respect to the distance
d
is defined as

\operatorname{Hdim}_{d}(K)\coloneqq\inf\{\delta>0\mid H^{\delta}(K)=0\}~,

where

H^{\delta}(K)=\lim_{r\to 0+}\inf\big\{\sum_{i=1}^{n}\mathrm{diam}(U_{i})^{\delta}\mid\bigcup_{i=1}^{n}U_{i}\supset K\ \operatorname{and}\ \mathrm{diam}(U_{i})<r\big\}~.

Remark 3.25
.

Note that if
0<t\leq 1
and
\lambda>0
, for a distance
d
,
\lambda d^{t}
is also a distance. Moreover, one can easily check from the definition that

\operatorname{Hdim}_{\lambda d^{t}}(K)=\frac{1}{t}\operatorname{Hdim}_{d}(K)

for
0<t\leq 1
and
\lambda>0
.

From the explicit formula of the visual distance
D_{\mathbf{1}}
in Proposition
3.24
, we can further deduce the Hausdorff distance of
\Gamma
:

Corollary 3.26
.

The Hausdorff dimension of
\Gamma
for the visual distance
D_{\mathbf{1}}
on
\partial_{\infty}\mathbb{H}^{\infty}
is
2
.

Proof.

The distance
\sin(\angle(\zeta,\eta))
is at first order the distance
\angle(\zeta,\eta)
on the round circle, which makes the circle of Hausdorff dimension one. Then the desired result follows from Remark
3.25
.
∎

More generally, if we consider other formulae of the visual distance in real hyperbolic space, then the result of the Hausdorff dimension of the exotic embedding of the boundaries
\partial_{\infty}\mathbb{H}^{n}\to\partial_{\infty}\mathbb{H}^{\infty}
will become a consequence of the quasi-isometry. Indeed, recall again that for
n\geq 2
, the exotic representations of
\Isom(\mathbb{H}^{n})\to\Isom(\mathbb{H}^{\infty})
are completely classified in
[
MP14
]
and are parametrised by
0<t<1
. Each exotic representation induces an orbit map
f^{n}_{t}\colon\mathbb{H}^{n}\to\mathbb{H}^{\infty}
verifying

\left|d_{\mathbb{H}^{\infty}}(f^{n}_{t}(x),f^{n}_{t}(y))-td_{\mathbb{H}^{n}}(x,y)\right|\leq C

(3.8)

for some constant
C>0
and any
x,y\in\mathbb{H}^{n}
. Recall also that the
Gromov product
on
\mathbb{H}^{n}\times\mathbb{H}^{n}
is defined as

\langle x,y\rangle_{z}\coloneqq\frac{1}{2}\big(d_{\mathbb{H}^{n}}(x,z)+d_{\mathbb{H}^{n}}(y,z)-d_{\mathbb{H}^{n}}(x,y)\big)

and can extends to
\partial_{\infty}\mathbb{H}^{n}
by

\langle\xi,\eta\rangle_{z}\coloneqq\lim_{\begin{subarray}{c}x\to\xi\\
y\to\eta\end{subarray}}\langle x,y\rangle_{z}~.

The same definition is valid for
\mathbb{H}^{\infty}
as well, see again
[
DSU17
, Section 3.5]
.

Proposition 3.27
.

Let
f^{n}_{t}\colon\mathbb{H}^{n}\to\mathbb{H}^{\infty}
be the quasi-isometric embedding as above. Then
f^{n}_{t}
extends continuously to the boundary
\partial f^{n}_{t}\colon\partial_{\infty}\mathbb{H}^{n}\hookrightarrow\partial_{\infty}\mathbb{H}^{\infty}
. Moreover, the Hausdorff dimension of
\partial f^{n}_{t}(\partial_{\infty}\mathbb{H}^{n})
with respect to the visual distance on
\partial_{\infty}\mathbb{H}^{\infty}
is
\frac{n-1}{t}
.

Proof.

Let
D_{o}^{n}
be the visual distance on
\partial_{\infty}\mathbb{H}^{n}
with respect to the unique fixed point
o\in\mathbb{H}^{n}
. Let us assume for consistency
f_{t}^{n}(o)=\mathbf{1}\in\mathbb{H}^{\infty}
and let
D_{\mathbf{1}}
be the visual distance as above. Recall from
[
DSU17
, Section 3.5]
that for
\xi,\eta\in\partial_{\infty}\mathbb{H}^{n}
,

D_{o}^{n}(\xi,\eta)=e^{-\langle\xi,\eta\rangle_{o}}

where
\langle\xi,\eta\rangle_{o}
is the Gromov product in
\mathbb{H}^{n}
. By (
3.8
), the embedding
f_{t}^{n}
is quasi-isometric, thus extends to the boundary. The continuity of
\partial f_{t}^{n}
can be seen from the comparison

\big(D^{n}_{o}(\xi,\eta)\big)^{t}=e^{-t\langle\xi,\eta\rangle_{o}}\approx e^{-\langle\partial f_{t}^{n}(\xi),\partial f_{t}^{n}(\eta)\rangle_{\mathbf{1}}}=D_{\mathbf{1}}\big(\partial f_{t}^{n}(\xi),\partial f_{t}^{n}(\eta)\big)~,

where
A\approx B
means that there exists a constant
c>1
such that
c^{-1}B\leq A\leq cB
. In turn, the two distances
(D^{n}_{o})^{t}
and
D_{\mathbf{1}}
are bi-Lipschitz and thus
\partial f_{t}^{n}
is a continuous map. Moreover, as
\partial f_{t}^{n}(\partial_{\infty}\mathbb{H}^{n})
is the continuous image of a compact set, it is itself compact and we can compute its Hausdorff dimension. Since
\operatorname{Hdim}_{D_{o}^{n}}(\partial_{\infty}\mathbb{H}^{n})=n-1
, the desired result soon follows from Remark
3.25
.
∎

4
Comparison with different constructions

In this section, we will caracterize the hyperbolic embedding of convex bodies in more detail. We then make a connection with kernels of hyperbolic type,
and end this section by comparing our construction of the exotic representations with the others.

4.1
Uniqueness of the embedding

Given a continuous representation
r\colon\operatorname{PSL}_{2}(\mathbb{R})\to\Isom(\mathbb{H}^{\infty})
, if there exists a unique fixed point
o\in\mathbb{H}^{\infty}
of
r(\operatorname{SO}_{2}(\mathbb{R}))
, then the orbit map of
o
is the only
r
-equivariant map. By Lemma
3.5
, we can deduce:

Lemma 4.1
.

The map
\textsc{supp}\big|_{\mathcal{E}_{c}^{\pi}}\colon\mathcal{E}_{c}^{\pi}\to\mathbb{H}^{\infty}
is the only
\rho
-equivariant map from
\mathcal{E}_{c}^{\pi}
into
\mathbb{H}^{\infty}
.
\Box

We now prove that, up to a convexity condition,
\textsc{supp}\big|_{\mathcal{E}_{c}^{\pi}}
extends uniquely to

\mathcal{K}_{c}^{\pi}
.
Let us define

l(K_{1},K_{2},t)\coloneqq\pi\frac{(1-t)K_{1}+tK_{2}}{\textsc{area}((1-t)K_{1}+tK_{2})}\in\mathcal{K}_{c}^{\pi}

for any
K_{1},K_{2}\in\mathcal{K}_{c}^{\pi}
and
0\leq t\leq 1
. In Lemma
4.1
, the
\rho
-equivariant map
\textsc{supp}\big|_{\mathcal{E}_{c}^{\pi}}
can be uniquely extended from
\mathcal{E}_{c}^{\pi}
to the whole
\mathcal{K}_{c}^{\pi}
, under the convexity condition that is given explicitly below:

Proposition 4.2
.

Suppose that
f\colon\mathcal{K}_{c}^{\pi}\to\mathbb{H}^{\infty}
is a continuous
\rho
-equivariant map such that
l(K_{1},K_{2},[0,1])
is sent to the geodesic segment between
f(K_{1})
and
f(K_{2})
for all
K_{1},K_{2}\in\mathcal{K}_{c}^{\pi}
, then
f
coincides with
supp
.

For proving Proposition
4.2
, let us see the following elementary lemma:

Lemma 4.3
.

Let
OAB
be a triangle in an affine space and let
m_{OA},m_{OB}
be the midpoints of the segments
[OA]
and
[OB]
. For any convex combination
P_{t}=(1-t)A+tB\in[A,B]
, where
t
is a dyadic number in
(0,1)
, one can construct the segments
[O,P_{t}]
with only straight lines passing through the points
O,A,B,m_{OA},m_{OB}
or the intersection points of already constructed lines. Moreover, each of these points
P_{t}
is adherent to the set of all such intersection points.

Proof.

It suffices to construct the midpoint
P_{1/2}
of the segment
[A,B]
. This allows to decompose the triangle
OAB
into two subtriangles
OAP_{1/2}
and
OP_{1/2}B
and iteratively divide these triangles to construct every dyadic point on
[A,B]
. Since the segment
[O,P_{1/2}]
is a median of the triangle
OAB
, it passes through its barycenter which is the intersection point between the two segments
[A,m_{OB}]
and
[B,m_{OA}]
, see Figure
4
. Moreover,
[m_{OA},m_{OB}]
and
[A,B]
being parallel, Thales’s theorem ensures that the intersection point between
[O,P_{1/2}]
and
[m_{OA},m_{OB}]
is at equal distance from
O
and
P_{1/2}
, allowing to iterate this procedure.

For the density part of the statement, observe that iterating the construction of Pappus’s theorem from the two triples of points
(m_{OA},m_{OP},m_{OB})
and
(A,P_{1/2},B)

4.2
Kernels of hyperbolic type

The notion of
kernel of (real) hyperbolic type
was introduced in
[
MP19
]
to embed a set into some real hyperbolic space, and it is used to construct exotic representations. However, we remind the reader that one should not confuse it with a kernel of complex hyperbolic type, both of which are defined in
[
Mon20
]
. Without further mention, all kernels of hyperbolic type in the sequel will be of real hyperbolic type.

Definition 4.4

(Kernel of hyperbolic type)
.

Let
X
be a non-empty set. A function
\beta\colon X\times X\to\mathbb{R}
is a
kernel of (real) hyperbolic type
if it is symmetric, non-negative, and equal to
1
on the diagonal with

\displaystyle\sum_{i,j=1}^{n}c_{i}c_{j}\beta(x_{i},x_{j})\leq\left(\sum_{k=1}^{n}c_{k}\beta(x_{k},x_{0})\right)^{2}

(4.1)

for all
n\geq 1
, any
x_{0},x_{1},\dots,x_{n}\in X
and any
c_{1},\dots,c_{n}\in\mathbb{R}
.

This notion is closely related to another notion called
kernel of positive type
. Indeed, by rearranging the terms in (
4.1
), we can see that
\beta(\cdot,\cdot)
is a kernel of hyperbolic type if and only if for one (and thus for any)
z\in X
, the function

\displaystyle N_{z}(x,y)\coloneqq\beta(x,z)\beta(y,z)-\beta(x,y)

(4.2)

is a kernel of positive type,
i.e.
a symmetric function satisfying the inequality

\sum_{i,j=1}^{n}c_{i}c_{j}N(x_{i},x_{j})\geq 0

for all
n\geq 1
, any
x_{1},\dots,x_{n}\in X
and any
c_{1},\dots,c_{n}\in\mathbb{R}
.

Given a kernel of positive type
N\colon X\times X\to\mathbb{R}
, the
GNS construction
(for Gelfand, Naimark, and Segal) will produce a Hilbert space
\mathcal{H}
, unique up to isomorphism, and a map
h\colon X\to\mathcal{H}
such that
\langle h(x),h(y)\rangle_{\mathcal{H}}=N(x,y)
for any
x,y\in X
. This space is a space of functions generated by
\Phi_{x}(y)\coloneqq N(x,y)
. For a proof of this, we refer the reader to
[
BdlHV08
, Theorem C.1.4]
.

Referring to the hyperboloid model
\mathbb{H}^{\infty}\subset\mathbb{R}\oplus\mathcal{H}
, given a non-void set
X
, if a function
\beta\colon X\times X\to\mathbb{R}
is a kernel of hyperbolic type and if
N_{x_{0}}\colon X\times X\to\mathbb{R}
is an associated kernel of positive type, then the map into hyperbolic space
\phi\colon X\to\mathbb{H}^{\infty}
will be given by

\phi(x)\coloneqq\big(\beta(x,x_{0}),h(x)\big)~,

where
x_{0}\in X
is a fixed base point.

For any quadratic form defining a
\mathbb{H}^{\infty}
as in (
2.1
), the restriction of
B
to
\mathbb{H}^{\infty}
is a kernel of hyperbolic type. Indeed, (
4.1
) is the reversed Cauchy–Schwarz inequality, see (
2.2
):
if
X=\sum_{i=1}^{n}c_{i}x_{i}
and
Y=x_{0}
, then by bilinearity
(
4.1
) is equivalent to

1=B(X,X)=B(X,X)B(Y,Y)\leq\left(B(X,Y)\right)^{2}~.

In particular, the restriction
of our form
\mathbf{A}
to
\mathbb{H}^{\infty}
is also a kernel of hyperbolic type. Since the definition of kernel of hyperbolic type obviously
passes to the restriction to any subset,

\mathbf{A}
is also a kernel of hyperbolic type on
\textsc{supp}^{\pi}(\mathcal{K}_{c}^{\pi})
. Interpreting this fact in term of mixed-area (see Remark
2.11
), we obtain the following result:

Proposition 4.5
.

Let
K_{0},K_{1},\ldots,K_{n}
be symmetric plane convex bodies of positive area. Then for any
c_{1},\ldots,c_{n}\in\mathbb{R}
, then

\textsc{area}(K_{0})\sum_{i,j=1}^{n}c_{i}c_{j}\textsc{area}(K_{i},K_{j})\leq\left(\sum_{k=1}^{n}c_{k}\textsc{area}(K_{k},K_{0})\right)^{2}~.

We remark that the coefficients can be negative. If
K=c_{1}K_{1}+\cdots+c_{n}K_{n}
with all
c_{i}
being positive, then by properties of the mixed area, the inequality
above writes

\textsc{area}(K_{0})\textsc{area}(K)\leq\left(\textsc{area}(K,K_{0})\right)^{2}~,

which is the Minkowski inequality (
2.15
).

For convex bodies (non necessary symmetric) of higher dimensions (including infinite ones), the polarisation of the second intrinsic volume is also a kernel of hyperbolic type
[
DF22
,
Lon25
]
, hence, the result of Proposition
4.5
also holds in those cases.

4.3
Relation with the Delzant–Monod–Py construction

We remark that on the same space, one can define different kernels which may yield embeddings of the space into
\mathbb{H}^{\infty}
with quasi-isometric images. But these quasi-isometries between different images may not necessarily reduce to an isometry.

For example, on the one hand,
[
Mon20
, Corollary 8.3]
states that
e^{\frac{1}{2}d_{\mathbb{H}^{2}}}
is also a kernel associated to a representation

\rho^{\prime}\colon\operatorname{PSL}_{2}(\mathbb{R})\simeq\Isom(\mathbb{H}^{2})\hookrightarrow\Isom(\mathbb{H}^{\infty})~,

which yields a quasi-isometric
\rho^{\prime}
-equivariant embedding
\psi\colon\mathbb{H}^{2}\hookrightarrow\mathbb{H}^{\infty}
such that, by Lemma
4.6
,

\displaystyle\cosh\Big(d_{\mathbb{H}^{\infty}}\big(\psi(A(i)),\psi(i)\big)\Big)=e^{\frac{1}{2}d_{\mathbb{H}^{2}}(A(i),i)}=\left(\frac{\|A\|^{2}_{F}}{2}+\sqrt{\frac{\|A\|^{4}_{F}}{4}-1}\right)^{\frac{1}{2}}

(4.3)

for any
A\in\operatorname{SL}_{2}(\mathbb{R})
.

Recall that
\|A\|_{F}
is the
Frobenius norm
and is given by

\left\|\begin{pmatrix}a&b\\
c&d\end{pmatrix}\right\|_{F}=\sqrt{a^{2}+b^{2}+c^{2}+d^{2}}~.

The following distance formula is elementary:

Lemma 4.6
.

For any
A,B\in\operatorname{SL}_{2}(\mathbb{R})
,
d_{\mathbb{H}^{2}}(A(i),B(i))=\cosh^{-1}\left(\|B^{-1}A\|_{F}^{2}/2\right)
.
\Box

On the other hand, appealing to Lemma
3.12
, we have

\displaystyle\cosh\Big(d_{\mathbb{H}^{\infty}}(\iota(A(i)),\iota(i))\Big)=\frac{1}{2\pi}\int_{0}^{2\pi}\big|A^{\intercal}(\cos\theta,\sin\theta)^{\intercal}\big|~\mathrm{d}\theta

(4.4)

for any
A\in\operatorname{SL}_{2}(\mathbb{R})
. It is clear that the two quantities (
4.3
) and (
4.4
) are in general different when
A\notin\operatorname{SO}_{2}(\mathbb{R})
, since (
4.4
) is an elliptic integral in terms of the entities of the matrix
A
, while (
4.3
) is an elementary function of these entities. Hence, the associated embeddings
\psi
and
\iota
of
\mathbb{H}^{2}
into
\mathbb{H}^{\infty}
are quasi-isometric but not isometric to each other. This is because the representation
\rho^{\prime}
is not irreducible.

Suppose now we have two irreducible representations
\operatorname{PSL}_{2}(\mathbb{R})\to\Isom(\mathbb{H}^{\infty})
and the
\operatorname{PSL}_{2}(\mathbb{R})
-orbits with respect to these two representations of the unique fixed point by
\operatorname{SO}_{2}(\mathbb{R})
in
\mathbb{H}^{\infty}
are quasi-isometric, then
[
MP14
, Theorem B]
states that this quasi-isometry must reduce to an isometry. In
[
DP12
]
and
[
MP14
]
, using a different kernel of hyperbolic type, one can also produce an exotic representation
\rho_{t}
of
\operatorname{PSL}_{2}(\mathbb{R})
in
\Isom(\mathbb{H}^{\infty})
, see the Introduction.
From what was said just above, we know that
f_{\frac{1}{2}}(\mathbb{H}^{2})
is isometric to

\iota(\mathbb{H}^{2})
. In the rest of this section, we check this fact by a direct computation.

Let us first recall the construction from
[
DP12
,
MP14
]
. Consider the action of
\operatorname{PSL}_{2}(\mathbb{R})\simeq\Isom(\mathbb{H}^{2})
on the boundary
\partial\mathbb{H}^{2}\simeq\mathcal{S}^{1}
of
\mathbb{H}^{2}
induced by its isometric action on the hyperbolic plane. As before, we parameterise the unit circle
\mathcal{S}^{1}
by
(\cos(\theta),\sin(\theta))
for

. Then we can also define an action of
\operatorname{PSL}_{2}(\mathbb{R})
on

\displaystyle A.f\coloneqq\mathrm{Jac}(A^{-1})^{\frac{3}{2}}f\circ A^{-1}~,

(4.5)

for every

and

, where
\mathrm{Jac}(A)
is the Jacobian of
A\colon\mathcal{S}^{1}\to\mathcal{S}^{1}
with respect to the angular measure
\mathrm{d}\theta
on
\mathcal{S}^{1}
. We remark that although the two constructions eventually pass to a
\operatorname{PSL}_{2}(\mathbb{R})
-action on some space of functions on

) is very different from the action defined in (

) using the support functions of convex bodies.

Now we embed
\mathcal{S}^{1}

by

for

, let
e_{n}\colon\mathcal{S}^{1}\to\mathbb{C}

\mathcal{L}\colon L^{2}(\mathcal{S}^{1};\mathbb{C})\to L^{2}(\mathcal{S}^{1};\mathbb{C})

and

. Consider the bilinear form

\langle f,h\rangle_{\mathcal{L}}\coloneqq\frac{1}{2\pi}\int_{0}^{2\pi}f\overline{\mathcal{L}(h)}~\mathrm{d}\theta

defined for

. We remark that the
\operatorname{PSL}_{2}(\mathbb{R})

\mathcal{S}^{1}
defined above will also induce a group action on

Restrict
\mathcal{L}
to
L^{2}(\mathcal{S}^{1})

\mathcal{S}^{1}

\mathbf{1}\in L^{2}(\mathcal{S}^{1})
the constant function on
\mathcal{S}^{1}
of value

. Let

\mathcal{X}\coloneqq\operatorname{PSL}_{2}(\mathbb{R}).\mathbf{1}\subset L^{2}(\mathcal{S}^{1})

be the orbit of the constant function
\mathbf{1}
under the action of
\operatorname{PSL}_{2}(\mathbb{R})
described above.
[

,
MP14

showed that the bilinear form

\operatorname{SO}_{2}(\mathbb{R})<\operatorname{PSL}_{2}(\mathbb{R})

is exactly

Let
A_{t}\coloneqq\mathrm{diag}(e^{t},e^{-t})\in\operatorname{PSL}_{2}(\mathbb{R})
for

A_{t}^{-1}=A_{-t}

Lemma 4.7
.

For
t\geq 0
and every
\theta\in[0,2\pi)
, the Jacobian of the map
A_{-t}\colon\mathcal{S}^{1}\to\mathcal{S}^{1}
on the unit circle with respect to the angular measure

is given by

\mathrm{Jac}(A_{-t})(\theta)=\frac{1}{|\cosh(t)-\sinh(t)e^{i\theta}|^{2}}~.

Proof.

The action of
\operatorname{PSL}_{2}(\mathbb{R})
on the unit circle
\mathcal{S}^{1}\subset\mathbb{C}
factors through the homographic
\mathrm{SU}(1,1)
-action via the conjugation

\displaystyle\operatorname{PSL}_{2}(\mathbb{R})

\displaystyle\to\mathrm{SU}(1,1)

\displaystyle A

\displaystyle\mapsto CAC^{-1}

where

C=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}\begin{pmatrix}1&-i\\
1&i\end{pmatrix}~.

Hence, we have
\mathrm{Jac}(A_{-t})=\mathrm{Jac}(D_{-t})
, where

D_{-t}=\begin{pmatrix}\cosh(t)&-\sinh(t)\\

Now a simple computation of the derivatives yields the desired formula.
∎

We notice that
\mathcal{L}(\mathbf{1})=\mathbf{1}
and
A_{t}.\mathbf{1}=\mathrm{Jac}(A_{-t})^{\frac{3}{2}}\mathbf{1}

\displaystyle\cosh\big(d_{\mathbb{\mathbb{H}^{\infty}}}(\iota_{\mathcal{L}}(A_{t}.\mathbf{1}),\iota_{\mathcal{L}}(\mathbf{1}))\big)=\langle A_{t}.\mathbf{1},\mathbf{1}\rangle_{\mathcal{L}}=\frac{1}{2\pi}\int^{2\pi}_{0}\frac{\mathrm{d}\theta}{|\cosh(t)-\sinh(t)e^{i\theta}|^{3}}~.

(4.6)

As commented earlier, since our representation

by Corollary

and

i.e.
the formulae (

) and (

) are the same.

Before showing this, we will need an auxiliary lemma. First, we define

I(k)\coloneqq\int_{0}^{\frac{\pi}{2}}\frac{\mathrm{d}u}{\big(1-k^{2}\sin^{2}(u)\big)^{\frac{3}{2}}}~,

E(k)\coloneqq\int_{0}^{\frac{\pi}{2}}\sqrt{1-k^{2}\sin^{2}(u)}~\mathrm{d}u~,

and

K(k)\coloneqq\int_{0}^{\frac{\pi}{2}}\frac{\mathrm{d}u}{\sqrt{1-k^{2}\sin^{2}(u)}}~.

We recall that
K(k)
is called the
complete elliptic integral of the first kind
, while
E(k)
is called the
complete elliptic integral of the second kind
.

Lemma 4.8
.

For any
0<k<1
, we have

\displaystyle I(k)=\frac{E(k)}{1-k^{2}}~.

(4.7)

Proof.

Recall a standard relation between the complete elliptic integrals and their derivatives (see for example
[
WW20
, §22.736]
):

K^{\prime}=\frac{E}{k(1-k^{2})}-\frac{K}{k}~.

Now we can check that
I=kK^{\prime}+K
. Indeed, we can compute

K^{\prime}(k)=\int_{0}^{\frac{\pi}{2}}\frac{k\sin^{2}(u)}{\big(1-k^{2}\sin^{2}(u)\big)^{\frac{3}{2}}}~\mathrm{d}u~,

so

kK^{\prime}(k)+K(k)=\int_{0}^{\frac{\pi}{2}}\frac{k^{2}\sin^{2}(u)}{\big(1-k^{2}\sin^{2}(u)\big)^{\frac{3}{2}}}~\mathrm{d}u+\int_{0}^{\frac{\pi}{2}}\frac{1-k^{2}\sin^{2}(u)}{\big(1-k^{2}\sin^{2}(u)\big)^{\frac{3}{2}}}~\mathrm{d}u=I(k)~.

Combining the two relations, we get the desired result.
∎

The proof of the isometry between the two images relies on simplifying the distance formula into standard elliptic integrals of the second kind.

Proposition 4.9
.

For
t\geq 0
, we have

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2\pi}\int^{2\pi}_{0}\frac{\mathrm{d}\theta}{|\cosh(t)-\sinh(t)e^{i\theta}|^{3}}=\frac{1}{2\pi}\int^{2\pi}_{0}\left|\begin{pmatrix}e^{t}&0\\
0&e^{-t}\end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix}\cos{\theta}\\
\sin{\theta}\end{pmatrix}\right|\mathrm{d}\theta~.

(4.8)

In turn, the images
\textsc{supp}^{\pi}(\mathcal{E}^{\pi}_{c})=\iota(\mathbb{H}^{2})
and
\iota_{\mathcal{L}}(\mathcal{X})
are isometric.

Proof.

Since
\iota_{\mathcal{L}}
and
\iota
are both
\operatorname{PSL}_{2}(\mathbb{R})
-equivariant, by identifying
\mathbf{1}\in\mathcal{X}
and
D\in\mathcal{E}^{\pi}_{c}
, up to translations and rotations by elements in
\operatorname{PSL}_{2}(\mathbb{R})
, to show that the two images are isometric, it suffices to prove for any
t\geq 0
,

d_{\mathbb{\mathbb{H}^{\infty}}}(\iota_{\mathcal{L}}(A_{t}.\mathbf{1}),\iota_{\mathcal{L}}(\mathbf{1}))=d_{\mathbb{\mathbb{H}^{\infty}}}(\iota(A_{t}D),\iota(D))~,

which further reduces to showing the equality (
4.8
).

Let us denote by
I_{1}(t)
the left-hand side of (
4.8
) and by
I_{2}(t)
the right-hand side of (
4.8
). First, notice that

\displaystyle|\cosh(t)+\sinh(t)e^{i\psi}|^{2}=\cosh(2t)+\sinh(2t)\cos(\psi)~.\

(4.9)

So by exploiting the trigonometric identities, we have

\displaystyle I_{1}(t)

\displaystyle\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\psi=\theta-\pi}}{{=}}\frac{1}{2\pi}\int^{\pi}_{-\pi}\frac{\mathrm{d}\psi}{|\cosh(t)+\sinh(t)e^{i\psi}|^{3}}

\displaystyle\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\eqref{eq: trigo norm}}}{{=}}\frac{1}{2\pi}\int^{\pi}_{-\pi}\big(\cosh(2t)+\sinh(2t)\cos(\psi)\big)^{-\frac{3}{2}}~\mathrm{d}\psi

\displaystyle\stackrel{{\scriptstyle 2u=\psi}}{{=}}\frac{1}{\pi}\int^{\frac{\pi}{2}}_{-\frac{\pi}{2}}\big((\cosh(2t)+\sinh(2t))-2\sinh(2t)\sin^{2}(u)\big)^{-\frac{3}{2}}~\mathrm{d}u

\displaystyle=\frac{2}{\pi}\int_{0}^{\frac{\pi}{2}}\big(e^{2t}-(e^{2t}-e^{-2t})\sin^{2}(u)\big)^{-\frac{3}{2}}~\mathrm{d}u

\displaystyle=\frac{2e^{-3t}}{\pi}\int_{0}^{\frac{\pi}{2}}\big(1-(1-e^{-4t}))\sin^{2}(u)\big)^{-\frac{3}{2}}~\mathrm{d}u

\displaystyle=2e^{-3t}I(k)/\pi~,

where we set
k\coloneqq\sqrt{1-e^{-4t}}\in(0,1)
,
viz.

t>0
. Now using the relation (
4.7
), on the one hand we can deduce that for all
t>0

I_{1}(t)=2e^{t}E(k)/\pi~.

On the other hand, we observe that

\displaystyle I_{2}(t)

\displaystyle=\frac{e^{t}}{2\pi}\int_{0}^{2\pi}\sqrt{1-k^{2}\sin(u)^{2}}~\mathrm{d}u=\frac{2e^{t}}{\pi}\int_{0}^{\frac{\pi}{2}}\sqrt{1-k^{2}\sin(u)^{2}}~\mathrm{d}u=2e^{t}E(k)/\pi~.

Hence, we can conclude that
I_{1}(t)=I_{2}(t)
for all
t>0
. For the case
t=0
, the verification is straightforward.
