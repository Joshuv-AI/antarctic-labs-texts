Draf III in the Acute Phase of Pott's Puffy Tumor: A Scoping Review
Authors: Sahil Kakar, Haris Ali, Husnaa Fathima Ali Azamathullah Khan, Rameez Amjad, Akshara Sharma, Muhammad Daoud Tariq, Muhammad Sohaib Shahid, Shahzada Khuram Ahmed, Haissan Iftikhar
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTBackgroundPott's puffy tumor (PPT) is a rare but serious sequela of frontal sinusitis characterized by osteomyelitis of the frontal bone with subperiosteal abscess formation. Surgical management has traditionally been delayed until resolution of the acute infection. This scoping review evaluates the safety and outcomes of performing “Hot” Draf III (modified Lothrop) surgery during the acute phase of PPT.MethodsA review was conducted in accordance with PRISMA guidelines. Literature searches were performed across PubMed, Embase, Scopus, and ClinicalTrials.gov without date restriction. Eligible studies included patients undergoing Draf III surgery acutely (within 28 days of presentation) for PPT. Data were extracted on demographics, surgical approach, complications, and reintervention rates. Pooled analysis was performed using a random‐effects model with Freeman–Tukey transformation to stabilize variance.ResultsFive studies met the inclusion criteria, encompassing nine patients (seven males, two females; mean age 50.3 years). All underwent acute Draf III surgery, primarily via endoscopic approaches (88.9%). Meta‐analysis demonstrated a pooled complication rate of 0% (95% CI: 0%–40%) and a reintervention rate of 3% (95% CI: 0%–71%), with no significant heterogeneity (I² = 0%).Conclusion“Hot” Draf III surgery appears to be a safe and effective management option for PPT during the acute phase, offering low complication and reintervention rates. While these findings support early surgical intervention, larger, prospective studies are required to validate long‐term outcomes and define patient selection criteria.Keywords: Draf III, endoscopic modified lothrop, frontal bone osteomyelitis, frontal sinus surgery, Pott's puffy tumor

Introduction

Pott's puffy tumor (PPT) is a rare clinical entity characterized by frontal bone osteomyelitis with associated subperiosteal abscess. It was initially described by Sir Percival Pott [1] in 1768 in a case of head trauma. Bacterial infiltration from the nasal tract leads to the inflammation of the frontal recess, infection in the frontal sinus, eventually leading to the necrosis of the frontal bone. The resulting collection of purulent matter and debris leads to symptoms such as headache and facial pain, forehead edema, and purulent discharge from the frontal scalp [1]. On pus swab cultures, Staphylococcus spp., Streptococcus spp. including highly pyogenic forms, and aerobic bacteria are implicated as causative agents [2, 3]. As a result, many cases of PPT are treated with prolonged courses of high‐dose broad‐spectrum antibiotics, with surgical drainage once acute infection has subsided [4].

Etiology of Potts Puffy Tumor varies by age group. In children, frequent upper respiratory tract infections are a predisposing factor, whereas in adults the most common cause is chronic rhinosinusitis (CRS) [5, 6]. Both result in the stenosing of the frontal sinus, resulting in infection and abscess collection in the subperiosteal space. PPT was thought to be common in children and adolescents, but emerging literature disputes the favorability of any age group [4].

Emerging surgical modalities favor an endoscopic endonasal approach as opposed to the traditional open approach [7]. The minimally invasive nature of endoscopic sinus surgery affords several advantages such as a lower rate of surgical site complications (meningitis, cavernous sinus thrombosis) and quicker recovery times [8]. The Draf III surgery, also known as the modified Lothrop procedure, has been employed successfully to operate on patients with PPT via the creation of a common frontal sinus. In 2020, Pendolino et al. described the successful treatment of several patients with PPT by Draf III over the period of 1 year [1]. We suggest the term “Hot“ Draf III to describe the surgery in the active phase of PPT at the time of initial presentation. The last decade alone has seen several cases reported of successful treatment of PPT via Hot Draf III [9].

The aim of this scoping review was to report the outcomes for Hot Draf III procedures in patients presenting with PPT.

Methods

PICO and Eligibility Criteria

This review was conducted in accordance with the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta‐Analyses (PRISMA) guidelines [10]. The research question was framed using the Population, Intervention, Comparison, Outcome (PICO) model. The population under investigation consisted of patients who underwent Draft III surgery as an acute intervention, within 28 days of presentation, for PPT. The comparator is medical management with Ⅳ antibiotics. The outcomes included the rate of re‐intervention and complications. The exclusion criteria were: (1) review articles; (2) letters to the editor; (3) commentaries; (4) conference abstracts; (5) editorials; (6) non‐English articles; and (7) invasive fungal sinusitis cases. Due to the limited available literature, we also included case reports and case series in our analysis. Our PRISMA flow sheet can be seen in Figure 1.

Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta‐Analyses (PRISMA) search flow diagram.

Search Strategy

The literature search was conducted across four databases, including Medline (PubMed), ClinicalTrials.gov, Embase, and Scopus. The following keywords were utilized: Draf III, Endoscopic Modified Lothrop Procedure, Endoscopic Sinus Surgery, Frontal Sinus Surgery, Draf IIb, Pott's Puffy Tumour, Frontal Bone Osteomyelitis, Pott's Tumour, and Pott's Puffy. The final search date was November 2024. There was no date or study design restriction. We restricted our search to the English language.

Data Screening

All papers retrieved with the search strings were uploaded onto Rayyan.AI. All duplicates were removed automatically. Three independent reviewers (S.K., H.A., H.F.A.A.K.) then screened the titles and abstracts of identified articles, after which the full text of selected studies was reviewed. In the event of any disagreements regarding the inclusion or exclusion of a study, resolution was achieved through discussion. Should consensus not be reached, the lead author (H.I.) was consulted for final approval. Additionally, further searches were conducted by entering the titles of the included studies into Google and reviewing the first 10 pages of results to identify any studies meeting the eligibility criteria.

Data Extraction

Data were extracted in duplicate for each study, including the study ID, location, participant demographics (country, sample size, and age), study methodology (study design, method of allocation, participant and outcome masking, participant exclusion, and proportion of losses to follow‐up), and intervention. The outcome of interest was re‐intervention and complication rates. All data were recorded on a pre‐formatted Excel spreadsheet.

Statistical Analysis

All statistical analyses were performed using Stata v18.5. Because the cohorts were small and some contained zero events, we analyzed study effects on the Freeman–Tukey double‐arcsine scale to stabilize variances. For reporting on the proportion scale, θ and its confidence limits were back‐transformed.

Pooled effects were obtained with a random‐effects model using restricted maximum likelihood (REML). Uncertainty in the pooled effect was quantified with the truncated Knapp–Hartung adjustment. We summarized heterogeneity with τ2 and I 2. A p value of less than 0.05 was considered statistically significant in all analyses.

Ethical Considerations

This scoping review was conducted using publicly available data from previously published studies. No primary data collection involving human participants or animals was undertaken, and as such, ethical approval was not required. The review adhered to established guidelines, such as the PRISMA (Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta‐Analyses) statement, to ensure methodological rigor and transparency.

The review included data from five studies on nine patients, all of which were included in the analysis. The male patients had a higher proportion with seven patients (77.8%) compared to two patients (22.2%) being female. Age data were available for all patients, with a mean age of 50.3 years. Cultures and Medical Management

Culture data were available in all patients. The most common organism isolated was Pseudomonas spp., found in three of these patients (33.3%). Additionally, four of the nine patients (44.4%) did not isolate any organisms (Table 2).

All patients were treated with antibiotics alongside surgical management. The duration of antibiotics was available for 8/9 of these patients (88.9%), with an average duration of 49 days (Table 2).

Surgical Complication and Re‐Intervention

Complications

A total of five studies reported postoperative complications. Across nine patients, no complications were observed. The pooled complication rate was 0% (95% CI, 0%–40%) (Figure 4). Inter‐study heterogeneity was negligible (I² = 0.0%). The overall pooled effect was not statistically significant (p = 1.00) (Table 3).

Reintervention

A total of four studies reported on reintervention. Across eight patients, one reintervention was identified. The pooled reintervention rate was 3% (95% CI, 0%–71%), as shown in Figure 3. Inter‐study heterogeneity was negligible (I² = 0.0%). The overall pooled effect was not statistically significant (p = 0.65).

Qualitative Analysis

Most studies had a low risk of bias in critical domains such as patient demographics, patient history, clinical condition description, diagnostic methods, intervention clarity, and outcome reporting (Figure 2). All five studies satisfied 10/13 criteria of the JBI appraisal tool. Follow‐up time was the most poorly reported metric reported with only 4/6 studies reporting.

Discussion

This review examines five studies in which “Hot“ Draf III sinusotomy was used to treat acute frontal sinusitis presenting as PPT and includes data from nine patients in the meta‐analysis. Our paper looks at purely bacterial etiologies of PPT and not those caused by Aspergillus or Mucor, as our primary aim was to evaluate the surgical management of frontal sinus osteomyelitis, a clinically different entity from invasive fungal sinusitis. Sandoval et al. described that PPT can present across all age groups but is more common in pediatric and adolescent populations due to their relatively hypoplastic frontal sinuses [15]. PPT is more common in males than in females not withstanding age, as was redemonstrated in our review at a ratio of 3.5:1, consistent with previous reports; this has previously been reported to be as high as 9:1 [16, 17, 18].

The mainstay of treatment for PPT has been described as IV antibiotics for 4–6 weeks accompanied by frontal sinus drainage [19, 20, 21]. The endoscopic endonasal approach is safe and effective, sometimes used alongside the percutaneous endoscopic approach to enhance surgical visualization [22] The Draf III procedure is a radical frontal drainage surgery which forms a common frontal sinus cavity and is performed in a wide range of frontal sinus pathologies such as CRS [23] and has also been described in the treatment of PPT [1, 7, 9]. Since PPT leads to erosion and subsequent destruction of the frontal bone, definitive surgical management is necessary to drain the subperiosteal abscess and reduce the risk of recurrence and complications. All patients in our review underwent surgery within the acute period (within 28 days). Sideris et al. documented that radical frontal drainage surgeries are typically performed acutely in cases of PPT with complications, though they recommend a 20‐day delay in operating when symptoms are resolving with antibiotics [24].

The findings from our meta‐analysis provide additional context to the complication and re‐intervention rates for Draf III procedures in the acute setting. The low pooled complication rate and heterogeneity indicate consistency across studies. This suggests that complication rates following acute Draf III procedures are relatively low, though the wide confidence interval reflects uncertainty due to a small sample size.

Similarly, the meta‐analysis estimated a pooled re‐intervention rate of 3% (95% CI: 0%–71%), again with no observed heterogeneity (I² = 0%, p = 0.694). But the consistency across studies suggests that Draf III remains a reliable surgical approach with a low likelihood of requiring secondary intervention.

These findings reinforce the safety and effectiveness of Draf III in the acute setting, with complication and re‐intervention rates that appear lower than previously reported for broader PPT management. However, the small sample size and wide confidence intervals highlight the need for further studies with larger cohorts to validate these results.

Limitations

The data set consisted primarily of case reports and small case series, limiting generalizability due to variability and heterogeneity. A key limitation was the lack of patient‐level data in complication reporting, making it unclear whether multiple complications were attributable to the same patient. Future studies should aim to distinguish between per‐patient and per‐procedure complications for better accuracy. Another limitation was the lack of long‐term follow‐up; most studies did not report outcomes beyond 2 years. This makes it difficult to assess recurrence rates and delayed complications.

Conclusion

The pooled rates of re‐intervention in patients undergoing Hot Draf III were 3% and complication rates were 0%. These support the notion that the acute use of Draf III in the management of PPT is a safe procedure with reasonable outcomes. Our scoping review lends credence to further studies on acute surgery for this rare clinical entity with the aim to reduce life‐threatening complications and the need for multiple interventions.
