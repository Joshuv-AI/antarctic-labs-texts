Dynamic Context Tuning for Retrieval-Augmented Generation: Enhancing Multi-Turn Planning and Tool Adaptation Thanks: This research was conducted independently. No external funding was received.
Jubin Abhishek Soni, Rajesh Kumar Pandey, Amit Anand, Aniket Abhishek Soni

Abstract
Retrieval-Augmented Generation (RAG) has advanced large language models (LLMs) by grounding their outputs in external tools and knowledge sources. However, existing RAG systems are typically limited to static, single-turn interactions with fixed toolsets, making them ill-suited for dynamic domains such as healthcare and smart homes, where user intent, available tools, and context evolve over time. We present Dynamic Context Tuning (DCT) , a lightweight framework that extends RAG to multi-turn dialogue and evolving tool environments without requiring retraining. DCT integrates an attention-based context cache to track relevant past information, LoRA-based retrieval to dynamically select domain-specific tools, and efficient context compression to keep inputs within LLM context limits. Through experiments on both synthetic and real-world benchmarks, DCT achieves a 14% improvement in plan accuracy and a 37% reduction in hallucinations, while matching GPT-4 performance at significantly lower cost. Moreover, DCT generalizes to previously unseen tools, enabling scalable and adaptable AI assistants across a wide range of dynamic environments.



I
Introduction

Large Language Models (LLMs) have shown strong performance in executing complex tasks by interfacing with external tools, such as APIs and databases
[
1
]
. Retrieval-Augmented Generation (RAG) enhances LLMs by grounding outputs in retrieved knowledge, improving factuality and reducing hallucinations
[
2
]
. However, most RAG systems assume static, single-turn settings and struggle with multi-turn dialogues involving contextual ambiguity and tool variability
[
3
]
.

For example, the query “Remind John about his meeting tomorrow” requires resolving ambiguous references (e.g., “his meeting”), temporal reasoning, and tool interaction (e.g., calendar APIs). Traditional methods
[
4
]
often require extensive retraining to accommodate new tools or domains, limiting scalability and responsiveness in practical deployments.

These challenges are particularly pronounced in real-world applications like healthcare, smart assistants, and education, where toolsets evolve, interactions are long-term, and compute resources are constrained
[
5
,
6
]
. Additionally, static context windows often lead to context overflow or degraded model performance over extended dialogues
[
7
]
.

To address these issues, we propose
Dynamic Context Tuning (DCT)
, a flexible RAG-based framework designed for dynamic, multi-turn tool use. DCT introduces three key innovations:

•

Multi-Turn Context Cache
: Tracks dialogue history to improve disambiguation and continuity across turns.

•

Domain-Adaptive Tool Retrieval
: Enables fast adaptation to new tools using LoRA
[
8
]
.

•

Lightweight Context Compression
: Condenses dialogue history to efficiently manage LLM context limits
[
9
]
.

We evaluate DCT on tasks in medical decision support and IoT orchestration. It improves multi-turn task accuracy by 14%, reduces hallucinations by 37%, and achieves GPT-4 Chain-of-Thought performance with 58% lower inference costs. DCT also generalizes well to new tools, ensuring robust performance in dynamic, resource-constrained settings.

DCT advances the deployment of scalable, context-aware, and tool-adaptive LLMs for practical AI assistants.

II
Related Work

II-A

Tool-Augmented Language Models

Integrating LLMs with external tools has emerged as a promising strategy to enhance task completion and reduce hallucinations. Early systems like REaLM
[
10
]
introduced document-based retrieval to ground model outputs, while recent frameworks such as Chameleon
[
11
]
and Toolformer
[
12
]
explored self-supervised or modular API interactions for tool use. These systems, however, largely operate under static toolsets and assume clean, single-turn prompts.

Contextual tool usage remains underexplored. While InfoXLM
[
13
]
and multilingual models
[
14
]
showcase cross-task and cross-lingual generalization, they fall short on procedural or temporal reasoning necessary for dynamic tools. Even advanced models like GPT-4
[
7
]
show limitations in reference resolution, particularly in dialogic contexts, which can also be helpful in cybersecurity techniques
[
15
]
. Our work addresses these issues by maintaining a memory-augmented cache that improves tool invocation over evolving dialogue contexts.

II-B

Conversational Retrieval-Augmented Generation

Conversational RAG systems extend retrieval to incorporate dialogue history for more coherent responses. Self-RAG
[
16
]
enables on-the-fly evidence gathering using introspective token cues, while FLARE
[
17
]
anticipates future queries to preemptively fetch relevant documents. Despite these innovations, these approaches generally assume static corpora and do not support active tool invocation.

More recent work, such as Instruct-RAG
[
3
]
, explores few-shot prompting with retrieved examples for multi-hop QA. However, these models are not designed for dynamic tools or procedural actions. FLARE and related methods struggle with ambiguous entities in conversations—such as resolving “his calendar” across agents. Our method integrates context caching with runtime retrieval to overcome these limitations in procedural multi-turn settings.

II-C

Efficient Retrieval and Tool Adaptation

Scalable retrieval remains a bottleneck, particularly in real-time or resource-constrained environments. ColBERT
[
4
]
introduces token-wise late interaction, enabling accurate passage search with fine-grained relevance estimation. Concurrently, PRP
[
18
]
uses pairwise ranking via LLMs, trading efficiency for precision. Foundational approaches like BPR
[
19
]
remain useful in low-resource setups due to their simplicity and implicit feedback alignment.

On the tool adaptation front, LoRA
[
8
]
allows for efficient fine-tuning of LLMs by introducing low-rank trainable matrices within transformer blocks. While LoRA has shown promise in generation and summarization, its potential for real-time retrieval and tool routing remains underutilized. We extend LoRA to enable adaptive tool retrievers, allowing rapid integration of new APIs without retraining the base model.

II-D

Summary of Gaps

Across the literature, three gaps are apparent: (1) lack of robust support for multi-turn, context-sensitive tool planning; (2) rigidity in tool-use architectures that inhibit rapid adaptation; and (3) inefficiency in current retrieval methods for low-latency deployment. Our proposed framework,
Dynamic Context Tuning (DCT)
, addresses these challenges by combining dialogue-aware memory, LoRA-based retriever tuning, and compact history compression, as discussed in Section
III
.

III
Methodology

We propose
Dynamic Context Tuning (DCT)
, a retrieval-augmented generation (RAG) framework that mitigates core limitations in production-level assistants—particularly in the areas of ambiguous turn resolution, restricted context windows, and brittle tool generalization. The architecture, illustrated in Figure
1
, is composed of three primary components:

1.

Multi-Turn Context Cache:
A memory module that tracks intents, tool usage, and references across dialogue history to resolve ambiguities and enable coherent multi-turn understanding.

2.

Lightweight Context Compression:
A span-based summarization layer that reduces token usage while preserving essential details like parameters, tool entities, and temporal relations.

3.

Domain-Adaptive Tool Retriever:
A trainable retriever that supports evolving and domain-specific toolsets using parameter-efficient fine-tuning techniques such as LoRA.

Fig. 1:
Overview of the Dynamic Context Tuning (DCT) architecture, structured around three key modules: Multi-Turn Context Cache, Lightweight Context Compression, and Domain-Adaptive Tool Retrieval.

III-A

Multi-Turn Context Cache

In order to track tool usage, user goals, and ambiguous references across turns, we implement a
multi-turn cache
storing intent and context representations in a structured fashion. This cache takes the form of a key-value store, where keys correspond to intent embeddings
\mathbf{e}_{i}
obtained from a frozen GTR-T5-XL encoder
[
20
]
, and values contain structured tool invocations (name, parameters), entity references, and associated timestamps.

When a new user input
u_{t}
arrives, we encode it as a query vector
\mathbf{q}_{t}
using the same encoder. Cache entries are scored via a weighted combination of semantic similarity and recency:

\text{Score}(\mathbf{e},\mathbf{q}_{t})=\alpha\cdot\cos(\mathbf{e},\mathbf{q}_{t})+(1-\alpha)\cdot\text{recency}(\mathbf{e}),\quad\alpha=0.7

(1)

For ambiguous anaphora resolution (e.g., “set that reminder”), we apply multi-head attention over cache embeddings:

\mathbf{a}_{i}=\text{softmax}\left(\frac{\mathbf{q}_{t}\mathbf{W}_{Q}(\mathbf{e}_{i}\mathbf{W}_{K})^{\top}}{\sqrt{d}}\right),\quad\mathbf{c}_{t}=\sum_{i}\mathbf{a}_{i}\cdot(\mathbf{e}_{i}\mathbf{W}_{V})

(2)

The resulting contextual vector
\mathbf{c}_{t}
is concatenated with
\mathbf{q}_{t}
and passed to downstream modules to guide both compression and tool retrieval.

III-B

Lightweight Context Compression

To maintain compatibility with LLM token budgets while retaining critical semantic content, we introduce a context compression module with two key stages:

Salient Span Extraction

We train a BiLSTM-CRF model
[
9
]
to extract spans relevant to downstream tool selection. The model is trained to label tokens corresponding to four categories:

•

Tool Invocations
: e.g., “send email,” “set alarm”

•

Parameters
: arguments like “tomorrow at 9 AM” or “volume to 70%”

•

Temporal References
: expressions like “next week” or “in 3 hours”

•

Entities
: named items such as “Alex,” “kitchen speaker,” or “Calendar B”

Controlled Summarization

Extracted spans are passed to a controlled summarization step using a distillation-tuned version of GPT-3.5-Turbo. Prompts specify fixed token budgets (typically 100–150 tokens) and enforce structural constraints, such as grouping by tool and preserving named entities verbatim. We observed a mean compression ratio of 63% across datasets, with minimal degradation in utility (BERTScore
>0.92
).

This ensures that only semantically relevant content is forwarded to the LLM or retrieval module, improving latency and reducing context drift.

III-C

Domain-Adaptive Tool Retrieval

To support growing tool catalogs and emerging verticals, DCT incorporates a domain-adaptive tool retriever capable of both generalization and rapid domain tuning. The retriever is initialized as a LambdaMART-RRF ensemble
[
21
]
over tool documentation embeddings and is refined via supervised contrastive learning using tool call logs.

LoRA-Based Adaptation

To avoid full retraining for each new domain
\mathcal{T}_{\text{new}}
, we inject
LoRA modules

[
8
]
into the encoder architecture. Specifically, for any projection matrix
\mathbf{W}
, the LoRA-augmented version is:

\mathbf{W}^{\prime}=\mathbf{W}+\mathbf{B}\mathbf{A}^{\top}

(3)

Where
\mathbf{A}\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times r}
and
\mathbf{B}\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times r}
are low-rank matrices (typically
r=4
). This introduces less than 0.2% overhead while enabling fast adaptation to new tools or APIs without catastrophic forgetting.

Contrastive Loss Training

Given a positive tool match
p
and
N
negative samples
\{n_{1},\dots,n_{N}\}
, we optimize the retriever with contrastive loss:

\mathcal{L}_{\text{ret}}=-\log\frac{e^{s_{p}/\tau}}{e^{s_{p}/\tau}+\sum_{n=1}^{N}e^{s_{n}/\tau}},\quad\tau=0.07

(4)

Where
s_{p}
and
s_{n}
are similarity scores computed via dot product and
\tau
is a temperature parameter. This encourages the retriever to rank the correct tool higher in contextually complex queries.

III-D

LLM Generation and Feedback Loop

The final input to the LLM consists of compressed context
\tilde{c}_{t}
and the retrieved tool specification. The generation process uses OpenAI’s GPT-4-turbo model with postprocessing constraints enforced via function-calling or tool-use syntax (depending on platform capabilities).

If the response fails to meet user expectations (e.g., missing parameters, wrong tool), a feedback loop is triggered: the model re-invokes the compression or retrieval stage, optionally updating the cache based on corrections or clarifications. This loop supports robustness in live settings where user revisions are frequent.

III-E

Training and Evaluation

Datasets

•

Synthetic Multi-Turn Dialogues (10K):
Generated with templated tool usage across 4 domains: productivity, smart home, communication, and healthcare.

•

Real-World Assistant Logs (1K):
Collected from production deployment, covering 231 unique tools with anonymized user queries and tool responses.

Training Objectives

Overall training combines contrastive retrieval and hallucination minimization:

\mathcal{L}=\lambda_{1}\mathcal{L}_{\text{ret}}+\lambda_{2}\mathcal{L}_{\text{hallucinate}},\quad\lambda_{1}=0.8,\lambda_{2}=0.2

(5)

\mathcal{L}_{\text{hallucinate}}
penalizes generation outputs where tools are invoked with incorrect parameters or outside the supported API schema.

Curriculum Learning Schedule

1.

Pretraining on General Queries:
200K examples to build base retrieval and summarization capabilities.

2.

Multi-Turn Fine-Tuning:
10K examples introducing ambiguity resolution and cache usage.

3.

Domain-Specific LoRA Adaptation:
2K examples per vertical for high-accuracy performance in narrow domains.

III-F

Implementation Details

The full DCT pipeline is implemented in PyTorch with modular design for deployment on varied device tiers:

•

Quantized Embeddings (INT8):
For edge-device retrieval and cache access.

•

Batched Candidate Ranking:
With top-
k
filtering (typically
k=16
or
32
) to minimize latency.

•

Containerized Deployment:
With latency instrumentation and retraining hooks.

V
Discussion

The deployment of Dynamic Context Tuning (DCT) in real-world systems raises critical ethical challenges, particularly in privacy, bias, and environmental impact. First, while our multi-turn context cache enhances conversational continuity, storing dialogue history introduces privacy risks. For example, cached entries like “adjust insulin dose for Patient X” could expose sensitive health data if breached. To mitigate this, we propose a federated learning framework
[
22
]
where context storage and retrieval occur locally on user devices, with only anonymized tool retrieval patterns shared to the central model. This approach reduces exposure of raw dialogue data by 92% compared to cloud-based caching, as measured by differential privacy metrics (
\epsilon=1.5
,
\delta=10^{-5}
)
[
23
]
. However, even federated systems risk inferring personal details from tool usage patterns—e.g., frequent “chemotherapy scheduling” API calls may reveal a cancer diagnosis. Regular audits using tools like TensorFlow Privacy’s membership inference tests
[
6
]
are essential to prevent such leaks.

Second, our reliance on synthetic training data introduces demographic bias risks. Despite persona diversification, GPT-4-generated dialogues underrepresent non-Western naming conventions (e.g., only 12% of synthetic user names were of Asian or African origin) and regional tool preferences (e.g., WhatsApp APIs appeared 5× less frequently than iMessage in non-U.S. contexts). Such biases could propagate to downstream applications—e.g., a voice assistant recommending “CVS Pharmacy” over “Guardian Health” for users in Singapore. To address this, we implement fairness checks using FairFace
[
24
]
and counterfactual augmentation
[
25
]
, which improved tool recommendation parity by 34% on our geographic bias test set.

Third, DCT’s environmental footprint requires scrutiny. Training our largest model (LambdaMART-RRF + LoRA) emitted approximately 287 kg CO
2
eq, comparable to a cross-country flight
[
20
]
. While 23% lower than GPT-4’s estimated footprint for equivalent tasks, this remains unsustainable at scale. Transitioning to sparse Mixture-of-Experts architectures
[
26
]
could reduce emissions by 41% without accuracy loss, as shown in our preliminary experiments.

VI
Conclusion

This paper presents Dynamic Context Tuning (DCT), a Retrieval-Augmented Generation (RAG) framework designed to address the challenges of dynamic tool integration, multi-turn context handling, and efficiency. DCT integrates three key innovations: (1) a hierarchical multi-turn context cache for disambiguation, (2) LoRA-based parameter-efficient domain adaptation, and (3) lightweight context compression to respect LLM token limits. Empirical evaluations demonstrate that DCT improves plan accuracy by 14% and reduces hallucinations by 37%, with a significant reduction in inference costs.

DCT’s contributions include: (1) improving recall@5 by 23× with minimal parameter increase, (2) reducing tokens by 63% without accuracy loss, and (3) mitigating tool obsolescence in IoT and clinical decision support. Despite promising results, limitations in niche domains, latency, and reliance on synthetic data are identified.

In the future, we plan to address the limitations in niche domains, reduce latency for edge devices, and enhance the model’s ability to handle low-data domains. We also aim to mitigate biases in tool selection and improve cross-lingual retrieval for more diverse applications.

Overall, DCT offers a scalable solution for dynamic, multi-turn tasks, with applications across healthcare, IoT, and other real-world domains.
