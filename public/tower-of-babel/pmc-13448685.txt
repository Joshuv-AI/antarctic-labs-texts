Ecology and bioreceptivity of basaltic rocks from Xitle volcano in Mexico
Authors: Maria F Martínez-Báez Téllez, Maria del Pilar Ortega-Larrocea, Marie- Nöelle Guilbaud, Laurent Jouffret, Benjamin van Wyk de Vries
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractMonogenetic basaltic volcanoes, the most common volcano type on Earth, produce rock surfaces with diverse morphological and textural characteristics promoting wide biodiversity. We assess ecological colonization related to volcanic rock bioreceptivity for biological rock crust communities on Xitle volcano (Mexico City). We assessed the five main volcanic geoforms present: crater, lava tube, promontory, crevasse wall and flat lobe. Climatic conditions (insolation, altitude, humidity, temperature) and biological crust diversity were registered. Thin sections were made for rock physical properties: total porosity, pore size and surface texture. Mineral analysis through microprobe and X-ray diffraction was done. For the rock-life interface, we used optical, petrographic, scanning electron and confocal microscopes. The crust communities contain crustose, foliose, powdery and fruticose lichens; mosses, liverworts, algae, cyanobacteria and bacterial biofilms. The rocks vary significantly in porosity, texture and the community structure. Alpha diversity correlates with porosity, except for the tube being a completely different environment. Beta diversity analysis shows most similarity for flat lobe and promontory (43%). We conclude that rock properties like porosity are important for biodiversity and extrinsic factors like geomorphology have a major role. We introduce bioreceptivity study into natural field sites, broadening bioreceptivity research. We show that it is a useful ecological concept to explore the multiple ecological niches of lava flows and we illustrate the great biodiversity of basaltic volcanoes, and by extension the value of basalt for ecological construction.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-026-48812-9.Keywords: Bioreceptivity, Basalt, Xitle volcano, Colonization, Biological rock crust, Lava, Primary ecological successionSubject terms: Biological techniques, Ecology, Microbiology, Biogeochemistry, Ecology, Environmental sciences, Solid Earth sciences, Materials science

Introduction

Volcanic eruptions create new surfaces where life can establish. Among the factors that come together for ecosystem development in these surfaces, intrinsic rock properties and small-scale landforms (geoforms) contribute to biodiversity via bioreceptivity. We investigate secondary bioreceptivity sensu Sanmartin et al., 2021, related to the different basaltic volcanic geoforms for the development of biological rock crust (often abbreviated to BRC), which are pioneer communities developing on rock surfaces. Our case study is Xitle volcano, part of Chichinatzin volcanic field, sited in Mexico City, chosen as it is a relatively young (1700 years-old) eruption, where bare rock and biological rock crusts are particularly well displayed1–3. Xitle has the common basaltic composition, and is thus widely representative, especially for the globally distributed monogenetic volcanic fields4.

We introduce the concept of bioreceptivity applied to volcanic terrain ecology, then we look at the establishment of life on volcanic rocks on Xitle. We take bioreceptivity into a natural field site, extending its use as a concept important in understanding volcanic landscape for a range of factors, such as biodiversity, primary ecological succession, soil formation, ecological services, urban biodiversity and sustainable planning of cities that use volcanic rocks as a building material.

Bioreceptivity

The relationship between volcanic rocks and life is direct, as intrinsic properties of rocks can favor the establishment and development of certain life forms. The bioreceptivity concept was developed originally for building materials5, referring to conditions that allow not only temporary passage of organisms, but also their settlement, development and reproduction. The concept of bioreceptivity has grown to include the possible impact of life development in cultural heritage conservation and more recently in urban planning for the development of green walls6–11.

Bioreceptivity studies have been primarily made on building materials using methods based on laboratory analysis. We integrate this concept to natural spaces, particularly basaltic volcanoes. Field studies like this are lacking, needed and they are particularly called for in the most recent review of bioreceptivity12. For this reason, we take a more ecological approach, using a maximum of field observations and non-destructive techniques, with limited sampling. This is partly needed as the sites are nature reserves so the least intrusive methods are required. This is also true for monuments, so the natural constraints can potentially be used as a guide for these. Thus, this study broadens the concept of bioreceptivity, linking it to ecological colonization of volcanic rocks, as the term implies an ecological relationship between geodiversity (the rock) and biodiversity (the life)5,13.

Bioreceptivity is related to the rock type, including its chemical composition and physical characteristics such as hardness or porosity5,14. These properties, in their initial state, make up primary bioreceptivity and control the colonization potential. As the rock is colonized, secondary bioreceptivity is created by changes made on the substrate by biological action. Climatic conditions (rainfall, temperature, air pollution) as well as other environmental factors of the geoform such as the slope, aspect, size of a feature (See12, Table 3) and geometry interact with primary bioreceptivity to control colonization. Geometry impacts the influence of environmental factors such as retention of moisture15. While material properties matter, the environment can often dominate in determining colonization success12,16–18.

Bioreceptivity is complex, with a dynamic set of parameters that change over time. One material can be highly bioreceptive to certain biological groups or in a particular environment, while not necessarily exhibiting the same propensity elsewhere12.

It has been emphasized how material properties such as porosity, roughness, water retention potential, and chemical/biochemical substrate availability govern microorganism (algae, bacteria, lichens) and cryptogamic vegetation colonization19. A quantitative index has been set up for granitic rocks used for construction, finding that both extent of growth and color change can be integrated to assess susceptibility of stone to biofilm development20. Life establishment on rock surfaces is strongly governed by substrate properties, particularly surface roughness, porosity, mineralogy and microtopography, which control water retention, nutrient availability and facilitation of hyphae anchoring21–24. Many studies focus on laboratory single-species colonization, but understanding of multi-species succession, climate variability and real‐world durability is still lacking25.

Establishment of life on rocks

The establishment and development of life on volcanic rocks depends on many factors. Bioreceptivity, is one factor, and whether primary or secondary, refers to the properties of the rock itself, complemented by extrinsic factors such as accessibility17, which is the colonization potential from environmental conditions around the rock (humidity, temperature, sunlight). Other biological factors play a role like richness and abundance of nearby organisms, their dispersal methods, life cycle and energy requirements. It is the combination of all these factors that control life establishment on volcanic substrates26. Volcanic rock bioreceptivity

Volcanoes are similar to buildings in that they appear suddenly and then are colonized by life: volcanoes are even called edifices. This is in contrast with other geological formations, which are exposed slowly by erosion.

As magma rises to the surface, decompression forms gas bubbles. Part of this gas escapes into the atmosphere, and bubbly magma that erupts explosively forms vesicular-rich fragments (scoria) that accumulate to form a scoria cone27. The less-bubbly magma erupts effusively, feeding lava flows28. The erupted lava can take different forms depending on the pre- eruption relief, eruption type and composition29–31. On the surface, lavas cool rapidly (minutes to hours for the surface, days for the interior) forming dark-colored glassy rock, containing small crystals and vesicles (bubbles).

Lavas have morphologically very complex surfaces31 and may be covered by broken and twisted fragments ('A'ā lavas) or by a continuous crust that solidified as it flowed (called pāhoehoe). These surface types contain their own morphological forms like channels and levées, lobes, tumuli, ropey lavas, crevasses (inflation cracks) and tubes. All these create a variety of microenvironments that may be a factor in bioreceptivity of the rock26. Xitle volcano has a representative range of these features (Fig. 1).

Biological rock crusts

The first successional stages in rock colonization are given by biological communities that establish themselves directly, named biological rock crusts, where the rock itself is the habitat. These crusts can be formed by different proportions of cryptogamic organisms (bacteria, lichens, bryophytes, terrestrial algae, cyanobacteria, fungi, bacteria) strongly attached to the rock32–35. Biological rock crusts are structured communities embedded within a self-produced extracellular polymeric substance (EPS) matrix. This matrix mediates cell adhesion to surfaces, retains water and nutrients, and facilitates physical and chemical interactions between microorganisms and rock36.

Biological rock crusts can develop on the surface (epilithic) or inside the rock (endolithic), either on natural outcrops or on monuments and buildings and are valuable for examining biodiversity and ecological relationships with rocky substrates37,38.

Xitle volcano context

We focus on Xitle volcano in the Chichinautzin volcanic field, an area of high endemism and biological richness39. Xitle is a scoria cone and lava field with an area of 80 km2 (Fig. 2). The lavas are mainly pāhoehoe, with some 'A'ā 2. The lava field hosts a wide heterogeneity of ecosystems40–43 and has geoheritage value to Mexico City where it is located44,45.

The Xitle eruption occurred about 1700 years ago1 and although there is already a scarcely-developed soil and different ecosystems established in parts, many rocky outcrops remain, where biological rock crusts develop. Hence, Xitle volcano offers an invaluable case study for biological colonization processes in lava surfaces. Its basic composition and geoforms are representative of those generally found in monogenetic volcanic fields, which are extensive on Earth21,31,46.

The Xitle scoria cone is 3100 m above sea level (asl), and the crater about 100 m deep. The scoria cone is 200 m high and is feeds a lava field that descends over a 10 km distance and a 600 m height to the Mexico City basin. The cone itself and proximal lava flows are in conservation lands and remain partly unaffected by urbanization down to about 2500 masl, where Mexico City’s outer suburbs encroach. Within the city, a large protected area of lava flows is found in the Reserva Ecológica del Pedregal de San Ángel (REPSA) that is a nature reserve in the Central UNAM (Universidad Nacional Autónoma de México) campus.

Materials and methods

Sampling sites

The study sites were chosen to represent the main features and microenvrionments that are characteristic of the exposed (e.g., unvegetated) volcanic surfaces2 (Fig. 1). They were identified as: (1) the scoria cone crater, (2) a lava tube, (3) the surface of a low-elevation promontory, (4) a vertical wall (crevasse) inside a high tumulus and finally, (5) the ropy surface of a sheet lobe (flat lobe) (see pictures of the sites in Figs. 3 and 4 below). The first three sites (crater, tube and promontory) are near the emission source in the proximal, highest part of the volcano (ca. 2950 m asl), while the last two sites (crevasse and flat lobe) are on the distal, lowest part of the lava field (ca. 2250 m asl), inside the UNAM Central Campus (Fig. 2).

The geoform types (Fig. 1) were chosen as the most representative microenvironment features on the volcano. Four sites are surface features and include the wall of a crevasse cutting into the lava surface, while the tube site is inside the flow (Fig. 3). The tube site is an important end member of lava flow physical features, extending to the 3D nature for the lava. In fact, each site has some internal features, at a smaller scale to the tube, and the crevasse is partly enclosed, while the other sites have fractures, bubbles that give them an interior 3D morphology.

The sites were selected as the most representative of their geoform, from hundreds of possibilities, also for low anthropic disturbance, but easy access. One representative example was chosen as the landforms repeat the same characteristics. This was verified by extensive visual surveys of landforms on the volcano and previous work3,47. For example, (1) The scoria crater sampling site is part of a 100 m2 broad area of similar geoform, and was primarily chosen from being far from any anthropic interference. (2) The flat lobe site, and the crevasse site is a geoform repeated with the same morphological features in thousands of sites, and again was both accessible and hidden in the REPSA reserve, (3) The promontory is one of numerous that repeat the same basic features. (4) The tube site is one of about 10 tubes on the flow and was chosen, as the one that had the most common features, was little visited but accessible by walking (others required ropes). Also, the detail required for this in-depth analysis was too time consuming for choosing multiple sites, a more superficial larger analysis could follow using the detailed knowledge gained here.

At each site a geoform and microenvironment characterization was made. Geographic coordinates were taken with a Garmin GPS and climatic conditions were recorded with HOBO sensors (temperature, relative humidity and light intensity) for 10 days in April 2024. This was made to see differences between the microenvironments during the dry season.

Thin sections: Biological rock crust-covered rock fragments of approximately 20 cm diameter were collected from each site. Three thin sections were prepared from each site (15 in total). The sample was mounted on a glass slide using an epoxy resin, with a thickness of 30 µm, allowing optical, petrographic microscopy, SEM and microprobe analysis. Thin sections were cut perpendicular to the surface, intersecting it. Others were made a few cm inside the sample.

The thin sections were scanned and digitized for image analysis vesicularity (porosity and pore radius) using Image ProPlus software. Porosity was defined as the total percentage of void space in the rock. Thirty plagioclase crystals (length and width) were measured on each thin section to determine variability in crystal size.

Microprobe: A microprobe study was carried out on thin sections from the five study sites. The microprobe analysis was done on plagioclase and olivine crystals and glass. The selected points were mostly analyzed at the center of the crystals. Some crystal profiles were made from rim to center to check for compositional variations (Fig. 4). The crystals and glass were analyzed with the Cameca SX Five electron microprobe at Laboratoire Magmas et Volcans (LMV, Clermont-Ferrand, France) to determine the concentrations of major elements. The analytical parameters for crystals were a voltage of 15 kV, a current of 15 nA and a focused beam. For glass, we used a broader beam of 20 µmn. The average errors (2σ) associated with the procedure are 0.50 wt% for SiO2, 0.06 wt% for TiO2, 0.07 wt% for Al2O3, 0.22 wt% for Cr2O3, 0.27 wt% for FeO, 0.10 wt% for MnO, 0.33 wt% for MgO, 0.06 wt% for CaO, 0.05 wt% for Na2O and 0.02 wt% for K2O.

X-ray diffraction: Powder X-ray diffraction (XRD) patterns were obtained using a Malvern Panalytical XPert Pro diffractometer equipped with a Cu source monochromated in the Kα wavelength (1,541,874 Å) with a Kα2/Kα1 intensity ratio of 0,50. Scans were collected in the range 5° to 70°, with a step size of 0.033° and counting time of 240 s. Diffraction patterns were analyzed using X’Pert HighScore Plus Software48. Measurements were made directly on intact sample sections fixed on a standard sample holder.

Confocal microscopy: Confocal laser scanning microscopy (CLSM) with a Zeiss LSM800 coupled with fluorescence with lasers at 560 nm (green), 480 nm (infrared), 405 nm (magenta) and 640 nm (blue) were used to identify the structure of the biofilms. Small fragments (> 1 cm3) of rock covered with biofilms were documented. This technique allows resolution of different groups in an apparently homogeneous matrix because of the natural fluorescence emitted from different cell wall compositions by different organisms. This high-resolution laser microscopy gives three-dimensional arrays of very thin optical sections. A composite image of all channels of 210 slices of 369.93 nm was produced. These allowed us to observe the cellular arrangement and the interaction of the biofilms with the rocky substrate.

Scanning electron microscopy (SEM): SEM was done at LMV on the thin sections as well as on uncoated rock fragments to document the morphology of biofilms as well as physical bioweathering features associated with different biological groups. A JEOL JSM5910 LV equipment was used, a voltage of 15 Rv, 80 mA (filament heating), a spot size of 50 nm and a working distance of 20 nm, at high vacuum on thin sections and low vacuum on uncoated rock fragments.

Biodiversity analysis: Biodiversity was measured in each site using the richness and abundance of main functional groups as a criterion. To do so, we applied a method adapted for rocks from biological soil crust monitoring49 by Martínez-Báez (2020)3. A canvas grid with 20 cm2 interior subquadrants was superimposed on each biological rock crust (Figure S2). Three subquadrants were selected to measure the coverage of each biological group, including photosynthetic and bacterial biofilm coverage giving a total of 1200 cm2 measured at each site. The measurements were done by applying clear gridded acetate on top of each selected subquadrant and outlines of each biological group were drawn to later count the area covered.

For the identification of biological groups, particular traits from each group were observed under stereoscopic microscopy. Biofilms were considered to be visibly homogeneous in terms of color and texture. Slides were prepared for observation under optical microscopy to determine the main groups comprising each biofilm. For bacterial biofilms, SEM microscopy was performed (Figure S2).

For lichens, the following morphological characteristics were observed to describe the different groups: thallus colorations when dry and wet, texture, presence of isidia and/or soreids, branching patterns, marginal edges, thallus size, presence of rhizines and shape and size of apothecia when present.

Bryophytes were classified as mosses and liverworts. The following characteristics were considered for the description of the different morphotypes: in gametophytes, leaf size and color, and stomata; in sporophytes: capsule, foot and seta; in liverworts, thallus color and shape, presence of rhizoids and capsule. Details on the biological survey are shown in supplementary material (Figure S1).

To evaluate alpha diversity, the Shannon-Weiner index (S) was calculated with the richness and abundance (coverage) data for each geoform and beta diversity was calculated with the Jaccard index (S) to evaluate compositional similarity among sites50.

Site descriptions

After the field observations, a description of each site was made, as summarized in Fig. 5.

Description of the five representative geoform sites. (A) Crater, (B) Tube, (C) Promontory, (D) Crevasse wall, (E) Flat lobe. Each site is shown as a photograph (with the pictogram, given in Fig. 1), an annotated sketch of the photograph, and a cartoon section sketch.

The crater site is scoria scree on the northeast inner crater slopes (Fig. 5A). The rocks are mobile (loose) and have a lot of space between them, allowing circulation of air and water, and particle infiltration in between the rocks. To the east side of the scree there are pine trees while the west side is mainly grassland (Fig. 5A).

The lava tube is 140 m long and about three meters in diameter. The floor is made up of large blocks fallen from the roof as well as pāhoehoe and 'A'ā residual lava. The walls and ceiling are rough rock with lava stalactites and great diversity of textures and fractures (Fig. 5B).

The promontory is located on lavas close to the base of the Xitle cone. The promontories are created by channel levées, in this case, while lower on the flow they are caused by inflation (e.g., tumuli). The promontory is four meters long and about two meters wide. It has surfaces with different inclinations and directions and is made up of big blocks with fractures and cracks, making it a highly geodiverse landform (Fig. 5C).

The crevasse wall was from a tumulus in the REPSA. It is 3 m in depth, 5 m long and 2 m wide. The wall has a bubblier surface at the top becoming more massive at the bottom (Fig. 5D).

The flat lobe with ropey lavas is also located inside REPSA. It is 6 m2 and has no inclination (completely flat). The surface is bubbly and there are cracks across the ropes and below infolds (Fig. 5E). The last two sites (crevasse and flat lobe) are within a xeric shrubland, in contrast to the upper conifer forest.

The climatic factors that were measured at each site are shown in supplementary material (Figure S2). Among all sites, the lava tube has the lowest and most stable temperature with an average of 10 °C and no major oscillations over 24 h during several days. The lava tube has a near constant humidity, while the promontory and crater show greater changes throughout the day. The flat lobe recorded temperatures of up to 63 °C during maximum insolation, it receives 12 h insolation per day, and there is sparse surrounding vegetation, with little shade. The crater recorded the highest insolation of all sites and has little vascular vegetation around the sampling site. The crevasse had lower insolation, and the lava tube had none.

Petrology

Petrographic microscopy, microprobe and XRD analysis confirm that the petrology at the five sites is similar. In all cases it is the same basaltic rock with a predominance of plagioclase and olivine crystals embedded in a volcanic glass and microcrystal matrix, as described previously4. Coupled microscopy and bioreceptivity

Figure 12 illustrates the different and complementary information provided by the microscopy techniques to document the microscale interaction between the lava substrate and the biological crust. In situ observations of the rock-crust interface in thin sections show that vesicles are mostly occupied by mosses (Fig. 12A,B), while lichens are found in areas with smoother, more exposed surfaces (Fig. 12C). Hence, lichens settle in the uppermost up-fold zones of the ropey lavas (Fig. 12D) while bryophytes (mosses and liverworts) grow within in-folds, fractures, small cracks and vesicles. Photosynthetic biofilms develop first in irregular textures and vesicles and then extend to some smoother zones (Fig. 12F).

(H) SEM image of the same biofilm shows the filamentous cells encrusted on the rock surface.

In the crevasse wall, what in the field looks like a seemingly homogeneous dark biofilm (Fig. 12E), is actually a multi-species consortium of photosynthetic organisms such as terrestrial algae and cyanobacteria (Fig. 12G). This crust complexity is found in all observed biofilms, in ropey lava, crevasse wall and lava tube; the biofilms with homogeneous appearance are actually complex multispecific communities with a strong interaction with the rocky substrate where they are attached. In addition, SEM allowed us to observe rock bioreceptivity at a very small scale, disclosing microenvironmental preferences of these organisms. It is possible to see that the biofilm development closely follows the texture of the rock, where the organisms are more abundant in areas with small cracks and vesicles (Fig. 12H).

Discussion

Xitle is a suitable site for ecological colonization study and bioreceptivity of a basaltic scoria cone and lavas, because it is young, well preserved, with a well-developed range of volcanic geoforms and is accessible. Our strategy was to choose five representative sites to study in depth, to provide the first steps and a detailed foundation for any subsequent extensive work.

Our results confirm that the wide geodiversity found is directly influencing the biodiversity of biological rock crusts. The lavas of Xitle, despite having the same bulk rock and mineralogical composition, have a wide range of geoforms and variable porosity, which offer diverse ecological niches for the establishment of life.

We studied a natural object, adapting and testing methods to propose an approach to study bioreceptivity in a natural volcanic location. We characterized secondary bioreceptivity including the physical and chemical properties of basaltic rock, measured surrounding climatic conditions and measured biological diversity through richness and abundance. This gave a complete description of the bioreceptivity and ecological colonization for different volcanic geoforms.

We first discuss the results found regarding the intrinsic rock properties of composition and texture and then relate that with the bioreceptivity of each studied site, especially the extrinsic factors. Multiple factors are interacting and contributing to the observed relationships, however certain factors stand out as important. Subsequent multivariate analysis could be useful for more extensive data sets, but this requires the knowledge and understanding from this in-depth analysis before proceeding.

Variation of rock properties

The properties of each geoform are shaped by its history, such as the lava dynamics of inflation, flow and crust production that produced ropey lavas, promontories, lobes and inflation cracks (crevasses)28,29.

Crystal size is associated with the lava cooling rate. The smallest crystal size was in the crater scoria, as those clasts cooled fast in the air. The lava tube crystal size is associated with slow cooling in a heated insulated tube. The ropey lavas also had long travel but cooled when the lava came to the surface. The crevasse contains lava that slowly cooled internally prior to be quenched when exposed to the air by lava inflation and cracking, and so has the largest crystal size.

In addition to geology, the microclimatic conditions (altitude, orientation, inclination) together with the surrounding ecosystem, influence the diversity within the biological rock crust at each site. These factors dictate bioreceptivity, in the sense that the geoform creates the extrinsic receptivity for the microclimate, aspect and orientation. The surrounding vegetation is also partly controlled by the bioreceptivity in these areas, so accessibility and bioreceptivity work together to provide an overall ecological situation.

We found that rock porosity is one of the main characteristics directly controlling bioreceptivity (Fig. 11), as there are no other notable variations in chemistry or petrology. This relationship between the physical structure of the rock and preferential colonization has already been found in other rocks such as dolomites, marbles, limestones, sandstones and granites7,20,51–54 but had not been evidenced for volcanic rocks.

We found that porosity is the main physical property for colonization potential. This is supported by other work, in dolostone, for example, total porosity and pore radius differences determined different colonization stages and conditioned bioweathering52. The porosity promotes water infiltration and circulation, providing access to internal zones. Vesicles and voids retain moisture and facilitate windblown particle and organic matter accumulation.

Ecology and bioreceptivity of the sites

The following section discusses site-by-site results as they are site specific. Then, more general findings across the sites are discussed. This is because each geoform has very different features from the others (a reflection of the geodiversity), but they do share common aspects (found by the in-depth analysis) that allow generalization.

The biological rock crusts in the crater scoria were the most diverse (by alpha diversity) probably due to the high rock porosity, which causes high textural diversity. In addition, this site is formed of many different loose scoria fragments where each single block has its own microenvironment varying as it is transported downslope. This provides, in a small area (over a few 10’s of cm) many diverse microhabitats, for example lichens of different growth forms (crusty, foliose, powdery, fruticose) find a variety of conditions as degrees of sunlight exposure and relative humidity vary in each block.

In addition, the crater scoria harbors diverse bryophytes, explained by the preference of moss for vesicles and because moisture is conserved between and in the fragments. Organic matter carried by the slope gradient accumulates between the spaces and favors more life development (it is worth noting that any disturbance, by paths or natural movement, returns the site to a more dynamic lichen and moss receptive state). The crater is the most fragile and changeable site, in contrast to the lava flows, which have a firm, unchanging base4.

The lava tube is a subterranean geoform, devoid of sunlight and with a partly enclosed atmosphere with constant daily and seasonal temperature and humidity. This provides a stable environment; nutrients are delivered mainly by water infiltration. The absence of sunlight has favored the development of chemolithotrophic metabolisms55,56. We have also seen troglobitic fauna inside the tube, indicating that a whole food chain has developed inside the tubes57. Although the tube has the lowest indices of biodiversity, there are multi-specific bacterial biofilms that cover almost the entire surface of the tube (Figure S3): this suggests that the tube biodiversity could be unexpectedly rich, as recently suggested in other areas57–59.

The promontory morphology has areas of rock orientated in all directions and inclinations, exposed and hidden, this makes for very diverse secondary bioreceptivity (Fig. 5C). It has areas of variable textures, bubble size, bubble density, and gaps between blocks. This offers a very large variety of microenvironments and favors a greater biodiversity, as is seen in the Shannon index (2.6).

The crevasse wall is the surface geoform with the lowest biological diversity. This is probably because the wall texture is mostly smooth and homogeneous, with the least amount of porosity. In addition, because of its vertical orientation, it does not receive much light and is also shaded by the wall in front of it. Significantly, the smooth wall surface has crusty micro-lichen species that were not found at other geoforms. This indicates that the shaded, smooth surface is bioreceptive to other species compared to other sites. The extreme end-member of this trend is the dark tube.

The ropey flat lobe harbors biological rock crusts with a high biodiversity index. Most of these crusts are made up of different types of lichens, probably due to the high solar insolation and temperatures (Fig. 8). The lichens have a wide tolerance to the exposed conditions, due to physiological adaptations, such as accessory photosynthetic pigments and resistance to prolonged dry periods60,61. The ropey lavas are a good site to study microenvironmental preferences and bioreceptivity, as at a smaller, cm-scale we observe different effects. Specific organisms occupy cracks and fissures of rocks avoiding isolation and gaining shelter, trapped nutrients, moisture, thus providing themselves with greater microenvironmental moisture32,62.

Ecological colonization and bioreceptivity

Colonization at each site responds to changes in climatic conditions and nearby vegetation. For example, surrounding vegetation has a role in diversity by providing a species source, and is itself conditioned by the bioreceptivity of those areas. The geoform itself has an important role. We observe that secondary bioreceptivity in terms of rock orientation and inclination directly controls outcrop-scale microclimatic conditions.

The subsurface tube site has important extrinsic influence (the lack of light), which sets the conditions for specific life forms. Then, rock properties like the cave’s diverse microtextures exert a control on local colonization. This is seen to a lesser extent in the shaded crevasse wall, where specific lichen species are found that are not present in more exposed sites.

The relationship between petrology and biological colonization extends beyond basaltic field sites. In controlled experiments with different rock types, the chemical composition of basalt—rich in Ca, Fe, and Mg and low in silica compared with other volcanic rocks —has been shown to favor cyanobacterial growth63. The composition, along with high micro-porosity and reactive mineral phases, contribute to basalt’s generally higher bioreceptivity relative to other volcanic rocks63. Microtextures and mineral alteration importance in microbial attachment has also been demonstrated in rock incubation experiments, where microbial cells and filaments penetrated natural crevices and modified basaltic surfaces through biofilm development64. This is what we have observed in this natural study (e.g. Figure 12).

Together, these studies establish that bioreceptivity is a multifactorial property, emerging from the interaction between rock structure (porosity, vesicles, fractures), composition and alteration state. For the basaltic flows of Xitle, these findings indicate that areas with greater vesicularity and thus porosity are the most bioreceptive.

Our observations from Xitle can be interpreted through the conceptual framework of bioreceptivity5. According to this framework, bioreceptivity arises from a combination of physical, chemical, and environmental factors inherent to the substrate while including extrinsic factors12. For basalts, these include surface roughness, porosity, mineral composition, and the presence of glassy or Fe(II)-rich phases that enhance microbial attachment and metabolic activity63. Applying this to basalt suggests that freshly exposed glassy rock would exhibit high primary bioreceptivity due to its reactive mineral surfaces and micro-porosity, while subsequent weathering processes, like the formation of clays or oxide coatings, would further increase secondary bioreceptivity by improving water retention and nutrient adsorption62,64–67. Therefore, the observed patterns of microbial colonization on basaltic rocks, including ours on Xitle, reflect an interaction between substrate-intrinsic bioreceptivity and extrinsic environmental inputs.

Perspectives for the application of bioreceptivity

The results have implications for basalt-constructed monuments and building conservation. Recently, building construction and green walls, favoring vegetation and rock crust has been proposed, as they add value due to the ecosystem services that these communities provide, such as carbon sequestration, oxygen release, temperature regulation, nitrogen fixation, noise damping, and facilitation of vascular plant germination33,35,37,38,68.

This Xitle study confirms that porous surfaces are highly viable for bioreceptivity, that vertical areas are less bioreceptive than inclined ones, and that flat surfaces can be extreme, but if rough, can offer increased diversity. The provision of enclosed spaces mimicking tubes, and other volcanic voids could help increase biodiversity especially if provided with nutrient input.

By copying lava diversity, it would be possible to mimic nature in a construction. In addition, in urban environments already built on volcanic lava (like parts of Mexico City, and many other cities), construction could harmoniously meld with the environment using local resources. This could preserve the best basalt ecological services, such as water infiltration and retention, ecosystem diversity, carbon capture, nitrogen fixation, pollutant capture and temperature regulation.

Exploring bioreceptivity of Xitle basalts has allowed us to explore the origins and extent of biodiversity in this young, fresh volcanic environment. It also has allowed us to understand basaltic rocks as important diverse ecological niches for the development of life. This work has very broad significance, as basaltic landforms dominate monogenetic volcanic fields, shield volcanoes forming a large proportion of arc and rift stratovolcanoes.

Recommendations on further study

Laboratory bioreceptivity studies usually include coupling of different microscopies (SEM, TEM, petrographic) and methods like mercury intrusion porosimetry (MIP) and Brunauer–Emmett–Teller (BET) for porosity. For field sites, however, these latter methods are destructive or need sample processing52,67. Raman spectroscopy has been used to complement SEM for basaltic rock in Svalbard, Norway66 and different Raman laser wavelengths are key to organic and inorganic identification.

For biological group identification, further field studies should include quantitative and qualitative assessments such as coverage, appearance and biomolecular techniques such as DNA or RNA are a further, challenging step25. Laboratory cultivation could be done, but this is only possible for a fraction of the extracted microorganisms. Knowing the crust species’ identity would allow us to explore more deeply bioreceptivity relationships, given that each species has specific requirements, preferences, tolerance ranges and interaction with substrate and with other organisms.

Further research on basaltic substrate colonization should explore links between rock properties, microbial metabolisms and environmental variables that define primary and secondary bioreceptivity. Integrating high-resolution mineralogical and microstructural analyses with metagenomic and metatranscriptomic approaches could clarify how mineral phases and microtextures influence microbial community structure and function9,14,19,32,56,62.

At volcanic sites such as Xitle, sampling across lava flows of different ages with microclimate and moisture monitoring would help determine how time and abiotic factors—temperature, humidity, and nutrient deposition—modulate both primary and secondary bioreceptivity8,12,14,18,20. Moreover, interdisciplinary approaches linking geomicrobiology and ecology can reveal how microbial weathering contributes to long-term pedogenesis and ecosystem establishment on volcanic terrains35,62. Such efforts would expand the bioreceptivity framework and could be useful analogs for early Earth and environments where basaltic substrates dominate like other planets.

Conclusion

We have studied the geodiversity and ecology of bioreceptivity of different contrasting and representative geoforms on the Xitle volcano scoria cone and lava flows. Xitle rocks have a limited range of bulk composition, and small differences in crystal size and glass composition. The basalts vary more in textural properties, such as bubble contents, porosity and surface texture that are important intrinsic bioreceptivity factors.

In addition to differences between geoforms, climatic and microclimatic factors vary, due to site location and the orientation and inclination of geoform components (secondary bioreceptivity). Surrounding vegetation at each site also varies, which gives a different accessibility to each geoform, potentially influencing biological diversity. Thus, bioreceptivity and accessibility are part of the overall intimately-linked geodiversity and ecological diversity of the sites35.

The Xitle biological rock crusts contain 14 different morphological groups in all the sites. Each geoform has a distinct biological rock crust assemblage related to its specific primary and secondary bioreceptivity.

The geoform site with the highest diversity (S, Shannon index) is the crater scoria, followed by the promontory and the flat lobe of ropey lavas, and this correlates with porosity from bubbles. The crevasse wall site has lower diversity and a particular lichen assemblage reflecting the effect of reduced light exposure. The lava tube has the lowest morphological group diversity, as the crusts are composed only of bacterial biofilms. The lack of light in the tube is a secondary bioreceptivity factor created by the specific subsurface conditions.

It should be noted that each biofilm on the different tube morphological features is specific and this needs to be explored in further research.

In the same rock there may be different degrees of bioreceptivity, depending on biological group microenvironmental preferences. Thus, mosses are associated with vesicles, in-folds and fissures, while lichens are found in smoother, more exposed areas.

The more textured and less exposed rocky areas are more bioreceptive to photosynthetic biofilms. In the lava tube, stalactites and other protuberances of the lava tube have a smooth surface but a very porous interior and they are almost completely covered inside and out by bacterial biofilms.

Differences in the properties of the lavas do not just create variable bioreceptivity, but make them specifically bioreceptive to different groups. For example, while ropey flat lobe lavas can be very bioreceptive to crustose lichens, scoria from the crater is more bioreceptive to mosses.

This information can also be used in conservation at sites of interest built with basalt and also in ecological building planning and green walls.

Knowing the bioreceptivity of basaltic volcanoes, such as Xitle is relevant for our understanding of the highly important but understudied relationship between geodiversity and biodiversity. It can help us predict life establishment after volcanic eruptions, in most types of volcanic field, as the features observed are common lava landforms globally and possibly rocky planets.

Natural field sites may be hard to study for bioreceptivity as they present complexity and uncertainty not present in the laboratory, but they have great potential because of the diversity of features and interactions. Nature can inspire human works, through rigorous evidence and reasoning69.
