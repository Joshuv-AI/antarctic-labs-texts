Virulence patterns of Colletotrichum truncatum isolates causing soybean anthracnose disease and identification of resistance sources
Authors: Saloni Mandloi, Pooja Dhaka, Vennampally Nataraj, Vibha Pandey, Sanjeev Kumar, Laxman Singh Rajput, Giriraj Kumawat, M. Shivakumar, Palak Solanki, Satish Kumar Nagar, Hemant Singh Maheshwari, Vangala Rajesh, Milind B. Ratnaparkhe, Sanjay Gupta, K. H. Singh
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Anthracnose disease caused by Colletotrichum truncatum is a major disease in soybean in India. In the current study, a total of sixty isolates of C. truncatum were tested for their virulence against three soybean genotypes viz., EC 18,596, JS 20–34, and JS 95 − 60. Among them, based on AUDPC (Area Under Disease Progress Curve), CT-18 (OP133629) was found to be the most virulent isolate, followed by CT-12 (OR405122), CT-48 (OR975577) and CT-2 (OR975570). Through pooled ANOVA (Analysis of variance), it was found that there were significant Isolate (I) × Genotype (G) interactions (p < 0.001). Further, a total of ninety-five soybean genotypes were screened against CT-18 (OP133629), where no genotype was found highly resistant. However, seven genotypes viz., NRC 130 (DS = 1.80), NB 208 (DS = 1.85), AGS 136 A (DS = 2.00), NRC 202 (DS = 2.00), NRC 152 (DS = 2.00), EC 34,106 (DS = 2.00) and CAT 1504 (DS = 2.00) were found to be resistant. A sub-set of seventy-one agronomically superior genotypes and a high-yielding variety JS 20–98 were used in molecular diversity analysis. Out of them, one soybean genotype EC 572,136 was ungrouped, while the remaining 71 genotypes were grouped into six major clusters. Cluster I contained only three genotypes - EC 572,109, JS 20–20, and JS 20–38. Cluster II includes EC 34,372, EC 39,150, and EC 34,106. Cluster III consists - JS 90 − 41, TGX 854 − 429, JS 20–98. Cluster IV consists of only two genotypes - FASSY COOK and AGS 163 A. Cluster V includes 6 genotypes EC 915,933, NRC 130, PP 6, NB 208, MAUS 795 and CAT 1504. Cluster VI was subdivided into VIa and VIb. Cluster VIIa consists of JS 95 − 60, YP 34, M 43, M 41 and M 21. The remaining 49 genotypes were included in cluster VIIb. Diverse resistant genotypes will further be utilized in breeding for anthracnose resistant varieties.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12870-025-07680-7.

Introduction

Owing to its multi-faced industrial and health benefits, soybean is rightly named as “golden bean”. It is the principal oilseed crop in India, cultivated over 12.07 million hectares, with 13.98 million tonnes production and 1158 kilo gram/hectare productivity [1]. Though India ranks fifth in soybean production, its productivity is significantly low compared to other predominant soybean- growing countries like China, Brazil, USA, and Argentina. Rainfed cultivation and several biotic stresses are the main contributors to its lower productivity in India. Anthracnose disease in soybeans is due to a hemi-biotrophic fungal pathogen, Colletotrichum truncatum [2]. It is one of the most economic diseases in India, causing about 115 kilo gram yield loss per hectare for every 1% increase in disease severity, and about 64.8% yield loss was reported due to this disease [2, 3]. Soybean is vulnerable to this fungus at all its growth stages. It is seed-borne in nature, causing pre-emergence damping off and affecting the plant stand in the field. Further, cotyledons, when infected, result in cankers, and the infection further disseminates to the young stems. Under severe conditions, numerous cankers develop on the stem, resulting in seedling death. Foliar symptoms include cankers on the petiole, leaf rolling, and necrotic veins, leading to pre-mature defoliation. On the infected pods, several irregularly shaped necrotic areas are observed. Under severe conditions, especially when infection occurs during the early pod development stage, necrotic lesions result in pod blanking. Pod blanking is a condition where seeds are underdeveloped or not at all developed in the infected pod [4].

A detailed understanding of the virulence pattern and genetic basis of the host resistance is of paramount importance in tackling this disease strategically. To our knowledge, there is no systematic study carried out on pathogenicity characterization and understanding of the virulence pattern in C. truncatum against soybeans. Indian mega-varieties like JS 95 − 60 and JS 335 have become highly susceptible to this disease, and pose a threat to sustainable soybean production. Nevertheless, there is a modest pace of advances in soybean resistance breeding and genetics against C. truncatum. A few studies reported resistance sources under hot-spot conditions [5, 6] and through artificial in-vivo screening [7, 8]. Artificial in-vivo screening is effective in the identification of potential donors, since the disease escape is minimized significantly. This also can facilitate the identification of isolate specific resistance sources and isolate specific resistance genes. However, it has some limitations, such as the initial establishment and maintenance of screening facility, the influence of micro-climate fluctuations, labour intensive, and challenges in large scale screening due to limited space constraints. A rapid in-vitro screening method was developed and successfully demonstrated by Zhu et al. [9] to avoid many of these constraints. Since soybean has a narrow genetic base, identifying diverse parents and employing them in hybridization is a crucial step for the genetic improvement of different economic traits. Genetic diversity analysis based on molecular markers is more precise and accurate as compared to morphology and phenotype-based diversity analysis. The Simple sequence repeats (SSR) are PCR (Polymerase Chain Reaction) based co-dominant molecular markers that are abundant across the plant genome with a high polymorphic rate. These have been extensively employed in molecular diversity analysis for different traits across different crop species [10–13]. In soybeans, SSR markers have been extensively used in diversity analysis [14–18]. With this backdrop, considering the economic importance of anthracnose disease in soybean, the current study aimed at the characterization of pathogenicity of C. truncatum isolates, understanding the virulence pattern among these isolates, identification of potential soybean donors for anthracnose resistance and molecular diversity analysis of the genotypes under study.

Materials and methods

Pathogenicity test of isolates

To study the differential ability of isolates to infect soybean genotypes, a total of sixty isolates of C. truncatum were tested against three soybean genotypes, namely JS 95 − 60, JS 20–34, and EC 18,596. These three genotypes were chosen based on their differential disease reaction under field conditions, EC 18,956 being resistant, while JS 20–34 is moderately resistant and JS 95 − 60 is highly susceptible. The isolates were collected from different soybean growing regions of India, and are being maintained at ICAR-National Soybean Research Institute, Indore, India (Figs. 1 and 2). Frequently, the pathogen virulence is maintained through inoculating the host (Glycine max (L.) Merr. cultivar JS 95 − 60) and the pathogen is re-isolated from infected tissue showing characteristic anthracnose disease symptoms. Details of the isolates are given in Table S1. The seven days old pure cultures of the isolates were used for the preparation of inoculum. For this, the mycelia of these pure cultures were crushed in a mortar pestle with 10 ml of sterilized distilled water and the solution was filtered using a muslin cloth and filtrate was collected in a beaker. The solution was vortexed thoroughly to extract the spores, which were transferred to 50 ml tubes and were stirred using a magnetic stirrer. The resultant solution was filtered using muslin cloth and the spores were collected. The spore concentration was adjusted (to 104 spores per ml) using a haemocytometer. All three genotypes were grown under controlled conditions in a polyhouse, and the screening method employed was detached pod inoculation [9]. Pods at the grain filling stage (R5-R6) [19] were collected in an ice box and were surface sterilized with 1% sodium hypochlorite solution. Immediately after surface sterilization, pods were dipped in a spore suspension solution for 30 s. After inoculation, the excess conidial suspension was drained from the pods and transferred to moistened filter paper in petri dishes, followed by another moistened filter paper onto the inoculated pods. Such a process was adopted to ensure optimum moisture and relative humidity for disease development. The petri dishes were sealed with paraffin strips under aseptic conditions and were then transferred to an incubator at 26 °C. The experiment was conducted in a 3 × 60 factorial design with three replications, each replicate containing five pods. For each genotype-isolate combination, a control was maintained where distilled water was used for soaking the pods, instead of spore suspension. Disease symptoms start to appear 24 h after inoculation. Therefore, disease assessment was based on percent disease incidence (PDI) 24 h, 48 h, and 72 h post-inoculation. Pathogenicity assessment of isolates was done based on area under the disease progress curve (AUDPC). Based on PDI, AUDPC the was calculated as per Shaner and Finney [20].

Evaluation of soybean genotypes for anthracnose resistance

Using a highly virulent isolate, ninety-five early maturing soybean genotypes, including two susceptible checks (JS 95 − 60 and JS 90 − 41) were screened for anthracnose resistance through in-vitro pod inoculation. Details of the genotypes are given in Table S2. All the genotypes were grown under controlled conditions in a polyhouse at 28 ± 2 °C. At the R5-R6 growth stage, green pods from each genotype were harvested for inoculation. Inoculum preparation and inoculation was carried out as per the procedure described in earlier section. As mentioned in the above section, a control was maintained for each genotype. Disease resistance evaluation was carried out in a randomized complete block design with four replications of five pods per replication. Percent disease incidence was recorded 24 h, 48 h, and 72 h post-inoculation, and disease assessment was based on 0–5 disease scale at 72 h post-inoculation [21] (Table 1). AUDPC was calculated as per Shaner and Finney [20].

Molecular diversity analysis

Seventy-one agronomically superior genotypes of the current study and a high-yielding soybean variety- JS 20–98 were used in diversity analysis, using 40 SSR markers distributed across the soybean genome [22]. Soybean genomic DNA was isolated using the CTAB method [23]. Isolated DNA was purified and quantified using the nanodrop. The PCR reaction (Thermocycler: Applied Biosystems, USA) was carried out with a thermal profile set up at an initial denaturation temperature of 94 °C for 3 min, followed by 38 cycles of denaturation (94 °C for 40 s), annealing (55 °C for 60 s), and extension (72 °C for 60 s). Subsequently, the amplified SSR fragments were size-separated on metaphor agarose (3.5%) gels containing ethidium bromide (0.5 µg/ml) in 1X TAE buffer. Finally, the gels were photographed under UV illumination using a transilluminator (SynGene, UK). The SSR marker analysis was carried out as per Amrate et al. [17]. All the polymorphic markers in each genotype were recorded for the number of alleles present in the particular marker. Bands were scored as 1 (presence) or 0 (absence) for each allele and missing bands were scored as 9. The Polymorphic information content (PIC) value for each marker was computed as per Botstein et al. [24].

Where, Pi is the ith allele frequency among the genotypes under study and was computed for each SSR marker locus. Jaccard’s similarity coefficient was used to estimate the genetic similarity among genotypes. The resulting similarity matrix was further analysed using the UPGMA (unweighted pair-group method arithmetic average) clustering algorithm for construction of the dendrogram.

Statistical analysis

Prior to analysis of variance (ANOVA), data on AUDPC was normalized by the best transformation method “Ordered Quantile normalizing transformation” identified through the R package ‘bestNormalize’ [25]. Two-way ANOVA (Factorial) in case of virulence characterization was carried out using SAS software version 9.2 [26]. One-way ANOVA and likelihood ratio test (LRT) in case of genotype screening experiment were carried out using the R package “metan” [27]. The Least significant difference (LSD) test was carried out using the R package “agricolae” [28]. The phylogenetic tree was constructed based on the genotypic data on polymorphic SSR markers by using the software NTSYSpc version 2.2 [29].

Results

Testing the virulence of isolates

Sixty C. truncatum isolates were subjected to virulence test against three soybean genotypes viz., JS 95 − 60, EC 18,596, and JS 20–34, through pod inoculation method under in vitro conditions (Fig. 3). The mean area under the disease progress curve (AUDPC) across the three genotypes ranged from 40.27 to 92.40. Highest AUDPC was recorded in case of CT-18 (92.40) followed by CT-12 (90.55), CT-48 (88.70) and CT-2 (87.96), while least AUDPC was noted in case of CT-10 (40.27) followed by CT-38 (41.29), CT-59 (45.46) and CT-44 (45.92) (Table 2). The genotype-isolate combination-wise AUDPC was given in Table S3. The least AUDPC was found in genotype EC 18,596 against CT-44 (7.77) followed by EC 18,596 against CT-28 (9.72) and EC 18,596 against CT-59 (9.72) while the highest AUDPC was recorded in genotype JS 20–34 against CT-48 (104.16) followed by JS 20–34 against CT-2 (103.88) and JS 20–34 against CT-31 (103.61). In the case of EC 18,596, the least AUDPC was recorded against CT-44 (7.77), followed by CT-28 (9.72) and CT-59 (9.72), while the highest AUDPC was observed against CT-12 (75.00) followed by CT-31 (74.72) and CT-54 (70.27). In the case of JS 95 − 60, the least AUDPC was recorded against CT-38 (40.55), followed by CT-10 (43.88) and CT-4 (57.77), while the highest AUDPC was recorded against CT-24 (101.27) followed by CT-31 (98.88) and CT-48 (98.61). Similarly, in the case of JS 20–34, the least AUDPC was noted against CT-10 (62.22), followed by CT-59 (64.44) and CT-44 (67.22) while the highest AUDPC was observed against CT-48 (104.16) followed by CT-2 (103.88) and CT-31 (103.61). Through two-way ANOVA (factorial (3 × 60)), it was found that the isolate effect, genotype effect and genotype × isolate interaction effect were significant at p < 0.001. The total variation was predominantly contributed by the genotypic effect (Total sum of squares = 314.69), followed by Isolate effect (Total sum of squares = 137.57) and Genotype × Isolate effect (Total sum of squares = 45.33) (Table 3).

Screening of soybean genotypes against the most virulent isolate-OP133629

Ninety-five soybean genotypes including two susceptible checks- JS 95 − 60 and JS 90 − 41 were screened for resistance against the most virulent isolate OP133629. Based on disease score at 72 h post inoculation, out of ninety-five genotypes, no genotype was found highly resistant, however, seven genotypes viz., NRC 130 (DS = 1.80), NB 208 (DS = 1.85), AGS 136 A (DS = 2.00), NRC 202 (DS = 2.00), NRC 152 (DS = 2.00), EC 34,106 (DS = 2.00) and CAT 1504 (DS = 2.00) were found to be resistant. Twenty-seven genotypes were found to be highly susceptible; of them, the highest disease score was recorded in EC 34,372 and M24 (DS = 5.00), followed by EC 457,464 (DS = 4.95), JS 20–34 (DS = 4.95) and JS 90 − 41 (DS = 4.85). Out of the remaining, forty-five genotypes were susceptible, and sixteen genotypes showed moderate resistance reaction (Table 4; Fig. 4). Disease scores 24 h and 48 h post-inoculation were given in Table S4 and Table S5. Disease resistance assessment was also carried out based on AUDPC. Least AUDPC was observed in the genotype NB 208 (3.05) followed by NRC 152 (3.50), EC 34,106 (3.50) and NRC 130 (3.60), while highest AUDPC was recorded in EC 34,372 (86.72) followed by M24 (78.37), JS 20–34 (77.37) and EC 457,464 (73.75). (Table 5; Fig. 5). Through likelihood ratio test (LRT), genotypic effects for disease score 24 post-inoculation (DS24hpi), disease score 48 post-inoculation (DS48hpi), disease score 72 post-inoculation (DS72hpi) were found to be significant (p < 0.001) (Table 6). Likewise, through one-way ANOVA, it was found that the genotypic main effects for AUDPC were significant (Table 7).

Molecular characterization of soybean genotypes differing in response to anthracnose disease

The genomic DNA was extracted from 72 genotypes, and SSRs were amplified using 40 primers from 20 chromosomes. The sequence details of the SSR primers used in the current study were given in Table S6. All the genotypes evaluated for the molecular diversity with 40 SSR primers showed variation in their banding pattern. Of these 40 SSR primer pairs, all were found to be amplified, and 38 of them showed polymorphism (Fig. 6). A total of 109 SSR alleles were amplified, with an average of 2.72 alleles per locus. Six of the 107 polymorphic DNA bands that were produced by amplification of 38 polymorphic SSR primers were unique. According to the SSR analysis, the polymorphism rate observed was 95%. Maximum scorable polymorphic DNA bands were observed using the primer Sat_190 (5 alleles) and Sat_390 (5 alleles), followed by sat-196 (4 alleles), BE806308 (4 alleles), sat_362 (4 alleles) and satt244 (4 alleles). The least number of bands were observed in the primer satt575 (1 allele) and satt447 (1 alleles) (Monomorphic), followed by satt724, satt230 (2 alleles) and sat_381, sat_412 (3 alleles). The size of the allele fragments ranged from 80 to 420 bp. The PIC value, for the 38 polymorphic SSR markers ranged from 0.205 in SSR primer Satt484 to 0.827 in SSR primer Sat_390, with 0.484 being the average PIC/locus (Table 8). Nineteen SSR primers viz. Sat_390, Satt244, Satt717, Satt406, Satt252, Satt554, Sat_218, Sat_393, Sat_276, Satt588, Satt651, BE806308, Satt310, Satt022, Satt200, Sat_190, Satt126, Sat412, Satt396 showed PIC values more than 0.5 indicating that these primers may be considered more informative. Primers showing the PIC values between 0.26 and 0.50 may be grouped as moderately informative primers. The primers Satt724 and Satt484 showing PIC values less than 0.25 were regarded as less informative. Out of 107 polymorphic alleles identified, 6 alleles (5.6%) were unique and were amplified in only one genotype. They were; Sat_190 (100 bp size) in NRC-150, Sat_190 (130 bp) in NRC-142, BE806308 (240 bp) in M-45, Satt362 (260 bp) in NRC-138, Sat412 (320 bp) in JS21-05 and Satt249 (190 bp) in JS20-38 (Table 9). These genotypes also have low AUDPC. All 107 SSR alleles were used for the genetic diversity analysis. One soybean genotype, EC 572,136 was ungrouped, while the remaining 71 genotypes were grouped into six major clusters. Cluster I contained only three genotypes - EC 572,109, JS 20–20, and JS 20–38. Cluster II includes EC 34,372, EC 39,150, and EC 34,106. Cluster III consists - JS 90 − 41, TGX 854 − 429, JS 20–98. Cluster IV consists of only two genotypes - FASSY COOK and AGS 163 A. Cluster V include 6 genotypes EC 915,933, NRC 130, PP 6, NB 208, MAUS 795 and CAT 1504. Cluster VI was sub-divided into VIa and VIb. Cluster VIIa consists of JS 95 − 60, YP 34, M 43, M 41 and M 21. The remaining 49 genotypes were included in cluster VIIb (Fig. 7).

Discussion

Anthracnose disease caused by C. truncatum is a devastating disease in Indian soybeans [5]. Under congenial conditions, it can result in a 100% yield reduction in susceptible soybean cultivars [2]. Understanding the nature and pattern of virulence can aid in designing strategic resistance breeding programs. The genetic makeup of fungal isolates is associated with the climatic conditions, agronomic practices and genetic base of the commercial cultivars [30, 31]. Soybean anthracnose is reported to be a complex disease caused by several Colletotrichum species, with C. truncatum being the most predominant pathogen [30, 32]. The C. truncatum isolates under study were collected from the infected soybean plants across the major soybean growing regions of the country. It indicates the high prevalence of this species causing anthracnose disease in Indian soybean, which may be attributed to its seed-born nature and efficient long-range dissemination mechanism [31].

The four most virulent isolates identified in the current study were collected from Madhya Pradesh region of India, indicating the potentiality of this region as a hot spot for extensive studies on the characterization of the pathogen under study and resistance breeding programs against it. This is the major soybean growing region, where two mega-varieties; JS 335 and JS 95 − 60 rendered susceptible to anthracnose disease, indicating the need for a continuous anthracnose resistance breeding program for soybean sustainability [2]. The isolate characterization carried out in this study was primitive and needs to be explored further. With the advancements in cost-effective next-generation sequencing technologies and availability of genomic resources of Collectotrichum species, and soybean, our future studies will be on dissecting the molecular basis virulence, host resistance and host-pathogen interactions in soybean- Collectotrichum pathosystem. This can aid in designing better and novel strategies to manage anthracnose disease in soybean.

Out of ninety-five soybean genotypes evaluated, no genotype was found highly resistant, therefore, an extensive germplasm screening must be carried on to identify potential donors against the disease. A resistant genotype NRC 130, identified in the current study is early maturing, while another resistant genotype NRC 152 is early maturing and free from KTI (Kunitz trypsin inhibitor) and lox2 (lipoxygenase 2) [33, 34]. High-yielding early maturing and anthracnose-resistant food grade soybean varieties can be developed by using these two genotypes as parents in hybridization programs. On the other hand, genotypes such as EC 34,372 and EC 457,254, which are resistant under hot spot conditions at ICAR-National Soybean Research Institute [5], were found to be susceptible to the most virulent isolate of the current study. This underscores the importance of identifying location-specific resistance sources and employing them in development of regional soybean cultivars. Further, the diverse virulence ability of the isolates and the significant isolate × genotype interaction suggests that there is a need for identifying stable and multi-isolate resistant genotypes for deploying durable horizontal resistance against anthracnose disease. This also necessitates identification of races and host-differentials for better understanding of host-pathogen interactions and identification of host-resistance genes against specific races [2].

The purpose of molecular diversity was to identify diverse groups of genotypes and utilising them in breeding for high-yielding and anthracnose resistance. The high-yielding soybean variety JS 20–98 was included in molecular diversity analysis to identify anthracnose resistant genotypes diverse from this variety. Resistant genotypes- NRC 202 and NRC 152 which were found to be diverse from JS 20–98 will be employed in hybridization with this variety to develop superior and high-yielding segregants. Further, resistant genotypes NRC 202 and EC 34,106 falls in diverse groups, hybridization among them could results in genotypes with improved resistance. The high frequency of polymorphism and a notable mean PIC (Polymorphic Information Content) value in the current study with previous research [14, 17]. However, the lower number of alleles per locus indicated a relatively limited genetic diversity among the genotypes examined in this study. The diversity analysis revealed varying degrees of informativeness, as indicated by the range of PIC and allele richness and the unique alleles underscores the presence of rare genetic variants within the genotypes analysed. Using Jaccard’s similarity coefficient and UPGMA (Unweighted Pair-Group Method with Arithmetic mean) cluster analysis, genetic relationships among soybean genotypes were assessed. The formation of six major clusters illustrated distinct genetic groupings within the population. Notably, resistant genotypes were identified within certain clusters, indicating their potential for breeding programs aimed at enhancing resistance traits. Clusters V and VI, with diverse genotypes, suggest opportunities for hybridization and introgression to enhance genetic variability in soybean breeding programs. The subdivision of Cluster VI into VIa and VIb indicated finer-scale genetic differentiation, useful for developing targeted breeding strategies. However, the presence of ungrouped genotypes, such as EC 572,136, points to the complexity of genetic relationships, possibly due to admixture or genetic drift, warranting further investigation into their genetic structure for insights into their evolutionary history and breeding potential. The SSR markers Satt 390, Satt 554 and Satt 310 which could differentiate resistant and susceptible genotypes (data not shown) will be validated for their association with the resistance, through genome-wide association studies (GWAS) and bi-parental mapping.

Conclusion

Current study aimed at virulence characterization of C. truncatum isolates infecting soybean, identification of potential anthracnose resistance donors and molecular diversity analysis among the soybean genotypes. Out of sixty isolates, OP133629 was found to be the most virulent isolate, followed by OR405122, OR975577 and OR975570. Through pooled ANOVA, it was found that there was significant Isolate × Genotype interactions. Among the ninety-five soybean genotypes screened against OP133629, no genotype was found highly resistant However, seven genotypes viz., NRC 130, NB 208, AGS 136 A, NRC 202, NRC 152, EC 34,106 and CAT 1504 were found to be resistant. Based on molecular diversity analysis, resistant genotypes- NRC 202 and NRC 152 were found to be diverse from high-yielding variety JS 20–98. They will be employed in hybridization with this variety to develop high-yielding anthracnose resistant cultivars. Hybridization among the diverse resistant genotypes like NRC 202 and EC 34,106 can aid in developing genotypes with improved resistance.
