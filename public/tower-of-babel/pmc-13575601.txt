High Prevalence of Brucella microti, Co‐Infections With Other Zoonotic Pathogens, and Public Health Risk Related to Overpopulated Common Voles (Microtus arvalis), Czech Republic
Authors: Petra Strakova, Martina Dokulilova, Marta Heroldova, Andrea Fortova, Josef Suchomel, Ivo Rudolf, Jiri Salat
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTThe common vole ( Microtus arvalis ) is one of the most abundant rodent species in Europe and is known for its cyclic population outbreaks, which result in significant crop damage and an increased risk of transmission of zoonotic pathogens to humans. In this study, we confirmed that common voles are an important reservoir of multiple zoonotic pathogens in South Moravia, Czech Republic, with a high prevalence of infection and frequent co‐infections. Overall, we found 70 (50%), 72 (51.4%), 50 (35.7%) and 35 (25%) positive voles for Leptospira spp., Bartonella spp., Brucella spp. and TULV, respectively. Our results highlight the significant epidemiological role of this species in agricultural ecosystems. Therefore, further studies are needed to monitor vole populations and other small mammal species in this area to better understand pathogen dynamics and assess potential risks to human and animal health.Keywords: bartonella, brucella, hantavirus, leptospira, voles

Introduction

Rodents are one of the main reservoirs of zoonotic pathogens worldwide. They enable the maintenance and transmission of those pathogens due to their high abundance, rapid reproduction and occurrence in urban and peri‐urban settings [1]. The common vole ( Microtus arvalis ) is one of the most abundant rodent species in Europe. This species is particularly interesting for its cyclic population outbreaks, with an abundance of 3–5 years [2], in the agricultural landscape resulting in significant damage in crops and a higher risk of transmission of zoonotic pathogens to humans [3].

Some of their population cycles are characterized by extraordinary population peaks, both in terms of the number of voles and the damage caused. Such an outbreak was recorded in 2019 and affected a few countries in Central and Western Europe [3]. In the Czech Republic, the total damage was estimated at more than 1.65 billion CZK or approximately 61 million Euros [4]. The most affected areas were South and Central Moravia. By May, the economic damage threshold was already breached due to high numbers of voles in spring, and the extremely large adjoining fields of winter rape and cereal that are typical in South Moravia [5].

In this context, the aim of the presented work was to screen common voles trapped during the ongoing population outbreak in South Moravia to evaluate the prevalence of selected pathogens in this area. Monitoring common vole populations is therefore essential not only for prediction of future population outbreaks, which is important for farmers and agriculture in general, but also for better understanding of pathogen ecology and evaluating the health risks to humans and animals resulting from their overpopulation.

Material

Small mammals were trapped using snap‐traps in two main locations near Brno city (Mokra‐Horakov 49°13′ N 16°45′ E, Brno‐Turany 49°8′ N 16°39′ E) over three consecutive nights during the population outbreak in June 2019. These locations are part of South Moravia in the Czech Republic. This is an intensively agricultural area, where cereals and winter rape are the dominant crops. The total sowed area is over 317,000 ha. Cereals are grown on 194,000 ha (which is the second largest sowed area in the Czech Republic), of which wheat ( Triticum aestivum ) covers over 113,000 ha and barley ( Hordeum vulgare ) covers over 35,000 ha. The sowed area of winter rape ( Brassica napus ) is ranked at third place (approximately 43,000 ha), immediately after forage crops (almost 45,000 ha). This shows the considerable importance of cultivated cereals and winter rape in the study area [6]. We set eight trap lines in total: in the Mokra‐Horakov area, three transects in a winter wheat field (trapping Sites 1–3); and in the Brno‐Turany area, two transects in a winter wheat field (trapping Sites 4 and 5), one transect in a winter rape (trapping Site 6) and two transects in a spring barley field (trapping Sites 7 and 8) [4]. All aspects of trapping complied with EU Council directive 86/609/EEC on the experimental use of animals. A total of 199 animals representing two species (197 common voles— M. arvalis and 2 wood mice— Apodemus sylvaticus ) were trapped. In the first trapping location, Mokra‐Horakov, 63 animals were trapped. In the second trapping location, Brno‐Turany, 136 animals were trapped (Table 1). In both locations, the highest number of voles was trapped in wheat fields (63 in Mokra‐Horakov and 64 in Brno‐Turany), followed by spring barley field and winter rape field (48 and 22, respectively, both only in Brno‐Turany).

Methods

For Leptospira spp. and Brucella spp. screening, kidney samples were used for DNA extraction. For Bartonella spp. investigation, spleen samples were used for DNA extraction (QIAamp DNA Mini kit, QIAGEN). For Orthohantavirus tulaense (TULV) investigation, RNA was extracted from lung samples (QIAamp Viral RNA Mini Kit, QIAGEN) followed by transcription to cDNA using ProtoScript II First Strand cDNA Synthesis Kit (New England Biolabs). Leptospira spp. DNA in samples was detected using a PCR of the LipL32 gene [7], Bartonella spp. DNA using PCR of the 16S‐23S ITS region [8], Brucella spp. DNA using PCR of bcsp31 gene for pan‐Brucella screening [9] and the genomic island of 12 kb for specific B. microti screening [10], and TULV RNA using nested PCR for viral RNA‐dependent RNA polymerase gene [11]. All PCR reactions were carried out using Aptamer Hot Start Master Mix (TopBio). PCR products of positive samples were purified using the Wizard SV Gel and PCR Clean‐Up System (Promega) and sent for Sanger sequencing at Eurofins Genomics (Ebersberg, Germany). The obtained sequences were analyzed and modified using BioEdit version 7.7.1 [12] and MEGA 11 [13] software packages and were subsequently compared with available sequences in NCBI BLAST and NCBI GenBank databases. The effects of sex and crop type on infection and coinfection prevalence were analyzed using Fisher's exact test (VassarStats).

For detection of Brucella DNA, two molecular approaches were used. When using pan‐Brucella PCR, 40 (28.6%) positive samples were detected, while with specific PCR for B. microti , 28 (20%) samples were found positive. Overall, 50 (35.7%) individuals were positive for brucellae. From each group of positive animals, 10 representative samples from both trapping locations were selected for sequencing. Obtained sequences from each group were identical (GenBank accession numbers for pan‐Brucella sequences—PZ453065–PZ453074; GenBank accession numbers for B. microti‐specific sequences—PZ493602–PZ493609). Brucella species are very closely related to each other and obtained sequences from pan‐Brucella PCR are very short, that is why this method was used to identify genus, since our sequences were 100% identical with other members of genus Brucella (incl. B. abortus ). To get more information about species and based on the history of the occurrence of B. microti in South Moravia, we decided to use B. microti specific PCR. Our sequences were 100% identical with two available sequences of B. microti in GenBank (CP001579 and AM943814)—one originating from infected common vole trapped in South Moravia during epizootic in 1999–2003 [14, 15] and second from soil sample taken in 2007 from the same trapping site where infected common vole was trapped [16]. Since pan‐Brucella sequences obtained in this study were 100% identical with other members of genus Brucella and B. microti ‐specific sequences were 100% identical with only two available sequences in GenBank, we could not create appropriate phylogenetic trees. In Central Europe, anti‐Brucella antibodies were detected in seroprevalence studies done mostly in wild boards, less in brown hares, rodents and insectivores [17]. Later, B. microti DNA was identified in mandibular lymph nodes in red fox from Lower Austria [18] and from submandibular lymph nodes of wild boar in Hungary [19]. Molecular surveys in rodents and shrews in Germany confirmed presence of brucellae in common voles (12.3%), bank voles (17.1%), Apodemus mice (17.1%) and shrews (8%). They did not find B. microti in tested animals, but their sequences were closely related to Brucella sp. recovered from red foxes in Austria [20]. Hubalek et al. [21] described a case of human infection caused by B. microti, where a mammalogist was bitten by infected common vole, showed typical signs of disease including a small abscess, and even developed specific antibodies. As suggested in literature, B. microti is probably a soil bacterium with a sporadic transmission to rodents and other mammals. In the case of foxes, B. microti is likely to be transmitted by ingestion and in wild boars probably by indigestion or by rooting in the infected soil. The transmission among rodents is likely by biting or scratching. It is assumed that these animals serve as reservoirs/carriers of this bacterium and can maintain and spread this pathogen [22]. Recently, Freddi et al. [23] detected B. microti in domestic small ruminants in three farms in France. Three Brucella seropositive animals (each from different farms, two sheep and one goat) did not show any sign of brucellosis infection and B. microti was detected in reproductive tissues, including genital and retromammary lymph nodes that suggests a risk associated with the consumption of unpasteurized dairy products. Nevertheless, no milk samples were tested in this study. The low detection rate (one infected animal per farm) indicates rather accidental transmission of B. microti , and on two farms, they stated possible contact of farm animals with wildlife (wild boars, deer, hares, foxes, etc.).

Orthohantavirus tulaense (TULV) was detected in 35 voles (25%) and 10 representative samples were selected for sequencing. Based on BLAST analyses of sequences of partial L segment and the position of our sequences (sequence identity ranging from 93.2% to 100%, GenBank accession numbers: PZ453061‐PZ453064) in the created phylogenetic tree, we were able to confirm the presence of TULV clustering with already published TULV sequences obtained from common voles trapped in the Czech Republic specifically from the region of South Moravia (Figure 1). According to available literature dealing with the presence of different orthohantaviruses in the Czech Republic, TULV is the most frequent hantavirus detected in wild rodents, followed by Orthohantavirus dobravaense and Orthohantavirus puumalaense orthohantavirus [24, 25, 26]. In the Netherlands, they trapped common voles in the two regions located in the north and in the south of the Netherlands. The prevalence of TULV in common voles ranged from 12% to 45% in the northern region, while the prevalence of TULV in the southern region was 41%. The obtained sequences clustered with German, Luxembourg and French sequences [27]. During small mammals screening for zoonotic pathogens in Austria, TULV was detected in 2 out of 15 (13.3%) common voles. These two sequences clustered with sequences from already published sequences from Austria, the Czech Republic and Slovakia [28]. Korva et al. [29] confirmed TULV RNA in 5 common voles (33.3%), 1 field vole (12.5%) and 1 common pine vole (100%) in Slovenia. Schmidt‐Chanasit et al. [30] demonstrated TULV host sharing in M. arvalis and M. agrestis over a long time period in several regions of Germany. Later, Schmidt et al. [31] presented a prevalence of TULV in common voles (16.7%), field voles (9.2%) and water voles (10%) collected from Germany, France and Luxembourg. They concluded a hypothesis that once TULV entered the common vole population, it was able to persist over years. Our data obtained in this study together with available sequences from common voles trapped in Moravia confirmed this hypothesis since there is evidence of long‐time occurrence of TULV in the South Moravia region since 1991 (Figure 3). Schmidt et al. [32] investigated the spatial and temporal dynamics of TULV prevalence in common voles in 4 regions of Germany for 3 years. They detected specific TULV‐antibodies in 9% of animals and the mean prevalence of TULV RNA ranged between 7.3% and 27.4% based on trapping sites. Obtained sequences were assigned to TULV clades CEN.N and CEN.S as expected [33, 34]. In the case of our obtained sequences, they are assigned to TULV clade EST.S.

Leptospira DNA was detected in 70 voles (50%), and 10 representative samples were selected for sequencing. Obtained sequences were 100% identical (GenBank accession numbers: PZ453075‐PZ453083). Based on BLAST analyses of sequences of partial lipl32 gene and the position of our sequences in the created phylogenetic tree, we were able to confirm the presence of L. kirschneri in all sequenced samples (Figure 2). Treml et al. [35] examined small mammals for Leptospira serotypes trapped in 2004–2008 in South Moravia, Czech Republic. They found 173 (7.9%) positive for serotypes L. grippotyphosa, L. bulgarica, L. sejroe and L. icterohaemorrhagiae/copenhageni. In common voles, 83 voles out of 578 were positive for L. grippotyphosa (80×) and L. grippotyphosa × L. bulgarica (3×). Similarly, Zakovska et al. [36] investigated 855 rodents and insectivores for Leptospira serotypes captured in 2010–2015 in Moravia. Overall, antibodies to Leptospira sp. were detected in 6.1% animals. In this study, only six common voles were trapped, and antibodies were found in 2 of them (33.3%). In Switzerland, Adler et al. [37] detected Leptospiral DNA in 12.6% of trapped animals (mice, voles and shrews) but no common voles were included in this study. Mayer‐Scholl et al. [38] examined small mammals from most of the federal states of Germany with an overall prevalence of leptospiral DNA in 10% of all animals. Microtus voles ( M. arvalis and M. agrestis ) were the most Leptospira spp. positive species (13%) and L. kirschneri was confirmed in 89% of common voles, followed by L. borgpetersenii . Similarly, Fischer et al. [39] also carried out a Leptospira spp. study in small mammals trapped in 4 federal states of Germany in 2010–2014. Out of 3 950 animals, 524 (13.3%) were tested positive for Leptospiral DNA. They confirmed that voles ( M. arvalis —30% and M. agrestis —29.7%) were more frequently positive for specifically L. kirschneri than any other trapped species (mice, bank voles or shrews). In the Netherlands, Krijger et al. [40] studied wild rodents and insectivores for Leptospira spp. and Toxoplasma gondii. Overall prevalence of Leptospira spp. positive animals was 5.3% but only 2 out of 8 common voles were positive for Leptospira spp. DNA, specifically L. kirschneri . Jeske et al. [41] screened striped field mice for hantavirus, Leptospira spp. and vector‐borne pathogens from Austria. They found one mouse positive for L. borgpetersenii , while L. kirschneri was detected in one common vole and one bank vole as well as L. interrogans in one yellow‐necked mouse from previous study. In Lithuania, Leptospira spp. DNA was present in small mammals with an overall prevalence of 4.4%. Further analysis confirmed L. kirschneri in common vole, yellow‐necked mouse, striped field mouse and bank vole. In this study, prevalence was much lower than in other European countries and in contrast, the highest Leptospira spp. prevalence was found in yellow‐necked mice [42]. In two parks in Lyon, France, Garcia‐Lopez et al. [43] studied small mammals (rodents and shrews) as reservoirs for Leptospira spp. They found an overall prevalence of 11.4% and confirmed the presence of Leptospira spp. in all tested animals except M. arvalis , which was surprising regarding other already mentioned studies.

In this study, Bartonella spp. DNA was detected in 72 voles (51.4%) and 10 representative samples were selected for sequencing. Based on BLAST analyses of sequences of partial ITS region and the position of our sequences in the created phylogenetic tree, we were able to confirm the presence of two rodent‐associated bartonellae: B. taylorii and B. grahamii , with sequence identity ranging from 92.2% to 100% (Figure 3, GenBank accession numbers: PZ453630–PZ453637) which are in accord with published literature. According to published studies in Europe, the prevalence of Bartonella spp. ranges from 17% to over 60% based mostly on used methodology, and region and/or rodent species examined [44]. Obiegala et al. [45] examined small mammals from Germany and Czech Republic. Bartonella spp. DNA was detected overall in 67.3% animals with 102/174 (58.6%) positive in Germany and in 114/147 (only M. arvalis , 77.6%) in the Czech Republic. Knap et al. [44] carried out a survey in Slovenian rodents ( A. flavicollis , A. agrarius and C. glareolus ) with an overall Bartonella spp. prevalence of 40.4%. They confirmed the presence of B. taylorii , B. grahamii , B. doshiae and B. birtlesii . In Slovakia, the overall prevalence of Bartonella spp. in rodents reached up 64.8%, with prevalence of 61.1% in M. arvalis. In common voles, they identified B. taylorii and B. rochalimae [46]. In Lithuania, Lipatova et al. [47] investigated small mammals (incl. M. arvalis ) with overall prevalence of Bartonella species of 23.7%. They detected B. grahamii , B. taylorii and B. rochalimae not only in rodents but also in their ectoparasites. Szewczyk et al. [48] confirmed the presence of Bartonella spp. in rodents in the Chernobyl Exclusion Zone, with overall prevalence of 38.9%. Obtained sequences were identical to B. grahamii and B. taylorii .

To sum up the obtained results, the infection with one pathogen (48 individuals, 34.3%; 39 females and 9 males), or co‐infection with two (51 individuals, 36.4%; 43 females and 8 males), three (23 individuals, 16.4%; 9 females and 14 males) or even with four (2 individuals, 1.4%; both males) pathogens was observed. The co‐infections were detected more frequently. Males were significantly more likely to be positive than females, both for infection with at least one pathogen (Fisher's exact test, p = 0.024), for co‐infection with at least two pathogens (Fisher's exact test, p = 0.017), and for co‐infection with three or more pathogens (Fisher's exact test, p < 0.0001). Both wood mice and 16 (11.4%, all females) common voles were negative for all tested pathogens. Infection with Bartonella spp. or Leptospira spp. was found more frequently in both trapping locations. The results of infected animals also correspond with the number of trapped animals in those trapping sites. The highest number of infected common voles was found in wheat fields, followed by spring barley fields and winter rape fields. Crop type had a significant effect on pathogen positivity (Fisher's exact test, p < 0,0001). Individuals captured in oilseed rape were much less frequently positive for at least one pathogen (36.36%) than those captured in wheat (91.49%) or barley (97.14%). The results obtained in this study including co‐infections are as expected because mainly adult individuals were examined. Co‐infections with more pathogens detected in rodents are known in the literature ([49, 50], Jeske et al. 2021).

Conclusion

In this study, we confirmed the common voles as an important reservoir of multiple zoonotic pathogens in South Moravia, Czech Republic, with a high prevalence of infection and frequent co‐infections. The detection of Leptospira kirchnerii, Bartonella taylorii and Bartonella grahamii , Brucella microti and Orthohantavirus tulaense highlights the significant epidemiological role of this species in agricultural ecosystems. The high infection rates likely reflect the population density and age structure during period of overpopulation. Our findings also support the hypothesis of long‐term presence of these pathogens in South Moravia, particularly TULV and saprophytic B. microti . Therefore, there is need for further studies to monitor vole populations and other small mammal species in this area to better understand pathogen dynamics and assess potential risks to human and animal health.
