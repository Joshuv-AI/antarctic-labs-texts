Integrated chips: An interdisciplinary evolution in the Post-Moore Era
Authors: Ninghui Sun, Ming Liu
Published: 2024

For decades, Moore's Law [1], which predicted that the number of transistors on a chip would double every two years, has been a key driver of computing power growth. However, as transistor scaling approaches its physical limits [2], challenges like heat dissipation, rising power density, and quantum effects are making it increasingly difficult to sustain this progress. In response, systems of integrated chips [3], constructed by assembling multiple chiplets through 2.5D/3D integration, provide an innovative solution to improving overall system performance. Different from transistor scaling, chiplet-based integrated chips represent a third path to boosting integrated circuit performance rather than relying on aggressive fabrication processes and emerging device technologies only. Advanced packaging techniques [[4], [5], [6], [7]], such as hybrid bonding, along with innovative interconnect interface, allow integrated chips to overcome the size limitations of monolithic reticles and adopt a modular design approach. This enables the seamless integration of diverse chiplets within a single package. These integrated chips are vital for high-performance computing (HPC) applications, where minimizing latency, maximizing bandwidth, and optimizing energy efficiency are critical. The success of this technology is exemplified in commercial designs such as Nvidia's H100/B200 GPUs [8], AMD's MI300 [9], and Intel's Sapphire Rapids [10], which demonstrate their effectiveness in advancing next-generation supercomputing infrastructures. Integrated chips and chiplet technologies represent a fundamental shift in the design and implementation of next-generation computing systems, ensuring continued performance scaling in the post-Moore era despite the physical limitations of traditional transistor technology.

Addressing the challenges of the post-Moore era requires the convergence of multiple disciplines. Transitioning from monolithic to multi-chiplet designs involves partitioning, combining, and integrating chiplets—a process that requires expertise across fields like computer architecture, multi-physics simulation, and advanced CMOS circuit design [[11], [12], [13], [14], [15]]. The interplay between these domains is crucial for overcoming limitations in heat dissipation, power efficiency, and interconnect performance. For example, in electronic packaging, multi-physics simulations combine electrical, thermal, and mechanical fields to ensure reliable performance under various operational stresses. Moreover, energy-efficient die-to-die interconnects require complex mixed-signal circuit designs, which incorporate electromagnetic field simulation. The integration of numerous chips, including wafer-level-scale chips [16,17] and chiplet technology [15,[18], [19], [20], [21], [22], [23], [24], [25]], underscores the importance of cross-layer collaboration. During the fabrication of integrated chips, transitioning from traditional PCB and substrate techniques to advanced manufacturing processes [[26], [27], [28], [29]], such as photolithography, etching, and damascene copper interconnects, is essential for achieving micron- and nanoscale precision in integrated chip design. These advanced techniques involve the use of silicon interposers or bridges, but they also present new challenges. For example, fabricating through-silicon vias (TSVs) and managing warping in ultra-thin interposers over areas larger than three reticle sizes require a multidisciplinary approach. Successfully addressing these challenges requires expertise from diverse fields, including materials science, mechanical engineering, and semiconductor physics.

This issue of Fundamental Research focuses on recent technological developments in integrated chips from various perspectives, such as circuit innovations, computer architecture, and advanced packaging technology. It features three review articles, one perspective article, and one original research paper. Han et al. [30] analyze the area-wall problem and propose the Big Chip as a novel solution to enhance performance, presenting a performance model for evaluating Big Chip and discussing its architectural design, as well as future trends in its development. Cheng et al. [31] present a transformer-in-package DC-DC converter using glass-based fan-out wafer-level packaging, which improves power density and efficiency. Zhu et al. [32] review the progress in digitalized analog integrated circuits, including amplifiers, analog-to-digital converters, phase-locked loops, and power supplies, all of which are critical for energy-efficient, high-speed die-to-die interfaces. From this review, they identify emerging trends and future directions for digitalized analog circuits. Chen et al. [33] discuss the challenges and advancements in packaging technologies such as 3D IC, fan-out packaging, and chiplet integration, while also pointing to opportunities in standardization, co-design tools, and multi-scale modeling for sustainable development. Yu et al. [34] provide an overview of multi-physics coupling and recent advancements in multi-scale simulation techniques, exploring how these methods can be applied to various aspects of electronic packaging and outlining future directions. These articles offer valuable insights into integrated chip development, advance the field, and highlight future research directions.
