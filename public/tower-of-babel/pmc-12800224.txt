Deep learning-enabled cherry price forecasting and real-time system deployment across multi-market supply chains in India
Authors: F A Shaheen, Aqib Gul, Nazir Ganai, Masroor Majid, Mudasir Rashid, Abid Sultan
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractFor high-value perishable goods such as cherries, accurate price forecasting is crucial for the supply chain, stabilizing producer income, informing policy interventions & real-time decision support at various stakeholder levels. This study enquires whether Deep Learning (DL) architectures can be implemented in real time for daily advisory tools while simultaneously surpassing conventional statistical and Machine Learning (ML) models in predicting cherry prices. To tackle this issue, daily cherry price data from five wholesale markets in India were utilized for the period 2012–2024 to evaluate six forecasting methodologies, i.e., Seasonal Auto-regressive Integrated Moving Average (SARIMA), Prophet, Random Forest (RF), Extreme Gradient Boosting (XGBoost), Long Short-Term Memory (LSTM), and Transformer. The results show that deep learning models are better at capturing non-linear price changes than statistical or tree-based methods. The LSTM and Transformer models consistently performed better across all error metrics (MAE, RMSE, sMAPE, MFE, NMBE, and DA). During the 2025 cherry season, the best-performing LSTM was used as a live, web-based forecasting system that accepted real-time submissions from market officials and produced daily predictions based on field conditions. After the season, we checked the actual prices against the forecasted values and found that they were correct with more than 92 per cent accuracy for market-variety-grade combinations. In major markets like Azadpur, Narwal, and Parimpora, the sMAPE was often below 5–10 per cent, and the error margins (MAE 5–8; RMSE 8–12) were low. Further, the Diebold–Mariano (DM) test showed that deep learning was statistically superior to the baselines. The current study presents a novel and comprehensive operational methodology for advanced Artificial Intelligence (AI) models in the agriculture & allied sectors of India, establishing a scalable framework for incorporating ML/DL into agricultural market intelligence and advisory systems.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-025-30980-9.Subject terms: Engineering, Mathematics and computing

Introduction

Cherry (Prunus avium) is a highly valued horticultural crop cultivated globally for its flavor and nutritional value. The top three producers in the world are Iran, the United States of America, and Turkey, with India ranked 26th1. The majority of India’s cherry production comes from the Himalayan state of Jammu and Kashmir (J&K)2. Cherry production in J&K has grown from about 3.8 thousand tonnes to nearly 12 thousand tonnes over the past three decades, reaching a peak of 13 thousand tonnes in 20132 and further increasing to around 15.5 thousand tonnes in 2019–20203. Nearly 9% of the Gross State Domestic Product (GSDP) comes from the horticulture sector, and premium Kashmiri cherries are a significant cash crop and exportable commodity2. The global and regional importance of cherry cultivation emphasizes the need for efficient market mechanisms and price stability for farmers and traders alike.

However, cherries are seasonal and perishable; weather-related supply shocks or market gluts often cause price volatility. The commodity is extremely sensitive to supply and demand dynamics in real time, and these fluctuations are made worse by the absence of extended storage options4. Such market volatilities present serious obstacles for policymakers seeking stable agricultural markets and have a direct impact on farmer incomes and consumer affordability5. As a result, timely and accurate price forecasting tools are essential and of the need of the hour. Forecasting helps optimize inventory and logistics, plan for harvest & marketing, and provides the government with real-time information for successful policy implementation5,6.

Motivation

In the past, researchers have extensively used time-series modeling techniques to address price volatility in perishables. Because of their ease of use and strong statistical properties, techniques such as the Auto-Regressive Integrated Moving Average (ARIMA) and its seasonal variant (SARIMA) have been used for a long time to forecast agricultural prices. These models have also been widely used across crop types, from perishables such as tomatoes & mangoes to staples such as rice & maize. They are good at capturing trend and seasonality in univariate datasets7–10. However, when applied to highly non-linear or abruptly fluctuating data, the predictive performance of these models deteriorates11,12. While some improvements are provided by variations such as ARIMA + GARCH, they still rely on linear assumptions that may not fully capture the complexity of commodity price behavior. Although hybrid designs that use machine-learning techniques, such as random forests, for lag selection and combine ARIMA and LSTM models have increased robustness for volatile agricultural price series, they still depend on fixed parametric structures and are susceptible to regime changes13. Likewise, data science pipelines that combine traditional time-series models with modern forecasting tools for agricultural products indicate that we need to move away from univariate specifications towards more complex, feature-aware systems14.

Recent research has moved towards the use of artificial intelligence (AI) and machine learning (ML) techniques that do not require prespecified functional forms to address these constraints. More flexibility in identifying nonlinear patterns in agricultural time series has been demonstrated by Artificial Neural Networks (ANNs), Support Vector Machines (SVMs), Random Forests (RFs), and Gradient Boosting frameworks such as XGBoost9–12. Weng et al.12 showed, for example, that a neural network trained on a sizable dataset of horticultural prices performed better than a tuned ARIMA model over several forecasting horizons. Similarly, XGBoost has been effectively used to forecast rice production, greatly surpassing conventional methods15,16. Building on these developments, deep learning models have become popular for forecasting in agriculture, especially Recurrent Neural Networks (RNNs) and LSTM architectures17,18. For instance, recent research shows that deep learning architectures like LSTM and GRU can perform much better than traditional benchmarks for agricultural commodity prices, especially when nonlinearity and moderate volatility are strong19. These models have demonstrated strong performance in price and yield forecasting and are particularly good at learning long-term dependencies from large, high-dimensional datasets20–22. However, they have drawbacks like high processing costs, a need for a lot of data, and difficulties in capturing long-range dependencies or handling sparse data23.

By addressing some of the drawbacks of RNNs, transformer-based models that use attention mechanisms have opened new possibilities for time-series forecasting24,25. Transformers are especially helpful for capturing irregular seasonal trends and multi-year cycles in agricultural prices because they can model longer temporal sequences without depending on recurrence. Recent Meta-transformer frameworks that use Transformer encoders and metaheuristic optimization for hyperparameter tuning show even greater gains for perishable or seasonal agricultural goods. This indicates that attention-based architectures could work well in this area4. There is still a noticeable research gap in applying ML & DL techniques to temperate fruit markets, despite the expanding corpus of literature on agricultural price forecasting using these techniques. High-value & seasonal fruits like cherries have been overlooked in previous research, especially in the regional horticulture sectors like J&K, which have mainly concentrated on staple cereals or commonly traded vegetables. Targeted forecasting models that account for the specific market dynamics, perishability, and regional concentration of cherry cultivation in J&K are desperately needed. Mixture-of-experts architectures for forecasting vegetable prices, which dynamically choose specialized predictors across different types of goods, also show that ensemble and attention-inspired designs can deal with cross-commodity heterogeneity better than any one model26.

Therefore, the design, benchmarking, and operationalization of a real-time price forecasting framework for cherry markets is the specific goal of this study. Also, it focuses on answering the research question of whether DL architectures can offer better accuracy, robustness, and grade-wise fidelity than statistical and ML baselines. Moreover, the study aims to predict that DL models will not only perform better in prediction but also demonstrate operational dependability when applied to real-world market settings. Specifically, the research creates a decision-support system that directly connects AI-driven market intelligence to physical agricultural practices and policies by integrating real-time validation and providing farmers and other stakeholders with daily price forecasts as value-added advisory services. Previous AI-enabled price-forecasting studies in agriculture have relied mainly on ex post evaluation (based on historical data) or produced coarse, low-frequency outputs. To bridge this gap between methodological benchmarking and actionable decision support, the current robust & novel ML/DL based methodological architecture was developed and tested, which helps in (i) providing market-variety-grade-specific forecasts at daily frequency, (ii) validating model superiority with both conventional error metrics and DM test, and (iii) operationalising the best model in a live deployment that ingests official market entries and publishes farmer-facing advisories in real time.

Related studies

Traditional models for perishable fruit price forecasting

Early methods for predicting the prices of perishable fruits (and other agricultural commodities) relied primarily on econometric and traditional time-series models. Because of their adaptability in simulating trends and seasonality, ARIMA models, in particular, have become the standard. Fruit price series have been frequently analyzed using ARIMA and its variants, such as seasonal ARIMA and ARIMAX with exogenous inputs, which often yied accurate short-term forecasts27,28. For instance, Niu et al.29 used an ARIMA model for short-term price prediction of Hebei pear and found it effective for capturing the seasonal patterns of fruit prices. In the Indian context, Pardhi et al.9 demonstrated that an ARIMA model could reasonably forecast mango prices during the peak season in a significant market, achieving a one-step-ahead forecast with Mean Absolute Percentage Error (MAPE) below 10%.

These results confirmed that ARIMA can capture the seasonal spike (e.g., price doubling at the season start) with acceptable accuracy. Other statistical models, such as regression with seasonal dummies and volatility models (e.g., ARCH/GARCH), have also been explored to handle price fluctuations. For example, a study28 on apple prices in Himachal Pradesh compared ARIMA with a GARCH extension to account for heteroskedasticity, and found that ARIMA(6,1,1) with GARCH(1,1) provided the best fit to the historical price series. Although these conventional models are easy to understand, they often fall short of capturing the intricate, nonlinear dynamics of markets for perishable produce because they assume linear or predefined structures already exist in the datasets. Classical models only partially address the weather, disease, and supply gluts that cause sudden price swings in seasonal fruits and vegetables (e.g., via seasonal terms or differencing). In recent years, this constraint has enabled more sophisticated predictive techniques.

It is important to remember that traditional models are still competitive in some situations. An ARIMA model, for example, performed better than an LSTM network for predicting the prices of certain vegetables (such as lotus root and greens), particularly in capturing abrupt swings and linear trends, according to a recent comparative study30. The authors noted that the simpler ARIMA model produced fewer errors in those datasets, despite the complexity of the LSTM, highlighting that classical models can still be reliable and accurate in situations requiring high interpretability or limited data. On the other hand, recent research demonstrated the opposite. Badal et al. (2023)31 evaluated feed-forward ANN, GARCH, and ARIMA for tomato price forecasting in the Indian markets, and the neural network obtained the highest accuracy. These contradictory results imply that although contemporary ML frequently performs well, the relative effectiveness of conventional versus sophisticated models may vary depending on the data properties and the commodity price behaviour. Even as the field moves towards non-linear approaches, ARIMA and related time-series models are still commonly used for benchmarking and serve as a helpful baseline overall.

Machine learning and deep learning approaches

Researchers have increasingly used ML and deep learning (DL) techniques to improve forecasting performance for perishable commodities, driven by the growing availability of price data and computing power. ML/DL techniques, unlike parametric time-series models, can identify multivariate dependencies and non-linear patterns in the data. Recent findings also show that hybrid ensemble learning frameworks that use a single meta-learning system to combine multiple ensemble strategies, such as bagging, boosting, stacking, and dagging, can achieve more accurate and stable predictions for highly unstable financial time series32. However, in practice, there hasn’t been much use of traditional machine learning algorithms (such as gradient boosting, random forests, or support vector regression) for predicting fruit prices. Recent literature suggests that very few studies have examined advanced ML specifically for fruits or vegetables, and the majority of price-prediction studies for fresh produce still use basic models (ARIMA, simple ANN, SVM) 33–37. However, Zhang et al.38 presented a LightGBM boosting model for fresh agricultural prices (including bananas) and reported better accuracy and stability than ARIMA, SVM, and feed-forward neural nets, primarily based on an ensemble tree-based approach. LightGBM demonstrated the promise of contemporary ML by achieving the lowest error and proving greater resilience as the forecast horizon increased in their evaluation across several commodities.

The ability of DL techniques to automatically recognize intricate time-series patterns has made them popular. Forecasting agricultural prices has extensively used recurrent neural networks, particularly LSTM networks. Static models are unable to learn non-linear relationships and long-term dependencies19. Empirical studies19 consistently show that LSTM-based models outperform traditional models for volatile crop prices across various error metrics. For instance, when predicting apple prices in the Indian market, Thakur et al.28 discovered that an LSTM-RNN produced lower RMSE and MAPE than the top ARIMA and GARCH models. The advantage of LSTM in capturing nonlinear price-driving factors (weather shocks, supply lags, etc.) was demonstrated by its superior ability to fit the actual price trajectory and to generalize beyond the training data. According to Yin et al. (2020)39, researchers also found that proposed STL-LSTM networks reduce prediction error in the vegetable price data, particularly for abrupt price spikes. This is mainly because LSTM can model irregular jumps and seasonal effects in time series. With this method, accuracy increased significantly. For highly seasonal price series, MAPE dropped to about 7%, and the STL preprocessing and attention produced a prediction accuracy that was roughly 12% higher than a vanilla attention-LSTM without any decomposition.

There has also been an investigation into other deep architectures, such as for crop price forecasting. Murugesan et al. (2022)40 assessed variants such as CNN-LSTM hybrids, stacked LSTM layers, bi-directional LSTM, and even ConvLSTM. These tests demonstrate how deep models can be customized to capture various aspects of price behavior, such as cycles, trends, and volatility, and frequently outperform individual traditional or machine learning models in terms of predictive performance. Although RNN/LSTM variants are still the most widely used in fruit price applications, convolutional neural networks and hybrid deep networks have also been tested alongside recurrent models41–43. Using data on vegetable prices, Wu and Sun (2025)26 assessed state-of-the-art models (including more recent Transformers such as PatchTST and “iTransformer”) and found that these DL models performed well overall. Sari et al. (2024)44 used a neural network optimised by a genetic algorithm for Extreme Learning Machines (ELMs) to forecast the prices of 11 major agricultural commodities. Their ELM-GA model outperformed both a comparator LSTM-GA model and a benchmark ARIMA across all 11 commodities, even during the high volatility of 2022’s price swings. In complex, real-world datasets, ML techniques (such as optimised ELMs or hybrid networks) can outperform both conventional models and conventional deep networks. This study was one of the first to predict a large set of commodities over extended periods successfully.

Case studies on various individual fruits further illustrate the benefits of advanced models. Ma et al. (2023)44 used an LSTM model and a back-propagation neural network to forecast daily mango prices with remarkably high accuracy. In testing, the hybrid BP-LSTM model outperformed a standalone BP network and a single LSTM, achieving an MAPE below 0.2% and an R2 of 0.99. These findings demonstrated that meticulously crafted DL models (and their ensembles) can fit the real price trajectories of highly volatile, seasonal fruits with previously unheard-of accuracy. Recent studies also highlight the significance of tuning and model generalisation. Nayak et al. (2025)4 presented a meta-transformer framework that combines Transformers with metaheuristic algorithms for hyperparameter optimisation. Their Transformer models (with PSO/GWO/WOA optimisers) produced 70–90% lower error than a GARCH benchmark when applied to potato prices. This is part of a larger movement to improve forecasting accuracy and stability by combining cutting-edge DL architectures with optimisation methods.

Real-time forecasting and deployment frameworks

The shift to real-time forecasting and deployable systems is an equally significant component of related work. Still, the majority of the studies mentioned previously focus on modeling accuracy using historical data. Researchers have proposed hybrid architectures combining machine learning models with classical time-series components and stochastic simulation to predict agricultural commodity prices. These models would link forecasting performance with risk analysis and food security concerns at both the national and regional levels45. Forecasts of fruit prices are most helpful in practice when they are promptly provided to farmers, traders, and policymakers so they can make informed decisions on storage, market timing, and policy interventions. This has also been recognized by the recent studies as well, primarily based upon the fact that the forecasting frameworks’ scalability and latency are essential in addition to the accuracy4. Deployment architectures that can continuously update forecasts and ingest real-time data, such as daily market arrivals and prices, are gaining high popularity among researchers and practitioners. Some studies have also suggested pipeline designs in which cloud-based predictive models receive fresh price data and produce forecasts that farmers can view on a user dashboard or mobile app. It can be challenging to guarantee low latency in such systems, particularly because deep ensembles and transformer models can be computationally demanding.

Outside academia, the push for real-time price forecasting is evident in industry initiatives and government programs. Large agribusiness companies and retailers have begun investing in AI-driven price prediction systems. Notably, Walmart announced trials of a ML price-forecasting tool to predict the future prices and availability of fresh produce and other staples46. The objective is to anticipate price fluctuations to optimize inventory and procurement, which is a clear indication that real-time forecasting is transitioning from research to operational practice. Digital agriculture platforms and agri-tech startups also offer forecasting services. To bridge the gap between sophisticated predictive analytics and real-world farm-level decision-making, evolving platforms in India, such as StarAgri and Cropway, provide AI-driven advisory services, offer real-time commodity price forecasts to assist farmers in choosing which crop to plant or when to sell47,48. These deployment frameworks often integrate a user-friendly interface (mobile apps, SMS alerts) with back-end predictive models that are updated daily or weekly.

Recent research in this area has not only enhanced modelling techniques but also increasingly considered model deployment, particularly towards delivering accurate forecasts in a timely, usable format. At the macro level, adaptive food price forecasting systems are now being deployed to improve public information and guide policy responses during periods of rapid economic change, underscoring the importance of operational, uncertainty-aware forecasts for decision-makers49. However, there are still problems in this area, such as how well the model performs, how easy it is for end users to understand, how good the data is, and how well it fits. Contemporary research is aiding the development of prototype systems that demonstrate how an automated system could deliver an effective forecast, such as predicting cherry prices in the near future. Besides, these frameworks demonstrate how model updating and streaming data-handling methods have improved over time. Also, recent research indicates that enhancing forecast accuracy is merely the initial step; the subsequent step is ensuring that these forecasts are applicable in real time to support effective financial decisions in perishable sectors. In India, though, platforms like e-NAM or private agri-tech advisories mostly share static analytics or observed market prices and not AI-enabled predictive forecasts. Moreover, there is a dearth of studies that have integrated advanced DL architectures with prospective, real-time implementation during an operational horticultural season. Consequently, this study is a unique effort to implement and validate a forecasting system in the Himalayan states of India, particularly in perishable fruit markets.

Materials and methods

This section presents the end-to-end methodological framework adopted for forecasting cherry prices using AI/ML models. It includes data collection, preprocessing, model training, evaluation, and deployment strategies.

Study context and market selection

To model and predict price dynamics in the regional cherry value chain, the current study was conducted across important cherry markets in Jammu and Kashmir (J&K), India, and a primary external wholesale market in Delhi (the national capital). The markets included in the study are presented in Fig. 1 and cover Azadpur Delhi, Parimpore Srinagar, Zazna Ganderbal, Aglar Shopian, and Narwal Jammu. The market selection was primarily governed by contributions to overall price discovery mechanisms for cherry commodities, both within and outside the J&K region, as well as by their role in regional trade volumes and spatial linkages. During the marketing season, the main hubs for the aggregation and exchange of cherry produce in the Kashmir Valley are the horticultural clusters of Parimpore Srinagar, Zazna Ganderbal, and Aglar Shopian. To facilitate inter-regional trade flows between Kashmir and the rest of the nation, Narwal Jammu serves as an intermediary nodal market and accounts for inter-state demand dynamics, price transmission behavior, and external market signals influencing cherry prices in J&K.

Study area and major markets receiving cherry produce from Jammu & Kashmir.

Regarding varietal considerations, the study focused on the cherry variety Makhmali (Guigne Noir Hative), which is widely grown across Jammu and Kashmir’s major cherry-producing belts and accounts for a significant share of both market value and trade volume. To properly capture the diversity in supply chains and customer preference structures typical of large-scale inter-state markets, the analysis in the Azadpur market included both Makhmali and Mishri (Bigarreau Noir Grossa) varieties. Three well-known commercial quality grades, namely Super, Special, and Fancy, that are primarily determined by size, color, firmness, and general aesthetic parameters, have a significant impact on price formation and consumer preferences. Therefore, they were included in the data collection based on market segmentation and price differentiation analysis.

Data collection and curation

The daily wholesale cherry price data were obtained directly from official market records maintained by the local horticultural boards and Agricultural Produce Market Committees (APMCs) in each study market. The research team made several visits to the market across different marketing seasons to ensure they had full access and coverage. They worked closely with multiple stakeholders, including market secretaries, licensed traders, commission agents, and auctioneers, to collect data. The collected data was digitized and exported to Microsoft Excel spreadsheets for analysis. Moreover, during data handling, certain discrepancies have been observed, primarily due to variation in reporting formats across APMCs and the lack of records from the off-season. To address these problems, data preprocessing was performed. This ensured that model training and evaluation used only consistent, high-quality data. For the authenticity and data accuracy, a double-entry validation protocol was used. Any differences during the process were cross-checked with the original registered data. Also, random spot checks were conducted to reduce transcription and encoding errors. Though due care and caution were taken in handling and analyzing the data, the possibility of bias may still exist in the collected data beyond the authors’ control.

Data architecture: temporal scope and sale period segmentation

The data used in this study included daily cherry produce price observations gathered from the chosen markets over a range of time periods, contingent on the commercial significance of the cherry trade in each area and the accessibility of official records. Historical records span 2015–2024 for Makhmali and 2012–2024 for Mishri at Azadpur Market, 2019–2024 for Ganderbal, Parimpore Srinagar, and Aglar Shopian in the Kashmir valley, and 2016–2024 for Narwal Jammu. These differences in temporal coverage were due to the registers not being consistently archived across markets, and only the years for which files were accessible during field visits had their datasets digitised. Real-time forecasting for the 2025 marketing season was conducted after models were developed and validated using these historical datasets, enabling a prospective comparison of model performance against observed market prices.

The daily frequency at which all datasets were recorded reflects the level of detail at which cherry prices are reported and exchanged in wholesale markets throughout the marketing season. The analysis was explicitly limited to the specified sale periods of each year, which corresponded to active cherry trading in the respective markets due to the highly seasonal nature of cherry cultivation and market activity. The agro-climatic factors affecting cherry ripening, harvest schedules, and market arrivals were taken into consideration when determining these sale times. Specifically, the Mishri variety was sold later in the season (10 June–15 July in Azadpur) than Makhmali (20 May–30 June in Azadpur; May–June windows in valley markets). As an inter-state nodal hub, Narwal (Jammu) had the longest trading window among regional markets (15 May–10 July). Data engineering and transformation pipeline

To ensure accuracy, consistency, and statistical validity, datasets were methodically pre-processed before model training. Occasionally, the resulting daily time series contained missing entries, transcription errors, and scale inconsistencies because the original market registers were kept on paper before digitisation. Thus, preprocessing was developed to enhance the numerical stability of machine learning and deep learning architectures (e.g., gradient-based training scaling) and to meet the statistical assumptions of traditional time-series models (e.g., stationarity for SARIMA). Imputation of missing values, stationarity diagnostics and differencing when required, and normalisation of inputs for neural models comprised the three primary steps of the preprocessing pipeline.

Missing-value imputation

A systematic three-step imputation method was used to resolve these discrepancies. The forward-fill (ffill) method was initially used to fill in missing values by carrying forward the most recent valid observation. A backward-fill (bfill) was used wherever a forward-fill was not feasible, such as when a missing entry occurred at the start of the series. Forecasting models and theoretical formulations

Modeling was performed using a variety of techniques, from statistical baselines to advanced DL architectures, to predict cherry prices across selected markets with high accuracy. A realistic depiction of price discovery conditions was enabled by training the models on active trading windows determined by empirical sale periods. A crucial part of the study was the modeling phase, which allowed for both prospective real-time deployment and retrospective validation.

Seasonal autoregressive integrated moving average (SARIMA)

An extension of the ARIMA model, SARIMA accounts for both seasonal and non-seasonal components in time series data, making it especially well-suited to capturing strong intra-annual seasonality. All models used in the study underwent the same training, validation, and test procedures to ensure comparability and reproducibility. The 2024 data were set aside for testing, while the training set included all observations for each market-variety-grade series from the beginning of the historical sales period through the penultimate year available, i.e., 2023 in the present case. The final 10% of observations in each training series was set aside as a validation part for early stopping and model selection. By avoiding look-ahead bias and mirroring the intended real-time forecasting scenario, this design ensured that the split points reflected the actual marketing windows for each variety and market.

To prevent over-tuning and ensure a fair comparison, model-specific settings were deliberately kept light and symmetrical. We ran a compact grid over (p,d,q,P,D,Q) {0,1} for SARIMA, setting the seasonal period “s” to the dominant within-season periodicity inferred from the ACF of each series (daily data usually yielded a weekly cycle, s≈7). AIC on the training window selected the final specification, and no data from the test window was used. Prophet only trained during the training window and used its default additive trend/seasonality with automatic changepoints (yearly and weekly components enabled). To control variance, we constructed univariate lag features for tree ensembles and capped the look-back horizon; XGBoost and RF used up to 60 lags. Simple forward validation on the held-out validation slice was used to select the number of lags (minimizing validation MSE); the other hyperparameters (RF n_estimators = 100, XGB n_estimators = 100, learning_rate = 0.1, max_depth = 3, subsample = 1.0) were set to library defaults. The input sequence length was selected from {3, 4, 5} based on validation loss, and neural models were trained on standardized targets (StandardScaler fit on the training set). The LSTM employed batch size 32, a single hidden layer of about 100 units with Adam, up to 100 epochs, and early stopping (patience = 10, best weights restored). With embedding dim = 32, feed-forward dim = 128, four encoder layers, Adam at 1e-3, up to 100 epochs, and the same early-stopping protocol, the Transformer was a lightweight encoder. All models were trained on identically masked sale-period days, with identical chronological train/validation/test splits that matched the actual season of each market variety. No exogenous variables were used. Conventional k-fold cross-validation was not applied, as it is unsuitable for autocorrelated time-series data. Instead, chronological splits aligned with market-specific sale windows were used, with a final validation slice within the training set for model selection and early stopping.

To ensure the results can be reproduced, random seeds were set where libraries allow (e.g., sklearn random_state = 42; similar seeds for NumPy, TensorFlow, and PyTorch). The computational environment used Python 3.10.16 (Anaconda distribution) on Windows 10 (64-bit, Intel i7 CPU), with core libraries including NumPy 2.1.3, pandas 2.3.0, scikit-learn 1.7.0, statsmodels 0.14.5, Prophet 1.1.6, XGBoost 3.0.4, TensorFlow 2.19.0/Keras 2.10.0, and PyTorch 2.3.1. We used Matplotlib 3.10.3 and Seaborn 0.13.2 to create the visualisations.

Real-time forecasting deployment architecture

The model chosen for operational deployment was the one that performed the best overall across test datasets, as demonstrated by the lowest error scores (MAE, RMSE, and MSE), the highest directional accuracy, and statistically superior results verified by the DM test. The chosen model was incorporated into a lightweight web service architecture to ensure the forecasting system’s real-time usability, scalability, and accessibility. The completed model was loaded into a Python-based FastAPI backend after being serialized in Keras format, primarily because of its asynchronous, high-performance features, which make it ideal for handling multiple API calls. Programmatic access to forecasts based on user-specified market, fruit, variety, grade, and date-range inputs is enabled by the API’s exposure of forecasting services via HTTP endpoints. To ensure continuous availability and automatic scaling, this FastAPI application was containerized and deployed to Render, a fully managed cloud platform. Agile development and smooth forecasting logic updates were made possible by Render’s integration with Git-based CI/CD pipelines and its capacity to manage web services without incurring server overheads.

JavaScript-based frontend components were used to integrate the deployed API into the official Market Intelligence Cell (MIC) website so that users and stakeholders could access the forecasts. Results and discussion

Descriptive statistics and price variability

The descriptive analysis of cherry prices across five major wholesale markets in Table 2 reveals significant inter-market, inter-varietal, and intra-varietal (grade-wise) differences on both mean prices and price volatility. The highest mean prices were consistently observed at Azadpur market, Asia’s largest wholesale fruit hub and a significant re-export location. Misri cherries were significantly more expensive, the Super grade averaged at ₹179.55/kg (± ₹80.52), while Makhmali Super grade was sold at ₹133.02/kg (± ₹51.37). Nonetheless, Misri Super’s coefficient of variation (C.V.) of 44.85% also shows significant price volatility, indicating sensitivity to supply and quality variations at Azadpur43.

This implies that while higher grades maintain more stable pricing, particularly in upstream markets like Azadpur and Ganderbal, lower grades are more vulnerable to the market-induced volatility. Similar observations have been reported in China54 on apple and grape markets, where both showed significant price fluctuations over a decade of data, with grapes in particular exhibiting a high-risk, high-return volatility profile compared to the more stable apple prices. Typical fruit price volatility can be on the order of 20–25% C.V. in well-supplied markets55, so the 40–50% C.V. observed for lower-grade cherries in some markets here signals exceptionally high instability. The fact that the most central market (Azadpur) and the top grades show lower relative variability is consistent with the idea that broader market access and better-quality control help stabilize prices. In contrast, peripheral markets and lower grades face disproportionately larger swings53,56.

Temporal dynamics and seasonal trends

The intra-seasonal swings and grade-specific stratification observed in actual trading environments are not adequately captured by descriptive statistics, despite their ability to provide helpful aggregate measures of mean prices and variability. To overcome this, we examined temporal price dynamics across markets and grades to better understand how volatility develops during the marketing season. These daily patterns are depicted in Fig. 10, which emphasises sharp intra-seasonal fluctuations, especially in peripheral markets, as well as the durability of grade-wise price tiers. A study on citrus fruits in Nepal found that prices are lowest right after harvest and then rise to a peak before the next harvest, reflecting strong seasonal production patterns57. The pricing of the Makhmali vs. Misri varieties in Azadpur Market was evidently tiered, with Super grades regularly fetching the highest prices, followed by Special and Fancy. With Super grade prices peaking above ₹350/kg during peak trading periods, the Misri variety in particular showed noticeable price spikes at several intervals, suggesting periods of high demand and potentially reduced supply from origin markets. Price trends in Ganderbal and Parimpore markets were comparatively smoother, with the former showing less volatility and a more distinct division between grades over the trading period. Compared with the Azadpur market, Ganderbal’s Super grade prices maintained a narrower variance, occasionally reaching ₹200/kg.

Time-series trends of average cherry prices (₹/kg) across multiple markets for different grades.

Market-specific differences in volatility observed in cherry trading are likewise reported in broader agricultural markets. In general, large terminal markets tend to have more stable prices, whereas local or aggregation markets experience sharper fluctuations. A multi-market analysis of Indian apple trade revealed that price variation was lowest in Delhi’s Azadpur terminal market (approx. 6%) and much higher in a producing-area market like Shimla/Dhalli (approx. 17%)58. Delhi’s market stability was attributed to its central hub status for the arrival of fruits from different states and for imports. In contrast, primary assembly markets with fewer supply sources experience frequent shocks and short-term price jumps, as in the Shopian and Narwal markets, where prices rise and fall when local harvest volume fluctuates. These differences indicate that larger, well-integrated markets can better absorb supply shocks, while smaller regional markets are more susceptible to local supply–demand imbalances, corroborating the role of demand elasticity and market integration in price stability.

DL models, particularly LSTM networks, have repeatedly achieved superior accuracy across diverse forecasting datasets69,70,72. Building on this foundation, the attention mechanism in Transformers effectively captured complex cherry price patterns even under high variability25. In volatile scenarios, some studies have even augmented DL models with regime-switching techniques (e.g., Hidden Markov Models) to bolster forecasting stability73, further underscoring the importance of resilience in such contexts. By contrast, the traditional statistical models, viz., SARIMA and Prophet, showed higher error margins and lower DA, especially for the higher-grade cherries. This result is in line with prior findings that traditional-based methods struggle with complex or non-stationary series and that Prophet, while helpful in capturing seasonality, can lag in accuracy when abrupt short-term fluctuations dominate74.

Essentially, without extensive tuning or stationarity assumptions, these older-generation models cannot fully accommodate the intricate patterns in the cherry price data. Similarly, the tree-based ML models (RF and XGBoost) exhibited limitations in modeling the temporal dependencies inherent in price series. Their performance was somewhat inconsistent, reasonable in less volatile conditions, but deteriorating in highly dynamic market regimes. Indeed, studies have noted that XGBoost and similar models often rely on manual feature engineering to incorporate temporal signals and still fall short of recurrent neural networks in chaotic settings70. The radar charts strongly support the choice of DL architectures as the most trustworthy tools for cherry price forecasting under a range of market conditions. These models not only keep bias low (as seen in near-zero MFE and NMBE) but also maintain high directional reliability, which is crucial for stakeholders. The results here align with the broader consensus in recent forecasting literature, DL models, especially LSTM networks, offer the most reliable and robust performance for commodity price prediction across volatile environments75.

To summarize performance trends across all markets & grades and ensure they match the radar plots, two small & useful views were added: a Sunburst & a PCA plot. The former (Fig. 15) shows RMSE hierarchically, i.e., Market → Variety → Grade → Model. This makes it easy to see how accurately the forecasts were across the whole experimental design. The PCA biplot (Fig. 16) shows the six standardized metrics (MAE, RMSE, sMAPE, DA, MFE, NMBE) on two main components, i.e., PC1 & PC2. This gives a joint view of the overall error magnitude (PC1) and the error directionality/bias structure (PC2). Both visuals back up the quantitative model performance metrics with LSTM, making the most compact & consistent cluster. Transformers are competitive but show a little more variability in directional metrics and in classical (SARIMA, Prophet). At the same time, tree-based ensembles (RF, XGB) are spread out across the PCA space, suggesting mixed and less reliable performance profiles. These complementary summaries help confirm that the benefits of DL models are not just due to individual metrics but hold across different types of errors and market-grade situations.

These insights support the broader findings emerging from the time-series forecasting literature where state-of-the-art DL models provide the most reliable and robust performance for commodity market forecasting across a variety of unstable, real-world conditions68–70,72,74. The evidence from our results and comparative analysis strongly suggests that stakeholders seeking accurate and dependable price forecasts in cherry markets (and similar agricultural domains) will benefit significantly from leveraging LSTM-based approaches, potentially augmented with hybrid techniques, rather than relying solely on traditional time-series models. Each model has its place, but when it comes to capturing the intricate volatility of seasonal trends, shocks, and non-linear growth in these markets, DL significantly dominates.

Out-of-sample forecasting and real-time validation

For live deployment, the study operationalized the best-performing model (i.e., LSTM) into a lightweight forecasting pipeline tailored to sale-day windows. The deployed architecture used Min–Max scaled targets with a sequence length “L” chosen from [10, 60] via internal validation using MSE. The final model comprised LSTM (100, return_sequences = True) → Dropout(0.2) → LSTM (50) → Dropout(0.2) → Dense (1), optimized with Adam (MSE loss) and early stopping (patience = 5, restore_best_weights = True). Recursive multi-step forecasts were generated for a 15-day horizon by advancing the input window. For dashboard communication, simple heuristic bands (± 1.96 × forecast-path s.d.) were displayed alongside point forecasts. All runs were restricted to achieve sale days, and extensive logging enabled traceability in deployment.

The best-performing model selected during the validation phase was operationalized by deploying a live web-based forecasting system before the 2025 cherry marketing season. Through a secure online interface, market functionaries from designated markets in Delhi and Jammu & Kashmir actively used the system to enter daily price data (see Fig. 17). To generate daily forecasts under realistic, field-level conditions, these real-time entries served as truly unseen, out-of-sample inputs. An end-to-end operational pipeline was completed with forecasts generated automatically and made available via an interactive dashboard. To evaluate the model’s generalization performance at the end of the season, predicted prices were compared with actual recorded prices in reverse order. The implemented system provided a scalable model for AI-integrated agri-market intelligence solutions, achieving consistently high predictive accuracy across markets, varieties, and grades. Such practical integration of AI in agriculture is being pursued globally, as researchers advocate the development of free LSTM-driven price forecasting platforms for small producers to support real-time decision-making76.

Overview of the deployed smart forecasting system interface, illustrating the data entry module, market database dashboard, model selection interface, and forecast output.

The forecasting system remained operationally strong during the cherry marketing season (15 May to 15 July), recording 348 valid entries across 51 active days, for an effective uptime of about 82.3% (Table 6). The system worked well with low latency; the forecasting API responded in about 250 ms, and the supporting endpoints (/preview-data, /latest-prices, /confirm-data) returned results in 5 to 200 ms. Logs showed that core endpoints were 100% reliable and that the dashboard was consistently available, demonstrating that the system was technically stable during real-time deployment. This quick response time is fine for web services; if it takes longer than 300 ms, users might stop using the service, but response times of less than a second keep the experience smooth77. This level of availability meets or exceeds the usual high-reliability goals, which are often 99.9% uptime for important systems78. On the adoption side, officials from selected markets submitted data daily. There were between 1 and 100 entries in each peripheral market, with 87 in Zazna-Ganderbal and 100 in Aglar-Shopian. This ensured that both major production areas and terminal markets were represented, as cherry farming is done chiefly in districts like Ganderbal (which grows about 60% of Kashmir’s cherries) and Shopian79,80. The platform was technically stable in real time and had sufficient performance headroom to handle regular use without issues, as evidenced by high uptime and quick responses. Figure 18 shows the number of forecasting submissions each day during the harvest period, along with a 7-day smoothed trend line that helps show the system’s stability and usefulness. The figure shows that data steadily came in during the cherry marketing season (mid-May to mid-July), and activity then continued for other goods afterward. The continued submission patterns show that institutions adopted the system and that the platform stayed active and reliable after the cherry season.

Table 7 summarizes the performance of the deployed LSTM model and shows clear stratification across market-variety-grade combinations, with consistently low error metrics and high directional accuracy. The model achieved 95.8% accuracy, a remarkably low sMAPE of 4.31%, and minimized MAE and RMSE of 6.28 and 9.45, respectively, in the Azadpur market for Makhmali Super cherries. Exceptionally, LSTM applications in other crop markets reported percentage errors in the high single digits to low teens5, underscoring the exceptional precision of the 4.31% sMAPE obtained in the present study. Similarly, the forecasts in the Narwal Fancy and Shopian Special grades demonstrated strong agreement with observed trends, registering low bias (NMBE under 4%) and high model accuracy (92.8% and 93.95%), confirming the robustness and dependability of the deployed model in the pragmatic settings. This level of robustness aligns with global findings that DL methods can handle nonlinear market fluctuations and maintain high accuracy82, confirming the model’s reliability.

The Shopian Fancy case is an intriguing exception. However, its MFE and NMBE show a propensity to overestimate, with values of 16.37 and 28.41, respectively. Its RMSE and MAE are high (17.07 and 16.37, respectively), indicating volatility in the raw error values. Other studies have addressed similar issues by augmenting LSTM models with statistical or decomposition techniques to capture abrupt swings, for instance, combining LSTM with ARIMA for linear trends83,84 or applying seasonal-trend decomposition (STL) before LSTM to handle seasonal spikes5. These approaches could potentially mitigate the overshooting observed in Shopian Fancy. Despite this, the grade maintained an accuracy of 71.58%, suggesting that the forecasting consistency in that sub-market was acceptable but not very reliable. Similarly, performance in Ganderbal varied greatly by grade. The Special grade encountered significant difficulties, with the highest sMAPE at 25.3%, suggesting potential structural deviations or increased volatility in the price signal during the test period. In contrast, the Fancy grade saw a well-balanced forecast with MAE at 5.43 and sMAPE at 7.83%. In addition to volatility, however, the relatively weaker performance in Shopian Fancy and Ganderbal Special is plausibly linked to structural deviations in the underlying market signals, such as late‐season regime shifts, thinner trading volumes, and irregular reporting patterns. These conditions likely led to data sparsity and abrupt changes that a single-regime LSTM, trained on comparatively short sequences, could not fully capture. By contrast, the Narwal and Azadpur models consistently maintained high accuracy (above 92% across multiple segments), indicating strong generalization capacity in line with market-level trends. Although the Special grade continued to function reliably, the models at Parimpora showed slightly more dispersion, with the Fancy grade accuracy falling to 89.6% due to a higher negative bias (NMBE of –10.38). In addition to confirming the chosen models’ resilience, these test results show how well they can adapt to real-time changes in crop prices across a variety of geographic areas.

The model maintained a smoother output, indicating conservative estimation in noisy conditions, a known behavior of regularized LSTM architectures trained with dropout and early stopping, even though some markets, such as Ganderbal, showed higher variance in the actual prices. The model’s ability to maintain fine-grained pricing differentiation, particularly between Super, Special, and Fancy classifications, is further supported by a comparative analysis of predictive accuracy across grades. The Bland–Altman plots (Fig. 19) provide a reliable diagnostic for agreement between the test and validation phases. With mean differences centered close to zero and the residuals primarily clustering within the 95 percent limits of agreement, there is no evidence of systemic bias during the shift from training-validation to unseen test deployment.

These findings together demonstrate that the proposed LSTM-based architecture is a strong contender for implementation in real-time agricultural market advisory systems, as it is both statistically consistent and operationally robust. Consistent with earlier reports highlighting the superior performance of deep learning models in complex forecasting tasks85,86, it is positioned as a strong decision-support tool within the larger agri-market intelligence framework due to its ability to capture both inter-grade variations and market-specific volatility under real-world conditions.

Policy implications and limitations

The results of this research have significant policy implications for the formulation of agricultural market intelligence frameworks in India, particularly for high-value perishable commodities such as cherries. DL models, especially LSTMs, have been shown to capture nonlinear price dynamics better and were successfully used in a real-time setting during the 2025 season. The study results showed that it is possible to incorporate advanced forecasting tools into current national and state-level horticultural trade initiatives in the hub and terminal markets. The proposed system aligns with Jammu and Kashmir’s Digital Agriculture Mission, the Electronic National Agricultural Market (e-NAM), and the Holistic Agriculture Development Programme (HADP). Adding real-time AI forecasting to these programs could make price intelligence a digital public good, thereby opening price discovery, reducing price volatility, and helping the Government of India achieve its long-term goal of doubling farmers’ incomes. The study provides data-driven evidence that precise price forecasting enables farmers, traders, and policymakers to make proactive, informed decisions, increasing transparency, reducing post-harvest losses, and improving market timing and inventory management. Such model architectures can identify intricate nonlinearities and provide actionable intelligence on real-time market dynamics, potentially significantly reducing price risk and supporting income stability. The results support the developed forecasting system through its usefulness and by identifying DL decision support systems as a game-changing tool for boosting market efficiency, strengthening the resilience of food systems, and promoting inclusive agricultural development, especially in data-constrained economies.

One of the most significant problems in horticultural marketing is the risk posed by price volatility, which often leaves smallholders unaware of the value of a season’s harvest. The system tested in the study provided stakeholders with insights to mitigate this risk and help them make effective interventions based on short-term & accurate forecasts. Also, forecasting can inform the timing and scale of the Market Intervention Scheme (MIS), when to release funds from the Price Stabilisation Fund (PSF), and when to provide targeted subsidies for cold storage and transport in case of a harvest glut. Through this novel initiative, the state can shift from reactive crisis management to proactive crisis management, stabilizing incomes and lessening market shocks. This system can also be essential for improving market governance and openness. Information asymmetry remains a significant factor in Indian agricultural markets. Farmers usually accept the price, while traders and middlemen have substantial power to negotiate. Putting forecasting outputs into APMCs, horticulture directorates, and NABARD- or FPO-led platforms can make daily price information available to everyone, helping farmers make smart decisions about when and where to sell their produce. The framework’s ability to grow provides policy designers with more options during any crisis.

Moreover, the architecture can easily be applied to other high-value perishable goods, such as apples, plums, pears, and vegetables, which are subject to volatility, perishability, and broken marketing channels. By connecting forecast dashboards to state market networks, farmer advisory apps like Kisan Sarathi, and new digital infrastructures like AgriStack, AI-enabled intelligence can help farmers and traders make sound decisions daily. Also, this system can be replicated and scaled up in other states across different agricultural commodities, thus creating a national system of innovative data-driven agricultural marketing structure. These systems can also be an essential source of information for policy analysts seeking to address climatic variations that accompany market volatility. This integration would make supply chains more resilient in the face of real-world challenges.

However, these advantages can be leveraged only when institutional mechanisms adopt AI-based forecasting from the grassroots level to the state level, and ultimately to export stages. Public–Private Partnerships (PPPs) may be encouraged to use such developed platforms to improve their purchasing and shipping plans. This kind of institutional convergence would ensure that AI-powered forecasts aren’t just research prototypes but also have industrial and commercial applications. This system can also be advantageous for integrating smallholders, tribal growers, and women farmers in remote areas into extended value chains and for providing them with timely information about forecasts through easy-to-use channels such as SMS alerts, IVR systems, or community-based digital kiosks. Through these pragmatic uses of the developed models, the multiple stakeholders, especially policymakers, can promote inclusive growth and development across the various landholdings, regions, and markets. Besides, it will also help bridge the digital divide and social gaps in India, especially in the areas with resource constraints. The developed system provides a robust Data Driven Intelligence System (DDIS) to mitigate price volatility, stabilize farmer incomes, enhance market transparency, foster resilience to external shocks, and promote inclusive development.

Even with these strengths, some weaknesses limit the models’ generalizability & scalability. The current implementation used only endogenous price histories and did not include any external factors, such as weather shocks, arrival data, transport bottlenecks, or policy changes. This omission limits the model’s capacity to predict structural breaks or sudden shocks that frequently induce volatility in fresh produce markets. The system was successfully tested with officials in selected markets and needs to be tested at the farm-gate or grassroots level. DL models may not be widely adopted in low-resource or rural contexts due to their computational complexity and resource intensity. Interpretability and trust among grassroots stakeholders are also hampered by the risk of overfitting and by a lack of transparency into model behavior. The models may not be as applicable to other commodities or regions without retraining, as the training dataset was geographically and temporally confined to the J&K and Delhi markets, despite its high intra-seasonal resolution. Finally, to increase robustness across variables, hybrid ensemble approaches and more detailed, cross-sectoral data integration are required, as evidenced by inconsistent performance across some grades.

Conclusion

This study illustrated the novel real-time implementation of DL models for forecasting in horticultural commodities in India, thereby bridging a significant gap in the literature concerning high-value & perishable crops. The research systematically benchmarks six models, i.e., SARIMA, Prophet, Random Forest, XGBoost, LSTM, and Transformer, across five major markets, demonstrating that deep learning approaches, especially LSTM and Transformer, consistently surpass traditional statistical and tree-based methods. These models maintained their market-variety-grade-wise fidelity and high predictive accuracy, even when markets were unstable and competing commodities were present. The distinctive contribution lies in implementing the LSTM model within a live online system during the 2025 cherry season, enabling real-time out-of-sample forecasting based on daily market submissions. Comparative validation confirmed with temporal consistency, low bias drift, and robust adaptation to intra-seasonal volatility, highlighting the model’s practical reliability. The analysis also showed how grade differentiation and market structure affect price changes, supporting the economic rationale for market-variety, grade-based stratification. The system provides stakeholders, such as farmers, traders, and policymakers, with a novel, tested tool for making real-time decisions. The study developed a credible framework for integrating AI-based forecasting into India’s agricultural marketing system by assessing its robustness through several statistical tests and visual diagnostics. This method is a scalable plan for bringing AI-enabled market intelligence to other high-value crops in the future. This will help keep income stable, markets more transparent, and markets more resilient, especially for perishable goods.
