Abstract
. We prove that if two algebraic ( n − 1 ) (n-1) -dimensional cones P , R ⊂ ℂ n P,R\subset\mathbb{C}^{n} are ambient homeomorphic, then their bases B P B_{P} and B R B_{R} have the same Euler characteristic. As an application, we show our main result: if ( X , 0 ) , ( Y , 0 ) ⊂ ( ℂ n , 0 ) (X,0),(Y,0)\subset(\mathbb{C}^{n},0) are germs of analytic hypersurfaces, which are ambient bi-Lipschitz equivalent and m 0 ​ ( X ) = 2 m_{0}(X)=2 , then also m 0 ​ ( Y ) = 2 . m_{0}(Y)=2. . As another application, we also obtain a complete positive answer to the Metric Arnold-Vassiliev Problem, which says that if two holomorphic functions have zeros that are ambient bi-Lipschitz invariant, then the Hessian matrices of the functions have the same rank.

1.
Introduction

The following conjecture is a metric version of the famous Zariski multiplicity problem (see
[
23
]
):

Conjecture A.

Let
X,Y\subset\mathbb{C}^{n}
be two complex analytic hypersurfaces . If their germs at zero are ambient bi-Lipschitz homeomorphic, then their multiplicities
m(X,0)
and
m(Y,0)
are equal.

This conjecture is still open. In
[
7
]
we have proved that this conjecture follows from the following metric version of Zariski problem B (see
[
23
]
):

Conjecture B.

Let
C(X),C(Y)\subset\mathbb{C}^{n}
be two
n-1
dimensional algebraic cones with bases
X,Y.
If there is a bi-Lipschitz homeomorphism

\varphi\colon(\mathbb{C}^{n},C(X),0)\to(\mathbb{C}^{n},C(Y),0),

then
X
is homeomorphic to
Y.

In
[
8
]
, we answered the above question partially: we show that if two algebraic
n-1
dimensional cones
C(X),C(Y)\subset\mathbb{C}^{n}
with isolated singularity are homeomorphic, then they have the same degree and, in particular,
X
is homeomorphic to
Y.

We see that homeomorphic cones of dimension
n
in
\mathbb{C}^{n+1}
with smooth bases have homeomorphic bases. If bases are non-smooth, the problem whether bases are homeomorphic is a difficult open problem which implies the metric version of the Zariski multiplicity Conjecture (see
[
7
]
). However, in the general case, we can prove the following result which slightly confirms a positive answer to this problem:

Theorem
2.4
.

Let
P=C(X),Q=C(Y)\subset\mathbb{C}^{n+1}
be
n
dimensional algebraic cones, which are ambient homeomorphic. Then
\chi(X)=\chi(Y).

As an application, we show the main result of this paper: we show that Conjecture A is true for multiplicity two, that is, if
(X,0),(Y,0)\subset(\mathbb{C}^{n},0)
are germs of analytic hypersurfaces, which are ambient bi-Lipschitz equivalent and
m_{0}(X)=2
, then also
m_{0}(Y)=2
(see Theorem
3.1
).
In particular, this implies that Conjecture B is true if the degree of
C(X)
is
2.
(see Lemma
3.2
).

We give also an application of our results to the Arnold-Vassiliev problem about topological invariance of the rank of the Hessian of a holomorphic function. To be more precise, let
f\colon(\mathbb{C}^{n},0)\to(\mathbb{C},0)
be the germ of a holomorphic function at the origin. Let
(V(f),0)
be the germ of the zero set of
f
at the origin. We recall the
corank
of a germ of a holomorphic function
f\colon(\mathbb{C}^{n},0)\to(\mathbb{C},0)
the corank of the Hessian matrix
f
at
0
. We denote the corank of
f
by
{\rm crk}(f)
. The
rank
of
f
is
{\rm rk}(f)=n-{\rm crk}(f)
.

In 1975 ((cf.
[
1
, p. 284]
)), Vassiliev asked if the corank of a holomorphic function is a topological invariant. This problem was explicitly stated by Arnold in his book (see
[
1
, Problem 1975-14]
), this is why here we call this problem as
Arnold-Vassiliev’s problem
. More precisely, we have the following problem:

Problem 1.1

(Arnold-Vassiliev’s problem)
.

Let
f,g\colon(\mathbb{C}^{n},0)\to(\mathbb{C},0)
be germs of holomorphic functions with isolated singularities. If there is a homeomorphism
\varphi\colon(\mathbb{C}^{n},0)\to(\mathbb{C}^{n},0)
such that
\varphi(V(f))=V(g)
, then is it true that
{\rm crk}(f)={\rm crk}(g)
?

This problem was positively answered in
[
17
]
with the additional hypotheses that the homeomorphism
\varphi\colon(\mathbb{C}^{n},0)\to(\mathbb{C}^{n},0)
is bi-Lipschitz and satisfies
f=g\circ\varphi
. So, it is natural to ask the following.

Problem 1.2

(Metric Arnold-Vassiliev’s problem)
.

Let
f,g\colon(\mathbb{C}^{n},0)\to(\mathbb{C},0)
be germs of holomorphic functions with isolated singularities. If there is a bi-Lipschitz homeomorphism
\varphi\colon(\mathbb{C}^{n},0)\to(\mathbb{C}^{n},0)
such that
\varphi(V(f))=V(g)
, then is it true that
{\rm crk}(f)={\rm crk}(g)
?

In this paper, we give a complete positive answer to Problem
1.2
. Indeed, we give the following more general answer, where we do not require that the involved holomorphic functions have isolated singularities.

Theorem
4.1
.

Let
f,g\colon(\mathbb{C}^{n},0)\to(\mathbb{C},0)
be two reduced holomorphic functions which are singular at the origin. Assume that there is a bi-Lipschitz homeomorphism
\phi\colon(\mathbb{C}^{n},0)\to(\mathbb{C}^{n},0)
that sends the zeros of
f
onto the zeros of
g
. Then
f
and
g
have the same corank.

2.
Algebraic Cones

We start with a definition:

Definition 2.1
.

Let
X\subset\mathbb{CP}^{n}
be an algebraic variety. We assume
\mathbb{CP}^{n}
to be a hyperplane at infinity of
\mathbb{CP}^{n+1}
. Then by an algebraic cone
\overline{C(X)}\subset\mathbb{CP}^{n+1}
with base
X
we mean the set

\overline{C(X)}=\bigcup_{x\in X}\overline{O,x},

where
O
is the center of coordinates in
\mathbb{C}^{n+1}\subset\mathbb{CP}^{n+1}
, and
\overline{O,x}
means the projective line which goes through
O
and
x.
By an affine cone
C(X)
we mean
\overline{C(X)}\setminus X.

By the link of
C(X)
we mean the set
L=\{x\in C(X):||x||=1\}.

The following theorem is well-known (see e.g.,
[
13
]
)

Theorem 2.2
.

Let
V\subset\mathbb{C}\mathbb{P}^{n+1}
be a smooth algebraic hypersurface with degree
d
. Then the integral (co)homology of V is torsion free, and the corresponding Betti numbers are given as follows:

(1)
b_{i}(V)=0
for
i\not=n\ odd\ or\ i\not\in[0,2n].

(2)
b_{i}(V)=1
for
i\not=n
even and
i\in[0,2n].

(3)
b_{n}(V)=\frac{(d-1)^{n+2}+(-1)^{n+1}}{d}+\frac{3(-1)^{n}+1}{2}.

The following result was proved in
[
8
]
.

Theorem 2.3
.

Let
P=C(X),R=C(Y)\subset\mathbb{C}^{n+2}
be two algebraic
n+1
dimensional cones with smooth bases
X,Y.
If
P,R
are homeomorphic, then
{\rm deg}\ P={\rm deg}\ R.
In particular,
X
and
Y
are also homeomorphic.

Now we are ready to prove:

Theorem 2.4
.

Let
P=C(X),Q=C(Y)\subset\mathbb{C}^{n+1}
be
n
dimensional algebraic cones, which are ambient homeomorphic. Then
\chi(X)=\chi(Y).

Proof.

Let
f=0,g=0
be reduced equation of cones
P
and
Q
respectively and let
d_{f}=mult_{0}P,d_{g}=mult_{0}g.
The global fibration given by
f,g
are equivalent to local. Hence to compute the Lefschetz number of a monodromy
h
given by fibration
f:\mathbb{C}^{n+1}\setminus P\to\mathbb{C}^{*}
we can assume that the monodromy
h
is a geometric i.e.
h_{f}(z)=2\pi i/d_{f}z
. We start with the following lemma which follows from Theorem 3.3 and Remark 3.4 in
[
12
]
:

Lemma 2.5
.

Let
f,g:\mathbb{C}^{n+1}\to\mathbb{C}
be two reduced holomorphic functions. If germs
(V(f),0)
and
(V(g),0)
are ambient homeomorphic, then the induced maps in homology of the monodromies of
f
and
g
at
0
are conjugated. In particular, the monodromies of
f
and
g
at
0
have the same Lefschetz number and
\chi(F_{f})=\chi(F_{g}).

Hence for
0<k<d_{f}
, we have
\Lambda(h_{f}^{k})=0
, where
\Lambda(h_{f}^{k})
denotes the Lefschetz number of
h_{f}^{k}.
Similarly for
0<k<d_{g}
, we have
\Lambda(h_{f}^{k})=0.

It follows from Lemma
2.5
that
\Lambda(h_{f}^{k})=\Lambda(h_{g}^{k})
for all natural
k.
We have
\Lambda(h_{f}^{d_{f}})=\chi(F_{f})
and
\Lambda(h_{g}^{d_{g}})=\chi(F_{g})
.
Hence if
\chi(F_{f})=\chi(F_{g})\not=0
we have
d_{f}=d_{g}
. Now consider a natural mapping
F_{f}\ni x\to[x]\in\mathbb{P}^{n}\setminus X
, which is a covering of degree
d_{f}.
Hence
\chi(F_{f})=d_{f}\chi(\mathbb{P}^{n}\setminus X)
. Similarly

\chi(F_{g})=d_{g}\chi(\mathbb{P}^{n}\setminus Y)
.
This means by Lemma
2.5
that
\chi(\mathbb{P}^{n}\setminus X)=\chi(\mathbb{P}^{n}\setminus Y)
. But
\chi(\mathbb{P}^{n}\setminus X)=n+1-\chi(X)
,
\chi(\mathbb{P}^{n}\setminus Y)=n+1-\chi(Y)
, hence
\chi(X)=\chi(Y).

If
\chi(F_{f})=0
, then we have by the same argument
\chi(\mathbb{P}^{n}\setminus X)=\chi(\mathbb{P}^{n}\setminus Y)=0
and
\chi(X)=\chi(Y)=n+1.

∎

3.
Hypersurfaces with multiplicity two

In this section, we prove our main result:

Theorem 3.1
.

Let
(X,0),(Y,0)\subset(\mathbb{C}^{n},0)
be germs of analytic hypersurfaces, which are ambient bi-Lipschitz equivalent. If
m_{0}(X)=2
, then also
m_{0}(Y)=2.

Let
S
be a tangent cone to
X
at
0
and
T
be a tangent cone to
Y
at
0.
Hence in some coordinates
S=\{z\in\mathbb{C}^{n}:z_{1}^{2}+...+z_{r}^{2}=0\},r\leq n.
Thus
S=S^{\prime}\times\mathbb{C}^{n-r}
, when
S^{\prime}=\{z\in\mathbb{C}^{r}:z_{1}^{2}+...+z_{r}^{2}=0\}.
In particular
Sing(S)=\{0\}\times\mathbb{C}^{n-r}.

The case
r\leq 2
follows from
[
9
, Proposition 1.6]
(see also
[
10
, Theorem 5.3]
). Hence we can assume
r\geq 3
.
Let
T
be a tangent cone at
0
to
Y.
By
[
22
]
there is a bi-Lipschitz homeomorphism
\phi:\mathbb{C}^{n}\to\mathbb{C}^{n}
such that
\phi(S)=T.

It is enough to prove the following

Lemma 3.2
.

Let
S
be an irreducible algebraic cone of degree two. Let
T
be another algebraic cone. Assume that there is ambient bi-Lipschitz mapping
\phi:(\mathbb{C}^{n},{S},0)\to(\mathbb{C}^{n},T,0).
Then
\deg\ T=2.
Moreover, we can find a linear isomorphism

L:S\to T.

Proof.

We can assume that
S=\{z\in\mathbb{C}^{n}:z_{1}^{2}+...+z_{r}^{2}=0\},3\leq r\leq n.
Assume that our Lemma is false. Let
d>2
be a minimal degree of an algebraic cone
T
such that there is a bi-Lipschitz mapping
\phi:S\to T.

Since smoothness is
a bi-Lipschitz invariant and
T
is bi-Lipschitz equivalent to
S
we have that also
Sing(T)
in some system of coordinates is equal to
\{0\}\times\mathbb{C}^{n-r}
(see
[
22
]
and
[
21
]
). Additionally, we can choose this system of coordinates so generic, that

T^{\prime}=T\cap\mathbb{C}^{r}\times\{0\}
is a cone with isolated singularity.

Now consider two points
z,w\in Sing(S).
Hence
z,w\in\{0\}\times\mathbb{C}^{n-r}
There is a translation
L:\mathbb{C}^{n}\ni x\mapsto x+w-z\in\mathbb{C}^{n}
, which is of course bi-Lipschitz and which preserves
S.
Hence
(\mathbb{C}^{n},S,z)
is bi-Lipschitz equivalent to

(\mathbb{C}^{n},S,w).
Since
(\mathbb{C}^{n},S)
is bi-Lipschitz equivalent to
(\mathbb{C}^{n},T)
we have that the same is true for
T.

Indeed, we have a family of bi-Lipschitz mappings
\psi_{v}=\phi L_{v}\phi^{-1}:\mathbb{C}^{n}\to\mathbb{C}^{n}
, where
v\in\mathbb{C}^{n}
and
L_{v}:\mathbb{C}^{n}\ni x\mapsto x+v\in\mathbb{C}^{n},
which preserves
T.

In particular, if
z\in Sing(T)
then we have the bi-Lipschitz mapping
d\psi_{\phi^{-1}(z)}\circ\phi:(\mathbb{C}^{n},T_{0}S=S)\to(\mathbb{C}^{n},T_{z}T)
(
T_{z}S,T_{z}T
denote here tangent cones at the point
z
to
S,T
appropriately and
d\psi_{\phi^{-1}(z)}
denotes the Sampaio derivation, see
[
22
]
). By our assumption, we have either deg
T_{z}=\deg\ T
or deg
T_{z}=2.
If deg
T_{z}=\deg\ T

on the dense subset of
Sing(T)
, then we have that for every point
x\in Sing(T)
we have
mult_{x}T=deg\ T,
because
mult
is lower semicontinous.

Let
\overline{T}
be a projective closure of
T.
Since
mult
is lower semicontinous we have that
mult_{z}\overline{T}=\deg T
also for
z\in\overline{Sing(T)}.
Let a linear space
W
be a part at infinity of
\overline{Sing(T)}.
Take a point
w\in W,a\in T^{\prime}
and consider a line
l=\overline{(w,a)}.
By the Bezout Theorem, we have
l\subset T.
This means that the projection
\pi:T\to T^{\prime}
along
\{0\}\times\mathbb{C}^{n-r}
(i.e. the projection with the center
W
) has
\{a\}\times\mathbb{C}^{n-r},a\in T^{\prime}
as fibers, i.e.,
T=T^{\prime}\times\mathbb{C}^{n-r}.
This means that
S^{\prime*}
and
T^{\prime*}
have the same homotopy type. Thus, as in Theorem
2.3
we have
\deg\ S=\deg\ T
- a contradiction.

Hence we can assume that on the dense subset of
Sing(T)
we have
mult=2.
Take
z\in Sing(T)=\{0\}\times\mathbb{C}^{n-r}.
Since
T_{z}T
is quadratic and bi-Lipschitz equivalent to
T
and
\mathbb{C}^{n-r}\times\{0\}\subset T_{z}T:=T_{z}
we have
Sing(T_{z})=\{0\}\times\mathbb{C}^{n-r}.
In particular for
x\in\{0\}\times\mathbb{C}^{n-r}
we have
mult_{x}T_{z}=2.
Since
mult
is lower semicontinous we have that
mult_{y}\overline{T_{z}}=2
also for
y\in\overline{Sing(T_{z})}.
Let
T_{z}^{\prime}=T_{z}\cap\mathbb{C}^{n-r}\times\{0\}.
Thus the projection
\pi:T_{z}\to T^{\prime}_{z}
along
\{0\}\times\mathbb{C}^{n-r}
has
\{a\}\times\mathbb{C}^{n-r},a\in T^{\prime}_{z}
as fibers, i.e.,
T_{z}=T^{\prime}_{z}\times\mathbb{C}^{n-r}.
Denote coordinates in
\{0\}\times\mathbb{C}^{n-r}
by
x
and coordinates in
\mathbb{C}^{r}\times\{0\}
by
t.
Hence
T_{z}
has equation

\Sigma^{r}_{i,j=1}\alpha_{ij}(x)t_{i}t_{j}=0,

where
z=(0,x).
Let
A=det[\alpha_{ij}(x)].
Note that
A
is a non-zero polynomial on a dense subset of
\{0\}\times\mathbb{C}^{n-r}
, because all tangent cones of degree two have the same rank
r={\rm rank}\ S.
Moreover,
mult\ {T_{z}}=2
on
\mathbb{C}^{n-r}\setminus\{A\}=0\}
(here we identify
\{0\}\times\mathbb{C}^{n-r}
with
\mathbb{C}^{n-r}
) and all
\alpha_{ij}
vanish on
A=0
, because the tangent cone if it is quadratic has to have the same rank as
S.
Let
A=\prod^{s}_{j=1}a_{j}^{k_{j}}
be a decomposition of
A
into irreducible factors.

Fix
i,j
and consider the polynomial
\alpha_{i,j}(x):=\alpha.
Thus

\alpha:=b(x)\prod^{s}_{j=1}a_{j}^{r_{j}},

where
GCD(b,A)=1.
We first show that
b
is a constant. Let
f
be a homogeneous reduced polynomial that describes
T.
We know that
\frac{\partial^{2}f}{\partial t_{i}\partial t_{j}}(x,0)=2\alpha
. Let us assume that
a_{1}
has a minimal degree. We can choose a generic system of coordinates so that
\frac{\partial a_{1}}{\partial x_{1}}(x,0)\not=0.
Hence on
\{a_{1}=0\}
we have

\frac{\partial^{r_{1}}f}{\partial t_{i}\partial t_{j}\partial^{r_{1}}x_{1}}(x,0)=Cb(x)\prod^{s}_{i=2}a_{i}^{r_{i}}(\frac{\partial a_{1}}{\partial x_{1}}(x,0))^{r_{j}},

where
C
is a non-zero constant. Take a point
x\in\{a_{1}=0\}
in this way that
b(x)\not=0
,
\frac{\partial a_{1}}{\partial x_{1}}(x,0)\not=0
and
a_{i}(x)\not=0
for
i>1.
Then deg
T_{(x,0)}T
has degree at most
d-(\deg\ b+\Sigma^{s}_{i=2}\deg\ r_{i}a_{i})<d.
This is a contradiction. Hence
b
is a constant. In a similar way we can prove that
s=1.
Finally, we show that
a_{1}
is a linear polynomial. In fact, we have on
\{a_{1}=0\}
:

\frac{\partial^{r_{1}+2}f}{\partial t_{i}\partial t_{j}\partial^{r_{1}}x_{1}}(x,0)=C(\frac{\partial a_{1}}{\partial x_{1}}(x,0))^{r_{1}},

where
C
is a non-zero constant. Take a point
x\in\{a_{1}=0\}
in this way that
\frac{\partial a_{1}}{\partial x_{1}}(x,0)\not=0
. Then deg
T_{(x,0)}T
has degree at most
2+r_{1}.
If
a_{1}
is not a linear polynomial, then
2+r_{1}<d
. This is a contradiction.
We can assume that
a_{1}=x_{1}.
Thus

\frac{\partial^{2}f}{\partial t_{i}\partial t_{j}}(x,0)=x_{1}^{d-2}c_{ij},

where
c_{ij}
are constants.

Now consider
\frac{\partial^{3}f}{\partial t_{i}\partial t_{j}\partial t_{k}}(x,0)=\beta.
If
d>3
we have again
\beta=0
on
x_{1}=0,
because otherwise on
\{x_{1}=0\}\subset Sing(T)
we can find a tangent cone to
T
of degree less than
d.
Similarly as above, we see that

\beta=Cx_{1}^{d-3}
where
C
is a constant. We can continue in this manner to obtain from the Taylor formula:

f(x,t)=\Sigma^{d}_{k=2}1/k!\Sigma_{i_{1},i_{2},...,i_{k}}x_{1}^{d-k}c_{i_{1},i_{2},...,i_{k}}t_{i_{1}}t_{i_{2}}...t_{i_{k}}.

On the other hand, we know that
T
is bi-Lipschitz equivalent to
S
, in particular, by Theorem
2.4
the bases of these two cones have the same Euler characteristic. This implies that also the bases of cones

\tilde{S}^{\prime}=\{(x,t)\in\mathbb{C}\times\mathbb{C}^{r}:t_{1}^{2}+...+t_{r}^{2}=0\}

and

\tilde{T}^{\prime}=\{(x,t)\in\mathbb{C}\times\mathbb{C}^{r}:\Sigma^{d}_{k=2}1/k!\Sigma_{i_{1},i_{2},...,i_{k}}x^{d-k}c_{i_{1},i_{2},...,i_{k}}t_{i_{1}}t_{i_{2}}...t_{i_{k}}=0\}

have the same Euler characteristic.

Indeed, let
B,C
be bases of the cones
\tilde{S}^{\prime},\tilde{T}^{\prime}
respectively. Consider a new cone

\mathbb{C}\times\tilde{S}^{\prime}
with base
B^{\prime}.
Then
B^{\prime}
in
\mathbb{P}^{r+1}
is a cone over
B
with a vertex
A=(1:0:...:0),
i.e., it is a line bundle over
B
plus one point. Hence,
\chi(B^{\prime})=\chi(B)+1
. By the same reason, the base
C^{\prime}
the cone
\mathbb{C}\times\tilde{T}^{\prime}
has Euler characteristic
\chi(C^{\prime})=\chi(C)+1
. Thus if the Euler characteristics of new bases coincide, then also Euler characteristics of old bases coincide. The result is obtained by induction.

Observe that bases
B
and
C
have only one singular point
(1:0:0:....:0).
Moreover the Milnor number at this point is the same at
B
and
C
and it is equal to one, because both of these singularities are quadratic singularities, i.e., they can be described as
\{z\in\mathbb{C}^{r}:z_{1}^{2}+...+z_{r}^{2}+{\it higher\ terms\ in\ z}=0\}.
Now we use the following theorem, which is an easy consequence of the Parusiński result from
[
19
]
:

Theorem 3.3
.

Let
X\subset\mathbb{C}\mathbb{P}^{n}
be of degree
d
complex projective hypersurface. Assume that
X
has only isolated singular points
x_{1},...,x_{k}
and the Milnor number of
X
at
x_{i}
is equal
\mu_{i}.
Then
the Euler characteristic of
X
is given by the formula:

\chi(X)=(n+1)-\frac{1}{d}(1+(-1)^{n}(d-1)^{n+1})+(-1)^{n}\Sigma^{k}_{i=1}\mu_{i}.

By Theorem
3.3
we have
r+1-\frac{1}{d}(1+(-1)^{r}(d-1)^{r+1})\pm 1=r+1-\frac{1}{2}(1+(-1)^{r}(2-1)^{r.+1})\pm 1.
Since the function
f(x)=\frac{(x-1)^{r+1}+(-1)^{r}}{x}
increases for
x\geq 2
, we get
d=2
, which is a contradiction.

Hence deg
T=2.
Moreover, the quadratic functions which describes
S
and
T
have the same rank equal to
n-\dim Sing(S)=n-\dim Sing(T).
This means that they are linearly equivalent, hence there is a linear isomorphism
L:\mathbb{C}^{n}\to\mathbb{C}^{n}
, such that

L(S)=T.

∎

Remark 3.4
.

In theorem
3.1
it is important that
X,Y
are hypersurfaces. Indeed, we can construct a quadratic three dimensional cone
X\subset\mathbb{C}^{7}
, which is ambient bi-Lipschitz equivalent to a non-quadratic three dimensional cone
Y\subset\mathbb{C}^{7}.
It can be easily deduced from

[
4
]
and
[
2
]
.

4.
Answer to Metric Arnold-Vassiliev’s problem

As a direct consequence, we obtain a complete positive answer to Metric Arnold-Vassiliev’s problem.

Theorem 4.1
.

Let
f,g\colon(\mathbb{C}^{n},0)\to(\mathbb{C},0)
be germs of holomorphic functions which are singular at the origin. If there is a bi-Lipschitz homeomorphism
\varphi\colon(\mathbb{C}^{n},0)\to(\mathbb{C}^{n},0)
such that
\varphi(V(f))=V(g)
, then
{\rm rk}(f)={\rm rk}(g)
.
