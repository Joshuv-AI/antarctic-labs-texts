A Novel BCL2 Translocation and Concurrent Chromosome 13q14 Deletion in Chronic Lymphocytic Leukemia: A Case Report
Authors: Riku Das, David S. Bosler, Caroline Astbury
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTDeletions at 13q14 or unbalanced translocations involving this region are the most common chromosomal abnormalities observed in B‐cell chronic lymphocytic leukemia (CLL). This locus is known to harbor the tumor suppressor microRNAs, miR‐15a/16‐1, loss of which leads to increased expression of the anti‐apoptotic protein Bcl2 at the post‐transcriptional level, ultimately promoting leukemic cell survival. In this report, we describe a novel DLEU2 ::BCL2 fusion identified by next‐generation sequencing in an individual in their 70s with CLL. The fusion preserved the BCL2 coding sequence while disrupting the DLEU2/miR‐15a/16‐1 region. RNA expression analysis demonstrated a marked increase in BCL2 transcripts. Fluorescence in situ hybridization revealed that this rearrangement was associated with both monoallelic and biallelic deletions at 13q14. These findings suggest that insertion of BCL2 into the 13q14 locus may both truncate DLEU2, thereby impairing the tumor‐suppressive function of miR‐15a/16‐1, and place BCL2 under the control of the DLEU2 promoter, contributing to increased transcriptional activity. This case highlights a potential dual mechanism of Bcl2 dysregulation in CLL and underscores the pathogenic significance of rare 13q14 translocations, further expanding the understanding of genomic complexity in this disease.Trial RegistrationThe authors have confirmed clinical trial registration is not needed for this submission.

Approximately 82% of B‐cell chronic lymphocytic leukemia (CLL), which is the most common adult leukemia, shows chromosomal abnormalities by interphase fluorescence in situ hybridization (FISH) [1]. Common cytogenetic alterations are 13q abnormalities, 11q deletions, trisomy 12, and 17p/TP53 deletions. Among these, deletion of 13q14 is the most prevalent, occurring in about 55% of cases [1], and when present as the sole cytogenetic abnormality, it is considered a favorable prognostic indicator [2].

The 13q14 locus contains a minimally deleted region (MDR) that encompasses the DLEU2 gene encoding a long non‐coding RNA and a cluster of microRNAs, namely miR‐15a and miR‐16‐1 (miR‐15a/16‐1), located within intron 4 of DLEU2 [2, 3]. MiR15a/16‐1 are transcribed from the DLEU2 promoter and function as potent tumor suppressors controlling B‐cell proliferation. Deletion of these causes CLL in vivo [4], consistent with findings that approximately 68% of CLL cases show reduced or absent miR‐15a/16‐1 expression [2].

Loss of 13q14 in CLL may arise either through interstitial deletions at 13q14 or translocations involving 13q14 breakpoints [5, 6, 7]. Because translocations involving 13q14 do not have a recurrent partner gene, their pathogenic impact is thought to stem primarily from loss of tumor suppressor function at 13q14. Whether such rearrangements may also promote proto‐oncogene activation of fused genes remains unclear. Here, we report a novel 13q14 translocation involving BCL2 gene in a case of both monoallelic and biallelic 13q14 deletions.

An individual in their 70s presented with marked thrombocytopenia, leukocytosis with absolute lymphocytosis and absolute neutropenia, as well as mild normocytic anemia. Their medical history was significant for CLL, as well as deep vein thrombosis and splenomegaly. There was no documented history of prior CLL‐directed therapy. A bone marrow biopsy showed extensive marrow involvement of persistent/recurrent CLL/small lymphocytic lymphoma with panhypoplasia of the bone marrow. Flow cytometry confirmed these findings, identifying a CD5(+)/CD19(+)CD23(+)‐positive lambda monotypic B cell population, accounting for more than 90% of the cells.

A 107‐gene hematologic neoplasm fusion next‐generation sequencing (NGS) panel was performed on the bone marrow using anchored multiplex PCR‐based target enrichment (Integrated DNA Technologies, Boulder, CO, USA), paired‐end sequencing (Illumina, San Diego, CA, USA) and a customized clinically validated bioinformatics pipeline. NGS analysis revealed a novel fusion, DLEU2::BCL2, with 14.3% supporting reads spanning the fusion junction. The fusion joined DLEU2 exon 1 to the 5' untranslated region of BCL2 exon 2, preserving the DLEU2 promoter and the entire translated region of BCL2 (Figure 1 and Figure 2A). Heat map analysis of RNA expression revealed markedly elevated BCL2 transcripts compared to a negative patient (Figure 2B). Considering this individual's history of CLL, this fusion was reported as potentially clinically significant. The individual was subsequently treated with Gazyva, which led to stabilization of CLL, although complicated by neutropenia and thrombocytopenia.

Cytogenetic analysis on the bone marrow metaphase cells showed a normal male karyotype (data not shown), with no detectable 13q14 abnormalities. Subsequently, an interphase FISH targeting the 13q14/D13S319 (orange) and the 13q34/LAMP1 telomeric (aqua) regions (Abbott, Abbott Park, IL, USA, Figure 2C) was performed. As shown in Figure 2D, four representative cells showed two aqua signals confirming the expected 13q copy number. In 65% of cells, only a single orange signal was observed (Figure 2D, upper panel), consistent with a monoallelic 13q14 deletion, while 12% of cells lacked any orange signals (Figure 2D, lower panel), indicating a biallelic deletion.

BCL2 is a key driver of CLL pathogenesis and its overexpression is associated with adverse clinical features [8]. Loss of miR‐15a/16‐1 stabilizes BCL2 mRNA transcripts, increases Bcl2 protein expression, and promotes CD5+ B‐cell survival [2]. Additional mechanisms of BCL2 upregulation are also described, including genetic amplification, epigenetic modifications, and NF‐κB signaling activation [9]. Our findings showed a novel DLEU2::BCL2 rearrangement with increased BCL2 transcript levels. Here, BCL2 overexpression may result from loss of the DLEU2/miR‐15a/16‐1 tumor suppression region, resulting in post‐transcriptional BCL2 stabilization and placement of BCL2 gene under the regulatory control of the DLEU2 promoter may result in BCL2 transcriptional upregulation.

DLEU2 promoter is active in normal B cells and maintains physiological expression of miR‐15a/16‐1, inhibiting uncontrolled proliferation [10]. This promoter lies within exon 1 of DLEU2, and deletion of this MDR (∼8.5 kb) functionally impairs miR‐15a/16‐1 activities even without genomic loss of the miR‐15a/16‐1 locus [11]. Because loss of miR‐15a/16‐1 does not impair transcriptional activity of DLEU2 [4], the preserved DLEU2 promoter in our case may constitutively drive BCL2 expression from the fusion gene, while the absence of miR‐15a/16‐1 may relieve post‐transcriptional control of the BCL2 transcript.

Breakpoints involving the 13q region are heterogeneous, often consisting of unbalanced translocations with diverse partners [12]. This genetic heterogeneity contributes to variable clinical outcomes and complicates diagnosis and treatment. In our case, loss of the 13q14 signal by FISH supports an unbalanced translocation, consistent with previously reported CLL‐associated rearrangements. Monoallelic 13q14 deletion is more common than biallelic loss in CLL [1], although both may coexist, particularly in cases with 13q translocations [13]. Whether these clones arise independently or through sequential acquisition of a second‐hit translocation remains unclear [7, 13, 14]. In our case, the biallelic deletion clone comprised 12% of cells by FISH, closely matching the 14% DLEU2::BCL2 fusion reads detected by NGS. Although the two methods measure different parameters, the concordance suggests that insertion of the BCL2 into the DLEU2 locus may contribute to the biallelic 13q14 deletion. However, involvement of the second deleted 13q14 allele remains unconfirmed and would require metaphase FISH analysis. Alternatively, the 14% of the cells harboring the DLEU2::BCL2 fusion may represent a distinct clone from the biallelic FISH‐positive population. This is because the clonal populations identified by FISH (65% monoallelic and 12% biallelic deletions) and NGS (12% fusion‐positive cells) together approximate to the overall CLL population (∼90%) detected by flow cytometry.

The 13q14 deletion detected by FISH was not identified by karyotyping, likely because the interstitial deletion was below the resolution of conventional cytogenetics (∼5 Mb). In addition, 13q14 abnormalities are frequently underdetected by karyotyping because lymphoid cells have lower mitotic activity than myeloid cells in cultures [5].

In summary, the discovery of this novel fusion contributes to the growing complexity of genomic abnormalities in CLL. This study also suggests that a gene fusion in the 13q14 region could lead to proto‐oncogenic activation of BCL2, resulting in excessive production of the anti‐apoptotic protein. Mechanistic studies are needed to fully explore this pathway of BCL2 expression in CLL.
