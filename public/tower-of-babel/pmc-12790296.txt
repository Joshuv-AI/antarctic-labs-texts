A case of left bundle branch block, prematurely interrupted
Authors: Rakesh Latchamsetty, Fred Morady
Published: 2025

Case presentation

A 75-year-old male with hypertension, dyslipidemia and coronary artery disease presents for cardiac rehabilitation. He recently received percutaneous coronary interventions with stents placed to his ostial to mid Left Circumflex artery and ostial to mid Ramus Intermedius artery. He is currently without cardiac symptoms and obtained a routine twelve lead electrocardiogram (ECG) prior to initiating his cardiac rehabilitation session (Fig. 1). His prior ECG obtained 3 months ago is also available for review (Fig. 2).

Discussion

The first three full QRS complexes of our patient's ECG show a complete left bundle branch block with a sinus cycle length of 790 ms and a PR interval of 210 ms (Fig. 3).

The next QRS complex follows a premature atrial complex and is more narrow with only a left anterior fascicular block. The exact PR interval is difficult to measure due to superimposition onto the preceding T wave but certainly appears less than 210 ms.

This complex is followed by a compensatory pause as the sinus node recovers and the remaining QRS complexes show only a left anterior fascicular block. The sinus rate during this portion of the ECG is slower than at the start with a cycle length of 850–900 ms and the PR interval remains the same at 210 ms.

Following a PAC, one would normally expect a similar or wider QRS complex as compared to the previous complex. The narrower QRS following the PAC in this ECG is suspected to be from supernormal conduction over the left posterior fascicle (blocked on the prior complexes). This phenomenon has been shown to occur when a PAC occurs preferentially just after the T wave of the preceding complex [1]. Another possibility to consider would be balanced delay following the PAC of both the right bundle branch and the left posterior fascicle; however, for this to be the case, the PR interval following the PAC should be longer than the prior PR interval, which is not the case in this example.

The conduction over the right bundle branch and left posterior fascicle in the remaining complexes in this example can most easily be explained by the slower heart rate during this portion of the ECG. The left posterior fascicle exhibits a rate related block or delay which is manifest at the faster heart rate at the start of the ECG (Fig. 4).

Two other explanations were considered. First, one can hypothesize a phase 4 block of the left posterior fascicle at the start of the ECG that is reset by the PAC [2]. What makes this unlikely is that there is a long pause following the PAC during which we would have expected the phase 4 block to recur with the remaining QRS complexes exhibiting a complete left bundle branch block. Second, we can also consider retrograde concealed conduction into the left posterior fascicle in the beginning of the tracing that perpetuates and resolves following the PAC [3]. However, in this scenario the PAC would also have been expected to exhibit a complete left bundle branch block.

We therefore conclude that the left posterior fascicle in this ECG exhibits a rate related block that resolves at the lower heart rate at the terminal portion of the ECG, and the PAC exhibits supernormal conduction with conduction enabled over the left posterior fasicle. While this situation would be considered benign, given the relatively low heart rate at which the posterior fascicle showed block, the patient may progress to a complete left bundle branch block in the future.

Editorial commentary (Brian Olshansky)

This is an interesting and provocative EKG! However, the mechanism for the change in the QRS from a LBBB to LAFB (patient’s baseline) with, and following, the PAC is speculative but unlikely due to supernormal conduction.

Supernormal conduction is rare and usually explained by some other physiologic phenomenon. Supernormal conduction is highly unlikely here. While it may explain only the single beat following the PAC, it does not explain tonic narrowing after that.

LBBB aberration could be due to a relatively faster (albeit slow) rate. The LBBB at this rate may indicate conduction system disease. The reason for the rate slowing with the change from LBBB to LAFB is uncertain but this rate-dependent change may be phase 3 block even if it does not explain the QRS following the PAC.

While the narrower QRS following the PAC remains obscure, linking with retrograde concealed conduction, broken by the early ectopic beat, could be an explanation. Linking, however, is unproven, perhaps, even unlikely.

The PR interval is shorter with the ectopic beat but the location from which it arises remains uncertain. The PR interval alone does not determine conduction times via the AV node or His Purkinje system. A gap phenomenon, however, is unlikely based on the timing of the beat.

No data show initiation of the LBBB and no data indicate repetition of the LBBB or this phenomenon.

Based on the information provided, a definitive understanding of the mechanism is not established. Supernormal conduction, even for the single QRS complex after the PAC, is not proven.
