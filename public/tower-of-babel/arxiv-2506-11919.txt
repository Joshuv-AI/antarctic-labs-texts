Effectiveness of Counter-Speech against Abusive Content: A Multidimensional Annotation and Classification Study
Greta Damo, Elena Cabrio Serena Villata

Abstract
Counter-speech (CS) is a key strategy for mitigating online Hate Speech (HS), yet defining the criteria to assess its effectiveness remains an open challenge. We propose a novel computational framework for CS effectiveness classification, grounded in social science concepts. Our framework defines six core dimensions – Clarity, Evidence, Emotional Appeal, Rebuttal, Audience Adaptation, and Fairness – which we use to annotate 4,214 CS instances from two benchmark datasets, resulting in a novel linguistic resource released to the community. In addition, we propose two classification strategies, multi-task and dependency-based, achieving strong results (0.94 and 0.96 average F1 respectively on both expert- and user-written CS), outperforming standard baselines, and revealing strong interdependence among dimensions.

Introduction

The rise of social media has intensified concerns over online Hate Speech (HS). Counter-speech (CS), non-aggressive responses that challenge hate using facts or alternative views, has emerged as a promising mitigation strategy
Schieb and Preuss 2016
;
Benesch 2014

CS is also valued for its potential to shift beliefs and reduce future hate

whereas user and LLM responses tend to be more generic and emotional

;
Tekiroğlu et al. 2022
;
Tekiroğlu et al. 2020

This study evaluates expert- and user-written CS effectiveness using two benchmark datasets

Related Work

CS, introduced as a mitigation strategy by
Susan et al. 2016
, has not yet been extensively explored from a detection standpoint. Among the studies,

Poudhar et al. 2024
find that a one-shot prompted LLM achieves promising accuracy in classifying manually labeled CS strategies.

Mathew et al. 2018
analyze HS/CS tweet pairs, using a boosting algorithm with TF-IDF and lexical features, which achieves 0.77 F1.
Mathew et al. 2019
also analyze CS and neutral comments on YouTube, finding that TF-IDF vectors combined with logistic regression achieve 0.73 F1.

Ziems et al. 2020
analyze tweets during the COVID-19 crisis, achieving 0.49 F1 for CS.
Garland et al. 2020
apply an ensemble learning algorithm to classify HS and CS tweets, achieving F1 scores between 0.76 and 0.97.
A key gap in the literature is the lack of standardized evaluation methods for assessing
CS. Several studies propose evaluation criteria, but no unified framework emerged.
Zheng et al. 2023
assess CS effectiveness through human judgments, focusing on comfort for targets and bystander empathy.
Chung et al. 2021
and
Zhu and Bhat 2021
similarly rely on human ratings based on qualities such as informativeness, coherence, and stance.
Ashida and Komachi 2022
evaluate CS suitability using offensiveness and stance, while
Jones et al. 2024
propose an LLM-based evaluation framework, grounded in NGO guidelines, which shows strong alignment with human judgments.
Yu et al. 2024
introduce a conversation-level incivility metric, arguing that CS should be judged by its downstream impact on discourse.
We advance beyond previous studies by introducing a human-annotated resource that captures fine-grained dimensions of structural and rhetorical message quality, and by developing classification methods that leverage this framework.

3
Effectiveness Metrics

We propose a framework to evaluate CS effectiveness across six dimensions: Clarity, Evidence, Emotional Appeal, Rebuttal, Audience Adaptation, and Fairness.

Clarity
ensures structural coherence and logical flow, allowing to follow texts effectively
Persing and Ng 2013
;
Wachsmuth et al. 2016
;
Wachsmuth et al. 2017
;
Stapleton and Wu 2015
.

Evidence
strengthens persuasiveness through relevant support such as statistics or expert testimony
Wachsmuth et al. 2017
;
Rinott et al. 2015
;
Rahimi et al. 2014
;
Reinard 1988
.

Emotional Appeal
, a rhetorical strategy, shows that empathy and emotion can enhance impact and share
Wachsmuth et al. 2017
;
Aristotle 2007
;
Govier 2010
;
Hangartner et al. 2021
;
Chen et al. 2022
;
Marone 2015
.

Rebuttal
reflects the ability to address and counter opposing views, improving credibility and persuasion
Wachsmuth et al. 2017
;
Aristotle 2007
;
Toulmin 1958
;
Damer 2009
;
Granger et al. 2009
;
O’Keefe 1999
;
Onoda et al. 2015
.

Audience Adaptation
involves tailoring language to the audience’s style and knowledge, improving relatability
Pickering and Garrod 2004
;
Danescu-Niculescu-Mizil et al. 2011
;
Wang et al. 2014
.

Fairness
promotes respectful tone and avoids dehumanization, supporting ethical discourse
Cialdini 1993
;
Schreier et al. 1995
.

Categorical Dimensions (1–3 Likert Scale)

Clarity

3: Clear, logically structured, directly addresses the HS topic.

2: Mostly clear or slightly generic.

1: Vague or incoherent.

Evidence

3: Multiple pieces of information as supporting evidence.

2: One supporting information.

1: No information as evidence.

Rebuttal

3: Multiple rebuttals targeting specific parts of the HS.

2: One rebuttal.

1: No rebuttal.

Fairness

3: Respectful, no swearing, no attacks against the hater.

2: Mostly polite, no attacks, mild offensive.

1: Aggressive, including swearing and personal attacks.

Binary Dimensions (0/1)

Emotional

Appeal

1: Language that evokes emotions (either positive or negative).

0: Neutral tone and language.

Audience

Adaptation

1: Matches HS tone/complexity.

0: Mismatched HS tone or complexity.

Datasets Description

We extend the annotation on two existing datasets:
CONAN

Chung et al. 2019
and the
Twitter Dataset

Albanyan and Blanco 2022
. We select CONAN as it is the first expert-curated dataset of HS/CS pairs, widely used as a benchmark. It is a multilingual (English, French, Italian) dataset centered on Islamophobia, containing 4,078 expert-annotated HS/CS pairs. Through translation and paraphrasing, it is expanded to 14,988 pairs. For our experiments, we retain only the English, non-augmented instances, resulting in 3,847 pairs. The Twitter Dataset
2

2

contains 5,652 hateful tweets and replies from social media (Twitter/X), capturing the brevity and style typical of online discourse
Bonaldi et al. 2024
. We focus on the subset labeled as CS, where a clear target is identifiable, yielding 367 HS/CS pairs.
Together, these datasets form a combined benchmark of 4,214 HS/CS pairs.

We define annotation guidelines to label CS effectiveness dimensions.
To consolidate these guidelines, a pilot study was conducted on 50 HS/CS pairs from each dataset, involving three annotators

5
Experimental Setting

We use BERT-based classifiers to predict CS effectiveness scores across the six dimensions, relying on
bert-base-uncased

Devlin et al. 2018
trained on the combined dataset of 4,214 HS/CS pairs, and cross-dataset evaluations.
We use BERT as the sole model to focus on improving it through our framework. By not comparing across different architectures, we isolate the effect of our multi-task and dependency modeling, ensuring that observed gains result from our contributions. We assess the following configurations (Appendix
B
for details):

Bert_CS
: CS text embeddings are used as input. Binary dimensions are trained using Binary Cross-Entropy (BCE) loss function, and categorical ones with Cross-Entropy (CE).

Bert_CS_HS
: identical to Bert_CS, but the input is the concatenation of HS and CS texts embeddings for more context.

Multi-task_divided
trains binary dimensions jointly (BCE for Emotional Appeal, Focal Loss for Audience Adaptation) and categorical ones separately (CE), assuming partial correlation.

Multi-task_united
combines all dimensions in a single model using the sum of their respective loss functions, assuming stronger interdependence.

Dependency_matrix_3e
introduces a learnable matrix to capture pairwise relationships between dimensions, and is trained for 3 epochs, while
Dependency_matrix_6e
for 6 epochs to assess the effect of extended training.

Cross-Domain Generalization.

In the CONAN
\rightarrow
Twitter setting,
all models show a marked performance drop, with Dependency_m._6e performing best (0.66) alongside Dependency_m._3e and Multi-task_u.
Emotional Appeal declines the most, suggesting a domain shift in how emotional content is conveyed. Clarity, Fairness, and Rebuttal scores are also low, reflecting the less structured nature of user-written CS.
In the reverse setting, Twitter
\rightarrow
CONAN, results are similar. Multi-task_d. achieves the highest avg. F1 score (0.66), followed closely by Dependency_m._6e (0.65). The remaining models obtain lower average F1 scores, indicating less robust generalization in this transfer setup. Models trained on expert-written CS
seem more adaptable, but Emotional Appeal remains challenging across domains, ranging from 0.14 to 0.55. In contrast, Audience Adaptation remains consistently high.
In-Domain Evaluation.
Training and testing on the same dataset (Appendix

) confirm that dependency-based models consistently perform best,
further supporting the benefit of modeling interrelations between dimensions in classification.

7
Conclusion

As a first contribution of this paper, we release a novel linguistic resource that merges the CONAN and Twitter datasets for CS, manually annotated with six dimensions of CS effectiveness. As a second contribution, we present a framework for automatically classifying CS effectiveness across these dimensions. Results show that the dependency-based model outperforms BERT baselines with 0.96 F1 on both expert- and user-written CS. Multi-task models also surpass BERT with 0.94 F1, confirming that effectiveness dimensions are interdependent and benefit from explicit dependency modeling.
Our architecture-agnostic framework improves BERT and generalizes to other transformers.
A deep linguistic analysis highlights which dimensions need improvement for CS evaluation.

8
Limitation

Our framework for assessing CS effectiveness dimensions focuses on key aspects such as structural coherence, linguistic strategies, content strength, and rhetorical impact. Given the vast body of literature on counter-speech in social sciences, we focused on the most relevant studies, but acknowledge that further conceptualizations exist.

Another limitation concerns validating the effectiveness of CS with human users. Future work should assess whether CS aligned with our dimensions effectively influences discourse, fosters empathy toward targets, induces belief change in users, and reduces hateful content from the original authors.

Regarding the data, we acknowledge that our dataset is imbalanced, with a higher number of counter-speech examples generated by experts compared to those from online users, a common trait in counter-speech corpora due to the scarcity of high-quality, user-generated responses
Susan et al. 2016
;
Mathew et al. 2019
;
Chung et al. 2019
;
Qian et al. 2019b
.
Additionally, some dimensions, particularly Audience Adaptation, exhibit class imbalance, which may affect model performance. To improve robustness, future studies should leverage larger and more balanced datasets.

Despite these limitations, our study introduces a new evaluation framework for counter-speech effectiveness and provides a model setup that achieves strong classification results. With this work, we aim to support and strengthen the community effort towards the refinement of counter-speech evaluation frameworks and the improvement of automatic approaches for counter-speech effectiveness assessment and generation.

9
Ethical Statement

We are aware that our study involves human exposure to hateful and potentially upsetting content. For this reason, we followed the mitigation strategies proposed by
Vidgen et al. 2019
to protect annotators’ mental health. Participants gave their consent to voluntarily participate in the annotation, and were informed that they could withdraw from the study at any time. Moreover, each participant was allocated a small subset of the data (100 examples), and a generous time frame of about three weeks to mitigate prolonged exposure to potentially distressing language.
We plan to share the data with the new annotations for research purposes only.

Appendix B
Additional Experimental Details

In this section, we describe the model configurations used in our experiments. The goal of these experiments is to classify the effectiveness dimension scores for a given counter-speech response.
More specifically, we use
bert-base-uncased
with 110M parameters.
In all configurations, the input length is controlled by setting
max_length
to 128. The
batch_size
is 16, the
random_state
is set to 42, 0, 1, 2, and 3, and the
learning_rate
is 2e-5. All the inputs are tokenized by using
BertTokenizer
, and the process includes 3 training epochs (6 for two configurations).
For performing the experiments, we concatenate both CONAN and Twitter datasets, and then we split it into train, validation, and test subsets, with a percentage of 70-10-20. We also perform cross-validation, by using a single dataset for training and validation, and the other for testing. The experiments were run on one A100 GPU.

We use the following loss functions:

•

Binary Cross-Entropy Loss (BCE)
: Used for binary classification tasks, it measures the difference between predicted probabilities and actual labels.

•

Cross-Entropy Loss (CE)
: Generalized for multi-class classification, CE extends BCE to multiple classes by computing the log loss over softmax-normalized probabilities.

•

Focal Loss
: an extension of CE/BCE, focal loss addresses class imbalance by down-weighting easy samples and focusing on hard ones. It introduces a modulating factor to reduce the contribution of well-classified examples.

We also provide more details regarding our model contributions:

•

Multi-task_divided
. We take the binary dimensions, and we train
bert-base-uncased
on them separately, then we combine the two losses (BCE and Focal Loss, for imbalanced datasets) to make the predictions. Then, we take the multi-label dimensions, we train them separately with Cross Entropy Loss, and, at the end, we combine all the losses, and we make the predictions on the test set. This configuration assumes that dimensions, for which the losses are added together, are somehow correlated.

•

Multi-task_united
. Here, we take all the dimensions together, both binary and multi-label, and we train them with their respective loss functions (BCE, Focal Loss, and Cross-Entropy loss). Then we aggregate all the losses, and we predict the results for all of them. All predictions are done with the same Multi-task bert model. Again, the configuration assumes that dimensions are somehow correlated.

•

Dependency_learnable
. All the dimensions are considered together, by training
bert-base-uncased
on them. Each dimension has its own loss function (BCE for emotional appeal, Focal Loss for audience adaptation, Cross Entropy Loss for all the multi-label dimensions), which are summed up at the end. In addition, there is a new parameter called
dependency-matrix
. Its rows and columns contain values between 0 and 1, that are weights accounting for the dependency between pairs of dimensions. Its weights are randomly initialized, and their best value is learned during the training phase. We run the model for 3 and 6 epochs.

We experiment with two types of input embeddings: (i) concatenated Hate Speech (HS) and Counter Speech (CS) embeddings to provide broader context and (ii) CS embeddings alone. The latter consistently yields slightly better performance. Consequently, we adopt CS-only embeddings for all configurations in the main part of the paper (
Multi-task_d.
,
Multi-task_u.
,
Dependency_m._3e
, and
Dependency_m._6e
). For completeness, Table
6
also reports the average results over five runs for models using concatenated CS+HS embeddings.

Appendix E
Error Analysis

In this section, we discuss some examples to better understand the models’ behaviors, in particular on the most difficult class (emotional appeal) and the one achieving almost perfect F1 score (audience adaptation).

Audience Adaptation
.
It consistently achieves high F1 scores (0.99–1.00) across models, likely due to the similarity in linguistic structure and content between CS and HS. For example, consider the following HS/CS pair from CONAN:

HS
:
"Islam is not a religion that professes peace and it does not make any good men. It condemns women and encourages delinquency. Lawbreakers are not all of a certain race or religion, but only one race and religion make lawbreakers."

CS
:
"Today, in many Muslim-majority communities, women receive equal, even higher in some places, treatment as men. For example, many women work as scientists, engineers, lawyers, etc."

Both speeches aim to persuade a specific group by matching linguistic complexity, thus ensuring audience adaptation. The CS provides counter-examples to the HS claims, using similarly complex and detailed arguments. This makes it easier for the classifier to recognize this dimension, as both speeches maintain a similar linguistic and argumentative structure. However, this near-perfect performance could reflect data bias, suggesting that audience adaptation might be too easily detected, due to the high class imbalance.
Further error analysis should investigate whether models are truly understanding adaptation strategies or simply leveraging surface-level cues.

Emotional Appeal.

Emotional appeal is challenging to classify due to its subjective nature and context-dependent expression. For example, consider the following counter-speeches from Twitter and CONAN:

Twitter
:
"@user this isn’t funny at all. don’t make jokes of the situation when rene herself is affected by it. don’t get her dragged even more and delete this."

This CS is direct and uses imperatives to express frustration and urgency, relying on internet culture and social cues like tagging someone and using second-person pronouns ("you").

CONAN
:
"I thought we’d stopped exporting our convicted criminals last century. Now you advocate exporting people without a trial or a conviction."

This example uses sarcasm, rhetorical questioning, and historical references to evoke moral outrage and challenge the opposing argument. The emotional appeal here is more subtle and indirect, contrasting with the explicit frustration in the Twitter example.
A model trained solely on Twitter might miss these subtleties found in expert-written CS. To improve emotional appeal classification, domain adaptation or dataset augmentation could help models recognize diverse emotional expressions.
