The predicable dance of random walk local limit theorems on finitely-generated abelian groups
Evan Randles, Yutong Yan

Abstract
In random walk theory, it is customary to assume that a given walk is irreducible and/or aperiodic. While these prevailing assumptions make particularly tractable the analysis of random walks and help to highlight their diffusive nature, they eliminate a natural phenomenon: the dance . This dance can be seen, for example, in the so-called simple random walk on the integers where a random walker moves back and forth between even and odd integers. It can also be seen in the random walk on the integer lattice that takes only those steps available to a knight on a chess board. In this work, we develop a general Fourier-analytic method to describe random walks on finitely-generated abelian groups, making no assumptions concerning aperiodicity or irreducibility. Our main result is a local central limit theorems that describes the large-time behavior of the transition probabilities for any random walk whose driving measure has finite second moments. Generalizing the ubiquitous Gaussian approximation, our asymptotic is given as a product of two functions, one that describes the diffusion and another that describes the dance. Interestingly, our “dance function” is gotten as a Haar integral over a subgroup of the group’s Pontryagin dual. Our results recapture many long-standing results, especially the local limit theorems made famous by F. Spitzer’s classic text, Principles of Random Walk .

random walks on abelian groups, local limit theorems

Introduction

In this article, we develop a Fourier-analytic method to study random walks on finitely-generated abelian groups. Our approach generalizes and departs from more standard ones that assume walks be irreducible and/or aperiodic
[
KS65
,
S76
,
Wo00
]
. While some approaches do consider periodic walks, large-time asymptotic behavior is often obtained by replacing a periodic walk by a lazy aperiodic one for which the analysis is far more tractable. Irreducible walks are less frequently considered as, in the words of F. Spitzer, they are said to simply be
posed on the wrong group
[
S76
]

For those probabilities satisfying the mild assumption of having finite second moments, we obtain large-time asymptotics for the transition probabilities of random walks in the form of local (central) limit theorems.

Let

with

For

\Omega(p)=\{\xi\in\widehat{G}:\widehat{p}(\xi)\in\mathbb{S}\}.

Just as we saw in the introduction, this simple set characterizes the “dance” of random walk precisely. The following proposition plays a key role in this.

Proposition 2.2
.

For
p\in\mathcal{M}(G)

\Omega(p)
is a closed subgroup of

and therefore a compact abelian group. For each
\xi\in\Omega(p)

\widehat{p}(\xi)=\chi_{\xi}(x)

. In particular, if

, then
\widehat{p}(\xi)=1

\xi\in\Omega(p)

Proof.

Because
\sum_{x}p(x)=1

\xi\in\Omega(p)

\absolutevalue{\sum_{x\in G}p(x)\chi_\xi(x)}=1,

guarantees that every term in the sum

must have the same phase. This ensures at once that
\widehat{p}(\xi)=\chi_{\xi}(x)

We now show that

\displaystyle\widehat{p}(\xi-\xi^{\prime})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{x\in G}p(x)\chi_{\xi}(x)\chi_{-\xi^{\prime}}(x)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{x\in\operatorname{Supp}(p)}p(x)\chi_{\xi}(x)\overline{\chi_{\xi^{\prime}}(x)}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{x\in\operatorname{Supp}(p)}p(x)\widehat{p}(\xi)\widehat{p}(\xi^{\prime})^{-1}

\displaystyle\widehat{p}(\xi)\widehat{p}(\xi^{\prime})^{-1}

and so

2.2
,
\chi_{\xi}(x)=\widehat{p}(\xi)
for all
\xi\in\Omega(p)
. Consequently,

\Theta_{p}(1,x)=\int_{\Omega(p)}\widehat{p}(\xi)\chi_{\xi}(-x)\,d\omega_{p}(\xi)=\int_{\Omega(p)}\chi_{\xi}(x)\chi_{\xi}(-x)\,d\omega_{p}(\xi)=\int_{\Omega(p)}1\,d\omega_{p}(\xi)=\omega_{p}(\Omega(p))>0

where we have used the homomorphism property of characters. Thus
\operatorname{Supp}(p)\subseteq\operatorname{Supp}(\Theta(1,\cdot))
and we have verified the base case.

Assume now that the assertion is true for
n\geq 1
and suppose that
p^{(n+1)}(x)>0
for
x\in G
. In this case,

\sum_{y\in G}p^{(n)}(x-y)p(y)>0

and therefore
x-y\in\operatorname{Supp}(p^{(n)})
for some
y\in\operatorname{Supp}(p)
. For this
y
, our induction hypothesis gives us
\Theta_{p}(n,x-y)>0
and so

\Theta_{p}(n+1,x)=\int_{\Omega(p)}\widehat{p}(\xi)^{n}\widehat{p}(\xi)\chi_{\xi}(-x)\,d\omega_{p}(\xi)=\int_{\Omega(p)}\widehat{p}(\xi)^{n}\chi_{\xi}(y-x)d\omega_{p}(\xi)=\Theta_{p}(n,x-y)>0

where we have used the fact that
\widehat{p}(\xi)=\chi_{\xi}(y)
for all
\xi\in\Omega(p)
thanks to Proposition
2.2
. Hence
\operatorname{Supp}(p^{(n+1)})\subseteq\operatorname{Supp}(\Theta_{p}(n+1,\cdot))
and our proof is complete.
∎

As we discussed in the introduction, we can also recognize
\Theta_{p}
as an indicator function.

Proposition 2.4
.

Let
p\in\mathcal{M}(G)
and let
G_{p}
be the subgroup as given in (
6
). Then,
G_{p}
is the annihilator subgroup of
\Omega(p)
, i.e.,

G_{p}=\Omega(p)^{\dagger}:=\{x\in G:\chi_{\xi}(x)=1\quad\mbox{for all}\quad\xi\in\Omega(p)\}

and, for any

,

\Theta_{p}(n,x)=\Theta_{p}(x-nx_{0})=\omega_{p}(\Omega(p))\mathds{1}_{G_{p}}(x-nx_{0})

for all
n\in\mathbb{N}_{+}
and
x\in G
.

Proof.

Throughout the proof, we shall suppress our notation’s dependence on
p
and write
\Omega=\Omega(p)
,
\omega=\omega_{p}
, and
\Theta=\Theta_{p}
. If
y=x-x_{0}\in\operatorname{Supp}(p)-x_{0}
for
x_{0}\in\operatorname{Supp}(p)
, then

\chi_{\xi}(y)=\chi_{\xi}(x-x_{0})=\chi_{\xi}(x)\overline{\chi_{\xi}(x_{0})}=\widehat{p}(\xi)\overline{\widehat{p}(\xi)}=1

whenever
\xi\in\Omega
by virtue of Proposition
2.2
. Thus,
\Omega^{\dagger}
is a subgroup containing
\operatorname{Supp}(p)-x_{0}
and so
G_{p}\subseteq\Omega^{\dagger}.
Observe that, if
\xi\in G_{p}^{\dagger}
(i.e.,
\chi_{\xi}(x)=1
for all
x\in G_{p}
), then

\displaystyle\widehat{p}(\xi)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{x\in\operatorname{Supp}(p)}p(x)\chi_{\xi}(x)=\chi_{\xi}(x_{0})\sum_{x\in\operatorname{Supp}(p)}p(x)\chi_{\xi}(x-x_{0})\in\mathbb{S}

since
\operatorname{Supp}(p)-x_{0}\in G_{p}
. In other words, we have
G_{p}^{\dagger}\subseteq\Omega
. Using the definition of
\dagger
and making use of
[
Fo95
, Proposition 4.38]
, it follows that

\Omega^{\dagger}\subseteq\left(G_{p}^{\dagger}\right)^{\dagger}=G_{p}

since

is necessarily closed (as

carries the discrete topology). Thus,
\Omega^{\dagger}=G_{p}
.

Given
x_{0}\in\operatorname{Supp}(p)
, the identity

\Theta(n,x)=\Theta(x-nx_{0})

follows by virtually the same argument used in the previous proposition (making use of Proposition
2.2
and the homomorphism property of characters). Thus, to complete the proof, it suffices to prove that

\Theta(x)=\omega(\Omega)\mathds{1}_{\Omega^{\dagger}}(x).

(10)

If
x\in\Omega^{\dagger}
,
\chi_{\xi}(-x)=\overline{\chi_{\xi}(x)}=1
for all
\xi\in\Omega
and so

\Theta(x)=\int_{\Omega}1\,d\omega=\omega(\Omega)=\omega(\Omega)\mathds{1}_{\Omega^{\dagger}}(x).

Observe that, for any
x\in G
and
\xi_{0}\in\Omega
, the invariance of the Haar measure
\omega
and the homomorphism property of characters guarantee that

\Theta(x)=\int_{\Omega}\chi_{\xi_{0}+\xi}(-x)\,d\omega(\xi)=\chi_{\xi_{0}}(-x)\int_{\Omega}\chi_{\xi}(-x)\,d\omega(\xi)=\chi_{\xi_{0}}(-x)\Theta(x).

In other words, for any
x\in G
and
\xi_{0}\in\Omega
, we have

\Theta(x)\left(1-\overline{\chi_{\xi_{0}}(x)}\right)=0.

Now, in the case that

, there must be some
\xi_{0}\in\Omega
with
\chi_{\xi_{0}}(x)\neq 1
. With this, the above identity ensures that

and hence (

) is true for all

.

∎

As we discussed in the introductory section, it is customary in random walk theory to study random walks that are (or are modified to be) both irreducible and aperiodic. It is under these hypotheses that local limit theorems are often established, absent of a prefactor
\Theta_{p}
. The following proposition shows why this is the case.

Proposition 2.5
.

Let
p\in\mathcal{M}(G)
and assume that
p
drives a random walk on
G
that is both aperiodic and irreducible. Then
\Omega(p)=\{0\}
,
\Theta_{p}\equiv 1
, and
G_{p}=G
.

While this proposition is a corollary of Proposition
2.7
below (for
s=1
), we have decided to give a distinct proof here that completely determines
\Omega(p)
under the given hypotheses.

Proof.

In view of the preceding proposition, we need only to show that
\Omega(p)=\{0\}
. To this end, let
\xi\in\Omega(p)
. Using the identity
(\widehat{p})^{n}=\widehat{p^{(n)}}
for
n\in\mathbb{N}_{+}
, we see easily that
\xi\in\Omega(p^{(n)})
for all
n\in\mathbb{N}_{+}
. Using our hypotheses that the random walk driven by
p
is both aperiodic and irreducible, for each
x\in G
, it is easy to find
n=n_{x}\in\mathbb{N}_{+}
for which
p^{(n)}(0)
and
p^{(n)}(x)
are both positive, i.e.,
\{0,x\}\subseteq\operatorname{Supp}(p^{(n)})
. By virtue of Proposition
2.2
(applied to
p^{(n)}
), we have

\widehat{p^{(n)}}(\xi)=\chi_{\xi}(x)=\chi_{\xi}(0)=1

since
\xi\in\Omega(p^{(n)})
. As
x
was arbitrary, we can conclude that
\chi_{\xi}(x)=1
for all
x\in G
and so, because characters separate points, it can only be the case that
\xi=0
.
∎

In the slightly more general case that
p\in\mathcal{M}(G)
drives an irreducible walk on
G
with period

, we can make use of Proposition
2.3
and

to obtain useful information about

and

. To this end, let’s first treat a corollary of Propositions

and

.

Lemma 2.6
.

Let
p\in\mathcal{M}(G)
and let

be as given in (
6
). If
p
drives an irreducible walk on
G
of period
s
, then, for any
x_{0}\in\operatorname{Supp}(p)
,
sx_{0}\in G_{p}
.

Proof.

We take
s
to be the period of

, i.e.,
s=\gcd R
where

R=\{n\in\mathbb{N}_{+}:p^{(n)}(0)>0\}

is the set of return times to zero, and fix
x_{0}\in\operatorname{Supp}(p)
. It follows that there is a (finite) collection
n_{1},n_{2},\dots,n_{K}\in R
with
s=\gcd(n_{1},n_{2},\dots,n_{K})
. Appealing to Propositions
2.3
and
2.4
, we find

0<\Theta(n_{k},0)=\omega_{p}(\Omega(p))\mathds{1}_{G_{p}}(0-n_{k}x_{0})

so that

for every

(since

is a group). Thanks to Bezout’s identity, there are integers
a_{1},a_{2},\dots,a_{K}
for which
s=a_{1}n_{1}+a_{2}n_{2}+\cdots+a_{K}n_{K}
and consequently

sx_{0}=a_{1}n_{1}x_{0}+a_{2}n_{2}x_{0}+\cdots a_{K}n_{K}x_{0}\in G_{p},

∎

Proposition 2.7
.

Let
p\in\mathcal{M}(G)
, define
G_{p}
as above, and take
x_{0}\in\operatorname{Supp}(p)
. If
p
drives a random walk on
G
that is irreducible and periodic of period
s
, then the cosets

G_{p},G_{p}+x_{0},\,G_{p}+2x_{0},\dots,\mbox{and }\,\,G_{p}+(s-1)x_{0}

form a partition of
G
. In particular,

G/G_{p}=\{G_{p},G_{p}+x_{0},G_{p}+2x_{0},\dots,G_{p}+(s-1)x_{0}\}

and
[G:G_{p}]=s
.

Proof.

Let
p\in\mathcal{M}(G)
be irreducible and periodic of period
s
. For each
x\in G
, irreducibility gives
n\in\mathbb{N}
for which
x\in\operatorname{Supp}(p^{(n)})
and, by virtue of Propositions
2.3
and
2.4
, we have

0<\Theta(n,x)=\omega_{p}(\Omega(p))\mathds{1}_{G_{p}}(x-nx_{0})=\omega_{p}(\Omega(p))\mathds{1}_{G_{p}+nx_{0}}(x)

so that
x\in G_{p}+nx_{0}
. Hence

G=\bigcup_{n=1}^{\infty}(G_{p}+nx_{0}).

Making an appeal to Lemma
2.6
,
sx_{0}\in G_{p}
and so

G=\bigcup_{k=1}^{s}(G_{p}+kx_{0})=\bigcup_{k=0}^{s-1}(G_{p}+kx_{0}).

To complete the proof of the proposition, it remains to show that the cosets
G_{p},\,G_{p}+x_{0},\dots,G_{p}+(s-1)x_{0}
are all distinct. To do this, we shall make use of the following identity which says that these cosets are precisely the periodic classes of the random walk: For

,

G_{p}+kx_{0}=\{x\in G:p^{(ns+k)}(y)>0\,\,\mbox{ for some }n\in\mathbb{N}\}.

(11)

Using basic techniques of Markov/random walks theory, the verification of this identity is straightforward
[
Ch60
,
Wo00
]
. For completeness, we have given a proof in Section
A
of the appendix.

Armed with (

), suppose that
y\in(G_{p}+kx_{0})\cap(G_{p}+jx_{0})
for

and select natural numbers

and

for which
p^{(ns+j)}(y)>0
and
p^{(ms+k)}(y)>0.
Since
p
is irreducible, let’s also choose
l
for which
p^{(l)}(-y)>0
. With this, we see that

p^{(ns+j+l)}(0)\geq p^{(l)}(-y)p^{(ns+j)}(y)>0\hskip 28.45274pt\mbox{and}\hskip 28.45274ptp^{(ms+k+l)}(0)\geq p^{(l)}(-y)p^{(ms+k)}(y)>0.

By the

, it follows that

divides both

and

and so it must divide

. Of course,

and so

. Thus, the cosets are distinct and our proof is complete.
∎

Theorem 3.5
.

Let

2.

\frac{p^{(n)}(x)+p^{(n+1)}(x)+\cdots+p^{(n+(s-1))}(x)}{s}=\frac{1}{\operatorname{Tor}(G)}K_{\varphi_{*}(p)}^{n}(\varphi(x))+o(n^{-d/2})

Proof.

n^{d/2}\absolutevalue{\frac{e^{-\frac{y\cdot\Gamma^{-1}y}{2(n+k)}}}{(n+k)^{d/2}}-\frac{e^{-\frac{y\cdot\Gamma^{-1}y}{2n}}}{n^{d/2}}}\leq\left(\left(1-\frac{n^{d/2}}{(n+k)^{d/2}}\right)+\left(1-e^{-\frac{k(y\cdot\Gamma^{-1}y)}{2n(n+k)}}\right)\right)e^{-\frac{y\cdot\Gamma^{-1}y}{2(n+k)}}=o(1)

\displaystyle\frac{p^{(n)}(x)+p^{(n+1)}(x)+\cdots+p^{(n+(s-1))}(x)}{s}

\displaystyle\frac{1}{\operatorname{Tor}(G)}\left(\frac{1}{s}\sum_{k=0}^{s-1}\Theta_{p}(n+k,x)\right)K^{n}(\varphi(x))+o(n^{-d/2})

\displaystyle\frac{1}{\operatorname{Tor}(G)}K^{n}(\varphi(x))+o(n^{-d/2})

Before we conclude this section, let’s turn our attention briefly to non-Gaussian behavior. Random walks on finite/compact non-abelian groups have been studied extensively, especially given their applications to card shuffling. References are too extensive to list here, so we point to the notes/surveys
[

. As aperiodic and irreducible walks on finite/compact groups converge to the uniform measure
U=U_{G}
, it’s helpful to measure this convergence by means of the total variation norm; this is given by

\|\mu-\nu\|=\sup_{A\subseteq G}\absolutevalue{\mu(A)-\nu(A)}=\frac{1}{2}\sum_{x\in G}\absolutevalue{\mu(x)-\nu(x)}

for measures
\mu,\nu
on
G
; the latter equality holding when
G
is countable. In these terms, for an irreducible and aperiodic walk
p
on a finite (or compact) group

\|p^{(n)}-U\|
converges exponentially to
0
as

and the precise constants that quantify such exponential convergence give rise to cut-off phenomena. For these results, in addition to the above references, we point the reader to
[
K59
,
DSh81
,
AD86
,
AD87
]
. In the case of finitely-generated abelian groups, the following theorem gives a compatible result without making any assumptions on irreducibility/aperiodicity.

Theorem 3.6
.

Let
G
be a finitely-generated abelian group. For
p\in\mathcal{M}(G)
, suppose that
G_{p}
is finite, let
p_{A}
denote the pushforward measure of
p
onto
A=\operatorname{Tor}(G)
and set

\rho=\max\left\{\absolutevalue{\widehat{p_A}(\alpha)}:\alpha\in\widehat{A}\setminus\Omega(p_{A})\right\}\in[0,1).

Then, for any

,

\|p^{(n)}-\tau_{nx_{0}}(U_{G_{p}})\|\leq\frac{\absolutevalue{G_p}-1}{2}\rho^{n}

for

; here,

denotes the uniform distribution on

and

is the translation operator

for

This section is dedicated to proving Theorem

. For reasons that will become clear from structure theorems, we shall begin our study by focusing on certain random walks on the group

d_{B}

are positive and treating this correctly is the primary aim of the present development. For simplicity, we shall write elements of

\widehat{H}=\widehat{A}\times\mathbb{T}^{d_{B}}\times\mathbb{T}^{d_{C}}

with elements expressed as
(\alpha,\beta,\gamma)
and the corresponding homomorphisms from

for each
(\alpha,\beta,\gamma)\in\widehat{H}

(P1)

There is some element
c_{0}\in\mathbb{Z}^{d_{C}}
for which

\operatorname{Supp}(p)\subseteq A\times\mathbb{Z}^{d_{B}}\times\{c_{0}\}.

(P2)

The dimension
d_{B}\geq 1
and the support’s projection on

\operatorname{Proj}_{\mathbb{Z}^{d_{B}}}(\operatorname{Supp}(p))=\{b\in\mathbb{Z}^{d_{B}}:p(a,b,c)>0\,\,\mbox{for some }(a,b,c)\in H\},

-dimensional set in the sense of Definition
3.4
.

We remark that, in the case that
d_{C}=0
, we will take Property

to be automatic.

Assuming that
p
satisfies
(P1)
, let’s make some observations. First, define
p_{AB}\in\mathcal{M}(A\times\mathbb{Z}^{d_{B}})
by

p_{AB}(a,b)=p(a,b,c_{0})

for
(a,b)\in A\times\mathbb{Z}^{d_{B}}
. With this, observe that

\widehat{p}(\alpha,\beta,\gamma)=\sum_{(a,b,c)\in H}p(a,b,c)\chi_{\alpha}(a)e^{i\beta\cdot b}e^{i\gamma\cdot c}=e^{i\gamma\cdot c_{0}}\sum_{(a,b)\in A\times\mathbb{Z}^{d_{B}}}p_{AB}(a,b)\chi_{\alpha}(a)e^{i\beta\cdot b}=e^{i\gamma\cdot c_{0}}\widehat{p_{AB}}(\alpha,\beta)

for
(\alpha,\beta,\gamma)\in\widehat{H}
. From this, we immediately obtain the following result.

Lemma 4.1
.

If

satisfies Property
(P1)

\Omega(p)=\Omega(p_{AB})\times\mathbb{T}^{d_{C}}

and

\widehat{p}(\alpha,\beta,\gamma)=\widehat{p_{AB}}(\alpha,\beta)e^{i\gamma\cdot c_{0}}

for all
(\alpha,\beta,\gamma)\in\widehat{H}
.

In the non-trivial case that
d_{B}\geq 1
, let’s now define
p_{B}\in\mathcal{M}(\mathbb{Z}^{d_{B}})
by

p_{B}(b)=\sum_{a\in A}p_{AB}(a,b)=\sum_{a\in A}p(a,b,c_{0})

(14)

for
b\in\mathbb{Z}^{d_{B}}
. We have

Lemma 4.2
.

Let
p
satisfy
(P1)
and assume

. For any
(\alpha_{0},\beta_{0})\in\Omega(p_{AB})
,

\widehat{p_{AB}}(\alpha_{0},\beta_{0}+\eta)=\widehat{p_{AB}}(\alpha_{0},\beta_{0})\widehat{p_{B}}(\eta)

for all
\eta\in\mathbb{T}^{d_{B}}

Proof.

Fix
(\alpha_{0},\beta_{0})\in\Omega(p_{AB})
and observe that
\chi_{\alpha_{0}}(a)e^{i\beta_{0}\cdot b}=\widehat{p_{AB}}(\alpha_{0},\beta_{0})
for all

\displaystyle\widehat{p_{AB}}(\alpha_{0},\beta_{0}+\eta)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{(a,b)\in A\times\mathbb{Z}^{d_{B}}}p_{AB}(a,b)\chi_{\alpha_{0}}(a)e^{i(\beta_{0}+\eta)\cdot b}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{(a,b)\in\operatorname{Supp}(p_{AB})}p_{AB}(a,b)\chi_{\alpha_{0}}(a)e^{i\beta_{0}\cdot b}e^{i\eta\cdot b}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{(a,b)\in\operatorname{Supp}(p_{AB})}p_{AB}(a,b)\widehat{p_{AB}}(\alpha_{0},\beta_{0})e^{i\eta\cdot b}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\widehat{p_{AB}}(\alpha_{0},\beta_{0})\sum_{(a,b)\in A\times\mathbb{Z}^{d_{B}}}p_{AB}(a,b)e^{i\eta\cdot b}

\displaystyle\widehat{p_{AB}}(\alpha_{0},\beta_{0})\widehat{p_{B}}(\eta)

thanks to the Fubini-Tonelli theorem.

The following lemma is fairly standard and proofs can be found (essentially) in

Lemma 4.3
.

Let
p\in\mathcal{M}_{2}(H)
satisfy Properties
(P1)
and
(P2)
. Then
p_{B}
has finite second moments (i.e.,
p_{B}\in\mathcal{M}_{2}(\mathbb{Z}^{d_{B}})
) and drives a genuinely
d_{B}

\mathbb{Z}^{d_{B}}
with mean
\mu_{B}\in\mathbb{R}^{d_{B}}
and
d_{B}\times d_{B}
covariance matrix
\Gamma_{B}
given, respectively, by

\{\mu_{B}\}_{k}=\sum_{b\in\mathbb{Z}^{d_{B}}}b_{k}p_{B}(b)

and

\{\Gamma_{B}\}_{k,l}=\left(\sum_{b\in\mathbb{Z}^{d_{B}}}b_{k}b_{l}p_{B}(b)\right)-\{\mu_{B}\}_{k}\{\mu_{B}\}_{l}=\sum_{b\in\mathbb{Z}^{d_{B}}}(b_{k}-\{\mu_{B}\}_{k})(b_{l}-\{\mu_{B}\}_{l})p_{B}(b)

for
k,l=1,2,\dots,d_{B}
. Furthermore,
\Gamma_{B}
is symmetric positive-definite and

\operatorname{Log}(\widehat{p_{B}}(\eta))=i\mu_{B}\cdot\eta-\frac{\eta\cdot(\Gamma_{B}\eta)}{2}+o(\absolutevalue{\eta}^{2})

(15)

as
\eta\to 0
; here
\operatorname{Log}
denotes the principal branch of the logarithm.

Proof.

Consider the finite symmetric generating set
\mathcal{H}
of
H
defined by

\mathcal{H}=\{(a,0,0):a\in A\}\cup\{(0,\pm e_{j},0):j=1,2,\dots,d_{B}\}\cup\{(0,0,\pm e_{j}):j=1,2,\dots,d_{C}\}.

Using this set of generators, the norm
\absolutevalue{\cdot}_{\mathcal{H}}
on
H
satisfies
\absolutevalue{b}\leq\absolutevalue{h}_{\mathcal{H}}
for every
h=(a,b,c)\in H
; here
\absolutevalue{b}
denotes the Euclidean (or taxicab) norm on
\mathbb{Z}^{d}
. Thus, by the definition of
p_{B}
and Tonelli’s theorem, we have

\sum_{b\in\mathbb{Z}^{d_{B}}}\absolutevalue{b}^{2}p_{B}(b)=\sum_{(a,b,c)\in H}\absolutevalue{b}^{2}p(a,b,c)\leq\sum_{h\in H}\absolutevalue{h}_{\mathcal{H}}^{2}\,p(h)<\infty

since
p\in\mathcal{M}_{2}(H)
. Correspondingly,
p_{B}\in\mathcal{M}_{2}(\mathbb{Z}^{d_{B}})
. Using the definition of
p_{B}
, we also observe that

\operatorname{Supp}(p_{B})=\operatorname{Proj}_{\mathbb{Z}^{d_{B}}}(\operatorname{Supp}(p))

and so Property
(P2)
is equivalent to the condition that
p_{B}
cannot be supported on any affine subspace of
\mathbb{R}^{d_{B}}
; this is precisely what it means for the random walk driven by
p_{B}
to be genuinely
d_{B}
dimensional.

Define
f(\eta)=\operatorname{Log}(\widehat{p_{B}}(\eta))
and observe that, because
p_{B}\in\mathcal{M}_{2}(\mathbb{Z}^{d_{B}})
and
\widehat{p_{B}}(0)=1
,

is

on a neighborhood of

with

f(\eta)=0+\sum_{k}\partial_{j}f(0)\eta_{k}+\frac{1}{2}\sum_{k,l}\partial_{k}\partial_{l}f(0)\eta_{k}\eta_{l}+o(\absolutevalue{\eta}^{2})

as
\eta\to 0
where the summations are taken for
k,l=1,2,\dots,d_{B}
. By direct calculation, we find that

\partial_{k}f(0)=\frac{1}{\widehat{p_{B}}(0)}\sum_{b\in\mathbb{Z}^{d_{B}}}ib_{k}p_{B}(b)=i\sum_{b\in\mathbb{Z}^{d_{B}}}b_{k}p_{B}(b)=i\{\mu_{B}\}_{k}

for
k=1,2,\dots,d_{B}
. Similarly,

\partial_{k}\partial_{l}f(0)=\{\mu_{B}\}_{k}\{\mu_{B}\}_{l}-\sum_{b\in\mathbb{Z}^{d_{B}}}b_{k}b_{l}p_{B}(b)=-\{\Gamma_{B}\}_{kl}

for
k,l=1,2,\dots,d_{B}
. Thus,

\operatorname{Log}(\widehat{p_{B}}(\eta))=f(\eta)=i\mu_{B}\cdot\eta-\frac{\eta\cdot\Gamma_{B}\eta}{2}+o(\absolutevalue{\eta}^{2})

as
\eta\to 0
. As
\Gamma_{B}
is clearly symmetric, it remains to prove it is positive definite and so, equivalently, that its quadratic form
\eta\mapsto\eta\cdot\Gamma_{B}\eta
is positive definite. To this end, observe that

\displaystyle\eta\cdot\Gamma_{B}\eta

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{k,l}\eta_{k}\left(\sum_{b\in\mathbb{Z}^{d_{B}}}(b_{k}-\mu_{k})(b_{l}-\mu_{l})p_{B}(b)\right)\eta_{l}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{b\in\mathbb{Z}^{d_{B}}}\sum_{k,l}\eta_{k}(b_{k}-\mu_{k})\eta_{l}(b_{l}-\mu_{l})p_{B}(b)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{b\in\mathbb{Z}^{d_{B}}}\left[\eta\cdot(b-\mu)\right]^{2}p_{B}(b)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{b\in\operatorname{Supp}(p_{B})}\left[\eta\cdot(b-\mu)\right]^{2}p_{B}(b)

where, for simplicity, we have written

. If this expression were zero for some

would be orthogonal to the set of vectors

\operatorname{Supp}(p_{B})
. In particular, this would imply that
\operatorname{Supp}(p_{B})-\mu
is contained in the subspace perpendicular to

\eta\cdot\Gamma_{B}\eta=\sum_{b\in\operatorname{Supp}(p_{B})}[\eta\cdot(b-\mu)]^{2}p_{B}(b)>0

\eta\neq 0

.

. Since

\absolutevalue{\widehat{p_{AB}}(\alpha_0,\beta_0+\eta)}<1

We are now in a position to introduce the Gaussian density/attractor that will appear in our local limit theorems. We start by introducing the heat kernel

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{(2\pi)^{d_{B}}}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d_{B}}}e^{-t\frac{\beta\cdot\Gamma_{B}\beta}{2}}e^{-i\beta\cdot b}\,d\beta

\displaystyle\frac{1}{(2\pi t)^{d_{B}/2}\sqrt{\operatorname{det}(\Gamma_{B})}}\exp\left(-\frac{b\cdot(\Gamma_{B}^{-1}b)}{2t}\right)

Remark 5
.

In the case that
d_{C}=0
and
p=p_{AB}\in\mathcal{M}_{2}(A\times\mathbb{Z}^{d_{B}})
satisfies Properties
(P1)
(automatically) and
(P2)
, the final paragraph of the proof of Lemma
4.7
shows that
\omega_{p}
is counting measure on
\Omega(p_{AB})=\Omega(p)
.

We now turn to the general situation and let
G
be a finitely-generated abelian group and
p\in\mathcal{M}_{2}(G)
. To complete our proof of Theorem
3.1
in the case that
G_{p}
is infinite, we shall produce an isomorphism between
G
and
H=A\times\mathbb{Z}^{d_{B}}\times\mathbb{Z}^{d_{C}}
to push forward
p\in\mathcal{M}_{2}(G)
to an element of
\mathcal{M}_{2}(H)
satisfying
(P1)
and
(P2)
with
\rank(G_p)=d_{B}
. Still, it isn’t clear that such a
p
-adapted isomorphism exists automatically; we know only that
G\cong\operatorname{Tor}(G)\times\mathbb{Z}^{k}
for some natural number
k
because
G
is finitely-generated and abelian
[
Ja85
, Theorem 3.13]
. Given such an isomorphism, our first lemma will allow us to show that
p
’s pushforward into
\mathbb{Z}^{k}
is
\rank(G_p)
dimensional. The second (and final) lemma will allow us to use this dimension information to “twist” space in such a way that gives the isomorphism from
G
into
H
with
p
satisfying
(P1)
and
(P2)
.

Lemma 4.8
.

Let
G
be a finitely-generated abelian group and
\psi:G\to\operatorname{Tor}(G)\times\mathbb{Z}^{k}
an isomorphism. Given a subset
X
of
G
containing
0
, denote by
\langle X\rangle
the subgroup of
G
generated by
X
and define
Y=\operatorname{Proj}_{\mathbb{Z}^{k}}\circ\psi(X)
. If
\rank(\langle X\rangle)=d
, then the set
Y\subseteq\mathbb{Z}^{k}
is
d
dimensional.

Proof.

If
d=0
, then all elements of
X
must have finite order and correspondingly must be sent to
0
in
\mathbb{Z}^{k}
by the homomorphism
\operatorname{Proj}_{\mathbb{Z}^{k}}\circ\psi
. Correspondingly,
Y
is
0
dimensional. We now assume that
d\geq 1
. Since
\langle X\rangle
is itself a finitely-generated abelian group of rank
d
, there is a set of generators of the form

\{x_{1},x_{2},\dots,x_{d}\}\cup\{z_{1},z_{2},\dots,z_{n}\}

where
\operatorname{Tor}(\langle X\rangle)=\{z_{1},z_{2},\dots,z_{n}\}
and the list
x_{1},x_{2},\dots,x_{d}
is (maximally)
\mathbb{Z}
-linearly independent. For each
j=1,2,\dots,d
, set

y_{j}=\operatorname{Proj}_{\mathbb{Z}_{k}}\circ\psi(x_{j})

and observe that, since the list
\{x_{1},x_{2},\dots,x_{d},z_{1},z_{2},\dots,z_{n}\}
generates
X
, then

Y\in\mbox{span}\{y_{1},y_{2},\dots,y_{d}\};

here, we are using the fact that the projection/quotient map collapses
\operatorname{Tor}(\langle X\rangle)\subseteq\operatorname{Tor}(G)
into
0\in\mathbb{Z}^{k}
. To complete the proof, we must show two things:

1.

The collection
\{y_{1},y_{2},\dots,y_{d}\}
is
\mathbb{R}
-linearly independent.

2.

No proper subset of
y_{1},y_{2},\dots,y_{d}
spans
Y
.

To see the first item, we first observe that
y_{1},y_{2},\dots,y_{d}
are
\mathbb{Z}
-linearly independent. This follows immediately from the fact that they are the images of the
\mathbb{Z}
-linearly independent collection
x_{1},x_{2},\dots,x_{d}
under the isomorphism followed by the projection/quotient map (which kills only torsion elements of
G
). Now, consider the equation

\alpha_{1}y_{1}+\alpha_{2}y_{2}+\cdots+\alpha_{d}y_{d}=0.

If this equation had some non-zero solution over the real numbers, then Gaussian elimination would easily give us a basis for the solution space (with, at least, one non-zero vector). Further, since the vectors
y_{1},y_{2},\dots,y_{d}
all have integer entries, the elementary row operators of the elimination yield a non-zero solution
(\alpha_{1},\alpha_{2},\dots,\alpha_{d})\in\mathbb{Q}^{d}
which can be scaled to be a non-zero member of
\mathbb{Z}^{d}
. Of course, this is impossible because we know that
y_{1},y_{2},\dots,y_{d}
are
\mathbb{Z}
-linearly independent. We remark that another argument for
\mathbb{R}
-linear independence can be made on the basis that
\mathbb{R}
is flat as a
\mathbb{Z}
module.

To see the second item, suppose to reach a contradiction that some proper subset of
\{y_{1},y_{2},\dots,y_{d}\}
spans
Y
, e.g., without loss of generality, assume that

Y\subseteq\mbox{span}\{y_{2},y_{3},\dots,y_{d}\}.

Since
\langle X\rangle
has rank
d
, there must be some
x\in X
for which
x=r_{1}x_{1}+\cdots+r_{d}x_{d}+t_{1}z_{1}+\cdots+t_{n}z_{n}
with
r_{1}\neq 0
. Here, we have
y=\operatorname{Proj}_{\mathbb{Z}^{k}}\circ\psi(x)=r_{1}y_{1}+r_{2}y_{2}+\cdots+r_{d}y_{d}\in Y.
Since, by our supposition,
y\in\mbox{span}\{y_{2},\dots,y_{d}\}
, we also have
y=s_{2}y_{2}+\cdots s_{d}y_{d}
. Thus

0=y-y=r_{1}y_{1}+(r_{2}-s_{2})y_{2}+\cdots+(r_{d}-s_{d})y_{d},

but this is impossible because
y_{1},y_{2},\dots,y_{d}
are linearly independent over
\mathbb{Z}
and, here,
r_{1}\neq 0
.
∎

While the following lemma is straightforward, its proof requires some delicate linear algebra and so we have decided to give a complete proof in Appendix
B
.

Lemma 4.9
.

Let
S\subseteq\mathbb{Z}^{k}
be
d
dimensional for some
1\leq d<k
. Then, there exists
\Phi\in\mathrm{Aut}(\mathbb{Z}^{k})
and

for which

\displaystyle\Phi(S)\subseteq\mathbb{Z}^{d}\times\{w\}

and
\operatorname{Proj}_{\mathbb{Z}^{d}}(\Phi(S))
is
d

\operatorname{Proj}_{\mathbb{Z}^{d}}
denotes the projection from
\mathbb{Z}^{k}
onto the first

coordinates.

We are now in a position to prove the main theorem.

.

We take

and, for

where

(27)

Appendix A
Cosets and Periodic Classes Coincide

In this section, we prove the identity (
11
) used in the proof of Proposition
2.7
. This is the following lemma.

Lemma A.1
.

Let
p\in\mathcal{M}(G)
drive an irreducible walk on
G
of period
s\in\mathbb{N}_{+}
, let
G_{p}
be the subgroup of
G
defined by (
6
), and, for each
k\in\mathbb{N}
, define

C_{k}=\{y\in G:p^{(ns+k)}(y)>0:n\in\mathbb{N}\}.

Then, for any
x_{0}\in\operatorname{Supp}(p)
and
k\in\mathbb{N}
,

G_{p}+kx_{0}=C_{k}.

Proof.

We fix
x_{0}\in\operatorname{Supp}(p)
and treat first the case that
k=0
. To this end, we shall first prove the
C_{0}
is a subgroup of
G
. Since
p
is irreducible and periodic of period
s
, we immediately find that
0\in C_{0}
and so
C_{0}
is non-empty. For
x\in C_{0}
, we can choose
l\in\mathbb{N}_{+}
for which
p^{(l)}(-x)>0
since
p
is irreducible. With this
l
and
n\in\mathbb{N}
for which
p^{(ns)}(x)>0
, we see that

p^{(l+ns)}(0)\geq p^{(l)}(-x)p^{(ns)}(x)>0

and hence
s|(l+ns)
since
p
is periodic of period
s
. Consequently,
s|l
showing that
-x\in C_{0}
whenever
x\in C_{0}
. Given
x,y\in C_{0}
, let
n
and
m
be natural numbers for which
p^{(ns)}(x)>0
and
p^{(ms)}(y)>0
. Then,
p^{((n+m)s)}(x+y)\geq p^{(ns)}(x)p^{(ms)}(y)>0
so that
x+y\in C_{0}
. We have shown that
C_{0}
is a subgroup of
G
.

As
G_{p}=\langle\operatorname{Supp}(p)-x_{0}\rangle
in view of (
6
) and Lemma
1.2
, to see that
G_{p}\subseteq C_{0}
, it suffices to show that
\operatorname{Supp}(p)-x_{0}\subseteq C_{0}
. To this end, let
y=x-x_{0}\in\operatorname{Supp}(p)-x_{0}
and, using the irreducibility of
p
, select
m
for which
p^{(m)}(-x_{0})>0
so that

p^{(m+1)}(0)\geq p^{(m)}(0-x_{0})p(x_{0})>0.

By the
s
-periodicity of
p
, there is some natural number
n
for which
ns=m+1
and therefore

p^{(ns)}(y)=p^{(m+1)}(x-x_{0})\geq p^{(m)}(-x_{0})p(x)>0

since
x\in\operatorname{Supp}(p)
. Thus
y\in C_{0}
and we have shown that
G_{p}\subseteq C_{0}
.

To conclude that
G_{p}=C_{0}
, it remains to prove that
C_{0}\subseteq G_{p}
. To this end, let
y\in C_{0}
and select
n
such that
p^{(ns)}(y)>0
. Since the random walk has reached
y
at step
ns
, there must be some chain formed by elements in
p
’s support that reaches
y
at time
ns
. In other words, there are elements
x_{1},x_{2},\dots,x_{m}\in\operatorname{Supp}(p)
and natural numbers
n_{1},n_{2},\dots,n_{m}
for which
ns=n_{1}+n_{2}+\cdots+n_{m}
and
y=n_{1}x_{1}+n_{2}x_{2}+\cdots+n_{n}x_{n}
.
With this, observe

\displaystyle y

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle n_{1}(x_{1}-x_{0})+n_{2}(x_{2}-x_{0})+\cdots+n_{M}(x_{m}-x_{0})+(n_{1}+n_{2}+\cdots+n_{m})x_{0}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle n_{1}(x_{1}-x_{0})+n_{2}(x_{2}-x_{0})+\cdots+n_{m}(x_{m}-x_{0})+nsx_{0}.

Recalling from Lemma
2.6
,
nsx_{0}\in G_{p}
and so that above gives
y\in G_{p}
and so
G_{p}=C_{0}
.

With the
k=0
case complete, it is now easy to prove that
G_{p}+kx_{0}=C_{k}
for
1\leq k\leq s-1
. Indeed, for a fixed
y=x+kx_{0}\in G_{p}+kx_{0}=C_{0}+kx_{0}
, let
n
be a natural number for which
p^{(ns)}(x)>0
and observe that

p^{(ns+k)}(y)\geq p^{(ns)}(x)p^{(k)}(kx_{0})\geq p^{(ns)}(x)p(x_{0})^{k}>0.

Thus,
y\in C_{k}
and we have shown that
G_{p}+kx_{0}\subseteq C_{k}
. To prove the reverse inclusion, let
y\in C_{k}
and take
n
for which
p^{(ns+k)}(y)>0
. Writing
(n+1)s=ns+k+(s-k)
we have

p^{((n+1)s)}(y+(s-k)x_{0})\geq p^{(ns+k)}(y)p^{(s-k)}((s-k)x_{0})>0.

Consequently,
y+(s-k)x_{0}\in C_{0}=G_{p}
. Since
sx_{0}\in G_{p}
,
-sx_{0}\in G_{p}
and so
y-kx_{0}=y+(s-k)x_{0}-sx_{0}\in G_{p}
whence

We have proven that
G_{p}+kx_{0}=C_{k}
for

. The full result, for

, now follows immediately upon inspecting the definition of
C_{k}
and recalling that

by virtue of Lemma
2.6

Lemma B.2
.

For a positive integer
k
, let
S\subseteq\mathbb{Z}^{k}
. If
S
is contained in some
k-1
dimensional affine subspace of
\mathbb{R}^{k}
, then there exists
\Phi\in\mathrm{Aut}(\mathbb{Z}^{k})
such that

\displaystyle\Phi(S)\subseteq\mathbb{Z}^{k-1}\times\{w\}

for some
w\in\mathbb{Z}
.

Proof.

It suffices to find a matrix
\Phi\in\mathrm{Aut}(\mathbb{Z}^{k})
such that, for some fixed
w\in\mathbb{Z}
,

\Pi_{k}(\Phi x)=w

for all
x\in S
; here,
\Pi_{k}
denotes the
k
th-coordinate projection. When
S
is empty or
k=1
, the statement is immediate. Otherwise, let
b_{1},\dots,b_{l}\in\mathbb{Z}^{k}
(for
1\leq l<k
) be a basis of the subspace generated by
S-x_{0}
for some fixed
x_{0}\in S
. We claim that there is
a=(a_{1},a_{2},\dots,a_{k})\in\mathbb{Z}^{k}
whose entries are relatively prime and for which

a\cdot b_{j}=0

(40)

for every
j=1,\dots,l
. To see this, using our hypothesis that
l<k
, the standard algorithm to produce orthogonal subspaces via Gaussian elimination gives
a\in\mathbb{Q}^{k}
for which (
40
) holds. If this algorithm yields
a\in\mathbb{Z}^{k}
, we can factor out the greatest common divisor of entries, if necessary, to produced our desired
a
. Otherwise, let
n
be the smallest natural number for which
na\in\mathbb{Z}^{k}
and observe that the entries of
na
must be relatively prime for otherwise a smaller
n
could have been gotten by factoring out the greatest common divisor. With this
n
in hand, our desired element is produced by replacing
a
by
na
.

Since each
x\in S
can be expressed in the form
x=(x-x_{0})+x_{0}=\beta_{1}b_{1}+\beta_{2}b_{2}+\cdots\beta_{l}b_{l}+x_{0}
, we have

\Phi x=\beta_{1}\Phi b_{1}+\beta_{2}\Phi b_{2}+\cdots+\beta_{l}\Phi b_{l}+\Phi x_{0}

for any
k\times k
matrix
\Phi
. Thus, if the last row of
\Phi
is
a=(a_{1},a_{2},\dots,a_{k})
, we immediately find that

\Pi_{k}(\Phi x)=\Pi_{k}(\Phi x_{0})=:w

for all
x\in S
by virtue of (
40
). With this observation, we appeal to Lemma
B.1
to produce
\Phi\in\operatorname{Gl}(\mathbb{Z}^{k})
whose last row is
a=(a_{1},a_{2},\dots,a_{k})
and has
\operatorname{det}(\Phi)=\gcd(a_{1},a_{2},\dots,a_{k})
. Since the entries of
a
are relatively prime,
\operatorname{det}(\Phi)=1
which ensures that
\Phi^{-1}
has integer entries. Consequently,
\Phi\in\mathrm{Aut}(\mathbb{Z}^{k})
and our proof is complete.
