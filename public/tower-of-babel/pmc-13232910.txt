Dexmedetomidine versus usual care in adult patients with septic shock receiving vasopressors
Authors: Isaac B Wolfkind, Luke T Geftos, William K Mitchell, Ronan J McOsker, Jose L Puglisi, Baljit S Gill
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBACKGROUNDSeptic shock is characterised by sepsis-induced hypotension refractory to fluid resuscitation efforts. Further treatment often requires mechanical ventilation, typically requiring the use of sedatives to provide analgesia, sedation and anxiolysis. One sedative, dexmedetomidine, is an α2 agonist that has demonstrated potential in improving haemodynamic and clinical outcomes in recent randomised controlled trials. However, support for these claims has been inconsistent across individual studies.OBJECTIVEThis meta-analysis aimed to assess the efficacy of dexmedetomidine in comparison to other sedatives.DESIGNSystematic review of randomised controlled trials with meta-analyses.DATA SOURCESThe search was performed across five electronic databases (Embase, PubMed, Scopus, MEDLINE and clinicaltrials.gov) from inception up to June 2025.ELIGIBILITY CRITERIAWe included randomised controlled studies that utilised adult patients diagnosed with septic shock and receiving sedation with dexmedetomidine or any other. Excluded types of studies included non-RCTs, animal studies, paediatric cases, procedural sedation, trials without reported outcomes and studies that do not report desired outcomes.RESULTSOur meta-analysis included eight randomised controlled trials (n = 702 patients), which revealed that dexmedetomidine significantly decreased the day-2 Sequential Organ Failure Assessment (SOFA) score (P = 0.009) and the incidence of acute kidney injury (AKI) (P = 0.004). Importantly, dexmedetomidine significantly increased the duration of hospital stay (P = 0.01) and the incidence of hypotension (P = 0.04). There were non-significant decreases in 28-day mortality (P = 0.08), duration of mechanical ventilation (P = 0.28), duration of intensive care unit (ICU) stay (P = 0.74), delirium incidence (P = 0.60), bradycardia incidence (P = 0.14), 24-h serum IL-6 levels (P = 0.18), 24-h serum CRP levels (P = 0.44) and 24-h serum lactate levels (P = 0.93).CONCLUSIONDexmedetomidine may improve early organ dysfunction; however, evidence for long-term clinical benefits remains limited.

Introduction

Sepsis impacts millions of lives annually and poses a substantial detriment to health care systems and patient outcomes.1–3 Septic shock is defined as a low systolic (<90 mmHg) or mean arterial blood pressure (MAP) (<65 mmHg) alongside symptomatic signs of hypoperfusion.4 This hypotensive state is accompanied by increased risk for fulminant myocardial dysfunction, acute respiratory distress syndrome, disseminated intravascular coagulation and acute kidney injury (AKI).5–7 Treatment regimens revolve around fluid resuscitation, vasopressor administration and therapy aimed at the infectious source to avoid the development of multiple organ dysfunction syndrome and death.8,9 Clinically, the risk of ICU mortality from septic shock is commonly evaluated through the Acute Physiology and Chronic Health Evaluation ‘II’ (APACHE) and Sequential Organ Failure Assessment (SOFA).1,4,10

Patients experiencing septic shock typically require moderate sedation due to the invasive nature of septic shock management in the intensive care unit (ICU), namely, mechanical ventilation to reduce unnecessary oxygen expenditure (VO2).11 Dexmedetomidine (DEX), a commonly used ICU sedative, has demonstrated potential in improving haemodynamic outcomes in sepsis. DEX is a selective alpha-2 adrenergic agonist and thus exerts a sympatholytic effect to induce reductions in blood pressure and heart rate. Despite the decrease in sympathetic tone, DEX has demonstrated a biphasic and dose-dependent response to haemodynamic parameters in stable, non-septic patients, with hypotension observed at low plasma concentrations and hypertension at high plasma concentrations due to peripheral alpha-2-mediated vasoconstriction.12 Higher doses of DEX, therefore, may offer peripheral vasoconstriction to restore mean arterial pressure (MAP) and central venous pressure (CVP), with the potential to restore haemodynamics and reduce vasopressor requirements. Importantly, the haemodynamic impact of DEX exhibits both inter-individual and intra-individual variability throughout the course of septic illness, probably reflecting evolving pathophysiology and pharmacokinetic–pharmacodynamic alterations during prolonged ICU stay.13,14 In addition, the dynamic analgesic properties of DEX demonstrate an opioid-sparing effect, protecting respiratory stability through diminished concurrent medication requirements.15 Furthermore, DEX may play a role in immunomodulation, with preliminary evidence observing significant decreases in TNF-alpha, IL-1, IL-6, C-reactive protein (CRP) and lactate levels when compared to other sedative agents.16–18

Despite DEX's mechanistic potential, it has yielded mixed results in comparison to other sedatives. For instance, one randomised controlled study (RCT) found that DEX significantly reduced the duration of stay in the ICU in comparison to propofol.19 However, a meta-analysis in 2023 found no difference in the duration of ICU stay but noted significant reductions in the duration of mechanical ventilation.20 In addition, recent articles have demonstrated increased incidences of severe adverse events (SAEs) with α2 agonist usage. As recently as May 2025, a large-scale RCT not only found no superiority of DEX or clonidine to propofol regarding time to extubation, mortality or time to discharge from the ICU, but also noted a higher prevalence of severe bradycardia.21 The same study also reported higher rates of SAEs throughout patients stay in the ICU when utilising DEX.21 Other studies have also reported higher incidences of bradycardia, hypotension or asystole when using DEX or clonidine as sedation strategies compared to usual care.22,23 These recent reports of increased SAE occurrences when using α2 agonists warrant scrutiny of the safety of DEX as a sedative agent.

In addition, there have been several key RCTs that provided additional data on inflammatory and renal function markers. This systematic review and meta-analysis aim to evaluate whether DEX can improve outcomes in ICU patients with septic shock in comparison to other sedatives, referred to henceforth as ‘usual care’, with heterogeneity of propofol, midazolam, fentanyl and ketamine across studies. Inclusion of data from newly available RCTs serves to provide an updated outlook on the safety and efficacy of DEX in the setting of septic shock.

Methods

Protocol and guidance

The study protocol was conducted following Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Review and Meta-Analysis (PRISMA) 2020 guidelines and according to the protocol registered in the PROSPERO database (CRD420251078427).

Data sources and search strategies

A systematic search was conducted using Covidence review software to identify relevant experimental studies evaluating DEX efficacy in patients with septic shock. The search was performed across five electronic databases (Embase, PubMed, Scopus, MEDLINE and clinicaltrials.gov) from inception through June 2025. The search terms included ‘dexmedetomidine’ and ‘septic shock’.

Eligible studies for this review met the PICOS (participants, interventions, comparators, outcomes, and study design) criteria.24 The population of interest included post-operative adult patients (>18 years) diagnosed with septic shock and receiving sedation for mechanical ventilation. The interventions included sedation with DEX at any dosing regimen. The comparators included any non-DEX sedatives used in standard ICU practice, administered per institutional protocols or study-defined sedation strategies. The primary outcome measured was mortality at 28 days. Secondary outcomes were SOFA scores, incidences of delirium, renal outcomes (e.g. serum creatinine, AKI incidence), haemodynamic outcomes (e.g. hypotension, bradycardia, vasopressor dose), inflammatory markers, duration of mechanical ventilation, ICU and hospital length of stay and adverse events. The types of studies included were randomised controlled trials. Excluded types of studies included non-RCTs, animal studies, paediatric cases, procedural sedation, trials without reported outcomes and studies that do not report desired outcomes.

Study screening and selection

Articles were screened using Covidence software. Screening occurred in two phases: title and abstract screening, followed by full-text screening. At both stages, two independent reviewers (IBW, LTG, WKM and RJM) screened each article and categorized them as ‘include’, ‘exclude’ or ‘maybe’. Studies advanced only if both reviewers agreed. Disagreements were resolved by a third reviewer (IBW).

Study appraisal of quality

The risk of bias for each included study was evaluated by the Cochrane Risk of Bias 2 (RoB2) tool.25 Using the updated guidelines, we graded each study by reliability and assessed for factors that could lead to increased bias for each randomised controlled trial. Each study was graded as high risk of bias, some concerns, or low risk of bias. The domains of interest included: bias arising from the randomisation process, bias due to deviations from intended intervention, bias due to missing outcome data, bias in measurement of the outcome and bias in selection of the reported result. Studies were graded independently by two reviewers, and disagreements between reviewers were resolved by consensus. The quality of evidence of each eligible study was assessed through a GRADE analysis and was stratified as high, moderate, low or very low quality.26 Only studies that achieved a high quality of evidence were included.

Given that most included randomised controlled trials did not designate mortality as their primary endpoint, this outcome was considered indirect evidence according to GRADE criteria. Therefore, the certainty of evidence for mortality and biochemical outcomes (e.g. CRP and lactate) was downgraded due to indirectness and outcome prioritisation.

Data extraction

Once the full-text review was completed, extraction occurred on the included articles from text, tables, graphs and other figures via independent, dual review by two reviewers. From the studies included, data were extracted on 28-day mortality, duration of ventilation, ICU and hospital length of stay, delirium incidence, hypotension incidence, bradycardia incidence, SOFA scores (day 2), 24-h serum IL-6 levels, 24-h serum CRP levels and 24-h serum lactate levels. Outcomes of interest that could not be extracted due to insufficient data included vasopressor duration, haemodynamics (e.g. MAP, cardiac output), serum IL-1, TNF-alpha, serum creatinine and renal replacement therapy. If the required information was unclear, attempts to contact the original study authors for missing data were not made. Data provided as the median with interquartile ratio (IQR) or minimum and maximum were approximated to the mean using methods described by Wan et al. (2014).27

Statistical analysis

This meta-analysis synthesised published aggregate data; therefore, a conventional a priori sample-size calculation was not applicable. The number of eligible trials and participants determined the information size. Precision of pooled effects was judged primarily by the width of the 95% confidence interval (CI) for each meta-analytic estimate, consistent with guidance from Cumming (2012).28

Effect measures

For continuous outcomes (e.g. SOFA score, lactate, CRP, ICU/hospital length of stay, duration of mechanical ventilation), we calculated mean differences (MD) with 95% CIs using the inverse-variance method. For dichotomous outcomes (e.g. AKI, bradycardia, hypotension, delirium, 28-day mortality), our primary effect measure was the risk ratio (RR) with 95% CIs. Because some readers and prior syntheses prefer odds ratios (ORs), we also performed OR-based sensitivity analyses; conclusions were based on the RR unless otherwise stated.

Meta-analytic model and heterogeneity

All primary analyses used a random-effects model (DerSimonian–Laird), irrespective of I2, to account for potential between-study variation. We report τ2 (between-study variance) and I2 to describe heterogeneity. A fixed-effect (inverse-variance/Mantel–Haenszel) model was performed as a sensitivity analysis. Where τ2 is estimated to zero, random-effect and fixed-effect results coincide.

Zero cells and sparse data

For dichotomous outcomes with zero events in one arm of a study, we applied a Haldane–Anscombe continuity correction (+0.5 to each cell of that study's 2 × 2 table) before computing log-RRs (and log-ORs in sensitivity analyses). No correction was applied when both arms had non-zero counts.

Missing data

For each randomised trial, we extracted the number (and proportion) of participants with missing primary-outcome data (Appendix 1, Supplement Table S1). Trials explicitly reporting no loss to follow-up for the primary outcome (Ezz Al-Regal et al.16 and Gheibiet al.29) were recorded as having 0% missing data. Where per-protocol exclusions occurred (Cioccari30), we report counts excluded from the randomised set (two per group). Where missingness was not reported,17,18,31,32 we marked ‘not reported’ and used the analysed sample sizes shown in tables/figures.

Small-study effects and publication bias

Because several endpoint-specific syntheses included fewer than 10 studies, we did not construct funnel plots or apply statistical tests for small-study effects (low power and high risk of misinterpretation in small meta-analyses). Instead, we emphasised within-study risk-of-bias assessment.

We conducted a leave-one-out (LOO) influence analysis for every pooled outcome. For each iteration, one study was omitted, and the random-effects model was re-fit; we recorded the pooled effect and 95% CI, τ2 and I2. A study was labelled potentially influential if its omission met any of the following prespecified criteria:

(1)changed the magnitude of the pooled point estimate by at least 20%;

changed the magnitude of the pooled point estimate by at least 20%;

(2)altered statistical significance (95% CI crossed the null when it previously did not, or vice versa); or

Statistical software and thresholds

Analyses were performed in Review Manager (version 5.4.1, Cochrane) using inverse-variance methods for continuous outcomes and Mantel–Haenszel methods for dichotomous outcomes with a DerSimonian–Laird random-effects specification. Two-sided P < 0.05 was considered statistically significant; interpretation emphasised effect sizes and CIs over dichotomous P values.

Out of eight qualified studies, four reported 28-day mortality (n = 425 patients). Meta-analysis of the studies showed no significant difference in 28-day mortality in patients receiving DEX versus those receiving usual care; OR = 0.70 (95% CI, 0.47 to 1.04); P = 0.08 (Fig. 3).

Secondary outcomes

Results comparing the duration of mechanical ventilation (n = 397 patients) in patients receiving treatment with DEX with patients receiving usual care yielded no significant differences; MD = 0.30 (95% CI, −0.25 to 0.86); P = 0.28 (Fig. 4). Duration of ICU stay (n = 574 patients) was not significantly different between the DEX-treated groups and those receiving usual care; MD = −0.36 (95% CI, −2.44 to 1.73); P = 0.74 (Fig. 5). However, duration of hospital stay (n = 263 patients) was found to be significantly increased in the DEX-treated groups compared to those receiving usual care; MD = 1.11 (95% CI, 0.23 to 1.99); P = 0.01 (Fig. 6). Results comparing delirium incidence (n = 155 patients) showed no significant difference between DEX groups and usual care groups; OR = 0.81(95% CI, 0.37 to 1.78); P = 0.60 (Fig. 7). A significant increase in hypotension incidence (n = 155 patients), OR = 2.69 (95% CI, 1.04 to 6.91); P = 0.04 (Fig. 8); and upward trend in bradycardia incidence (n = 155 patients), OR = 2.33 (95% CI, 0.75 to 7.23); P = 0.14 (Fig. 9), were found in DEX patients compared to those receiving usual care. Comparison of day-2 SOFA scores (n = 291 patients) showed significantly higher scores; MD = −0.80 (95% CI, −1.40 to −0.20); P = 0.009 (Fig. 10). In addition, the incidence of AKI (n = 224 patients) was significantly lower in the DEX groups; OR = 0.44 (95% CI, 0.25 to 0.77); P = 0.004 (Fig. 11). There were no significant differences found between DEX-treated and usual care groups in 24-h serum IL-6 levels (n = 152 patients); MD = −31.82 (95% CI, −78.04 to 14.41); P = 0.18 (Fig. 12); 24-h serum CRP levels (n = 114 patients); MD = 12.57 (95% CI, −19.08 to 44.22); P = 0.44 (Fig. 13); and 24-h serum lactate levels (n = 114 patients); MD = 0.02 (95% CI, −0.50 to 0.54); P = 0.93 (Fig. 14).

Discussion

This systematic review and meta-analysis of eight randomised controlled trials of 702 septic shock patients demonstrates that DEX significantly decreased the day-2 SOFA score and incidence of AKI compared to usual care. These findings suggest that DEX may exert organ-protective effects. However, this clinical benefit did not translate into significant reductions in 28-day mortality or duration of stay in the ICU. Despite an improvement in early organ dysfunction and hospital stay, the incidence of hypotension increased significantly, with no significant change in the incidence of bradycardia. Although there was a downward trend in 28-day mortality (P = 0.08), these results suggest that the adverse effects of hypotension and bradycardia have a more significant impact on patient outcomes. In addition, the type of patients included, such as septic shock patients, might require longer care regardless, and may also contribute to the increased hospital stay.

This association with increased adverse outcomes raises concern about potential haemodynamic instability, which may, in turn, increase vasopressor requirements in patients and further hypoperfusion in septic shock patients already experiencing impaired end-organ perfusion. Although our meta-analysis was unable to analyse haemodynamic parameters or vasopressor use, several included studies found no significant difference in vasopressor requirements. Alternatively, some studies found significant increases in MAP and SBP following DEX administration, while others noted no significant changes.

DEX's anti-inflammatory effects remain to be fully elucidated, but one major mechanism of action may be through the downregulation of TNF-alpha via binding to alpha2-adrenoceptors on macrophages.35 This, in turn, decreases IL-6 production and CRP release from hepatocytes. Another hypothesis is an alpha-2 agonist-mediated suppression of cytokine release through the alpha-7 nicotinic acetylcholine receptor (alpha7nAchR)-dependent and toll-like receptor 4 (TLR4)-MyD88-NF-kB pathways.18,36 Other studies have suggested that DEX inhibits hypoxia-inducible factor 1-alpha, a transcription factor responsible for the production of pro-inflammatory cytokines in response to hypoxia and inflammation.37,38 Despite previous animal and human studies showing attenuated inflammation following treatment with DEX, our study found that DEX did not significantly improve 24-h serum IL-6, CRP or lactate levels.17,39,40 This discrepancy may be explained by the extreme upregulation of factors such as TNF-alpha and IL-6 following exposure to circulating endotoxins in septic shock, which may mask the anti-inflammatory effects of DEX.41 In addition, hyperlactatemia is provoked through mechanisms other than catecholamine-induced vasoconstriction.32 Thus, lactate's multifactorial nature prevents its regulation through the singular alpha2-agonist pathway by DEX. Our meta-analysis also aimed to evaluate IL-1, TNF-alpha and ESR. Although some studies independently found that DEX significantly reduced serum IL-1 and TNF-alpha levels, we were unable to analyse these outcomes due to heterogeneity between measurements.17,18,42

Furthermore, DEX failed to significantly reduce the duration of mechanical ventilation or the incidence of delirium. Previous meta-analyses in septic patients have suggested that DEX may instead confer an advantage over conventional sedatives by improving these outcomes.43,44 These differences in results may be due to broader patient data and lighter sedation requirements. In addition, two of the three studies reported delirium using the RASS, while the other used the Confusion Assessment Method for the ICU (CAM-ICU). Although these are validated instruments to evaluate delirium, this lack of uniformity may introduce potential for bias.

The clinical results of our study conflict with prior studies, although our analysis of biochemical markers remains novel. One prior meta-analysis also found that DEX significantly decreased SOFA score and failed to influence 28-day mortality or duration of ICU stay.20 However, they noted that DEX significantly improved the duration of mechanical ventilation, while the present study found no significance. These discrepancies may be attributed to our inclusion of new RCTs. In addition, our statistical analysis utilised a method to convert median to mean, while the previous meta-analysis treated these terms synonymously.

This study contains several potential limitations. Firstly, although the GRADE assessment indicated a high level of certainty, the relatively small number of included RCTs (n = 8) limits power. In addition, several included trials evaluated DEX primarily for haemodynamic or organ function outcomes rather than mortality, making the pooled mortality analysis an indirect measure. Consequently, while mortality was analysed quantitatively, it should be interpreted as a secondary endpoint with limited generalisability.

Secondly, given the small number of patients for some outcomes, studies with a larger number of patients carry a heavy weight (>70%) in the forest plots. This increases the risk of type I errors despite the absence of heterogeneity. Consistent with our protocol, we did not assess small-study effects because less than 10 studies were available; visual inspection of effect sizes versus study precision did not suggest a consistent pattern, but such qualitative impressions are inherently underpowered and should not be over-interpreted.

Thirdly, the cumulative sample size is modest, and event counts are limited in some trials. Without TSA, we cannot formally rule out type I or type II error or quantify the required information size for ‘firm evidence’. Our random-effects model, LOO analyses and prediction interval mitigate, but do not eliminate, this risk.

Due to the low number of studies, we grouped all patients together who received DEX, regardless of dosage or method of administration, hindering investigation into dose-dependent effects of DEX. In addition, the lack of haemodynamic and vasopressor data reduces the identified impact of DEX on AKI and mortality. Thus, future studies with a larger patient pool, controlled doses and haemodynamic measurements are needed to truly assess DEX's efficacy. Furthermore, comparisons across studies may be skewed based on the heterogeneity in DEX administration and baseline patient characteristics, such as severity of illness, APACHE II, SOFA and RASS scores, which directly affect the generalizability of the findings. Lastly, comparing DEX to heterogeneous sedation practices precludes meaningful comparisons to any specific agent. Future directions require specific subgroup analysis for better characterisation of DEX's performance compared to current standards.

Conclusion

DEX treatment may offer early improvements in organ dysfunction, but long-term clinical benefits have yet to be supported. Future large-scale trials are warranted to stratify septic shock patients to determine if any may benefit from DEX sedation strategies.
