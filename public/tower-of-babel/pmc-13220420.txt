Interrupted suture vs continuous suture for primary closure of the common bile duct after laparoscopic common bile duct exploration in patients with choledocholithiasis
Authors: Tailai An, Yan Wang, Lingna Deng, Yawei Wang, Linsen Liu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractPurposeWe performed the present study to compare the interrupted suture technique and the continuous suture technique after laparoscopic common bile duct exploration (LCBDE) for choledocholithiasis.MethodsClinical data from patients with choledocholithiasis who underwent LCBDE were retrospectively collected. Patients were divided into an interrupted suture group and a continuous suture group on the basis of the method used to suture the common bile duct. The preoperative, intraoperative and postoperative parameters of the interrupted suture group were compared with those of the continuous suture group.ResultsThe baseline characteristics of the continuous suture group did not significantly differ from those of the interrupted suture group. Compared with the interrupted suture technique, the continuous suture technique was superior in terms of operation time (P = 0.006). The postoperative outcomes in the continuous group did not significantly differ from those in the interrupted group. Furthermore, we performed a subanalysis limited to LCBDE procedures performed by high-volume surgeons, revealing that it took less time to perform LCBDE with continuous primary suturing (P < 0.001).ConclusionFor patients with choledocholithiasis who underwent LCBDE, the continuous suture technique did not significantly differ from the interrupted suture technique in terms of postoperative complications; however, compared with the interrupted suture technique, the continuous suture technique was associated with a significantly shorter operation time. Thus, the continuous suture technique shows comparable safety and effectiveness for patients with choledocholithiasis who are undergoing LCBDE with a shorter operation time, which, however, should be verified by prospective RCT studies taking surgeon experience into consideration.Keywords: Choledocholithiasis, Interrupted suture, Continuous suture, Primary closure, Laparoscopic common bile duct exploration, High-volume surgeons

Introduction

In China, choledocholithiasis is a common disease of the biliary system [1]. Choledocholithiasis can cause a series of diseases, such as acute cholangitis, acute biliary pancreatitis and sometimes even septic shock or death [2]. Therefore, optimizing the therapeutic plan for patients with choledocholithiasis is highly important.

Currently, choledocholithiasis is treated using either a one-step method, which includes laparoscopic cholecystectomy (LC) and common bile duct exploration (LCBDE), or a two-step method, which includes endoscopic retrograde cholangiopancreatography (ERCP) and subsequent laparoscopic cholecystectomy (or LC and subsequent ERCP) [3–5]. Compared with the two-step method, the one-step method is considered a simpler procedure with a lower failure rate, lower costs, and a shorter hospital stay [6–8]. Furthermore, during the one-step method, surgeons can prevent sphincter of Oddi dysfunction [6–8]. Moreover, two methods are considered suitable for closing the common bile duct after laparoscopic exploration. The first method is conventional T-tube drainage, and the second method is primary closure of the common bile duct. According to several previous studies, primary closure is superior to conventional T-tube drainage in terms of many aspects [9–11].

Either interrupted sutures or continuous sutures can be used to close the common bile duct. Compared with the continuous method, the interrupted method is more commonly adopted. The primary advantage of the interrupted method is that the suture tension can be modulated according to the tension of each knot [12, 13]. However, the interrupted suture technique is time-consuming and taxing, which prolongs the operation [12, 13]. The continuous suture technique is easy to perform and thus shortens the overall operation time [11, 12]. However, in the event of suture failure or knot loosening, a bile fistula may occur. When the CBD is too slender, stricture of the common bile duct caused by continuous suture is another main concern. Thus, the optimal suture method remains unclear and controversial.

To address this controversy, we performed this retrospective study to compare the feasibility and safety (intraoperative and postoperative outcomes) of continuous sutures with those of interrupted sutures and thus provide some suggestions for future clinical practice.

Materials and methods

Study design and study population

The present study involved a single institution, and all the data were retrospectively collected. The inclusion criteria were as follows: the presence of common bile duct stones proven by magnetic resonance cholangiopancreatography (MRCP), a common bile duct diameter ≥ 8 mm, and laparoscopic common bile duct exploration with primary closure of the common bile duct. All the procedures were performed by surgeons at our center, and all the surgeons performed these procedures under the same surgical principles. The exclusion criteria were as follows: ERCP performed among patients with a common bile duct diameter ≥8 mm; incomplete clinical or follow-up information; emergency operation; concomitant acute obstructive suppurative cholangitis; concurrent hepatolithiasis; Mirizzi syndrome; carcinoma of the gallbladder or the bile duct; T-tube drainage; and hematological diseases known to significantly increase the risk of cholelithiasis. All the patients provided written informed consent. The study was conducted in adherence to the Declaration of Helsinki [14].

ERCP or common bile duct exploration

At our center, both laparoscopic common bile duct exploration and ERCP are routine practices. For patients suffering from choledocholithiasis, both ERCP and laparoscopic common bile duct exploration could be performed to extract common bile stones. However, at our center, MRI+MRCP was routinely performed to help us accurately measure the diameter of the common bile duct. Common bile duct exploration was recommended for patients whose common bile duct was ≥ 8 mm, while ERCP was recommended for those whose common bile duct was < 8 mm. ERCP was not the recommended first choice for patients whose common bile duct was ≥ 8 mm, since common bile duct stones could be efficiently removed during common bile duct exploration while efficiently preserving sphincter of Oddi function.

Surgical procedures

All patients were placed in the supine position, and then general anesthesia was induced. After the intraperitoneal pressure reached 12 mmHg or higher, we inserted a 10 mm trocar into the peritoneal cavity from below the umbilicus. A 5 mm trocar was inserted into the abdominal cavity in the right midclavicular region, and a 10 mm trocar was inserted into the abdominal cavity in the subxiphoid area to facilitate manipulation of the choledochoscope. The detailed procedures for laparoscopic cholecystectomy and common bile duct exploration were as follows. Calot’s triangle was carefully dissected, and the cystic artery was cut off after being ligated using Hem-o-lok clips. The cystic duct was ligated using a Hem-o-lok clip but was not cut off since an intact cystic duct could be used as traction during common bile duct exploration and stone extraction. The common bile duct was exposed using both separating forceps and electrocoagulation. A 10–15 mm longitudinal incision was made on the anterior wall of the common bile duct using scissors or electrocoagulation; however, scissors were preferred. A choledochoscope was inserted into the common bile duct to perform a full examination. The gallstones were removed using a wire basket under the guidance of a choledochoscope (EVIS EXERA III, CV-190, Olympus, Beijing, China). The common bile duct was finally sutured with 4–0 or 5–0 PDS II (polydioxanone synthetic absorbable suture II) threads using either the continuous technique or the interrupted technique.

Precautions during choledochotomy

During choledochotomy, we tried our best to minimize the use of monopolar or bipolar electrocoagulation to reduce the risk of biliary fistula and biliary stricture development. During choledochoscopy for common bile duct exploration, normal saline was used to irrigate the common bile duct at low pressure to avoid postoperative cholangitis. We barely needed to drain the fluid within the common bile duct. However, during common bile duct exploration, two pieces of gauze were packed into the hepatorenal recess and foramen of Winslow to minimize the spread of spilled fluid and therefore prevent abdominal infection.

Continuous suture technique

First, at 2 mm from the cut edge on the bile duct, the needle was passed from the exterior to the interior and then removed from the interior to the exterior at 2 mm from the cut edge on the bile duct. The first knot was tied and tightened, and the tail end was cut. Always remember that the knot should be outside the bile duct. The first knot was tied to prevent the thread from slipping. After we tied the first knot, we continuously sutured to a length of 2 mm and a margin of 1 mm. We tied a knot, then we cut the tail end, marking the completion of the continuous suture.

Interrupted suture technique

First, at 2 mm from the cut edge on the bile duct, the needle was passed from the exterior to the interior and then removed from the interior to the exterior at 2 mm from the cut edge on the bile duct. The knot was tied and tightened, and the thread was cut off. Importantly, each knot must remain outside the bile duct. The other stitches were managed in a similar way until the entire incision was closed. As in continuous suture, the length should be 2 mm, and the margin should be 1 mm. All the stitches should be uniform, and all the knots should be tied tightly to avoid bile leakage or hemorrhage. A drainage tube with multiple side holes was placed into the peritoneal cavity, extending from the Winslow foramen to the hepatorenal recess, to observe potential bleeding or bile fistula development. Continuous or interrupted?

The suture method was determined on the basis of multiple factors instead of one single factor. The first factor was the diameter of the common bile duct. Usually, for patients with a smaller common bile duct (between 8 mm and 1 cm), interrupted sutures are recommended. The second factor was the thickness of the common bile duct, and interrupted sutures are preferred to close thin common bile ducts. The third was inflammation of the common bile duct, and interrupted sutures should be used to close an edematous and fragile common bile duct. The fourth factor was surgeon experience. The continuous suture technique should be performed by an experienced surgeon. The last factor was patient choice. At our center, we explain the advantages and disadvantages of each method in detail, and the patient and their trustee make the final decision. However, there are no definite criteria for selecting continuous sutures or interrupted sutures.

4 − 0 or 5 − 0 PDS-II?

At our center, surgeons use 4 − 0 or 5 − 0 PDS-II for primary closure. There are no definite criteria for selecting the suture technique. For sutures with 4 − 0 or 5 − 0 PDS-II, the following factors were considered. The first factor was the diameter of the common bile duct. For patients with a common bile duct diameter > 1 cm, 4 − 0 PDS-II was preferred. For patients with a common bile duct diameter between 8 mm and 1 cm, 5 − 0 PDS-II was preferred. The second factor was the thickness of the common bile duct. For patients with a thick common bile duct, 4 − 0 PDS-II was preferred. For patients with a thin common bile duct, 5 − 0 PDS-II was preferred. The third factor was inflammation of the common bile duct, and interrupted sutures should be used for an edematous and fragile common bile duct. However, these factors should be considered comprehensively when choosing the optimal suture technique.

Senior or junior surgeons?

Surgeons who had at least 5 years, experience in hepatobiliary surgery were defined as senior surgeons. Contrarily, junior surgeons were defined as those whose experience in hepatobiliary surgery was between 3 years and 5 years. Then, we assessed the distribution of LCBDE procedures among surgeons with different experience. Additionally, we calculated how many LCBDE procedures each surgeon performed. Surgeons who had performed no fewer than 30 LCBDE procedures were defined as high-volume surgeons while those had performed fewer than 30 LCBDE procedures were recorded as low-volume surgeons.

Definitions and diagnoses

In this study, a biliary fistula was diagnosed on the basis of drainage fluid with a bilirubin concentration at least 3 times the serum bilirubin concentration on or after postoperative day 3. Common bile duct stricture was diagnosed on the basis of a combination of symptoms, laboratory test results indicating cholestasis, and typical findings on MRCP or ERCP scans. During common bile duct exploration, a choledochoscope was inserted into the common bile duct to perform a full examination and thus ensure that all stones had been extracted.

All patients were followed for at least 6 months postoperatively. Common bile stones identified within 6 months after common bile duct exploration were defined as remnant stones, whereas those identified beyond 6 months were defined as recurrent stones. Intra-abdominal hemorrhage was diagnosed on the basis of a combination of drainage fluid characteristics, a decrease in hemoglobin levels, changes in vital signs, and CT findings. The Clavien–Dindo system was adopted to assess the severity of postoperative complications [15].

Postoperative care

On the first day after surgery, routine blood tests and liver function tests were performed, and patients were instructed to consume low-fat liquid food. On the fourth or fifth day after the operation, the drainage tube was typically removed if less than 50 ml of fluid was present and if no bile fistula or hemorrhage occurred. Afterward, the patients were discharged.

Follow-up

All patients were followed for at least 6 months unless otherwise contraindicated. The specific time points were 3 weeks, 3 months and 6 months after surgery. Patients were instructed to visit the doctor if they experienced any abdominal discomfort. A comprehensive physical examination, routine blood tests, liver function tests and ultrasonography were routinely performed at each visit. Computed tomography (CT) or MRCP was performed only if necessary. The last follow-up was December 31, 2024.

The follow-up included consultation of discomforts, physical examination, ultrasonography examination. When biliary colic, jaundice and cholangitis occurred or abnormal liver function was detected, MRI+MRCP would be recommended to assess whether stricture or stone recurrence existed. However, sometimes, asymptomatic dilation of common bile duct might also been detected by ultrasonography. Then MRI+MRCP would be recommended to assess the cause of common bile duct dilation. However, sometimes, ERCP would also be needed to confirm the diagnosis of common bile duct stricture.

Statistical analysis

Numerical parameters are presented as the mean and standard deviation (SD), while categorical parameters are presented as numbers and percentages. Student’s t test was used to compare numerical parameters, and the χ² test or Fisher’s exact test was used to compare categorical parameters. Statistical Product and Service Solutions 21 (SPSS 21, IBM, Armonk, NY, USA) was used to perform all the statistical analyses mentioned above. Adjusted, effect-size–centered analyses were performed to aid comparisons of key binary outcomes between the two groups. Standardized differences were calculated to assess the balance of baseline characteristics between the two groups. Additionally, adjusted odds ratios/mean differences with 95% confidence intervals (95%CIs) were calculated. All the statistical analyses in this study were two-sided in nature, and P values less than 0.05 were considered to indicate statistical significance.

Results

ERCP instead of common bile duct exploration for patients with a common bile duct diameter ≥ 8 mm

At our center, ERCP was not the first choice for patients with a common bile duct diameter ≥ 8 mm given that during common bile duct exploration, common bile duct stones could be removed and sphincter of Oddi function could be preserved. However, 36 patients with a common bile duct diameter ≥ 8 mm underwent ERCP instead of common bile duct exploration.

These patients underwent ERCP because of poor physical status (n = 10), advanced age (n = 6), giant periampullary diverticulum (n = 5), abnormal coagulation function (n = 3), liver cirrhosis (n = 4), patient preference (n = 5) and severe adhesion caused by pancreatitis (n = 3).

Patient selection and follow-up

A total of 648 patients were ultimately included in this study. The details of the inclusion and exclusion process is shown in Fig. 2. Two hundred ninety-four patients were assigned to the continuous suture (CS) group, and 354 patients were assigned to the interrupted suture (IS) group. Patients were followed for at least 6 months. Patients who were followed for less than 6 months were excluded because common bile duct stones that were identified beyond 6 months after LCBDE were defined as recurrent stones. We calculated the completion rates at 3 weeks, 3 months and 6 months. The completion rates at 3 weeks, 3 months and 6 months were 98.4%, 94.8% and 92.1%, respectively. Similarly, the percentage of patients who were lost to follow-up at 3 weeks, 3 months and 6 months was 1.6%, 5.2% and 7.9%, respectively. The follow-up period ranged between 6.1 months and 130.1 months. Furthermore, the median follow-up period (IQR) was 19.04 (12.73).

In the IS group, 49 patients experienced postoperative complications, i.e., bile fistula (15, 4.2%), CBD stricture (7, 2%), acute pancreatitis (2, 0.6%), pneumonia (7, 2.0%), acute cardiovascular events (4, 1.1%), intra-abdominal hemorrhage (6, 1.7%), urinary retention (2, 0.6%), urinary infection (2, 0.6%), cardiac failure (2, 0.6%) and respiratory failure (2, 0.6%). In the CS group, 31 patients experienced postoperative complications, i.e., bile fistula (13, 4.4%), CBD stricture (6, 2.0%), pneumonia (3, 1.0%), acute cardiovascular events (4, 1.4%), intra-abdominal hemorrhage (1, 0.3%), urinary retention (2, 0.7%), cardiac failure (1, 0.3%) and respiratory failure (1, 0.3%). One patient (0.3%) in the CS group died within 30 days after the operation.

A total of 7 patients experienced postoperative intra-abdominal hemorrhage. Five cases of intra-abdominal hemorrhage were caused by biliary fistula, and 2 cases occurred because of unknown reasons. However, all 7 patients were managed conservatively without reoperation or intervention measures. Since no patients underwent reoperation or intervention, we did not know the exact hemorrhage sites. Among the 28 patients who had biliary fistulas, 21 patients were managed conservatively without ERCP or reoperation, while the remaining 7 patients underwent ERCP/intraductal stent insertion. Among the 13 patients who were diagnosed with remnant stones, stone extraction via ERCP was recommended, and the remnant stones were successfully extracted. Among these 21 patients who had recurrent stones, 15 underwent ERCP, 3 underwent a second common bile duct exploration, and 3 were managed conservatively. A total of 4 patients (3 from the CS group and 1 from the IS group) died within 30 days after the operation. All 4 patients died because of comorbidities related to senior age (88, 85, 83 and 77 years old). Three patients died because of coronary heart disease (88, 85 and 83), and one died because of chronic kidney disease (77).

Follow-up

As mentioned above, all the patients received follow-up instructions from the surgeon. The follow-up period was at least 6 months. The shortest follow-up period was 10 months, and the longest follow-up period was 28 months. Compared with the IS group, the CS group did not significantly differ in terms of CBD stricture rates (P = 0.954) or rates of recurrent common bile duct stones (P = 0.116).

In the IS group, 39 patients experienced postoperative complications, i.e., bile fistula (13, 4.3%), CBD stricture (6, 2%), acute pancreatitis (1, 0.3%), pneumonia (5, 1.7%), acute cardiovascular events (3, 1.0%), intra-abdominal hemorrhage (5, 1.7%), urinary retention (2, 0.7%), urinary infection (1, 0.3%), cardiac failure (2, 0.7%) and respiratory failure (1, 0.3%). Two patients (0.7%) in the IS group died within 30 days after the operation. In the CS group, 18 patients experienced postoperative complications, i.e., bile fistula (7, 3.2%), CBD stricture (2, 0.9%), pneumonia (2, 0.9%), acute cardiovascular events (3, 1.4%), intra-abdominal hemorrhage (1, 0.5%), urinary retention (1, 0.5%), cardiac failure (1, 0.5%) and respiratory failure (1, 0.5%). One patient (0.5%) in the CS group died within 30 days after the operation.

A total of 6 patients experienced postoperative intra-abdominal hemorrhage. Four cases of intra-abdominal hemorrhage were caused by biliary fistula, and 2 cases occurred because of unknown reasons. However, all 6 patients were managed conservatively without reoperation or intervention measures. Since no patients underwent reoperation or intervention, we did not know the exact hemorrhage sites. Among the 20 patients who had biliary fistulas, 14 patients were managed conservatively without ERCP or reoperation, while the remaining 6 patients underwent ERCP/intraductal stent insertion. Among the 10 patients who were diagnosed with remnant stones, stone extraction via ERCP was recommended, and the remnant stones were successfully extracted. Among these 19 patients who had recurrent stones, 14 underwent ERCP, 2 underwent a second common bile duct exploration, and 3 were managed conservatively. A total of 3 patients (1 from the CS group and 2 from the IS group) died within 30 days after the operation. All 3 patients died because of comorbidities related to senior age (88, 85 and 83 years old). These three patients died because of coronary heart disease.

Discussion

For patients who suffer from cholecystolithiasis and concurrent choledocholithiasis, either a one-step method or a two-step method should be adopted. The one-step method is associated with a high success rate, low costs, short hospital stay and good preservation of sphincter of Oddi function [16, 17]. After common bile duct exploration and stone extraction, the optimal method for closing the common bile duct is still under debate. However, primary closure of the common bile duct is superior to conventional T-tube drainage in terms of many aspects [18–20].

Two methods for suturing the open common bile duct have been proposed, i.e., the interrupted suture technique and the continuous suture technique. However, studies comparing interrupted suture and continuous suture techniques are still scarce. Thus, we performed the present study to compare the safety and effectiveness of these two methods for closing the common bile duct. The results of this study revealed that the continuous suture technique was superior to the interrupted suture technique in terms of operation time. Additionally, the continuous suture technique did not significantly differ from the interrupted suture technique in terms of estimated blood loss, blood transfusion, ICU stay, total hospital stay, postoperative hospital stay, remnant stone, recurrent stone, bile fistula, CBD stricture, acute pancreatitis, pneumonia, acute cardiovascular events, intra-abdominal hemorrhage, fever, urinary retention, urinary infection, cardiac failure, respiratory failure or 30-day mortality. Therefore, considering these results, we can conclude that the continuous suture technique is a safe choice and does not impose any additional postoperative risks.

A biliary fistula is the most concerning complication after primary closure of the common bile duct. In the present study, 15 patients (4.2%) in the IS group and 13 patients (4.4%) in the CS group developed a biliary fistula, suggesting that the CS technique is as effective as the IS technique in preventing the formation of a biliary fistula (P = 0.908). Among the 28 patients who had biliary fistulas, 21 patients were managed conservatively without ERCP or reoperation, while the remaining 7 patients underwent ERCP and intraductal stent insertion. Apart from biliary fistula, stricture of the bile duct is another concern. Fortunately, the rates of common bile duct stricture was not significantly different between the CS group and the IS group. Moreover, we compared the CS technique and the IS technique in terms of recurrent stones and found that compared with the IS technique, the CS technique did not significantly differ in terms of recurrent stones.

Some studies have compared the CS technique and the IS technique after laparoscopic common bile duct exploration, and some studies have compared the CS technique and the IS technique for bile duct anastomosis during liver transplantation. In one study, the researchers reported that different suture techniques did not significantly affect the risk of biliary complications such as biliary fistula and bile duct stricture development [19]. The advantages of the interrupted suture technique include better views and few attempts to grasp the posterior wall, making the interrupted suture technique easy to perform. The superiority of the continuous suture technique lies mainly in its shorter operation time. However, due to the retrospective nature of this study, surgeons, experience and case selection might still confound the observed difference in operative time and this observed difference in operative time could not solely attributed to the suturing method itself. We assessed distribution of surgery volume and performed a subanalysis of LCBDE procedures performed by high-volume surgeons and revealed that operation time for the LCBDE plus CS was shorter than that for the LCBDE plus IS. However, prospective randomized controlled trial (RCT) studies are still needed to further verify findings of our study. Therefore, we concluded that as long as suturing or anastomosis can be accurately performed, the suturing technique is not a determining factor for reducing the incidence of biliary complications.

To the best of our knowledge, few studies have investigated this aspect. In 2025, Sun et al. reported similar results to our study [13]. However, in their study, only 120 patients were included [13]. Similarly, in 2022, Wu et al. reported a shorter operation time and less estimated blood loss associated with continuous sutures [12]. Like the study by Sun et al., only a small number of patients (n = 125) were included. Thus, we included more postoperative outcomes, such as fistula, stricture, hemorrhage, and 30-day mortality, in this study. Additionally, we assessed the follow-up duration and the characteristics of recurrent stones. Moreover, we assessed the distribution of surgery volume and performed a subanalysis of LCBDE procedures performed by high-volume surgeons, revealing that operation time for the LCBDE plus CS was shorter than that for the LCBDE plus IS. Compared with previous studies, this study provides some useful suggestions for future clinical practice.

Despite the optimistic results of our study, the shortcomings of this study should not be neglected. First, this study is retrospective in nature, and selection bias was unavoidable. Selection biases existed in patient selection and suture method selection. At our center, for patients with a common bile duct diameter ≥ 8 mm, laparoscopic common bile duct exploration is recommended instead of ERCP to preserve sphincter of Oddi function. However, in some circumstances, ERCP is recommended. In this study, 36 such patients underwent ERCP because of age, frailty, or anatomical complexity. Accordingly, these patients were excluded from our study, which might have biased the surgical cohort toward healthier individuals. However, for a successful laparoscopic common bile duct procedure, a good health status is needed. Additionally, we included a few common parameters to assess health status, such as ASA score and comorbidities. Thus, the biases related to patient selection in this study were acceptable. Moreover, biases related to surgeon experience might also impact conclusions of our study despite the fact we further performed a subanalysis of LCBDE procedures performed by high-volume surgeons. We defined surgeons who had performed no fewer than 30 cases of laparoscopic common bile duct exploration with primary closure, which had not been reported by other studies. Thus results of subgroup analysis of procedures performed by high-volume surgeons might be different from those reported by other studies. Ultimately, there was no definite recommendation regarding the optimal suturing method and biases related to selection of the suturing method exist, which also might affect findings of this study. Thus, prospective RCT studies are needed to further verify our conclusions. Second, this study was performed at a single center, and multicenter studies are needed to identify the optimal suture technique for biliary surgery. Third, the etiology of cholecystolithiasis and choledocholithiasis in China might differ from that in other countries, and the conclusions of this study should not be directly applied to patients in other countries. Fourth, it was impossible for us to compare the suturing time for the CS technique with that for the IS technique since suturing time was not recorded. According to two previous studies, the CS technique was with shorter suturing time than the IS technique [12, 13]. However, some shortcomings of these two studies should be specified. First, they were retrospective studies including only 125 or 120 patients and prospective studies including a larger study population were needed to assess the application of the CS technique. Second, the authors of these two studies did not take surgical experience into consideration when comparing the suturing time for the CS technique with that for the IS technique. Thus, further prospective randomized controlled trial (RCT) studies including a larger study population and taking surgical experience into consideration are warranted. Despite these shortcomings of this study, it still could provide some clinically relevant suggestions given the fact that our study was one of the not so many studies including a large study population and simultaneously taking surgical experience into consideration.

Conclusions

For patients with choledocholithiasis who underwent LCBDE, the continuous suture technique did not significantly differ from the interrupted suture technique in terms of postoperative complications; however, compared with the interrupted suture technique, the continuous suture technique was associated with a significantly shorter operation time. Thus, for patients with choledocholithiasis who undergo LCBDE, the continuous suture technique shows comparable safety and effectiveness with a shorter operation time, which, however, should be verified by prospective RCT studies taking surgeon experience into consideration.
