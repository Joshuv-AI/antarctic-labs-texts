ResPlan: A Large-Scale Vector-Graph Dataset of 17 000 Residential Floor Plans
Mohamed Abouagour, Eleftherios Garyfallidis

Abstract
We introduce ResPlan , a large-scale dataset of 17,000 detailed, structurally rich, and realistic residential floor plans, created to advance spatial AI research. Each plan includes precise annotations of architectural elements (walls, doors, windows, balconies) and functional spaces (e.g., kitchens, bedrooms, bathrooms). ResPlan addresses key limitations of existing datasets such as RPLAN [ 12 ] and MSD [ 11 ] by offering enhanced visual fidelity and greater structural diversity, reflecting realistic and non-idealized residential layouts. Designed as a versatile, general-purpose resource, ResPlan supports a wide range of applications—including robotics, reinforcement learning, generative AI, virtual/augmented reality, simulations, and game development. Plans are available in both geometric and graph-based formats, enabling direct integration into simulation engines and fast 3D conversion. A key contribution is an open-source pipeline for geometry cleaning, alignment, and annotation refinement. Additionally, ResPlan includes structured representations of room connectivity, supporting graph-based spatial reasoning tasks. Finally, we present comparative analyses with existing benchmarks and outline several open benchmark tasks enabled by ResPlan. Ultimately, ResPlan offers a significant advance in scale, realism, and usability, providing a robust foundation for developing and benchmarking next-generation spatial intelligence systems.

Introduction

Floor plans are fundamental abstractions of indoor environments, encoding the spatial organization of rooms, structural elements, and access points. They are critical to a wide range of domains, including architectural design, robotics, indoor navigation, and generative modeling. In recent years, the availability of annotated floor plan datasets has catalyzed progress in tasks such as floor plan generation, image-to-plan translation, and embodied agent navigation. However, existing datasets often exhibit limitations in terms of structural complexity, geometric fidelity, and representation format.

For example, the widely used
RPLAN
dataset
[
12
]
provides over 80,000 annotated floor plans in raster format. Despite its scale, RPLAN predominantly consists of simple, single-unit layouts with mostly rectangular room shapes. Moreover, its representation leads to irregular door symbol geometries and overlapping elements (all depicted in a single image layer), hindering precise geometric analysis and complicating downstream applications. More recently, the Modified Swiss Dwellings (
MSD
) dataset
[
11
]
addressed some of these issues by introducing a collection of multi-unit building layouts comprising 5,300 floor plans and 18,900 distinct apartments. While the MSD corpus provides valuable coverage of multi-unit floor plates, each record represents an entire floor plate encompassing multiple residential units. As a result, researchers aiming to extract self-contained apartment layouts must undertake non-trivial preprocessing. This includes filtering geometries by apartment identifiers and reconstructing missing architectural features—such as party walls and entrance doors—that are implicit or absent once adjacent units are excluded. This requirement introduces significant data-engineering overhead and reduces the dataset’s immediate utility for applications that assume complete, standalone unit-level floor plans.

Beyond RPLAN and MSD, other datasets exhibit their own shortcomings. The
LIFULL Homes dataset

[
7
]
contains millions of floor plan images scraped from a real-estate platform, but only a small fraction of these plans have been fully vectorized or semantically annotated, greatly limiting their utility for direct use in machine learning pipelines. For instance, the Raster-to-Graph system by Hu et al.
[
4
]
produced a vectorized subset of about 10,800 plans from LIFULL, but this remains a tiny portion of the overall corpus. Conversely,
CubiCasa5K

[
6
]
offers 5,000 floor plan images with rich annotations (over 80 object categories including furniture), yet its scale is relatively small for training modern models. Collectively, these limitations highlight the gap between currently available datasets and the needs of applications requiring high-fidelity, unit-level representations of interior spaces.

2
Our Contribution

To address this gap, we introduce
ResPlan
, a new large-scale dataset of high-fidelity residential floor plans designed to support both generative and simulation-based applications. ResPlan comprises 17,000 vector-based floor-plan instances, each capturing rich architectural details and diverse spatial configurations—ranging from standard apartments to complex, multi-wing homes. Unlike previous datasets, ResPlan is created with simulation readiness and structured representation in mind: all layouts are provided in a clean vector-graphic format and include graph-based representations of room connectivity, enabling seamless integration with learning models, virtual environments, and robotic simulators.

In summary, the key contributions of ResPlan are:

•

Scale and Structural Complexity:
ResPlan significantly advances the scale and complexity of available floor plan data. It contains 17k layouts encompassing a broad variety of residential designs, including many with irregular (non-rectangular) room shapes and intricate topologies. The dataset spans a wide distribution of home sizes, including several examples with five or more bedrooms (configurations that are rare or absent in prior datasets), thereby exceeding the diversity of layouts found in earlier benchmarks.

•

Realism and Annotation Quality:
All floor plans are derived from real-world building designs (collected from public real estate listings), ensuring realistic architectural features and proportions. A rigorous cleaning process and manual verification have been applied to guarantee precision—especially for critical elements like wall alignments and door placements. This results in high-fidelity layouts that closely reflect practical design choices.

•

Rich Semantic Annotations:
Each floor plan is annotated with a standardized taxonomy of room types and structural components. We unify inconsistent nomenclature across sources (e.g.,
MasterRoom
or
GuestRoom
are both normalized to
Bedroom
) and clearly distinguish structural elements (e.g., interior vs. exterior walls). These semantic labels are embedded in the vector data, making it straightforward to query functional spaces (bedrooms, kitchens, bathrooms, living areas, balconies, etc.) and structural elements (doors, windows, walls) across all layouts.

•

Graph-Structured Representation:
In addition to geometric floor plan data, ResPlan provides an explicit graph representation for each layout. Rooms are represented as nodes (with attributes such as room type and area), and adjacencies between rooms are captured as typed edges (e.g., door or open-arch connections). This graph structure enables structured reasoning and facilitates the use of graph neural networks, constraint solvers, or symbolic planners on floor plan data (illustrated in Fig.
3
).

•

Simulation-Ready Vector Format and Tools:
ResPlan’s vector-graphic format allows direct conversion to 3D environments without laborious preprocessing. All walls, doors, and windows are represented as clean polygons with consistent thickness, ensuring they can be extruded into watertight 3D meshes. We provide an open-source processing pipeline that converts raw floor plan drawings into this standardized format, enabling researchers to reproduce our data construction. We also propose a suite of benchmark tasks (with evaluation metrics) built on ResPlan to spur future research (see Section
5
).

Related Work

Floor Plan Datasets.

A number of floor plan datasets have been introduced in recent years, varying significantly in scale, annotation detail, and representation format. Among the largest is
RPLAN

[
12
]
, which contains 80,788 densely annotated rasterized floor plans collected from the Asian real estate market. Each example is a
256\times 256
pixel image with multi-channel annotations denoting room types and structural components. RPLAN’s release (alongside an interior layout generation model) catalyzed several subsequent works in data-driven layout synthesis. However, the dataset is limited by its focus on single-family apartments and its raster-based representation, which complicates geometric analysis, 3D reconstruction, and integration with graph-based models. For instance, researchers have had to vectorize the raster images (using tools like Shapely) to perform topological operations or to extract connectivity graphs. Additionally, rooms of the same type are encoded in the same image layer, leading to overlaps (e.g., adjacent door symbols) that make it difficult to disambiguate certain structural elements.

The
LIFULL Homes dataset

[
7
]
provides millions of floor plan images scraped from a Japanese real estate platform. Despite its tremendous scale, only a subset of these plans are available in a structured (vectorized or labeled) format, limiting their immediate utility without extensive preprocessing or automatic vectorization. For example, Hu
et al.
(2024) proposed a
Raster-to-Graph
framework that automatically vectorizes floor plan images; this yielded a cleaned subset of about 10.8k LIFULL plans with semantic labels, but that is still a small fraction of the full collection.
CubiCasa5K

[
6
]
offers 5,000 floor plan images in SVG format, covering over 80 object categories including detailed furniture symbols. While CubiCasa5K emphasizes rendering diversity and annotation richness (beyond just walls and rooms), its relatively small size restricts its usefulness for training large models or learning rare layout patterns.

More recently, the
Modified Swiss Dwellings (MSD)
dataset
[
11
]
introduced greater structural diversity, including 5.3k floor plans comprising 18.9k individual dwelling units. Unlike RPLAN (consisting solely of individual unit layouts), each MSD sample represents an entire building floor with multiple apartments, making MSD a valuable benchmark for multi-unit building modeling. The MSD authors highlight the limitations of single-unit datasets in modeling inter-apartment structures, showing that models trained on simpler datasets underperform when confronted with MSD’s hierarchical, multi-unit configurations. Notably, MSD provides data in multiple modalities—raster images, vector polygons, and graph structures—facilitating both geometric and relational analysis. Its graph representation (with rooms as nodes and typed edges for adjacencies) reflects a growing trend toward topology-centric encodings of spatial environments, a direction also embraced in our proposed ResPlan dataset.

Synthetic and Simulation-Oriented Datasets.

Complementary to real-world datasets, several synthetic datasets have been developed to support algorithmic generation and simulation.
Architext

[
2
]
procedurally generates diverse floor plans using CAD-like programmatic scripts, offering clean annotations and controlled geometric variation. While synthetic datasets like Architext are valuable for covering corner cases and generating unlimited samples, they may lack the nuanced constraints and patterns found in human-designed spaces. In the domain of embodied AI,
ProcTHOR

[
1
]
generates interactive 3D house environments procedurally, supporting large-scale training of virtual agents. ProcTHOR enables scalable multi-room simulations, though its algorithmically generated layouts can deviate from realistic architectural design principles. In contrast, ResPlan is grounded in real residential plans sourced from public architectural and real-estate documents, offering both realism and structural consistency while remaining suitable for simulation and graph-based applications.

Graph-Based Representations.

An increasing number of floor plan generation methods incorporate graph-based representations to model spatial relationships. These methods typically encode rooms as nodes (with attributes such as function or area) and adjacencies as typed edges. Examples include
House-GAN++

[
8
]
and
Graph2Plan

[
3
]
, where layout generation is conditioned on an input graph structure to improve plausibility and diversity. Graph2Plan in particular extracts room adjacency graphs from RPLAN images and uses a retrieve-and-adjust pipeline to synthesize new layouts. The MSD dataset’s adoption of
networkx
-based graphs, and our similar approach in ResPlan, illustrate the field’s shift toward structured, relational data formats. Such representations enable metric-based analyses (e.g., connectivity or centrality measures) and facilitate applying graph neural networks or symbolic reasoning for layout optimization and validation.

The emergence of diffusion-based layout generators (e.g.,
HouseDiffusion

[
10
]
and
GSDiff

[
5
]
) further underscores the need for high-quality training data. ResPlan’s realism and structured format make it well-suited to such state-of-the-art generative models. By addressing gaps in realism, structure, and modality, ResPlan serves as a versatile benchmark for both architectural layout generation and spatial intelligence tasks.

Dataset Description

Data Collection and Processing.

The ResPlan dataset was constructed through a multi-stage pipeline that converts raw floor plan drawings into clean, annotated vector representations. The floor plans were sourced from publicly available real-estate listing documents, which often provided architectural drawings in CAD or PDF formats. We developed a custom parser to extract polygonal geometries and textual labels from these drawings. During preprocessing, we applied a series of geometric cleaning steps: wall segments were snapped into alignment (ensuring that walls that should be colinear or orthogonal were properly merged or intersected), small gaps or overlaps in polygons were corrected, and spurious artifacts (e.g., duplicate lines or tiny sliver polygons) were removed. We also enforced a uniform wall thickness across each layout to achieve consistency when differentiating interior and exterior boundaries.

Annotation refinement was a critical part of the pipeline. We unified diverse labeling conventions across sources into a consistent semantic schema. For example, source-specific labels like
Master Bedroom
or
Guest Room
were normalized to a standard
bedroom
category, and all textual annotations were mapped to one of the predefined room types or structural elements. We clearly distinguished structural components by type: for instance, interior partition walls versus shared walls between units were identified using simple heuristics (a shared wall is typically an interior wall that has another dwelling unit on its opposite side and contains no window openings). Importantly, we ensured that every door polygon aligns with an opening in the adjacent wall polygon; this was verified and adjusted both algorithmically and through manual inspection. A focused manual verification pass was conducted to check each floor plan for errors, with particular attention to door placement and connectivity integrity. This quality-control step corrected any remaining misalignments or mislabeled spaces (e.g., occasionally merging a very small closet with an adjacent room if it was below a minimal area threshold).

The outcome of this pipeline is a set of consistently structured floor plan files with high geometric and semantic fidelity. The entire data processing pipeline (implemented in Python) is open-sourced alongside the dataset to ensure reproducibility and to allow researchers to extend or modify the data construction process.

Data Format and Schema.

Each ResPlan sample is stored as a single JSON-serializable dictionary whose keys are semantic categories (e.g.,
bedroom
,
bathroom
,
wall
,
door
, etc.) and whose values are polygon geometries (
Polygon
or
MultiPolygon
objects) in plan-view coordinates. This uniform schema guarantees that all records can be parsed with a single, consistent code path. If a category is absent from a particular layout, its value is an empty geometry (
MULTIPOLYGON EMPTY
), so downstream pipelines never need special-case checks for missing keys. The dictionary preserves the original polygon ordering exported from the CAD parser, and we exploit this stable index to create a one-to-one mapping between room polygons and graph vertices when constructing the adjacency graph (described below).

Spatial Primitives.

Rooms and exterior spaces are captured with sub-centimeter fidelity as individual polygons grouped by function. Primary residential space types—
bedroom
,
bathroom
,
kitchen
, and general
living
areas—are included whenever they exist in the source drawing. Secondary functional areas such as
balcony
,
storage
,
parking
,
pool
, and
stair
appear when applicable and default to empty geometry otherwise. We do not explicitly label extremely small closets or utility nooks as separate spaces; such tiny areas are either merged into adjacent rooms or left unlabeled to avoid ambiguity. A dedicated
inner
polygon is provided that encloses all inhabitable interior space (the footprint inside all interior walls), while an optional
land
polygon records the parcel or lot boundary. These
inner
and
land
(or
garden
) layers enable users to compute metrics like total indoor area or site coverage without requiring external GIS data. (In our JSON schema,
garden
is used as a synonym for
land
in cases where a private yard area is delineated outside the building envelope.)

Structural Elements.

Walls, doors, and windows are represented as coplanar polygons with a common thickness (stored in a metadata field
wall_depth
). Because all structural elements share the same depth, these layers can be geometrically combined or subtracted without artifacts—supporting tasks such as automatically “sealing” a unit by filling in door gaps, or replacing external window openings with solid wall segments to simulate different facade configurations. Standard interior and exterior doors are collectively stored under the
door
category; the primary entrance door of the unit is additionally isolated as
front_door
to simplify scenarios (e.g., in robotics) that require identifying a unique entry/exit point. Each window (or glass sliding door) is represented as a polygon in the
window
layer, likewise matching the wall depth.

Neighboring Units.

Walls shared with an adjacent apartment (party walls) are represented in a separate
neighbor
layer. These shared walls are identified using simple architectural heuristics: for example, a wall segment is classified as
neighbor
if it is an interior wall that has another dwelling unit on its opposite side (as indicated by the source metadata or by the absence of an external boundary at that location). By definition,
neighbor
walls never contain window openings. In combination with the
inner
polygon, this allows users to algorithmically distinguish between exterior perimeter walls and interior partition walls using boolean set operations alone (e.g., subtracting
neighbor
segments from the full interior boundary yields the true exterior facade outline).

Meta-data.

Every floor plan record includes additional metadata attributes. These include a
unitType
label (categorical values such as
Apartment
,
Villa
,
Townhouse
,
IndependentHouse
, etc.), which describes the type of residential unit; an
area
value indicating the total internal floor area in square meters (gross floor area, GFA); and a numeric
id
or
planID
corresponding to the original source file. These fields enable filtered selections or stratified analyses—for instance, one can subset the dataset to only large single-family homes vs. compact apartments, or condition a generative model on the
unitType
attribute.

Graph Construction.

As a final step of preprocessing, we convert each floor plan layout into a room connectivity graph. In this graph, each node corresponds to a room polygon (with stored attributes such as the semantic label, polygon centroid coordinates, and room area), and each edge indicates a direct adjacency relationship between two rooms. We classify edges by connection type:
door
edges connect rooms that share a doorway;
arch
edges connect rooms separated by a large open passage (without a door); and
shared-wall
edges indicate two rooms that are adjacent with a wall between them but no direct opening. Because the polygon ordering in the JSON is fixed and consistent, users can easily cross-reference nodes in the graph with the exact polygon geometry in
O(1)
time—without needing complex spatial queries or geometric matching. This dual representation means algorithms can seamlessly fuse Euclidean features (from geometry) with relational features (from the graph).

These design choices make
ResPlan
immediately usable in geometry-centric workflows, graph-neural-network pipelines, and 3D simulation engines without costly intermediate processing.

ResPlan’s collection encompasses a wide range of home sizes and layouts. Table

The majority of floor plans (nearly 50%) contain two bedrooms, but a substantial number of larger units (616 with four bedrooms, and 29 with five or more) are present—configurations that are rare or absent in most prior datasets. This diversity in room counts, along with variations in other aspects (such as the presence of balconies, internal stairs for multi-story units, etc.), contributes to making ResPlan a robust and challenging dataset for spatial AI tasks.

5
Potential Applications and Benchmark Tasks

ResPlan’s rich, structured, and simulation-ready nature supports a wide range of downstream applications, and it enables the formulation of new benchmark tasks for spatial AI. Below, we outline several key applications and propose four representative tasks that leverage ResPlan’s unique combination of geometry, semantics, and topology. For each task, we discuss possible evaluation metrics and relevant baseline approaches. We will also provide baseline implementation code for these tasks to facilitate consistent benchmarking on ResPlan.

5.1
Applications

•

Generative Architectural Design:
ResPlan enables training and evaluation of advanced generative models (e.g., GANs, diffusion models, graph-based models) to synthesize realistic floor plans. The data supports conditional generation scenarios such as producing a layout to fit a given building boundary, fulfilling a desired room program, or following a provided adjacency graph or textual description.

•

Spatial Reasoning and AI:
The dual geometric and graph representations facilitate high-level spatial inference tasks. Examples include automatic room-type recognition from unlabeled geometry, functional consistency checks (e.g., verifying that kitchens connect to dining areas), evaluating navigation graph properties (like shortest path lengths for emergency egress), or predicting how people would move through the space.

•

Robotics and Embodied AI:
ResPlan’s vector format allows direct extrusion into 3D environments, enabling realistic simulation for robotics tasks. An embodied agent or robot can be placed in a house layout converted from ResPlan to practice navigation, search-and-rescue, or indoor mapping tasks. Because the data is real and structurally detailed, policies trained in these layouts could transfer better to real-world indoor environments.

•

VR/AR and Game Development:
Developers can rapidly populate virtual worlds with structurally rich residential interiors by using ResPlan layouts as templates. This reduces manual design overhead in VR/AR training scenarios and accelerates level design in games. The clean vector nature of the layouts makes it straightforward to adapt or stylize them for different aesthetics.

5.2
Benchmark Tasks

We propose four example tasks that can be constructed as benchmarks using ResPlan. These tasks are designed to bridge geometric understanding, semantic interpretation, and topological reasoning:

Task 1: Constrained Floor Plan Generation.

Objective:
Generate a valid floor plan layout given high-level constraints. For example, the input may specify a fixed building boundary polygon, a desired list of rooms (with counts and types), and optionally an adjacency graph or placement constraints for certain rooms. The goal is to produce a floor plan (as vector polygons) that satisfies these constraints.

Evaluation:
We evaluate both constraint satisfaction and realism. Constraint satisfaction can be measured by geometric overlap (e.g., boundary IoU between the generated layout and the target boundary), room count and type accuracy, and graph edit distance comparing the connectivity of the generated layout to the input adjacency requirements. Realism and diversity can be assessed via metrics like the Fréchet Inception Distance (FID) between generated layouts and the real dataset distribution, as well as qualitative architectural plausibility checks. Relevant baseline approaches include data-driven models such as House-GAN++
[
8
]
, Graph2Plan
[
3
]
, and modern diffusion-based generators like HouseDiffusion
[
10
]
, which can be adapted to this conditional generation setting.

Task 2: Semantic Room Labeling.

Objective:
Infer the semantic category of each room given an unlabeled floor plan geometry. In this task, the input is a floor plan represented only by its geometry (e.g., walls delineating spaces), without any room type labels. The output should assign a room type (bedroom, kitchen, bathroom, etc.) to each distinct room region.

Evaluation:
Since ground-truth labels are available in ResPlan, standard classification metrics can be used. We report per-room labeling accuracy, as well as micro-averaged precision, recall, and F1-score across all room instances. If evaluating on a pixel or area basis, mean IoU for each room class can also be computed. Baseline methods for this task could include rule-based heuristics (e.g., using room size and connectivity to guess function), convolutional neural network models treating it as an image segmentation or object detection problem, or graph neural network classifiers operating on the room adjacency graph (where the task becomes node classification). Prior work has shown that graph neural networks can achieve around 80% accuracy on similar room-classification tasks using simpler floor plan data
[
9
]
, indicating ample room for improvement with ResPlan’s richer information.

Task 3: Plan-Graph Bidirectional Translation.

This is a two-part task exploring the correspondence between floor plan geometry and its graph representation:
(a)
Plan-to-Graph:
Given a floor plan (geometry with no connectivity info), extract the room adjacency graph, including classifying edge types (door, arch, etc.).
(b)
Graph-to-Plan:
Generate a plausible floor plan layout given an input room adjacency graph (with node labels and optionally node areas).

Evaluation:
For plan-to-graph (graph extraction), we measure the correctness of the derived graph structure against the ground-truth graph from ResPlan. This can be quantified by graph edit distance (GED) or simpler metrics like precision/recall on edges (i.e., did the method correctly detect adjacency between every pair of rooms that share a door?). Edge-type classification accuracy is also evaluated. For graph-to-plan (graph-conditioned generation), evaluation can mirror Task 1: we assess how well the generated layout’s topology matches the input graph (e.g., comparing the input graph to the graph extracted from the generated plan via GED), as well as the geometric plausibility of the output. Overlap metrics (IoU) can be used if a reference layout exists (in cases where the input graph actually came from a specific real plan). Baseline methods include Graph2Plan
[
3
]
for the graph-to-plan direction, and straightforward geometric algorithms (or learned CNN/GNN models) for plan-to-graph extraction. Notably, ResPlan provides ground-truth pairings of plans and graphs, enabling supervised training for both directions of this translation task.

Task 4: 3D Layout Reconstruction.

Objective:
Convert a 2D floor plan into a plausible 3D indoor environment model. Given ResPlan’s 2D vector layout, the goal is to produce a 3D reconstruction with walls extruded to the correct height, doors and windows modeled as openings, and a ceiling/floor added—resulting in a closed 3D mesh suitable for simulation.

Evaluation:
If ground-truth 3D models were available, one could use volumetric IoU or mesh-to-mesh distance metrics to evaluate reconstruction fidelity. In the absence of true 3D ground truth for each plan, we focus on evaluating the quality and realism of the generated 3D model. Important factors include physical correctness (e.g., walls are vertical and aligned, no gaps between walls and ceiling), watertightness of the mesh, and preservation of the 2D layout structure. Automated checks (such as verifying that each room is properly enclosed) can serve as proxies. A baseline approach is a rule-based extrusion: extrude all walls to a default height (e.g., 3 m), cut out door/window openings as per the 2D plan, and cap the structure with a flat roof. More sophisticated approaches might predict ceiling heights or multi-level structures if additional cues are given. This task validates that ResPlan’s 2D data can be reliably used to generate 3D environments for simulation.

Discussion

ResPlan represents a significant step forward in providing large-scale, high-quality data for spatial AI research. Its core strengths—the substantial number of detailed, complex unit-level floor plans, the dual geometric (vector) and topological (graph) representations, rich semantic annotations, and the focus on simulation-readiness—collectively address critical limitations found in prior datasets. The provision of an open-source data processing pipeline further enhances its value by promoting transparency and enabling reproducibility. By offering data that are both realistic and immediately usable, ResPlan lowers the barrier to entry for developing and evaluating complex spatial models, and it has the potential to accelerate progress in areas like generative architectural design, embodied AI, and robotic navigation.

However, like any dataset, ResPlan has certain limitations that suggest avenues for future work:

•

Single-Floor Focus:
ResPlan currently consists of individual floor plans, each typically representing a single-story dwelling unit (e.g., one apartment or one floor of a house). The dataset does not explicitly link multiple floors of the same building or provide multi-story building assemblies. This focus limits direct applicability to tasks requiring multi-floor reasoning or complete building generation. While multi-level residential layouts are less common in the source data (and some multi-story houses are represented as separate plans for each floor), extending ResPlan to capture vertical connections (stairs/elevators) between floors of the same unit is an area for future expansion.

•

Implicit 3D Information:
Although ResPlan’s 2D layouts are “simulation-ready” for 2.5D extrusion, the dataset does not include explicit 3D features such as ceiling heights (beyond assuming a default height), detailed roof structures, or information about window sill and door heights. Generating a full 3D BIM (Building Information Model) or detailed interior rendering from the floor plan alone requires additional assumptions or external data. Future work could enrich ResPlan by incorporating typical height metadata or associating floor plans with 3D scans to provide ground-truth 3D information.

•

Lack of Interior Object Annotations:
We have intentionally focused ResPlan on the architectural structure and primary spatial compartments (rooms). The dataset does not annotate furniture, appliances, or other interior objects. This choice ensures consistency and clarity of the structural data, but it means ResPlan is not directly suitable for tasks that require detailed furniture placement or interior arrangement (in contrast to a dataset like CubiCasa5K, which includes furniture layouts). Incorporating furniture and finer interior details—either by augmenting ResPlan with synthetic furniture placement or by linking it with a complementary interior dataset—is a possible direction to broaden its utility.

Dataset Availability.

ResPlan is being released for public use as a community resource. Table
4
summarizes the key components provided. The dataset of 17k floor plans (geometry and annotations) is available in a compact JSON format, accompanied by rendered image previews for quick visualization. For each plan, we also provide a derived room adjacency graph (as a JSON or NetworkX object) representing the connectivity structure. In addition, the complete data processing pipeline used to create ResPlan—from parsing the raw plans to cleaning and annotation—is open-sourced as a Python package. Documentation and usage examples are included to guide users in loading the data, converting it to other formats, or applying the benchmark tasks described in this paper. All data and code are released under a permissive open-source license, allowing free use for research and development.

Conclusion

This paper introduced
ResPlan
, a large-scale dataset of 17,000 high-fidelity, structurally complex, and semantically rich residential floor plans. Provided in both clean vector and graph-based formats, ResPlan is designed to overcome key limitations of existing floor plan datasets, offering an immediately usable resource for a diverse range of applications in spatial AI—including generative architectural design, robotics and navigation, spatial reasoning, and virtual environment creation. The dataset is distinguished by its realism, detailed annotations, the complexity of its layouts, and an accompanying open-source processing pipeline that ensures data quality and consistency.

We detailed the dataset’s characteristics, its collection and refinement process, and proposed several benchmark tasks to spur further research and facilitate standardized evaluation. Quantitative comparisons (see Table
1
and Table
2
) underscore ResPlan’s advantages in scale and diversity, while qualitative examples illustrate its richness. Our discussion highlighted ResPlan’s strengths and also acknowledged current limitations, which in turn suggest directions for future work (such as multi-floor data and enhanced 3D or interior-detail annotations).

ResPlan is publicly released, including the full dataset and processing code, to encourage broad adoption. We hope that ResPlan will serve as a foundational resource for the community—enabling the development, evaluation, and benchmarking of next-generation spatial intelligence algorithms. By providing a platform that combines realistic complexity with structured representations, ResPlan has the potential to foster significant advancements in our ability to computationally understand, reason about, and generate complex indoor environments.
