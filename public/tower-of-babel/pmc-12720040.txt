Advances in pathogen detection of Pneumocystis jirovecii pneumonia
Authors: Lu Bai, Yuanyuan Li, Yantong Li, Chengping Hu, Bingrong Zhao
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Highlights•Comprehensive summary of P. jirovecii detection methodologies, including culture, cytology, serology, and nucleic acid amplification testing.•Systematic analysis of clinical specimen utility and constraints, delineating advantages, limitations, and applicable scenarios for diverse sample types.•Multidimensional comparison of diagnostic techniques, synthesizing performance characteristics (sensitivity, specificity, scope) to guide individualized clinical decision-making and optimize diagnostic efficiency.Key Words: Pneumocystis jirovecii pneumonia, Pathogen detection, Cytological test, Serological test, Nucleic acid amplification test

Introduction

Pneumocystis jirovecii pneumonia (PJP) is an opportunistic fungal infection caused by alveolar invasion of P. jirovecii,1 initially prevalent in HIV-infected individuals.2 With increasing use of chemoradiotherapy, immunotherapy, and solid organ/hematopoietic stem cell transplantation, PJP incidence in non-HIV populations has risen steadily.3,4 These patients often present with more acute onset, non-specific symptoms, heterogeneous radiographic manifestations, and higher co-infection risks,5 exhibiting over 80% severe disease rates6 and near-100% mortality with delayed treatment.7 Early diagnosis and prompt anti-Pneumocystis therapy significantly reduce PJP-related mortality. Thus, precise early identification is critical for initiating targeted treatment and improving survival outcomes.

Multiple pathogen detection techniques, including culture, cytology, serology, and nucleic acid amplification tests (NAAT), utilizing diverse specimens have been applied to P. jirovecii identification. Each technique carries distinct advantages and limitations (Table 1). Context-specific selection of specimens and assays enhances diagnostic efficiency. This review comprehensively analyzes current P. jirovecii detection techniques, comparing their clinical utility to guide personalized diagnostic decision-making.

Detection specimens

Microscopic identification of P. jirovecii cysts or trophozoites in qualified lower respiratory tract specimens remains the gold standard for PJP diagnosis.8 While sputum sampling is safe and repeatable with 98.6% specificity, its sensitivity is limited to 50% due to oropharyngeal contamination.9 Most PJP patients present with dry cough, which often yields inadequate samples. Hypertonic saline-induced sputum can improve specimen quality, especially in patients with scanty sputum, yet its sensitivity remains operator- and patient-dependent, with additional risks of hemoptysis and pathogen transmission.10 Bronchoalveolar lavage fluid (BALF) is considered the optimal diagnostic specimen,11 with sensitivity and specificity exceeding 90%.9,12 A meta-analysis demonstrated that among HIV-infected patients with PJP, BALF maintained a diagnostic yield of 51% to 65%, even when sputum tests were negative.11 Clinical practice requires considering equipment availability, technical expertise, patient tolerance, and complications risks. When bronchoscopy is contraindicated, non-directed bronchial lavage with respiratory support is a viable alternative.13 Though transbronchial or lung biopsies offer high sensitivity, their invasiveness confines utility primarily to animal experiments.12 Upper respiratory specimens (e.g., oral washes,14 nasopharyngeal aspirates15) and blood16 have been reported but show limited detection rates and serve only as ancillary options, ideally combined with non-invasive respiratory samples.

Host immune status, timing of specimen collection relative to symptom onset, standardization of sampling procedures, specimen storage conditions, and timeliness of transport all critically affect the pathogen detection rates. Clinicians should individualize specimen selection based on clinical context while ensuring sample integrity and prompt processing to maximize detection sensitivity.

Detection techniques

Culture

Early attempts to culture P. jirovecii using co-culture systems with WI38 cells, human embryonic fibroblasts, A549 alveolar epithelial cells, or chicken embryo lung cells17, 18, 19 failed to achieve sustained axenic growth. The absence of a reliable in vitro culture system remains the primary obstacle for P. jirovecii propagation. In 2014, Schildgen et al.20 reported successful P. jirovecii cultivation in a three-dimensional air-liquid interface model using BALF specimens and CuFi-8 airway epithelial cells, a differentiated pseudostratified airway epithelial cell line. However, subsequent studies could not replicate this system,21 potentially due to low microbial burden, antibiotic residues, or interference from non-pathogenic components (e.g., host cells, cytokines) in BALF.22 Furthermore, the fundamental obstacles to in vitro culture of P. jirovecii focus on the biological characteristics of the strain and the challenges of existing technologies. The cell wall of P. jirovecii is rich in β-glucans but deficient in ergosterol, rendering conventional fungal culture media inadequate for supporting its cell wall synthesis and maintenance, and P. jirovecii requires sexual reproductive cycle for survival, which occurs exclusively in vivo. Moreover, P. jirovecii exhibits obligate host specificity, replicating only within the human alveoli. Its entire life cycle depends on the specific alveolar microenvironment and immune status of the host. Current in vitro systems are difficult to replicate the growth environment and the complex regulation of the host immune system.23, 24, 25 Thus, developing optimized P. jirovecii culture methodologies represents an unmet need in clinical.

Cytology

Conventional staining microscopy

P. jirovecii remains unculturable in routine in vitro systems. Direct visualization of cysts or trophozoites via staining constitutes definitive diagnostic evidence.8,25 Established staining methods include trophozoite-targeting (e.g., Giemsa, Wright-Giemsa, Diff-Quik, and Papanicolaou's stain) and cyst-targeting, such as Grocott methenamine silver (GMS) and toluidine blue O stains are summarized in Table 2. GMS demonstrates high specificity for cysts and is recommended as the preferred detection method, albeit with limited sensitivity.26, 27, 28 While conventional staining is cost-effective and widely accessible across healthcare settings,5 it suffers from prolonged processing time,29 variable sensitivity (31%–90%),5,27,30 and susceptibility to variables including fungal burden, specimen type, technical expertise, and prior anti-Pneumocystis prophylaxis.5,28,31 These limitations are exacerbated in non-HIV immunocompromised hosts, where lower cyst density and higher inflammatory cell count elevate false-negative rates.32,33 Consequently, staining alone is not recommended for PJP screening in critically ill patients. A sequential approach using Giemsa stain for initial screening followed by GMS confirmation of characteristic cysts is advised. Repeat testing is warranted for high-suspicion cases with initial negative results.

Immunofluorescence microscopy

Immunofluorescence microscopy (IFM) demonstrates superior sensitivity (55%–90%) compared to conventional staining.33,34 Its diagnostic threshold requires only visual confirmation of cysts, enabling results within 40 minutes,35 while IFM enhances detection sensitivity (48%–100%) over conventional methods,14,36 it requires high equipment costs for fluorescence microscopes and has lower specificity than traditional staining, elevating the false-positive rates.2 Given this profile, IFM is recommended as an initial high-sensitivity screening tool, with positive results requiring confirmatory testing to mitigate misdiagnosis risks.

Flow cytometry

Flow cytometry (FC) automates the detection of P. jirovecii by using fluorescently labeled antibodies against whole-cell or specific antigens, thereby confirming P. jirovecii presence. This method reduces subjectivity associated with manual microscopy, shortens detection time, and enhances sensitivity.37,38 Notably, FC enables P. jirovecii detection in clinical specimens with low fungal burdens, overcoming limitations of microscopy that depend on fungal load.39, 40, 41 Additionally, FC assesses microbial viability, facilitating applications in antimicrobial susceptibility testing, monitoring therapeutic responses, and drug screening.42,43 Specialized viability stains can further exclude live cells to evaluate P. jirovecii vitality in samples.44 However, FC's reliance on fluorescent labeling makes it susceptible to non-specific antibody-antigen binding and variable dye affinity for target biomolecules.45 Instrument maintenance, including preheating, laser calibration, and cleaning after each use, adds operational complexity.46 Consequently, FC remains largely confined to preliminary P. jirovecii research in immunology and microbiology laboratories and is not yet recommended for routine clinical diagnosis of PJP.

Serology

Antigen-antibody assays

Multiple serological antigen-antibody assays, including direct fluorescent antibody assays, indirect immunofluorescence, enzyme immunoassays, and enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay (ELISA), have been applied to detect P. jirovecii ELISA is the most widely used method. Native antigens (e.g., Meu10) and recombinant antigens derived from the major surface glycoprotein (MSG) successfully identify P. jirovecii-pecific immunoglobulin antibodies.14 P. jirovecii-infected hosts produce Meu10 antibodies that circulate in peripheral blood,47 and anti-MSG immunoglobulin G (IgG) or immunoglobulin M (IgM) antibodies against P. jirovecii's cell wall component MSG.48 Recombinant MSG fragments spanning the full gene length facilitate antibody detection.

Compared to cytological methods, serological antigen-antibody assays offer shorter turnaround times and higher sensitivity. However, their clinical utility is limited by factors such as immune status and antibody kinetics. Single antibody titer elevation lacks diagnostic significance, as prior infection or subclinical exposure can produce antibodies.14 A 4-fold increase or seroconversion is more indicative.49,50 Yet studies show 4-fold titter increases in 10% of healthy individuals within a year,51 questioning the value of paired acute-convalescent serum testing. Antibody production is delayed (peaking 3–4 weeks post-improvement), limiting utility in acute cases.2,52 Thus, serological antigen-antibody assays are primarily used in epidemiological studies and as supplementary confirmatory tests.

Serological markers

Serum (1,3)-β-D-glucan

Serum (1,3)-β-D-glucan (BDG), a key component of the P. jirovecii cell wall, is released into the bloodstream during infection, enabling non-invasive detection.48 The 2016 European Conference on Infections in Leukemia recommends serum BDG for PJP diagnosis and treatment decision-making.33 Meta-analyses confirm high sensitivity (91%–98%) and negative predictive value,53, 54, 55 supporting its role in ruling out PJP,5,25 particularly in low-risk patients.56 Higher sensitivity is observed in HIV-infected vs. non-HIV-infected individuals (86% vs. 94%).56,57 Elevated BDG correlates with disease progression and poor prognosis,58,59 though this relationship remains unconfirmed.60, 61, 62

False negatives and low specificity (79%–86%) are key limitations.63 BDG is pan-fungal, with elevations seen in other fungi (e.g., Candida, Aspergillus),56 and influenced by specimen contamination, hemodialysis, intravenous immunoglobulins/albumin, bacteremia, and drug use.64, 65, 66, 67 Truncation values (e.g., < 80 pg/mL for exclusion, > 400 pg/mL for diagnosis) have been proposed,68,69 but lack standardization due to variability in study cohorts and techniques.70,71 Thus, BDG should be interpreted with clinical pretest probability, and positive results require confirmatory testing with lower respiratory tract specimens.5,54

Lactate dehydrogenase

Lactate dehydrogenase (LDH), released from damaged lung tissue, increases in PJP patients,72 reflecting inflammation and disease severity.5,73 It shows high sensitivity (66%–91%) and predicts poor prognosis during treatment.74,75 However, LDH is non-specific (36%–52%), as elevations occur in various inflammatory and destructive conditions.14,72,73 Combining BDG and LDH (e.g., BDG ≥ 400 ng/L and LDH ≥ 350 U/L) improves specificity (84%), though cutoff values vary by method and instrument.63

Other markers

Reduced S-adenosylmethionine (SAM), elevated Krebs von den Lungen-6 (KL-6), and increased interleukin-8 have been studied76, 77, 78 but show poor sensitivity and specificity.77, 78, 79 Combining BDG and KL-6 may achieve higher sensitivity (94.3%) and specificity (89.6%), particularly in patients unable to undergo bronchoscopy, though further validation is needed.63,77 Surfactant protein (SP), particularly SP-A and SP-D, have also been investigated as non-specific potential biomarkers for fungal respiratory infections, but their role remains unexplored in PJP patients. Given that PJP involves alveolar epithelial damage, researchers hypothesize that SP-A and SP-D levels may also be elevated during infection. This represents a promising avenue for future investigation.25

Several host-response biomarkers, such as CD3+, CD4+, and CD8+ T-cell counts, have been identified as risk factors for PJP development. However, cutoff values vary across studies, and immunological profiles differ among patients with various causes of immunodeficiency. Specifically, reduced CD4+ T-cell counts serve as a reliable indicator in HIV-positive PJP patients, whereas CD3+ T-cell counts, as an important measure of immune status, show significant reductions in solid organ transplant recipients with PJP. Furthermore, models combining CD8+ T-cell counts and LDH levels show outstanding value for predicting PJP in HIV-negative patients receiving immunosuppressive therapy.80

NAAT

The rapid convergence of advanced disciplines such as genomics, engineering, and nanoscience has propelled the development of NAAT for respiratory specimens, including polymerase chain reaction (PCR), loop-mediated isothermal amplification (LAMP), recombinase polymerase amplification (RPA), and next-generation sequencing. These advancements have significantly enhanced the diagnostic capacity for P. jirovecii and have also facilitated notable advances in the detection of pathogen virulence factors and drug-resistant genes.

PCR

PCR, first used in 1990 to detect P. jirovecii-DNA in BALF, offers high sensitivity (91.3%–99.7%) and specificity (79%–96%).14,81,82 It enables detection of low fungal loads28 but struggles to differentiate colonization from infection, with positive results potentially indicating colonization, latent reactivation, or early infection.83,84 Over-reliance may lead to overdiagnosis.32 Real-time quantitative PCR (qPCR) enhances accuracy by quantifying fungal load, distinguishing colonization from infection, monitoring treatment response, and detecting sulfonamide resistance mutations (e.g., deoxyhypusine synthase-homo sapiens).20,85, 86, 87 However, qPCR is affected by specimen quality, timing, and host immune status.32,88,89 DNA persistence post-treatment and RNA degradation complicate interpretation.30,90,91 Standardization of specimen handling, cross-platform comparability, and cutoff values (e.g., 1.6 × 10⁶ copies/L or 2.0 × 106 copies/L for infection) remain challenges.88,92, 93, 94 The 2016 European Conference on Infections in Leukemia recommends interpreting PCR results with clinical context rather than quantitative thresholds, and low-load cases may require prophylaxis.33

Digital droplet PCR (ddPCR) is a novel technology based on water-oil emulsion droplet technique that can accurately quantify trace samples, particularly at low copy numbers.95 Compared to conventional molecular methods, ddPCR offers several advantages, including higher sensitivity and specificity, absolute quantification without standard curve, improved reproducibility, and enhanced resistance to PCR inhibitors.96 Studies96 using this technology for P. jirovecii detection have reported 100% sensitivity and 94.3% specificity. Moreover, ddPCR accurately quantifies fungal load. Higher copies are associated with more severe clinical manifestations, like lower oxygenation indices, partly reflecting PJP severity.95 Thus, patients with high copies may require more urgent initiation of anti-P. jirovecii therapy. Additionally, ddPCR demonstrates utility in distinguishing P. jirovecii colonization from infection by applying different thresholds. However, as P. jirovecii burden differs between HIV-positive and HIV-negative PJP patients, diagnostic thresholds must be interpreted using population-specific criteria (13.98 copies/μL for HIV-positive vs. 4.5 copies/μL for HIV-negative individuals).96,97 Therefore, ddPCR can be a valuable additional diagnostic test for patients with a high clinical suspicion of PJP but negative microscopy or other test results, particularly those with suspected low fungal burden.

LAMP

LAMP amplifies DNA/RNA under constant temperature, simplifying PCR steps. It offers rapid results (10–30 minutes), cost-effectiveness, and visual detection,85,98 making it suitable for emergency or low-resource settings.14,98 LAMP shows higher specificity than PCR (no cross-reactivity with other fungi) and comparable sensitivity (87.5%–95.4%).14,99, 100, 101 However, false negatives have been reported,98 and its inability to quantify fungal load limits differentiation of colonization from infection.85,98 Despite potential, LAMP requires further validation in high-quality studies.

RPA

As an isothermal amplification technique, RPA can amplify nucleic acids within 20 minutes at 37–42°C without complex thermal cycling equipment, making it ideal for time-sensitive diagnostics.102 The clustered regularly interspaced short palindromic repeat-associated protein (CRISPR/Cas) system was originally characterized as a bacterial adaptive immune mechanism. Owing to its high specificity and programmability, it has been repurposed for various molecular diagnostic applications. Among its variants, CRISPR/Cas12a is particularly useful for pathogen detection due to its trans-cleavage activity and ability to target DNA. Researchers have combined RPA with CRISPR/Cas12a technology, targeting the highly replicated mitochondrial small subunit rRNA gene of P. jirovecii to achieve rapid and cost-effective detection. This method demonstrates a diagnostic accuracy of 93.33% and exceptional sensitivity. P. jirovecii can be achieved at very low copy numbers (1 copy/reaction). It is particularly suitable for screening high-risk populations, such as HIV-positive individuals or those receiving immunosuppressive therapy. They may harbor low pathogen levels without overt symptoms. The rapid turnaround time and relatively straightforward protocol make this assay applicable for low-resource settings and rapid point-of-care diagnostics without the complex thermal cycling equipment.103,104 However, the inability to differentiate colonization from true infection is still a major limitation. Existing studies are predominantly small-scale and single center. Future multicenter research should validate the clinical utility of the RPA-CRISPR/Cas12a assay by applying it to diverse respiratory patient cohorts and benchmarking it against qPCR, thereby enhancing its external validity.

Metagenomic next-generation sequencing (mNGS)

mNGS, a high-throughput sequencing technique, enables unbiased, simultaneous detection of pathogens, including P. jirovecii, in 24–48 hours.105 It is particularly valuable for critically ill patients with mixed infections, as it identifies novel or rare pathogens and is unaffected by prior antibiotic use.106,107 mNGS sensitivity (nearly 100%) and specificity (96.3%) exceed traditional methods, with BALF and blood showing good consistency.108, 109, 110, 111 Notably, mNGS maintains 91.3% positivity for PJP despite prior antibiotic exposure, outperforming conventional methods (43.5%).112 It is particularly advantageous for critically ill patients, as it helps secure crucial intervention time while minimizing the impact of empirical treatment and prior prophylactic medication on diagnostic accuracy.112 P. jirovecii fragments can enter the peripheral blood circulation from the respiratory tract.113 Multiple studies have confirmed a strong concordance between mNGS results from BALF and blood samples,110,111 suggesting that blood-based mNGS could serve as a viable alternative for patients from whom BALF cannot be obtained, especially critically ill patients unable to tolerate bronchoscopy. Furthermore, mNGS can also detect mutations associated with resistance to trimethoprim and sulfamethoxazole (TMP-SMX),87 thereby guiding therapeutic decision-making for PJP patients. Lu et al.106 suggested that the P. jirovecii sequence read count from mNGS might indicate PJP severity. However, in clinical practice, disease severity is multifactorial and is also influenced by patient age, underlying conditions, and immune status. Therefore, this hypothesis requires further validation. What's more, high costs, lack of standardized workflows, human DNA interference, difficulty interpreting low-load results (colonization vs. infection), and cannot assess viability or perform phenotypic susceptibility testing limit its routine use.114,115

While mNGS demonstrates advantages for PJP diagnosis in immunocompromised hosts, particularly for critically ill and complex cases with suspected co-infections, any results should not directly guide clinical decisions alone. These findings must be integrated with the clinical context, including host factors, clinical presentation, laboratory and imaging results, and treatment response. Future priorities include standardizing analytical thresholds, validating combined biomarkers, and elucidating resistance mechanisms.

Targeted next-generation sequencing (tNGS)

tNGS, a cost-effective alternative to mNGS, enriches target pathogens using predefined primers, balancing sensitivity and throughput.116 It demonstrates comparable performance to mNGS at less than half the cost. In immunocompromised populations, such as those with malignancies, tNGS also accurately detects P. jirovecii. Comparative studies have shown that mNGS does not yield a higher detection rate for P. jirovecii than tNGS. Consequently, tNGS could become the preferred diagnostic technique for non-critical immunocompromised patients with lower respiratory tract infections.117,118 Similar to mNGS, tNGS faces challenges in distinguishing pathogens from colonizers and contaminants. Although pre-sequencing PCR amplification enhances sensitivity for low-abundance microbes, it also increases contamination risk. Researchers suggest optimizing PCR and probe concentrations to improve tNGS sensitivity for clinically relevant pathogens and reduce false positives, but this approach narrows the detection spectrum.118 Direct comparisons of mNGS, tNGS, and conventional diagnostic techniques for P. jirovecii within uniform patient cohorts are still lacking. Establishing validated thresholds for tNGS represents a critical future direction for its clinical application. Additionally, as pathogen epidemiology is geographically specific and involves rare and emerging pathogens, tNGS panels may not fully meet the diagnostic needs of all regions.116 Future studies should focus on aggregating larger pathogen datasets to develop more region-specific tNGS assays.

In summary, tNGS is not the first-line technique for critically ill patients with co-infections involving nonspecific (e.g., non-tuberculous mycobacteria) or other rare pathogens. However, given its low cost and high sensitivity, tNGS is highly recommended for primary care settings and non-critically ill patients. It can significantly improve diagnostic accuracy, reduce antibiotic misuse, and alleviate the economic burden on patients.117

Combination testing

Since each single detection technique has inherent limitations, mNGS is costly and not yet routinely available in hospitals, the integration of multi-omics approaches should be considered. Previous studies2,33 have proposed that hematology patients suspected of PJP should undergo simultaneous qPCR and IFM testing. A positive result in both tests confirms the diagnosis, and vice versa. If IFM is negative but qPCR is positive, a high fungal load indicates PJP, whereas a low load warrants further testing, including BDG. If IFM is positive but PCR is negative, the result is considered technically uncertain, and the reliability of both results may be questioned.2 Morjaria et al.119 reported that combining BDG and PCR testing on respiratory specimens significantly improves detection rates. Given the pan-fungal nature of BDG, PCR is recommended for initial screening. Positive PCR results are identified as dependent on lung load and confirmed with BDG testing. When BALF specimens are unavailable, serum BDG testing can serve as an alternative.2

Combining BDG with LDH levels allows for a completely non-invasive approach, achieving a specificity of 84% (BDG: 400 pg/mL; LDH: 350 U/L). However, these cutoff values require further validation.2 A further multicenter study evaluating BDG, LDH, KL-6, and SAM for PJP diagnosis, with the best overall performance being BDG in combination with KL-6 (sensitivity: 94%, specificity: 90%).120

Distinguishing colonization from true infection

The advent of more sensitive diagnostic tests has led to the increasing detection of P. jirovecii in asymptomatic individuals or those without typical PJP symptoms. Distinguishing colonization from true infection remains one of the most critical and unresolved challenges in PJP diagnosis. Given that symptoms or imaging may not be reliable, diagnostic tests for colonization should be considered. qPCR shows potential for identifying colonization based on target gene copy number and pre-defined cut-off values. Studies96,121 have attempted to use dual qPCR cut-off values to discriminate between colonization and infection. Based on cycle threshold (Ct) values, P. jirovecii-positive cases can be categorized as follows: infection (Ct ≤ 32), colonization (Ct > 35), and an indeterminate gray zone (32 < Ct ≤ 35).121 However, no universal consensus exists on these cut-off values, as they can vary across assays and patient populations. The frequent presence of an indeterminate gray zone complicates interpretation. For cases within this zone, clinicians must base treatment decisions on other clinical and paraclinical findings. Furthermore, the extrapolation of such qPCR-based strategies is limited by heterogeneity in PCR targets, PJP definitions, host characteristics, sample sizes, and DNA extraction/quantification methods.122,123 Therefore, optimal differentiation thresholds must be validated for specific patient strata. Additionally, changes in immune status must be considered, as the progression from P. jirovecii colonization to infection represents a continuous spectrum. This underscores the importance of real-time and repeated testing.122 BDG may also aid in distinguishing colonization from infection (colonization: 49 pg/mL vs. infection: 173 pg/mL), but it should not be used as a standalone test.2

Integrating qPCR with other biomarkers, such as BDG, could yield a more accurate and comprehensive assessment. For instance, using BDG to verify a positive qPCR result (with a proposed cut-off of 496.45 pg/mL) achieved an area under the curve of 0.817 for predicting infection. However, such thresholds are often derived from specific study cohorts and can fluctuate with sample size.121,123 Consequently, it is generally recommended that in P. jirovecii-positive individuals, qPCR be used to interpret each BDG value, and to determine the threshold, vice versa, in order to screen out the truly infected PJP.123,124 Given that qPCR is less quantitatively precise than ddPCR, researchers propose that using ddPCR (with a cut-off of 13.98 copies/μL) may yield more reliable results.96

Recent genomic and transcriptomic data may advance this field. Studies utilizing RNA sequencing of the trophic life form after antibiotic pressure have identified uniquely expressed genes in the trophic phase that could serve as novel diagnostic targets. Consequently, PCR markers specific to the trophozoite life-form may provide a useful diagnostic tool for infection.125,126

Summary and perspectives

PJP remains a life-threatening opportunistic infection in immunocompromised populations, with rising mortality despite improved prophylaxis. Delayed diagnosis leads to rapid progression and irreversible organ damage, highlighting the need for timely and accurate detection. Current techniques, including culture (unreliable), cytology (specific but insensitive), serology (limited by kinetics), and molecular methods (sensitive but interpretation challenges), each have limitations. mNGS and tNGS show promise in complex cases but require standardization. Given the high risk of co-infections and the necessity of differentiating colonization from infection, an integrated diagnostic approach combining cytological, serological, and molecular assays is recommended for rapid and accurate diagnosis. Particularly in critically ill patients, the early adoption of a comprehensive testing strategy with PCR and mNGS as its core components is strongly recommended. Future research should focus on developing non-invasive markers, improving assay standardization, and integrating multi-technique approaches to enhance diagnostic accuracy and guide personalized therapy (Fig. 1).

The diagnosis decision framework for clinicians. When PJP is suspected, respiratory specimens (preferably BALF) and blood specimens should be collected. A comprehensive diagnostic workup comprising cytological, serological, and molecular testing should be initiated concurrently. For severe cases, a comprehensive testing strategy centered on PCR combined with mNGS is strongly recommended.
