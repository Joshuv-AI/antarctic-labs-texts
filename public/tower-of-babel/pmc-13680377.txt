Human targeting of morphologically unique fishes amplifies the risk of functional erosion
Authors: Pierre Bouchet, Sébastien Brosse, Aurèle Toussaint
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Human societies are drawing on wild species for food, fuel, medicines, and recreation. Yet, it remains unclear whether different types of human use target species with common or unique ecological roles. Here we show that humans use a fourth (26%) of the freshwater fish species and these species occupy almost half (45.2%) of the global spectrum of fish morphological diversity. Yet, used species harbour unique trait combinations, that differ according to the way humans use the species such as fisheries, aquaculture, game fish and aquarium. Moreover, the species targeted by humans are characterised by traits that are predominantly associated with threatened species. By simulating the extinction of threatened species used by humans, we revealed a pronounced and non-random erosion of the morphological spectrum. Understanding the trait-based impacts of human use is thus essential to buffer human impact on natural resources. Conservation should therefore safeguard threatened species but also preserve irreplaceable trait combinations that support unique ecological strategies.

Introduction

For millennia, human societies have drawn on wild species for food, fuel, medicines, ornamentation, recreation, and tradition1,2. This deep and ongoing reliance spans all major taxonomic groups, influencing both human societies and the evolutionary paths of the species human societies use3–5. The human uses of biodiversity have rarely been random; across plants, animals, and fungi, exploited species disproportionately share traits such as large body size6, rapid growth7, conspicuous appearance8,9, or high nutritional value10. These traits enhance their utility, accessibility, or appeal and thus make them prime targets for harvest, trade, or domestication.

Yet, traits are also strongly linked to species’ ecological roles11, influencing how organisms move, feed, interact, and shape their environments. As a result, exploitation of species driven by culture or economy has ecological consequences for the functioning of ecosystems12. For instance, the disappearance of large mammals due to human pressure in Europe has drastically modified ecosystems, shifting them from megafauna-rich landscapes with extensive grazing and nutrient redistribution to more homogenised forests and simplified food webs13.

Today, harvest and trade of more than 50,000 wild species sustain the livelihoods of hundreds of millions of people over the world2,14. But as demand rises and exploitation intensifies, concerns are growing over the sustainability of these interactions, their ecological impacts, and the urgent need to align conservation efforts with development goals and human well-being15,16. Understanding the ecological role of the species used by humans is hence becoming a key issue for both biodiversity conservation and sustainability of human societies.

With the rise of trait-based approaches and the availability of large databases, it is now possible to assess the functional diversity of major taxa of interest for humans17,18. However, it remains unclear whether the functional traits targeted by humans for food, ornament or recreation are supported by a large range of species or only a few ones, and if those species are currently threatened. This represents a critical blind spot, as most conservation frameworks—including the IUCN Red List19, CITES regulations20, and IPBES assessments2—primarily evaluate species’ threat status or trade volumes, but rarely account for their functional roles21. Yet, exploiting species with unique trait combinations might increase extinction risks for species playing key roles in ecosystems. Integrating trait-based perspectives into conservation frameworks is therefore requested to provide a robust basis for identifying priority species and ensuring that conservation efforts safeguard not only biodiversity itself, but also the ecological strategies essential for human well-being.

Here, we focus on freshwater fishes, which number over 18,000 species22 and are intensively used by humans for protein supply, recreation and ornament23,24. We adopt a macroecological perspective to capture the global footprint of human uses on freshwater fish functional diversity, necessary to identify broad, cross-continental patterns. We combine morphological traits from FishMORPH25, the most accurate database on morphological traits of freshwater fish worldwide and FishBase26 for human uses, to investigate whether humans target species with particular morphological traits. We then investigate if these traits are commonly shared or specific to a few morphologically distinctive species. We finally use simulations to quantify to what extent the extinction of threatened species used by humans could reshape the morphological spectrum of freshwater fishes over the globe. We here expect that humans select a non-random subset of the global fish morphological spectrum and that this subset differs according to human uses. If this is true, testing whether species that support unique trait combinations—that are often threatened27–29—are used by humans is of particular importance, as human pressure on those species could trigger extinction risks.

To evaluate whether the extinction of species that are both used by humans and threatened with extinction would also disproportionately erode the morphological diversity, we simulated sequential species loss scenarios based on IUCN threat levels and calculated the loss of morphological richness following the potential extinction of threatened freshwater fish species used by humans. There, negative values indicate a decrease in the relative abundance of trait values associated with a given region of morphospace. Consequently, the positive values indicate a relative increase arising purely from this mathematical property of quantile differences. From an ecological point of view, those areas cannot be interpreted as an increase of species with such ecological strategies, since no species are introduced. However, they reflect a relative increase in the proportional representation of those trait combinations among surviving species, as losses elsewhere mathematically elevate their dominance within the post-extinction trait spectrum. In addition, we identified morphospace cells that became empty following extinctions and expressed this lost space as a proportion of the total morphospace occupied by the IUCN-assessed used species spectrum.

The loss of both used and threatened species (Table 2) would strongly affect the global morphological diversity with a loss of 4.70 % under the scenario NT (i.e., removing all threatened (i.e., CR, EN, and VU) and near threatened (NT) species. This loss of morphological diversity substantially exceeded the mean loss expected under the null model (1.12%, Supplementary Table 2) if only threatened species (i.e., CR, EN, and VU) and near threatened (NT) species were randomly removed (SES = -24.78, P = 0.001, Fig. 4). This pattern was consistent across all usages and scenarios but varied in terms of intensity. The loss of morphological diversity was particularly marked for species occupying peripheral regions of the morphospace (Fig. 4). These findings indicated that species which were both threatened and subject to human exploitation tended to occupy irreplaceable positions in the morphospace. Discussion

Freshwater fish species used by humans do not represent a random sample from the global spectrum of morphological diversity. Instead, they are disproportionately selected for their unique morphological trait combinations. Although the fish species used by humans represent a fourth (26%) of the freshwater fish species, they occupy 45.2% of the total morphospace. These results highlight how human activities draw on a wide array of functional strategies to meet various needs. Since the use of species by humans is considered a major threat to biodiversity30,31, targeting species with unique trait combinations can thus amplify the risk of losing ecological strategies that no other species can replace. This was confirmed by simulating the extinction of threatened species used by humans. This resulted in a pronounced and non-random erosion of the global morphological spectrum, with potential implications for the ecological integrity and functional resilience of freshwater ecosystems worldwide. Since the distinct uses of fish by humans target different regions of the morphospace, conservation actions should be directed toward specific human activities to safeguard the ecological strategies that underpin both Nature’s Contributions to People14 and the long-term resilience of ecosystems32.

Broad evidence across taxa demonstrates specific use by human societies of large-bodied mammals6, slow-growing trees33, or brightly coloured birds9. Human societies use a broad range of fish morphologies, because fish are used for a wide array of purposes spanning from fisheries to aquaculture and recreation through angling (game fish) and ornament (aquarium). Yet, considering those four main usages independently disentangles the links between fish morphology and human uses, with each human use targeting a distinct area of the morphospace (Fig. 1). Aquarium trade distinctly targeted species with upward-facing mouths and large eyes (e.g., guppy, Poecilia reticulata). Such preferences, driven by aesthetic considerations34, are not only based on colouration patterns but also on morphology, as suggested for reef fishes by Langlois et al. (2022)35. In addition, human selection was also driven by practicality for aquarium keeping36, and thus preferentially selects small-bodied and small-mouthed species (Fig. 2c) (e.g., Loricariids such as Ancistrus dolichopterus, or goldfish, Carassius auratus). These morphological profiles often correspond to low trophic-level guilds—omnivores, planktivores, and insectivores12,37—but do not exclude other strategies, such as some predatory species that often attract fish hobbyists due to their unusual colour or morphology (e.g. knife fishes such as Chitala blanci represented on the Fig. 1). Conversely, aquaculture targeted preferentially large-bodied benthivores, with downturned mouths (e.g., African sharptooth catfish, Clarias gariepinus) and demersal predators such as the Indian knifefish (Chitala chitala) (Fig. 2d). Game fishing similarly selected for large-bodied species, with large mouths (e.g., largemouth bass, Micropterus salmoides, lake trout, Salvelinus namaycush) (Fig. 2e). Aquaculture and game fishing therefore select large species, with traits related to high growth rates and efficient resource utilisation38,39. Such selection of particular morphologies for species used for ornament, recreation and aquaculture contrasts with the morphology of the fish species exploited by fisheries, which have a broad morphological diversity. Fisheries target small-bodied prey species, such as the silver cyprinid (Rastrineobola argentea) in Lake Victoria40 or the vendace (Coregonus albula) in Northern hemisphere41. Their abundance and small size facilitate easy handling and widespread use42. Conversely, large-bodied predators, such as northern pike (Esox lucius), are also prized targets for their substantial meat yield and economic importance43. The coexistence of these morphologically distinct species within fisheries underscores the multifaceted nature of human harvesting strategies, which span trophic levels and ecological roles, reflecting varied dietary, cultural and economic demands23. At the same time, the prominence of small-bodied species in fisheries may also reflect a “fishing down the food web” process, whereby the depletion of large, high-trophic-level species leads to increased exploitation of smaller, lower-trophic-level species44. It also underscores the exploitation of the full range of available species in commercial fisheries, which is particularly true in the southern half of the globe45,46.

Moreover, whatever the fish use, we here show that freshwater fish species exhibiting unique morphological trait combinations were preferentially used by humans (Fig. 3). The foregoing implies that exploited species might play critical roles in ecosystems, not insured by other species gathering similar trait comparisons47. Therefore, the less insured functions appear to be the most used by humans. This is particularly marked for the species used in aquaculture and game fishing, as these two human uses preferentially target morphologically distinct species that carry unique ecological strategies30. Those activities indeed target a few large species and that are for most of them morphologically unique27. The overexploitation of such specialised species could modify the functional structure of fish assemblages and potentially skew ecosystem processes such as nutrient cycling and trophic interactions48,49. For instance, largemouth bass (Micropterus salmoides) is heavily cultured and a popular game fish, but its overexploitation has been shown to downgrade lake food webs and fundamentally change associated ecosystem processes by reducing its control effect on lower trophic levels50. This is also the case for other species targeted by anglers, that are often large-bodied and top predators, playing a key role in ecosystems through top-down effects50.

Simulating the extinction of the threatened species used by humans resulted in a marked erosion of the freshwater fish morphological diversity across the globe (Fig. 4, Supplementary Table 2). The sequential removal of species from the most threatened (CR) to the least threatened (NT) categories was designed to reflect a realistic temporal gradient of extinction risk, whereby the most critically endangered species are expected to disappear first, followed by species of progressively lower threat status28. This erosion was not caused by a particular use of fish, and the extinction of threatened fish, whatever the usage, will result in a higher-than-expected decline in fish functional diversity over the globe. Such extinction will disproportionately affect regions of the morphospace occupied by species with specific ecological strategies, such as large-bodied species with predatory (e.g., Boal fish, Wallago attu) or benthivorous (Sturgeons, Acipenser spp, Huso huso) habits (Fig. 4), that are known to represent the most threatened regions of the morphospace27. Thus, species that are simultaneously used by humans and threatened require particular attention, not only to ensure the sustainability of the species, but also to ensure food security, livelihoods, and ecosystem health across the globe51.

Our results underscore a dual effect. On one hand, humans disproportionately target morphologically irreplaceable species, amplifying the risk of losing unique ecological functions. This threat was, for instance, mentioned for marine fish in the northern Atlantic region, where sharks represent functionally unique large-bodied predators overexploited by benthic trawls52. This was also observed in mammals and birds, where the most functionally distinct species tend also to be the most threatened according to the IUCN categories53. But on the other hand, the species used by humans—particularly in managed systems like aquaculture or through cultural and economic value—may provide some level of protection or conservation incentive. That is the case for common carp (Cyprinus carpio), which is threatened in its native range, but globally propagated by aquaculture, making it one of the most common freshwater fish across the globe and even a potential pest for native faunas54. Species valued for food, trade, or recreation may hence benefit from propagation, habitat protection, or legal safeguards, acting as a buffer against extinction. Recreational fishing, when stringently regulated, can be managed sustainably and even foster population recovery55,56. Such “trophy hunting” activity could be viewed as a way to protect target species from overfishing by providing more money income than fisheries57. Likewise, community-based management has proven successful for high-value species like Arapaima (Arapaima gigas) in Brazil, where protecting lakes for subsistence fisheries led to a 193% population increase and improved household incomes58. Beyond this win–win outcome, Arapaima plays an umbrella species role, and its management also benefits other threatened species, including turtles and caimans59.

Although human use can support conservation60, we still need to stay cautious about the possible overreliance on utilitarian approaches, which risks excluding species and habitats with no obvious human value61. Conservation cannot depend solely on market-based incentives, and protection must extend beyond use; utilitarian logic alone cannot resolve ecological crises61.

In this context, human use considerations complement, but cannot replace intrinsic, non-instrumental reasons to conserve. Trait-based approaches are thus providing a framework to identify functionally irreplaceable species that are disproportionately exposed to human use. The foregoing approaches offer complementary information to traditional conservation assessments that have largely focused on species richness and extinction risk, overlooking the erosion of functional traits that underpin ecosystem resilience. Yet, evidence of global functional homogenisation in freshwater fish faunas highlights the urgent need to complement taxonomic indicators with functional metrics62. Integrating functional diversity into conservation planning consistently improves the spatial efficiency63 and robustness of priority areas64. Similar insights have emerged from reef systems, where high species richness does not necessarily guarantee functional insurance, underscoring that conserving diversity hotspots alone is insufficient when the goal of conservation prioritisation is to secure the functional insurance of biota65. In practical terms, our results suggest that functional metrics could be directly incorporated into management frameworks. For instance, fisheries institutions such as the Food and Agriculture Organisation (FAO) could promote the exploitation of functionally redundant species while imposing stricter quotas on functionally unique ones, thereby reducing the functional vulnerability of targeted assemblages to enhance ecosystem stability66. At the international level, trade regulations such as CITES could also integrate trait-based indicators—particularly functional distinctivenes—to better safeguard ecological functions21,52. This is consistent with recent studies on reptile trade revealing strong functional biases favouring large-bodied, long-lived taxa, underscoring that extinction risk alone is insufficient to preserve key functional roles67. Expanding such frameworks to include functionally irreplaceable but non-threatened species could prevent pre-emptive functional loss in vulnerable ecosystems. Together, these perspectives advocate for a shift from purely taxonomic conservation paradigms toward approaches explicitly incorporating functional indicators as a core dimension of biodiversity value.

While our analysis provides new insights into the functional consequences of human uses on the global diversity of freshwater fishes, our results remain limited by the global extent of the study. Such a global approach captures trends that are essential to provide a broad understanding of how human uses filter functional diversity by preferentially targeting morphologically distinctive species and, consequently, how their disappearance would erode the global freshwater fish morphospace. Yet, the results we report will hold at finer scales because species designed as functionally distinct over the globe would remain in that category when considering ecoregions, basins or sites. Yet such a global-scale approach overlooks regional contrasts and thus omits functionally unique species within a region or a river basin, making it necessary for further studies to disentangle how the intensity and nature of human uses shape functional diversity across different regions29. Furthermore, our analysis is restricted to the morphological consequences of direct exploitation by humans, while indirect threats (e.g., river damming, habitat destruction, pollution, and invasive species) remain one of the primary drivers of freshwater biodiversity loss globally68. Quantifying the morphological consequences of such indirect pressures remains an important avenue for future research.

Our findings reveal a global, non-random pattern in the morphological traits of freshwater fish species used by humans, with strong implications for ecosystem functioning and conservation. Human activities selectively target functionally distinctive species, intensifying the risk of losing irreplaceable ecological strategies. Yet, use can also confer protection, especially when tied to cultural or economic value. This duality highlights the need to move beyond taxonomic conservation toward frameworks that integrate functional diversity metrics. By identifying which traits and ecological strategies are most vulnerable, trait-based approaches offer actionable insights for reconciling sustainable use with biodiversity protection. These results underscore the urgency of integrating functional indicators into international policy, management, and trade regulation to safeguard the resilience of freshwater ecosystems and, more broadly, the global biota.

Methods

Data collection

Morphological traits and phylogenies

We used morphological traits from the FishMORPH database25, the most complete database of morphological traits for freshwater fishes. This database included eleven morphological traits related to the locomotion and feeding strategies of fishes12. These traits have been measured on 8970 species (see Brosse et al. (2021)25 for details on database completion). Briefly, nine morphological traits were obtained from measures on side-view photographs of a single adult specimen. Intraspecific morphological trait variability was not considered in this study, as it hardly affects functional diversity at the large spatial resolution considered69. The morphological traits considered were body elongation (BEl), body lateral shape (BLs), Relative eye size (REs) and vertical eye position (VEp), relative maxillary length (RMl) and oral gape position (OGp), pectoral fin size (PFs) and pectoral fin vertical position (PFv), and caudal peduncle throttling (CPt). In addition, fish maximum standard body length (SBl) and maximum body mass (Bm) have been retrieved from FishBase26. Information on morphological measures and trait calculation is provided in Brosse et al. (2021)25.

Due to the loss of body parts or the quality of fish pictures, some traits have not been measured for some species. Overall, 26.57% of the values were missing in the raw morphological trait dataset. We imputed those missing values using a machine learning trait imputation procedure (i.e., ‘missForest’ v1.5 package available in R70), commonly used in macroecology71,72. Before imputation, traits have been log10-transformed, centred, and scaled. As suggested by Penone et al. (2014)72, we considered evolutionary relationships in the imputation process by including phylogenetic eigenvectors as additional predictors, such that missing values for each morphological trait were imputed from all informed morphological traits plus the first ten phylogenetic eigenvectors derived from the square-root-transformed cophenetic distance matrix of the fish phylogeny73. To evaluate the accuracy of trait imputation in a phylogenetically informed framework, we used a simulation-based procedure following Toussaint et al. (2021)28. We evaluated imputation uncertainty using the procedure used by Carmona et al. 202127. For that, we artificially masked a subset of species with fully informed trait values (10% per iteration, 100 iterations) following the structure of missing values in the original dataset (see details Supplementary Table 3). We then compared the position of those species in the morphological space between the observed position (i.e., with all informed trait values) and the imputed position (i.e., with informed plus artificial missing trait values) using normalised root mean squared error (NRMSE, see details in Supplementary Table 3). Across 100 bootstraps, the mean RMSE of species’ positions in morphological space was 5.47% ± 1.52% of axis ranges (Supplementary Table 3), indicating minimal distortion from imputation. To test the robustness of a single imputation, we compared single imputation values to a mean imputation value obtained with multiple imputations (Supplementary Table 4). Multiple imputations were performed based on 100 independent missForest imputations using the same parameters but randomised seeds. For each species × trait combination with missing data, we quantified (i) variability across imputations, (ii) deviation of the multiple-imputation mean from our reference single-imputation values, and (iii) impacts on the multidimensional functional space. Differences ranged from 0.68% to 3.70%, which were comparable to the multiple imputation variability (ranging from 0.737% to 3.43%). This suggests the choice between single and multiple imputation exerts only a minor influence relative to inherent stochasticity in the procedure. Procrustes analyses of all pairwise comparisons among the 100 imputed datasets further confirmed high geometric stability of the functional space (mean r = 0.9952; range: 0.9907–0.9999), with minimal residual deviation (mean sum of squares = 0.0095). These results indicate our principal findings are robust to imputation uncertainty. We thus conclude that single and multiple imputation are functionally equivalent here, with imputation uncertainty exerting negligible effects on species positions within functional trait space.

Taxonomy was standardised using the R package ‘taxize’ (v0.10.0)74. All names were resolved with the Global Biodiversity Information Facility (GBIF) Backbone Taxonomy75,76. The species names that were not standardised using ‘taxize’ were checked based on the taxonomic classifications provided by Fricke et al. (2025)75. After taxonomy homogenisation, our analyses are based on 8,970 fish species with informed morphological traits.

Human uses

Data on human uses were retrieved from the FishBase database26, which considers the fisheries, aquarium trade, aquaculture and game fish as the four main categories of fish use. We used two complementary approaches to extract this information from FishBase. First, we extracted all available information on species uses through the R package ‘rfishbase’(v5.0.1)77. Yet, we noticed that not all fields were systematically filled for all species, leading to potential gaps in usage assignment. To address this limitation and complete the dataset, we developed a web scraping protocol using the R package ‘rvest’ (v1.0.4)78 to directly retrieve usage information from the website (www.fishbase.se). We used automated scripts to extract the “Human uses” section of each species. To assess the potential bias introduced by the web scraping method, we conducted additional analyses comparing the results derived from each data source independently and the merged dataset. We tested whether the inclusion of species identified through data scraping altered the structure of trait-based morphological spaces for each usage category. Scraped data contributed an additional 1.7% of usage records (see Supplementary Table 5), with the most notable increase observed in the game fish category with a 14.65% usage records from web scrapping. To assess the impact of these additions, we recalculated morphological spaces and visually compared them with and without scraped species (Supplementary Fig. 4). Morphological structure remained globally unchanged, indicating limited influence of the added data.

Fisheries’ interest referred to the species captured in the wild and used as a protein supply for humans. Freshwater fisheries provide essential protein for over 150 million people, particularly in low-income countries79, and account for ∼12 million tons of annual harvest24. Fisheries interest was determined from FAO data24, which classifies species from “highly commercial” to “no interest.” To restrict the analysis to intensively exploited taxa, only species labelled as “highly commercial” or “commercial” were retained (n = 1688; 75.52% of all fish used in fisheries), hereafter referred to as species of fishery interest.

Aquaculture interest relied on the species cultured in ponds, lakes, and cages, excluding ornamental trade. Aquaculture has surpassed capture fisheries in volume, reaching 130.9 million tonnes in 202224. Aquaculture interest was derived from FAO data, which categorises species as “never/rarely,” “experimental,” “likely future use,” or “commercial.” Only species labelled as “commercial” were considered of aquaculture interest (n = 237; 89.77% of aquaculture-used species).

Ornament referred to the species used in ornamental trade in ponds or aquaria. Species kept only in public aquariums were not considered in this category. Aquarium trade use was classified into “never/rarely,” “potential,” “show aquarium,” and “commercial.” We retained only species categorised as “commercial” (n = 1505), which account for most aquarium-traded species (97.66%) and were commonly sold in aquarium shops worldwide. Categories such as “potential” or “show aquarium” were excluded due to their limited or non-commercial exploitation.

Game use referred to the fish targeted by anglers. It was identified based on FishBase records, which report 389 species targeted in game fishing. This information primarily stems from the annual World Record Game Fishes list published by the International Game Fish Association (IGFA)80.

Each category of human use was not mutually exclusive: a given species can be assigned to multiple human uses. For instance, the common carp, Cyprinus carpio, is used in all categories, i.e., fisheries, aquaculture, aquarium trade and game fish.

Morphospace

We built a morphological trait space (hereafter called morphospace) of freshwater fish using a principal component analysis (PCA) on the 11 log-transformed and scaled morphological traits, summarising the main axes of morphological trait variation. We kept the first four PC axes, which summarise 74.02% of the morphological trait variability in agreement with the Horn’s parallel analysis available in the ‘paran’ package81. We assessed the contribution of each morphological trait along each of the four PC axes by calculating the mean and standard deviation of the PC scores for each axis (1-4). We evaluated the probabilistic distribution of the species within the morphospace by performing multivariate kernel density using the ‘TPD’ and ‘ks’ packages82,83. We divided the four-dimensional space into 810,000 cells, 30 per dimension, as the best compromise between data visualisation and computation time27.

Biodiversity indices

Taxonomic diversity was calculated as the number of species for each usage (species richness), and morphological diversity was calculated as the volume of morphospace occupied by those species (morphological richness, hereafter called FRic84), measured using the ‘TPD’ package82. We expressed the amount of morphospace of each usage as a percentage of the volume occupied by all freshwater fish species (Supplementary Table 6). To quantify the morphological redundancy between the species belonging to the four usages for which we have morphological traits, we measured their overlap in the morphospace using the ‘TPD’ package82. The overlap index varies between 0 and 1, where a value of 1 indicates no overlap (complete dissimilarity in trait space) and a value of 0 indicates full overlap (no dissimilarity in trait space) (Table 1).

Morphological distinctiveness of species used by humans

To quantify how morphologically distinct a species is within the global species pool, we calculated its morphological distinctiveness as the average distance from that species to all other species, using the ‘funrar’ package85. By doing this, we captured how unique an organism is in terms of its morphological traits, and quantified it by measuring its morphological distance relative to all other species18.

To test whether morphologically distinctive species are more likely to be used by humans, we fitted a binomial generalised additive model (GAM) with a logit link, using species use (as a binary response) as a function of their distinctiveness score. The effect of morphological distinctiveness was evaluated using the approximate significance test of the smooth term provided by the ‘mgcv’ package86 (χ2 statistic and associated P value, Supplementary Table 7), and model fit was summarised using the percentage of deviance explained.

As a complementary analysis, we also calculated species uniqueness, defined as the distance to the nearest neighbour in the trait space. These two metrics were positively correlated (Pearson’s r = 0.69, t = 8,968, p < 2 ×10-16, 95% CI = [0.68, 0.70], two-sided; Supplementary Fig. 5), indicating that morphologically distinct species in the global pool also tend to be isolated from their closest neighbours in morphospace.

Simulating threatened species extinction

We considered the extinction risk of the 2334 freshwater fish species used in our study using the IUCN Red List categories. Among them, 65 species are classified as Critically Endangered (CR), 117 as Endangered (EN), 121 as Vulnerable (VU), and 93 as Near Threatened (NT), totalling 396 species (17.0%) that we considered as “threatened” in our study. We also identified 139 as Data Deficient species. The proportion of threatened and used species (i.e., 396/2,334) is lower than the global estimate of threatened freshwater fish species19 (26.3%, 4064 out of 15,433 assessed species).

To assess whether the species both used by humans and threatened to extinction depict disproportionately unique morphological traits, we quantified the loss of morphological diversity due to the loss of species both used and threatened compared to the loss of species threatened but not used by humans. For that, we used scenarios where threatened species were sequentially removed according to their extinction risk, starting with the most threatened species (i.e., Critically Endangered species, noted -CR), followed by scenarios removing species with less threatened status (Toussaint et al. 2021)28. Scenarios were named by the least threatened category considered: -EN: CR + EN removed; -VU: CR + EN + VU removed; -NT: CR + EN + VU + NT removed. These scenarios mimic a temporal gradient from the loss of the most threatened species (i.e., Critically Endangered) which are more likely to disappear first, to the extinction of species with lower extinction risks28. For each scenario and usage category, morphological richness (FRic) was recalculated using the trait probability density (TPD) framework.
