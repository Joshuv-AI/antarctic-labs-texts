Design and Preclinical Validation of an Eyeglass-Coupled Perilimbal Antenna for Retinal Prostheses With a Biocompatible Graft Technique
Authors: Cheng-Yung Lee, Tso-Ting Lai, Chien-Yu Chiang, Yun-Chen Wang, San-Yuan Lin, Yi-Ju Chiang, Yun-Ta Yang, Chang-Hao Yang, Long-Sheng Fan
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
PurposeTo present a novel eyeglass-coupled perilimbal antenna for retinal prostheses and evaluate preclinical biocompatibility and ocular surface effects using a refined, graft-assisted implantation technique.MethodsThe structural, mechanical, and functional properties of the antenna were assessed, including buffering systems for tissue resilience. Coupling between the eyeglass-rim–mounted emitter and perilimbal receiver was optimized through finite-element electromagnetic simulation and validated in electrolyte solution. A preclinical study was performed in 11 eyes of six Lanyu minipigs, in which antennas were implanted using a refined surgical technique and covered with amniotic membrane, porcine small intestinal submucosa, or pericardium graft. Postoperative evaluations over 6 months included serial external eye photography, anterior segment optical coherence tomography (AS-OCT), tear secretion testing, tear cytokine profiling, and conjunctival impression cytology.ResultsAll eyes showed stable healing without exposure, infection, or granulation. ASOCT revealed no significant change in perilimbal conjunctival thickness (400.5 ± 89.6 µm baseline vs. 399.4 ± 158.2 µm at 6 months; P = 0.612). Coverage materials thinned significantly (360.7 ± 119.5 µm at 1 month vs. 71.4 ± 103.0 µm at 6 months; P = 0.018) without adverse ocular effects. Transient postoperative elevations of tear cytokines (IL-1β, IL-8, IL-6) normalized over time. Tear production, conjunctival redness, and goblet cell density returned to baseline levels.ConclusionsThe eyeglass-coupled perilimbal antenna enabled efficient transmission with preserved ocular surface integrity. Graft-assisted implantation ensured stability and biocompatibility, serving as a safe interface for retinal prostheses.Translational RelevanceA novel perilimbal antenna design enables efficient wireless transmission and biocompatible integration for next-generation retinal prostheses.

Introduction

Retinal prostheses represent an important advance in vision restoration, offering patients with severe visual impairment the possibility of regaining partial function.1–8 These devices typically use an external camera or an integrated image sensor, a signal transmission unit, and a retinal stimulation array to bypass degenerated photoreceptors and, directly or indirectly through bipolar cells, to activate retinal ganglion cells, thereby generating patterned visual perception. Initially designed for advanced retinitis pigmentosa, their use has been extended to other causes of outer retinal degeneration, such as inherited retinal dystrophies and late-stage dry age-related macular degeneration.4,8

Despite these achievements, current prosthetic systems face significant limitations that restrict their clinical utility. High-resolution visual restoration requires not only advances in electrode design but also stable and efficient transmission of external signals and energy for stimulation or signal processing into the eye.9–11 In chip-based devices such as the Argus II (Cortigent, Valencia, CA), orbital placement of the antenna complicates surgery, risks tissue disruption, and hinders long-term stability. Photovoltaic-based systems avoid implanted antennas by using near-infrared light from external glasses projected onto a relatively small intraocular stimulating array, providing central prosthetic vision on top of natural peripheral vision but with a field of view constrained by implant coverage and optical projection geometry.12,13 These antenna-free approaches nonetheless highlight the remaining need for transmission interfaces that combine efficient coupling with long-term ocular biocompatibility.

In this study, we present a novel perilimbal antenna system engineered to provide a mechanically compliant interface with the ocular surface while efficiently coupling to external eyewear. Placement in the perilimbal region minimizes orbital tissue disruption and facilitates surgical accessibility. In this study, we evaluated the surgical feasibility, biocompatibility, and functional performance of the system in a longitudinal preclinical model, integrating refined surgical techniques and biological coverage grafts. This work sought to establish a stable, biologically integrated signal transmission platform to support the development of next-generation retinal prostheses.

Methods

Animals

All procedures were approved by the Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee of National Taiwan University and complied with National Institute of Health guidelines and the ARVO Statement for the Use of Animals in Ophthalmic and Vision Research. Six female Lanyu minipigs (Sus scrofa, >6 months old) contributed 11 eyes: three received perilimbal antenna implantation with double-layer amniotic membrane, two with pericardium graft, two with small intestinal submucosa graft, one underwent sham surgery (peritomy, scleral/muscle exposure, scleral flap, closure), and three served as untreated controls. Perilimbal implantation preserved uveal integrity and visual function. Minipigs were housed individually with limited tactile contact and environmental enrichment.

All surgical procedures and ocular examinations, both preoperatively and postoperatively, were performed under general anesthesia. Anesthesia was induced after premedication with atropine (40 µg/kg, intramuscular [IM]), azaperone (0.4 mg/kg, IM), and Zoletil 100 (4.4 mg/kg, IM; Virbac, Carros, France), followed by isoflurane inhalation (2% for induction and 1.5%–3% for maintenance) delivered via endotracheal intubation. Prophylactic cefazolin (20 mg/kg, intravenous [IV]) was administered before surgery. Animals were mechanically ventilated with 30% O2 at a rate of 10 to 12 breaths/min and a tidal volume of 7 to 8 mL/kg, with continuous physiological monitoring maintained throughout the procedure.

At surgery completion, isoflurane was stopped, oxygen raised to 100%, and ventilation adjusted until spontaneous breathing resumed. Animals were extubated, maintained on oxygen until awake, then monitored during recovery. Postoperatively, implanted eyes received topical tetracycline ointment and prednisolone acetate (1%) twice daily (BID) for 7 days. Systemic therapy for 7 days included cephalexin (500 mg BID), diclofenac (25 mg BID), serrapeptase (5 mg/kg BID), and tranexamic acid (250 mg BID).

Retinal Prosthesis Manufacture and Preparation

The retinal prosthesis used in this study, HARP4K RPS (Iridium Medical Technology Company, Hsinchu City, Taiwan) (Fig. 1), is designed for patients with advanced geographic atrophy. The system has three components: HARP4K implant (with surgical accessories: three carriers and one marking template), HARP4K eyeglasses, and HARP4K fitting console. The implant consists of an elliptical retinal chip (with a packaged size of 4.9 × 4.7 mm) containing photosensors, microelectronic circuitry, and a sputtered iridium oxide film (SIROF) microelectrode array encapsulated in passivation layers. The chip converts incident images into electrical signals to stimulate retinal bipolar cells following subretinal placement. It is connected to a flexible polymer-based substrate (“Flex”) incorporating embedded gold conductors, which also form the secondary inductive coil (paired with a primary coil in the eyeglasses); springs; and fixation suture holes. The Flex is externally encapsulated with a medical-grade silicone elastomer layer to electrically insulate the conductors and to provide long-term biocompatibility with tissues.14 Device material safety testing is included in the Supplementary Materials (Supplementary Method, Supplementary Table S1). The Flex conforms to the ocular curvature to facilitate stable implantation. Carriers protect the chip and guide insertion into the subretinal space near the fovea. We used a marking template, identical in size and curvature to the implant, preoperatively to verify fit and to mark scleral incision and fixation sites.

Surgical Procedure for Perilimbal Antenna Implantation

Perilimbal antenna implantation was performed via an ab externo approach (Fig. 2). Because this study focused on biocompatibility of the perilimbal antennae system and surgical effects of biomaterial coverage, only antenna placement was carried out. After lateral canthotomy and 360° peritomy, all rectus muscles and the superior oblique muscle were identified and secured with 2-0 silk traction sutures. A scleral flap was created at the excision site to expose the uvea. The retinal prosthesis was transected at the neck, leaving the perilimbal antenna intact. The circular antenna was positioned and secured with 9-0 nylon, and the transected neck was inserted beneath the scleral flap and fixed with 9-0 nylon without uveal penetration.

Coverage materials included amniotic membrane (AMNIOAID, HCT Regenerative, New Taipei City, Taiwan), porcine pericardium (ReadiGraft; LifeNet Health, Virginia Beach, VA), and porcine small intestinal submucosa (SIS; BioSIS ECM; Vetrix, Cumming, GA). The amniotic membrane was applied as a double layer for reinforcement. Each material was prepared by excising a central circular portion from square sheets; the circular graft was placed over the scleral flap, and the hollowed square graft was draped over the antenna. Both were secured with interrupted 8-0 Vicryl sutures (Ethicon, Raritan, NJ), followed by peritomy closure with the same material.

External Eye Photography, Fluorescein Staining, and Cobalt Blue Light Examination

External eye examinations were performed using an operating microscope video system to evaluate for antenna exposure, wound dehiscence, melting, infiltration, perforation, and infection. Findings were documented when present. Fluorescein staining was conducted by instilling 1% fluorescein sodium onto the palpebral conjunctiva which was distributed by blinking. The ocular surface was examined under cobalt blue illumination with a binocular indirect ophthalmoscope (SIGMA 250 LED; HEINE Optotechnik, Gilching, Germany), and photographs were taken to document conjunctival or corneal epithelial defects.

Evaluation of Conjunctival Injection

Bulbar conjunctival injection was used as a marker of ocular surface inflammation. External eye photographs were obtained at baseline; on postoperative days 3, 6, 10, 14, and 28; and monthly thereafter for 6 months. Images were preprocessed with inverse gamma correction, non-uniform illumination correction, white balance adjustment, and standard gamma correction.15 Quantification was performed in ImageJ (National Institutes of Health, Bethesda, MD). A region of interest (ROI) was selected in each image, and red, green, and blue (RGB) channels were converted to the CIELAB color space, or L*a*b*, where color L* denotes lightness (0–100), a* the green–red axis (–60 to +60), and b* the blue–yellow axis (–60 to +60).16 Mean L* and a* values within the ROI were measured,17 and the erythema index was calculated as a*/L*.

Anterior Segment Spectral-Domain Optical Coherence Tomography

Anterior segment imaging was performed preoperatively and monthly using a spectral-domain optical coherence tomography (OCT) system (SPECTRALIS; Heidelberg Engineering, Heidelberg, Germany) with a corneal anterior module. Pachymetry mode was applied to capture limbal-centered images, defined by the first hyporeflective corneal boundary. Raster scans perpendicular to the limbus were acquired in four quadrants (superior, inferior, nasal, and temporal) with gentle ocular manipulation. At least three scans per quadrant were obtained; scans lacking a visible scleral spur were excluded. Measurements were averaged across quadrants. Coverage material thickness and conjunctival thickness overlying the antenna were quantified with the anterior segment OCT (AS-OCT) software (Heidelberg Eye Explorer). Perilimbal conjunctival curvature was measured by a four-step manual method: (1) the limbus point was defined as the first corneal boundary, (2) a 1000-µm circle was centered on this point, (3) the conjunctival surface between the limbus and circle intersection was delineated, and (4) the average radius of curvature was calculated using the ImageJ Fiji Kappa plugin.

Non-Anesthetic Schirmer Test for Tear Secretion

The Schirmer test was performed without topical anesthesia at baseline; on postoperative days 3, 6, 14, 21, and 28; and at 6 months. A Schirmer strip (Omni Schirmer Tear Test Strips; Omni Lens Pvt. Ltd., Gujarat, India) was folded and inserted into the lower conjunctival fornix between the palpebral and bulbar conjunctiva. The eyelid was gently closed for 5 minutes, after which the length of the moistened area was measured in millimeters using the manufacturer's scale. Strips were immediately frozen on dry ice and stored at −80°C for subsequent protein extraction and analysis.

Tear Protein Extraction

Tear proteins were extracted from Schirmer strips (5 mm) following a modified protocol.18 Strips were cut, incubated in 150-µL phosphate-buffered saline (PBS) on ice with shaking for 1.5 hours, and centrifuged at 12,000 rpm at 4°C for 10 minutes. Residual fragments were processed using the “piggyback method” (23-gauge needle–pierced tube nested in a 1.5-mL tube) and centrifuged at 13,000 rpm at 4°C for 3 minutes. Protein concentrations were measured spectrophotometrically (NanoVue Plus; GE HealthCare, Chicago, IL), and samples stored at –80°C.

Cytokine Array of Tear Fluid

Tear cytokines were measured in experimental and control eyes at baseline and at postoperative days 3, 6, 10, 14, and 28 using a porcine cytokine antibody array (10 targets; Abcam, Cambridge, UK). Arrays were processed per the manufacturer's instructions: blocking, incubation with diluted tear proteins (100 µL), and detection with biotinylated antibodies and Cy3-equivalent streptavidin, followed by washing, drying, and scanning. Cytokine concentrations were quantified against slide-based standard curves.

Conjunctival Impression Cytology

Impression cytology was performed using cellulose acetate filters (25 mm and 0.22 µm; MilliporeSigma; Burlington, MA) cut into quadrants and marked for orientation. After topical anesthesia and removal of excess fluid, filters were applied to the bulbar conjunctiva across the limbus for 5 to 10 seconds with gentle digital pressure, then removed with blunt forceps and fixed in 75% ethanol for 5 minutes at room temperature. Filters were placed epithelial side up and stained with periodic acid–Schiff (PAS) and hematoxylin (PAS-2-IFU; ScyTek Laboratories, Logan, UT). Samples were treated with periodic acid for 10 minutes, rinsed four times with double-distilled water, incubated with Schiff's reagent for 30 minutes, and washed under running tap water. Sections were then counterstained with Mayer's hematoxylin, dehydrated, cleared, and mounted in xylene and synthetic resin. Goblet cell mucin stained pink (PAS), and nuclei appeared blue (hematoxylin).

Statistical Analysis and Graphical Representation

Statistical analyses were performed using SPSS Statistics 19 (IBM, Chicago, IL). Graphs were generated in Excel (Office LTSC Professional Plus 2024; Microsoft Corp., Redmond, WA). Data were log-transformed when necessary to achieve normality, assessed by the Shapiro–Wilk test. Depending on distribution, parametric or nonparametric tests were applied. Results are presented as mean ± SD, and P < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

Design of Perilimbal Antenna and Radiofrequency Properties of the Coupled System

A three-part mechanical buffer was integrated into the neck and springs of the retinal prosthesis to improve compliance and accommodate ocular movement (Figs. 1b, 1c). The perilimbal antenna functioned as the secondary receiving coil, capturing inductive power from the primary transmitting coil embedded in the eyeglass frame (Figs. 3a–c). This power, up to 20 mW, was sufficient to drive the complementary metal-oxide-semiconductor (CMOS) subretinal chip, which converts visual input into spatiotemporal electrical patterns to stimulate retinal neurons (Fig. 1d). Anatomical constraints required the secondary coil to be implanted circumferentially around the limbus beneath the conjunctiva. The secondary coil was designed to provide strong electromagnetic coupling, biocompatibility, mechanical durability, and surgical feasibility while avoiding bulky components that could irritate ocular tissues. Fabricated on a thin flexible substrate with gold traces embedded in a dielectric layer, the coil had an overall thickness of approximately 40 µm.

Because image processing was integrated into the subretinal chip, video transmission between the external camera and implant was unnecessary. This allowed use of a low-frequency radiofrequency (RF) link for low-bandwidth, bidirectional communication. A proprietary amplitude-shift keying/load-shift keying (ASK/LSK)–based protocol, rather than standard Wi-Fi or Bluetooth bands, was adopted to enhance security and minimize interference.

Finite element method (FEM) simulations were used to optimize the spiral secondary coil and its coupling with the irregularly shaped primary coil. A first-order equivalent circuit model was developed (Fig. 3), and the voltage transfer function between primary coils (Vin) and secondary coils (Vout) was expressed as (1)VoutVin=ω2ω02kLs/Lpk2+1QpQs-1-ω2ω02+j1-ω2ωo21Qp-1Qswhere subscripts p and s denote the primary and secondary antenna, respectively; L is the inductance; Q is the quality factor (QP = ωLp/Rp, Qs = ωLs/[Rs + RL]), where R represents the resistance, Rp and Rs are the general resistance of primary antennae circuit and secondary antennae circuit, respectively, and RL is the load (or the resistance) of the retinal chip; ω is the operating frequency; ω0 is the resonant angular frequency; k is the coupling coefficient; and j denotes the imaginary unit (-1). At resonance, the impedance becomes purely resistive, and maximum power transfer is achieved when kc=1QpQs.

Physiological factors were incorporated into simulations. Immersion of the secondary coil in electrolytic fluids reduced the resonant frequency and quality factor due to parasitic capacitances. Additional corrections accounted for proximity effects, RF skin effects, and radiation losses.

To validate these results, network analyzer testing (E5061B; Keysight Technologies, Santa Rosa, CA) was performed with the secondary coil immersed in saline (Fig. 3d). With coils separated by >2 cm and tuned below 10 MHz, the inductive link achieved approximately 60% coupling efficiency. Detailed engineering aspects of the device will be reported separately.

Preclinical Study of Perilimbal Antenna Implantation With Coverage Materials

In the preclinical study, none of the eyes that underwent implantation surgery exhibited device exposure, conjunctival melting, infectious complications, or granulation tissue related to impaired conjunctival healing during the 6-month postoperative follow-up. The ocular surface remained stable and intact in all animals (Table 1). In eyes receiving perilimbal antenna implantation with coverage material, mean perilimbal conjunctival thickness measured 444.7 ± 91.4 µm at postoperative month 1 (POM1) and 399.4 ± 158.2 µm at POM6, with no significant difference from the preoperative baseline of 400.5 ± 89.6 µm (P = 0.398 and P = 0.612, respectively) (Table 1). Longitudinal analysis likewise showed no significant postoperative change (P = 0.196) (Table 2). Differences among coverage materials were minimal (Table 2; Supplementary Table S2).

In contrast, all implanted coverage materials exhibited progressive thinning, decreasing from 360.7 ± 119.5 µm at POM1 to 71.4 ± 103.0 µm at POM6. Linear mixed-effects analysis confirmed a significant reduction over time (P = 0.002) (Table 2). Subgroup analyses by material type did not reach statistical significance (Supplementary Table S2).

As an indicator of perilimbal conjunctival bulging, the mean radius of curvature significantly decreased at POM1 (2.36 ± 0.81 mm) compared with baseline (9.29 ± 3.83 mm; P = 0.018) (Table 1). The curvature gradually normalized over time, with the mean radius increasing to 8.02 ± 3.73 mm by POM6 (P = 0.018; longitudinal analysis P = 0.011) (Tables 1, 2).

Tear cytokine analysis revealed a transient postoperative inflammatory response (Table 3). Among the evaluated cytokines, IL-1β and IL-8 demonstrated the most pronounced early postoperative elevations, with subsequent normalization within approximately 2 weeks. IL-1β levels rose significantly from baseline to postoperative day 3 (POD3) (P = 0.005; 95% confidence interval [CI], 2.8–7.2) and decreased from POD3 to POD28 (P = 0.013; 95% CI, −4.0 to −0.99). Similarly, IL-8 levels increased sharply from baseline to POD3 (P < 0.001; 95% CI, 6.2–7.4) and declined from POD3 to POD28 (P < 0.001; 95% CI, −8.5 to −5.4). IL-6 also exhibited a significant postoperative rise (POD3 to baseline: P = 0.036; 95% CI, 0.37–5.6) with normalization by POD14 (P = 0.028; 95% CI, −3.0 to −0.35); however, the reduction by POD28 was not statistically significant compared with POD3 (P = 0.369; 95% CI, −6.7 to 3.4).

Discussion

This study introduces a novel, accessible, and efficient antenna model for retinal prosthesis and demonstrated its biocompatibility in a minipig preclinical study using a refined surgical technique with coverage graft materials. Existing approaches highlight the challenges of antenna placement. The Argus II chip–based system placed its antenna in the orbital region, but maintaining stability in delicate ocular tissue has proved difficult.6 Photovoltaic-based devices bypass antennas by transmitting signals via light stimulation.4,11 Here, our proposed perilimbal antenna offers an alternative: its external placement simplifies implantation, improves accessibility for maintenance, and minimizes orbital disruption.

Mechanical compliance is critical for neural prosthetics, as rigid devices risk chronic inflammation or microtrauma.19,20 Because retinal prostheses mechanically couple extraocular structures to the neuroretina, incorporating buffers is essential to reduce injury during ocular movements and to prevent migration of the stimulating pad (Figs. 1b, 1c). Subconjunctival implants, however, are vulnerable to conjunctival thinning, erosion, and exposure, as seen with anti-glaucoma devices.21,22 To address this, coverage grafts are often used.23–25 Early experiments showed that perilimbal antenna implantation without grafts increased wound dehiscence and exposure risk. Refinement of the surgical technique, including coverage grafts, careful bare sclera preparation, advancement of Tenon's capsule, and secure circumferential suturing, eliminated exposure and associated complications.

Coverage materials have distinct characteristics for ocular surface applications.24,26 Amniotic membrane promotes wound healing,27,28 porcine small intestinal submucosa has been used in animal corneal repair,29,30 and human pericardium has shown safety in Argus II implantation.31 In this study, amniotic membrane, SIS, and pericardium all maintained surface integrity and prevented device exposure (Table 1; Supplementary Table S2). The observed reduction in graft thickness over 6 months suggests that their role is less structural reinforcement than scaffolding for adhesion and conjunctival wound healing. Consistent with this, endpoint histology showed preserved conjunctival architecture adjacent to the implanted antenna (Supplementary Fig. S1), and anterior segment indocyanine green angiography demonstrated perilimbal vascular features comparable between implanted and healthy eyes (Supplementary Fig. S2), supporting long-term tissue compatibility.

A potential concern with perilimbal elevation is tear film disturbance leading to corneal dellen, as described after trabeculectomy or subconjunctival silicone oil.32–34 Although rare, such complications may cause perforation35 and are difficult to manage.36 In this study, no dellen, epithelial defects, or infections were detected, and imaging confirmed stable conjunctival curvature (Figs. 4b, 5c), indicating that the antenna conforms to the ocular surface without compromising tear dynamics.

Tear cytokines influence conjunctival wound healing and serve as biomarkers of ocular surface health.37,38 Proinflammatory mediators such as TNF-α, IL-1β, IL-6, IL-8, IL-12, and IFN-γ are associated with injury,39–44 whereas TGF-β exerts anti-inflammatory effects.45 Postoperative analysis revealed transient increases in TGF-β and IL-8 with rapid normalization (Fig. 5), consistent with a controlled wound-healing response rather than chronic inflammation.

The Schirmer I test assesses both basal and reflex aqueous tear secretion and was influenced by multiple factors.46,47 Despite lower baseline tear production compared with humans, postoperative increases likely reflected transient irritation, and normalization suggested preserved ocular surface integrity and intact sensory pathways. Importantly, long-term dry eye did not develop. Goblet cell density, another marker of ocular surface health,48–50 remained stable at 6 months (Table 1; Fig. 4e), further indicating an absence of chronic inflammation.

As neural prosthetics evolve toward more precise and efficient integration,51,52 a stable and biocompatible interface for retinal stimulation remains a critical challenge. This interface consisted of neuroretina stimulation and signal transmission. The perilimbal antenna system, validated here with a refined surgical approach and graft coverage, offers advantages over orbital placement of antennae systems: reduced surgical trauma, proximity for efficient coupling, and easier revision if needed. Its compatibility with the ocular surface also suggests potential utility in other biomedical implants as devices continue to miniaturize.

This study has several limitations. The sample size was relatively small, as the study was designed as a pilot feasibility and safety evaluation; therefore, definitive conclusions regarding differences among graft materials could not be drawn. Future studies with larger cohorts will be required to validate these findings and enable formal comparative analyses. Nevertheless, this proof-of-concept study provides preliminary evidence supporting the feasibility of this approach for retinal prosthesis development. The porcine ocular anatomy may not fully mirror human physiology, particularly given the greater robustness of the porcine corneal epithelium; nonetheless, the absence of adverse events supports the feasibility of translation to human studies. Finally, a perilimbal implant could potentially complicate future ocular surgeries, although appropriate design refinements may help mitigate this risk.

In conclusion, this preclinical study demonstrated that the perilimbal antenna system provides a stable, biocompatible, and efficient interface for retinal prostheses. By reducing surgical complexity while preserving ocular surface health, this approach offers a promising foundation for future retinal prosthetic systems and potentially for broader applications in biomedical implantation.
