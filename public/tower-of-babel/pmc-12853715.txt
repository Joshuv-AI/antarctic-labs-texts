Association between inflammatory markers and Parkinson’s disease risk: a cross-sectional, propensity score-matched analysis of NHANES data
Authors: Yue Sun, Shuang Li, Shengming Shi, Yan Liu
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundParkinson’s disease (PD) is a chronic neurodegenerative disorder characterized by motor and non-motor symptoms, with neuroinflammation hypothesized to contribute to its pathogenesis. Systemic inflammation, as indicated by elevated peripheral inflammatory markers, has been implicated in PD; however, the relationship between complete blood count (CBC)-derived inflammatory markers and the presence of PD remains unclear.MethodsWe analyzed data from the National Health and Nutrition Examination Survey (NHANES) spanning 1999 to 2018, including 15,349 participants. Propensity score matching was employed to balance baseline characteristics between PD and non-PD groups. Multivariate logistic regression models were used to evaluate the association between CBC-derived inflammatory markers, particularly the neutrophil-to-lymphocyte ratio (NLR), and the presence of PD.ResultsAfter multivariable adjustment, elevated NLR was significantly associated with higher odds of PD. Individuals in the highest NLR quartile had higher odds of PD compared to those in the lowest quartile (OR: 2.18, 95% CI: 1.04–4.68; P for trend < 0.0001). This association was consistent across subgroups, including gender, diabetes status, hypertension, obesity, smoking, and alcohol consumption. Other CBC-derived markers, such as the monocyte-to-lymphocyte ratio (MLR), platelet-to-lymphocyte ratio (PLR), systemic immune-inflammation index (SII), and systemic inflammation response index (SIRI), did not show consistent associations with the presence of PD.ConclusionsElevated NLR is significantly associated with the presence of PD among U.S. adults, independent of key confounders. However, due to the cross-sectional study design and reliance on self-reported PD status, causality cannot be inferred, and misclassification bias cannot be excluded. Further longitudinal studies are warranted to validate these findings and clarify the temporal relationship between systemic inflammation and PD.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12883-025-04598-3.

Background

Parkinson’s disease (PD) is a chronic progressive neurodegenerative disorder characterized by motor symptoms, including tremor, rigidity, bradykinesia, and postural instability, as well as non-motor manifestations such as cognitive impairment, depression, and autonomic dysfunction [1, 2]. The pathological hallmarks of PD include the loss of dopaminergic neurons in the substantia nigra pars compacta and the presence of intracellular inclusions known as Lewy bodies, which contain aggregated α-synuclein [3, 4]. Despite extensive research, the precise etiology of PD remains elusive. However, accumulating evidence suggests that chronic inflammation plays a crucial role in the pathogenesis and progression of the disease [5–7]. Recent advances in microbiome research have highlighted the significant role of the gut microbiome in modulating neuroinflammatory processes that are critical in the pathogenesis of PD. Studies suggest that gut dysbiosis can influence systemic inflammation levels and contribute to the neurodegenerative processes observed in PD through the production of pro-inflammatory metabolites and modulation of the gut-brain axis [8]. Including the dynamics of gut microbiota provides a broader perspective on the inflammatory pathways involved in PD, offering potential avenues for novel therapeutic interventions.

Neuroinflammation, mediated by activated microglia and astrocytes, is a well-established feature of PD [9, 10]. Recent studies have identified elevated peripheral inflammatory markers, such as C-reactive protein (CRP) and interleukin-6 (IL-6), in PD patients compared to healthy controls, suggesting systemic inflammation as a potential contributor to neurodegeneration [11–15]. In addition to the local inflammatory response in the central nervous system, systemic inflammation has also been implicated in the pathogenesis of PD [16, 17].

In recent years, there has been a growing interest in the use of complete blood count (CBC)-derived inflammatory markers as potential biomarkers for various diseases, including neurodegenerative disorders [18]. These markers, which include the monocyte-to-lymphocyte ratio (MLR), neutrophil-to-lymphocyte ratio (NLR), platelet-to-lymphocyte ratio (PLR), systemic immune-inflammation index (SII), systemic inflammation response index (SIRI), and platelet-to-lymphocyte ratio-to-mean platelet volume ratio (PIV), have been shown to reflect the systemic inflammatory status and have prognostic value in various conditions, such as cancer and cardiovascular diseases [19–21]. However, the utility of CBC-derived inflammatory markers, such as NLR, remains underexplored in large-scale studies. Despite increasing recognition of the role of inflammation in neurodegeneration, there remains a lack of large-scale, population-based research that systematically compares multiple CBC-derived inflammatory markers and clarifies their diagnostic and prognostic relevance in PD. Most existing studies have been limited by small sample sizes and heterogeneous methodologies, and no prior analysis has comprehensively evaluated these markers within a nationally representative cohort. This gap underscores the need for robust, population-level analyses to clarify their diagnostic and prognostic relevance.

The National Health and Nutrition Examination Survey (NHANES) is a cross-sectional, population-based survey conducted by the Centers for Disease Control and Prevention (CDC) in the United States [22]. The survey collects comprehensive health and nutrition data from a nationally representative sample of the civilian, non-institutionalized U.S. population. The NHANES database provides a high-quality, nationally representative dataset ideally suited for epidemiological studies on PD and systemic inflammation. To further reduce confounding bias and enhance the validity of group comparisons, propensity score matching was employed, allowing for more balanced baseline characteristics between PD cases and controls.

The aim of this study was to examine the association between levels of inflammatory markers and the presence of PD. Understanding the role of systemic inflammation in PD may provide valuable insights into the pathogenesis of the disease and facilitate the development of novel diagnostic and therapeutic strategies. Among CBC-derived indices, NLR is hypothesized to have the strongest association with PD due to its unique ability to integrate information from both innate and adaptive immune responses. Prior studies suggest that NLR may be particularly sensitive to neurodegenerative processes, as alterations in neutrophil and lymphocyte counts can reflect underlying immune dysregulation relevant to PD pathophysiology.

Methods

Study population

The study utilized data from the NHANES from 1999 to 2018. A total of 101,316 participants were included in the NHANES 1999–2018 dataset. The initial sample included 101,316 participants. Participants with missing data on key variables, including blood cell counts and essential demographic or clinical information, were excluded from the analysis. Exclusion criteria were applied, removing participants under the age of 40 (n = 65,064) and those missing data on blood cell count or basic information (n = 20,903). After these exclusions, the study sample consisted of 15,349 participants, including 135 individuals withPD and 15,214 non-PD participants (Fig. 1). The proportion of missing data was relatively small and did not exhibit patterns suggestive of systematic bias after exclusion. Therefore, we opted not to perform multiple imputations. To minimize potential confounding effects, propensity score matching (PSM) was performed at a 1:2 ratio, matching each PD participant with two non-PD participants based on age, gender, hypertension, diabetes, and obesity. The final matched sample included 135 PD participants and 270 non-PD participants (Fig. 1).

Data collection and variable definitions

Demographic and socioeconomic data, including age, sex, race/ethnicity, education level, marital status, and family income, were collected through household interviews. Body mass index (BMI) was calculated as weight in kilograms divided by height in meters squared. Smoking status was categorized as never, former, or current smoker. Alcohol consumption was classified as never, ≤ 1 drink per week, 2–5 drinks per week, or > 5 drinks per week. Hypertension was defined as systolic blood pressure ≥ 140 mmHg, diastolic blood pressure ≥ 90 mmHg, or the use of antihypertensive medications. Diabetes was defined as a self-reported physician diagnosis, the use of antidiabetic medications, or a fasting plasma glucose ≥ 126 mg/dL.

Parkinson’s disease was identified according to the participant’s use of specific Parkinson’s medications, since direct self-reported diagnosis of PD was not available in the NHANES dataset [23, 24]. We acknowledge that reliance on self-reported diagnosis and medication data may introduce misclassification bias, as some cases may be undiagnosed or inaccurately reported. This limitation was considered in the interpretation of our findings. CBC data, including white blood cell count, lymphocyte number, monocyte number, segmented neutrophils number, eosinophils number, basophils number, red blood cell count, and platelet count, were obtained from the NHANES laboratory files.

CBC-derived inflammatory markers

The CBC parameters are derived using the Beckman Coulter® method, which combines counting and sizing with an automatic device for diluting and mixing samples. For more details, refer to Chap. 7 of the NHANES Laboratory/Medical Technologists Procedures Manual (https://wwwn.cdc.gov/nchs/nhanes/search/datapage.aspx?Component=Laboratory). For the primary analyses, each marker was categorized into quartiles based on their distribution among the study population [25, 26].

Statistical analysis

Baseline comparisons

Baseline characteristics between PD and non-PD groups were compared using the chi-square test for categorical variables (using the stats, tableone package) and the Wilcoxon rank-sum test for continuous variables. The Anderson-Darling normality test (nortest package) was applied to assess the normality of continuous variables.

Association analysis

Associations between blood cell counts (and their ratios) and PD were assessed using logistic regression models (stats, broom, survey package), reporting odds ratios (ORs) and 95% confidence intervals (CIs). Three models were constructed:

Model 1: unadjusted; Model 2: adjusted for sociodemographic factors (age, gender, race, education, income, marital status); Model 3: further adjusted for health-related factors (body mass index, smoking, alcohol consumption, hypertension, diabetes, obesity).

P-values for trend were calculated by treating quartiles as continuous variables in the logistic regression.

Subgroup and interaction analyses

Subgroup analyses were performed according to gender, diabetes, hypertension, obesity, smoking, and alcohol consumption. Interaction terms between subgroups and CBC markers were included in multivariable logistic regression models to test for statistical interaction.

Restricted cubic spline analysis

Restricted cubic spline (RCS) analysis (rms package) was conducted to examine potential nonlinear relationships between NLR and PD risk. The optimal cutoff value for NLR was determined using RCS analysis with three knots.

Propensity score matching

To minimize confounding, PSM was implemented using the nearest neighbor method at a 1:2 ratio (one PD case matched with two controls) with the MatchIt package. The propensity score model included age, sex, hypertension, obesity, and diabetes. Standardized mean differences (SMDs) for each covariate were calculated before and after matching (cobalt package), with SMD < 0.1 indicating good balance.

Survival analysis

For mortality analyses, NHANES data were linked to the National Death Index (NDI) to obtain mortality status and follow-up time. Kaplan-Meier survival curves and log-rank tests (survival and survminer packages) were used to compare survival among different quartiles of blood cell count ratios. Cox proportional hazards models were applied to estimate hazard ratios (HRs) and 95% CIs for mortality outcomes, with adjustments as described above.

Sensitivity analysis

To further control for potential confounding by medications that may alter systemic inflammation, we performed sensitivity analyses by including anti-diabetic and anti-hypertensive medication use as additional covariates in the multivariable logistic regression models. We constructed three separate models: one adjusting for anti-diabetic medications, one for anti-hypertensive medications, and one adjusting for both. The results are presented in Supplementary Tables S1–S3.

Software

All statistical analyses were performed using R software (version 4.2.0). A two-sided P-value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Ethical approval

All methods were carried out in accordance with relevant guidelines and regulations. Study protocols for the NHANES were approved by the National Center for Health Statistics Ethics Review Board, and all the participants signed an informed consent form before participating in the study. Informed consent was obtained from all subjects and/or their legal guardians. All information from the NHANES is available and free for the public, and therefore does not require the consent of a human research ethics committee. Information about the NHANES database can be found at https://www.cdc.gov/nchs/nhanes/index.htm.

Results

Study population characteristics

The study included a total of 15,349 participants from the NHANES 1999–2018 dataset. Before PSM, significant differences were observed between the non-PD and PD groups in terms of age, family income, marital status, race, Poverty income ratio (PIR), alcohol consumption, hypertension, diabetes, white blood cell count, lymphocyte number, segmented neutrophils number, and red blood cell count (Table 1). To minimize the influence of potential confounding factors, 1:2 PSM was performed, resulting in 270 non-PD participants matched with 135 PD participants. After propensity score matching, the baseline characteristics between the PD and non-PD groups were substantially improved. As shown in Fig. 2 and Supplementary Fig. 2, the absolute SMDs for all matched covariates—including age, sex, hypertension, diabetes, and obesity—were reduced to below 0.1, indicating that the matching process effectively balanced the primary variables between groups.

Association between NLR and PD risk

The distributions of inflammatory indexes, including NLR, are illustrated in Supplementary Fig. 1. All markers showed right-skewed distributions with a concentration of values at the lower end and long tails extending toward higher values, highlighting the variability and non-normality of these parameters in the study population. The primary focus of this study was to evaluate the relationship between NLR, a readily available inflammatory marker, and the presence of PD. In the multivariate logistic regression analysis, higher NLR levels were significantly associated with higher odds of having PD (Table 2). Compared to the lowest NLR quartile (Q1), the ORs and 95% confidence intervals (CIs) for PD in Q2, Q3, and Q4 were 1.250 (0.634–2.489, P = 0.521), 1.803 (0.950–3.493, P = 0.076), and 2.750 (1.505–5.174, P = 0.001), respectively (P for trend < 0.0001). The association remained significant after adjusting for potential confounders, including age, gender, BMI, family income, marital status, race, education, PIR, smoking, alcohol consumption, hypertension, obesity, and diabetes, in Model 2 and Model 3, with ORs (95% CIs) of 2.357 (1.220–4.668, P = 0.012) and 2.176 (1.042–4.681, P = 0.043) for the highest versus lowest NLR quartile, respectively. These findings suggest that elevated NLR is independently associated with the presence of PD, supporting the role of systemic inflammation in PD pathogenesis. However, due to the cross-sectional design, causality cannot be inferred. Further studies are needed to clarify the potential role of systemic inflammation in PD.

Association between inflammatory markers and PD risk

In addition to NLR, the associations between other inflammatory markers (MLR, PIV, PLR, SII, and SIRI) and the presence of PD were also investigated (Table 2). Higher levels of PLR and SIRI were significantly associated with higher odds of having PD in the univariate analysis (Model 1), with ORs (95% CIs) of 1.820 (1.025–3.258, P = 0.043) and 1.890 (1.050–3.482, P = 0.037) for the highest versus lowest quartile, respectively. However, these associations were attenuated and no longer statistically significant after adjusting for potential confounders in Model 2 and Model 3. No significant associations were observed between MLR, PIV, or SII and the presence of PD in any of the models. These results highlight the importance of considering potential confounding factors when examining the relationship between inflammatory markers and PD, and suggest that NLR may be more robustly associated with the presence of PD compared to other inflammatory markers. To account for the potential confounding effects of medications that may influence systemic inflammation, we conducted sensitivity analyses by further adjusting for anti-diabetic medications (Supplementary Table S1), anti-hypertensive medications (Supplementary Table S2), and both types of medications simultaneously (Supplementary Table S3). After these adjustments, participants in the highest NLR quartile (Q4) consistently had significantly higher odds of having Parkinson’s disease compared to those in the lowest quartile (Q1), with odds ratios of 2.190 (95% CI: 1.046–4.725, P = 0.042), 2.167 (95% CI: 1.036–4.670, P = 0.044), and 2.180 (95% CI: 1.040–4.711, P = 0.043), respectively.

Subgroup analysis and interaction tests

To explore the potential effect modification by various factors on the association between NLR and the presence of PD, subgroup analysis and interaction tests were performed (Fig. 3). The association between higher NLR and the presence of PD was more pronounced in males (OR 3.403, 95% CI 1.295–9.863, P = 0.018), non-diabetic individuals (OR 2.444, 95% CI 1.225–5.021, P = 0.013), non-hypertensive individuals (OR 3.600, 95% CI 1.295–10.96, P = 0.019), non-obese individuals (OR 4.750, 95% CI 2.017–12.33, P < 0.001), never smokers (OR 4.210, 95% CI 1.727–11.22, P = 0.003), and those who consumed alcohol less than once (OR 3.194, 95% CI 1.540–7.016, P = 0.003). However, the interaction tests did not reveal any statistically significant effect modification (all P for interaction > 0.05), indicating that the association between NLR and the presence of PD was consistent across different subgroups.

Optimal cutoff value of NLR for PD risk

To further investigate the non-linear relationship between NLR and The presence of PD, an RCS analysis was conducted (Fig. 4). The analysis demonstrated a non-linear relationship, with a cutoff value of NLR at 2.15 corresponding to an OR of 2.68 (95% CI 1.03–7.01, P = 0.044) for the presence of PD. This finding suggests that individuals with an NLR above 2.15 had significantly higher odds of having PD compared to those with lower NLR values. To further investigate potential non-linear associations between inflammatory indices and the presence of PD, we conducted RCS analyses for MLR, PIV, PLR, SII, and SIRI (Supplementary Fig. 3). These models provide a flexible approach to modeling continuous exposures and help to mitigate concerns about power loss and overfitting associated with categorization. The RCS analyses revealed that, among these indices, only SII demonstrated a statistically significant non-linear association with the presence of PD (P = 0.032), indicating that higher SII values were associated with increased odds of PD. For MLR, PIV, PLR, and SIRI, no significant non-linear relationships with the presence of PD were observed.

Survival analysis

To examine the possible association between inflammatory marker levels and survival among PD patients, Kaplan-Meier survival curves were plotted to compare survival probabilities among different quartiles of inflammatory markers (Fig. 5A-F). No statistically significant differences in survival were observed for MLR (P = 0.068), NLR (P = 0.140), PIV (P = 0.500), PLR (P = 0.210), SII (P = 0.990), or SIRI (P = 0.240) quartiles. Although these findings do not support a significant association between inflammatory markers and survival in PD patients, further studies with larger sample sizes and longer follow-up periods may be needed to confirm these results.

Discussion

NLR is considered a marker of systemic inflammation, reflecting the balance between neutrophil-mediated acute inflammatory responses and lymphocyte-mediated regulatory pathways [29, 30]. Elevated NLR has been recognized not only as a simple, cost-effective, and readily available clinical marker but also as a prognostic indicator in various diseases [30, 31], including cardiovascular diseases, cancers, and chronic inflammatory conditions [32, 33]. In PD, inflammation is increasingly recognized as associated with disease progression. Neuroinflammation, characterized by activation of microglia and astrocytes in the brain, is a well-documented phenomenon in PD [34]. Our study identified a significant association between elevated NLR and the presence of PD in nationally representative sample from NHANES. The association persisted after adjustment for a comprehensive set of confounders, which is consistent with the hypothesis that systemic inflammation is related to PD pathogenesis. The observed dose-response relationship, with higher NLR quartiles associated with increased PD risk, is consistent with prior work s reporting associations between inflammatory processes and neurodegeneration [35–37]. The optimal cutoff value of NLR determined through RCS analysis further suggests its potential utility as a biomarker for identifying individuals with a higher likelihood of having PD.

However, several important limitations must be considered. First, our analysis is based on a cross-sectional design, which precludes any inference of temporal or causal relationships between NLR and PD. It remains unclear whether elevated NLR precedes PD onset or results from disease-related processes. Reverse causation is possible: individuals with PD may have higher NLR due to underlying disease activity, comorbidities, or associated infections, rather than NLR being a true risk factor for PD. This is a recognized limitation of cross-sectional studies, and even longitudinal cohorts face challenges in establishing causality [38].

Secondly, the lack of data on PD severity, duration, or clinical stages in NHANES may influence the observed associations, as advanced stages of PD could coincide with comorbidities or infections that elevate inflammatory markers. That study reported an association between NLR and PD progression, suggesting that our observed NLR-PD association might partially reflect undetected differences in disease severity [39]. Third, the analytic subset used in this study, while derived from a high-quality and nationally representative dataset, was relatively small. This sample size limits statistical power, particularly in subgroup analyses, and may reduce the generalizability of our findings. We have therefore moderated our claims regarding the robustness of the dataset and the general applicability of our results. And another key limitation of our study is the ascertainment of PD status based on self-report and relevant medication use, which may be subject to misclassification. Such misclassification is likely to be non-differential with respect to inflammatory marker levels, potentially biasing observed associations toward the null.

In comparing our findings with previous research, both consistencies and discrepancies are apparent. Several longitudinal cohort studies have reported that elevated markers of systemic inflammation, including NLR, are associated with a higher likelihood of developing PD [40, 41]. However, not all studies have found significant associations, particularly after multivariable adjustment or in populations differing by age, ethnicity, or comorbidity burden. Our findings that other inflammatory markers (MLR, PIV, PLR, SII, SIRI) were not robustly associated with PD after adjustment are consistent with some reports, but contrast with others where markers such as PLR or SII have shown associations [42, 43]. These differences may reflect variations in study design, population characteristics, and analytical approaches.

Future research should address these limitations. Prospective cohort studies with repeated measurement of inflammatory markers prior to PD diagnosis are needed to clarify the temporal sequence and causal nature of these associations. Mechanistic studies are warranted to explore the biological pathways linking systemic inflammation and neurodegeneration. Additionally, intervention trials examining whether modulation of systemic inflammation is associated with changes inPD risk or progression would provide important insights. Expanding research to diverse populations and integrating genetic and environmental data may help to unravel the complex etiology of PD. In conclusion, our findings indicate that higher NLR is associated with the presence of PD, independent of established risk factors. However, due to the cross-sectional design, modest sample size, and potential for unmeasured confounding, these results should be interpreted with caution. Validation in prospective cohorts and mechanistic studies is needed before NLR can be considered a clinically useful biomarker or a potential target for intervention in PD.
