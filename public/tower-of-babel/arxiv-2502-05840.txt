The memory of ω \omega -regular and BC ⁡ ( 𝚺 2 0 ) {\color[rgb]{0.0195,0.2109,0.2539}\mathrm{BC}(\mathbf{\Sigma}^{0}_{2})} objectives
Antonio Casares, Pierre Ohlmann

Abstract
In the context of 2-player zero-sum infinite duration games played on (potentially infinite) graphs, the memory of an objective is the smallest integer k k such that in any game won by Eve, she has a strategy with ≤ k \leq k states of memory. For ω \omega -regular objectives, checking whether the memory equals a given number k k was not known to be decidable. In this work, we focus on objectives in BC ⁡ ( 𝚺 2 0 ) {\color[rgb]{0.0195,0.2109,0.2539}\mathrm{BC}(\mathbf{\Sigma}^{0}_{2})} , i.e. recognised by a potentially infinite deterministic parity automaton. We provide a class of automata that recognise objectives with memory ≤ k \leq k , leading to the following results: • for ω \omega -regular objectives, the memory over finite and infinite games coincides and can be computed in NP ; • given two objectives W 1 W_{1} and W 2 W_{2} in BC ⁡ ( 𝚺 2 0 ) {\color[rgb]{0.0195,0.2109,0.2539}\mathrm{BC}(\mathbf{\Sigma}^{0}_{2})} and assuming W 1 W_{1} is prefix-independent, the memory of W 1 ∪ W 2 W_{1}\cup W_{2} is at most the product of the memories of W 1 W_{1} and W 2 W_{2} . Our results also apply to chromatic memory, the variant where strategies can update their memory state only depending on which colour is seen.

Introduction

Context: Strategy complexity in infinite duration games

We study infinite duration games on graphs in which two players, called Eve and Adam, interact by moving a token along the edges of a (potentially infinite) edge-coloured directed graph. Each vertex belongs to one player, who chooses where to move next during a play. This interaction goes on for an infinite duration, producing an infinite path in the graph. The winner is determined according to a language of infinite sequences of colours
W
, called the objective of the game; Eve aims to produce a path coloured by a sequence in
W
, while Adam tries to prevent this.
This model is widespread for its use in verification and synthesis
[
14
]
.

In order to achieve their goal, players use strategies, which are representations of the course of all possible plays together with instructions on how to act in each scenario.
In this work, we are interested in optimal strategies for Eve, that is, strategies that guarantee a victory whenever this is possible. More precisely, we are interested in the complexity of such strategies, or in other words, in the succinctness of the representation of the space of plays.

Positionality.

The simplest strategies are those that assign in advance an outgoing edge to each vertex owned by Eve, and always play along this edge, disregarding all the other features of the play.
All the information required to implement such a strategy appears in the game graph itself.
Objectives for which such strategies are sufficient to play optimally are called
positional
(or memoryless).
Understanding positionality has been the object of a long line of research. The landmark results of Gimbert and Zielonka
[
21
]
and Colcombet and Niwiński
[
17
]
gave a good understanding of which objectives are bi-positional, i.e. positional for both players.

More recently, Ohlmann proposed to use universal graphs as a tool for studying positionality (taking Eve’s point of view)
[
27
]
.
This led to many advances in the study of positionality
[
2
,
25
]
, and most notably, a characterisation of positional
\omega
-regular
objectives by Casares and Ohlmann
[
10
]
, together with a polynomial time decision procedure (and some other important corollaries, more discussion below).

Strategies with memory.

In many scenarios, playing optimally requires distinguishing plays that end in the same vertex.
A seminal result of Büchi and Landweber
[
7
]
states that in finite games where the objective is an
\omega
-regular
language, the winner has a winning strategy that can be implemented by a finite automaton processing the edges of the game; this result was later extended to infinite game graphs by Gurevich and Harrington
[
22
]
.
Here, the states of the automaton are interpreted as memory states of the strategy, and a natural measure of the complexity of a strategy is the number of such states.
More precisely, the memory of an objective
W
is the minimal
k
such that whenever Eve wins a game with objective
W
, she has a winning strategy with
k
states of memory.
For
\omega
-regular
objectives, this is always finite
[
7
,
22
]
, while the case of positionality discussed above corresponds to memory
k=1
.

Characterising the memory of objectives has been a recurrent research subject since the 1990s.
Notable results include the characterisation for Muller objectives by Dziembowski, Jurdziński, and Walukiewicz
[
18
]
, or for closed objectives by Colcombet, Fijalkow and Horn
[
15
]
.
However, these are all rather restricted classes of
\omega
-regular
objectives.
The problem of deciding the memory of
\omega
-regular
objectives has been raised in many occasions (see for instance
[

[

,
[

, or
[

]
),
but prior to this work, even computing the memory of open

-regular
objectives was not known to be decidable.

Chromatic memory.

In the special case where the automata implementing strategies are only allowed to read the colours on the edges of the game graph, instead of the edges themselves, we speak of chromatic memory.
In his PhD thesis, Kopczyński showed that, for
prefix-independent

\omega
-regular
objectives and over finite game graphs, the

can be computed in exponential time
[
23

.
Recently, it was shown that computing the

of some restricted subclasses of

-regular
objectives is in fact

[
12
]
and for topologically open or closed objectives
[

]
.
However, the chromatic and the unconstrained memory of objectives may differ, even exponentially

12
,
8
]
.

2.3

We now introduce and discuss the key notion used in our main characterisation, which adapts the notion from

A

The first two items above state that

This creates a tension with the third item, which states that even with all these added transitions, the

Let us focus on the third item for now, which will lead to a correct definition for

Take an accepting run

[d_{2}]^{*}_{\mathrm{odd}}
if
\tau^{\prime}<_{i_{T}+1}\tau
).
As
z\leq x^{(0)}_{2}
, we have
q_{2}\xrightarrow{\varepsilon:z}q^{\prime}_{2}
. Also,
q_{1}\xrightarrow{\varepsilon:x^{(0)}_{1}-1}q^{\prime}_{1}
.
Using point (3) of the definition of
\overline{\mathcal{T}}
, we have
\tau\xrightarrow{(x^{(0)}_{1}-1,z):2i_{T}+2}\tau^{\prime}
.
We conclude that
r\xrightarrow{\varepsilon:x^{(0)}}r^{\prime}
.

b) Either
{\color[rgb]{0.0195,0.2109,0.2539}\mathsf{ind}}_{\tau}(x^{(0)}_{1})\leq i_{T}
or
{\color[rgb]{0.0195,0.2109,0.2539}\mathsf{ind}}_{\tau}(x^{(0)}_{2})\leq i_{T}
.

We assume w.l.o.g. that
{\color[rgb]{0.0195,0.2109,0.2539}\mathsf{ind}}_{\tau}(x^{(0)}_{1})<{\color[rgb]{0.0195,0.2109,0.2539}\mathsf{ind}}_{\tau}(x^{(0)}_{2})
.
We let
x^{(0)}=2{\color[rgb]{0.0195,0.2109,0.2539}\mathsf{ind}}_{\tau}(x^{(0)}_{1})
; note that
\tau\xrightarrow{(x^{(0)}_{1},x^{(0)}_{2}-1):x^{(0)}}
and
\tau\xrightarrow{(x^{(0)}_{1}-1,x^{(0)}_{2}-1):x^{(0)}-1}
in
\mathcal{T}
.
We verify the two cases highlighted above.

1
.

We have
q_{l}\xleftrightarrow{\varepsilon:x^{(0)}_{l}-1}q_{l}
for
l=1,2
.
As
\tau[..i_{T}]=\tau^{\prime}[..i_{T}]
, thanks to rule (1) in the definition of
\overline{\mathcal{T}}
, we have
\tau\xleftrightarrow{(x^{(0)}_{1}-1,x^{(0)}_{2}-1):x^{(0)}-1}\tau^{\prime}
, and so we get
r\xleftrightarrow{\varepsilon:x^{(0)}-1}r^{\prime}
, as required.

2
.

We have one of the transitions
q_{1}\xrightarrow{\varepsilon:x^{(0)}_{1}}q_{1}^{\prime}
or
q_{1}^{\prime}\xrightarrow{\varepsilon:x^{(0)}_{1}}q_{1}
; assume we are in the first case. Since
q_{2}\xleftrightarrow{\varepsilon:x^{(0)}_{2}-1}q^{\prime}_{2}
, we also have
\tau\xrightarrow{(x^{(0)}_{1},x^{(0)}_{2}-1):x}\tau^{\prime}
, so we conclude that
r\xrightarrow{\varepsilon:x}r^{\prime}
.∎

This concludes the proof of Theorem
10
.

5
Conclusions and open questions

We characterised objectives in
{\color[rgb]{0.0195,0.2109,0.2539}\mathrm{BC}(\mathbf{\Sigma}^{0}_{2})}
with
memory
(or
chromatic memory
)
\leq k
as those recognised by a well-identified class of automata.
In particular, this gives the first known characterisation of (
chromatic
)
memory
for
\omega
-regular
objectives, and proves that it is decidable (in fact even in
NP
) to compute it.
We also showed that for
\omega
-regular objectives
,
memory
(and also
chromatic memory
) coincides over finite or arbitrary game graphs.
Finally, we settled (a strengthening of) Kopczyński’s conjecture for
{\color[rgb]{0.0195,0.2109,0.2539}\mathrm{BC}(\mathbf{\Sigma}^{0}_{2})}
objectives.
We now discuss some directions for future work.

Exact complexity of computation of memory.

We established that computing the (
chromatic
)
memory
of an
\omega
-regular

objective
is in
NP
.
In fact, computing the
chromatic memory
is
NP
-hard already for simple classes of objectives, such as Muller
[
12
]
or safety ones
[
3
]
.
However, no such hardness results are known for non-chromatic memory.

Question 1
.

Given a deterministic parity automaton
\mathcal{A}
and a number
k
, can we decide whether the
memory
of
L(\mathcal{A})
is
\leq k
in polynomial time?

This question is open already for the simpler case of regular open objectives (that is, those recognised by reachability automata).
A related question is whether one can improve on the triply exponential bound that we establish for the size of games witnessing
memory

>k
(see Proposition
19
).

Assymetric 1-to-2-player lifts.

A celebrated result of Gimbert and Zielonka
[
21
]
states that if for an
objective

W
both players can play optimally using positional strategies in finite games where all vertices belong to one player, then
W
is bipositonal over finite games. This result has been extended in two orthogonal directions: to objectives where both players require finite chromatic memory
[
6
,
5
]
(symmetric lift for memory), and to
\omega
-regular
objectives where Eve can play positionally in
1
-player games
[
10
]
(asymmetric lift for positionality).
In this work, we have not provided an asymmetric lift for memory, as in most cases no such result can hold.
For
{\color[rgb]{0.0195,0.2109,0.2539}\mathrm{BC}(\mathbf{\Sigma}^{0}_{2})}
objectives, it is known to fail already for positional objectives
[
20
, Section 7]
.
For non-chromatic memory, it cannot hold for
\omega
-regular
objectives neither, because of the example described below.

Proposition 42
.

Let
\Sigma_{n}=\{1,\dots,n\}
. For every
n
, the
objective

W_{n}=\{w\in\Sigma_{n}^{\omega}\mid w\text{ contains two different letters infinitely often}\}

has
memory

2
over games where Eve controls all vertices and
memory

n
over arbitrary games.

Proof.

The fact that
W_{n}
has
memory

n
follows from
[
18
]
.
We show that in every game with
objective

W_{n}
and all vertices controlled by Eve, if she can win, she has a winning strategy with
memory

2
.
Let
G
be such a game.
By prefix-independence of
W_{n}
, we can assume that Eve wins no matter what is the initial vertex of
G
.
For each vertex
v\in V(G)
, let
\chi_{1}(v)
be the smallest element in
\Sigma_{n}
such that there is a path starting in
v
that produces a colour
c
, and fix one such finite path
\pi_{v}^{1}=v\xrightarrow{uc}v^{\prime}
of minimal length.
Let
\chi_{2}(v)
be the second smallest such element (which exists, as Eve wins the game), and fix a finite path
\pi_{v}^{2}
of minimal length producing it.
Note that if
v\xrightarrow{u}v^{\prime}
is a path in
G
, then
\chi_{1}(v)\leq\chi_{1}(v^{\prime})
.

We define a
2
-memory strategy for Eve as follows: when in a vertex
v
and
memory state

1
, she will take the first edge from
\pi_{v}^{1}
.
If this edge has colour
\chi_{1}(v)
, we update the
memory state
to
2
, and keep it
1
on the contrary.
When in the
memory state

2
, she will take the first edge from
\pi_{v}^{1}
, and update the
memory state
to
1
if and only if this edge has colour
\chi_{2}(v)
.

We show that this strategy ensures the
objective

W
. Let
v_{0}\xrightarrow{c_{1}}v_{1}\xrightarrow{c_{2}}\dots
be an infinite play consistent with this strategy.
Let
a=\limsup\chi_{1}(v_{i})
.
We claim that we produce both
a
and a colour
>a
infinitely often.
Let
i_{0}
be large enough so that
\chi_{1}(v_{i})>a
for
i\geq i_{0}
. Note that in this case, if
v_{i}\xrightarrow{u}v_{i^{\prime}}
is a path that does not contain colour
\chi_{2}(v_{i})
, then
\chi_{2}(v_{i^{\prime}})=\chi_{2}(v_{i})
.
If in step
i
we are in
memory state

1
,
in exactly
|\pi_{v_{i}}^{1}|
steps we will produce output
\chi_{1}(v_{i})
and change the
memory state
to
2
.
Likewise, by the remark above, if we are in the
memory state

2
, in
|\pi_{v_{i}}^{2}|
steps we will produce
\chi_{2}(v_{i})>a
and change to the
memory state

1
.
This concludes the proof.
∎

In his PhD thesis, Vandenhove conjectures that an asymmetric lift for
chromatic memory
holds for
\omega
-regular
objectives
[
30
, Conjecture 9.1.2]
. This question remains open.

Question 2
.

Is there an
\omega
-regular

objective
with
chromatic memory

k
over games where Eve controls all vertices and
chromatic memory

k^{\prime}>k
over arbitrary games?

Further decidability results for memory.

As mentioned in the introduction, many extensions of
\omega
-automata (including deterministic
\omega
-Turing machines and unambiguous
\omega
-petri nets
[
19
]
) compute languages that are in
{\color[rgb]{0.0195,0.2109,0.2539}\mathrm{BC}(\mathbf{\Sigma}^{0}_{2})}
.
We believe that our characterisation may lead to decidability results regarding the
memory
of objectives represented by these models.

Objectives in
\Delta_{0}^{3}
.

Some of the questions answered in this work in the case of
{\color[rgb]{0.0195,0.2109,0.2539}\mathrm{BC}(\mathbf{\Sigma}^{0}_{2})}
objectives are open in full generality, for instance, the generalised Kopczyński’s conjecture. A reasonable next step would be to consider the class
\mathbf{\Delta}^{0}_{3}=\mathbf{\Sigma}^{0}_{3}\cap\mathbf{\Pi}^{0}_{3}
.
Objectives in
\mathbf{\Delta}^{0}_{3}
are those recognised by max-parity automata using infinitely many priorities
[
28
]
.
Our methods seem appropriate to tackle this class, however, we have been unable to extend the extraction lemma (Lemma
12
) used in the proof of Section
3.1
.

Appendix A
Proof of the structuration theorem

We give a proof of Theorem
4
.

See
4

The idea is to use choice arenas, which were introduced in Ohlmann’s PhD thesis
[
26
, Section 3.2 in Chapter 3]
for positionality, and then adapted to memory in
[
10
]
.

Proof.

Let
H
be the game defined as follows.
The set of vertices is
V(G)\sqcup\mathcal{P}_{\neq\varnothing}(V(G))
, where
\mathcal{P}_{\neq\varnothing}(V(G))
is the set of non-empty subsets
X
of
V(G)
, partitioned into
V_{\text{Adam}}=V(G)
and
V_{\text{Eve}}=\mathcal{P}_{\neq\varnothing}(V(G))
.
The initial vertex is
v_{0}
, the one of
G
.
Then the edges are given by taking those of
G
, and then adding
v\xleftrightarrow{\varepsilon}X
whenever
v\in X
.
The objective is
W
.

In words, when playing in
H
, Adam follows a path of his choice in
G
, except that at any point, he may choose a set
X
containing the current vertex
v
, and allow Eve to continue the game from any vertex of her choice in
X
.
In some sense, Adam can hide the current vertex; this is especially true if Eve is required to play with finite memory.

Since
G

satisfies

W
, Eve wins, simply by going back to the previous vertex
v
every time Adam picks an edge
v\xrightarrow{\varepsilon}X
.
(Formally, the corresponding winning strategy has vertices
V(G)\sqcup\{(v,X)\mid v\in X\}
, projection
\pi(v)=v
and
\pi(v,X)=X
, edges
E(G)\cup\{v\xleftrightarrow{\varepsilon}(v,X)\mid v\in X\}
, and initial vertex
v_{0}
.)
Therefore by our assumption on
W
, there is a (
chromatic
) winning strategy
S
with memory
k
, i.e.
V(S)=V(H)\times k
.

Now we define
G^{\prime}
by
V(G^{\prime})=V(G)\times k
, initial vertex
(v_{0},m_{0})
, the initial vertex of
S
, and with the edges from
E(S)\cap(V(G^{\prime})\times(\Sigma\cup\{\varepsilon\})\times V(G^{\prime}))
, together with edges
(v,m)\xrightarrow{\varepsilon}(v^{\prime},m)
whenever there is
X\ni v,v^{\prime}
such that
(X,m)\xrightarrow{\varepsilon}(v^{\prime},m)\text{ in }S
.

We prove that
G^{\prime}

satisfies
the conclusion of the theorem, except for well-foundedness which is dealt with below.

G^{\prime}
is a
k
-blowup of
G
.

This is because
S
is a strategy, and vertices in
V(G)
belong to Adam in
H
.
Therefore, for each
(v,m)\in V(G^{\prime})
, and each edge
v\xrightarrow{c}v^{\prime}
in
G
,
v\xrightarrow{c}v^{\prime}
is also an edge in
H
, thus there is
m^{\prime}
such that
(v,m)\xrightarrow{c}(v^{\prime},m^{\prime})
is an edge in
G^{\prime}
.

In the chromatic case,
G^{\prime}
is chromatic.

This is because
S
is chromatic, therefore by definition of
G^{\prime}
, it is chromatic with the same chromatic update function.

G^{\prime}
is
k
-wise
\varepsilon
-complete
.

Since it is a graph, we should prove that for each
v,v^{\prime}
and each
m
, either
(v,m)\xrightarrow{\varepsilon}(v^{\prime},m)
or
(v^{\prime},m)\xrightarrow{\varepsilon}(v,m)
in
G
.
This follows from applying the definition of
G^{\prime}
to
X=\{v,v^{\prime}\}
, since either
(X,m)\xrightarrow{\varepsilon}v
or
(X,m)\xrightarrow{\varepsilon}v^{\prime}
is an edge in
S
(because
S
is without dead-ends).

G^{\prime}
satisfies
W
.

This is because
S
is a winning strategy, and every path in
G^{\prime}
corresponds to a path in
S
, by replacing each edge
(v,m)\xrightarrow{\varepsilon}(v^{\prime},m)
by
(v,m)\xrightarrow{\varepsilon}X\xrightarrow{\varepsilon}(v^{\prime},m)
.

There remains a slight technical difficulty, which is that
G^{\prime}
may have
\xrightarrow{\varepsilon}
-cycles (in fact, it even has all
\varepsilon
-self-loops, by applying the definition to
X
being a singleton).
However for each
memory state

m
, the relation
\xrightarrow{\varepsilon}
has the property that for every subset of states
X
, there is
v\in X
(which should be seen as a minimal element) such that every
v^{\prime}\in X

satisfies

(v^{\prime},m)\xrightarrow{\varepsilon}(v,m)
in
G^{\prime}
.

Therefore for each
m
, and each
X\subseteq V(G)
such that
X\times\{m\}
is a strongly connected component for
\xrightarrow{\varepsilon}
in
G^{\prime}
, we pick an arbitrary strict well-order
\xrightarrow{}
over
X
which extends the
\varepsilon
-edges already present in
G
over
X
.
This is possible because
G
is assumed
G
to be
well-founded
(and it is necessary so that the obtained graph remains a
k
-blowup of
G
).
Finally, we rewire
\varepsilon
-edges over
X\times\{m\}
so that they correspond to
\xrightarrow{}
; it is not hard to see that the above points are not broken by this construction.
∎
