On the P P -vertex problem in Bipartite Graphs
G. Arunkumar, Puja Samanta ∗

Abstract
. Property ( P ) (P) , introduced in recent work and rooted in the classical theory of Parter vertices, concerns the existence of a nonsingular matrix A ∈ S ⁡ ( G ) A\in S(G) for which every vertex of G G is a P P -vertex. Previous investigations have fully characterized the property for trees, established it for cycles, extended it to unicyclic graphs, and shown that bipartite graphs with a perfect matching always satisfy property ( P ) (P) . However, whether the converse holds for connected bipartite graphs remains open in general. In this paper, we make progress toward answering this question on multiple fronts. We first prove that every connected bipartite graph satisfying property ( P ) (P) must be balanced, providing a fundamental necessary condition. We further establish complete characterizations for several significant families of bipartite graphs. Specially, we show that every connected bipartite graph of order at most 8 8 has property ( P ) (P) if and only if it has a perfect matching, and more generally, that a connected bipartite graph of order 8 + 2 ​ k 8+2k with at least k k pendant edges satisfies property ( P ) (P) exactly when it has a perfect matching. We further prove that within the class of triangular bipartite graphs, property ( P ) (P) is equivalent to the existence of a perfect matching, providing a full characterization for this broad structural subclass. In addition, we introduce the threaded union over a graph, a general operation for assembling larger graphs from smaller components, and show that threaded union over a tree–cycle block graph preserves property ( P ) (P) . This significantly generalizes earlier result about joining two graphs with property ( P ) (P) by a single edge preserves property ( P ) (P) . Collectively, these results sharpen the structural understanding of property ( P ) (P) , strengthen its connection to matching theory in bipartite graphs, and introduce a versatile construction that generates broad new families of graphs guaranteed to satisfy property ( P ) (P) .

The notion of

Recently, Howlader et al. introduced the terminology

A graph

The behavior of property
(P)
has been studied for several basic graph families. For cycles, it was shown in
[
1
, Theorem 4.2]
that every cycle
C_{n}
has property
(P)
.
Recently, Howlader et al. extended this line of investigation to unicyclic graphs
[
9
, Theorem 6.1]
, thereby advancing the study of
P
-vertex problems beyond trees and cycles to this natural next class of graphs.

Parallel to these developments, property
(P)
has also been investigated in the context of bipartite graphs. A fundamental contribution in this direction is due to Sharma et al.
[
16
]
, who proved that every bipartite graph containing a perfect matching satisfies property
(P)
.

Theorem 1.5
.

[
16
, Theorem 3.1]

If a bipartite graph
G
has a perfect matching, then
G
has property
(P)
.

Moreover, for two important subclasses–trees and unicyclic bipartite graphs–the situation is even more rigid: the existence of a perfect matching is both necessary and sufficient: a tree or unicyclic bipartite graph has property
(P)
if and only if it admits a perfect matching
[
16
, Theorem 3.3, Theorem 3.8]
.

However, the full converse statement:

"If a connected bipartite graph has property
(P)
, then it has a perfect matching."

remained unsolved. The authors verified this implication under additional hypotheses
[
16
, Theorem 3.12]
, leaving the general necessity question open.

In this work we make substantial progress toward resolving this gap. We first show that any bipartite graph with property
(P)
must be balanced, a fundamental structural constraint and a necessary condition for the existence of a perfect matching. Building on this observation, we develop several new criteria that further clarify when property
(P)
forces a perfect matching in the bipartite setting. We completely classify all connected balanced bipartite graphs of order at most eight, proving that such a graph has property
(P)
precisely when it possesses a perfect matching. This characterization extends naturally to larger balanced bipartite graphs with pendant edges: any connected graph on
8+2k
vertices that contains at least
k
pendant edges satisfies property
(P)
if and only if it contains a perfect matching. Additionally, we introduce the class of triangular bipartite graphs, defined by the existence of an ordering of the partite sets for which the biadjacency matrix becomes upper triangular, and we prove that within this class, property
(P)
is again equivalent to the existence of a perfect matching. These results collectively establish broad new families of bipartite graphs for which property
(P)
and perfect matching coincide.

Sharma et al. further showed that certain non-bipartite graphs with a unique perfect matching also satisfy property
(P)
, provided an additional structural requirement is met
[
16
, Theorem 4.1, Theorem 4.3]
.

It is important to emphasize that the correspondence between property
(P)
and the existence of a perfect matching does not hold for arbitrary graphs. Certain non-bipartite graphs–such as odd cycles–possess property
(P)
despite lacking a perfect matching. This observation highlights that the equivalence established for trees and its partial extension to bipartite graphs cannot be generalized without additional structural constraints. Consequently, understanding the precise boundaries of this correspondence becomes a natural and intriguing direction for further investigation.

In the same work
[
16
]
, the authors proved a useful constructive principle: if two graphs
G
and
H
each has property
(P)
, then the graph obtained by joining them with a single edge also has property
(P)
. This provides a basic mechanism for building larger graphs from smaller ones known to satisfy the property.
Motivated by this considerations, the present work is to generalize and systematize such constructive principles. In Section
3
we introduce the
threaded union over graph
, a new operation that extends the "single-edge join" used in
[
16
]
to a significantly broader setting. Instead of attaching two graphs along a single edge, our construction allows an arbitrary graph to serve as a base along which component graphs are attached according to its adjacency relations.

A key observation is that if each component has property
(P)
after deleting a suitable edge set, then the entire threaded union inherits property
(P)
. This leads to unified framework for building large and intricate families of graphs–all guaranteed to have property
(P)
–from simpler building blocks. It also subsumes, as a special case, the edge-joining result of Sharma et al.
To illustrate the breadth of this framework, we apply it to various classical graph families, including lollipop graphs, barbell graphs, and generalized barbell graphs, and we determine conditions under which these graphs have property
(P)
. These examples demonstrate the versatility of the threaded union and its role in extending the landscape of graphs known to satisfy property
(P)
.

2.
Bipartite Graphs

In this section, we study property
(P)
within the class of bipartite graphs.

Building on the foundational results of
[
16
]
, we establish necessary conditions for a bipartite graph to satisfy property
(P)
, beginning with the observation that any such graph must be balanced. This leads to refined understanding of how property
(P)
interacts with matchings and parity structures in bipartite graphs, and positions our results as a partial converse to Theorem
1.5
.

We denote by
G(X,Y;E)
a bipartite graph
G
with bipartition
(X,Y)
. Further, if
|X|=|Y|
, then
G
is said to be
balanced
bipartite graph.

For a bipartite graph
G:=G(X,Y;E)
and a subset
S\subseteq X
, the
neighborhood
of
S
is defined as

N(S):=\{y\in Y:\exists\ x\in S\text{ with }(x,y)\in E\}.

The following classical theorem characterizes the existence of a perfect matching in bipartite graphs.

Theorem 2.1

(Hall’s Theorem)
.

[
14
, Page no. 13]

A bipartite graph
G:=G(X,Y;E)
, with
|X|=|Y|
, has a perfect matching if and only if

|N(S)|\geq|S|,

for all
S\subseteq X
.

Definition 2.2
.

For a matrix
M=(m_{ij})\in\mathbb{R}^{m\times n}
, the
support
of
M
is

\operatorname{supp}(M):=\{(i,j)\in\{1,\dots,m\}\times\{1,\dots,n\}:m_{ij}\neq 0\}.

Two matrices
A,B\in\mathbb{R}^{m\times n}
are said to have the same
zero-nonzero pattern
if

\operatorname{supp}(A)=\operatorname{supp}(B).

Given a bipartite graph
G=G(U,V;E)
with
|U|=m
and
|V|=n
, we say that a matrix
M\in\mathbb{R}^{m\times n}
has the
zero-nonzero pattern of
G
when

\operatorname{supp}(M)=\{(i,j):(u_{i},v_{j})\in E\}.

Theorem 2.3
.

Let
G
be a bipartite graph. If
G
has property
(P)
, then
G
must be a balanced bipartite graph.

Proof.

Let the bipartition of
G
be
U=\{u_{1},u_{2},\dots,u_{m}\}
and
V=\{v_{1},v_{2},\dots,v_{n}\}
.

Since
G
has property
(P)
, there exists a nonsingular matrix
A=(A_{ij})\in\mathbb{R}^{(m+n)\times(m+n)}
with
A\in S(G)
such that

\operatorname{det}(A(i))=0\text{ for all }i\in\{1,2,\dots,m+n\}.

Throughout the discussion we index the rows and columns of
A
so that the first
m
rows and columns correspond to vertices of
U
, and the last
n
rows and columns correspond to vertices of
V
.

With this convention, any matrix
A\in S(G)
naturally takes the block form

A=\begin{bmatrix}X&D\\
D^{\top}&Y\end{bmatrix},

where
X\in\mathbb{R}^{m\times m}
and
Y\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times n}
are diagonal matrices, and
D\in\mathbb{R}^{m\times n}
has the zero-nonzero pattern of
G
.

Let
A^{-1}
be partitioned compatibly as

A^{-1}:=\begin{bmatrix}P&Q\\
Q^{\top}&R\end{bmatrix},

where
P\in\mathbb{R}^{m\times m}
and
R\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times n}
.

Since
\operatorname{det}(A(i))=0
for all
i
, so,
(A^{-1})_{ii}=0
for all
i
. Hence,
P_{ii}=0
for all
i\in\{1,2,\dots,m\}
and
R_{jj}=0
for all
j\in\{1,2,\dots,n\}
.

From
AA^{-1}=I_{m+n}
, we obtain the block equations:

(2.1)

XP+DQ^{\top}=I_{m},

(2.2)

D^{\top}Q+YR=I_{n},

(2.3)

XQ+DR=\textbf{0},

(2.4)

D^{\top}P+YQ^{\top}=\textbf{0}.

Taking the trace of (
2.1
) and using
(XP)_{ii}=0
(since
X
is a diagonal matrix and
P
has zero diagonal entries) for all
i\in\{1,2,\dots,m\}
,

(2.5)

\operatorname{Tr}(XP+DQ^{\top})=\operatorname{Tr}(DQ^{\top})=m.

Taking the trace of (
2.2
) and using
(YR)_{jj}=0
(since
Y
is a diagonal matrix and
R
has zero diagonal entries) for all
j\in\{1,2,\dots,n\}

(2.6)

\operatorname{Tr}(D^{\top}Q+YR)=\operatorname{Tr}(D^{\top}Q)=n.

Now, from (
2.5
) and (
2.6
),

m=\operatorname{Tr}(DQ^{\top})=\operatorname{Tr}(QD^{\top})=\operatorname{Tr}(D^{\top}Q)=n.

Hence, we obtain
m=n
, which implies that

|U|=|V|.

Therefore,
G
is a balanced bipartite graph.
∎

Remark 2.4
.

An immediate consequence of the Theorem
2.3
is that any unbalanced bipartite
graph fails to satisfy property
(P)
. Thus, balance is a necessary structural requirement for bipartite graphs to satisfy property
(P)
.

We record a related observation from existing results. Since every
k
-regular bipartite graph admits a
perfect matching, and bipartite graphs with a perfect matching are known to satisfy
property
(P)
(Theorem

), it follows that all
k

(P)
.

This fact is independent of Theorem

, but we include it here for completeness.

Lemma 2.5
.

[
14
, Exercises 1.4.2(i)]

For any
k>0
, every
k
-regular bipartite graph has a perfect matching.

Proof.

Let
G:=G(A,B;E)
be a
k
-regular bipartite graph, so, every vertex in
G
has degree
k
. Counting the number of edges by degrees on each side gives

k|A|=\sum_{a\in A}\deg(a)=|E|=\sum_{b\in B}\deg(b)=k|B|,

hence,
|A|=|B|
.

Now, for any subset
S\subseteq A
, its neighborhood
N(S)=\{b\in B:\exists\ a\in S\text{ with }(a,b)\in E\}
.

Double-counting the edges between
S
and
N(S)
gives

k|S|=\sum_{a\in S}\deg_{N(S)}(a)\leq\sum_{b\in N(S)}\deg(b)=k|N(S)|,

hence,
|S|\leq|N(S)|
for every such subset.

Therefore, by Hall’s Theorem,
G
has a perfect matching.
∎

Every

Since every

Theorem 2.7
.

[

Let

.

For a graph

The next result recalls an obstruction to property

arising from vertices incident with multiple pendant edges. In
[

, Definition 4.2]
, an

is defined as a graph

containing a vertex

Theorem 2.8
.

[
9

Any antenna graph does not have property
(P)
.

We now combine this obstruction with Hall’s theorem to completely characterize the property
(P)
for all connected bipartite graphs of order at most

.

Theorem 2.9
.

Let
G
be a connected bipartite graph of order at most
8
. Then
G
has property
(P)
if and only if
G
has a perfect matching.

Proof.

Let
G=G(A,B;E)
be a connected bipartite graph. If
G
has a perfect matching, then by Theorem
1.5
it follows that
G
has property
(P)
.

For the converse, assume
G
has property
(P)
, hence by Theorem
2.3

G
is a balanced bipartite graph. Suppose that
G
does not have a perfect matching. By Hall’s theorem, there exists a subset
S\subseteq A
such that

|N(S)|<|S|.

Since,
G
is balanced and of order at most
8
, we have
|A|=|B|\leq 4
. As,
G
is connected,

1<|S|\leq 3.

Case 1:

|S|=2

Then
|N(S)|=1
. Thus the unique vertex in
N(S)
is adjacent to two pendant vertices in
S
, so
G
contains a vertex with two pendant vertices adjacent to it. By Theorem
2.8
,
G
does not have property
(P)
.

Case 2:

|S|=3

Then
|A|=|B|=4
, and hence,
|N(S)|\leq 2
.

•

If
|N(S)|=1
, the unique vertex in
N(S)
is adjacent to three pendant vertices in
S
. By Theorem
2.8
,
G
does not have property
(P)
.

•

If
|N(S)|=2
. Let

A-S=\{v\},\ \ \ \ B-N(S)=\{w_{1},w_{2}\}.

Since,
w_{1}
and
w_{2}
have no neighbors in
S
, connectivity forces

to be adjacent to both of them. Moreover, to maintain connectivity of

, the vertex

must also be adjacent to at least one vertex in

. Thus

has at least two pendant neighbors, again, by Theorem

,

does not have property
(P)
.

In all cases, the failure of Hall’s condition implies the presence of a vertex with more than one pendant vertices adjacent to it, which in turn guarantees that
G
does not have property
(P)
.

Therefore, a connected balanced bipartite graph
G
of order at most
8
has property
(P)
if and only if it has a perfect matching.
∎

.

Let
G
be a connected bipartite graph of order

has property

if and only if

has a perfect matching.

Proof.

The forward direction is immediate from Theorem

We prove the converse:

Assume
G
has property
(P)
, then by Theorem

is a connected balanced bipartite graph. We will show that

The proof is by induction on

For

, the order of

is

. By Theorem

, any connected bipartite graph of order at most

has property

Assume the statement holds for

is a connected bipartite graph of order

and

has at least

has property
(P)
if and only if
G
has a perfect matching.

Now, Let
G
be a connected balanced bipartite graph of order

with property
(P)
and containing

Case 1:
Some vertex of

is incident with two or more pendant edges.

Then

is an antenna graph. By Theorem

, contradiction.

Thus, if

(P)
, every pendant edge must adjacent to distinct vertex.

Definition 2.14

Definition 2.15

Given that

(p,q)

In the following result, we show that every complete graph has property

Theorem 3.19
.

The complete graph

has the property
(P)
, for all

Proof.

We consider a specific family of matrices for

Let
A_{n}[\alpha]
be the

matrix with diagonal entries equal to
\alpha
and all off-diagonal entries equal to 1.

A_{n}[\alpha]=\begin{bmatrix}\alpha&1&\dots&1\\
1&\alpha&\dots&1\\
\vdots&\vdots&\ddots&\vdots\\
1&1&\dots&\alpha\end{bmatrix}

This matrix can be expressed as

where

is the

is the
n\times n

To prove this theorem, we will find a value

such that for the matrix

-vertex condition is met for all vertices. We will construct a nonsingular matrix

such that
\operatorname{det}(A_{n}(i))=0
for all

.

The proof is based on a recursive hypothesis: the appropriate diagonal value
\alpha_{n}
for the matrix

is a root of the determinant of the matrix
A_{n-1}[x]
. That is,
\operatorname{det}(A_{n-1}[\alpha_{n}])=0
.

Now, the determinant of the matrix
A_{k}[\alpha]
,
\operatorname{det}(A_{k}[\alpha])=(\alpha+k-1)(\alpha-1)^{k-1}
.
We seek a value
\alpha_{n}
such that
\operatorname{det}(A_{n-1}[\alpha_{n}])=0
. We have:

(\alpha_{n}+(n-1)-1)(\alpha_{n}-1)^{(n-1)-1}=(\alpha_{n}+n-2)(\alpha_{n}-1)^{n-2}=0

This yields two potential solutions for
\alpha_{n}

\alpha_{n}=1
or
\alpha_{n}=2-n
. The choice

results in

, which does not satisfy the

-vertex condition. We choose
\alpha_{n}=2-n

Let’s define our matrix for

as

. We must verify two conditions.

(1)

The eigenvalues of
A_{n}[2-n]
are obtained by substituting
\alpha=2-n
and
k=n
:

•

\lambda_{1}=(2-n)+n-1=1

•

\lambda_{2}=(2-n)-1=1-n

For

is nonsingular.

(2)

A principal submatrix
A_{n}[2-n](i)
is formed by deleting row and column
i
from the matrix
A_{n}[2-n]
.
A_{n}[2-n](i)=A_{n-1}[2-n]
. We need to find the nullity of this submatrix.

Let’s compute the determinant of

.

\displaystyle\operatorname{det}(A_{n-1}[2-n])

\displaystyle=((2-n)+(n-1)-1)((2-n)-1)^{(n-1)-1}

\displaystyle=(2-n+n-2)(1-n)^{n-2}

\displaystyle=(0)(1-n)^{n-2}=0

Since the determinant is zero, the matrix is singular. Therefore,
\operatorname{det}(A_{n}[2-n](i))=0

We have shown that for the matrix

\operatorname{det}(A_{n})\neq 0

The constructed matrix has diagonal entries equal to

3.7.
Generalized threaded union over a graph

In the definition of the threaded union (Definition
3
),
each component
G_{i}
is attached to the rest of the construction through a

single
designated vertex, called the bail vertex.

This restriction ensures a one-to-one correspondence between the vertices of the
thread graph
H
and the attachment points in the resulting graph
T_{H}[\mathcal{G}]
.

However, in many natural constructions, a subgraph may connect to its neighbors
through
multiple
distinct vertices. To capture such configurations while maintaining a controlled attachment pattern, we extend the notion of threaded union as follows.

Definition 3.25

(Generalized threaded union over a graph)
.

Let
H
be a simple graph with vertex set
V(H)=\{v_{1},v_{2},\dots,v_{n}\}
.
For each vertex
v_{i}\in V(H)
, let
\deg_{H}(v_{i})=t_{i}
.

Let

\mathcal{G}=\{(G_{i},B_{i}):1\leq i\leq n\}

be a collection of connected graphs
G_{i}
, where each
G_{i}
has vertex set

V(G_{i})=\{v^{(i)}_{1},v^{(i)}_{2},\dots,v^{(i)}_{n_{i}}\},

and is equipped with a distinguished subset

B_{i}=\{b^{(i)}_{1},b^{(i)}_{2},\dots,b^{(i)}_{r_{i}}\}\subseteq V(G_{i}),

called the
set of bail vertices
of
G_{i}
, satisfying
1\leq r_{i}\leq t_{i}
.

These bail vertices serve as the designated attachment points through which
G_{i}

connects to other components along
H
.

The
generalized threaded union
of the family
\mathcal{G}
over
H
,
denoted by
T^{\ast}_{H}[\mathcal{G}]
, is the graph defined by

V(T^{\ast}_{H}[\mathcal{G}])=\bigcup_{i=1}^{n}V(G_{i}),

and

E(T^{\ast}_{H}[\mathcal{G}])=\left(\bigcup_{i=1}^{n}E(G_{i})\right)\cup\Big\{(b^{(i)}_{p_{ij}},\,b^{(j)}_{p_{ji}}):(v_{i},v_{j})\in E(H)\Big\},

where for each edge
(v_{i},v_{j})\in E(H)
, exactly one pair of bail vertices

b^{(i)}_{p_{ij}}\in B_{i}
and
b^{(j)}_{p_{ji}}\in B_{j}
are chosen and connected by an edge.

Thus, we begin with the disjoint union of all
G_{i}
, and for each edge
(v_{i},v_{j})
of
H
,
we add precisely one edge between selected bail vertices of the corresponding components.
The condition
r_{i}\leq\deg_{H}(v_{i})
ensures that each
G_{i}
has enough bail vertices
to accommodate all of its incident connections in
H
.

The resulting graph
T^{\ast}_{H}[\mathcal{G}]
is called the
generalized threaded union

of
\mathcal{G}
over
H
.

When each component
G_{i}
has exactly one bail vertex (that is,
r_{i}=1
for all
i
), the above construction reduces to the ordinary threaded union
T_{H}[\mathcal{G}]
. Thus, the generalized threaded union provides a natural extension that accommodates graphs where components attach to their neighbors through multiple distinct vertices.

Remark 3.30

Theorem
