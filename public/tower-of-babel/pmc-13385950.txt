Full scale optimization of an anaerobic/aerobic/anoxic process for nitrogen removal from low-strength municipal wastewater
Authors: Zeming An, Xinjie Gao, Jing Ding, Xiaoxin Cao, Liang Zhang, Yongzhen Peng
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Achieving advanced nitrogen removal in municipal wastewater treatment plants remains challenging under low-strength influent and seasonal temperature fluctuations. Here, we optimized a full-scale anaerobic/aerobic/anoxic process (40,000 m3/d) and evaluated its year-round performance for influent with biochemical oxygen demand of 49.1 ± 9.9 mg/L and total nitrogen of 18.2 ± 1.4 mg/L. By reducing the air-water ratio to 0.39 and shortening the anaerobic hydraulic retention time to 1.8 h, the system achieves effluent total nitrogen as low as 3.3 mg/L and maintains 4.1 mg/L even as the temperature declines from 24.6 °C to 11.3 °C. The combined measures preserve intracellular carbon in the anaerobic zone and limit aerobic over-oxidation, enabling sustained endogenous denitrification in the downstream anoxic zone and clarifier. Endogenous denitrifiers and hydrolytic bacteria are selectively enriched, and functional genes associated with intracellular carbon metabolism and denitrification show higher abundance. Compared to the parallel anaerobic/anoxic/oxic process treating the same influent, chemical use decreases by 40%, sludge production by 8%, and aeration energy by 73%. This work demonstrates that full-scale implementation of this process provides an energy-efficient solution for low-strength municipal wastewater treatment.

Introduction

Biological nitrogen removal in municipal wastewater treatment plants (MWWTPs) is increasingly constrained by the prevalence of low-strength influent conditions (typically characterized by COD < 150 mg/L and carbon-nitrogen ratios < 3) and seasonal temperature variability1,2. In many urban drainage systems, combined sewer configurations and intensified precipitation events continuously dilute municipal wastewater, leading to reduced concentrations of biodegradable organics and nutrients such as nitrogen and phosphorus. Such influents typically exhibit both low organic loading and low C/N ratios, which are often insufficient to meet the metabolic and electron donor requirements of activated sludge microorganisms3,4. Simultaneously, seasonal temperature drops exacerbate these metabolic limitations by impeding the mobilization kinetics of intracellular carbon reserves. At lower temperatures, the enzymatic hydrolysis and utilization rates of stored carbon polymers, such as polyhydroxyalkanoates (PHA) and glycogen (Gly), are significantly suppressed, thereby limiting the electron supply for endogenous denitrification5,6. Together, these factors represent significant operational constraints for achieving stable and efficient nitrogen removal in modern wastewater treatment practice7.

The complexity of this issue is increased by the tightening of global nitrogen discharge standards. Excessive nitrogen discharge contributes to eutrophication and algal blooms in receiving waters worldwide8,9. In response, regulatory agencies are implementing stricter discharge limits, with effluent total nitrogen (TN) concentrations mandated below 10 mg/L in some regions and as low as 3–6 mg/L in sensitive catchments10,11. However, as influent concentrations decrease, the marginal cost of pollutant removal rises, as achieving advanced nitrogen removal requires increased energy and chemical inputs12,13. Aeration accounts for 50%–70% of total electricity demand in conventional activated sludge systems, and the air-water ratio is a critical determinant of treatment efficiency and energy intensity14. Furthermore, external carbon dosing constitutes the largest chemical input for biological nitrogen removal, typically representing 50%–70% of total reagent costs, while directly determining denitrification rates and contributing substantially to the carbon footprint of MWWTPs15. As a result, conventional biological processes that depend on both high aeration intensity and supplemental carbon sources are increasingly unsustainable for low-strength municipal wastewater treatment16.

In this context, advanced nitrogen removal process innovations that promote energy efficiency, reduce resource consumption, and a low carbon footprint are essential. Recent studies have highlighted the anaerobic/aerobic/anoxic (AOA) process as a promising alternative for carbon-limited conditions17. The core of this process is to fully capture influent organics and store them as intracellular carbon in the anaerobic zone. However, an overly prolonged anaerobic hydraulic retention time (HRT) can inadvertently trigger the secondary endogenous consumption of newly synthesized intracellular carbon to meet basal microbial maintenance energy needs18. Concurrently, a substantial risk of carbon loss emerges in the intervening aerobic zone, where heterotrophic bacteria preferentially utilize dissolved oxygen (DO) as the terminal electron acceptor to oxidize these finite intracellular reserves19. Thus, unnecessary consumption of carbon sources must be curtailed during both the anaerobic and aerobic phases. Subsequently, in the anoxic zone, the stored intracellular carbon is mobilized to drive endogenous denitrification20. Yet, this step is fundamentally constrained by inherent kinetic limitations. Because endogenous denitrification is driven by the degradation of intracellular polymers, it proceeds at an inevitably slower rate than conventional exogenous denitrification, thereby dictating the necessity for a prolonged anoxic HRT17. While lab-scale and pilot-scale studies have reported stable TN removal performance, along with notable sludge reduction and energy efficiency, these kinetic and metabolic challenges are severely exacerbated at full scale21,22. It remains unresolved whether the AOA process can consistently maintain robust performance under low-strength influent and seasonal temperature fluctuations through HRT redistribution and intracellular carbon management. Furthermore, the underlying ecological mechanisms concerning how these macroscopic operational controls shape microbial community dynamics to sustain such intracellular metabolism remain poorly understood.

This work proposes a full-scale optimization strategy focusing on the regulation of internal carbon pools to achieve process stability and energy efficiency. We evaluate this strategy in a 40,000 m3/day AOA configuration at an MWWTP in southern China. The system operates under year-round low-strength influent and seasonal temperature fluctuations without external carbon addition. During the full annual cycle investigation, we systematically adjust macroscopic controls including the air-water ratio and HRT to govern intracellular carbon dynamics. We evaluate the feasibility and reliability of this internal carbon-driven process by comprehensively assessing its nitrogen removal performance, energy efficiency, and sludge reduction. Furthermore, we investigate microbial community succession and metagenomic functional profiles to elucidate the underlying ecological responses to these operational adjustments. Finally, we discuss the fundamental mechanisms driving the efficiency and stability of the optimized AOA process, offering insights for its future application as a high-efficiency and low-carbon alternative.

Results

Overall nitrogen performance of the full-scale AOA process

To achieve advanced nitrogen removal from low-strength municipal wastewater, a full-scale AOA system was optimized and monitored (Fig. 1). Throughout 2024, the full-scale AOA system was operated continuously under varying seasonal conditions (n = 366 daily samples). As shown in Fig. 2a, ambient air temperature ranged from −2.5 °C to 29.5 °C, exhibiting a typical subtropical seasonal pattern with a gradual rise, peak in midsummer, and decline toward winter. To aid in interpreting climatic impacts, monthly average precipitation and ambient temperature data are summarized in Supplementary Fig. 1. In contrast, influent temperature showed a more moderate fluctuation, rising from 12.8 °C to 24.6 °C and then decreasing to 11.3 °C, largely buffered by the sewer network and plant hydraulics. During the initial commissioning period (Day 24–64), partial monitoring data were not available due to ongoing infrastructure upgrades, including influent diversion and clarifier retrofitting. Influent total nitrogen (TN) remained relatively stable over the year, averaging 18.2 ± 1.4 mg-N/L. The influent chemical oxygen demand (TCOD) exhibited significant fluctuations, with an average concentration of 102.4 mg/L. Biological oxygen demand (BOD5) concentrations remained relatively stable at 49.1 ± 9.9 mg/L. Consequently, the average influent BOD5/TN ratio was only 2.7, which is classified as low influent strength, a well-documented challenge for achieving stable biological nitrogen removal23. It should be noted that the relatively low variance in influent concentrations is attributed to the plant’s pump-controlled influent regulation and the buffering capacity of the sewer network, which dampens the acute impacts of rainfall dilution. The COD removal efficiency (CRE) remained stable at 90.4 ± 3.6% throughout the year. The average effluent TN across four defined operational phases was 9.2, 4.6, 3.3, and 4.1 mg-N/L (Fig. 2b), corresponding to nitrogen removal efficiencies (NRE) of 49.5%, 74.7%, 81.1%, and 76.6%, respectively. The highest performance occurred in Phase Ⅲ, coinciding with peak water temperatures and optimized HRT allocation. Remarkably, even in Phase Ⅳ, when the system faced the dual stress of influent temperature declining from 24.6 °C to 11.3 °C and hydraulic load increased from 3.2 × 104 to 4.0 × 104 m3/day, the system maintained an NRE of 76.6%, substantially higher than the pre-optimization baseline of 49.5%. This stability under combined low-temperature and high-flow conditions demonstrates the robustness of the optimized AOA process. In terms of NH4+ removal, influent NH4+ concentrations remained stable at 11.5 ± 1.9 mg-N/L throughout the year. Effluent NH4+ concentrations across the four phases were 2.5, 0.6, 0.7, and 1.4 mg-N/L, respectively. The NH4+ removal efficiency (ARE) approached 94.4% in Phases Ⅱ and Ⅲ, and remained above 87.5% in Phase Ⅳ, despite seasonal cooling and increasing influent loading (Fig. 2c). Spatial distributions of nutrient removal and intracellular carbon utilization

To elucidate the spatial differentiation of nitrogen removal performance across functional zones of the full-scale AOA system, pollutant profiles were analyzed along the anaerobic/aerobic/anoxic/clarifier path during Phase I and Phase Ⅳ operations (Fig. 3a, b). The influent entering the biological tank was characterized by low organic strength, with soluble chemical oxygen demand (sCOD) around 30 mg/L and TN approximately 15 mg/L, yielding a sCOD/TN ratio of 2.0, which reflects a typical low-strength municipal wastewater condition. In Phase I, substantial residual nitrate carried by external recirculation persisted in the anaerobic zone, which likely inhibited intracellular carbon storage by diverting electrons toward premature denitrification. This competition with PHA synthesis constrained endogenous denitrification in the downstream anoxic zone, where the endogenous denitrification rate (EDNR) averaged only 0.04 mg-N/g VSS/h, and final effluent TN remained at 8.4 ± 1.4 mg-N/L. Following the optimization in Phase IV, the extended hydrolysis zone played a pivotal role in regulating the carbon-nitrogen balance. Since this zone exclusively receives return activated sludge (RAS), the extended retention time facilitated the hydrolysis of decayed biomass and extracellular polymeric substances (EPS), thereby releasing biodegradable endogenous carbon. This internally generated carbon was effectively utilized to denitrify the residual nitrate carried in the RAS before it mixed with the influent. Consequently, nitrate was fully depleted prior to entering the anaerobic zone. This effective pre-denitrification alleviated electron competition and raised the functional C/N ratio, enabling more effective intracellular carbon accumulation and subsequent utilization. Consequently, EDNR in the anoxic zone increased to 0.11 mg-N/g VSS/h, and effluent TN was reduced to 3.4 ± 0.2 mg-N/L. Across both phases, the anoxic zone consistently contributed the largest share of nitrogen removal (38.6% in Phase I and 42.1% in Phase Ⅳ) (Fig. 3c). Notably, the secondary clarifier also played a non-negligible role, with its denitrification contribution amounting to 24.5% in Phase I and 12.8% in Phase Ⅳ. Acting as an extended anoxic environment, the clarifier further reduced residual nitrate from 1.9 to 1.2 mg-N/L in Phase Ⅳ, enhancing overall system performance. This significant denitrification contribution was supported by the extended clarifier HRT (5 h), which facilitated intensified endogenous respiration. To mitigate the risk of sludge flotation induced by N2 production within the sludge blanket, short-duration low-intensity aeration (HRT = 0.5 h, DO < 0.5 mg/L) was applied at the end of the anoxic zone as a degassing measure to strip residual gas bubbles and improve sludge settleability. To further elucidate intracellular carbon metabolism, the spatial distribution of intracellular Gly and PHA was examined (Fig. 3d). In the anaerobic zone, 3.91 mmol C/L of Gly was hydrolyzed to provide energy and reducing power for PHA synthesis and polyphosphate release. In the aerobic zone, 0.07 mmol C/L of PHA was consumed to support microbial growth, while Gly was replenished as an intracellular energy reserve. In the anoxic zone, both PHA and Gly exhibited decreasing trends, indicating their utilization as electron donors for denitrification22. The combined reduction of these storage polymers corresponded to 45.54 mg COD/L, which is theoretically sufficient to reduce approximately 15.9 mg-N/L of nitrate25. However, this theoretical capacity exceeded the observed nitrate reduction, suggesting that a fraction of the intracellular carbon was also allocated to cellular maintenance, endogenous respiration, and partial biomass synthesis during the anoxic phase. Such electron partitioning is consistent with endogenous metabolism under carbon-limited conditions, where stored polymers simultaneously sustain denitrification and microbial survival. Notably, PHA and Gly levels in RAS increased to 0.24 and 24.42 mmol C/L, indicating that microbial decay and lysis enriched these intracellular carbon pools and contributed additional electron donors for denitrification. This observation corroborates the role of the clarifier as a functional extension of the anoxic zone. In contrast, in Lines 2 and 3, which received the same influent but were operated at higher aeration intensities, PHA concentrations remained below 0.05 mmol C/L, and Gly levels were also markedly lower than in Line 1 (Supplementary Fig. 2), leaving insufficient intracellular carbon to sustain effective denitrification in the downstream anoxic zones.

In addition, to validate the contribution of the extended hydrolysis zone, the profiles of 19 intracellular free amino acids (FAAs) were analyzed (Fig. 3e). In Phase I, owing to the limited retention time in the hydrolysis zone, intracellular FAA concentrations were negligible (<1.0 mg COD/L) across all functional zones, indicating insufficient endogenous protein hydrolysis (Supplementary Fig. 3). In contrast, following the process optimization in Phase IV, a substantial accumulation of intracellular FAAs was observed. Among them, glutamic acid (GLU) exhibited the most pronounced spatial gradient, with concentrations rising from 0.18 mg COD/L in the hydrolysis zone to a peak of 4.18 mg COD/L in the anaerobic zone, then decreasing slightly to 3.29 mg COD/L in the aerobic zone and sharply declining to 0.25 mg COD/L in the anoxic zone. Similar trends were observed for nine branched-chain amino acids such as isoleucine (ILE) and leucine (LEU), suggesting their metabolic transformation and consumption during endogenous denitrification. Notably, intracellular FAA concentrations peaked in the anaerobic zone rather than in the upstream sludge hydrolysis zone. This spatial distribution reflects a metabolic transition of the biomass from an endogenous “famine” state to an exogenous “feast” state26. In the hydrolysis zone, biomass is exposed only to RAS without external carbon input, and microbial activity is primarily directed toward the slow hydrolysis of intracellular proteins and extracellular polymeric substances released from decaying biomass. The resulting amino acids are largely utilized for cellular maintenance under endogenous respiration, preventing significant intracellular accumulation. Upon entering the anaerobic zone, the introduction of influent organic matter triggers a robust anabolic response, where microorganisms rapidly synthesize and pool intracellular FAAs as a strategic reserve for the subsequent extended anoxic phase. The primary advantage of the preceding hydrolysis zone, therefore, lies in its role as a metabolic buffer that depletes residual nitrate, thereby optimizing the metabolic environment for the rapid synthesis of these intracellular reserves in the anaerobic zone. These findings highlight the important role of intracellular FAAs as supplementary electron donors under low-carbon conditions, in agreement with recent evidence that FAAs can sustain denitrification under carbon-limited conditions27,28.

Optimization of air-water ratio and flow regulation for enhanced system performance

The air-water ratio, defined as the volumetric ratio of aeration air to influent flow, serves as a critical operational lever for balancing nitrification performance, denitrification potential, and energy consumption in biological nutrient removal systems29. Excessive aeration ensures NH4+ oxidation but suppresses denitrification by accelerating the non-productive consumption of intracellular carbon and elevating DO carryover to downstream anoxic zones. Conversely, insufficient aeration risks incomplete nitrification and elevated effluent NH4+. Therefore, identifying an optimal aeration “sweet spot” is essential for AOA systems treating low-strength wastewater. To this end, a dynamic aeration control strategy was implemented across four phases (Fig. 4a–d). During Phase I, the absence of active aeration control resulted in a highly variable air-water ratio (0.98 ± 0.27) and substantial DO fluctuations (1.91 ± 1.81 mg/L) (Supplementary Fig. 4a–c), which led to inconsistent nitrification and elevated effluent NH4+ levels. In response, Phase Ⅱ involved a substantial increase in aeration intensity, raising the air-water ratio to nearly 2.0, while simultaneously reducing influent flow (32,000 m3/day) to extend HRT. This combination stabilized nitrification but risked excessive oxidation of intracellular carbon pools. Subsequently, a controlled decremental aeration strategy was applied to gradually reduce air input while maintaining ARE. After Day 188, the air-water ratio stabilized at a lower level of 0.56 ± 0.13, with effluent NH4+-N concentrations approaching zero, demonstrating that sufficient nitrification could be maintained at reduced aeration rates. In Phase Ⅳ, influent flow was restored to the design capacity (40,000 m3/day), while the air-water ratio was further stabilized at 0.39 ± 0.15, representing a 60% reduction compared with Phase I. DO also decreased to 0.33 ± 0.29 mg/L. The impact of this combined regulation was substantial: compared with Phase I, effluent NH4+-N and TN concentrations decreased by 20% and over 50%, respectively (Supplementary Fig. 4d–f). These outcomes align with recent findings that precision aeration control can simultaneously optimize nitrogen removal while reducing energy consumption by 15%–40%30–32. Ecological response of the microbial community to operational optimization

To further validate the ecological response of the system to the implemented operational adjustments, activated sludge samples were collected on Days 66, 164, 219, and 298, corresponding to the four defined operational phases. High-throughput 16S rRNA gene sequencing revealed that the microbial community structure was dynamically shaped by the varying selective pressures of each phase (Fig. 5a). Hierarchical clustering indicated that Phase Ⅱ had the most distinct community structure, primarily driven by the high air-water ratio and reduced influent loading, which favored oxygen-tolerant taxa while suppressing denitrifiers. At the phylum level, Proteobacteria and Bacteroidota, which are central to organic degradation and nitrogen transformation, recovered significantly in Phase Ⅳ after the strategic reduction of aeration, confirming that the optimized environment favored these key functional taxa33. Chloroflexi, often linked to polysaccharide degradation and high-load tolerance, remained consistently abundant, supporting process stability34. Actinobacteriota, involved in hydrolysis and acidogenesis, produce extracellular enzymes that cleave macromolecules, thereby facilitating downstream denitrification processes35,36. At the genus level, detailed clustering analysis of key functional groups was conducted (Fig. 5b). Under the high air-water ratios of Phase Ⅱ, Nitrospira (4.82%) was enriched, whereas the lower oxygen affinity of AOB led to a decrease in Nitrosomonas. However, in Phase Ⅳ, although Nitrosomonas (0.13%) was affected by seasonal cooling, Ellin6067 (1.76%) increased, emerging as the primary nitrifier adapted to the optimized low-DO environment. The transition toward a maintenance-dominated regime in the anoxic zones successfully enriched endogenous denitrifiers, including Candidatus Competibacter (0.18%), Dechloromonas (0.32%), Comamonadaceae (2.20%) and Denitratisoma (2.87%). Their abundance trends corroborate the metabolic shift induced by long-term carbon limitation37–39. Furthermore, the dedicated hydrolysis tank consistently supported hydrolytic groups such as Microtrichales (3.15%), Saprospiraceae (4.46%) and Saccharimonadales (2.82%). Their presence likely enhanced the conversion of particulate and complex organics into available intermediates, supporting endogenous denitrification under carbon-limited conditions40–42. Notably, the emergence of Candidatus Brocadia (0.02%) in Phase Ⅳ suggests that the optimized low-DO and residual NH4+ conditions created a niche for anaerobic ammonium oxidation (anammox), providing additional potential for energy-efficient nitrogen removal43,44.

Metagenomic insights into nitrogen metabolism and carbon regulation

To further elucidate the functional mechanisms underpinning the observed nitrogen removal efficiency and carbon utilization capacity, metagenomic sequencing was performed on sludge samples from the four phases. Functional gene abundance was annotated and quantified based on TPM, with a focus on genes involved in nitrogen metabolism and intracellular carbon pathways, including Gly, PHA, and Glu. As shown in Fig. 5c, nitrogen metabolism genes exhibited temporal dynamics that aligned with the macroscopic operational changes. During Phase Ⅱ, characterized by high air-water ratios and elevated DO, the abundance of amoCAB genes (encoding ammonia monooxygenase) peaked at 43.4 TPM, indicating strong activation of aerobic ammonia oxidation. However, the subsequent enrichment of denitrification genes (nirS, norB and nosZ) in Phase Ⅲ reflects the improved endogenous carbon availability and reduced oxidative stress achieved through aeration optimization. Even during Phase Ⅳ, when the temperature declined to 11.3 °C, their abundance remained relatively high, underscoring the functional resilience of denitrifiers under seasonal cooling. Genes associated with anammox (e.g., hzs, hdh) were consistently detected but at very low abundance, suggesting that anammox had not yet established ecological significance under the studied conditions.

Intracellular carbon metabolism genes also revealed adaptive responses (Fig. 5d). Genes involved in the synthesis and mobilization of multiple intracellular pools—specifically Gly turnover (e.g., glgA, glgB, glgP), PHA synthesis (e.g., phaA, phaB, phaC), and Glu transport and deamination (e.g., gdhA, gltB, gltD)—progressively increased from Phase I to Phase III. The enrichment of these genes reflects a strengthened microbial capacity to pool diverse carbon reserves as operational conditions were optimized. In Phase Ⅱ, the moderate expression of these genes despite higher temperatures suggests that excessive DO-induced oxidative stress was a primary bottleneck for carbon accumulation, which was subsequently resolved by reducing the air-water ratio45. In Phase Ⅳ, despite the decline in both temperature and gene abundance, effluent TN remained at around 4 mg/L, indicating that transcriptional activity of key carbon metabolism genes persisted above functional thresholds. This highlights that intracellular carbon pools provided a metabolic buffer against seasonal cooling, enabling sustained endogenous denitrification.

Discussion

From a kinetic standpoint, the superior nitrogen removal performance of the optimized AOA process under low-strength influent can be rationalized using the classical Lawrence-McCarty models for activated sludge systems. In such carbon-limited environments, external organic substrates are insufficient to support continuous microbial growth, necessitating a metabolic shift toward a maintenance and endogenous respiration regime. The long SRT (50 day) and extended anoxic HRT provided conditions for this transition, enabling microorganisms to utilize intracellular polymers and decayed biomass as internal electron donors. These storage compounds buffered the adverse effects of low-strength influent through a hierarchical electron supply mechanism46,47. Specifically, PHA, as a readily accessible polymer, is rapidly depolymerized to fuel the initial stage of denitrification. Gly serves as a secondary, intermediate-rate electron donor through the glycolytic pathway. FAAs, particularly those derived from the slow hydrolysis of intracellular proteins, provide a chronic and sustained electron flux. This diverse electron donor pool ensures that the denitrification process does not prematurely cease during the extended anoxic HRT, even after rapidly biodegradable reserves are exhausted. This metabolic strategy is reflected in the low observed biomass yield (Yobs = 0.10–0.20 g VSS/g COD), which is substantially below the typical range of 0.40–0.50 g VSS/g COD reported for conventional activated sludge processes48,49. Such a low yield indicates that the majority of captured organic carbon was channeled into maintenance energy and endogenous electron supply rather than being assimilated into new cell mass. Concurrently, operational optimization—including the reduced anaerobic HRT to prevent premature carbon loss and the optimized aeration to minimize aerobic over-oxidation—collectively established a steady-state balance. The endogenous metabolic process provided a continuous and buffered electron supply, whereas the suppressed growth rate reduced the overall substrate demand. Consequently, even under seasonal temperatures down to 11.3 °C, the AOA system maintained efficient and stable nitrogen removal, demonstrating that the metabolic synergy of PHA, Gly, and FAAs serves as a sustainable driver for advanced nitrogen removal in low-strength municipal wastewater.

The optimization strategy in this study focused on the coordinated regulation of air-water ratios and anaerobic HRT, which together dictated the capture and subsequent utilization of intracellular carbon. Shortening the anaerobic HRT avoided unnecessary oxidation of influent organics and facilitated more effective storage of intracellular carbon50. At the same time, stepwise reduction of the air-water ratio limited aerobic overconsumption of these storage compounds, ensuring that a greater fraction of intracellular carbon was preserved and transferred to the anoxic zone51. Despite the air-water ratio being reduced to below 0.5, effluent NH4+-N concentrations remained near zero and effluent TN was halved compared with Phase I. These results demonstrate that stable nitrification can be sustained without high DO levels, challenging the conventional assumption that strong aeration is a prerequisite for reliable ammonia oxidation. Economic and environmental assessments further highlight the advantages of this approach (Fig. 6 and Supplementary Fig. 6). To ensure a rigorous evaluation, the performance of the optimized AOA (Line 1 of the Stage 2 Project) was benchmarked against two parallel control groups within the same WWTP receiving identical influent: (1) non-optimal AOA (parallel Lines 2 and 3 of the Stage 2 Project), which operated under the same configuration but maintained the original HRT distribution without optimization and used conventional high aeration strategies, and (2) conventional anaerobic/anoxic/oxic (A2O) lines from the Stage 1 Project operated under standard design parameters. Compared with the parallel A2O process, chemical consumption decreased by about 40%, mainly due to the elimination of external carbon dosing and reduced demand for coagulants. Aeration energy consumption declined by 73%, while return pump consumption declined by 19% owing to the removal of nitrified liquor circulation. Although agitator energy increased by 35% due to longer anoxic mixing, the overall electricity consumption of the biological reactors decreased by approximately 25%. Excess sludge production also declined by 8%, further lowering downstream treatment requirements. Collectively, these improvements demonstrate that advanced nitrogen removal (<5 mg/L TN) can be achieved under low-strength wastewater conditions while simultaneously reducing operational costs and carbon footprints. Beyond process efficiency, this work establishes the seasonal resilience of the optimized AOA process. By leveraging intracellular carbon storage and microbial adaptability, the system sustained robust nitrogen removal even under seasonal temperature decline to 11.3 °C, conditions that typically destabilize nitrification and denitrification in conventional processes. Methods

Field site description

This study was carried out at a full-scale MWTTP located in Guiyang, a southern city in China (26°39′N, 106°50′E). The region is characterized by a subtropical monsoon climate with distinct wet and dry seasons. The average annual precipitation is 1072.8 mm, with rainfall primarily concentrated in the summer months (detailed monthly precipitation and temperature profiles are provided in Supplementary Fig. 1). The plant has a design treatment capacity of 160,000 m3/day, primarily treating domestic sewage. After preliminary treatment through coarse screens, fine screens and aerated grit chambers, the influent enters a biological treatment system consisting of four parallel lanes, each with a capacity of 40,000 m3/day and configured with the AOA process. Lane 1 was selected for this study. The influent flow was regulated by inlet pumps, and the overall HRT ranged from 12 to 16 h. During the operational period, the sludge retention time (SRT) was maintained at approximately 50 days, and the mixed liquor suspended solids (MLSS) concentration was controlled at 5822 ± 1358 mg/L with f values of 0.6–0.7. Influent temperature varied seasonally between 11.3 °C and 24.6 °C. The detailed characteristics of the influent are summarized in Supplementary Table 1.

Process optimization, retrofit, and monitoring

A year-long operational optimization strategy divided the study into four stages (Table 1). Phase I represented the initial baseline configuration, which included a 0.5 h hydrolysis zone, 3.0 h anaerobic zone, 4.0 h aerobic zone, and 4.5 h anoxic zone. In Phase Ⅱ, aeration was intensified by increasing the air-water ratio, followed by a gradual decrease, while influent flow was reduced to 32,000 m3/day. The last corridor in the aerobic zone was switched to the anoxic zone to expand the denitrification capacity. Phase Ⅲ involved a strategic optimization of the anaerobic HRT by implementing a backward displacement of the influent point (Supplementary Fig. 7). The primary ambition of this adjustment was to curtail the secondary endogenous consumption of intracellular carbon reserves, which typically occurs during overly prolonged anaerobic contact50. By effectively halving the actual anaerobic HRT, we prioritized the preservation of stored carbon for the subsequent endogenous denitrification stage. As a functional consequence of this displacement, the initial section of the reactor was designated as a sludge hydrolysis zone. This zone was utilized to receive the RAS exclusively, allowing for the endogenous depletion of residual NO3˗ prior to the introduction of influent carbon. In Phase Ⅳ, the influent flow was restored to full capacity (40,000 m3/day), maintaining a stable low air-water ratio and optimized HRT distribution. The first three phases corresponded to the seasonal temperature rise, whereas the final phase reflected performance under decreasing temperature conditions. All optimization and retrofit strategies were implemented within the constraints of the original bioreactor structure, without major civil changes. Daily monitoring included influent TCOD, sCOD, NH4+-N, and TN, as well as effluent TCOD, sCOD, NH4+-N, NO2−-N, NO3−-N, and TN concentrations. Additionally, full-process spatial sampling campaigns were conducted monthly, covering key compartments. Wastewater sampling and analysis

Wastewater quality parameters (COD, NH4+-N, NO3−-N, NO2−-N and TN) were determined following standard methods52. COD was measured via dichromate digestion. NH4+-N, NO3−-N and NO2−-N were analyzed using spectrophotometric methods. TN was determined by alkaline persulfate digestion followed by UV detection. DO and temperature were monitored in situ using portable probes (Hach, USA).

Intracellular carbon determination

To evaluate intracellular carbon storage and metabolic intermediates, sludge samples were collected from key zones (hydrolysis, anaerobic, aerobic, anoxic, and clarifier) and subjected to biochemical analyses of PHA, Gly, and free FAAs (Supplementary Note 1). PHA was extracted and quantified using the classical gas chromatography (GC) method with methanolysis of intracellular polymers53. The organic phase containing methyl esters of PHA monomers was analyzed using a GC-FID system (Agilent 7890A, USA) equipped with a DB-1 column. Calibration curves were constructed using commercial poly(3-hydroxybutyrate-co-3-hydroxyvalerate) standards. Gly content was determined via the anthrone-sulfuric acid colorimetric method54. Absorbance was measured at 625 nm using a UV-Vis spectrophotometer (UNICO 2100, US), with glucose as the calibration standard. FAAs were quantified following a modified trichloroacetic acid extraction method27. Quantitative analysis was conducted using high-performance liquid chromatography (ACQUITY UPLC® I-Class, Waters, USA) equipped with an ACQUITY BEH Amide Column. Nineteen common FAAs were identified and quantified using authenticated standards (Sigma-Aldrich, USA). All analyses were performed in triplicate.

Microbiological analysis

Activated sludge samples were collected at four time points (Day 66, Day 164, Day 219 and Day 298) for microbial community analysis. The V3-V4 region of the 16S rRNA gene was amplified and sequenced on an Illumina MiSeq platform. Raw reads were quality filtered, trimmed, and merged using USEARCH, and clustered into operational taxonomic units (OTUs) at 97% similarity. Taxonomic assignments were performed against the SILVA 138 database. The raw sequencing data generated in this study have been deposited in the NCBI Sequence Read Archive (SRA) under the accession number PRJNA1307748.

Metagenomic sequencing and analysis

To elucidate the functional genes in the microbial community, metagenomic sequencing was performed on all four samples. DNA extraction used the DNeasy PowerSoil Kit (Qiagen, Germany). Libraries were constructed (350 bp insert) using the NEBNext Ultra DNA Library Prep Kit (NEB, USA) and sequenced (2 × 150 bp) on an Illumina NovaSeq 6000 platform. Reads were quality filtered with fastp and assembled using MEGAHIT. Open reading frames (ORFs) were predicted with Prodigal and clustered with CD-HIT at 95% identity. Functional annotation targeted nitrogen metabolism and intracellular carbon metabolism pathways. Searches used DIAMOND BLASTP with an E-value cutoff of 1e−5. Gene abundances were normalized to transcripts per million (TPM). Visualization of abundance profiles used ggplot2 and pheatmap in R (v4.2.1). All metagenomic sequencing data have been deposited in the NCBI SRA under the accession number PRJNA1310670.

Data calculation and statistical analysis

The observed yield coefficient (Yobs) was calculated using the Lawrence-McCarty microbial growth kinetic model55. The EDNR was calculated based on the linear slope of NO3−-N reduction normalized to MLVSS concentration (Supplementary Note 2). Statistical analyses including mean comparisons, one-way ANOVA, Pearson correlation, and hierarchical clustering were conducted using R software (v4.2.1). The significance of the results was evaluated by an analysis of variance. p > 0.05 was regarded as statistically insignificant, whereas p < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.
