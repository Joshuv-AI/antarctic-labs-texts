Application of Human Umbilical Cord Mesenchymal Stem Cells and Their Derived Exosomes in Gastrointestinal Diseases
Authors: Zhiliang Mai, Ningning Yue, Hailan Zhao, Lisheng Wang, Defeng Li
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractGastrointestinal (GI) diseases are increasing in prevalence worldwide and represent a major public health burden. However, current treatments remain limited and are often associated with adverse effects, highlighting the need for innovative therapeutic strategies. Human umbilical cord mesenchymal stem cells (hUC-MSCs) have emerged as promising candidates because of their anti-inflammatory, immunomodulatory, and regenerative properties. Accumulating evidence indicates that many of their therapeutic effects are mediated by extracellular vesicles, particularly hUC-MSC-derived exosomes (hUC-MSC-exos). This review first summarizes the composition and therapeutic mechanisms of hUC-MSCs and hUC-MSC-exos in GI diseases. We discuss in detail their roles in immune regulation, gut microbiota modulation, and metabolic regulation. Additionally, we highlight their potential as natural therapeutic agents and drug delivery vehicles, as well as recent engineering strategies designed to improve their therapeutic efficacy, stability, storage, and targeted delivery. We further summarize current clinical evidence. Despite encouraging preclinical findings, clinical evidence remains limited, and their effects in gastrointestinal tumors are context-dependent, with both tumor-promoting and tumor-suppressive activities reported. Further studies are needed to characterize the functional components of hUC-MSCs and hUC-MSC-exos and to clarify their effects on gastrointestinal tumors through rigorous in vivo investigations.

The Search Strategy

A systematic literature search was conducted in PubMed without language restrictions to identify published studies relevant to the scope of this review. The search covered studies published over the past five years, from 2021 to 2026. Only studies specifically investigating hUC-MSCs or hUC-MSC-exos were included, whereas studies focusing exclusively on MSCs, extracellular vesicles, or secretomes derived from other tissue sources were excluded. Detailed search terms and strategies are provided in Table S1, and the literature screening and selection process is summarized in Figure S1.

Introduction

Gastrointestinal (GI) diseases, including inflammatory bowel disease (IBD), gastric ulcers, small intestinal diseases, colorectal cancer (CRC), and gastric cancer (GC), continue to pose a major global public health challenge. Pharmacotherapy remains a mainstay for many non-malignant GI diseases, whereas surgery is often an essential component of treatment for GI malignancies.1 However, repeated or prolonged drug administration often provides only partial or transient relief and may cause adverse effects, including drug dependence, GI mucosal injury, gut microbiota dysbiosis, impaired nutrient absorption, and liver and kidney damage.2,3 Collectively, these limitations underscore the urgent need for safer and more effective therapeutic strategies for GI diseases.

In recent years, regenerative medicine has increasingly focused on mesenchymal stem cells (MSCs) as promising therapeutic candidates because of their self-renewal capacity, multipotency, and immunomodulatory functions.4,5 MSCs can be isolated from a variety of tissues, including the umbilical cord, adipose tissue, dermis, tendons, bone marrow, and dental pulp.6 Although MSCs from different tissue sources exhibit distinct biological properties, they generally meet the minimal criteria used to define MSCs: (1) plastic adherence under standard culture conditions; (2) expression of CD105, CD73, and CD90, with little or no expression of CD45, CD34, CD14 or CD11b, CD79a or CD19, and HLA-DR; and (3) the capacity to differentiate into osteoblasts, adipocytes, and chondroblasts under standard in vitro conditions.7,8 Among these tissue sources, MSCs isolated from human umbilical cord tissue—hereafter referred to as human umbilical cord mesenchymal stem cells (hUC-MSCs)—have attracted particular interest. Compared with MSCs derived from adult tissues, hUC-MSCs can be obtained through a non-invasive collection procedure and generally exhibit robust ex vivo expansion, multilineage differentiation capacity, and low immunogenicity.9 Moreover, compared with bone marrow-derived MSCs (BM-MSCs) and adipose tissue-derived MSCs (AD-MSCs), hUC-MSCs have been reported to proliferate more rapidly and display stronger migratory responses towards both unstimulated mononuclear cells and activated lymphocytes in mixed lymphocyte reactions.10 Collectively, these characteristics may enhance the ability of hUC-MSCs to respond to inflammatory and tumour-associated signals, thereby supporting their therapeutic application in immune-mediated and neoplastic diseases. A comparative overview of MSCs derived from different tissue sources is provided in Table 1. Beyond these intrinsic cellular properties, the therapeutic effects of hUC-MSCs are substantially mediated by their secretome, which includes cytokines, growth factors, and extracellular vesicles (EVs), particularly hUC-MSC-derived exosomes (hUC-MSC-exos). Importantly, the composition and functional activity of the hUC-MSC secretome are shaped by the local microenvironment and can be further modulated by preconditioning strategies, including exposure to inflammatory cytokines, hypoxia, and three-dimensional culture.11 Through these environmentally responsive paracrine mechanisms, hUC-MSCs and hUC-MSC-exos can regulate immune responses, restore tissue homeostasis, and promote tissue repair. While several reviews have summarized MSC biology and clinical use, very few have focused specifically on hUC-MSCs and hUC-MSC-exos, which may offer unique therapeutic advantages over other MSC sources. Here, we provide a comprehensive and critical overview of recent advances in the biological characteristics, molecular cargo, mechanisms of action, and therapeutic applications of hUC-MSCs and hUC-MSC-exos in GI diseases. Furthermore, this review explores the enhancement strategies, mechanism, and future perspectives for the optimization and clinical translation of hUC-MSCs and hUC-MSC-exos.

Characteristics of hUC-MSCs and hUC-MSC-Exos

hUC-MSCs are multipotent stromal cells isolated primarily from Wharton’s jelly, the gelatinous tissue surrounding the umbilical vessels.28 Compared with MSCs derived from other sources, hUC-MSCs offer distinct advantages for clinical translation owing to their superior proliferative capacity, low immunogenicity, and notably potent paracrine activity.12 Studies show that hUC-MSCs produce considerably higher concentrations of cytokines and growth factors in their conditioned media than bone marrow-derived MSCs, often at levels 10- to 100-fold greater, suggesting a correspondingly enhanced therapeutic potential.13 Consistent with this enhanced secretory capacity, comparative studies have shown that hUC-MSCs secrete significantly higher levels of IL-6, IL-8, G-CSF, and MCP-1 than MSCs derived from other sources, highlighting their distinctive capacity to modulate inflammatory microenvironments.14,15 In line with these findings, comparative proteomic studies indicate that hUC-MSCs secrete a broader and more enriched repertoire of bioactive molecules than other tissue-derived MSCs, with reduced levels of MMPs and extracellular matrix (ECM) proteins that may limit tissue degradation while favoring chemokine and anti-inflammatory cytokine release.16,17

hUC-MSCs exhibit low MHC class I and minimal MHC class II and costimulatory molecule expression, thereby reducing their immunogenic profile. This low immunogenicity makes them suitable for allogeneic transplantation, as they do not efficiently activate allogeneic T cells in vitro.29 In addition to these cellular advantages, exosomes secreted by hUC-MSCs, an important subtype of EVs, have attracted increasing attention because they can transmit bioactive cargos while avoiding some limitations associated with cell-based transplantation. hUC-MSC-exos are nanovesicles with diameters ranging from 30 to 200 nm,30 and are enriched in diverse bioactive molecules. Notably, hUC-MSC-exos contain canonical EV markers, such as the tetraspanin family proteins CD63, CD81, and CD9, as well as heat shock proteins, ALIX, and TSG101.31 Beyond these structural markers, hUC-MSC-exos are enriched in functional cargos, including growth factors, cytokines, and microRNAs, which may collectively contribute to many of the therapeutic effects attributed to hUC-MSCs.32,33 Among these cargos, growth factors represent a critical component of the hUC-MSC secretome, including VEGF, TGF-β, HGF, PDGF, FGFs, EGF, and IGF, which promote angiogenesis, tissue remodeling, and immune modulation.34,35 Cytokines and chemokines further regulate intercellular communication and immune responses. Anti-inflammatory cytokines such as IL-10 and TGF-β suppress immune overactivation, while chemokines such as IL-8 and MCP-1 guide immune cell recruitment and angiogenesis, thereby aiding tissue repair. Additionally, miRNAs are particularly important functional mediators that have emerged as key post-transcriptional regulators.36 Studies have demonstrated that miR-29a-3p, miR-100-5p, miR-125b-5p, and other miRNAs can be found in hUC-MSC-exos, where they regulate inflammation, apoptosis, and oncogenic signaling pathways.37,38 Conversely, tumor-promoting miRNAs such as miR-183-5p, miR-372-3p, are reportedly absent or minimally expressed, highlighting the potential safety and therapeutic relevance of hUC-MSC-exos in oncology-related applications12 (Figure 1). Taken together, these findings highlight the unique biological properties and therapeutic potential of hUC-MSCs and their exosomes as promising tools for cell-based and cell-free regenerative therapy. Their high proliferative capacity, low immunogenicity, potent paracrine activity, and exosome-mediated delivery of functional cargos collectively support their value in regenerative medicine and immune-related diseases. With continued advances in exosome biology and bioengineering technologies, hUC-MSC-based and hUC-MSC-exo-based approaches may provide safer, more effective, and more precise therapeutic options in the future.

Mechanism of hUC-MSCs and hUC-MSC-Exos for GI Diseases

Epitranscriptomic Regulation and Post-Translational Modification

The molecular cargo carried by hUC-MSC-exos can regulate post-translational modifications and epitranscriptomic processes, thereby exerting therapeutic effects through multiple molecular mechanisms. In macrophages, hUC-MSC-exos promoted M2 polarization by upregulating the m6A methyltransferase METTL3 and the m6A reader YTHDF1, which enhanced the m6A modification and expression of Slc37a2 mRNA and consequently suppressed pro-inflammatory responses.39 Similarly, hUC-MSC-exosomal miR-320d disrupted the association of ALKBH5 and YTHDF2 with BCAT2 mRNA, thereby increasing the m6A modification of BCAT2 mRNA and enhancing BCAT2 expression. This mechanism inhibited macrophage ferroptosis and thereby alleviated IBD.40 Beyond m6A-dependent RNA regulation, hUC-MSC-exos can also modulate post-translational protein modifications. In intestinal epithelial cells, hUC-MSC-exos reversed epithelial-to-mesenchymal transition (EMT) by increasing the O-GlcNAcylation of RACK1, thereby ameliorating intestinal inflammation and reshaping the gut microbiota.41 hUC-MSC-exos also activated the SIRT1–FXR signaling axis in macrophages, leading to reduced FXR acetylation and subsequent inhibition of NLRP3 inflammasome activation.42 The regulatory effects of hUC-MSC-derived products on protein modifications may also contribute to their antitumor activity. In CRC, hUC-MSCs enhanced the activities of O-glycosyltransferases, including T-synthase and C3GnT, thereby remodeling O-glycan profiles and inhibiting the proliferation and migration of Tn-positive cancer cells.43 Collectively, these findings indicate that hUC-MSC-exos and hUC-MSCs exert therapeutic effects by coordinating epitranscriptomic regulation and post-translational modifications, thereby modulating RNA fate, protein activity, and downstream inflammatory and tumor-associated signaling pathways (Figure 2). Modulating the Immune Response

Macrophages

Macrophages are key immune targets of hUC-MSC-exos, which regulate macrophage polarization and inflammatory cell-death programs in both inflammatory and neoplastic settings. Following mesenteric administration, hUC-MSC-exos delivered miR-21-5p to macrophages, where it targeted SPRY2, activated ERK signaling, and promoted M2 polarization, thereby alleviating colitis.44 Similarly, hUC-MSC-exosomal miR-203a-3p suppressed Caspase-4-dependent macrophage pyroptosis and consequently ameliorated colitis.45 hUC-MSC-exos also protected against intestinal inflammation by regulating macrophage ferroptosis. Specifically, they upregulated farnesoid X receptor (FXR) expression, thereby inhibiting macrophage ferroptosis and alleviating IBD.46 Moreover, hUC-MSC-exos carrying miR-378a-5p suppressed NLRP3 inflammasome activation and blocked macrophage pyroptosis, thereby protecting against DSS-induced colitis.47 Beyond inflammatory diseases, hUC-MSC-exos can exert distinct effects on macrophage polarization within the tumor microenvironment. In CRC, hUC-MSC-exosomal miR-1827 downregulated SUCNR1, thereby inhibiting pro-tumorigenic M2 macrophage polarization and suppressing colorectal cancer metastasis.48 Collectively, these findings demonstrate that hUC-MSC-exos exert context-dependent immunomodulatory effects by coordinating macrophage polarization and regulated cell-death pathways (Figure 3). T Cells

hUC-MSCs and their exosomes profoundly shape T-cell responses in IBD, primarily by altering the balance between regulatory and effector subsets. In inflammatory settings, hUC-MSCs promote immune tolerance by expanding regulatory T-cell populations while suppressing pro-inflammatory effector T cells. Intraperitoneally injected hUC-MSCs rapidly migrated to mesenteric lymph nodes, where hUC-MSC-derived TGF-β1 promoted local regulatory T-cell (Treg) differentiation and suppressed T helper 17 (Th17) responses; this effect was abolished following TGFB1 knockdown.49 Similarly, hUC-MSCs promoted the expansion of type 1 regulatory T cells (Tr1) in the spleen and mesenteric lymph nodes (MLNs) through indoleamine 2,3-dioxygenase-dependent mechanisms, while reducing the proportions of Th1 and Th17 cells.50 Consistent with these findings, hUC-MSC-exos shifted the Treg/Th17 balance toward an anti-inflammatory phenotype.51 Additionally, hsa-miR-27b-3p carried by hUC-MSC-exos targeted PIK3CA and inhibited the PI3K/AKT/STAT3 signaling pathway, thereby restoring the Th17/Treg balance in the colonic lamina propria and markedly alleviating DSS-induced colitis.52 Collectively, these findings illustrate that hUC-MSCs and hUC-MSC-exos fine-tune T-cell immunity, fostering Treg/Tr1-mediated tolerance in colitis.

Other Immune Cells

Beyond their well-characterized actions on macrophages and T cells, hUC-MSCs and hUC-MSC-exos also modulate other immune populations. Notably, hUC-MSCs can facilitate the generation and functional maturation of specialized innate lymphoid cell subsets. For example, hUC-MSCs were used as a good manufacturing practice-compliant feeder layer to efficiently generate functional IL-22-producing group 3 innate lymphoid cells (ILC3s) from cord blood-derived CD34⁺ progenitor cells, providing a potentially scalable source of ILC3s for the treatment of inflammatory diseases and cancer.53 Additionally, hUC-MSC-exos protected intestinal barrier integrity by suppressing mast cell activation through modulation of the IL-33/ST2 signaling axis.54 Collectively, these findings extend the immunomodulatory repertoire of hUC-MSC-based products beyond macrophages and T cells, highlighting their potential to support the generation of functional ILC3s and restrain mast cell–mediated inflammation, thereby promoting immune homeostasis and intestinal barrier protection (Figure 4). Regulating the Homeostasis of Gut Microbiota

Table 2 summarizes the evidence supporting the regulatory effects of hUC-MSCs and their exosomes on the gut microbiota. hUC-MSCs and their exosomes exert protective effects in colitis in part by reshaping the intestinal microbial ecosystem and its metabolic output. Faecal microbiota transplantation and antibiotic depletion experiments further demonstrated that hUC-MSC treatment increased the abundance of short-chain fatty acid (SCFA)-producing bacteria, including Akkermansia, Faecalibaculum, and Clostridia_UCG-014, thereby increasing SCFA production and restoring T-cell immune homeostasis.55 Consistently, hUC-MSC treatment upregulated beneficial bacteria such as Lactobacillus, Blautia, Clostridia, and Helicobacter in DSS-induced colitis, and this modulation was linked to MUC1 pathway regulation.56 In TNBS-induced colitis, hUC-MSCs similarly prevented microbial dysbiosis by increasing the relative abundances of Bacteroidetes, Firmicutes, and Tenericutes while reducing Proteobacteria, together with correcting disturbances in sulfur and riboflavin metabolism.57 Beyond the effects of the parental cells, hUC-MSC-exos restored the relative abundances of Firmicutes and Bacteroidetes and shifted the overall microbial community toward that of healthy controls. This restoration was accompanied by increases in potentially beneficial genera, including Akkermansia, Ruminococcaceae_UCG-014, Alistipes, Lactococcus, and Bifidobacterium, as well as coordinated changes in the metagenomic and metabolomic profiles mediated through the gut metagenome–metabolome–FXR axis.58 The microbiota-modulating effects of hUC-MSCs were also closely associated with intestinal immune regulation. Specifically, hUC-MSC administration reduced the abundance of dominant bacterial taxa associated with dysbiosis in DSS-induced colitis, while increasing Tregs and IgA⁺ plasma cells in gut-associated lymphoid tissues and altering the proportion of IgA-coated bacteria.59 Similarly, in another TNBS-induced colitis model, hUC-MSCs alleviated clinical symptoms and colonic histopathological damage while restoring both microbial and immune homeostasis, as evidenced by increased abundances of beneficial bacteria, including Lactobacillus murinus and Lactobacillus johnsonii, and an elevated Firmicutes-to-Bacteroidetes ratio.60 Altogether, these findings highlight that hUC-MSC- and hUC-MSC-exo-based therapies rebalance the gut microbiota, promote microbial SCFA production, and engage microbiota–metabolite–immune crosstalk to alleviate intestinal inflammation. Metabolic Reprogramming

Beyond immunomodulation, hUC-MSCs and hUC-MSC-exos can directly regulate metabolic and redox pathways to attenuate tissue injury and suppress tumour progression. In colitis, hUC-MSC-exos carrying miR-129-5p target ACSL4 and inhibit lipid peroxidation and ferroptosis. Mechanistically, these exosomes restore the system Xc−/GSH/GPX4 antioxidant axis and reduce DMT1-mediated iron uptake, thereby limiting toxic phospholipid peroxide accumulation and promoting intestinal repair.61 In CRC, hUC-MSC-exos enriched with miR-486-5p suppress glycolysis and stemness by directly targeting NEK2, which markedly reduces tumour cell proliferation and metastasis.62 Complementing these exosome-mediated effects, hUC-MSCs undergo metabolic reprogramming characterized by enhanced glucose and lipid uptake, phosphoinositide signalling, and membrane and protein recycling; this reprogrammed state ultimately suppresses CRC cell proliferation and metabolic activity.63 Collectively, these findings demonstrate that hUC-MSCs and hUC-MSC-exos exert tissue-protective and antitumour effects by reprogramming metabolic and redox networks, thereby suppressing ferroptotic injury in colitis while limiting glycolysis, stemness, and proliferative metabolism in CRC (Figure 5). Natural hUC-MSCs and hUC-MSCs-Exos for GI Diseases

Intestinal Diseases

IBD

hUC-MSCs and hUC-MSC-exos have shown considerable therapeutic potential in IBD through complementary anti-inflammatory, regenerative, immunomodulatory, and anti-fibrotic mechanisms. Intravenously administered hUC-MSCs ameliorated IBD in rats by downregulating pro-inflammatory cytokines, including IL-1β and IL-17A, as well as other inflammatory mediators, such as HMGB1 and NLRP3.64 At the molecular level, hUC-MSC-exos alleviated IBD through the lnc78583-mediated regulation of the miR-3202/HOXB13 pathway.65 Several studies further indicate that the promotion of epithelial regeneration and mucosal repair represents a major mechanism underlying the therapeutic effects of hUC-MSCs and their exosomes. Liang et al66 demonstrated that intraperitoneally administered hUC-MSC-exos promoted the regeneration of intestinal stem cells and the intestinal epithelium in colitis by delivering key miRNAs that activate the Wnt/β-catenin signaling pathway. Yang et al67 further demonstrated that intraperitoneally administered hUC-MSC-exos protected against experimental IBD by enhancing mucosal barrier repair and restoring intestinal immune homeostasis through TSG-6-mediated mechanisms. Similarly, intraperitoneally administered hUC-MSCs alleviated UC by reducing endoplasmic reticulum stress through IRE1-mediated XBP1 splicing, thereby decreasing epithelial cell apoptosis and promoting mucosal repair.68 Beyond epithelial regeneration, hUC-MSC-exos also modulate vascular remodeling and immune responses during intestinal inflammation. Specifically, hUC-MSC-exos ameliorated experimental IBD by regulating lymphangiogenesis through the miR-302d-3p/VEGFR3/AKT signaling pathway.69 They also prevented the exacerbation of colitis through miR-146a-mediated suppression of SUMO1.70 In addition to these regenerative, vascular, and immunomodulatory effects, accumulating evidence supports the potent anti-fibrotic activity of hUC-MSC-exos in IBD. Choi et al71 demonstrated that hUC-MSC-exo-conditioned medium inhibited TGF-β1-induced fibrotic activation of human intestinal myofibroblasts in vitro and, when administered intraperitoneally, attenuated intestinal fibrosis in chronic DSS-induced colitis by blocking the Rho/MRTF/SRF signaling pathway. Consistently, intravenously administered hUC-MSC-exos alleviated DSS-induced intestinal fibrosis by suppressing ERK phosphorylation, decreasing the expression of profibrotic molecules, and inhibiting the proliferation and migration of intestinal fibroblasts.72 These findings highlight the considerable therapeutic potential of hUC-MSCs and hUC-MSC-exos for the treatment of IBD and its associated complications. Other Intestinal Diseases

Beyond IBD, hUC-MSCs and hUC-MSC-exos have demonstrated therapeutic potential in a broad range of intestinal injuries and disorders. In models of radiation-induced intestinal injury, orally administered hUC-MSC-exos alleviated acute intestinal damage by activating the Nrf2/HO-1/NQO1 antioxidant pathway.73 Similarly, intravenously administered hUC-MSC-exos attenuated inflammatory responses, reduced intestinal epithelial cell death, and promoted mucosal regeneration, thereby ameliorating radiation-induced intestinal injury.74 Intraperitoneal administration of hUC-MSCs also prolonged survival in rats with radiation enteritis.75 Furthermore, intraperitoneally administered hUC-MSCs alleviated radiation-induced colorectal fibrosis (RICF), indicating their potential to mitigate both acute tissue injury and chronic fibrotic remodeling following radiation exposure.76 hUC-MSCs and their exosomes also exert protective effects against intestinal barrier disruption caused by other pathological insults. Intraperitoneally administered hUC-MSC-exos attenuated antibiotic-induced intestinal mucosal barrier injury by suppressing excessive autophagy-mediated apoptosis through the S100G/mTOR signaling pathway.77 In sepsis-induced intestinal injury, hUC-MSC-exos restored the dysregulated miR-218-5p/CBX8 axis, thereby preserving intestinal homeostasis.78 Moreover, intravenously administered emodin-loaded hUC-MSC-exos synergistically alleviated acute pancreatitis-associated intestinal injury by suppressing NLRP3 inflammasome activation, pyroptosis, reactive oxygen species production, and pro-inflammatory cytokine expression.79 Protective effects have also been observed in neonatal intestinal disorders. Intraperitoneally administered hUC-MSCs ameliorated necrotizing enterocolitis (NEC) and reduced associated intestinal, pulmonary, and neuronal injury.80 Consistently, hUC-MSC-exos alleviated NEC by reducing excessive intestinal autophagy and inflammatory responses in intestinal epithelial cells while enhancing their migratory capacity.81 In addition, intravenously administered hUC-MSCs alleviated irritable bowel syndrome (IBS) in rats by homing to colonic tissues, reducing the expression of inflammatory mediators, restoring intestinal barrier protein expression, and maintaining the abundance and diversity of the gut microbiota.82 During allergic responses, hUC-MSCs maintained epithelial homeostasis and intestinal barrier integrity in food allergy-associated enteritis by modulating the Bax/Bcl-2 pathway in histamine-stimulated intestinal mucosa and thereby exerting anti-apoptotic effects.83 Finally, hUC-MSC-exos significantly promoted anal fistula wound healing in rats through activation of the HIF-1α/TGF-β/Smad signaling pathway.84 Collectively, these findings underscore the broad therapeutic potential of hUC-MSCs and hUC-MSC-exos across diverse intestinal disorders by limiting inflammation and cell death, preserving epithelial barrier integrity, promoting tissue repair, and restoring intestinal homeostasis.

Pro-Tumorigenic Effects

In gastrointestinal malignancies, hUC-MSCs and hUC-MSC-exos may exert context-dependent pro-tumorigenic effects. In colorectal cancer, interactions between hUC-MSCs and colorectal cancer stem cells promoted the expression of epithelial-to-mesenchymal transition (EMT)-related proteins through activation of the PI3K/AKT signaling pathway, potentially enhancing tumor aggressiveness.85 Similarly, in gastric cancer, hUC-MSC-exosomal miR-301b-3p promoted multidrug resistance by suppressing TXNIP expression. This mechanism enhanced the proliferation and migration of cisplatin/vincristine-resistant gastric cancer cells, inhibited apoptosis, and accelerated tumor growth in nude mice.86

Anti-Tumorigenic Effects

In contrast to the pro-tumorigenic findings described above, accumulating evidence indicates that hUC-MSC-based and hUC-MSC-exo-based interventions can also exert tumor-suppressive effects. In vitro, hUC-MSCs induced apoptosis in HT-29 cells.87 Consistently, hUC-MSC-exos promoted apoptosis in CRC cells.88 Furthermore, hUC-MSC-conditioned medium (hUC-MSC-CM) inhibited CRC cell growth by activating AMPK and suppressing mTOR signaling, thereby inducing autophagy.89 hUC-MSC-exos also transferred miR-431-5p to CRC cells, which suppressed PRDX1 expression and consequently inhibited tumor cell growth.90 In vivo, intraperitoneally administered hUC-MSCs suppressed AOM/DSS-induced colitis-associated colorectal cancer while alleviating colitis, partly by reducing the proportion of pro-inflammatory macrophages.91 In addition to the intrinsic antitumor activity of hUC-MSCs, engineered hUC-MSCs have been investigated as tumor-targeted delivery platforms. For example, hUC-MSCs co-expressing TNFSF18 and CXCL9 produced the strongest inhibition of CT26 tumor growth, whereas TNFSF4-expressing hUC-MSCs exhibited the weakest antitumor effect.92 Moreover, hUC-MSC-exos served as delivery vehicles for a phosphorodiamidate morpholino oligomer targeting miR-146b-5p (PMO-146b), thereby suppressing colon cancer progression.93 Beyond CRC, hUC-MSC-derived miR-655-3p inhibited the progression of esophageal squamous cell carcinoma (ESCC) by suppressing HIF-1α activation through the LMO4/HDAC2 axis.94 In gastric cancer (GC), intravenously administered hUC-MSC-exos loaded with miR-13896 inhibited tumor growth and metastasis by targeting ATG2A-mediated autophagy, demonstrating the potential of engineered hUC-MSC-exos as an antitumor delivery platform.95

Collectively, whether hUC-MSCs and hUC-MSC-exos ultimately promote or suppress tumor progression is likely determined by the interplay among their functional cargo, the molecular characteristics of tumor cells, and the host immune microenvironment. Importantly, some of the currently reported tumor-related effects are based primarily on in vitro observations and therefore may not fully recapitulate the complex interactions within the tumor microenvironment in vivo. Given the increasingly important role of antitumor immunity in cancer therapy, future studies should prioritize physiologically relevant in vivo models and systematically investigate how hUC-MSCs and hUC-MSC-exos influence tumor-infiltrating immune cells and antitumor immune responses before definitive conclusions regarding their effects on cancer progression can be drawn. Moreover, the intrinsic heterogeneity of unmodified hUC-MSCs and hUC-MSC-exos may contribute to variable biological effects and raises potential concerns regarding the promotion of tumor-cell proliferation, metastasis, or therapeutic resistance. In contrast, rationally engineered hUC-MSCs and hUC-MSC-exos carrying defined antitumor cargos, such as tumor-suppressive miRNAs, anti-miRNAs, or immunostimulatory molecules, combined with rigorous optimization of dose, targeting, and safety, may provide a more controllable platform for the development of antitumor therapies.

Gastric Diseases

hUC-MSC-based interventions have also shown regenerative potential in gastric mucosal diseases. Intravenously administered hUC-MSCs exerted regenerative effects against Helicobacter pylori-associated chronic atrophic gastritis (CAG) through the antimutagenic activities of IL-10, CD36, and ATG5, together with the tumor-suppressive effects of STC-1, TSP-1, and 15-PGDH.96 Similarly, intravenously administered hUC-MSCs migrated to the injured gastric mucosa and facilitated tissue repair. Mechanistically, hUC-MSC treatment increased the secretion of pepsinogen I (PGI) and gastrin-17 (G-17), reduced the levels of inflammatory cytokines, and attenuated gastric fibrosis and mucosal cell apoptosis, thereby alleviating CAG.97 Submucosal administration of hUC-MSCs also reversed CAG in rabbits by suppressing gastric mucosal cell apoptosis.98 Beyond CAG, intravenously administered hUC-MSC-CM promoted gastric ulcer healing through TRIM29-mediated activation of ERK/AKT signaling, which enhanced cell proliferation and cell-cycle progression while reducing apoptosis.99 Collectively, these findings support the therapeutic potential of hUC-MSC-based interventions for gastric mucosal diseases through complementary anti-inflammatory, anti-apoptotic, anti-fibrotic, and regenerative mechanisms. However, the available evidence has largely relied on systemic or invasive local administration, which may limit the practicality of repeated treatment for gastric diseases. Given the anatomical accessibility of the gastrointestinal tract and the potential advantages of non-invasive delivery, future studies should evaluate whether orally administered hUC-MSC-derived products can retain biological activity, achieve effective gastric delivery, and provide comparable therapeutic efficacy and safety.

Pharmacological Preconditioning and Culture Optimization

Preconditioning strategies, including small-molecule modulation, have emerged as important approaches to enhance the efficacy of hUC-MSC-based products. Among these approaches, pharmacological preconditioning can enhance hUC-MSC metabolism, survival, and immunoregulatory activity across diverse disease models. In a colitis model, wogonin-preconditioned hUC-MSCs administered intraperitoneally exhibited enhanced glycolysis through activation of AKT signaling and increased expression of IL-10, IDO, and ARG1, thereby improving their therapeutic efficacy;100 In vitro, resveratrol promoted hUC-MSCs into a fibroblast-like activation state by stimulating collagen synthesis and AKT phosphorylation while suppressing pro-inflammatory and pro-apoptotic mediators, including IL-6, TGF-β, caspase-9, and Bax.101 Quercetin-preconditioned hUC-MSCs also exerted stronger immunomodulatory effects after intraperitoneal administration, reducing colonic inflammation through the IL-10/JAK/STAT signaling axis.102 Similarly, berberine activated AMPK signaling, upregulated GLUT1, and promoted aerobic glycolysis and TSG-6 expression, thereby enhancing the immunoregulatory function and therapeutic efficacy of hUC-MSCs in colitis.103 Carotene pretreatment likewise increased the expression of immunoregulatory mediators, including TGF-β1, COX-2, and IDO; following intraperitoneal administration, these preconditioned cells increased the abundance of Treg cells and M2 macrophages and alleviated colitis.104 Daily intraperitoneal administration of 2 × 109 thrombin-preconditioned hUC-MSC-derived exosomes alleviated necrotizing enterocolitis.105 By contrast, in another colitis study, intraperitoneally administered hUC-MSCs, either alone or combined with mesalazine, improved disease outcomes, whereas exosomes alone did not produce significant benefits.106 These contrasting findings underscore that the therapeutic performance of hUC-MSC-derived exosomes may depend on their preparation, modification, dose, and disease context.

Inflammatory priming provides another strategy for enhancing the immunosuppressive phenotype of hUC-MSCs. Pretreatment with TNF-α and IFN-γ shifted hUC-MSC metabolism toward glycolysis and increased IDO and TSG-6 expression, thereby strengthening their anti-inflammatory activity after intravenous infusion.107 Consistently, combined IFN-γ and TNF-α stimulation synergistically induced PD-L1 expression and improved the therapeutic efficacy of hUC-MSCs in ulcerative colitis (UC).108 Moreover, pretreatment with M1 macrophage-conditioned medium enhanced hUC-MSC survival and anti-inflammatory activity, resulting in greater improvements in colitis severity and inflammatory tissue damage.109

Beyond pharmacological and inflammatory priming, optimization of the culture microenvironment can further improve therapeutic performance. Under hypoxic conditions, trichostatin A pretreatment upregulated CXCR4 and the adenovirus receptor, thereby enhancing the tumor tropism of intravenously infused hUC-MSCs and improving oncolytic adenoviral delivery to p53-deficient colorectal tumors.110 Three-dimensional Matrigel culture similarly reprogrammed high-passage hUC-MSCs toward a YAP1-positive state, activated Hippo pathway-associated remodeling, and promoted TGF-β1 release; after intravenous infusion, these cells modulated T-cell subsets and ameliorated both colitis and diabetes.111 In addition, intestinal epithelial cell-conditioned medium promoted the differentiation of hUC-MSCs toward an intestinal epithelial-like phenotype, as indicated by sucrase expression.112 Importantly, not all exposures are beneficial: diesel exhaust particles impaired hUC-MSC migration and osteogenic differentiation, thereby weakening their therapeutic efficacy in colitis.113

Overall, these preconditioning strategies—ranging from pharmacological activation of key pathways to three-dimensional culture and combination with conventional therapies—significantly augment the immunomodulatory, anti-inflammatory, and tumor-targeting capabilities of hUC-MSC-based products.

Gene-Engineered hUC-MSCs and Exosomes as Targeted Therapeutic Platforms

Genetic modification strategies have significantly broadened the therapeutic scope of hUC-MSCs and their exosomes. For inflammatory disorders such as IBD, these strategies have primarily been designed to enhance tissue regeneration, immunomodulation, and intestinal homing. For example, a non-integrating human artificial chromosome vector encoding IL-22 and RSPO1 was introduced into “rejuvenated” hUC-MSCs with extended proliferative capacity, providing a novel cell-based therapeutic strategy for UC.114 Similarly, intravenous administration of hUC-MSCs stably overexpressing HIF-1α alleviated IBD by modulating macrophage polarization through the PI3Kγ pathway.115

Additional genetic and phenotype-directed interventions have been developed to strengthen the immunoregulatory activity of hUC-MSCs. Activation of the inducible caspase-9 (iCasp9) system with AP1903 generated apoptotic hUC-MSCs that retained potent immunosuppressive and anti-inflammatory properties both in vitro and in vivo.116 CD317 promoted the formation of a CD317–lipid raft–TNFR1 complex, thereby upregulating TSG-6 expression and enhancing the immunosuppressive capacity of hUC-MSCs; intravenous administration of these cells subsequently ameliorated colitis.117 Moreover, CD126(hi) hUC-MSCs increased TGF-β1 and IL-10 levels in DSS-treated mice, accompanied by an expansion of regulatory T cells.118 Pharmacological inhibition of PARP or genetic deletion of PARP1 further potentiated the immunoregulatory response of MSCs to IFN-γ and TNF-α by enhancing STAT1 phosphorylation at Tyr701 and increasing the expression of immunosuppressive mediators.119 In addition, adenovirus-mediated ATG5 overexpression enhanced hUC-MSC autophagy, immunoregulatory activity, and antioxidant capacity. Following intravenous administration, ATG5-overexpressing hUC-MSCs alleviated colitis by suppressing IL-17/NF-κB inflammatory signaling, increasing the secretion of the anti-inflammatory metabolite PGD2, and enriching butyrate-producing bacteria.120

Genetic engineering has also expanded the antitumor applications of hUC-MSCs and their exosomes. In GC, hUC-MSC-derived exosomes carrying lipocalin-type prostaglandin D synthase (L-PGDS) reduced the expression of the stemness-associated factors OCT4, NANOG, and SOX2 while inhibiting STAT3 phosphorylation.121 Furthermore, hUC-MSCs co-overexpressing CXCR4 and LIGHT exhibited enhanced tumor tropism and significantly reduced tumor volume, increased necrotic areas, and decreased the Ki-67 proliferation index compared with control cells in vivo.122

Collectively, these engineering approaches endow hUC-MSCs and hUC-MSC-derived exosomes with more precisely defined therapeutic functions, including cytokine delivery, macrophage reprogramming, enhanced immunosuppression, autophagy regulation, tumor targeting, and controllable safety-switch activation. These advances substantially strengthen their therapeutic potential in both IBD and GI malignancies.

Material Engineering and Biomimetic Strategies for Optimized Delivery

To overcome the physiological barriers that limit the stability, bioavailability, and tissue targeting of hUC-MSC-exos, diverse material-engineering strategies have been developed to improve their therapeutic performance. For gastrointestinal delivery, hUC-MSC-exos were encapsulated in poly(lactic-co-glycolic acid) (PLGA) microparticles, enabling oral administration for the treatment of IBD.123 In a complementary approach, hUC-MSC-exos were lyophilized with trehalose and mannitol as cryoprotectants, thereby preserving their particle concentration and size distribution during long-term storage. The lyophilized vesicles were subsequently encapsulated in enteric-coated capsules to protect them during gastrointestinal transit and promote intestinal release. Oral administration of this formulation effectively alleviated experimental colitis in rats.124 Biomimetic membrane engineering and cargo-loading technologies have also been used to improve systemic delivery and antitumor activity. hUC-MSC-exos were fused with neutrophil membranes to generate hybrid biomimetic vesicles with enhanced tumor-targeting capacity and prolonged blood circulation.125 In addition, a sequential combination of sonication and electroporation enabled efficient loading of miR-145 into hUC-MSC-derived small extracellular vesicles. The resulting miR-145-loaded sEVs suppressed colorectal cancer growth in vitro and, following tail-vein administration, in vivo by downregulating fascin actin-bundling protein 1 (FSCN1).126 For localized and sustained delivery, hUC-MSC-exos were incorporated into gelatin sponge scaffolds to generate a composite system termed GS Hypo-Exos. By integrating sustained exosome release with immunomodulatory activity, this system promoted structural and functional tissue repair, reduced TNF-α levels, and increased the expression of IL-4, VEGF, and TGF-β1, thereby facilitating fistula healing.127 Collectively, these material-engineering strategies improve the oral stability, systemic circulation, cargo-loading efficiency, tissue targeting, and local retention of hUC-MSC-exos, thereby expanding their therapeutic applications in IBD, CRC, and fistula repair.

Clinical Translation of hUC-MSCs and hUC-MSC-Exos Therapies

Table 4 summarizes early clinical evidence suggesting that the immunomodulatory properties of hUC-MSCs and hUC-MSC-exos may translate into therapeutic benefits for patients with refractory IBD. In a single-center Phase II trial, direct injection of hUC-MSC-exos into fistula tracts of patients with perianal Crohn’s disease (CD) resistant to anti-TNF-α therapy resulted in complete fistula closure in 60% (12/20) of patients and closure of 69.7% of treated tracts, accompanied by reduced local inflammation and enhanced tissue regeneration.128 A pilot study in five patients with refractory perianal fistulae similarly observed complete healing in three patients after hUC-MSC-exos injection, with no local or systemic adverse events.129 Consistent with these findings, a single intralesional injection of allogeneic hUC-MSCs in patients with complex, treatment-refractory perianal CD significantly improved the Perianal Crohn’s Disease Activity Index, pelvic MRI-based score, Crohn’s Disease Activity Index scores, and quality-of-life score. At week 52, the probability of remaining recurrence-free was 70%.130 For luminal disease, both systemic and combined local–systemic administration strategies have shown preliminary efficacy. A Phase I trial of intravenous hUC-MSCs in six patients with active UC reported sustained safety and complete clinical remission (Mayo score 0) in three of five patients followed up for 24 months.131 Furthermore, combined endoscopic submucosal and intravenous administration of hUC-MSCs in 17 patients with medically intractable CD led to an endoscopic response (≥50% SES-CD reduction) in 47% of patients and mucosal healing in 17.65%, with transcriptomic evidence of enhanced barrier integrity and attenuation of TNF, IL-17, and TLR signaling pathways.132 In another study, repeated intravenous infusion of hUC-MSCs alleviated the clinical symptoms and signs of Crohn’s disease, as reflected by improvements in body weight, Crohn’s Disease Activity Index scores, C-reactive protein levels, and erythrocyte sedimentation rate.133 Similarly, in patients with moderate-to-severe ulcerative colitis, intravenous hUC-MSC therapy achieved clinical response and remission rates of 80.8% and 46.2%, respectively, at 2 months, and 75.0% and 37.5%, respectively, at 6 months. Endoscopic and histological assessments further demonstrated improvement of the colonic mucosa in treatment responders.134 Collectively, these early studies support the clinical feasibility and potential efficacy of hUC-MSC- and hUC-MSC-exo-based therapies across both perianal and luminal IBD, although confirmation in larger randomized controlled trials remains necessary. Conclusions and Future Perspective

Collectively, hUC-MSCs and their exosomes hold considerable promise in regenerative medicine, particularly for GI diseases. Both hUC-MSCs and hUC-MSC-exos deliver a rich repertoire of bioactive molecules, including non-coding RNAs and proteins, which modulate critical processes such as immune regulation, gut microbiota homeostasis, protein translation, and post-translational modifications. Their capacity to orchestrate these mechanisms renders them promising therapeutic candidates for GI diseases. Recent advances in engineering strategies, including genetic modification, preconditioning, and other approaches, have further augmented the therapeutic potential of hUC-MSCs and hUC-MSC-exos. Strategies such as hypoxic preconditioning and biomaterial-assisted engineering, including neutrophil membrane-based approaches, have improved the stability, delivery efficiency, sustained release, and tissue targeting of hUC-MSC-exos, thereby enhancing their therapeutic performance at sites of injury.

Despite these encouraging developments, several major challenges must be addressed before the broader clinical translation of hUC-MSCs and hUC-MSC-exos. First, standard procedures for the isolation, purification, and characterization of various subtypes of hUC-MSCs and hUC-MSC-exos are urgently required. Future research should prioritize standardized isolation protocols, rigorous experimental design, and comprehensive safety evaluations. Second, the application of hUC-MSCs and hUC-MSC-exos in cancer therapy requires particular caution because both pro-tumorigenic and anti-tumor effects have been reported. More comprehensive in vitro and in vivo studies, together with human organoid-based models, are therefore needed to define tumor-specific responses and determine the therapeutic sensitivity of different cancer types to hUC-MSC- and hUC-MSC-exo-based interventions. Given the important role of immunotherapy in cancer treatment, future studies should also systematically investigate how hUC-MSCs and hUC-MSC-exos influence tumor-associated immune populations, particularly cytotoxic T cells and Tumor-Associated Macrophages (TAM). Such studies may help clarify their compatibility with immune checkpoint blockade and provide a rationale for potential combination strategies with therapies targeting PD-1 or related immune checkpoints. Third, for GI diseases, oral administration may offer practical advantages over intravenous or intraperitoneal delivery, including greater patient acceptability, direct access to the gastrointestinal tract, and potentially reduced systemic clearance. However, the limited stability of hUC-MSCs and hUC-MSC-exos in the harsh gastrointestinal environment currently restricts oral application, highlighting the need for delivery systems that protect these products from gastric acidity, digestive enzymes, and other luminal stresses while enabling site-specific release. Accordingly, optimization of production yield, long-term stability, formulation, storage, transportation, and oral delivery, together with robust quality-control standards, will be essential for clinical translation. The optimal dose for human application also remains to be established. With the rapid development of organoid technologies, patient-derived gastrointestinal organoids may provide physiologically relevant platforms for evaluating therapeutic responses, comparing dose ranges, and facilitating individualized treatment optimization before clinical application. In parallel, a comprehensive understanding of the cellular origin, molecular composition, and bioactive cargo of hUC-MSCs and hUC-MSC-exos will be essential for evaluating their in vivo safety, biological activities, and batch-to-batch consistency.

Overall, overcoming these translational challenges through standardized manufacturing, optimized delivery strategies, and rigorous safety evaluation will be critical for realizing the full therapeutic potential of hUC-MSCs and hUC-MSC-exos in GI diseases.
