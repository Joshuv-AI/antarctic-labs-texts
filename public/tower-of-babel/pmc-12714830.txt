Risk factors for bacterial translocation after loop ileostomy closure in patients with colorectal cancer
Authors: Toshiyuki Adachi, Yusuke Inoue, Satomi Okada, Takayuki Miyoshi, Nozomi Ueki, Hirokazu Kurohama, Yuki Matsuoka, Akihiko Soyama, Kazuma Kobayashi, Tomohiko Adachi, Kengo Kanetaka, Susumu Eguchi
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
PurposeAlthough rare, septic shock can develop following the closure of an ileostomy created during colorectal cancer surgery. In such cases, bacterial translocation (BT) is considered the primary diagnosis, and appropriate treatment is provided. Herein, we investigated the risk factors of BT following ileostomy closure after colorectal cancer surgery.MethodsA retrospective analysis was conducted using the colorectal cancer database of Nagasaki University, focusing on 91 patients who received ileostomy closure after colorectal cancer surgery. The patients were divided into two groups based on the occurrence of BT, defined as fever exceeding 38 °C without an identifiable cause, and data regarding patient background, surgical factors, and postoperative factors were assessed.ResultsBT occurred in 12 of 91 patients. No significant differences were observed between the groups of patients in terms of background factors but C-reactive protein levels on postoperative days 3 and 7 were significantly higher in the BT group than in the non-BT group (BT group vs. non-BT group [median], day 3: 6.64 mg/dL vs. 3.79 mg/dL, p = 0.0026; day 7: 5.10 mg/dL vs. 1.52 mg/dL, p = 0.0007). Additionally, the rate of postoperative adjuvant chemotherapy administration was significantly higher in the BT than in the non-BT group (BT group vs. non-BT group: 83.3% vs. 43.0%, p = 0.0123). The pathological findings from resected specimens showed that mucosal height was lower in the anal side than in the oral side.ConclusionAdjuvant chemotherapy may significantly increase the risk of BT after ileostomy closure following colorectal cancer surgery.

Introduction

Loop ileostomy plays a crucial role in reducing postoperative complications following colorectal cancer surgery [1, 2]. It is particularly valuable for rectal cancer surgery in patients at risk of anastomotic leakage, as it has been shown not to worsen this complication [3]. When the risk of anastomotic leakage is ruled out postoperatively, a loop ileostomy closure is performed. However, after closure, some patients develop a fever of unknown origin, even after ruling out common perioperative complications such as surgical site infections and anastomotic leakage. Clinically, fevers are often attributed to bacterial translocation (BT)—a phenomenon first described in 1979—which occurs when the intestinal barrier function is compromised by factors such as prolonged fasting, weakened intestinal mucosa, or both local and systemic immune suppression; in such cases, bacteria and related toxins pass through the intestinal epithelium and enter the bloodstream and lymphatic tissues [4, 5]. In patients who undergo a loop ileostomy, the distal portion of the colon remains in a prolonged fasting state, potentially increasing the risk of BT. However, not all patients who undergo loop ileostomy closure develop BT [6]. The aim of this study was to identify the risk factors for BT occurrence to optimise postoperative management.

Methods

Data source and study population

This retrospective study evaluated data from patients with colorectal cancer who underwent surgery at the Department of Surgery, Nagasaki University Hospital, Japan, between December 2015 and January 2024. Of the 1,970 patients registered in the Nagasaki University Colorectal Cancer Database during this period, 91 patients who underwent diverting stoma creation during primary colorectal cancer resection or emergency ileostomy creation for anastomotic leakage after colorectal cancer surgery were included.

The study was approved by the Institutional Review Board of Nagasaki University Hospital (Approval No. 19102141) and conducted in accordance with the principles of the Declaration of Helsinki. All patients were informed about the study and provided consent for data collection.

Surgical procedure

To prevent contamination of the surgical field, the oral and anal sides of the ileostomy outlet were temporarily closed with purse-string sutures using silk thread, and the operative field was disinfected using alcohol swabs and a povidone-iodine solution. A skin incision was made along the margin of the stoma, followed by dissection down to the rectus sheath. Special care was taken to minimise defects in the rectus sheath during entry into the abdominal cavity. Adhesiolysis and mobilisation were performed by dissecting along the ileostomy tract. Intestinal reconstruction was performed using a functional end-to-end anastomosis technique. Closure of the rectus sheath was performed using absorbable monofilament sutures containing antimicrobial agents. To prevent surgical site infection, the skin was partially closed using purse-string sutures.

Study design

BT lacks a definitive diagnostic method and is, therefore, typically diagnosed by exclusion. In clinical practice, this involves ruling out other potential causes of postoperative fever, such as anastomotic leakage, surgical site infection, complications related to general anaesthesia, pneumonia, atelectasis, and drug-induced fever. Herein, BT was defined as a body temperature exceeding 38 °C, where no clear source of infection could be identified after a comprehensive examination. The patients were divided into two groups: BT (n = 12; 13.2%) and non-BT (n = 79; 86.8%). Data collection and evaluation

We retrospectively extracted the following prospectively accumulated data from the colorectal cancer database at Nagasaki University: sex, age, history of oral steroid use, and history of type 2 diabetes (patient background). Additionally, we collected the following perioperative data: initial colorectal cancer diagnosis, initial surgical procedure, date of initial surgery, and date of loop ileostomy closure. Collected postoperative data included white blood cell counts and C-reactive protein (CRP) levels on postoperative days 1, 3, and 7 following ileostomy closure; blood culture results for febrile patients; administration of postoperative adjuvant chemotherapy; and the type, duration, and completion rate of chemotherapy. Pathological evaluation was conducted using specimens marked with sutures on the oral and anal sides. Measurements were performed on haematoxylin and eosin-stained specimens by a pathologist using whole slide imaging technology (Philips IntelliSite Pathology Solution, Ultrafast Scanner, Image Management System; Philips, Best, the Netherlands). We performed a pathological examination of the ileal sections resected during ileostomy closure in the last three cases and compared the mucosal height in the oral and anal sides of the ileum.

Statistical analysis

Continuous and categorical data are presented as medians (interquartile range [IQR]) and percentages, respectively. All statistical analyses were performed using JMP Pro 16 for Windows (Microsoft Corp., Redmond, WA, USA). Categorical variables were analysed using the chi-square or Fisher’s exact tests. Continuous variables were compared using the Mann–Whitney U test. Statistical significance was defined as p < 0.05. The Pearson’s chi-square test was used to compare categorical variables among the three preoperative treatment groups (no therapy, neoadjuvant chemotherapy, and neoadjuvant chemoradiotherapy).

Results

Patient background factors

In our patients with colorectal cancer, the tumour locations were as follows: one in the descending colon (1.1%), five in the sigmoid colon (5.5%), and 85 in the rectum (93.4%). Regarding preoperative nutritional status, the median total protein and serum albumin levels were 7.4 g/dL and 4.1 g/dL, respectively. Regarding medical history, 16 patients (17.8%) had diabetes mellitus and none had a history of oral steroid use. Emergency ileostomy owing to postoperative anastomotic leakage was required in 11 patients (12.1%). Preoperative treatment was administered to 22 patients (24.2%), including 8 (8.8%) who received neoadjuvant chemotherapy and 14 (15.4%) who received neoadjuvant chemoradiotherapy. The median waiting period for ileostomy closure was 170 days (Table 1).

Intraoperative and postoperative factors

The median operative time for primary tumour resection was 431 min, with a median blood loss of 50 mL. For stoma closure, the median operative time was 99 min, with a median blood loss of 25 mL. Blood test results showed that on postoperative days 1, 3, and 7, the white blood cell counts were 8.3 × 10⁹/L, 6.3 × 10⁹/L, and 5.7 × 10⁹/L, respectively, and the median CRP levels were 2.42 mg/dL, 4.45 mg/dL, and 1.62 mg/dL, respectively. Major complications (Clavien–Dindo grade ≥ III) occurred in four patients (4.4%). The complications included paralytic ileus requiring fluoroscopic intervention, endoscopic haemostasis for anastomotic bleeding, re-creation of a stoma due to a seminal vesicle fistula, and bleeding at the wound site. Postoperative paralytic ileus occurred in six patients (6.6%), and no cases of prolonged ileus were observed. The median time to first flatus and first bowel movement was two days. The median length of hospital stay after surgery was 9 days. The rate of postoperative chemotherapy administration following colorectal cancer surgery was 48.3% (Table 2).

Comparison between the BT and non-BT groups

No significant differences in the patient background data were observed between the groups. Additionally, no significant differences in the operative time for the first primary tumour resection, amount of bleeding, operative time for stoma closure, or the amount of bleeding during stoma closure were observed between the groups. Regarding postoperative blood test findings, no significant differences in the white blood cell counts were observed between the groups on postoperative days 1, 3, or 7. However, CRP levels on postoperative days 3 and 7 were significantly higher in the BT group than in the non-BT group. No significant intergroup differences were observed in the incidences of major complications (Clavien–Dindo grade ≥ III). There were also no significant differences between the two groups in the incidence of postoperative paralytic ileus or in the postoperative time to first flatus or first bowel movement. Moreover, the length of hospital stay after surgery was significantly longer in the BT group than in the non-BT group. The rate of introduction of postoperative adjuvant chemotherapy was significantly higher in the BT group (10 cases [83.3%]) than in the non-BT group (34 cases [43.0%]). However, no significant differences were observed between the groups in terms of the timing of chemotherapy initiation or completion rate (Table 3). Among the 12 cases, blood cultures were positive in two cases. Klebsiella aerogenes was detected in one case, representing bacteraemia. Cutibacterium acnes was detected in the second case but was judged to be due to contamination. Discussion

This study investigated the risk of BT following ileostomy closure after colorectal cancer surgery. Herein, we demonstrated that postoperative adjuvant chemotherapy was a risk factor for BT following ileostomy closure.

The concept of BT, first reported in 1979 by Berg and Owens [5], refers to the phenomenon in which intestinal bacteria and their products breach the gut barrier and migrate across the intestinal mucosa into the bloodstream or lymphatic system, eventually spreading throughout the body. Under normal conditions, the intestinal mucosa and its defence mechanisms prevent the translocation of bacteria, confining them within the intestinal lumen. However, various pathological conditions and factors, such as surgery, fasting, and chemotherapy, can impair gut barrier function and increase the likelihood of BT occurrence. When a diverting stoma is created after colon cancer surgery, the area on the anal side of the stoma is considered to be in an artificial state of fasting. In this state, the mucosa generally atrophies, and the tight junctions of the intestinal mucosa weaken [7–9]. Additionally, chemotherapy promotes intestinal mucosal damage; platinum drugs, in particular, strongly induce mucosal damage by promoting the expression of Toll-like receptor 4 [10]. Because most postoperative adjuvant chemotherapies used in colon cancer treatment are oxaliplatin-based, intestinal mucosal atrophy is likely to occur [11]. Herein, 66.7% of patients who developed BT received oxaliplatin-based postoperative adjuvant chemotherapy. Pathological examination of specimens resected during ileostomy closure showed no differences between the groups with regard to the submucosal and muscle layers. However, measurement of the mucosal height from the muscularis mucosae on the anal and oral sides revealed mucosal atrophy of approximately 20–70%. Early repair of atrophied mucosa is important to reduce the occurrence of BT. Glutamine is a conditionally essential amino acid that promotes the proliferation of intestinal epithelial cells and induces the expression of tight junction proteins, thereby regulating intestinal permeability. Oral nutritional supplements containing glutamine administered in the early postoperative period are hypothesised to be effective in preventing BT [12]. Several methods have been reported to be successful in preventing BT following ileostomy closure. One such method involves injecting probiotics or short-chain fatty acids into the distal bowel prior to ileostomy closure, which reduces the incidence of colitis observed during postoperative colonoscopy [13, 14]. These and our findings suggest that the administration of probiotics before ileostomy closure is beneficial for patients undergoing adjuvant chemotherapy following stoma creation. Further investigation of this approach is warranted to confirm its efficacy.

This study has several limitations. First, this study was limited by its retrospective design. Second, as this study was based on data from a small cohort at a single institution, further investigations across multiple institutions with larger case numbers are desirable. Third, the evolving definition of BT represents a limitation. The concept of BT has evolved significantly in recent years. Berg et al. were the first to propose diagnostic criteria for BT as follows: (1) no other source of infection can be identified; (2) positive cultures from mesenteric lymph nodes, blood (either portal or systemic), ascites, or organs (such as the liver or spleen) show bacterial species identical to those within the intestinal tract; and (3) bacteria are observed within the intestinal mucosa under microscopy [5]. However, meeting these criteria is nearly impossible in clinical practice. Garcia-Tsao et al. developed a mouse portal hypertension model and demonstrated that the rate of BT to mesenteric lymph nodes was 92% in the acute phase and 20% in the chronic phase, while no bacteria were isolated from blood during either phase [15]. Furthermore, Qiao et al. reported that the blood culture positivity rate in patients with BT undergoing elective abdominal surgery was 4.8%, indicating the extremely low sensitivity of blood cultures in detecting BT [16]. MacFie et al. examined 927 patients who underwent laparotomy and found that BT to mesenteric lymph nodes was observed in 14.0% of patients, among whom 42.3% subsequently developed postoperative sepsis. These findings suggest that, even in the absence of positive blood cultures, clinical signs such as fever and systemic inflammatory response syndrome may serve as important indicators of BT [17]. Recently, the concept of BT has expanded to include not only the effects of live bacteria but also systemic inflammation mediated via gut immunity by various bacterial DNA fragments, damage-associated molecular patterns, and other mediators. This broader view makes a definitive diagnosis challenging, often necessitating an exclusionary approach based on patient vital signs in clinical settings [18–20]. Therefore, we defined BT as the presence of fever exceeding 38 °C, after excluding potential sources of infection, such as surgical site infection and anastomotic leakage. However, in this study, BT diagnosis was based on the presence of fever, and it was not possible to completely exclude fever attributable to cytokine release in the early postoperative period. In fact, 5 of the 12 patients (41.7%) with BT developed fever on postoperative days 1–2 (Fig. 3). A comparison of the inflammatory profiles between patients who developed fever before postoperative day 3 and those who developed fever on or after postoperative day 3 revealed that the postoperative day 1 C-reactive protein level was significantly higher in the group with fever before postoperative day 3, suggesting that surgery-related inflammatory cytokines may have been involved in the development of fever (Table 4). To address the potential confounding effect of cytokine-mediated fever in the early postoperative period, we conducted an additional analysis limited to the seven patients who developed fever on or after postoperative day 3. This subset analysis not only confirmed the higher proportion of patients receiving postoperative adjuvant chemotherapy in the BT group, consistent with the main analysis, but also revealed a significantly longer interval until stoma closure (Table 5). These findings, obtained after minimizing the potential bias of early postoperative inflammatory responses, strengthen the robustness of our primary conclusion by demonstrating that the association between adjuvant chemotherapy and BT remained significant, even after minimizing the influence of early postoperative inflammatory responses. Further, they suggest that prolonged mucosal atrophy induced by both cytotoxic chemotherapy and fasting may play an important role in the pathogenesis of BT. Nevertheless, in the absence of reliable biomarkers for BT, it remains impossible to completely determine the cause of fever; hence, some uncertainty persists in diagnosing BT and interpreting its causal relationship. Moreover, adjuvant chemotherapy inevitably prolongs the interval until ileostomy closure, making it challenging to determine which factor exerts a stronger influence on BT development. Nevertheless, our findings show that either condition, or their synergistic effect, contributes to mucosal atrophy, and further investigation is warranted to elucidate these mechanisms. Fourth, the influence of chemotherapy should be assessed not only in terms of mucosal atrophy but also from nutritional and immunological perspectives. Chemotherapy may reduce dietary intake and cause haematological toxicities such as cytopenia, potentially leading to malnutrition and immunosuppression. These conditions themselves may contribute to the development of BT. In this study, however, comparisons of nutritional and immunological parameters before and after chemotherapy within the BT group revealed no significant differences (Table 6).
