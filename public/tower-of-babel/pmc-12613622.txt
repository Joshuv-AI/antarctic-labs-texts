Global burden and trends of common head and neck cancers between 1990 and 2021: findings from the Global Burden of Disease Study 2021
Authors: Jianli Lin, Bingqin Xie, Xuehan Yi, Shanshan Wu, Yanmei Ji, Enling Xu, Qiuping Zhuo, Guohui Li, Rongrong Fu, Jing Wang, Baochang He, Fa Chen
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundThe burden of head and neck cancers (HNC) is a significant global health challenge, necessitating a comprehensive analysis of incidence and mortality trends. This study aimed to estimate the lastest disease burden and change trends of HNC in 2021 and examine trends from 1990 to 2021 using the Global Burden of Disease (GBD) Study 2021.MethodsWe comprehensively focused on all HNC in GBD, including lip and oral cavity cancer (LOC), nasopharynx cancer (NPC), other pharynx cancer (OPC), and larynx cancer (LC), estimating incidence, prevalence, disability-adjusted life years (DALYs), and mortality rates, including age-standardized rates (ASR) and 95% uncertainty intervals (UIs) on global, regional, and national levels.ResultsOur results revealed that, in 2021, there were approximately 0.91 million new cases of HNC globally, representing significant increases from 1990, with specific increases in LOC (142.18%), NPC (55.89%), OPC (163.17%), and LC (60.48%). Additionally, around 0.5 million deaths were attributed to HNC. Notably, age-standardized incidence of nasopharyngeal cancer is increasing from 1990 to 2019, but decreasing from 2019 to 2021. Age-standardized mortality for LOC have declined among men but are rising among women between 1990 and 2021 (male: -6.61%, female: 7.95%). The burden of these HNC predominantly affected middle-aged and older populations. As the socio-demographic index rises, the incidence of LOC and OPC displayed a J-shaped relationship, but nasopharyngeal cancer, revealed a downward trend.ConclusionsThe global burden of HNC grew steadily from 1990 to 2021, the variation in incidence and mortality rates across regions highlights the need for tailored prevention and treatment strategies to address the changing HNC landscape.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12903-025-07140-6.Keywords: Global burden of disease, Head and neck cancer, Incidence, Mortality, Trends

Background

Despite advancements in medical technology, the diagnosis and treatment of head and neck cancers (HNC) impose substantial burdens on healthcare professionals. HNC encompasses a range of carcinomas in the upper gastrointestinal tract, primarily affecting the oral cavity, pharynx, larynx, and sinus cavities [1, 2]. These cancers are classified into subtypes: lip and oral cavity cancer (LOC), nasopharyngeal cancer (NPC), other pharyngeal cancer (OPC), and laryngeal cancer (LC). Tobacco [3] and alcohol [4] consumption are well-established risk factors, while genetic predispositions [5], human papillomavirus (HPV) infection [6], and betel quid chewing [7] also contribute to HNC development. Initial symptoms, including oral ulcers and persistent hoarseness [8], can mimic other conditions, leading to diagnostic delays and increased patient anxiety. Consequently, incidence and prevalence rates for HNC have not significantly declined, maintaining a persistent disease burden.

The Global Burden of Diseases (GBD) database is an essential resource for understanding the prevalence and burden of diseases worldwide [9]. In 2020, approximately 20 million new cancer cases and 10 million cancer-related deaths were reported [10]. Although the disease burden of HNC does not rank among the top five cancer subcategories, its impact on public health is considerable. The GBD study from 2019 revealed notable global variations in HNC burden and trends [11], identifying 370,000 new cases and 199,000 deaths from LOC, alongside 167,000 new OPC cases and 114,000 deaths [12]. For NPC, 176,501 incident cases were recorded, resulting in 71,610 fatalities [13], while there were 209,149 cases of LC with approximately 123,356 deaths [14]. These data indicates an increasing trend in HNC incidence. A comprehensive review from 1990 to 2019 showed a rise in age-standardized incidence rates (ASIR) for HNC, even as age-standardized death rates (ASDR) and age-standardized mortality rates (ASMR) declined [11]. Given recent diagnostic advancements [2] and the effects of the COVID-19 pandemic [15, 16], an assessment of HNC trends from 2019 to 2021 is imperative, yet few systematic GBD studies [17–19] have analyzed the disease burden and epidemiological trends of major HNC subtypes from 1990 to 2021.

This study aimed to leverage the GBD 2021 database to assess the global disease burden of LOC, NPC, OPC, and LC. The analyses encompass incident cases, prevalent cases, disability-adjusted life years (DALYs), and deaths while also calculating age-standardized rates of incidence, prevalence, DALYs, and mortality from 1990 to 2021, and further stratified by global region, including 21 GBD regions and 204 countries and territories, as well as by sex, age, and socio-demographic index (SDI). The goal is to provide a thorough overview for researchers and offer essential insights for policymakers, facilitating enhanced global prevention and treatment strategies for head and neck malignancies.

Methods

Overview

The GBD 2021 utilized 328,938 data sources to estimate incidence, prevalence, DALYs, and mortality rates for 371 diseases and injuries across various demographics, including age, sex, 21 regions, 204 countries and territories, and socioeconomic groups [20]. Each GBD iteration supersedes previous estimates, ensuring that updated data and methodologies are consistently applied across the time series. For GBD 2021, data on head and neck cancer estimates were sourced from administrative records, clinical trials, disease registries, surveys, and vital statistics, available through scientific journals, reports, online databases, and other resources. This study, drawing on data from the Institute for Health Metrics and Evaluation (IHME), extracts global statistics on incidence, prevalence, DALYs, and mortality rates for common head and neck cancers—including LOC, NPC, OPC, and LC—by sex and SDI, within the 0–95 age range from 1990 to 2021, accessible via the Global Health Data Exchange (GHDx) platform. Data acquisition, and analytical methods for the the 2021 GBD study are detailed in prior literature [21],the specific flowchart can refer to Figure S1. To avoid data duplication, all data has been meticulously sourced from reputable public databases following thorough screening to ensure quality assurance [22]. The University of Washington's Institutional Review Board had reviewed and approved the GBD study, and this study was exempt from informed consent and ethical review due to the use of an anonymized, publicly available database.

Definition

In the GBD 2021, lip and oral cancers were identified as malignant neoplasms of the lips and oral cavity, classified under ICD-10 codes, C00-C08.9, D10.0-D10.5, and D11-D11.9, as well as ICD-9 codes 140–145.9 and 210.0–210.6. Nasopharyngeal cancer was defined using ICD-10 codes C11-C11.9 and D10.6, and ICD-9 codes 147–147.9 and 210.7–210.9. Other pharynx cancers (excluding nasopharyngeal) were categorized under ICD-10 codes C09-C10.9 and C12-C13.9, and ICD-9 codes 146–146.9 and 148–148.9. Laryngeal cancer was designated by ICD-10 codes C32-C32.9 and D02.0, along with ICD-9 codes 161–161.9, 212.1, and 231.0. This study presents comprehensive estimates of head and neck cancers from 1990 to 2021, stratified by age, sex, and SDI. Age was categorized in five-year intervals (≤ 5, 6–9, 10–14, 15–19, … ≥ 95 years), while gender classifications included male, female, and both sexes. The SDI serves as an aggregate measure reflecting a country's development status [21], incorporating per capita income, educational attainment, and fertility rankings, with values ranging from 0 to 1.

Incidence, prevalence, DALYs and deaths estimates

In the GBD 2021, incidence and prevalence were modeled using DisMod-MR 2.1, a Bayesian meta-regression tool that provides consistent estimates of prevalence, incidence, remission, and mortality by sex, location, year, and age group. In some countries, though cancer incidence data were available, mortality data lacked comprehensive collection by population registries. To enhance the data for mortality modeling, incidence data were converted to mortality estimates utilizing modeled mortality-incidence ratios (MIRs), derived from cancer registry data corresponding to the same year [12]. Morbidity estimates were calculated by dividing the final mortality figures by the relevant MIRs [12]. DALYs capture years of healthy life lost due to premature death and disability, calculated as the sum of years of life lost (YLLs) and years lived with disability life (YLDs) [21].

Most estimates of causes of death from diseases and injuries were generated using the Cause of Death Envelopment Model (CODEm), which employed an ensemble of statistical models and systematically assessed various covariate combinations for predictive validity. This approach enabled the estimation of mortality by location, age, sex, and year for specific cause [15]. Cause-specific mortality estimates from the GBD 2021, standardized by age demographics, were reported as deaths per 100,000 population. To assess temporal changes, we calculated the percentage change from 1990 to 2021 and the annualized rate of change, determined by the difference between the natural logarithms of the values at the beginning and end of the interval divided by the number of years encompassed.

Statistical analysis

In this study, estimates are presented as absolute numbers and age-standardized rates per 100,000 population. The age standardization rate (ASR) was calculated using the GBD global standard population [23]. The latest GBD 2021 analysis reduced the number of calculations from 1,000 to 500, as simulations indicated that this reduction did not impact the final estimates or their uncertainties. Each calculation was performed 500 times to obtain sample-level estimates, with the final estimates derived from the average of these samples. The 95% Uncertainty Intervals (UIs) correspond to the 2.5th and 97.5th percentiles of the sample. Uncertainty was accounted for at each stage of the estimation process [15]. Count and percentage data in tables were rounded to two decimal places, and all analyses were conducted using R software (version 4.3.3) and GraphPad Prism 8.

Results

Global burden and trend of LOC

In 2021, global cases of LOC reached 421,580 (95% UI, 389,880–449,780), with deaths at 208,380 (95% UI, 191,290–224,160). This represents a staggering increase of 142.18% (95% UI, 120.08%−159.79%) and 113.94% (95% UI, 91.02%−132.72%) since 1990 (Figure S2 clearly shows that the incidence and deaths of LOC in 2021 increased among the four types of HNC). The age-standardized incidence rate (ASIR) and mortality rate (ASMR) for LOC in 2021 were 4.88 (95% UI, 4.52–5.20) and 2.42 (95% UI, 2.23–2.60) per 100,000, reflecting changes of 14.29% (95% UI, 3.97%−22.48%) and −1.19% (95% UI, −11.58 to 7.30) from 1990. The corresponding EAPC for each revealed the same trend (Table 1). Overall, while the crude ASIR is on the rise, the ASMR shows little significant change (Figure S3A-D).

Global burden and trend of NPC

In 2021, there were an estimated 118,880 global cases of NPC, marking a 55.89% increase since 1990. Correspondingly, NPC-related deaths numbered approximately 75,360 in 2021, reflecting a 16.06% rise over the same period (Figure S2 provides a more visual representation of this change in NPCs). Notably, the age-standardized incidence rate (ASIR) of NPC decreased from 1.74 per 100,000 in 1990 to 1.38 in 2021, while the age-standardized mortality rate (ASMR) declined from 1.52 to 0.87 per 100,000. The EAPC also showed similar trend results (Table 1). Over the period from 1990 to 2021, the age-standardized prevalence rate (ASPR) increased, while ASIR, age-standardized disability rate (ASDR), and ASMR experienced a downturn (Figure S3A-D).

In terms of regional variations, East Asia reported the highest ASIR of NPC at 3.41 per 100,000 in 2021, whereas Andean Latin America had the lowest at 0.13 per 100,000 (Table S1, Figure S4A). Southeast Asia exhibited the highest ASMR at 1.60 per 100,000, compared to Andean Latin America's lowest rate of 0.13 per 100,000 (Table S2, Figure S4D). Among the 204 countries and territories in the GBD study, Ghana registered the lowest ASIR and ASMR (Fig. 1B), both at 0.01 per 100,000 (Table S1-S2), while Malaysia showed the highest ASIR at 6.09 and ASMR at 4.76 per 100,000 (Fig. 1F, Table S1-S2). From 1990 to 2021, the overall age-standardized incidence and mortality rates of NPC exhibited a slight decline in conjunction with rising SDI across the 21 regions analyzed (Fig. 2B and 3B, Table S3).

Sex-specific trends indicated similar decreases in age-standardized incidence, with a decline of 14.88% for males and 32.82% for females from 1990 to 2021(Fig. 4A). The percentage change in age-standardized deaths fell by 39.42% for males and 49.65% for females during the same timeframe (Fig. 4D). In 2021, the highest absolute number of new cases was observed in the 50–54 age group, after which cases slightly declined (Fig. 5A). Conversely, mortality rates initially increased, peaking in the 55–59 age group before declining (Fig. 5D). Additional information regarding the prevalence and DALYs of NPC can be found in the supplementary materials (Tables S4-5, Figures S5-8).

Global burden and trend of OPC

In 2021, the global incidence of OPC reached 169,820 cases (95% UI, 159,850–179,700), marking a 163.17% increase since 1990 (95% UI, 142.89%−183.87%). Deaths attributed to OPC in the same year were estimated at 98,440 (95% UI, 91,570–105,480), demonstrating a 121.98% rise from 1990 (95% UI, 98.77%−145.20%). Figure S2 indicates an increase in the proportion of OPCs within HNC. The age-standardized incidence rate (ASIR) for OPC stood at 1.93 per 100,000 (95% UI, 1.82–2.05), reflecting a 24.60% increase (95% UI, 15.09%−34.30%) compared to 1990. The age-standardized mortality rate (ASMR) was recorded at 1.13 per 100,000 (95% UI, 1.05–1.21), showing a slight variation of 4.13% (95% UI, −6.67%−14.85%) since 1990. The EAPC from 1990 to 2021 were 0.68 (95% UI, 0.60, 0.75) for ASIR, and 0.17 (95% UI: 0.12, 0.23) for ASMR, respectively (Table 1). Over the same period, ASIR and ASMR both rose slightly (Figure S3A-D).

Among the 21 GBD regions, Central Europe reported the highest ASIR for OPC at 3.94 per 100,000 (95% UI, 3.57–4.34), while Oceania exhibited the lowest at 0.15 per 100,000 (95% UI, 0.12–0.19) in 2021 (Table S1, Figure S4A). South Asia had the highest ASMR for other pharynx cancers at 3.23 per 100,000 (95% UI, 2.84–3.62), with Oceania again at the lower end at 0.14 per 100,000 (95% UI, 0.11–0.17) (Table S2, Figure S4D). Among 204 countries and territories, Ghana reported the lowest ASIR [0.02 (95% UI, 0.01–0.02)] and ASMR [0.01 (95% UI, 0.01–0.02)] for OPC in 2021, while Hungary [6.19 (95% UI, 5.01–7.39)] and India [3.30 (95% UI, 2.87–3.74)] had the highest rates (Fig. 1C, 1G; Table S1-2). From 1990 to 2021, the global trend in age-standardized incidence rates of OPC exhibited a 'J' shape, with a decline followed by an increase as SDI rose, reaching its lowest point mid-way (Fig. 2C). In contrast, the overall ASMR for other pharynx cancers showed little fluctuation despite rising SDI across the 21 regions (Fig. 3C, Table S3).

From 1990 to 2021, the age-standardized incidence rates of OPC rose by 29.42% in females and 23.34% in males (Fig. 4A). A similar trend was observed in mortality: age-standardized deaths increased by 3.86% in males and 3.20% in females during the same period (Fig. 4D). Globally, OPC incidence cases were absent before the 20–24 age group, subsequently peaking in the 60–64 age group by 2021 (Fig. 5A). Conversely, the number of death cases from OPC initially increased but declined after the 60–64 age group in 2021 (Fig. 5D). Data on OPC prevalence and DALYs are provided in the supplementary materials (Tables S4-5, Figures S5-8).

Global burden and trend of LC

In 2021, the global incidence of LC reached 200,800 cases (95% UI, 186,940–216,100), marking a 60.48% increase (95% UI, 48.46%−74.30%) since 1990. The global mortality from LC was 117,250 deaths (95% UI, 109,350–125,950) in 2021, which represents a 36.67% increase (95% UI, 24.52%−48.70%) compared to 2019 (Table 1). Figure S2 visually illustrates the reduction in the proportion of LC within the disease burden of HNC. The ASIR of LC globally was 2.29 (95% UI, 2.13–2.47) per 100,000 in 2021, reflecting a decline of 25.34% (95% UI, −30.86% to −19.00%) since 1990. Likewise, the ASMR decreased by 37.09% (95% UI, −42.62% to −31.64%), with a rate of 1.35 (95% UI, 1.26–1.45) per 100,000 during the same period. The EAPC of LC from 1990 to 2021 were − 1.09 (95% UI: − 1.16, − 1.01) for ASIR, and − 1.39 (95% UI: − 1.48, − 1.30) for ASMR (Table 1). Overall, the crude ASIR, ASPR, ASDR, and ASMR exhibited a stable trend from 1990 to 2021 (Figure S3A-D).

Regionally in 2021, the highest age-standardized incidence rate of LC was found in Central Europe [4.46 (95% UI, 4.07–4.87) per 100,000], while the lowest was in Oceania [0.56 (95% UI, 0.44–0.74) per 100,000] (Table S1, Figure S4A). The Caribbean recorded the highest ASMR at [2.69 (95% UI, 2.33–3.13) per 100,000], contrasting with the lowest ASMR in the High-income Asia Pacific region [0.31 (95% UI, 0.27–0.35) per 100,000] (Table S4, Figure S6D). Nationally, Monaco had the highest ASIR [10.44 (95% UI, 7.80–14.22) per 100,000], while Kiribati reported the lowest ASIR [0.23 (95% UI, 0.16–0.32) per 100,000] (Fig. 1D, Table S1). In terms of mortality, Kiribati exhibited the lowest ASMR at [0.22 (95% UI, 0.16–0.30) per 100,000], whereas Montenegro had the highest ASMR at [4.67 (95% UI, 3.61–6.10) per 100,000] (Fig. 1H, Table S2). Between 1990 and 2021, the general ASIR of LC increased slightly alongside rising SDI across 21 GBD regions (Fig. 2D, Table S3). The ASMR exhibited a similar trend, peaking in the mid-SDI range before declining as SDI increased (Fig. 3D, Table S3).

From 1990 to 2021, the change in age-standardized incidence of LC was −28.27% in males and −10.86% in females (Fig. 4A). Correspondingly, age-standardized deaths changed by −39.68% in males and −25.86% in females over the same period (Fig. 4D). The absolute numbers of incident and mortality cases increased with age, peaking in the 65–69 age group for both genders in 2021 (Fig. 5A-D). Overall, laryngeal cancer cases remained lower than those of lip and oral cavity cancers across all age groups. Comprehensive details regarding the prevalence and DALYs for LC are presented in the supplementary materials (Tables S4-5, Figures S5-8).

Discussion

This study represents the first comprehensive analysis of the global incidence, prevalence, DALYs, and mortality associated with LOC, NPC, OPC, and LC from 1990 to 2021, based on the GBD 2021 study. Our findings indicate a significant increase in new cases and deaths due to LOC, NPC, OPC, and LC in 2021 compared to 1990, with LOC showing the highest incidence and mortality. By 2021, lip and oral cancers had emerged as the most burdensome, with cases more than doubling since 1990, followed by LC, OPC, and NPC. This ranking aligns with previous GBD studies from 2017 [24] and 2019 [25]. Notably, OPC cases and deaths demonstrated the fastest growth over these decades, potentially linked to rising HPV infections, which are a major risk factor; it is estimated that over 70% of oropharyngeal cancers in Europe and the United States are attributed to HPV [26]. Furthermore, the detection rates of HPV in oropharyngeal cancers are increasing [27], and projections suggest that HPV-associated oropharyngeal cancers may soon outnumber cervical cancers in Europe [28]. In contrast, nasopharyngeal cancer has exhibited the slowest rise among the four cancers, with a modest 5.43% increase in DALYs since 1990.

The global disease burden of head and neck cancers is complex and exhibits significant regional variability. The GBD 2021 study reports ASR and temporal trends for head and neck cancers from 1990 to 2021 across 21 regions and 204 countries. Our analysis reveals that the highest ASIR and ASDR primarily occur in Europe and Asia, correlated with specific regional habits and environmental factors. For instance, betel chewing among Southeast Asians is associated with elevated oral cancer rates, irrespective of smoking status [7]. In contrast, outdoor occupations such as fishing [29], farming [30], and sailing [31] in Europe and Australia are linked to increased lip cancer rates due to solar radiation exposure [32, 33]. Despite improvements in global economic and medical standards since 1990, disparities in cancer diagnosis and treatment technology persist across nations. Notably, Palau, Malaysia, Hungary, and Monaco report the highest ASIR and ASMR, while the lowest rates are seen in Ghana, Sao Tome and Principe, Kiribati, Samoa, and Guam—countries marked by low income [34], limited cancer surveillance, and underdeveloped healthcare systems [35, 36].

The SDI, a composite measure of education, economic status, and fertility, is employed in GBD research to analyze disease trends relative to development. Our findings indicate that as SDI increases, age-standardized incidence and mortality rates for head and neck cancers show variable trends. Notably, while NPC incidence declines with rising SDI, incidences of other cancers increase. Conversely, age-standardized mortality rates for all four cancers decrease with SDI, except for OPC. This trend may reflect enhanced research and implementation of effective viral vaccines in more developed regions, which help mitigate NPC's impact. Higher standards of living are often associated with increased exposure to common risk factors like alcohol and tobacco, contributing to higher cancer incidence rates. Furthermore, advancements in cancer detection technologies alongside greater public awareness in developed nations likely lead to improved cancer prognosis and increased survival rates [12–14].

The study indicates significant gender diversity in the age-standardized rates of lip and oral cavity cancer, nasopharyngeal cancer, other pharyngeal cancers, and laryngeal cancer from 1990 to 2021. While the burden of oral cancer has risen steadily among females, reflected in increasing age-standardized incidence and prevalence rates, males have experienced a decline in ASDR and ASMR for oral cancer. Additionally, the rise in alcohol consumption during the menopausal years (ages 40–49) has been linked to changes in sex hormone levels in women [37], affecting oral cancer development and treatment [38]. Previous research [39] suggests that the burden of oral cancer among women is quietly increasing relative to men and may eventually surpass it. Although the global burden of nasopharyngeal cancer has decreased in both genders since 1990, the decrease in ASIR and ASMR has been less pronounced in men, indicating a higher global burden in this group. This highlights a need for improved prevention and treatment awareness among men. Evidence [40] also suggests that premenopausal and menopausal women have better prognoses for nasopharyngeal cancer than men, contributing to the lower burden in females. As of 2021, the age-standardized incidence and mortality rates of oropharyngeal cancer exceeded those of 1990 for both sexes, reflecting a similar disease burden across genders. From 1990 to 2021, the global burden of laryngeal cancer decreased significantly in both men and women, with a sharper decline in men. Research [41] indicates that female laryngeal cancer patients have superior prognoses, contributing to the negligible decrease in prevalence and mortality rates among women relative to men.

This study offers researchers in related fields a comprehensive overview of lip and oral cavity, pharyngeal, and laryngeal cancers, providing a contemporary scientific foundation for prevention and treatment strategies. However, our study has limitations. Primarily, it only offers a broad characterization of the four head and neck cancers without delving into the specific impacts of associated risk factors, such as tobacco use and alcohol consumption. Additionally, the analysis is constrained by the GBD database, which focuses on just four cancer sites, limiting its comprehensiveness. A more detailed classification, including tonsillar cancers and a breakdown of oral cancers into subtypes like tongue, buccal, and gingival cancers, could enhance research efforts and facilitate deeper investigations into the characteristics of head and neck cancers. Moreover, while the GBD 2021 study aims to provide a comprehensive overview of global cancers, it is hampered by regional disparities in economic and medical development, resulting in incomplete cancer data. Consequently, this study serves as a broad overview rather than a precise cancer mapping. The global COVID-19 pandemic from 2019 to 2021 further complicated disease surveillance and prevention efforts, introducing biases in data collection that must be acknowledged. Lastly, this study is descriptive and exhibits cross-sectional bias, lacking confirmation of causal relationships between head and neck cancer and variables such as year, sex, age, geography, and risk factors. Future research should focus on detailed cohort studies or experimental designs to establish these associations more effectively.

Conclusion

In conclusion, our study indicates that the global burden of head and neck cancer has steadily increased from 1990 to 2021, particularly among middle-aged and older populations, despite regional and national variations in incidence and mortality rates. This trend is also reflected in rising SDI. Notably, significant differences in disease burden trends were observed between gender subgroups, suggesting that hormonal changes may have an influence, in addition to other factors, on the development of these cancers. Our findings underscore the persistent and substantial burden of head and neck cancer over the past three decades and highlight the ongoing challenges in prevention and treatment. To mitigate this pressing issue, public health strategies should prioritize evidence-based interventions, including tobacco cessation programs, alcohol reduction initiatives, HPV vaccination campaigns (particularly for oropharyngeal cancers), promotion of diets rich in fruits and vegetables to address nutritional deficiencies, routine oral health screenings, and early detection protocols for high-risk groups such as smokers and heavy drinkers. Future research should focus on understanding regional disparities and developing culturally appropriate, cost-effective prevention strategies tailored to specific populations and socioeconomic contexts.
