Protocol for mapping RNA G-quadruplex for chromatin-bound RNA using d-rG4-seq
Authors: Yong Woo Lee, Vered Levy, Jeannie T Lee
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
SummaryHere, we present a protocol for using d-rG4-seq, a technique for mapping RNA G-quadruplex (rG4) for chromatin-bound RNA. We describe steps for identifying in vivo rG4 structures based on differential sensitivity of rG4 to dimethyl sulfate (DMS) modification, folding in the presence of monovalent cations, K+ versus Li+, and reverse transcriptase (RT) readthrough when folded. We then detail procedures for isolating RNA from the chromatin-bound fractions to enrich for epigenetic regulators and comparing in vitro versus in vivo profiles.For complete details on the use and execution of this protocol, please refer to Lee et al.1Subject areas: Genomics, Molecular Biology, Gene Expression

Before you begin

This protocol describes a method for identifying RNA G-quadruplex (rG4) structures inside cells, as originally described for our analysis of Xist RNA.1 The method incorporates previously established protocols to identify RNA duplexes2 and RNA G-quadruplexes.3,4,5,6 We purify chromatin-associated RNAs from ES cells to enrich for epigenetic regulators (Figure 1A). For in vitro analysis, we do not include a DMS treatment step (-DMS, Figure 1A). We fragment the RNA, ligate adaptors, and denature the RNA to eliminate secondary structures. We then renature the RNA in the presence of either monovalent cation K+ (which enables rG4 to form stably) or Li+ (which does not stabilize rG4). We next reverse-transcribe the RNA using Superscript III RT, perform size-selection, and clone the products for sequencing. In principle, rG4s that reform in vitro following denaturation would occur only in K+, but not Li+. Because RT cannot process past a folded rG4, we can infer the formation of rG4 structures by observing a chain-termination event (“RT-Stop”) following sequencing.3 For in vivo analysis, we modify the pipeline by first treating cells with DMS in vivo to capture native RNA conformation (+DMS, Figure 1A). We then denature and renature the RNA in the presence of K+ versus Li+ prior to RT-primer extension analysis — performed as for in vitro analysis.

The d-rG4-seq pipeline

(A) d-rG4-seq procedure summary. In vitro analysis does not utilize the DMS treatment step (-DMS). The in vivo analysis begins with DMS treatment to capture in-cell RNA conformations (+DMS). All other steps are the same for in vitro and in vivo analyses. Chromatin-bound fraction is isolated to enrich the non-coding RNAs involved in the epigenetic regulations. RNAs are further selected with PolyA+ selection and heat fragmented. Size selection is performed to obtain RNA methylation-dependent RT-stop products. Adaptor-ligated RNA is initially size-selected by gel elution, targeting lengths between 85 and 105 nucleotides. During reverse transcription, RT-stops occur in the rG4 folding regions and DMS-modified adenine and cytosine nucleotides. RT-stop products are isolated by size selection of cDNA fragments shorter than full-length products (75–105 nt). Additional RT-stop product enrichment is performed by excising the PCR products in the 78–105 nt length range.

(B) RNA and cDNA fragments in the indicated length are selected in 8% denaturing and native gels. SYBR gold staining is used to visualize the nucleic acids. DMS-treated (DMS+) and non-treated (DMS-) RNA samples are reverse transcribed with K+ and Li+ containing buffers.

DMS can methylate 3 of 4 nucleosides in RNA: Adenosine (A) at the N1 position, cytosine (C) at the N3 position, and guanine at the N7 position. When RNA is in rG4 conformation, N7-G is protected from DMS because of its involvement in G:G hydrogen bonding within the tetrad, whereas N1-A and N3-C are not protected because they are not involved in Hoogsteen base-pairing. Thus, following a round of denaturation and renaturation, if the G-rich region did not fold into rG4 after denaturation, we infer that the N7-G was DMS-methylated in cells and therefore did not adopt an rG4 conformation in vivo. If a G-rich region could fold into rG4 following denaturation, the N7-G must have been protected from DMS in vivo and we could therefore infer the occurrence of an rG4 in vivo. In this case, the rG4 would refold following denaturation in the presence of K+ and create RT-stops. Therefore, the presence of DMS-resistant K+-dependent RT-stops peaks within a G-tract indicates a folded rG4 in cells. In this way, in vitro and in vivo rG4 profiles can be surveyed through differential RNA sensitivities to DMS within G-tracts and differential renaturation under K+ versus Li+ conditions. To increase detection sensitivity of this procedure, we enrich RT-stop products using a size-based isolation approach2,3 (Figure 1B). The enriched RT-stop cDNA libraries are then deeply sequenced using the Illumina platform. We use rG4Seeker and StructureFold software to analyze RNA structure from in vivo DMS modification profiles. This protocol focuses on the d-rG4-seq method and provides guidance on the initial in silico analysis.

CRITICAL: DMS is a highly toxic compound that is volatile to the air. To minimize exposure, DMS treatments are done in a chemical fume hood. Consult with the institute safety manager before starting.

DMS treatment to cultured cells

Timing: 2–3 h

DMS treatment converts RNA structural features to the forms of RNA base methylations. The covalently attached RNA methylation patterns persist during RNA purification and fragmentation under denaturing conditions (Figure 1A).

1.Grow 8 × 107 mouse embryonic stem cells (mES) per experimental condition.

Grow 8 × 107 mouse embryonic stem cells (mES) per experimental condition.

Isolation of chromatin-bound fraction

Timing: 6–8 h

Reverse transcription (RT)-Stop library preparation

Timing: 4 days

G-quadruplex structure formation is K+ dependent and causes reverse transcriptase to stall (RT-stop) at the 3′ end of the rG4 motifs. Therefore, frequent RT-stop sites in the presence of K+ can be interpreted as signatures of rG4 structure formation. In control reactions, Li+ is used to balance the ion concentration instead of K+. To create a comprehensive map of global RNA structure patterns, RNA samples are prepared from DMS-treated and untreated cells and then subjected to reverse transcription in K+ and Li+ buffers. This combination of sample conditions enables the study of both rG4 and RNA duplex structures.

Expected outcomes

A noise-to-signal estimate can be obtained by examining the base composition of the nucleotide triplet upstream of strong RT-stops (Figure 3A). RT-stops in K+ samples tend to occur just upstream of consecutive Guanines. Notably, this G-enrichment is observed under K+ folding but is not under Li+ folding (Figure 3A) — an indication that the procedure likely succeeded. In our previous analysis, we observed 69% Gs in the K+ sample and 33% Gs in the Li+ control. We also note that, relative to non-treated samples, base compositions at the RT-stop in DMS-treated samples occur predominantly at As and Cs, consistent with the sensitivity of As and Cs to DMS modification (Figure 3B). — a further indication of procedural success.

Examples of d-rG4-seq outcomes are illustrated in Figure 4. K+-dependent strong RT-stop sites are readily observable at the 3′ ends of rG4 motifs (Figure 4A). These in vitro rG4s are widespread across the transcriptome, including many long non-coding RNAs like Xist, Malat1, and Neat1.6 By providing properly generated K+ and Li+ RT-stop libraries, rG4Seeker identifies thousands of in vitro rG4 sites from non-DMS (in vitro) treated samples. Only a small percentage (1%–3%) of these in vitro rG4 sites show specific occurrences of DMS-resistant in vivo rG4 folding sites. For example, K+ dependent RT-stops are significantly protected from DMS modification at one of the rG4 sites in Ddx17 RNA (Figure 4B, upper panel). However, most K+ dependent RT-stop sites are eliminated following DMS treatment. In the case of unfolded rG4 motifs in Pdrg1, three K+ dependent RT-stop sites are completely lost following DMS treatment (Figure 4B, lower panel). In sum, our d-rG4-seq protocol identifies stably folded in vivo rG4 sites with single-nucleotide resolution. A significant increase in sensitivity achieved through subcellular fractionation allows us to detect low-abundance transcripts, including many long non-coding RNAs. This accurate determination of in vivo RNA structures would be crucial for understanding the complex roles of non-coding RNAs associated with epigenetic processes and disease states.

Quantification and statistical analysis

qPCR analysis was conducted using the CFX96 Touch Real-Time PCR Detection System (Bio-Rad). Native and denaturing acrylamide gel images were visualized with the Amersham Imager 600 (GE Healthcare). RT-stop frequency from the sequencing data was quantified and analyzed using rG4Seeker and StructureFold. Statistical tests of in vitro rG4 sites were performed using a noise-adjusted binomial model of rG4Seeker and the DMS sensitivity of rG4 sites was tested with the BINOM.DIST function of Microsoft Excel.

For more in-depth analysis of base-pair RNA structure assays with DMS modification patterns, please refer to the StructureFold manual.15

Limitations

d-rG4-seq is a population-based epitranscriptomic assay and may underestimate the number of folded rG4s. Our study showed that >5% of the 5000–6000 in vitro rG4’s are detected in vivo, but we believe that additional rG4’s may fold too transiently to be detected in a bulk RNA-seq analysis. It is possible that many, if not all, of the thousands of rG4 motifs may fold in vivo in select conditions. Bulk RNA-seq data do not capture the dynamic nature of RNA folding and unfolding in living cells. To address these challenges, single-molecule approaches such as smStructure-seq18 may provide a more comprehensive and accurate picture at the structure isoform level. We also note that our rG4-calling method depends on strong RT-Stops in the presence of K+ but not Li+. However, it is known that some robust rG4’s (such as those in TERRA RNA) can fold in the presence of Li+. Thus, the method likely under-calls the total number of rG4’s both in vitro and in vivo. We also draw the reader’s attention to previously published affinity-based rG4 studies using rG4 ligands that demonstrate rG4 formation across a wide range of transcripts.19,20,21,22,23,24 These studies tend to have more limited resolution with peak sizes typically spanning a few hundred nucleotides and may over-call rG4s due to ligand stabilization. Nevertheless, comparing results of d-rG4-seq with those from rG4-ligand affinity-based methods may provide a comprehensive view of all potential rG4-forming sites in vivo.

Troubleshooting

Problem 1

RNA degradation occurs during the chromatin RNA purification step (Step 5. Extraction of chromatin-bound RNAs).

Potential solution

If RNA degrades during chromatin RNA purification, it leads to a biased 3′ distribution of sequencing reads. This bias would result in weaker resolving power for the upstream regions of transcripts. Check the RNA integrity by gel electrophoresis or microfluidics-based platforms like TapeStation. RNA degradation may also occur due to improper handling of cultured cells. We commonly observed RNA degradation when frozen cell pellets were thawed before being resuspended in NIB buffer. To prevent this, ensure the remaining part of the pellet remains in contact with dry ice while resuspending the upper part of the pellet.

Problem 2

Inefficient adaptor ligation to the fragmented RNA (Step 8. Adaptor Ligation).

Potential solution

RNA should be highly pure. Use a PAGE gel purification kit with silica columns instead of manual RNA preparation. The high viscosity of PEF8000 can make handling difficult, the reaction solution should be well-mixed by repeated pipetting. Insufficient adenylation of adaptor oligo could cause the ligation issue. 5′ adenylation of adaptor oligo could be monitored with 15% Urea acrylamide gel electrophoresis by +1 nucleotide size shift of the band.

Problem 3

Loss of ribonucleic acid content after the denaturing gel purification (Step 10. reverse transcription (RT)-stop library preparation).

Potential solution

A progressive reduction of nucleic acid material during repeated gel elution can be a major challenge of this process. To mitigate this, always keep the excised gel fragment as small as possible. This can be achieved by limiting the gel electrophoresis run to the minimal time necessary for size separation. Also, make sure the excised gel piece is well fragmented during the gel purification step.

Problem 4

Too many PCR duplicates are removed after deduplication (Steps 12–16, RT-stop reads preprocessing).

Potential solution

This problem is generally associated with the inefficiency of RT-stop library preparation. Low amount of RT-product leads to limited complexity of the PCR templates. A general rule of thumb is to keep the total number of PCR cycles necessary for amplifying the sequencing library in a proper range. The total number of PCR cycles using 630–631 primers and the final Illumina oligos should not exceed 15. Otherwise, a substantial portion of the sequencing reads may derive from PCR duplication.
