Broad Autism Phenotype Questionnaire – Translation and Validation in a South Indian Language
Authors: Betsy Baby, Sandhya Pushpagiri, P Ravi Prasad Varma, LS Saritha, SJ Dhiya, Shoba S Meera, M Rekha, Manju Mohan, Soumya Sundaram
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
Background and Objectives:Broad Autism Phenotype (BAP) refers to specific behaviors or minor deficits in social/communication skills not meeting the diagnostic criteria for autism spectrum disorders (ASDs). The BAP Questionnaire (BAPQ) measures aloof personality (AP), rigid personality (RP), and pragmatic language (PL) deficits of BAP. The study objective is to translate and validate BAPQ into Malayalam, a South Indian language, and to establish new cut-off scores for detecting BAP in the Indian population.Methods:BAPQ was translated into Malayalam (BAPQ-M), following which it was tested for reliability and internal consistency and factor analysis was conducted in 40 parents of ASD and neurotypical children. The receiver operating characteristic curve analysis generated new cut-off scores.Results:The content validity index was ≥ 0.8 for each item. For the self and informant versions, Cronbach’s α was 0.753 and 0.807, respectively, while Spearman’s correlation coefficient was 0.953 (P < 0.001) and 0.918 (P < 0.001). The items demonstrated good communality (0.532–0.870), and on factor rotation, 12 components had eigenvalues > 1, which explained 72.3% of the total variance. The new cut-off scores for total estimate, AP, RP, and PL subscales were 2.60 (sensitivity 0.825; specificity 0.575; odds ratio [OR] 6.38, 95% confidence interval [CI] 2.28–17.84; P < 0.001), 2.60 (sensitivity 0.60; specificity 0.75; OR 4.50, 95% CI 1.73–11.70; P 0.002), 2.90 (sensitivity 0.8; specificity 0.475; OR 3.62, 95% CI 1.342–9.77; P 0.011), and 2.70 (sensitivity 0.5; specificity 0.825; OR 4.71, 95% CI 1.69–13.13; P 0.003), respectively.Conclusions:BAPQ-M has good psychometric properties for determining the presence of BAP among the people of Kerala, a South Indian state.

Introduction

The term “broad autism phenotype” (BAP) refers to specific behaviors or minor deficits in social or communication skills that do not meet the diagnostic criteria for autism spectrum disorder (ASD).[123] Personality traits such as aloofness, rigidity, and anxiousness, and pragmatic language (PL) inadequacies are the defining characteristics of BAP. Other associated features include depression, anxiety, obsessive–compulsive disorders, and social phobia.[45] These traits observed in parents or other relatives of people with ASD are attributed to shared genetic and environmental influences.[367] These can negatively impact parenting practices, interventions, and their mental health.[8] Thus, assessing BAP in parents may contribute to developing rehabilitation programs that promote parent–child interactions and their outcomes.

Autism Family History Interview, Modified Personality Assessment Schedule, Broad Autism Phenotype Symptom Scale, Pragmatic Rating Scale, Autism Quotient, Social Responsiveness Scale, and Broad Autism Phenotype Questionnaire (BAPQ) are the various methods for detecting BAP.[910] Among them, BAPQ is a valid and reliable questionnaire with high sensitivity (81.8%) and specificity (78.1%) for detecting BAP. It is translated and validated in nine languages, including Hindi and Kannada.[1112] The cut-off scores have yet to be established for the Indian population. This questionnaire is also used for deep endophenotyping in large-scale genetic studies in ASD.[13] Because of its clinical and research applications, BAPQ has been chosen to measure BAP in our population.

The prevalence of ASD in Kerala is 23.3 per 10,000, which contributes to a major burden, necessitating the use of translated and culturally appropriate assessment tools.[14] Hence, this study was conducted to translate BAPQ into Malayalam (BAPQ-M), the regional language of Kerala, and to evaluate its psychometric properties. We also aimed to establish new cut-off scores for determining BAP in our population.

Methods

Our Institutional Ethics Committee approved the conduct of the study (SCT/IEC/1253/2018).

Description of BAPQ

BAPQ consists of 36 statements in three domains (subscales): aloof personality (AP), rigid personality (RP), and PL. The questionnaire has self- and informant versions, which have to be completed by both parents of the child. Each statement is scored on a 6-point Likert scale based on how often it relates to the rater (1 – very rarely, 2 – rarely, 3 – occasionally, 4 – somewhat frequently, 5 – often, and 6 – very frequently). Averaging responses within each subscale yields subscale scores; the final score is the average of these three subscale values. A total score is derived for the self-reported version (TS score) and the informant version (TI score), and the total best estimate score (TE) is the average of TS and TI scores. The scores range from 1 to 6, and a person is classified as BAP + or BAP- based on the BAPQ cut-off scores.[9]

Translation of BAPQ into Malayalam (BAPQ-M)

The translation and cultural adaptation guidelines of the International Society for Pharmacoeconomics and Outcomes Research (ISPOR) and the World Health Organization (WHO) were used to translate BAPQ from English to Malayalam.[1215] The steps consisted of forward translation, reconciliation, back translation, face validation, and cognitive debriefing [Figure 1].

Permission was obtained from the original developers of BAPQ (JP) to use the instrument. Forward translation to Malayalam was done by two independent translators (EB and SLS) who were fluent in Malayalam and English. Rather than an exact word-to-word translation, the conceptual equivalent of the statement was targeted during the translation process. A third independent translator (IDR) reviewed the two forward-translated versions for reconciliation and formulated a single version. The discrepancies were discussed with the principal investigator (SS), and the first version of the translated questionnaire (V1) was drafted. The back translation of V1 into English was done by two experts (HK and LP) who were proficient in both English and Malayalam and who had no prior knowledge of the questionnaire. The principal investigator and the second translator reviewed the two versions and finalized the back-translated version. The translated Malayalam and back-translated English versions were sent to the questionnaire’s original authors for back-translation review (JP and SSM). They suggested modifying items 3, 18, and 19 in V1. These items alone were subjected to forward translation, reconciliation, and backward translation, which were approved by the questionnaire developers, and version 2 (V2) of Malayalam was finalized.

BAPQ-M was then subjected to face validation by 10 experts – two neurologists, three psychologists, two speech-language pathologists, one occupational therapist, one psychometrician, and a special educator who comprehended the idea under investigation and its construct. Face validity, also known as logical validity, is a subjective assessment of whether the test/instrument measures what it intends to measure. The experts were asked to rate the entire scale on the following aspects: (i) relevance of the translated questions for assessing BAP, (ii) grammatically correct language usage, (iii) whether the questionnaire can be administered quickly, (iv) ease of understanding the language, (v) ease of understanding the instructions to be followed in filling the questionnaire, (vi) to check for common errors like double-barreled and confusing terms or leading questions, and (vii) formatting errors like alignment, spacing, and font type and size. All the experts felt that the questionnaire was relevant and appropriate for assessing BAP. The majority (nine experts) felt that the language was grammatically correct and easily understandable and the questionnaire could be administered quickly. Three of them suggested that the instructions for filling out the questionnaire could be made easier and simpler, as it had typographic errors and confusing phrases. The Malayalam instruction section was revised to make it more comprehensible and suitably structured by increasing the font size with enough spacing to make it more readable; thereafter, the third version (V3) was drafted. This was approved by the principal investigator.

Cognitive debriefing of V3 was performed on parents (five spousal pairs each) of autism children and neurotypical children to check for comprehensibility, appropriate interpretation of the items, and cultural relevance of the translation. Interview method was used for cognitive debriefing on the following aspects: (i) comprehension of each item, (ii) any confusion or doubts regarding any words or phrases, (iii) better alternatives to any terms, (iv) difficulty deciding on the responses to be selected, (v) clarity of the response options, and (vi) general comments if there is any. The cognitive debriefing results were reviewed by the principal investigator and one of the forward translators (SLS) against the original version of the instrument. Based on the review, items 7 and 10 were modified for ease of understanding. Thereafter, the questionnaire underwent proofreading for typographic and grammatical errors by two experts who were experienced and skilled in Malayalam. The Malayalam version of the questionnaire was then finalized (BAPQ-M) [Supplementary Table 1]. A final report was generated detailing the entire translation process and cultural adaptation decisions, which is available with the principal investigator.

Psychometric analysis of BAPQ-M

BAPQ-M was subjected to content validity, test–retest reliability, and component analysis for psychometric testing.

Content validity

BAPQ-M was administered to a group of 10 experts in the field of neurodevelopmental disorders and they were asked to rate the relevance of the individual items for measuring BAP. The item-level content validity index (I-CVI) was calculated, and values greater than 0.78 were considered acceptable.[16] The scale-level CVI (S-CVI) was calculated by averaging the I-CVI of each item.

Reliability analysis

Internal and external consistency of the questionnaire was calculated using Cronbach’s alpha and test–retest reliability, respectively. The sample size computed for measuring internal consistency with an expected α of 0.65, a precision level of 0.1, and a confidence level of 95% for the 36-item scale at 10% dropout was 42.[17] Cronbach’s alpha was determined for both versions, and values between 0.6 and 0.7 indicate acceptable reliability, while values between 0.8 and 0.95 indicate very good reliability. Alpha > 0.95 suggests redundancy of items.[16] For test–retest reliability, the questionnaire was administered to 10 fathers each of neurotypical and autism children twice at an interval of 15 days (T1 and T2), and the scores were analyzed using Spearman’s correlation coefficient (rho) and Wilcoxon signed-rank performance test. A rho value of + 1 indicates a perfect positive association, whereas -1 indicates a perfect negative association.

Component analysis

Principal component analysis (PCA) was used to explore the factor structure, that is, to investigate the interrelationship of variables by reducing the number of observed variables into a smaller set of primary components.[18] The sample adequacy was verified by the Kaiser–Meyer–Olkin (KMO) score, and Bartlett’s test of sphericity was used to determine whether or not the data were normally distributed. A KMO score greater than 0.5 and Bartlett’s test significance level lower than 0.05 indicate that the data are significantly correlated and are eligible for factor analysis.[19] PCA findings were reported using “component loadings,” which are correlation coefficients between test items and identified components of analysis. Loading levels above 0.75 were regarded as strong, those between 0.75 and 0.50 were considered moderate, and those between 0.50 and 0.30 were labeled as “weak” loadings.[20] Communality scores for both the self-reported and informant-reported versions were calculated. When a variable was found to have more than one significant loading (cross-loading), the varimax rotation method was applied to eliminate any cross-loadings and the eigenvalue was determined. Communality values close to 1 and an eigenvalue greater than 1 are considered ideal.[21]

Sensitivity and specificity

New cut-off scores for the current study population, which is racially and culturally different, were established using the receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curve and the Youden index (J statistic). The ROC curve was created by plotting and joining all matching true-positive rates (sensitivity) against the false-positive rates. The area under the curve (AUC) from the ROC curve was calculated; the larger the AUC (0.5–1), the better cut-off scores can be established.[22] The Youden index was obtained by the formula sensitivity + specificity - 1. The maximum value of the Youden index is 1, indicating a perfect test, and the minimum value is 0.[23] The sensitivity and specificity of the new cut-off scores for BAP + and their diagnostic odds ratio (OR) were calculated.[24]

Data collection for psychometric testing

A cross-sectional research design with a purposive sampling method was used in this study. Forty parents of children with ASD and neurotypical children who had a minimum of 10 years of education and whose mother tongue is Malayalam were included. Foster parents, stepparents, parents with a diagnosis of ASD, and other guardians were excluded. Informed written consent was obtained, and confidentiality of data and identity was assured. Demographic variables such as parental age, sex of the child, and socioeconomic status (based on a socioeconomic card issued by the government) were collected. Once the self-report section was completed, the participants were instructed to complete the informant version of their spouse, thus giving two sets of values for each participant.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analysis was conducted using Statistical Package for the Social Sciences version 25. Scores for each domain (AP, RP, and PL) and TS, TI, and TE scores were calculated. Numerical variables were summarized into means and standard deviations, and categorical variables into proportions. Student’s t-test was used to compare means, and the Chi-square or Fisher’s exact test was used to compare proportions. The effect size was calculated using Cohen’s D method (d), and the value ranges from 0 to 1, with higher values indicating a greater effect size.[25]

Reliability tests

Cronbach’s alpha for the self- and informant-reported versions were 0.753 and 0.807, respectively, suggesting an acceptable level of reliability [Table 3]. The reliability of the subscales PL (self) and RP (self and informant) in the ASD group was poor (<0.5), but the subscales in the neurotypical group had good reliability. In the test–retest analysis, the self and informant versions had rho values of 0.953 (P < 0.001) and 0.918 (P < 0.001), respectively, indicating a significant correlation. The Wilcoxon signed-rank test values for T1 and T2 were 0.438 and 0.574, respectively, and the difference was 0.136, which is close to 0. Hence, no significant changes in participants’ responses on retest were observed.

Component analysis

The KMO measures of sampling adequacy for the self and informant versions were 0.531 and 0.553, respectively. Bartlett’s test of sphericity calculated by the Chi-square test was 1026.583 (P < 0.001) and 1298.774 (P < 0.001) for the self and informant versions, respectively. Since the KMO measure was more than 0.5 and Bartlett’s test of sphericity was significant, the data was eligible for dimensionality reduction. For the self-reported version, no significant cross-loadings were obtained in PCA; however, items 6, 13, 20, 28, 35, and 36 were found to have negligible component loadings. No significant cross-loadings were obtained for the informant version, but items 12, 17, 22, 25, and 36 showed very weak component loadings. For the self-reported version, all 36 items demonstrated moderate to high communality values, the lowest being 0.532 (Q19) and the highest being 0.849 (Q23) [Table 4]. Upon factor rotation, 13 components (eigenvalue ranging from 1.39 to 2.722) were extracted, which explained 71.7% of the total variance. For the informant-reported version, items demonstrated communality ranging from 0.561 (Q25) to 0.870 (Q28), indicating good communality, that is, shared variances of the items. The rotated component matrix using varimax yielded 12 components (eigenvalue ranging from 1.45 to 3.28), which explained 72.3% of the total variance. The component analysis values, along with the communality and eigenvalue scores, indicate that there is some latent variance with which the manifest variance (items of BAPQ-M) can be explained.

Discussion

In this study, BAPQ was translated and culturally adapted to Malayalam, a regional language, as BAPQ-M in accordance with ISPOR and WHO standards.[1526] BAPQ was chosen because it is a simple, reliable, and validated tool that has been translated and culturally adapted across different linguistic populations and provides quantitative data on BAP severity.[91227] Cronbach’s alpha values of 0.753 and 0.807 and Spearman correlation coefficient values of 0.953 and 0.918 for self-reported and informant versions, respectively, indicate good psychometric properties. PCA for the scale found a latent component structure with low cross-loadings for the scale. For the South Indian population, new cut-off scores with moderate sensitivity and specificity for categorizing respondents as BAP + or BAP- were also established.

The self and informant versions of BAPQ-M had acceptable to very good internal consistency; however, in the ASD parent group, α was less than 0.5 in the RP and PL subscales of both self-reported and informant versions. However, the neurotypical parent group demonstrated very good reliability for total and subscale scores for both the self and informant versions. The low reliability scores in these subscales among parents of ASD children could be attributed to the sample’s cultural and semantic diversity. It could also be ascribed to the response and social desirability biases, which refer to respondents’ proclivity to provide answers that they believe are socially desirable rather than being accurate ones.[28] Knowing the partner rating might have also operated as a pressure trigger. The neurotypical group’s greater education status may have resulted in a better understanding of the concept and meaning of the questionnaire, which could explain their higher internal consistency scores when compared to the ASD parent group. The more pronounced BAP features in the ASD parent group may also account for their deviant responses.[27]

BAP distribution was significantly greater in the ASD parent group in the current study, and the effect was larger for total PL and TE scores. Previous research had also consistently found that parents of ASD children had higher mean BAPQ values than parents of neurotypical children.[91012272930] Because of cultural and demographic variations from the Western population on which the initial cut-off was based, we generated new cut-off scores for BAPQ-M for detecting BAP in our population. Despite the fact that BAPQ has been translated into Hindi and Kannada, no standard cut-off scores for our population had previously been developed.[1112] The new cut-off scores for TE, total AP, RP, and PL were 2.60, 2.60, 2.90, and 2.70, respectively, which are much lower than the previous cut-off values of 3.15, 3.25, 3.50, and 2.75, respectively [Supplementary Table 2].[9] Variability in personality structure among the Indian population might result in differences in how individuals respond to particular assessments or questionnaires. Similarly, cultural and societal heterogeneity in the Indian population might alter individuals’ attitudes, beliefs, and behaviors, affecting the study findings.[31] Reduced sample size and sample distribution could be other explanations for the lower cut-off scores.

BAPQ-M has undergone meticulous translation and cultural adaptation and has shown good psychometric properties in the preliminary analysis. BAP of parents may not be evident during routine interviews, but could be detected by this questionnaire.[910] With early recognition of the BAP traits of parents, therapists can effectively formulate family-centered interventions, ensuring a positive outcome. Since BAP traits are heritable and reflect the underlying genetic susceptibility for autism, this questionnaire can be utilized for deep endophenotyping in clinical and genomic research studies.[91013] Moreover, BAPQ can assist the clinician in more effective parental education and genetic counseling for diagnostic genetic testing.[32]

Limitations of the study

The primary limitation of the study was the small sample size. However, a sample of 80 participants was considered adequate for the preliminary validation.[17] We obtained satisfactory results in reliability and component analysis, but the robust estimation of internal consistency and factor analysis was limited by a small sample. Furthermore, we were unable to statistically explore the differences in the subscale reliability scores between the neurotypical and ASD groups. Logistic regression analysis could not be applied to find potential demographic and clinical variables influencing psychometric properties.

Response bias is another limitation of a questionnaire-based survey. This self-reported questionnaire is susceptible to social desirability bias, which occurs when participants reply in a socially acceptable manner, rather than offering honest responses.[28] The socioeconomic and educational disparities between the ASD and neurotypical groups may have contributed to response bias. Cultural prejudice, semantic problems, and cognitive barriers of the participants may have had an impact on the responses. Administrator bias during cognitive debriefing should not be ignored since it could explain the respondent’s inaccurate judgment and response persuasion. The study was carried out in a single state of India, which restricts the generalizability of the findings. Therefore, validating BAPQ-M in the general population with a large sample size can generate better psychometric values with accurate cut-off scores of particularly good sensitivity and specificity.

Conclusion

BAPQ-M has good psychometric properties for determining the presence of BAP among the people of Kerala, a South Indian state. The newly defined cut-off scores enhance the questionnaire’s utility in clinical and research practice. The current translation has the opportunity for modest adjustments, necessitating validation in a large sample.
