Temperature–Dependent Dielectric Function of Calcium Fluoride
T. Das, D. Alam, C. A. Ullrich, U. D. Jentschura

Abstract
Calcium fluoride (fluorspar, CaF 2 ) is of consummate importance for the manufacturing of optical components. In many cases, its wide transmission band eliminates the need for antireflective coatings. It is thus of interest to study its optical properties as a function of temperature. The optical properties are mainly determined by a strongly temperature-dependent infrared (IR) peak, and a series of nearly temperature-independent ultraviolet (UV) peaks. We find that the temperature dependence of the IR peak can be modeled, to good accuracy, by a radiation-reaction improved coupled-oscillator model, with temperature-dependent parameters. For the UV peaks, we find a convenient functional form which covers both the real as well as the imaginary parts of the dielectric function and provide a comparison to first-principles calculations. The result is a compact functional form for the dielectric function of undoped CaF 2 in the frequency range 0 < ω < 1.8 ​ a . u . 0<\omega<1.8\,{\rm a.u.} , and in the temperature range 22 ∘ ​ C < T < 500 ∘ ​ C 22^{\circ}\mathrm{C}<T<500^{\circ}\mathrm{C} . With the help of the temperature-dependent dielectric function, we obtain temperature-dependent values of the short-range and long-range asymptotics of atom-surface interactions with CaF 2 , for hydrogen, as well as ground-state and metastable helium.



I
Introduction

Due to its wide transmission band associated with a nearly transparent region
in the visible range of the optical spectrum, calcium fluoride (CaF
2
,
commonly referred to as fluorspar) has many technological applications.
E.g.
, it is used for anti-reflective coatings on lenses
Bannon 1946
,
transparent substrates in photolithography
Bloomstein et al. 1997
, and as a
substrate material for infrared spectroscopy
Mayerhöfer et al. 2020
. The material
is subjected to extreme thermal conditions in instances such as optical
elements in high-energy lasers
Klein 2006
. Other environments with extreme
conditions include the Near–Infrared Spectrometer and Photometer (NISP) on the
Euclid satellite
Leviton et al. 2015
. Accurate knowledge of the optical
and dielectric properties of CaF
2
and their temperature dependence is thus
beneficial for these applications. A convenient functional form, to describe
the dielectric function over wide ranges of temperature and frequency, is also
required and desirable for the calculation
of atom-surface interactions and quantum-reflection
studies.

The structure of the dielectric function of CaF
2
is characterized by
a giant infrared (IR) resonance, where the
maximum modulus of the
real part of the dielectric function
exceeds the value of
|{\mathrm{Re}}(\epsilon)|>80
,
while rapidly changing its sign at the resonance,
and a series of peaks in the ultraviolet (UV).
The IR peak has recently been
analyzed in Ref.
Passerat de Silans et al. 2009
, while
the transparent region within the visible range
has been analyzed in relatively recent
articles
Li 1980
;
Daimon and Masumura 2002
;
Firoz et al. 2008
;
Leviton et al. 2015
;
Kelly-Gorham et al. 2017
;
Zheng et al. 2023
.
By contrast, data collected
for the IR region in Ref.
Bezuidenhout
,
while capturing the main characteristics of the
frequency-dependent dielectric function,
must be considered outdated at the current stage. For the UV region,
we use data from Refs.
Tousey 1936
;
Lemonnier et al. 1965
.
The data collected in Ref.
Lemonnier et al. 1965

display five overlapping resonances
in the UV range
0.35\,{\rm a.u.}<\omega<1.3\,{\rm a.u.}

(here, “a.u.” stands for atomic units,
where the photon energy is measured in Hartrees,
see Chap. 2 of Ref.
Jentschura and Adkins 2022
).
It is essential that the data from Ref.
Lemonnier et al. 1965

display clear excitonic signatures at a
UV photon energy of
\hbar\omega\approx 10.5
eV.
Namely, apart from the fact that excitonic effects dominate the optical properties
of wide-gap insulators close to the optical gap,
these features are also essential for the comparison of experimental
data to first-principles calculations, which
are reported here using
time-dependent density-functional theory (TDDFT).

We have recently investigated the dielectric functions
of intrinsic silicon
Moore et al. 2022
;
Moore et al. 2025

and
\alpha
-quartz
Jentschura 2024

and found that a radiation-reaction improved coupled-oscillator model
(Lorentz–Dirac model) is very well suited
for the description of both real and
imaginary parts of the dielectric function.
In Ref.
Das et al. 2025
, it was
shown that model calculations based on coupled
damped oscillators support the functional form employed in the
Lorentz–Dirac model used in
Refs.
Moore et al. 2022
;
Jentschura 2024
;
Moore et al. 2025
.
Essentially, the coupled-oscillator model
leads to a representation of the optical response
of the medium in terms of damped oscillator eigenmodes
with complex oscillator strengths.
Similar approaches have been
discussed in Eq. (1) of Ref.
Ribarsky
for rutile, in
Eq. (4) of Ref.
Tropf
for cubic thallium, and in
Eq. (1) of Ref.
Palik and Khanna
for sodium nitrate.
Hence, it is indicated to investigate
if the same analytic model,
which has a comparatively simple analytic form,
can be successfully applied
to other technologically important substances
such as CaF
2
. As a byproduct of our investigations,
we shall thus aim to update and improve
the four-term Sellmeier model that is
otherwise used in Ref.
Daimon and Masumura 2002

to describe the experimental data for CaF
2
.

Here, we confirm that, although a
traditional Sellmeier-type model is well suited
in representing the optical data
in the transparent region, it fails to represent the complicated
structure found in the UV region, and in the IR region.
Our first aim is to find a unified functional
form which, for room temperature,
covers the entire spectral range from the IR to the UV.
Our second aim is to describe the temperature dependence
of the dielectric function.
In Ref.
Passerat de Silans et al. 2009
, the temperature dependence
of the IR peak was described using an elaborate functional form.
The temperature dependence in
the visible region has been analyzed
by temperature-dependent Sellmeier functional forms
in Refs.
Leviton et al. 2015
and
Zheng et al. 2023
.
Our aim is to represent the temperature dependence of the dielectric
function using an economic functional form that captures the temperature
dependence in the IR and visible region and also reproduces the peaks in the UV
region which are mostly temperature independent,
and to confront the results with first-principles calculations
in the UV region.

This paper is organized as follows.
In Sec.
II.1
, we analyze, very briefly, the
structure of the IR and UV resonances of CaF
2
.
The IR peak, including its temperature dependence,
is analyzed in Sec.
II.2
.
The UV peaks are analyzed in Sec.
II.3
.
Results for the temperature- and frequency-dependent
dielectric function are discussed in Sec.
II.4
.
In Sec.
III
,
we provide additional insight into the UV response of CaF
2
through TDDFT.
Atom-surface potentials are calculated in Sec.
IV
.
Conclusions are reserved for Sec.
V
.
A statistical analysis of the fit is described in
Appendix
A
, and
an illustrative discussion of the natural cleavage
planes is contained in Appendix
B
.

II
Dielectric Function of CaF
2

II.1
Resonances

A careful examination of the experimental data obtained
in Refs.
Passerat de Silans et al. 2009
;
Li 1980
;
Daimon and Masumura 2002
;
Firoz et al. 2008
;
Leviton et al. 2015
;
Kelly-Gorham et al. 2017
;
Zheng et al. 2023
;
Tousey 1936
;
Lemonnier et al. 1965
reveals the presence
of one dominant IR resonance and a total of five UV resonances
(see Fig.
1
).
The Lorentz–Dirac model is thus used in the form
(see Refs.
Moore et al. 2022
;
Moore et al. 2025
)

\epsilon(T_{\Delta},\omega)=1+\sum_{k=1}^{k_{\rm max}=6}\frac{a_{k}(\omega_{k}^{2}-{\mathrm{i}}\gamma_{k}^{\prime}\omega)}{\omega_{k}^{2}-\omega^{2}-{\mathrm{i}}\omega\gamma_{k}}\,.

(1)

Here,

T_{\Delta}=(T-T_{0})/T_{0}

(2)

is the reduced temperature.
The value of
T_{0}=293\,{\rm K}

(as opposed to, say,
T_{0}=295\,{\rm K}
) is
used here for room temperature, in accordance with
Refs.
Moore et al. 2022
;
Moore et al. 2025
.

The identification is as follows:
The
\omega_{k}
are the resonance frequencies,
the
\gamma_{k}
are the width parameters,
the
\gamma^{\prime}_{k}
are the radiation-reaction parameters,
and the parameters
a_{k}
represent the amplitudes.
The relation of the generalized
Sellmeier model (Lorentz–Dirac model)
given in Eq. (
1
) to
coupled oscillators and radiative reaction
is discussed in Ref.
Das et al. 2025
.

II.2
Giant IR Peak

The very careful investigation in Ref.
Passerat de Silans et al. 2009

is our primary resource for temperature-dependent
data on the frequency-dependent
dielectric function around the
IR resonance at
\omega\approx 1.18\times 10^{-3}\,{\rm a.u.}
.
However, even if one were to restrict the discussion
to the IR region alone, it would be inconsistent to
obtain the fitting parameters for the
coupled-oscillator model given in Eq. (
1
) based on IR
data alone, because the functional form
given in Eq. (
1
) is not restricted
to a particular range of frequency values,
and a change in the fitting parameters for
the IR region may thus influence the behavior of
\epsilon(\omega)
in
the UV region as well.
Thus, in order to allow for a smooth transition to the transparent (visible)
and UV region, we use temperature-dependent data from
Refs.
Passerat de Silans et al. 2009
;
Zheng et al. 2023
in order to fit the IR parameters,
i.e.
,
the temperature-dependent parameters
a_{1}
,
\omega_{1}
,
\gamma_{1}
and

\gamma^{\prime}_{1}
, in the temperature range
22^{\circ}\mathrm{C}<T<500^{\circ}\mathrm{C}
.
For room temperature, we have checked the consistency
of the used data sets with Refs.
Kelly-Gorham et al. 2017
;
Li 1980
;
Daimon and Masumura 2002
;
Firoz et al. 2008
.
Results are communicated in Table
1
.

We define the dimensionless reduced-temperature parameter
T_{\Delta}
as

T_{\Delta}=\frac{T-T_{0}}{T_{0}};\qquad T_{0}=293\rm K\,,

(3)

and find that the IR parameters for different temperatures
can be expressed succinctly with a cubic polynomial,
as follows,

\displaystyle a_{1}=

\displaystyle\;C_{0}^{(a_{1})}+C_{1}^{(a_{1})}\,T_{\Delta}+C_{2}^{(a_{1})}T_{\Delta}^{2}+C_{3}^{(a_{1})}\,T_{\Delta}^{3}\,,

(4a)

\displaystyle\omega_{1}=

\displaystyle\;C_{0}^{(\omega_{1})}+C_{1}^{(\omega_{1})}\,T_{\Delta}+C_{2}^{(\omega_{1})}T_{\Delta}^{2}+C_{3}^{(\omega_{1})}\,T_{\Delta}^{3}\,,

(4b)

\displaystyle\gamma_{1}=

\displaystyle\;C_{0}^{(\gamma_{1})}+C_{1}^{(\gamma_{1})}\,T_{\Delta}+C_{2}^{(\gamma_{1})}T_{\Delta}^{2}+C_{3}^{(\gamma_{1})}\,T_{\Delta}^{3}\,,

(4c)

\displaystyle\gamma^{\prime}_{1}=

\displaystyle\;C_{0}^{(\gamma^{\prime}_{1})}+C_{1}^{(\gamma^{\prime}_{1})}\,T_{\Delta}+C_{2}^{(\gamma^{\prime}_{1})}T_{\Delta}^{2}+C_{3}^{(\gamma^{\prime}_{1})}\,T_{\Delta}^{3}\,.

(4d)

Numerical results are presented in Table
2
.
In contrast to Refs.
Moore et al. 2022
;
Moore et al. 2025
,
we find that quadratic polynomials in
T_{\Delta}

are insufficient to capture the observed temperature
dependence; third-order polynomials lead to a
satisfactory fit.

II.3
UV Peaks and Transparent Region

For the UV region, the main source of data is from Ref.
Lemonnier et al. 1965

(see also Fig.
1
). In the visible (transparent) region,
there are no discernible resonances.
Hence, for temperature-dependent high-precision data in the transparent region,
we use the most recent, high-precision data set from Ref.
Zheng et al. 2023
,
which is more detailed than the data set provided
in Ref.
Leviton et al. 2015
and, as we have checked, consistent with
the data sets of Refs.
Firoz et al. 2008
;
Kelly-Gorham et al. 2017
;
Li 1980
;
Daimon and Masumura 2002
,
which had been obtained for temperatures very close to room temperature.
The inclusion of the data sets relevant
to the transparent region ensures a smooth transition into the
visible optical range. The coefficients for
2\leq k\leq 6

resulting from the nonlinear fit are reported in Table
3
.

For the UV peaks, we assume a temperature-independent model,
for reasons described in the following.
Temperature-dependent data for the dielectric function have been
obtained in Refs.
Kelly-Gorham et al. 2017
;
Zheng et al. 2023
,
for the intermediate range
corresponding to the visible spectrum.
In this region, CaF
2
is largely transparent.
From an inspection of the data presented in
Refs.
Kelly-Gorham et al. 2017
;
Zheng et al. 2023
, it becomes clear that,
near the transition to the UV,
both the real as well as the imaginary parts
of the dielectric function change by less than a percent
between 273 K and 773 K (
0\,^{\circ}\mathrm{C}
and
500\,^{\circ}\mathrm{C}
).
For the IR and visible range, and the transition region to the
ultraviolet, we conservatively estimate the general
accuracy of our fit to be on the order of about 5 %,
even if the statistics of our fit in this region
would suggest a smaller uncertainty (see Appendices
A.1

and
A.2
, as well as
Tables
4
and
5
). The conservative estimate
of a 5 % uncertainty takes care of conceivable unaccounted
systematic effects in the experimental data,
as obtained in the ellipsometry
measurements
Li 1980
;
Daimon and Masumura 2002
;
Firoz et al. 2008
;
Leviton et al. 2015
;
Kelly-Gorham et al. 2017
;
Zheng et al. 2023
,
and is in line with typical accuracy limits of ellipsometry
measurements for comparable materials
Moore et al. 2022
;
Moore et al. 2025
.
The 5 % uncertainty is larger than the
observed temperature variation in that same transition region.
We are thus safe to assume that, within the accuracy of our fit,
the temperature dependence in the UV can be ignored.
This conjecture is tied to the fact that the UV resonances arise from
electronic interband transitions, and it appears that
the band gap is virtually independent of thermal effects.
By contrast, the IR peaks are related to
vibrational excitations of the crystal lattice.

In the fitting, we have been
careful to avoid introducing a conceivable, numerically small
spurious negative imaginary part of the dielectric
function,
i.e.
, to implement the
condition
{\mathrm{Im}}[\epsilon(\omega)]\geq 0

II.4
Results

The fitting procedures outlined in the
previous sections lead to a satisfactory
representation of the optical data
for CaF
2
in the temperature
range
22^{\circ}\mathrm{C}<T<500^{\circ}\mathrm{C}
.

In this section, we discuss the zero-temperature optical spectra of CaF

Onida et al. 2002

10.424

To investigate the optical response of CaF

Sun et al. 2020

;

combining a fraction of nonlocal (Hartree-Fock) exchange with semilocal density
functional approximations; here, the mixing parameter is determined via the
inverse static dielectric constant of the material. As shown in previous work

Sun et al. 2020
;
Sun and Ullrich 2021
;
Alam et al. 2025

functionals are essentially as accurate as the Bethe-Salpeter equation (which
is generally considered as most reliable method
for calculating optical properties of materials
Onida et al. 2002
),
but at a fraction of the computational cost. In particular,
excitonic effects in optical spectra are reproduced with quantitative accuracy;
by contrast, random-phase-approximation (RPA)
spectra fail to account for excitons, although otherwise they
often tend to capture the optical response qualitatively correctly.

notice that the spectra were calculated with a numerical broadening of

calculated along the (100) and (111) crystallographic directions, and the
results show that there is very little difference (apart from the RPA peak
around 30 eV which is enhanced if calculated along (111)).
In Ref.
Lemonnier et al. 1965
, the experimental data were taken for cleaved
single-crystalline samples. Since the standard cleavage plane for
crystals with a face-centered cubic (fcc) lattice is the (111) plane
(see also Appendix
B

TDDFT results along (111) in comparison with experiment and with our fit.

The RPA spectra, which can be viewed as the independent-particle response of
the material, are obtained without any dynamical many-body effects;
this means, in particular, that RPA does not include
electron-hole interactions and hence does not produce any excitons. The first
prominent RPA peak of Im
[\epsilon(\omega)]
at
15 eV can be directly related to
the DOS shown in Fig.
7
: it is caused by transitions between the
highest occupied valence bands (which produces a sharp peak at the Fermi level)
and the fourth and fifth conduction band (which produce a strong and very sharp
peak at 15 eV). RPA transitions to the lower conduction band edge have a much
smaller oscillator strength, giving rise only to a small shoulder around
12.5 eV.

The hybrid-TDDFT spectrum, by contrast, agrees very well with the experimental
data as shown in Fig.
9

Im
[\epsilon(\omega)]
in the energy range between 10 and 15 eV, due to excitonic
interactions. The first excitonic peak right at the band gap is more prominent
in the calculation compared to experiment; the binding energy
of the first exciton is found to be
1.2 eV along (100) and 1.5 eV along (111). For Re
[\epsilon(\omega)]
, the
experimental data show a very pronounced three-fold splitting, which is also
seen in the TDDFT-calculated curve,
although the third peak only appears as a small shoulder.

IV
Atom–Surface Potentials

IV.1
Short–Range Limit

It is well known (see Appendix A of Ref.
Das et al. 2024

and references therein) that the Casimir interaction
between an electrically neutral atom and a dielectric
surface is approximated by
a
1/z^{3}
formula for short range,
which transitions into a

1/z^{4}
dependence for long range,
before transiting into an
extreme-long-range,
1/z^{3}
regime,
(for distances above the thermal wavelength),
where the interaction energy
between the atom and the solid
is numerically small and proportional
to the absolute temperature.
Suitable functional forms for the
atom-surface interactions in intermediate
regions have been discussed in Ref.
Jentschura and Moore 2023
.
In the short-range limit, the atom-surface potential
V(z)
, with
z
denoting
the atom-surface distance can be expressed as,

V(z)=-\frac{C_{3}}{z^{3}},\quad a_{0}\sim d\ll z\ll z_{\rm crit}\,.

(5)

Here,
a_{0}
is the Bohr radius,

\alpha
is the fine structure constant,
and
d
is a characteristic
distance scale of the crystal lattice.
Further clarifications can be found in Eq. (2) of
Ref.
Crépin et al. 2019
as well as Refs.
Klimchitskaya 2025
;
Jentschura 2025
.
Via the definition of a reference plane
z_{0}
, the validity of the
short-distance expansion can be extended to the
physisorption range
Zaremba and Kohn 1976
;
Jentschura 2025
.
The reference-plane position
z_{0}
is
roughly equal to the center of the induced charge density
inside the solid (see Ref.
Zaremba and Kohn 1976
,
Chap. 2 of Ref.
Bruch et al. 1997
,
and Chap. 6 of Ref.
Liebsch 1997
).
For the physisorption range
z\gtrsim a_{0}
,
one replaces
1/z^{3}
in Eq. (
5
) by
1/(z-z_{0})^{3}

(see Refs.
Zaremba and Kohn 1976
;
Jentschura 2025
).

The critical distance
z_{\rm crit}
,
which marks the upper limit of the applicability
of the short-range expansion, is given by
Das et al. 2024

z_{\rm crit}=\frac{a_{0}}{\alpha}\sqrt{\frac{\alpha_{\rm a.u.}(0)}{Z}}\,,

(6)

where
\alpha_{\rm a.u.}(0)
is the
static polarizability of the atom,
measured in atomic units.
(Of course, strictly speaking,
we use atomic units throughout this article,
so that the indication of
a_{0}
,
which is unity in atomic units,
could be considered as redundant.
However, we still indicate
a_{0}

in some of our formulas for clarity.)

In atomic units, the
C_{3}
coefficient can be written as

C_{3}=\frac{1}{4\pi}\int_{0}^{\infty}{\mathrm{d}}\omega~\alpha_{\rm a.u.}({\mathrm{i}}\omega)\frac{\epsilon({\mathrm{i}}\omega)-1}{\epsilon({\mathrm{i}}\omega)+1},

(7)

where
\alpha({\mathrm{i}}\omega)
is the dynamic polarizability
of the atom, evaluated at imaginary frequency argument,
and
\epsilon({\mathrm{i}}\omega)
denotes the
dielectric function, evaluated likewise at imaginary frequencies.

The
C_{3}
coefficients for hydrogen, ground state helium and metastable helium
(
2\,{}^{3}S_{1}
) interacting with CaF
2
are shown in Fig.
10
.
For the IR, and the visible range,
we conservatively assume a
5\%
uncertainty in our
dielectric function model, as explained in Sec.
II.3
.
For the UV range, we conclude that either an unknown systematic effect
in the experiment or the approximate character of
our first-principles calculations
must be responsible for the discrepancies between
theory and experiment as reported
in Figs.
6
and
9
. These discrepancies are
also manifest in the comparatively large
statistical uncertainties of the fit parameters
listed in Tables
4
and
5

(for details of the statistical analysis,
see Appendix
A
). We have checked that the UV region has a
negligible influence on the calculation of the
C_{3}

coefficient. Hence, the
5\%
uncertainty in our
dielectric function model, in the relevant
frequency range, translates into a 5 %
uncertainty in our
C_{3}
coefficients.
For the dynamic polarizability of hydrogen,

\alpha({\mathrm{i}}\omega)
, we use the analytic expression in Eq. (4.161) of
Ref.
Jentschura and Adkins 2022
. For ground state and metastable helium, the
polarizability is calculated using fully corrected basis sets of exponential
basis functions, as outlined in Ref.
Das et al. 2024
.
Numerical values are presented in Table.

The surprisingly smooth dependence of the
C_{3}

coefficient on temperature, as evident from Fig.

despite the rather drastic dependence of the

finds an explanation in the remarkable
reduction of the temperature dependence
after the rotation of the
\omega

contour into the complex plane.

In the distance range

IV.3
Intermediate Region: Onset of Retardation

The transition from the short-range (van der Waals) to the long-range
(Casimir-Polder) asymptotics, due to the retardation effect, has been discussed
in detail in Ref.
Das et al. 2024
. The intermediate region between the two
asymptotics can be conveniently
studied using an effective “local” exponent
{n_{\mathrm{eff}}}
, defined
in terms of the logarithmic derivative of the interaction potential
V(z)
,

n_{\rm eff}=\frac{z}{V(z)}\frac{{\mathrm{d}}V(z)}{{\mathrm{d}}z}=\frac{{\mathrm{d}}\ln(|V(z)|)}{{\mathrm{d}}\ln z},

(11)

where, the logarithm of the potential refers to the logarithm of its numerical
value, expressed in atomic units. For a potential
V(z)=V_{0}z^{n}
, the
effective exponent,
{n_{\mathrm{eff}}}
, evaluates to exactly
n
, that is,
one would obtain

n_{\rm eff}=-3
for a potential of the functional
form
V(z)=-C_{3}/z^{3}
,
while
n_{\rm eff}=-4
for a potential of the functional
form
V(z)=-C_{3}/z^{4}
.

We have used
Das et al. 2024
the value of
{n_{\mathrm{eff}}}=-3.25

to mark the distance
z_{\rm br}
where the short-range expansion breaks
down. The dependence of
{n_{\mathrm{eff}}}
on the atom-wall distance
is shown in Fig.
12
, for
hydrogen, ground-state helium, and metastable (
2\,{}^{3}S_{1}
) helium
interacting with CaF
2
at room temperature.
We observe a small but interesting hump of the effective exponent

{n_{\mathrm{eff}}}
toward a partial restoration of the nonretarded functional dependence
in between
10^{4}
and
10^{5}
atomic units. This hump is due to the
presence of the giant IR resonance of CaF
2
.
We recall that retardation sets in
(see Chap. 5 of Ref.
Jentschura and Adkins 2022
) when the virtual photons,
which mediate the atom-surface interaction, acquire a
phase commensurate with unity during their travel
to and from the surface. This is the case when the largest characteristic
frequency
\omega_{\rm ch}
of the combined atom-solid system,
the atom-wall distance
z
, and the speed of light
c
fulfill
the condition
\omega_{\rm ch}\,z/c\approx 1
.
In typical cases,
\omega_{\rm ch}
is determined by the
atom’s characteristic frequency, and this observation leads to
the estimate (
6
). For CaF
2
, the giant IR resonance
is so strong that, between
10^{4}
and
10^{5}
atomic units, the
effective exponent
{n_{\mathrm{eff}}}
displays a (very partial) restoration
toward the nonretarded interaction. However, even in the
distance region between
10^{4}
and
10^{5}
atomic units, the
restoration is only partial, and the effective exponent
does not rise above
-3.6
, near the second hump,
for all systems under study here
(see Fig.
12
). In Table
7
, we compare
the breakdown distance,
z_{\mathrm{br}}
, with the critical distance,
z_{\mathrm{cr}}

(defined in Eq.
6
).
We also include the
z_{\mathrm{br}}
values for the
same atoms interacting silicon and gold, as presented in
Ref.
Das et al. 2024
. A good order-of-magnitude agreement
between
z_{\mathrm{cr}}
and
z_{\mathrm{br}}
is found, just like in
Ref.
Das et al. 2024
for silicon and gold.

V
Conclusions

In this paper we have
shown that the dielectric function of CaF
2

can be described to very good accuracy
by a radiation-reaction improved coupled-oscillator model,
of the functional form given in Eq. (
1
),
with six resonance terms.
Five of these describe distinct peaks in the UV,
while a giant, characteristic IR resonance,
which is a collective resonance
of the crystal lattice (phonon resonance).
The very pronounced (“giant”) IR resonance
is clearly discernible in both the
real part of
\epsilon(\omega)
, shown in Fig.
2
,
as well as in the peak of the imaginary part,
shown in Fig.
3
.
The height of the IR peak decreases with
temperature, as is evident from Figs.
2
and
3
.

Our results profit from the seminal paper
Passerat de Silans et al. 2009
,
where the temperature-dependence of the IR peak was
analyzed in detail (and very involved functional forms were used in the
fits). We find that the
radiation-reaction improved coupled-oscillator model,
given in Eq. (
1
), provides for a convenient and simple
functional form. We have recently shown
Das et al. 2025

that the
\gamma^{\prime}_{k}
parameters can arise from the coupling
of different resonances. The IR resonance
constitutes a relatively isolated resonance which does not
significantly overlap with the UV peaks,
and, hence, the coupling to other resonances can be
assumed to be small (there could still be
radiative-reaction terms contributing to
\gamma^{\prime}_{1}
).
As evident from Table
1
,
the
\gamma^{\prime}_{1}
parameter is significantly smaller
than
\gamma_{1}
for all temperatures, consistent with the
observations reported in Ref.
Das et al. 2025
.

For the UV peaks, by contrast, the
\gamma^{\prime}_{k=2,3,4,5,6}

parameters are larger than the corresponding
\gamma_{k}

parameters (with the exception of
k=5
, where
the two parameters are nearly equal in magnitude).
These resonances correspond to lattice-modified
molecular transitions (interband transitions), and a considerable coupling
between them can be expected. TDDFT-based first-principles calculations
support our results, showing that the optical properties of CaF
2
in the region
at and below the band gap are dominated by excitonic effects.

One observation is interesting in this context. The pronounced
IR peak has the desired structure and does not create a
negative imaginary part (
\gamma_{1}>\gamma^{\prime}_{1}
for
all temperatures). However, the UV peaks are intertwined
and do not have this property,
i.e.
,
the overlapping resonances do not fulfill the
condition
\gamma_{k}>\gamma^{\prime}_{k}
separately.
The same behavior has been observed for
intrinsic silicon in Refs.
Moore et al. 2022
;
Moore et al. 2025
.

The temperature-dependent universal
parameters of our model are given in
Tables
2
and
3
.
The functional form of the temperature-dependence
of the parameters of the IR peak
is given in Eq. (
4
).
Our fit covers the range
0<\omega<1.8\,{\rm a.u.}
and

0<T_{\Delta}<1.6\,{\rm a.u.}
,
where
T_{\Delta}=(T-T_{0})/T_{0}
,
and
T_{0}=293\,{\rm K}
is the room temperature
[see Eq. (
3
)].
The statistical quality of our fit is
discussed in Appendix
A
,
and an application to atom-surface
interactions is presented in Sec.
IV
.
The latter illustrates that,
after a rotation of the argument of the
dielectric function into the complex plane,

\omega\to{\mathrm{i}}\omega
, the temperature-dependence
becomes smoother, and the
atom-surface interaction potentials
display only a marginal variation with the
temperature-dependent dielectric function.

The “giant IR resonance” is very much
temperature-dependent, and we here obtain
a simple parameterization.
The results obtained here for CaF
2
are, in some sense,
more satisfactory than those obtained for intrinsic Si
in Refs.
Moore et al. 2022
;
Moore et al. 2025
,
because of the availability of experimental
data over a larger frequency range (
\omega\leq 1.8\;{\rm a.u}

versus
\omega\leq 0.16\;{\rm a.u}
).
Furthermore, we here show that our
radiation-reaction improved Sellmeier model
is applicable in the case of a rather unusual
material (in terms of the optical properties),
where a giant IR resonance is largely separated
from a series of overlapping UV peaks,
by a transparent optional region.
For the UV region, it would be beneficial,
eventually, to experimentally confirm, with updated data sets,
the results reported in
Ref.
Tousey 1936
;
Lemonnier et al. 1965
(the latter have been
obtained 88 and 59 years ago, respectively).
However, we reemphasize that
the data published in Ref.
Lemonnier et al. 1965

allow us to devise a consistent model for the time being.
We confirm the surprising success of the
radiation-reaction improved coupled-oscillator model
(Lorentz–Dirac model)
for the description of the temperature-dependent
dielectric function of technologically important
materials for calcium fluoride.

A.1
Parameter Uncertainty

As outlined in Sec.
II.3
, our estimate for the accuracy
of our fitting (in the IR and visible range)

with the accuracy limits of ellipsometry
measurements for comparable
materials
Moore et al. 2022

Moore et al. 2025

It is still interesting to compare this estimate
with the results of a standard statistical analysis
of the available data, and of the fits.
We consider the general situation of
data points
(x_{i},y_{i})
which need to be fitted to a model

f(x;\vec{P})
where
\vec{P}
is the set of fitting
parameters
Press et al. 1986
;
Press et al. 2007
.
For least-squares-fitting, we define a merit function,
\chi^{2}
as

\chi^{2}=\sum_{i=1}^{N}\left(\frac{y_{i}-f(x_{i};\vec{P})}{\sigma_{i}}\right)^{2}.

(12)

Here,
N
is the total number of available data points, and
\sigma_{i}
denotes
the uncertainty associated with each data point. Then, we can determine the
best-fit parameters by minimizing
\chi^{2}
. In order to
estimate the uncertainties in
the fit parameters, we first need to calculate the “curvature matrix”

\bm{U}
which can be defined as follows
[see Eq. (14.4.8) of
Ref.
Press et al. 1986
and Eq. (15.5.8) of
Ref.
Press et al. 2007
],

U_{mn}=\frac{1}{2}\frac{\partial\chi^{2}}{\partial P_{m}\partial P_{n}}.

(13)

The number of fitting parameters is
M
;
so, the indices
m
and
n
assume values
1\leq m,n\leq M
.
The inverse of
\bm{U}
is commonly referred to as the
covariance matrix
\bm{C}
. Its off-diagonal elements give the
covariance between the fitting parameters, and the diagonal elements
lead to the standard variances
\sigma^{2}(P_{m})

of the fitting parameter,
P_{m}
[see
Eq. (14.3.15) of Ref.
Press et al. 1986
and Eq. (15.4.15)
ff. of Ref.
Press et al. 2007
].
One has the relation

\sigma^{2}(P_{m})=C_{mm}=\left(U^{-1}\right)_{mm}.

(14)

After carrying out the differentiation with
respect to the parameters in Eq. (
13
),
we get the following result
[see Eq. (14.4.7) of Ref.
Press et al. 1986
and
Eq. (15.5.7) of Ref.
Press et al. 2007
],

\begin{split}U_{mn}&=\sum_{i=1}^{N}\frac{1}{\sigma_{i}^{2}}\left(\frac{\partial f(x_{i};\vec{P})}{\partial P_{m}}\frac{\partial f(x_{i};\vec{P})}{\partial P_{n}}\right.\\
&\quad\left.-(y_{i}-f(x_{i};\vec{P}))\frac{\partial^{2}f(x_{i};\vec{P})}{\partial P_{m}\mathop{}\!\partial P_{n}}\right).\end{split}

(15)

The second derivative term is usually ignored in practice because,
for a successful fitting,
(y_{i}-f(x_{i};\vec{P}))

should approximately cancel out in the sum. However, the inclusion of

Press et al. 1986
and
Eq. (15.5.11) of Ref.
Press et al. 2007

U\rightarrow\bm{U}

\bm{U}_{mn}=\sum_{i=1}^{N}\frac{1}{\sigma_{i}^{2}}\left(\frac{\partial f(x_{i};\vec{P})}{\partial P_{m}}\frac{\partial f(x_{i};\vec{P})}{\partial P_{n}}\right).

(16)

\sigma_{i}

\sigma

Press et al. 1986

Press et al. 2007

(17)

A.2
Coefficient of Determination

One might ask if our fit has been successful.
A general measure of the quality of the fit is the
coefficient of determination, commonly referred
to as
R^{2}
.
The most general definition of
R^{2}
is given as
Table 1.5, page 15 of Ref.
Rawlings et al. 2001

\displaystyle R^{2}

\displaystyle=1-\frac{SS_{\rm res}}{SS_{\rm tot}},

(18ta)

\displaystyle SS_{\rm res}

\displaystyle=\sum_{i}(y_{i}-f(x_{i}))^{2},

(18tb)

\displaystyle SS_{\rm tot}

\displaystyle=\sum_{i}(y_{i}-\bar{y})^{2}\,.

(18tc)

Here,
SS_{\rm res}
is the “residual sum of squares”,
and
SS_{\rm tot}
is the “total sum of squares”.
Furthermore,
\bar{y}
is the mean of
\{y_{i}\}
.
For all temperatures considered in our fitting,
we obtain values of
R^{2}>0.995
as presented in Fig.
13
.
A coefficient of determination approximately
equal to unity implies that the fitting procedure
was successful.
