Focal ischemia induces genome-wide accumulation of R-loops in the brain
Authors: Vijay Arruri, Suresh L Mehta, Raghu Vemuganti
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractIschemic stroke induces rapid changes in transcriptional regulatory mechanisms across diverse genomic regions of the brain. These alterations fine-tune gene expression patterns to orchestrate post-stroke pathological responses. Precise control of these processes is critical for limiting brain damage and facilitating long-term neurological recovery. During transcription, RNA can hybridize with the DNA template to form three-stranded RNA:DNA hybrids, termed R-loops. Accumulation of R-loops leads to altered transcriptional fidelity, DNA damage, and genomic instability in several pathological conditions. We currently evaluated the effect of stroke on R-loops in mice subjected to transient focal ischemia. Focal ischemia significantly induced R-loop accumulation and DNA damage in the peri-infarct cortex compared with sham. Immunoprecipitated RNA:DNA hybrids sequencing showed strand and site-specific accumulation of R-loops across the genome, with increased R-loops signal at long-noncoding RNA-associated genomic regions. Gene ontology analysis of genes associated with significantly altered R-loops indicated that they regulate several biological functions, including action potential, axonogenesis, apoptosis, chromatin remodeling, dendrite morphogenesis, and synapse organization, which play a significant role in functional recovery after stroke. Overall, our results indicated a potential role of R-loops in modulating ischemic stroke pathophysiology. Targeting R-loops might be a promising strategy to improve post-stroke recovery.Keywords: MT: Non-coding RNAs, RNA:DNA hybrids, stroke, lncRNA, DNA damage, gene expression

Introduction

Transcriptional regulatory mechanisms are essential for the physiologic function of the brain, and their failure might lead to pathologic brain damage following an acute insult such as stroke.1 During transcription, the nascent RNA transiently anneals to DNA within the active site of the RNA polymerase, displacing the non-template DNA strand, forming a temporary RNA:DNA hybrid.2 These transient three-stranded structures, consisting of an RNA:DNA hybrid and a displaced single-stranded DNA, are known as R-loops.2 Physiologically, R-loop homeostasis is tightly regulated by ribonucleases, RNA helicases, and DNA repair enzymes.3 While R-loops are physiological byproducts of transcription, their unscheduled formation and uncontrolled accumulation can induce replication stress and DNA double-strand breaks (DSBs).4 This ultimately results in genome instability, which underlies abnormal cell growth in cancer and progressive neuron loss in neurodegenerative diseases.5,6,7 R-loop dysregulation is also implicated in repeat expansion disorders and motor neuron disease.7 For example, in Friedreich’s ataxia, intronic GAA repeat expansion in the frataxin (FXN) gene promotes R-loop accumulation at the FXN locus, which impairs FXN transcription in patient lymphoblastoid cells.8 Mutations in genes involved in R-loop regulation are linked to juvenile amyotrophic lateral sclerosis.9 R-loop accumulation correlated with DNA damage in astrocytes derived from induced pluripotent stem cells featuring Aicardi-Goutières syndrome.10 This fuels NLRP3 inflammasome and stimulator of interferon gene-mediated responses by increasing the secretion of inflammatory mediators such as TNF-α and interleukin-8, suggesting the pathological role of R-loops in mediating inflammation.10 While R-loops are shown to regulate DNA damage and genome stability in multiple diseases, their role in ischemic stroke remains unexplored. In this study, we mapped genome-wide R-loops in the ischemic brain (Figure 1A), defined their temporal dynamics after injury, and demonstrated their pathological significance.

Results

Focal ischemia enhanced R-loops accumulation and DNA damage

DNA dot blotting in the peri-infarct cortex showed significantly increased accumulation of R-loops following transient middle cerebral artery occlusion (MCAO; ∼1.75-fold at 6 h; ∼4-fold at 12 h, and ∼3.39-fold at 24 h, p < 0.05) compared with sham (Figure 1B). A representative image of the peri-infarct area is shown in Figure S1A. Validation of the S9.6 antibody is presented in Figure S1B, and methylene blue staining used as a loading control for the R-loops dot blot is shown in Figure S1C. We further evaluated whether post-stroke R-loop accumulation was associated with DNA damage. Increased colocalization of R-loops marker (S9.6) with the DNA DSBs marker (γH2AX) was observed at 12 h of reperfusion compared with sham (Figures 1C and S1D). Together, these results demonstrate that enhanced R-loop accumulation after stroke is a proximal trigger of DNA damage signaling pathways, a hallmark pathological process associated with increased post-stroke brain damage. Co-immunostaining with cell-type-specific markers showed predominant S9.6 colocalization with NeuN-positive neurons (Figure S2A) followed by Iba1-positive microglia (Figure S2B), with minimal signal in GFAP-positive astrocytes (Figure S2C). These findings suggest that ischemia disrupts R-loop homeostasis primarily in neurons (Figure S2).

Genome-wide R-loops mapping revealed a significant accumulation of R-loops at several genomic locations

Building on the DNA dot blotting results showing enhanced accumulation of unscheduled R-loops after stroke, we performed DRIPc-seq to characterize their genome-wide distribution. We observed a significant increase in R-loops peaks spanning the promoter, gene body, and terminator regions in the peri-infarct cortex following transient MCAO (240 upregulated and 540 downregulated at 6 h; 12,578 upregulated and 892 downregulated at 12 h, and 2,543 upregulated and 2,286 downregulated at 24 h, log2fold change ≥ 2.5 and FDR < 0.05) compared with sham (Figure 1D). CPM-normalized DRIPc-seq read density plotted from −5 kb upstream of the TSS (5′ end) through the gene body to +5 kb downstream of the TES (3′ end) is shown in Figure 1E.

Characterization of overall distribution of R-loops in the genome showed that the peaks increase significantly in the promoters (by 1.4-fold at 12 h and 1.9-fold at 24 h) and terminators (1.3-fold at 12 h and 1.7-fold at 24 h) following focal ischemia compared with sham, indicating a shift of R-loop burden toward transcription start and end regions as reperfusion time progresses (Figure 1F). The top 10 genes ranked by fold-change in R-loop enrichment at each time point are listed in Tables S2–S4. These results suggest that transcriptional reprogramming following ischemia promotes R-loop accumulation at distinct genomic sites during the acute phase of stroke.

Elevated promoter-associated R-loop peaks are linked to several post-stroke pathological processes

R-loop signal intensity distribution in the standardized gene transcription unit at 6, 12, and 24 h reperfusion following transient MCAO compared with sham is shown in Figures 2A–2C. Gene Ontology analysis showed that elevated unscheduled R-loops between 6 and 24 h of reperfusion (Figures 2D–2F) in the peri-infarct cortex were linked to several key biological pathways involved in post-stroke pathophysiology. At 6 h of reperfusion, R-loops were predominantly enriched at genes regulating axonogenesis, neuronal action potential, and chromatin remodeling, indicating alterations in neuronal excitability and chromatin architecture immediately after stroke (Figure 2D). At 12 h of reperfusion, the R-loop enrichment pattern broadened to include genes involved in apoptotic signaling, alongside persistent enrichment of axonogenesis and chromatin remodeling, suggesting that R-loop accumulation may contribute to activation of cell death programs (Figure 2E). At 24 h of reperfusion, genes associated R-loops shifted toward pathways involved in synapse organization, axon guidance, and continued chromatin remodeling, highlighting impaired neuronal connectivity and maladaptive structural plasticity during the later stages of reperfusion (Figure 2F). Collectively, these findings suggest that genes with elevated-promoter-associated R-loop peaks dynamically engage distinct injury-related pathways over time, initially impacting neuronal excitability, progressively driving apoptotic processes, and ultimately affecting synaptic integrity and network rewiring, thereby contributing to the evolving molecular landscape of post-stroke brain damage.

Stroke promotes enrichment of unscheduled R-loops at long noncoding RNA genes

To determine whether noncoding regions of the genome are vulnerable to R-loop accumulation after stroke, we examined R-loop enrichment across annotated long noncoding RNA (lncRNA) loci. Stroke induced a time-dependent increase in unscheduled R-loop signals at lncRNA loci, with pronounced enrichment at 12 and 24 h of reperfusion compared with sham (Figures S3A and S3B), suggesting sustained transcriptional stress and impaired resolution of RNA:DNA hybrids in noncoding genomic domains. Given the regulatory role of lncRNAs in chromatin organization, transcriptional control, and neuronal stress response, elevated R-loops at these loci may contribute to widespread dysregulation of gene expression patterns following stroke. Discussion

Ischemic stroke triggers widespread changes in chromatin accessibility and transcriptional regulation that profoundly influence the post-stroke pathological processes. However, the mechanistic link between dysregulated transcription and DNA damage remains unclear. R-loops are stable RNA:DNA hybrids that regulate transcription by modulating promoter activation/repression, RNA polymerase II pausing and termination, enhancer-promoter communication, and local chromatin organization.11,12,13,14 We presently show that R-loop dynamics alter during the critical window between the acute reperfusion phase (6 h) and the evolving damage phase (24 h), during which neuronal survival under ischemic stress becomes decisive. By mapping genome-wide, strand-specific R-loops across these time points of reperfusion after focal ischemia, we identified previously unrecognized patterns of aberrant R-loops, which might indicate nucleic acid stress. We also identified that many biological pathways associated with R-loops altered during the acute period after stroke are putative mediators of ischemic brain damage.

Post-stroke DNA damage is a major driver of ischemic pathology, as the rapid accumulation of base lesions and strand breaks during reperfusion activates multiple pro-death signaling pathways, especially up to 24 h of reperfusion.15 Within this window, reactive oxygen species generated during reperfusion promote oxidative base lesions and single and double-strand DNA breaks in peri-infarct neurons.15 While multiple DNA repair pathways, including base excision repair and DSB repair, are promptly activated, the severity of post-ischemic oxidative stress often surpasses their functional capacity, resulting in sustained DNA damage.15,16 During the early phase of reperfusion, neurons in the ischemic cortex typically exhibit substantial DNA damage, with active repair responses still capable of influencing survival.17 By 24 h of reperfusion, extensive DNA strand breaks and ongoing DNA damage signaling lead to apoptotic and necrotic neuronal death, thereby promoting infarct expansion and worsening of functional outcomes.15,18 Thus, the evolving phase of reperfusion represents a decisive period during which the balance between DNA damage and repair capacities determines whether vulnerable neurons recover or die. DNA damage marked by abundant DSBs is the most extensively studied marker for active post-stroke DNA damage.15 We observed a pronounced increase in R-loops between 6 and 24 h of reperfusion, together with increased colocalization of S9.6 and γH2AX signals, indicating that genomic regions of elevated R-loop accumulation coincide with sites of DSBs in DNA. Given that unscheduled R-loops can destabilize the genome by exposing single-stranded DNA and interfering with transcriptional progression, leading to DSBs, the association between rising R-loop levels and increased γH2AX signaling suggests that R-loop accumulation might contribute to early post-ischemic DNA damage.

Genome-wide profiling studies showed that R-loop formation is far more widespread in mammalian genomes than previously recognized, with these structures detected at tens of thousands of genomic loci.19,20,21 Consistent with these reports, we observed a dynamic and site-specific shift in R-loops-prone regions at thousands of genomic loci in the ischemic brain as the reperfusion time progressed. This indicates a previously unrecognized RNA:DNA hybrid-mediated, transcription-associated genomic stress after stroke. The metagene profile shows that R-loop signal is not static across reperfusion time points. Instead, there is a pronounced increase in R-loop occupancy at 12 h after MCAO, followed by a relative reduction or redistribution by 24 h. This suggests that R-loop homeostasis is highly sensitive to the ischemia-reperfusion phase, with 12 h representing a potential acute stress window of widespread R-loop accumulation, while 24 h may reflect partial resolution, transcriptional suppression, cellular vulnerability, or redistribution of R-loops to specific genomic regions. Gene Ontology (GO) analysis of unscheduled R-loops at promoter regions revealed distinct, time-dependent pathway engagement after stroke. Between 6 and 24 h of reperfusion, R-loops accumulated at genes controlling neuronal excitability, axonal/synaptic remodeling, cell death, and chromatin organization, showing both phase-specific and shared pathway signatures. These findings indicate that ischemia-induced unscheduled R-loops are enriched at genes regulating key post-stroke injury and repair pathways, linking unscheduled R-loops to the evolving molecular landscape of post-stroke brain damage.

LncRNAs regulate the function of several DNA-binding and chromatin-modifying proteins by acting as scaffolds and molecular decoys depending on the context.22,23 Emerging evidence indicates a bidirectional relationship in which lncRNAs regulate R-loop homeostasis, while R-loops, in turn, modulate lncRNA biogenesis and function.24 R-loops at promoters, terminators, and enhancers act as intrinsic RNA polymerase II promoters, driving antisense lncRNA and enhancer RNA transcription at many mammalian genes.25 Intriguingly, lncRNAs use R-loops as functional partners. For example, antisense lncRNA DSP-AS1 forms promoter R-loops in cis to recruit TET3 and promote DNA demethylation and transcription of the neighboring DSP gene in wound healing.26 LncRNA lnc530 binds directly to R-loops and assembles a DDX5/TDP-43 complex that limits unscheduled R-loop accumulation and guards genome stability in embryonic stem cells.27 Dysregulated transcription of extended lncRNAs increases their susceptibility to R-loop formation, which potently induces replication stress and DNA damage.28 These results indicate the role of lncRNAs in R-loop homeostasis as well as suggest that aberrant lncRNA-R-loop crosstalk induces DNA damage and compromises genomic integrity. We observed a significant enrichment of R-loops at multiple lncRNA loci between 12 and 24 h of reperfusion, which indicates that lncRNA coding regions of the DNA are critical targets of R-loop dysregulation in post-ischemic brain.

Overall, our data indicate that stroke induces global accumulation of aberrant R-loops in the ischemic brain, and these structures are associated with DNA damage signaling. Additionally, stroke promotes a dynamic shift in genome-wide R-loop landscape across the evolving phase of reperfusion, with R-loop-enriched loci mapping to genes that regulate biological pathways involved in ischemic pathophysiology. Notably, lncRNA loci emerge as prominent R-loop-prone regions in the ischemic brain, highlighting noncoding genomic elements as hotspots of unscheduled R-loops after stroke. Thus, targeting post-stroke unscheduled R-loops might be a promising strategy to improve stroke recovery. A limitation of this study is the use of only male mice. Given the influence of sex on stroke pathophysiology, future studies with the inclusion of female mice are needed to determine whether stroke-induced changes in R-loop homeostasis are sex-dependent.

Materials and methods

Focal ischemia was induced in adult male C57BL/6J mice by 1 h intraluminal MCAO under isoflurane anesthesia, and the sham mice underwent identical surgery without occlusion.29 Mice were euthanized at 6, 12, and 24 h reperfusion. All procedures were approved by the Research Animal Resources and Care Committee at the University of Wisconsin-Madison. Global R-loops were assessed by S9.6 dot blot in peri-infarct cortical DNA. Genome-wide R-loops were profiled by DNA-RNA immunoprecipitation followed by cDNA sequencing (DRIPc-seq). DAVID bioinformatics database was used to conduct GO analysis.30 Please refer to the Supplemental Methods for details.
