Bacillus subtilis Intraspecies Interactions Shape Probiotic Activity Against Salmonella Typhimurium
Authors: Eva Kovačec, Barbara Kraigher, Eli Podnar, Bram Lories, Hans Steenackers, Ines Mandic‐Mulec
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTCommercial probiotics are often formulated as multi‐strain cocktails, but the effects of social interactions, particularly between strains of a species, are often neglected, despite their potential to contribute to higher‐order interactions where these interactions could affect those with a third party. In this study, we investigated the probiotic potential of a collection of Bacillus subtilis strains against Salmonella Typhimurium in single‐strain and mixed cultures. The results indicate a promising probiotic potential of B. subtilis as 38 out of 39 strains significantly inhibited the growth of S. Typhimurium. Next, we tested the effect of mixing B. subtilis strains that differ in their inhibitory potency against S. Typhimurium. The results show that strong inhibition by one strain can be significantly reduced by mixing with a less effective strain. Moreover, mixing similarly effective strains mostly resulted in a decreased growth inhibition of the pathogen. Additionally, we found a group of highly aggressive strains, which completely eliminated other B. subtilis strains in the two‐strain mixtures. Overall, this work shows that intraspecies interactions between B. subtilis strains can significantly alter the probiotic effect against S. Typhimurium, which is of great importance for future research on the development of multi‐strain probiotics.Keywords: Bacillus subtilis , higher‐order interactions, intraspecies interactions, multi‐strain probiotic, probiotics, Salmonella enterica Typhimurium

Introduction

Bacteria from the genus Salmonella represent the second most frequently reported causative agent of foodborne outbreaks in the EU. The European Food Safety Authority has estimated that the overall economic burden of human salmonellosis could be as high as 3 billion EUR per year (EFSA and ECDC 2017, 2021). The intensive use of antibiotics has driven the evolution of multi‐resistant strains that are no longer susceptible to the most commonly used antibiotics, posing the need to develop alternative antimicrobials (Fàbrega and Vila 2013). Probiotic bacteria represent a promising alternative as they are predicted to combat gastrointestinal pathogens through mechanisms that directly impact pathogen's cross‐feeding activities, competition for nutrients/space, production of antimicrobials and quorum sensing in addition to shaping host–microbe interactions (Grumet, Tromp, and Stiegelbauer 2020).

Probiotics can be divided into mono‐strain or multi‐strain/multi‐species probiotic formulations. It is often assumed that mixtures of different probiotic strains have a stronger effect on pathogen performance and treat a wider range of diseases (Kwoji et al. 2021). It was suggested that probiotic mixtures can be more effective in inhibiting the pathogenic bacteria than single‐strain probiotics (Chapman, Gibson, and Rowland 2011, 2012). However, although mixtures of probiotic strains may have a broader efficacy spectrum and occupy a broader niche space, their synergistic effects cannot be guaranteed. Hence, it is important to carefully design mixed‐species/mixed‐strains probiotics to avoid cross‐inhibition, even between closely related strains (Be'er et al. 2009; Chapman, Gibson, and Rowland 2012).

Many extended reviews are dealing with the question as to whether single‐strain probiotics or multi‐strain probiotic mixtures are better or equivalent for efficacy and in summary they give conflicting findings (Timmerman et al. 2004; Chapman, Gibson, and Rowland 2011; McFarland 2021). Some studies conclude that probiotic mixtures show greater than mono‐strain probiotics efficacy (Timmerman et al. 2004; Chapman, Gibson, and Rowland 2011). In contrast, McFarland (2021), who compared studies in vivo, and Tejero‐Sariñena et al. (2013), who conducted research in vitro, stated that multi‐strain mixtures are not significantly more effective than single‐strain probiotics. Overall, there is a lack of literature comparing single‐strain and multi‐strain mixtures in regard to their antagonistic activity against pathogens (Ouwehand et al. 2018) and research addressing which species within a mixture enhance the preparations' effectiveness are scarce (Chapman, Gibson, and Rowland 2011; Kwoji et al. 2021; McFarland 2021). Commercial probiotics are often formulated as multi‐strain products; however, their survival and efficacy have often been investigated at the single‐strain products (Forssten and Ouwehand 2017). Recently, Fredua‐Agyeman et al. (2017) reported that none of the multi‐strain probiotic products available in the United Kingdom contained all the labelled strains, possibly due to competitive social interactions between the strains.

Bacillus sp. are attractive as probiotic supplements due to the production of spores, which have a long shelf life, survive low pH in the human digestive tract and are heat‐resistant. Bacillus sp. therefore usually have lower effective doses than those required for lactic acid bacteria (Cutting 2011). Many strains of the genus Bacillus inhibit the growth of Salmonella in vitro (Wolfenden et al. 2010; Prieto et al. 2014), but this property is strain‐specific and can be the result of the action of antimicrobial agents (Tellez et al. 2012; Erega et al. 2021, 2022; Podnar et al. 2022) or limiting access to the nutrients. B. subtilis is also known as a probiotic that inhibits infections caused by pathogenic bacteria such as S. enterica , C. perfringens , C. jejuni and E. coli (La Ragione et al. 2001; Cutting 2011; Thirabunyanon and Thongwittaya 2012; Hayashi et al. 2018; Šimunović et al. 2022). B. subtilis can produce numerous reported antibiotics, as 4%–5% of its genome is allocated to the production of antimicrobials (Stein 2005; Chapman et al. 2013). For example, the well‐characterised lipopeptide biosurfactant surfactin inhibits S. enterica adhesion to PVC and disrupts the already formed biofilms of Salmonella (Mireles, Toguchi, and Harshey 2001; Zezzi do Valle Gomes and Nitschke 2012), while bacteriocin Bac‐IB17 possesses inhibitory effect against Salmonella typhi (Ansari et al. 2012). Additionally, it was recently shown that the production of the polyketide bacillaene underlies the strong inhibitory activity of B. subtilis strain PS‐216 against enteropathogenic bacteria such as S. Typhimurium and Campylobacter jejuni (Erega et al. 2021, 2022; Podnar et al. 2022).

B. subtilis strains are also known for their fascinating variety of social behaviours (Kalamara et al. 2018). The recently discovered kin discrimination between B. subtilis conspecifics isolated from a soil aggregate revealed that these bacteria selectively direct the antagonistic behaviour towards the non‐kin members of their own species (Stefanic et al. 2015) or closely related Bacillus species (Lyons and Kolter 2017) according to their genetic similarity. This implies that closely related strains (kin) do not attack each other, whereas those that are less related (non‐kin) do. This behaviour was identified as kin discrimination and linked to specific genetic loci encoding attack and defence factors, genes involved in surface modifications and/or extracellular matrix components (Lyons et al. 2016). Kin groups were defined phenotypically, i.e. strains were classified as kin (here referred to as compatible) if they were able to merge their swarms at the swarm encounter area and as non‐kin (incompatible) if a distinct boundary was formed between swarms. Moreover, a close kinship was also reflected in intimate spatial mixing of highly related kin strains and competitive spatial exclusion of the less‐related non‐kin B. subtilis strains within one swarm (Stefanic et al. 2015, 2021; Lyons et al. 2016; Bolješić et al. 2022; Kraigher et al. 2022). Although intraspecies interactions can play a crucial role in the exclusion of a virulent strain (Leslie et al. 2021), they are rarely considered in probiotic mixtures; they may contribute to higher‐order interactions and modify the intended effect of the consortium (Verdú et al. 2023).

We here investigated a collection of 39 B. subtilis strains isolated from soil microscale (from two 1 cm3 soil samples) to test their inhibitory potential against S. Typhimurium. Previous work showed that these microscale strains diversified into three quorum sensing types (pherotypes), three ecotypes and 12 kin types and that these traits shape the outcome of their pairwise competition (Stefanic and Mandic‐Mulec 2009; Stefanic et al. 2015, 2021; Bolješić et al. 2022; Kraigher et al. 2022). In this work, we aimed to (1) evaluate the strength of antagonism against S. Typhimurium for each of 39 single B. subtilis strains; (2) determine to what extent mixing of strains differing in the strength of antagonism influences the overall inhibition activity against S. Typhimurium; (3) investigate whether by increasing the number of selected strains with the highest probiotic potential in multi‐strain probiotic mixtures will increase the antagonistic strength compared to single strains; and (4) verify the effect of swarming compatibility/kinship between the strains on the inhibition efficacy of the mixtures.

Experimental Procedures

Bacterial Strains

The bacterial strains used in the study are listed in Table S1. All 39 B. subtilis wild‐type strains were isolated from two 1‐cm3 soil samples sampled at the river bank in Slovenia (Stefanic and Mandic‐Mulec 2009). The reference strain B. subtilis PS‐216 carried resistance to chloramphenicol and was used in experiments with mixtures of multiple B. subtilis strains. In the experiments where the effects of aggressive PS‐strains were tested against other PS‐strains, the target strains also carried chloramphenicol resistance marker to allow their CFU quantification. Salmonella enterica ssp. enterica serovar Typhimurium SL1344 carried the chloramphenicol resistance marker.

B. subtilis and S. Typhimurium Culture Preparation for Static Assay

Strains were maintained at −80°C in 15% (w/w) glycerol. Bacteria were first revitalised on tryptic soy agar (TSA, Conda, Spain) plates. Grown colonies served as inoculum for overnight cultures, which were incubated at 37°C in tryptic soy broth (TSB, Conda, Spain), shaking at 200 rpm for 16 h. Where needed, the media was supplemented with appropriate antibiotic: ampicillin (Amp) of 100 μg/mL or chloramphenicol (Cm) of 10 μg/mL.

Evaluation of B. subtilis PS‐strain effects on S. Typhimurium growth in static assay was performed as described in Podnar et al. (2022). Briefly, overnight cultures were centrifuged and pellets were resuspended in fresh TSB medium to remove antibiotics. In order to standardise the number of bacteria (~107 cells/mL), the absorbance (A650nm) was adjusted to ~0.1 a.u. and ~ 0.2 a.u for S. Typhimurium and all B. subtilis strains, respectively. S. Typhimurium was mixed with different B. subtilis strains (individual or selected multiple‐strain mixtures) at a 1:1 ratio. Further, 100 μL of co‐culture sample was transferred into the wells of the 96‐well microtiter plate with F‐bottom (Cellstar, Greiner Bio‐One, Austria) and incubated in the dark for 24 h at 37°C in static conditions. The control monoculture of S. Typhimurium was prepared at a 1:1 ratio with fresh TSB medium.

Bacterial Growth Determination

For enumeration of bacterial populations, the number of cells at the beginning and at the end of the experiment in mono‐ and co‐cultures was determined by CFU counts. Samples were disrupted by vigorous pipetting and vortexing and then diluted and plated on LB agar plates or on LB plates with appropriate antibiotics to determine CFU/mL. All results are presented as log10 CFU/mL.

Statistical Analyses

All the experiments were performed in at least three independent biological replicates; each biological replicate also included at least two technical repetitions. All data are presented as mean values ± standard error. Bacterial counts were analysed by Student's t‐test for paired‐sample comparisons or by one‐way analyses of variance (ANOVA) with accompanying Dunnett's or Tukey's post hoc test (p ≤ 0.05) for multi‐sample comparison. All statistical analyses were performed using Statistica software (Statsoft 8, USA).

Results

Distribution of Antagonistic Potential Against S. Typhimurium Among Sympatric B. subtilis Isolates

To evaluate the distribution of antagonistic potential among B. subtilis isolates, we used the collection of 39 B. subtilis strains isolated from the soil microscale (Stefanic and Mandic‐Mulec 2009). Each isolate was co‐cultured with S. Typhimurium in a static assay in tryptic soy broth (TSB) (Podnar et al. 2022), and survival of the pathogen was determined by plating CFU on antibiotic agar.

In this study, B. subtilis PS‐216 served as a reference strain as its antagonism against S. Typhimurium (Podnar et al. 2022) and its probiotic potential in broilers (Šimunović et al. 2022) was already documented. The results show that 38 out of the 39 tested B. subtilis strains significantly inhibited S. Typhimurium growth (Figure 1). Only B. subtilis strain PS‐261 had no detectable effect on the pathogen's growth (Figure 1). The reduction of S. Typhimurium growth in liquid co‐culture ranged from 1.2 to 2.6 log (CFU/mL) as compared to S. Typhimurium monoculture. Based on these results, and by comparison to the reference PS‐216 strain, we divided B. subtilis isolates according to the strength of S. Typhimurium growth inhibition into three groups: strong strains (n = 1; > 2.4 log inhibition), intermediate strains (n = 34; between 2.4 and 1.6 log inhibition) and weak strains (n = 4; < 1.6 log inhibition) (Figure 1). To simplify, the strains that have statistically significant higher activity than reference strain PS‐216 are considered as strong, and strains with a statistically significant lower activity than B. subtilis PS‐216 are considered as weak strains.

Mixing Intermediately Efficient Strain PS‐216 With Strong or Intermediate PS‐Strains Reduced the Total Inhibition Efficiency

The popularity of using multi‐species probiotics has been increasing recently, and it is generally assumed that inoculants with increased genetic diversity will improve the total probiotic activity as compared to single‐strain probiotics (Kwoji et al. 2021). Although it is generally recommended to characterise the probiotic species and their relationships before combining them in a multi‐strain probiotic preparation, not many producers consider this recommendation (Fredua‐Agyeman et al. 2017). We therefore aimed to examine how mixing the PS‐216 reference strain with other B. subtilis strains differing in strength of antagonism against S. Typhimurium will change the probiotic efficacy of the mixture in comparison to single strains. In addition, we aimed to evaluate whether the compatibility (defined in swarming experiments as kin vs. non‐kin interactions) of these B. subtilis strains with strain PS‐216 will have an impact on the antagonistic strength of the mixture (Table S2). We hypothesised that in the case of compatible strains, there will be no strong effect on the antagonism of the strongest antagonist, while in incompatible strain mixtures, the antagonistic effect will decrease. To evaluate the above predictions, we mixed various B. subtilis isolates with PS‐216 at a 1:1 ratio, taking into account their antagonistic activity against S. Typhimurium (Figure 1—weak, intermediate, strong), as well as their compatibility with PS‐216 (Stefanic et al. 2015).

In the case of strong strains, mixing reduced the inhibitory activity compared to the strongest strain, which can be seen in Figure 2 and Table 1. Surprisingly, the inhibitory activity of the mixtures was even reduced as compared to the individual activity of the weaker strain PS‐216 alone (Table 1). This suggests that the intraspecies competition between the Bacillus strains could reduce the interspecies competitive potential of the mixtures.

Interestingly, mixing a weak strain with the intermediate PS‐216 had also a distinct effect, as the total inhibition activity of the mixtures increased significantly for the four tested strain combinations (significance for two out of four strains) but only when looking from the perspective of the weak strain. Specifically, we observed an increase in inhibition as compared to the inhibitory activity of the individual weak strain and a decrease in the cumulative activity compared to PS‐216 monoculture (Table 1). In all tested cases, the antagonistic strength of PS‐216 thus decreased in mixtures with a weak strain, suggesting that a weak strain dilutes out the PS‐216 antagonism.

When pairs of two intermediate strains were mixed, the inhibitory effect of the individual strains was again higher than that of mixtures of strains. Addition of PS‐216 to different intermediate strains reduced the total inhibitory activity of the mixtures, as compared to the individual strain, irrespective of the swarming compatibility between the strains (Table 1). On average, this observed inhibitory activity was lower than when strong strains were mixed with the intermediate PS‐216 (Figure 2). Similar reductions were observed when comparing to PS‐216 alone (Table 1). It is compelling that compatible strains also interfere with each other in competition against other species. This may arise from the use of different model systems (swarm encounter test) for determining compatibility groups compared to the approach employed in this study (static assay) but may be due to unknown interactions that are different in communities with three members versus interactions in two‐strain co‐cultures. Interestingly, of the tested combinations, only the PS‐108/PS‐216 incompatible pair preserved the efficacy of the individual strains. It is possible that a complete exclusion of one strain by another occurred early during competition, which we have further investigated in a section below or that this pair did not show adversary towards each other in the liquid culture, which is less likely.

Mixing Highly Efficient Strains Did Not Improve the Total Probiotic Potential

In this part, we have studied the effect of mixing two or three strongly antagonistic B. subtilis strains on the inhibitory activity against S. Typhimurium compared to the individual strains. We found that mixing the strongest strain in our collection, PS‐15, with PS‐263 and/or PS‐218, which had a slightly lower inhibitory activity, significantly decreased the antagonistic activity of the mixture compared to PS‐15 alone (Figure 3A). However, the antagonistic activity of PS‐263 and PS‐218 was not significantly changed in the presence of PS‐15 (Figure 3A), which might be explained by the lower individual activity of these two strains compared to PS‐15 and their competitive advantage in strain mixtures.

Furthermore, we were interested if the previously determined swarming compatibility between the strains plays a role in reducing the effect of the most effective PS‐strains in our static assay. Within the collection of PS‐strains, PS‐15 is not compatible with any other strain, while PS‐263 and PS‐218 have each in our collection one compatible strain (Table S3; PS‐20 and PS‐217, respectively) (Stefanic et al. 2015). As the strains PS‐20 and PS‐217 are both intermediately effective strains, we selected two additional intermediate strains PS‐216 and PS‐68 that are incompatible with both PS‐263 and PS‐218. The results indicate that the total inhibition activity in mixtures is again lower in comparison to the intermediate strain alone; however, it was slightly less reduced when highly effective strains were mixed with their compatible intermediately effective strain than when two incompatible strains were present in the mixture. This suggests that competition with incompatible strains might negatively influence the antagonism against the third competitor when mixing strongly inhibiting strains with intermediates (Figure 3B).

Intraspecies Interactions Between B. subtilis Isolates Are Intricate and Can Be Extremely Competitive

To further dig into the importance of interactions between B. subtilis soil isolates in co‐cultures with S. Typhimurium in a static assay, we followed the fitness outcome for each of the two B. subtilis strains in the mixture with S. Typhimurium. We hereto focused on combinations with the PS‐216 reference strain. PS‐216 was labelled with Cm resistance (and the red fluorescent protein mKate2), while the partner PS‐strain was unlabelled (wild type), which enabled us to differentiate between the two B. subtilis strains in the mixture.

In all tested combinations, both B. subtilis strains, regardless of whether they were compatible or incompatible, were still detectable at the end of the experiment at around 107–108 CFU/mL, except for the mixture of PS‐216 and PS‐108 where only the latter was detected after 24 h and was referred to as a ‘killer’ strain (Figure 4A). This finding supports the observation from previous experiments where the PS‐108/PS‐216 incompatible pair preserved the efficacy of the individual strain PS‐108 (Figure 2). However, when both strains are still present at significant density in co‐culture, the CFU of each strain in B. subtilis co‐cultures is not a reliable predictor of the antagonism against the pathogenic bacteria as antagonism may be shaped by higher‐order interactions involving a third party (Verdú et al. 2023).

As the most remarkable competition outcome in static liquid culture was the complete exclusion of PS‐216 from the mixture by the incompatible ‘killer’ strain PS‐108, we tested four additional strains from the collection that are compatible with PS‐108 but not PS‐216 (PS‐109, PS‐119, PS‐130 and PS‐131) (Stefanic et al. 2015) for antagonism against PS‐216. The results indicate a complete exclusion of PS‐216 in all tested cases (Figure 4B), suggesting that compatible strains might possess a similar array of antimicrobial weapons for intraspecific combat. In addition, we showed here that the killer strain PS‐108 can outcompete many other PS‐strains in a static assay (Figure 4C), emphasising its general antagonistic effect against B. subtilis strains from the microenvironment collection.

Discussion

Our study shows a widespread probiotic potential of B. subtilis isolates as 30 out of 38 tested B. subtilis isolates showed comparable or even stronger antagonism to S. Typhimurium as PS‐216 (most strains ~2 log, strong strains up to 2.6 log). This is consistent with earlier studies showing that B. subtilis is very successful in competitively excluding Salmonella in vitro (Thirabunyanon and Thongwittaya 2012; Ramlucken et al. 2020) and in the chickens' gut (La Ragione and Woodward 2003; Knap et al. 2011; Shivaramaiah et al. 2011; Hayashi et al. 2018). A study testing the antagonism of 117 isolates of Bacillus species from chicken gastrointestinal tracts against seven foodborne pathogens including S. Typhimurium identified only 10 Bacillus isolates with high antimicrobial activity (with an inhibition zone exceeding 10 mm on agar medium) (Thirabunyanon and Thongwittaya 2012), suggesting that the description of antimicrobial potency of an isolate is relative and influenced by the specific assay employed in the study and specific strain/species. The novelty of our study is that we provide evidence on the variation in antagonistic strength against S. Typhimurium among sympatric isolates of B. subtilis in relation to antagonism against a pathogen, which to our knowledge has not been studied yet. This is especially important also because bacterial strains can readily diversify within their ecological niches, thus shaping survival and competitiveness of the colonising species (Hibbing et al. 2010). Our data also suggest that the strain‐specific properties appear to be the main factor shaping the antagonism's strength, which is in line with the generally accepted view that the outcomes generated with a specific probiotic strain cannot be generalised to all probiotics (Grumet, Tromp, and Stiegelbauer 2020).

Intraspecific interactions can be synergistic, additive or antagonistic with respect to the probiotic potential (Kwoji et al. 2021). We discovered that the interactions between sympatric Bacillus strains are neither synergistic nor clearly additive but mostly antagonistic and cause a negative effect on the sum efficacy. When strong strains were mixed with the intermediate PS‐216 reference strain, the overall inhibitory effect of the mixture against S. Typhimurium was lower compared to even the least effective intermediate strain. These results are pointing out that interactions between strains in a probiotic mixture can reduce the wanted effect of the most powerful strain, which is consistent with the findings of Leslie et al. (2021). The observed outcome might be explained by competition between the sympatric strains, such as exploitative competition for limiting resources (nutrients/space) and interference competition achieved through the action of different antimicrobial metabolites (Mitri and Foster 2013; Coyte and Rakoff‐Nahoum 2019). Next to competitive interactions, there can also be other mechanisms involved in reduced probiotic activity in the mixtures. For example, one probiotic strain could enhance the tolerance of Salmonella to the inhibitory effect of another probiotic strain or one probiotic strain might inhibit the competitive mechanisms that another probiotic strain uses against Salmonella. Our results furthermore show that intermediately antagonistic strains alone have a greater effect on the inhibition of S. Typhimurium than two intermediate strains in a mixture, which probably engage in intraspecific competition. The effect of mixing weaker strains with the intermediate reference PS‐216, on the other hand, appeared to be additive—meaning that the inhibition activity lies in between both strains in the mixture. Altogether, this suggests that a stronger competition towards S. Typhimurium might imply also a stronger antagonistic intraspecies interaction leading to a lower total mixture efficacy. We have further studied the mixing of two and even three strong B. subtilis antagonists in mixture and found that mixing significantly decreased the antagonistic activity compared to the individual effect of the strongest strain from our collection, namely, PS‐15. This is in line with many studies, which indicated that the choice of strain, and not the number of strains, is the most important factor impacting probiotic efficacy, as the action of probiotics is both strain‐ and disease‐specific (Collado, Meriluoto, and Salminen 2007; Tejero‐Sariñena et al. 2013; Yoon et al. 2014; McFarland, Evans, and Goldstein 2018; McFarland 2021). However, there has been large inconsistency in the effects of the probiotic mixtures in the literature (Lambo, Chang, and Liu 2021), and many extended reviews comparing single‐strain probiotics or multi‐strain probiotic mixtures are revealing conflicting findings (Timmerman et al. 2004; Chapman, Gibson, and Rowland 2011; McFarland 2021).

The impact of kinship of the B. subtilis strains (swarm compatibility vs. incompatibility) on the efficacy of the strain combination appears to be dependent on the inhibitory strength of the individual B. subtilis strains. The analysis of four housekeeping genes (gyrA, rpoB, dnaJ, recA) (Stefanic et al. 2015) as well as later studies of ANI (average nucleotide identity) (Stefanic et al. 2021) revealed that strains used in our study are highly similar when the housekeeping genes or the core genomes are compared. However, B. subtilis kin (=compatible) strains had ANI between 99.93% and 99.99%, while less genetically similar strains named non‐kin (=incompatible) had ANI between 98.73% and 98.84%. Lyons et al. (2016) have shown that B. subtilis genomes contain a multitude of kin discrimination‐associated loci that contribute to the combinatorial system of compatibility between strains. When highly effective B. subtilis strains were mixed with compatible/incompatible intermediate strains, the compatible strains reduced the activity of the mixture to a lower extent than non‐compatible strains. This suggests that competition with incompatible strains, which may fire intraspecies toxin attack, could negatively influence the antagonism against the third pathogenic competitor. When mixing intermediate strains together, this impact of compatibility on the antagonistic activity against S. Typhimurium was not detected, as the negative effect of mixing on probiotic potential was comparable in two compatible and two incompatible mixtures. However, the static assay used in this study presents a different experimental model compared to that of Stefanic et al. (2015), where kin groups (compatibility types) were determined by a swarming encounter assay. Thus, growth conditions could affect the level of competition, as has been observed before. Indeed, in a swarming competition assay with non‐kin B. subtilis strains, a complete spatial exclusion of strains was observed in a mixture (Kraigher et al. 2022), while in a pellicle formed by two non‐kin strains, both strains persisted (especially when spores were used as inoculum), albeit in different ratios than that in the initial inoculum (1:1) (Bolješić et al. 2022).

Our data further show that the fitness impact that both B. subtilis strains have on each other in the presence of S. Typhimurium strongly depends on the specific B. subtilis strain combination. In the majority of the combinations with the reference PS‐216 strain, both B. subtilis strains were still detectable and present in similar densities. Our results show less intense competition (as estimated from CFU counts) between two non‐kin strains in the presence of the third competitor, namely, S. Typhimurium, and in a different model system as compared to competition previously observed between two non‐kin B. subtilis swarms or in pellicles (Bolješić et al. 2022; Kraigher et al. 2022). This could be at least in part explained by the metabolic burden of the fluorescent protein mKate2 in PS‐216, which may have decreased the competitiveness of PS‐216, while in previous experiments, both strains in the mixtures were labelled with fluorescent proteins.

The co‐existence of three genotypes with different competitiveness is also consistent with uneven rock–paper–scissors models, which predict the co‐existence of three genotypes even if one genotype is weaker than the other two (Menezes, Moura, and Pereira 2019). One of the initially tested B. subtilis strains (PS‐108), however, completely outcompeted PS‐216. Also, the four strains from the collection, which are compatible with the killer strain PS‐108, turned out to completely exclude PS‐216, suggesting that compatible aggressive strains might possess a similar arsenal of antimicrobial weapons. This is in agreement with the genomic analyses, which have shown that more closely related strains share a greater number of antagonistic genes (responsible for the synthesis of antibiotics/bacteriocins) than more phylogenetically distant strains of one species (Lyons and Kolter 2017). Finally, we showed here that the killer strain PS‐108 can also outcompete many other PS‐strains in a static assay, emphasising its general antagonistic effect against other B. subtilis strains.

Overall, we can conclude that mixing of strains can decrease or increase the final probiotic effect, depending on the efficacy of the individual strains in the mixture and their interactions. In general, our results indicate that random mixing of strains will most likely result in a decreased probiotic activity. This means that besides considering individual properties of each strain, it is essential to test their efficacy in combination when designing probiotic cocktails. Our findings are based on sympatric B. subtilis isolates and could also therefore be indicative of the impact on the efficacy of evolutionary probiotic diversification in the gut after administration (Mallon, Van Elsas, and Salles 2015). Moreover, our study is important because it demonstrates that characterisation of individual strains should be complemented by studies in mixed cultures to get better insights into their potential functioning in complex microbial consortia. Our findings are among the first, demonstrating a negative impact of intraspecies interactions on the antagonistic activity against pathogens of potential probiotic strains. Although the conclusions based on in vitro experiments have to be taken with caution and validated by in vivo experiments, this work provides novel insights that are important for the design of multi‐strain probiotic consortia.

Conclusions

By following the ‘bottom‐up’ approach, we first evaluated the potential antagonistic effect of individual strains from the collection of sympatric B. subtilis soil isolates and demonstrated their varied and widespread growth inhibition potential against S. Typhimurium SL1344. Next, by mixing Bacillus strains with different or similar inhibition activity, we showed that, contrary to the synergistic assumptions, the total inhibitory effect was mostly reduced and was often lower than that of the least effective strain by itself. These findings, together with the identification of highly aggressive strains that can completely eliminate other B. subtilis strains, highlight the importance of evaluating intraspecies interactions when designing mixed probiotic preparations.
