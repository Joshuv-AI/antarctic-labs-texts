On the Computation of Newton Polytopes of Eliminants
Rafael Mohr, Yulia Mukhina

Abstract
For systems of polynomial equations, we study the problem of computing the Newton polytope of their eliminants. As was shown by Esterov and Khovanskii, such Newton polytopes are mixed fiber polytopes of the Newton polytopes of the input equations. We use their results in combination with mixed subdivisions to design an algorithm computing these special polytopes. We demonstrate the increase in practical performance of our algorithm compared to existing methods using tropical geometry and discuss the differences that lead to this increase in performance. We also demonstrate an application of our work to differential elimination.

Introduction

Problem Statement & Motivation.

Let
F:=\left\{f_{0},\dots,f_{k}\right\}\in\mathbb{C}[\mathbf{x}^{\pm},\mathbf{y}^{\pm}]
be a sequence of

k+1
Laurent polynomials in two sets of variables

\mathbf{x}:=\left\{x_{1},\dots,x_{n-k}\right\}
and
\mathbf{y}:=\left\{y_{1},\dots,y_{k}\right\}
. If

F
defines a complete intersection
X:=\mathbf{V}(F)
in the

n
-dimensional torus
\mathbb{T}^{n}:=(\mathbb{C}\setminus\left\{0\right\})^{n}
, then the projection
of
X
to the
n-k
-dimensional torus
\mathbb{T}_{\mathbf{x}}^{n-k}
corresponding
to the variables
\mathbf{x}
is a hypersurface
\mathbf{V}(g)
,

g\in\mathbb{C}[\mathbf{x}^{\pm}]
. Recall that the
Newton polytope
of a
polynomial is defined as the convex hull of its support.

The goal of this paper is to obtain a new algorithm which computes the
Newton polytope of
g
from the Newton polytopes of the
f_{i}
.
Such an algorithm can be used, for example, to estimate the size of

g
and to check whether it is feasible to compute
g
using symbolic
elimination techniques such as Gröbner bases (see e.g. Chapters 2 and
3 in
[
CLO15
]
).

Moreover, if there is a way to sample points on
X
, then knowing the
Newton polytope of
g
can be used to interpolate
g
by solving a
linear system, see e.g.
[
RST25
]
for details. This is in
particular the case for
implicitization problems
: in this case

n-k=k+1
(i.e. there is one more variable in
\mathbf{x}
than in
\mathbf{y}
) and
the
f_{i}
are of the form
f_{i}=x_{i}-h_{i}
for certain

h_{i}\in\mathbb{C}[\mathbf{y}^{\pm}]
. The problem of eliminating the variables in

\mathbf{y}
is then equivalent to finding a defining equation
g
for the image of
the map

\displaystyle\varphi:\;

\displaystyle\mathbb{T}^{k}\rightarrow\mathbb{T}^{k+1}

\displaystyle y\mapsto(h_{0}(y),\dots,h_{k}(y)).

Methodology & Main Results.

Our algorithm is fundamentally based on the work by

[
EK08
]
. In this work it is shown that, if the coefficients
of the
f_{i}
are generic and upon an appropriate choice of
g
, the
Newton polytope of
g
is the
mixed fiber polytope

F_{p}(\Delta_{0},\dots,\Delta_{k})\subset\mathbb{R}_{\mathbf{x}}^{n-k}
of the Newton polytopes

\Delta_{i}
of the
f_{i}
, a notion that was independently also
introduced in
[
McM04
]
. This construction is based on the
famous BKK-Theorem (
[
Ber75
,
Kou76
,
Kho95
]
, see also
[
CLO05
]
) which estimates the number
of nonzero roots of a square polynomial system in terms of the
mixed volume
of its Newton polytopes. Mixed volumes relate to
volumes (as a certain multilinear, symmetric extension of volumes to
multiple arguments) as mixed fiber polytopes relate to
fiber
polytopes
. The notion of fiber polytopes was introduced in

[
BS92
]
. As the name suggests, fiber polytopes relate to
the fibers of a projection of polytopes. A useful way to think about
them is that the fiber polytope of a linear projection of polytopes

p:\Delta\rightarrow p(\Delta)
is the “average fiber” of this
projection. In our case, the projection with respect to which we will
compute
mixed
fiber polytopes is the projection

p:\mathbb{R}^{n}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}_{\mathbf{y}}^{k}
which maps support vectors of monomials in

\mathbb{C}[\mathbf{x}^{\pm},\mathbf{y}^{\pm}]
to their entries corresponding to the variables
in
\mathbf{y}
.

As was shown in the seminal work
[
HS95
]
, mixed volumes can
be computed from
mixed subdivisions
of collections of
polytopes. Roughly speaking, a subdivision of a collection of
polytopes is a certain partition of the Minkowski sum of these
polytopes into smaller polytopes (called
cells
). In

[
HS95
]
it is shown that, given a
mixed
subdivision, the
mixed volume is a sum of the usual volumes of some of the cells of
this subdivision. Numerous algorithms exist to compute mixed
subdivisions, see e.g.
[
VGC96
,
MTK07
,
Mal17
,
GL00
,
DGH98
,
Che19
,
Jen16
]
.

We design an analogous approach for mixed fiber polytopes: Theorem 12
in
[
EK08
]
describes the vertices of the mixed fiber
polytope
F_{p}(\Delta_{0},\dots,\Delta_{k})
of the Newton polytopes

\Delta_{0},\dots,\Delta_{k}
of
f_{0},\dots,f_{k}
as sums of mixed fiber
polytopes associated to certain
k
-dimensional faces of the Minkowski
sum
\Delta
of the
\Delta_{i}
. For such a
k
-dimensional face the
associated mixed fiber polytope consists of a single point. The
relevant
k
-dimensional faces of
\Delta
induce a
coherent

subdivision of the images
p(\Delta_{i})
. Our main theoretical
result is then a formula to compute the desired vertex of

F_{p}(\Delta_{0},\dots,\Delta_{k})
from a mixed subdivision refining this
given coherent subdivision (
Theorem

3.3
).

Based on
Theorem

3.3
we obtain a new algorithm to compute vertices
of mixed fiber polytopes (
Algorithm

1
). This algorithm is a
vertex oracle
for the mixed fiber polytope of interest, i.e. it
computes, given a generic covector
\gamma\in(\mathbb{R}^{n-k})^{*}
, the vertex of
the mixed fiber polytope on which
\gamma
is minimized. From such a vertex
oracle, all vertices can then be recovered using the algorithm given
in
[
Hug06
]
.

Related Works & Context.

One way to solve the problem we are interested in is based on
techniques using
sparse resultants
(see e.g.
[
EKK12
,
EKP10
,
EKP07
,
EK03
,
Emi+13
]
and

[
GKZ94
]
for a comprehensive introduction to sparse
resultants). Namely, the Newton polytope of the desired eliminant
g

is a projection of the Newton polytope
\Delta_{R}
of the sparse

(p(\Delta_{0}),\dots,p(\Delta_{k}))
-resultant. This latter
polytope
\Delta_{R}
can be computed by enumerating its vertices using a
combinatorial procedure.
Note that the Newton polytope
\Delta_{R}
lives in a space of dimension
equal to the sum of the number of vertices of the
p(\Delta_{i})
,
making the enumeration of all its vertices potentially burdensome. For
implicitization of curves or surfaces this is solved in

[
EKP07
,
EKP10
]
, where the enumeration procedure can be
refined to directly compute the needed projection of
\Delta_{R}
.

We further mention the work
[
HS14
]
which, compared to
our algorithm, includes also a numerical component. Here the Newton
polytope of a polynomial
g
is computed when one can evaluate
g
or
produce
witness sets
on
\mathbf{V}(g)
, i.e. points on the
intersection of
\mathbf{V}(g)
with a linear subspace of suitable
dimension.

The strategy to compute Newton polytopes of eliminants that is closest
to our method uses
tropical elimination theory

[
MS15
,
SY08
,
ST07
,
RST25
,
STY07
]
. Section 4 in
[
ST07
]
explicitly
demonstrates that mixed fiber polytopes can be computed using tropical
geometry. To highlight the differences with our algorithm let us
sketch partially how tropical elimination works. With generic input

F:=\left\{f_{0},\dots,f_{k}\right\}\in\mathbb{C}[\mathbf{x}^{\pm},\mathbf{y}^{\pm}]
and
X:=\mathbf{V}(F)
one
starts by computing the
tropicalization

\trop(X)\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}
. Here,

\trop(X)
consists of the support of a subfan of the
normal fan

of the Minkowski sum of the Newton polytopes
\Delta_{i}
of the
f_{i}

together with associated
multiplicities
. These multiplicities
are certain mixed volumes of faces of the
\Delta_{i}
. The
tropicalization
\trop(X)
is then projected to the space

\mathbb{R}^{n-k}_{\mathbf{x}}
. This projection yields again a vertex oracle
for
F_{p}(\Delta_{0},\dots,\Delta_{k})
.

While tropical elimination works for more general problems than
considered here (see e.g. Section 3 in
[
RST25
]
, i.e. also for
certain classes of varieties
X
which are not complete
intersections), our algorithm, which does not use any tropical
geometry, offers two practical advantages in the considered setting:
First, the mixed volumes needed to compute
\trop(X)
are computed
from mixed subdivisions in dimension
k+1
. Our algorithm necessitates
the computation of mixed subdivisions in dimension
k
, i.e. dimension
one less. A more substantial improvement comes from the fact the we
avoid explicit computations with the Minkowski sum of the

\Delta_{i}
. To illustrate the resulting improvement, consider the
following example:

Example 1.1
.

In
\mathbb{C}[x_{0},x_{1},x_{2},x_{3},y_{1},y_{2},y_{3}]
we let

\displaystyle f_{0}:=x_{0}-(y_{1}y_{2}+y_{3}+1),f_{1}:=x_{1}-(y_{1}y_{3}+y_{2}+1),

\displaystyle f_{2}:=x_{2}-(y_{1}y_{3}+y_{1}+1),f_{3}:=x_{3}-(y_{1}^{3}+y_{2}^{5}+y_{3}^{7}).

This example appears on page 113 of
[
SY08
]
. Let
X

be the algebraic set defined by the
f_{i}
and let
\Delta_{i}
be the
Newton polytope of
f_{i}
for each
i=0,\dots,3
. The Minkowski sum

\Delta:=\Delta_{0}+\dots+\Delta_{4}
has 651 faces of dimension
4
. The
computation of
\trop(X)
requires the computation of one mixed
volume for each of these faces. Our algorithm extracts the mixed
fiber polytope
F_{p}(\Delta_{0},\dots,\Delta_{3})
with just 38
computations of mixed subdivisions.

Indeed, note that the Minkowski sum of
\Delta_{0},\dots,\Delta_{k}
can
have a number of faces in the worst case equal to the product of the
number of faces of the
\Delta_{i}
, see e.g.
[
Gr“”u03
]
.

We highlight these two practical advantages experimentally with a set
of examples, see
Section

4
. These experiments are performed with
an implementation of our algorithm in the programming language

julia

[
Bez+17
]
, comparing it to

TropicalImplicitization.jl
which is written in
julia

as well. This latter package implements the computation of mixed fiber
polytopes based on tropical geometry for implicitization problems, as
in
[
RST25
,
SY08
,
ST07
]
. On the chosen
examples our software outperforms tropical implicitization by several
orders of magnitude.

2.2
Vertices of Mixed Fiber Polytopes

Let
\Delta_{0},\dots,\Delta_{k}\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}
be polytopes. We will compute the
mixed fiber polytope
F_{p}(\Delta_{0},\dots,\Delta_{k})
by providing a

vertex oracle
, i.e. a function that returns for a generic
covector
\gamma\in(\mathbb{R}^{n-k})^{*}
the unique point of

F_{p}(\Delta_{0},\dots,\Delta_{k})
on which
\gamma
is minimized. Note that any
polytope is the convex hull of its vertices. Let us first introduce
some associated notation and recall some standard definitions:

For a compact subset
S\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}
and covector

\gamma\in(\mathbb{R}^{n})^{*}
we denote by
S^{\gamma}
the set of points in

S
where
\gamma
is minimized.

Definition 2.5

(Face, Vertex, Normal Cone)
.

A face of a polytope
\Delta\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}
is a set of the form

\Delta^{\gamma}
for some
\gamma\in(\mathbb{R}^{n})^{*}
. Every face of a polytope is
a polytope itself.

The vertices of
\Delta
are the
0
-dimensional
faces, they consist of singletons.

The normal cone

\operatorname{N}(\Phi,\Delta)
of a face
\Phi
of
\Delta
is defined as

\operatorname{N}(\Phi,\Delta):=\left\{\gamma\in(\mathbb{R}^{n})^{*}\;\middle|\;\Phi\subseteq\Delta^{\gamma}\right\},

Any normal cone is a
convex polyhedral cone
, i.e. it can be
written as the set of all linear combinations with non-negative
coefficients of a finite set of vectors. Recall also that, for a
polytope
\Delta\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}
and a face
\Phi
of
\Delta
, we have

\dim(\operatorname{N}(\Phi,\Delta))=n-\dim(\Phi)
. Here, the dimension of a
convex polyhedral cone is again defined to be the dimension of its
affine span. This implies in particular that
\Delta^{\gamma}
is a vertex
for all
\gamma\in(\mathbb{R}^{n})^{*}
lying outside a union of finitely many
subspaces of
(\mathbb{R}^{n})^{*}
.

Recall further that, for a collection of polytopes

\Delta_{0},\dots,\Delta_{k}\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}
, the faces of the Minkowski sum

\Delta:=\Delta_{0}+\dots+\Delta_{k}
are sums of faces of the
\Delta_{i}
: For
a covector
\gamma\in(\mathbb{R}^{n})^{*}
we have

\Delta^{\gamma}=\Delta_{0}^{\gamma}+\dots+\Delta_{k}^{\gamma}.

Thus, by a face of a tuple of polytopes

\Delta_{\bullet}:=(\Delta_{0},\dots,\Delta_{k})
we mean a tuple of the form

\Phi_{\bullet}:=(\Delta_{0}^{\gamma},\dots,\Delta_{k}^{\gamma})
for a covector

\gamma\in(\mathbb{R}^{n})^{*}
. By the dimension of
\Phi_{\bullet}
we mean the dimension of
the corresponding face of
\Delta
and by the normal cone

N(\Phi_{\bullet},\Delta_{\bullet})
the normal cone of the corresponding face of

\Delta
. For some integer
d\in\mathbb{N}
we denote by

\mathcal{F}_{d}(\Delta_{\bullet})
the set of
d
-dimensional faces of
\Delta_{\bullet}
.

Subdivisions of Point Configurations

A

Analogously to mixed fiber polytopes, the mixed volume of a collection
of polytopes
\Delta_{1},\dots,\Delta_{r}\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}
is the coefficient of

\lambda_{1}\dots\lambda_{r}
in the polynomial

\operatorname{vol}(\lambda_{1}\Delta_{1}+\dots+\lambda_{r}\Delta_{r})
in variables

\lambda_{1},\dots,\lambda_{r}
. A mixed subdivision of an underlying point
configuration of
\Delta_{1},\dots,\Delta_{r}
now allows to explicitly
compute this mixed volume as the sum of volumes of certain cells in
this subdivision. This is the content of Theorem 2.4 in

[
HS95
]
which we now adapt to our situation.

Recall that, using
Theorem

2.6
, the computation of the mixed
fiber polytope of convex polytopes
\Delta_{0},\dots,\Delta_{k}
reduces to
the case where
p
induces a bijection from

\Delta_{0}+\dots+\Delta_{k}
to
p(\Delta_{0}+\dots+\Delta_{k})
. In the
following lemma we denote by
\varepsilon_{i}\in\mathbb{Z}^{k+1}
the vector with

0
in the
i
th entry and
1
everywhere else.

Lemma 3.1
.

Let
\mathcal{A}:=(\mathcal{A}_{0},\dots,\mathcal{A}_{k})
be a point configuration in

\mathbb{Z}^{n}
. Denote
\Delta_{i}:=\operatorname{Conv}(\mathcal{A}_{i})
and assume that

p
induces a bijection from
\Delta_{0}+\dots+\Delta_{k}
to

p(\Delta_{0}+\dots+\Delta_{k})
. Let
S
be a mixed subdivision of

p(\mathcal{A}):=(p(\mathcal{A}_{0}),\dots,p(\mathcal{A}_{k}))
. Then

F_{p}(\Delta_{0},\dots,\Delta_{k})=\sum_{i=0}^{k}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}C\in S\\
\operatorname{type}(C)=\varepsilon_{i}\end{subarray}}\operatorname{vol}(C)\cdot\pi(p|_{\mathcal{A}_{i}}^{-1}(C_{i})).

Proof.

For
\lambda\in\mathbb{R}^{k+1}
, denote

\Delta_{\lambda}:=\lambda_{0}\Delta_{0}+\dots+\lambda_{k}\Delta_{k}
. First note that

F_{p}(\Delta_{\lambda})=\sum_{C\in S}F_{p}(\lambda_{0}\operatorname{Conv}(C_{0})+\dots+\lambda_{k}\operatorname{Conv}(C_{k})).

Therefore we assume that
p(\mathcal{A})
defines a mixed subdivision of
itself. This is the case if, denoting
d_{i}:=\dim(p(\Delta_{i}))
,
we have
\sum_{i=0}^{k}d_{i}=k
. Since
p
is linear and induces a
bijection from
\Delta_{\lambda}
to
p(\Delta_{\lambda})
for

\lambda_{0},\dots,\lambda_{k}>0
, it also induces a bijection from

\Delta_{i}
to
p(\Delta_{i})
. Further, if

s_{i}:p(\Delta_{i})\rightarrow\Delta_{i}
denotes the corresponding inverse map,
then
s_{i}
is an affine map.

Additionally, since
\sum_{i=0}^{k}d_{i}=k
we can choose, for each

i=0,\dots,k
, a (possibly empty) set of coordinates
x_{i}
on

\langle p(\mathcal{A}_{i})\rangle
such that
x:=(x_{0},\dots,x_{k})
gives
coordinates on
\langle p(\mathcal{A})\rangle
. If we now define

s(x):=\sum_{i=0}^{k}s_{i}(x_{i})
then the Minkowski integral of

\Delta_{\lambda}
consists of the singleton

\displaystyle\int_{p(\Delta_{\lambda})}s(x)dx

\displaystyle=\sum_{i=0}^{k}\int_{\lambda_{i}p(\Delta_{i})}s_{i}(x_{i})dx_{i}\cdot\prod_{j\neq i}\operatorname{vol}(p(\lambda_{j}\Delta_{j}))

\displaystyle=\sum_{i=0}^{k}\lambda_{i}^{d_{i}+1}\prod_{j\neq i}\lambda_{j}^{d_{j}}\int_{\Delta_{i}}s_{i}(x_{i})dx_{i}\cdot\prod_{j\neq i}\operatorname{vol}(p(\Delta_{j}))

\displaystyle=\prod_{i=0}^{k}\lambda_{i}^{d_{i}}\sum_{i=0}^{k}\lambda_{i}\int_{p(\Delta_{i})}s_{i}(x_{i})dx_{i}\cdot\prod_{j\neq i}\operatorname{vol}(p(\Delta_{j})).

Hence we see that the coefficient of
\lambda_{0}\dots\lambda_{k}
in this integral
is nonzero if and only if, for some
i
, we have
d_{i}=0
and for all

j\neq i
we have
\lambda_{j}=1
. In this case

\int_{p(\Delta_{i})}s_{i}(x_{i})dx_{i}
is just the singleton

\mathcal{A}_{i}
and

\prod_{j\neq i}\operatorname{vol}(p(\Delta_{j}))=\operatorname{vol}(p(\Delta))
. This concludes
the proof.

.

\mathcal{A}:=(\mathcal{A}_{0},\dots,\mathcal{A}_{k})

p(\mathcal{A}_{0})=\left\{0,d_{0}\right\},\quad p(\mathcal{A}_{1})=\left\{0,d_{1}\right\}.

A mixed subdivision of
p(\mathcal{A})
is given by the two cells

\displaystyle C_{0}:=(C_{0,0},C_{0,1}):=(\left\{0\right\},\left\{0,d_{1}\right\}),

\displaystyle C_{1}=(C_{1,0},C_{1,1}):=(\left\{0,d_{0}\right\},\left\{d_{1}\right\}),

see also
Example

2.2
. We further compute

\displaystyle p|_{\mathcal{A}_{\gamma,0}}^{-1}(C_{0,0})=\left\{\begin{pmatrix}d_{0}\\
0\end{pmatrix}\right\};\;\operatorname{vol}(C_{0})=d_{1},

\displaystyle p|_{\mathcal{A}_{\gamma,1}}^{-1}(C_{1,1})=\left\{\begin{pmatrix}0\\
d_{1}\end{pmatrix}\right\};\;\operatorname{vol}(C_{1})=d_{0}.

Plugging this into
Theorem

3.3
yields

\displaystyle F_{p}(\Delta_{0},\Delta_{1})^{\gamma}

\displaystyle=d_{1}\cdot\pi\left(\left\{\begin{pmatrix}d_{0}\\
0\end{pmatrix}\right\}\right)+d_{0}\cdot\pi\left(\left\{\begin{pmatrix}0\\
d_{1}\end{pmatrix}\right\}\right)

\displaystyle=d_{1}\cdot\left\{d_{0}\right\}+d_{0}\cdot\left\{0\right\}=\left\{d_{0}d_{1}\right\}

as expected.

An Application to Differential Elimination

An interesting potential application of our algorithm comes
from
differential elimination
, which we illustrate here.

We consider a
dynamical system
of the shape

x^{\prime}_{1}-g_{1}=\cdots=x^{\prime}_{n}-g_{n}=0,

(1)

where
g_{i}\in\mathbb{C}[x_{1},\dots,x_{n}]
. The
x_{i}
should be thought of as
differentiable functions
x_{i}(t)
in a time variable

t
. Frequently, one wishes to compute a
minimal
differential
relation
f_{\text{min}}
satisfied by the coordinate
x_{1}
of a solution
vector
(x_{1},\dots,x_{n})
to (
1
). This problem was recently
considered by the second author of this work in
[
MP25
]
. It
turns out that
f_{\text{min}}\in\mathbb{C}[x_{1},x_{1}^{\prime},\dots,x_{1}^{(n)}]
. In

[
MP25
]
, a polytope
\Delta_{\text{min}}
containing the
Newton polytope of
f_{\text{min}}
was constructed by giving explicit
inequalities satisfied by the elements in the support of
f_{\text{min}}

(Theorem 1 in
[
MP25
]
). Then one can compute
f_{\text{min}}
out
of
\Delta_{\text{min}}
(Algorithm 1 in
[
MP25
]
) using an
evaluation-interpolation approach by solving a linear system with number of
equations and variables equal to the number of lattice points in

\Delta_{\text{min}}
.

We consider an example. Write
d:=\deg g_{1}
,

D:=\max_{2\leq i\leq n}\deg g_{i}
and consider the system

\begin{cases}x^{\prime}_{1}=x_{1}^{2}+x_{1}x_{2}+x_{2}^{2}+1,\\
x_{2}^{\prime}=x_{2}.\end{cases}

The bounds obtained in
[
MP25
]
depend on
n,d
and
D
. In
this case
n=2,d=2,D=1
. We introduce a new variable
y
and write

y,x_{1}^{\prime},x_{1}^{\prime\prime}
as polynomials in the variables
x_{1},x_{2},x_{1}^{\prime}
:

\begin{split}&y=x_{1},\\
&x_{1}^{\prime}=x_{1}^{2}+x_{1}x_{2}+x_{2}^{2}+1,\\
&x_{1}^{\prime\prime}=x^{\prime}_{1}(2x_{1}+x_{2})+x_{2}(2x_{2}+1).\end{split}

(2)

From this system, one can then compute
f_{\text{min}}
by eliminating the
variables
x_{1},x_{2}
.

Note that to the system (
2
) we can also apply our
algorithm by computing the mixed fiber polytope w.r.t. the variables

y,x_{1},x_{1}^{\prime}
. This mixed fiber polytope will contain the Newton
polytope of
f_{\text{min}}
and
f_{\text{min}}
can then be computed using this
mixed fiber polytope in combination with Algorithm 1 in

[
MP25
]
. It is then interesting to ask how many lattice
points this mixed fiber polytope has compared to
\Delta_{\text{min}}
,
both from a theoretical viewpoint (i.e. is it possible to improve the
bounds depending on
n,d
and
D
in
[
MP25
]
using mixed
fiber polytopes) and from a practical viewpoint (i.e. is it beneficial
to use mixed fiber polytopes instead of
\Delta_{\text{min}}
for
specific dynamical systems to exploit their sparsity patterns).

For the case
d\leq D
, the obtained bounds were shown to be sharp in

[
MP25
]
but obtaining sharp bounds for the case
d>D

remains open.

In
Table

2
we compare the number of lattice points in

\Delta_{\text{min}}
with the number of lattice points in the
associated mixed fiber polytope. The column
\%
shows the difference
between these numbers as a percentage of the first number. The first 6
rows correspond to dynamical systems with random dense polynomials

g_{i}
satisfying the indicated degree constraints. For the case

n=4,d=3,D=2
, we were unable to compute all integer points in the
polytope extracted out of
[
MP25
]
within twelve hours of
computation. The last three rows of
Table

2
correspond
to specific choices of sparse
g_{i}
.
