Toxicity profile of standardized Krishna Vajra Abhraka bhasma made from biotite mica
Authors: Vijaya Anil Pandit, Sharwan Kumar Singhal, Gourav Bharat Deshmane, Ranjana Ajit Sahasrabuddhe, Manjiri Neelkanth Karandikar, Madhuri Sadashiv Pawar, Vishakha Sumant Kulkarni, Jayshree Shriram Dawane, Asmita Ashish Wele
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractIntroductionThe Bhasmas, herbo-minerals, are reported to produce adverse effects when used clinically. Therefore, the present study aimed to assess the safety of Krishna Vajra Abhraka Bhasma (KVB), through acute and subacute toxicity testing.Materials and MethodsAfter ethics committee approval, total 66 Wistar albino rats weighing 180–200 gms were used. Experiments were conducted as per CCSEA guidelines. In acute toxicity study, a single limit dose of 2000 mg/kg of KVB with honey was administered to six female rats. Subacute toxicity studies involved 60 Wistar albino rats of either sex, randomly divided into six groups and received KVB in low (highest clinical dose extrapolated to rats), medium (double the low dose) and high (double the medium dose) dose administered orally for 28 days.ResultsNo mortality or adverse effects on rat's general behaviour or internal organs was observed in acute toxicity study The results of subacute study indicated no significant differences in body and organ weights, hematological or renal function between control and treated groups. However, mid and high doses of KVB revealed reversible, dose-dependent hepatotoxicity through biochemical and histopathological evaluation, while the satellite high dose group did not exhibit this effect, confirming it as drug-induced hepatotoxicity.ConclusionThe acute toxicity results positioned KVB in class V (safe) of the GHS classification. Subacute toxicity findings suggested that while low therapeutic doses were non-toxic, mid and high doses induced reversible hepatotoxicity, emphasizing the importance of careful dosage considerations and patient monitoring in Ayurvedic practice. The study contributes valuable insights into the safety profile of KVB, addressing concerns for both users and practitioners of Ayurvedic medicine.Keywords: Organ toxicity, Herbo-mineral agents, Krishna vajra abhraka bhasma, Liver toxicity, Kidney function

Introduction

Ayurveda is a well-known ancient Indian heritage. It is a traditional medicinal system used by majority of India's population. With the availability of rapidly acting modern medicine agents, use of traditional medicines dwindled. But Ayurveda has survived the test of time mainly because of its holistic approach and strong conceptual base. It is practised extensively in India. Its globalisation is round the corner with many groups across the globe undertaking studies for better understanding of its concepts and practice [1].

Ayurveda mainly uses two types of pharmaceuticals-herbal and herbo-mineral agents. When only herbal agents are used, apart from the variation in the amount of active ingredient, its short shelf life is the major concern. This problem is overcome by the addition of mineral agents, but metal toxicity becomes a cause of concern. Though safety aspects like Good Agricultural Practises (GAP), Good Field Collection Practises (GFCP) and Good Manufacturing Practises (GMP) are meticulously laid down [2], there are many reports of toxicity with the use of ayurvedic herbo-mineral agents [3,4].

Krishna Vajra Abhraka Bhasma (KVB) is the bhasma prepared from biotite (mica) with the addition of various herbs. KVB is used in the variety of clinical conditions. The herbal component varies with the indication of use. We were trying to prepare a formulation for chronic asthma and therefore, method number 9 from the text Rasatarangini was chosen [5].

With the reported toxicity of Bhasma and even the herbal agents, WHO has emphasised the need to carry out toxicity studies for Ayurveda formulations [6,7]. Central Council for Research in Ayurvedic Sciences (CCRAS) also has come up with the guidance document to evaluate safety of all Ayurveda, Siddha and Unani products, which is in tune with the guidelines laid down by Organization for Economic Co-operation and Development (OECD) [8].

Keeping this in mind, an observational study was conducted to evaluate safety of KVB prepared in our laboratory by acute and subacute toxicity testing.

Material and methods

Drug

In the present study, Krishna Vajra Abhraka Bhasma was prepared in our laboratory following shodhan, dhanyabraka and bhasmikaran processes. For the process of bhasmikaran, four herbs were used – decoction of aerial roots of Ficus benghalensis lin (Vata), whole plant of Solanum xanthocarpum (Kantakari), leaves of Adathoda vasica (Vasa) and fruit of Terminalia bellerica (Bibhitak) was used in the bhasmikaran process. Leaves of Ricinus communis (Erand) were used for wrapping the pellets while heating.

Animals and housing

Approval was obtained from Institutional Animal Ethics Committee (IAEC) of Bharati Vidyapeeth Deemed to be University Medical college, Pune. (No.- BVDUMC/68/2004–2005). Wistar albino rats of either sex, weighing 180 ± 20 g (age about 2.5–3 months), were used for the study. The animals were housed at temperature of 25 ± 20C, relative humidity 45–50% and 12:12 light dark cycles. They received rat pellets (Nav Maharashtra Chakan Mills Ltd, India) and aqua-guard water at libitum.

All the experiments were conducted as per CCSEA guidelines.

KVB administration

The highest therapeutic dose was extrapolated for rats, which was 22.5 mg/kg and was used as low dose [9]. 45 mg/kg was used as medium dose (MD, double of low dose) and 100 mg/kg as high dose (HD, about double of MD). The test drug was mixed with honey (vehicle) and administered orally with spatula (licking).

Acute toxicity: (OECD guideline- 423)

Acute oral toxicity was carried out according to OECD-423 [8] and CCESA guidelines [10]. Number of animals (sample size) included for the study was as per OECD guideline.

Experiment was carried out on six young, healthy, nulliparous, and non-pregnant female Wistar albino rats. KVB was administered in the dose of 2000 mg/kg (limit dose).

The animals were fasted overnight before dosing. On Day 1, three animals were dosed and observed for mortality at 30 min, 90 min, 6 h and at 24 h. With no mortality observed in these animals, additional three animals were treated with the same dose, on the next day. All the animals were followed up daily at 10 a.m. for total 14 days.

Apart from mortality, the animals were observed for changes in general parameters like food consumption, behavior, pattern of respiration, fur, skin, eyes or other physiological changes. On day 15, the animals were sacrificed, all the organs were carefully inspected for gross pathological changes and lungs, trachea, liver, stomach, spleen and kidneys were collected for histopathological evaluation.

Subacute toxicity: (OECD guideline- 407)

As per the guideline, thirty male and thirty female healthy Wistar albino rats were used for the study. 5 animals of same sex were selected by simple random sampling for each cage and total twelve cages were prepared. One male and one female cage of animals were selected to include ten animals per group by simple random sampling to form a group. Six groups were made and treated as shown Table 1

Study interventions.

Dosing was done once daily at 10 a.m. for 4 weeks. All the animals received treatment for 28 days. For further two weeks, animals were maintained without any treatment in both the satellite groups (groups 5 & 6).

Initial body weight of all the animals was recorded (using digital balance of Masters company, India) and the dose of KVB was calculated accordingly. The dose of KVB was modified according to the change in the body weight of the animals on weekly basis.

Along with the mortality, all rats were observed for any physiological and behavioral changes. The food and water consumption were checked daily. At the end of the study i.e. day 29 for animals in group 1 to 4 and day 43 for animals in groups 5 & 6, were lightly anaesthetized and blood samples collected from retro-orbital plexus in pre labelled EDTA and heparinized vials for hematological and biochemical analysis respectively.Animals were then euthanized and opened to observe any gross pathological changes. Brain, eye, stomach, intestine, liver, kidney, adrenals, spleen, heart, thymus, thyroid, trachea and lungs, gonads, accessory sex organs, urinary bladder were collected, weighed (using digital balance of Denver instrument company, U.S. SI-234) and processed for histopathological examination.

Hematological, biochemical & pathological evaluation staff were blinded for accurate outcome assessment while sending samples. Only JRF working on the project was aware of the group allocation.

Hematological analysis

Hematological analysis was done by using automatic hematology analyzer (Advia 120, Bayer Inc, Germany). Parameters included were-erythrocyte count, hemoglobin, hematocrit, mean cell volume (MCV), mean corpuscular hemoglobin (MCH), mean corpuscular hemoglobin concentration (MCHC), platelet count, total and differential leukocyte count.

Bio-chemical analysis

Biochemical analysis included glucose, urea, creatinine, total cholesterol, triglycerides, aspartate transaminase (AST), alanine transaminase (ALT), alkaline phosphatase (ALP), total protein, albumin, calcium and phosphorus. All biochemical tests were carried out using fully automated biochemistry analyzer (Humastar 300, Human GmbH, Germany). Serum electrolytes (sodium and potassium) were assayed using electrolyte analyzer (Humalyte, Human GmbH, Germany).

Histopathological study

All the internal organs, as mentioned above, were washed thoroughly and placed into 10 % buffered formalin solution. They were then trimmed and transferred to the cassettes to make paraffin blocks. From these blocks, 4 μm sections were made and stained with hematoxylin-eosin for histopathological examination.

Statistical analysis

All hematological & biochemical results were expressed as the mean ± SD and were analyzed statistically by one -way Analysis of Variance (ANOVA) followed by Tukey's post hoc test using software Graph Pad Prism (Graph Pad Software, USA) version 6.0.

P < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Discussion

Nano medicine is the emerging branch in modern medicine and has found its applications in diagnosis and treatment. Bhasmas, used in traditional ayurvedic medicine are considered to be bio-inorganic multi-elemental nanoparticles, which are in clinical use for various ailments, over a long time [11]. These agents are considered to be potent drugs, having long shelf life [12]. KVB prepared in our laboratory was subjected to characterization studies. It was found to have square shaped crystalline nanoparticles with mean size of 92.3 nm (SEM) and the presence of oxides of various minerals Si, Mg, 0, Fe, Ca, Na, C, K and Al (EDAX, XRD). Organic functional groups from cow milk and selected herbs seemed to have got attached to it (FTIR, NMR) [13]. Thus, KVB prepared in our laboratory was found to be Nano particulate herbo-mineral agent.

Routinely, to reduce toxicity and enhance potency, herbs and minerals are subjected to shodhan (detoxification) process prior to use in any formulation [14], indicating awareness of ayurvedic scholars of the toxicity profile and the methods of its amelioration. Large number of heating cycles in the bhasmikaran process synthesize a completely new molecule which is fine and light and acquires the quality to move fast. Considering changed structure and properties of the mineral in bhasmikaran process, it was important to understand its toxicity status. Ayurvedic textual references for testing of toxicity are not available and no toxicity is reported in Ayurvedic texts to Abhraka bhasma. Recent studies evaluating acute toxicity of KVB are available [15,16]. But the manufacturing process of KVB used in these studies was different from the one we used. Therefore, the available toxicity information could not be extrapolated to our formulation. Also, safety evaluation after its long-term administration was not available. Additionally, there is limited knowledge about the side effects produced by nano particulate agents and regulatory bodies require their toxicity studies to be carried out [6]. So, acute & sub-acute toxicity studies of KVB were carried out.

Acute oral toxicity in rats using mica ash ∼ abhraka bhasma with different vehicles such as honey, corn oil and water was assessed by Srinivasan et al. (2010), using in-vitro and in-vivo methods. Single oral dose administration of mica ash in 2000 mg/kg body weight was used for this purpose. This dose was reported not to produce any systemic toxicity. So, we performed acute toxicity testing of our formulation using “limit dose” of 2000 mg/kg. Nulliparous, non-pregnant female albino Wistar rats, were used for this study because females are known to be slightly more sensitive than males. Honey was chosen as the vehicle for administration of KVB, as per ayurvedic texts [17]. This concept has been proved by Srinivas A et al. (2010) by showing that honey has the ability to hold the mica ash for long periods of time in the digestive system when compared to water or corn oil [15].

Acute toxicity: The basic purpose of acute toxicity testing is to classify the text substance into Globally Harmonized System of Classification (GHS) of chemicals, to know its toxic potential [18,19]. Acute toxicity study was carried out according to the OECD guideline 423. There was steady increase in the body weight (Table 2) and general behavior of all the animals was normal (Table 3). Gross necropsy and histopathology of lungs, liver, spleen, kidney did not reveal any toxicity. Our results were in tune with other acute toxicity reports of KVB [15,16].

Subacute toxicity: The essence of toxicity testing is not just to check the safety of a test substance but also, to characterize qualitatively the dose and duration specific toxic effects it can produce to develop safety guidelines for its clinical use [20]. In chronic asthma, KVB treatment is generally given for three months. With the comparison of life spans of rat and human, one day in rat is supposed to be equal to 30 days in human [21]. So, subacute toxicity by administration of KVB for 28 days was done, which would give sufficient idea about the development of human toxicity on its long term use (840 days i.e. roughly 2.5 years). Sub-acute toxicity was carried out using OECD 407guidelines [22]. All the organ systems were studied as per OECD guidelines with special emphasis on hemopoetic, liver and kidney as many metals can produce toxicity on these organs [23].

There was no mortality in any of the groups and gradual increase in weight similar to control group was observed (Table 4), which is general indicator of toxicity of any drug [24].

The hemopoetic system is the most sensitive index of physiological and pathological changes produced by toxic minerals which can be easily extrapolated to human [25,26]. In our study, no significant difference was seen in hematological parameters between KVB treated and control groups. (Table 4, Table 5).

Liver is exposed to maximum concentration of orally administered agents and so, it is one of the main organs of drug induced toxicity. Hepatotoxicity is one of the important causes of post marketing drug withdrawals and also responsible for halting the drug development process [27].

Biochemical parameters-transaminases (AST, aspartate transaminase, present in many organs and ALT-alanine transaminase, specific in liver) along with serum alkaline phosphatase give clue regarding the type of liver injury, if present. KVB induced significant elevation of transaminases (mainly ALT) with non-significant increase in alkaline phosphatase (Table 7), which was indicative of hepatocellular type of liver damage [28]. Drug induced liver damage causing jaundice due to reduced bilirubin processing can be life threatening. But bilirubin level was not increased with KVB (Table 7), implying that the liver injury was mild. In acute liver injury, triglyceride levels may increase which in turn may increase uric acid levels [29]. Increase in triglyceride and uric acid levels in MD & HD KVB groups, indicated acute liver injury (Table 7). On histopathology, fatty change was observed in the liver in MD & HD dose (Fig. 2, Fig. 2). All biochemical and histopathological changes reversed in HD satellite group (Table 7), confirming that the liver injury was drug induced (in medium and high dose) and was reversible. Herbs like Ricinus communis, Solanum xanthocarpum and Terminalia bellerica, used in bhasmikaran process are reported to have hepato-protective action [[30], [31], [32]]. It is likely that these herbs could have protected liver in low dose but could not do so when the dose of KVB was increased.

Conclusion

KVB was found to be nontoxic in acute toxicity study at the Limit dose (2000 mg/kg) i.e. LD50 > 2000 mg/kg and so, it can be placed in class V of GHS classification.

The only toxicity found in subacute toxicity testing was dose dependent, reversible hepatotoxicity produced in medium and high dose of KVB which reversed in the satellite group. The therapeutic dose range used clinically did not reveal any toxicity. But for safety purpose, liver enzymes should be monitored when KVB is used clinically on long term basis. No other system was seen to get affected by KVB.
