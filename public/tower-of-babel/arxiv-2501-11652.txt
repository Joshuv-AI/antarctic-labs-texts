A new method to solve a reflection equation with piecewise constant arguments 1,2
Alberto Cabada and Paula Cambeses Franco

Abstract
In this work, we study nonlocal differential equations with particular focus on those with reflection in their argument and piecewise constant dependence. The approach entails deriving the explicit expression of the solution to the linear problem by constructing the corresponding Green’s function, as well as developing a novel formula to delineate the set of parameters involved in the analyzed equations for which the Green’s function exhibits a constant sign. Furthermore, we demonstrate the existence of solutions for nonlinear problems through the utilisation of diverse results derived from fixed-point theory. The aforementioned methodology is specifically applied to the linear problem with periodic conditions v ′ ​ ( t ) + m ​ v ​ ( − t ) + M ​ v ​ ( [ t ] ) = h ⁡ ( t ) v^{\prime}(t)+mv(-t)+Mv([t])=h(t) for t ∈ [ − T , T ] t\in[-T,T] , proving several existence results for the associated nonlinear problem and precisely delimiting the region where the Green’s function H m , M H_{m,M} has a constant sign. The equations studied have the potential to be applied in fields such as biomedicine or quantum mechanics. Furthermore, this work represents a significant advance, as it is the first time that equations with involution and piecewise constant arguments have been studied together.

Introduction

In the expansive and continuously evolving field of mathematics, differential equations occupy a pivotal position in the modelling and comprehension of phenomena across a range of disciplines. However, the difficulties associated with modelling complex systems, where interactions are not solely local or where changes occur in discrete intervals, have prompted the development of novel classes of differential equations. Notable among these are nonlocal differential equations and differential equations with piecewise constant arguments, which have applications in diverse fields including quantum mechanics and biomedicine.

The concept of non-locality has been one of the most fascinating and controversial topics in the field of modern physics. Albert Einstein, one of the most prominent theoretical physicists of the 20th century, was profoundly sceptical about the notion that physical effects could be transmitted instantaneously over a distance. This scepticism is reflected in his phrase “spooky action at a distance”
[
13
]
, which he used to describe quantum entanglement, a phenomenon in which two entangled particles can influence each other instantaneously, regardless of the distance between them. Einstein insisted that a complete theory of the universe should respect the principle of locality, where “nothing can travel faster than light”
[
17
]
.

Despite his reservations, the predictions of quantum mechanics challenged this intuition. The EPR (Einstein-Podolsky-Rosen) experiment, proposed by Einstein himself in collaboration with Boris Podolsky and Nathan Rosen in 1935
[
14
]
, sought to demonstrate that quantum mechanics was incomplete precisely because it permitted the existence of nonlocal correlations. In his own words, “I cannot believe that God plays dice with the universe,” reflecting his disagreement with the probabilistic and nonlocal nature of quantum predictions. Despite Einstein’s opposition, subsequent experiments, including those conducted by John Bell
[
4
]
and Alain Aspect
[
3
]
, have validated the reality of quantum entanglement and established nonlocal effects as a fundamental aspect of the quantum realm.

Moving away from physics, such types of equations also appear in biomedicine. Non-locality manifests itself in the way we model complex systems that cannot be fully described by local interactions. For example, the dynamics of infectious disease transmission may involve nonlocal effects due to global human mobility and long-range interactions between individuals.

A key approach to modelling complex systems is the use of differential equations with piecewise constant arguments. These equations model situations where the dependent variables or their derivatives are evaluated at discrete points in time or space, usually following periodic or constant subdivisions of the domain. This approach is particularly useful for systems where events occur at regular intervals or where conditions change abruptly.

These equations are used extensively in control engineering, where systems are monitored and adjusted at regular intervals. A typical example is digital controllers that sample state variables and make corrections at discrete intervals. In biomedicine, they are used to model physiological rhythms, such as the electrical activity of the heart, which is measured at regular intervals, or in drug dosing, where doses are administered at fixed times
[
18
]
. In economics, these equations are fundamental in describing phenomena where adjustments are made at specific times, such as price adjustments at regular intervals or investment decisions made at discrete points in time
[
15
]
.

In light of the aforementioned considerations, this paper will concentrate on the study of functional equations with involution and piecewise constant dependence. Involution equations are a particular type of nonlocal equation characterized by the fact that the function composed with itself is the identity. Two of the most prominent examples of involution are reflection and inversion. The study of these differential equations commenced with Silberstein in 1940, who analyzed the resolution of the equation
v^{\prime}(t)=v(\frac{1}{t})

[
21
]
. Since then, many authors have worked on this type of problem
[
2
,
16
,
20
,
12
]
. On the other hand, the study of differential equations with piecewise constant arguments began in the early 1980s and has since been widely treated in the literature
[
19
,
22
,
1
,
9
]
. In their study, techniques from both differential equations and difference equations have been combined. This work constitutes, as far as we know, the first time these types of equations are analyzed together. Let’s approach this problem using the theory of Green’s functions and analyzing their properties. We will focus, in particular, on studying solutions with a constant sign. This phenomenon is of interest because many quantities that arise in the context of the problems modelled by the equations considered can only take non-negative values. These include, for example, pressure, power, temperature in Kelvin and the number of people affected by a disease.

We will structure the article as follows. In Section
2
, we will introduce the necessary preliminaries for the rest of the work. Subsequently, in Section
3
, we will derive the expression for the Green’s function for a problem that involves both involution and reflection. In Section
4
, we will study the properties of these functions and present a new method that enables us to delineate the region where the function maintains a constant sign. This method will be applied to a previously known case. Next, in Section
5
, we will analyze a first-order differential equation with reflection and piecewise constant arguments, focusing on the region where its Green’s function exhibits a constant sign. Finally, in Section
6
, we will apply the concepts introduced and study the existence of a solution for a nonlinear problem using the monotone method. We will also provide a numerical approximation of this solution.

2
Preliminaries, hypotheses and main assumptions

The utilisation of Green’s functions is an invaluable tool in the resolution of ordinary differential equations. Accordingly, we will initially present the concept of Green’s functions and subsequently examine their properties.

The following general problem of order
n
is considered

L_{n}\,v(t)=\sigma(t),\;t\in J,\quad V_{i}(v)=h_{i},\;i=1,\ldots,n,

(2.1)

along with the boundary conditions

V_{i}(v)\equiv\sum_{j=0}^{n-1}\left(\alpha_{j}^{i}\,v^{(j)}(a)+\beta_{j}^{i}\,v^{(j)}(b)\right),\qquad i=1,\ldots,n,

(2.2)

where

L_{n}\,v(t)\equiv v^{(n)}(t)+a_{1}(t)\,v^{(n-1)}(t)+\cdots+a_{n-1}(t)\,v^{\prime}(t)+a_{n}(t)\,v(t),\quad t\in J:=[a,b],

(2.3)

being
\alpha_{j}^{i}
,
\beta_{j}^{i}
, and
h_{i}
real constants for all
i=1,\ldots,n
and
j=0,\ldots,n-1
, and
\sigma,\text{ }a_{k}\in\mathcal{L}^{1}(J)
for all
k=1,\ldots,n
.
\mathcal{L}^{1}(J)
is the set of 1-integrable functions, i.e.:

\mathcal{L}^{1}(J)=\{f\textup{ Lebesgue measurable function }|\textup{ }\int_{J}{|f|}<\infty\}.

In this situation, we seek solutions that belong to the space

W^{n,1}(J)=\{v\in{\cal C}^{n-1}(J),\textup{ }v^{(n-1)}\in\mathcal{AC}(J)\},

where
\mathcal{AC}(J)
is the set of absolutely continuous functions on
J
.

A common way to approach this problem is to find the inverse operator
L_{n}^{-1}
. In this case, when the uniqueness of the solution can be guaranteed, the solution can be written in the form
v(t)=L_{n}^{-1}\sigma(t)
. It is in this context that we can refer to the Green’s function,
G:J\times J\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
, associated with the linear problem of order
n
(
2.1
)–(
2.2
). This function is unique and corresponds to the integral kernel of the inverse operator
L_{n}^{-1}
, meaning that it satisfies

v(t)=\int_{a}^{b}{G(t,s)\sigma(s)\mathrm{d}s},\textup{ for all }t\in J.

In reference
[
6
]
, Section 1.4, it is proved a number of properties that the Green’s function must satisfy, which allows us to define it axiomatically as follows.

Definition 2.1
.

We say that
G\in\mathcal{C}^{n-2}(J\times J)\cap\mathcal{C}^{n}(J\times J)\setminus\{(t,t),t\in J\}
is the Green’s function related to Problem (
2.1
)–(
2.2
) if and only if it is the unique solution of problem

L_{n}(G(t,s))=0,\quad t\in J\setminus\{s\},\quad V_{i}(G(\cdot,s))=0,\,i=1,\ldots,n,

for any
s\in(a,b)
fixed.
Moreover, it satisfies the following jump condition at the diagonal of the square of definition:
For each
t\in(a,b)
, there exist and are finite, the lateral limits

\frac{\partial^{n-1}}{\partial t^{n-1}}G(t^{-},t)=\frac{\partial^{n-1}}{\partial t^{n-1}}G(t,t^{+})\quad\text{and}\quad\frac{\partial^{n-1}}{\partial t^{n-1}}G(t,t^{-})=\frac{\partial^{n-1}}{\partial t^{n-1}}G(t^{+},t),

and additionally,

\frac{\partial^{n-1}}{\partial t^{n-1}}G(t^{+},t)-\frac{\partial^{n-1}}{\partial t^{n-1}}G(t^{-},t)=\frac{\partial^{n-1}}{\partial t^{n-1}}G(t,t^{-})-\frac{\partial^{n-1}}{\partial t^{n-1}}G(t,t^{+})=1.

This work will address both ordinary differential equations and nonlocal equations involving involution and piecewise constant arguments. Accordingly, we will undertake a review of the concept of involution
[
23
]
and the methodology employed in the study of differential equations with involution.

Definition 2.2
.

Let
A\subset\mathbb{R}
be a set containing more than one point and

is not the identity

f^{2}\equiv f\circ f=Id\textup{ on }A

f=f^{-1}\textup{ on }A.

If

, we say that

is a strong involution.

v(0)=\int_{-T}^{T}{G_{m}(0,s)\sigma(s)\mathrm{d}s}-M\int_{-T}^{T}{G_{m}(0,s)v(0)\mathrm{d}s}

from which we deduce that

v(0)\left(1+M\int_{-T}^{T}{G_{m}(0,s)\mathrm{d}s}\right)=\int_{-T}^{T}{G_{m}(0,s)\sigma(s)\mathrm{d}s}.

On the other hand,

\displaystyle v(t)

\displaystyle=\int_{-T}^{T}{G_{m}(t,s)\sigma(s)\mathrm{d}s}-M\int_{-T}^{T}{G_{m}(t,s)v(0)\mathrm{d}s}

\displaystyle=\int_{-T}^{T}{G_{m}(t,s)\sigma(s)\mathrm{d}s}-M\frac{\int_{-T}^{T}{G_{m}(0,s)\sigma(s)\mathrm{d}s}}{1+M\int_{-T}^{T}G_{m}(0,r)\mathrm{d}r}\int_{-T}^{T}{G_{m}(t,r)\mathrm{d}r}.

From the above, we can conclude that

H_{m,M}(t,s)=G_{m}(t,s)-M\frac{\int_{-T}^{T}{G_{m}(t,r)\mathrm{d}r}}{1+M\int_{-T}^{T}{G_{m}(0,r)\mathrm{d}r}}G_{m}(0,s).

(3.7)

As we will see next, expressions (
3.6
) and (
3.7
) will be of great importance, as they will allow us to deduce properties of the function
H_{m,M}(t,s)
from the already known Green’s function
G_{m}(t,s)
.

4
Constant sign characterization and properties of Green’s functions

Throughout this section we will develop techniques and comparison principles to obtain the explicit expression of the Green’s functions related to different problems and to analyze the set of parameters involved, with a view to establishing whether they have a constant sign on the square
J\times J
.

We will start by deriving a formula that, in a particular case, allows us to establish a relationship between the Green’s functions
H_{m_{0},M_{0}}
and
H_{m_{1},M_{1}}
associated with the problem (
3.1
) for the parameters
m=m_{0}
and
M=M_{0}
, and
m=m_{1}
and
M=M_{1}
, respectively. Subsequently, a novel methodology will be devised with the objective of establishing a relationship between the parameters
m
and
M
associated with problem (
3.1
) that delineates the regions where the Green’s function exhibits a constant sign. Finally, we will apply this new methodology to a previously studied problem and verify that it yields the already known results.

4.1
Relationship between Green’s functions
H_{m_{0},M_{0}}
and
H_{m_{1},M_{1}}

In this part, we will deduce a relationship between the Green’s functions associated with Problem (
3.1
) as a function of the parameters
m
and
M\in\mathbb{R}
. We will follow the ideas developed in
[
10
]
.

Let
M_{0}^{i}
,
M_{1}^{i}
,
m_{0}^{i}
and
m_{1}^{i}
,
i=1,\ldots n-1
be real constants. We consider two distinct problems:

\displaystyle L_{n}v_{0}(t)+\sum_{i=0}^{n-1}m_{0}^{1}\,v_{0}^{(i}(-t)+\sum_{i=0}^{n-1}M_{0}^{i}\,v_{0}^{(i}([t])

\displaystyle=\sigma(t),\quad t\in J,\quad V_{i}(v_{0})=0.

(4.1)

\displaystyle L_{n}v_{1}(t)+\sum_{i=0}^{n-1}m_{1}^{i}\,v_{1}^{(i}(-t)+\sum_{i=0}^{n-1}M_{1}^{i}\,v_{1}^{(i}([t])

\displaystyle=\sigma(t),\quad t\in J,\quad V_{i}(v_{1})=0.

(4.2)

From the two previous expressions, we arrive at the following equality for all
t\in J
:

\displaystyle L_{n}v_{0}(t)+\sum_{i=0}^{n-1}m_{1}^{i}v_{0}^{(i}(-t)+\sum_{i=0}^{n-1}M_{1}^{i}v_{0}^{(i}([t])

\displaystyle\quad=\sum_{i=0}^{n-1}(m_{1}^{i}-m_{0}^{i})v_{0}^{(i}(-t)+\sum_{i=0}^{n-1}(M_{1}^{i}-M_{0}^{i})v_{0}^{(i}([t])+\sigma(t),\quad t\in J,\quad V_{i}(v_{0})=0.

Let
H_{m_{0},M_{0},c}(t,s)
denote the Green’s function associated with Problem (
4.1
) and
H_{m_{1},M_{1},c}(t,s)
denote the Green’s function associated with Problem (
4.2
). It follows that

\displaystyle v_{0}(t)=\int_{-T}^{T}{H_{m_{0},M_{0},c}(t,s)\sigma(s)\mathrm{d}s},

\displaystyle v_{1}(t)=\int_{-T}^{T}{H_{m_{1},M_{1},c}(t,s)\sigma(s)\mathrm{d}s}.

Therefore, we have that

\displaystyle v_{0}(t)

\displaystyle=\int_{-T}^{T}H_{m_{1},M_{1},c}(t,s)\left(\sum_{i=0}^{n-1}(m_{1}^{i}-m_{0}^{i})v_{0}^{(i)}(-s)+\sum_{i=0}^{n-1}(M_{1}^{i}-M_{0}^{i})v_{0}^{(i)}(s)\right)\,\mathrm{d}s

\displaystyle\quad+\int_{-T}^{T}H_{m_{1},M_{1},c}(t,s)\sigma(s)\,\mathrm{d}s

\displaystyle=\sum_{i=0}^{n-1}(m_{1}^{i}-m_{0}^{i})\int_{-T}^{T}H_{m_{1},M_{1},c}(t,s)\left(\int_{-T}^{T}\frac{\partial}{\partial s^{i}}H_{m_{0},M_{0},c}(-s,r)\sigma(r)\,\mathrm{d}r\right)\,\mathrm{d}s

\displaystyle\quad+\sum_{i=0}^{n-1}(M_{1}^{i}-M_{0}^{i})\int_{-T}^{T}H_{m_{1},M_{1},c}(t,s)\left(\int_{-T}^{T}\frac{\partial}{\partial s^{i}}H_{m_{0},M_{0},c}(s,r)\sigma(r)\,\mathrm{d}r\right)\,\mathrm{d}s

\displaystyle\quad+\int_{-T}^{T}H_{m_{1},M_{1},c}(t,s)\sigma(s)\,\mathrm{d}s

\displaystyle=\sum_{i=0}^{n-1}(m_{1}^{i}-m_{0}^{i})\int_{-T}^{T}\left(\int_{-T}^{T}H_{m_{1},M_{1},c}(t,r)\frac{\partial}{\partial r^{i}}H_{m_{0},M_{0},c}(-r,s)\,\mathrm{d}r\right)\sigma(s)\,\mathrm{d}s

\displaystyle\quad+\sum_{i=0}^{n-1}(M_{1}^{i}-M_{0}^{i})\int_{-T}^{T}\left(\int_{-T}^{T}H_{m_{1},M_{1},c}(t,r)\frac{\partial}{\partial r^{i}}H_{m_{0},M_{0},c}(r,s)\,\mathrm{d}r\right)\sigma(s)\,\mathrm{d}s

\displaystyle\quad+\int_{-T}^{T}H_{m_{1},M_{1},c}(t,s)\sigma(s)\,\mathrm{d}s.

Thus, since previous equalities hold for any
\sigma\in\mathcal{L}^{1}(J)
, we finally arrive at

\begin{split}H_{m_{0},M_{0},c}(t,s)&=\sum_{i=0}^{n-1}(m_{1}^{i}-m_{0}^{i})\int_{-T}^{T}{H_{m_{1},M_{1},c}(t,r)\frac{\partial}{\partial{r}^{i}}H_{m_{0},M_{0},c}(-r,s)\mathrm{d}r}\\
&\quad+\sum_{i=0}^{n-1}(M_{1}^{i}-M_{0}^{i})\int_{-T}^{T}{H_{m_{1},M_{1},c}(t,r)\frac{\partial}{\partial{r}^{i}}H_{m_{0},M_{0},c}([r],s)\mathrm{d}r}+H_{m_{1},M_{1},c}(t,s).\end{split}

(4.3)

In the particular case when
m_{0}^{i}=m_{1}^{i}
and
M_{0}^{i}=M_{1}^{i}
for
i=1,\ldots,n-1
, we will denote
m_{0}^{0}=m_{0}
,
m_{1}^{0}=m_{1}
,
M_{0}^{0}=M_{0}
, and
M_{1}^{0}=M_{1}
. Thus, we arrive at the following expression:

\begin{split}H_{m_{0},M_{0}}(t,s)&=(m_{1}-m_{0})\int_{-T}^{T}{H_{m_{1},M_{1}}(t,r)H_{m_{0},M_{0}}(-r,s)\mathrm{d}r}\\
&\quad+(M_{1}-M_{0})\int_{-T}^{T}{H_{m_{1},M_{1}}(t,r)H_{m_{0},M_{0}}([r],s)\mathrm{d}r}+H_{m_{1},M_{1}}(t,s).\end{split}

(4.4)

From expression (
4.4
), we can obtain a series of properties of these Green’s functions.

4.2
Constant sign region of
H_{m,M}

The following section will focus on an analysis of solutions with constant signs. Our objective is to delineate, as a function of the parameters
m
and
M
, the region where the Green’s function
H_{m,M}(t,s)
assumes a positive or negative value for all
(t,s)\in J\times J
. The following section outlines the steps to determine when the function is positive. The procedure is identical in the case where the objective is to ensure that the function is negative.

This procedure is valid provided that the parameters
(m,M)
are not eigenvalues of the problem under consideration. It is therefore necessary to calculate these values in advance and, based on them, to study the values of the parameters
(m,M)
where the Green’s function is positive.

Our main objective will be to find, if it exists, the smallest
M_{0}\in\mathbb{R}
such that

\min_{(t,s)\in J\times J}H_{m,M_{0}}(t,s)=0

for each fixed
m\in\mathbb{R}
. If for some
m\in\mathbb{R}
, the region where the Green’s function is positive is non-empty, and if
\overline{M}
satisfies
H_{m,\overline{M}}>0
, then, by the decreasing property of
H_{m,M}
with respect to
M
, as stated in Proposition
4.1
,
H_{m,M}\geq 0
if
M
is greater than the largest eigenvalue less than
\overline{M}
and
M\leq M_{0}
with
M_{0}>\overline{M}
.

From equation (
4.5
), it can be verified that the following equality holds (provided the integral is non-zero):

M_{0}=\frac{-H_{m,M_{0}}(t,s)+H_{m,M_{1}}(t,s)}{\int_{-T}^{T}{H_{m,M_{1}}(t,r)H_{m,M_{0}}([r],s)}\mathrm{d}r}+M_{1}\quad\textup{ a.e }(t,s)\in J\times J.

Since we are looking for
M_{0}
such that
\min_{(t,s)\in J\times J}H_{m,M_{0}}(t,s)=H_{m,M}(\hat{t},\hat{s})=0
for some
(\hat{t},\hat{s})\in J\times J
, then
M_{0}
must satisfy:

M_{0}=\frac{H_{m,M_{1}}(\hat{t},\hat{s})}{\int_{-T}^{T}{H_{m,M_{1}}(\hat{t},r)H_{m,M_{0}}([r],\hat{s})}\mathrm{d}r}+M_{1}.

(4.6)

Consequently, for each fixed
m
and
M_{1}
, we define the operator
\overline{T}_{m}:\mathbb{R}\times J\times J\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
as follows:

\overline{T}_{m}(M_{0},t,s)=\frac{H_{m,M_{1}}(t,s)}{\int_{-T}^{T}{H_{m,M_{1}}(t,r)H_{m,M_{0}}([r],s)}\mathrm{d}r}+M_{1}.

(4.7)

The objective would be to find the smallest
M_{0}
such that

M_{0}=\overline{T}_{m}(M_{0},\hat{t},\hat{s})

for some
(\hat{t},\hat{s})\in J\times J
.

Note that the point
(\hat{t},\hat{s})
depends on
M_{0}
and
m
, but not on
M_{1}
.

The expression (
4.6
) is independent of the parameter
M_{1}
. Therefore, as long as
M=0
is not an eigenvalue of the considered problem, we can take
M_{1}=0
, which simplifies the calculations. The expression to be studied then becomes:

M_{0}=\frac{H_{m,0}(\hat{t},\hat{s})}{\int_{-T}^{T}{H_{m,0}(\hat{t},r)H_{m,M_{0}}([r],\hat{s})}\mathrm{d}r}.

(4.8)

In most cases, it will not be easy to find the point
(\hat{t},\hat{s})\in J\times J
where the function
H_{m,M_{0}}
attains its minimum on
J\times J
and this minimum is
0
. In such cases, it will be necessary to approach the problem differently.

If, for some
m\in\mathbb{R}
, there exists a
\overline{M}\in\mathbb{R}
such that the Green’s function
H_{m,\overline{M}}
is well-defined and positive on
J\times J
, then
M_{0}
will be the smallest value such that
M_{0}>\overline{M}
and satisfies the equality,

M_{0}=\overline{T}_{m}(M_{0},\hat{t},\hat{s})

for some
(t,s)\in J\times J
.

It is easy to verify that, if
H_{m,0}\neq 0
, we can write

M_{0}=\overline{T}_{m}(M_{0},t,s)-\frac{H_{m,M_{0}}(t,s)}{H_{m,0}(t,s)}\overline{T}_{m}(M_{0},t,s).

(4.9)

Furthermore, in the points
(t,s)\in J\times J
where
\frac{\partial H_{m,M}}{\partial{t}}
and
\frac{\partial H_{m,M}}{\partial{s}}
with
M=M_{0}
and
M=0
are well defined, we can differentiate the previous expression and obtain the following equalities.

\displaystyle\frac{\partial}{\partial{t}}\overline{T}_{m}(M_{0},t,s)

\displaystyle=\frac{\frac{\partial H_{m,M_{0}}(t,s)}{\partial{t}}H_{m,0}(t,s)-H_{m,M_{0}}(t,s)\frac{\partial H_{m,0}(t,s)}{\partial{t}}}{H_{m,0}(t,s)^{2}}\overline{T}_{m}(M_{0},t,s)

\displaystyle-\frac{H_{m,M_{0}}(t,s)}{H_{m,0}(t,s)}\frac{\partial\overline{T}_{m}(M_{0},t,s)}{\partial{t}}

and

\displaystyle\frac{\partial}{\partial{s}}\overline{T}_{m}(M_{0},t,s)

\displaystyle=\frac{\frac{\partial H_{m,M_{0}}(t,s)}{\partial{s}}H_{m,0}(t,s)-H_{m,M_{0}}(t,s)\frac{\partial H_{m,0}(t,s)}{\partial{s}}}{H_{m,0}(t,s)^{2}}\overline{T}_{m}(M_{0},t,s)

\displaystyle-\frac{H_{m,M_{0}}(t,s)}{H_{m,0}(t,s)}\frac{\partial\overline{(}M_{0},t,s)}{\partial{s}}.

Then, if
(\hat{t},\hat{s})\in J\times J
satisfies
H_{m,M_{0}}(\hat{t},\hat{s})=0
,
\frac{\partial H_{m,M_{0}}(\hat{t},\hat{s})}{\partial{t}}=0
and
\frac{\partial H_{m,M_{0}}(\hat{t},\hat{s})}{\partial{s}}=0
, it is a critical point of
\overline{T}_{m}(M_{0},t,s)
.

Moreover if
\frac{\partial^{2}H_{m,M}(\hat{t},\hat{s})}{\partial{t}^{2}}
and
\frac{\partial^{2}H_{m,M}(\hat{t},\hat{s})}{\partial{s}^{2}}
with
M=M_{0}
and
M=0
are well defined then

\frac{\partial^{2}\overline{T}_{m}(M_{0},\hat{t},\hat{s})}{\partial{t}^{2}}=\frac{\frac{\partial^{2}H_{m,M_{0}}(\hat{t},\hat{s})}{\partial{t}^{2}}}{H_{m,0}(\hat{t},\hat{s})}\overline{T}_{m}(M_{0},\hat{t},\hat{s})

and

\frac{\partial^{2}\overline{T}_{m}(M_{0},\hat{t},\hat{s})}{\partial{s}^{2}}=\frac{\frac{\partial^{2}H_{m,M_{0}}(\hat{t},\hat{s})}{\partial{s}^{2}}}{H_{m,0}(\hat{t},\hat{s})}\overline{T}_{m}(M_{0},\hat{t},\hat{s}).

Taking this into account we can conclude that if
(\hat{t},\hat{s})\in J\times J
is a regular point of
H_{m,M}
with
M=M_{0}
and
M=0
it is a minimum or a maximum of
\overline{T}_{m}(M_{0},t,s)
.

If, for example, we identify a point
(t_{m},s_{m})\in J\times J
such that
M_{0}\geq\overline{T}_{m}(M_{0},t_{m},s_{m})
and the global maximum of
\overline{T}_{m}(M_{0},t,s)
, with
(t,s)\in J\times J
is achieved, it is clear that the point where this maximum is reached will be our point of interest. This is because, by continuity, it is at this point that the equality
M_{0}=\overline{T}_{m}(M_{0},t,s)
will be satisfied for the smallest possible
M_{0}
. The reasoning is analogous when working with
M_{0}\leq\overline{T}_{m}(M_{0},t_{m},s_{m})
and the global minimum.

Looking for a minimum or a maximum of
\overline{T}_{m}(M_{0},t,s)
with
(t,s)\in J\times J
can be computationally much more efficient than searching for a minimum of
H_{m,M_{0}}
. This is because the integral can be divided into different intervals, requiring the evaluation of
H_{m,M_{0}}
only at integer values of
t\in J
, rather than for all
t\in J
. And, evaluating
H_{m,M_{0}}
for large values of
T
can be very costly.

Following a completely analogous reasoning, but starting now from expression

H_{m_{0},M}(t,s)=H_{m_{1},M}(t,s)+(m_{1}-m_{0})\int_{-T}^{T}{H_{m_{1},M}(t,r)H_{m_{0},M}(-r,s)}\mathrm{d}r,

we would arrive at

m_{0}=\frac{H_{m_{1},M}(\hat{t},\hat{s})}{\int_{-T}^{T}{H_{m_{1},M}(t,r)H_{m_{0},M}(-r,\hat{s})}\mathrm{d}r}+m_{1}

(4.10)

where now
(\hat{t},\hat{s})\in J\times J
is the point where the function
H_{m_{0},M}
attains its minimum on
J\times J
and equals zero.

The previous formula is independent of the parameter
m_{1}
, and when it is not an eigenvalue of the considered problem, we will take
m_{1}=0
, which simplifies the calculations. Assuming that for each fixed
M\in\mathbb{R}
, there exists
\overline{m}
such that
H_{\overline{m},M}>0
, we would then seek the smallest
m_{0}>\overline{m}
that satisfies the following equality:

m_{0}=\frac{H_{0,M}(\hat{t},\hat{s})}{\int_{-T}^{T}{H_{0,M}(\hat{t},r)H_{m_{0},M}(-r,\hat{s})}\mathrm{d}r}.

The case when the point
(\hat{t},\hat{s})\in J\times J
, where the function
H_{m_{0},M}
attains its minimum, is unknown will also be analogous.

4.3
Application of the method to a known problem

In

.

m+M\neq 0
. Let

. Then, the unique solution of Problem (

2.

.

We will attempt to reach an analogous result considering the new method previously outlined.

To begin, we consider the Green’s function for Problem (
4.11
) with
M=0
, that is,

v^{\prime}(t)+m\,v(t)=\sigma(t),\quad t\in I=[0,T],\quad v(0)=v(T)

(4.12)

with
m\in\mathbb{R}
and
T\in(0,1]
.

As it is proved in
[
5
]
, we have that the expression of the Green’s function related to (

Proposition 4.5
.

For all values of the parameters
m
and
M\in\mathbb{R}
for which we can ensure the uniqueness of solution of Problem (

H_{m,M}(t,s)=-H_{-m,-M}(T-t,T-s).

Proof.

Let
v
be the unique solution of Problem (
4.11
) for a given function
\sigma\in\mathcal{L}^{1}(I)
. We define
w(t)=v(T-t)
. Then, from Problem (
4.11
) we deduce that

w^{\prime}(t)=-v^{\prime}(T-t)=m\,v(T-t)+M\,v([T-t])-\sigma(T-t),\quad t\in I,\quad w(0)=w(T).

From which we obtain that

w^{\prime}(t)-m\,w(t)-M\,w([t])=-\sigma(T-t),\quad t\in I,\quad w(0)=w(T).

Therefore, on the one hand, we would have that

w(t)=-{\int_{0}^{T}}{H_{-m,-M}(t,s)\sigma(T-s)\mathrm{d}s}=-\int_{0}^{T}{H_{-m,-M}(t,T-r)\sigma(r)\mathrm{d}r}.

On the other hand,

v(T-t)=\int_{0}^{T}{H_{m,M}(T-t,s)\sigma(s)\mathrm{d}s}.

From the two previous expressions and by the uniqueness of the Green’s function, we deduce that

H_{m,M}(t,s)=-H_{-m,-M}(T-t,T-s)\,\textup{ for all }t,s\in I,

and this concludes the proof.
∎

Obviously, we also obtain that
m+M=0
is a straight line of eigenvalues for Problem (

shared in Proposition

\displaystyle\overline{H}_{-m,-M}(s^{+},s)-\overline{H}_{-m,-M}(s^{-},s)=1.

Proposition 5.19
.

If the function
\overline{H}_{m,M}
is positive,

and

, then the minimum of
\overline{H}_{m,M}
can only be at

, where

is an integer value (either

or
s^{-}
) or at

.

Proof.

According to parts
1
,
3
,
4
and
5
of Proposition
5.6
we have the following properties:

•

\overline{H}_{m,M}(\cdot,s)
is well-defined and continuous for all
t\in\hat{J}
,

,

•

\frac{\partial\overline{H}_{m,M}(t,s)}{\partial{t}}<0
for all

,

,
t\neq-s
,

,

•

\overline{H}_{m,M}(s^{+},s)-\overline{H}_{m,M}(s^{-},s)=\overline{H}_{m,M}(s,s^{-})-\overline{H}_{m,M}(s,s^{+})=1
for all
s\in(-T,T)
,
s\notin D
,

•

\overline{H}_{m,M}(T,s)=\overline{H}_{m,M}(-T,s)
for all

.

From these properties, we can deduce that the minimum of
\overline{H}_{m,M}
occurs at

. As in the case where

, we introduce the function

, defined as

Upon calculating the second derivative, we find:

\displaystyle q^{\prime\prime}(s)

\displaystyle=-2m^{2}(\overline{H}_{m,M}(s^{-},s)+\overline{H}_{m,M}((-s)^{+},-s))

\displaystyle-m\,M(\overline{H}_{m,M}(-[s],s)+\overline{H}_{m,M}([s],-s)+\overline{H}_{m,M}([s],-s)).

When
m>0
and
M>0
, the function

is concave. Moreover

is well-defined and
\mathcal{C}^{\infty}
on
\hat{J}\setminus D
. Additionally, it is easy to verify that

.

Considering all the above, we are able to prove the result.
∎

In the general case, when

Proposition 5.20
.

When the function

is positive, it can only attain its minimum at an integer value of

) with

Taking into account Proposition
5.15
, we deduce that when

is positive,

has a constant sign (where it is well-defined). Specifically,

\frac{\partial\overline{H}_{m,M}(t,s)}{\partial s}=\begin{cases}>0,&\text{if }m>0,\\[8.0pt]
<0,&\text{if }m<0.\end{cases}

From this, we conclude that the function
\overline{H}_{m,M}(t,\cdot)
is monotonic. Considering Proposition
5.6
, we have that:

1.

The function
\overline{H}_{m,M}(t,\cdot)
is continuous for all

,

2.

\overline{H}_{m,M}(t,T)=\overline{H}_{m,M}(t,-T)

We deduce that when the function

is increasing (i.e.,

), the minimum can only occur at an integer value of

(which could be

or

) with

with

is decreasing (i.e.,

(either

or

∎

Even in the case where we do not know the point

where the function
\overline{H}_{m,M}
attains its minimum when it is positive, we can numerically approximate the region where it has a constant sign. In general, this can be useful because it allows us to delineate this region knowing only the form of the Green function.

We present Python code that allows us to approximate this region for the function
\overline{H}_{m,M}
without making any conjectures. We divide the procedure into three cases:

•

Case
m>0
and
M>0
.

This is the simplest case because we already know that

\min_{(t,s)\in\hat{J}\times\hat{J}}\overline{H}_{m,M}=\min{\{q(s^{+}),q(s^{-}),q(T)\}},

where
s\in\hat{J}
is an integer value. To approximate the region, we can use numerical variations of any of the methods previously studied, such as the fixed-point method or solving for the roots where the minimum of the function equals zero. For instance, using the second approach, we evaluate
q(T)
,
q(s^{+})
and
q(s^{-})
where
s\in\hat{J}
represents integer values, for different pairs of
(m,M)
. Subsequently, we compute the minimum value for each pair
(m,M)
. We then represent the region in terms of the values of
m
and
M
where this minimum is greater than zero. This region coincides with the region where the function
\overline{H}_{m,M}
is positive.

•

Case
m<0
and
M>0
.

In this case, we know that the minimum can only occur at integer values of
s\in\hat{J}
. Here, we use a numerical variation of the fixed-point method, as it is computationally much more efficient. This efficiency arises because it requires far fewer evaluations of the function
\overline{H}_{m,M}
, which depends on the inverse of a matrix whose size increases with
T
. From equation (
4.7
), we know that

\displaystyle\overline{T}_{m}(M,t,s)

\displaystyle=\frac{\overline{G}_{m}(t,s)}{\int_{-T}^{T}\overline{G}_{m}(t,r)\overline{H}_{m,M}([r],s)\,\mathrm{d}r}

(5.13)

\displaystyle=\frac{\overline{G}_{m}(t,s)}{\overline{H}_{m,M}([-T],s)\int_{-T}^{[-T]}\overline{G}_{m}(t,r)\,\mathrm{d}r+\cdots+\overline{H}_{m,M}([T],s)\int_{[T]}^{T}\overline{G}_{m}(t,r)\,\mathrm{d}r}.

For any
m<0
, following Theorem
5.4
we know that the function
\overline{G}_{m}
is not positive, and thus there exists at least one point
(t_{m},s_{m})\in\hat{J}\times\hat{J}
verifying that
\overline{G}_{m}(t_{m},s_{m})<0
. From equation (
4.5
) with
M_{1}=0
, we deduce that
\int_{-T}^{T}\overline{G}_{m}(t_{m},r)\overline{H}_{m,M_{0}}([r],s)\,\mathrm{d}r<0
. Finally, taking into account equation (
4.9
), we have

M_{0}>\overline{T}_{m}(M_{0},t_{m},s_{m}),

and since
(\hat{t},\hat{s})\in\hat{J}\times\hat{J}
is necessarily a maximum or minimum of
\overline{T}_{m}
, we will search for a maximum. If the maximum is reached, we will keep that value.

Thus, the Python code will compute the value of
\overline{T}_{m}
for different pairs of values
(m,M)
and search for the maximum for values
t\in\hat{J}
and integer
s
(
s^{+}
or
s^{-}
),
s\in\hat{J}
. Given this, we will represent the region, as a function of the parameters
m
and
M
, where this maximum value (
\max_{(t,s)}{\overline{T}_{m}(M,t,s)}
) is less than
M
. This region must coincide with the region where
\overline{H}_{m,M}
is positive.
To optimize the process, the code will precompute values of
\overline{H}_{m,M}([t],s)
and the different integrals of
\overline{G}_{m}
to avoid repetitive calculations in the loops. Furthermore, parallelization techniques will also be employed to improve efficiency. The Python code is included in the appendix.

•

Case
m<0
and
M<0
.

In this case, the minimum of the function
\overline{H}_{m,M}
may occur when

takes integer values, for any

, or when

with

. For integer values of

6
Existence of solution to nonlinear problems

In this section, we will present some results that allow us to deduce the existence of solutions for nonlinear problems with periodic boundary conditions.

We will obtain existence results by using the monotone method, and employing the concepts of lower and upper solutions.

6.1
Monotone method

One of the most common techniques for studying the existence and, in some cases, the construction of solutions to differential equations is the method of lower and upper solutions.

We will follow the classical approach for this type of problem (see
[
5
]
and references therein) and
[
12
]
to establish conditions under which the first order periodic problem with reflection and piecewise constant arguments,

v^{\prime}(t)=f(t,v(-t),v([t]))\quad\textup{a.e. }t\in\hat{J},\quad v(-T)=v(T),

(6.1)

with
T>0
have solution.

We assume that
f:\hat{J}\times\mathbb{R}\times\mathbb{R}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
is a Carathéodory function, that is:

•

For almost every
t\in\hat{J}
, the function
(x,y)\in\mathbb{R}\times\mathbb{R}\rightarrow f(t,x,y)\in\mathbb{R}
is continuous.

•

For all
(x,y)\in\mathbb{R}\times\mathbb{R}
, the function
t\in\hat{J}\rightarrow f(t,x,y)
is Lebesgue measurable.

•

For all
R>0
, there exists
h_{R}\in\mathcal{L}^{1}(\hat{J})
such that, if
|x|<R
and
|y|<R
, then

|f(t,x,y)|\leq h_{R}(t)\textup{ a.e. }t\in\hat{J}.

Definition 6.1
.

We say that
\alpha\in AC(\hat{J})
is a lower solution of (
6.1
) if
\alpha
satisfies

\alpha^{\prime}(t)\geq f(t,\alpha(-t),\alpha([t]))\textup{ a.e. }t\in\hat{J},\quad\alpha(-T)-\alpha(T)=0.

Definition 6.2
.

We say that
\beta\in AC(\hat{J})
is an upper solution of (
6.1
) if
\beta
satisfies

\beta^{\prime}(t)\leq f(t,\beta(-t),\beta([t]))\textup{ a.e. }t\in\hat{J},\quad\beta(-T)-\beta(T)=0.

We now present a theorem in line with the monotone method of lower and upper solutions that ensures the existence of a solution for Problem (
6.1
) under certain additional conditions.

Theorem 6.3
.

Let us assume that the following hypotheses hold:

1.

There exist
\alpha
and
\beta
, a pair of lower and upper solutions of Problem (
6.1
), such that
\beta\leq\alpha
on
[-T,T]
.

2.

The function
f
is a Carathéodory function satisfying

f(t,x_{1},y_{1})-f(t,x_{2},y_{2})\geq-m(x_{1}-x_{2})-M(y_{1}-y_{2})

for almost every
t\in\hat{J}
, with
\beta(-t)\leq x_{2}\leq x_{1}\leq\alpha(-t)
and
\beta([t])\leq y_{2}\leq y_{1}\leq\alpha([t])
.

3.

The pair
(m,M)
satisfies that the Green’s function
\overline{H}_{m,M}
associated with Problem (
5.1
)–(
5.2
) is positive. If
T\in(0,1]
, this holds when
m\in(-\frac{\pi}{4T},\frac{\pi}{4T})
and
-m<M<\frac{m}{2}(-1+\cot(mT))
.

Then, there exist two monotone sequences
(\alpha_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
and
(\beta_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
, decreasing and increasing respectively, with
\alpha_{0}=\alpha
,
\beta_{0}=\beta
, which converge uniformly to the extremal solutions on
[\beta,\alpha]
of Problem (
6.1
), respectively.

Proof.

Consider the Problem

\begin{split}v^{\prime}(t)+mv(-t)+Mv([t])&=f(t,\gamma(-t),\gamma([t]))+m\gamma(-t)+M\gamma([t])\textup{ for almost all }t\in\hat{J},\\
\quad v(-T)&=v(T)\end{split}

(6.2)

where
\gamma\in\mathcal{L}^{1}(\hat{J})
verifies that
\beta\leq\gamma\leq\alpha
. Then, by Condition
2
, we know that

\begin{split}&(\alpha-v)^{\prime}(t)+m(\alpha-v)(-t)+M(\alpha-v)([t])\\
&\geq f(t,\alpha(-t),\alpha([t]))-f(t,\gamma(-t),\gamma([t]))+m(\alpha-\gamma)(-t)+M(\alpha-\gamma)([t])=h\gamma(t)\geq 0\textup{ a.e }t\in\hat{J}\end{split}

(\alpha-v)(-T)=(\alpha-v)(T).

From the previous inequalities and the regularity of function
\gamma
, we know that
h_{\gamma}\in\mathcal{L}^{1}(\hat{J})
and we have that

(\alpha-v)(t)=\int_{-T}^{T}{\overline{H}_{m,M}(t,s)h_{\gamma}(s)\mathrm{d}s}\geq 0\,\forall\,t\in\hat{J}.

Let us now consider
v_{i}=\mathcal{T}\gamma_{i}
, where the operator
\mathcal{T}\gamma_{i}
is given by

(\mathcal{T}\gamma_{i})(t):=\int_{-T}^{T}{\overline{H}_{m,M}(t,s)[f(s,\gamma_{i}(s),\gamma_{i}([s]))+m\gamma_{i}(-s)+M\gamma_{i}([s])]\mathrm{d}s}.

(6.3)

It is easy to see that the operator
\mathcal{T}
is continuous.
Moreover,
v_{i}
is the unique solution of Problem (
6.2
) for
\gamma=\gamma_{i}\in\mathcal{L}^{1}(J)
, and suppose that
\beta\leq\gamma_{1}\leq\gamma_{2}\leq\alpha
. Then,

\begin{split}(v_{2}-v_{1})^{\prime}(t)+m(v_{2}-v_{1})(-t)+M(v_{1}-v_{2})([t])&=f(t,\gamma_{2}(-t),\gamma_{2}([t]))-f(t,\gamma_{1}(-t),\gamma_{1}([t]))\\
&\quad+m(\gamma_{2}-\gamma_{1})(-t)+M(\gamma_{2}-\gamma_{1})([t])\geq 0\textup{ en }\hat{J}.\end{split}

(v_{2}-v_{1})(-T)=(v_{2}-v_{1})(T).

Therefore,
v_{1}\leq v_{2}
on
\hat{J}
.

Consequently, we can construct the mentioned sequences
(\alpha_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
and
(\beta_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
as follows. We take
\alpha_{0}=\alpha
,
\beta_{0}=\beta
,
\alpha_{n+1}=\mathcal{T}\alpha_{n}
and
\beta_{n+1}=\mathcal{T}\beta_{n}
for all
n\in\mathbb{N}
. Moreover, the sequences
(\alpha_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
and
(\beta_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
are monotone non increasing and non decreasing respectively, and are bounded on
[\beta,\alpha]
. By Dini’s Theorem, we can ensure that both sequences convergence uniformly on
\hat{J}
.

It is easily verified that the sequences
(\alpha_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
and
(\beta_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
converge to the extremal solutions
\phi=\mathcal{T}\phi
and
\psi=\mathcal{T}\psi
of Problem (
6.1
).
∎

In a similar manner, we arrive at the following Theorem.

Theorem 6.4
.

Assume that the following hypotheses are satisfied:

1.

There exist
\alpha
and
\beta
, a pair of lower solution and upper solution of Problem (
6.1
), such that
\alpha\leq\beta
on
[-T,T]
.

2.

The function
f
is a Carathéodory function satisfying

f(t,x_{1},y_{1})-f(t,x_{2},y_{2})\leq-m(x_{1}-x_{2})-M(y_{1}-y_{2})

for almost every
t\in\hat{J}
with
\alpha(-t)\leq x_{2}\leq x_{1}\leq\beta(-t)
and
\alpha([t])\leq y_{2}\leq y_{1}\leq\beta([t])
.

3.

The pair
(m,M)
satisfies that the Green’s function
\overline{H}_{m,M}
associated with Problem (
5.1
)–(
5.2
) is negative. When
T\in(0,1]
, this holds for
m\in(-\frac{\pi}{4T},\frac{\pi}{4T})
and
-\frac{m}{2}(1+\cot(mT))<M<-m
.

Then, there exist two monotone sequences
(\alpha_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
,
(\beta_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
, increasing and decreasing respectively, with
\alpha_{0}=\alpha
,
\beta_{0}=\beta
, which converge uniformly to the extremal solutions on
[\alpha,\beta]
of (
6.1
), respectively.

To approximate the sequences
(\alpha_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
and
(\beta_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
, we start with
\alpha_{0}=T/2
and
\beta_{0}=-T/2
, and calculate the successive elements iteratively such that
\alpha_{n+1}=\mathcal{T}(\alpha_{n})
and
\beta_{n+1}=\mathcal{T}(\beta_{n})
, where
\mathcal{T}
is given by equation (
6.3
). We will approximate the integral using Riemann sums, with
2n_{1}
being the number of intermediate points and deltaX the step between each point. In this case, to obtain an accurate result, we only need to calculate
n=10
iterations for each sequence.

To optimize the process, we will pre-compute the evaluations of the function
\overline{H}_{m,M}
that we need and store them in a matrix.

We find that by approximating the integral with Riemann sums, we achieve satisfactory results. Otherwise, we could try using other methods, such as the trapezoidal rule, Simpson’s method, or Monte Carlo methods.
