Tuft cells protect against intestinal inflammation through histone deacetylase 3
Authors: Emily M. Eshleman, Taylor Rice, Amanda Waddell, Samarth Kumar, Bailey J. Didriksen, Laura Engleman, Sybele Vidyant, Mohammed Sayed, Rebekah Karns, Hee-Woong Lim, Lee A. Denson, Theresa Alenghat
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
While intestinal tuft cells are well known to promote type 2 immunity, their role in inflammatory bowel disease (IBD) remains unclear. Here, we show that tuft cells are reduced in the distal intestine of pediatric IBD patients and that tuft cell-deficient mice exhibited increased susceptibility to intestinal inflammation. Histone deacetylase 3 (HDAC3) expression in intestinal stem cells is essential for tuft cell differentiation. Interestingly, single-cell analyses revealed that HDAC3 was also enriched in mature tuft cells compared to other differentiated epithelial populations. Generation of tuft cell-specific HDAC3 knockout mice (HDAC3ΔTuft) showed that expression of canonical tuft cell pathways were impaired in HDAC3ΔTuft mice, suggesting that HDAC3 regulates tuft cell-intrinsic function. HDAC3ΔTuft mice did not exhibit a significant difference in tuft cell numbers, however loss of HDAC3 specifically in tuft cells promoted colitis. Transcriptional analyses of ileal and colonic epithelium demonstrated dampened repair responses in HDAC3ΔTuft mice relative to control mice. Furthermore, HDAC3 inhibition in human colonoids similarly downregulated tuft cell-associated cytokines and eicosanoid pathways that promote epithelial repair. Collectively, these data indicate that enhancing tuft cell function may prevent or treat IBD, and that tuft cell-intrinsic regulation by HDAC3 limits susceptibility to intestinal damage and inflammation.

Introduction

Inflammatory bowel disease (IBD), encompassing Crohn’s disease and ulcerative colitis, is a chronic, relapsing disorder of the gastrointestinal tract characterized by dysregulated immune responses and epithelial damage in the intestine. The intestinal epithelium serves as a critical interface between the luminal environment and the host immune system and is essential for maintaining barrier integrity and orchestrating immune homeostasis. Intestinal epithelial cells are not passive physical barriers but actively regulate inflammation through the secretion of antimicrobial peptides, cytokines, and metabolic signals that shape immune cell function.1 Disruptions in epithelial function, whether through genetic mutations, microbiota dysbiosis, or environmental stressors, can contribute to epithelial barrier breakdown, aberrant immune activation, and chronic inflammation, all common characteristics of IBD.2,3 Thus, understanding fundamental intestinal epithelial biology is essential for identifying novel therapeutic targets to restore intestinal immune homeostasis and mitigate disease progression.

Tuft cells are a rare, specialized epithelial lineage that play a critical role in intestinal immunity by sensing luminal cues and coordinating type 2 immune responses. Characterized by their unique morphology and expression of chemosensory and immune-modulatory molecules, tuft cells act as sentinels within the intestinal epithelium, detecting microbial and helminth-derived signals through taste-receptor signaling pathways.4–7 Upon activation, tuft cells produce interleukin-25 (IL-25), which initiates a feedforward loop involving group 2 innate lymphoid cells (ILC2s) and downstream cytokine signaling to regulate mucosal immunity and tissue homeostasis.8–10

Emerging evidence suggests that intestinal tuft cells contribute to epithelial regeneration, tissue repair, and reduction in chronic inflammation.11–15 Consistent with recent findings in adult IBD patients,15,16 we have found that newly-diagnosed pediatric IBD patients exhibit reduced numbers of intestinal tuft cells, provoking the hypothesis that tuft cells may have a broad functional role in controlling intestinal inflammation. Indeed, genetic ablation of tuft cells increased susceptibility to dextran sulfate sodium (DSS)-induced damage and inflammation, while tuft cell expansion provided protection. In recent work, we found that stem cell expression of the epigenetic modifying enzyme, histone deacetylase 3 (HDAC3), is essential for tuft cell differentiation.17 In addition to stem cells, we found that HDAC3 is also highly enriched in mature tuft cells, however its role in tuft cells is not known. Interestingly, tuft cell-specific deletion of HDAC3 in mice impaired expression of tuft cell cytokines, and altered receptor signaling and tissue repair pathways. Furthermore, mice with loss of HDAC3 function only in tuft cells were more susceptible to colitis, and patient-derived organoid studies indicated parallel regulation in human colon. Therefore, enhancing tuft cell responses, potentially through HDAC3, may be a therapeutic strategy for treating or preventing intestinal inflammation.

Results

Tuft cells are reduced in the intestine of pediatric IBD patients

Previous studies have demonstrated that adult Crohn’s disease (CD) and ulcerative colitis (UC) patients have reduced intestinal tuft cell numbers.15,16 To investigate whether tuft cells are changed at the time of IBD diagnosis, we analyzed tuft cell-associated gene expression data from intestinal biopsies of non-inflamed control and newly-diagnosed, treatment-naive pediatric CD and UC patients.18,19 Biopsies were obtained during initial diagnostic endoscopy. The distal small intestine or ileum is a common site of inflammation in CD, which can also involve the colon, whereas UC is primarily restricted to the colon and rectum.18–21 Tuft cell-associated genes, including essential lineage transcription factors POU class 2 homeobox 3 (POU2F3), co-activator POU Class 2 Homeobox Associating Factor 2 (POU2AF2), and taste-cell ion channel Transient Receptor Potential, Melastatin 5 (TRPM5), were significantly reduced in ileal biopsies from pediatric CD patients (n = 243) compared to control non-IBD samples (n = 43) (Fig. 1A).18,22 Furthermore, fluorescence tissue staining revealed that tuft cells were reduced in ileal biopsies collected from an independent cohort of CD patients, compared to controls (Fig. 1B, C). In addition to CD, pediatric UC patients (n = 43) similarly exhibited decreased tuft cell-associated genes in rectal biopsies, compared to non-IBD controls (n = 55) (Fig. 1D).19 Consistent with these transcriptional differences, tuft cells were reduced in the colon of pediatric UC patients compared to colonic tissue from healthy non-IBD controls (Fig. 1E, F). Even though patient heterogeneity is expected, these data, in combination with past adult studies, suggest that decreased tuft cells in the distal intestine represents a shared feature of CD and UC patients at the time of initial diagnosis.

Loss of tuft cells increases susceptibility to intestinal damage and inflammation

Reduction in intestinal tuft cell numbers in IBD patients provoked the hypothesis that tuft cells may promote protection against intestinal inflammation. To test this, mice lacking tuft cells through genetic deletion of the tuft cell-specific transcription factor Pou2f3 (Pou2f3−/−) were subjected to DSS-induced colitis. Immunofluorescence staining confirmed the absence of tuft cells in the colonic epithelium of Pou2f3−/− mice compared to wild-type littermate controls (Fig. 2A, B). Following DSS treatment, tuft cell-deficient mice displayed greater weight loss compared to wild-type controls (Fig. 2C). Consistent with increased intestinal injury, DSS-treated Pou2f3−/− mice developed markedly shorter colons compared to wild-type controls (Fig. 2D), while colon lengths were unaffected under naïve conditions (Supplementary Fig. S1A). The colonic mucosa of Pou2f3−/− mice contained more neutrophil accumulation relative to controls (Fig. 2E; Supplementary Fig. S1B). This was accompanied by elevated levels of the proinflammatory cytokine tumor necrosis factor α (TNFα) (Fig. 2F; Supplementary Fig. S1C) and increased luminal concentrations of the inflammatory biomarker lipocalin-2 (Fig. 2G). Histological examination confirmed colitis in control mice, and more severe, multifocal ulceration, crypt loss, and inflammatory cell infiltration in colons harvested from DSS exposed tuft cell-deficient Pou2f3−/− mice (Fig. 2H, I). Taken together, these findings suggest that tuft cells improve preservation of intestinal integrity and restrain inflammation during colitis.

Tuft cell expansion decreases susceptibility to colitis

Tuft cells can be expanded by administration of the metabolite succinate, which activates a tuft cell-ILC2 feed-forward loop that increases tuft cell numbers.5–7 Consistent with prior studies, succinate administration increased tuft cell abundance in the colon (Fig. 3A, B; Supplementary Fig. S2A) and ileum (Supplementary Fig. S2B, C). To determine whether increased tuft cell numbers protect against intestinal inflammation, tuft cell sufficient mice (Pou2f3+/+) were treated with succinate prior to DSS exposure. Succinate administration reduced DSS-induced intestinal damage and inflammation, as evidenced by decreased weight loss (Fig. 3C), longer colon lengths (Fig. 3D), and decreased fecal lipocalin-2 levels (Fig. 3E). In contrast to published findings for the small intestine,7 succinate treatment did not alter colon length in naïve mice (Supplementary Fig. S2D). The ileum has been implicated as playing a role in mouse models of colitis, including DSS,23–25 and, consistent with previous work,26–29 villus blunting and mild inflammatory cell infiltration occurred in the ileum of mice with DSS colitis (Supplementary Fig. S2E). Interestingly, histological analyses of the colon demonstrated reduced tissue injury in succinate-treated mice, whereas vehicle-treated animals exhibited epithelial damage, ulceration, crypt loss, and inflammatory cell infiltration in the distal and proximal colon (Fig. 3F, G; Supplementary Fig. S2E). To determine whether tuft cells mediate this outcome, we compared Pou2f3−/− mice. In contrast to tuft cell-sufficient Pou2f3+/+ mice, succinate had no effect on DSS-driven weight loss (Fig. 3C), colon length (Fig. 3D), or lipocalin-2 concentration (Fig. 3E) in tuft cell-deficient Pou2f3−/− mice. Furthermore, histological examination of the colon demonstrated severe, multifocal epithelial ulceration, crypt loss, and inflammatory cell infiltration in Pou2f3−/− mice in both the absence and presence of succinate (Fig. 3F, G; Supplementary Fig. S2E). Together, these data demonstrate that tuft cells decrease susceptibility to damage-induced colitis and suggest that increasing tuft cells may have therapeutic potential for preventing or treating intestinal inflammation.

Histone deacetylase 3 in tuft cells instructs protective epithelial barrier function

Previous work has shown that the epigenetic modifying enzyme HDAC3 is essential for maintaining intestinal barrier function, controlling inflammation, and promoting tuft cell differentiation.17,30–33 Interestingly, single-cell analyses of intestinal epithelial data34 revealed that Hdac3 expression was enriched in tuft cells compared to other differentiated epithelial cell lineages (Fig. 4A). Deletion of HDAC3 at the progenitor stage or using the villin-cre model precludes direct assessment of HDAC3 function in mature tuft cells.17,33 Thus, to directly investigate the tuft cell-intrinsic role of HDAC3, we generated a new mouse model to specifically deplete HDAC3 in committed tuft cells (HDAC3ΔTuft) (Fig. 4B). To comprehensively assess how HDAC3 impacted tuft cell-intrinsic pathways, tuft cells were sort-purified from the ileum of HDAC3FF and littermate HDAC3ΔTuft mice and subjected to RNA-sequencing. These analyses identified numerous genes that were significantly upregulated (red) and downregulated (blue) in tuft cells following HDAC3 deletion (Fig. 4C). Tuft cells use G protein chemosensory signal transduction pathways to detect luminal signals and express eicosanoids, such as prostaglandins, that improve mucosal integrity and tissue repair.35 Consistent with these roles, tuft cells from HDAC3ΔTuft mice exhibited downregulation of multiple key pathways, including: (i) chemosensory G-protein signaling and taste-cell pathways, such as Gnat3 and Gnb1, which encode subunits of gustducin, as well as Trpm5 and choline O-acetyltransferase (Chat) (green); (ii) eicosanoid and prostaglandin biosynthesis, including Arachidonate 5-lipoxygenase (Alox5), Prostaglandin-endoperoxide Synthase 1 (Ptgs1), Prostaglandin-endoperoxide synthase 2 (Ptgs2), and Leukotriene C4 synthase (Ltc4s) (purple); and (iii) regulation of inflammation, including Il25 and Tslp (pink) (Fig. 4D). These results suggest that HDAC3 broadly regulates sensory and immunomodulatory functions of tuft cells.

Tuft cells are key initiators of type 2 immune responses in the intestine through secretion of several cytokines and lipid mediators which activate and sustain group 2 innate lymphoid cells (ILC2s).8–10 Consistent with reduced expression of tuft cell effector mediators, local ILC2 populations were reduced in both the ileal (top panel) and colonic (bottom panel) lamina propria of HDAC3ΔTuft mice (Fig. 4E). Although tuft cell transcriptional profiling focused on the ileum where tuft cells are more abundant, RNA-sequencing datasets from IL-25 reporter-sorted tuft cells5 demonstrated comparable Hdac3 expression in tuft cells from the ileum and colon (Supplementary Fig. S3A). While regional differences in tuft cell receptor expression have been described,5 these findings suggest that HDAC3 may play a conserved role in regulating tuft cell programs in the distal intestine.

Tuft cell-intrinsic HDAC3 is required for protection against damage-induced colitis

Given that tuft cells decrease the severity of colitis and HDAC3 directs functional transcriptional programs in tuft cells, we next tested whether tuft cell-intrinsic HDAC3 is required for protection against intestinal damage and inflammation. To address this, littermate HDAC3FF and HDAC3ΔTuft mice were subjected to DSS-induced colitis. HDAC3ΔTuft mice exhibited increased weight loss compared to HDAC3FF controls (Fig. 5A). Furthermore, DSS-treated HDAC3ΔTuft mice displayed significantly shorter colons than HDAC3FF mice (Fig. 5B), whereas baseline colon lengths were similar (Supplementary Fig. S3B). Examination of the lamina propria revealed increased neutrophil infiltration in the colon of HDAC3ΔTuft mice relative to floxed controls (Fig. 5C; Supplementary Fig. S3C). In addition, mice lacking tuft cell-intrinsic HDAC3 demonstrated elevated levels of the inflammatory marker lipocalin-2 in their stool (Fig. 5D). Histologically, colonic tissue from HDAC3ΔTuft mice displayed more severe epithelial erosion, crypt loss, and inflammatory infiltrates compared to controls (Fig. 5E, F). Tuft cell numbers did not differ significantly in the distal intestine of HDAC3FF and HDAC3ΔTuft mice (Supplementary Fig. S3D, E), suggesting that the susceptibility to colitis does not primarily reflect loss of tuft cells. Instead, these data demonstrate that HDAC3 in tuft cells functions to improve their protective capacity during colonic damage and inflammation. To test whether tuft cell-mediated protection induced by succinate also requires HDAC3, HDAC3FF and HDAC3ΔTuft mice were treated with succinate prior to DSS exposure. Consistent with earlier data, succinate protected HDAC3FF control mice from DSS-induced colitis, as evidenced by reduced body weight loss (Fig. 5G), less colonic shortening (Fig. 5H), and lower TNFα production (Fig. 5I). In contrast, succinate failed to protect HDAC3ΔTuft mice (Fig. 5G–I), indicating that succinate-induced protection depends, in part, on HDAC3-sensitive programs in tuft cells.

HDAC3 regulates tuft cell gene expression in murine and human colon

Consistent with decreased mRNA profiles in ileal tuft cells (Fig. 4D), flow cytometric analyses revealed reduced protein expression of ALOX5 and CHAT in tuft cells in the ileum of HDAC3ΔTuft mice compared to HDAC3FF controls (Fig. 6A; Supplementary Fig. S4A–C). Interestingly, similar reduction in ALOX5 and CHAT were observed in tuft cells in the colon (Fig. 6B), supporting that HDAC3-dependent mRNA regulation corresponds with protein in tuft cells of the ileum and colon. To then broadly investigate the role of tuft cell-intrinsic HDAC3 in regulating pathways that affect repair and immune responses in the colon, we directly compared colonic epithelium of naïve HDAC3FF and HDAC3ΔTuft mice. While loss of tuft cell-intrinsic HDAC3 had little to no impact on tuft cell numbers in the colon (Supplementary Fig. S3D, E), the expression of several tuft cell-associated genes, including the tuft cell-specific cytokines, and eicosanoid and epithelial restitution genes, were reduced in the colon of HDAC3ΔTuft mice relative to controls (Fig. 6C), mirroring regulatory pathways identified in the ileum (Fig. 4D).

To determine whether this regulatory function occurs in human intestine, colonic organoids (colonoids) generated from biopsies of healthy, non-IBD patients were employed. Colonoids were cultured in differentiation media supplemented with IL-13 to induce tuft cell differentiation.14,17 Interestingly, treatment of human colonoids with the HDAC3-selective inhibitor (HDAC3i) RGFP966 for 24 hrs had no effect of tuft cell numbers or POU2F3 expression (Fig. 6D, E), however tuft cell effector genes that were altered in mice were also markedly suppressed in human colonic epithelium due to HDAC3 inhibition (Fig. 6E). Taken together, these findings identify HDAC3 as a conserved regulator of transcriptional programs that underlie tuft cell function and mucosal repair.

Discussion

Tuft cells are specialized epithelial sentinels that have emerged as central mediators of intestinal immunity. While the role of tuft cells in type 2 immunity and host defense is well established,4–10 their contribution in intestinal inflammation and IBD remains less understood. In this study, we provide new evidence that tuft cells dampen damage-induced colonic inflammation and maintain mucosal homeostasis and we identify HDAC3 as a central regulator of tuft cell biology.

Prior work showed that HDAC3 is required in intestinal stem cells to permit tuft cell differentiation.17,33 Deletion of HDAC3 at this progenitor stage results in failure to generate tuft cells. Here, we extend these findings by showing that HDAC3 is required cell-intrinsically within committed tuft cells to support their functional programs. Loss of HDAC3 in tuft cells impairs key effector programs, including chemosensory signaling, eicosanoid production, and type 2 cytokine expression. Genes central to taste receptor signaling, including Gnat3 and Gnb1, which encode critical subunits of the G protein gustducin, and Trpm5, are downregulated, consistent with prior work showing these components are required for berberine signaling and protection against colitis.36 Taken together, these findings indicate that HDAC3 functions upstream of taste receptor signaling to instruct tuft cell effector programs and that loss of tuft cell-intrinsic HDAC3 increases susceptibility to colonic damage and inflammation, establishing HDAC3 as a critical regulator of mucosal protection.

Tuft cells produce a variety of cytokines and immunomodulatory factors, and it is likely that the pathways involved are multifactorial. Tuft cells are the primary source of intestinal IL-25, and while IL-25 is traditionally associated with type 2 immunity, its role in colitis appears to be context-dependent. For instance, studies have shown that neutralization or deletion of IL-25 or its receptor IL-17RB limits inflammation in murine colitis models,37,38 however it has also been found that exogenous IL-25 administration protects in the same models.39 Furthermore, IL-25 can also suppress Th17 and Th1 responses,40–42 suggesting tuft cells may dampen pathogenic T cell responses that drive inflammation. In addition to IL-25, tuft cells secrete TSLP, which suppresses Th1/Th17-mediated inflammation and protects against DSS-induced colitis.43

Our analyses revealed that tuft cell-intrinsic loss of HDAC3 downregulates transcriptional networks involved in eicosanoid biosynthesis, including genes required for prostaglandin and leukotriene production. This is particularly relevant in the context of IBD as leukotrienes have been associated with exacerbating inflammation,44,45 while the prostaglandins PGE2 and PGD2 promote barrier integrity, tissue repair, and immune regulation.46–52 Therefore, tuft cells may direct the balance between inflammatory and reparative responses in the intestine through HDAC activity. In addition to lipid mediators, recent studies also showed that tuft cells can produce acetylcholine via CHAT expression.53,54 Acetylcholine production is necessary to enhance goblet cell mucus secretion and Paneth cell antimicrobial peptide production,55–57 both crucial for reinforcing barrier integrity and limiting intestinal inflammation. Consistent with this, colonic delivery of acetylcholine has been shown to reduce DSS-induced colitis,58 highlighting a protective role of this pathway. Together, these findings reinforce that tuft cells act as central regulators of intestinal homeostasis by coordinating regulation of diverse factors, and identify HDAC3 as a key molecular switch that can calibrate effector pathways in tuft cells to limit inflammation.

Beyond these effector pathways, recent work has shown that tuft cells can serve as a reserve stem cell population that is capable of de-differentiating from tuft cells into stem cells to facilitate epithelial regeneration following injury.14,59 In addition, tuft cells can survive severe damage induced by irradiation and subsequently regenerate all intestinal epithelial cell types.14 Interestingly, Lgr5+-expressing intestinal stem cells that maintain epithelial turnover and differentiation during homeostatic conditions are rapidly lost during DSS-induced colitis.60 Therefore, the regenerative capacity of tuft cells is likely crucial for maintaining and restoring the epithelial barrier during inflammation. Consistent with this, loss of tuft cells through deletion of the protein kinase, DCLK1, worsens radiation-induced injury, further supporting a role for tuft cells in tissue repair and barrier restoration.11–13

Our work demonstrates that tuft cells decrease susceptibility to epithelial damage during DSS-induced colitis. Consistently, succinate, a metabolite that increases tuft cells, reduced inflammation in both DSS-induced colitis and a murine model of ileitis in a tuft cell-dependent manner.15 Despite the epithelial-restricted nature of succinate sensing through tuft cells, other immune cells can also respond to succinate. In fact, a recent study reported that succinate destabilizes FoxP3 expression in regulatory T cells and worsens colitis.61 Succinate does not alter colonic regulatory T cells in our facility, suggesting that succinate-dependent pathways may be context-specific with factors such as microbiota composition and disease model altering the outcome.

Although DSS primarily induces colonic injury, ileal backwash and associated mild pathology have been documented.26–29 Crohn’s disease frequently involves both the ileum and colon, and reductions in tuft cell numbers are observed in both UC and CD, suggesting that loss of tuft cells may be a shared feature contributing to intestinal inflammation. Consistent with this, the ileum has also been implicated in mouse models of colitis.23–25 Our analyses demonstrate that there are shared tuft cell transcriptional programs in both the colon and ileum that are regulated by HDAC3. These data reflect direct comparisons to control cells from the same region of the intestine, as opposed to unmatched sites. However, cells can have distinct region-specific functional roles in the small and large intestine. Given that the Pou2f3 models employed target tuft cells throughout the gastrointestinal tract, a limitation of this study is that we cannot distinguish relative contributions of ileal tuft cells from those in the colon, or decipher whether one location directly underlies the colitis phenotype. Nonetheless, these findings indicate that there are transcriptional targets in tuft cells of the ileum and colon that are similarly regulated by HDAC3, and that functional impairment of tuft cells may compromise epithelial repair and immune regulation, pre-disposing to more severe inflammation.

Reduced tuft cell numbers are a shared feature in the intestine of adult and pediatric UC and CD patients. Decreased tuft cells at the time of diagnosis provokes the possibility that impaired tuft cell activity may be an early factor that initiates or promotes disease pathogenesis. Our finding that loss of tuft cells in mice promotes more severe colitis further highlights tuft cells as a potentially critical cellular population that may alter IBD development and/or response to treatment. Interestingly, helminth infections that expand tuft cell numbers have shown potential efficacy in clinical trials for treatment of IBD.62–66 Future studies should investigate how current immune-targeted therapeutics affect tuft cells in IBD patients and test whether diet- and microbial- based approaches that induce tuft cell activity can limit chronic inflammation in IBD. HDAC3 regulates tuft cell differentiation as well as functional pathways in mature tuft cells, revealing that this enzyme may broadly instruct tuft cell biology. Thus, therapeutic strategies aimed at promoting tuft cell expansion as well as augmenting HDAC3 activity may restore intestinal homeostasis and represent new avenues for treating IBD.

Materials and methods

Mice and murine colitis model.

Pou2f3−/− mice were purchased from Jackson Laboratories and maintained in our specific-pathogen free colony at Cincinnati Children’s Hospital Medical Center (CCHMC). HDAC3FF were generated as previously described67 and crossed to Pou2f3-ERT2Cre68 to generate HDAC3ΔTuft mice. Sex- and age-matched littermate controls were used for all studies. Mice were housed up to 4-per cage in a ventilated cage system in a 12-h light/dark cycle, with free access to water and food. For induction of colitis, mice were administered 2.5% dextran sulfate-sodium (DSS: Fisher Scientific) in drinking water for 7–9 days. HDAC3ΔTuft mice were given 1 mg of tamoxifen daily via intraperitoneal injection for 5 consecutive days prior to colitis induction, then every other day until harvest. For succinate treatment, mice were provided 150 mM sodium succinate hexahydrate (Alfa Aesar) in drinking water 7 days prior to and throughout the duration of DSS treatment. All mouse studies were conducted with approval by Animal Care and Use Committees at CCHMC and followed standards set forth by Animal Research Reporting of In Vivo Experiments.

Patient samples.

Tuft cell-associated genes were compared using published RNA-sequencing analyses from the Crohn’s & Colitis Foundation–sponsored multicenter RISK study that enrolled pediatric non-IBD control, CD, and UC patients.18,19 Ileal biopsies were analyzed between control (n = 43) and CD patients (n = 243);18,22 rectal biopsies were compared between control (n = 55) and UC patients (n = 43).19 All patients were treatment-naïve, with biopsies obtained during the initial diagnostic endoscopy. Tissue staining was performed on ileal biopsies (CD) or sigmoid biopsies (UC) within the Discover Together Biobank at Cincinnati Children’s Research Foundation (IRB: 2017–3726).

Human organoid culture and immunostaining.

De-identified rectal biopsies were obtained from pediatric patients undergoing endoscopic analyses. Donors were recruited prospectively as controls for participation in the IBD Biorepository at CCHMC (IRB 2011–2285) and histologic analyses confirmed patients as healthy non-IBD controls. Rectal biopsies were incubated in 2 mg/mL collagenase type I (Invitrogen) at 37°C for 5–15 mins with vigorous mixing as previously described.17,69 Crypts were plated in Matrigel with Human Organoid Growth Media (StemCell) supplemented with 10 μM Y-27632 and 10 μM SB431542. For experiments, organoids were split and cultured in Human Organoid Differentiation Media (StemCell) supplemented with 5 μM DAPT. Organoids were treated with 20 ng/mL of human IL-13 (PeproTech) for 96 hrs then treated with 10 μM of RGFP966 (HDAC3i) or DMSO (vehicle) control for 24 hrs and assessed for gene expression or microscopy. For imaging, organoids were plated in 8-well u-slide (ibidi) and fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde for 20 min. Organoids were permeabilized for 20 min in PBS containing 0.5% Triton X-100 followed by a 30 min incubation in blocking buffer (PBS, 0.2% Triton X-100, 0.05% Tween, 1% BSA, and 1% normal donkey serum). Organoids were stained overnight at 4°C in blocking buffer containing DAPI (Sigma), AlexaFluor-647-Phalloidin (Invitrogen), and AlexaFluor-488-anti-pEGFR (ab205827, Abcam). Organoids were washed, covered with mounting media (ibidi), and imaged using a confocal microscope (Nikon).

Cell isolation.

The large intestine and ileum were harvested, opened, and washed in PBS. For IECs, tissue was placed in pre-warmed strip buffer (PBS, 5% FBS, 1 mM EDTA, 1 mM DTT) and incubated at 37°C at 45-degree angle with shaking at 180-rpm for 15-min. For lamina propria isolation, tissue was washed with PBS to remove EDTA and DTT then incubated in pre-warmed digestion buffer (RPMI with 1-mg/mL Collagenase/Dispase (Sigma)) at 37°C at 45-degree angle with shaking at 180-rpm for 30 min. After incubation, the tissue was vortexed and passed through a 70 μM cell strainer.

Flow cytometry.

Cells were stained for flow cytometry using the following antibodies diluted in FACS Buffer (2% FBS, 0.01% Sodium Azide, PBS): BV711-anti-CD326 (EpCAM) (Clone:G8.8, BioLegend), BUV395-anti-CD45.2 (Clone:104, BD Biosciences), APC-eFluor-780-anti-CD24 (Clone: M1/69, Invitrogen), PerCP-eFluor710-anti-Ly6G (Clone:1A8-Ly6g, eBioscience), eFluor-450-anti-CD11b (Clone:M1/70, eBioscience), PE-anti-SiglecF (Clone: E50–2440, BD Bioscience), PE-Dazzle-anti-TNFα (Clone: MP6-XT22, BioLegend), PerCP-710-anti-CD3 (Clone: 17A2, eBioscience), FITC-anti-CD90.2 (Clone: 53–2.1, eBioscience), PeCy7-anti-CD127 (Clone: A7R34, eBioscience), 647-anti-GATA3 (Clone: L50–823, BD Biosciences), APC-eFluor-780-anti-KLRG1 (Clone: 2F1, eBioscience), APC-anti-rabbit IgG F(ab’)2 (#31984, Invitrogen), 647-anti-goat IgG (H + L) (705–605–147, Jackson ImmunoResearch), 594-anti-rabbit IgG F(ab’)2 (711-586-152, Jackson ImmunoResearch), anti-Chat (AB144P, Sigma), anti-Alox5 (SAB1410449, Sigma), and anti-DCLK1 (ab31704, Abcam). For TNFα staining, cells were stimulated with phorbol 12-myristate 13-acetate (PMA) 50 ng/mL (Sigma-Aldrich), ionomycin 1 ug/mL (Sigma-Aldrich) and brefeldin A 10 μg/mL for 3–4 hrs at 37°C. Dead cells were excluded with the Fixable Aqua Dead Cell Stain Kit (Invitrogen). The BD Fix/Perm kit was used for intracellular staining. Samples were acquired on the BD LSRFortessa and analyzed with FlowJo Software (Treestar).

qPCR Gene expression.

Lipocalin-2 ELISA.

Fecal pellets were homogenized in PBS at a concentration of 100-mg/mL then centrifuged at high-speed for 10-mins. Supernatants were collected and lipocalin-2 levels were determined using mouse Lipocalin-2/NGAL (R&D Systems) following the manufacturer’s instructions.

Tissue histology and immunofluorescence.

Colonic tissue sections were fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde overnight at 4°C then paraffin embedded, sectioned, and stained with hematoxylin and eosin. Blind histologic scoring was performed by a board-certified veterinary pathologist based on the following criteria: inflammatory infiltration (1–5), submucosal edema (1–5), and severity of epithelial ulceration/dysplasia (1–5), for an overall maximum score of 15. For immunofluorescence, sections were deparaffinized, rehydrated, and heated in antigen retrieval buffer (Tris-EDTA buffer with 0.05% Tween-20, pH 9.0 or sodium citrate buffer, pH 6.0) for 20 min using a water bath at 95°C. Sections were washed in PBS and blocked in PBS containing 0.1% Triton X-100 and 5% normal donkey serum (NDS) (Jackson ImmunoResearch) for 45 min at room temperature. Sections were incubated with rabbit anti-DCLK1 (ab31704, Abcam), AlexaFluor-488-anti-pEGFR (ab205827, Abcam), and mouse anti-E-cadherin (610182, BD Bioscience) overnight at 4°C, washed three times in PBS containing 0.05% Tween-20, and then incubated with AlexaFluor-488-donkey anti-rabbit IgG (H + L) (Jackson ImmunoResearch: 711–545–152) and AlexaFluor-555-donkey anti-mouse IgG (H + L) (Abcam: ab150110) for 1 h at room temperature. To visualize the nuclei, sections were incubated in PBS containing DAPI for 10 min at room temperature. Stained sections were washed three times in PBS containing 0.05% Tween-20 before mounting with ProLong Gold Antifade Mountant (Invitrogen). Images were acquired with a Nikon A1 inverted confocal microscope. Images were processed using Nikon NIS-elements software and Fiji: ImageJ. Human tuft cells were quantified as the number of pEGFR+ cells per 40x field.

Bulk and single-cell RNA sequencing.

For tuft cell-specific RNA sequencing, ileal tissue was harvested from HDAC3FF and HDAC3ΔTuft mice and cut into 1-cm pieces. Epithelial cells were sequentially isolated using warm Strip Buffer (PBS, 5% FBS, 1 mM EDTA, 1 mM DTT) as described above, after which tissue was transferred to ice-cold Shaking Buffer (PBS, 43.3 mM sucrose, 54.9 mM sorbitol, 10 μM Y-27632) and shaken by hand for 3–5 min to release crypt-associated cells. Crypts were digested in 5% TrypLE at 37°C at 45°angle at 180 rpm for 10–30 min to obtain single-cell suspensions. Stripped and crypt-derived epithelial cell fractions were combined and stained for flow cytometry. Tuft cells were identified as Live, CD45−, EpCAM+, CD24hi, SiglecF+ and sorted on an Aria II (BD Biosciences) using a 100 μm nozzle into Collection Buffer (Advanced DMEM + 50% FBS + 10 μM Y-27632). For global expression analyses, RNA was isolated from 20,000–40,000 purified tuft cells using a Micro RNeasy Kit (Qiagen) and subjected to Illumina next-generation sequencing. Sequencing reads were trimmed to remove bar codes and mapped to the mouse genome (mm10) using Bowtie2.70 Differential expression analysis was performed using DESeq2 within Seqmonk (V1.47.1) (p < 0.05, fold change > 1.5).71 Ontological analysis was performed using the ClueGO application in Cytoscape and included biological processes, molecular function, immune system processes, and pathways (KEGG, Wikipathways, and Reactome). Significance cutoff for inclusion of a gene in the ontological analysis was FDR < 0.1, with no fold change requirement. Ontologies required significance at a corrected p-value < 0.05 with GO-term fusion and medium network specificity. RNA-seq data is accessible in GEO (GSE331163). For analysis of Hdac3 expression, ileal and colonic tuft cells sorted from Il25-Flare reporter mice were analyzed using RNA-seq data generated in Nadjsombati et al.5 Sequencing reads were trimmed and mapped to the mouse genome (mm10) using Bowtie2.70 Differential expression analysis was performed using DESeq2 within Seqmonk (V1.47.1).71 Hdac3 expression across epithelial cell lineages was analyzed using ileal single-cell RNA-seq dataset from Haber et al. (https://singlecell.broadinstitute.org/single_cell/study/SCP44/small-intestinal-epithelium)34

Statistical analysis.

Results are expressed as mean ± SEM. Statistical significance was determined with the Student’s t-test or one-way ANOVA. Results were considered significant at *p < 0.05; **p < 0.01; ***p < 0.001, ****p < 0.0001. Statistical significance was calculated using Prism version 7.0 (GraphPad Software).
