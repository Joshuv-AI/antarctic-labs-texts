The association between estimated glucose disposal rate and the prevalence and mortality of chronic kidney disease: a cross-sectional study with linked mortality follow-up
Authors: Pengjie Gao, Suining Xu, Yan Zhuo, Yiran Yan, Kamila Kamili, Danni Wang, Xiyang Li, Haijian Xing, Gang Tian
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundMetabolic disorders represented by insulin resistance (IR) are at risk of chronic kidney disease (CKD). Estimated glucose disposal rate (eGDR) reflects IR. The relationship between eGDR and CKD was unclear. This study aimed at discussing the association between eGDR and the prevalence of CKD in general population and the mortality of CKD patients, and compare it with other IR indicators.MethodsThe data from the National Health and Nutrition Examination Survey (NHANES) were used to conduct a cross-sectional study with linked mortality follow-up. The association between eGDR and CKD prevalence was determined using logistic regression, restricted cubic spline (RCS) analysis and stratified analysis. Receiver-operating characteristic (ROC) curves, weighted quantile sum (WQS) model, random forest and extreme gradient boost (XGBoost) machine learning models were performed to explore the importance of IR indicators components and CKD risk factors. The association between eGDR and mortality was analyzed by sub-distribution hazard model in CKD patients.ResultsAmong 29,621 participants finally included, the median eGDR was 8.64 mg/kg/min and the CKD prevalence was 12.47%. Logistic regression and stratified analysis showed eGDR was associated with CKD prevalence independently, especially in people aged 40–60 years, with overweight or impaired glucose tolerance. RCS curve indicated the association between decreased eGDR and increased CKD risk was a U-shaped curve. ROC analysis showed eGDR assessed the CKD prevalence better. WQS model implied blood glucose control level was the main influencing factor in IR components. In machine learning models, the weights of age, eGDR, uric acid and heart failure were high. During 71 months follow-up, the all-cause mortality was 23.33% and cardiovascular disease (CVD) mortality was 8.9%. Sub-distribution hazard model showed eGDR independently predicted all-cause mortality rather CVD mortality in CKD patients after adjusting for confounding factors.ConclusionseGDR was a better indicator to assess CKD risk in general population and could predict all-cause mortality rather CVD mortality in CKD patients.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s40001-025-03388-4.Keywords: Estimated glucose disposal rate, Chronic kidney disease, Insulin resistance

Background

Chronic kidney disease (CKD) is a common chronic noncommunicable disease worldwide. It is expected to rise to the fifth leading cause of death in the world by 2040 [1]. The progression of CKD causes various complications and leads to adverse outcomes, especially cardiovascular death. However, CKD is called the "silent killer" owing to the symptom invisibility and insufficient awareness in early stage [2, 3]. The new concept, cardiovascular–kidney–metabolic (CKM) syndrome also emphasizes the close reaction among metabolic disorders, cardiovascular diseases, and CKD [4].

Insulin resistance (IR) signifies the weakened sensitivity to insulin in skeletal muscle, adipose tissue or hepatocytes, thus reducing glucose uptake and utilization [5]. Early CKM syndrome components including diabetes, hypertension and obesity, which characterized by IR, may develop into CKD with the diseases progressing. The interaction between IR and CKD further leads to poor prognosis [6, 7]. With society concern about metabolic disorders growing, the treatment rates for hypertension, diabetes, dyslipidemia and hyperuricemia are gradually increasing, especially the application of cardiorenal metabolically protective drugs such as sodium glucose co-transporter-2 inhibitors, which improves the CKD incidence and prognosis. However, early identification and improvement of IR may prevent CKD in general population and improve the prognosis in CKD patients.

The glucose disposal rate acquired by the hyperinsulinemic–euglycemic clamp technique is the benchmark to quantify insulin sensitivity [8], but it is invasive and expensive. In recent years, the homeostatic model assessment for IR (HOMA-IR), triglyceride–glucose (TyG) index and its derivative indexes have been widely used as alternative IR indicators. The study showed TyG index and TyG–waist circumference-height ratio (WHtR) had stronger ability to predict CKD [9]. However, IR is influenced by multiple factors including glucose metabolism, lipid metabolism, abnormal blood pressure and obesity. eGDR, which was once used to assess the severity of IR in type 1 diabetes [10], includes more IR components. The recent studies have shown eGDR was closely related to nephropathy and cardiovascular disease (CVD) in patients with diabetes [11, 12], and could predict CVD incidence and all-cause mortality in nondiabetic people [13, 14].

However, the association between eGDR and the prevalence and mortality of CKD is still unclear, and whether eGDR is better than other IR indicators is still controversial. We designed this study to explore above issues through the National Health and Nutrition Examination Survey (NHANES) database.

Methods

Study design and participants

NHANES is a survey that collects cross-sectional data on the health information of the United States residents. The National Center for Health Statistics (NCHS) Research Ethics Review Board reviewed and gave approval to the survey. All participants signed the written consent. Given the public nature of the NHANES database, ethical approval was not required for our study.

This study included 91,351 participants in the NHANES database from 2001 to 2018. Participants were excluded if: (1) age < 18 years old; (2) pregnant; (3) complicated with urinary stones and malignancies; (4) missing key data such as waist circumference (WC), glycosylated hemoglobin (HbA1c), estimated glomerular filtration rate (eGFR) and urinary albumin-to-creatinine ratio (UACR). 29,621 participants were finally included. Insulin resistance indicators

IR indicators include metabolic score for IR (METS-IR), HOMA-IR, TyG index and its derivative indicators such as TyG-body mass index (BMI), TyG-WC, TyG-WHtR, visceral adiposity index (VAI), lipid accumulation product (LAP), etc. According to previous studies results [9], we selected HOMA-IR, TyG index and TyG-WHtR to compare with eGDR. The calculation formula of IR indicators in this study was shown below. We divided participants into 4 groups using eGDR cutoff values (< 4, 4–5.99, 6–7.99, ≥ 8), which reflect the mortality difference in diabetic patients with different IR severity [15, 16], and eGDR quartile (≤ 5.84, 5.85–8.64, 8.65–10.30, > 10.31).

Anthropometric data (including height, weight, BMI, WC, etc.) are available on the NHANES website. WC was taken at the horizontal plane located by intersection point of the superior border of the ilium and the midaxillary line. Detailed measurement was implemented according to Anthropometry Procedures Manual. Fasting plasma glucose (FPG) and insulin levels were detected after at least 8-h fast overnight.

Definition of chronic kidney disease

Participants with either albuminuria or low eGFR were diagnosed as CKD. Albuminuria was defined as UACR ≥ 30 mg/g, and low eGFR was defined as eGFR < 60 ml/min/1.73 m2. The formula described in Chronic Kidney Disease Epidemiology Collaboration was used to calculate eGFR (The detailed formula was described in Supplementary File of Methods 1.1) [17].

Definitions of covariates

We extracted blood pressure data and laboratory test data from NHANES database, including 2-h post-load plasma glucose (2 h-PG), HbA1c, lipid data, liver and kidney function data. The data of hypertension, prediabetes and diabetes history, disease treatment information, coronary artery disease (CHD) and heart failure (HF) history, gout history, smoking, drinking and physical activity habits were also extracted. Participants were considered to be receiving hypertension or dyslipidemia therapy if they answered "yes" to the question: "Are you now taking prescribed medicine for high blood pressure/hypertension?" or "Are you now taking prescribed medicine to lower blood cholesterol?". Participants were asked "Are you now taking diabetic pills to lower blood sugar? These are sometimes called oral agents or oral hypoglycemic agents." and "Are you now taking insulin?". Patients were considered to be receiving diabetes therapy if "Yes" was answered to either question.

Any of the following conditions was regarded as hypertension: (1) reported hypertension in questionnaire interview; (2) taking antihypertensive drugs or receiving antihypertensive treatment; (3) the average of three blood pressure measurements met the systolic blood pressure (SBP) ≥ 140 mmHg and/or diastolic blood pressure (DBP) ≥ 90 mmHg. Any of the following criteria was defined as diabetes: (1) reported diabetes in questionnaire interview; (2) receiving diabetes treatment; (3) HbA1c level ≥ 6.5%; (4) FBG level ≥ 7.0 mmol/L; (5) 2 h-PG ≥ 11.1 mmol/L. Participants who met any of the following criteria were diagnosed as prediabetes: (1) reported prediabetes in questionnaire interview; (2) FBG level ≥ 6.1 mmol/L and < 7.0 mmol/L; (4) 2 h-PG ≥ 7.8 mmol/L and < 11.1 mmol/L. Diagnostic threshold of hyperuricemia was serum uric acid (UA) ≥ 420 μmol/L in males or ≥ 360 μmol/L in females.

Definition of mortality

The participants death status until December 31, 2019 was determined using the NHANES public mortality file. The study endpoints contained all-cause mortality and CVD mortality. The cause of death was coded according to the codebook for the NCHS 2019 public-use linked mortality files. Death from heart diseases (054–068) or cerebrovascular disease (070) was defined as CVD mortality.

Statistical analysis

NHANES had a complex, multistage, stratified design with sampling weights to accurately estimate the prevalence of diseases. Sampling weights were used to adjust for unequal probabilities of being selected and possible bias due to nonresponse in accordance with the NHANES analysis guidelines. We used multiple imputation to fill missing values. Detailed processes of weighting and imputation were presented in Supplementary File of Methods 1.2 and 1.3.

This study used Kolmogorov–Smirnov test combined with the frequency distribution histogram to determine the normality of continuous variable data. Group comparison analysis of continuous variables was performed using variance test or Kruskal–Wallis test. Categorical variables were compared using the chi-squared test or Fisher's exact test.

Logistic regression and restricted cubic spline (RCS) curves were used to analyze the relationship between eGDR, included IR indicators and CKD and its components, adjusting for confounding factors based on comparison analysis results and previous studies. We set knots number for the RCS curves as 4. To ensure comparability among the splines for different indicators and outcomes, we chose the median value of each indicator as the reference point for the RCS curves. Sensitivity analysis was performed after lowering hypertension diagnostic criteria to SBP ≥ 130 mmHg and/or DBP ≥ 80 mmHg. Stratified analysis was used to analyze the differences in subgroups divided by age, gender, glucose status, BMI, etc.

The ability of different IR indicators, four components of eGDR to identify CKD and its components was compared using the area under the curve (AUC) calculated by the receiver-operating characteristic (ROC) analysis. Meanwhile, machine learning methods, including random forest, extreme gradient boosting (XGBoost) and light gradient boosting machine (LightGBM), were used to evaluate the importance of variables in the prediction and identification of CKD based on sample size and purpose of this study. 70% participants were divided into training set randomly, while 30% in validation set to obtain a prediction model. The shapley additive explanations (SHAP) value of each variable in the model was calculated. The higher the value, the greater the weight, and the more important the role in identifying CKD. We also used the weighted quantile sum (WQS) regression model to calculate the weights (range from 0 to 1 with a cumulative sum of 1) assigned to each component involved in IR indicators to estimate the importance of each contributed to CKD. To control its variability, we repeated the step 1000 times using bootstrap resampling methods.

Finally, the differences in all-cause mortality and CVD mortality after dividing participants into two groups according to whether eGDR was ≥ 8 were compared by cumulative incidence curves and Fine-Gray test. The association between eGDR and mortality in CKD patients was analyzed by sub-distribution hazard model taking competing risk of mortality.

Two-tailed P < 0.05 was statistically significant. All analyses were performed by R software (version 4.3.2).

Results

Baseline characteristics

Participants were 45.51 ± 18.32 years old on average, and 50.43% were female. The prevalence of CKD was 12.47%, albuminuria was 7.65%, and low eGFR was 5.35%. CKD patients were older, with more females. Most of them had smoking habits and history of diabetes, hypertension and HF, and had fewer physical activity habits. CKD patients had higher BMI, WC, FPG, HbA1c, triglycerides and UA levels. TyG index, TyG-WHtR and HOMA-IR were higher while eGDR was lower (median = 5.78) in CKD patients (Table 1). Meanwhile, the baseline characteristics after grouping according to eGDR cutoff values and quartile were compared. The results showed that as eGDR increased, WC, FPG, HbA1c, triglycerides and UA levels were lower. CKD prevalence was lowest in group with eGDR ≥ 8 and the fourth quartile (Supplementary Table 2–1 and 2–2).

Associations between eGDR and CKD

Logistic regression showed that the lower the eGDR, the higher the CKD risk. Low eGDR was independently associated with high CKD risk after adjusting for confounding factors. The CKD risk increased by 0.12 times for every 1 unit decrease in eGDR. EGDR was also independently associated with albuminuria and low eGFR (Table 2). When eGDR was divided into four groups by two standards, the CKD risk in all three groups with eGDR < 8 increased compared with eGDR ≥ 8 group, while the CKD risk in the third quartile decreased when compared with the fourth quartile group (Supplementary Table 2–3 and 2–4). The U-shaped relationship between eGDR and CKD, and its components observed in the RCS analysis supported the above results (Fig. 2A–C). When eGDR was 8.81, the CKD risk was the lowest (Odds Ratio (OR) = 0.90, 95% confidence interval (CI) 0.87–0.93, P for nonlinear < 0.001). Sensitivity analysis suggested that eGDR calculated with new hypertension criteria was still an independent risk factor for CKD and the RCS curve was still U shaped (Supplementary tables 2–5, Supplementary Fig. 2–1).

Other three IR indicators and three components of eGDR were included in logistic regression to analyze their relationship with CKD and its components. The results showed that only TyG-WHtR and HOMA-IR were independently related to CKD risk after adjusting for confounding factors (Table 2). All three components of eGDR were significantly associated with CKD in unadjusted models. However, the strengths of these associations varied considerably after considering confounding effect (Supplementary Table 2–6). Further RCS analysis showed a J-shaped curve existed in the relationship between TyG index and CKD risk, while the association between TyG-WHtR and HOMA-IR and CKD risk were U-shaped (Fig. 2D–F).

Stratified analysis

Stratified analysis showed that reduced eGDR increased CKD risk more in people aged 40–60 years. From normal to prediabetes to diabetes, the correlation between eGDR and CKD risk increased gradually as the blood glucose metabolism degree became more serious. The reduced eGDR increased CKD risk more in overweight than obesity people. Regardless of whether people had physical activity habits, increased eGDR was associated with reduced risk of CKD, and it is more obvious in people without exercise habits. There was no interaction between eGDR and hyperuricemia/gout and smoking in increasing CKD risk. The similar results were also observed in the association between eGDR and albuminuria (Fig. 3, Supplementary Fig. 2–2).

Variables importance comparison in CKD identification

ROC analysis showed eGDR had the strongest ability to identify CKD, followed by TyG-WHtR, with the AUC of 0.71 and 0.64, respectively (Fig. 4A, Table 3). In terms of the ability to identify albuminuria and low eGFR, eGDR was also superior to other IR indicators. The similar trend was presented when calculating eGDR by lower hypertension criteria (Supplementary Fig. 2-3A-C, Supplementary Tables 2–7). The results also showed that eGDR outperformed the individual components of its formula in discriminating CKD (Supplementary Fig. 2-3E-F, Supplementary Table 2–8).

Machine learning methods were performed to compare the importance of variables. The Beeswarm plot obtained by the random forest method showed the weight of eGDR was second only to age, and the waterfall plot obtained by the XGBoost method showed that the weight of eGDR was just lower than UA, age, and HF. Two SHAP values of eGDR were both higher than those of HOMA-IR, TyG index and TyG-WHtR, indicating that eGDR performed better in identifying CKD (Fig. 4D). The lightGBM model also showed eGDR was an important risk factor (Supplementary Fig. 2–4).

WQS analysis showed that among the components in four IR indicators, HbA1c had the largest contribution weight to CKD identification (weight = 0.47), followed by FPG (weight = 0.23). The presence of hypertension and WC also had a relatively large contribution weight, while fasting insulin and triglycerides accounted for a smaller proportion. This result could explain why eGDR was superior to other IR indicators and had the strongest ability to identify CKD (Fig. 4B).

Association between eGDR and mortality in CKD patients

The cumulative incidence curves indicated the median follow-up time was 71 months, (interquartile range, 40–104 months). In CKD patients, the all-cause mortality and CVD mortality of eGDR ≥ 8 group were both lower than that of eGDR < 8 group (Fig. 5). Sub-distribution hazard models indicated eGDR could predict all-cause mortality, but not CVD mortality in CKD patients after confounding factors adjustment (e.g. age, uric acid, heart failure etc.) (Table 4).

Discussion

This study determined the association between eGDR and CKD prevalence and mortality using NHANES database. The main results were as follows: (1) eGDR was independently associated with the prevalence of CKD, especially in middle-aged people with overweight or obesity and impaired glucose tolerance. The association between eGDR and increased CKD risk was a U-shaped curve. (2) Compared with the TyG index, HOMA-IR and TyG-WHtR, eGDR could better identify CKD, and the extent of glycemic control was the main influencing factor among IR components. (3) eGDR could independently predict the all-cause mortality in CKD patients.

EGDR was used to assess the severity of IR initially and was associated with all chronic complications and prognosis in type 1 diabetes patients [12, 18, 19]. Recently, Penno et al. reported that eGDR was associated with different diabetic nephropathy phenotypes [20]. This study further confirmed it and demonstrated the independent correlation between eGDR and the prevalence of CKD and its components in general population for the first time. However, a cohort study showed eGDR had no predictive value for renal function decline during follow-up [21]. This may because it included mainly albuminuric diabetic nephropathy phenotypes. Therefore, the relationship between eGDR and different CKD phenotypes needs to be further explored.

The relationship between IR indicators and CKD was considered to be stronger in patients with hyperuricemia and smoking [9]. This study did not find an interaction between hyperuricemia, smoking and eGDR, while only the relationship between eGDR and albuminuria was interactive with smoking. Studies focused on the interaction effect of UA and smoking in the relationship between IR and CKD are needed in the future. This study found that more significant association between eGDR and CKD risk existed in middle-aged people with overweight and impaired glucose tolerance, which is similar to previous studies results [9]. This may be attributed to the milder degree of metabolic dysregulation which have not yet reached a threshold sufficient to cause significant CKD in the younger and normal weight group. Even if metabolic diseases are present in these groups, the shorter duration of these disorders may not result in significant renal impairment. In addition, individuals with lower body weight may be affected by sarcopenia or other conditions that contribute to CKD. Long-term follow-up is necessary to further evaluate the risk of CKD in these groups. The slightly decreased OR value of obesity group may be related to the ceiling effect caused by excessive metabolic risk factors complicated with obesity. However, we emphasized the still significant association between eGDR and CKD in obesity patients. Moreover, although the ORs across groups differed only slightly and their 95% CIs overlapped, the interaction effect remained significant statistically. This suggested that there were statistically detectable differences in the scale and context of the effects between these subgroups [22]. These results suggest that the stratification of CKD risk using eGDR in different subgroups is meaningful.

HOMA-IR was associated with the prevalence but not incidence of CKD [23]. In addition, the value of the HOMA-IR measurement is limited since insulin level is not routinely measured in nondiabetic people and is easily affected by insulin, insulin secreting drugs or sensitizing agents. The study has revealed that TyG index was associated with CKD but not low eGFR [24], which was consistent with our results. However, the relationship still remains controversial. Moreover, some patients with relatively low TyG index still had high risk of adverse cardiovascular events [25, 26]. Previous studies demonstrated eGDR was better than TyG index and HOMA-IR when predicting mortality in nonalcoholic fatty liver patients [14]. In general population, TyG index and TyG-WHtR were considered to be more effective in identifying CKD than other IR indicators [9]. However, this study did not analyze the eGDR in identifying CKD. Therefore, we included IR indicators with better CKD identification capability in previous studies and compared them with eGDR. The results showed that eGDR was superior to other included IR parameters when using both ROC method and machine learning methods, which can fit the data without linear assumptions mathematically and accurately. However, it's worth noting that the XGBoost model suggested that IR indicators should be evaluated comprehensively in combination with advanced age, high UA, and cardiorenal syndrome caused by HF when assessing CKD risk.

WQS model result further supports that eGDR is superior to other IR indicators in identifying CKD. This also suggests that in CKM syndrome, glucose and blood pressure disorders impact more on the development of CKD, while dyslipidemia has a relatively small impact. Central obesity and ectopic adiposity deposition cause organs IR [27]. IR in liver and skeletal muscle lead to increased hepatic glycogenolysis and decreased muscle glycogen synthesis, causing increased FPG [28, 29], while IR in adipose tissue cause adipocyte lipolysis and hypertriglyceridemia [30]. IR-related renin–angiotensin–aldosterone system (RAAS) activation causes hypertension, and poor glycemic control aggravates IR [31], thus causing inflammatory factors release, oxidative stress, microvascular endothelial dysfunction and renal protective factors decline [32–34], ultimately leading to the occurrence of CKD. He et al. showed combining TyG and eGDR to classify the population could predict CVDs better [35]. Therefore, combining multiple IR indicators may achieve accurate risk stratification by covering more IR-related components. The results also suggest that in reducing the adverse effects of IR on CKD, controlling blood glucose level, especially FPG, up to standard is the top priority. Furthermore, special attention should be paid to lowering blood pressure and reducing weight. On this basis, dyslipidemia therapy can further reduce residual CKD risk.

On the one hand, IR can cause CKD. On the other hand, CKD induces IR through uremic toxins accumulation, inflammation, metabolic acidosis, etc. [6]. Studies have shown that hyperinsulinemia occurred in the early stage of CKD before eGFR decreased [36]. The mutual promotion worsens the prognosis of CKD patients. Peng et al. confirmed that eGDR was correlated with both all-cause and CVD mortality in nondiabetic CKD patients [37]. However, our study showed that the significant association between eGDR and CVD mortality in CKD patients disappeared after adjusting for confounding factors such as SBP, cholesterol, and UA. This suggests that the association may be mediated by poor control of CVD risk factors or CKD complications. When considering that our study included all CKD patients, further analysis included patients with different causes and severity levels of CKD should be performed to further clarify the predictive value of eGDR for CVD mortality. In addition, the mediating effects of cardiometabolic risk factors on the relationship between eGDR and CVD mortality should be analyzed. However, it is undeniable that IR and CKD promote interactively. Assessing IR severity in CKD patients can predict the prognosis.

Logistic regression results showed that a stepwise decrease in CKD risk as eGDR increased when classified by cutoff values, while revealed a nonlinear relationship between eGDR and CKD when divided by eGDR quartile. This may be attributed to statistical bias resulting from unequal sample sizes across groups. RCS analysis further confirmed a U-shaped association between eGDR and CKD. Previous study showed the relationship between reduced eGDR and renal function progression in diabetic patients is linear [38], while Shen et al. confirmed that IR indicators and CKD prevalence were U-shaped [35]. Liang et al. suggested that very high eGDR may reflect hyperinsulinemia and excessive insulin sensitivity, which is associated with sympathetic activity, sodium retention, and vascular stress, potentially promoting the progression of CKM syndrome [39]. We speculate that when eGDR is very high, patients may have other CKD risk factors such as sarcopenia or other underlying etiologies contributing to CKD. However, due to limited information collected in this study, we were unable to perform further analyses. Future research is needed to explore the reasons behind the mild increase in CKD risk associated with very high eGDR. Nevertheless, it is certain that when eGDR ≤ 8, the risk of CKD is significantly elevated. Study has shown that the eGDR control level is closely related to the incidence of stroke [40]. Considering our results and eGDR threshold in previous studies, we suggest that it is more appropriate to control eGDR at 8–10 mg/kg/min, guiding the risk factors management to prevent CKD. In addition, simple self-assessment of CKD risk using the implication of each component in eGDR (control level of blood glucose, hypertension or not and WC) can be applied in daily life, especially in patients with overweight, obesity and abnormal glucose metabolism. CKD patients can also use this method to assess their risk of death.

This study had several strengths. First, considering IR is associated with many CKD-related risk factors interacting each other complicatedly. We adjusted for more confounding factors and reduced bias as much as possible based on the massive data in NHANES database. Moreover, we analyzed the weights in IR components, explained the differences among IR indicators in CKD identification and ranked the importance of CKD risk factors based on the WQS model and two machine learning models to guide the individualized management of risk factors. Third, our follow-up time was as long as 71 months, which was enough to observe the adverse effects caused by IR in CKD patients.

This study had certain limitations. First, this study is a cross-sectional study in studying the relationship between eGDR and CKD prevalence, which cannot infer the causal relationship. Long-term cohort studies to confirm the relationship between eGDR and CKD morbidity, and to evaluate whether interventions on IR-related risk factors guided by eGDR can prevent CKD or improve the prognosis of CKD patients are needed. Second, though we have considered the effects of hemoglobin concentration, hypertension diagnostic criteria and other factors on eGDR, and performed confounding factors adjustment and sensitivity analysis, we cannot exclude the effects of other potential confounding factors. Third, this study is limited to the American population. Future studies should involve different countries and ethnics to further examine the association between eGDR and CKD prevalence and mortality.

Conclusion

Estimated glucose disposal rate was a better indicator to assess CKD risk in general population, and could predict all-cause mortality rather CVD mortality in CKD patients. Evaluation of eGDR is helpful for risk stratification, among which blood glucose control level is particularly important. Using the eGDR controlling strategy to guide risk factors management may help reduce CKD risk and improve the CKD prognosis.
