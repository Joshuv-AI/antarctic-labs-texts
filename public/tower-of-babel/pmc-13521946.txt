Circularly polarized electroluminescence from topologically chiral [2]catenane-based neutral radicals with tunable deep-red emission
Authors: Yu Wang, Xiao-Qin Xu, Zhiwen Gao, Xue Li, Yiming Yang, Wen-Long Zhao, Meng Li, Wei Wang, Chuan-Feng Chen, Hai-Bo Yang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Stable organic radicals with unique luminescence have demonstrated great importance in photoelectromagnetic materials and have found broad applicability in the field of organic light-emitting diode (OLEDs). Aiming to further extend their application into chiral optoelectronic devices, by employing topologically chiral [2]catenane as the key chiral skeleton, this work presents the first successful construction of circularly polarized OLEDs (CP-OLEDs) based on donor–acceptor (D–A•) type neutral tris(2,4,6-trichlorophenyl)methyl (TTM) radicals. Remarkably, the topologically chiral skeleton, in combination with its controllable dynamic features, enables the system to exhibit a luminescence dissymmetry factor (|gPL|) value as high as 7.1 × 10-3, as well as reversible circularly polarized luminescence (CPL) on/off switching in film state. Importantly, the resulting CP-OLEDs exhibit deep red emission, a maximum external quantum efficiency (EQEmax) of 3.92%, and a |gEL| value of 7.2 × 10-3. These findings demonstrate the successful development of not only luminescent radicals with unique switchable CPL performances, but also the first CP-OLEDs with promising device performance based on topologically chiral radical emitter, providing a distinctive design principle and a versatile platform for the creation of next-generation smart CP-OLEDs.

Introduction

Pure organic radicals1–4 with both luminescence and chemical stability have garnered significant attention due to their unique open-shell electronic structures and promising applications in information storage5–8, fluorescence sensing9–13, and optoelectronic devices14–18. Among these, the neutral tris(2,4,6-trichlorophenyl)methyl (TTM) radical has emerged as a particularly attractive candidate19–23. The remarkable chemical stability of the TTM radical primarily arises from the strong steric protection provided by bulky ortho-chlorine atoms, which effectively shield the central carbon radical from undesired tail-to-tail σ-dimerization. Moreover, TTM radicals exhibit unique photoluminescence properties under ambient conditions and possess excellent structural derivatization, making them a promising platform for the development of advanced luminescent materials24–31. Notably, due to the inherently low photoluminescence quantum yield (PLQY) of the TTM radical core (ϕ = 0.02 in dichloromethane), donor–acceptor (D–A•) derivatives—constructed by combining the electron-deficient TTM radical (A•) with electron-rich donor groups (D)—have been developed to significantly enhance PLQY via intramolecular charge transfer and energy level tuning guided by the Aufbau principle, thereby enabling their application in high-efficiency organic light-emitting diodes (OLEDs)32–39. Notably, the interesting propeller chirality of TTM radicals also gives rise to circularly polarized luminescence (CPL) at low temperatures40–42, but the low racemization barrier leads to rapid enantiomeric interconversion at room temperature, thereby limiting their applicability in chiral optoelectronic devices. To enable their application scope as promising chiral luminescent materials, external chiral elements (such as planar, axial, and helical chirality) have been introduced into the TTM core to develop red and near-infrared CPL-active materials (Fig. 1, top)43–47. However, limited configurational stability and inefficient chirality transfer remain major obstacles, leading to low luminescence dissymmetry factors (in most cases, |glum| ≈ 10−4 − 10−3) and hindering the further development of circularly polarized OLEDs (CP-OLEDs).Fig. 1Schematic illustration of the design concept.Design strategies for efficient and chiral luminescent TTM radicals (top); integration of TTM radicals with topologically chiral [2]catenanes towards the construction of switchable CPL emitters and CP-OLEDs (bottom).

To address this challenge, the development of stable and efficient chiral sources is of critical importance. In recent years, mechanically interlocked molecules (MIMs), such as rotaxanes and catenanes, have attracted increasing interest as versatile platforms for chiral optoelectronic materials, owing to their unique topological constraints, well-defined co-conformational dynamics, and enhanced configurational stability48. In particular, considerable effort has been devoted to incorporating luminescent chromophores or radical units into rotaxane and catenane architectures to investigate their optical and magnetic properties.49,50. Although several elegant examples of CPL-active rotaxanes and catenanes have been reported to date51–54, the overall number of such mechanically interlocked systems remains limited, and radical-containing MIMs are even less explored, especially with respect to their integration into electroluminescent devices. In this context, topological chirality55–60 based on MIMs has emerged as a promising chiral element, not only offering exceptional configurational stability and efficient chirality expression, but also providing an advantageous platform for CPL switching61,62, high-performance optoelectronic devices63, and spin filtering64. Among diverse topologically chiral molecules, [2]catenanes composed of two “oriented” macrocycles serve as classical examples that combine efficient chirality induction and transfer with unique dynamic features, allowing precise regulation of chirality expression via stimuli-responsive co-conformational switching65–68. In addition, the rational incorporation of functional groups into the topologically chiral [2]catenane skeleton can endow the overall chirality and enable dynamic chiral functions. Therefore, leveraging the unique doublet red emission of TTM radicals in combination with topologically chiral [2]catenanes as a chiral source offers a promising strategy for developing red-emissive materials with high |glum| and dynamic CPL behavior, facilitating the design of advanced chiroptical devices (Fig. 1, bottom). Notably, to the best of our knowledge, CP-OLEDs based on chiral luminescent radicals have not yet been investigated.

Herein, the first CP-OLEDs based on luminescent radicals were successfully constructed by incorporating carbazole-derived TTM radicals into a topologically chiral [2]catenane skeleton via efficient post-functionalization. Notably, the topologically chiral skeleton preserved the high PLQY of the D-A•-type TTM radical (up to 0.45) while providing a mechanically chiral framework for chiroptical expression, resulting in a photoluminescence dissymmetry factor (|gPL|) of 7.1 × 10−3 in neat film. Furthermore, benefiting from the dynamic [2]catenane skeleton, the addition of sodium ions induces co-conformational regulation, which in turn alters the spatial arrangement of the TTM radicals, resulting in a hypochromatic shift of the emission wavelength and CPL on/off switching. Importantly, the good solubility and configurational stability of the topologically chiral radical emitters facilitated the solution processing of a red-emitting CP-OLED, which achieved an EQEmax of 3.92% and a CP electroluminescence (CP-EL) performance with a |gEL| value of 7.2 × 10−3.

Results and discussion

Synthesis, characterizations, and chiroptical properties

The construction of efficient topologically chiral TTM radical emitters critically depends on the selection of appropriate chromophores and a suitable chiral skeleton. In this study, the carbazole-derived TTM radicals, previously demonstrated as highly efficient emitters in OLEDs33,35, were selected as the key red-emitting groups. Meanwhile, by leveraging our established post-functionalization strategy for topologically chiral [2]catenanes bearing amine reactive sites61, the chromophore was efficiently incorporated into the topologically chiral skeleton. Based on this strategy, N-p-tolyl carbazole derivative was first subjected to a Suzuki-Miyaura coupling reaction with HTTM to afford the mono-substituted intermediate S3, followed by bromination with NBS to yield compound CzHTTMBr in 67% yield. Subsequently, a nucleophilic substitution with optically pure topologically chiral [2]catenane (Rmt)/(Smt)-1 furnished the radical precursor (Rmt)/(Smt)-2 in 57% yield61,69. Finally, oxidative dehydrogenation of the Csp3 center in intermediate (Rmt)/(Smt)-2 using p-chloranil afforded the corresponding air-stable radicals (Rmt)/(Smt)-3 in 97% yield (Fig. 2a). In addition, the TTM radical-modified macrocycle 6 was synthesized as a model compound under similar conditions, using macrocycle 4 as the starting material. All key compounds were characterized by 1H and 13C NMR and high-resolution electrospray ionization mass spectrometry (HR-ESI-MS) (See details in Section B of Supplementary Information). The peak at m/z = 1220.1404, corresponding to [(racmt)-3 + 2H]2+, was observed in the mass spectrum and closely matched the calculated value (m/z = 1220.1405) (Fig. 2b), while minor deviations in the isotopic intensity distribution were observed due to the presence of multiple chlorine atoms, and the HRMS value reported here corresponds to the simulated most intense isotopic peak70. Notably, the open-shell electronic nature of the resultant carbon-centered radicals was confirmed by pronounced signal broadening in the 1H NMR spectra and electron paramagnetic resonance (EPR) measurements (g = 2.0028) (Fig. 2c). To gain insight into the electronic structure of the open-shell spin radicals, the geometry of the diradical was optimized using DFT calculations at the UB3LYP/6-31G* level for singlet and triplet ground states71,72 (Fig. 3, see details in Section D of Supplementary Information). Conformational analysis identified eight representative co-conformations in total, including four singlet-state conformers and four triplet-state conformers. Among them, the lowest-energy singlet co-conformation was found to be 6.32 kcal mol−1 lower in energy than the lowest-energy triplet co-conformation, supporting the singlet state as the more stable electronic state under the present computational conditions (Supplementary Figs. 86–89). Notably, the optimized geometries of the singlet and triplet states show certain differences, due to variations in spin multiplicity and the flexible [2]catenane skeleton. At the orbital level, the open-shell singlet state is characterized by spin-polarized occupation of a pair of nearly degenerate frontier orbitals, in which the same spatial orbitals are singly occupied by α and β electrons. This electronic arrangement allows efficient orbital relaxation and minimizes unfavorable spatial congestion within the topologically constrained framework. In contrast, the triplet state requires parallel-spin occupation of distinct spatial orbitals, whose separation and reorganization are severely restricted by the mechanically interlocked [2]catenane skeleton, leading to pronounced orbital distortion and substantial geometric frustration, and consequently a much higher-energy structure. Similarly, a comparable electronic structure to the D-A• type TTM radical33 was observed in macrocycle 6, further confirming that the TTM unit preserves its characteristic open-shell configuration within different molecular skeletons. Cyclic voltammetry experiments further supported the conventional Aufbau electronic structure: the oxidation potentials of (racmt)-3 and 6 were measured at + 0.87 V and + 0.86 V, respectively—significantly lower than that of TTM ( + 1.08 V)34 reported—indicating substantial hybridization between the TTM and carbazole orbitals and a notable contribution of the CT configuration to the ground state.Fig. 3Theoretical calculation analysis of topologically chiral [2]catenane 3.The optimized geometry, molecular orbitals and energies of (Rmt)-3 in the singlet state (a) and triplet state (b) calculated by DFT (UB3LYP/6-31G*).

Subsequently, the basic photophysical properties of (racmt)-3 and 6, including solvent-dependent UV–vis absorption and fluorescence emission behaviors, were systematically investigated (Fig. 4a, b and Supplementary Figs. 22, 23). Both radicals exhibit two strong absorption bands at approximately 375 and 420 nm across various solvents, which are attributed to localized π–π* transitions originating from the carbazole-modified TTM radical segment. In addition, the weak absorption band around 620 nm is assigned to the characteristic transition of the organic radical and exhibits a slight bathochromic shift as the solvent polarity increases. Under 375 nm excitation, (racmt)-3 in cyclohexane solution exhibits two emission bands at 681 and 746 nm, with an absolute PLQY of 36%, comparable to that of carbazole-derived TTM radicals33. Notably, in this asymmetric D–A• system, the dual emission behavior, especially in nonpolar solvents, are attributed to the intrinsic excited-state characteristics of the TTM-based emitters, likely involving vibronic coupling and minor excited-state structural relaxation, in line with previous reports on luminescent TTM radicals26,73. Furthermore, the PLQY in various solvents exhibits a non-monotonic dependence on solvent polarity, reaching maximum values in low-to-medium polarity solvents and decreasing in more polar environments due to enhanced nonradiative decay (Supplementary Fig. 27). With increasing solvent polarity, the PL spectra exhibit a significant bathochromic shift and broadening, especially when dichloromethane and tetrahydrofuran are used as solvents, the emission band extends into the near-infrared region around 755 nm, indicating that its emission originates from a CT excited state. In addition, quantified by the ET(30)74, the emission maximum of (racmt)-3 exhibits a systematic bathochromic shift, indicative of moderate positive solvatochromism, while tetrahydrofuran shows a slight deviation from this trend due to its unique coordination and donor properties (Supplementary Fig. 24). Meanwhile, the emission spectra remain largely similar upon variation of the excitation wavelength, although more noticeable changes in band shape and the apparent emission maximum are observed in THF and CH2Cl2, likely because the broad CT-type emission in these solvents is more sensitive to conformational and solvation heterogeneity (Supplementary Fig. 25). Notably, the fluorescence lifetimes of (racmt)-3 and 6 in chloroform are 19.6 ns and 23.6 ns, respectively (Supplementary Figs. 28, 29). The shorter fluorescence lifetimes observed in more polar solvents are likely due to enhanced nonradiative decay associated with stabilization of the CT excited state12. The chiroptical properties of the enantiomers (Rmt)-3 and (Smt)-3 in solution were investigated using circular dichroism (CD) and circularly polarized luminescence (CPL) spectroscopy. At room temperature (25 °C), the CD signals are weak, suggesting a limited ground-state chirality expression of the system. To further probe the origin of the weak chiroptical response, variable-temperature CD measurements were performed from 20 °C to − 20 °C (Supplementary Figs. 30, 31). Upon cooling, weak but discernible mirror-image Cotton effects gradually increased, mainly around 320 nm, indicating that reduced thermal motion suppressed co-conformational averaging, thereby enhancing topological chirality expression. This behavior can be attributed to the intrinsically weak chromophoric nature of the topologically chiral [2]catenane scaffold, together with the pronounced steric bulk of the carbazole-modified TTM radicals, which restricts effective electronic coupling within the still relatively flexible co-conformational skeleton. As a result, the system may be considered to exhibit cryptochirality in solution78–80, manifested by weak Cotton effects that becomes more evident as co-conformational dynamics are suppressed at lower temperatures. Next, the CPL spectra were recorded to probe the chiral properties of their excited states. In dilute solution, no reliable CPL signals could be detected under either room-temperature or low-temperature conditions, indicating that the CPL response is negligible in the solution phase for this system (Supplementary Figs. 34–40). In light of the few cases where solvents significantly affect the CPL properties44,46 of TTM radicals and considering their excellent luminescence performance in the solid state, we further measured their CPL spectra in the casting film. To our delight, the restricted molecular motion in the casting films suppresses co-conformational averaging and enables a more stable excited-state chiroptical expression. As a result, the topologically chiral enantiomers exhibit average gPL values of − 0.71 × 10−2 and + 0.67 × 10−2 in the 695–705 nm range, representing the highest reported among known chiral radical luminescent materials (Fig. 4c–d and Supplementary Table 4). Notably, the solid-state CPL data were obtained by averaging measurements collected at multiple detection angles as well as under rotation around the light source, which effectively minimizes artifacts and significantly reduces the possibility of false signals (Supplementary Figs. 42–45). In addition, control experiments using cast films of different thicknesses showed that the observed solid-state CPL response is also affected by film thickness (Supplementary Figs. 46, 47). Thicker films exhibited reduced fluorescence and weaker CPL signals, while atomic force microscopy (AFM) measurements indicated poorer film-forming quality and more pronounced aggregation at higher concentrations. These observations suggest that film-related optical and morphological factors should also be considered when interpreting the solid-state CPL response. In this sense, the pronounced film-state CPL may be regarded as a more effective excited-state readout of the cryptochirality of the [2]catenane. In addition, although pronounced CPL is observed in the film state, the corresponding CD response remains weak (Supplementary Figs. 32, 33), possibly due to the relatively poor film-forming quality revealed by AFM analysis, which may hinder the emergence of a distinct solid-state Cotton effect. Nevertheless, the weak film-state CD response and the thickness-dependent CPL behavior suggest that an extrinsic contribution, arising from circular polarization of the emitted light during propagation through the chiral film, cannot be completely ruled out and may partially contribute to the observed CPL signal. Furthermore, based on the lowest-energy singlet co-conformation identified from the conformational analysis, the gPL value of (Rmt)-3 was estimated from excited-state calculations to be + 4.70 × 10−3 (Supplementary Fig. 91). In this calculation, the emissive transition was evaluated from the optimized S1 geometry and corresponds to the S1 → S0 emission process. Conformational analysis further revealed multiple accessible singlet and triplet co-conformations for this flexible mechanically interlocked system, indicating that its chiroptical response is strongly co-conformation-dependent (Supplementary Tables 6, 7). The difference between the calculated and experimental values may therefore arise from conformational flexibility, together with the absence of explicit intermolecular and solid-state effects in the model. The combined experimental and theoretical results indicate that the observed film-state CPL mainly originates from the chiroptical expression of specific accessible co-conformations. In solution, rapid co-conformational averaging prevents a stable manifestation of excited-state chirality, whereas in the film state, restricted molecular motion suppresses this averaging and enables a more persistent chiral excited-state response.

Tunable emission and switchable chiroptical responses

Following the successful construction of topologically chiral radical emitters exhibiting excellent CPL performances, we further investigated the effects of precise co-conformation regulation on its photophysical and chiroptical behaviors by leveraging the controllable dynamic features of the topologically chiral skeleton. The slight bathochromic shift and broadening of the PL spectra of (racmt)-3 compared with that of 6 suggest weak excited-state coupling between the two TTM radicals, although no direct evidence for a distinct excimer species has been obtained81,82. Together with the stable co-conformations revealed by computational simulations, this implies that (racmt)-3 predominantly adopts State I in solution. The crown ether-like units in the topologically chiral catenane skeletons, known for their established host–guest responsiveness63, can selectively bind one equivalent of sodium ions, thereby inducing a co-conformational change to the chromophore-distant State II (Fig. 5a). This co-conformational switch changes the spatial arrangement of the TTM radical, potentially modulating the properties of its ground and excited states. To confirm this hypothesis, owing to signal broadening of radical compound (racmt)-3 in the NMR spectra, we conducted 1H NMR studies using its precursor (racmt)-2 instead, where the addition of sodium ions induced significant upfield shifts of aliphatic protons H16-H19, confirming the formation of State II (Fig. 5b and Supplementary Figs. 48–50). Furthermore, fluorescence spectroscopy was used to further demonstrate the co-conformational transformation by adding 1.0 equivalent of sodium ions to (racmt)-3 in various solvents. Notably, overall hypochromatic shifts in fluorescence spectra were observed in CHCl3 and diethyl ether, from 719 nm to 705 nm and from 716 nm to 703 nm, respectively, accompanied by a slight increase in intensity, which is attributed to the increased rigidity of State II and the enlarged distance between intramolecular chromophores that weakens the coupling effect (Fig. 5d and Supplementary Figs. 52–57). However, in n-hexane, cyclohexane, toluene, diisopropyl ether, and tetrahydrofuran, the transformation to State II is likely hindered by factors such as π–π stacking interactions, and competitive coordination of sodium ions by solvent molecules (Supplementary Figs. 58–69). The observed hypsochromic shift upon Na⁺ addition is consistent with an increased distance between the intramolecular chromophores and a corresponding weakening of this excited-state coupling. In addition, EPR spectra demonstrated that the two radicals within (Rmt)-3 remained spatially separated in both State I and State II, before and after sodium ion addition, with no evident spin–spin interaction observed (Fig. 5c), consistent with the absence of excimer emission in the PL spectra. Following the successful realization of the sodium-induced co-conformational switching of (racmt)-3, the switchable chiroptical properties of enantiopure (Rmt)-3 and (Smt)-3 were further explored by CD and CPL spectra. Due to the cryptochirality of State I and the demonstrated weakening of topological chirality transfer upon co-conformational fixation, no obvious Cotton effect was observed in the CD spectra of (Rmt)-3 and (Smt)-3 upon addition of sodium cations in different solvents. Interestingly, this co-conformational switching process enables precise regulation of CPL behavior in the solid state. Specifically, upon the addition of one equivalent of sodium ions, the emission peak undergoes a noticeable hypsochromic shift from 707 nm to 695 nm, which is accompanied by the complete disappearance of the CPL signal, thus achieving a distinct CPL on/off switching (Fig. 5e). This switching behavior is attributed not to the loss of topological chirality in State II, but to the fact that the Na⁺-stabilized co-conformation places the two radical chromophores in a spatial arrangement less favorable for excited-state chiroptical expression. Furthermore, it is worth emphasizing that the CPL signal can be completely restored by introducing [2.2.2]cryptand, which captures sodium ions and induces the system to revert back to its original State I co-conformation (Supplementary Figs. 72–75). Overall, these results indicate that the pronounced CPL observed in this system originates from the synergistic interplay between the topologically chiral framework and a semi-flexible co-conformational environment. While the catenane skeleton provides the fundamental chiral framework, strong CPL is observed only when the fluorophores adopt a co-conformational arrangement favorable for excited-state chiroptical expression; complete conformational locking into an unfavorable arrangement, in contrast, suppresses the CPL response. As the first switchable CPL system based on luminescent radicals, this design uniquely combines high PLQY with large |gPL| values, making it a promising candidate for an emerging class of smart red-emitting CP-EL materials.

Circularly polarized-electroluminescence performances

Inspired by the good solubility and chiroptical properties, solution-processed OLEDs based on (racmt)-3, (Rmt)-3, and (Smt)-3 were successfully fabricated to evaluate their EL and CP-EL performances, using the following device configuration: ITO/(PEDOT:PSS: PFI, 40 nm)/PVK (Poly(N-vinylcarbazole, 10 nm))/TPBi (1,3,5-Tris(1-phenyl-1H-benzimidazol-2-yl)benzene): (racmt)-3/(Rmt)-3/(Smt)-3 enantiomers (1-10 wt%, 30 nm)/B3PYMPM (4,6-Bis(3,5-di(pyridin-3-yl)phenyl)−2-methylpyrimidine, 30 nm)/LiF (1 nm)/Al (100 nm) (Supplementary Fig. 76). Upon electrical excitation, excitons are predominantly generated via a sequential charge-trapping mechanism, owing to the large energy bandgap of TPBi relative to that of the TTM-based dopant, which facilitates efficient charge trapping on the radical emitter. To optimize the doping ratio of the host material, a series of binary emissive layers were prepared by mixing TPBi with varying concentrations of (racmt)-3: device A with 1 wt%, device B with 3 wt%, device C with 5 wt%, and device D with 10 wt% of (racmt)-3 (Fig. 6a, b). As a result, all devices exhibited stable EL without significant spectral shifts across a driving voltage range of 7 – 20 V, highlighting both the excellent molecular stability and the rational design of the device structure (Fig. 6c). However, as the doping concentration increases from 1% to 10%, the electroluminescence wavelength of the device expands from 692 nm to 712 nm due to the significant enhancement of intermolecular interactions (Supplementary Figs. 77–80). Among them, device B exhibits deep-red emission with a peak wavelength of 696 nm, and its EL spectra indicate complete energy transfer. Remarkably, without any auxiliary doping, it achieves an EQEmax of 3.72%, representing a commendable performance among reported solution-processed OLEDs based on radical luminescent materials (Fig. 6d). In contrast, the devices fabricated using macrocyclic 6 under identical conditions exhibited EL at 698 nm and achieved an EQEmax of 4.70% at a 1% doping concentration (Supplementary Figs. 81–83). However, at a doping ratio of 10%, the EL spectrum exhibits a 10 nm bathochromic shift compared to the catenane-based device, which can be attributed to the presence of the [2]catenane skeleton limiting the intermolecular stacking of radical chromophores in the solid state. Furthermore, building on the optimized device structure with the best performance, corresponding to Device B at a dopant concentration of 3%, CP-OLEDs incorporating the enantiomers (Rmt)-3 and (Smt)-3 were subsequently fabricated. The EL performance of the chiral devices closely matches that of Device B, indicating that the topologically chiral emitters maintain their configurational stability throughout the device fabrication process, with key device parameters remaining comparable and varying only within the normal experimental variability commonly observed for OLED devices. Specifically, (Rmt)-3 and (Smt)-3 exhibit maximum EL emission at 694 nm, with maximum radiance of 0.043 and 0.049 W sr-1 m2, and EQEmax of 3.92% and 3.65%, respectively (Supplementary Fig. 84). Importantly, the first CP-OLED based on luminescent radicals exhibits mirror-symmetric CP-EL signals with gEL values of + 5.8 × 10−3 and – 7.2 × 10−3 for (Rmt)-3 and (Smt)-3, respectively, demonstrating that the topologically chiral [2]catenane skeleton can impart chiroptical activity to luminescent radicals in electroluminescent devices (Fig. 6e, f).Fig. 6Optimized electroluminescent properties of radicals-based CP-OLEDs.a Device structures of emitters and (b) molecular structures of used materials of devices. c Normalized EL spectra measured at 10 V. In summary, this study successfully developed the first organic radical emitters incorporating topological chirality, in which the chiroptical contribution and controllable dynamic features of the topologically chiral skeleton enabled a |gPL| value as high as 7.1 × 10-3 alongside reversible CPL on/off switching. Furthermore, by exploiting the configurational stability inherent to topological chirality, CP-OLED based on topologically chiral radical emitters were fabricated via solution processing for the first time. These devices exhibit deep red emission, EQEmax of 3.92%, and a |gEL| value of 7.2 × 10−3, representing a significant advancement in the performance of chiral radical luminescent materials. This work not only confirms the strong potential of chiral luminescent radicals with doublet emission as candidate materials for CP-OLEDs, but also highlights the introduction of topological chirality as a promising approach for developing smart devices with improved yet tunable performances.

Methods

Nuclear magnetic resonance spectroscopy experiments

1H NMR and 13C NMR spectra were recorded on Bruker 500 MHz Spectrometer (1H: 500 MHz; 13C: 126 MHz) at 298 K. The 1H and 13C NMR chemical shifts are reported relative to residual solvent signals. 2D-NMR spectra (1H-1H COSY, NOESY) were recorded on Bruker 500 MHz Spectrometer (1H: 500 MHz) at 298 K.

Optical and chiroptical property characterizations

UV–vis spectra and steady-state fluorescence spectra were recorded in a quartz cell (light path 5 mm) on a Shimadzu UV2700 UV–visible spectrophotometer and a Shimadzu RF-6000 fluorescence spectrophotometer. Emission lifetime measurements were performed using a Fluorescence FL-QM-LT steady-state/transient fluorescence spectrometer (λex = 375 nm). Absolute photoluminescence quantum yields (PLQYs) were measured on the same instrument equipped with an integrating sphere (λex = 370 nm). CD and CPL spectra were measured on a Chirascan Series Spectrometer (Applied Photophysics Ltd, UK) at room temperature. Circularly polarized electroluminescence (CP-EL) measurements were conducted utilizing a commercialized instrument, JASCO CPL-300 spectrophotometer. Solid-state CPL measurements were performed from four different detection angles, acquiring two scans for each angle. The final CPL spectrum was obtained by averaging these datasets, which effectively suppresses random noise and minimizes instrumental artifacts, thereby ensuring the reliability of the measured CPL signals.

Theoretical calculation

All theoretical calculations were performed by the Gaussian 16 package, details of which were given in Supplementary information. The atomic coordinate files obtained after DFT optimization are included as Supplementary Source Data in the Supplementary Files.

Optoelectronic device fabrication

Indium tin oxide (ITO) coated glass with a sheet resistance of 10 Ω per square was used as the anode substrate. Before the fabrication of devices, the ITO glass substrates were cleaned with Decon 90, rinsed in ultrapure water and ethanol, dried in an oven at 110 °C, and finally treated with O2 plasma for two minutes to enhance the surface work function of the ITO anode. Subsequently, 40 nm-thin PEDOT: PSS/PFI mixture was spin-coated on the ITO anode substrate as hole-injecting layer, then annealed at 120 °C for 15 min. Then, the solutions of PVK(Poly(N-vinylcarbazole), 1-10 wt% (racmt)-3/(Rmt)-3/(Smt)-3 (in chlorobenzene) were sequentially spin-coated onto hole-injecting layer to form the emitting-layer, followed by annealing at 110 °C for 30 min and 20 min, respectively. Finally, B3PYMPM, LiF and Al were consecutively evaporated onto the emitting layer in a vacuum deposition chamber. The electroluminescence and current-voltage-luminance characteristics of the devices were measured with a computer-controlled Spectrascan PR 670 spectrophotometer and Keithley 2400 SourceMeter after device packaging.
