Incidence of avoidant/restrictive food intake disorder in children and adolescents across the UK and Ireland: a BPSU and CAPSS surveillance study
Authors: Javier Sanchez-Cerezo, Josephine Neale, Nikita Julius, Richard M Lynn, Lee Hudson, Dasha Nicholls
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
Abstract AbstractObjectiveWe aimed to estimate the incidence of avoidant/restrictive food intake disorder (ARFID) in children and adolescents (CA) presenting to secondary care in the UK and Republic of Ireland (ROI).DesignThis observational surveillance study used the British Paediatric Surveillance Unit and the Child and Adolescent Psychiatry Surveillance System. Monthly electronic reports were submitted by consultant paediatricians and child and adolescent psychiatrists from 1 March 2021 to 31 March 2022, with outcomes assessed at a 1-year follow-up.SettingThe UK and ROI.ParticipantsClinician-reported data on children and young people aged 5–17 (inclusive) in contact with paediatric services or CA mental health services for a new diagnosis of ARFID.Primary outcome measuresAnnual incidence rates (IRs) estimated as confirmed new cases per 100 000 population at risk.Results319 newly diagnosed cases of ARFID were reported over the 13-month surveillance period and assessed as eligible for inclusion. The mean age of the sample was 11.2 years (SD=3.8). The sample consisted of 145 females (45.5%). Most cases were of white British ethnicity (71.2%). The observed IR (IR0) in the UK was 2.79 per 100 000 CA (95% CI 2.48 to 3.13), with a higher IRo in males (2.98 per 100 000 CA (95% CI 2.55 to 3.49)) than in females (2.58 per 100 000 CA (95% CI 2.18 to 3.07)). Comorbidity was common in this sample with anxiety and autism spectrum disorder. At follow-up, 54.8% of cases had improved according to clinicians’ clinical impression.ConclusionsWe conducted the first study reporting estimates of incidence of ARFID in CA in the UK and ROI presenting to secondary care. Our finding that ARFID is a disorder of relatively low incidence in CA should help inform service planning and resource allocation, as well as the development of evidence-based interventions.Keywords: EPIDEMIOLOGIC STUDIES, PAEDIATRICS, Child & adolescent psychiatry, Eating disorders

Introduction

Avoidant/restrictive food intake disorder (ARFID) is defined by a persistent disturbance in feeding or eating, that results in at least one of the following: weight loss or insufficient weight gain; nutritional deficiencies; reliance on enteral feeding or nutritional supplements; or substantial impact on psychosocial functioning. ARFID was first introduced as a distinct diagnosis in the Diagnostic and Statistical Manual of Mental Disorders (5th ed.; DSM-5) in 2013,1 and it has since been included in the International Classification of Diseases 11th Revision.2 Prior to its inclusion in the DSM5, people with ARFID, depending on their age and presentation, were diagnosed with Feeding Disorder of Infancy and Early Childhood, Eating Disorder Not Otherwise Specified or other terminology (eg, selective eating, food avoidance emotional disorder, functional dysphagia) that were not formally diagnoses.3 4 The DSM-5 describes that food restriction in ARFID can stem from heightened sensitivity to sensory characteristics of food—such as its texture, colour or temperature; from a lack of interest in eating or food; or from anxiety related to food, including fears of negative experiences like choking or vomiting.1 These three presentations are not mutually exclusive, and a combined presentation is more common than one specific subtype in some settings.5,7

Understanding the epidemiology of a disorder is essential for assessing clinical service needs and identifying at-risk populations. A recent systematic review found that epidemiological studies on ARFID in children and adolescents (CAs) vary widely, with prevalence estimates ranging from 0.3% to 64%, depending on study setting, methodology and sample characteristics.8 9 To date, only one study has reported incidence of ARFID, which is more useful than prevalence for predicting clinical care demand. This was a surveillance study from Canada which found that the incidence of ARFID was 2.02 per 100 000 CAs aged 5–18 years presenting to paediatricians.10 Surveillance methodology has previously proved helpful in the study of paediatric eating disorders and associated issues that need clinical and research focus in order to develop appropriate care.8 11 The number of CAs with symptoms of ARFID presenting to secondary care in the UK and Republic of Ireland (ROI) is currently unknown which is problematic with regard to understanding need and planning appropriate care.

We, therefore, conducted a surveillance study to estimate the incidence of ARFID in CAs presenting to secondary care in the UK and ROI; to describe clinical characteristics, psychiatric comorbidities, family history and management plans and to assess outcome at 1 year.

Methods

Design

We undertook an observational surveillance study in collaboration with the British Paediatric Surveillance Unit (BPSU) and the Child and Adolescent Psychiatry Surveillance System (CAPSS). The BPSU was established in 1986 in the UK and ROI to facilitate the study of rare paediatric conditions.12 In 2007, this was supplemented with the launch of the CAPSS, the first mental health-specific surveillance system.13 These surveillance systems work by sending monthly electronic reporting ecards listing health conditions currently under study to all consultant paediatricians (BPSU) and consultant child and adolescent (CA) psychiatrists (CAPSS) in the UK and ROI. The reporting ecards are returned to the surveillance systems who inform the research team when a clinician reports a case. Reports of no case provide the denominator. A questionnaire is then sent to the reporting clinician for further details about the case. BPSU surveyed 4298 consultant paediatricians and CAPSS surveyed 695 consultant CA psychiatrists during the study period.

Case definition

We developed the surveillance case definition (online supplemental file 1) which was approved by both BPSU and CAPSS committees based on modified DSM-5 diagnostic criteria for ARFID1 and was included in the monthly ecard sent to consultant paediatricians and CA psychiatrists. This surveillance case definition was purposely broad to ensure that all cases were recognised and reported.12 We confirmed the cases of ARFID by using the analytic case definition (a more detailed case definition used by the study team) after reporting clinicians completed a questionnaire with details of the case (online supplemental file 2). In any unclear cases, we reached a consensus between the study team on whether the criteria for ARFID had been met. We did not include the identified duplicate case reports in the final dataset.

Data collection

We ascertained newly diagnosed cases of ARFID over a 13-month period (from 1 March 2021 to 31 March 2022), as is customary with surveillance studies. Follow-up took the form of data collection at 12 months after a case of ARFID was reported through a second questionnaire sent to reporting clinicians. We collected and managed study data by using Research Electronic Data Capture14 15; a secure, web-based software platform hosted at Imperial College London. We developed a questionnaire specifically for this study. We asked reporting clinicians to report patients’ sex in the questionnaire.

Statistics

We analysed the data in Stata (V.17, StataCorp) and Microsoft Excel. Our analyses were descriptive. Newly diagnosed cases of ARFID were classified into three different age groups: 5–9 years, 10–13 years and 15–17 years.

We calculated the observed incidence rates (IR0), defined as the number of new cases during a specified period of time in a population at risk for developing the disease, as follows: the number of confirmed new cases of ARFID in the 13-month surveillance period converted to 12 months ((N cases over 13 months/13)×12), divided by the population at risk in UK and ROI and multiplied by 100 000 to give the rate per 100 000 young people. We assumed the population at risk as the total population of children and young people. We obtained the data for the total population of children for 2021 aged 5–17 (inclusive) from the Office of National Statistics (UK)16 and the Central Statistics Office (ROI).17

IR0=(confirmed new cases converted to 12 months)/total population×100 000.

For each IR, we calculated the total, age-group-specific and sex-specific annual IRs for ARFID for the year 2021 and 95% CIs.

Public and patient involvement

One adult patient representative and one child patient representative with ARFID contributed to the development and design of the study. We also had feedback from the charities ARFID Awareness UK and Autistica.

Role of the funding source

The funding source had no role in study design, data collection, data analysis, data interpretation or writing of the report.

Results

Case ascertainment can be seen in figure 1. The response rate to the monthly electronic reporting ecards was 78.5% for BPSU and 47.5% for CAPSS. We received 81.2% of the initial questionnaires that were sent to paediatricians (BPSU) reporting positive cases of ARFID and 78.1% of those sent to CA psychiatrists (CAPSS). A total of 917 potential cases of ARFID were reported (569 from BPSU and 348 from CAPSS) over 13 months. Of those, 319 CAs with ARFID aged 5–18 years (mean age=11.2 years; (SD=3.8; range 5.00–17.99); female=45.5%) living in the UK and ROI met the analytic case definition and were included. Only four duplicates between both surveillance units were identified. Most cases were of white British ethnicity (71.2%). Nearly half of the sample (44.1%) was prepubertal and the majority were born at term. We show the demographic characteristics of the incident cases in table 1. We report the proportion of the included sample notified from each region within the UK and ROI in table 2. England notified 83.1% of new ARFID cases; Wales notified 3.1%; Scotland notified 7.2%; Northern Ireland notified 3.8% and the ROI notified 2.2%. Two cases were reported from the Crown Dependencies. 126 cases were included in the 5–9 years group; 100 cases in the 10–13 years group and 93 cases in the 14–17 years group.

Incidence rates

Table 3 shows the IR0 of ARFID by sex and by age group. The cases from the Crown Dependencies were not included in the IR calculation. The IR0 in the UK including all age groups was 2.79 per 100 000 young people (95% CI 2.48 to 3.13), with a higher IR0 in males (2.98 per 100 000 young people (95% CI 2.55 to 3.49)) than in females (2.58 per 100 000 young people (95% CI 2.18 to 3.07)). The highest IR0 for males in the UK was in the group aged 5–9 years (4.22 per 100 000 young people (95% CI 3.41 to 5.21)). The IR0 for females in the UK was higher than for males in the group aged 10–13 years (3.02 per 100 000 young people (95% CI 2.28 to 4.01)) and in the group aged 14–17 years (3.40 per 100 000 young people (95% CI 2.58 to 4.48)). The IR0 in England including all age groups and both sexes was 2.81 per 100 000 young people (95% CI 2.48 to 3.19). Similarly, the IR0 in Scotland was 2.76 per 100 000 young people (95% CI 1.80 to 4.22). The lowest IR0 across the UK was reported in Wales (1.99 per 100 000 young people (95% CI 1.05 to 3.80)) while the highest was in Northern Ireland (3.44 per 100 000 young people (95% CI 1.91 to 6.20)). The IR0 in the ROI including all age groups and both sexes was 0.73 per 100 000 young people (95% CI 0.34 to 1.57). The IR0 in the total population, including the UK and ROI, was 2.63 (95% CI 2.34 to 2.94).

Psychiatric comorbidities and family history

Table 4 displays the past and present psychiatric comorbidities and psychiatric family history for the included cases. Anxiety (40.8%), deliberate self-harm (DSH) (8.8%), depression (6.3%) and obsessive–compulsive disorder (OCD) (5.6%) were the most common past mental illness comorbidities in the sample. Regarding current comorbidities, anxiety was the most common (43.9%), followed by depression (7.8%) and OCD (5.3%). Comorbidities with neurodevelopmental disorders were common, the highest being with autism spectrum disorder (ASD) (43.9%), followed by learning disabilities (LD) (20.4%) and attention-deficit/hyperactivity disorder (ADHD) (7.2%). 55 cases (17.2%) had both ASD and present anxiety. The most common past and current mental illnesses in the family history were depression (14.7 and 8.5%, respectively), anxiety (13.5 and 11.6%) and OCD (3.1 and 2.2%). Family history of ASD was reported in 13.5% of the sample and ADHD in 5.0%.

Management plans

Regarding the outpatient management of cases, medical outpatient monitoring was used in 211 new cases of ARFID (66.1%), dietetic advice in 246 cases (77.1%), psychoeducation (ie, provision of information about the disorder to the patient and their families) in 165 cases (51.7%), medication in 75 cases (23.5%), tube feeding in 21 cases (6.6%), nutritional supplements in 133 cases (41.7%), individual psychological therapy in 85 cases (26.7%), family therapy in 58 cases (18.2%) and input from another specialty in 94 cases (24.5%). In total, 37 new cases of ARFID (11.6%) were admitted to a paediatric inpatient unit or day care and 12 cases (3.8%) were admitted to a psychiatric inpatient unit or day care. Regarding psychiatric medication, 36 cases (11.3%) were prescribed antidepressants, 8 cases (2.5%) were prescribed antipsychotics, 11 cases (3.5%) were prescribed stimulants, and 31 cases (9.7%) were prescribed melatonin. Regarding the follow-up plans, clinicians reported that 186 cases (58.3%) were going to be followed up with the same clinician that completed the questionnaire, 28 cases (8.8%) were going to be referred to a medical specialist, 67 cases (21.0%) to a psychiatric specialist, 54 cases (16.9%) to a psychologist, 79 (24.8%) to another healthcare provider and 18 cases (5.6%) discharged to primary care.

Outcomes at 1-year follow-up

Follow-up data were available for 197 (61.8%) participants (113 from BPSU and 84 from CAPSS) (figure 1). At follow-up, the sample consisted of 109 males (55.3%) and 88 females (44.7%). According to the reporting clinicians’ overall clinical impression, 19 cases (9.6%) improved without treatment, 89 cases (45.2%) improved with treatment, 5 cases (2.5%) changed in presentation, 46 cases (23.4%) persisted unchanged, 6 cases (3%) worsened and for 32 cases (16.2%) the reporting clinicians reported that outcome was unknown.

We asked clinicians to report in the follow-up questionnaire if any concurrent mental disorder, neurodevelopmental disorder diagnosis or disability emerged since completion of the baseline questionnaire: 5 cases (2.5%) were diagnosed with depression, 13 cases (6.6%) with anxiety, 2 cases (1.0%) with OCD, 4 cases (2.0%) with ADHD, 1 case with substance misuse (0.5%), 17 cases (8.6%) with ASD, 3 cases (1.5%) with LD, 1 cases (0.5%) with DSH, 2 cases (1.0%) with anorexia nervosa and 4 cases (2.0%) with other eating disorder.

Regarding the management clinicians reported the following in the follow-up questionnaire: medical outpatient monitoring was used in 137 cases of ARFID (69.5%), dietetic advice in 165 cases (83.8%), psychoeducation in 108 cases (54.8%), medication in 52 cases (26.4%), tube feeding 21 cases (10.7%), nutritional supplements in 78 cases (39.6%), individual psychological therapy in 52 cases (26.4%), family therapy in 36 cases (18.3%) and input from another specialty in 47 cases (23.9%). Regarding the follow-up plans, clinicians reported that 186 cases (58.3%) were going to be followed up with the same clinician that completed the questionnaire, 28 cases (8.8%) were going to be referred to a medical specialist, 67 cases (21.0%) to a psychiatric specialist, 54 cases (16.9%) to a psychologist, 79 (24.8%) to another healthcare provider and 18 cases (5.6%) discharged to primary care. In total, 22 cases of ARFID (11.2%) were admitted to a paediatric inpatient unit or day care and 3 cases (1.5%) were admitted to a psychiatric inpatient unit or day care. Regarding the follow-up plans, 95 cases (48.2%) were going to be followed up with the same clinician that completed the questionnaire, 15 cases (7.6%) were going to be referred to a medical specialist, 22 cases (11.2%) to a psychiatric specialist, 23 cases (11.7%) to a psychologist, 27 (13.7%) to another healthcare provider and 62 cases (31.5%) discharged to primary care.

At follow-up, clinicians reported that there was a safeguarding referral in 7 cases (3.6%), other social care support in 12 cases (6.1%), special educational assessment in 8 cases (4.1%), special educational provision (including change of school) in 13 cases (6.6%) and other agencies or referrals in 22 cases (11.2%).

Discussion

This is the first study reporting IRs of ARFID in CAs using paediatric surveillance methodology in the UK and ROI. We found that the IRo of ARFID in the UK was 2.79 per 100 000 young people aged 5–17 years. Our results are similar to those reported by Katzman et al10 who first reported an IR of ARFID in CAs in Canada of 2.02 per 100 000 young people, a slightly lower rate than in the UK. The methodology used in the Canadian study was very similar to the present study. However, they only surveyed paediatricians while in our study we included cases reported by both paediatricians and CA psychiatrists. A previous surveillance study of childhood eating disorders (using DSM-IV criteria) conducted in the UK and ROI including children aged 5–12 years reported an IR of 3.01 per 100 000.18 Some of those identified cases would now be diagnosed with ARFID, with the latent class analysis in Pinhas et al19 suggesting a proportion of 34% (55 cases). These differences could be explained by increasing recognition of childhood onset eating disorders, and ARFID specifically, and/or increasing incidence of eating disorders in childhood since these studies were performed.

We found that the IR in males was higher in the youngest age group (aged 5–9 years) with an IR of 4.22 per 100 000 young people than in the other two age groups. In contrast, the IR in females was higher than that of males in the two older age groups. Overall, including all age groups, the IR in the UK of males (2.98 per 100 000) was higher than that of females (2.58 per 100 000). In contrast, the Canadian study10 found a higher incidence in females (2.54 per 100 000) than among males (1.52 per 100 000). Contrary to the initial ARFID studies that were conducted in specialised eating disorders services,20,25 our study had a slightly higher proportion of males (54.5%) than females (45.5%) which is consistent with prior studies using general population samples26,28 and supports the idea that ARFID is equally common in boys and girls or even more frequent in boys.8 We speculate that the different IRs in our sample between sexes and age groups are related to different drivers of food avoidance or restriction with younger boys having more sensory sensitivities and comorbidity with ASD and older girls having more lack of interest in eating and fear of consequences of eating and comorbid emotional disorders.7

The IR in the ROI, 0.73 per 100 000 children and young people, was lower probably due to a lower reporting pattern and might not be due to actual lower IRs. Some prior surveillance studies using the BPSU and CAPSS methodology have used assumptions to account for the incidence among unreturned cards and uncompleted questionnaires.29 30 If those assumptions are used, the IRs are considerably higher (we provide the IRs for the UK using similar assumptions in online supplemental file 3. The capture–recapture method31 was not applied in this study because it was felt that the potential for paediatricians and CA psychiatrists to see all cases was questionable, given the lack of duplicates between the two.

Regarding accessibility to health services, our study refers to contact with general paediatric services (both acute and community) and mental health services, both of which are widely available but require referral through primary care and health inequalities may occur at both levels of access. However, ethnic minorities have historically been less likely to receive a diagnosis or treatment for an eating disorder.32 In our study, the cohort reported by paediatricians consisted of a slightly wider range of ethnic minorities (68.3% of the paediatric cohort were individuals identified as white British against 75.4% of the psychiatric). Efforts should be made to reduce any ethnic inequalities in accessing healthcare.

We found that CAs with ARFID often present with a comorbid psychiatric disorder, with anxiety the most common. This is important because the presence of anxiety or other mental disorder indicates that potentially treatable factors may contribute to the presentation, and that assessment for psychological or pharmacological intervention is warranted. In our sample, 39.7% of patients seen in paediatric care were referred to mental health or psychology services. Other common comorbidities were neurodevelopmental disorders, especially ASD and LD. The rate of comorbidity with ASD in our sample was high, with 44% of the sample having a diagnosis of ASD. Other studies conducted in the UK have also reported high comorbidity of ASD and ARFID. For example, in the study by Watts et al.33 28% of their sample of CAs with ARFID had comorbid ASD and a further 23% had suspected ASD; and in the study by Farag et al34 almost 55% of patients with ARFID referred to a tertiary care paediatric feeding clinic had a comorbid diagnosis of ASD. However, in the Canadian surveillance study of ARFID, the rate of ASD was much lower, around 8% of the sample. Overall, our results are consistent with prior literature which describes that usually CA with ARFID present with a psychiatric comorbidity.8 35 We found that it is not uncommon that CAs with ARFID have a family history of psychiatric disorders, most commonly past and/or current anxiety and depression, and ASD.

Clinicians reported that management plans for CAs with ARFID most commonly included dietetic advice, medical monitoring and psychoeducation. Almost 60% of cases were going to be followed up by the same clinicians who reported the cases and only 18 cases (5.4%) were going to be discharged to primary care. Among clinicians who reported they would follow up on the case themselves, 75% completed the follow-up questionnaire. The number of cases requiring hospital treatment was low in our sample, with 37 new cases of ARFID (11.6%) being admitted to a paediatric inpatient unit or day care and 12 cases (3.8%) to a psychiatric inpatient unit or day care.

The most commonly used psychiatric medications were selective serotonin reuptake inhibitors, followed by melatonin, stimulants and antipsychotics, which we speculate were likely used to treat the comorbidities. As a new diagnosis, evidence for pharmacological interventions for ARFID is still limited.

According to clinicians’ clinical impression, almost half of the cases for which information was available improved with treatment and 10% improved without treatment at follow-up. Most common emergent psychiatric disorders at follow-up were anxiety and ASD. Two cases were diagnosed with anorexia nervosa and four with another eating disorder (not bulimia nervosa) at follow-up. Further research using a longitudinal design should explore the temporal stability of the ARFID diagnosis and identify factors that predispose to a diagnosis of anorexia nervosa. Reported management and follow-up plans were similar in the baseline and follow-up questionnaire but the proportion of cases discharged to primary care increased almost sixfold at 1-year follow-up.

Strengths and limitations of this study

Using active surveillance methodology enabled ascertainment of national prospective data from paediatricians and CA psychiatrists in the UK and ROI. This large sample included CYP of different ages and geographical areas, ensuring data were representative. Our study is the first to report outcomes at 1 year in a large representative sample of CAs with ARFID. Our results are of relevance primarily to the UK and Ireland but may be of value to other European countries.

It has been suggested that the current ARFID DSM-5 diagnostic criteria may not ensure optimal diagnostic validity to be useful in epidemiological research, given it is an umbrella term encompassing a number of presentations.36 Surveillance methodology is limited to cases seen in teams that have a consultant paediatrician or CA psychiatrist, so ARFID cases treated in primary care or in psychology or dietetic-led services will not be included and so does not provide accurate data on community prevalence. Case ascertainment in surveillance studies also relies on clinicians’ ability to make accurate diagnoses. In recent years, clinicians have become more familiar with ARFID, but diagnostic challenges still exist. For example, children with ASD often present with feeding difficulties37 and identifying those that reach the diagnostic threshold for ARFID may be difficult. There might be difficulties when applying the ARFID diagnostic criteria in situations when there are comorbid psychiatric or medical conditions that may cause restrictive eating and food avoidance or selectivity. Another limitation is that we did not include children under 5 years of age. The DSM-5 states that ARFID can be diagnosed across the lifespan1 but we excluded children under 5 years because CA psychiatrists do not see this population; picky or fussy eating is common in younger children,38 and therefore, may be more difficult to differentiate from cases of ARFID and carries the risk of overreporting in children under 5 years of age; and we also wanted to compare IRs with those reported in Canada.10

In our study, data were collected using a questionnaire developed by the authors and diagnoses were inferred on the basis of operational criteria. We modelled our definition based on the Canadian surveillance study10 to allow for comparisons. Diagnoses were not validated using a clinical instrument such as the Pica, ARFID and Rumination Disorder Interview (PARDI).39 The PARDI was not available when the study protocol was designed. Neither were any screening tool such as the Eating Disturbances in Youth-Questionnaire28 or the Nine-Item ARFID Screen that would have helped to increase diagnostic accuracy.40 Sex was reported in the questionnaire by the responsible clinician and it was limited to male or female, which did not allow to report intersex individuals.

The response rate to the initial ecard from CAPSS was lower than in previous studies (47.5%) and the BPSU response rate was also slightly lower than is typical. This may in part be a result of the clinical pressures services experienced during the COVID-19 pandemic. During the COVID-19 pandemic, there was a well-recognised increase observed worldwide with regard to eating disorder psychopathology.41 However, a recent systematic review has suggested that the increase did not apply to ARFID presentations.42

The lower number of new cases of ARFID in ROI may be due to an actual lower incidence or to a different reporting pattern to the BPSU and CAPSS. In our prospective research design, loss to long-term follow-up may introduce bias in data analysis for longitudinal findings. Due to the nature of the data, we were not able to compensate for missing data in the analyses.

Conclusion

This is the first study reporting estimates of incidence of ARFID in CAs in the UK and ROI using surveillance methodology. Our findings suggest that ARFID is a disorder of relatively low incidence in CAs. Our results show that boys have a higher incidence than girls during childhood while the opposite happens during adolescence. These findings will influence service planning and resource allocation, as well as the development of evidence-based interventions. Further surveillance studies should be conducted including children under 5 years of age what will help to better understand early presentations of ARFID. Future research should also take into account cases seen in primary care and psychology or dietetic-led services to report more accurate IRs and include long-term prospective studies that explore the course and diagnostic stability of ARFID.
