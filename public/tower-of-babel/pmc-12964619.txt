Association of GLP-1 receptor agonists with herpes risks in diabetes mellitus: a target trial emulation
Authors: Chi-Hsien Huang, I-Hui Chiang, I-Cheng Lu, Po-Hsuan Lai, Pei-Lun Liao, Jing-Yang Huang, Ming-Ta Hsieh, Chi-Wei Lin, I-Ting Liu, James Cheng-Chung Wei
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundGLP-1 receptor agonists (GLP-1RA) are effective for glycemic control and cardiometabolic outcomes, but their infectious safety, particularly regarding herpes simplex virus (HSV) and herpes zoster (HZ), is underexplored. This study evaluated the association between GLP-1RA therapy and risks of HSV and HZ infections in patients with DM, compared to sodium-glucose co-transporter-2 inhibitors (SGLT-2i) and dipeptidyl peptidase-4 inhibitors (DPP-4i).MethodsWe conducted a retrospective cohort analysis using target trial emulation principles on de-identified electronic health records from the TriNetX U.S. Network (2015–2022). Adults aged ≥ 18 years with diabetes prescribed biguanides were included. New users of GLP-1RA were compared to DPP-4i users in the first cohort and SGLT-2i users in the second cohort. Primary outcomes were HSV and HZ incidence, analyzed with Kaplan–Meier curves and Cox proportional hazards models.ResultsAmong 95,190 GLP-1RA vs. DPP-4i pairs and 82,789 GLP-1RA vs. SGLT-2i pairs, GLP-1RA therapy was associated with higher risks of HSV (HR = 1.387, 95% CI = 1.249–1.539) and HZ (HR = 1.294, 95% CI = 1.203–1.392) compared to DPP-4i and HSV (HR = 1.277, 95% CI = 1.132–1.442) and HZ (HR = 1.18, 95% CI = 1.085–1.284) compared to SGLT-2i. Younger patients (18–50 years) on GLP-1RAs had elevated risks for HSV (HR = 1.632) and HZ (HR = 1.553). Female GLP-1RA users showed increased HSV risk (HR = 1.51), and poorly controlled patients (HbA1c ≥ 7%) had higher HSV risks (HR = 1.195). Herpes zoster vaccination mitigated these risks.ConclusionsGLP-1RA use is associated with an increased risk of HSV and HZ infections compared with SGLT-2i or a DPP-4i use, especially in younger, female, and poorly controlled T2DM patients. Vaccination against herpes zoster is essential in mitigating these risks.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12916-025-04606-w.

Background

The introduction of sodium-glucose co-transporter 2 inhibitors (SGLT-2i), glucagon-like peptide-1 receptor agonists (GLP-1RA), and dipeptidyl peptidase-4 inhibitors (DPP-4i) has significantly advanced therapeutic strategies for managing type 2 diabetes mellitus (DM), offering improved glycemic control and notable cardiovascular and renal benefits [1–3]. Additionally, the neuroprotective properties of GLP-1 and its agonists are well-documented. Studies highlight their potential in reducing neuroinflammation, oxidative stress, and apoptosis while promoting neurogenesis and synaptic plasticity [4]. These mechanisms have shown promise in neurodegenerative diseases, including stroke, Parkinson’s disease, Alzheimer’s disease, and amyotrophic lateral sclerosis, where GLP-1RAs have demonstrated efficacy in reducing pathological markers and improving cognitive function in preclinical research [5, 6].

Despite their efficacy, the safety profiles of SGLT-2i and GLP-1RA, particularly concerning infectious disease risks and gastrointestinal adverse events, remain a subject of ongoing investigation [7–9]. For instance, SGLT-2i have been associated with an increased risk of genital mycotic infections, likely due to persistent glycosuria, which facilitates the growth of pathogenic bacteria and fungi [7]. Furthermore, risks of urinary tract infections, volume depletion, and ketoacidosis have also been reported, highlighting the need for vigilant monitoring of patients on SGLT-2i [8, 9]. GLP-1RA have been linked to gastrointestinal issues such as intolerable nausea and vomiting, and in rare cases, pancreatitis [2, 10]. DPP-4i generally have fewer adverse effects but have been associated with nasopharyngitis, headache, and an increased risk of acute pancreatitis and pancreatic cancer [11]. An increased rate of hospitalization has been reported with saxagliptin; however, this does not appear to be a class effect [11].

Herpes simplex virus (HSV) and varicella-zoster virus (VZV) infections represent significant clinical challenges, particularly in patients with DM [12, 13]. The prevalence of HSV and VZV infections is notably higher among diabetic patients compared to the general population, which may be attributed to the immunocompromised state associated with DM [14, 15]. Studies have shown that diabetic patients have a significantly higher incidence of herpes zoster (HZ) (9.3 cases per 1000 person-years) and experience greater severity and prolonged chronic pain compared to non-diabetic individuals [14, 16]. Additionally, diabetic patients are more susceptible to severe complications, such as secondary bacterial infections and postherpetic neuralgia, following episodes of herpes zoster [14]. Therefore, measures to mitigate risk factors, including avoiding immunosuppressive agents and implementing proactive immunization strategies, are crucial, necessitating increased surveillance in this population.

Emerging data suggest that GLP-1 RA might reduce inflammatory responses and modulate immune responses through the adenosine monophosphate kinase (AMPK) pathway. This modulation occurs through the upregulation of AMPK via cyclic adenosine monophosphate (cAMP) accumulation, potentially impacting infection risk [17]. Conversely, the negative caloric balance induced by SGLT-2i has also been reported to activate AMPK [18]. Therefore, both drug classes may activate the AMPK pathway and subsequently promote the proliferation of regulatory T lymphocytes [17]. This proliferation is crucial in modulating immune responses and may influence the pathogenesis and reactivation of herpes infections [19].

Direct clinical evidence linking these medications to herpes infections remains limited. Two large cohort studies found no increased risk of HZ associated with SGLT-2i use, and these agents served as negative control outcomes in sensitivity analyses [20, 21]. In contrast, a national cohort study of Asian patients with diabetes reported an elevated risk of HZ with short-term use of DPP-4i [22]. Conducting a randomized controlled trial (RCT) to compare the risk of herpes virus infection across these drug classes would be exceedingly challenging. Such a trial would require a large sample size and extended follow-up to capture a sufficient number of infection events, making it logistically and financially impractical [23]. Ethical and feasibility concerns also limit the design of such trials—for instance, it may not be justifiable to randomize patients to specific agents solely to assess safety outcomes, especially when some newer agents offer established cardiovascular and renal benefits. Notably, no head-to-head RCTs have directly compared GLP-1RAs with SGLT-2is or DPP-4is for clinical outcomes [24].

To address this evidence gap, we conducted an observational study designed to emulate a target trial, following the framework proposed by Hernán and Robins [25, 26]. We explicitly defined the protocol of an ideal trial, specifying the eligible population with diabetes, the treatment strategies (initiation of GLP-1RA vs. SGLT-2i vs. DPP-4i), the follow-up period, and the primary outcome (incident herpes infection). This emulation approach allows for causal inference by aligning the observational analysis with the methodological principles of an RCT [25]. The central causal question posed in our study was: “What would be the risk of herpes virus infection (herpes simplex or herpes zoster) if a patient with diabetes initiated a GLP-1RA, compared with initiating an SGLT-2i or a DPP-4i?” We aimed to provide a robust estimate of the comparative risk of herpes infection associated with these commonly prescribed glucose-lowering therapies.

Methods

Study design and data source

This retrospective cohort study analyzed de-identified electronic health records from the TriNetX Network, a global, federated research platform that aggregates anonymized patient-level data on diagnoses, procedures, medications, laboratory results, and genomic profiles. The dataset for this analysis obtained from a subset of the network, comprising records from 64 healthcare organizations across the USA. TriNetX maintains data quality through a validated three-pillar framework of conformance, completeness, and plausibility: heterogeneous source data are harmonized to standardized vocabularies (ICD, LOINC, and SNOMED); comprehensive clinical and demographic elements are captured to ensure full representation of each patient; and automated distributional checks of laboratory and other variables verify consistency with real-world clinical patterns [27, 28]. All diagnoses were mapped to ICD-10-CM codes, with platform-integrated crosswalks assuring concordance with legacy ICD-9-CM terminology. Data were queried and analyzed on June 21, 2024. The study followed the Strengthening the Reporting of Observational Studies in Epidemiology (STROBE) guidelines and received ethical approval from the Institutional Review Board of Chung Shan Medical University Hospital (IRB number: CS2-21,176).

In accordance with our data-use agreements and privacy regulations, individual-level data cannot be made publicly available; all analyses were conducted entirely within the TriNetX platform. Qualified investigators with appropriate institutional affiliations may request access to TriNetX through a licensing agreement. For detailed information about TriNetX, please visit their website: https://trinetx.com/?mc_cid=7e2ecd5bc5&mc_eid=%5BUNIQID%5D.

Target trial emulation

To approximate causal inferences that might arise from a RCT, we applied target trial emulation principles [29]. The hypothetical target trial would randomize adults with DM to either: GLP-1RA therapy or DPP-4i therapy (Cohort 1), or GLP-1RA therapy or SGLT-2i therapy (Cohort 2), with the goal of evaluating time to HSV or HZ infection (ICD-9-CM codes 054, 053; ICD-10-CM codes B00, B02). We excluded patients with a history of herpes zoster prior to the index date, aligning with the hypothetical trial’s exclusion of individuals with active or recent infections. In our emulated design, the index date corresponded to the date of first prescription of a GLP-1RA or comparator medication. Follow-up continued from initiation until the first occurrence of HSV or HZ, treatment change, or end of available data. By specifying these elements—eligibility criteria, treatment strategies, assignment procedures, follow-up period, and outcomes—our retrospective analysis mimics the structure of a pragmatic RCT where the randomization is replaced by comprehensive propensity score matching. We have established the specifications for this trial corresponding to each target outcome (Additional file 1: Table S1–S2).

Study cohorts

We included adults (≥ 18 years) with diabetes mellitus—identified by ICD-9-CM code 250 and cross-mapped to ICD-10-CM codes E08–E13—who received at least one prescription for a biguanide (ATC code A10BA) between 1 January 2015 and 31 December 2022. The analysis focused on three classes of antidiabetic medications: GLP-1RAs, DPP-4is, and SGLT-2is. The study was divided into two cohorts: the first compared users of GLP-1RAs with users of DPP-4is, and the second compared users of GLP-1RAs with users of SGLT-2is. Patients with a history of herpes zoster prior to the index date or those who received alternative treatments on or after the index date were excluded from both cohorts. Outcomes and covariates

The primary outcomes were the incidence of herpes simplex infection (ICD-9-CM codes 054, ICD-10-CM code B00) and herpes zoster infection (ICD-9-CM codes 053, ICD-10-CM code B02). The secondary outcome was the incidence of postherpetic polyneuropathy (ICD-9-CM codes 053.13, ICD-10-CM code B02.23). Demographic characteristics—age at index, sex, race, ethnicity, and body mass index (BMI)—were obtained from patient-attribute tables; BMI was analyzed both as a continuous variable and categorically (< 25, 25–29.9, and ≥ 30 kg/m2), and race/ethnicity followed TriNetX harmonized classifications. Health-care utilization, derived from encounter tables, included numbers of outpatient visits, hospital admissions, and emergency department visits. Lifestyle factors identified through diagnosis codes comprised tobacco use, nicotine dependence, and alcohol-related disorders. Socioeconomic disadvantage was approximated with ICD-10-CM Z55–Z65 (“persons with potential health hazards related to socioeconomic and psychosocial circumstances”), a set of World Health Organization and Centers for Disease Control and Prevention–recognized social-determinant codes that capture specific socioeconomic challenges affecting health outcomes [30]. Comorbidities—including hypertension, hyperlipidemia, chronic obstructive pulmonary disease (COPD), ischemic heart disease, asthma, substance-related mental and behavioral disorders, other heart diseases, chronic kidney disease (CKD), liver disease, cerebral infarction, neoplasms, rheumatoid arthritis, other rheumatoid arthritis, systemic lupus erythematosus (SLE), psoriasis, and obesity—and exposure to antihyperglycemic agents (sulfonylureas, α-glucosidase inhibitors, thiazolidinediones, SGLT-2i, DPP-4i, and other glucose-lowering drugs) were identified from diagnostic and prescription records. Statistical analyses

To mitigate confounding by indication and other baseline differences, we utilized the TriNetX built-in function to perform 1:1 propensity score matching, ensuring comparable baseline characteristics between groups [31]. The standardized mean difference (SMD) was calculated to assess balance in baseline characteristics, with an SMD > 0.1 indicating imbalance. To handle data while preserving the original dataset’s integrity without listwise deletion or imputation, patients with available BMI values were categorized into three clinically relevant strata: normal weight (BMI < 25 kg/m2), overweight (BMI 25 to < 30 kg/m2), and obese (BMI ≥ 30 kg/m2). Propensity score matching was then performed separately within each BMI stratum. Kaplan–Meier analysis was conducted to estimate the cumulative incidence probabilities of herpes simplex and herpes zoster infections at daily intervals. Cox proportional hazards models were used to evaluate infection risks, with the proportional hazards’ assumption tested using the generalized Schoenfeld residuals method. Hazard ratios (HRs) and 95% confidence intervals (CIs) for herpes simplex and herpes zoster infections were estimated with Cox proportional-hazards regression. The proportional-hazards assumption was tested with Schoenfeld residuals. Analyses were run in R (version 4.0.2) using the survival package (v3.2–3) within the TriNetX Analytics platform. Two-sided p values < 0.05 were considered statistically significant.

Subgroup analyses were stratified by sex, age, HbA1c levels (< 7% vs. ≥ 7%), individual GLP-1RA (exenatide, liraglutide, lixisenatide, dulaglutide, and semaglutide), and herpes zoster vaccination status, which was further categorized by vaccine type (live-attenuated Zostavax vs. recombinant adjuvanted Shingrix). Vaccination status was coded as follows: Zostavax—NDC 1292422 and CPT 90736 (live attenuated varicella-zoster virus, Oka-Merck strain); Shingrix—CPT 90750 and NDC 1986821 (recombinant subunit varicella-zoster virus glycoprotein E). We conducted sensitivity analyses using follow-up windows of 1, 3, 5, and 9 years. Because treatment persistence can affect long-term risk estimates, we performed an additional analysis restricted to participants who filled at least two prescriptions within the first year, using this threshold as a proxy for sustained exposure.

Results

Participant characteristics

The first cohort, comparing patients using GLP-1RA to those using DPP-4i, initially included 5,394,231 patients diagnosed with diabetes from the TriNetX database. After excluding patients with prior herpes infection and applying a new-use design, 155,793 GLP-1RA users and 138,295 DPP-4i users remained. Following propensity score matching, the final cohort comprised 95,190 GLP-1RA users and 95,190 DPP-4i users. Similarly, the second cohort, comparing GLP-1RA users to SGLT-2i users, started with 128,001 GLP-1RA users and 108,488 SGLT-2i users after applying the same exclusions. Propensity score matching resulted in 82,789 users in each group. The methodology and participant selection process are outlined in Fig. 1 a and b, while the mean and median follow-up times across drug cohorts are presented in Additional file 1: Table S4. Compared with GLP‑1RA users, mean follow‑up differed by < 3 months in both DPP‑4i and SGLT‑2i cohorts, yielding only modest person‑year variations.

The composite and individual risks of herpesviral and herpes zoster infection

The baseline characteristics of the GLP-1RA versus DPP-4i and GLP-1RA versus SGLT-2i cohorts after 1:1 propensity score matching are summarized in Table 1. The mean age of GLP-1RA and DPP-4i users was 58.7 years, with standard deviations of 11.4 and 12.1, respectively (standardized mean difference [SMD] = 0.0033). For GLP-1RA and SGLT-2i users, the mean ages were 58.2 and 58.1 years, respectively, both with a standard deviation of 12.0 (SMD = 0.0093). The proportion of female participants was similar across groups, comprising 45.3% in the GLP-1RA group and 45.5% in the DPP-4i group (SMD = 0.0044), and 41.7% and 42.2% in the GLP-1RA and SGLT-2i cohorts, respectively (SMD = 0.0114). Racial composition was comparable, with non-Hispanic/Latino individuals making up the largest proportion in each cohort. Other key baseline characteristics, such as BMI, healthcare utilization, comorbidities, and diabetes medications, were well-balanced across the matched cohorts, evidenced by minimal SMDs. These balanced characteristics enable reliable comparisons in subsequent outcome analyses. Subgroup analyses

In the subgroup analysis (Additional file 2: Fig. S1), the risk of composite outcomes associated with GLP-1RA use was comparable between female and male users when compared with DPP-4i or SGLT-2i (Additional file 1: Table S6). However, the risk of HSV infection was higher in females receiving GLP-1RA compared to DPP-4i (HR: 1.51, 95% CI: 1.322–1.725) than in males (HR: 1.12, 95% CI: 0.931–1.336) (Additional file 1: Table S7). This elevated risk for women was not observed with HZ infection (Additional file 1: Table S8). These findings suggest a sex-specific susceptibility, indicating that women may be at a higher risk of HSV infection when treated with GLP-1RA compared to DPP-4i.

On the other hand, younger individuals (aged 18–50) using GLP-1RA demonstrated a significantly higher composite risk compared to DPP-4i users (HR: 1.553, 95% CI: 1.362–1.771) than those older than 50 years (HR: 1.174, 95% CI: 1.097–1.256) (Additional file 2: Fig. S1, Additional file 1: Table S6). Specifically, younger GLP-1RA users (18–50 years) exhibited a more pronounced increase in hazard ratios for HZ infections (HR:1.632, 95%CI:1.363–1.954) compared to DPP-4i (Additional file 1: Table S8). A similar pattern of increased risk among younger individuals was also evident in comparisons between GLP-1RA and SGLT-2i.

The type of GLP-1RA therapy appeared to influence the risk of composite outcomes (Additional file 1: Table S9). Notably, lixisenatide users had the highest risk, with a hazard ratio of 2.002 (95% CI: 1.302–3.079) compared to DPP-4i users. Semaglutide also demonstrated an elevated risk (HR: 1.600, 95% CI: 1.471–1.740). When compared to SGLT-2i, lixisenatide and semaglutide were similarly associated with increased risks, with hazard ratios of 2.448 (95% CI: 1.373–4.366) and 1.520 (95% CI: 1.377–1.677), respectively.

Our analysis indicated no statistically significant difference in the risk of postherpetic polyneuropathy between GLP-1RA users and those using DPP-4i or SGLT-2i (Additional file 1: Table S10). These findings suggest that GLP-1RA therapy may not substantially influence the incidence of postherpetic polyneuropathy.

Stratified analyses by HbA1c levels showed no significant differences in risk among patients with HbA1c < 7%, regardless of the comparator drug (Additional file 1: Table S11). However, in patients with HbA1c ≥ 7%, GLP-1RA use was associated with a higher risk of composite outcomes and herpes simplex infection compared with DPP-4i (HR:1.125, 95% CI: 1.022–1.238) and (HR:1.269, 95% CI:1.078–1.493). Similarly, GLP-1RA use slightly increased the risk of herpes simplex infection compared with SGLT-2i in patients with HbA1c ≥ 7% (HR:1.195, 95% CI: 1.000–1.426).

In patients vaccinated against herpes zoster, GLP-1RA use did not significantly increase the risk of composite outcomes, herpes simplex, or herpes zoster infections compared with DPP-4i or SGLT-2i use (Table 3). However, in unvaccinated patients, GLP-1RA use was associated with a higher risk of composite outcomes (HR: 1.227, 95% CI: 1.145–1.315) and herpes simplex (HR:1.347, 95% CI: 1.202–1.509) and herpes zoster (HR:1.165, 95% CI: 1.068–1.269) infections compared with DPP-4i. When compared with SGLT-2i, GLP-1RA use increased the risk of herpes simplex infection (HR:1.216, 95% CI: 1.057–1.398) in unvaccinated patients, but no differences were observed for composite outcomes or herpes zoster infections. Sensitivity analysis

The duration of follow-up revealed that the risk of composite outcomes increased over time among GLP-1RA users (Additional file 1: Table S13). At the 1-year mark, the HR compared to DPP-4i was 1.121 (95% CI: 1.014–1.240), rising to 1.321 (95% CI: 1.244–1.404) at 9 years. A similar pattern was observed in the comparison with SGLT-2i, with the HR increasing to 1.201 (95% CI: 1.120–1.287) over 9 years. Based on the intention-to-treat analysis, GLP-1RA therapy was linked to a consistently higher risk of the composite herpes outcome than DPP-4i (adjusted HR = 1.06, 95% CI 1.01–1.11), whereas no excess risk was seen when GLP-1RA was compared with SGLT-2i (Additional file 1: Table S14). Excluding patients with unknown race or ethnicity in the sensitivity analysis produced results consistent with the primary analysis (Additional file 1: Table S15). Among patients with ≥ 2 prescription fills, the adjusted hazard ratios for the composite herpes outcome were directionally consistent with the primary analysis (Additional file 1: Table S16), indicating that prolonged GLP-1RA exposure may continue to confer an elevated risk.

Discussion

To our knowledge, this is the first study to demonstrate an increased risk of herpes infection associated with GLP-1RA therapy compared to SGLT-2i and DPP-4i. This risk was notably higher among unvaccinated individuals, suggesting that herpes zoster vaccination may help mitigate this elevated risk. Additionally, the findings emphasize the importance of considering both sex and age when assessing GLP-1RA risk profiles, as younger individuals and females appear particularly susceptible to specific adverse outcomes.

Earlier research on SGLT-2i generally found no increased risk of herpes infections, serving as a negative control in sensitivity analyses [20, 21]. This underscores the potential uniqueness of the observed risks with GLP-1RAs and emphasizes the need for further comparative evaluations to confirm these differential effects. The immunomodulatory roles of GLP-1RAs, potentially mediated through AMPK pathway activation, may partly explain these distinctions, as this mechanism could influence viral reactivation [17]. Moreover, our findings suggest that this heightened susceptibility is more pronounced in younger patients and females. This age-related disparity may reflect variations in immune response, as younger individuals on GLP-1RAs might experience heightened inflammatory modulation, potentially disrupting immune balance and increasing susceptibility to HZ. Similar patterns have been reported with other drug classes, such as DPP-4i, where younger patients also exhibited a higher infection risk of HZ [22]. Further research is needed to elucidate the mechanisms underlying these differential infection risks across age groups.

Hormonal regulation may influence HSV reactivation or susceptibility through the immune-modulatory effects of GLP-1 [32]. Estrogen regulates GLP-1 receptor expression in peripheral tissues, potentially altering the immune effects of GLP-1RAs and amplifying infection risk in a sex-specific manner [33]. These findings suggest that GLP-1RA therapy may interact with sex-specific hormonal and immune dynamics, thereby increasing HSV risk among female patients.

The stratified analysis emphasizes the importance of individualized counselling before initiating diabetic agents in patients with a history of herpes infections. While no differences in the risk of HSV or HZ infections were observed between GLP-1RA and SGLT-2i in patients with HbA1c ≥ 7%, the significantly increased risk of HSV infections associated with GLP-1RA compared to DPP-4i underscores the need for periodic monitoring. For patients with a history of recurrent or prior HSV infections, tailored counseling is essential to ensure the prompt recognition and management of characteristic skin lesions before initiating GLP-1RA therapy.

Notably, no increased risk of herpes infection was observed among GLP-1RA users who received HZ vaccination prior to initiating therapy. A meta-analysis reported that the live-attenuated zoster vaccine (LZV) reduced HZ incidence by 48% in adults with diabetes, while the recombinant zoster vaccine (RZV) demonstrated a higher efficacy of 91% [34]. Importantly, our study uniquely incorporated vaccination status into the analysis, highlighting its significant role in mitigating infection risks among GLP-1RA users. Notably, our findings revealed cross-protective effects of HZ vaccination against HSV infection. These results emphasize the importance of integrating HZ immunization into the routine care of diabetic patients on GLP-1RA therapy, especially for those with suboptimal glycemic control. Proactive vaccination strategies could reduce the burden of infectious complications and improve treatment outcomes in this vulnerable population.

This study has several strengths. Leveraging the TriNetX network, we assembled a large, geographically diverse cohort encompassing a broad spectrum of racial and ethnic groups and spanning both private and public insurance coverage. The use of target trial emulation and rigorous propensity-score matching helped minimize confounding. Prespecified subgroup analyses explored effect modification by age, sex, and vaccination status. The longitudinal design allowed for a precise evaluation of temporal trends in infection risk. However, we acknowledge that the generalizability of our findings is limited. In particular, these results may not extend to low-resource settings or regions where GLP-1RA therapies are not widely available, given differences in healthcare infrastructure and access to these medications. Second, because this retrospective analysis relies on de-identified, aggregated data from the TriNetX Research Network, under-reporting or misclassification of herpes infections and the inability to validate comorbidities with ≥ 2 ICD-coded encounters on separate dates may introduce misclassification bias. Third, because our analysis relied on electronic health records, some confounders may have remained unmeasured. Specifically, we lacked data on diabetes duration, severity, and complications; lifestyle factors such as diet, physical activity, and sleep; medication dose and adherence; and complete vaccination histories. To mitigate cofounding by indication, we incorporated surrogate indicators of baseline glycemic control (HbA1c ≥ 7% vs. < 7%) and adjusted for a comprehensive set of comorbidities. Nonetheless, residual confounding from unmeasured variables may still persist. Fourth, missing data for several variables—including BMI, race/ethnicity, and other sociodemographic factors—may have introduced selection bias and reduced the precision of our effect estimates, although multiple sensitivity analyses produced consistent results. Fifth, because TrinetX does not capture treatment duration, dose titration, or medication adherence, unmeasured variation in these factors may have influenced our results. Although the sensitivity analysis restricted to patients with ≥ 2 prescription refills showed hazard ratios consistent with the primary analysis, we cannot entirely rule out residual adherence bias. Future prospective studies are warranted to validate these findings and further investigate the underlying mechanisms.

Conclusions

Our findings suggest that GLP-1RAs are associated with an increased risk of HSV and HZ infections compared to SGLT-2i and DPP-4i, particularly among younger and female patients. Notably, herpes zoster vaccination appears to mitigate these risks, underscoring the value of immunization strategies for individuals initiating GLP-1RA therapy. Future randomized controlled trials are warranted to evaluate the protective effect of herpes zoster vaccination in patients initiating GLP-1RAs and to compare the safety profiles of different GLP-1RA formulations.
