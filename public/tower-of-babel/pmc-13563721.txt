Temporal and spatial patterns of arthropod-related mortality in Iran: a nationwide analysis (2016–2023)
Authors: Parisa Soltan-Alinejad, Reza Ebrahimoghli, Elaheh Kazemi, Amin Ataey, Eslam Moradi-Asl
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundThis research was conducted to assess the arthropod-related mortality in Iran for the period 2016–2023.MethodsThe information on arthropod-related mortality was collected from the Iranian Ministry of Health and Medical Education. The descriptive analyses and charts were created using R version 3.4. ArcMap GIS 10.8.1 software was utilized to design and produce the study map. Temporal trends were examined using the Mann–Kendall test and Theil–Sen slope estimator, while seasonal patterns were assessed via the Hirsch–Slack modified test, Poisson regression with Fourier terms (to estimate annual percent change), chi-square goodness-of-fit, Kruskal–Wallis, Rayleigh’s test, and STL decomposition. A p-value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.ResultsThe highest arthropod-related mortality rates were observed in the southern and southeastern provinces, particularly Sistan and Baluchestan and Hormozgan, likely due to favorable ecological conditions supporting high densities of medically important arthropods. Among all recorded cases, the most frequent cause of death was ICD-10 code X22 (contact with venomous arthropods), accounting for 219 fatalities. A pronounced seasonal pattern was evident, with mortality peaking significantly during the warmer months of the year.ConclusionsEffective reduction of arthropod-related mortality requires timely, location-specific public health strategies, particularly in summer and in southern and southeastern Iran.Keywords: Arthropod vectors, Vector-borne diseases, Insect bites and stings, Mortality

Background

Iran's diverse ecosystems and rich arthropod fauna make the country particularly susceptible to health problems from venomous bites, stings, and other arthropod-related injuries [1, 2]. Arthropod bites and stings pose a significant public health challenge worldwide due to their capacity to induce immediate toxic effects and facilitate the transmission of infectious diseases [3]. Arthropods, especially mosquitoes, rank among the most lethal organisms affecting human health, mainly because of the diseases they transmit. Each year, diseases transmitted by mosquitoes lead to more than one million fatalities, with malaria responsible for approximately 597,000–608,000 deaths, predominantly in children under the age of five, and over 263 million cases documented in 2023 [4]. Each year, around 50,000 cases of scorpion stings are reported in Iran. Some epidemiological studies have shown that scorpion stings are the leading cause of envenomation in Iran, particularly in the southwestern regions [5, 6]. Spider bites are uncommon; however, they can sometimes result in severe systemic effects, including pulmonary edema, cardiac irregularities, coagulopathies, and death. Bites from various other arthropods, such as centipedes and millipedes, happen infrequently and are seldom lethal, generally leading to localized pain and inflammation [7]. Thus, the investigation of arthropods and the assessment of health risks arising from human interaction with them should be regarded as a fundamental priority, since these organisms are key contributors to the transmission of vector-borne pathogens, the induction of allergic and toxic responses, and the broader challenges faced by public health systems. This study aimed to investigate the documented arthropod-related mortality in Iran during 2016 to 2023, utilizing national mortality data categorized under the International Statistical Classification of Diseases and Related Health Problems (ICD-10) [8]. This study provides a comprehensive overview of the epidemiological patterns, temporal distribution, and potential public health implications of arthropod-related mortality, thereby contributing to a deeper understanding of their burden and informing future strategies for prevention and control.

Methods

Study area

Iran is located in southwestern Asia and covers a wide range of geographical and climatic conditions. The country encompasses diverse ecosystems, including humid and temperate regions along the northern Caspian Sea coast, arid and semi-arid areas in the central plateau and eastern regions, mountainous areas in the west and northwest, and warm coastal environments in the south and southeast. The northern regions are characterized by a humid subtropical climate, whereas central and eastern Iran are predominantly dry and desert-like. The western and northwestern parts of the country are largely mountainous, with colder climates and higher precipitation, while southern and southeastern regions experience hot climates influenced by the Persian Gulf, Gulf of Oman, and desert environments. These geographical and climatic variations contribute to differences in arthropod diversity, distribution, and potential exposure risks across the country [9].

Data collection

The data on arthropod-related mortality in Iran from 2016 to 2023 were obtained through official correspondence with the National Mortality Registration Portal of the Ministry of Health and Medical Education of Iran, classified according to ICD-10 codes [8].

Data analyses

To account for differences in provincial population size, crude mortality rates were calculated and expressed as deaths per 100,000 population for 2016–2023. Crude mortality rates were calculated for the entire study period (2016–2023) by dividing the total number of recorded deaths in each province during the study period by the corresponding provincial population and multiplying by 100,000. Provincial population estimates from the 2016 National Population and Housing Census were used as the population denominator because this was the most recent officially available provincial population dataset. Thus, the reported crude mortality rates represent cumulative mortality rates for the 2016–2023 study period rather than annual mortality rates.

Temporal trends were assessed with the non-parametric Mann–Kendall test applied to the annual death counts, both for the series as a whole and separately for each disease group, with the magnitude of change quantified by the Theil-Sen slope estimator and its distribution-free 95% confidence interval. Because the monthly series is strongly seasonal, the seasonal (Hirsch-Slack) modification of the Mann–Kendall test was additionally applied to the 96 monthly observations, treating the twelve Iranian (Jalali) months as blocks. Monthly death counts were then modelled with a Poisson time-series regression containing a linear term for calendar time and two pairs of Fourier (harmonic) terms representing annual and semi-annual periodicity; the annual percent change (APC) was derived from the time coefficient as (exp(12*beta)−1) × 100. Overdispersion was assessed by the ratio of the Pearson chi-square statistic to its residual degrees of freedom (1.17), and residual serial correlation by the Ljung-Box test at lag 12. Seasonality was evaluated with a chi-square goodness-of-fit test against a month-length-weighted uniform distribution, the Kruskal–Wallis test across the four seasons, the Rayleigh test of circular uniformity applied to individual dates of death, and STL (seasonal-trend decomposition by loess) of the monthly series. All analyses were performed in R version 4.3.3; two-sided p-values below 0.05 were considered statistically significant.

Result

Arthropod-borne viral fevers

Unspecified arthropod-borne viral fever (ICD-10: A94)

The ICD-10 code A94 is identified as "Unspecified arthropod-borne viral fever." This covers: Fever due to an unspecified arbovirus and infection resulting from an unspecified arbovirus. It is often termed "Arboviral fever NOS" or "Arbovirus infection NOS." According to recorded statistics, 11 deaths in Iran have been attributed to an unknown arbovirus infection transmitted through arthropod bites. Of these cases, 27% were men and 73% were women, with an average age of 46.9 years. The fatalities were reported in Sistan and Baluchestan (n=5; 0.180 deaths per 100,000 population), Semnan (n=1; 0.142), Isfahan (n=1; 0.020), Kohgiluyeh and Boyer-Ahmad (n=1; 0.140), Tehran (n=1; 0.008), Chaharmahal and Bakhtiari (n=1; 0.106), and Mazandaran (n=1; 0.030) provinces. Nearly half of the cases (45%) occurred in Sistan and Baluchestan Province. The temporal distribution of recorded deaths during 2016–2023 showed considerable variation, with the highest mortality observed in 2022 (n=3), followed by 2023, 2021, and 2017 (n=2 each), while 2018 and 2016 each recorded one death.

Crimean-Congo hemorrhagic fever (ICD-10: A98.0)

Crimean-Congo Hemorrhagic Fever (CCHF) is a severe viral hemorrhagic fever. It is primarily transmitted through tick bites or contact with infected animal or human body fluids. Between 2016 and 2023, 63 deaths attributable to CCHF were recorded in Iran. Of these cases, 76.2% (n=48) were male, and 23.8% (n=15) were female, with a mean age of 17.5 years. Mortality was reported across various Provinces, with the highest numbers recorded in Kermanshah (n=6; 0.307 deaths per 100,000 population), Sistan and Baluchestan (n=6; 0.216), Chaharmahal and Bakhtiari (n=2; 0.211), Fars (n=9; 0.186), Yazd (n=2; 0.176), and Khorasan Razavi (n=10; 0.155). Additional fatalities occurred in South Khorasan (n=1; 0.130), Kerman (n=4; 0.126), Gilan (n=3; 0.119), Hormozgan (n=2; 0.113), Golestan (n=2; 0.107), Ardabil (n=1; 0.079), Alborz (n=2; 0.074), Isfahan (n=3; 0.059), Hamadan (n=1; 0.058), Lorestan (n=1; 0.057), Tehran (n=5; 0.038), West Azerbaijan (n=1; 0.031), East Azerbaijan (n=1; 0.026), and Khuzestan (n=1; 0.021).

The temporal distribution of recorded deaths showed variation over the study period, with the highest mortality observed in 2017 (n=12), followed by 2018 (n=11), 2023 (n=9), 2022 and 2019 (n=7 each), 2020 and 2016 (n=6 each), and 2021 (n=5).

The deceased comprised a 72-year-old man and two children aged 11 months and 11 years, with all fatalities occurring in 2019.

Temporal trends and seasonality

As shown in Table 1, the Mann–Kendall test detected no statistically significant monotonic trend in arthropod-related mortality. The overall annual series was flat (Kendall’s τ = 0.25, p = 0.454), and the same conclusion held for the monthly series (p = 0.949), for the seasonal (Hirsch–Slack) Mann–Kendall test (Z = 1.20, p = 0.231), and for each of the four disease groups. The formal seasonality tests are collected in Table 2. Every test rejected a uniform distribution of deaths—the twelve-month chi-square (χ2 = 155.4), the four-season chi-square (χ2 = 117.2), the Kruskal–Wallis test (χ2 = 22.6), the Rayleigh test, and the likelihood-ratio test for the harmonic terms (χ2 = 177.5) were all significant at p < 0.001. Deaths peaked in Tir (June–July, n = 71) and fell to a minimum in Dey (December–January, n = 7), a peak-to-trough ratio of 10.1; summer accounted for 43.2% of deaths and winter for 5.5%. Table 3 reports the Poisson time-series regression, in which monthly counts were modelled with a linear time term (t) and two Fourier harmonic pairs (s1, c1, s2, c2). Consistent with Table 1, the time term was not significant (IRR = 1.001, p = 0.558), confirming a stable long-term level, whereas the harmonic terms captured the strong seasonal signal quantified in Table 2 (annual percent change +1.3%, 95% CI −2.9% to +5.6%).

Discussion

The provincial distribution of mortality in Iran, as shown in Fig. 1, reveals significant regional differences in the number of deaths across ICD-10 categories. According to the data, Sistan and Baluchestan Province (n=134; 4.83 deaths per 100,000 population) had the highest mortality rate associated with arthropod contact. This was followed by Hormozgan (n=56; 3.15), Kohgiluyeh and Boyer-Ahmad (n=8; 1.122), Kerman (n=32; 1.01), and Chaharmahal and Bakhtiari (n=8; 0.84) Provinces, which ranked second to fifth, respectively. In contrast, significantly lower rates are observed in the northern and central regions of the country, including Mazandaran (n=1; 0.03 deaths per 100,000 population, Markazi (n=1; 0.07), Zanjan (n=1; 0.09), Tehran (n=13; 0.10), and Qazvin (n=2; 0.157). This geographic gradient may reflect differences in ecological and climatic conditions across provinces. As shown in Fig. 2, cumulative arthropod-related mortality rates were generally higher in southern and southeastern provinces and lower in northern and central regions of Iran.

Annual arthropod-related mortality in Iran by disease group, 2016–2023

A strong seasonal pattern was observed in arthropod-related mortality, with a marked peak during the warmer months. Nevertheless, despite this pronounced summer predominance, a notable increase in mortality during the autumn and winter months has been observed from 2018 to 2023 (Fig. 5). Milder and shorter winters can prolong the activity season of disease vectors and venomous arthropods, potentially extending the transmission window for arboviral diseases into the cooler months. The observed trend may be consistent with the influence of climate variability; however, no causal relationship can be inferred from the present descriptive study, and further ecological and climatological investigations are needed to examine this hypothesis.

Arthropod-borne viral fevers

The high crude mortality rate of CCHF in provinces including Kermanshah, Sistan and Baluchestan, Chaharmahal and Bakhtiari, Fars, Yazd, and Khorasan Razavi may be associated with a combination of factors such as high-risk occupational exposure (involving livestock and slaughtering) [10], the existence of tick vectors including Hyalomma sp. (i.e., Hyalomma marginatum, H. dromedarii, H. anatolicum, H. lusitanicum, H. asiaticum) and Rhipicephalus sanguineus, Haemaphysalis concinna, and infected livestock [11–16], favorable ecological conditions for vector activity [17], and, in some cases, endemic border-trade dynamics such as Pakistan and Afghanistan [17, 18]. Therefore, it is essential to implement strengthened surveillance, control of livestock movement, tick management, and targeted educational initiatives for high-risk occupations in these provinces.

Parasitic protozoan diseases

Sistan and Baluchestan Province has the highest malaria burden in Iran, particularly due to P. vivax and, to a lesser extent, P. falciparum [19]. Several factors, including cross‑border transmission, high vector activity, and rural occupational exposure, may contribute to this burden [20, 21]. Although malaria-related mortality and severe complications such as splenic rupture are relatively uncommon, the continued transmission of malaria in Sistan and Baluchestan underscores the need to strengthen vector control, enhance surveillance, and ensure early diagnosis and prompt treatment.

Leishmaniasis remains an important vector-borne disease in Iran, with cutaneous leishmaniasis (CL), including both anthroponotic (ACL) and zoonotic (ZCL) forms, accounting for the vast majority of reported cases. Between 1983 and 2012, an average of 18,884 CL cases were reported annually, corresponding to an incidence of approximately 32 cases per 100,000 population, although the true burden is likely underestimated because of underreporting. In contrast, visceral leishmaniasis (VL) occurs less frequently but is associated with substantially greater morbidity and mortality. About PKDL, during 2001–2002, two confirmed cases of PKDL caused by Leishmania infantum were identified in the northwestern part of Iran [22].

The persistence of endemic foci is influenced by a combination of ecological conditions, reservoir hosts, vector distribution, human activities, and environmental changes [23]. Three cases of mortality attributed to ICD-10 code B55.0 were documented in the present study, involving patients aged 11 months, 11 years, and 72 years. Although the number of fatalities was small, these findings suggest that severe outcomes may occur in both pediatric and older populations. This observation is consistent with previous studies showing that young children and older adults are at increased risk of severe visceral leishmaniasis because of age-related differences in immune competence [24–27]. The only fatal case of cutaneous leishmaniasis was also observed in an older adult (72 years), further suggesting that advanced age may contribute to unfavorable outcomes in the rare instances of fatal CL.

Bites and stings by non-venomous arthropods

Although non-venomous arthropod bites and stings accounted for a smaller proportion of arthropod-related mortality compared with venomous exposures, they still represent a relevant public health concern in Iran. The occurrence of fatalities across 20 provinces indicates a widespread geographic distribution. This broad distribution suggests that fatal outcomes are influenced not only by arthropod presence but also by regional differences in exposure patterns [5, 28, 29]. However, these factors were beyond the scope of the present study, and further ecological, epidemiological, and socio-demographic research is required to clarify their potential roles in shaping these mortality patterns.

Injury, poisoning, and other consequences of external causes

Overall, the findings demonstrate a substantial geographic heterogeneity in mortality associated with arthropod-related injuries and envenomation in Iran. Scorpion stings represented the largest mortality burden among venomous arthropod-related causes, with the highest crude mortality rates observed in southern and southeastern provinces, particularly Sistan and Baluchestan and Hormozgan, highlighting these regions as priority areas for targeted prevention and control strategies. Fatalities from wasp, hornet, and honeybee stings, although accounting for a smaller number of deaths, appeared to exhibit distinct spatial patterns, with higher rates observed in Ardabil. This might suggest the influence of local ecological conditions, occupational exposures such as beekeeping, and outdoor activities that could increase human contact with these insects [29]. Deaths from spider bites in Iran have only been reported in Golestan province, likely due to greater human exposure in these areas, resulting from a wider distribution of spiders in the northern regions [30]. Although fatalities from centipede, millipede, ant, and caterpillar exposures were rare compared with scorpion stings, their occurrence across several provinces indicates that these lesser-known arthropods still contribute to the overall mortality burden highlight the need for increased awareness among healthcare providers and the public regarding the potential severity of these envenomations.

Conclusion

This study provides a national overview of arthropod-related mortality in Iran during 2016–2023, demonstrating substantial geographic and temporal variation. The reported Arthropod-Related Mortality, in descending order of frequency, was injury, poisoning, and certain other consequences of external causes (n=283); CCHF (n=63); bites or stings by non-venomous arthropods (n=41); malaria (n=17); unspecified arthropod-borne viral fever (n=11); and leishmaniasis (n=4). Scorpion-related mortality accounted for the largest burden among arthropod-related mortality, with higher mortality rates mainly observed in southern and southeastern provinces. These findings provide a clearer picture of the national burden and geographic distribution of arthropod-related mortality in Iran and may serve as a basis for future epidemiological and ecological investigations.
