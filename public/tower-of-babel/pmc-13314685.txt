Advanced liver dissection with left triangular ligament section for enhanced surgical access to right posterior lesions
Authors: Quentin Chenevas-Paule, Sarah Moenne-Loccoz, Giorgio Bianchi, Alexandre Lamercerie, Julio Abba, Mircea Chirica, Edouard Girard
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundLaparoscopic liver resection (LLR) has gained worldwide acceptance over the past decade, though its adoption remains incomplete due to technical challenges. These challenges, particularly in replicating traditional open surgery techniques, make controlling hemostasis and liver mobilization more difficult. The IWATE scoring system classifies the complexity of LLR, with expert-level procedures involving difficult-to-access segments. This study investigates whether extensive dissection around the inferior vena cava (IVC), including sectioning the left triangular ligament, improves liver rotation and access to right posterior lesions, potentially enhancing surgical exposure in LLR.MethodsDissections were performed at the Laboratory of Anatomy of the French Alps University. A formalin-preserved cadaveric model was developed to establish steps for liver dissection and liver rotation measurement, considering the inferior vena cava (IVC) as the vertical axis. Three key dissection steps were defined, focusing on the ligaments and the IVC. Liver rotation was measured using 30 newtons traction applied via the round ligament.Results30 dissections were conducted. The rotation angles, measured before and during the three surgical dissection steps, progressively increased with median values of 36°, 45°, 71°, and 87°. Angulation gains of 9° [6–13], 19° [17–31], and 13° [10–21] were observed between each surgical step, with a negative correlation found between liver weight and the angle of rotation during the later steps.ConclusionsExtensive dissection of the IVC, combined with sectioning of the left triangular ligament, is a useful surgical maneuver to increase liver rotation, providing better access to posterior liver segments during laparoscopy.

Introduction

Laparoscopic liver resection (LLR) has increased worldwide during the last decade [1–3] but with incomplete adoption [4] due to technical difficulties. The main surgical inconveniences of laparoscopic approach arise from the technical challenges of replicating traditional laparotomy maneuvers through laparoscopy. Key differences between open and laparoscopic approaches include hemostasis control [5] and liver mobilization [6].

The IWATE difficulty scoring system [7–9] classifies LLR into four difficulties levels, the expert level includes posterior sectionectomy involving either segment 7 or 8. This high level of expertise is related to the difficulty to access the posterolateral segments due to anatomical liver position and the need of extensive liver mobilization, which leads to limited access to posterior lesions.

Right liver mobilization has been standardized [10, 11]. The main concept involves dissecting the liver ligaments to enable progressive rotation around the IVC axis. To improve access to postero-superior lesions, additional dissection steps have been proposed [12]. Specifically, dissecting the retro-hepatic vena cava, along with sectioning the adrenal gland vein and the hepato-caval (Makuuchi) ligament, can enhance liver mobilization. However, these steps are technically challenging and could lead to significant bleeding. Additionally, we propose that complete liver liberation, including dissection of the left triangular ligament, could improve liver rotation around the IVC. To date, this additional step has not been described for exposure of right postero-superior lesions.

The aim of our study is to determine how much extensive dissection of the inferior vena cava (IVC), combined with the sectioning of the left triangular ligament, can improve liver rotation. We hypothesize that these additional and challenging steps can enhance the angle of liver rotation and be beneficial for laparoscopic surgical exposure of right posterior lesions.

Methods

Anatomical dissections were conducted at the Laboratory of Anatomy of the French Alps University (LADAF) in partnership with the Department of Digestive Surgery at Grenoble University Hospital. Initially, a cadaveric model was developed to establish the steps for liver dissection, methods for liver traction, and a reproducible measurement of the rotation angle. Subsequent dissections were performed following this model. The inferior vena cava (IVC) was considered as the vertical axis for liver rotation. All anatomical models were formalin-preserved cadavers, prepared using a standardized weight-based process.

Age at death, body mass index, medical and surgical history, liver weight, and the formalin preservation delay were recorded for each cadaver. Bodies with prior dissections or damage to the liver ligaments (round, falciform, coronary, triangular) were excluded from the study. The same operators performed all dissections using a sub-costal laparotomy approach.

Three steps of liver dissection were defined. The first step involved the division of the falciform ligament up to the confluence of the main hepatic veins. The second step focused on dissecting the anterior and posterior layers of the coronary ligament and the right triangular ligament. The third step included dissecting the right part of the retro-hepatic inferior vena cava (IVC), sectioning the right adrenal gland vein, and the hepato-caval ligament (Makuuchi ligament). During this final step, the left coronary ligament was also sectioned.

Before any dissection and traction, a superficial mark was placed on the liver surface in vertical alignment with the IVC axis to serve as the reference line for measuring the rotation angle. For each of the three steps of surgical mobilization, the right hemi-liver was lifted and pulled perpendicularly to the left side with a constant traction of thirty newtons. This traction was applied through the round ligament using a newton meter, ensuring a horizontal direction that allowed for centrifugal traction from the IVC to the left (Fig. 1).

For each traction, marks were placed on the liver surface in vertical alignment with the IVC axis. After the dissection, the liver was completely removed along with the IVC, and the angles between the superficial marks and the reference line were measured (Fig. 2).

To investigate the potential relationship between anatomical factors such as liver weight and Body Mass Index (BMI) and their influence on liver rotation, correlation matrices were constructed.

Statistical analyses were conducted using Prism GraphPad® software version 8.2.1. The correlation matrix employed the Pearson coefficient correlation (R), with statistical significance defined at p < 0.05.

All cadaver dissections adhered to funeral regulations governing donations, with no requirement for declaration to the CNIL (Commission Nationale de l’Informatique et des Libertés) data protection commission.

Results

Surgical dissections were conducted on 30 cadavers (16 females and 14 males) with a mean age of 85 years. None of the anatomical models had a history of liver surgery, while 6 had undergone previous cholecystectomy. The median post-mortem delay was 36 days.

In Fig. 3, absolute values of the rotation angle were depicted both before dissection and during the three defined surgical steps, with a traction force of thirty newtons. Even without surgical dissection, the median rotation angle after traction was 36°. The median values of the rotation angles were 45°, 71°, and 87° respectively, for each step of surgical dissection. The box plot represents the interquartile range, while the whiskers indicate the 10th and 90th percentiles.

Figure 4, illustrates the angulation gain between the surgical steps of liver dissection. The steps 0 represents the angulation gain of 37° [20–49] between the liver in anatomical position and with a thirty newtons traction without any dissection. The angulation gains are 9° [6–13], 19° [17–31], 13° [10–21] respectively between two consecutive surgical steps. On the box plot, the angulation gains after IVC dissection associated with the left triangular ligament section can reach 30°. One third of the values are between 20 and 30°.

Figure 5, illustrates a negative correlation between the angle of rotation and liver weight. This correlation is statistically significant for the second and third steps of dissection. However, there is no statistically significant correlation between the angle of liver rotation and BMI, although a trend is observable in the later steps of dissection. As anticipated, liver weight and BMI are positively correlated, with an R value of 0.470 and p = 0.010.

Discussion

The main concept presented in our study is to use extensive IVC dissection associated with sectioning of the left triangular ligament to better expose segments 7 and 8. This concept is related to basic anatomical principles. The rotation of the liver centered on the IVC axis is limited by liver ligamentous attachments. The common surgical exposure of segments 7 and 8 relies on the dissection of the right and falciform ligaments.

In this study, we demonstrate that extensive dissection of the inferior vena cava (IVC) combined with the sectioning of the left triangular ligament can significantly enhance liver rotation around the IVC axis, thereby improving surgical exposure. This concept is even more relevant for lightweight livers, with an angulation gain that could reach 20° after the last step of dissection. It is also interesting to note that the angulation gains during the right triangular ligament dissection (19° [17–31]) is close to the angle obtained during an extensive dissection associated with left triangular ligament Sect. (13° [10–21]). During challenging LLR, achieving a 10° to 20° difference in angulation when aligning the surgical tools with the parenchymal plane may prevent the need for conversion to laparotomy. After an extensive dissection, the median absolute value of rotation is 87°, which is nearly 90°. This indicates that it is possible to perform a liver transection in a plane that was initially perpendicular to the optical port plane, before any liver mobilization. This knowledge of angle rotation values can also be useful for planning, on preoperative CT scans, the degree of liver dissection required for right posterior lesions.

Our study has several limitations. First, the use of formalin-preserved cadaveric models is associated with reduced tissue laxity, suggesting that the degree of liver rotation observed in this setting may underestimate what can be achieved during live surgery. In addition, the model was configured using an open approach, as the altered mechanical properties of formalin-preserved specimens do not allow for the establishment and maintenance of a stable pneumoperitoneum, thereby limiting the feasibility of a reproducible laparoscopic setup.

Second, all specimens were non-cirrhotic, which may limit the generalizability of our findings, as fibrosis and decreased tissue compliance in cirrhotic livers can restrict mobilization and reduce liver rotation.

Finally, the relatively small number of models may be considered a limitation. However, except for baseline measurements without dissection, the rotation angle values showed limited variability, supporting the consistency of our findings.

We chose to use the round ligament for traction. The gallbladder may have been removed in previous surgeries, and the pulling force is more limited. With a thirty-newton traction, no liver decapsulation was observed when using the round ligament. This traction force can be applied during a laparoscopic approach with a limited risk of liver damage. However, livers with steatosis may limit the force that can be applied during mobilization due to an increased risk of parenchymal injury. In actual surgical procedures, traction on the round ligament is performed using fenestrated forceps placed in the epigastric port.

Fiorentini et al [13] present various abdominal liver mobilization options. The ‘up-to-down’ dissection method they describe is similar to our concept of complete liver dissection, excluding the explicit sectioning of the left triangular ligament. To our knowledge, our work is the first to provide a precise and quantitative description of the rotation angle achieved during surgical liver mobilization, offering objective data that may help anticipate the degree of rotation achievable preoperatively and better guide intraoperative strategy. The dissection of the left triangular ligament is not commonly described as an additional surgical step for the exposure of right posterior lesions. Since it is easy and safe to perform laparoscopically, we can reasonably assume that no comorbidities would be associated with this procedure. No comorbidities related to left triangular ligament is commonly reported in the literature during LLR.

Conclusion

Extensive dissection of the IVC, combined with sectioning of the left triangular ligament, is a useful surgical maneuver to increase liver rotation, providing better access to posterior liver segments during laparoscopy.
