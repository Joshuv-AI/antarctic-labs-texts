Let
n\in\mathds{N}
and let
K\subseteq\mathds{R}^{n}
be closed.
Real algebraic geometry studies the cone

\mathrm{Pos}(K):=\big\{f\in\mathds{R}[x_{1},\dots,x_{n}]\,\big|\,f(x)\geq 0\ \text{for all}\ x\in K\big\}

of polynomials which are non-negative on

.
There is an enormous amount of literature on this still active field.

It is therefore very surprising that linear operators

T:\mathds{R}[x_{1},\dots,x_{n}]\to\mathds{R}[x_{1},\dots,x_{n}]

are hardly studied.
In fact, very little was know so far about these operators, especially when they have to preserve
K
-positivity, i.e.,

T\mathrm{Pos}(K)\subseteq\mathrm{Pos}(K).

The aim of this lecture is to present the recent developments in this field, i.e., the progress made in the works
dD 24

dDS 25

dDL 25

dD 25

The material presented here is for one semester of two weekly 1.5 hours lectures including a weekly exercise session of approximately 45 minutes.

The course is for master students in mathematics.
Besides a bachelor in mathematics (i.e., especially basic knowledge in analysis and linear algebra), it is recommended that the students resp. the reader has a basic knowledge in (real) algebraic geometry (i.e., non-negative polynomials), (partial) differential equations, Lie groups and algebras, and operator theory.

Moments and Moment Functionals

Our main aim is to study linear operators

Restrictions of the Support of Representing Measures

For a function
f:\mathcal{X}\to\mathds{R}
on any set
\mathcal{X}

\mathcal{Z}(f):=\{x\in\mathcal{X}\,|\,f(x)=0\}=f^{-1}(\{0\})

Theorem 1.7
.

Let
(\mathcal{X},\mathcal{A})
be a measurable space, let
\mathcal{V}
be a real vector space of measurable functions
f:\mathcal{X}\to\mathds{R}
, and let

L:\mathcal{V}\to\mathds{R}

be a moment functional.
If there exists a function
g\in\mathcal{V}
with

g\geq 0\quad\text{and}\quad L(g)=0,

then

\mu\big(\mathcal{X}\setminus\mathcal{Z}(g)\big)=0

for every representing measure
\mu
of
L

\mu
of
L
is supported on
\mathcal{Z}(g)
.

Proof.

Define

A_{0}:=\{x\in\mathcal{X}\,|\,g(x)\geq 1\}=g^{-1}([1,\infty))

and

A_{n}:=\left\{x\in\mathcal{X}\,\middle|\,n^{-1}>g(x)\geq(n+1)^{-1}\right\}=g^{-1}([(n+1)^{-1},n^{-1}))

for all
n\in\mathds{N}
.
Since
g
is measurable and
[1,\infty)
and
[(n+1)^{-1},n^{-1})
are Borel sets for all
n\in\mathds{N}
,
A_{n}\in\mathcal{A}
for all
n\in\mathds{N}_{0}
.
Then

0\leq\mu(A_{n})=\int_{\mathcal{X}}\chi_{A_{n}}(x)\penalty\ \mathrm{d}\mu(x)\leq(n+1)\int_{A_{n}}g(x)\penalty\ \mathrm{d}\mu(x)\leq(n+1)\cdot L(g)=0

for all
n\in\mathds{N}_{0}

\mu(A_{n})=0
for all
n\in\mathds{N}_{0}

Hence,

\mu\big(\mathcal{X}\setminus\mathcal{Z}(g)\big)=\mu\left(\bigcup_{n\in\mathds{N}_{0}}A_{n}\right)=\sum_{n\in\mathds{N}_{0}}\mu(A_{n})=0.\qed

4
Richter’s Theorem

Moment functionals and measures can be quite difficult to describe.
However, truncated moment functionals always have very simple representing measures as the next result shows.

Richter’s Theorem 1.13

(
(
Ric 57
, Satz 4)
)
.

Let
(\mathcal{X},\mathcal{A})
be a measurable space, let
\mathcal{V}
be a finite-dimensional real vector space of measurable functions
f:\mathcal{X}\to\mathds{R}
, and let

L:\mathcal{V}\to\mathds{R}

be a moment functional.
Then
L
has a finitely atomic representing measure

\mu=\sum_{i=0}^{k}c_{i}\cdot\delta_{x_{i}}

with at most
k\leq\dim\mathcal{V}
atoms, i.e., there exist constants
c_{1},\dots,c_{k}>0
and pairwise different points
x_{1},\dots,x_{k}\in\mathcal{X}
such that

L(f)=\sum_{i=1}^{k}c_{i}\cdot f(x_{i})

for all
f\in\mathcal{V}
.

Proof.

We proceed via induction over
n:=\dim\mathcal{V}
.

n=1
:

If
L(f)=0
for all
f\in\mathcal{V}
, then
\mu=0
is a representing measure of
L
.
Hence, assume without loss of generality that
L(f)\neq 0
for a
f\in\mathcal{V}\setminus\{0\}
.
Fix
f\in\mathcal{V}\setminus\{0\}
.
Since
L
is a moment functional, there exists a point
x\in\mathcal{X}
such that

f(x)\neq 0\quad\text{and}\quad\mathrm{sgn}L(f)=\mathrm{sgn}f(x).

Then

L(g)=g(x)\cdot\frac{L(f)}{f(x)}=\int_{\mathcal{X}}g(y)\penalty\ \mathrm{d}\mu(y)\qquad\text{with}\qquad\mu=\frac{L(f)}{f(x)}\cdot\delta_{x}

for any
g\in\mathcal{V}
, since
\mathcal{V}
is
1
-dimensional and hence
g=c\cdot f
with
c=\frac{g(x)}{f(x)}
.

n\to n+1
:

Assume the theorem holds for all
\dim\mathcal{V}=1,\dots,n
.
We need to prove it for
\dim\mathcal{V}=n+1
.
Set

\tilde{\mathcal{L}}:=\mathrm{conv}\,\mathrm{cone}\,\{l_{x}\,|\,x\in\mathcal{X}\}.

Then

\tilde{\mathcal{L}}\subseteq\mathcal{L}.

Since
\mathcal{V}
is finite-dimensional,
\tilde{\mathcal{L}}
and
\mathcal{L}
are finite-dimensional and have non-empty interior with

\mathrm{int}\,\tilde{\mathcal{L}}\subseteq\mathrm{int}\,\mathcal{L}.

Assume

\mathrm{int}\,\tilde{\mathcal{L}}\neq\mathrm{int}\,\mathcal{L}

and let

L\in\mathrm{int}\,(\mathrm{int}\,\mathcal{L}\setminus\tilde{\mathcal{L}})=\mathrm{int}\,\mathcal{L}\setminus\overline{\tilde{\mathcal{L}}}.

Then there exists a
f\in\mathcal{V}\cong\mathcal{V}^{**}
such that

f(x)=l_{x}(f)>0\qquad\text{for all}\ x\in\mathcal{X}

and

L(f)<0.

(3)

Since
L\in\mathrm{int}\,\mathcal{L}\subseteq\mathcal{L}
is a moment functional and
f\in(\mathcal{V}_{+})
,

L(f)\geq 0.

This is a contradiction to (
3
), i.e., we proved
\mathrm{int}\,\tilde{\mathcal{L}}=\mathrm{int}\,\mathcal{L}
.

Now let

L\in\partial\mathcal{L}\cap\mathcal{L}

be a moment functional on the boundary of the truncated moment cone.
Then there exists a

f\in\mathcal{V}_{+}\subseteq\mathcal{V}\cong\mathcal{V}^{**}

such that

L(f)=0.

By
1.7
, every representing measure
\mu
of
L
is supported on
\mathcal{Y}:=\mathcal{Z}(f)
.
Let
\mathcal{W}
be a
n
-dimensional subspace of
\mathcal{V}
such that

\mathcal{V}=\mathcal{W}+\mathds{R}\cdot f,

i.e.,
L
lives only on
\mathcal{W}|_{\mathcal{Y}}
since
L(f)=0
.
Hence,
\dim\mathcal{W}|_{\mathcal{Y}}=n
and the theorem holds by the induction hypothesis.
∎

To work with linear operators on polynomials we give at first representations of them.

Theorem 3.1

(folklore,
canonical representation
)
.

Let
n\in\mathds{N}
and let

T:\mathds{R}[x_{1},\dots,x_{n}]\to\mathds{R}[x_{1},\dots,x_{n}]

be linear.
Then, for all
\alpha\in\mathds{N}_{0}^{n}
, there exist unique polynomials
q_{\alpha}\in\mathds{R}[x_{1},\dots,x_{n}]
such that

T=\sum_{\alpha\in\mathds{N}_{0}^{n}}q_{\alpha}\cdot\partial^{\alpha}.

(14)

The representation (
14
) is called
canonical representation
of
T
.

Proof.

We proceed by induction over
d=|\alpha|\in\mathds{N}_{0}
.

d=0
:

Set

q_{0}:=T1,

i.e.,
q_{0}
is unique and
T_{0}:=q_{0}
represents
T
on
\mathds{R}[x_{1},\dots,x_{n}]_{\leq 0}=\mathds{R}
.

d\to d+1
:

Assume, for
d\in\mathds{N}_{0}
, we have unique
q_{\alpha}\in\mathds{R}[x_{1},\dots,x_{n}]
for all
\alpha\in\mathds{N}_{0}^{n}
with
|\alpha|\leq d
such that

T_{d}:=\sum_{\alpha\in\mathds{N}_{0}^{n}:|\alpha|\leq d}q_{\alpha}\cdot\partial^{\alpha}

represents
T
on
\mathds{R}[x_{1},\dots,x_{n}]_{\leq d}
.
Let
\beta\in\mathds{N}_{0}^{n}
with
|\beta|=d+1
.
Then

Tx^{\beta}=\sum_{\alpha\in\mathds{N}_{0}^{n}:|\alpha|\leq d}q_{\alpha}\cdot\partial^{\alpha}x^{\beta}+q_{\beta}\cdot\partial^{\beta}x^{\beta}

implies

q_{\beta}:=\frac{1}{\beta!}\cdot\left(Tx^{\beta}-\sum_{\alpha\in\mathds{N}_{0}^{n}:|\alpha|\leq d}q_{\alpha}\cdot\partial^{\alpha}x^{\beta}\right),

i.e.,
q_{\beta}
is unique and

T_{d+1}:=\sum_{\alpha\in\mathds{N}_{0}^{n}:|\alpha|\leq d+1}q_{\alpha}\cdot\partial^{\alpha}

represents
T
on
\mathds{R}[x_{1},\dots,x_{n}]_{\leq d+1}
with unique
q_{\alpha}
for all
\alpha\in\mathds{N}_{0}^{n}
with
|\alpha|\leq d+1
.

In summary, (

) represents

on all
\mathds{R}[x_{1},\dots,x_{n}]
with unique

for all
\alpha\in\mathds{N}_{0}^{n}
.

Then

(

9
Representation of Diagonal Operators on
\mathds{R}[x_{1},\dots,x_{n}]

A special class of linear operators on
\mathds{R}[x_{1},\dots,x_{n}]
are diagonal operators.

Definition 3.4
.

Let
n\in\mathds{N}
.
A linear operator

T:\mathds{R}[x_{1},\dots,x_{n}]\to\mathds{R}[x_{1},\dots,x_{n}]

is called
diagonal
, if

Tx^{\alpha}=t_{\alpha}x^{\alpha}\quad\text{with}\quad t_{\alpha}\in\mathds{R}

for all
\alpha\in\mathds{N}_{0}^{n}
.
The sequence
t=(t_{\alpha})_{\alpha\in\mathds{N}_{0}^{n}}
is called
diagonal sequence
of
T
.

Clearly, a diagonal operator is uniquely determined by its diagonal sequences and every real sequence
t=(t_{\alpha})_{\alpha\in\mathds{N}_{0}^{n}}
gives a diagonal operator.

Theorem 3.5

(
(
dDL 25
, Rem. 4.2)
)
.

Let
n\in\mathds{N}
and let

T:\mathds{R}[x_{1},\dots,x_{n}]\to\mathds{R}[x_{1},\dots,x_{n}]

be a linear operator.
Then the following are equivalent:

(i)

T
is a diagonal operator, i.e.,
Tx^{\alpha}=t_{\alpha}x^{\alpha}
with unique
t_{\alpha}\in\mathds{R}
for all
\alpha\in\mathds{N}_{0}^{n}
.

(ii)

The operator
T
is of the form

T=\sum_{\alpha\in\mathds{N}_{0}^{n}}\frac{c_{\alpha}}{\alpha!}\cdot x^{\alpha}\cdot\partial^{\alpha}

(16)

for a unique real sequence
c=(c_{\alpha})_{\alpha\in\mathds{N}_{0}^{n}}
.

If one of the equivalent statements (i) or (ii) holds, then the diagonal sequence
t=(t_{\alpha})_{\alpha\in\mathds{N}_{0}^{n}}
and the sequence
c=(c_{\alpha})_{\alpha\in\mathds{N}_{0}^{n}}
of coefficients
c_{\alpha}
fulfill the relations

t_{\alpha}=\sum_{\beta\in\mathds{N}_{0}^{n}:\ \beta\preceq\alpha}\binom{\alpha}{\beta}\cdot c_{\beta}\qquad\text{and}\qquad c_{\alpha}=\sum_{\beta\in\mathds{N}_{0}^{n}:\ \beta\preceq\alpha}(-1)^{|\alpha-\beta|}\cdot\binom{\alpha}{\beta}\cdot t_{\beta}

(17)

for all
\alpha\in\mathds{N}_{0}^{n}
.

Proof.

(i)
\Rightarrow
(ii):

At first we prove (
16
) by induction over
d=|\alpha|
.

d=0
:
Set

c_{0}:=T1=t_{0}.

Then
T
is represented by
T_{0}:=c_{0}=t_{0}
on
\mathds{R}[x_{1},\dots,x_{n}]_{\leq 0}=\mathds{R}
.

d\to d+1
:
Assume for all
\alpha\in\mathds{N}_{0}^{n}
with
|\alpha|\leq d
there are unique
c_{\alpha}
such that
T
is represented by

\sum_{\alpha\in\mathds{N}_{0}^{n}:|\alpha|\leq d}\frac{c_{\alpha}}{\alpha!}\cdot x^{\alpha}\cdot\partial^{\alpha}

on
\mathds{R}[x_{1},\dots,x_{n}]_{\leq d}
.
Let
\beta\in\mathds{N}_{0}^{n}
with
|\beta|=d+1
.
Then

t_{\beta}x^{\beta}=Tx^{\beta}=\sum_{\alpha\in\mathds{N}_{0}^{n}:|\alpha|\leq d}\frac{c_{\alpha}}{\alpha!}\cdot x^{\alpha}\cdot\partial^{\alpha}x^{\beta}+\frac{c_{\beta}}{\beta!}\cdot x^{\beta}\cdot\partial^{\beta}x^{\beta}

implies

c_{\beta}x^{\beta}=Tx^{\beta}-\sum_{\alpha\in\mathds{N}_{0}^{n}:|\alpha|\leq d}\frac{c_{\alpha}}{\alpha!}\cdot x^{\alpha}\cdot\partial^{\alpha}x^{\beta}

with unique
c_{\beta}\in\mathds{R}
.

In summary, (
16
) represents
T
on
\mathds{R}[x_{1},\dots,x_{n}]
.

(ii)
\Rightarrow
(i):

By (
16
),

Tx^{\alpha}=\sum_{\beta\in\mathds{N}_{0}^{n}}\frac{c_{\beta}}{\beta!}\cdot x^{\beta}\cdot\partial^{\beta}x^{\alpha}=\sum_{\beta\in\mathds{N}_{0}^{n}:\beta\preceq\alpha}\binom{\alpha}{\beta}\cdot x^{\alpha}=t_{\alpha}x^{\alpha}

with
t_{\alpha}\in\mathds{R}
, i.e., we proved (i).

We already proved the first equation of (
17
) in the step “(ii)
\Rightarrow
(i)”.
The second equation in (
17
) is given as Problem
3
.
∎

The Fréchet Space
\mathds{C}^{\mathds{N}_{0}^{n}}

Definition 4.1
.

A
Fréchet space
is a topological vector space which is

(i)

metrizable (in particular, it is Hausdorff),

(ii)

complete, and

(iii)

locally convex.

Example 4.2

(
(
Trè 67
, p. 91, Exm. III)
)
.

Let
n\in\mathds{N}
.
The space

\mathds{C}^{\mathds{N}_{0}^{n}}:=\big\{s=(s_{\alpha})_{\alpha\in\mathds{N}_{0}^{n}}\,\big|\,s_{\alpha}\in\mathds{C}\ \text{for all}\ \alpha\in\mathds{N}_{0}^{n}\big\}

of all complex sequences indexed by
\mathds{N}_{0}^{n}
equipped with the semi-norms

\big|(s_{\alpha})_{\alpha\in\mathds{N}_{0}^{n}}\big|_{d}:=\sup_{\alpha\in\mathds{N}_{0}^{n}:|\alpha|\leq d}|s_{\alpha}|

for all
d\in\mathds{N}_{0}
is a Fréchet space.
To see this, let

\iota:\mathds{N}_{0}^{n}\to\mathds{N}_{0}

be a bijection.
Then a metric is given by

m(s,t):=\sum_{\alpha\in\mathds{N}_{0}^{n}}\frac{1}{2^{\iota(\alpha)}}\cdot\frac{|s_{\alpha}-t_{\alpha}|}{1+|s_{\alpha}-t_{\alpha}|}

for any
s=(s_{\alpha})_{\alpha\in\mathds{N}_{0}^{n}}
and
t=(t_{\alpha})_{\alpha\in\mathds{N}_{0}^{n}}\in\mathds{C}^{\mathds{N}_{0}^{n}}
.
To understand the topology of
\mathds{C}^{\mathds{N}_{0}^{n}}
it is sufficient to understand the convergence

s^{(k)}\xrightarrow{k\to\infty}s

for sequences

s=(s_{\alpha})_{\alpha\in\mathds{N}_{0}^{n}},\ s^{(k)}=(s_{k,\alpha})_{\alpha\in\mathds{N}_{0}^{n}}\in\mathds{C}^{\mathds{N}_{0}^{n}}.

From the semi-norms
|\,\cdot\,|_{d}
we find

\displaystyle s^{(k)}\xrightarrow{k\to\infty}s\quad

\displaystyle\Leftrightarrow\quad\big|s-s^{(k)}\big|_{d}\xrightarrow{k\to\infty}0\ \text{for all}\ d\in\mathds{N}_{0}

\displaystyle\Leftrightarrow\quad s_{k,\alpha}\xrightarrow{k\to\infty}s_{\alpha}\ \text{for all}\ \alpha\in\mathds{N}_{0}^{n},

i.e., the topology of
\mathds{C}^{\mathds{N}_{0}^{n}}
is generated by the coordinate-wise convergence.

\circ

Remark 4.3
.

Let
n\in\mathds{N}
. Then

\mathds{C}^{\mathds{N}_{0}^{n}}\cong\mathds{C}[[x_{1},\dots,x_{n}]].

Example 4.4
.

Every Banach space is a Fréchet space.

\circ

Remark 4.5
.

\mathds{C}^{\mathds{N}_{0}^{n}}
is a Fréchet space that is not a Banach space.

\circ

Lemma 4.6

(
(
dD 24
, Cor. 3.7 (i))
)
.

Let
n\in\mathds{N}
and let
K\subseteq\mathds{R}^{n}
be closed.
Then the cone

\mathcal{S}(\mathds{R}[x_{1},\dots,x_{n}]:K\to\mathds{R},\{x^{\alpha}\}_{\alpha\in\mathds{N}_{0}^{n}})

of
K
-moment sequences is closed in the Fréchet topology of
\mathds{C}^{\mathds{N}_{0}^{n}}
.

Proof.

Let
s^{(k)},s\in\mathcal{S}
with
s^{(k)}\xrightarrow{k\to\infty}s
in
\mathds{C}^{\mathds{N}_{0}^{n}}
.
Then

\underbrace{L_{s^{(k)}}(p)}_{\geq 0}\quad\xrightarrow{k\to\infty}\quad L_{s}(p)\geq 0

for all
p\in\mathrm{Pos}(K)
, since
\deg p<\infty
.
Hence, by
2.11
,
s\in\mathcal{S}
.
∎

The LF-Space
\mathds{C}[x_{1},\dots,x_{n}]

Definition 4.7
.

Let
E
be a complex vector space such that

E=\bigcup_{n\in\mathds{N}}E_{n}

with Fréchet spaces
E_{n}
for all
n\in\mathds{N}
and the natural injections
E_{n}\hookrightarrow E_{n+1}
, i.e.,
E_{n}\subseteq E_{n+1}
, is an isomorphism.
If
E
is equipped with the topology such that a convex
V
is a neighborhood of zero in
E
if and only if
V\cap E_{n}
is a neighborhood of zero in
E_{n}
for all
n\in\mathds{N}
, then
E
is called a
LF-space
.
The set
\{E_{n}\}_{n\in\mathds{N}}
is called a
defining sequence
of
E
.

The LF stands for
limit of Fréchet spaces
.

Example 4.8
.

Let
n\in\mathds{N}
and set

E_{d}:=\mathds{C}[x_{1},\dots,x_{n}]_{\leq d}

for
d\in\mathds{N}_{0}
.
Since
E_{d}
are finite dimensional, the
E_{d}
are Fréchet spaces.
Since

\mathds{C}[x_{1},\dots,x_{n}]=\bigcup_{d\in\mathds{N}_{0}}E_{d},

we can equip
\mathds{C}[x_{1},\dots,x_{n}]
with the topology by
4.7
.
Then
\mathds{C}[x_{1},\dots,x_{n}]
is a LF-space.

\circ

We will see a simpler version how to understand the topology of
\mathds{C}[x_{1},\dots,x_{n}]
.

Proposition 4.9

(
(
Trè 67
, Prop. 13.1)
)
.

Let
E
be an LF-space, let
\{E_{n}\}_{n\in\mathds{N}}
be a defining sequence of
E
, let
F
be an arbitrary locally convex topological vector space, and let

l:E\to F

be linear.
Then the following are equivalent:

(i)

l:E\to F
is continuous.

(ii)

For all
n\in\mathds{N}
,

l|_{E_{n}}:E_{n}\to F

is continuous.

Example 4.10
.

Let
s\in\mathds{C}^{\mathds{N}_{0}^{n}}
.
Then the Riesz functional

L_{s}:\mathds{C}[x_{1},\dots,x_{n}]\to\mathds{C}

is continuous, since

E_{d}=\mathds{C}[x_{1},\dots,x_{n}]_{\leq d}

are finite dimensional and every linear functional on a finite dimensional space are continuous.

On the other side, every linear functional

l:\mathds{C}[x_{1},\dots,x_{n}]\to\mathds{C}

is uniquely determined by

s_{\alpha}:=l(x^{\alpha})

for all
\alpha\in\mathds{N}_{0}^{n}
, i.e.,

L_{\cdot}:\mathds{C}^{\mathds{N}_{0}^{n}}\to\big(\mathds{C}[x_{1},\dots,x_{n}]\big)^{*},\quad s\mapsto L_{s}

is an isomorphism where
E^{*}
is the dual of
E
, i.e., the set of all continuous linear functionals on a topological vector space
E
.
Hence, the Riesz functional
L_{s}
is the dual pairing map

\langle\,\cdot\,,\,\cdot\,\rangle:E^{*}\times E,\quad(s,p)\mapsto\langle s,p\rangle:=L_{s}(p)

between

E^{*}=\mathds{C}^{\mathds{N}_{0}^{n}}\qquad\text{and}\qquad E=\mathds{C}[x_{1},\dots,x_{n}].

Theorem 4.11

(
(
Trè 67
, Thm. 13.1)
)
.

Any LF-space is complete.

In the following continuation of
4.8
we give a simpler interpretation of the topology of the LF-space
\mathds{C}[x_{1},\dots,x_{n}]
.

Example 4.12

(
4.8
continued)
.

Let
n\in\mathds{N}
and let

p=\sum_{\alpha\in\mathds{N}_{0}^{n}}p_{\alpha}\cdot x^{\alpha},\quad p_{k}=\sum_{\alpha\in\mathds{N}_{0}^{n}}p_{k,\alpha}\cdot x^{\alpha}\quad\in\mathds{C}[x_{1},\dots,x_{n}]

for all
k\in\mathds{N}
.
Then

p_{k}\xrightarrow{k\to\infty}p\quad\text{in}\ \mathds{C}[x_{1},\dots,x_{n}]

if and only if

\langle s,p_{k}\rangle\xrightarrow{k\to\infty}\langle s,p\rangle\quad\text{for all}\ s\in\mathds{C}^{\mathds{N}_{0}^{n}}

(18)

by
4.10
.
For

\delta_{\alpha}:=(\delta_{\alpha,\beta})_{\beta\in\mathds{N}_{0}^{n}}\in\mathds{C}^{\mathds{N}_{0}^{n}}

with
\alpha\in\mathds{N}_{0}^{n}
, (
18
) implies

p_{k,\alpha}=\langle\delta_{\alpha},p_{k}\rangle\xrightarrow{k\to\infty}\langle\delta_{\alpha},p\rangle=p_{\alpha}

for all
\alpha\in\mathds{N}_{0}^{n}
, i.e., we have in
\mathds{C}[x_{1},\dots,x_{n}]
coordinate-wise convergence.

We now prove that (
18
) also implies

\sup_{k\in\mathds{N}}\deg p_{k}<\infty.

To prove this assume

\deg p_{k}\xrightarrow{k\to\infty}\infty,

i.e., w.l.o.g.
\deg p_{k}=k
.
For each
k\in\mathds{N}
, let
\alpha_{k}\in\mathds{N}_{0}^{n}
be such that

p_{k,\alpha}=\langle p_{k},\delta_{\alpha_{k}}\rangle\neq 0.

Hence, there exists a
s\in\mathds{C}^{\mathds{N}_{0}^{n}}
such that

\langle s,p_{k}\rangle=k\to\infty.

But this contradicts (
18
), i.e., we have that

p_{k}\xrightarrow{k\to\infty}p\quad\text{in}\ \mathds{C}[x_{1},\dots,x_{n}]

if and only if

\sup_{k\in\mathds{N}}\deg p_{k}\leq D<\infty\quad\text{and}\quad p_{k}\to p\ \text{in}\ \mathds{C}[x_{1},\dots,x_{n}]_{\leq D}.

In summary, we proved that to understand the LF-topology of
\mathds{C}[x_{1},\dots,x_{n}]
it is sufficient to understand the convergence in it.
And similar to
\mathds{C}^{\mathds{N}_{0}^{n}}
, in
\mathds{C}[x_{1},\dots,x_{n}]
we have the coordinate-wise convergence with the additional uniform degree boundedness of the
p_{k}
.

\circ

Lemma 4.13
.

Let
n\in\mathds{N}
and let
K\subseteq\mathds{R}^{n}
be closed.
Then

\mathrm{Pos}(K)

is closed in the LF-topology of
\mathds{C}[x_{1},\dots,x_{n}]
.

Proof.

Let
p_{k},p\in\mathds{R}[x_{1},\dots,x_{n}]
with

p_{k}\quad\xrightarrow{k\to\infty}\quad p\quad\text{in}\ \mathds{C}[x_{1},\dots,x_{n}].

Then

\sup_{k\in\mathds{N}}\deg p_{k}\leq D<\infty

for some
D\in\mathds{N}
,

p_{k}\to p\quad\text{in}\ \mathds{C}[x_{1},\dots,x_{n}],

and hence

\underbrace{p_{k}(x)}_{\geq 0}\quad\xrightarrow{k\to\infty}\quad p(x)\geq 0

for all
x\in K
, i.e.,
p\in\mathrm{Pos}(K)
.
∎

Lemma 5.4

(
(
dDS 25
, Lem. 3.3)
)
.

Let
q_{\alpha}\in\mathds{R}[x_{1},\dots,x_{n}]
for all
\alpha\in\mathds{N}_{0}^{n}

be closed and non-empty.
If

K
-positivity preserver, then

(\alpha!\cdot q_{\alpha}(y))_{\alpha\in\mathds{N}_{0}^{n}}

(K-y)
-moment sequence for all

Proof.

Let
y\in K
and define

L_{y}:\mathds{R}[x_{1},\dots,x_{n}]\to\mathds{R},\quad f\mapsto L_{y}(f):=(T_{y}f)(y).

Since

is a
K
-positivity preserver,

L_{y}(f)=(T_{y}f)(y)\geq 0

for all

Hence,

is a
K
-moment functional by
2.11
, i.e., there exists a (Radon) measure
\mu_{y}
with

\sum_{\alpha\in\mathds{N}_{0}^{n}}q_{\alpha}(y)\cdot f^{(\alpha)}(y)=(T_{y}f)(y)=L_{y}(f)=\int_{K}f(x)\penalty\ \mathrm{d}\mu_{y}(x)

for all

.
Using the Taylor formula

f(x)=\sum_{\alpha\in\mathds{N}_{0}^{n}}\frac{f^{(\alpha)}(y)}{\alpha!}\cdot(x-y)^{\alpha}

we obtain

\sum_{\alpha\in\mathds{N}_{0}^{n}}q_{\alpha}(y)\cdot f^{(\alpha)}(y)=\sum_{\alpha\in\mathds{N}_{0}^{n}}\frac{f^{(\alpha)}(y)}{\alpha!}\cdot\int(x-y)^{\alpha}\penalty\ \mathrm{d}\mu_{y}(x)

(20)

for all
f\in\mathds{R}[x_{1},\dots,x_{n}]
.
For
\beta\in\mathds{N}_{0}^{n}

f_{\beta}(x):=(x-y)^{\beta}.

Then

f^{(\alpha)}_{\beta}(y)=\beta!\cdot\delta_{\alpha,\beta}.

Hence, by
Equation

20

\beta!\cdot q_{\beta}(y)=\int_{K}(x-y)^{\beta}\penalty\ \mathrm{d}\mu_{y}(x)=\int_{K-y}z^{\beta}\penalty\ \mathrm{d}\mu_{y}(z+y),

i.e.,

(\alpha!\cdot q_{\alpha}(y))_{\alpha\in\mathds{N}_{0}^{n}}

is a
(K-y)
-moment sequence with representing measure
\nu_{y}(\,\cdot\,):=\mu_{y}(\,\cdot\,+y)

∎

Definition 6.1
.

Let
n\in\mathds{N}
.
A
Lie group

G\times G\to G,\quad(\sigma,\tau)\mapsto\sigma\tau^{-1}

.

Examples 6.2
.

Let
n\in\mathds{N}
.

(a)

The Euclidean space
\mathds{R}^{n}
with the vector addition is a Lie group.

(b)

\mathds{C}^{*}:=\mathds{C}\setminus\{0\}
with the multiplication is a Lie group.

(c)

The product
G\times H
of two Lie groups
G
and
H
is itself are Lie group with the product manifold structure and the direct product group structure:

(\sigma_{1},\tau_{1})\cdot(\sigma_{2},\tau_{2})=(\sigma_{1}\sigma_{2},\tau_{1}\tau_{2}).

(d)

The manifold
\mathrm{Gl}(n,\mathds{R})
of all non-singular
n\times n
-matrices with real entries are a Lie group under matrix multiplication.

\circ

Definition 6.3
.

A Lie algebra
\mathfrak{g}
over
\mathds{R}
is a real vector space
\mathfrak{g}
together with a bilinear operator

[\,\cdot\,,\,\cdot\,]:\mathfrak{g}\times\mathfrak{g}\to\mathfrak{g},

called the

(i)

and

(ii)

[[x,y],z]+[[y,z],x]+[[z,x],y]=0

for all

.

Remark 6.4
.

We see that Lie groups are finite dimensional.
However, our Lie algebra definition covers also the infinite dimensional cases.

\circ

Examples 6.5
.

Let

.

(a)

Any (finite or infinite dimensional) vector space becomes a Lie algebra if all brackets are set to zero.

(b)

The vector space

of all

-matrices form a Lie algebra, if we set

for all

.

(c)

A

-dimensional vector space with basis

,

becomes a Lie algebra if we set

[x,x]=[y,y]=0\qquad\text{and}\qquad[x,y]=y

and extend it bilinearly with

.

(d)

The vector space
\mathds{R}^{3}
with the bilinear operation

\circ

(see e.g.

Matrix Lie Groups and the Matrix Exponential Function

While abstract Lie groups come in every color, the simpler examples are matrix Lie groups.
These are even sufficient for our study.

Definition 6.11
.

Let
n\in\mathds{N}
.
We define the
matrix exponential function

\exp:\mathds{R}^{n\times n}\to\mathds{R}^{n\times n},\quad A\mapsto\exp A:=\sum_{k\in\mathds{N}_{0}}\frac{A^{k}}{k!}.

Remark 6.12
.

For short, we also write

e^{A}:=\exp A.

Lemma 6.13
.

The matrix exponential is well-defined, i.e.,

\exp A\in\mathds{R}^{n\times n}

for all
A\in\mathds{R}^{n\times n}
.

Proof.

Let
\|\cdot\|
be the spectral norm on
\mathds{R}^{n\times n}
:

\|A\|:=\max_{x\in\mathds{R}^{n}\setminus\{0\}}\frac{\|Ax\|}{\|x\|}.

Clearly,

\|A+B\|\leq\|A\|+\|B\|\qquad\text{and}\qquad\|AB\|\leq\|A\|\cdot\|B\|

for all
A,B\in\mathds{R}^{n\times n}
.
Hence,

\|\exp A\|\leq\sum_{k\in\mathds{N}_{0}}\frac{\|A\|^{k}}{k!}=e^{\|A\|}<\infty

for all
A\in\mathds{R}^{n\times n}
.
Hence, in the
\exp
definition we have bounded dominance and therefore convergence in
\mathds{R}^{n\times n}
.
∎
