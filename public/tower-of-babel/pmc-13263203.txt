Comparative in vivo assessment of surgical smoke particle exposure from different energy modalities during gynecologic laparoscopy
Authors: Samer El-Safadi, Carolin Elena Bautista, Ivo Meinhold-Heerlein, Thilo Schwandner, Felix Zeppernick, Isabela Perales, Tatiana Pfiffer, Andrea Plaikner, Christhardt Köhler, Giovanni Favero
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundMinimally invasive surgery (MIS) is widely used in gynecology because of its perioperative advantages over open surgery. However, concerns regarding the oncologic safety of MIS in early-stage cervical cancer have renewed interest in potential mechanisms of tumor cell dissemination during laparoscopic procedures. Surgical smoke generated by electrosurgical instruments has emerged as one possible factor. Although composed mainly of water vapor, surgical plume contains particulate matter produced during thermal tissue destruction and has been associated with occupational exposure risks and impaired intraoperative visibility. Nevertheless, quantitative in vivo data on particle generation during laparoscopic surgery remain limited.ObjectiveThis prospective study is the first to quantify particle size and concentration in electrosurgical smoke during laparoscopic procedures, assessing their potential role in tumor recurrence.MethodsUsing advanced laser-based detection, particle emissions were measured during laparoscopic surgeries for benign diseases in 40 patients. Data were analyzed to assess correlations between particle load, surgical duration, and instrument type (monopolar vs. bipolar).ResultsMonopolar devices produced approximately 100 times more particles than bipolar instruments, highlighting a substantial disparity in emission. While bipolar tools generated significantly fewer and larger particles, monopolar energy produced mostly small ones (0.3–5 µm). The overwhelming particle load from monopolar electrosurgery was strongly correlated with longer operative times and more intensive instrument use.ConclusionMonopolar electrosurgical instruments generated substantially higher concentrations of surgical smoke particles than bipolar devices during laparoscopic surgery, particularly in the fine particle range. Although measured during benign gynecologic procedures and without biological plume analysis, these findings provide important in vivo data on particle generation. Improved understanding of surgical smoke dynamics may inform strategies to reduce aerosol exposure and guide future investigations in minimally invasive gynecologic oncologic surgery, particularly in the context of cervical cancer.

What does this study add to the clinical work

Introduction

Minimally invasive surgery (MIS) has become widely adopted in gynecologic practice because of its well-established advantages over open surgery, including reduced postoperative pain, shorter hospital stays, and faster recovery [1]. The development and widespread use of electrosurgical instruments have been central to this progress, enabling efficient tissue dissection, coagulation, and hemostasis during laparoscopic procedures [2].

The use of electrosurgical devices inevitably produces surgical smoke, also known as surgical plume. While primarily composed of water vapor, this smoke also contains particulate matter generated during thermal tissue destruction [3]. Studies have shown that surgical smoke may carry cellular debris, blood components, chemical compounds, and potentially infectious biological material, including fragments of human papillomavirus (HPV). [4, 5] HPV infection is strongly linked to the development of cervical carcinoma, being present in approximately 99% of cases [6].

Beyond its composition, surgical smoke has raised increasing concerns regarding operating room safety [7]. Fine particles generated during electrosurgery can remain airborne for prolonged periods and may penetrate deeply into the respiratory tract [8]. In addition, high concentrations of surgical smoke may impair intraoperative visibility, potentially affecting surgical precision and operative efficiency [9]. Consequently, understanding the factors influencing surgical smoke generation has become an important topic in minimally invasive surgery.

The quantity and characteristics of surgical smoke depend on several variables, including tissue type, energy settings, duration of activation, and the electrosurgical modality used [10]. Experimental studies have demonstrated that different energy devices produce distinct particle concentrations and size distributions [11]. In particular, monopolar and bipolar electrosurgical instruments have been shown to generate markedly different particle profiles under laboratory conditions [12–14]. Despite increasing interest in surgical smoke, direct in vivo measurements during laparoscopic procedures remain limited. Most previous investigations have relied on experimental models or measurements of ambient operating room air rather than direct quantification of particle emissions during active electrosurgical use [15, 16]. Therefore, the aim of the present prospective study was to quantify particle generation during laparoscopic gynecologic surgery using real-time laser-based particle detection. Specifically, the study assessed particle concentration and size distribution generated during electrosurgical coagulation and compared emissions associated with monopolar and bipolar instruments.

Although the procedures included in this study were performed for benign gynecologic indications and no biological analysis of the vapor was conducted, improved understanding of particle generation during electrosurgery may provide important insights into surgical smoke exposure during minimally invasive procedures. Such knowledge may help inform strategies to reduce aerosol exposure, improve intraoperative visibility, and guide future investigations into the potential clinical implications of surgical smoke in gynecologic surgery.

In this broader context, recent discussions regarding the oncologic safety of minimally invasive surgery in early-stage cervical cancer—particularly following the publication of the LACC trial—have further highlighted the importance of understanding factors associated with laparoscopic surgical environments [17–19]. While the present study does not address oncologic outcomes, the characterization of particle generation during electrosurgery may contribute to the ongoing evaluation of surgical smoke in both benign and oncologic minimally invasive procedures.

Methods

The study was conducted using prospectively collected data at St. Marienkrankenhaus in Frankfurt, Germany, in collaboration with the Department of Gynecology at Justus Liebig University Giessen. It included 40 voluntary female patients who underwent planned laparoscopic surgery for benign gynecological conditions between August 2015 and February 2016. All procedures were performed by a single experienced surgeon (SE-S). Ethical approval was granted in December 2013 by the Ethics Committee of the Faculty of Medicine at Justus Liebig University Giessen (Application No. 193/13), and all patients provided written informed consent.

The inclusion criterion was an indication for laparoscopic surgery. Exclusion criteria included comorbidities contraindicating laparoscopy and lack of signed informed consent. For each patient, demographic and procedural data were recorded, including age, weight, surgical indication, duration of surgery, instruments used, frequency of instrument use, number of trocars, and history of prior surgeries. Additionally, perioperative particle generation was measured and documented.

All procedures were performed under standardized conditions to ensure comparability. Patients were positioned in lithotomy with Trendelenburg tilt. Pneumoperitoneum was established using CO₂, with intraabdominal pressure limited to 15 mmHg. A total of four balloon-tipped trocars (6-mm and 12-mm; Applied Medical, USA) were used in all cases. The type and placement of trocars, patient positioning, and insufflation parameters were kept constant throughout the study.

The number of particles released and their size distribution according to the type of electrocautery used were quantified using a laser measuring device (Abakus® mobile air) and corresponding analysis software (Markus Klotz GmbH, Bad Liebenzell, Germany). The experimental setup is illustrated in Fig. 1. A connector was attached to one trocar to connect a reusable silicone tube while keeping the trocar’s release valve initially closed (Fig. 2). The other end of the tube was connected to an antistatic hose, which was linked to one branch of a Y-piece. A sterile air filter with an emergency release valve was attached to the second branch of the Y-piece. The third branch, combining both inflows, was connected to the dilution stage, which was then connected via an antistatic hose to the particle counter.Fig. 1Diagram of the experimental setupFig. 2Connection of the tube to the trocar

Connection of the tube to the trocar

The particle counter was connected to a power supply and to a laptop containing the measurement software. The Abakus® mobile air particle counter uses an LSD 328 sensor to measure particles in the size range of 0.3–10 µm. A vacuum pump ensured a continuous airflow of 28.3 l/min. The optical system consists of a laser diode and a 90° scattered light collection optic. Continuous measurement mode was applied throughout the experiment. The particle counter recorded date, time, sample identification, measurement duration, pause time, and particle counts for predefined size channels.

For each operation, the number of particles and their size distribution were measured using a standardized procedure. Prior to measurement, the tubing system was flushed using the sterile air filter to remove background airborne particles. The antistatic hose was clamped before entering the Y-piece, and the release valves of both the trocar and the sterile air filter were opened. Continuous particle measurement was then initiated.

During surgery, the number of trocars and the frequency of monopolar and bipolar electrosurgical instrument use were recorded. When an electrosurgical instrument was used for the first time, the time and type of instrument were documented. The clamp on the antistatic hose was released for 60 s to direct the surgical vapor toward the measuring device and was then closed again. This process was repeated every five minutes throughout the procedure. Particle sizes of 0.3 µm, 0.5 µm, 1 µm, 3 µm, 5 µm, and 10 µm were measured. The dilution factor of 1:100 applied by the dilution stage was corrected by multiplying the measured values by 100.

Statistical analysis was performed using SPSS version 27.0.1.0. Descriptive statistics included mean, median, percentiles, minimum, maximum, and frequencies, presented in tables and graphical formats. Confirmatory analyses were conducted using the Mann–Whitney U test and analysis of variance (ANOVA). To counteract alpha error accumulation, the Bonferroni correction was applied. Exploratory analyses included boxplots and regression analyses. Statistical significance was set at p ≤ 0.05.

Results

The data were collected more than 8 years ago; however, publication was intentionally delayed to allow comprehensive validation of the in vivo measurement methodology and comparison with findings from subsequent studies. Advances in analytical techniques during this period enabled a more robust and clinically meaningful interpretation of the results. Importantly, the fundamental mechanisms of particle generation during electrosurgical energy application, as well as the underlying principles of bipolar and monopolar energy delivery, have remained unchanged in modern surgical systems. Consequently, the findings remain methodologically relevant to current surgical practice.

In the 40 surgeries performed, monopolar and bipolar coagulation instruments were used with varying frequency. The bipolar instrument was the most frequently used, being employed in 38 of the 40 surgeries (95%). On average, bipolar cautery was applied 48 times per procedure, with a median of 37.5 applications and a maximum of 134. In contrast, the monopolar instrument was used in 14 surgeries (35%), with an average of 8.4 applications per surgery and a maximum of 55 applications in a single procedure.

Eleven different indications for laparoscopic surgery were identified. The most common indications were combined hysterectomy with adnexectomy and ovarian cystectomy, each occurring in eight cases and together accounting for 40% of all procedures. Adnexectomy and hysterectomy alone were the next most frequent indications, each performed in six cases, representing 30% of the total. Most patients had undergone previous surgical procedures: 29 patients (72.5%) reported at least one prior surgery.

Surgery duration varied substantially depending on the type of procedure. Laparoscopic hysterectomy was the longest procedure, with a mean duration of 172.33 min. Combined hysterectomy with adnexectomy lasted approximately 2 h on average. More than half of all surgeries exceeded 1 h, although 4 of the 11 surgical types had mean durations of less than 1 h.

The total number of particles generated during coagulation was calculated by summing particle counts across all measured size categories for each surgery. This resulted in a mean total particle count of 111,282,941 per procedure. The maximum recorded particle count was 677,330,000, while the minimum was 13,500 particles. Hysterectomies were associated with both the longest operative times and the highest particle counts. During these procedures, both monopolar and bipolar instruments were frequently used, suggesting that surgery duration and additional monopolar use may be contributing factors to increased particle generation. Conversely, procedures with shorter durations and less frequent use of coagulation instruments showed lower particle counts.

Because monopolar coagulation was never used as the sole instrument, total particle counts attributable to monopolar use could only be assessed indirectly by comparing procedures with combined monopolar and bipolar use to those using bipolar coagulation alone. The bar chart illustrates that the mean total particle count for combined instrument use was substantially higher than for bipolar-only use (Fig. 3). However, as procedures involving both bipolar and monopolar instruments often corresponded to longer and more complex operations, surgical duration and the number of instrument activations represent potential confounding factors. Therefore, to isolate the effect of electrosurgical modality on particle generation, a multivariable linear regression analysis was performed. Two groups were included in this analysis: procedures using bipolar energy only (bipolar-only) and those using a combination of bipolar and monopolar energy (bipolar + monopolar). The natural logarithm of the total particle count (ln_total_count) was used as the dependent variable because the raw particle counts exhibited a strongly right-skewed distribution. The regression model included electrosurgical modality, surgery duration, and the total number of coagulation activations as independent variables. To account for skewness in these variables, both surgical duration and the total number of activations were log-transformed prior to inclusion in the model.

In the multivariable analysis, the combined use of bipolar and monopolar energy remained significantly associated with a higher total particle count, even after adjustment for surgical duration and the total number of coagulation activations. These results indicate that the electrosurgical modality itself contributes independently to particle generation during surgical procedures and that the observed differences cannot be explained solely by longer operative times or a greater number of instrument activations.

To further investigate how particle size influences these differences, particle counts across individual size categories were compared between combined monopolar–bipolar use and bipolar-only use. An analysis of variance (ANOVA) was conducted on logarithmized total particle counts, with pairwise comparisons across particle sizes. The analysis revealed statistically significant differences between the two categories for all particle sizes prior to correction, with p-values below 0.01.

Given the multiple comparisons performed, p-values were adjusted using the Bonferroni correction. After adjustment, significant differences in total particle counts remained for the five smallest particle sizes (0.3 µm, 0.5 µm, 1 µm, 3 µm, and 5 µm). For the largest particle size (10 µm), no statistically significant difference was observed, as the corrected p-value of 0.054 slightly exceeded the significance threshold of α ≤ 0.05.

The corresponding boxplots illustrate these findings visually (Fig. 5). For particle sizes ranging from 0.3 µm to 5 µm, the median particle counts were substantially higher in the category involving additional monopolar use compared to bipolar-only use. For the 10 µm particle size, the medians of the two categories were similar, consistent with the non-significant ANOVA result. Overall, the magnitude of the difference between medians decreased as particle size increased, indicating that smaller particles are more strongly affected by the use of monopolar coagulation. The boxplots also show that the spread of values for the smallest particle sizes was narrower when monopolar was used in addition to bipolar, whereas bipolar-only use exhibited a particularly wide spread at the 10 µm particle size. Discussion

In recent decades, endoscopic techniques have become well established and continuously developed in gynecologic surgery. Electrosurgical instruments were essential for this progress, enabling complex laparoscopic procedures. Concurrently, research increasingly focused on identifying and minimizing complications associated with laparoscopy. Since the mid-1990s, numerous in vitro and in vivo studies have analyzed vapors and particles generated during electrosurgery, the resulting thermal tissue damage, and potential risks to operating room personnel [20]. In contrast, the present study is the first to quantify and compare the number and size of particles generated in vivo during laparoscopic gynecologic procedures using monopolar and bipolar instruments.

When comparing particle numbers from bipolar coagulation alone with those from combined monopolar and bipolar use, a marked increase in total particle count was observed with additional monopolar application. This difference was statistically significant in the Mann–Whitney U test. The mean total particle count during combined use was approximately 100 times higher than during bipolar-only use (3.24 × 108 vs. 4.89 × 106). These findings align with prior data showing that vapor from monopolar instruments is substantially more concentrated than from bipolar devices [21]. Weld et al. reported that monopolar vapor was 82 times more concentrated than bipolar vapor and 721 times more concentrated than vapor generated by Ultracision instruments [14].

Detailed analysis revealed a pronounced increase in particles sized 0.3 µm, 0.5 µm, 1 µm, 3 µm, and 5 µm with additional monopolar use. Differences in smaller particle sizes confirm that in vitro particle distributions are also present in vivo. Farrugia et al. demonstrated that monopolar coagulation produced smaller median particle sizes than bipolar coagulation in uterine tissue blocks [11]. Similarly, Weld et al. reported higher numbers of small particles with monopolar instruments in vitro [14]. These observations align with the present findings. Differences in total particle counts decreased as particle size increased (Fig. 6), indicating that bipolar instruments generate more large particles, while monopolar instruments predominantly produce smaller ones.

Potential hazards of surgical smoke include respiratory exposure to fine particles (< 1 µm), which can penetrate deep into the lungs, and potential dissemination of viruses (particularly HPV) or bacteria [4, 5]. High concentrations can also impair intraoperative visibility, prolonging surgery and increasing operator fatigue [21]. Larger particles may deposit in the upper airway, while smaller particles remain airborne longer, highlighting the importance of both particle count and size in assessing exposure risk [22–26].

Compared with studies measuring particle concentrations in operating rooms, particles sized 0.5 µm and 5 µm initially rise sharply and decrease after ~ 10 min, whereas 0.3 µm particles increase later, as reported by Li et al. [5]. Although temporal particle development was not assessed here, a linear correlation between bipolar instrument use frequency and total particle count was observed. Consequently, modifications in surgical technique, such as preferential use of bipolar or ultrasonic instruments, intermittent activation, and moderate power settings, may reduce particle generation and exposure while also shortening operation times [27, 28].

Although initially motivated by concerns regarding oncologic surgery, the present findings have broader implications for multiple laparoscopic procedures, including routine gynecologic and abdominal surgery. By demonstrating differences in particle generation between monopolar and bipolar instruments, this study provides guidance on instrument choice and operative technique to improve safety and reduce aerosol exposure in a wide range of surgical settings.

Tissue damage and particle formation are influenced by coagulation mode. Higher current strength increases thermal injury, with effects depending on current density, exposure duration, waveform, and tissue type [20, 25]. In this study, only the frequency—not the duration—of instrument use was recorded, preventing definitive conclusions regarding cumulative thermal effects. Intermittent coagulation (e.g., 5-s activation with 5-s pause) can reduce tissue damage [20]. Surgical technique and surgeon experience also affect particle production, as do patient-specific factors such as tissue composition, with higher fat content increasing vapor generation [20, 26].

Particle generation may be reduced through preferential use of bipolar or ultrasonic instruments at moderate settings with intermittent activation [27]. Combining multiple functions in a single device may further reduce operative time and particle production [28]. Consequently, these findings have implications beyond oncologic surgery: optimizing instrument choice, energy settings, and activation patterns may improve safety, visibility, and efficiency in a wide range of minimally invasive procedures, including routine gynecologic, visceral, and urologic endoscopic surgeries.

Since the LACC study, concerns have been raised regarding laparoscopic oncologic surgery [17]. Surgical smoke can contain tissue fragments, viruses, and bacteria, and monopolar instruments generate the highest particle counts. [29–31] While these factors may contribute to exposure risk, operative time, uterine manipulators, and pneumoperitoneum also influence particle exposure and intra-abdominal conditions [32, 33]. The present study showed a linear increase in particle counts with longer procedures, emphasizing the importance of optimizing surgical duration. Reducing operative time through improved electrosurgical instruments, increased use of sealing devices, and refined surgical techniques may enhance safety, as demonstrated by Plante and SHAPE investigators [34, 35].

Limitations of this study include a non-oncologic population, the absence of biological analysis of particles, and the inability to isolate the contribution of monopolar instruments. Despite these constraints, the study provides valuable in vivo data on particle number and size, confirming trends previously described in vitro. The pronounced increase in small particle counts with monopolar use raises safety concerns and highlights the need for further investigation.

Future studies should systematically analyze particle composition (viral / HPV, bacterial, or cellular), map particle size distribution across different procedures, compare emerging sealing technologies, and evaluate how operative time and technique modifications affect particle generation. Such research could inform protocols to reduce surgical smoke exposure and improve intraoperative safety across a wide range of minimally invasive procedures.
