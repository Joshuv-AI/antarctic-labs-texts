Multi-domain temporal patterns reveal stable community membership but dynamic interactions in the coastal microbiome
Authors: Elvira Rey Redondo, Wenqian Xu, Yangbing Xu, Ruixian Sun, Siu Hei Wan, Shara K K Leung, Charmaine C M Yung
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundMarine microbial communities drive global biogeochemical cycles and oceanic food webs, yet our understanding of their holistic temporal dynamics remains limited, particularly in the South China Sea. Most studies have focused on specific taxonomic groups or single temporal scales, leaving a gap in comprehensive, multi-domain, and multi-timescale analyses.ResultsUsing an integrated multi-omics approach that combined metagenomic, metatranscriptomic, and metaviromic analyses, we conducted time-series sampling over 48-h periods during winter and summer to investigate microbial community dynamics in the coastal South China Sea. Seasonal transitions were identified as the primary drivers of community shifts, with diel variations playing a secondary role across all taxonomic domains. Within seasons, diel changes followed a progressive trajectory rather than recurring cyclic patterns. Eukaryotic communities exhibited the most pronounced temporal fluctuations, while prokaryotic and viral communities displayed remarkable stability. Unlike previous coastal studies, viral communities maintained high similarity between seasons, suggesting the presence of a persistent viral reservoir in this region. Gene expression analysis revealed dynamic population shifts in photosynthetic microorganisms, with Mamiellophyceae green algae and their associated Prasinovirus displaying pronounced seasonal and diel rhythmicity.ConclusionsThis study provides novel insights into the temporal dynamics of microbial communities and host-virus interactions in the South China Sea. The stability of viral communities, coupled with synchronised host-virus activities, highlights potential mechanisms supporting ecosystem resilience in this coastal region. These findings enhance our understanding of marine ecosystem processes and establish a robust framework for exploring microbial responses to environmental changes on both diel and seasonal scales.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s40793-025-00803-5.Keywords: Diel cycles, Seasonal dynamics, Multi-omics, Viral-host interactions, Marine microbes

Background

Marine microbial communities, comprising diverse prokaryotes, eukaryotes, and viruses, play fundamental roles in global biogeochemical cycles and oceanic food webs. These microorganisms drive key processes such as primary production, nutrient cycling, and carbon sequestration, thereby supporting ecosystem functionality and resilience [1, 2]. Advances in high-throughput sequencing technologies have revolutionised our understanding of the ocean microbiome, enabling comprehensive genetic and functional characterisation of microbial communities across spatiotemporal scales [3, 4]. While metagenomics establishes biodiversity baselines and potential metabolic capacity [5, 6], metatranscriptomics reveals active gene expression and real-time functional responses [7–9]. Integration of these complementary approaches enhances our understanding of marine ecosystem functionality and resilience across spatiotemporal scales [10–14].

Despite these technological advances, critical knowledge gaps persist in our understanding of whole-community dynamics across multiple temporal scales. Most studies have focused on individual domains, such as prokaryotes [3, 15], microeukaryotes [12, 16, 17], or viruses [14, 18], while integrated investigations encompassing all three domains are rare [4, 19]. This gap is particularly pronounced in ecologically significant regions like the South China Sea, one of the Earth’s largest marginal seas, where holistic analyses of microbial community dynamics are still limited.

Microbial communities exhibit dynamics across distinct temporal scales. Diel studies reveal light-dependent processes, circadian rhythms, and short-term interactions [20–22], whereas seasonal and interannual investigations uncover succession patterns and community responses to prolonged environmental changes [17, 23, 24]. Although studies integrating multiple temporal scales remain uncommon [14, 25, 26], such approaches are crucial for differentiating short-term fluctuations from long-term trends, validating findings across time frames, and revealing complex interactions that single-scale analyses may overlook.

This study addresses these knowledge gaps by examining the microbial community dynamics across diel and seasonal scales in the coastal South China Sea. Using an integrated multi-omics approach combining metagenomic, metatranscriptomic, and metaviromic analyses with environmental measurements, we aim to: (1) characterise the taxonomic and functional composition of the entire microbial community, including prokaryotes, eukaryotes, and viruses; (2) elucidate the temporal dynamics across multiple timescales; and (3) identify key biotic and abiotic factors shaping these dynamics. By adopting a cross-domain, multi-timescale perspective, this study provides critical insights into the mechanisms that sustain microbial ecosystem functionality and resilience.

Methods

Sample collection

Seawater samples were collected from 2 m depth using a manual pump into 20 L carboys at a coastal site in Hong Kong (22°20′16.5″N 114°16′09.3″E) during the winter (January 20–22, 2021) and summer (August 30-September 1, 2021) monsoon seasons. Nine samples were collected at 6-h intervals over 48 h during each season, starting at 14:00 (winter sunrise/sunset: 07:05/18:05; summer: 06:05/18:40). Winter sampling days were dry (no precipitation) and sunny, whereas summer sampling day 1 (30th August) was dry followed by moderate to heavy rainfall on days 2–3 (11.5 mm of rainfall on 31st August and 16 mm on 1st September as determined by the Hong Kong Observatory at Kau Sai Chau station [27], located 4 km from the sampling site).

Environmental parameters (temperature, salinity, pH, dissolved oxygen) were measured in situ with a multiparameter sonde (YSI EXO3, Yellow Springs Instruments, USA). Global solar radiation (GSR) data were obtained from the Hong Kong Observatory’s Kau Sai Chau station [28]. Nutrient concentrations (ammonia nitrogen (NH4+), nitrate (NO3−), nitrite (NO2−), silicate (SiO32−), total phosphorus (TP), and orthophosphate phosphorus (PO43−)) were determined using spectroscopy (colorimetry and photometry) methods following APA standard procedures [29]. For chlorophyll-a analysis, 25 mL seawater was filtered through 0.2 μm PCTE membranes (Whatman), extracted in 90% acetone for 20 h at 4 °C in darkness, centrifuged, and quantified using a Trilogy fluorometer (Turner Designs).

Filtration of replicate 20 L samples began within minutes of collection from the pier. The entire sequential size-fractionation process for a complete set of replicates was finished in approximately two hours. This involved passing the sample through a 50 μm mesh (prefilter), followed by 0.8 μm and 0.2 μm filters (142 mm Whatman) using a peristaltic pump. This separated particle-attached (0.8–50 μm) from free-living (0.2–0.8 μm) microorganisms, an approach which expedited filtration and ensured adequate sequencing coverage for both fractions, which were later combined for analysis. Viral particles in the final filtrate were concentrated via iron chloride flocculation (1 mg Fe L−1 seawater) and collected on 1 μm polycarbonate membranes [30]. Samples collected on 0.8 μm and 0.2 μm filters were flash-frozen and stored at -80°C until nucleic acid extraction, while virus-retaining filters were maintained at 4°C in darkness.

Nucleic acid extraction and sequencing

DNA and RNA were co-extracted from 0.8 and 0.2 μm filters using ZymoBIOMICS DNA/RNA and Direct-zol RNA Miniprep Kits (Zymo Research, USA), while viral DNA was extracted from 1 μm filters using Wizard DNA Purification Resin columns (Promega, USA). The extracted genomic DNA and total RNA were sent to Novogene (Tianjin, China) for library preparation and sequencing. DNA quality was verified by 1% agarose gel electrophoresis before fragmentation to 300–500 bp using a Covaris M220 Focused-Ultrasonicator (Covaris Inc., USA). Libraries were prepared using NEXTFLEX Rapid DNA-Seq 2.0 kits and sequenced on an Illumina Novaseq 6000 PE150 platform (Illumina Inc., USA) at 10 Gb depth. For the total RNA samples, ribosomal RNA was depleted using Ribo-Zero Plus kits (Illumina Inc., USA), and remaining RNA was fragmented to 250–300 bp and reverse-transcribed using NEBNext Ultra RNA Library Prep Kit (New England Biolabs, USA). Metatranscriptomic libraries were sequenced on Illumina Novaseq 6000 PE150 platform (Illumina Inc., USA) at 10 Gb depth.

Bioinformatic analysis

All reads including metagenomic (metaG), metaviromic (metaV) and metatranscriptomic (metaT) reads were trimmed and quality controlled with trimmomatic v0.39 [31] (LEADING:3 TRAILING:3 SLIDINGWINDOW:4:20 MINLEN:50) and FastQC [32]. MetaG and metaV reads were assembled using MEGAHIT v1.2.9 with default parameters [33], while metaT reads were ribodepleted using SortMeRNA v4.3.4 [34] with the smr_v4.3_default_db before assembly using rnaSPAdes v4.0.0 [35] with default parameters [17]. Contigs < 200 bp were removed using SeqKit v2.1.0 [36].

Cellular component annotation

MetaG and metaT assemblies underwent open reading frame (ORF) prediction using Prodigal v2.6.3 with the -p meta option [37] after validation against 18S-V4 marker-based [38] and FragGeneScan [39] methods. Predicted gene sequences from both the metaG and metaT datasets were concatenated and clustered using CD-HIT v4.8.1 at 95% identity and 90% alignment [40, 41]. Non-redundant genes were taxonomically annotated using DIAMOND v2.0.15 (blastx E-value = 1e−5, fast mode, outfmt = 102) against NCBI-nr (March 2023) using a lowest common ancestor (LCA) algorithm for taxonomic classification [42]. Taxonomic IDs were converted to named taxa using R package taxonomizr v0.10.6 [43]. Abundance was quantified using Salmon v1.10.1 in meta mode [44] and normalised to Transcripts Per Million (TPM) using Salmon quantmerge. The taxonomic annotations were integrated with Salmon TPM using “summarizeAbundance.py” from EasyMicrobiome v1.18 [45], consolidating OTU (operational taxonomic unit) outputs at the genus level or grouping taxa at higher taxonomic levels (family, order, or class) when genus-level assignment was not supported by sufficient sequence similarity or database coverage. Eukaryotic annotations were manually curated to remove multicellular organisms and their parasites.

Viral component annotation

Viral sequences from the metaG, metaT (cell-fraction viruses), and metaV (free viruses) data were processed using an established pipeline [46, 47]. MEGAHIT/RNAspades contig assemblies were analysed through Virsorter2 [48] in conjunction with checkV v0.8.1 [49] to extract the contigs of dsDNAphage, ssDNA, RNA and nucleocytoplasmic large DNA viruses (NCLDV) (–min-length 1500 bp) [50]. Viral contigs were clustered into vOTUs (viral OTUs) using ClusterGenomes v0.9.0 [51] with MUMmer4 [52]. ORFs were predicted using Prodigal (-p meta) and annotated with CAT v6.0.1 [53] using DIAMOND v2.1.8.162 against Viral RefSeq (release 214 from 2022) and default settings, followed by manual curation to align with current ICTV classifications [54]. vOTUs were considered supported when > 50% of ORFs had hits (ranked by bit-score), with unsupported vOTUs classified as Nucleocytoviricota, Uroviricota, or Other. Supported vOTUs were assigned taxonomy using the LCA principle. Abundance was quantified by mapping reads using Bowtie2 v2.2.5 [55] and processed with SAMtools v1.10 [56] to generate a vOTU abundance matrix [57]. Counts were normalised to TPM [58] and consolidated at the genus where possible, or grouped at higher taxonomic levels.

Taxonomic analysis and visualisation

Taxonomic data were processed separately for prokaryotes, eukaryotes, and viruses. For all domains, taxa that could not be resolved to the genus level were retained and grouped at the highest taxonomic level to which they could be confidently assigned. Groups (including unclassified) merged at the phylum/clade/class/family level with ≥ 1% mean seasonal abundance were retained, with others consolidated as “Other”. Relative abundances were visualised as barplots using ChiPlot [59], including specific analyses of Chlorophyta and Phycodnaviridae. Relative expression was calculated as log2(metaT/metaG + 1). The use of “ + 1” minimises any spurious expression levels resulting from metaG annotation error, since in this study the exact numbers were not the focus, but rather the relative differences in expression between taxa. Ratios > 1 indicate increased transcriptomic activity above genetic potential, = 1 indicates equilibrium, and < 1 indicates reduced activity. Analysis focused on taxa with ≥ 1% mean abundance in at least one season (yielding 35 prokaryotic, 31 eukaryotic, and 30 viral OTUs). Expression patterns were visualised using ChiPlot heatmaps. Temporal community changes were assessed by calculating Bray–Curtis Dissimilarities using the vegan R package [18, 60].

Taxonomic co-occurrence networks

Time-series associations between OTUs (TPM > 100 per season) and environmental metadata were analysed using eLSA [61] with default robustz normalisation [62] and a maximum delay of 2. Indirect environmental correlations were filtered out using EnDED v1.0.1 [63] with the combined four-method approach, requiring consensus across all four methods to classify associations as indirect, revealing 0.49% (January) and 0.29% (August) environmentally-driven associations. Significant associations were retained (p < 0.001, q < 0.1, local similarity score ≥ 0.8) following established thresholds [63–66], yielding 2483 and 2410 edges for January and August, respectively. Networks were visualised in Gephi v0.10.1 [67] using the Fruchterman Reingold layout [68], highlighting cell–cell, cell-virus, and virus-virus interactions.

Environmental factor correlations

We validated our site’s environmental measurements against data from the Hong Kong Environmental Protection Department’s Port Shelter station PM7, located approximately 4 km from our sampling location [69] (Supp. Table TS1). Variables were excluded if they exhibited opposing seasonal patterns between our measurements and EPD reference data, such as chlorophyll-a which showed contrasting summer versus winter dominance between datasets. Remaining factors were tested for collinearity using Spearman’s rank correlation coefficients (SRCC), removing pairs with |SRCC|> 0.7 (Supp. Table TS2). Temperature, salinity, pH and global solar radiation were retained for downstream analysis. Redundancy Analysis (RDA) was carried out based on the Hellinger transformation of abundant taxonomic group TPM data (TPM > 500) using vegan R package [60], since the lengths of the first axis from detrended correspondence analysis (DCA) were lower than 2.5.

Mamiellophyceae and Prasinovirus diel and seasonal abundances

Chlorophyta (dominated by Mamiellophyceae) and Phycodnaviridae (dominated by Prasinovirus) temporal patterns were visualised with ChiPlot. Reference genomes were obtained for seven Mamiellophyceae (excluding chloroplast and mitochondria sequences, including Mantoniella tinhauana RCC11003-PRJNA1044521) and 23 marine prasinovirus species from NCBI. Quality-controlled metaG/T/V reads were mapped using BBMap v38.96 [70] with a stringent 99% identity threshold. The resulting alignments were compiled using pileup.sh from the BBMap suite and the mapping values were normalised to TPM [58].

Expression and functional profiling

Assembled metaG (MEGAHIT) and metaT (RNAspades) contigs were processed using MetaCerberus v1.4 [71], employing FragGeneScan v1.1.0 [39] for ORF prediction and HMMER v3.4 [72] for alignment and annotation against all default databases, with default parameters (E-value = 1e−9). Predicted ORFs were concatenated and dereplicated with CD-HIT and quantified using Salmon as above.

For expression analysis, ORFs with > 100 metaT counts per season were analysed using DESeq2 v1.44.0 [73] via tximport v1.32.0 [74] in R, following the established protocol [14]. The counts were DESeq2 normalised, then rlog transformed. The top 1000 most time-varying genes were clustered into co-occurring groups using DP_GP_cluster [75] and visualised.

For functional characterisation, Salmon ORF counts (metaG and metaT) were converted to TPM and combined with KOFam annotations (MetaCerberus HMMER outputs) using summarizeAbundance.py. Relative expression of KO groups was determined using these TPM values by calculating log2(metaT/metaG + 1) as before and visualised as heatmaps using ChiPlot.

Results and discussion

Hierarchical community structure and seasonal patterns

Our analysis of temporal community dynamics in the South China Sea (SCS) using Bray–Curtis dissimilarity (BCD) revealed hierarchical patterns across taxonomic supergroups (eukaryotes, prokaryotes, viruses) and multi-omics datasets (metaG, metaT, metaV) (Fig. 1A-G). Prokaryotic dynamics best represented overall community patterns, reflecting their predominance among operational taxonomic units (OTUs) (Fig. 1C, D and Supp. Fig. S1). Between-season dissimilarities consistently exceeded within-season variations across all groups throughout the 7-month sampling period, except in August prokaryotic metaG samples (T4, 08:00), which showed annotation issues, as discussed later. Within seasons, community dissimilarity progressively increased throughout the diel cycle, rather than displaying daily reversion patterns (Supp. Fig. S1). August samples demonstrated higher taxonomic variability than January, except for the eukaryotic metaG component (Fig. 1A). A clear hierarchy in community stability emerged, with eukaryotes showing the highest temporal dissimilarity, followed by prokaryotes, cell-fraction viruses (in the metaG and metaT data), and free viruses (in the metaV data).

The viral populations at our study site demonstrated remarkable stability (between-season BCD 0.14) compared to previous observations from the San Pedro Ocean Time-series (SPOT) in coastal California (BCD 0.60) [18]. While methodological differences in viral contig assembly and taxonomic assignment preclude direct comparisons, this stability strongly supports the viral seed bank hypothesis, which posits that marine environments sustain diverse viral reservoirs with distinct temporal abundance patterns [76, 77]. The lower between-season dissimilarity in our study suggests the presence of an extensive viral reservoir in the SCS, potentially driven by enhanced viral diversity [78], local environmental conditions [79], and host community stability [80]. Our multi-omics approach further provided insights into both active and dormant viral populations, underscoring their role as environmental buffers [81]. However, the exclusion of unclassified viral contigs (“unclassified Virus” and “unclassified Uroviricota” as seen in Supplementary Fig. S3B and C) from BCD calculations could bias interpretations, underscoring the need to improve viral taxonomy and classification frameworks to enable more accurate and comprehensive assessments of viral dynamics.

Taxonomic composition shows multi-scale temporal patterns

Our multi-omics analysis revealed distinct temporal patterns across marine microbial communities, with seasonal and diel signatures evident within each major taxonomic group. Eukaryotic communities demonstrated the most pronounced temporal shifts (Fig. 2A), characterised by seasonal succession from winter-dominant Cryptophyta and Chlorophyta to summer-dominant Bacillariophyta. Cryptophyta exhibited elevated transcriptional activity during winter, consistent with their adaptation to colder waters [82]. Within Chlorophyta, Mamiellophyceae emerged as a particularly important group (Supp. Fig. S2A). Their winter dominance reflects adaptations to cooler temperatures. Dinophyta exhibited a contrasting pattern, with low metagenomic but high transcriptomic abundance in summer, highlighting the importance of multi-omics approaches in identifying functionally significant taxa that might otherwise be overlooked by DNA-based methods alone [83, 84]. This discrepancy suggests that Dinophyta maintain a relatively stable genomic presence but exhibit transcriptional bursts in response to environmental stimuli or specific life-cycle stages.

In contrast, prokaryotic communities demonstrated greater temporal consistency (Supplementary Fig. S3A), with Alphaproteobacteria, Gammaproteobacteria, and other Pseudomonadota maintaining dominance across seasons in both metaG and metaT datasets (Fig. 2B). However, this apparent stability at higher taxonomic levels likely conceals finer-scale seasonal switching between closely related OTUs [85], reflecting niche differentiation that preserves ecosystem functionality across environmental gradients. Cyanobacteriota deviated from this pattern, showing increased summer abundance (metaG) and metabolic activity (metaT), consistent with their known preference for warmer, well-lit conditions [86–88].

Viral communities exhibited more complex temporal dynamics. A significant proportion of viral contigs remained unclassified (Supplementary Fig. S3B and C), but host-linked seasonality was evident among classified viruses (Fig. 2C). For example, bacteria-infecting Uroviricota, particularly Demerecviridae and Autographiviridae, showed higher abundance in summer, while eukaryote-infecting Phycodnaviridae (Nucleocytoviricota) showed higher abundance in winter that closely tracked host dynamics. Within Phycodnaviridae, prasinoviruses and chloroviruses dominated across all datasets, with prasinoviruses especially abundant in the metaV (free virus) fraction, suggesting active infection and lysis events (Supp. Fig. S2B). The Mamiellophyceae-Prasinovirus system exemplified the intricate host-virus interactions persisting across seasons while exhibiting dynamic activity that likely contributes to viral seed bank maintenance. These temporal patterns highlight the coordinated evolutionary responses of host and viral populations to shifting environmental conditions.

A notable exception to host-virus synchrony was observed in Kyanoviridae, which displayed dataset-specific temporal patterns that diverged from those of their Cyanobacteriota hosts. Their metagenomic summer abundance contrasted with metatranscriptomic and metaviromic winter abundance, suggesting a seasonal shift from lysogenic to lytic cycles, driving enhanced free virus production and transcriptional activity during winter months [89]. This finding provides new insights into the complex strategies viruses employ to maintain their populations across seasonal transitions.

Discordance between genomic abundance and transcriptomic activity

To understand the relationship between presence and activity in marine microbial communities, we examined the relative expression levels (metaT:metaG ratio) of the top 20 most abundant OTUs across major microbial groups (Fig. 3A-C, Supplementary Fig. S4). This analysis revealed striking discrepancies between genomic abundance and transcriptional activity, with distinct patterns emerging across domains. An anomaly at the August-T4 timepoint showed artificially elevated metaT:metaG ratios due to under-annotation of metagenomic data, with a higher proportion of unclassified sequences compared to other timepoints. Despite TPM normalisation, this deflated overall taxonomic levels, producing spuriously high ratios. Higher unannotated ORF proportions in metaG-August-T4 relative to other samples, such as August-T8 (collected the same season and time), are evident in Supplementary Fig. S5 and Table TS3. The absence of similar patterns in functional or taxonomic expression from August-T8 (Supp. Table TS3) suggests the anomaly stems from technical DNA processing issues rather than biological variation.

Eukaryotic taxa displayed the highest and most variable expression ratios, with some groups reaching five-fold higher levels compared to prokaryotes (Fig. 3A). Their expression profiles highlighted complex microdiversity that transcends traditional phylogenetic groupings, echoing recent findings on fine-scale diversity in marine microbial communities [85, 90, 91]. Cryptophyta (including Guillardia, unclassified Geminigeraceae, Teleaulax, and unclassified Cryptophyceae) consistently exhibited high expression throughout the year with increased abundance in winter, reflecting their documented adaptations to lower temperature and light conditions [92]. Similarly, Mamiellophyceae, the sole abundant Chlorophyta representative, showed distinct seasonal expression patterns. Taxa such as Micromonas, Ostreococcus, and unclassified Mamiellophyceae showed highest abundance in winter, with daily maxima at 14:00, aligning with photosynthesis gene expression and underscoring their diel-responsive role in coastal microbial ecosystems. Haptophyta (Chrysochromulina, Phaeocystis, and unclassified taxa) showed elevated metaT/metaG ratios in winter, contrasting with prior observations of increased autumn abundance [23]. This unexpected winter activity may reflect unexplored cold-adaptation strategies in these taxa.

Dinophyta revealed complex seasonal variations: Prorocentrum showed extremely high transcriptomic activity in summer, consistent with its preference for warmer waters [93]. In contrast, Symbiodinium and unclassified Dinophyceae showed weaker seasonal variation. Bacillariophyta (Chaetoceros, Thalassiosira, Guinardia, and unclassified taxa) showed higher relative genomic abundance in summer but exhibited stable transcriptomic activity across seasons. This stability resulted in lower summer metaT:metaG ratios (Supplementary Fig. S4A), likely reflecting a post-bloom decline in activity due to resource limitation [94, 95]. This discordance between genomic abundance and transcriptomic activity, widely observed in marine systems [96–98], underscores the complexity of relationships between population size and metabolic activity. The multi-omics approach employed here revealed patterns that single-omic studies might overlook, providing deeper insights into marine microbial community dynamics.

Prokaryotic taxa generally showed low expression levels relative to their high genomic abundance (Fig. 3B). This pattern aligns with findings in other marine systems and suggests diverse life strategies or ecological roles among abundant taxa [97]. For example, Synechococcus demonstrated clear summer preference in both genomic and transcriptomic data (Supp. Fig. S4B), consistent with its known preference for warm water in temperate regions [99]. Notably, prokaryotes, even photosynthetic ones, lacked the pronounced diel rhythms observed in eukaryotes like Mamiellophyceae, raising fundamental questions about the evolutionary and ecological drivers of microbial time-keeping mechanisms [100].

While cell-fraction viruses generally maintained low relative expression (Fig. 3C), analysis of the top 20 most abundant viral taxa revealed three distinct clusters: (1) medium–low variable expression, exemplified by Cafeteriavirus (Cafeteria roenbergensis virus), which showed highest abundance in summer, contrasting with its Stramenopiles host’s increased winter abundance. This inverse relationship suggests a potential regulatory role for the virus in host population dynamics, possibly contributing to the host’s summer decline; (2) consistently low expression, including Phycodnaviridae OTUs (Prasinovirus, Chlorovirus), Immutovirus, and Elemovirus, which showed no synchronisation with their eukaryotic and Flavobacteriia hosts [101]; and (3) high seasonal expression, represented by Haifavirus. Haifavirus showed strong winter expression and summer diel patterns with daytime peaks (T1,4,5,8,9). These patterns suggest Haifavirus synchronises with host photosynthetic activity [102, 103], even though its host, Prochlorococcus, maintained low and stable expression with no apparent co-expression pattern (Supp. Fig. S6). These findings highlight the diverse infection strategies of viruses in marine systems. The limited co-expression patterns observed between viruses and their hosts likely reflect complex natural dynamics influenced by infection stages, host resistance mechanisms, and environmental conditions [104] that necessitate more targeted study with these methods. To further investigate these relationships and identify potential novel virus-host pairs, we employed time-delayed co-occurrence network analysis to uncover putative biotic interactions across the entire microbial community.

Dynamic microbial interactions reveal a stability-flexibility paradox across seasons

Using Extended Local Similarity Analysis (eLSA) with Environmentally-Driven Edge Detection (EnDED), we constructed seasonal co-occurrence networks capturing time-delayed correlations in curated OTU abundances. Networks retained only significant edges (p < 0.001) with local similarity scores > 0.8, representing strong putative biotic interactions for each season (Fig. 4A). Community composition showed remarkable stability across seasons, with 406 shared nodes representing 75.2% of winter and 56.4% of summer OTUs (> 100 TPM). However, this taxonomic consistency contrasted sharply with dynamic interaction patterns—these shared nodes accounted for just 1.3% of all edges, indicating substantial reorganisation of community interactions while maintaining stable membership. This finding aligns with previous temporal studies where no associations showed 100% recurrence across networks generated from monthly, multi-year sampling [62], emphasising the inherent variability of microbial interactions even over extended time scales. The contrast between stable composition and dynamic interactions underscores a fundamental paradox in marine microbial ecosystems. While most OTUs persist across seasons, their interactions undergo extensive rewiring. This suggests that marine community resilience may depend more on interaction flexibility than taxonomic turnover.

Seasonal networks exhibited distinct structural properties. The summer network contained more nodes but lower edge density compared to winter, a pattern consistent with Mediterranean studies showing maximum edge density at lower temperatures [62]. Both seasonal networks showed predominantly positive correlations (Fig. 4B), with winter showing a higher proportion of positive associations. This suggests that winter conditions fostered mutualistic interactions and reduced competition, supporting evidence that environmental stress can enhance microbial cooperation [105]. Conversely, summer populations experienced stronger competitive pressures, consistent with observations of increased viral diversity and competition during warmer seasons [106]. Viral processes, which intensify host competition, likely contributed to the reduced edge density observed in summer. Temporal analysis revealed that most winter interactions were non-delayed (96.3% of edges), suggesting rapid responses to environmental stressors operating on short, hourly timescales [107]. In contrast, summer interactions likely involved more complex, time-delayed dynamics, reflecting competition and viral-mediated processes. These patterns highlight how environmental conditions influence not only the structure of microbial interactions but also their temporal characteristics.

Virus-virus interactions dominate seasonal networks

A detailed analysis of interaction types—categorised into cell–cell, cell-virus, and virus-virus co-occurrences—revealed that virus-virus interactions were the dominant type, comprising nearly half of all strong edges across both seasons. These associations were predominantly positive, especially in winter, and likely reflect coordinated infection cycles, cooperative behaviours, or competitive exclusion of shared hosts [64, 104, 108]. The prevalence of virus-virus interactions underscores the ecological significance of viruses as central regulators of microbial diversity and ecosystem functionality, a pattern observed in other marine systems where viral dynamics are modulated by temperature and resource availability [78, 109].

Seasonal network architecture further highlighted the importance of virus-virus interactions. In winter, a large virus-virus cluster dominated the network, accompanied by smaller, less-connected cell-virus and cell–cell clusters. This suggests heightened viral coordination under environmental stress, with synchronous viral activity potentially shaping host populations during colder months. In summer, the network shifted structurally: a prominent eukaryote-dominated cell–cell cluster emerged alongside smaller virus-virus clusters. Among these, the Kyanoviridae family stood out, with 22 OTUs forming 22 predominantly positive intra-group associations. This clustering pattern suggests shared host ranges, similar replication strategies, or coordinated responses to environmental changes among Kyanoviridae members.

Taxonomic patterns in seasonal interactions

Taxonomic groups which showed significant seasonal dynamics displayed contrasting interaction patterns (Fig. 5A–F). In winter, Mamiellophyceae exhibited high intra-group clustering, with five undelayed, highly positive edges connecting five of seven OTUs (Fig. 5A). These associations suggest either strong mutualistic relationships or shared responses to environmental factors, such as nutrients, that were not captured by EnDED filtering. Conversely, summer Cyanobacteriota exhibited unexpectedly low connectivity, with only one positive edge observed within the phylum (Fig. 5C). This isolation is intriguing given the taxonomic relatedness of Cyanobacteriota groups (e.g., Synechococcales, Synechococcaceae, Synechococcus). Such low connectivity suggests ecological or physiological factors may suppress Cyanobacteriota interactions during summer, warranting further investigation.

Cross-domain interactions and host-virus relationships

Although seasonal taxonomic groups exhibited distinct intra-group patterns, the majority of their interactions extended beyond group boundaries, revealing the prevalence of cross-domain interactions in marine microbial communities. Mamiellophyceae (eukaryote), Cyanobacteriota (prokaryote), and Kyanoviridae (virus) primarily formed edges with OTUs from other groups (Fig. 5). This interconnectedness highlights the necessity of a holistic perspective when studying microbial dynamics, as focusing exclusively on within-group relationships would overlook the broader ecological context. These findings reinforce the idea that marine microbial community structure is shaped by cross-domain interactions, which are critical for ecosystem functionality across seasonal transitions.

Unexpected patterns emerged in virus-host relationships as well. Despite Kyanoviridae’s established association with Cyanobacteriota, only one significant edge was observed between these groups (Fig. 5D), linking an unclassified Cyanobacteriota OTU to Ahtivirus. Similarly, Mamiellophyceae OTUs displayed no strong co-occurrences with their known pathogen, Prasinovirus. Although all seven Mamiellophyceae OTUs exhibited weakly negative correlations with Prasinovirus during winter, these associations were statistically insignificant (Supplementary Table TS4). Aggregating Prasinovirus species into a single OTU likely masked finer-scale host-virus relationships, suggesting the need for higher taxonomic resolution in future studies.

Our co-occurrence networks reveal ecosystem-wide processes that traditional approaches often overlook, highlighting the central role of viruses as regulators of diversity and ecosystem function. By implementing stringent thresholds and removing environmentally driven edges, the networks likely captured both direct and indirect biotic interactions. For example, viral lysis of a host species may create ecological niches for unrelated taxa, resulting in positive correlations between viruses and algae despite no direct interactions. These cascading effects illustrate how network analysis can uncover indirect but ecologically significant relationships. While distinguishing direct from indirect associations remains a challenge, our findings underscore the adaptability of marine microbial communities. Resilience in these systems appears to depend on interaction flexibility and cross-domain relationships rather than taxonomic turnover alone. This adaptive strategy is particularly valuable in fluctuating coastal ecosystems, where environmental conditions drive continuous reorganisation of microbial networks. The observed stability-flexibility paradox highlights a fundamental mechanism by which marine microbial communities maintain ecosystem functionality in the face of environmental variability.

Environmental forces in microbial dynamics

Temperature emerged as the primary force shaping microbial community structure in our environmental analysis, which focused on four key non-collinear parameters: temperature, pH, salinity, and global solar radiation (Supplementary Fig. S7). Redundancy Analysis (RDA) demonstrated that temperature dominated RDA1, explaining over 90% of variance, while pH and solar radiation influenced RDA2, with taxa clustering along these environmental gradients (Fig. 6). This temperature dominance aligns with previous research establishing it as a master variable in marine microbial ecology [85, 110]. We selected these parameters after identifying significant discrepancies between laboratory-measured nutrient data and EPD-reported values, which precluded the use of nutrient parameters in our analysis.

Different taxonomic groups exhibited distinct thermal preferences corresponding to their seasonal dynamics. Among eukaryotes [111], Cryptophyta, Chlorophyta, and Stramenopiles (excluding Bacillariophyta and Oomycota) favoured lower temperatures, matching their highest abundance in January (Fig. 2A) and supporting recent thermal ecology findings [112, 113]. Conversely, Bacillariophyta and Dinophyta thrived in warmer conditions, consistent with studies on diatom thermal preferences [114, 115] and dinoflagellate responses to ocean warming [116, 117]. Interestingly, Cercozoa showed strong thermophilic tendencies despite lacking seasonal dominance, suggesting their optimal growth conditions may extend beyond our site’s parameters.

Prokaryotic communities demonstrated similar temperature-driven niche partitioning. Cyanobacteriota demonstrated clear thermophilic behaviour in both metaG and metaT datasets, corresponding to their increased summer abundance (Fig. 2B). Heterotrophic Kiritimatiellota, Planctomycetota, and Deltaproteobacteria also preferred warmer temperatures, while Candidatus Dadabacteria and archaeal Nitrososphaerota favoured colder conditions.

Viral families showed more subtle temperature responses compared to cellular organisms (Fig. 6), reflecting complex virus-host-environment interactions. Demerecviridae showed consistent association with higher temperatures across all datasets (metaG, metaV, and metaT) (Fig. 2C). Kyanoviridae demonstrated complex temperature-dependent behaviour: while abundant in metaG at high temperatures alongside their potential cyanobacterial host, they showed inverse patterns in the metaV and metaT datasets. Their elevated winter expression despite reduced host abundance (Fig. 2C) suggests a temperature-mediated infection strategy switch: favouring latent infections during warm periods despite abundant hosts, while pursuing active lytic infections in colder conditions. This hypothesis is supported by their cold-temperature preference in the metaV fraction, though confirmation requires targeted investigation of specific virus-host pairs.

Mamiellophyceae and associated Prasinovirus dynamics

The strong temperature-driven responses observed in our environmental analyses, coupled with correlations identified in our multi-omics and network analyses, motivated a focused investigation into the dynamics of Mamiellophyceae and their associated prasinoviruses. These groups, well-represented in genomic resources, were analysed using two complementary methodological approaches to characterise their temporal patterns: (1) an assembly-based method combining assembly, gene prediction, and DIAMOND annotation with Salmon abundance estimation (Supplementary Fig. S8A and C), and (2) direct reference genome mapping (Supplementary Fig. S8B and D).

Both approaches identified Micromonas (M. commoda and M. pusilla) as the dominant genus, but the assembly-based approach underestimated Ostreococcus, Bathycoccus, and Mantoniella abundances and showed less stable diel patterns. Additionally, a substantial proportion of operational taxonomic units (OTUs) were classified as “unclassified Mamiellales” (Supplementary Fig. S8A). In contrast, reference genome mapping provided complete species-level resolution (Supplementary Fig. S8B), enabling the detection of these genera with greater precision. Similar methodological disparities were observed in the analysis of prasinoviruses. The assembly-based approach left approximately 50% of viral contigs unclassified (Supplementary Fig. S8C). While both methods identified Bathycoccus viruses as the dominant Prasinovirus group, followed by Micromonas pusilla viruses, only the reference-based approach captured the ecological significance of Ostreococcus viruses, particularly OlV2 in cellular fractions. Furthermore, clear seasonal and diel patterns were detected for OlV2 and MpV-SP1 (Supplementary Fig. S8D) using reference mapping. Unexpectedly, the assembly-based approach detected freshwater species, including Monomastix sp. OKE-1 [118, 119], Yellowstone lake phycodnaviruses [120], and Dishui lake phycodnaviruses [121]. These detections likely reflect database annotation artifacts rather than true ecological presence, highlighting a limitation of assembly-based methods in marine datasets.

Each analytical approach had distinct strengths and limitations. The assembly-based methods excelled at uncovering novel diversity, particularly for less-characterised taxa, but struggled with resolving closely related species, such as Bathycoccus strains [122], and showed lower accuracy for well-characterised genera. In contrast, reference mapping provided superior resolution for known species and precise temporal patterns, but only for taxa with well-annotated genomes. These complementary strengths highlight the need for expanded genomic resources for marine microbial and viral communities. Improved reference genomes, particularly for understudied taxa like Mamiellophyceae and their prasinoviruses, are essential for achieving higher-resolution ecological insights in future multi-omics analyses.

Functional plasticity in marine microbial assemblages

To understand the functional dynamics underlying community structure and interactions, we analysed temporal expression patterns in metatranscriptomic ORFs. By combining DESeq2 and DP_GP_cluster, we examined the top 1000 most time-varying ORFs across all supergroups, uncovering distinct seasonal and diel expression patterns that complemented previous taxonomic and co-occurrence findings. Temporal analysis revealed 22 season-variable clusters with clear winter-summer differentiation and 19 timepoint-variable clusters with season-specific patterns, consistent with documented temporal fluctuations in marine microbial communities [14, 21, 123]. Clusters with contrasting expression patterns exhibited distinct functional compositions (Fig. 7). For instance, cluster 10 (Fig. 7B) showed high winter expression with strong diel variability, while cluster 16 (Fig. 7D) exhibited the opposite pattern. Cluster 16 was enriched in photosynthesis-related genes (82%), whereas cluster 10 contained less than 1%, aligning with studies linking photosynthesis gene expression to seasonal changes [124, 125]. While most highly variable clusters showed daytime expression dominance, some clusters (e.g., Cluster 10, Fig. 7B) exhibited nighttime expression peaks, indicating that our analysis captured the most variable genes which happen to be predominantly daytime-active, rather than suggesting an absence of nighttime gene expression in the community.

Photosynthesis-related genes demonstrated nuanced regulation beyond a simple summer–winter dichotomy. For example, cluster 2 (Fig. 7A) demonstrated enhanced expression of photosynthesis genes during winter, contrasting with the summer-dominated expression pattern in cluster 16 (Fig. 7D). This temporal partitioning suggests sophisticated seasonal regulation of photosynthetic processes, reflecting adaptation strategies of photosynthetic organisms to environmental changes. To validate these functional distinctions and uncover regulatory mechanisms, future studies should employ comprehensive functional enrichment techniques to compare cluster-specific gene compositions to community-wide background ratios. Such validation could reveal previously unrecognised seasonal adaptations in marine photosynthetic communities.

Focusing on highly time-variable clusters potentially overlooks relatively stable “core” genes, but this approach effectively revealed important patterns in community functional organisation. Large gene clusters exhibited coordinated expression shifts in response to environmental changes, while smaller clusters highlighted specialised functional niches. These findings align with studies identifying both core and variable gene expression patterns in marine microbial communities [126, 127]. Together, they underscore the complexity of microbial ecosystems, where stable core functions coexist with rapidly adapting specialised processes.

Dynamic gene expression in response to seasonal and diel cues

Functional annotation of metaG and metaT assemblies using FragGeneScan and MetaCerberus (KOFam-KEGG database) further characterised transcriptional responses to seasonal and diel cues. Principal Component Analysis (PCA) of the metaT dataset revealed distinct seasonal clustering along PC1, which explained 34.4% of the variance, while PC2 accounted for 9.8% (Supplementary Fig. S9). August samples formed a compact cluster indicative of more homogeneous summer expression, while January samples exhibited greater variability. Diel clustering of functional genes revealed intricate temporal patterns that deviated from strict timepoint groupings. Instead, expression patterns reflected both time-specific responses and gradual transitions between adjacent timepoints. For instance, photosynthesis-related genes exhibited strong diel rhythms, consistent with previous studies [128, 129], while other functional groups displayed more gradual responses to environmental changes. This interplay between rapid diel cycles and slower environmental drivers highlights the dynamic regulation of microbial gene expression.

Clustering of the top 30 most abundant genes based on their relative expression patterns (Fig. 8) revealed three distinct functional groups. The first cluster, dominated by energy metabolism genes, exhibited variable expression without clear temporal patterns, reflecting microbial adaptability to fluctuating energy demands. The second cluster contained genetic information processing genes, which displayed stable, low-level expression characteristic of housekeeping functions, consistent with their essential role in cellular maintenance [4]. The third cluster, composed predominantly of photosynthesis-associated genes, showed notable seasonal and diel patterns, including elevated expression during winter. The distinct expression behaviours of these clusters underscore the dual nature of microbial ecosystems, where stable core functions coexist with dynamic, environmentally responsive processes.

Among photosynthesis-associated genes, phycobilisome components displayed intriguing seasonal dynamics (data not shown in Fig. 8 as their relative expression was low throughout, instead shown separately in Supp. Fig. S11). Most components, such as allophycocyanin, phycocyanin, and phycocyanobilin, followed the expected summer-dominant expression patterns [4, 111], albeit mild. However, the phycoerythrin beta chain, a component found in cyanobacteria, red algae, and cryptophytes, exhibited unexpectedly elevated expression during winter. This contrasted not only with prior observations of higher summer expression [4, 111] but also with the phycoerythrin alpha chain, which followed the expected summer-dominant pattern. This divergence in seasonal expression between alpha and beta chains is surprising, given their typically coordinated roles in the protein complex [130]. The winter dominance of the beta chain may reflect either annotation issues or increased winter abundance of Cryptophyta, which are known to thrive under low-irradiance conditions [131]. These results suggest that phycoerythrin expression may be more nuanced than previously thought, reflecting both organismal adaptations and environmental influences.

Plastocyanin, a component primarily expressed by phototrophic protists, provided key insights into seasonal adaptations, despite falling below the abundance threshold for Fig. 8 (data not shown). Its modest but consistent elevation during winter (mean 1.4 versus summer mean 1.2) aligned with previous findings [111, 132] and reflected the seasonal dynamics of phototrophic protists, which are known to dominate colder waters. This winter-biased expression suggests a compensatory mechanism through which these organisms fine-tune their photosynthetic machinery to maintain efficiency under reduced light conditions, a characteristic trait of organisms adapting to low-irradiance environments.

Similarly, Photosystem II genes, critical components of oxygenic photosynthesis in both prokaryotic and eukaryotic photosynthesisers [133], exhibited striking temporal patterns, with significantly higher activity observed in January (Fig. 8). This winter dominance was not limited to the most abundant transcripts; 82% of the other 39 Photosystem I and II proteins also showed elevated winter expression, though their levels fell below the visualisation threshold. Such widespread upregulation of photosystem genes suggests a robust compensatory mechanism to sustain energy production in the euphotic zone, despite the lower levels of winter irradiation. These findings are consistent with previous observations [111, 134], supporting the hypothesis that photosynthetic organisms adjust their metabolic pathways to cope with seasonal reductions in light availability.

The diel regulation of Photosystem II genes during winter further highlighted the precision of their temporal control. The three most abundant genes within this category exhibited clear daily rhythms, with expression peaking at maximum irradiance (14:00; T1, T5, T9) and declining to troughs near dawn (08:00; T4, T8, shortly after sunrise at 07:05). These robust daily cycles, consistent with prior findings [124], indicate the fine-tuned regulation of photosynthetic machinery to optimise light utilisation during periods of variable irradiance.

Broader analysis of functional categories (Supplementary Fig. S10) reinforced the seasonal and diel patterns observed, with photosynthesis metabolism emerging as the most highly expressed category, showing pronounced winter dominance across all genes above the 500 TPM threshold. This functional group exhibited elevated winter expression while maintaining the diel rhythms seen in individual Photosystem II components, reflecting a sophisticated regulatory network governing photosynthetic processes. These findings highlight the temporal plasticity of marine microbial communities, showcasing their ability to optimise photosynthetic efficiency and sustain energy production across varying seasonal and daily conditions. Such adaptability underscores the critical role of environmental drivers in shaping microbial gene regulation and ecosystem functionality.

Conclusions

This study provides a comprehensive view of the temporal dynamics of microbial communities in the coastal South China Sea, with key insights into how cross-domain interactions and host-virus dynamics contribute to ecosystem stability and resilience. By integrating metagenomic, metatranscriptomic, and metaviromic data across diel and seasonal timescales, we uncovered a hierarchy of stability within microbial domains: eukaryotes exhibited the greatest temporal variability, prokaryotes showed intermediate dynamics, and viruses displayed remarkable stability. This exceptional stability in viral communities strongly supports the viral seed bank hypothesis, which posits that marine ecosystems harbour a persistent reservoir of diverse viral populations that buffer environmental fluctuations.

The observation of dynamic rewiring in microbial interactions, despite relatively stable taxonomic composition, highlights the critical role of interaction flexibility in maintaining marine ecosystem functionality. This "stability-flexibility paradox" suggests that microbial resilience in fluctuating environments depends less on taxonomic turnover and more on the capacity of microbial communities to reorganise their interactions in response to environmental changes. The synchronised seasonal and diel rhythms observed between Mamiellophyceae and prasinoviruses further underscore the importance of host-virus dynamics as central regulators of microbial community structure and function. Such tightly coupled interactions likely play a pivotal role in sustaining the productivity and stability of coastal ecosystems.

Our findings have significant implications for understanding the resilience of marine ecosystems in the face of global environmental change. The stability of viral populations, coupled with their ability to mediate host population dynamics, points to evolutionary and ecological mechanisms that buffer microbial communities against environmental variability. These mechanisms may serve as critical safeguards for ecosystem functionality in the context of increasing anthropogenic pressures, such as ocean warming and nutrient shifts.

While our community-level analysis revealed prominent patterns in photosynthesis-related gene expression, the complex dynamics of virus-host interactions, including host immunity responses, require more targeted investigation. Future studies focusing specifically on immunity-related pathways (such as CRISPR-Cas systems and restriction-modification mechanisms) would benefit from higher temporal resolution sampling, enrichment approaches, and strain-resolved analyses to capture the rapid molecular interactions that underlie the broader community patterns observed here.

By employing a multi-omics approach, we provide a robust framework for investigating microbial community dynamics across spatial and temporal scales. This study also highlights the value of integrating taxonomic, functional, and interaction data to uncover patterns that single-scale analyses might overlook. The insights presented here not only advance our understanding of the South China Sea microbiome but also offer a model for exploring microbial responses to environmental drivers in other coastal systems worldwide. More targeted future investigations of virus-host functional dynamics using the methods employed in this study will be invaluable in understanding specific microbial interactions and the impacts on the community at large. Particularly, detailed examination of host immunity gene expression patterns and their temporal coordination with viral activity cycles would provide crucial mechanistic insights into the stability-flexibility paradox observed in marine microbial communities. Integrating microbial dynamics into conservation strategies will be essential for predicting and mitigating the impacts of global change on marine biodiversity and ecosystem functionality.
