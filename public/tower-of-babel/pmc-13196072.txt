Rapid expansion of human brucellosis in Yunnan, China
Authors: Zhiguo Liu, Binbin Yu, Lihua Yang, Mengyao Sun, Xinqi Wang, Peng Wang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractHuman brucellosis has expanded continuously in Yunnan Province, China; however, its epidemiological dynamic remains unclear. In the present study, we used joinpoint regression and space–time cluster analyses to illustrate the epidemic profile of human brucellosis in Yunnan. From 2006 to 2024, a total of 7360 cases were reported; the incidence rate increased from 0.002/100,000 (n = 1) in 2006 to 4.526/100,000 (n = 2,124) in 2024. Human brucellosis exhibited two upward stages: a significantly increasing stage from 2006 to 2014 (annual percent change [APC]=76.04, p < 0.05) and another continuously rising stage from 2014 to 2024 (APC = 30.24). With 69.47% of cases (5,113/7,360) in male individuals and 30.53% in (2,247/7,360) female ones. Among the total, 83.72% (6,162/7,360) of cases were reported in farmers and fell into the age group 45–59 years (41.14%, 3028/7,360). Most cases were reported from May to August, and the disease showed a significant seasonal trend. Human brucellosis in all 16 cities of the province exhibited a significantly increasing trend; the range of average APC in the 16 cities was from 63.36 (p < 0.05) in Kunming to 162.75 in Yuxi (p < 0.05). The number of reported cases expanded from one county in 2006 to 101 counties in 2024. In 2024, the incidence in 53 counties was greater than 2.00/100,000, indicating that human brucellosis has become an endemic disease in Yunnan Province. Spatiotemporal scan analysis identified two high-rate clusters (designated I and II), with High-High cluster counties exhibiting a continuous east-to-west expansion across Yunnan. Introduction

Human brucellosis, caused by Brucella spp., is the world’s most widespread zoonosis. The disease has undergone substantial epidemiological shifts in recent decades owing to evolving sanitary measures, socioeconomic factors, political dynamics, and global trade patterns [1]. Brucella spp. are facultative intracellular pathogens capable of replicating within phagocytic cells, with humans serving as accidental hosts. Transmission occurs via ingestion, inhalation, or direct inoculation through conjunctival or cutaneous exposure [2]. The disease remains highly prevalent across Asia and Africa, particularly in West Asia (Syria, Iran), Central Asia (Kyrgyzstan), East Asia (Mongolia, China), and North/East Africa (Algeria, Kenya) [3]. Based on conservative estimates, the annual global incidence reaches 2.1 million cases, with most occurring in Africa and Asia. However, certain regions in the Americas and Europe remain concerning [4]. This zoonotic disease predominantly circulates among animal reservoirs, posing serious human health risks, especially in areas lacking proper sanitation, food safety protocols, and veterinary health care systems [5]. Human brucellosis typically presents with major clinical manifestations including fatigue, arthralgia, and pyrexia. This debilitating condition often results in nutritional metabolic dysregulation and immunosuppression [6].

During 2004–2018, a total of 524,980 brucellosis cases in humans were recorded in China, with a significantly higher average annual incidence in 2012–2018 versus 2004–2011 [7]. The proportion of brucellosis cases reported in southern China nearly tripled from 2.0% (4,310 cases) in 2019 to 5.38% (10,363 cases) in 2023, indicating a large southern expansion of disease transmission [8]. Human brucellosis in Yunnan Province (southwest China) has exhibited continual growth since its initial detection in 2006, with annual cases surging to 2124 (4.526/100,000 population) in 2024, indicating worsening endemicity. Human brucellosis represents a serious and growing public health threat in the region; yet, critical gaps persist in understanding the spatiotemporal dynamics and epidemiological progression of this disease. In this comprehensive study, we systematically characterized the disease’s evolutionary trajectory across Yunnan Province from 2006 to 2024, generating evidence-based insights to inform targeted surveillance strategies and precision public health interventions.

Methods

Data acquisition, processing, and visualization

Surveillance data on human brucellosis cases and corresponding incidence rates for Yunnan Province (2006–2024) were procured from the Yunnan Provincial Disease Control and Prevention Information System. Human brucellosis cases diagnosis followed the updated Guidelines for the Diagnosis of Human Brucellosis (WS 269–2019) issued by the National Health Commission of China [9]. Briefly, a SAT titer ≥ 1:100 in combination with compatible clinical manifestations and epidemiological history was considered confirmatory. Subsequent processing and analysis using Excel 2021 were focused on delineating occupational, gender, and age distribution patterns, alongside characterizing temporal trends via reported cases and weekly incidence. The marked upsurge initiating in 2019 and culminating in 2024 warranted a dedicated examination of the geographic distribution for these 2 years, visualized at the county level using ArcGIS 10.7. Moreover, a comparative assessment integrating livestock farming statistics (current through 2022) with disease incidence (2008–2022) was executed.

Joinpoint regression analysis of human brucellosis from 2006 to 2024

To delineate significant inflection points in human brucellosis incidence (2006–2024), we examined the data using joinpoint regression analysis, consistent with conventional analytical frameworks [10]. Incidence was calculated in units per 100,000 person-years by a direct standardization method, and their long-term change trends from 2006 to 2024 were analyzed using the Joinpoint Regression Program (v5.2.0.0) (National Cancer Institute, Bethesda, MD, USA). The model automatically determined the optimal number of inflection points, annual rate of change (APC), and average annual rate of change (AAPC), and 95% confidence intervals were calculated. Excel 2021 was used to organize the data. Statistical significance for all tests was set at p < 0.05.

Retrospective space‒time analysis scanning analysis

To detect high-risk spatiotemporal clusters, we employed Kulldorff’s scan statistic using SaTScan software (v10.1.3) [11]. Input files including case counts, population data, and geographic coordinates (county centroids) were formatted as comma-separated values. Numerous studies have explored optimal parameter selection for scan statistics, with a consensus emerging that the geographical scope of any single detected cluster should be constrained to no more than 15% of the total number of administrative units within the study area [12, 13]. In accordance with prior epidemiological evidence from the region, a discrete Poisson model was utilized to detect spatiotemporal clusters, the analytical framework incorporated parameter constraints limiting the maximum spatial cluster size to 10% of the population at risk and the maximum temporal window to 30% of the study period [14]. Human brucellosis in Yunnan exhibits a distinct seasonal peak from May to July, which directly informed our parameter selection by setting the maximum temporal scan window to 3 months (30% of the annual cycle) for each year. Statistically significant clusters were identified and ranked based on the log-likelihood ratio (LLR) test [15]. To assess significance, we conducted 999 Monte Carlo simulations, rejecting the null hypothesis of random spatiotemporal distribution for clusters with p < 0.05. The scanning window exhibiting the maximum log-likelihood ratio (LLR) is identified as the most likely cluster, while additional windows achieving statistical significance are classified as secondary clusters.

Results

Epidemic overview of human brucellosis from 2006 to 2024

From 2006 to 2024, surveillance data revealed 7360 recorded cases of human brucellosis, yielding an average annual incidence of 0.82 cases per 100,000 population (387.37 cases/year). In the most recent year (2024) alone, 2124 cases were reported. Joinpoint regression analysis revealed two distinct epidemiological phases: a rapid growth period during 2006–2014 (APC = 76.04, p < 0.01), followed by a sustained elevation phase from 2014 to 2024 (APC = 30.24) (Fig. 1 and Table 1A). The number of reported human brucellosis cases rose continually from one case in 2006 to 2124 cases in 2024, corresponding to an increase in the incidence rate from 0.0022/100,000 to 4.53/100,000. The average number of cases (incidence (/100,000)) annually in the two stages was 30 (0.06/100,000) and 709 (1.50/100,000), respectively. Based on the AAPC, the disease increased continually from 2006 to 2024 (AAPC = 48.90, p < 0.01; Table 1B). Based on the incidence, the disease epidemic exhibited a sporadic nature prior to 2020, with an annual incidence rate below 0.79/100,000. However, the disease became endemic from 2022 to 2024, with an annual incidence rate greater than 2.0/100,000. These data suggest that the human brucellosis epidemic in Yunnan has undergone a significant change from sporadic shifts to endemic disease. The sheep inventory increased sharply from 843.27/10,000 in 2008 to 1600.35/10,000 in 2016 and then fluctuated and declined to 1371.89/10,000 in 2022 (Fig. S1).

Gender, occupation, age, and time distribution profiles of human brucellosis

Among 7360 cases comprising 5113 (69.47%) in male individuals and 2247 (30.53%) in female ones, the sex ratio was 2.28:1. The incidence by sex exhibited obvious differences over time based on the joinpoint regression analysis. In male individuals, incidence increased substantially from 2006 to 2015 (APC = 65.04, p < 0.01), with subsequent growth continuing at a slower rate from 2015 to 2024 (APC = 29.73, p < 0.01). Similarly, female incidence also showed a rising trend, consisting of two distinct phases: a steep initial increase from 2007 to 2011 (APC = 239.76, p < 0.01), followed by a more gradual but sustained rise from 2011 to 2024 (APC = 37.49, p < 0.01). (Fig. 2A). These data suggest that the number of cases in women has increased significantly. In terms of occupation, at least 15 different careers were involved, with the most cases reported in farmers, accounting for 83.72% (6,162/7,360), followed by 237 in students (3.22%, 237/7,360) and 158 in homemakers (2.15%, 158/7,360); the fewest cases were reported among teachers (n = 10, 10/7,630) (Fig. 2B). Cases were distributed in the age range of 0–85 years, with most cases reported in the age group 50–54 (n = 1,176, 15.98%), followed by 1003 (13.15%) in the age group 55–59 years and 849 (11.13%) in the group aged 45–49 years. Notably, 195 cases were reported in children younger than age 10 years (Fig. 2C and Table S1). Based on the month, most cases were reported in July, followed by June and May. The epidemic of this disease showed seasonal characteristics, peaking in May–August (I), with additional peaks in March, April, and September–November (II); the fewest reported cases were in January, February, and December (III) (Fig. 2D).

Family cluster cases and delayed diagnosis of cases

Between 2012 and 2024, 512 brucellosis cases were identified within family clusters, of which 43 were pediatric. The first report of two cases from the same family was in 2012, and the number of affected families increased from one family in 2012 to 75 families in 2024; the corresponding number of cases increased from two cases in 2012 to 153 cases in 2024. The ratio range for the number of cases related to a family cluster to the number of annual reported cases was 3.51%–10.78%, and the sex ratio was 1:1. Most cases were reported in farmers aged 40–50 years. These data imply that family cluster infections are a potential risk factor for an increased number of human brucellosis cases among children.

A total of 7561 case investigation forms were completed from 2006 to 2024; the median diagnostic interval between reported cases was 12.0 days. The median diagnostic interval was 50 days for the first case in 2006; this decreased gradually to 11 days in 2024. The duration of the maximum diagnostic interval was 2716 days, and the minimum diagnostic interval was 7 days (Table S2). These data suggest that delays in the diagnosis of human brucellosis cases have decreased. However, related publicity and education targeting high-risk populations should be strengthened.

Geographic distribution profile of human brucellosis from 2006 to 2024

Reported cases were distributed across all 16 cities of Yunan Province. The most cases were reported in Qujing City (n = 2,071), followed by 2037 in Kunming City and 1195 in Honghe Hani and Yi Autonomous Prefecture. The range of the number of cases throughout the remaining cites was from five cases in Dehong Dai and Jingpo Autonomous Prefecture to 591 cases in Zhaotong City. The highest average annual incidence was in Qujing City (1.87/100,000), followed by 1.38/100,000 in Honghe Hani and Yi Autonomous Prefecture and 1.33/100, 000 in Kunming City. The incidence rate in other cities ranged from 0.02/100,000 in Dehong Dai and Jingpo Autonomous Prefecture to 0.92/100,000 in Yuxi City. On the county level, the number of counties with reported cases increased from one county in 2006 to 49 counties in 2019 (Fig. 3A), and this eventually expanded to 101 counties in 2024 (out of 129) in 2024 (Fig. 3B). The incidence rate in 40 counties was less than 2.00/100,000 in 2019, and the incidence in nine counties was greater than 2.00/100,000; only one county had an incidence of 28.42/100,000 (Fig. 3A). By contrast, the incidence in 53 counties was greater than 2.00/100,000 in 2024, and the incidence in 16 counties was greater than 10.00/100,000, with the highest incidence being 76.80/100,000 (Fig. 3B). The most cases (n = 928) were reported in Shilin Yizu Autonomous County, followed by 881 cases in Luliang County and 425 cases in Luxi County, corresponding to average annual incidence rates of 19.91/100,000, 7.76/100,000, and 5.60/100,000, respectively. The range of the number of cases in other counties was from 1 to 400 cases (Table S2). These findings document the progressive county-level spread and transition of human brucellosis into an endemic disease across Yunnan Province, underscoring the urgent need for adapted prevention and control measures.

Joinpoint regression analysis for the incidence rate of human brucellosis from 2006 to 2024

Joinpoint regression analysis revealed that human brucellosis in all 16 cities exhibited a significantly increasing trend; the range of AAPC in the 16 cities was from 63.36 in Kunming to 162.75 in Yuxi (p < 0.05, 14 cities) (Table 2). There were three joinpoints in Chuxiong and Wenshan, with a significant increasing trend from 2015 to 2024 (APC = 13.6598, p < 0.05) and from 2017 to 2024 (APC = 15.31, p < 0.05), respectively. This was followed by two joinpoints in Qujing, Yuxi, Baoshan, Puer, Lincang, Honghe, Xishuangbanna, Dali, Nujiang, and Diqing. Zero joinpoints were found in four cities, namely, Kunming (APC = 63.37, p < 0.05), Zhaotong (APC = 159.99, p < 0.05), Dehong (APC = 62.07, p < 0.05), and Lijiang (APC = 122.66, p < 0.05) (Table 2). The observed geographic expansion of human brucellosis cases likely reflects the translocation of infected livestock, highlighting critical gaps in animal movement controls and the urgent need for enhanced veterinary surveillance systems.

Spatiotemporal cluster analysis of human brucellosis from 2006 to 2024

Based on retrospective spatiotemporal analysis using SaTScan v10.1 with a discrete Poisson model, nine statistically significant brucellosis clusters (p < 0.05) were identified under the parameter constraints of a maximum spatial cluster size of 10% of the population at risk and a maximum temporal window of 30% of the study period. The primary cluster (Cluster 1), exhibiting the strongest epidemiological association, encompassed seven counties—Luxi, Shizong, Shilin, Mile, Qiubei, Luliang, and Yiliang during the 2020–2024 timeframe. This cluster recorded 2448 observed cases, approximately 19.7 times the expected number, with a relative risk (RR) of 29.23, p < 0.001, indicating exceptionally high transmission intensity (Fig. 4). The secondary clusters (Clusters 2–9), characterized by varying spatiotemporal patterns and relative risks ranging from 2.80 to 8.56, were predominantly concentrated in the post-2020 period. These included regions such as Huize, Dongchuan, Qiaojia, Xuanwei, Zhanyi, Ludian, and Xundian (Cluster 2); Suijiang, Shuifu, Yanjin, Daguan, Yongshan, Yiliang, Weixin, and Zhaoyang (Cluster 3); Yongren, Dayao, Yuanmou, Huaping, Yao’an, Wuding, Mouding, YongSheng, Binchuan, Xiangyun, Luquan, Lufeng, Nanhua, Heqing, and Fumin (Cluster 4); Hongta, Jiangchuan, Jinning, Eshan, Tonghai, Huaning, Yimen, Anning, and Chengjiang (Cluster 5). These findings reveal significant spatiotemporal clustering of brucellosis in specific geographic regions of central, eastern, and northern Yunnan, providing critical evidence for the implementation of targeted regional control strategies.

Discussion

In this study, we used comprehensive epidemiological data including reported cases and incidence rates, as well as joinpoint regression and space–time cluster analyses, to elucidate the evolving trends of human brucellosis in Yunnan Province. Between 2006 and 2024, a total of 7360 cases were reported, with the incidence rate rising markedly from 0.002/100,000 (n = 1) in 2006 to 4.526/100,000 (n = 2,124) in 2024. This upward trend in Yunnan reflects the national re-emergence of human brucellosis as a major public health threat in China since 1995, which peaked around 2014. Historically confined to northern pasturelands, the disease has progressively expanded geographically, first to adjacent agro-pastoral zones, and more recently to southern coastal and southwestern regions like Yunnan. [16]. The period from 2005 to 2021 witnessed a sustained nationwide rise in human brucellosis incidence across China. This uptrend was marked by particularly accelerated growth in the southern, central, and southwestern parts of the country, driving a progressive southward migration of the disease’s geographic center from its historical northern endemic bases toward southwestern China [17]. Analysis of national incidence data using joinpoint regression revealed significant upward trends during the period 2019–2023 in 29 provinces. Among these, the southern regions of Guizhou, Yunnan, Sichuan, Jiangxi, and Guangxi demonstrated the most conspicuous acceleration in case numbers [8]. The present study corroborates previous analyses indicating that the rising incidence of human brucellosis in Yunnan is primarily facilitated by the introduction of infected cattle and sheep. This is largely a consequence of insufficient quarantine protocols amid the struggle to keep pace with the burgeoning livestock economy [18]. Contributing factors encompass environmental conditions, animal husbandry practices, herd size, livestock translocation, and rising lamb/beef consumption [3]. A molecular investigation of Brucella strains from Zhejiang Province showed that the introduction of infected sheep led to a brucellosis outbreak/epidemic, eventually causing multiple laboratory infection events [19]. The accelerating spread of human brucellosis cases across Yunnan suggests that severe animal Brucella infections may be prevalent, necessitating enhanced zoonotic disease surveillance and innovative control strategies.

Demographic analysis identified a clear predominance of cases among male individuals (69.47%) and individuals working within the farming sector (83.72%), with the highest burden among those aged 45–59 years (41.14%). This pattern strongly implicates frequent, direct contact with livestock as the core driver of transmission. Supporting this, existing evidence confirms that key risk factors for brucellosis encompass occupational exposure, contact with aborted animals, and ingestion of high-risk foods like unpasteurized milk and raw cheese [20]. During the period 2012–2016, a total of 244,348 human brucellosis cases were identified in China’s notifiable disease system, demonstrating significant age-specific variation. Analysis of age-stratified incidence rates showed the highest disease occurrence among individuals aged 40–65 years, suggesting this economically active population has elevated zoonotic exposure through livestock-related activities [21]. The disease demonstrates strong seasonality, with case numbers beginning to rise in early spring, peaking in May, and maintaining elevated levels throughout summer—a pattern demanding seasonally targeted control measures [22]. Jiangsu Province (southeastern China) reported 1347 brucellosis cases during 2006–2021, with significant age stratification (69.72% occurring among those aged 40–69 years). Surveillance data revealed both a sustained upward temporal trend and marked seasonality, with consistent annual peaks from April to June [23]. Therefore, a targeted intervention program for high-risk populations during seasons with high incidence is important to improve prevention awareness.

In the present study, the number of family related cases increased from 2 in 2012 to 153 in 2024, and the number of affected families increased from 1 in 2012 to 75 in 2024. A previous report showed that four family cluster cases from a common source, together with an epidemiological investigation, demonstrated a link between the four human cases and a herd of imported goats [24]. Household members commonly consume shared dairy products, attend to infected animals, have similar habits, and share living conditions; therefore, families are at risk of contracting brucellosis [25]. This result underscores that screening household members of index cases and individuals who live near infected household members is an efficient approach to improve the detection of brucellosis exposure.

Spatiotemporal cluster analysis revealed a significant spatial expansion of high-risk areas from central to northwestern Yunnan, evidenced by a substantial increase in the number of affected counties. This finding situates Yunnan within the larger national context, where the period 2019–2023 is defined by stable, high-risk foci in northern China and the concurrent emergence of new, secondary clusters in the south, indicating a notable geographic penetration of the disease [8]. Further, Yunnan borders Sichuan and Guizhou provinces and Guangxi Zhuang Autonomous Region, where human brucellosis is prevalent, and a considerable number of Brucella strains have been isolated from the abovementioned regions. Genotyping analysis has identified northern China-prevalent sequence types and multiple locus variable-number tandem repeat analysis (MLVA) profiles among Sichuan’s B. melitensis isolates, providing molecular evidence for interprovincial transmission via livestock movement [26]. MLVA-16 genotypes of Brucella strains shared between Guizhou and multiple provinces (Inner Mongolia, Xinjiang, Guangdong, Fujian, Qinghai, Shaanxi, and Yunnan) indicate interregional transmission via movement of infected animals or animal products. Without effective animal brucellosis control, human infections may continue escalating in this region.

Human brucellosis incidence is closely linked to intensive livestock production systems, where the clustering of farms acts as a key driver of elevated zoonotic transmission risk [27]. Key determinants influencing brucellosis spread include livestock demographics, animal-derived product yields, regional pastoral economics, and climate patterns (temperature, humidity, and seasonal fluctuations) [28]. This study calls for the immediate implementation of an integrated control strategy to counter the growing threat of human brucellosis. The strategy must simultaneously enforce stricter movement controls on livestock from high-risk counties, deploy compulsory ring vaccination in areas with persistent high-incidence clusters to interrupt transmission at its source, and foster cross-regional data sharing for coordinated surveillance and early warning. By linking these components, we can develop a proactive defense system to contain the epidemic in Yunnan and mitigate its spillover risk to adjacent areas.

Although this study delineates the epidemiological expansion of human brucellosis, the interpretations are constrained by data availability. The case counts likely reflect, rather than fully capture, the actual incidence owing to the inherent limitations of passive reporting systems. Furthermore, in the absence of integrated data on key risk factors such as animal movements and environmental correlates, the precise drivers behind the geographic spread and cluster formation of this disease remain less clear. These aspects should be considered when translating the findings into targeted interventions.

Conclusion

The human brucellosis epidemic in Yunnan Province has exhibited a pronounced upward trend and extensive geographic dissemination, according to our analysis of its incidence, distribution, and demographic patterns. Cases have risen sharply, primarily involving male individuals, farmers, and people aged 45–59 years. The number of affected counties surged from 1 in 2006 to 101 in 2024, confirming the disease’s endemic status in the region. This situation calls for comprehensive control strategies. Whole-genome sequencing of local Brucella strains could deliver key evidence for pinpointing infection sources.
