Drosophila melanogaster mitigates gastro-oral infections by stimulating pathogen expulsion
Authors: Shreya Verma, Sushovan Bhattacharyya, Meghana Tare, Sandhya Amol Marathe
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundFood-borne pathogens, particularly belonging to the Enterobacteriaceae family, are a major cause of gastrointestinal infections in humans. While Drosophila melanogaster has been widely explored to study antimicrobial responses, most studies rely on septic injury or direct injection routes that bypass the gut. A coordinated set of antimicrobial defenses acts to counteract the invading bacteria, with some variations depending on the entry route into the host. Herein, we tracked the dynamics of Enterobacteriaceae pathogens in Drosophila using a natural infection route.ResultsMost bacterial species were cleared within 48 h post-infection (hpi). We did not observe any significant mortality, indicating robust infection control. At 4 hpi, a substantial increase in reactive oxygen species (ROS) production was observed, followed by a decrease at 24 hpi and a resurgence at 48 hpi. Background

In view of the vast research conducted on host-pathogen interactions, the mechanisms underlying variations in the individual responses to similar Gram-negative bacterial infections remain less understood. To explore this, we utilized Drosophila melanogaster as a host model organism to understand the immune responses that are coordinated and regulated at the whole organism level. The increasing use of Drosophila in the studies of infectious diseases and control is due to its degree of genetic and molecular conservation with vertebrates, particularly in innate immune cascades, transduction pathways, and transcriptional regulators [1]. Moreover, it has been used to study the pathogenesis of a wide variety of microorganisms, as it also serves as a model of low microbiome complexity [2].

To combat bacterial infections, Drosophila relies solely on its innate immune system, as it is devoid of adaptive immune response for its defense [3]. The innate responses are categorized as cellular and humoral, where cellular responses are phagocytosis and encapsulation, while the humoral responses include the activation of evolutionarily conserved immune response cascades, the immune deficiency (IMD), and Toll pathways [4]. In cellular responses, hemocytes produce reactive oxygen species (ROS) during bacterial infections. The major source of ROS is nicotinamide adenine dinucleotide phosphate (NADPH) oxidases (Nox) [5] and dual oxidases (Duox) derived from hemocytes [6]. In Drosophila, ROS production as early as 30 min after pathogen ingestion is a conserved response to infection. However, ROS can have a destructive impact on gut mucosal cells; therefore, its activity must be tightly regulated to minimize host tissue injury [7]. While many previous studies have highlighted the role of ROS in the gut beyond their antibacterial activity, emerging evidence suggests that ROS can modulate gut functions [8].

The ROS produced in response to bacterial infection triggers visceral spasms mediated by Transient Receptor Potential cation channel A1 (TRPA1). These visceral spasms induce intestinal contractions, expediting bacterial elimination from the host [9]. The interaction between oxidative stress and the mechanosensory pathway seems to be conserved, as Drosophila TRPA1 is a homolog of mammalian TRP receptors responsive to stressful conditions [10]. For infection studies, usually flies are infected with bacteria, either through the septic injury method, where bacteria are directly introduced into the body cavity, bypassing the epithelial barriers; or, through the oral infection route, where bacteria are fed to the flies. A few pathogens, such as Serratia marcescens (S. marcescens), Escherichia coli (E. coli), Salmonella enterica subspecies enterica serovar Typhimurium (S. Typhimurium), and Enterobacter cloacae (E. cloacae), have been extensively studied in Drosophila using a septic injury model to assess the biological responses encompassing survival rates, change in gut morphology, alterations in triglyceride levels, activation of IMD pathway, and bacterial load [11–13]. However, this does not represent the gut-specific immunocompetence, relevant to the natural route of infection. To address this gap, we developed a Drosophila oral infection model in this study by feeding bacterial suspensions to the flies. This natural infection model enabled us to track the fate of orally ingested bacteria and characterize the gut immune responses to specific Gram-negative bacteria, including E. coli, S. Typhimurium, Klebsiella pneumoniae (K. pneumoniae), and E. cloacae.

Our study aims to investigate how Drosophila mounts distinct immune responses to specific pathogens when infected orally. Our results indicate that bacterial expulsion in Drosophila oral infection model is mediated by ROS and TRPA1. We observe that the interplay between oxidative stress and sensory receptor orchestrates a pathogen-specific gut strategy, minimizing gut damage while clearing the bacteria.

Results

Infection of Drosophila with different bacteria caused species-specific colonization and clearance

Traditionally, Drosophila culture is also supplemented with preservatives like Methyl Paraben (Nipagin) and Propionic acid to ensure healthy conditions for the cultures. During oral infection studies, these preservatives may affect the results in different ways. To ensure that these antimicrobial agents do not impact the bacterial survival and clearance, we tested the antibacterial activity of these agents specifically against the pathogens used in the study. The results indicated that these agents, at a concentration generally added to fly food, exhibited antibacterial activity against E. coli, S. Typhimurium, K. pneumoniae, and E. cloacae (Supplementary file 1). Thus, in all further infection experiments, the flies were fed on the food without adding these agents.

The presence of bacteria and the duration of their presence in Drosophila were monitored using fluorescence microscopy and colony forming unit (CFU) analysis after feeding the flies RFP-/m-cherry-tagged bacteria. We observed strong red fluorescence in the fly’s abdomen (Fig. 1A and B) and high CFU (106-107/fly) in the flies immediately after bacterial feeding (Fig. 1C), indicating bacterial ingestion by the flies. Within 24 h post-infection (hpi), the CFU reduced to ~ 104 CFU/fly in the flies fed with E. coli, a non-pathogenic species to Drosophila [14] (Fig. 1C). This indicates efficient (~ 99.7% killing) elimination of E. coli by the Drosophila. Similar results were not obtained with all pathogenic bacteria. Within 24 hpi, bacterial numbers remained sufficiently high in the fly body (Fig. 1C) - S. Typhimurium (~ 5 × 105 CFU/fly), K. pneumoniae (~ 5 × 106 CFU/fly) and E. cloacae (~ 108 CFU/fly). Compared to the ingested amount, the S. Typhimurium numbers dropped by ~ 99% (5 × 107 to 4.45 × 105 CFU/fly), K. pneumoniae numbers dropped by 96.3% (1.5 × 108 to 5.5 × 106 CFU/fly), while those of E. cloacae increased by 4.8% (3.45 × 107 to 1.67 × 108). By 48 and 72 hpi, the numbers of K. pneumoniae and S. Typhimurium dropped close to those of E. coli (> 99% clearance of ingested numbers from the fly body). However, for E. cloacae, bacterial numbers decreased gradually and remained higher than those of any other bacteria up to 72 hpi (Fig. 1C). We also performed a CFU assay at 15 days post-infection (dpi), at which point no bacteria were recovered from any infection group, indicating complete clearance of all the pathogens.

Despite the presence of E. cloacae (3.7 × 105 CFU/fly) in Drosophila and other bacteria (103 CFU/fly) till 72 hpi, the survivability of infected flies was not compromised up to 30 dpi (Supplementary file 2). This suggested that oral bacterial infection was efficiently controlled by the host’s innate immune responses.

Bacterial infection triggers host intestinal ROS production in a dynamic pattern

It has been previously reported that ROS production is stimulated by natural bacterial infection through the expression of Nox and Duox enzymes, which help clear the pathogen in Drosophila [15]. Therefore, the ROS production was examined in the fly gut and the carcass (whole body minus the dissected gut), accounting for localized and systemic oxidative stress, respectively. ROS levels in the fly gut were found to be elevated promptly after the infection (after 4 hpi) across all infection groups (Fig. 2A), suggesting rapid immune sensing. However, at 24 hpi, the ROS levels in pathogen-fed flies were species-specific, remaining unchanged in some and decreasing in others in the gut (Fig. 2B). Nevertheless, by 48 hpi, ROS resurge was observed in the guts for all pathogen-infected flies, with E. cloacae showing the least among the pathogens tested (Fig. 2C). Flies infected with E. coli did not exhibit any surge at either 4 hpi or at 48 hpi, underscoring that ROS activation depends upon bacterial pathogenicity. Notably, no significantly enhanced levels were observed in the carcass, indicating the localized immune response. We next assessed the induction of Nox and Duox transcript levels in the fly guts. The expression data aligns with the ROS data. At 24 hpi, Nox levels are unaffected in the guts of K. pneumoniae- and S. Typhimurium-fed flies. In E. coli- and E. cloacae-fed flies, it was downregulated by 2.4- and 3.04-fold, respectively (Fig. 2D). However, Duox levels in S. Typhimurium-, E. coli-, and E. cloacae-fed flies were unaffected, while in K. pneumoniae-fed flies, it was upregulated by ~ 2-fold. By 48 hpi, Duox were upregulated by 2-3-fold in all infected groups (Fig. 2E), while the Nox levels were 1.5-2 fold high only in the S. Typhimurium and K. pneumoniae-fed flies, consistent with ROS production in the gut (Fig. 2B and C). The data suggest that Duox levels are induced to a greater extent than Nox levels, suggesting that Duox may be the major contributor to ROS production. Altogether, these results demonstrate that infection by these bacteria triggers ROS production in the fly gut primarily through Duox activation, and to a lesser extent via Nox.

To further confirm the role of ROS in bacterial clearance from the host, we performed a survival assay in flies having ubiquitously silenced Duox by RNA interference (Actin-Gal-4 > DuoxIR). We found that the survival of Duox knockdown flies infected with the pathogens remains unaltered (Supplementary file 3) as compared to their respective control flies (Actin-Gal-4 > UAS-GFP).

Enteric infection in Drosophila did not compromise gut barrier integrity

ROS is known to impair the enterocytes, affecting the intestinal integrity. Thus, the impact of altered ROS on the intestinal integrity of infected flies was monitored using a Smurf assay. The Smurf phenotype is characterized by the leakage of an ingested blue dye from the gut lumen into the body cavity resulting in a whole-body blue coloration and indicating loss of gut barrier integrity. Breaching of gut integrity has been shown to be closely associated with disruption of intestinal homeostasis [16]. None of the infected flies exhibited Smurf phenotype and the barrier integrity remained intact (Fig. 3A-F). We next assessed their ability to clear the dye from their gastrointestinal tract. All the flies cleared the dye from their gut, with no residual dye visible in their abdomens (Fig. 3B′-F′), illustrating the proper gut functioning and intestinal motility.

TRPA1 mediates the bacterial expulsion of most of the ingested bacteria

As ROS showed dynamic changes without Smurf phenotype, we hypothesized that bacterial clearance involves ROS along with other mechanisms and therefore tried to understand how ROS may influence intestinal contractions involved in bacterial expulsion. Previous studies show that TRPA1 in enteroendocrine cells links ROS to bacterial shedding through ROS-induced defecation [17] TRPA1, activated by stimuli such as ROS, regulates intestinal muscle activity in Drosophila after bacterial ingestion [9, 18].

To test our hypothesis, we quantified TRPA1 expression in the infected fly guts at 4 hpi, 24 hpi, 36 hpi and 48 hpi (Fig. 4A–C and Supplementary file 4). TRPA1 was upregulated at 4 hpi in the guts of S. Typhimurium and E. cloacae-infected flies by 1.9 and 1.4-fold, respectively, while it remained uninduced in the guts of K. pneumoniae-infected flies (Supplementary file 4). At 24 hpi, the induction was more significant, with 3.4-, 2.5-, and 3.3-fold increases in E. coli-, S. Typhimurium-, and E. cloacae-infected flies, respectively (Fig. 4A), whereas with K. pneumoniae, induction was insignificant (1.2-fold). By 36 hpi, TRPA1 induction in E. coli-, S. Typhimurium-, K. pneumoniae-, and E. cloacae-infected flies was 4.72-, 7.0-, 1.69-, and 3.5-fold, respectively. But induction in K. pneumoniae- and E. cloacae-infected flies was insignificant (Fig. 4B). However, at 48 hpi, it remained unchanged in all infected groups, though in pathogen-infected flies it showed a trend of downregulation (Fig. 4C).

The sustained upregulation of TRPA1 could promote intestinal contraction, facilitating bacterial expulsion [9]. Therefore, we assessed the bacteria shed by the infected flies using CFU analysis at every 12 h starting at 24 hpi up to 48 hpi (Fig. 4D). In accord with TRPA1 expression, we observed that all three pathogens were shed by the infected flies, though we did not observe a temporal correlation between bacterial shedding and TRPA1 expression. Notably, although TRPA1 expression was induced in E. coli-infected flies, no colonies were recovered in the shedding experiment, whereas in K. pneumoniae-infected flies, the bacteria were shed without sufficient TRPA1 induction.

Further, we examined the intestinal width of infected flies using light microscopy and observed a reduction in gut width at 24 hpi (Supplementary file 5 A-C). Compared to the uninfected control, the S. Typhimurium- and E. cloacae-infected flies showed a 1.2-fold decrease in gut width, while K. pneumoniae-infected flies showed a 1.14-fold decrease (Supplementary file 5 C). As expected, the gut width of E. coli-infected flies was not significantly different from the uninfected fly gut width.

To further substantiate our results, we assessed TRPA1 expression and bacterial shedding at 24 hpi in the Duox knockdown flies. A significant reduction in TRPA1 expression was observed in Duox knockdown flies infected with S. Typhimurium (~ 4.8 fold down), and E. cloacae (~ 6.5 fold down) as compared to the infected control (Actin-GFP) flies (Fig. 4E). TRPA1 expression in K. pneumoniae-infected Duox knockdown flies was down, but the difference was not statistically significant. At 24 hpi, compared with infected-control (Actin-GFP) flies, bacterial shedding in infected-Duox knockdown flies was ~ 3-fold and ~ 1.6-fold lower for E. cloacae and K. pneumoniae infection, respectively, but it was not significantly different for the S. Typhimurium-infected group (Fig. 4F). Notably, over the next 12 h (from 24 hpi to 36 hpi), the bacterial shedding substantially reduced in S. Typhimurium- and E. cloacae-infected Duox knockdown flies compared to infected Actin-GFP control flies (Fig. 4G). The shedding was 190- and 27.3-times less in S. Typhimurium- and E. cloacae-infected flies, respectively, but was statistically insignificant for the S. Typhimurium-infected group (Fig. 4G).

Thus, our data collectively suggest that Duox-mediated TRPA1 regulation may facilitate intestinal contractions that expel bacteria, demonstrating that a combination of ROS and gut motility is involved in bacterial clearance.

Discussion

In this study, we employed an ecologically relevant natural feeding route in the Drosophila melanogaster infection model to investigate the gut defense response against a diverse spectrum of Gram-negative bacteria. Here, we highlighted that Drosophila mounts a specific response depending upon the bacterial species it encounters. Unlike previous studies [3, 19], we excluded antimicrobial additives from the fly food to ensure infection outcomes precisely reflect host–pathogen interactions. We demonstrated that the induction of ROS in response to pathogens possibly leads to TRPA1-mediated intestinal contractions, expelling the ingested bacteria. Our work further emphasizes the importance of gut contractions as a non-immune mechanism, beyond their well-established functions like aiding the efficient nutrient absorption and regulating feeding behavior [20].

Our observation of gradual bacterial clearance (> 99% of ingested numbers) within two days of infection highlights the efficiency of the Drosophila innate immune system and Duox/TRPA1 pathway in resolving infection. This corroborates the findings of an earlier study [21] where it was reported that the number of flies with no CFU increased over time after feeding on Pseudomonas entomophila. Our study shows that clearance and persistent infections are both probable outcomes of the bacterial infection. We observed that E. coli, S. Typhimurium, and K. pneumoniae were effectively cleared from the fly body by 48 hpi. However, E. cloacae was not cleared from the fly body until 72 hpi (105 CFU/fly) without impairing host homeostasis, with the highest bacterial burden in the infected flies and lowest ROS induction at 48 hpi. These results are in congruence with the reported findings that E. cloacae is characterized by lower virulence, which likely contributes to its ability to maintain stable colonization by avoiding significant harm to the host [22].

Notably, we observed that the enteric pathogens used in our study did not compromise the gut barrier integrity and hence, could not establish systemic infection. However, previous studies have reported that bacteria like S. marcescens, when ingested, enter the body cavity but do not kill their host rapidly, unlike in septic injury models, where it can disrupt gut barrier integrity and kill the host within a day after infection. Such findings highlight that different pathogens exhibit different levels of virulence in two infection models, likely influenced by their exposure to midgut defenses [11].

Several infection studies in Drosophila addressing gut immunity help us to understand how the host resists and recovers from microbial infections [23]. Resistance includes the localized production of ROS and AMPs [24], while resilience depends upon the ability of intestinal epithelium to regenerate and maintain gut homeostasis. In the current work, effective clearance of the blue food from the intestines of infected flies indicated resilience of the intestinal barrier following infection.

We observed dynamic induction of intestinal ROS on infection with S. Typhimurium, K. pneumoniae, and E. cloacae. A ROS surge at 4 hpi is consistent with the pathogen colonization in the flies, except for the non-pathogenic E. coli. This could be due to exposure to bacterial pathogen associated molecular patterns, such as lipopolysaccharide, which are rapidly detected by pathogen recognition receptors. Interestingly, ROS levels declined at 24 hpi, accompanied by the downregulation of Nox and Duox, members of the NADPH oxidase family. Previous studies have also discussed the efficacy of the intestinal ROS in blocking the pathogenic bacteria in the anterior section of the intestine, as early as 15 min of ingestion [17]. Reportedly, this rapid oxidative burst limits bacterial proliferation, subsequently clearing the bacteria [18]. Previous studies have also established that Duox is primarily responsible for the ROS production in the gut epithelium [25], so we utilised Duox knockdown flies in our study. However, Duox knockdown flies did not exhibit increased susceptibility to S. Typhimurium, K. pneumoniae, and E. cloacae, leaving the role of ROS in eliminating these pathogens unclear. Although ROS is important for gut homeostasis, its overproduction can cause damage to intestinal cells [26]. The decline in ROS levels at 24 hpi could be due to the antioxidant activity of the immune system, like the immune-regulated catalase [27], which plays a crucial role in mitigating the host damage post-infection. However, this reduction in ROS did not affect bacterial survival. Instead, all the pathogens, except E. cloacae, were cleared within 48 hpi.

Later at 48 hpi, a surge in ROS was again observed throughout the infection groups. This elevated ROS at later stages may have functioned by exerting an antimicrobial effect against the persisted bacteria [28]. Athough there is a surge in ROS upon infection in wildtype flies, flies lacking the Duox gene exhibit no significant mortality, indicating that ROS alone does not play a role in clearing the infection. Reportedly, elevated levels of ROS during infection stimulate the TRPA1 receptor in gut enteroendocrine cells, thereby activating the production of neuropeptides, such as Diuretic Hormone 31 [17]. TRPA1 stimulation enhances gut motility, increasing defecation rates and promoting the proliferation of epithelial stem cells. These processes work together to facilitate gut clearance and support tissue regeneration following infection. Notably, TRPA1 is also involved in inflammatory bowel diseases, where it affects the gut inflammation and has dual roles: pro- and anti-inflammatory roles [29], making it an attractive target to treat inflammatory disorders.

Our findings suggest that TRPA1 activation correlates with the rapid ROS production/upregulation of Duox and shedding of S. Typhimurium and E. cloacae. At some time points, there was a lag in ROS/Duox induction, TRPA1 expression, and bacterial expulsion. For instance, TRPA1 was downregulated at 48 hpi, but the bacteria were expelled by the flies. We propose that TRPA1 induction at 36 hpi may be responsible for the expulsion of bacteria recovered in the subsequent 12 h (36–48 hpi). We also observed that shedding decreased over time (24 to 48 hpi), consistent with reductions in internal bacterial load and TRPA1 expression, which also decreased by 48 hpi. This suggests that TRPA1-driven bacterial expulsion likely persists until 48 hpi, clearing most ingested bacteria. Interestingly, in the Duox knockdown condition, bacterial (S. Typhimurium and E. cloacae) expulsion is negatively affected along with TRPA1 downregulation. We therefore propose that the TRPA1/Duox axis may play an important role in clearing some of the pathogens in flies.

Notably, although ROS levels were high in K. pneumoniae-infected flies, TRPA1 was not induced, yet these flies expelled K. pneumoniae. This suggests that the fly may use a TRPA1(or TRPA1 K, TRPA1 H and TRPA1 G variants)-independent mechanism to expel K. pneumoniae. Further, in E. coli-infected flies, TRPA1 was upregulated, but no colonies were recovered in the shedding experiment. Since this study utilised a non-pathogenic strain of E. coli, we can infer that it lacks defences against the host’s immune response and is likely to be killed by the fly gut’s immune response. Thus, no bacterial recovery in the shedding assay. Nonetheless, other mechanisms, such as alternative mechanosensory pathways, altered gut transit time, or differential bacterial sensitivity to basal ROS, cannot be ruled out as contributing to the differential elimination of these bacteria from the fly body.

Further, a mild reduction in the gut width at 24 hpi of flies infected with pathogenic bacteria suggests a compromised gut. Hence, our work on understanding gastro-oral infections, oxidative stress, and gut contractions, in context to different pathogens, sheds light on possible mechanisms involved in eliminating pathogens from the gut over time. Additionally, it warrants future studies of anatomical and molecular alterations in the intestine, which appear to be conserved across species, providing a potential model to study the propulsion of luminal contents and the maintenance of intestinal homeostasis.

Conclusions

Collectively, our findings underscore that the immune system of Drosophila is capable of both discerning between different bacterial species and adapting its response to bacteria in a tissue-specific manner. The differential activation and regulation of pathways in response to pathogens warrant further study to understand if these responses can be used for repurposing the existing methods of treatment for pathogen clearance. Clinically, gut motility disorders, such as Intestinal Bowel Syndrome and bacterial gastroenteritis, are linked with intestinal muscular contractions. In many instances, drugs are prescribed to control intestinal spasms to relieve the pain, which might delay the clearance of the infection. Our findings suggest that intestinal muscular contractions could help get rid of bacterial infection.

Methods

Fly stocks

Oregon-R flies were used in the study. Flies carrying Duox RNAi (a kind gift from Professor Anurag Sharma, NITTE, India) driven by ubiquitously expressed Actin-Gal4 (y1w*;P{Act5C−GAL4}25FO1/CyO; TM6B, Tb1; a kind gift from Professor Manish Jaiswal, TIFR, Mumbai, India) was used to knock down Duox to investigate the role of ROS in bacterial clearance. As a control UAS-GFP reporter flies (w1118;P{UAS-GFP.S65T}T2; a kind gift from Professor Subhash C Lakhotia, BHU, India) were crossed with same Actin-Gal 4 driver Experimental F1 progeny were selected by screening against Curly (Cy) and Tubby (Tb) phenotypic markers, yielding the experimental genotype: Actin-Gal4 > UAS-Duox RNAi and Control flies (Actin-Gal4 > UAS-GFP).

The efficiency of the Duox knockdown was confirmed via RT-qPCR (Supplementary file 6). Drosophila stocks were maintained at 25°C on a 12:12 h light: dark cycle.

Bacterial strains

Bacterial strains used in this study were E. coli, S. Typhimurium, S. marcescens, K. pneumoniae, and E. cloacae. The RFP expressing strains of E. coli and S. Typhimurium were obtained through their electro-transformation with plasmid pFPV-mcherry (Addgene plasmid #20956), while those of K. pneumoniae and E. cloacae were obtained by transformation with plasmid pEB2-2ndVal mRFP1 (Addgene plasmid #104025). RFP expressing strains were cultured in Luria Broth medium supplemented with antibiotics for their selection: Ampicillin 100 µg/mL (Himedia, India) was used for E. coli and S. Typhimurium, while Kanamycin 50 µg/mL (Himedia, India) was used for K. pneumoniae and E. cloacae. Prior to each oral infection assay, the transformed bacteria were streaked onto antibiotic-selective media to confirm the plasmid retention.

Fly infection model and survival assays

The oral infection model was adapted from Siva-Jothy [19], with some modifications. Overnight grown bacteria were subcultured in 10 mL of fresh media containing suitable antibiotics and allowed to grow until the culture OD600 was between 0.3 and 0.5. These bacterial cultures were centrifuged, and the pellet was resuspended in 500 µL of 5% sucrose solution prepared in sterile phosphate buffered saline (PBS). Whatman filter papers soaked with 200 µL of these bacterial suspensions were placed inside the empty culture vials. Thirty age-matched male and female adult flies (3–5 days old) per experimental group were subjected to 3 h of starvation, then allowed to feed on the bacterial cultures for 4 h. Flies fed only a 5% sucrose solution prepared in sterile PBS were used as a negative control. Following oral infection, the infected flies were transferred to the standard cornmeal agar devoid of antimicrobials, such as propionic acid and nipagin.

The number of dead flies was recorded daily up to 30 days post-infection. Each day, surviving flies were transferred to the new vial containing antimicrobial-free standard cornmeal agar. Survival data were presented as Kaplan–Meier curves plotting the percentage of surviving flies over time and were compared using the Log-rank (Mantel–Cox) test.

Measurement of bacterial ingestion via fluorescence microscopy

To quantify the ingestion of RFP-tagged bacteria, fluorescence was monitored after oral infection. Following the 4 h of oral infection, 5–6 infected flies were randomly collected from each infection group. Flies were anaesthetized and positioned in a way that their ventral abdomen is exposed. Images were captured at 5x magnification. Fluorescence intensity within the abdomen was quantified using Zen (blue edition) Software.

Measurement of fly bacterial load by CFU assay

Bacterial colonization in the fly body was determined as the number of CFU at 0-, 24-, 48-, and 72-hpi using the standard plate count method using the fluorescently tagged strains. Infected flies (three technical replicates for three different biological replicates) were gently washed thrice with 1 mL of sterile PBS. The washed flies (2 each for a single technical replicate) were then homogenized in 500 µL of sterile PBS, using a bead beater at 20 Hz frequency for a 2-minute cycle (20 s homogenization, followed by 10 s on ice). The homogenate was diluted serially 1:10 and plated onto selective media to estimate bacterial load, as described previously [30].

Evaluation of gut permeability and dye clearance in flies

Following the 4 h of oral feeding, using the method described above, 20 flies for each group were transferred to standard cornmeal agar food containing 2.5% (wt./vol.) bromophenol blue for 12 h. A fly was categorized as Smurf (observed using a microscope) if the dye could be observed outside the intestine [31] and the blue color was visible throughout its body.

For the dye clearance assay, separate sets of flies were used. These flies (20 flies per test group) were fed with the dye containing food for 12 h as described above. After the examination for Smurf phenotype (if any), the flies were transferred to standard cornmeal agar food for 12 h to assess their ability to clear the dye from the gut [32].

Photographs of the representative flies were taken using the Optika microscope camera (Optika, Italy).

Estimation of ROS

Infected fly guts were isolated, and both the gut and the carcass were added to 1.5 mL Eppendorf tubes containing 20 mM Tris Buffer. Tissue samples (23 guts and carcasses per infection group) were ground using a pestle and then centrifuged. The supernatants were transferred to a fresh Eppendorf tube, and protein concentrations of the lysate were quantified using the Bradford assay and the lysates amounting to 50 µg/µL protein were incubated with 5 µM DCF-DA for 60 min. Following this, fluorescence was measured with excitation at 490 nm and emission at 530 nm.

Measurement of bacterial shedding from infected flies

As described by Siva-Jothy [19], the infected flies were individually transferred to an Eppendorf tube containing 50 µL of standard cornmeal agar and subsequently transferred to a fresh tube every 24 h. Bacterial shedding was quantified at 24 hpi and subsequently at 12-hour intervals up to 48 hpi using a serial dilution method by plating the tube contents onto appropriate media.

Analysis of immune gene expression

Total RNA was isolated from 40 to 50 fly guts and/or 10–15 whole adult flies using the TRIzol reagent (Himedia, India) as specified in an earlier study [33]. Subsequently, 800 ng of total RNA was reverse transcribed to cDNA using Verso cDNA synthesis kit (AB1453A; Thermo Scientific, MA, USA) followed by qPCR reactions using gene-specific primers (Supplementary file 7). Data is expressed as the relative fold change in the transcript abundance compared to its uninfected control.

Measurement of gut width

To assess morphological changes at 24 hpi, 5–6 guts were dissected from the flies. The samples were imaged using confocal microscopy with DIC settings. For each gut, multiple images were captured across different anatomical sections of the gut. To determine the overall gut width, the width was measured at several points within each section; these values were then averaged to provide a representative width for that specific region. Finally, the total gut width was calculated by averaging the measurements across all recorded sections, as depicted in Supplementary file 5B.

Statistical analysis

All statistical analyses were performed using GraphPad Prism version 10 (GraphPad Software, MA, USA). For intergroup comparison, one-way ANOVA, followed by Dunnett’s post-hoc test, was used. For experiments involving multiple groups, a two-way ANOVA followed by Dunnett’s or Sidak’s post hoc test was used. The survival curves were compared using the Log-Rank (Mantel-Cox) test. An unpaired t-test was used to assess the efficiency of Duox knockdown in flies. All data are presented as mean ± standard deviation. p-values less than 0.05 were considered statistically significant. All experiments were performed with at least three independent biological replicates.
