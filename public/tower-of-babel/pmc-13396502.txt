Broadly quantifying SSP elements for 188 countries to 2150 in International Futures
Authors: Jonathan D. Moyer, Barry B. Hughes, Mohammod Irfan, José Solórzano, Caleb L. Petry, Yutang Xiong, Taylor Hanna, Collin J. Meisel, Deva Sahadevan, Ethan Sullivan
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
We extend the quantification of the Shared Socioeconomic Pathways (SSPs) narratives by projecting 188 socioeconomic indicators for 188 countries to 2150 using the International Futures model. The variables span demographics, conflict, economics, education, health, infrastructure, and governance using a fully integrated model. We address existing limitations related to a) misalignment between quantification and scenario narratives; b) inconsistencies driven by use of unconnected models; c) the omission of indicators; and d) absence of projections into the 22nd century. Here we show projections highly correlated with existing forecasts that also lead to lower economic growth because they are driven by broader factors including conflict, governance, infrastructure, and human development, which in turn leads to lower resource demand. We also introduce government spending measures by sector that can be used to connect the SSPs to broader theoretical understandings of future patterns of mitigation and adaptation in the face of climate change.

Introduction

The Shared Socioeconomic Pathways (SSPs) are five alternative narratives centered around challenges to adaptation and mitigation in relation to climate change1,2. There are two approaches to operationalizing the SSPs at a country and global level of analysis: (1) quantified SSP elements are produced by operationalizing the narratives themselves in a model by developing scenarios; and (2) SSP extensions are generated using those quantified SSP elements as model inputs without making additional scenario assumptions3. A variety of groups have created quantified SSP elements that include demographics4,5, economic growth6, and urbanization7, while SSP extensions exist for a variety of applications, including intrastate conflict8, governance9, inequality10, and health11. The SSPs narratives do not directly incorporate climate policy or the impact from climate change. Instead, they outline alternative socioeconomic development trajectories that can be coupled to climate outcomes from natural-system models, such as those in the Representative Concentration Pathways (RCPs)12 and Shared Climate Policy Assumptions13.

While an important component used in the study of human-environment interactions, the SSP quantifications are not without criticism. Firstly, some growth trajectories have been deemed unrealistic in their portrayal of development pathways14. Secondly, some argue that the operationalization of certain narratives overlooks development pathways that emphasize efficiency and dematerialization instead of growth15,16. Thirdly, the core quantified series used in most long-term studies rely on models focused on a limited number of interacting development systems, lacking broader endogenization4,6,17. This presents two challenges: (1) potential logical inconsistencies in forecasts that are conceptually related but not dynamically connected, and (2) the omission of key relationships in models, such as the impact of civil wars on economic growth, leading to biased results18,19. Lastly, existing SSP quantifications are through 2100; as we move through the 21st century, it becomes increasingly critical to understand human-environment interactions beyond this timeframe20.

This paper addresses these concerns by introducing a set of 188 quantified SSP elements for 188 countries, projected to 2150 using the International Futures (IFs) integrated assessment model17. These series—designed to be consistent with the existing SSP narratives and generally in line with assumptions made by others in their operationalization—encompass a broader spectrum of indicators than previous core SSP sets, including detailed demographic projections (age-sex quinquennial data), economic indicators21,22, government spending/transfer patterns3, education levels23, income inequality24, governance quality25, conflict probability18, and access to essential services such as electricity, sanitation, and water26. Instead of relying on SSP projections as inputs to generate some of our series, we generate these projections by operationalizing the SSP narratives in IFs by developing alternative scenarios, creating quantified SSP elements and not SSP extensions23:344.

To generate these series, we employed a structured approach starting with a careful analysis of the SSP narratives2, and projections by others4,6,8–11,27–29. We started by comparing SSP2 with the IFs “Current Path” (CP) scenario, a dynamic reference pathway that aligns with baseline expectations of future development17. Next, we investigated how others have operationalized the SSPs. We map broad assumptions made by other researchers and aligned our scenario interventions with them. This process aligned the model’s outputs with the broader SSP narrative framework and the work of others operationalizing the SSPs, while also leveraging the IFs model’s capacity to model complex endogenous relationships. The methodology section outlines our work, and the Supplemental Information provides more details about model assumptions.

We argue that our approach to operationalizing the SSP narratives improves upon existing SSP operationalizations. Firstly, IFs operationalizations more closely align with the original SSP narratives. This includes: (a) eliminating economic convergence in SSP4; (b) driving high-income fertility rates to replacement in SSP5; (c) reducing high-income (all income groupings discussed follow the World Bank) growth in SSP1; and (d) higher long-term population growth in SSP3. Secondly, utilizing a single integrated framework, IFs quantifies elements previously treated as SSP extensions, such as conflict and governance. Thirdly, quantified elements, including water, sanitation, sectoral government spending, and poverty by income threshold, are introduced. Finally, the projections extend to 2150, enabling more extensive climate modeling.

Results

The IFs quantification of the SSP narratives, which we refer to as the IFs SSPs for brevity, demonstrate broad comparability with existing SSP quantifications in magnitude and trend across indicators (Fig. 1). Globally, IFs population projections are, on average, 1.3% lower than their corresponding projections in the existing SSPs in 2050 (range: −2.6 to 1.2%) and 7.4% lower in 2100 (range: −12.9 to −0.2%). Similarly, gross domestic product (GDP) projections show limited differences, 10% lower in 2050 (range: −13.9 to −4.4%) and 11.4% lower in 2100 (range: −22.8 to −3.8%). GDP per capita projections are also lower, showing an 8.7% reduction in 2050 (range: −13.4 to −3.4%) and a 4.2% reduction in 2100 (range: −15.7 to 8.1%). A country-level correlational analysis between existing economic projections and IFs SSPs reveals a high degree of correlation (Fig. 2). Figure 1 compares the IFs SSPs with existing SSP series across key indicators, including population, education, migration, births, deaths, inequality, and conflict probability4,8,10,28. Population projections in the IFs SSPs generally follow established patterns, albeit with notable differences. IFs SSPs project populations in 2050 ranging from 9.8 billion in SSP3, 9.7 billion in SSP4 and SSP2, to 9 billion in SSP1 and SSP5. This gap widens by century’s end, ranging from 12.5 billion in SSP3 to 6.9 billion in SSP1, and expands considerably by 2150, ranging from 18.0 billion in SSP3 to 3.7 billion in SSP1. The most substantial divergence from existing SSPs occurs in SSP1 and SSP5, with a global population of 87.1% and 90.7%, respectively, of previously published projections by 2100. Regionally, IFs SSPs project higher population growth in lower-income countries, with population levels 1–7% higher in Africa and 3–9% higher in low-income countries by 2050.

These differences arise from alternative assumptions regarding births, deaths, and migration. In the IFs SSPs, initial birth rates are comparable to existing projections4, but IFs subsequently projects lower overall birth rates (averaging 84.1% of existing SSPs by 2050), except for SSP5, which reflects narrative-driven replacement fertility in high-income countries. This pattern holds across regions and income groups, with the exception of higher birth rates in low-income countries under SSP2.

Mortality trends in IFs SSPs are broadly similar yet exhibit notable variations. In SSP1, SSP2, and SSP5, IFs projects slightly higher mortality earlier in the time horizon (3.6% higher for SSP1, 2.5% for SSP2, and 4.9% for SSP5 in 2050), primarily due to higher mortality in high-income countries. IFs SSP4 diverges most considerably, projecting lower mortality in lower-income regions (30.9% lower in low-income and 22.2% lower in lower-middle-income countries in 2050). Nevertheless, global mortality in IFs SSP4 increases from 7.8 deaths per thousand in 2025 to 12.1 in 2150.

Migration, the third component, also shows large differences. All IFs SSPs begin with 14.2 million annual migrants globally in 2025, while other SSPs have varying starting points (3.8 to 15.5 million). IFs projections show both higher and lower migration for extreme scenarios when compared to existing projections (196 million fewer in SSP3 and 202 million more in SSP5 from 2025 to 2100), with similar trends in other scenarios. These demographic results influence life expectancy projections, which are generally similar to existing work, with an average difference of 0.7% in 2050. Life expectancies in SSP1 and SSP5 approach 100 years near the end of the century in both IFs and existing SSPs. IFs SSP4 projects higher life expectancy due to lower mortality. Notable regional differences include more optimistic health outcomes in lower-income countries across IFs SSPs.

IFs SSPs project lower educational attainment across most scenarios compared with existing published work, with values for SSP1, SSP2, SSP3, and SSP5 5.5% lower by 2050 compared with earlier projections4. The exception is SSP4, which projects 12.8% higher average global attainment by century’s end, driven by inequality-focused operationalization. Most gains in IFs SSP4 are in high-income countries, with 7.3% higher attainment in 2050. Within country inequality in IFs SSP4 for education is focused on gender related interventions (see Supplemental Information).

Income inequality in IFs SSPs begins lower than existing SSPs averaging 86.7% of previous levels in 2025. Inequality in IFs SSP1 decreases over time, while SSPs 2–5 become less equal. IFs projections for civil war onset show a much larger distribution compared with existing projections. In IFs SSP3, conflict probability increases from 0.13 in 2025 to 0.27 by 2075. Conversely, SSP1 shows a dramatic reduction to 0.01 by 2070. SSPs 2 and 5 show gradual reductions, reaching 0.05 and 0.04 by 2100, respectively. Greatest differences are observed in countries with low existing conflict probabilities, particularly in SSP3.

Scatter plots compare country-year differences, while box and whisker plots display average rates and distributions. While IFs SSPs project lower country-level growth, strong positive correlation exists, with squared Pearson correlation coefficients above 0.57, except for SSP4 from 2051 to 2100.

While similar in trend, IFs SSP economic growth projections are generally lower30 than existing projections6,28, with a 11.4% average reduction in 2100 GDP. The most substantial difference is in SSP5, where the existing operationalizations have been criticized for projecting excessively high growth14. IFs SSP5 projects growth from $99 trillion (All monetary values are expressed in constant 2017 U.S. dollars) in 2025 to $776 trillion by 2100, compared to $1,005 trillion in existing projections, a $229 trillion difference. All IFs SSPs project lower economic output: SSP1 (−6.3%), SSP2 (−19.4%), SSP3 (−4.9%), and SSP4 (−13.7%). From 2025 to 2100, IFs SSPs imply a reduction in energy consumption by 6.0% (SSP1), 15.1% (SSP2), 33.9% (SSP3), 18.7% (SSP4), and 19.5% (SSP5).

IFs GDP per capita projections are also lower, averaging 9.8% less in 2100. The largest reductions are in SSP2 (−20.9%), SSP5 (−20.4%), and SSP4 (−7.5%), though absolute values in our projections still increase substantially in all of these scenarios. SSP1 is the only scenario with higher GDP per capita (5.8%) in the long run, driven by slower growth in population.

Finally, another key area of difference lies in SSP1, the sustainable development scenario. IFs projects a convergence of low, low-middle, and upper-middle-income countries towards high-income countries in the 21st century. This result reflects the narrative assumptions about a sustainable and equitable development future. In contrast, the Dellink et al.6 projections for SSP1 maintain considerable divergence across income groups, including between low and high-income economies, even by the end of the century.

The IFs SSP1 operationalization intentionally simulates long-term reductions in high-income economic production. This is driven by narrative-based assumptions of reduced consumption and government policies aimed at redistribution and restructuring of economic systems to prioritize sustainability and equity. This emphasis on reduced economic production in high-income economies is a distinct feature of the IFs SSP projections compared to Dellink’s. It reflects a shift in societal values and priorities towards a more sustainable and equitable future.

In SSP1, the model reflects the narrative’s assumptions by simulating a reduction in the average working week across the time horizon. This highlights that the IFs SSP1, despite its unique approach to economic production and working hours, can still achieve comparable levels of poverty reduction to existing SSP1 projections.

These detailed spending projections are not static; they evolve across different socioeconomic pathways and over time. This dynamic aspect allows researchers to explore how different societal choices and priorities might influence government spending patterns. For instance, a future with greater emphasis on sustainability might see increased investment in renewable energy infrastructure and social welfare programs, while a world characterized by conflict might prioritize military spending. Importantly, these government spending projections are directly linked to other aspects of the IFs model, such as economic production, population growth, and education levels. This integrated approach enables a more comprehensive understanding of how different socioeconomic factors interact and shape government priorities. This information can be invaluable for policymakers and researchers seeking to understand the potential impacts of various policies and interventions.

Discussion

We argue that these projections improve on existing work because they: (1) more closely align with the original SSP narratives; (2) occur within one model framework, providing internal model-based logical consistency; (3) and allow researchers to analyze the future over longer-term time horizons. Our approach improves upon existing work by directly imposing scenario-based parameter adjustments within an integrated model more broadly rather than relying on exogenous driving factors. Treating these drivers endogenously allows the model to capture interdependent feedbacks and cascading effects that methodologies based on externally specified driver trajectories often overlook, with some resulting differences in projected pathways. Specifically, our results reduce economic growth in SSP1 and enforce global economic divergence in SSP4 relative to existing work, which more closely aligns with narrative emphasis on sustainable dematerialization and rising international inequality, respectively.

This broader modeling effort can help expand the userbase for SSPs32, including for academic disciplines that would greatly benefit from indicators, such as those related to water, sanitation, sectoral government spending, and poverty by income thresholds. These quantified SSP elements allow for more academic disciplines to connect their work, for example, that requires estimates of government spending by sector, into the broader research efforts to understand human-natural system interaction33,34.

However, making projections about the world at a country level more than a century into the future is a fraught task. As the previous 125 years shows, while some durable trends persisted, there were dramatic transformations in social and human development that were impossible to anticipate35. Inventions like the airplane, computer, and liquid fueled rocket fundamentally changed industries and economies36. The development of mass vaccination and breakthroughs like penicillin dramatically increased life expectancy37. And the rise and fall of ideologies such as communism and neoliberalism have remolded societal fabrics and geopolitical dynamics, leading to hot and cold wars, the legacies of which continue to shape and reshape the world today38.

We do not pretend to know what the world in 2150 will look like and rely on the existing SSP narratives to drive a representation of future socio-economic development that is stylized and incomplete. The uncertainties associated with future developments in artificial intelligence39, political norms and practices40, as well as unforeseen social and environmental tipping points41 and disruptive events will characterize the long-term future in ways that cannot be anticipated.

While uncertain, it is also important to think about and consider long-term development patterns for many reasons. First, some long-term implications of human-nature interactions can only be understood over very long time horizons, such as the implications of some climatic tipping points, such as glacier ice sheet melting. Second, one utility of long-term projections is helping develop and shape our collective “mental models” about future challenges and opportunities to help us better understand how choices and trends today will impact development for future generations. Third, some moral frameworks would argue that we have an ethical obligation to future generations to interrogate how our actions today will impact their future wellbeing.

Methods

Our approach to operationalizing quantified SSP elements began with a detailed review of the SSP narratives. This review specifically focused on socioeconomic dynamics, deliberately excluding environmental or energy aspects of the narratives from this initial phase. The foundational step involved comparing the behavior of the IFs model’s CP scenario with SSP2, designated as the “middle-of-the-road” scenario17. The CP in IFs is designed to represent a “most likely” development trajectory, encompassing long-term trends and dynamics across a wide array of indicators for 188 countries, including agriculture42,43, conflict18, climate22,44, demographics, economics21,24,30, education23,45,46, energy47,48, governance18,25,31, gender49, health50–52, infrastructure26, international relations3,53, and technology54.

In IFs, countries interact through international patterns of migration, trade, investment, remittances, and the threat of international conflict. The CP avoids dramatic technological shifts or other extreme outcomes, thereby closely resembling the SSP2 narrative. This alignment was validated by comparing the IFs CP with existing quantified SSP elements, which generally demonstrated similar behavior. A crucial adjustment for this analysis involved modifying the CP to eliminate the direct impacts of climate change, ensuring that the quantified SSP elements produced align with the core purpose of the SSPs, which primarily frame alternative development trajectories without directly incorporating climate policy or impact from climate change.

Following the establishment of the SSP2 baseline, the remaining four SSPs were operationalized through a two-step process. First, an extensive review of existing literature was conducted to understand how other researchers have translated the qualitative SSP narratives into quantitative changes within their respective scenarios. Second, “anchor points” identified from the SSP narratives were prioritized during the operationalization of these alternative scenarios. These anchor points represented either absolute or relative declarations about future socioeconomic development, such as “global population…levels off in the second half of the century,” which helped to orient the IFs model to the narrative framework. Combining the insights from the literature review and our in-depth knowledge of the IFs platform, a series of specific model adjustments were developed. These adjustments, justified in detail later in the methodology, were designed to accurately reflect each SSP narrative within the IFs framework. This process allowed for the creation of quantified SSP elements using the IFs system that cut across a broader range of domains and issue areas compared to existing elements, fostering greater internal logical consistency in the forecasts. Within the demographic module, IFs accounts for changes in population flows and stocks distributed by age and sex, with births endogenously driven by fertility rates, and deaths subtracted from age and sex groups. Migration, while not endogenously modeled, is based on external long-term flow rate projections. The economic module is a recursive dynamic computable general equilibrium tool that employs a Cobb–Douglas production function with a Solow residual and represents six sectors. Economic growth in IFs is driven by production across these six sectors, with multifactor productivity playing a crucial role, influenced by factors like workforce health and education, infrastructure, innovation capacity, and governance. The model also includes detailed representations of government finance, simulating revenue from various taxes, expenditures across sectors like education, health, and infrastructure, and transfers to households. Education in IFs models schooling by grade and sex from primary through tertiary levels, influencing adult educational attainment and having a relationship with productivity and economic outcomes. The health model projects age- and sex-specific mortality across 15 distinct causes of death, integrating both distal and proximate drivers of health outcomes. Governance is conceptualized through three components: security, state institutional capacity, and inclusivity, with the model projecting potentials for state failure, government revenues, and the quality of governance. Finally, IFs includes an infrastructure representation that captures various dimensions of access, such as electricity, mobile broadband, and water and sanitation, driven by government finance and having forward linkages to other sectors like health and productivity. These interconnected modules allow for a holistic and dynamic simulation of development pathways.

Understanding the SSPs

The SSPs are a set of five alternative narratives intended to inform the quantification of future states of human development that frame uncertainty related to challenges to adaptation and mitigation related to climate change1,2. There are two distinct approaches to producing SSP projections. Those who produce quantified SSP elements take the SSP narratives and make scenario based assumptions about how the narratives should impact a projection filtered through a model. An alternative approach to forecasting the SSPs relies on taking these quantifications and using them as exogenous inputs into existing models, which produces SSP extensions3. A variety of groups have created quantified SSP elements which include demographics4,5, economic growth6, and inequality10, while SSP extensions also exist for a variety of applications, including intrastate conflict8, governance9, and health11. The SSPs do not directly include climate policy or the impact from climate change. Instead they frame alternative development trajectories that can be connected to natural-system modeling, such as the RCPs12, along with shared climate policy assumptions13.

The various quantifications of the SSPs have been widely used across academic disciplines to analyze future patterns of socioeconomic development in conjunction with other tools. Examples of this work are broad and are widely featured in scientific assessments, like the Intergovernmental Panel on Climate Change and the United Nations Environment Programme’s Global Environmental Outlooks. A database project called the International Committee on New Integrated Climate Change Assessment Scenarios (ICONICS) was started to gather examples of this research and promote its further use and development13,32.

While the SSPs have been widely developed and used, they have also been criticized. Some have criticized particular scenarios for unrealistic assumptions, particularly SSP514. Other criticisms focus on the inability of the SSPs to capture long-term shocks19. Yet others have been concerned with the SSPs ability to capture important policy responses to environmental crises, like dematerialization.

Most of the quantified SSP elements that have been introduced in literature focus on a sub-set of indicators from a particular domain or issue area. For example, the economic projections are created using an economic model that exogenously represents a wide variety of factors, such as advances in education, health, governance, and infrastructure to model future development pathways6. The demographic and educational results are produced using a similar framework, which connects improved education access with reduced fertility rates, but a wide range of drivers of changing fertility, migration, and mortality are treated exogenously4.

In this paper, we introduce quantified SSP elements created using the IFs system that cut across a broader range of domains and issue areas compared with existing quantified SSP elements. This produces forecasts that maintain much more internal logical consistency that can be used alongside existing quantified SSP elements in future research. The series introduced here include the following variables and issue areas: demographics (births; migration; age structure), economics (inequality; value add by sector (agriculture, energy, information communication technology, materials, manufacturing, services); gross domestic product (GDP) at MER or purchasing power parity (PPP); education (attainment by sex); governance (civil war onset probability; effectiveness, revenue, spending by sector (education, health, infrastructure, military, other) and transfers (pensions and welfare); health (mortality by cause, age, and sex for communicable disease (five categories), non-communicable disease (seven categories) and injuries (three categories); and infrastructure (access to electricity; access to safe sanitation; access to safe water). We provide these forecasts for 188 countries from 2024 to 2150.

Modeling the SSPs in IFs

We started by reading narratives line-by-line and drawing core “anchor points” that could be used to empirically benchmark scenarios; these factors shaped the foundation of our scenario analysis and are described below. We looked for either absolute or relative statements about how socioeconomic development would unfold in the future, including phrases like, “global population…levels off in the second half of the century,” to help us orient the IFs model to the narrative framework. The line-by-line representation of the narratives is represented in Supplementary Data 1. In addition to “anchor point” terms, we also gathered information on how others had operationalized these scenarios in other publications. In general, other scholars have represented their interventions using broad language, like economic openness is “medium” in SSP1 and SSP2. These results are presented in Supplementary Data 3.

With these two pieces of information in mind, we then evaluated how the IFs CP scenario behaved relative to the SSP2 narrative. In the past, the IFs CP projection showed lower economic growth30, reflecting other criticism of SSP growth trajectories14. With the more recent updates of the SSP projections, the GDP growth trajectories have slowed, putting the IFs CP and SSP2 more closely in line in terms of economic growth. Demographically, the IFs population forecasts for the CP were slightly higher than those of the previous IIASA revision, though subsequent revision lowered those of the SSPs and IFs is now slightly higher than the more recent version (see Fig. 1). To adjust the IFs CP to match the SSP2 assumptions, we removed forward impacts from climate change in particular and also made additional adjustments to reflect model preferences (for example, turning more established versions of the education and WASH models on). See Supp Data 3 for detailed descriptions of each parameter intervention across the SSPs presented here.
