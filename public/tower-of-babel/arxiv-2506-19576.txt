Bayesian community detection in assortative stochastic block model with unknown number of communities
Martina Amongero, Pierpaolo De Blasi, [4pt] ESOMAS Department, University of Torino, Italy

Abstract
Structured data in the form of networks is increasingly common in a number of fields, including social sciences, biology, physics, computer science, and many others. A key task in network analysis is community detection, which typically consists of dividing the nodes into groups such that nodes within a group are strongly connected, while connections between groups are relatively scarcer. A generative model well-suited for the formation of such communities is the assortative stochastic block model, which prescribes a higher probability of a connection between nodes belonging to the same block rather than to different blocks. A recent line of work has utilized Bayesian nonparametric methods to recover communities in the SBM by placing a prior distribution on the number of blocks and estimating block assignments via collapsed Gibbs samplers. However, efficiently incorporating the assortativity constraint through the prior remains an open problem. In this work, we address this gap, aiming to study the effect of enforcing assortativity on Bayesian community detection and so identify under what scenario it pays its dividends in comparison with standard SBM. We illustrate our findings through an extensive simulation study.



Keywords
: Community detection, Gibbs-type prior, network, stochastic block model

1
Introduction

Available data in the form of networks has gained increasing attention in modern research, with applications ranging from biology (e.g., brain activity, protein interaction networks), to medicine (e.g., evolution of infectious disease), and economics (e.g., social networks and advertising policies).
The study of community structures of networks has been a popular topic in Statistics. We speak of
assortative
communities
(
Newman, 2010
)
when there are more connections within groups than between them. There exist several approaches to finding communities, either heuristic or model-based. The first category includes hierarchical clustering
(
Newman, 2004
)
, spectral clustering
(
Rohe et al., 2011
;
Lei and Rinaldo, 2015
)
, and maximization of modularity
(
Newman and Girvan, 2004
;
Blondel et al., 2008
;
Zhao et al., 2011
)
.
In model-based approaches, rather than simple features of the network structure, communities are an intrinsic part of the generating process: the probability of nodes being connected or not depends on them being part of the same community or different communities. The most popular probabilistic model used to represent networks is the stochastic block model (SBM) of
Holland et al., 1983
. In its simplest form, it comprises a
k\times k
symmetric matrix
P=(P_{j\,l})
of probabilities of connection between nodes of equal (
j=l
) or different blocks (
j\neq l
), and a
k
-vector
\pi=(\pi_{j})
of probabilities that a node is assigned to a given block thus determining how much balanced – i.e. similar in size – the blocks are.

SBM has been extensively studied in terms of theoretical guarantees for community recovery such as detection thresholds
(
Mossel et al., 2015
;
Mossel et al., 2016
;
Abbe et al., 2016
)
and minimax rates
(
Zhang and Zhou, 2016
;
Gao and Ma, 2021
)
. These guarantees can be expressed in terms of the number of nodes and edges, together with the number of blocks
k
, the balance (or imbalance) of blocks, and the gap between the smallest within-block probability,
p=\min_{j}P_{j\,j}
, and the largest between-block probability,
q=\max_{j<l}P_{j\,l}
. We referto the model as
assortative
SBM whenever
p>q

(
Amini and Levina, 2018
)
, a condition that makes blocks consistent with communities in the assortative sense.

Communities can be recovered via maximum likelihood estimation
(
Amini et al., 2013
;
Bickel and Chen, 2009
)
,
semidefinite programming relaxations
(
Amini and Levina, 2018
)
, Bayesian inference
(
Snijders and Nowicki, 1997
;
van der Pas and van der Vaart, 2018
)
, and mean field variational methods
(
Bickel et al., 2013
;
Zhang and Zhou, 2020
)
.
The number of blocks
k
is of pivotal importance – as in more traditional clustering applications – in that it determines the intrinsic dimension of the model. Various approaches have been proposed to select
k
such as modularity maximization
(
Zhao et al., 2011
)
, the integrated complete data log-likelihood
(
Côme and Latouche, 2015
)
, likelihood ratio tests
(
Wang and Bickel, 2017
)
, information criteria
(
Saldaña et al., 2017
)
, variational methods
(
Latouche et al., 2012
;
Zhang and Yang, 2024
)
.
Recently, there has been a surge of interest in Bayesian community detection that incorporates the choice of
k
by placing a prior distribution on it. Block assignments are then estimated via collapsed Gibbs samplers
(
McDaid et al., 2013
;
Geng et al., 2019
;
Legramanti et al., 2022
)
.
Attempts to incorporate assortativity in Bayesian inference are scarce. In the case of fixed
k
, we refer to
Peng and Carvalho, 2016
;
Gribel et al., 2021
for the degree-corrected SBM
(
Karrer and Newman, 2011
)
and
Gopalan et al., 2012
for mixed-membership SBM

(
Airoldi et al., 2008
)
. To the best of our knowledge, the only attempt in the unknown-
k
setting is the technical report by
Jiang and Tokdar, 2021
. Enforcing the assortativity constraint
q<p
prevents the use of independent and conjugate priors on connection probabilities. MCMC methods heavily rely on marginalization over model parameters, so a trade-off arises between algorithmic efficiency and flexibility in model specification arises, especially in the context of collapsed Gibbs samplers with
k
unknown.

In this paper we propose a probabilistic framework for simultaneous estimation of the number of communities
k
and the community memberships in assortative SBMs.
The two main contributions are a novel prior specification on connection probabilities that enforces the assortativity constraint
q<p
, and an efficient Gibbs sampler for community detection when
k
is unknown. We use synthetic networks to investigate the advantages of assortative SBM in community detection. In particular we focus on recovery regimes in which posterior inference benefits from imposing an assortative condition through the prior, accounting for the computation overhead due to the lack of conjugacy.

The rest of the paper is organized as follows. In Section 2, we introduce the SBM, and discuss how to incorporate the assortativity constraint in the prior specification of the probability matrix
P
. In Section 3, we show how to extend existing algorithms to assortative modeling, building on the work of
Neal, 2000
and
Geng et al., 2019
. Finally, we present a simulation study with networks generated from benchmark graphs that account for heterogeneity in node degree distributions and community sizes
(
Lancichinetti et al., 2008
)
. Conclusions are presented in Section 4.

2
Bayesian stochastic block modeling

In this section we introduce stochastic block modeling and Bayesian community detection with
k
fixed. Then we discuss how to include the assortative condition through the prior and illustrate its importance on a syntetic network.

Consider an undirected graph with no self-loops made of
n
nodes, labeled
1,\ldots,n
, described by a symmetric
n\times n
adjacency matrix
A
, with entry
A_{ij}=1
if node
i
and node
j
are connected and
A_{ij}=0
otherwise. Each node belongs to one of
k
mutually exclusive groups, called blocks, labelled
1,\ldots,k
, that share a similar connectivity patterns according to

{p}(A_{ij}=1)=P_{z_{i}z_{j}}

independent across
i\neq j
. Here
P=(P_{ab})
is the connectivity matrix, a
k\times k
symmetric matrix with
P_{ab}\in(0,1)
being the probability of an edge between nodes of blocks labelled
a
and
b
. Also
z_{i}\in\{1,\ldots,k\}
is the block assignment, or label, of the
i
th node. We will refer to
z=(z_{1},\ldots,z_{n})
as the vector of block assignments.
Finally, let
\pi=(\pi_{1},\ldots,\pi_{k})
be probabilities of community assignments, so that
{p}(z_{i}=a)=\pi_{a}
for
a=1,\ldots,k
independently across
i=1,\ldots,n
. In summary,

\displaystyle z_{i}|\pi

\displaystyle\overset{\text{iid}}{\sim}\text{Multinomial}(1,\pi),\quad i=1,\ldots,n

\displaystyle A_{ij}|z,P

\displaystyle\overset{\text{ind}}{\sim}\text{Bernoulli}(P_{z_{i},z_{j}}),\quad i<j

The complete likelihood is given by

\displaystyle{p}(A,z|P,\pi)=\prod_{i<j}P_{z_{i},z_{j}}^{A_{ij}}(1-P_{z_{i},z_{j}})^{1-A_{ij}}\prod_{i}\pi_{z_{i}}

\displaystyle=\prod_{a\leq b}P_{ab}^{O_{ab}(z)}(1-P_{ab})^{n_{ab}(z)-O_{ab}(z)}\prod_{a}\pi_{a}^{n_{a}(z)}

(1)

where

n_{a}(z)=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\mathds{1}(z_{i}=a)

is the number of nodes labelled
a
,

O_{ab}(z)=\sum_{i\neq j}\mathds{1}(z_{i}=a,z_{j}=b)A_{ij},\quad n_{ab}(z)=\sum_{i\neq j}\mathds{1}(z_{i}=a,z_{j}=b),

are the number of edges between nodes labelled
a
and
b
by the labelling
z
, and the maximum number of edges that can be created between nodes labeled
a
and
b
, respectively. As for the latter, we have

n_{ab}(z)=\begin{cases}n_{a}(z)n_{b}(z),&a\neq b\\
n_{a}(z)(n_{a}(z)-1)/2,&a=b.\end{cases}

We complete the Bayesian model by specifying a prior on
\pi
and
P
. The prior on
\pi
is symmetric Dirichlet

\pi\sim\text{Dirichlet}(\gamma,\ldots,\gamma),

(2)

for
\gamma>0
. The
standard
prior specification on
P
is to have
P_{a}b
beta distributed and independent across
a
and
b
:

P_{ab}\overset{\text{iid}}{\sim}\text{beta}(\alpha,\beta),\quad 0\leq a\leq b\leq k

(3)

for
\alpha,\beta>0
. The convenience is that one can exploit the standard beta-binomial update to derive the conditional of
P
given
z
and
A
:

\displaystyle P_{ab}|A,z

\displaystyle\overset{\text{ind}}{\sim}\text{beta}(O_{ab}(z)+\alpha,n_{ab}(z)-O_{ab}(z)+\beta).

(4)

We then sample from the conditional distribution of
z
given
A
and
P
by integrating out the probabilities
\pi
.
According to (
2
), the conjugacy between the multinomial and Dirichlet distributions gives the marginal probability of label assignments

{p}(z)=\frac{\Gamma(\gamma k)}{\Gamma(\gamma)^{k}\Gamma(n+\gamma k)}\prod_{a}\Gamma(n_{a}(z)+\gamma)

from which one obtains the
prior predictive
probabilities

{p}(z_{i}=a|z_{-i})=\frac{n_{a}(z_{-i})+\gamma}{n-1+\gamma k}

(5)

where
z_{-i}
is the vector of
n-1
assignments but the
i
th one. The full conditional of
z_{i}
is

\displaystyle{p}(z_{i}=a|z_{-i},A,P)

\displaystyle=\frac{{p}(z_{i}=a,A|z_{-i},P)}{{p}(A|z_{-i},P)}={p}(z_{i}=a|z_{-i})\frac{{p}(A|z_{i}=a,z_{-i},P)}{{p}(A|z_{-i},P)}

\displaystyle={p}(z_{i}=a|z_{-i})\frac{{p}(A|z_{i}=a,z_{-i},P)}{{p}(A_{-i}|z_{-i},P){p}(A_{i}|A_{-i},z_{-i},P)}

\displaystyle\propto{p}(z_{i}=a|z_{-i})\frac{{p}(A|z_{i}=a,z_{-i},P)}{{p}(A_{-i}|z_{-i},P)}

(6)

that is proportional to the product of the prior predictive probability and the
likelihood contribution to
z_{i}=a
:

\frac{{p}(A|z_{i}=a,z_{-i},P)}{{p}(A_{-i}|z_{-i},P)}=\prod_{j>i}P_{a\,z_{j}}^{A_{ij}}(1-P_{a\,z_{j}})^{(1-A_{ij})}\prod_{j<i}P_{z_{j}\,a}^{A_{ji}}(1-P_{z_{j}\,a})^{(1-A_{ji})}=\prod_{j\neq i}P_{a\,z_{j}}^{A_{ij}}(1-P_{a\,z_{j}})^{(1-A_{ij})}

(7)

Here
A_{i}
refers to the connections involving the
i
th node and
A_{-i}
to the
(n-1)\times(n-1)
adjacency matrix upon removal of the
i
th node.

2.1
Assortative SBM

There exist two different assortative conditions, cf.
Amini and Levina, 2018
. The
strong
assortative condition requires that any within probability is larger than the maximum between probability:

\max_{a\neq b}P_{ab}<\min_{a}P_{aa},

(8)

The weak condition relaxes the above by requiring a diagonal dominance of the matrix
P
:

q^{*}_{a}<P_{aa}\quad\text{for all }a,

(9)

where

q^{*}_{a}=\max_{b=a,c\neq a}P_{bc}=\max_{b\neq a,c=a}P_{bc}
.
We discuss first how to induce a stochastic order
q<p
between two random probabilities
p,q\in[0,1]
. A

q|p,u\sim u\,p
.
computationally convenient scheme consists in introducing an auxiliary variable
\epsilon\in[0,1]
, referred to as the
cutoff
, and set both
p
and
q
conditional on
\epsilon
as truncated on
(\epsilon,1)
and
(0,\epsilon)
, respectively. For illustration purposes we confine to
\epsilon
uniformly distributed on
(0,1)
,
\epsilon\sim\text{U}(0,1)
, extensions to
\text{beta}(\alpha,\beta)
distribution can be worked out similarly. We have

\displaystyle\epsilon\sim U(0,1),

\displaystyle p|\epsilon\sim U(\epsilon,1),\quad q|\epsilon\sim U(0,\epsilon).

The marginal and joint densities are then found to be:

\displaystyle f(p)=-\log(1-p),\quad f(q)=-\log q

\displaystyle f(p,q)=\int_{q}^{p}x^{-1}(1-x)^{-1}\mathrm{d}x=\log\frac{p(1-q)}{q(1-p)}\mathds{1}_{\{q<p\}}

Figure
1
visually represents these densities.

Figure 1:

The left panel illustrates the marginal of
p
and
q
, the grey horizontal line marks the uniform density. The right panel shows the joint density which is supported on
q<p
.

Henceforth we analyze here strong assortativity (
8
) and discuss how to implement weak assortativity (
9
) in Section
5
. We have in place of (
3
),

\displaystyle\epsilon

\displaystyle\sim U(0,1),

(10a)

\displaystyle P_{aa}|\epsilon

\displaystyle\overset{\text{iid}}{\sim}\text{U}(\epsilon,1),\quad a=1,\ldots,k,

(10b)

\displaystyle P_{ab}|\epsilon

\displaystyle\overset{\text{iid}}{\sim}\text{U}(0,\epsilon),\quad 1\leq a<b\leq k.

(10c)

As for the conditional joint distribution of
P
and
\epsilon
given
A
and
z
, we obtain

\{P_{aa}\}_{a},\{P_{ab}\}_{a<b},\epsilon|A,z\propto\epsilon^{-k(k-1)/2}(1-\epsilon)^{-k}\\
\prod_{a}P_{aa}^{O_{aa}(z)}(1-P_{aa})^{n_{aa}(z)-O_{aa}(z)}\mathds{1}_{\{P_{aa}>\epsilon\}}\prod_{a<b}P_{ab}^{O_{ab}(z)}(1-P_{ab})^{n_{ab}(z)-O_{ab}(z)}\mathds{1}_{\{P_{ab}<\epsilon\}}.

which leads to the following update for
P
:

\displaystyle P_{aa}|\epsilon,A,z

\displaystyle\sim\text{beta}_{(\epsilon,\ 1)}(O_{aa}(z)+1,n_{aa}(z)-O_{aa}(z)+1),\quad 1\leq a\leq k,

(11)

\displaystyle P_{ab}|\epsilon,A,z

\displaystyle\sim\text{beta}_{(0,\ \epsilon)}(O_{ab}(z)+1,n_{ab}(z)-O_{ab}(z)+1),\quad 1\leq a<b\leq k.

To sample from truncated beta, one can use numerical approximation of the incomplete beta function or use data augmentation as in
Damien and Walker, 2001
. As for the cut off
\epsilon
, we have

\epsilon|P,A,z\propto\epsilon^{-k(k-1)/2}(1-\epsilon)^{-k}\mathds{1}_{q<\epsilon<p\}},\quad p=\min_{a}P_{aa},\ q=\max_{a<b}P_{ab}.

(12)

Taking inspiration again from
Damien and Walker, 2001
, we can sample from Equations (
12
) by introducing a latent variable

which has joint density with
\epsilon
given by

\epsilon,y|P\propto\epsilon^{-k(k-1)/2}\mathds{1}_{\{y<(1-\epsilon)^{-k},\ q<\epsilon<p\}}

The full conditional for
y
is given by the uniform density on the interval
(0,(1-\epsilon)^{-k})
. The full conditional for
\epsilon
is

\epsilon|y,P\propto\epsilon^{-k(k-1)/2}\mathds{1}_{\{\max\{q,1-y^{-1/k}\}<\epsilon<p\}}

This is easily sampled using the inverse cdf technique.

2.2
Illustrative example

We consider a synthetic network generated according to a SBM and intentionally designed to highlight the effect of enforcing the assortativity constraint in Bayesian community detection. Specifically, the example demonstrates that without enforcing assortativity the posterior can fail to recover the communities. We work with a fixed and exactly specified number of communities.

The network, shown in the left panel of Figure
2
, consists of
3
communities: a large community (the core) with 60 nodes and dense internal connections (shown in red), and two smaller communities (the peripheries), each with 20 nodes (shown in green and blue). The two peripheries are each sparsely connected internally and with limited connections between each other. Connections are generated according to the connectivity matrix:

P=\begin{pmatrix}0.30&0.085&0.085\\
0.085&0.13&0.01\\
0.085&0.01&0.13\end{pmatrix}.

(13)

The adjacency matrix is shown in the right panel of Figure
2
. Detection of the two smaller communities is expected to be difficult because of both the limited number of connections within and between them and of their similar connectivity patterns with the central communities. We discuss this in more details later.

Figure 2:
Left: synthetic network featuring a large and densely connected community (red) with links to peripheral nodes, which belong to two smaller communities (blue and green). Right: adjacency matrix with node memberships shown by the colored bar.

We use Algorithms

2

P_{ab}
in the standard case, i.e.
\alpha=\beta=1

We run each algorithm 2000 times after a burn-in of 500 iterations. We repeat each run

Out of the 100 runs, the assortative method successfully identified 3 communities every times, whereas the standard method never did. The posterior clusterings of a representative run are displayed in Figure
3

To put the above results into context, the SBM used to generate the network displayed in Figure
2
corresponds to a weak-recovery scenario with respect to detecting the two peripheries is concerned. Consider the subnetwork consisting of these nodes, so
n=40
in total, with within-block probability of

, and a between-block probability of

. The Kensten-Stigum bound is barely satisfied

1
.
We recall here that the Kensten-Stigum bound characterizes the conditions under which polynomial-time algorithms can find a partition correlated with a
planted partition
– that is, equally sized groups with common within- and between-block probabilities. Therefore, when generating networks according to
P
in (
13

P_{13}
and
P_{23}
equal, the peripheral nodes behave similarly in their connections to the core, causing the likelihood to favor the two-cluster solution. To illustrate this, consider the complete likelihood (
1
) of the two competing clusterings.

, where

13

\hat{\pi}=(0.6,0.2,0.2)
represents the relative sizes corresponding to

. This is to be contrasted with
L^{\star}=p(A,z^{\star}|P^{\star},\hat{\pi}^{\star})
where
z^{\star}
is the label assignment corresponding to the two-cluster solution,
\hat{\pi}^{\star}=(0.6,0.4)
, and

is the corresponding

connectivity matrix

P^{\star}=\begin{pmatrix}0.30&0.085\\
0.085&0.068\end{pmatrix}.

Here
0.068
is the average degree for a peripheral node.
It turns out that

\log L_{\text{true}}=-1791.792

compared to

\log L^{\star}=-1800

when using for
A
the expected adjacency matrix, i.e., the node-wise connectivity matrix. When using the actual adiacency matrix as in Figure
3
, the values become
-2150.93
and
-2151.107
, respectively. Thus, we observe two nearly equivalent local maxima in the likelihood, which are likely to appear as posterior modes in the standard SBM as well. Enforcing assortativity through the prior prevents the posterior from placing mass around the
unfavorable mode
corresponding to the two-cluster solution. Therefore, the assortative SBM has an advantage in cases of intermediate signal strength.

To illustrate this, we conducted a more extensive simulation by generating

). For each network, we ran both the standard and the assortative Gibbs samplers for

The standard SBM estimated three clusters in
5

68

49

35

18

2
is representative of these

In conclusion, incorporating assortativity into Bayesian community detection can yield significantly different clustering results. In the next section, we address the case where the number of communities
k

3
Unknown number of communities

A key challenge in community detection is the estimation of the number of groups
k
. Bayesian inference is particularly well-suited for this task, as it offers a probabilistic framework for the joint inference of
k
, label assignments
z
and model parameters. By placing a prior distribution to
k
, one can construct an MCMC that updates
k
at each iteration, along with
z
and
P
. However this approach often requires requires complex MCMC moves to explore parameter spaces of varying dimension, such as reversible jump of
Green, 1995
, that is known to suffer from a lack of scalability and mixing issues. Depending on the model specification and prior choices, different marginalization strategies can be employed. For instance
McDaid et al., 2013
considered Poisson-weighted edges, and proposed integrating out both the assignment probabilities
\pi
and the block parameters, thereby extending the allocation sampler of
Nobile and Fearnside, 2007
to perform MCMC sampling over
k
and
z

This approach can also suffer from mixing issues due to the complexity of Metropolis moves that simultaneously update
(k,z)
. A related strategy is the microcanonical SBM formulation introduced by
Peixoto, 2017

z
(see below). Moreover, a maximum entropy prior is placed on block parameters, which induces an hard constraint on the number of connections between communities.

k

4
Simulation study

In Section
2.2
we considered a synthetic network where the inclusion of the assortativity constraint in Bayesian SBM estimation significantly improved community detection. Here, we further investigate the benefits of assortativity when the number of communities
k
is unknown. To this end, we conduct an extensive simulation study generating networks according to
Lancichinetti et al., 2008
, which provides state of the art benchmarks for evaluating community detection algorithms. Networks are generated so to reflect realistic features that the SBM fails to capture, such as flexible degree and community size distributions. Each network is generated with parameters including the number of nodes
n
, average degree
\langle d\rangle
, maximum degree
d_{max}
, mixing parameter
\mu
(ratio of out-group to total connections), and power-law exponents
t_{1}
and
t_{2}
controlling degree and community size distributions, respectively. Community sizes ranges between
n_{min}
and
n_{max}
. We generated
N=100
networks with
n=200
,
t_{1}=t_{2}=2
,
n_{min}=5
,
n_{max}=50
,
d_{max}=49
, and for each combination of mixing parameter
\mu\in\{0.1,\dots,0.6\}
and mean degree
\langle d\rangle\in\{10,15,20,25\}
,
24
combinations in total. We repeated the same experiment with
N=50
realizations for
n=400
nodes (results not reported here). The choices follow the guidelines of
Lancichinetti et al., 2008
to ensure realistic and challenging conditions for community detection.

Each network is analyzed by three runs of the assortative SBM Gibbs sampler (Algorithm
4
, referred to as
a
-sbm) and three runs of the standard Gibbs sampler (Algorithm
3
, referred to as
sbm
). Different label initializations are used in each run to ensure robustness. Each run generates
900
posterior samples after a burn-in of
100
iterations. These three sets of
900
posterior samples, henceforth referred to as individual
run
, are used to compute the potential scale reduction factor
\hat{R}

(
Gelman et al., 1995
, Chapter 11)
for the parameter
P_{z_{1}z_{1}}
. Convergence is assessed using the standard practical threshold
\hat{R}<1.1
.

The results are summarized in Figure
6
. Each row corresponds to a different average degree
\langle d\rangle
arranged in increasing order. The horizontal axis in each plot represents the mixing parameter
\mu
. The left panel displys the percentage of runs that achieved convergence (
\hat{R}<1.1
). For these converged runs, the middle panel shows the adjusted Rand index (ARI) comparing the posterior clusterings to the true community assignments, while the right panel presents the error in estimating the number of communities

.

As expected, a higher average degree
\langle d\rangle
corresponds to a stronger signal in the network, leading to more accurate community detection. Performance also varies with the mixing parameter
\mu
; in particular, it deteriorates as
\mu
increases, reflecting the less distinct community structure.
As shown in Figure
6
, the assortative SBM consistently outperforms standard SBM in terms of ARI. A similar trend is observed in the recovery of the number of communities. Both methods tend to overestimate the number of components when
\mu
is small, with this tendency diminishing as
\mu
increases. For low average degrees, both methods tend to underestimate
k
when
\mu
is large. For denser networks, the overall estimation error decreases, and the assortative method shows a slight tendency to overestimate
k
compared to the standard SBM. Overall, the assortative SBM achieves lower error, indicating improved precision in estimating the number of communities.

Interestingly, the number of runs for which the samplers achieved convergence (
\hat{R}_{1}<1.1
) also varies with
\mu
. For moderate to high average degrees, smaller values of
\mu
are associated to higher convergence rates for both the non-assortative and assortative methods. Notably, the assortative sampler consistently shows a greater proportion of converged chains compared to the non-assortative counterpart. This improved convergence is likely due to the reduced posterior space induced by the assortativity constraint, which limits the number of possible configurations, see discussion in Section
2.2
.

5
Discussion

In this paper, we investigated the role of assortativity in Bayesian community detection based on stochastic block modeling. We focus on the case where the number of communities is unknown and estimated together with node partition. We introduce an hard constraint by placing a prior on the edge probabilities which which enforces stronger connectivity within than between communities. Despite the loss of conjugacy and associated analytical tractability, we developed an efficient marginal Gibbs sampler for posterior inference. Through a motivating example, we argued that enforcing assortativity helps identify communities in weak recovery regimes by steering the posterior away from clustering solutions that contradict the assortative structure.
An extensive simulation study based on benchmark networks further confirmed that the proposed method improves community detection accuracy, particularly in challenging settings characterized by low signals and heterogeneity in node degree and community sizes.

We intend to extend the proposed estimation procedure to accommodate
weak assortativity
(
9
), where the edge probability within a community is constrained to be greater than the probabilities between that community and others. This can be achieved by introducing a cut off variable for each community and appropriately modifying the Gibbs sampler. As discussed in
Jiang and Tokdar, 2021
, weak assortativity
offers a minimal yet effective modeling condition for recovering the node partition through accurate estimation of the node-wise connectivity matrix. While theoretical results for community detection remains limited, see
Gao et al., 2020
;
Geng et al., 2019
, investigating posterior asymptotics to support our empirical findings represents a promising direction for future work. We also aim to speed up mixing and improve computational efficiency of the marginal Gibbs sampler by exploring batch updates rather than sequential update of label assignments, in line with ideas put forward by
Zhang and Zhou, 2020
.
