The future of prostate cancer screening
Authors: eClinicalMedicine
Published: 2025

In November, 2025, the UK National Screening Committee (NSC) advised against population-wide prostate specific antigen (PSA)-based prostate cancer screening for all men in their draft recommendations. Instead, the committee recommends a more narrowly targeted PSA screening every 2 years for men aged 45–61 years with confirmed BRCA1 or BRCA2 gene mutations. With the finalised recommendations due in March next year, there is likely to be much debate over this decision in the coming months.

Prostate cancer represents a substantial public health challenge. In the UK, more than 63,000 men are diagnosed and more than 12,000 die from the disease each year. Globally, it is the most common cancer among men in more than 110 countries and is a leading cause of cancer mortality with 397,430 related deaths reported in 2022. The 2024 Lancet Commission on prostate cancer estimated that prostate cancer cases will rise from 1.4 million in 2020 to 2.9 million by 2040, with deaths increasing by approximately 85%. Prostate cancer screening has been a contentious issue due to the consequential uncertainty of the results and the delicate balance between benefits and harms. PSA testing has been widely implemented in clinical practice but is mired in controversy because of the likelihood of overdiagnosis and overtreatment. Although early detection is important, PSA-based screening might detect cancers that could be slow-growing and never have led to morbidity or mortality but miss some of the faster-growing more aggressive cancers that present a greater risk to health.

With the 23-year follow-up of the European Study of Prostate Cancer Screening showing that PSA-based screening led to a 13% relative reduction in the risk of prostate-cancer-specific mortality, and the CAP trial in the UK showing reduced prostate cancer deaths at a median follow-up of 15 years, there is little room for doubt that PSA-based screening does reduce mortality to some extent. However, this is somewhat offset by studies showing that screening could cause harm. A population-based study across 26 European countries found an up to 20-fold variation in prostate cancer incidence contrasted by a corresponding five-fold variation in mortality between 1980 and 2020, indicative of overdiagnosis. The PIVOT trial demonstrated that radical prostatectomy did not significantly improve survival over observation in men with low-risk, primarily PSA-detected cancers, and microsimulation analyses have suggested that modest survival benefits from PSA screening can be offset by quality-of-life losses due to overtreatment. Unnecessary further investigations such as biopsies and early intervention with surgery or radiotherapy can result in morbidity, with potential harms such as urinary incontinence, sexual dysfunction, and reduced physical functioning.

The NSC decision on one hand makes sense as a precautionary approach reflecting long-standing concern that routine population-level PSA testing might subject many men to unnecessary investigations and treatment without proportional benefit. On the other hand, specific population groups such as Black men have disproportionate burden and more frequently present with advanced disease, and excluding these groups from targeted early detection programmes leaves these individuals at greater risk. Opportunistic PSA testing will remain available and can be requested by men aged older than 50 years, but many men with risk factors might not know that they have increased risk. BRCA mutations are rare, and genetic testing is not done routinely, so status is unknown for most men, which limits the effect of the targeted screening recommendation. The decision is also contrasted by other international recommendations, such as that from the European Commission in 2022 recommending national prostate cancer screening across all 27 member states of the EU. Subsequently, the PRAISE-U project is evaluating the feasibility, efficacy, and cost-effectiveness of screening, with the findings to be reported in April, 2026. Ultimately, policies on screening programmes need continuous refinement to ensure that they do more good than harm.

Modern diagnostic pathways with PSA followed by pre-biopsy MRI and targeted biopsy, and subsequent active surveillance and refinements in therapy, such as focal therapy, image-guided radiotherapy, and robotic surgery, hold potential to reduce harms of overdiagnosis and overtreatment compared with screening in earlier eras. Additionally, research is ongoing using artificial intelligence to aid in the accuracy of prostate cancer diagnosis, which could improve detection of clinically significant cases while reducing false positives. Furthermore, the TRANSFORM trial, the largest prostate cancer screening trial of this generation, will test modern multimodal screening strategies at scale, with different combinations of PSA, genetic testing, and rapid MRI-based diagnostics in men aged 45–74 years in the UK. Results of large-scale modern trials such as these and others of new approaches are eagerly awaited to see how they might change the risk–benefit balance, which could create a new evidentiary foundation for targeted screening programmes.

For now, clinicians should be guided by individual risk and evolving evidence, and shared decision making should be promoted in primary care for men with identifiable risk factors, with an aim to give balanced information about the benefits and harms of PSA testing. Considering the NSC recommendation, opportunistic PSA testing should be made safer by standardising follow-up pathways and using evidence-based testing. Currently, rates of PSA testing are low among men in their 50s and high among men older than 70 years (the age group who are least likely to benefit from screening), which represents an inappropriate distribution and should be considered in the approach to opportunistic testing.

The NSC recommendation should be viewed as interim guidance while the results of definitive, modern trials that could change the way we think about prostate cancer screening are awaited. If TRANSFORM and similar studies confirm that contemporary approaches can save lives with minimal harm, we must be ready to adapt screening policies to ensure maximum benefit. Until then, shared decision making and careful evidence-based stewardship remains the best course.
