The free propagator of strongly anisotropic systems with free surfaces
M.A. Shpot

Abstract
A brief overview of fluctuation-induced forces in statistical systems with film geometry at the critical point and the calculation of Casimir amplitudes, which characterize these forces quantitatively, is presented. Particular attention is paid to the special features of strongly anisotropic m m -axis systems at the Lifshitz point, specifically, in the case of a “perpendicular” orientation of surfaces with free boundary conditions. Beyond the simplest one-loop approximation, calculations of Casimir amplitudes are impossible without knowledge of the Gaussian propagator, which corresponds to the lines of Feynman diagrams in the perturbation theory. We present an explicit expression for such a propagator in the case of an anisotropic system confined by parallel surfaces perpendicular to one of the anisotropy axes. Using this propagator, we reproduce the one-loop result derived earlier in an essentially different way. The knowledge of the propagator provides the possibility of higher-order calculations in perturbation theory.



1
Fluctuation-induced forces in constrained systems

Theoretical studies of spatially constrained systems are particularly important because all real physical objects have boundary surfaces and finite dimensions. Moreover, such objects exhibit effects that are absent in ideal model systems without spatial constraints. One example is the Casimir effect in critical systems, which involves the emergence of fluctuation-induced forces of attraction or repulsion between boundary surfaces. This effect arises from long-range fluctuations as temperature approaches the critical point of a phase transition in infinite volume. The magnitude of such fluctuation-induced interactions can be measured experimentally
[
1
,
2
,
3
]
and is determined by the values of the so-called Casimir amplitudes. The Casimir effect in critical systems (or thermodynamic Casimir effect) is considered in a number of reviews:
[
4
,
5
,
6
,
7
,
8
]
. The last one pays particular attention to experimental aspects.

The name of the effect comes from the Dutch physicist Casimir, who showed in 1948
[
9
]
that parallel conducting plates placed in vacuum at distance
L
are attracted due to the existence of zero vacuum fluctuations of the electromagnetic field.
Casimir calculated the interaction energy
\delta E(L)
between these surfaces per unit area
and obtained the result

\delta E(L)/A=\hbar c\Big(-\frac{\pi^{2}}{720}\Big)\,L^{-3},

(1)

where
A
is the area of the plates. Subsequently, the coefficient
-\pi^{2}/720

became known as the Casimir amplitude. By differentiating
\delta E(L)/A
with respect to
L
, Casimir derived an expression for the attractive force between the plates as

|\mathcal{F}|=\hbar c\,\frac{\pi^{2}}{240}\,L^{-4}=0.013\,L_{\mu}^{-4}\;{\rm dyne/cm^{2}},

(2)

where
L_{\mu}
is the distance between surfaces measured in microns. He noted that this force could be measured experimentally, which was successfully accomplished in
[
10
]
.

The Casimir effect was introduced to statistical physics through the remarkable work
of Fisher and de Gennes in 1978
[
11
]
.
They predicted similar attraction between parallel surfaces immersed in a fluid
at the critical point.
Based on scaling analysis of the singular part of the free energy of such a system,
these authors obtained for the interaction energy of surfaces the estimate

\delta E(L)/A\sim-k_{B}T\,L^{-2},

(3)

which is the analog of Casimir’s formula (
1
) in statistical physics with the Boltzmann constant
k_{\rm B}
and the absolute temperature
T
.
In three-dimensional space, the corresponding attractive force decreases according to the power law
\sim L^{-3}
.

In 1981, Symanzik
[
12
]
showed that an analog of the Casimir effect exists
in massless (super)renormalizable quantum field theory with
g\phi^{4}
interaction in

d=4-\varepsilon
dimensions and film geometry
\infty^{d-1}\times L
.
He proved the finiteness of Casimir energy
\delta E(L)
for a pair of
parallel surfaces with zero Dirichlet boundary conditions to all orders of perturbation theory, and obtained an explicit expression for this energy to first order in the coupling constant
g

[
12
, p. 12]
. In
[
12
]
this result is presented without computational details. It was later reproduced in the context of statistical physics in
[
13
, (5.11)]
using momentum representation. Its derivation in real space using
integrals of products of Hurwitz zeta functions was performed in
[
14
]
.

It turned out that in
d
-dimensional space,
\delta E(L)\sim L^{d-1}
. This scaling dependence generalizes the powers of
L
that appear in formulas (
1
) and (
3
) across the interval
2\leq d\leq 4
, where the corresponding Casimir force scales as

F\sim L^{-d}
. In the marginal case
d=2
, a logarithmic correction appears.
According to Fisher and de Gennes
[
11
]
,
\delta E(L)\sim(\ln L)/L
.

Formally, the same theory in the field-theoretic approach describes
critical phenomena in classical statistical systems.
It was precisely in this context that Krech and Dietrich
[
13
,
15
]
performed their calculations.
Here, Casimir amplitudes were first determined for statistical physics systems
with
n
-component order parameters and various boundary conditions
using the Wilson-Fisher
\varepsilon
-expansion
[
16
]
.
This work stimulated a large number of theoretical
[
4
,
17
,
6
,
18
]
,
experimental
[
1
,
2
,
3
]
and
numerical
[
19
,
20
,
21
,
22
,
23
]
studies
of the Casimir effect in statistical systems.
Long-range forces of similar character are extremely important for nanophysics and
practical applications in producing devices of minimal size. Problems of this type are reviewed in
[
24
]
; for a quite recent survey see
[
25
]
.

In their pioneering works, both Symanzik and Krech and Dietrich determined Casimir amplitudes only to first order in
\varepsilon
. Subsequently, Diehl Grüneberg and Shpot
[
26
]
extended the calculation to next order for systems with periodic and special boundary conditions. They showed that due to the infrared behavior of certain Feynman diagrams, the
\varepsilon
-expansion of Casimir amplitudes loses analyticity beyond first order for these specific boundary conditions.
Instead of the expected
O(\varepsilon^{2})
corrections,
O(\varepsilon^{3/2})
terms were discovered, and their coefficients were calculated explicitly.
It was also shown that such
\varepsilon
-expansions contain not only integer powers of
\varepsilon
, but also fractional powers
\varepsilon^{k/2}
with
k\geq 3
and powers of
\ln\varepsilon
.
The key insight was to isolate the dangerous zero mode and construct the associated effective Hamiltonian
\mathcal{H}^{(d-1)}_{\rm eff}
by integrating out non-zero modes.

Until this point, we have discussed homogeneous systems obeying isotropic scale invariance. This means that in the absence of boundaries, these systems remain ”self-similar” when distances
\Delta x
along any direction are transformed via
\Delta x=\ell\Delta x^{\prime}
, where
\ell
is an arbitrary scale factor.
In homogeneous systems, the correlation length
\xi
exhibits the same power law
singularities
\sim|T/T_{c}-1|^{-\nu}
in all directions as the temperature approaches the critical point
T_{c}
.

However, systems with inhomogeneities that break global translational invariance have one or several special directions (anisotropy axes) along which the scale factor must be changed by a non-trivial power
\ell^{\theta}
with respect the original
\ell=\Delta x_{\beta}/\Delta x_{\beta}^{\prime}
related to the remaining (”usual”) directions.

In this case, correlation lengths
\xi_{\beta}
and
\xi_{\alpha}
obey
the asymptotic power laws
\xi_{\beta,\alpha}\sim|T/T_{c}-1|^{-\nu_{\beta,\alpha}}

with different exponents
\nu_{\beta}
and
\nu_{\alpha}=\theta\nu_{\beta}
;
the quantity
\theta
is called the anisotropy exponent.
Systems of this kind are said to possess anisotropic scale invariance
[
27
,
28
,
29
]
and are termed
strongly anisotropic systems
.
Significant differences of physical properties along different axes cannot be eliminated by
merely changing the values of theoretical parameters.

In nature, there exist many physical objects with such properties.
Important examples are magnets such as MnP
[
30
,
31
,
32
]
,
solids with structural phase transitions
[
33
,
34
]
,
liquid crystals
[
35
,
36
,
37
]
, and ferroelectrics
[
38
,
39
,
40
]
.
They are characterized by complex phase diagrams with a special (multi)critical
Lifshitz point
[
41
,
42
,
43
,
44
]
, in the vicinity of which
anisotropic scale invariance is valid.

The theory of spatial constraining effects in such systems is still in the development stage
[
45
,
46
,
47
,
48
,
49
]
.
Since strongly anisotropic systems have different physical properties
in
\alpha\,
- and
\beta\,
-directions, it is expected that the presence of surfaces leads
to more complex effects compared to the case of isotropic systems.
Indeed, in the presence of anisotropy, forces induced by restricting the fluctuations in space differ both quantitatively and qualitatively from those considered at the beginning of this section.
In particular, the critical Casimir effect at the Lifshitz point depends on the orientation of boundaries with respect to the directions of anisotropy axes.
Thus, in
[
45
]
, two fundamentally different variants of surface orientation were identified:
“parallel” orientation, in which surfaces are parallel to all anisotropy axes, and
“perpendicular”, when one of the anisotropy axes is orthogonal to the surfaces.
The Casimir forces arising in strongly anisotropic systems
obey the scaling laws
[
45
]

\displaystyle{\mathcal{F}}_{C}\mathop{\propto}_{L\to\infty}\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}\sigma^{-m/4}\Delta^{\rm BC}_{\|}L^{-\lambda_{\|}},\\
\sigma^{(d-m)/(4\theta)}\Delta^{\rm BC}_{\perp}L^{-\lambda_{\|}/\theta},\end{array}\right.\quad\mbox{where}\quad\lambda_{\|}=d-m+\theta m,

and
m
is the number of anisotropy axes.
Here
\Delta^{\rm BC}_{\|}
and
\Delta^{\rm BC}_{\perp}
are Casimir amplitudes
corresponding to parallel and perpendicular orientations. Their values also depend on boundary conditions at the surfaces (as indicated by the superscripts “
{\rm BC}
”).
A note on the scale factor
\sigma^{1/4}
can be found in the context of eq. (
17
) below.

The decay exponent
\lambda_{\perp}=\lambda_{\|}/\theta
for
perpendicular orientation is
1/\theta\approx 2
times larger
than for parallel orientation, and the corresponding long-range effective forces
manifest themselves, accordingly, to a significantly lesser degree.
The estimate
1/\theta\approx 2
stems from the observation that at the Lifshitz point
[
41
,
42
,
43
,
44
]

the anisotropy index
\theta
is given by
\theta=(2-\eta_{\perp})/(4-\eta_{\|})
, where
the critical correlation exponents
\eta_{\perp}
and
\eta_{\|}
are small.
In the framework of the
\varepsilon
-expansion, both of them are of order
O(\varepsilon^{2})

[
50
,
51
]
.

2
Thermodynamics of the critical Casimir effect in strongly anisotropic systems

In anisotropic systems with film geometry, the dependence of Casimir forces on surface orientations manifests itself in the scaling behavior of the basic thermodynamic quantity, the
residual free energy

f_{\rm res}^{a,b}(L)
.
This important quantity is conventionally defined as a part of the free energy density per unit area (see e.g.
[
26
]
),

f_{L}\equiv\lim_{A\to\infty}\frac{F}{Ak_{B}T}=L\,f_{\infty}+f_{s}^{a}+f_{s}^{b}+f_{\rm res}^{a,b}(L)\;.

(7)

Here,
F=-k_{B}T\ln Z
is the total free energy of a
d
-dimensional system in a slab of thickness
L
,
Z
is the corresponding partition function, and
A
is the area
of (infinite)
(d-1)
-dimensional hypersurfaces constraining the layer.
Further,
f_{\infty}
is the free energy density per unit volume of the bulk system without geometric constraints,
and
f_{s}^{a}+f_{s}^{b}
is the total free energy density per unit area of both surfaces.
The indices
a
and
b
denote both the surfaces themselves and the type of boundary conditions for each of them: These boundary conditions can be different.
The last term in (
7
) is the residual free energy
f_{\rm res}^{a,b}(L)
— the key thermodynamic quantity in our analysis.
Beyond its usual dependence on the temperature and slab thickness
L
, in the case of strongly anisotropic systems the orientation of surfaces has essential significance
[
45
]
.
Thus, in dealing with strongly anisotropic systems,
we shall denote the parallel and perpendicular orientations by the index
\iota=\left\{\|,\perp\right\}
.

According to the finite-size scaling theory
[
52
,
45
]
, on sufficient large length scales, the function
f_{\rm res}^{a,b}(L)
attains the scaling form

f_{\rm res}^{a,b}(L)\approx L^{-\zeta_{\iota}}\,\Theta_{\iota}^{a,b}(L/\xi_{\infty})\,.

(8)

Here,
\xi_{\infty}
is the correlation length of the bulk system
and
\Theta_{\iota}^{a,b}(x)
is a universal scaling function depending
on the bulk universality class of the system, its geometry, types of boundaries and their orientation with respect to the anisotropy axes.
The decay exponent
\zeta_{\iota}
is also universal. In the special case of
isotropic systems (
m=0
) with short-range interactions, it is defined only by the space dimension
d
and equals
\zeta_{\iota}=d-1
(cf.
[
26
]
).
For an
m
-axial strongly anisotropic system in
d
dimensions, we have (cf. (
1
) and
[
45
, (1.4)]
)

\zeta_{\|}=d-m+\theta\,m-1\quad\quad\mbox{and}\quad\quad\zeta_{\perp}=(d-m)/\theta+m-1\;.

(9)

Both the decay exponents
\zeta_{\iota}
and the Casimir amplitudes

\Delta_{\iota}^{\rm BC}
with
\iota=\|
and
\perp

depend not only on boundary conditions at surfaces, but also on their orientation in anisotropic space.

When the temperature equals the critical temperature of the infinite system and the bulk correlation length
\xi_{\infty}
becomes infinite,
the quantity
f_{\rm res}^{a,b}(L)
behaves as

\displaystyle f_{{\rm res}}^{a,b}(L)\approx\Theta_{\iota}^{a,b}(0)\,L^{-\zeta_{\iota}}\equiv\Delta^{\rm BC}_{\iota}\,L^{-\zeta_{\iota}}

(10)

in the limit
L\to\infty
.
For a given medium and type of surfaces, the value of the scaling function
\Theta_{\iota}^{a,b}(0)

determines the Casimir amplitude,
\Theta_{\iota}^{a,b}(0)\equiv\Delta_{\iota}^{\rm BC}
, the proportionality constant that already appeared in (
1
).
Again, it is a universal quantity depending only on the most important general characteristic features of the system.

The force induced by long-range fluctuations between surfaces at the Lifshitz point is given by

\frac{{\mathcal{F}}_{C}}{k_{B}T_{LP}}=-\frac{\partial}{\partial L}f_{\rm res}^{a,b}(L;T=T_{LP})=\zeta_{\iota}\Delta_{\iota}^{\rm BC}L^{-(\zeta_{\iota}+1)},\qquad\iota=\left\{\|,\perp\right\}.

(11)

This is equivalent to the formula (
1
) above.
Negative values of Casimir amplitudes
\Delta^{\rm BC}
correspond to
attractive forces between surfaces, while positive values correspond to repulsive ones.

The relations (
1
) and (
11
) are quite general. They are valid for

d
-dimensional short-ranged interaction systems with an arbitrary number of anisotropy axes

m
and non-classical value of the anisotropy exponent
\theta
.
In the special case of classical Gaussian theories with
d=3
,
m=2
and

\theta=1/2
, equations (
1
) and (
11
) reduce to that predicted in

[
53
]
:
{\mathcal{F}}_{\|}\propto\sigma^{-1/2}L^{-2}
and

{\mathcal{F}}_{\perp}\propto\sigma^{1/2}L^{-4}
.

The first non-classical expressions for Casimir amplitudes
\Delta_{\iota}^{\rm per}

for strongly anisotropic slab systems with different boundary conditions
and both variants of surface orientation were obtained in
[
45
]
.
The case of free Dirichlet-type boundary conditions combined with the perpendicular orientation is the most complex to treat, and we shall focus on it in what follows.

3
Anisotropic systems with free boundary conditions

Model systems with free boundary conditions
are attractive from a physics perspective because they
provide a more or less realistic representation of corresponding spatially confined physical objects.
However, the absence of translational invariance (unlike periodic boundary conditions)
in such systems significantly complicates their mathematical description.
The presence of anisotropy further complicates the situation.

The case of parallel surface orientation in systems with
free boundary conditions is technically simpler,
and in Ref.
[
45
]
it was possible to calculate the corresponding Casimir amplitudes
to order
O(\varepsilon)
(where
\varepsilon=4+m/2-d
), as well as in the spherical limit
of the Stanley
n
-vector model,
n\to\infty
.
Imposing free boundary conditions on surfaces of an isotropic system
implies that it undergoes an ordinary transition at the critical point,
T=T_{c}

[
54
]
.
When considering perpendicular surface orientation and the Lifshitz point in stongly anisotropic systems, free boundary conditions (
{\rm F}
) correspond to the couple of conditions

\phi=\partial_{n}\phi=0

(12)

at the boundary
[
55
]
, where
\partial_{n}
denotes the derivative along the inward normal to the surface, that is,
along one of the anisotropy axes.
For this geometry, in Ref.
[
45
]
the Casimir amplitude

\Delta^{\rm FF}_{\perp,\rm LP}
was calculated
in the Gaussian approximation, which yielded only the leading term
of zeroth order in the possible
\varepsilon
-expansion.
The superscript
{\rm FF}
indicates that the same free boundary conditions apply to the both boundaries of the film.

To obtain this result, the required partition function
Z
was written in
[
45
]

in the form of a functional integral

Z=\int\!\mathcal{D}\phi\;{\rm e}^{-\mathcal{H}_{0}^{\rm LP}[\phi]}\prod_{\bm{r}}\prod_{j=1}^{2}\delta\left(\bm{\phi}(\bm{r},z_{j})\right)\,\delta\left(\partial_{z}\bm{\phi}(\bm{r},z_{j})\right),

(13)

similarly as in the approach used in
[
56
,
57
]
.
Here, the functional integration extends over field configurations

\bm{\phi}(\bm{x})=\{\phi_{i}(\bm{x}),\,i=1,\ldots,n\}

for all
\bm{x}
in infinite space
\mathbb{R}^{d}
,
while the geometric constraints together with the necessary boundary conditions are imposed by introducing
appropriate delta functions. These delta functions ensure
that the surface fields and their normal derivatives vanish on surfaces
passing through the points
z_{1}=0
and
z_{2}=L
, and also separate the interior
volume of the film of interest from the rest of space outside its boundaries
[
12
,
58
,
59
]
.
According to the present perpendicular configuration, the
z
-axis
coincides with one of the
\alpha
-directions (see page
1
).
Finally,
\mathcal{H}_{0}^{\rm LP}[\phi]
is the

translationally invariant
Gaussian effective
Hamiltonian of the bulk system directly at the Lifshitz point
[
41
]
.
It contains only quadratic terms in the field,

\mathcal{H}_{0}^{\rm LP}[\phi]=\frac{1}{2}\int d^{d-m}x_{\beta}\int d^{m}x_{\alpha}\Big(|\nabla_{\beta}\bm{\phi}|^{2}+|\Delta_{\alpha}\bm{\phi}|^{2}\Big),

(14)

where the gradient operator
\nabla_{\beta}
acts along the
\beta
-directions in
the subspace
\mathbb{R}_{\beta}^{d-m}
, and
the Laplacian operator
\Delta_{\alpha}\equiv\nabla_{\alpha}^{2}
acts along the anisotropy axes
in the subspace
\mathbb{R}_{\alpha}^{m}
of the full physical space of dimension
d
.

The result of the functional integration in (
13
), performed in
[
45
]
, is
a representation of the Casimir amplitude
\Delta^{\rm FF}_{\perp,\rm LP}(d,m,n)

with
1\leq m\leq d
in the form of a double integral (at the endpoints of the interval,
m=1
and
m=d
, it reduces to single integrals)

\displaystyle\Delta^{\rm FF}_{\perp,\rm LP}=

\displaystyle\frac{n}{2}K_{d-m}K_{m-1}\int_{0}^{\infty}dp\,p^{d-m-1}\int_{0}^{\infty}dq\,q^{m-2}

\displaystyle\ln\!\Big[2{\rm e}^{-2\kappa_{+}}\!\Big(\mbox{ch\,}2\kappa_{+}+\frac{\kappa_{+}^{2}}{\kappa_{-}^{2}}\cos 2\kappa_{-}-1-\frac{\kappa_{+}^{2}}{\kappa_{-}^{2}}\Big)\Big],

(15)

where

\kappa_{\mp}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}\sqrt{\sqrt{p^{2}+q^{4}}\mp q^{2}}\,,

(16)

and
p
and
q
are the absolute values of momentum variables conjugate to the position vectors

\bm{x}_{\beta}\in\mathbb{R}_{\beta}^{d-m}
and
\bm{x}_{\alpha}\in\mathbb{R}_{\alpha}^{m-1}
.
We recall that in the present perpendicular orientation all vectors

\bm{x}_{\beta}\in\mathbb{R}_{\beta}^{d-m}
and
\bm{x}_{\alpha}\in\mathbb{R}_{\alpha}^{m-1}

lie in
(d{-}1)
-dimensional hyperplanes

\mathbb{R}_{\beta}^{d-m}\oplus\mathbb{R}_{\alpha}^{m-1}
parallel to the surfaces
.

We emphasize that the result (
3
) was obtained using an artificial procedure
without explicit knowledge of the Gaussian propagator for the considered system.
We cannot proceed in the same way to derive higher orders of perturbation theory
in powers of
\varepsilon=4+m/2-d
.
To construct higher-order Feynman diagrams, it is necessary to know the free propagator of the theory. This result will be presented in the next section.

4
Free propagator of a film with perpendicular orientation of free surfaces

At the Lifshitz point, the Gaussian theory of a film system with perpendicular orientation of free surfaces is described by the effective Hamiltonian (
14
), in which the spatial integration along one of the anisotropy axes,
z\equiv x_{\alpha}^{(m)}
, is restricted to a finite interval
[0,L]
:

\mathcal{H}_{0\perp}^{\rm LP}[\phi]=\frac{1}{2}\int d^{d-m}x_{\beta}\int d^{m-1}x_{\alpha}\int_{0}^{L}dz\Big(|\nabla_{\beta}\bm{\phi}|^{2}+\sigma_{0}|\Delta_{\alpha}\bm{\phi}|^{2}\Big).

(17)

For completeness, we have included here the dimensional coefficient
\sigma_{0}
in front of
|\Delta_{\alpha}\bm{\phi}|^{2}
. This parameter provides the scale
\sigma_{0}^{1/4}
for ”measuring” distances in the parallel
\alpha
-subspace. The classical dimension of this scale factor is
[\sigma_{0}^{1/4}]=[x_{\beta}/x_{\alpha}^{1/2}]
.
Its renormalized counterpart
\sigma
(see
[
50
,
51
,
45
]
) appeared above in relations (
1
). However, for notational simplicity we set
\sigma_{0}=1
in what follows. If needed, the dependence on this parameter can be recovered by scaling arguments.
The momenta conjugate to the perpendicular and parallel coordinates
\bm{x}_{\beta}
and
\bm{x}_{\alpha}
will be denoted, as before, by
\bm{p}
and
\bm{q}
, respectively, with absolute values
p
and
q
.

To find the mixed momentum-coordinate
pqz
-representation of the free propagator of a film with free surfaces of perpendicular orientation at the Lifshitz point,

G_{\perp}^{\rm FF}(p,q;z_{1},z_{2})
, we must solve the inhomogeneous fourth-order differential equation with a Dirac delta function on the right-hand side,

\left[\Big(\frac{d^{2}}{dz_{1}^{2}}-q^{2}\Big)^{2}+p^{2}\right]G_{\perp}^{\rm FF}(p,q;z_{1},z_{2})=\delta(z_{1}-z_{2}),

(18)

for
z_{1},z_{2}\in[0,L]
subject to four boundary conditions (cf. (
12
))

\left.G_{\perp}^{\rm FF}(p,q;z_{1},z_{2})\right|_{z_{i}\in\mathcal{B}_{i}}=\partial_{z_{i}}\left.G_{\perp}^{\rm FF}(p,q;z_{1},z_{2})\right|_{z_{i}\in\mathcal{B}_{i}}=0\quad\;\mbox{with}\quad i=1,2,

(19)

where
\mathcal{B}_{1}
and
\mathcal{B}_{2}
denote the boundaries at
z=0
and
z=L
.

For compactness, we use simplified symbolic notation, in terms of which equation (
18
) is written as

\bm{L}\,G(x,\xi)=\delta(x-\xi),\quad\quad\mbox{where}\quad\quad\bm{L}\equiv\frac{d^{4}}{dx^{4}}-q_{1}\frac{d^{2}}{dx^{2}}+q_{0},

(20)

and
G(x,\xi)
is the Green’s function.
The constant coefficients of the differential operator
\bm{L}
are denoted by
q_{1}
and
q_{0}
to maintain contact with the notation in
[
60
]
, which will be extensively used in what follows. For our equation (
18
) we have

q_{1}=2q^{2}=2(\kappa_{+}^{2}-\kappa_{-}^{2})\;\quad\mbox{and}\quad\;q_{0}=p^{2}+q^{4}=(\kappa_{-}^{2}+\kappa_{+}^{2})^{2},

(21)

where the combinations
\kappa_{\mp}=(1/\sqrt{2})\sqrt{\sqrt{p^{2}+q^{4}}\mp q^{2}}
, as in (
3
).

The general theory of Green’s functions can be found in many monographs on differential equations and methods of mathematical physics (see, e.g.,
[
61
,
62
,
63
]
).
In these works, the treatment of equations of order higher than second is either too abstract for practical purposes or is limited to descriptions of examples involving only
\bm{L}
with
q_{1}=q_{0}=0
.
On the other hand, our problem is a special case of the Sturm-Liouville problem for fourth-order differential equations. This has been solved by Everitt in 1957
[
60

with elegance and at the same time on a very practical level.
In what follows, we derive the explicit expression for the propagator

G_{\perp}^{\rm FF}(p,q;z_{1},z_{2})
from equation (

The general properties of the Green’s function

are well known

,

,

•

as a function of
x
, for
x\in[0,\xi[
and
x\in]\xi,L]
it satisfies the homogeneous equation
\bm{L}\,G(x,\xi)=0
with prescribed initial conditions and has continuous derivatives up to fourth order.

•

at
x=\xi
its third derivative with respect to
x
has a finite jump given by
1
1

1

This can be easily seen by integrating both sides of the equation
(
20
) from
\xi-\epsilon
to
\xi+\epsilon
and taking into
account that the value of the integral of the delta function in this

By writing differential equations in (
18
) and (

)
we use the sign convention opposite to that employed in, e.g.,

[
62
]
and
[
60
]

[

6
Concluding remarks

In this work, we have calculated the free propagator
G_{\perp}^{\rm FF}(p,q;z,z^{\prime})
for a strongly anisotropic system with film geometry, where the free surfaces are oriented perpendicular to one of the anisotropy axes.
Among all geometric configurations and boundary conditions considered in
[
45
]
, this particular case proved to be technically the most challenging.
Consequently, in
[
45
]
we restricted our analysis to the Gaussian approximation for this configuration.
To calculate the corresponding Casimir amplitude, we employed a specialized procedure (outlined on page
3
) that circumvented the need for an explicit Gaussian propagator.
In section
5
, we have reproduced this nontrivial one-loop result using an alternative approach based on the explicit expression for the propagator
G_{\perp}^{\rm FF}(p,q;z,z^{\prime})
given in (
38
).

The calculation of
O(\varepsilon)
corrections and higher remains an interesting open problem for future work. At the next order of the
\varepsilon
-expansion, computing the Casimir amplitude
\Delta_{\perp}^{\rm FF}(m)
requires evaluation of the two-loop diagram
.
We believe that for actual calculations at order
O(\varepsilon)
, it is appropriate to use the scheme developed for homogeneous systems by Symanzik
[
12
]
and worked out in detail in
[
14
]
.
For strongly anisotropic systems with the perpendicular surface orientation considered in the present work, the overall structure of calculations and subtractions of divergent terms should remain unchanged.
At the same time, the corresponding integrands will become more complicated, which will eventually necessitate numerical computations.
