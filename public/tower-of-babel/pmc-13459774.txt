Synthetic lethality between PBRM1 deficiency and PARP inhibitors: exploiting G2/M checkpoint arrest in colorectal cancer
Authors: Mohd Tarequl Islam, Haiwei Quan, Shuangquan Chen, Zhiguang Xu, Mingzhou Guo, Zhibin Wang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundPolybromo 1 (PBRM1), encoding the BAF180 subunit of the polybromo-associated BAF (PBAF) chromatin-remodeling complex, is commonly lost or mutated across malignancies. Despite its prevalence, tailored therapeutics for PBRM1-defective cancers remain limited. PBRM1 loss is associated with elevated replication stress and DNA damage responses, implying dependence on compensatory repair pathways. Given the lack of genotype-matched drugs, exploiting DNA-repair dependencies may provide a precision option for PBRM1-deficient disease. We pursued a synthetic-lethality strategy in colorectal cancer to test whether clinically used PARP inhibitors selectively suppress PBRM1-deficient cells and to define the linked cell-cycle and stress-response mechanisms. We also compared PARP inhibition with broader chromatin-remodeler targeting.MethodsIsogenic PBRM1−/− HCT116 colorectal carcinoma cells were generated by CRISPR/Cas9 lentiviral editing using sgRNAs cloned into lenti-CRISPR-V2. Knockout was confirmed by Western blotting, Sanger sequencing, and RT-qPCR. Background

PBRM1 is an important tumor suppressor gene that codes for the BAF180 subunit of the PBAF chromatin remodeling complex. It contributes to controlling transcription and maintaining genomic stability (Chabanon et al. 2021; Li and Zou 2024; Mashtalir et al. 2018; Pawłowski et al. 2013; Varela et al. 2011). Loss-of-function mutations or decreased expression of PBRM1 are commonly identified in malignancies, especially common in clear cell renal cell carcinoma, and also detected in other aggressive cancers (Benusiglio et al. 2015; Carril-Ajuria et al. 2019; Dai et al. 2023; Li and Zou 2024; Pawłowski et al. 2013). PBRM1 mutations are present in approximately 28% to 55% of clear cell renal cell carcinomas (ccRCCs) and are considered early driver events that commonly occur after VHL alteration (Carril-Ajuria et al. 2020; Gad et al. 2021). But our present study uses HCT116 colorectal cancer cells as the primary isogenic model, and this sequence clarifies the canonical genomic context of PBRM1 loss. Other aggressive malignancies also have PBRM1 abnormalities, encompassing 11% to 59% of chordomas, 12% to 23% of cholangiocarcinomas, 7% to 20% of mesotheliomas, 12% of endometrial carcinomas, and 3% of non-small cell lung cancers (Carril-Ajuria et al. 2019; Choudhary; Gad et al. 2021; Nargund et al. 2017). Although PBRM1 mutations are common, there are no targeted therapies currently available specifically for PBRM1-defective malignancies. Consequently, synthetic lethality-based strategies have gained attention as a means of identifying vulnerabilities that arise specifically from PBRM1 loss and may be selectively exploited for therapeutic benefit (Gu et al. 2022; Morel et al. 2017; Zimmer et al. 2023).

Synthetic lethality refers to a genetic interaction in which simultaneous disruption of two genes or pathways results in selective cancer cell death, whereas disruption of either gene or pathway alone is compatible with cell survival. This strategy has become an important approach for targeting malignancies driven by loss of tumor suppressor function (Kaelin Jr 2005; Li et al. 2026; Wu et al. 2025). In PBRM1-deficient circumstances, elevated replication stress and DNA damage phenotypes engender reliance on compensatory repair mechanisms (Espana-Agusti et al. 2017). Orthogonal functional genetic and small-molecule screens have delineated synthetic lethal interactions between PBRM1 deficiency and DNA repair inhibitors, notably PARP inhibitors, with validation across many in vitro systems models (Chabanon et al. 2021; Concannon et al. 2023; Zimmer et al. 2023). In terms of how they work, cells with PBRM1 defects show signs of replication stress and more DNA damage signaling, which can get worse when PARP is blocked (Ray Chaudhuri and Nussenzweig 2017). Previous studies reported that olaparib augments 5-fluorouracil cytotoxicity in mismatch repair-deficient colorectal carcinoma cells (de Castro e Gloria et al. 2021). In contrast, the present study evaluates olaparib treatment in PBRM1-deficient HCT116 cells, a model in which PBRM1 deficiency has been associated with G2/M arrest (Wang et al. 2017) and promoted cell death in PBRM1-deficient cells (Fultang et al. 2024).

Our findings demonstrate that PBRM1 knockout cells exhibit selective sensitivity to PARP inhibitors, particularly olaparib and rucaparib, through synthetic lethal interactions. This sensitivity is associated with impaired clonogenic survival (Chabanon et al. 2021), enhanced G2/M cell cycle arrest. In contrast, multi-target chromatin remodeler inhibitors showed limited genotype-specific effects. Collectively, these results show PBRM1 loss as a potential predictive biomarker and support the clinical relevance of PARP inhibitor–based therapies in PBRM1−/− cells.

Materials and methods

Cell lines and culture

Human HCT116 and 293T cells and mouse MC38 and B16F10 cells were obtained from ATCC. HCT116 cells were cultured in McCoy’s 5 A medium (Gibco) supplemented with 10% FBS and 1% penicillin-streptomycin. B16F10, MC38, and 293T cells were cultured in DMEM basic medium (Gibco) supplemented with 10% FBS and 1% penicillin-streptomycin. All cells were maintained in a humidified incubator at 37 °C with 5% CO₂. All cells were routinely screened and confirmed to be mycoplasma-free.

sgRNA oligonucleotide design, annealing, and ligation with lenti-CRISPR-V2

sgRNAs were designed using the online CHOPCHOP tool (https://chopchop.cbu.uib.no/), and all oligonucleotides were synthesized by Sangon Biotech (Shanghai, China). Oligonucleotides were phosphorylated and annealed using T4 PNK (BioLabs). In parallel, lenti-CRISPR-V2 plasmids were digested with BsmBI-V (BioLabs) and purified using the EasyPure Quick Gel Extraction Kit (TransGen Biotech). Annealed sgRNA oligonucleotides were ligated into the digested lenti-CRISPR-V2 plasmid using T4 DNA Ligase (BioLabs). DH5α competent cells were transformed with the ligation mixture. Plasmid DNA was extracted using the FastPure Plasmid Mini Kit (Vazyme), and the sequence of each sgRNA expression plasmid was validated by Sanger sequencing. Lentivirus preparation and generation of PBRM1−/− cells

PBRM1 knockout was generated in HCT116, MC38, and B16F10 cells using CRISPR/Cas9-mediated gene editing. Cells were transduced with lentiviral particles carrying the PBRM1 knockout constructs and then selected with puromycin (Beyotime) for 10 days at 2–8 µM/mL. Single-cell knockout colonies were established, and polyclonal and monoclonal knockout populations were verified by western blotting, Sanger sequencing, and qPCR. To produce lentiviruses, CRISPR V2 plasmid, pMD2.G, and psPAX2 were co-transfected into 293T cells using Lipofectamine 3000 reagent, P3000 reagent, and Opti-MEM (Invitrogen). At 48 h post-transfection, cell supernatants were collected and filtered through a 45-µm strainer. Supernatants were used immediately for transduction or stored at -80 °C for long-term use. Lentiviral supernatants were combined with polybrene and used to infect the indicated cell lines.

Western blot analysis

Total cellular protein was extracted using Mammalian Active Protein Extraction Reagent, Protease Inhibitor Cocktail, and Phosphatase Inhibitor Cocktail (MedChemExpress). Proteins were separated by 7.5% SDS-PAGE and transferred onto PVDF membranes (Sigma-Aldrich; pore size, 0.2 μm). Membranes were incubated overnight at 4 °C with the indicated primary antibodies, including anti-PBRM1 (Bethyl, 1:10000, Cat # A301-591 A-5) and anti-actin (Abcam, 1:5000, Cat # ab179467), and were subsequently incubated with the corresponding HRP-conjugated secondary antibodies (Abcam, 1:15000, Cat # ab6721) for 1 h at room temperature. Target proteins were detected using ECL Substrate (Biosharp) and an iBright 750 Imager (Invitrogen, Thermo Fisher Scientific).

RNA isolation and RT-qPCR

Total RNA was extracted from cultured cells using TRIzol reagent (Invitrogen, USA). Reverse transcription was performed using EasyScript All-in-One First-Strand cDNA Synthesis SuperMix for qPCR with One-Step gDNA Removal (TransGen Biotech). qPCR was performed using PerfectStart Green qPCR SuperMix (TransGen Biotech). Cell survival assays

Short-term cell survival assays were performed in 96-well plates. Exponentially growing cells were seeded in quintuplicate at a density of 1000 cells per well. Drug or vehicle control was added 24 h after seeding, and cells were continuously exposed to treatment for 5 days. The concentrations used ranged from 0 to 6 µM. After the exposure period, cell viability was assessed using the CCK-8 assay (MedChemExpress) on a VARIOSKAN LUX plate reader (Thermo Fisher Scientific), and survival fractions were calculated relative to the DMSO-treated control.

Colony-formation assays

Colony-formation assays were performed in 6-well plates. Exponentially growing cells were seeded at a density of 1000 cells per well. Olaparib, rucaparib, AU-24,118, or vehicle control was added 24 h after seeding, and cells were exposed to treatment for 12 days and drug-containing medium was replaced every 2 days. The concentrations used ranged from 0 to 6 µM. Cells were then fixed with 0.5% crystal violet in methanol for 20 min. Colonies were counted using ImageJ software, and colony survival (%) were calculated relative to the DMSO-treated control.

Cell-cycle assays

PBRM1+/+ and PBRM1−/− cells grown in 6-well plates were treated with olaparib, rucaparib, or AU-24,118 for 24 h at concentrations ranging from 0 to 6 µM. For cell-cycle analysis, cells were harvested and stained with propidium iodide (PI; Yeasen). Cell fluorescence was analyzed using a NovoCyte Advanteon VBR flow cytometer (Agilent).

Flow cytometric analysis of annexin V/PI double-stained cells

To quantitatively assess treatment-induced apoptosis, flow cytometric analysis was performed using Annexin V and propidium iodide (PI) dual staining. Briefly, PBRM1+/+ and isogenic PBRM1−/− HCT116 cells were harvested 24 h after the indicated treatments. Cells were washed with cold phosphate-buffered saline (PBS) and resuspended in 1× Annexin V binding buffer (BD Biosciences) at a density of approximately 1 × 10⁶ cells/mL. Following the manufacturer’s protocol for the Annexin V-FITC Apoptosis Detection Kit (BD Biosciences), cell suspensions were incubated with fluorescein isothiocyanate (FITC)-conjugated Annexin V and PI (Yeasen) for 15 min at room temperature in the dark. Data were analyzed using NovoExpress software, and apoptotic rates were expressed as the combined percentage of early and late apoptotic cells (total Annexin V+ population). Statistical comparisons of apoptotic indices between genotypes and treatment conditions were performed to evaluate the functional role of PBRM1 in modulating apoptotic sensitivity.

RNA-seq analysis

RNA-seq was performed using PBRM1+/+ and PBRM1−/− HCT116 cells treated with olaparib or vehicle control. Gene-level count data were analyzed in R using the DESeq2 package. Genes with total counts fewer than 10 across all samples were removed before analysis. Differential expression was analyzed using a model including genotype, treatment, and their interaction term. Comparisons included olaparib versus control in PBRM1+/+ cells, olaparib versus control in PBRM1−/− cells, PBRM1−/− versus PBRM1+/+ cells under control conditions, PBRM1−/− versus PBRM1+/+ cells under olaparib treatment, and the genotype-by-treatment interaction effect. Differentially expressed genes were visualized using volcano plots, with |log2 fold change| > 0.58 and p < 0.01 used as thresholds. Gene set enrichment analysis was performed, as we did previously (Li et al. 2015), using the fgsea package. Hallmark, Gene Ontology Biological Process, and KEGG pathway gene sets were obtained from MSigDB using the msigdbr package. Genes were ranked by the DESeq2 Wald statistic, and enrichment analysis was performed with gene set sizes between 15 and 500. Enriched pathways were visualized using dot plots and ridge plots.

Statistical analysis

The statistical significance of differences between control and test groups was assessed using a t-test or two-way ANOVA in GraphPad Prism 6 (GraphPad Software). P values < 0.05 were considered statistically significant. *P < 0.05; **P < 0.01; ***P < 0.001; ****P < 0.0001; ns, not significant.

Results

PARP inhibitors are synthetic lethal with PBRM1-deficient human colorectal carcinoma cells

To screen and identify the PBRM1 target drug, we first generated PBRM1 knockout (KO) HCT116, MC38, and B16F10 cells by the CRISPR-Cas9 gene editing system (Li et al. 2023). We used PBRM1 isogenic cell pairs, which ensure that the identification effects work in one specific genetic background. As confirmed and verified PBRM1 KO through Western blot (WB), Sanger sequencing, and qPCR analysis, our knockout approach successfully abolished PBRM1 protein (Figs. 1A-C). We also checked the growth rate of HCT116 isogenic cell pairs through CCK-8 (Fig. 1D). We used four confirmed HCT116 PBRM1−/− clones (sg2.4, sg3.8, sg3.13, and sg7.3) and two other PBRM1−/− cell lines, MC38 & B16F10 (Figs. 1E-F), for the synthetic lethal screening. Among the four PBRM1 knockout clones created, the sg7.3 clone was chosen for the synthetic lethal drug screening.

We next used PBRM1-deficient cells to assess potential vulnerabilities using a panel of four drugs. These included the PARP1 inhibitors olaparib and rucaparib, the multi-target inhibitor AU-24,118 (against PBRM1, SMARCA2, and SMARCA4), and the SMARCA2/4-targeting PROTAC AU-1530. A CCK-8 assay was used to determine the drug inhibition rate following exposure to concentrations from 0 to 6 µM (Fig. 2), with B16F10 PBRM1−/− (Fig. 2C, F, I and L) and MC38 PBRM1−/− (Fig. 2B, E and H) showing resistance to four compounds except for AU-1530 sensitivity to MC38 PBRM1−/− at 3 µM (Fig. 2K). However, the mean viability at 6 µM was higher than that at 3 µM. This non-monotonic pattern was not interpreted as evidence of a dose-dependent biological effect, but rather as variability among replicate measurements at the highest tested concentration. Consistent with this interpretation, AU-1530 did not meet the selectivity threshold in MC38 PBRM1−/− cells, with an SI < 2 (Selectivity Index = IC50 WT / IC50 KO), and was therefore not classified as selectively active in this model. On the other hand, HCT116 PBRM1−/− (Fig. 2A and D, and 2G) showed sensitivity to PARPi (olaparib and rucaparib) and AU-24,118, where SI > 2, but AU-1530 showed resistance. In the CCK-8 screening, we assessed the sensitivity of various PBRM1−/− cell lines to PARP inhibitors. Notably, HCT116 PBRM1−/− cells exhibited significant sensitivity to both olaparib and rucaparib, with reduced cell viability observed at relatively low concentrations. In contrast, PBRM1−/− MC38 and B16F10 cells did not show similar sensitivity to PARP inhibitors, suggesting a resistance to treatment. Although PARP inhibitor sensitivity was observed in HCT116 PBRM1−/− cells, this effect was not observed in the mouse models MC38 and B16F10. This suggests that the synthetic lethality observed in HCT116 cells is context-dependent and may not be generalized to all PBRM1-deficient cell lines. Taken together, these results demonstrate that PBRM1 deficiency confers selective sensitivity to PARP inhibitors in HCT116 human colorectal carcinoma cells, supporting a synthetic lethal interaction between PBRM1 loss and PARP inhibition.

PBRM1 deficiency selectively sensitizes colorectal cancer cells to PARP inhibition but not to multi-target chromatin remodeler inhibition in clonogenic survival assays

Colony formation assays reveal that PBRM1 loss markedly sensitizes HCT116 colorectal cancer cells to PARP inhibitors, but not to the multi-target chromatin remodeler inhibitor AU-24,118. Treatment with olaparib (Fig. 3A-B) resulted in a dose-dependent reduction in clonogenic survival, with PBRM1−/− cells exhibiting near-complete suppression at 6 µM, whereas wild type HCT116 cells remained relatively resistant. Similarly, rucaparib (Fig. 3C-D) profoundly impaired colony formation in PBRM1-deficient cells across all concentrations, with minimal survival at ≥ 3 µM. In contrast, the multi-target inhibitor AU-24,118, which targets PBRM1, SMARCA2, and SMARCA4 (Fig. 3E-F), potently reduced colony formation in both wild-type and knockout cell lines to almost a similar extent, indicating that its efficacy is independent of PBRM1 status. Because each inhibitor was analyzed in an independent clonogenic assay, colony numbers in vehicle-treated controls may vary between experiments. Accordingly, drug effects were interpreted using colony survival (%), defined as the percentage of colonies after treatment. Quantitative analysis of colony survival confirms that PBRM1-deficient cells are significantly more sensitive to olaparib and rucaparib, particularly at lower doses, while AU-24,118 showed the same activity in both genetic backgrounds. These findings suggest that PBRM1 deficiency specifically confers hypersensitivity to PARP inhibition. This supports the potential of PBRM1 loss as a predictive biomarker for PARP inhibitors while emphasizing distinct mechanistic vulnerabilities.

PBRM1 loss sensitizes cells to PARP inhibitor-induced G2/M arrest but not to multi-target chromatin inhibition

Cell cycle analysis reveals that genetic ablation of PBRM1 specifically alters the response to PARP inhibitors, inducing a pronounced G2/M phase arrest (Dale Rein et al. 2015), while the response to a multi-target chromatin remodeler inhibitor remains consistent between genotypes. Treatment with olaparib (Figs. 4A-B) resulted in a dose-dependent accumulation of PBRM1−/− cells in the G2/M phase, with a corresponding decrease in the G1 and S phase populations, indicating a robust arrest at the G2/M checkpoint. Parental HCT116 cells exhibited a markedly less severe G2/M accumulation under the same treatment. A nearly identical pattern was observed with rucaparib (Figs. 4C-D), where PBRM1−/− cells displayed a significant increase in the G2/M fraction, reinforcing that PBRM1 deficiency creates a specific vulnerability to PARP inhibitor-mediated cell cycle disruption. In striking contrast, the multi-target inhibitor AU-24,118, which targets PBRM1, SMARCA2, and SMARCA4 (Figs. 4E-F), elicited a profoundly different cell cycle outcome. Both PBRM1+ /+ and PBRM1−/− cells responded to AU-24,118 in the same way. The quantitative data confirm that the hypersensitivity of PBRM1-deficient cells is uniquely linked to the efficacy of PARP inhibitors in provoking a G2/M arrest, a phenotype classically associated with unrepaired DNA damage and checkpoint activation. Conversely, AU-24,118’s activity bypasses this specific vulnerability. These results delineate a clear mechanistic divergence: PBRM1 loss selectively predisposes cells to PARP inhibitor toxicity by exacerbating a DNA damage-induced G2/M checkpoint arrest. This underscores PBRM1’s functional role in the DNA damage response and validates its loss as a specific biomarker for sensitivity to PARP inhibition.

Loss of PBRM1 enhances apoptotic sensitivity to olaparib in HCT116 cells

Here, our experimental results suggest a link between PBRM1 loss and the enhanced sensitivity to olaparib, supporting the hypothesis of synthetic lethality. To determine how PBRM1 deficiency alters the response of colorectal cancer cells to PARP inhibition, we compared olaparib-treated PBRM1-knockout HCT116 cells with olaparib-treated wild-type HCT116 cells and with DMSO controls. The volcano plot (Fig. 5A) demonstrates broad differential gene expression, with several genes selectively upregulated in the knockout background, indicating that PBRM1 loss changes the cellular response to PARP inhibition rather than simply reflecting baseline differences. This transcriptional shift is further supported by the enrichment analysis in Fig. 5B, where pathways related to the mitotic G2/M transition checkpoint, cell-cycle checkpoint signaling, DNA replication, and establishment of mitotic spindle localization are enriched in olaparib-treated PBRM1-KO cells compared with olaparib-treated wild-type cells. These findings are consistent with checkpoint activation and impaired cell-cycle progression, suggesting a G2/M arrest-like state in the PBRM1-deficient background. In Fig. 5C, gene ontology (GO) biological process analysis of olaparib-treated PBRM1-KO cells relative to DMSO-treated PBRM1-KO controls further illustrates enrichment of DNA repair, DNA damage signaling, DNA integrity checkpoint signaling, DNA replication, double-strand break repair, recombinational repair, and mitotic G2/M transition checkpoint pathways. Similarly, Fig. 5D confirms enrichment of pathways linked to DNA-dependent DNA replication, negative regulation of cell-cycle progression, and checkpoint control. Together, Fig. 5 indicates that olaparib imposes greater DNA replication stress in PBRM1-deficient cells, leading to enhanced checkpoint engagement and defective progression through G2/M.

To further demonstrates the functional consequence of altered stress response and support a model of synthetic lethality between PBRM1 loss and olaparib treatment, we generated heatmap. Olaparib-treated PBRM1-KO cells upregulate PRKAA2, CSRNP3, FAM111B, MANCR, PURPL, DKK1, PIK3R1, CLCA2, and other genes associated with cell-cycle disruption and replication stress (Fig. 6A). Transcription factor-level analysis (Fig. 6B) further reveals enrichment of the CSRNP_N family in the PBRM1-KO group, suggesting activation of a distinct stress-responsive transcriptional program. Morphologically, Fig. 6C shows that under DMSO both wild-type and PBRM1-KO cells maintain better adherent growth, whereas olaparib treatment leads to increased cell rounding, detachment, and reduced confluence, most prominently in the PBRM1-KO cells, consistent with increased apoptosis. This is confirmed in Fig. 6D, where flow cytometry reveals a greater apoptotic fraction in olaparib-treated PBRM1-KO cells than in olaparib-treated wild-type cells, especially at higher olaparib concentrations. Finally, Fig. 6E validates the RNA-seq findings by qPCR, showing significant CSRNP3 upregulation in PBRM1-KO cells following olaparib exposure.

Collectively, these results indicate that PBRM1 loss creates a vulnerability to PARP inhibition, such that olaparib triggers unresolved DNA replication stress, stronger G2/M checkpoint activation, and ultimately enhanced apoptotic cell death, consistent with synthetic lethality in PBRM1-deficient HCT116 cells.

Discussion

Our study demonstrates a novel synthetic lethality interaction between PBRM1 deficiency and PARP inhibitors, particularly olaparib and rucaparib, in human colorectal carcinoma cells. PBRM1, a critical component of the SWI/SNF chromatin remodeling complex, is frequently mutated or deleted in various cancers, including clear cell renal cell carcinoma (ccRCC) and other aggressive malignancies (Yao et al. 2023). Although its frequency, treatment approaches aimed at PBRM1-deficient malignancies are yet inadequately explored (Aili et al. 2021).

Our experimental investigations here revealed that the absence of PBRM1 enhances the sensitivity of colorectal cancer cells to PARP inhibitors, indicating a potential strategy for targeted therapy in this specific patient population (Zhang et al. 2025). The synthetic lethality identified between PBRM1 and PARP inhibition in our research is corroborated by the increased susceptibility of PBRM1-deficient HCT116 cells to both olaparib and rucaparib. This was seen in decreased clonogenic survival (Lai et al. 2022), since PBRM1 mutant cells demonstrated nearly total suppression at lower concentrations of PARP inhibitors (Fang et al. 2018). The detected hypersensitivity was corroborated in G2/M cell cycle arrest experiments, wherein PBRM1-deficient cells exhibited a significant accumulation in the G2/M phase, indicative of unrepaired DNA damage (Feng et al. 2022). The G2/M checkpoint arrest has been previously associated with DNA damage response and PARP inhibition, further corroborating our findings that PBRM1 deficiency intensifies DNA damage signaling (Fernando et al. 2021). While we did not assess DNA damage in this study (e.g., by measuring γH2AX or using comet assays), the G2/M checkpoint arrest observed upon PARP inhibition is consistent with replication stress and DNA damage signaling, as supported by RNA-seq analysis. Pathways related to DNA damage response, DNA repair, recombinational repair, and checkpoint regulation were enriched in PBRM1−/− cells treated with olaparib, suggesting that DNA damage and replication stress contribute to the G2/M arrest phenotype. We acknowledge that DNA damage markers would have strengthened this interpretation, but our current results provide support for this mechanism from gene expression. The multi-target chromatin remodeler inhibitor AU-24,118 (He et al. 2024), which also targets PBRM1, did not demonstrate the same genotype-specific effects as the PARP inhibitors. Both wild-type and PBRM1-deficient cells exhibited comparable sensitivity to AU-24,118, indicating that its efficacy is independent of PBRM1 status. This distinction underscores the specificity of the synthetic lethality between PBRM1 and PARP inhibitors (Helleday 2011), indicating that targeting the PBRM1 pathway with PARP inhibitors may represent a more efficacious therapeutic approach than utilizing broad-spectrum chromatin remodeling inhibitors.

Furthermore, our results indicate that PBRM1-deficient cells demonstrate increased apoptotic sensitivity to olaparib, an effect that is absent in wild-type cells. Flow cytometric analysis demonstrated an elevated rate of apoptosis in PBRM1 mutant cells subjected to olaparib treatment relative to controls. The RNA-seq analyses indicated the activation of stress response genes, such as CSRNP3, in PBRM1-deficient cells subjected to olaparib treatment, demonstrating the cellular reaction to genotoxic stress. The up-regulation indicates a possible mechanism by which PBRM1-deficient cells exhibit increased susceptibility to DNA damage-induced apoptosis upon treatment with PARP inhibitors. The greater vulnerability to olaparib exemplifies synthetic lethality, wherein the absence of PBRM1 collaborates with olaparib’s PARP inhibition to provoke cell death in the lack of DNA repair capability (Gu et al. 2022).

However, the sensitivity was not observed in mouse-derived models, such as MC38 and B16F10 PBRM1−/− cells, which did not exhibit a similar response to the treatment. Our data indicate that the synthetic lethal interaction between PBRM1 deficiency and PARP inhibition is context-dependent. In HCT116 cells, this interaction is likely due to the DNA replication stress induced by PBRM1 loss. In mouse-derived PBRM1−/− models, including MC38 and B16F10, we did not observe a similar sensitivity, suggesting the involvement of additional genetic and environmental factors. These may include compensatory DNA repair mechanisms, lineage-specific responses, or differences in drug metabolism. The species difference between HCT116 and mouse models also warrants further investigation to determine whether these findings can be extended to other human and mouse tumor models.

In summary, our findings demonstrate a synthetic lethal interaction between the loss of PBRM1 and olaparib treatment, resulting in enhanced apoptosis and the upregulation of stress response genes, thereby underscoring the therapeutic potential of employing PARP inhibitors such as olaparib in PBRM1-deficient tumors. The current investigation contributes to the expanding body of information indicating that synthetic lethality may serve as an effective approach for targeting malignancies characterized by specific genetic anomalies, such as PBRM1 mutations (Kaelin Jr 2005). However, additional investigation is necessary to assess the therapeutic relevance of PARP inhibitors in individuals with PBRM1-deficient malignancies. Clinical trials should focus on assessing the therapeutic efficacy of PARP inhibitors, especially in colorectal and renal malignancies. In addition, the identification of biomarkers, such as CSRNP3, that predict sensitivity to PARP inhibitors could enhance patient stratification and treatment outcomes. It is essential to investigate combination medicines that address both PBRM1 deficiency and alternative DNA repair mechanisms. Integrating PARP inhibition with drugs that intensify replication stress or DNA damage may enhance treatment efficacy. The advancement of precision medicines that exploit genetic vulnerabilities, such as PBRM1 deficiency, presents promising prospects for enhancing treatment outcomes in tumors typically resistant to standard therapies.

Conclusions

In conclusion, our work identifies a cell-line-specific synthetic-lethal vulnerability in PBRM1-deficient colorectal cells. Our findings suggest that PBRM1 loss in HCT116 cells sensitizes them to PARP inhibition, but this effect appears context-dependent. The lack of response in other PBRM1−/− cell lines, such as MC38 and B16F10, underscores the need for further investigation into the specific DNA repair or replication-stress mechanisms that govern the PARP inhibitor response. Additional validation in diverse cellular models is necessary to determine whether PBRM1 deficiency represents a broad synthetic-lethality target in cancers. Mechanistically, PARP inhibition intensified G2/M checkpoint arrest and promoted apoptosis in the absence of PBRM1, accompanied by reactivation of stress-response genes such as CSRNP3. In contrast, multi-target chromatin remodeler inhibition lacked genotype specificity. These findings support PBRM1 as a predictive biomarker and motivate clinical evaluation plus biomarker-guided combination strategies to improve patient stratification and overcome therapeutic resistance.
