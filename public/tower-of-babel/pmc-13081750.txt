PARP7 inhibition and a STING agonist potentiate radiation-induced immunogenicity in glioblastoma
Authors: Luca Lippert, Ava Kleinwächter, Anna Ohradanova-Repic, Filip Horvat, Lisa-Marie Appel, Lena Walch, Niccolò Bragato, Jakob Wimmer, Hanna Wopfner, Anna Röhrer, Magdalena Engl, Kapil Upadhyaya, Joachim Widder, Michael S. Cohen, Dea Slade
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTGlioblastoma (GBM) is the most lethal form of brain cancer with an immunosuppressive tumor microenvironment (TME). Radio- and chemotherapy can modify the TME and elicit anti-tumor immunity through the STING and type I interferon (IFN-I) pathways. STING agonists or DNA damage response inhibitors can potentiate the effects of radiation by targeting the IFN-I pathway. Here, we examined the immunogenic response of GBM cells to hypofractionated or single-dose radiation combined with a STING agonist or inhibitors targeting immune signaling (PARP7), the DNA damage response, cell cycle checkpoints or autophagy. We found that hypofractionated radiation elicited a ferroptosis-dependent IFN-I response and that the PARP7 inhibitor KMR-206 and the STING agonist diABZI enhanced the effects of radiation by activating immune cells. In patient-derived GBM cancer stem cells with low STING levels, the PARP7 inhibitor was more effective at inducing immunogenic signaling compared to the STING agonist. Our results highlight the potential of PARP7 inhibitors as a new treatment to boost anti-tumor immunity in GBM, which may be more advantageous compared to STING agonists given that STING is often downregulated in GBM.

Introduction

Glioblastomas (GBMs) are grade IV gliomas of the central nervous system and the most common and aggressive type of brain malignancy.1 Patients treated according to the current standard of care have a median survival of only 9 months2 and show resistance to conventional therapy, including radiation, chemotherapy with temozolomide and immunotherapy.3 Different treatment options beyond radiation and chemotherapy have been developed, including anti-angiogenic drugs, like Bevacizumab,4 but also locoregional treatment strategies, like the tumor-treating fields device.5 However, all these treatment options marginally improve patients' overall survival.

GBMs are immunologically cold tumors with immunosuppressive TME, low tumor mutational burden, low T-cell infiltration, and deficient immune activation.3 Upregulation of multiple immune checkpoints in GBM tumor cells such as PD-L1, which inhibits CD8+ and CD4+ T-cell activation, spurred new approaches based on immune checkpoint blockade in GBM, which failed in clinical trials.3 Moreover, inhibition of tumor-associated macrophages (TAMs), which enhance the invasion of GBM stem cells and promote T-cell exhaustion, also failed to show a clinical benefit when combined with radiation and temozolomide.3 Identifying new treatment strategies that would boost the anti-tumor immune response in GBM may help in combatting this hitherto incurable tumor.

The key pathway that drives the anti-tumor immunity is the type I interferon (IFN-I) response. The IFN-I response is initiated by the cytosolic nucleic acid sensing pathways cGAS-STING (for DNA) and RIG-I-MAVS (for RNA), which are induced upon viral infection as part of our innate immune response but also due to cytosolic accumulation of DNA or RNA after exposure to genotoxic stress-inducing agents such as radiation.6 After sensing cytosolic nucleic acids, these pathways are activated and initiate a signaling cascade that culminates in the expression of type I interferons and interferon-stimulated genes (ISGs). ISGs include cytokines and chemokines that attract and activate immune cells (e.g., dendritic cells, natural killer cells, and T cells) to kill cancer cells.7,8

Cytoplasmic dsDNA is sensed by cGAS, leading to cGAS dimerization and catalytic activation.7 This results in the production of the second messenger cGAMP, which binds to STING and triggers a conformational change that activates the TBK1 kinase. The phosphorylated STING-TBK1 complex recruits and phosphorylates the transcription factor IRF3, which dimerizes and translocates to the nucleus, where it induces the transcription of interferon beta-1 (IFNB1). Alternatively, STING can activate the transcription factor NF-κB in TBK1/IKKε- and IRF3-dependent manner.9‐11 While cGAS senses cytoplasmic dsDNA, RIG-I/MDA-5 sense dsRNA, oligomerize and bind to MAVS, which activates the same downstream signaling as STING to induce IFN-I expression.8

Radiation, topoisomerase I and topoisomerase II inhibitors, taxol (microtubule-stabilizing antimitotic agent) and temozolomide (alkylating agent) were shown to induce the IFN-I response in different cancer types by inducing DNA damage and the formation of immunostimulatory cytoplasmic dsDNA.12‐16 Compared to conventional X-ray irradiation, carbon ion (C-ion) irradiation can improve the clinical outcome by depositing the highest dose to a confined region in the tumor, sparing healthy tissue, and inducing a stronger anti-tumor immunogenic response.17 C-ion therapy is more effective against GBM stem cells and hypoxic conditions and was shown to improve the overall survival, progression-free survival, and tumor local control in patients.18

The IFN-I response can also be induced by clinically relevant small-molecule inhibitors targeting DNA damage response (PARP1/2, ATM), replication stress response (ATR, CHK1), G2/M checkpoint (WEE1), and IFN-I signaling (PARP7).19,20 PARP1/2 inhibitors such as olaparib block single-strand break repair (SSBR) and induce replication stress due to PARP1/2 trapping at DNA damage sites, ATM kinase inhibitors block double-strand break repair (DSBR) and weaken G1/S and G2/M checkpoints, ATR and CHK1 kinase inhibitors impair replication stress response at stalled replication forks and weaken the G2/M checkpoint, WEE1 kinase inhibitors block the G2/M checkpoint, while PARP7 inhibitors induce the IFN-I response.19‐21 Cells with weakened cell cycle checkpoints progress to mitosis despite unrepaired DNA damage, which promotes micronuclei accumulation. Cytoplasmic dsDNA can be eliminated through autophagy, and autophagy inhibition has been shown to boost the IFN-I response.22,23 All combined, the IFN-I response can be enhanced through: (1) the induction of DNA damage; (2) deficient DNA repair; (3) uncontrolled cell cycle progression; (4) the activation of immune signaling pathways; and (v) the inhibition of pathways that eliminate immunostimulatory cytoplasmic dsDNA, such as autophagy. To date, CHK2 inhibition or the STING agonist diABZI in combination with radiation has been shown to induce the STING pathway and sensitize GBM to immune checkpoint blockade.24,25

Here, we examined the effects of radiation and various chemotherapeutic drugs and small-molecule inhibitors and agonists on immunogenic signaling in four GBM cell lines with variable STING, MAVS, and pRelA (NF-κB subunit p65) levels and found that hypofractionated radiation (3 × 8 Gy) induces the accumulation of cytoplasmic dsDNA and immunogenic effects in GBM cell lines with variable magnitude and mechanistic properties. We found that the IFN-I response is the dominant STING-, NF-κB- and ferroptosis-dependent immunogenic pathway in U251 and KALS-1 cells, which can be induced by hypofractionated radiation alone or in combination with PARP7i/diABZI. Autophagy and apoptosis were both found to dampen radiation-induced immunogenic effects in U251 and KALS-1. MAVS-, NF-κB- and ferroptosis-dependent IFN-I signaling was induced in T98G cells irradiated with 3 × 8 Gy or 8 Gy and was enhanced when 3 × 8 Gy was combined with diABZI or PARP7i. Radiation led to a strong induction of repetitive elements in T98G cells, and autophagy seemed to promote (rather than impair) immunogenic effects in this cell line. GB-1 cells were least responsive to different treatments and induce pro-survival oncogenic pathways upon radiation. Combinatorial immunogenic effects of diABZI or PARP7i and radiation resulted in the potent activation of various immune cell types (monocytes, NK, NKT, CD4+ and CD8+ T cells). Unlike diABZI, PARP7i did not enhance radiation-induced expression of the pro-tumorigenic cytokine TGF-β and induced pro-inflammatory cytokines in patient-derived GBM cancer stem cells (CSCs) that express low STING levels. Collectively, our results reveal the heterogeneity of GBM cells with regard to radiation-induced immunogenicity, prompting personalized treatment strategies to screen patient-derived cells before applying radiation and combinatorial treatments, among which the use of PARP7 inhibitors and STING agonists seems to be most promising.

Methods

Cell culture

The human GBM cell lines U251 and T98G cell lines were cultured in Dulbecco's modified Eagle's medium (DMEM) medium (Gibco, 41965-039), supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS) (Sigma, F7524-500ml) and 1% penicillin/streptomycin (P/S) (Gibco, 15140-122). GB-1 and KALS-1 cell lines were cultured in Roswell Park Memorial Institute (RPMI) 1640 medium (Gibco, 21875-034), supplemented with 10% FBS, 1% P/S and 1% L-glutamine (Gibco, 25030-024). LK7 and LK17 patient-derived glioblastoma cancer stem cells were cultured in NeuroCult NS-A basal medium (Stemcell, 05751) supplemented with 10% NeuroCult NS-A proliferation supplement (Stemcell, 05751), 100 ng/mL rh bFGF (Stemcell, 78003), 100 ng/mL rhEGF (Stemcell, 78006), and 0.002% heparin (Stemcell, 07980). The cell lines were cultured at 37 °C in a humidified atmosphere with 95% air and 5% CO2. The cells were irradiated at a confluency of 70%–80%. To allow for vertical flask positioning in irradiation workflows, the cell culture flasks were filled with air-bubble-free unsupplemented DMEM growth medium prior to radiation, which was removed immediately after irradiation and replaced by fresh, supplemented medium. The sources of all the cell lines used in this study are provided in Supplementary Table 1.

Cell line generation

CRISPR/Cas9 STING and MAVS KOs were generated using gRNAs targeting exon 3 and 5, respectively, and were cloned between BbsI sites under the U6 promoter in plasmids encoding Cas9-EGFP or Cas9-Cherry (pX458). gRNA sequences for human STING were 5′-CACTGCAGAGATCTCAGCTG-3′ and 5′-TATCTGCGGCTGATCCTGCC-3′. The gRNA sequences for human MAVS were 5′-GTAGATACAACTGACCCTGT-3′ and 5′-TGTGTTCACAGGCATCAAGG-3′. The cells were FACS-sorted for GFP- and mCherry-positive signals 72 h after transfection with polyethylenimine (PEI; Polysciences) and cultured for several days after sorting in order to allow for cell recovery. After sufficient recovery, the cells were FACS-sorted for GFP- and mCherry-negative cells were seeded as single cells in 96-well plates. After clonal expansion, surviving clones were expanded, genomic DNA was isolated, and the Cas9 target region was PCR-amplified and sequenced. Loss of protein expression was confirmed by western blot. All the cell lines used are listed in Supplementary Table 1.

Irradiation and treatments

The cells were irradiated with 8 Gy of 200 kV X-rays or C-ions. Additionally, a fractionation regimen of 3 × 8 Gy was implemented for the X-ray experiments but not for C-ions due to limited beam time availability. X-ray irradiations were administered using a horizontal irradiation cabinet (YXLON, TU32-D03, 20 mA, 5.5FOC; filtration: 3 mm Be + 3 mm Al + 0.5 mm Cu), and C-ion irradiations were all performed using the particle synchrotron at MedAustron with a horizontal experimental beam line. For C-ion experiments, customized cell holders were manufactured and dosimetrically verified to enable cell irradiation in water, in accordance with a recent report of a National Cancer Institute special panel.26 The positioning uncertainty was estimated to be 0.3 mm.27 A spread-out Bragg peak (SOBP) width of 4 cm and a range at the 80% dose level (R80) of 10.6 cm (corresponding to a C-ion energy of 285 MeV/u) was achieved for both setups (ChamberFlask and T25 flasks). The dose-averaged LET (LETD) was derived from GATE/Geant4-based Monte Carlo simulations, taking into account the beam nozzle of the experimental beam line at MedAustron. All primary and secondary particles were included in the calculation of the LETD. The scoring geometry was a cylinder with a diameter of approximately 80 mm. At 8 cm, the depth corresponding to the cell placement in the center of the SOBP, the LETD is 58 keV/μm. Treatments are indicated in Supplementary Table 2. The treatment concentrations and durations are indicated in the Figure legends.

Cell lysis, SDS-PAGE, and western blotting

The cell pellets were resuspended in lysis buffer [50 mM Tris-Cl pH 7.4, 250 mM NaCl, 5 mM EDTA, 50 mM NaF, 1 mM Na3VO4, 1% NP40, 1 mM PMSF, 1% of protease and phosphatase inhibitor cocktail (Thermo Fisher Scientific, D12345) and 1× PhosSTOP (Merck, 4906837001)] and were lysed for 20 min on ice before centrifugation at 4 °C for 10 min. The protein concentration in the supernatant was estimated by Bradford assay. 20 µg lysate per lane was loaded onto SDS‒PAGE gels. The molecular weight marker used was PageRuler™ Plus Prestained Protein Ladder, 10 to 250 kDa (Thermo Fisher Scientific, 26619). Transfer onto a nitrocellulose membrane was performed in transfer buffer (25 mM Tris, 192 mM glycine) containing 10% ethanol at 30 V o/n. Membranes were blocked for 1 h and incubated with a primary antibody o/n on a roller at 4 °C. Phosphorylated and the corresponding total protein were run on separate gels. Each gel had its own loading control. The membranes were washed three times for 10 min in TBS-T (0.1% Tween in TBS), incubated in HRP-conjugated secondary antibody for 2 h at 4 °C, and the chemiluminescent signal was detected on a ChemiDoc MP imaging system (Bio-Rad) operated by Bio-Rad Image Lab Touch Software (version 2.3.0.07) and analyzed using Bio-Rad Image Lab Software (version 5.2.1). The antibodies used for western blotting are listed in Supplementary Table 3. All western blot experiments were performed in three biological replicates. Uncropped blots and quantifications are included in Supplementary Data 3–5. The “relative intensity” was calculated as the fold change value over that of the control samples, normalized to that of the loading control. Phosphorylated proteins were analyzed by calculating the ratio of phosphorylated proteins to their respective native counterparts.

Immunofluorescence microscopy

For immunofluorescence analyses of dsDNA, GBM cells were seeded in Nunc™ Lab-Tek™ ChamberFlasks one day prior to irradiation treatment. For cell fixation, the cells were treated with 4% paraformaldehyde (Carl Roth), followed by permeabilization with 0.1% Tween 20 (Sigma Aldrich) and 0.01% Triton X-100 (Sigma Aldrich) in PBS and blocked with 1% BSA (Sigma Aldrich) in PBS. A dsDNA-targeting primary antibody (Abcam, ab27156, 1:400 dilution) was applied o/n at 4 °C, followed by a 1 h incubation at room temperature with Alexa Fluor 488-conjugated goat anti-mouse IgG (Abcam, ab150117, 1:400 dilution). DAPI-containing mounting medium (VectaShield, H-2000) was used to mount the cells, and the slides were evaluated using fluorescence microscopy. The LSM980 inverse confocal microscope (Zeiss) equipped with a Plan-Apochromat 63×/1.4 Oil DIC (WD 0.13 mm), a 405 nm laser diode (30 mW), a 488 nm laser diode (30 mW), and a 561 nm DPSS laser (25 mW) was used to acquire images. The laser power, excitation wavelength range and pinhole size were chosen appropriately for each channel using negative controls for alignment. Zen software (version 3.3, Zeiss) was used to acquire images, and Fiji (ImageJ) was used for processing. ROIs were defined around single cells, and a threshold of 20000 was set. The “Analyze Particles” option in Fiji was used to analyze the fluorescence signals. The corrected total cell fluorescence (CTCF) was calculated for each individual cell for dsDNA intensity measurements, followed by measurement of the integrated density, area of the respective cell and mean fluorescence of the background. CTCF was calculated as follows: CTCF = Integrated density—(area of the selected cell * mean fluorescence of the background).

RNA isolation and library preparation

Cell pellets were resuspended in 1 mL TRAzol (Neo-biotech). After the addition of 200 µL chloroform (Applichem), the lysate was vortexed and centrifuged at maximum speed (21,100 × g) for 15 min at 4 °C. The aqueous layer was transferred to a new tube and precipitated with 0.5 mL isopropanol. The RNA pellet was isolated by centrifugation for 30 min at 4 °C, washed with 1 mL 75% ethanol, re-centrifuged for 10 min, dried and resuspended in 70 µL RNase free water. 20 µg RNA was treated with 40 U DNaseI (Roche) for 30 min at 37 °C and subsequently purified by phenol–chloroform extraction followed by precipitation with ethanol. For RNA-seq, GBM cells were mixed with 10% mouse embryonic fibroblasts (MEFs) as a spike-in control prior to RNA isolation. RNA isolation was performed as described above. RNA-seq libraries preparation was performed using the NEBNext Poly(A) mRNA Magnetic Isolation Module (NEB) and NEBNext Ultra II Directional RNA Library Prep Kit for Illumina (NEB) according to the manufacturer's instructions using 600 ng total RNA as input. The VBCF NGS facility performed sequencing on a NovaSeq X sequencing system (Illumina) in read mode SR100.

Reverse transcription and real-time qPCR

1 µg of RNA was reverse transcribed using LunaScript RT SuperMix (NEB). cDNA was diluted 1:5 in H2O. qPCR was performed on a Bio-Rad CFX Opus 96 cycler operated by Bio-Rad CFX Maestro software (Version 2.3) using iQTM Multiplex Powermix (Bio-Rad). IFNB1, CXCL10, PD-L1 (CD274), IL-6 (IL6), TNFα (TNF) and TBP levels were quantified by multiplexing RT-PCR using the commercially available primers (human IFNB1 UniqueAssayID: qHsaCEP0054112, human CXCL10 UniqueAssayID: qHsaCEP0053880, human IL6 UniqueAssayID: qHsaCEP0051939, human TBP UniqueAssayID: qHsaCIP0036255, human CD274 UniqueAssayID: qHsaCIP0039192, human TNF UniqueAssayID: qHsaCEP0040184). qPCR data were analyzed and plotted using GraphPad Prism (9.1.1). Experiments were performed in 3 biological replicates and each sample was measured in 3 technical replicates.

RNA-seq data analysis

RNA-seq data from GBM cells were processed using nf-core/rnaseq pipeline (10.1038/s41587-020-0439-x). Data was quantified using combined GRCh38/hg38 and the mm10 versions of the human and mouse spike-in transcriptomes (downloaded from the ENSEMBL database28 using SALMON29 with default parameters). Only reads uniquely mapping to combined human and mouse genome assemblies were quantified. The quantified data were processed using tximport30 and differential expression analysis was done using DESeq2.31 Genes with fewer than 5 reads in all the biological replicates of one condition were filtered out before differential expression analysis. Genes were defined as differentially expressed if they had a minimum absolute log2-fold change of 1, and a BH adjusted p value less than 0.05. Genes from Hallmark gene sets from the Molecular Signatures Database (MSigDB)32 were tested for enrichment among expressed genes using Gene set enrichment analysis (GSEA) algorithm33 implemented in the clusterProfiler R package.34 Gene sets with adjusted p-value < 0.05 were defined as enriched. For the analysis of the repetitive genomic elements, the reads were first trimmed by Trim Galore 0.6.1035 with default parameters and then aligned to the concatenated human (GRCh38/hg38) and mouse (mm10) genome index using STAR 2.7.11b36 with parameters chosen to optimize alignment for the quantification of transposable elements:37

Custom.gtf file was created from RepeatMasker (Smit, A. F. A., Hubley, R. & Green, P. RepeatMasker Open-4.0 (RepeatMasker, 2013–2015) annotation: simple repeats and low-complexity regions were removed, and repeat names (repName) values were set as gene_id. Differential expression analysis was done using DESeq2 on all quantified genes and repeats with more than 5 reads in all biological replicates of one condition. The RNA-seq data have been deposited in ArrayExpress under the accession code: E-MTAB-14858.

Clonogenic survival assay

200 kV X-rays or C-ion irradiations were used for clonogenic survival assays. For T98G, 0.2 × 106, for U251, 0.3 × 106, for GB-1, 0.4 × 106 and for KALS-1, 0.35 × 106 cells were seeded in Nunc™ Lab-Tek™ ChamberFlasks (170920) 24 h prior to irradiation. The cell seeding number was optimized according to the cell size and doubling time to achieve 70% confluency at irradiation. KMR-206 (300 nM) was added 2 h prior to irradiation and removed 24 h after irradiation. Immediately after irradiation, the cells were harvested, diluted with an appropriate volume of supplemented medium and a previously established, cell line-specific cell count, listed in Supplementary Table 4, was seeded on 6-well plates. After a cell line-dependent incubation period of 10 d for U251, 12 d for T98G, 9 d for GB-1 and 11 d for KALS-1 cells, the cells were fixed with 96% methanol and stained with 0.5% crystal violet. Colonies that had more than 50 cells were counted. Survival curve fitting was calculated using the linear‒quadratic (LQ) equation. For each value of the delivered physical dose in Gy, surviving fractions were calculated in relation to the plating efficiency of non-irradiated control samples. Mean and standard deviation were derived from a minimum of 3 independent experiments. Standard errors result from error propagation. Survival curve fitting of the linear‒quadratic model was done by applying a 1/σ-weighted minimum chi-square estimation to the linear‒quadratic model.

Flow cytometry analysis of C11-BODIPY

U251, T98G, and KALS-1 cells were cultured in T25 flasks (Sarstedt) with and without 5 µM liproxstatin-1 2 h prior to irradiation, and irradiated with 8 Gy for three consecutive days. On the third day, 4 h after the last irradiation, the cells were harvested, split, and half were filtered through strainer caps and incubated with 500 µL of medium containing 5 µM BODIPY 581/591 C11 (Thermo Fisher Scientific, D3861) and Fixable Viability Dye eFluor™ 450 (eBioscience, 65-0863, 1:2000) for 30 min at 37 °C. Flow cytometry was performed on a Bio-Rad ZE5 Cell Analyzer using 405 nm, 488 nm, and 561 nm lasers for excitation as well as 460/22, 525/35, and 615/24 detection filters for viability dye eFluor™ 450, BODIPY oxidized, and BODIPY reduced respectively. The cells were gated for intact cells, two times for single cells, and downsampled with Plugin “DownSampleV3” to a total of 3000 cells. The levels of lipid peroxidation were determined by gating for C11-BODPIY-positive cells (Supplementary Figure 11), and ordinary one-way ANOVA with multiple comparison (comparing means between groups) was conducted using GraphPad Prism V10.

ATP assay

Cells were trypsinized and counted after the last irradiation. For each sample, 3000 cells were seeded per well of a 96-well plate. After 72 h, 100 µL of cell-free supernatant was transferred to a flat bottom 96-well plate. 100 µL of CellTiter-Glo® 2.0 (Promega) solution was added per well, plates were mixed on an orbital shaker for 2 min, followed by 10 min incubation in the dark. Luminescence was measured using Varioskan® Flash (Thermo Fisher Scientific). 96-well plates with attached cells were washed with PBS, fixed with methanol for 15 min and stained with 0.5% crystal violet for 30 min. After drying, 150 µL of 1% SDS was added to each well, and the plates were incubated until complete dissolution of the crystal violet. Absorbance was measured at 590 nm and used to normalize the luminescence values. ATP assay data was analyzed and plotted using GraphPad Prism (9.1.1). Experiments were performed in 3 biological replicates and each sample was measured in 3 technical replicates.

ELISA

The cells were cultured and treated in T25 flasks. 72 h after the last irradiation, 1 mL of the supernatant was transferred to a 1.5 mL Eppendorf tube for further processing. The remaining supernatant was collected in 15 mL tubes, and the cells were trypsinized, resuspended in the supernatant collected in 15 mL tubes to retain dead cells and counted for data normalization. TGF-β release was analyzed using TGF-beta 1 Quantikine ELISA Kit (R&D systems, DB100B). Analysis was performed according to the protocol supplied by the manufacturer, using the Varioskan Flash Microplate Reader (Thermo Fisher Scientific). ELISA data was analyzed and plotted using GraphPad Prism (9.1.1). Experiments were performed in 3 biological replicates and each sample was measured in 3 technical replicates.

Isolation of peripheral blood mononuclear cells (PBMCs)

PBMCs were obtained from the venous blood of three healthy human donors in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki and approved by the Ethics Committee of the Medical University of Vienna (2001/2018, 1238/2024). Written informed consent was obtained from all participants. PBMCs were isolated by density gradient centrifugation (800 × g without break for 20 min) using Lymphoprep (Axis-Shield), followed by two washes with Ca2+/Mg2+-free phosphate-buffered saline (PBS) and cell counting. The cells were sedimented by centrifugation (350 × g, 5 min) and directly resuspended in fresh, cell-free supernatants collected from GBM cell lines 72 h after the last irradiation. 2 × 106 cells/mL of PBMCs were resuspended in fresh, supplemented DMEM or RPMI medium as a control. PBMCs were analyzed by spectral flow cytometry after 44 h of culturing at 37 °C in a humidified atmosphere with 5% CO2.

Spectral flow cytometry

PBMCs were harvested and detached from cell culture plates using ice-cold PBS with 1.5 mM EDTA. After pelleting by centrifugation (350 × g, 5 min) and washing with PBS, the cells were stained with the LIVE/DEAD Fixable Blue Dead Cell Stain Kit (Thermo Fisher Scientific, L23105, 1:500 final concentration) for 20 min at room temperature, followed by two washing steps with pre-cooled staining buffer (PBS containing 1% BSA and 0.02% NaN3). The cells were resuspended in Brilliant Stain Buffer (BD Biosciences, 566349) supplemented with 3.2 mg/mL human IgG (Beriglobin P; CSL Behring) and 20 μL/mL True-Stain Monocyte Blocker (BioLegend, 426102) and incubated for 30 min on ice. 2 × 106 cells were stained using the full immunophenotyping panel, which consisted of 31 mAbs against surface antigens prediluted in Brilliant Stain Buffer, as indicated in Supplementary Table 5. In parallel, a control lineage panel consisting of 12 mAbs was used to stain 106 cells. Pre-diluted chemokine receptor mAbs against CCR7, CCR4, CCR5, CCR6 and CXCR3, as well as a TCRγδ mAb, were added prior to the remaining 25 mAbs in order to enhance the staining. The cells were incubated for 30 min on ice in complete mAb cocktail. Cytek FSP CompBeads (Cytek Biosciences, #B7-10011) or non-stained cells were used in parallel for single stains and controls for unmixing. Following mAb staining, the samples were washed two times with staining buffer, and data was acquired immediately using a Cytek Aurora spectral flow cytometer equipped with SpectroFlo software (Cytek Biosciences). Data unmixing was performed with SpectroFlo software, and further analysis was performed using FlowJo 10 software (BD Biosciences). In order to remove artifacts, debris and doubles, all samples were pre-gated by evaluating the scatter profiles, and subsequently gated for individual PBMC subsets in accordance with the literature39 and displayed in Supplementary Figure 14. Data from the lineage control stain was used for setting the gates for individual activation markers, which allowed for evaluation of the percentage of activated cells within individual PBMC lineages in the fully stained samples. The dendritic cell populations were evaluated using a monocyte control stain due to the absence of an HLA-DR mAb in the lineage stain.

Results

Radiation activates the type I interferon response in GBM cell lines

To test whether genotoxic stress can induce the IFN-I response in GBM, we treated four GBM cell lines, U251, T98G, GB-1, and KALS-1, with 3 × 8 Gy of X-rays on three consecutive days and collected samples 24 h and 72 h after the last treatment. The hypofractionated dose of 3 × 8 Gy was used instead of a single high dose because it was previously shown to be more immunogenic in breast cancer and pancreatic cancer cells.40,41 In addition, we treated the cells with Taxol, oxaliplatin (crosslinking agent), irinotecan (topoisomerase I inhibitor) or temozolomide for 72 h. Cytoplasmic dsDNA was analyzed by immunofluorescence microscopy, and the expression levels and phosphorylation status of STING and the IFN-I cascade (STING, IRF3, TBK1, and STAT1) by western blotting, and the expression of IFNB1, CXCL10, PD-L1, and IL-6 by RT-qPCR. IFNB1 expression can be induced by the IRF3 or NF-κB transcription factors, CXCL10, and PD-L1 are ISGs, while CXCL10 can also be induced directly by NF-κB in an IFNB1-independent manner. IL-6 is induced directly by NF-κB.42‐44 Two out of four GBM cell lines, U251 and T98G, showed a strong induction of the IFN-I response after radiation (3 × 8 Gy 72 h), as judged by an increase in pSTAT1 (Figure 1A), as well as IFNB1 and CXCL10 (Figure 1B). A considerably weaker radiation-induced IFN-I response was observed in the GB-1 and KALS-1 cell lines (Figure 1). In KALS-1, radiation induced pSTAT3, a known antagonist of STAT1,45 which may account for the observed reduction in STAT1 and CXCL10 levels (Figure 1). In GB-1 cells, we failed to detect IFNB1 expression by RT-qPCR although the expression of CXCL10 was induced after radiation. The induction of IL-6 after radiation in GB-1 cells may explain the IFNB1-independent upregulation of CXCL10 through the NF‒κB axis.42 All four cell lines showed radiation-induced accumulation of cytoplasmic dsDNA as a signal for the activation of the STING pathway (Supplementary Figure 1). Collectively, these results show that the IFN-I response can be induced with radiation in GBM cell lines at variable magnitudes.

Radiation-induced type I interferon response in GBM cell lines is STING/MAVS- and NF-κB-dependent

We next analyzed whether the IFN-I response is dependent on STING, MAVS or NF-κB by generating CRISPR/Cas9 KOs of STING or MAVS in GBM cell lines or by treating them with the NF-κB inhibitor (NF-κBi) BI605906. BI605906 inhibits IKKβ and thereby prevents the activation of RelA (p65).46 In U251, radiation-induced IFNB1, pSTAT1 and CXCL10 were severely reduced in STING KO or NF-κBi-treated cells (Figures 2 and 3). In T98G, radiation-induced IFNB1 expression and pSTAT1 were partly MAVS- and STING-dependent but not NF-κB-dependent, whereas CXCL10 and IL-6 induction were dependent on MAVS, STING, and NF-κB (Figures 2 and 3, Supplementary Figure 2). In GB-1, CXCL10, and IL-6 induction were STING/MAVS-independent, and only CXCL10 (but not IL-6) levels were reduced upon NF-κB inhibition (Figures 2 and 3, Supplementary Figure 2). In KALS-1, IFNB1, pSTAT1, and CXCL10 were STING- and NF-κB-dependent, whereas IL-6 was only NF-κB-dependent (Figures 2 and 3).

A comparative analysis of STING levels across the four GBM cell lines showed highest STING levels in KALS-1, which may explain why the radiation-induced IFN-I response is dependent on STING in KALS-1 (Supplementary Figure 3A). Moreover, of all the cell lines, KALS-1 has the highest basal levels of STAT1, pSTAT1, and IFNB1 (Supplementary Figure 3), which aligns with the weak induction of this pathway after radiation (Figures 1–3). T98G showed the highest levels of MAVS, pTBK1, pRelA, and IL-6 (Supplementary Figure 3A and B), which correlates with the MAVS- and NF-κB-mediated radiation responses in these cells (Figures 2 and 3, Supplementary Figure 2). Despite expressing STING, GB-1 cells did not induce IFNB1, pSTAT1, and ISGs such as RIG-I and PD-L1 but showed the highest induction of non-canonical NF-κB signaling (Supplementary Figure 3). cGAS was not detectable in U251 and T98G and showed weak levels in GB-1 and KALS-1, suggesting that other dsDNA sensors such as IFI16,47 which is expressed in all cell lines, may functionally replace cGAS.

Overall, our data suggest that hypofractionated radiation induces (1) STING- and NF-κB-dependent IFN-I signaling in U251 and KALS-1, (2) MAVS-, STING- and NF-κB-dependent IFN-I signaling in T98G, and (3) STING/MAVS-independent non-canonical NF-κB signaling in GB-1 cells (Figures 2 and 3, Supplementary Figures 2 and 3).

Combinatorial treatments enhance radiation-induced type I interferon and NF-κB response in GBM

Given that the STING agonist diABZI was recently shown to synergize with radiotherapy to induce anti-tumor immunity in GBM25 and various inhibitors of DNA damage response, cell cycle checkpoints and IFN-I signaling have immunogenic effects, we tested combinatorial effects of 3 × 8 Gy X-ray with the PARP1/2 inhibitor olaparib, the specific PARP1 inhibitor AZD5305, the PARP7 inhibitor KMR-206, the STING agonist diABZI, the ATM inhibitor AZD0156, the ATR inhibitor AZD6738 (ceralasertib), the CHK1 inhibitor prexasertib, and the WEE1 inhibitor AZD1775 (adavosertib) (Figure 4, Supplementary Figures 4 and 5).

In U251, only PARP7i combined with 3 × 8 Gy showed an increase in CXCL10 levels relative to radiation alone (Figure 4A). In T98G, diABZI and PARP7i showed synergistic effects with 3 × 8 Gy, which was evident from increased IFNB1, CXCL10, PD-L1, and IL-6 (Figure 4B). As in the case of U251, PARP7i showed the strongest effect on CXCL10 upregulation in combination with 3 × 8 Gy in T98G (Figure 4B). In GB-1, only diABZI slightly increased CXCL10 expression (Figure 4C). In KALS-1, PARP7i in combination with 3 × 8 Gy showed a stronger induction of IFNB1 and IL-6 compared to 3 × 8 Gy alone, whereas radiation dampened PARP7i stimulatory effects on CXCL10 (Figure 4D, Supplementary Figure 5B), as previously observed with radiation alone (Figures 1–3).

Taken together, PARP7i showed immunostimulatory effects in U251, T98G, and KALS-1, while immunostimulatory effects of the STING agonist diABZI were observed in T98G and GB-1.

Genome-wide transcriptional effects of radiation, PARP7 inhibitor, and STING agonist in GBM cells

Based on the above findings, we performed RNA-seq analysis of all four GBM cell lines treated with 3 × 8 Gy, PARP7i, or diABZI, alone or in combination with 3 × 8 Gy (Supplementary Data 1 and 2). In U251, 3 × 8 Gy alone or in combination with PARP7i or diABZI induced the expression of more than 1000 genes and repressed more than 500 genes, with the most pronounced transcriptional changes resulting from 3 × 8 Gy in combination with diABZI (Figure 5A). The key enriched pathways among the upregulated genes in all three conditions were interferon alpha and gamma, NF-κB and JAK-STAT (Figure 5D), in accordance with the STING- and NF-κB-dependent regulation of the IFN-I response in U251 cells (Figures 2 and 3). PARP7i and diABZI alone induced only 6 and 4 genes, respectively, among which were the immune response genes CXCL8 (PARP7i), OASL, MX1, and ISG15 (diABZI).

Of all the cell lines, T98G showed the strongest response to 3 × 8 Gy and combinations, with more than 2700 upregulated and more than 1500 downregulated genes (Figure 5A). Pathway analysis revealed enrichment of NF-κB signaling, the inflammatory response, KRAS signaling, interferon alpha and gamma among the upregulated genes and the G2/M checkpoint, E2F and Myc targets among the downregulated genes (Figure 5D). 616 genes that were specifically upregulated in 3 × 8 Gy with PARP7i were enriched in the inflammatory response, while PARP7i alone did not induce immune response genes (Figure 5E). Among the most highly upregulated genes after 3 × 8 Gy was IL1B (interleukin-1 beta) (Figure 5E), which activates the NF-κB pathway. On the one hand, IL-1β has been shown to polarize macrophages towards the M1 pro-inflammatory state, but on the other hand, it can also dampen anti-tumor immunity by recruiting myeloid-derived suppressor cells (MDSCs) and T-regulatory cells (Tregs), thus promoting a pro-tumorigenic microenvironment.48

In GB-1 cells, 3 × 8 Gy alone or in combination with PARP7i or diABZI induced the expression of more than 600 genes and repressed more than 400 genes (Figure 5A). For the upregulated genes, the most highly enriched pathways were E2F signaling, the G2/M checkpoint, NF-κB signaling and Myc targets, whereas interferon alpha signaling was enriched among the downregulated genes (Figure 5D). These results are in accordance with the weak and NF-κB-dependent response of GB-1 cells to radiation, as well as the induction of non-canonical NF-κB signaling (Supplementary Figure 3), which was shown to block IFNB1 expression by preventing the recruitment of canonical NF-κB to the IFNB1 promoter and by epigenetic silencing.49,50 In addition to the downregulation of immunostimulatory pathways, the counterproductive effects of radiation in GB-1 also entail upregulation of pro-survival and oncogenic pathways (e.g., E2F, Myc, and TGF-β) (Figure 5D).

In KALS-1 cells, the number of upregulated and downregulated genes was higher (>2000 and >1000 respectively) and combinatorial treatment with PARP7i or diABZI resulted in the upregulation of both the NF-κB and interferon alpha and gamma pathways with concomitant downregulation of Myc targets (Figure 5A and D), confirming our previous results regarding the immunostimulatory effects of these combinatorial treatments. 536 genes that were upregulated only for 3 × 8 Gy with PARP7i are enriched in transcription regulation, while 326 genes that were upregulated only for 3 × 8 Gy with diABZI are enriched in the IRF1, STAT2 and ISGF3 motifs, all related to interferon signaling. Importantly, PARP7i and diABZI in combination with 3 × 8 Gy specifically upregulated various ISGs, including IFIT1, CCL5 and CXCL10 in KALS-1 cells (Figure 5E).

RNA-seq analyses additionally revealed radiation-induced expression of repetitive elements such as long terminal repeats (LTRs), especially in T98G (181 after 3 × 8 Gy) and KALS-1 (176 after 3 × 8 Gy) (Figure 5B). In all four GBM cell lines, radiation derepressed MER51D and LTR12D (LTR/ERV1 family), and also MER53 and ACRO1 in combination with PARP7i (Figure 5C). The upregulation of repetitive elements may explain the strongest inflammatory response in T98G compared to other GBM cells and its dependence on MAVS as the central hub for RNA sensing and innate immune signaling.

STING agonist and PARP7 inhibitor potentiate immunogenic effects of single-dose radiation

Based on the observed synergistic effects of radiation and small-molecule inhibitors or agonists, we tested whether synergistic effects could be achieved with single-dose radiation (8 Gy) and whether particle radiation with C-ions has superior effects compared to conventional photon radiation with X-rays.

Single-dose radiation alone did not induce an IFN-I response in U251 cells (Supplementary Figures 6A and 7A). The STING agonist diABZI showed a strong induction of pSTING, pIRF3, pSTAT1 and IFNB1 when combined with 8 Gy X-ray or C-ions, while ATRi and WEE1i showed modest effects (Supplementary Figures 6A and 7A). Interestingly, 8 Gy radiation reduced IL-6 levels (Supplementary Figure 6A), which was not observed upon 3 × 8 Gy (Figure 4A). In T98G, 8 Gy X-rays or C-ions showed an increase in pSTAT1, IFNB1, CXCL10, IL-6, and PD-L1 (Supplementary Figures 6B and 7B). PARP7i further enhanced the effect of radiation on the expression of CXCL10 and IL-6, while diABZI enhanced the expression of IFNB1, IL-6 and PD-L1 (Supplementary Figure 6B). This finding confirms that IFNB1 and NF-κB signaling are induced by radiation in T98G and can be enhanced with diABZI and PARP7i. C-ions showed a stronger effect compared to X-rays, particularly concerning CXCL10 and IL-6 induction in combination with PARP7i (Supplementary Figure 6B). In GB-1, single-dose radiation showed a weak induction of pSTAT1, IL-6, and CXCL10 comparable to 3 × 8 Gy (Supplementary Figures 6C and 8A). Despite a strong increase in pSTING, diABZI only slightly increased CXCL10 levels in combination with 8 Gy radiation in GB-1 cells, which was comparable to 3 × 8 Gy (Figure 4C). KALS-1 responded differently to PARP7i depending on the radiation modality: in combination with X-rays, IFNB1, and CXCL10 levels were increased, whereas in combination with C-ions, they were reduced (Supplementary Figure 6D). In KALS-1, pSTAT1 was induced by PARP7i independent of radiation (Supplementary Figure 8B). These results are at variance with 3 × 8 Gy combined with PARP7i, whereby IFNB1 was increased and CXCL10 was reduced (Figure 4D). diABZI showed no effect on IFNB1 in combination with 8 Gy radiation, as was the case for 3 × 8 Gy (Figure 4D, Supplementary Figure 6D).

Taken together, IFNB1 appears to be the dominant immunogenic pathway in U251, which can be induced by PARP7i combined with hypofractionated radiation (3 × 8 Gy) or diABZI combined with single-dose radiation (8 Gy X-ray or C-ions). In T98G, C-ions in combination with diABZI/PARP7i showed superior immunogenic effects compared to X-rays. GB-1 were largely unresponsive, while KALS-1 showed consistent induction of IFNB1, CXCL10 and IL-6 in response to single-dose X-rays combined with PARP7i.

Autophagy inhibition modulates radiation-induced type I interferon response in GBM

Autophagy was shown to eliminate cytoplasmic dsDNA and degrade key effectors of the STING pathway, while autophagy inhibitors were shown to promote radiation-induced anti-tumor immunity.22,23,51‐54 Thus, we tested whether the autophagy inhibitor SAR405 that targets early-stage autophagy can potentiate the effects of hypofractionated radiation (3 × 8 Gy) on the IFN-I response in GBM. Among the four GBM cell lines, the expression of the autophagy marker LC-3 was the highest in U251 (Supplementary Figure 3). Accordingly, SAR405 enhanced radiation-induced immunogenic signaling in U251 and to a lesser extent also in GB-1, but had the opposite effect on T98G (Supplementary Figure 9). In KALS-1, SAR405 showed synergistic effects with radiation as concerns IFNB1, IL-6, and PD-L1, but radiation dampened the SAR405-mediated induction of CXCL10 (Supplementary Figure 9), in accordance with previous results (Figures 1–4). Taken together, autophagy inhibition shows differential effects on immunogenic signaling in GBM cells.

Cell death pathways contribute to radiation-induced type I interferon response in GBM

Irradiation leads to dose-dependent cell death, with KALS-1 being the most sensitive, followed by GB-1, T98G, and U251 based on clonogenic assays (Supplementary Figure 10A). As expected, all the cell lines were more sensitive to C-ions because of their higher ionization density (quantified by linear energy transfer—LET) (Supplementary Figure 10A). PARP7i increased the sensitivity of all cell lines except for GB-1 to X-ray irradiation but had little effect on C-ion-induced lethality (Supplementary Figure 10A).

Cell death pathways can have pro- or anti-immunogenic effects; apoptosis counteracts the IFN-I response due to caspase-mediated cleavage of cGAS, MAVS, and IRF3,55,56 necroptosis exerts immunogenic effects due to membrane rupture and the release of damage-associated molecular patterns (DAMPs),57 while ferroptosis was reported to have both pro- and anti-immunogenic effects.58 The necroptosis marker MLKL was most highly expressed in untreated T98G cells, while the ferroptosis markers COX2 (PTGS2), ACSL4 and TFR1 were induced in irradiated T98G and KALS-1 cells, especially in combination with PARP7i (Figures 3 and 5E, Supplementary Figure 3). Lipid peroxidation, as assessed by C11-BODIPY staining using flow cytometry, was increased in irradiated U251, T98G, and KALS-1 cells, which may suggest the induction of ferroptosis59 (Figure 6A and Supplementary Figure 11). NF-κB inhibition reduced COX2 upregulation in irradiated T98G and KALS-1 cells (Figure 3), suggesting that ferroptosis may contribute to the immunogenic effects of radiation in these cells. To further probe the contribution of cell death pathways to radiation-induced immunogenic effects, we treated GBM cell lines with the apoptosis inhibitor Q-VD-Oph, the necroptosis inhibitor necrostatin-1, and the ferroptosis inhibitor liproxstatin-1 and exposed them to 3 × 8 Gy radiation. In U251, T98G, and KALS-1, ferroptosis inhibitor dampened the radiation-induced IFN-I response (Figure 6B, Supplementary Figure 10B). In T98G, necroptosis inhibitor reduced IFNB1 and IL-6, but not CXCL10 after radiation. Apoptosis inhibition increased the IFN-I response in U251; in KALS-1 cells, IFNB1 was increased, whereas CXCL10 and IL-6 were reduced. In GB-1, cell death inhibitors had no effect on the IFN-I response. Taken together, our results suggest that ferroptosis promotes the immunogenic effects of radiation in U251, T98G, and KALS-1 cells, whereas apoptosis exerts anti-immunogenic effects in some GBM cells.

Immune cell activation by irradiated GBM cells

To measure the functional outcome of immunogenic signaling induced by radiation, the PARP7 inhibitor KMR-206 and the STING agonist diABZI in GBM cells, we measured the release of ATP, which acts as a DAMP and is used as a marker of immunogenic cell death (ICD).60 ATP release was induced in U251 cells treated with 3 × 8 Gy and diABZI, T98G treated with diABZI alone or diABZI/PARP7i in combination with 3 × 8 Gy, and in KALS-1 treated with diABZI alone (Supplementary Figure 12A). The release of ATP from dying cells into the extracellular space activates the P2X7 purinergic receptors on dendritic cells and causes P2X7/NLRP3 receptor-dependent activation of the inflammasome and the release of proinflammatory cytokines such as interleukin-1β (IL-1β).61,62 IL-1β promotes the activation of CD8+ T cells61 and was induced in all irradiated GBM cells (Figure 5E).

To measure the activation of immune cells, we incubated freshly isolated peripheral blood mononuclear cells (PBMCs) with the cell-free supernatants from GBM cells exposed to 3 × 8 Gy without or with PARP7i or diABZI, followed by the PBMC analysis using spectral flow cytometry (Figure 7, Supplementary Figure 13). Lineage markers allowed us to differentiate between monocytes, natural killer (NK) cells, natural killer T (NKT) cells, and CD4+ and CD8+ T cells (Supplementary Figure 14), and additional well-known activation-induced surface markers were used to delineate their activation state.63,64 For monocytes, we assessed co-stimulatory ligands CD80, CD86, and CD25, which are all involved in antigen presentation and T-cell activation. The activation of lymphocytes, with a focus on subsets (NK, NKT, CD4+, and CD8+ T cells) that are crucial in anti-tumor immune responses, was scored based on the early-to-intermediate activation markers CD25, CD38, and CD69. To assess chemokine binding to immune cells and their potential chemotactic capacity, we analyzed the surface expression of CCR5 (a receptor for the proinflammatory chemokines CCL3, CCL4, CCL5 that we previously found to be highly expressed by activated myeloid cells65) and CXCR3 (a receptor for the IFN-inducible chemokines CXCL9, CXCL10, and CXCL11).66,67 CCR5 and CXCR3 are preferentially expressed in myeloid and lymphoid cells, respectively, and play a pivotal role in immune cell recruitment to sites of inflammation, including tumors.66,67 Upon chemokine binding, these receptors are cleared from the cell surface by the process of ligand-induced internalization.68,69

Supernatants from untreated U251 cells showed immunostimulatory effects by upregulating CD80 on monocytes, which was not the case for T98G and KALS-1 cells (Figure 7). Supernatants from U251 treated with 3 × 8 Gy + diABZI induced the expression of CD25 on the surface of all the examined immune cells. Combinations between 3 × 8 Gy and PARP7i or diABZI induced CD69 on NK and NKT cells, while 3 × 8 Gy + diABZI induced CD38 and CD69 not only on NK and NKT cells but also on CD4+ and CD8+ T cells (Figure 7). The observed activation of T cells is due to cytokines released from cancer cells (and not direct activation through TCR binding to peptide-MHC), also referred to as bystander T cell activation.70 Reduced surface expression of the chemokine receptors CCR5 and CXCR3 on NKT and CD8+ T cells was observed after 3 × 8 Gy in combination with PARP7i and diABZI, suggesting the internalization of the receptors following chemokine engagement (Supplementary Figure 13).

Radiation alone was sufficient to induce the expression of CD25, CD80, and CD86 on monocytes treated with T98G and KALS-1 supernatants, which was further increased in combination with PARP7i (T98G) and diABZI (T98G and KALS-1). Supernatants from T98G and KALS-1 treated with 3 × 8 Gy + diABZI strongly upregulated CD25, CD38 and CD69 on the surface of NK, NKT, CD4+, and CD8+ T cells, while 3 × 8 Gy + PARP7i upregulated CD69 on NK and NKT cells (Figure 7). CCR5 and CXCR3 were downregulated in NKT and CD8+ T cells upon exposure to supernatants from T98G cells treated with 3 × 8 Gy in combination with diABZI, while CCR5 was also downregulated by 3 × 8 Gy with PARP7i (Supplementary Figure 13). Supernatants from KALS-1 cells treated with 3 × 8 Gy with PARP7i or diABZI reduced CCR5 on monocytes, NKT and CD8+ T cells and CXCR3 on CD8+ T cells (Supplementary Figure 13).

The stronger and broader effects of the STING agonist diABZI compared to PARP7i may result from direct activation of immune cells by diABZI, which is present in the supernatant. RT-qPCR analysis of immune cells indeed showed upregulation of IFNB1, CXCL10, IL-6, and TNF, with IFNB1 being most highly induced after 3 × 8 Gy with diABZI, CXCL10 after 3 × 8 Gy with PARP7i, and IL-6 and TNF showing comparable induction after 3 × 8 Gy with PARP7i or diABZI (Supplementary Figure 15).

Collectively, these results reveal that GBM cells exposed to radiation and diABZI can activate monocytes, NK, NKT, CD4+, and CD8+ T cells, while PARP7i combined with radiation shows immunostimulatory effects on NK and NKT cells. Both combinations can attract NKT and CD8+ cells via chemokines.

Importantly, PARP7 inhibition efficiently induced pSTAT1, CXCL10, and IL-6 in combination with radiation in LK17, which was not the case for diABZI (Figure 8A–D), highlighting the potential benefits of PARP7i over STING agonists in GBM patients with low STING levels.

Discussion

Targeting various players in the DNA damage response, replication stress, cell cycle regulation, immune signaling, and autophagy was shown to induce anti-tumor immunity through activation of the IFN-I response. This applies to PARP1/2 inhibitors in BRCA-deficient ovarian cancer, triple-negative breast cancer, colorectal cancer as well as lung cancer,74‐79 ATM inhibitors in pancreatic cancer, breast cancer, and melanoma,80,81 ATR inhibitors in hepatocellular and colorectal cancer as well as melanoma,82‐84 CHK1 and WEE1 inhibitors in lung cancer,85 PARP7 inhibitors in breast and lung cancer,20,86,87 STING agonists in breast, colon, and pancreatic cancer,14,88‐90 and autophagy inhibitors in breast cancer, lung cancer, and melanoma.22,52,53 ATR inhibitors showed synergistic effects with radiation in hepatocellular and colorectal cancer,82,83 while STING agonists showed synergistic effects with radiation in colorectal, hepatocellular, lung and pancreatic cancer, as well as GBM.14,91‐93 In GBM, the STING agonist diABZI in combination with radiation was shown to induce the STING pathway and sensitize GBM to immune checkpoint blockade.25

We examined the immunostimulatory effects of all the above inhibitors in four different GBM cell lines expressing variable amounts of STING, MAVS, RelA (p65 NF-κB), autophagy and ferroptosis markers. We found that in three out of four cell lines, radiation induced the IFN-I response, which was dependent on STING and NF-κB (U251 and KALS-1) or MAVS, STING and NF-κB (T98G) (Figures 1–3, Supplementary Figure 3). Among all the tested compounds, the PARP7 inhibitor KMR-206 and the STING agonist diABZI enhanced the radiation-induced IFN-I response (Figures 4 and 5, Supplementary Figure 6) and the activation of immune cells, including monocytes, CD4+ and CD8+ T cells (diABZI), and NK and NKT cells (diABZI and PARP7i) (Figures 7 and 8E, Supplementary Figure 13).

Moreover, we found that ferroptosis promoted the IFN-I response in all three cell lines, whereas apoptosis had an anti-immunogenic effect in U251 and KALS-1 cells (Figure 6). The effect of ferroptosis inhibition was most striking in T98G cells, which expressed the highest levels of the ferroptosis marker COX2 in response to radiation (Figure 5, Supplementary Figure 3). Ferroptosis is a regulatory cell death pathway, which depends on iron and accumulation of reactive oxygen species (ROS) that induce lipid peroxidation and membrane rupture.58 Induction of ferroptosis by the SCL7A11 inhibitor erastin or the GPX4 inhibitor RSL3 was shown to sensitize GBM cells to temozolomide94,95 while radiation was shown to induce ferroptosis and increased ferroptosis correlates with a better response to radiotherapy.96 However, ferroptosis was also shown to contribute to the immunosuppressive tumor microenvironment through enrichment of Tregs and M2-polarised macrophages.97 COX2 or prostaglandin-endoperoxide synthase 2 (PTGS2) is induced by interleukin-1β98 and ferroptosis downstream of lipid hydroperoxides.99 It produces prostaglandin E2 (PGE2), which can enhance tumor proliferation and invasion, and induce immune suppression.100 Induction of co-stimulatory ligands on monocytes (CD80, CD86, CD25) by irradiated T98G cells (Figure 7) may argue in favor of immunostimulatory effects of ferroptosis in T98G cells.

T98G also showed the highest radiation-induced upregulation of hERVs (Figure 5), which may explain MAVS-dependent IFN-I response in these cells. Transposable elements induced by radiation potentiate anti-tumor immune response through RIG-I-MAVS signaling.101,102 Moreover, they can activate the IFN-I response by acting as enhancers for STAT1.103 For example, MER41B hERV, which is upregulated in T98G and KALS-1, has a STAT1-binding motif. Deletion of MER41 located 50 kb upstream of the AIM2 interferon-stimulated gene impairs its expression and inflammasome assembly.103

In three out of four cell lines (U251, GB-1, and KALS-1), autophagy inhibition enhances radiation-induced immunogenic signaling, which is in line with previous reports demonstrating the clearance of immunostimulatory mitochondrial DNA as well as cGAS, STING, and IRF3 by the autophagy machinery23 (Supplementary Figure 9). Surprisingly, autophagy inhibition had the opposite effect in T98G cells. This effect might be caused by a reduction in pro-immunogenic ferroptotic cell death. Autophagy inhibition can prevent the degradation of ferritin, which leads to a reduction in free iron and a reduction in ferroptotic cell death as a result.104

GB-1 cells did not induce an IFN-I response upon radiation exposure, as evident from the lack of expression of IFNB1 (by RT-qPCR) and ISGs (based on RNA-seq). This may be explained by radiation-induced activation of the non-canonical NF-κB pathway, which antagonizes canonical NF-κB that promotes IFNB1 induction after radiation.49,50 The lack of IFNB1 expression was previously observed in pancreatic cancer cells MIA PaCa-2 and TNBC cells MDA-MB-231, which also show a strong induction of non-canonical NF-κB signaling.41,54 Moreover, GBM patients that underwent radiation showed a better overall survival in the case of low expression of non-canonical NF-κB.49 Given that GB-1 cells were largely unresponsive to radiation and combinatorial treatments, high levels of non-canonical NF-κB (RelB, NFKB2) may be used as a predictive biomarker that would dismiss these treatment options for GBM patients.

Hypofractionated radiation (3 × 8 Gy X-rays) was more (U251, T98G) or equally (GB-1, KALS-1) immunogenic compared to single-dose radiation (Figure 1, Supplementary Figure 6). Hypofractionated radiation has been shown to be more advantageous compared to single high-dose radiation due to multiple doses allowing a greater proportion of cells to be in a radiosensitive phase of the cell cycle (S-phase), lower toxicity for normal cells that can repair sublethal damage between doses, and stronger immunogenic effects via the release of DAMPs, which prime dendritic cells to activate anti-tumor T cells.105 PARP7i potentiated the IFN-I response induced by hypofractionated radiation in U251 but in KALS-1 PARP7i had an equally strong effect in combination with hypofractionated or single-dose X-ray irradiation, whereas in T98G, PARP7i was most potent in combination with single-dose C-ion radiation. The STING agonist diABZI potentiated the IFN-I response induced by single-dose radiation in U251, showed stronger effects in combination with hypofractionated radiation in T98G and had no combinatorial effect in KALS-1. These results suggest that PARP7i and diABZI can potentiate the immunogenic effects of single-dose radiation, alleviating the need for multi-fraction radiation.

The PARP7 inhibitor KMR-206 was shown to be a more selective PARP7 inhibitor compared to RBN-2397, with a 75-fold higher selectivity for PARP7 over PARP2.106 PARP7 inhibitors were shown to increase PARP7 protein levels and induce IFNB1 and CXCL10 expression in CT-26 colon cancer cells.106 PARP7 inhibition destabilizes the transcription factor FRA1, resulting in increased IRF1 expression and consequently also IRF1-regulated expression of apoptosis and immune signaling genes through the RIG-I-MAVS/IRF3 axis.107 Moreover, PARP7 inhibition promotes the interaction between IRF3 and the transcriptional co-activator CBP/p300 thereby inducing the expression of IFNB1.108 Cell line-independent effect suggests that direct targeting of the signaling pathway through PARP7 inhibition in combination with radiation may be clinically more straightforward compared to other treatment strategies that focus on increasing the levels of immunostimulatory cytoplasmic dsDNA (e.g., PARP1/2i, ATMi, ATRi, WEE1i). Moreover, PARP7i-induced IFN-I response seems to be more advantageous compared to STING agonists given that GBM cells express variable STING levels (Supplementary Figure 3) and that STING is often downregulated in GBM patients.71 Indeed, patient-derived GBM CSCs with low STING levels did not respond to radiation and diABZI but induced pro-inflammatory cytokines (CXCL10, IL-6) when radiation was combined with PARP7i (Figure 8). Finally, unlike PARP7i, diABZI enhanced the release of TGF-β (Supplementary Figure 12B), which is known as a tumorigenic factor that promotes GBM proliferation, invasion, angiogenesis, and immunosuppression.109 Our results therefore consolidate PARP7 as a new target for activating anti-tumor immunity in glioblastoma.
