The basal ganglia and the epilepsies
Authors: Willian Lazarini‐Lopes, Logan Perry, Patrick A Forcelli
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractThe basal ganglia are an evolutionarily conserved neural circuit best known for the selection and sequencing of voluntary movement. Increasing evidence, however, indicates that these structures also play an important role in regulating pathological brain states, including seizures. Early pharmacological and lesion studies demonstrated that manipulation of basal ganglia output nuclei, particularly the substantia nigra pars reticulata, profoundly alters seizure susceptibility across multiple experimental models. Subsequent work expanded this view, revealing that several basal ganglia structures, including the striatum, subthalamic nucleus, globus pallidus, and superior colliculus, participate in a distributed network capable of modulating seizure initiation, propagation, and generalization. Modern systems neuroscience approaches have begun to define the specific cell types and projection‐defined pathways through which these effects are mediated. Together, these findings support the concept that the basal ganglia function as a “seizure‐gating” network that interacts with thalamocortical and brainstem arousal systems to regulate cortical synchronization. Understanding how these circuits influence seizure dynamics may enable new therapeutic strategies that strengthen endogenous seizure‐suppressing networks rather than targeting the seizure focus alone.Plain Language SummaryThe basal ganglia are a group of deep brain structures best known for helping to start and stop movements. INTRODUCTION

The architecture of the basal ganglia has been conserved through at least 560 million years of vertebrate evolution. While best known for the gating of voluntary movement, 1 in higher‐order vertebrates, this network contributes to a much broader range of functions, including procedural learning, emotion, and language processing, among other modalities of cognition. 2 The basal ganglia facilitate the computation of these diverse tasks using a generalized flow of information, acting together as an integration and broadcast hub between the brainstem, thalamus, and cortex. 3 In concert with these other regions, the basal ganglia form a powerful circuit loop for transforming, sequencing, and selecting streams of motor and non‐motor information. These same circuit properties that enable the basal ganglia to gate behavior also position the network to regulate pathological brain states, including the initiation, propagation, and termination of seizures. Indeed, experimental work beginning with foundational studies by Karen Gale and colleagues in the early 1980s demonstrated that the basal ganglia can potently modulate seizure activity. 4 , 5 However, despite nearly half a century of accumulating experimental evidence, the contributions of the basal ganglia to epilepsy remain underappreciated. Here, we will first review the architecture of the basal ganglia network, and then the evidence supporting a role for basal ganglia nuclei in the control of seizures.

THE BASAL GANGLIA CLASSICAL ANATOMY AND FUNCTIONS

Information enters the basal ganglia via projections to the striatum (comprising the caudate nucleus, putamen, and nucleus accumbens) and to the subthalamic nucleus (STN). In the standard model of basal ganglia function, input projections to the striatum from cortex and thalamus can be mapped to functionally specialized anatomical regions (but see 6 , 7 ) and behavioral functions. The major outputs of the basal ganglia are mediated through inhibitory (GABAergic) efferent pathways from the internal segment of the globus pallidus (GPi; entopeduncular nucleus in rats) and the substantia nigra pars reticulata (SNr). Output projections from the GPi and SNr tonically inhibit several thalamic nuclei and select brainstem nuclei, including the superior colliculus (SC) and pedunculopontine nucleus (PPN). Anatomically adjacent to the SNr, the substantia nigra pars compacta (SNc) integrates network state and modulates network activity through dopaminergic projections. Projections from the SNc to the striatum are of particular importance, tempering the baseline sensitivity to and coordinating the processing of input signals. 8 Together, these five nuclei, striatum, STN, GPi, SNr, and SNc, as well as the external segment of the globus pallidus (GPe) form the primary nodes of the basal ganglia network (see Figure 1).

These regions are connected through three classical pathways, the so‐called direct, indirect, and hyperdirect paths, which are best characterized for their effects on movement initiation. 9 , 10 , 11 , 12 In this view, strong inhibition from the GPi and SNr culminates in a “no‐go” or “stop” signal by inhibiting the thalamus. Conversely, transient pauses in inhibition from these output regions result in a “go” or “move” signal. More recent studies have added complexity to this view through the identification of a striatopallidal “go” pathway, parallel striasomal pathways controlling dopamine neurons, and arkypallidal neurons in the GPe that project to striatum. 13 , 14 , 15 Moreover, growing data during behavior suggest that both direct and indirect pathways display concurrent activation during movement, 16 and that the population level activity of these pathways encodes behaviors in a manner more complex than “go or no‐go”. 17

Starting in the striatum, glutamatergic afferents from the cortex, thalamus, and brainstem nuclei, along with dopaminergic SNc projections, synapse on GABAergic spiny projection neurons (SPNs). 18 , 19 , 20 , 21 Striatal SPNs express one of several dopamine receptor subtypes, but can be broadly characterized as D1‐expressing or D2‐expressing neurons. D1‐expressing neurons comprise the direct pathway, and D2‐expressing neurons comprise the indirect pathway. 22 D1 SPNs project directly to the GPi and SNr. Excitation of these D1 SPNs results in reduced inhibitory outputs from the GPi and SNr, a “move” signal. 23 D2 SPNs synapse in the GPe. 21 Neurons in the GPe are tonically active and GABAergic, therefore inverting the inhibitory output of D2 SPNs. 23 These GPe neurons synapse in the STN, the only glutamatergic region in the basal ganglia. Finally, STN glutamatergic neurons synapse in the GPi and SNr, completing the indirect pathway. 21 Excitation of D2 SPNs in the striatum thus inhibits GPe projections to the STN, disinhibiting the STN and increasing glutamatergic drive to the GPi and SNr, resulting in a “stop” signal with respect to movement. 23 Similarly, the hyperdirect pathway is comprised of excitatory inputs from cortex, thalamus, and brainstem to the STN; activation of these inputs increases STN activity, providing another “stop” signal. 12

Balance of these pathways enables intentional movements while simultaneously disabling unintentional movement. Clinical examples of basal ganglia pathway imbalance are most apparent in the hypokinesis of Parkinsonism and hyperkinesis of Huntington's disease. In Parkinsonism, loss of SNc dopaminergic neurons shifts the striatal output toward activation of the indirect pathway, classically presenting with a shuffling, rigid gait. Conversely, the preferential loss of D2 SPNs in the early stages of Huntington's disease swings the network toward direct pathway activation, causing the presentation of chorea. 24 Though these simplifications disregard other basal ganglia connections, loop computations, and intranetwork oscillations, they nonetheless offer clinically relevant intuition into the computations of this circuit.

Thalamic relay nuclei serve as the primary conduit of cortical bound information processed in the basal ganglia. 21 The inhibitory projections from the GPi and SNr target multiple thalamic nuclei, including ventral anterior (VA), ventrolateral (VL), mediodorsal (MD), and intralaminar nuclei, with each nucleus participating in functionally disparate network loops. 25 Motor outputs are routed through the VA/VL nuclei to premotor and primary motor cortices, while associative and limbic loops are routed through MD and intralaminar nuclei to prefrontal and cingulate areas. 25 At baseline, tonic GPi and SNr inhibition dampens thalamocortical excitability. 21 Task‐driven disinhibition of these projections increases thalamic throughput, facilitating the selection, sequencing, and reinforcement of motor and cognitive programs. 26 In addition to gating motor output, thalamic neurons integrate coordination feedback from the cerebellum and attentive selection from cortical inputs, amplifying, synchronizing, and reshaping signals before broadcasting to motor cortex. 27

Bypassing the thalamus and motor cortex entirely, major outputs of the basal ganglia target the superior colliculus and the PPN in the brainstem. 28 These targets, which may be considered part of an extended basal ganglia‐brainstem network, provide a direct route for the basal ganglia to influence arousal. Projections to the superior colliculus shape orienting and attentive actions. In this pathway, inhibitory efferents from the SNr innervate the intermediate and deep layers of the superior colliculus, regions critical for generating eye, head, and body orienting movements. 29 , 30 , 31 As with thalamic targets, tonic SNr inhibition suppresses collicular motor programs at baseline, while transient disinhibition permits the rapid release of contextually appropriate responses, most apparently expressed as saccadic eye movements. 32 , 33 , 34 In the rostral tegmental pons, the PPN links basal ganglia output to arousal, postural control, and gait pattern generation. 35 Inhibitory projections from both the GPi and SNr converge on the PPN, modulating its cholinergic, glutamatergic, and GABAergic neuronal populations to support these diverse functions. 36 Through these connections, the basal ganglia influence the excitability of the mesencephalic locomotor region and regulate transitions between motor states, including rest and graded locomotion. 37 Taken together, these brainstem projections underscore the basal ganglia's role as a fundamental motor selection mechanism, one that long predates the expansion of neocortical motor control.

Beyond the control of movement, the extended basal ganglia act as a critical regulator of behavioral and electrographic arousal states. For example, activation of the superior colliculus evokes species‐typical approach and avoidance responses and desynchronizes the cortex. 29 , 38 , 39 , 40 The PPN, a component of the brainstem reticular formation, exhibits strong modulation by sleep/wake status and has ascending projections that, as with the superior colliculus, potently modulate cortical synchronization. 41 , 42 Thus the extended basal ganglia are functionally positioned as regulators of synchronization and arousal—properties that are directly relevant to the pathological synchronization observed in epilepsy.

THE BASAL GANGLIA BEYOND MOVEMENT CONTROL: EARLY INSIGHTS OF BASAL GANGLIA INVOLVEMENT IN SEIZURE REGULATION

John Hughlings Jackson, in 1867, 43 recognized the striatum as, “a nervous organ that stands as a governing link betwixt the cerebral hemisphere and a particular muscular region” and that while “the [cerebral] hemisphere is the chief seat of the mind…through the corpus striatum the rest of the nervous system acts on the limbs.” Jackson hypothesized that the striatum must be involved in the paroxysmal motor manifestations of epilepsy, and he was prescient, as always. Nearly a century later, Walker and colleagues demonstrated robust recruitment of basal ganglia nuclei during evoked seizures in macaques. 44 , 45 , 46 Similar observations were subsequently reported in patients by Spiegel and colleagues, 47 who went on to test unilateral pallidotomy in five cases, observing improvement of seizures in three of the five cases. 48 Shortly thereafter, Jinnai and colleagues reported that bilateral lesions to Forel's field H, which contains axons of the pallidothalamic tract, improved generalized tonic–clonic seizures in 15 of 19 cases. 49 While these early studies viewed the basal ganglia primarily as a pathway for seizure propagation, work just over the horizon would reveal a different role: the basal ganglia may instead function as a gate regulating seizure expression.

THE SUBSTANTIA NIGRA AND EPILEPSY—A BIT OF HISTORY

In 1980, Karen Galea and Michael Iadarola reported that following treatment with vigabatrin elevation of nerve terminal pools of GABA in the substantia nigra correlated closely with the onset and time course of anticonvulsant activity against maximal electroshock seizures in rats. 5 They went on to demonstrate that focal infusion of GABAA receptor agonist in the substantia nigra, but not adjacent regions, robustly protected against maximal electroshock seizures. 4 This was confirmed in the amygdala kindling model by McNamara and colleagues, whereas Garant and Gale reported that lesions prevented seizures evoked by bicuculline and maximal electroshock. 50 , 51 Over a relatively brief period following the initial observations from Gale, the impact of nigral inhibition was tested in a wide range of seizures, including amygdala kindling, 51 , 52 audiogenic seizures, 53 ethanol withdrawal seizures, 54 kainic acid‐induced status epilepticus, 55 and several models of absence seizures. 56 The first discussion of the substantia nigra as a “gate” came in a 1985 review by Gale, who said, “We therefore propose that nigral outputs are capable of facilitating seizure propagation and can function as a gating mechanism for the generalization of convulsive activity.” 57 Highlighting the rapid progress, Antoine Depaulis and colleagues reviewed the evidence for the “nigral inhibitory system” in 1994, with over 50 publications in a little more than a decade. 58 An overview of the basal ganglia circuitry and epilepsy is shown in Figure 2.

NICO AND THE SUBSTANTIA NIGRA

Nico Moshéb and colleagues brought the nigra as a target into focus as a developmental regulator of seizures. In 1984, they reported that although the SNr is metabolically active in pubescent and adult animals during kainic acid‐induced seizures, it is not recruited in 15‐ to 18‐day‐old pups. 59 Shortly thereafter, they demonstrated that intranigral muscimol as well as intranigral THIP robustly suppresses seizures in adult animals but paradoxically facilitated them in immature rats. 60 , 61 These findings led them to propose that “the altered responsivity of the GABAergic nigral system in immature rats may be responsible in part for the increased seizure susceptibility of the developing central nervous system.” 62 Consistent with this idea, they demonstrated reduced GABAA receptor binding sites in the immature, as compared to adult rat SNr, 63 and later demonstrated that GABAB receptors in the nigra mediate the anticonvulsant effect of vigabatrin in developing animals. 64 , 65 , 66

Nico's group also identified striking regional differences in the SNr. Inhibition of the anterior‐medial SNr robustly suppresses seizures, whereas inhibition of the posterior‐lateral SNr produced proconvulsant effects. 67 , 68 Classic tracing studies demonstrate that projections from the SNr to the SC originate in the rostral two‐thirds of the SNr, with greater density of labeled neurons in the ventral SNr. 69 More recent retrograde viral tracing studies indicate that the anterior SNr projects robustly to the SC, whereas the posterior SNr projects to hindbrain regions including the dorsal raphe, medullary reticular formation, and the pontine reticular formation. 70 While several groups have reported seizure‐associated topography in the SNr in rodents, 67 , 68 , 71 , 72 there are well‐established differences in both functional and anatomical topography of the SNr between rodents and the primate brain. For example, we have shown in macaques that focal muscimol infusion produces site‐specific motor abnormalities (arm vs. leg vs. head dyskinesias); however, a similar topography has not been observed in rodents. 31 , 73 Moreover, while SNr output neurons in the rat are highly collateralized (e.g., a neuron that projects to thalamus may also target SC and PPN), these pathways are anatomically segregated in the primate brain, 74 , 75 , 76 , 77 , 78 which may lead to different functional outcomes associated with nigral activation or inhibition. 79

This functional organization is further shaped by sex‐specific developmental trajectories. 80 , 81 Velíšková and colleagues demonstrated that while intra‐nigral muscimol is proconvulsant in 15‐day‐old male rats, it had little effect in 15‐day‐old female rats. In older males, the SNr segregates into a proconvulsant (posterior) and an anticonvulsant (anterior) zone. By contrast, in females, the entire SNr appears to be anticonvulsant. 80 , 82 , 83 These differences likely reflect sex‐, age‐, and subregion‐dependent expression patterns of GABAA receptor subunits, 84 and depend on a postnatal testosterone surge. 80 At the physiological level, Galanopoulou and colleagues demonstrated sex‐specific differences in GABA‐mediated signaling within the SNr, including differences in tonic GABA currents and receptor subunit expression, 85 divergent inhibitory postsynaptic current kinetics, 86 and earlier maturation of GABAergic transmission from depolarizing to hyperpolarizing in females than in males. 87 This latter finding may reflect sex‐specific expression of the potassium‐chloride cotransporter KCC2. 87 , 88

FROM THE SUBSTANTIA NIGRA TO BROADER BASAL GANGLIA TARGETS

Despite being an “output nucleus” of the basal ganglia, the substantia nigra provided the conceptual entry point for understanding the role of the basal ganglia in epilepsy. While the SNr remains the best‐studied node within this network, subsequent work has demonstrated that multiple extended basal ganglia structures, including the striatum, globus pallidus, superior colliculus, and subthalamic nucleus, also participate in the regulation of seizure activity. Quite remarkably, modulation of extended basal ganglia has consistently produced multipotent effects across a range of seizure types. In the sections below, we first outline the historical perspective and then highlight some more recent findings and future directions.

STRIATUM: THE UPSTREAM REGULATOR OF SUBSTANTIA NIGRA ACTIVITY

As the primary input structure of the basal ganglia, the striatum integrates glutamatergic projections from cortex and thalamus with dopaminergic modulation from the substantia nigra pars compacta. This anatomical position places the striatum in a strategic position to influence basal ganglia output and, consequently, the propagation of seizure activity. In line with this anatomy, several early pharmacological studies demonstrated that manipulations of striatal neurotransmission can profoundly alter seizure susceptibility.

One of the first indications that the striatum can participate in seizure control came from studies showing that intra‐striatal microinjection of N‐methyl‐D‐aspartate (NMDA) suppressed behavioral and electrographic limbic seizures in fully kindled rats. 89 Similarly, bilateral intrastriatal injections of the GABA antagonist bicuculline conferred protection against seizures triggered by systemic pilocarpine and kainic acid. 90 This effect was notable because NMDA receptor activation or GABA receptor blockade are typically pro‐convulsant in most brain regions, suggesting that excitation of striatal neurons may engage downstream inhibitory basal ganglia circuits that counteract seizure propagation.

Striatal dopamine may also contribute to seizure regulation. Supporting this, apomorphine, a D1 and D2 agonist, when injected into the striatum, but not in the neocortex, suppressed limbic seizures induced by pilocarpine. This effect was recapitulated with a D2‐selective agonist, but not with a selective D1 agonist. Moreover, the apomorphine antiseizure effect was blocked by haloperidol, a D2 antagonist. 91 These data implicated a differential role for the striatal direct (D1) and indirect (D2) pathways.

Additional evidence for striatal involvement in seizure regulation comes from genetic models of absence epilepsy. In Genetic Absence Epilepsy Rats from Strasbourg (GAERS), dopaminergic modulation of seizure activity is anatomically specific within the striatum. Microinjections of dopaminergic agents into the dorsal striatum produce mixed motor and electrographic effects, whereas injections targeting the nucleus accumbens selectively alter spike‐and‐wave discharges (SWDs). 92 Activation of either D1 or D2 receptors suppresses SWDs, while antagonism of these receptors increases their occurrence. Notably, combined low‐dose activation of both receptor classes produces additive suppression of SWDs, indicating cooperative dopaminergic control of absence seizure activity within striatal circuits. 92

SUBTHALAMIC NUCLEUS AND GLOBUS PALLIDUS

Evidence from genetic models of absence epilepsy also implicates the STN in seizure regulation. The STN provides a major source of glutamatergic excitation to the SNr and thus occupies a key position for modulating basal ganglia output. From a “substantia nigra‐centric” view of basal ganglia seizure control, decreasing activity in the STN or increasing activity in the GPe would be expected to suppress seizures. Consistent with this role, pharmacological inhibition of the STN suppresses seizure activity in several models. In GAERS, intra‐STN administration of GABA agonists reduces SWDs, likely by silencing the excitatory STN–SNr pathway. 93 Similarly, lesion studies demonstrate that bilateral, but not unilateral, ablation of the STN significantly reduces the duration of SWDs in GAERS, although the overall frequency of discharges is unchanged. 94 With respect to limbic seizures, microinjection of a GABAA receptor agonist into the STN reduced motor seizures triggered by amygdala kindling, 95 flurothyl, 96 and suppressed cluster seizures triggered by bicuculline infusion into the “area tempestas” located in the anterior piriform cortex. 97 Given the utility of deep brain stimulation targeted to the STN in Parkinson's disease, it is no surprise that electrical stimulation of the STN has also been deployed in models of epilepsy. Electrical stimulation of the STN produces frequency‐dependent suppression of flurothyl‐evoked seizures, 98 prevents the secondary generalization of kainic acid‐evoked seizures, 99 but does not protect against maximal electroshock seizures. 100 Interestingly, vigabatrin injected into the STN also protects against seizures. 101 , 102 In humans, while the cohorts are small (3–7 patients), deep brain stimulation in the STN has been reported to: (1) reduce interictal spikes and high‐frequency oscillations in the motor cortex, 103 (2) reduce seizures in drug‐resistant juvenile myoclonic epilepsy, 104 (3) reduce seizures in patients with central localization, 105 and (4) reduce seizures with focal onset seizures. 106 Consistent with this, STN‐targeted DBS suppresses seizures in the cortical penicillin model in non‐human primates. 107

Modulation of the pallidum, including the internal (GPi/entopeduncular nucleus) and external (GPe) segments, has been among the least examined extended basal ganglia targets in epilepsy. Focal activation of either the GPi or the ventral pallidum suppresses absence seizures in the GAERS model. 108 However, effects against both limbic seizures and maximal electroshock seizures have been inconsistent, with some studies reporting protection and others reporting no effect. 97 , 109 , 110 , 111 , 112 By contrast, the low‐frequency electrical stimulation of the GPe reduces amygdala‐kindled, maximal electroshock, and pentylenetetrazole‐evoked seizures, 113 and the GPe is robustly engaged by seizures in primate models. 46 , 114

EXTENDED BASAL GANGLIA TARGETS: SUPERIOR COLLICULUS AND PEDUNCULOPONTINE NUCLEUS

We refer to the superior colliculus and PPN as components of an extended basal ganglia network because both structures receive output from, and send input back to, basal ganglia nuclei. Through these reciprocal connections, activity in the basal ganglia can influence brainstem structures that regulate behavioral and electrographic arousal, while brainstem activity can in turn modulate basal ganglia output. In this way, these circuits function analogously to dopaminergic modulation within the basal ganglia, shaping the flow of information through basal ganglia loops and influencing the gating of cortical activity. Because both the superior colliculus and PPN have ascending projections capable of altering cortical state and synchronization, their integration into basal ganglia circuitry provides a pathway through which the basal ganglia may regulate large‐scale cortical dynamics.

Aside from the substantia nigra, the superior colliculus is among the most extensively studied nodes within basal ganglia‐brainstem circuits implicated in seizure control. Early pharmacological studies demonstrated that activation of neurons in the deep and intermediate layers of the superior colliculus (DLSC) suppressed seizures. 115 , 116 , 117 Similarly, as described above, pharmacological silencing of activity in the SNr, the major inhibitory input to the DLSC, suppresses seizures, leading to the proposal that the DLSC serves as a key downstream mediator of nigral seizure control. Consistent with this model, lesions of the DLSC abolish the anticonvulsant effects produced by SNr inhibition. 118

The seizure suppressive effects of superior colliculus activation extend across a range of models. Focal pharmacological activation of the superior colliculus protects against clonic seizures 119 and against absence‐like discharges in several rat models. 117 , 120 , 121 The superior colliculus has also been extensively implicated in audiogenic seizure (AGS) circuits, with convergent evidence from two independently developed genetic models: the genetically epilepsy prone rat (GEPR), studied extensively by Carl Faingold, Ron Browning, Phil Jobe, and colleagues, and the Wistar audiogenic rat (WAR), studied extensively by Norberto Garcia‐Cairasco and colleagues. Neurons within the DLSC show increased firing during AGS, 122 , 123 ablation of the SC reduces AGS, 124 and precollicular transections leave intact the ability to generate AGS, suggesting that the circuitry required for AGS expression is confined to the brainstem. 125 Blockade of NMDA receptors reduces AGS, suggesting an essential role for glutamatergic drive in the SC. 126 , 127 Interestingly, muscimol in the SC reduces AGS, an effect that is somewhat paradoxical in light of the broader seizure‐suppressive effects of this structure. 71 , 128 GABA antagonism within the SC has produced conflicting results on AGS, while some studies have reported facilitation, 128 others have reported no effect, 71 and others have reported a reduction. 129 This may differ based on both the model (GEPR‐3, GEPR‐9, and WAR), the phase of the AGS (e.g., wild running, clonic seizures, and tonic seizures), and the site within the SC. Our own work has demonstrated that optogenetic activation of the SC, or inhibition of the nigrotectal pathway, suppresses seizures in the GEPR‐3 strain. 130 , 131 Despite this convergence at the circuit and behavioral level, the two models may not converge structurally: while GEPR‐3 rats display increased SC volumes, WAR rats do not. 132

Anatomical and functional mapping studies from Peter Redgrave and colleagues identified a potent anticonvulsant region encompassing aspects of the lateral DLSC, which mediates the effects of nigral inhibition, can trigger potent cortical desynchronization, and sends robust descending projections to the reticular formation. 40 , 121 , 133 , 134 , 135 , 136 , 137 Thus, the DLSC is well positioned anatomically to influence seizure networks through both ascending and descending projections.

Descending outputs from the DLSC target the pontine reticular formation, including the PPN. 137 , 138 Pharmacological studies examining the PPN have produced mixed results, likely reflecting the complex local microcircuitry and the differing contributions of discrete neuronal populations, including cholinergic, glutamatergic, and GABAergic cells. For example, while blockade of NMDA receptors in the PPN reduces pilocarpine‐evoked status epilepticus, 139 GABA antagonist infusion protected against pentylenetetrazol‐evoked seizures 140 and SWDs in the GAERS model 141 and GABA agonist infusion was reported to be without effect. 142 The mechanisms underlying PPN control of seizures remain uncertain, but are likely mediated through a combination of ascending cholinergic and glutamatergic projections as well as descending projections to the nucleus reticularis pontis oralis, a critical site in the generation of tonic seizure activity. 141 , 143 , 144 , 145 , 146 , 147 , 148

MODERN SYSTEMS NEUROSCIENCE AND BASAL GANGLIA SEIZURE CONTROL

Although early studies established a clear role for basal ganglia structures in seizure control, the circuit mechanisms underlying these effects remained poorly defined. The development of modern systems neuroscience tools now allows interrogation of specific cell types and pathways within the basal ganglia, providing new insight into how these circuits regulate seizure activity.

Recent studies from our lab and from others have uncovered new dynamics with respect to the SNr and seizure control. We recently reported that during SWDs in the WAG/Rij model of absence seizures, approximately half of SNr neurons increase firing while extracellular GABA levels decrease, indicating reduced nigral inhibition. Restoring inhibition through optogenetic stimulation reduces seizure burden, supporting a “failed inhibition” model of nigral seizure control. 149 We have also reported the impact of projection‐specific optogenetic silencing of nigral output pathways, which directly demonstrated that nigrotectal projections to the deep layers of the superior colliculus exert broad anticonvulsant effects, whereas nigrotegmental projections to the PPN selectively modulate absence seizures and may exacerbate other seizure types. 131 A recent study from Jiang and colleagues demonstrated that low‐frequency stimulation of the anterior SNr, but not the posterior SNr, suppresses seizures across multiple models, an effect associated with increased GABA release from disinhibitory inputs. 150 A study from the same group demonstrated that a disinhibitory SNr–parafascicular thalamic circuit can amplify seizures in temporal lobe epilepsy through suppression of thalamic GABAergic neurons by SNr parvalbumin‐positive cells. 151 Providing direct evidence for an STN‐SNr role in the control of seizures, Xue and colleagues reported that deep brain stimulation in the STN was effective at controlling cortically evoked seizures and that selective inhibition of the projection from the STN to the SNr was both necessary and sufficient for the effect. 152

The striatum has also seen renewed interest as a target. We recently reported that optogenetic manipulation of the dorsal striatum demonstrates bidirectional control across absence, temporal lobe epilepsy, and status epilepticus models: activation suppresses seizures, whereas inhibition facilitates seizure expression. 153 Recent work from Jaideep Kapur's lab has suggested that frontal lobe seizures preferentially activate D2 receptor‐expressing neurons of the indirect pathway, and that pharmacological activation of D2 receptors exerts anticonvulsant effects against frontal lobe seizures. 154 , 155

We have also further characterized the superior colliculus as a broad‐spectrum seizure control node. Optogenetic activation of the DLSC suppresses seizures across diverse networks, including forebrain, brainstem, and thalamocortical circuits. 130 , 156 , 157 Suggesting these effects are persistent, chronic optogenetic activation of the DLSC reduced spontaneous seizures in the rat pilocarpine model. 157 Intriguingly, and consistent with the notion that failure of basal ganglia protective mechanisms may result in a seizure‐prone state, neuronal activity in the DLSC decreases seconds before seizure onset, and closed‐loop stimulation triggered by seizure detection is more effective than open‐loop stimulation in terminating absence seizures. 156

Beyond the role of these circuits in seizure control, the extended basal ganglia interact with brainstem arousal systems. In particular, the PPN plays an important role in ictal cortical slow‐wave activity, 158 , 159 and suppression of PPN neuronal activity may be secondary to loss of excitatory input during seizures. 160 Finally, a recent computational study suggests that ascending PPN input to the cortex also may play a role in gating absence seizures, 161 a finding that is consistent with unpublished data from our laboratory.

While early clinical observations demonstrated engagement of basal ganglia nuclei during seizures in patients, the mechanistic evidence for the role of basal ganglia in seizures has been largely derived from rodent studies. Two recent studies have extended this to non‐human primates. First, Connolly and colleagues demonstrated that temporal lobe seizures in primates entrain activity in the SNr in a pattern that depends on seizure onset and offset dynamics. 162 This raises the possibility that some seizure types may be more sensitive to basal ganglia‐targeted manipulations.

Secondly, we recently documented a well‐defined region highly susceptible to bicuculline‐induced seizures in the piriform cortex in macaques. This site, just posterior to the junction of the frontal and temporal lobes, may represent a functional homolog of the rodent area tempestas originally described by Karen Gale. 163 We further demonstrated that seizures evoked from this region in macaques are potently suppressed by focal inhibition of the SNr, 164 in line with prior rodent studies, 165 and providing an important cross‐species validation of the circuit. Complementary human lesion network analyses show that networks associated with seizures and parkinsonism are topographically opposed, with the substantia nigra exhibiting opposite connectivity relationships with each disorder, and that lesions associated with epilepsy were negatively connected to the substantia nigra, suggesting that the functional topography of this network with respect to seizures may also be preserved in the human brain. 166 , 167

ONE CIRCUIT, MANY EFFECTS

One of the consistently most intriguing aspects of the basal ganglia and seizures is the clear, multipotent effect modulating this circuit has across a wide range of seizure types. As discussed above, each node of this circuit has demonstrated effects against focal onset seizures, generalized onset seizures, absence seizures, motor seizures, seizures originating in limbic structures, brainstem nuclei, and thalamocortical circuits. How can one network have such diverse impacts? Several (non‐exclusive) hypotheses may explain these effects. First, output nuclei of the basal ganglia send direct projections to the thalamus, providing a feasible target for modulating activity at a node that is engaged during the propagation and generalization of virtually all seizure types, regardless of their cortical or subcortical site of origin. Indeed, neuroimaging data in patients with temporal lobe epilepsy (TLE) suggest abnormal functional connectivity within basal ganglia‐thalamus loops. 168 , 169 Second, the basal ganglia converge on key brainstem structures that are critical regulators of cortical arousal and synchronization, including the SC, PPN, and NRPO. Because seizure activity is characterized by pathological synchronization, engaging these desynchronizing inputs to cortical and subcortical regions is expected to broadly desynchronize, and therefore oppose, seizure activity. Third, beyond these shared targets, the outputs of the extended basal ganglia directly target diverse nodes engaged by seizures, such that specific outputs may directly gate specific seizure types. For example, projections to brainstem nuclei are likely critical for gating tonic–clonic seizure expression, whereas thalamic projections may preferentially gate absence seizure expression – consistent with the projection‐specific effects of nigral output silencing described above.

CONCLUSION

The basal ganglia do much more than motor output. Here, we have described evidence, accumulated over more than four decades, that the basal ganglia should be viewed not simply as motor control structures, but as distributed regulators of network excitability capable of gating seizure expression. Multiple nodes of the extended basal ganglia, including the striatum, SNr, the STN, the globus pallidus, and the superior colliculus, can powerfully modulate seizures across diverse models, seizure types, and onset zones. This circuit integrates cortical and thalamic input and, through an extended basal ganglia‐brainstem‐thalamocortical network, is ideally positioned to influence cortical synchronization, arousal, and seizures. While classic approaches using focal pharmacology defined the organization of these circuits, newer approaches have allowed for cell‐type‐specific and projection‐pathway‐defined modulation to directly test long‐standing hypotheses and improve seizure control. These findings position the basal ganglia as a promising target for neuromodulation in epilepsy, where strengthening endogenous “seizure‐stopping” networks may prove more effective than targeting the seizure focus alone.
