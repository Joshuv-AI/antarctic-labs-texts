Exploring the challenges and impact of work-related shoulder disorders on North American firefighters: an interpretive description
Authors: Temitope A Osifeso, Joy C MacDermid, Dorala Berinyuy, Mike Szekeres, Pulak Parikh, Kenneth J Faber
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractRationaleFirefighters (FFs) are exposed to physically demanding tasks that increase their risk of work-related shoulder disorders (WSDs). Despite the critical nature of their work, qualitative research remains limited in exploring the impact of WSDs on their well-being, as well as the challenges they encounter within the workplace and healthcare system.ObjectivesTo explore: (1) The impact of WSDs on the well-being and job performance of FFs, (2) The occupational health challenges encountered by FFs with WSDs during recovery.MethodsA total of 14 FFs (Males = 9, Females = 5) between 26 and 63 years old experiencing WSDs were purposefully sampled by sex and geographical location. Data was collected through virtual one-on-one semi-structured interviews and transcribed verbatim. Interpretive description methodology was used, and data was analyzed using thematic analysis.FindingsFirefighters with WSDs indicated that pain and mobility limitations significantly impaired their ability to perform critical job tasks such as lifting, reaching, and administering life-saving interventions. In their personal lives, WSDs disrupt routine activities, fitness, and family interactions. Sleep deprivation due to pain and shift work further exacerbates physical and cognitive strain, impacting their job performance. Occupationally, FFs reported lost work time, financial strain, and career setbacks due to modified duties, often leading to feelings of guilt, isolation, and diminished professional identity. Additionally, operational challenges related to poorly designed firefighting equipment and protective gear were reported to increase the risk of re-injury, particularly for female FFs and those with smaller statures. Lastly, healthcare challenges such as lack of tailored health care protocols and suboptimal intervention programs were reported.ConclusionThis study underscores the complex interplay of physical, occupational, and psychosocial factors in shaping firefighters’ experiences with WSDs. This emphasizes the need for ergonomic improvements, better healthcare protocols, and psychosocial support to improve the well-being of FFs with WSDs.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12982-025-01131-2.Keywords: Firefighters, Shoulder disorders, Musculoskeletal disorders, Work health, Interpretive description

Introduction

Firefighters (FFs) are predicted to be injured every 8 min [1]. These injuries are due to the unpredictability of firefighting tasks, harsh environmental conditions, and the high physical demands associated with firefighting [2, 3]. Work-related musculoskeletal disorders (WRMSDs) including work-related shoulder disorders (WSDs) are the most common cause of morbidity among firefighters (FFs) during fireground and non-fireground operations [4–7]. Work-related shoulder disorders are the third most reported WRMSDs among FFs [8], often resulting from overexertion, victim rescue, heavy lifting, and awkward or non-ergonomic postures in confined spaces [2, 9].

For firefighters, whose roles require peak physical performance and teamwork, WSDs not only compromise their ability to perform life-saving tasks but also have far-reaching consequences on their personal lives, career sustainability, and psychological well-being [7, 10–12]. For example, a US study of 249 FFs by Phelp et al. [9] reported that FFs who reported work-related injuries such as WSDs indicated lower levels of respect from peers and superiors, fewer job rewards in the form of income, and limited opportunities for promotion compared to their counterparts with no work-related injuries. Another study by Seabury et al., [13] showed that firefighters recovering from WRSDs, including WSDs take nearly twice as long to return-to-work (RTW) compared to the average working population.

Likewise, FFs who sustain shoulder injuries often encounter considerable financial burdens due to extensive medical treatments and income loss during prolonged recovery [5, 9, 14]. Research in the U.S by Hanson et al. [15] highlighted that firefighter leaders incurred the highest expenses for shoulder-related medical care, averaging around $14,619 per claim. Such injuries frequently necessitate extended time away from active duty, leading to financial hardships for injured firefighters and operational strain on fire departments due to staffing shortages and increased overtime costs. Further, a systematic review in Canada by Nazari and colleagues showed that the prevalence of shoulder injuries was 23% among Canadian FFs [6] while another study between 2010 and 2014 showed a 12% prevalence of WSDs among US FFs [16]. Despite the prevalence, economic and cost burden of WSDs in this population, there is limited research exploring the impact of these injuries, particularly from the firefighters’ own perspectives.

Several quantitative studies have established that WRMSDs including WSDs experienced by FFs can have serious implications, such as reduced productivity, limitations at work, higher medical expenses, and lost time at work or absenteeism [4, 12, 16, 17]. However, qualitative studies exploring the depth of how FFs experience and interpret the impact of WSDs on their personal and professional lives are scarce. Further, there is limited qualitative evidence on the immediate or long-term occupational health challenges from the perspective of FFs. To bridge these gaps, the aim of this study was to explore: (1) How do WSDs impact the well-being and job performance of FFs, (2) What are the occupational health challenges encountered by FFs with WSDs.

Methods

Study design

Interpretive description (ID), a qualitative methodology designed for the investigation of clinical phenomena, was employed in this study [18]. This inductive approach was utilized because it is not tied to a strict philosophical theory but is practical and methodologically flexible, aiming to generate knowledge that informs professional practice and can address our objectives of the impact and challenges encountered by FFs with WSDs. Interpretive description enables clinical researchers to develop a better understanding of the subjective reality of a population of interest. Further, it acknowledges individual narratives while also recognizing the existence of shared realities [19]. These shared realities were synthesized to generate interpretive insights that provide comprehensive and contextualized interpretations capable of informing and advancing clinical practice. Additionally, ID is grounded in the constructivist paradigm, which is well-suited for investigating intricate, experience-based inquiries, particularly within applied healthcare contexts. This paradigm emphasizes the co-construction of knowledge, whereby researchers actively engage with participants to uncover the meanings and contextual intricacies essential for generating actionable insights.

Sampling, recruitment, and consent

Purposeful sampling was used to recruit North American career and volunteer FFs who work in different ranks, geographical areas, and types of communities (i.e., rural to large cities). Eligible participants were recruited between September 26th, 2023, and June 25th, 2024. Recruitment was conducted through multiple strategies, including advertisements distributed via firefighter organizations, union channels, professional networks (e.g., Canadian Association of Fire Chiefs [CAFC], FIREWELL), and social media platforms such as Facebook, X, and LinkedIn. Written and oral informed consent were also provided to participants before participation in the semi-structured interviews. To ensure anonymity, the personal factors of participants were not recorded and FFs were labeled in accordance with the position they were interviewed. Furthermore, deliberate efforts were made to ensure adequate representation of female FFs, as well as gender and ethnic minorities within the study. The sample size was continuously evaluated to assess data sufficiency, ensuring comprehensive coverage of the research objectives and capturing the full complexity of the phenomenon under investigation.

Data collection

Qualitative data was collected through semi-structured interviews conducted using Zoom, a password-protected video conferencing platform. Data was stored on a passworded encrypted Western University one drive of the principal investigator (JMD) after each interview. Verbal consent was obtained prior to each interview, which lasted approximately 45 min to 1 h. To foster co-construction of the phenomenon under investigation, participants were given the option to choose their preferred interview format, including online (Zoom), telephone, or in-person. The semi-structured interview guide (see Appendix 1) was developed based on the research team’s empirical expertise, contextual understanding of the objectives, and a review of the relevant literature ​ [20]​. The guide provided a structured framework for discussions while allowing the interviewer to explore emerging themes and probe for additional insights. The interviews were conducted and transcribed verbatim by an experienced qualitative researcher (TO) while adhering to the structured questions outlined in the interview guide. The interviewer employed prompts and follow-up questions as needed to clarify responses and explore emerging themes. All interviews were audio-recorded and subsequently transcribed verbatim. Reflective notes were documented following each interview to capture contextual observations and preliminary interpretations. The interview guide was iteratively refined throughout the data collection process as new insights emerged following interviews from participants. During data collection, revisions were also informed through collaborative team discussions with co-authors possessing extensive expertise in firefighter and musculoskeletal health research. Data collection followed an iterative approach, with each interview informing subsequent data collection to enhance depth and contextual understanding.

Data analysis

Data from semi-structured interviews were anonymized and imported into NVivo 14 software for organization and transparency. Analysis occurred concurrently with data collection, guided by ID methodology [19]. Thematic analysis and inductive coding were employed to identify, examine, and describe patterns or themes, adhering to Braun and Clarke’s six-stage framework: data immersion, generating initial codes, identifying themes, reviewing themes, defining and naming themes, and producing the report [21]. Reflexive journals and memos were used to critically examine researchers’ personal, professional, and ontological assumptions, ensuring rigor in the analytical process. The first author (TO) transcribed the data and was immersed in the transcripts through repeated readings to identify significant issues, potential themes, and patterns. A pilot open coding of one transcript was independently conducted by three co-authors (TO, DB, and JMD) to establish coding consistency. Discrepancies were resolved through team discussions, and the remaining 13 transcripts were independently coded by two co-authors (TO and DB). An iterative approach was adopted, with reflexive notes recorded during and after interviews to maintain analytical coherence. As the analysis progressed, interpretations evolved beyond the initial theoretical framework. For instance, our study set out to explore the impact of WSDs on career sustainability among FFs. Deeper analysis revealed nuanced insights into the systematic challenges encountered while trying to navigate health care including the sub-optimal healthcare interventions and the impact on their well-being and recovery.

Researcher positionality

As a black female doctoral candidate with a physiotherapy background and prior research experience with firefighters, my positionality is shaped by my intersectional identities, professional expertise, and commitment to improving the occupational health outcomes of FFs. My physiotherapy background provides a clinical lens to understand the biomedical and rehabilitation challenges of WSDs, while my previous work with FFs offers insights into their unique occupational demands, musculoskeletal health and recovery experiences.

I acknowledge the power dynamics as an outsider to the firefighter community and the potential influence of my sex and ethnicity on interactions with the predominantly male-dominated firefighter community. To navigate these dynamics, I employed reflexivity by memoing to document my bias and pre-understandings, while also building trust and rapport with my participants. My constructivist epistemological stance reinforces the co-creation of knowledge, ensuring that participants’ voices remain central to the research process. By critically acknowledging my positionality, my findings were both methodologically rigorous and deeply reflective of firefighters lived experiences rather than my interpretations alone.

Rigor and trustworthiness

To ensure the rigor and trustworthiness of this study, several strategies were employed, aligning with the principles of ID methodology [22]. First, credibility was established through prolonged engagement with participants, allowing for in-depth exploration of their experiences. Second, transparency in the decision-making process was achieved through a detailed audit trail, documenting all methodological decisions and analytical processes. Third, transferability was enhanced by providing rich, contextual descriptions of the findings, enabling readers to assess the applicability of the results to other settings. Fourth, analytical logic and reflexivity was ensured by the critical examination of our biases, assumptions, and preconceptions throughout the study. This was achieved by memo writing and iterative questioning to ensure that themes emerge from the data rather than being imposed by our own preunderstandings. Finally, triangulation was ensured through peer debriefing. This was done with senior colleagues with several years of experience and expertise in occupational and upper extremity health research reviewing the analysis to minimize researcher bias.

Results

A total of 17 firefighters were interviewed for this study, of which 14 firefighters were included in the analysis (see Table 1). Three imposters, despite passing eligibility screening, were excluded post-interview due to inconsistencies indicating they were not firefighters. Twelve interviews were conducted via Zoom and two by telephone. Participants primarily reported rotator cuff or labral tears. Majority of the participants were Caucasian (n = 13), with one ethnic and one gender minority. The reported years of service among participants ranged from 5 to 33 years. Twelve firefighters were full-time career firefighters in team lead roles, while two were a mix of frontline career and volunteer firefighters (see Table 1).

Major themes

The analysis of the data revealed themes that capture the multifaceted impacts, challenges and experiences of FFs with WSDs (See Fig. 1). Four themes emerged in relation to the impact of WSDs on the well-being and job performance of FFs. These include: (1) physical impact; (2) occupational impact, (3) and psychosocial impact. Two themes emerged describing the challenges of FFs with WSDs during recovery. They include (4) operational challenges, and (5) healthcare challenges.

Impact of WSDs among firefighters

Theme 1: Physical Impact

Pain and mobility limitations impacts personal and professional well-being

Firefighters described their pain as shooting, stabbing, persistent or intermittent depending on the nature or type of WSDs. Moreover, the pain experienced from these WSDs were noted to be sometimes severe that it might result in mobility restrictions, directly impacting their ability to perform their job-related tasks effectively. For instance, FF05 shared that “…it would be a very sharp stabbing pain, yes… If the pain was dull enough, then I had a pretty good range of motion. …you know, clearly if I overextended or again reached behind my back the pain would limit that action, The only thing that would have impacted the range of motion was the pain…” These WSDs or symptoms were said to sometimes compromise critical job functions such as administering lifesaving procedures for victims. A firefighter, FFP15, stated that, “When I went to do CPR, I couldn’t depress downwards. The pain was unbearable, and I realized I couldn’t even lift my arm.” Majority of FFs also reported the significant impact of mobility limitation in carrying out common routine firefighting tasks, FFP07 claimed that “…the things that were greatly affected were overhead movements so on the fire ground that means everything from something as simple as closing one of the doors on our apparatus because they open upward due to the hinges on the top. Like having to switch and reach with my other arm instead of “throwing a ladder” or raising a ladder to rescue a potential victim…”. Similarly, FF 12 added that, “Yeah, it (shoulder injury) made it very difficult to perform movements, you know, overhead movements, pulling ceilings and salvage and overhaul, things like that.”

In relation to their personal lives, majority of FFs claimed that they experienced severe shoulder pain and mobility restrictions due to WSDs while performing their daily activities or hobbies, outside of work or at home. Notably, FFs shared that regular routine activities like grooming, cleaning and cooking were often met with difficulty due to their WSDs. For example, FF09 reported that, “Even something as simple as pulling a shirt over my head became a painful ordeal.” FF15 also expressed that, “At home, I work out, I clean the house, I cook. I love to bake… even just stirring things, wasn’t good when it (shoulder pain) was really bad. So, I felt very limited. I was pretty upset about it” Another firefighter, FF03, stated that his shoulder injury significantly restricted family leisure or play time, “…my daughters were little, so I couldn’t necessarily pick them up like dads do. And so yeah, that kind of day-to-day playing with the kids was out of the question… (uncomfortable laugh)”. Another firefighter, FF06, added that: “…You know, I have three (young) kids and holding them is rough, especially on my left side, overtime it took a toll on me, and I wasn’t able to. So, … I put them down and I kind of force them to walk”.

Additionally, maintaining fitness is a critical goal for most FFs, but shoulder injuries were reported to frequently limit their ability to engage in major physical activities like strength training, high intensity interval training or team sports. These injuries were stated to often affect firefighters’ ability to carry out their routine fitness activities as they believed it might lead to a more chronic form of WSD. Hence, physical activities that were once simple and enjoyable were noted to have now become very challenging. This narrative was acknowledged by FFP07, “I would say, I love to go to the gym to stay fit for work and just for fun and my activities at the gym all had to change, and I had to modify every movement and even now, six months later, I still have some movements that I can’t quite get to, yet improving every day but kind of like it’s a long and slow process.” Similarly, this sentiment was echoed by another firefighter, FF11, “…I was having, you know, agitation like mood swings due to the (shoulder) injury and my break in like my normal routine in terms of working out”.

Sleep deprivation impacts job performance

Many FFs shared that sleep is an essential aspect of recovery from WSDs. They reported that proper sleep helps with pain regulation, but their regular long night shifts often disrupt their sleep patterns which may impede quicker recovery from the shoulder injury. Similarly, FFs claimed that their sleep routine can be disrupted from pain in their shoulder which may also result in delayed recovery. Therefore, FFs with WSDs who experience poor sleep routines either from shoulder pain or long night shifts reported having reduced physical capacity which compromised their ability to perform job-related tasks that require being fully alert or needed physical strength and endurance. For instance, FF09 mentioned, “…we run a good amount of our calls after midnight between the hours of midnight and 8:00am when we get off shift. So, sleep is a big part of it and you know, sleep and recovery go hand in hand. So, when you’re not getting that sleep your body’s not effectively recovering itself from the day…” FF17 also claimed that, “…guess at night generally it’s more difficult to be feeling ready and warmed up for an emergency situation, cause if it’s after 10:00 PM. We are responding from our beds, so the alarms wake us up and then we have to be ready to go and potentially use our shoulder in an intense way within a few minutes”.

Additionally, FFs mentioned that experiencing a shoulder injury often results in poor sleep routines which increases their pain sensitivity leading to poor emotional regulation and increased irritability. This was noted to affect interpersonal relationships within the team, potentially disrupting interpersonal communication between team members, which are vital for effective firefighting operations. FF13 shared that, “…I would say that it (shoulder pain) definitely impeded my ability to sleep because there was sort of no comfortable position as a side sleeper to sort of mitigate the pain. So, it actually was severe enough to wake me up …So for me, the lack of sleep just made me just generally more sensitive, the inabilities and the sort of loss of strength or even the appeared loss of strength I was concerned how that would cause my peers to perceive me.”

Theme 2: Occupational Impact

Lost work time impacts team dynamics, organizational and personal finances

Firefighters expressed major concerns relating to taking extended periods off work to recover from WSDs. They emphasized that other team members may become physically strained and fatigued possibly increasing their risk for WRMSDs including WSDs among the fewer team members. They stated that lost work time due to WSDs can affect team dynamics as this increases the workload on other team members affecting their overall job performance during firefighting tasks. FF17 shared that, “In each of those time periods (2020 and 2024), I had to take time off from full duties to recover. It meant not being able to perform my job as a firefighter…Sometimes we can allocate tasks according to ability, but when we can’t, …That definitely increases their workload (on other team members).”

Other firefighters further added that the absence of a firefighter due to a WSD may cause a shift in roles and responsibilities among other FFs because firefighting relies heavily on teamwork and defined roles. Hence, when a firefighter is limited by WSDs, others may need to take on different or additional roles which may affect team efficiency and performance, especially if the replacement is also recovering from WSDs thereby risking public safety. FF06 noted that “…doing a lot of the heavy lifting and heavy work, and especially at a catastrophic event, you’re in there working and you’re by yourself and you know where normally there’d be 8 to 10 people there, it’s just four… And now, not only do I have to take all the tools by myself, while with my group of three, because one person has to stay with the fire truck to pump water. The other three of us now have to carry all the tools and everything needed to fight that fire by ourselves up to that 10th floor…it’s been detrimental, and my shoulder pains were even worse…” Similarly, FF16, mentioned that “…The fewer hands, the heavier the work, the more work there is to do”. Absenteeism due to WSDs was also reported to impact firefighting organizations in terms of financial cost and burden. FF05 shared that, “…So, you know, on one hand, we have expenses that come out of our self-insured health plan fund or if it’s work related, it comes out of the workers compensation risk fund because the city of xxx is self-insured. We pay, you know, the city ultimately pays 100% for those injuries. But then on the backside of it, there’s also paying time and half per hour of whomever is riding in my spot because I can’t be there. So, there’s also that kind of hidden payroll cost that doesn’t get looked at often as when you start calculating the true cost of an (shoulder) injury.”

Additionally, some FFs were reported to face lost wages, specifically if they exhaust their paid leave and must take unpaid leave. This loss of income can be financially straining, particularly if the recovery period is extended. Firefighters claimed they often supplement their income with other side jobs or overtime shifts but experiencing WSDs can prevent them from taking on these extra shifts, resulting in a significant reduction in earnings. FF01 explained, “…So, it took me off the job for about six months and as a side note, most firefighters have side jobs, other forms of employment, you know. So, I stopped working my side jobs. It was a loss of income…”.

Modified duties impacts career trajectory

The need for modified duties among FFs with WSDs was stated to be seen as a professional setback for most active duty or frontline FFs. They shared that being on modified duties often means being temporarily removed from frontline tasks that involve physical exertion, limiting their exposure to more demanding and diverse situations. FFP07 stated that, “You’re 100% ready to come back to work (firefighting) and you’re pulled off the floor, so I got stationed downtown with day staff, and I ended up working Monday through Friday, 7:30 to 3:30…” This was reported to affect their ability to maintain and develop skills that are critical for advancement within the fire service. Firefighters expressed that experiencing a shoulder injury was a challenging ordeal as the return of an injured firefighter might require adjustments if their capabilities have changed due to the shoulder injury. FF08 remarked, “…the injury greatly affected my ability to do tasks that we are required to do, lifting and just various things that if I had to move my shoulder to do it, it hurts so you know, we tried to, I say we, but my supervisors tried to put me in roles that required less manual labor…”.

Some FFs revealed that being on modified duties might also give a perception that they are less capable, which could influence decisions about promotions or special assignments. This perception was noted to impact their reputation and the opportunities that are available in relation to their career progression relative to peers who are not on modified duties. FF11 shared that, “…I’m on modified duties right now and I had done it about a year ago too, so my role was to assist our training division with training new recruits that were just in the process of being trained as firefighters, as well as counting inventory, our medical supplies and then going forward with my modified duties. Now I’m instructing our firefighters on different things job wise…as well as preparing an inventory list for a new fire truck that’s about to go into service…”.

Theme 3: Psychosocial Impact

Psychological strain impacts personal and professional identity

Majority of FFs reported the significant psychological toll of their shoulder injuries which often lead to stress, anxiety, and a diminished sense of self-worth. FF06 reported that: “…It (shoulder injury) takes a major toll and then I can’t stop thinking about it. And then I constantly relive it because the other thing too is we’re playing with people’s lives, right? So, there’s people’s lives involved here, and it’s hit or miss, on one that shoulder injury might act up and there are a lot of days where it is fine, like there’s zero issues. But then there’s the time where I’m doing that extreme circumstance, and I have to have it on my left side (affected) just because of the way the accident is, I have no choice but to go in on my left side. I try to compensate with my right, but it doesn’t always work and mentally that just messes with me…”.

Other firefighters expressed concerns about a fear of re-injury, which can lead to anxiety about performing their duties. This fear can sometimes be debilitating and affect their confidence in both professional and personal settings. For example, FF17 shared, “Mentally I would say that at times I fear that when an intense situation comes that I might not be able to perform the task that’s asked of me because of my shoulder injury and the mental strain of that is sometimes difficult because I have a fear that it could result in someone else being hurt, maybe one of my colleagues if I couldn’t adequately perform my duties, or perhaps a member of the public not getting the help they need at the time they need because of it (shoulder injury)…”.

Further, FFs expressed reduced confidence in their physical abilities following their shoulder injury as they often identify strongly with their physical capabilities and their role in the fire service. An injury that limits these abilities were indicated to lead to a sense of “identity loss” and diminished self-esteem, due to the impact on their work identity or how they view themselves at work. FF13 noted that, “I think initially for the first while, you know there’s shame and there’s kind of embarrassment. And then there’s kind of like, you know, there’s a huge, actual emotional impact. I mean the sense of like what’s wrong with me? Why can’t I muscle through…” This sentiment was echoed by FF08 stating, “… it’s always in the back of my mind. Hey, if I fall again and land on the shoulder, or if I lift something the wrong way and my shoulder muscles are engaged. You know what? What if I tear something or compress that joint. Again, what’s going to happen? So it’s always in the back of my mind…” Additionally, some FFs even considered quitting their jobs due to a sense of perceived loss of professional purpose and identity. FFP 07 expressed that, “…being someone who is very physically active and who puts a lot of value in my own strength and ability to help others. I felt very much like I didn’t have that purpose anymore because that was all taken away from the physical aspect anyway, …. so, it was it was very, (uncomfortable pause) I would say it was near debilitating. I’ve never really thought about quitting my job as hard as I did after, after that (shoulder injury), if I’m being honest…”.

Social and professional isolation slows recovery

Shoulder injuries among FFs were reported to often lead to feelings of isolation, both socially and professionally. These injuries were claimed to prevent FFs from participating fully in team activities. Being on modified duties or away from their team due to injury were said to lead to feelings of isolation. Firefighters acknowledged being a close-knit community and being separated due to WSDs from their social and support network can increase feelings of loneliness and detachment. FF17 noted that, “…at times I feel I can’t participate as fully in things as I would like at work, maybe even in the social environment of the work, whether that’s when we’re at the station, you know, playing a game of basketball or even working out physically in the gym at work…”.

Shoulder injuries were noted to also lead to professional isolation, as FFs often felt disconnected from their teams or unable to participate fully in work activities. Firefighters reported identifying strongly with their active, physical roles, so when injured, they reported feeling as though they are not fully contributing to members of the team, FFP07 stated that, “… So, because I had that change in schedule at work, I was away from my crew, so it was very isolating… due to the nature of change, the time schedule, I was kind of secluded.”

Challenges of WSDs among firefighters

Theme 4: Operational Challenges

Equipment and gear-related challenges exacerbate shoulder (re)-injury

Firefighters reported that most designs of firefighting equipment and gear often do not accommodate their diverse needs, particularly among those with smaller stature experiencing WSDs. Notably, some female FFs claimed that most equipment or gear are not designed for their body type and the physically demanding nature of firefighting exacerbates these challenges especially among those with WSDs. FF17 explained, “I feel like most firefighting tools and equipment and gear are designed more for the stature of men and my body is not like that, that it sometimes becomes more difficult to do the task. So, for example the tools that we use to do overhead work primarily a pike pole, which is, you know, I’m significantly shorter than my colleagues, so reaching overhead with that tool. I have a lot less control over the tool because the handle is so much longer and it’s hard to control because I’m shorter. It puts extra strain on my shoulder…”.

Many firefighters, particularly those who do not fit the standard physical profile struggle with equipment that is not designed for their body type. This lack of customization in tools and gear was reported to exacerbate existing shoulder injuries and increase the risk for new injuries. This was claimed to occur because the equipment often required unnatural movements or excessive force that strains the shoulder, preventing proper healing and potentially leading to chronic pain conditions. One firefighter, FF06, commented, “…the tools are 20 to 30pounds actually maybe they even heavier than that. I’ve never actually weighed our auto X tools, but they’re very heavy tools that the average adult can’t pick it up easily. So, there’s auto X tools that are moving and ripping cars apart, and you’re holding it while you’re cutting a car apart or ripping it apart. That takes quite a bit of toll on your shoulders and at some points you may even have it on your (affected) shoulders while you’re extricating, and it all depends on the situation…”.

Theme 5: Health Care Challenges

Lack of tailored healthcare protocols and interventions hinder recovery

Firefighters reported the lack of a comprehensive and formal healthcare protocol prior to or following their shoulder injuries. These protocols were reported to typically include preventive measures, training in proper techniques, and regular physical assessments. The absence of such protocols was identified as one of the occupational health challenges leading to a higher incidence of shoulder injuries as preventive steps are not systematically implemented. Many FFs explained that there is a strong desire for more comprehensive training on how to prevent shoulder injuries, but nothing has been put in place to help mitigate the risk for WSDs. FF17 suggested, “It would be helpful if our employer provided education on shoulder health…I should say, but I don’t see any sort of education offered on shoulder health…”.

Furthermore, some FFs mentioned that they encountered some barriers in the health care system while recovering from their shoulder injury. They felt that the process of getting approved for healthcare coverage was long and daunting. For instance, FF11, revealed his concerns on the challenging process to get coverage for his injury: “It’s been very difficult dealing with the (work injury claims organization) on the matter. It’s been a frustrating and stressful thing to deal with there, so it would be nice to have a better process to take care of that.” In relation to appropriate healthcare management, some FFs claimed that most healthcare professionals were not well versed about appropriately managing conditions specific to their unique population. Limited understanding of firefighters’ unique physical demands by healthcare providers was reported to cause delayed or suboptimal care. A firefighter, FF01, expressed that he experienced delayed medical intervention which further worsened his shoulder condition. “Well, I believe that was part of the problem, so everybody was concerned about the fractures and the vertebrae. And although my chief complaint was shoulder pain, they didn’t really do much with the shoulder pain. They were worried about my back, so eventually I got an MRI with my shoulder which showed soft tissue damage, but unbeknownst to anybody at the time, I had a nerve impingement that eventually killed that nerve. So, I’ve got some shoulder mobility limitations that are permanent now (very long silence).” Another firefighter, FF03 echoed similar concerns, “We all knew what the problem was, it was obvious on the MRI. It was obvious to the physicians. We still had to go through a period of a cortisone shot, physical therapy and we knew, I knew in my heart, in my head and I guess physically, that was not going to work. And I felt like we wasted some time without going directly to, …fixing the problem early on as opposed to going through other steps. …six months later, when I finally got surgery, …it didn’t make any sense to me prior to the surgery, I think that was probably the most frustrating piece of it…that was for me a frustrating process to go through.”

Discussion

The findings from our study provide a nuanced understanding of how WSDs affect firefighters’ personal and professional lives, while also identifying interconnected systemic challenges that exacerbate these shoulder injuries. The emerged themes provide deeper insights into the unique experiences of firefighters emphasizing the urgent need for targeted interventions to mitigate the burden of WSDs. The theme of physical impact aligns with existing literature on the debilitating effects of musculoskeletal injuries including WSDs on the well-being and job performance of FFs [15, 23]. For instance, a focus group study in the US by Conrad et al. [23] reported that the WSDs experienced by FFs often led to pain, reduced mobility, and long recovery periods. However, our study extends prior research by highlighting the cascading effects of WSDs on firefighters’ personal lives, including difficulties with routine activities like grooming, cooking, and family responsibilities. These impacts underscore the pervasive nature of WSDs, which extend beyond the workplace to disrupt familial and social roles.

Sleep deprivation further compounds the physical impact of WSDs, with FFs reporting disrupted sleep patterns due to shoulder pain and irregular shift work. Firefighters in our study claimed that poor sleep routine affected interpersonal relationships and impaired their job performance by reducing emotional resilience, physical capacity and alertness during emergencies. This is consistent with a study by Lim et al. [24] linking sleep disturbances to increased injury risk and decreased occupational performance, emphasizing the need for tailored interventions to address sleep hygiene and enhance recovery in this population.

The occupational impact of WSDs was equally significant, with FFs expressing concerns about lost work time, financial strain, and perceived career setbacks. Evidence has shown that extended absences due to WSDs increased the workload on colleagues, straining team dynamics and potentially elevating the risk of WSDs among remaining team members [15, 23, 25]. These findings echo research on the ripple effects of absenteeism in high-risk occupations like firefighting. Moreover, the financial burden of unpaid leave and lost income from side jobs reported by FFs in our study highlight their economic vulnerability during prolonged recovery periods.

Modified duties, while necessary for recovery, were perceived as a professional setback, limiting firefighters’ exposure to critical tasks and hindering skill development. Evidence has shown that this perception of diminished capability was reported to influence career progression, as firefighters on modified duties were often viewed as less capable by peers and supervisors [2, 23]. Likewise, a recent study in Canada reported that many FFs believed that those modified duties often felt insignificant or unrelated to their skills, contributing to stigma and frustration, so most FFs prematurely RTW further increasing their risk of WSDs [26]. This finding reinforces the need for organizational policies that support injured firefighters’ reintegration into frontline roles while addressing the stigma associated with modified duties.

The psychosocial impact of WSDs was profound, with FFs reporting stress, anxiety, and a diminished sense of self-worth. The fear of re-injury and reduced confidence in their physical abilities led to a sense of “identity loss,” as FFs struggled to reconcile their injuries with their self-image as strong, capable first responders. This finding agrees with previous research that reported a significant association between psychological symptoms such as emotional stress and musculoskeletal injuries like WSDs among FFs [2, 11]. However, our study adds depth to this literature by emphasizing the emotional and social isolation experienced by injured FFs, who often felt disconnected from their teams due to the inability to participate in work-related or social activities.

Systemic challenges that exacerbate WSDs, including poorly designed equipment or gears and inadequate healthcare protocols were also identified by FFs. Female FFs and other FFs with smaller statures, reported that standard-issue gear was ill-suited to their body types, further exacerbating their shoulder injury during recovery. This aligns with a study by McQuerry et al. [27] who showed that female FFs frequently wear personal protective clothing with significant fit issues increasing their safety risks in relation to occupational exposure or injuries like WSDs. This finding calls for holistic ergonomic redesigns that accommodate the diverse physical profiles of FFs to satisfy their physical and psychological needs [28].

The lack of tailored healthcare protocols and delayed medical interventions hindered recovery among FFs in our study. One of the critical findings is the significant challenges FFs face in accessing and navigating healthcare protocols for WSDs. Firefighters reported a glaring absence of comprehensive healthcare protocols specifically designed to address WSDs in their profession. They expressed the lack of access to specialized care that considers the physical demands and risks inherent to their work. This lack of tailored care or interventions leaves FFs vulnerable to preventable injuries and suboptimal recovery outcomes.

The absence of preventive measures, such as regular physical assessments and ergonomic fitness training, further compounds the problem. Previous research showed that FFs have emphasized the need for proactive strategies to mitigate the risk of WRMSDs including WSDs, such as fitness training [29] or proper ergonomic techniques that simulate their firefighting duties [30]. Without such measures, FFs are left to navigate the physical demands of their job without adequate guidance, increasing their susceptibility to WSDs.

Delayed medical interventions were a recurring theme in this study, with FFs reporting significant barriers to timely and effective care. Many participants described lengthy processes to obtain approval for treatments, such as imaging, physical therapy, or surgery. These delays not only prolong recovery but also increase the risk of chronic shoulder pain and permanent disability.

In some cases, FFs felt that healthcare providers lacked an understanding of the unique physical demands of their profession, leading to suboptimal care. This finding agrees with a study by Killip et al. [26] who reported that many healthcare providers lack familiarity with the specific tasks, gear, and environmental conditions that FFs face daily. This might lead to generic rehabilitation plans that may not fully address the complex needs of FFs, resulting in prolonged recovery periods. Further, this study showed systemic barriers within the healthcare system that hinder firefighters’ access to care. These barriers include bureaucratic hurdles, limited availability of specialized services, and a lack of coordination between employers, insurers, and healthcare providers. These systemic inefficiencies not only delay recovery but also contribute to the financial and emotional burden of FFs with WSDs. This agrees with previous studies that showed that FFs often face significant out-of-pocket expenses and lost income due to prolonged recovery periods, further exacerbating the challenges they face [9, 14, 25].

Implications for practice and policy

Our findings reinforce the need for a holistic approach to addressing WSDs, one that considers not only the immediate physical symptoms but also the broader implications for firefighters’ professional identity, team dynamics, and overall well-being. Organizational interventions, such as improved equipment design, structured RTW policies, tailored healthcare protocols, and comprehensive training on injury prevention, are critical to mitigating the risk of WSDs and supporting firefighters’ recovery. Additionally, addressing the psychosocial impact of WSDs through mental health support and fostering a culture of inclusivity within firefighting teams can help reduce feelings of isolation and enhance resilience.

Limitations

While this study offers valuable insights into the physical, occupational, and psychosocial impacts of WSDs, several limitations must be acknowledged to contextualize the findings and guide future research. First, our study primarily included FFs from North America, which may limit the generalizability of the findings to other firefighting populations in other regions. This may be due to the cultural, organizational, or regional differences in firefighting practices, and healthcare access in other firefighting regions.

Second, our study relied on self-reported data, which is inherently subject to recall bias and social desirability bias. Firefighters may have underreported or overreported their experiences, including the severity of WSDs due to concerns about job security, stigma, or personal perceptions of pain and disability. Additionally, the retrospective nature of our semi-structured interviews may have led to inaccuracies in recalling the timing, severity, or specific details of their injuries and recovery processes.

Lastly, while our study highlighted the experiences of female FFs and those with smaller statures, it did not fully explore other intersectional factors that may influence the impact of WSDs. For example, the interplay of gender, and ethnicity, in shaping access to healthcare, recovery outcomes, and occupational support were not thoroughly examined. This limits the study’s ability to address the unique challenges faced by marginalized subgroups within the firefighting population.

Conclusion

This study illuminates the far-reaching consequences of WSDs among FFs, revealing a complex interplay of physical, occupational, and psychosocial impact including the systematic challenges they face during recovery. Notably, pain, functional limitations and disrupted sleep were reported to compromise critical job performance affecting their well-being and overall quality of life. The psychological impact was marked by guilt, isolation, and diminished identity illuminating the silent burden carried by those navigating modified duties or prolonged recovery. Systemic challenges such as ergonomically inadequate equipment as it relates to the lack of sex- and size-inclusive gear disproportionately endanger female FFs and those with smaller body frames. Furthermore, persistent gaps in healthcare access, including the absence of tailored protocols specific to FFs, amplify the risk of chronicity and hinder timely RTW. These findings call for an urgent re-evaluation of prevention, rehabilitation, and RTW strategies within the fire service, ones that are inclusive, evidence-informed, and contextually grounded in the lived experiences of FFs with WSDs.
