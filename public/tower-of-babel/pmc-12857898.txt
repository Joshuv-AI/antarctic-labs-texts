Association of Vitamin B12 and Vitamin D Levels with Glycemic Control in Patients with Type 2 Diabetes Mellitus
Authors: Umesh P. Singh, Dheerendra K. Mishra, Pooja Gangwar, Pramod Kushwaha
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Background:Type 2 diabetes mellitus (T2DM) is a growing global health concern, especially in low- and middle-income countries like India. Optimal glycemic control remains a cornerstone of diabetes management in primary care. Emerging evidence suggests that micronutrient deficiencies, particularly vitamin B12 and vitamin D, may adversely affect glucose metabolism and insulin sensitivity, yet their routine assessment is often overlooked in clinical practice.Objectives:To evaluate the association between serum vitamin B12 and vitamin D levels with glycemic control in patients with T2DM and to explore their potential as modifiable risk factors in primary care-based diabetes management.Methods:This cross-sectional study enrolled 500 patients with T2DM attending the outpatient clinic of Shyam Shah Medical College, Rewa, in 2024. Data collected included demographics, BMI, HbA1c, fasting and postprandial glucose, vitamin B12, 25-hydroxyvitamin D, lipid profile, and renal function tests. Patients were categorized based on glycemic control (Group A: HbA1c < 7%, Group B: HbA1c ≥ 7%). Statistical analyses included t-tests, Pearson correlation, and multivariate regression.Results:Group A had significantly higher vitamin B12 (674.2 ± 609.5 pg/mL) and vitamin D levels (31.20 ± 13.80 ng/mL) compared to Group B (457.76 ± 463.6 pg/mL and 26.76 ± 20.63 ng/mL, respectively; P < 0.01). HbA1c showed inverse correlations with both vitamins. Multivariate analysis confirmed both as independent predictors of glycemic control.Conclusions:Vitamin B12 and D deficiencies are significantly associated with poor glycemic control and may serve as modifiable targets in diabetes care, warranting routine screening in primary care settings.

Introduction

Type 2 diabetes mellitus (T2DM) is a chronic, progressive metabolic disorder marked by persistent hyperglycemia due to insulin resistance and beta-cell dysfunction. It poses a major global health burden, with an estimated 537 million individuals affected in 2021—a figure projected to escalate to 783 million by 2045.[1] The disease contributes significantly to morbidity and mortality, with long-term complications including cardiovascular disease, nephropathy, neuropathy, and retinopathy. In primary care settings, where the majority of T2DM cases are diagnosed and managed, achieving and sustaining optimal glycemic control is a key objective to prevent complications and enhance patient outcomes.

While lifestyle interventions and pharmacologic therapies remain the cornerstone of T2DM management, growing evidence suggests that micronutrient deficiencies, particularly of vitamin B12 and vitamin D, may adversely impact glycemic control. These findings have important implications for primary care physicians (PCPs), who are often the first point of contact in the management of diabetes and are well positioned to implement cost-effective, preventive strategies that address both pharmacologic and nutritional aspects of care.

Vitamin B12, a water-soluble micronutrient, plays a critical role in DNA synthesis, red blood cell production, and neurologic function. Several studies have linked vitamin B12 deficiency with T2DM, especially among patients on long-term metformin therapy. Metformin, the widely used first-line antidiabetic agent, is known to impair intestinal absorption of vitamin B12, with deficiency observed in up to 30% of patients receiving prolonged treatment.[2] This deficiency may lead to elevated homocysteine levels, diabetic neuropathy, and increased oxidative stress—all of which can worsen insulin resistance and glycemic control.[34]

Recent investigations support a connection between low vitamin B12 levels and poor glycemic indices. For instance, Rizzo et al.[5] demonstrated that patients with T2DM and B12 deficiency had significantly higher HbA1c levels, while Satapathy S et al. reported that B12 supplementation improved insulin sensitivity in diabetic individuals.[6] However, evidence remains mixed, necessitating further population-specific studies. This is especially critical in South Asian populations like India, where vegetarian dietary patterns and limited animal protein intake predispose individuals to B12 deficiency.[7]

Vitamin D, a fat-soluble vitamin produced primarily through sunlight exposure, has also been implicated in glucose metabolism. It enhances insulin sensitivity and secretion, modulates immune responses, and exerts anti-inflammatory effects that may mitigate insulin resistance.[8] Several observational and interventional studies link low serum vitamin D levels with an increased risk of developing T2DM. For example, Harris E et al.[9] conducted a meta-analysis showing a significant association between low vitamin D levels and T2DM risk, while Nazarian S et al.[10] reported that vitamin D supplementation improved insulin sensitivity and delayed diabetes onset in high-risk individuals. In established T2DM, deficiency of vitamin D has been associated with poorer glycemic control and increased complications.[11]

Mechanistic studies suggest that vitamin D improves beta-cell function and upregulates insulin receptor expression.[12] Moreover, Strange RC et al.[13] found a correlation between vitamin D deficiency and features of metabolic syndrome among diabetic patients. The relevance of these findings is particularly notable in India, where vitamin D deficiency affects over 80% of the population due to limited sun exposure, urban pollution, and poor dietary intake.[14] Given the parallel high burden of T2DM and vitamin D deficiency, this dual nutritional-metabolic challenge represents an area of clinical importance, especially in primary care practice.

In this context, primary care physicians play a pivotal role in the early detection and management of vitamin deficiencies as part of holistic diabetes care. Addressing modifiable micronutrient imbalances through simple, evidence-based interventions can enhance glycemic control, reduce complications, and minimize the need for complex pharmacologic escalation.

This study was designed to examine the association between serum levels of vitamin B12 and vitamin D with glycemic control among patients with T2DM attending a tertiary care center in India. The findings aim to inform primary care strategies and support integrative, nutrition-sensitive approaches to diabetes management.

Materials and Methods

Study design

This was a descriptive cross-sectional study conducted at Shyam Shah Medical College, Rewa, Madhya Pradesh, India, from January 1, 2024 to December 31, 2024. The study aimed to evaluate the relationship between vitamin B12, vitamin D levels, and glycemic control in patients diagnosed with T2DM. The ethical approval was obtained from the Institutional Ethics Committee prior to the commencement of the study and informed consent as taken from study participants.

Study population

The study included patients who were diagnosed with T2DM and attended the outpatient diabetes clinic and IPD at the Department of Medicine, Shyam Shah Medical College, during the study period.

Sample size calculation

The sample size for this cross-sectional descriptive study was calculated based on the estimated prevalence of Vitamin B12 and Vitamin D deficiencies among patients with T2DM. Previous studies report a prevalence of approximately 30% for Vitamin B12 deficiency and 80% for Vitamin D deficiency. Using the formula for estimating a single population proportion at a 95% confidence level and a 5% margin of error, the minimum sample size required was calculated to be 323 (based on the lower prevalence of B12 deficiency, which yields a higher sample size requirement). To account for potential nonresponders and incomplete data, a 20% buffer was added, resulting in an adjusted sample size of approximately 388. However, a total of 500 participants were ultimately enrolled to enhance statistical power and allow for subgroup analysis.

Statistical analysis

The data were analyzed using IBM SPSS Statistics for Windows (version 22.0). Continuous variables were presented as mean ± standard deviation (SD), and categorical variables were expressed as frequencies and percentages. The normality of data was assessed using the Shapiro–Wilk test. For normally distributed continuous variables, comparisons between two groups (HbA1c < 7 vs. HbA1c ≥ 7) were performed using an independent t-test, while non-normally distributed data were analyzed using the Mann–Whitney U test.

Correlation analyses were performed to assess the relationship between vitamin B12, vitamin D levels, and glycemic control parameters using Pearson’s correlation (for normally distributed data) and Spearman’s rank correlation (for non-normally distributed data). Multiple linear regression analysis was conducted to explore the independent effects of vitamin B12 and vitamin D on glycemic control, adjusting for confounders such as age, BMI, and duration of diabetes. A P value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant for all analyses.

Ethical considerations

The study was approved by the Institutional Ethics Committee (IEC) of Shyam Shah Medical College, Rewa. Informed consent was obtained from all participants prior to their inclusion in the study. All participants were informed about the purpose of the study, confidentiality of their data, and their right to withdraw from the study at any time.

Results

This study aimed to investigate the relationship between vitamin B12 level, vitamin D level, and glycemic control in patients with T2DM. The patients were stratified into two groups based on HbA1c levels: Group 1 (HbA1c < 7, n = 120) and Group 2 (HbA1c ≥ 7, n = 380).

A total of 500 participants were enrolled in the study, with 235 (47.0%) males and 265 (53.0%) females. Regarding the duration of diabetes, 190 participants (38.0%) had been diagnosed for less than 5 years, 210 (42.0%) for 5–10 years, and 100 (20.0%) for more than 10 years. In terms of residence, 285 participants (57.0%) were from rural areas, while 215 (43.0%) resided in urban settings.

The study analyzed the clinical, biochemical, and glycemic parameters by stratifying participants into two groups based on their HbA1c levels: Group 1 (HbA1c < 7, n = 120) and Group 2 (HbA1c ≥ 7, n = 380). Discussion

This cross-sectional study evaluated the association between serum levels of vitamin B12 and vitamin D and glycemic control among patients with T2DM, along with related biochemical parameters such as lipid profile and renal function. Our findings show that individuals with suboptimal glycemic control (HbA1c ≥ 7%) had significantly lower serum concentrations of both vitamin B12 and vitamin D. Additionally, these patients exhibited dyslipidemia and impaired renal markers, painting a broader picture of metabolic disturbance. These outcomes not only reinforce existing literature on the metabolic importance of these micronutrients but also underscore their practical significance in day-to-day diabetes management, particularly within primary care settings.

Vitamin B12, essential for DNA synthesis, erythropoiesis, and neural health, is especially vulnerable to deficiency in diabetic patients—primarily due to long-term metformin therapy. Metformin impairs vitamin B12 absorption via calcium-dependent mechanisms in the ileum,[15] resulting in clinically relevant deficiency in up to 30–40% of chronic users.[16] In our study, serum vitamin B12 levels were markedly lower in patients with poor glycemic control (457.76 ± 463.6 pg/mL) than in those with good control (674.2 ± 609.5 pg/mL). This aligns with Indian findings by Hanumanthu A et al.,[17] who reported a twofold increase in neuropathy among vitamin B12-deficient diabetic patients. B12 deficiency is mechanistically linked to elevated homocysteine, oxidative stress, and vascular dysfunction,[4] all of which are pertinent to the metabolic and microvascular complications that T2DM patients face. For primary care physicians, this emphasizes the necessity of vitamin B12 screening in routine diabetes follow-up, particularly in patients experiencing neuropathic symptoms or long-term metformin therapy.

Vitamin D has also emerged as a central player in glycemic homeostasis due to its regulatory effects on insulin sensitivity, β-cell function, and systemic inflammation. Our results revealed significantly lower vitamin D levels in patients with poor glycemic control (26.76 ± 20.63 ng/mL) compared to those with controlled diabetes (31.20 ± 13.80 ng/mL; P = 0.007). Vitamin D receptors are expressed in β-cells and insulin-target tissues, and their activation improves insulin release and sensitivity.[12] Inflammatory cytokines such as IL-6 and TNF-α, which contribute to insulin resistance, are downregulated by vitamin D.[18] Several trials have reported improved insulin sensitivity with vitamin D supplementation in deficient individuals.[19] Considering the widespread prevalence of vitamin D deficiency in India, exceeding 80% due to limited sun exposure, cultural clothing, and inadequate diet,[14] primary care providers must recognize the importance of evaluating vitamin D status during diabetic checkups, especially in patients with poor glycemic outcomes or fatigue, muscle weakness, or recurrent infections.

From a cardiovascular risk standpoint, our study identified higher triglyceride levels and lower HDL levels in poorly controlled patients—classic markers of diabetic dyslipidemia. Triglycerides were 180.56 ± 71.78 mg/dL in the HbA1c ≥ 7% group, versus 152.19 ± 58.09 mg/dL in the <7% group (P < 0.001); HDL values were 35.90 ± 8.39 mg/dL vs. 40.22 ± 4.93 mg/dL, respectively (P < 0.001). These findings are consistent with those of Khan et al.,[20] underscoring the strong relationship between glycemic control and lipid metabolism. Notably, even modest reductions in HDL have been associated with increased cardiovascular risk in patients with T2DM, highlighting the potential clinical significance of our observation. Though LDL and total cholesterol were higher in the poorly controlled group, the differences were not statistically significant, likely may be attributable to widespread statin use among participants, which could have masked potential associations with glycemic status. For primary care clinicians, these findings reinforce the utility of comprehensive lipid monitoring in diabetic care beyond glucose control alone.

Renal parameters also differed significantly between groups. Serum urea and creatinine were elevated in the poorly controlled cohort (46.70 ± 39.99 mg/dL and 1.49 ± 1.43 mg/dL) compared to those with better glycemic control (29.21 ± 10.66 mg/dL and 1.00 ± 0.38 mg/dL), with P values < 0.001. These findings are supported by Shurraw S et al.[21] and Al-Rubeaan et al.,[22] who described the progression of nephropathy with rising HbA1c levels. Early detection of renal involvement, often first indicated by subtle biochemical abnormalities, is particularly crucial in primary care, where simple interventions can delay or prevent advanced renal disease.

Collectively, the data highlight that poor glycemic control in T2DM is not an isolated issue of hyperglycemia but reflects a cluster of interrelated disturbances including micronutrient deficiencies, dyslipidemia, and nephropathy. This underlines the importance of a **multifaceted, integrative management approach**. In the context of primary care, where most diabetes cases are managed long-term, this study advocates for the inclusion of routine screening for vitamin B12 and D, lipid profiles, and renal function markers as part of standard diabetes protocols.

Clinical implications for primary care physicians

Given the high burden of diabetes in India and the frontline role of primary care physicians in its management, the findings from this study offer practical, actionable insights. Primary care physicians should consider incorporating:

Routine screening for vitamin B12 and D, especially in patients on long-term metformin, those with suboptimal glycemic control, or with clinical features like neuropathy, fatigue, or myalgia.

By embedding these elements into routine practice, primary care physicians can provide more holistic, preventive, and individualized care to T2DM patients.

Limitations

This study’s cross-sectional design limits the ability to draw causal inferences. The absence of data on dietary intake of vitamin B12 and vitamin D, physical activity, sun exposure, and metformin duration may act as confounding variables. Additionally, although the sample size was robust, findings from a single-center study may not be generalizable across all population groups. Future longitudinal cohort studies and randomized controlled trials are warranted to validate these associations and explore causality.

Conclusion

In summary, our findings reaffirm the critical role of vitamin B12 and D sufficiency in maintaining optimal glycemic control among patients with T2DM. Moreover, the study identifies significant associations between poor glycemic control and dyslipidemia as well as early renal dysfunction. These observations advocate for a comprehensive, integrative approach to diabetes care particularly within the primary care setting. Routine screening and timely correction of micronutrient deficiencies, coupled with regular metabolic monitoring, can enhance clinical outcomes and quality of life. For primary care physicians, adopting this expanded approach represents a practical and impactful strategy for managing the complex metabolic landscape of diabetes.
