Evaluation of INR101, a PSMA-Targeted 18F PET Tracer, for suspected prostate cancer: A multicenter phase I/IIa trial with histopathologic confirmation
Authors: Jigang Yang, Wuzhou Wan, Jinming Zhang, Ning Zhang, Xing Yang, Wei Wang, Huan Ma, Yanfeng Xu, Zhizhen Fu, Xuejuan Wang, Yuan Liu, Mingshuai Wang, Fugeng Liu, Yue Guo, Yachao Liu, Xiaojun Zhang
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundProstate cancer (PCa) is one of the most common malignancies among men worldwide. Early detection relies heavily on imaging accuracy, yet conventional modalities such as CT and MRI have limitations in identifying early-stage lesions. INR101 is a novel 18F-labeled PSMA-targeted PET tracer with high binding affinity, favorable biodistribution, and promising lesion detection capabilities for initial PCa diagnosis.Materials and methodsINR101 was prepared and confirmed on radiochemical purity by HPLC to be > 93%. Entire production process was carried out in a manufacturing facility compliant with cGMP standards, and each production batch was tested for sterility and endotoxin levels. This multicenter, prospective, open-label Phase I/IIa trial included 8 healthy male volunteers (Phase I) and 24 treatment-naive patients with suspected prostate cancer (PSA ≥ 3 ng/mL, scheduled for biopsy; Phase IIa). Phase I evaluated safety, pharmacokinetics, biodistribution, and dosimetry of INR101 (9 ± 1 mCi). Phase IIa randomized patients 1:1 to 7 ± 1 mCi (n = 12) or 9 ± 1 mCi (n = 12) groups, with PET/CT scans at 80 ± 10 and 120 ± 10 min post-injection. Primary endpoints: Phase I (safety/dosimetry); Phase IIa (diagnostic efficacy vs. biopsy pathology). Exploratory endpoint: INR101 vs. mpMRI. The study was approved by ethics committees (2024LP01109) and registered (ChiCTR20240522, NCT06472531).ResultsIn Phase I, INR101 showed rapid systemic distribution with primary uptake in the liver, kidneys, and salivary glands and a mean effective radiation dose was 7.6 ± 1.2 mSv. In Phase IIa trial, sensitivity, specificity, and accuracy of the low-dose group (7 ± 1 mCi), reached 100% (95% CI:39.8%-100.0%), 85.7% (95% CI:42.1%-99.6%), and 90.9% (95% CI:58.7%-99.8%), respectively; while that of the high-dose group (9 ± 1 mCi) were 87.5% (95% CI: 47.35%, 99.68%), 75.0% (95% CI: 19.41%, 99.37%), and 83.3% (95% CI: 51.59%, 97.91%). INR101 PET/CT demonstrated higher specificity and accuracy than multiparametric MRI. [low-dose group (7 ± 1 mCi): specificity 85.7% (95% CI:42.1%-99.6%) vs. mpMRI 60.0% (95% CI:14.7%-94.7%); accuracy 90.9% (95% CI:58.7%-99.8%) vs. mpMRI 78.3% (95% CI:56.3%-92.5%)], with no statistical significance (p > 0.05). No serious adverse events occurred in either Phase I or IIa.ConclusionINR101 is safe and well-tolerated, with high diagnostic accuracy for detecting PSMA-positive prostate lesions. Optimal imaging was achieved at 7 ± 1 mCi with an uptake time of 80–120 min. Its favorable pharmacokinetics and superior diagnostic performance with low urinary tract interference suggest that INR101 PET/CT may be a valuable tool for early PCa detection, meriting further validation in larger clinical studies.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s13550-025-01358-9.

Introduction

Prostate cancer (PCa) is one of the most common and lethal malignancies among men worldwide [1]. Accurate diagnosis and precise staging are crucial for informing treatment decisions and enhancing patient outcomes [2]. However, conventional imaging modalities, including computed tomography (CT) and multiparametric magnetic resonance imaging (mpMRI), have limited sensitivity for early-stage disease, often leading to delayed or inaccurate diagnoses [3, 4]. These limitations highlight the need for more sensitive and specific imaging techniques to improve lesion characterization and diagnostic confidence [5].

Prostate-specific membrane antigen (PSMA), a transmembrane protein highly overexpressed in most PCa cells, has emerged as a valuable molecular target for imaging. PSMA-targeted positron emission tomography/computed tomography (PET/CT) using radiolabeled ligands such as 18F-DCFPyL and 68Ga-PSMA-11 has demonstrated high diagnostic performance in PCa [6, 7]. Nevertheless, variability in tracer uptake, differences in clearance kinetics, and nonspecific background activity can compromise diagnostic accuracy, particularly in anatomically complex regions or tumors with low PSMA expression [8]. These challenges underscore the need for the ongoing development of novel tracers with optimized pharmacokinetics and reduced off-target uptake to further improve lesion visualization.

INR101 is a novel 18F-labeled PSMA-targeted PET tracer designed to overcome the limitations of conventional radiotracers. It combines high binding affinity with favorable pharmacokinetics and reduced urinary excretion, thereby minimizing bladder activity and improving image visibility in the pelvic region [9–11]. Its pharmacological profile is comparable to that of other radiotracers under investigation, such as 18F-PSMA-7Q and 18F-PSMA-1007, which have demonstrated improved detection of local and nodal recurrence [11, 12]. Preclinical and early clinical evaluations further suggest that INR101 enables high-contrast imaging with minimal uptake in non-target tissues, including the brain, and is primarily eliminated through renal and hepatobiliary pathways.

This multicenter, prospective, open-label Phase I/IIa clinical trial systematically evaluated INR101 in healthy volunteers and patients with suspected PCa. The primary objectives were to assess the safety, biodistribution, pharmacokinetics, and radiation dosimetry of INR101 (Phase I), and to determine its diagnostic performance in detecting PCa lesions relative toprostate biopsy (Phase IIa). Secondary objectives included comparing INR101 PET/CT with mpMRI and identifying optimal imaging parameters, including administered dose activity, post-injection imaging time, acquisition duration, and reconstruction parameters.

Method

Radiopharmaceutical Preparation

INR101 is a novel 18F-labeled PET radiotracer targeting PSMA. It is composed of a NOTA-P137 chelator coordinated with an 18F-Al complex. The radiochemical design aims to ensure high PSMA binding affinity, favorable biodistribution, and rapid clearance from non-target tissues, thereby optimizing the tumor-to-background contrast. The molecular formula of INR101 is C₄₅H₅₅Al18FN₉O₁₅, and its molecular weight is 1006.97 g/mol. The formulation is a sterile injectable solution with a radioactivity concentration ranging from 40 to 1500 MBq/mL (final radioactivity before injection). Radiolabeling of INR101 was conducted under GMP conditions by Yunnan Baiyao, with a radiochemical purity exceeding 93%, the molar activity of INR101 in the final formulation was between 279 and 3031GBq/µmol. As shown in Fig. 1A, the chemical structure of INR101 features a macrocyclic NOTA chelator conjugated to a PSMA-binding motif via a suitable linker. This configuration supports strong in vivo stability and high imaging performance.

In details, NOTA-P137 precursor was prepared using the solid-phase synthesis method, with reference to previous literatures [11. 12, 13]; systhesis of NOTA-P137 detials was described in “Supplement Information File”. Chemical purity of labelling precursor NOTA-P137 produced under GMP conditions was no less than 97% (by HPLC, retention time 16.6 min).

18F isotope was captured on an anion-exchange cartridge QMA cartridge (pretreated with 5 mL of saline and 10 ml of sterile water for injection, Waters Corporation, USA) and eluted with 0.3 mL of saline. A 1:1 mixture solution of KHP (0.5 M) and 95% ethanol, containing 18F-(37to111 GBq, starting activity), AlCl3 (10 µL, 20 mM) and NOTA-P137(500 µg), was reacted at 90 °C for 10 min. After that reaction solution was diluted with 3 mL of H2O and purified by semi-preparative chromatography, followed by Sep-Pak C18-Light cartridge (Waters Corporation, USA), which was pretreated with 10 ml of ethanol and H2O. The final product was obtained by eluting the cartridge with 2 mL of 60% ethanol, diluting it to 20mL with saline and passing through a 0.2 mm sterile filter membrane (Millex™). More than 20 batches of the product intended for clinical supply were tested for radiochemical purity by HPLC (> 93%). Entire production process was carried out in a manufacturing facility compliant with cGMP standards, and each production batch was tested for sterility and endotoxin levels to meet standard specifications for sterile injectable solutions.

Study design

This multicenter, prospective, open-label Phase I/IIa clinical trial was conducted to evaluate the safety, pharmacokinetics (PK), biodistribution, radiation dosimetry, and diagnostic performance of INR101. The trial consisted of two sequential components: Phase I involved healthy male volunteers, and Phase IIa enrolled patients with suspected PCa. The study was conducted at two academic centers in China: the Chinese People’s Liberation Army General Hospital (PLAGH) and Beijing Friendship Hospital, Capital Medical University. The study was conducted in accordance with the principles outlined in the Declaration of Helsinki and the Good Clinical Practice (GCP) guidelines. The protocol was approved by the ethics committees of the participating institutions (approval number: 2024LP01109), and written informed consent was obtained from all participants. The trial was registered with ClinicalTrials.gov (NCT06472531) with Registration date (2024-03-15) preceding first enrollment (2024-05-22), complying with ICMJE guidelines and WHO standards.

Dose selection rationale

The administered dose range for INR101 was informed by preclinical and pilot clinical studies. An earlier study demonstrated that a dose of 5.88 ± 0.57 mCi provided high-contrast imaging with no adverse events and a radiation dose profile comparable to that of other approved agents [13–15]. Subsequent pilot study showed favorable lesion detection at 6.9 ± 1.0 mCi. Based on these findings and dosing practices for other 18F-labeled PSMA tracers such as 18F-DCFPyL and 18F-PSMA-1007, which typically adopt imaging doses ranging from 7 to 10 mCi for clinical PET imaging [11, 16], a dose range of 7–10 mCi was selected for this study. Accordingly, a dose of 9 ± 1 mCi was used in Phase I to evaluate the upper bound of clinical application based on preclinical safety data (no AEs at 5.88 ± 0.57 mCi [7]. In Phase IIa, the diagnostic efficacy of two dose groups —7 ± 1 mCi (low dose) and 9 ± 1 mCi (high dose)—was compared based on 7 mCi achieving sufficient imaging (SUVmax = 19.7 ± 14.0) with a 22% lower radiation dose (5.4 mSv vs. 7.6 mSv) than 9 mCi. Imaging time (80–120 min) was selected based on Phase I biodistribution (T/B ratio = 5.8 at 80 min, 8.2 at 120 min; no significant increase after 120 min). The dose and time selections in Phase IIa aim to cover the expected maximum clinical dose while ensuring radiation safety.

Population, participant allocation and randomization

Phase I enrolled healthy male volunteers to assess the safety, PK, biodistribution, and radiation dosimetry of INR101. In Phase IIa, patients with elevated PSA levels and no prior diagnosis of PCa were enrolled to evaluate the diagnostic performance of INR101 PET/CT. Suspected prostate cancer patients (scheduled for prostate biopsy) were selected instead of pathologically confirmed cases because: (1) Biopsy pathology served as the gold standard for evaluating diagnostic efficacy (sensitivity/specificity), which is unavailable in confirmed patients; (2) This cohort aligns with clinical diagnostic scenarios, as the target indication is early PCa detection (per NMPA guidelines for radiopharmaceuticals). Patients in Phase IIa were randomized to receive either 7 ± 1 mCi or 9 ± 1 mCi of INR101 using a block randomization procedure. Prostate biopsy served as the diagnostic reference standard.

Eligibility criteria

Two distinct participant groups were enrolled in the study: healthy male volunteers for Phase I and treatment-naive patients with suspected PCa for Phase IIa.

Phase I (Health Volunteers)

Eligible participants were male, aged ≥ 18 years, with no significant medical history. Key inclusion requirements included normal physical examination and electrocardiography (ECG) findings, baseline laboratory values within reference ranges, abnormalities having no clinical significance, and the ability to comply with study procedures. Volunteers were required to use effective contraception for at least 3 months after radiotracer administration and provide written informed consent.

Exclusion criteria included any history of malignancy, cardiovascular or psychiatric disorders, abnormal liver or renal function, or any condition likely to interfere with radiotracer metabolism or PET/CT imaging.

Phase IIa (Suspected PCa Patients)

Eligible participants were male, including 24 male patients, aged ≥ 45 years, with elevated serum PSA levels (≥ 3 ng/mL) and a high clinical suspicion of PCa. All patients were scheduled for their first prostate biopsy. These participants were newly diagnosed, treatment-naive, and scheduled to undergo their first prostate biopsy.

Exclusion criteria included a prior diagnosis of PCa or treatment, active malignancies (except localized, untreated PCa), significant metabolic or cardiovascular diseases, hepatic or renal dysfunction, or any condition likely to affect radiotracer distribution or image quality. A complete list of criteria is provided in Supplemental File 1. Written informed consent was obtained from all participants before the trial.

Study endpoints

The study endpoints of the Phase I study were the safety endpoints, biodistribution, radiation dosimetry, and PK endpoints of INR101 in healthy male volunteers. The safety endpoints was assessed after intravenous administration of INR101, including adverse events (AEs), clinical laboratory tests before and after administration, 12-lead ECG, physical examinations, and vital signs. Biodistribution and radiation dosimetry evaluations included organ-level standard uptake values (SUVs), percent injected dose (%ID), residence time, absorbed dose, and effective whole-body radiation exposure dose. PK parameters were assessed in whole blood, plasma, and urine to determine the systemic distribution and clearance profile of INR101 in aims to optimize clinical dosage and imaging time points.

The primary endpoint of the Phase IIa trial was the diagnostic performance (sensitivity, specificity, accuracy, positive predictive value (PPV), and negative predictive value (NPV) of INR101 PET/CT with different tracer doses and imaging times at the prostate-region level, with prostate biopsy histopathology as the reference standard in aims to determine the optimal imaging conditions (dose and timing). The secondary endpoint was a safety evaluation on dose of INR101 by PET/CT in patients with suspected prostate cancer, including AEs, laboratory tests before and after administration, 12-lead ECG, physical examinations, and vital signs. The exploratory endpoint included Comparative diagnostic performance (sensitivity, specificity, and accuracy) of INR101 PET/CT versus multiparametric MRI at the prostate-region level, using prostate biopsy pathology as the reference standard. A full list of observational endpoints is provided in Supplemental File 1. For suspicious lesions, targeted punctures of 2 to 4 cores were performed for each lesion [18]. An ultrasound probe was inserted transrectally, and a puncture guide was used. Imaging assessment

All INR101 PET/CT scans were acquired using standardized protocols to ensure consistency across sites. Imaging was performed using Biograph mCT PET/CT scanners (Siemens Healthineers, Erlangen, Germany). Participants received an intravenous injection of INR101 at either 7 ± 1 mCi or 9 ± 1 mCi, followed by whole-body PET/CT scans extending from the mid-thigh to the vertex of the skull. PET images were acquired in 3D mode with 2–5 min per bed position. Data were reconstructed using ordered subset expectation maximization (OSEM) with time-of-flight (TOF) and point spread function (PSF) modeling, incorporating corrections for attenuation (low-dose CT), scatter, randoms, and decay. All quantitative measurements, including maximum standardized uptake value (SUVmax), tumor-to-liver (T/L) ratio, and tumor-to-blood (T/B) ratio, were performed on images generated using these harmonized parameters. The PET/CT imaging was assessed by two nuclear medicine physicians with over 8 years of experience, who received pre-study training on INR101 imaging features and SUV measurement, with calibration using 10 historical PCa PET/CT cases. Inter-reader agreement was conducted and showed a Cohen’s Kappa of 0.83 (95%CI:0.61–1.00.61.00, p < 0.001), which is considered as an “almost perfect” as per Landis-Koch criteria. Discrepant cases were resolved by a senior physician (15 + years) using ‘SUVmax>10 and T/B > 5 as priority criteria.

Phase I imaging procedures

Eight healthy male volunteers underwent six serial PET/CT scans following a single administration of INR101 (9 ± 1 mCi). Imaging was performed at approximately 5 ± 5, 30 ± 5, 60 ± 10, 120 ± 10, 180 ± 20, and 240 ± 20 min post-injection to assess biodistribution, PK, and radiation dosimetry.

Phase IIa imaging procedures

Patients in the Phase IIa cohort underwent PET/CT imaging at both 80 ± 10 min and 120 ± 10 min post-injection, following reference [11]. Image interpretation followed a binary classification (positive/negative) based on visual assessment, with abnormal radiotracer uptake in the prostate greater than surrounding normal tissue considered positive. Scans were independently interpreted by two board-certified nuclear medicine physicians, who were blinded to clinical and pathological data; a third senior physician adjudicated discrepancies. Quantitative parameters (SUVmax, T/L, and T/B ratios) were used to evaluate lesion contrast and inform optimal imaging dose and time.

Multiparametric MRI was performed on 1.5T scanners (GE Signa HDxt/Siemens Prisma) with abdominal phased-array coils; sequences included T2WI (axial/coronal, 3 mm slice), DWI (b = 0,1000,2000 s/mm²), and dynamic contrast-enhanced (DCE) MRI. PI-RADS v2.1 was used. The multiparametric MRI was interpreted according to PI-RADS version 2.1. Lesions were scored on a 5-point scale, with scores 1–3 defined as negative and scores 4–5 as positive. Based on mpMRI lesion location (urologists blinded to mpMRI scores but not the images), with 2–4 additional cores for lesions > 5 mm were subjected to biopsy. All mpMRI scans were independently reviewed by two radiologists with more than 5 years of experience, and discrepancies were resolved by a senior radiologist with over 10 years of experience.

PK assessment (Phase I)

PK analysis was conducted in healthy male volunteers enrolled in Phas I. Venous blood samples were collected at predefined time points: baseline (pre-dose, within 60 min before injection) and at approximately 2 ± 1, 5 ± 2, 10 ± 2, 15 ± 2, 30 ± 5, 60 ± 15, 120 ± 15, 240 ± 15, and 360 ± 15 min post-injection of INR101. Additionally, urine samples were collected at five intervals (0–90, 90–180, 180–240, 240–360, and 360–480 min) to quantify radiotracer excretion. Plasma and urine radioactivity measurements were used to calculate key PK parameters, including maximum plasma concentration (Cmax), time to reach maximum concentration (Tmax), and elimination half-life (t1/2).

Safety monitoring (Phase I and IIa)

All participants in both Phase I and Phase IIa underwent comprehensive safety assessments throughout the study. Safety monitoring included the evaluation of adverse events (AEs), clinical laboratory tests, 12-lead electrocardiograms (ECGs), physical examinations, and vital signs (blood pressure, heart rate, respiratory rate, and body temperature). AEs were coded using the Medical Dictionary for Regulatory Activities (MedDRA) and graded according to the Common Terminology Criteria for Adverse Events (CTCAE), version 5.0.

Statistical analysis

All statistical analyses were conducted according to a pre-specified analysis plan using SAS software (version 9.4 or higher; SAS Institute lnc., Cary, NC). Continuous variables were summarized as mean ± standard deviation (SD) and assessed for normality using the Shapiro-Wilk test. For clinical interpretability, minimum and maximum values were also reported in the supplementary materials. Categorical variables were summarized as counts (n) and percentages (%).

Diagnostic performance was evaluated using sensitivity, specificity, accuracy, PPV, and NPV, each reported with corresponding 95% confidence intervals (CIs). Cohen’s kappa statistics consistency testing was used to assess the agreement between INR101 PET/CT findings and histopathology, and the results were interpreted according to the Landis and Koch criteria.

Comparisons between different imaging conditions (e.g., low-dose vs. high-dose; 80 min vs. 120 min) were performed using univariate and multivariate logistic regression models to identify predictors of diagnostic accuracy. Comparisons between INR101 PET/CT and mpMRI were evaluated using McNemar’s test for paired binary outcomes.

All statistical tests were two-sided, and a p-value < 0.05. was considered statistically significant. No imputation was performed for missing data; the patient (n = 1) without matched histopathology results was excluded from diagnostic performance analyses.

Results

All Phase I participants received a single intravenous dose of 9 ± 1 mCi INR101 (Fig. 1B). In Phase IIa, 24 patients were randomized to receive either a low-dose (7 ± 1 mCi) or high-dose (9 ± 1 mCi) of INR101 (n = 12 per group) (Fig. 1C). One patient in the low-dose group did not undergo prostate biopsy and was excluded from efficacy analysis, resulting in a total of 23 patients in the complete analysis set (Table 1).

Safety

INR101 was well tolerated in both Phases I and IIa (Table 2). In Phase I, no serious adverse events (SAEs) were reported. One participant (12.5%) experienced a Grade 1 episode of hyperuricemia, which was mild and self-resolved. In Phase IIa, two participants (8.3%) experienced mild, transient adverse events, including one case of dizziness and one case of fatigue, both of which resolved spontaneously without treatment. No SAEs occurred, and no participants withdrew from the study due to adverse events.

Normal organ biodistribution

INR101 demonstrated consistent physiological uptake in major organs across both Phases I and IIa participants. Serial PET/CT imaging revealed prominent tracer accumulation in the salivary glands, liver, kidneys, spleen, and gallbladder, with clearance primarily via renal and hepatobiliary pathways (Fig. 2A). In one representative Phase IIa case, PET/CT images acquired at 80 min (left) and 120 min (right) post-injection clearly delineated the PCa lesion with high tracer uptake and excellent contrast relative to background tissues (Fig. 2B).

Lesion uptake characteristics (T/B, T/L, SUVmax)

In Phase IIa, INR101 demonstrated progressively increased uptake in primary PCa lesions over time. The mean SUVmax of PCa lesions increased from 19.7 ± 14.0 to 24.5 ± 20.1 in the low-dose group, and from 15.8 ± 6.6 to 18.9 ± 8.5 in the high-dose group, at 80 and 120 min post-injection, respectively (Fig. 4A). The T/L ratio and T/B ratio were used to assess lesion contrast. In the low-dose group, the T/L ratio increased from 2.5 ± 2.3 at 80 min to 2.8 ± 2.8 at 120 min. In the high-dose group, it increased from 1.8 ± 0.9 to 2.1 ± 1.2 over the same time points (Fig. 4B). This pattern suggests improved lesion contrast with delayed imaging, particularly at the lower dose.

Pharmacokinetics

In Phase I, INR101 demonstrated rapid systemic absorption following intravenous injection, with a median time to peak blood concentration (Tmax) of approximately 3 min (range: 1.2–3 min). The mean maximum concentration (Cmax) in whole blood was 0.017 ± 0.002 (%ID/mL), and the elimination half-life was 78.0 ± 8.8 min (Fig. 5). The mean area under the concentration–time curve from time zero to the last measurable time point (AUC0 − t) was 0.013 ± 0.002 (min*%ID/mL), with AUC0-∞ at 0.786 ± 0.090 (min*%ID/mL). A similar PK profile was observed in plasma (Table 3).

Radiation dosimetry

In Phase I, organ-specific and whole-body radiation dosimetry for INR101 was assessed using serial PET/CT imaging and time-activity curve analysis. Tracer uptake in major organs demonstrated dynamic redistribution and clearance over time. The dosimetry was calculated using OLINDA/EXM v2.2; phantom model: ICRP 110 adult male (70 kg, 175 cm); VOI definition: Manual segmentation of organs (liver, kidneys, salivary glands); remainder-of-body: Uniform distribution assumption; uncertainties: CV < 5% (repeated VOI measurements). The liver exhibited an early accumulation of 11.13 ± 2.09% ID at 5 min post-injection, which increased to 12.88 ± 2.22% ID at 30 min, then gradually declined to 4.38 ± 0.72% ID by 240 min. Similar time-dependent kinetics were observed in the lungs (6.41 ± 1.52%ID at 5 min, steadily declining to 0.67 ± 0.16%ID at 240 min), kidneys (rising from 3.98 ± 0.27%ID at 5 min to 5.57 ± 0.48%ID at 60 min, then decreasing to 3.00 ± 0.28%ID at 240 min) and heart (5.24 ± 0.83%ID at 5 min, noticeably decreasing to 0.30 ± 0.05%ID at 240 min) (Fig. 6A). Whole-body activity declined progressively over time, consistent with rapid systemic clearance (Fig. 6B).

Overall diagnostic accuracy

In Phase IIa, the INR101 PET/CT scan demonstrated high diagnostic accuracy in detecting PCa lesions. Per-patient metrics showed that INR101 reached a sensitivity of 95.0% (19/20), specificity of 66.7% (2/3), NPV of 66.7% (2/3), and Kappa of 0.58 (95% CI:0.21–0.95, p = 0.003). Per-lesion metrics showed that among 32 lesions (28 malignant and 4 benign), INR101 demonstrated a sensitivity of 92.9% (26/28), specificity of 75.0% (3/4), and a Kappa of 0.65 (95% CI:0.42–0.88, p < 0.001).

Particularly in the low-dose group (7 ± 1 mCi), at 80 min post-injection, INR101 achieved a sensitivity of 100.0%, specificity of 85.7%, and accuracy of 90.9%. The PPV was 80.00%, and the NPV was 100.0%. Agreement between PET/CT results and histopathology was strong, with a Youden index of 0.86, and a Cohen’s kappa coefficient of 0.81 (p = 0.0060), indicating near-perfect concordance (Table 4).

Discussion

This study systematically evaluated the optimal imaging conditions for INR101 PET/CT in the initial diagnosis of PCa. Our findings indicate that both radiotracer dose and imaging time influence image quality and lesion contrast. While the higher dose group (9 ± 1 mCi) demonstrated a slightly improved signal-to-noise ratio and image clarity, diagnostic performance metrics did not differ significantly from those of the lower dose group (7 ± 1 mCi). A 7 mCi dose was sufficient for reliable lesion detection, highlighting INR101’s dose flexibility within clinically acceptable ranges. This is consistent with dosing guidelines for other 18F-labeled PSMA tracers such as 18F-DCFPyL (PYLARIFY) [13], which recommends a dose of 296–370 MBq (8–10 mCi). Notably, the higher specificity observed in the low-dose group may reflect reduced background signal and lower nonspecific uptake, thereby enhancing lesion-to-background contrast.

The novelty of this study lies in its systematic investigation of the interaction between radiotracer dose and imaging time, a topic rarely explored in early-phase PSMA tracer trials. These findings suggest that lower doses of INR101 are adequate for clinical use, while longer uptake times improve contrast without compromising specificity. However, the relatively small sample size (n = 32) remains a limitation, and validation in larger, multicenter trials is warranted. Additionally, the study did not include head-to-head comparisons with other approved tracers such as 68Ga-PSMA or 18F-PSMA. Further comparative trials are necessary to establish the clinical advantages of INR101 over existing options.

We also compared imaging at 80 and 120 min post-injection. Delayed imaging at 120 min resulted in higher SUVmax values, improved tumor-to-background ratios, and lower background activity in the blood pool and muscle. These enhancements contributed to improved lesion conspicuity, particularly in regions where background signal may obscure uptake. Although no statistically significant differences were observed in diagnostic metrics between time points, the visual contrast was often superior at 120 min. These results align with prior studies of the 18F-PSMA-1007, where delayed imaging increased SUVmax value by approximately 40% and improved lesion detectability. Specifically, previous studies have reported SUVmax values ranging from 12.0 to 19.6 for 18F-PSMA-1007 in primary prostate tumors [19]. INR101 demonstrated comparable SUVmax values, alongside similarly low urinary clearance, which contributed to a favorable lesion-to-background contrast and improved pelvic lesion visibility. While physiological uptake in the liver may increase at later time points [20], this effect was minimal within the 80–120-minute window and did not compromise image interpretation. It is worth noting that no significant differences were observed in the quantitative parameters (SUVmax, T/L, T/B ratios) between the 80-min and 120-min imaging time points; however, in a few cases, lesion conspicuity appeared to be subjectively improved at 120 min. This may be attributed to subtle differences in background clearance or lesion contrast over time. Although not formally assessed, subjective lesion visibility appeared better at 120 min, suggesting this as the preferred imaging time when feasible.

Urinary excretion is a key factor limiting lesion visibility in pelvic imaging. Traditional PSMA tracers such as 68Ga-PSMA-11 and 18F-DCFPyL are predominantly renally excreted, often leading to high bladder radiation activity that obscures the visibility of prostate and pelvic lymph node lesions [21]. Current imaging guidelines recommend several strategies to mitigate this, including hydration, forced diuresis, or delayed voiding.

INR101, similar to 18F-PSMA-1007, demonstrated low urinary excretion, with less than 10% of the injected dose eliminated in the urine within 8 h [22, 23]. This property markedly improves visualization of the prostate and surrounding pelvic structures. For example, while 18F-DCFPyL exhibits approximately 11% urinary excretion within 2 h, 18F-PSMA-1007 showed less than 1.5% [23]. INR101’s low urinary clearance, combined with high lesion uptake and minimal background noise at 120 min post-injection, supports its utility as an effective imaging agent for the initial evaluation of PCa.

Across both imaging time points and dose groups, INR101 PET/CT demonstrated consistent diagnostic performance, characterized by high sensitivity, specificity, and inter-rater agreement, as indicated by the Youden index and Cohen’s Kappa coefficient. Although more lesions were visibly prominent at 120 min, most were detectable by 80 min, indicating that faster imaging protocols remain clinically feasible. When imaging logistics allow, a 120-minute uptake period is recommended for optimal contrast and diagnostic confidence. These findings are in line with previous studies of 18F-DCFPyL and 18F-PSMA-1007, which also showed increased SUVmax and lesion contrast at later time points without compromising diagnostic accuracy [24, 25]. Given the small sample size (n = 23), the confidence intervals remain wide. Although INR101 PET/CT demonstrated numerically higher specificity and accuracy than mpMRI, these findings should not be interpreted as evidence of superiority. Larger Phase III trials are required to confirm and validate these differences.

INR101 offers several potential advantages over existing PSMA-targeted tracers. Compared with 68Ga-PSMA-11, 18F-labeled tracers provide higher spatial resolution and allow for centralized production and broader distribution, due to 18F’s shorter positron travel distance (0.63 MeV vs. 1.90 MeV) and longer half-life (110 vs. 68 minutes) [26]. Prior study suggests that 18F-PSMA PET imaging is diagnostically comparable to, or better than, 68Ga-based imaging [27]. While this study did not include direct comparisons to other 18F-labeled PSMA tracers, INR101’s favorable pharmacokinetic profile, minimal urinary clearance, and high lesion contrast are promising. Future trials should compare INR101 head-to-head with approved agents such as 18F-DCFPyL or 18F-PSMA-1007 to determine its relative clinical value.

Conclusion

This study demonstrates that INR101 PET/CT provides high-quality diagnostic imaging for PCa at a dose of 7 ± 1 mCi, with optimal imaging performance observed within 80–120 min post-injection. Although the lesion SUVmax value, T/B ratio, and T/L ratio were higher at 120 min, the diagnostic performance was comparable between the 80- and 120-minute time points. These findings highlight INR101’s flexibility in imaging protocols and support 120 min as the preferred imaging time when feasible. The tracer’s favorable pharmacokinetics, low urinary excretion, and high lesion-to-background contrast position INR101 as a promising agent for initial PCa detection, warranting further validation in larger and comparative studies.
