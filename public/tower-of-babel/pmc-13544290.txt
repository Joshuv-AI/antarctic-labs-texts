Concurrent transfusion-related acute lung injury and posterior reversible encephalopathy: A rare multisystem imaging presentation
Authors: Aman Mamodia, Suman Singhal, Narayan Solanki, Harsh Mahajan, Arun Agarwal
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Transfusion-related acute lung injury (TRALI) is a leading cause of transfusion-associated morbidity and mortality. Posterior reversible encephalopathy syndrome (PRES) following transfusion is uncommon, and its concurrent occurrence with TRALI has rarely been described in the literature. We report a 65-year-old male with ascending colon carcinoma status post right hemicolectomy on adjuvant chemotherapy who developed acute hypoxemia and bilateral pulmonary infiltrates within 3 hours of packed red blood cell transfusion, consistent with TRALI. He subsequently developed acute neuropsychiatric manifestations, including agitation, headache, visual disturbances, abnormal behavior, nystagmoid eye movements, and gaze deviation. Magnetic resonance imaging demonstrated bilateral frontoparietal vasogenic edema without diffusion restriction, consistent with PRES. Both pulmonary and neurological abnormalities resolved substantially on follow-up. No pulmonary or neurological deficits were detected in sequential monthly follow-ups. Alternative causes, including transfusion-associated circulatory overload, sepsis-related acute respiratory distress syndrome, and sepsis-associated encephalopathy, were considered and are discussed. This case highlights the importance of recognizing rare, overlapping pulmonary and neurological transfusion complications and considering confounding illness in their diagnosis.

Introduction

TRALI is defined as acute noncardiogenic pulmonary edema occurring within 6 hours of blood product transfusion. It typically presents with hypoxemia and bilateral pulmonary infiltrates in the absence of circulatory overload, and is thought to result from a 2-hit mechanism in which recipient inflammatory priming is followed by donor-derived antibody-mediated neutrophil activation and pulmonary endothelial injury [1,2].

PRES is a clinico-radiological syndrome characterized by vasogenic edema classically affecting the parieto-occipital regions, attributed to relative sparing of sympathetic innervation in the posterior circulation. PRES has been reported in association with hypertension, eclampsia, cytotoxic and immunosuppressive drugs, sepsis, and, less commonly, blood transfusion, particularly during rapid correction of severe chronic anemia [[3], [4], [5], [6]].

We could not identify a prior report describing concurrent TRALI and PRES arising from the same transfusion episode, although both entities have separately been linked to transfusion-triggered endothelial and capillary dysfunction, and a shared pathophysiological pathway has been proposed. We therefore present this as an apparently rare combination and discuss confounding illness that must be considered before attributing either finding solely to transfusion.

Case presentation

A 65-year-old male with carcinoma of the ascending colon status post right hemicolectomy, receiving adjuvant oxaliplatin and capecitabine chemotherapy, presented with weakness, poor oral intake, and progressive dyspnea on adjuvant chemotherapy. He was admitted with severe sepsis with procalcitonin level was raised (0.44 ng/mL) secondary to bilateral bronchopneumonia and complicated urinary tract infection, along with multiorgan dysfunction, chemotherapy-induced enteropathy, pancytopenia, and severe hypoalbuminemia. Following standard intensive care management, his condition improved, and he was transferred to the general ward.

Fourteen days later, he was found to have severe anemia (hemoglobin 6.9 g/dL) with leukopenia and mild thrombocytopenia, attributed to chemotherapy-induced myelosuppression, nutritional deficiency, and occult GI blood loss. One unit of single donor PRBCs was transfused.

Within 3 hours of transfusion, the patient developed acute chest pain, severe hypoxemia (SpO2 60%), dyspnea, tachycardia (heart rate 103 bpm), hypotension (90/66) and bilateral lung crepitations, necessitating transfer to the medical intensive care unit.

Chest radiograph revealed bilateral diffuse symmetric air-space opacities without cardiomegaly or pleural effusion. Echocardiography demonstrated preserved systolic function (ejection fraction 60%) with no evidence of cardiogenic pulmonary edema, evidence of cardiac failure or volume overload. His NT-proBNP was within normal range [63 pg/mL]. Findings consistent with noncardiogenic pulmonary edema [1,2] and suggesting against transfusion-associated circulatory overload (TACO).

Based on clinical presentation, imaging findings, and exclusion of cardiac causes, a diagnosis of TRALI type II was established. It was type II as he had been treated for bilateral bronchopneumonia over previous 2 weeks (a risk factor for ARDS) but acute deterioration in respiratory status immediately after transfusion was believed to be due to the transfusion event [1]. Management consisted of respiratory support via high-flow nasal cannula. Negative to equal fluid balance was kept. The transfusion reaction reported to the blood bank.

After 6 hours the patient developed agitation, headache, abnormal behavior, blurred vision, nystagmoid eye movements, and gaze deviation. Brain MRI showed bilateral asymmetric T2/FLAIR hyperintensities at the gray–white matter junction of the frontoparietal lobes with associated gyral thickening. Diffusion-weighted imaging revealed hyperintensity without corresponding ADC reduction, indicating vasogenic rather than cytotoxic edema. No abnormal contrast enhancement or susceptibility blooming was observed. Flow void of dural venous sinuses are preserved with contrast opacification on contrast images.

Follow-up MRI at 4 week interval demonstrated near-complete resolution of the vasogenic edema, with minimal residual cortical T1 hyperintensity in bilateral frontal lobes suggestive of cortical laminar necrosis. Pulmonary infiltrates resolved within 4 days on chest radiograph with interval chest radiograph did not reveal any abnormality.

Discussion

TRALI is thought to result from a 2-hit mechanism: recipient inflammatory priming followed by donor-derived antibody-mediated neutrophil activation, leading to pulmonary endothelial injury and capillary leakage. In this case, the diagnosis was supported by the rapid onset of symptoms within 3 hours of transfusion, severe hypoxemia, bilateral infiltrates, absence of cardiac dysfunction, and eventual radiographic resolution. TACO was considered unlikely given the preserved echocardiographic systolic function. ARDS secondary to sepsis and/or pneumonia was also considered, given the patient's severe sepsis; however, the close temporal relationship to transfusion, favored TRALI, although a contribution from sepsis-related lung injury cannot be entirely excluded, given the well-recognized overlap between TRALI and ARDS of other etiologias in critically ill patients.

The patient's severe sepsis and pancytopenia are important confounders that merit direct consideration. Sepsis is itself a recognized cause of ARDS and can independently produce diffuse bilateral pulmonary infiltrates that are radiographically indistinguishable from TRALI; the temporal relationship to transfusion and the pattern of rapid resolution favored a transfusion-related process. Similarly, sepsis is an established independent precipitant of both PRES and sepsis-associated encephalopathy, through systemic cytokine release and endothelial dysfunction. We cannot entirely exclude a contribution from sepsis to the neurological findings; however, the closer temporal association of both the pulmonary and neurological findings with the transfusion episode, rather than with the onset or peak severity of sepsis, was felt to support transfusion as the principal trigger in this case.

Neurological manifestations likely reflect systemic endothelial dysfunction and blood–brain barrier disruption, producing vasogenic edema characteristic of PRES. The distribution in this case, involving the frontoparietal gray–white matter junction, is atypical for classic PRES, which most commonly affects the parieto-occipital lobes. Atypical variants involving the frontal lobes, watershed zones, brainstem, and basal ganglia are increasingly recognized, particularly in PRES associated with sepsis, cytotoxic or immunosuppressive therapy, and transfusion, where the proposed mechanism relates more to diffuse systemic endothelial and cytokine-mediated blood–brain barrier disruption than to the posterior-circulation-specific autoregulatory failure classically described in hypertensive PRES.

Given this atypical distribution, several alternative diagnoses were considered. Hypoxic-ischemic injury was considered unlikely given the absence of diffusion restriction and a pattern of gray–white junction involvement rather than the classic watershed or diffuse cortical ribboning pattern seen with global hypoxia. Toxic-metabolic encephalopathy was considered given the patient's chemotherapy and critical illness, but the focal pattern of signal change and its reversibility were more consistent with PRES than a global metabolic process. Sepsis-associated encephalopathy typically produces a more diffuse, symmetric white-matter change without the discrete gyral vasogenic oedema and gyral thickening described here. Cortical venous thrombosis was considered given the prothrombotic state associated with sepsis and pancytopenia, however venous sinuses are well opacified with contrast and the absence of restricted diffusion or hemorrhage argued against this diagnosis.

The residual cortical T1 hyperintensity on follow-up MRI, suggestive of laminar necrosis, is noteworthy, as this finding is not typical of fully reversible PRES and can indicate localized irreversible cortical injury, however on neurological examination no residual neurological deficit seen.

PRES has previously been reported in association with blood transfusion, particularly during rapid correction of severe chronic anemia [[3], [4], [5], [6]], and a shared pathophysiological pathway between transfusion-related PRES and TRALI-like mechanisms, involving rapid hematocrit and viscosity change together with endothelial injury, has been proposed [7]. We are not aware of a prior report describing concurrent TRALI and PRES following the same transfusion episode, and present this as an apparently rare, rather than definitively unreported, combination. This case underscores the importance of considering sepsis and pancytopenia as confounding contributors when either pulmonary or neurological complications follow transfusion in critically ill patients, and highlights the role of multimodality imaging in supporting the diagnosis and monitoring reversibility.

Conclusion

Concurrent TRALI and PRES is a rare but clinically significant transfusion-related complication, particularly in patients with confounding illness such as sepsis and pancytopenia. Awareness of this association, careful exclusion of alternative diagnoses, and multimodality imaging facilitate early diagnosis, appropriate management, and monitoring of reversibility.
