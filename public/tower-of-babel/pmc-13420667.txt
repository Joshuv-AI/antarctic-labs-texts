First Reported Extravascular-ICD Insertion in a Patient With Prior Sternotomy for Valvular Surgery
Authors: Guillaume Serzian, Fiona Ecarnot, Marc Badoz
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundThe extravascular implantable cardioverter-defibrillator (EV-ICD) is a novel nontransvenous system designed to prevent sudden cardiac death while avoiding complications associated with transvenous leads. Insertion after prior sternotomy is currently considered contraindicated and to our knowledge has not previously been reported.Case SummaryA 68-year-old man with prior Bentall procedure developed anthracycline-induced cardiomyopathy requiring ICD insertion for primary prevention. The need to preserve venous access and failed subcutaneous ICD screening limited the available options. Preprocedural imaging demonstrated a measurable retrosternal space without significant adhesions. EV-ICD insertion was successfully performed without resistance during retrosternal tunneling, and the lead was appropriately positioned. Defibrillation testing was successful, and the postoperative course was uneventful.DiscussionMedian sternotomy may result in variable retrosternal adhesions and anatomical changes. In this setting, insertion of a retrosternal defibrillation lead requires careful anatomical assessment and meticulous technique.Take-Home MessageEV-ICD insertion may be feasible in carefully selected patients with prior sternotomy.Key words: cardiac surgery, EV-ICD, retrosternal lead, sternotomy, valve prosthesis

History of Presentation

A 68-year-old man with NYHA functional class II dyspnea was referred to our hospital for electrophysiology evaluation and consideration of implantable cardioverter-defibrillator (ICD) insertion for primary prevention.

Past Medical History

A mechanical aortic valve was implanted in 2016 following a Bentall procedure for bicuspid aortic valve associated with ascending aortic dilation. A 25-mm mechanical valved conduit was subsequently implanted via a longitudinal median sternotomy. In 2023, the patient was treated for diffuse large B-cell lymphoma with an anthracycline-containing chemotherapy regimen. He developed chronic thrombosis of the right subclavian vein related to an implanted port catheter, which was removed. The patient subsequently developed toxic cardiomyopathy with severe impairment of left ventricular ejection fraction (25%-30%) despite optimal medical therapy. Other medical history included moderate chronic kidney disease and paroxysmal atrial fibrillation. The patient’s lymphoma had relapsed and required a new course of chemotherapy.

Investigations

Preoperative computed tomography scan showed a retrosternal space measuring approximately 5.6 mm at the narrowest point (Figure 1). No adhesion of the right ventricle or pericardium to the sternum was identified.

Preprocedural Computed Tomography Scan

Assessment of the retrosternal space and localization of the sternal wires. The retrosternal space is measurable, with a minimal distance between the heart and sternum of approximately 5 mm. No adhesion of the heart to the sternum is observed. There is only minimal replacement of fat by fibrosis.

Management

There was no indication for pacing or cardiac resynchronization therapy. According to the hematology team, the prognosis remained favorable, with a life expectancy of several years. After multidisciplinary discussion among hematologists, heart failure cardiologists, and electrophysiologists, insertion of a subcutaneous implantable cardioverter-defibrillator (S-ICD) was initially planned to preserve the last remaining subclavian venous access (in view of upcoming chemotherapy) and to minimize infectious risk. The procedure needed to be performed as early as possible to allow for adequate wound healing before initiation of chemotherapy, which was expected to last several months. However, S-ICD screening failed. Therefore, insertion of an extravascular implantable cardioverter-defibrillator (EV-ICD) was chosen.

The procedure was performed under general anesthesia with endotracheal intubation. After a transverse subxiphoid incision extending from the midline to the costal margin, access to the retrosternal space was obtained by finger dissection, without evidence of adhesions. The tunneling was performed along the left side of the sternum starting from the left portion of the incision to avoid proximity to the sternal wires, which could potentially generate lead noise. No resistance was encountered during very slow and cautious retrosternal tunneling. The position of the tunneling tool was verified in the anteroposterior view (0°) and the transverse view (90° LAO), and then the sheath position was confirmed by injection of iodinated contrast medium (Figure 2). The injection did not reveal any abnormal opacification of the pericardium, pleura, or heart. The lead was then deployed and assumed its expected configuration without constraint within the retrosternal space (Video 1). EV-ICD insertion was successfully completed despite post-surgical mediastinal anatomical changes. Defibrillation testing was successful with the first shock at 30 J. High- and low-voltage impedances were 77 Ω and 450 Ω, respectively. The pacing threshold was 10 V at 2 ms, and sensing amplitude was 1.4 mV without visible P waves.

The postoperative course was uncomplicated. Postoperative chest radiograph (Figure 3) showed no lead displacement. The patient did not experience chest pain or complications except for a small pocket hematoma that resolved spontaneously.

Anteroposterior Chest X-Ray Post-Insertion

Positioning of the lead and the generator is conventional on the postoperative x-ray film. The lead is distant from the sternotomy wires on this view. No complications are observed.

Outcome and Follow-Up

The patient was discharged on postoperative day 1. Remote monitoring at 1 month and 8 weeks showed no inappropriate therapy or abnormal lead noise (Figure 4), and device impedance remained stable. No clinical events have occurred since insertion.

Remote Monitoring Follow-Up at 8 Weeks Post-Insertion

Remote monitoring tracing at 8 weeks post-Insertion demonstrates no lead noise, with absent or minimally visible P waves (P/R ratio <1/10).

Discussion

The EV-ICD represents a major evolution in strategies for the prevention of sudden cardiac death by combining the advantages of a fully extravascular system with the ability to deliver antitachycardia pacing, a smaller device size, and greater longevity compared with the S-ICD. Patients with a history of cardiac surgery or sternotomy were excluded from the pivotal study.1 Sternotomy is therefore currently considered a contraindication to EV-ICD insertion.

A 2024 case report described an uncomplicated insertion in a patient with a history of mitral valve repair performed through a right-sided thoracotomy without sternotomy.2 However, median sternotomy represents a different clinical scenario. It exposes patients to the risk of retrosternal adhesions, which may vary in severity and lead to significant alterations of the retrosternal space. Adhesions range from partial replacement of retrosternal fat by fibrotic scar tissue to flattening of the cardiac structures against the posterior table of the sternum, with no visible space between the heart and the sternum.3 These anatomical changes may make retrosternal tunneling more challenging and increase the risk of periprocedural cardiac injury. The extent of adhesions and the proximity of cardiac structures to the sternum vary depending on surgical closure techniques and individual patient factors.4 However, according to Chen et al,5 the distance between the heart and the sternum after sternotomy, although reduced, generally remains measurable, suggesting the potential feasibility of substernal ICD lead insertion in patients post-sternotomy.5 Imaging is therefore essential pre-insertion in this setting to evaluate the size of the retrosternal space, the sternum–right ventricular distance, the presence and extent of adhesions with loss of the retrosternal fat plane replaced by fibrosis, and the possible presence of chronic pericardial effusion.

In addition, the standardized insertion technique, with careful advancement of the tunneling tool under fluoroscopic guidance, helps minimize the risk of mechanical complications. In patients with prior sternotomy, tunneling should be performed even more slowly and cautiously, with immediate interruption in case of any unusual resistance. Iodinated contrast medium has already been used to confirm the pleural position of the EV-ICD lead.6 Here, we chose to use iodinated contrast medium in addition to the usual procedure. It provides additional assistance in confirming the correct position of the sheath in case of doubt.

Furthermore, the presence of prosthetic material or anatomical alterations related to prior surgery may potentially influence lead parameters, including sensing, impedance, and defibrillation testing. This was not observed in our patient, in whom electrical parameters were comparable to those reported in the literature1,7 with successful defibrillation at the first shock at 30 J. Potential contact with sternal wires could theoretically damage the lead and result in mid- or long-term device dysfunction. For this reason, the lead was positioned considering the location of the sternal wires on preprocedural computed tomography imaging to maintain the greatest possible distance from them. At 1-month follow-up, lead parameters remained stable, and no electrical noise was detected. Close follow-up with remote monitoring is therefore essential in this context.

The role of the EV-ICD in the decision-making algorithm for patients with valvular heart disease requiring an ICD also warrants consideration. These patients are often relatively young, carry prosthetic valves, and frequently require lifelong anticoagulation. In this setting, avoiding a transvenous system may be particularly advantageous to reduce the risk of major complications related to endovascular leads.8 In addition, potential failure of S-ICD screening, the ability to deliver antitachycardia pacing in these patients at risk of monomorphic ventricular tachycardia, the smaller device profile, and longer battery longevity compared with the S-ICD—thereby potentially reducing exposure to wound-healing complications—represent additional advantages of the EV-ICD in this population.

Nevertheless, the safety of EV-ICD insertion after sternotomy cannot be generalized from a single case, as this represents to our knowledge the first published report. Each type of prior cardiac surgery carries specific procedural risks. For example, the presence of internal mammary artery grafts may introduce additional hazards during substernal tunneling. Further reports and longer follow-up will therefore be required to better establish the safety of EV-ICD insertion in patients with prior sternotomy.

Conclusions

In this patient with prior sternotomy for valvular heart surgery, EV-ICD insertion was successfully performed without complications. In this setting, insertion should be considered only after thorough anatomical assessment with preprocedural imaging and with appropriate procedural expertise. Larger datasets are required to better define the safety and efficacy of EV-ICD in patients with prior sternotomy.
