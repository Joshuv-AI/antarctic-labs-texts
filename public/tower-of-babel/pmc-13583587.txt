Differentiation of human embryonic stem cells into adrenal chromaffin-like cells with catecholamine markers, excitability, and Ca2+ signaling
Authors: Hilda Angélica Martínez-Becerril, Alejandro Salazar-Salgado, Óscar Javier Parada-Parra, Arturo Hernández-Cruz
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
SummaryHere, we present a protocol for differentiating human embryonic stem cells into adrenal chromaffin-like cells that express catecholaminergic markers (TH, DβH, PNMT, and CgA) and exhibit excitability and Ca2+ signaling. We describe the key steps for forming embryoid bodies (EBs) and subsequently directing them toward caudal neural commitment via Wnt/TGF-β regulation, followed by neural crest cell specification through BMP2 and FGF2 exposure, differentiation into chromaffin-like cells, and finally extended culture with BMP4 and glucocorticoids for physiological maturation.Subject areas: Developmental biology, Neuroscience, Stem Cells, Tissue Engineering

Before you begin

The protocol below describes the specific steps needed to differentiate H9 human embryonic stem cells (hESCs) into chromaffin-like cells; however, it could be used to differentiate any hESC line. Prepare all media before use to ensure optimal conditions for cell growth and differentiation. Prepare all media and reagents under sterile conditions. Recombinant proteins should be aliquoted according to manufacturer instructions and can be stored for up to 12 months at −20°C, except dexamethasone, which should be stored at −80°C. Supplement all factors in the medium just before use. If alternative reagents are used, validate them at the recommended concentration and adjust if necessary. Perform all steps under sterile conditions, as hESC cultures are not supplemented with antibiotics.

Innovation

Although hESC-derived chromaffin-like cells have been generated previously,1 their functional properties have not been examined. The hESC-CCs generated with this protocol not only express the expected catecholaminergic markers but also show distinct physiological properties, including action potential firing and intracellular Ca2+ signaling. This outcome was achieved through two key modifications. First, retinoic acid was added early (days 3–4) to drive caudalization and trunk neural crest specification.2 Also, BMP4 and glucocorticoids were applied at the last stage to push cells toward a more mature chromaffin phenotype.3 Together, these changes yield a more robust, replicable, and physiologically relevant human chromaffin cells (CCs) model for exploring their biology in health and disease.

Differentiation of CNPs into neural crest cells

Timing: 6 days

The next step directs caudalized neural progenitors toward NCPCs with sympathoadrenal potential via synergistic signaling, which occurs over 6 days, from day 5 to day 11.5

Expected outcomes

This protocol uses a defined 3D culture system to differentiate H9 human embryonic stem cells into chromaffin-like cells (hESC-CCs) over approximately 50 days (Figure 1). The expected outcome is a cell population in which approximately 20% display chromaffin-like morphology (Figure 3A, arrowheads; Figure 4, hESC-CCs; Figure 5H) and express biochemical markers. Each well in a 6-well ultra-low attachment plate yields roughly 4.5 million cells, or nearly 760,000 hESC-CCs.

(A) Morphological progression of cell cultures through the differentiation protocol. Representative brightfield images showing 2D cultures of cells dissociated from the spheres at different stages of the differentiation protocol. Between days 20 and 50, the appearance of chromaffin-like hESC-CCs is evident (arrowheads). Calibration bars: 50 and 200 μm.

(B) Summary of the main aspects of the different phases of the differentiation protocol.

Immunofluorescence confirms the expression of key catecholaminergic markers, including tyrosine hydroxylase (TH), dopamine β-hydroxylase (DβH), phenylethanolamine N-methyltransferase (PNMT), and chromogranin A (CgA) (Figures 5A–5D). In our hands, approximately 42.4 ± 2.4 % (SEM) of hESC-CCs are TH-positive (n= 53 individual quantified fields), of which 43.99 ± 4.58% (SEM) co-express DβH and 52.13% ± 4.3 % (SEM) are positive for PNMT, indicating the acquisition of both noradrenergic and adrenergic features. Figure S1 illustrates a positive control with bovine CCs expressing specific markers, Besides showing chromaffin-like morphology (Figures 4 and 5H), hESC-CCs exhibit functional properties similar to primary mammalian CCs2: They generate action potentials in response to suprathreshold depolarizing current injection (Figure 5F), and exhibit increasing voltage-gated sodium currents in response to depolarizing steps of voltage pulses (Figure 5G). They also show transient intracellular Ca2+ elevations in response to high-K+ stimulation (Figure 5E). These findings, together with the expression of CaV1.2 Figure 5D), support the presence of functional voltage-gated Ca2+ channels required for excitation-secretion coupling.7,8 Overall, our protocol yields hESC-CCs with molecular and functional properties similar to those of primary mammalian CCs, providing a robust in vitro model for studies of human sympathoadrenal biology and disease.

Limitations

This protocol is sensitive to the initial quality of hESC cultures, including colony morphology, viability, and pluripotency status. Proliferation and differentiation efficiency can vary across cell lines. Variations in timing, cell density, medium changes, or growth factor handling can affect both the differentiation efficiency and the maturation of hESC-CCs. In addition, the protocol requires a prolonged culture period, which may be too long for some research applications. While our hESC-CCs demonstrate consistent reproducibility across differentiation batches, functional validation by Ca2+ imaging and electrophysiology is highly recommended and may not be possible in every laboratory. Although the differentiation efficiency is acceptable, TH-positive cells represent 42.4% ± 2.4 (SEM) of the total DAPI-stained population. Within the TH-positive cells, 43.99 ± 4.58% also express DβH, and 52.13% ± 4.3% express PNMT, suggesting the development of both noradrenergic and adrenergic chromaffin-like cells. This cell heterogeneity may restrict large-scale applications. Despite these limitations, the protocol provides a human 3D differentiation system that yields hESC-CCs with molecular and functional features consistent with CC identity, making it useful for studies of human sympathoadrenal biology, disease modeling, and pharmacological testing.

Troubleshooting

Problem 1

hESC colonies detach from the well before reaching the confluence of 80%–90% necessary to initiate the differentiation protocol (Step 1). This problem usually reflects suboptimal starting cultures and incomplete Matrigel® coating.

Potential solution

Check the Matrigel concentration to ensure it is within the optimal range by verifying the batch-specific optimal dilution to account for batch-to-batch variability. Also, consider using a new hESC vial to ensure genetic integrity and optimal cellular health.

Problem 2

High rates of cell death or non-EB formation in the first 48 hours of differentiation after seeding in AggreWell™ 800 (Step 2). This problem could indicate poor hESC viability or quality, or a suboptimal plating density.

Potential solution

Do not exceed 5 min of TrypLE™ incubation. Dissociate cells gently, use wide-bore pipette tips for EB handling, and ensure that the medium is supplemented with 10 μM Y-27632 during the first 24 h after seeding to enhance survival. If this doesn’t resolve the problem, confirm that hESCs are highly viable and assess pluripotency integrity by immunostaining for relevant markers, such as NANOG, OCT2, or SOX4.

Problem 3

EBs show variable size after AggreWell™ 800 seeding (Steps 2 and 3). Low viability, inaccurate cell counts, uneven seeding, or insufficient centrifugation can compromise uniform EB formation.

Potential solution

Seed the higher density (10000 cells) per microwell and ensure cells are evenly distributed by pipetting them into each well. Then, retrieve the medium and slowly pipette it into the center.

Problem 4

Inefficient caudalization or poor transition to neural crest progenitor cells (Steps 4 and 5). Variation in growth factor activity, RA handling, incomplete medium exchange, or inconsistent culture density can reduce lineage specification.

Potential solution

Use validated aliquots of CHIR99021, SB431542, BMP2, FGF2, and RA; avoid repeated freeze-thaw cycles; protect RA from light; perform complete medium changes at the indicated times; and maintain consistent sphere density across wells.

Problem 5

Low yield or incomplete differentiation of TH-positive chromaffin-like cells (Step 6). This problem may result from variability in the starting hESC cultures, inconsistent sphere maintenance, or suboptimal exposure to BMP4, dexamethasone, and PMA during maturation.

Potential solution

Start with healthy, undifferentiated cultures, maintain uniform sphere density, closely follow the differentiation and maturation schedule, and refresh the medium and supplements at the recommended intervals, as small deviations can affect the final yield.

Problem 6

High background or weak immunofluorescence signal (Step 9). This problem can result from old fixative, insufficient blocking, excessive antibody concentration, or photobleaching.

Potential solution

Use fresh PFA, perform blocking and permeabilization as indicated, optimize primary and secondary antibody dilutions, include a no-primary-antibody control to assess background staining, and protect fluorophores from light during secondary antibody and DAPI incubations.

Problem 7

Weak or absent Ca2+ responses during Fluo-4 AM imaging (Step 10). This problem may result from poor dye loading, unhealthy cells, inadequate perfusion, or incorrect preparation of the high-K+ solution.

Potential solution

Prepare fresh Fluo-4 AM 2 μM working solution, ensure adequate loading time, verify continuous perfusion and stimulus delivery, and confirm the composition and pH of Tyrode’s and high-K+ solutions before imaging.
