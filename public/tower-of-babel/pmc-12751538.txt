Associations between sleep duration with depression and the mediating role of physical activity in patients with chronic respiratory diseases: evidence from three nationwide population-based studies
Authors: Yuanhao Tong, Huayu Li, Mingrui Duan, Qinyuan He, Ziang Gao
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundChronic obstructive pulmonary disease (COPD), asthma, and asthma-COPD overlap (ACO) are common chronic respiratory diseases that could cause severe mental disorders. Sleep duration and physical activity (PA) are related to depression in various diseases. However, their roles in patients with chronic respiratory diseases remain unknown.ObjectiveThis study was designed to investigate the potential relationship among sleep duration, PA, and depression in patients with respiratory disorders.MethodsData from CHARLS, KNHANES, and NHANES were collected. Generalized linear model (GLM) was applied to investigate the link among sleep duration, PA, and depression. Subgroup analyses were performed. Restricted cubic splines (RCS) were applied to investigate possible nonlinear correlations between sleep duration and depression. Additionally, mediation analysis assessed whether PA mediated the association between sleep duration and depression. Network analysis were performed to explore the core symptoms.ResultsSleep duration was significantly associated with depression. Gender-stratified analyses showed a stronger association with depression in males in CHARLS and NHANES. Subgroup analysis by respiratory disease subtypes (COPD, asthma, and ACO) revealed consistent inverse associations between sleep duration and depression across all cohorts. RCS revealed that sleep duration exhibited a nonlinear association with depression severity in three cohorts. Mediation analysis demonstrated that PA accounted for 24.1 % (CHARLS), and 25.5 % (KNHANES) of the sleep duration - depression relationship. According to strength centrality, node CESD3 ‘Felt sadness’ and node CESD2 ‘Trouble focus’ represented the most central positions of the network in CHARLS. In KNHANES and NHANES, node PHQ2 ‘Feeling down, hopeless’ and node PHQ6 ‘Guilt’ as the core symptoms.ConclusionThis study confirms that sleep duration is consistently linked to depression in COPD, asthma, and ACO patients across three countries. PA partially mediates this connection. Key depressive symptoms (e.g., sadness, guilt) were most strongly tied to sleep and activity patterns. Clinicians should consider both sleep and physical activity when managing depression in these patients. Further research is needed to confirm causality and refine interventions.Supplementary informationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12888-025-07592-8.Keywords: COPD, Asthma, Depression, Physical activity, Sleep, Mediating effect

Introduction

Chronic obstructive pulmonary disease (COPD) and asthma stand out as the leading chronic respiratory diseases, with global prevalence estimates of about 212 million and 262 million individuals, respectively [1, 2]. COPD involves ongoing airway obstruction and ranked as the third most common cause of death worldwide in 2019, featuring prominent symptoms such as difficulty breathing, chronic cough, and excessive mucus [3]. Asthma is a chronic inflammatory airway disease triggered by allergens, air pollution, exercise, or respiratory infections [4]. Although asthma can generally be controlled with medications, severe attacks may still result in hospitalization or emergency care [5]. Asthma-COPD overlap (ACO) refers to individuals exhibiting features of both conditions, typically presenting with symptoms of both diseases, more complex disease management, increased frequency of shortness of breath, and higher rates of hospitalization [6, 7].

Chronic respiratory diseases are frequently associated with an elevated risk of depression. Research has indicated that depression occurs in 10% to 42% of individuals with COPD, with rates potentially rising to 82% during episodes of acute flare-ups [8]. Furthermore, COPD has been found to heighten the risk of depression, while conversely, depression can exacerbate the risk of COPD flare-ups and contribute to the incidence of related conditions, such as emphysema or chronic bronchitis [9, 10]. About 16.7% of patients with asthma reported depression [11, 12].

Sleep is vital for everyone. Researchers have revealed that insomnia has a close connection with suicidal behaviors [13, 14]. Sleep duration and quality are frequently compromised by chronic respiratory diseases [15]. This deterioration can be attributed to physiological changes during sleep, such as suppression of respiratory drive, alterations in functional residual capacity, and shifts in ventilation-perfusion ratios, which result in hypoventilation accompanied by hypoxemia and hypercapnia [16]. In patients with COPD, these changes exacerbate the severity of hypoxemia, and the supine position further aggravates airflow obstruction, leading to increased gas trapping and ventilation-perfusion mismatch [17]. Similarly, asthma symptoms often worsen at night, driven by factors including airway inflammation, increased airway resistance, sleep-related reductions in lung volume, and narrowing of the upper airway in the supine position [18–22]. A study conducted among asthma patients demonstrated that sleep deprivation has detrimental effects on both objective and subjective daily symptoms and significantly impairs quality of life [23].

The influence of sleep on depression has been extensively documented in prior research [24]. Specifically, inappropriate sleep duration has been linked to an increased likelihood of both the onset and recurrence of depression. Short sleep duration exacerbates fatigue and intensifies negative emotions [25, 26]. In addition, in unhealthy long sleep duration is associated with decreased endorphin secretion and heightened sensitivity to stress stimuli [27]. Consequently, both short and long sleep durations contribute to an elevated risk of depression [28].

Physical activity (PA) was proved to effectively alleviate symptoms of depression [29]. Specifically, aerobic exercise, resistance training, or meditative practices have all been shown to reduce depression. Mechanistically, PA increases circulating levels of anti-inflammatory cytokines and neurotrophic factors, such as brain-derived neurotrophic factor (BDNF) [30]. Furthermore, exercise stimulates various neuroplastic processes implicated in depression, while also reducing inflammation and enhancing resilience to oxidative and physiological stress [31]. A Mendelian randomization study has confirmed that higher levels of PA, as indexed by objective accelerometer data, are associated with reduced odds of major depression [32]. Some studies have indicated that PA can improve insomnia as well as anxiety and depression scores, suggesting that it may act as a mediator between unhealthy sleep patterns and depression [33].

In current research, network analysis has emerged as an innovative method widely employed to examine the complex interactions among psychological symptoms. This approach conceptualizes psychological symptoms as nodes within a dynamic network, with edges representing correlations, making it particularly suitable for addressing multifaceted mental issues [34].

While previous studies have established associations between sleep duration, PA, and depression, the precise mechanisms governing these relationships remain poorly understood. To address this gap, our study sought to (1) examine the dose-response relationship between sleep duration and depression, and (2) assess the potential mediating role of PA in this association among patients with chronic respiratory diseases (COPD, asthma, and ACO) across three nationally representative datasets. Furthermore, we employed network analysis to examine the complex interrelationships between depressive symptoms, sleep duration, and PA. This approach enabled us to identify core symptoms within the network structure, offering valuable insights for developing targeted intervention strategies.

Materials & methods

Study population

The Chinese cohort was derived from China Health and Retirement Longitudinal Study (CHARLS), a nationally representative longitudinal survey targeting adults aged ≥ 45 years. Trained interviewers collected demographic, behavioral, and clinical data via computer-assisted personal interviews, supplemented by anthropometric and laboratory assessments. The 2015 CHARLS dataset was selected for this analysis due to its comprehensive coverage of comorbidities, psychological health, and objective biomarkers. Participants were excluded if they met any of the following criteria (1): over 85 years old (2), not self-reported a physician-confirmed diagnosis of COPD/asthma (3), incomplete depressive symptom scores (4), missing sleep duration or PA records, and (5) missing data on key covariates (sociodemographic factors, health behaviors, and anthropometric measurements). After exclusions, 2,187 eligible individuals were retained (Figure S1).

The Korean cohort utilized data from the Korea National Health and Nutrition Examination Survey (KNHANES) 2014. The KNHANES is a nationwide survey aimed at calculating statistics with national representation and reliability and consists of three survey sections, including medical screening, health interview, and nutrition interview. KNHANES employs a two-stage stratified cluster design, sampling households and districts to represent non-institutionalized citizens aged ≥ 1 year. Participants underwent health interviews, medical examinations, and nutrition assessments by multidisciplinary teams using mobile units. Written informed consent was obtained, with incentives provided post-survey. From 7,551 initial participants in 2014, exclusions included (1): unverified COPD/asthma status (2), incomplete depression metrics (3), missing sleep duration or PA data, and (4) absent covariates (e.g., sociodemographics, anthropometrics). The final analytic sample comprised 461 subjects (Figure S1).

The U.S. data were extracted from the National Health and Nutrition Examination Survey (NHANES) 2007 – 2018 database. NHANES combines interviews, physical exams, and laboratory tests. Exclusion parameters included (1): incomplete respiratory disease diagnostics (2), undocumented depressive symptom scores (3), insufficient sleep metrics and PA documentation, and (4) missing data on key covariates (sociodemographic factors, health behaviors, and anthropometric measurements). After applying exclusion filters, 1,858 individuals were incorporated into the analysis (Figure S1).

Definitions of COPD, asthma, and ACO

In the CHARLS database, COPD and asthma were defined based on self-reported physician diagnoses. Participants were classified as having COPD or asthma if they answered ‘Yes’ to the following questions: ‘Has a doctor ever diagnosed you with asthma?’ or ‘Has a doctor ever diagnosed you with COPD?’

In the KNHANES database, COPD was identified based on pulmonary function tests, including measurements of forced vital capacity (FVC, % predicted; mean ± SE), forced expiratory volume in one second (FEV1, % predicted; mean ± SE), and the calculated FEV1/FVC ratio (mean ± SE). Individuals with an FEV1/FVC ratio below 0.7 were diagnosed with COPD. Asthma was assessed separately through positive responses to a standardized survey item: ‘Has a physician ever diagnosed you with asthma?’

In the NHANES database, COPD was defined if any of the following criteria were met (1): an FEV1/FVC ratio less than 0.70 following β2-adrenergic bronchodilator use (2); a self-reported medical diagnosis of emphysema; or (3) a combination of smoking history, chronic bronchitis, and medication use (such as selective phosphodiesterase-4 inhibitors, mast cell stabilizers, leukotriene modifiers, or inhaled corticosteroids) in those aged 40 years or older. Asthma was defined by (1): a self-reported physician diagnosis (2); ongoing use of asthma medications; or (3) the absence of smoking history, chronic bronchitis, or emphysema in individuals under 40 years using specific respiratory drugs, including selective phosphodiesterase-4 inhibitors, mast cell stabilizers, leukotriene modifiers, or inhaled corticosteroids.

Individuals who fulfilled the criteria for both asthma and COPD were classified into the ACO group.

Data collection

Depression

In the CHARLS dataset, depressive symptoms were measured using the 10-item version Center of Epidemiologic Studies Depression Scale (CESD-10), a widely validated tool for detecting depression in Chinese adults [35]. The instrument consists of 10 questions; each rated on a 4-point scale from 0 (none) to 3 (almost every day). Total scores range from 0 to 30, with values of 10 or above suggesting clinically relevant depressive symptoms [36].

By comparison, NHANES and KNHANES applied the Patient Health Questionnaire-9 item (PHQ-9), an extensively validated 9-item questionnaire for depression diagnosis [37]. PHQ-9 scores span from 0 to 27, with higher scores reflecting higher severity. A cutoff value of 10 or greater is commonly employed, demonstrating a sensitivity of 88% and a specificity of 85% in identifying major depressive disorder (MDD) against structured clinical evaluation [38].

In this study, the term ‘depression’ is used to represent varying symptom levels rather than a strict clinical diagnosis. For analyses, participants with COPD, asthma, or ACO were dichotomized into two categories: those without depression and those meeting the threshold for depression.

Sleep duration

In CHARLS, KNHANES, and NHANES, sleep duration was assessed using self-reported questionnaires.

Physical activity

In CHARLS, PA was assessed through a questionnaire adapted from the short-form International Physical Activity Questionnaire (IPAQ) [39]. Participants reported the type (vigorous, moderate, walking), duration, and frequency of activities in a typical week. Unlike IPAQ, this version collected PA over ‘a usual week’, did not include sedentary behavior, and used categorical time intervals rather than continuous values. For analysis, the midpoint of each time category was applied, and metabolic equivalents (METs) were assigned following IPAQ scoring guidelines: 8.0 for vigorous activity (e.g., running, farming), 4.0 for moderate activity (e.g., brisk walking, tai chi), and 3.3 for light activity (e.g., casual walking). Only activities lasting ≥ 10 minutes were included [36]. Although IPAQ was originally designed for adults under 70 years, previous studies have confirmed its validity among older populations [40].

In the KNHANES, PA was measured using the Global Physical Activity Questionnaire (GPAQ) developed by the WHO [41]. Energy expenditure was expressed as MET-minutes per week, calculated by multiplying activity duration by intensity (vigorous = 8 METs; moderate = 4 METs). Activities were classified by domain—work, leisure, and commuting—and then summed to estimate total PA.

In NHANES, PA data were also collected using a GPAQ-based survey. Participants reported the type, frequency, and duration of activities lasting ≥ 10 minutes during the past 30 days, covering leisure, transportation (walking, cycling), and occupational domains. Moderate activities were defined as those causing a slight increase in breathing or heart rate, while vigorous activities caused a substantial increase. Both paid/unpaid work and recreational exercise were included.

Covariates

In CHARLS and KNHANES, potential confounders included sociodemographic variables (age, gender, residence, education, marital status), health behaviors (alcohol consumption, tobacco consumption, body mass index [BMI]), and chronic conditions (hypertension, diabetes, heart disease, dyslipidemia, and other common illnesses). BMI was calculated as weight (kg)/height (m2), with weight and height measured using standardized equipment.

For NHANES, covariate selection was informed by prior studies and conceptual relevance. Data were obtained from standardized questionnaires and included age, gender, race/ethnicity, education, marital status, poverty income ratio (PIR), alcohol consumption, tobacco consumption, and chronic conditions (hypertension and diabetes, heart disease, dyslipidemia, and other common illnesses). Race/ethnicity was classified as Mexican American, non-Hispanic Black, non-Hispanic White, other Hispanic, and other races. Education was grouped as less than or equal to middle school, completed high school, above high school. Marital status was categorized as married, never married, or other. Alcohol intake was defined by reporting ≥ 12 drinks per year. Smoking status was determined by lifetime cigarette use (≥100) and current smoking behavior. Chronic conditions were identified by self-report.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analyses were performed in R (version 4.4.2). Statistical significance was defined as a two-tailed p-value below 0.05. For continuous variables, results were summarized as means ± standard deviations or medians (interquartile ranges), based on their distribution. Categorical variables were expressed as counts and percentages. Group comparisons between participants with and without depression were conducted using Chi-square (χ2) tests for categorical data. Normally distributed continuous variables were compared via ANOVA, while non-parametric alternatives (Mann-Whitney U tests) were applied to skewed data.

To investigate links between sleep duration, PA, and depression, generalized linear model (GLM) analysis was applied. Given that sleep duration, PA, and depression scores were treated as continuous variables and exhibited mild skewness rather than count distributions, the GLM was specified with a Gaussian family and an identity link function. Robust standard errors were applied to account for potential heteroscedasticity. Gender-specific analyses were conducted to explore potential moderation effects. Subgroup analysis was performed for participants with COPD, Asthma, and ACO to determine whether the relationships among the three variables differed across different respiratory disease subtypes. Restricted cubic splines (RCS) were applied to investigate possible nonlinear correlations between sleep duration and depression. Additionally, mediation analysis assessed whether PA mediated the correlation between sleep duration and depression. Bootstrap resampling (5000 iterations) was used to estimate indirect effects, the proportion mediated, and their significance.

Network analysis was performed to explore the interactions among concrete depressive symptoms, sleep duration, and PA. Using the qgraph package, an undirected correlation network with a spring layout was constructed, positioning highly interconnected nodes toward the center. A Least Absolute Shrinkage and Selection Operator (LASSO) network was applied, leveraging statistical regularization to reduce false connections and generate a streamlined network structure [42]. We calculated three standardized centrality indices: strength (sum of connected edge weights), betweenness (frequency as a bridge in shortest paths), and closeness (average proximity to other nodes) using the centralityPlot function from the qgraph package to evaluate node importance. Given that closeness and betweenness rely on global network topology and exhibit lower reliability in limited datasets, we prioritized node strength due to its robustness in reflecting direct associations and reduced sensitivity to structural noise [43]. The higher the strength value, the more significant the node’s influence within the network. To assess network reliability, bootstrapping was performed via the bootnet package. A case-dropping subset bootstrap approach (1000 iterations, 8 cores) was used to determine the correlation stability (CS) coefficient, which indicates the maximum case exclusion proportion while preserving a 0.7 correlation in 95% of samples [44]. A CS coefficient above 0.25 is deemed adequate, though values surpassing 0.5 are ideal. Additionally, non-parametric bootstrapping (2000 iterations, 8 cores) estimated 95% confidence intervals (CIs) for edge weights, with narrower CIs reflecting greater precision.

Results

Participant characteristics

Demographic and clinical characteristics of patients with COPD/asthma/ACO from CHARLS (n = 2,187), KNHANES (n = 461), and NHANES (n = 1,858) are presented in Tables S1. In CHARLS, patients with depression had higher BMI (p = 0.028) and lower alcohol consumption (p = 0.027). KNHANES participants with depression exhibited lower education levels (p = 0.014). The NHANES data revealed broader disparities among individuals with depression, including differences in age, BMI, PIR, gender, race, education, marital status, hypertension, and diabetes (all p < 0.05). Notably, shorter sleep duration and reduced PA were consistently linked to depression in all three cohorts (all p < 0.05).

As shown in Table 1, sleep duration was inversely associated with depression across all cohorts. After adjustment for confounders, the association remained significant in CHARLS (β = −0.473, 95% CI: −0.617, −0.328), KNHANES (β = −1.104, 95% CI: −1.277, −0.931), and NHANES (β = −0.263, 95% CI: −0.407, −0.118) (all p < 0.001). In CHARLS and NHANES, the association was stronger in males. Subgroup analyses showed consistent inverse associations between sleep duration and depression across COPD, asthma, and ACO subtypes in all cohorts (Fig. 1A). In CHARLS, adjusted β coefficients were −0.470 (95% CI: −0.641, −0.299) for COPD, −0.393 (95% CI: −0.801, −0.016) for asthma, and −0.552 (95% CI: −0.891, −0.212) for ACO (all p < 0.05), while similar patterns were observed in KNHANES and NHANES. Results of RCS analysis

To further elucidate the dose-response relationship between sleep duration and depressive symptoms, we conducted fully adjusted RCS analysis across three national cohorts. Notably, sleep duration exhibited a nonlinear association with depression severity in all cohorts (all p < 0.001). Specifically, in the CHARLS cohort, CESD-10 scores progressively decreased with longer sleep duration. Conversely, in the KNHANES cohort, PHQ-9 scores declined with increasing sleep duration but plateaued beyond a threshold of 8.46 hours. The NHANES cohort demonstrated a U-shaped relationship: PHQ-9 scores initially decreased, reaching a nadir at 7.67 hours of sleep, and subsequently increased with further sleep extension (Fig. 2).

In CHARLS, the total effect of sleep duration on depression was −0.473 (95% CI: −0.633, −0.308), with a substantial proportion mediated by PA of 0.241 (95% CI: 0.158, 0.389). The KNHANES data revealed an even stronger mediation effect, with a total effect of −1.104 (95% CI: −1.309, −0.911) and a mediation proportion of 0.455 (95% CI: 0.372, 0.545). In contrast, the mediation effect was not significant in NHANES. Gender-stratified analysis revealed important differences in mediation patterns. In CHARLS, the mediation proportion was slightly higher in males (β = 0.255, 95% CI: 0.144, 0.401) compared to females (β = 0.221, 95% CI: 0.112, 0.370). Conversely, KNHANES demonstrated a stronger mediation effect in females (β = 0.540, 95% CI: 0.412, 0.660) than in males (β = 0.405, 95% CI: 0.306, 0.505).

Network analysis of depressive symptoms, sleep duration, and PA

To delineate the concrete depressive symptoms and their interrelationships with sleep duration and PA, we constructed network models across all three cohorts (Fig. 4). Centrality of all nodes was illustrated in Figs. S2 - S4. In CHARLS, node CESD3 ‘Felt sadness’ and node CESD2 ‘Trouble focus’ exhibited the most central positions in the network, as evidenced by their highest strength centrality. In KNHANES and NHANES, according to the strength centrality, both cohorts identified node PHQ2 ‘Feeling down, hopeless’ and node PHQ6 ‘Guilt’ as the core symptoms. Figures S5 - S7 illustrated that the edge weights from the original sample aligned with those in the bootstrapped samples, while 95% CIs derived from bootstrapping were tight, suggesting strong reliability in the network model. Discussion

The relationships between sleep duration, PA, and depression across three countries

Chronic respiratory diseases and the comorbidity result in substantial economic burden and mental damage, highlighting urgent need for increased attention and proper management. In our study, three nationally representative cohorts were utilized: CHARLS of China, NHANES of America, and KNHANES of Korea. In the analysis across these three national cohorts, a significant negative correlation was observed between sleep duration and depression among patients with chronic respiratory diseases, and a similar significant negative correlation was found between PA and depression, except for asthma patients in America. The results align with previous studies conducted by Zhai et al. and Roberts et al. in total population [26, 45].

In the American cohort, PA showed no correlation with depression among asthma patients. This may potentially be due to the episodic nature of asthma, characterized by acute attacks that onset and resolve abruptly [46]. Repeated instances of asthma exacerbations triggered during PA may heighten patients’ anxiety and apprehension toward exercise, thereby diminishing its psychological benefits [12]. In contrast, COPD typically follows a prolonged disease trajectory, with patients often being older and having adapted to coexisting with the condition [47]. Appropriate levels of PA were proved to enhance lung function in COPD patients, while simultaneously improving social engagement and self-efficacy [9].

PA mediates the correlation between sleep duration and depression in the Chinese and Korean population

Significant mediation by PA was observed in both Chinese and Korean populations, whereas no significant mediation effect was detected in the American cohort. After adjusting for covariates, the mediating effect coefficients for PA were −0.114 (China) and −0.502 (Korea), accounting for 24.1% and 45.5% of the total effect, respectively. In terms of gender-specific analysis, in the Chinese cohort, the total effect of sleep duration on depression was stronger in males than in females. Conversely, in the Korean cohort, the association was more pronounced in females compared to males. The proportion of the total effect mediated by PA varied by gender: in China, PA explained 25.5% of the effect in males and 22.1% in females, while in Korea, the mediated proportions were 40.5% (males) and 54.0% (females).

This finding is interesting and thought-provoking. In Korea, the total effect of sleep duration on depression and the mediating effect of PA are significantly higher than in China. Comparatively, Korean society is characterized by high work pressure and extended working hours, leading to generally shorter sleep durations, which amplify the adverse effects of sleep on depression [8]. In contrast, certain Chinese traditions, such as afternoon napping, may attenuate this effect. Besides, Koreans may exhibit greater awareness of mental health issues due to public health campaigns, increasing sensitivity to depressive symptoms, and thereby strengthening the observed associations [48]. In China, however, mental health problems may be stigmatized, resulting in underestimating of depressive symptoms and consequently weakening the detected effects [19]. Additionally, the mediating role of PA may be more pronounced in Korea, where exercise is more prevalent, whereas CHARLS primarily involved retired populations with lower activity levels and weaker exercise habits, leading to a weaker observed mediating effect of PA [49].

Gender-specific findings indicated that Chinese males and Korean females exhibited higher total and mediating effects. In the Chinese cohort, males may experience greater occupational stress and familial responsibilities, which could contribute to sleep deprivation and increased susceptibility to depression among those with chronic respiratory diseases, potentially amplifying the impact of shorter sleep duration [50]. In Korean females, factors such as hormonal fluctuations and psychosocial stressors may increase vulnerability to depressive symptoms and sleep disturbances [51]. Additionally, some evidence suggests that Korean women may more frequently engage in physical activity as a coping strategy, including yoga or walking groups, which could mitigate depressive symptoms [52].

However, PA did not demonstrate mediating effects of in NHANES populations. There may be some possible explanations. First, cultural differences, such as the degree of destigmatization of depressive symptoms, self-perceived sleep duration and reporting habits, and the way daily living is self-assessed, may influence the assessment of mediation effects [53]. Second, differences among the three cohorts in sleep duration, physical activity levels, and disease diagnoses inevitably introduce biases. Moreover, potential confounders such as exposure to air pollution, pain and anxiety levels, and medication use were not controlled for in the data, which could weaken the mediating effect [54].

Dose-response association of sleep duration with depression severity

RCS analysis revealed distinct dose-response relationships between sleep duration and depressive symptoms across Chinese, Korean, and US cohorts, underscoring the influence of cultural and biological factors. Specifically, the linear decrease in CESD-10 scores with longer sleep duration in the CHARLS cohort suggested that extending sleep may be particularly beneficial in reducing depression severity among Chinese individuals, potentially due to high levels of occupational stress and familial responsibilities that exacerbate sleep deprivation. In contrast, the plateau effect in the KNHANES cohort beyond 8.46 hours and the U-shaped curve in the NHANES cohort, with a nadir at 7.67 hours, indicated an optimal sleep threshold that varies by population, possibly modulated by lifestyle factors such as PA or hormonal influences.

Several studies have demonstrated that short and long sleep durations were associated with multiple adverse health outcomes. Zhai et al. found that short and long sleep duration were significantly associated with the risk of depression using meta-analysis [45]. Winer and colleagues reported that extreme sleep duration—such as 6 hours or less, or 9 hours or more—was related to cognitive impairments, depression, and elevated body mass index [55]. In mechanical aspect, through large-scale genome-wide association studies, researchers discovered that short sleep duration is closely associated with genes such as FOXP2, MAD1L1, and TCF4, while long sleep duration is linked to genes including PTPLB and LMO7. Extreme sleep durations exhibit shared genetic risks with depression, schizophrenia, and metabolic disorders, and Mendelian randomization analysis confirms that short sleep duration increases the risk of depression and that a bidirectional causal relationship exists with long sleep duration [56]. Short sleep can provoke inflammatory responses and HPA-axis activation, which are linked to mood disturbances, and may be particularly deleterious in individuals with CRD due to nocturnal dyspnea and hypoxemia [57]. Long sleep, by contrast, may signal higher disease burden, multimorbidity, reduced daytime activity, or undiagnosed sleep disorders, all of which can contribute to depressive symptoms [58]. Similar to our findings, Li et al. has reported that in the U.S. population, sleep duration has a ‘U’-type association with depression [59]. The varying shapes across CHARLS, KNHANES, and NHANES may probably arise from cohort-specific distributions of age, comorbidity, obesity, medication use (including antidepressants and sleep aids), smoking and alcohol use, SES, and cultural norms around sleep and mood reporting. Our study extends this relationship to respiratory disease contexts, highlighting the need for culturally tailored interventions. However, given potential biases that were not addressed, further studies with more rigorous and in-depth examinations are essential.

Network analysis revealed the core symptoms

The network analysis revealed distinct core depressive symptoms across the three cohorts, highlighting the heterogeneity in depressive symptomology among patients with COPD/asthma/ACO. In the CHARLS cohort, ‘Felt sadness’ and ‘Trouble focusing’ emerged as the most central nodes, suggesting that affective and cognitive symptoms may be particularly interconnected in Chinese populations, potentially exacerbated by sociocultural factors such as occupational stress and familial responsibilities. In contrast, both KNHANES and NHANES identified ‘Feeling down, hopeless’ and ‘Guilt’ as central, which aligns with American and Korean conceptualizations of depression, emphasizing emotional despair and self-reproach. The strong interrelationships between these core symptoms and sleep duration/PA underscore the amplifying effect of sleep deprivation on depression, consistent with our hypothesis that short sleep duration exacerbates depressive risks in chronic respiratory diseases. Moreover, the high accuracy of the network models supports the robustness of the findings, providing a reliable basis for understanding symptom dynamics.

The central roles of specific depressive symptoms in our network models can be attributed to several underlying mechanisms. Biologically, chronic inflammation from COPD and asthma may disrupt sleep architecture and exacerbate emotional dysregulation, with symptoms like ‘Felt sadness’ acting as hubs that propagate distress through interconnected pathways. Psychologically, cognitive symptoms such as ‘Trouble focusing’ could stem from rumination, a common feature in depression amplified by sleep deprivation. Future mechanistic studies, such as those incorporating biomarkers or longitudinal data, could further elucidate these pathways. Consistent with the findings of this study, a network analysis of anxiety and depression among Chinese elderly individuals indicated that ‘Felt sadness’ represents the central symptom, exhibiting significant differences from populations in other countries and underscoring the unique depressive symptom network in Chinese older adults [60] In a network analysis examining sleep quality, depression, and anxiety in Chinese nurses, the most central symptom was identified as ‘Depressed affect’, with insomnia emerging as the most common bridge symptom [61].

These findings have important implications for clinical practice and public health, particularly in managing depression among patients with chronic respiratory diseases. From a network theory perspective, treating the central symptoms can help improve related psychopathology. For ‘Felt sadness’, this core symptom can be addressed with an intervention including emotional regulation and behavioral activation, such as emotion regulation techniques, behavioral activation, and emotional-cognitive integration from cognitive-behavioral therapy, supplemented by sleep hygiene strategies and physical activity promotion [55]. As to the ‘Guilt’ symptom, the intervention mainly based on cognitive reconstruction, self-forgiveness, and self-compassion training can be considered, combined with problem-solving therapy, to help patients reduce self-blame and guilt, thereby lowering other depressive symptoms. Moreover, the interventions should be integrated with sleep management and exercise to achieve synergistic effects [62]. Given the cultural variations observed, tailored approaches are essential. For instance, exercise-based interventions could be adapted for Chinese and Korean populations to mitigate the exacerbating effects.

Strengths and limitations

As far as we know, this study pioneers in exploring the association between sleep duration, PA, and depression in patients with chronic respiratory diseases. We employed multiple methods to explore the potential correlation and mediating effect. The results provide evidence of the association between sleep duration, depression, and PA could mediate this link. Furthermore, core symptoms were dug out, which could be potential targets for clinical intervention.

This study has several limitations. First, COPD and asthma diagnoses varied across cohorts: CHARLS relied on self-reports, while KNHANES and NHANES used objective measures. This heterogeneity in included criteria inevitably introduces bias. Self-reported diagnoses may be subject to recall bias and information bias, whereas objective diagnostic standards are more objective and standardized. In addition, physical activity and sleep duration were self-reported. Future research could enhance accuracy by employing objective measures, such as polysomnography and actigraphy. Notably, ‘depression’ reflects symptom severity assessed by CESD-10 or PHQ-9, which are screening tools rather than clinical diagnoses. Thus, findings pertain to depressive symptom severity, not confirmed depression. Third, the samples from the three countries differed in socio-cultural backgrounds, which may have influenced PA, sleep, and mental health. Moreover, some potential confounders, including family history of depression, diet, metabolism, hormones, and genes were not measured. Despite adjusting for known factors, it remains difficult to fully control for variables like psychosocial stress, medication details, and disease severity. Therefore, results should be interpreted cautiously. Last, this study’s cross-sectional analysis has limitations for causal inference: exposure, mediator, and outcome measures are captured at the same time point, lacking clear temporal ordering. Future studies should prioritize longitudinal designs, conducting cross-lagged mediation analyses on time-series data or multi-time-point structural equation modeling, to better control for temporal order and strengthen causal inference. Nevertheless, the consistent association between sleep duration and depression risk across Chinese, Korean, and U.S. cohorts suggests a potential shared underlying mechanism, providing valuable evidence for future research. Therefore, randomized controlled trials testing PA interventions in high-risk groups could provide evidence for reducing depression burden in patients with chronic respiratory diseases.

Conclusion

This study reveals the correlation between sleep duration and depression in patients with COPD, asthma, and ACO. Physical activity could mediate this correlation in the Chinese and Korean population and show gender heterogeneity. Dose-response association of sleep duration with depression severity is distinct among the three cohorts. Network analysis reveals core symptoms, and focused interventions can more effectively manage depression in patients with chronic respiratory diseases.
