N2G calibrator: a cross-subject adversarial learning framework for neural signal-driven gait tracking in Parkinson’s disease
Authors: Jin Woo Choi, Helen M. Bronte-Stewart
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Adaptive deep brain stimulation (aDBS) has enabled machine learning models to track motor states from neural signals with improved accuracy, aiming to provide electrical stimulation accordingly. Such data-driven techniques necessitate extensive user-specific, synchronized kinematic and neural data collection involving repetitive tasks and additional sensors to quantify continuous movements, due to variations in neural signals among individuals. In this study, we introduce the Neural-to-Gait Calibrator Framework (N2GCF), a cross-subject deep learning framework that leverages collective neural data to track gait performance of users with Parkinson’s disease. Our framework utilizes domain adversarial learning to map a target user’s unlabeled neural features with labeled data from other individuals, removing the need for synchronous gait recording systems thereby allowing personalized model calibration outside equipped clinical settings. The framework’s ability was demonstrated through a significant reduction in error rates compared to models trained with data from other individuals without calibration, with its architecture achieving performance comparable to that of models trained directly with labeled target data. Our N2GCF provides an effective biomarker reflecting gait performance for aDBS systems despite being trained with only unlabeled neural signals for the target, enabling the model to be established in any location where stepping in place can be performed.

Introduction

Adaptive deep brain stimulation (aDBS) has recently gained attention as an emerging treatment for Parkinson’s disease (PD) due to its ability to sense neural signals in real time while simultaneously providing electrical stimulation to the brain through implanted leads1–6. Unlike continuous DBS, which provides a constant amount of stimulation throughout therapy, aDBS adjusts stimulation based on predicted symptoms as reflected by neural signals4–7. By tailoring stimulation to effectively handle motor symptoms, aDBS tends to reduce unnecessary stimulation thereby lessening potential side effects such as speech problems8–10.

One of the actively researched symptoms for aDBS is gait5,11,12. Previous studies have shown that local field potentials (LFPs) retrieved from the subthalamic nucleus (STN) contain information related to gait symptoms in people with PD (PWP). With signals in the beta frequency range (13–30 Hz) being the key neural features reflecting gait-related symptoms such as freezing of gait and bradykinesia13–15, current algorithms driving the aDBS utilize oscillatory neural characteristics to predict symptom severity and adjust stimulation accordingly. On many instances, beta power and beta bursts have been used with single- or dual-threshold algorithms to increase or decrease stimulation amplitude5,16,17. Due to the variations of such neural characteristics between individuals, many of the parameters and thresholds related to these neural features are determined manually by experts through data visualizations18,19. These approaches include assessing the movement performance of PWP and manually mapping it to visually identified neural signal characteristics, and repeated trial-and-error testing is typically needed in order to effectively configure aDBS therapy.

Recent literature has applied machine learning methods to provide more personalized therapy for individuals while also reducing the efforts required for setting up aDBS20. These data-driven approaches tend to map neural signals to quantified kinematic traits using the data acquired from individuals, aiming to optimize parameters that had to be determined manually. Prior studies have focused on developing data-driven techniques related to gait. For instance, in our recent study, a deep learning-based regression model, N2GNet, was proposed to translate neural signals into gait performance that was quantified by measuring weight shifts during repetitive stepping in place tasks performed on force plates21. Deep learning has also been employed to correlate neural signals with activation of leg muscles, aiming to identify dysfunctional gait during walking22. Along with other methods linking neural signals to various movement symptoms3,23–25, machine learning approaches have shown their potential to provide a broader range of relevant neural inputs for aDBS.

Although data-driven approaches have shown feasibility toward aDBS therapy, these algorithms require considerable amounts of synchronized neural and kinematic data acquired specifically from the target user before they can be effectively employed21,22,26,27. Particularly for algorithms involving neural signals, it is challenging to apply previously collected data from other individuals to a new target user due to the variability in neural characteristics across individuals28,29. Furthermore, the data collection process requires extra sensors or measurement systems such as accelerometers, electromyography, dynamometric chairs, or force plates, to record kinematic data synchronously with neural signals21,22,30,31. These requirements may hinder individuals from effectively using machine learning methods to establish or update their personalized aDBS therapy, especially in urgent scenarios or at-home settings where only the implanted aDBS system is available, with no supplementary sensors to simultaneously capture kinematic traits.

In this study, we present the Neural-to-Gait Calibrator Framework (N2GCF), a cross-subject deep learning approach based on domain adversarial training that selectively aligns neural and gait data from source individuals with a target user’s neural signals for personalized gait tracking. Our calibrator framework does not require any gait measurements to be synchronously recorded with the neural signals from the target user, allowing them to establish a personalized gait tracking deep learning model simply by simulating walking in place. With our recurrent selective source scheme that utilizes maximum mean discrepancy (MMD), the framework aimed to continuously track and guide its feature extractor to rely more on sources whose extracted neural features closely resembled those of the target individual throughout its training process. We utilized one of the deep learning models introduced from the previous study as the baseline model for our framework, which mapped STN LFPs to gait performance using simultaneously recorded neural data and quantified weight shifts collected during stepping in place tasks21. In the current study, our framework was trained and evaluated under two different conditions: 1) an unsupervised approach where neural signals and quantified gait performance labels from source participants are used while only neural signals from the target participant are used to calibrate and finalize the regression model, and 2) a semi-supervised approach where gait performance labels from the target participant are involved only in model selection for its final establishment. By evaluating the performance of our calibrator framework under the two distinct conditions, we aimed to evaluate the framework’s practical applicability in real-life scenarios and quantify its capability in reducing feature discrepancies independently of the strategies used for model selection.

Methods

Participants

The data used for this study are a subset of the data from a previous study21. Data collected from seventeen participants diagnosed with PD, who were able to perform all three stepping in place (SIP) trials without exhibiting gait freezing throughout the entire duration of any trial, were included in the study. Participants had an investigative neurostimulator (Activa PC+S, Medtronic, PLC) implanted that was connected to bilateral DBS leads placed in the STNs. The study was approved by the Stanford University Institutional Review Board (25916 and 30880) and by the Food and Drug Administration with an Investigational Device Exemption (G130186). All participants provided written consent prior to their study participation, and the study was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki.

Data description

The data from each participant included three chronologically ordered SIP trials, where the trials were conducted in separate visits with at least a 1-month gap in between (mean interval: 5 months between the earliest and intermediate trials, 7 months between the intermediate and latest trials). Participants performed each SIP trial in an off-medication state and without DBS stimulation. Prior to the SIP trial, the stimulation was in the OFF state for at least 15 min, and the participants were asked to stand on the force plate system while wearing a safety harness.

A single SIP trial included one continuous period of repetitive stepping in place. In the initial phase of the SIP trial, participants were asked to stand still on the two force plates and maintain a motionless state. Upon receiving a start cue, participants were instructed to begin stepping alternately in place on the force plates at their own comfortable pace (Fig. 1a). Dataset formation

The LFP signals retrieved from the participants were recorded with a 422 Hz sampling rate and were downsampled to 211 Hz. A band-pass filter between 8 and 100 Hz frequency range was applied to the LFP signals to exclude frequency bands that may contain excessive noise, while still preserving a broad frequency range for the deep learning model to consider. The force plate data were acquired using either Neurocom (Neurocom Inc, Clackamas, OR, USA), which was measured with a 100 Hz sampling rate, or Bertec (Bertec Corporation, Columbus, OH, USA), which was sampled at 1000 Hz but downsampled to 100 Hz for consistency with the Neurocom data. The force plate data were low-pass filtered at 2 Hz to remove possible noise not involved with weight shifts associated with alternate stepping.

Gait performance was quantified using weight shift measurements, following methods similar to those described in the previous study21. The force plate data were initially rescaled by dividing the values by the participant’s weight, estimated from the data retrieved while the participant was standing still on the two force plates. Data from the two force plates at each sampling time point were then integrated into one value by considering the greater one and then symmetrically reflecting values from one force plate around the value equivalent to half the weight of the participant. Quantified changes in the resulting sequence of values over a specific time window were used to represent participants’ weight shifts for the corresponding time interval. These procedures were done considering possible variations in participants’ weight across different visits, as well as limitations of the force plate in tracking weight shifts when one foot is entirely off the plate. The resulting weight shift outcomes, which we refer to as gait performance, and LFP signals were used to train and evaluate our framework (Fig. 1b).

The dataset from each SIP trial was structured such that the last 5 s of the LFP signals at each time point served as data, and the weight shift value of the last 2-s window at the corresponding time point served as a label for each sample. To balance the amount of data between the multi-participant source and the single-participant target, source data was sampled at every 1-s stride while target data was sampled with a 0.1-s stride. Hence, the data structure simulates tracking of gait performance using the most recent 5 s of neural data, with the output generated at 0.1-s intervals (Fig. 1c).

Framework training and evaluation protocol

Training and evaluation of our framework were performed in a cross-subject manner and through cross-validation, where each participant served once as the target while the remaining participants served as sources. The framework was trained on the labeled first trial data of source participants, guided by the unlabeled calibration set which was the second trial data of the target participant. Performance of the trained framework was measured through the labeled calibration set, as well as the follow-up set which is the target participant’s third trial data collected after a substantial interval following the second trial (Fig. 1d). Thus, from the framework’s perspective, the calibration data are not entirely new as their unlabeled portion was considered during training, whereas the follow-up data represent entirely unseen data for measuring performance.

To match the ranges between training and calibration data, labels across the three trials were rescaled such that the highest value in the first trial was set to 1 for each source participant, whereas the highest value from the second trial was mapped to 1 for the target participant. When applying domain adversarial training, pairs of source training data and target calibration data were used to learn the framework’s parameters, and were also used for MMD-related approaches of our framework. Each data sample from the sources was paired with a data sample from the target in order to match the number of samples between each source and target pair, simplifying the training process. These sample pairs were determined in a randomized manner and were constantly renewed at each epoch, indicating that the sample pairs would change from epoch to epoch. The performance of our framework was evaluated by computing the Mean Absolute Error (MAE) and Mean Squared Error (MSE) on the target’s calibration and follow-up datasets, representing prediction accuracy at the time of calibration and after a significant number of days had elapsed. These two error rates served as metrics for comparing the performance of different framework designs used in our study.

When training the model in a subject-dependent manner, the first trial from the target participant was used instead of the source data. For model selection involving the use of L1 loss with the validation data from source participants, their second trials were used.

N2G calibrator framework architecture

Our overall N2GCF architecture extends one of the model designs introduced in our previous study21 and can be separated into three different components each having a specific purpose in line with adversarial training from prior work32: a feature extractor, a regressor, and a domain discriminator (Fig. 2). The feature extractor receives LFP signals from the two leads as input and learns relevant features based on the labeled LFP data from the sources and the unlabeled LFP from the target. The regressor takes the features from the feature extractor and maps them to gait performance values. The domain discriminator, which considers both the features from the feature extractor and the modified features processed using the output of the regressor, aims to learn domain-related features from the given inputs. The major role of the domain discriminator thus lies in predicting whether the given features originate from the sources or the target. The feature extractor used for our study begins with a feature extraction block composed of a depthwise one-dimensional convolutional neural network (CNN), a batch normalization layer, a square activation function, an average pooling layer, and another batch normalization layer. This feature extraction block is followed by an oscillatory feature squeeze-and-excitation block, where the feature dimension is squeezed and excited for each temporal dimension sample using shared encoding and decoding layers, with ReLU and Sigmoid activations placed in between and after these layers, respectively, maintaining its output shape the same as its input shape. The feature extractor lastly includes a bi-directional long short-term memory (LSTM) with three stacked layers, which inputs rescaled features derived by performing element-wise multiplication between the outputs obtained before and after the squeeze-and-excitation block. N2GCF architecture.

The architecture of our N2GCF is composed of a feature extractor, regressor, and domain discriminator.

For the framework in this study, bias was not used in the layers of the feature extractor except for its squeeze-and-excitation block, and it was also not used in the layers of the regressor. As a whole, the framework architecture takes LFP signals of a 5-s time window from the two leads through the feature extractor and produces a single gait performance value corresponding to the last 2 s of the time window from the regressor, while information regarding the discriminability of features related to the source or target is made available by the domain discriminator for adversarial training.

Maximum mean discrepancy-based recurrent source selection

Our recurrent source selection scheme is designed to guide our N2GCF to focus more on source participants whose neural characteristics are more similar to those of the target participant throughout the training process. As the framework progresses through adversarial training, we utilize MMD37,38 computed from the outcomes of the feature extractor and regressor to measure discrepancies between sources and target features, serving as an indicator of which source data should be considered further. Source selections occur after each epoch using training and calibration data of source and target participants, respectively, and the updated subset of sources is taken into account for training the framework in the subsequent epoch. All sources are included in the very first epoch of training, as the subset of sources has not yet been established.

Conditions and metrics for framework model establishments

We explored the performance of our calibrator framework using two distinct conditions: unsupervised and semi-supervised. The framework itself was trained without labels from the target participant, and the sole difference between the conditions was the method for model selection. In the semi-supervised condition designed to explore the architecture’s ability to extract domain-invariant features, the framework utilized the labeled calibration data from the target to select the model epoch with the lowest L1 loss. As for the unsupervised condition resembling a realistic application where labels from the target are not available, we explored two different model selection strategies: a loss-based approach that selected the model with the lowest L1 loss using source participants’ validation data, and our MMD-based approach that selected the model with the lowest average MMD across each source’s training and the target’s calibration features. For this MMD metric, we used a concatenated vector of both conditional and marginal features to consider both conditional and marginal alignments. These two methods for the unsupervised condition were applied to explore the influence of model selection approaches on performance outcomes44,45.

Framework ablation study and benchmark comparisons

We performed an ablation study to explore the effectiveness of our calibrator framework by comparing its performance over other possible design choices (Fig. 3). We compared six different designs derived during our framework development: baseline model (BM), baseline model with recurrent source selection (BMRS), our domain adversarial training framework structure with no recurrent selection (DANS), framework with recurrent-selected subset applied only from domain discriminator to feature extractor (DARS_DW), framework with recurrent-selected subset applied only to the regressor and feature extractor (DARS_RW), and our complete framework where the subset was applied to both discriminator-related and regressor-related parts (N2GCF). In baseline-based designs (BM and BMRS), the models only included the feature extractor and regressor, without the domain discriminator, indicating that no adversarial training was applied to the model. All three components were utilized in other design choices, and their designs differed based on whether recurrent source selection was applied to backpropagation from the regressor, or from the domain discriminator, or both, or none. Our feature extractor, domain discriminator, and regressor architecture was utilized as the backbone structure for the approaches we compared, and the output from the regressor replaced the classifier output of the original work for deriving conditional features. This adaptation was held as these approaches were originally designed for classification tasks, whereas our gait tracking task warrants a regression framework. We also reported error measures using a distribution-aware null model, which utilized Kernel Density Estimation (KDE)48,49 to randomly sample labels from the sources’ training data label distribution while also clipping the values to their range, as a reference for a random chance level. The reported error was averaged over ten runs per target to get a stable estimate.

Behavioral analysis and performance interpretations

To investigate how recurrent selection occurred for our N2GCF training, we analyzed how frequently each source subject was selected across training epochs until the minimal L1 loss was achieved on the calibration data of the target, as well as how selections varied throughout the epochs. A sensitivity analysis was also conducted on our framework with varying kernel bandwidths for our MMD-based approaches to investigate the influence of MMD parameters on our N2G calibrator’s performance. To additionally provide insight into the clinical relevance of our framework, we used Kendall’s tau coefficient to quantify the strength of the correlation between the framework’s predictions and our derived gait performance measures. The correlation outcomes from our framework were compared to those between beta power and gait performance, as beta power is a widely established biomarker for PD-related motor symptoms13,16,50. The beta power was calculated from LFPs using the same 2-s time windows, corresponding to the intervals of gait performance measurement from the force plates.

Local and global variance analyses that utilize the variance of the calibrator’s outcomes to investigate the framework’s sensitivity towards specific features were conducted with inspiration from previous studies21,51–54. The local variance was computed by inputting the band-pass filtered signal of a specific sub-frequency band into the framework and measuring the variance of the output from the regressor prior to applying its ReLU activation. Conversely, the global variance was calculated by removing that specific sub-frequency band signal and measuring the output variance with the remaining frequency signals. For each target participant and for each variance analysis type, these measures from different sub-frequencies were normalized into variance ratios, ensuring the ratios summed to 1. Thus, a high local ratio indicated a strong direct influence of that frequency band on the calibrator’s outcomes, while a low global ratio signified a strong interactive influence on the calibrator’s predictions. These ratios were calculated using target participants’ calibration and follow-up sets, with frequency bands split into four sub-frequencies: alpha (8–13 Hz), beta (13–30 Hz), low-gamma (30–70 Hz) and high-gamma (70–100 Hz).

Settings for framework training

The methods and analysis scripts were implemented in Python, and the components of our framework were designed using Pytorch. The model was trained with the NVIDIA GeForce RTX 4090 GPU. We utilized adaptive moment estimation (ADAM) for optimization and used a batch size of 16 for the source domain. The maximum number of epochs for training was limited to 200, and model selection was held only on models established after the first 5 epochs. Adopting from previous work that applied domain adversarial training32, the learning rate was adjusted with the following formula to maintain a low error rate on source domains throughout adaptation toward the target domain: 10\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$l{r}_{x}=\frac{l{r}_{0}}{{(1+\alpha \cdot x)}^{\beta }},$$\end{document}lrx=lr0(1+α⋅x)β, where x represents the proportion of elapsed batches relative to the total number of batches considering the maximum epoch limit, and α and β are constants of 10 and 0.75, respectively. The initial learning rate lr0 was set to 1e-5 in this study.

Statistical analysis

We utilized non-parametric tests to account for the relatively small number of result samples. For statistical comparisons regarding the error rate results retrieved from the three representative models under unsupervised and semi-supervised conditions, we used the Wilcoxon signed-rank test for pairwise comparisons between each model pair. We conducted and reported the results using the Friedman test for multiple comparisons as a reference. The Wilcoxon signed-rank test was also used for pairwise comparisons of the Kendall correlation coefficients involving beta power and the N2G calibrator’s predictions from the two conditions. Bonferroni correction was employed to adjust for multiple comparisons, and the threshold for significance was defined as 0.05 after the correction.

a Average mean absolute error (MAE) and mean squared error (MSE) rates on the calibration and follow-up sets from the semi-supervised condition, including six different design choices derived from our N2GCF and five different benchmark domain adaptation approaches. b Comparisons between validation loss-based model selection and our MMD-based model selection in the unsupervised condition, using the framework designs related to our N2G calibrator. c Sensitivity analysis regarding changes in kernel bandwidth for the MMD-based measures. d Local and global variance ratio results using N2GCF trained in the unsupervised and semi-supervised conditions. Error bars represent the standard error.

The results of comparing six different designs related to our framework indicated that our final calibrator framework was able to outperform all other design choices on both calibration and follow-up sets in terms of both average MAE and MSE metrics. Specifically, average MAE and MSE rates were improved from both the calibration and follow-up datasets when our recurrent source selection was applied towards backpropagation passing the regressor and feature extractor (BMRS vs. BM and DARS_RW vs. DANS), whereas applying recurrent source selection only on the backpropagation path from the domain discriminator to the feature extractor exhibited smaller or no error rate reductions (DARS_DW vs. DANS). Methods that utilized adversarial learning techniques exhibited lower error rates in both calibration and follow-up sets compared to when adversarial learning was not employed, regardless of whether our recurrent source selection was applied towards the regressor and feature extractor (N2GCF vs. BMRS and DARS_RW vs. BMRS) or not (DANS vs. BM). Comparisons with five other domain adaptation approaches adapted to our regression task indicated that our full N2GCF structure was able to achieve lower average error rates in both our calibration and follow-up sets, with both MAE and MSE metrics.

Within the designs derived from our calibrator framework, two different model selection metrics for obtaining the model from those formed across training epochs were investigated under the unsupervised condition: one approach using L1 loss computed with the validation data from the source participants, and another using our MMD-based approach. As can be seen in both MAE and MSE metrics using the calibration set from Fig. 6b, the utilization of the MMD-based approach for model selection resulted in lower error rates compared to using the validation loss calculated with the data from sources. Similar patterns were observed for most of the investigated design choices when further evaluated with the follow-up data, where the models obtained using the MMD-based approach exhibited lower error rates than the models obtained using validation loss. Out of all the framework designs trained and selected in a cross-subject manner, N2GCF with MMD-based model selection exhibited the lowest error rates, lessening the performance gap towards the model trained and selected in a subject-dependent way and using labels from the target’s calibration data for model selection (BM_Target).

Sensitivity analysis with different MMD kernel bandwidths

Additionally, to explore the influence of MMD parameters in our approach, a sensitivity analysis regarding different MMD kernel bandwidths was performed. The bandwidth of the respective kernel was applied in both the recurrent source selection and the model selection where the MMD was used. As shown in Fig. 6c, N2GCF from the semi-supervised condition consistently exhibited lower error rates in both the calibration and follow-up sets compared to the calibrator models established using two different selection metrics from the unsupervised condition. The calibrator trained using the semi-supervised condition also exhibited the lowest variability in error rates across different kernel bandwidths. As for the two unsupervised conditions, MMD-based model selections consistently outperformed models selected using validation loss from sources while also demonstrating lower variability across different kernel bandwidths.

Local and global variance analyses

Figure 6d shows the local and global variance ratio results for the N2GCF computed using the target participants’ calibration and follow-up sets. As seen in the local variance ratio results from both the unsupervised and semi-supervised conditions, signals from the beta band had the highest variance ratio regardless of the applied dataset. As for the global variance ratio results, removing the beta band component showed the lowest variance ratio, followed by the removal of the low-gamma band, across all conditions and datasets.

Discussion

The aim of our N2GCF is to leverage existing synchronized LFP and weight shift data collected from other individuals to reduce the data collection effort required from the target user when establishing their personalized gait-tracking deep learning model for aDBS and eliminating the need for additional kinematic inputs. The framework achieves this by directing the model to learn domain-invariant features, producing a calibrated feature extractor and a regressor that form a user-specific model capable of translating the target individual’s LFP signals into gait performance in real time. Beyond providing predictions that exhibited a stronger correlation with gait performance than beta power, a standard biomarker for PD-related gait symptoms, our framework also effectively calibrated data from source individuals to the target user by reducing both MAE and MSE in neural-to-gait translation compared to the non-calibrated model. Our calibrator thus facilitates: (1) reducing the amount of data needed from the target user by substituting labeled training data from the target user with labeled data from source individuals, and (2) eliminating the need for synchronously recorded gait measurements from the target user, as only their neural signals are necessary for calibration. Our study possesses novelty in that we apply adversarial training to the regression task involving the use of DBS systems, where continuous weight shift values serve as labels for the framework model while using LFPs from the leads as input data.

Our approach focuses on a widely acknowledged limitation that neural signal classification models generally exhibit lower performance when trained on data from individuals other than the target user’s own labeled data55–57. This drop in performance may be due to differences in neural characteristics across participants, as shown in Supplementary Fig. 2, which visualized power spectral density (PSD) data with global structure-preserving dimensionality reduction techniques58–60. We aimed to address such data misalignments through an unsupervised domain adaptation approach that reduces the discrepancy between the extracted features of informative sources and the target, as demonstrated in Fig. 5d and Supplementary Fig. 1. Our MMD-based recurrent source selection was further applied to identify sources whose extracted features could not align closely with the target based on the updated model from the latest epoch, and subsequently limit the influence of these sources in the following epoch. This approach could further reduce calibration errors, lowering both MAE and MSE rates in the semi-supervised condition to a level that was no longer significantly different from those obtained using models trained with the target user’s labeled data. Beyond previous studies involving neural signals that applied adversarial training to classification tasks43,47,61, our study highlights the importance of limiting the influence of neural data from sources that are irrelevant to the target participant, even in the regression task.

The results of using our MMD-based model selection, an approach used in our framework to determine one of the models generated from training epochs for final establishment, show that selecting models based on validation loss from source data may not serve as an optimal strategy for regression tasks. With labels from the target data unavailable, unsupervised conditions require more complex metrics for selecting reliable models. While using validation loss is a common practice for classification tasks in deep learning and is also occasionally used in unsupervised domain adaptation with the data from sources62–64, our comparison between validation loss-based selection and our MMD-based selection indicated consistently higher error rates for the validation loss-based approach across multiple model designs. Aligning with previous studies that highlight the importance of model selection metrics for unsupervised domain adaptation in classification tasks65–67, our study emphasizes that using suitable metrics for obtaining final models is also critical for our regression task. Comparable to the validation loss-based selection approach that does not use additional complex methods, our current model selection directly utilizes features already available in our framework through our feature extractor and regressor, and simply selects the models with the lowest average MMD calculated. By taking conditional features into account that consider the distribution of regression outcomes through the use of predicted outcomes from the regressor, which is absent in the validation loss-based model selection, our MMD-based approach obtained lower error rates. Beyond our simple metric, further enhancements in model selections can be done in advance specifically focusing on our regression task, where the distribution of continuous label values is uneven across trial recordings due to the inconsistent elicitation of symptoms in PWP.

Compared to the error rates assessed using calibration data, results from the follow-up data exhibited relatively smaller performance differences between models with weaker statistical measures, especially in the semi-supervised condition. This may be due to the fact that neural signals within an individual can change over extended periods of time. It has been observed in prior works that the aperiodic components of neural signals in PWP change over time depending on multiple factors including the severity of their symptoms, cognitive impairment, and influences from neural plasticity68–70. These changes in the model’s performance due to changes in neural signals over time highlight the need for a practical recalibration method such as our N2GCF. Unlike conventional subject-dependent deep learning methods that require synchronous neural and kinematic recordings, our N2GCF requires only their neural signals while the user simulates simple stepping in place. This shifts the paradigm for real-world settings where current commercial aDBS systems do not support kinematic data integration. By eliminating the need for clinic visits that involve specialized synchronous neural and kinematic recording equipment, our approach allows users to update their personalized gait tracking models from anywhere they can walk in place, even at home. As data-driven methods would require periodic model recalibration, our approach contributes to the aDBS field by removing the burden of repeated clinic visits and making these technologies more accessible.

Analysis using Kendall correlation demonstrates that our N2GCF provides a more advanced biomarker for tracking gait than beta power, which is used in many studies to monitor motor symptoms in PD13,16,20,50. The predictions from the calibrator established with the unsupervised condition exhibited a stronger average correlation with gait than beta power, and this improvement was even statistically significant for the calibrator established in the semi-supervised condition. An important key distinction is that our calibrator aims to predict quantified gait performance directly, whereas beta power is limited to indicating indirect relative changes in movement. This transition from a relative indicator to a quantitative and objective marker suggests that our calibrator framework can provide clinicians with more direct and comprehensive information for tracking individual gait status or related symptoms. By using Kendall’s coefficient that evaluates the ordinal association rather than the magnitude of error, we could directly compare our quantitative predictions against the relative fluctuations of beta power. This analysis, which provides a measure of clinical relevance by benchmarking against beta power that is inaccessible through MAE and MSE, showed that our calibrator still had a stronger association with gait performance than beta power, even when disregarding their actual magnitudes. The coefficient results also imply that the framework training process was able to automatically identify and capture key neural characteristics, including those from beta fluctuations, from the broad frequency range of neural signals. With a stronger relative correlation with gait than beta power and a lower error rate in predicting gait performance magnitude than other framework designs, our calibrator demonstrates its ability to provide an enhanced and clinically relevant biomarker.

While beta power is an important biomarker for predicting motor symptoms, its utility can be enhanced by considering it with other features, such as the overall spectral signal trend or more stable frequency bands15,68,71,72. Furthermore, training deep learning models with a broad range of frequency signals improved gait performance predictions compared to training only with the beta band21. Our adversarial learning approach maintained the importance of beta band signals while also integrating a broader frequency band, as demonstrated through our local and global variance ratio analyses. The local variance ratio analysis consistently exhibited the highest value for the beta band among the four sub-bands, indicating that it was the primary signal influencing the calibrator’s output. We could also observe a noticeably low global variance ratio for the low-gamma band compared to the other sub-bands besides beta, suggesting that low-gamma signals may also have an influential interaction effect on the calibrator’s predictions. Along with our N2GCF showing greater average Kendall correlations with gait than beta power, results from our variance ratio analyses highlight the contributions of frequency bands other than beta toward such enhanced predictions. These findings align with previous studies emphasizing that incorporating a broader frequency range of signals can improve gait-related state prediction compared to using the beta band signals alone. The reduction in MAE and MSE compared to other frameworks further indicates that our N2GCF effectively bridged the gap between the target and source domains while maintaining the extraction of useful neural features across broad frequency signals. Our calibrator thus aims to provide objective gait performance measures from neural signals by effectively mapping labeled data from sources to the target user’s unlabeled calibration data, thereby reducing the data collection effort.

An important point to note is that our feature extractor, domain discriminator, and regressor architecture was incorporated into the approaches we used for benchmark comparisons. This adaptation was essential as many established domain adaptation methods, particularly within neural signal processing, were originally developed for classification rather than for our regression task of tracking gait performance represented with labels of continuous values. While the results of these adapted models should thus be interpreted carefully, our analysis conveyed two notable findings. As expected, approaches that enabled the domain discriminator or discriminators to consider conditional features derived from the feature extractor and the regressor, simultaneously along with marginal features, exhibited lower error rates than others. Unexpectedly, however, approaches that only considered marginal features outputted from the feature extractor did not improve performance, and in some cases, performed worse than the baseline model that did not involve any domain adaptation process. This shows that for regression tasks involving variable features across domains such as neural signals, solely focusing on marginal distribution alignment may not be effective for domain adaptation.

By combining a regression-based model with a domain adversarial learning framework, our proof-of-concept study demonstrates a path toward more advanced, adaptive and responsive stimulation therapies for future DBS. Our regression design of providing continuous output opens the door for stimulation to be adjusted in proportion to gait fluctuations. This approach would enable a more direct stimulation control by allowing the stimulation to respond to predicted gait quality, unlike current threshold-based methods that use a fixed-rate stimulation change driven by less direct markers like beta power. Its higher resolution outcomes, in contrast to those from classification models, are intended to enable a more dynamic and personalized therapy by leading to richer stimulation changing options that directly reflect a user’s real-time gait changes. Furthermore, the use of domain adaptation mitigates the extensive data collection burden on the target participant, making this data-driven technique more accessible by converting a formal data collection session into a simple calibration step. While further work is necessary to incorporate the stimulation component for deployment in aDBS, the high-resolution gait performance predictions available from our calibrator framework would mark a critical step toward developing more efficient, personalized, and immediately responsive stimulation control.

There are some limitations in our study and further areas for improvement in our N2GCF. One limitation is the relatively small sample size compared to other fields due to the challenges of recruiting participants with implanted aDBS systems. Thus, our statistical analyses may not be powerful and the results may need careful interpretation. Another limitation is that our study focused primarily on the stepping in place task. This was done to prevent falls by using a harness to protect participants during their off-medication and off-stimulation state, and to enable continuous gait measurement through the force plates. In addition, we identified two cases where our approach could not converge data distributions between the frequently selected sources and the target during training, leading to poor performance characterized by having higher MAE and MSE than the baseline model without adversarial learning and by exhibiting lowest Kendall correlation in both our calibration and follow-up sets (Supplementary Fig. 3). With these limitations, future work can be done with our study serving as a benchmark. Improvements could be made to prevent the aforementioned failure cases, such as by developing more detailed and dynamic approaches for the MMD-based source and model selection approaches. Gathering more data from other participants could broaden the selection choices for our recurrent source selection, potentially improving performance. Beyond our stepping in place task, a critical next step would involve applying our concept to tasks that more closely resemble real-life scenarios, such as free walking and turning. This will require establishing a safe experimental environment suited for these tasks that accounts for the possibility of symptom occurrence, and developing reliable gait quantification metrics for continuous data labeling to enable the deployment of these deep learning-based methods. Designing a lightweight feature extractor and regressor suitable for implantable systems is another important next step towards real-life applications. Lastly, a key future direction is to implement stimulation control policies based on our regression framework and compare their clinical effects against strategies derived from other formulations, such as classification models. Pursuing these directions would further enhance the clinical applicability of deep learning-based neural signal processing for aDBS.

Conclusion

In this study, we propose a deep learning-based framework that aims to map STN LFPs into gait performance while requiring only unlabeled LFP data from the target user for its training. Our N2GCF achieves this by employing adversarial learning to calibrate labeled data from other source individuals to the target user’s neural signals. N2GCF enhances its adversarial learning process by utilizing our recurrent source selection that continuously identifies and considers data from sources whose neural signal features more closely align with those of the target throughout training. N2GCF demonstrated its efficacy by enhancing prediction performance compared to those without calibration and also showing better prediction performance than other alternative adversarial training approaches. The core advantage of our calibrator framework is that it eliminates the need for specialized synchronous kinematic and neural data recording equipment for target users, allowing users to establish their personalized gait tracking models from anywhere they can simulate simple stepping in place. Our approach thus marks a notable advancement for future data-driven aDBS applications by removing the burden of repeated clinic visits involving specialized equipment, as frequent model recalibration is necessary due to changes in an individual’s neural signals over time. Overall, N2GCF enhances the accessibility of data-driven techniques by offering a simplified process for applying personalized models capable of translating users’ LFP signals into gait performance in real time.
