Primary tumor resection in de novo metastatic breast cancer from an oligometastatic perspective: A systematic review and meta-analysis
Authors: Chen Wu, Xiang Li, Shiyang Liu, Litong Yao, Tianyi He, Yusong Wang, Haoran Dong, Shuyi Niu, Mozhi Wang, Yingying Xu
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
SummaryWhether patients with de novo metastatic breast cancer (de novo MBC) benefit from primary tumor resection (PTR) is controversial, and the efficacy of treatment of primary tumor in patients with oligometastases is uncertain. A comprehensive search was conducted for studies published from January 1, 2008 to January 1, 2024 that compared PTR with no PTR for de novo MBC that quantified the extent of metastasis. Ten studies and 83,559 patients with de novo MBC were included. A meta-analysis showed a significant improvement in overall survival after PTR compared with no PTR (hazard ratio [HR], 0.60; 95% confidence interval [CI], 0.53–0.67; p < 0.00001). Similar results were seen in patients with 1 metastatic organ, ≤2 metastatic organs, and ≤3 metastatic organs. Oligometastatic patients are expected to be a beneficial subgroup for PTR in patients with MBC, especially when metastasis occurs in only one organ.Subject areas: health sciences, medicine, medical specialty, internal medicine, oncology

Introduction

Metastatic breast cancer (MBC) usually progresses from treated early-stage breast cancer, with distant metastases presenting at the time of initial diagnosis (de novo MBC) in 3.5%–10% of all newly diagnosed patients with breast cancer in Western countries.1 Patients with MBC have a low 5-year survival rate of about 25% and a short median survival time of about 18–26 months.2,3

MBC is a difficult-to-treat disease but can be improved with appropriate treatment strategies, with systemic therapy based on chemotherapy, targeted therapy, and endocrine therapy being the standard treatment modalities.4,5,6 Local treatment for MBC has been controversial and is not routinely recommended.7 Currently, local treatment is used to consolidate the effect of systemic therapy in the case of good response to systemic drug therapy and can be undertaken in selected patients with curative intent.8,9,10

The oligometastatic paradigm was formally defined by Hellman and Weichselbaum in 1995. This paradigm suggests that radical treatment of metastases may have curative potential in some tumors where metastatic capacity is in a state of limitation. Although only local therapy of metastases was emphasized, its view that metastases are a function of both tumor size and tumor progression (hereafter referred to as the metastatic function) implies that there is a behavioral proximity between the locally and widely metastatic states that can benefit from primary tumor resection (PTR)11 (Figure 1).

Oligometastatic breast cancer (OMBC) is a distinctive subset of MBC, of which the definition is constantly being updated, and currently arbitrarily defined as a limited number of metastatic lesions (up to 5, with some studies suggesting 3, not necessarily within the same organ) based on multi-histology studies, while other statements suggest that there may never be an exact cutoff defining the number of metastases that would benefit from treatment.6,12,13 The ideal definition should shift from the previous number of metastases to a definition based on indicators reflecting tumor biology such as gene mutation profiles and microRNA expression profiles (e.g., circulating tumor cells, miR-200 family, etc.).2 Although there is no uniform definition of OMBC, it is now widely accepted that OMBC is the state that falls between local metastasis and extensive metastasis that may achieve complete remission and a long survival and may benefit from aggressive local therapies. While metastasis-directed local therapies for OMBC have been widely explored, PTR has rarely been explored.14,15

Given the existence of subgroups of MBC that could benefit from PTR and the biological characteristics of oligometastatic disease suggesting that OMBC could benefit from local therapies, we hypothesized that OMBC could be a subgroup of MBC that could benefit from PTR. Therefore, our study aimed to select studies that quantified the extent of metastasis from all studies exploring the prognostic impact of PTR on MBC and to pool them by meta-analysis to help screen for subgroups of beneficiaries among patients with MBC undergoing PTR.

Methods

Literature review and study identification

The Population, Intervention, Control, Outcomes, and Study design method was used to determine the inclusion criteria for the literature16,17 (Table S1). The Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-analyses reporting guideline and the Meta-analysis of Observational Studies in Epidemiology reporting guideline were used.17,18 The search strategy was breast cancer, metast∗ or oligometast∗, and surgery, which was applied to query PubMed/MEDLINE, Embase, ClinicalTrials.gov, and World Health Organization International Clinical Trials Registry Platform (WHO ICTRP) to broadly capture existing literature in English from January 1, 2008, to January 1, 2024. We used Zotero 6.0 for title, abstract, and full-text screening. The results of a search of ClinicalTrials.gov and WHO ICTRP are presented in Table S2.

Data collection and extraction

Data were collected independently by two reviewers (C.W. and S.L.). Discrepancies were resolved by a third reviewer (X.L.). All data were extracted from primary publications and their associated online appendices.

The first author, publication year, number of patients, age, extent of metastasis, primary outcomes, and secondary outcomes were obtained from each included study. Both local and systemic treatment data were extracted. The efficacy outcome of interest was overall survival (OS). We then extracted the hazard ratios (HRs) and respective 95% confidence intervals (CIs) for OS.

Study equality and risk of bias

The quality of each randomized controlled study (RCT) and observational study was assessed using the Cochrane risk of bias tool and the Newcastle-Ottawa Scale (NOS), respectively (Figure S1; Table S3). The Cochrane risk of bias tool indicates the risk of bias as low, high, or unclear risk, including selection, performance, detection, wear and tear, reporting, and other aspects of bias. For NOS, a maximum of 9 points were allocated to each study and aspect including selection, comparability, and outcome. Observational studies with a score of 0–4 were defined as low-quality evidence, whereas studies with a score of 5–9 were defined as high-quality evidence. Two reviewers independently scored (C.W. and S.L.), and disagreements were resolved by consultation with a third reviewer (X.L.). All RCTs and observational studies were scored and recorded. Funnel plots were used to assess the publication bias of studies.

Statistical analysis

Heterogeneity was assessed using the I2 statistics. When I2 > 50%, a random-effects model was used, otherwise a fixed-effects model was used. We selected the HR and its 95% CI as the effect size in the meta-analysis. Statistical significance was defined as p < 0.05. The results of the meta-analysis were reported as a classic forest plot. Sensitivity analysis was performed by step-by-step exclusion of each study from the main analysis. All statistical analyses were performed using RevMan software, version 5.4 (Cochrane Collaboration).

Definition of OMBC and non-OMBC

Five different definitions of OMBC were selected for this study, including limited extent of metastasis (≤3 metastases or ≤2 metastatic sites), ≤3 metastases, 1 metastatic site, ≤2 metastatic site, and ≤3 metastatic sites. Meanwhile, polymetastatic breast cancer (PMBC) was defined in three ways, >1 metastatic site, >2 metastatic sites, and >3 metastatic sites.

OS in limited extent of metastasis

PTR, primary tumor resection.

OS in OMBC defined as a limited extent of metastasis

When OMBC was defined as a limited extent of metastasis, eight subgroups in six studies were included in a meta-analysis, which showed a significant improvement in OS after PTR compared with no PTR (HR, 0.60; 95% CI, 0.53–0.67; p < 0.00001) (Figure 3). Two of these studies examined OS when there were <3 metastases and the remaining studies examined OS when there were limited metastatic sites.

OS in OMBC defined as limited metastatic sites

When OMBC was defined as 1 metastatic site (HR, 0.57; 95% CI, 0.51–0.63; p < 0.00001) (Figure 4A), ≤2 metastatic sites (HR, 0.58; 95% CI, 0.51–0.65; p < 0.00001) (Figure 4B), and ≤3 metastatic sites (HR, 0.58; 95% CI, 0.52–0.65; p < 0.00001) (Figure 4C), similar results were obtained.

In addition, three retrospective studies excluded from the meta-analysis also reported significant results related to OS in OMBC. Huang et al. reported median OS in PTR versus no PTR when metastatic sites were ≤3 (35 months vs. 22 months; p = 0.014).21 Nguyen et al. reported 5-year Kaplan-Meier (KM) OS in PTR versus no PTR when the metastatic site was one (25.1% vs. 17.8%, p = 0.001) and the metastases were 1–4 (8.4% vs. 6.3%, p = 0.02).25 Yang et al. also obtained a significant result when metastatic sites were ≤2 (HR = 0.56, p < 0.001).27

OS in OMBC defined as ≤ 3 metastases

In contrast to the aforementioned results, when OMBC was defined as ≤ 3 metastases, the result was not significant (HR, 0.86; 95% CI, 0.50–1.46; p = 0.58) (Figure 5). This may be because different metastatic organs may cause different results.

OS in OMBC defined as ≤3 metastases

OS in PMBC

We also performed meta-analyses of the subgroups of PMBC. The result was significant only when metastatic sites were >1 (HR, 0.51; 95% CI, 0.33–0.79; p < 0.00001) (Figure S4A), whereas the results for the other groups were not significant (Figures S4B and S4C). As PMBC is defined, the more severe the metastatic extent, the more patients tend not to benefit from surgery. In addition, two retrospective studies excluded from the meta-analysis reported significant results related to OS in PMBC. Nguyen et al. reported 5-year KM OS in PTR versus no PTR when metastatic sites were >2 (8.4% vs. 6.3%, p = 0.02) and metastases were >3 (12.9% vs. 11.6%, p = 0.008).25 Yang et al. also obtained a significant result when metastatic sites were >2 (HR = 0.65, p < 0.001).

Surgical modality

As Table 2 showed, in three studies, the majority of surgical patients underwent a modified radical mastectomy,19,21,26 and in another three studies, the majority of surgical patients underwent mastectomy.22,24,27 In one study, more than half of the patients who underwent surgery had breast-conserving surgery.25 The remaining three studies did not mention the type of PTR or the proportion of each modality. All surgical patients included in one study had lymph node dissection,19 and most surgical patients in one study had lymph node dissection (89.6%).21 The remaining studies did not mention the type of surgery for lymph nodes or the percentage of each modality. In one study, patients who underwent surgery for metastases were excluded.27 Other studies did not mention about local management of metastases.

Only one retrospective study explored the impact of different procedures on OS in patients with MBC with a single metastatic organ in subgroup analyses, with the result that lumpectomy and mastectomy had a similar prolonging effect on the OS of patients with single metastatic organ (HR, 0.57; CI, 0.54–0.61 vs. HR, 0.62; CI, 0.60–0.65).20

Timing of surgery

Both prospective studies performed systemic therapy followed by surgery,19,22 whereas the study by Kolben et al. and Ma et al. operated on the majority of patients first (70.9% and 74%),23,24 and the other studies did not specify the proportion of patients with different timing of surgery.

Ma et al. compared the OS of surgical patients with different timing of surgery. The results showed that patients who underwent surgery after systemic therapy had a better survival rate than those who underwent surgery immediately.24

Discussion

The role of PTR in novo MBC has been controversial. Of interest, evidence from both retrospective and prospective subgroup analyses suggested that local therapy may benefit some cases of metastatic disease, which needs to be explored in depth. To explore whether patients with OMBC might benefit from PTR, this study conducted a meta-analysis of relevant studies. This is the first meta-analysis, to our knowledge, to explore the role of PTR in OMBC. Our analysis suggests that the OS of patients with MBC will benefit from PTR when the extent of metastasis is limited, especially when there is only one metastatic site.

A prospective study by Flanegan et al. suggests that PTR is useful when kidney cancer is in stage IV.28 The current prospective studies on PTR in de novo MBC have yielded negative results, except for MF07-01, which yielded positive results.19,22,29,30,31 The different results imply that PTR cannot be used as a routine treatment for its uncertain survival benefit in patients with de novo MBC. Of interest, subgroup analyses provided clues that ER+ or HER2+ patients with single bone metastasis may benefit from PTR.29 Yet it remains unclear whether patients with MBC should undergo PTR. In our study, the survival benefit was significant when metastatic sites were >1, but not when the metastatic sites were >2, or >3, suggesting that it is the patients with MBC with milder degree of metastasis who can benefit from PTR. It proves that this may be due to the broad definition of MBC, ranging from a relatively mild degree to a relatively heavy degree of metastasis, which may influence the extent to which a patient benefits from PTR. Thus, the key to identifying the subgroups that benefit from PTR may be to distinguish between different degrees of metastasis.

Currently, we usually define metastatic cancers with different degrees of metastasis in terms of oligometastasis versus polymetastasis. OMBC, as an intermediate state, resides between locally metastatic breast cancer and PMBC in terms of both biological and clinical features,32 implying that it is more likely to benefit from multimodality treatment, in which PTR should also be taken into account. At present, the conventional therapy for OMBC is metastasis-directed local therapy. A meta-analysis clarified the survival advantage associated with resection of isolated breast cancer liver metastasis,33 a retrospective study showed that resection of metastases improved survival in patients with single-site liver, lung, and brain metastases,20 and another retrospective study showed that in the subgroup of patients with solitary brain metastases, solitary lung metastases, and luminal A subtype with solitary brain metastases, the prognosis of surgery for metastatic lesions is better.34 However, the efficacy of PTR remains unclear. The results of this work suggest that patients can benefit from PTR when OMBC is defined as limited metastatic extent, which provides evidentiary support for screening subgroups of patients with MBC for benefit from PTR.

However, due to the gradual updating, previous studies have not been well aligned on the definition of OMBC, with some defining it as a limited number of metastatic sites and others as a limited number of metastases. In our study, when defining OMBC in terms of metastatic sites, OS after PTR was beneficial when the number of metastatic sites was 1, ≤2, and ≤3. In contrast, when defining OMBC in terms of metastases, OS after PTR did not benefit when the number of metastases was ≤3. This result may be due to the existence of prognostic differences in different organs, which cannot be distinguished in this study because the data used are from subgroups.

To date, some biological evidence supports the benefit of PTR on survival in patients with MBC. Firstly, PTR can lead to rebound antibody and cell-mediated responses that activate the immune response and improve drug efficacy in mice with primary tumor and metastatic disease.35 In addition, drug-resistant “tumor stem cells” are prevalent in the primary tumor,36 and the concept of “cell seeding” suggests that cells released into the bloodstream by the primary tumor will return to the primary tumor, which therefore activates these tumor stem cells.37 Therefore, PTR can reduce tumor stem cells, block self-seeding, and thus alleviate primary tumor drug resistance.

Limitations of the study

There are limitations to this meta-analysis. This meta-analysis has some limitations. This study is a meta-analysis of subgroup analyses, so the lack of further exploration of OMBC subgroups leads to the inability to further explore the heterogeneity of metastasis sites and other aspects between studies, which need to be further explored in subsequent experiments. In addition, metastasis size as an indicator to define OMBC, and therapies other than PTR, including metastasis-directed therapies, systemic therapies, especially targeted therapies, and immunotherapies, were not considered in this study, which we will consider in future studies. These data need to be confirmed by prospective randomized trials with innovative designs. When de novo MBC benefits little from PTR and at the same time OMBC benefits little from metastasis-directed local therapies, perhaps we need to consider both.
