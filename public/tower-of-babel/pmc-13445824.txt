Evaluating the grade and IDH mutation status of gliomas by using intravoxel incoherent motion (IVIM) MRI and DSC perfusion MRI
Authors: Celal Tacyildiz, Kerim Aslan, Lütfi İncesu, Suna Yergin Tacyildiz, Barış Genç
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundThis study aimed to evaluate the diagnostic performance of intravoxel incoherent motion (IVIM) imaging and DSC perfusion MRI to predict the IDH mutation status of gliomas and differentiate high-grade gliomas (HGG) from low-grade gliomas (LGG).MethodsIn this retrospective study, IVIM parameters, including perfusion fraction (f), tissue diffusion (D), and pseudodiffusion (D*), were obtained using a double exponential IVIM model in 61 patients with a pathological diagnosis of glioma. Imaging was performed on a 3 Tesla MRI scanner using only four b-values (0, 50, 400, and 800 s/mm²). The diagnostic performance of rCBV, f, D, D*, ADC, and age parameters in distinguishing HGG from LGG and predicting IDH mutation status in gliomas was evaluated using ROC curve analysis, and their diagnostic powers were compared using the DeLong test.ResultsrCBV, f, and age were higher in patients with HGG, whereas D and ADC were higher in patients with LGG (p < 0.001). rCBV showed the highest AUC for HGG–LGG differentiation (AUC = 0.983), without statistically significant superiority over f, D, or ADC by DeLong testing. rCBV, f, and age were higher in patients with IDH-wild type (IDH-WT) gliomas, whereas D and ADC values were higher in IDH-mutant type (IDH-MT) gliomas (p < 0.05). D showed the highest observed AUC for predicting IDH mutation status (AUC = 0.894) without statistically significant superiority over rCBV, f, or ADC by DeLong testing.ConclusionsIVIM D obtained from a simplified four-b-value protocol may provide complementary information for predicting IDH mutation status in gliomas. However, these preliminary findings require validation in larger, multicenter cohorts.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12880-026-02461-6.

Background

Gliomas are the most common type of primary brain tumors. Adult diffuse gliomas are classified by the World Health Organization (WHO) according to IDH mutation status and other molecular alterations as astrocytoma IDH-mutant (MT), oligodendroglioma IDH-MT and 1p/19q-co-deleted, and glioblastoma IDH-wildtype (WT) [1]. Accurate preoperative grading and predicting IDH mutation status are crucial for treatment planning and prognosis. IDH-MT gliomas are less aggressive than IDH-WT gliomas. While biopsy provides a definitive diagnosis, it is invasive and may pose risks, particularly in deep brain areas [2]. Therefore, brain gliomas should be graded using imaging methods, and IDH mutation status should be determined prior to surgery.

Conventional MRI provides essential anatomic detail but is limited for accurate tumor grading. Gliomas alter the diffusion signaling properties of tissues by altering water distribution in the intracellular and extracellular spaces. Diffusion-weighted imaging (DWI) and ADC can be used to distinguish the IDH mutation status of gliomas [3]. Conventional DWI uses a monoexponential model to describe the decay of signal at b-values. Perfusion-weighted imaging with DSC provides information about tumor vascularity and is used to evaluate gliomas [4]. However, the use of contrast media is limited in patients with impaired renal function, contrast media allergy, and pediatric patients.

IVIM is an advanced magnetic resonance imaging modality that integrates perfusion and diffusion information without the use of contrast agents [5]. Le Bihan et al. developed a bi-exponential model to distinguish the molecular diffusion of water from the microcirculation of blood inside the capillary network. In this model, IVIM uses multiple b-values in DWI sequences to distinguish molecular diffusion from microcirculatory perfusion. The technique allows extraction of parameters such as tissue diffusion (D), pseudodiffusion (D*), and perfusion fraction (f) [6]. While f and D* are perfusion-related parameters, D is a diffusion-related parameter. IVIM-derived f is a parameter that represents the fraction of capillary microcirculation. IVIM-derived D* is a parameter that represents the rapid perfusion-related signal decay arising from capillary microcirculation, with a more pronounced effect at low b-values [7]. Although the IVIM-derived D value is similar to ADC, ADC incorporates both diffusion and perfusion-related effects within tissue, whereas IVIM-derived D represents pure molecular tissue diffusion independent of perfusion-related effects. Although most IVIM studies employ more than 10 b-values for accurate parameter estimation, this procedure increases scan time. Simplified IVIM protocols using fewer b-values could improve clinical feasibility. Hino et al. showed that three b-values may be sufficient to estimate the basic IVIM parameters [8]. Using only four b-values shortens scan time and may improve clinical feasibility. Luo et al.’s meta-analysis examined studies employing varying b values to differentiate between high-grade and low-grade glial tumors [9].

Although conventional IVIM protocols recommend utilizing 10 or more b-values for the precise calculation of D* and f, we sought to determine whether a simplified approach employing only 4 b-values may still provide valuable clinical insights in a reduced timeframe. We also aimed to compare this with DSC perfusion, a radiological imaging technique that substantially aids in glioma grading.

Methods

Study design and participants

This retrospective, single-center study has been approved by our hospital’s institutional ethics committee, and the requirement for informed consent was waived (OMÜ KAEK, Decision No: 549). Adult patients (> 18 years) who underwent brain MRI at our hospital between November 2020 and November 2023 and were pathologically diagnosed with glioma were included. Tumors were classified according to the 2021 World Health Organization classification of central nervous system tumors using integrated histomolecular diagnoses from the pathological reports. Histological tumor type and WHO grade were recorded together with IDH mutation status. For the grade-based analysis, WHO grade 1–2 tumors were categorized as low-grade gliomas (LGG), whereas WHO grade 3–4 tumors were categorized as high-grade gliomas (HGG). For the molecular analysis, patients were divided into IDH-MT and IDH-WT groups [1].

Patients who had received chemotherapy, radiotherapy, invasive treatment, or steroid therapy before MRI were excluded. After these exclusions, 100 patients were considered eligible for further evaluation. Subsequently, 39 patients were excluded because histopathological confirmation was unavailable. After these exclusions, the final cohort consisted of 61 patients (24 females, 37 males). An overview of the patient selection process is shown in the flow diagram (Fig. 1). The mean age of patients is 55 ± 16 years. Our study included 14 IDH-MT and 47 IDH-WT patients; 51 had HGG and 10 had LGG.

MR acquisition

MRI examinations were performed using a 3T scanner (Ingenia, Philips Healthcare, Best, The Netherlands) with a 32-channel head coil.

We used a three-dimensional (3D) T1-weighted (T1W) conventional gradient echo sequence, also known as 3D T1-fast field echo, with or without gadolinium. The T1W axial conventional spin-echo sequence had a repetition time (TR) of 600 ms, an echo time (TE) of 10 ms, a section thickness ranging from 1 to 5 mm, a field-of-view (FOV) of 230, a matrix size of 220 × 180, and a number of excitations (NEX) equal to 1. 3D fluid attenuation inversion recovery (FLAIR) coronal (TR/TE/inversion time, 4800/363/1650 ms; section thickness, 2 mm; NEX, 2); T2-weighted (T2W) sagittal turbo spin-echo sequence (TR/TE, 3000/80 ms; section thickness, 5 mm; matrix, 261 × 384; NEX, 1–2); T2W axial turbo spin-echo sequence (TR/TE, 3000/80 ms; section thickness, 5 mm; matrix, 261 × 384; NEX, 1–2); and diffusion-weighted images were obtained with spin-echo and echo-planar sequences with four b-values (0, 50, 400, and 800 s/mm2). Other diffusion parameters were as follows: TR/TE, 4557/113 ms; section thickness, 2.3 mm; FOV, 224 × 224 mm2, matrix, 112 × 114; and NEX, 1. The number of axial slices is 60 for each patient. The total scan time for diffusion-weighted images was 70 s.

The imaging parameters of the DSC-MRI were as follows: TR/TE, 1400/30 msec; FA, 90; FOV, 240 mm; 15–20 slices; matrix, 128 × 128; slice thickness, 5 mm; intersection gap, 1 mm. Before acquisition of DSC perfusion images, a 0.05-mmol/kg dose of gadobutrol was administered as a preload to help correct for leakage effects. DSC perfusion imaging was performed with a bolus of 4 mL/s containing 0.05 mmol/kg gadobutrol, followed by flushing with 20 mL saline and using a gradient echo echo-planar imaging sequence. The total gadolinium dose was 0.1 mmol/kg (0.05 mmol/kg preload + 0.05 mmol/kg bolus). Leakage correction was performed using the Boxerman–Schmainda–Weisskoff method. DSC-MRI was acquired with a total acquisition time of approximately 91 s.

Evaluation of lesions and image processing

From the IVIM imaging data, diffusion coefficient (D), perfusion fraction (f), and pseudodiffusion coefficient (D*) maps were obtained using the open-source ADCmap Plugin (Version 1.9, http://web.stanford.edu/~bah/software/ADCmap/versions.html), running on OsiriX Lite, Version 11.0.1, (https://www.osirix-viewer.com/osirix/osirix-md/download-osirix-lite/). The plugin performs voxel-wise fitting of DWI data using a bi-exponential IVIM model, S(b)=S0[(1 − f)e− bD+fe− bD∗], fitted with a Levenberg–Marquardt nonlinear least-squares algorithm. S(b) is the signal at b, and S0 is the signal at b = 0. Quantitative measurements were performed using manually placed circular ROIs. ROI placement was standardized using conventional MRI sequences as anatomical references. Contrast-enhanced T1-weighted images and FLAIR images were reviewed to identify the solid tumor component, while cystic, necrotic, hemorrhagic, peritumoral edema, and large vascular areas were carefully avoided. ROIs were first delineated on post-contrast T1-weighted and FLAIR images and propagated to parametric maps to minimize parameter-dependent selection bias. Three ROIs, approximately 0.26 cm² in size, were placed on the solid tumor component, and the mean values were calculated. The same ROIs were used for all IVIM-derived maps, ADC maps, and DSC rCBV maps (Figs. 2 and 3).

We used the Philips IntelliSpace® automated MR Neuro Perfusion application on the workstation to calculate rCBV values. To calculate rCBV values, an ROI of similar shape and size to the ROI placed on the glioma was placed on the contralateral normal-appearing white matter. To obtain rCBV, the values of the solid component of the tumor were divided by the values of the normal-appearing white matter.

To assess interobserver reproducibility, all quantitative measurements were independently performed by two radiologists with 5 and 9 years of experience in neuroimaging. The readers were blinded to each other’s measurements, and ROI placement was performed according to a predefined anatomical and imaging-based protocol.

Statistical analysis

Normality was assessed using the Shapiro–Wilk test. Continuous variables are reported as median (IQR) and mean ± SD. Because several variables were non-normally distributed, between-group comparisons were performed using the Mann–Whitney U test. Statistical significance was set at p < 0.05.

Diagnostic performance for glioma grading and IDH mutation status prediction was evaluated using receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curve analysis. AUCs with 95% CIs, p-values for AUCs, optimal cut-off values, sensitivity, and specificity were calculated. Optimal thresholds were determined using the Youden index. To internally validate the ROC-derived AUC estimates, stratified bootstrap resampling was performed with 1000 iterations. In each iteration, positive and negative cases were resampled separately with replacement to preserve the original class distribution. Bootstrap-validated mean AUCs and percentile-based 95% CIs were calculated for all ROC-derived diagnostic parameters, including rCBV, f, D, D*, ADC, and age, and are presented in Supplementary Table 1.

Pairwise AUC comparisons were performed using the DeLong test and reported as AUC differences with 95% CIs and p-values. For HGG–LGG differentiation, rCBV was used as the reference parameter because it showed the highest observed AUC in the ROC analysis. For IDH mutation status prediction, D was used as the reference parameter because it showed the highest observed AUC. Pairwise DeLong comparison results are presented in Supplementary Tables 2 and 3.

Sample size consideration

Given the retrospective design of the study, an a priori sample size calculation was not performed before patient inclusion. Instead, we included all consecutive adult patients who underwent brain MRI during the predefined study period and had histopathologically confirmed glioma with adequate imaging quality. Thus, the final sample size was determined by the number of eligible cases available during the study period. To partially address the limited sample size and class imbalance, we performed internal validation using 1000 stratified bootstrap resampling iterations for the ROC-derived AUC estimates. Nevertheless, the relatively small number of LGG and IDH-MT cases may limit the precision of subgroup estimates and pairwise AUC comparisons; therefore, the findings should be interpreted as preliminary and should be validated in larger, multicenter cohorts.

Correlations between rCBV and IVIM-derived parameters were assessed using Spearman’s rank correlation coefficient. Correlation strength was interpreted as negligible for |ρ| < 0.20, weak for 0.20–0.39, moderate for 0.40–0.59, strong for 0.60–0.79, and very strong for ≥ 0.80. Statistical analyses were performed using IBM SPSS Statistics, version 26.0, and Python. Descriptive statistics and group comparisons were performed in SPSS, whereas ROC-based analyses, DeLong pairwise AUC comparisons, and stratified bootstrap internal validation were performed in Python.

Results

Correlations of rCBV and IVIM parameters

Using Spearman’s rank correlation analysis, f showed a strong positive correlation with rCBV (Spearman’s ρ = 0.637, p < 0.001), D showed a moderate negative correlation with rCBV (ρ = −0.568, p < 0.001), and D* showed a weak positive but statistically non-significant correlation with rCBV (ρ = 0.222, p = 0.097).

Interobserver agreement

Interobserver reproducibility was good across all parameters: rCBV = 0.836 (95% CI, 0.74–0.90), f = 0.812 (95% CI, 0.71–0.88), D = 0.828 (95% CI, 0.73–0.89), D* = 0.80 (95% CI, 0.70–0.87), ADC = 0.821 (95% CI, 0.72–0.88). All ICC values were statistically significant (p < 0.001), indicating good interobserver agreement and supporting the reproducibility of the standardized manual ROI-based measurements.

Discussion

In this study, rCBV showed the highest observed AUC for differentiating HGG from LGG, whereas IVIM-derived D showed the highest observed AUC for predicting IDH mutation status. IVIM imaging may serve as a contrast-free alternative, especially for patients with contrast allergy, renal impairment, or children requiring repeated scans. Guo and Jiang demonstrated that Dmin and ADCmin could be used to differentiate HGG from LGG and predict IDH mutation status, with Dmin showing better diagnostic performance than ADCmin. Consistent with these findings, IVIM-derived D showed a higher AUC than ADC for predicting IDH mutation status in our cohort [10].

M. Yu et al. found that the rADC, rD, and rD* values were significantly higher in patients with IDH-MT gliomas than in those with IDH-WT gliomas [11]. In our study, we found that ADC and D values were significantly higher in glioma patients with IDH mutation compared to IDH-WT glioma patients, but D* values were not statistically significant in distinguishing HGG from LGG or in predicting IDH mutation in glioma patients. C. Wang and H. Dong found no significant difference in D* values between the IDH-WT and IDH-MT glioma groups in their study involving 72 patients with high-grade glioma, similar to our study.

The interpretation of D* values in our study should be considered limited. D* is the IVIM parameter most strongly influenced by rapid signal decay at very low b-values and is generally less stable than D and f. This limited low-b-value (< 200) sampling may have reduced the reliability and sensitivity of D* estimation and may explain why D* did not show significant differences between HGG and LGG or between IDH-WT and IDH-MT gliomas. Paganelli et al., in their segmented IVIM study aimed at optimizing b-value schemes for brain diffusion MRI, assumed that normal brain parenchyma behaves as a low-perfused tissue within the context of IVIM modeling. This assumption was supported by the finding that the in vivo perfusion fraction values measured in white and gray matter were close to those of the simulated low-perfusion regime. The authors also demonstrated that, when the number of b-values was reduced using a backward elimination strategy, D* generally made the largest contribution to the increase in total estimation error. This indicates that, among the IVIM parameters, the pseudodiffusion coefficient D* is the most sensitive to b-value reduction, particularly in low- and medium-perfusion regimes. Accordingly, although simplified protocols using only four b-values may be applicable for rapid imaging in highly perfused organs such as the liver, the authors stated that at least 10 b-values are generally required to obtain high-quality IVIM parameter maps in the brain [12]. In our simplified four-b-value protocol, the stability of the D value obtained from IVIM is due to the fact that it is dependent on signal attenuation at high b-values, where the contribution of microvascular pseudodiffusion becomes less pronounced. However, theoretically, D and f values can be calculated using only three b-values [13]. Reducing the number of b values may lead to shorter scan times and increased clinical efficiency. Hino et al. used three b-values (0, 300, and 1000 s/mm2) and 13 b-values (0, 10, 20, 30, 50, 80, 100, 200, 300, 400, 600, 800, and 1000 s/mm2) to differentiate high- and low-grade glial tumors using IVIM images obtained from 31 patients. According to this study, the f-values calculated with three b-values showed good agreement with the values obtained using 13 b-values, and simplified IVIM imaging with three b-values was found to be clinically effective in differentiating HGGs and LGGs [8].

In our study, the mean rCBV values of IDH-WT glioma patients were found to be approximately 2 times higher than the mean values of IDH-MT glioma patients. Álvarez-Torres, M. d. M. et al. found that the rCBV value was 2-2.5 times higher in the IDH-WT group than in IDH-MT [14].

Choi et al. developed an interpretable deep learning approach using DSC perfusion MRI to predict IDH mutation status. Consistent with our findings, their study showed that patients in the IDH-WT group were significantly older and had significantly higher mean rCBV values than those in the IDH-MT group [15]. Heo et al. used a deep learning approach based on DSC-MRI to predict local progression in patients with adult-type diffuse glioma, grade 4. They further demonstrated that multiparametric MRI incorporating conventional MRI and DSC-PWI predicted local progression with higher sensitivity than conventional MRI alone [16]. While these DSC-MRI studies rely on contrast-enhanced perfusion data and advanced deep learning analyses, our study utilized clinically interpretable quantitative parameters and directly compared DSC-derived rCBV with non-contrast IVIM-derived parameters for both glioma grading and IDH mutation prediction.

Federau et al. showed that IVIM f and DSC parameter rCBV, which is a prognostic factor showing survival rate in glial tumors, from 16 b-valued (0, 10, 20, 40, 80, 110, 140, 170, 200, 300, 400, 500, 600, 700, 800, 900 s/mm2) diffusion images in 27 patients. According to this study, the survival rate of patients with glioma decreased with increasing maximum IVIM f and rCBV values [17].

In our study, the ADC value in IDH-MT glioma patients was found to be significantly higher than in the IDH-WT patient group, consistent with the literature.

T. Alrashidi and colleagues demonstrated that D values increased more significantly in the group receiving temozolomide and MN58b treatment in a mouse model of glioblastoma compared to ADC [18]. These findings suggest that IVIM-derived parameters may have potential for monitoring treatment-related microstructural changes.

In our study, consistent with the existing literature, age differed significantly between HGG and LGG as well as between IDH-WT and IDH-MT gliomas. Age is biologically associated with IDH status and tumor grade, as it reflects the age-dependent histomolecular distribution of diffuse gliomas. Although older patients are more likely to have IDH-WT and/or higher-grade tumours, the independent prognostic impact of age appears to be limited in molecularly defined IDH-MT gliomas in previous studies, and it has been suggested that age may be acting primarily as a surrogate of IDH status [19, 20].

There are several potential advantages of IVIM imaging over contrast-enhanced perfusion techniques. It provides diffusion- and perfusion-related information without the administration of exogenous contrast media, which may be useful in patients with renal impairment, contrast allergy, or those requiring repeated follow-up imaging [21]. Unlike DSC MR imaging, IVIM maps can be generated from diffusion images obtained with only a few b-values, making them more accessible for a larger number of patients.

IVIM imaging has some limitations. Because IVIM is based on diffusion-weighted imaging, it is vulnerable to susceptibility artifacts. In brain IVIM imaging, CSF spaces and cerebral vessels may also affect parameter estimation. Therefore, ROI placement should be carefully restricted to the solid tumor component using conventional MR images as anatomical guidance, while avoiding cystic, necrotic, hemorrhagic, edematous, and large vascular areas.

Our study has several limitations. First, this was a retrospective single-center study with a limited sample size. Second, the study population was imbalanced, with substantially fewer LGG and IDH-MT gliomas than HGG and IDH-WT gliomas. Although stratified bootstrap internal validation was performed, the lack of external validation remains a major limitation. Third, quantitative measurements were based on manually placed ROIs. Although ROI placement was standardized using anatomical reference images and interobserver agreement was good for all parameters, ROI-based measurements may not fully capture intratumoral heterogeneity. Fourth, the simplified four-b-value IVIM protocol limits the interpretation of D* values, because accurate D* estimation requires adequate sampling of very low b-values. Therefore, the non-significant D* findings should primarily be interpreted as a methodological limitation of the simplified protocol. In addition, other glioma molecular biomarkers, such as 1p/19q codeletion and O6-methylguanine-DNA methyltransferase (MGMT) promoter methylation status, were not included in our study because they were not present in our patients’ pathological data.

In the literature, there is no consensus on how many b-values and which b-values should be used for IVIM parameters obtained from diffusion images in glioma patients. IVIM parameters obtained using different numbers and b-values have been used in various studies.

Conclusion

In this retrospective single-center cohort, IVIM-derived D showed the highest observed AUC for predicting IDH mutation status, whereas rCBV showed the highest observed AUC for differentiating HGG from LGG. A simplified four-b-value IVIM protocol may provide complementary non-contrast information in glioma assessment; however, the findings should be interpreted cautiously because of the limited sample size, class imbalance, lack of external validation, and reduced reliability of D* estimation. Larger multicenter studies using standardized IVIM protocols are required to confirm these results.
