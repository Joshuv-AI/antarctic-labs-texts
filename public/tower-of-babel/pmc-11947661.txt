Finding of the positive impact of glucose on the production of indican over indigo in engineered Escherichia coli
Authors: Hyun Jin Kim, Yeda Lee, Yuni Shin, Suhye Choi, Jinok Oh, Suwon Kim, Jungoh Ahn, Kwon-Young Choi, Jeong Chan Joo, Shashi Kant Bhatia, Yung-Hun Yang
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Abstract Indigo is a plant-based natural blue dye that can be produced via chemical synthesis and biological pathways. However, the toxic reduction processes and intracellular production of indigo through microbial metabolism are often limited by insolubility of indigo and complex downstream processing, causing environmental issues in the dyeing processes. Additionally, indican, a precursor of indigo with a glucose moiety, is highly soluble and can be easily converted into indoxyl by β-glucosidase, forming indigo under mild conditions. We constructed an indican-producing strain Escherichia coli BL21 HI201 by introducing a UDP-glycosyltransferase (ugt) into an indoxyl production system containing tryptophanse (tnaA) and flavin-containing monooxygenase (FMO) genes, enabling conversion of tryptophan into indican. Testing of the effect by various carbon sources suggested that glucose is one of the major factors affecting the ratio of indigo to indican, and increase in glucose concentration to more than 1.5% could produce sole indican without indigo. Under optimal conditions, E. coli BL21 HI201 biosynthesized 5.65 mM indican from tryptophan. Additionally, after deletion of various β-glucosidase genes, the bglA knockout strain E. coli BL21 HI204 produced more indican, achieving 6.79 mM after 24 hr of cultivation. This study demonstrated the strategic production of indican through the installation of a production system, deletion of a byproduct pathway, and control of glucose concentration.One-Sentence SummaryThis paper demonstrates the strategic enhancement of indican production in genetically engineered Escherichia coli BL21 by optimizing metabolic pathways and controlling glucose concentrations.Keywords: Indigo, Indican, UDP-D-glucose, UDP-glycosyltransferase

Introduction

Indigo is a blue dye that has been used for centuries in textiles, artwork, and cosmetics (Fabara & Fraaije, 2020b; Inoue et al., 2021). Although chemical synthesis methods have been developed to replace traditional extraction from plants such as Indigoferas sp. and Polygonum tinctorium, they involve the use of harmful chemicals such as formaldehyde, hydrogen cyanide, sodamide, and strong alkalis, leading to severe wastewater and toxicity issues (Yamamoto et al., 2011; Minami et al., 2015; Wang et al., 2019). Consequently, an interest in developing alternative biotechnological methods for indigo production is growing (Du et al., 2018).

Producing indigo through microorganism-based biotechnological methods is more environmentally friendly than traditional chemical synthesis, because they reduce the use of harmful chemicals and minimize waste (Yin et al., 2021; Zhang et al., 2022). Previous studies have reported indigo production using genetically engineered microorganisms such as Escherichia coli, Pseudomonas putida, Comamonas, and Acinetobacter (Pathak & Madamwar, 2010; Qu et al., 2012; Ahn et al., 2023). These microorganisms can convert substrates such as indole and tryptophan into indigo via the introduction of monooxygenase or dioxygenase enzymes (Han et al., 2008; Jiang et al., 2022). In a previous study, tryptophanase and flavin-containing monooxygenase (FMO) enzymes were introduced into an E. coli strain, and the tryptophan transporter genes aroP and tnaB were co-expressed, achieving a high indigo production titer of 8.77 mM (2.3 g/L) (Kim et al., 2024). However, the indigo produced by microorganisms remains insoluble, accumulates intracellularly, and requires complex processes for recovery and purification (Jin et al., 2022). Additionally, its distinctive blue color poses challenges for industrial use owing to difficulties in washing and other processes (Namgung et al., 2019).

Indican is a natural compound found in specific plants that produce indigo, such as Indigofera tinctoria and P olygonum tinctorium (Inoue et al., 2017). It acts as a precursor to indigo and consists of indoxyl and glucose groups linked via glycosylation (Gilbert et al., 2000). Indican synthesis is catalyzed by the enzyme UDP-glycosyltransferase (UGT), which facilitates the reaction between indoxyl and UDP-glucose (Petermeier et al., 2021). Furthermore, indican can be converted back into indigo through the action of β-glucosidase enzymes, and a biosynthetic pathway has been identified in previous studies (Inoue et al., 2018). Unlike indigo, indican is water-soluble and colorless, making it more convenient for industrial processing and applications (Luo et al., 2023). In addition, the enzymatic conversion pathway of indican offers the potential for more environmentally friendly indigo production methods (Inoue et al., 2020).

UGT is an enzyme that facilitates the transfer of glycosyl groups from activated donor molecules, such as UDP-glucose, to acceptor molecules, which can be small organic molecules, lipids, or proteins (Fan et al., 2017; Wang et al., 2023). The expression of UGT in E. coli induces the synthesis of glycosylated compounds such as siamenoside I, rosin, and gastrodin (Zhou et al., 2017; Xu et al., 2022; Cui et al., 2023a). To overcome the limitations imposed by the expensive sugar donor UDP-glucose, UDP-glucose regeneration systems by introducing enzymes such as sucrose synthase (Trobo-Maseda et al., 2020; Liu et al., 2021). Additionally, efforts have been made to overexpress UGT in microorganisms to utilize the intracellular UDP-sugar pool to produce glycosylated compounds (De Bruyn et al., 2015).

In this study, we designed an integrated process to improve indican production in E. coli, ultimately increasing the large-scale production and industrial applicability of indican with less indigo in culture systems. We constructed an indican-producing strain by co-expressing tryptophanase and oxygenase enzymes for tryptophan metabolism, along with enzymes that induce indoxyl glycosylation in E. coli BL21 strain. Furthermore, we evaluated the carbon sources affecting the ratio of indigo to indican with a significant production yield. This strategy focuses on maximizing the indican yield in E. coli BL21 through genetic engineering and media control, potentially paving the way for more sustainable and large-scale applications of bio-indigo.

Materials and Methods

Chemicals

Tryptophan, indican, indigo, and uracil were purchased from Sigma–Aldrich (St. Louis, MA, USA). Indoles were obtained from Alfa Aesar (Haverhill, MA, USA). Dimethyl sulfoxide (DMSO) was purchased from SAMCHUN Chemicals (Seoul, South Korea). Isopropyl-β-d-thiogalactopyranoside (IPTG) (>99%) was procured from Biosesang Co. (Seongnam, Korea). All other medium components were purchased from Difco (Detroit, MI, USA).

Plasmid Construction and Strains

The bacterial strains and plasmids used in this study are listed in Table 1. Escherichia coli DH5α and E. coli BL21(DE3) were used as hosts for genetic recombination and indican and indigo production, respectively. For tryptophan metabolism, a pCDFduet-1 vector containing the tryptophanase gene (tnaA) and the flavin containing monooxygenase gene was used. TnaA was derived from E. coli K12 and FMO was obtained from Methylophaga aminisulfidivorans. For glycosylated indoxyl, a pETduet-1 inserted with the P. tinctorium-derived UDP-glucosyltransferase gene (ugt) was used. The genes were inserted into plasmids by transformation using restriction enzymes and T4 ligation, and transformed into E. coli DH5α by heat shock.

Construction of Chromosomal DNA Deletion Mutants

To compare the effects of deleting β-glucosidase genes on indican accumulation, single- and double-knockout strains of bglA and bglB were constructed. A chromosomal DNA deletion mutant strain of E. coli BL21 was generated using the CRISPR/Cas9-based genome editing system. CRISPR/Cas9 genome editing was directed toward the target region based on a pCas9- and pgRNA-containing two-plasmid system. The pCas9 plasmid comprises Cas9 and λ-Red, whereas the pgRNA plasmid consists of the sgRNA sequence and the N20 sequences. The pgRNA targets the N20 sequence of the knockout target gene, which was added to the 5′ end of the primer. This was amplified using reverse polymerase chain reaction (PCR) followed by self-ligation. The editing template was created by amplifying approximately 500 bp upstream and downstream overlaps of the target gene from the genome via PCR, and each pair was connected to obtain a gene cassette.

Escherichia coli BL21(DE3) containing pCas9 was cultured in Lysogeny broth (LB) medium at 30 °C and induced with 10 mM arabinose for λ-Red induction. Cells were prepared in 100 μL aliquots for electroporation, mixed with 250 ng pgRNA and a 500 ng editing template, and then electroporated at 2.5 kV using a 2-mm Gene Pulser cuvette (Bio-Rad, Hercules, CA, USA). Subsequently, 900 μL of LB medium was added. After incubating at 30 °C for 4 hr, the cells were plated on LB plates containing kanamycin (50 μg/mL) and ampicillin (100 μg/mL) for selection. Colonies were selected and screened for deletion by PCR and sequencing.

Media, and Culture Conditions

Cells were pre-cultured and maintained at 37 °C in LB containing 10 g/L tryptone, 5 g/L yeast extract, and 5 g/L sodium chloride. Pre-culture was performed using a single colony of E. coli BL21 (DE3) from LB agar plates in 5 mL LB medium. The culture medium was incubated at 37 °C for 18 hr and shaken at 200 rpm. To produce indican, the cell culture was inoculated at a 1:50 (v/v) dilution into 5 mL of Terrific broth (TB; 12 g/L casein, 24 g/L yeast extract, 9.4 g/L dipotassium phosphate, and 2.2 g/L monopotassium phosphate) containing 10 mM tryptophan, 20 g/L glucose, and 3 mM uracil. Appropriate concentrations of antibiotics (100 μg/mL spectinomycin and 50 μg/mL ampicillin) were supplied for plasmid stability. When OD600 reached 0.8 after approximately 2 hr at 37 °C, 1 mM of IPTG was added to induce culture. Cell culture was moved to 30 °C for 18 hr of culture.

Optimization of Indican Production Medium

The production of indigo and indican in the strains E. coli BL21 HI101 and E. coli BL21 HI201, which were constructed for indigo and indican production, was compared based on different carbon sources. Indigo production in the E. coli BL21 HI101 strain was conducted in TB media with 10 mM tryptophan and 1% carbon source. The carbon sources used were glucose, xylose, fructose, galactose, glycerol, acetate, citrate, lactate, sucrose, and lactose, with a control condition where only tryptophan was added without any carbon source. Indican production in the E. coli BL21 HI201 strain was carried out in TB media with the addition of 10 mM tryptophan and 3 mM uracil, along with 1% of the carbon sources used in the previous indigo production experiment. The appropriate glucose concentration for large-scale indican production was determined by using TB media with 10 mM tryptophan and 3 mM uracil, supplemented with glucose as the substrate at concentrations of 0, 0.2, 0.4, 0.6, 0.8, 1, 1.5, 2, 2.5, and 3%.

To optimize the media composition for indican production, parameters such as media, temperature, IPTG concentration, pH, and tryptophan concentration were tested. All cultures were performed in media supplemented with 10 mM tryptophan, 2% glucose, and 3 mM uracil.

An equal amount of cells (OD600 of 0.8) was added to the reaction mixture and cultured at 37 °C. When the OD600 reached 0.8, IPTG induction was performed, followed by 18 hr of incubation to compare indican production. LB, TSB, TB, and 2X YT media were used for medium optimization, and incubation temperatures were tested at four different temperatures of 20, 25, 30, and 37 °C after IPTG induction. Induction condition optimization was performed by adjusting the IPTG concentrations to 0, 0.01, 0.025, 0.05, 0.1, 0.25, 0.5, and 1 mM, and pH levels were compared at 6, 7, 8, 9, and 10. Finally, the concentration of tryptophan was optimized by adding 0, 5, 10, 15, 20, and 25 mM tryptophan under the previously determined optimal conditions (TB medium, 30 °C, 1 mM IPTG induction, pH 8).

Determination of Bio-Indigo

To analyze indigo production, culture samples were centrifuged at 13 000 rpm for 15 min, washed twice with deionized water, and suspended in 1 mL DMSO. The suspended samples were sonicated with a bead beater homogenizer and centrifuged at 5 500 rpm for 1 min. The resulting supernatant was diluted to the appropriate concentration with DMSO and transferred to 96-well plates for spectrophotometric analysis using a UV–Vis spectrophotometer SpectraMax M2 (Molecular Devices). The absorbance was measured at 620 nm for indigo quantification (Cho et al., 2023).

Determination of Bio-Indican

Indican and tryptophan concentration were quantified using an high-performance liquid chromatography (HPLC) system equipped with a UV–Vis detector (YL-9100; South Korea). Samples were detected at 280 nm using a reverse phase C18 column (ZORBAX SB-C18 column, 4.6 × 250 mm, 5 μm particle size; Agilent Technologies, USA). The oven temperature was constantly maintained at 35 °C during operation. Water and 100% methanol were used as mobile phases A and B, respectively. The flow rate was maintained at 0.8 mL/min, using the following gradient program (A: B, v/v), 0–20 min, 80:20; 20–25 min, 20:80; 25–34 min, 20:80; 34–38 min, 80:20; and 38–40 min, 80:20. For sample preparation, the supernatant was filtered using a 0.22-μm Millex-GP syringe filter unit and diluted with HPLC-grade water. The filtered and diluted samples were transferred into vials and used to detect tryptophan and indole. The retention times were 10.5 and 15.3 min, respectively (Cho et al., 2023).

Results and Discussion

Introduction of Glycosyltransferase for Indican Production

In microbial metabolism of tryptophan, tryptophanase converts tryptophan into indole, which is then converted to indoxyl by FMO enzyme (Han et al., 2008; Du et al., 2022). Indoxyl spontaneously oxidizes and dimerizes to form the blue dye, indigo (Fabara & Fraaije, 2020a). However, indigo is insoluble in water and accumulates intracellularly, necessitating additional processes for its recovery and purification (Fräbel et al., 2018).

Therefore, this study proposes a new strategy for large-scale indigo production by producing indican, the indigo precursor, through biotechnological engineering. Hence, we aimed to produce indican, a glycosylated form of indoxyl, by introducing a ugt into a previously established indoxyl production system. An E. coli BL21 HI201 strain was constructed by transforming E. coli with both ugt-expressing plasmids and indigo-producing plasmids, which expressed FMO and tnaA (Fig. 1). Glycosyltransferases induce indoxyl glycosylation in the presence of UDP-d-glucose, resulting in the production of indican (Hsu et al., 2018). The indican produced is colorless, water-soluble, and released outside the cell, where it can be found in the supernatant (Song et al., 2010).

Construction of indican-producing strains through the introduction of ugt in indigo-producing strains.

Considering that the indican production system produces a certain amount of indigo, which requires further cleaning of apparatuses such as flasks and fermenters, and indican needs sugar moieties such as glucose, the impact of various carbon and sugar sources needs to be examined. In addition, because a higher ratio of indican to indigo could make the processes easier and cleaner, this point was considered. The addition of glucose significantly increased the ratio of indigo to indican compared to previous studies (Hsu et al., 2018).

Effects of Carbon Sources in Indigo and Indican Production

Indican is a glycosylated form of indoxyl in which the indoxyl group is bonded to glucose (Hsu et al., 2018). Previous studies have shown that carbon sources, especially glucose, can inhibit the expression and activity of tnaA, which is necessary for the conversion of tryptophan into indole in E. coli, thereby negatively affecting tryptophan metabolism (Li & Young, 2014). Therefore, the production of indigo and indican was compared based on different carbon sources in a previously established indigo-producing strain, E. coli BL21 HI101, and a newly constructed indican-producing strain E. coli BL21 HI201. Indigo production decreased in the presence of carbon sources, with almost no indigo being produced when glucose, xylose, or fructose were added (Fig. 2a). With the addition of disaccharides such as sucrose and lactose, indigo production decreased by half. In the control, 2.57 mM indigo was produced without a carbon source. These results suggest that the presence of carbon sources negatively affects tryptophan metabolism, leading to reduced indigo production.

Effects of carbon sources on indigo and indican production. (a) Indigo production. (b) Indican production. (c) Indican production according to glucose concentration.

Indican production of the glycosyltransferase-introduced E. coli BL21 HI201 was conducted in TB medium supplemented with tryptophan, uracil, and 1% carbon source. Uracil was used as a co-substrate to aid the supply of UDP for UDP-glucose, which is used in the conversion of indoxyl into indican. In contrast to indigo, indican production was the highest (4.14 mM) when glucose was added, whereas indigo production was minimal (0.12 mM) (Fig. 2b). This was because the added glucose was used as a substrate for the glycosylation of indoxyl, and thus did not interfere with tryptophan metabolism. The addition of carbon sources other than glucose did not significantly increase indican production. Therefore, glucose was used as a co-substrate for indican production in subsequent experiments. Considering that the glucose in the UDP-glucose system was natively installed in E. coli using glucokinase (glk), phosphoglucomutase (pgm), and UTP-glucose-1-phosphate uridylyltransferase (galU), the addition of glucose seemed to supply more UDP-glucose, resulting in increased production of indican. In addition, as shown in Fig. 2a, indican production appeared to be increased by the glucose-led inhibition of indigo.

Therefore, the optimal glucose concentration for large-scale indican production was determined. Indican production increased sharply with an increase in glucose concentration from 0 to 2%. The highest indican production was observed with 2% glucose, at an yield of 4.97 mM (Fig. 2c). In addition, as the glucose concentration increased, indigo production decreased, and no indigo was produced at concentrations above 1.5%, suggesting that glucose could be a major effector to control the ratio of indican to indigo. In subsequent experiments, 2% glucose was used as the substrate for indican production.

Optimization of Indican Production

Several medium compositions were optimized for the mass production of indican. First, the medium was optimized. Indican production was the highest in TB medium (4.84 mM), which was more than twice the amount produced in LB medium (Fig. 3a). The amount of residual tryptophan was also the lowest in the TB medium (5.02 mM) among the four media. This indicates that TB is the most suitable cultivation medium for indican production.

Optimization of culture condition. Indican production by PtUGT co-expression in the FMO- and tnaA-expressing strains according to (a) media, (b) temperature, (c) IPTG concentration, and (d) pH.

The cultivation temperature was then subsequently optimized. The tertiary structure of proteins is affected by temperature, which influences protein denaturation, misfolding, and enzymatic activity. This optimization process aimed to identify the optimal induction temperature for active protein expression and produce more biological indican. After inoculation and cultivation at 37 °C, the strains were induced with IPTG at an OD600 of 0.8 and then incubated at four different temperatures. Indican production was the highest at 30 °C, reaching 4.74 mM (Fig. 3b). Therefore, in subsequent experiments, the strains were maintained at 30 °C after IPTG induction.

Various concentrations of IPTG were added to optimize induction conditions. After inoculation, when the OD600 reached 0.8, different concentrations of IPTG were added, and the cultures were incubated at 30 °C for a total of 18 hr. Indican was not produced without IPTG, and its production increased at higher IPTG concentrations, reaching a maximum of 5.01 mM at 1 mM IPTG induction (Fig. 3c). Residual tryptophan decreased as the IPTG concentration increased and remained constant at concentrations above 0.25 mM.

Next, the pH of the culture medium was optimized. Uracil was used to supply UDP, but owing to the water solubility issues of uracil, 1 M NaOH was used for dissolution, which increased the pH. Therefore, the optimized culture medium was adjusted to different pH levels (6, 7, 8, 9, and 10). The pH of the control was 7.23. Indican production was the highest at pH 8 (5.51 mM), which was 1.25 times higher than that produced in the control (4.41 mM) (Fig. 3d).

Effect of Tryptophan Concentration on Indican Production and Time-Dependent Monitoring of the Reaction

The substrate tryptophan, along with glucose, is the most important component in the production of indican. To determine the optimal concentration of tryptophan, different concentrations of tryptophan were added under the previously established optimal conditions and used for culturing E. coli BL21 HI201. Indican production increased rapidly with an increase in tryptophan concentration from 0 to 15 mM. When tryptophan concentrations exceeded 15 mM, indigo production increased slightly, but remained stable. Indican production was the highest at 25 mM tryptophan, reaching 6.55 mM (Fig. 4a). Therefore, considering the yield relative to the substrate, 10 mM tryptophan was selected as the optimal substrate concentration for indican production.

Adjustment of substrate and culture time to increase indican production under optimal conditions. (a) Indican production according to tryptophan concentration. (b) Time-dependent monitoring of indican production in E. coli.

Time-dependent monitoring of the indican-producing strain was performed to determine the optimal time for indican production. The E. coli BL21 HI201 cells were cultured in TB medium supplemented with 10 mM tryptophan, 2% glucose, and 3 mM uracil. The total cultivation time was 26 hr, with cells harvested every 2 hr for quantification of indican, indigo, and residual tryptophan. Indican production gradually increased, reaching a maximum of 5.66 mM after 18 hr of cultivation, after which it decreased (Fig. 4b). The amount of residual tryptophan in the supernatant decreased over time and stabilized after 18 hr. Therefore, the optimal time for indican production was determined to be 18 hr.

Meanwhile, indigo production gradually increased over time, reaching 0.6 mM after 26 hr. At the optimal time of 18 hr, the indican-to-indigo ratio was 31.4. Previous studies using glycerol as a substrate reported an indican-to-indigo ratio of 15, which represents a significant increase of 2.09 times. This indicated that the addition of glucose as a carbon source was crucial for enhancing the indican/indigo ratio, supporting the notion that the introduction of glucose can regulate the production of indican and indigo.

Deletion of β-Glucosidase (bgl) Gene to Prevent Reconversion of Indican to Indoxyl

In the E. coli BL21 HI201 strain, indican production gradually increased after cultivation but sharply decreased after 18 hr. In contrast, indigo production gradually increased over time, even after 18 hr. Indican has previously been reported to be hydrolyzed by various β-glucosidases, and E. coli contains several natural β-glucosidase genes (Song et al., 2010). It is possible that indican produced by β-glucosidase genes was hydrolyzed into indoxyl, which then dimerized to form indigo. Therefore, to prevent the reconversion of indican to indoxyl, several β-glucosidase genes in E. coli were knocked out (Fig. 5a).

Deletion of the β-glucosidase (bgl) gene to prevent reconversion of indican to indoxyl. (a) Conversion of indican into indoxyl by β-glucosidase. (b) Comparison of indican production in several β-glucosidase knockout strains. (c) Time-dependent indican production and indigo accumulation under optimal conditions in the bglA knockout strain.

Escherichia coli has two well-known glucosidases: β-glucosidase A (BglA) and β-glucosidase B (BglB). Previous studies have shown that deletion of bglA significantly reduces the hydrolysis of indican (Hsu et al., 2018). In this study, we constructed single and double knockout strains of bglA and bglB to compare the effects of deleting these β-glucosidase genes on indican accumulation. The knockout single and double mutants were transformed with both the indigo-producing plasmids containing FMO and tnaA and the ugt-containing plasmid, resulting in the indican-producing knockout strains E. coli BL21 HI202, HI203, and HI204. Indican production in the knockout strains was compared under the optimized conditions. Indican production in the bglA knockout strain was 6.4 mM, which was 1.15 times higher than that in the control (Fig. 5b). Additionally, the dry cell weight was higher in the bglA knockout strain. In contrast, for bglB, both single and double mutants produced less indican than the control. Therefore, the E. coli BL21 HI204 strain with the bglA knockout was selected for the large-scale microbial production of indican.

Time-dependent monitoring was performed to determine the maximum titer of indican production in E. coli BL21 HI204. The E. coli BL21 HI204 strain was cultured in TB medium supplemented with 25 mM tryptophan, 2% glucose, and 3 mM uracil. The total cultivation time was 32 hr, with cells harvested every 2 hr for the quantification of indican and residual tryptophan. Indican production gradually increased, reaching a maximum of 6.78 mM after 24 hr of cultivation. The amount of residual tryptophan in the supernatant gradually decreased over time. Indigo production started to appear slightly after 20 hr but was not significant. This was likely due to a decrease in glucose concentration over time during cell growth and indican production, resulting in no further production of indican and increased production of indigo. In this study, more indican was produced through glucose regulation and the deletion of β-glucosidase genes. After 24 hr of cultivation, we achieved an indican to indigo ratio of 161.4, establishing an efficient indicant production system.

Conclusions

In conclusion, this study presents a strategy using genetic engineering and microbial systems for sustainable and efficient production of indican. We co-expressed FMO and tnaA genes, with UDP-glycosyltransferase (ugt), which is involved in indoxyl glycosylation, in E. coli to produce indican, a key precursor in indigo biosynthesis. The introduction of ugt enabled the glycosylation of indoxyl to produce indican. Unlike indigo biosynthesis, glucose proved to be a strategic enhancer of indican production. The addition of glucose reduced the amount of indigo produced and increased indicant production. By optimizing the cultivation conditions and conducting time-dependent monitoring, we recorded a maximum indican production of 5.65 mM. In Addition, we compared the effects of deleting various β-glucosidase genes to prevent the reconversion of indican to indoxyl. The bglA knockout strain produced up to 6.78 mM indican under optimal conditions. Although previous studies have focused on in vivo indican production through genetic engineering, this study specifically demonstrates the potential to adjust the production ratio by regulating glucose, reducing the production of indigo, and increasing the production of its precursor, indican. Producing indican can mitigate the challenges of recovery and quantitative limitations associated with indigo insolubility and may serve as a biosynthetic model for other natural products. Further research is required to increase production yields; however, overall, this study highlights the production of indican using genetic engineering and control of media to avoid colored byproducts.
