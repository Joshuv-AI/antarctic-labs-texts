A high-quality chromosome-level genome assembly of apple of Peru (Nicandra physalodes)
Authors: Guoqian Hao, Lan Yu, Mingcheng Wang, Heliang He
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractNicandra physalodes, a member of the Solanaceae family, is known for its medicinal potential and strong natural insect-repellent properties, which are mainly attributed to its bioactive withanolides and alkaloids. Despite its ecological and pharmacological significance, genomic information for this species has remained limited. Here, we generated a chromosome-level reference genome for N. physalodes based on PacBio high-fidelity (HiFi) long-read sequencing and Hi-C scaffolding. The assembled genome is 933.97 Mb in size, with a contig N50 of 87.37 Mb, and 99.95% (933.54 Mb) of the sequences anchored to 10 pseudochromosomes. Repetitive elements account for 73.06% of the genome, and 27,925 protein-coding genes were predicted, 97.81% of which were functionally annotated. This genomic resource provides a valuable foundation for investigating the genetic basis of specialized metabolite biosynthesis, insect resistance, and environmental adaptation in N. physalodes, as well as for comparative studies within the Solanaceae family.

Background & Summary

The Solanaceae family comprises approximately 90 genera and more than 3,000 species, including many economically important crops such as Solanum, Capsicum, and Nicotiana1. Members of this family are renowned for producing a wide array of specialized metabolites, particularly alkaloids, which serve as chemical defenses and have significant pharmacological applications2–4. Advances in genomics have greatly enhanced our understanding of Solanaceae biology5,6. High-quality reference genomes of model species such as S. lycopersicum (tomato)7, S. tuberosum (potato)8, C. annuum (pepper)9, and N. tabacum (tobacco)10 have provided key insights into metabolic pathway diversity and adaptive evolution. However, genomic resources remain limited for many non-model Solanaceous plants with unique biochemical and ecological characteristics6,11. Expanding genomic studies beyond major crop genera is therefore crucial for uncovering novel metabolic pathways and understanding chemical defense and adaptation in this family.

Nicandra physalodes (commonly known as the apple of Peru or shoo-fly plant) is a distinctive member of the Solanaceae family recognized for its medicinal value and pronounced insect-repellent properties. This annual herb has an erect, ridged stem reaching 0.4–1.5 m in height, with ovate to elliptic leaves and attractive pale blue, bell-shaped flowers up to 4 cm in diameter that produce distinctive inflated calyces enclosing yellow, berry-like fruits. Native to South America and now naturalized in many tropical and temperate regions worldwide, it commonly grows in disturbed habitats such as roadsides, wastelands, and cultivated fields. The common name “shoo-fly plant” derives from its traditional use in deterring insect pests. Phytochemical analyses have identified a rich array of bioactive metabolites, including withanolides and tropane-related alkaloids such as nicandrenone, tropinone, and hygrine, which exhibit antioxidant, antimicrobial, and insect-deterrent activities12,13. These compounds constitute the chemical basis of the plant’s defense strategies and contribute to its ecological role in natural pest management and ecological adaptability across diverse environments6,14. In addition, the plant has long been used in traditional medicine as an analgesic, antibacterial, anti-inflammatory, and diuretic agent14. Owing to its pharmacological potential and remarkable environmental resilience, N. physalodes represents a valuable system for exploring the genetic mechanisms underlying specialized metabolism and stress responses. However, the molecular foundations of these adaptive and bioactive traits remain largely unknown due to the lack of genomic resources.

Here, we present a high-quality, chromosome-level genome assembly of N. physalodes generated using PacBio high-fidelity (HiFi) long-read sequencing combined with Hi-C scaffolding. The assembled genome enables comprehensive annotation of repetitive elements and protein-coding genes (PCGs), providing a robust framework for investigating the genomic architecture of this species. This reference genome offers a valuable resource for elucidating the genetic basis of specialized metabolite biosynthesis, adaptive evolution, and insect resistance in N. physalodes. Moreover, it enriches the genomic landscape of the Solanaceae family, facilitating comparative analyses and advancing our understanding of the evolutionary diversification of plant chemical defense systems.

Methods

Sample collection, DNA and RNA sequencing

To sequence the genome of N. physalodes, healthy young leaves were collected from an individual plant (Fig. 1A) located in Emeishan City, Sichuan Province, southwest China (29.58178°N, 103.43085°E). High-molecular-weight DNA was extracted using a modified CTAB protocol15, followed by the construction of SMRTbell libraries according to the PacBio 15-kb protocol. Circular consensus sequencing (CCS) was performed using the PacBio Revio platform to generate HiFi long reads, which were subsequently used for genome survey analysis and de novo assembly. Additionally, Hi-C libraries were constructed from fresh leaf tissues following a standard protocol involving formaldehyde crosslinking, chromatin digestion with the restriction enzyme MboI, biotin labeling, ligation, and DNA purification. The libraries, with insert sizes ranging from 300 to 500 bp, were sequenced on the Illumina NovaSeq 6000 platform, generating 150 bp paired-end reads for Hi-C scaffolding analysis. Total RNA was extracted from young leaves and fully opened flowers of the same N. physalodes plant, collected during the flowering period in July 2023. RNA-seq libraries were constructed using the TruSeq Stranded mRNA-Seq protocol and sequenced on the Illumina NovaSeq 6000 platform with 150 bp paired-end reads to support genome evaluation and annotation. In total, 30.92 Gb of HiFi reads, 148.09 Gb of Hi-C reads, and 15.39 Gb of RNA-seq reads were generated in this study (Table 1).

Genome assembly

A k-mer (k = 19) frequency distribution was generated from HiFi reads using Jellyfish v1.1.1216 and analyzed with GenomeScope217 to survey the genome of N. physalodes before assembly. This analysis identified a total of 29,806,259,107 k-mers, showing a major peak at a depth of 32. The GenomeScope2 model estimated a genome size of 850.26 Mb, with a heterozygosity rate of 0.152% and a repeat content of 58.9% (Fig. 1B). HiFi reads were processed using the CCS workflow in SMRT Link v8.0 (PacBio) and assembled with hifiasm v0.19.918 under default parameters, yielding a primary assembly of 791 contigs with a total length of 979.89 Mb. Potential redundant haplotypes were removed with Purge Haplotigs v1.1.119. To screen for potential contamination, the purged contigs were aligned against the NCBI Nucleotide database; sequences showing similarity to bacteria, archaea, viruses, mitochondria, or chloroplasts were filtered out. Additionally, HiFi reads were realigned to the polished contigs using minimap2 v2.2820, and contigs with low coverage (<5) or abnormal GC content (>45%) were removed based on GC-depth analysis of 10-kb windows. Subsequently, Hi-C reads were aligned to the assembled contigs using Juicer v1.8.821, and uniquely mapped reads were employed by 3D-DNA software22 to anchor and orient the contigs into pseudochromosomes. Hi-C contact maps were manually inspected to correct misassemblies, yielding a finalized chromosome-level genome assembly. The assembled genome of N. physalodes totaled 933.97 Mb and consisted of 24 contigs with an N50 of 87.37 Mb (Fig. 2a; Table 2). Notably, 99.95% (933.54 Mb) of the assembled sequences were anchored to 10 pseudochromosomes (Fig. 2b), consistent with the chromosome number reported for N. physalodes in The Chromosome Counts Database23. The pseudochromosomes ranged in size from 63.99 Mb to 108.33 Mb, comprising three gapless chromosomes, six with a single gap, and one containing three gaps (Table 3).

Genome annotation

Repetitive elements were identified through a combination of de novo and homology-based approaches using RepeatModeler v2.0.524 and RepeatMasker v4.0.925, following the method described by Wang et al.26. In total, 682.34 Mb of repetitive sequences were detected, accounting for 73.06% of the N. physalodes genome (Fig. 2c; Table 4). Long terminal repeat (LTR) retrotransposons constituted the predominant class, occupying 535.08 Mb (57.29%) of the genome. The remaining repetitive elements included unclassified sequences (100.23 Mb), DNA transposons (29.84 Mb), long interspersed nuclear elements (LINEs; 15.35 Mb), simple repeats (2.42 Mb), and short interspersed nuclear elements (SINEs; 2.01 Mb) (Table 4).

After masking all repetitive elements, PCGs in the N. physalodes genome were predicted using three complementary approaches. First, homology-based prediction was performed by aligning protein sequences from six representative plant species—S. lycopersicum ITAG3.27, S. tuberosum v4.038, C. annuum v29, Nicotiana tabacum v4.510, Arabidopsis thaliana TAIR1027, and Petunia inflata v1.028—to the assembled genome using BLASTN v2.2.31 + 29, followed by gene structure refinement with GeneWise v2.4.130. Second, RNA-seq reads were assembled with Trinity v2.8.431, and the resulting transcripts were aligned to the genome for gene model reconstruction using PASA v2.3.332. Third, AUGUSTUS v3.2.333 was trained using high-confidence gene models (with more than two exons and coding sequences longer than 1,500 bp) derived from PASA annotations, and the optimized parameters were subsequently applied for de novo gene prediction. Finally, all predicted models were integrated into a nonredundant consensus gene set using EVidenceModeler v1.1.134.

A total of 27,925 PCGs were annotated in the N. physalodes genome, with average lengths of 4,471 bp for transcripts, 1,289 bp for coding sequences, 227 bp for exons, and 681 bp for introns (Table 2). Among these, 27,904 genes were anchored to the 10 pseudochromosomes (Table 3). Gene function annotation was performed by aligning all predicted protein sequences against public databases, including SwissProt, TrEMBL35, InterPro36, Gene Ontology (GO)37, and the Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG)38. In total, 27,313 PCGs (97.81%) were successfully annotated in at least one database, while 612 genes (2.19%) remained unannotated (Fig. 3). Among the annotated genes, 26,228 (93.92%) matched entries in TrEMBL, 26,022 (93.19%) in InterPro, and 18,013 (64.50%) in SwissProt. Functional pathway assignment in KEGG covered 9,501 genes (34.02%), and 24,092 genes (86.27%) were classified into GO categories. These results demonstrate a high level of annotation completeness and provide a comprehensive foundation for downstream functional and comparative genomic analyses.

Comparative genomic analysis

To investigate the evolutionary dynamics of N. physalodes and its related species, we performed gene family clustering analysis using OrthoFinder v3.0.1b139 on the protein sequences of N. physalodes and six other plant species: S. lycopersicum, S. tuberosum, C. annuum, Nicotiana tabacum, P. inflata, and A. thaliana (as outgroup). A total of 36,594 orthogroups were identified, of which 1,470 were single-copy orthogroups shared by all seven species. Protein sequences of these single-copy orthogroups were individually aligned using MAFFT-linsi v7.52540, and then concatenated into a supermatrix. A maximum likelihood phylogenetic tree was constructed using RAxML v8.2.1241 under the PROTGAMMAILGX model with 500 fast bootstrap replicates. As a result, N. physalodes clustered together with the clade comprising S. lycopersicum, S. tuberosum, and C. annuum with 100% bootstrap support (Fig. 4A). Divergence time analysis using MCMCTREE in PAML v4.10.742 estimated that N. physalodes diverged from the Solanum-Capsicum clade approximately 23.4 million years ago.

To gain further insights into gene family dynamics, we performed a CAFE analysis (v3.1)43 and identified that 72 gene families were significantly expanded (P < 0.01) in N. physalodes, while 115 gene families were significantly contracted (Fig. 4A). Notably, expanded families were significantly enriched in GO terms related to terpenoid biosynthesis (GO:0016106, GO:0016102, GO:0016114), indole alkaloid biosynthesis (GO:0035835), and defense response (GO:0009682, GO:0052544, GO:0009694) (Fig. 4B). These expansions likely contribute to the biosynthesis of bioactive compounds such as withanolides and tropane-related alkaloids, as well as the strong insect-repellent properties of N. physalodes.

Data Records

The genome assembly of N. physalodes and all associated sequencing data have been deposited in the NCBI database under BioProject accession PRJNA117632044. The assembled genome is available in GenBank under accession JBMWEB01000000045. Raw sequencing datasets, including PacBio HiFi and Hi-C reads, are available in the Sequence Read Archive (SRA) under accessions SRR3117652846 and SRR3118020447. The RNA-seq data have been deposited in the SRA under accessions SRR3107868348 and SRR3107868449. Additionally, the genome assembly, along with annotations of repetitive elements, gene structures, and functional features, has been archived in Figshare50.

Technical Validation

The quality of the N. physalodes genome assembly was evaluated using multiple complementary approaches. First, RNA-seq reads were aligned to the final assembly using HISAT2 v2.1.051, achieving an overall alignment rate of 98.68%, indicative of high assembly completeness. Second, Benchmarking Universal Single-Copy Orthologs (BUSCO, v4.1.2)52 analysis was conducted on both the genome assembly and gene annotation using the “embryophyte_odb10” dataset. The results showed 98.20% complete BUSCOs at the assembly level and 93.56% at the protein level (Table 2). The base-level accuracy and completeness were evaluated using a Merqury analysis (v1.3)53, resulting in a quality value of 67.32 and a k-mer (k = 21) completeness score of 99.41% (Fig. 5). To further evaluate the gene annotation, we employed the GAQET2 pipeline54. The annotation achieved a PSAURON score of 97.1, indicating high structural accuracy of the gene models. OMArk analysis showed 88.31% consistency and 84.30% completeness based on Solanaceae-specific ortholog groups. DETENGA identified less than 1% (0.97%) of potential transposable element (TE)-related genes under the strictest criterion. Finally, the LTR Assembly Index was calculated using LTR_retriever v3.0.155, yielding a score of 17.42, consistent with a reference-quality genome. Collectively, these results demonstrate the high completeness, accuracy, and integrity of the N. physalodes genome assembly.
