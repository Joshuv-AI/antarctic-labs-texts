Atovaquone-induced oxidative stress activates the pentose phosphate pathway and Immunogenic cell death in ovarian cancer
Authors: Mayra Betancourt Ponce, Nicha Boonpattrawong, Alexander J. Fagan, Michael Newton, Manish Patankar, Lisa Barroilhet
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Atovaquone, an FDA-approved oxidative phosphorylation (OXPHOS) inhibitor, has shown promise in the treatment of epithelial ovarian cancer (EOC), the deadliest gynecologic malignancy. However, the precise mechanisms underlying its antitumorigenic effects remain unclear. We employed a longitudinal transcriptomic approach to characterize the molecular effects of atovaquone on EOC cells. Our findings demonstrate that atovaquone disrupts cellular homeostasis and metabolism, activates stress responses, and primes immune recognition. We observed temporal downregulation of genes and pathways involved in key cellular processes, such as the cell cycle and DNA replication, which correlated with reduced proliferative capacity. Atovaquone also downregulated both OXPHOS and glycolysis while upregulating the pentose phosphate pathway, suggesting a metabolic shift toward redox homeostasis restoration in response to severe oxidative stress. Consistent with oxidative stress, we found that atovaquone activated endoplasmic reticulum (ER) stress, which is linked to immunogenic cell death. During ER stress, calreticulin, a damage-associated molecular pattern (DAMP), translocates to the plasma membrane, where it promotes immune recognition. We observed that calreticulin was upregulated on the plasma membrane of atovaquone-treated EOC cells. Additionally, we detected increased levels of other DAMPs, such as high mobility group box 1 (HMGB1) and mitochondrial transcription factor A (TFAM), in the supernatants of atovaquone-treated cells, indicating the release of immunogenic molecules. Moreover, increased expression of ligands for activating receptors of NK cells was observed, and coculture experiments revealed enhanced NK cell activity toward atovaquone-treated cells. These results highlight atovaquone’s potential to activate immune responses, offering a new avenue for combination therapies in EOC treatment.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-025-25704-y.

Introduction

Epithelial ovarian cancer (EOC) is the most common cause of gynecologic cancer deaths worldwide. Owing to suboptimal screening methods and late symptomatic presentation, over 70% of patients are diagnosed at advanced stages (stages III and IV) and have disseminated disease1,2. In patients with advanced disease, cytoreductive surgery is followed by adjuvant chemotherapy, which typically consists of a combination of platinum and taxane agents2,3. Although this treatment regimen is often initially effective for disease control, most patients experience disease recurrence and ultimately succumb to their disease3. Currently, the 5-year survival rates for EOC patients with stages III and IV disease are 41% and 20%, respectively1, highlighting the need for improved treatments.

Targeting oxidative phosphorylation (OXPHOS) has emerged as a promising treatment strategy for cancers, including EOC4,5. OXPHOS is the primary metabolic process by which cells produce energy through the reduction of oxygen in the mitochondrial electron transport chain. The therapeutic avenue of targeting OXPHOS in cancer has been unexplored because cancer cells rely on glycolytic metabolism for rapid energy production and proliferation, a process known as the Warburg effect6. However, more recent observations have shown that subsets of tumor cells preferentially undergo OXPHOS or can adopt oxidative metabolism through metabolic plasticity, a process linked to disease progression and chemoresistance6–8, emphasizing its clinical relevance. In EOC, OXPHOS has been linked to tumor invasiveness, stemness, and platinum drug resistance9–11, making this pathway an attractive therapeutic target.

Accumulating evidence suggests that OXPHOS inhibitors, such as the small molecules IM156 and IACS-010759, have strong cytotoxic and antiproliferative effects in preclinical tumor models12,13. However, the clinical success of these compounds has been limited by their toxicity and off-target effects14,15. In this study, we focused on an alternative agent with a safe and well-tolerated toxicity profile, atovaquone16,17.

Atovaquone is an FDA-approved OXPHOS inhibitor that was originally developed for malaria treatment and prevention and is now being repurposed as an anticancer agent18,19. It targets complex III of the electron transport chain by acting as a competitive inhibitor of one of the complex’s main components, ubiquinol20. Our laboratory previously demonstrated that the OXPHOS inhibitor atovaquone has antitumorigenic effects on EOC4. Given these findings, we hypothesized that atovaquone might have molecular effects secondary to oxidative stress that render EOC cells susceptible to this treatment, which has not yet been elucidated. This study expounds on our previous findings and describes the multifaceted effects of atovaquone in EOC cells, which are critical for its use as an anticancer agent.

We adopted a transcriptomic approach to characterize global gene expression changes induced by atovaquone in EOC cells. Our findings revealed that atovaquone-mediated oxidative stress can trigger downstream metabolic adaptations, including the activation of the pentose phosphate pathway, and cellular events, such as the endoplasmic reticulum stress response, that increase EOC cell susceptibility to cytotoxicity and immune activation. These results highlight that, beyond OXPHOS inhibition, atovaquone exposes molecular vulnerabilities in EOC cells that can be therapeutically targeted. Moreover, our results reinforce the emerging potential of OXPHOS inhibition as a viable approach for cancer treatment.

Methods

Cell lines and cell culture

The OVCAR3 and OVCAR5 cell lines of female sex were utilized. The cells were maintained in the recommended culture media and incubated at 37 °C in a humidified atmosphere with 5% CO2. OVCAR3 cells were purchased from the American Type Culture Collection (ATCC, #HTB-161) and OVCAR5 cells from Sigma Aldrich (#SCC259). The cell lines were used within six months of purchase or validated via single tandem repeat (STR) analysis performed by the Translational Research Initiatives in Pathology (TRIP) laboratory at the University of Wisconsin, Madison. The cell lines were tested for mycoplasma contamination regularly via the Mycoscope™ PCR Mycoplasma Detection Kit (Genlantis, Avantor, #10497-510), and all the experiments were performed on mycoplasma-negative cells.

Reagents and preparation

Atovaquone (purity ≥ 98%) was purchased from TCI America (Avantor, #A2545200MG) and solubilized in DMSO (Thermo Fisher Scientific, #J66650. AP) at 10 mg/ml. Tauroursodeoxycholic acid (TUDCA) was purchased from Selleck Chemicals (#S3654) and solubilized in DMSO at 100 mg/ml. Stocks were stored at −20 °C and thawed as needed.

Cell viability assays

Cell viability was measured via 3-(4,5-dimethylthiazol-2-yl)-2,5-diphenyltetrazolium bromide (MTT) assays as previously described4,21. The cells were seeded in 96-well plates and incubated overnight. The cells were then treated with the indicated agents or vehicle (DMSO) for 24 and 48 h. Following treatment, MTT (Sigma‒Aldrich, #475989) solution (0.2 mg/ml in PBS) was added to the wells, and the cells were incubated at 37 °C. After 3 h, the medium containing the MTT solution was removed, and 100 µl of DMSO was added to each well. The absorbance was measured at 570 nm via a SpectraMax M3 microplate reader at the Cancer Pharmacology Laboratory at the University of Wisconsin, Madison.

Quantitative polymerase chain reaction (qPCR)

The cells were treated with atovaquone or vehicle (DMSO) and collected at the indicated time points via TRIzol (Thermo Fisher Scientific, #15596026). RNA extraction was performed via the RNeasy Mini Kit (Qiagen, #74106), and reverse transcription was performed via the QuantiTect Reverse Transcription Kit (Qiagen, #205314). The primers used were purchased from Integrated DNA Technologies. GAPDH and PPIA were used as housekeeping genes. The primer sequences can be found in Supplemental Table 1. Power SYBR™ Green PCR Master Mix (Thermo Fisher Scientific, #4368577) was utilized, and the reactions were run in a StepOnePlus Real-Time PCR system (Applied Biosystems).

Western blots

The cells were washed with PBS and lysed in RIPA lysis buffer (Thermo Fisher Scientific, #NC9839321) containing protease and phosphatase inhibitors. Protein concentrations were measured via the Pierce BCA protein assay (Thermo Fisher Scientific, #23227). Lysates equivalent to 25–30 µg of total protein were electrophoresed, blotted onto PVDF membranes, and probed with primary and secondary antibodies. The primary antibodies used were against phosphorylated EIF2α (Cell Signaling Technology, #3597), EIF2α (Cell Signaling Technology, #9722), CHOP (Cell Signaling Technology, #2895), G6PD (Cell Signaling Technology, #D5D2), and SLC7A11 (Cell Signaling Technology, #D2M7A). Beta actin (Santa Cruz Biotechnology, #sc-47778) was used as a control. The secondary antibodies (IRDye), intercept antibody diluents, and blocking buffer were purchased from LICOR (#926-32211, #926-68070, #927-65001, #927-60001). Protein bands were imaged and quantified via the Odyssey DLx imager. When incubating with multiple antibodies, primary antibodies from different host species were selected. The secondary antibodies used emitted fluorescence at different spectra, which minimized signal interference.

Enzyme-linked immunosorbent assays

OVCAR3 cells were treated with atovaquone or vehicle (DMSO) at the indicated concentrations. At 24- and 48-hours posttreatment, culture supernatants were collected and analyzed via HMGB1 (#ABIN6574155) and TFAM (#ABIN6960058) ELISA kits purchased from Antibodies-Online. HMGB1 and TFAM concentrations were normalized to total protein concentrations for each condition, which were quantified via the Pierce BCA protein assay.

Colorimetric assays

The glutathione (GSH) and glutathione disulfide (GSSG) ratios were measured via the GSH/GSSG-Glo™ Assay (Promega, #V6611). OVCAR3 and OVCAR5 cells were seeded in 96-well plates and incubated overnight. The cells were then treated with the indicated agents or vehicle (DMSO). After 24 h, the assay was performed, and the GSH/GSSG concentrations were calculated as described by the supplier.

6-PGA concentrations were measured via a 6-phosphogluconate (6-PGA) Assay Kit (Abcam, #ab211071). OVCAR3 and OVCAR5 cells were treated in 6-well plates with atovaquone (30 µM) or vehicle (DMSO) for 24 h. The cells were then collected, and the assay was performed as described in the protocol. The concentrations were calculated as described by the supplier.

Seahorse

The oxygen consumption rate (OCR) and extracellular acidification rate (ECAR) were measured via a Seahorse XFe96 Analyzer. The cells were seeded in a 96-well plate supplied with the Seahorse XF Glycolysis Stress Kit (Agilent, #103020-100) and incubated overnight at 37 °C with 5% CO2. The cells were then treated with atovaquone (30 μM) or vehicle (DMSO) for 24 h. The cells were then washed and incubated with atovaquone or vehicle in glycolytic stress test media without glucose for 1 h at 37 °C without CO2 to reach the ideal pH and temperature conditions according to the Glycolysis Stress Kit protocol. The assay was run with a 3-min mix and 3-min read cycle, during which the OCR and ECAR readings were obtained. The readings were performed after injections of glucose (10 mM), followed by oligomycin (2 μM) and then 2DG (100 mM). After the assay, the total protein content in each well was quantified via a BCA assay. The ECAR and OCR values were normalized to the total protein content. Normalized ECAR values were used to calculate the nonglycolytic acidification rate, glycolysis, glycolytic capacity, and glycolytic reserve via Seahorse Wave Software (Agilent).

Immunofluorescence staining

For the calreticulin experiments, OVCAR3 and OVCAR5 cells were treated with atovaquone or vehicle with or without TUDCA for 24 h. Following fixation and permeabilization, the cells were stained with AlexaFluor 488-conjugated calreticulin (Cell Signaling Technology, #62304), PE-conjugated EpCAM (Abcam, #ab112068), and DAPI (Thermo Fisher Scientific, #D1306) antibodies. Images were taken with a Leica Thunder microscope and analyzed via ImageJ software. The mean fluorescence for calreticulin was calculated in regions of interest (membranes) identified via EpCAM staining.

For the chimera experiments, OVCAR3 cells were treated with atovaquone or vehicle for 48 h. Following fixation (no permeabilization), the cells were incubated with NKp46 (R&D Systems, #1850-NK) or NKG2D (R&D Systems, #1299-NK) human recombinant chimeras overnight. The cells were then stained with a FITC-conjugated anti-human IgG Fc antibody (BioLegend, # 410720) and DAPI. Images were taken with a Leica Thunder microscope and analyzed via ImageJ software. The percentage of cells stained with FITC and the mean fluorescence of FITC for each image were calculated.

Flow cytometry analysis of coculture experiments

Natural killer (NK) cells were isolated from whole blood via the RosetteSep™ Human NK Cell Enrichment Cocktail from Stemcell Technologies (#15065). The samples were then stained with Zombie NIR™ Fixable Dye (BioLegend, #423106). OVCAR3 cells were pretreated with atovaquone for 24 h and then stained with PE-conjugated EpCAM and Zombie NIR™ Dye. NK and OVCAR3 cells were coincubated in Dulbecco’s phosphate-buffered saline with 2% fetal bovine serum for one hour before the addition of the eBioscience™ protein transport inhibitor cocktail (Thermo Fisher Scientific, #00-4980-03). The cells were cocultured for an additional 4 h. The cells were stained with FITC-conjugated NKp46 (BioLegend, #331922) and APC-conjugated CD107 (BioLegend, #328620). They were then fixed, permeabilized, and stained with BV711-conjugated IFNγ (BioLegend, #502540). The specific antibody clones and volumes used are detailed in Supplemental Table 2. Data were collected via a ThermoFisher Attune flow cytometer at the Flow Cytometry Laboratory at the University of Wisconsin Carbone Cancer Center and analyzed via FlowJo software.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analysis for the methodology described above was performed via GraphPad Prism (V10.0.2) software. A minimum sample size of 3 biological replicates was used to determine significance. Significance thresholds were set at P values < 0.05.

RNA sequencing

OVCAR3 cells were treated with atovaquone or vehicle (DMSO) at the indicated concentrations and collected at different time points after treatment with TRIzol. Three biological replicates were used for each experimental condition. RNA extraction was performed via the RNeasy Mini Kit from Qiagen. Library preparation and RNA sequencing were performed by Novogene. Gene expression counts were obtained via RSEM with the STAR aligner. The DESeq2 package was used for normalization and differential expression analysis, modeling the log-transformed counts of each gene as a linear combination of treatment, time, and their interaction. Because the expression profiles of the initial sequencing batch differed from those of subsequent batches, we included a batch effect in the linear model. This adjustment allowed the first batch to have a distinct intercept, capturing different baseline mean expression levels while preserving considtent time and treatment effects across all samples. A likelihood ratio test was performed on each gene to test for the significance of the treatment against the null hypothesis (gene expression depends only on time and the batch). The resulting p values were corrected for the false discovery rate (FDR) via the Benjamini‒Hochberg procedure. Pathway enrichment analysis was conducted with the PathfindR package. Significance thresholds were set at P values < 0.05.

The raw bulk sequencing data can be found in the Gene Expression Omnibus (GEO) database (accession number GSE288243). The processed sequencing data, along with the replication code, can be found at https://github.com/ajfagan/ovcar3-atovaquone-rnaseq.

Results

Atovaquone induces oxidative stress and halts normal cellular processes in EOC cells

We previously demonstrated that atovaquone has cytotoxic effects on EOC4. To further understand the molecular effects of atovaquone and identify response pathways that could be further targeted for EOC therapies, we characterized transcriptional changes over time in EOC cells following atovaquone treatment. We first determined the approximate half-maximal inhibitory concentrations (IC50s) of atovaquone in human EOC cell lines, which allowed us to identify gene expression changes over time without exerting full toxicity (Fig. 1A). We then performed RNA sequencing to determine longitudinal changes in gene expression in the well-established EOC cell line OVCAR3 following atovaquone treatment with IC50 concentrations of atovaquone at different timepoints.

Our analysis revealed concentration- and time-dependent transcriptional changes beginning at 8 h posttreatment. The number of significantly differentially expressed genes in atovaquone-treated OVCAR3 cells increased over time from 10 at 8 h to 1760 at 48 h in cells treated with the IC50 and from 228 at 8 h to 2861 at 48 h in cells treated with the IC75. Nonetheless, a substantial overlap of genes was observed across the different time points, particularly between 24 and 48 h (Supplemental Fig. 1). These findings suggest that these genes are consistently affected by atovaquone and may play important roles in the response to treatment.

Many of the gene expression changes identified at 8 h and sustained over time were associated with oxidative stress response pathways (Fig. 1B). This included the upregulation of genes involved in redox homeostasis, such as SLC7A11, MTHFD2, SOD2, and GADD45A, and the downregulation of TXNIP, a gene encoding a thioredoxin-interacting protein that inhibits the activity of the antioxidant thioredoxin. Additionally, we observed the upregulation of genes involved in nuclear DNA damage repair, including UNC5B, DDIT4, and RBBP8, and mitochondrial DNA repair genes, such as LONP1. These findings align with previous data showing that atovaquone increases intracellular reactive oxygen species (ROS) and double-stranded DNA breaks in EOC cells, indicating the induction of oxidative stress following treatment4. Moreover, these findings support the idea that the oxidative effects of atovaquone precede other observed molecular changes, as stated in our hypothesis.

Atovaquone treatment significantly and consistently affected genes involved in essential cellular functions. These changes included the downregulation of mitotic checkpoint and DNA replication initiation genes (Fig. 2A), suggesting the impaired proliferative ability of EOC cells. Additionally, atovaquone significantly downregulated genes associated with RNA and protein homeostasis, such as those involved in the spliceosome, proteasome, ribonucleoprotein complex, serine and arginine-rich splicing factors, and ribosomal components (Fig. 2A).

Pathway analysis supported these findings. Using the PathfindR model, which integrates active subnetwork data from a protein interaction network (PIN) with pathway enrichment analysis via the Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG)22–25, we identified pathways that were significantly modulated by atovaquone treatment with plausible functional relevance (Fig. 2B). Notably, the proteasome, spliceosome, and ribosome pathways were among the top differentially expressed pathways, with the majority of the genes being downregulated (Supplemental Fig. 2A). The cell cycle pathway included both upregulated and downregulated genes; however, most of the upregulated genes were associated with the DNA damage response during the synthesis phase, whereas most downregulated genes were associated with cell cycle progression in the G2 and M phases of the cell cycle (Supplemental Fig. 2B).

In addition to disrupting normal cell processes, atovaquone significantly altered key pathways involved in metabolism, protein processing, and immune activation. The following sections delve deeper into the broad impact of atovaquone and identify the molecular vulnerabilities of EOC cells that may make them particularly susceptible to OXPHOS inhibitors.

Atovaquone disrupts EOC cell metabolism

Atovaquone targets complex III of the mitochondrial electron transport chain6. As expected, oxidative phosphorylation (OXPHOS) was one of the top pathways significantly impacted by atovaquone treatment, with most of the differentially expressed genes within this pathway showing downregulation (Fig. 2B, Supplemental Figs. 2A and 3). These findings confirm that OXPHOS is adversely targeted by atovaquone and suggest that cells treated with atovaquone do not transcriptionally compensate for the impaired OXPHOS machinery. Seahorse data further support this finding, showing a decreased oxygen consumption rate (OCR), indicative of reduced OXPHOS activity in these cells (Fig. 3A).

Given the reduction in OXPHOS, we anticipate that cells might upregulate their glycolytic functions to sustain energy production, as observed in other tumor models19. Instead, we observed a decreased extracellular acidification rate (ECAR), which is often used as a surrogate of glycolysis, following atovaquone treatment (Fig. 3B). Atovaquone did not seem to influence basal glycolysis, but it significantly decreased the glycolytic capacity and glycolytic reserve of EOC cells (Fig. 3C,D), ultimately disrupting the ability of these cells to rely on either glycolytic or oxidative pathways for energy demands. Moreover, these findings suggest that EOC cells treated with atovaquone could be energetically compromised.

Metabolic shift aids in redox homeostasis

Given that atovaquone-treated cells undergo oxidative stress following an increase in oxygen radicals4 and have impaired metabolism, we hypothesized that cells might prioritize antioxidant pathways over energy production pathways. RNA sequencing data revealed that while the expression of glycolysis-specific genes (PGI and ALDOA) was downregulated, the expression of pentose phosphate pathway (PPP) genes, especially those involved in the oxidative phase of the pathway (G6PD and PGLS), was significantly upregulated in response to atovaquone (Fig. 4A). Further molecular analysis revealed the upregulation of hexokinase 2 (Fig. 4B) and G6PD (Fig. 4C, Supplemental Fig. 4A), which are essential for glucose entry into the PPP. Additionally, we observed an increase in the PPP intermediate 6-phosphogluconate (6-PGA), another indicator of PPP activation in atovaquone-treated cells (Fig. 4D,E).

The PPP serves as an important antioxidant mechanism, as it provides a major source of the reducing agent nicotinamide adenine dinucleotide phosphate (NADPH)26. Other pathways also contribute to the NADPH pool, including the folate cycle in one-carbon metabolism27. Multiple genes involved in the mitochondrial folate cycle were upregulated in response to atovaquone (Fig. 4F). For example, MTHFD2 and ALDH1L2, which encode the enzymes that catalyze the NAPDH-producing steps in this pathway, were upregulated. Conversely, DHFR, which encodes dihydrofolate reductase, an enzyme in the folate cycle that consumes NAPDH, was downregulated, suggesting that cells increase NADPH production to aid in oxidative stress resolution.

NADPH is crucial for regenerating the major antioxidant glutathione (GSH) from its oxidized form glutathione disulfide (GSSG)27. We observed a significant decrease in overall GSH and the GSH/GSSG ratio at 24 h postatovaquone treatment (Fig. 4G,H), indicating that GSH is actively consumed at a greater rate in atovaquone-treated cells than in vehicle-treated cells to mitigate oxidative stress.

Pathway analysis confirmed that the ‘glutathione metabolism’ pathway was significantly affected by atovaquone (Fig. 2B, Supplemental Fig. 2A). Other sources of GSH include de novo synthesis, which begins with the synthesis of γ-glutamylcysteine by gamma-glutamylcysteine synthetase (GCL)28. Within the ‘glutathione metabolism’ pathway in our analysis, the genes encoding the catalytic (GCLC) and modifier (GCLM) subunits of GCL were upregulated following atovaquone treatment (Fig. 4I). This step in the pathway is limited by cysteine availability, especially under stress conditions. Cells can increase cysteine availability through mechanisms such as the trans-sulfuration pathway (TSP) and increasing cysteine influx into the cells. Genes encoding cystathionine gamma-lyase (CTH) and cystathionine beta-synthase (CBS), the main enzymes in the TSP, were also upregulated following atovaquone treatment (Fig. 4I). In addition, we observed a significant increase in the expression of SLC7A11, which encodes the cystine/glutamate antiporter xCT responsible for importing cystine into cells, which can then be reduced to cysteine and be used in the TSP (Fig. 4I, Supplemental Fig. 4B). Overall, we observed a shift away from ATP-producing pathways and toward antioxidant mechanisms in response to atovaquone.

Atovaquone activates stress responses in EOC cells

Given that cells are actively combating the effects of increased ROS, we wanted to investigate other stress response mechanisms that could be at play. Oxidative stress is a well-known trigger of endoplasmic reticulum (ER) stress due to its damaging effects on protein folding and transport functions29,30. ER stress activates a series of pathways, collectively known as the unfolded protein response (UPR), that aid in mitigating stress by increasing the efficiency of ER chaperone proteins and temporarily halting translation31. However, if ER stress is prolonged or severe, the UPR can lead to apoptosis30,31.

Pathway analysis revealed ‘protein processing in the endoplasmic reticulum’ as a significantly affected pathway (Fig. 2B, Supplemental Fig. 5A). Within this pathway, the downregulated subset included genes encoding ER chaperones, protein processing machinery, and protein quality control components (Fig. 5A). On the other hand, the upregulated subset included genes involved in ER-associated protein degradation, ER quality control, and key UPR components (Fig. 5A). These results suggest that atovaquone induces ER stress, during which cells prioritize the mitigation of protein damage over maintaining protein homeostasis.

To confirm that ER stress occurred in atovaquone-treated EOC cells, we assessed the expression of molecular markers of the UPR. At 8 h posttreatment, we observed increased phosphorylation of eukaryotic translation initiation factor 2 A (EIF2A), one of the most upstream factors in the UPR signaling network (Fig. 5B). We also observed upregulated expression of two major UPR-induced transcription factors, activating transcription factor 4 (ATF4) and DNA damage-inducible transcript 3 (DDIT3), at 24 h posttreatment (Fig. 5C–E).

DDIT3 encodes the C/EBP homologous protein (CHOP), a central regulator of apoptosis32,33. CHOP expression was significantly greater in atovaquone-treated cells than in control cells. CHOP-regulated genes that have been linked to cell death, including TRIB3, TNFRSF10B, GADD34, KLHDC7B, and the BH3-only protein-encoding genes BBC3, PMAIP1, and HRK, were also significantly upregulated (Fig. 5F). Taken together, these results led us to hypothesize that atovaquone-induced ER stress was severe enough to trigger cell death. To assess the role of ER stress in reducing cell viability, we cotreated cells with atovaquone and tauroursodeoxycholic acid (TUDCA), an ER stress inhibitor. Compared with treatment with atovaquone alone, cotreatment with TUDCA significantly improved cell viability (Supplemental Fig. 5B), indicating that ER stress induction is a critical mechanism driving cell death in EOC cells following atovaquone treatment.

Atovaquone increases immunogenic signals in EOC cells

The induction of severe ER stress by atovaquone not only leads to apoptosis but also has potential implications for immunogenicity. Severe ER stress and subsequent cell death are known to cause immunogenic changes, as cells under these conditions can release molecules that act as danger signals, known as damage-associated molecular patterns (DAMPs)34. DAMPs can recruit and activate innate immune cells, leading to immunogenic cell death34,35. Given the marked upregulation of ER markers and proapoptotic factors observed in atovaquone-treated cells, we were interested in studying the immunogenic consequences of ER stress induced by atovaquone.

Calreticulin (CRT) is a well-characterized DAMP in the context of ER stress. Under normal conditions, CRT is an ER chaperone with important functions in calcium regulation and protein folding35. Following ER stress, CRT is translocated from the ER to the cell surface, where it can engage the immune system35,36. Given that atovaquone leads to ER stress, we hypothesized that the expression of plasma membrane CRT would be increased in atovaquone-treated EOC cells compared with controls. At 24 h posttreatment, we performed immunofluorescence staining in OVCAR3 and OVCAR5 cells. We then measured the mean gray values (fluorescence intensity) of calreticulin in surface areas identified by the epithelial surface marker EpCAM. These findings revealed increased surface expression of CRT in atovaquone-treated cells (Fig. 6A‒C). Moreover, compared with their atovaquone-only treated counterparts, cells cotreated with TUDCA and atovaquone presented a decrease in the mean fluorescence intensity of surface calreticulin.

In addition to CRT, we also evaluated key DAMPs following atovaquone treatment. High mobility group box 1 (HMGB1) is normally located in the nucleus, where it functions as a DNA chaperone to maintain chromatin structure35. Mitochondrial transcription factor A (TFAM) is essential for mitochondrial DNA homeostasis37,38. HMGB1 and TFAM are released from the nucleus or mitochondria, respectively, of stressed or dying cells into extracellular spaces and activate immune responses35,38. Thus, we hypothesized that these proteins would be increased in the supernatants of EOC cells following atovaquone treatment. At 24 and 48 h, we collected the supernatants and measured the HMGB1 and TFAM concentrations. We observed a significant increase in the levels of both of these proteins in atovaquone-treated EOC cell supernatants compared with those in control supernatants at 24 and 48 h posttreatment (Fig. 6D,E). Taken together, these data suggest that atovaquone is capable of increasing the number of DAMPs in EOC cells and might be able to induce immune responses.

EOC cells treated with atovaquone activate natural killer cells

Pathway analysis revealed several immunogenic pathways that were differentially expressed following atovaquone treatment. These pathways included natural killer (NK) cell-mediated cytotoxicity, the chemokine signaling pathway, and cytokine‒cytokine receptor interaction pathways (Fig. 2B). Among these genes, genes encoding ligands for NK cell cytotoxicity receptors, including ULBP1, which is recognized by the NKG2D receptor on NK cells, were upregulated (Fig. 7A)39. We also found increased expression of CXCL16, as well as death receptors 4 and 5, TNFRSF10A and TNFRSF10B, respectively (Fig. 7B), which collectively suggest a proimmunogenic profile in atovaquone-treated cells.

These findings led us to test whether atovaquone-treated EOC cells could be recognized by NK receptors. After 48 h of atovaquone treatment, we incubated OVCAR3 and OVCAR5 cells with chimeras of the receptors NKG2D and NKp46, which have been recently shown to recognize calreticulin40. We observed that a greater percentage of atovaquone-treated cells than vehicle-treated cells were recognized by both NKG2D. In addition, atovaquone-treated cells presented greater fluorescence intensity for NKG2D and NKp46 than did vehicle-treated cells (Fig. 7C–F, Supplemental Fig. 6A–D). These findings suggest that NK cell receptors can recognize EOC cells and that this recognition is further enhanced by atovaquone treatment.

The immunogenic potential of atovaquone is further emphasized by changes in DAMPs, including CRT surface exposure and HMGB1 and TFAM release. These molecules can recruit and activate immune cells, including NK cells, which play important roles in antitumor immunity. On the basis of our transcriptional findings, the increase in the release of DAMPs, and increased NK cell receptor recognition, we tested whether atovaquone-treated EOC cells would increase NK cell activity. We developed a coculture assay in which primary NK cells were incubated with EOC cells pretreated with atovaquone for 24 h (Fig. 8A). After 5 h of coculture, we observed a significant increase in the expression of CD107, a marker of NK cell degranulation (Fig. 8B). Additionally, we observed significant upregulation of the cytokine interferon-gamma (Fig. 8C). These results suggest that, in addition to inducing cytotoxicity, atovaquone treatment has the potential to prime EOC cells to NK cell-mediated immune responses.

Discussion

In the last few decades, OXPHOS has garnered interest as a target for anticancer treatments given its association with tumor progression and chemoresistance7,8,10. Atovaquone is an FDA-approved OXPHOS inhibitor that has shown activity against EOC4. In this study, we characterized the pleiotropic effects of atovaquone on EOC cells through a longitudinal RNA sequencing approach. We show that atovaquone disrupts redox homeostasis, alters cellular metabolism, activates stress response pathways, and enhances immunogenic signaling in EOC cells, highlighting its potential as a combination therapeutic agent.

Following atovaquone treatment, we observed significant changes in transcriptional programming related to redox homeostasis, such as the upregulation of antioxidant genes. These results align with previous observations of increased ROS and double-stranded DNA breaks in atovaquone-treated cells4, suggesting that atovaquone leads to oxidative stress in EOC cells. Concurrently, our data revealed the downregulation of genes critical for fundamental cellular processes, including the cell cycle, DNA replication, RNA splicing, and protein homeostasis. Taken together, these findings suggest that atovaquone-treated cells are in a stressed state and fail to maintain normal cellular functions, potentially increasing their susceptibility to other therapies. For example, Coates et al. (2020) reported that atovaquone potentiated carboplatin’s cytotoxic effects in several non-EOC cell lines, mainly through the effects of oxidative stress. Here, we show that atovaquone not only causes significant stress but also directly decreases cell proliferation and survival.

This shift toward stress response pathways and away from homeostasis is further supported by the observed changes in metabolic signaling. Our data confirmed that atovaquone successfully disrupted OXPHOS, as evidenced by decreased oxygen consumption rates and downregulation of OXPHOS-associated genes. The downregulation of OXPHOS genes could be an additional protective mechanism against oxidative stress since the mitochondrial electron transport chain is a major source of ROS6.

Given the inability to produce OXPHOS-derived ATP, we hypothesized that cells would upregulate glycolysis to meet their energetic demands. Surprisingly, our data revealed that not only were glycolytic genes downregulated but that the ECAR, glycolytic capacity, and glycolytic reserves were also significantly reduced in atovaquone-treated cells. These findings contradict previous studies reporting increased ECAR, glycolytic reserves, and glycolytic capacity following atovaquone treatment19. These differences in glycolytic responses could be attributed to variations in experimental conditions and nutrient availability. Using the Glycolysis Stress Kit protocol, we exposed the cells to a 10 mM concentration of glucose throughout the assay following a one-hour starvation period, in contrast to the 80 mM glucose concentrations used in the previous study19. Higher glucose availability may encourage cells to utilize it for energy production, particularly in the context of OXPHOS inhibition. Additionally, variations in oxidative stress levels and antioxidant capacities likely contribute to the contrasting regulation of metabolic pathways in response to atovaquone. The suppression of glycolysis and promotion of antioxidant mechanisms in response to oxidative stress that we have observed in the EOC models are congruent with previous reports41.

While functional data show that atovaquone-treated cells are energetically deficient, transcriptional data suggest that cells are not compensating for these insults. Rather, the observed upregulation of pentose phosphate pathway (PPP) and folate cycle genes suggests a metabolic shift aimed at increasing glutathione production to maintain redox balance rather than energy production. This highlights another potential therapeutic approach through the targeting of the PPP or folate metabolism to further decrease the antioxidant capacity of cells. These approaches have been explored previously as anticancer strategies42–45 and are important vulnerabilities highlighted by atovaquone treatment.

The accumulation of ROS is known to alter protein structure and interactions. These changes in protein homeostasis can be sensed by the endoplasmic reticulum (ER) and result in ER stress. We found that atovaquone also triggered ER stress and activated the unfolded protein response (UPR). While chronic adaptive UPR activation has been linked to protumorigenic properties, severe UPR induction can trigger cell death33. The significant upregulation of UPR markers such as ATF4 and CHOP indicated severe ER stress leading to apoptotic rather than prosurvival signaling. Moreover, we demonstrated that cotreatment with an ER stress inhibitor, TUDCA, improved cell viability, which supports the critical role of ER stress in atovaquone-induced cytotoxicity. ER stress inducers have also been explored as anticancer treatments46,47, with another potential role of atovaquone as an adjuvant treatment.

In addition to upregulating the cell death machinery, the UPR has also been linked to immunogenic cell death through the damage-associated molecular pattern (DAMP) protein calreticulin36. We show, for the first time, that atovaquone increased calreticulin cell surface exposure in EOC cells, which was dampened by TUDCA cotreatment. We also found that atovaquone-treated cells release other DAMPs, including HMGB1 and TFAM, that can potentially increase their immunogenicity. Moreover, RNA sequencing analysis revealed that atovaquone significantly impacted multiple immune-related pathways. Among these, atovaquone increased NK cell ligand expression, and we showed that NK cell receptors exhibited increased binding to atovaquone-treated EOC cells compared with controls. Given these findings, we tested whether atovaquone-treated cells could stimulate immune responses. Our coculture experiments demonstrated that natural killer (NK) cells exposed to atovaquone-treated cells presented increased activation and cytokine expression.

Other studies have shown the antitumor immunogenic effects of atovaquone, including the suppression of myeloid-derived suppressor cells and regulatory T cells in a breast cancer model48, a decrease in exhausted and increase in effector CD8 + T cells in a lung cancer model49, and synergistic effects between atovaquone and anti-PDL1 therapy in a colorectal cancer model50. We are the first to show that atovaquone promotes NK cell activity, which holds great promise for cellular immunotherapies51,52. Future studies should focus on identifying the activating signals from atovaquone-treated EOC cells and exploring the in vivo effects of atovaquone on intratumoral NK cell activity.

Atovaquone could be combined with immunotherapy or given prior to immunotherapy to increase its immunogenic potential. Not only does atovaquone induce direct cytotoxicity, but it also highlights EOC cell vulnerabilities that could make them susceptible to other treatments, making this drug a promising candidate for adjuvant therapy. EOC patients often experience disease recurrence and chemoresistance. Thus, more recent efforts have focused on combination strategies to amplify anticancer responses and achieve synthetic lethality. Studies examining the combination of immunotherapies with standard chemotherapies (carboplatin and paclitaxel) have revealed no additional benefits in terms of progression-free survival53,54, which could be due to the immune-depleting effects of standard chemotherapies. Our results showing that atovaquone can increase immunogenic signaling and NK cell activity present a window of opportunity for successful immunotherapy combinations in EOC. Although these findings are limited by the in vitro design of the assays, which precludes assessment of potential in vivo limitations, such as immune-evasive mechanisms, they provide a strong foundation for ongoing and novel studies.

Taken together, the results of this study highlight the ability of atovaquone to disrupt multiple EOC cell pathways, from the cell cycle and metabolism to stress responses and immune activation. These mechanistic insights pave the way to several therapeutic avenues that have yet to be explored in EOC and provide a strong rationale for combination therapies to address this challenging disease. Our future work will focus on validating these findings in preclinical in vivo models and further investigating other immunomodulatory pathways that could be influenced by atovaquone.
