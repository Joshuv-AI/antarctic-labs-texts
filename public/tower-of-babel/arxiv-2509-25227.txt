Homogeneity
Martin Himmel

Abstract
. The four types of homogeneity—additive, multiplicative, exponential, and logarithmic—are generalized as transformations describing how a function ff changes under scaling or shifting of its arguments. These generalized homogeneity functions capture different scaling behaviors and establish fundamental properties of f f . Such properties include how homogeneity is preserved under function operations and how it determines the transformation behavior of related constructions like quotient functions. This framework extends the classical concept of homogeneity to a wider class of functional symmetries, providing a unified approach to analyzing scaling properties in various mathematical contexts.

1.
Introduction

Let
T\subset(0,+\infty)
be a set which is closed with respect to multiplication and
I\subset{\mathbb{R}}
an interval.
A function
f:I\to{\mathbb{R}}
is called homogeneous,
if there is a function
\textsc{m}:T\to{\mathbb{R}}
such that

f(tx)=m(t)f(x)

holds for all
x\in I
and
t\in T
.
We take this definition as a starting point to investigate a more general notion, namely

Definition 1
.

Let
I\subset{\mathbb{R}}
be a non-empty interval and
T\subset{\mathbb{R}}
a semigroup with respect to multiplication such that
TI\subset I
.
A function
f:I\to{\mathbb{R}}
is called homogeneous with respect to the function
\textsc{m}:I\times T\to{\mathbb{R}}
, if

f(tx)=\textsc{m}(x,t)f(x),\qquad x\in I,t\in T.

In this case we call
m
homogeneity function of
f
. If
T=I
, since multiplication of real numbers is commutative,
we have

\textsc{m}(x,t)f(x)=\textsc{m}(t,x)f(t),\qquad x\in I,t\in T,

and thus

\textsc{m}(x,t)=\frac{f(t)}{f(x)}\textsc{m}(t,x),\qquad x\in I,t\in T.

Obviously, the homogeneity function
m
of a function
f:I\to{\mathbb{R}}
is given by

\textsc{m}(x,t)=\frac{f(tx)}{f(x)},\qquad x\in I,t\in T,

whenever
f(x)\neq 0
; moreover, the homogeneity function is undetermined at the zeros of
f
.
Similarly, neglecting for a moment domain issues, a function
f
is called translative, if
there is a function
a
such that

f(x+t)=a(t)+f(x),

for all
x
and
t
,
motivating us to introduce translativity functions in the following

Definition 2
.

Let
I\subset{\mathbb{R}}
be a non-empty interval and
T\subset{\mathbb{R}}
a semigroup with respect to addition such that
I+T\subset I
.
A function
f:I\to{\mathbb{R}}
is called translative, or additively homogeneous,
with respect to the function
\textsc{a}:I\times T\to{\mathbb{R}}
, if

f(x+t)=\textsc{a}(x,t)+f(x),\qquad x\in I,t\in T.

In this case we call
\textsc{a}=\textsc{a}_{f}
translativity function (or, more systematically, an additive homogeneity function) of
f
.

Two other notions, which seem very natural from the perspective of functional equations,
are exponential and logarithmic homogeneity.

Definition 3
.

Let
I\subset{\mathbb{R}}
be a non-empty interval and
T\subset{\mathbb{R}}
be a semigroup with respect to addition.
A function
f:I\to{\mathbb{R}}
is called exponentially homogeneous with respect to the function
\textsc{e}:I\times T\to{\mathbb{R}}
, if

f(x+t)=\textsc{e}(x,t)f(x),\qquad x\in I,t\in T.

In this case we call
e
(or
\textsc{e}_{f}
to emphasize the dependency on the function
f
) an exponential homogeneity function of
f
.

Definition 4
.

Let
I\subset{\mathbb{R}}
be a non-empty interval and
T\subset{\mathbb{R}}
be a semigroup with respect to multiplication.
A function
f:I\to{\mathbb{R}}
is called logarithmically homogeneous with respect to the function
\textsc{l}:I\times T\to{\mathbb{R}}
, if

f(tx)=\textsc{l}(x,t)+f(x),\qquad x\in I,t\in T.

In this case we call
l
a logarithmic homogeneity function of
f
.

Remark 1
.

When clear from the context, we sometimes suppress
the dependency on
f
in the homogeneity function; otherwise we write

\displaystyle\textsc{a}_{f}(x,t)=f(x+t)-f(x)

for the additive homogeneity function,

\displaystyle\textsc{m}_{f}(x,t)=\frac{f(tx)}{f(x)}

for the multiplicative homogeneity function,

\displaystyle\textsc{e}_{f}(x,t)=\frac{f(x+t)}{f(x)}

for the exponential homogeneity function, and

\displaystyle\textsc{l}_{f}(x,t)=f(tx)-f(x)

for the logarithmic homogeneity function.
Note that for multiplicative and exponential homogeneity functions some care has to be taken due to the fact that
f
appears in the denominator.
Moreover, for multiplicative and logarithmic homogeneity functions we find it more natural to write
tx
instead of
xt
, which of course does not matter, as long we are in domains of real numbers, where multiplication is commutative.
Without mentioning explicitly we assume
everything to be well-defined,
which implies suitable relations between the intervals
I
and
T
to hold (cf. Definition 1 to 4).
The input function
f
of the respective homogeneity functions is also referred to as generator.

1.1.
Relation between Types of Homogeneity

Under suitable assumptions on the function domain these four notions of homogeneity are equivalent.
Let
I=(0,+\infty)
and
f:I\to(0,+\infty)
a function and
\textsc{m}:I\times T\to(0,+\infty)
,

\textsc{m}(x,t)=\frac{f(tx)}{f(x)}
its (multiplicative) homogeneity function.
Since
I\subset(0,+\infty)
, for every
x\in I
there is a
u\in{\mathbb{R}}
with
e^{u}=x
.
Similarly, if
T\subset(0,+\infty)
, for every
t\in T
there is
s\in{\mathbb{R}}
with
e^{s}=t
.
Thus,

\displaystyle\textsc{m}_{f}(x,t)

\displaystyle=\textsc{m}_{f}(e^{u},e^{s})

\displaystyle=\frac{f(e^{u+s})}{f(e^{u})}

\displaystyle=\textsc{e}_{f\circ\exp}(u,s).

Hence,
f
is multiplicatively homogeneous of degree
m
iff
g:=f\circ\exp:{\mathbb{R}}\to(0,+\infty)

is exponentially homogeneous of degree
\textsc{e}_{g}:{\mathbb{R}}^{2}\to(0,+\infty)
defined by

\textsc{e}_{g}(u,s):=\frac{g(u+s)}{g(s)}
.
In other words,
the multiplicative homogeneity function of
f
is the exponential homogeneity function of
g
.
So for positive functions when dealing with positive homogeneity
we may equivalently deal with exponential homogeneity on the whole real numbers .

Similarly, the function
f:I\to{\mathbb{R}}
is translative (or, in other words, additively homogeneous) of degree

\textsc{a}:I\times T\to{\mathbb{R}}
, defined by
\textsc{a}(x,t)=f(x+t)-f(x)
,
with
I,T\subset{\mathbb{R}}
, if and only if,
since for every
x\in I
and for every
t\in T
there exist
u,s\in(0,+\infty)
with
x=\log{u}
and
t=\log{s}
,
the function
g:=f\circ\log:(0,+\infty)\to{\mathbb{R}}
is lagrithmically homogeneous of degree

\textsc{l}:(0,+\infty)^{2}\to{\mathbb{R}}
defined by
\textsc{l}(u,s):=\textsc{a}_{f}(\log{u},\log{s})
,
or equivalently,
iff the function
h:=\exp\circ f\circ\log:(0,+\infty)\to(0,+\infty)
is multiplicatively homogeneous of degree

\textsc{m}_{h}:(0,+\infty)^{2}\to(0,+\infty)
.

1.2.
Symmetry of Homogeneity Functions

In general, the form of homogeneity functions
excludes symmetry.
Homogeneity functions are symmetric only for constant functions.
For multiplicative and logarithmic homogeneity functions, respectively,
the result relies on the commutativity of multiplication;
for additive and exponential homogeneity functions, respectively,
it is based on
commutativity of addition.

Remark 2
.

Assume
\textsc{m}_{f}(x,t)=\textsc{m}_{f}(t,x)
for all
x
and
t
,
where
f:I\to(0,+\infty)
and
I\subset{\mathbb{R}}
an interval.
By the definition of multiplicative homogeneity functions,

\frac{f(tx)}{f(x)}=\frac{f(xt)}{f(t)},\qquad x,t.

Since real multiplication is abelian,
we have
f(x)=f(t)
for all
x
and
t
.
For
x=t
, this is no restricting condition.
Otherwise, namely if
f(x)=f(t)
for all
x\neq t
1
1

1

Recall that in this paper we assume
T
and
I
to be real intervals.
,
describes the function to be ”maximally non-injective”,
or, in other words,
f
is constant.

The proof for logarithmic homogeneity functions
is very similar.

Remark 3
.

Assume
\textsc{l}_{f}(x,t)=\textsc{l}_{f}(t,x)
for all
x
and
t
.
By the definition of logarithmic homogeneity functions,

{f(tx)}-{f(x)}={f(xt)}-{f(t)}.

we have
f(x)=f(t)
for all
x
and
t
,
implying
f(x)=f(t)
for all
x\neq t
.
Thus,
f
is constant.

The proof for the other two types of homogeneity functions
relies heavily on the commutativity of addition.

Remark 4
.

Assume
\textsc{a}_{f}(x,t)=\textsc{a}_{f}(t,x)
for all
x
and
t
.
Thus,

{f(x+t)}-{f(x)}={f(t+x)}-{f(t)},\qquad x,t.

\Leftrightarrow

{f(x)}={f(t)},\qquad x,t.

\Leftrightarrow

f
is constant.

The proof for exponential homogeneity functions is left to the reader.

1.3.
Algebraic Properties of Homogeneity Functionals

Here we state some algebraic properties such as
linearity or multiplicativity
of homogeneity functions when considered as functionals.

1.3.1.
Additive Homogeneity

Remark 5

(Linearity of
\textsc{a}_{\cdot}(x,t)
)
.

The additive homogeneity functional is linear, i.e., it is

(1)

additive:

\displaystyle\textsc{a}_{f+g}(x,t)

\displaystyle=({f+g})(x+t)-{(f+g)(x)}

\displaystyle=f(x+t)+g(x+t)-f(x)-g(x)

\displaystyle=f(x+t)-f(x)+g(x+t)-g(x)

\displaystyle=\textsc{a}_{f}(x,t)+\textsc{a}_{g}(x,t),\qquad x,t;

(2)

homogeneous:

\displaystyle\textsc{a}_{\lambda f}(x,t)

\displaystyle=({\lambda f})(x+t)-{(\lambda f)(x)}

\displaystyle=\lambda f(x+t)-\lambda f(x)

\displaystyle=\lambda\left(f(x+t)-f(x)\right)

\displaystyle=\lambda\cdot\textsc{a}_{f}(x,t),\qquad x,t;

moreover,

a_{\cdot}(x,t)
is not multiplicative:

\displaystyle\textsc{a}_{fg}(x,t)

\displaystyle=({fg})(x+t)-{(fg)(x)}

\displaystyle=f(x+t)g(x+t)-f(x)g(x)

\displaystyle\neq\textsc{a}_{f}(x,t)\cdot\textsc{a}_{g}(x,t)

\displaystyle=\left({f(x+t)-f(x)}\right)\left({g(x+t)-g(x)}\right),\qquad x,t;

Since the additive homogeneity functional is not multiplicative in general,
it is reasonable to

•

ask when (i.e. for which functions
f
and
g
) it is multiplicative,

•

investigate
\textsc{a}_{fg}(x,t)-\textsc{a}_{f}(x,t)\cdot\textsc{a}_{g}(x,t)

(or

\frac{\textsc{a}_{fg}(x,t)}{\textsc{a}_{f}(x,t)\cdot\textsc{a}_{g}(x,t)}
)
measuring the deviation of the additive homogeneity functional from being multiplicative,

For given
f,g
, it may also be fruitful to address
whether the homogeneity functional is multiplicative on a smaller set(
conditional multiplicativity
).

The first question is answered in the following

Remark 6

(Multiplicativity of
\textsc{a}_{\cdot}(x,t)
)
.

The additive homogeneity functional is multiplicative
if, and only if, the functions are proportional, i.e.,
if there is
c\in{\mathbb{R}}
such that
f=cg
.

Proof.

\displaystyle\textsc{a}_{fg}(x,t)

\displaystyle=({fg})(x+t)-{(fg)(x)}

\displaystyle=f(x+t)g(x+t)-f(x)g(x)

\displaystyle=\underbrace{(f(x+t)-f(x))}_{=\textsc{a}_{f}(x,t)}\underbrace{(g(x+t)+g(x))}_{=\textsc{a}_{g}(x,t)}-\underbrace{(f(x+t)g(x)-f(x)g(x+t)}_{=:R_{f,g}(x,t)},\qquad x,t;

If
R_{f,g}(x,t)
vanishes, i.e., if
f(x+t)g(x)=f(x)g(x+t)
,
or equivalently,

\displaystyle\frac{f(x+t)}{g(x+t)}=\frac{f(x)}{g(x)},\qquad x,t,

the additive homogeneity functional is multiplicative.
Setting
t:=-x+y
and
h:=\frac{f}{g}
, we get that the multplicativity of the additive homogeneity functional is equivalent to

h
being constant, which means
f=cg
for some
c\in{\mathbb{R}}
as claimed.
The converse is easy to verify.

1.3.2.
Exponential Homogeneity

Remark 7

(Multiplicativity of
\textsc{e}_{\cdot}(x,t)
)
.

The exponential homogeneity functional is multiplicative, and thus homogeneous, but not additive:

Proof.

\displaystyle\textsc{e}_{fg}(x,t)

\displaystyle=\frac{({fg})(x+t)}{{(fg)(x)}}

\displaystyle=\frac{f(x+t)g(x+t)}{{f(x)g(x)}}

\displaystyle=\textsc{e}_{f}(x,t)\cdot\textsc{e}_{g}(x,t),\qquad x,t;

Since in general
\textsc{e}_{\cdot}(x,t)
is not additive,
it is reasonable to take a closer look at

\displaystyle\textsc{e}_{f+g}(x,t)-\textsc{e}_{f}(x,t)-\textsc{e}_{g}(x,t)

\displaystyle=-\frac{f(x+t)g(x)}{(f+g)(x)f(x)}-\frac{g(x+t)f(x)}{(f+g)(x)g(x)},\qquad x,t;

Remark 8

(Additivity of
\textsc{e}_{\cdot}(x,t)
)
.

The exponential homogeneity functional is additive only if
f\equiv 0
or
f=-g
.

Proof.

Assuming
\textsc{e}_{f+g}(x,t)=\textsc{e}_{f}(x,t)+\textsc{e}_{g}(x,t)
, setting
t=0
, we have

\displaystyle\left(\frac{f(x)}{g(x)}\right)^{2}=-\left(\frac{f(x)}{g(x)}\right),\qquad x,t;

Thus, the function
h:=\frac{f}{g}

satisfies

\displaystyle h(x)\left(h(x)+1\right)=0,\qquad x,t,

which gives the claim.

1.3.3.
Multiplicative Homogeneity

Remark 9

(Linearity of
\textsc{m}_{\cdot}(x,t)
)
.

Let
I,T\subset{\mathbb{R}}
be intervals such that
T\cdot I\subset I
and
1\in T
.
The multiplicative homogeneity functional
\textsc{m}_{\cdot}(x,t)
is

(1)

additive only for zero functions or additive inverses of each other,
i.e.,
f\equiv 0
or
f=-g
;

(2)

homogeneous only for
\lambda=1
.

(3)

never logarithmic;

(4)

exponential if

g(x)=\frac{af(x)}{f(x)-a}.

for some non-zero
a\in{\mathbb{R}}
such that
f(x)\neq a
for all
x
in the domain.

Proof.

(1)

Assuming additivity of
\textsc{m}_{\cdot}(x,t)
, we have, for all
x,t
,

\displaystyle\textsc{m}_{f+g}(x,t)

\displaystyle=\frac{({f+g})(tx)}{{(f+g)(x)}}

\displaystyle=\frac{{f(tx)+g(tx)}}{{f(x)+g(x)}}

\displaystyle=\textsc{m}_{f}(x,t)+\textsc{m}_{g}(x,t)

\displaystyle{=}\frac{{f(tx)}}{{f(x)}}+\frac{{g(tx)}}{{g(x)}};

Thus,

\displaystyle\frac{{f(tx)}}{{g(tx)}}

\displaystyle=-\left(\frac{{f(x)}}{{g(x)}}\right)^{2},\qquad x,t,

and consequently

\displaystyle\frac{{f(y)}}{{g(y)}}

\displaystyle=-\left(\frac{{f(x)}}{{g(x)}}\right)^{2},\qquad x,y;

Put
h:=\frac{{f}}{{g}}
; thus
h(y)=-(h(x))^{2}
for all
x,y
.
Setting
x=y
, we obtain
that
h
is constant, say
h(x)=:C
.
Hence,

C=-C^{2}
, which gives us
C=0
or
C=-1
.
By the definition of
h
, this means that
f\equiv 0
(together with
g\neq 0
arbitrary), or
f=-g
as claimed.

(2)

Since for all
x,t
and
\lambda\neq 0

\displaystyle\textsc{m}_{\lambda f}(x,t)

\displaystyle=\frac{\lambda{f(tx)}}{\lambda{f(x)}}

\displaystyle=\textsc{m}_{f}(x,t),

\textsc{m}_{\cdot}(x,t)
is homogeneous of order
0
,
thus homogeneous of order
1
only for
\lambda=1
.

To see this, assume that
m
is homogeneous of degree
p
:

\displaystyle\textsc{m}_{\lambda f}=\lambda^{p}f\textsc{m}_{f}.

Multiplying both sides by
\lambda^{q}
gives us
\lambda^{q}\textsc{m}_{\lambda f}=\lambda^{p+q}\textsc{m}_{f}
.
If
p+q=1
, we get that
\lambda
is a
q
-th root of unity.
In the real case this means
\lambda=1
.
.

(3)

Assuming
\textsc{m}_{fg}(x,t)=\textsc{m}_{f}(x,t)+\textsc{m}_{g}(x,t)

for all
x,t
,
gives us

\displaystyle\frac{f(tx)g(tx)}{f(x)g(x)}

\displaystyle=\frac{g(tx)}{g(x)}+\frac{f(tx)}{f(x)}

\displaystyle=\frac{g(tx)f(x)+f(tx)g(x)}{g(x)f(x)}.

Dividing both sides by the left-hand side yields

\displaystyle\frac{g(tx)f(x)+f(tx)g(x)}{f(tx)g(tx)}

\displaystyle=\frac{f(x)}{f(tx)}+\frac{g(x)}{g(tx)}

\displaystyle=1,\qquad x,t;

Setting here
t=1
gives
1+1=1
, a contradiction.

(4)

For the multiplicative homogeneity functional to be exponential,
assume
f
and
g
such that

(1.1)

\textsc{e}_{f+g}(x,t)=\textsc{e}_{f}(x,t)\cdot\textsc{e}_{g}(x,t).

Then

\frac{f(tx)+g(tx)}{f(x)+g(x)}=\frac{f(tx)}{f(x)}\cdot\frac{g(tx)}{g(x)}.

Multiplying both sides by
f(x)+g(x)
and
f(x)g(x)
, we get

[f(tx)+g(tx)]f(x)g(x)=f(tx)g(tx)[f(x)+g(x)].

Rearranging:

f(x)g(x)[f(tx)+g(tx)]=f(tx)g(tx)[f(x)+g(x)].

Dividing by
f(x)g(x)f(tx)g(tx)
(assuming nonzero values):

\frac{1}{f(tx)}+\frac{1}{g(tx)}=\frac{1}{f(x)}+\frac{1}{g(x)}.

Thus,
x\mapsto\frac{1}{f(x)}+\frac{1}{g(x)}
is
0
-homogeneous
(scale-invariant), hence constant:

\frac{1}{f(x)}+\frac{1}{g(x)}=c\neq 0.

Solving for
g(x)
gives us

g(x)=\frac{1}{c-\frac{1}{f(x)}}=\frac{f(x)}{cf(x)-1}.

under the assumption that
f(x)\neq\frac{1}{c}
for all
x\in I
.

Let
c=\frac{1}{a}
to obtain

g(x)=\frac{af(x)}{f(x)-a}.

with
\quad f(x)\neq a
for all
x\in I
.

Conversely, if
g(x)=\frac{af(x)}{f(x)-a}
, then

f(x)+g(x)=\frac{(f(x))^{2}}{f(x)-a},\quad f(tx)+g(tx)=\frac{f(tx)^{2}}{f(tx)-a},

so

\textsc{e}_{f+g}(x,t)=\frac{f(tx)^{2}(f(x)-a)}{f(x)^{2}(f(tx)-a)}.

Also,

\textsc{e}_{f}(x,t)=\frac{f(tx)}{f(x)},\quad\textsc{e}_{g}(x,t)=\frac{g(tx)}{g(x)}=\frac{f(tx)(f(x)-a)}{f(x)(f(tx)-a)},

and their product is

\textsc{e}_{f}(x,t)\cdot\textsc{e}_{g}(x,t)=\frac{f(tx)^{2}(f(x)-a)}{f(x)^{2}(f(tx)-a)}=\textsc{e}_{f+g}(x,t).

This completes the proof.

1.3.4.
Logarithmic Homogeneity

Remark 10

(Linearity of
\textsc{l}_{\cdot}(x,t)
)
.

The logarithmic homogeneity functional
\textsc{l}_{\cdot}(x,t)
is linear, i.e., it is

(1)

additive and

(2)

homogeneous;

moreover, it is multiplicative only if the functions are proportional;

Proof.

(1)

To prove additivity of
\textsc{l}_{\cdot}(x,t)
, observe that

\displaystyle\textsc{l}_{f+g}(x,t)

\displaystyle=(f+g)(tx)-(f+g)(x)

\displaystyle=f(tx)+g(tx)-f(x)-g(x)

\displaystyle=f(tx)-f(x)+g(tx)-g(x)

\displaystyle=\textsc{l}_{f}(x,t)+\textsc{l}_{g}(x,t),\quad x,t.

(2)

For the homogeneity of
\textsc{l}_{\cdot}(x,t)
, verify that

\displaystyle\textsc{l}_{\lambda f}(x,t)

\displaystyle=(\lambda f)(tx)-(\lambda f)(x)

\displaystyle=\lambda(f(tx)-f(x)

\displaystyle=\lambda\textsc{l}_{f}(x,t),\quad x,t.

(3)

Assuming
\textsc{l}_{\cdot}(x,t)
to be multiplicative yields

\displaystyle\textsc{l}_{fg}(x,t)

\displaystyle=(fg)(tx)-(fg)(x)

\displaystyle=f(tx)g(tx)-f(x)g(x)

\displaystyle=\underbrace{(f(tx)-f(x))}_{=\textsc{l}_{f}(x,t)}\underbrace{(g(tx)+g(x))}_{=\textsc{l}_{g}(x,t)}-\underbrace{(f(tx)g(x)-f(x)g(tx)}_{=:R_{f,g}(x,t)},\qquad x,t;

Thus
R_{f,g}\equiv 0
, hence
f(tx)g(x)=f(x)g(tx)
,
or equivalently,

\displaystyle\frac{f(tx)}{g(tx)}=\frac{f(x)}{g(x)},\qquad x,t.

Setting
t:=\frac{y}{x}
and
h:=\frac{f}{g}
, we get that the multplicativity of the logarithmic homogeneity functional is equivalent to

h
being constant, which means that there is
c\in{\mathbb{R}}
such that
f=cg
.
The converse implication is easy to verify.

1.3.5.
Inversion of Generators

In terms of inversion the following properties hold true:

\displaystyle\textsc{e}_{\frac{1}{f}}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\textsc{e}_{f}},

and

\displaystyle\textsc{m}_{\frac{1}{f}}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\textsc{m}_{f}};

Analogously, we have for additive inverses of the generator
for all
x,t
:

\displaystyle\textsc{a}_{-{f}}

\displaystyle=-\textsc{a}_{{f}},

and

\displaystyle\textsc{l}_{-{f}}

\displaystyle=-\textsc{l}_{{f}};

2.
Some examples of homogeneity functions

In this section we consider homogeneity functions corresponding to some classical generators.
We start with regular solutions to one of the Cauchy functional equations
[
5
, 128-130]
, namely:

•

additive functions: lines through the origin;

•

multiplicative functions: power functions;

•

exponential functions: functions of exponential type;

•

logarithmic functions: scaler multiples of logarithmic functions

2.1.
Additive function

The (multiplicative) homogeneity function of
f:{\mathbb{R}}\to{\mathbb{R}}
defined by
f(x)=cx
, for
c\in{\mathbb{R}}
,
c\neq 0
, arbitrarily fixed,
is clearly

\displaystyle\textsc{m}(x,t)

\displaystyle=\frac{f(tx)}{f(x)}

\displaystyle=\frac{c({tx})}{cx}

\displaystyle=t

\displaystyle=\frac{f(t)}{c}

\displaystyle=\frac{f(t)}{f(1)},

the identity function on
{\mathbb{R}}\setminus\{0\}
only depending on
t
.

The additive homogeneity function reads

\displaystyle\textsc{a}(x,t)

\displaystyle=f(x+t)-f(x)

\displaystyle={c({x+t})}-{cx}

\displaystyle=ct

\displaystyle=f(t).

The logarithmic homogeneity function reads

\displaystyle\textsc{l}(x,t)

\displaystyle=f(tx)-f(x)

\displaystyle={c({tx})}-{cx}

\displaystyle=cx(t-1)

\displaystyle=f(x)\frac{f(t-1)}{f(1)}

\displaystyle=f(x)\textsc{m}(x,t-1).

The exponential homogeneity function reads

\displaystyle\textsc{e}(x,t)

\displaystyle=\frac{f(x+t)}{f(x)}

\displaystyle=\frac{c(x+t)}{cx}

\displaystyle=1+\frac{t}{x}

\displaystyle=1+s

\displaystyle=1+\frac{f(t)}{f(x)}

where
s:=\frac{t}{x}
as above.

2.2.
Power functions

The homogeneity function of
f:(0,+\infty)\to(0,+\infty)
defined by
f(x)=x^{p}
, for
p\in{\mathbb{R}}
arbitrarily fixed,
is clearly

\displaystyle\textsc{m}(x,t)

\displaystyle=\frac{f(tx)}{f(x)}

\displaystyle=\frac{(tx)^{p}}{x^{p}}=t^{p}.

Thus, the homogeneity function of a smooth multiplicative function is a multiplicative function independent of
x

and of the same degree as
f
.

Its additive homogeneity function reads

\displaystyle\textsc{a}(x,t)

\displaystyle=f(x+t)-f(x)

\displaystyle={(x+t)^{p}}-{x^{p}}.

The logarithmic homogeneity function reads

\displaystyle\textsc{l}(x,t)

\displaystyle=f(tx)-f(x)

\displaystyle={(tx)^{p}}-{x^{p}}

\displaystyle=x^{p}(t^{p}-1).

The exponential homogeneity function reads

\displaystyle\textsc{e}(x,t)

\displaystyle=\frac{f(x+t)}{f(x)}

\displaystyle=\frac{(x+t)^{p}}{x^{p}}

\displaystyle=\left(1+\frac{t}{x}\right)^{p}.

Note that, for power functions,
e
is homogeneous of degree zero,
i.e.
\textsc{e}(sx,st)=\textsc{e}(x,t)
for all
x,t,s\in(0,+\infty)
.

Since the identity function is both additive and multiplicative,
we have the following

Remark 11
.

Let
f:I\to{\mathbb{R}}
with
f(x)=x
.
Then:

\displaystyle\textsc{a}_{id}(x,t)

\displaystyle={f(x+t)}-{f(x)}

\displaystyle=t,\qquad x,t;

\displaystyle\textsc{l}_{id}(x,t)

\displaystyle=\frac{f(tx)}{f(x)}

\displaystyle=t,\qquad x,t;

\displaystyle\textsc{l}_{id}(x,t)

\displaystyle={f(tx)}-{f(x)}

\displaystyle=x(t-1),\qquad x,t;

\displaystyle\textsc{e}_{id}(x,t)

\displaystyle=\frac{f(x+t)}{f(x)}

\displaystyle=1+\frac{t}{x},\qquad x\neq 0,t;

2.3.
Exponential functions

Let
f:{\mathbb{R}}\to(0,+\infty)
with
f(x)=a^{x}
for some
a>0
be a smooth exponential function.
Its multiplicative homogeneity function reads

\displaystyle\textsc{m}(x,t)

\displaystyle=\frac{f(tx)}{f(x)}

\displaystyle=\frac{a^{tx}}{a^{x}}

\displaystyle=a^{tx-x}

\displaystyle=a^{x(t-1)}

\displaystyle=f(x(t-1)).

The additive homogeneity function reads

\displaystyle\textsc{a}(x,t)

\displaystyle=f(x+t)-f(x)

\displaystyle=a^{x+t}-{a^{x}}

\displaystyle=a^{x}\left(a^{t}-1\right)

\displaystyle=f(x)\left(f(t)-1\right).

The logarithmic homogeneity function reads

\displaystyle\textsc{l}(x,t)

\displaystyle=f(tx)-f(x)

\displaystyle={a^{tx}}-{a^{x}}

\displaystyle=a^{x}\left(a^{tx-x}-1\right)

\displaystyle=f(x)\left(f(x(t-1))-1\right)

\displaystyle=f(x)\left(\textsc{m}_{f}{(x,t)}-1\right).

The exponential homogeneity function reads

\displaystyle\textsc{e}(x,t)

\displaystyle=\frac{f(x+t)}{f(x)}

\displaystyle=\frac{a^{x+t}}{a^{x}}=a^{t}

\displaystyle=f(t).

2.4.
Logarithmic functions

The homogeneity function of
f:(0,+\infty)\to{\mathbb{R}}
defined by
f(x)=c\log{x}
, for arbitrary
c\in{\mathbb{R}}
,
c\neq 0
,
is clearly

\displaystyle\textsc{m}(x,t)

\displaystyle=\frac{f(tx)}{f(x)}

\displaystyle=\frac{c\log(tx)}{c\log{x}}

\displaystyle=1+\frac{\log{t}}{\log{x}}

\displaystyle=1+\frac{f(t)}{f(x)}.

The additive homogeneity function reads

\displaystyle\textsc{a}(x,t)

\displaystyle=f(x+t)-f(x)

\displaystyle={c\log(x+t)}-{c\log{x}}

\displaystyle=c\log{\left(1+\frac{t}{x}\right)}

\displaystyle=f{\left(1+s\right)},

where
s:=\frac{t}{x}
.
Here, similarly as for the exponential homogeneity function
in case of power functions,
the additive homogeneity function

a
is homogeneous of degree
0
.

The logarithmic homogeneity function reads

\displaystyle\textsc{l}(x,t)

\displaystyle=f(tx)-f(x)

\displaystyle={c\log(tx)}-{c\log{x}}

\displaystyle=c\log{\left(\frac{tx}{x}\right)}

\displaystyle=c\log{t}

\displaystyle=f(t),

a logarithmic function, which depends only on the variable
t
.

The exponential homogeneity function reads

\displaystyle\textsc{e}(x,t)

\displaystyle=\frac{f(x+t)}{f(x)}

\displaystyle=\frac{c\log{(x+t)}}{c\log{x}}

\displaystyle=\frac{\log{(x+t)}}{\log{x}},

where
1\neq x>0
and
x>-t
.
If
x=1
and
t=0
, since formally
\textsc{e}(1,0)=\frac{0}{0}
,
we obtain by L’Hospital

\displaystyle\textsc{e}(1,0)

\displaystyle=\left.\frac{\frac{1}{{x+t}}}{\frac{1}{{x}}}\right|_{x=1}^{t=0}

\displaystyle=\left.\frac{x}{x+t}\right|_{x=1}^{t=0}

\displaystyle=1.

2.5.
Sine function

The homogeneity function of
f:{\mathbb{R}}\to(0,+\infty)
defined by
f(x)=\sin{x}

is clearly

\displaystyle\textsc{m}(x,t)

\displaystyle=\frac{f(tx)}{f(x)}

\displaystyle=\frac{\sin(tx)}{\sin{x}}

\displaystyle=\frac{e^{i(tx-\frac{\pi}{2})}+e^{-i(tx-\frac{\pi}{2})}}{e^{i(x-\frac{\pi}{2})}+e^{-i(x-\frac{\pi}{2})}}.

The additive homogeneity function reads

\displaystyle\textsc{a}(x,t)

\displaystyle=f(x+t)-f(x)

\displaystyle={\sin(x+t)}-{\sin{x}}

\displaystyle=\sin{x}\cos{t}+\sin{t}\cos{x}-\sin{x}

\displaystyle=\sin{x}(\cos{t}-1)+\sin{t}\cos{x}

\displaystyle=2\sin{\frac{t}{2}}\cos{\left(\frac{t}{2}+x\right)}.

Use the sum-difference-formula

\displaystyle\sin{A}-\sin{B}

\displaystyle=2\cos{\left(\frac{A+B}{2}\right)}\sin{\left(\frac{A-B}{2}\right)}

and
\cos(x-\frac{\pi}{2})=\sin(x)
to verify the last step.

The logarithmic homogeneity function reads

\displaystyle\textsc{l}(x,t)

\displaystyle=f(tx)-f(x)

\displaystyle=\sin(tx)-\sin{x}

\displaystyle=-2\sin\left(\frac{x}{2}(1-t)\right)\cos\left(\frac{x}{2}(1+t)\right).

The exponential homogeneity function reads

\displaystyle\textsc{e}(x,t)

\displaystyle=\frac{f(x+t)}{f(x)}

\displaystyle=\frac{\sin(x+t)}{\sin{x}}

\displaystyle=\frac{\sin{x}\cos{t}+\sin{t}\cos{x}}{\sin{x}}

\displaystyle=\cos{t}+\sin{t}\cot{x}.

2.6.
Cosine function

The homogeneity function of
f:{\mathbb{R}}\to{\mathbb{R}}
defined by
f(x)=\cos{x}

is clearly

\displaystyle\textsc{m}(x,t)

\displaystyle=\frac{f(tx)}{f(x)}

\displaystyle=\frac{\cos(tx)}{\cos{x}}

\displaystyle=\frac{e^{itx}+e^{-itx}}{e^{ix}+e^{-ix}}.

For integer values of
t
,
we can express
cos(tx)
using Chebyshev polynomials of the first kind
[
6
]
, denoted by
T_{t}
.
These polynomials are defined by
T_{t}(x)=\cos(t\arccos(x))
for
t\in{\mathbb{N}}_{0}
,
and satisfy

\displaystyle\cos(tx)=T_{t}(x),

where
t
is the degree of the polynomial,
Alternatively,
T_{t}
can be introduced
by the recurrence relation

T_{t}(x)=2xT_{t-1}(x)-T_{t-2}(x)(x)

with the two base cases
T_{0}(x)=1
and
T_{1}(x)=x
.

The additive homogeneity function reads

\displaystyle\textsc{a}(x,t)

\displaystyle=f(x+t)-f(x)

\displaystyle={\cos(x+t)}-{\cos{x}}

\displaystyle=\cos{x}\cos{t}-\sin{t}\sin{x}-\cos{x}

\displaystyle=\cos{x}(\cos{t}-1)-\sin{x}\sin{t}

\displaystyle=-2\sin{\left(x+\frac{t}{2}\right)}\sin{\frac{t}{2}}.

The logarithmic homogeneity function reads

\displaystyle\textsc{l}(x,t)

\displaystyle=f(tx)-f(x)

\displaystyle=\cos{(tx)}-\cos{x}

\displaystyle=-2\sin\left(\frac{x(t-1)}{2}\right)\sin\left(\frac{x(t+1)}{2}\right).

To obtain the final form of the additive and logarithmic, respectively, homogeneity function, use the sum-difference-formula

\cos{A}-\cos{B}=-2\sin{\left(\frac{A+B}{2}\right)}\sin{\left(\frac{A-B}{2}\right)}.

The exponential homogeneity function reads

\displaystyle\textsc{e}(x,t)

\displaystyle=\frac{f(x+t)}{f(x)}

\displaystyle=\frac{\cos(x+t)}{\cos{x}}

\displaystyle=\frac{\cos{x}\cos{t}-\sin{x}\sin{t}}{\cos{x}}

\displaystyle=\cos{t}-\tan{x}\sin{t}

\displaystyle=\frac{e^{it}+e^{-i(2x+t)}}{1+e^{-2ix}}.

3.
Homogeneity and the Harmonic Mean

The concept of homogeneity is not limited to single variable functions,
but works as well for functions of several variables.
To illustrate this, we analyze the homogeneity properties of the harmonic mean

H:(0,+\infty)^{2}\to(0,+\infty)
given by

\displaystyle H(x,y)=\frac{2xy}{x+y}.

3.1.
Multiplicative Homogeneity

The harmonic mean is
positively homogeneous of degree 1
, meaning its multiplicative homogeneity function is simply:

\displaystyle\textsc{m}_{H}(x,y,t):=\frac{H(tx,ty)}{H(x,y)}=t,

which is the identity function on
(0,+\infty)
.
This shows perfect scalability under uniform rescaling of the variables.

3.2.
Additive homogeneity

The additive homogeneity function
reveals how
H
changes under translation

\displaystyle\textsc{a}_{H}(x,y,t)

\displaystyle:=H(x+t,y+t)-H(x,y)

\displaystyle=\frac{t[(x-y)^{2}+t(x+y)]}{(x+y+2t)(x+y)}.

This shows that the translation effect depends on both the scale
t
and the initial disparity between
x
and
y
.

3.3.
Exponential homogeneity

The exponential homogeneity function,
defined by

\textsc{e}_{H}(x,y,t):=\frac{H(x+t,y+t)}{H(x,y)}
,
measures the relative change under translation,
reads here

\displaystyle\textsc{e}_{H}(x,y,t)=\frac{(x+t)(y+t)(x+y)}{xy(x+y+2t)}.

3.4.
Logarithmic homogeneity

For homogeneous means, the logarithmic homogeneity function simplifies to

\displaystyle\textsc{l}_{H}(x,y,t)

\displaystyle:=H(tx,ty)-H(x,y)

\displaystyle=(t-1)H(x,y)

\displaystyle=\frac{2(t-1)xy}{x+y}.

By the relation between the harmonic, arithmetic and geometric mean,
namely, for all
x,y>0
,

\displaystyle H(x,y)

\displaystyle=\frac{2xy}{x+y}

\displaystyle=\frac{G^{2}(x,y)}{A(x,y)},

where

H,G:(0,+\infty)^{2}\to(0,+\infty)

with
G(x,y):=\sqrt{xy}
and
A:{\mathbb{R}}^{2}\to{\mathbb{R}}
with
A(x,y):=\frac{x+y}{2}
,
and the fact that the arithmetic mean is both translative and homogeneous,
i.e.,

\displaystyle A(x+t,y+t)

\displaystyle=t+A(x,y),\quad x,y,t\in{\mathbb{R}},

and

\displaystyle A(tx,ty)

\displaystyle=tA(x,y),\quad x,y,t\in{\mathbb{R}},

the homogeneity functions of
H
can be expressed in terms of the corresponding ones of
G^{2}
and
A
.

•

Scaling
: Multiplicative homogeneity shows
H
scales linearly with its arguments

•

Translation
: Additive homogeneity reveals the change depends on both the translation magnitude
t
and initial disparity
(x-y)^{2}

•

Symmetry
: All forms respect the symmetry
H(x,y)=H(y,x)

•

Special Case
: When
x=y
, by reflexivity of means, all homogeneity functions simplify significantly:

\displaystyle\textsc{a}_{H}(x,x,t)

\displaystyle=t

\displaystyle\textsc{e}_{H}(x,x,t)

\displaystyle=1+\frac{t}{x}

\displaystyle\textsc{l}_{H}(x,x,t)

\displaystyle=(t-1)x

4.
Homogeneity functions of only one variable

We wonder when a homogeneity function is independent of
x
or
t
.
A differentiable function is independent of a variable if its partial derivative with respect to this variable vanishes.
We obtain the following

Theorem 1

(Multiplicative Homogeneity)
.

Let
I\subset(0,+\infty)
be an interval and
f:I\to(0,+\infty)
a differentiable function.
Define the
multiplicative homogeneity function
of
f
as:

\textsc{m}_{f}(x,t)=\frac{f(tx)}{f(x)},\quad x\in I,t>0.

Then:

(1)

\textsc{m}_{f}
is independent of
x

if, and only if,

f
is a power law:

f(x)=Cx^{k}\quad\text{for constants }C>0,k\in\mathbb{R}.

(2)

\textsc{m}_{f}
is independent of
t

if, and only if,

f
is constant.

Proof.

(1)

(Independence of
x
)
:
(
\Rightarrow
) Suppose
\textsc{m}_{f}
is independent of
x
. Then:

\frac{\partial}{\partial x}\left(\frac{f(tx)}{f(x)}\right)=\frac{tf^{\prime}(tx)f(x)-f(tx)f^{\prime}(x)}{f(x)^{2}}=0.

This implies the key identity:

t\frac{f^{\prime}(tx)}{f(tx)}=\frac{f^{\prime}(x)}{f(x)}.\quad(*)

Let
k:=\frac{xf^{\prime}(x)}{f(x)}
(logarithmic derivative). Then:

\text{From }(*):\quad t\cdot\frac{k}{tx}=\frac{k}{x}\implies\frac{k}{x}=\frac{k}{x},

which holds for all
k
. Thus,
k
must be constant. Integrating
\frac{f^{\prime}(x)}{f(x)}=\frac{k}{x}
gives:

\ln f(x)=k\ln x+\ln C\implies f(x)=Cx^{k}.

(
\Leftarrow
) If
f(x)=Cx^{k}
, then:

\textsc{m}_{f}(x,t)=\frac{C(tx)^{k}}{Cx^{k}}=t^{k},

which is independent of
x
.

(2)

(Independence of
t
)
:
(
\Rightarrow
) Assume
\textsc{m}_{f}
is independent of
t
. Then:

\frac{\partial}{\partial t}\left(\frac{f(tx)}{f(x)}\right)=\frac{xf^{\prime}(tx)}{f(x)}=0.

Since
f(x)>0
, we have
xf^{\prime}(tx)=0
. For
x\neq 0
,
f^{\prime}(tx)=0
for all
t>0
, so
f
is constant.

(
\Leftarrow
) If
f
is constant,
\textsc{m}_{f}(x,t)=1
is trivially independent of
t
.

Theorem 2

(Multiplicative Homogeneity (Non-Differentiable Case))
.

Let
I\subset(0,+\infty)
be an interval and
f:I\to(0,+\infty)
a continuous function.
Define the
multiplicative homogeneity function
as:

\textsc{m}_{f}(x,t)=\frac{f(tx)}{f(x)},\quad x\in I,t>0.

Then:

(1)

\textsc{m}_{f}
is independent of
x

if and only if

f
is a power law:

f(x)=Cx^{k}\quad\text{for constants }C>0,k\in\mathbb{R}.

(2)

\textsc{m}_{f}
is independent of
t

if and only if

f
is constant.

Proof.

(1)

(Independence of
x
)
:
(
\Rightarrow
) If
\textsc{m}_{f}(x,t)=g(t)
for some
g
, then:

f(tx)=f(x)g(t).

This is the well-known semi-pexiderized multiplicative Cauchy functional equation. For continuous
f
, the only non-zero solutions are power functions:

f(x)=Cx^{k},

where
C>0
and
k\in\mathbb{R}
are constants (proven by taking logarithms and reducing to the additive Cauchy equation).

(
\Leftarrow
) If
f(x)=Cx^{k}
, then:

\textsc{m}_{f}(x,t)=\frac{C(tx)^{k}}{Cx^{k}}=t^{k},

which depends only on
t
.

(2)

(Independence of
t
)
:
(
\Rightarrow
) If
\textsc{m}_{f}(x,t)=h(x)
, then:

f(tx)=f(x)h(x).

For fixed
x
, the right-hand side is independent of
t
, so
f(tx)
must be constant in
t
. Thus:

f(tx)=f(x)\quad\forall t>0,

implying
f
is constant (set
t=y/x
for
y\in I
).

(
\Leftarrow
) If
f
is constant,
\textsc{m}_{f}(x,t)=1
trivially.

For additive homogeneous functions we have

Theorem 3
.

Let
I\subset{\mathbb{R}}
be an interval and
f:I\to(0,+\infty)
be a differentiable function.
Then the additive homogeneity function of
f
is independent of the variable

(1)

x
, if and only if

f^{\prime}
is
t
-periodic
for all
t

(thus
f
is
t
-periodic plus a linear term);

(2)

t
, if, and only if,
f
is constant on an interval.

Proof.

(1)

Since
f
is differentiable and
\textsc{a}_{f}
independent of
x
, we have

\frac{\partial}{\partial x}\textsc{a}_{f}(x,t)=1f^{\prime}(x+t)-f^{\prime}(x)=0,

whence
f^{\prime}(x+t)=f^{\prime}(x)
as claimed.

(2)

Assume
\textsc{a}_{f}
independent of
t
.
Since
f
is differentiable we have

\frac{\partial}{\partial t}\textsc{a}_{f}(x,t)=f^{\prime}(x+t)-0=0.

Thus,
f^{\prime}(x+t)=0
,
which means that
f
is constant on an interval.

Theorem 4

(Exponential Homogeneity)
.

Let
I\subset\mathbb{R}
be an interval and
f:I\to(0,+\infty)
a differentiable function.
Define the
exponential homogeneity function
of
f
as:

\textsc{e}_{f}(x,t)=\frac{f(x+t)}{f(x)},\quad x,x+t\in I.

Then:

(1)

\textsc{e}_{f}
is independent of
x

if, and only if,

\textsc{e}_{f}(x,t)=e^{t\cdot\frac{f^{\prime}(x)}{f(x)}},

i.e., when
f
is an exponential function
f(x)=Ce^{kx}
with
C,k\in\mathbb{R}
.

(2)

\textsc{e}_{f}
is independent of
t

if, and only if,

f^{\prime}\equiv 0
on
I
,
i.e., when
f
is constant.

Proof.

(1)

(Independence of
x
)
:
Assume
\textsc{e}_{f}
is independent of
x
. Then
\frac{\partial}{\partial x}\textsc{e}_{f}(x,t)=0
.
Computing the derivative:

\frac{\partial}{\partial x}\left(\frac{f(x+t)}{f(x)}\right)=\frac{f^{\prime}(x+t)f(x)-f(x+t)f^{\prime}(x)}{f(x)^{2}}=0.

This implies:

\frac{f^{\prime}(x+t)}{f(x+t)}=\frac{f^{\prime}(x)}{f(x)}\quad x,t.

Thus, the logarithmic derivative
\frac{f^{\prime}}{f}
is constant. Let
k:=\frac{f^{\prime}(x)}{f(x)}
.
Integrating yields:

\ln f(x)=kx+C\quad\Rightarrow\quad f(x)=Ce^{kx}.

Conversely: For
f(x)=Ce^{kx}
, we have:

\textsc{e}_{f}(x,t)=\frac{e^{k(x+t)}}{e^{kx}}=e^{kt},

which is clearly independent of
x
.

(2)

(Independence of
t
)
:
Assume
\textsc{e}_{f}
is independent of
t
. Then
\frac{\partial}{\partial t}\textsc{e}_{f}(x,t)=0
.
Computing the derivative:

\frac{\partial}{\partial t}\left(\frac{f(x+t)}{f(x)}\right)=\frac{f^{\prime}(x+t)}{f(x)}=0.

Since
f(x)>0
, we get
f^{\prime}(x+t)=0
for all
x+t\in I
, hence
f^{\prime}\equiv 0
and
f
is constant.

Conversely: If
f
is constant, then
\textsc{e}_{f}(x,t)=1
is trivially independent of
t
.

Theorem 5

(Logarithmic Homogeneity)
.

Let
I\subset\mathbb{R}
be an interval and
f:I\to\mathbb{R}
be a differentiable function. Define the
logarithmic homogeneity function
as:

\textsc{l}_{f}(x,t)=f(tx)-f(x),\quad x\in I,t>0.

Then:

(1)

Independence of
x
:

(a)

If
0\in I
, then
\textsc{l}_{f}
is independent of
x

if, and only if,

f
is constant.

(b)

If
0\notin I
, then
\textsc{l}_{f}
is independent of
x

if, and only if,
:

f(x)=\begin{cases}k\log x+C&\text{for }I\subset(0,+\infty)\\
k\log(-x)+C&\text{for }I\subset(-\infty,0)\end{cases}

where
k,C\in\mathbb{R}
.

(2)

Independence of
t
:

\textsc{l}_{f}
is independent of
t

if and only if

f
is constant.

Proof.

(1)

Independence of
x
:

(
\Rightarrow
) Assume
\textsc{l}_{f}
is independent of
x
. Then:

\displaystyle f(tx)-f(x)

\displaystyle=g(t)\quad\text{for some function }g

\displaystyle\frac{d}{dx}[f(tx)-f(x)]

\displaystyle=0

\displaystyle tf^{\prime}(tx)-f^{\prime}(x)

\displaystyle=0

\displaystyle tf^{\prime}(tx)

\displaystyle=f^{\prime}(x)

Let
x=1
:

f^{\prime}(t)=\frac{f^{\prime}(1)}{t}

Integrating gives:

f(t)=f^{\prime}(1)\log|t|+C.

(a)

For
0\in I
, differentiability at
0
requires
f^{\prime}(1)=0
, so
f
is constant.

(b)

For
0\notin I
, we obtain logarithmic forms as stated.

(
\Leftarrow
) For the logarithmic forms:

\textsc{l}_{f}(x,t)=k\log(tx)-k\log x=k\log t

which is independent of
x
. The constant case is trivial.

(2)

Independence of
t
:

(
\Rightarrow
) Assume
\textsc{l}_{f}
is independent of
t
:

f(tx)-f(x)=h(x)

Setting
t=1
implies
h(x)=0
, so
f(tx)=f(x)
for all
t>0
. Thus
f
is constant.

(
\Leftarrow
) If
f
is constant,
\textsc{l}_{f}(x,t)=0
is trivially independent of
t
.

5.
Equality problem of homogeneity functions

If functions
f
and
g
coincide, they obviously have the same homogeneity function.
On the other hand,
if homogeneity functions of same type coincide, their generator
do not necessarily have to agree.

Remark 12

(Equality of Multiplicative Homogeneity Functions)
.

Let
I,T\subset{\mathbb{R}}
be non-empty intervals such that
T\cdot I\subset I
.
Assume

\displaystyle{\textsc{m}_{f}}(x,t)={\textsc{m}_{g}}(x,t),\qquad x\in I,t\in T,

for given functions
f,g:I\to(0,+\infty)
.
Then
f
and
g
are proportional, i.e. there is
c>0
such that

\displaystyle f(x)=cg(x),\qquad x\in I.

Proof.

Assuming the equality of two multiplicative homogeneity functions for non-vanishing functions
f
and
g
we have

\displaystyle\frac{f(tx)}{f(x)}=\frac{g(tx)}{g(x)},\qquad x\in I,t\in T,

whence

\displaystyle\frac{f(tx)}{g(tx)}=\frac{f(x)}{g(x)},\qquad x\in I,t\in T.

Thus, the function
h:=\frac{f}{g}
is homogeneous of degree zero, which means that
h
is constant, say
h(x)=c
.
By the definition of
h
, we have
f=cg
as claimed.

Remark 13

(Equality of Additive Homogeneity Functions)
.

Let
I,T
be real non-empty intervals such
I+T\subset I
.
Assume

\displaystyle\textsc{a}_{f}(x,t)=\textsc{a}_{g}(x,t),\qquad x\in I,t\in T,

for given functions
f,g:I\to{\mathbb{R}}
.
Then
f
and
g
agree up to a constant, i.e. there is
c\in{\mathbb{R}}
such that

\displaystyle f(x)=c+g(x),\qquad x\in I.

Proof.

Assuming the equality of two additive homogeneity functions, we have

\displaystyle f(x+t)-f(x)=g(x+t)-g(x),\qquad x\in I,t\in T,

whence

\displaystyle f(x+t)-g(x+t)=f(x)-g(x),,\qquad x\in I,t\in T.

Thus, the function
h:={f}-{g}
is
t
-periodic for all

t\in T
, which means, since
T
is an interval, that
h
is constant,
say
h(x)=c
.
By the definition of
h
, we have
f=c+g
as claimed.

Remark 14

(Equality of Logarithmic Homogeneity Functions)
.

Let
I,T
be real non-empty intervals such that
T\cdot I\subset I
.
Assume

\displaystyle\textsc{l}_{f}(x,t)=\textsc{l}_{g}(x,t),\qquad x\in I,t\in T,

for given functions

.
Then

and

agree up to a constant, i.e. there is

such that

\displaystyle f(x)=c+g(x),\qquad x\in I.

Proof.

Assuming the equality of two logarithmic homogeneity functions, we have

\displaystyle f(tx)-f(x)=g(tx)-g(x),\qquad x\in I,t\in T,

whence

\displaystyle f(tx)-g(tx)=f(x)-g(x),,\qquad x\in I,t\in T.

Thus, the function
h:={f}-{g}
is
0
-homogeneous,
which means that
h
is constant,
say
h(x)=c
.
By the definition of
h
, we have
f=c+g
as claimed.

Remark 15

(Equality of Exponential Homogeneity Functions)
.

Let
I,T
be real non-empty intervals such that
I+T\subset I
..
Assume

\displaystyle{\textsc{e}_{f}}(x,t)=\textsc{e}_{g}(x,t),\qquad x\in I,t\in T,

for given functions

.
Then

and

are proportional, i.e. there is positive

such that

\displaystyle f(x)=cg(x),\qquad x\in I.

Proof.

Assuming the equality of two exponential homogeneity functions for non-vanishing functions

and

we have

\displaystyle\frac{f(x+t)}{f(x)}=\frac{g(x+t)}{g(x)},\qquad x\in I,t\in T,

\displaystyle\frac{f(x+t)}{g(x+t)}=\frac{f(x)}{g(x)},\qquad x\in I,t\in T.

Thus, the function

is
t
-periodic for all
t\in T
,
which means that

is constant, say

.
By the definition of

,
we have

as claimed.

The Cauchy equations and Homogeneity Functions

The four types of Cauchy equations
can be expressed in terms of homogeneity functions -
how exactly we see in the following

Remark 16
.

Let
I\subset{\mathbb{R}}
be a non-empty interval and

a function.
Then

is additive if, and only if,

(1)

its additive homogeneity function satisfies

\displaystyle\textsc{a}_{f}(x,t)=f(t),\qquad x,t;

(2)

its exponential homogeneity function satisfies

\displaystyle\textsc{e}_{f}(x,t)=1+\frac{f(t)}{f(x)},\qquad x,t;

Proof.

(1)

(\Rightarrow)

Assume

is additive, i.e.

\displaystyle f(x+y)=f(x)+f(y)

for all

.
Thus, for all

\displaystyle=f(x+t)-f(x)

\displaystyle=f(x)+f(t)-f(x)

\displaystyle=f(t),

for all

as claimed.

(\Leftarrow)
Vice versa, assuming
\textsc{a}_{f}(x,t)=f(t)
, we have, by the definition of the additive homogeneity function,

\displaystyle f(x+t)-f(x)

\displaystyle=f(t),

(2)

(\Rightarrow)

\displaystyle=\frac{f(x+t)}{f(x)}

Similarly, the exponential Cauchy equation can be expressed in terms of

7.
Homogeneity functions of Cauchy Quotients

Here we consider the four types of Cauchy quotients as functionals
and ask how the respective kind of homogeneity of the generator is transported.
Let us recall the Cauchy quotients: for an arbitrary function
f:(0,+\infty)\to(0,+\infty)

and
k\in{\mathbb{N}}
,
k\geq 2
, arbitrarily fixed,
we define

\displaystyle A_{f}(x_{1},\ldots,x_{k})

\displaystyle=\frac{f(x_{1})+\cdots+f(x_{k})}{f(x_{1}+\cdots+x_{k})},

\displaystyle B_{f}(x_{1},\ldots,x_{k})

\displaystyle=\frac{f(x_{1})\cdot\cdots\cdot f(x_{k})}{f(x_{1}+\cdots+x_{k})},

\displaystyle L_{f}(x_{1},\ldots,x_{k})

\displaystyle=\frac{f(x_{1})+\cdots+f(x_{k})}{f(x_{1}\cdot\cdots\cdot x_{k})},

\displaystyle P_{f}(x_{1},\ldots,x_{k})

\displaystyle=\frac{f(x_{1})\cdot\cdots\cdot f(x_{k})}{f(x_{1}\cdot\cdots\cdot x_{k})},

as the additive, exponential (traditionally known as beta-type function), logarithmic and multiplicative Cauchy quotient.
Assume that
f
has (multiplicative) homogeneity function
\textsc{m}_{f}=:m
, i.e.

f(tx)=m(x,t)f(x),\qquad x\in I,t\in T.

Then we have

P_{f,k}(tx_{1},\ldots,tx_{k})=\frac{m(x_{1},t)\cdots m(x_{k},t)}{m(x_{1}\cdots x_{k},t^{k})}P_{f,k}(x_{1},\ldots,x_{k}),\qquad x_{1},\ldots,x_{k}\in I,t\in T,

and

B_{f,k}(tx_{1},\ldots,tx_{k})=\frac{m(x_{1},t)\cdots m(x_{k},t)}{m(x_{1}+\cdots+x_{k},t)}B_{f,k}(x_{1},\ldots,x_{k}),\qquad x_{1},\ldots,x_{k}\in I,t\in T.

More systematically:

Given the homogeneity functions:

\displaystyle\textsc{a}_{f}(x,t)=f(x+t)-f(x),

\displaystyle\textsc{m}_{f}(x,t)=\frac{f(tx)}{f(x)},

\displaystyle\textsc{e}_{f}(x,t)=\frac{f(x+t)}{f(x)},

\displaystyle\textsc{l}_{f}(x,t)=f(tx)-f(x).

\displaystyle\text{(1) Additive Cauchy quotient }A_{f}:

\displaystyle A_{f}(x_{1},\ldots,x_{k})=\frac{f(x_{1})+\cdots+f(x_{k})}{f(x_{1}+\cdots+x_{k})},

\displaystyle A_{f}(x_{1}+t,\ldots,x_{k}+t)=\frac{(f(x_{1})+\textsc{a}_{f}(x_{1},t))+\cdots+(f(x_{k})+\textsc{a}_{f}(x_{k},t))}{f(x_{1}+\cdots+x_{k})+\textsc{a}_{f}(x_{1}+\cdots+x_{k},kt)}.

\displaystyle\text{(2) Multiplicative Cauchy quotient }P_{f}:

\displaystyle P_{f}(x_{1},\ldots,x_{k})=\frac{f(x_{1})\cdots f(x_{k})}{f(x_{1}\cdots x_{k})},

\displaystyle P_{f}(tx_{1},\ldots,tx_{k})=\frac{\textsc{m}_{f}(x_{1},t)f(x_{1})\cdots\textsc{m}_{f}(x_{k},t)f(x_{k})}{\textsc{m}_{f}(x_{1}\cdots x_{k},t^{k})f(x_{1}\cdots x_{k})}

\displaystyle=\frac{\textsc{m}_{f}(x_{1},t)\cdots\textsc{m}_{f}(x_{k},t)}{\textsc{m}_{f}(x_{1}\cdots x_{k},t^{k})}P_{f}(x_{1},\ldots,x_{k}).

\displaystyle\text{(3) Exponential Cauchy quotient }B_{f}:

\displaystyle B_{f}(x_{1},\ldots,x_{k})=\frac{f(x_{1})\cdots f(x_{k})}{f(x_{1}+\cdots+x_{k})},

\displaystyle B_{f}(x_{1}+t,\ldots,x_{k}+t)

\displaystyle=\frac{\textsc{e}_{f}(x_{1},t)f(x_{1})\cdots\textsc{e}_{f}(x_{k},t)f(x_{k})}{\textsc{e}_{f}(x_{1}+\cdots+x_{k},kt)f(x_{1}+\cdots+x_{k})}

\displaystyle=\frac{\textsc{e}_{f}(x_{1},t)\cdots\textsc{e}_{f}(x_{k},t)}{\textsc{e}_{f}(x_{1}+\cdots+x_{k},kt)}B_{f}(x_{1},\ldots,x_{k}).

\displaystyle\text{(4) Logarithmic Cauchy quotient }L_{f}:

\displaystyle L_{f}(x_{1},\ldots,x_{k})=\frac{f(x_{1})+\cdots+f(x_{k})}{f(x_{1}\cdots x_{k})},

\displaystyle L_{f}(tx_{1},\ldots,tx_{k})=\frac{(f(x_{1})+\textsc{l}_{f}(x_{1},t))+\cdots+(f(x_{k})+\textsc{l}_{f}(x_{k},t))}{f(x_{1}\cdots x_{k})+\textsc{l}_{f}(x_{1}\cdots x_{k},t^{k})}.

Note that especially the homogeneity properties of multiplicative and exponential Cauchy quotients are remarkable,
since they factor and respect the form of the same Cauchy quotient!

Decomposition into even and odd part

A function
f:{\mathbb{R}}\to{\mathbb{R}}
may be uniquely decomposed into a sum of
an even and odd part, namely

(7.1)

f(x)=\underbrace{\frac{f(x)+f(-x)}{2}}_{=:f_{e}(x)}+\underbrace{\frac{f(x)-f(-x)}{2}}_{=:f_{o}(x)},\qquad x\in{\mathbb{R}}.

Obviously,
f_{e}
is an even and
f_{o}
an odd function,
i.e.
f_{e}(-x)=f_{e}(x)
and
f_{o}(-x)=-f_{o}(x)
for all
x\in{\mathbb{R}}
.
By conjugation,
from inside with logarithm and from outside with exponential,
this decomposition has some multiplicative pendant for positive functions.
Thus, applying the exponential function to both sides of (
7.1
)
and noting that for every
x\in{\mathbb{R}}
there is a unique
u\in(0,+\infty)
such that
x=\log{u}
, we get

(7.2)

e^{f(\log{u})}=e^{\frac{f(\log{u})+f(-\log{u})}{2}}e^{\frac{f(\log{u})-f(-\log{u})}{2}},\qquad u\in(0,+\infty).

Since
-\log{u}=\log{\left(\frac{1}{u}\right)}
for positive
u
, we have

e^{f(x)}=\sqrt{e^{f(\log{u})\cot f(\log{\frac{1}{u}})}}\cdot\sqrt{e^{f(\log{u})-f(\log{\frac{1}{u}})}},\qquad u\in(0,+\infty),

whence

e^{f(\log{u})}=\sqrt{e^{f(\log{u})\cdot f(\log{\frac{1}{u}})}}+\sqrt{e^{f(\log{u})-f(\log{\frac{1}{u}})}},\qquad u\in(0,+\infty).

Thus, the function
g:=\exp\circ f\circ\log:(0,+\infty)\to(0,+\infty)
satisfies

g(u)=\underbrace{\sqrt{g(u)g\left(\frac{1}{u}\right)}}_{g_{me}(u)}\underbrace{\sqrt{\frac{g(u)}{g\left(\frac{1}{u}\right)}}}_{g_{mo}(u)}\qquad u\in(0,+\infty).

The function
g_{me}
is called the multiplicative even part,
and
g_{mo}
the multiplicative odd part of
g
.
A positive function which coincides with its multiplicative even part [with its multiplicatively odd part],
i.e.
g_{me}=g
[
g_{mo}=g
],
is called multiplicatively even [multiplicatively odd].

It follows that a multiplicatively even function is characterized by
g(u)=g(\frac{1}{u})
(multiplicatively even functions remain invariant under inversion)
and a multiplicative odd function by
g(\frac{1}{u})=\frac{1}{g(u)}
.

In connection with the decomposition of a function into even and odd part the arithmetic mean plays a prominent role, namely

\displaystyle f_{e}(x)

\displaystyle=\frac{f(x)+f(-x)}{2}

\displaystyle=A(f(x),f(-x))

and

\displaystyle f_{o}(x)

\displaystyle=\frac{f(x)-f(-x)}{2}

\displaystyle=A(f(x),-f(-x))

where
A:{\mathbb{R}}^{2}\to{\mathbb{R}}
defined by
A(x,y)=\frac{x+y}{2}
is the arithmetic mean.

Later on,
when decomposing a positive function into multiplicative even and multiplicative odd part,
the geometric mean appeared naturally, since

\displaystyle g_{me}(u)

\displaystyle={\sqrt{g(u)g(1/u)}}

\displaystyle=G(g(u),g\left(1/u\right))

and

\displaystyle g_{mo}(u)

\displaystyle={\sqrt{\frac{g(u)}{g\left(1/u\right)}}}

\displaystyle=G(g(u),1/g(1/u))

where
G:(0,+\infty)^{2}\to(0,+\infty)
defined by
G(x,y)=\sqrt{xy}
is the geometric mean
(and reflection at the
y
-axis becomes inversion).

More generally, we may ask how a (possibly non quasi-arithmetic) mean
M:I^{2}\to I

gives rise to a suitable decomposition of a given function defined on this interval.

To understand a little better the decomposition of a function defined on the reals
into odd and even part and how conjugation gives rise to a positive function

8.1.
Sine function

Let
f:{\mathbb{R}}\to[-1,1]

.

for all

for all

.

Cosine function

Let
g:{\mathbb{R}}\to[-1,1]
be

.

g(-x)=g(x)
for all
x\in{\mathbb{R}}
.
Its exponential conjugate
h:=\exp\circ f\circ\log
reads
h:(0,+\infty)\to[\frac{1}{e},e]
with

satisfies

for all

.

Power functions

Let

be

for

fixed.
Apparently,
f
is even for
n
even, and
f
is odd for odd exponents.
Thus,

g:(0,+\infty)\to{\mathbb{R}}
defined by

g(u):=e^{f(\log{u})}=e^{(\log{u})^{n}}=u^{(\log{u})^{n-1}}

is multiplicatively even when

is even, and multiplicatively odd when

is odd.

8.4.
Exponential functions

Let
f:{\mathbb{R}}\to(0,+\infty)
be defined by

for some positive

.
Its decomposition into even and odd part reads

and

.
Its exponential conjugate
g:(0,+\infty)\to(0,+\infty)
defined by

\displaystyle g(u)

\displaystyle=e^{a^{\log{u}}}

\displaystyle=u^{\log{a}}

,
and multiplicatively odd part

.

9.
Means in a linear space and how to get their weight functions

The classical notion of real means using inequalities can be generalized to mean in a linear space
(cf.
[
3
]
).
In a nutshell, a mean in a linear space (of
k
variables,
k\geq 2
) is an affine combination of its variables where coefficients may depend on the variables.
To illustrate this idea, we consider the case of
k=2
variables.
For simplicity, we suppress in our notation dependencies on the variables in the weight functions, for which we usually use Greek letters.
Thus,
\lambda
denotes in fact a function of several variables, for instance
\lambda=\lambda(x,y)
in the two-variable case.
A two-variable mean in a linear space
M:I^{2}\to I
is thus a function of the form

M(x,y)=\lambda x+(1-\lambda)y

where
\lambda:I^{2}\to[0,+\infty]
is the weight function of the mean.
Since
M(x,y)=y+\lambda(x-y)
for all
x,y\in I
,
we easily obtain
\lambda=\frac{M(x,y)-y}{x-y}
whenever
x\neq y
.
On the diagonal, i.e., when the variables are all equal, the weight functions of a mean are indeterminate.
How about the three variables case?
Let
M:I^{3}\to I
be a three variable mean:

\displaystyle M(x,y,z)

\displaystyle=\lambda x+\mu y+(1-(\lambda+\mu))z

\displaystyle=\lambda(x-z)+\mu(y-z)+z.

The problem to find their two weight functions
\lambda,\mu:I^{2}\to[0,+\infty]
is slightly more involved.
We have

(9.1)

\displaystyle M(x,x,z)=(\lambda+\mu)(x-z)+z,\qquad{x\neq y},

thus

(9.2)

\lambda+\mu=\frac{M(x,x,z)-z}{x-z},\qquad x\neq z,

and

\displaystyle M(x,y,y)

\displaystyle=\lambda x+(1-\lambda)y

\displaystyle=\lambda(x-y)+y.

Consequently,

\lambda=\lambda(x,y,y)=\frac{M(x,y,y)-y}{x-y},\qquad x\neq y,

and thus, by (
9.2
),

\mu=\frac{M(x,x,z)-z}{x-z}-\frac{M(x,y,y)-y}{x-y},\qquad x\neq y,x\neq z.

Remark 20
.

For a mean of
k
variables,
k\in{\mathbb{N}},k\geq 2
,
M:I^{k}\to I
,
(x_{1},\ldots,x_{k})\mapsto M(x_{1},\ldots,x_{k})

the
i
-th weight function, which is the function in front of the
i
-th variable,
equals the mean evaluated at the
i
-th variable at the
i
-th position and the
k
-th at all other positions minus the
z
-th variable devided by the difference of the
i
-th and the
k
-th variable, for
1\leq i\leq k-1
.
After having all
k-1
independent weight functions at our disposition, the remaining one in front of the last variable equals
1-(\lambda_{1}+\cdots+\lambda_{k-1})
.

Remark 21
.

Deriving the weight functions for a general three variable mean,
we did a serious mistake:
putting
x=y
, we obtained an expression for
\lambda+\mu
,
and putting
y=z
, an expression for
\lambda
.
In the end (namely when we went back to our original equation),
these additional assumptions (”conditional diagonality”) fell under the table.
The problem here is that the 1st summand is defined for

x\neq z
and the 2nd summand for
x\neq y

Our derivations lack rigour.

10.
Means and their Weight Functions

By the preceding section the weight function of a bivariable mean
M:I^{2}\to I
is given by

(10.1)

\lambda(x,y)=\frac{M(x,y)-y}{x-y},\qquad x\neq y;

\lambda(x,x)
is undetermined.

It is of main interest to charaterize a mean by its weight function.
More generally, when
M
is any bivariable function on some intervall
I
,
we call the function
\lambda
defined by (
10.1
) its weight function.

Let us consider next a toy example where
M
is a beta-type function, i.e.

M(x,y)=\frac{f(x)f(y)}{f(x+y)},\qquad x\neq y.

Its weight function is given by

\lambda_{f}(x,y)=\frac{f(x)f(y)-yf(x+y)}{(x-y)f(x+y)}

It is known that a bivariable beta-type function is a mean if
f(x)=2x

resulting in the weight function

\lambda_{H}(x,y)=\frac{y}{x+y}.

So a smooth additive function generates the harmonic mean and the corresponding weight
\lambda_{H}
is a rational function.

If
f
is exponential, the beta-type weight function becomes

\lambda_{f}(x,y)=\frac{1-y}{x-y},\quad x\neq y,

having the following relation with the weight function
\lambda_{H}

\lambda_{f}(x,y)=\lambda_{f}(x+1-2y,1-y).
