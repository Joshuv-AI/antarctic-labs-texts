MMS-VPR: A Fine-Grained Multimodal Street-Level Visual Place Recognition Dataset and Evaluation Benchmark for Dense Pedestrian Environments
Yiwei Ou, Xiaobin Ren, Ronggui Sun, Guansong Gao, Kaiqi Zhao, Manfredo Manfredini

Abstract
Existing visual place recognition (VPR) datasets predominantly rely on vehicle-mounted imagery, offer limited multimodal diversity, and underrepresent dense pedestrian street scenes, particularly in non-Western urban contexts. We present MMS-VPR , a challenging fine-grained multimodal benchmark for pedestrian-level place recognition in dense urban environments. MMS-VPR comprises 110,529 images and 2,527 video clips across 208 fine-grained location classes within a ∼ \sim 70,800 m 2 compact urban district in Chengdu, China—with adjacent classes separated by as little as 10–20 m. Unlike large-area VPR datasets, MMS-VPR elevates difficulty through visual ambiguity: repetitive storefronts, dense occlusion, and rapid viewpoint changes that challenge both CNN-based and Transformer-based state-of-the-art methods. Field data were collected in 2024 following a systematic multi-direction, dual-perspective protocol; social media imagery spans seven years (2019–2025), providing long-term appearance change across 31,726 geolocated photographs. Each location features comprehensive day–night coverage, multiple viewing angles, and multimodal annotations including GPS coordinates, timestamps, semantic text, and an explicit spatial graph with space syntax enrichments. We release MMS-VPRlib , a unified benchmarking platform consolidating 22 baselines spanning shallow ML, CNN, Transformer, graph neural network (GNN), and multimodal architectures under a standardized, reproducible pipeline. Controlled modality ablation studies confirm that video temporal features are the most powerful single modality (+15.8 % accuracy over image-only), semantic text provides superior coarse retrieval (R@5 = 0.988 alone), and full multimodal fusion achieves 0.981 accuracy---a +19.2 % gain over image-only---while graph-structured modeling provides additional signal in fine-grained structural subsets. To address these limitations, we introduce MMS-VPR for street-level VPR evaluation in dense urban environments. The dataset covers 208 fine-grained locations in Chengdu, China, containing 110,529 images and 2,527 videos aligned with semantic text annotations and graph-structured location labels. It has four key characteristics:
(1) Pedestrian-only streets
—systematic field collection in dense commercial districts inaccessible to vehicles, providing human-centric viewpoints for AR and navigation applications;
(2) Day & night coverage
—balanced temporal sampling across daytime (7AM-5PM) and nighttime (6PM-10PM) periods, enabling illumination-robust place recognition;
(3) Multimodality
—integration of images, videos, and rich textual annotations (GPS coordinates, spatial identifiers, shop names/signage, street properties, space syntax metrics), organized in an explicit graph structure supporting diverse learning paradigms;
(4) Long temporal span
—combining systematic field collection in 2024 and seven years of public social media data (2019-2025) at the same locations, providing both fine-grained temporal granularity and extended coverage for modeling urban evolution. All released visual data are downsampled to a longest side of 256 pixels, and faces, license plates, and other personally identifiable details are anonymized before release.

Evaluation scope and benchmark positioning

2.1
Intended evaluative role

MMS-VPR is intended to evaluate whether a VPR system can recognize fine-grained pedestrian locations under conditions that are common in human-centered urban navigation but underrepresented in vehicle-oriented benchmarks. Formally, the main task is closed-set place recognition: given a query image, video clip, text record, or fused multimodal input, a model predicts one of
C=208
location classes. A top-1 prediction is correct if and only if the predicted class matches the ground-truth location label. We report Accuracy, macro Precision, macro Recall, macro F1, and Recall@K.
The benchmark supports the following evaluative claims: 1) robustness to pedestrian-scale viewpoint change and day-night illumination variation; 2) benefits and limits of multimodal inputs, especially visual media plus semantic signage or shop-name text;
3) relative behavior of generic visual backbones, VPR-specific retrieval objectives, and vision-language pretrained models; 4) reproducibility and cross-dataset comparability through a shared library.

Notably, the benchmark does not claim to measure cross-city generalization by itself, because the physical site is a single district. It also does not provide calibrated 6DoF camera poses or an evaluation for person identification, tracking, or surveillance. Spatial graph and space-syntax annotations are released as auxiliary metadata; the main benchmark does not rank methods by those attributes unless a graph-aware protocol is explicitly selected.

2.2
Comparison with existing datasets and benchmark tools

We argue that pedestrian-level VPR should be evaluated as a distinct setting rather than treated as a small variant of vehicle-view VPR. A user, robot, or AR device in a pedestrian commercial district must distinguish adjacent intersections, storefronts, alleys, and squares under four simultaneous sources of difficulty: (i) restricted human-scale viewpoints instead of vehicle panoramas; (ii) day-night illumination shifts and artificial lighting; (iii) dynamic occlusion by crowds and temporary commercial displays; and (iv) semantic ambiguity, where visually similar facades are disambiguated by text, signs, and spatial context. 3.1
Site Selection

We selected Chengdu Taikoo Li, a
\sim
70,800 m
2
open-air commercial
district in Chengdu’s CBD (Figure
1
), for three reasons:
(1)
spatial openness
—natural lighting, varied weather, and high crowd density create
genuine environmental complexity;
(2)
functional diversity
—retail, dining, leisure, and cultural spaces ensure rich spatial
semantics and dense pedestrian traffic, producing a high degree of visual and semantic variation over a compact area;
(3)
urban integration
—seamless connection to surrounding urban pulic streets makes the environment representative of globally common
pedestrianized commercial districts.
Crucially, this environment challenges place recognition with
repetitive visual elements

(franchise storefronts, identical signage across locations) and dense occlusion patterns
that indoor mall datasets
[
39
]
and large-area outdoor datasets both fail to capture.
Detailed site characteristics are in Appendix
B.2
.

3.2
Systematic Data Collection Framework

To maximize reproducibility, we use consumer smartphones (iPhone XS Max, 11 Pro Max)
for all visual capture: 4032
\times
3024 images and 1920
\times
1080 video at 30fps.
The framework is grounded in three evidence-based principles
(Table

).

Principle 1: Four-Direction Coverage.

VPR performance degrades up to 40% under viewpoint mismatch
[
8
]
.
We systematically capture all four cardinal directions (N, S, E, W) for each street segment,
providing coverage matching real-world social media content patterns rather than
360
∘
panoramas that introduce geometric distortion
[

]
.

Principle 2: Dual-Perspective Capture.

Pedestrians naturally employ two viewing strategies in high-rise environments:
forward-looking (0
∘
) for navigation and upward-looking (30–60
∘
) for landmark
recognition
[
45
,
20
]
.
Incorporating upward-facing imagery improves place recall by 15–30% where building
facades serve as primary discriminative features
[
25
]
.
We capture each view at horizontal (0
∘
) and upward (45
∘
) angles.

Principle 3: Balanced Day–Night Coverage.

Illumination variation causes 50–80% performance drops for daytime-trained
models
[
48
,
5
]
.
Social-media imagery in commercial districts exhibits pronounced temporal patterns tied to
visitor activity rhythms
[
61
]
.
We enforce equivalent data volume during daytime (7AM–5PM) and nighttime (6PM–10PM).

Dual-Source Data Integration

Field Collection (2024).

Systematic on-site collection yielded 78,575 images and 2,527 video clips

and

4
MMS-VPR Dataset

4.1
Task Formulation

We formulate VPR as closed-set classification over
C=208
locations.
Each location constitutes one class, uniquely identified by its position in the spatial graph.
Given a query (image, video, or text, or any combination), a model predicts class

\hat{c}\in\{0,\ldots,207\}
.
A prediction is correct if and only if
\hat{c}
matches the ground-truth location label.
Performance is measured by Accuracy, Precision, Recall, and F1 over a held-out test split
(per-location 80/20 stratified split ensuring each class appears in both training and test).
This closed-set formulation reflects the intended deployment scenario: a system trained
on a known urban district that must perform real-time pedestrian localization within it.

4.2
Multimodal Data Composition

MMS-VPR integrates three complementary modalities unified through a spatial graph:

Image Data.

110,529 georeferenced images from two sources:
(1)
Field-collected
—78,575 images including raw photographs and 1 Hz-extracted frames,
covering balanced day–night periods under four-direction, dual-perspective protocols;
(2)
Social media
—31,954 curated photographs from Weibo (2019–2025).
Resolution is down-scaled from 4032
\times
3024 (original) to 256
\times
192 (preprocessed).

Video Data.

2,527 video clips (20–60 seconds, 256
\times
144 at 30fps).
Videos provide motion dynamics absent in stills, supporting temporal architectures
(LSTM, GRU, 3D-CNN, Video Transformers).

Textual Data.

Each location is annotated with: (1)
Spatial identifiers
encoding graph topology
(e.g., “Eh-5-3”), enabling adjacency matrix construction for GNNs;
(2)
Visible signage
—discriminative store names and landmark text from
OCR and manual verification (e.g., “Starbucks, Adidas, LEGO”);
(3)
GPS coordinates
;
(4)
Space syntax metrics
—quantitative spatial configuration from city science
(provided for future research; see Appendix
B.6
).

G=(V,E)

3

Nodes
(
|V_{node}|=81

i
-
j
”;

(
|V_{h}|=61

-

(

(

MMS-VPRlib Benchmark

In view of the limited support for modern algorithms and datasets in existing VPR benchmarks (Table
1
, Panel B), we propose MMS-VPRlib, a unified benchmark supporting multimodal inputs, diverse VPR datasets, and state-of-the-art VPR models. Unlike VPR-Bench
[

Data Format and Pre-processing.

MMS-VPRlib accepts PNG, JPEG, MP4, and TXT inputs.
Pre-processing includes resolution standardization, 1 Hz frame extraction for video,
modality-specific normalization, and principled 80/20 per-location train–test splitting.
Multi-modal data undergo signal enhancement (low-light correction, deblurring,
text normalization) to reduce spurious dataset bias.

Conclusion

We present MMS-VPR, a fine-grained multimodal benchmark that elevates VPR difficulty to a
new level by targeting 208 pedestrian-level location classes within a compact urban district
of only
\sim
70,800 m
2
,
with sub-20 m class separation and severe inter-class visual ambiguity.
MMS-VPR uniquely integrates images, videos, semantic text, spatial graph structure,

B.3.1
Advantages Over Indoor Mall Datasets

Previous urban commercial-environment VPR datasets primarily focus on interior spaces of enclosed indoor malls. For instance,
Lee et al. [39]
proposed a localization dataset of indoor shopping mall spaces in Seoul, South Korea. However, these VPR datasets present several limitations:

•

Low visual diversity:
Interior mall spaces typically feature controlled, artificial lighting conditions with minimal temporal variation, limiting the model’s ability to recognize identical places under different lighting environments.

•

Insufficient spatial uniqueness:
Interior commercial spaces, particularly brand stores (e.g., Louis Vuitton, Nike, Gucci), often employ standardized design languages across different locations worldwide. This lack of uniqueness impedes the development of place recognition algorithms with global applicability.

•

Limited environmental complexity:
Indoor environments lack natural variations in lighting, weather, and occlusion patterns that characterize real-world urban settings.

•

Restricted generalization:
Models trained on interior mall datasets may struggle to generalize to the diverse conditions found in outdoor urban environments.

MMS-VPR addresses these limitations by focusing on outdoor pedestrian streets within a precinct mall setting. These spaces feature:

•

Environmental variability:
Comprehensive surrounding urban context features, skylines, natural lighting conditions, and varying pedestrian flows that ensure absolute and global uniqueness of place recognition results.

•

Semantic richness:
Varied functions including shopping, dining, leisure, art, and cultural spaces that provide diverse contextual cues for recognition.

•

Structural openness:
Long sightlines, rich occlusion patterns, and varied viewpoints that challenge and improve model generalization.

•

Temporal robustness:
Environmental variation across day/night cycles and seven years of social media imagery, providing a more robust training foundation for real-world place recognition algorithms.

These features make it particularly suitable for examining the performance of place recognition algorithms in real-world urban scenarios with complex pedestrian dynamics, addressing a significant gap in existing place recognition datasets.

Illustration of the effective field of view (FOV) of human eyes.

For urban environments with tall buildings, capturing visual features from different perspectives is essential. While a single horizontal perspective may suffice for low-density, low-rise urban areas, high-rise environments require additional angles to comprehensively capture spatial characteristics.

We employ two shooting angles to simulate human viewing behaviors in high-rise urban settings:

1.

0° horizontal angle: Representing the standard forward-looking perspective when walking — to capture typical eye-level architectural features.

2.

45° upward angle: Simulating the natural head tilt when observing tall buildings — to capture upper-level urban context and building skylines.

These two perspectives, combined with the four directional views, ensure comprehensive capture of panoramic visual features. Additionally, we use both 0.5x (ultra-wide) and 1x (standard) camera modes to account for the variety of device settings used in social media content creation.

B.6
Space Syntax Annotations

Traditional VPR datasets provide only visual appearance and coordinates, overlooking spatial
configuration context that shapes human navigation
[
31
,
47
]
.
We integrate
space syntax
theory
[
30
,
29
]
to enrich
annotations with spatial configuration metrics, enabling future research in:
(1) topological context modeling;
(2) flow-aware retrieval;
(3) hierarchical place search;
(4) cross-city transfer.

B.6.1
Metric Computation

Space syntax comprises two main metrics.

Integration
(global accessibility; higher = centrally located, easily accessible streets):

\text{Integration}_{i}=\frac{k-2}{2\!\left(\frac{1}{k-1}\sum_{j\neq i}d(i,j)-1\right)}

(1)

where
d(i,j)
is shortest-path distance between streets
i
and
j
.

Betweenness
(through-movement potential; higher = primary route, heavier pedestrian flow):

\text{Betweenness}_{i}=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}j\neq i\\
k\neq i,\,k\neq j\end{subarray}}\frac{\sigma_{jk}(i)}{\sigma_{jk}}

(2)

where
\sigma_{jk}(i)
counts shortest paths passing through street
i
.
We employ both
angular distance

d_{a}=\sum_{\text{turns}}\theta/90^{\circ}

and
weighted distance

d_{w}=0.5\cdot\text{norm}(d_{e})+0.5\cdot\text{norm}(d_{a})
,
yielding four properties per street (angular integration, weighted integration, angular
betweenness, weighted betweenness).

B.6.3
Planned Future Experiments

Space syntax annotations are dataset-level enrichments, not current benchmark inputs.
Concrete next steps enabled by these annotations include:
(1)
Flow-weighted graph retrieval
—using betweenness as edge weights in GNN;
(2)
Hierarchical search
—using integration values to stratify candidate locations
before fine-grained matching;
(3)
Context-aware query routing
—weighting model confidence by spatial
accessibility scores.
These experiments require specialized architectures beyond the current benchmark scope
and are left for future work.

Data Governance and Licensing

All data in MMS-VPR are released under the

Creative Commons Attribution–NonCommercial–ShareAlike 4.0
International (CC BY-NC-SA 4.0)
license.
This applies uniformly to both field-collected and social media imagery,
ensuring non-commercial use, privacy protection, and prevention of dataset
misuse.

Field-collected data (78,575 images, 2,527 videos).

Collected with written permission from Chengdu Taikoo Li management for
non-commercial academic research.
Data collection was confined to publicly accessible outdoor pedestrian areas.

Weibo-sourced data (31,954 images).

All Weibo images are:
(1) publicly posted content on the Weibo platform;
(2) anonymised via automated face detection/pixelation and license plate masking;
(3) resolution-limited to a maximum long edge of 256 pixels, making individual
identification technically infeasible;
(4) restricted to
non-commercial academic use
under CC BY-NC-SA 4.0.

As a fallback for practitioners requiring unrestricted redistribution,
we provide precomputed CLIP ViT-B/32 embeddings (512-dim) for the Weibo
subset, preserving multimodal utility without redistribution concerns.
The field-collected subset alone constitutes a self-contained, fully
functional multimodal VPR benchmark.

Appendix C
Ethics Statement

Our research fully adheres to the NeurIPS Code of Ethics.

Written Institutional Permission.

All field data were collected following written permission from Chengdu
Taikoo Li management, authorising non-commercial academic data collection
and publication.
Collection was confined to publicly accessible outdoor pedestrian areas.

Privacy Protection.

All images and videos—field-collected and Weibo-sourced—underwent:
(1) automated face detection and pixelation;
(2) automated license plate detection and masking;
(3) resolution standardisation to a maximum long edge of 256 pixels
(images: 256
\times
192; video frames: 256
\times
144).
At 256 px, individual identification is technically infeasible even without
face blurring, consistent with Mapillary Vistas
[
44
]
.
All media were manually reviewed post-processing to verify complete anonymisation.

Public Space Context.

Data were captured exclusively in publicly accessible outdoor commercial
spaces where pedestrians naturally expect to be observable.
No private, restricted, or indoor spaces were captured.
Collection used handheld consumer devices at natural pedestrian eye level,
avoiding surveillance-style elevated viewpoints.

Dual-Use Risks and Mitigation.

The dataset’s fine-grained spatial structure could theoretically be misused
for location-based profiling.
We mitigate this through: (1) face/plate anonymisation, (2) 256 px
resolution limit, (3) CC BY-NC-SA 4.0 licensing prohibiting commercial use,
and (4) usage guidelines in the Hugging Face data card explicitly
discouraging downstream surveillance applications.
MMS-VPR is designed to advance research in navigation, AR, and robotics—not
surveillance.

Appendix D
Broader Impacts

MMS-VPR is designed to advance research in pedestrian-level navigation,
augmented reality, urban analytics, and autonomous systems.
The dataset’s fine-grained spatial structure and multimodal richness
enable development of more robust, real-world-deployable place recognition
systems.

Potential negative impacts are mitigated by the CC BY-NC-SA 4.0 license,
anonymisation at capture time, and explicit guidance against surveillance
applications in the dataset card.
We encourage users to consult the dataset documentation and adhere to the
ethical guidelines outlined in Appendix
C
before deployment
in any downstream application.
