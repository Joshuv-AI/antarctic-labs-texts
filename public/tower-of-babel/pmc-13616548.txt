Systemic Oxalosis Mimicking Transthyretin Amyloid Cardiomyopathy
Authors: Bhavya Ancha, Virginia Hahn, Charles Steenbergen, Cheng-Ying Ho, Ahmad Kohsar, Emily Brown, Mark Ranek, Claire Brookmeyer, Michael Polydefkis, Joban Vaishnav
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundTechnetium-99m pyrophosphate (Tc-99m PYP) scintigraphy is a noninvasive imaging modality in the diagnosis of transthyretin amyloid cardiomyopathy (ATTR-CM), though false positive results may result in adverse clinical outcomes.Case SummaryA 53-year-old man with a history of heart failure with reduced ejection fraction and end-stage renal disease presented with upper extremity weakness and hypervolemia. Tc-99m PYP scintigraphy was suggestive of ATTR-CM. However, transthyretin genetic testing was negative, and endomyocardial biopsy was without evidence of amyloid deposition. The patient later died, and autopsy revealed myocardial oxalate deposition concerning for primary vs secondary hyperoxaluria.DiscussionAlthough Tc-99m PYP scintigraphy is a sensitive and specific modality for the detection of ATTR-CM, clinicians should recognize other infiltrative cardiomyopathies causing false positivity such as systemic oxalosis.Key words: cardiomyopathy, diastolic heart failure, nuclear medicine

History of Presentation

A 53-year-old Black man with a history of heart failure with reduced ejection fraction (HFrEF), anuric end-stage renal disease (ESRD) managed with peritoneal dialysis (PD), type 2 diabetes mellitus (DM), and hypertension (HTN) presented with bilateral upper-extremity weakness. On examination, he had jugular venous distension to the earlobe and 1+ bilateral pitting edema. There was visible muscular atrophy of the bilateral forearms and thenar areas. Strength testing revealed 4/5 grip strength in the upper extremities and 3/5 strength in the lower extremities.

Past Medical History

The patient had been diagnosed with chronic kidney disease 5 years before presentation, attributed to long-standing HTN and DM. His chronic kidney disease progressed to ESRD requiring PD 2 years later. He was diagnosed with HFrEF 3 months before his current presentation during a hospitalization for volume overload.

Differential Diagnosis

The combination of bilateral upper-extremity weakness, HFrEF, N-terminal pro–B-type natriuretic peptide of 135,660 pg/mL, and troponin of 150 ng/L raised concern for amyloidosis. Although the markedly elevated N-terminal pro–B-type natriuretic peptide exceeds levels typically observed in cardiac amyloidosis, the finding was interpreted in the context of underlying ESRD, which can significantly elevate levels and limit their diagnostic specificity. Other conditions that were considered included cervical myelopathy, chronic inflammatory demyelinating polyradiculoneuropathy, diabetic neuropathy, and other inherited neuropathies.

Investigations

Transthoracic echocardiography demonstrated moderate global hypokinesis with an estimated left ventricular ejection fraction of 30%-40%, moderate concentric hypertrophy of the left ventricle (interventricular septal diastolic thickness: 1.47 cm), grade II diastolic dysfunction, and bi-atrial enlargement (Figure 1). The right ventricle size and systolic function were preserved, with tricuspid annular plane systolic excursion of 2.2 cm. Additional findings included mild mitral regurgitation, mild aortic stenosis, and trace pericardial effusion.

Light chain testing—including serum and urine protein electrophoresis with immunofixation and serum free light chain assay—was within normal limits. Technetium-99m pyrophosphate (Tc-99m PYP) with single-photon emission computed tomography imaging demonstrated diffusely increased radiotracer uptake (grade 3) along the left ventricular myocardium and heart-to-contralateral chest ratio of 2.27, consistent with transthyretin-related cardiomyopathy (ATTR-CM).

Electromyography and nerve conduction studies revealed severe and diffuse loss of sensory and motor amplitudes in the upper extremities, with mixed axonal and demyelinating features, favoring diffuse polyneuropathy. Nerve conduction studies could not be performed in the distal legs because of previous amputations. The diffuse features of his demyelination were greater than expected for diabetic neuropathy, suggesting an occult secondary process. Nerve and muscle biopsy were recommended but deferred by the patient.

Management (Medical/Interventions)

The patient underwent genetic testing for transthyretin (TTR), which was negative, ruling out hereditary ATTR. Given the patient's relatively young age and the rapid clinical decline—features atypical for wild-type ATTR-CM—an endomyocardial biopsy (EMB) was performed, which did not demonstrate amyloid deposition by Congo red staining. He was suspected of having HF secondary to ESRD and inadequate dialysis on PD, for which transition to intermittent hemodialysis was recommended.

Outcome and Follow-Up

The patient was discharged with outpatient follow-up but was later admitted with acute hypercapnic respiratory failure requiring intubation. His hospital course was complicated by tamponade and cardiac arrest, followed by resuscitation. Because of a poor prognosis, he transitioned to comfort-focused care and died shortly thereafter.

An autopsy demonstrated abundant oxalate crystal deposition in the left ventricle myocardium, septum, and kidneys (Figure 2). Bilateral kidneys had stones present in the renal calyces. Postmortem mass spectrometry analysis demonstrated negative ethylene glycol levels in kidney tissue, minimizing suspicion for toxic ingestion. Given the low suspicion for systemic oxalosis before autopsy, the patient did not undergo genetic or urine testing in his lifetime, nor was oxalate deposition specifically evaluated for on his EMB pre mortem. Preliminary postmortem genetic testing for known primary hyperoxaluria–associated genes (AGXT, GRHPR, HOGA1) via the RenalZoom panel was negative, raising concern for gene-elusive primary hyperoxaluria vs secondary hyperoxaluria.

Discussion

Contemporary diagnosis of ATTR-CM typically relies on grade 2 or 3 uptake on nuclear scintigraphy in the setting of negative light chain testing.1 TTR gene sequencing is necessary to distinguish hereditary TTR from wild-type TTR.1 This case illustrates a rare cause of false positive Tc-99m PYP scintigraphy. Although previous studies report sensitivity exceeding 99% and a specificity of 86% for the diagnosis of ATTR-CM, there is increased recognition of diagnostic limitations, including false positive results.2, 3, 4, 5 Diagnostic estimates are largely derived from cohorts with high pretest probability, often consisting of patients referred to specialized amyloidosis centers. Thus, diagnostic performance may be lower in broader populations, particularly when pretest probability is lower. In our patient, the combination of negative TTR genetic testing, atypical demographic characteristics, and a clinical trajectory more rapid than typical for wild-type ATTR-CM ultimately prompted EMB for further evaluation. Tc-99m PYP primarily binds to myocardial microcalcifications; thus, theoretically any disease process caused by calcium-containing deposits can act as a substrate for radiotracer binding and mimic ATTR on scintigraphy.2, 3, 4, 5 Identified culprits including light chain amyloid cardiomyopathy, hydroxychloroquine toxicity, hyperphosphatemia, and, rarely, alternative cardiomyopathies, such as hypertrophic cardiomyopathy, have been described.2, 3, 4 Oxalate deposition is another such example, though there is only one prior case report describing this entity.5

Hyperoxaluria may be classified as either primary or secondary in etiology. Primary hyperoxaluria, which has 3 known subtypes, is an autosomal recessive condition characterized by impaired metabolism of oxalate, leading to increased urinary oxalate excretion and eventual systemic deposition due to progressive renal failure (Table 1).6 Clinical manifestations include recurrent oxalate nephrolithiasis, nephrocalcinosis, progressive nephropathy, and systemic deposition causing cardiomyopathy, neuropathy, retinopathy, and pathologic fractures.6 Type 1 typically presents early in childhood as renal failure, whereas type 2 can be more indolent and manifest as renal failure in adulthood.6 In our patient, the absence of symptomatic nephrolithiasis and coexisting HTN and DM likely obscured recognition of hyperoxaluria as an underlying etiology of renal disease. The patient never had a kidney biopsy, which may have allowed for an earlier diagnosis. Postmortem genetic testing was negative for known genes associated with primary hyperoxaluria, though this does not exclude the diagnosis, as prior studies demonstrate that a subset of patients with a clinical phenotype consistent with primary hyperoxaluria lack identifiable mutations, reflecting genetic heterogeneity.7 Additionally, because pathogenic variants associated with primary hyperoxaluria have been characterized predominantly in populations of European ancestry, disease-causing variants in under-represented populations may be less recognized.7 Secondary hyperoxaluria is primarily driven by increased intestinal oxalate absorption or excessive oxalate intake, although ethylene glycol ingestion is an important toxic etiology.8 In patients with ESRD, impaired renal clearance may additionally result in systemic oxalate accumulation and tissue deposition.9 In our patient, serum oxalate levels were not obtained during his lifetime, precluding definitive classification. Furthermore, his long-standing PD, which is generally less effective than hemodialysis for oxalate clearance, likely contributed to progressive oxalate accumulation.6 Although the extensive multiorgan involvement suggests an underlying primary process, a definitive distinction between primary hyperoxaluria and secondary oxalosis cannot be made.

There is only one other case report describing hyperoxaluria-mediated cardiomyopathy diagnosed by echocardiogram, cardiac magnetic resonance (CMR) imaging, and EMB.10 Cardiac amyloidosis and oxalosis may be indistinguishable on an echocardiogram alone, whereas CMR imaging can provide some diagnostic clarity. Given the rarity of the disease, CMR patterns in systemic oxalosis are not well defined, though it has been shown that systemic oxalosis can demonstrate a heterogeneous, diffuse subendocardial late gadolinium enhancement (LGE) that can involve the entire myocardium. Although ATTR-CM typically demonstrates subendocardial or transmural LGE, it is important to note that CMR imaging findings may also be patchy or even absent in a subset of patients, particularly early in the disease course or in less advanced phenotypes (Table 1).1,10 Because of clinical instability and ESRD, our patient did not undergo CMR imaging. Systemic oxalosis and amyloidosis share substantial clinical overlap, including nephropathy, neuropathy, and restrictive cardiomyopathy. ESRD further complicates the evaluation of suspected ATTR-CM as imaging features, including increased wall thickness and biatrial enlargement, may overlap. Additionally, light chain testing may be more challenging to interpret in ESRD, complicating the exclusion of AL amyloidosis.1 Moreover, suboptimal oxalate clearance in patients receiving hemodialysis predisposes to systemic oxalate deposition, which may confound the interpretation of Tc-99m PYP scintigraphy, as was observed in our patient.9 Collectively, these factors reduce the specificity of ATTR-CM diagnostic algorithms in patients with ESRD and support consideration of histopathologic confirmation when clinical features are discordant. Unfortunately, in our case, following a negative EMB, systemic oxalosis was not considered, reflecting both the rarity of disease and its under-recognition as a potential cause of cardiomyopathy.6

Conclusions

This case highlights systemic oxalosis as a rare but highly morbid cause of false-positive myocardial uptake on Tc-99m PYP scintigraphy. As nuclear scintigraphy is increasingly utilized in the evaluation of ATTR-CM, clinicians must remain vigilant of causes of false positivity, especially when the clinical picture is not consistent with the presentation of ATTR-CM. Recognition of systemic oxalosis as a potential mimic of ATTR-CM is critical as reliance on Tc-99m PYP or established diagnostic pathways alone may lead to delays in appropriate management and misdiagnosis.
