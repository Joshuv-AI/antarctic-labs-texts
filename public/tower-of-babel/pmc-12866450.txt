Triglyceride glucose index and atherogenic index of plasma predict rapid kidney function decline in cardiovascular–kidney–metabolic syndrome: the first longitudinal evidence from CHARLS
Authors: Xueqi Chen, Yifan Wang, Mengying Dong, Yao Ma, Yun Bo, Yan Wang, Xueyi Xia, Weihong Zhao, Xiaohua Pei
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundCardiovascular–kidney–metabolic (CKM) syndrome involves interconnected cardiovascular, renal, and metabolic dysfunctions. While rapid kidney function decline (RKFD) portends poor prognosis in CKM patients, reliable early predictors remain limited. The triglyceride–glucose (TyG) index and atherogenic index of plasma (AIP) have recently emerged as promising indicators of metabolic dysregulation, but their predictive value for RKFD in CKM syndrome requires further investigation.MethodsWe conducted a prospective cohort analysis of 2763 participants aged ≥ 45 years with CKM syndrome using data from the China Health and Retirement Longitudinal Study (CHARLS). The main outcome measured was the occurrence of RKFD, characterized by an annual estimated glomerular filtration rate decline at least 3 mL/min/1.73 m2 during follow-up. Multivariable logistic regression was employed to examine associations between the TyG index, AIP and RKFD, with dose–response relationships assessed using restricted cubic splines. Subgroup analyses evaluated effect modifications by key covariates, with multiplicity adjustment. A mediation analysis was conducted to explore potential mediators.ResultsDuring 4-year follow-up, 278 cases (10.1%) of RKFD were identified. In the fully adjusted model, each one-unit increase in the TyG index and AIP was associated with 1.80-fold and 3.01-fold higher risks of RKFD, respectively. Restricted cubic spline analysis confirmed nearly linear dose–response relationships (both p-nonlinear > 0.05). Inclusion of the TyG index or AIP enhanced both discrimination and reclassification beyond conventional markers. No significant interactions were observed in subgroup analyses (all P for interaction > 0.05). Uric acid significantly mediated the associations of baseline TyG index and AIP with RKFD, accounting for 17.59% and 17.69% of the total effects, respectively.ConclusionsOur findings demonstrate the utility of the TyG index and AIP as effective biomarkers for stratifying the risk of RKFD in the clinical management of CKM syndrome. Background

The cardiovascular–kidney–metabolic (CKM) syndrome [1–3] is characterized by the intricate interaction of cardiovascular diseases, renal impairment, and metabolic abnormalities, culminating in detrimental health consequences. In this context, rapid kidney function decline (RKFD) is both a marker for chronic kidney disease (CKD) progression and an independent early warning for adverse outcomes in CKM syndrome patients [4]. Early identification and intervention of patients at risk for RKFD are crucial to mitigating its long-term consequences.

The pathophysiology of CKM syndrome involves multifactorial mechanisms [5]. The dysregulation of glycolipid metabolism underlies CKM syndrome pathogenesis. Recent research indicates that non-traditional lipid parameters, such as the triglyceride–glucose (TyG) index and atherogenic index of plasma (AIP), are crucial in predicting negative renal outcomes [6–9]. The TyG index, in particular, is recognized as a dependable surrogate marker for insulin resistance (IR) [10, 11]. AIP as a crucial lipid metabolism marker, offers unique clinical value in the comprehensive management of CKM disorders [12]. Although prior studies have shown positive correlations between TyG index and AIP with kidney function decline, their prognostic value specifically in individuals with CKM syndrome remains unclear.

This study enrolled participants from the China Health and Retirement Longitudinal Study (CHARLS) to investigate the association between baseline TyG index, AIP levels and RKFD risk in individuals with CKM syndrome stages 1–4. These findings aim to enhance understanding of renal health in this population and guide comprehension treatment strategies for improved long-term outcomes.

Methods

Study design and study population

This study utilized data from the CHARLS, a nationally representative cohort survey conducted across 28 provinces in China. The initial survey conducted during 2011–2012 (Wave 1), included 17,708 participants. Subsequent assessments occurred in 2013 (Wave 2), 2015 (Wave 3), 2018 (Wave 4), and 2020 (Wave 5). Blood biochemical measurements were only available in Waves 1 (2011–2012) and 3 (2015). Therefore, this study used Wave 1 as the baseline and Wave 3 as the follow-up for assessing kidney function decline. Detailed documentation is available in the literature [13]. CHARLS adheres to the Declaration of Helsinki’s ethical guidelines and has received ethical approval from Peking University’s Institutional Review Board (IRB 00001052–11015). Participants gave written informed consent before joining the study.

The study enrollment process is depicted in Fig. 1. From the initial 17,708 participants in Wave 1, we sequentially excluded individuals with missing age or sex data (n = 175), those aged < 45 years (n = 602), participants lacking CKM stage information or classified as CKM stage 0 (n = 9041), individuals with baseline estimated glomerular filtration rate (eGFR) < 15 mL/min/1.73 m2 (n = 1328), those missing the TyG index or AIP data (n = 614), and those without follow-up eGFR data in Wave 3 (n = 3185), resulting in a final analytical cohort of 2763 eligible participants.

Data collection and definition

We collected data on the following covariates: (1) sociodemographic factors, including age, sex, educational level, marital status and residence; (2) lifestyle factors, including smoking status and alcohol consumption; (3) physical measurements: body mass index (BMI), waist circumference (WC), systolic blood pressure (SBP) and diastolic blood pressure (DBP); (4) comorbidities: hypertension, diabetes mellitus, metabolic syndrome, dyslipidemia treatment, and depression; and (5) laboratory tests: C-reactive protein (CRP), hemoglobin (Hb), white blood cell count (WBC), platelet count (PLT), fasting blood glucose (FBG), glycated hemoglobin (HbA1c), serum creatinine (SCr), cystatin C (CysC), blood–urea–nitrogen (BUN), uric acid (UA), total cholesterol (TC), triglyceride (TG), high-density lipoprotein cholesterol (HDL-C), and low-density lipoprotein cholesterol (LDL-C).

Educational level was categorized as middle school or above versus elementary school or below. Marital status was grouped as cohabitation (married/partnered) versus living alone (separated/divorced/widowed/never married). Smoking and alcohol use were classified as “yes” or “no” based on self-reported history of ever smoking or consuming alcoholic beverages. Hypertension was defined as SBP at or above 140 mmHg, DBP at or above 90 mmHg, a self-reported physician diagnosis or current use of antihypertensive medication. Diabetes mellitus was diagnosed based on FBG reaching 126 mg/dL or higher, HbA1c at 6.5% or greater, a self-reported physician diagnosis or use of insulin or oral hypoglycemic agents. Metabolic syndrome was diagnosed when at least three of the following components were abnormal: abdominal obesity which was defined as a waist circumference of ≥ 90 cm for men or ≥ 80 cm for women, reduced HDL-C below 40 mg/dL for men or below 50 mg/dL for women, elevated TG measuring 150 mg/dL or above, elevated blood pressure with SBP at least 130 mmHg or DBP at least 85 mmHg or ongoing antihypertensive treatment, or impaired fasting glucose of 100 mg/dL or higher. Dyslipidemia treatment indicated current use of lipid-lowering medications, which included either Western modern medicine or traditional Chinese medicine. Depression was assessed using the ten-item Center for Epidemiologic Studies Depression Scale, with scores ≥ 10 indicating clinically significant symptoms.

Calculation of TyG index and AIP

In this study, the TyG index and AIP were primary exposure variables, defined as TyG = ln[TG (mg/dL) × FBG (mg/dL)/2] [10] and AIP = log[TG (mg/dL)/HDL-C (mg/dL)] [14].

Assessment of study outcome

RKFD was characterized by an annual decline in eGFR of at least 3 ml/min/1.73m2 during the follow-up period, calculated as (baseline eGFR–follow-up eGFR)/follow-up time in years [15]. The eGFR determined using the 2021 chronic kidney disease–epidemiology collaboration (CKD–EPI) creatinine-cystatin C equation [16], which incorporates both SCr and CysC levels for improved accuracy in renal function assessment.

Assessment of CKM syndrome across stages 1–4

Participants were categorized according to the American Heart Association (AHA) CKM staging criteria [1]: Stage 1 involves excessive adiposity with metabolic dysregulation, including obesity, abdominal obesity, prediabetes; Stage 2 includes established metabolic risk factors, including hypertriglyceridemia, hypertension, diabetes mellitus, or metabolic syndrome, along with moderate-to-high risk CKD; Stage 3 involves subclinical cardiovascular disease (CVD), very high-risk CKD or a high predicted 10-year CVD risk by the Framingham risk score [17]; and Stage 4 is characterized by self-reported physician-diagnosed CVD, encompassing conditions, such as heart attack, coronary heart disease, angina, congestive heart failure, other heart problems, and stroke. CKD was defined as either a self-reported physician diagnosis or an eGFR < 60 mL/min/1.73 m2, with staging according to the Kidney Disease Improving Global Outcomes eGFR criteria, based exclusively on eGFR thresholds due to the unavailability of albuminuria data. CKM syndrome stages were consolidated into advanced (stages 3–4) and non-advanced (stages 1–2) categories to ensure adequate statistical power [18]. CKM syndrome definitions are detailed in Table S1 (Additional file).

Statistical analysis

Multiple imputation of missing data was performed using the MICE package, with details on the missingness patterns provided in Table S2 (Additional file). Participants were stratified into quartiles (Q1–Q4) based on their TyG index and AIP values, with Q1 representing the lowest and Q4 the highest values for each index. Continuous variables were reported as medians (interquartile ranges, IQRs) and compared using the Mann–Whitney U or Kruskal–Wallis tests. Categorical variables were conveyed as n (%) and analyzed using the chi-square test or Fisher's exact test.

Given the stable follow-up duration (median 4.0 years, IQR 4.0–4.1), multivariable logistic regression was used to estimate odds ratios (ORs) with 95% confidence intervals (95% CIs) for the associations of the TyG index and AIP with the study outcomes [19]. Covariates were selected based on: (1) established risk factors for renal impairment identified in prior literature; and (2) variables that showed significant differences (p < 0.05) across TyG index or AIP strata [19]. All candidate covariates exhibit acceptable multicollinearity (VIF < 10; Table S3, Additional file). The hierarchical regression models were constructed as follows: Crude model. Model 1 was adjusted for age, sex, marital status, residence, education level, hypertension, diabetes mellitus and depression. Model 2 included additional adjustments for dyslipidemia treatment, smoking status, alcohol consumption, BMI, baseline eGFR, and BUN. Dose–response relationships of the TyG index and AIP with RKFD were evaluated using restricted cubic splines (RCS) within adjusted logistic regression models.

Multivariable-adjusted logistic regression models were constructed, including a baseline model and enhanced models that incorporated the TyG index, AIP, relevant metabolic components (FBG, TG, HDL-C, and LDL-C), Cystatin C, and inflammatory markers (CRP). Model discrimination was assessed using the area under the receiver operating characteristic curve (AUC). Reclassification ability was evaluated using net reclassification improvement (NRI) and integrated discrimination improvement (IDI). All effect estimates are reported with 95% CIs calculated from 1000 bootstrap replicates. Subgroup analyses were conducted to evaluate potential effect modifications by key factors including age, sex, marital status, residence, BMI, metabolic syndrome, hypertension, diabetes, and CKM stage. Interaction terms were incorporated into regression models, with significance assessed using the Wald test. A Bonferroni correction was applied to control for multiple comparisons across all subgroup analyses. Finally, a mediation analysis was performed to evaluate whether UA mediated the associations of the TyG index and AIP with RKFD, with full adjustment for confounders. Sensitivity analyses were conducted using three complementary approaches to evaluate the robustness of the primary findings: (1) modified outcome analysis using a relative eGFR decline of ≥ 25% as an alternative endpoint; (2) complete-case analysis after excluding observations with any missing values; and (3) recalculation of the outcome using the 2021 CKD–EPI creatinine equation.

Analyses were executed using R version 4.4.2 with a two-tailed alpha level of 0.05.

Results

Participants in the highest TyG and AIP quartiles were more likely rural residents and exhibited distinct metabolic profiles, including higher BMI, WC, blood pressure, and greater prevalence of diabetes mellitus, hypertension, metabolic syndrome, and advanced CKM syndrome (Tables 1 and 2). They showed elevated baseline eGFR, UA, FBG, HbA1c, TC, TG, and CRP, along with lower HDL-C (Table S4).

Associations of the TyG index and AIP with RKFD

Table 3 presents the multivariable logistic regression analysis of the associations between the TyG index, AIP, and RKFD. Both indices, analyzed as continuous variables and by quartiles, showed significant positive associations with RKFD across all models. In the fully adjusted model (Model 2), each one-unit increase in the TyG index and AIP was associated with a 1.80-fold (95% CI 1.46–2.24; p < 0.001) and 3.01-fold (95% CI 2.02–4.51; p < 0.001) increased risk of RKFD, respectively. Compared with the lowest quartile (Q1), the highest quartile (Q4) of the TyG index and AIP was associated with a 2.96-fold (95% CI 1.94–4.62; p < 0.001) and 2.85-fold (95% CI 1.88–4.42; p < 0.001) increased risk of RKFD, respectively, with significant dose–response trends. Restricted cubic spline analysis further confirmed consistent and approximately linear associations between both indices with RKFD risk (p for nonlinearity > 0.05 for both; Fig. 2).

Incremental predictive values of the TyG index and AIP indices

The predictive performance of the baseline model (AUC = 0.764; 95% CI 0.731–0.796) was significantly improved by including either the TyG index (AUC = 0.777, 95% CI 0.747–0.808; p = 0.005) or the AIP (AUC = 0.776, 95% CI 0.745–0.807; p = 0.009). Furthermore, both composite indices substantially improved reclassification (all p < 0.05): the TyG index yielded an NRI (categorical) of 0.049, an NRI (continuous) of 0.299, and an IDI of 0.013; the corresponding values for the AIP were 0.065, 0.322, and 0.014. Both composite markers outperformed single metabolic markers, CysC, and CRP (Table 4).

Subgroup and sensitivity analyses

Subgroup analyses (Fig. 3) demonstrated that elevated levels of both the TyG index and AIP were consistently associated with an increased risk of RKFD across most predefined subgroups, with no significant interactions observed (all p for interaction > 0.05). This association was particularly strong in several high-risk subpopulations, including those with adults aged 60 years or older, males, individuals living alone, those with non-diabetic patients. A notable exception was found among participants without metabolic syndrome, in whom neither biomarker showed a significant association with RKFD risk.

Sensitivity analyses (Tables S5–S7) consistently demonstrated robust associations between both the TyG index and AIP with an increased risk of RKFD, across multiple alternative modeling approaches and endpoint definitions.

Mediating role of UA

Mediation analyses showed that UA significantly mediated the association of baseline TyG and AIP with RKFD. Specifically, UA accounted for 17.59% of the total effect for TyG and 17.69% for AIP (both p < 0.001; Fig. 4).

Discussion

To our knowledge, this is the first longitudinal study to demonstrate that both the TyG index and AIP are significantly and independently associated with an increased risk of RKFD in a nationally representative Chinese population with CKM syndrome. A clear dose–response relationship was observed, and these associations remained robust across extensive sensitivity analyses. Furthermore, both indices provided incremental predictive value beyond conventional markers, highlighting their potential utility as integrated biomarkers for the early identification of individuals at heightened risk of renal function decline.

The TyG index has emerged as a robust biomarker of IR and is well-established to be associated with a range of metabolic disorders [20]. Similarly, the AIP, a composite metric derived from HDL-C and TG levels, exhibits superior predictive value compared to individual lipid parameters [21] and functions as an independent positive predictor of renal function decline [3]. Recent evidence further indicates that both the TyG index and AIP are strongly associated with advanced CKM syndrome [22], as well as increased risks of stroke [23, 24], cardiovascular disease [25–27], and mortality [12] in this population. Nevertheless, their ability to predict the risk of RKFD across the full clinical spectrum of CKM syndrome remains inadequately investigated.

Our findings align with the growing recognition of the critical role of insulin resistance and atherogenic dyslipidemia in the progression of renal impairment. A retrospective cohort study of 27,407 hospitalized Chinese patients reported a significant U-shaped association between the TyG index and renal function decline, with an optimal renal protective TyG threshold of 8.88 that remained consistent across all stages of CKM syndrome [28]. The increased risk at lower TyG levels may reflect a catabolic state associated with malnutrition–inflammation, often observed in hospitalized populations with generally poorer nutritional status. Further supporting its predictive utility, a Japanese study of 10,758 individuals found that a TyG index ≥ 8.76 was associated with a 2.25-fold higher risk of future renal decline, defined as a ≥ 25% reduction in eGFR from baseline [29]. The TyG index has also demonstrated prognostic value across diverse clinical contexts, including older adults [30], patients with CKD [31], those with primary membranous nephropathy [32], and hypertensive patients [33]. Notably, a prospective study of 9360 type 2 diabetes patients revealed significant effect modification by sex, with women in the highest TyG tertile facing a 46% greater risk of CKD [7]. Similarly, the AIP has emerged as a powerful predictor of renal outcomes. In a CHARLS-based prospective cohort of participants without baseline CKD, AIP was identified as the most robust lipid-derived indicator for predicting RKFD [8]. A Korean prospective cohort study further confirmed that elevated AIP levels are independently associated with a 31% increased risk of CKD progression among adults with metabolic disorders, and its inclusion significantly improved predictive performance over conventional models [3]. Moreover, a longitudinal study demonstrated that the AIP serves as an independent predictor of RKFD in Chinese adults aged ≥ 45 years with initially preserved renal function [34].

Mechanically, the kidneys serve as the primary organ for systemic insulin clearance and glucose reabsorption. Chronic hyperglycemia, dyslipidemia and IR collectively contribute to renal inflammation and fibrosis, ultimately leading to irreversible structural and functional damage [35]. IR and hyperglycemia suppress matrix metalloproteinases and dysregulate growth factors, thereby promoting mesangial expansion, arteriolar hyalinization, tubular atrophy and interstitial fibrosis [36, 37]. The pathophysiological mechanisms linking elevated AIP to RKFD remain incompletely understood, though current evidence implicates multifactorial pathways: (1) As a surrogate for atherogenic small dense LDL-C particles [14, 38], AIP promotes lipid deposition within the kidneys and facilitates foam cell formation. Subsequent ectopic lipid accumulation in renal parenchyma [39] cells such as podocytes and tubular epithelial cells trigger oxidative stress and pro-inflammatory cytokine release via oxidized LDL-C mechanisms [18, 19], establishing a vicious cycle of glomerular and tubular injury. Furthermore, the well-established role of AIP in endothelial dysfunction and systemic inflammation collectively accelerates the progression of CKD. (2) Contemporary studies demonstrate that elevated AIP values correlate strongly with IR severity and exhibit significant associations with both the progression of IR and the pathogenesis of type 2 diabetes mellitus [40–42]. AIP exacerbates β-cell dysfunction and glycemic dysregulation, while insulin resistance-induced lipoprotein lipase impairment further aggravates dyslipidemia.

Subgroup analyses indicated that metabolic status significantly influenced the associations of the TyG index and AIP with RKFD. No significant association was observed in individuals without metabolic syndrome, likely due to their relatively preserved metabolic health, in which renal impairment is less driven by insulin resistance or atherogenic dyslipidemia. The limited sample size and low event rate in this subgroup may have also reduced statistical power to detect a modest effect. In contrast, both indices showed stronger associations with RKFD in non-diabetic subgroups, highlighting the potential role of metabolic risk management in attenuating kidney injury [43]. The weaker associations observed in diabetic patients may stem from altered lipid metabolism under chronic insulin resistance, as well as confounding by intensive glucose-lowering and lipid-lowering treatments, which suppress TyG and AIP values and reduce their prognostic accuracy. Conversely, in non-diabetic individuals, both biomarkers remained highly sensitive to early dysmetabolic changes, effectively detecting subclinical risk and demonstrating strong predictive utility for renal functional decline. Together, these findings indicate that the clinical usefulness of the TyG index and AIP is context-specific, and both biomarkers are particularly valuable for early risk stratification before overt disease progression or the initiation of intensive pharmacological therapy.

Previous studies have found that elevated serum uric acid levels may damage the kidneys by influencing inflammation, oxidative stress, and mitochondrial function [44]. In the elderly population, elevated uric acid levels exacerbate the risk of a significant decline in eGFR [45]. Multiple studies have reported the coexistence of dyslipidemia and hyperuricemia [46]. The interplay between these factors is driven by complex mechanisms and shaped by a multitude of influences. This study provides the first evidence that lipids can also exert an indirect regulatory effect on renal function by mediating uric acid levels, thereby identifying uric acid as a significant mediating factor in lipid-associated nephrotoxicity.

Several limitations should be acknowledged. First, the retrospective design may introduce reporting and selection biases. Second, the absence of dynamic monitoring of the TyG index and AIP levels may lead to underestimation of long-term exposure effects. Third, the absence of data on specific nephroprotective agents (e.g., renin–angiotensin–aldosterone system inhibitors, sodium–glucose cotransporter 2 inhibitors, and mineralocorticoid receptor antagonists) and key clinical parameters (e.g., urinary albumin‑to‑creatinine ratio) in CHARLS may introduce residual confounding and limits the alignment of the operational CKM syndrome definition with established diagnostic criteria. Furthermore, renal function was assessed at only two timepoints over a 4-year follow-up period. This limited longitudinal data may not fully account for transient fluctuations, underlying kidney pathologies, or the long-term course of kidney function decline. Finally, as the analysis was limited to an Asian population, caution is warranted when generalizing these findings to other ethnic groups.

Conclusions

This study demonstrates that elevated levels of the TyG index and AIP are significantly associated with an increased risk of RKFD among patients with CKM syndrome. Integrating the TyG index or AIP with conventional risk factors improves prognostic accuracy and supports earlier identification of high-risk individuals who might benefit from intensified renal protective interventions.
