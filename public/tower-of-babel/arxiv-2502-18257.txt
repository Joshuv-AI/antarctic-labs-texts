Tensor extriangulated categories
Raphael Bennett-Tennenhaus, Isambard Goodbody, Janina C. Letz, Amit Shah

Abstract
. A tensor extriangulated category is an extriangulated category with a symmetric monoidal structure that is compatible with the extriangulated structure. To this end we define a notion of a biextriangulated functor 𝒜 × ℬ → 𝒞 {\mathcal{A}}\times{\mathcal{B}}\to{\mathcal{C}} , with compatibility conditions between the components. We have two versions of compatibility conditions, the stronger depending on the higher extensions of the extriangulated categories. We give many examples of tensor extriangulated categories. Finally, we generalise Balmer’s classification of thick tensor ideals to tensor extriangulated categories.

Primary 18M05; Secondary 18E05, 18G80, 18A23, 18G99

1.
Introduction

Monoidal categories play an important role in algebra, representation theory and various other areas. The module category of a commutative ring and of a cocommutative Hopf algebra over a field naturally possess a monoidal structure. These give rise to tensor triangulated categories and finite tensor categories, which have become their own areas of study. The symmetric monoidal structure is at the heart of tensor triangular geometry, allowing one to attach a topological space to a tensor triangulated category; see
[
Bal05
]
. Further, a monoidal category is the natural setting for the categorical notion of (co)algebra objects.

The aim of this paper is to define a compatibility condition of a monoidal structure with an extriangulated structure. Extriangulated categories were introduced by Nakaoka and Palu
[
NP19
]
, and provide a generalisation of both exact and triangulated categories. An extriangulated structure is given by a class of extriangles, generalising exact sequences and exact triangles, and extension functors, generalising
\operatorname{Ext}^{1}
of exact categories and
\operatorname{Hom}(-,\Sigma-)
of triangulated categories.

We introduce
biextriangulated
functors in
Section

3.11
building on extriangulated functors introduced in
[
BTS21
]
. Roughly speaking, a biextriangulated functor is a functor
{\mathcal{A}}\times{\mathcal{B}}\to{\mathcal{C}}
that is extriangulated in each component, and where each associated natural transformation between extension functors is compatible with the other component. For many purposes this definition is adequate, though it does not recover bitriangulated functors, which involve
\operatorname{Hom}(-,\Sigma^{2}-)
. To recover the notion of a bitriangulated functor we introduce the notion of a
strong
biextriangulated functor for which we use
higher extensions
.

For exact and triangulated categories the higher extensions are
\operatorname{Ext}^{n}(-,-)
and
\operatorname{Hom}(-,\Sigma^{n}-)
. In
Section

2
we recall the construction of higher extension functors: in the presence of enough injectives or projectives following
[
HLN22
]
; and using coends following
[
GNP21
]
. When any two of these constructions exist they are isomorphic. The higher extensions come equipped with a cup product. We show that whenever higher extensions exist, the corresponding cup product is compatible with any extriangulated functor, natural transformation or adjunction.

Finally, in
Section

4
we define a category to be
monoidal extriangulated
if it is monoidal, extriangulated, the tensor product is biextriangulated and the associator and unitors are extriangulated natural transformations. If the monoidal structure is symmetric we say the category is
tensor extriangulated
. The higher extensions of the unit, together with the cup product, are the homogeneous components of a graded ring. This ring generalises the
Ext algebra
of finite tensor categories as well as the
graded central ring
(of the unit) from tensor triangular geometry; see for example
[
EO04
]
and
[
Bal10a
]
. In
Lemma

4.5
we see this is a particularly nice ring when the monoidal structure is a strong biextriangulated category:

Lemma A
.

If
({\mathcal{A}},\mathbb{E},\mathfrak{s},\otimes,\mathbbm{1})
is a strong monoidal extriangulated category then the cup product makes
\mathbb{E}^{*}(\mathbbm{1},\mathbbm{1})=\{\mathbb{E}^{n}(\mathbbm{1},\mathbbm{1})\}_{n\geqslant 0}
into a graded-commutative graded ring.

At the end of
Section

3
and in
Section

5
we present various examples of (strong) biextriangulated functors and (strong) monoidal extriangulated categories:

•

exact category of (bi)additive functors; see
Examples

3.21
and
5.1
;

•

(stabilisation of the) category of matrix factorisations; see
Example

3.26
;

•

category of flat modules over a commutative ring; see
Example

5.2
;

•

(stabilisation of the) category of modules over a cocommutative Hopf algebra over a field; see
Example

5.7
; and

•

extriangulated structure induced by pure-exact triangles; see
Section

5.14
.

We also look into the behaviour of (strong) biextriangulated functors under stabilisation of extriangulated categories in
Sections

3.22
and
5.4
and extriangulated substructures in
Section

5.12
.

In closing we generalise Balmer’s classification
[
Bal05
]
of radical thick tensor ideals of a tensor triangulated category to tensor extriangulated categories. We use the approaches of Kock and Pitsch
[
KP17
]
and Buan, Krause and Solberg
[
BKS05
]
, and the proofs can be directly transferred to the extriangulated setting.

Theorem B
.

Let
({\mathcal{A}},\mathbb{E},\mathfrak{s},\otimes,\mathbbm{1})
be a tensor extriangulated category. We assume the class of radical thick tensor ideals
\Rad({\mathcal{A}})
is a set. Then there exists a spectral space
\operatorname{BSpc}({\mathcal{A}})
and a bijection

\Rad({\mathcal{A}})\longleftrightarrow\left\{\text{Thomason closed subsets of }\operatorname{BSpc}({\mathcal{A}})\right\}\makebox[0.0pt][l]{\,.}

This result is contained in
Theorem

6.8
. We compute the spectral space
\operatorname{BSpc}({\mathcal{A}})
for the category of projective modules over various commutative rings in
Section

6.11
and for stabilisations of extriangulated categories in
Section

6.15
.

2.
Higher extensions in extriangulated categories

The notion of extriangulated categories generalises exact categories and triangulated categories.
They were introduced by Nakaoka and Palu
[
NP19
]
.
There exist notions of higher extensions in exact categories (under some mild conditions) and in triangulated categories.
These have been unified for extriangulated categories in
[
LN19
,
HLN22
,
GNP21
]
. In this section we recall the definitions and constructions and discuss some properties.

2.1.
Extriangulated categories

An extriangulated category
{\mathcal{A}}=({\mathcal{A}},\mathbb{E},\mathfrak{s})
consists of an additive category
{\mathcal{A}}
, a functor
\mathbb{E}\colon{\mathcal{A}}^{\mathrm{op}}\times{\mathcal{A}}\to{\mathcal{A}}b
, additive in each component, to the category
{\mathcal{A}}b
of abelian groups, and a correspondence
\mathfrak{s}
associating an equivalence class
\mathfrak{s}(d)
of a 3-term complex
X\to W\to Y
to each
X,Y\in{\mathcal{A}}
and
d\in\mathbb{E}(Y,X)
, satisfying some axioms; see
[
NP19
, Definition 2.12]
.
By an
extriangle
we mean a representative of an equivalence class given by
\mathfrak{s}
, written

\begin{array}[]{cc}X\xrightarrow{f}W\xrightarrow{g}Y\mathrel{\mathchoice{\mathrel{}\mathrel{\mathop{\dabar@\dabar@}\limits^{\hbox{$\scriptstyle\,d$}}}\mathrel{\mathchar 0\symAMSa 04B}{}}{\mathrel{}\mathrel{\mathop{\dabar@\dabar@}\limits^{\hbox{$\scriptstyle\,d$}}}\mathrel{\mathchar 0\symAMSa 04B}{}}{\mathrel{}\mathrel{\mathop{\dabar@\dabar@}\limits^{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle\,d$}}}\mathrel{\mathchar 0\symAMSa 04B}{}}{\mathrel{}\mathrel{\mathop{\dabar@\dabar@}\limits^{\hbox{$\scriptstyle\,d$}}}\mathrel{\mathchar 0\symAMSa 04B}{}}}&\text{where }\mathfrak{s}(d)=[X\xrightarrow{f}W\xrightarrow{g}Y]\,.\end{array}

Let
R
be a commutative ring. An extriangulated category
({\mathcal{A}},\mathbb{E},\mathfrak{s})
is
R
-linear
, if the underlying category
{\mathcal{A}}
is
R
-linear, the functor
\mathbb{E}
factors through the forgetful functor
\operatorname{Mod}(R)\to{\mathcal{A}}b
and the induced functor
{\mathcal{A}}^{\mathrm{op}}\times{\mathcal{A}}\to\operatorname{Mod}(R)
is
R
-bilinear; see
[
INP24
, Section 3.1]
. We denote the functor
{\mathcal{A}}^{\mathrm{op}}\times{\mathcal{A}}\to\operatorname{Mod}(R)
also by
\mathbb{E}
. Every extriangulated category is trivially
\mathbb{Z}
-linear.

Like exact and triangulated categories, to be an extriangulated category is to equip a category with additional structure; it is not a property of the category. Any exact or triangulated category has a natural extriangulated structure:

Example 2.2
.

Let
{\mathcal{E}}
be a (Quillen-)exact category. This means
{\mathcal{E}}
is equipped with a class of short exact sequences. Exact categories were introduced in
[
Qui73
, Section 2]
, for more details see
[
Büh10
]
. When
{\mathcal{E}}
is an essentially small category or
{\mathcal{E}}
has enough
{\mathcal{E}}
-projectives or enough
{\mathcal{E}}
-injectives, then
{\mathcal{E}}
is an extriangulated category with
\mathbb{E}(Y,X)\coloneqq\operatorname{Ext}^{1}_{{\mathcal{E}}}(Y,X)
, the collection of Baer equivalence classes
[X\to W\to Y]
, and
\mathfrak{s}
the identity; for more details see
[
NP19
, Example 2.13]
.
The assumptions on
{\mathcal{E}}
are sufficient to ensure that
\mathbb{E}(Y,X)
is a set.

Example 2.3
.

Let
{\mathcal{T}}
be a triangulated category with suspension functor
\Sigma
. This means
{\mathcal{T}}
is equipped with a class of exact triangles. Triangulated categories were introduced by Verdier
[
Ver96
]
. Then
{\mathcal{T}}
is an extriangulated category with

\mathbb{E}(Y,X)\coloneqq\operatorname{Hom}_{{\mathcal{T}}}(Y,\Sigma X)\quad\text{and}\quad\mathfrak{s}(d)\coloneqq[X\to\Sigma^{-1}\cone(d)\to Y]

for any
d\in\mathbb{E}(Y,X)
; for more details see
[
NP19
, Proposition 3.22(1)]
.

Other examples of extriangulated categories include extension-closed full subcategories of triangulated categories
[
NP19
, Remark 2.18]
, and certain ideal quotients of exact categories
[
NP19
, Proposition 3.30]
. We discuss the latter in more detail in
Section

3.22
.

In
Examples

2.3
and
2.2
a notion of higher extensions exists. Motivated by higher extensions for exact categories, there are two constructions for higher extensions in extriangulated categories: via coends by
[
GNP21
]
when the underlying category is essentially small, and via syzygies or cosyzygies by
[
LN19
,
HLN22
]
when the extriangulated category has enough projectives or injectives, respectively. In the following sections we recall both constructions.

2.4.
Higher extensions via coends

We briefly recall the definition of a coend. Let
\mathsf{F}\colon{\mathcal{A}}^{\mathrm{op}}\times{\mathcal{A}}\to{\mathcal{B}}
be a functor where
{\mathcal{A}}
is essentially small and
{\mathcal{B}}
has set-indexed colimits. A
coend
of
\mathsf{F}
is the coequaliser of the diagram

{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\coprod\limits_{V,V^{\prime}\in\sk({\mathcal{A}})}\coprod\limits_{f\in\operatorname{Hom}_{{\mathcal{A}}}(V,V^{\prime})}\mathsf{F}(V^{\prime},V)}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\coprod\limits_{W\in\sk({\mathcal{A}})}\mathsf{F}(W,W)\makebox[0.0pt][l]{\,,}}

defined by the morphisms
\mathsf{F}(f,V)
and
\mathsf{F}(V^{\prime},f)
where
\sk({\mathcal{A}})
denotes the skeleton of
{\mathcal{A}}
, ensuring the coproducts are set-indexed.
In particular a coend is a colimit and, if it exists, is unique up to unique isomorphism. It is denoted by
\int^{W}\mathsf{F}(W,W)
, and is equipped with morphisms
\mathsf{F}(X,X)\to\int^{W}\mathsf{F}(W,W)
for every
X\in{\mathcal{A}}
.

Let
R
be a commutative ring and let
({\mathcal{A}},\mathbb{E},\mathfrak{s})
be an essentially small
R
-linear extriangulated category. The
higher extensions
are functors

(2.4.1)

\begin{gathered}\mathbb{E}^{n}\colon{\mathcal{A}}^{\mathrm{op}}\times{\mathcal{A}}\to\operatorname{Mod}(R)\quad\text{given by}\\
\mathbb{E}^{0}(Y,X)\coloneqq\operatorname{Hom}_{{\mathcal{A}}}(Y,X)\quad\text{and}\quad\mathbb{E}^{n}(Y,X)\coloneqq\int^{W}\mathbb{E}^{n-1}(W,X)\otimes_{R}\mathbb{E}(Y,W)\end{gathered}

for
n\geq 1
; that is, the object
\mathbb{E}^{n}(Y,X)
is the coend of the functor
\mathbb{E}^{n-1}(-,X)\otimes_{R}\mathbb{E}(Y,-)
. By
[
Lor21
, Proposition 2.2.1]
, we have
\mathbb{E}^{1}=\mathbb{E}
. Using the universality of the colimit, it is straightforward to check that
\mathbb{E}^{n}(Y,X)
is functorial in
X
and
Y
, and using also that tensoring over
R
is right exact and commutes with colimits, one has natural isomorphisms

(2.4.2)

\int^{W}\mathbb{E}^{i}(W,X)\otimes_{R}\mathbb{E}^{j}(Z,W)\xrightarrow{\cong}\mathbb{E}^{n}(Z,X)

for any integers
i,j\geq 0
with
i+j=n
. This immediately yields the
cup product

(2.4.3)

\smile\colon\mathbb{E}^{i}(Y,X)\otimes_{R}\mathbb{E}^{j}(Z,Y)\to\mathbb{E}^{n}(Z,X)

given by composing the morphism in (
2.4.2
) with the canonical morphism

\mathbb{E}^{i}(Y,X)\otimes_{R}\mathbb{E}^{j}(Z,Y)\to\int^{W}\mathbb{E}^{i}(W,X)\otimes_{R}\mathbb{E}^{j}(Z,W)\,.

This is motivated by
[
GNP21
, Definition 3.10]
where the cup product is introduced for
i+j=1
. We have

(2.4.4)

f\smile d=\mathbb{E}^{n}(Z,f)(d)\quad\text{and}\quad e\smile g=\mathbb{E}^{n}(g,X)(e)

for morphisms
f\colon Y\to X
and
g\colon Z\to Y
in
{\mathcal{A}}
and
d\in\mathbb{E}^{n}(Z,Y)
and
e\in\mathbb{E}^{n}(Y,X)
.

This construction of higher extension groups for exact categories goes back to
[
Yon60
, Section 4]
. For extriangulated categories this was foreshadowed in
[
NP19
, Remark 5.10]
for the case where
i=j=1
in
Eq.

2.4.3
.
It then was extended to any
i,j\geq 0
in
[
GNP21
]
.

Remark 2.5
.

The construction in
Eq.

2.4.1
can be done more generally to obtain a “folding” of
R
-bilinear functors. This defines a tensor product of
R
-bilinear functors. The tensor product already appears in
[
Yon60
, Section 4]
; for a short survey, see
[
EK20
]
. We discuss this later in
Example

3.21
.

Example 2.6
.

Let
{\mathcal{E}}
be an exact category that is essentially small. We consider
{\mathcal{E}}
as an extriangulated category, as in
Example

2.2
. Roughly speaking, the coend acts as gluing exact sequences. This means
\mathbb{E}^{n}(Y,X)
is the set of equivalence classes

[X=X_{0}\to X_{1}\to\cdots\to X_{n+1}=Y]\,,

where
X_{1},\dots,X_{n}\in{\mathcal{E}}
and there is a commutative diagram of the form

{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces X^{0}}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces X^{1}}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\cdots}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces X^{n}}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces X^{n+1}}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces W^{0}}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces W^{1}}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces W^{n-1}}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces W^{n}}

such that

W_{i}\to X_{i+1}\to W_{i+1}
is exact for each
i=0,\dots,n-1
; see
[
Yon54
, Section 3.4]
and
[
Yon60
, Section 4.3]
.
The cup product is given by

\displaystyle\smile[Y=Y_{0}\to\cdots\to Y_{m+1}=Z]

\displaystyle=[X=X_{0}\to\cdots\to X_{n}\to Y_{1}\to\cdots\to Y_{m+1}=Z]

where the morphism
X_{n}\to Y_{1}
is the composition
X_{n}\to Y\to Y_{1}
.

Example 2.7
.

Let
{\mathcal{T}}
be a triangulated category with suspension functor
\Sigma
. We consider
{\mathcal{T}}
as an extriangulated category, as in
Example

2.3
. By
[
GNP21
, Corollary 3.23]
we have

\mathbb{E}^{n}(Y,X)=\operatorname{Hom}_{{\mathcal{T}}}(Y,\Sigma^{n}X)\,,

and for
d\in\mathbb{E}^{i}(Y,X)
and
e\in\mathbb{E}^{j}(Z,Y)
the cup product is

d\smile e=(\Sigma^{j}d)\circ e\in\operatorname{Hom}_{{\mathcal{T}}}(Z,\Sigma^{i+j}X)=\mathbb{E}^{i+j}(Z,X)\,.

2.8.
Higher extensions via syzygies or cosyzygies

We recall from
[
NP19
, Section 3.4]
the definition of an extriangulated category with enough projectives. Let
({\mathcal{A}},\mathbb{E},\mathfrak{s})
be an extriangulated category. An object
P\in{\mathcal{A}}
is
\mathbb{E}
-projective
, if
\mathbb{E}(P,X)=0
for any
X\in{\mathcal{A}}
. We say
({\mathcal{A}},\mathbb{E},\mathfrak{s})
has
enough
\mathbb{E}
-projectives
if for any
X\in{\mathcal{A}}
there exists an
\mathbb{E}
-projective object
P
and an extriangle
W\to P\to X\dashrightarrow
. The object
W
is called a
syzygy
of
X
. The syzygy of an object
X
need not be unique. When
{\mathcal{A}}
has enough projectives, we fix a choice of syzygy, and denote it by
\Omega X
; cf.
[
HLN22
, Assumption 3.3]
. We set

\Omega^{0}X\coloneqq X\quad\text{and}\quad\Omega^{n}X\coloneqq\Omega(\Omega^{n-1}X)

for
n\geq 1
.

Let
R
be a commutative ring. In an
R
-linear extriangulated category
({\mathcal{A}},\mathbb{E},\mathfrak{s})
with enough projectives, the
higher extensions
are functors

(2.8.1)

\begin{gathered}\mathbb{E}^{n}_{\Omega}\colon{\mathcal{A}}^{\mathrm{op}}\times{\mathcal{A}}\to\operatorname{Mod}(R)\quad\text{given by}\\
\mathbb{E}^{0}_{\Omega}(Y,X)\coloneqq\operatorname{Hom}_{{\mathcal{A}}}(Y,X)\quad\text{and}\quad\mathbb{E}^{n}_{\Omega}(Y,X)\coloneqq\mathbb{E}(\Omega^{n-1}Y,X)\end{gathered}

for
n\geq 1
; these functors are well-defined and independent of the choice of syzygy by
[
HLN22
, Proposition 3.4]
.

Dually, in an extriangulated category
({\mathcal{A}},\mathbb{E},\mathfrak{s})
an object
I\in{\mathcal{A}}
is
\mathbb{E}
-injective
, if
\mathbb{E}(Y,I)=0
for any
Y\in{\mathcal{A}}
. We say
({\mathcal{A}},\mathbb{E},\mathfrak{s})
has
enough
\mathbb{E}
-injectives
if for any
X\in{\mathcal{A}}
there exists an
\mathbb{E}
-injective object
I
and an extriangle
X\to I\to W\dashrightarrow
. The object
W
is a
cosyzygy
of
X
. In an
R
-linear extriangulated category
({\mathcal{A}},\mathbb{E},\mathfrak{s})
with enough injectives we fix a choice of coszyzy
\Sigma X
for every
X\in{\mathcal{T}}
. The
higher extensions
are the functors

(2.8.2)

\begin{gathered}\mathbb{E}^{n}_{\Sigma}\colon{\mathcal{A}}^{\mathrm{op}}\times{\mathcal{A}}\to\operatorname{Mod}(R)\quad\text{given by}\\
\mathbb{E}^{0}_{\Sigma}(Y,X)\coloneqq\operatorname{Hom}_{{\mathcal{A}}}(Y,X)\quad\text{and}\quad\mathbb{E}^{n}_{\Sigma}(Y,X)\coloneqq\mathbb{E}(Y,\Sigma^{n-1}X)\end{gathered}

for
n\geq 1
; these functors are well-defined and independent of the choice of cosyzygy by
[
HLN22
, Proposition 3.4]
.

When the extriangulated category
({\mathcal{A}},\mathbb{E},\mathfrak{s})
has enough
\mathbb{E}
-injectives and enough
\mathbb{E}
-projectives, the definitions of higher extensions via syzygies and cosyzygies are naturally isomorphic by
[
LN19
, Lemma 5.1]
.

Example 2.9
.

Let
{\mathcal{T}}
be a triangulated category with suspension
\Sigma
. We consider
{\mathcal{T}}
as an extriangulated category as in
Example

2.3
. The zero object is the only
\mathbb{E}
-projective object, and for any object
X
there exists an exact triangle
\Sigma^{-1}X\to 0\to X\xrightarrow{\id_{X}}X
. Hence
{\mathcal{T}}
has enough
\mathbb{E}
-projectives and
\Sigma^{-1}X
is a syzygy of
X
. Dually, any triangulated category has enough injectives and the suspension
\Sigma X
is a cosyzygy of
X
. It is straightforward to see that the higher extensions of any triangulated category
{\mathcal{T}}
are given by

\mathbb{E}_{\Sigma}^{n}(Y,X)=\operatorname{Hom}_{{\mathcal{T}}}(Y,\Sigma^{n}X)\cong\operatorname{Hom}_{{\mathcal{T}}}(\Sigma^{-n}Y,X)=\mathbb{E}_{\Omega}^{n}(Y,X)\,.

Next we discuss the cup product on
\mathbb{E}_{\Omega}
. The construction for
\mathbb{E}_{\Sigma}
works analogously. Let
R
be a commutative ring and let
({\mathcal{A}},\mathbb{E},\mathfrak{s})
be an
R
-linear extriangulated category with enough
\mathbb{E}
-projectives. For an object
W\in{\mathcal{A}}
there exists an
\mathbb{E}
-projective object
P
and an extriangle
\Omega W\to P\to W\dashrightarrow
. For any object
Y
this extriangle induces a surjective map of
R
-modules

\operatorname{Hom}_{{\mathcal{A}}}(\Omega W,Y)\to\mathbb{E}(W,Y)\to 0

which is natural in
Y
; see
[
NP19
, Corollary 3.12]
. Then the cup product for
\mathbb{E}_{\Omega}
is defined as

(2.9.1)

\begin{gathered}\mathbb{E}_{\Omega}^{i}(Y,X)\otimes_{R}\mathbb{E}_{\Omega}^{j}(Z,Y)\to\mathbb{E}_{\Omega}^{i}(\Omega^{j}Z,X)\cong\mathbb{E}_{\Omega}^{i+j}(Z,X)\,,\\
d\otimes e\mapsto d\smile e\coloneqq\mathbb{E}_{\Omega}^{i}(f_{e},X)(d)\end{gathered}

where
f_{e}
is a pre-image of
e
under the surjective map above for
W=\Omega^{j-1}Z
.

By
[
HLN22
, Section 3.1]
the cup product is independent of the pre-image, and there are natural isomorphisms
\mathbb{E}_{\Omega}^{k}(\Omega^{\ell}-,-)\cong\mathbb{E}_{\Omega}^{k+\ell}(-,-)
.

2.10.
Comparison

When
({\mathcal{A}},\mathbb{E},\mathfrak{s})
is essentially small and has enough
\mathbb{E}
-injectives or
\mathbb{E}
-projectives, then the definitions of higher extensions are naturally isomorphic by
[
GNP21
, Corollary 3.21]
.
The proof in
loc.cit.
involves proving that the higher extensions and cup products are universal among bifunctors satisfying similar properties; see
[
GNP21
, Proposition 3.20]
.
We give an alternative proof, as well as a proof that the cup products are compatible with these natural isomorphisms. The main argument for both statements is contained in the following technical lemma.

Lemma 2.11
.

Let
R
be a commutative ring and
({\mathcal{A}},\mathbb{E},\mathfrak{s})
be an essentially small
R
-linear extriangulated category with enough
\mathbb{E}
-projectives. There exist
R
-module homomorphisms
\varphi_{i,j}(Y,X)\colon\mathbb{E}^{i}(\Omega^{j}Y,X)\to\mathbb{E}^{i+j}(Y,X)
for
i,j\geq 0
and objects
X,Y\in{\mathcal{A}}
, such that:

(1)

\varphi_{i,j}(Y,X)
is surjective when
i=0
and
j>0
;

(2)

\varphi_{i,j}(Y,X)
is an isomorphism when
i\geq 1
or
j=0
;

(3)

\varphi_{i,j}(Y,X)
is natural in
X
;

(4)

\varphi_{i,j}(Y,X)
is natural in
Y
in the sense that the associated diagram commutes for any lift
\Omega^{j}Y\to\Omega^{j}Y^{\prime}
of a morphism
Y\to Y^{\prime}
; and

(5)

the morphisms are compatible with the cup product

Remark 2.13
.

Let
({\mathcal{A}},\mathbb{E},\mathfrak{s})
be an extriangulated category. Assume that higher extensions exist. Then
\mathbb{E}^{*}(X,X)=\{\mathbb{E}^{n}(X,X)\}_{n\geqslant 0}
together with the cup product is a graded ring for any
X\in{\mathcal{A}}
. Moreover, for any
X,Y\in{\mathcal{A}}
the cup product induces a
\mathbb{E}^{*}(X,X)
-
\mathbb{E}^{*}(Y,Y)
-bimodule structure on
\mathbb{E}^{*}(Y,X)=\{\mathbb{E}^{n}(Y,X)\}_{n\geqslant 0}
.

Note that there is a category where the objects are the objects of
{\mathcal{A}}
, where
\mathbb{E}^{*}(X,Y)
is the set of morphisms

, and where the cup product defines composition. This is a special case of the
additive tensor category
in
[
Sim79
]
.

Remark 2.14
.

In connection with the cup product it is common to introduce a Koszul sign convention; see
[
ML95
, Theorem III.9.1]
. This means, for
d\in\mathbb{E}^{i}(Y,X)
we define morphisms

\displaystyle d_{*}

\displaystyle\colon\mathbb{E}^{j}(W,Y)\to\mathbb{E}^{i+j}(W,X)\,,

\displaystyle\quad e

\displaystyle\mapsto d\smile e\quad\text{and}

\displaystyle d^{*}

\displaystyle\colon\mathbb{E}^{j}(X,Z)\to\mathbb{E}^{i+j}(Y,Z)\,,

\displaystyle\quad c

\displaystyle\mapsto(-1)^{ij}c\smile d

that are natural in
W
and
Z
, respectively. For
i=0
one has

and

. Further, the natural transformations for

and

extend those in
[

, Definition 3.1]
. The Koszul sign convention does not appear in any of the previous works on the cup product of an extriangulated category.

For a triangulated category

the sign seems artificial, but when

is the derived category of an exact category it is standard: An element in

is considered a degree

.

Remark 3.2
.

The definition of an extriangulated natural transformation can be phrased using the cup product. Explicitly, a natural transformation
\eta\colon(\mathsf{F},\beta)\Longrightarrow(\mathsf{G},\gamma)
is extriangulated if and only if

\eta_{X}\smile\beta_{Y,X}(d)=\gamma_{Y,X}(d)\smile\eta_{Y}

for any
d\in\mathbb{E}(Y,X)
. This means the natural transformation is compatible with the connecting map from
\operatorname{Hom}
to
\mathbb{E}
.

Specialising to exact or triangulated categories, these notions recover the classical definitions:

Example 3.3
.

Let
\mathsf{F}\colon{\mathcal{E}}\to{\mathcal{F}}
be an exact functor of exact categories; this means any exact sequence is mapped to an exact sequence. The functor
\mathsf{F}
induces a map
\beta_{Y,X}\colon\operatorname{Ext}^{1}_{{\mathcal{E}}}(Y,X)\to\operatorname{Ext}^{1}_{{\mathcal{F}}}(\mathsf{F}(Y),\mathsf{F}(X))
, which is natural in
X
and
Y
since
\mathsf{F}
preserves pushout and pullback squares; for the latter see
[
Büh10
, Proposition 5.2]
. If we view
{\mathcal{E}}
and
{\mathcal{F}}
as extriangulated categories as in
Example

2.2
, then
\mathsf{F}
, together with this natural transformation
\beta
, is an extriangulated functor.

Conversely, it is clear that any extriangulated functor between exact categories is exact; see
[
BTS21
, Theorem 2.34]
.

Lastly, we note that any natural transformation of exact functors is automatically extriangulated
[
NOS22
, p. 349]
; see also
[
BTHSS23
, Example 5.4]
for details.

Example 3.4
.

Let
(\mathsf{F},\tau)\colon{\mathcal{S}}\to{\mathcal{T}}
be a triangulated functor of triangulated categories; this means
\tau\colon\mathsf{F}\Sigma\to\Sigma\mathsf{F}
is a natural transformation and, for any exact triangle

[

, Theorem 2.33]

Conversely, let

3.7.
Extriangulated adjunction

Let

(\mathsf{F},\beta)\colon({\mathcal{A}},\mathbb{E},\mathfrak{s})\to({\mathcal{B}},\mathbb{F},\mathfrak{t})\quad\text{and}\quad(\mathsf{G},\gamma)\colon({\mathcal{B}},\mathbb{F},\mathfrak{t})\to({\mathcal{A}},\mathbb{E},\mathfrak{s})

be extriangulated functors. Following
[
BTHSS23
, Definition 4.9(i)]
, we say
(\mathsf{G},\gamma)
is an
extriangulated right adjoint
of
(\mathsf{F},\beta)
and
(\mathsf{G},\gamma)
is an
extriangulated left adjoint
, if
(\mathsf{F},\mathsf{G})
is a pair of adjoint functors and the unit and counit witnessing the adjunction are extriangulated natural transformations. In this situation we say
((\mathsf{F},\beta),(\mathsf{G},\gamma))
is a
pair of extriangulated adjoint functors
.

Requiring that the unit and counit are extriangulated is superfluous for triangulated category. However, for exact categories it is necessary: For example, if
R
is a commutative ring and
X
is a flat non-projective
R
-module, then in the adjunction
-\otimes_{R}X\dashv\operatorname{Hom}_{R}(X,-)
the functor
-\otimes_{R}
is exact while
\operatorname{Hom}_{R}(X,-)
is not.

A consequence of this definition is that we obtain an adjunction relation on the higher extensions.

Lemma 3.8
.

Let
(\mathsf{F},\beta)\colon({\mathcal{A}},\mathbb{E},\mathfrak{s})\to({\mathcal{B}},\mathbb{F},\mathfrak{t})

\mathbb{E}(Y,\mathsf{G}(X))\xrightarrow{\beta_{Y,\mathsf{G}(X)}}\mathbb{F}(\mathsf{F}(Y),\mathsf{F}(\mathsf{G}(X))\xrightarrow{(\varepsilon_{X})_{*}}\mathbb{F}(\mathsf{F}(Y),X)\end{gathered}

Proof.

We show that the composition

The proof that the composition

is the identity is similar and omitted.
Consider the following diagram:

{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\mathbb{F}(\mathsf{F}(Y),X)}

3.11.

Loosely speaking, a biextriangulated functor is a bifunctor that is extriangulated functor in each component, satisfying an additional compatibility condition between the components.

3.16.
Strong biextriangulated functors

We now consider a stronger compatibility condition between the components of a biextriangulated functor. This will resolve the mismatch between biextriangulated and bitriangulated functors of triangulated categories, while not affecting the match between biextriangulated and biexact functors of exact categories. A possible disadvantage of this definition is that we need to assume that higher extensions exist, however these always exist for triangulated categories.

Definition 3.17
.

Let
\mathsf{F}=(\mathsf{F},\varphi,\psi)\colon({\mathcal{A}},\mathbb{E},\mathfrak{s})\times({\mathcal{B}},\mathbb{F},\mathfrak{t})\to({\mathcal{C}},\mathbb{G},\mathfrak{u})

in
\mathbb{G}^{2}(\mathsf{F}(X,Y),\mathsf{F}(X^{\prime},Y^{\prime}))
.

Proposition 3.20
.

Let
(\mathsf{F},\varphi,\psi)\colon{\mathcal{S}}\times{\mathcal{T}}\to{\mathcal{U}}
be a strong biextriangulated functor of triangulated categories. With

\displaystyle\vartheta_{X,Y}

\displaystyle\coloneqq\varphi^{Y}_{\Sigma X,X}(\id_{\Sigma X})\colon\mathsf{F}(\Sigma X,Y)\to\Sigma\mathsf{F}(X,Y)\quad\text{and}

\displaystyle\zeta_{X,Y}

\displaystyle\coloneqq\psi^{X}_{\Sigma Y,Y}(\id_{\Sigma Y})\colon\mathsf{F}(X,\Sigma Y)\to\Sigma\mathsf{F}(X,Y)

the functor
(\mathsf{F},\vartheta,\zeta)
is a bitriangulated functor.

Moreover, there is a one-to-one correspondence between bitriangulated functors and strong biextriangulated functors on triangulated categories.

Proof.

We assume that
(\mathsf{F},\varphi,\psi)\colon{\mathcal{S}}\times{\mathcal{T}}\to{\mathcal{U}}
is a biextriangulated functor of triangulated categories, and that
\vartheta
and
\zeta
are given as above. From
Example

3.4
we know that
\mathsf{F}
, together with
\vartheta
and
\zeta
, is a triangulated functor in each component. It remains to show
Eq.

3.15.1
. By
Example

2.7
this is equivalent to

\zeta_{X,Y}\smile\vartheta_{X,\Sigma Y}=-\vartheta_{X,Y}\smile\zeta_{\Sigma X,Y}\,.

This is just a specialisation of the definition of a strong biextriangulated functor.

For the second part, it remains to show that the biextriangulated functor that is induced by a bitriangulated functor is strong. This is straightforward to check using
Examples

2.7
and
3.4
.
∎

We end this section with examples of biextriangulated functors. For derived categories of module categories, the derived tensor product and derived Hom-functor are biextriangulated. However, for module categories with the abelian exact structure the tensor product and the Hom-functor need not be biextriangulated. This can be rectified by restricting to flat modules.

Example 3.21
.

Let
R
be a commutative ring. Let
{\mathcal{X}}
be an essentially small
R
-linear category. Let
\operatorname{Mod}({\mathcal{X}})
and
\operatorname{Mod}({\mathcal{X}}^{\mathrm{op}})
be the categories of

-linear covariant and contravariant functors, respectively, of the form
{\mathcal{X}}\to\operatorname{Mod}(R)
. For
\mathsf{F}\in\operatorname{Mod}({\mathcal{X}}^{\mathrm{op}})

\otimes_{{\mathcal{X}}}\colon\operatorname{Mod}({\mathcal{X}}^{\mathrm{op}})\times\operatorname{Mod}({\mathcal{X}})\to\operatorname{Mod}(R)\,,\quad(\mathsf{F},\mathsf{G})\mapsto\int^{W}\mathsf{F}(W)\otimes_{R}\mathsf{G}(W)\,;

see

. When

is a category with one object, the endomorphism ring is an

Let

\operatorname{Mod}({\mathcal{X}})

such that

The subcategory

(respectively,
\operatorname{Mod}({\mathcal{X}}^{\mathrm{op}})

With the abelian exact structure on

Thus this restriction is a strong biextriangulated functor by

.

The functor categories and their subcategories of flat functors are strongly connected to the category of modules over a non-unital

-algebra. For a small

-linear category

the

]
.

3.22.
Stabilisation of extriangulated categories

There are a few ways to construct extriangulated categories. One is as a stabilisation of an exact category. We will see that any biextriangulated functor induced on the stabilisation from a biexact functor is strong.

Let
({\mathcal{A}},\mathbb{E},\mathfrak{s})
be an extriangulated category. Let
{\mathcal{I}}
be a full subcategory of
{\mathcal{A}}
that is closed under finite direct sums and consists of objects that are
\mathbb{E}
-projective and
\mathbb{E}
-injective; for the definition of the latter see
Section

2.8
.
The
stabilisation of
{\mathcal{A}}
along
{\mathcal{I}}
, denoted by
{\underline{{\mathcal{A}}}_{{\mathcal{I}}}}
, has the same objects as
{\mathcal{A}}
and the morphisms are defined by

\operatorname{Hom}_{{\underline{{\mathcal{A}}}_{{\mathcal{I}}}}}(X,Y)\coloneqq\operatorname{Hom}_{{\mathcal{A}}}(X,Y)/[{\mathcal{I}}](X,Y)\,,

where
[{\mathcal{I}}](X,Y)
is the two-sided ideal of morphisms
X\to Y
in
{\mathcal{A}}
factoring through objects in
{\mathcal{I}}
.

By
[
NP19
, Proposition 3.30]
, the extriangulated structure on
{\mathcal{A}}
descends to the stabilisation
{\underline{{\mathcal{A}}}_{{\mathcal{I}}}}
. The extriangulated structure
({\underline{\mathbb{E}}},{\underline{\mathfrak{s}}})
on
{\underline{{\mathcal{A}}}_{{\mathcal{I}}}}
is as follows: For objects
X,Y\in{\underline{{\mathcal{A}}}_{{\mathcal{I}}}}
, we have

{\underline{\mathbb{E}}}(Y,X)=\mathbb{E}(Y,X)\quad\text{and}\quad{\underline{\mathfrak{s}}}(d)=[X\xrightarrow{[f]}W\xrightarrow{[g]}Y]

where
\mathfrak{s}(d)=[X\xrightarrow{f}W\xrightarrow{g}Y]
.

Lemma 3.23
.

Let
({\mathcal{A}},\mathbb{E},\mathfrak{s})
be an extriangulated category and
{\mathcal{I}}
a full subcategory closed under finite direct sums that consists of objects that are
\mathbb{E}
-projective and
\mathbb{E}
-injective. If
{\mathcal{A}}
has enough
\mathbb{E}
-projectives or
\mathbb{E}
-injectives, then so does
{\underline{{\mathcal{A}}}_{{\mathcal{I}}}}
.

Furthermore, whenever higher extensions are well-defined in
({\mathcal{A}},\mathbb{E},\mathfrak{s})
, then they are well-defined in
({\underline{{\mathcal{A}}}_{{\mathcal{I}}}},{\underline{\mathbb{E}}},{\underline{\mathfrak{s}}})
through the same construction, and

{\underline{\mathbb{E}}}^{n}(X,Y)=\mathbb{E}^{n}(X,Y)

for all
X,Y\in{\mathcal{A}}
and
n\geq 1
.

Proof.

The first claim holds, as under stabilisation
\mathbb{E}
-projective and
\mathbb{E}
-injective objects become
{\underline{\mathbb{E}}}
-projective and
{\underline{\mathbb{E}}}
-injective objects, respectively, and stabilisation preserves extriangles. The second claim is straightforward to check as the construction of the higher extensions commutes with the stabilisation.
∎

Lemma

3.24
below recovers the
n=1
case of
[
BTHSS23
, Example 5.9]
where
(\mathsf{F},\beta)
is the identity,
{\mathcal{I}}=0
and

consists of all the projective-injectives.

Lemma 3.24
.

Let
(\mathsf{F},\beta)\colon({\mathcal{A}},\mathbb{E},\mathfrak{s})\to({\mathcal{B}},\mathbb{F},\mathfrak{t})
be an extriangulated functor. Let
{\mathcal{I}}
and
{\mathcal{J}}
be full subcategories of
{\mathcal{A}}
and
{\mathcal{B}}

If
\mathsf{F}({\mathcal{I}})\subseteq{\mathcal{J}}
, then
(\mathsf{F},\beta)
induces an extriangulated functor

({\underline{\mathsf{F}}},{\underline{\beta}})\colon({\underline{{\mathcal{A}}}_{{\mathcal{I}}}},{\underline{\mathbb{E}}},{\underline{\mathfrak{s}}})\to({\underline{{\mathcal{B}}}_{{\mathcal{J}}}},{\underline{\mathbb{F}}},{\underline{\mathfrak{t}}})\makebox[0.0pt][l]{\,.}

By assumption we have
\mathsf{F}([{\mathcal{I}}](X,Y))\subseteq[{\mathcal{J}}](\mathsf{F}(X),\mathsf{F}(Y))

Proposition 3.25
.

Let
(\mathsf{F},\varphi,\psi)\colon({\mathcal{A}},\mathbb{E},\mathfrak{s})\times({\mathcal{B}},\mathbb{F},\mathfrak{t})\to({\mathcal{C}},\mathbb{G},\mathfrak{u})

Stabilisation of an extriangulated category is motivated by the stabilisation of Frobenius exact categories. An exact category
{\mathcal{E}}
is
Frobenius
, if it has enough projectives and enough injectives and the projectives and injectives coincide. In this situation, the stabilisation
{\underline{{\mathcal{E}}}}={\underline{{\mathcal{{\mathcal{E}}}}}_{{\mathcal{I}}}}
with respect to the subcategory of projective-injective objects
{\mathcal{I}}
is triangulated; see
[
Hap88
, I.2]
. As every biexact functor is strong when viewed as a biextriangulated functor, such a functor always induces a strong biextriangulated functor on any of its stabilisations by
Proposition

3.25
.

Example 3.26
.

Let
S
be a regular local ring. A
matrix factorisation of
f\in S
is a pair of maps of free modules
(\Phi\colon F\to G,\Psi\colon G\to F)
such that

\Phi\Psi=f\id_{G}\quad\text{and}\quad\Psi\Phi=f\id_{F}\,.

The free modules
F
and
G
have the same rank, and when they have rank
n
, we say
(\Phi,\Psi)
has
size
n
. Matrix factorisations were introduced in
[
Eis80
, Section 5]
; also see
[
Yos90
, Chapter 7]
. We denote by
\operatorname{mf}_{S}(f)
the category of matrix factorisations of
f
. Then
\operatorname{mf}_{S}(f)
is Frobenius and the only indecomposable projective-injective objects are
(\id_{S},f\id_{S})
and
(f\id_{S},\id_{S})
. The stabilisation
{\underline{\operatorname{mf}}}_{S}(f)
is equivalent as a triangulated category to the stablisation of the category of maximal Cohen–Macaulay modules over
S/(f)
; see
[
Eis80
, Section 6]
and
[
Yos90
, Theorem 7.4]
.

Suppose
K
is a field and that
S=K\llbracket x_{1},\ldots,x_{s}\rrbracket
and
T=K\llbracket y_{1},\ldots,y_{t}\rrbracket
are power series rings. We fix
f\in S
and
g\in T
. The tensor product of
(\Phi,\Psi)\in\operatorname{mf}_{S}(f)
and
(\Phi^{\prime},\Psi^{\prime})\in\operatorname{mf}_{T}(g)
is defined as

(\Phi,\Psi)\mathrel{\hat{\otimes}}(\Phi^{\prime},\Psi^{\prime})\coloneqq\left(\begin{pmatrix}\Phi\otimes\id&\id\otimes\Phi^{\prime}\\
-\id\otimes\Psi^{\prime}&\Psi\otimes\id\end{pmatrix},\begin{pmatrix}\Psi\otimes\id&-\id\otimes\Phi^{\prime}\\
\id\otimes\Psi^{\prime}&\Phi\otimes\id\end{pmatrix}\right)\,;

see
[
Yos98
, Definition 2.1]
. The tensor product is a matrix factorisation of
f+g
over
S\otimes_{K}T
, and this yields a functor

\mathrel{\hat{\otimes}}\colon\operatorname{mf}_{S}(f)\times\operatorname{mf}_{T}(g)\to\operatorname{mf}_{S\otimes_{K}T}(f+g)\,.

By
[
Yos98
, Lemmas 2.2, 2.8]
, this functor is biexact, and, by
[
Yos98
, Lemma 2.3]
, it induces a bitriangulated functor on the stabilisations. However, as

(\Phi,\Psi)\mathrel{\hat{\otimes}}(\id_{T},g\id_{T})\cong(\id_{S\otimes_{K}T},(f+g)\id_{S\otimes_{K}T})\oplus((f+g)\id_{S\otimes_{K}T},\id_{S\otimes_{K}T})\,,

the tensor product does not induce a functor on the intermediate stabilisations. In particular, it does not induce a tensor product on the category
{\underline{\operatorname{mf}}}_{S}(f)_{\mathcal{I}}
for
{\mathcal{I}}=\{(\id,f\id)\}
, which is equivalent to the category of maximal Cohen–Macaulay modules over
S/(f)
; see
[
Yos90
, Theorem 7.4]
.

There is another tensor product, called the multiplicative tensor product, defined in
[
Fom22
, Definition 2.6]
. While it is a biexact functor, it does not respect the projective-injective objects, and hence does not induce a functor on the stabilisation.

4.
Tensor extriangulated categories

In this section, we introduce tensor extriangulated categories, generalising the notion of a tensor triangulated category; cf.
[
HPS97
, Appendix A.2]
.

4.1.
Monoidal categories

Recall that a
monoidal category

({\mathcal{A}},\otimes,\mathbbm{1})
is a category
{\mathcal{A}}
together with a functor

\otimes\colon{\mathcal{A}}\times{\mathcal{A}}\to{\mathcal{A}},

called the
tensor product
, and a
unit
object
\mathbbm{1}\in{\mathcal{A}}
equipped with isomorphisms

\displaystyle\alpha_{X,Y,Z}\colon X\otimes(Y\otimes Z)\overset{\cong}{\longrightarrow}(X\otimes Y)\otimes Z\,,

\displaystyle\lambda_{X}\colon\mathbbm{1}\otimes X\overset{\cong}{\longrightarrow}X\quad\text{and}\quad\rho_{X}\colon X\otimes\mathbbm{1}\overset{\cong}{\longrightarrow}X

that are natural in each argument, satisfying some coherence axioms. A monoidal category is
symmetric
if, in addition, there exists an isomorphism

\sigma_{X,Y}\colon X\otimes Y\overset{\cong}{\longrightarrow}Y\otimes X\,,

also natural in each argument, satisfying some coherence axioms. For a detailed definition see
[
ML98
, XI.1]
, and
[
Kel64
]
for minimally sufficient coherent axioms.

4.2.
Tensor extriangulated categories

We now have all the ingredients to define a monoidal extriangulated category. Roughly speaking, it is an extriangulated category with a monoidal structure that respects the extriangulated structure. Explicitly, this means:

Definition 4.3
.

We call a tuple

({\mathcal{A}},\mathbb{E},\mathfrak{s},\otimes,\mathbbm{1})

a
monoidal extriangulated category
if:

(1)

({\mathcal{A}},\otimes,\mathbbm{1})
is a monoidal category;

(2)

({\mathcal{A}},\mathbb{E},\mathfrak{s})
is an extriangulated category;

(3)

\otimes=(-\otimes-,\varphi,\psi)\colon({\mathcal{A}},\mathbb{E},\mathfrak{s})\times({\mathcal{A}},\mathbb{E},\mathfrak{s})\to({\mathcal{A}},\mathbb{E},\mathfrak{s})
is a biextriangulated functor; and

(4)

the natural isomorphisms
\alpha,\lambda,\rho
from the monoidal structure are extriangulated natural transformations in each variable.

We say a monoidal extriangulated category
({\mathcal{A}},\mathbb{E},\mathfrak{s},\otimes,\mathbbm{1})
is
strong
, if the biextriangulated functor
(-\otimes-,\varphi,\psi)
is strong (see
Definition

3.17
).

A
tensor extriangulated category
is a monoidal extriangulated category where the monoidal structure is symmetric and the corresponding natural transformation
\sigma
is extriangulated in each variable.

Condition
Item

4
has various consequences. For example, it yields

(4.3.1)

\varphi^{\mathbbm{1}}_{X,Y}=(\rho^{-1}_{Y})_{*}\circ(\rho_{X})^{*}\quad\text{and}\quad\psi^{\mathbbm{1}}_{X,Y}=(\lambda^{-1}_{Y})_{*}\circ(\lambda_{X})^{*}\,.

In particular,
\varphi^{\mathbbm{1}}
and
\psi^{\mathbbm{1}}
are natural isomorphisms. Further, in a tensor extriangulated category, the natural transformations
\varphi
and
\psi
from the tensor product determine each other; explicitly

(\sigma_{X^{\prime},Y})_{*}\circ\varphi^{Y}_{X,X^{\prime}}=(\sigma_{X,Y})^{*}\circ\psi^{Y}_{X,X^{\prime}}

as
\sigma
is an extriangulated natural isomorphism in each variable.

4.4.
Ring of higher extensions

Recall from
Remark

2.13
that
\mathbb{E}^{*}(X,X)
with the cup product is a graded ring. Similarly as for triangulated categories, a tensor structure makes the graded endomorphism ring graded-commutative.

Lemma 4.5
.

Let
({\mathcal{A}},\mathbb{E},\mathfrak{s},\otimes,\mathbbm{1})
be a strong monoidal extriangulated category. We assume that higher extensions are well-defined in
({\mathcal{A}},\mathbb{E},\mathfrak{s})
. Then
\mathbb{E}^{*}(\mathbbm{1},\mathbbm{1})
with the cup product is a graded-commutative graded ring.

Proof.

Let
d\in\mathbb{E}^{i}(\mathbbm{1},\mathbbm{1})
and
e\in\mathbb{E}^{j}(\mathbbm{1},\mathbbm{1})
. Then

\displaystyle d\smile e

\displaystyle=(\varphi^{\mathbbm{1}})^{i}_{\mathbbm{1},\mathbbm{1}}(d)\smile(\psi^{\mathbbm{1}})^{j}_{\mathbbm{1},\mathbbm{1}}(e)

\displaystyle=(-1)^{ij}(\psi^{\mathbbm{1}})^{j}_{\mathbbm{1},\mathbbm{1}}(e)\smile(\varphi^{\mathbbm{1}})^{i}_{\mathbbm{1},\mathbbm{1}}(d)

\displaystyle=(-1)^{ij}e\smile d\makebox[0.0pt][l]{\,.}

For the first and third equalities we implicitly use a higher version of
Eq.

4.3.1
.
The second equality follows from
Lemma

3.18
.
∎

Example 4.6
.

In
Example

2.3
we explained that any triangulated category is naturally an extriangulated category. By
Proposition

a strong biextriangulated functor on a triangulated category is a bitriangulated functor. Hence the definition of a strong tensor extriangulated category recovers the notion of a tensor triangulated category in the sense of

\alpha

The extension ring
\mathbb{E}^{*}(\mathbbm{1},\mathbbm{1})

A symmetric monoidal category

5.
Examples of tensor extriangulated categories

By construction the notion of a tensor extriangulated category generalises that of a tensor triangulated category. In comparison, there seem to be multiple different notions of a tensor exact category. Often the monoidal structure on an exact or abelian category is left exact, but not necessarily exact. In
Example

3.21
we defined a biexact functor by restricting the tensor product to flat functors.
In
Example

5.1
we obtain a monoidal structure, which is not symmetric, by extending the tensor product to bimodules.

Example 5.1
.

Let
R
be a commutative ring and
{\mathcal{X}}
a small
R
-linear category. Let
\operatorname{Bimod}({\mathcal{X}},{\mathcal{X}})
be the category of
R
-bilinear functors
{\mathcal{X}}^{\mathrm{op}}\times{\mathcal{X}}\to\operatorname{Mod}(R)
. We extend the functor
\otimes_{\mathcal{X}}
of
Example

3.21
to a functor

\begin{gathered}\otimes_{\mathcal{X}}\colon\operatorname{Bimod}({\mathcal{X}},{\mathcal{X}})\times\operatorname{Bimod}({\mathcal{X}},{\mathcal{X}})\to\operatorname{Bimod}({\mathcal{X}},{\mathcal{X}})\,,\\
(\mathsf{F},\mathsf{G})\mapsto\left((X,Y)\mapsto\int^{W}\mathsf{F}(W,Y)\otimes_{R}\mathsf{G}(X,W)\right)\,.\end{gathered}

It is straightforward to check that
\operatorname{Bimod}({\mathcal{X}},{\mathcal{X}})
equipped with
\otimes_{{\mathcal{X}}}
is a monoidal category and
\operatorname{Hom}_{{\mathcal{X}}}(-,-)
serves as the monoidal unit; see
[
EK20
, Proposition 1]
. Now consider the subcategory
\operatorname{Flat}({\mathcal{X}},{\mathcal{X}})
of
\operatorname{Bimod}({\mathcal{X}},{\mathcal{X}})
consisting of
R
-bilinear functors
\mathsf{F}
such that
-\otimes_{{\mathcal{X}}}\mathsf{F}
and
\mathsf{F}\otimes_{\mathcal{X}}-
are exact. Then
\operatorname{Flat}({\mathcal{X}},{\mathcal{X}})
equipped with
\otimes_{\mathcal{X}}
and
\operatorname{Hom}_{{\mathcal{X}}}(-,-)
is a monoidal extriangulated category.

When
A
is the functor ring of
{\mathcal{X}}
(see
Example

3.21
), then there is an equivalence of monoidal categories
\operatorname{Bimod}({\mathcal{X}},{\mathcal{X}})\to\operatorname{Mod}(A^{\mathrm{op}}\otimes_{R}A)
under which
\otimes_{{\mathcal{X}}}
corresponds to
\otimes_{A}
; see
[
EK20
, Proposition 2]
.

The monoidal structure in the previous
Example
is generally not symmetric. One obtains a symmetric monoidal structure for commutative rings:

Example 5.2
.

Let
R
be a commutative ring
with local units
; this means that for any
r_{1},\dots,r_{n}\in R
, there exists an idempotent
e\in R
such that
er_{i}=r_{i}=r_{i}e
for each
i
. The standard tensor product
\otimes_{R}
is a symmetric monoidal structure on the category of unital
R
-modules
\operatorname{Mod}(R)
; an
R
-module
M
is
unital
provided
M=RM
. However, the tensor product is generally only left exact. An
R
-module
F
is
flat
, if
F\otimes_{R}-
is exact. The subcategory of flat
R
-modules, denoted
\operatorname{Flat}(R)
, is an extension-closed subcategory of
\operatorname{Mod}(R)
, and hence inherits an exact structure. Hence
(\operatorname{Flat}(R),\otimes_{R},R,\operatorname{Ext}_{R}^{1})
is a tensor extriangulated category. The ring of extensions of the unit
R
is
\mathbb{E}^{*}(R,R)=\operatorname{Ext}^{*}_{R}(R,R)=R
.

A big class of extriangulated categories, that are neither triangulated or exact, are the extension-closed subcategories of triangulated categories; see
[
NP19
, Remark 2.18]
. In general, extension-closed subcategories of a tensor triangulated category need not inherit the monoidal structure. However, in some situations we obtain a tensor extriangulated category.

Example 5.3
.

Let
R
be a commutative ring. The derived category
\operatorname{D}(\operatorname{Mod}(R))
of
R
-modules is a tensor triangulated category with tensor product
\otimes^{\mathbf{L}}_{R}
and unit
R
. The standard t-structure
({\mathcal{U}},{\mathcal{V}})
on
\operatorname{D}(\operatorname{Mod}(R))
is given by

\begin{gathered}{\mathcal{U}}\coloneqq\set{X\in\operatorname{D}(\operatorname{Mod}(R))}{\operatorname{H}_{<0}(X)=0}\quad\text{and}\\
{\mathcal{V}}\coloneqq\set{X\in\operatorname{D}(\operatorname{Mod}(R))}{\operatorname{H}_{>0}(X)=0}\,,\end{gathered}

the subcategories of complexes with homology concentrated in non-negative and non-positive degrees, respectively. As
{\mathcal{U}}
and
{\mathcal{V}}
are extension-closed subcategories of
\operatorname{D}(\operatorname{Mod}(R))
, they inherit an extriangulated structure. Further, the aisle
{\mathcal{U}}
is closed under the tensor product; that is
{\mathcal{U}}\otimes^{\mathbf{L}}_{R}{\mathcal{U}}\subseteq{\mathcal{U}}
and
R\in{\mathcal{U}}
. Hence
({\mathcal{U}},\otimes^{\mathbf{L}}_{R},R)
is a tensor extriangulated category.

One gets further examples by taking the intersection of
{\mathcal{U}}
with the full subcategory of right-bounded complexes. When
R
is noetherian, then the intersection of
{\mathcal{U}}
with the right-bounded derived category of finitely generated modules yields a tensor extriangulated category as well.

5.4.
Stabilisation of extriangulated categories

In
Section

3.22
we gave conditions when a (strong) biextriangulated bifunctor induces a (strong) biextriangulated bifunctor on the stabilisation with respect to a subcategory of projective-injective objects. We now focus on monoidal extriangulated categories. We say an extension-closed subcategory
{\mathcal{I}}
of a monoidal extriangulated category
({\mathcal{A}},\otimes,\mathbbm{1})
is a
two-sided tensor ideal
, if
{\mathcal{A}}\otimes{\mathcal{I}}\subseteq{\mathcal{I}}
and
{\mathcal{I}}\otimes{\mathcal{A}}\subseteq{\mathcal{I}}
. Then the following result is a direct consequence of
Proposition

3.25
:

Lemma 5.5
.

Let
({\mathcal{A}},\otimes,\mathbbm{1})
be a monoidal extriangulated category. If
{\mathcal{I}}
is a two-sided tensor ideal consisting of objects that are
\mathbb{E}
-projective and
\mathbb{E}
-injective, then
\underline{{\mathcal{A}}}_{\mathcal{I}}
inherits the monoidal structure form
{\mathcal{A}}
. In particular,
(\underline{{\mathcal{A}}}_{\mathcal{I}},\underline{\otimes},\mathbbm{1})
is a monoidal extriangulated category. When
({\mathcal{A}},\otimes,\mathbbm{1})
is tensor extriangulated then so is
(\underline{{\mathcal{A}}}_{\mathcal{I}},\underline{\otimes},\mathbbm{1})
. When
({\mathcal{A}},\otimes,\mathbbm{1})
is strong, then so is
(\underline{{\mathcal{A}}}_{\mathcal{I}},\underline{\otimes},\mathbbm{1})
. ∎

Lemma 5.6
.

Let
({\mathcal{A}},\mathbb{E},\mathfrak{s},\otimes,\mathbbm{1})
be a closed tensor extriangulated category.
Then the
\mathbb{E}
-projectives form a tensor ideal.

Proof.

Let
X,Y\in{\mathcal{A}}
.
We are assuming
-\otimes X
has an extriangulated right adjoint
\operatorname{hom}(X,-)
.
By
Lemma

3.8
this means
\mathbb{E}(Y\otimes X,-)\cong\mathbb{E}(Y,\operatorname{hom}(X,-))
.
To say that an object
Z
is
\mathbb{E}
-projective is equivalent to saying that
\mathbb{E}(Z,-)=0
.
Altogether we have shown that if
Y
is
\mathbb{E}
-projective then so is
Y\otimes X
.
Since we are working in a tensor extriangulated category, the tensor product is symmetric, and so we have shown that the
\mathbb{E}
-projectives form a tensor ideal.
∎

Example 5.7
.

Let
H
be a cocommutative Hopf algebra over a field
k
. For
H
-modules
M
and
N
the comultiplication induces an
H
-module structure on
M\otimes_{k}N
. By
[
Mar83
, Chapter 12, Proposition 3]
, the tensor product is an exact, symmetric monoidal structure on
\operatorname{Mod}(H)
. Hence
(\operatorname{Mod}(H),\otimes_{k},k,\operatorname{Ext}_{H}^{1})
is a tensor extriangulated category.

Further, a cocommutative Hopf algebra
H
over a field
k
is a Frobenius algebra by
[
LS69
]
. Hence
\operatorname{Mod}(H)
, as well as the full subcategory of finite-dimensional
H
-modules
\operatorname{mod}(H)
, is Frobenius exact. It is well-known that
H\otimes_{k}M
is free for every
H
-module
M
; see for example
[
Mar83
, Chapter 12, Proposition 4]
. Hence, every non-trivial tensor ideal of
\operatorname{Mod}(H)
contains all projective objects. In particular, there are exactly two tensor ideals of
\operatorname{Mod}(H)
consisting of projective-injective objects, namely the zero subcategory and the full subcategory of projective-injective objects. Hence, the strong tensor extriangulated category
\operatorname{Mod}(H)
induces one other strong tensor extriangulated category via stabilisation, namely the tensor triangulated category
{\underline{\operatorname{Mod}}}(H)
.

A special case of a finite-dimensional cocommutative Hopf algebra is a group algebra
kG
for a finite group
G
.

5.8.
Dualisable objects

Example

5.7
is an instance of an abelian
rigid
monoidal category, that is an abelian monoidal category in which all objects are dualisable; see
[
Ulb90
, p. 254, Examples]
. We show that the existence of dualisable objects makes the category Frobenius.

Let
({\mathcal{A}},\otimes,\mathbbm{1})
be a closed monoidal category. The
dualisation functor
is given by

(-)^{\vee}\coloneqq\operatorname{hom}(-,\mathbbm{1})\colon{\mathcal{A}}^{\mathrm{op}}\to{\mathcal{A}}\,.

An object
X
in
{\mathcal{A}}
is called
strongly dualisable
, if the natural transformation

X^{\vee}\otimes Y\to\operatorname{hom}(X,Y)

is an isomorphism; see
[
LMSM86
, Chapter III, §1]
. When the monoidal product is symmetric, then there exists a natural transformation
X\to(X^{\vee})^{\vee}
. This is an isomorphism if
X
is strongly dualisable by
[
LMSM86
, Proposition 1.3]
.
Note that if
({\mathcal{A}},\mathbb{E},\mathfrak{s},\otimes,\mathbbm{1})
is closed tensor extriangulated and if
X
is strongly dualisable then the natural isomorphisms
X^{\vee}\otimes-\to\operatorname{hom}(X,-)
and
X\otimes-\to(X^{\vee})^{\vee}\otimes-
are compositions of extriangulated natural transformations, and hence extriangulated.

Proposition 5.9
.

Let
({\mathcal{A}},\mathbb{E},\mathfrak{s},\otimes,\mathbbm{1})
be a closed tensor extriangulated category.
If each object is strongly dualisable, then
\mathbb{E}
-projectives are the same as
\mathbb{E}
-injectives.

Proof.

Let
X,Y\in{\mathcal{A}}
.
Since
X
is strongly dualisable and
{\mathcal{A}}
is symmetric we have

\mathbb{E}(Y,X)\cong\mathbb{E}(Y,\operatorname{hom}(X^{\vee},\mathbbm{1}))\cong\mathbb{E}(Y\otimes X^{\vee},\mathbbm{1})\cong\mathbb{E}(X^{\vee}\otimes Y,\mathbbm{1})\cong\mathbb{E}(X^{\vee},Y^{\vee})\,;

the second and fourth isomorphisms hold by
Lemma

3.8
.
Hence
X
is
\mathbb{E}
-injective if and only if
X^{\vee}
is
\mathbb{E}
-projective, and
Y
is
\mathbb{E}
-projective if and only if
Y^{\vee}
is
\mathbb{E}
-injective.

Let
U\to V\to W\dashrightarrow
be an extriangle in
{\mathcal{A}}
. Since
X
is strongly dualisable, the functor
\operatorname{hom}(X^{\vee},-)\cong(X^{\vee})^{\vee}\otimes-\cong X\otimes-
is an extriangulated right adjoint to
-\otimes X^{\vee}
. Combining the adjunction with the unitor we obtain a diagram

{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\operatorname{Hom}_{{\mathcal{A}}}(X^{\vee},U)}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\operatorname{Hom}_{{\mathcal{A}}}(X^{\vee},V)}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\operatorname{Hom}_{{\mathcal{A}}}(X^{\vee},W)}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\mathbb{E}(X^{\vee},U)}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\operatorname{Hom}_{{\mathcal{A}}}(\mathbbm{1},X\otimes U)}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\operatorname{Hom}_{{\mathcal{A}}}(\mathbbm{1},X\otimes V)}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\operatorname{Hom}_{{\mathcal{A}}}(\mathbbm{1},X\otimes W)}

which commutes and in which every row is exact and every vertical map is an isomorphism; see

.

Suppose

-projective.
By

we have that

Thus

\mathbb{E}

\mathbb{E}
-injective.

If instead

\mathbb{E}

is
\mathbb{E}
-projective by the first argument above, and so
X^{\vee}
is
\mathbb{E}
-injective by the second, and so
(X^{\vee})^{\vee}
is
\mathbb{E}
-projective by the first, and

since

is strongly dualisable.
∎

Corollary 5.10
.

Let
({\mathcal{A}},\mathbb{E},\mathfrak{s},\otimes,\mathbbm{1})
be a closed tensor extriangulated category in which each object is strongly dualisable. If
\mathbbm{1}
has a
\mathbb{E}
-projective cover or a
\mathbb{E}
-injective envelope, then
{\mathcal{A}}
is Frobenius.

Proof.

Thanks to
Proposition

5.9
it is enough to check that
{\mathcal{A}}
has enough
\mathbb{E}
-projectives and
\mathbb{E}
-injectives. If

is a
\mathbb{E}
-projective cover of

then

-projective cover of

{\mathcal{A}}
has enough
\mathbb{E}
-projectives. Further, the dual

of the
\mathbb{E}
-projective cover

is a
\mathbb{E}
-injective cover. Hence by a similar argument as above

has enough
\mathbb{E}
-injectives.
∎

Remark 5.11
.

In a closed symmetric monoidal category the dual
X^{\vee}
of a strongly dualisable object
X
is a right and left dual of
X
. In this setting, assuming that all objects are strongly dualisable is equivalent to assuming the category is rigid. However, for an extriangulated tensor category that is rigid, it is not clear whether the functor given by the left or right duals is an extriangulated functor. In the case of abelian categories this can be resolved using the universal properties of kernels and cokernels; see
[
EGNO15
, Proposition 4.2.9]
.

5.14.
Pure-exact extriangulated structure

In the next example we discuss a special case of extriangulated subfunctors for compactly generated triangulated categories. The extriangles arising are precisely the pure-exact triangles.

We recall some basic definitions concerning triangulated categories. Let
{\mathcal{T}}
be a triangulated category with suspension
\Sigma
. An object
X
is
compact
if the functor
\operatorname{Hom}_{{\mathcal{T}}}(X,-)\colon{\mathcal{T}}\to{\mathcal{A}}b
preserves coproducts, and we write
{\mathcal{T}}^{\mathrm{c}}
for the full subcategory of compact objects. The triangulated category
{\mathcal{T}}
is
compactly generated
, if it has small coproducts,
{\mathcal{T}}^{\mathrm{c}}
is essentially small and for any non-zero object
Y
in
{\mathcal{T}}
there exists an object
X\in{\mathcal{T}}^{\mathrm{c}}
such that
\operatorname{Hom}_{{\mathcal{T}}}(X,Y)\neq 0
.

Proposition 5.15
.

Let
({\mathcal{T}},\otimes,\mathbbm{1})
be a compactly generated closed tensor triangulated category, considered as a tensor extriangulated category
(\mathcal{T},\mathbb{E},\mathfrak{s},\otimes,\mathbbm{1})
. In the notation of
Section

5.12
, we have that
(\mathcal{T},\mathbb{E}_{{\mathcal{T}}^{\mathrm{c}}},\mathfrak{s}_{{\mathcal{T}}^{\mathrm{c}}},\otimes,\mathbbm{1})
is a tensor extriangulated category.

We first recall the definition and some basic properties of phantom maps from
[
Kra00
]
as they are closely connected to the extriangulated substructure
\mathbb{E}_{{\mathcal{T}}^{\mathrm{c}}}
.

Let
\operatorname{Mod}(({\mathcal{T}}^{\mathrm{c}})^{\mathrm{op}})
be the category of additive functors
({\mathcal{T}}^{\mathrm{c}})^{\mathrm{op}}\to{\mathcal{A}}b
. This is an abelian AB5 category. Let
\mathbf{Y}\colon{\mathcal{T}}\to\operatorname{Mod}(({\mathcal{T}}^{\mathrm{c}})^{\mathrm{op}})
be the
restricted Yoneda functor
, defined on an object
X
by the restriction
of
\operatorname{Hom}_{{\mathcal{T}}}(-,X)
to
{\mathcal{T}}^{\mathrm{c}}
and likewise on morphisms. An additive functor
\mathsf{F}\colon{\mathcal{T}}\to\operatorname{Mod}(({\mathcal{T}}^{\mathrm{c}})^{\mathrm{op}})
is
cohomological
if any exact triangle

(5.15.1)

{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces X}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces Y}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces Z}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\Sigma X}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces f}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces g}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces h}

induces an exact sequence

in

. In particular, the restricted Yoneda functor
\mathbf{Y}
is cohomological.

A morphism

is called

Lemma 5.16
.

Let
({\mathcal{T}},\otimes,\mathbbm{1})
be a closed tensor triangulated category. Let

{\mathcal{T}}
. If

As phantom morphisms are closed under composition with any other morphism, it is enough to show

is phantom when

is phantom. We give a proof for the former, the latter holds by symmetry.

The restricted Yoneda functor
\mathbf{Y}
preserves coproducts. Since

is a left adjoint for any object

, it also preserves coproducts. It follows that the composition
\mathsf{F}\coloneqq\mathbf{Y}\circ(-\otimes X)
is a cohomological functor
{\mathcal{T}}\to\operatorname{Mod}(({\mathcal{T}}^{\mathrm{c}})^{\mathrm{op}})
that preserves coproducts.

Let

be a phantom morphism. By
[

, Corollary 2.5]
we have that
\mathbf{Y}(f\otimes\id_{X})=\mathsf{F}(f)=0
as
\mathsf{F}
is cohomological and preserves coproducts.
∎

We can now give an explicit description of the extriangulated substructure
\mathbb{E}_{{\mathcal{T}}^{\mathrm{c}}}
from

. If

, then

for every

with in
{\mathcal{T}}
. Hence

if and only

for every map

with

. That means
\mathbb{E}_{{\mathcal{T}}^{\mathrm{c}}}(Y,X)
is the subset of phantom maps in
\operatorname{Hom}_{{\mathcal{T}}}(Y,\Sigma X)
, and

is an extriangle if and only if

is a pure-exact triangle.

Example 5.17
.

Let

The derived category

We claim that the extriangulated structure on

Finally we prove of

6.
Classification of tensor ideals

The goal of this
Section
is the classification of the radical thick tensor ideals in an essentially small tensor extriangulated category.
In
[
BKS05
, Sections 7 and 8]
lattices of thick tensor ideals were considered for both abelian and triangulated categories simultaneously.
The arguments for the classification of thick tensor ideals in a triangulated category of
[
Bal05
]
and
[
KP17
]
generalise straight away to the extriangulated case. For convenience we give the details of some important parts.

6.1.
Tensor ideals

Let
({\mathcal{A}},\otimes,\mathbbm{1})
be a symmetric monoidal category. A non-empty strictly full subcategory
{\mathcal{I}}
of
{\mathcal{A}}
is a
tensor ideal
, if for each
X\in{\mathcal{A}}
and
Y\in{\mathcal{I}}
, we have
X\otimes Y\in{\mathcal{I}}
.

For an object
X\in{\mathcal{A}}
and a positive integer
n
, we set
X^{\otimes n}\coloneqq X\otimes\dots\otimes X
, the
n
-fold tensor product of
X
. For convenience we also set
X^{\otimes 0}\coloneqq\mathbbm{1}
.

We say a tensor ideal
{\mathcal{I}}
is:

(1)

radical
if,
for all
X\in{\mathcal{A}}
, we have that
X^{\otimes n}\in{\mathcal{I}}
for some
n\geq 1
implies
X\in{\mathcal{I}}
; and

(2)

prime
if, for all
X,Y\in{\mathcal{A}}
, we have that
X\otimes Y\in{\mathcal{I}}
implies
X\in{\mathcal{I}}
or
Y\in{\mathcal{I}}
.

Note that any prime tensor ideal is radical.

Let
({\mathcal{A}},\mathbb{E},\mathfrak{s})
be an extriangulated category. We say a non-empty full subcategory
{\mathcal{I}}
is
thick
, if it is closed under direct summands and has the
2-out-of-3 property
; that is, for any extriangle
X\to Y\to Z\dashrightarrow
, if any two of
X
,
Y
, or
Z
lie in
{\mathcal{I}}
, then so does the third.

Let
{\mathcal{C}}
be a collection of objects of
{\mathcal{A}}
. We denote by
\thick^{\otimes}({\mathcal{C}})
the smallest thick tensor ideal containing
{\mathcal{C}}
, and by
\rad({\mathcal{C}})
the smallest radical thick tensor ideal containing
{\mathcal{C}}
. These categories exist as the intersection of (radical) thick tensor ideals is again a (radical) thick tensor ideal. As taking the thick tensor closure is a finite process, the radical closure can be described as

(6.1.1)

\rad({\mathcal{C}})=\set{X\in{\mathcal{A}}}{X^{\otimes n}\in\thick^{\otimes}({\mathcal{C}})\text{ for some }n\geq 1}\,.

For a tensor extriangulated category
(\mathcal{A},\otimes,\mathbbm{1})
we denote by
\Rad({\mathcal{A}})
the class of all radical thick tensor ideals of
{\mathcal{A}}
. When
{\mathcal{A}}
is essentially small, then
\Rad({\mathcal{A}})
is a set. In
Proposition

6.5
we show that, if
\Rad({\mathcal{A}})
is a set, it has a rich poset structure.

6.2.
Lattices and frames

For completeness we recall the terminology and fix notation; we loosely follow
[
Joh82
]
. Recall, in a poset
L
the
join
(respectively,
meet
) of a subset
S\subseteq L
is the smallest (respectively, largest) element that contains (respectively, is contained in) every element of
S
. We denote the join of
S
by
\bigvee S
and the meet of
S
by
\bigwedge S
. For elements
a,b\in L
we write
a\vee b=\bigvee\{a,b\}
and
a\wedge b=\bigwedge\{a,b\}
.

A
lattice
is a poset
L
in which every finite, non-empty subset has a join and a meet. A lattice
L
is
distributive
if
L
has a
greatest element

1=\bigwedge\emptyset
, a
least element

0=\bigvee\emptyset
and it satisfies the
distributive law
; that is

a\wedge(b\vee c)=(a\wedge b)\vee(a\wedge c)\,,\quad\text{or equivalently}\quad a\vee(b\wedge c)=(a\vee b)\wedge(a\vee c)

for any
a,b,c\in L
. A
morphism of distributive lattices
is a map that respects the order, finite meets, finite joins, and the greatest and least element.

A lattice
F
is a
frame
if the join of any subset exist and it satisfies the
infinite join-distributivity law
; that is

a\wedge\bigvee S=\bigvee\set{a\wedge s}{s\in S}

for any
a\in F
and any subset
S\subseteq F
. A
morphism of frames
is a morphism of distributive lattices that preserves arbitrary joins.

6.3.
Stone and Hochster duality

The prototypical example of a frame is the set of all open subsets of a topological space. In fact, there is a functor

\Omega\colon\mathcal{T}\!\mathit{op}\to\mathcal{F}\mathit{rm}^{\mathrm{op}}

from the category of topological spaces with continuous maps to the category of frames. This functor has a right adjoint, given by the prime spectrum of a frame
(F,\leq)
: An element
a\in F
is
(meet-)prime
, if
b\wedge c\leq a
implies that either
a\leq b
or
a\leq c
. The
prime spectrum of
F
is the set of all prime elements in
F
endowed with a topology given by the closed sets
V(a)=\set{p}{a\leq p}
. We denote the right adjoint by
\pt
, due to the fact that prime elements in a frame correspond to points; for more details see
[
Joh82
, II.1.3]
.

The adjoint functors
(\Omega,\pt)
restrict to an equivalence between the category of sober spaces and the category of spatial frames. This is called
Stone duality
; for more details see
[
Joh82
, II.1.7]
. This equivalence restricts further to an equivalence of the category of spectral spaces and the category of coherent frames. We recall the latter two notions.

An element
a
in a frame
F
is
compact
if for each subset
S
of
F
with
a\leq\bigvee S
there is a finite subset
T\subseteq S
such that
a\leq\bigvee T
. A frame
F
is
coherent
if the greatest element
1
is compact,
a\wedge b
is compact for any compact elements
a,b\in F
, and for each
a\in F
there exists a subset
S
of compact elements satisfying
a=\bigvee S
. A
morphism of coherent frames
is a morphism of frames that preserves compact elements.

In a coherent frame
F
the set of compact elements is a distributive sublattice of
F
, and the frame
F
can be recovered from the distributive lattice of compact elements. In fact, there is an equivalence
\mathcal{C}\mathit{oh}\to\mathcal{DL}\mathit{at}
from the category of coherent frames to the category of distributive lattices; see
[
Joh82
, II.3.2]
.

On the other hand, a topological space
X
is
spectral
, if

(1)

X
is quasi-compact;

(2)

the quasi-compact open subsets of
X
are closed under finite intersections and form a basis of open sets of the topology on
X
; and

(3)

X
is
sober
; that is every non-empty irreducible closed subset has a generic point;

see
[
Hoc69
]
. A
morphism of spectral spaces
is a continuous map such that the preimage of every quasi-compact open set is again quasi-compact. We denote the category of spectral spaces by
\mathcal{S}\mathit{p}
. The spectral spaces are precisely those topological spaces that occur as the Zariski spectrum of a commutative ring; see
[
Hoc69
]
.

Putting everything together, we obtain a sequence of equivalences:

{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\mathcal{S}\mathit{p}}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\mathcal{C}\mathit{oh}^{\mathrm{op}}}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\mathcal{DL}\mathit{at}^{\mathrm{op}}}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\mathcal{DL}\mathit{at}^{\mathrm{op}}}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\mathcal{C}\mathit{oh}^{\mathrm{op}}}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\mathcal{S}\mathit{p}\makebox[0.0pt][l]{\,.}}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\Omega}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\pt}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces(-)^{\mathrm{c}}}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces(-)^{\mathrm{op}}}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces(-)^{\mathrm{c}}}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\pt}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\Omega}

Here, the functor
(-)^{\mathrm{op}}
sends a lattice to its opposite lattice, which has the same underlying set and the reversed order. Note, that this is not a contravariant functor. The composition of these equivalences yields a functor
\mathcal{S}\mathit{p}\to\mathcal{S}\mathit{p}
, called
Hochster duality
. This equivalence is usually denoted by
(-)^{\vee}
, and
(X^{\vee})^{\vee}=X
for a spectral space
X
.

6.4.
Lattice of tensor ideals

Let
({\mathcal{A}},\otimes,\mathbbm{1})
be a tensor extriangulated category. The meet of two radical thick tensor ideals is given as their intersection. The join of a set of radical thick tensor ideal
S
is

\bigvee S=\rad(\bigcup_{{\mathcal{I}}\in S}{\mathcal{I}})\,.

The least element is the nilradical ideal
\rad(\{0\})
and the greatest element is the whole category
{\mathcal{A}}
. Using the same arguments as for the triangulated case in
[
KP17
, Theorem 3.1.9]
, we show the following:

Proposition 6.5
.

Suppose
(\mathcal{A},\otimes,\mathbbm{1})
is a tensor extriangulated category. If
\Rad({\mathcal{A}})
is a set, then it is a coherent frame.

Proof.

We first show the infinite join-distributive law. Let
{\mathcal{I}}\in\Rad({\mathcal{A}})
and
S\subseteq\Rad({\mathcal{A}})
. We show
{\mathcal{I}}\wedge\bigvee S\leq\bigvee_{{\mathcal{J}}\in S}({\mathcal{I}}\wedge{\mathcal{J}})
, as the opposite inclusion always hold. For
X\in{\mathcal{I}}\wedge\bigvee S
we define

{\mathcal{J}}_{X}\coloneqq\set{Y\in\bigvee S}{X\otimes Y\in\bigvee_{{\mathcal{J}}\in S}({\mathcal{I}}\wedge{\mathcal{J}})}\,.

It is straightforward to check that
{\mathcal{J}}_{X}
is a radical thick tensor ideal. As
{\mathcal{J}}_{X}\geq{\mathcal{J}}
for all
{\mathcal{J}}\in S
we get

{\mathcal{J}}_{X}\geq\bigvee S\ni X\,.

Hence
X\otimes X\in\bigvee_{{\mathcal{J}}\in S}({\mathcal{I}}\wedge{\mathcal{J}})
, and as
\in\bigvee_{{\mathcal{J}}\in S}({\mathcal{I}}\wedge{\mathcal{J}})
is radical, this means
X\in\bigvee_{{\mathcal{J}}\in S}({\mathcal{I}}\wedge{\mathcal{J}})
. This shows
\Rad({\mathcal{A}})
is a frame.
Note that the compact objects in
\Rad({\mathcal{A}})
are all of the form
\rad(X)
for an object
X\in{\mathcal{A}}
.

Let
X\in{\mathcal{A}}
and assume
\rad(X)\subseteq\bigvee S
for some
S\subseteq\Rad({\mathcal{A}})
. Then
X^{\otimes n}\in\thick^{\otimes}(\bigcup_{{\mathcal{J}}\in S}{\mathcal{J}})
. Taking the thick tensor closure is a finite process, hence there exists a finite subset
T\subseteq S
such that
X^{\otimes n}\in\thick^{\otimes}(\bigcup_{{\mathcal{J}}\in T}{\mathcal{J}})
. Hence
X\in\bigvee T
.

For coherence it remains to show that the meet of
\rad(X)
and
\rad(Y)
for any
X,Y\in{\mathcal{A}}
is
\rad(X\otimes Y)
. Since
\rad(X)
is a radical thick tensor ideal containing
X
it also contains
X\otimes Y
, and so
\rad(X)\geq\rad(X\otimes Y)
. Similarly
\rad(Y)\geq\rad(X\otimes Y)
. Thus
\rad(X)\wedge\rad(Y)\geq\rad(X\otimes Y)
, and we now require the reverse inclusion. Let
U\in\rad(X)\wedge\rad(Y)
. Then
U^{\otimes 2}\in\rad(X\otimes Y)
and
U\in\rad(X\otimes Y)
by
Eq.

6.1.1
. This finishes the proof.
∎

6.6.
Classification

For the classification it remains to apply Stone and Hochster duality to
Proposition

6.5
. For tensor triangulated categories this result is due to Balmer
[
Bal05
]
. The lattice-theoretic proof relies on work by
[
KP17
]
and
[
BKS05
]
. However, first we describe the prime elements of
\Rad({\mathcal{A}})
.

Lemma 6.7
.

Let
(\mathcal{A},\otimes,\mathbbm{1})
be a tensor extriangulated category such that
\Rad({\mathcal{A}})
is a set. A radical thick tensor ideal
{\mathcal{P}}
is prime in
\Rad({\mathcal{A}})
if and only if
{\mathcal{P}}
is a prime thick tensor ideal.

Proof.

We assume
{\mathcal{P}}
is prime as an element in the lattice
\Rad({\mathcal{A}})
. Suppose
X\otimes Y\in{\mathcal{P}}
for some objects
X,Y\in{\mathcal{A}}
. Then

{\mathcal{P}}\geq\rad(X\otimes Y)=\rad(X)\wedge\rad(Y)

using an intermediate claim proven in
Proposition

6.5
. By assumption, we have that
\rad(X)\leq{\mathcal{P}}
or
\rad(Y)\leq{\mathcal{P}}
. Hence
X\in{\mathcal{P}}
or
Y\in{\mathcal{P}}
.

Conversely, we assume that
{\mathcal{P}}
is a prime thick tensor ideal. Let
{\mathcal{I}},{\mathcal{J}}\in\Rad({\mathcal{A}})
such that
{\mathcal{I}}\cap{\mathcal{J}}\leq{\mathcal{P}}
and
{\mathcal{I}}\not\leq{\mathcal{P}}
. We need to show
{\mathcal{J}}\leq{\mathcal{P}}
. Let
X\in{\mathcal{I}}
with
X\notin{\mathcal{P}}
. Then
X\otimes Y\in{\mathcal{P}}
for every
Y\in{\mathcal{J}}
. As
{\mathcal{P}}
is a prime thick tensor ideal, this yields
Y\in{\mathcal{P}}
for every
Y\in{\mathcal{J}}
. Hence
{\mathcal{J}}\leq{\mathcal{P}}
.
∎

Theorem 6.8
.

Let
(\mathcal{A},\otimes,\mathbbm{1})
be a tensor extriangulated category such that
\Rad({\mathcal{A}})
is a set. We let
\operatorname{BSpc}({\mathcal{A}})
be the set of all prime thick tensor ideals in
{\mathcal{A}}
and endow it with a topology given by the closed sets

\supp(X)\coloneqq\set{{\mathcal{P}}\in\operatorname{BSpc}({\mathcal{A}})}{X\notin{\mathcal{P}}}

for any
X\in{\mathcal{A}}
. Then there is a bijection

\displaystyle\Rad({\mathcal{A}})\leftrightarrow\set{\text{Thomason closed subsets of }\operatorname{BSpc}({\mathcal{A}})},

\displaystyle{\mathcal{I}}\mapsto\supp({\mathcal{I}})=\bigcup_{X\in{\mathcal{I}}}\supp(X)\quad\text{and}\quad\set{X\in{\mathcal{A}}}{\supp(X)\subseteq V}\mapsfrom V\,.

Proof.

As the prime thick tensor ideals are precisely the prime elements of
\Rad({\mathcal{A}})
, we have

\operatorname{BSpc}({\mathcal{A}})=(\pt(\Rad({\mathcal{A}})))^{\vee}

as topological spaces. The classification follows from the description of the functors
\pt
and
(-)^{\vee}
.
∎

The topological space
\operatorname{BSpc}({\mathcal{A}})
is called the
Balmer spectrum
of
{\mathcal{A}}
.

6.9.
Functorality

Let
(\mathsf{F},\beta)\colon({\mathcal{A}},\otimes_{\mathcal{A}},\mathbbm{1}_{\mathcal{A}})\to({\mathcal{B}},\otimes_{\mathcal{B}},\mathbbm{1}_{\mathcal{B}})
be a tensor extriangulated functor. Then

\displaystyle\rad(\mathsf{F}(\bigvee S))=\rad(\bigcup_{X\in S}\mathsf{F}(X))=\bigvee_{X\in S}\rad(\mathsf{F}(X))\quad\text{and}

\displaystyle\rad(\mathsf{F}(\rad(X)\wedge\rad(Y)))=\rad(\mathsf{F}(X\otimes Y))=\rad(\mathsf{F}(X))\wedge\rad(\mathsf{F}(Y))

for any set of objects
S
in
{\mathcal{A}}
and objects
X
and
Y
in
{\mathcal{A}}
. Hence

\rad(\mathsf{F}(-))\colon\Rad({\mathcal{A}})\to\Rad({\mathcal{B}})

is a morphism of coherent frames. Applying Stone and Hochster duality we obtain a continuous map

\operatorname{BSpc}({\mathcal{B}})\to\operatorname{BSpc}({\mathcal{A}})\quad\text{given by}\quad{\mathcal{Q}}\mapsto\mathsf{F}^{-1}({\mathcal{Q}})\,.

Lemma 6.10
.

Let
(\mathsf{F},\beta)\colon({\mathcal{A}},\otimes_{\mathcal{A}},\mathbbm{1}_{\mathcal{A}})\to({\mathcal{B}},\otimes_{\mathcal{B}},\mathbbm{1}_{\mathcal{B}})
be an essentially surjective tensor extriangulated functor. Then the induced map
\operatorname{BSpc}({\mathcal{B}})\to\operatorname{BSpc}({\mathcal{A}})
is injective. If for every extriangle
X\to Y\to Z\dashrightarrow
in
{\mathcal{B}}
there exists an extriangle
X^{\prime}\to Y^{\prime}\to Z^{\prime}\dashrightarrow
with
\mathsf{F}(X^{\prime})\cong X
,
\mathsf{F}(Y^{\prime})\cong Y
and
\mathsf{F}(Z^{\prime})\cong Z
, then the injective map is an embedding.

Proof.

The injectivity is clear from the definition of the induced map.

For the second claim it is enough to show
{\mathcal{I}}\subseteq\mathsf{F}^{-1}({\mathcal{Q}})
if and only if
\rad(\mathsf{F}({\mathcal{I}}))\subseteq{\mathcal{Q}}
for any radical thick tensor ideal
{\mathcal{I}}\subseteq{\mathcal{A}}
and any
{\mathcal{Q}}\in\operatorname{BSpc}({\mathcal{B}})
. By assumption we have
\rad(\mathsf{F}({\mathcal{I}}))=\mathsf{F}({\mathcal{I}})
. Hence the forward direction holds. The backward direction is clear.
∎

The Balmer spectrum has been computed for many tensor triangulated categories, like the perfect complexes of a commutative ring
[
Hop87
,
Nee92
,
Tho97
]
and the stable module category of a group algebra
[
BCR97
]
. In the following we describe the Balmer spectrum of some of the examples from
Section

5
.

6.11.
Projective modules

Let
R
be a commutative ring with local units. In
Example

5.2
we saw that the category of flat modules
\operatorname{Flat}(R)
is a tensor extriangulated category. This category need not be essentially small. However, the subcategory of finitely presented flat modules is essentially small and so we may consider its Balmer spectrum. In fact, a module is finitely presented and flat if and only if it is finitely presented and projective. In the following we compute the Balmer spectrum of
\operatorname{proj}(R)
for some classes of commutative rings
R
.

Note that the exact structure on
\operatorname{proj}(R)
is the split exact structure, so the radical thick tensor ideals are precisely the radical tensor ideals that are closed under finite coproducts and direct summands.

Lemma 6.12
.

Let
R
be a commutative ring that satisfies one of the following conditions:

(1)

R=S[x_{1},\dots,x_{n}]
, the polynomial ring over a principal ideal domain
S
;

(2)

R
is a local ring; or

(3)

R
is a Dedekind domain.

Then
\operatorname{BSpc}(\operatorname{proj}(R))=\{\rad(0)\}
; that is the Balmer spectrum consists of one point.

Proof.

In the cases
Items

1
and
2
every finitely generated projective
R
-module is free; for
Item

1
this follows from the Quillen–Suslin theorem and for
Item

2
from Kaplansky’s theorem; see for example
[
Lan02
, Chapter XXI, Theorem 3.7]
and
[
Kap58
, Theorem 2]
, respectively. Hence there are exactly two radical thick tensor ideals:
\rad(0)
and
\operatorname{proj}(R)
, and
\rad(0)
is the only prime thick tensor ideal.

In the case
Item

3
the finitely projective
R
-modules are precisely the torsion-free modules. In particular, any ideal is projective. Let
I
and
J
be non-zero ideals of
R
. By
[
Eis95
, Exercise 19.5]
, we obtain
J\oplus I^{2}\cong JI\oplus I
. Since
I
is projective, we have
JI\cong J\otimes I
. This means
J\in\thick(I)
. Furthermore, any finitely generated projective
R
-module is of the form
R^{n}\oplus I
for some ideal
I
; see
[
Eis95
, Exercise 19.6]
. So
\operatorname{proj}(R)
has two radical thick tensor ideals, namely
\rad(0)
and
\operatorname{proj}(R)
, and
\rad(0)
is the only prime thick tensor ideal.
∎

Lemma 6.13
.

Let
R=R_{1}\times\ldots\times R_{n}
be a product of commutative rings. Then

\operatorname{BSpc}(\operatorname{proj}(R))=\operatorname{BSpc}(\operatorname{proj}(R_{1}))\times\ldots\times\operatorname{BSpc}(\operatorname{proj}(R_{n}))

with the product topology.

Proof.

By induction it is enough to show the claim for
n=2
. Let
R=S\times T
. As an
R
-module
R\cong S\oplus T
and every
R
-module decomposes as
M\cong(S\otimes_{R}M)\oplus(T\otimes_{R}M)
. In particular,
M
is a finitely presented projective
R
-module if and only if
S\otimes_{R}M
and
T\otimes_{R}M
are finitely presented projective over
S
and
T
, respectively. The map

\begin{gathered}\Rad(\operatorname{proj}(R))\to\Rad(\operatorname{proj}(S))\times\Rad(\operatorname{proj}(T))\,,\\
{\mathcal{I}}\mapsto\set{S\otimes_{R}M}{M\in{\mathcal{I}}}\times\set{T\otimes_{R}M}{M\in{\mathcal{I}}}\end{gathered}

has an inverse given by

\rad({\mathcal{I}}\cup{\mathcal{J}})\mapsfrom{\mathcal{I}}\times{\mathcal{J}}

where we view the objects in
{\mathcal{I}}
and
{\mathcal{J}}
as
R
-modules using restriction. This bijection is an isomorphism of coherent frames and hence it yields the desired isomorphism of the Balmer spectra.
∎

Note that a commutative noetherian ring is hereditary if and only if it is regular of Krull dimension 1; see for example
[
End61
, Corollary 3]
. For such rings
Corollary

6.14
describes the Balmer spectrum.

Corollary 6.14
.

If
R
is a commutative hereditary noetherian ring, then the Balmer spectrum
\operatorname{BSpc}(\operatorname{proj}(R))
is the finite set of connected components of the Zariski spectrum
\operatorname{ZSpec}(R)
equipped with the discrete topology, and
|\operatorname{BSpc}(\operatorname{proj}(R))|
is equal to the number of indecomposable factors of
R
.

Proof.

Note that
R
cannot contain an infinite set of primitive idempotents since it is noetherian; see for example
[
Lam01
, Proposition 22.2]
. By
[
Ber71
, Theorem 4.4]
, see also
[
CJ83
, p. 44, Corollary 1]
, the ring
R
is a finite product of Dedekind domains and fields. Hence the claim follows from
Lemmas

6.12
and
6.13
.
∎

6.15.
Stabilisation of extriangulated categories

We discussed the stabilisation of a tensor extriangulated category in
Section

5.4
. In the stabilisation
\underline{{\mathcal{A}}}_{\mathcal{I}}
the objects in
{\mathcal{I}}
are isomorphic to
0
. Hence there are fewer radical thick tensor ideals in
\underline{{\mathcal{A}}}_{\mathcal{I}}
than in
{\mathcal{A}}
. In fact, the identity induces a tensor extriangulated functor
{\mathcal{A}}\to\underline{{\mathcal{A}}}_{\mathcal{I}}
, and as a direct consequence of
Lemma

6.10
we obtain:

Lemma 6.16
.

Let
({\mathcal{A}},\otimes,\mathbbm{1})
be a tensor extriangulated category and
{\mathcal{I}}
a tensor ideal consisting of objects that are
\mathbb{E}
-projective and
\mathbb{E}
-injective. Then

\operatorname{BSpc}(\underline{{\mathcal{A}}}_{\mathcal{I}})=\set{{\mathcal{P}}\in\operatorname{BSpc}({\mathcal{A}})}{{\mathcal{P}}\supseteq{\mathcal{I}}}

with the subspace topology. ∎

Example 6.17
.

Let
H
be a cocommutative Hopf algebra over a field
k
. We assume that
H
is finite-dimensional over
k
. Then the category finite-dimensional
H
-modules is essentially small, and by
Example

5.7
it becomes a tensor extriangulated category with
(\otimes_{k},k)
. Further,
\operatorname{mod}(H)
is a Frobenius exact category, and there exists exactly one non-trivial thick tensor ideal consisting of objects that are projective and injective. We denote by
{\underline{\operatorname{mod}}}(H)
the stabilisation of
\operatorname{mod}(H)
with respect to this tensor ideal. Hence under the induced morphism of coherent frames

\Rad(\operatorname{mod}(H))\to\Rad({\underline{\operatorname{mod}}}(H))

every ideal has precisely one pre-image except the zero ideal in
{\underline{\operatorname{mod}}}(H)
, which has exactly two pre-images: the zero ideal and the ideal of all projective objects.

Let
G
be a finite group. By
[
BCR97
]
,

\operatorname{BSpc}({\underline{\operatorname{mod}}}(kG))=\operatorname{Proj}(\operatorname{H}^{*}(G,k))\,,

where
\operatorname{H}^{*}(G,k)
is the group cohomology of
G
and
\operatorname{Proj}(\operatorname{H}^{*}(G,k))
the set of homogenous primes without the irrelevant ideal with the Zariski topology. Then

\operatorname{BSpc}(\operatorname{mod}(kG))=\operatorname{Spec}(\operatorname{H}^{*}(G,k))

where the latter is the set of all homogenous prime ideals; also see
[
Kra24
, Example 17]
.

Example 6.18
.

Let
({\mathcal{A}},\otimes,\mathbbm{1})
be an additive monoidal category and
\operatorname{Ch}({\mathcal{A}})
the category of chain complexes. The degree-wise split exact sequences endow
\operatorname{Ch}({\mathcal{A}})
with an exact structure. It is straightforward to check that
\operatorname{Ch}({\mathcal{A}})
is a tensor extriangulated category with the tensor product induced by
\otimes
and the unit the complex with
\mathbbm{1}
concentrated in degree zero. Moreover, the exact structure on
\operatorname{Ch}({\mathcal{A}})
is Frobenius and the projective objects are precisely the complexes of the form
\cone(\id_{X})
for any complex
X
; these are precisely the contractible complexes.

We endow
{\mathcal{A}}
with the split exact structure. Then there is a bijection

\displaystyle\Rad({\mathcal{A}})\to\set{{\mathcal{J}}\in\Rad(\operatorname{Ch}({\mathcal{A}}))}{{\mathcal{J}}\subseteq\operatorname{Proj}(\operatorname{Ch}({\mathcal{A}}))}

\displaystyle\text{given by}\quad{\mathcal{I}}\mapsto\rad(\set{\cone(\id_{M})}{M\in{\mathcal{I}}})\,.

Hence there may exist indeterminate extriangulated stabilisations between the category of chain complexes
\operatorname{Ch}({\mathcal{A}})
and its homotopy category

\operatorname{K}({\mathcal{A}})=\operatorname{Ch}({\mathcal{A}})/\operatorname{Proj}(\operatorname{Ch}({\mathcal{A}}))\,;

compare with
Lemmas

6.13
and
6.14
.
