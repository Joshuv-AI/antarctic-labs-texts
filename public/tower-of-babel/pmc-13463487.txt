White Matter and Perivascular Imaging Changes in Alzheimer's Disease and Cerebral Amyloid Angiopathy
Authors: Debina Laishram, Guangwei Du, Sangam Kanekar, Nandakumar Nagaraja
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTObjectivePeak‐width of skeletonized mean diffusivity (PSMD) and diffusion tensor imaging–analysis along the perivascular space (DTI‐ALPS), reflecting white matter integrity and glymphatic function, are altered in Alzheimer's disease (AD). We evaluated whether these biomarkers differ between AD participants with and without concomitant cerebral amyloid angiopathy (CAA).MethodsThe study included 50 AD participants with mild cognitive impairment/mild dementia, and intermediate to high AD neuropathologic change at autopsy. AD was categorized as AD with CAA and AD without CAA based on CAA neuropathology. We evaluated global and regional (frontal, parietal, temporal and occipital) PSMD; left, right and mean DTI‐ALPS indices and their association with clinical measures [Clinical dementia rating sum‐of‐boxes (CDR‐SB) from CDR Dementia Staging Instrument, mini mental state examination (MMSE), cognitive composites: memory, processing speed, executive function, and language].ResultsAD participants with CAA (n = 17) had higher global [4.02 ± 1.44 (mean ± SD × 10−4 mm2/s) vs. 3.12 ± 0.91, β = −0.80, 95% CI (−1.42, −0.18), p = 0.012] and occipital PSMD [4.02 ± 1.10 vs. 3.00 ± 1.08, β = −0.88, 95% CI (−1.51, −0.26), p = 0.026] than those without CAA. No PSMD metric was associated with any clinical measure. However, imaging‐by‐group interactions showed global PSMD associated with language [β = −0.89, 95% CI (−1.53, −0.26), p = 0.027] and parietal PSMD with language [β = −1.05, 95% CI (−1.74, −0.36), p = 0.014] and memory [β = −0.83, 95% CI (−1.38, −0.28), p = 0.015]. DTI‐ALPS indices did not differ by group. Higher mean and right DTI‐ALPS indices were associated with preserved language function [mean: β = 9.33, 95% CI (2.05, 16.62), p = 0.036; right: β = 7.75, 95% CI (1.54, 13.95), p = 0.043] without imaging‐by‐group interactions.InterpretationGlobal and occipital PSMD may help identify AD participants with concomitant CAA.Keywords: ALPS index, Alzheimer's disease, cerebral amyloid angiopathy, diffusion tensor imaging, peak‐width of skeletonized mean diffusivity

Introduction

Alzheimer's disease (AD) is the most common neurodegenerative disorder characterized by progressive memory loss and cognitive decline due to the accumulation of extracellular amyloid‐β (Aβ) plaques and intracellular tau neurofibrillary tangles leading to synaptic dysfunction and neuronal loss [1, 2]. Cerebral amyloid angiopathy (CAA) is a closely related condition characterized by Aβ deposition within cortical and leptomeningeal vessel walls [3].

AD and CAA share Aβ dysregulation as a central mechanism [3, 4]. CAA‐related vascular injury impairs perivascular Aβ clearance, leading to vascular and parenchymal Aβ accumulation that promotes AD progression [3]. Conversely, AD pathology can worsen CAA by disrupting perivascular drainage and clearance pathways [3, 5, 6]. CAA may also accelerate neurodegeneration and cognitive decline, contributing to earlier dementia onset in AD [4, 7], partly by promoting tau pathology [8, 9].

Moderate to severe CAA co‐occurs in approximately 48% of AD cases, complicating diagnosis and treatment, particularly as anti‐Aβ therapies may differentially affect vascular and parenchymal Aβ [6, 10]. Despite progress, distinguishing AD from AD with coexisting CAA remains challenging. Magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) detects CAA‐related hemorrhagic features such as microbleeds and superficial siderosis at late stage of the disease, and conventional positron emission tomography lacks specificity for distinguishing amyloid deposition in AD versus CAA [6, 11]. This underscores the need for advanced imaging to better distinguish overlapping pathologies before hemorrhage occurs.

The peak‐width of skeletonized mean diffusivity (PSMD) is a sensitive marker of white matter injury, outperforming white matter hyperintensity (WMH) volume and standard diffusion tensor imaging (DTI) metrics such as fractional anisotropy (FA), mean diffusivity (MD), axial and radial diffusivity [12]. Higher PSMD has been reported in mild cognitive impairment (MCI), AD, cerebral small vessel disease (cSVD) including CAA and cerebral autosomal dominant arteriopathy with subcortical infarcts and leuko‐encephalopathy (CADASIL) though it is not specific to a single vascular or neurodegenerative disorder [12]. Global PSMD is higher in probable CAA compared with healthy controls [13, 14, 15], MCI [13], AD [13], non‐cSVD [16], and non‐CAA patients with cognitive complaints [17], but similar to cSVD forms [16]. It is also higher in CAA‐MCI versus non‐CAA‐MCI [18] and in late‐onset AD versus older cognitively normal controls [19]. In CAA, higher global PSMD correlates with poorer processing speed [13, 14, 15, 16, 18, 20], executive function [14, 15, 16, 17], and memory [14, 15, 17]. Moreover, global (but not regional) PSMD may predict cognitive decline and progression from healthy controls to MCI and AD [21]. Regionally, PSMD is higher in frontal, parietal, temporal, and occipital areas in late‐onset AD versus older cognitively normal controls [19], and the occipital‐frontal PSMD gradient is higher in probable CAA than in cSVD and non‐cSVD [16].

DTI analysis along the perivascular space (DTI‐ALPS) index derived from diffusion MRI serves as a marker of glymphatic function, which clears neurotoxic proteins from the brain [22]. It quantifies water diffusivity within the perivascular space running along medullary veins perpendicular to the lateral ventricle in the right–left direction (x‐axis). In this region, projection fibers run head‐to‐toe (y‐axis) adjacent to the lateral ventricle and association fibers (superior longitudinal fasciculus) run anterior–posterior (z‐axis) adjacent to the projection fibers [22, 23]. Perivascular alterations along the x‐axis affect diffusion in projection and association fibers along the y‐ and z‐axes, respectively. Thus, the DTI‐ALPS index derived from x‐, y‐, and z‐axis diffusivities may reflect perivascular and glymphatic function [22, 23]. Lower DTI‐ALPS index values have been associated with age [24], cognitive decline in AD [22, 24, 25, 26], vascular cognitive impairment and dementia (VCID) in cSVD [23, 27, 28, 29, 30] and global amyloid burden [24].

This study evaluates PSMD and DTI‐ALPS index in a clinically and neuropathologically verified AD cohort with and without CAA pathology, using data from The National Alzheimer's Coordinating Center (NACC) which has not been previously examined. We examine global and regional (frontal, parietal, temporal, and occipital) PSMD, along with left, right, and mean DTI‐ALPS indices to explore their potential as biomarkers for identifying CAA in AD.

Methods

Data Source and Study Participants

The study dataset was obtained from the NACC under a signed data use agreement [31]. We analyzed data from 33 Alzheimer's Disease Research Center (ADRC) participants from visits conducted from September 2005 to March 2025. All contributing ADRCs obtained informed consent from their participants and maintained local institutional review board (IRB) approval before submitting data to NACC.

Participants were included if they met all of the following criteria: (1) autopsy at death and at least one MRI during study visits (variables NACCAUTP and NACCMRSA) [32]; (2) AD neuropathologic change (ADNC) reported as not AD, low, intermediate, or high, and CAA reported as none, mild, moderate, or severe in neuropathology [33]; (3) a DTI scan acquired on a 3‐Tesla (3T) MRI to derive the DTI‐ALPS index and PSMD; and (4) a clinical diagnosis of dementia due to AD at the final visit before death.

The National Institute of Aging—Alzheimer's Association (NIA‐AA) ADNC ABC criteria were used to diagnose and stage AD [34, 35]. ADNC severity was classified as none, low, intermediate, or high based on amyloid β plaque (A), Braak neurofibrillary tangle stage (B), and CERAD (Consortium to Establish a Registry for AD) neuritic plaque score (C). CAA severity was graded as none, mild, moderate, or severe using a semi‐quantitative assessment of overall neocortical amyloid angiopathy adapted from prior work [36, 37]. In this study AD and CAA were defined based on the following neuropathological and clinical criteria. AD was defined as intermediate or high ADNC with documented dementia at the last visit before death (NACCUDSD) and AD as the presumptive etiology (NACCALZD). CAA was defined as moderate or severe CAA on neuropathology. Boston 2.0 criteria were not applied owing to unavailable susceptibility‐weighted images. Participants were classified into two groups based on CAA neuropathology: AD with CAA (moderate/severe CAA) and AD without CAA (no/mild CAA).

Participants were excluded for the following reasons: (1) ADNC and CAA reported as not assessed, missing or unavailable (not routinely collected before 2014); (2) MRI unavailable from NACC, lacked DTI, or had unsuitable DTI parameters (non‐3 T, b‐value < 1000 or > 1300 s/mm2, slice thickness > 3 mm or registration concerns such as visible distortion and poor image quality after non‐linear registration, judged unsuitable for analysis); (3) clinical‐pathologic mismatch, including moderate/severe ADNC with normal cognition, MCI due to AD, or non‐AD diagnosis; or conversely no/low ADNC with clinical diagnosis of dementia due to AD, MCI due to AD, or non‐AD dementia; (4) no AD dementia with no/low ADNC; (5) missing ApoE ε4 genotype.

The following data elements were included: (1) demographics (2) medical comorbidities (3) clinical measures [Clinical Dementia Rating sum of boxes (CDR‐SB) from the CDR Dementia Staging Instrument and Mini Mental State Examination (MMSE) scores, and the computed neuropsychological composites: memory (Logical Memory immediate and delayed), processing speed (Trail Making A, Digit Span forward, and Wechsler Adult Intelligence Scale Digit Symbol), executive function (Trail Making B and Digit Span backward), and language (Boston Naming and Animal Fluency), Table S1] (4) ApoE genotype and number of ApoE ε4 alleles and (5) neuropathology data as described in Supplemental Methods [32, 33].

Of 51,836 participants in the NACC dataset, after applying the inclusion and exclusion criteria, 50 participants with clinical AD dementia and intermediate/high ADNC were included, of whom 17 had concomitant CAA and 33 did not (Figure 1; full cohort derivation in Supplemental Methods).

Imaging Protocol

The DTI, T1‐weighted (T1w) and T2‐weighted Fluid‐Attenuated Inversion Recovery (T2‐FLAIR) images were acquired on 3T scanners (Siemens TrioTim, Siemens Verio, and GE Discovery MR750). DTI used 2D axial echo‐planar imaging at b‐values of 1000 s/mm2 (n = 14) and 1300 s/mm2 (n = 36). Full acquisition parameters are provided in Table S2 and Supplemental Methods.

Image Processing and Analysis

PSMD and DTI‐ALPS index used a shared two‐step preprocessing pipeline. First, Functional Magnetic Resonance Imaging of the Brain (FMRIB) Software Library's (FSL) topup was used to estimate susceptibility‐induced off‐resonance distortions which are incorporated into FSL's eddy to correct for susceptibility, head motion, and eddy‐current–induced distortions; corrected images were smoothed with a Gaussian filter to reduce residual noise [38, 39, 40, 41, 42]. In‐scanner head motion was estimated from the FSL eddy outputs as the per‐volume root‐mean‐square (RMS) displacement (volume‐to‐volume and cumulative) [43] and as a translation‐based Euclidean distance combining the mean absolute translation along each axis [44]. Second, diffusion tensors were fitted voxelwise with FSL's dtifit, yielding eigenvalues (L1–L3), eigenvectors (V1–V3), FA, MD, and tensor components along the three principal axes (Dxx, Dyy, Dzz).

PSMD

We computed both global and regional PSMD. Global PSMD followed the MarkVCID MRI PSMD Kit protocol [45, 46], using FSL's Tract‐Based Spatial Statistics (TBSS) for skeletonization and histogram analysis of FA and MD images. FA images were skeletonized to extract central white matter tracts, MD maps were projected onto the skeleton, and a white matter mask was applied. Histogram analysis then yielded the 5th and 95th percentile MD values, representing the upper and lower extremes of diffusivity distribution within the skeletonized white matter tracts. PSMD was then calculated as the difference between the 5th and 95th percentiles (P5 and P95), normalized by a factor of one million on the skeletonized MD map.

We computed regional PSMD in frontal, parietal, temporal, and occipital white matter. The lobar masks were derived from FreeSurfer. Each participant's T1‐weighted image was processed with recon‐all, generating cortical parcellation based on the Desikan–Killiany atlas [47] and corresponding white matter segmentations [48, 49]. For each lobe, the cortical and white matter labels were binarized and combined to create a single lobar mask, with deep white matter within the centrum semiovale assigned to the corresponding lobe. These masks were applied to the skeletonized MD map to compute regional PSMD. Within each masked skeleton, we calculated the 5th and 95th percentiles of non‐zero MD values and defined regional PSMD as their difference, using the same procedure as for global PSMD.

DTI‐ALPS Index

We registered each subject's FA map to the Montreal Neurological Institute (MNI) FMRIB58_FA_1mm template using rigid‐body alignment, and diffusion tensors were spatially normalized while preserving orientation. The head tilt angle, estimated from pre‐ and post‐registration transformation matrices, was ≤ 30° for all participants, which supports reproducibility as described by Taoka et al. [50].

A color FA map was generated by scaling the primary eigenvector (V1) by FA with tracts color‐coded by orientation (red right–left, blue superior–inferior, green anterior–posterior) to distinguish projection (blue) along y‐axis and association (green) fibers of superior longitudinal fasciculus along the z‐axis. Four 5 mm diameter spherical ROIs were placed at the level of the lateral ventricle body: in each hemisphere, one on projection fibers and one on adjacent association fibers oriented perpendicular to the x‐axis. From these ROIs, we extracted diffusivity values (Dxx, Dyy, Dzz) to compute left, right, and mean DTI‐ALPS indices using Dxxproj (x‐axis diffusivity in the projection fibers), Dxxassoc (x‐axis diffusivity in the association fibers), Dyyproj (y‐axis diffusivity in the projection fibers), and Dzzassoc (z‐axis diffusivity in the association fibers). White Matter Hyperintensity Volume

Total intracranial volume (ICV) was estimated as FreeSurfer's estimated total intracranial volume (eTIV) from each participant's T1‐weighted image [49]. WMH were segmented using LST‐AI, a deep‐learning ensemble for automated lesion segmentation [51], followed by visual inspection for quality assurance.

Among 50 participants, 49 had usable T2‐FLAIR images (16 AD with CAA, 33 AD without CAA) and were included in the WMH analysis using a lesion probability threshold of 0.5. Segmentation errors were manually corrected in ITK‐SNAP [52] and final lesion volumes were extracted in cubic centimeters. Normalized WMH (nWMH) volume was computed relative to ICV and total brain volume.

All steps in the DTI image processing pipeline were implemented using Python (version 3.9.13) and shell scripts that integrated tools from FSL (6.0.7.18) and FreeSurfer (7.3.2).

Statistical Methods

Categorical variables were summarized as frequencies (percentages) and continuous variables as mean ± standard deviation (SD). Group comparisons used Chi‐square or Fisher's exact tests for categorical data and ANOVA or Kruskal–Wallis tests for continuous data, depending on normality. Normality was assessed with the Shapiro–Wilk test and visual inspection of histograms and Q–Q plots. Clinical measures were analyzed at two timepoints relative to MRI: baseline (0 ± 1 year) and 1–4 years after MRI.

Associations between AD with and without CAA and global/regional PSMD (frontal, parietal, temporal, occipital) were examined using linear regression. Cumulative vascular risk score was calculated as a sum of three (yes/no) vascular risk factors—hypertension, hyperlipidemia, and > 100 cigarette smoking history. Each scored 0 or 1, where 0 was absent and 1 was present. Model 1 was adjusted for age at MRI, sex, education, and cumulative vascular‐risk score (range 0–3). Model 2 added ApoE ε4 status to model 1. Model 3 further included normalized WMH volume (nWMH/ICV) as a covariate because both PSMD and DTI‐ALPS can be influenced by WMH burden [12, 14, 16, 17, 23]. The left, right and mean DTI‐ALPS indices were analyzed using the same approach. Bonferroni correction was applied to four regional PSMD metrics (frontal, parietal, temporal and occipital) and three DTI‐ALPS indices (left, right and mean). Global PSMD was reported separately as a single whole brain measure.

To evaluate whether CAA affected regional patterns of white matter injury, pairwise PSMD gradients were computed for each participant by subtracting PSMD values between lobes, yielding six measures: occipital–frontal, occipital–parietal, occipital–temporal, temporal–frontal, temporal–parietal, and parietal–frontal. Gradient normality was verified with the Shapiro–Wilk test. Group differences in gradients were tested with linear regression adjusted for age at MRI, sex, education, and the cumulative vascular‐risk score, with Bonferroni correction across the six gradient comparisons.

Longitudinal change in six clinical measures (CDR‐SB, MMSE, and four cognitive composites: memory, processing speed, executive function, and language) and their associations with the imaging metrics were analyzed using linear mixed‐effects models with random intercepts. Analyses included two timepoints: baseline (0 ± 1 year) and follow‐up (1–4 years). Timepoint was coded as a binary indicator (baseline vs. 1–4 years), group as a binary indicator (AD with CAA vs. AD without CAA), and imaging metrics as a continuous predictor. CDR‐SB and MMSE models were adjusted for age at MRI, sex, education, and cumulative vascular‐risk score, whereas cognitive composite z‐score models were adjusted for vascular‐risk score only because the composites were standardized to sex‐, age‐, and education‐adjusted normative data.

Two models were constructed. First, each clinical measure was modeled as a function of timepoint, study group, and their interaction to estimate main time effects and time‐by‐group differences. Second, separate models were fit for PSMD and DTI‐ALPS indices, in which each clinical measure was modeled as a function of the imaging metric, timepoint, study group, imaging‐by‐group, and imaging‐by‐timepoint interaction terms. Bonferroni correction was applied for the second set of models to account for multiple comparisons across five PSMD metrics and three DTI‐ALPS metrics.

Statistical significance was defined at p ≤ 0.05. Confidence intervals are reported at the 95% level. All analyses were conducted in Stata version 18 SE.

Results

AD with CAA participants were 75.9 ± 7.4 years old at MRI (29% female), and AD without CAA participants were 73.3 ± 9.1 years old (39% female; Table 1). All were White. Groups did not differ in demographics, CDR‐SB, MMSE, or nWMH volume. At MRI, both were in the MCI/mild dementia stage (CDR‐SB = 5.4 ± 2.9 vs. 5.4 ± 3.5). Compared with AD without CAA, the AD with CAA group had higher rates of atrial fibrillation (24 vs. 0%; p = 0.004) and ApoE ε4 homozygosity (53 vs. 6%; p < 0.001). No significant group differences were observed in other neuropathological measures, including cortical or hippocampal atrophy, hippocampal sclerosis, infarcts, primary parenchymal hemorrhages, microbleed or TDP‐43 pathology (Table S3). No old cerebral microbleeds were identified in either group.

Assessment of PSMD

Global PSMD was higher in AD with CAA than AD without CAA (4.02 ± 1.44 vs. 3.12 ± 0.91 × 10−4 mm2/s; β = −0.80, 95% CI (−1.42, −0.18), p = 0.012) in model 1 (Table 2 and Figure 2). Discussion

We examined PSMD and DTI‐ALPS indices as potential imaging biomarkers of CAA in AD, assessing their association with clinical measures and whether these differed between AD with and without CAA. AD participants with concomitant CAA had higher global and occipital PSMD than those without CAA. PSMD was not associated with clinical measures overall; however, imaging‐by‐group interactions indicated that associations of global and parietal PSMD with language, and parietal PSMD with memory, were stronger in AD with CAA. In contrast, DTI‐ALPS index did not differ between groups, although higher mean and right DTI‐ALPS indices were associated with preserved language function without imaging‐by‐group interactions. These findings suggest that global PSMD may serve as a non‐hemorrhagic imaging biomarker of CAA in AD and that higher PSMD is more strongly linked with poorer language performance in AD with CAA than in AD without CAA.

Our finding of higher global PSMD in AD with CAA than in AD without CAA mirrors prior reports of increased PSMD in CAA vs. non‐CAA individuals without cognitive impairment [17] and in CAA‐MCI vs. non‐CAA MCI [18]. Because PSMD, a fully automated histogram‐based marker, is elevated in both cSVD and in neurodegenerative disorders such as AD, the higher global PSMD in AD with concomitant CAA (a form of cSVD) likely reflects additive or synergistic effects of these pathologies beyond those seen in AD without CAA. This association remained significant after adjustment for WMH volume, which can influence PSMD, suggesting that PSMD captures microstructural injury beyond lesion burden visible on conventional MRI. Moreover, these group differences were already present at MCI or mild dementia stage, approximately 6 years before death, corresponding to the disease stage at which anti‐Aβ therapies are typically considered.

None of the participants in either group had cerebral microbleeds or primary parenchymal hemorrhage; therefore, our AD with CAA cohort would not have met Boston criteria for CAA based solely on hemorrhagic MRI markers. This suggests that PSMD may serve as a potential imaging biomarker for pre‐hemorrhagic CAA in individuals with AD, helping to identify patients more likely to harbor underlying CAA when considering anti–Aβ therapies. Such a marker could also inform monitoring for treatment‐related adverse events, including amyloid‐related imaging abnormalities (ARIA), which occur more frequently in individuals with MRI evidence of CAA such as cerebral microbleeds.

Spatial variations in PSMD have been examined because CAA preferentially affects posterior brain regions, especially the occipital and parietal lobes where vascular amyloid deposition and related white matter injury are most prominent [53, 54]. This regional vulnerability is thought to lead to greater loss of white matter integrity in posterior compared with anterior regions, manifesting as disproportionately higher PSMD in posterior lobes, and prior work has reported higher occipital PSMD and increased occipital‐frontal PSMD gradient in CAA [16]. In our cohort, regional PSMD increases were again most pronounced in the occipital lobe relative to frontal and temporal lobes consistent with posterior vulnerability; but we did not observe a significant occipital‐frontal PSMD gradient in the CAA group, unlike previous report [16]. A prior study in late‐onset AD showed elevated PSMD across all lobes (frontal, parietal, temporal, and occipital) compared with cognitively normal older adults [19], and in our AD cohort we likewise observed increased frontal, temporal, and occipital PSMD. Against this background of elevated occipital and frontal PSMD, any occipital‐frontal PSMD difference was likely attenuated, rendering the gradient statistically non‐significant in our AD with CAA group.

PSMD reflects the heterogeneity of voxel‐wise MD values along major white matter tracts and serves as a marker of white matter microstructural integrity. In a large multi‐cohort study, despite substantial variation in acquisition protocols, conventional DTI metrics (mean, axial, and radial diffusivity) showed a U‐shaped relationship with age, whereas PSMD increased monotonically across adulthood, rising slowly until about the sixth decade and then more steeply thereafter [55]. Thus, PSMD can be interpreted more reliably than other DTI measures across heterogeneous datasets [56], is considered a sensitive marker of the aging process [55], and captures additional white matter alterations related to disease states beyond normal aging.

PSMD was not associated with cognitive measures in the overall AD cohort. Among AD participants with CAA, however, higher global and parietal PSMD were associated with more language decline and higher parietal PSMD with more memory decline, compared with AD without CAA. Prior work has shown that higher global PSMD in individuals meeting Boston criteria for CAA is consistently associated with poorer processing speed, executive function, and memory [13, 14, 15, 16, 17], and that carriers of Dutch‐type hereditary CAA similarly show worse cognition, particularly in processing speed, with higher PSMD [20]. These findings suggest that in the context of AD, CAA may preferentially worsen the memory and language domains typically affected in AD, rather than the processing speed and executive functions emphasized in prior CAA studies.

Lower DTI‐ALPS index values are associated with older age and cognitive decline in AD and cSVD, supporting its interpretation as a marker of glymphatic dysfunction and associated white matter damage [22, 23, 24, 25, 26, 27, 28, 29, 30]. Consistent with this, higher DTI‐ALPS index in our AD cohort was associated with preserved language function. However, it did not distinguish AD with CAA from AD without CAA, suggesting limited utility as a biomarker of CAA in AD.

PSMD and DTI‐ALPS index are derived from white matter diffusion metrics and can be influenced by WMH burden. In prior reports, WMH volume was an independent predictor of elevated PSMD in CAA after adjusting for demographics, vascular risk factors, intracerebral hemorrhage, and cortical thickness [14], and its association with non‐hemorrhagic markers of cSVD in CAA was robust [16]. In the DTI‐ALPS index, ROIs at the level of the body of the lateral ventricle may overlap WMH, altering the radial diffusivity measurement. We therefore adjusted for normalized WMH volume as a covariate. Notably, the CAA effect on global and occipital PSMD persisted after WMH adjustment, indicating that PSMD captures CAA‐related microstructural injury that is independent of and not explained by macroscopic WMH lesion burden.

Our study showed higher rates of ApoE ε4 homozygosity and atrial fibrillation in AD with CAA. Although ApoE ε4 homozygosity has been associated with CAA [3], data on association of ApoE ε4 with atrial fibrillation are uncertain [57, 58], and the co‐occurrence of both in AD with CAA is concerning. These patients face increased ischemic stroke risk from atrial fibrillation, while anticoagulation in ApoE ε4 carriers has been associated with a threefold higher risk of intracranial hemorrhage [59].

This study has several limitations that should be considered when interpreting the results. The relatively small and unequal group sizes (17 vs. 33) may have reduced the study's statistical power and, consequently, our sensitivity to detect subtle microstructural differences between groups, particularly in the DTI‐ALPS index assessment. In addition, the cross‐sectional design with a single MRI timepoint precluded assessment of longitudinal changes in PSMD and DTI‐ALPS over the course of disease progression.

PSMD, although fully automated and highly reproducible within standardized protocols, is a dispersion metric that reflects heterogeneity in MD values across the white matter skeleton. It is not disease‐specific and may be elevated in various vascular and neurodegenerative conditions [12]. Furthermore, its relationship with cognition may be attenuated in older populations where hippocampal atrophy and amyloid burden play larger roles in cognitive decline.

MD is sensitive to b‐value, with MD values decreasing as b‐value increases (e.g., from 800 to 1300 s/mm2), because the Gaussian diffusion tensor model incompletely captures the true non‐Gaussian diffusion at higher b‐values [60]. To reduce acquisition‐related heterogeneity, we excluded 15 participants scanned with b=3000s/mm2, because mean diffusivity—and therefore MD‐histogram–derived PSMD—becomes increasingly biased at higher b‐values, where the conventional Gaussian tensor model inadequately captures non‐Gaussian diffusion behavior [55]. Although prior cerebral amyloid angiopathy studies have used b = 700 s/mm2 for PSMD, only one participant in our dataset was acquired with this b‐value and was therefore excluded [16, 18]. We thus restricted our analytic cohort to participants with b=1000and1300s/mm2, thereby minimizing systematic b‐value–dependent variability in MD and PSMD estimates. Notably, the MarkVCID consortium's standardized PSMD protocol used a single b‐value of 1000 s/mm2 [45].

The lack of formal DTI harmonization in this study is a limitation, as site and scanner effects can introduce variability that may confound group comparisons [61]. We mitigated this concern by restricting analyses to 3T scanners, applying standardized preprocessing, and confirming no group differences in scanner platform, b‐value, or head motion (Table S4). Global, frontal, temporal, and parietal PSMD were associated with scanner and b‐value (Table S5), but occipital PSMD showed no such association and retained its group difference. Formal harmonization was not feasible due to only five participants scanned on the two Siemens platforms, limiting estimation of scanner effects; however, residual scanner‐related variability may persist.

The DTI data were acquired across multiple scanner platforms with heterogeneous acquisition parameters. Although the DTI‐ALPS index has been shown to be relatively robust across scanners when imaging parameters are fixed, it remains sensitive to changes in TE, number of diffusion‐encoding directions, imaging plane, and head positioning [50, 62]. Moreover, prior work suggests that the DTI‐ALPS index performs best at intermediate b‐values around 700 s/mm2, whereas the commonly used b = 1000 s/mm2 may be suboptimal due to reduced accuracy despite improved precision. In our study, b‐values were either 1000 or 1300 s/mm2, which may have contributed to the lack of association of DTI‐ALPS index in differentiating AD with CAA from AD without CAA [63].

DTI‐ALPS index has inherent methodological limitations. The index quantifies water diffusivity along the presumed direction of perivascular spaces rather than within them [27], and perivascular spaces make up only a small fraction of total white matter volume, making it unlikely that DTI‐ALPS can reliably distinguish perivascular water movement from other directional diffusion, such as along fiber tracts [64]. In addition, the ALPS index is strongly influenced by white matter geometry—including fiber crossings, axonal dispersion, and undulations—which can inflate radial diffusivity asymmetry and confound interpretation of the ALPS index as a direct marker of glymphatic function [65]. Furthermore, the ROI‐based approach used to calculate the ALPS index evaluates only a single region adjacent to the lateral ventricle body, potentially missing perivascular diffusivity changes in other brain regions [66]. Hence, these considerations suggest that DTI‐ALPS should be interpreted as reflecting predominant water diffusivity in the radial direction at the lateral ventricle level rather than as a direct measure of glymphatic system function, consistent with the recent recommendations of the method's original developers [23].

All participants in this study were white, which limits the generalizability of our findings to other racial and ethnic groups. Future work should include more diverse populations to determine whether these imaging biomarkers perform similarly across different demographic groups.

In conclusion, higher global and occipital PSMD may help identify AD with concomitant CAA. Associations between global and parietal PSMD with poorer language performance were stronger in AD with CAA than without CAA. Higher mean and right DTI‐ALPS indices were associated with preserved language function.
