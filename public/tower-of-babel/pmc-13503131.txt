Impact of lung allocation policy reforms on center volume redistribution and survival after lung transplantation
Authors: Michael T Olson, Sreeja Biswas Roy, Vladislav Muldiiarov, Mark Shacker, Lara W Schaheen, Rajat Walia, Ross M Bremner, Michael A Smith, Ashwini Arjuna
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractIntroductionRecent policy reforms, including the 2017 implementation of a 250 nautical mile (nm) donor sharing radius and the 2023 introduction of the Composite Allocation Score (CAS), have substantially changed lung allocation in the United States. The effects of these policy reforms on the structural organization of lung transplant programs and associated outcomes remain unclear.MethodsAdult lung transplant recipients in the OPTN/UNOS STAR registry between January 1, 2015 and December 31, 2025 were identified. Transplants performed during the COVID-19 pandemic period (March 2020 to June 2021) were excluded. Allocation eras were defined as lung allocation score-donor service area (LAS-DSA) (January 2015 to November 2017), LAS-250 nm (November 2017 to March 2023), and CAS (March 2023 to December 2025). National concentration of transplant activity across centers was assessed using the Herfindahl–Hirschman Index (HHI) and Gini coefficient. Center volume dynamics were evaluated, and 1-year survival was assessed using Kaplan–Meier and multivariable Cox regression analysis.ResultsAmong 25,460 lung transplant recipients, national transplant activity became progressively less concentrated across eras. HHI declined from 0.0273 in the LAS-DSA era to 0.0237 in the CAS era (p<0.001), while the Gini coefficient decreased from 0.436 to 0.384 (p<0.001). Median annualized center transplant volume increased from 23.8/year in the LAS-DSA era to 39.8/year in the CAS era (p=0.051). Unadjusted 1-year survival improved across eras (89.2% LAS-DSA, 89.3% LAS-250 nm, 91.7% CAS; log-rank p<0.001). In adjusted analysis, the CAS era was associated with lower mortality than the LAS-250 nm era (aHR 0.74, 95% CI 0.65-0.83; p<0.001).ConclusionsLung allocation reform significantly redistributed transplant activity across U.S. centers while improving survival.Keywords: composite allocation score, lung allocation score, lung transplantation, organ allocation, transplant center volume

Major policy reforms have reshaped lung allocation in the United States (U.S.) over the past decade. Historically, lung allocation relied on donor service areas (DSAs), which geographically restricted organ distribution and contributed to regional disparities in access to transplant.1, 2 In November 2017, the Organ Procurement and Transplantation Network (OPTN) implemented an emergency policy change replacing DSA-based allocation with a 250 nautical mile (nm) donor sharing radius.3 More recently, in March 2023, the Composite Allocation Score (CAS) was introduced as part of the continuous distribution framework.4 Under this system, the fixed geographic sharing radius was eliminated and replaced with a distance-based allocation score that incorporates medical urgency, predicted post-transplant survival, candidate biology, patient access, and logistical efficiency.5 This policy change formally marked the transition away from the Lung Allocation Score (LAS) era.

While these policy changes were designed to improve fairness and access to transplantation, they may also alter the structural organization of transplant programs. Redistribution of donor organs across broader geographic regions may influence center-level transplant volumes, potentially affecting program sustainability and outcomes. Prior studies have demonstrated associations between center volume, postoperative complications, and survival after lung transplantation, with thresholds anywhere between 20 and 40 annual transplants cited as clinically meaningful.6, 7, 8, 9, 10, 11

However, the effects of recent allocation reforms on the distribution of transplant activity across centers remain incompletely characterized. Accordingly, we examined changes in national transplant concentration, center transplant volume, and post-transplant survival.

Materials and methods

Data source and study population

This retrospective cohort study utilized data from the United Network for Organ Sharing (UNOS) Standard Transplant Analysis and Research (STAR) thoracic dataset, which includes all thoracic organ transplants performed in the U.S. All adult (≥18 years) lung transplants performed between January 1, 2015 and December 31, 2025 were included. January 1, 2015 was chosen as a start date for this study window because it approximately coincided with the 2015 LAS model revision by the Thoracic Organ Transplantation Committee.12 Exclusion criteria were: missing transplant date, transplants performed outside the study window, pediatric lung transplants (age <18 years), non-lung thoracic transplants, en bloc and lobar transplants, and multi-organ transplants. To minimize confounding from pandemic-related disruptions to transplant activity and donor availability, transplants performed during the COVID-19 pandemic period (March 1, 2020 through June 30, 2021) were excluded from the primary analysis, but retained for a sensitivity analysis.

Allocation eras

Three allocation eras were defined based on major national policy changes. The LAS-DSA era was defined as transplants from January 1, 2015 to November 23, 2017, the LAS-250 nm era was defined as transplants from November 24, 2017 to March 8, 2023, and lastly the CAS era was defined as transplants from March 9, 2023 to December 31, 2025 (with exclusion of the COVID-19 era as defined above).

Definitions of structural metrics

National concentration of lung transplant activity across centers was quantified using the Herfindahl-Hirschman Index (HHI) and Gini coefficient, 2 standard measures of market concentration and inequality, respectively. The HHI was calculated as the sum of the squared market shares of each transplant center, where market share represents the proportion of national lung transplant volume performed by a given center during a specified time interval. HHI values range from 0 to 1, with higher values indicating greater concentration of transplant activity among fewer centers. Conversely, lower HHI values reflect a more distributed system. The Gini coefficient was used to quantify inequality in the distribution of transplant volume across centers. The Gini coefficient ranges from 0 to 1, where 0 represents perfect equality (all centers have similar transplant volumes) and 1 represents maximal inequality (all transplants are performed at a single center). Lower Gini values indicate a more equitable distribution.

Center transplant volume was defined as the number of lung transplants performed at each transplant program during a given year. For comparison of eras of differing durations, annualized center volume was calculated by dividing the total number of transplants performed at a center during an era by the era’s duration in years. A threshold of 33 annualized lung transplants per year was used to categorize center volume and characterize volume dynamics. This threshold was selected based on the most contemporary UNOS registry analysis characterizing the nonlinear relationship between center volume and 1-year survival.6 Yang et al.6 demonstrated incremental survival improvements in centers with up to 33 transplants per year, with no additional benefit observed beyond that threshold. To evaluate dynamics for mid-to-larger volume programs, we used a threshold of 50 annualized transplants per year, which corresponded approximately to the upper quartile of volumes across centers during the baseline LAS-DSA era.

Outcomes

The primary outcome was the national concentration of lung transplant activity across centers, evaluated to quantify redistribution of transplant volume following major allocation policy changes, particularly the CAS. Secondary outcomes evaluated how the national redistribution of transplant activity was associated with changes in center-level transplant volume and 1-year post-transplant survival, compared across allocation eras and across center volume trajectories. Lastly, we evaluated waitlist mortality across allocation eras as a tertiary analysis.

Statistical analysis

Recipient characteristics, donor characteristics, and transplant procedure types were summarized using standard descriptive statistics. Continuous variables were reported as median with interquartile range (IQR), and categorical variables were reported as counts and percentages. Differences in characteristics across allocation eras were evaluated using the Kruskal–Wallis test for continuous variables and the chi-square test for categorical variables. Comparisons across eras were descriptive; pairwise comparisons were not performed.

National concentration of lung transplant activity was evaluated using quarterly estimates of the HHI and Gini coefficient. Temporal changes in these metrics were analyzed using interrupted time-series analysis with segmented regression, with interruption points corresponding to the implementation of the 250 nm allocation policy in November 2017 and the introduction of CAS in March 2023.

Center-level structural metrics were summarized by allocation era, including median annualized center volume, the number of active transplant centers, and the proportion of centers with fewer than 33 and 50 annualized lung transplants. To evaluate center volume dynamics across eras, centers were classified according to volume trajectory relative to the 33- and 50-transplant thresholds, and transitions between volume categories were visualized using a Sankey plot.

Unadjusted 1-year post-transplant survival was estimated using the Kaplan–Meier method with administrative censoring at 365 days after lung transplant and compared across allocation eras and across center volume trajectory groups. Median follow-up per era was estimated using the reverse Kaplan–Meier method. Multivariable Cox proportional hazards regression was used to evaluate adjusted associations between allocation era, center volume trajectory, and mortality. Covariates were selected a priori based on clinical relevance and included: allocation era, center volume trajectory group, recipient demographics (e.g., age, sex, body mass index, race/ethnicity), primary diagnosis group, pre-transplant support measures (e.g., ECMO and mechanical ventilation at transplant), and donor/transplant factors (e.g., donor age, ischemic time, and single versus bilateral lung transplant). A complete-case analysis was performed for multivariable modeling. Missing data were minimal (<1% across modeled variables). Robust standard errors were clustered at the transplant center level to account for within-center correlation.

Waitlist outcomes were compared across allocation eras using Pearson's chi-square tests. Person-time was calculated from listing to final waitlist disposition, and event rates were expressed per 100 person-years. Freedom from waitlist death or clinical deterioration was also estimated using the Kaplan–Meier method and compared across allocation eras.

All tests were two-sided, with p-values <0.05 considered statistically significant. All statistical analyses were performed using R Studio version 4.5.2 (R Foundation for Statistical Computing, Vienna, Austria).

Ethical and regulatory considerations

This study used publicly available, de-identified national registry data provided by UNOS under a data-use agreement. All study activities complied with the OPTN data use policy. Institutional review board approval was not required for this study, as it met the criteria for exempt human subjects’ research. This study was designed, conducted, and reported in accordance with the Strengthening the Reporting of Observational Studies in Epidemiology guidelines for observational research.13

Results

Cohort derivation

A total of 237,532 thoracic transplant records were identified in the OPTN/UNOS STAR registry. Sequential exclusions included: missing transplant date (n=75,671), transplants performed outside the study window (n=90,211), pediatric transplant recipients (n=5593), non-lung thoracic transplants (n=37,006), en bloc and lobar transplants (n=320), multi-organ transplants (n=270), and lastly, lung transplants performed during the COVID-19 pandemic period (n=3271). The final analytic cohort included 25,460 adult lung-only transplant recipients performed at identifiable U.S. transplant centers during the study period: 6372 (25.0%) recipients during LAS-DSA, 10,022 (39.4%) recipients during LAS-250 nm, and 9066 (35.6%) during CAS.

Recipient, donor, and transplant characteristics by era

Recipient characteristics across allocation eras are summarized in Table 1. Median recipient age increased modestly over time, from 60 years in the LAS-DSA era to 63 years in the CAS era (p<0.001). The distribution of underlying diagnoses also shifted, with a greater proportion of recipients transplanted for restrictive lung disease in later eras. Racial and ethnic composition changed modestly across eras, with an increasing proportion of Hispanic and Asian recipients. The use of extracorporeal membrane oxygenation (ECMO) as a bridge to transplant increased over time, and median waitlist times declined across eras.

Transplant procedure types also changed significantly. The proportion of bilateral lung transplants increased from 72.8% in the LAS-DSA era to 76.5% in LAS-250 nm and 83.2% in the CAS era, whereas single lung transplants decreased from 27.2% to 23.5% and 16.8%, respectively (p<0.001).

National concentration of lung transplant activity

Measures of national concentration of lung transplant activity declined progressively across allocation eras. The HHI decreased from 0.0273 in the LAS-DSA era to 0.0258 in the LAS-250 nm era and 0.0237 in the CAS era (p<0.001). Similarly, the Gini coefficient decreased from 0.436 to 0.400 and 0.384, respectively (p<0.001). The proportion of national transplant volume performed by the ten highest-volume centers also declined across eras, from 38.8% in the LAS-DSA era to 35.6% in the LAS-250 nm era and 33.4% in the CAS era (p<0.001). In interrupted time-series analysis, the LAS-250 nm era was associated with a significant decrease in the quarterly Gini slope (β=−0.00550 per quarter, p=0.007), indicating reduced inequality in the distribution of transplant activity across centers following implementation of the 250 nm allocation policy. In contrast, the CAS era was associated with a significant positive change in quarterly Gini slope (β=0.00791 per quarter, p<0.001), suggesting a modest rebound in concentration following the substantial decentralization observed during the LAS-250 nm era. Despite this rebound, concentration remained lower during the CAS era than in prior eras (Figure 1, Figure 2). Center-level structural metrics

The number of active lung transplant centers increased from 66 during the LAS-DSA era to 70 during the LAS-250 nm era and 71 during the CAS era (p=0.589). Median annualized center transplant volume increased across eras (p=0.051), from 23.8/year (IQR 13.9-45.9) during LAS-DSA to 29.7/year (IQR 15.4-47.2) during LAS-250 nm and 39.8/year (IQR 18.3-67.3) during CAS. The proportion of centers performing <33 annualized lung transplants did not differ significantly across eras (p=0.094), but declined from 41 (62.1%) during LAS-DSA to 38 (54.3%) during LAS-250 nm and 31 (43.7%) during CAS. In contrast, the proportion of centers performing at least 50 annualized lung transplants differed significantly across eras (p=0.011), increasing from 16 centers (24.2%) during LAS-DSA and 16 centers (22.9%) during LAS-250 nm to 31 centers (43.7%) during CAS.

Center volume trajectories

LAS-DSA to LAS-250 nm

Among 65 centers active in both eras, 32 centers (49.2%) remained persistently low-volume (<33 annualized transplants/year) between the LAS-DSA and LAS-250 nm eras, while 8 centers (12.3%) grew from <33 to ≥33 annualized transplants/year. Only 2 centers (3.2%) declined from ≥33 to <33 annualized transplants/year during this period, whereas 23 centers (35.4%) remained persistently ≥33 (Figure 3A). Using a threshold of 50 annualized transplants/year, 46 centers (70.8%) remained persistently <50, 3 centers (4.6%) increased from <50 to ≥50, 3 centers (4.6%) declined from ≥50 to <50, and 13 centers (20.0%) remained persistently ≥50.

LAS-250 nm to CAS

In contrast, transitions between the LAS-250 nm and CAS eras demonstrated substantial upward movement in center volume. Among centers active in both eras (n=66), 8 centers (12.1%) increased from <33 to ≥33 annualized transplants/year and 32 centers (48.5%) remained persistently ≥33. The remaining 26 centers (39.4%) active in both eras were stable at <33 transplants/year. Notably, there were no programs with ≥33 annualized transplants/year that fell to <33 (Figure 3B). Similar findings were observed using a threshold of 50 annualized transplants/year. Fifteen centers (22.7%) increased from <50 to ≥50, 16 centers (24.2%) remained persistently ≥50, and no centers declined below the threshold.

Unadjusted survival

Median follow-up was 107.5 months in the LAS-DSA era, 60.4 months in the LAS-250 nm era, and 11.9 months in the CAS era. The potential for complete 1-year follow-up was available for 100% of recipients in the LAS-DSA and LAS-250 nm eras and 62.7% of recipients in the CAS era. Unadjusted 1-year survival differed across allocation eras (Figure 5A). Estimated survival was 89.2% (95% CI 88.4-90.0) in the LAS-DSA era, 89.3% (95% CI 88.6-89.9) in the LAS-250 nm era, and 91.7% (95% CI 91.1-92.3) in the CAS era (log-rank p<0.001). Within the CAS era, survival varied according to center volume trajectory (Figure 5B). Centers maintaining ≥33 annualized transplants/year demonstrated the highest 1-year survival at 92.2% (95% CI 91.6-92.8), followed by centers transitioning from <33 to ≥33 annualized transplants/year at 90.6% (95% CI 88.8-92.4), while centers that remained <33 annualized transplants/year had the lowest survival at 90.0% (95% CI 88.3-91.7) (log-rank p=0.010).

Multivariable Cox proportional hazards modeling

In adjusted modeling, the CAS era was associated with a 26% lower risk of 1-year mortality compared with the LAS-250 nm era (adjusted hazard ratio [aHR] 0.74, 95% CI 0.65-0.83; p<0.001), while mortality risk was similar between the LAS-DSA and LAS-250 nm eras (aHR 1.10, 95% CI 0.98-1.23; p=0.092). Center-volume trajectory was independently associated with survival. Compared with centers remaining below 33 annualized transplants/year, centers maintaining ≥33 annualized transplants/year demonstrated lower mortality (aHR 0.74, 95% CI 0.63-0.88; p<0.001). Centers transitioning from <33 to ≥33 annualized transplants/year did not demonstrate significantly different mortality compared with persistently low-volume centers (aHR 0.89, 95% CI 0.74-1.07; p=0.222). Additional predictors of mortality included recipient age, diagnosis group, ECMO and mechanical ventilation as a bridge to transplant, donor age, and ischemic time (Table 3).

Waitlist outcomes

The candidate-level waitlist cohort is summarized in Figure S1. Candidate-level waitlist outcomes differed significantly across allocation eras (Table S2). Waitlist mortality declined progressively from 6.9% in the LAS-DSA era to 5.2% in the LAS-250 nm era and 2.3% in the CAS era (p<0.001). When accounting for time at risk, waitlist mortality decreased from 12.7 deaths per 100 person-years during the LAS-250 nm era to 8.8 deaths per 100 person-years during the CAS era, while the rate of waitlist death or clinical deterioration decreased from 24.1 to 17.2 events per 100 person-years. Kaplan–Meier analysis demonstrated significant differences in freedom from waitlist death or clinical deterioration across allocation eras (log-rank p<0.001) (Figure S2). Candidates in the CAS era experienced the highest freedom from adverse waitlist outcomes, whereas outcomes were similar between the LAS-DSA and LAS-250 nm eras over long-term follow-up.

Sensitivity analysis

As a sensitivity analysis, transplants performed during the COVID-19 pandemic period were reincorporated into the LAS-250 nm era, and all structural metrics were recalculated. Inclusion of pandemic-era transplants did not materially alter the observed trends. The HHI still decreased progressively across eras (LAS-250 nm: 0.0233 to CAS: 0.0221), as did the Gini coefficient (0.432-0.420, respectively). Similarly, the proportion of the total national transplant volume performed by the ten highest-volume centers declined from 36.0% in the LAS-250 nm era to 33.4% in the CAS era.

Discussion

In this national analysis of lung transplantation across major recent allocation policy reforms, we observed several important structural and clinical trends. First, lung transplant activity became progressively less concentrated across U.S. centers over time, as demonstrated by declining HHI and Gini coefficient values. Second, this redistribution occurred through expansion across the transplant system, including growth of previously lower-volume (<33 annualized transplants/year) programs as well as continued increases in transplant volume among established high-volume centers. Third, early post-transplant outcomes improved in the CAS era, with higher 1-year survival than prior allocation eras. Finally, center-volume dynamics were independently associated with outcomes; centers maintaining higher transplant volumes demonstrated better survival than persistently low-volume programs. Together, these findings suggest that allocation reforms not only redistributed transplant activity across centers but did so in a way that coincided with improved early post-transplant outcomes.

Changes in recipient and donor characteristics across allocation eras provide important context for interpreting these findings. Recipients transplanted in later eras were modestly older and more frequently had restrictive lung disease, reflecting broader shifts in the lung transplant candidate population. At the same time, the racial and ethnic composition of transplant recipients evolved, with increasing representation of Hispanic and Asian patients. These trends are consistent with prior work suggesting that broader geographic sharing policies may improve access to transplant among historically underrepresented groups.14, 15, 16 Median waitlist time also declined substantially across eras, suggesting that changes in allocation policy may have improved efficiency of organ distribution. Complementing these post-transplant findings, waitlist mortality also declined progressively across allocation eras. The reduction in waitlist mortality, alongside shorter waitlist times, broader racial distributions, and improved post-transplant survival, suggest that, importantly, allocation reform has improved the full spectrum of the transplant pathway.

Donor characteristics similarly evolved, with increases in donor age, procurement distance, ischemic time, and utilization of extended-criteria donors, including DCD and machine-perfused grafts. Notably, older donor age (aHR 1.01 per year) and ischemic time (aHR 1.03 per hour) remained independently associated with mortality in the overall cohort, yet recipients transplanted during the CAS era experienced improved survival despite receiving organs from older donors with substantially longer ischemic times and increased utilization of DCD donors. This apparent paradox likely reflects advances in contemporary lung transplant that have mitigated the historical risks associated with extended-criteria lungs, including improvements in donor management, organ preservation strategies, expanded utilization of ex-vivo lung perfusion, and earlier transplant of recipients prioritized under CAS before ongoing deconditioning. Our group recently showed that in contemporary practice, recipient risk is the predominant driver of graft survival compared to donor risk, and extended-criteria lungs are not associated with a long-term survival penalty in low-risk recipients.17

Importantly, the observed improvements in 1-year survival during the CAS era should also be interpreted in the context of secular trends in lung transplantation. Improvements in recipient outcomes observed during later allocation eras cannot be attributed solely to allocation policy changes. Confounding from temporal improvements in transplant care remains possible, even after adjusting for recipient, donor, and transplant characteristics. Nonetheless, our findings suggest a structural mechanism underlying improved outcomes. Historically, lung transplant activity was concentrated among relatively few high-volume centers; however, we have shown that activity has steadily become less concentrated and more equitable, resulting in expansion of existing programs. Moreover, programs with the highest volumes experienced improved survival compared to low-volume centers.

These findings are particularly relevant in light of the recent OPTN modification increasing the weight assigned to placement efficiency and donor–recipient proximity within the CAS. Although the impact of this revised scoring system on transplant activity distribution remains unknown, our findings demonstrate that allocation policy changes can substantially influence center volume and market concentration. Therefore, the present study provides an important baseline against which the effects of the revised CAS framework can be evaluated. Ongoing monitoring of transplant access, center-level volume distribution, and recipient outcomes following implementation of these policy modifications will be essential to ensure that the gains observed during the initial CAS era are preserved.

Several limitations should be considered when interpreting these findings. First, registry data lack detailed information regarding center-level practices, perioperative management strategies, and institutional resources that may influence outcomes. Second, follow-up in the CAS era remains relatively short because of the recent implementation of the allocation policy and the administrative end of registry follow-up. Although only 62.7% of CAS recipients had the potential for complete 1-year follow-up, Kaplan–Meier methods appropriately accounted for administrative right-censoring, and true loss to follow-up was rare (<1% across all allocation eras). Longer-term follow-up will be important to determine whether the observed improvements in early survival persist. Third, the use of a 33-transplant annual threshold to categorize center experience represents a simplified proxy for program capacity and may not fully capture the complex relationship between institutional experience and outcomes. Lastly, this study cannot comment on whether the observed growth in center volumes was driven by increased donor utilization, increased donor availability, or other factors, and this remains an important area for future study. Despite these limitations, this analysis provides a comprehensive national assessment of structural changes in lung transplantation following allocation reform.

In conclusion, major lung allocation policy reforms were associated with measurable redistribution of transplant activity across U.S. centers. Structural analysis demonstrated progressive decentralization of transplant volume across allocation eras. During the CAS era, a substantial proportion of centers increased transplant volumes above clinically meaningful thresholds. Importantly, these changes occurred alongside improved 1-year post-transplant survival compared with prior allocation eras. Ongoing work is needed to further elucidate the mechanisms underlying these improvements in early survival and to determine whether these gains persist in longer-term outcomes. Continued evaluation of allocation policy and center-level performance will be important to understand the durability and drivers of these trends.
