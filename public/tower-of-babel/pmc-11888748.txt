Effect of Polydopamine Nanoparticle-Induced Surface Modification on Demineralized Dentin for Enhanced Functional Remineralization
Authors: Manavalan Madhana Madhubala, Sekar Mahalaxmi
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTBackground:Development of biomimetic nanomaterials for remineralization therapy is an important strategy in minimally invasive dentistry. The size of the therapeutic agent has a great influence on bioavailability, penetration ability, and larger surface area for interaction in hard tissue remineralization through the nonclassical crystallization pathway. This study aimed to comparatively evaluate the remineralization-inducing potential of polydopamine (PDA) and polydopamine nanoparticles (nPDs) on artificially demineralized dentin.Methodology:nPDs were prepared using commercially procured dopamine hydrochloride by the wet precipitation method. Obtained particles were characterized using scanning electron microscopy–energy-dispersive X-ray spectroscopy (SEM-EDX), transmission electron microscopy, particle size analysis and zeta potential determination via dynamic light scattering with a laser particle analyser, X-ray diffraction, X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy, and attenuated total reflectance Fourier transform infrared spectroscopy. The cytocompatibility of nPD and PDA was assessed by (3-(4,5-dimethythiazol-2-yl) 2,5-diphenyl tetrazolium bromide) assay on human dental pulpal stem cell lines. A total of 66 demineralized dentin slabs of 3 mm × 3 mm × 1 mm dimension were immersed in 2 mg/ml of freshly prepared PDA and nPD solutions for 12 hours, followed by reimmersion in calcium and phosphate solution at 37°C days for 10 days. Samples immersed in deionized water were used as a control group. All the specimens were subjected to various remineralization testing methods using SEM-EDX, Vicker’s microhardness, and micro-Raman spectroscopic analysis. The Shapiro–Wilk test was used to assess the normality of the distribution, followed by one-way ANOVA for intergroup analysis and post hoc Tukey’s test for multiple comparisons within groups.Results:nPD exhibited a nanospherical morphology with positive zeta potential. nPD revealed the polymeric amorphous nature with characteristic functional groups and exhibited >80% relative cell viability. nPD promoted superior remineralization ability by the formation of hydroxyapatite closely mirroring the Ca/P ratio of natural dentin with a hardness value significantly closer to the baseline, which was further evidenced by higher-intensity peaks of phosphate, amide, and proline on micro-Raman spectroscopic analysis.Conclusion:Dentin remineralization is more strongly facilitated by nanometric PDA than PDA, which has an enhanced functional remineralization effect.

INTRODUCTION

There is a noticeable increase in oral health awareness among many individuals, even in developing countries. Maintaining the structural integrity of dentin, an essential component of the tooth, is necessary for dental health. Dentinal lesions develop due to normal and pathological tooth wear and can present as abfraction, abrasion, or erosion.[1] The clinical presentation can vary depending on the nature and intensity of the underlying etiological factors, which is initiated by the dissolution of the mineral phase of dentin, gradually exposing its internal collagen matrix. This condition occurs when the balance between dentin demineralization and remineralization is disrupted, resulting in a dominance of demineralization processes.[1] Several agents, such as fluoride, casein phosphopeptide-amorphous calcium phosphate (CPP-ACP), beta-tricalcium phosphate, tetra calcium phosphate, nanohydroxyapatite, and bioactive glass, are widely used to promote dentin remineralization.[2] However, dentin biomineralization achieved by these materials is superficial, with a limited infiltration depth without functional remineralization.[3]

Biomimetic mineralization involves a specialized mechanism in which the residual collagen matrix serves as a template, facilitating the deposition of minerals within the fibrils under the regulated influence of biomolecules.[3] The primary requirement for functional remineralization is the presence of remnant apatite seed crystallites in the exposed dentin. Consequently, the epitaxial growth of hydroxyapatite (HAp) crystals takes place over the existing seed crystallites facilitated by noncollagenous proteins (NCPs), which are known for inducing heterogeneous nucleation within a demineralized collagen matrix.[345] However, with the currently available biomaterials, achieving HAp nucleation and growth in inter- and intrafibrillar areas with a hierarchical structure and native dentin formation is still questionable.

Current research focuses on bioactive materials that mimic NCPs which can actively induce biomineralization of dentin, leading to HAp crystallization within the collagen matrix at an intrafibrillar level in an ordered alignment.[6] Over a decade, there has been extensive utilization of the natural phenomenon of mussel adhesion for modifying biomaterial surfaces.[7] Dopamine serves as a synthetic counterpart to the specialized adhesive foot protein Mefp-5 (Mytilus edulis foot protein-5), which is derived from mussels. The oxidative polymerization of dopamine in aqueous solution results in the spontaneous formation of polydopamine (PDA). It regulates the interfacial energy of the substrate due to the catechol groups present on its surface by functioning as an effective surface modifier.[8] It strongly adheres to any substrate even under wet conditions.[9] PDA-coated surfaces enable seamless integration of HAp on a wide range of substrates, aligning with the c-axis and resembling the natural growth of biominerals. When tested on titanium dental implants, PDA-coated surfaces efficiently initiated calcium phosphate nucleation rapidly with improved osseointegration.[10] PDA has been shown to promote dentin mineralization by facilitating the attachment to calcium ions and concentrating them at the interface, thereby accelerating the formation of HAp acting as the NCP analog.[11]

It is known that nanoparticles have emerged as a great tool for modifying biomaterials to improve tissue assembly and functionality. Recently, PDA nanoparticles (nPDs) have also been used extensively in drug delivery and functional modification of various biomaterial surfaces.[12] In comparison to PDA, nanoparticle-deposited polydopamine (nPD) offers a larger specific surface area, facilitating interactions with a greater number of cells and biofunctional molecules. Additionally, nPD demonstrates enhanced anti-inflammatory, antioxidant, and photothermal conversion properties, along with improved adhesiveness and hydrophilicity.[1314]

While significant progress has been made in multiple nPD applications in the biomedical field, their application in dentin remineralization has not yet been attempted. Hence, the objective of this study is to comparatively evaluate the remineralization-inducing potential of PDA and nPD pretreatment on demineralized dentin surfaces using surface and elemental analysis, microhardness evaluation, and micro-Raman analysis on chemical changes. The null hypothesis is that there is no difference in the dentin remineralization effect between PDA and nPD.

MATERIALS AND METHODS

The study protocol was duly submitted to the Institutional Review Board of SRM Dental College, SRMIST, Chennai, Tamil Nadu, India, and approval was obtained (SRMDC/IRB/2019/1). Dopamine hydrochloride, ammonium hydroxide (L-DOPA) (38–40%), and all other chemicals employed in this study were procured from Merck India, Mumbai, India, with analytical grade.

Preparation of PDA

L-DOPA (2 mg/ml) and 12.11 mg of Tris buffer powder were carefully measured using a digital electronic balance. The powders were combined in a beaker containing 10 mL of distilled water, and the mixture was stirred using a magnetic stirrer operating at 1000 rpm (Remi Equipment, Mumbai, Maharashtra, India) for 10 minutes to achieve uniform dispersion of the powder in the solution. The solution was sealed for 30 h to enable oxidative polymerization, resulting in the formation of PDA. The prepared PDA solution was directly used for experimentation at the required volumes.[11]

Preparation and characterization of nPD

Nanoparticles were prepared according to the previous method demonstrated by Liu et al.[15] Ammonia solution (0.8 mL, NH4OH, 28–30%) was combined with deionized water (90 mL) and ethanol (40 mL) under gentle stirring for 10 minutes at room temperature. Following this, dopamine hydrochloride (0.5 g) was dissolved in 10 mL of deionized water and added to the mixture. The combined solution was stirred using a magnetic stirrer for another 10 minutes. The mixture was kept at room temperature, and the reaction was allowed to occur for 30 h. Subsequently, the solution was centrifuged for 15 minutes at 12000 rpm and washed thrice with deionized water. The resulting material was vacuum-desiccated at 60°C, and nPD particles were obtained. To prepare an experimental nPD slurry for remineralization application, 1.8 g of nPD was mixed with 0.3 mL of distilled water using a magnetic stirrer for 5 minutes.

The obtained nPD particles were examined via scanning electron microscopy (SEM) (Apreo S; Thermo Scientific, MA) and transmission electron microscopy (TEM) (JEOL-JEM 2100 Plus, JEOL, Japan) to determine the surface morphology and particle size, respectively. Approximately 2 mg of the nPD particles was dispersed in 40 mL of phosphate-buffered saline (pH approximately 7) and sonicated in an Ultrasonicator (Hielscher UP100H tip Sonicator) for 10 min. This solution was used for particle size analysis and zeta potential determination via dynamic light scattering (DLS) with a laser particle analyzer (Zetasizer, Malvern, UK). The elemental composition was analyzed using an X-ray diffractometer (XRD) (D8 Advance, Bruker, MA, USA) in reflection mode with Cu Kα radiation (λ = _1.5406 Å). The data were analyzed in the 2θ range from 5 to 90° with a scanning step of 2° per minute. The functional groups of nPD were analyzed by performing attenuated total reflectance Fourier transform infrared (ATR-FTIR) spectroscopy (Alpha II, Bruker, MA, USA) in the spectral range of 400–4000 cm–1 and at a spectral resolution of 4 cm–1 using the KBr pellet technique. Chemical analysis was also done using X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy (XPS) (PerkinElmer 5400 small-spot ESCA spectrometer).

Dentin slab preparation

The collection of tooth samples followed the ethical standards given according to the 1964 Declaration of Helsinki amendments. The teeth were extracted after providing written informed consent. Using G*Power 3.1 software and data from a pilot study, the sample size for the research was calculated. The analysis with a mean effect size of 1.0 ± 0.65, an 85% power, and a 5% significance level (P < 0.05) required 22 samples per group. A total of 66 human mandibular molars free of caries, cracks, and developmental defects were collected and immersed in 0.1% thymol solution until use. All the teeth were decoronated at the cemento-enamel junction using a diamond disc under copious water cooling. The occlusal surface of each tooth was cut perpendicular to the long axis to expose the dentin, and one more cut was made 2.5 mm apical to the exposed dentin surface and dentin slabs of 3 mm × 3 mm × 1 mm dimension were obtained. The exposed, coronal dentin surface was polished with 600, 800, 1000, and 2000-grit abrasive papers (Buehler EcoMet 5, Lake Bluff, IL, USA) under running water and ultrasonically cleaned for 10 min between each grit (Ultrasound Aczet CUB 22, Ltd., Mumbai, India) to remove the smear layer. Representative intact dentin samples were subjected to surface and elemental evaluation using SEM-EDX and chemical analysis using micro-Raman spectroscopy (Horiba LabRAM HR, Montpellier, France). After marking on the pulpal side of the slab for easy identification of the experimental coronal surface, all the dentin slabs were subjected to evaluation of baseline microhardness (BMH) values using a microindenter (DUH-211; Zwick, Germany).

Cell proliferation assay for cytotoxicity

Human dental pulp stem cells (hDPSCs) were used for in vitro cytotoxicity evaluation. They were cultivated in alpha-minimal essential medium (α-MEM) supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum, 100 U/mL penicillin, 100 μg/mL streptomycin, and 0.25 μg/mL amphotericin B (Sigma-Aldrich Corporation, St. Louis, MO) at 37°C in a 95% humidified atmosphere and 5% CO2. Approximately 3 × 104 cells were seeded in the well plate (approximately 25% confluency) in 1 mL of cell medium containing extracted samples in different dilutions (1:1, 1:2, 1:4, and 1:8 ratio), and the plates were incubated at 37°C in a humidified chamber. The sample-extracted medium was replaced every 24 h, and untreated hDPSCs served as controls. The cell viability was assessed using 3-dimethylthiazol-2,5-diphenyltetrazolium bromide (MTT) assay after 24 h. Briefly, the culture medium was removed and 40 μL of MTT solution (5 mg/mL in PBS) was added to the wells for 4 h and 100 µL of dimethyl sulfoxide (DMSO) was further added to each well to solubilize the crystals. The cell number was determined from the absorbance of formazan at 570 nm using a microplate reader (Read Well Touch, India). The values were obtained as optical density (OD), and relative cell viability was expressed as a percentage of absorbance relative to the untreated cells. All experiments were conducted in triplicates.

Experimental procedure

The demineralization and remineralization protocols for the experimental samples were followed according to de Lima et al.[16] Briefly, dentin slabs were acid-etched by applying 37% phosphoric acid with a disposable brush on the dentin surface for 1 minute and rinsed thoroughly with deionized water. After this, all the dentin slabs were subjected to hardness evaluation to obtain the microhardness value of demineralized dentin (DMH) testing. Then all the dentin slabs were randomly categorized into three groups (n = 22) based on the experimental immersion treatment: group 1, deionized water (DI); group 2, PDA; and group 3, nPD. All the dentin slabs were immersed in their respective experimental solutions for 12 hours at 37°C. Meanwhile, a required amount of calcification solution (CS) was prepared by mixing 1.55 M phosphate (KH2PO4), 1 mg/L fluoride (NaF), 2.58 mM calcium (CaCl2·2H2O), and 180 mM NaCl, followed by buffering with the addition of 50 mM trihydroxymethyl aminomethane Tris-hydrochloric acid.(11) The pH of the solution was stabilized at 7.4 ± 0.1 using 0.1 M HCl and 0.1 M NaOH. The solution was stored at 4°C before use.[16] All the dentin samples were placed separately in closed polyethylene tubes containing 10 mL of CS. The tubes were placed in a shaking incubator at 37°C for 10 days, during which CS was replenished every day. On the 10th day, the samples were cleaned thoroughly with deionized water and completely dehydrated. After the incubation period, the dentin samples were analyzed by SEM-EDX (n = 5), VMH evaluation (n = 12), and micro-Raman spectroscopy (n = 5).

Surface and elemental analysis using SEM-EDX

The surface morphology of the samples was observed under SEM. A groove was made on the dentin samples, which were then split into two halves. In one half of each disc, the occlusal surface was observed to assess the extent of tubule occlusion. The longitudinal section was examined to evaluate the penetration of intratubular precipitates. The samples were gradually dehydrated using ethanol till a critical point was reached and then sputter-coated with gold. After drying, they were mounted on aluminum stubs. The scanning procedure was then conducted using SEM operated at 20 kV. The analysis was performed at magnifications of 5000×, 10,000×, and 50,000× to examine the surface morphology and elemental composition in detail. Quantitative elemental analysis of Ca and P and Ca/P ratio was assessed using EDX.

Surface microhardness evaluation

The microhardness values of all the dentin slabs were measured after remineralization (RMH) using a VMH tester. The indentations were made at three locations at a distance of at least 100 μm between each indent with a load of 10g with a dwell time of 20 s. The mean of the three values was averaged to obtain the microhardness value for each specimen. The consecutive set of indentations was carefully placed to avoid overlapping with the previous indentations. Finally, all the values of BMH, DMH, and RMH of each sample of all the groups were tabulated for statistical analysis.

Micro-Raman spectroscopic analysis

The chemical composition of amides and calcium phosphate elements on the dentin surface (n = 5) was analyzed using a micro-Raman spectrometer. Raman spectra were obtained with a 532 nm laser source at an intensity of 25 mW. The spectra were recorded in the range of 400–3000 cm-¹ with a spectral resolution of 4 cm-¹ using a step size of 1 μm and an integration time of 8 s per exposure. Measurements were taken at three distinct locations, and the average intensity for each specimen was determined. The data were imported into Origin software (OriginLab, Northampton, MA) and plotted as Raman shift versus mean intensity. The original spectra were subjected to baseline correction and smoothing to remove laser-induced fluorescence. Both the crystalline and organic phases of dentin were analyzed by examining the spectroscopic peaks.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analysis was done with SPSS software, version 22.0 (IBM SPSS Statistics; IBM Corp.). The Shapiro–Wilk test was utilized to assess the normality of the distribution. As the results exhibited normal distribution, parametric statistical tests, specifically one-way ANOVA for intergroup analysis and multiple comparisons post hoc Tukey’s test for intragroup analysis, were employed. The significance level was set at P < 0.05.

Surface and elemental analysis using SEM-EDX

The surface of intact dentin revealed intact dentinal tubules occluded with homogeneous crystal deposits covering most of its surface and an intertubular dentin structure with a residual smear layer adsorbed on the surface [Figure 6a]. Conversely, demineralized dentin showed larger and more open dentinal tubules, with a few mineral contents on its surface and with the absence of peritubular or interubular dentin [Figure 6b and c]. On longitudinal examination/cross-sectional SEM (CSSEM), the dentinal tubules appeared more prominent and open due to the removal of the surrounding mineralized matrix, and the highly mineralized peritubular dentinal lining of the tubules was partially or completely lost, contributing to the increased tubule size and porosity [Figure 7a]. However, postremineralization samples showed dispersed HAp crystals deposited on the dentin surface and precipitated crystals within the dentin tubules. On comparative evaluation, dentin immersed in DI water showed considerably fewer crystal precipitates [Figure 6d-f], with dentinal tubules partially filled with mineral precipitates with less depth and uniformity on CESEM [Figure 7b]. In nPD-treated samples, dentinal tubules were completely occluded by well-defined crystals with dense and homogeneous intertubular precipitation of HAp and intrafibrillar mineral deposits [Figure 6j-l]. In PDA-treated dentin, the degree of crystal precipitation decreased with partial circumferential deposition of mineral crystals in the intertubular space [Figure 6g-i]. On CESEM [Figure 7d], the nPD group showed more of spherical agglomerates of precipitation interconnected with each other with a crystallographic pattern than the PDA group. In PDA, dentinal tubules were partially occluded by the HAp crystals with less density and homogeneity [Figure 6c].

Micro-Raman spectroscopic analysis

In intact dentin, the observed peaks were present at 430, 530, 960, and 1070 cm-1, attributed to phosphates (PO43-) and carbonates (CO32-), respectively. The peaks corresponding to amide I and III at 1665 cm-1 and 1242 cm-1, as well as the peak associated with CH2 expression at 1452 cm-1, were attributed to the organic collagen matrix phase [Figure 9]. In demineralized samples, there was a significant decrease in both the inorganic and collagen phases, as evidenced by lower spectral heights of the phosphate, amide I and III, and CH2 peaks.[1920] However, a notable increase in the intensity of PO43- signals was observed in the nPD, PDA, and control groups. There was a substantial difference in the intensity of phosphate (PO43-) or carbonate (CO32-) peaks between the PDA and control groups. However, the highest intensity peaks for both the inorganic and collagenous phases were observed in samples treated with nPD compared to those in the other groups. In addition, a characteristic peak around 870 cm-1 attributed to proline and hydroxyproline content of dentinal collagen was seen to be more pronounced in nPD than PDA, depicting its effect on the collagen protective effect. The apatite-to-matrix ratio was calculated by determining the ratio between the intensity peaks of 960 cm-1 (PO43-) to 1452 cm-1 (CH2 band), which varied among all the test groups (3.29 and 1.32) for intact and demineralized dentin, and 1.52, 1.21, and 1.90 for control, PDA, and nPD-treated dentin, respectively.

DISCUSSION

The preservation of dentinal tissue is an important area of concern for restoring the biomechanical properties of teeth. Conventional dentin mineralization involves the classical pathway of crystallization with surface deposition of large calcium phosphate on the collagen substrate of dentin.[21] Such simple deposition of minerals prevents the replication of the exact arrangement of apatite within the collagen and hence prevents long-term stable mineralization.[2223]

Biomimetic remineralization is a concept that focuses on the quality of apatite precipitation within the collagen fibrils of demineralized tissue rather than surface crystallization.[3] The organic content of dentin mainly initiates and regulates mineralization processes by nucleation and growth of HAp crystals through their functional domains. The development of various NCP analogs that have better interactions with organic substrates is required to induce mineral deposition on par with the native structure, organization, and functionality of the material.[624]

The concept of dentin biomimetic remineralization includes the activity of metastable amorphous calcium phosphate (ACP) nano precursors facilitated by the stabilizing influence of NCPs in hard tissues.[25] The NCP analogs initiate the formation of prenucleation clusters which subsequently aggregate to generate amorphous calcium phosphate nanoparticles. These nanoparticles localize within the intrafibrillar regions of the collagen fibrils. Following this, there is a crystalline alignment of HAp deposition within the interstitial gap zones of the intrafibrillar regions of collagen molecules.[26] PDA significantly induces the degree of mineralization in demineralized dentin.[1027] However, this is the first study to comparatively evaluate nPD and PDA as an NCP analog to determine its effect on the remineralization of demineralized dentin.

nPD is a biocompatible nanocarrier that can be used within the therapeutic window to enhance bioactivity.[2829] In this study, nanoparticle-deposited polydopamine (nPD) was synthesized via the oxidative self-polymerization of dopamine for 24 h in a mixed solution consisting of water, ammonia, and ethanol at a pH of 12.5. nPD comprises covalently linked dihydroxyindole, indoledione, and dopamine monomers.[17] In alkaline pH environments, dopamine undergoes a transformation into dopamine quinone, which then irreversibly undergoes intramolecular cyclization through 1,4 Michael addition. This leads to the conversion of dopamine quinone into leuko-dopamine chrome, which further oxidizes and rearranges to form 5,6-dihydroxyindole, as hypothesized by Hong and coworkers.[30]

The presence of an indole group was confirmed by the FTIR analysis, which showed absorption peaks at 1595 cm−1 and 1514 cm-1, confirming the successful preparation of nPD. Various characterization techniques confirmed the nanocrystalline form of PDA as evidenced by SEM and TEM analysis with a positive zeta potential. Preparations characterized by a high absolute value of zeta potential demonstrate stability against coagulation. This stability arises from the increased repulsive electrostatic force among particles, which is a consequence of the higher zeta potential.[31]

XPS analysis of the nanoparticles provides information on the elemental composition and chemical and electronic states of the atoms within a material. Figure 4b presents the deconvoluted C1s, O1s, and N1s spectra along with the corresponding binding energy spectra. The C/N/O ratio of the nPD was found to be 13.4:1.0:4.4 (C/O ≈ 3.5), closely matching the theoretical composition of dopamine, which is C13NO4.[32] The C1s spectrum includes a peak at 284−285.5 eV, attributed to C−H, aromatic, and adventitious carbon; a peak at 285.5−287.5 eV, associated with carbon bonded to nitrogen or hydroxyl groups; and a smaller peak at 287.5−289.0 eV, corresponding to oxidized carbon, specifically carbonyl carbon in the quinone form of the catecholate moiety.[33] The N1s spectrum shows peaks at 399 eV, indicative of pyridinic or aromatic nitrogen (C = N), and at 400.1 eV, representing pyrrolic nitrogen (nitrogen in a five-membered ring, C−N−C). The O1s spectrum exhibits double peaks at 532−533 eV for C-O groups and at 530.5−531.5 eV for C = O groups. These values are consistent with those reported for melanin-type materials, suggesting the presence of functional domains aiding in the molecular attachment to the substrate.[3435] This could be attributed to the better remineralization activity of nPD than PDA.

In this study, the samples were treated with 37% phosphoric acid for 1 minute, following a modified protocol described by de Lima et al.[16] to prevent a higher level of demineralization in the dentinal surface. This was done to simulate the salivary environment with ionic sources, where minimal remnant crystals serve as the nucleation sites for the formation of new crystals. A very minimal deposition of HAp minerals on DI-treated demineralized dentin discs after immersion in CS implies that the dentin surface is unable to initiate the remineralization of collagenous fibrils, even in a supersaturated solution in the absence of NCP analog. This was in accordance with de Lima et al.[16] As followed by de Lima et al. 2020[16], 12 h of immersion in the experimental solution is followed to simulate the oral environment of mimicking the role of noncollagenous proteins in vivo and to enable the polymer consisting of catechol groups to modify demineralized dentin surface properties, promoting mineral formation.

The null hypothesis was rejected in this study as there was a statistical difference between PDA and nPD. The results of this study demonstrated that the remineralization process in demineralized dentin could be successfully induced by coating PDA and nPD on demineralized dentin. This could be attributed to the surface-anchored catecholamine moieties of the PDA-assisted HAp formation. These results are in accordance with Zhou et al.,[11] who showed dentinal tubular occlusion with PDA following 7 days of remineralization. PDA strongly adheres to various substrates under wet conditions. PDA enhances the hydrophilicity of dentin, improving its wettability and potential for mineralization. PDA can function as an anchor for grafting biofunctional molecules through functional groups such as thiols, imines, and amines via secondary reactions.[36] On the other hand, the negatively charged catechol groups on dopamine have a high affinity for calcium ions, which accounts for the active role of these groups in regulating the mineralization of apatite. Thus, PDA and nPD showed better remineralization potential on demineralized dentin. These findings corroborate with the findings of Choi et al.[37]

However, appreciable differences exist between PDA and nPD on microhardness and tubular occlusion ability. Compared with those of PDA, the nPD-treated dentin slabs exhibited the highest MH and maximum mineral deposition characterized by completely covered, well-defined crystals of calcium with dense and homogeneous deposition in intratubular dentin. This could be attributed to the size of the PDA which could significantly influence the factors determining dentin remineralization. The impressive crystallinity properties of nPD can be attributed to its nanometric particle size, elevated surface reactivity, and better-targeted delivery resulting in increased deposition of Ca2+ and PO43- ions taken from CS, thereby enhancing the formation of HAp.

EDX mapping confirmed that the minerals formed by nPD had Ca/P ratios significantly similar to those of the intact dentin group; however, PDA formed apatite crystals with a Ca/P ratio of 1.4, suggesting the formation of an octa-calcium phosphate phase. Thus, nPDs aid in the formation of a more native form of dentin owing to the bottom-up concept of particle-mediated nano precursor assembly and mesocrystalline transformation in the biomineralization process.[10111238]

This was further corroborated by the micro-Raman spectroscopic analysis, which showed a significant increase in the phosphate peaks and collagen peaks of amide I, III, and CH2 in the nPD group than in the PDA group, suggesting greater collagenous remineralization of dentin. The expression of these amide peaks could be attributed to the improvement in collagen stability by polydopamine.[39] Earlier studies have shown that PDA-incorporated dentin adhesives enhance MMP inhibition, thereby stabilizing collagen.[40] Qu et al.[10] demonstrated that dentin collagen treated with PDA exhibited arrays of mineral strands aligned with the c-axis of collagen fibrils which were characteristic of the diffraction planes of hydroxyapatite (HAp) and showed continuous ring patterns in selected area electron diffraction (SAED) analysis. Notably, the promoting effect of functional remineralization was attributed to the adsorbed nPD on demineralized dentin, which increased the interfacial energy between the liquid-like nano precursors and collagen. Thereby, there is a reduction in the local heterogeneous nucleation barrier followed by an accelerated phase transformation process.

Moreover, the relatively high concentrations of C and O ions on the surface of the nPD are strongly adsorbed on the dentin surface. Experiments have shown that the surface functional groups of nPD, such as NH2, hydroxyl-terminated carboxyl groups, aromatic nitrogen C = N, and pyrrolic nitrogen, as determined by XPS, also have a strong affinity for the carboxyl and OH- groups present on the dentin substrate.[32] Moreover, the characteristic stretching vibration of nPD observed at the catechol hydroxyl C–O and C–N (1290 cm−1) positions of indolequinone structures, as shown in the FTIR spectrum, could be attributed to the better binding of nPD than PDA on the substrate. The changes in dispersibility and the presence of surface charges due to ionizable groups, especially carboxylic acids and carbonyl/carboxyl carbons, as well as the presence of protonated free amino groups might interact with the π-electrons of the intact aromatic dihydroxyindole moieties to encourage binding with the calcium ions of teeth.[41] Thus, the assisted HAp formation approach works well for the growth of HAp crystals, regardless of the material type and morphology. Thus, the results of our study indicate the possible use of nPD and PDA-based remineralizing agents to treat dentinal hypersensitivity.

The limitations of this study include that the in vitro testing method does not consider dynamic oral conditions. Moreover, various testing methods utilizing pH cycling and/or thermocycling, increased periods of immersion, and longevity of the remineralization effect have not been carried out. As this was a preliminary study to determine the impact of nPD on the induction of remineralization, immersion in CS was performed just to compare its influence with the action of PDA. Furthermore, in vitro experiments such as TEM and microradiography analysis of interfibrillar dentin remineralization can be carried out before the translation of these materials as viable products for clinical trials.

CONCLUSION

Within the limitations of this study, it can be concluded that dentin remineralization is more strongly facilitated by nanometric PDA than by PDA, which has an enhanced functional remineralization effect. The superior remineralization effect of nanometric PDA (nPD) offers significant clinical advantages in the management of dentin-related issues, such as dental caries and dentin hypersensitivity. By facilitating more effective remineralization, nPD can be integrated into therapeutic agents to strengthen dentin tissue, enhance the longevity of restorative treatments, and potentially reduce the need for more invasive dental procedures and enhance action in minimally invasive dentistry and preventive care, offering a targeted approach to repairing damaged dentin at the molecular level.
