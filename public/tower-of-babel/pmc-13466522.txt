Uterine torsion caused by a giant uterine fibroid in late pregnancy: a case report and literature review
Authors: Yi Zhu, Songhui Zhang, Qiuchan Lin, Liping Song, Lili Guo, Yanhong Guo
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundUterine torsion during pregnancy is a relatively rare obstetric emergency that can occur in all stages of pregnancy. Among all kinds of acute abdomen during pregnancy, uterine torsion during pregnancy is a special type that is rare and poses a significant threat to the safety of mothers and infants. Failure to recognize clinical signs promptly and the absence of established diagnostic and treatment protocols represent persistent difficulties in managing obstetric emergencies.Case presentationThis study presents a case of uterine torsion caused by a giant uterine leiomyoma during the third trimester of pregnancy, in which the fetus was presented in the transverse position. Intraoperative examination revealed that the uterus had undergone a clockwise rotation of > 90°. Manual detorsion was initially attempted; however, owing to the obvious space-occupying effect of fibroids, restricted operative space, and markedly increased myometrial tone, multiple reductions were unsuccessful. The uterus was then rotated to the right about 45°, and an incision was created in the lower uterine segment on the posterior wall. A viable neonate was delivered in the cephalic presentation. The newborn achieved an Apgar score of 10 at both 1 and 5 min post‑delivery. Postoperatively, the patient made an uneventful recovery and was discharged according to the planned schedule.ConclusionsUterine torsion is a rare yet life-threatening obstetric emergency, characterized by a lack of specific clinical manifestations. The present case underscores the importance of early recognition, particularly in patients presenting with large fibroids and an abnormal fetal lie. Meticulous intraoperative decision-making is essential to optimize maternal and neonatal outcomes. When manual or surgical reduction proves unfeasible intraoperatively, a posterior cesarean delivery or other tailored surgical strategies may be employed to safeguard maternal and fetal well‑being and thereby enhance pregnancy prognosis.Keywords: Uterine torsion, Leiomyoma, Pregnancy

Background

Uterine torsion [1–3] is defined as a rotation of the uterus exceeding 45° around its longitudinal axis. During pregnancy, the clinical presentation of uterine torsion is highly variable.Some patients may be asymptomatic; however, uterine torsion can lead to severe complications.These include dystocia, severe abdominal pain, placental abruption, uterine rupture, fetal distress, and even maternal or fetal death [4, 5]. Due to its rarity and non-specific presentation, uterine torsion is often misdiagnosed or diagnosed intraoperatively. Consequently, early and accurate diagnosis is frequently unattainable, and the majority of cases are only conclusively identified intraoperatively. We report a case of uterine torsion caused by a large leiomyoma (> 10 cm in diameter) and review the relevant literature to highlight key diagnostic and management considerations.

Case presentation

A 29-year-old primigravida at 38+ 6 weeks of gestation was admitted. She was admitted to our hospital on December 24, 2024 due to “38+ 6 weeks of menopause, pregnancy with huge uterine fibroids”.

The patient has regular menstrual cycles prior to conception. Her last menstrual period was March 25, 2024, corresponding to an estimated due date of January 1, 2025. No gynecologic ultrasonography was performed prior to pregnancy. At 6⁺⁵ weeks of gestation, transvaginal ultrasound confirmed an intrauterine pregnancy with a viable embryo. At the same gestational age, a uterine fibroid was noted in the anterior uterine wall, measuring approximately 75 × 67 × 74 mm.It was learned that the patient had experienced abdominal pain at 16 weeks of gestation, which lasted for 2 days and resolved spontaneously with rest, with no recurrence of abdominal pain thereafter during the pregnancy, and the patient attended regular antenatal visits at our institution. Apart from the uterine leiomyoma, no other abnormalities were detected. At 22+ 2 weeks of gestation, ultrasound demonstrated enlargement of the fibroid to 108 × 87 × 93 mm, with the placenta located on the posterior wall. At 38+ 2 weeks of pregnancy, a follow-up pelvic ultrasound revealed a transverse fetal lie, the placenta being located on the anterior uterine wall. The uterine leiomyoma was situated in the right anterior wall of the lower uterine segment, exhibiting an intramural location, and measured 86 × 81 × 95 mm. Given the transverse lie and fibroid obstructing the lower uterine segment, elective cesarean delivery was planned. Accordingly, the patient was admitted to our institution for the planned elective cesarean delivery. Past medical, surgical, and family histories were unremarkable .

The admission physical examination results are as follows: body temperature 36.6 °C, pulse 100 beats per minute, blood pressure 114/70 mmHg, respiratory rate 20 breaths per minute. Fundal height 34 cm, abdominal circumference 102 cm. A firm mass measuring approximately 10 × 9 cm was palpable in the lower right abdomen.

There was no tenderness or peritoneal signs, and no uterine contractions were detected. The fetus was in a transverse lie with a left shoulder presentation, with a fetal heart rate of 150 beats per minute, and the presenting part was high and floating. After admission, relevant examinations were performed. Laboratory investigations showed hemoglobin of 108 g/L and platelet count of 171 × 10⁹/L. The coagulation function showed: fibrinogen (Fib) 4.12 g/L, D-dimer (D-D) 2.12 mg/L. Cardiotocography showed a reactive non-stress test. In addition, no abnormalities were found in routine blood and urine tests or biochemistry.Obstetric ultrasonography performed after admission revealed: a peak systolic/diastolic ratio of the fetal umbilical artery of 2.38, and a fetal presentation in the left anterior scapular (LScA) position. Preoperative ultrasound localization showed that the uterine leiomyoma was located on the right side of the uterus, the internal cervical os was located 3 cm to the left of the abdominal midline, and the fetal head was located in the left iliac fossa.

Given the transverse fetal lie and the uterine leiomyoma occupying the lower uterine segment, We anticipated several potential complications during the surgery, including difficult fetal extraction, iatrogenic injury, significant postpartum hemorrhage, and challenging intraoperative hemostasis. Therefore, we made thorough preparations in advance, including blood transfusion preparation, preparation for uterine artery ligation, and equipment for pelvic vascular interventional embolization. Since the patient had no acute abdominal pain preoperatively, uterine torsion was not suspected.

The surgery was successfully performed in the operating room under combined spinal-epidural anesthesia on the morning of December 25, 2024. A transverse lower abdominal incision was performed. Upon entering the abdominal cavity, the uterus was appeared normal in color, the left adnexa were rotated to the abdominal midline, the left round ligament and broad ligament were under tension, and the parauterine vessels were significantly engorged (Fig. 1). A uterine leiomyoma measuring approximately 12 cm in diameter was observed protruding outward at the right posterior uterine wall near the 8 o’clock position, and was classified as FIGO type 6. Based on these findings, we determined that the uterus had twisted to the right by more than 90 degrees, with the fetal head located in the left iliac fossa. During the surgery, manual uterine repositioning was first attempted. However, due to the mass effect of the leiomyoma, limited pelvic space, and extremely high uterine tension, multiple attempts at repositioning failed. Forceful repositioning was avoided due to the risk of serosal injury and vascular rupture. Additionally, considering that uterine torsion repositioning might cause dislodgment of thrombi accumulated in the vessels, leading to severe complications such as pulmonary embolism, fetal delivery was prioritized after the repositioning attempts failed.

After further rotating the uterus approximately 45 degrees to the right, the posterior uterine wall was partially visualized, where the myometrium was relatively thin. A posterior lower uterine segment incision was performed to facilitate delivery.

(Fig. 2) .A live male infant weighing 3.2 kg was delivered with Apgar scores of 10 at 1 and 5 min, and the placenta and membranes were delivered completely. The uterus was exteriorized from the abdominal cavity and restored to its normal anatomical position. Subsequent examination verified the leiomyoma situated on the anterior uterine wall (Fig. 3), with bilateral adnexa appearing unremarkable. The uterus was then sutured layer by layer in line with physiological anatomical structures (Fig. 4).The intraoperative blood loss was 700 mL. Considering the patient’s mild anemia preoperatively, we promptly transfused 2 units of compatible packed red blood cells. During the surgery, the patient had poor uterine contractions and inadequate formation of the lower uterine segment, so a Bakri uterine balloon tamponade was inserted. A consultation with a gynecologist was conducted. It was considered that the leiomyoma had a broad base, and the uterine blood supply during pregnancy was extremely rich. Additionally, the patient had poor uterine contractions during the surgery, which made leiomyoma removal at this time highly likely to cause uncontrollable massive hemorrhage. Therefore, myomectomy was not performed. The patient’s condition was explained in detail to her family intraoperatively.

Postoperatively, the patient was administered intravenous antibiotics for infection prophylaxis, along with intravenous oxytocin to enhance uterine contraction. The total amount of vaginal bleeding within 24 h postpartum was 759 mL. On the third day after surgery, a reexamination of the complete blood count revealed: hemoglobin (HGB) 95 g/L, platelet count (PLT) 172 × 10⁹/L.The coagulation tests revealed: fibrinogen (Fib) 3.28 g/L, D-dimer (D-D) 4.97 mg/L. The patient’s D-dimer level was higher postoperatively compared to preoperatively, which was considered to be attributed to local blood stasis secondary to uterine torsion and a systemic hypercoagulable state induced by surgical trauma. Physical thromboprophylaxis (pneumatic therapy) and encouragement of early ambulation were implemented to prevent postpartum thrombosis. The patient had an uneventful postoperative recovery and was discharged on postoperative day 4.Final diagnoses included a term pregnancy at 39 weeks of gestation in a primipara with fetal left shoulder transverse presentation delivered via posterior uterine cesarean section, pregnancy complicated by giant uterine leiomyomas, gravid uterine torsion, and moderate anemia.

A follow-up ultrasound examination performed 3 months postpartum revealed: a large mass visible in the anterior wall of the uterus, measuring 85 mm × 73 mm × 82 mm, with mixed internal echogenicity and an intact surface capsule. The uterus was displaced to the right horizontal position, the incision on the posterior uterine wall was healing well, the endometrial line was clear, and there was no bleeding or placental membrane residue in the uterine cavity (Figs. 5, 6 and 7). No significant abnormalities were observed in the bilateral adnexal areas. The patient was advised to use contraception for 2 years and to undergo follow-up ultrasound every 3–6 months to evaluate the fibroid size and associated symptoms. If the fibroid persists and causes menorrhagia, compressive symptoms, or impairs quality of life, laparoscopic myomectomy is recommended 6 months to 1 year postpartum.

Discussion

Pregnancy complicated by uterine fibroids [6] is an extremely common clinical condition. Clinically, Uterine fibroids are common in pregnancy; however, uterine torsion remains a rare complication. The etiology of uterine torsion is multifactorial and often related to uterine asymmetry that factors related to uterine torsion [7] are closely associated with the location of the uterine fibroids.

Analysis of high-risk factors for uterine torsion in pregnancy

The exact pathogenesis of uterine torsion during pregnancy remains unclear. In the late stages of normal pregnancy, due to the obstructive effect of the left sigmoid colon, the uterus undergoes varying degrees of rotation (typically less than 30°) [8]. As gestational age advances, the cervix uteri and lower uterine segment gradually lengthen and soften [9], with tissues becoming congested and loose. If factors promoting excessive uterine activity are present at this time, the uterus is highly prone to torsion. When the uterus rotates more than 45° along its long axis, it can be diagnosed as pathological uterine torsion associated with pregnancy [10]. Dua et al. [11] also suggest that 87.77% of uterine torsion during pregnancy is caused by structural abnormalities of the uterus. During the progression of pregnancy, as the fetus gradually enlarges and the activity level of pregnant women increases in the third trimester, the cervix uteri and lower uterine segment lengthen and soften, their supporting tissues become congested and softened, and factors such as uterine asymmetry and gravity [12] can easily lead to uterine torsion. Structural uterine abnormalities account for a significant proportion of cases [13], with bicornuate uterus being the most common, followed by septate uterus and unicornuate uterus [14]. Additionally, pathological pelvic conditions, such as pelvic or spinal abnormalities, large pelvic masses, and pelvic adhesions, may also induce uterine torsion during pregnancy [15]. Abnormal fetal positions (such as transverse lie), lax abdominal walls in multipara, and sudden changes in posture by pregnant women [16] can similarly induce uterine torsion. In this case, the patient had pregnancy complicated by a large uterine fibroid in the lower anterior wall. Under the influence of pregnancy hormones, the parametrial tissues became lax, and fetal growth exacerbated the shift in the uterine center of gravity. Under the combined effects of these factors, uterine torsion ultimately occurred.

Clinical manifestations and signs of uterine torsion in pregnancy

Uterine torsion during pregnancy mainly occurs in the mid-to-late stages and often presents as an acute abdomen. According to a summary by Piot et al., among the symptoms of uterine torsion during pregnancy, pain is the most common, accounting for 95%, while shock, urinary symptoms, vaginal bleeding, and other symptoms also occur in a certain proportion, with only 6% of patients being asymptomatic. Additionally, abnormal fetal heart rate and abnormal umbilical artery Doppler findings are often detected [17, 18]. Given its low incidence, subtle prodromal manifestations and nonspecific clinical presentations, uterine torsion is difficult to diagnose precisely before surgery, and it is easily misdiagnosed as conditions such as ectopic pregnancy, torsion of pelvic masses, and degeneration of uterine fibroids. Cesarean delivery is a major surgical procedure, with maternal morbidity and the risk of postpartum complications significantly higher than those of vaginal delivery, especially in cases complicated by large uterine fibroids [19]. The vast majority of cases are diagnosed during laparotomy or surgery, with uterine torsion ranging from 90 to 180 degrees. In midline laparotomy incisions, engorged and tortuous uterine vessels are visible; when torsion exceeds 180 degrees, the posterior uterine wall flips to the anterior, and due to excessive torsion leading to circulatory stasis, the uterus appears purplish-blue. A study by Kremer et al. [20] found that uterine torsion is often accompanied by changes in placental position. For example, a placenta originally described as anterior may suddenly become lateral or posterior. However, when the placenta is attached to the fundal wall or when uterine torsion reaches 360 degrees, it becomes difficult to make judgments based on this indicator. In this case, the patient showed changes in placental position during late-pregnancy ultrasound, which necessitates vigilance for the occurrence of uterine torsion. Nicholson et al. [21] reported that during MRI examination for uterine torsion in pregnancy, a characteristic sign may appear: “a linear X-shaped structure with low signal intensity in the mid-to-upper segment of the vaginal wall, while the lower segment of the vaginal wall maintains an H-shaped structure.” The accompanying images in the article are classic, providing rare prenatal imaging diagnostic data for case reports of uterine torsion cases. Therefore, imaging can not only provide early warning and specific diagnosis of uterine torsion, but also provide key support for timely clinical intervention and improvement of maternal and fetal prognosis.In this case, since the patient had no discomfort before surgery, no MRI was performed preoperatively. During cesarean delivery, the uterus was found to be twisted 90 degrees to the right. Although the left round ligament and broad ligament were tense, and the left parametrial tissues were congested, the uterine surface appeared normal, and the adnexa showed no signs of ischemia, which explains why the patient had no abdominal pain symptoms in the third trimester of pregnancy.

Treatment and prognosis analysis of uterine torsion in pregnancy

The prognosis of uterine torsion during pregnancy is associated with the timeliness of diagnosis and the appropriateness of treatment [22]. Timely and accurate diagnosis combined with appropriate treatment is crucial for improving maternal and neonatal prognosis. (1) Early and mid-pregnancy: Once uterine torsion is confirmed, conservative management and positional adjustment can be attempted first; if acute abdominal pain does not resolve, surgical intervention is indicated. The purpose of surgery is to reposition the uterus and address the underlying cause of torsion. (2) Late pregnancy or during delivery: If uterine torsion during pregnancy is confirmed, emergency caesarean section should be performed promptly. During surgery, the doctor must make a decision based on the condition regarding whether to perform torsion reduction, cesarean section, myomectomy, or hysterectomy. If uterine ischemia, necrosis, or infection is present, emergency hysterectomy may be required.

Uterine torsion is rare but has a clear risk of recurrence. Its recurrence is closely associated with uterine structural abnormalities, especially uterine fibroids and uterine malformations, as well as pregnancy status.In clinical evaluation and postoperative management, identifying and dealing with these risk factors is essential to prevent recurrence.

Uterine torsion in non-pregnant individuals is exceptionally rare. Only a few hundred cases have been reported in the global literature. It occurs more frequently in women with a retroverted uterine position. Nearly all cases are associated with identifiable organic etiologies. The most frequent cause is giant uterine leiomyoma, followed by congenital uterine anomalies, ligamentous laxity, and pelvic adhesions, among others. An uneven distribution of uterine mass coupled with reduced ligamentous elasticity diminishes uterine stability, such that minor positional changes may precipitate torsion. The Clinical features are typically nonspecific, including lower abdominal pain, nausea, vomiting, and urinary or defecation disturbances, which may readily lead to preoperative misdiagnosis as adnexal torsion, appendicitis, or intestinal obstruction; consequently, the preoperative detection rate remains exceedingly low. Imaging modalities such as CT or MRI may demonstrate a “whirlpool” sign. Management primarily entails emergency surgery; simple detorsion carries a high risk of recurrence, and prognosis largely hinges on timely intervention. By contrast, uterine torsion in the third trimester, although rare overall, is relatively more prevalent than in non-pregnant states. It predominantly arises after 28 weeks of gestation, often superimposed on a background of mild to moderate uterine dextroposition. Risk factors comprise uterine anomalies, leiomyomas, abnormal fetal lie, and polyhydramnios; nevertheless, many cases occur in the absence of overt organic pathology. Presentation typically includes acute abdominal pain, uterine tenderness, and evidence of fetal compromise. Given its implications for maternal and fetal safety, the condition is considered particularly perilous. Preoperative diagnosis remains challenging, and the majority of cases are confirmed intraoperatively during cesarean delivery. The mainstay of treatment is emergency cesarean section coupled with uterine detorsion, aiming to preserve the uterus and reproductive potential whenever feasible; hysterectomy is reserved for cases of uterine ischemia or necrosis deemed nonviable.

However, repositioning may lead to complications such as thromboembolism (including thrombus detachment and embolism) [24]. Conclusions

Pregnancy-related uterine torsion is an uncommon but life-threatening obstetric complication with non-specific clinical manifestations, which severely endangers maternal and fetal safety. Clinicians should maintain high vigilance and include this condition in differential diagnosis for pregnant women suffering from acute abdominal pain or carrying relevant high-risk factors. Detailed medical history inquiry and comprehensive clinical assessment help reduce misdiagnosis, and timely diagnosis and proper treatment are critical to improving maternal and neonatal prognosis. This case highlights the necessity of early identification, especially in patients complicated with large uterine fibroids and abnormal fetal position. Prudent intraoperative judgment contributes to favorable pregnancy outcomes, and individualized surgical approaches including posterior cesarean section can be adopted when manual or surgical repositioning fails to protect maternal and fetal health.
