Prolonged S-adenosylmethionine exposure is associated with poorer treatment response and adverse outcomes in breast cancer: a retrospective cohort study
Authors: Jiaru Gao, Yuanxiang Lu, Zhiyuan Zhang, Maosen Zhai, Shuxin Kong, Linjiao Jia, Gaoqiang Liang, Jinting Liu, Zhuang Ma, Wenwen Li, Changzheng Zhu, Tianyu Zhang, Wentao Li
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundHepatoprotective regimens are frequently used during chemotherapy for breast cancer, but their effects on oncologic outcomes remain unclear. S-adenosylmethionine (SAMe), a universal methyl donor, may influence treatment response through epigenetic and epitranscriptomic regulations.MethodsWe retrospectively analyzed data from 1013 consecutive women with primary breast cancer treated at Zhengzhou University People’s Hospital between January 1, 2018 and January 1, 2020, and who underwent surgery followed by standard adjuvant chemotherapy. Overall survival (OS) and disease-free survival (DFS) were compared across hepatoprotective regimens. Propensity score matching (1:2) was performed to balance baseline characteristics between SAMe-treated and untreated patients. Receiver operating characteristic (ROC) analysis identified the optimal exposure threshold for recurrence discrimination, and survival was further assessed according to SAMe exposure duration using Cox proportional hazards models. Transcriptomic data from the Cancer Transcriptome Relationships Database (CTR-DB) were analyzed for SAMe-related pathways. In an exploratory neoadjuvant cohort (n = 62), intratumoral SAMe levels, m6A/m5C/m7G signals and METTL3/METTL14 expression were quantified in post-treatment tumor tissues.ResultsSAMe exposure during adjuvant chemotherapy was associated with shorter OS and DFS than other or no hepatoprotectants (both P < 0.05). After matching, prolonged SAMe exposure (≥ 14days) remained associated with worse OS and DFS than non-SAMe exposure (both P < 0.05), and this association persisted after multivariable adjustment. CTR-DB analyses revealed enrichment of methionine metabolism and RNA methylation-related pathways in non-responsive tumours. In the neoadjuvant cohort, tumours from patients with poor radiologic response showed higher intratumoral SAMe levels, stronger m6A signals, and higher METTL3 and METTL14 expression. Higher m6A levels were also associated with poorer MRI-based response.ConclusionsProlonged SAMe exposure during chemotherapy was associated with poorer treatment response and worse survival. Transcriptomic and tissue-level findings further support a possible link with altered m6A methylation. These results indicate that a cautious and standardized approach to SAMe use during chemotherapy may help limit treatment resistance and improve long-term outcomes.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12957-026-04440-1.

Introduction

Breast cancer is one of the most common malignancies in women and remains a major threat to women’s health [1, 2]. Current treatment strategies include surgery, chemotherapy, radiotherapy, endocrine therapy, and targeted agents, with chemotherapy remaining a central component of systemic management [3]. Chemotherapy suppresses tumor growth by disrupting DNA/RNA synthesis and repair, arresting the cell cycle, inhibiting angiogenesis, and remodeling cellular metabolism [4]. However, treatment-related toxic effects frequently complicate the delivery of chemotherapy. Among these, liver dysfunction is common and may lead to treatment interruption, delay, or dose modification. Accordingly, hepatoprotective drugs are widely used during chemotherapy in routine practice [5, 6]. Although their short-term supportive value is well recognized, their potential impact on tumour outcomes has received far less attention. Emerging evidence suggests that some hepatoprotective agents may disturb redox balance and epigenetic regulation in cancer cells, thereby inadvertently favouring chemoresistance [7, 8]. However, direct evidence in breast cancer remains limited.

Chemoresistance remains a major challenge in breast cancer. Multiple mechanisms have been implicated, including altered drug transport, changes in metabolic processing, and dysregulation of survival pathways [9]. Recently, epigenetic and epitranscriptomic regulation has emerged as an important layer of treatment response [10]. In particular, N6-methyladenosine (m6A) RNA methylation has been linked to tumour progression, therapeutic response, and adaptive resistance in several cancers, including breast cancer [11]. These observations raise the possibility that RNA methylation contributes to variability in chemotherapy sensitivity.

S-adenosylmethionine (SAMe) is of particular interest in this context because it functions as a universal methyl donor and may therefore have relevance beyond supportive care alone [12]. Altered methyl-donor availability has been reported to influence tumour cell behaviour, while dysregulated m6A signalling has been associated with treatment resistance [13, 14].Taken together, these findings suggest a plausible biological link between SAMe exposure and chemotherapy response. Nevertheless, the clinical relationship between SAMe exposure during chemotherapy and breast cancer outcomes has not been systematically evaluated.

In this study, we first compared survival outcomes across different hepatoprotective strategies in a large adjuvant breast cancer cohort. We then focused on SAMe and examined whether longer exposure was associated with worse prognosis after propensity score matching. Finally, we explored RNA methylation-related findings using public transcriptomic data and an exploratory neoadjuvant cohort with tissue and imaging analysis. Through this approach, we aimed to assess the clinical association between SAMe exposure and breast cancer outcomes, while exploring whether these findings were accompanied by changes related to m6A methylation.

Materials and methods

Patients

This retrospective study included 1013 consecutive women with primary breast cancer who were treated at Zhengzhou University People’s Hospital between January 1, 2018 and January 1, 2020. Patients in the overall cohort underwent curative surgery and received guideline-concordant adjuvant or neoadjuvant chemotherapy in routine clinical practice. Follow-up was censored on January 1 2025. Clinical, pathological, treatment, and follow-up data were retrieved from the hospital information system and telephone follow-up records.

In the overall cohort, patients were classified according to the hepatoprotective drug used during chemotherapy, including magnesium isoglycyrrhizinate (MIG), glutathione (GSH), S-adenosylmethionine (SAMe), or no hepatoprotective drug. This cohort was used to compare the survival outcomes of different hepatoprotective strategies.

The inclusion criteria for the analytic adjuvant cohort were as follows: (i) female sex; (ii) histologically confirmed primary invasive breast cancer; (iii) curative surgery at Zhengzhou University People’s Hospital with planned adjuvant or neoadjuvant chemotherapy; (iv) available records of chemotherapy and hepatoprotective drug use, including SAMe; and (v) complete key clinicopathological data and follow-up information. The exclusion criteria were as follows: (i) distant metastasis at diagnosis or palliative surgery only; (ii) history of other malignant tumours; (iii) severe comorbidities or poor performance status precluding standard chemotherapy; (iv) missing essential baseline, treatment, or outcome data; and (v) loss to follow-up.

After applying these criteria, 100 SAMe-treated patients and 213 non-SAMe patients entered the analytic adjuvant cohort (n = 313) for propensity score matching (PSM) and survival analyses. PSM at a 1:2 ratio generated a matched cohort of 82 SAMe-treated and 164 non-SAMe patients (n = 246).

An exploratory neoadjuvant cohort was retrospectively assembled for tissue and imaging analysis. This cohort included patients with histologically confirmed primary invasive breast cancer who received neoadjuvant chemotherapy at Zhengzhou University People’s Hospital, were exposed to SAMe during treatment, had available post-treatment tumour tissue and evaluable breast MRI data and had available post-treatment tumour tissue obtained at surgery after completion of neoadjuvant chemotherapy. Of the 72 potentially eligible patients with available post-treatment tumour tissue and MRI data, 10 were excluded because of unqualified specimens, incomplete clinical records, or loss to follow-up. The final exploratory neoadjuvant cohort included 62 patients with post-treatment tumour tissue, imaging data, and follow-up information.

This study was approved by the Ethics Committee of Zhengzhou University People’s Hospital. Written informed consent was obtained from all participants or their legal guardians.

Definitions and study endpoints

Liver function was monitored before each chemotherapy cycle using serum alanine aminotransferase(ALT) and aspartate aminotransferase(AST) levels as primary markers. Hepatoprotective treatment was generally initiated in the presence of clinically relevant liver dysfunction during chemotherapy [6, 15]. Consequently, chemotherapy was withheld under these conditions and resumed once liver enzyme levels returned to the normal range. The SAMe exposure duration was defined as the total number of administration days during the chemotherapy course. Moreover, the cumulative dose was estimated from the manufacturer’s recommendations and included both oral and intravenous routes. Data were extracted from electronic medical records and verified by telephone follow-up when necessary.

Adverse events were graded according to the Common Terminology Criteria for Adverse Events (CTCAE), version 5.0 [16].

For this analysis, exposure duration in non-SAMe patients was coded as 0 days. Based on the ROC result, patients were categorized into three groups: non-SAMe, short exposure (< 14 days), and prolonged exposure (≥ 14 days). ROC analysis was performed in the matched cohort to identify the SAMe exposure duration that provided the greatest discrimination for recurrence. Baseline liver function referred to liver-related laboratory values measured before chemotherapy initiation. Overall survival (OS) was defined as the interval from the date of surgery to death from any cause or last follow-up. Disease-free survival (DFS) was defined as the interval from the date of surgery to recurrence, metastasis, death, or last follow-up. In the exploratory neoadjuvant cohort, radiologic response was assessed using breast MRI according to RECIST version 1.1. A favourable response was defined as complete response (CR) or partial response (PR), whereas a poor radiologic response was defined as stable disease (SD) or progressive disease (PD).

Chemotherapy regimens and treatment monitoring

Adjuvant and neoadjuvant chemotherapy regimens followed contemporary national and international clinical guidelines [17]. Adjuvant chemotherapy typically begins approximately two weeks after surgery [18]. Most patients received one of the following regimens: AC-T (doxorubicin plus cyclophosphamide followed by paclitaxel or docetaxel); TCb (paclitaxel or docetaxel plus carboplatin); TC (paclitaxel or docetaxel plus cyclophosphamide); or AC (doxorubicin plus cyclophosphamide). For neoadjuvant therapy, the most common regimens were six cycles of TAC (taxane [paclitaxel or docetaxel], doxorubicin, and cyclophosphamide) or TCb.

During chemotherapy, complete blood count, liver and renal function tests, and tumour markers were routinely monitored before each cycle. Physical examination and breast ultrasound were also performed regularly during treatment. In the neoadjuvant setting, breast MRI was generally performed every 2 cycles or when clinically indicated to assess response. Most patients received 4 to 8 cycles of chemotherapy, depending on the molecular subtype, treatment setting, and interim response.

Follow-up

Patients were followed up every 3 months during the first 2 years after surgery, every 6 months during years 3 to 5, and annually thereafter. Follow-up evaluations included outpatient visits, inpatient records, and telephone interviews when necessary.

Routine follow-up assessments included physical examination, breast and regional ultrasound, chest imaging, abdominal ultrasound or computed tomography, and bone scan when clinically indicated. Survival status, recurrence, and metastasis were recorded throughout the follow-up.

Public database and pathway analysis

Public transcriptomic data were obtained from the Cancer Transcriptome Relationships Database (CTR-DB, https://bioinfo.uth.edu/CTR-DB/). The CTR_Microarray_41-I dataset (GSE66305) was used for analysis. This dataset included pretreatment tumour samples from patients with HER2-positive breast cancer who were treated with neoadjuvant TFEC plus trastuzumab.

Tumours were classified as responding or non-responding according to the CTR-DB annotation. Differentially expressed genes between the two groups were subjected to Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) pathway enrichment analysis to identify pathways associated with treatment non-response. In parallel, gene set enrichment analysis (GSEA) was used to estimate pathway activity in individual samples. Particular attention was paid to pathways related to amino acid metabolism and RNA methylation, especially cysteine and methionine metabolism. Heatmaps were generated to visualize pathway activity patterns across samples.

Exploratory neoadjuvant cohort analyses

Quantification of intratumoral SAMe levels

Intratumoral SAMe levels were measured in post-treatment tumour tissues from the exploratory neoadjuvant cohort using an enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay (ELISA) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Briefly, 20–50 mg of tumour tissue was homogenized, and the lysate was centrifuged at 12,000 rpm for 10–15 min at 4 °C. The supernatant was collected for SAMe measurement, and the absorbance was measured at 450 nm. Total protein concentration was determined using a bicinchoninic acid (BCA) assay for normalization. Intratumoral SAMe concentrations were expressed as ng/mg protein. All measurements were performed in triplicate.

Immunofluorescence staining for m6A, m5C and m7G

Formalin-fixed, paraffin-embedded tumor tissues from the neoadjuvant cohort were cut into 4 μm sections. After deparaffinization and rehydration, antigen retrieval was performed in sodium citrate buffer (pH 6.0) at 95 °C for 15 min. The sections were then permeabilized with 0.3% Triton X-100 and blocked with 5% bovine serum albumin at room temperature. Slides were incubated overnight at 4 °C with primary antibodies against m6A (1:200; Proteintech, #68055-1-Ig), m5C (1:200; HUABIO, #HA601350), and m7G (1:200; Proteintech, #68302-1-Ig). After washing, the sections were incubated with Alexa Fluor 488-conjugated secondary antibody (1:500; Thermo Fisher) for 1 h, and the nuclei were counterstained with DAPI (1 µg/mL; Sigma). The slides were mounted using an antifade medium. Fluorescence images were acquired using a Leica confocal microscope (Leica Microsystems) and analyzed using LAS X software under identical exposure settings. All images were captured under identical exposure parameters to ensure comparability across samples.

Quantification of RNA methylation signals

Fluorescence intensities of m6A, m5C, and m7G staining were quantified using the ImageJ software (NIH, USA) with identical analysis settings across samples. For each tumor sample, at least five randomly selected fields within the tumor regions were analyzed under identical exposure conditions. The mean fluorescence intensity (MFI) was measured in regions of interest corresponding to tumor cell areas, and background fluorescence was subtracted. For each modification, the average MFI from multiple fields was calculated to represent the relative RNA methylation level per sample. All image quantifications were performed by two independent observers who were blinded to the clinical information.

RT-qPCR analysis of methyltransferase expression

To assess methyltransferase expression, tumour tissues from 10 patients with favourable radiologic responses and 10 patients with poor radiologic responses were analyzed using RT-qPCR. Total RNA was extracted using TRIzol, and 1 µg of RNA was reverse-transcribed using a standard cDNA synthesis kit. RT-qPCR was performed on a QuantStudio 3 system using SYBR Green Master Mix and gene-specific primers for METTL3 and METTL14 (Supplementary Table S1). GAPDH was used as the internal control. Relative expression levels were calculated using the 2^−ΔΔCt method.

MRI assessment

Breast MRI was performed before and after neoadjuvant chemotherapy in accordance with established clinical guidelines and institutional protocols. Tumour response was assessed by changes in tumour burden on MRI [19]. Response categories were defined according to RECIST version 1.1. Waterfall plots were used to illustrate the maximum percentage change in tumour burden from baseline.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analyses were performed using SPSS(version 26.0) and R(version 4.3.2). Categorical variables were summarized as numbers and percentages and compared using the chi-square test or Fisher’s exact test, as appropriate. Continuous variables are expressed as the mean±standard deviation or median with interquartile range, and were compared using the Student’s t test or Mann–Whitney U test according to data distribution.

PSM was performed in the analytic adjuvant cohort using a 1:2 nearest-neighbour algorithm without replacement and a caliper width of 0.02. The variables entered into the propensity score model were age, menopausal status, TNM stage, molecular subtype, histological grade, Ki-67 index, chemotherapy regimen, hepatitis infection, and baseline liver-related variables. Covariate balance before and after matching was assessed using standardized mean differences (SMDs), with an absolute SMD < 0.1 considered acceptable.

Receiver operating characteristic (ROC) analysis was performed in the matched cohort to identify the optimal cut-off value for SAMe exposure duration for recurrence discrimination. Patients without SAMe exposure were coded as 0 day. The optimal cutoff value was determined using the maximum Youden index and was subsequently used for exposure stratification in downstream analyses.

Survival curves were estimated using the Kaplan-Meier method and compared using the log-rank test. Cox proportional hazards regression models were used to evaluate the association between SAMe exposure duration and survival outcomes in the matched cohort. Three progressively adjusted multivariable models were prespecified for OS. Model 1 was adjusted for TNM stage; Model 2 was additionally adjusted for targeted therapy; and Model 3 was further adjusted for chemotherapy regimen, hepatitis infection, and baseline ALT. For DFS, multivariable Cox regression was performed using clinically relevant covariates.

Restricted cubic spline (RCS) analysis was performed among patients treated with SAMe to explore the association between exposure duration and survival risk. The spline model was adjusted for TNM stage, chemotherapy regimen, baseline ALT, baseline AST, hepatitis infection, and targeted therapy. Because multiple liver-related variables were correlated, transaminase after chemotherapy was selected as an additional adjustment variable in sensitivity analyses among patients treated with SAMe. This choice was made to capture liver injury at the time of SAMe initiation while minimizing potential collinearity.

In the exploratory neoadjuvant cohort, Spearman’s rank correlation analysis was used to assess the correlations between cumulative SAMe exposure duration and intratumoral SAMe levels, and between intratumoral SAMe levels and m6A fluorescence intensity. ROC analysis was performed using post-treatment m6A fluorescence intensity to identify the threshold that best discriminated radiologic response. Patients were then categorized into m6A-low and m6A-high groups for subsequent response and survival analyses. Multivariable logistic regression was used to evaluate factors associated with poor radiologic response.

Proportional hazards assumption was assessed before interpreting of the Cox models. All statistical tests were two-sided, and P < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

Patient characteristics

The overall cohort comprised 1013 consecutive women with primary breast cancer, including 300 in the MIG group, 300 in the GSH group, 208 in the SAMe group, and 205 in the no-hepatoprotective drug group, with a median follow-up of 5.6 years (range 5–7 years; Supplementary Table 2).

For the SAMe-focused analysis, 378 patients were screened initially. After applying the predefined criteria, 100 SAMe-treated patients and 213 non-SAMe patients entered the analytic adjuvant cohort. Following 1:2 propensity score matching, the matched cohort included 82 SAMe-treated and 164 non-SAMe patients, with acceptable balance across the prespecified covariates (Table 1 and Supplementary Table 3; Supplementary Fig. 1).

Multivariable survival analyses in the matched cohort

In the multivariable Cox regression analyses, prolonged SAMe exposure (≥ 14 days) remained associated with worse OS than non-SAMe exposure across all three prespecified models (Table 4; Fig. 4). The hazard ratio was 3.231 (95% CI 1.786–5.844, P < 0.001) in Model 1, 3.528 (95% CI 1.939–6.420, P < 0.001) in Model 2, and 3.302 (95% CI 1.752–6.228, P < 0.001) in Model 3. In contrast, short exposure was not significantly associated with OS in any model.

Tissue and imaging findings in the exploratory neoadjuvant cohort

In post-treatment tumour tissues from the exploratory neoadjuvant cohort, intratumoral SAMe levels were higher in tumours from patients with poor radiologic response than in those from patients with favourable response (Fig. 5C; P < 0.001). Representative immunofluorescence images showed stronger m6A staining in tumours from patients with poor radiologic response than in those from patients with favourable responses (Fig. 5D). Quantitative analysis confirmed a higher mean fluorescence intensity of m6A in the poor-response group (Fig. 5E; P < 0.001), whereas differences in m5C and m7G were modest and not statistically significant. At the tissue level, intratumoral SAMe concentration was positively correlated with m6A fluorescence intensity (Fig. 5F; Spearman ρ = 0.4897, P < 0.001). In addition, cumulative SAMe exposure duration was positively correlated with post-treatment intratumoral SAMe levels (Supplementary Fig. 3; Spearman ρ = 0.5402, P < 0.001). At the tissue level, the intratumoral SAMe concentration correlated positively with m6A fluorescence intensity (Fig. 5F; P < 0.001). The mRNA expression levels of METTL3 and METTL14 were also higher in tumours from patients with poor response (Fig. 5G; both P < 0.001).

Using post-treatment m6A mean fluorescence intensity, ROC analysis yielded an AUC of 0.877 and identified 46.4 as the cut-off for defining the m6A-low and m6A-high groups (Fig. 5H; Table 5). Representative MRI scans and waterfall plots showed less tumour shrinkage in the m6A-high group than in the m6A-low group (Fig. 5I and J). Consistently, the objective response rate according to RECIST version 1.1 was lower in the m6A-high group than in the m6A-low group (29.16% vs. 71.05%, P = 0.005; Fig. 5K; Table 5).

Prolonged SAMe exposure (≥ 14 days) was also more frequent in the m6A-high group than in the m6A-low group (70.83% vs. 39.47%, P = 0.016). After adjustment for TNM stage, chemotherapy regimen category, targeted therapy, and gastrointestinal adverse reactions during chemotherapy, prolonged SAMe exposure remained associated with a higher likelihood of poor radiologic response (Supplementary Table 6).

Overall, these findings suggested that higher tissue m6A levels are associated with poorer radiologic responses and less favourable long-term outcomes in this exploratory cohort.

Discussion

In this retrospective study, prolonged SAMe exposure during chemotherapy was associated with worse outcomes in breast cancer. In the overall cohort, patients who received SAMe had shorter overall and disease-free survival than those who received magnesium isoglycyrrhizinate, glutathione, or no hepatoprotective drug. In the matched cohort, SAMe-treated patients also had shorter overall and disease-free survival than non-users. When exposure duration was considered, the adverse signal was concentrated in patients with prolonged exposure. Patients treated for 14 days or longer had the poorest survival, whereas those treated for < 14 days did not show a clear difference from the non-SAMe group. In multivariable Cox models, prolonged exposure remained associated with worse overall survival across all three prespecified models, and the same pattern was observed for disease-free survival. Spline analysis also supported an overall association between longer exposure duration and poorer survival. Taken together, these findings suggest that exposure duration may be more informative than simple ever-use status.

This question is clinically relevant because SAMe is used in some patients who develop liver dysfunction during chemotherapy. In routine practice, its use is mainly guided by liver enzyme abnormalities and the need to maintain treatment continuity, whereas potential effects on tumor biology receive much less attention [20–22]. SAMe has been used as a hepatoprotective agent because it can replenish hepatic methyl pools, support glutathione synthesis, and mitigate treatment-related liver injury [20, 23]. However, the oncologic implications of prolonged exposure during chemotherapy remain uncertain. Our findings therefore raise a clinically relevant concern, but they do not support a practice-changing conclusion.

A key result of this study was a duration-related risk pattern. ROC analysis identified 14 days as the exposure threshold with the best discrimination for recurrence in this dataset. This threshold was useful for stratified analysis. It should not be interpreted as a biologically validated cut-off point or a clinically established safe limit. Most SAMe-treated patients received the drug for < 14 days, and prolonged exposure occurred in a smaller subgroup. This subgroup had the poorest overall survival and disease-free survival, and the hazard ratios remained elevated after adjustment. This pattern makes the signal clinically relevant, however, external validation is still needed.

The mechanistic findings add biological plausibility, but they remain exploratory. Accumulating evidence indicates that dysregulated epigenetic regulation, especially m6A methylation, is involved in treatment resistance [10, 24]. In several tumor types, higher global m6A levels or abnormal m6A regulator activity have been linked to poor outcomes, ferroptosis resistance, and drug resistance [13, 14, 25, 26]. SAMe occupies a central position in one-carbon and methionine metabolism and serves as a universal methyl donor for DNA, RNA, and histone methylation [27, 28]. Experimental studies have also shown that malignant cells are highly dependent on methyl-donor availability [29–31]. In our study, non-response tumors in the public cohort showed enrichment of cysteine and methionine metabolism. In the neoadjuvant cohort, poor MRI response was accompanied by higher intratumoral SAMe levels, stronger m6A signals, and higher METTL3 and METTL14 expression. Patients with high m6A signals also had worse long-term outcomes. These findings are consistent with a possible link between prolonged SAMe exposure and an unfavorable m6A-related state. However, they do not establish that SAMe supplementation directly causes m6A remodeling or chemotherapy resistance. The positive correlation between cumulative SAMe exposure duration and post-treatment intratumoral SAMe levels further supports an exposure-tissue association in this exploratory cohort.

Some prior studies have suggested that SAMe can exert context-dependent antitumor effects, including remethylation of promoter regions and suppression of proto-oncogene transcription in selected models [32]. This highlights the complexity of SAMe biology. At the same time, other studies have suggested that excessive methyl-donor availability may reshape cellular energetics, alter methylation patterns, and influence downstream signaling under stress conditions [33, 34]. Functionally, enhanced m6A deposition on specific transcripts may stabilize mRNAs involved in redox control, anti-apoptotic signaling, or drug efflux, whereas SAMe-driven flux through the methionine-homocysteine-cysteine axis may augment glutathione biosynthesis and blunt ROS accumulation [35–38]. In tumors with high epigenetic plasticity, this possibility deserves attention. However, the present study was not designed to test these mechanisms directly. We did not measure downstream target transcripts, ferroptosis markers, antioxidant enzymes, or drug efflux activity.

Another issue is the modeling of liver function and exposure were modeled. We used ALT as the main liver-related covariate in the additional models and sensitivity analyses. AST and ALT are both markers of hepatocellular injury, but AST is less liver-specific because it is also present in several non-hepatic tissues [39]. Therefore we selected one prespecified liver-related marker to reduce collinearity among correlated liver enzymes. We also defined SAMe exposure as the total number of administration days across the full chemotherapy course. This reflected the structure of the dataset and the way supportive treatment is used in practice. Breast cancer chemotherapy is delivered over repeated cycles, and SAMe may be prescribed intermittently when liver dysfunction appears during treatment. Therefore the present dataset was therefore better suited for the analysis of cumulative treatment days than for modeling time-varying pharmacologic exposure.

This study has several strengths. It evaluated a clinically relevant but underexplored supportive care exposure in a relatively large real-world breast cancer cohort with a long follow-up. The analysis considered both exposure status and exposure duration. The findings were supported by matched analyses, multivariable survival models, and sensitivity analyses. We also linked the clinical results to public transcriptomic data and tissue-level validation. These complementary analyses do not eliminate bias, but they strengthen the relevance of the observed association and support the need for further studies.

The clinical implications of our findings should be interpreted conservatively. Our findings do not support the universal avoidance of SAMe during chemotherapy. They also do not define a validated safe duration threshold [40]. If excessive SAMe favors an m6A-related state associated with treatment resistance, its use during chemotherapy may need to be more carefully individualized [41]. However, our findings suggest that SAMe, although prescribed as a hepatoprotective agent after the onset of liver dysfunction during chemotherapy, should not automatically be regarded as a clinically irrelevant concomitant medication when treatment response and survival are evaluated. In practice, the indication for SAMe initiation and the duration of exposure should be documented more explicitly, and SAMe exposure may need to be considered a potentially relevant treatment-related variable in prognostic analyses rather than being overlooked as background medication.

Several limitations should be acknowledged. This was a retrospective study from a single center in China. Supportive care practice may differ across institutions and regions. Therefore the generalizability of the findings remains uncertain. In the neoadjuvant cohort, all patients had received SAMe, so there was no non-SAMe tissue control group. The tissue analyses focused on global m6A, m5C, and m7G signals and selected methyltransferases, but did not identify downstream target transcripts or functional pathways. Future studies should include multicenter validation, time-varying treatment-intensity data, and mechanistic experiments in patient-derived models.

Conclusion

In conclusion, prolonged SAMe exposure during chemotherapy was associated with poorer survival and reduced neoadjuvant response in breast cancer. This association was accompanied by enrichment of m6A-related pathways and an intratumoral phenotype characterized by higher m6A signals and increased METTL3/METTL14 expression. These findings encourage careful evaluation of SAMe exposure duration during chemotherapy to preserve treatment sensitivity and improve long-term outcomes.
