When Lower-Order Terms Dominate: Adaptive Expert Algorithms for Heavy-Tailed Losses
Antoine Moulin, Emmanuel Esposito, Dirk van der Hoeven

Abstract
We consider the problem setting of prediction with expert advice with possibly heavy-tailed losses, i.e. the only assumption on the losses is an upper bound on their second moments, denoted by θ \theta . We develop adaptive algorithms that do not require any prior knowledge about the range or the second moment of the losses. Existing adaptive algorithms have what is typically considered a lower-order term in their regret guarantees. We show that this lower-order term, which is often the maximum of the losses, can actually dominate the regret bound in our setting. Specifically, we show that even with small constant θ \theta , this lower-order term can scale as K ​ T \sqrt{KT} , where K K is the number of experts and T T is the time horizon. We propose adaptive algorithms with improved regret bounds that avoid the dependence on such a lower-order term and guarantee 𝒪 ⁡ ( θ ​ T ​ log ⁡ ( K ) ) \mathcal{O}(\sqrt{\theta T\log(K)}) regret in the worst case, and 𝒪 ⁡ ( θ ​ log ⁡ ( K ​ T ) / Δ min ) \mathcal{O}(\theta\log(KT)/\Delta_{\min}) regret when the losses are sampled i.i.d. from some fixed distribution, where Δ min \Delta_{\min} is the difference between the mean losses of the second best expert and the best expert. Additionally, when the loss function is the squared loss, our algorithm also guarantees improved regret bounds over prior results.



1
Introduction

We study the problem of prediction with expert advice
(
Vovk 1990
;
Littlestone and Warmuth 1994
)
, a sequential decision-making setting over
T
rounds where each round
t
goes as follows. The learner selects a probability distribution
p_{t}\in\mathcal{P}=\bigl\{p\in\mathbb{R}^{K}\colon p(i)\geq 0,\langle p_{t},\mathds{1}\rangle=1\bigr\}
over
K
experts, suffers some loss
f_{t}(p_{t})\in\mathbb{R}
, and subsequently observes the loss function
f_{t}\colon\mathcal{P}\to\mathbb{R}
. The goal in this problem, also known more briefly as the experts setting, is to control the (pseudo-)regret

\displaystyle R_{T}=\max_{i\in\left[K\right]}R_{T}\left(e_{i}\right)=\max_{i\in\left[K\right]}\mathbb{E}\left[\sum_{t=1}^{T}\left(f_{t}\left(p_{t}\right)-f_{t}\left(e_{i}\right)\right)\right]\,,

where the expectation is with respect to the randomness in
(f_{t})_{t\in[T]}
, and
e_{i}\in\mathcal{P}
is the
i
-th standard basis vector. The pseudo-regret measures the expected difference between the learner’s cumulative loss and that of the best expert in hindsight. Throughout the paper, we assume that either the losses or the
outcomes
are heavy tailed, and provide a concrete answer to the concluding remarks of
Mhammedi et al. 2019
that highlight the challenge of dealing with infrequent large values in the full-information setting. Such conditions arise in various practical settings involving noisy data, outliers, or mechanisms like those in local differential privacy
(
van der Hoeven 2019
)
.

A primary goal of this paper is to develop algorithms that achieve sub-linear regret without prior knowledge of a bound on the second moment of the losses, outcomes, or range of the losses
\max_{t\in\left[T\right],p\in\mathcal{P}}\left|f_{t}\left(p\right)\right|
. We will refer to such algorithms as
loss-range adaptive algorithms
. Crucially, unlike the related multi-armed bandit problem, where adaptivity to an unknown second moment is generally impossible without further assumptions
(
Genalti et al. 2024
)
, we demonstrate this is achievable in the experts setting. Our second goal is to aim for algorithms that exhibit strong guarantees both in worst-case scenarios and in more benign stochastic environments, often referred to as
best-of-both-world guarantees
.

While several loss-range adaptive algorithms exist for linear losses,
f_{t}\left(p\right)=\left\langle p,\ell_{t}\right\rangle=\sum_{i=1}^{K}p(i)\ell_{t}(i)
for some
\ell_{t}\in\mathbb{R}^{K}
, they are not designed to handle heavy-tailed losses
(
Blackwell 1956
;
Cesa-Bianchi et al. 2007
;
de Rooij et al. 2014
;
Orabona and Pál 2015
;
Mhammedi et al. 2019
)
. Standard algorithms achieve
\mathcal{O}\left(M\sqrt{T\log K}\right)
regret for losses bounded as
\max_{t,i}\left|\ell_{t}(i)\right|\leq M
. However, if the losses are drawn from some distribution supported on
\mathbb{R}
and such that
\max_{t,i}\mathbb{E}\bigl[\ell_{t}(i)^{2}\bigr]\leq\theta
for some
\theta>0
, the regret guarantees of existing algorithms can degrade to
\mathcal{O}\bigl(\sqrt{KT}\bigr)
, which is exponentially worse in
K
. The issue lies in what prior work consider a lower-order term, namely
\max_{t,i}\left|\ell_{t}(i)\right|
. Indeed, while this term is innocuous for bounded losses, we demonstrate that for heavy-tailed losses with second moment
\theta=\mathcal{O}(1)
, it can be as large as
\Omega\bigl(\sqrt{KT}\bigr)
, thereby becoming the dominant factor in the regret bound.

This paper introduces adaptive algorithms that overcome this limitation. Specifically:

1.

For linear losses, under Assumption
2.1
, Algorithm
1
achieves
\mathcal{O}(\sqrt{\theta T\log(K)})
worst-case regret, effectively removing the detrimental
\sqrt{K}
dependency.

2.

Under Assumption
2.1
, Algorithm
2
achieves
\mathcal{O}(\sqrt{\theta T\log(KT)})
worst-case regret, while also providing a
\mathcal{O}(\theta\log(KT)/\Delta_{\min})
regret in self-bounded environments (Assumption
2.3
), where
\Delta_{\min}
is the gap in expected loss between the two best experts. This contrasts with prior algorithms whose guarantees would still be hampered by the
\mathcal{O}\bigl(\sqrt{KT}\bigr)
term.

3.

For the squared loss,
f_{t}\left(p\right)=\left(\left\langle p,\mathbf{z}_{t}\right\rangle-y_{t}\right)^{2}
, where expert predictions
\mathbf{z}_{t,i}
are bounded by
Y
and the second moment of the outcomes
y_{t}
is bounded by
\sigma
, Algorithm
3
achieves a regret of
\mathcal{O}\left(\left(Y^{2}+\sigma\right)\log\left(K\right)\right)
, avoiding a
\mathcal{O}\left(T\right)
regret that can arise for heavy-tailed outcomes.

The remainder of the paper is structured as follows: Section
2
presents the problem settings and our main results. Section
3
discusses related work in more detail. In Section
4
, we establish why terms previously considered “lower-order” become dominant under heavy-tailed losses for existing algorithms. Section
5
presents our algorithms and sketches their regret analyses, highlighting the techniques used to circumvent the challenges posed by the lack of control over the range of losses. We conclude with a discussion of future work in Section
6
.

2
Preliminaries and Results

We consider two settings. The first, sometimes referred to as the “Hedge setting”
(
Littlestone and Warmuth 1994
)
, can be seen as a special case of the experts setting
(
Vovk 1990
)
with linear losses. In the second, we consider quadratic losses. Throughout the paper, we assume
K,T\geq 2
.

2.1
Hedge Setting

In this section, each round
t=1,\ldots,T
, goes as follows: the learner issues
p_{t}\in\mathcal{P}
, suffers loss
\left\langle p_{t},\ell_{t}\right\rangle
, and then observes
\ell_{t}\in\mathbb{R}^{K}
.
Let
\mathbb{E}_{t}\left[\cdot\right]=\mathbb{E}\left[\cdot\!\mid\!\mathcal{F}_{t}\right]
, where
\mathcal{F}_{t}
is the sigma-field generated by the history
(\ell_{1},\dots,\ell_{t-1})
up to round
t
. We will use the following assumption in the Hedge setting.

Assumption 2.1

(finite second moments)
.

There exists a scalar
\theta\in\mathbb{R}
such that
\mathbb{E}_{t}\bigl[\ell_{t}\left(i\right)^{2}\bigr]\leq\theta
for any
t\in\left[T\right]
and any
i\in\left[K\right]
.

This assumption is more general than the standard bounded losses assumption: if
|\ell_{t}(i)|\leq M
for all
i\in\left[K\right]
,
t\in\left[T\right]
, then
2.1
is satisfied with
\theta=M^{2}
. We
do not assume
to know
\theta
, which is the central algorithmic challenge in the Hedge setting. One of our main results is the following.

Theorem 2.2
.

Consider the Hedge setting and suppose
2.1
holds. Then, there exists an algorithm that, without prior information on the losses, guarantees that
R_{T}=\mathcal{O}\bigl(\sqrt{\theta T\log(K)}\bigr)
.

This is achieved by
Algorithm

2
, which we discuss later in Section
5.2
. The proof can be found in Appendix
C
. While such a result seems expected, we found that loss-range adaptive algorithms in the literature can only guarantee
\mathcal{O}\bigl(\sqrt{\theta T\log(K)}+\sqrt{KT}\bigr)
regret. We detail prior results in
Section

3
and their issues in
Section

4
.

The following assumption allows us to obtain better regret bounds.

Assumption 2.3
.

There exist
\Delta_{\min},C>0
and a unique
i^{\star}=\argmin_{i\in[K]}\E\left[\sum_{t=1}^{T}\ell_{t}(i)\right]
such that

R_{T}\geq\mathbb{E}\Bigl[\sum_{t=1}^{T}\bigl(1-p_{t}\left(i^{\star}\right)\bigr)\Delta_{\min}\Bigr]-C.

This is known as a self-bounded environment
(
Zimmert and Seldin 2021
)
and has been studied in the Hedge setting by
Amir et al. 2020
. We have the following result under this assumption.

Theorem 2.4
.

Consider the Hedge setting and suppose
2.1
holds. Then, there exists an algorithm that, without prior information on the losses, guarantees

R_{T}=\mathcal{O}\bigl(\sqrt{\theta T\log(KT)}\bigr).

Furthermore, if
2.3
also holds, then the same algorithm simultaneously guarantees

R_{T}=\mathcal{O}\Bigl(\frac{\theta\log(KT)}{\Delta_{\min}}+\sqrt{\frac{C\theta\log(KT)}{\Delta_{\min}}}\Bigr).

This is achieved by
Algorithm

1
, which we discuss in
Section

5.1
together with a sketch of the proof. We provide a full proof in Appendix
B
.

2.2
Quadratic Losses

Here we introduce a second setting we consider. In each round
t\in\left[T\right]
, the learner observes the expert predictions
\left(\mathbf{z}_{t,i}\right)_{i\in\left[K\right]}
, issues a prediction
\mathrlap{\hskip 0.5pt\overline{\scalebox{.8}[1]{\phantom{$\mathbf{z}$}}}}\mathbf{z}_{t}=\left\langle p_{t},\mathbf{z}_{t}\right\rangle
, suffers the quadratic loss
f_{t}\left(p_{t}\right)=\left(\mathrlap{\hskip 0.5pt\overline{\scalebox{.8}[1]{\phantom{$\mathbf{z}$}}}}\mathbf{z}_{t}-y_{t}\right)^{2}
, and then observes
y_{t}
. We have the following result.

Theorem 2.5
.

Consider quadratic losses and suppose that
\max_{t,i}\left|\mathbf{z}_{t,i}\right|\leq Y
and
\max_{t}\mathbb{E}_{t}\left[y_{t}^{2}\right]\leq\sigma
. Then there exists an algorithm such that

R_{T}=\mathcal{O}\left(\left(Y^{2}+\sigma\right)\log\left(KT\right)\right).

We prove this result in Appendix
D
. A sketch of the proof is provided in Section
5.3
. Our analysis could also be extended to strongly convex losses. We leave this extension for future work.

Notation.
For any
t\in\left[T\right]
,
i\in\left[K\right]
, let
r_{t}\left(i\right)=\left\langle p_{t},\ell_{t}\right\rangle-\ell_{t}\left(i\right)
be the instantaneous regret,
v_{t}(i)=r_{t}(i)^{2}
, and
\bar{v}_{t}=\sum_{i=1}^{K}p_{t}(i)v_{t}(i)
be the variance of
\ell_{t}\left(I_{t}\right)
with
I_{t}\sim p_{t}
. Furthermore, let
\bar{V}_{T}=\sum_{t=1}^{T}\bar{v}_{t}
,
V_{T}(i)=\sum_{t=1}^{T}v_{t}(i)
,
M_{T}=\mathbb{E}\left[\max_{t\in\left[T\right],i\in\left[K\right]}\left|r_{t}\left(i\right)\right|\right]
, and
L_{T}=\mathbb{E}\left[\max_{t\in\left[T\right],i\in\left[K\right]}\left|\ell_{t}\left(i\right)\right|\right]
. Finally, we denote by
i^{\star}\in\argmin_{i\in\left[K\right]}\mathbb{E}\left[\sum_{t=1}^{T}\ell_{t}\left(i\right)\right]
the best expert in hindsight.

Scale-free algorithms.

A related but different objective is obtaining scale-free or equivalently scale-invariant algorithms. An algorithm is said to be scale-free if the predictions of the algorithm do not change if the sequence of losses is multiplied by a positive constant. While scale-free algorithms are loss-range adaptive the converse is not necessarily true.
Mhammedi 2022
provide a generic wrapper to make any algorithm scale-free, under some mild assumptions on the algorithm. However, this comes at the cost of an additive
M_{T}
. The algorithms of
de Rooij et al. 2014
;
Orabona and Pál 2015
are known to be scale-free. Unfortunately, it is not clear whether our algorithms are also scale-free.

Adaptive algorithms for bounded losses.

If we assume that losses are bounded,
e.g
.
,
\ell_{t}(i)\in[0,1]

Gaillard et al. 2014

Koolen et al. 2016

\mathcal{O}\bigl(\sqrt{T\log(K)}\bigr)
regret without further assumptions on the loss and
\mathcal{O}(\log(K)/\Delta_{\min})
regret under
2.3
with
C=0
. This also implies that the results of
Cesa-Bianchi et al. 2007
;
de Rooij et al. 2014
;
Koolen and van Erven 2015
;
Chen et al. 2021
all lead to small regret under
2.3

while also being robust to more difficult environments.
Mourtada and Gaïffas 2019
show that the exponential weights algorithm with a decreasing learning rate guarantees
\mathcal{O}(\log(K)/\Delta_{\min})
regret under

Online learning with heavy-tailed losses.

To the best of our knowledge, we are the first to consider heavy tailed losses in the expert setting. A related setting is studied by
Bubeck et al. 2013
, who introduce the multi-armed bandits with heavy tails setting. The main difference with our setting is that the learner only gets to see the loss for the chosen action each round, which significantly reduces the amount of available feedback to the learner. Remarkably, without further assumptions, one cannot adapt to
\theta

Genalti et al. 2024

4
Lower-Order Terms with Unbounded Losses

In this section, we show that what have been considered lower-order terms in the literature may actually dominate regret bounds.

4.1
Lower-Order Terms in the Hedge Setting

We provide three results that imply that if a regret bound contains
M_{T}
or
L_{T}
, then this “lower-order term” can dominate the regret bound. We rely on the following observation, proved in Appendix
A
.

Lemma 4.1
.

Fix any
n\in\mathbb{N}\setminus\{0\}
. Let
X_{1},\dots,X_{n}
be non-negative i.i.d. random variables such that

X_{j}=\begin{cases}0&\text{w.p. }1-\frac{1}{n}\\
\sqrt{n}&\text{w.p. }\frac{1}{n}\end{cases}\;.

for each
j\in[n]
. Then,
\E[X_{j}^{2}]=1
for any
j\in[n]
and

\frac{1}{2}\sqrt{n}\leq\E[\max\{X_{1},\dots,X_{n}\}]\leq\sqrt{n}
.

While the distribution in
Lemma

4.1
might appear unnatural, the Fréchet distribution, which is often used in economics, satisfies similar properties as the distribution of
Lemma

4.1
; see
Lemma

A.1
in Appendix
A
.
Lemma

4.1
immediately leads to the following result.

Proposition 4.2
.

There exists a distribution for
\ell_{1},\ldots,\ell_{T}
that satisfies
2.1
with
\theta=4
such that
L_{T}\geq\frac{1}{2}\sqrt{KT}
.

Proof.

Simply choose
\ell_{t}(i)=\mu_{t,i}+\xi_{t,i}\varepsilon_{t,i}
where
\mu_{t,i}\in[0,1]
is chosen arbitrarily,
\xi_{t,i}
is a Rademacher random variable, and
\varepsilon_{t,i}
follow the distribution specified in
Lemma

4.1
with
n=KT
. Then,
Lemma

4.1
provides the result after using
\E_{t}[\ell_{t}(i)^{2}]\leq 2\mu_{t,i}^{2}+2\E_{t}[\varepsilon_{t,i}^{2}]\leq 4
.
∎

In the worst case,
Proposition

4.2
implies that any algorithm with a regret bound of the form
R_{T}\leq\sqrt{\theta T\log(K)}+L_{T}
will be dominated by
L_{T}
for large enough
K
. Existing algorithms sometimes have a
M_{T}\leq 2L_{T}
term in the regret instead. It is natural to question whether
M_{T}
can be small enough. The following proposition shows that
M_{T}
, like
L_{T}
, can be prohibitively large.

Proposition 4.3
.

Suppose one can guarantee a bound
R_{T}\leq B_{T}^{\star}+M_{T}
, with
B_{T}^{\star}\geq 0
. There exists a distribution with
\theta=4
such that
B_{T}^{\star}+M_{T}\geq\frac{1}{2}B_{T}^{\star}+\frac{1}{8}\sqrt{KT}-1
.

The proof of
Proposition

4.3
can be found in
Appendix

A
. While
Proposition

4.3
shows that any regret bound with a term
M_{T}
is
\mathcal{O}\bigl(\sqrt{KT}\bigr)
,
Theorem

2.2
guarantees that
Algorithm

2
achieves
R_{T}=\mathcal{O}\bigl(\sqrt{\theta T\log(K)}\bigr)
. For loss sequences that satisfy
2.3
, the situation is even more dire, as one then hopes to guarantee
\mathcal{O}(\theta\log(K)/\Delta_{\min})
regret.
Proposition

4.3
shows that if one has a regret bound of the form
\theta\log(K)/\Delta_{\min}+M_{T}
, in the worst case this bound is
\mathcal{O}\bigl(\sqrt{KT}\bigr)
. In contrast, in
Theorem

2.4
we provide a
\mathcal{O}(\theta\log(KT)/\Delta_{\min})
regret bound, which is exponentially better in
K
and
T
. Since these results only affect upper bounds on the regret, one can ask whether existing algorithms could benefit from a more careful analysis. We do not think this is possible for existing algorithms and sketch an argument in
Appendix

A
. Furthermore, to illustrate their failure modes, we compare empirically those algorithms with the ones we introduce in Section
5
in Appendix
F
.

4.2
Lower-Order Terms for the Squared Loss

Since the squared loss is exp-concave (or mixable), an appealing approach is to use exponential weights with a suitable learning rate to obtain a regret bound that is seemingly independent of
T
. Indeed, a simple calculation shows that the function
g_{t}(\cdot)=(y_{t}-\cdot)^{2}
is
(2\max_{t,i}(\mathbf{z}_{t,i}-y_{t})^{2})^{-1}
-exp-concave. If one knows
\max_{t,i}(\mathbf{z}_{t,i}-y_{t})^{2}
, this would lead to the following bound
(
Vovk 1990
)
:

R_{T}\leq 2\log(K)\cdot\mathbb{E}\Bigl[\max_{t,i}\,\left(\mathbf{z}_{t,i}-y_{t}\right)^{2}\Bigr]

(1)

However, the
\max_{t,i}\left(\mathbf{z}_{t,i}-y_{t}\right)^{2}
factor might lead to a poor guarantee. Suppose that
\left|\mathbf{z}_{t,i}\right|\leq Y
and that
\mathbb{E}\left[y_{t}^{2}\right]\leq\theta<\infty
. If

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[\max_{t,i}\left(\mathbf{z}_{t,i}-y_{t}\right)^{2}\right]

\displaystyle\geq\mathbb{E}\left[\max_{t}y_{t}^{2}-2Y|y_{t}|\right]\geq\mathbb{E}\left[\max_{t}|y_{t}|\right]^{2}-2Y\mathbb{E}\left[\max_{t}|y_{t}|\right]

5
Algorithms

In this section, we present two types of algorithms and provide a sketch for their regret analysis. The first algorithm, called
LoOT-Free OMD
(
Lo
wer-
O
rder
T
erm
Free
), is an instance of Online Mirror Descent (OMD), whereas the second, called
LoOT-Free FTRL
, is an instance of Follow The Regularized Leader (FTRL). Both algorithms are run on clipped versions of the instantaneous regrets, but one could obtain similar worst-case guarantees by running the algorithms on clipped versions of the losses.

Before we describe the algorithms in more detail, we first need some definitions. For any
t\in[T]
, let
\eta_{t}\in\mathbb{R}_{>0}^{K}
be a vector of learning rates and
\psi_{t}:x\in\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}^{K}\mapsto\left\langle\Diag\left(\eta_{t}^{-1}\right)x,\log x\right\rangle
be the regularizer, where
y^{-1}
is the coordinate-wise inverse,
\log y
is the coordinate-wise logarithm, and
\Diag(y)
is the diagonal matrix with diagonal entries
y_{1},\dots,y_{K}
for any
y\in\mathbb{R}^{K}
. Note that, for any
x\in\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}^{K}
, the gradient of
\psi_{t}
is given by
\nabla\psi_{t}\left(x\right)=\Diag\left(\eta_{t}^{-1}\right)\log x+\eta_{t}^{-1}
. Consider also its Bregman divergence, defined for any
x\in\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}^{K}
and
y\in\mathbb{R}_{>0}^{K}
as
D_{t}\left(x\|y\right)=\psi_{t}\left(x\right)-\psi_{t}\left(y\right)-\left\langle\nabla\psi_{t}\left(y\right),x-y\right\rangle=\sum_{i=1}^{K}\frac{1}{\eta_{t,i}}\left[x_{i}\log\left(\frac{x_{i}}{y_{i}}\right)-x_{i}+y_{i}\right]
. Finally, denote by
\mathcal{P}_{\alpha}=\{p\in\mathcal{P}:\min_{i}p_{i}\geq\alpha/K\}
the probability simplex truncated by
\alpha/K
, for any
\alpha\in[0,1]
.

LoOT-Free OMD

The main challenge in designing an algorithm for losses for which you do not know the range comes from proving the stability of the algorithm, which is to say that
p_{t}(i)\approx p_{t+1}(i)
. An analysis based on strong convexity circumvents this challenge.
Orabona and Pál 2015
show that if the regularizer
\phi
is strongly convex with respect to some norm
\|\cdot\|
, the regret can be
\mathcal{O}\Bigl(\sqrt{\max_{p\in\mathcal{P}}\phi(p)\sum_{t=1}^{T}{\|\ell_{t}\|_{\star}^{2}}}\Bigr)
, where
\|\cdot\|_{\star}
is the dual norm. However, if one uses the (shifted) negative Shannon entropy as
\phi
, this will lead to a
\mathcal{O}\Bigl(\sqrt{\log(K)\sum_{t=1}^{T}{\|\ell_{t}\|_{\infty}^{2}}}\Bigr)
bound, which is
\mathcal{O}(\sqrt{KT})
in the worst case. A more careful analysis that avoids strong convexity arguments can be found in
de Rooij et al. 2014
, but unfortunately this analysis does not avoid a problematic lower-order term.
To avoid such issues, we make use of the multi-scale entropic regularizer of
Bubeck et al. 2019
. If the range of the losses is known a-priori,
Bubeck et al. 2019
show that for bounded losses and known
\max_{t}|\ell_{t}(i)|
the regret against expert
i
then scales as
\E[\max_{t}|\ell_{t}(i)|]\sqrt{T\log(K)}
. However, this alone is not sufficient, as it is not clear how to prove that the algorithm is stable without an a-priori uniform bound on the losses, nor is such a regret bound sufficiently small. Instead, we carefully clip the losses when they are excessively large.
Combining these ideas leads to the following guarantee for
Algorithm

1
with
\alpha=\frac{1}{T}
and
\beta=\sqrt{\log(KT)}
:

\displaystyle\sum_{t=1}^{T}r_{t}(i^{\star})=\mathcal{O}\left(\sqrt{\log(KT)\left(\bar{V}_{T}+V_{T}\left(i^{\star}\right)\right)}\right)\;.

(2)

Notice that the above upper bound holds with probability one for any sequence of losses and, in turn, leads to the guarantees of
Theorem

2.4
.
A full proof of the above result can be found in Appendix
B
. Here we provide some intuition.

Proof sketch of
Theorem

2.4
.

First, observe that
Algorithm

1
is an instance of OMD adopting the time-varying regularizer
\psi_{t}
with some additional tricks. We only update
p_{t}
if
\sum_{s\leq t}\bar{v}_{s}>0
. The only case where this is not true is if in all rounds up to and including round
t
,
r_{t}(i)=0
for all
i\in[K]
, in which case we can simply ignore these rounds in the analysis. From standard OMD analysis (see for example
Orabona 2023
), we know that if we run OMD on losses
\widetilde{\ell}_{t}
, for any fixed
j\in[K]
,

\displaystyle\sum_{t=1}^{T}\bigl(\langle p_{t},\widetilde{\ell}_{t}\rangle-\widetilde{\ell}_{t}(j)\bigr)

\displaystyle=\mathcal{O}\biggl(\frac{\log(K/\alpha)}{\eta_{T,j}}+\sum_{t=1}^{T}\sum_{i=1}^{K}\eta_{t,i}\widetilde{p}_{t}(i)\widetilde{\ell}_{t}(i)^{2}\biggr)\;,

where
\widetilde{p}_{t}(i)=p_{t}(i)\exp\bigl(-\eta_{t,i}\widetilde{\ell}_{t}(i)\bigr)
. At this point, we face our main challenge. We would like to show that
\widetilde{p}_{t}(i)\approx p_{t}(i)
. To do so, we would need to show that
|\eta_{t,i}\widetilde{\ell}_{t}(i)|\leq 1
. We force this to be true by simply setting
\widetilde{\ell}_{t}(i)=0
if
|r_{t}(i)|>\frac{1}{\eta_{t,i}}
and
\widetilde{\ell}_{t}(i)=-r_{t}(i)
otherwise. Of course, there is a price to pay for clipping the losses, but this price is negligible:

\displaystyle\bigl|\widetilde{\ell}_{t}(i)-(-r_{t}(i))\bigr|

\displaystyle=|r_{t}(i)|\mathds{1}\biggl(|r_{t}(i)|>\frac{1}{\eta_{t,i}}\biggr)=\frac{r_{t}(i)^{2}}{|r_{t}(i)|}\mathds{1}\biggl(|r_{t}(i)|>\frac{1}{\eta_{t,i}}\biggr)\leq\eta_{t,i}v_{t}(i)\;.

(3)

Thus, after noting that
\ell_{t}
and
-r_{t}
only differ from a constant,
we have that the regret against expert
j
is

\sum_{t=1}^{T}\bigl(\langle p_{t},\ell_{t}\rangle-\ell_{t}(j)\bigr)=\sum_{t=1}^{T}\bigl(\langle p_{t},-r_{t}\rangle-(-r_{t}(j))\bigr)\,,

and we can replace
-r_{t}
by the truncated losses
\widetilde{\ell}_{t}
by paying the cost shown in
Equation

3
and use the guarantee from OMD

\displaystyle\sum_{t=1}^{T}\bigl(\langle p_{t},\ell_{t}\rangle-\ell_{t}(j)\bigr)

\displaystyle=\mathcal{O}\Biggl(\sum_{t=1}^{T}\bigl(\langle p_{t},\widetilde{\ell}_{t}\rangle-\widetilde{\ell}_{t}(j)\bigr)+\sum_{t=1}^{T}\Bigl(\eta_{t,j}v_{t}(j)+\sum_{i=1}^{K}p_{t}(i)\eta_{t,i}v_{t}(i)\Bigr)\Biggr)

\displaystyle=\mathcal{O}\Biggl(\frac{\log(K/\alpha)}{\eta_{T,j}}+\sum_{t=1}^{T}\Bigl(\eta_{t,j}v_{t}(j)+\sum_{i=1}^{K}p_{t}(i)\eta_{t,i}v_{t}(i)\Bigr)\!\Biggr)\;.

After controlling the sum by using the definition of
\eta_{t,i}
, the bound in
Equation

2
follows as

\displaystyle\sum_{t=1}^{T}\Bigl(\eta_{t,j}v_{t}(j)+\sum_{i=1}^{K}p_{t}(i)\eta_{t,i}v_{t}(i)\Bigr)

\displaystyle\leq\beta\sum_{t=1}^{T}\Biggl(\frac{v_{t}(j)}{\sqrt{V_{t}(j)}}+\frac{\bar{v}_{t}}{\sqrt{\bar{V}_{t}}}\Biggr)\leq 4\beta\sqrt{\bar{V}_{T}+V_{T}(j)}\,,

where the first inequality follows from the definition of
\eta_{t,i}
and the second follows from, for example, Lemma 4.13 in
Orabona 2023
.
∎

Regarding computational aspects, note the optimization problem defining
p_{t+1}
is done on a truncated simplex, thus it does not have a closed-form solution but can be computed efficiently with a line-search as done by
Chen et al. 2021
.

5.2
FTRL-based Algorithm

Taking some ideas from the previous OMD-based algorithm, we derive an instantiation of FTRL described by
Algorithm

2
in Appendix
C
.
For FTRL we also use the multi-scale entropic regularizer
\psi_{t}
and a similar clipping of the losses to resolve the same issues that follow from the lack of any prior knowledge of the loss range.
However, the FTRL framework has some fundamental differences compared to OMD, which in turn require further care. To understand these differences, we will sketch the proof of
Theorem

2.2
below, whose result is obtained by
Algorithm

2
with
\beta=\sqrt{\log(K)}
. The detailed proof of
Theorem

2.2
can be found in Appendix
C
. Here we provide the main ideas.
We actually prove a stronger regret guarantee than provided in
Theorem

2.2
:

\displaystyle\sum_{t=1}^{T}r_{t}(i^{\star})

\displaystyle=\mathcal{O}\left(\sqrt{\log(K)\bigl(\bar{V}_{T}+V_{T}(i^{\star})\bigr)}+\frac{1}{K}\sum_{i=1}^{K}\sqrt{V_{T}(i)}\right)\;,

(4)

which then implies the statement of
Theorem

2.2
.

To prove this regret bound we make use of the standard FTRL analysis (e.g., see
Orabona 2023
) with some additional tricks. Note that, differently from
Algorithm

1
, we replace the multi-scale entropic regularizer
\psi_{t}
with its Bregman divergence. This is to ensure the monotonicity of the regularizer, i.e.,
\varphi_{t+1}(x)\geq\varphi_{t}(x)
for all
x\in\mathcal{P}
. This monotonicity is necessary for the proof of the FTRL regret bound. The fact that we use the Bregman divergence generated by
\psi_{t}
rather than
\psi_{t}
directly as the regularizer also allows us to avoid the
\log(T)
term of the OMD regret bound, at the cost of a
\frac{1}{K}\sum_{i=1}^{K}\sqrt{\sum_{t=1}^{T}v_{t}(i)}
term in the regret. While this term is prohibitively big when we try to obtain improved regret bounds under
2.3
, in expectation this term scales as
\mathcal{O}\bigl(\sqrt{\theta T}\bigr)
under
2.1
, thus preserving the desired final bound.

Proof sketch of
Theorem

2.2
.

Since the
p_{t}
in
Algorithm

2
comes from an instance of FTRL over the losses
\widetilde{\ell}_{t}
, we can apply a standard FTRL bound (for example, Lemma 7.14 of
Orabona 2023
) to see that, for any fixed
j\in[K]
,

\sum_{t=1}^{T}\bigl(\langle p_{t},\widetilde{\ell}_{t}\rangle-\widetilde{\ell}_{t}(j)\bigr)=\mathcal{O}\left(\frac{\log K}{\eta_{T,j}}+\frac{1}{K}\sum_{i=1}^{K}\frac{1}{\eta_{t,i}}+\sum_{t=1}^{T}\sum_{i=1}^{K}\eta_{t-1,i}\widetilde{p}_{t}(i)\widetilde{\ell}_{t}(i)^{2}\right)\;,

(5)

where
\widetilde{p}_{t}(i)=p_{t}(i)\exp\bigl(-\eta_{t-1,i}\widetilde{\ell}_{t}(i)\bigr)
.
Here we reach the main challenge of adapting FTRL to our setting.
The third term illustrates crucial differences compared to OMD: the learning rate
\eta_{t-1,i}
instead of
\eta_{t,i}
appears as a multiplicative factor and in the definition of
\widetilde{p}_{t}
.
We resolve this issue by rescaling the loss
-r_{t}(i)
by a factor
b_{t-1}(i)/b_{t}(i)
in the definition of
\widetilde{\ell}_{t}(i)
. Still, this rescaled loss can be too big, which is why we also clip the loss when necessary.
Specifically, the clipping with a rescaling factor ensures, by the definitions of
b_{t}(i)
and
\eta_{t,i}
, that
\eta_{t-1,i}\widetilde{\ell}_{t}(i)^{2}\leq\frac{b_{t-1}(i)}{b_{t}(i)}\eta_{t-1,i}r_{t}(i)^{2}=\eta_{t,i}v_{t}(i)
and, similarly, that
|\eta_{t-1,i}\widetilde{\ell}_{t}(i)|\leq 1
.
Thus, we can bound the third term on the right hand side of
Equation

5
by
\mathcal{O}\Bigl(\sum_{t=1}^{T}\sum_{i=1}^{K}\eta_{t,i}p_{t}(i)v_{t}(i)\Bigr)
, which we already know how to control from the analysis of
Algorithm

1
.

Another consequence of the new definition of
\widetilde{\ell}_{t}
is that it increases the cost of clipping the losses. In comparison to
Equation

3
, it now additionally presents terms

\displaystyle|r_{t}(i)|\left(1-\frac{b_{t-1}(i)}{b_{t}(i)}\right)\mathds{1}\left(|r_{t}(i)|\leq\frac{1}{\eta_{t,i}}\right)

\displaystyle\leq\frac{1}{\eta_{t,i}}-\frac{1}{\eta_{t-1,i}}

but the sums involving them are also nicely behaved.
Combining all these observations leads to
Equation

4
.
∎

While the optimization problem suggests
p_{t}
is given by a softmax function, the coordinate-dependent learning rate prevents us from computing the normalization constant in closed-form (see Appendix
C.1
for details). However, we can still compute it efficiently with a line-search as in Algorithm 1 from
Bubeck et al. 2019
.

5.3
Algorithm for the Squared Loss

The algorithm we use for the squared loss,
Algorithm

3
can be found in
Appendix

D
. It is exactly the same as
Algorithm

1
, but then run on the losses

\displaystyle\ell_{t}\left(i\right)=\mathbf{z}_{t,i}\left(\mathrlap{\hskip 0.5pt\overline{\scalebox{.8}[1]{\phantom{$\mathbf{z}$}}}}\mathbf{z}_{t}-y_{t}\right)+\frac{1}{2}\left(\mathbf{z}_{t,i}-y_{t}\right)^{2}\,,

where
\mathrlap{\hskip 0.5pt\overline{\scalebox{.8}[1]{\phantom{$\mathbf{z}$}}}}\mathbf{z}_{t}=\langle p_{t},\mathbf{z}\rangle
.
The inspiration from this surrogate loss comes from two inequalities for the squared loss:

\displaystyle(a-y)^{2}-(b-y)^{2}

\displaystyle=2(y-a)(b-a)-(a-b)^{2}\;,

\displaystyle\Bigl(\frac{a+b}{2}-y\Bigr)^{\!2}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{(a-y)^{2}}{2}+\frac{(b-y)^{2}}{2}-\frac{(a-b)^{2}}{8}\,.

By carefully applying these inequalities we find that, for any fixed
j\in[K]
,

\displaystyle\sum_{t=1}^{T}\bigl((\bar{\mathbf{z}}_{t}-y_{t})^{2}-(\mathbf{z}_{t,j}-y_{t})^{2}\bigr)

\displaystyle\quad\leq\sum_{t=1}^{T}\biggl(\sum_{i=1}^{K}p_{t}(i)\ell_{t}(i)-\ell_{t}(j)-\sum_{i=1}^{K}p_{t}(i)\frac{(\mathbf{z}_{t,i}-\bar{\mathbf{z}}_{t})^{2}}{8}-\frac{(\mathbf{z}_{t,j}-\bar{\mathbf{z}}_{t})^{2}}{2}\biggr).

The negative quadratics on the right-hand-side of the equation above allow us prove the regret bound of
Theorem

2.5
. We show that, for any fixed
j\in[K]
,

\displaystyle R_{T}(e_{j})

\displaystyle=\mathcal{O}\!\Biggl(\E\left[\sqrt{\log(KT)(\bar{V}_{T}+V_{T}(j))}\right]-\E\left[\sum_{t=1}^{T}\!\left(\sum_{i=1}^{K}\frac{p_{t}(i)(\mathbf{z}_{t,i}-\bar{\mathbf{z}}_{t})^{2}}{8}+\frac{(\mathbf{z}_{t,j}-\bar{\mathbf{z}}_{t})^{2}}{2}\right)\right]\Biggr)

\displaystyle=\mathcal{O}\!\left((Y^{2}+\sigma)\log(KT)\right)\;.

A detailed proof can be found in
Appendix

D
.

6
Future Work

One direction to explore is whether the
\log(T)
factor in
Theorem

2.4
is necessary to obtain best-of-both-worlds guarantees. Potentially, an improved analysis of the FTRL algorithm will do the trick, as we know that OMD can be inferior to FTRL
(
Amir et al. 2020
)
. While we understand how to obtain best-of-both-worlds guarantees for FTRL with bounded losses
(
Mourtada and Gaïffas 2019
;
Amir et al. 2020
)
, it is unclear how to adapt these analyses to our setting. Another interesting direction is to see whether the ideas in
Section

5.3
can be extended to strongly convex and exp-concave losses. We believe that the former is relatively straightforward, but the latter might be highly challenging. Another relatively straightforward extension could be to adapt to any moment of the loss,
i.e
.
, adapt to
\E_{t}[|\ell_{t}(i)|^{\alpha}]
for some
\alpha>1
, without the prior knowledge of
\alpha
or an upper bound for
\E_{t}[|\ell_{t}(i)|^{\alpha}]
.

Appendix A
Technical Results for Section
4

In Section
4
, we discussed lower bounds for the lower-order terms. These lower bounds are achieved by adopting specific binary random variables in the construction of the losses.
This is shown in
Lemma

4.1
and its proof is provided in what follows.

A.1
Proof of Lemma
4.1
and Fréchet Distributions

Proof of
Lemma

4.1
.

The fact that
\E[X_{j}^{2}]=1
follows by an immediate calculation after observing that each
X_{j}
is a binary random variable. By independence, we also have that
\E[\max_{j\in[n]}X_{j}]=\sqrt{n}\bigl(1-(1-\frac{1}{n})^{n}\bigr)
. The result now follows from observing that
1/2<1-(1-\frac{1}{n})^{n}\leq 1
, where we used the inequality
1-x\leq\exp(-x)
.
∎

Here, we now discuss another family of random variables that guarantee similar properties.
This family is that of Fréchet distributions
[
Fréchet 1927
]
—see also
Muraleedharan et al. 2011
for reference.

We denote by
\Frechet(\alpha,s,m)
the Fréchet distribution whose parameters are the shape parameter
\alpha>0
, the scale parameter
s>0
, and the location (of the minimum) parameter
m\in\mathbb{R}
.
Its cumulative distribution function (CDF) is

\mathbb{P}(X\leq x)=\exp\left(-\left(\frac{x-m}{s}\right)^{-\alpha}\right)

for
x>m
, where
X\sim\Frechet(\alpha,s,m)
.
If
\alpha>1
, its expected value corresponds to
\E[X]=m+s\Gamma(1-1/\alpha)
, where
\Gamma(z)=\int_{0}^{\infty}t^{z-1}\exp(-t)\mathrm{d}t
for
z>0
is the Gamma function.

Lemma A.1
.

Fix any
n\in\mathbb{N}\setminus\{0\}
.
Let
X_{1},\dots,X_{n}\sim\Frechet(\alpha,s,0)
be i.i.d. random variables with
\alpha>1
and
s>0
.
Then, for any
1\leq\beta<\alpha
and any
j\in[n]
,

\E[X^{\beta}_{j}]=s^{\beta}\Gamma\left(1-\frac{\beta}{\alpha}\right)\;.

Furthermore,

\E[\max\{X_{1},\dots,X_{n}\}]=n^{1/\alpha}s\Gamma\left(1-\frac{1}{\alpha}\right)\;.

Proof.

All results are standard. Here we provide explicit calculations for completeness.
First, observe that for any
X\sim\Frechet(\alpha,s,0)
we have that

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\left(X^{\beta}\leq x\right)=\mathbb{P}\left(X\leq x^{1/\beta}\right)=\exp\left(-\left(\frac{x^{1/\beta}}{s}\right)^{-\alpha}\right)=\exp\left(-\left(\frac{x}{s^{\beta}}\right)^{-\alpha/\beta}\right)\;,

showing that
X^{\beta}\sim\Frechet(\alpha/\beta,s^{\beta},0)
.
As a consequence,
\E[X^{\beta}]=s^{\beta}\Gamma(1-\beta/\alpha)
since
\alpha/\beta>1
.

Second, let
Y=\max\{X_{1},\ldots,X_{n}\}
and
y>0
. By independence, one has

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\left(Y\leq y\right)

\displaystyle=\mathbb{P}\left(\bigcap_{j\in\left[n\right]}\{X_{j}\leq y\}\right)=\prod_{j\in\left[n\right]}\mathbb{P}\left(X_{j}\leq y\right)

\displaystyle=\prod_{j\in\left[n\right]}\exp\left(-\left(y/s\right)^{-\alpha}\right)=\exp\left(-\left(\frac{y}{n^{1/\alpha}s}\right)^{-\alpha}\right)\;,

which means that
Y\sim\Frechet(\alpha,n^{1/\alpha}s,0)
. Since
\alpha>1
, its expectation is thus
\E[Y]=n^{1/\alpha}s\Gamma\left(1-1/\alpha\right)
.
∎

Keeping the results from the lemma above in mind, we can consider a more specific case where the Fréchet random variables have scale
s=1
and
\alpha>2
.
Then, for
n=KT
and
\beta=2
, we clearly have that their second moment equals
\Gamma(1-2/\alpha)
, whereas the expectation of their maximum corresponds to
(KT)^{1/\alpha}\Gamma(1-1/\alpha)
.
This shows that the i.i.d. Fréchet random variables as described above behave somewhat similarly to the random variables of
Lemma

4.1
we employed in our lower bounds.

Moving back to the original construction from
Lemma

4.1
, we can adopt it to prove the main lower bound on any regret guarantee containing the
M_{T}
term (or a constant fraction of it).
This result from
Section

4
is stated in
Proposition

4.3
and here we provide its proof.

A.2
Proof of Proposition
4.3

Proof.

Let
\ell_{t}(i)=\mu_{t,i}+\xi_{t,i}\varepsilon_{t,i}
, where
\mu_{t,i}\in[-1,1]
,
\xi_{t,i}
is sampled from a Rademacher distribution, and
\varepsilon_{t,i}
is sampled from the distribution specified in
Lemma

4.1
with
n=KT
. Let
\sum_{t=1}^{T}\mu_{t,K}=0
and let
\sum_{t=1}^{T}\mu_{t,i}>0
for
i\neq K
. By assumption, we have that

\displaystyle R_{T}=\E\left[\sum_{t=1}^{T}\Bigl(\sum_{i=1}^{K}p_{t}(i)\ell_{t}(i)-\ell_{t}(K)\Bigr)\right]

\displaystyle=\E\left[\sum_{t=1}^{T}\sum_{i=1}^{K}p_{t}(i)\ell_{t}(i)\right]\leq B^{\star}_{T}+\E\left[\max_{t\in[T],i\in[K]}|r_{t}(i)|\right]\;.

Suppose that
\tfrac{1}{2}\E\bigl[\max_{t\in[T],i\in[K]}|\ell_{t}(i)|\bigr]\geq\E\bigl[\max_{t\in[T],i\in[K]}|r_{t}(i)|\bigr]
. Then,

\displaystyle\E\left[\max_{t\in[T],i\in[K]}|r_{t}(i)|\right]

\displaystyle=\E\left[\max_{t\in[T],i\in[K]}\biggl|\ell_{t}(i)-\sum_{j=1}^{K}p_{t}(j)\ell_{t}(j)\biggr|\right]

\displaystyle\geq\E\left[\max_{t\in[T],i\in[K]}\ell_{t}(i)-\max_{t\in[T]}\sum_{j=1}^{K}p_{t}(j)\ell_{t}(j)\right]

\displaystyle\geq\E\left[\max_{t\in[T],i\in[K]}|\ell_{t}(i)|-\sum_{t=1}^{T}\sum_{j=1}^{K}p_{t}(j)\ell_{t}(j)\right]

\displaystyle=\E\left[\max_{t\in[T],i\in[K]}|\ell_{t}(i)|\right]-R_{T}

\displaystyle\geq\E\left[\max_{t\in[T],i\in[K]}|\ell_{t}(i)|\right]-B^{\star}_{T}-\E\left[\max_{t\in[T],i\in[K]}|r_{t}(i)|\right]

\displaystyle\geq\E\left[\max_{t\in[T],i\in[K]}|\ell_{t}(i)|\right]-B^{\star}_{T}-\frac{1}{2}\E\left[\max_{t\in[T],i\in[K]}|\ell_{t}(i)|\right]

and thus

\displaystyle B_{T}^{\star}+\E\left[\max_{t\in[T],i\in[K]}|r_{t}(i)|\right]

\displaystyle\geq B_{T}^{\star}+\frac{1}{2}\E\left[\max_{t\in[T],i\in[K]}|r_{t}(i)|\right]

\displaystyle\geq\frac{1}{2}B_{T}^{\star}+\frac{1}{4}\E\left[\max_{t\in[T],i\in[K]}|\ell_{t}(i)|\right]

\displaystyle\geq\frac{1}{2}B_{T}^{\star}+\frac{1}{4}\E\left[\max_{t\in[T],i\in[K]}|\varepsilon_{t,i}|\right]-1

\displaystyle\geq\frac{1}{2}B_{T}^{\star}+\frac{1}{8}\sqrt{KT}-1\;,

where the last inequality follows from
Lemma

4.1
.

Otherwise, in the case where
\tfrac{1}{2}\E\bigl[\max_{t\in[T],i\in[K]}|\ell_{t}(i)|\bigr]<\E\bigl[\max_{t\in[T],i\in[K]}|r_{t}(i)|\bigr]
we have

\displaystyle B_{T}^{\star}+\E\left[\max_{t\in[T],i\in[K]}|r_{t}(i)|\right]\geq B_{T}^{\star}+\frac{1}{2}\E\left[\max_{t\in[T],i\in[K]}|\ell_{t}(i)|\right]\geq B_{T}^{\star}+\frac{1}{4}\sqrt{KT}\;.

Finally, we have
\E_{t}[\ell_{t}(i)^{2}]\leq 2\mu_{t,i}^{2}+2\E_{t}[\varepsilon_{t,i}^{2}]\leq 4
.
∎

A.3
Discussion on analyses of existing algorithms

Our results from
Section

4
show that the lower-order terms are a problematic component of the regret bounds of existing algorithms.
In particular, we have shown that the lower-order terms can be larger than the ideal
\sqrt{\theta T\log K}
in the case of heavy-tailed losses with bounded second moments.
It is important to note that our general argument does not depend on the specific algorithm we consider, but rather on regret bounds involving terms such as
L_{T}
or
M_{T}
.
Hence, generally speaking, one may believe that the presence of
L_{T}
or
M_{T}
in the regret guarantees could be an artefact of a loose analysis.
However, here we show that this is not the case for most existing algorithms.

To illustrate, let us consider a simplified view that captures the essential mechanism.
The algorithms in
Table

1
are effectively sophisticated modifications of the exponential weights (EW) algorithm designed to adapt to
H_{T}=\max_{t,i}|\ell_{t}(i)|
.
Their sampling distributions are of the form
p_{t}(i)\propto\exp(-\eta_{t}\ell_{t}(i))
, where we assume a fixed learning
\eta>0
for simplicity.
A crucial aspect of these adaptive algorithms is choosing a learning rate related to
H_{T}
, often resembling
\eta=\min\bigl\{\alpha,\frac{1}{H_{T}}\bigr\}
, where
\alpha>0
represents some cap and where we assume that
H_{T}
is known.
Their choice ensures the control of terms like
\log\bigl(\E_{i\sim p_{t}}[\exp(-\eta\ell_{t}(i))]\bigr)
, which is an essential component in any regret analysis of EW-based algorithms.

Now, we construct a sequence of losses for which the regret of such an algorithm is always at least
\E[\log(4/3)/\eta]
.
Suppose
K\geq 5
and let
\ell_{t}(i)=1+\varepsilon_{t,i}
for each
i\in[K-1]
, where
\varepsilon_{t,i}
follows the same distribution as in
Lemma

4.1
; for action
K
, we instead choose
\ell_{t}(K)=0
.
By Jensen’s inequality and the definition of
p_{t}
, we have that

\displaystyle R_{T}

\displaystyle=\E\left[\sum_{t=1}^{T}\langle p_{t},\ell_{T}\rangle\right]

\displaystyle\geq\E\left[-\frac{1}{\eta}\sum_{t=1}^{T}\log\bigl(\E_{i\sim p_{t}}\left[\exp(-\eta\ell_{t}(i))\right]\bigr)\right]

\displaystyle=\E\left[\frac{1}{\eta}\log(K)-\frac{1}{\eta}\log\left(\sum_{i=1}^{K}\exp\left(-\eta\sum_{t=1}^{T}\ell_{t}(i)\right)\right)\right]

\displaystyle\geq\E\left[\frac{1}{\eta}\log(K)-\frac{1}{\eta}\log\bigl(1+(K-1)\exp\left(-\eta T\right)\bigr)\right]\;,

where the last inequality is due to the definition of
\ell_{t}(i)
.
It is safe to assume that
\eta T<1
, as otherwise we would obtain a vacuous upper bound from the standard EW analysis.
Therefore, we have that

\displaystyle R_{T}

\displaystyle\geq\E\left[\frac{1}{\eta}\log(K)-\frac{1}{\eta}\log\left(1+(K-1)/2\right)\right]

\displaystyle\geq\E\left[\frac{1}{\eta}\log(K)-\frac{1}{\eta}\log\left(3(K-1)/4\right)\right]

\displaystyle\geq\E\left[\frac{1}{\eta}\right]\log(4/3)

\displaystyle\geq L_{T}\log(4/3)\;,

where the last inequality follows from the definitions of
\eta
and
L_{T}=\E[H_{T}]
.
This suggests the regret is lower bounded by a term proportional to the maximum loss range.
For algorithms like Squint that run EW on the surrogate losses like
\ell_{t}(i)-\eta\ell_{t}(i)^{2}
, a similar but slightly more involved argument can be made.
Consequently, the
L_{T}
(or
M_{T}
) term appears to be unavoidable in the regret of these algorithms.

Similarly, regarding
Section

4.2
, the existing algorithms for the squared loss setting are also fundamentally related to EW, adapted to quadratic losses.
Their learning rates are typically dependent on the range
\max_{t}|\mathrlap{\hskip 0.5pt\overline{\scalebox{.8}[1]{\phantom{$\mathbf{z}$}}}}\mathbf{z}_{t}-y_{t}|
in a manner analogous to the dependence on
H_{T}
described above.
Therefore, we believe a similar limitation applies, making it unlikely that a different analysis of previous algorithms could circumvent the dependence on lower-order terms in their regret.

\displaystyle\leq\eta_{t,i}\left(\frac{1}{\eta_{t,i}^{2}}-\frac{1}{\eta_{t-1,i}^{2}}\right)

(7)

\displaystyle=\frac{\max\left\{\sum_{s\leq t}\bar{v}_{s},\sum_{s\leq t}v_{s}\left(i\right)\right\}-\max\left\{\sum_{s<t}\bar{v}_{s},\sum_{s<t}v_{s}\left(i\right)\right\}}{\beta\sqrt{\max\left\{\sum_{s\leq t}\bar{v}_{s},\sum_{s\leq t}v_{s}\left(i\right)\right\}}}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{\max\left\{\bar{v}_{t},v_{t}\left(i\right)\right\}}{\beta\sqrt{\max\left\{\sum_{s\leq t}\bar{v}_{s},\sum_{s\leq t}v_{s}\left(i\right)\right\}}}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{\bar{v}_{t}+v_{t}\left(i\right)}{\beta\sqrt{\sum_{s\leq t}\bar{v}_{s}}}\;.

(8)

Therefore, adding everything up

\displaystyle\sum_{t=1}^{T}\sum_{i=1}^{K}\left(\frac{1}{\eta_{t,i}}-\frac{1}{\eta_{t-1,i}}\right)p_{t}\left(i\right)

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{t=1}^{T}\sum_{i=1}^{K}\frac{\bar{v}_{t}+v_{t}\left(i\right)}{\beta\sqrt{\sum_{s\leq t}\bar{v}_{s}}}p_{t}\left(i\right)

\displaystyle=\frac{2}{\beta}\sum_{t=1}^{T}\frac{\bar{v}_{t}}{\sqrt{\sum_{s\leq t}\bar{v}_{s}}}

(
p_{t}\in\mathcal{P}
, definition
\bar{v}_{t}
)

\displaystyle\leq\frac{4}{\beta}\sqrt{\sum_{t=1}^{T}\bar{v}_{t}}\;,

where the last inequality follows again from
Orabona 2023
. Putting it back into the previous inequality, we get

\displaystyle\sum_{t=2}^{T}\left(D_{t}\left(u_{i^{\star}}\|p_{t}\right)-D_{t-1}\left(u_{i^{\star}}\|p_{t}\right)\right)

\displaystyle\leq\frac{\log\left(K/\alpha\right)}{\eta_{T,i^{\star}}}+\frac{4}{\beta}\sqrt{\sum_{t=1}^{T}\bar{v}_{t}}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{\log\left(K/\alpha\right)}{\beta}\left(\sqrt{\sum_{t=1}^{T}v_{t}\left(i^{\star}\right)}+\sqrt{\sum_{t=1}^{T}\bar{v}_{t}}\right)+\frac{4}{\beta}\sqrt{\sum_{t=1}^{T}\bar{v}_{t}}\;.

Finally, the regret incurred by OMD on the truncated losses is bounded by

\widetilde{R}_{T}\left(e_{i^{\star}}\right)\leq\left(\sqrt{\alpha T}+3\beta+\frac{4+\log\left(K/\alpha\right)}{\beta}\right)\sqrt{\sum_{t=1}^{T}\bar{v}_{t}}+\left(\sqrt{\alpha T}+\frac{\log\left(K/\alpha\right)}{\beta}\right)\sqrt{\sum_{t=1}^{T}v_{t}\left(i^{\star}\right)}\;.

Step 2: control the cost of truncation.

We have

\displaystyle\mathtt{trunc}_{T}\left(p_{1:T}\right)

\displaystyle=\sum_{t=1}^{T}\sum_{i=1}^{K}p_{t}\left(i\right)\left(\bar{\ell}_{t}\left(i\right)-\widetilde{\ell}_{t}\left(i\right)\right)

\displaystyle=\sum_{t=1}^{T}\sum_{i=1}^{K}p_{t}\left(i\right)\bar{\ell}_{t}\left(i\right)\mathds{1}\left(\left|\bar{\ell}_{t}\left(i\right)\right|>\eta_{t,i}^{-1}\right)

(definition of
\widetilde{\ell}_{t}
)

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{t=1}^{T}\sum_{i=1}^{K}p_{t}\left(i\right)\frac{\bar{\ell}_{t}\left(i\right)^{2}}{\left|\bar{\ell}_{t}\left(i\right)\right|}\mathds{1}\left(\left|\bar{\ell}_{t}\left(i\right)\right|>\eta_{t,i}^{-1}\right)

\displaystyle\left(p_{t}\geq 0\right)

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{t=1}^{T}\sum_{i=1}^{K}p_{t}\left(i\right)\eta_{t,i}\bar{\ell}_{t}\left(i\right)^{2}.

Plugging the definition of the learning rates
\eta_{t}
, we obtain

\displaystyle\mathtt{trunc}_{T}\left(p_{1:T}\right)

\displaystyle\leq\beta\sum_{t=1}^{T}\sum_{i=1}^{K}p_{t}\left(i\right)\frac{\bar{\ell}_{t}\left(i\right)^{2}}{\sqrt{\max\left\{\sum_{s=1}^{t}\bar{v}_{s},\sum_{s=1}^{t}v_{s}\left(i\right)\right\}}}

(definition of
\eta_{t}
)

\displaystyle\leq\beta\sum_{t=1}^{T}\sum_{i=1}^{K}\frac{p_{t}\left(i\right)\bar{\ell}_{t}\left(i\right)^{2}}{\sqrt{\sum_{s=1}^{t}\bar{v}_{s}}}

\displaystyle=\beta\sum_{t=1}^{T}\frac{\bar{v}_{t}}{\sqrt{\sum_{s=1}^{t}\bar{v}_{s}}}

\displaystyle\leq 2\beta\sqrt{\sum_{t=1}^{T}\bar{v}_{t}}\;,

where the last inequality follows from
Orabona 2023
. Likewise,

\mathtt{trunc}_{T}\left(-e_{i^{\star}}\right)\leq 2\beta\sqrt{\sum_{t=1}^{T}v_{t}\left(i^{\star}\right)}\;.

Overall, our regret is bounded by

\displaystyle\sum_{t=1}^{T}\left\langle p_{t}-e_{i^{\star}},\ell_{t}\right\rangle

\displaystyle\quad\leq\left(\sqrt{\alpha T}+5\beta+\frac{4+\log\left(K/\alpha\right)}{\beta}\right)\sqrt{\sum_{t=1}^{T}\bar{v}_{t}}+\left(\sqrt{\alpha T}+\frac{\log\left(K/\alpha\right)}{\beta}+2\beta\right)\sqrt{\sum_{t=1}^{T}v_{t}\left(i^{\star}\right)}\,.\qed

Theorem

B.2
is a more general result and it is indeed stronger than what we originally stated in
Theorem

2.4
.
In particular, we are able to show that the former result implies the latter under
2.1
.
This is illustrated by the following corollary.

Corollary B.3
.

Suppose
2.1
holds.
Then,
Algorithm

1
with
\alpha=\frac{1}{T}
and
\beta=\sqrt{\log(KT)}
guarantees

R_{T}=\mathcal{O}\left(\sqrt{\theta T\log(KT)}\right)\;.

Proof.

Recall that, by
Theorem

B.2
,
Algorithm

1
with
\alpha=\frac{1}{T}
and
\beta=\sqrt{\log(K)}
already guarantees

\sum_{t=1}^{T}\left\langle p_{t}-e_{i^{\star}},\ell_{t}\right\rangle=\mathcal{O}\left(\sqrt{\log(KT)\left(\sum_{t=1}^{T}\bar{v}_{t}+\sum_{t=1}^{T}v_{t}(i^{\star})\right)}\right)

(9)

for any sequence of losses.
Now, focus on the
\bar{v}_{t}
and
v_{t}(i)
terms.
First, for any
i\in[K]
, we can observe that
v_{t}(i)
satisfies

\displaystyle\E_{t}[v_{t}(i)]

\displaystyle=\E_{t}\left[\left(\ell_{t}(i)-\sum_{j=1}^{K}p_{t}(j)\ell_{t}(j)\right)^{\!2}\right]

(definition of
v_{t}(i)
)

\displaystyle=\E_{t}\left[\left(\sum_{j=1}^{K}p_{t}(j)\left(\ell_{t}(i)-\ell_{t}(j)\right)\right)^{2}\right]

\displaystyle\leq\E_{t}\left[\sum_{j=1}^{K}p_{t}(j)(\ell_{t}(i)-\ell_{t}(j))^{2}\right]

(Jensen’s inequality)

\displaystyle\leq 2\E_{t}[\ell_{t}(i)^{2}]+2\E_{t}\left[\sum_{j=1}^{K}p_{t}(j)\ell_{t}(j)^{2}\right]

(using
(a-b)^{2}\leq 2a^{2}+2b^{2}
and
p_{t}\in\mathcal{P}
)

\displaystyle=2\E_{t}[\ell_{t}(i)^{2}]+2\sum_{j=1}^{K}p_{t}(j)\E_{t}\left[\ell_{t}(j)^{2}\right]

\displaystyle\leq 4\theta\;,

where the last inequality follows by
2.1
and the fact that
p_{t}\in\mathcal{P}
.
Then, we can move to
\bar{v}_{t}
and notice that, by its definition, it satisfies

\displaystyle\E_{t}[\bar{v}_{t}]

\displaystyle=\E_{t}\left[\sum_{i=1}^{K}p_{t}(i)v_{t}(i)\right]

\displaystyle=\E_{t}\left[\sum_{i=1}^{K}p_{t}(i)\left(\ell_{t}(i)-\sum_{j=1}^{K}p_{t}(j)\ell_{t}(j)\right)^{\!2}\right]

(definition of
v_{t}(i)
)

\displaystyle\leq\E_{t}\left[\sum_{i=1}^{K}p_{t}(i)\ell_{t}(i)^{2}\right]

(using
\mathbb{V}[X]\leq\E[X^{2}]
)

\displaystyle=\sum_{i=1}^{K}p_{t}(i)\E_{t}\left[\ell_{t}(i)^{2}\right]

\displaystyle\leq\theta\;,

where the last inequality holds by both
2.1
and the fact that
p_{t}\in\mathcal{P}
.

At this point, we can observe that
R_{T}=\max_{i^{\star}\in[K]}\E[\left\langle p_{t}-e_{i^{\star}},\ell_{t}\right\rangle]
, i.e., it corresponds to the expected value of the left-hand side of
Equation

9
.
We can then focus on the expected value of its right-hand side (ignoring constant factors) and, by applying Jensen’s inequality with respect to the square root and the tower rule of expectation, we infer that

\displaystyle\E\left[\sqrt{\log(KT)\sum_{t=1}^{T}\left(\bar{v}_{t}+v_{t}(i^{\star})\right)}\right]

\displaystyle\leq\sqrt{\log(KT)\E\left[\sum_{t=1}^{T}\bigl(\E_{t}\left[\bar{v}_{t}\right]+\E_{t}\left[v_{t}(i^{\star})\right]\bigr)\right]}

\displaystyle\leq\sqrt{5\theta T\log(KT)}\;.

This concludes the proof.
∎

B.2
Self-Bounded Environments

In this section, we provide a regret bound for self-bounded environments defined in
Zimmert and Seldin 2021
.

Theorem B.4
.

Let
2.1
,
2.3
hold, and consider Algorithm
1
run with parameters
\alpha\in\left(0,1\right]
,
\beta>0
. We denote
C_{3}\left(K,T\right)=2\sqrt{\alpha T}+7\beta+\frac{4+2\log\left(K/\alpha\right)}{\beta}
. Then, we have

\mathbb{E}\left[R_{T}\right]\leq\begin{cases}\frac{16\,C_{3}\left(K,T\right)^{2}\theta}{\Delta_{\min}}+\frac{8\,C_{3}\left(K,T\right)\sqrt{\theta C}}{\sqrt{\Delta_{\min}}}&\text{if}\;C\leq\frac{16\,C_{3}\left(K,T\right)^{2}\theta}{\Delta_{\min}}\\
\frac{8\,C_{3}\left(K,T\right)\sqrt{\theta C}}{\sqrt{\Delta_{\min}}}&\text{otherwise}\end{cases}\,.

Furthermore, setting
\alpha=\frac{1}{T}
and
\beta=\sqrt{\log\left(KT\right)}
gives
C_{3}\left(K,T\right)=\mathcal{O}\left(\sqrt{\log\left(KT\right)}\right)
and

\mathbb{E}\left[R_{T}\right]\leq\begin{cases}\frac{3600\log\left(KT\right)\theta}{\Delta_{\min}}+120\sqrt{\frac{\log\left(KT\right)\theta C}{\Delta_{\min}}}&\text{if}\;C\leq\frac{16\,C_{3}\left(K,T\right)^{2}\theta}{\Delta_{\min}}\\
120\sqrt{\frac{\log\left(KT\right)\theta C}{\Delta_{\min}}}&\text{otherwise}\end{cases}\,.

Proof.

From Jensen’s inequality applied to the regret bound proven for adversarial environments in Theorem
B.2
, we have

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[R_{T}\left(e_{i^{\star}}\right)\right]

\displaystyle\leq C_{1}\left(K,T\right)\sqrt{\sum_{t=1}^{T}\sum_{i=1}^{K}\mathbb{E}\left[p_{t}\left(i\right)\left(\ell_{t}\left(i\right)-\sum_{j=1}^{K}p_{t}\left(j\right)\ell_{t}\left(j\right)\right)^{2}\right]}

\displaystyle\quad+C_{2}\left(K,T\right)\sqrt{\sum_{t=1}^{T}\mathbb{E}\left[\left(\ell_{t}\left(i^{\star}\right)-\sum_{j=1}^{K}p_{t}\left(j\right)\ell_{t}\left(j\right)\right)^{2}\right]}\,,

where
C_{1}\left(K,T\right)=\sqrt{\alpha T}+5\beta+\frac{4+\log\left(K/\alpha\right)}{\beta}
, and
C_{2}\left(K,T\right)=\sqrt{\alpha T}+\frac{\log\left(K/\alpha\right)}{\beta}+2\beta
. Observe that for any
t\in\left[T\right]
, the expectation in the first term is a variance that can be bounded by the second moment (
\mathbb{V}\left[X\right]\leq\mathbb{E}\left[X^{2}\right]
for any random variable
X
)

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{t}\left[\sum_{i=1}^{K}p_{t}\left(i\right)\left(\ell_{t}\left(i\right)-\sum_{j=1}^{K}p_{t}\left(j\right)\ell_{t}\left(j\right)\right)^{2}\right]

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}_{t}\left[\sum_{i=1}^{K}p_{t}\left(i\right)\left(\left(\ell_{t}\left(i\right)-\ell_{t}\left(i^{\star}\right)\right)-\sum_{j=1}^{K}p_{t}\left(j\right)\left(\ell_{t}\left(j\right)-\ell_{t}\left(i^{\star}\right)\right)\right)^{2}\right]

\displaystyle\leq\mathbb{E}_{t}\left[\sum_{i\neq i^{\star}}p_{t}\left(i\right)\left(\ell_{t}\left(i\right)-\ell_{t}\left(i^{\star}\right)\right)^{2}\right]\,.

Further, using the inequality
\left(a+b\right)^{2}\leq 2\left(a^{2}+b^{2}\right)
for any
a,b\in\mathbb{R}
,

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{t}\left[\sum_{i=1}^{K}p_{t}\left(i\right)\left(\ell_{t}\left(i\right)-\sum_{j=1}^{K}p_{t}\left(j\right)\ell_{t}\left(j\right)\right)^{2}\right]

\displaystyle\leq 2\mathbb{E}_{t}\left[\sum_{i\neq i^{\star}}p_{t}\left(i\right)\left(\ell_{t}\left(i\right)^{2}+\ell_{t}\left(i^{\star}\right)^{2}\right)\right]

\displaystyle\leq 4\theta\left(1-p_{t}\left(i^{\star}\right)\right)\;,

where the last inequality is by Assumption
2.1
and
\left\langle p_{t},\mathds{1}\right\rangle=1
. Likewise, for the second term we have by Jensen’s inequality

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{t}\left[\left(\ell_{t}\left(i^{\star}\right)-\sum_{j=1}^{K}p_{t}\left(j\right)\ell_{t}\left(j\right)\right)^{2}\right]

\displaystyle\leq\mathbb{E}_{t}\left[\sum_{j\neq i^{\star}}p_{t}\left(j\right)\left(\ell_{t}\left(i^{\star}\right)-\ell_{t}\left(j\right)\right)^{2}\right]

\displaystyle\leq 2\mathbb{E}_{t}\left[\sum_{j\neq i^{\star}}p_{t}\left(j\right)\left(\ell_{t}\left(i^{\star}\right)^{2}+\ell_{t}\left(j\right)^{2}\right)\right]

\displaystyle\leq 4\theta\left(1-p_{t}\left(i^{\star}\right)\right)\;,

where the last inequality is again by Assumption
2.1
and
\left\langle p_{t},\mathds{1}\right\rangle=1
. Denote
\Delta_{\min}=\min_{i\neq i^{\star}}\Delta_{i}
, and
C_{3}\left(K,T\right)=C_{1}\left(K,T\right)+C_{2}\left(K,T\right)
. Combining the above and Assumption
2.3
, we find that for any
\lambda\in\left(0,1\right]
,

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[R_{T}\right]

\displaystyle=\left(1+\lambda\right)\mathbb{E}\left[R_{T}\right]-\lambda\mathbb{E}\left[R_{T}\right]

\displaystyle\leq\left(1+\lambda\right)C_{3}\left(K,T\right)\sqrt{4\theta\,\mathbb{E}\left[\sum_{t=1}^{T}\left(1-p_{t}\left(i^{\star}\right)\right)\right]}-\lambda\Delta_{\min}\mathbb{E}\left[\sum_{t=1}^{T}\left(1-p_{t}\left(i^{\star}\right)\right)\right]+\lambda C

\displaystyle=\sqrt{4\left(1+\lambda\right)^{2}C_{3}\left(K,T\right)^{2}\theta\,\mathbb{E}\left[\sum_{t=1}^{T}\left(1-p_{t}\left(i^{\star}\right)\right)\right]}-\lambda\Delta_{\min}\mathbb{E}\left[\sum_{t=1}^{T}\left(1-p_{t}\left(i^{\star}\right)\right)\right]+\lambda C\,.

Using the inequality of arithmetic and geometric means,
i.e
.

\left|ab\right|=\inf_{\gamma>0}\frac{a^{2}}{2\gamma}+\frac{\gamma b^{2}}{2}
,

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[R_{T}\right]

\displaystyle=\inf_{\gamma>0}\left\{\frac{4\left(1+\lambda\right)^{2}C_{3}\left(K,T\right)^{2}\theta}{\gamma}+\gamma\mathbb{E}\left[\sum_{t=1}^{T}\left(1-p_{t}\left(i^{\star}\right)\right)\right]\right\}

\displaystyle\quad-\lambda\Delta_{\min}\mathbb{E}\left[\sum_{t=1}^{T}\left(1-p_{t}\left(i^{\star}\right)\right)\right]+\lambda C

\displaystyle\leq\frac{4\left(1+\lambda\right)^{2}C_{3}\left(K,T\right)^{2}\theta}{\lambda\Delta_{\min}}+\lambda C

\displaystyle\leq\frac{16\,C_{3}\left(K,T\right)^{2}\theta}{\lambda\Delta_{\min}}+\lambda C\,,

where the first inequality is by taking
\gamma=\lambda\Delta_{\min}
, and the second is by
1+\lambda\leq 2
. In particular, setting
\lambda=\min\left\{1,\frac{4C_{3}\left(K,T\right)\sqrt{\theta}}{\sqrt{\Delta_{\min}C}}\right\}
gives

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[R_{T}\right]

\displaystyle\leq\frac{16\,C_{3}\left(K,T\right)^{2}\theta}{\Delta_{\min}}\max\left\{1,\frac{\sqrt{\Delta_{\min}C}}{4C_{3}\left(K,T\right)\sqrt{\theta}}\right\}+\min\left\{1,\frac{4C_{3}\left(K,T\right)\sqrt{\theta}}{\sqrt{\Delta_{\min}C}}\right\}C

\displaystyle\leq\frac{16\,C_{3}\left(K,T\right)^{2}\theta}{\Delta_{\min}}+\frac{8\,C_{3}\left(K,T\right)\sqrt{\theta C}}{\sqrt{\Delta_{\min}}}\,.

If
C>\frac{16\,C_{3}\left(K,T\right)^{2}\theta}{\Delta_{\min}}
, then
\lambda=\frac{4C_{3}\left(K,T\right)\sqrt{\theta}}{\sqrt{\Delta_{\min}C}}
and the previous inequality can be improved to

\mathbb{E}\left[R_{T}\right]\leq\frac{8\,C_{3}\left(K,T\right)\sqrt{\theta C}}{\sqrt{\Delta_{\min}}}\,.

Recall that
C_{3}\left(K,T\right)=2\sqrt{\alpha T}+7\beta+\frac{4+2\log\left(K/\alpha\right)}{\beta}
. Setting
\alpha=\frac{1}{T}
and
\beta=\sqrt{\log(KT)}
gives
C_{3}\left(K,T\right)\leq 6+9\sqrt{\log\left(KT\right)}\leq 15\sqrt{\log\left(KT\right)}
since
\log\left(KT\right)\geq 1
. Finally, we obtain

\mathbb{E}\left[R_{T}\right]\leq\begin{cases}\frac{3600\log\left(KT\right)\theta}{\Delta_{\min}}+120\sqrt{\frac{\log\left(KT\right)\theta C}{\Delta_{\min}}}&\text{if}\;C\leq\frac{16\,C_{3}\left(K,T\right)^{2}\theta}{\Delta_{\min}}\\
120\sqrt{\frac{\log\left(KT\right)\theta C}{\Delta_{\min}}}&\text{otherwise}\end{cases}\,.

In the special case of a stochastic environment,
C=0
thus we set
\lambda=1
and obtain
\mathbb{E}\left[R_{T}\right]=\mathcal{O}\left(\frac{\theta\log\left(KT\right)}{\Delta_{\min}}\right)
.
∎

Algorithm 2

LoOT-Free FTRL

In
Algorithm

2
we only update the prediction
p_{t}
if
\sum_{s\leq t}v_{s}>0
. The only case where this is not true is if in all rounds up to and including round
t
,
r_{t}(i)=0
for all
i\in[K]
, meaning that the cumulative regret up to round
t
is null. In this case, we can simply ignore these rounds in the regret analysis, which is what we do: throughout this section we assume that
v_{1}>0
without loss of generality.

Theorem C.1
.

Consider
Algorithm

2
with parameter
\beta>0
, providing predictions
p_{1},\dots,p_{T}\in\mathcal{P}
over a sequence of losses
\ell_{1},\dots,\ell_{T}
.
Then, with probability one, for any
i^{\star}\in[K]
we have

\displaystyle\sum_{t=1}^{T}\left\langle p_{t}-e_{i^{\star}},\ell_{t}\right\rangle

\displaystyle\quad\leq\left(\frac{\log(K)}{\beta}+2\beta\right)\sqrt{\sum_{t=1}^{T}v_{t}(i^{\star})}+\left(\frac{5+\log(K)}{\beta}+5\beta\right)\sqrt{\sum_{t=1}^{T}\bar{v}_{t}}+\frac{1}{\beta}\sum_{i=1}^{K}\frac{1}{K}\sqrt{\sum_{t=1}^{T}v_{t}(i)}\;.

Furthermore, setting
\beta=\sqrt{\log(K)}
gives

\sum_{t=1}^{T}\left\langle p_{t}-e_{i^{\star}},\ell_{t}\right\rangle=\mathcal{O}\left(\sqrt{\log(K)\left(\sum_{t=1}^{T}\bar{v}_{t}+\sum_{t=1}^{T}v_{t}(i^{\star})\right)}+\frac{1}{K}\sum_{i=1}^{K}\sqrt{\sum_{t=1}^{T}v_{t}(i)}\right)\;.

Proof.

Let
i^{\star}\in[K]
be our point-mass comparator and let
\bar{\ell}_{t}=-r_{t}
for any
t\in[T]
.
First, observe that the regret can be equivalently rewritten as

\sum_{t=1}^{T}\left\langle p_{t}-e_{i^{\star}},\ell_{t}\right\rangle=\sum_{t=1}^{T}\left\langle p_{t}-e_{i^{\star}},\bar{\ell}_{t}\right\rangle\;;

this follows from the fact that replacing
\ell_{t}
with
\bar{\ell}_{t}
only leads to a difference
\left\langle p_{t}-e_{i^{\star}},\ell_{t}-\bar{\ell}_{t}\right\rangle=\left\langle p_{t}-e_{i^{\star}},c\cdot\mathds{1}\right\rangle=\left\langle p_{t},c\cdot\mathds{1}\right\rangle-\left\langle e_{i^{\star}},c\cdot\mathds{1}\right\rangle=c-c=0
for the constant
c=\left\langle p_{t},\ell_{t}\right\rangle
, by definition of
\bar{\ell}_{t}
.
As in the proof of
Theorem

B.2
, we take the same definitions of
\mathtt{trunc}_{T}
and
\widetilde{R}_{T}
, and consider the following regret decomposition:

\sum_{t=1}^{T}\bigl\langle p_{t}-e_{i^{\star}},\ell_{t}\bigr\rangle=\underbrace{\sum_{t=1}^{T}\left\langle p_{t}-e_{i^{\star}},\bar{\ell}_{t}-\widetilde{\ell}_{t}\right\rangle}_{=\mathtt{trunc}_{T}(p_{1:T})+\mathtt{trunc}_{T}(-e_{i^{\star}})}+\underbrace{\sum_{t=1}^{T}\left\langle p_{t}-e_{i^{\star}},\widetilde{\ell}_{t}\right\rangle}_{=\widetilde{R}_{T}(e_{i^{\star}})}\;,

where the first term is the cost of truncating the losses, and the second is the regret of FTRL on the truncated losses
\widetilde{\ell}_{1},\dots,\widetilde{\ell}_{T}
.
Let us first focus on the former term.

Before we proceed, one final remark is in order.
Recall that
Algorithm

2
performs predictions defined such that

p_{t}=\argmin_{p\in\mathcal{P}}\sum_{s=1}^{t-1}\left\langle p,\widetilde{\ell}_{s}\right\rangle+D_{t-1}(p\|p_{0})

for any
t>1
, where
p_{0}=\frac{1}{K}\mathds{1}\in\mathcal{P}
is the uniform distribution, while
p_{1}=p_{0}
.
Observe that we can define
\eta_{0,i}=\beta/\sqrt{\bar{v}_{1}}
(never used nor set by
Algorithm

2
) for any
i\in[K]
and, thus, we can equivalently denote
p_{1}
as

p_{1}\in\argmin_{p\in\mathcal{P}}D_{0}(p\|p_{0})

because
D_{0}(p\|p_{0})=\frac{\sqrt{\bar{v}_{1}}}{\beta}\sum_{i=1}^{K}\bigl(p(i)\log(p(i)/p_{0}(i))-p(i)+p_{0}(i)\bigr)
is minimized at
p_{0}
. We remark that, while this step appears to require knowledge of
\bar{v}_{1}
before computing the prediction
p_{1}
, it is only part of the analysis and not algorithmically performed.

Step 1: control the cost of truncation.

We can begin by focusing on
\mathtt{trunc}_{T}(p_{1:T})
. Observe that

\displaystyle\mathtt{trunc}_{T}(p_{1:T})

\displaystyle=\sum_{t=1}^{T}\left\langle p_{t},\bar{\ell}_{t}-\widetilde{\ell}_{t}\right\rangle

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{t=1}^{T}\sum_{i=1}^{K}p_{t}(i)\bigl|\bar{\ell}_{t}(i)-\widetilde{\ell}_{t}(i)\bigr|

\displaystyle=\sum_{t=1}^{T}\sum_{i=1}^{K}p_{t}(i)\biggl(\left|\bar{\ell}_{t}(i)\right|\Bigl(1-\frac{b_{t-1}(i)}{b_{t}(i)}\Bigr)\mathds{1}\Bigl(\left|\bar{\ell}_{t}(i)\right|\leq\frac{1}{\eta_{t,i}}\Bigr)+\left|\bar{\ell}_{t}(i)\right|\mathds{1}\Bigl(\left|\bar{\ell}_{t}(i)\right|>\frac{1}{\eta_{t,i}}\Bigr)\biggr)\,,

where the last equality follows by definition of
\widetilde{\ell}_{t}(i)
, after observing that
b_{t-1}(i)\leq b_{t}(i)
.
Now, by using the definitions of
b_{t}(i)
and
\eta_{t,i}
, observe that

\displaystyle\sum_{i=1}^{K}p_{t}(i)\left|\bar{\ell}_{t}(i)\right|\Bigl(1-\frac{b_{t-1}(i)}{b_{t}(i)}\Bigr)\mathds{1}\Bigl(\left|\bar{\ell}_{t}(i)\right|\leq\frac{1}{\eta_{t,i}}\Bigr)

\displaystyle\quad=\sum_{i=1}^{K}p_{t}(i)\frac{\left|\bar{\ell}_{t}(i)\right|}{b_{t}(i)}\bigl(b_{t}(i)-b_{t-1}(i)\bigr)\mathds{1}\Bigl(\left|\bar{\ell}_{t}(i)\right|\leq\frac{1}{\eta_{t,i}}\Bigr)

\displaystyle\quad=\sum_{i=1}^{K}p_{t}(i)\eta_{t,i}\left|\bar{\ell}_{t}(i)\right|\biggl(\frac{1}{\eta_{t,i}}-\frac{1}{\eta_{t-1,i}}\biggr)\mathds{1}\Bigl(\left|\bar{\ell}_{t}(i)\right|\leq\frac{1}{\eta_{t,i}}\Bigr)

\displaystyle\quad\leq\sum_{i=1}^{K}p_{t}(i)\biggl(\frac{1}{\eta_{t,i}}-\frac{1}{\eta_{t-1,i}}\biggr)

\displaystyle\quad\leq\frac{2\bar{v}_{t}}{\beta\sqrt{\sum_{s\leq t}\bar{v}_{s}}}\;,

for
t>1
, where the last inequality holds by
Equation

8
given that the learning rates
\eta_{t,i}
have the same definition; the same bound holds similarly for
t=1
by observing that
b_{0}(i)=0
and, hence, we have

\displaystyle\sum_{i=1}^{K}p_{1}(i)|\bar{\ell}_{1}(i)|\mathds{1}\Bigl(|\bar{\ell}_{1}(i)|\leq\frac{1}{\eta_{1,i}}\Bigr)

\displaystyle\leq\frac{1}{\beta}\sum_{i=1}^{K}p_{1}(i)\sqrt{\max\{\bar{v}_{1},v_{1}(i)\}}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{1}{\beta}\sqrt{\sum_{i=1}^{K}p_{1}(i)\max\{\bar{v}_{1},v_{1}(i)\}}\leq\frac{\sqrt{2\bar{v}_{1}}}{\beta}\leq\frac{2\bar{v}_{1}}{\beta\sqrt{\bar{v}_{1}}}\;,

where the second step follows by Jensen’s inequality.
At the same time, we have that

\displaystyle\sum_{i=1}^{K}p_{t}(i)\left|\bar{\ell}_{t}(i)\right|\mathds{1}\Bigl(\left|\bar{\ell}_{t}(i)\right|>\frac{1}{\eta_{t,i}}\Bigr)

\displaystyle=\sum_{i=1}^{K}p_{t}(i)\frac{\bar{\ell}_{t}(i)^{2}}{\left|\bar{\ell}_{t}(i)\right|}\mathds{1}\Bigl(\left|\bar{\ell}_{t}(i)\right|>\frac{1}{\eta_{t,i}}\Bigr)

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{i=1}^{K}\eta_{t,i}p_{t}(i)\bar{\ell}_{t}(i)^{2}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{\beta\bar{v}_{t}}{\sqrt{\sum_{s\leq t}\bar{v}_{s}}}\;.

We can therefore combine the above inequalities and, together with
Orabona 2023
, obtain that

\mathtt{trunc}_{T}(p_{1:T})\leq\biggl(\beta+\frac{2}{\beta}\biggr)\sum_{t=1}^{T}\frac{\bar{v}_{t}}{\sqrt{\sum_{s\leq t}\bar{v}_{s}}}\leq 2\biggl(\beta+\frac{2}{\beta}\biggr)\sqrt{\sum_{t=1}^{T}\bar{v}_{t}}\;.

Similarly, we can see that
\mathtt{trunc}_{T}(-e_{i^{\star}})
similarly satisfies

\displaystyle\mathtt{trunc}_{T}(-e_{i^{\star}})

\displaystyle=\sum_{t=1}^{T}\left\langle-e_{i^{\star}},\bar{\ell}_{t}-\widetilde{\ell}_{t}\right\rangle

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{t=1}^{T}\bigl|\bar{\ell}_{t}(i^{\star})-\widetilde{\ell}_{t}(i^{\star})\bigr|

\displaystyle=\sum_{t=1}^{T}\biggl(\left|\bar{\ell}_{t}(i^{\star})\right|\Bigl(1-\frac{b_{t-1}(i^{\star})}{b_{t}(i^{\star})}\Bigr)\mathds{1}\Bigl(\left|\bar{\ell}_{t}(i^{\star})\right|\leq\frac{1}{\eta_{t,i^{\star}}}\Bigr)+\left|\bar{\ell}_{t}(i^{\star})\right|\mathds{1}\Bigl(\left|\bar{\ell}_{t}(i^{\star})\right|>\frac{1}{\eta_{t,i^{\star}}}\Bigr)\biggr)\,.

Using similar calculations as before, it follows that

\displaystyle\sum_{t=1}^{T}\left|\bar{\ell}_{t}(i^{\star})\right|\Bigl(1-\frac{b_{t-1}(i^{\star})}{b_{t}(i^{\star})}\Bigr)\mathds{1}\Bigl(\left|\bar{\ell}_{t}(i^{\star})\right|\leq\frac{1}{\eta_{t,i^{\star}}}\Bigr)

\displaystyle\leq\frac{1}{\beta}\sum_{t=1}^{T}\bigl(b_{t}(i^{\star})-b_{t-1}(i^{\star})\bigr)=\frac{1}{\eta_{T,i^{\star}}}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{1}{\beta}\sqrt{\sum_{t=1}^{T}\bar{v}_{t}}+\frac{1}{\beta}\sqrt{\sum_{t=1}^{T}v_{t}(i^{\star})}\;,

where we used the subadditivity of the square root in the last inequality, and also that

\sum_{t=1}^{T}\left|\bar{\ell}_{t}(i^{\star})\right|\mathds{1}\Bigl(\left|\bar{\ell}_{t}(i^{\star})\right|>\frac{1}{\eta_{t,i^{\star}}}\Bigr)\leq\sum_{t=1}^{T}\eta_{t,i^{\star}}\bar{\ell}_{t}(i^{\star})^{2}\leq\beta\sum_{t=1}^{T}\frac{v_{t}(i^{\star})}{\sqrt{\sum_{s\leq t}v_{s}(i^{\star})}}\leq 2\beta\sqrt{\sum_{t=1}^{T}v_{t}(i^{\star})}\;,

where the last inequality follows again by
Orabona 2023
.
Hence, we similarly conclude that

\mathtt{trunc}_{T}(-e_{i^{\star}})\leq\frac{1}{\beta}\sqrt{\sum_{t=1}^{T}v_{t}}+\biggl(2\beta+\frac{1}{\beta}\biggr)\sqrt{\sum_{t=1}^{T}v_{t}(i^{\star})}\;,

which implies that the total cost for truncating the losses is bounded from above as

\mathtt{trunc}_{T}(p_{1:T})+\mathtt{trunc}_{T}(-e_{i^{\star}})\leq\biggl(2\beta+\frac{5}{\beta}\biggr)\sqrt{\sum_{t=1}^{T}\bar{v}_{t}}+\biggl(2\beta+\frac{1}{\beta}\biggr)\sqrt{\sum_{t=1}^{T}v_{t}(i^{\star})}\;.

Step 2: control the regret of FTRL.

Let us now focus on the latter term in the regret decomposition, that is, the regret of FTRL on the losses
\widetilde{\ell}_{1},\dots,\widetilde{\ell}_{T}
.
Consider any
t\in\left[T\right]
and define

\widetilde{p}_{t+1}=\argmin_{p\in\mathbb{R}^{K}}\left\{\left\langle p,\widetilde{\ell}_{t}\right\rangle+D_{t-1}\left(p\|p_{t}\right)\right\}\;.

Note that for any
i\in\left[K\right]
,
\widetilde{p}_{t+1}(i)=p_{t}(i)\exp\bigl(-\eta_{t-1,i}\widetilde{\ell}_{t}(i)\bigr)\leq 3p_{t}(i)
by construction of
\widetilde{\ell}_{t}
, which is such that

\left|\eta_{t-1,i}\widetilde{\ell}_{t}\left(i\right)\right|=\eta_{t-1,i}\left|\bar{\ell}_{t}(i)\right|\frac{b_{t-1}(i)}{b_{t}(i)}\mathds{1}\Bigl(\left|\bar{\ell}_{t}(i)\right|\leq\frac{1}{\eta_{t,i}}\Bigr)=\eta_{t,i}\left|\bar{\ell}_{t}(i)\right|\mathds{1}\Bigl(\left|\bar{\ell}_{t}(i)\right|\leq\frac{1}{\eta_{t,i}}\Bigr)\leq 1

for any
i\in[K]
and
t>1
, while it immediately holds for
t=1
since
b_{0}(i)=0
for any
i
.

Let
\varphi_{t}=D_{t-1}(\cdot\|p_{0})
be the regularizer used in the FTRL update.
Observe that
\varphi_{t}
is twice-differentiable with Hessian having inverse
\left(\nabla^{2}\varphi_{t}(x)\right)^{-1}=\Diag(\eta_{t-1}\odot x)
, and that
\varphi_{t+1}(x)\geq\varphi_{t}(x)
for all
x\in\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}^{K}
.
Then, by standard results on FTRL with time-varying regularizers
\varphi_{1},\dots,\varphi_{T+1}
(e.g., see
Orabona 2023
), we obtain

\widetilde{R}_{T}(e_{i^{\star}})\leq\varphi_{T+1}(e_{i^{\star}})+\frac{1}{2}\sum_{t=1}^{T}\left\|\widetilde{\ell}_{t}\right\|^{2}_{\left(\nabla^{2}\varphi_{t}(z_{t})\right)^{-1}}

(10)

for some point
z_{t}
on the line segment between
p_{t}
and
\widetilde{p}_{t+1}
.
The point
z_{t}
is such that
z_{t}(i)\leq\max\{p_{t}(i),\widetilde{p}_{t+1}(i)\}\leq 3p_{t}(i)
for any
i\in[K]
.
Therefore, given the definition of the local norm, the second term of
Equation

10
satisfies

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}\sum_{t=1}^{T}\left\|\widetilde{\ell}_{t}\right\|^{2}_{\left(\nabla^{2}\varphi_{t}(z_{t})\right)^{-1}}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\sum_{t=1}^{T}\sum_{i=1}^{K}\eta_{t-1,i}z_{t}(i)\widetilde{\ell}_{t}(i)^{2}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{3}{2}\sum_{t=1}^{T}\sum_{i=1}^{K}\eta_{t-1,i}p_{t}(i)\widetilde{\ell}_{t}(i)^{2}

(using
z_{t}(i)\leq 3p_{t}(i)
)

\displaystyle\leq\frac{3}{2}\sum_{t=1}^{T}\sum_{i=1}^{K}\eta_{t-1,i}p_{t}(i)\bar{\ell}_{t}(i)^{2}\cdot\frac{b_{t-1}(i)^{2}}{b_{t}(i)^{2}}

(using
|\widetilde{\ell}_{t}(i)|\leq\tfrac{b_{t-1}(i)}{b_{t}(i)}|\bar{\ell}_{t}(i)|
)

\displaystyle=\frac{3\beta}{2}\sum_{t=1}^{T}\sum_{i=1}^{K}p_{t}(i)\bar{\ell}_{t}(i)^{2}\cdot\frac{b_{t-1}(i)}{b_{t}(i)^{2}}

(definition of
\eta_{t-1,i}
)

\displaystyle\leq\frac{3\beta}{2}\sum_{t=1}^{T}\sum_{i=1}^{K}\frac{p_{t}(i)\bar{\ell}_{t}(i)^{2}}{b_{t}(i)}

(using
b_{t-1}(i)\leq b_{t}(i)
)

\displaystyle\leq\frac{3\beta}{2}\sum_{t=1}^{T}\frac{\bar{v}_{t}}{\sqrt{\sum_{s\leq t}\bar{v}_{s}}}

(definition of
b_{t}(i)
and
\bar{v}_{t}
)

\displaystyle\leq 3\beta\sqrt{\sum_{t=1}^{T}\bar{v}_{t}}\;,

where the last inequality follows again by
Orabona 2023
.
On the other hand, the first term of
Equation

10
is such that

\displaystyle\varphi_{T+1}(e_{i^{\star}})

\displaystyle=\frac{\log(K)-1}{\eta_{T,i^{\star}}}+\frac{1}{K}\sum_{i=1}^{K}\frac{1}{\eta_{T,i}}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{\log(K)-1}{\beta}\left(\sqrt{\sum_{t=1}^{T}v_{t}(i^{\star})}+\sqrt{\sum_{t=1}^{T}\bar{v}_{t}}\right)+\frac{1}{\beta}\sqrt{\sum_{t=1}^{T}\bar{v}_{t}}+\frac{1}{\beta}\sum_{i=1}^{K}\frac{1}{K}\sqrt{\sum_{t=1}^{T}v_{t}(i)}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{\log(K)-1}{\beta}\sqrt{\sum_{t=1}^{T}v_{t}(i^{\star})}+\frac{\log(K)}{\beta}\sqrt{\sum_{t=1}^{T}\bar{v}_{t}}+\frac{1}{\beta}\sum_{i=1}^{K}\frac{1}{K}\sqrt{\sum_{t=1}^{T}v_{t}(i)}\;.

Combining all the above results together leads to

\displaystyle\sum_{t=1}^{T}\left\langle p_{t}-e_{i^{\star}},\ell_{t}\right\rangle

\displaystyle\quad\leq\left(\frac{\log(K)}{\beta}+2\beta\right)\sqrt{\sum_{t=1}^{T}v_{t}(i^{\star})}+\left(\frac{5+\log(K)}{\beta}+5\beta\right)\sqrt{\sum_{t=1}^{T}\bar{v}_{t}}+\frac{1}{\beta}\sum_{i=1}^{K}\frac{1}{K}\sqrt{\sum_{t=1}^{T}v_{t}(i)}\;.\qed

C.1

.

.

Suppose
2.1
holds.
Then,
Algorithm

with
\beta=\sqrt{\log(K)}
guarantees

Proof.

Recall that, by

,

guarantees

\sum_{t=1}^{T}\left\langle p_{t}-e_{i^{\star}},\ell_{t}\right\rangle=\mathcal{O}\left(\sqrt{\log(K)\left(\sum_{t=1}^{T}\bar{v}_{t}+\sum_{t=1}^{T}v_{t}(i^{\star})\right)}+\frac{1}{K}\sum_{i=1}^{K}\sqrt{\sum_{t=1}^{T}v_{t}(i)}\right)

for any sequence of losses.
Additionally, in a similar way as in the proof of

C.1
Computing the Update in Algorithm
2

We briefly discuss the update defining
p_{t+1}
in
Algorithm

2
. For any
\eta\in\mathbb{R}_{>0}^{K}
, consider an optimization problem of the form

\inf_{p\in\mathcal{P}}\left\langle p,L\right\rangle+D\left(p\|q\right)
, where
L\in\mathbb{R}^{K}
,
q\in\mathcal{P}
, and
D\left(p\|q\right)=\sum_{i=1}^{K}\frac{1}{\eta_{i}}\left[p(i)\log\left(\frac{p\left(i\right)}{q\left(i\right)}\right)+p\left(i\right)-q\left(i\right)\right]
. As the probability simplex is compact and the mapping
p\mapsto\left\langle p,L\right\rangle+D\left(p\|q\right)
is continuous, the infimum is attained at some
p^{\star}\in\mathcal{P}
. The Lagrangian
\mathcal{L}\left(p,\lambda\right)
of the optimization problem is defined for
p\in\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}^{K}
and
\lambda\in\mathbb{R}
as

\mathcal{L}\left(p,\lambda\right)=\left\langle p,L\right\rangle+\sum_{i=1}^{K}\frac{1}{\eta_{i}}\left[p\left(i\right)\log\left(\frac{p\left(i\right)}{q\left(i\right)}\right)-p\left(i\right)+q\left(i\right)\right]+\lambda\left(\left\langle p,\mathds{1}\right\rangle-1\right)\,.

For any
j\in\left[K\right]
, differentiate with respect to
p\left(j\right)
to get

\partial_{p\left(j\right)}\mathcal{L}\left(p,\lambda\right)=L\left(j\right)+\frac{1}{\eta_{j}}\left[\log\left(\frac{p\left(j\right)}{q\left(j\right)}\right)+1-1\right]+\lambda\,.

Setting it to zero, we get

p^{\star}\left(j\right)=q\left(j\right)\exp\left(-\eta_{j}\left[L\left(j\right)+\lambda\right]\right)\,.

One then wants to find the value of
\lambda
by enforcing the constraint on
p^{\star}
, namely
\left\langle p^{\star},\mathds{1}\right\rangle=1
, which gives

\sum_{i=1}^{K}q\left(i\right)\exp\left(-\eta_{i}\left[L\left(i\right)+\lambda\right]\right)=1\,.

If the learning rate did not depend on the coordinate, we could take the term that depends on
\lambda
out of the sum to get a closed-form solution. Plugging it back into
p^{\star}
would give a softmax distribution, but this is not possible here. Instead, one can efficiently compute the normalization constant
\lambda
with a line-search.

Appendix E
Comparison with
Gökcesu and Kozat 2022

In this section, we provide further details about the comparison with
Gökcesu and Kozat 2022
.
The first regret guarantees we compare against are those provided by their Theorem IV.7 and Theorem V.2.
One may immediately observe that those regret bounds present an additive term
E_{T}
(multiplied by a logarithmic factor) equivalent to our
M_{T}=\max_{t,i}|r_{t}(i)|
, for which we already prove that the
\sqrt{KT}
lower bound holds even with i.i.d. losses.

Hence, the main comparison is mainly with respect to Corollary IV.8 and Corollary V.3 in
Gökcesu and Kozat 2022
.
The proof of Corollary IV.8 and Corollary V.3 rely on their Lemma IV.1, which requires that, in the notation of
Gökcesu and Kozat 2022
,
\eta_{t}|l_{t,m}-\mu_{t}|\leq 1
for all
t
and all
m
for Equation (b) in the proof of Lemma IV.1 to be true.
However, with the learning rates given in Corollary IV.8 and Corollary V.3,
\eta_{t}|l_{t,m}-\mu_{t}|\leq 1
does not hold. In Corollary IV.8 they choose
\eta_{t}\geq\sqrt{\frac{1}{\sum_{s=1}^{t}\E_{p_{s}}[(l_{s,m}-\mu_{s})^{2}]}}
, which with
\mu_{s}=\min_{m}l_{s,m}
for
t=1
with
l_{1,m}=0
if
m\neq K
and
l_{1,K}=1
, can be seen to lead to
\eta_{t}|l_{t,m}-\mu_{t}|\geq\sqrt{K}>1
. With Corollary V.3, we run into a similar issue. We do not see a way to fix these issues.

We ran the experiments for

We ran two sets of experiments.
In the first set of experiments the losses mimic the construction we used in

The expert losses were equal to

\displaystyle X_{t,i}=\begin{cases}0&\text{w.p. }1-1/T\\
\sqrt{KT}&\text{w.p. }1/T\end{cases}\,.

In the second set of experiments we use a similar construction, where the losses were equal to
\ell_{t}(i)=\mathds{1}[i\neq 1]+\varepsilon_{t,i}

\varepsilon_{t,i}=\zeta_{t,i}\widetilde{X}_{t,i}

\zeta_{t,i}

\displaystyle\widetilde{X}_{t,i}=\begin{cases}0&\text{w.p. }1-1/T\\

The algorithms we implemented were Squint with the improper prior
[
Koolen and van Erven 2015
]
, AdaHedge
[
de Rooij et al. 2014
]
, and an instance of Exponential Weights (EW) algorithm akin to the algorithm of
[
Cesa-Bianchi et al. 2007
]

Specifically, we ran the FTRL version of exponential weights on the instantaneous regrets with learning rate
\eta_{t}=\min\Bigl\{\frac{1}{\max_{t,i}|\ell_{t}(i)|},\sqrt{\frac{\log(K)}{\sum_{s\leq t}\bar{v}_{t}}}\Bigr\}
.
We gave this instance of EW the maximum loss, otherwise it would not provide any guarantees.
We could have also opted for a doubling trick, but this is known to deteriorate performance.
Likewise, we gave Squint the value of
\max_{t,i}|\ell_{t}(i)|
, as this is a required parameter for Squint.
The algorithm of
[
Mhammedi et al. 2019
]
could have instead been used to learn
\max_{t,i}|\ell_{t}(i)|
online, but seeing that a similar idea as the doubling trick is part of their algorithm, we suspect this would only deteriorate performance.

In the first set of experiments we expect Squint, AdaHedge, and EW to perform poorly due to the issues described in
Section

4
.
In the second set of experiments we expect similar behaviour from all algorithms.

As can be seen from the results in

1
, algorithms not tailored to adapt to
\theta
fare considerably worse in the heavy-tailed loss setting we consider.
We therefore conclude that the lower-order terms in the regret bounds of these algorithms are not an artefact of the analysis, but rather represent the problematic behaviour of these algorithms in the face of heavy-tailed losses.

The results for the second set of experiments is similarly as expected with one exception: the performance of Squint.
Squint fares considerably worse than the other algorithms.
However, upon inspection, it seems that the performance of Squint is still below what is predicted by theory.
The regret bound of Squint contains a
15\log(1+K(2+\log(T+1)))
term, which for the smallest values of

is slightly larger than
71

All experiments were run on a Macbook Air with 8GB of RAM and an Apple M2 processor.
