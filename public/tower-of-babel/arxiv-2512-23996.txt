State Space Estimation for DPOR-based Model Checkers
A. R. Balasubramanian, Mohammad Hossein Khoshechin Jorshari, Rupak Majumdar, Umang Mathur, Minjian Zhang

Abstract
. We study the estimation problem for concurrent programs: given a bounded program P P , estimate the number of Mazurkiewicz trace–equivalence classes induced by its interleavings. This quantity informs two practical questions for enumeration-based model checking: how long a model checking run is likely to take, and what fraction of the search space has been covered so far. We first show the counting problem is # ​ P \#\textsf{P} -hard even for restricted programs and, unless P = NP , inapproximable within any subexponential factor, ruling out efficient exact or randomized approximation algorithms. We give a Monte Carlo approach to find a poly-time unbiased estimator: we convert a stateless optimal DPOR algorithm into an unbiased estimator by viewing its exploration as a bounded-depth, bounded-width tree whose leaves are the maximal Mazurkiewicz traces. A classical estimator by Knuth, when run on this tree, gives an unbiased estimation. In order to control the variance of the estimation, we apply stochastic enumeration by maintaining a small population of partial paths per depth whose evolution is coupled. We have implemented our estimator in the JMC model checker and evaluated it on shared-memory benchmarks. We find that with modest budgets, our estimator yields stable estimates—typically within a 20% band— within a few hundred trials, even when the state space has 10 5 10^{5} – 10 6 10^{6} classes. We also show how the same machinery estimates model-checking cost by weighting all explored graphs, not only complete traces. Our algorithms provide the first provable poly-time unbiased estimators for counting traces—a problem of considerable importance when allocating model checking resources.



1.
Introduction

Given a concurrent program, the
estimation problem
asks, how many different behaviors (up to a certain size) does it have?
It is a natural, counting analogue of decision questions such as reachability.
It is also a practical one:
the estimation problem can help answering two
high-level quantitative questions that arise when using an enumeration-based bounded model checker:

“How long should we expect the model checker to run?”
and

“What fraction of the state space has the model checker covered so far?”

Reliable answers to these questions have the potential to significantly enhance
the usability and adoption of model checkers
(
33
;
26
)
, by enabling software teams
to allocate computational budgets more effectively, predict the remaining
verification effort, and meaningfully compare competing verification tools.
As model checkers become an integral part of the modern software development toolkit

(
29
;
36
;
17
;
18
;
34
;
2
;
1
;
31
;
52
;
21
;
26
;
8
;
42
;
16
;
54
)
,
these resourcing questions become increasingly important.

In this work, we develop systematic solutions to the estimation problem.
As a first step, we must answer what we mean by a behavior, since
different model checkers induce different notions of behaviors.
A naive interleaving-based model checker treats every thread schedule as a distinct execution.
In contrast, a checker based on partial-order reduction
(
24
;
29
;
22
;
25
;
3
;
2
;
1
;
34
)

collapses all schedules within the same equivalence class,
thereby exploring a significantly smaller space.
Additional heuristics may reduce the space further, e.g., by defining coarser equivalences based on symmetry.

We focus on behaviors induced by Mazurkiewicz’s trace equivalence, where two executions are considered equivalent
if one can be obtained from the other by repeated swapping of adjacent commutative operations.
Our choice is guided, on the one hand, by the huge success of this notion in model checking

(
29
;
25
;
22
;
2
;
34
)

and, on the other, by the recent emergence of many
optimal
exploration algorithms that guarantee that each equivalence class is visited exactly once
(
2
;
34
)
.
In this setting, the estimation problem asks:

Estimation problem:
Given a bounded program P, how many Mazurkiewicz equivalence classes do its interleavings induce?

In the presence of optimality, the estimation problem is directly related (but not identical) to the cost of model checking.

We first ask whether the above estimation problem can be solved
tractably (i.e., in polynomial time in the size of the program).
The situation is decidedly bleak:
we show that the problem is #P-hard even for highly restricted concurrent programs.
Informally, this #P-hardness results implies that the task is at least as hard as
counting the satisfying assignments of a Boolean formula (#SAT), a canonical
counting problem for which no efficient algorithm is believed to exist.
In fact, we show that unless
P
=
NP
, we cannot even
approximate
the number of equivalence classes
up to a subexponential factor!
In summary, neither exact nor even moderately precise approximate
counting is feasible in general.

Although these hardness results rule out efficient exact or approximate
counting, they do not prevent us from obtaining statistical or
Monte Carlo

estimates of the behavioral-space size.
The statistical formulation of our problem asks for an
unbiased estimator
:

Unbiased estimation problem:
Given a bounded program P, give a polynomial-time randomized procedure whose expected value is the number of equivalence classes.

A natural starting point for deriving such an estimator is Knuth’s classical
backtracking estimator
(
32
)
, which approximates the number of leaves in a
combinatorial search tree by performing a randomized exploratory
walk, recording the branching factors encountered along each walk, and
computing a weighted product that yields an unbiased estimator of the total
number of leaves.

However, if one attempts to apply this Knuth-style strategy directly to the
program’s interleaving space, the
underlying exploration structure is no longer a tree: different equivalence classes
overlap and merge extensively, forming a directed acyclic graph (DAG).
On a DAG, Knuth’s procedure is no longer an unbiased estimator.

Our main insight is that a stateless optimal
DPOR
algorithm can be converted to an unbiased estimator!
This is because such algorithms have several crucial properties.
First, their optimality guarantee implies that the underlying state space explored by the algorithm defines a tree structure.
Second, since they are stateless, the next decisions depend solely on the current state and not on the history of the search.
Third, any one exploration of the algorithm is bounded polynomially in the size of the program.

We make this intuition formal in the context of the
Trust
algorithm
(
34
)
for stateless optimal
DPOR
.
We show that the underlying state space traversed by
Trust
is a tree, whose depth and width are bounded by a quadratic function of the program size,
and whose leaves are exactly the maximal Mazurkiewicz traces.
Thus, estimating the leaves of this tree solves the estimation problem.
Thus, we can apply the Knuth estimator to this (implicitly constructed) tree and get an unbiased estimator.
The algorithm is surprisingly simple: we run
Trust
, but instead of systematically exploring all successors, we randomly pick one.
At the end, we output the product of the number of choices we had along the path.
(Of course, the number of leaves can be exponential in the program size.)

While the estimator is unbiased, its variance can still be exponentially large,
reflecting the inherent hardness of approximation.
However, in many practical situations, a large variance often occurs not because of inherent computational complexity but because the underlying tree is “skewed”.
In the
Trust
algorithm, some explored paths are linear in the size of the program while others can
be quadratic—a random walk procedure is much more likely to visit the “shallow” leaves than the “deep” ones.

In order to reduce variance, we use a trick from Monte Carlo estimation called
stochastic enumeration

(
45
;
51
)
or weighted ensemble
(
30
)
.
Instead of one random walk, as Knuth’s estimator does, we maintain a small population of partial paths at each depth of the
tree.
Whenever a path ends, the population is redistributed to reflect the structure.
The overall procedure carefully controls how branches
are selected so that no single highly unbalanced subtree dominates the
estimate.
This preserves unbiasedness of estimation while substantially smoothing out the contribution
of deep but large subtrees.
As our evaluation shows, on a broad range of bounded concurrent programs the
resulting estimator attains stable estimates with a modest number of samples,
suggesting that this variance-reduction strategy is well suited to realistic
model-checking workloads.

We have implemented our estimator in the
DPOR
-based stateless Java model checker JMC and evaluated them on a suite of bounded concurrent programs, including concurrent data structures
and synthetic benchmarks designed to exercise different concurrency patterns.
We show that the estimator based on
Trust
, combined with stochastic enumeration, consistently achieves stable estimates within a small number of trials—typically a few hundred—even
for behavior spaces ranging from about
10^{5}
to
10^{6}

executions, with relative error usually within a 20% band.
On the other hand, each aspect is crucial: on these benchmarks, a DAG-based estimator or an estimator that does not perform stochastic enumeration, do not converge.

We note that the combination of
Trust
, tree-estimation, and stochastic enumeration exploits a number of subtle but important properties of the
Trust
algorithm.
An alternative approach would be to apply Pitt’s procedure
(
44
)
, a generalization of Knuth’s procedure that corrects for DAGs.
Indeed, we show that Pitt’s procedure can be implemented in a
DPOR
algorithm.
However, while Pitt’s estimator is unbiased, our experience is that its variance is too high to be of practical use: even when a program has exactly one maximal trace,
the variance can be exponential.
Second, it is crucial that the underlying optimal
DPOR
algorithm is history independent.
In an optimal model checker like Nidhugg
(
1
)
, every execution graph is visited through a unique path, implicitly inducing a tree.
However, the task of sampling paths in such a tree (in the style of Knuth’s estimator) can be non-trivial.
This is because, to decide whether a given execution graph is a child of another, the model checker may have to consult the history of the exploration,
possibly paying exponential cost, and losing the poly-time sampling property.
Third, one could try to reduce variance by a number of heuristics that perform importance sampling in various ways.
However, in practice, the performance of importance sampling is very sensitive to good estimations of the relative sizes of different options.
These estimations are difficult to come by for practical benchmarks, other than by computationally expensive nested Monte Carlo searches.

We also explore the theoretical question whether there is a good approximate counter that uses
subexponential
time.
We find that, surprisingly, there is an unbiased estimator with low variance that runs in time
\tilde{O}(\sqrt{N})
, where
N
is the number of maximal traces!
While theoretically optimal (there is a
O(\sqrt{N})
lower bound
(
47
)
), the polynomial factors appearing in the algorithm are too large for empirical evaluation.

Contributions.

In summary, this paper makes the following contributions:

•

We formulate the estimation problem for Mazurkiewicz traces and show that it is #P-hard
and
inapproximable within any subexponential factor unless
P
=
NP
.

•

We show how a stateless optimal
DPOR
algorithm can be modified to produce a poly-time unbiased estimation, and show how a population-based
estimation strategy reduced the variance of the estimation (
section

4
,
section

4.3
).

•

We evaluate our estimation procedure on a wide variety of shared-memory concurrency benchmarks in the context of the
JMC
model checker (
section

6
).
The summary of our experiments is that our estimator provides accurate and robust estimates of the number of equivalence classes, as well as for the cost of model checking,
while using a small population budget and a modest number of samples.

2.
The State Estimation Problem

2.1.
Programming Model and Partial Order Semantics

We consider a multithreaded programming model, in which a set of
threads
concurrently read and write
shared locations
.
Each thread performs sequential computation, including reads and writes to shared locations and deterministic sequential control flow.
We refrain from providing a concrete syntax.
Instead, following
(
34
)
, we define a partial-order semantics using
execution graphs
.

We assume a set of locations
\mathsf{Loc}
and a set of values
\mathsf{Val}
.
An event,
e\in\mathsf{Event}
, is either the initialization event
\mathsf{init}
, or a thread event

{\langle t,i,\ell\rangle}
where
t\in\mathsf{Tid}
is a thread identifier unique for each thread,

i\in\mathsf{Idx}\mathrel{\vbox{\halign{\hfil#\hfil\cr$\scriptscriptstyle\boldsymbol{\triangle}$\cr\kern 0.6458pt\cr$=$\cr}}}\mathbb{N}
is an index inside each thread, and

\ell\in\mathsf{Lab}
is a label that is either a
write label

\mathsf{W}(l,v)
or a
read label

\mathsf{R}(l,v)
for a location
l\in\mathsf{Loc}
and value
v\in\mathsf{Val}
.
When applicable, the functions
\mathsf{tid}
,
\mathsf{idx}
,
\mathsf{loc}
,
\mathsf{val}
, and
\mathsf{op}
, return the thread identifier, index,
location, value of an event, and type of event’s label respectively.
An event is called a read event if its label is a read label and an event is called
a write event if it is either
\mathsf{init}
or its label is a write label.

An
execution graph

G=(E,{{\color[rgb]{0,0.6,0}\mathsf{rf}}},{{\color[rgb]{1,0.5,0}\mathsf{mo}}})
consists of:

(1)

a set
E
of events, containing
\mathsf{init}
, such that no two events have both the same thread identifier and the same index; We use
G.\mathsf{R}
to denote the set
of all read events in
E
and
G.\mathsf{W}
to denote the set of all write events in
E
.
Also, we use subscripts to further restrict those sets like
G.\mathsf{W}_{l}
is the set of all write events in
E
whose location is
l
.

(2)

a partial order
{{\color[rgb]{0,0.6,0}\mathsf{rf}}}\subseteq G.\mathsf{W}\times G.\mathsf{R}
, called the
reads-from
relation, that relates each read event to the
(unique) write event to the same location from which the read gets its value. Formally,

(a)

\forall e\in G.\mathsf{R}:\exists e^{\prime}\in G.\mathsf{W}_{\mathsf{loc}(e)}:(e^{\prime},e)\in{{\color[rgb]{0,0.6,0}\mathsf{rf}}}.

(b)

\forall(e_{1},e_{2})\in{{\color[rgb]{0,0.6,0}\mathsf{rf}}}:\mathsf{op}(e_{2}.\ell)=\mathsf{R}\wedge(e_{1}=\mathsf{init}\vee(\mathsf{op}(e_{1}.\ell)=\mathsf{W}\wedge\mathsf{loc}(e_{1})=\mathsf{loc}(e_{2}))
.

(c)

(e_{1},e_{2})\in{{\color[rgb]{0,0.6,0}\mathsf{rf}}}\wedge(e_{3},e_{2})\in{{\color[rgb]{0,0.6,0}\mathsf{rf}}}\implies e_{1}=e_{3}
.

(3)

a strict partial order
{{\color[rgb]{1,0.5,0}\mathsf{mo}}}\subseteq\bigcup_{l\in\mathsf{Loc}}G.\mathsf{W}_{l}\times G.\mathsf{W}_{l}
, called the
modification order
, which is a total order on
the write events on the same location. Formally,

(a)

\forall(e_{1},e_{2})\in{{\color[rgb]{1,0.5,0}\mathsf{mo}}}:e_{1}\neq e_{2}\wedge e_{1},e_{2}\in G.\mathsf{W}\wedge(\mathsf{loc}(e_{1})=\mathsf{loc}(e_{2})\vee e_{1}=\mathsf{init}\vee e_{2}=\mathsf{init}).

(b)

\forall l\in\mathsf{Loc}:\forall e_{1},e_{2}\in G.\mathsf{W}_{l}:e_{1}\neq e_{2}\implies(e_{1},e_{2})\in{{\color[rgb]{1,0.5,0}\mathsf{mo}}}\text{ or }(e_{2},e_{1})\in{{\color[rgb]{1,0.5,0}\mathsf{mo}}}\text{, but not both.}

We define a
program order

{{\color[rgb]{0.25,0.25,0.25}\mathsf{po}}}
relation as follows:

{{\color[rgb]{0.25,0.25,0.25}\mathsf{po}}}\triangleq\{(\mathsf{init},e)\mid e\in E\setminus\mathsf{init}\}\cup\{(e_{1},e_{2})\mid e_{1},e_{2}\in E\wedge\mathsf{tid}(e_{1})=\mathsf{tid}(e_{2})\wedge\mathsf{idx}(e_{1})<\mathsf{idx}(e_{2})\}

An execution graph is
sequentially consistent
if
{{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}\mathsf{sc}}}\triangleq({{\color[rgb]{0.25,0.25,0.25}\mathsf{po}}}\cup{{\color[rgb]{0,0.6,0}\mathsf{rf}}}\cup{{\color[rgb]{1,0.5,0}\mathsf{mo}}}\cup{{\color[rgb]{0.18,1,0.7}\mathsf{fr}}})^{+}
is irreflexive, where
{{\color[rgb]{0.18,1,0.7}\mathsf{fr}}}\triangleq({{\color[rgb]{0,0.6,0}\mathsf{rf}}}^{-1};{{\color[rgb]{1,0.5,0}\mathsf{mo}}}).

The semantics of
P
is the set of its sequentially consistent execution graphs.

Given a graph
G
and a set
F
of its nodes, we write
G|_{F}
for the restriction of the graph to
F
and
G\setminus F
for
G|_{E\setminus F}
.

2.2.
A Transition System on Execution Graphs

The operational semantics of the programming language defines a transition system on execution graphs.
Instead of a detailed syntax, we assume that there is a function
\mathsf{next}_{P}(G)
which takes
an execution graph and returns a
set of possible next events from the program
P
: the next operation
by one of the unblocked threads, after the events in
G
have transpired.
If
\mathsf{next}_{P}(G)
is empty, then there are no remaining operations and the program has terminated.

We define a labeled transition system (
\mathsf{LTS}
)
\mathcal{T}(P)
on the set of sequentially consistent execution graphs.
First, for any execution graph
G
and location
l
, we define
G.{{\color[rgb]{1,0.5,0}\mathsf{mo}}_{l}}^{\max}
as the unique maximal event
e\in G.\mathsf{W}_{l}
in the
G.{{\color[rgb]{1,0.5,0}\mathsf{mo}}_{l}}
ordering.
Intuitively,
G.{{\color[rgb]{1,0.5,0}\mathsf{mo}}_{l}}^{\max}
is the last write to the location
l
in
G
.
The labeled transition system
\mathcal{T}(P)=(S,S_{0},\xrightarrow{\mathsf{Tid}})
is defined as:

(1)

S
is the set of consistent execution graphs.

(2)

S_{0}
is the execution graph
G_{\mathsf{init}}=({\{\mathsf{init}\}},\emptyset,\emptyset)
consisting of the singleton node
\mathsf{init}
.

(3)

\mathsf{Tid}
is the set of thread identifiers.

There is an edge
G_{1}\xrightarrow{t}G_{2}
if
there is and
e\in\mathsf{next}_{P}(G_{1})
with
t=\mathsf{tid}(e)
, and

(1)

G_{2}.E=G_{1}.E\cup{\{e\}}
.

(2)

G_{2}.{{\color[rgb]{0,0.6,0}\mathsf{rf}}}=\begin{cases}G_{1}.{{\color[rgb]{0,0.6,0}\mathsf{rf}}}&\mathsf{op}(e.\ell)=\mathsf{W}\\
G_{1}.{{\color[rgb]{0,0.6,0}\mathsf{rf}}}\cup\{(G_{1}.{{\color[rgb]{1,0.5,0}\mathsf{mo}}_{\mathsf{loc}(e)}}^{\max},e)\}&\text{otherwise}\end{cases}

(3)

G_{2}.{{\color[rgb]{1,0.5,0}\mathsf{mo}}}{}=\begin{cases}G_{1}.{{\color[rgb]{1,0.5,0}\mathsf{mo}}}{}&\mathsf{op}(e.\ell)=\mathsf{R}\\
G_{1}.{{\color[rgb]{1,0.5,0}\mathsf{mo}}}\cup\{(e^{\prime},e)\mid e^{\prime}\in G_{1}.W_{\mathsf{loc}(e)}\}&\text{otherwise}\end{cases}

An execution graph is
reachable
if
G_{\mathsf{init}}\xrightarrow{\cdot}^{*}G
.
An execution graph is
maximal
if it has no successors,
i.e.,
\mathsf{next}_{P}(G)=\emptyset
.
We write
C(P)
for the number of maximal reachable execution graphs in
\mathcal{T}(P)
.

Note that
\mathcal{T}(P)
is a DAG but not necessarily a tree: two
different paths in the exploration can reach the same execution graph.
For example, if two threads each read a variable, the two possible thread schedules both lead to the same execution graph.

2.3.
Counting Execution Graphs

When counting execution graphs, we assume that the runs of
P
are bounded (by some number
n
).
To simplify notation, we will assume in the following that we have “compiled” the number
n

into the program (e.g., by unrolling loops an appropriate number of times) and
therefore, every run of the program is already bounded by the size of the program.
Thus, we will omit the
n
in our results and assume
n=|P|
.

Our goal is to count the number
C(P)
of reachable maximal execution graphs of
P
.
To this end, we define the following counting problem.

Problem 1 (The Counting Problem).

Given as input a program
P
, output the number of maximal reachable execution graphs
C(P)
in
\mathcal{T}(P)
.

Theorem 2.1.

The Counting Problem is
\#\mathsf{P}
-complete.

\#\mathsf{P}
-hardness holds even if either the number of threads or the number of shared locations is constant.

Recall that
\#\mathsf{P}
is the complexity class associated with counting the number
of accepting computations of a non-deterministic Turing machine (see, e.g.,
(
5
)
).
In particular, the problem of counting the number of satisfying assignments of a SAT formula is

\#\mathsf{P}
-complete.
Hence,
\#\mathsf{P}
-complete problems are at least as hard as NP-complete problems, which
gives strong evidence against the existence of any polynomial-time
algorithms for the Counting Problem.
In light of this, we can relax the notion of the Counting Problem so that we only need an approximate count,
rather than an exact count.

Problem 2 (The Approximate Counting Problem).

A randomized algorithm
A
is an
(f(n),\rho)
-approximate counter if, for every program
P
of size
n
, we have

\operatorname{Pr}[C(P)/f(n)\leq A(P,\rho)\leq C(P)f(n)]\geq 1-\rho

Note that since
C(P)
is always at most an exponential function in the size of
P
,
the least non-trivial approximation that we can ask for is an approximation
with a sub-exponential ratio.
However, even for this very weak approximation ratio, we show that it is highly unlikely that a (randomized) polynomial-time algorithm exists, by means of the following theorem.

Theorem 2.2.

Unless RP = NP, there is no randomized polynomial-time
(2^{n^{1-\epsilon}},\rho)
-approximate
counter for
\epsilon>0
independent of the input.

Here RP is the class of problems which admit randomized polynomial-time algorithms. (For a formal definition, see
(
5
)
). Hence, this theorem puts hard limits on the Approximate Counting Problem.
Furthermore, unless P = NP, a similar argument shows that there is no deterministic polynomial-time
algorithm that gives a sub-exponential approximation to
C(P)
.
(We defer the proofs of both of these theorems to
appendix

A
).

In light of these hardness results, we now present some randomized algorithms that run in polynomial time and offer an
unbiased estimate
of
C(P)
, i.e., the expected value returned by these algorithms will be
C(P)
.

3.
Poly-Time Unbiased Estimation Algorithms

Let
P
be a program and
\mathcal{T}(P)
its corresponding transition system.
A randomized algorithm
A
that maps programs
P
to natural numbers
is an
unbiased estimator
for
C(P)
if
the expected value of
A
on the input

C(P)
.
It is poly-time if it runs it time polynomial in
|P|
.
In this section, we present two poly-time unbiased estimators for
C(P)

3.1.
Knuth Estimator

If
\mathcal{T}(P)
is a tree, there is a simple unbiased estimator for
C(P)
by Monte Carlo estimation.
The algorithm, given below, was described by Knuth
(
32
)
.

Algorithm K

(
32
)
.
Given a tree
T
, run the following randomized procedure.
Starting at the root,
for each node
n_{i}
with
d_{i}
children, choose one of its
d_{i}
children uniformly at random and move there, until reaching a leaf
n_{k}
.
Stop and output
d_{1}\cdot d_{2}\cdot\ldots\cdot d_{k-1}
as an estimate of the number of leaves of
T
.

Proposition 3.1.

(
32
)

If
\mathcal{T}(P)
is a tree,
Algorithm K
is a poly-time unbiased estimator for
C(P)
.

We observe that each path
n_{1}\ldots n_{k}
of the tree from its root
n_{1}
to a leaf
n_{k}
is reached by the random walk with probability
p(n_{1},\dots,n_{k})=1/(d_{1}d_{2}\ldots d_{k-1})
. Since the output of our algorithm for the path
n_{1},\dots,n_{k}

is exactly
1/p(n_{1},\dots,n_{k})
, it follows that the expected value of our output
is simply the number of all possible paths
n_{1},\dots,n_{k}
,
which is exactly the number of leaves.

3.2.
Pitt Estimation

The above mentioned disadvantage of
Algorithm K
on DAGs can be rectified:

(

showed that

can be adapted to estimate the number of sinks in a DAG.
The key idea is to correct for overcounting by dividing the multiplicative weight at each step by the number of incoming edges to the current node.

Algorithm P

(

.
Given a DAG

Starting at the root, for each node

with

children and

incoming edges,
pick a child uniformly at random and move there, until reaching a node

with no outgoing edges.
Stop and output
\frac{d_{1}}{e_{1}}\ldots\frac{d_{k}}{e_{k}}

In the algorithm, we assume
e_{1}=1
for the root and
d_{k}=1
for the leaf.

For any program

is a poly-time unbiased estimator for

4.

4.1.

In order to circumvent the high variance of

Unlike

.
Second, as the following example demonstrates, when

4.4.
Related Work: The GenMC Estimator

The state estimation problem was also considered in
(
33
)
, where the authors give a randomized procedure based on the
Trust
algorithm.
However, as we show below, that estimator is not an unbiased estimator; thus, even with exponentially many samples,
it has no guarantees to converge to the correct answer.
Experimentally, the estimator converges to incorrect values quite frequently.

The procedure is also based on the
Trust
algorithm, and proceeds as follows.
First, it preferentially schedules writes over reads.
For each read or write event, it explores one of the forward-revisits uniformly at random.
Instead of computing the estimate on the fly, it records the number of values each read can read from.
In case a new write is added and
Trust
detects that a backward revisit would be required, the estimator
does not perform the backward revisit but increments the map entry for any read that could read from this new value by one.
At the end, it takes the product of the “possible reads-from” counts for every read and the “possible modification order"
for every write to produce the final estimate.

Intuitively, the estimator assumes one can generate all graphs in
\mathcal{T}(P)

without any backward revisits, simply by scheduling write events before read events.
When this assumption holds, the estimate is unbiased.
However, the assumption may not hold.
Since the algorithm forbids backward revisits, it may omit graphs
reachable only via backward revisits and produce a biased estimate.

Example 4.7.

one where the line

The algorithm in
(

)
runs as follows.
It first schedules
t_{1}
and writes 1 to
x
.
Then since both threads are enabled with a read, the scheduler picks one uniformly at random.
A case analysis of the two possibilities shows that the expected value of the estimator is
3.5.

5.
A Sub-Exponential Time Approximate Counter

In the previous subsections, we had presented various (randomized) polynomial-time algorithms in order to estimate the number of complete execution graphs of the given concurrent program. Due to the
\#\mathsf{P}
-hardness result of approximation (Theorem
2.2
), all
of these algorithms, in the worst case, are going to have exponentially high variance.
In light of this, we can ask the following question:
What if we relax the requirement that our estimation algorithm must work in polynomial time?
Of course, if we allow the algorithm to work in exponential time, then we can get zero variance,
by traversing the entire
Trust
tree completely deterministically.
Hence, in order for this question to make sense, we ask whether there is an

(r,1/4)
-approximate counter for constant
r
that runs in sub-exponential time.
Surprisingly, we prove that such an estimation algorithm exists.

Theorem 5.1.

For any constants
r
and
\rho
, there is a randomized
\tilde{O}(\sqrt{C(P)})
-time
(r,\rho)
-approximate counter.

The idea behind the algorithm goes back to Stockmeyer
(
47
)
and we sketch the main construction.
To describe our algorithm, we assume that we are given a concurrent program
P
,
which defines the tree
\mathcal{D}(P)
.
As mentioned before, if
d
is the size of the program
P
,
this tree has height
d^{2}
and an inspection of the construction shows that the degree of each node is at most
d^{2}
.
However, every node need not have degree
d^{2}
. In order to make this tree uniform, we add dummy nodes to each node of
\mathcal{D}(P)
so that the degree of each node becomes
d^{2}
.
Call the resulting tree
\mathcal{H}(P)
. Note that
\mathcal{H}(P)

is the complete
d^{2}
-ary tree of height
d^{2}
.
(We will never explicitly construct
\mathcal{D}(P)
nor
\mathcal{H}(P)
during our algorithm;
these are just defined implicitly).

The algorithm distinguishes two situations: either
C(P)
is “small” (less than a parameter that we will select later)
or “big” (bigger than the parameter).
If
C(P)
is big, then we can draw a number of samples and return the fraction of samples in
\mathcal{D}(P)
as our estimate.
Using Chebyshev bounds, we can bound the error in the procedure.
On the other hand, if
C(P)
is small, we can enumerate all elements by a binary search.
The binary search uses an inorder numbering of all the nodes of the tree.
The numbering is used to define intervals of leaves, and the search checks if there is a leaf of
\mathcal{D}(P)
in a given interval.
This query becomes the same as checking if a given path in the tree
\mathcal{H}(P)
can be extended to a maximal execution graph in
\mathcal{D}(P)
, for which we can use
Trust
.

Finally, we pick the parameter
\sqrt{C(P)}
for which we can bound the entire run time by
\tilde{O}(\sqrt{C(P)})
,
We provide the details of the construction in
appendix

B
.

Theorem

5.1
is extremely surprising: we get an estimate for
C(P)
by making (roughly)
\sqrt{C(P)}
runs of the model checker!
Consider a program with about 1M executions; with about 1K runs, we can get a good estimate with high probability!
The catch is that the search has a high polynomial cost
O(|P|^{6})
and so we did not implement it.

6.2.
Phase
(A)
: Convergence of Estimation

We first eplore if
TeStor
’s estimation converges with a modest sampling budget.

Setup

We consider the estimator has succeeded if it converges to an acceptable range around the real count.
More specifically, we declare convergence when the running mean of the estimator enters the ±20% relative error band and remains within it for a stability window of

50
iterations, while also satisfying a flatness condition (the moving average changes by less than a
2\%
relative threshold for a stability window of 100 iterations).
We evaluate four configurations:
TeStor
with stochastic enumeration budgets
1, 5, 20, and 50.
Each configuration is executed for a sequence of
2000
runs, and to account for randomness, each sequence is repeated with five independent random seeds.
A configuration is deemed to successfully converge if it converges under at least three of the five seeds.
To assess the effectiveness of our estimators, we focus on two metrics:
(1) the relative error after 2000 runs, computed as
(estimate count) / exact count; and
(2) the trials-to-convergence, which is reported only when a configuration converges on a benchmark and is averaged over the seeds for which it converged.

Benchmarks

For this evaluation phase, we selected 13 benchmarks that illustrate the characteristic behavior patterns of the two estimators. The first five benchmarks (presented in table
1
) correspond to typical uses of concurrent data structures, including LinkedList, MSqueue, AMGStack, and TimestampStack. These programs exhibit high thread contention, yielding large and complex behavior spaces. The remaining 8 (presented in table
2
) are synthetic benchmarks designed to isolate specific concurrency patterns and stress particular aspects of the estimators’ convergence behavior.
We omitted the others because they are dominated by blocked executions—we will return to them in Phase (B).

Results Summary

Table

1
and
2
present the results of the evaluation.
For each estimator configuration, we report (i) the average relative error after 2,000 trials, averaged over all 5 seeds,
(ii) the success ratio (i.e., the fraction of seeds that satisfy the convergence criterion), and
(iii) when this ratio exceeds 0.6 (at least 3 out of 5 seeds), the average number of trials-to-convergence over the converged seeds(rounded to int).

We note that the baseline Algorithm P failed to converge on every benchmark.

Convergence::

Positively, we observe
TeStor
with SE budgets of 20 and 50 converges on all benchmarks within a very small number of trials—typically a few hundred—even when the underlying behavior space is much larger, ranging from 30 thousand up to 1.7 million. We therefore believe that our evaluation demonstrates the practicality of our estimators.
On the other hand,
TeStor
with a budget of 1 (Algorithm T) failed to converge on all benchmarks except BigO.

Relative Error::

TeStor
with SE budgets of 20 and 50 generally yields very small relative errors after 2000 runs. While a budget of 50 does not show a clear advantage in achieving convergence compared to a budget of 20, it does provide a noticeable improvement in the final relative error. We also observe that, for most benchmarks, whenever a configuration reported convergence, the relative error induced by the configuration remains well within the
20\%
band, indicating that our convergence criterion is appropriately chosen.

JMC

We now apply
TeStor
to predict the cost of model checking.
Our experiments focus on evaluating how accurately our estimator approximates the cost induced by
JMC
.

Cost Model

In estimating the cost of model checking, we consider not just the maximal executions but also other execution graphs constructed by the model checker.
We add the following nodes and costs.

Inconsistent graphs::

In estimating
C(P)
, we ignored execution graphs that are inconsistent in
\mathcal{D}(P)
. However,
JMC
needs to construct these graphs, check consistency, and filter them.
To model this cost, we represent inconsistent successors as leaves in the tree.

Weights::

Algorithm K and Algorithm S extend naturally to estimating the total weight of a weighted tree, instead of only counting leaves
(
32
;
51
)
.
This is crucial for designing an estimator of
JMC
’s total cost:
JMC
induce some cost over all execution graphs, not only to maximal ones.
By estimating the total weight of the corresponding tree, we obtain a direct estimator for the total cost induced by
JMC
.
For our evaluation, we assign a uniform cost to every execution graph explored by
JMC
.
One can make this more precise, e.g., by assigning a cost proportional to the size of the graph.

Blocked executions::

Finally, we also represent blocked executions in the tree.

Summary of results

In Table
3
, we repeat the Phase
(A)
evaluation with budget 20 on the same benchmarks, and additionally include six benchmarks where
JMC
explores many blocked executions,
to estimate the cost.
Our first observation is that
TeStor
with a budget of 20 requires a similar number of trials to converge across most of the benchmarks presented earlier.
The only exception is Sigma, where the success ratio fell from 1.0 to 0.6.
The additional benchmarks are notably more challenging:
TeStor
converged under all seeds on only 2 of 7 and failed to converge on 1.
The average relative error increased by roughly an order of magnitude, yet remains within—or close to—the 20% band.

Another observation is that benchmarks with a similar number of complete executions can differ dramatically in the cost required to fully traverse their execution spaces.
For example, incNTest and FineCounter have the same number of complete executions (
518400
), but incNTest has
683859
total execution graphs, whereas FineCounter has
2191297
.
This supports our conjecture that the total number of execution graphs is a better proxy for the cost of model checking than the number of complete executions alone.

Overall, our experiments indicate that
TeStor
is stable and robust for estimating the cost of model checkers,
delivering accurate estimates with modest budgets and moderate numbers of trials across a diverse set of benchmarks.

7.
Related Work

In this paper, we have studied the problem of estimating the size of the space that a model checker explores on a program. Similar problems have been studied in the literature, albeit with different approaches. Penix and Visser
(
43
)
add standard code-coverage metrics to Java PathFinder
(
26
)
, tracking which bytecode instructions and branch outcomes are exercised during state-space exploration.
Bron, Ziv, and Ur
(
12
)
introduce synchronization coverage for concurrent Java programs, defining a small set of concurrency-specific coverage tasks (for example, whether a synchronized block ever caused another thread to block) and treating full coverage as a practical stopping criterion.
Nevertheless, code coverage and its derivatives are known to be imprecise measures for the space of a program
Chockler, Kupferman, and Vardi
(
15
)
propose mutation-based coverage metrics for model checking that ask which states, transitions, paths, and subformulas of a finite-state model actually matter for establishing
M\models\varphi
.
Taleghani and Atlee
(
49
)
estimate state-space coverage in explicit-state model checking by sampling unexplored transitions using BFS and extrapolating the number of remaining states. However their estimator is heuristic and comes with no guarantees of unbiasedness or convergence.
Path profiling proposed by Ball and Larus
(
6
)
is another dynamic coverage metric: instead of asking which blocks or edges are covered, it measures how often each control-flow path in a procedure is executed under a given workload.
These approaches describe which parts of the system and specification are exercised, or roughly how much of the explicit state graph has been visited, but not how far verification has progressed within the underlying execution space. In contrast, our contribution serves as a quantitative complement, estimating the size of that space and giving a quantitative notion of convergence.

Purely statistical techniques have also been proposed in recent fuzzing literature; for example the use of good Turing estimator
(
11
;
10
;
9
)
or the use of saturation or past coverage trends have been proposed to predict the expected time to full coverage of the fuzzing campaign
(
38
;
37
)
. As such, these problems are specific to purely memoryless testing techniques like greybox and blackbox fuzzing, that aim to discover as many bugs as possible.
Our setting is different: we aim to count semantic execution equivalence classes of a concurrent program, and we design estimators with explicit mathematical guarantees for this counting problem (unbiasedness), rather than heuristic progress indicators for fuzzing campaigns.

Our work is also close in spirit to approximate counting on automata. In this context, a long standing open problem was that of approximating the number of words of a given length
n
accepted by a given NFA. It was recently shown to admit an FPRAS (fully polynomial randomized approximation scheme)
(
4
)
, and the algorithm has since then been subsequently improved as well
(
39
;
40
)
.
Problem
1
as considered in this paper can be reduced to such automata counting by taking the local state-space automaton of each thread, forming their partially synchronous product, intersecting with the regular language of lexicographic normal forms of trace equivalence, and finally asking how many words of some appropriate length the resulting automaton accepts. However, the product automaton is exponential in the number of threads and variables, so even FPRAS algorithms for approximate counting on automata become exponential in the number of threads and are not competitive. A similar observation applies to propositional model counting: one can, at least in principle, encode our problem as a #SAT instance and appeal to approximate counters such as ApproxMC
(
13
)
. However,#SAT is at least as hard as Problem
1
, so this approach is not attractive for our setting.

8.
Conclusion

In this paper, we looked at the problem of estimating the size of the space explored by a model checker on a bounded concurrent program. Even though there has been previous work on related questions, all of them were based on heuristic approaches without any theoretical results. We lay the theoretical foundations for this problem, both by exhibiting hardness results as well as by coming up with the first poly-time unbiased estimator for the problem. An implementation of our procedure on a wide range of shared-memory concurrency benchmarks shows that our estimator provides robust estimates whilst only using a modest number of samples.

this tree has height
d^{2}
and an inspection of the construction shows that the degree of each node is at most
d^{2}
.
However, every node need not have degree
d^{2}
. In order to make this tree uniform, we add dummy nodes to each node of
\mathcal{D}(P)
so that the degree of each node becomes
d^{2}
.
Call the resulting tree
\mathcal{H}(P)
. Note that
\mathcal{H}(P)

is the complete
d^{2}
-ary tree of height
d^{2}
.
(We will never explicitly construct
\mathcal{D}(P)
nor
\mathcal{H}(P)
during our algorithm;
these are just defined implicitly).

We define an
inorder numbering
of
\mathcal{H}(P)
, which associates a subset of numbers to each node of
\mathcal{H}(P)
recursively in the following way: We start at the root with a variable called count initialized to 0. For each
i
from 1 to
d^{2}
, we recursively compute the inorder numbering of the
i^{th}
subtree of the root. At the end of this computation, let the value of count be
n_{i}
. We increment
n_{i}
by 1,
add
n_{i}+1
to the inorder numbering of the root and move to the next iteration of the loop (i.e., to
i+1
). Note that the base case of the recursion is when we arrive at a leaf, in which case we will simply increment count by 1 and add this incremented value to the inorder numbering of the leaf and go back to its parent. In this way, we associate a subset of numbers to each node of
\mathcal{H}(P)
.

For any node
u
, let
M(u)
be the subset of numbers associated to
u
by the inorder numbering. Let
S(u)
and
B(u)
be the smallest and biggest numbers appearing in
M(u)
respectively. It is easy to see that
M(u)
is a single number for each leaf
and otherwise, consists of exactly
d^{2}
numbers. Furthermore,
M(u)\cap M(v)=\emptyset

for any two distinct nodes
u,v
.
Hence, from the construction, it follows that each number between
1
and
N=(d^{2})^{d^{2}}+(d^{2})[{(d^{2})^{d^{2}+1}}-1]
is assigned to exactly one node.

Now, for any pair of numbers
1\leq a\leq b\leq N
, let
[a,b]
denote the interval of natural numbers between
a
and
b
. Notice that if
M(u)\cap[a,b]\neq\emptyset

and
M(v)\cap[a,b]\neq\emptyset
, then the least common ancestor
w
of
u,v
also satisfies
M(w)\cap[a,b]\neq\emptyset
. Hence, for each interval
[a,b]
, there
is a unique node
w_{a,b}
such that
M(w_{[a,b]})\cap[a,b]\neq\emptyset
and
every node
u
satisfying
M(u)\cap[a,b]\neq\emptyset
belongs to the subtree of
w_{[a,b]}
.

Based on this notion of inorder numbering, we now present our estimation algorithm. Our estimation algorithm works on a parameter
\theta
and consists of two acts: one where we operate under the assumption that the number of leaves of
\mathcal{D}(P)\leq\theta
and another where we operate under the assumption that the number of leaves of
\mathcal{D}(P)>\theta
.

Act One: Number of leaves of
\mathcal{D}(P)\leq\theta
.

In order to describe the first act of the algorithm, we need some notation and some small observations. Recall that any number between 1 and
N
is assigned to exactly one node. Therefore, we can order the nodes of
\mathcal{H}(P)
totally as follows:

u\preceq v
iff
S(u)<S(v)
. We now make the following series of observations.

Observation 1:

We can compute the inorder numbering
M(w)
of any node as follows. We start by setting
u
to be the root and a counter
x
set to 0. Intuitively
x+1
will denote the smallest number that can appear in the inorder numbering of any descendant of
u
.
At each step, we find the unique child
v
of
u
that contains
w
as its descendant.
Suppose
v=w
. If
v
is a leaf, then we simply return
\{x+1\}
.
If
v
is an internal node at level
d-j
(for some
j
), we return
\{x+N_{j-1}+1,x+2N_{j-1}+2,\dots,x+d^{2}N_{j-1}+d^{2}\}
where
N_{j-1}=(d^{2})^{j-1}+d^{2}[(d^{2})^{j}-1]
. Otherwise, if
v
is the
i^{th}
child for some
i\geq 1
and
v
is at level
d-j
(for some
j
), then we set
x=x+(i-1)N_{j-1}+(i-1)
and set
u
to
v
and continue. It can be easily checked that this procedure correctly computes
M(w)
and
runs in time
d^{2}
.

Observation 2:

Given any node
u
of
\mathcal{H}(P)
, we can check if
there is a leaf of
\mathcal{D}(P)
at the subtree rooted at
u
. Indeed, if
u

corresponds to a dummy node, i.e., not a node of
\mathcal{D}(P)
, then
no such node will exist. On the other hand, if
u
is a node of
\mathcal{D}(P)

then indeed there will be a leaf of
\mathcal{D}(P)
at the subtree rooted at
u
.
Hence, this check can be simply reduced to checking if
u
is a node of
\mathcal{D}(P)
,
which can be done quite efficiently.

Observation 3:

Given any interval
I=[a,b]
, we can find the node

w_{I}
as follows: We initially begin with the node
u
set to the root and an interval
I^{\prime}
set to
[1,N]
. In each step, we will maintain the invariant that
w_{I}
will be a descendant of
u
,
u=w_{I^{\prime}}
and also that
I\subseteq I^{\prime}
. Indeed, this is true at the beginning.
Suppose at some step, we have reached
u
with interval
I^{\prime}
such that
u=w_{I^{\prime}}

and
I\subseteq I^{\prime}
. If
u
is a leaf, then
w_{I}
must be
u
and so we are done.
Otherwise let
M(u)
be
\{x_{1},\dots,x_{d^{2}}\}
and let
I^{\prime}=[a^{\prime},b^{\prime}]
. If
I
is completely contained
inside any one of
I^{\prime}_{1}=[a^{\prime}+1,x_{1}-1],I^{\prime}_{2}=[x_{1}+1,x_{2}-1],[x_{2}+1,x_{3}-1],\dots,I^{\prime}_{d^{2}}=[x_{d^{2}-1}+1,b^{\prime}-1]
, then we pick the unique
j
such that
I
is completely contained inside
I^{\prime}_{j}
. Then we update
u
to be the
j^{th}
child of
u
and update
I^{\prime}
to be
I^{\prime}_{j}
. (Notice that the invariant is satisfied in this case). Otherwise,
it must be the case that
u=w_{I}
and so we just output
u
. In this way, by going down the tree, in
d^{2}
steps, we can find the node
w_{I}
.

Now, let
v_{1},v_{2},\dots
be the leaves of
\mathcal{D}(P)
(the
Trust
tree, not the complete
d^{2}
-ary tree) ordered according to the
\preceq
ordering. Using the above mentioned observations and notations, we will now describe an algorithm which will find the nodes
v_{1},v_{2},\dots,v_{\theta}
. It does so in
\theta
many phases, where in the
i^{th}
phase it will find the node
v_{i}
.

The
i^{th}
phase will operate in
d^{2}
many subphases. At the start of each subphase
we will maintain a node
z
of
\mathcal{D}(P)
and an interval
I=[a,b]
such that

z=w_{I}
. (For the first subphase of the first phase,
z
is taken to be the root and
I
is taken to be
[1,N]
). For each subphase, we will always maintain the invariant that the node
v_{i}
we are looking for will be a
descendant of
z
,
S(v_{i})\in I
and
v_{i-1}
will not be a descendant of
z
. (Notice that this invariant is definitely satisfied at the beginning of the first subphase of the first phase).

In the
j^{th}
subphase, given the node
z
(with corresponding inorder numbering
M(z)
) and the interval
I=[a,b]
, we proceed as follows. If
a=b
, then by our invariants, it follows that
v_{i}=z
and so we return
z
. Otherwise, we loop over all of the
d^{2}
children of
z
(say
u_{1},u_{2},\dots,u_{d^{2}}
such that
u_{1}\preceq u_{2}\preceq\dots\preceq u_{d^{2}}
).
When looping over
u_{k}
, we first compute
M(u_{k})
using Observation 1. If
M(u_{k})\cap I=\emptyset
, then we move to
u_{k+1}
in the loop. Further, using Observation 2, we check if there is some node of
\mathcal{D}(P)
that is present in the subtree rooted at
u_{k}
. If not, then also we move to
u_{k+1}
in the loop.
Else, it immediately follows that
v_{i}
must be a descendant of
u_{k}
.
Hence, we move to the
(j+1)^{th}
subphase with the node
u_{k}
and compute the new interval
I^{\prime}
as follows: Let
x
be the
(k-1)^{th}
number appearing in
M(z)
if
k-1\geq 1
, otherwise let
x
be
a
. Let
y
be the
(k+1)^{th}
number appearing in
M(z)
if
k+1\leq d^{2}
, otherwise let
y
be
b
. We then set
I^{\prime}
to be
[x,y]
. It can be easily verified that
u_{k}=w_{I^{\prime}}
. Furthermore because
v_{i}
must be a descendant of
u_{k}
, it follows that
S(v_{i})\in I^{\prime}
. Hence the invariants for the next subphase are satisfied.

Note that in each subphase, we are going one level down the tree. Hence there can be at most
d^{2}
subphases in each phase. Furthermore, due to our invariants, it follows
that when the
i^{th}
phase ends, we have arrived at the node
v_{i}
with an interval
I
of the form
[a,a]
. We then start the
(i+1)^{th}
phase by setting our interval to be
[a+1,N]
and computing the corresponding node
w_{a+1,N}
using Observation 3.
It easily follows that our invariant is satisfied at the beginning of the
(i+1)^{th}
phase. This proves that the algorithm finds the nodes
v_{1},v_{2},\dots,v_{\theta}
.

Let us analyse the running time of this algorithm. Notice that each subphase of each phase
takes at most
O(d^{4})
time, in order to go over all of the possible children of
z
and compute each of their inorder numberings. Since there are
d^{2}
subphases in total and
\theta
phases overall, it follows that the total amount of time spent in this act is
O(d^{6}\theta)
. Note that if the number of leaves of
\mathcal{D}(P)
is indeed
at most
\theta
, then our algorithm will stop with this act and output all of them.
Since it uses no randomization, in this case, it will output the exact number of leaves
of
\mathcal{D}(P)
with zero variance.

Second Act: Number of leaves of
\mathcal{D}(P)>\theta
.

Suppose we executed the first act of our algorithm to completion. It follows that there
are at least
\theta
many leaves of
\mathcal{D}(P)
. At this point, we initiate the second act of our algorithm. Let
M=((d^{2})^{d^{2}+1})-1
be the total number of nodes
of
\mathcal{H}(P)
. We take
z
independent random walks and count the number of times we hit a maximal execution graph (say
y
). Then we output
(M/z)\cdot y
.

Let
X
be the random variable corresponding to the value that we output.
It is easy to see that the expected value of
X
is the precise number
of leaves of
\mathcal{D}(P)
. Let us now count the variance of this approach, noting that
we are guaranteed that the number of leaves of
\mathcal{D}(P)
is at least
\theta

and therefore the probability of hitting a maximal execution graph along a random walk
is at least
p=\theta/M
.

Let
Y
be the random variable which counts the number of successful samples from
z
independent random walks. Since this is essentially a binomial distribution it follows that
the variance of
Y
is
zp(1-p)
. Hence, the variance of
X
is the variance of

\frac{M}{z}\cdot Y
which is
\frac{M^{2}}{z^{2}}\cdot zp(1-p)=\frac{M^{2}p(1-p)}{z}
.
Let
\sigma
be the square root of the variance of
X
.

Now, suppose we are given numbers
r,\rho
and we have to choose a
z
so that the random variable
X
is at most a factor of
r
away from the total number of leaves of
\mathcal{D}(P)
with probability at least
1-\rho
, i.e., we want to choose a
z
so that
\mathbb{P}(X\geq r\mu)
is at most
\rho
. Taking
z=\frac{1}{\rho}\cdot\frac{1}{(r-1)^{2}}\cdot\left(\frac{M}{\theta}-1\right)
, by Chebyshev’s inequality we get that

\mathbb{P}(X\geq\frac{1}{\sqrt{\rho}}\sigma+\mu)\leq\rho

Unraveling the expression
\frac{1}{\sqrt{\rho}}\sigma+\mu
with the chosen value of
z
,
we get that it is equal to
r\mu
. Hence, the probability that
X\geq r\mu
is at most
\rho
, as desired.

Note that the running time of the second act of the algorithm primarily depends only on
z
. Further note that
z=\alpha M/\theta
where
\alpha
is a constant depending only on
r,\rho
.
It follows that if we set
\theta=M^{1/2}
, then the second act of the algorithm runs in time
O(M^{1/2})
.

Wrapping up:

Overall, we see that if we set
\theta=M^{1/2}
, then
for any numbers
r,\rho
, our algorithm runs in time
O(d^{6}M^{1/2})
and returns
a value that is at most a factor of
r
away from the leaves of
\mathcal{D}(P)
with probability at least
1-\rho
. Hence, at the cost of a sub-exponential running time,
we obtain a
(r,\rho)
-approximate counter.
