High-power laser powder bed fusion of pure copper for simultaneous achievement of high density and electrical conductivity
Authors: Won-Jung Oh, Youngkyun Son, Dong-Hyun Kim, Chung-Soo Kim
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Pure copper is in high demand for energy and electronic applications due to its superior electrical and thermal conductivities. However, producing dense metals such as copper by laser powder bed fusion (L-PBF) has long been challenging due to their high infrared reflectivity and thermal conductivity, which can destabilize melting and process stability. In this study, a high-power infrared fiber laser system was employed to characterize the processing parameters, enabling high density and electrical conductivity without post-heat treatment or observable optical degradation during processing. A critical volumetric energy density of approximately 228.76 J mm−3 was identified as the threshold for stable melting, achieved at laser powers ≥ 700 W and scanning speeds ≤ 650 mm s−1. In addition, a linear energy density ≥ 1.0 J mm−1 was required to maintain consistent densification behavior. Under these optimal conditions, the fabricated parts achieved a maximum relative density of 98.9% and an electrical conductivity of up to 100.0% IACS, demonstrating performance comparable to that of wrought copper. Electron backscatter diffraction analysis revealed that electrical conductivity was influenced by both porosity and crystallographic orientation. Specifically, a higher ⟨101⟩ texture fraction and a lower grain-boundary density resulted in reduced electron scattering. These results demonstrate that high-power infrared L-PBF can reliably fabricate pure copper with industrial-level stability in terms of bulk density and conductivity, providing guidelines for the large-scale manufacturing of future electrical and thermal components.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-025-32382-3.

Introduction

Pure copper has excellent electrical (~ 58 MS m−1) and thermal (~ 400 W m−1 K−1) conductivities, making it essential in electrical, electronic, and energy systems. The demand for small, high-efficiency parts with complex geometries has fueled interest in additive manufacturing (AM) processes that can achieve near-net-shape fabrication with minimal material waste1–5.

In recent years, a range of AM processes with copper powder as feedstock—including material extrusion (MEX), binder jetting (BJT), vat photopolymerization (VPP), directed energy deposition (DED), and powder bed fusion (PBF)—have been developed and commercialized, as shown in Table 1. These processes can be classified into two groups according to their energy-input modes: indirect methods, such as MEX, BJT, and VPP, which use polymer binders followed by sintering; and beam-based methods, such as DED and PBF, which employ lasers or electron beams to melt and solidify directly5–12.

Indirect AM processes have several limitations for copper components. In MEX and BJT, after the binder is removed and sintered, the remaining porosity reduces not only density but also electrical conductivity7–9,12. Similarly, VPP is based on UV curing of metal–resin suspensions, thereby limiting achievable density and feature size6.

However, beam-based AM processes, especially laser-based PBF, generally achieve greater density and conductivity owing to the complete melting of the powder. Among these, laser powder bed fusion (L-PBF) using an infrared laser (λ ≈ 1064–1094 nm) has been extensively studied and widely adopted1,2,10,13–17. Yet copper’s high infrared reflectivity (90–98%) and therma conductivity severely restrict the energy absorption provided by conventional infrared lasers18. This lead to cause unstable melt pools, insufficient fusion between tracks, or damage to mirrors and protective windows by optical effects10,19,20. To alleviate these issues, alternative energy sources—such as green or blue lasers and electron-beam melting (EBM)—as well as geometric modifications such as reduced layer thickness and smaller laser spot size, have been investigated to increase local energy density1,21–25.

These methods yield significantly better absorptivity but their limitations are as follows: (1) green/blue lasers and EBM systems involve relatively higher initial costs and smaller build areas22,24,25, (2) finer layers and smaller spot sizes may reduce productivity and potentially reduce overall heat input per unit time, contributing to lower density and electrical conductivity in the absence of post-processes such as hot isostatic pressing10,16.

As summarized in Fig. 1, various process parameters of PBF have been investigated to fabricate pure copper components, and several recent diode-laser-based PBF cases are also included among these studies. However, when the discussion is confined to a typical laser beam diameter of 0.1 mm, most existing studies have focused on low-power levels (≤ 500 W), leaving the high-power levels largely unexplored. In particular, only a few attempts have systematically examined how wide hatch distances can be combined with high laser power to achieve a more refined energy input and mitigate keyhole-type melting.

In contrast to earlier studies reporting optical-mirror degradation at infrared powers approaching ~ 800 W due to overmelting-induced back-reflections, the present work20 employs a high-power regime (700–900 W) supported by real-time thermal monitoring, which enables selective interruption of overheating layers and prevents optical exposure to uncontrolled reflected energy. This capability allows safe and systematic exploration of the high-power parameter space to expand the process window for dense pure-copper L-PBF. The effects of laser power, scanning speed, and hatch distance on printability, densification behavior, and electrical performance were systematically analyzed, and the correlations between these parameters and the resulting properties were examined. Within the investigated L-PBF parameter space, the fabricated pure-copper specimens exhibited relative densities exceeding 98.9% ad electrical conductivities above 100.0% ICS, without post-heat treatment. These results demonstrate that optimized high-power L-PBF parameters can reliably produce high-density and high-conductivity copper components, establishing a practical framework for process control and performance prediction.

Experimental methods

Powder materials and characterization

Feedstock material (CuCP for AMCM M290 1 kW powder) was obtained from EOS. The powder showed a nearly spherical construction and a uniform distribution of particle sizes for easy layer spreading during the L-PBF process. The particle size distribution, calculated by laser diffraction, was measured as D10 = 12.0 μm, D50 = 27.7 μm, and D90 = 49.2 μm. Figure 2 provides a representative powder morphology and distribution curve, where a typical SEM micrograph of the pure copper powder shows a typical inset of well-rounded particles with clean surfaces. The powder was used in the as-received condition without any surface treatment or preheating prior to fabrication. Such morphological uniformity and small size ranges enabled a stable flow of powders and constant powder-bed formation throughout processing.

L-PBF processing conditions

Pure copper specimens were fabricated by the AMCM M290 system with a 1 kW infrared laser source. The build procedure was performed under an inert-gas (argon, 99.9% purity) atmosphere to prevent oxidation during laser exposure. The process parameters—mainly the laser power, scanning speed, hatch distance, and layer thickness—were systematically adjusted to provide the highest relative density and electrical conductivity to the generated parts.

To allow a systematic comparison between the combined effects of these parameters, the energy input was expressed via both volumetric energy density (VED) and linear energy density (LED). The VED is the amount of energy provided per unit volume of material, and the LED is the amount supplied per unit scan length, with a main effect on melt-pool behavior along the laser track. The design of experiments (DOE) for specimen fabrication is structured to ensure that all process parameters generate diverse data, as shown in Table 2. The ‘Basic’ test for pure copper printability was performed with 8 × 8 × 8 mm3 cubic samples. The ‘Basic’ test considered three parameters: laser power (400, 500, 600, 700, 800, and 900 W), scanning speed (450, 550, 650, 750, 850, and 950 mm s−1), and hatch distance (0.13, 0.15, and 0.17 mm).

Subsequently, three parameter-based experiments were designed to investigate the effects of process conditions on porosity, relative density, and electrical conductivity. To carry out these tests, cylindrical specimens with a diameter of 15.5 mm and a height of 2 mm were fabricated to provide sufficient area for both density and conductivity measurements. Experiment ‘A’ involved combinations of laser power (700, 800, and 900 W), scanning speed (600, 650, 700, 750, and 800 mm s−1), and hatch distance (0.18, 0.21, and 0.24 mm). Experiment ‘B’ was developed using the Latin hypercube sampling method to generate random parameter sets within the same range as Experiment ‘A’, thereby improving data diversity. The scanning technique employed a bidirectional stripe pattern with skywriting on both turning points (Fig. 3(left)). A 12 mm stripe width and a 0.09 mm hatch distance between melt tracks were kept for proper overlap to assure consistent thermal distribution over the build area. The stripe direction of successive layers was rotated by 67° to mitigate directional bias and reduce thermal accumulation. The next layer of copper powder was spread over the previous layer and selectively melted by the laser beam, forming a continuous melt pool as depicted schematically in Fig. 3(right).

All specimens were fabricated in the as-built condition without any post-heat treatment or hot-isostatic pressing. For electrical conductivity measurements, the sample surfaces were polished to an average roughness of Ra = 0.28 μm to ensure measurement repeatability and minimize surface-related resistance effects35. The processing parameters described above were selected to achieve high relative density and superior electrical conductivity in pure copper fabricated by L-PBF.

Relative density and electrical conductivity measurement

The relative density of the fabricated samples was determined with the Archimedes method following ASTM B962. They were weighed in air and in deionized water. The temperature of the deionized water was determined for each measurement and corrected for a temperature-dependent variation in its specific gravity. For each process condition, three specimens were measured to ensure reproducibility, and the averaged values were obtained. The Archimedes results were validated through optical microscopy of polished cross-sections to confirm the presence or absence of internal porosity or lack-of-fusion defects.

The electrical conductivity of the as-built specimens was measured using an eddy-current instrument (COM-20, PCE instruments, Germany) in accordance with ASTM E1004. Prior to measurement, the specimen surface was polished to an average roughness of Ra = 0.28 μm to minimize surface resistance effects and ensure measurement repeatability. Electrical conductivity values were expressed as percentages of the International annealed copper standard (% IACS). For each specimen, three measurements were taken at different surface locations, and the mean value was recorded.

Microstructural observation

Microstructural observation was carried out to examine the melt-pool morphology, pore distribution, and grain structure of the fabricated specimens.

Samples were sectioned perpendicular to the build direction, corresponding to planes parallel to the deposited layers, and were taken from the central region of the parts. Each specimen was mounted, ground sequentially with abrasive papers up to 4000 grit, and mechanically polished using a 1 μm diamond suspension to obtain a mirror-like surface. To reveal the grain boundaries and melt-pool structure, the polished samples were chemically etched in a distilled-water–nitric-acid solution (1:1 by volume) for 40–60 s. The etched cross-sections were examined using optical microscopy to evaluate pore morphology. For crystallographic analysis, electron backscatter diffraction (EBSD) was performed only on representative samples that exhibited meaningful differences in relative density or electrical conductivity. The EBSD data were used to identify grain orientation, morphology, and texture evolution under the corresponding process conditions. All microstructural observations were conducted on as-built specimens without any post-heat treatment, in order to directly assess the influence of the L-PBF process parameters on solidification behavior and microstructural anisotropy.

Result and discussion

Printability evaluation

In the basic experiment, cubic samples were fabricated to evaluate the printability of pure copper using an infrared L-PBF system. The powder bed was stacked to a height of 12 mm, and additive manufacturing was carried out until a build height of 8 mm was reached. During fabrication, a real-time monitoring system automatically terminated the process when overheating or geometric distortion was detected, in order to prevent total build failure and equipment damage.

Among the 108 experimental builds, 19 depositions were automatically interrupted due to overmelt-induced collapse, as indicated by the red marks in Fig. 4. The termination typically occurred around layer 36, corresponding to approximately 1480 μm above the build plate, where the molten pool became unstable and the upper layers lost mechanical support.

The overmelting behavior observed in the basic experiment is consistent with previous studies on infrared L-PBF of copper, which have reported that excessive volumetric energy input drives the melt pool from conduction mode into an unstable keyhole regime19,20. Due to copper’s high infrared reflectivity and high thermal conductivity, localized heat accumulation intensifies once keyholing initiates, resulting in deep vapor depressions, irregular melt-pool walls, and pore entrapment. As the melt pool deepens through successive layers, the geometrical support weakens, and the part collapses, triggering early build termination. The recurring collapse height observed in this study suggests that the instability is driven by cumulative thermal buildup rather than a single-layer defect.

The maximum VED of a successfully printed specimen (Basic 91) was 228.76 J mm−3, while the minimum VED of an overmelted specimen (Basic 34) was 230.77 J mm−3, as shown in Fig. 5. Therefore, this study proposes that L-PBF conditions with a VED of ≤ 228.76 J mm−3 are suitable for the stable fabrication of pure copper using infrared laser powder bed fusion.

Process window

Deposition with a fully dense structure is essential for the reliable industrial application of parts manufactured by L-PBF. Therefore, experiments and analyses were conducted to establish a process window as a function of laser power, scanning speed, and hatch distance. To clearly visualize the process stability, three classes were defined based on the relative density of the fabricated samples, as shown in Fig. 6: (1) Fully dense (relative density ≥ 98%), (2) Low dense (relative density < 98%), and (3) Overmelt (process termination due to instability).

Fig. 6Classification for process window. As shown in Fig. 7, fully dense samples were obtained only when the laser power was ≥ 700 W and the scanning speed was ≤ 650 mm s−1, consistent with the printability threshold identified in “Printability evaluation” section . This trend is consistent with previous findings showing that copper’s high reflectivity and thermal conductivity require sufficiently high energy input to achieve stable melt-pool formation and complete inter-track fusion10. Furthermore, the same study reported that excessive energy input can drive the melt pool into an unstable keyhole regime, producing vapor-depression growth, pore entrainment, and ultimately part collapse.

Relative density

In the previous section, it was demonstrated that fully dense samples can be fabricated within an appropriate range of laser power and scanning speed, while the hatch distance primarily influenced the onset of overmelting rather than densification itself. Although VED is widely used to evaluate volumetric melting behavior, our results showed that one of its constituent variables—hatch distance—did not significantly affect density within the investigated range (0.13–0.24 mm). Therefore, hatch distance mainly governed overmelting rather than contributing directly to relative density. After excluding the fixed layer thickness and the hatch distance with limited influence on densification, the remaining effective variables were laser power and scanning speed, which together define the LED. Consequently, the relative density is observed to correlate mainly with laser power and scanning speed, and this relationship can be expressed in terms of the LED defined by Eq. (2), which represents the ratio of laser power to scanning speed.

The relative density of all specimens produced across Experiments Basic, A, B, and C was analyzed as a function of LED, as shown in Fig. 9. The relative density exhibited a logarithmic increase with increasing LED, approaching a plateau at high energy input. When the LED was lower than approximately 1.0 J mm−1, the measured density showed large deviations due to incomplete melting and pore formation, as also observed in the “Low Dense” region in Fig. 9. Conversely, at higher LED values, the deviation of relative density progressively decreased, indicating stable melt-pool formation and uniform layer bonding.

Fig. 9Relative density of specimens; a Regression of relative density according to linear energy density, b Relative density results for each hatch distance.

Relative density of specimens; a Regression of relative density according to linear energy density, b Relative density results for each hatch distance.

As shown in Fig. 9b, the variation of hatch distance did not significantly affect the overall densification trend. This result confirms that, within the studied range (0.13–0.24 mm), the energy overlap between adjacent scan tracks remained sufficient to achieve near-complete fusion.

The dependence of density on both LED and VED was further examined through Experiment C, where the volumetric energy density was fixed at 164.8 J mm−3 to isolate the effect of local line energy. The results, summarized in Fig. 10, show that at a relatively low LED (~ 1.19 J mm−1), the relative density decreased as the laser power increased, even under constant volumetric energy input. This phenomenon is attributed to the larger variation in scanning speed (Δv ≈ 169 mm s−1) required to maintain a fixed VED, which in turn caused fluctuations in bead width and localized pore formation.

On the other hand, at a relatively high LED (~ 1.58 J mm−1), the variation in scanning speed was minor, and the relative density remained nearly constant regardless of laser power. These findings indicate that a sufficiently high LED is essential to stabilize melt-pool geometry and minimize density deviation, even when VED is fixed. Therefore, this study proposes LED ≥ 1.0 J mm−1 as the threshold for achieving dense, dimensionally stable copper parts in infrared L-PBF.

Electrical conductivity

Electrical conductivity is one of the most sensitive indicators of microstructural integrity in additively manufactured pure copper, as it is strongly affected by porosity, grain boundary density, impurity segregation, and crystallographic orientation. Samples for electrical conductivity measurement were fabricated considering the 14-mm probe contact area of the eddy-current testing system, as shown in Fig. 11a. The as-built surface exhibited an average roughness of Ra ≈ 8.27 μm (as-built), which can introduce measurement deviations of 10–30% in measured conductivity depending on surface condition. Therefore, as described in “Relative density and electrical conductivity measurement” section, all specimens were polished to a surface roughness below Ra = 1 μm prior to measurement, as illustrated in Fig. 11b.

Specimens fabricated by experiments A, B and C; a after print, b after polishing surface.

The measured electrical conductivity values for all specimens are summarized in Fig. 12. A linear relationship was observed between electrical conductivity, relative density, and linear energy density (LED). This behavior is consistent with prior studies on L-PBF copper, which have shown that residual porosity acts as a dominant electron-scattering source, reducing electrical performance even when pore fractions are below 2%10,17,36. In medium-power infrared systems, insufficient energy absorption typically limits relative density to 80–90%, yielding conductivities of only 50–70% IACS due to lack-of-fusion defects and irregular melt tracks10,36,37. Conversely, when porosity is minimized through higher energy input or enhanced absorptivity, the conductivity of L-PBF copper can approach 90–96% IACS10,29,38.

Fig. 12Electrical conductivity according to linear energy density. The inset shows an optical microscope image of specimen section.

Electrical conductivity according to linear energy density. The inset shows an optical microscope image of specimen section.

As illustrated in Fig. 12, the increase in electrical conductivity follows a nearly linear trend with both LED and relative density. Specimens fabricated in the low-LED regime (< 1.0 J mm−1) like specimen A15 exhibit pronounced lack-of-fusion pores and irregular melt tracks, which lead to substantially reduced conductivity despite similar nominal energy inputs. In contrast, specimens produced above the critical LED threshold (specimen A16) demonstrate sharply reduced pore volume and a dense, uniform microstructure, resulting in conductivity values approaching 100% IACS. The convergence of conductivity toward bulk-Cu levels above ~ 98.5% relative density confirms that pore elimination is the primary factor governing electrical performance in L-PBF copper.

The dependence of conductivity on energy input can be explained through the concept of a critical energy-density threshold required for complete melting. Previous literature has shown that, in L-PBF copper, stable melt-pool formation and full densification occur only when the applied volumetric energy density (VED) exceeds a process-specific minimum10,37. Electron-beam PBF research similarly reported that near-zero porosity and bulk-like conductivity are obtained only when the beam energy surpasses the critical melting regime39,40. Our results are fully consistent with these observations. Conductivity increased monotonically with LED relative density. When LED was below 1.0 J mm−1, insufficient local energy input caused incomplete fusion and irregular porosity, leading to degraded electrical performance. Once LED exceeded 1.0 J mm−1, corresponding to VED conditions above the printability threshold, the conductivity rapidly converged toward 99–100% IACS.

Although densification is the primary factor governing conductivity, variations among samples with similar densities indicate additional microstructural contributions. EBSD analysis revealed that specimens produced under higher LED conditions exhibited larger grains and lower grain-boundary density. Since grain boundaries acts as electron-scattering centers, a greater mean linear intercept (MLI) reduces scattering probability and thereby enhances conductivity. To further investigate these influences, EBSD analysis was conducted on representative specimens (A2, A15, and A33), as shown in Fig. 13, indicating that grain coarsening contributes positively to electrical conductivity once densification is achieved. The inverse-pole-figure (IPF) maps revealed that electrical conductivity is affected not only by porosity but also by grain morphology and MLI. With increasing LED, both grain size and MLI increased, leading to improved conductivity due to reduced electron scattering at grain boundaries. High-LED specimens display larger equiaxed grains and reduced grain-boundary density, lowering the number of electron-scattering sites. The difference between specimens A33 and A02, both exhibiting similar densities but different grain sizes, shows that grain-boundary reduction can account for conductivity improvements of 0.5–1.5% IACS within the high-density regime.

Electrical conductivity exhibited noticeable orientation-dependent behavior. Copper has a face-centered cubic (FCC) lattice with anisotropic electron mobility along <001>, <101>, and <111> directions. Previous studies have shown that the < 101 > orientation provides favorable pathways for electron transport due to its lower effective electron mass and reduced electron-phonon scattering probability compared with other crystallographic directions41,42. This arises from the anisotropic curvature of the Fermi surface in FCC metals, where electron group velocities are directionally dependent and the <101> direction offers higher band dispersion and enhanced carrier mobility.

Figure 14 confirms that crystallographic texture plays an additional role in determining conductivity. Even among specimens with comparable relative density and grain size, those a higher <101> fraction exhibit noticeably improved conductivity. The progressive conductivity increase from A29 (35.58% <101>) → A31 (56.79% <101>) → A16 (73.40% <101>) demonstrates that the evolution of <101>-oriented grains, driven by directional thermal gradients and melt-pool solidification, enhances electron transport efficiency. These results are consistent with reports that copper films and drawn wires with higher <101> alignment exhibit reduced electrical resistivity due to minimized scattering at both grain boundaries and within the crystal lattice41,42.

Collectively, these findings indicate that crystallographic texture constitutes a third-level mechanism improving electrical conductivity in L-PBF Cu, complementing the dominant effect of porosity elimination and the secondary effect of grain coarsening.

Conclusion

In this study, high-power infrared L-PBF was then used to fabricate dense, electrically conductive components of pure copper without any visible optical degradation or instability throughout processing. Suppose the infrared system is well-tuned by optimizing itself. In that case, the results demonstrate that high-reflectivity materials can be processed stably, and the integrity of the process in infrared-based systems is maintained. By methodical parameter study, this work determined quantitative process–structure–property relationships for infrared L-PBF of pure copper:
