Effects of E-cigarette and tobacco use on heart rate variability: a case-control study
Authors: Volkan Çamkıran, Batool Achmar, Ahmad Achmar, İlhan Kutlu, Emel Kurul, Özlem Unay Demirel, Hüseyin Tunç, Emrah İpek
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
This study aims to examine heart rate variability (HRV) parameters, hemodynamic parameters, and serum cotinine levels difference across the following groups: tobacco smokers, e-cigarette users and non-smokers. This study explicitly examined healthy people. Ninety healthy volunteers, aged between 18 and 45, were enrolled in the study. Participants were divided into three groups of thirty each: e-cigarette users, tobacco smokers and non-smokers. HRV data were examined in both the frequency and temporal domains. Additionally, statistical analyses were performed in Python 3.8 using the scikit-learn, pandas, and SciPy packages. Tobacco smokers had a significantly longer smoking history than e-cigarette users (p = 0.027). The baseline systolic blood pressure (SBP) and mean blood pressure (MBP) of the groups varied considerably (p = 0.037 and p = 0.021, respectively), with the non-smoker group exhibiting significantly higher SBP and MBP. For diastolic blood pressure (DBP), the change was borderline (p = 0.073) but nearly significant. All HRV variables displayed standard deviation of normal-to-normal intervals (SDNN): -0.73 ms, root mean square of successive differences (RMSSD): -0.86 ms, proportion of consecutive RR intervals that is different by more than 50 ms (pNN50): -0.60% year and triangular interpolation of normal-to-normal intervals (TINN): -4.07 ms annually as age increased (all p ≤ 0.01). No statistically significant differences in cotinine levels or HRV indices were observed among tobacco smokers, e-cigarette users and non-smokers in this exploratory study. The long-term effects of nicotine products require more research, including longitudinal designs, higher cohort sizes and other physiological and biochemical inflammatory indicators, such as endothelial function markers and cytokines.

Introduction

Smoking is a well-established public health problem leading to disabilities and death throughout the globe. It has a crucial role in major preventable diseases including atherosclerotic cardiovascular disease, stroke and cancer. Despite increased awareness regarding its harms, it continues to contribute to impaired health status globally1. In recent years, the prevalence of smoking tends to decrease worldwide however a certain period is needed to observe the health benefits of smoking cessation especially in chronic disorders2. Nevertheless, electronic cigarettes (EC) have been gaining popularity, particularly among younger individuals3,4. There are several reports and ongoing research that EC can be as harmful as tobacco cigarettes (TC) despite previous conflicting results from some studies5,6. While electronic cigarettes are regularly used to help people quit smoking, research shows that they are frequently used in conjunction with other tobacco products3. Besides data regarding acute adverse effects of ECs, several pathophysiologic mechanisms, including oxidative stress, inflammation, and autonomic nervous system dysregulation, have been linked to the adverse cardiovascular effects associated with smoking5. Heart rate variability (HRV), as a non-invasive applicable tool for the diagnosis of autonomic dysfunction, measures the variation of consecutive R-R intervals on electrocardiogram7. HRV indicates the balance between sympathetic and parasympathetic tones and is accepted as a biomarker for general health status. For an optimally functioning autonomic nervous system (ANS), higher HRV is needed which shows an increased ability of the heart to effectively adapt to prompt ANS changes7,8. In this case-control study, we aimed to compare HRV parameters as indicators of autonomic nervous system function among tobacco smokers, e-cigarette users, and healthy non-smokers.

Materials and methods

Study population and design

A total of 90 healthy volunteers (32 males, 58 females) between the ages of 18–45 were included in the study. They were recruited for the study by face-to-face interviews or online surveys. Participants were recruited through convenience sampling from the survey/interview dataset including the university students and hospital employees who met the inclusion criteria. Thirty individuals per group (non-smokers, tobacco cigarette smokers and e-cigarette users) were included to ensure equal group sizes for comparison. The demographic and lifestyle information of the individuals were collected at the beginning of the study. This basic information included the age, height in meters, weight in kilograms, smoking status including duration (years) and daily number of tobacco and/or electronic cigarettes, brand names, nicotine concentration and aroma preferences. The data regarding health status includes the presence of any chronic illness, pregnancy, medications including vitamin supplements and nicotine replacement therapy, alcohol use and drug abuse. Body mass index (BMI) was calculated by the formula: weight in kilograms/square of height in meters. Obese and underweight individuals (BMI equals or higher than 30 kg/m² and equals or lower than 18 kg/m², respectively), pregnant women, individuals with any chronic or acute illness including asthma, hypertension, heart disease, diabetes, hyperlipidemia, chronic kidney disease, and active infection or cancer, the ones who consume illicit drugs and 2 or more alcoholic drinks/day, prescription drug users (except oral contraceptives) including licensed nicotine replacement therapies, and athletes were excluded. The healthy individuals fulfilling the study criteria were divided into 3 groups. The first group was explicit TC smokers for at least 6 months. The second group was explicit electronic cigarette smokers for at least 6 months. The third group was “control group” including non-smokers that are not exposed to cigarette smoke for more than 2 h daily for at least 6 months. Participants reporting concurrent use of both tobacco cigarettes and e-cigarettes were not included in the study. Each study group included 30 self-identified e-cigarette smokers, 30 self-identified TC smokers and 30 self-identified non-smoker control group. To reveal the chronic or non-acute effects and to prevent interference of acute withdrawal of electronic or TC, subjects were asked to refrain from e-cigarettes and smoking tobacco for twelve hours prior to the study. Alcohol, caffeine and nicotine intake are also restricted. The systolic and diastolic blood pressures were measured in both arms by a single study nurse by using the one calibrated sphygmomanometer and the greater arm values were recorded for everyone. In addition, measurements were obtained between 9:00 am and 2:00 pm to minimize the influence of circadian variation. The blood samples were taken to measure serum cotinine levels to show tobacco exposure in the study population. Serum cotinine was measured as an objective biomarker of recent nicotine exposure but was not used as the primary criterion for participant classification because it reflects recent exposure rather than long-term smoking behavior.

Blood Test

Heparinized tubes were used to collect blood samples from the participants, which were carefully done by a professional nurse in the study hospital. The blood samples were drawn 10 min after the HRV measurements. The left antecubital vein was used to collect blood in all patients. The blood sample was centrifuged, and the separated serum was stored at -40° C for cotinine analysis. Thermo Scientific DRI Cotinine Assay was utilized to measure cotinine levels by an analyzer (ARCHITECT c16000 clinical chemistry analyzer; Abbott Laboratories, Abbott Park, IL, USA).

HRV measurements

The HRV measurements were performed in the supine position in a quiet hospital room with a controlled temperature of 21 degrees Celsius (C). During measurements, no mobile phones or other digital devices were permitted, and unnecessary speech was prohibited during data collection. The HRV test was carried out after 15 min of rest in the supine position. To avoid the influence of circadian rhythm on autonomic tone, participants were studied between 9 a.m. and 2 p.m. In addition, HRV was measured with the Polar H10 heart rate sensor (Polar Electro Oy, Kempele, Finland), which has been validated against standard ECG for RR interval acquisition and has shown excellent agreement for resting HRV values in healthy individuals. Although ECG remains the gold standard for HRV measurement, previous studies have demonstrated that the Polar H10 provides reliable RR interval recordings suitable for short-term HRV analysis under resting conditions9,10. The participants wore the chest strap sensor during the entire 7-minute recording session. The Polar H10 was linked via Bluetooth to the Kubios HRV mobile application (Kubios Oy, Kuopio, Finland), which enabled data collection in real time. The beat-to-beat R-R interval was recorded using the Kubios application which subsequently provided accurate HRV readings. After completing the recording, the data was exported in an appropriate format to undertake further studies by the Kubios HRV software. This approach is in accordance with standard methods of HRV data collection and analysis as provided in the Kubios HRV Scientific User’s Guide11. The time and frequency domain HRV parameters were analyzed in this study. The time-domain HRV parameters including the standard deviation of normal-to-normal intervals (SDNN) (shows the overall variation within the RR interval series), the root mean square of successive differences (RMSSD), and the proportion of consecutive RR intervals that is different by more than 50 ms (pNN50) (shows parasympathetic cardiac modulation) and triangular interpolation of normal-to-normal intervals (TINN) (This is baseline width of the distribution that approximates the normal to normal (NN) interval distribution.)12,13. The frequency domain analysis which converts signals into frequency bands by fast Fourier transform (FFT) is represented by very low frequency (VLF) (0.003–0.04 Hz) (represents sympatho-vagal balance)14, low frequency (LF) (0.04–0.15 Hz) (shows both sympathetic and vagal tone) and high frequency (HF) (0.15–0.40 Hz) (shows parasympathetic modulation)12. The LF/HF ratio indicates a balance between sympathetic and parasympathetic tone. A low LF/HF ratio means parasympathetic, but a high LF/HF ratio shows sympathetic dominance7,11,12. Total power (TOTpow_FFT) shows the total variance in heart rate pattern during recording and is reported as an average of every 5 min15.

The study was approved by Istinye University Faculty of Medicine Ethics Committee with the protocol number: 24–169, and written informed consent was obtained from each study participant. All methods were performed in accordance with the relevant guidelines and regulations.

Statistical analyses

Statistical analyses were performed in Python 3.8 using the scikit-learn, pandas and SciPy packages. Continuous variables are summarized as median/ interquartile range (IQR), mean ± standard deviation and categorical variables as counts and percentages. For comparison of categorical variables across groups and any expected cell count < 5, then Fisher’s exact test is used. To compare non-smokers, tobacco smokers and e-cigarette users, we first assessed normality with the Shapiro–Wilk test. The non-normal variables were compared using the Kruskal–Wallis test (with Dunn’s multiple comparisons). One-way ANOVA (Analysis of Variance) was used to compare the means of the groups. Multivariate linear regression analysis was performed to search for the association between smoking status, age, sex and BMI with heart-rate variability indices and serum cotinine levels. Beta and 95% confidence intervals were utilized to search for the relationship between determinants and smoking groups. All regression models were adjusted for the same covariate set (smoking status, age, sex and BMI); given the exploratory nature of the study, no formal correction for multiple comparisons was applied across models, and individual associations should be interpreted with caution. A critical p-value of 0.05 was chosen, and contingency tables yielding p < 0.05 were considered to indicate statistically significant differences between study groups. Prior to the study, we estimated the sample size required for the primary comparison (one-way ANOVA across three groups; α = 0.05, power = 0.80). Detecting a conventional medium effect (Cohen’s f = 0.25; η² ≈ 0.06) would have required approximately 53 participants per group (total N ≈ 159), whereas a medium-to-large effect (f = 0.33; η² ≈ 0.10) would have required 31 participants per group (total N ≈ 93). Owing to the strict eligibility criteria (healthy volunteers aged 18–45, free of chronic disease and confounding medication or lifestyle factors) and the limited pool of eligible individuals reachable through convenience sampling, the target of ~ 53 participants per group could not be attained within the study period. The sample was therefore capped at 30 participants per group (N = 90), balanced across the three groups to preserve comparability.

To contextualize the statistical power of the achieved sample, we performed a sensitivity analysis. With n = 30 per group (N = 90), the study had 80% power to detect an effect size of Cohen’s f = 0.33 (η² ≈ 0.10), corresponding to a medium-to-large effect. In pairwise terms, this corresponds to detectable between-group differences of roughly 12 ms for SDNN and 15 ms for RMSSD. The study was therefore likely underpowered to detect small-to-moderate but potentially clinically relevant differences in HRV parameters. Accordingly, the findings should be interpreted as exploratory, and the absence of statistically significant differences should not be interpreted as evidence of equivalence between groups.

Results

This dataset comprises 90 participants, divided into three equal groups of 30 each: non-smokers (N), tobacco cigarette smokers (T), and electronic cigarette users (E). It includes 114 variables in total. The demographic continuous variables are gender, age, height, weight, BMI and smoking duration. There were 58 females (64%), and 32 (36%) males in the total study group. There was no significant difference between study groups in terms of basic demographic characteristics. In our data, tobacco smokers reported a median smoking duration of 5.0 [3.0–9.5] years, while e-cigarette users had a shorter history averaging 3.0 [1.8–4.5] years; non-smokers, by definition, had no prior smoking exposure. Smoking duration was significantly higher in tobacco smokers than the e-cigarette users (p = 0.027). Table 1 demonstrates the key demographic characteristics. There were no statistically significant differences in the median [IQR] values of age, height, weight, and BMI among the three groups (p-values ranging from 0.351 to 0.584). Similarly, the distribution of sex was balanced across groups (p = 0.712). However, the duration of prior use differed significantly between the “Smoker” and “E-cigarette” groups (p = 0.027), indicating distinct levels of exposure across these groups. Statistically significant differences between groups were observed for baseline SBP and MBP (p = 0.037 and p = 0.021, respectively) with significantly higher SBP and MBP values in the non-smoker group. For DBP, the difference approached significance however remained borderline with p = 0.073. Serum cotinine levels showed no statistically significant difference among groups (p = 0.118). In summary, while demographic and basic anthropometric characteristics were balanced across groups, some differences were observed in smoking exposure and certain hemodynamic parameters. Regarding e-cigarette exposure characteristics, 23 of 30 e-cigarette users (77%) used nicotine-containing products (one used a nicotine-free product; data missing for six). Users reported a range of commercially available e-cigarette devices and brands, and reported flavors were broadly distributed between fruit/sweet (n = 12) and mint/menthol (n = 11) categories. Notably, 20 of 30 e-cigarette users (67%) reported a prior history of conventional tobacco smoking, with a median prior smoking duration of 10.5 [5.2–15.0] years, indicating substantial former use within this group. In the comparison of median heart rate (HR) and HRV parameters by smoking status, there was not any significant difference between study groups. Table 2 summarizes the findings regarding HR and HRV parameters by smoking status. The median [IQR] values of both time-domain (Mean HR, Mean RR, SDNN, RMSSD, pNN50, TINN) and frequency-domain (VLF, LF, HF power, LF/HF ratio, total power) parameters did not differ significantly across the three groups. All Kruskal–Wallis p-values ranged between 0.387 and 0.977, remaining well above the threshold for statistical significance (p < 0.05). These findings indicate that, regardless of smoking status (non-smoker, smoker, e-cigarette user), there is no statistically significant difference in ANS indicators under resting conditions among groups (Table 2). However, given the exploratory design and limited sample size, the lack of statistical significance should not be interpreted as evidence of equivalence between groups. In the multivariate model, a significant decrease was observed in all HRV indices with increasing age (SDNN: −0.73 ms per year; RMSSD: −0.86 ms per year; pNN50: −0.60% per year; TINN: −4.07 ms per year; all p ≤ 0.01). However, no statistically significant differences were found in HRV indices or serum cotinine levels between tobacco cigarette smokers, e-cigarette users, and non-smokers (all p > 0.2). The effect of sex was significant particularly for SDNN (+ 7.02 ms; p = 0.049) and TINN (+ 53.67 ms; p = 0.044), with higher values observed in males for these two indices. In the cotinine model, serum cotinine levels were not significantly associated with sex (β = −8.53; p = 0.176), while BMI showed a borderline inverse association with cotinine levels (β = −1.81; p = 0.051) (Table 3).

Discussion

This study investigated the effects of smoking status—specifically tobacco smoking, EC use, and non-smoking—on HRV, hemodynamic parameters, and serum cotinine levels in a sample of healthy adults. The findings provide several insights into autonomic nervous system function, assessed by HRV, as well as hemodynamic characteristics associated with smoking and vaping, as well as the potential influence of demographic variables such as age and sex. The findings of the study demonstrated some interesting and controversial results regarding autonomic responses among EC users, tobacco smokers and non-smokers. Herein we aimed to show the probable chronic effects of several smoking modalities on HRV after 12 h of cessation. Despite clear differences in smoking duration between tobacco cigarette smokers and e-cigarette users, no significant differences were found in key demographic and anthropometric characteristics among the three groups, including age, sex distribution, height, weight, and BMI. This balanced distribution strengthens the internal validity of the study and supports a more accurate attribution of observed physiological differences to smoking status rather than confounding variables.

Interestingly, the non-smoker group exhibited significantly higher systolic blood pressure (SBP) and mean blood pressure (MBP) values compared to the other groups. Although diastolic blood pressure (DBP) did not reach statistical significance, the trend toward higher values in non-smokers necessitates further investigation. In the previous literature, it was reported that acute elevation in blood pressure was common in smokers due to nicotine-induced sympathetic activation and vasoconstriction16. One possible explanation is that acute nicotine tolerance in habitual smokers may lead to blunted hemodynamic responses at rest, whereas non-smokers maintain a baseline sympathetic tone that appears comparatively higher17. Another explanation is the study measurements may not reflect the usual blood pressures of the individuals including smokers18. In the study by Primatesta P. et al., they did not find any independent differences of clinical significance in BP values between smokers and non-smokers. They reported that BP differences associated with smoking differed with age and between men and women and underlined the confounding effects of BMI and alcohol intake18. Although parameters such as test scheduling, 12 h of abstinence from smoking, coffee, and alcohol, and the same qualified nurse doing the measurement for all individuals were used in our study. Psychological stress, habitual physical activity, food habits, and sleep quality are among the remaining confounding factors that may contribute to the observed discrepancy.

Serum cotinine levels—a biomarker of nicotine exposure—were not significantly different among groups, which may reflect variability in individual metabolism or timing of last nicotine use19. In the multivariable model, serum cotinine levels were not significantly associated with sex (β = −8.53; p = 0.176). By contrast, BMI showed a borderline inverse association with cotinine levels (β = −1.81; p = 0.051). This finding may be compatible with the pharmacokinetic behavior of cotinine, as circulating concentrations can be influenced by distribution volume and clearance. Therefore, differences in body size or BMI may partly contribute to variability in serum cotinine concentrations for a comparable level of nicotine exposure20. However, interestingly, higher levels of cotinine in non-smokers may reflect second-hand smoke exposure, undisclosed nicotine use, or inaccuracies in self-reported smoking status19,21. We acknowledge that the overlap in serum cotinine concentrations across the study groups raises the possibility of exposure misclassification, which may have reduced our ability to detect true differences in HRV between the smoking groups.

The absence of significant differences in HRV parameters across smoking groups suggests that under resting conditions, ANS function—as assessed through both time-domain (e.g., SDNN, RMSSD, pNN50) and frequency-domain (e.g., LF, HF, LF/HF ratio) indices— was not significantly altered by either tobacco or electronic cigarette use. This is consistent with some previous reports indicating that habitual smokers may develop adaptive mechanisms that mask autonomic dysregulation at rest, with changes becoming more apparent under stress or during recovery phases22–24. The absence of statistically significant HRV differences relative to non-smokers may partly reflect the resting measurement condition, the 12-hour abstinence period, exposure heterogeneity, or limited statistical power. Given the cross-sectional design, no causal conclusion can be drawn regarding the effect of short-term abstinence from TC or EC on autonomic balance. Our multivariate analysis revealed an inverse association between age and HRV indices, confirming well-documented age-related declines in autonomic flexibility25,26. Each year of increased age was associated with significant reductions in SDNN, RMSSD, pNN50, and TINN, underscoring age as a major determinant of cardiac autonomic tone. Sex differences were also evident, with males demonstrating higher values in SDNN and TINN. These findings are consistent with prior studies showing that males may exhibit greater overall HRV, potentially due to differences in parasympathetic and sympathetic balance or cardiovascular conditioning27,28. The higher HRV indices in males may reflect a more adaptive autonomic profile, although the clinical implications of these differences remain to be clarified27.

Although this study did not find significant differences in HRV across smoking groups and non-smokers, the results do not preclude the potential for long-term autonomic dysfunction related to chronic smoking29. The relatively short smoking duration among e-cigarette users (median 3.0 years) compared to tobacco smokers (median 5.0 years) could partially account for the lack of observed differences. It is possible that longer exposure durations or dynamic assessments (e.g., during exercise or postural changes) may reveal more pronounced group disparities29. Additionally, the elevated BP in non-smokers observed in this cohort highlights the complexity of cardiovascular regulation and suggests the need for further research into baseline autonomic tone and environmental or psychological stressors that may influence these parameters in non-smokers.

Study limitations

There are several limitations in our study. Being a single center study with relatively lower number of patients is the major limitation of this study. Time to HRV analysis may have some effect on the results that was determined from previous studies. The relatively shorter period of EC use of the patients is another limitation. In addition, e-cigarette exposure was characterized only partially. Although device brand, nicotine content (presence/absence) and flavor category were recorded, the nicotine concentration (mg/mL) and puffing behavior (e.g., puffs per day) were not systematically quantified (available for only 5 of 30 users) and therefore could not be included as covariates. Moreover, two thirds of e-cigarette users had a prior history of tobacco smoking; this heterogeneity in product use and prior use may have influenced autonomic responses and limits the specificity of comparisons between exposure groups. Furthermore, cotinine levels in the blood were not significantly different across the groups, which is misleading in the context of our study because it implicates that a non-smokers may have the same cotinine level as a tobacco and EC smoker. However, we would like to underline that the groups were appropriately classified based on face-to-face interviews or online questionnaires completed by participants. As for the cotinine biomarker, we want to emphasize that it is an acute biomarker that indicates the participants’ exposure within the recent 1–3 days, thus its elevation might be caused by passive exposure or unnoticed purposeful smoke, which is a significant drawback in this study. Therefore, exposure misclassification cannot be excluded and may have biased the comparisons toward the null, potentially masking true differences in HRV between the study groups.

Conclusion

There were no significant differences in resting HRV parameters between tobacco cigarette smokers, e-cigarette users, and nonsmokers in this investigation. These findings, however, should be interpreted with caution due to the small sample size, cross-sectional design, and potential residual confounding factors. Larger longitudinal studies are required to better understand the long-term autonomic and cardiovascular effects of smoking and e-cigarette usage.
