Whole-genome analysis reveals genetic diversity and selection pressure in Sichuan donkey
Authors: Cong Li, Jiale Han, Tingjin Chang, Halima Jafari, Qiwen Yang, Jiajun Guo, Chuzhao Lei, Ruihua Dang
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundSichuan donkeys are small-statured donkeys native to the plateau and mountainous regions of southwestern China. They are well-suited for transportation tasks in mountainous terrain and exhibit remarkable adaptability to the harsh environment, characterized by low temperatures and hypoxia. Adaptation to the local environment has shaped their unique genomic characteristics and is an important source of genetic variation. However, the genome-wide landscape of Sichuan donkeys remains undescribed.ResultsIn this study, we obtained whole-genome sequencing data from 17 Sichuan donkeys and combined this data with published data of 99 donkeys from 9 other donkey breeds. We aimed to elucidate the population structure, genetic diversity, genetic differentiation, and selection pressure of Sichuan donkeys at the whole-genome level. Population structure and genetic diversity analysis showed that Sichuan donkeys were less influenced by the hybridization of foreign donkey breeds. They maintained a relatively pure lineage of Chinese native donkeys and exhibited higher genetic diversity. The study also found that Sichuan donkeys were genetically closest to Tibetan and Yunnan donkeys. Although their effective population size around 1000 years ago was smaller compared to Tibetan and Yunnan donkeys, it was still larger than that of other donkey breeds. Moreover, selective signature analysis (θπ, CLR, FST, θπ-ratio) identified several candidate genes associated with hypoxia adaptation (EGLN3, RORA, CACNA2D3) and cold adaptation (NCOA2, SLC1A1, PAM, PLPPR1, and ZNF668). The most important finding is that EGLN3 shows different nucleotide diversity, Tajima’D value, and haplotype in donkeys living at different altitudes, suggesting it may be an important candidate gene for Sichuan donkeys to adapt to the cold hypoxic environment in the plateau.ConclusionThis study clarified the genetic diversity, genetic differentiation, and effective population size of Sichuan donkeys by comparing them with other donkey breeds. Our findings contribute to deeper understanding of the high-altitude adaptability of Sichuan donkeys, and provide valuable information for the conservation and breeding of the breed.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12864-025-12254-w.

Background

Donkeys were domesticated in Africa around 5000 B.C.E. and spread to Eurasia around 2500 B.C.E. [1, 2]. Since then, they have played an indispensable role in human migration, trade, and agriculture. Two donkey lineages were expanded into China through different migration events [3]. With nearly 4,000 years of donkey husbandry, China possesses one of the world’s most abundant donkey resources [4]. Various local donkey breeds are distributed across central, northeastern, and western China [5], and they represent valuable genetic resources.

Sichuan donkeys are mainly distributed in Ganzi Tibetan Autonomous Prefecture in Southwestern China's Sichuan province and are used for piggybacking and hauling cargo in remote mountainous areas [6]. As part of the Qinghai-Xizang Plateau, Ganzi Tibetan Autonomous Prefecture has an average altitude of 3,500 m and is characterized by low temperatures, hypoxia, and strong ultraviolet radiation. The harsh environmental conditions present significant challenges to survival, yet Sichuan donkeys have evolved remarkable adaptations to cope with these conditions, making them an ideal model for understanding genetic mechanisms that facilitate adaptation to low temperatures and hypoxia at high altitudes.

Previous research has explored the genetic mechanisms underlying high-altitude adaptation in several domestic animals, including chickens [7], pigs [8], yaks [9], goats [10], sheep [11], and horses [12]. Key genes involved in hypoxia response, such as HBB, EPAS1, and HIF-1α, have been identified as crucial for high-altitude adaptation in many species [13–16]. Similarly, studies on geographically similar species like kiangs and Tibetan donkeys have shown adaptation to low-oxygen environments through the EPAS1 and EGLN1 genes, respectively [17]. These findings suggest that they may have independently acquired high-altitude adaptation via distinct biological pathways. Given that high-altitude adaptation in livestock is a complex trait influenced by multiple genetic loci, understanding the genomic basis of this adaptation in Sichuan donkeys has significant implications for population genetics and environmental adaptation research.

In this study, we comprehensively elucidated the genomic characteristics and selection pressure in Sichuan donkeys using whole-genome sequencing data from 17 Sichuan donkeys. This research contributes to the conservation of the genetic resources of local donkey breeds and provides valuable insights for the breeding of high-performing livestock. By uncovering the unique genetic adaptations of Sichuan donkeys, this research aims to support the sustainable development of China's donkey industry and preserve its rich genetic diversity.

Methods

Samples and sequencing

In total, 17 blood samples from Sichuan donkeys were collected from Jiulong County, Ganzi Tibetan Autonomous Prefecture, Sichuan Province, China (Fig. 1A). Blood samples were collected from the jugular veins by veterinarians, without the use of anesthesia or euthanasia. After collection, local disinfection was performed to prevent infection. Genomic DNA was extracted from these samples using the standard phenol–chloroform protocol, and the extraction quality was verified by agarose gel electrophoresis [18, 19]. Qualified DNA samples were then sent to the Novogene Bioinformatics Institute (Beijing, China) for sequencing. The raw genome data were generated using the Illumina HiSeq 2000 platform (Table S1). Additionally, this study incorporated published whole-genome resequencing data from two wild ass breeds (Equus hemionus, abbreviated as EH, n = 8; Equus asinus africanus, abbreviated as EA, n = 8) and eight domestic donkey breeds (Australian, n = 8; Ethiopia, n = 8; Kenya, n = 10; Spanish, n = 10; and four Chinese native donkey breeds: Guanzhong, n = 10; Yunnan, n = 7; Tibetan, n = 20; Sichuan, n = 10), totaling 116 donkeys for a comprehensive analysis (Table S2).Fig. 1Population structure and relationship of 116 donkeys. Reads mapping and SNP calling

Quality control of the sequencing data was performed using Trimmomatic software [20]. The MEM module of Burrows-Wheeler Aligner (BWA) was used to align the clean reads to the Equus asinus reference assembly (EquAsi1.0) [21]. The Picard toolkit (https://github.com/broadinstitute/picard) was used to sort and remove duplicate reads. Subsequently, the GATK was utilized to call and filter single-nucleotide polymorphisms (SNPs) based on the following parameters: Qual By Depth < 2.0, Fisher Strand > 60.0, Mapping Quality < 40.0, Mapping Quality Rank Sum Test < − 12.5, Read Pos Rank Sum Test < − 8.0, Strand Odds Ratio > 3.0, and the mean sequencing depth of variants (all individuals) ‘ < 1/3 × and > 3 × ’. SNPs with minor allele frequency less than 0.05 and missing rate greater than 0.1 were also filtered out [22]. Finally, SNPEff was used to annotate the remaining SNPs [23].

Population genetic structure analyses

SNPs with high levels of pairwise linkage disequilibrium (LD) were pruned using PLINK [24], with the parameter ‘-indep-pairwise 50 5 0.2’ [25]. The filtered dataset was then used for population genetic structure analysis of 116 donkeys, including Admixture analysis, principal component analysis (PCA), and neighbor-joining (NJ) tree analysis. ADMIXTURE was used to assess the ancestral origin of each individual [26], considering 2 to 5 clusters (K). PCA was performed using the SmartPCA module in the EIGENSOFT package [27]. Next, a pairwise genetic distance matrix was constructed with PLINK, and the NJ-tree was built through MEGA [28], with the African wild ass (Equus asinus africanus) as the outgroup, and then visualized by iTOL [29].

Genetic diversity

All sites were phased using Beagle [30]. Individuals resulting from non-random mating were excluded based on Identity-By-State (IBS) information. A similar number of individuals were randomly selected from each of the other breeds (Table S3). PLINK was used to calculate the length and number of runs of homozygosity (ROH) for each individual with the following parameters: -homozyg-density 50 homozyg-window-het 1 -homozyg-window-missing 2. VCFtools was used to calculate the total length of ROH (LROH) in the genome [31]. The genomic inbreeding coefficient based on Runs of Homozygosity (FROH) was estimated for each individual using the formula: FROH = LROH/Lauto, where Lauto is the total length of the autosomes in the donkey genome [32]. Furthermore, the nucleotide diversity (Pi) of each domestic donkey breed was calculated by VCFtools, with a window size of 50 kb and a step size of 20 kb. Next, PopLDdecay was used to calculate and visualize the linkage disequilibrium decay between pairwise SNPs [33]. Additionally, eight samples were randomly selected for each breed (Table S4). Based on the reported mutation rate (I = 7.242 × 10−⁹) and generation interval (g = 8) [34], SMC + + was used to simulate the historical effective population size (Ne) of eight domestic donkey breeds [35].

Genomic signatures of positive selection

To identify selection signatures within the Sichuan donkey population, we employed the θπ statistic calculated by VCFtools and the composite likelihood ratio (CLR) method computed by SweepFinder2 [36]. Subsequently, VCFtools was used to calculate the fixation index (FST) and the nucleotide diversity ratio (θπ-ratio) statistics, and these statistics were used to detect the potential selective regions between Sichuan donkey and six other domestic donkey breeds (Australian, Ethiopia, Kenya, Spanish, Guanzhong, Yunnan). The θπ values, CLR scores, FST, and θπ-ratio were calculated using a sliding window approach with a window size of 50 kb and a step size of 20 kb. For all four methods, the top 1% windows were defined as candidate regions, which were subsequently annotated using Bedtools [37, 38]. We used KOBAS to perform KEGG pathway enrichment analysis of candidate genes [39], and KEGG pathways were considered significantly enriched when the P value was less than 0.05.

Results

Sequence read depth and genomic features

Genome data from 17 sequenced Sichuan donkeys and 99 publicly available donkey genomes were aligned to the reference genome. In total, 8.3 billion filtered reads were generated for the Sichuan donkey population, with a mapping rate of 99.76% and a sequencing depth of 18.74 × (Table S1). In terms of the number of SNPs, the largest number of biallelic SNPs (8,099,743) was detected in the aligned reads of Sichuan donkey (Table S5). Further statistical analysis was performed on the shared and specific SNPs among Chinese domestic donkey breeds, including Sichuan donkey, Guanzhong donkey, Tibetan donkey and Yunnan donkey. In total, 3,359,388 SNPs were shared across all four Chinese donkey breeds. Each breed-specific SNPs were identified, with 986,317 variants exclusive to Sichuan donkey (Fig. S1).

Genetic structure analysis

To elucidate the population genetic structure and phylogeny of Sichuan donkey, Admixture, PCA, and NJ-tree analyses were performed on the genome data of 100 domestic donkeys and 16 wild donkeys. The Admixture analysis results showed that when K = 2, domestic donkeys and the African wild ass had the same ancestry composition, which was distinct from that of the Asian wild ass. When K = 4, Chinese domestic donkeys exhibited a different ancestral composition from foreign domestic donkeys. This pattern became increasingly evident when K = 5, indicating the complex genetic composition of the domestic donkey population (Fig. 1B). In the PCA, PC1 accounted for 4.45% of the total variation and effectively separated the Asian wild ass from the domestic donkeys. PC2 explained 2.57% of the total variation and further distinguished the African wild ass from the domestic donkeys (Fig. 1C). The NJ-tree analysis revealed that Sichuan donkeys were closely related to the other three Chinese domestic donkey breeds, consistent with the findings from the Admixture and PCA analyses (Fig. 1D). To further explore the phylogenetic relationships among Sichuan donkey and other Chinese domestic donkey breeds (Guanzhong, Tibetan, and Yunnan donkey), the same analyses were performed on 72 donkeys, with the African wild ass as an outgroup. In this PCA, PC1 explained 4.27% of the total variation, while PC2 accounted for 1.41% and further separated the Chinese domestic donkey breeds (Fig. S2). Combining the results from the NJ-tree and Admixture analyses, we observed that Sichuan donkey has a close genetic relationship with Tibetan donkey, which may be attributed to their close geographical proximity (Fig. S3, S4).

Genetic diversity analysis

The results of Pi indicated that Sichuan donkey had the highest Pi value (0.000719), while Australian donkey had the lowest (0.000573) (Fig. 2A, Table S6). The average ROH length of each breed showed that Australian donkey had the longest ROH, followed by Spanish donkey. Sichuan donkey had a longer average ROH than Yunnan donkey but shorter than Tibetan donkey (Fig. 2B). The FROH reflects the degree of inbreeding within the population, and the result showed that Sichuan donkey had a lower inbreeding level (FROH = 0.171251), followed by Guanzhong donkey (FROH = 0.172257) and Tibetan donkey (FROH = 0.174661), whereas Australian donkey had the highest level of inbreeding (FROH = 0.257509) (Fig. 2C, Table S6). Additionally, genetic differentiation between breeds was evaluated using FST, revealing a differentiation range from 0.01 to 0.15. The Australian donkey showed significantly higher genetic differentiation from other breeds, while most other breeds exhibited relatively small differences (Fig. 2E). The Ne analysis indicated that the Ne of all domestic donkey breeds decreased to varying degrees about 100,000 years ago, with a further decrease in Ne observed for Sichuan donkeys about 20,000 years ago. Yunnan donkey showed a similar Ne pattern to Sichuan donkey, which may be related to their close geographical proximity (Fig. 2F).

Genomic signatures of positive selection

To investigate the selection pressure within the Sichuan donkey population, θπ and CLR analyses were used to detect genomic regions under selection. These methods identified 965 and 434 genes, respectively (Fig. 3A, Tables S7, S8). Forty-nine genes overlapped between the two analyses, suggesting that these genes have been subjected to strong selective pressure. These candidate genes are associated with various biological functions, such as innate immunity (BPIFB2, BPIFB3, and BPIFB6) [40, 41], blood pressure regulation (SLC26A4, PDE1A) [42, 43], motion features (MRC2, BCAP29, COL24A1, ITGA9, and FOXS1) [44–48], thermoregulation (NCOA2, PAM, and SLC1A1) [49–51], DNA damage repair (TLK2, SUPT16H, ERCC6L2, and TPX2) [52–55], meat quality (MYLK2, CTDSPL) [56, 57], and reproduction (SUN5, MAST2) [58, 59].Fig. 3Analysis of the signatures of positive selection in the genome of Sichuan donkeys. Meanwhile, selective sweep analysis was conducted between 27 Sichuan donkeys and 6 other domestic donkey breeds using FST and θπ-ratio methods (Fig. 3B, Tables S9, S10). FST identified 443 genes, while θπ-ratio identified 456 genes, with 84 genes overlapping between the two analyses (Table S11). Seven common candidate genes (CCSER1, FGD6, VEZT, SLC26A4, SIK3, MAGI2, and RPA4) were identified by all four selection signal methods (Fig. 3C). Among the 84 candidate genes, several are associated with high-altitude adaptation (CACNA2D3, EGLN3, and RORA) [60–62], cold adaptation (PLPPR1) [63], growth and development (PGM2L1, CCSER1, NPAS3) [64–66], reproductive performance (INSL6, SLC38A2) [67, 68], and immunity (RNF43, TNFSF15) [69, 70]. Moreover, EGLN3, MTMR4, and ZNF668 have been identified as significant candidate genes for high-altitude pulmonary hypertension and environmental adaptation in humans [71–73]. Notably, EGLN3 exhibited distinct haplotypes in Sichuan and Tibetan donkeys compared to the other six domestic donkey breeds, suggesting that it may be a key candidate gene related to high-altitude adaptation (Fig. 3D). Furthermore, KEGG enrichment analyses of the 84 overlapping candidate genes identified 32 significantly enriched pathways, primarily clustered in signal transduction, metabolism, and endocrine processes (Table S12).

Discussion

Sichuan donkeys, bred in Ganzi Tibetan Autonomous Prefecture in southwestern China, possess valuable genetic traits that render them an irreplaceable resource in remote mountainous regions. These traits include excellent adaptability to challenging conditions such as low temperatures, hypoxia, and intense ultraviolet radiation, as well as their small stature, flexibility, and strong disease resistance [6]. However, in recent years, the development of mechanization has led to a sharp decline in the Sichuan donkey population, posing significant challenges to their conservation. Therefore, a comprehensive analysis using a wide range of genome sequence variation data is essential for the scientific conservation and rational utilization of Sichuan donkeys.

Our population structure analysis revealed that the African wild ass is the ancestor of modern domestic donkey breeds (K = 2) (Fig. 1B), which is consistent with previous findings [74]. Further, our combined PCA and NJ tree analyses demonstrated that Chinese domestic donkeys share a closer genetic relationship than with foreign breeds (Fig. 1C, D). Specifically, Sichuan donkeys and Tibetan donkeys exhibited a relatively close genetic relationship (Fig. S3). These results indicate that the genomic composition of Chinese domestic donkeys is influenced by geographical location. To further investigate the genetic diversity of these breeds, we conducted a statistical analysis of the ROH segments across each domestic donkey breed. Our results showed that the average length of the ROH segments in Chinese domestic donkeys was relatively low. Compared with other domestic donkey breeds, the Sichuan donkey population exhibited a higher level of nucleotide diversity, a lower LD decay value, and a lower inbreeding coefficient, all of which indicated a greater degree of genetic diversity. Therefore, Sichuan donkey, as an important genetic resource, possesses a rich source of genetic variation, which is crucial for future breeding efforts.

Furthermore, we used four methods (θπ, CLR, FST, and θπ-ratio) to conduct a selective scan of the genome of Sichuan donkey. These analyses helped to elucidate the selection pressures acting on Sichuan donkey. Specifically, the θπ and CLR analyses identified candidate genes related to immunity and low-temperature adaptation. The BPI-fold-containing family genes, including BPIFB2, BPIFB3, and BPIFB6, were identified as key candidates. These genes are primarily expressed in the nasopharynx, oral cavity, nasal cavity, and respiratory tract. They can specifically bind to Gram-negative bacteria to exert antibacterial effects and participate in host defense and innate immunity [41]. This suggests that this gene family may serve as a valuable reference for the prevention and treatment of respiratory diseases in animals. Additionally, we identified genes associated with lipid metabolism and thermogenesis-regulated biological processes, such as NCOA2, SLC1A1, and PAM. NCOA2, a member of the nuclear hormone receptor coactivator family, promotes adipogenesis by interacting with PPARγ and reduces energy expenditure by competing with SRC-1 for PGC-1α [51]. NCOA2-null mice exhibited enhanced adaptive thermogenesis and increased energy expenditure under cold exposure [75]. Similarly, eliminating PAM expression in forebrain neurons could produce peripheral vasoconstriction and core heat gain, thereby improving thermoregulation during cold [50], and eliminating PAM expression in cardiomyocytes could increase heat production by increasing UCP1 expression [76]. EAAT3 encoded by the SLC1A1 gene, stimulates sympathetic nerves to promote BAT thermogenesis to initiate cold defense responses when activated by coding signals [77]. In conclusion, NCOA2, SLC1A1, and PAM likely contribute to the enhanced cold tolerance of Sichuan donkeys by promoting heat production and improving thermoregulation. These findings provide insights into the genetic basis of cold adaptation in Sichuan donkeys and highlight potential targets for further research on cold tolerance mechanisms.

FST and θπ-ratio were used to identify the selection signals in Sichuan donkey and six other domestic donkey breeds. Several candidate genes were associated with adaptive phenotypes, including EGLN3, which affects high-altitude adaptation in humans and pigs [61, 71], RORA and CACNA2D3, reported as candidate genes for high-altitude hypoxia adaptation in cattle and sheep, respectively [60, 62], and ZNF668 and PLPPR1, associated with the cold adaptation in humans and chickens [63, 73]. We also identified candidate genes that influence growth and meat quality, such as JAK2, VEZT and PGM2L1, which are associated with body size and muscle development in livestock [64, 65, 78]. FGD6 and MECOM are linked to the growth traits of cattle [79, 80]. MYO1C, associated with beef tenderness, is involved in actin cytoskeleton processes that affect meat tenderness and texture [81]. Moreover, genes related to adipose deposition, immunity, and reproduction traits were also identified, including ZNF189, PLGRKT, GSPT1, and AKAP6, which are linked to lipid metabolism and intramuscular fat deposition in humans [82], mice [83], pigs [84], cattle [85]. Other genes that may be involved in important functions of Sichuan donkeys include TNFSF15, which regulates uterine immunity in pigs [70]. NAALADL2 is related to the immunity of Pinan cattle [86]. INSL6 is closely associated with the male fertility of boars [67]. SLC38A2 is involved in testicular development and spermatogenesis in Tibetan sheep [68], and KCNIP4 is related to the reproductive traits of geese [87]. In summary, these candidate genes play an important role in the economic traits of livestock and poultry, providing a valuable reference for understanding the genetic basis of these traits in Sichuan donkeys. This research may guide future breeding and conservation efforts.

Conclusions

This study comprehensively demonstrated the genomic variations of Sichuan donkeys through whole-genome resequencing. The analysis of the population structure of domestic donkeys enhanced our understanding of the evolution and origin of Sichuan donkeys. The analysis of genetic diversity and Ne provided the basis for the genetic evaluation of Sichuan donkeys. Moreover, by detecting potential selected regions in the genome of Sichuan donkeys, a series of candidate genes associated with high-altitude adaptation were identified. These candidate genes reflect the unique characteristics of the breed and provide a theoretical foundation for the conservation and sustainable utilization of Sichuan donkeys.
