Optimizing Benzodiazepine Use for Chronic Insomnia: Insights from a Delphi Expert Consensus Study
Authors: Matteo Di Vincenzo, Umberto Albert, Bernardo Dell’Osso, Giovanni Martinotti, Maurizio Pompili, Andrea Fiorillo
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractIntroductionChronic insomnia is the most prevalent sleep-wake disorder, affecting more than 16% of the general population. Benzodiazepines (BDZs) represent a therapeutic option for this condition, given their rapid action and sleep-maintenance effects. BDZ use remains valid and widespread, even if, in some cases, it has been associated with misuse and tolerance when treatment is protracted. We carried out a Delphi study to reach an expert consensus on the appropriate use of BDZs and potential unclear aspects regarding their management of chronic insomnia, including special populations.MethodsA panel of 33 experts was asked to answer a questionnaire consisting of 35 questions, prepared by a Steering Committee. Items which did not reach a consensus (namely, agreement of more than 66% of responders) in the first in-person round were re-answered by experts in an online second round.ResultsExperts emphasized the importance of half-life, formulation, and usage patterns in guiding appropriate prescribing of BDZs. “As‑needed” administration was deemed preferable for specific populations—elderly and individuals with relevant physical comorbidities. However, consensus was not reached regarding the appropriateness of BDZ use for chronic insomnia in women during the first trimester of pregnancy or breastfeeding. The panel also highlighted the need to strengthen clinicians’ training and improve patients’ education.ConclusionA thorough clinical characterization of each patient with chronic insomnia should remain the cornerstone for guiding BDZ treatment decisions. Enhancing physicians’ training and strengthening patients’ education are essential steps toward optimizing BDZ use.Keywords: insomnia, sleep-wake disorder, benzodiazepines, sedative, hypnotic

Introduction

Chronic insomnia is the most common sleep-wake disorder, characterized by difficulty in initiating sleep, maintaining sleep, and/or waking earlier than expected in the morning. A person can be diagnosed with “insomnia disorder” if these struggles occur at least three nights a week, within a period of at least three months, even in the absence of comorbidities.1–3 This condition is estimated to affect more than 16% of the general population,4 ranking second among psychiatric and neurological conditions in Europe and posing significant levels of personal, social and economic burden.5–7 Moreover, associated daytime symptoms (eg, fatigue, drowsiness, dizziness, and low cognitive performance) highly impair global functioning and quality of life,8 making chronic insomnia a “24-hour” syndrome.9

Clinical management of patients with insomnia disorder requires an in-depth diagnostic evaluation aimed at characterizing nocturnal symptoms, personal features, bedtime routine and comorbidities, according to a comprehensive and tailored approach.10 Available treatments include non-pharmacological strategies (eg, cognitive behavioral therapy for insomnia, CBT-I) and medications, such as short- and intermediate-acting benzodiazepines (BDZs), whose recommended duration of use should not exceed 4 weeks,11 although in some cases this period may not be sufficient for the patient.

BDZs, which were first synthesized by Leo Sternbach in 1955,12 are allosteric modulators of the gamma-aminobutyric acid-A (GABA-A) receptor, acting as central nervous system depressants.13 Alongside their hypnotic function, these molecules also have anxiolytic, anticonvulsant, and myorelaxant effects, with a high therapeutic index and a rapid onset of action. Unlike barbiturates, they demonstrate non-lethal risk,14 and an antidote (ie, flumazenil) is available in the case of overdose.15 A variety of BDZ compounds are currently available, distinguished by pharmacokinetic characteristics, potency, half-life, duration of action (eg, long, intermediate, and short), and the presence of active metabolites.16

Due to their clinical effectiveness, safety, wide spectrum of functions and versatile profile of action, BDZs became among the most widely prescribed medications in several fields of medicine, being employed in the treatment of different pathological conditions other than insomnia disorder (eg, anxiety, psychomotor agitation, delirium, alcohol withdrawal, catatonia), as well as in the execution of diagnostic and surgical procedures.16,17 However, risks of prolonged use, misuse, and dependence have been documented among BDZs users,18,19 influencing the reputation of these drugs across decades20–23 and progressively increasing the number of detractors.24,25 Similarly, the introduction of other classes of medications in the market (eg, selective serotonin reuptake inhibitors, new-generation antipsychotics) has contributed to more attention on the correct use of BDZs in recent years, especially among new generations of medical residents and early-career clinicians.26

Given their high therapeutic potential, together with the risk of misuse, the use of BDZs cannot be left to chance. Indeed, they should be prescribed according to a clinical rationale, taking into account the different pharmacological characteristics (eg, half-life, duration of action), the diverse patients’ profiles and attitudes, and the possibility for prescribers to monitor follow-up.27

Currently available evidence on this topic remains heterogeneous, particularly with regard to clinically relevant issues related to the use of BDZs. Consequently, a conventional systematic review alone would not be sufficient to address several critical situations encountered in routine clinical practice. Similarly, the development of formal clinical practice guidelines generally requires a stronger and more consistent evidence base than is currently available. In this context, a Delphi consensus methodology represents an appropriate approach to systematically gather and synthesize expert opinion, identify areas of agreement, and provide practical guidance in a field where evidence remains limited, evolving, or insufficient to support definitive recommendations.

Based on these premises, we conducted a Delphi study to reach consensus and guide clinicians on the appropriate use and role of BDZs in the management of insomnia disorder. We focused on the most debated and controversial clinical issues (eg, use of BDZs in special populations, such as pregnant women, adolescents, and the elderly) and major clinical concerns (eg, risks of misuse and tolerance).

Materials and Methods

Study Design

The Delphi method is a systematic and structured technique used to reach a consensus on debated themes when substantial scientific evidence is not available.28 Expert opinions are collected through sequential rounds of evaluation, with anonymized answers and controlled feedback, aiming to promote convergence towards a shared position while minimizing interpersonal influence.29

Preparatory Phase

The preparatory phase of the current study was conducted by a Steering Committee composed of five key opinion leaders with consolidated and long-standing expertise in the fields of psychopharmacology and chronic insomnia management. A methodologist experienced in the Delphi process and a junior researcher contributed to this phase. The Steering Committee defined: 1) the scientific domains to be explored (eg, appropriate chronic insomnia management, use of BDZs in special clinical populations and specific conditions, recommended duration of treatment, pharmacological associations of BDZs, treatment personalization); 2) the structure of the questionnaire; 3) the consensus threshold; 4) the workflow between the first and the second round.

Questionnaire

The development of the Delphi questionnaire followed a multistep process, including: 1) review of the available evidence; 2) identification of critical aspects and major challenges met in clinical practice; 3) assessment and approval of the final draft of the Delphi questionnaire by external validators (see Members of validation panel).

The final Delphi questionnaire administered to expert panel consisted of thirty-five items grouped into ten statements: 1) chronic insomnia management; 2) pharmacological associations of BDZs with other classes of drugs; 3) use of BDZs in elderly patients; 4) use of BDZs in the treatment of chronic insomnia in patients with physical illnesses; 5) appropriate prescribing of BDZs in patients with alcohol and/or substance use disorder; 6) appropriate prescribing of BDZs in adolescent and young patients; 7) choice of BDZ molecule in the personalization of chronic insomnia treatment; 8) choice of pharmaceutical formulation (tablets vs oral solution) in the personalization of chronic insomnia treatment; 9) training and updating of clinicians on the rational use of BDZs; 10) promoting awareness among the general population on the responsible use of BDZs (Table 1).

Consensus Criteria

Levels of agreement for each item included the following options on a 5-point Likert scale: 1 = strong disagreement; 2 = disagreement; 3 = uncertainty; 4 = agreement; 5 = strong agreement. A negative agreement was reached if the sum of responses 1 and 2 was greater than 66%; a positive agreement was reached if the sum of responses 4 and 5 was also greater than 66%; no consensus was reached if the sum was below this threshold.30

Expert Panel

Panelists were selected based on their recognized clinical and scientific expertise in the field, as evidenced by their publication record, clinical experience, and involvement in scientific societies and research activities. A total of 33 experts were invited, and all agreed to participate; therefore, no invited expert declined participation. No conflicts of interest or industry affiliations were reported (see Expert Panel Composition).

Delphi Procedure

The first round was conducted in May 2025, during an in-person meeting among participants, who openly and spontaneously discussed the main topics proposed by the Steering Committee. The statements were not circulated to participants before the meeting and were presented for the first time during the questionnaire completion. Participants accessed the anonymous survey individually through a QR code and completed the questionnaire independently by using their own electronic device (laptop, tablet or smartphone).

For the second round in July 2025, the Steering Committee reviewed all statements that had not achieved consensus and identified relevant scientific evidence from the available literature. A summary document including balanced and objective evidence was distributed by Email to all participants in order to enable an informed re-evaluation while avoiding artificial pressure toward agreement. All participants received the same supporting materials and subsequently completed the questionnaire remotely. The purpose of this phase was not to “force consensus”, but to capture the clinicians’ updated perspectives after the provision of supporting scientific evidence.

Data Analysis

The main sociodemographic information of the expert members (eg, gender, age, medical specialization, geographical origin, years of clinical experience, membership in scientific societies) was collected and managed anonymously. For each item of the Delphi questionnaire, the following were computed: 1) absolute and percentage frequencies for all five Likert responses; 2) percentage of positive, negative, or absent consensus; 3) comparison between first and second round results, in order to assess the effect of the supporting literature.

Results

Delphi Panelists

All thirty-three experts were included in the analysis. Two-third of them were men (N=22) and the most represented age range was 56–65 years old (41%, N=13), with a prevalent clinical experience of 21–25 years (N=8). More than half of the group were from Northern Italy (N=18). Almost all were psychiatrists (94%, N=31); other members included one neurologist and one clinician specialized in both neurology and paediatrics. The group regularly attended scientific congresses or meetings; 12 responders were active members of national or international scientific societies (eg, Italian Society of Psychiatry, N=6) (Table 2).

Regarding the general aspects of chronic insomnia management, the expert panel agreed in considering sleep hygiene as the first approach (Item 1.1; 91%) and BDZs the first-line pharmacological treatment (Item 1.5; 94%). The usefulness of melatonin and/or supplements such as magnesium, passionflower, and valerian was recognized by 73% of responders (Item 1.4). Disapproval emerged with regard to the availability of CBT-I services in Italy (Item 1.3; 88%), while no consensus was reached in recognizing this strategy as a first-line treatment (Item 1.2; endorsed by 58% of responders). Also, experts did not find an agreement in considering the four-week period recommended by the guidelines as an adequate time for treating insomnia disorder with BDZs (Item 1.6; disapproved by 61%).

These medications were prescribed by 85% of panelists along with other drugs such as antihistamines, antipsychotics, and antidepressants (Item 2.3), while 9 in 10 responders disapproved of the combination of two BDZs at the same time (Item 2.1). Moreover, agreement was not obtained about the simultaneous prescription of a BDZ and a z-drug in the treatment of chronic insomnia (Item 2.2; disapproved by 61%).

Medium and long half-life BDZs in the elderly were considered to cause sedation, hangover, mental confusion, and risk of falls upon awakening (Item 3.4; 82%); 73% of experts also stated that the presence of cognitive deficits in this population contraindicates the use of these compounds (Item 3.5). Conversely, the sentences “Benzodiazepines used in the management of elderly patients with insomnia cause sedation, hangover, mental confusion, and risk of falls upon waking” (Item 3.1; endorsed by 48%), “All benzodiazepines can be used at low doses in elderly patients” (Item 3.2; disagreed by 55%) and “In elderly patients with insomnia, benzodiazepines should only be used as needed” (Item 3.3; disapproved by 48%) did not find the panelists in agreement.

Moreover, participants disregarded the presence of a physical illness as a contraindication to the use of BDZs (Item 4.1; 91%); as well, they disapproved the use “as needed” in patients with insomnia disorder and somatic multimorbidity (Item 4.4; 82%). Conversely, consensus was not obtained with regard to the general safeness of such molecules in patients with severe physical illness (Item 4.2, approved by 58%) and the role of long half-life compounds in sedation, hangover and mental confusion upon awakening in this frail population (Item 4.3; disagreed by 46%).

The absolute contraindication of all BDZs was disapproved in the case of patients with substance use disorder (Item 5.1; 85%), alcohol use disorder (Item 5.2; 67%), adolescents (Item 6.1; 79%), young adults (Item 6.2; 94%), women of childbearing age (Item 6.3; 100%), and pregnant women (Item 6.4; 79%). However, the sentences “In a pregnant patient (first trimester) with insomnia, the use of benzodiazepines is contraindicated” (Item 6.5; disapproved by 48%) and “In a patient breastfeeding with insomnia, the use of benzodiazepines is contraindicated” (Item 6.6; disagreed by 46%) did not get a consensus and needed to be reevaluated.

Regarding the duration of action, 70% of panelists agreed or strongly agreed that only short half-life BDZs can be used in the pharmacological management of patients experiencing difficulty sleeping (Item 7.1). Oral solution was considered more adequate for greater personalization of chronic insomnia treatment (Item 8.1; 91%) and emerged as a preference of patients over the tablets (Item 8.2; 79%); however, the sentence: “Benzodiazepines in oral solution (drops) are preferred by prescribers over tablet formulations” (Item 8.3) did not reach an agreement among the experts (43%).

The need to improve and update the training on the correct use of BDZs of clinicians (Item 9.1), trainees and medical students (Item 9.3) was declared by all or almost all panelists. The total sample endorsed promoting collaboration between psychiatrists and general practitioners (Item 9.2), as well as involving pharmacists in training courses on the responsible use of BDZs (Item 10.2). Lastly, 91% of them approved initiatives to raise awareness among the general population (Item 10.1).

Simultaneous use of BDZs and z-drugs was discouraged by 8 out of 10 participants (Item 2.2); seven in 10 also definitely disapproved of the safety of BDZs in patients with severe physical illness (Item 4.2).

Discussion

Benzodiazepines are recommended as pharmacological treatment for patients with insomnia disorder (in particular short and long half-life compounds), together with other classes of agents, such as BDZ receptor agonists, melatonin, and orexine receptor antagonists.11 BDZs have a high therapeutic index, rapidity of action, and non-lethal risks, but their intake should not exceed a period of 4 weeks.31,32 This recommendation is widely disregarded by prescribers in routine clinical practice. According to a survey involving more than 600 European medical professionals (general practitioners, psychiatrists, neurologists, and other sleep specialists), the average duration of BDZ treatment is 15.3 weeks, with peaks in Spain (23.0 weeks) and Italy (22.0 weeks).33 Long-term use of BDZs refers to a period longer than 6 months.34

While meta-analysis did not confirm the clinical effectiveness of these compounds in the chronic management of insomnia,35 protracted treatment may result in the development of tolerance, with the latter exhibiting an overall estimated prevalence of 2%, or more in certain vulnerable populations (eg, individuals with mental comorbidities, concomitant substance use, and the elderly).20,36

To fully exploit the therapeutic potential of these medications, some precautions and best practices should be implemented in daily practice.26,37 Indeed, an accurate clinical characterization of patients’ profiles could enable clinicians to promptly identify individuals at higher risk of adverse outcomes from BDZ use. Furthermore, the promotion of educational programs addressed to clinicians and lay people could contribute to increase knowledge and awareness on the pros and cons of these compounds.

According to our results, sleep hygiene is the first strategy to implement in the management of patients with insomnia disorder. The usefulness of melatonin and supplements or nutrients was also recognized. Furthermore, BDZs were considered the first-line pharmacological treatment, even though during the first round no agreement was reached on the adequacy of the 4-week duration, reflecting the attitudes of the Italian clinicians to use BDZs over the long term.33 The gap between recommendations and clinical practice also emerged with respect to the threshold of 3 months required by the main classification systems (eg, Diagnostic and statistical manual of mental disorders. 5th edition, text revision, DSM-5-TR; International Classification of Diseases 11th revision, ICD-11)2,3 to make a proper diagnosis of insomnia disorder, which is often disregarded by prescribers dealing with acute symptoms.38 Another critical item was the use of cognitive behavioral therapy for insomnia (CBT-I) as first-line treatment,11 that was definitively approved only in the second round. However, the limited availability of this strategy in Italian mental health services was considered the main barrier to its non-use, echoing an international scenario of a shortage of providers, limited knowledge, and low patients’ motivation.39

Based on their daily routine clinical practice, experts agreed that the concomitant use of different BDZs should be avoided in the treatment of chronic insomnia; on the contrary, they agreed to add other classes of drugs, including sedative antidepressants and antipsychotics, to BDZs. However, both antidepressants and antipsychotics are often used off-label and their use in chronic insomnia management is not supported by strong evidence in the absence of comorbid mental problems.40 The association of BDZs with a BDZ receptor agonist, namely z-drug, was discouraged by the panelists in the second round, after the evaluation of technical data sheets (eg, zolpidem) indicating an increased risk of sedation, alteration of psychomotor functions, and impairment of driving ability, as consequences of drug–drug interaction.31 Other reasons included a lack of robust evidence, poor literature, risk of receptor overlap, and scant benefit compared to alternative strategies.

A controversial issue is related to the use of BDZs in the elderly. Indeed, while some guidelines clearly recommend avoiding these drugs in older people,41,42 others state that BDZs can be used in this population for short-term and even longer when benefits overcome risks.43 The complexity of BDZ use in elderly people was reflected in the levels of uncertainty among our panelists. Indeed, participants decisively agreed that compounds with intermediate-to-long half-lives are associated with a higher risk of side effects due to greater accumulation. As well, they were resolute in considering cognitive impairment as a contraindication for BDZs administration in the elderly. However, in consideration of the occurrence of side effects in this population,44 they took further time, and only during the second round a consensus was obtained: benefits should overcome risks, and low dosage, short-term and intermittent therapies should be preferable. Moreover, the presence of comorbidity, frailty levels, and polytherapy was also accounted as red flags to be carefully considered by prescribers, since lower levels of drug toxicity over maximum efficacy have to be prioritized in this special population.

A debate was also launched on statement 4. In fact, more than 90% of panelists declared that the presence of “physical illness” without any specification does not represent a general contraindication for the use of BDZs. As well, the restriction of such drugs as medications “as needed” in patients suffering from insomnia disorder and somatic comorbidity was also disapproved during the first round. After thorough consideration of some clinical conditions mentioned as contraindications for BDZs in the technical data sheets (eg, myasthenia gravis, severe respiratory insufficiency, severe hepatic insufficiency, sleep apnea syndrome), the experts disregarded the item: “The BDZs are safe in patients with severe physical illnesses”. Moreover, in the second round they also decided to agree with the item 4.3, stating that long half-life BDZs cause detrimental effects in patients with somatic problems due to accumulation.45 With respect to these patients, emerging biomarker-based and machine-learning approaches may represent a potential avenue for more precise and individualized management of sleep disorders.46 Indeed, these tools could potentially improve patient stratification, identify subjects at increased risk of adverse outcomes, and support a more personalized risk-benefit assessment when considering pharmacological treatments, including BDZs.

The Delphi questionnaire highlighted that the use of BDZs cannot be absolutely contraindicated for treating chronic insomnia in patients with substance and/or alcohol use disorders, where the real risk of abuse, motivational aspects, and possibly follow-up monitoring should be carefully explored. In addition, BDZs can be used in these patients as replacement therapy.

Similarly, BDZs cannot be generally contraindicated in adolescents, young adults and women of childbearing age or during pregnancy. However, the items regarding the avoidance of BDZs during the first trimester of pregnancy and throughout the breastfeeding period remained without consensus after the second round. This finding reflects the cautious approach adopted by the expert panels in these sensitive clinical contexts, where residual uncertainties and concerns about potential adverse outcomes may influence clinical judgment despite the available evidence. Indeed, the first-trimester exposure to BDZs results in a small increased risk of congenital defects in offspring, especially when higher daily dosages are used.47 Furthermore, sedative effects of infant exposed to BDZs during lactation are rare;48 in these cases, short half-life molecules are preferable.49 Non-treated stress, severe insomnia, cortisol imbalances and abrupt suspensions of BDZs treatment actually represent major concerns in this vulnerable population.

Panelists were also asked to discuss the personalization of insomnia disorder management. While short-acting compounds emerged as the best choice in the case of sleep-onset insomnia, BDZs in oral solution were considered helpful in modulating and adjusting therapies. Moreover, drops resulted in being considered the preferred formulation by both patients and prescribers.

The expert group fully agreed on the need to improve training on the correct use of BDZs for all physicians, residents, and medical students; greater collaboration between psychiatrists and general practitioners in prescribing these drugs was also considered essential. In addition, the active involvement of pharmacists and the general public was also approved, with the aim of optimizing the use of drugs with great therapeutic potential, which require informed and responsible use.26

It is noteworthy that the use of BDZs for suicidal purposes is not usually lethal, unless combined with alcohol or in the presence of organ disease. On the contrary, in order to implement effective strategies for suicide prevention, it is essential to treat severe anxiety, insomnia, and agitation, which are well recognized as main predictors of suicidal behavior.50 In this framework, such compounds can have a protective potential.

The current study should be considered in the light of several limitations.

First, all panelists were based in Italy and therefore represented a context that may not reflect other countries, where prescribing practices, availability, and formulations of BDZs may differ. Specifically, more than half of the participants were from Northern Italy, which may have further limited the perspectives. It is also noteworthy that two out of three participants were male, which may have affected the representativeness of the sample and, consequently, the generalizability of the current findings. Moreover, the vast majority of participants were psychiatrists, with only one neurologist and one clinician with specialization in both neurology and pediatrics. The underrepresentation of specialists other than psychiatrists did not allow us to capture points of view from other fields of medicine. Therefore, a more gender-balanced, and geographically and professionally diverse panel of experts – including participants from different countries, settings, medical specialties and subspecialties – would be valuable to further increase the robustness and generalizability of the findings.

Second, two items related to the use of BDZs during the first trimester of pregnancy and the breastfeeding period did not reach consensus, leaving unresolved clinical challenges.

Third, although responses were collected anonymously, and no identifying information was recorded, the possibility that the in-person discussion influenced participants’ judgments cannot be excluded and should be considered when interpreting the findings.

Lastly, the selection of supporting evidence by the Steering Committee may have potentially influenced the panelists’ responses to some extent.

Given these reasons, despite our findings come from a methodologically valid process, they should be confirmed by more rigorous studies, such as systematic reviews and meta-analyses.

Conclusions

Benzodiazepines are among the most commonly used pharmacological compounds in the management of chronic insomnia, given their high therapeutic index, rapidity of action and ability to mantain sleep. However, risks of misuse and tolerance exist, particularly in the case of unmonitored assumption. The accurate characterization of each patient emerged as a key aspect of this study, highlighting the possibility of prescribing BDZs also in special populations (eg, elderly, young people, pregnant women) for whom available evidence is still scarce.

The present Delphi study collected and summarized the opinions of an expert panel based on daily clinical experience with BDZs: 1) these represent a very heterogeneous class of drugs with different actions and clinical aims; 2) half-life, formulation, and patterns of use are key aspects to be taken into account in clinical routine; 3) intermittent use is often preferable; 4) any inappropriate alternatives (antipsychotics, antihistamines) may lead to more risks than BDZs; 5) there is a need to improve medical training and patient education.
