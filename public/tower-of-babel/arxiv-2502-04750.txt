Tighter sparse variational Gaussian processes
Thang D. Bui thang.bui@anu.edu.au, Matthew Ashman mca39@cam.ac.uk, Richard E. Turner ret26@cam.ac.uk

Abstract
Sparse variational Gaussian process (GP) approximations based on inducing points have become the de facto standard for scaling GPs to large datasets, owing to their theoretical elegance, computational efficiency, and ease of implementation. This paper introduces a provably tighter variational approximation by relaxing the standard assumption that the conditional approximate posterior given the inducing points must match that in the prior. The key innovation is to modify the conditional posterior to have smaller variances than that of the prior at the training points. We derive the collapsed bound for the regression case, describe how to use the proposed approximation in large data settings, and discuss its application to handle orthogonally structured inducing points and GP latent variable models. Extensive experiments on regression benchmarks, classification, and latent variable models demonstrate that the proposed approximation consistently matches or outperforms standard sparse variational GPs while maintaining the same computational cost. An implementation will be made available in all popular GP packages.



1
Introduction

Gaussian processes (GPs)
(
Rasmussen & Williams 2006
)
provide a powerful framework for modelling probability distributions over functions, offering principled uncertainty quantification and ease of use. Their flexibility in encoding domain knowledge—such as smoothness, peridocity, or domain-specific structure—has led to widespread adoption across scientific and engineering applications. Exact inference in GP models poses significant computational challenges, requiring
\mathcal{O}(N^{3})
time and
\mathcal{O}(N^{2})
space complexity for
N
observations. A suite of approximations have been developed to address these limitations. Most notably, sparse variational Gaussian processes
(
Titsias 2009
;
Hensman et al. 2015
;
Matthews et al. 2016
, SVGP;)
address the poor computational complexity through the use of an approximate posterior distribution parameterised by a small set of
inducing points
.

The standard SVGP framework employs a structured variational approximation that factorises the posterior distribution over the unknown function
f
into two components:
q(f)=p(f|{\bm{u}})q({\bm{u}})
. Here,
p(f|{\bm{u}})
represents the GP prior distribution conditioned on the function values at inducing locations
{\bm{z}}
,
{\bm{u}}=f({\bm{z}})
. The second term,
q({\bm{u}})
, is modelled as a multivariate Gaussian distribution. Improved variational approximations have been developed—such as SOLVE-GP
(
Shi et al. 2020
)
—which use more sophisticated distributions for
q({\bm{u}})
.

This paper introduces a novel approach to improving SVGP approximations by modifying the conditional GP prior distribution at observed inputs, rather than focusing solely on the inducing point distribution. For Gaussian likelihoods, our approach yields a new and improved collapsed lower bound on the log marginal likelihood that involves
no additional variational parameters
. Furthermore, we show how the uncollapsed form of our bound facilitates the use of stochastic mini-batch optimisation and extends naturally to non-Gaussian likelihoods through a single additional variational parameter. We demonstrate the versatility of our method by integrating it with SOLVE-GP and extending it to sparse variational approximations in the GP latent variable model
(
Lawrence 2005
;
Damianou et al. 2016
, GPLVM;)
. Our results demonstrate that by targeting our improved lower bound, our approach consistently improves the predictive performance and log marginal likelihood estimates across a range of regression, classification, and latent variable modelling tasks.

Background

This section provides a concise introduction to pseudo-point based sparse variational Gaussian processes
(
Titsias 2009
;
Hensman et al. 2015
;
Matthews et al. 2016
, SVGP;)
. Consider GP regression with Gaussian observation noise:

\displaystyle p(f|\gamma)

\displaystyle=\mathcal{GP}(f;0,k_{\gamma}),

(1)

\displaystyle p({\bm{y}}|f,{\bm{x}},\sigma^{2})

\displaystyle={\mathcal{N}}({\bm{y}};f({\bm{x}}),\sigma^{2}{\mathbf{I}}),

(2)

where
{\bm{x}}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{N\times D}
and
{\bm{y}}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{N}
are the training inputs and corresponding noisy outputs,
f
denotes the unknown function mapping from input to output,
k_{\gamma}
is the covariance function governed by hyperparameters
\gamma
, and
\sigma^{2}
is the observation noise. These hyperparameters, denoted collectively as
\theta
, can be found by maximising the log marginal likelihood:

\displaystyle{\mathcal{L}}(\theta)={\bm{c}}-\frac{1}{2}{\bm{y}}^{\intercal}(\mathbf{K}_{\mathbf{ff}}+\sigma^{2}{\mathbf{I}})^{-1}{\bm{y}}-\frac{1}{2}\log|\mathbf{K}_{\mathbf{ff}}+\sigma^{2}{\mathbf{I}}|,

(3)

where
\mathbf{K}_{\mathbf{ff}}
is the covariance between training function values
{\bm{f}}=f({\bm{x}})
.
This objective takes
{\mathcal{O}}(N^{3})
to compute and is thus computationally prohibitive for large
N
. To sidestep this, we use an approximate posterior judiciously parameterised by a small set of pseudo-points or inducing points as follows:

\displaystyle q(f)=p(f_{\neq{\bm{f}},{\bm{u}}}|{\bm{f}},{\bm{u}})p({\bm{f}}|{\bm{u}})q({\bm{u}}),

(4)

where
{\bm{u}}=f({\bm{z}})\in{\mathbb{R}}^{M}
and
{\bm{z}}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{M\times D}
are the inducing outputs and inputs, respectively, and
M\ll N
. The conditional
q(f_{\neq{\bm{u}}}|{\bm{u}})
in the approximate posterior is chosen to match that in the prior, leading to the following variational objective,

\displaystyle{\mathcal{F}}_{0}(q({\bm{u}}),\theta)

\displaystyle=\left\langle\log\frac{p(f)p({\bm{y}}|f,{\bm{x}})}{q(f)}\right\rangle_{q(f)}=\left\langle\frac{\log\cancel{p(f_{\neq{\bm{f}},{\bm{u}}}|{\bm{f}},{\bm{u}})}\cancel{p({\bm{f}}|{\bm{u}})}p({\bm{u}})p({\bm{y}}|f,{\bm{x}})}{\cancel{p(f_{\neq{\bm{f}},{\bm{u}}}|{\bm{f}},{\bm{u}})}\cancel{p({\bm{f}}|{\bm{u}})}q({\bm{u}})}\right\rangle_{q(f)}

\displaystyle=-\mathrm{KL}[q({\bm{u}})||p({\bm{u}})]+\sum_{n}\int_{{\bm{u}},f(x_{n})}q({\bm{u}})p(f({x_{n}})|{\bm{u}})\log p(y_{n}|f({x_{n}})).

(5)

Titsias 2009
showed that when the likelihood is Gaussian, an analytic optimal form for
q({\bm{u}})
can be found,
q({\bm{u}})\propto p({\bm{u}}){\mathcal{N}}({\bm{y}};\mathbf{K}_{\mathbf{fu}}\mathbf{K}_{\mathbf{uu}}^{-1}{\bm{u}},\sigma^{2}{\mathbf{I}})
, and that a collapsed bound is also analytically available,

\displaystyle{\mathcal{F}}_{1}(\theta)

\displaystyle={\bm{c}}-\frac{1}{2}{\bm{y}}^{\intercal}(\mathbf{Q}_{\mathbf{ff}}+\sigma^{2}{\mathbf{I}})^{-1}{\bm{y}}-\frac{1}{2}\log|\mathbf{Q}_{\mathbf{ff}}+\sigma^{2}{\mathbf{I}}|-\frac{1}{2\sigma^{2}}\mathrm{trace}(\mathbf{D}_{\mathbf{ff}}),

(6)

where
\mathbf{Q}_{\mathbf{ff}}=\mathbf{K}_{\mathbf{fu}}\mathbf{K}_{\mathbf{uu}}^{-1}\mathbf{K}_{\mathbf{uf}}
and
\mathbf{D}_{\mathbf{ff}}=\mathbf{K}_{\mathbf{ff}}-\mathbf{Q}_{\mathbf{ff}}
. Crucially, the bound above can be computed in
{\mathcal{O}}(NM^{2})
. The non-collapsed bound in
eq.

5
is amenable to non-Gaussian likelihoods and data mini-batch settings
(
Hensman et al. 2015
, see e.g.,)
, further reducing the training computational complexity to
{\mathcal{O}}(BM^{2}+M^{3})
where
B
is the mini-batch size. Due to this small complexity and the ease of implementation, the above variational approach has arguably become the go-to sparse approximation in the GP literature. In this work, we will revisit its core assumption of matching prior and posterior conditionals and show that relaxing this assumption results in a tighter and more performant approximation.

3
A tighter variational approximation

The variational approximation in
eq.

4
is chosen such that the conditional
q({\bm{f}}|{\bm{u}})
identically matches the prior conditional
p({\bm{f}}|{\bm{u}})
. Instead, we propose using the following variational posterior,

\displaystyle q(f)=p(f_{\neq{\bm{f}},{\bm{u}}}|{\bm{f}},{\bm{u}})q({\bm{f}}|{\bm{u}})q({\bm{u}}),

(7)

where
q({\bm{f}}|{\bm{u}})={\mathcal{N}}({\bm{f}};\mathbf{K}_{\mathbf{fu}}\mathbf{K}_{\mathbf{uu}}^{-1}{\bm{u}};\mathbf{D}_{\mathbf{ff}}^{1/2}{\mathbf{M}}\mathbf{D}_{\mathbf{ff}}^{\top/2})
,
{\mathbf{M}}
is a diagonal matrix,
{\mathbf{M}}=\mathrm{diag}([m_{1},m_{2},\dots,m_{N}])
and
m_{n}>0
. Note that the mean of the prior conditional
p({\bm{f}}|{\bm{u}})={\mathcal{N}}({\bm{f}};\mathbf{K}_{\mathbf{fu}}\mathbf{K}_{\mathbf{uu}}^{-1}{\bm{u}};\mathbf{D}_{\mathbf{ff}})
is retained in
q({\bm{f}}|{\bm{u}})
. The resulting variational bound is,

\displaystyle{\mathcal{F}}_{2}(q({\bm{u}}),\theta,{\mathbf{M}})

\displaystyle=\left\langle\frac{\log\cancel{p(f_{\neq{\bm{f}},{\bm{u}}}|{\bm{f}},{\bm{u}})}p({\bm{f}}|{\bm{u}})p({\bm{u}})p({\bm{y}}|f,{\bm{x}})}{\cancel{p(f_{\neq{\bm{f}},{\bm{u}}}|{\bm{f}},{\bm{u}})}q({\bm{f}}|{\bm{u}})q({\bm{u}})}\right\rangle_{q(f)}

\displaystyle=-\mathrm{KL}[q({\bm{u}})||p({\bm{u}})]-\int_{\bm{u}}q({\bm{u}})\mathrm{KL}[q({\bm{f}}|{\bm{u}})||p({\bm{f}}|{\bm{u}})]

\displaystyle\qquad+\sum_{n}\int_{{\bm{u}},f(x_{n})}q({\bm{u}})q(f({x_{n}})|{\bm{u}})\log p(y_{n}|f({x_{n}})).

(8)

Due to the structure of the variational distribution, the middle term can be simplified to,

\displaystyle-\int_{\bm{u}}q({\bm{u}})\mathrm{KL}[q({\bm{f}}|{\bm{u}})||p({\bm{f}}|{\bm{u}})]=-\frac{1}{2}\mathrm{trace}({\mathbf{M}})+\frac{1}{2}\log|{\mathbf{M}}|+\frac{N}{2}=\frac{1}{2}\sum_{n}[1+\log(m_{n})-m_{n}]

Collapsed bound and optimal
{\mathbf{M}}

In the regression case, similar to the Titsias’ bound, we can obtain the optimal form for
q({\bm{u}})\propto p({\bm{u}}){\mathcal{N}}({\bm{y}};\mathbf{K}_{\mathbf{fu}}\mathbf{K}_{\mathbf{uu}}^{-1}{\bm{u}},\sigma^{2}{\mathbf{I}})
, leading to the following collapsed bound,

\displaystyle{\mathcal{F}}_{3}(\theta,{\mathbf{M}})

\displaystyle={\bm{c}}-\frac{1}{2}{\bm{y}}^{\intercal}(\mathbf{Q}_{\mathbf{ff}}+\sigma^{2}{\mathbf{I}})^{-1}{\bm{y}}-\frac{1}{2}\log|\mathbf{Q}_{\mathbf{ff}}+\sigma^{2}{\mathbf{I}}|-\frac{1}{2}\sum_{n}\left[\frac{m_{n}d_{n}}{\sigma^{2}}-1-\log(m_{n})+m_{n}\right]

Setting the partial derivatives of
{\mathcal{F}}_{3}(\theta,{\mathbf{M}})
wrt
m_{n}
to 0, we arrive at
\color[rgb]{0,0,1}{m_{n}=\frac{\sigma^{2}}{d_{n}+\sigma^{2}}}
and the following bound,

\displaystyle\color[rgb]{0,0,1}{{\mathcal{F}}_{4}(\theta)={\bm{c}}-\frac{1}{2}{\bm{y}}^{\intercal}(\mathbf{Q}_{\mathbf{ff}}+\sigma^{2}{\mathbf{I}})^{-1}{\bm{y}}-\frac{1}{2}\log|\mathbf{Q}_{\mathbf{ff}}+\sigma^{2}{\mathbf{I}}|-\frac{1}{2}\sum_{n}\log\left(1+\frac{d_{n}}{\sigma^{2}}\right)},

(9)

where
d_{n}
is the
n
-th element in the diagonal of
\mathbf{D}_{\mathbf{ff}}
,
d_{n}={k}_{f_{n}f_{n}}-\mathbf{k}_{f_{n}\mathbf{u}}\mathbf{K}_{\mathbf{uu}}^{-1}\mathbf{k}_{\mathbf{u}f_{n}}
.

Comparison to Titsias’ bound

When
{\mathbf{M}}
is the identity matrix, that is
m_{n}=1

\forall n
, the approximation in
eq.

7
become the Titsias’ variational approximation in
eq.

4
and the bound in
{\mathcal{F}}_{3}(\theta)
becomes the Titsias’ bound
{\mathcal{F}}_{1}(\theta)
in
eq.

6
. We note that that
F_{4}(\theta)
is tighter than
{\mathcal{F}}_{1}(\theta)
due to the inequality
\log(1+x)<x
for all
x>-1
. Our solution improves upon the solution to the Titsias’ bound by allowing the marginals of the conditional approximate posterior,
q(f(x_{n})|{\bm{u}})
, to have smaller variances than that of the conditional prior, since the optimal
m_{n}=\frac{\sigma^{2}}{d_{n}+\sigma^{2}}<1
. Intuitively, this reduces the strength of the coupling between
q({\bm{u}})
and
q({\bm{f}})
, enabling
q({\bm{f}})
to more freely model the data whilst allowing
q(f)
to be close to the prior elsewhere.

It is also worth noting that the middle term of our bound is always non-positive. One might think that adding this term to the bound would give a poorer approximation, yet, the improvement in the expected log-likelihood (due to the smaller predictive variances at the training points—see predictions below) can yield a larger improvement to counteract.

Stochastic mini-batch settings

The new bound can also handle data mini-batching, yielding an
unbiased
estimator of the uncollapsed bound in
eq.

8
as follows,

\displaystyle{\mathcal{F}}_{2}(q({\bm{u}}),\theta,{\mathbf{M}})

\displaystyle\approx-\mathrm{KL}[q({\bm{u}})||p({\bm{u}})]+\frac{N}{2B}\sum_{b}[1+\log(m_{b})-m_{b}]

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad+\frac{N}{B}\sum_{B}\int_{{\bm{u}},f(x_{b})}q({\bm{u}})q(f({x_{b}})|{\bm{u}})\log p(y_{b}|f({x_{b}})).

(10)

Non-Gaussian likelihoods and
m_{n}
parameterisation

One can parameterise
m_{n}
’s to satisfy their positive constraint and optimise them directly at the cost of having
N
extra parameters. However, the optimal form for
m_{n}
in the Gaussian likelihood setting suggests a more efficient parameterisation
m_{n}=\beta/(d_{n}+\beta)
with
\beta>0
shared across all data points. We will use the latter parameterisation for all of our experiments.

Predictions

The predictive mean and variance of the predictive distribution at a test input
x_{*}
are

\displaystyle\small m_{*}

\displaystyle=\mathbf{k}_{\mathbf{*u}}\mathbf{K}_{\mathbf{uu}}^{-1}{\mathbf{m}}_{\bm{u}},

(11)

\displaystyle v_{*}

\displaystyle=\mathbf{k}_{\mathbf{**}}-\mathbf{k}_{\mathbf{*u}}\mathbf{K}_{\mathbf{uu}}^{-1}\mathbf{k}_{\mathbf{u*}}+\mathbf{k}_{\mathbf{*u}}\mathbf{K}_{\mathbf{uu}}^{-1}{\mathbf{S}}_{\bm{u}}\mathbf{K}_{\mathbf{uu}}^{-1}\mathbf{k}_{\mathbf{u*}}-(\mathbf{k}_{\mathbf{*f}}-\mathbf{Q}_{\mathbf{*f}}){\mathbf{V}}_{\mathbf{ff}}(\mathbf{k}_{\mathbf{f*}}-\mathbf{Q}_{\mathbf{f*}}),

(12)

where
{\mathbf{V}}_{\mathbf{ff}}=\mathbf{D}_{\mathbf{ff}}^{-\top/2}({\mathbf{I}}-{\mathbf{M}})\mathbf{D}_{\mathbf{ff}}^{-1/2}
. Note that (i) we can compute the predictive mean at the same cost as previous sparse approximations, and (ii) the predictive variance at a training point can be approximated by
v_{n}=m_{n}d_{n}+\mathbf{k}_{f_{n}{\bm{u}}}\mathbf{K}_{\mathbf{uu}}^{-1}{\mathbf{S}}_{\bm{u}}\mathbf{K}_{\mathbf{uu}}^{-1}\mathbf{k}_{{\bm{u}}f_{n}}
. More generally, the variance at a new input that is not a training or inducing input is expensive due to the presence of
\mathbf{D}_{\mathbf{ff}}
in the last term. One path to address this could be to approximate
\mathbf{D}_{\mathbf{ff}}
by its diagonal matrix or to use only a subset of training points for this computation. However, we find that simply ignoring the last term at test time does not impact the predictive performance while substantially reducing the prediction cost (see
section

6.2
).

Connections to existing bounds

We can use the log-sum inequality
1
1

1

For non-negative numbers
a_{1},a_{2},\ldots,a_{n}
and
b_{1},b_{2},\ldots,b_{n}
,
\sum_{i=1}^{n}a_{i}\log\frac{a_{i}}{b_{i}}\geq\left(\sum_{i=1}^{n}a_{i}\right)\log\frac{\sum_{i=1}^{n}a_{i}}{\sum_{i=1}^{n}b_{i}}
with equality iff
a_{i}/b_{i}=\text{constant}
.
to bound the last term of our collapsed bound:

\displaystyle\sum_{n=1}^{N}\log\left(1+\frac{d_{n}}{\sigma^{2}}\right)\leq N\log\frac{\left[\sum_{n=1}^{N}\left(1+\frac{d_{n}}{\sigma^{2}}\right)\right]}{N}=N\log\left[1+\frac{\mathrm{trace}(\mathbf{K}_{\mathbf{ff}}-\mathbf{Q}_{\mathbf{ff}})}{N\sigma^{2}}\right].

(13)

Thus a looser collapsed bound can be obtained:

\displaystyle{\mathcal{F}}_{5}(\theta)={\bm{c}}-\frac{1}{2}{\bm{y}}^{\intercal}(\mathbf{Q}_{\mathbf{ff}}+\sigma^{2}{\mathbf{I}})^{-1}{\bm{y}}-\frac{1}{2}\log|\mathbf{Q}_{\mathbf{ff}}+\sigma^{2}{\mathbf{I}}|-\frac{N}{2}\log\left(1+\frac{\mathrm{trace}(\mathbf{K}_{\mathbf{ff}}-\mathbf{Q}_{\mathbf{ff}})}{N\sigma^{2}}\right).

This bound was derived by
Artemev et al. 2021
based on bounds of the quadratic and log-determinant terms in the exact log marginal likelihood. This is also tighter than the Titsias’ bound, that is
{\mathcal{F}}_{4}(\theta)\geq{\mathcal{F}}_{5}(\theta)\geq{\mathcal{F}}_{1}(\theta)
.

One can also view the proposed variational approximation as an instance of the sparse orthogonal approach of
Shi et al. 2020
in which there are two sets of inducing points
{\bm{u}}
and
{\bm{v}}
with
{\bm{v}}\vcentcolon={\bm{f}}
,
{\mathbf{m}}_{\bm{v}}\vcentcolon={\bm{0}}
and
{\mathbf{S}}_{\bm{v}}\vcentcolon=\mathbf{D}_{\mathbf{ff}}^{1/2}{\mathbf{M}}\mathbf{D}_{\mathbf{ff}}^{\top/2}
. However, this view does not suggest new insights or potential improvements. We will next discuss how to use the proposed variational approximation to improve the sparse orthogonal approach and in the latent variable settings.

4
Application to sparse orthogonal variational GPs

The sparse orthogonal approach (SOLVEGP) of
Shi et al. 2020
can be viewed as a structured approximation with two sets of pseudo-points
{\bm{u}}
and
{\bm{v}}
,

\displaystyle q(f)

\displaystyle=p(f_{\neq{\bm{f}},{\bm{u}},{\bm{v}}}|{\bm{f}},{\bm{u}},{\bm{v}})p({\bm{f}}|{\bm{u}},{\bm{v}})q({\bm{u}},{\bm{v}}),

\displaystyle q({\bm{u}},{\bm{v}})

\displaystyle={\mathcal{N}}({\bm{u}};{\mathbf{m}}_{\bm{u}},{\mathbf{S}}_{\bm{u}}){\mathcal{N}}({\bm{v}};\mathbf{K}_{\mathbf{vu}}\mathbf{K}_{\mathbf{uu}}^{-1}{\bm{u}}+{\mathbf{m}}_{\bm{v}},{\mathbf{S}}_{\bm{v}})

\displaystyle={\mathcal{N}}\left(\begin{bmatrix}{\bm{u}}\\
{\bm{v}}\end{bmatrix};\begin{bmatrix}{\mathbf{m}}_{\bm{u}}\\
\mathbf{K}_{\mathbf{vu}}\mathbf{K}_{\mathbf{uu}}^{-1}{\mathbf{m}}_{\bm{u}}+{\mathbf{m}}_{\bm{v}}\end{bmatrix},\begin{bmatrix}{\mathbf{S}}_{\bm{u}}&{\mathbf{S}}_{\bm{u}}\mathbf{K}_{\mathbf{uu}}^{-1}\mathbf{K}_{\mathbf{uv}}\\
\mathbf{K}_{\mathbf{vu}}\mathbf{K}_{\mathbf{uu}}^{-1}{\mathbf{S}}_{\bm{u}}&{\mathbf{S}}_{\bm{v}}+\mathbf{K}_{\mathbf{vu}}\mathbf{K}_{\mathbf{uu}}^{-1}{\mathbf{S}}_{\bm{u}}\mathbf{K}_{\mathbf{uu}}^{-1}\mathbf{K}_{\mathbf{uv}}\end{bmatrix}\right),

where
({\mathbf{m}}_{\bm{u}},{\mathbf{S}}_{\bm{u}})
and
({\mathbf{m}}_{\bm{v}},{\mathbf{S}}_{\bm{v}})
are the mean and covariance variational parameters. This approximation brings computational benefits over naively using a single set of pseudo-points with cardinality
M=M_{\bm{u}}+M_{\bm{v}}
while matching the latter’s performance. We will show that the same trick used for sparse variational GPs—relaxing the conditional matching assumption
q({\bm{f}}|{\bm{u}},{\bm{v}})=p({\bm{f}}|{\bm{u}},{\bm{v}})
— can improve SOLVEGP. In particular, similar to SVGP, we will use
q({\bm{f}}|{\bm{u}},{\bm{v}})={\mathcal{N}}({\bm{f}};\mathbf{K}_{\mathbf{f,uv}}\mathbf{K}_{\mathbf{uv,uv}}^{-1}[{\bm{u}}^{\intercal},{\bm{v}}^{\intercal}]^{\intercal};\mathbf{D}_{\mathbf{ff}}^{1/2}{\mathbf{M}}\mathbf{D}_{\mathbf{ff}}^{\top/2})
where
\mathbf{D}_{\mathbf{ff}}=\mathbf{K}_{\mathbf{ff}}-\mathbf{K}_{\mathbf{f,uv}}\mathbf{K}_{\mathbf{uv,uv}}^{-1}\mathbf{K}_{\mathbf{uv,f}}
,
{\mathbf{M}}
is a diagonal matrix,
{\mathbf{M}}=\mathrm{diag}([m_{1},m_{2},\dots,m_{N}])
and
m_{n}>0
. The resulting variational bound is

\displaystyle{\mathcal{F}}_{6}(q({\bm{u}},{\bm{v}}),\theta,{\mathbf{M}})

\displaystyle=\left\langle\frac{\log\cancel{p(f_{\neq{\bm{f}},{\bm{u}},{\bm{v}}}|{\bm{f}},{\bm{u}},{\bm{v}})}p({\bm{f}}|{\bm{u}},{\bm{v}})p({\bm{u}},{\bm{v}})p({\bm{y}}|f,{\bm{x}})}{\cancel{p(f_{\neq{\bm{f}},{\bm{u}},{\bm{v}}}|{\bm{f}},{\bm{u}},{\bm{v}})}q({\bm{f}}|{\bm{u}},{\bm{v}})q({\bm{u}},{\bm{v}})}\right\rangle_{q(f)}

\displaystyle=-\mathrm{KL}[q({\bm{u}})||p({\bm{u}})]-\mathrm{KL}[\tilde{q}({\bm{v}})||\tilde{p}({\bm{v}})]+\frac{1}{2}\sum_{n}[1+\log(m_{n})-m_{n}]

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad+\sum_{n}\int_{{\bm{u}},{\bm{v}},f(x_{n})}q({\bm{u}},{\bm{v}})q(f({x_{n}})|{\bm{u}},{\bm{v}})\log p(y_{n}|f({x_{n}})).,

(14)

where
\tilde{q}({\bm{v}})={\mathcal{N}}({\bm{v}};{\mathbf{m}}_{\bm{v}},{\mathbf{S}}_{\bm{v}})
,
\tilde{p}({\bm{v}})={\mathcal{N}}({\bm{v}};{\bm{0}},\mathbf{C}_{\mathbf{vv}})
, and
\mathbf{C}_{\mathbf{vv}}=\mathbf{K}_{\mathbf{vv}}-\mathbf{K}_{\mathbf{vu}}\mathbf{K}_{\mathbf{uu}}^{-1}\mathbf{K}_{\mathbf{uv}}
. Note that the predictive distribution at a training point can be approximated efficiently,
q(f(x_{n}))\approx{\mathcal{N}}(f(x_{n});m_{n},v_{n})
with

\displaystyle m_{n}

\displaystyle=\mathbf{k}_{f_{n}{\bm{u}}}\mathbf{K}_{\mathbf{uu}}^{-1}{\mathbf{m}}_{\bm{u}}+\mathbf{c}_{f_{n}{\bm{v}}}\mathbf{C}_{\mathbf{vv}}^{-1}{\mathbf{m}}_{\bm{u}},

\displaystyle v_{n}

\displaystyle=m_{n}(\mathbf{c}_{f_{n}f_{n}}-\mathbf{c}_{f_{n}{\bm{v}}}\mathbf{C}_{\mathbf{vv}}^{-1}\mathbf{c}_{{\bm{v}}f_{n}})+\mathbf{k}_{\mathbf{*u}}\mathbf{K}_{\mathbf{uu}}^{-1}{\mathbf{S}}_{\bm{u}}\mathbf{K}_{\mathbf{uu}}^{-1}\mathbf{k}_{\mathbf{u*}}+\mathbf{c}_{f_{n}{\bm{v}}}\mathbf{C}_{\mathbf{vv}}^{-1}{\mathbf{S}}_{\bm{v}}\mathbf{C}_{\mathbf{vv}}^{-1}\mathbf{c}_{f_{n}{\bm{v}}}

where
\mathbf{c}_{ab}=\mathbf{k}_{ab}-\mathbf{k}_{a{\bm{u}}}\mathbf{K}_{\mathbf{uu}}^{-1}\mathbf{k}_{{\bm{u}}b}
. Similar to SVGP, the predictive variance at a new test point is expensive due to the dependence on all training points. However, similar to the tighter approximation in
section

3
, we found that simply ignoring this difficult term works well in practice.

5
Application to Bayesian GP latent variable models

Consider a GP latent variable model
(
Lawrence 2005
, GPLVM;)
with Gaussian observation noise:

\displaystyle p({\bm{x}})={\mathcal{N}}({\bm{x}};0,{\mathbf{I}}),

\displaystyle p(f|\gamma)=\mathcal{GP}(f;0,k_{\gamma}),

\displaystyle p({\bm{y}}|f,{\bm{x}})={\mathcal{N}}({\bm{y}};f({\bm{x}}),\sigma^{2}{\mathbf{I}}).

Both the posterior
p(f|{\bm{y}})
and marginal likelihood
p({\bm{y}})
are intractable. Instead, we introduce an approximate posterior of the following form:

\displaystyle q(f,{\bm{x}})=q({\bm{x}})p(f_{\neq{\bm{f}},{\bm{u}}}|{\bm{f}},{\bm{u}})q({\bm{f}}|{\bm{u}},{\bm{x}})q({\bm{u}})

\displaystyle q({\bm{f}}|{\bm{u}},{\bm{x}})={\mathcal{N}}({\bm{f}};\mathbf{K}_{\mathbf{fu}}\mathbf{K}_{\mathbf{uu}}^{-1}{\bm{u}},\mathbf{D}_{\mathbf{ff}}^{1/2}{\mathbf{M}}({\bm{x}})\mathbf{D}_{\mathbf{ff}}^{\top/2}),

where
{\mathbf{M}}({\bm{x}})=\mathrm{diag}([m_{1}(x_{1}),m_{2}(x_{2}),\dots,m_{N}(x_{N})])
and
m_{n}(x_{n})>0
. Note that
q({\bm{f}}|{\bm{u}},{\bm{x}})
depends on
{\bm{x}}
through
\mathbf{K}_{\mathbf{fu}}
,
\mathbf{D}_{\mathbf{ff}}
and
{\mathbf{M}}({\bm{x}})
, and that when
{\mathbf{M}}
is the identity matrix, that is
m_{n}=1
, we obtain the variational approximation of
Damianou et al. 2016
. We can bound the log marginal likelihood as

\displaystyle{\mathcal{F}}(q(f,{\bm{x}}),\theta)

\displaystyle=-\mathrm{KL}[q({\bm{x}})\|p({\bm{x}})]-\mathrm{KL}[q({\bm{u}})\|p({\bm{u}})]+\frac{1}{2}\sum_{n}\left\langle 1+\log(m(x_{n}))-m(x_{n})\right\rangle_{q(x_{n})}

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad+\sum_{n}\int_{{\bm{u}},x_{n},f(x_{n})}q(x_{n})q({\bm{u}})q(f(x_{n})|x_{n},{\bm{u}})\log p(y_{n}|f(x_{n})).

We can obtain the collapsed bound by noting that the optimal form for
q({\bm{u}})
is given by

q({\bm{u}})\propto p({\bm{u}})\exp\left(\langle\log{\mathcal{N}}({\bm{y}};\mathbf{K}_{\mathbf{fu}}\mathbf{K}_{\mathbf{uu}}^{-1}{\bm{u}},\sigma^{2}{\mathbf{I}})\rangle_{q({\bm{x}})}\right).

Note also that

\int_{{\bm{u}},{\bm{f}}}q({\bm{u}})q({\bm{f}}|{\bm{u}})\log p({\bm{y}}|{\bm{f}})=\int_{{\bm{u}}}q({\bm{u}})\log{\mathcal{N}}({\bm{y}};\mathbf{K}_{\mathbf{fu}}\mathbf{K}_{\mathbf{uu}}^{-1}{\bm{u}},\sigma^{2}{\mathbf{I}})-\sum_{n}\frac{m_{n}(x_{n})d_{n}}{2\sigma^{2}}.

Together with Jensen’s inequality, we arrive at the collapsed bound

\displaystyle{\mathcal{F}}(q({\bm{x}}))

\displaystyle=-\mathrm{KL}[q({\bm{x}})\|p({\bm{x}})]-\frac{1}{2}\sum_{n}\left\langle\frac{m_{n}(x_{n})d_{n}}{2\sigma^{2}}-1-\log m_{n}(x_{n})+m_{n}(x_{n})\right\rangle_{q(x_{n})}

7
Summary

We build upon the standard sparse variational Gaussian process (SVGP) approximate posterior distribution through a simple modification to the conditional GP prior distribution at observed inputs. Using our proposed posterior approximation, we derive a collapsed bound which improves upon existing SVGP lower bounds to the log marginal likelihood, and an uncollapsed form which facilitates its application with non-Gaussian likelihoods and is compatible with stochastic mini-batch optimisation. Furthermore, we show how our approach can be used to improve non-standard SVGP posterior approximations, such as SOLVE-GP
(
Shi et al. 2020
)
.

Our empirical results demonstrate consistent improvements in both predictive performance and log marginal likelihood estimates across diverse applications, including regression, classification, and latent variables modelling tasks. The proposed posterior approximations can be easily applied to other settings such as deep GPs and convolutional GPs
(
Van der Wilk et al. 2017
;
Blomqvist et al. 2020
;
Sun et al. 2021
;
Bui et al. 2016
;
Salimbeni & Deisenroth 2017
)
.

Appendix A
An even tighter but expensive approximation

We consider a more general form for the conditional covariance of
q({\bm{f}}|{\bm{u}})
as follows:

\displaystyle q(f)

\displaystyle=p(f_{\neq{\bm{f}},{\bm{u}}}|{\bm{f}},{\bm{u}})q({\bm{f}}|{\bm{u}})q({\bm{u}}),

\displaystyle q({\bm{f}}|{\bm{u}})

\displaystyle={\mathcal{N}}({\bm{f}};\mathbf{K}_{\mathbf{fu}}\mathbf{K}_{\mathbf{uu}}^{-1}{\bm{u}};{\mathbf{C}}),

Again, we can also obtain the optimal form for
q(u)\propto p({\bm{u}}){\mathcal{N}}({\bm{y}};\mathbf{K}_{\mathbf{fu}}\mathbf{K}_{\mathbf{uu}}^{-1}{\bm{u}},\sigma^{2}{\mathbf{I}})
, leading to the following collapsed bound

\displaystyle{\mathcal{F}}_{6}(\theta)

\displaystyle={\bm{c}}-\frac{1}{2}{\bm{y}}^{\intercal}(\mathbf{Q}_{\mathbf{ff}}+\sigma^{2}{\mathbf{I}})^{-1}{\bm{y}}-\frac{1}{2}\log|\mathbf{Q}_{\mathbf{ff}}+\sigma^{2}{\mathbf{I}}|-\frac{1}{2}\mathrm{trace}[(\sigma^{-2}{\mathbf{I}}+\mathbf{D}_{\mathbf{ff}}^{-1}){\mathbf{C}}]-\frac{1}{2}\log|{\mathbf{C}}^{-1}\mathbf{D}_{\mathbf{ff}}|.

We can derive the optimal
{\mathbf{C}}
,
{\mathbf{C}}^{-1}=\mathbf{D}_{\mathbf{ff}}^{-1}+\sigma^{-2}{\mathbf{I}}
and the bound becomes:

\displaystyle{\mathcal{F}}_{8}(\theta)

\displaystyle={\bm{c}}-\frac{1}{2}{\bm{y}}^{\intercal}(\mathbf{Q}_{\mathbf{ff}}+\sigma^{2}{\mathbf{I}})^{-1}{\bm{y}}-\frac{1}{2}\log|\mathbf{Q}_{\mathbf{ff}}+\sigma^{2}{\mathbf{I}}|-\frac{1}{2}\log|{\mathbf{I}}+\sigma^{-2}\mathbf{D}_{\mathbf{ff}}|

\displaystyle={\bm{c}}-\frac{1}{2}{\bm{y}}^{\intercal}(\mathbf{Q}_{\mathbf{ff}}+\sigma^{2}{\mathbf{I}})^{-1}{\bm{y}}-\frac{1}{2}\log|\mathbf{Q}_{\mathbf{ff}}+\sigma^{2}{\mathbf{I}}|-\frac{1}{2}\sum_{n}\log(1+\sigma^{-2}\lambda_{n}(\mathbf{D}_{\mathbf{ff}})|,

where
\lambda_{n}({\mathbf{X}})
is the
n
-th eigenvalue of
{\mathbf{X}}
. The bound above is as expensive as the original log marginal likelihood.

Appendix B
Exploring alternative parameterisations for the conditional posterior

Instead of the general
{\mathbf{C}}
as above or the form considered in the main text
{\mathbf{C}}=\mathbf{D}_{\mathbf{ff}}^{1/2}{\mathbf{M}}\mathbf{D}_{\mathbf{ff}}^{\top/2}
, we consider two other parameterisations that might allow efficient collapsed/un-collapsed bounds and predictions. We first rewrite the uncollapsed bound and the predictive mean and variance here for clarity,

\displaystyle{\mathcal{F}}_{\textrm{uncollapsed}}

\displaystyle=-\mathrm{KL}[q({\bm{u}})||p({\bm{u}})]-\int_{\bm{u}}q({\bm{u}})\mathrm{KL}[q({\bm{f}}|{\bm{u}})||p({\bm{f}}|{\bm{u}})]+\sum_{n}\int_{{\bm{u}},f(x_{n})}q({\bm{u}})q(f({x_{n}})|{\bm{u}})\log p(y_{n}|f({x_{n}})),

\displaystyle m_{*}

\displaystyle=\mathbf{k}_{\mathbf{*u}}\mathbf{K}_{\mathbf{uu}}^{-1}{\mathbf{m}}_{\bm{u}},

\displaystyle v_{*}

\displaystyle=\mathbf{k}_{\mathbf{**}}-\mathbf{k}_{\mathbf{*u}}\mathbf{K}_{\mathbf{uu}}^{-1}\mathbf{k}_{\mathbf{u*}}+\mathbf{k}_{\mathbf{*u}}\mathbf{K}_{\mathbf{uu}}^{-1}{\mathbf{S}}_{\bm{u}}\mathbf{K}_{\mathbf{uu}}^{-1}\mathbf{k}_{\mathbf{u*}}-(\mathbf{k}_{\mathbf{*f}}-\mathbf{Q}_{\mathbf{*f}})(\mathbf{D}_{\mathbf{ff}}-{\mathbf{C}})(\mathbf{k}_{\mathbf{f*}}-\mathbf{Q}_{\mathbf{f*}}),

We first consider
{\mathbf{C}}={\mathbf{M}}^{1/2}\mathbf{D}_{\mathbf{ff}}{\mathbf{M}}^{1/2}
. While this allows efficient exact predictive marginal distributions at training points, the middle term in the bound is costly to compute due to the presence of
\mathbf{D}_{\mathbf{ff}}
:

\displaystyle-\int_{\bm{u}}q({\bm{u}})\mathrm{KL}[q({\bm{f}}|{\bm{u}})||p({\bm{f}}|{\bm{u}})]=-\frac{1}{2}\mathrm{trace}(\mathbf{D}_{\mathbf{ff}}^{-1}{\mathbf{M}}^{1/2}\mathbf{D}_{\mathbf{ff}}{\mathbf{M}}^{1/2})+\frac{1}{2}\log|{\mathbf{M}}|+\frac{N}{2}.

Another special case of the parameterisation presented in the main text is
{\mathbf{C}}=m\mathbf{D}_{\mathbf{ff}}
, i.e., a single
m
is shared across all training points. This conveniently leads to tractable exact predictive variances at training points,
v_{n}=md_{n}+\mathbf{k}_{f_{n}{\bm{u}}}\mathbf{K}_{\mathbf{uu}}^{-1}{\mathbf{S}}_{\bm{u}}\mathbf{K}_{\mathbf{uu}}^{-1}\mathbf{k}_{{\bm{u}}f_{n}}
. The middle term in the bound can be simplified to,

\displaystyle-\int_{\bm{u}}q({\bm{u}})\mathrm{KL}[q({\bm{f}}|{\bm{u}})||p({\bm{f}}|{\bm{u}})]=\frac{N}{2}[1+\log(m)-m].

In the regression case, this leads to the optimal
m=\sigma^{2}/(N^{-1}\sum_{n}d_{n}+\sigma^{2})
and the following collapsed bound,

\displaystyle{\mathcal{F}}_{9}(\theta)={\bm{c}}-\frac{1}{2}{\bm{y}}^{\intercal}(\mathbf{Q}_{\mathbf{ff}}+\sigma^{2}{\mathbf{I}})^{-1}{\bm{y}}-\frac{1}{2}\log|\mathbf{Q}_{\mathbf{ff}}+\sigma^{2}{\mathbf{I}}|-\frac{N}{2}\log\left(1+\frac{\sum_{n}d_{n}}{N\sigma^{2}}\right).

(15)

This bound is looser than the collapsed bound in
eq.

9
, due to the Jensen’s inequality
\log(1+\sum_{n}x_{n}/N)\geq N^{-1}\sum_{n}\log(1+x_{n})
.
